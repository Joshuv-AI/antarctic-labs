When Symmetries Twist:
Anomaly Inflow on Monodromy Defects

Abstract
Monodromy defects describe a dynamical termination of topological symmetry operators, and are sourced by a localized background magnetic flux. We study their properties in gapped SPT phases and, by inflow, in gapless theories with an anomalous symmetry. The background flux acts as a source for the anomaly, impacting the definition of the monodromy defect, which can only be defined as a domain wall between the symmetry generator and a topological order induced by the anomaly. The topological dressing has several consequences, such as the presence of protected chiral edge modes on the defect’s worldvolume, and the adiabatic pumping of gapless degrees of freedom bound to the localized flux. We verify our predictions in several examples, focusing on monodromy defects for anomalous chiral symmetries, both in the continuum and on the lattice.

Introduction

The space of defects in a quantum system has been the subject of intense recent study: defects arise naturally as impurities in condensed-matter setups, and serve as probes of strongly coupled bulk dynamics.
Topological defects in particular — i.e. symmetries
[

Much less is known about dynamical defects, which are ubiquitous in critical setups. These are often strongly coupled objects and cannot be fully captured by perturbative techniques outside of limited regimes.

Topological and dynamical defects are, however, intimately related: symmetries naturally act on extended excitations via the appropriate “higher” algebraic structure. Such action provides novel, non-perturbative, constraints on the physical properties of defects.

Monodromy defects and their symmetries

We start by reviewing the definition of monodromy defects
\textbf{M}(g)
and how global symmetries are realized in their presence. This will serve to set notation and ideas for the remainder of the paper. Parallel discussions can be found in several recent works
[
75
,
19
,
98
]
. A discussion in the context of quantum lattice models is deferred to Section
6
.
We will focus mainly on the case of a 0-form symmetry
\mathbf{G}
, which can be either discrete or continuous.

2.1
Monodromy defects

As explained in the introduction, monodromy
\textbf{M}(g)
defects can be understood as implementing (generically non-topological) terminations of bulk symmetry defects
U(g)
.
Alternatively, they describe a system on which twisted boundary conditions around the symmetry surface are imposed. Employing cylindrical coordinates around
\textbf{M}(g)

ds^{2}=d\rho^{2}+\rho^{2}d\theta^{2}+h_{ab}dy^{a}dy^{b}\,,

(2.1)

where
h_{ab}
is the induced metric on the defect’s worldvolume
\Sigma
, a fundamental field
\Phi
in a representation
R_{g}
of
\mathbf{G}
satisfies:

\Phi(\rho,\theta+2\pi,y^{a})=R_{g}^{-1}\cdot\Phi(\rho,\theta,y^{a})\,.

(2.2)

The appearance of
R_{g}^{-1}
stems from our conventions for the orientation of the defect.
For continuous
\mathbf{G}=U(1)
we write
g=e^{2\pi i\nu Q}
, with
Q
the
U(1)
charge generator and
\nu\in[0,1)
the
twist parameter
;
\nu
will play a central role throughout the paper.
In a critical setup, the
S^{1}
spanned by
\theta
can be made of fixed radius by a conformal transformation. In these coordinates, the space of states underlying the twisted field configurations differs from the Hilbert space of the theory without defect, and is often denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{g}
(the twisted Hilbert space).

We can also describe the monodromy defect as a disorder operator by introducing a locally flat, singular background gauge field
A
for
\mathbf{G}
with a prescribed monodromy around any circle
C
linking
\textbf{M}(g)
:

\exp\left(i\oint_{C}A\right)=g\in\mathbf{G}\,.

(2.3)

Notice that, for a non-abelian
\mathbf{G}
, this breaks the symmetry group to the centralizer
C_{\mathbf{G}}(g)
of
g
.
4
4

4

Recall that the centralizer
C_{\mathbf{G}}(g)
of
g
is defined as the set of elements
h\in\mathbf{G}
such that
h^{-1}gh=g
.

From this perspective, the monodromy defect constitutes an infinitely thin solenoid which is threaded by magnetic background flux for the bulk
\mathbf{G}
gauge field. Dialing the strength of the magnetic field describes different ways to “twist” the system and allows to adiabatically interpolate between monodromy defects for different
U(g)
s. Such interpolation allows us to describe a “family” of monodromy defects, labeled by the parameter
g\in\mathbf{G}
. Notice however that, contrary to most “family-style” studies, the tuning of
g
does not correspond to deformation by a
local
operator on
\textbf{M}(g)
.
5
5

5

However, monodromy defects are still bona-fide local dynamical objects, as they have e.g. a well defined displacement operator and defect OPE.

The definition of a monodromy defect is far from unique. Performing defect fusion with any genuine defect leads to new monodromy defects. In practice, we will implicitly assume that the defects studied are in some sense “minimal”, for example they minimize the appropriate defect central charge.

The concept of monodromy defects can be generalized to higher-form symmetries
\mathbf{G}^{(p)}
, describing a
(d-p-2)
-dimensional dynamical defect where the
(p+1)
-form background gauge field
B^{(p+1)}
has prescribed monodromy on the linking sphere
S^{p+1}
around
\textbf{M}(g)
:

\exp\left(i\oint_{S^{p+1}}B^{(p+1)}\right)=g_{p}\in\mathbf{G}^{(p)}\,.

(2.4)

Such defects do not describe twisted boundary conditions for
local
operators, but rather for extended, charged probes, such as Wilson lines. In this paper we will mainly focus on 0-form symmetries, but many of our findings generalize easily to higher-form symmetries.

Conformal map and the defect Hilbert space

For a critical theory, there are several different Weyl frames which make the operator content of the twist defect manifest. A particularly natural one is the “radial quantization” frame, which leverages the state-operator correspondence of a critical bulk system.

In practice, one performs a conformal mapping to
S^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}
. The
\mathbb{R}
factor represents time, and the remaining defect directions occupy an
S^{d-3}
inside the
S^{d-1}
. Precisely we have:

\rho=e^{\tau}\sin(u)\,,\ \ \ y^{a}=e^{\tau}\cos(u)n^{a}\,,

(2.5)

where
n^{a}
is a unit vector on
S^{d-3}
. The metric is (removing a Weyl factor of
e^{2\tau}
):

ds^{2}=d\tau^{2}+du^{2}+\cos^{2}(u)d\Omega_{d-3}^{2}+\sin^{2}(u)d\theta^{2}\,.

(2.6)

Infinite past corresponds to the point
\rho=y^{a}=0
on the defect worldvolume, so by the state-operator correspondence the partition function on this geometry computes the spectrum of local defect operators. For
d=(1+1)
, this recovers the radial quantization on the cylinder, while for
d=(3+1)
the
S^{3}
is parametrized in Hopf coordinates:

ds^{2}=d\tau^{2}+du^{2}+\cos^{2}(u)d\phi^{2}+\sin^{2}(u)d\theta^{2}\,.

(2.7)

Notice that fields can be mildly singular (but still square-integrable) at the defect’s location
u\sim 0
. This conformal map allows us to define a Hilbert space of states on
\textbf{M}(g)
, the
twisted
Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{g}
.

2.2
Global symmetries of
\textbf{M}(g)

We now discuss the symmetry preserved by a monodromy defect
\textbf{M}(g)
.

Internal Symmetry.

If
\mathbf{G}
is a continuous non-abelian group its current
J^{\mu}
is itself twisted by the boundary conditions. Thus, the only surviving bulk currents in the presence of
\textbf{M}(g)
are those which commute with (the adjoint action of)
g
.
This is the avatar of a well-known phenomenon: in the presence of monodromy the symmetry group preserved by the defect is reduced to the centralizer

C_{\mathbf{G}}(g)\subseteq\mathbf{G}\,.

(2.8)

If the symmetry is discrete, the same conclusion can be reached by considering the action of a bulk symmetry operator
U(h)
on the defect system, which sends
U(g)
to
U(h^{-1}gh)
.
Clearly, if
h
does not lie in the centralizer, the defect
\textbf{M}(g)
is mapped into
\textbf{M}(h^{-1}gh)
.
6
6

6

In other words, the family
{\textbf{M}(h^{-1}gh)}_{h\in\mathbf{G}}
is permuted by the bulk
\mathbf{G}
-action. In this work, however, we largely focus on the symmetry of a given fixed defect.

Notice that these observations do not say anything about whether
C_{\mathbf{G}}(g)
is broken by the monodromy defect itself. The precise breaking pattern depends on the microscopic definition of
\textbf{M}(g)
: for example, we could trigger symmetry breaking by turning on a relevant defect operator on
\textbf{M}(g)
.
Furthermore, the breaking
\mathbf{G}\to C_{\mathbf{G}}(g)
is not localized on
\textbf{M}(g)
, but sourced by the insertion of the topological defect
U(g)
. As such, it does not give rise to a well-defined tilt operator
[
104
,
51
,
58
,
79
]
on the
\textbf{M}(g)
worldvolume:

d\star J\neq t(y^{a})\delta(\Sigma)\,.

(2.9)

Consequently monodromy defects for different
g
cannot be reached from one another by a localized defect deformation. In this sense the family
{\textbf{M}(g)}_{g\in\mathbf{G}}
is rather different from the standard families of symmetry-breaking defects studied in the literature.

Spacetime symmetry.

Just as interesting is the interplay between the monodromy and the spacetime symmetry in the presence of the defect. Recall that a codimension-2 conformal defect preserves a subgroup:

SO(d-2,2)\times SO(2)\,,

(2.10)

of the bulk conformal group. The first factor describes the conformal symmetry on the defect’s worldvolume, while the second encodes transverse rotations. In the case of a monodromy defect
\textbf{M}(g)
, while the first factor is obviously preserved, the second is trickier to discuss.
A naive picture is that a transverse rotation also moves the attached topological surface
U(g)
, which can then be undone by its topological invariance. This is only correct up to the
\mathbf{G}
-action on charged fields, as we now make precise.

For a fixed
g\in\mathbf{G}
we can always go into an appropriate Cartan basis and represent the twist as a
U(1)
rotation by an angle
2\pi\nu
. In this case the (irreducible) components of
\Phi
transform under a
2\pi
transverse rotation as:

\Phi(\rho,\theta+2\pi,y^{a})=e^{2\pi i(-\nu n_{\Phi}+s)}\Phi(\rho,\theta,y^{a})\,,

(2.11)

where
s
is the spin of
\Phi
under the transverse
SO(2)
and
n_{\Phi}
is its
\mathbf{G}
-charge.
This immediately implies that the (naive) SO(2) quantum number
s
of
\Phi
is quantized as:

s\in-n_{\Phi}\nu+\mathbb{Z}\,.

(2.12)

Equivalently, the transverse
SO(2)
rotation gets entangled with the bulk
\mathbf{G}
symmetry: a
2\pi
rotation acts on charged fields as
e^{-2\pi i\nu Q}
.
For discrete
\mathbf{G}=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
this realizes the physical rotation as an
n
-fold cover of
SO(2)
,

1\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{n}\longrightarrow SO(2)_{\rm phys}\longrightarrow SO(2)\longrightarrow 1\,,

(2.13)

reflecting the fractional quantization of
s
in eq. (
2.12
).
For continuous
\mathbf{G}
, the mixing is unobstructed and can be undone by passing to the redefined generator

\widehat{Q}_{SO(2)}=Q_{SO(2)}+\nu Q\,,

(2.14)

which generates a compact
SO(2)
.

Monodromy defects as SPT probes

In this Section, we consider the definition of topological monodromy defects in a bulk Symmetry Protected Topological (SPT) phase. We will denote these objects by
\mathbf{T}(g)
, to distinguish them from their dynamical counterparts
\textbf{M}(g)
to which they will be related by inflow. We will connect this discussion with the one of anomalous gapless theories in Section
4
by considering edge modes of the SPT analysis.

However, there is also a physical motivation to consider this setup by itself: the monodromy defects
\mathbf{T}(g)
allow to
probe
the bulk SPT without resorting to boundary edge modes, by considering the response of the system to localized (background) fluxes instead. These properties provide natural generalizations of “string” order parameters considered in the SPT literature.

3.1
Decorated symmetry defects and slant product

(3.1)

3.2
The topological monodromy
\mathbf{T}(g)

Having discussed how to consistently decorate bulk symmetry defects in the bulk SPT, we now move to the question of their termination, which defines the topological monodromy defect
\mathbf{T}(g)
. We will use this to infer properties of the bulk SPT, and to then describe monodromy defects in gapless anomalous theories by inflow.

In the presence of a dynamical monodromy defect, the bulk defect
\widehat{U}(g)
must also be terminated topologically, as seen on the right side of Figure

To preserve bulk topological invariance, the SPT
\tau(g)

\mathbf{T}(g)

There is no unique choice of
\mathbf{T}(g)
. This follows e.g. from the ability of decorating it by a decoupled
(d-1)

In practice, however, there is often a “minimal” choice, which e.g. minimizes the partition function of
\mathbf{T}(g)
on compact manifolds. The exact realization of a monodromy defect will depend on the microscopic details of the theory, but a set of its topological properties, which descend from the bulk SPT, will be universal. Depending on
\tau(g)
we can encounter several distinct scenarios.

\mathbf{T}(g)
spontaneously breaks
\mathbf{G}

Often, a gapped boundary condition
\mathbf{T}(g)

\tau(g)
is a nontrivial SPT that exhibits
symmetry-enforced gaplessness

Several bulk realizations of this phenomenon are described, for example, in

\exp\left(i\int_{M}A^{*}\tau(g)\right)\neq 1\,.

(3.11)

As the edge modes of the monodromy defect
\textbf{M}(g)
must spontaneously break the residual

symmetry, the twisted Hilbert space becomes a direct sum:

\mathcal{H}_{g}=\bigoplus_{m}\mathcal{H}_{g}(m)\,,

where

corresponds to a vacuum of the topological order

A classic example appears when

This is also a recurrent example in (2+1)d type III anomalies: in this case the bulk theory is a class

and
\tau(g)\in H^{2}(C_{\mathbf{G}}(g),U(1))
a nontrivial class.
\mathbf{T}(g)

C_{\mathbf{G}}(g)

\mathbf{G}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{3}

\mathbf{T}(g)
transforms under an extension of
\mathbf{G}

In this case, the gapped boundary condition

A gapped

does not exist

The most extreme scenario is when the bulk gap closes in the presence of localized flux.

3.3
Spectral Pump and Anomaly in the space of defect couplings

An interesting signature of the anomaly arises when studying continuous symmetries with nontrivial
\pi_{1}(\mathbf{G})
, e.g.
\mathbf{G}=U(1)
.
In this setup we can envision adiabatically turning on a localized flux for the
\mathbf{G}

\exp\left(i\oint_{C}A(s)\right)=g(s)\,,\ \ \ g(0)=g(1)\,,\hskip 10.00002pts\in[0,1]

describing a nontrivial class

\tau(g)
must be invariant between the two endpoints, its representative can change:

\tau(g(1))=\tau(g(0))+d\omega(\gamma)\,.

(3.26)

Where we have highlighted the dependence on the homotopy class
\gamma

does not depend on small deformations of
\gamma

Following our previous discussion, we conclude that the monodromy defect
\mathbf{T}(g(1))
is modified by stacking with an invertible phase

\mathbf{T}(g(1))=\mathbf{T}(g(0))\otimes\omega(\gamma)\,.

(3.27)

For example, if

\omega(\gamma)

This is a monodromy-defect version of the Thouless pump
[

]

Similar phenomena appear in recent discussions of anomalies in the space of (defect) couplings
[

In this context, we should regard
\mathbf{T}(g)
as a defect family over
\mathbf{G}

\mathbf{T}(g)
to be symmetric for the construction to hold: the spectral pump probes the homotopy of
\mathbf{G}
rather than
C_{\mathbf{G}}(g)
.

The anomaly in the space of couplings also comes with another striking feature: since along the loop there is a nontrivial spectral flow, there must be a point at which level crossing happens. In higher dimension this usually means that we have an emergent symmetry

A

•

(3.28)

and the two theories are exchanged by the emergent symmetry at

•

(3.29)

thus, it remains consistent by absorbing the pumped SPT. Notice that in this case the anomaly must descend from the bulk by transgression.

We will see examples of such level crossing in Section

and Appendix

\textbf{M}(g)
in

•

•

Electrically charged
\mathbf{T}(g)

When the topological monodromy defect

We will not consider this scenario further in this work.

No topological

In this case, the monodromy defect is ill-defined, in the sense that it will not preserve the

5.1
Transgression and topological dressing

We now describe how to define monodromy defects for chiral fermions.

Weyl fermion

From the form of the ’t Hooft anomaly of the single Weyl fermion, one can immediately compute the transgression, where we set

5.2
Axial pump

We now describe the pump induced by inserting a
2\pi
axial flux into the thin solenoid representing the monodromy defect,
\nu\to\nu+1
. Following the general discussion of Section
3.3
, this loop traces a nontrivial element of
\pi_{1}(U(1)_{A})=\mathbb{Z}
and pumps an invertible phase
\omega(\gamma)
onto the worldvolume of
\textbf{M}(\nu)
.

For a single Weyl fermion the pumped SPT is a
U(1)_{1}
phase:

\omega(\gamma)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int A_{V}\,dA_{V}+\text{CS}_{g}\,.

(5.22)

For a Dirac fermion, the effect of the chiral transformation stacks between the
L
and
R
Weyl components and the pumped SPT is doubled,
2\cdot\omega(\gamma)
. By anomaly inflow, this requires surplus chiral edge modes localized on
\textbf{M}(2\pi)
: a single chiral
(1+1)d
Weyl in the Weyl-fermion case, and two
(1+1)d
Weyl fermions of the same chirality in the Dirac case.

This is the Callan–Harvey inflow mechanism
[
29
]
applied to a gapless bulk: the bound modes are the genuine
(1+1)d
Weyl edge modes of the pumped invertible
U(1)_{1}
SPT, and are resilient — they cannot be removed without breaking the anomalous axial symmetry explicitly.

We will show below, by direct analysis of the multi-valuedness of the b-function (Section
5.4
), that for most families of monodromy defects these bound modes do not correspond to a decoupled
(1+1)d
CFT, but to an interacting defect dressed by the topological orders identified above.

The axial pump also organizes the physics at the special point
\nu=1/2
: this is the level-crossing point along the loop
\nu\in[0,1)
, where the general argument of Section
3.3
predicts that the monodromy defect must either spontaneously break the emergent time-reversal symmetry as
\textbf{M}(1/2)^{+}\oplus\textbf{M}(1/2)^{-}
, or carry a topological order absorbing half of the pumped SPT. We worked this out explicitly in Section
3

5
will confirm that the first option (symmetry breaking) is the one realized.

5.3
A free fermion analysis

To better understand the physics of the monodromy defect, we can directly analyze the mode expansion of the free fermion in the background of a localized axial magnetic flux. Related studies have been performed in the past, see e.g.
[
19
]
for the vector case.

Throughout this analysis we distinguish two kinds of modes localized at the defect.
Singular modes
diverge as
r^{-\alpha}
at the location of the defect with
0<\alpha<1
and are normalizable.
Bound modes
correspond to the limiting case
\alpha=1
and are the genuine
(1+1)d
Weyl fermions localized on the defect’s worldvolume.

It is clear that the left and right modes (according to
\gamma_{5}
) are not mixed in the computation and can be treated separately.
We will denote the (1+1)d fermionic modes appearing in the bulk-to-defect OPE by:

\Psi=(\psi_{L},\,\psi_{R})\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\psi_{L}=(\chi_{L}^{+},\chi_{L}^{-})\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\psi_{R}=(\chi_{R}^{-},\,\chi_{R}^{+})\,.

(5.23)

where
L,R
identify the 4d chirality (i.e. the eigenvalue of
\gamma^{5}
) while
\pm
refers to the handedness of these fermions under the (1+1)d chiral symmetry localized on the defect. We choose the following basis of
\gamma

\gamma^{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma^{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&Z\\
-Z&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&X\\
-X&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma^{3}=\begin{pmatrix}0&Y\\
-Y&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(5.24)

With this choice of basis both
\gamma^{5}=i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{1}\gamma^{2}\gamma^{3}=\text{diag}(\mathbbm{1},-\mathbbm{1})
and the (1+1)d chirality matrix
\gamma_{0}\gamma_{1}
are diagonal. Notice that the 4d chirality eigenvalue flips the meaning of the 2d chirality.

Let us first understand the spectrum of a bulk Weyl fermion
\psi
in the monodromy background.
For simplicity, we will work on
\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
in cylindrical coordinates:

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+dx^{2}+dr^{2}+r^{2}d\theta^{2}\,,

(5.25)

with
\textbf{M}(g)

\psi(t,x,r,\theta+2\pi)=-e^{-2\pi i\nu}\psi(t,x,r,\theta)\,.

(5.26)

The minus sign corresponds to standard anti-periodic spin structure on the cycle surrounding the defect and we pick
\nu\in[0,1)
.
In our basis, the Weyl components correspond to eigenmodes of the 2d chirality, which we indicate by
\pm

\psi=(\psi_{+},\psi_{-})
. We expand the Weyl fermion in modes:

\psi(t,x,r,\theta)=e^{-iEt}e^{ipx}e^{im\theta}\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\begin{pmatrix}f_{+}(r)\\

The equations of motion, after some manipulation, take the form

\displaystyle f_{+}^{\prime}-\frac{m}{r}f_{+}=-i\left(E-p\right)f_{-}\,,

(5.28)

\displaystyle f_{-}^{\prime}+\frac{m}{r}f_{-}=-i\left(E+p\right)f_{+}\,.

(5.29)

These can be massaged into a Bessel equation:

f_{+}^{\prime\prime}+\left(k^{2}-\frac{(m-1)m}{r^{2}}\right)f_{+}=0\,,\hskip 20.00003ptk^{2}=E^{2}-p^{2}\,.

(5.30)

A similar equation holds for the right movers, with
m\to-m
. We want solutions which are regular at

and square-integrable. The solution takes the form:

\displaystyle\text{For }n>0:

\displaystyle(f_{+},f_{-})=C\sqrt{r}\left(J_{m-1/2}(kr),-i\frac{k}{E-p}J_{m+1/2}(kr)\right)\,,

(5.31)

\displaystyle\text{For }n<0:

\displaystyle(f_{+},f_{-})=\tilde{C}\sqrt{r}\left(J_{1/2-m}(kr),i\frac{k}{E-p}J_{-m-1/2}(kr)\right)\,.

In our chosen range for
\nu

n<0

For

\displaystyle\chi_{+}:\,\psi_{+}\sim r^{-\nu}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt

(5.32)

\displaystyle\chi_{-}:\,\psi_{-}\sim r^{-1+\nu}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\psi_{+}\sim r^{\nu}\,.

5.4
Spectral flow from the b-function

The aforementioned structure can be verified explicitly by computing defect central charges.
We will compute the change in the
b
-function of the monodromy defect as we pump a unit of flux
\nu\to\nu+1
.
This is defined as the coefficient of the (1+1)d Weyl anomaly which resides on the defect worldvolume
\Sigma
:

\delta_{\sigma}\log(Z_{\calD})=\frac{b}{24\pi}\int_{\Sigma}d^{2}x\sqrt{h}R_{\Sigma}\,.

(5.38)

In
[
19
]
, the
b
-function for the vector monodromy defect was computed by integrating the one-point function of the current operator
J_{\theta}
. The result is:

b_{V}(\nu,\xi)=\nu^{2}(2-\nu^{2}-2\xi(3-2\nu))+\xi\,,

(5.39)

where
\xi_{L}=\xi_{R}=\xi
is a vector-like mixing for the zero modes.
The b-function for a single chiral mode can be extracted from this by recalling that the modes are decoupled, and the symmetry
\nu\to 1-\nu
,
\xi\to 1-\xi
that exchanges chirality and the sign of the twist.
This implies that:

b_{L}(\nu\,,\xi)=b_{R}(1-\nu,1-\xi)=\frac{1}{2}b_{V}(\nu,\xi)\,.

(5.40)

Thus:

b_{A}(\nu,\xi_{L},\xi_{R})=b_{L}(\nu,\xi_{L})+b_{R}(1-\nu,1-\xi_{R})\,.

(5.41)

The vector and axial b-functions are multi-valued as
\nu\to\nu+1
:

b_{V}(1,0)-b_{V}(0,0)=1\,,\hskip 10.00002ptb_{A}(1,0,0)-b_{A}(0,0,0)=1\,.

(5.42)

As the monodromy defect at
\nu=1
is also a genuine defect, we can compute the change in its left and right central charges by:

\Delta c_{L}=\Delta b+\Delta k_{g}/2\,,\hskip 20.00003pt\Delta c_{R}=\Delta b-\Delta k_{g}/2\,,

(5.43)

where
k_{g}
is the coefficient of the gravitational anomaly on the defect.
For the vector monodromy defect
\Delta k_{g}=0
. The pumped degrees of freedom are thus non-chiral and we recover
\Delta c_{L}=\Delta c_{R}=1
. Furthermore, recalling our discussion of defect RG flows, the stable defect is actually given by
\textbf{M}(\nu,0)
for
\nu<1/2
and
\textbf{M}(\nu,1)
for
\nu>1/2
. Piecing together the two b-functions:

b_{\text{stable}}(\nu,\xi)=\begin{cases}b(\nu,\xi)\,\quad&0\leq\nu\leq 1/2\\
b(1-\nu,1-\xi)\,&1/2<\nu\leq 1,\end{cases}

(5.44)

we find a continuous and single valued b-function.
Thus, the pumped modes on the vector monodromy defect are not protected.

In the axial case, instead, we have
\Delta k_{g}=2
and the change in the central charges is:

\Delta c_{L}=2\,,\hskip 20.00003pt\Delta c_{R}=0\,.

(5.45)

As we have argued earlier, these modes are resilient and anomaly-induced, with chiral central charge
c_{-}=k_{g}
fixed by our arguments.

5.5
Defect modes, anyons and QED

We now discuss the relation between the defect singular modes and the anyons of
\mathbf{T}(\nu)
, and the conditions under which the two are decoupled (or not).

First, at generic
\nu
, the two are decoupled: anyons are nonlocal objects extending through
\mathbf{T}(\nu)
, and thus cannot appear in the OPE of any genuine bulk fermion.

Another way to excite the anyonic modes is to study the system in a background monopole configuration. Due to the symmetry fractionalization this excites a fractionally charged anyon on the
\textbf{M}(\nu)
worldvolume. The charge fractionalization can be read-off directly from the fractional Hall response.
Likewise, we can consider the dimensional reduction on
S^{2}
threaded with a unit of magnetic flux. This leads to a (1+1)d fermionic CFT, and the charge fractionalization can be read-off by the mixed vector-axial anomaly there.

A more interesting situation takes place if we instead study QED, i.e. we make the vector symmetry dynamical, denoting its gauge field by
a
.
In this case, it is by now well-known that
U(\nu)\times\mathbf{T}(\nu)
becomes a non-invertible duality defect
\mathbf{D}(\nu)
for rational
\nu

[
37
,
88
]
.
The theory
\mathbf{T}(\nu)
now explicitly interacts with the bulk gauge field by coupling to its field strength. This was the statement of symmetry fractionalization in the ungauged theory.

The gauge theory hosts several dynamical line operators. Wilson lines:

W_{n}[\gamma]=P\exp\left(in\int_{\gamma}a\right)=\exp\left(in\int_{\Sigma:\partial\Sigma=\gamma}da\right)\,,

(5.46)

and magnetic (’t Hooft) lines:

T_{k}[\gamma]=\exp\left(ik\int_{\Sigma:\partial\Sigma=\gamma}\star da\right)\,.

(5.47)

While the former can terminate on bulk Dirac fields, the latter cannot, as they carry charge under the magnetic one-form symmetry of the theory.
The situation is radically different in the presence of a duality monodromy defect
\textbf{M}(\nu)_{\mathbf{D}}
. Let us consider
\mathbf{D}(1/N)
for simplicity. This operator acts nontrivially on line defects by

T_{k}\mathbf{D}(1/N)=\mathbf{D}(1/N)W_{k/N}\,,

(5.48)

mapping a ’t Hooft line into an ill-quantized Wilson line (i.e. a monodromy defect for the magnetic one-form symmetry). In the presence of a monodromy defect, both objects can terminate on its worldvolume. The fractional gauge charge of the Wilson line is precisely matched by the anyonic excitations on
\textbf{M}(1/N)_{\mathbf{D}}
.
Thus, in the QED theory, the anyonic sector is not completely decoupled from the bulk and provides dynamical terminations of magnetic line operators, which only exist on the monodromy defect.

5.6
A fractional axion string

To conclude, we study another classic long distance model which allows to match the chiral anomaly. This is provided by a (3+1)d axion
\phi

[
2
,
3
,
113
,
116
]
. The chiral symmetry
U(1)_{A}
is transmuted into the shift symmetry
\phi\to\phi+\alpha
while the anomaly is matched by a background axion coupling:

S=\frac{f^{2}}{2}\int d^{4}x\,\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi+\frac{ik}{4\pi^{2}}\int\phi\,dA_{V}\wedge dA_{V}+i2k_{g}\int\phi\,\widehat{A}(R)\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\phi\simeq\phi+2\pi

(5.49)

Notice that, even though the IR theory is bosonic, the vortex core remembers the UV Spin
c
structure. This can also be seen by the structure of the matched anomaly, which contains a gravitational piece that is ill-defined on its own without the Spin
c
structure.
The case we will study here will have
k=k_{g}=1
corresponding to a UV Dirac fermion.

We again consider a monodromy defect
\textbf{M}(\nu)
for the axial symmetry by expanding in cylindrical coordinates (
5.25
) around a background:

d\phi=2\nu d\theta\,,\hskip 20.00003pt\nu=p/N\,.

(5.50)

The factor of
2
is a convention to match the UV completion of this symmetry via the Jackiw-Rossi model
[
83
]
. We will comment on this below.
It is well appreciated that, in the theory with the axion alone, this naive vortex configuration has logarithmically divergent energy. This can be regulated by embedding the axion model into a UV complete theory, with the UV fields confined into the vortex center.

First, let us look at the IR description. Substituting the profile (
5.50
) in the axion coupling gives rise to an ill-quantized Chern-Simons term:

\frac{ip}{2\pi N}\int A_{V}dA_{V}+ip/N\int\text{CS}_{g}\,.

(5.51)

Once again, we interpret this as stemming from the response of a FQH system. Notice however that, in this case, the FQH modes are completely decoupled from the axion, as perturbations above the winding background are independent of
\nu
.

To elucidate the physics of this mode we must consider a UV-completion of the model:

S_{\text{UV}}=\int d^{4}x\left(\partial_{\mu}\Phi\partial^{\mu}\Phi+i\overline{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\Psi-V(|\Phi|^{2})+(\Phi\overline{\Psi}P_{R}\Psi+\text{c.c.})\right)\,,\Psi=(\psi_{L},\psi_{R})\,.

(5.52)

\Phi=fe^{i\phi}
is a complex scalar, which condenses due to the potential:

\langle|\Phi|^{2}\rangle=R^{2}\neq 0\,,

(5.53)

giving mass to the fermion and leading to the IR axion.
The UV theory is invariant under an
(U(1)_{V}\times U(1)_{A})/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry:

\displaystyle U(1)_{V}:

\displaystyle\Psi\to e^{2\pi i\mu}\Psi\,,\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\Phi\to\Phi\,,

(5.54)

\displaystyle U(1)_{A}:

\displaystyle\Psi\to e^{-2\pi i\nu\gamma_{5}}\Psi\,,\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\Phi\to e^{-4\pi i\nu}\Phi\,.

Now we can analyze the vortex configuration in the UV-completed model. The twisted boundary conditions are:

\displaystyle\Phi(\theta+2\pi,x,t,r)=e^{-4\pi i\nu}\Phi(\theta,x,t,r)\,

(5.55)

\displaystyle\Psi(\theta+2\pi,x,t,r)=-e^{-2\pi i\nu\gamma_{5}}\Psi(\theta,x,t,r)\,.

Notice that the fermion b.c. are also twisted.
The scalar can be treated as a background, which develops a radial profile in the presence of the vortex:

\Phi=f(r)e^{-2i\nu\theta}\,,\hskip 10.00002ptf(r)\overset{r\to 0}{\longrightarrow}r^{|2\nu|}\,.

(5.56)

In genuine vortices the field must be single-valued, so
2\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
(the standard vortex corresponding to
\nu=1/2
), but this is not the case for the monodromy defect. However notice that, for any nonzero
\nu
, the vortex solution is still regular at the origin and square-integrable.

Outside the vortex the fermion is massive and can be integrated out. Inside the core we treat
f(r)\sim 0
and we are left with a massless twisted Dirac fermion, as in Section
5
.

Then, we can simply recycle our results and conclude that the vortex generically supports two singular chiral modes,

\chi_{L}^{+}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\chi_{R}^{+}\,,

(5.57)

localized in its core. We conclude that, in the axion model, the defect modes are really the Jackiw-Rossi fermions confined inside the vortex core, together with anyons attached to the topological lines of the dressing TQFT
\mathbf{T}(\nu)
, see Figure

6
Monodromy operators and defects on the lattice

To conclude, we briefly discuss the definition of monodromy defects in the context of quantum lattice models.

On the lattice we cannot leverage Lorentz invariance, and so we must explicitly distinguish between the monodromy defect (
\textbf{M}(g)
), describing a codimension-2 impurity in space, and a monodromy operator (
\mathcal{M}(g)

t

\mathcal{U}(g)

U(g)
instead. An in-depth discussion of the differences between operators and defects in the case of symmetries can be found in
[
115
]
.

For continuous symmetries the realization of chiral symmetries is notoriously subtle
[
102
,
103
]
, unless we work with an infinite-dimensional on-site Hilbert space.
A well-known example is the (modified) Villain model
[
132
,
121
,
76
,
63
]
, which thus provides a natural playground for our ideas.

We now briefly delve into the definition of the monodromy operators and defects on the lattice, and provide examples in the case of anomalous symmetries.

Monodromy defects

The monodromy defect
\textbf{M}(g)
, instead, can only be defined in more than one spatial dimension, and describes a configuration in which the dynamical impurity extends along time, while being localized at a codimension-2 surface in space. For example, in a (2+1)d system a monodromy defect will be localized e.g. at
x=y=0
and extend along time. See Figure
10

There are two natural ways to describe such a defect. The first is to introduce an external gauge field

\prod_{\ell\in P}\exp\left(iA_{\ell}\right)=1\,,

(6.5)

P

A_{\ell}

\prod_{\ell\in P_{\textbf{M}(g)}}\exp\left(iA_{\ell}\right)=g\,,

(6.6)

where
P_{\textbf{M}(g)}
is a plaquette which intersects the location of
\textbf{M}(g)
. The monodromy defect is thus identified with a fractional vortex-type configuration on the lattice.

6.1
Monodromy operators and defects in the Villain model

In this Section we reconsider the effect of a U(1) anomaly on lattice monodromy defects and operators, through the lens of the Villain model. We start in (1+1)d and then move onto the (3+1)d case.

(1+1) dimensional Villain model

The (modified) Villain model is described by the action
[
76
,
116
]
:

H=\sum_{j}\frac{1}{2M}\pi_{j}^{2}+\frac{M}{2}\sum_{i}\left(\phi_{i+1}-\phi_{i}-w_{i+1,i}\right)^{2}\,,

(6.9)

The fields
\phi_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are the Villain scalars, and we have introduced dynamical
\mathbb{Z}
-valued gauge fields
w_{i+1,i}\in\mathbb{Z}
to enforce their compactness. The canonical commutation relations are:

[\pi_{i},\phi_{j}]=i\delta_{ij}\,,\hskip 20.00003pt[w_{i+1,i},b_{j+1,j}]=-i\delta_{ij}\,,

(6.10)

where
b_{j+1,j}
is the canonically conjugated variable to
w_{i+1,i}
, which does not appear in the Hamiltonian.
Furthermore, we impose Gauss-law constraints enforcing the periodicity of the
\phi_{i}
fields:

\exp\left(2\pi i(\pi_{i}-b_{i+1,i}+b_{i,i-1})\right)=1\,,\hskip 20.00003pt\exp\left(2\pi iw_{j+1,j}\right)=1\,.

(6.11)

This implements the gauge symmetry
\phi_{i}\to\phi_{i}+n_{i},\,w_{i,i+1}\to w_{i,i+1}+n_{i+1}-n_{i}
.

The model has a
U(1)_{m}\times U(1)_{w}
symmetry, generated by the charges:

Q_{m}=\sum_{j}\pi_{j}\,,\hskip 20.00003ptQ_{w}=\sum_{j}\left(w_{j+1,j}+(\phi_{j+1}-\phi_{j})\right)\,,

(6.12)

we’ll use
q_{i}
to denote the quasi-local charges (i.e.
Q=\sum_{i}q_{i}
). Notice that we can also globally write
Q_{w}=\sum_{j}w_{j+1,j}
and
Q_{m}=\sum_{j}\left(\pi_{j}+b_{j+1,j}-b_{j,j-1}\right)
. The two different forms either commute locally with the Hamiltonian, or commute with the local constraints
[
116
]
. We will choose the former in the following discussion.
Let us consider the twisted Hilbert spaces. The truncated symmetry generators are:
13
13

13

In this Section, we use
U_{+}
to denote the truncation to half space.

U_{+}(\theta)=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}e^{i\theta q_{j}}\,,

(6.13)

and
H(\theta)=U_{+}(-\theta)HU_{+}(\theta)
. As commented above, the twisted Hamiltonian remains the same with our choices.
The twisted Hilbert space instead can be interpreted as a modification of the Gauss law constraints on a single link
w_{1,0}
:

e^{2\pi iw_{1,0}}=e^{i\theta}\,.

(6.14)

Thus, the
w
eigenvalue is fractional at the (1,0) link. We can reach this Hilbert space from the untwisted one by acting with a fractionalized vortex operator:

V_{\theta}=\exp\left(i\frac{\theta}{2\pi}b_{1,0}\right)\,,\hskip 20.00003pt|\psi_{\theta}\rangle=V_{\theta}|\psi\rangle\,.

(6.15)

This is the avatar of the twist field in the continuum model (see Appendix
A
). The winding charge of these states is fractional, and the symmetry realized on the twisted Hilbert space is an extension of
U(1)_{w}
by either
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
or
\mathbb{R}
. Upon performing a
2\pi
rotation, we see that our states are flowed by the vortex operator
V_{2\pi}
:

|\psi_{2\pi}\rangle=e^{ib_{1,0}}|\psi_{0}\rangle\,.

(6.16)

Let us describe the spectral flow in a different manner. Consider the alternative form of the charge:

Q_{m}=\sum_{j}\pi_{j}+b_{j+1,j}-b_{j,j-1}\,.

(6.17)

With this form, the operator
U_{+}(\theta)
keeps the constraints fixed. However the truncated operator now has a nontrivial spectral flow:

U_{+}(2\pi)=e^{ib_{1,0}}\,U_{+}(0)\,.

(6.18)

The operator
e^{ib_{1,0}}
is a (0+1)d SPT entangler between the trivial and the
U(1)_{w}
SPT

\omega(A_{w})=e^{i\int A_{w}}\,.

(6.19)

This follows from the fact that
e^{ib_{1,0}}
is charged under
U(1)_{w}
.

(3+1)d Villain model

The (3+1)-dimensional version of the Villain model also showcases a similar rich structure
[
68
,
125
,
100
]
.
We take the Hamiltonian to be:

H=\frac{1}{2M}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\,\in\,\text{sites}}\pi_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}+\frac{M}{2}\sum_{\ell\,\in\,\text{links}}\left(d\phi_{\ell}-w_{\ell}\right)^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{P\,\in\,\text{plaquettes}}dw_{P}^{2}\,.

(6.20)

The last term makes vortices of
w_{\ell}
energetically disfavored, while keeping
w_{\ell}
non-flat
[
100

Discussion

In this work we have examined the imprint of bulk ’t Hooft anomaly on dynamical monodromy defects through the lens of decorated domain walls —which generalize string order parameters— in massive SPT phases.
This is by no means a complete study, and there are several interesting open directions to pursue:

1.

Chiral modes on the lattice
It would be of great interest to realize the setup of Section
5
in a fermionic lattice theory. A natural setup is provided by Weyl semimetals
[
10
]
, where external axial gauge fields can be introduced via modulations in the UV fermionic lattice
[
50
]
. Similar effects have been predicted as a consequence of dislocations in the Weyl semimetal’s lattice
[
122
]
, see also
[
108
]
.

2.

Renyi and conical defects
Another class of defects which are closely related to monodromy defects are conical defects, which describe conical singularities in spacetime. This dictionary is precise when the deficit angle is
2\pi
times an integer. In this case the defect can be reinterpreted in the replica language as a monodromy defect arising from the
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
replica symmetry
[
81
,
27
,
28
,
21
,
62
]
.
Crucially, a conical geometry sources a nontrivial spacetime holonomy, hinting that gravitational anomalies could have interesting implications for entanglement entropy computations.

3.

Higher monodromy pumps:
if
\pi_{1}(\mathbf{G})\neq 0
, we have pointed out that an anomaly in the space of defect couplings leads to a pump of gapless modes on
\textbf{M}(g)
. It would be interesting to understand how higher homotopy groups can be detected by
\textbf{M}(g)
.

4.

SymTFT
Our results can be reinterpreted through the lens of the Symmetry TFT
[
18
,
14
,
42
]
, which would allow us to extend our observations to monodromy defects for non-invertible symmetries, which have seen little study in dynamical setups.

5.

Callan-Rubakov and Categorical Scattering
Scattering amplitudes in the presence of magnetic monopoles
[
110
,
111
,
30
,
31
,
130
,
131
,
99
]
suggest that operators in the twisted sectors of (anomalous) bulk symmetries could be excited in the out-going radiation.
18
18

18

See also
[
129
,
5
]
for studies of such processes for generic interfaces.
Monodromy defects act in some sense as “fractional” monopoles, binding chiral fermionic modes to their core. It is tempting to conjecture that bulk scattering experiments could detect these anomaly-induced modes.

has one-form symmetry charge
p
.
In the latter case the theory needs a Spin structure to be defined, and
p\simeq p+N
. In the former case, the theory is bosonic, and
p\simeq p+2N
.

When
N
is odd, and
p

c
structure, as the boson
L^{N}
always carries even electric charge for any choice of fractionalization.
When instead
p

N

c

Chiral central charge

For the bosonic case, the chiral central charge mod 8 is given by the Gauss sum:

e^{2\pi ic_{-}/8}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{\frac{2\pi ip}{2N}j^{2}}\,,

(D.3)

which can be evaluated to be
[
80
]
:

e^{2\pi ic_{-}/8}\equiv G_{N,p}=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{p/2}{N}\right)\epsilon(N)\,,\quad&N\,\text{odd}\,,p\,\text{even}\,,\\
\left(\frac{N/2}{p}\right)\epsilon(p)^{-1}e^{i\pi/4}\,,\quad&N\,\text{even}\,,p\,\text{odd}\,.\end{cases}

(D.4)

With
\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)
the Jacobi symbol and
\epsilon(s)=1
for
s=1\mod 4
and
\epsilon(s)=-1
for
s=3\mod 4
.

For Spin theories the central charge can only be detected mod 1/2. However, for their Spin
c
lift, this is not enough, as the invertible Spin TQFTs Spin(n)
1
are not compatible with the Spin
c
structure.
The correct observable in this case is the
\mathbf{CP}^{2}
partition function with
U(1)
flux, which is given in our case by
[
93
]
:

G_{N,p;\kappa}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{[a]}\exp\left(\frac{i\pi p}{N}(a^{2}-\kappa a\xi)\right)\,.

(D.5)

The second term being the charge of the fractionalized anyon times the (odd) flux
\xi
and
[a]
means equivalence modulo fusion with the transparent fermion
\psi
. In particular, for
\xi=1
this sum leads to the invariant
(c_{-}-\sigma_{H})\mod 8
, and can be used to compute the chiral central charge of the Spin
c
minimal theory.

Hall response

The Hall conductivity can be computed from the anomaly:

\Omega_{N,p}=\frac{2\pi p}{2N}\mathfrak{P}(B)\,,

(D.6)

which can be re-expressed using continuum fields
B_{c}
as:

\Omega_{N,p}=\frac{Np}{4\pi}B_{c}\wedge B_{c}+\frac{N}{2\pi}B_{c}\wedge\lambda\,,

(D.7)

where
\lambda
is a dynamical Lagrange multiplier restricting
B_{c}
to a
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge field. Plugging in the symmetry fractionalization:

B_{c}=\frac{\kappa}{N}F\,,

(D.8)

one finds the response:

\tau(N,p;\kappa)=\frac{p\kappa^{2}}{4\pi N}A\wedge dA\,.

(D.9)
