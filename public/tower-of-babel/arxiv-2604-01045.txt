Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs with the same Alexander polynomial
Hisaaki Endo, Kazunori Iwaki, Andrei Pajitnov

Abstract
. It is well known that for m ⩾ 2 m\geqslant 2 there are at most two non-equivalent m m -knots with diffeomorphic exterior. Such pair of knots will be called non-reflexive knot pair . A classical problem in topology is to determine all dimensions where such knot pairs exist. In 1976 Cappell and Shaneson gave a method of constructing non-reflexive knot pairs. In the present paper we construct an infinite family of new examples of Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs, and give examples of Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs that have the same Alexander polynomial but are inequivalent.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, a
C^{\infty}
embedding of
S^{m}
into
S^{m+2}
is called an
m
-knot.
Two
m
-knots are said to be equivalent if and only if there exists a self-diffeomorphism of
S^{m+2}
that maps one knot onto the other. It is a classical problem in knot theory whether in given
dimension there are inequivalent knots with diffeomorphic
complements.
For the case of classical knots (
m=1
), a theorem of Gordon and Luecke
[
5
, Theorem 1]
asserts that the knots are equivalent if the complements are homeomorphic.
Gluck
[
4
, Theorem 1.]
, Browder
[
1
, Corollary 3.]
, Lashof and Shaneson
[
9
, Theorem 1.5.]
proved that for
m\geq 2
there are at most
2
inequivalent
m
-knots with diffeomorphic complements.

A knot that is determined by its complement is called
reflexive
. In 1976, Cappell and Shaneson
[
2
]
constructed the first examples of non-reflexive knots for
m=3,4
.
Soon after that, Gordon
[
6
]
has constructed non-reflexive 2-knots.
In 1992, Suciu
[
12
]
constructed
non-reflexive
m
-knots for every
m\equiv 3{\rm\ or\ }4({\rm mod}\ 8)
.
In 1999, Gu and Jiang
[
7
]
constructed non-reflexive knots for
m=5,6
.

The paper
[
2
]
by Cappell and Shaneson contains a general method of constructing non-reflexive knots. Namely,
to each
(n\times n)
-matrix satisfying certain conditions
(see Definition
2.1
)
they associated two inequivalent
(n-1)
-knots with homeomorphic complements (here
n\geqslant 4
). Such matrices will be called

Cappell-Shaneson matrices
. These conditions turn out to be
conditions on the characteristic polynomial of the matrix.
A polynomial satisfying these conditions will be called

Cappell-Shaneson polynomial
and the pair of the two knots above will be called
Cappell-Shaneson knot pair
. The present paper is the sequel
of our work
[
3
]
where we enumerated all
Cappell-Shaneson polynomials of degrees 4,5, and found new families of Cappell-Shaneson polynomials
of degrees 6 and 7.

However, it remains unknown whether Cappell-Shaneson polynomials exist for
n\geq 8
.

In the present paper we prove that there are inequivalent Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs with the same Alexander polynomial. (All previously known Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs are determined by their Alexander polynomial.) We show in particular that there exist more than 10000 Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs with Alexander polynomial equal to
x^{5}-99x^{4}+197x^{3}-197x^{2}+100x-1
. Furthermore we give new families of Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs for
n=4,5,6,7
. We describe a complete algebraic invariant of Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs and provide a method to enumerate all knot pairs with given Alexander polynomial.

The present paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we first review Cappell and Shaneson’s method to construct non-reflexive knots.
Then we reduce the problem of classification of
Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs to a purely algebraic question concerning
classification of matrices.

This algebraic question is discussed in Section

3
.
To a given Cappell-Shaneson matrix
A
we associate
the ideal class monoid of the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}[\theta]

where
\theta
is a root of the characteristic polynomial of
A
.
We show that this invariant is a complete invariant of classes of

*
-equivalence of matrices and therefore a complete invariant
of classes of equivalence of Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs.

In Section
4
we compute ideal class monoids for several infinite families of
Cappell-Shaneson polynomials in degrees 4,5,6,7. We give new examples of Cappell-Shaneson
knot pairs, and examples of inequivalent Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs with same Alexander polynomial.

2.
Classification of Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs

In this section we prove a classification theorem for
Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs.
It turns out that the fundamental group of the complement is the complete invariant of such pairs, and these fundamental groups, in turn, are classified by equivalence classes of matrices
(with respect to a
*
-equivalence relation, see Definition

2.4
).
We begin by a brief recollection of Cappell-Shaneson construction from
[
2
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A matrix

A\in\operatorname{SL}(n;\mathbb{Z})
is called a
Cappell-Shaneson matrix
if it satisfies the following condition
\mathrm{CS}_{k}
for every integer

\mathrm{CS}_{k}

\det(I-\bigwedge^{k}A)=\pm 1

I
is the identity matrix and
\bigwedge^{k}A
is the
k
-th exterior power of
A
.

Let
A
be a Cappell-Shaneson matrix. We say
A
is
positive
if
(-1)^{n}\det(xI-A)>0
for every
x\in(-\infty,0)
.

The condition
\mathrm{CS}_{k}
is actually a condition on the characteristic polynomial
of the matrix (see
[
3
]
, Corollary 2.8).

Definition 2.2
.

Let
A
be a Cappell-Shaneson matrix. A monic polynomial

of degree

if it is the characteristic polynomial of a Cappell-Shaneson matrix of order

is called

is the characteristic polynomial of a positive Cappell-Shaneson matrix.

Let

We have fibration of

onto the circle

(1)

p_{A}:M_{A}\to{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}=C,\ \ p_{A}(x,t)=t

We denote by

(or just by

the zero section of this fibration.
The normal bundle to
C
in
M_{A}
is trivial; choose a framing
\phi
of
C

of
C
onto
D^{n}\times C
. The corresponding surgery replaces

by

that

The image of

(n-1)

(n-1)

S^{n+1}
. Its complement is diffeomorphic to
M_{A}\setminus C
.

Since there are exactly two classes of equivalence of framings of

M_{A}
(corresponding to 2 elements of
\pi_{1}(SO(n))
)
the construction above leads to two
(n-1)
-knots which will
be denoted by
K_{0}(A)
and
K_{1}(A)
. These knots have the following properties (see
[
2
]
, Sections 3,4):

(1)

The complement of
K_{i}(A)
in
S^{n+1}
is diffeomorhic
to
M_{A}\setminus C
(here
i=0,1
).

(2)

K_{0}(A)
is not equivalent to
K_{1}(A)
.

(3)

The Alexander polynomial of
K_{0}(A)
and
K_{1}(A)
equals
the characteristic polynomial of
A
.

Definition 2.3
.

The unordered pair
\{K_{0}(A),K_{1}(A)\}

will be called a
Cappell-Shaneson knot pair

and denoted also by
{\mathscr{P}}(A)
.
Two Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs

{\mathscr{P}}(A)
and
{\mathscr{P}}(B)

are called
equivalent
if

K_{0}(A)
is equivalent to
K_{0}(B)
or to
K_{1}(B)

(then necessarily
K_{1}(A)

is equivalent to
K_{1}(B)
or, respectively, to
K_{0}(B)
.
)

Observe that for
i=0,1
we have

(2)

\pi_{1}(S^{n+1}\setminus K_{i}(A))\approx\mathbb{Z}^{n}\rtimes_{A}\mathbb{Z},

Indeed,
the fundamental group of
S^{n+1}\setminus K_{i}(A)

is isomorphic to that of
M_{A}\setminus C
and this space is the mapping torus of the map
f_{A}
restricted to
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}\setminus\{\omega\}

(here
\omega
is the image of the point
0\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with respect to
the projection
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
)
We have also

(3)

H_{1}(S^{n+1}\setminus K_{i}(A))\approx{\mathbb{Z}}.

Now we can proceed to classification of
Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs.

Definition 2.4
.

Let

\operatorname{GL}(n;\mathbb{Z})
.
We say that

Denote

Theorem 2.6
.

Cappell-Shaneson knot pairs
{\mathscr{P}}(A)

and
{\mathscr{P}}(B)

are equivalent if and only if the matrices
A
and
B
are
*
-equivalent.

Proof.
If
\{K_{0}(A),K_{1}(A)\}
is equivalent to
\{K_{0}(B),K_{1}(B)\}
,
then all these four knots have diffeomorphic complements,
therefore the fundamental groups of the complements are isomorphic,
and Theorem

implies that
A
is
*
-equivalent to
B
.

To prove the inverse implication, observe that if

A=DBD^{-1}
with
D\in\operatorname{GL}(n;\mathbb{Z})

then a map

defined by the formula

(x,t)\mapsto(D^{-1}x,t)

is a well-defined diffeomorphism sending the circle

C_{A}\subset M_{A}

to the circle
C_{B}\subset M_{B}

Therefore the exteriors of the four knots
K_{0}(A),K_{1}(A),K_{0}(B),K_{1}(B)
are diffeomorphic. The knots

K_{0}(A)
and
K_{1}(A)
are not equivalent. For

there are at most
2

-knots with diffeomorphic complements (see
[

]
,
[

]
and
[

]
),
so

is equivalent to
K_{0}(B)
or to

,
and
{\mathscr{P}}(A)
is equivalent to
{\mathscr{P}}(B)
.

Furthermore, observe that
{\mathscr{P}}(A)
is equivalent to

. Indeed, the formula

determines a diffeomorphism from

3.
Classification of Cappell-Shaneson matrices

In this section, we introduce a method to obtain
*
-equivalence classes of Cappell-Shaneson matrices when a Cappell-Shaneson polynomial is given.

In the beginning, we collect some results for Cappell-Shaneson polynomials.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
7
, Theorem 3.2.]
)
.

Cappell-Shaneson polynomials are irreducible over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be an irreducible monic polynomial with
deg(f)=n
. For
A\in\operatorname{M}(n;\mathbb{Z})
,
f
is the characteristic polynomial of
A
if and only if
f(A)=O
.

Proof.

If
f
is the characteristic polynomial of
A
,
f(A)=O
is true by the Cayley-Hamilton theorem. We assume
f(A)=O
. Then, the minimal polynomial of
A
divides
f
. The minimal polynomial of
A
is equal to
f
since
f
is irreducible. The minimal polynomial of
A
is equal to the characteristic polynomial of
A
since both polynomials are monic polynomials with the same degree. Therefore,
f
is the characteristic polynomial of
A
.
∎

By Theorem
3.2
, if a polynomial
f
is a Cappell-Shaneson polynomial and
f(A)=O
for
A\in\operatorname{M}(n;\mathbb{Z})
, then
A
is a Cappell-Shaneson matrix. Therefore,
\{A\in\operatorname{M}(n;\mathbb{Z})\mid f(A)=O\}
is the set of Cappell-Shaneson matrices whose characteristic polynomial is
f
.

Next, we introduce an algebraic object called an ideal class monoid to classify Cappell-Shaneson matrices.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
R
be an integral domain and
\mathcal{I}(R)
the set of non-zero ideals. We define an equivalence relation
\approx
on
\mathcal{I}(R)
such that
I\approx J
if and only if there exist non-zero
\alpha,\beta\in R
such that
\alpha I=\beta J
. The general multiplication of ideals induces an operation in
\mathcal{I}(R)/\approx
, and this set becomes a commutative monoid. We call it
ideal class monoid
and write
C(R)
. We say that an ideal
I
is
invertible
if
[I]
is an invertible element in
C(R)
.

Theorem 3.4

(Latimer-MacDuffee
[
10
]
, Taussky
[
13
]
)
.

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be an irreducible monic polynomial. Let
\theta
be a root of
f(x)
. The following two maps are bijective and
\phi_{f}^{-1}=\psi_{f}
.

•

Let
v=[v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}]^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{Z}[\theta]^{n}
be an eigenvector of
A
and
S
a
\mathbb{Z}
-submodule of
\mathbb{Z}[\theta]
generated by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
. Define
\phi_{f}:\{A\in\operatorname{M}(n;\mathbb{Z})\mid f(A)=O\}/\sim_{C}\rightarrow C(\mathbb{Z}[\theta])
as
\phi_{f}([A])=[S]
.

•

Let
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
be a basis of an ideal
S
and
v=[v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}]^{\mathrm{T}}
. There is
A\in\operatorname{M}(n;\mathbb{Z})
such that
\theta v=Av
. Define
\psi_{f}:C(\mathbb{Z}[\theta])\rightarrow\{A\in\operatorname{M}(n;\mathbb{Z})\mid f(A)=O\}/\sim_{C}
as
\psi_{f}([S])=[A]
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be a Cappell-Shaneson polynomial. Let
\theta
be a root of
f(x)
. Then, there is a one-to-one correspondence between
C(\mathbb{Z}[\theta])
and
\{A\mid f(A)=O\}/\sim_{C}
.

The above discussion gives us how to classify Cappell-Shaneson matrices based on conjugacy. Furthermore, we use this formulation to classify
*
-equivalence classes.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
A\in\operatorname{SL}(n;\mathbb{Z})
be a Cappell-Shaneson matrix whose characteristic polynomial is
f
. Then
A^{-1}
is a Cappell-Shaneson matrix whose characteristic polynomial is
(-x)^{n}f(x^{-1})
. We write this polynomial as
f^{*}
and call it the signed reciprocal polynomial of
f
.

Proof.

Let
A\in\operatorname{SL}(n;\mathbb{Z})
be a Cappell-Shaneson matrix. There exists
A^{-1}\in\operatorname{SL}(n;\mathbb{Z})
.

\displaystyle\det(I-\bigwedge^{k}A^{-1})

\displaystyle=\det(I-(\bigwedge^{k}A)^{-1})

\displaystyle=\det(\bigwedge^{k}A)\det(I-(\bigwedge^{k}A)^{-1})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{{}_{n}C_{k}}\det(I-\bigwedge^{k}A)=\pm 1

since
\det(\bigwedge^{k}A)=1
and
\det(I-\bigwedge^{k}A)=\pm 1
.
So,
A^{-1}
is a Cappell-Shaneson matrix. In addition,
A^{-1}
satisfies positivity since

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\det(xI-A^{-1})

\displaystyle=\det(A)\det(xI-A^{-1})

\displaystyle=(-x)^{n}(-1)^{n}\det(x^{-1}I-A).

∎

Remark 3.7
.

Let
A\in\operatorname{SL}(n;\mathbb{Z})
be a Cappell-Shaneson matrix whose characteristic polynomial is
f
. When
n\geq 4
,
f\neq f^{*}
.

Proof.

If
f=f^{*}
, the characteristic polynomial of
A
is equal to that of
A^{-1}
. For an eigenvalue
\lambda
of
A
,
\lambda^{-1}
is also an eigenvalue of
A
. If
\lambda^{-1}=\lambda
,
\lambda=\pm 1
. This contradicts irreducibility of the characteristic polynomial. Therefore,
\lambda^{-1}\neq\lambda
and
1
is an eigenvalue of
\bigwedge^{2}A
. This contradicts the condition
\mathrm{CS}_{2}
of the definition of the Cappell-Shaneson matrices for
n\geq 4
.
∎

For two Cappell-Shaneson matrices
A,B
, let
f_{A},f_{B}
be the characteristic polynomial of
A,B
, respectively. If
A\sim_{*}B
, then
f_{A}=f_{B}
or

.

Therefore, the classification of Cappell-Shaneson matrices is equivalent to that of ideal classes. If we can compute the corresponding ideal class monoid explicitly, we can classify Cappell-Shaneson matrices for a given Cappell-Shaneson polynomial. We can sometimes compute it if
\mathbb{Z}[\theta]
is integrally closed. In that case, the ideal class monoid is an ideal class group. We can use many methods from the number theory.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
R
be an integral domain and

the fraction field of
R
. We say that

is
integrally closed
if and only if the following condition holds: if
\alpha\in K
is a root of a monic polynomial in
R[x]
,
\alpha\in R
.

Theorem 3.10

(
[
8
, Theorem 4.4]
)
.

If
\mathbb{Z}[\theta]
is integrally closed,

is a ideal class group.

Besides, we introduce Kummer-Dedekind criterion to consider Cappell-Shaneson matrices that are not
*
-equivalent to companion matrices.

Theorem 3.11

(
[
11
, Theorem 8.2]
)
.

Let
p
be a prime number and
f
an irreducible monic polynomial. We denote by
\theta
a root of
f
. Let us suppose
\bar{f}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
can be factorized into
\Pi_{i}\bar{g_{i}}^{e_{i}}
. Pick
g_{k}
which is a monic polynomial. Let

be a remainder when

is divided by

in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Then, an ideal
(p,g_{k}(\theta))
is invertible if and only if

or

in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
.

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
p
be a prime number and
f
an irreducible monic polynomial with
deg(f)=n
. We denote by
\theta
a root of
f
. Let us suppose
\bar{f}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
can be factorized into
\Pi_{i}\bar{g_{i}}^{e_{i}}
. Let us suppose
g_{k}(x)=x-b
. Let
r_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be a remainder when

is divided by
x-b
in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
. If
e_{k}\neq 1
and
p^{2}\nmid r_{k}
in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
, then
(p,\theta-b)
is not invertible and

is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
(p,\theta-b)
.

Proof.

(p,\theta-b)
is not invertible by Theorem
3.11
. Let
\langle p,\theta-b,\theta(\theta-b),\ldots,\theta^{n-2}(\theta-b)\rangle
be a set of all linear combinations, which is a subset of
(p,\theta-b)
.

We show
p\theta^{j},(\theta-b)\theta^{j}\in\langle p,\theta-b,\theta(\theta-b),\ldots,\theta^{n-2}(\theta-b)\rangle
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
to show
(p,\theta-b)
is a subset of
\langle p,\theta-b,\theta(\theta-b),\ldots,\theta^{n-2}(\theta-b)\rangle
. If
j=1,\ldots,n-2
,
(\theta-b)\theta^{j}\in\langle p,\theta-b,\theta(\theta-b),\ldots,\theta^{n-2}(\theta-b)\rangle
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
clearly. Let us suppose

where

.

(\theta-b)\theta^{n-1}=-(\theta-b)(q(x)-\theta^{n-1})+r_{k}

since

.
(\theta-b)\theta^{n-1}
is an element of
\langle p,\theta-b,\theta(\theta-b),\ldots,\theta^{n-2}(\theta-b)\rangle
since

and

. We realize that

for

through the Taylor expansion of

at

.
∎

This corollary shows how to find non-invertible ideal classes but can be used to find Cappell-Shaneson matrices that are not
*
-equivalent to companion matrices. That is because matrices corresponding to non-invertible ideal classes are not
*
-equivalent to companion matrices. Companion matrices correspond to the identity element by Theorem
3.4
and it is trivially invertible.
