Correlation between SHR and 28-day all-cause mortality risk in patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage
Authors: Jun Wang, Chao Tang, Zhijie Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) has recently been suggested as a dependable indication for predicting adverse outcomes in critically ill ICU patients. Previous studies have primarily focused on cardiovascular disorders include myocardial infarction and coronary artery disease, but the connection between SHR and prognosis in severely ill individuals with cerebrovascular disorders, particularly those with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage, remains unclear. This investigation aims to investigate the relationship between SHR and adverse outcomes in critically ill individuals experiencing spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage.MethodsThis study analyzed information collected from critically ill patients in the MIMIC database, grouped by tertiles of SHR, to demonstrate the clinical characteristics of the different groups of patients. The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality. Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier curves, with restricted cubic spline, were used to investigate the connection between SHR and risk of death in individuals with spontaneous cerebral hemorrhage. Additionally, subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate the effect of different baseline characteristics on the correlation between SHR and risk of death. Lastly, predictive models were constructed using machine learning algorithms to illustrate the predictive efficacy of SHR.ResultsThe study analyzed the data of 815 patients who met the inclusion criteria. The average age of all patients was 69.46 ± 15.05, and 432 patients were male (53.0%). Cox regression analysis demonstrated the connection of SHR with the risk of death as a continuous variable and a categorical variable.Subsequently, Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to illustrate the survival characteristics for the three cohorts of patients(P<0.001). Further restricted cubic splines showed a nonlinear connection between SHR and the risk of death; Higher SHR was significantly correlated with an elevated mortality risk in individuals diagnosed with cerebral hemorrhage (HR>1, p<0.05). In the subgroup analysis, higher SHR was consistently correlated with the mortality risk in the vast majority of subgroups. Finally, five machine learning models were built and ROC were plotted to demonstrate the predictive efficacy of SHR for poor prognosis.ConclusionSHR can be used as an noteworthy predictor of adverse outcome in patients with spontaneous cerebral hemorrhage. Higher SHR is strongly correlated with an elevated risk of unfavorable prognosis, particularly in non-diabetic patients. Additionally, it may help to risk stratify this specific population.

Introduction

Stroke is a prominent cause of mortality and disability globally, with significant economic and social costs [1]. Spontaneous cerebral hemorrhage is one of the major subtypes of stroke, with a mortality rate of up to 30%, and the majority of patients who survive suffer from varying degrees of disability and remain at risk for recurrent stroke [2, 3]. With the global aging of the population, the incidence of cerebral hemorrhage is expected to increase annually in the coming decades, and its occurrence is primarily a result of the failure to effectively manage controllable risk factors [4]. Therefore, it is crucial to effectively stratify the risk of hospitalized patients with severe stroke, develop corresponding management strategies, and achieve precise treatment [5].

In patients with cerebral hemorrhage, stress hyperglycemia often occurs. This is due to the excessive stress of the organism as well as severe glucose metabolism disorders leading to insulin resistance (IR). The patient’s blood glucose is usually higher than average at this time, and the stress hyperglycemia itself is an adaptive physiologic response to an acute illness in the face of a complex, changing environment. However, it may be commonly associated with hormonal regulation, immune response, and neural activity through potentially inducing endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress, thus also often leading to adverse consequences for the organism [6]. For stroke events, stress hyperglycemia has been proven to be highly related with poor outcomes in ischemic stroke occurrences [7, 8].

Effectively predicting the outcome of spontaneous cerebral hemorrhage improves our understanding of potential risk factors, which in turn helps us to target treatment. The clinical outcome of cerebral hemorrhage is affected by various factors, such as the duration of treatment, location and volume of bleeding, as well as individual patient characteristics. Hence, precisely forecasting the short-term prognosis of individuals with cerebral hemorrhage is challenging [4].

A new marker that effectively reflects stress status has recently been discovered: the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), based on glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and emergency admission glucose [5, 9]. A study reveals the effect of SHR on early neurologic injury and long-term prognosis for individuals with acute ischemic stroke [10]. Additionally, it can also function as a reliable indicator of hematoma extension and unfavorable prognosis in individuals with cerebral hemorrhage [11]. SHR has also been acknowledged as a promising predictor of negative outcomes in severely ill individuals by investigating the relationship between stress-induced high blood sugar levels and the severity of the disease [6]. However, the correlation between SHR and other types of stroke still seems to lack research. Prior research has predominantly concentrated on examining the correlation between SHR and ischemic stroke, including prolonged hospitalization, which leads to an increased healthcare burden [12]. Therefore, we would like to further explore the correlation between patients with spontaneous cerebral hemorrhage and adverse outcomes to understand patient survival characteristics and potential risk factors.

Methods

Study population

The study comprised patients who were diagnosed with spontaneous cerebral hemorrhage based on the ICD-9/10 criteria. These are the exclusion standards: (1) patients who were younger than 18 years old at admission; (2) 1595 patients who were not admitted to the ICU; (3) patients with several ICU admission records, only the initial data were extracted; (4) A total of 1240 individuals with incomplete clinical data, including SHR, were eliminated from the trial; Consequently, only 815 patients were ultimately included in the study.

Data extraction

Patient data were collected using PostgreSQL and NavicatPremium (version 16.1.7) software from the MIMIC-IV 2.2 database, an openly accessible critical care database comprising comprehensive information on hospitalizations at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center from 2008 to 2019, which has received approval from local ethics committee. The following variables were collected: age, gender, body mass index (BMI), ethnicity, diastolic blood pressure (DBP), systolic blood pressure (SBP), hemoglobin, leukocytes, platelets, (red cell distribution width) RDW, glucose, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), creatinine, urea nitrogen, laboratory parameters such as APTT, PT, and INR, and SOFA score, Sepsis3, GCS score, the SHR was calculated: SHR = Glucose/(1.59 * HbA1c-2.59). Glucose and HbA1c were obtained within 24 h of the first day of ICU admission. For the processing of missing data, we delete the variables with data missing rate > 10%, while the variables with missing range of 5–10% adopt multiple interpolation method, and the variables with data missing rate < 5% adopt mean filling method. Complications include Acute Kidney Injury(AKI), (chornic kidney disease)CKD, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pneumonia, stroke, etc. The diagnosis relies on ICD-9/ICD-10 standard. The main end points included 28-day all-cause mortality and survival time in ICU.

Statistical analysis

This study was statistically analyzed utilizing R software (version 4.5.2) and SPSS26. Patients were divided into three groups according to the tertiles of the SHR. We used the Shapiro–Wilk test to evaluate the normal distribution of continuous variables. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed with parametric methods. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR), and the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied for intergroup comparison. Categorical variables were described as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square test. LASSO regression was applied to screen potential confounding variables to alleviate multicollinearity and retain clinically meaningful predictors. The optimal lambda (λ) value was determined by cross-validation based on the minimum deviance criterion, and variables with non-zero regression coefficients at the optimal λ were retained as core covariates for subsequent multivariate Cox regression adjustment.Finally, four core covariates including age, Sepsis3, RDW and SOFA were selected for fully adjusted Cox regression models. Age reflects baseline physiological reserve of critically ill patients; Sepsis3 and SOFA scores quantify the severity of infection and multiple organ dysfunction, which are well-recognized prognostic indicators in ICU patients with intracerebral hemorrhage. RDW represents systemic inflammation, oxidative stress and nutritional status, and has been proven to be closely related to short-term mortality in stroke patients. These variables were therefore included in the adjusted model to eliminate confounding bias and objectively evaluate the independent association between SHR and 28-day all-cause mortality. To investigate the relationship between SHR and the risk of 28-day ICU death, a multivariate Cox proportional risk regression model was used to demonstrate the association as shown in Table 2. In addition, we applied Kaplan-Meier curve survival analysis to assess 28-day ICU mortality within the SHR group and to demonstrate differences in survival status between groups. The restricted cubic spline (RCS) curve was used to further clarify the nonlinear relationship between the SHR and the probability of death.

Subgroup analysis

We further performed subgroup analyses to stratify the results according to common different clinical characteristics such as: gender, diabetes, and hypertension and to calculate interactions between them. The model adjusted for potential confounders including: Age, Gender, WBC, RBC, RDW, Hemoglobin, Plt, Cre, BUN, Glu, Ca, K, input, Sepsis3, Ventilation, GCS, HBP, AMI, CKD, Cirrhosis, Pneumonia. and the results HR, etc. were plotted as forest plots. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Machine learning model building

We used the above data to develop five machine learning models to predict 28-day ICU mortality. It contains a variety of models, including Decision Trees, K-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN), Random Forests (RF), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). DT features straightforward interpretability via hierarchical decision partitioning but is susceptible to overfitting. k-NN classifies samples based on feature distance similarity and fits nonlinear data distributions. As an ensemble learning approach, RF synthesizes multiple decision trees to enhance model stability and generalization while mitigating overfitting. SVM applies kernel functions to map high-dimensional data and constructs optimal separating hyperplanes for classification. XGBoost, an optimized gradient boosting framework, captures complex nonlinear associations with embedded regularization to improve model robustness. All models were trained and validated with 5-fold cross-validation under unified data preprocessing standards to ensure fair comparison of predictive performance. ROC curve and AUC value were adopted to quantitatively evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of each model for predicting 28-day all-cause mortality.

Ethical statement and informed consent

Our institution’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval is exempt because this research involves analysis of an anonymous, publicly accessible third-party database. The actual identity data in the patient database is hidden. Therefore, the informed consent of patients is exempted. And all studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsink.

We also performed RCS curve analysis. As shown in Fig. 4, SHR and death risk generally show a nonlinear increasing relationship. Subgroup analysis

To further distinguish the connection between SHR and the risk of ICU 28-day mortality under different clinical characteristics, we also performed subgroup analyses, and the results are shown in Fig. 5. In subgroups defined by age, sex, and diabetes, hypertension, and CKD, the models were fully adjusted, and the T3 group almost always showed a greater risk of mortality(p < 0.05). And there was no notable interaction with each baseline characteristic (p < 0.05). However this association did not seem to be found in the diabetes subgroup. The results revealed a strong correlation between higher SHR and an elevated risk of 28-day ICU mortality in patients across most subgroups.

Machine learning model

All covariates with statistical significance (P < 0.05) were enrolled into the machine learning models, which were trained via 5-fold cross-validation. The model with optimal overall performance was selected as the final predictive model. The ROC curves of the five machine learning algorithms are presented in Fig. 6, with predictive efficacy quantified by AUC values. Among them, random forest (RF) yielded the highest AUC of 0.760, followed by Bag_tree (0.725), XGBoost (0.719), decision tree (DT, 0.691), and support vector machine (SVM, 0.680). As an ensemble learning algorithm, RF exhibits prominent strengths in mitigating overfitting and maintaining good generalization capability, achieving the best diagnostic performance for predicting 28-day poor prognosis in patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage when combining SHR with clinical indicators. By contrast, single decision tree showed the lowest predictive capacity and was more vulnerable to the interference of individual samples. SVM and XGBoost achieved moderate predictive performance with acceptable clinical value, though inferior to RF. The performance discrepancies across different models further validate the incremental prognostic value of SHR in critically ill patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage. The machine learning model incorporating SHR and clinical baseline characteristics enables individualized mortality risk stratification and may provide evidence for precise clinical intervention strategies.

Discussion

This study investigated the association between SHR and prognosis of ICU patients, and the findings indicated a strong correlation between elevated SHR and increased 28-day mortality in individuals diagnosed with spontaneous cerebral hemorrhage. The nonlinear correlation between SHR and ICU mortality was further demonstrated by RCS curve analysis. In addition, we conducted subgroup and interaction analyses to investigate the applicability of the results across various clinical characteristics (such as gender, hypertension, diabetes, CKD, Sepsis, etc.). Finally, we also evaluated the predictive efficacy of SHR by five different machine learning models. The association between SHR and 28-day mortality in individuals who have spontaneous cerebral hemorrhage is being investigated for the first time in this study.

Numerous previous studies have found that worsening clinical outcomes in ICU patients are often accompanied by stress hyperglycemia [13–15]. It may be triggered by insulin resistance, hyperactive inflammation, oxidative stress, hormonal abnormalities, etc., but the exact mechanism of action has not been fully elucidated [16, 17]. Further studies have found that repeated fluctuations in blood glucose intensify oxidative stress and induce endothelial cell apoptosis, among other things [18]. Previously, it was thought that stress hyperglycemia may be an adaptive physiological response of the body to face sudden and rapid changes in the environment [19]. However, as research has progressed, it has been found that stress hyperglycemia often leads to worsened clinical outcomes. A large NEJM controlled trial reduced mortality at 12 months in critically ill patients by intensive insulin therapy in critically ill patients [20]. There was also a study of critically ill patients in Australia and New Zealand, involving nearly 740,000 people, which showed that stress hyperglycemia was significantly associated with the risk of death in critically ill patients [21]. In ICU patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage, SHR-quantified stress hyperglycemia correlates significantly with 28-day all-cause mortality. Individualized glycemic management is preferable to aggressive control, and the optimal glycemic intervention strategies await validation by prospective RCTs specific to the ICH population.

Simply using admission blood glucose as a marker of stress hyperglycemia is not rigorous; there are numerous factors that affect blood glucose variability, including poor glycemic control in diabetic patients, among others. Calculating SHR as a marker of stress hyperglycemia by introducing a correction for the recent blood glucose level HbA1c is a more reasonable approach and reflects the body’s stress response more effectively. More and more studies on SHR are gradually being conducted, including coronary syndromes, myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, etc., which have a strong association with SHR [10, 22–26]. Numerous investigations have also discovered that SHR has the ability to predict adverse results for critically ill ICU patients [6, 27, 28]. However, no studies have been published on critically ill patients with spontaneous cerebral hemorrhage. Our work expands upon this existing knowledge to provide a clearer understanding of the relationship between SHR and negative outcomes in patients with spontaneous cerebral hemorrhage.

Notably, in subgroup analyses, we found significant associations of higher SHR with 28-day risk of death in the vast majority of subgroups, even after adjusting for confounding variables. Expanding on this, SHR in the T3 group was consistently and significantly associated with mortality risk across gender, hypertension, sepsis, and CKD subgroups. In diabetic patients, no association seems to be found between them. In contrast, a strong association between higher SHR and risk of death could be found in non-diabetic patients. Previous studies have similarly found this feature, with a stronger correlation between SHR and adverse prognosis in non-diabetic patients compared to diabetic patients. The study by Wei et al. also confirmed that the association between SHR and poor prognosis seems to apply only to non-diabetic individuals [29, 30]. This may be due to the long-term adaptation of diabetic patients to a state of chronic oxidative stress, as well as persistently high hyperglycemia levels, and the fact that diabetic patients treated with insulin also seem to benefit from its anti-inflammatory effects [31, 32].

However, this investigation still has limitations. First, our study subjects were primarily from the U.S. population, and the results may lack generalizability to individuals of different regions and ethnicities. Secondly, this is a retrospective study and a direct causal relationship between SHR and mortality risk cannot be inferred. Additional cohort studies are still required to further investigate their relationship. Third, a considerable number of patients were excluded due to incomplete SHR and clinical data, which may introduce potential selection bias. Excluded individuals with missing records might be implicitly accompanied by unreported severe illness status, which could correlate with disease severity and clinical outcomes. Although we adopted standardized missing data processing strategies according to different missing rates, the potential confounding effect from substantial case exclusion cannot be fully eliminated. Fourth, the MIMIC-IV database has inherent limitations in structured data collection: it lacks standardized imaging parameters for intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), such as hematoma location/volume and intraventricular hemorrhage. Nevertheless, the predictive value of SHR remained robust even after adjusting for confounding factors. Future studies should validate our findings using specialized databases that include structured ICH imaging data.

Conclusion

Our study further demonstrates that SHR can serve as an important predictor of mortality risk in patients with spontaneous cerebral hemorrhage. Even after accounting for the effects of confounding factors, a higher SHR remains strongly correlated with an adverse outcome. Especially in non-diabetic patients, it helps to risk stratify the management of this specific population. Among the machine learning models, the random forest model performs optimally and has good diagnostic efficacy for poor prognosis prediction.
