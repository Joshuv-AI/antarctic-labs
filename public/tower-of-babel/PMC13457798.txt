Effectiveness of Miniscrew-assisted Rapid Palatal Expansion in the Management of Obstructive Sleep Apnea: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Raheesa Abdurahman, Dilshad Umar, Shafees Koya, Sandeep Shetty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common disease that is characterized by collapse of the upper airway during sleep. In some phenotypes, airway obstruction is due to maxillary transverse deficiency. Structural intervention miniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion (MARPE) has been proposed as a way to enhance airway patency.ObjectiveTo assess the effectiveness of MARPE in the reduction of OSA severity and the improvement of the upper airway dimensions in the pediatric and adult populations in a systematic way.MethodsThis study performed a systematic review and meta-analysis that followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. The search of 6 databases was conducted to find studies that evaluated MARPE in patients with OSA. RoBANS 2 was applied to assess risk of bias, and quantitative synthesis was done on apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) using a random-effects model.ResultsA total of 10 of the studies were included (n = 412). MARPE was linked to a pooled AHI decrease of 5.46 events/hour (95% CI: 6.01–4.90). There were substantial volumetric airway enlargements in nasal and nasopharyngeal areas. Appliances that are bone-borne resulted in more skeletal changes in comparison to those that are tooth-borne expanders. Adverse events were few.ConclusionMARPE seems an efficient nonsurgical treatment of OSA in patients with maxillary constriction. It provides significant changes in AHI and airway volume, and the risk of complications is low. These findings have to be validated in larger and long-term trials.Keywords: Airway volume, Apnea–hypopnea index, Cone-beam computed tomography, Craniofacial morphology, Maxillary expansion, Miniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion, Obstructive sleep apnea

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a multifactorial respiratory disorder. It involves the repeated collapse of the upper airways during sleep, which leads to interrupted ventilation and sleep fragmentation. This on-and-off hypoxia-reoxygenation process triggers the systemic inflammatory pathways and changes autonomic tone, predisposing to broader morbidities such as cardiovascular disease, metabolic dysfunction, neurocognitive decline, and reduced quality of life.1 Epidemiological research indicates that OSA impacts close to 1 billion people worldwide, with the prevalence rate of between 9 and 38% in adults and >10% in children.2

Obstructive sleep apnea is traditionally viewed as a disease of obese middle-aged men, but is increasingly understood as strongly heterogeneous, with phenotypes that are determined not only by excess adiposity and airway compliance, but also by craniofacial anatomy, neuromuscular responsiveness, ventilatory control stability, and variable arousal threshold.3 Within this group, the maxillomandibular skeletal discrepancies, specifically transverse maxillary deficiency, have become one of the most important factors that lead to an increase in airway resistance and predispose to collapse during sleep.4 Such structural constraints make the nasal cavity and posterior airway space narrow, increase nasal resistance, and encourage mouth breathing, which in itself interferes with the coordination of airway muscles.5

The most effective nonsurgical treatment is continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), that is usually used as first-line treatment of OSA. Nevertheless, in practice, the compliance is often not optimal because of the discomfort, noise, and interference with the way of life. Other forms of treatment, such as mandibular advancement device, positional therapy, hypoglossal nerve stimulation, and upper airway surgery, are frequently prescribed to patients who have anatomical obstruction or cannot tolerate CPAP. Here, dentofacial orthopedics has become a prominent role in the management of OSA, especially in patients who have a definite maxillary constriction or craniofacial abnormalities.6

Rapid maxillary expansion (RME) is one of such orthodontic interventions that have been proven to be effective in the treatment of pediatric OSA by enlarging the nasal cavity, decreasing inspiratory resistance, and increasing the airway volume.7 RME is able to provide transverse skeletal expansion in growing patients whose midpalatal sutures are patent, and to restore nasal airflow dynamics. Nevertheless, in the case of adolescents and adults, when the midpalatal suture is more interdigitated, the traditional RME can lead to the tipping of the teeth and periodontal stress instead of actual skeletal expansion.8

To address these anatomical shortcomings, a minimally invasive procedure, miniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion (MARPE), can be used to accomplish orthopedic expansion in patients who are skeletally mature. MARPE delivers expansion forces to the maxillary bones through the use of temporary anchorage devices (TADs) placed in the palatal vault, and it does not require osteotomies to separate the midpalatal suture.9 This method does not only eliminate most of the disadvantages of tooth-borne RPE but also provides a nonsurgical alternative to surgically assisted maxillary expansion (SARME) in a selected group of adults.10

Over the past years, a growing number of studies have assessed the effects of MARPE on the volume and cross-sectional area of the upper airway and airflow resistance, mainly using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and computational fluid dynamics (CFD). Investigations have found that the width of the nasal cavity increases, the nasopharyngeal resistance decreases, and the inspiratory flow dynamics are enhanced after MARPE. Remarkably, such anatomical and physiological changes can be converted into functional respiratory outcomes, such as a decrease in snoring, better oxygen saturation, and normalization of the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI).11

Although these findings are promising, the evidence on MARPE as a treatment option of clinically diagnosed OSA is still scattered. Most of the studies are retrospective or observational, and the studies are heterogeneous in terms of diagnostic criteria, expansion protocols, follow-up duration, and outcome measures. Moreover, past systematic reviews have tended to concentrate on conventional RME in children, surgically assisted expansion, or oral appliances in general, but not specifically on MARPE12 as a treatment of sleep-disordered breathing.

An umbrella review by Ventura et al. (2022) identified the possible role of MARPE in craniofacial architecture alteration but stated that its clinical efficiency in OSA treatment was yet to be elucidated.9 In a similar way, a meta-analysis concentrated more on skeletal and dental outcomes, and little was mentioned about the respiratory or sleep parameters.12 In that regard, there is an urgent necessity to synthesize the existing evidence on the effectiveness of MARPE in enhancing polysomnographic and respiratory outcomes among patients with confirmed OSA.

Objective

The objective of this systematic review is to critically evaluate the effectiveness of MARPE in the management of OSA. Specifically, we aim to assess:

Changes in the AHI as the primary outcome;

Secondary outcomes including oxygen saturation (SpO₂), airway volume (via CBCT or MRI), sleep quality indices (e.g., Epworth Sleepiness Scale); and

This review aims to identify whether MARPE can be viewed as an effective adjunct or alternative to the established therapy in specific OSA populations by combining the data of observational and controlled clinical studies.

Methods

Protocol and Reporting Standards

This systematic review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.13 The PRISMA extension to nonhealth disciplines (O'Dea et al., 2021) was used to conduct methodological procedures to guarantee transparency and reproducibility.14 The protocol of the review was organized with the help of the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study Design), and the registration was provided to the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO). The process of review followed all the steps of the review process, search strategy, eligibility criteria, risk of bias and data synthesis, and contemporary best practices of systematic evidence synthesis in clinical and translational health research.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were deemed eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria:

Population: Patients of any age (children, adolescents, or adults) with a clinical and/or polysomnographically confirmed diagnosis of OSA, defined by an AHI threshold of ≥5 events/hour.

Intervention: Application of MARPE, including both bone-borne and hybrid devices, with or without auxiliary surgical support.

Comparator: Pretreatment baseline data, sham appliances, no intervention, or alternative maxillary expansion modalities such as conventional rapid palatal expansion (RPE).

Outcomes

Primary: Change in AHI following intervention, as measured by full-night in-laboratory polysomnography or home sleep apnea testing (HSAT).

Secondary: Changes in oxygen saturation (SpO₂), subjective sleep quality [e.g., Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS)], volumetric airway measurements (CBCT or MRI), and procedural adverse events.

Study Design

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), nonrandomized controlled trials, prospective and retrospective cohort studies, and case–control studies were eligible. Narrative reviews, case reports, animal studies, and conference abstracts were excluded.

These criteria reflect an inclusive approach to identifying real-world evidence while maintaining methodological rigor appropriate for meta-analysis.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across 6 electronic databases: PubMed, MEDLINE, Scopus, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, from inception to March 2025. To minimize publication bias, additional gray literature was screened using OpenGrey, Google Scholar, and the reference lists of all included articles and relevant systematic reviews.

Search terms included Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords such as: “Obstructive Sleep Apnea,” “OSA,” “Miniscrew-Assisted Rapid Palatal Expansion,” “MARPE,” “upper airway volume,” “CBCT,” and “polysomnography.” Boolean operators and database-specific syntax were applied to enhance sensitivity. The search strategy was developed using standardized MeSH terms and free-text keywords with appropriate Boolean operators, in accordance with PRISMA 2020 recommendations for reproducible and transparent reporting.

Study Selection

Titles and abstracts were subsequently screened by 2 reviewers after deduplication. Studies that matched the inclusion criteria or whose eligibility could not be determined clearly were retrieved in full text. The disputes were solved with discussion and consensus, or adjudication by a third reviewer in case of necessity. All the steps of study selection were recorded in a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram, which makes the reporting of study inclusion fully transparent and replicable.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The Risk of Bias Assessment Tool for Nonrandomized Studies, version 2 (RoBANS 2), was used to evaluate the risk of bias in included nonrandomized studies.15 Areas that are evaluated in this tool are (1) participant selection, (2) confounding factors, (3) exposure measurement, (4) blinding of outcome measurement, (5) outcome data completeness, and (6) selective reporting of outcomes. All the domains were classified as either low, high, or unclear risk. The evaluation was done by 2 reviewers independently, and disagreement was solved through consensus.

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis

Quantitative Synthesis

A meta-analysis was conducted when results were reported in 2 or more studies with adequate data by using random-effects model, which supposes that true effect measures across studies are heterogeneous. Continuous data (e.g., AHI, airway volume) were calculated as weighted mean differences (WMD) or standardized mean differences (SMD) with respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Heterogeneity and Bias

The I² statistic was used to measure between-study heterogeneity where 25, 50, and 75% represented low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. When 10 or more studies recorded an outcome, funnel plots and Egger regression asymmetry test were used to determine the risk of publication bias.

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

Subgroup analyses were specified in advance to examine effect modifiers: age-group (pediatric vs adult), severity of OSA at baseline, MARPE type (bone-borne vs hybrid), and study design (prospective vs retrospective). Sensitivity analyses were carried out by omitting high risk-of-bias and outlier-results studies.

Certainty of Evidence

The strength and consistency of the evidence were evaluated using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) framework. The certainty of each outcome was graded as high, moderate, low, or very low depending on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Results

Study Selection

The first database search produced 1,624 records. Following removal of duplicates, 1,302 titles and abstracts were screened. Out of these, 73 full-text articles were evaluated in terms of their eligibility. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were predetermined, and 10 studies were selected as part of the final review. Narrative Synthesis of Outcomes

Apnea–Hypopnea Index

Three articles indicated the pre- and post-MARPE AHI. Brunetto et al. (2022) have shown that AHI decreased by 65.3% in nonobese adults, and 78.5 and 35.7% of participants had a reduction of 50% and more percent and AHI of <5, respectively.16 On the same note, Kim et al. (2022) found that the AHI was reduced by 72.4% in the pediatric population after nasomaxillary skeletal expansion.17 The 2 studies employed the full polysomnography and similar follow-up periods.

Oxygen Saturation and Respiratory Measures

Brunetto et al. (2022) and Kim et al. (2022) reported important increases in mean SpO2 and minimum oxygen saturation.16,17 Storto et al. (2019) found better nasal inspiratory peak flow and oral expiratory peak flow, as well as higher maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures, which means better airway efficiency after expansion.18

Airway Volume (Cone-beam Computed Tomography-based)

All 5 studies evaluating upper airway dimensions via CBCT reported a significant increase in nasal cavity and nasopharynx volume following expansion.17–19 In a meta-analysis of 244 patients, Li et al. (2023) revealed substantial long-term growth in nasal cavity volume (WMD = 3,646 mm³; p = 0.005) and nasopharyngeal volume (WMD = 1,021 mm³; p < 0.001).22 Differences in the oropharyngeal area were less and more variable among studies.

Craniofacial and Structural Correlations

Kim et al. (2022) concluded that minimal cross-sectional area (MCA) was the most effective predictor of respiratory improvement as compared to skeletal width alone.17 Gupta et al. (2023) and Sader et al. (2023) provided evidence to the biomechanical value of MARPE by demonstrating the redistribution of stress and increased transverse dimensions in the maxilla on 3-dimensional assessment and finite element modeling.7,10 Important volumetric and clinical outcomes are given in Table 3.

Quantitative Synthesis (Meta-analysis)

Because of the lack of data, only 2 studies (Brunetto et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2022) could be included in the meta-analysis of AHI.16,17 A random-effects model demonstrated a pooled mean difference in AHI reduction of −5.46 events/hour (95% CI: −6.01 to −4.90), representing a statistically and clinically significant improvement. Adverse Effects

Adverse effects were minor and mostly transient. Brunetto et al. (2022) reported temporary increases in overjet,16 while lack of skeletal split in ~15% of patients. No serious complications were reported across included studies.

Discussion

The present systematic review is a critical assessment of existing evidence about the efficacy of MARPE in managing OSA. The results indicate that MARPE leads to results in statistically and clinically significant decrease in AHI accompanied by an increase in airway volume and oxygenation. These results suggest that MARPE is an effective nonsurgical supplemental treatment in the appropriate patient with transverse maxillary deficiency and upper airway morphology compromised by the transverse maxillary deficiency.

The pooled AHI change revealed in our meta-analysis was −5.46 events/hour, in line with the results of other clinical studies that indicated a decrease >65% in both adult and pediatric populations.16,17 Such gains can be compared to the ones achieved after the surgically assisted rapid palatal expansion (SARPE) procedure, which supports the effectiveness of MARPE, but with a lower degree of invasiveness and better tolerability by patients. In addition, this decrease is specifically applicable to persons shifting between moderate and mild OSA or mild OSA and disease remission, especially in groups that are not CPAP adherent.

Therapeutic mechanisms through which MARPE works are mainly anatomical and functional. The widening of the midpalatal suture leads to enlargement of the nasal cavity, the volume of the airways, and the decrease in nasal resistance, which combine to increase airflow during sleep. Besides remodeling of the skeleton, MARPE is also linked to a transition towards nasal breathing in patients with chronic mouth breathing that has been reported to show an increase in oropharyngeal muscular endurance and breathing efficiency. When combined with structural improvements, these functional adaptations alleviate airway collapsibility, which is a critical pathophysiologic characteristic of OSA.23

Imaging-based studies have reported the consistent presence of volumetric increases of the nasal and nasopharyngeal compartments, with a reported range of ~2,500 to >5,000 mm³ in the increase depending on the method and duration of follow-up.20 More to the point, MCA seems to be a better indicator of OSA outcomes than the overall airway volume, which speaks to the point of localized geometric remodeling.17 These findings agree with past investigations indicating that airflow disorder in OSA is focal, not diffuse, and in most cases, it is at a region of dynamic compliance like the velopharynx and base of the tongue.

Concerning appliance design, a number of studies have established that bone-borne or hybrid MARPE systems provide better skeletal results than tooth-borne expanders, especially in postadolescent individuals with ossified midpalatal sutures. This is supported by the findings of finite element modeling studies, which demonstrate a more favorable stress distribution over the maxillary complex, fewer dentoalveolar side effects, and a more symmetrical expansion with bone-borne devices.7 Such benefits are particularly applicable to adult patients, where the traditional RME is known to be hampered by elevated skeletal resistance.

Regarding the patient-reported outcome, a change in subjective sleep quality, daytime alertness, and snoring intensity was reported, with a limited number of studies using validated measures, including the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) or questionnaires on the quality of sleep.16 The wider psychosocial value of better sleep architecture, in terms of its impact on cognitive performance, mood, and metabolic control, are also under-investigated and should be subject to systematic study.

Notably, there were few adverse effects in relation to MARPE. The reported complications were temporary discomfort, gingival irritation, and in some patients, the inability to split midpalatal suture. They are significantly less severe than those related to SARPE or multilevel airway procedures, which further confirms positive risk–benefit data on MARPE.24

Clinical Implications

MARPE is a special chance to treat OSA at its structural basis in patients with diagnosable craniofacial and maxillary insufficiencies. As opposed to CPAP, which only splints open the airway, MARPE produces permanent anatomical remodeling. It is especially useful in growing adolescents, when skeletal changes are more readily achieved, but increasingly feasible in adults with selective patient choices and appropriate appliance design. In the light of these advantages, MARPE is to be regarded as an interdisciplinary treatment protocol with orthodontists, sleep specialists, and ENT specialists.

Limitations

Although the results are encouraging, the interpretation of the evidence is limited by several factors. Most notably, the retrospective or nonrandomized design of most studies prevents the establishment of causality. Second, there was a significant degree of heterogeneity in the protocols of interventions, demographics of patients, diagnostic thresholds, and imaging modalities. Third, the follow-up periods were not long (<12 months), and it was not possible to draw conclusions about the long-term stability of the airways and relapse. Also, the sleep architecture parameters, including arousal index or REM-specific AHI, were not consistent, even though they are important in clinical phenotyping Suzuki.22

Conclusion

Miniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion is one of the minimally invasive interventions in treating OSA patients with maxillary transverse deficiency. The systematic review and meta-analysis showed that MARPE has significant effects on the AHI, nasal airway volume, and oxygenation parameters with little adverse effects. These structural and functional enhancements make MARPE a feasible alternative or supplement to conventional therapies, especially among CPAP-intolerant patients or those who want a lasting anatomical solution.

Short-term results are always positive, but there is not much evidence in the long term. Miscellaneous issues that make comparing studies is the variability of design in appliances, protocols of expansion, and diagnostic criteria. However, the existing evidence highlights the significance of the skeletal input in OSA pathophysiology and the increasing role of orthodontically assisted airway expansion in the interdisciplinary care paradigm.

Longitudinal, high-quality, and randomized trials are required in the future to validate the long-term efficacy of MARPE and to optimize the protocols of patient selection. Until that time, clinicians must take into account MARPE in terms of personal craniofacial morphology, skeletal maturation, and patterns of airway obstruction.
