Comparison of the effectiveness of clinical judgment, wells score and revised geneva score in suspected pulmonary embolism
Authors: Muhammed Ikbal Sasmaz, Mustafa Yorgancioglu, Abdurrahman Oral, Ismail Uzkuc, Osman Acar, Akkan Avci, Mustafa Uçar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundShortness of breath and chest pain are the most common presenting symptoms of pulmonary embolism (PE); however, these findings lack both sensitivity and specificity. Therefore, clinical probability assessment remains a key component in the diagnostic evaluation of suspected PE. Clinical prediction rules such as the Wells and Revised Geneva scores are widely used to estimate pre-test probability.ObjectiveThis study aimed to compare emergency physicians’ clinical judgment with the Wells and Revised Geneva scores in predicting pulmonary embolism in patients presenting to the emergency department with suspected PE.MethodsThis prospective dual-center study included patients presenting to the emergency departments of two tertiary care hospitals with suspected PE. The evaluating emergency physician first assessed the clinical probability of PE (low, intermediate, or high) based solely on clinical judgment. Subsequently, the Wells and Revised Geneva scores were calculated independently by a second physician who was blinded to the initial clinical assessment. The diagnostic performance of clinical judgment, Wells score, and Revised Geneva score was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsA total of 248 patients with suspected PE were included in the study. Pulmonary embolism was confirmed in 38 patients (15.3%). Clinical judgment showed a numerically higher AUC compared with both the Wells and Revised Geneva scores. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.834 for clinical judgment, 0.766 for the Wells score, and 0.757 for the Revised Geneva score.ConclusionIn patients presenting with suspected pulmonary embolism, emergency physicians clinical judgment showed favorable diagnostic discrimination and a numerically higher AUC than the Wells and Revised Geneva scores. These findings suggest that clinical assessment remains a valuable component of the diagnostic approach to suspected PE.

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is the third leading cause of death from cardiovascular diseases after acute coronary syndrome and stroke [1]. Patients with PE most commonly present to the emergency department, where clinical manifestations may vary widely. Although dyspnea and chest pain are the most frequently reported symptoms, these findings lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity for the diagnosis of pulmonary embolism [2]. Therefore, assessment of clinical probability remains a cornerstone in the diagnostic evaluation of suspected PE.

Several clinical prediction rules have been developed to estimate the pre-test probability of pulmonary embolism. Among these, the Wells score and the Revised Geneva score are the most widely used scoring systems in clinical practice [3, 4]. These tools classify patients into low, intermediate, or high probability groups and are frequently incorporated into diagnostic algorithms. Current European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines recommend combining clinical probability assessment with D-dimer testing to guide further diagnostic evaluation in patients with suspected PE [5].

The Wells scoring system includes both objective and subjective clinical variables. One of its components, “an alternative diagnosis less likely than PE,” relies on the clinician’s judgment. Consequently, the probability category assigned to the same patient may vary depending on the clinician’s interpretation. This highlights the continuing importance of clinical judgment in the diagnostic evaluation of suspected pulmonary embolism.

Previous studies have also investigated the role of clinician gestalt in the diagnostic assessment of PE. In a prospective study including patients with suspected pulmonary embolism, Penaloza et al. demonstrated that unstructured clinician assessment performed similarly to established clinical prediction rules in estimating the probability of PE [6]. These findings suggest that physician judgment may capture additional clinical information that is not fully represented in structured scoring systems.

In routine emergency department practice, physicians frequently evaluate patients presenting with symptoms such as dyspnea, chest pain, or syncope and consider pulmonary embolism as part of the differential diagnosis. Based on clinical history, physical examination findings, and risk factors for venous thromboembolism, clinicians often form an initial estimation of PE probability even before applying formal clinical prediction rules.

Therefore, the present study aimed to compare emergency physicians’ clinical judgment with the Wells score and the Revised Geneva score in predicting pulmonary embolism among patients presenting to the emergency department with suspected PE.

Methods

Study design and study population

This prospective multicenter observational study was conducted in the emergency departments of two tertiary-care hospitals between March 1, 2022 and September 1, 2022.

Adult patients presenting to the emergency department with suspected pulmonary embolism (PE) were eligible for inclusion. Patients were considered to have suspected PE if they presented with symptoms suggestive of PE such as dyspnea, chest pain, hemoptysis, or syncope, or if they had risk factors for venous thromboembolism (VTE).

Patients were excluded if they were younger than 18 years of age, pregnant, or declined participation.

Risk factors for VTE were classified as strong or weak according to the categorization proposed by the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) [6]. Strong risk factors included active cancer, antiphospholipid syndrome, caesarean section, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, hospitalization for acute illness, major trauma or fracture, prolonged immobilization (> 3 days), and surgery lasting more than 30 min. Weak risk factors included chronic inflammatory diseases, nursing home residence, obesity, personal or family history of VTE, pacemaker implantation, prolonged travel (> 4 h), estrogen therapy, infection, minor trauma or fracture, pregnancy or puerperium, surgery lasting less than 30 min, and venous catheterization.

Consecutive adult patients presenting to the emergency departments with clinical suspicion of pulmonary embolism during the study period were prospectively evaluated for study inclusion.

All physicians participating in the study were experienced board-certified emergency medicine specialists with more than 5 years of independent post-residency clinical practice.

Clinical probability assessment

Upon admission, the first emergency physician evaluated each patient and estimated the probability of pulmonary embolism based on clinical history, physical examination findings, and the presence of VTE risk factors. The physician categorized the probability of PE as low, intermediate, or high based solely on clinical judgment, without using any formal scoring system. Clinical judgment regarding the probability of pulmonary embolism was established before the availability of D-dimer results and imaging studies.

Subsequently, a second physician, who was blinded to the initial clinician’s assessment, calculated the Wells score and the Revised Geneva score for each patient using the information recorded in the study form. Based on these scoring systems, patients were categorized into low, intermediate, or high probability groups.

Diagnostic evaluation for pulmonary embolism

Diagnostic evaluation was performed according to current guideline-based clinical practice.

Patients with low or intermediate clinical probability and a negative D-dimer test were considered unlikely to have pulmonary embolism, and PE was ruled out without further imaging.

Patients with positive D-dimer results or high clinical probability underwent computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) for confirmation or exclusion of PE.

Pulmonary embolism was defined as the presence of an intraluminal filling defect in the pulmonary arterial system on CTPA.

For each patient, vital signs, presenting symptoms, laboratory results, diagnostic imaging findings, final diagnosis, and clinical outcomes were recorded in the study database.

Patients who did not undergo CTPA and those with incomplete follow-up data were excluded from the final analysis.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Health Sciences Ethics Committee of Manisa Celal Bayar University (Approval date: 11 May 2022, Approval number: 20478486/1339). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their first-degree relatives before participation in the study.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) depending on the distribution of the data, while categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages.

Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables.

The diagnostic performance of clinical judgment, the Wells score, and the Revised Geneva score for predicting pulmonary embolism was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each method. For statistical analyses, clinical judgment categories were numerically coded as low = 1, intermediate = 2, and high = 3.

In addition, univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the association between each predictive method and the presence of pulmonary embolism. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated.

A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of these, 18 patients were excluded because CT pulmonary angiography was not performed, and 31 patients were excluded due to incomplete follow-up data. Consequently, 248 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Discussion

The main finding of the present study is that clinical judgment showed favorable diagnostic performance and a numerically higher AUC than the Wells score and the Revised Geneva score in patients presenting to the emergency department with suspected pulmonary embolism. Our results suggest that physician assessment may provide additional diagnostic value beyond structured clinical prediction rules.

Pulmonary embolism remains one of the leading causes of cardiovascular mortality worldwide and represents a major diagnostic challenge in emergency medicine [1]. Patients frequently present with nonspecific symptoms such as dyspnea, chest pain, or syncope, which complicates early diagnosis [2]. Because these clinical findings are not specific for pulmonary embolism, clinicians must rely on a combination of clinical evaluation, laboratory testing, and imaging methods.

To improve diagnostic efficiency and avoid unnecessary imaging, several clinical prediction models have been developed. Among these, the Wells score and the Revised Geneva score are the most widely used tools for estimating the pretest probability of pulmonary embolism [3, 4]. These models categorize patients into low, intermediate, or high probability groups and help guide further diagnostic testing such as D-dimer measurement and computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) [5].

In addition to structured scoring systems, clinicians frequently rely on their own clinical judgment when evaluating patients with suspected pulmonary embolism. Clinical gestalt reflects the physician’s overall assessment based on patient history, physical examination findings, and risk factors for venous thromboembolism. In a large prospective study, Penaloza et al. compared clinician gestalt with the Wells and Revised Geneva scores and demonstrated that clinician judgment performed similarly to validated clinical prediction rules in predicting pulmonary embolism [6].

Several studies have compared the Wells and Revised Geneva scoring systems with varying conclusions. Young et al. reported that the Wells score was not significantly associated with pulmonary embolism diagnosis in orthopedic trauma patients [7]. In contrast, Penaloza et al. reported that the Wells score may perform better than the Revised Geneva score in patients with a high pretest probability [8]. Several studies have suggested that the Wells score may demonstrate slightly better diagnostic performance than the Revised Geneva score in selected patient populations [8, 9]. However, other studies have suggested that both scoring systems have comparable diagnostic performance in emergency department settings [10].

One important limitation of the Wells score is the presence of a subjective component, namely the criterion “an alternative diagnosis less likely than pulmonary embolism.” This component depends on physician judgment and may vary between clinicians. Previous studies have demonstrated that this subjective element may contribute significantly to the predictive value of the Wells score [11, 12].

Another limitation of clinical scoring systems is that they include a limited number of predefined clinical variables. Certain important risk factors for venous thromboembolism, such as advanced age, trauma, surgery, or prolonged immobilization, have been associated with an increased risk of pulmonary embolism [13–15]. The pathophysiology of venous thromboembolism is classically explained by Virchow’s triad, which includes venous stasis, hypercoagulability, and endothelial injury [16].

In addition to these traditional risk factors, other conditions such as hormonal therapy and specific lifestyle-related factors may contribute to thromboembolism risk [17, 18]. Diagnostic tests including chest radiography, electrocardiography, arterial blood gas analysis, and laboratory markers such as D-dimer may support the diagnostic process but are not sufficient to confirm the diagnosis alone [2, 19]. Imaging modalities such as computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA), ventilation–perfusion scintigraphy, and echocardiography provide more reliable diagnostic information, while pulmonary angiography has historically been considered the gold standard method [20].

Our results showed favorable diagnostic performance of physician clinical judgment, with a numerically higher AUC than both the Wells and Revised Geneva scores. One possible explanation is that clinicians integrate multiple sources of clinical information simultaneously, including patient history, subtle symptoms, physical examination findings, and contextual risk factors. For example, prolonged air travel has been recognized as a potential risk factor for pulmonary embolism and may influence physician assessment even though it is not included in standard clinical prediction rules [21].

Our findings are also supported by previous studies evaluating clinician gestalt. Runyon et al. demonstrated that unstructured clinician estimates of pretest probability could perform similarly to established clinical decision rules in predicting pulmonary embolism in emergency department patients [22].

Despite these findings, clinical judgment has limitations. Unlike structured scoring systems, clinician gestalt lacks standardization and may vary depending on physician experience and training. Nevertheless, in high-volume emergency departments where rapid decision-making is required, physician assessment remains an important component of the diagnostic evaluation.

From a clinical perspective, our findings suggest that physician clinical judgment may remain an important component of the diagnostic evaluation of patients with suspected pulmonary embolism in the emergency department. Although validated clinical prediction rules such as the Wells and Revised Geneva scores provide standardized approaches for estimating pretest probability, experienced clinicians may integrate additional clinical information that is not fully captured by structured scoring systems. Therefore, clinical decision rules should be considered as supportive tools rather than replacements for physician judgment in the diagnostic assessment of pulmonary embolism.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of patients diagnosed with pulmonary embolism was relatively small. Second, clinical probability assessment based on physician judgment was performed by only four emergency physicians; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to physicians with different levels of experience or to other emergency department settings. Third, although the study included two centers, larger multicenter studies are needed to confirm the diagnostic value of clinical judgment compared with established clinical prediction rules. Fourth, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, which may limit the statistical power to detect small differences between diagnostic methods. Finally, a formal statistical comparison of the ROC curves was not performed. Therefore, although clinical judgment showed a numerically higher AUC than the Wells and Revised Geneva scores, this study cannot establish statistically confirmed superiority among these diagnostic approaches.

Conclusion

In patients with suspected pulmonary embolism, physician clinical judgment showed favorable diagnostic performance and a numerically higher AUC than the Wells and Revised Geneva scores. These findings suggest that physician assessment remains an important component of the diagnostic evaluation of pulmonary embolism in the emergency department. Further large-scale multicenter studies, including formal comparative analyses of diagnostic performance, are needed to better define the role of clinical judgment in combination with structured clinical prediction rules.
