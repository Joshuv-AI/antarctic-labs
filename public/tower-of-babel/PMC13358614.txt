Preservation of the right bronchial artery by maintaining the azygos arch during minimally invasive esophagectomy: a two-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yasuo Tsuda, Tomonori Nakanoko, Yasue Kimura, Keita Natsugoe, Qingjiang Hu, Tetsuro Kawazoe, Sho Nambara, Koji Ando, Mizuki Ninomiya, Eiji Oki, Tomoharu Yoshizumi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative pneumonia remains one of the most frequent and clinically important complications after esophagectomy for esophageal cancer. Research suggests that preservation of the bronchial arterial blood supply reduces pulmonary complications; however, a reproducible surgical strategy applicable to routine practice has yet been established. This study aimed to evaluate whether intentional preservation of the azygos arch as a structural strategy was associated with a reduced incidence of postoperative pneumonia.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed patients who underwent minimally invasive McKeown esophagectomy for esophageal cancer at two institutions between April 2015 and December 2023. Patients were classified according to whether an intentional azygos arch-preservation strategy was applied to structurally protect the right bronchial artery (RBA). Postoperative pneumonia was the primary outcome. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with postoperative pneumonia, adjusting for patient background, tumor characteristics, surgical approach, reconstruction route, preoperative treatment, year of surgery, and institution.ResultsPostoperative pneumonia occurred less frequently in patients managed with the azygos arch-preservation strategy than in those who underwent resection (8% vs. 17%, P=0.01). In multivariable analysis showed that, the azygos arch-preservation strategy was independently associated with a lower risk of postoperative pneumonia [adjusted odds ratio (OR) =0.21; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.08–0.53; P=0.001], whereas male sex and surgical approach were associated with postoperative pneumonia. Preservation of the azygos arch did not compromise upper mediastinal lymph node dissection.ConclusionsAn intentional azygos arch–preservation strategy during minimally invasive esophagectomy was independently associated with a reduced risk of postoperative pneumonia without compromising oncological adequacy. This reproducible surgical approach represents a practical strategy to improve postoperative respiratory outcomes in esophageal cancer surgery.Keywords: Esophageal cancer, esophagectomy, postoperative pneumonia, bronchial artery, azygos arch

Introduction

Postoperative pneumonia is one of the most serious complications following esophageal cancer surgery (1,2), and difficulty in sputum expectoration is a key contributing factor. Effective sputum expectoration depends not only on respiratory mechanics but also on the activity of the respiratory cilia. Reduced tracheobronchial blood flow can impair mucociliary clearance, resulting in atelectasis and subsequent postoperative pneumonia (3).

In thoracic esophageal cancer surgery, preservation of the bronchial arteries is expected to supply sufficient blood flow for the trachea and bronchus. However, bronchial arteries are thin and can be easily injured inadvertently during esophageal cancer surgery (4). Since the right bronchial artery (RBA) runs dorsal to the azygos vein arch (AVA), we hypothesized that preserving the RBA by maintaining the azygos arch would help avoid inadvertent injury to the RBA during esophagectomy and reconstruction.

Although preservation of the bronchial artery reduces postoperative pulmonary complications, direct identification and preservation of this vessel can be technically challenging (5). This difficulty is partly attributable to substantial anatomical variability of the bronchial arteries and limited intraoperative visibility, highlighting the potential utility of preoperative vascular mapping (6,7). Therefore, till date, a reproducible and standardized surgical strategy to protect the bronchial arterial blood supply in routine practice has not been fully established.

The azygos arch is a stable anatomical structure closely associated with the course of the RBA. We hypothesized that intentional preservation of the azygos arch could serve as a practical surgical strategy to structurally protect the RBA and thereby reduce postoperative pneumonia. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to evaluate the clinical impact of an azygos arch–preservation strategy on postoperative pneumonia after minimally invasive esophagectomy. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0098/rc).

Methods

Study population

Between April 2015 and December 2023, 375 patients with esophageal cancer who underwent McKeown esophagectomy (8) were reviewed at two institutions: the Department of Surgery and Science, Kyushu University, and Iizuka Hospital. Both participating institutions performed surgeries with and without an AVA-preservation strategy during the study period, and the surgical approach was independent of the specific institution. Patients were initially managed according to the intent to preserve the AVA at the start of upper mediastinal dissection. In cases where AVA preservation was initially intended but subsequently deemed unsafe because of tumor invasion, dense adhesions, or intraoperative bleeding, conversion to AVA resection was performed. The decision to preserve or resect the azygos arch was made intraoperatively based on tumor location, technical feasibility, and surgical judgment rather than predefined patient characteristics. Patients were classified according to the final surgical status (preserved vs. resected) for outcome analyses. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the association between an AVA-preservation strategy and the incidence of postoperative pneumonia within 30 days after esophagectomy.

Surgical procedure (Video S1)

Preservation of the azygos vein arch and right bronchial artery during thoracic esophagectomy. This video demonstrates the surgical technique for preserving the azygos vein arch and the right bronchial artery during upper mediastinal lymph node dissection in thoracic esophagectomy. Key anatomical landmarks and stepwise dissection strategies are shown to illustrate how vascular preservation can be achieved without compromising oncologic completeness.

Both participating institutions used similar perioperative management protocols for esophagectomy, including respiratory care and strategies for the prevention of postoperative pneumonia.

Robot-assisted procedures were performed using the da Vinci Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). The patient was placed in the prone position under general anesthesia using a single-lumen endotracheal tube. Left single-lung ventilation was performed using a blocker and the right thoracic cavity was approached. Thoracoscopically, the operation was started with a 12-mm port inserted between the 5th intercostal space, a camera port inserted between the 9th intercostal space, and 5-mm ports inserted between the 3rd and 7th intercostal spaces. In robotic surgery, 8-mm ports were inserted between the 3rd, 5th, 7th, and 9th intercostal spaces, with the 7th intercostal port used as the camera port. A 12-mm port for the assistant was inserted into the 6th intercostal space.

Step 1 (Figure 1): after making an incision at the pleura of the esophagus in the middle and lower mediastinum, the esophagus was mobilized from the aortic wall and pericardial surface during lymphadenectomy at the surrounding thoracic aorta and the cranial side of the diaphragm. Lymphadenectomy was performed at the tracheal bifurcation, and the pulmonary branch from the right vagus nerve was preserved.

After incision of the pleura around the AVA as the broken line, the esophagus was mobilized from the aorta. AVA, azygos vein arch.

Step 2 (Figure 2): after opening the pleura on the cranial side of the AVA, the RBA was mobilized from the tracheal membrane while maintaining its continuity with the AVA. Sufficient mobilization from the trachea to the vertebral and cardiac sides was required to obtain relevant mobility from the esophagus and trachea.

The RBA was mobilized from the tracheal membrane while maintaining continuity with the AVA. AVA, azygos vein arch; RBA, right bronchial artery; Rt., right.

Step 3 (Figure 3): the esophagus was mobilized from the tracheobronchial membrane and from the aortic side and then encircled. After completing the right upper mediastinal lymph node dissection, the esophagus was dissected using a linear cutter to pull the oral side of the esophagus out of the AVA. After removing the oral side of the esophagus, the left upper mediastinal lymph node dissection was completed, while preserving the AVA and RBA (9).

The left side of upper mediastinal lymph nodes were dissected while preserving the AVA. AVA, azygos vein arch; Lt., left.

Statistical analysis

All continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Univariate logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between candidate variables and postoperative pneumonia.

A prespecified full multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to evaluate the independent association between the AVA preservation strategy and postoperative pneumonia, adjusting for age, sex, pre-existing pulmonary disease, tumor location, clinical stage, preoperative treatment, surgical approach, reconstruction route, year of surgery, and institution.

Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using JMP® version 18.2.2.

As a sensitivity analysis, we constructed a reduced multivariable logistic regression model using a parsimonious set of variables selected based on univariate screening and clinical relevance. This analysis was performed to evaluate whether the association between the AVA-preservation strategy and postoperative pneumonia remained robust under an alternative modeling approach.

Discussion

Postoperative pneumonia remains one of the most clinically relevant complications after esophagectomy and is associated with prolonged hospitalization, impaired recovery, and poor long-term outcomes (10,11). In the present two-center retrospective cohort study, we demonstrated that an intentional AVA preservation strategy, during a minimally invasive McKeown esophagectomy, was independently associated with a significantly reduced risk of postoperative pneumonia, even after rigorous adjustment for patient-related factors, tumor characteristics, surgical approach, reconstruction route, institutional differences, and era effects.

The pathophysiology of postoperative pneumonia after esophagectomy is multifactorial. However, impaired airway clearance due to reduced mucociliary transport is considered a key mechanism. The vascular supply of the trachea originates predominantly from the lateral aspects, forming a critical vascular network near the tracheal membrane, as originally described by Salassa et al. in their classic anatomical study. This lateral vascular network is essential for maintaining the mucociliary function, and ischemic injury to this region may predispose patients to impaired airway clearance and postoperative pneumonia (12). Adequate perfusion of the tracheobronchial tree is essential for maintaining ciliary function, and ischemic injury to the tracheal or bronchial mucosa may lead to sputum retention, atelectasis, and subsequent pneumonia (13). Anatomically, the RBA frequently runs dorsal to the AVA, and inadvertent injury to this vessel can occur during upper mediastinal dissection or gastric conduit reconstruction. Preserving the AVA as a structural unit allows the RBA to be indirectly protected, thereby maintaining tracheobronchial blood flow without the need for direct and technically demanding arterial dissection.

Previous studies have reported the importance of bronchial artery preservation during esophagectomy and have demonstrated an association between bronchial artery resection and postoperative pulmonary complications, including pneumonia, in multivariable or propensity-adjusted analyses. However, most of these studies have focused on the presence or absence of bronchial artery preservation, which can be difficult to standardize in routine practice because of anatomical variations, vessel fragility, and limited intraoperative visibility. Preservation of the bronchial artery can be technically challenging because of its substantial anatomical variability and limited intraoperative visibility, particularly during minimally invasive esophagectomy. Previous studies using three-dimensional computed tomographic angiography have demonstrated marked inter-individual variations in bronchial artery anatomy, which complicates the direct identification and preservation of this vessel during surgery (7). In contrast, the present study did not aim to reevaluate the biological effects of bronchial artery preservation. Instead, we evaluated preservation of the azygos arch as an intentional and reproducible surgical strategy designed to structurally protect the RBA. Because bronchial arteries exhibit substantial anatomical variability and limited intraoperative visibility, direct preservation can be technically challenging during minimally invasive esophagectomy. The azygos arch, however, represents a stable anatomical landmark that can be consistently identified during surgery. Therefore, preserving this structure provides a reproducible approach to protecting the RBA without requiring direct arterial dissection. This distinction is clinically important because preservation of the AVA is a pragmatic approach that can be consistently applied across surgeons and institutions. Thus, the present study extends previous observations regarding bronchial artery preservation by translating them into a standardized surgical strategy that can be applied in routine minimally invasive esophagectomy.

Importantly, the independent association between the AVA preservation strategy and reduced postoperative pneumonia remained robust after multivariable adjustment. This finding suggests that the benefit observed in this study cannot be fully explained by differences in surgical approach, patient background, or perioperative era and supports the clinical relevance of this strategy in real-world practice. In the present cohort, retrosternal reconstruction was frequently used in both groups. This reflects the institutional reconstruction strategy at the participating centers, where the retrosternal route has historically been preferred for gastric conduit reconstruction following McKeown esophagectomy. Importantly, reconstruction route was included as a covariate in the multivariable model to minimize potential confounding. From this perspective, the present study extends previous observations regarding bronchial artery preservation by translating them into a standardized surgical concept with a measurable clinical impact. Notably, this association remained consistent in the reduced multivariable model that included only the variables identified by univariate screening (Table S1), supporting the robustness of the primary findings.

Male sex was independently associated with postoperative pneumonia in the multivariable analysis. This finding is consistent with those of previous reports and possibly reflects unmeasured confounding factors, such as smoking exposure, baseline pulmonary reserve, and respiratory muscle strength, rather than a direct biological effect of sex. Robot-assisted surgery was also associated with postoperative pneumonia in the multivariable model. This result should be interpreted cautiously, as robot-assisted procedures were preferentially selected for more complex cases and introduced later in the study period. Previous propensity score-matched analyses have highlighted the influence of patient selection and institutional experience on short-term outcomes after robot-assisted esophagectomy (14). Despite adjusting for the year of surgery, residual confounding factors related to case selection and learning curve effects could not be fully excluded. Sensitivity analysis limited to robot-assisted procedures showed a consistent direction of effect, although statistical significance was attenuated because of the limited sample size.

From an oncological standpoint, preservation of the AVA does not compromise the adequacy of upper mediastinal lymph node dissection. The number of dissected upper mediastinal lymph nodes was comparable between groups, indicating that this strategy can be safely integrated into standard oncological esophagectomy. In addition, no injury to the RBA was observed during esophagectomy or gastric conduit reconstruction through the mediastinal route, suggesting that preservation of the AVA is technically feasible and safe when appropriate mobilization is performed.

Careful preoperative and intraoperative recognition of the vascular anatomy is essential during minimally invasive esophagectomy, particularly in the presence of vascular anomalies. Previous case reports have demonstrated the feasibility of minimally invasive esophagectomy even in patients with major venous anomalies, underscoring the importance of a precise anatomical understanding during complex mediastinal dissection (15,16).

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the retrospective design carries an inherent risk of residual confounding despite a comprehensive multivariable adjustment. Second, the choice to preserve or resect the AVA was not randomized and may have been influenced by anatomical complexity or surgeon preference. Third, the perioperative management protocols and diagnostic sensitivity relating to pneumonia may have evolved over time. To address potential era effects, the year of surgery was included as a continuous covariate in the multivariable model. However, this variable was intended solely for adjustment and not for causal interpretation. Finally, detailed data on smoking history and pulmonary function were not uniformly available and could not be incorporated into the analysis.

Despite these limitations, the magnitude and consistency of the adjusted association observed in this study suggest that the intentional preservation of the AVA as a strategy to protect the RBA has meaningful clinical implications. Given its technical feasibility, reproducibility, and oncological safety, this approach represents a practical means of reducing the incidence of postoperative pneumonia after minimally invasive esophagectomy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study demonstrated that an intentional AVA preservation strategy during minimally invasive McKeown esophagectomy is independently associated with a reduced risk of postoperative pneumonia. Importantly, this strategy did not compromise the adequacy of the upper mediastinal lymph node dissection, thereby supporting its oncological safety.

Rather than reevaluating the biological effects of bronchial artery preservation, our findings suggest that preserving the AVA represents a reproducible surgical strategy for structurally protecting the RBA in routine practice. This approach may provide a practical means for improving postoperative respiratory outcomes after esophagectomy for esophageal cancer.
