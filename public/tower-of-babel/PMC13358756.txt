Intraoperative recurrent laryngeal nerve monitoring in esophagectomy with lymphadenectomy
Authors: Femke E. Lammes, B. Feike Kingma, Jelle P. Ruurda, Richard van Hillegersberg
Published: 2026

Esophagectomy with an adequate lymphadenectomy is a crucial part of the curative treatment for esophageal cancer. Worldwide, most surgeons prefer transthoracic esophagectomy with two-field (i.e., thoracic and abdominal) lymphadenectomy over transhiatal esophagectomy, aiming to optimize oncological outcomes (1). Three-field (i.e. cervical, thoracic, and abdominal) lymphadenectomy is frequently performed in Asian centers that predominantly treat squamous cell carcinomas located high in the mediastinum. However, in the West, the additional cervical dissection is preserved for selected patients. While cervical metastases have traditionally been considered as distant metastases (M+) in the West, the 7th American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system changed this insight and classifies cervical metastases as locoregional disease (N+) (2,3). Hence, three-field lymphadenectomy has a significant role in esophageal cancer surgery throughout the world.

In both cervical and transthoracic dissection of the paratracheal lymph nodes, the recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) may be contused or damaged. So, in either way two-field (i.e., including the upper mediastinal lymph node stations) or three-field lymphadenectomy, a meticulous dissection is mandatory along the RLN. This dissection is challenging since damage to the RLN can result in hoarseness and difficulty swallowing, potentially leading to aspiration pneumonia and subsequent respiratory problems. Efforts to reduce RLN injury rates are warranted, and in this context, Shigeno et al. investigated the use of intraoperative nerve monitoring (IONM) in minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE) with three-field lymphadenectomy (4). This large, national, multicenter retrospective cohort study from Japan included 4,855 esophageal cancer patients undergoing MIE with three-field lymphadenectomy from 348 hospitals, aiming to compare postoperative outcomes between patients in whom IONM was used versus patients who were not monitored. In the IONM group (n=502), the RLN was monitored during lymph node dissection by either intermittently or continuously monitoring. The use of IONM was associated with less postoperative RLN injury and respiratory complications when compared to a control group, although operating time was longer in the IONM group. These results are promising, however, there are several issues that hamper the clinical generalized applicability. Most importantly, it was unclear how patients were selected for IONM and a detailed description of the method or device was lacking. Furthermore, despite propensity score matching (PSM), bias may have persisted in this retrospective national study.

The study by Shigeno et al. poses relevant questions as to if and how IONM could have a role in reducing RLN in esophagectomy with two-field or three-field esophagectomy. In the setting of a two-field lymphadenectomy, the recent randomized REVATE trial showed that robot-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (RAMIE) improves lymph node yield while reducing the permanent RLN injury rate to only 6% as compared to 20% in conventional MIE (5). Hence, by offering enhanced three-dimensional vision and improved dexterity, robotic surgery already very effectively minimizes RLN injury rates and the additional value of IONM during the thoracic phase of RAMIE with two-field lymphadenectomy is expected to be limited. However, in complex cases in RAMIE with three-field lymphadenectomy, particularly patients with cervical lymph node metastasis preceded by neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, postoperative RLN injury rates can be as high as 45% (6). In such complex cases, technologies such as IONM that may reduce RLN should be evaluated. The reduced postoperative RLNP with IONM reported by Shigeno et al., from 10.4% to 3.0%, is in line with this. Suggesting that IONM may offer greater benefit in high-risk procedures with extensive lymphadenectomy rather than two-field approaches, particularly those performed using robotic surgery.

For esophagectomy, high-quality data on the efficacy of IONM in reducing RLN injury are lacking. Most studies are small retrospective case series rather than large prospective trials. Brunet et al. summarized in a review the potential advantages of both continuous or intermittent IONM during esophagectomy regarding RLN palsy outcomes and lymph node harvest. Although, they emphasized stronger evidence is required to define its clinical role of IONM (7).

Takeda et al. reported more specific diagnostic outcomes, who included 167 patients undergoing MIE with three-field esophagectomy, of whom 84 patients received intermittent IONM. They reported a negative predictive value (NPV) and positive predictive value (PPV) of 87% and 60%, respectively. This indicates that, in the absence of loss of signal (LOS), IONM is useful in ruling out RLNP in almost 90% of the cases. However, when LOS was observed, clinical judgement was required as only 60% of the cases actually had RLNP. RLNP was significantly lower in patients with IONM (6.0% vs. 21.2%, P=0.02) (8).

In contrast, IONM is well established and widely integrated into thyroid surgery. Modern IONM systems are compact and incorporated into the standard operating room workflow. Though, system setup, anesthesia protocol modifications and negotiation of the learning curve by both surgeons and anesthetists remain essential. IONM during thyroid surgery is performed either intermittent, which is most widely used, or continuous, which is rapidly evolving (9). A learning curve of at least 50–100 cases is reported, with an improvement in the NPV from 89.3% to 99% and the PPV from 55.6% to 75% for LOS (10,11). The PPV is particularly limited during the early phase of the learning curve. These findings suggest after adequate training, IONM is reliable for confirming normal nerve function (high NPV), which may support safer surgical dissection. However, in cases of LOS, surgical expertise remains crucial to differentiate true nerve injury from false-positive LOS, also in thyroid surgery (9).

Overall, reported predictive values appear higher and more consistent in thyroid surgery than in esophageal surgery, likely reflecting the greater degree of integration. While IONM in esophagectomy shows promising results, its predictive accuracy and true clinical impact require validation in large, high-volume prospective studies.

In summary, the study by Shigeno et al. highlights the potential additional value of IONM in patients treated with three-field MIE, showing decreased RLN injury and respiratory complication rates at the cost of longer operating times. However, to optimize the thoracic dissection along the RLN, robotic surgery is probably the most important development and the additional value of IONM should be evaluated specifically in the context of RAMIE. IONM may be useful during challenging cervical dissections in RAMIE with three-field lymphadenectomy for patients with cervical lymph node metastases, although no specific data are available on this. High-quality prospective studies should be conducted to identify the most effective method (i.e., intermittent versus continuous IONM) and role in daily care (i.e., routine versus selective use) in order to reduce postoperative morbidity in esophagectomy.
