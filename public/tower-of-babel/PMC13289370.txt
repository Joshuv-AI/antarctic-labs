Awareness and use of e-cigarettes among Filipino adults: evidence from the 2015 and 2021 Global Adult Tobacco Surveys
Authors: Rutcher Madera Lacaza, Edrei Yambao Udaundo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite substantial declines in smoking prevalence in the Philippines over the past decade, many adults who smoke continue to face challenges in quitting, and the growing popularity of e-cigarettes has introduced new challenges for public health. As awareness of e-cigarette availability expanded, particularly among young adults and people who smoke seeking alternatives, patterns of use and nicotine dependence became more prominent. In 2020, the government introduced an excise tax on e-cigarettes to curb use and mitigate health risks. We examine patterns of e-cigarette awareness and current use among Filipino adults using nationally representative data.MethodsWe analysed the 2015 and 2021 Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS) waves. Survey-weighted logistic regression assessed associations between sociodemographic factors, tobacco use status and quit intentions, media exposure and (1) awareness and (2) current use among those aware. Since current use was uncommon in some subgroups, we conducted a Firth bias-reduced logistic sensitivity analysis to address small-sample bias and quasi-separation.ResultsAwareness of e-cigarettes was widespread, but use was concentrated among specific groups. Young adults, males and individuals with higher educational attainment were more likely to be aware of and use e-cigarettes, with prevalence highest among people who smoke and intend to quit.ConclusionsE-cigarette awareness and use were disproportionately higher among young adults and people who currently smoke. These findings highlight the importance of continuous monitoring of e-cigarette trends and the need for targeted public health interventions to prevent nicotine dependence and reduce potential gateway effects. Strengthening regulatory oversight and sustaining public education efforts are crucial to protecting vulnerable populations, particularly the youth.

Introduction

Cigarette smoking remains a significant global health concern, particularly because many people who smoke struggle to quit successfully. While many people who smoke are motivated to quit and make attempts to do so, most are unsuccessful due to limited use of evidence-based treatments such as smoking cessation counselling and pharmacotherapies.1 2 In recent years, electronic cigarettes (also known as e-cigarettes) have been increasingly promoted as an alternative to traditional cigarettes, with proponents arguing that they help minimise smoking-related harms among adults addicted to nicotine.3 4

E-cigarettes are battery-operated products that typically contain nicotine, flavourings and other chemicals. The growing popularity, particularly among adolescents and young adults, has been attributed to a variety of flavours, discreteness, ease of access, desire to experiment, perceptions of being safer and aggressive marketing.59 Despite claims that e-cigarettes can aid smoking cessation, their long-term safety and health effects remain uncertain.10 Some evidence suggests that while they may help some people who smoke to quit,1113 they also have the potential to sustain nicotine dependence rather than support complete cessation.14

Concerns about e-cigarettes stem from their largely unregulated nature, which makes them prone to abuse and increased use among young people, thereby reducing the effectiveness of tobacco cessation efforts.15 According to the WHO,16 e-cigarette use among adolescents aged 13–15 years has reached alarming levels, with prevalence consistently exceeding that of adults across all WHO regions. Based on comparisons of Global Youth Tobacco Survey (GYTS) and Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS) data, approximately 9–11% of adolescents globally use e-cigarettes or other nicotine products, compared with adult prevalence rates that are typically below 3–4% in many low- and middle-income countries.1618 Supporting this pattern, a recent global meta-analysis estimated current e-cigarette use among adolescents at approximately 4.8%, with ever use reaching nearly 17%, highlighting the substantial scale of youth exposure to these products.19 In several high-income countries, such as Canada and the UK, e-cigarette use among adolescents and young adults has increased sharply, doubling or tripling in recent years, and now exceeds adult prevalence, highlighting a widening age gradient in use.16 These trends raise significant public health concerns, given evidence that early exposure to nicotine increases the risk of long-term dependence and may facilitate subsequent initiation of combustible tobacco use.

In response to the rapid rise in e-cigarette use, particularly among children and young people, many governments have introduced taxation, age restrictions and regulations on their sale and consumption.20 However, the effectiveness of these measures has been constrained by several challenges, including the expansion of online sales, opportunities for tax evasion and the rapid evolution of product designs and flavourings that disproportionately appeal to younger users. The WHO also does not endorse e-cigarettes as a smoking cessation aid, citing insufficient scientific evidence.21 Moreover, studies suggest that e-cigarettes contain potentially harmful chemicals, some of which are also present in traditional cigarettes, raising concerns about long-term health effects.2224 Their accessibility, marketing appeal and perceived reduced harm may further contribute to smoking initiation among adolescents and young adults, warranting the need for stronger and more coherent regulatory oversight.

In the Philippines, evidence indicates a rapid increase in e-cigarette use in the years preceding the introduction of excise taxes particularly among young people. Data from 2019 GYTS showed that one in seven Filipino adolescents aged 13–15 used e-cigarettes.25 The same survey also indicates that 25% have tried using e-cigarettes at least once in their life, which is twice the 12% recorded in 2015, signalling a growing risk of early nicotine dependence. Analysis of 2021 GATS microdata indicates that approximately 3% of Filipino adults report current e-cigarette use, while awareness is high at around 70%. Although excise taxes on heated tobacco products and vapour products were introduced on 1 January 2020, under Republic Act (RA) 1134626 and RA 1146727 primarily as a demand-reduction measure, these reforms were also designed to generate additional revenues earmarked for the government’s Universal Healthcare programme. While such dual-purpose ‘sin taxes’ have been effective in reducing consumption of combustible tobacco products, the 2021 GATS was conducted too soon after implementation to assess the medium-term impact of these fiscal measures on adult e-cigarette use.

This uncertainty has been further compounded by subsequent regulatory developments. The passage of RA 11900 (the Vape Law) in July 2022 lowered the minimum legal age for purchasing from 21 to 18 years old and formally authorised e-commerce sales of e-cigarettes, effectively expanding the market. It also transferred regulatory authority from the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI), and removed the two-flavour limit, allowing more enticing options for young users. Under the law, online sellers are required to register with the DTI and comply with age-verification and health warning requirements. While proponents argued that this law supports harm reduction and consumer freedom, public health organisations have expressed concerns about the potential increase in youth vaping and renormalisation of nicotine use.28 29 Moreover, concerns persist regarding enforcement gaps, given the continued presence of unregistered sellers and illicit products on online platforms, which may undermine the law’s regulatory intent and contribute to tax leakage.30

Against this backdrop, there is a need to evaluate population-level differences in awareness and use of e-cigarettes across survey waves among Filipino adults in the Philippines. While numerous studies have examined these patterns in high-income countries,3134 research from developing contexts and emerging markets, such as the Philippines, remains limited. We are aware of only a few studies that examined awareness and use of e-cigarettes and variations by socio-economic characteristics in the Philippines. One study analysed data from the 2019 GYTS, highlighting e-cigarette use among 13–15 years old students.25 Another study used data from the GATS across 14 countries, including the Philippines, to assess use of e-cigarettes among adults.35 To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine patterns of e-cigarette awareness and use among Filipino adults, using nationally representative GATS data from 2015 and 2021 survey waves, with attention to demographic characteristics, tobacco use status and quit intentions. Understanding how these factors influence e-cigarette use helps identify groups most likely to experiment with or adopt such products and clarifies whether use represents cessation efforts or continued nicotine dependence.

Methods

Data

In this study, we used data from the GATS, a repeated cross-sectional survey conducted about every 5 years. To date, three waves have been implemented in the Philippines (2009, 2015 and 2021). GATS systematically tracks tobacco and e-cigarette use among adults aged 15 years and older, along with key tobacco control indicators, employing a multistage, geographically clustered sampling design. We pooled the 2015 and 2021 GATS waves because these are the only nationally representative surveys conducted in the Philippines that contain e-cigarette questions. Data are publicly available through the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention website (link: https://nccd.cdc.gov/GTSSDataSurveyResources/Ancillary/DataReports.aspx?CAID=2). GATS was implemented by national authorities in collaboration with the WHO and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention under standardised protocols that include ethical review and informed consent from all participants. This study is a secondary analysis of anonymised public-use data and therefore did not require additional ethics approval.

Listwise deletion was applied to handle missing data. This approach was selected because missingness was largely confined to secondary adjustment variables (such as wealth and media exposure), while key study variables including e-cigarette awareness, current use and tobacco use status had minimal missing data. Given the predominantly categorical variables, pooled survey waves and the complexity of implementing multiple imputation within a survey-weighted framework, listwise deletion was considered a transparent and appropriate approach. After performing listwise deletion, our final study sample consisted of 29 456 participants (N2021 = 18 083 (61.4%), N2015 = 11 373 (38.6%)).

The study examines two key dependent variables: awareness of e-cigarettes and current e-cigarette use. Awareness of e-cigarettes was determined by asking respondents, ‘Have you ever heard of e-cigarettes?’ with those answering ‘yes’ classified as being aware. Current e-cigarette use was measured by asking participants, ‘Do you currently use e-cigarettes on a daily basis, less than daily, or not at all?’ Respondents who answered ‘daily’ or ‘less than daily’ were classified as people who currently use e-cigarettes.

Independent variables included people who currently use tobacco and people who do not currently use tobacco, determined based on responses to, ‘Do you currently smoke tobacco on a daily basis, less than daily, or not at all?’ Those who answered ‘daily’ or ‘less than daily’ were categorised as people who currently use tobacco, while those answering ‘not at all’ were classified as people who do not currently use tobacco. Tobacco products considered included manufactured cigarettes, cigars, pipes, hand-rolled cigarettes, kreteks and water pipes (Shisha/Nargile). Among those who did not currently use tobacco, people who previously used tobacco and people who never used tobacco were identified based on the question, ‘In the past, have you smoked tobacco on a daily basis, less than daily, or not at all?’ Respondents who answered ‘daily basis’ or ‘less than daily’ were classified as people who previously used tobacco, while those answering ‘not at all’ were categorised as people who have never used tobacco.

Interest in quitting smoking was evaluated through the question, ‘Which of the following best describes your thinking about quitting smoking?’. The response options were: (1) quit within the next month, (2) thinking about quitting within the next 12 months, (3) quit someday, but not within the next 12 months and (4) not interested in quitting. Respondents choosing ‘quit within the next month’, ‘thinking about quitting within the next 12 months’ or ‘quit someday, but not within the next 12 months’ were considered as people interested in quitting smoking.

Initially, tobacco status use and quit intentions were captured using separate variables. To simplify the analysis and avoid potential collinearity issues between tobacco status use and quit intentions, these variables were directly combined into a single variable that classified respondents into four distinct categories: people who have never used tobacco, people who currently use tobacco with no plan to quit, people who currently use tobacco with a plan to quit, and people who previously used tobacco.

Following the literature on the determinants of awareness and prevalence of e-cigarette use, control variables included: sex (1=female, 0=male), age (divided into 15–24 years old, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64 and 65 and above), education (ranging from some elementary to college and above), employment status (unemployed, employed, not in the labour force) and wealth.

Following recent studies of Cheng and Estrada,36 Palipudi et al,37 Rutstein and Johnson,38 a wealth index was constructed using principal component analysis (PCA) on survey items that ask respondents whether they had their own electricity, flush toilet, fixed telephone, mobile, television, radio, refrigerator, car, scooter, washing machine, CD/VCD/DVD player, component/karaoke, laptop, tractor and motorised boat (Cronbach’s α2021=0.762, α2015=0.817). Respondents were ranked according to their PCA-derived wealth scores and grouped into survey year-specific tertiles representing the lower, middle and upper thirds of the wealth distribution (tertile 1=low wealth, tertile 2=middle wealth, tertile 3=high wealth). Additional controls included type of residence (1=rural, 0=urban) and media exposure to the harms of e-cigarette use. Similarly, the media exposure index was built using PCA on questions that ask whether the respondent has been exposed to messages about the harms of cigarettes from newspapers and magazines, local television, radio, billboard, monorails, cinema advertisements, healthcare facilities and malls (Cronbach’s α2021=0.809, α2015=0.790). Respondents were likewise categorised into survey year-specific tertiles corresponding to low, moderate and high levels of media exposure. Each respondent was assigned the average media exposure index for his/her primary sampling unit or type of residence.

Statistical analyses

We computed the percentage of awareness about e-cigarettes, as well as the prevalence of current e-cigarette use. Relative to adults aged 15–24 years, all older age groups had lower odds of both e-cigarette awareness and current use. For example, individuals aged 65 years and older had markedly lower odds of awareness (OR=0.21) and current use (OR=0.02) compared with the youngest age group (p<0.001).

Educational attainment was positively associated with e-cigarette awareness. Compared with individuals with no formal education, college graduates had higher odds of awareness (OR ≈ 4.33; p<0.05). For current use, ORs increased with education level but estimates from the survey-weighted model were imprecise; most education categories were not statistically significant, and the association for college graduates was marginally significant (p<0.10).

Household wealth and urban residence were also strong predictors of both outcomes. Individuals in the highest wealth tertile had higher odds of awareness (OR=2.41) and current use (OR = 3.00) compared with those in the lowest wealth group. Urban residents similarly exhibited higher odds of both awareness and use than rural residents.

Media exposure is another important factor, with higher exposure linked to greater e-cigarette awareness and use. Individuals in the highest media-exposure tertile had higher odds of awareness (OR =1.76) and current use (OR=1.73) compared with those in the lowest exposure group (p<0.05).

Tobacco use status emerged as the most significant predictor of both outcomes. Compared with individuals who had never used tobacco, current tobacco users with no plans to quit had higher odds of awareness (OR=1.86) and substantially higher odds of current use (OR = 6.81). Former tobacco users also had significantly higher odds of current e-cigarette use (OR=6.86, p<0.01). Interestingly, among people who currently use tobacco, those intending to quit exhibited the highest odds of e-cigarette use (awareness OR=2.35; use OR=7.74), suggesting that contemplation of cessation is associated with greater uptake of e-cigarettes.

Sensitivity analysis

Since current use is uncommon in several subgroups, we re-estimated the use equation with Firth bias-reduced logistic regression as a robustness check. Directions of effects were unchanged. As expected under bias reduction, some large contrasts were attenuated toward the null while remaining significant (eg, female vs male OR ≈ 0.57; 65+ vs 15–24 OR ≈ 0.05; 2021 vs 2015 OR ≈ 1.97). Education effects for current e-cigarette use became consistently significant across levels, suggesting that sparse education×use cells contributed to imprecision in the survey-weighted model. By contrast, employment and the highest media-exposure tertile were not significant under Firth, indicating estimator-sensitive associations. Primary inference relies on the survey-weighted estimates, while the Firth results, presented in online supplemental appendix table A1, support the robustness of the main conclusions.

Discussion

This study examined e-cigarette awareness and use among Filipino adults, focusing on socio-economic factors, tobacco use status and quit intentions using pooled 2015 and 2021 GATS data. Awareness and current e-cigarette use were consistently higher among young adults, males, those with higher educational attainment and people who currently use tobacco, especially those intending to quit, relative to their reference groups. The relatively high prevalence among young adults is a major public health concern given the long-term risks of nicotine dependence. This pattern aligns with previous research highlighting the vulnerability of younger populations to e-cigarette initiation due to factors like peer influence, family use, susceptibility to marketing and misperceptions about the harms of e-cigarettes.3941 In the Philippines, the rapid proliferation of e-cigarettes through social media and online platforms, combined with recent regulatory changes under the Vape Law (eg, changes to minimum age requirements, authorisation of e-commerce sales), may have increased youth exposure to e-cigarette marketing, potentially heightening the risk of initiation among young adults. The higher prevalence of e-cigarette use among males observed in this study is also consistent with global trends.25 35 This difference may reflect gender-specific social norms, differential risk perceptions and marketing strategies that disproportionately target men.42 In the Philippine context, these patterns may be further shaped by social norms around masculinity and risk-taking that have been documented in the broader tobacco use literature, which can contribute to less restrictive social expectations for young men compared with young women.43 The association between educational attainment and e-cigarette use should be interpreted with caution. While some studies have reported a negative correlation between education and e-cigarette use,14 our results indicate a generally positive association, with point estimates consistently higher across education categories. Although estimates from the survey-weighted model were imprecise, bias-reduced sensitivity analyses using Firth logistic regression yielded more stable estimates, suggesting that higher educational attainment may be associated with greater awareness and current use (see online supplemental appendix table A1). One possible explanation is that individuals with higher educational attainment may experience greater exposure to e-cigarette marketing and information environments, which could facilitate experimentation and uptake, consistent with the findings of Simon, et al.44

The strong association between tobacco use status and e-cigarette use highlights the potential role of e-cigarettes in both smoking cessation and continued nicotine dependence. For some people who currently smoke, e-cigarettes can serve as a tool for quitting conventional cigarettes, potentially aiding cessation efforts. However, dual use, in which people who smoke also use e-cigarettes, raises concerns about sustained nicotine dependence.1 45 In the Philippine context, dual use may be partly driven by limited access to and utilisation of formal smoking cessation services. While the Department of Health has established a national quit line and cessation guidelines for smoking cessation across levels of care, available evidence suggests that cessation services remain unevenly accessible and underused, with many people who smoke unaware of or unable to engage with formal support.46 In this setting, e-cigarettes may be perceived as a more readily available or self-directed quitting aid, contributing to dual use rather than complete cessation, a pattern also observed in studies of Filipino adults who smoke.47 Such dual-use patterns may undermine quitting efforts, as individuals continue to consume nicotine from multiple sources. Although some people who smoke may transition to exclusive e-cigarette use, potentially reducing exposure to harmful substances found in combustible cigarettes, this does not necessarily eliminate nicotine dependence, raising questions about whether such transitions constitute cessation.

Beyond smoking status, our findings reveal clear socio-economic and informational gradients in e-cigarette awareness and use among Filipino adults. Higher household wealth was consistently associated with greater awareness and current use, suggesting that access to and uptake of e-cigarettes may be concentrated among more economically advantaged groups. In the Philippine setting, this pattern may reflect greater affordability of vaping devices and consumables, which remain relatively costly compared with conventional cigarettes, as well as greater access to online markets and delivery services.44 In addition, the positive association between media exposure and e-cigarette awareness and use underscores the role of the information environment in shaping behaviour. This finding is consistent with evidence that exposure to e-cigarette advertising and promotional content, particularly through digital and social media, plays a substantial role in increasing awareness and uptake.40 These findings point to the need for regulatory and public health strategies that account for socio-economic disparities and media environments in addressing e-cigarette use.16 20

This interpretation should be considered in light of the scope of the GATS, which does not capture concurrent use of e-cigarettes with newer oral nicotine products such as nicotine pouches or with stimulant products such as energy drinks, and potential interactions with these products therefore could not be examined and warrant further investigation.

Overall, these results suggest that e-cigarette use in the Philippines is not evenly distributed across the adult population, but is shaped by socio-economic position, access to information and smoking status. These patterns have important implications for regulation and public health communication, particularly in a policy environment characterised by expanding online sales and evolving marketing practices. Regulatory and educational strategies that account for socio-economic disparities and media exposure may therefore be essential for mitigating potential harms associated with e-cigarette use.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, awareness and use of e-cigarettes were based on self-reported information, which may be subject to recall and social desirability biases. Second, although the study spans the period before and after the implementation of the 2020 excise tax, we did not aim to estimate its impact because only the 2015 and 2021 GATS waves included questions on e-cigarette awareness and use. The 2021 GATS captures data collected less than 2 years after the excise tax took effect on 1 January 2020 making it too early to assess its full or medium-term effects. The 6-year interval between surveys also means that the observed increase in e-cigarette use may partly reflect growth that occurred between 2015 and before the tax’s implementation, making it unclear whether prevalence declined or stabilised afterward.

Third, although we controlled for key demographic and socio-economic factors, potential unobserved confounders such as evolving product designs, marketing strategies and changes in enforcement of e-cigarette regulations could not be accounted for. Furthermore, because GATS collects data from different individuals in each wave, the design does not permit tracking individual changes over time or establishing causal relationships.

Conclusion and Recommendation

This paper examined awareness and current e-cigarette use among Filipino adults using pooled data from the 2015 and 2021 rounds of the GATS. The analyses showed that awareness and current e-cigarette use were higher among young adults, males, individuals with higher socio-economic position (including education and household wealth) and people who currently use tobacco, particularly those intending to quit, compared with their reference groups. While e-cigarettes may serve as a potentially safer alternative to conventional cigarettes and a harm-reduction tool for some people who currently smoke, their increasing use and high awareness among young adults pose important public health concerns. The potential for sustained nicotine dependence and gateway effects emphasises the need for proactive measures to limit population-level risks.

Policy efforts should prioritise reducing e-cigarette use among groups with higher prevalence, particularly young adults and people who currently use tobacco. In addition, interventions should account for socio-economic and informational drivers of use, including household wealth, educational attainment and media exposure. Targeted education campaigns for the youth, stricter regulation of online sales and advertising and the full implementation of textual and graphic health warnings under the Vape Law are essential to curb rising use and limit exposure. Policymakers should also consider revising existing regulations to close gaps in the current law, such as reinstating a two-flavour limit to reduce appeal at the population level, raising the minimum purchase age back to 21 years and restoring regulatory authority to the FDA for stronger oversight of marketing, sales and product safety, such as child-resistant and tamper-evident packaging requirements. This oversight should also include limits on maximum nicotine concentration. These standards should be aligned with international norms, including volume limits on refill containers and a lower nicotine ceiling than the current 65 mg/mL cap, which exceeds the 20 mg/mL standard applied in many regulating countries. Manufacturers should also be required to disclose product ingredients and emissions to regulators and to notify authorities of new products within a defined period before their intended marketing date.

Although this study did not aim to evaluate the impact of the 2020 excise tax, the observed concentration of use among price-sensitive groups such as young adults suggests that aligning excise tax rates on e-cigarettes more closely with those on conventional cigarettes could further discourage uptake among these groups. Since the 2021 GATS captures data collected less than 2 years after the excise tax took effect, continued surveillance through future survey rounds is needed to assess the medium-term impacts of taxation and subsequent regulations, including age restrictions, online sales provisions, as well as the regulation of social media marketing, event sponsorship and celebrity or influencer endorsement. Further research should also explore the long-term health effects of e-cigarette use and monitor how evolving policies shape consumption patterns. To balance fiscal and public health goals, regulatory frameworks must remain adaptive to the rapidly evolving e-cigarette market, ensuring stronger protections for youth, people who have never used tobacco, and other vulnerable groups.
