The Last Galactic Firework: Timing the last significant merger with stars, globular clusters and ω \omega Centauri 2025 The Last Galactic Firework: Timing the last significant merger with stars, globular clusters and ω \omega Centauri – 7
Chervin F. P. Laporte, Matthew D. A. Orkney

Abstract
We present a robust method to empirically infer the timing of the last significant merger in the Milky Way which is tested against fully cosmological models of galaxy formation. We apply it to Milky Way subgiant stars with spectro-photometric ages, finding that the last significant merger (Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus, GSE), occurred ∼ 11 \sim 11\, Gyrs ago. This coincides with the birth of a coeval in-situ group of globular clusters (GCs), which constrains the merger-induced starburst (hereafter Tainá ) to have occurred at 11.2 ± 0.1 ​ Gyr 11.2\pm 0.1\,\rm{Gyr} , the most precise dating of this merger event. The GSE’s most metal-rich GCs were also born around this time ( τ = 10.9 ± 0.1 ​ Gyr \tau=10.9\pm 0.1\,\rm{Gyr} ) and likely formed during the merger interaction prior to disruption of the GSE. We argue that ω \omega Centauri is the most likely candidate for the surviving remnant of the GSE, and show that its stellar populations have final ages and metallicities consistent with the GSE GCs together with observational evidence it may have been affected by bar resonances. Furthermore, we argue that the mean metallicity for which stellar orbits transition from halo-like to disc-like kinematics shows an upward inflexion point at [ Fe / H ] ∼ − 1.33 [\rm{Fe/H}]\sim-1.33 , and this sets an upper-limit for the age when the disc was forming. To corroborate this, we identify proto-MW GCs with highly disc-like orbits that formed before the last significant merger (with ages up to τ = 13.0 ± 0.5 ​ Gyr \tau=13.0\pm 0.5\,\rm{Gyr} ). This places the disc formation time as far back as as z disc ​ form ≳ 4 z_{\rm disc\,form}\gtrsim 4 .

Introduction

In the current
\Lambda
CDM cosmological model
(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2014
)
, galaxies grow hierarchically
(
Efstathiou et al. 1985
)
through the condensation of gas inside dark matter haloes
(
White & Rees 1978
)
. Traces of this hierarchical growth can often be seen in their stellar halos, which form primarily from disrupted accreted systems
(
Bullock & Johnston 2005
)
. At the scale of the Milky Way (MW), the
\Lambda
CDM framework predicts that the vast majority of accreted stars in the stellar halo were formed within a relatively few massive satellites
(
Robertson et al. 2005
;
Monachesi et al. 2019
)
, typically 1 to 3 within
20\,\rm{kpc}
of the Galactic centre
(
Fattahi et al. 2020
)
. A direct corollary from this should follow: the age–metallicity relation of the stellar halo should be dominated by its most massive contributor. This should set the age limit for the last significant merger.

Over time, a disc galaxy will gradually build up its angular moment from high-redshift to the present-day. This can be witnessed in its present-day distribution of orbital circularity (or rotational velocity) monotonically rising as a function of metallicity
(
Belokurov & Kravtsov 2022
)
. In the MW, this so-called spin-up marks a region where the transition between velocity dispersion support and rotational support rises significantly. The sharp transition in the MW may not signal a rapid formation process
(
Belokurov & Kravtsov 2022
, as argued in)
, but rather reflect how the last significant merger can shape this curve
(
Chandra et al. 2024
;
Orkney & Laporte 2025
)
. Thus, one may draw a direct link between the time of pericentric passage and the spin-up metallicity. This in turn, through the age–metallicity relation of the stellar halo can be used to deduce the time of such an impact.

With the advent of Gaia’s first data release and following releases, it has become clear that the most massive contributor to the inner-stellar halo is the debris of the Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus

Methods

In this study, we use two catalogues. For globular clusters (GCs), we make use of the compilation from
VandenBerg et al. 2013

Xiang & Rix 2022

\tau/\sigma_{\tau}\leq 10

(
GRAVITY Collaboration et al. 2018
)

)

(U,\,V,\,W)_{\odot}=(12.9,\,245.6,\,7.78)\,\rm{km\,s^{-1}}

Schönrich et al. 2010
;
Reid et al. 2014

(
Belokurov et al. 2023

Next, we note that a subset of the MW GCs (both with halo and disc-like orbits) line up remarkably well with our timing of the merger as derived by the spin-up metallicity. In
Orkney & Laporte 2025

The GCs coincident with the time of merger and thus starburst are the following: NGC 5927, NGC 6304, NGC 6352, NGC 6366, NGC 6496, NGC 6624, NGC 6637, NGC 6652, and NGC 6838. We observe a broad range of orbital circularities for these GCs (see Fig

Orkney et al. 2026

involving star-forming gas with a wide range of kinematics with
-0.3<\eta<1.0

Orkney & Laporte 2025

4
discussion

Our estimates only considered GC data from
VandenBerg et al. 2013
. We deliberately did not use or mix data from other studies
(
Forbes & Bridges 2010
;
Dotter et al. 2011
;
Kruijssen et al. 2019
, e.g.)
for a number of reasons which we list below. The data in
Forbes & Bridges 2010
are a compilation of GC ages derived by different studies with different methodologies. As such, it does not represent a harmonised dataset. The ages in
Kruijssen et al. 2019
are the averaged values from several different studies. However, we argue that this naturally introduces biases because the source measurements were not derived using similar methodologies. This leaves us with the ages derived by
VandenBerg et al. 2013
, which to this day remains the largest dataset with ages derived in a systematic consistent manner containing 55 clusters. Furthermore, many of the GCs in
Dotter et al. 2010
;
Dotter et al. 2011
are also contained in the
(
VandenBerg et al. 2013
)
catalog.

Inspecting the
Pace 2025
local volume database, we noticed that most GCs with metallicities in the range
-0.8<\rm{[Fe/H]}<0.0
are already included in the
VandenBerg et al. 2013
catalog, with a few exceptions (E3, IC 4499, NGC 6342, NGC 6388, NGC 6441, NGC 6528, NGC 6553). Interestingly, 5 of these belong to the inner-region of the MW (NGC 6342, NGC 6388, NGC 6441, NGC 6528 and NGC 6553) and 3 share similar ages to the “Tainá” GCs. These are NGC 6441
(
Dotter et al. 2011
,
\tau=11.26\pm 0.90\,\rm{Gyr}
,)
, NGC 6528 and NGC 6553
(
Ortolani et al. 2025
, both with
\tau=11.0\pm 0.5\,\rm{Gyr}
,)
. If we were to consider these as additional in-situ GCs, this would imply that the majority of the MW’s surviving in-situ clusters were formed at a redshift of
z=2.5
. The tight age–metallicity sequence seen in these GCs is not a mere artifact; it is naturally expected for GCs that are born from a starburst. Moreover, the wide range of orbital circularities spanned by the GCs (
0\leq\eta\leq 1
) is consistent with predictions from numerical galaxy formation models of merger induced star formation
(
Orkney & Laporte 2025
)
.

4.1
Kraken/Heracles?

Our method has solely focused on the last significant merger, which we have associated with the GSE (which is the only ancient accretion event reported which has an unambiguous GC-stellar debris association

6

6

(

.
), however, we showed in
Orkney et al. 2022
that it should be possible to disentangle even earlier merger through starburst signatures (which could be accompanied by the formation of GCs) in order to constrain the existence of the putative Kraken/Heracles merger
(

;
Massari et al. 2019
;
Horta et al. 2021

We do not attempt this here, as the LAMOST subgiant sample of stars does not probe well enough the inner-most regions of the MW. This may be probed in the future with surveys like 4MOST
(
Chiappini et al. 2019

Recently,
Massari et al. 2026
reported the existence of three separate GC sequences belonging to the in-situ Galaxy, the GSE and possibly the Kraken merger. However, their result rests entirely on postulating that NGC 6121 (M4) belongs to the Kraken galaxy, i.e. making it ex-situ. This scenario at face value, may seem plausible when considering solely the age and metallicity of this GC, but less so when accounting for its orbit (or internal chemistry). NGC 6121 is the most metal-rich GC that
Massari et al. 2026
associate with Kraken, meaning it formed in the chemically enriched centre of its progenitor’s potential well. As Kraken accreted onto the ancient MW, its orbit decayed to lower energies due to dynamical friction, accompanied by the tidal stripping of stars and GCs. The tightly-bound central regions of Kraken would have been among the last ones to get stripped. NGC 6121 should therefore reside at the lowest orbital energies within the Kraken debris footprint. However, NGC 6121 occupies the highest energies among the Kraken GC candidates, so this scenario is not physically viable from a dynamical standpoint.

Aside from NGC 6121’s derived age, there is also a chemical argument to be made about its nature.
Carretta et al. 2013
report that NGC 6121 has a metallicity of
[\rm{Fe/H}]=-1.17
, a magnesium abundance of
[\rm{Mg/Fe}]=0.55\pm 0.01
and aluminium abundance of
[\rm{Al/Fe}]=0.58\pm 0.03

4.2
\omega
Cen and its link to GSE through 1:1 bar resonance

Finally, we note that
\omega
Cen’s most metal-rich stellar population
(
Clontz et al. 2024
)
shares the same age as the four most metal-rich GCs of the GSE (see Figure
1
). This signals that
\omega
Cen may very well be the nuclear star cluster of the GSE, as it seems highly unlikely that two separate galaxies with similar chemical evolutionary tracks could merge with the MW at the exact same time. This idea is not new
(
Freeman & Bland-Hawthorn 2002
, see)
. While a MW-like galaxy under the LCDM paradigm is predicted to be formed from 1 to 3 major building blocks
(
Robertson et al. 2005
;
Fattahi et al. 2020
)
, it would be difficult for ancient GCs (i.e. those that formed before the accretion of the GSE) to have retained their skewed orbital circularity distribution (
\eta>0
) if they had been bombarded by 3 relatively significant mergers in the first 3 Gyrs of the MW’s history. We thus argue that
\omega
Cen is most likely the nuclear star cluster of the GSE
(
Limberg et al. 2022
)
.

A common point of contention regarding association of
\omega
Cen to the GSE is that it is on a retrograde orbit in the MW, whereas most of the GSE debris is close to no net rotation. This has led some studies to suggest it may be related to another substructure, the
Sequoia

(
Myeong et al. 2019
)
. Whether the
Sequoia
is linked to the GSE event, or is its own independent substructure, is also not well established. A galaxy the mass of the GSE in the range
3\cdot 10^{8}<M/\rm{M_{\odot}}<1\cdot 10^{9}

(see
Belokurov et al. 2018
;
Helmi et al. 2018
;
Lane et al. 2023
, for various mass estimates)
, would have started out with a chemical gradient and dispersed its debris in non-trivial ways in the stellar halo
(
Jean-Baptiste et al. 2017
)
occupying a wide portion
E-L_{z}
space. In fact,
Koppelman et al. 2020
and
Amarante et al. 2020
present idealised N-body and hydrodynamical N-body models which show that
Sequoia
could indeed form the metal-poor tail of the GSE’s outer structure. The same has been shown also in cosmological simulations
(
Orkney et al. 2023
, e.g.)
.
Pagnini et al. 2025
recently identified a number of MW GCs to share chemical similarities with
\omega
Cen and proposed the existence of a common link between them through an ancient dwarf progenitor
Nephele
. The authors do not claim this galaxy to necessarily be of separate origin to the GSE. However, given the argument we presented in
4
, it becomes increasingly difficult to argue for a distinct origin of
Nephele
as two (or three if counting
Sequoia
) galaxies of similar masses to the GSE could have contributed to the stellar halo at the
same
time.

We argue that the constraints on the mass of the stellar halo
Deason et al. 2019
, ages of the stellar populations in GCs
(
VandenBerg et al. 2013
)
, the most metal-rich GSE GCs,
\omega
Cen
(
Clontz et al. 2024
)
and a LCDM prior
(
Robertson et al. 2005
)
, strongly disfavour the “unique” nature of
Sequoia
,
Nephele
and other debris sharing very similar properties to the GSE
(
Amarante et al. 2020
, see the discussions in )
. From a purely chemical perspective,
Souza et al. 2026
makes the clearest chemical argument for this.

As such, Occam’s razor postulates that the most significant merger was the GSE (see
4
and
3
), its dispersal across
E-L_{z}
was messy
(
Koppelman et al. 2020
;
Amarante et al. 2020
)
and
\omega
Cen must have been its nuclear star cluster
(see
Freeman & Bland-Hawthorn 2002
;
Limberg et al. 2022
, and the age–metallicity constraint from Fig.
4
)
. In this scenario the retrograde nature of the
Sequoia
is just a reflection of stripping of outermost material prior to radialisation of the Sausage
(
Amorisco 2017
)
.

This leaves us, however, with an apparent dynamical puzzle. How did
\omega
Cen lose enough energy to fall at lower depths than the majority of the GSE debris and why is it still on a retrograde orbit? It is not clear how dynamical friction (DF) alone, would work to this end, given DF in a MW-like potential does not circularise orbits but rather radialises
(
van den Bosch et al. 1999
;
Amorisco 2017
;
Vasiliev et al. 2022
, e.g.)
, so at face value
\omega
Cen ought to be at lower energies but with a near-zero angular momentum
(
Freeman & Bland-Hawthorn 2002
)
. Therefore, a mechanism beyond DF must be invoked. One class of dynamical mechanisms capable of simultaneously changing the energy and angular momentum of a halo star (or any collisionless tracers, i.e. GCs) are bar-driven resonances
Dillamore et al. 2022
;
Dillamore et al. 2023
. Using cosmological simulations,
Tomlinson et al. 2026
shows that the 1:1 resonance can take a star on slightly prograde orbit at high energy down to a retrograde one in the span of
1\,\rm{Gyr}
(see their Fig. 6) with changes in energy and angular momentum similar as those required to shift a tracer along the locus connecting the central GSE debris
(
Belokurov et al. 2023
)
to
\omega
Cen by
\Delta E\sim 5\cdot 10^{4}\,\rm{km^{2}\,s^{-2}}
and
\Delta L_{\rm{z}}\sim 500\rm{km\cdot s^{-1}\,kpc}
. We propose that this effect may have acted on
\omega
Cen to bring it at its current location in
E-L_{z}
space with respect to the rest of the GSE debris. Thus, it may not be inconceivable that such a mechanism could have operated on
\omega
Cen at some point during the remaining
11\,\rm{Gyr}
of its existence after the bar formed and evolved in the last
10-8\,\rm{Gyr}

(
Sanders et al. 2024
;
Haywood et al. 2024
)
during which the pattern speed may have changed.

Particularly,
Tomlinson et al. 2026
predict that such a resonance in the stellar halo in
E-L_{\rm{z}}
would appear as a ridge which would be chemically more metal-rich than its surroundings.
Belokurov & Kravtsov 2022
show that on average field halo and in-situ component of the MW separate rather well at metallicities
-1.7<{\rm{[Fe/H]}}<-0.5

4.3
Timestamps in the formation of the early Milky Way

In summary, both field stars and GCs paint a precise timeline for the formation of the proto-MW: high-
\alpha
disc formation at
5<z<2.5
, the merger with the GSE at 11 Gyrs ago or
z\sim 2.5
, and then the subsequent starbursts in the proto-MW and GSE progenitor galaxies. The merger also led to the formation of the
Splash

(
Di Matteo et al. 2019
;
Belokurov et al. 2020
)
, in which ancient proto-disc stars were liberated into the stellar halo or lost angular momentum. The abrupt drop in density of stars with metallicities in the range
-1<\rm{[Fe/H]}<-0.5
with ages younger than
\tau=10\,\rm{Gyr}
is a clear testament that the MW disc had a very quiescent later growth stage, only partially modulated by later accretion events such as the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy
(
Ibata et al. 1994
)
and the Large Magellanic Cloud
(
Weinberg & Blitz 2006
;
Laporte et al. 2018
, e.g.)
.

Furthermore, our timing of the last significant merger (
\tau_{\rm merger}\simeq\tau_{\text{Tain\'{a}}}=11.2\pm 0.1\,\rm{Gyr}
) consistently fits within the series of events which should have followed in the formation of the MW
\tau_{\rm disc\,formation}<\tau_{\rm merger}<\tau_{\rm\rm bar\,formation}
, whether one chooses
\tau_{\rm disc\,formation}\sim\tau_{\rm NGC\,6652}
as we have argued
(
Belokurov & Kravtsov 2024
, see also)
or take the estimate from Mira variables placing disc formation around
z\sim 3

(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. Indeed, bar formation is situated around
\tau_{\rm{bar\,formation}}\gtrsim 8\,\rm{Gyr}

(
Sanders et al. 2024
;
Haywood et al. 2024
)
.

4.4
Outlook

Although our technique is currently only applicable in the MW where we have exquisite 6D data, it could equally be extended to the extra-galactic realm in the not too distant future (e.g. with M31). Interestingly, it has recently been argued that Andromeda underwent a major merger some 2–3 Gyrs ago with an ancient galaxy
(
Hammer et al. 2018
;
D’Souza & Bell 2018
)
. Provided age estimates of GCs in M31 improve in the next years (e.g. with JWST), our method could presumably detect/infer even earlier mergers in Andromeda’s history going as far back as cosmic noon.

5
conclusions

In this contribution, we developed a novel method to probe the time of the last significant merger exploiting the tight link which should exist on the scales of MW-mass galaxies in
\Lambda
CDM between the circularity versus metallicity relation together with the age-metallicity relation of the stellar halo. We tested its efficacy on fully cosmological simulations of Milky Way-like galaxy formation and applied it on field stars and globular clusters (GCs) of the MW to show that:

1.

The last significant merger (GSE), happened
11.2\pm 0.1\,\rm{Gyr}
ago (see Fig.
3
). This is corroborated by two independent probes: field stars and a population of starburst globular clusters.

2.

This resulted in a starburst population of field stars and GCs (
Tainá
) : NGC 5927, NGC 6304, NGC 6352, NGC
6366, NGC 6496, NGC 6624, NGC 6637, NGC 6652, NGC
6838, NGC 6441, NGC 6528
and NGC 6553.

3.

During its last plunge towards the MW, the GSE underwent a simultaneous starburst at
\tau_{\rm GSE,\,starburst}=10.9\pm 0.1\,\rm{Gyr}
(see Fig.
3
). These GCs (NGC 362, NGC 1261, NGC 1851, NGC 2808) share similar ages and metallicities (
[\rm Fe/H]\sim-1.3
) which also coincide with the most metal-rich stellar population of
\omega
Cen (see Fig.
1
). We interpret this as a close chemical evolutionary link and association of
\omega
Cen with it being the nuclear star cluster of the GSE.

4.

The present-day orbital circularity distribution of field stars and GCs as a function of metallicity sets an upper-limit on the time of formation of the proto-disc and build-up of angular momentum prior to the GSE merger. This is situated at a time when the MW-progenitor reached a metallicity of
[\rm Fe/H]\sim-1.33
which NGC 6652 anchors at
\tau=13.0\pm 0.5\,\rm{Gyr}
(see Fig.
2
), which at face value would place this around a redshift of
z_{\rm disc\,form}\gtrsim 4
.

5.

We argue that
\omega
Cen is the nuclear star cluster of the last significant merger progenitor galaxy (which we identify as the GSE). This is corroborated by the fact that the GSE GCs age–metallicity relation directly follows that of
\omega
Cen’s stellar populations
(
Clontz et al. 2024
, see our Fig.
1
, left panel)
. Its location in the
E-L_{z}
may indicate the effect of a possible 1:1 resonance with the bar which may have acted on it somewhere in the last
11\,\rm{Gyr}
.

6.

We present tentative evidence of a metal-poor ridge of field stars in the stellar halo in
E-L_{z}
connecting the GSE debris to lower energies retrograde orbits close to the vicinity of
\omega
Cen (Fig.
5
). This indicates that it may have been affected by a bar-resonance
(
Tomlinson et al. 2026
)
.
