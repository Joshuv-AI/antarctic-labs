Association between violence from patients and caregivers and nursing work interruptions, burnout and quality of nursing care: a cross-sectional study in a South Korean tertiary hospital
Authors: Minju Lee, Mi Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo examine the associations between violence perpetrated by patients and caregivers and nursing work interruptions, burnout and quality of nursing care, and to explore potential mediation pathways among these variables.DesignA descriptive, cross-sectional correlational study using path analysis.SettingA tertiary university-affiliated hospital in South Korea.ParticipantsA convenience sample of 200 registered nurses providing direct patient care, recruited between September and October 2024.Main outcome measuresPatient and caregiver violence, nursing work interruptions, burnout and perceived quality of nursing care.ResultsThe average level of violence perpetrated by patients and caregivers was moderate, with verbal abuse being the most frequently reported type (mean=2.58 on a 5-point scale). Violence from patients and caregivers was significantly positively correlated with work interruptions (r=0.50, p<0.001) and burnout (r=0.32, p<0.001) and negatively correlated with quality of nursing care (r = –0.18, p=0.012). Path analysis indicated that violence from patients and caregivers was significantly associated with increased work interruptions (β=0.50, p<0.001) and burnout (β=0.22, p=0.003), while burnout was negatively associated with quality of nursing care (β = –0.37, p<0.001). Mediation analysis showed that burnout had a significant indirect association with the relationship between violence and quality of care (indirect effect = –0.054, p=0.001). A sequential indirect pathway from violence through work interruptions and burnout to quality of care was also statistically significant (indirect effect = –0.024, p=0.010).ConclusionsViolence perpetrated by patients and caregivers is associated with reduced quality of nursing care through a sequential process involving increased work interruptions and burnout. These findings highlight the importance of addressing both workflow disruptions and nurses’ psychological well-being through systemic interventions aimed at improving care quality.Keywords: Burnout, Professional; Nursing Care; Cross-Sectional Studies; Workplace

Introduction

Occupational violence—defined as verbal abuse, threats, physical aggression or assault occurring in the course of employment—is a growing global concern among healthcare workers.1 2 Between 8% and 38% of healthcare professionals have reported experiencing physical violence, and many more experience verbal abuse or intimidation, most often from patients or visitors.3 4 Nurses are particularly vulnerable because of their continuous patient contact and relational proximity to families and caregivers.5–7

In the USA, about 80% of nurses have experienced workplace violence,8 while in South Korea, 67.6% have reported verbal abuse and 25.2% have experienced physical aggression.9 Hospital violence includes any act of physical, psychological or sexual harm—ranging from verbal abuse to physical assault—that occurs in the workplace.10 11 Verbal abuse is the most common form and often stems from patients’ or caregivers’ frustration or emotional distress.12 13 Despite Korean governmental efforts, including the Manual for the Prevention and Response to Human Rights Violations in Medical Institutions (2018), violence in healthcare settings remains widespread.14

Work interruptions, defined as unexpected disruptions in task continuity caused by environmental or interpersonal factors,15 also pose a major threat to nursing performance. Nurses are interrupted approximately every 6 min, primarily because of communication demands or sudden requests from patients and caregivers.16 Unlike Western hospitals, where caregiver presence is relatively limited, Korean hospitals commonly allow 24-hour caregiver access, intensifying nurses’ emotional workload. Such interruptions not only fragment workflow but also heighten stress, leading to errors, emotional overreactions and reduced performance.17–19

In many healthcare systems, particularly in East Asia, nurses act as primary mediators between patients, families and institutional structures. This triadic dynamic fosters frequent interpersonal tension, occupational stress and conflict. Both internal and external violence heighten psychological distress, contributing to depression, post-traumatic stress and burnout.20 21 These experiences erode professional identity, increase turnover intention and ultimately compromise the quality of care.13 22

Burnout—characterised by emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation and reduced professional accomplishment—is a chronic response to occupational stress.23 24 In nursing, burnout not only reflects psychological fatigue but also represents a rupture in the caring relationship central to the discipline. Watson’s Theory of Human Caring posits that transpersonal relationships preserve human dignity and healing; repeated exposure to violence disrupts this foundation, leading to moral distress and compassion fatigue.25 26 Consequently, burnout undermines motivation, patient-centredness and safety, necessitating preventive strategies at both individual and organisational levels.22

Prior research indicates that violence27 28 and work interruptions19 contribute to burnout, which in turn reduces the quality of nursing care.4 29 Compromised care may result in inadequate treatment, delayed recovery and reduced patient satisfaction.30 Although previous studies have examined verbal abuse and task interruptions separately, few have explored how these stressors interact. Violence and excessive demands from patients and caregivers may simultaneously increase interruptions, leading to burnout and reduced quality of care.13 19 20 29 31 32 However, research in South Korea remains limited, particularly regarding the combined effects of violence and workflow disruption on nurses’ well-being and care outcomes.

Accordingly, this study aimed to examine the relationships among violence perpetrated by patients and caregivers, nursing work interruptions, burnout and quality of nursing care. By focusing on the Korean healthcare context—where family involvement is intensive and institutional protection remains limited—this study offers insights applicable to other healthcare systems experiencing similar structural challenges.

Conceptual model

This study examined the relationships among patient and caregiver violence, nursing work interruptions, burnout and quality of nursing care. The conceptual framework was developed by integrating existing empirical evidence with theoretical perspectives relevant to occupational stress in nursing practice.

Previous research has identified both workplace violence and work interruptions as key stressors in clinical settings. Exposure to violence from patients and caregivers has been associated with increased psychological distress, emotional exhaustion and diminished professional functioning among nurses.13 20 21 33 34 Likewise, frequent and unpredictable interruptions—often arising from patient or caregiver demands—can disrupt task continuity, increase cognitive workload and contribute to cumulative stress. 17 19 31 32 35 36 In addition, frequent work interruptions may compromise the quality and safety of nursing care through increased errors, delayed responses and reduced task performance.17–19 31 32 These factors have also been linked to higher levels of burnout, which in turn has been consistently associated with poorer quality of nursing care.4 22 29 30 37

Although work interruptions and burnout are closely related, they reflect different aspects of the work experience. Work interruptions occur at the task level and represent immediate disruptions in ongoing clinical activities, whereas burnout develops over time as a response to sustained exposure to occupational stressors. From this perspective, repeated interruptions in the work process may contribute to the development of burnout rather than functioning as an independent phenomenon.

Taken together, these findings suggest that patient and caregiver violence may be associated with the quality of nursing care through both work-related and psychological pathways. In particular, violence may be linked to increased work interruptions, which may contribute to burnout and subsequently to lower perceived quality of nursing care. However, given the cross-sectional nature of the data, the direction of these relationships should be interpreted with caution. Based on this framework, the present study aimed to examine the relationships among patient and caregiver violence, nursing work interruptions, burnout and quality of nursing care, and to explore potential mediation pathways among these variables. From a theoretical perspective, the proposed sequential ordering between work interruptions and burnout reflects their temporal and functional distinctions. Work interruptions represent immediate disruptions in task execution, whereas burnout reflects a cumulative psychological response to sustained occupational stress. Accordingly, interruptions may function as proximal stressors that contribute to the development of burnout over time. Although alternative configurations, such as parallel mediation or different directional orderings, are also plausible—particularly given the cross-sectional design—the current model was specified based on this theoretically grounded temporal progression.

The following research questions were addressed:

Q1. Is patient and caregiver violence associated with nursing work interruptions and burnout?

Q2. Are nursing work interruptions associated with burnout?

Q3. Are nursing work interruptions associated with the quality of nursing care?

Q4. Is burnout associated with the perceived quality of nursing care?

Q5. Is there evidence of an indirect association between patient and caregiver violence and quality of nursing care through burnout?

Q6. Is there evidence of a sequential indirect pathway from patient and caregiver violence through nursing work interruptions and burnout to quality of nursing care?

Methods

Design

This descriptive correlational study examined the relationships between patient and caregiver violence, nursing work interruptions, burnout and quality of nursing care, and explored the mediating roles of work interruptions and burnout using path analysis. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) guidelines (see online supplemental appendix 1 for details).

Participants

Participants were registered nurses from a tertiary university-affiliated hospital in South Korea who provided direct patient care and voluntarily consented to participate. Nurses in managerial or administrative positions without direct patient care responsibilities were excluded.

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power V.3.1.9.4.38 For correlational analysis with a medium effect size (f² = 0.50),39 a significance level (α) of 0.05 and statistical power (1−β) of 0.90, the minimum required sample size was 176. In addition, because the present study employed path analysis within a structural equation modelling (SEM) framework, recommendations regarding SEM sample size adequacy were also considered. Previous methodological studies have suggested that a sample size between 150 and 400 is generally acceptable for SEM analyses, depending on model complexity.40 Therefore, a target sample size of approximately 200 participants was considered appropriate to ensure adequate statistical power and model stability for the path analysis conducted in this study.

To account for a potential 5–10% attrition rate, 220 questionnaires were distributed. Of the 207 returned (response rate=94%), 200 were retained for analysis after excluding incomplete or invalid responses.

Measurements

All instruments were used with permission from the original or adapting authors.

Demographic and job-related characteristics

Demographic characteristics included gender, age, marital status, religion and educational level. Job-related variables included total clinical experience, tenure in the current department, department type and work shift pattern.

Patient and caregiver violence

Violence from patients and caregivers was measured using a tool developed by Son41 and adapted for nurses by Ha.42 The instrument comprises 16 items across three subdomains: verbal abuse (4 items), physical threats (5 items) and physical violence (7 items). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘never’) to 5 (‘very frequently’), with higher scores indicating more frequent experiences of violence. Cronbach’s α was 0.89 in Ha’s study42 and 0.94 in the present study.

Nursing work interruptions

Nursing work interruptions were assessed using a tool originally developed by Yu32 and revised by the present researcher in consultation with a nursing professor. The term ‘nursing work interruption’ was refined to specify interruptions arising from unreasonable demands and violence from patients or caregivers. The revised items were reviewed for clarity by seven nurses with more than 5 years of clinical experience. The final instrument included 12 items across two subdomains: human factors (6 items) and environmental factors (6 items). Each item was rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘rarely’) to 6 (‘more than five times per day’). Higher scores indicate more frequent interruptions. Cronbach’s α was 0.88 in the original study32 and 0.93 in the present study.

Burnout

Burnout was measured using the Maslach Burnout Inventory, adapted for Korean nurses by Ha.42 The scale consists of 22 items across three subdomains: emotional exhaustion (9 items), reduced personal accomplishment (8 items) and depersonalisation (5 items). Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (‘never’) to 6 (‘every day’). Items measuring personal accomplishment (items 10–17) were reverse-coded. Cronbach’s α values in Ha’s study42 were 0.84 (emotional exhaustion), 0.86 (personal accomplishment) and 0.70 (depersonalisation). In the present study, the corresponding α values were 0.94, 0.94 and 0.86, respectively, with an overall reliability coefficient of 0.92.

Quality of nursing care

Quality of nursing care was measured using four items from the Nurse-Reported Quality of Care Scale developed by Havaei et al43 and translated by Ku and Yu.39 Each item was rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘very poor’) to 4 (‘very good’), with higher scores indicating higher perceived care quality. Cronbach’s α was 0.80 in the original study,43 0.87 in Ku and Yu’s study39 and 0.86 in the present study.

Data collection and ethical considerations

Data were collected between September and October 2024 after obtaining approval from the Institutional Review Board of University Hospital and permission from the nursing department. The researcher visited the hospital, explained the study objectives and procedures and distributed self-administered questionnaires to nurses who voluntarily agreed to participate. Completion of each questionnaire took approximately 15 min. To ensure anonymity, participants sealed completed questionnaires in envelopes, which were collected directly by the researcher.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants received written and verbal explanations regarding the study’s purpose, procedures, confidentiality and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All data were anonymised, encrypted, used solely for research purposes and securely stored for 3 years before destruction.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows V.27.0 and IBM SPSS Amos V.18.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means and SDs, were used to summarise participants’ characteristics and key study variables. Differences according to general and job-related characteristics were examined using independent t-tests and one-way analysis of variance with Scheffé post hoc comparisons. For groups with fewer than 30 cases or non-normal distributions, non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis) were applied with appropriate post hoc analyses.

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine relationships among continuous variables, and data normality was assessed using standardised skewness and kurtosis values. Path analysis was performed using the maximum likelihood estimation method to test the hypothesised relationships among patient and caregiver violence, nursing work interruptions, burnout and quality of nursing care. Model fit for the modified model was evaluated using multiple fit indices, including χ² (p), df, χ²/df, Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Normed Fit Index (NFI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Root Mean Square Residual (RMR) and Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI).

Indirect effects (potential mediation effects) were examined by estimating total, direct, and indirect effects within the structural model. The statistical significance of indirect effects was assessed using bootstrapping procedures (5000 resamples) with 95% CIs, and effects were considered statistically significant when the CI did not include zero.

Results

Demographic and job-related characteristics of participants

Among the 200 participants, the majority were female (95.5%), with a mean age of 28.7±3.1 years. Most participants were single (78.5%) and held a bachelor’s degree (76.0%).

The mean total clinical experience was 5.75±3.00 years, and the mean tenure in the current department was 2.36±1.93 years. Participants were distributed across various departments, with the largest proportion working in medical wards (39.5%). Most participants (90.5%) worked rotating shifts, including night duty. Correlations between variables

Patient and caregiver violence showed a moderate positive correlation with nursing work interruptions (r=0.50) and a weak-to-moderate positive correlation with burnout (r=0.32). It was weakly negatively correlated with the quality of nursing care (r = –0.18).

Nursing work interruptions were weak-to-moderate positively correlated with burnout (r=0.31) and weakly negatively correlated with quality of nursing care (r = –0.21). Burnout demonstrated a moderate negative correlation with the quality of nursing care (r = –0.40).

These correlations are presented to describe the overall pattern of relationships among the study variables (table 3). Given the number of correlations examined, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and greater emphasis should be placed on the magnitude of associations rather than on statistical significance alone.

Path analysis of patient and caregiver violence, nursing work interruptions, burnout and quality of nursing care

To examine the relationships among patient and caregiver violence, nursing work interruptions, burnout and quality of nursing care, a path analysis was conducted based on the initial conceptual model derived from the theoretical framework (figure 1). The initial model included all theoretically plausible direct and indirect paths among the study variables.

Following evaluation of the initial conceptual model, a sequential mediation model was additionally evaluated to determine whether a more parsimonious and theoretically coherent structure could provide improved model fit and interpretability. In this process, the non-significant direct paths from patient and caregiver violence to quality of nursing care and from nursing work interruptions to quality of nursing care were removed.

The final model (figure 2) demonstrated excellent fit to the data (χ² = 1.84, p=0.398; χ²/df=0.92; CFI=1.000; TLI=1.000; NFI=0.985; RMSEA=0.000; RMR=0.015; GFI=0.995), all of which indicated excellent model fit according to conventional criteria. In the final model, patient and caregiver violence was positively associated with nursing work interruptions (B=0.73, β=0.50, p<0.001) and burnout (B=0.30, β=0.22, p=0.003). Nursing work interruptions were also positively associated with burnout (B=0.18, β=0.20, p=0.008). Burnout, in turn, was negatively associated with quality of nursing care (B=−0.18, β = –0.37, p<0.001) (table 4).

Discussion

This study identified a statistically significant sequential pattern of associations consistent with the proposed theoretical model, in which patient and caregiver violence was associated with increased nursing work interruptions and burnout, and these factors were associated with lower perceived quality of nursing care. Notably, this study is among the first to empirically demonstrate that violence and harassment from patients and caregivers can undermine the quality of nursing care through both work-related and psychological processes. This finding highlights that interpersonal aggression in clinical settings is not merely a psychosocial burden for nurses but also a determinant of care outcomes, underscoring the need for system-level prevention and support strategies.

The average score for nurses’ experiences of patient and caregiver violence over the past year was 2.15 out of 5, indicating that such incidents occurred occasionally. However, a closer examination revealed that verbal abuse was particularly prevalent (mean=2.98), followed by physical threats (2.27) and physical violence (1.58). These results are consistent with prior studies identifying verbal aggression as the most common yet under-recognised form of workplace violence in Korea.44 45 Despite national efforts—including the 2018 Manual for the Prevention and Response to Human Rights Violations in Medical Institutions—the problem persists. A 2021 national survey found that 67.6% of nurses had experienced workplace violence,9 while Park et al8 reported a prevalence of 71.7% among nurses in acute care hospitals. Alarmingly, only 16.3% of respondents considered their institutions’ responses effective, highlighting the gap between policy intent and implementation. These findings suggest that existing countermeasures remain inadequate and that practical implementation strategies, real-time reporting systems and psychological support services should be strengthened.

This study also identified departmental differences in perceived quality of nursing care. Nurses working in emergency departments reported the lowest care quality scores, whereas those working in intensive care units, surgical wards and integrated nursing care service wards reported higher levels. These findings mirror previous evidence showing that emergency nurses experience the highest levels of occupational stress, which negatively affect care performance and patient safety.46 Elevated job stress has also been associated with lower quality of care and reduced patient satisfaction.37 Therefore, targeted interventions for high-stress departments—such as appropriate staffing ratios, structured rest schedules and proactive mental health support—are warranted.

In this study, quality of nursing care was measured using nurses’ self-reported perceptions rather than objective patient outcomes. However, previous international studies, including those by Aiken et al,47 have shown that nurse-reported quality of care is significantly associated with patient safety, mortality and broader healthcare quality outcomes. Similarly, Ku and Yu39 reported that nurse-reported quality of care was associated with nursing productivity, organisational health and patient safety-related outcomes, supporting its usefulness as a meaningful proxy indicator in nursing research. Nevertheless, because nurses experiencing burnout or workplace violence may perceive care quality more negatively regardless of actual care performance, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting perceived rather than objectively verified quality of nursing care.

Path analysis showed that patient and caregiver violence was significantly associated with nursing work interruptions (β=0.50), consistent with earlier reports that aggressive or excessive demands from patients and caregivers disrupt nurses’ task continuity.20 32 Repeated interruptions fragment workflow, increase cognitive load and hinder concentration, creating chaotic and emotionally draining work environments. Nursing work interruptions were also found to be significantly associated with higher levels of burnout (β=0.20). This association aligns with prior conceptual analyses describing interruptions as chronic stressors that accumulate over time, leading to emotional exhaustion and decreased engagement.35 Rhu and Lee19 similarly found that frequent task interruptions evoke feelings of helplessness, frustration and fatigue—core components of burnout. In high-acuity settings, such disruptions often become normalised, fostering emotional disengagement and reduced motivation. Organisational strategies to mitigate these effects should include workflow standardisation, deployment of non-clinical support personnel and allocation of protected time for uninterrupted clinical tasks.

Burnout was significantly negatively associated with the quality of nursing care (β = –0.37). These findings reinforce evidence linking burnout to clinical errors, including patient falls and medication errors.19 48 Emotional exhaustion may lead nurses to disengage from patient-centred interactions or perform tasks mechanically, thereby weakening therapeutic relationships. Maslach and Jackson49 described how repeated exposure to violent or demanding work environments erodes emotional energy and professional fulfilment, leading to depersonalisation and moral fatigue. Such attitudinal deterioration has profound implications for patient safety and satisfaction.

International evidence also supports this mechanism. Poghosyan et al,29 in a multicountry study involving more than 53 000 nurses, found that depersonalisation reduced care quality by approximately 7%, while Alshehry et al37 demonstrated that uncivil behaviours from patients and family members undermined nurses’ emotional stability and self-esteem, resulting in suboptimal care. Collectively, these findings underscore that burnout among nurses represents a critical threat to healthcare quality and patient safety.

Mediation analysis further demonstrated that burnout serves as a key mediator in the relationship between patient and caregiver violence and quality of nursing care. Importantly, the findings also support a sequential mediation mechanism in which nursing work interruptions may function as an upstream work-related stressor associated with burnout, which in turn is associated with reduced quality of nursing care. These findings indicate that work-related disruptions are associated with greater psychological strain, which is further associated with poorer care outcomes.

Although the hypothesised sequence was grounded in theory and prior evidence, alternative or reciprocal relationships among violence exposure, work interruptions, burnout and perceived care quality may also be plausible. In addition, the non-significant direct pathways observed in the initial conceptual model may indicate that the associations of violence and work interruptions with care quality may operate primarily through indirect psychological mechanisms such as burnout. Therefore, the present model should be interpreted as a theory-guided statistical representation of cross-sectional associations rather than a confirmed causal process. Future longitudinal research is needed to examine competing models and clarify the temporal ordering among these variables.

This sequential mediation pathway suggests that interventions focusing solely on external stressors may be insufficient unless they are accompanied by psychological support systems that enhance nurses’ resilience. Recommended strategies include regular emotional debriefing, routine psychological screening and confidential access to professional counselling. Institutional investment in nurses’ well-being is not only ethically imperative but also strategically essential for maintaining consistent, high-quality care.

Although previous studies have explored violence and burnout independently, research addressing nursing work interruptions remains limited, particularly within the Korean healthcare context. Future studies should identify situational, organisational and cultural factors contributing to interruptions and examine their cumulative impact on patient safety. Hospital-level interventions may include visual cues such as ‘medication safety vests’, structured communication systems and workflow protection strategies to reduce unnecessary interruptions from patients and caregivers.32 Improving care quality therefore requires action beyond staffing adjustments and demands a cultural shift that recognises workplace violence as a public health and safety concern rather than a private occupational issue. Comprehensive strategies should encompass violence prevention training, immediate crisis response systems and trauma-informed recovery programmes to support affected staff.

Although this study focused on South Korea, the mechanisms identified are likely applicable to other healthcare systems in which nurses shoulder disproportionate emotional and operational burdens. These findings provide transferable insights for nurse leaders, policymakers and healthcare administrators seeking to mitigate violence and foster sustainable, safe clinical environments.

In practical terms, the findings underscore the need for proactive, system-wide strategies that simultaneously address violence prevention, nurses’ psychological well-being and workflow management. Such efforts may include de-escalation training, standardised incident response protocols, psychological support programmes, optimised staffing and systematic monitoring of workplace violence incidents. Implementing these multifaceted interventions may strengthen nurse resilience and contribute to safer, more sustainable healthcare environments.

Limitations

Although the present study reveals important findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationships among violence, work interruptions, burnout and quality of nursing care. Longitudinal or intervention studies are needed to verify the direction and temporal sequence of these relationships. Second, data were collected through self-reported questionnaires, which may involve response bias, recall error or under-reporting of violent incidents because of fear or desensitisation. Third, the sample was drawn from a single general hospital in South Korea using convenience sampling, which limits generalisability. Differences in institutional culture, staffing and policy implementation across settings may influence these associations; therefore, replication across diverse healthcare systems is recommended. Finally, although this study was conducted in a context characterised by strong family involvement in inpatient care, the identified mechanisms—particularly those related to nurses’ emotional and operational burdens—are likely relevant in other healthcare systems facing similar structural challenges, such as workforce shortages and inadequate protection against workplace violence.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that violence from patients and caregivers is associated with increased nursing work interruptions and burnout, and with lower perceived quality of nursing care. Importantly, the findings indicate that patient and caregiver violence was associated with care quality through a statistically significant sequential pathway in which work interruptions are associated with higher burnout, which in turn is associated with lower care quality. These findings highlight interpersonal aggression as a critical determinant of healthcare quality, extending workplace violence research beyond occupational safety to encompass patient safety and healthcare system performance.

The results underscore the urgent need for institutional and policy-level interventions to prevent workplace violence, protect nurses’ psychological safety and ensure continuity of care. Addressing both workflow disruptions and nurse burnout is essential for maintaining high-quality, safe and compassionate patient care. In addition, departmental variations in care quality suggest the importance of tailored staffing, resource allocation and organisational strategies. Although this study was limited by its single-institution and cross-sectional design, the identified mechanisms may be applicable to other healthcare contexts in which nurses face similar emotional and operational burdens. Future longitudinal and multicentre studies are warranted to confirm these pathways and support evidence-based improvements in nursing work environments.
