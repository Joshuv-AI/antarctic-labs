ABSTRACT
As energy demand rises, nuclear energy, particularly from reactors that use tristructural isotropic
(TRISO) fuels, has gained attention due to the fuel’s enhanced resistance to radiation damage and
high temperatures. This report investigates the modeling capabilities of the Gamma Detector
Response and Analysis Software (GADRAS) for TRISO fuels, focusing on the gamma signatures of
TRISO particles, which have not been extensively explored. Using the Monte Carlo N-Particle
(MCNP) code as a benchmark, we developed both homogeneous and heterogeneous models of
TRISO pebbles to analyze gamma spectra. Our findings reveal that the homogeneous and
heterogeneous models produced different gamma signatures. Additionally, the GADRAS
heterogeneous model significantly reduces computation times compared to MCNP, enabling
effective modeling of gamma signatures for safeguards applications. This advancement is essential
for the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) in detecting anomalies and potential smuggling
attempts in TRISO reactor fuel elements.

1. INTRODUCTION
1.1. Background
As demand for energy continues to increase, the subject of nuclear energy has become an
increasingly relevant topic, with various companies competing to create the next generation of
nuclear reactors, many of which bear little resemblance to the current fleet of reactors in use. To
power these reactors, dozens of different fuel types are being developed, with one of the most
promising being tristructural isotropic (TRISO) fuels. TRISO fuels consist of tiny particles of
uranium dioxide (UO2) or uranium carbide (UC) coated with various layers of carbon and silicon
carbide (SiC). Thousands of these particles, each roughly the size of a grain of sand, are then
suspended in a graphite matrix to form a fuel element [1].
TRISO fuel is a popular choice for next generation reactors due to its increased resistance to
radiation damage, corrosion, oxidation, and high temperature in comparison to more conventional
fuel. The multiple layers of carbon and SiC are designed to retain fission products even under the
extreme conditions observed in a nuclear accident, making TRISO fuels a safer and more robust
option for advanced reactor designs [2].
With growing interest in TRISO fuels comes the need to develop methods of simulating TRISO
measurements for safeguards applications. One of the most advanced and widely used tools for
simulating and analyzing gamma spectra is the Gamma Detector Response and Analysis Software
(GADRAS) [3] developed and maintained by Sandia National Laboratories. GADRAS has been
under active development since 1986 and is a deterministic radiation transport and detector response
code. Most computations take less than one second and produce more accurate results than other
radiation transport codes. This is due to GADRAS’s unique detector response function which
combines first principles physics interactions with empirical data for a given detector. GADRAS can
also rapidly model many radiation source materials; however, until now, GADRAS has been unable
to account for the true heterogeneous nature of TRISO fuels, forcing users to approximate TRISO
fuel elements as homogenous mixtures rather than thousands of individual particles.
Although significant literature exists on the impact various simplifications to TRISO fuels has on
criticality calculations [4,5,6,7], little has been published on their effects on the gamma spectrum,
making it unclear how homogenizing the fuel element influences the overall spectrum. With the
International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) continuing to express interest in using GADRAS as a
tool in safeguard applications and large-scale TRISO manufacturing moving closer to operation, it
was decided to expand GADRAS’s capabilities and develop its TRISO modeling abilities as part of
the DOE’s NA-241 Advanced Reactor International Safeguards Engagement (ARISE) Program.

1.2.        PBMR-400
One of the most popular reactor concepts utilizing TRISO fuels is the pebble bed reactor (PBR) – a
type of very-high-temperature reactor as defined by the Generation IV International Forum [8].
Traditionally graphite-moderated and gas cooled, pebble bed reactors diverge from more
conventional designs in that they use spherical fuel elements, referred to as pebbles. Although the
Chinese HTR-PR (High-Temperature-Reactor-Pebble-Bed-Module) is currently the only
commercially operated PBR in the world, there are numerous designs in active development, making
the PBR a strong contender to help shape the future of the nuclear industry [1,8,9,10].
Among the various PBR designs, the South African PBMR-400 has emerged as a particularly
influential concept, serving as an important benchmark in the reactor physics community.
Developed during the 2000s, the PBMR-400 has inspired numerous other PBR designs and has

The inner 5 cm of each pebble contains approximately 15,000 TRISO particles suspended in a

mm layer of particle-free graphite. Each fuel sphere has a total uranium loading of 9 grams, with an
enrichment of 9.6 wt% [1]. The individual particles are made up of 5 distinct layers (see Table 1 for
details). At the center of the particle is a fuel kernel made of UO2. Surrounding the fuel kernel is a
layer of porous carbon which acts as a buffer between recoiling fission products in the fuel and the
outer layers of the particle, as well as accommodating internal gas buildup and particle dimensional
changes. The outer layers of the particle consist of an inner layer of pyrolytic carbon, followed by a
layer of silicon carbide, all of which is surrounded by a final layer of pyrolytic carbon. The SiC layer
acts as the primary pressure vessel for the particle, in addition to being the main fission product
barrier. The layers of pyrolytic carbon are in place to provide additional diffusion barriers to fission

2. METHODS
2.1. MCNP
To assist in developing GADRAS’s ability to model TRISO fuels, the Monte Carlo N-Particle
(MCNP) code [13] was used as a baseline for comparison. MCNP, developed and maintained by Los
Alamos National Laboratory, is generally regarded as the gold standard for radiation transport codes
and has been used extensively in the modeling of TRISO fuels [1,2,4,5,6,10,11,12]. Previous work
has been done on developing a framework for coupling GADRAS’s detector response function with
MCNP’s radiation transport capabilities [14,15].
To begin, a homogenized model of a PBMR-400 pebble was first created in GADRAS, matching the
correct dimensions, isotopic ratios, and uranium loading (see Table 2). An individual TRISO particle
was also created in GADRAS. Using GADRAS’s feature to produce equivalent MCNP source terms
for spherical models, homogenous and heterogeneous models in MCNP were then created,
graphical representations of which can be seen in Figure 2, Figure 3, and Figure 4.
Two separate simulations were run in MCNP for each model – one using a photon source and
another using an electron source to capture the bremsstrahlung contribution. Simulations were run
using between 1e7 and 1e9 particles. An F1 photon tally was placed around the fuel pebble, and the
photon emissions were tallied according to GADRAS’s preferred tally bin structure. The F1 tallies
for the intrinsic gamma run and the electron-induced bremsstrahlung were combined before being
converted to GADRAS’s native .gam file format for source term specifications.
The heterogeneous MCNP models contained ~15,000 TRISO particles in a single TRISO pebble. In
the first method, a lattice structure was created such that the TRISO particles were evenly spaced
throughout the pebble. In the second, the TRISO particles were distributed randomly throughout
the interior of the TRISO pebble. To increase the computational speed of this method, the
individual TRISO particles were simplified to an inner fuel kernel and a single outer layer comprising
a mixture of the various layers of carbon and SiC, as can be seen in Figure 5. Due to MCNP’s
optimizations when using lattices, this simplification was not necessary for the first method.
Various fuel enrichments were modeled, including 3%, 5%, 9.6%, 10%, 15%, and 20%. As all
showed consistent results, an enrichment of 9.6% was selected as the primary enrichment to
examine in this report, as this is the enrichment used in the PBMR-400.

2.2.        GADRAS
The TRISO pebble was modeled both homogenously and heterogeneously within GADRAS for
comparison with the MCNP simulations. The homogenous model consisted of a single sphere with
the TRISO particles smeared throughout the pebble’s kernel, matching MCNP’s homogenous
material. The heterogeneous model was initially created with a .3dm file, consisting of ~15000
individual .1dm models each representing a single TRISO particle. As a result of this work, a TRISO
material type was implemented in GADRAS, allowing a single model to be used for the GADRAS
source term.

2.2.1. TRISO Material Specification
A TRISO material can now be specified during model construction within GADRAS (Figure 6).
First, users can create a model representing an individual TRISO particle as a single .1dm. To create
the pebble, users can create the pebble model as normal and specify the fill material for the interior
of the pebble. To add the TRISO particles to the interval, users can click on the TRISO checkbox
below the Material table and specify the TRISO particle model and the packing factor (or the
number of particles to add). The packing factor is defined as the percent of the volume of the
interval that is TRISO particles. The model can be saved as a .1dm and used as a source term in
GADRAS.

2.2.2. Spectrum Generation
Initially, the TRISO pebble was modeled as a .3dm with each of the ~15,000 TRISO particles as
individual models within the pebble’s TRISO material. Each of these particles was treated as a
GADRAS source term and meshed for its spectral impact. This resulted in a very long runtime (over
7 days) and never actually produced a suitable result. The GADRAS team then took efforts to create
a TRISO material term to speed up the calculations.
The nature of GADRAS’s 3D transport is described elsewhere [16,17], so only the modifications to
the routine are described in this report. Traditional radioactive materials are meshed adaptively to
ensure fast computation times while maintaining good accuracy. Due to the nature of TRISO pebble
materials, an adaptive mesh is not necessary and has been replaced with a point cloud with the
points located at random locations within the TRISO particles’ interval and corresponds to the
number of specified TRISO particles in the material. The location determination algorithm ensures
that individual TRISO particles do not overlap and are fully contained within the interval. The .gam
file (containing the 4-π photon emission data) associated with the TRISO particle is used as the
source term for the radiation being emitted from each location. Rays are cast from each TRISO
particle’s position to account for geometric and material attenuation effects. The spectral
contribution from each TRISO particle’s radiation emission is tallied on the detector face and put
through the detector response function to generate the full simulated spectrum. The detector in
question was a generic HPGe with an efficiency of 153%.

3. RESULTS
To ensure that GADRAS and MCNP produced consistent results, the spectrum from each code’s
homogenous model were compared, with no significant difference between the resulting spectra.
The spectra resulting from the two heterogeneous MCNP models (lattice and random placement)
were also compared, as can be seen in Figure 7. As the bremsstrahlung radiation is a secondary
effect, the electron transport was not included in this initial comparison; however, electron transport
was included in the results of Figure 8-Figure 12. The runtime for the randomly placed model was
significantly longer than the lattice structure, but there is less than 5% difference between these
spectra, primarily at low energy. The remainder of the research was done using the lattice structure
MCNP model. It should be noted, however, that because the tally was a sphere around the pebble
and not on a detector, there should be minimal impact from streaming pathways through the lattice
or other artifacts. A more realistic approach would be to tally at a specific detector location, as is
done in GADRAS.
Comparisons between heterogeneous and homogenous models revealed discrepancies in the gamma
spectra varying between 10-30%, with the error tending to decrease as energy increased. This can be
observed in Figure 7-Figure 12 and is believed to be caused by the difference in the average
attenuation encountered by photons when modeling a pebble as a homogenous mixture rather than
heterogeneously. All photons are generated within the high-density fuel element. When the pebble is
modeled homogenously, the average attenuation, particularly for particles closer to the surface of the
pebble, will be smaller than if it were modeled heterogeneously. When modeled heterogeneously,
photons will always have to travel through some amount of the dense UO2. This difference in
attenuation is likely the cause of the increased peak heights in the homogenous model. This may also
explain why the difference in peak heights decreases as energy increases, since higher energy
photons are less likely to interact in the material. The difference in continua is likely due to less
bremsstrahlung radiation being produced in the homogenous model. Electrons emitted primarily
from U-238 produce bremsstrahlung radiation very close to where they are born. As the probability
of bremsstrahlung radiation is proportional to Z2 (where Z is the atomic number), more electron
interactions in uranium should result in greater amounts of bremsstrahlung radiation than would be
seen in a homogenous mixture.
Comparisons between spectra generated from heterogeneous models in GADRAS and MCNP can
be seen in Figures 8-12. The spectra agree very well in all portions of the energy range, with some
discrepancies arising from differences in cross section data and decay data. The GADRAS
heterogeneous model can be simulated in roughly 60 seconds on a desktop computer, whereas the
MCNP model takes several hours to produce a similar result.

4. CONCLUSION
This study demonstrates that the gamma spectra produced by fresh TRISO fuel elements exhibit
significant differences when modeled as homogeneous versus heterogeneous mixtures. The
enhancements made to the GADRAS modeling capabilities now allow for the specification of a
TRISO material, enabling users to generate accurate gamma spectra for TRISO pebbles. Initial
attempts to simulate a heterogeneous TRISO pebble in GADRAS resulted in excessively long
runtimes, exceeding seven days, without yielding conclusive results. However, through the
advancements achieved in this project, the simulation time has been drastically reduced to
approximately 60 seconds, while equivalent MCNP runs require several hours to produce
comparable outcomes.

The gamma spectra generated by GADRAS and MCNP align closely across all energy regions,
validating the effectiveness of the new modeling approach. This capability is crucial for the IAEA as
it enhances their ability to accurately model the expected gamma signatures of TRISO reactor fuel
elements. Such advancements are vital for detecting anomalies and potential smuggling attempts,
thereby contributing to the safety and security of nuclear materials. Overall, this work not only
improves the efficiency of TRISO fuel modeling but also strengthens the tools available for nuclear
safeguards and regulatory oversight.
