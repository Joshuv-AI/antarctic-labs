Engineering of a wireless single capacitive discharge conductivity-clamped gene electrotransfer probe for naked nucleic acid therapeutics

Keng-Yin Lai, Stephen L Mow, Mathumathi Manoharan, Anastasia Thiessen, Georg von Jonquieres, Jeremy L Pinyon, Edward N Crawford, Gary D Housley, Amr Al Abed, Nigel H Lovell

Abstract

Pulsed electric field gene electrotransfer addresses the need for safe, directed “vector-free” delivery of DNA/mRNA therapeutics in deep tissue targets. We report the development of a disposable needle-type “electro-lens” probe for precision delivery of DNA/mRNA to tissues via translocation of nucleic acids into cells using focused single-pulse electric fields. We developed and manufactured a novel ganged linear bionic electrode array enhanced by incorporating integrated microfluidics to deliver naked DNA expression cassettes in a low-conductivity carrier (“conductivity-clamping,” CC). This system was packaged in a disposable wireless probe for single capacitive discharge conductivity-clamped gene electrotransfer (SCD-CC-GET), powered by an integrated capacitor with automated discharge mechanism. The electrode array was achieved by laser-etching parylene-insulated concentric stainless steel needles, with the lensing effect of the electric field tuned by varying the separation between elongated conductive elements. Our novel SCD-CC-GET probe is fully wireless and standalone, featuring integrated fluidics with minimal dead space, and an automated discharge mechanism. The probe was validated in vitro through HEK293 cell transfection of green fluorescent protein reporter plasmid DNA, where CC significantly expanded the transfection zone by up to 3×. In vivo proof of concept for deep tissue gene delivery by SCD-CC-GET was achieved in the mouse gastrocnemius muscle with long-term expression of mCherry reporter DNA. Our technology for GET is compact, low-cost, and fully wireless, with only a single point of insertion, and produces maximal electric fields at the target zone, allowing for more efficient gene therapies with minimal charge transfer, in a fine needle profile.

INTRODUCTION

Electrotransfer of large DNA molecules to target tissues is achieved with pulsed electric fields driving binding of the polyanionic DNA to the cathode-facing cell membrane. While electroporation arising from pulsed electric fields includes both reversible electroporation, which can be used to drive entry of small therapeutic molecules, and irreversible electroporation, which causes cell death and finds applications in the oncology field, gene electrotransfer (GET) refers to delivery to the plasma membrane of polyanionic DNA/mRNA, which codes transgenes, and is typically of MDa size—too large to directly enter porated cells. GET relies on active internalization of the DNA by endocytotic processes, which enables trafficking to the nucleus for episomal expression, while mRNA released into the cytoplasm can directly drive protein translation at the ribosomes. The threshold electric field strength (voltage differential across cellular dimensions) for GET is largely determined by the voltage in the field generated by the local current flow, and hence tissue conductivity is a significant factor in efficiency of nucleic acid molecule delivery to cell membranes. We have previously shown that use of low-conductivity carriers such as sucrose for the DNA is effective at achieving suprathreshold electric field strength (
∼ 120 V/cm) with minimal charge transfer for safe, directed gene expression in the cochlea and brain via this conductivity-clamped gene electrotransfer (CC-GET), when applied to the original “electro-lens” technology.

The efficiency of CC-GET is notable over conventional GET approaches, which have been applied across a wide range of tissue types, including muscle, cartilage, retina, lung, cochlea, and brain. Electric pulses are most commonly delivered via plate or needle electrodes arranged in parallel. Plate electrodes establish a constant electric field but are problematic for in vivo applications. Needle electrode configurations, one pole per needle, can be arranged in parallel, in a grid, triangular, or hexagonal pattern, with separation distances up to 3 mm. These arrangements are most compatible with superficial epidermal transfections relevant to DNA vaccine applications. In these “open-field” GET applications, current pulses must be sufficient to achieve supra-threshold electric field strengths to enable DNA uptake by the cells, but constrained by the potential for tissue damage via Joule heating. Typically, voltages in the range of hundreds of volts, achieved by passing hundreds of milliamps of current, are required to achieve the supra-threshold field strengths due to the distance between electrodes. With regard to pulse type, the two main protocols include exponentially decaying pulses and square-wave pulse trains. The benefits of exponentially decaying pulses are that capacitive discharge circuitry has less complexity than square-wave pulse controllers, with a fixed charge based on the set voltage. However, typically GET via pulsed electric fields uses square pulses to provide flexibility for pulse height and width and inter-pulse interval. Development of the electro-lens CC-GET technology by our group overcame the limitations of open-field electrode configurations by using a linear array-based electrode configuration (e.g., “tandem” ganged 4 + 4 anode/cathode design) to control shaped electric fields as an electro-lens [Bionic array-Directed Gene Electrotransfer (BaDGE)].

We have shown in an in vitro cell model, using reporter-encoding plasmid DNA, that BaDGE-generated electric fields adjacent to an eight-channel electrode array (derived from a cochlear implant) delineated the electro-lens—derived suprathreshold electric field, requiring minimal applied voltage/current. This was translated to an in vivo application, where BaDGE-based delivery of plasmid DNA encoding neurotrophins that stimulated regrowth of the cochlear nerve closed the neural gap with the cochlear implant bionic interface in deafened guinea pigs and cats. This was extended to proof-of-concept studies seeking to achieve precision neuromodulation of neuron subpopulations in the brain via gene augmentation relevant to treatment of focal neurological disorders such as Parkinson's disease. In those studies, CC-GET delivery of a plasmid DNA cocktail encoding a genetically encoded Ca2+ reporter and a channelrhodopsin enabled selective regulation of excitability measured by Ca2+ signal dynamics.

While initial development of the BaDGE system was performed using an eight-channel cochlear implant electrode array, we determined that the electro-lens derived suprathreshold electric field tuning could be achieved in a two-channel longitudinally extended anode and cathode configuration, which, with integrated fluidics for DNA/mRNA delivery and conductivity-clamping, would be ideal for deep tissue gene delivery targeting applications aligned with focal neurological disorders and vector-free DNA/RNA vaccine applications. This manuscript describes the biomedical engineering required to achieve this new focal gene electrotransfer technology, which addresses the limitations of current open-field electrode configurations, in which multiple invasive needle/plate electrodes are required, leading to high charge transfer requirements that impact tissue integrity and generate pain sensation. The present study reflects iterative development of a compact probe for conducting GET, with spatial control of DNA/mRNA solution dispersion relative to the pulsed electric field. This enabled a single point of insertion into the target tissue using a compact gene delivery probe with wireless charging and pulse delivery. The primary focus of this study is on the engineering of this novel nucleic acid delivery platform, centered on the development of the electro-lens technology. To this end, we report on the design and construction, with in vitro validation of GET of naked reporter DNA to adherent HEK293 cells, as well as in vivo validation of DNA delivery to mouse hindlimb skeletal muscle.

Hence, in our eventual design of the electro-lens, we advanced the concept by etching elongated electrode surfaces separated by an insulated tuning element on stainless steel (SS) concentric needles to achieve finer manufacturing tolerances for electro-lens production, facilitating ease of tissue penetration, while preserving flexibility in electrode lengths and separation distances inherent in creating the tunable focused electric field for GET. The electrical properties and suitability of SS as an electrode material instead of Pt/Ir for electro-lens production are assessed in the characterization studies.

FABRICATION OF WIRELESS CONCENTRIC NEEDLE BaDGE PROBES

Having demonstrated the feasibility of a combined fluid and electric pulse delivery probe, we moved toward improving the reliability and consistency of the design of the linear electrode array, and transitioned from the tethering external stimulator to wireless integrated capacitor charging. To achieve compactness and reduce cost, the linear electrode array changed from consisting of separate and isolated Pt/Ir tubes to being a pair of insulated concentrically aligned stainless steel (SS) needles with laser-etched electrode surfaces, which serve both functions of fluid and pulse delivery while minimizing probe diameter transitions. To achieve wireless operation, a needle hub was designed to incorporate an onboard capacitor that could be charged via contact points in a docking station, and then discharged via a switch. Such a GET probe would also be low-cost (due to the material choice and simplicity of electronics) and disposable, lending itself to applications such as naked DNA/mRNA vaccine immunotherapies in the field.

Design and construction of needle-type BaDGE GET electrode array

The novel advance in the design and construction of the linear electrode array was a technique in which the “electrodes” would be etched out from two concentric SS needles insulated from each other and attached onto either side of a printed circuit board (PCB) small enough to be integrated into a needle hub. Design and construction of internal electronics and housing for single capacitive discharge BaDGE GET probe

The combination of very high electric field compression achieved by the electro-lens along with conductivity-clamping (after Ref. ) meant that the charge transfer for suprathreshold field strengths sufficient to drive gene expression was minimal and compatible with a single pulse from a miniature capacitor. The design requirements for our new GET probe were thus (1) the ability to deliver a single exponentially decaying electric pulse via the two exposed electrodes, without the use of an external pulse generator or additional actions by the user—this would be achieved by housing a capacitor that could be charged up prior to gene delivery, and then be discharged into the tissue after gene delivery via a fully self-contained automated mechanism; (2) minimal dead space (and variance), preferably to below 50 μl, to reduce wastage of DNA/mRNA solution and ensure consistent dosage; and (3) consistent and reliable electrical discharges that are not negatively affected by the proximity of charged fluid during delivery of the solution.

The capacitor chosen for this probe was a 2.2 
μF multilayer ceramic capacitor (TDK, Japan) with a voltage rating of 450 V, the maximum voltage for this capacitor type. Typically, in the GET experiments performed with this probe, the capacitor would be charged up to 120–200 V, while the discharge time constant would depend on the resistance of the tissue and the electrode–electrolyte interface (controlled by “conductivity-clamping”). All PCBs were developed using a software called KiCad (open source), and manufactured by JLCPCB (JLCPCB, China). The components mounted on the top side of the PCB (spring and receptacle) were manufactured by MillMax (MillMax, USA). The hub pieces in which the circuitry was enclosed were designed primarily in SolidWorks (Dassault Systemes, France), and printed in 405 nm UV sensitive resin using an LCD-based SLA 3D printer, the Anycubic Photon Mono (Anycubic, China). In Secs. and , we detail the design of the discharge mechanism and associated circuitry.

Automated BaDGE single capacitive discharge mechanism

The design concept is illustrated in and . The BaDGE device consists of three main assemblies: a syringe sleeve, copper switch, and needle hub. The syringe is clipped into the sleeve, which incorporates a mechanism capable of detecting when the syringe has been fully depressed. The detection mechanism (a trigger) is situated at the base of the syringe plunger clip-on. This enables accurate detection of the end-of-stroke. The capacitor is discharged by closing the circuit when the plunger clip-on presses the trigger into the copper switch. This is visualized in . The copper switch consists of copper pads and a copper spring, which is pressed down via the trigger upon depression of the plunger. The trigger is able to travel up and down along a mechanical guideway. To implement all of the above, the entire mechanism is integrated into a custom syringe sleeve that clips onto any standard syringe, into which the hub (housing the needle electrode array and PCB) is then inserted.

Circuit design

The circuit design provides electrical connectivity between the switch assembly at the top end of the sleeve and the needle electrodes and needle hub at the lower end of the sleeve. The schematic in details all required interfaces and highlights critical connection points. The electrical connection between the upper and lower sections of the sleeve was achieved using a combination of copper tape and enameled wire. Connectivity to the PCB was established via enameled wires soldered to pogo pins, which make contact with designated PCB pads. On the other side of the PCB, the charging pins align with three contact points, allowing for safe and reliable charging of the device.

The two concentric needle electrodes must be connected separately. The outer electrode is connected via springs which snap onto the metal. The second connection from the inner needle to the PCB is made by insertion into a press-fit part (receptacle) on the PCB. Therefore, the parylene-C coating of the needle must first be removed at this connection point, prior to its connection. The final outcome can be seen in . The square pads designate where the springs are soldered. Additionally, test pads were implemented in order to monitor the circuit via an oscilloscope. On one side of the PCB, the pogo pins were pressed against the PCB surface and on the other side the charging pins were pressed against the pads. This design enabled the needle electrodes (in its hub) to remain separate from the rest of the device, which improved handling during fabrication, since the electrodes must remain sterile.

ENGINEERING CHARACTERIZATION

A number of characterization studies were conducted on the new wireless concentric SS needle GET probe to assess their operational reliability against the design criteria. The first study was to determine the amount of dead space volume within the probe, as this would affect the volume of solution required to be loaded into the syringes. Second, tests were conducted to determine the consistency of the capacitive discharges and their resulting waveforms, as an indication of the reliability of the onboard automated discharge mechanism. Finally, a study was conducted to compare the use of SS as an electrode material compared with Pt/Ir with respect to voltage losses across the electrode–electrolyte interfaces.

Electrical discharge properties

The capacitor discharge profiles upon switch closure were obtained by monitoring the voltage across the capacitor via test pads using an oscilloscope (DS1074, Rigol Technologies, China). Data were collected onto a USB storage device and subsequently processed using a Python script. This circuitry allows for the repeated charging and discharging of the capacitor across two electrodes. For each test, a 1 k 
Ω resistor coupled to the BaDGE probe electrodes was used to model resistance in tissue. For a 2.2 
μF capacitor, this equated to a theoretical 2.2 ms decay constant (
τ = RC). The capacitive discharge behavior of five switches (with n = 9, 11, 11, 11, 12 trials each) was assessed under identical test conditions, with the capacitors charged to approximately 30 V using an external power supply. The peak voltage levels were correct across all trials, and the time constants of the discharge aligned well with theoretical expectations in the majority of trials across all switches. Tiny pre-discharge bouncing was observed across switches, but this did not distort the ultimate discharge profiles. The switch press success rate was determined by evaluating the number of successful discharges over the total number of activations for each switch configuration. Overall, the average success rate in achieving a complete and clean discharge across the 54 trials from 5 different switches was 94.14%.

IN VITRO VALIDATION OF WIRELESS CONCENTRIC NEEDLE BaDGE PROBES

We validated the effectiveness of the single capacitive discharge SS concentric needle BaDGE GET probe using the in vitro HEK293 cell model with GFP reporter plasmid DNA delivery. This study considered the effect of manipulating the discharge time constant by controlling the local resistivity using a sucrose carrier (conductivity-clamping, after Ref. ). This was accomplished by adjusting the ratio of 0.9% phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and 10% sucrose within the suspension solution. Sucrose is a disaccharide that is not transported directly into cells and does not affect metabolism. μl of each solution. DISCUSSION AND FUTURE DEVELOPMENT

Device architecture and pulse delivery

A new BaDGE electro-lens GET probe was developed to provide a compact and wireless vector-free DNA delivery. This GET probe featured a linear array of two 2 mm conductive electrode surfaces at the distal ends of two concentric parylene-coated SS needles (
∼ 30 
μm internal separation) with a 1 mm insulated electro-lens element to provide a highly focused electric field for close-field GET. These dimensions were chosen to balance optimum electric field physics with the practical constraints of deep tissue targeting. Our modeling established that the predicted transfection zone was maximized with a tuning element of 1 mm compared to shorter distances, while greater distances (e.g., 4 mm) would lead to discontinuous zones resulting in poorer transfection. This total array length of 5 mm was also well aligned with in vivo validation, such as for targeting of gastrocnemius muscles or subcortical brain structures in rodent models. The practical manufacturing of the array is equally complex regardless of tuning element length; so, the technology can be easily adapted for smaller or larger targets. This indicates that the electro-lens technology is well suited for personalized nucleic acid therapeutics where gene therapy can be limited to the needed tissue target volume. For example, this is well aligned with the potential for solid tumor DNA/RNA vaccine applications.

Compared with current electrode arrangements, featuring a set of plate electrodes, or several needle electrodes, this GET probe requires only a single penetration, with the concentric needles providing both the fluid delivery and (single) pulsed electric field for GET. Hence, the GET probe is not only minimally invasive compared to current methods, but the “electro-lens” enables precision spatiotemporal control of gene delivery within predetermined tissue volumes. The use of SS as the electrode material, which was necessary for the concentric design, was also shown to have comparable utility to Pt/Ir with respect to voltage loss across the electrode–electrolyte interface and hence was deemed a suitable substitute. Plate and needle electrodes used for electroporation are frequently made of SS (AISI 304 or 316) or titanium, so the choice of material for the GET probe is not unusual.

A further distinguishing technical feature of the BaDGE GET probe was the onboard charging and discharge mechanism for providing delivery of the DNA without a connection to an external pulse generator. Conventional electroporation systems rely on bulky tethered external generators in order to deliver currents of up to an amp via square-wave pulse trains into the target tissue to achieve hundreds of volts across separated electrodes operating against standard tissue conductivity. Our GET probe integrates the discharge mechanism into the syringe housing sleeve in such a way that the discharge is automatic upon full depression of the syringe plunger, which triggers a copper switch and completes the circuit between the capacitor and electrodes. Intrinsically, the unprecedented efficiency of this electro-lens-based compression of the local electric field enables efficient gene expression with a minimal single capacitive discharge (SCD). A typical pulse protocol for an open-field electroporator of 3 × 52 ms pulses at 500 mA (e.g., CELLECTRA) delivers 78 mC of charge, which is heavily associated with irreversible electroporation, Joule heating, and potential tissue damage. By contrast, under the highest settings used in our study, a 2.2 
μF capacitor charged to 200 V transfers a mere 440 
μC of charge, which is 177 times less than that of the aforementioned typical system. Additionally, the maximum total energy delivered by a single discharge (where 
E=12CV2) is only 0.044 J. Even if all of the energy stored in the capacitor was transmitted as thermal energy, this amount of energy output is negligible and will not induce noticeable Joule heating or thermal damage in the tissue. Overall, the BaDGE GET probe, through the use of single capacitive discharge and conductivity-clamping, has been demonstrated here to be able to effectively achieve high transfection efficiency, while also being wireless, compact, and attachable to any commercially available Luer Lock syringe, with essentially zero additional dead space to the delivery system.

Biological validation

The BaDGE probe in this study was also shown to be highly capable of efficient transfection of HEK293 cells (), with the transfection efficiency being highly dependent on the immersion solution resistivity according to its PBS and 10% sucrose composition. From a 100:0 PBS:sucrose ratio, to a 1:99 ratio, the average time constant of discharge increased from 1.4 to 51.2 ms, corresponding to a resistance increase from 0.6 to 23.3 k
Ω (as the capacitor was 2.2 
μF). This had the effect of significantly increasing the size of the field of gene expression. shows how the total area of detected GFP reporter expression increases as the solution resistivity increases due to increasing sucrose. However, the null zone area (and radius) also increases correspondingly, which is attributable to factors including mechanical disruption from gassing and potentially from irreversible electroporation and thermal (caloric heating) effects at very high field strengths. The spatial variance in density of transfected HEK293 cells within the GET electric field threshold boundary are likely to reflect model conditions, as indicated, as well as potential distortion of the electric field due to the surface tension fluid meniscus effect around the needle probe. Nevertheless, this study confirmed that conductivity-clamping effectively boosts localized electric field strengths, allowing the single capacitive discharge to achieve large transfection zones.

The efficacy of the BaDGE GET system was also investigated through an in vivo longitudinal mouse hindlimb study. SCD-CC-GET delivery of a CMVp-mCherry reporter plasmid into the gastrocnemius muscle resulted in robust and stable expression that was maintained even up to 18 months after the procedure (). Importantly, the image shows that normal muscle fascicle histology was well preserved even at 18 months, which demonstrates the safety of the system and procedures, unlike the necrotic tissue damage that can result from conventional electroporation techniques. Significantly, the safety of conductivity-clamped gene electrotransfer has also recently been validated in a human clinical trial. Although this longitudinal in vivo study provides a robust proof of concept for the SCD-CC-GET delivery mechanism in a live animal model, we acknowledge that it represents a single demonstration. Comprehensive in vivo studies in various animal models to assess transfection efficiency and tissue safety concerns will be needed before the findings in this study can be generalized to broader therapeutic applications.

Translational considerations

There are a number of factors to be considered before the technology described in this study can be scaled up for mass manufacture and translated to clinical usage. From a manufacturing point of view, the material choice and construction of the needle array should be considered. Our current technology utilizes surgical-grade stainless steel needles insulated via vapor-deposited parylene-C, with one needle inserted concentrically within the other. Although the process has been highly refined, it represents the largest bottleneck, because of the reliance on an external facility, at high cost. Assembly of the concentric needles requires particular care to ensure that the 5 
μm insulating coating is not damaged. The attachment of the stainless steel needles onto the PCBs is also a manual process that requires either a specialized solder, or snap-fit spring fittings. For future development of this technology in preparation for mass manufacture, an exploration of biocompatible materials such as aluminum should be conducted. Aluminum needles can be hard anodized quickly to form an insulated layer, allowing for bulk insulation of needles to increase production speed and reduce per unit costs.

Sterility of the components is another factor relevant to clinical usage. The inherently modular design of the BaDGE GET system offers advantages in this regard. Because the needle hub assembly is a separate component to the automated discharge mechanism contained within the sleeve assembly, these parts can be manufactured, sterilized, and packaged separately. The syringes are of course packaged sterile as well. During usage, the clinician will take the sterile syringe from the package and load the DNA solution into it. Only after this will the two components of the BaDGE device be taken out of their sterile packaging and attached to the syringe. The needle guard, which is attached to the needle hub, and which has also been sterilized, protects the needles as the device is charged. When the device has been charged, the needle guard is removed, and immediately the needle is inserted into the patient, minimizing the exposure time to potential pathogens.

Finally, regulatory considerations should also be considered. Our BaDGE system relies on a comparatively simple discharging system, featuring only passive components, no software, and no high voltage/current circuitry. Although the initial voltage (120–200 V) is high, a typical discharge is complete within 5–10 ms and represents a fixed charge, a point of differentiation from conventional current or voltage-controlled electroporation systems. As such, we anticipate that obtaining device safety approval through the regulatory pathway should be significantly less complicated compared to conventional electroporation systems.

Current limitations

While the SCD-CC-GET platform described in the present study successfully demonstrates wireless gene delivery and transfection of plasmid DNA to cellular and animal models, several limitations still need to be addressed in future developments.

The finite element computational modeling in COMSOL preformed in Sec. intentionally utilized a simplified homogeneous model with a globally set 1 S/m tissue conductivity. This was because the intention was to analyze the tuning of the electro-lens element geometry on the resulting electric field profile. The actual conductivity in tissue would be more heterogeneous and anisotropic, and so these simulations do not predict the absolute transfection volume that would be achieved in vivo. A more rigorous computational modeling study using this electrode geometry layout has been performed in our prior work, in which we model the anisotropic conductivity within human brain structures and simulate electric field profiles and predict gene transfection efficacy.

Another limitation of the technology itself is that the discharge profile of the SCD pulse is dependent on the target tissue and cannot be strictly controlled. The capacitor and delivery voltage is fixed, so the total charge delivered (
Q=CV) is known and constant; however, the discharge time constant (
τ=RC) is dependent on the total system resistance of the target. Our conductivity-clamping utilizes this to increase the time constant by artificially increasing the target resistance, as demonstrated in earlier studies as well as in the present study, where it enables a fixed charge delivery to have increased efficiency at transferring the polyanionic DNA/RNA species to the plasma membrane. While our working baseline assumes a tissue resistance of approximately 2 
kΩ with conductivity-clamping, the reality is that variability in clamping efficiency of extracellular tissue conductivity (e.g., due to connective tissue or fat deposits) will impact the resulting efficiency of gene transfection. Application of this electro-lens gene transfer technology in different therapeutic indications will therefore require appropriate in vivo validation.

Because our BaDGE GET probe is specifically designed for single use, and is thus disposable, we believe that long-term durability of the hardware is not a major concern. There is, however, a risk of user error due to the assembly of the modular parts, such as due to misalignment, which may result in the syringe plunger failing to activate the copper switch at the end of its travel. Future designs will increase the keying between assembly components to make it impossible to incorrectly align parts.

CONCLUSION

A single concentric needle system housed in a compact hub incorporating a capacitor and switch was designed and manufactured to provide a GET probe allowing for the delivery of DNA transgenes. The intrinsic fluidics delivered the DNA in a carrier solution designed to reduce local tissue conductance, thereby boosting the electric field strength achieved by the discharge of the capacitor. Given that the translocation of the polyanionic DNA (or mRNA) to the cell plasma membrane requires a minimum electric field strength, this “conductivity-clamping,” demonstrated by the effect of serial dilutions of saline with 10% sucrose in the carrier, proved highly effective at enhancing the transfection volume for the fixed charge, where the boundary of the GFP fluorescence positive cells was a biomarker for this electric field threshold. The modeling and in vitro validation of spatiotemporal control of GET were found to be highly translatable to the delivery of naked mCherry reporter-encoding DNA to mouse hindlimb muscle fibers, where safety and efficient long-term expression were confirmed.

METHODS

As the engineering development and validation of our technology are presented in an integrated format throughout the manuscript, Sec. provides a concise consolidated summary of the principal experimental methodology.

Computational modeling

Computational models were developed in COMSOL Multiphysics 5.5 (COMSOL, Sweden). Electrodes were modeled as cylinders with lengths of 2 mm, diameter of 400 
μm, and separation distances of 0.2, 0.4, 1, and 4 mm. Both the electrodes and surrounding volume were meshed using tetrahedrals with a maximum element size of 100 
μm. Stationary electric currents physics was applied to the model with the surrounding volume set to a conductivity of 1 S/m and zero current flux at the boundaries. The outer surface of one electrode was set as the ground reference while the other was set to 8 V. Electric potential and electric fields in the stimulation volume were simulated for each condition.

Concentric needle probe manufacturing

Needle assemblies were manufactured by first insulating a 51.5 mm 34-gauge needle and 45 mm 28-gauge needle with 5 
μ of parylene-C coating, using vapor deposition. The 34-gauge needle was then threaded through the 28-gauge needle, protruding by 3 mm at the cutting edge, and fixed in place with polyurethane. 2 mm of insulation was then etched away from the distal end of each concentric needle using an 193 nm excimer laser, leaving an insulated gap of 1 mm between the two uninsulated “electrodes.” The needle assembly was then attached to a PCB via a set of spring contacts for the outer 28-gauge needle and a press-fit receptacle for the inner 34-gauge needle. This PCB, featuring contact pads for charging, was inserted into a zero-dead space custom hub.

To complete the system, a syringe was placed inside a three-part sleeve, to which the hub housing the needle assembly was connected. Within the sleeve, a copper switch was created using a set of copper pads and springs, which are electrically connected to a capacitor and to the PCB in the needle hub via connector pins. When the syringe plunger is fully depressed, the copper switch circuit is closed, causing each side of the capacitor to be electrically connected to the two electrodes, producing a discharge in the target.

Engineering characterization

Electrical discharge efficacy and accuracy were evaluated by monitoring the capacitor voltages using a DS1074 oscilloscope. 1k resistors were coupled to the electrodes, and the capacitors (2.2 
μF) were repeatedly charged to 30 V via an external power supply, and discharged across the test resistor. Noise profiles were monitored, and the discharge time constants were recorded, with an expected value of 2.2 ms. In total, 54 trials were conducted across 5 probes.

To compare the impedance of stainless steel electrodes to Pt/Ir, electrical impedance spectroscopy was performed. SS and Pt/Ir GET probes (with paired electrodes) were suspended in 100 ml of 0.9% saline, with EIS performed using a CHI604E electrochemical analyzer. 12 points were obtained per decade, between 1 and 100 000 Hz. Recorded impedances were normalized according to electrode surface area.

Voltage loss at the electrode–electrolyte interfaces was measured using a recording electrode mounted on a T-LS13-M motorized XY stage. Electrodes were immersed in 0.9% saline. The recording electrode sampled 13 points along each electrode, touching the electrode, with a resolution of 150 
μm, and 13 points parallel to each electrode offset by 150 
μm. At each recording site, a full exponential decay discharge from 200 V was produced across the electrodes, and the resulting voltage profile was recorded. At each location along the electrodes, the voltage loss across the bilayer was calculated by comparing the peak voltage at 150 
μm offset from the electrode with the peak voltage touching the electrode. 4 pairs of electrodes for both the SS and Pt/Ir types were evaluated.

In vitro validation

HEK293 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with 5% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin culture media according to standard cell culture protocols. GFP reporter plasmid DNA (CMVp-hrGFPnls) was prepared according to standard protocols using the Qiagen maxiprep kits. Plasmid DNA was suspended in mixtures of standard PBS (0.9% NaCl) and 10% sucrose solution, with ratios of 100%, 37%, 10%, 3.7%, or 1% PBS (vs sucrose). For each set of transfections, a BaDGE GET probe was rested on an 18 mm round coverslip seeded with a monolayer of HEK293 cells. 200 
μl of the PBS/sucrose solution, containing GFP plasmid DNA at a concentration of 0.5 mg/ml, was then pipetted onto the coverslip, fully immersing the electrodes. The 2.2 
μF capacitor in the GET probe, charged to 200 V, was then discharged across the electrodes to transfect the cells.

After the coverslips were incubated in a CO2 incubator for 3 days, the cells on the coverslips were fixed in 4% paraformaldyhyde for 15 min, then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X100 in 4% donkey serum, incubated with 1:500 rabbit - anti GFP antibody in 0.1% Triton overnight, incubated with Alexa488-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG H&L secondary antibody in 0.1% for 4 h, before being mounted. These mounted coverslips were imaged using a LSM 710 confocal laser scanning microscope with a 5×/0.16 objective and argon ion 488 laser.

In vivo validation

Validation in a long-term mouse model was performed by injecting 50 
μl of CMVp-mCherry plasmid DNA suspended in 10% sucrose at 2 
μg/
μl into the left gastrocnemius muscle through the concentric needle BaDGE GET probe. The mouse was anesthetized using isoflurane (1.5%) under standard operating procedures. Immediately after delivery of the plasmid DNA solution, instantaneous gene electrotransfer to the muscle fibers was achieved by discharging the capacitor coupled to the electro-lens probe (2.2 
μF at 120 V). The mouse was euthanized 18 months later via injection of sodium pentobarbital according to standard procedures. The mouse was then fixed using PFA fixation, and then the gastrocnemius muscle was cryosectioned (50 
μm). Sections were imaged using the LSM 710 confocal laser scanning microscope for mCherry fluorescence (61 nm Ex, 567–633 nm Em).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 11. One-way and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests were performed after validating normal distribution of the data using the D'Agostino–Pearson omnibus normality test. Holm–Sidak multiple pairwise post hoc comparisons were then undertaken to evaluate individual factors. Relationships were deemed significant for p

≤ 0.05. Mean values reported are presented with 
± standard error of the mean (SEM).
