Understanding sustainability of measurement-based care in child and adolescent mental health services: a study protocol
Authors: Kalee Jack, Graham Gaine, Kewir Dufe, Kimberly Manalili, Ahlam Zidan, Erin McCabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMeasurement-based care (MBC) is the routine use of patient-reported outcome measures to inform treatment planning in mental health care. MBC provides clinicians with real-time indicators of clients’ symptoms and functioning, and organizations with aggregate outcome data to inform service evaluation. While our understanding of how to implement MBC is growing, there is very little evidence on how to sustain MBC within child and adolescent mental health services (CAMHS). The purpose of this project is to describe the conditions, and processes that promote sustainment of MBC in eight community child and adolescent mental health treatment programs.MethodsWe will use an explanatory, sequential mixed-methods design. Quantitative data on MBC sustainability will be derived from clinician surveys and the provincial electronic medical record. Following Yin’s multiple-case study methodology, qualitative data will be collected through interviews to explore factors that promoted sustainability across sites. We use the Dynamic Sustainability Framework as the theoretical model for this study and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research. The proposed project will take place in eight public CAMHS clinics from a single urban jurisdiction. Participants will include clinical staff from the sites and clients ages 11–17.DiscussionThis study will examine sustainment of MBC in CAMHS settings after external implementation support has been withdrawn. Knowledge of specific events, processes, and contextual conditions within clinics in this study that supported MBC sustainment will help practitioners implement and sustain MBC in CAMHS.Trial registrationNot applicable.

• This study provides one of the first examinations of how district health leaders in a low-resource setting perceive the feasibility of implementing the WHO LIVE LIFE suicide-prevention framework.

• It shows how an implementation-champion strategy, guided by the i-PARIHS framework, can be used to prepare district-level health providers for integrating global mental-health guidance into routine systems.

• It identifies specific contextual, cultural, and structural conditions that shape readiness for suicide-prevention implementation in rural Uganda.

• Findings offer practical direction on how to design facilitation, training, and system supports for district-level adoption of suicide-prevention interventions in similar settings.

Background

The youth mental health crisis continues to grow, with an estimated 1 in 5 children and adolescents experiencing mental health conditions [1, 2]. Access to high-quality and evidence-based care is critical for the development and future success of children and adolescents with mental health concerns. Measurement-based care (MBC) is an evidence-based, patient-centred approach with the potential to enhance child and adolescent mental health services (CAMHS) at both the individual and system levels [3].

MBC involves administering youth patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) before or during an appointment, which are standardized and validated measures of youths’ functioning and well-being. At the start of treatment sessions, the clinician and youth collaboratively review the PROM scores (and trends over time) to improve communication and guide treatment planning. This process is repeated routinely during treatment, allowing for timely re-evaluation and adaptation of the treatment plan (e.g., adding a medication, changing the psychotherapy intervention) [4, 5]. At the individual level, MBC benefits youth by considering their self-reported health status and well-being when making clinical decisions, enhancing engagement in treatment decisions, and strengthening the patient-provider relationship [6]. MBC also helps identify youth at risk of poor outcomes, enabling timely treatment adjustments [7, 8]. The evidence for MBC effectiveness in adults is strong and is growing in youth populations [5]. MBC has been shown to be effective in improving symptom severity and rate of symptom reduction in youth [4, 5, 7, 9–12]. A meta-analysis by Rognstad et al. [7] demonstrated the effectiveness of MBC over usual care (effect size = 0.29, p < 0.001) [7].

At the system-level, aggregated MBC data are used for service evaluation and informing organizational and government policy makers [3, 5, 9]. The benefits of MBC are higher when all CAMHS within a health system adopt an integrated MBC model, in which PROM data can be utilized by all practitioners across the patient care pathway [5, 8]. This integration enables tracking of patient outcomes across services, improves care coordination, provides deeper insights into patient needs, and contributes to a learning health system with potential to deepen our understanding of the most effective mental health treatments for youth [5, 13–15].

Despite its established benefits, integrating MBC into CAMHS as standard practice remains limited [5, 16, 17]. Implementation is complex, requiring change management at the organizational, information technology, provider and patient levels [4, 9]. In CAMHS, these challenges are compounded by factors such as family involvement and limited availability of PROMs among youth [4, 5, 9, 18]. Strategies for successful implementation of MBC in CAMHS, include clinician training, ongoing supportive coaching, audit and feedback, technological supports and leadership engagement [19]. While there is a growing understanding of how to successfully implement MBC in CAMHS, to our knowledge, factors that support its long-term integration into practice have not been examined [19].

Sustainability, defined as the continued use of an evidence-based practice components at sufficient intensity to maintain its desired health outcomes, is a persistent challenge in implementation science [20, 21]. To date, implementation science more broadly, and MBC in CAMHS more specifically, has focused on understanding factors and strategies that support initial implementation. However, maximizing the long-term impact of innovations such as MBC requires a deliberate shift towards understanding and promoting sustainability [20]. Without an understanding of the processes and conditions that support the sustained use of MBC within CAMHS, youth with mental health challenges are unlikely to have consistent access to this evidence-based practice, contributing to suboptimal mental health outcomes. As well, the substantial investments made by CAMHS administrators to implement MBC are at risk of being lost if the practice is not sustained over the long term.

Purpose

The purpose of this project is to describe conditions and processes that promote sustainability of MBC in CAMHS. There are two main objectives: (1) to assess sustainment of MBC across eight CAMHS clinics, and (2) to analyze factors contributing to sustainment across sites.

Methods

Design

We will use an explanatory, sequential mixed-methods design which will consist of quantitative data from electronic medical record and clinician survey data used to assess sustainment (i.e., fidelity to MBC practices, factors contributing to sustainment) (Objective 1), followed by a comparative case study [22] using qualitative interviews for an in-depth exploration of the conditions, processes and mechanisms that supported MBC sustainability success across sites (Objective 2). In this study, quantitative analyses will serve a descriptive role to guide sampling and data collection for Objective 2, while qualitative inquiry provides the primary explanatory account of sustainment. Findings are integrated at two points, first, during the transition from Objective 1 to Objective 2, to guide sampling and data collection for Objective 2. Second, quantitative sustainment findings and qualitative data are integrated into the comparative case study analysis. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Board at the University of Alberta (Pro00156712).

We are using an Integrated Knowledge Translation (iKT) approach, which engages knowledge users (i.e., those impacted by the research) throughout the research process [23] producing project findings that will be immediately usable for health system partners. We will have an advisory council of five clinical supervisors (i.e., direct supervisors of clinicians) and managers from the eight community mental health clinics. Youth engagement, defined as the meaningful and active collaboration between the research team and youth with lived experience on research design and knowledge translation, is a component of our iKT strategy [24].

Theoretical framework

This study uses the Dynamic Sustainability Framework (DSF) as its theoretical framework [21]. The DSF conceptualizes sustainability as a product of the “fit” between three key elements over time: (1) the intervention (i.e., MBC practices, the PROM), (2) the practice setting (i.e., clinic structures, staffing, workflows, client populations), and (3) the ecological system (i.e., organization structures, policies, networks) where practice settings exist [21]. Figure 1 (see additional information) illustrates the DSF applied to this project. The DSF considers change in each of these elements to be constant, and that sustainability success depends on continuously optimizing the fit between the intervention, the practice setting and the ecological system over time [21]. We will use the DSF to examine how sites adapted MBC and/or their practice setting to maintain “fit” with local clinic needs and within the broader system, and how these adaptations helped or hindered sustainment of MBC.

Setting and context of implementation

The proposed project will take place in eight CAMHS clinics within Edmonton, Canada, operated by the provincial publicly funded mental health service provider. Edmonton is Canada’s fifth-largest city, with a population of 1.2 million as of 2024 [25]. All 8 study sites are described as individual, family, group and walk-in mental health services for moderate to severe levels of distress or impairment due to mental health concerns. Services are goal-focused, delivered primarily by clinical social workers and psychologists, with some consultative psychiatry services. In 2024–2025, clinics averaged from 3.8 to 35.4 new clients per month, had a mean 1.5 clinic visits per month (similar across clinics), and mean client age ranged from 13.56 to 14.83 years. Across all clinics, the typical presenting problems are similar, primarily anxiety, behaviour/aggression, inattention, and mood.

In March 2022, the service provider adopted a systematic approach to the implementation of MBC in its CAMHS community mental health services, rolling MBC out widely to eight community mental health clinic sites in February 2023. The implementation process was planned using the Quality Implementation Framework, a four-phase step-by-step implementation process model which was designed to guide the “how-to” processes of implementation [26]. The Theoretical Domains Framework guided the assessment of barriers and facilitators, which were then mapped onto implementation strategies using the Behaviour Change Wheel [27]. Implementation strategies included staff engagement, didactic education, skills training, and supportive coaching. Due to changes in health system resources, initial implementation was supported, however support for full implementation and evaluation was withdrawn. As a result, the support and evaluation of MBC sustainability was not conducted. Preliminary electronic health record (EHR) data and discussion with clinical supervisors suggest variable sustainment across sites since February 2023.

Intervention description

The MBC program is integrated in the EHR. Clients between 11 and 17 years of age were administered a PROM called the Young Person’s Clinical Outcomes in Routine Evaluation (YP-CORE) [28, 29]. YP-CORE is a short, clinically feasible 10-item global measure of psychological distress in young people. It is a freely available measure with established norms, reliable change indices, and clinically significant cut-points, which makes it ideal for broad use within the health system [30]. Objective 1. Assess the sustainment of MBC across eight CAMHS clinics

Design

We are using a retrospective, observational study design to assess the degree to which MBC practices were sustained across all 8 clinical sites where MBC was implemented. A secondary aim is to examine equity in MBC delivery.

Outcomes

We will examine fidelity over time (i.e., the extent to which MBC was used as intended) to the four components of MBC (see Table 1). The primary implementation outcome will be an empirically validated MBC Fidelity Index, which combines the two components of MBC considered to be most essential (i.e., administration of a PROM, collaborative re-evaluation of treatment plan informed by PROM scores) [10, 32]. We will also examine equitable delivery of MBC by examining completion rates by subgroup variables (e.g., age, gender, primary diagnosis, preferred language).

Participants and data collection

Data from youth who attended each of the 8 sites for individual therapy between January 2022 and January 2026 will be extracted from the EHR. For each client, this will include the number of encounters, number of YP-CORE administrations, YP-CORE scores, and demographic and clinical information (age, gender, sex, primary diagnosis, language preferred).

All clinical staff (addiction counsellors, mental health therapists, occupational therapists, recreational therapists, nurses, psychiatrists, psychologists, social workers, peer support workers, rehabilitation assistants), clinical supervisors, administrative support, and managers at all sites will be invited to complete an online survey as part of a staff meeting containing demographic questions (role, profession, years in practice, clinic, age, gender), closed-ended questions about feasibility and acceptability, and open-ended questions about their perceptions of events, processes or conditions that enabled or hindered the sustainment of MBC. For clinicians, it will also include two validated scales assessing: (1) MBC self-efficacy, (2) self-reported fidelity to MBC components (see Table 1) [16]. For these scales, clinicians will be prompted to recall the past 3 months. There are approximately 63 staff across the study sites. With an anticipated 70% response rate, we estimate a total of 44 staff survey participants.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis will be conducted at the clinic level. For the staff survey, descriptive statistics will be calculated for each scale and closed-response questions (i.e., median and interquartile range and/or mean and standard deviations, depending on distribution of responses or the type of response scale), and scales will be scored according to developers’ recommendations.

For each clinic, we will assess fidelity to components of MBC as follows:

Routine administration of MBC – the YP-CORE completion rate for each client will be calculated by dividing the number of YP-CORE administrations by total number of opportunities for administration (i.e., number of clinical encounters). MBC fidelity index

The MBC Fidelity Index will be calculated by multiplying the mean YP-CORE administration rate for October 2025 to January 2026 (the last 4 months of available data) by the average reported collaborative re-evaluation of treatment plan informed by YP-CORE scores.

To assess equity in MBC delivery, we will examine YP-CORE completion rates by subgroup using independent samples t-tests for sex and preferred language (i.e., English or not) or one-way ANOVA for age, gender, and primary diagnosis. Qualitative data and scale scores collected through the survey will be analyzed alongside interview data in Objective 2.

Objective 2. Analyze factors contributing to sustainment across sites

Design

To examine sustainment across sites, we will use Yin’s multiple-case study methodology, which offers a structured approach to investigating complex phenomena embedded in their real-world contexts [22]. This approach is well-suited to our study, as the DSF conceptualizes sustainability as a dynamic process of continually optimizing the “fit” between the intervention (i.e., MBC) and its evolving context (i.e., the practice setting and broader ecological system) [21]. Multiple case study methodology allows us to explore how this “fit” unfolds over time within and across sites. Each clinic represents a distinct “case”, allowing for both within-case and cross-case analysis. Data from Objective 1 will be used to inform Objective 2.

Participants

The research team, youth research partners, advisory council and CYF leadership will meet to select 4 clinics for this objective, aiming for 2 clinics from the highest quartile of the MBC Fidelity Index (i.e., “high sustainment success”), and 2 from the lowest quartile (i.e., “low success”), also striving for variation in clinic setting (i.e., client population served, size of clinic, etc.).

At each sampled site, we will purposively recruit a range of informants involved in MBC, specifically 3–4 clinicians, 1–2 clinical supervisors, 1 manager, and 1 administrative support person from each site. All staff will be recruited through an email from the clinic manager. Staff will be invited to email the principle investigator to schedule an interview.

Adolescents aged 12–17 will be recruited through posters in clinic waiting areas. Interested clients will contact the principal investigator by email to schedule an interview. The inclusion criterion is that the client received services at the site and participated in MBC. We will also recruit 4–5 clients (ages 12–18 years old) per site (~ 5 clients per site over 4 sites). Assent will be obtained from children aged 14 years and under, with parental consent. Children aged 15–18 will be assessed using 4 yes/no questions to determine whether they understand the purpose of the study, that participation is voluntary, that they can withdraw at any time, and that their participation will not affect the care they receive. If they answer all 4 questions correctly, they will be considered a mature minor and can consent to participation on their own behalf. We will base final sample sizes on the principle of information power [33]. Based on our research team’s prior work, these numbers are feasible and likely sufficient to provide information power [34].

Data collection

We will conduct one-on-one semi-structured interviews with all participants using Zoom and we will audio-record and transcribe the interviews. Interviews will last 30–45 min. The staff interview guide will be informed by findings from Objective 1 and structured by DSF domains (i.e., intervention, practice setting, ecological context). We will ask about participants’ perceptions of the acceptability of MBC and appropriateness of this MBC design for their populations, and how that has changed over time. There will also be questions about changes in implementation support over time and their impact on sustainability. The client interview guide will be informed by the theoretical framework of acceptability [35] asking about the perceived fit of the MBC program to their needs (e.g., is YP-CORE relevant to their experience, is MBC helpful in their care), their MBC experiences (e.g., how did they experience filling out the PROM, discussing with their therapist), as well as their suggestions for improving the MBC program (e.g., how to improve client engagement with MBC, how therapists should be using the PROM scores).

Pre-implementation readiness survey

Data from a pre-implementation readiness survey administered by implementation practitioners during the initial implementation (March 2022) across all 8 sites will be included, along with data collected for Objectives 1 and 2, as described below. This survey employed validated scales assessing attitudes toward MBC, as described above, to determine clinic readiness for implementation.

Data analysis and integration of quantitative and qualitative data

Data analysis will follow Yin’s case study methods [22], emphasizing both within- and cross-case exploration, guided by the Dynamic Sustainability Framework (DSF). We will use an abductive approach, combining deductive coding based on DSF domains (e.g., intervention adaptation, contextual shifts, ecological influences) with inductive coding to capture emergent themes from participant accounts. For each clinic, we will develop a detailed case summary that synthesizes quantitative sustainment findings and from the pre-implementation readiness survey (Objective 1), as well as adaptations to MBC and qualitative evidence of contextual influences over time (Objective 2). Cross-case comparison will be conducted using a matrix to systematically explore how DSF constructs manifested across clinics and to identify factors associated with sustainment success.

Data management

Data will be stored in a de-identified state, with a file containing participant IDs linked to names stored in a separate, password-protected file. Data will be stored on-site in a secure server at the University of Alberta and destroyed after 5 years, in accordance with University of Alberta policy.

Discussion

MBC is an adaptable intervention that can be delivered by a range of clinicians, implemented with digital tools, and integrated across diverse clinical settings, with both transtheoretical and transdiagnostic relevance [4, 36, 37]. Effective MBC generally includes four components: (1) administration of an assessment tool; (2) review of scores and score trajectories by the clinician and client; (3) shared decision-making about whether to adjust the treatment plan based on these scores; and (4) longitudinal repetition of these steps [4]. MBC designs can vary considerably depending on the context in which they will be used, including differences in the constructs being monitored, the specific assessment tools used, the frequency of MBC cycles (e.g., at each visit or biweekly), and the processes through which tools are administered and feedback is provided to clinicians and clients.

A recent study by Williams et al. (2025) examined the sustainment of MBC in outpatient youth mental health clinics using a randomized implementation trial comparing a Leadership and Organizational Change for Implementation (LOCI) strategy with technical assistance alone [32]. They found that while MBC completion rates and symptom improvement did not differ between groups, sites receiving the LOCI strategy demonstrated higher MBC fidelity 13–26 months after implementation initiation. However, overall rates of MBC measure administration remained low (9–12%) and were lower in LOCI sites [32]. This suggests that leadership-focused training can enhance fidelity to the MBC model over time but is insufficient to improve overall rates of measure administration. Notably, systematic investigation of MBC sustainment in CAMHS beyond this study is limited, leaving critical gaps in understanding how MBC practices are maintained in practice.

Chambers et al. (2013) proposed the Dynamic Sustainability Framework, which emphasizes the evolving fit among the intervention (e.g., MBC), the practice setting (e.g., CAMHS clinics), and the broader ecological system (e.g., health system structure, policies, and political context). Given the inherent adaptability of MBC, the present study takes a different approach to understanding sustainment: rather than focusing solely on leadership strategies with a rigid MBC design, it seeks to examine how interactions between the intervention, the practice setting, and the ecological context may influence the alignment of MBC in real-world conditions. By exploring how MBC may be adapted within the practice settings, or how changes in the setting shape the intervention, this study aims to generate insights into the conditions that support the long-term sustainability of MBC. Findings from this work may also be transferable to other evidence-based practices in community mental health settings, helping to guide strategies for implementation and sustainment of interventions beyond MBC.
