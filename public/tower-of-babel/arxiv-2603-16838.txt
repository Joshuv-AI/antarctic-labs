Complex Wannier centers and drifting Wannier functions in non-Hermitian Hamiltonians Preprint: APS/123-QED
Pedro Fittipaldi de Castro, Yifan Wang, Wladimir A. Benalcazar

Abstract
The extension of topological band theory to non-Hermitian Hamiltonians with line energy gaps remains largely unexplored, despite early indications of rich underlying physics. In this setting, Wilson loops—the quantities underlying polarization—generally become nonunitary, yet the physical consequences of this nonunitarity have remained unclear. Within the framework of biorthonormal quantum mechanics, we introduce the concept of complex Wannier centers, defined from the gauge-invariant eigenvalues of nonunitary Wilson loops. Complex Wannier centers acquire physical meaning through reciprocity breaking in their associated Wannier functions: when the centroid of a Wannier function shifts into the complex plane, it acquires an effective momentum offset that produces directional drift over time. We analyze how symmetries constrain complex Wannier centers and identify symmetry-protected Wannier configurations in pseudo-Hermitian Hamiltonians, where the centers are either real or form complex-conjugate pairs, as determined by conserved “Krein signatures” of the projected metric operator of pseudo-Hermiticity. We further show that the Krein structure of the Wilson loop can establish a bulk–boundary correspondence: in a system with anticommuting pseudo-Hermitian metric and (pseudo) inversion symmetries, the behavior of complex Wannier centers predicts the existence of a filling anomaly in the occupied bands and whether the resulting edge modes experience gain or loss. Finally, we propose a photonic waveguide implementation of this system that enables experimental tests of our predictions.

In addition, we show that the intrinsically non-Hermitian phenomenon of symmetry ramification

As a concrete example, we consider inversion and pseudo-inversion symmetries, which ramify into distinct symmetry branches in non-Hermitian systems. When both branches are simultaneously present, they enforce pseudo-Hermiticity of the Wilson loop. If the corresponding symmetry operators mutually anticommute, a BBC follows: the real parts of the Wannier centers track the presence of a filling anomaly in the occupied bands and the associated edge-localized modes, while the imaginary parts determine dynamical stability, predicting whether these modes undergo amplification or attenuation.

The theoretical foundation of the paper is laid out in Sec. II, where we introduce biorthogonal and nonunitary Wilson loops and complex Wannier centers, together with a physical interpretation of the imaginary parts of Wannier centers. We corroborate these theoretical findings through dynamical simulations of Wannier functions. In Sec. III, we focus on pseudo-Hermitian (pH) Hamiltonians and the Wannier configurations protected by the total Krein signature carried by the Wannier functions. Using a one-dimensional model, we verify that Krein signatures determine whether degenerate Wannier centers remain real or split into complex-conjugate pairs. In Sec. IV, we analyze the ramification of inversion symmetry induced by non-Hermiticity and derive the constraints that its symmetry branches impose on the Wilson loop. We investigate both commuting and anticommuting realizations of inversion and pseudo-inversion symmetries. While both cases constrain the Wannier spectrum, the commuting case is more restrictive and forbids nontrivial Krein collisions, whereas the anticommuting case allows such collisions and thereby gives rise to a bulk–boundary correspondence, as demonstrated by an explicit example that can be implemented in multimode photonic systems.

II.2
Drifting Wannier functions

To understand the physics of
\kappa
, consider a one-dimensional crystal with
N_{\mathrm{occ}}=1
occupied band. In the Hermitian limit, a Wannier function
\ket{W_{x_{0}}}
is a state exponentially localized and peaked at a point
x_{0}+\nu
in space, where
x_{0}
is a unit cell coordinate (which we take to be
x_{0}=0
for simplicity) and
\nu
, the purely real WC of a Hermitian insulator, is the coordinate relative to the center of the unit cell. For each orbital component
\alpha=1,\dots,N
, we define the position-space amplitude

F_{\alpha}(x)\equiv(\bra{x}\otimes\bra{\alpha})\ket{W_{0}},

(17)

and its corresponding weight profile
\rho_{\alpha}(x)\equiv|F_{\alpha}(x)|^{2}
, typically smooth and symmetric about its maximum at
x=\nu
. Expanding near the peak, we may approximate the localized envelope by a Gaussian,

F_{\alpha}(x)\approx F_{\alpha}(\nu)\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-\nu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right],

(18)

where the width
\sigma
is fixed by the curvature of
\rho_{\alpha}(x)
at
x=\nu
,

\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}=-\frac{\rho_{\alpha}(\nu)}{\rho_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}(\nu)}.

(19)

Now, imagine that we weakly break non-Hermiticity so that the Wilson loop (a Berry phase for
N_{\mathrm{occ}}=1
) acquires a small nonunitary part and the corresponding WC becomes complex,

\nu\to\nu+\mathrm{i}\kappa.

(20)

For
|\kappa|\ll 1
, one expects the WF to remain close to its Hermitian limit. In this regime, the leading effect of a small imaginary WC is the displacement of the centroid of the envelope (
18
) from the real axis
(x=\nu)
onto the complex plane
(x=\nu+\mathrm{i}\kappa)
, giving us

F_{\alpha}(x)\approx F_{\alpha}(\nu+\mathrm{i}\kappa)\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-\nu-\mathrm{i}\kappa)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right].

(21)

Expanding the square in the expression above yields

F_{\alpha}(x)\approx F_{\alpha}(\nu+\mathrm{i}\kappa)e^{-\frac{(x-\nu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\kappa}{\sigma^{2}}(x-\nu)}e^{\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},

(22)

so that, up to an overall normalization factor, the non-Hermitian Wannier function differs from the Hermitian one only by a plane wave factor with an effective momentum

k_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx\frac{\kappa}{\sigma^{2}}.

(23)

In summary, our heuristic argument suggests that, to a first approximation, the imaginary part
\kappa
of the Wannier center manifests as a linear phase gradient in space, i.e., a momentum shift, superimposed on the WF envelope.

To make our interpretation of
\kappa
more rigorous, we must first carefully specify what we mean by the term “Wannier function”, as it has two definitions that coincide in one-dimensional Hermitian Hamiltonians, but are not equivalent upon breaking Hermiticity.

II.2.4
Robustness of drifting Wannier functions

in the thermodynamic limit

At this stage, it is natural to ask whether the dynamical consequences of a complex Wannier center persist in the thermodynamic limit. On the one hand, line-gapped non-Hermitian Hamiltonians generically exhibit a nonunitary Wilson loop and complex Wannier centers
z=\nu+\mathrm{i}\kappa
, which can produce an effective momentum shift
k_{\mathrm{eff}}
and directional drift of Wannier wave packets.
On the other hand, the projected position operator is constructed from the Resta operator (
27
), and its eigenvalues obey [c.f. Eq. (
26
)]

|\lambda_{x}|=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi\kappa}{\ell}\right)=1+\frac{2\pi\kappa}{\ell}+\mathcal{O}(\ell^{-2}).

(44)

Note from (
44
) that the deviation from
|\lambda_{x}|=1
scales as
1/\ell
and thus vanishes in the thermodynamic limit. In other words, although the Wilson loop of a line-gapped non-Hermitian Hamiltonian is generically nonunitary even as
\ell\to\infty
, its biorthogonal projected position operator becomes asymptotically unitary—in the sense that its eigenvalues approach the unit circle—because the Resta operator (
27
) itself contains an explicit factor of
1/\ell
.

However, this asymptotic unitarity of the projected position operator
does not
imply that the imaginary part
\kappa
of the WC becomes unphysical. The situation is fully analogous to the Hermitian case where the real Wannier center
\nu
yields a finite bulk polarization even though
e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\nu/\ell}\to 1
as
\ell\to\infty
. Likewise, the effective momentum shift on Wannier functions depends on
\kappa
but not on
\ell
[cf. (
23
) and (
43
)], so the asymptotic unitarity of the projected position operator has no bearing on the nonreciprocal dynamics encoded by the complex Wannier centers. Rather,
\kappa
encodes a real dynamical effect that survives in the thermodynamic limit.

III
Wannier functions and spectra of pseudo-Hermitian Hamiltonians

A Hamiltonian
H
is pseudo-Hermitian (pH) if there exists a Hermitian and invertible operator
\xi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
(often called
metric
) such that
43

H^{\dagger}=\xi\ H\ \xi^{-1}.

(58)

The special case
\xi=\mathbb{I}
reduces to Hermiticity. Here, we focus on the cases where the pseudo-Hermitian metric acts nontrivially only on the internal (orbital) degrees of freedom,

\xi=\mathbb{I}_{L}\otimes\eta,

(59)

so that the Bloch Hamiltonian is also pH with a
k
-independent metric,

h^{\dagger}(k)=\eta\ h(k)\ \eta^{-1}.

(60)

An immediate consequence of (
60
) is that the right and left eigenvectors of
h_{k}
are not independent but related by
\eta
. Indeed, if
\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}
is a right eigenvector with eigenvalue
E_{k,n}
, then

h^{\dagger}(k)(\eta\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}})=\eta h(k)\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}=E_{k,n}(\eta\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}).

(61)

Eq. (
61
) implies
\eta\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}
is a right eigenvector of
h(k)^{\dagger}
with eigenvalue
E_{k,n}
and, hence, a left eigenvector of
h(k)
with energy
E_{k,n}^{*}
. Consequently, the spectrum of a pH Hamiltonian consists of real or complex-conjugate pairs of eigenvalues.

Since the occupied subspace is defined as the span of the eigenvectors with
\mathrm{Re}[E_{k,n}]\leq E_{0}
[c.f. (
2
)] and the real part of an eigenvalue is invariant under complex conjugation, it follows that
\eta\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}
lies in the occupied subspace if and only if
\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}
does. Therefore, the matrix of occupied right eigenvectors
R_{k}
[c.f. (
9
)] and the left set of eigenvectors
\tilde{L}_{k}=\eta R_{k}
both span the same subspace, even though they are not necessarily biorthonormal:

\tilde{L}_{k}^{\dagger}R_{k}=R_{k}^{\dagger}\eta R_{k}=M_{k},

(62)

where
M_{k}
is an
N_{\mathrm{occ}}\times N_{\mathrm{occ}}
invertible Hermitian matrix. Defining a new set of left vectors,

L_{k}=\tilde{L}_{k}M_{k}^{-1}=\eta R_{k}M^{-1}_{k},

(63)

leads to biorthonormality,

L_{k}^{\dagger}R_{k}=M_{k}^{-1}M_{k}=\mathbb{I}_{N_{\mathrm{occ}}}.

(64)

III.1
Pseudo-unitary Wilson loop

In Appendix
H
, we show that the relations (
63
) and (
64
) imply that the Wilson loop is
pseudo-unitary
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{-1}_{k}

\displaystyle=M_{k}^{-1}\mathcal{W}_{k}^{\dagger}M_{k},

(65)

where
M_{k}
plays the role of a metric in the occupied subspace.

Now, let
\ket{w^{R,L}_{\lambda}}
denote the right/left eigenvectors of the Wilson loop with eigenvalue
\lambda
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{k}\ket{w^{R}_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\lambda\ket{w^{R}_{\lambda}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\dagger}_{k}\ket{w^{L}_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{*}\ket{w^{L}_{\lambda}}.

(66)

The pseudo-unitary condition (
65
) implies that
M_{k}\ket{w^{R}_{\lambda}}
is a right eigenstate of
\mathcal{W}_{k}^{\dagger}
with eigenvalue
1/\lambda
and therefore a left eigenvalue of
\mathcal{W}_{k}
with eigenvalue
1/\lambda^{*}
(see Ref.
42
and Appendix
I
). As a result, the spectrum of pseudo-unitary Wilson loops consists of unimodular eigenvalues
|\lambda|=1
or inverse-conjugate pairs
(\lambda,1/\lambda^{*})
. If
|\lambda|=1
, we know that the imaginary part
\kappa
of the WC is zero and the corresponding Wannier function is reciprocal. Now, for a nontrivial pair
\lambda_{2}=1/\lambda_{1}^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|\lambda_{2}|=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\Big|\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}\Big|=-\kappa_{1}.

(67)

Hence, for pseudo-unitary Wilson loops, the WCs are either real or come in complex-conjugate pairs
(\nu+\mathrm{i}\kappa,\nu-\mathrm{i}\kappa)
, the latter case generally corresponding to counterpropagating Wannier functions.

Taking determinants on both sides of (
65
) gives

|\mathrm{det}\mathcal{W}_{k}|=1.

(68)

On the other hand, since
\det\mathcal{W}_{k}=\prod_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{occ}}}\lambda_{n}
,
we may write

|\det\mathcal{W}_{k}|=\exp\!\left(2\pi\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{occ}}}\kappa_{n}\right).

(69)

Therefore, although individual Wannier functions may be nonreciprocal
(\kappa_{n}\neq 0)
, pseudo-unitarity enforces a global reciprocity condition,

\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{occ}}}\kappa_{n}=0.

(70)

In other words, when the Wilson loop is pseudo-unitary—or more generally, when it does not satisfy (
16
)—nonreciprocity appears at the level of individual channels, not as a net flow.

III.2
Krein signatures of Wannier centers

Beyond these spectral constraints, pseudo-unitarity endows the space of Wilson loop eigenvectors with an indefinite inner product determined by
M_{k}
. For two right eigenvectors
\ket{w^{R}_{\lambda}}
and
\ket{w^{R}_{\mu}}
, we define the
Krein overlap

[M_{k}]_{\lambda\mu}=\langle w^{R}_{\lambda}|M_{k}\ket{w^{R}_{\mu}}.

(71)

Using
M_{k}\mathcal{W}_{k}^{-1}=W^{\dagger}_{k}M_{k}
, which follows from (
65
), we obtain the condition

(\lambda^{*}\mu-1)[M_{k}]_{\lambda\mu}=0,

(72)

which means that Wilson loop eigenvectors with nonvanishing Krein overlap necessarily have eigenvalues related by
\mu=1/\lambda^{*}
. In particular, if
\ket{w^{R}_{\lambda}}
has a nontrivial
Krein norm
(self-overlap), then its eigenvalue
\lambda
is unitary and its WC is purely real,

[M_{k}]_{\lambda\lambda}\neq 0\Rightarrow|\lambda|=1\ \ \text{and}\ \ \kappa=0.

(73)

The sign of the Krein norm defines the
Krein signature

s(\lambda)
of a Wilson loop eigenvector
\ket{w^{R}_{\lambda}}
,

s(\lambda)=\begin{cases}-1,\quad[M_{k}]_{\lambda\lambda}<0,\\
+1,\quad[M_{k}]_{\lambda\lambda}>0.\end{cases}

(74)

The Krein stability theorem (see, for instance, Refs.
20
;
12
;
51
) states that the Krein signature of a vector is smooth under perturbations and can only change or become ill-defined if the corresponding eigenvalue becomes degenerate with another one of opposite Krein signature. In our setting, this implies that a unimodular Wilson loop eigenvalue
|\lambda_{1}|=1
cannot leave the unit circle under deformations that preserve pseudo-unitarity unless it collides with another unimodular eigenvalue
|\lambda_{2}|=1
carrying the opposite Krein signature. At such a “Krein collision”, the Krein norms
[M_{k}]_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{1}}
and
[M_{k}]_{\lambda_{2}\lambda_{2}}
cancel, and the pair of eigenvalues may move away from the unit circle as inverse-conjugate pairs
(\lambda,1/\lambda^{*})
, corresponding to complex-conjugate WCs
(\nu+\mathrm{i}\kappa,\nu-\mathrm{i}\kappa)
.

III.3
Krein signature as a chiral charge

The Krein signature has a natural interpretation in terms of the Wannier Hamiltonian
h_{W}(k)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}\log\mathcal{W}_{k}
, whose eigenvectors are the same as those of the Wilson loop, and its eigenvalues are simply the WCs,

h_{W}(k)\ket{w^{R}_{\lambda}}=(\nu+\mathrm{i}\kappa)\ket{w^{R}_{\lambda}}.

(75)

Since
\mathcal{W}_{k}
is pseudo-unitary,
h_{W}(k)
is automatically pH with respect to the same metric
M_{k}
,

h_{W}^{\dagger}(k)=M_{k}^{-1}h_{W}(k)M_{k}.

(76)

As a consequence, the Hermitian part of the Wannier Hamiltonian, defined as
h^{(h)}_{W}(k)=\frac{1}{2}[h_{W}(k)+h^{\dagger}_{W}(k)]
, commutes with the metric,

[M_{k},h^{(h)}_{W}(k)]=0,

(77)

while the anti-Hermitian part, defined as
h^{(a)}_{W}(k)=\frac{1}{2}[h_{W}(k)-h^{\dagger}_{W}(k)]
,
anticommutes
with it,

\{M_{k},h_{W}^{(a)}\}=0.

(78)

Therefore, the metric
M_{k}
plays the role of a chiral operator and maps the eigenstates of the Wannier Hamiltonian with
+\mathrm{i}\kappa
to those with
-\mathrm{i}\kappa
. We can thus say that Wannier functions associated with complex-conjugate WCs are counter-propagating chiral partners related by
M_{k}
.

For
\kappa=0
, the Wilson loop eigenvector
\ket{w^{R}_{\lambda}}

\kappa=0
.
We now verify these constraints in concrete examples.

Pseudo-Hermiticity, together with crystalline symmetries such as inversion, can stabilize Wannier configurations that have no analogue in Hermitian Hamiltonians. More generally, this enrichment reflects the broader principle of
symmetry ramification
in non-Hermitian systems, whereby a symmetry that is unique in the Hermitian limit splits into distinct symmetry branches that independently constrain the energy spectrum and the Wilson loop. In the next section, we analyze this mechanism for crystalline (pseudo-)inversion and show how its ramified symmetry branches enforce characteristic patterns of Wannier-center flows.

IV
Symmetry ramification and Wilson loops

In Hermitian Hamiltonians, the statements

\displaystyle h(k)

\displaystyle=Sh(\pm k)S^{-1},\quad h(k)^{\dagger}=Sh(\pm k)S^{-1},

(81)

are equivalent as symmetry constraints, since
h(k)^{\dagger}=h(k)
. Both express that the Bloch Hamiltonian is symmetric under an invertible (linear or antilinear) operator
S
, which may either preserve (
+k
) or reverse
(-k)
momentum. In non-Hermitian Hamiltonians, however, these relations are no longer identical. The similarity condition

h(k)=Sh(\pm k)S^{-1}

(82)

acts within the right (and separately within the left) eigenspaces and may connect states at different momenta, but it does not relate right and left eigenvectors. In contrast, conjugation-type symmetry

h^{\dagger}(k)=Sh(\pm k)S^{-1},

(83)

of which pseudo-Hermiticity is the special
S^{\dagger}=S
and
+k
case, does relate right and left eigenvectors. Consequently, the conditions (
82
) and (
83
) impose distinct constraints on the biorthogonal Berry connection (
12
) and Wilson loop (
14
). This branching—where a single Hermitian symmetry becomes two inequivalent non-Hermitian symmetry classes—is known as
symmetry ramification

27
. Let us now take the concrete example of a crystalline symmetry and see how its ramification affects the Wannier spectrum.

IV.1
Inversion symmetry as a similarity constraint

The most familiar definition of inversion symmetry (IS) is

h(k)=I_{s}h(-k)I_{s}^{-1},

(84)

where
I_{s}^{\dagger}=I_{s}
is the spatial inversion operator, which also obeys
I_{s}^{2}=\mathbb{I}_{N}
and has eigenvalues
\pm 1
. If
\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}
is a right energy eigenstate with eigenvalue
E_{k,n}
, we have

h(-k)I_{s}\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}=I_{s}h(k)\ket{\psi^{R}_{k,n}}=E_{k,n}I_{s}\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}},

(85)

so
I_{s}\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}
is also a right eigenstate at
-k
with the same eigenvalue
E_{k,n}
. Similarly,
I_{s}\ket{u^{L}_{k,n}}
is a left eigenstate of
h(-k)
. In Appendix
K
, we define a map between the
k
and
-k
eigenspaces through the sewing matrix

B^{s}_{k}=L^{\dagger}_{-k}I_{s}R_{k},

(86)

which is unitary due to the biorthonormality of
L_{k}
and
R_{k}
, and show that the Wilson loop obeys

\mathcal{W}_{k}=(B^{s}_{k})^{-1}\mathcal{W}_{-k}^{-1}B^{s}_{k},

(87)

and actually becomes
pseudo-involutory
at high-symmetry points (HSPs)
k_{I}=\{0,\pi\}
,

\mathcal{W}_{k_{I}}=(B^{s}_{k_{I}})^{-1}\mathcal{W}_{k_{I}}^{-1}B^{s}_{k_{I}}.

(88)

Since the sewing matrix is invertible,
\mathcal{W}_{k_{I}}
and
\mathcal{W}^{-1}_{{k_{I}}}
must share the same set of eigenvalues. Thus, the Wannier spectrum is symmetric under

\lambda\leftrightarrow 1/\lambda\Leftrightarrow e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}z}\leftrightarrow e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}z},

(89)

where
z=\nu+\mathrm{i}\kappa
are the complex Wannier centers. Such symmetry can manifest in two ways: (i) isolated real Wannier centers with
\kappa=0
and
\nu=\{0,\frac{1}{2}\}
, or (ii) inversion-related pairs
(\nu,\kappa)
and
(-\nu,-\kappa)
. Case (i) occurs when the Wilson loop at
k_{I}
commutes with the projected inversion operator
B^{s}_{k}
, so that its eigenvectors can be chosen as inversion eigenstates. In contrast, case (ii) arises when
B^{s}_{k_{I}}
and
\mathcal{W}_{k_{I}}
do not commute; in this situation inversion sends each Wilson loop eigenstate to a distinct partner, enforcing the paired structure
z\leftrightarrow-z

IV.2
Pseudo-inversion as a conjugate-type symmetry

We define pseudo-inversion symmetry (pIS) as

h(k)^{\dagger}=I_{c}h(-k)I_{c}^{-1},

where
I_{c}^{\dagger}=I_{c}

I_{c}^{2}=\mathbb{I}_{N}

IV.3
Combined effects of inversion and pseudo-inversion

After discussing how inversion (IS) and pseudo-inversion (pIS) separately constrain the biorthogonal Wilson loop and the Wannier spectrum, we now analyze
their combined action. In non-Hermitian Hamiltonians, IS and pIS arise from the
ramification of Hermitian inversion into two distinct symmetry types: a
similarity constraint acting within the right and left eigenspaces, and a
conjugation-type constraint relating right to left eigenvectors. Although these two involutory operators need not coincide, one may consider models in which both symmetries are present simultaneously and represented by distinct operators.

A natural question, then, is whether IS and pIS together impose additional
constraints on the Wilson loop beyond those enforced by each symmetry
individually. To address this, we first derive the algebraic relation that
follows whenever both symmetries hold. Inversion symmetry imposes the
similarity relation (
82
)
while pseudo-inversion imposes the conjugation condition (
83
). Combining the two yields

\displaystyle h(k)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=I_{c}\,h(-k)\,I_{c}^{-1}

\displaystyle=I_{c}\!\left(I_{s}h(k)I_{s}^{-1}\right)\!I_{c}^{-1}

\displaystyle=(I_{c}I_{s})\,h(k)\,(I_{c}I_{s})^{-1}.

(95)

IV.3.1
Commuting symmetries

If the IS ans pIS symmetry operators commute, the object

\eta\equiv I_{c}I_{s}

(96)

is a Hermitian and involutory operator,

\displaystyle\eta^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=(I_{s}I_{c})^{\dagger}=(I_{c})^{\dagger}I_{s}^{\dagger}=I_{c}I_{s}=I_{s}I_{c}=\eta,

\displaystyle\eta^{2}

\displaystyle=I_{s}I_{c}I_{s}I_{c}=I_{s}I_{c}^{2}I_{s}=\mathbb{I}_{N},

which, according to (
95
), implements pseudo-Hermiticity:

h(k)^{\dagger}=\eta\,h(k)\,\eta^{-1}.

(97)

We therefore obtain a set
\{I_{s},I_{c},\eta\}
of mutually commuting operators with eigenvalues
\pm 1
. At the HSPs
\{0,\pi\}
, they also commute with the Bloch Hamiltonian
h(k_{I})
so there is an energy eigenbasis
\{\ket{u}^{R}_{k,n},n=1,\dots,N_{\mathrm{occ}}\}
such that

\displaystyle\eta\ket{u^{R}_{k_{I},n}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k_{I}}\ket{u^{R}_{k_{I},n}},

\displaystyle I_{s}\ket{u^{R}_{k_{I},n}}

\displaystyle=\xi_{k_{I}}\ket{u^{R}_{k_{I},n}},

\displaystyle I_{c}\ket{u^{R}_{k_{I},n}}

\displaystyle=\chi_{k_{I}}\ket{u^{R}_{k_{I},n}},

where
\sigma_{k_{I}},\xi_{k_{I}},\chi_{k_{I}}\in\{-1,1\}
. In other words, the energy eigenstates at HSPs transform according to one-dimensional irreducible representations (irreps) of the three symmetry operators. Moreover, acting with (
96
) on
\ket{u^{R}_{k_{I}}}
yields a constraint between the eigenvalues of
\eta
,
I_{s}
, and
I_{c}
,

\sigma_{k_{I},n}=\xi_{k_{I}}\chi_{k_{I}}.

(98)

Thus, specifying the irreps of all three symmetries at every HSP is redundant: from now on, we adopt
\xi_{k_{I}}
and
\chi_{k_{I}}
as our independent indices.

\displaystyle[\xi]=\#\xi^{(+)}_{0}-\#\xi^{(+)}_{\pi},

is the number of

I_{s}
in the occupied subspace at the HSP

for

, and classify topological phases by

IV.3.2
Anticommuting symmetries

If the IS and pIS symmetry operators anticommute, then the operator
\eta
defined in (
96
) is anti-Hermitian and squares to minus one,

\displaystyle\eta^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=(I_{s}I_{c})^{\dagger}=(I_{c})^{\dagger}I_{s}^{\dagger}=I_{c}I_{s}=-I_{s}I_{c}=-\eta,

\displaystyle\eta^{2}

\displaystyle=I_{s}I_{c}I_{s}I_{c}=-I_{s}I_{c}^{2}I_{s}=-\mathbb{I}_{N}.

We therefore introduce

\tilde{\eta}\equiv\mathrm{i}\eta=\mathrm{i}I_{s}I_{c},

(100)

which
anticommutes
with
I_{s}
and
I_{c}
, satisfies
\tilde{\eta}^{\dagger}=\tilde{\eta}
and
\tilde{\eta}^{2}=\mathbb{I}_{N}
, and implements pseudo-Hermiticity,

h(k)^{\dagger}=\tilde{\eta}\,h(k)\,\tilde{\eta}^{-1}.

(101)

The three symmetry generators are thus Hermitian involutions that mutually anticommute,

A_{i}^{\dagger}=A_{i}
,
A_{i}^{2}=\mathbb{I}_{N}
, and
\{A_{i},A_{j}\}=0
for
i\neq j
,
with
A_{i}\in\{I_{s},I_{c},\tilde{\eta}\}
. They furnish a representation of the complex Clifford algebra

\{A_{i},A_{j}\}=2\delta_{ij}\mathbb{I}_{N}.

(102)

At inversion-invariant momenta
k_{I}\in\{0,\pi\}
the occupied subspace is invariant under the symmetry
actions, so the induced representation on the occupied subspace is well-defined. In a biorthonormal
occupied basis
\{|u^{R}_{k_{I},n}\rangle,\langle u^{L}_{k_{I},n}|\}_{n=1}^{N_{\rm occ}}
we define

[A_{i}^{\rm occ}(k_{I})]_{mn}\equiv\langle u^{L}_{k_{I},m}|\,A_{i}\,|u^{R}_{k_{I},n}\rangle.

(103)

These
N_{\rm occ}\times N_{\rm occ}
matrices satisfy the same Clifford relations

\{A_{i}^{\rm occ}(k_{I}),A_{j}^{\rm occ}(k_{I})\}=2\delta_{ij}\mathbb{I}_{N_{\rm occ}}
.

In contrast to the commuting case, the Clifford algebra admits no one-dimensional irreducible representations. The smallest representation therefore occurs at
N_{\rm occ}=2
, where the occupied subspace furnishes the unique two-dimensional irreducible representation. Up to a unitary change of basis one may choose

A_{i}^{\rm occ}(k_{I})=\sigma_{i},\qquad i=x,y,z.

(104)

In particular, the three symmetry generators cannot be simultaneously diagonalized, and the occupied subspace cannot be decomposed into independent symmetry sectors.

At high-symmetry momenta
k_{I}
, the Wilson loop
\mathcal{W}_{k_{I}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{k_{I}}

\displaystyle=(B^{c}_{k_{I}})^{-1}\mathcal{W}_{k_{I}}^{\dagger}B^{c}_{k_{I}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{k_{I}}

\displaystyle=(B^{s}_{k_{I}})^{-1}\mathcal{W}_{k_{I}}^{-1}B^{s}_{k_{I}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{k_{I}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=M_{k_{I}}^{-1}\mathcal{W}_{k_{I}}^{\dagger}M_{k_{I}},

(105)

as per Eqs. (
65
), (
87
), and (
94
), with sewing matrices acting within the same two-dimensional irreducible space. These relations imply that the Wilson-loop spectrum is invariant as a set under Hermitian conjugation and inversion,

\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{W}_{k_{I}})=\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{W}_{k_{I}}^{\dagger})=\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{W}_{k_{I}}^{-1}).

(106)

For
N_{\mathrm{occ}}=2

\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}^{*}
and
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{2}^{*}
, which implies

and therefore real eigenvalues; or the two eigenvalues are exchanged,
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}^{*}

and
\theta_{2}=-\theta_{1}
.

We now impose the second equality in (

\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\}=\{1/\lambda_{1},1/\lambda_{2}\}

. Writing

\lambda_{2}=e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}

In terms of the complex Wannier centers

(107)

the two possibilities become

\displaystyle\mathrm{(i)}:\quad\nu_{1}=\nu_{2}\in\{0,1/2\},\qquad\kappa_{1}=-\kappa_{2}\in\mathbb{R},

(108)

(109)

V
Discussion

We have shown necessary and sufficient conditions for biorthogonal Wilson loops to break unitarity, yielding complex Wannier centers in non-Hermitian bands. More importantly, we demonstrated that the imaginary parts of such complex Wannier centers are associated with an imbalance in the momentum-weight distribution of Wannier functions and often act as their effective average momenta. These momentum shifts lead to observable drifts of Wannier functions in real space, thus establishing a direct dynamical manifestation of complex Wannier centers and providing a practical route for probing non-Hermitian Wannier configurations. We expect that these dynamical features will affect transport, for example, in the adiabaticity limits in Thouless pumps.

In pseudo-Hermitian Hamiltonians, such as those in models (
79
) and (
45
), preparing localized Wannier functions and adiabatically tuning a parameter would reveal whether a pair of Wannier centers carries equal or opposite Krein signatures: only the latter undergo Krein collisions and acquire imaginary parts, producing a measurable drift, whereas the former remain stationary.
The relationship between non-Hermitian Wannier Hamiltonians with complex spectra and the edge Hamiltonians of these systems has remained largely unexplored. Here we show that, in a system with anticommuting pseudo-Hermiticity and (pseudo) inversion symmetries, the Krein structure of the Wilson loop establishes a bulk–boundary correspondence between complex Wannier centers and the spectrum of edge states. In addition, we present a possible experimental realization of that system in a well-known platform.

Wannier functions also govern the emergence and symmetry character of solitons bifurcating from linear bands in weakly nonlinear lattices
51
. This suggests an avenue to engineer topologically nonreciprocal solitons. Existing proposals rely on gain–loss mechanisms that generically introduce amplification or acceleration
14
;
32
;
18
;
69
;
36
;
37
;
60
. In contrast, solitons that inherit the profile of non-Hermitian Wannier functions would propagate unidirectionally with constant amplitude and velocity, offering a more robust, spectrally cleaner mechanism for realizing nonreciprocal wave packets.

Finally, by clarifying the structure and transitions of complex Wannier centers, this work lays the foundation for a systematic program of non-Hermitian Wannier topology. Our results indicate that symmetry constraints—–particularly pseudo-Hermiticity and inversion-type symmetries—–organize non-Hermitian bands into distinct Wannier symmetry classes with well-defined invariants, suggesting a non-Hermitian analogue of the Wannier-based classification familiar from Hermitian band theory
27
.

Appendix B
Gauge freedom in non-Hermitian Hamiltonians

Let
R_{k}
and
L_{k}
be the
N\times N_{\mathrm{occ}}
operators collecting the occupied right and left
Bloch eigenvectors, as defined in Eq. (
9

L_{k}^{\dagger}R_{k}=\mathbb{I}_{N_{\mathrm{occ}}}.

(124)

The choice of basis within the occupied subspace is not unique:
one may perform a
k

R_{k}\to R_{k}^{\prime}=R_{k}\,U_{k},\qquad L_{k}\to L_{k}^{\prime}=L_{k}\,(U_{k}^{-1})^{\dagger},

(125)

where
U_{k}\in GL(N_{\mathrm{occ}},\mathbb{C})
is an invertible matrix.
This transformation preserves biorthonormality,
since
L_{k}^{\prime\dagger}R_{k}^{\prime}=(U_{k}^{-1})(L_{k}^{\dagger}R_{k})U_{k}=\mathbb{I}_{N_{\mathrm{occ}}}

Importantly, the biorthogonal projector onto the occupied subspace,

P_{k}=R_{k}L_{k}^{\dagger},

(126)

P_{k}^{\prime}=R_{k}^{\prime}L_{k}^{\prime\dagger}=R_{k}U_{k}\left[(U_{k}^{-1})^{\dagger}\right]^{\dagger}L_{k}^{\dagger}=R_{k}U_{k}(U_{k}^{-1})L_{k}^{\dagger}=P_{k}.

(127)

B.1
Wilson line elements and Wilson loops

The biorthogonal Wilson line element connecting neighboring momenta is

G_{k}\equiv G_{k+\Delta,k}=L_{k+\Delta}^{\dagger}R_{k},

(128)

where
\Delta=2\pi/\ell
for a discretization of the Brillouin zone into
\ell
points.
Under the gauge transformation (
125

G_{k}
transforms as

G_{k}\to G_{k}^{\prime}=L_{k+\Delta}^{\prime\dagger}R_{k}^{\prime}=(U_{k+\Delta}^{-1})\,G_{k}\,U_{k},

(129)

i.e., it is gauge-covariant.

The biorthogonal Wilson loop based at
k
is defined as the ordered product

\mathcal{W}_{k}=\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}G_{k+j\Delta},

(130)

where the product is ordered along increasing momentum.
Using Eq. (
129
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}G_{k+j\Delta}^{\prime}=\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\left[(U_{k+(j+1)\Delta}^{-1})\,G_{k+j\Delta}\,U_{k+j\Delta}\right]

\displaystyle=U_{k+\ell\Delta}^{-1}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}G_{k+j\Delta}\right)U_{k}.

(131)

Since

corresponds to the same crystal momentum, we may choose a periodic gauge
such that
U_{k+2\pi}=U_{k}
, implying

\boxed{\mathcal{W}_{k}^{\prime}=U_{k}^{-1}\,\mathcal{W}_{k}\,U_{k}.}

(132)

Therefore, the Wilson loop transforms by similarity, and its eigenvalues

are gauge invariant.
Thus, the associated (possibly complex) Wannier centers

are also gauge-invariant quantities.

B.2
Gauge dependence of the Berry connection

In the continuum limit, the Wilson line element may be written as

G_{k}=\mathbb{I}-i\Delta A(k)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})
, where

A(k)=-iL_{k}^{\dagger}\partial_{k}R_{k}
is the biorthogonal Berry connection.
Under Eq. (
125

A(k)\to A^{\prime}(k)=U_{k}^{-1}A(k)U_{k}-iU_{k}^{-1}\partial_{k}U_{k}.

(133)

is gauge dependent pointwise, while the Wilson loop spectrum remains gauge invariant
as shown above.

Appendix C
Conditions for nonunitarity of the biorthogonal Wilson loop

Let
R_{k}

(L_{k})
be the operator defined in (
9
) containing the right (left) eigenvectors of the Bloch Hamiltonian
h_{k}
in the occupied bands. The biorthogonal Wilson line is defined as

G_{k+\Delta,k}=L^{\dagger}_{k+\Delta}R_{k},

(134)

where
\Delta=\frac{2\pi}{\ell}
. In the thermodynamic limit
\ell\to\infty
(
\Delta\to 0
), we have

G_{k+\Delta,k}=L^{\dagger}_{k}R_{k}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta)=\mathbb{I}_{N_{\mathrm{occ}}}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta),

(135)

which naively may induce one to think that the Wilson loop,

\mathcal{W}=\lim_{\Delta\to 0}\prod_{k}G_{k+\Delta,k},

(136)

is unitary. However, we shall now see that the
\mathcal{O}(\Delta)
terms in (
135
) give non-unitary contributions that remain finite in the thermodynamic limit. Concretely, we have

\displaystyle G_{k+\Delta,k}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}+\Delta(\partial_{k}L^{\dagger}_{k})R_{k}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}-\Delta(L^{\dagger}_{k}\partial_{k}R_{k})+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}-\mathrm{i}\Delta A_{k}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2}),

(137)

where
A_{k}=-\mathrm{i}L_{k}^{\dagger}\partial_{k}R_{k}
is the biorthogonal Berry connection. Thus, in the thermodynamic limit, the Wilson loop from
k
to
k+2\pi
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\int_{k}^{k+2\pi}A(k^{\prime})dk^{\prime}\right).

(138)

Therefore, we have

\frac{d\mathcal{W}_{k}}{dk}=-\mathrm{i}A_{k}\mathcal{W}_{k},

(139)

where we have used
A_{k+2\pi}=A_{k}
.

Now, consider
Q=\mathcal{W}_{k}^{\dagger}\mathcal{W}_{k}
. If
\mathcal{W}
is unitary, then
Q=\mathbb{I}_{N_{\mathrm{occ}}}
and thus
\frac{dQ}{dk}=0
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{dQ}{dk}

\displaystyle=\frac{d\mathcal{W}_{k}^{\dagger}}{dk}\mathcal{W}_{k}+\mathcal{W}_{k}^{\dagger}\frac{d\mathcal{W}_{k}}{dk}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\mathcal{W}_{k}^{\dagger}(A^{\dagger}_{k}-A_{k})\mathcal{W}_{k}

\displaystyle=-2\mathrm{i}\mathcal{W}_{k}^{\dagger}A^{(a)}_{k}\mathcal{W}_{k},

(140)

where

A^{(a)}_{k}=\frac{A_{k}-A^{\dagger}_{k}}{2}

(141)

is the anti-Hermitian part of the Berry connection. In particular, for Hermitian Hamiltonians, the Berry connection is also Hermitian, implying
\frac{dQ}{dk}=0
and a unitary Wilson loop. But this is still not a sufficient condition for the Wilson loop to be nonunitary. By using the formula

\frac{d}{dk}\log\det\mathcal{W}=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathcal{W}^{-1}\frac{d\mathcal{W}}{dk}\right]

(142)

and employing equation (
139
), we obtain

\frac{d}{dk}\log\det\mathcal{W}=\mathrm{i}\mathrm{Tr}A(k),

(143)

and

\log\det\mathcal{W}=\mathrm{i}\int_{k}^{k+2\pi}\mathrm{Tr}A(k^{\prime})dk^{\prime}.

(144)

Note that the expression above is gauge-invariant. Although
A(k)
transforms according to (
133
), the cyclic property of the trace yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}A^{\prime}(k)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}A(k)-\mathrm{i}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{-1}_{k}\partial_{k}U_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}A(k)-\partial_{k}\log\det U_{k},

(145)

so

\displaystyle\int_{k}^{k+2\pi}\mathrm{Tr}[A^{\prime}(k^{\prime})-A(k^{\prime})]dk^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\log\det U_{k+2\pi}-\log\det U_{k}

\displaystyle=0.

(146)

Now, noting that

\log\det\mathcal{W}=\log|\det\mathcal{W}|+\mathrm{i}\ \mathrm{arg}\det\mathrm{W},

(147)

and using (
144
), we have

\displaystyle\log|\det\mathcal{W}|

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\log\det\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\left[\mathrm{i}\int_{k}^{k+2\pi}\mathrm{Tr}A(k^{\prime})dk^{\prime}\right].

(148)

But since the imaginary component of the trace must come solely from the anti-Hermitian part and unitary matrices satisfy
|\det\mathcal{W}|=1
and
\log|\det\mathcal{W}|=0
, we see that the Wilson loop cannot be unitary if

\int_{k}^{k+2\pi}\mathrm{Tr}A^{(a)}(k^{\prime})dk^{\prime}\neq 0.

(149)

Appendix E
Inequivalence of projected-position and Bloch-Fourier definitions of Wannier functions in non-Hermitian Hamiltonians

In this Appendix, we prove that, in a generic non-Hermitian band, Wannier functions defined as eigenstates of the projected position operator cannot be written as equal-weight Bloch–Fourier superpositions of Bloch eigenstates.
The obstruction is a gauge-invariant non-uniform distribution of momentum-space weights.

Let
\ket{W_{x}}
be a projected-position Wannier function of a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, defined as an eigenstate of the projected position operator,

X_{p}\ket{W_{x}}=\lambda_{x}\ket{W_{x}},

where

(161)

where

is understood modulo the Brillouin zone.

Since

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\,\ket{k}\otimes\ket{w_{k}}

(162)

where
\ket{w_{k}}
is the orbital content of
\ket{W^{R}_{x}}

for the case of

Using Eqs. (

\displaystyle X_{p}\ket{W_{x}}

\displaystyle=P\sum_{k}a_{k}\,\ket{k+\Delta}\otimes\ket{u^{R}_{k}}

\displaystyle\langle u^{L}_{k}|u^{R}_{k-\Delta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u^{L}_{k}|u^{R}_{k}\rangle-\Delta\langle u^{L}_{k}|\partial_{k}u^{R}_{k}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})

where

Taking the absolute value then yields, to leading order in

\left|\langle u^{L}_{k}|u^{R}_{k-\Delta}\rangle\right|\approx\exp\!\left[\Delta\,\mathrm{Im}\,A_{k}\right].

Meanwhile, the eigenvalues of the projected position operator can be written as

not

k
when
\mathrm{Im}\,A(k)
is not uniform, implying that
\ket{W_{x}^{R}}
cannot be written as an equal-weight Fourier superposition of Bloch eigenstates.
Solving (
172

a(k)
yields

|a(k)|=|a(k_{0})|\exp\left[\int_{k_{0}}^{k}(\mathrm{Im}\,A(k^{\prime})-\kappa)\,dk^{\prime}\right].

(175)

In the normalized gauge
\|u^{R}_{k}\|^{2}=1
, the norm (or “weight”) of the orbital part
\ket{w_{k}}=a(k)\ket{u_{k}^{R}}
as a function of
k
is therefore

\displaystyle\|w_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=|a(k)|^{2}\cdot\|u^{R}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=|a(k_{0})|^{2}\exp\left[2\int_{k_{0}}^{k}(\mathrm{Im}\,A(k^{\prime})-\kappa)\,dk^{\prime}\right],

(176)

which is generically non-uniform in
k
.

At this point, one may wonder if the expression (
176
) is gauge invariant, since any rescaling of the eigenstates of the Bloch Hamiltonian

\ket{u^{\prime R}_{k}}=e^{f(k)}\ket{u^{R}_{k}},\quad\bra{u^{\prime L}_{k}}=e^{-f(k)}\bra{u^{L}_{k}},

(177)

for any periodic
f(k)\in\mathbb{C}

a_{k}
in (

a_{k}^{\prime}=e^{-f(k)}a_{k}.

(178)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|w_{k}^{\prime}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=|a_{k}^{\prime}|^{2}\cdot\|u^{\prime R}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=|e^{-f(k)}|^{2}|a_{k}|^{2}\cdot|e^{f(k)}|^{2}\|u^{R}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|w_{k}\|^{2}.

(179)

Therefore, the non-uniform “weight” distribution of the momentum components of projected-position Wannier functions of non-Hermitian Hamiltonians is gauge invariant.
Since
\|w_{k}\|^{2}
is non-uniform in the normalized gauge discussed above, it must be non-uniform in any gauge.
We thus conclude that such functions cannot be expressed in Bloch–Fourier form (
24
) under any choice of gauge.

Finally, let us re-express (

\displaystyle\|u_{k}^{R}\|^{2}=\|u_{k_{0}}^{R}\|^{2}\exp\left(\int_{k_{0}}^{k}\partial_{k^{\prime}}\log\|u^{R}_{k^{\prime}}\|^{2}\,dk^{\prime}\right).

(180)

Therefore, (
176

\displaystyle\|w_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=|a(k_{0})|^{2}\|u_{k}^{R}\|^{2}\exp\left[2\int_{k_{0}}^{k}(\mathrm{Im}\,A(k^{\prime})-\kappa)\,dk^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=|a(k_{0})|^{2}\|u_{k_{0}}^{R}\|^{2}\exp\Big[2\int_{k_{0}}^{k}\Big(\mathrm{Im}\,A(k^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k^{\prime}}\log\|u^{R}_{k^{\prime}}\|^{2}-\kappa\Big)\,dk^{\prime}\Big]

\displaystyle=\|w_{k_{0}}\|^{2}\exp\left[2\int_{k_{0}}^{k}(\Gamma(k^{\prime})-\kappa)dk^{\prime}\right],

(181)

where
\|w_{k_{0}}\|^{2}=|a(k_{0})|^{2}\|u_{k_{0}}\|^{2}
and

\Gamma(k)=\mathrm{Im}\,A(k)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k}\log\|u^{R}_{k}\|^{2}

(182)

are gauge-invariant quantities, as we prove below.

Under the non-unitary gauge transformation (
177

A(k)=-\mathrm{i}\langle u_{k}^{L}|\partial_{k}u_{k}^{R}\rangle
transforms as

\displaystyle A^{\prime}(k)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\langle u_{k}^{L}e^{-f(k)}|\partial_{k}\!\left(e^{f(k)}u_{k}^{R}\right)\rangle=A(k)-\mathrm{i}\,\partial_{k}f(k),

(183)

(184)

Moreover,

transforms as

\|u_{k}^{R\,\prime}\|^{2}=e^{2\mathrm{Re}\,f(k)}\|u_{k}^{R}\|^{2}
, implying

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k}\log\|u_{k}^{R\,\prime}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k}\log\|u_{k}^{R}\|^{2}+\partial_{k}\mathrm{Re}\,f(k).

(185)

Combining Eqs. (
184
) and (
185

\Gamma(k)\equiv\mathrm{Im}\,A(k)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k}\log\|u_{k}^{R}\|^{2}

is gauge invariant:
\Gamma^{\prime}(k)=\Gamma(k)
.
Finally, in the normalized gauge
\|u_{k}^{R}\|^{2}=1
one has
\Gamma(k)=\mathrm{Im}\,A(k)
, and hence

\overline{\Gamma}\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\Gamma(k)\,dk=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\mathrm{Im}\,A(k)\,dk\equiv\kappa.

(187)

Appendix F
Effect of symmetries on the momentum-weight distributions of Wannier functions

In this appendix we analyze how linear symmetries that map
k\to-k
constrain the biorthogonal Berry connection

A(k)=-\mathrm{i}\bra{u^{L}_{k}}\partial_{k}\ket{u^{R}_{k}},

(194)

and consequently the gauge-invariant quantity

\Gamma(k)=\mathrm{Im}A(k)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k}\log\|u^{R}_{k}\|^{2},

(195)

which controls the
k
-space distribution of projected-position Wannier functions.
We restrict to a single isolated and nondegenerate band.

F.1
Similarity-Type Linear Symmetry

Consider a Bloch Hamiltonian satisfying

h(k)=Sh(-k)S^{-1},

(196)

where
S
is a
k
-independent unitary operator.

Let
\ket{u_{k}^{R}}
be a right eigenvector,

h(k)\ket{u_{k}^{R}}=E(k)\ket{u_{k}^{R}}.

(197)

Using the symmetry,
h(k)S=Sh(-k)
, we find

h(k)(S\ket{u_{-k}^{R}})=Sh(-k)\ket{u_{-k}^{R}}=E(-k)(S\ket{u_{-k}^{R}}).

(198)

Since the band is nondegenerate and isolated,
E(-k)=E(k)
and therefore

\ket{u_{k}^{R}}=e^{f(k)}S\ket{u_{-k}^{R}},

(199)

with
f(k)\in\mathbb{C}
.

A similar argument for left eigenvectors yields

\bra{u_{k}^{L}}=\bra{u_{-k}^{L}}S^{-1}e^{-f(k)},

(200)

where biorthonormality enforces the inverse exponent.

Berry Connection Transformation

We compute

\displaystyle A(k)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\bra{u_{k}^{L}}\partial_{k}\ket{u_{k}^{R}}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\bra{u_{-k}^{L}}S^{-1}e^{-f(k)}\partial_{k}\!\left[e^{f(k)}S\ket{u_{-k}^{R}}\right].

(201)

Since
S
is
k
-independent,

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\!\left[e^{f(k)}S\ket{u_{-k}^{R}}\right]

\displaystyle=(\partial_{k}f(k))e^{f(k)}S\ket{u_{-k}^{R}}+e^{f(k)}S\,\partial_{k}\ket{u_{-k}^{R}}.

(202)

Using biorthonormality
\braket{u_{-k}^{L}|u_{-k}^{R}}=1
, we obtain

A(k)=-\mathrm{i}\partial_{k}f(k)-\mathrm{i}\bra{u_{-k}^{L}}\partial_{k}\ket{u_{-k}^{R}}.

(203)

By definition,

A(-k)=-\mathrm{i}\bra{u_{-k}^{L}}\partial_{(-k)}\ket{u_{-k}^{R}}.

(204)

Since
\partial_{(-k)}=-\partial_{k}
, we have

A(-k)=+\mathrm{i}\bra{u_{-k}^{L}}\partial_{k}\ket{u_{-k}^{R}}.

(205)

Therefore,

\boxed{A(-k)=-A(k)+\mathrm{i}\partial_{k}f(k)}.

(206)

Taking imaginary parts,

\boxed{\mathrm{Im}A(-k)=-\mathrm{Im}A(k)+\partial_{k}\mathrm{Re}f(k)}.

(207)

Norm Transformation

From the sewing relation,

\|u_{-k}^{R}\|^{2}=e^{2\mathrm{Re}f(k)}\|u_{k}^{R}\|^{2}.

(208)

Taking logarithms and differentiating with respect to
k
,

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k}\log\|u_{-k}^{R}\|^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k}\log\|u_{k}^{R}\|^{2}-\partial_{k}\mathrm{Re}f(k).

(209)

Transformation of
\Gamma(k)

Combining the previous results,

\displaystyle\Gamma(-k)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Im}A(-k)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k}\log\|u_{-k}^{R}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\Gamma(k).

(210)

Thus,

\boxed{\Gamma(-k)=-\Gamma(k)}.

(211)

It follows that

\overline{\Gamma}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}dk\,\Gamma(k)=0,

(212)

and therefore

\|w_{k}\|^{2}=\|w_{-k}\|^{2}.

(213)

Unitary similarity (inversion-type) symmetry forbids nonreciprocal drift in a single-band system.

F.2
Linear Conjugation Symmetry

Now consider a symmetry of the form

Sh(k)S^{-1}=h^{\dagger}(-k),

(214)

with
S
unitary and
k
-independent. Acting on a right eigenvector,

h^{\dagger}(-k)\,S\ket{u_{k}^{R}}=E(k)\,S\ket{u_{k}^{R}}.

(215)

Thus
S\ket{u_{k}^{R}}
is proportional to a left eigenvector at
-k
,

\ket{u_{-k}^{L}}=e^{f(k)}S\ket{u_{k}^{R}}.

(216)

The symmetry therefore maps right and left eigenvectors onto one another. A direct computation yields

A(-k)=A(k)^{*}+\mathrm{i}\partial_{k}f(k),

(217)

so that

\boxed{\mathrm{Im}A(-k)=\mathrm{Im}A(k)+\partial_{k}\mathrm{Re}f(k)}.

(218)

Combining with the norm contribution (
209
) gives

\boxed{\Gamma(-k)=\Gamma(k)}.

(219)

Thus
\Gamma(k)
is generically even and its Brillouin-zone average need not vanish. In contrast to the similarity case, this symmetry enforces an asymmetric momentum distribution

\|w_{-k}\|^{2}\neq\|w_{k}\|^{2}

(220)

whenever
\Gamma(k)
has a nontrivial dependence in
k
.

Summary

For a single isolated band:

•

A unitary similarity symmetry
Sh(k)S^{-1}=h(-k)
enforces
\Gamma
odd and forbids nonreciprocal drift.

•

A linear conjugation symmetry
Sh(k)S^{-1}=h^{\dagger}(-k)
makes
\Gamma
even and allows nonzero drift.

Appendix G
Drift velocity of wave packets in non-Hermitian Hamiltonians with real energies

Consider the state

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(0)}

\displaystyle=\int dk\ket{k}\otimes\ket{w_{k}}

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(t)}

[I,h(\boldsymbol{k}_{I})]=0
, i.e., the Bloch Hamiltonian commutes with the inversion operator. As a result, the energy eigenstates at HSPs can be chosen to be simultaneous eigenstates of the inversion operator. Thus, for the sewing matrix at
{\boldsymbol{k}}_{I}
, we have

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\bra{w_{\boldsymbol{k}_{I}}^{m}}h(\boldsymbol{k}_{I})I-Ih(\boldsymbol{k}_{I})\ket{w_{\boldsymbol{k}_{I}}^{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}_{I}}^{m}-\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}_{I}}^{n})\bra{w_{\boldsymbol{k}_{I}}^{m}}I\ket{w_{\boldsymbol{k}_{I}}^{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}_{I}}^{m}-\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}_{I}}^{n})[B_{\boldsymbol{k}_{I}}]^{mn},

(256)

i.e.,
B_{\boldsymbol{k}_{I}}
is diagonal over non-degenerate eigenstates and has eigenvalues
\pm 1
.

Ref.
1
shows that the irreducible representations (irreps) of inversion symmetry for the occupied bands at all inversion-invariant momenta
\boldsymbol{k}_{I}
—equivalently, the eigenvalues of
B_{\boldsymbol{k}_{I}}
—can be used to determine the corresponding Wannier centers, which we now reproduce for completeness. The results are summarized in Tables

\end{array}\right),

(

K.2
Inversion symmetry for NH Hamiltonians

The similarity-type branch of inversion symmetry for NH Hamiltonians is defined as

h(k)=I_{s}h(-k)I_{s}^{-1},

(262)

where
I_{s}^{\dagger}=I_{s}
and
I_{s}^{2}=\mathbb{I}
. If
\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}
is an energy eigenstate with eigenvalue
E_{k,n}
, we have

h(-k)I_{s}\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}=I_{s}h(k)\ket{\psi^{R}_{k,n}}=E_{k,n}I_{s}\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}},

(263)

so
I_{s}\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}
is a right eigenstate at
-k
with the same eigenvalue
E_{k,n}
. Now, consider the projector onto the occupied subspace associated with momentum
-k
,

P_{1,-k}=R_{-k}L_{-k}^{\dagger}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{occ}}}\ket{u^{R}_{-k,n}}\bra{u^{L}_{-k,n}}.

(264)

Since
I_{s}\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}
lies in the subspace spanned by
\ket{u^{R}_{-k,n}}
, the object in (
264
) acts trivially on
I_{s}R_{k}
:

\displaystyle I_{s}R_{k}

\displaystyle=R_{-k}L_{-k}^{\dagger}I_{s}R_{k}

\displaystyle=R_{-k}B^{s}_{k},

(265)

where we have defined the
sewing matrix

B^{s}_{k}\equiv L_{-k}^{\dagger}I_{s}R_{k}.

(266)

If
\ket{u^{L}_{k,n}}
is an left eigevector of
h(k)
with eigenvalue
E_{k,n}
we have

\bra{u^{L}_{k,n}}I_{s}h(-k)=\bra{u^{L}_{k,n}}h(-k)I_{s}=E^{*}_{k,n}\bra{u^{L}_{k,n}}I_{s},

(267)

so
I_{s}\bra{u^{L}_{k,n}}
lies in the subspace spanned by
\bra{u^{L}_{-k,n}}
. Therefore, we may also obtain a sewing relation for the left eigenstates:

\displaystyle L^{\dagger}_{k}I_{s}

\displaystyle=L^{\dagger}_{k}I_{s}R_{-k}L_{-k}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=B^{s}_{-k}L_{-k}^{\dagger}.

(268)

Now, acting with
I_{s}
on (
265
) and using
I_{s}^{2}=\mathbb{I}
, we get

\displaystyle R_{k}

\displaystyle=I_{s}^{2}R_{k}

\displaystyle=I_{s}R_{-k}B^{s}_{k}.

(269)

But we know, also from (
265
), that
I_{s}R_{-k}=R_{k}B^{s}_{-k}
, so

\displaystyle R_{k}=R_{k}B^{s}_{-k}B^{s}_{k}.

(270)

Multiplying both sides by
L_{k}^{\dagger}
and using the biorthonormality condition
L_{k}^{\dagger}R_{k}=\mathbb{I}
yields

B^{s}_{-k}B^{s}_{k}\Leftrightarrow(B^{s}_{k})^{-1}=B^{s}_{-k}.

(271)

Using
I_{s}^{2}=\mathbb{I}
and the sewing relations (
265
) and (
268
) allow us to express the biorthogonal Wilson line element (
10
) as

\displaystyle G_{k}

\displaystyle=(L_{k+\Delta}^{\dagger}I_{s})(I_{s}R_{k})

\displaystyle=(B^{s}_{-k-\Delta}L^{\dagger}_{-k-\Delta})(R_{-k}B^{s}_{k})

\displaystyle=(B^{s}_{k+\Delta})^{-1}G^{-1}_{-k}B^{s}_{k}.

(272)

Therefore, the Wilson loop becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{k}

\displaystyle=G_{k}G_{k+\Delta}\cdots G_{k+2\pi-\Delta}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}\big[(B^{s}_{k_{j}+\Delta})^{-1}G^{-1}_{-k_{j}}B^{s}_{k_{j}}\big]

\displaystyle=(B^{s}_{k+2\pi})^{-1}\big[G_{-k}G_{-(k+\Delta)}\cdots G_{-(k+2\pi-\Delta)}\big]^{-1}B^{s}_{k}

\displaystyle=(B^{s}_{k})^{-1}\,\mathcal{W}_{-k}^{-1}\,B^{s}_{k}=B^{s}_{-k}\,\mathcal{W}_{-k}^{-1}\,B^{s}_{k},

(273)

that is, it is pseudo-involutory at HSPs.

Now, let us investigate how the possible symmetry representations at HPSs constrain the Wannier for two occupied bands.

K.3
Pseudo-inversion symmetry for NH Hamiltonians

We define pseudo-inversion symmetry as

h(k)^{\dagger}=I_{c}h(-k)I_{c}^{-1},

(279)

where again
I_{s}^{\dagger}=I_{c}
and
I_{c}^{2}=\mathbb{I}_{N}
. If
\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}
is a right eigenvector of
h(k)
, then

h(-k)^{\dagger}I_{c}\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}=I_{c}h(k)\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}=E_{k,n}I_{c}\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}},

(280)

so that
I_{c}\ket{u^{R}_{k,n}}
is a right eigenstate of
h(-k)^{\dagger}
with eigenvalue
E_{k,n}
, and therefore a left eigestate of
h(-k)
with eigenvalue
E^{*}_{k,n}
. Therefore,
I_{c}R_{k}
lies in the subspace spanned by
L_{-k}
. For that reason, the projector

P_{2,-k}=L_{-k}R_{-k}^{\dagger}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{occ}}}\ket{u^{L}_{-k,n}}\bra{u^{R}_{-k,n}}

(281)

acts trivially on
I_{c}R_{k}
. Hence,

\displaystyle I_{c}R_{k}

\displaystyle=L_{-k}R_{-k}^{\dagger}I_{c}R_{k}

\displaystyle=L_{-k}B^{c}_{k},

(282)

where we have defined the sewing matrix

B^{c}_{k}=R_{-k}^{\dagger}I_{c}R_{k}.

(283)

Similarly, one can show that
I_{c}L_{k}
lies in the subspace spanned by
R_{-k}
, so the projector

P_{1,-k}=R_{-k}L_{-k}^{\dagger}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{occ}}}\ket{u^{R}_{-k,n}}\bra{u^{L}_{-k,n}}

(284)

acts trivially on
I_{c}L_{k}
. Hence,

\displaystyle I_{c}L_{k}

\displaystyle=R_{-k}L_{-k}^{\dagger}I_{c}L_{k}

\displaystyle=R_{-k}\tilde{B}^{c}_{-k},

(285)

where

\tilde{B}^{c}_{-k}=L_{-k}^{\dagger}I_{c}L_{k}.

(286)

Using (
283
), (
286
) and
I_{c}^{2}=\mathbb{I}_{N}
, we apply
I_{c}
twice to
R_{k}
:

\displaystyle R_{k}

\displaystyle=I_{c}^{2}R_{k}

\displaystyle=I_{c}(I_{c}R_{k})

\displaystyle=I_{c}(L_{-k}B^{c}_{k})

\displaystyle=(I_{c}L_{-k})B^{c}_{k}

\displaystyle=(R_{k}\tilde{B}^{c}_{k})B^{c}_{k}

\displaystyle=R_{k}\tilde{B}^{c}_{k}B^{c}_{k}.

(287)

Left-multiplying by
L_{k}^{\dagger}
and using biorthonormality
L_{k}^{\dagger}R_{k}=\mathbb{I}_{N_{\mathrm{occ}}}
, we obtain

\mathbb{I}_{N_{\mathrm{occ}}}=L_{k}^{\dagger}R_{k}=L_{k}^{\dagger}R_{k}\tilde{B}^{c}_{k}B^{c}_{k}=\tilde{B}^{c}_{k}B^{c}_{k}.

(288)

Thus
\tilde{B}^{c}_{k}
is the (left) inverse of
B^{c}_{k}
on the occupied subspace. Since these are finite-dimensional matrices, the left inverse coincides with the right inverse, and we conclude

\tilde{B}^{c}_{k}=\big(B^{c}_{k}\big)^{-1}.

(289)

Now, we can write the biorthogonal Wilson line element (
10
) as

\displaystyle G_{k}

\displaystyle=(L^{\dagger}_{k+\Delta}I_{c})(I_{c}R_{k})

\displaystyle=(B_{k+\Delta})^{-1}r_{-k-\Delta}L_{-k}B^{c}_{k}

\displaystyle=(B_{k+\Delta})^{-1}G^{\dagger}_{-k-\Delta}B^{c}_{k}.

(290)

Therefore, the Wilson line from
k_{i}
to
k_{f}
as defined in (
13
) becomes

\displaystyle W_{k_{f}\leftarrow k_{i}}

\displaystyle=(B^{c})_{k_{f}}^{-1}W^{\dagger}_{-k_{f}\leftarrow-k_{i}-\Delta}B^{c}_{k_{i}}

\displaystyle\approx(B^{c})_{k_{f}}^{-1}W^{\dagger}_{-k_{f}\leftarrow-k_{i}}B^{c}_{k_{i}}

(291)

in the thermodynamic limit
\Delta\to 0
. Let the Wilson line sweep the entire BZ, and we obtain the following relation for the Wilson loop,

\mathcal{W}_{k}=(B^{c}_{k})^{-1}W^{\dagger}_{-k}B^{c}_{k},

(292)

so it is pseudo-Hermitian at HSPs.
