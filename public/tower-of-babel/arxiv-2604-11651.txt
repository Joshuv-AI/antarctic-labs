The Borsuk number of a graph
José Cáceres, Delia Garijo, Alberto Márquez, Rodrigo I. Silveira

Abstract
The Borsuk problem asks for the smallest number of subsets with strictly smaller diameters into which any bounded set in the d d -dimensional space can be decomposed. It is a classical problem in combinatorial geometry that has been subject of much attention over the years, and research on variants of the problem continues nowadays in a plethora of directions. In this work, we propose a formulation of the problem in the context of graphs. Depending on how the graph is partitioned, we consider two different settings dealing either with the usual notion of diameter in abstract graphs, or with the diameter in the context of continuous graphs, where all points along the edges, instead of only the vertices, must be taken into account when computing distances. We present complexity results, exact computations and upper bounds on the parameters associated to the problem.

This problem is especially challenging not only in the continuous setting (where the new connections may join any two points along the edges of the graph) but also in the discrete case (where the connections are new edges connecting two vertices.) Our results.

After introducing the required definitions and notation in Section
2
, we define the two variants of Borsuk number of a graph: the continuous variant (Section
3.1
) and the discrete variant (Section
3.2
). In analogy with Borsuk’s original problem, in both subsections, we discuss whether the Borsuk number can be upper bounded by a constant. Although this question remains open in the continuous setting, we make some non-trivial progress, presenting an upper bound that is linear on the number of vertices of the graph. In the discrete case, we construct examples of graphs with linear Borsuk number (in the number of vertices.)

Section
4
addresses complexity questions. We prove the NP-completeness of the problem of deciding whether the Borsuk number of a graph is below a given threshold, in both the discrete and the continuous setting. Our study relates Borsuk’s problem in graphs to other well-known problems in graph theory and discrete geometry: the

problem.

In Section

large class of continuous geometric graphs that satisfy a natural property of monotonicity with respect to the intersecting lines. Our main result establishes a non-trivial upper bound for their Borsuk number which, informally, depends on the structure of shortest paths of a continuous geomeric graph.

Section

We conclude in Section
7

2.1
Diameter: discrete and continuous variants

A
path

P
in a graph is a sequence of vertices and edges, where each edge connects two consecutive vertices in the sequence. The
length
of the path,
|P|
, is the sum of the lengths of its edges. Notice that, while in a geometric graph, the length–edges are determined by the Euclidean distance between the segments endpoints, in an abstract graph, all length–edges are equal to
1
.
The
distance
between two vertices
u,v
in a graph
G=(V,E)
, denoted by
d_{G}(u,v)
, is the length of a shortest path connecting them; we use
d_{T}(u,v)
when the graph is a tree
T
.
The
diameter
of
G
is defined as

{\rm diam}(G):=\max_{u,v\in V}d_{G}(u,v)
.

The concepts of path and distance naturally extend from a geometric graph
G
to a continuous geometric graph
\mathcal{G}
. In the continuous setting, paths are defined not only by edges, but also by at most two fragments of edges (which are also segments with their usual metric
and measure). For points
p,q\in\mathcal{G}
, we use
pq

p,q
, and
d_{\mathcal{G}}(p,q)
to refer to their distance (
d_{\mathcal{T}}(p,q)
for continuous geometric trees
\mathcal{T}
).
The distance between the two endpoints of an edge
e
is
|e|
. Thus, if two points
p=(uv,u,\lambda)
and
q=(uv,u,\lambda^{\prime})
lie on the same edge
uv
, then
d_{\mathcal{G}}(p,q)=|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|
.

The
diameter

{\rm diam}(\mathcal{G}):=\max_{p,q\in\mathcal{G}}d_{\mathcal{G}}(p,q)

Notice that the notation

In contrast, in the continuous case, the diameter can increase after an edge insertion.
This is because, in continuous graphs, all points on the new edge participate in the diameter computation, thus the inserted edge can contain a point whose farthest point is at a larger distance than the original diameter.

Definitions of Borsuk number

3.1
Continuous variant

We partition a continuous geometric graph
\mathcal{G}
by a sequence of cuts with straight lines.

A line
\ell
naturally divides
\mathcal{G}
into subgraphs.
However, by cutting one connected graph along
\ell
we may obtain multiple subgraphs (see Figure
4
), thus distorting the core idea of Borsuk’s original problem. To remain in the same spirit, our aim would be that each cut in a connected graph produces two connected subgraphs.
To guarantee this, after cutting we add to the subgraphs obtained in each halfplane (determined by
\ell
) the longest segment in
\ell
that has its endpoints in
\mathcal{G}\cup\ell
; this maximal segment is denoted by

s_{\ell}
.
In this way, the partition results in exactly two connected geometric subgraphs of
\mathcal{G}\cup\ell
, which are defined as:

\mathcal{G}_{\ell}^{+}:=(\ell^{+}\cap\mathcal{G})\cup s_{\ell}\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathcal{G}_{\ell}^{-}:=(\ell^{-}\cap\mathcal{G})\cup s_{\ell},

where
\ell^{+}
and
\ell^{-}
are, respectively, the open half-planes above and below
\ell
(right and left when the line is vertical). We also insert in both subgraphs,
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}^{+}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}^{-}
, a new vertex at each intersection point of
\ell
with
\mathcal{G}
. Figure
4
illustrates the partition method by a line
\ell
.

In addition to preserving the original idea of Borsuk’s problem, our partitioning method has natural applications in network design: given a road network, the goal would be to construct a new road (crossing it) to decrease the maximum distance in each of the resulting parts (compared to the original network); the new road would be our segment
s_{\ell}
.

An ordered set of lines
(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{k})
partitions a graph
\mathcal{G}
sequentially: each line
\ell_{i}
is inserted only into one of the existing subgraphs, producing two new subgraphs of the types
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}^{+}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}^{-}
. At the end of the process, one obtains
k+1
geometric subgraphs of
\mathcal{G}\cup\ell_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\ell_{k}
. To simplify notation, we denote them by
\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{k+1}
, omitting the subgraph that has been partitioned at that stage of the process, and the line used to partition.

\mathcal{G}
by a line
\ell

\mathcal{G}_{\ell}^{+}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}^{-}
(segment
s_{\ell}

We define the
Borsuk number of
\mathcal{G}
, and denote it by
b(\mathcal{G})
, as the minimum cardinality of a partition of
\mathcal{G}
by an ordered set of lines
(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{k})
into subgraphs
\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{k+1}
(of
\mathcal{G}\cup\ell_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\ell_{k})
such that:

\max\{{\rm diam}(\mathcal{G}_{1}),\ldots,{\rm diam}(\mathcal{G}_{k+1})\}<{\rm diam}(\mathcal{G}).

Figure
5

5

\ell
through its center point, we obtain two subgraphs (of
\mathcal{G}\cup\ell
)
that have a diameter smaller than that of
\mathcal{G}
, and so
b(\mathcal{G})=2
(best possible).
The example in Figure
5
b shows a 4-star graph, which requires at least two lines to decrease the diameter of the original graph, giving Borsuk number three.

Note that the definition of Borsuk number (in this continuous setting) does not require the graph
\mathcal{G}
to be connected. For a disconnected
\mathcal{G}
, which has infinite diameter, any partition by lines must in fact connect the components in order to reduce the diameter of the original graph to a finite value. In Section
4
, we use a construction with a disconnected
\mathcal{G}
to address a complexity question, but in the remainder of the paper we assume that
\mathcal{G}
is connected, as this is a natural property of the problem.

We shall use the term
correct partition
to refer to a partition of a graph in which all resulting subgraphs indeed have a smaller diameter than that of the original graph.

As Borsuk’s conjecture holds for bounded sets in the plane, that is,
b(X)\leq 3
for any bounded set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, one of the main open questions in this continuous setting is whether
b(\mathcal{G})
can be upper bounded by a constant. The following proposition constitutes a first answer to this question, giving an upper bound that is linear on the number of vertices of
\mathcal{G}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Given a continuous geometric graph
\mathcal{G}
with
n\geq 3
vertices,
b(\mathcal{G})\leq n-1
.

Proof.

Consider a direction that is not parallel to any of the edges of
\mathcal{G}
; assume, for simplicity, that this is the vertical direction.
We divide
\mathcal{G}
by
n
vertical lines, one through each vertex, to create
n-1
graphs, denoted by
\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{n-1}
. Thus, there are
n-1
vertical strips, each containing two vertices of the original graph, and all edges go from the initial vertical line defining the strip to the other vertical line of the strip.

We are going to prove that in each
\mathcal{G}_{i}
, there are two points of the original graph realizing the diameter of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
, and that their distance is smaller than the diameter of
\mathcal{G}
.

Consider a graph
\mathcal{G}_{i}
, and a diametral pair of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
formed by
p
and
q
.

If both
p
and
q
are part of
\mathcal{G}
, either they belong to the same edge of
\mathcal{G}
or to two different edges.
If they lie on the same edge, since
n\geq 3
, their distance must be smaller than
{\rm diam}(\mathcal{G})
.
Otherwise, their distance in
\mathcal{G}_{i}
is smaller than their distance in
\mathcal{G}
, because the vertical edges of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
do not exist in
\mathcal{G}
.
Therefore, we have that
d_{\mathcal{G}_{i}}(p,q)
is strictly smaller than
{\rm diam}(\mathcal{G})
.

Otherwise, at least one of
p
or
q
lies on one of the two vertical lines defining
\mathcal{G}_{i}
.

Since there are no vertices in the interior of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
, the shortest path between
p
and
q
is not unique: there must exist two shortest paths between
p
and
q
such that the union of both paths is a trapezoid (with the parallel edges being segments of the vertical lines) or a triangle (with one vertical side).
In the following, refer to Figure
6
.

In the case of the trapezoid, consider two diagonally opposite vertices of the trapezoid,
p^{\prime}
and
q^{\prime}
, and the longest path along that trapezoid joining them.
That path contains a vertical segment, that without loss of generality we assume that starts at
p^{\prime}
.
Then, if
p^{\prime\prime}
is the diametral point to
p^{\prime}
in the trapezoid, it is straightforward to see that
p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
, and that
d_{\mathcal{G}_{i}}(p,q)=d_{\mathcal{G}_{i}}(p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime})
. But

d_{\mathcal{G}}(p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime})>d_{\mathcal{G}_{i}}(p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime})
.

In the case of the triangle, let
u,p,v
be its vertices.
Assume without loss of generality that
u
and
p
lie on the same vertical line bounding
\mathcal{G}_{i}
, thus
v
lies on the other line—and must be a vertex of
\mathcal{G}
.
The diameter of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
is given by any point on this triangle and its corresponding diametral point, also on the triangle.
Consider
u
and its diametral point
u^{\prime}
.
Now, consider a point
u^{\prime\prime}
very close to
u^{\prime}
, on the same edge as
u^{\prime}
, such that the shortest path in
\mathcal{G}_{i}
between
u
and
u^{\prime\prime}
goes through
p
(and the vertical segment of the triangle).
Both
u
and
u^{\prime\prime}
exist in
\mathcal{G}
, but their distance is larger there, since the vertical segment does not exist in
\mathcal{G}
.
Then,
{\rm diam}(\mathcal{G}_{i})=d_{\mathcal{G}_{i}}(u,u^{\prime})<d_{\mathcal{G}}(u,u^{\prime\prime})\leq{\rm diam}(\mathcal{G})
.
∎

3.2
Discrete variant

We now consider partitions of a connected abstract graph
G
by deleting edges; here all edges have the same length, equal to 1. (We do not deal with the case of deleting vertices as our interest relies on the distances between vertices of the original graph.)

Edge deletion is a fundamental operation in graph theory. As in the continuous variant, this partitioning method preserves the core idea of Borsuk’s original problem: by deleting one edge, we generate at most two subgraphs. In addition, there are multiple applications related with edge deletion; for example, this operation can be seen as removing some connection between the individuals in a social network, where the distance is a key parameter to measure the behavior of the network.

We define the
Borsuk number of a (connected abstract) graph
G
, and denote it by
b(G)
, as the minimum cardinality of a partition of
G
(by deleting edges) into subgraphs
G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}
(of
G
) such that:

\max\{{\rm diam}(G_{1}),\ldots,{\rm diam}(G_{k})\}<{\rm diam}(G).

Recall that, in the discrete variant, the diameter of a graph is the maximum distance between any two vertices.

The following observation exhibits some simple examples.

Observation 3.2
.

(i)

If
G
is a path or a cycle of even length,
b(G)=2
.

(ii)

If
G
is a cycle of odd length,
b(G)=3
.

(iii)

If
G
is a star graph on
k+1
vertices,
b(G)=k
.

Proof.

For paths and cycles of even length it suffices to remove, respectively, one and two edges to obtain two subgraphs with a diameter smaller than the original graph, and so the Borsuk number is
2
. If we delete only two edges in a cycle of odd length, one of the resulting subgraphs will have the same diameter as
G
; this forces to delete three edges giving rise to three subgraphs, leading to Borsuk number
3
.
To decrease the diameter of a star, one needs to remove all but one edge, thus for a star with
k+1
vertices, this generates a partition into
k
subgraphs, leading to Borsuk number
k
.
∎

In Section
6
, we study the Borsuk number of an arbitrary tree, in both the discrete and the continuous settings. We show that while
b(\mathcal{T})
is upper bounded by a constant for any continuous geometric tree
\mathcal{T}
, in the discrete case, the Borsuk number can be linear in the number of vertices of the graph, as it happens for the star. This linearity also occurs in other families of graphs, such as unicycle graphs (which comes from the scenario in trees) and maximal outerplanar graphs that are not trees. Figure
7
shows examples of the latter case: the graph on the left has diameter two, so the connected components obtained after removing the edges must have either diameter one or zero; this implies the deletion of at least
n-2+\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor
edges for a fan with
n+1
vertices, resulting in Borsuk number
\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
. This argument can be adapted for maximal outerplanar graphs with diameter larger than two, such as the example illustrated in Figure
7
(right).

4
Complexity

In this section, we prove that deciding whether the Borsuk number of a graph is below a given threshold is an NP-complete problem, in both the discrete and the continuous variants.

In the discrete setting, this problem is related to the
minimum clique cover problem
.
A
clique cover
of a graph
G
is a partition of its vertex set into cliques (i.e., subsets of vertices that induce a complete graph.) The
clique cover number
of
G
, denoted by
\theta(G)
, is the minimum size of a clique cover, that is, the minimum number of cliques that cover all vertices. The minimum clique cover problem seeks for a minimum clique cover.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
G
is a non-complete connected graph, then
b(G)\leq\theta(G)
. Furthermore, if
{\rm diam}(G)=2
then
b(G)=\theta(G)
.

Proof.

The vertex set of
G
can be decomposed into
\theta(G)
disjoint subsets, each being a clique. By removing from
G
all the edges connecting the
\theta(G)
distinct induced subgraphs by the cliques, we obtain
\theta(G)
subgraphs, each with diameter at most 1. Therefore,
b(G)\leq\theta(G)
.

If
{\rm diam}(G)=2
, all the subgraphs of any partition determining the Borsuk number
b(G)
must have diameter at most
1
. Hence, the vertex set of each subgraph must be a clique. Then, any such partition determines a clique cover of
G
; this implies
\theta(G)\leq b(G)
, which gives
\theta(G)=b(G)
since
G
is a non-complete graph.
∎

A reduction to the decision version of the minimum clique cover problem, which is a classical NP-complete problem
[
27
]
, allows us to establish the analogous result for the Borsuk number.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected graph, and let
k
be a positive integer number. The problem of deciding whether
b(G)<k
is NP–complete.

Proof.

Let
G
be a graph such that
{\rm diam}(G)\geq 2
(otherwise,
G
is a complete graph, and so
b(G)=|V|
, since all edges must be removed to decrease the diameter from
1
to zero).

The
cone

C_{G}
of
G
is the graph obtained from
G
by adding a new vertex adjacent to all the vertices in
G
. Note that
b(C_{G})\leq b(G)+1
: simply consider the partition determining the Borsuk number of
G
, and remove all previously added edges to obtain a partition with one more subgraph (the new vertex).

Since
{\rm diam}(C_{G})=2
, by Lemma
4.1
we have
\theta(C_{G})=b(C_{G})
.
Further, the graph
G
has a clique cover of size
k
if and only if
C_{G}
has a clique cover of size
k
. Thus, we have
b(G)\leq\theta(G)=\theta(C_{G})=b(C_{G})\leq b(G)+1
(the first inequality given by Lemma
4.1
). The result then follows from the NP-completeness for the minimum clique cover
[
27
]
, which is known to be NP-complete even for graphs of diameter 2.
∎

In the continuous setting, the decision problem for the Borsuk number is related to the
point line cover problem
: for a given set of points in the plane, the goal is to find a set of lines of minimum cardinality such that each point lies on at least one line. This is a well-studied problem in discrete geometry, which was shown to be NP-hard by Megiddo and Tamir in 1982
[
30
]
. Surprisingly, to the best of our knowledge, variants of the point line cover problem where the objects used to cover the points are disjoint have only been studied recently in
[
21
,
18
]
. The authors in
[
18
]
use either disjoint line segments (also used in
[
21
]
) or guillotine cuts (which are, essentially, also disjoint) to cover the points. See Figure
8
for an example of both problems. In particular, they prove the following theorem, which allows us to prove the NP-completeness of the decision problem for the Borsuk number.

n=9

Theorem 4.3

(
[
18
]
)
.

Given a set
S
of points in the plane and a positive integer
k
, the problem of determining if all points in
S
can be covered by
k
guillotine cuts is NP-complete.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a continuous geometric graph, and let
k
be a positive integer number. The problem of deciding whether
b(\mathcal{G})<k
is NP–complete.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the empty graph on
n
vertices; since
\mathcal{G}
is non-connected, its diameter is infinity. If we insert a line
\ell
to “partition”
\mathcal{G}
into the graphs
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}^{+}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}^{-}
, the result is that we connect a number of vertices of
\mathcal{G}
with the segment
s_{\ell}
. The graphs
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}^{+}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}^{-}
consist of the segment
s_{\ell}
(duplicated to belong to each graph) and the isolated vertices in, respectively,
\ell^{+}
and
\ell^{-}
, which have not been connected by inserting
\ell
. While any isolated vertices remain after partitioning, the diameter of the corresponding graph will be the same as that of
\mathcal{G}
(infinity). Moreover, each inserted line
\ell
divides only one of the existing graphs into two graphs of the type
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}^{+}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}^{-}
, and therefore
b(\mathcal{G})-1
is the minimum number of lines
\ell
needed to cover all isolated vertices. In fact, the objects we are using to cover the vertices of
\mathcal{G}
can be seen as a line, half-lines, and segments, where each object is inserted in only one existing region and divides it into two regions. This is a covering by guillotine cuts; thus,
b(\mathcal{G})-1
is the minimum number of guillotine cuts needed to cover all the points (vertices of
\mathcal{G}
.) The result then follows from Theorem
4.3
.
∎

5
Borsuk number of monotone continuous geometric graphs

We further explore the problem of how large the Borsuk number of a continuous graph might be by studying this parameter for a large class of continuous geometric graphs, those that are
monotone
. Our main result in this section provides an upper bound on their Borsuk number (Theorem
5.4
).

Let
\mathcal{G}
be continuous geometric graph, and let
\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{F}_{I}
be the part of the plane determined by the graph itself and all its interior faces. The graph
\mathcal{G}
is said to be
monotone with respect to a line
\ell
or
\ell
-monotone
if the intersection of any line perpendicular to
\ell
with
\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{F}_{I}
is either empty, a single point, or a segment; Figure
9
shows an example. Without loss of generality, throughout this section we assume that the line
\ell
is horizontal, and thus
\ell^{\perp}
is vertical.

For an
\ell
-monotone geometric continuous graph
\mathcal{G}
, and a vertical line
\ell^{\perp}
that is being translated from left to right (parameterized by the
x
-coordinate), we define the functions:

h^{+}(x):={\rm diam}\left(\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\ell^{\perp}(x)}\right)\quad{\rm and}\quad h^{-}(x):={\rm diam}\left(\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x)}\right).

Lemma 5.1
.

The function
h^{-}(x)
(resp.,
h^{+}(x)
) is monotone and increasing (resp., decreasing).

Proof.

We argue with
h^{-}(x)={\rm diam}\left(\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x)}\right)
; the argument for

h^{+}(x)
is analogous.

Let
\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})
and
\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})
, with
x_{1}<x_{2}
, be two vertical lines that intersect
\mathcal{G}
.
As infinite point sets,
(\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})^{-}\cap\mathcal{G})\subset(\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})^{-}\cap\mathcal{G})
. Recall that
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}=(\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})^{-}\cap\mathcal{G})\cup s_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}
and
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})}=(\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})^{-}\cap\mathcal{G})\cup s_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})}
. Refer to Figure
10
. We claim that
h^{-}(x_{1})\leq h^{-}(x_{2})
.

Consider the endpoints
u,v
of segment
s_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}
in
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}
.
When moving the line from
\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})
to
\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})
, segment
s_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}
disappears, but
u
and
v
remain connected through a path in
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})}
, whose length cannot be smaller than that of
s_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}
.
The same happens for any two points of
s_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}\cap\mathcal{G}
that are connected by part of the segment
s_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}
(instead of the whole segment), see Figure
10

Thus, the distance in
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})}
between any two points of
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}\setminus\{s_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}\}\subset\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})}
is at least their distance in
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}
(notice that the shortest paths connecting the points may not use the lines).
In particular, if there exists a diametral pair
p,q
of
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}
located on
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}\setminus\{s_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}\}
, we have
d_{\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}}(p,q)\leq d_{\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})}}(p,q)
. This implies
{{\rm diam}}(\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})})\leq{\rm diam}(\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})})
.

Suppose now that all diametral pairs
p,q
of
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}
satisfy that one of the points, say
q
, is on
s_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}
.
The fact that
\mathcal{G}
is
\ell
-monotone guarantees that
q
belongs to an interior face of
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})}
.

If there is a pair
p,q
such that the point
q
is not located on the boundary of the outer face of
\mathcal{G}
, we can take the horizontal line passing through
q
, which will eventually intersect some vertex or edge at a point

; see Figure

We have
d_{\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}}(p,q)<d_{\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})}}(p,r)
; indeed, any path from
p
to

must cross
\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})
, and, since

\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})
,

is closer to that crossing point than

. Hence,
{{\rm diam}}(\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})})\leq{\rm diam}(\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})})
.

Otherwise, for every diametral pair
p,q
of
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}
, the point
q
lies on
s_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}
and on the boundary of the outer face of
\mathcal{G}
, then the point
r
may not exist. In that case, one can simply move along the boundary of this outer face to obtain a point at a larger distance than
q
(from
p
) in
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})}
. Notice that the monotonicity hypothesis guarantees that this is the only situation in which the point

may not exist.

Finally, note that
p
and

cannot be both on

since they are diametral. Thus, in every case, we have

{{\rm diam}}(\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{1})})\leq{\rm diam}(\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\ell^{\perp}(x_{2})})
, that is,
h^{-}(x_{1})\leq h^{-}(x_{2})
.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

The associated functions
h^{+}(x)
and
h^{-}(x)
of an
\ell
-monotone continuous geometric graph
\mathcal{G}
are upper bounded by
{\rm diam}(\mathcal{G})
.

To reach the main result in this section, it remains to introduce one final ingredient: the concept of
diametral set
.

For any continuous geometric graph
\mathcal{G}
(not necessarily monotone), the diametral set
D_{\mathcal{G}}(p,q)
associated to a diametral pair
p,q
of
\mathcal{G}
is defined as the union of all the shortest
pq
-paths. The set
D_{\mathcal{G}}(p,q)
is a subset of
\mathcal{G}
. Notice that, for example, a cycle has an infinite number of diametral pairs of points (see Figure
3
), but only one distinct diametral set, which is the whole cycle (the union of the two diametral paths for each diametral pair is the same, the cycle). Thus, while a graph can have an infinite number of diametral pairs of points, this is not the case for diametral sets, as we prove in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a continuous geometric graph with
n
vertices. The number of distinct diametral sets of
\mathcal{G}
is in
O(n^{2})
.

Proof.

There always exists a diametral pair of
\mathcal{G}
formed by either: (i) two vertices, (ii) two points on distinct non-pendant edges,
4
4

4

A
pendant vertex
is a vertex of degree 1. A
pendant edge
is that incident with a pendant vertex.
or (iii) a pendant vertex and a point on a non-pendant edge (see, for instance,
[
9
, Lemma 6]
). Figure
11
illustrates these types of diametral pairs of points of
\mathcal{G}
.
Moreover, two distinct diametral pairs lying on the same two edges have the same diametral paths, that is, they define the same diametral set: indeed, if a point
p
is part of a diametral pair
p,q
, and lies on a non-pendant edge, then there exist two shortest
pq
-paths, each passing through one of the endpoints of the edge (see
[
9
, Lemma 5]
). Thus, every diametral pair of points of
\mathcal{G}
can be seen as a pair that may be (i) vertex-vertex, (ii) edge-edge, or (iii) vertex-edge.
As a consequence, we obtain that there are
O(n^{2})
distinct diametral sets.
∎

Next, we establish a linear upper bound (in the number of disjoint diametral sets) for the Borsuk number of monotone graphs.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an
\ell
-monotone continuous geometric graph, and let
k\geq 1
.
If
\mathcal{G}
has at most
k
disjoint diametral sets,
then
b(\mathcal{G})\leq k+2
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
be the set of all diametral sets
D_{\mathcal{G}}(p,q)
of
\mathcal{G}
, say that there are
k
distinct diametral pairs
p,q
in
\mathcal{G}
. By Lemma
5.3
, we have
k=O(n^{2})
. Without loss of generality, we assume that no two vertices of
\mathcal{G}
have the same
y
-coordinate.

By Corollary
5.2
, to reduce the original diameter when cutting
\mathcal{G}
by lines, it suffices to intersect all diametral sets in
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
with lines perpendicular to
\ell
, as the new points on the cutting lines cannot cause an increase of
{\rm diam}(\mathcal{G})
. Moreover, for each diametral set
D_{\mathcal{G}}(p,q)
, it suffices to intersect a single diametral path between
p
and
q
, since this decreases the distance between
p
and
q
, and hence they are no longer diametral.

We claim that all sets can be intersected using
k+1
lines. Indeed, project each diametral set
D_{\mathcal{G}}(p,q)
onto the line
\ell
so that each set determines an interval on the line. Then, sort the intervals by their rightmost extreme. We denote them by
[p_{i},q_{i}]
,
1\leq i\leq k
, where
q_{j}<q_{t}
for
j<t
. Set
\varepsilon
smaller than half the distance between any two consecutive points in the set
\{p_{i},q_{i}\,|\,1\leq i\leq k\}
. The line
{\ell_{1}^{\perp}}:x=q_{1}-\varepsilon
crosses the first interval
[p_{1},q_{1}]
and all the intervals that intersect with that. The intervals that are not crossed by
{\ell_{1}^{\perp}}
are disjoint with
[p_{1},q_{1}]
, which means that the diametral sets that gave rise to the corresponding intervals cannot intersect.

We now discard from
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
all the elements crossed by
{\ell_{1}^{\perp}}
to obtain a subset
\mathcal{D}_{1}(\mathcal{G})
of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
, and repeat the same process to obtain a new line
{\ell_{2}^{\perp}}
and a new subset
\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{G})
of
\mathcal{D}_{1}(\mathcal{G})
. This produces a sequence of subsets of diametral sets
\mathcal{D}_{0}(\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\supset\mathcal{D}_{1}(\mathcal{G})\supset\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{G})\ldots
satisfying that the diametral sets in
\mathcal{D}_{i-2}(\mathcal{G})\backslash\mathcal{D}_{i-1}(\mathcal{G})
do not intersect those in
\mathcal{D}_{i-1}(\mathcal{G})\backslash\mathcal{D}_{i}(\mathcal{G})
. Hence, we can find at most
k+1
of the lines defined above, otherwise we would have
k+1
disjoint diametral sets. These
k+1
lines split
\mathcal{G}
in, at most,
k+2
parts, each with diameter smaller than
\mathcal{G}
, since they intersect all diametral sets of
\mathcal{G}
. Therefore,
b(\mathcal{G})\leq k+2
.
∎

We note that the previous bound can be attained, at least for
k=1
.

Observation 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{W}_{33}
be the wheel graph on 33 vertices embedded in the plane such that its outer boundary is a regular 32-sided polygon, and the distance from the wheel center to each polygon vertex is one (see Figure
12
.) Then,
b(\mathcal{W}_{33})=3
.

Proof.

Each side of the outer boundary has length
\omega=2\sin(\pi/32)\approx 0.19
. Any two vertices of the polygon are connected by a path of length two, through the wheel center.
This path is shorter than going along the boundary as soon as the other vertex is more than ten vertices away along the boundary (since
11\omega>2
).
It follows that the diametral pairs of this continuous graph are given by pairs of midpoints of polygon sides that are at distance
2+\omega\approx 2.19
.
In fact, each midpoint has nine points at exactly that distance, corresponding to the midpoint exactly opposite, plus those of the first four sides neighboring the opposite side, in each direction. Figure
12
shows the nine diametral pairs involving one of the midpoints.

The wheel graph on 33 vertices attains the bound of Theorem
5.4
for
k=1
; the black thick points indicate the nine diametral pairs involving a point
p
.

Next we argue that subdividing by one line is not enough to decrease the diameter of
\mathcal{W}_{33}
.
Any line intersecting the wheel will leave at least 15 complete triangles of the wheel on one side.
These triangles are contiguous, and form a fan.
The diameter of any such a fan with 13 or more triangles remains the same as the original one,
2+\omega
.

It follows that two lines are necessary. Moreover, they are also sufficient, since two parallel lines at a very small distance that enclose the center will result in three subgraphs with smaller diameter.
Therefore,
b(\mathcal{W}_{33})=3=k+2
.
∎

5.1
Convex monotone graphs

An
\ell
-monotone continuous geometric graph
\mathcal{G}
is said to be

if all its interior faces are convex with respect to

(i.e., every line perpendicular to
\ell

Proposition 5.6
.

The associated functions
h^{+}(x)
and
h^{-}(x)
of an
\ell
-convex graph
\mathcal{G}
are continuous.

Proof.

Consider the
n-1
strips determined by lines perpendicular to
\ell
through each vertex of
\mathcal{G}
; we denote them by
\ell^{\perp}(x_{i})
with
1\leq i\leq n=|V(\mathcal{G})|

x_{i}<x_{j}
for
i<j
. Refer to Figure

. (Again, without loss of generality, we assume that no two vertices of
\mathcal{G}
have the same

-coordinate.)

We claim that, for every

, the function
h^{-}(x)
is continuous for

(informally, when the perpendicular line to
\ell

Indeed, consider an interval
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
(which determines a strip), a line
\ell^{\perp}(x_{j})

Let

be diametral points of

. Suppose first that neither

nor

are on

Further,

When

Disjoint diametral sets

The upper bound of Theorem

(not necessarily monotone) might admit, taking into account the planarity constraint. This problem is far from being solved; our aim in this section is to provide a first insight into the structure of the graphs that contain disjoint diametral sets.

Let

6
Borsuk number of trees

In Observation
3.2
we exhibit examples of trees where the Borsuk number, in the discrete case, can be easily computed, showing in particular that this parameter can be linear in the number of vertices. In this section, we first compute the Borsuk number of an arbitrary tree
T
, and then we move to the continuous version of the problem, which behaves differently.

Proposition 6.1
.

The Borsuk number of any tree
T
with
n
vertices can be computed in
O(n)
time. Furthermore,

(i)

If the center of
T
is not a unique vertex, then
b(T)=2
.

(ii)

If the center of
T
is a unique vertex
v
, then
b(T)=b(T^{\prime})=\delta_{T^{\prime}}(v)
, where
T^{\prime}
is the subtree of
T
induced by the vertices of all diametral paths, and
\delta_{T^{\prime}}(v)
is the degree of
v
in
T^{\prime}
.

Proof.

If the center of
T
consists of two adjacent vertices, it suffices to remove the edge incident to both vertices to obtain two trees with smaller diameter (since all diametral paths contain that edge); this gives
b(T)=2
.
When the center is a single vertex
v
, we need to delete all edges incident to
v
that are in a diametral path except one, that is,
\delta_{T^{\prime}}(v)-1
edges, which gives rise to
\delta_{T^{\prime}}(v)
connected components, and so
b(T)=b(T^{\prime})=\delta_{T^{\prime}}(v)
.

Since the center of a (weighted) tree can be computed in linear time
[
24
]
, so does its Borsuk number.
∎

While the value of
b(T)
depends on the center of the tree
T
, next we show that the Borsuk number of a continuous geometric tree
\mathcal{T}
is upper bounded by a constant. We begin with a technical lemma, which establishes that lines intersecting a tree at its center cannot cause an increase of the diameter of the original tree.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a continuous geometric tree with center point
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
, and let
\ell
be a line that passes through
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
. Then,

\max\{{\rm diam}(\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}),{\rm diam}(\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{-})\}\leq{\rm diam}(\mathcal{T}),

(1)

Proof.

We prove the lemma for
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}
; the arguments for
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{-}
are equivalent.
Recall that
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}=(\ell^{+}\cap\mathcal{T})\cup s_{\ell}
.
Since, by definition, the center point
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
is the midpoint of all diametral paths of
\mathcal{T}
, it suffices to show that
d_{\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}}(p,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}})\leq{\rm diam}(\mathcal{T})/2
for every point
p\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}

(the distance between two diametral points would then be at most
{\rm diam}(\mathcal{T})
).
This upper bound for
d_{\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}}(p,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}})
is clear when
p\in\mathcal{T}
, since
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
is the center of
\mathcal{T}
. Now, suppose that
p\in s_{\ell}\setminus\{\mathcal{T}\}
. Starting at
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
, walk through segment
s_{\ell}
(in the direction to
p
), and consider the first point
q\in s_{\ell}\cap\mathcal{T}
that is located after
p
. Since, in
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}
, the distances of
p
and
q
to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
are given by the lengths of the straight-line segments
\overline{p\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}}
and
\overline{q\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}}
, we have

d_{\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}}(p,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}})<d_{\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}}(q,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}})<d_{\mathcal{T}}(q,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}})\leq\frac{{\rm diam}(\mathcal{T})}{2}.

∎

Remark 6.3
.

Lemma
6.2
also holds for lines that intersect the tree, not at the center, but infinitely close to it. The difference in the proof would be that
d_{\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}}(p,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}})
and
d_{\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}}(q,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}})
are not given exactly by the lengths of the segments
\overline{p\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}}
and
\overline{q\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}}
, but we must add a term that tends to zero (the distance from the closest point in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}\cap\ell
to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
.)

Remark 6.4
.

In the proof of Lemma
6.2
, we show that if
\mathcal{T}
is cut by a line that goes through its center
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
, the distance from
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
to any point on the resulting graphs is at most
{\rm diam}(\mathcal{T})/2
, and strictly smaller when the point is on the line but not on the tree. Using Remark
6.3
, this can be extended to lines that intersect the tree infinitely close to the center.

Proposition 6.5
.

If
\mathcal{T}
is continuous geometric tree, then

.

Proof.

Let
\ell
be a line that passes through the center point
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
of
\mathcal{T}
, and divides the tree into two graphs,
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}=(\ell^{+}\cap\mathcal{T})\cup s_{\ell}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{-}=(\ell^{-}\cap\mathcal{T})\cup s_{\ell}
. Assume, without loss of generality, that
\ell
does not contain any edge incident or passing through
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
.
By Lemma
6.2
, the diameters of
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}
satisfy (

) and, moreover, by Remark

d_{\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}}(p,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}})\leq{\rm diam}(\mathcal{T})/2

(the same for
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{-}
.) We distinguish two cases.

Case 1. The center
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
is not a vertex of
\mathcal{T}
.

We prove that, in this case,

. This implies that
\max\{{\rm diam}(\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}),{\rm diam}(\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{-})\}<{\rm diam}(\mathcal{T})

.
Note that the center
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
is located in the interior of an edge
uv
with, say,

and

Let

.

If one of the points is not a leaf, then

, since the distance in
\mathcal{T}
between the points before inserting

was already smaller than the diameter of
\mathcal{T}
.

If
p
and

are leaves of
\mathcal{T}
, see Figure
17

since a shortest
pq

Assume now that only one of the points is on the tree, say,

and

.

Case 2. The center

We now turn to the problem of computing the Borsuk number of a continuous geometric tree

. By Proposition

, this is the same as distinguishing between

With lines that go exactly through the center

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a continuous geometric tree with center point
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}

\{d_{\mathcal{T}}(t_{i},\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}})\}_{i\geq 0}

Since

We generate a sequence of lines

(by the triangle inequality, it would also shorten that distance using any line in

), or

(ii)

does not shorten the distance (in
\mathcal{T}
) from
v
to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
using
\widetilde{\ell}_{\mathcal{W}}
(again, by triangle inequality, it would not shorten that distance using any line in
\mathcal{W}
), or

(iii)

shortens the distance from
v
to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
(in
\mathcal{T}
) using
\widetilde{\ell}_{\mathcal{W}}
but not using
\ell_{\mathcal{W}}
. Then, we can split
\mathcal{W}
into two double wedges
\mathcal{W}_{1}
and
\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{1}
such that all lines of
\mathcal{W}
shortening that distance using edge
e
are in
\mathcal{W}_{1}
.

We claim that each edge
e
contributes to the splitting of at most one double wedge, and the boundary between
\mathcal{W}_{1}
and
\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{1}
can be computed using the law of cosines. Indeed, consider the set
\mathcal{S}
of lines that go through the center
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
and intersect edge
e
. We parametrize the edge
e
by
\lambda
so that each fixed value of
\lambda
corresponds to a line in
\mathcal{S}
.
Since
e
is a fixed edge, the distance in
\mathcal{T}
from one of its endpoints to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
is a fixed value, which we denote by
a
.
Hence, for each value of
\lambda
, there is a unique line in
\mathcal{S}
that can satisfy the law of cosines, as Figure
20
b illustrates.
This means that one edge
e
can cause the division of at most one double wedge
\mathcal{W}
.
Therefore, after all possible subdivisions, we still have
O(n)
double wedges, and all lines in each double wedge have the same metric behavior. Moreover, for a given double wedge
\mathcal{W}
, the value that determines the boundary between
\mathcal{W}_{1}
and
\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{1}
(parametrizing the sub-segment with endpoints
\widetilde{\ell}_{\mathcal{W}}\cap e
and
{\ell}_{\mathcal{W}}\cap e
) can be computed, by the law of cosines, as:

\sqrt{b^{2}+c^{2}-2bc\cos\gamma},

where
b
(resp.,
c
) is the length of the segment with endpoints
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
and
\widetilde{\ell}_{\mathcal{W}}\cap e
(resp.,
{\ell}_{\mathcal{W}}\cap e
), and
\gamma
is the angle formed by
\ell_{\mathcal{W}}
and
\widetilde{\ell}_{\mathcal{W}}
in the wedge crossed by
e
.

Since the edge is fixed, the distance
a
and the angle
\alpha
are also fixed, and therefore, the law of cosines forces a unique value of
\lambda
.

The conditions of cases 1 and 2 can be checked in linear time for each of the
O(n)
combinatorially different classes of candidate lines (note that convex hulls do not need to be computed to check the conditions, since it is enough to check extreme lines). For each class, take any line
\ell
as a representative. If we find a line in those cases,
b(\mathcal{T})=2
. Otherwise, our candidate line for having Borsuk number
2
must satisfy the conditions of case 3. Then, we define a matrix
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{T})
whose columns are indexed by the diametral leaves
v
of
\mathcal{T}
, and the rows by the double wedges
\mathcal{W}
; the entry
v\mathcal{W}
is
0
if the distance from
v
to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
(in
\mathcal{T}
) is shortened using the lines of
\mathcal{W}
, and
\pm 1
otherwise:
+1
if
v
is above
\mathcal{W}
, and
-1
if it is below
\mathcal{W}
(i.e., one side and the other determined by the wedge). Since there are
O(n)
leaves and
O(n)
wedges, the matrix
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{T})
has size
O(n^{2})
.

The Borsuk number of
\mathcal{T}
(in case 3) is
2

if and only if there is a row in
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{T})
with either at most one entry
+1
or at most one entry
-1
. Indeed, a non-zero entry in
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{T})
means that there is a diametral leaf
v
and a class of combinatorially equivalent lines such that no line in the class decreases the distance from
v
to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
. Hence, a row of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{T})
with at most one
+1
entry, say, at entry
v\mathcal{W}
, tells us that there is a class of combinatorially equivalent lines, associated to the double wedge
\mathcal{W}
, such that:

•

no line in
\mathcal{W}
decreases
d_{\mathcal{T}}(v,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}})
;

•

all lines in
\mathcal{W}
decrease the distance (in
\mathcal{T}
) from
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
to all diametral leaves with entry
0
;

•

all diametral leaves corresponding to entry
-1
are on the other side (than
v
) of
\mathcal{W}
.

By Lemma
6.6
we can take a line
\ell
intersecting
\mathcal{T}
at a point infinitely close to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
, parallel to any line of the class, and leaving
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
on the same half-plane as
v
. Thus, we divide
\mathcal{T}
into the graphs
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{-}
in such a way that
v
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
are in the same new graph, and all diametral leaves whose distance to the center is not decreased are in the other; suppose that
v,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}
. It holds that
{\rm diam}(\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+})<{\rm diam}(\mathcal{T})
, since
the unique distance from a diametral leaf of
\mathcal{T}
to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
that is not decreased in
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}
is that from
v
, and to reach distance
{\rm diam}(\mathcal{T})
, there must be at least two such diametral leaves. Moreover, the diameter of
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{-}
is also strictly smaller than that of
\mathcal{T}
, since the center
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{T}}
is located in
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{+}
and so, for any two diametral leaves of
\mathcal{T}
that are in
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{-}
, their path in
\mathcal{T}
is shortened by some segment contained in
\ell
.

Therefore, in each case, we can determine whether there is a line giving a correct partition in
O(n^{2})
time, if so,
b(\mathcal{T})=2
; otherwise,
b(\mathcal{T})=3
.

Concluding remarks

We extended the widely-studied Borsuk’s problem to the context of graphs. Our approach either partitions an abstract graph by removing edges, or a continuous plane geometric graph by a sequence of line cuts. In both cases, one could propose other partitioning methods; however, we believe that ours are natural, since, as explained before, they preserve the core idea of Borsuk’s original problem, and there are real-world applications (in, for example, social networks studies or network design) related with our approach.

There are many questions that remain open. A challenging problem is to improve our bounds for the Borsuk number of continuous geometric graphs; in particular, to determine whether this parameter can be upper bounded by a constant (in analogy with Borsuk’s original problem). We believe that further exploration of the structure of diametral sets in plane continuous geometric graphs can contribute to progress in this problem.
Finally, it would also be interesting to design efficient algorithms to compute the Borsuk number, and to characterize the graphs with a given Borsuk number, for instance, with Borsuk number two.
