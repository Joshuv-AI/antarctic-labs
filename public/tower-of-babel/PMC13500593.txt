Clinicoradiological differentiation of TERT promoter-mutant molecular glioblastoma versus histological glioblastoma: a single-center cohort study of 105 IDH-wildtype tumors
Authors: Jian Jiang, Tao Han, Hongyu Zhang, Peng Zhang, Liangcai Bai, Junlin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo compare the clinicopathological and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) characteristics and prognostic implications between molecular glioblastoma (mol-GBM) and histological glioblastoma (hist-GBM) according to the 2021 WHO classification of central nervous system tumors (CNS5), we conducted a retrospective study of 105 patients with IDH-wildtype gliomas diagnosed between 2016 and 2022. Patients were reclassified into hist-GBM (n = 70) and mol-GBM (n = 35), with mol-GBM defined by TERT promoter mutation. Comparative analyses of clinical, molecular, and radiological features were performed. Overall survival (OS) was assessed in a subgroup of 96 patients using Kaplan–Meier and multivariable Cox regression models. Patients with mol-GBM were significantly younger (median age: 53 vs. 55.5 years, p = 0.046, median difference = − 2.5, 95% CI: −7.434 to 2.434), exhibited lower Ki-67 proliferation indices (30 vs. 40%, p = 0.002, median difference = − 10.0, 95% CI: −16.638 to − 3.362), and had lower p53 mutation rates (65.6 vs. 84.1%, p = 0.040, OR = 0.367, 95% CI: 0.138 to 0.973). MRI analysis demonstrated that mol-GBM was associated with less extensive peritumoral edema (major edema: 54.3 vs. 68.6%; p = 0.006) and lower tumor heterogeneity grades (Grade 3: 20.0% vs. 44.2%, p = 0.001, OR = 4.360, 95% CI: 1.950 to 9.746). No significant differences were observed in quantitative enhancement parameters or apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values. Survival analysis revealed a non-significant trend toward improved OS in the mol-GBM group (median OS: 17.0 vs. 13.0 months, log-rank p = 0.091). In multivariable analysis, GBM subtype was not an independent predictor of survival (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.829, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.432 to 1.591, p = 0.573). In conclusion, TERT promoter-mutant mol-GBM is characterized by a distinct clinicoradiological profile, including younger age at diagnosis and less aggressive imaging features. Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most aggressive and least survivable primary malignant brain tumor, originating from glial cells. It accounts for approximately 45.2% of primary malignant brain and central nervous system tumors1 and about 16% of all brain tumors2. The fifth edition of the World Health Organization Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System (WHO CNS5), published in 2021, introduced significant updates to the definition of GBM: ① It removed the isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutant type of GBM from the 2016 classification and retained only the IDH-wildtype GBM; ② Diffuse and astrocytic gliomas of grades 2 and 3 with IDH-wildtype, as defined in the 2016 guidelines, are now classified as GBM if they meet any one of the following criteria: Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) amplification, chromosome + 7 / -10, or telomerase reverse transcriptase promoter mutation (TERT-p mutation)3. This leads to two subtypes: ① Histological GBM (hist-GBM), which conforms to the histological features of WHO grade 4 tumors and also meets the molecular pathological characteristics of GBM; ② Molecular GBM (mol-GBM), which shows histological features of WHO grade 2–3 astrocytomas but meets the diagnostic requirements of GBM based on molecular pathology4.

Studies have demonstrated that mol-GBM may represent an early stage in the natural history of GBM, during which classic histological features such as microvascular proliferation and pseudopalisading necrosis are absent but may develop later in the disease course5. The prognostic significance of mol-GBM remains under investigation, with reported survival outcomes varying from non-significant trends to statistically significant advantages over hist-GBM6,7. This inconsistency highlights the importance of characterizing mol-GBM in well-defined patient cohorts. Consequently, early identification of mol-GBM holds substantial clinical importance. Radiologically, mol-GBM is characterized by reduced contrast enhancement and a lower incidence of intratumoral necrosis on imaging8. Although recent studies have utilized quantitative imaging biomarkers, including diffusion (apparent diffusion coefficient, ADC) and perfusion (relative cerebral blood volume, rCBV) parameters, to characterize mol-GBM and differentiate it from lower-grade gliomas9, a systematic comparison focusing specifically on the distinctions between mol-GBM and hist-GBM using a comprehensive set of conventional and quantitative MRI features has not yet been fully established. This study aims to systematically compare the clinical, molecular, and MRI characteristics of mol-GBM and hist-GBM to identify distinguishing features and evaluate their prognostic implications.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study received approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee of our institution and was conducted in strict accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (Ethics Approval Number: 2021 A-622).

Patient selection and cohort establishment

The patient selection process is outlined in Fig. 1. We initially screened 241 adult patients with a histopathological diagnosis of IDH-wildtype diffuse glioma who underwent surgical resection or biopsy at our institution between June 2016 and December 2022.

Flowchart of patient selection and study process.

Cohort selection adhered to the 2021 WHO CNS5 diagnostic criteria for IDH-wildtype glioblastoma. This integrated diagnosis requires IDH-wildtype status plus definitive evidence of glioblastoma-level malignancy, which can be established through either histological or molecular features. Accordingly, patients were categorized into two subgroups:

Histological glioblastoma (hist-GBM): Defined by the presence of classic histological features, specifically microvascular proliferation and/or necrosis.

Histological glioblastoma (hist-GBM): Defined by the presence of classic histological features, specifically microvascular proliferation and/or necrosis.

Molecular glioblastoma (mol-GBM): Defined by a TERT promoter mutation in the absence of the histological features listed above. Because EGFR amplification and chromosome + 7/−10 were not routinely assessed in clinical practice, the present study focused on this TERTp-mutant subgroup.

Molecular glioblastoma (mol-GBM): Defined by a TERT promoter mutation in the absence of the histological features listed above. Because EGFR amplification and chromosome + 7/−10 were not routinely assessed in clinical practice, the present study focused on this TERTp-mutant subgroup.

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Supplementary Material 1), 94 IDH-wildtype/TERTp-wildtype cases were excluded, as they could not be definitively classified due to the absence of routine testing for EGFR amplification or chromosomal alterations. Consequently, the final cohort comprised 105 patients: 35 mol-GBM and 70 hist-GBM. A sensitivity analysis was performed to compare baseline characteristics between the included and excluded cohorts.

Clinical, pathological, and MRI data were collected from our hospital’s Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) and Hospital Information System (HIS). Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time interval from the date of surgery to the date of death or the last follow-up (censored on December 31, 2024). Follow-up involved review of clinical follow-up records and MRI images, supplemented by telephone interviews.

Molecular diagnostics

Molecular profiling was performed on postoperative tumor tissues using the following techniques:

IDH1/2 mutation analysis

IDH1 hotspot mutations were detected via an Amplification Refractory Mutation System (ARMS)-based fluorescent PCR assay on a SLAN-96 S system, utilizing mutation-specific primers and FAM/VIC dual-labeled probes. Mutation status was determined based on Cycle threshold (Ct) and ΔCt values under stringent quality control.

TERT promoter mutation analysis

Mutations at the C228T and C250T hotspots were identified by direct Sanger sequencing. The process involved PCR amplification, product purification, and sequencing on an ABI 3500Dx genetic analyzer.

MGMT promoter methylation analysis

Methylation status was assessed by Methylation-Specific PCR (MSP) following bisulfite conversion of genomic DNA. Real-time fluorescence PCR was performed using GAPDH as an internal control, and a ΔCt value ≤ 11 (FAM-HEX) was defined as methylation-positive.

Although the brain tumor methylation classifier was not routinely employed, the integrated diagnosis of mol-GBM was strictly established according to the WHO CNS5 criteria, based on the above molecular results in conjunction with histopathological findings.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections to evaluate protein expression.

p53 status

p53 expression was assessed by IHC. Given the correlation between aberrant protein accumulation and TP53 mutation, cases exhibiting strong nuclear staining in > 10% of tumor nuclei were classified as p53-positive; all others were classified as p53-negative.

Ki-67 proliferation index

The Ki-67 labeling index (LI) was determined using a monoclonal mouse anti-human Ki-67 antibody (MX006, 1:200; Maxim). The LI was quantitatively assessed as a continuous variable by counting positive tumor nuclei in the area of highest staining density (hotspot). The final Ki-67 index was reported as the average percentage from three independent counts of 1000 cells each. For comparative analyses, a cutoff of 20% was used to categorize tumors into low- (LI < 20%) and high-proliferation (LI ≥ 20%) groups, consistent with established glioblastoma prognostic thresholds10.

MRI acquisition

All preoperative MR imaging scans were performed on a 3-Tesla MR scanner (MAGNETOM Verio, Siemens, Germany). Contrast enhanced T1-weighted (CE-T1w), T2-weighted imaging fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2flair), T1-weighted (T1w), T2-weighted (T2w), and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) sequences were applied for the following analyses. The acquisition parameters were summarized in Supplementary Material 1.

Image analysis

All imaging data were independently evaluated by two senior neuroradiologists (with 11 and 18 years of experience) in a blinded manner, differences of opinion were resolved through discussion, resulting in a final common assessment. This consensus-building step occurred after the initial interpretations but prior to the calculation of Kappa values comparing the two radiologists. However, for intra-observer agreement, only one radiologist repeated the assessment 4 weeks later. Based on the VASARI (Visually AcceSAble Rembrandt Images) assessment11, the following VASARI parameters were recorded: tumor location, side of tumor epicenter, eloquent brain areas, number, enhancement quality, enhancement types, proportion of non-enhancing tumor (nCET), heterogeneity12, proportion of tumor necrosis, cystic components, multifocal or multicentric lesions, definition of T2WI margins, proportion of edema13, edema crossing midline, presence of hemorrhage, diffusion characteristics, deep white matter and invasion, tumor crossing midline, satellite lesions, longest diameter of FLAIR/T2WI abnormality. Calcification was identified based on preoperative cranial CT images.

Quantitative parameters, including T2W signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values (ADCmean, ADCmax, ADCmin, rADC), and quantitative enhancement indicators (enhancement rate before and after contrast administration, tumor enhancement SNR, enhancement ratio)12, were measured. ADC values were measured on the solid tumor component, avoiding necrosis, cystic change, hemorrhage, and calcification. For each lesion, three regions of interest (ROI) were manually placed, with ROI sizes ranging from 10 to 120 mm², and the mean ADC was calculated for analysis. Tumor heterogeneity grading criteria, the degree of peritumoral edema, methods for the measurement and calculation of quantitative parameters were summarized in Supplementary Material 1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data are summarized as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentages) and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from surgery to death from any cause or the last follow-up. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared between molecular and histological subtypes with the log-rank test. A risk table indicating the number of patients at risk at predetermined time points is provided below the survival curve.

A multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was constructed to identify independent predictors of OS. Given the number of events (n = 57), the model was designed to be parsimonious. It was pre-specified to include GBM subtype (the primary variable of interest) and Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), based on their established clinical prognostic value14. Additionally, variables with P < 0.10 in univariable analysis were considered for inclusion. All candidate variables selected in this manner underwent assessment for multicollinearity using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a VIF ≥ 5 indicating substantial collinearity and leading to exclusion. The results are reported as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Post-hoc power analyses were performed for key comparisons using observed effect sizes. Effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals are reported for key findings to reflect estimation precision. Corresponding power values are presented in the Results section.

Results

Observer agreement

In assessing both inter-observer and intra-observer agreement, we utilized Cohen’s kappa coefficient. For inter-observer agreement, the kappa coefficient ranged from 0.706 to 0.818, which indicates substantial agreement between different observers across all VASARI features. As for the intra-observer agreement, evaluations were conducted four weeks apart, yielding a kappa coefficient of 0.983, highlighting that individual observers were highly consistent in their evaluations across different time points.

Sensitivity analysis for selection bias

Sensitivity analysis comparing the included IDH-wildtype TERT-mutant (IDHw TERTm) GBM cohort (N = 105) with the excluded IDH-wildtype TERT-wildtype (IDHw TERTw) tumors (n = 94) revealed significant differences in key baseline characteristics. The IDHw TERTm cohort was significantly older at diagnosis (median 54 vs. 47 years, p = 0.001) and exhibited a distinct age distribution (p = 0.003), with a lower proportion of patients under 50 years old (32.4% vs. 56.4%). Tumor location profiles also differed, with a higher prevalence in the temporal lobe (48.6% vs. 31.9%, p = 0.013) and parietal lobe (23.8% vs. 12.8%, p = 0.044) in the IDHw TERTm group. No significant differences were observed in sex distribution (p = 0.706) or other tumor locations. These results demonstrate distinct clinical profiles between the included and excluded cohorts, thereby underscoring the necessity of a separate analysis for the IDHw TERTm population (Supplementary Table 1).

Clinical characteristics

The median age of mol-GBM patients was 53 years (age range = 21‒76), with the highest proportion of patients aged 50‒59 years (42.8%, 15/35). The sex ratio was 1.92:1 (male: female). The majority of the patients were urban residents (62.9%, 22/35), and all patients were of Han Chinese.

The median age of hist-GBM patients was 55.5 years (age range = 31‒77 years), with the highest proportion of patients aged 60‒69 years (31.4%, 22/70). The sex ratio was 1.19:1 (male: female). 60.0% (42/70) of the patients were urban residents, and 88.6% (62/70) were of Han Chinese, while 11.4% (8/70) belonged to ethnic minorities.

Between-group comparisons showed that mol-GBM patients were significantly younger than hist-GBM patients (median difference − 2.5, 95% CI − 7.434 to 2.434, p = 0.046, power = 0.169), with a distinct peak age distribution (50–59 years vs. 60–69 years; p = 0.018). Regarding ethnicity, all mol-GBM patients were Han Chinese, compared with 88.6% in the hist-GBM group (p = 0.050).

The clinical symptoms of GBM patients mainly included epilepsy, focal neurological deficits, cognitive dysfunction, and high intracranial pressure symptom. In this group of cases, intracranial hypertension symptoms such as headache were the main clinical manifestations, accounting for 57.1% (20/35) of mol-GBM patients and 62.9% (44/70) of hist-GBM patients. There was no significant statistical difference in clinical symptoms between the two groups.

Immunohistochemistry and MGMT gene promoter methylation status

In mol-GBM, 85.7% (30/35) were WHO grade 3 tumors, and 80.0% (28/35) were histologically classified as anaplastic astrocytoma, with 5.7% (2/35) being WHO grade III anaplastic oligoastrocytoma NOS. The Ki-67 index of mol-GBM was 30% (range: 10%‒40%), and the positive rate of p53 was 65.6% (21/32); the Ki-67 index of hist-GBM was 40% (range: 30%‒45%), and the positive rate of p53 was 84.1% (53/63). There was a statistically significant difference in the Ki-67 index and p53 positivity rate between the two groups (p < 0.05).

Specifically, the Ki-67 index differed significantly (median difference − 10.0%, 95% CI − 16.638 to − 3.362, p = 0.002, power = 0.467), and p53 positivity was lower in mol-GBM (OR = 0.367, 95% CI 0.138–0.973, p = 0.040, power = 0.515).

The incidence of MGMT gene promoter methylation (MGMTmet) in mol-GBM was 60.0% (21/35); the incidence of MGMTmet in hist-GBM was 58.6% (41/70), and there was no significant difference in the incidence of MGMTmet between the two groups. MRI features

Mol-GBM predominantly occurred in the left side and frontal lobe, while hist-GBM was more common in the right side and temporal lobe. The proportion of multi-lobar involvement cases in the two groups was 48.6% (17/35) and 41.4% (29/70), respectively. The tumor volume was large, with 48.6% (17/35) of mol-GBM and 50.0% (35/70) of hist-GBM having a tumor volume greater than 5 cm. The majority of tumors in both groups exhibited ill-defined margins (mol-GBM: 65.7%; hist-GBM: 61.4%). Midline crossing growth was observed in 17.1% (6/35) of mol-GBM and 15.7% (11/70) of hist-GBM.

The proportion of tumors with heterogeneity grades 0–1 in mol-GBM was 57.1% (20/35), higher than that in hist-GBM (22.9%, 16/70), while the proportion of tumors with heterogeneity grades 2–3 was 42.9% (15/35) in mol-GBM, lower than that in hist-GBM (77.1%, 54/70). The difference in the presence of heterogeneity (homogeneous vs. heterogeneous) was significant (p = 0.011), with homogeneous tumors observed only in the mol-GBM group (11.4% vs. 0% in hist-GBM). The heterogeneity grade distribution also differed significantly (OR = 4.360, 95% CI 1.950–9.746, p = 0.001, power = 0.983). There was no significant difference in T2W SNR between the two groups.

The overall prevalence of contrast enhancement was high in both cohorts, with a non-significantly lower rate in mol-GBM (94.1% [32/34] vs. 100% [70/70]; p = 0.105). A significant difference was observed in enhancement patterns: ring enhancement was less common in mol-GBM (32.4% [11/34] vs. 50.0% [35/70]), whereas the rates of diffuse enhancement were comparable (38.2% [13/34] vs. 41.4% [29/70]). Conversely, a small subset (5.9%, 2/34) of mol-GBM showed no enhancement. Furthermore, no significant differences were found in quantitative enhancement metrics, including the pre-to-post-contrast enhancement rate, tumor enhancement SNR, and enhancement ratio.

There was no significant difference in the ADCmean, ADC min, ADC max and rADC of the solid region of the tumor between the two groups.

Five cases in the mol-GBM group had no peritumoral edema, while all hist-GBM cases exhibited peritumoral edema. The overall severity of peritumoral edema differed significantly between the two groups (p = 0.006), with a higher proportion of major edema in hist-GBM (68.6% vs. 54.3%). Peritumoral edema in both groups was predominantly irregular in morphology (indentation type, radial type). The proportion of cases with irregular peritumoral edema in the two groups was 71.4% (25/35) and 87.1% (61/70), respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups. Cohort for survival analysis

For the overall survival analysis, 9 patients (3 with the molecular subtype and 6 with the histological subtype) were excluded due to loss to follow-up, resulting in an analytical cohort of 96 patients. The baseline characteristics of this survival cohort were consistent with those of the overall population, indicating that the exclusion did not introduce substantial selection bias (Table 3).

Survival analysis

Survival analysis revealed a clinically relevant trend favoring the mol-GBM subtype. The mol-GBM group showed a prolonged median overall survival compared to the hist-GBM group (17.0 months vs. 13.0 months), although this difference was not statistically significant per the log-rank test (p = 0.091; Fig. 6). Notably, the Breslow test, which places greater weight on early survival differences, was significant (p = 0.043), suggesting a potential early survival advantage in the mol-GBM cohort.

The difference in survival, assessed by the log-rank test, did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.091). The number of patients at risk at each time point is shown below the graph.

In the univariable Cox regression model, the mol-GBM subtype was associated with a non-significant trend toward a reduced risk of death (HR = 0.61, 95% CI 0.34‒1.10; p = 0.102; Supplementary Table 2). Ki-67 index was identified as a significant prognostic factor (HR = 1.02 per 1% increase, 95% CI 1.00‒1.03, p = 0.047, power = 0.747). However, after adjustment for age, Ki-67 index, and KPS in the multivariable model, this association was substantially attenuated and no longer statistically significant (HR = 0.83, 95% CI 0.43–1.59; p = 0.573; Supplementary Table 3). The attenuation of the effect suggests that the observed survival advantage was largely attributable to the more favorable prognostic profile (younger age and lower Ki-67 index) within the mol-GBM group, rather than an independent effect of the molecular subtype itself.

Discussion

Based on the new GBM classification proposed by the 2021 WHO CNS5, previous conclusions regarding the clinical, radiological, molecular, and prognostic features of GBM may no longer be compatible with current clinical practice8. The uncertainty surrounding the diagnostic and treatment principles for mol-GBM as a new subtype is also increasing15, which makes it imperative to re-evaluate the classification and characteristics of GBM. Notably, the present study focused on TERT promoter-mutant molecular glioblastoma (TERTp-mut mol-GBM), which represents a common but not exhaustive subset of molecular GBM as defined by CNS5 (which also includes EGFR amplification and chromosome + 7/−10). This distinction is important when interpreting our findings, as the characteristics described herein may not be directly generalizable to other molecular subgroups.

Our comparative analysis identified a critical clinical challenge associated with mol-GBM. The survival analysis cohort (N = 96) was balanced with respect to median age at diagnosis (Table 3), despite a higher proportion of patients under 65 years in the mol-GBM group. The balance in median age between groups enhances the clinical relevance of the observed trend: the mol-GBM group showed a non-significant tendency toward longer overall survival (17.0 vs. 13.0 months; log-rank p = 0.091). Notably, the proportion of patients with Ki-67 > 20% was significantly lower in the mol-GBM group (53.1% vs. 85.9%, p < 0.001; OR = 0.193, 95% CI: 0.073–0.51; power = 0.927), highlighting a robust distinction in proliferative activity between subtypes that may underlie the observed survival trend. This finding aligns with results reported by Ramos-Fresnedo et al.6, but contrasts with the statistically significant survival advantage observed by Lee et al.7. The trend was further supported by univariable Cox regression analysis (HR = 0.61, 95% CI 0.34–1.10, p = 0.102). To assess potential confounding factors, multivariable analysis was conducted. After adjusting for age, KPS, and Ki-67 index, the association was substantially attenuated and no longer statistically significant (HR = 0.83, 95% CI 0.43–1.59; p = 0.573), indicating that the observed survival advantage in our cohort was not independently associated with mol-GBM status but likely attributable to more favorable prognostic profile, including younger age and lower tumor proliferative activity within the mol-GBM group.

In this retrospective cohort, where treatment decisions were not guided by prospective molecular subtyping, the attenuation of the survival difference may reflect a specific clinical risk we aim to highlight: the potential for initial undertreatment. The less aggressive radiological phenotype of mol-GBM increases the likelihood of misclassification as a lower-grade glioma, which may lead to suboptimal surgical resection and delayed initiation of chemoradiotherapy. This interpretation is supported by evidence from a prior prospective study16, in which patients with molecularly defined glioblastoma who received more aggressive clinical management exhibited significantly improved survival (23.8 vs. 16.2 months).

In our cohort, patients with mol-GBM were significantly younger than those with hist-GBM (median age 53 years [IQR 47‒57] vs. 55.5 years [49‒65], p = 0.046), with a distinct peak age distribution (50‒59 years vs. 60‒69 years, p = 0.018). Glioblastoma is typically diagnosed in older populations, with peak incidence between 65 and 75 years of age17. Our findings are consistent with the previously reported average diagnostic age for mol-GBM of approximately 50 years8,18, and suggest that glioblastoma in younger adults should prompt heightened clinical suspicion for this molecular subtype.

Epidemiological studies have consistently reported a higher incidence of brain gliomas in males19. This finding is consistent with the established epidemiological pattern, as tumors in our cohort were predominantly observed in male patients (65.7% in mol-GBM vs. 54.3% in hist-GBM, p = 0.072). Beyond incidence, our analysis revealed potential sex-specific disparities. Female patients exhibited a notably higher prevalence of MGMT promoter methylation in both mol-GBM (75.0% vs. 52.2% in males) and hist-GBM (65.6% vs. 52.6%). An apparent divergence was also observed in radiological presentation: female mol-GBM patients demonstrated less extensive peritumoral edema (41.7% with minor edema and 8.3% with no edema) and a greater frequency of tumor homogeneity (25.0% vs. 4.3% in males). These observed sex-specific differences in imaging and molecular profiles may be attributed to the neuroprotective effects of estrogen and sexually dimorphic patterns of molecular expression20–23. However, a formal assessment of menopausal status was not feasible in this retrospective cohort, as detailed menstrual history is not routinely recorded in standard neurosurgical documentation, leading to insufficient data across the female patient population. Therefore, evaluating the influence of hormonal status represents an important direction for future research using prospectively collected datasets. Our results underscore that patient sex is not only relevant to disease susceptibility but also critical for the interpretation of molecular markers and imaging phenotypes in the development of personalized therapeutic strategies23.

Consistent with their aggressive biological behavior, both subtypes exhibited high Ki-67 indices, though values were significantly lower in mol-GBM (30% [IQR 10‒40] vs. 40%= [30‒45], p = 0.002). The Ki-67 index reflects the tumor proliferation rate and degree of malignancy and is widely used to differentiate high-grade from low-grade gliomas3,24. A higher Ki-67 index is associated with malignant transformation, shorter recurrence intervals, and poor prognosis24,25. We observed a significantly higher p53 mutation rate in hist-GBM (84.1%) compared to mol-GBM (65.6%, p = 0.040), highlighting a fundamental divergence in tumor biology. Alterations in the p53 pathway are considered key factors in the progression from low-grade to high-grade gliomas26. The proportion of MGMT promoter methylation was comparable between the two cohorts (60.0% vs. 58.6%, p = 0.888); however, the notable sex-specific disparity noted above underscores the importance of considering both subtype and patient sex when interpreting this predictive biomarker. MGMT promoter methylation status is a well-established predictor of sensitivity to temozolomide in IDH-wildtype glioblastomas27.

Our study revealed significant differences in key imaging features between mol-GBM and hist-GBM. A lower proportion of mol-GBMs exhibited major peritumoral edema (54.3% vs. 68.6%, p = 0.006). Peritumoral edema is associated with tumor malignancy, and multiple genes, signaling pathways, and biological processes contribute to its formation28. Major peritumoral edema promotes tumor infiltration into the surrounding tissues, which expands the tumor’s growth range29. Furthermore, mol-GBMs were more likely to present with homogeneous appearance on MRI (11.4% vs. 0% in hist-GBM, p = 0.011). We acknowledge that the macroscopic heterogeneity grading used in this study, while practical and reproducible, simplifies the complex nature of glioblastoma heterogeneity. True heterogeneity is a multidimensional phenomenon encompassing genetic, cellular, and microenvironmental diversity30,31. Our primary objective was to determine whether a simple, visually assessable feature on routine MRI could serve as a clinically useful tool for distinguishing mol-GBM from hist-GBM, rather than to fully characterize the underlying biological complexity. The significant association observed suggests that these macroscopic imaging patterns may reflect biologically meaningful differences relevant to subtyping. The contrast enhancement rate in our mol-GBM cohort (94.1%) exceeds prior reports6,7. This discrepancy underscores both the broad imaging spectrum of mol-GBM and the potential for preoperative underestimation of its tumoral aggressiveness. This finding can be attributed to two main factors. First, although TERT promoter mutations are less specific for IDH-wildtype glioblastoma compared with EGFR amplification or the 7/10 signature32, the high contrast enhancement rate in our TERTp-mut cohort still reflects their high-grade biology. This suggests that these tumors may represent a distinct biological subgroup. Second, patients with brain tumors presenting with space-occupying effects and clinical symptoms are more likely to undergo surgical intervention, and these tumors are also more frequently observed to exhibit contrast enhancement on neuroimaging.

ADC values did not differ significantly between cohorts, suggesting limited utility of diffusion metrics for subtyping. Notably, multifocal lesions were common in both groups (mol-GBM: 34.3%; hist-GBM: 28.6%). The prevalence of multifocal lesions reinforces that mol-GBM, despite its less aggressive imaging phenotype, retains the malignant propensity for diffuse spread typical of IDH-wildtype glioblastoma33,34. Patients with multifocal tumors have a significantly shorter median overall survival compared to those with solitary lesions33, and the frequency of multiple lesions is significantly higher in IDH wild-type GBM than in IDH-mutant astrocytomas35.

It is essential to recognize that mol-GBM remains underdiagnosed in clinical practice, primarily because it may lack the hallmark imaging features of classical glioblastoma. Less typical contrast enhancement patterns may lead to misclassification as a lower-grade glioma and delay in diagnosis. Thus, reliance on conventional neuroimaging alone is inadequate and substantially increases the risk of missed diagnoses.

Our findings confirm that no single radiological feature is pathognomonic for mol-GBM. Rather, accurate identification and optimal clinical management necessitate an integrated diagnostic approach. Clinical and imaging characteristics that should raise suspicion include younger patient age, less aggressive radiological features—such as mild peritumoral edema, homogeneous tumor architecture, and faint or regular ring-like enhancement—and supportive molecular markers, including lower frequency of p53 mutations, as well as a trend toward higher MGMT promoter methylation in female patients observed in this cohort. When this constellation of features raises preoperative suspicion for mol-GBM, maximal safe resection should be performed to obtain adequate tissue for definitive molecular testing. Upon histomolecular confirmation, standard chemoradiotherapy should be initiated without delay to ensure treatment intensity equivalent to that administered for hist-GBM, along with a structured follow-up plan. Critically, the potentially misleading radiological appearance must not result in therapeutic complacency at any stage of management.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, it was a retrospective analysis based on a relatively small dataset from a single institution. Second, due to the lack of comprehensive molecular testing (e.g., EGFR amplification and chromosome + 7/−10), two related limitations should be noted. IDHw/TERTw tumors were excluded because they could not be definitively classified according to CNS5 criteria; sensitivity analysis revealed significant differences in age and tumor location, underscoring the need for dedicated investigation into both the IDHw/TERTm population and the poorly characterized IDHw/TERTw subgroup. Moreover, our definition of mol-GBM was restricted to TERTp-mut cases; therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to other molecular GBM subtypes (e.g., EGFR-amplified or + 7/−10). Third, due to the extended duration of case collection, MRI examinations predominantly consisted of routine imaging protocols. Future studies should include more prospective, multicenter, and advanced MRI-based investigations to further explore the characteristics of mol-GBM and address current research and clinical knowledge gaps.
