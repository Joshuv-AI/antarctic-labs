The Association of Hearing Impairment and Behavioral and Psychological Symptoms in Community-Dwelling Patients with Alzheimer’s Disease
Authors: Yu-Ru Lin, Ming-Che Chang, Yu-Chun Tung, Sheng-Chieh Lin, Wen-Fu Wang, Kai-Ming Jhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to investigate the relationship between hearing impairment and the longitudinal changes in behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) in patients with Alzheimer’s disease (AD).Materials and MethodsThis case–control study enrolled 387 participants newly diagnosed with AD. Participants’ hearing status was initially assessed based on their need for hearing assistance during neuropsychological tests. BPSD was evaluated using the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) at baseline and a 1-year follow-up visit. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to examine the association between hearing status and the presence of BPSD.ResultsA higher percentage of participants in the hearing-impaired group exhibited moderate to severe apathy (26% vs. 14%, p=0.01) and sleep disturbance (24% vs. 14%, p=0.042) after 1 year. Hearing impairment was found to be an associated factor for the presence of sleep disturbance (odds ratio [OR] = 1.88; 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.09–3.21) and moderate-to-severe apathy (OR=1.97; 95% CI=1.03–3.72) after 1 year. After adjusting for age, sex, education, baseline Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) and baseline Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) scores, hearing impairment remained an independent associated factor for the risk of worsening symptoms in apathy and sleep disturbance symptoms, with ORs of 2.06 (95% CI=1.13–3.70, p=0.016) and 2.13 (95% CI=1.18–3.80, p=0.012), respectively.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that hearing impairment is associated with the longitudinal worsening of sleep and apathy symptoms in patients with AD over a 1-year follow-up period. Dementia care teams should place greater emphasis on these non-cognitive symptoms and provide appropriate management strategies.Keywords: sensory loss, auditory, sleep disturbance, insomnia, apathy

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common type of dementia and affects approximately 50 million people worldwide.1 It is characterized by progressive cognitive decline, leading to significant impairment in daily functioning and quality of life. In addition to memory loss and cognitive deficits, patients with AD often exhibit a range of behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD). These symptoms can include agitation, depression, anxiety, hallucinations, and sleep disturbances, and they tend to worsen as the disease progresses.2 Understanding the factors that influence different neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) is crucial for improving the management and care of individuals with AD. Among the diverse array of BPSD, apathy and sleep disturbances are particularly prevalent and debilitating in patients with AD.3 Apathy, characterized by a loss of motivation and emotional indifference, is one of the most common neuropsychiatric symptoms and is strongly associated with rapid functional decline and increased caregiver burden.3,4 Similarly, sleep disturbances frequently occur as dementia progresses, severely impacting the quality of life for both patients and their families.5 While several factors have been linked to the exacerbation of these symptoms, the potential role of sensory impairment, particularly hearing loss, as a longitudinal contributor to the worsening of apathy and sleep remains insufficiently explored.6–8

Hearing impairment, also known as presbycusis when related to aging, is a common condition that affects approximately one-third of people over the age of 65 and two-thirds of those over 75.9 It is typically characterized by a gradual loss of hearing, particularly at higher frequencies, making it difficult to understand speech, especially in noisy environments. This sensory loss can significantly impact an individual’s social interactions, leading to feelings of isolation and depression.10 Emerging research has highlighted a potential link between hearing impairment and the progression of AD. Studies have shown that untreated hearing loss is associated with an increased risk of cognitive decline and dementia, suggesting that auditory deprivation may contribute to neurodegeneration.11 Hearing loss is a modifiable risk factor for developing cognitive decline and dementia among older adults.11,12 The use of hearing aids (HA) and other assistive devices can mitigate some of these effects, improving communication and potentially reducing cognitive decline in later life.13 The exact mechanisms underlying this relationship remain unclear. Current research hypotheses suggest that hearing impairment may exacerbate social isolation, increase cognitive load, and lead to changes in brain structure and function.12

Previous literature discussing the association between hearing impairment and NPS is relatively limited. One small-scale study found that patients with dementia with poorer visual acuity and self-reported hearing problems have three times greater rates of having NPS (95% confidence interval (CI): 1.72–11.78).14 These sensory impairments also cause additional distress to the patients’ friends and family.15 A cross-sectional study indicated that more severe hearing loss correlates with more and worse NPS, suggesting that addressing hearing problems could help prevent and treat NPS.16 Therefore, understanding and addressing hearing impairment in AD could play a crucial role in managing their cognitive and behavioral symptoms. Individuals with both AD and hearing impairment may experience a more pronounced exacerbation of BPSD, underscoring the importance of examining this relationship further.

While previous research has established a cross-sectional correlation between hearing loss and the overall severity of neuropsychiatric symptoms, there is a critical lack of longitudinal data identifying which specific BPSD sub-domains are most susceptible to decline over time in the context of auditory deprivation. Specifically, it remains unclear whether hearing impairment acts as a constant baseline burden or if it actively accelerates the 12-month trajectory of symptoms like apathy or sleep disturbance. By delineating the longitudinal impact on individual symptom clusters rather than aggregate scores, this study aims to provide more precise insights for targeted clinical interventions.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This retrospective case–control study enrolled patients at the time of their initial diagnosis of AD between March 2020 and June 2024. All baseline assessments, including hearing status and BPSD evaluation, were conducted during the same diagnostic visit to ensure a consistent starting point for the one-year follow-up. Participants of this study underwent a preliminary interview of their cognitive status, BPSD, and education levels (in years), and a follow-up check of their cognition and BPSD status 1 year after by clinical psychologists face-to-face in the hospital. They were managed by a collaborative multidisciplinary care team consisting of clinical physicians, dieticians, nursing case managers, occupational therapists, pharmacists, physical therapists, psychologists, and social workers.17 Besides the first assessment and the follow-up evaluation after 12 months, patients also received multiple check-ins by the care team members to make sure their needs were catered to and transferred to suitable long-term care resources.18,19 The dedicated team intervention improved care quality and passed the Disease-Specific Care (DSC) Certification Program for dementia held by the Joint Commission of Taiwan (JCT) in July 2021.20,21 The information mentioned above was all documented in the digital medical records in the Changhua Christian Hospital.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Changhua Christian Hospital (CCH IRB 240723), and informed consent was also waived since the data used were derived from the anonymized electronic files.

Measurement of BPSD

The BPSD of participants was measured with the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI), a standardized clinical interview administered by trained clinical psychologists to the patients’ primary caregivers. The NPI consists of 12 items (delusions, hallucinations, agitation, depression, anxiety, euphoria, apathy, disinhibition, irritability, aberrant motor activity, sleep disturbance, and appetite change). For each domain, caregivers first complete a screening question; if a behavioral change is present, the frequency (scored 1–4) and severity (scored 1–3) of the symptom are rated. The total score for each domain is the product of Frequency times Severity (range 0–12). The evaluation was conducted initially (as a baseline) and 12 months after. In each category, patients with an NPI score (severity-cross-frequency) ≥1 were defined as the presence of that NPS, and a score ≥4 was defined as moderate-to-severe symptoms. The total NPI score was the summation score of the 12 items. Worse NPS (symptoms progression) one year later was defined as an increased NPI score in each category (follow-up NPI score minus baseline NPI score in each NPI category >0).

Measurement of Hearing Status

All cognitive and neuropsychiatric assessments were conducted one-on-one by one of three clinical psychologists in a dedicated, sound-isolated room at Changhua Christian Hospital. To eliminate inter-rater variability, these examiners underwent synchronized training and established a formal consensus on the assessment protocols prior to the study.

Participants were categorized into the “No hearing impairment” group if they could consistently receive and understand verbal messages at a normal conversational volume without the need for any assistive devices, such as behind-the-ear, in-the-ear, or in-the-canal HAs, or hand-held amplifiers. Conversely, participants were defined as “Hearing impaired” if they required any form of auditory assistance to communicate. This included patients who: (1) Utilized their own personal hearing aids during the assessment. (2) Required the examiner to provide a hand-held hearing amplifier because they could not hear at a normal volume. (3) Required the examiner to significantly raise their voice to be understood. To maintain longitudinal stability and prevent sensory variability from confounding the outcomes, hearing status was reassessed at the one-year follow-up visit. Any participant whose hearing capacity fluctuated or varied significantly between the initial and final visits was excluded from the final analysis.

Measurement of Patient Features

The baseline profile of patients’ age, gender, education level (in years), and clinical dementia rating (CDR) scales22 was evaluated, along with their NPI score and hearing status. Among patients with any type of dementia, only patients with newly diagnosed AD were enrolled, using the diagnostic criteria according to the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA).23,24 The CDR is a global scale used to assess cognitive and functional performance in six domains: memory, orientation, judgment and problem solving, community affairs, home and hobbies, and personal care.22 In this study, global CDR scores were utilized both as a baseline characteristic and as a covariate in the multivariable logistic regression models to adjust for the baseline severity of cognitive impairment. To evaluate the longitudinal association between hearing impairment and the worsening of neuropsychiatric symptoms, multivariable logistic regression models were performed. Crucially, to account for potential ceiling effects and baseline clinical differences, each model was adjusted for the respective baseline NPI domain score, in addition to age, gender, education, and baseline CDR score.

Statistical Analyses

All the data used in this study were generated by R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The study designated the participants’ baseline hearing status as the independent variable, while the participants’ BPSD outcome, assessed through NPI scores, served as the dependent variable. Categorical data were assessed by Pearson’s Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Numerical data were analyzed using the Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank sum test. A multivariable logistic regression model was employed to investigate the association between hearing status and the presence of BPSD. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05.

Discussion

In this study, hearing impairment was longitudinally associated with the worsening of apathy and sleep disturbance over the course of one year. Patients with hearing impairment showed a higher tendency to develop moderate-to-severe apathy symptoms; their increase in apathy scores was significantly greater compared to those without hearing impairment. Additionally, a higher proportion of patients with hearing impairment experienced sleep disturbances, with a marked rise in moderate-to-severe sleep problems after one year.

Apathy is typically defined as a loss of drive or indifference, and its severity in patients with AD correlates with higher economic resource utilization.4 Previous studies have identified genetic, neurobiological, and underlying personality traits as apathy onset and deterioration factors in patients with AD.25 In this study, the association between hearing impairment and the increased likelihood of apathetic symptoms, as well as the prevalence of moderate-to-severe symptoms, can be explained by several factors. Firstly, deteriorated hearing in older adults affects facial emotion recognition, which involves atrophy of emotion-processing neural structures.26 The loss of emotional perception would lead to patients appearing to be indifferent to others, and thus scoring higher in the apathy subdomain of NPI. Besides, with the increase in hearing impairment, there are higher levels of physical, mental, and social function malfunction,6 and communication difficulties can heighten patients’ resistance to care.7 A meta-analysis on the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on NPS of dementia revealed that social isolation exacerbates apathetic symptoms.27 Furthermore, the loss of social interaction can lead to brain atrophy, especially when social connections enhance neurogenesis and synaptic density in brains,28 and reduced cortical thickness is linked to more severe apathy.25 Finally, the inability to hear conversations among family members may be misinterpreted as a lack of interest or affection, which should also be considered.

In the sleep and nighttime behavior category of NPI, patients are assessed based on their sleeping difficulties.2,29 The following theories might explain the association of abnormal nighttime behavior and hearing impairment found in this study. First, hearing loss is a significant factor contributing to tinnitus, which is the conscious perception of an auditory sensation without a corresponding external stimulus.30,31 Multiple studies have shown a strong association between tinnitus and insomnia,30,31 and according to a cohort study conducted by Lasisi, A. O., and Gureje, O., up to 73.4% of patients experience difficulty maintaining sleep.32 Moreover, reduced physical activity levels brought about by hearing deficits can also relate to poor sleep. As mentioned, auditory impairment is tied to lower levels of social function and physical activity,6 and a higher risk of physical disability.8 A randomized controlled trial by Iuliana Hartescu et al concluded that engaging in at least 150 minutes of moderate- to vigorous-intensity physical activity per week can significantly reduce insomnia severity.33 Lastly, the interaction between sleep apnea and hearing impairment should be considered. Sleep disturbances associated with sleep apnea include frequent nighttime awakenings and excessive daytime sleepiness. Several studies have shown an association between sleep apnea and hearing impairment, although the causal relationship remains unclear.34,35 Current theory suggests that intermittent hypoxia in sleep apnea may cause earlier cochlear damage,36,37 but continuous positive airway pressure does not significantly prevent this issue.35,36

Previous studies have shown that hearing impairment is associated with the onset of dementia.10–12 Sensory dysfunction in older adults can disrupt brain structure, leading to cognitive and memory decline through neuropathological processes.38 In a community-based cohort study, patients with dementia who have visual or hearing problems were found to have three times greater rates of having NPS (95% CI: 1.72–11.78).14 Another cross-sectional study suggested that more severe hearing problems correlate with more and worse NPS.16

Analyzing individual BPSD is important, especially since it may be crucial for providing better support to caregivers.39 Nonetheless, the longitudinal relationship between hearing problems and the 12-month trajectory of individual BPSD symptoms is a relatively unexplored area, on which only a few previous studies have been conducted. While our data revealed an association between hearing impairment and the apathetic or sleep-related aspects of BPSD, previous research, though limited, has suggested links to depressive and agitation symptoms. A cross-sectional study found more and worse depressive symptoms in people with cognitive and hearing impairments.16 While no significant mean resolution of depression was observed, a pilot study also indicated that after one month of HA use, patients with dementia who initially had a high symptom burden showed improvements in depression and overall neuropsychiatric outcomes.40 The depressive symptoms in these patients can be explained by difficulties in daily activities and social engagement brought about by auditory malfunction,41 or the direct structural brain damage from hearing loss itself.42 Additionally, among patients with dementia, communication and speech barriers can relate to more severe agitation.43 However, this connection between reduced auditory function and depressive or agitational symptoms in patients with dementia was not observed in our analysis. The best explanation for this discrepancy is that NPS fluctuates over years, and our study’s one-year span limits our ability to conclude long-term longitudinal changes in these symptoms.44,45 In as short a period as two weeks, the NPI score for depressive symptoms can alternate significantly, with up to 65.5% variation observed in individuals.46

Given that our study and several previous research showed an association between worsened NPS and hearing impairment, addressing the hearing problem might be the key to improving patients’ neuropsychiatric outcomes or lessening friends’ or families’ burden,15 and hearing loss itself reduces patients’ quality of life.47 HAs seem to be a straightforward intervention. Older adults with dementia tend to cease HA use over time, especially within the first year of usage.48 According to a cross-sectional study, patients without dementia had lower odds of concurrent mild behavioral impairment with HA use.49 One recent randomized controlled trial concluded that HAs are useful in reducing 3-year cognitive decline in older adults at risk of cognitive dysfunction or lower baseline cognitive scores.50 However, the efficacy of HAs in improving BPSD remains undetermined. After HA fitting, some reports indicate reduced behavioral problems in patients with AD.51 Conversely, a double-blind randomized controlled trial found no significant effect of HA use on behavioral symptoms, functional status, or quality of life in patients with AD and hearing loss.52 In light of these inconsistent results, more structured controlled trials are required in the future to provide better evidence for HA use in patients with AD. In the meantime, other auditory interventions like communication strategies or cochlear implantation should also be explored in patients with AD.53 However, it is important to note that the actual usage and compliance of hearing aids were not evaluated in this cohort. Consequently, any potential for HA to alleviate or prevent BPSD symptoms remains purely speculative at this stage. Future prospective research that includes longitudinal monitoring of hearing aid adherence is necessary to determine their true clinical efficacy in this population.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include: 1) Analysis of BPSD changes in each category, standardized by NPI score; 2) Follow-up of NPI scores after one year. Limitations include: (1) Sample and Setting: Data were collected from a single center in Taiwan, resulting in a limited sample size that may affect generalizability. (2) Assessment Method: We utilized a functional rather than objective audiometric assessment. Cognitive deficits in AD, such as impaired attention or verbal comprehension, may mimic auditory deficits during testing. This “cognitive-auditory” overlap suggests that high cognitive load might confound the hearing-impaired categorization. Future research using non-verbal measures (eg, Auditory Brainstem Response) is needed to isolate peripheral hearing loss from cognitive processing difficulties. (3) Temporal Scope: The one-year follow-up provides a valuable longitudinal snapshot but precludes assessment of long-term trajectories or late-stage effects. (4) Statistical Adjustments: We did not adjust p-values for multiple comparisons across the 12 BPSD domains. Consequently, our significant findings for apathy and sleep disturbance should be viewed as hypothesis-generating. (5) Modeling Constraints: Our longitudinal analysis utilized a binary outcome (worsened vs. not worsened) for Table 5. While this effectively captures the presence of clinical decline, it does not quantify the absolute magnitude of symptom change, nor does it differentiate between incident (new-onset) and progressive (worsening of existing) symptoms. Although our models were fully adjusted for baseline NPI domain severity to minimize potential ceiling effects, future studies employing continuous outcome modeling and larger cohorts are warranted to refine these longitudinal associations.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings indicate that among community-dwelling patients with AD, hearing impairment is independently associated with a higher likelihood of worsening apathy and sleep disturbances over one year. While the causal nature of this relationship remains to be fully elucidated, the presence of hearing deficits may serve as a clinical indicator for the potential exacerbation of specific neuropsychiatric symptoms. Dementia care teams should remain vigilant regarding the hearing status of patients, as addressing sensory barriers may be a component of comprehensive BPSD management. However, as hearing status in this study was determined by functional assessment rather than objective audiometry, these clinical implications should be interpreted with caution and validated through standardized testing in future practice.
