Validation of the 2022 German trauma team activation criteria: a national registry study with focus on geriatric-specific modifiers
Authors: P. Faul, P. Hagebusch, R. Lefering, U. Schweigkofler, M. Münzberg, T. Jakobi, D. A. Koch, the Committee on Emergency Medicine, Intensive Care and Trauma Management (Sektion NIS) of the German Trauma Society (DGU)
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe 2022 revision of the German Trauma Society’s (DGU) trauma team activation (TTA) criteria introduces geriatric-specific modifiers to enhance the sensitivity of risk stratification in older (elderly) trauma patients. This study evaluates the validity of these updated criteria using national registry data, with particular focus on mortality gradients across activation levels and the prognostic value of newly incorporated modifiers.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort analysis of patients aged ≥ 60 years, defined as elderly trauma patients, from the TraumaRegister DGU® (TR-DGU) between 2018 and 2023. Cases were stratified according to TTA status based on both the 2016 and 2022 DGU guidelines: high risk of severe injury (HRSI), moderate risk of severe injury (MRSI), or None (provider decision). The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Subgroup analyses examined the impact of pre-injury anticoagulation, systolic blood pressure (SBP) 91–100 mmHg, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) 13–14, and multiple serious injuries, defined as Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) ≥ 3 in two or more body regions.ResultsAmong 66,273 elderly trauma patients, mortality according to the 2022 criteria showed a consistent risk gradient: 20.2% (high risk of severe injury, HRSI), 9.0% (moderate risk of severe injury, MRSI), and 4.2% (None). In contrast, the 2016 criteria yielded inconsistent patterns, including higher mortality in the “None” category. New modifiers were strongly predictive of mortality: multiple serious injuries in ≥ 2 regions (16.7% vs. 6.7%), SBP 91–100 mmHg (13.1% vs. 7.3%), and GCS 13–14 (12.5% vs. 5.6%). Pre-injury anticoagulation was associated with increased mortality across all subgroups (29.3% vs. 20.3%).ConclusionThe 2022 TTA criteria improve the alignment between triage criteria and observed mortality risk, particularly among elderly patients. The addition of geriatric-specific modifiers enhances predictive accuracy and clinical applicability. Future work should quantify implications for overtriage and resource allocation to support evidence-based trauma system optimization.

Introduction

Trauma continues to impose an immense global health burden, accounting for approximately 4.4 million injury-related deaths annually, equating to 8% of all global mortality [1]. In high-income settings, trauma often ranks as the leading cause of death among individuals under 50 years of age, with increasing contributions across elderly age groups as populations age [2, 3].

Rapid recognition and early mobilization of trauma care resources—commonly via TTA—are core tenets of trauma care. TTA protocols typically combine physiological thresholds (e.g., hypotension, impaired consciousness), mechanism-of-injury criteria, and special considerations like age or anticoagulation status [4]. However, overtriage (unnecessary activations) and undertriage (missed critical cases) persist as challenges worldwide, with undertriage rates in modern systems reportedly ranging up to 20% [5].

Elderly trauma patients demonstrate distinct physiology—altered tachycardic response, impaired vascular reactivity, and frequent comorbidities—that often render standard activation thresholds insensitive [6]. As a result, undertriage in elderly trauma is substantial, with one recent German multicenter cohort revealing a 53.8% undertriage rate among patients ≥ 70 years of age, who despite comparable severity, received TTA only 15.8% of the time (vs. 31.8% in younger groups), and experienced more than threefold higher early mortality (1.8% vs. 0.5%) [7]. Another German registry study reported a 15.3% undertriage rate in severely injured elderly (median age 80 vs. 55 years) [8].

The DGU has leveraged its TR‑DGU and national TraumaNetzwerk to evaluate the real-world performance of TTA criteria over time. Earlier versions of the German S3 Guideline on Treatment of Polytrauma and Severely Injured Patients (e.g., the 2016 version) were strongly influenced by mechanism-driven criteria, with significant prevalence of overtriage [5, 9, 10]. Overtriage rates in international literature vary widely—from 12% to over 70%—while undertriage remains particularly pronounced in elderly adults [9, 11–14].

Accordingly, the 2022 DGU guideline update introduced refinements to the moderate-risk category (MRSI) and added geriatric-specific modifiers: systolic blood pressure 91–100 mmHg, Glasgow Coma Scale score 13–14, and AIS ≥ 3 injuries in two or more body regions [15]. These were meant to improve sensitivity in elderly patients, whereas HRSI remained focused on classical physiological indicators of critical risk and prehospital emergency interventions. Existing literature supports the utility of such geriatric-aware adjustments [13, 14]. Moreover, pre-injury anticoagulation—common in the elderly—increases mortality and intracranial hemorrhage risk, supporting its consideration within TTA protocols [16–18].

Using aggregated TR‑DGU registry data, we aimed to describe and compare in-hospital mortality across TTA categories defined by the 2016 and 2022 DGU guidelines, with specific focus on geriatric-specific MRSI elements—systolic blood pressure 91–100 mmHg, GCS 13–14, AIS ≥ 3 in ≥ 2 regions—and pre-injury anticoagulation status. We present age-stratified mortality findings to provide a robust epidemiological validation of the updated criteria in elderly trauma patients.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the TR-DGU of the DGU, including the years 2018–2023. The TR-DGU was founded in 1993. The aim of this multi-centre database is a pseudonymized and standardized documentation of severely injured patients. Data are routinely documented during clinical care in four consecutive time phases from the site of the injury until discharge from hospital: (A) Pre-hospital phase, (B) Emergency room and initial surgery, (C) Intensive care unit and (D) Discharge. A separate ward or floor phase is not documented as an independent TR-DGU phase; overall in-hospital outcomes are captured at discharge. The documentation includes detailed information on demographics, injury pattern, comorbidities, pre- and in-hospital management, course on intensive care unit (ICU), relevant laboratory findings including data on transfusion and outcome of each individual. The inclusion criterion is admission to hospital via emergency room with subsequent ICU care or arrival at the hospital with vital signs followed by death before ICU admission. The infrastructure for documentation, data management, and data analysis is provided by AUC – Academy for Trauma Surgery (AUC - Akademie der Unfallchirurgie GmbH), a company affiliated to the German Trauma Society [19]. The scientific leadership is provided by the Committee on Emergency Medicine, Intensive Care and Trauma Management (Sektion NIS) of the German Trauma Society. The participating hospitals submit their data pseudonymized into a central database via a web-based application. Scientific data analysis is approved according to a peer review procedure laid down in the publication guideline of TR-DGU. The participating hospitals are primarily located in Germany (90%), but a rising number of hospitals of other countries contribute data as well (at the moment from Austria, Belgium, China, Finland, Luxembourg, Slovenia, Switzerland, The Netherlands, and the United Arab Emirates). Currently, more than 30,000 cases from around 700 hospitals are entered into the database per year. Participation in TR-DGU is voluntary. For hospitals associated with TR-DGU, however, the entry of at least a basic data set is obligatory for reasons of quality assurance.

This analysis is an extension of the previously registered project 2022-029 and is registered within the TR-DGU under the ID TR-DGU-2025-003.

This study adhered to the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement”, and was performed in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki and its modifications.

Pseudonymized data were retrieved from the registry and analyzed retrospectively. Data quality was ensured by predefined plausibility checks and standardized variable definitions within TR‑DGU. All analyses adhered to TR‑DGU publication by‑laws; case counts and denominators are reported for transparency. This study represents a retrospective mapping analysis, in which registry cases were reclassified according to both the 2016 and 2022 guideline criteria based on available registry data. Therefore, the study evaluates the theoretical performance and discriminatory capacity of the updated criteria rather than their prospective clinical implementation.

Inclusion criteria

The study period was 2018–2023. We included primary admissions to TR-DGU-participating hospitals in Germany with TTA, initial management in the trauma room, and subsequent admission to an intensive care unit (ICU). Eligible patients were trauma patients aged ≥ 60 years. The age threshold of ≥ 60 years was selected to allow inclusion of patients in whom early age-related physiological changes may already influence trauma outcomes. Patients had to meet the TR-DGU registry inclusion criteria, defined as a maximum Abbreviated Injury Scale (MAIS) score ≥ 3 or a MAIS score of 2 with ICU admission. Injury severity was coded using the AIS, version 2005 update 2008, as applied in the TR-DGU during the study period.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded inter‑hospital transfers (secondary admissions), patients dead on arrival without trauma room management, and cases with missing key variables required for analyses (e.g., systolic blood pressure or Glasgow Coma Scale). When variables required for subgroup analyses were missing, cases were excluded from the relevant subgroup only (complete‑case approach).

Data collection

Exposure definitions and group assignment

TTA criteria were defined according to the German guideline frameworks and mapped within TR‑DGU fields as follows [15]:

HRSI, high risk of severe injury: activation based on physiological compromise. Within TR‑DGU, most HRSI criteria are explicitly documented and were identified directly (Table 1).

MRSI, moderate risk of severe injury: activation based on mechanism and, in the 2022 update, geriatric-specific modifiers. Mechanism-based triggers (e.g., high fall) were captured where documented. For motor vehicle collisions (MVC), MRSI was considered present when the mechanism was coded as MVC and no higher priority HRSI criterion was present. For the 2022 mapping, geriatric modifiers were additionally flagged: systolic blood pressure (SBP) 91–100 mmHg, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) 13–14, and multiple serious injuries (AIS ≥ 3) in two or more body regions (Table 2). AIS-based injury patterns were used retrospectively as surrogates for anatomical injury burden and were not intended to represent criteria available for real-time prehospital triage decision-making.

None: cases in which no HRSI or MRSI criterion could be identified in the registry fields; these activations were assumed to reflect provider decision.

Subgroups

Age strata used for primary analyses were defined in 5-year intervals (60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, and ≥ 80 years), with a pooled 60–80 years category. Pre-injury anticoagulation included vitamin K antagonists and direct oral anticoagulants. Anatomical injury burden was categorized as AIS ≥ 3 injuries in < 2 vs. ≥ 2 distinct body regions. Consistent with the national guideline defining SBP < 90 mmHg as an HRSI trigger, values recorded as 90 mmHg were treated as ≤ 90 mmHg (critical) because approximately 27% of cases in the critical range are recorded at exactly 90 mmHg. GCS was categorized as 15 versus 13–14 for analyses of geriatric MRSI items; GCS < 13 corresponds to an HRSI criterion.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was in‑hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes (where available) included intensive care unit length of stay and total hospital length of stay. For the present analyses based on aggregated tables, results are reported for mortality only.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages; continuous variables as medians with quartiles. Group comparisons for categorical outcomes used the Chi‑squared test. Age‑stratified mortality proportions were compared across TTA strata (HRSI, MRSI, None) and by guideline mapping (2016 vs. 2022). Predefined subgroup analyses evaluated mortality by anticoagulation status, by AIS ≥ 3 injuries in ≥ 2 regions, by SBP 91–100 vs. > 100 mmHg, and by GCS 13–14 vs. 15, each stratified by presence/absence of HRSI. Two‑sided p‑values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 29.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethics

The study used pseudonymized registry data and was conducted in accordance with TR‑DGU governance. The scientific dataset was further blinded for hospital name and date/time of admission. Approval for scientific evaluation of TR‑DGU data is granted by the DGU Sektion NIS. Data were initially collected for external quality control, with written informed consent by the patients.

Results

Study population

A total of 66,273 trauma patients from 674 hospitals participating in the TR-DGU were included in the analyses. Patients were stratified according to TTA criteria based on the 2016 and 2022 guidelines of the German Trauma Society (DGU): HRSI, MRSI, and None (provider decision). Mortality rates were analyzed by age groups (60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, and ≥ 80 years) and by presence or absence of specific MRSI modifiers (Table 2).

Anticoagulation and mortality

In elderly trauma patients (≥ 60 years), overall in-hospital mortality was 29.3% in those with pre-injury anticoagulation compared to 20.3% in patients without. Within TTA subgroups: HRSI mortality was 43.0% with anticoagulation versus 33.6% without; MRSI mortality was 17.5% versus 8.1%; and None mortality was 9.6% versus 4.1%. In the subset of elderly patients in the “None” category without other criteria, mortality was 9.6% with anticoagulation compared with 4.1% without.

Serious injuries in multiple body regions and mortality

The effect of anatomical injury was assessed using AIS ≥ 3 in ≥ 2 regions. Without HRSI: mortality was 12.5% with AIS ≥ 3 in ≥ 2 regions compared with 6.7% with < 2 regions. At ≥ 80 years, mortality was 20.1% versus 11.2%. With HRSI present: mortality was 32.6% with AIS ≥ 3 in ≥ 2 regions compared with 19.3% with < 2 regions. At ≥ 80 years, mortality was 44.4% versus 27.9%.

Validation of updated 2022 criteria vs. 2016

In the 2022 version of the German S3 Guideline on the Treatment of Polytrauma and Severely Injured Patients, age-stratified mortality showed a consistent gradient across TTA groups (Fig. 1B; Table 3). At 60–64 years: HRSI 12.7%, MRSI 1.2%, None 0.9%. At 65–69 years: HRSI 19.0%, MRSI 1.9%, None 1.1%. At 70–74 years: HRSI 23.9%, MRSI 3.8%, None 1.9%. At 75–79 years: HRSI 31.2%, MRSI 8.3%, None 3.8%. At ≥ 80 years: HRSI 47.5%, MRSI 16.6%, None 8.8%.

In the 2016 guideline version, mortality patterns differed (Fig. 1A; Table 3). At age 60–64 years: HRSI 13.9%, MRSI 0.9%, None 3.0%. At 65–69 years: HRSI 20.7%, MRSI 1.5%, None 4.4%. At 70–74 years: HRSI 26.5%, MRSI 2.9%, None 8.0%. At 75–79 years: HRSI 34.3%, MRSI 4.1%, None 11.5%. At ≥ 80 years: HRSI 50.6%, MRSI 11.8%, None 19.2%.

Discussion

The present analysis demonstrates that the update of the German trauma guidelines in 2022 substantially improves alignment of TTA criteria with outcome risk. In contrast to the 2016 version, where patients meeting moderate-risk criteria (MRSI) paradoxically exhibited lower mortality than those activated only by provider decision, the updated framework establishes a clear hierarchy in which mortality is highest in patients with high-risk criteria (HRSI), intermediate in those with MRSI, and lowest in the “None” group [10, 20], as shown in Fig. 1. This change provides a transparent and reproducible allocation of patients to activation criteria, which is essential for both clinical practice and benchmarking of trauma system performance [5, 21].

The introduction of geriatric-specific criteria into the MRSI category addresses a longstanding gap in trauma care [14, 22]. With the demographic shift towards an aging population, elderly trauma patients represent an increasing proportion of the severely injured cohort. In Germany and other European countries, the proportion of patients aged ≥ 70 years in trauma registries has risen steadily over the past decade [8]. This development has major implications, as elderly patients show higher mortality, longer hospital stays, and more frequent complications compared to younger adults with comparable injury severity [7, 23]. At the same time, elderly trauma patients are particularly prone to undertriage due to blunted physiological responses such as a preserved heart rate or systolic blood pressure that may still appear within “physiological limits” [6, 13].

The explicit addition of systolic blood pressure 91–100 mmHg as a trigger for MRSI reflects this phenomenon. The GCS of 13–14 in elderly patients recognizes that mild alterations in consciousness in the geriatric group could be due to an intracranial injury, which may be otherwise underestimated when only a threshold of GCS < 13 is applied [24]. Finally, the addition of AIS ≥ 3 injuries in two or more body regions acknowledges that the cumulative burden of moderate injuries across multiple regions confers significant mortality risk in the elderly, even when no single injury meets classical “anatomic” high-risk criteria [25, 26]. Each of these modifications represents a reproducible trigger for TTA.

While the addition of geriatric-specific criteria improves the sensitivity of TTA, it may also carry the risk of increased overtriage [9]. Expanding the spectrum of moderate-risk criteria inevitably raises the number of patients triggering activation, some of whom may ultimately not require the full resources of a trauma team. Given the finite availability of surgical, anesthesiologic, and radiological capacity in most centers, unnecessary activation has the potential to divert personnel from other critical tasks, strain system efficiency, and increase costs [5, 27, 28]. Therefore, while the 2022 criteria represent an important step toward reducing undertriage in elderly trauma patients, their impact on overtriage is essential to optimize trauma system performance. The ultimate goal is to minimize preventable deaths while safeguarding finite medical resources. International consensus statements state that acceptable overtriage rates may range between 25 and 35%, whereas undertriage should remain below 5% [29].

Accordingly, further evaluation of the 2022 guideline update should not only confirm its ability to reduce undertriage, but also quantify its impact on overtriage and system resource allocation. Prospective registry studies are needed to determine whether the revised criteria achieve a good balance of sensitivity and specificity in real-world practice.

Nevertheless, the validation of these criteria in the present registry analysis confirms their clinical utility. Mortality increased consistently when the geriatric-specific thresholds were met, whether defined by low-normal systolic blood pressure, GCS 13–14, or multiple-region injury. Importantly, these patterns held true even in the absence of high-risk physiological derangements, demonstrating that these newly defined MRSI elements capture a subset of elderly patients at relevant risk who would otherwise fall into the “None” group. The result is a more precise and transparent stratification of trauma patients.

Another important observation in our study is the effect of pre-injury anticoagulation, which was associated with significantly higher mortality across all TTA strata. This finding is consistent with prior registry studies showing that anticoagulated trauma patients have increased rates of intracranial hemorrhage, transfusion, and mortality [17, 30, 31]. Given the widespread and increasing use of vitamin K antagonists and direct oral anticoagulants in the elderly population, the integration of anticoagulation status into triage and activation algorithms should be strongly considered in future guideline revisions [18].

Overall, these findings underscore the importance of a precise and reproducible TTA process. Accurate TTA ensures timely availability of surgical, radiological, and intensive care resources, which is critical for preventing avoidable mortality and morbidity [32]. By addressing demographic realities and formally integrating geriatric-specific risks into the triage algorithm, the 2022 guideline update represents a significant step toward more effective trauma care for elderly patients.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the analysis is based on retrospective registry data and is therefore subject to documentation bias, coding inaccuracies, and unmeasured confounding. In addition, this study represents a retrospective mapping analysis and does not evaluate the prospective clinical implementation of the 2022 criteria. Second, the “None” group is inherently heterogeneous and may include both undertriaged high-risk patients and lower-risk cases activated due to clinical uncertainty. Third, AIS-based variables were used retrospectively as surrogates for anatomical injury burden and are not applicable for real-time prehospital triage decision-making. Fourth, the use of a complete-case approach may have introduced selection bias, particularly if missing data were not randomly distributed across patient subgroups. Fifth, the inclusion criteria of the TR-DGU focus on patients requiring ICU admission or those who died early, which may lead to an overrepresentation of more severely injured patients and limit generalizability to less severely injured populations. Finally, the primary outcome was in-hospital mortality; functional outcomes, quality of life, and long-term disability were not assessed. Alternative age cutoffs and trauma systems outside Germany may yield different results.

Since April 2025, TTA criteria have been explicitly and systematically documented within the TR-DGU, which will enable more accurate evaluation of guideline implementation and facilitate future analyses of field triage.

Conclusion

This study validates the updated 2022 DGU TTA criteria using national registry data. In contrast to the 2016 version, the new framework provides a transparent allocation of patients, with mortality consistently highest in HRSI, intermediate in MRSI, and lowest in patients without criteria. The addition of geriatric-specific criteria—systolic blood pressure 91–100 mmHg, GCS 13–14, and AIS ≥ 3 injuries in multiple regions—proved effective in identifying elderly trauma patients at increased risk.
