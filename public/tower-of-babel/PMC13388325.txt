Guidelines for daily sodium intake: a counterpoint to the current recommendation for low sodium intake
Authors: Andrew Mente
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeveral health organizations suggest that people should consume less sodium, aiming for an intake below 2.3 g per day on the premise that lowering sodium intake will lower blood pressure and, in turn, reduce the incidence of cardiovascular disease. However, these guidelines were created without effective means of maintaining low-sodium diets in real life, and strong evidence is lacking that shows low sodium intake leads to fewer cardiovascular events compared with average intake levels. This review evaluates whether the advice to consume less sodium is backed by solid evidence. It argues that most people consume a moderate amount of sodium (3 to 5 g per day), which is associated with the lowest risk of cardiovascular disease, and recommends a target below 5 g per day. Results from large randomized controlled trials evaluating the effect of sodium reduction on clinical events and mortality are awaited. This is particularly relevant in clinical practice, where sodium-containing medications such as sodium oxybate (SO), a mainstay in narcolepsy treatment, contribute to daily intake but are not considered by sodium guidelines. Clinicians should evaluate each patient’s total sodium intake from both diet and medications and make personalized recommendations based on their cardiovascular and renal risk profile.Keywords: sodium, guidelines, cardiovascular disease, medications, pharmacologic, sodium oxybate, narcolepsy

Introduction

Several health organizations recommend low sodium intake (<2.3 g/day) for entire populations [1–3]. This advice is based on the idea that lowering sodium intake will reduce blood pressure (BP), which in turn lowers cardiovascular disease (CVD) and deaths in the population [4]. However, despite population-wide initiatives, there is no evidence that it is feasible to sustainably lower sodium intake in entire populations or that lowering sodium intake from average levels (3000–4000 mg/day) to low levels (<2300 mg/day) would reduce the risk of CVD or total mortality [5]. Additionally, these guidelines may not suit individuals in a clinical setting, given that sodium restriction may pose several risks such as hyponatremia, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) activation, or insulin resistance—especially in older adults or those with multiple comorbidities [6, 7].

Sodium is an essential nutrient that plays a central role in fluid balance, nerve function, and vascular regulation. On this basis, it is expected that sodium has a physiologic “healthy” range of intake, as with other essential electrolytes [8, 9]. Approximately 95% of the world’s population consumes 3 to 6 grams of sodium daily, meaning that current guidelines recommending intake below 2.3 g/day for entire populations are well below typical consumption levels [10]. Further, these targets are not routinely achieved in any population, and growing evidence suggests that very low sodium intake may not confer additional benefit and could be associated with harm [5, 11]. Cohort studies have consistently shown a J-shaped association between sodium intake and CV outcomes, with the lowest risk observed among individuals consuming 3 to 5 g/day [5, 9, 11–17]. To date, no observational study or clinical trial has demonstrated that sodium intake below 2.3 g/day, compared with average consumption, significantly reduces the risk of CV events [5, 18].

Guideline recommendations—often derived from short-term BP trials and population-level modeling—may not adequately reflect individual variation in sodium needs or account for the feasibility of sustained restriction. This is particularly relevant in clinical practice, where sodium-containing medications such as SO, a mainstay in narcolepsy treatment, contribute significantly (eg <1640 mg of sodium) to daily intake—nearly reaching the current recommended limit [19, 20]. As such, understanding the nuances of sodium physiology, the limitations of existing evidence, the individual variation in sodium needs, and the practical realities of sodium intake—including from therapeutic agents—is essential for optimizing individual patient care.

Setting the bar for sodium intake—but is the bar too low?

High sodium intake has long been considered as a modifiable risk factor for elevated BP and hypertension. Excess dietary sodium contributes to increases in BP through both pressure-dependent and pressure-independent mechanisms, including endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, inflammation, and left ventricular hypertrophy [21]. These associations have informed decades of public health policy advocating for sodium restriction as a primary preventive measure against CVD.

Global sodium intake recommendations reflect broad public health goals but vary by region. The World Health Organization (WHO) advises that sodium intake for adults be limited to 2000 mg/day (<5 g/day of salt) and set a goal in 2013 for a 30% population-level reduction by 2025 to curb noncommunicable diseases [1]. The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) promotes substantial reductions in sodium intake from ~3400 mg/day to a target of 2300 mg/day through food industry reformulation and education [2]. For individuals at high risk of hypertension or CVD, such as those with diabetes, the American Heart Association (AHA) and American College of Cardiology (ACC) advocate an even stricter target of less than 1500 mg/day [22]. In contrast, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) takes a more moderate position, designating 2.0 g/day as both safe and adequate, but declining to set a tolerable upper intake level due to insufficient evidence of harm at higher intakes [3]. This reflects a flexible, individualized approach rather than a rigid limit. This view is reinforced by the 2023 European Society of Hypertension (ESH) and 2024 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines, which recommend sodium restriction (<100 mmol/day or ~2.3 g/day) for individuals with hypertension, CVD, or chronic kidney disease (CKD), while stopping short of endorsing universal restriction for the general population [18, 23].

Both EFSA and European specialty societies emphasize the limited outcome-level data supporting a sodium intake of less than 2.3 g/day. While sodium reduction lowers BP, existing guidelines were developed without evidence of effective interventions to achieve sustained low sodium intake in free-living individuals and without high-quality evidence that low sodium intake, compared with average intake, reduces CVD events [5, 18, 23].

Furthermore, current guidelines often fail to account for non-dietary sources of sodium, such as medications. Sodium-containing drugs—including effervescent formulations and SO, used in the treatment of narcolepsy—can contribute significantly to daily intake. At therapeutic doses, SO can add up to 1640 mg of sodium per day, potentially pushing patients above recommended limits even with dietary compliance [19, 20]. This underscores the need for clinicians to consider total sodium exposure—dietary and pharmacologic—when managing patients with comorbid hypertension, CVD, or CKD.

Finally, individual variability in sodium sensitivity complicates the application of a “one-size-fits-all” guideline. Factors such as age, ethnicity, kidney function, baseline BP, and genetic predisposition influence an individual’s response to sodium intake [21]. While some individuals benefit from moderate restriction, others—particularly normotensive adults without risk factors—may experience little or no reduction in BP or CVD risk with low-sodium diets.

In light of these considerations, experts increasingly support a more individualized approach to sodium management. While public health strategies should continue to target high sodium consumption in at-risk populations, overly conservative targets may be neither feasible nor beneficial for the general population. Until large-scale randomized trials confirm long-term benefits of very low sodium intake, current guidelines should be interpreted with clinical judgment and adapted to each patient’s comorbidities, lifestyle, and total sodium exposure [21, 24, 25].

Physiologic and psychological considerations at very low sodium intake

Sodium is an essential nutrient, and both deficiency and excess can have harmful effects on health. Intake below 1.5 g/day may increase CV, metabolic, and neurocognitive risks, particularly in older adults or those with comorbidities such as diabetes or heart failure [26, 27]. Intervention studies have shown that low sodium intakes activate the RAAS, increasing renin, aldosterone, and sympathetic tone, which may increase the risk of vascular damage and CVD [27, 28]. A 2023 meta-regression found that intake below 100 mmol/day strongly activates the RAAS, with elevated plasma renin and aldosterone levels that may negate CV benefit and increase metabolic risk [29, 30]. Additional intervention studies have linked low-sodium diets to elevated fasting insulin and reduced insulin sensitivity, with one 2022 trial showing worsened glucose metabolism despite improved BP [31, 32]. In frail or elderly patients, especially those on diuretics or selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), overly restrictive sodium intake has been associated with hyponatremia, falls, and cognitive changes [33, 34]. Studies show that hyponatremia prevalence exceeds 10% with either agent alone and rises to ~13% when both are combined [35, 36]. This underscores the need for caution when applying low-sodium targets to frail or multimorbid individuals. These risks underscore the importance of individualizing sodium recommendations and avoiding rigid population-based targets in vulnerable groups.

Low sodium intake: cardiovascular health benefit or risk?

Blood pressure: clinical trials and observational evidence

Robust evidence from clinical trials shows that reducing dietary sodium intake lowers BP, with the magnitude of benefit most pronounced in individuals with hypertension or prehypertension. However, much of this evidence is from short-term studies with relatively small sample sizes. Multiple meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials report dose-dependent reductions in systolic BP ranging from ~2 to 9 mm Hg, depending on baseline BP, sodium intake level, and trial duration [27, 37].

Sodium recommendations have been largely based on the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH)-Sodium trial, a 30-day proof-of-concept feeding trial which demonstrated that lowering sodium intake can significantly lower BP in people consuming a low amount of potassium, particularly in high-risk groups [24, 38]. In this trial, participants who followed the DASH diet and reduced sodium intake to ~1.5 g/day experienced systolic BP reductions of up to 7 mm Hg compared with those consuming 3.3 g/day, with greater effects seen among individuals with elevated baseline BP and low potassium intake (~1600 mg/day) [37, 38]. These findings have been influential in recommendations to lower sodium intake to below 2.3 g/day and ideally to 1.5 g/day for the entire adult population. However, there are no data indicating that such large reductions in sodium intake would be achievable or sustainable in real-world settings.

The Trials of Hypertension Prevention (TOHP) I and II demonstrated more modest reductions in systolic BP (~1.2/0.7 mm Hg) in the intervention groups; however, sodium intakes below 2.3 g/day were not sustained beyond 6 months of follow-up [39]. In TOHP II, mean sodium intake was reduced to ~3.1 g/day in the intervention group and ~3.9 g/day in the control group over a 3-year period, highlighting both the clinical benefit of sodium reduction and the difficulty of achieving sodium intakes below 2.3 g/day in real-world settings [39]. The TONE trial showed that older adults following a sodium-restricted diet required fewer antihypertensive medications and maintained better BP control [40]. However, in both the TONE and TOHP trials, the control group did not receive a dietary counseling intervention (eg, advice on healthy diet irrespective of salt intake). As a result, these trials were unable to determine the independent effects of sodium reduction separate from overall improvements in diet quality. A Cochrane meta-analysis also found meaningful BP reductions in adults with hypertension and normal BP, though long-term outcome data were limited [41]. Recent findings reaffirm the BP-lowering effects of sodium reduction. A 2023 crossover trial found that more than 90% of participants experienced BP reductions with lower sodium intake, regardless of hypertension status or medication use [42]. Still, BP response varies by salt sensitivity, which depends on age, genetics, potassium intake, and comorbidities [43].

Observational studies provide additional, albeit more nuanced, evidence of the sodium–BP relationship. The INTERSALT study found a significant positive association between sodium excretion and BP across populations; however, this relationship was weaker and inconsistent at the individual level, particularly after adjusting for potential confounders such as age and body mass index [27, 44]. Similarly, the INTERMAP study, which included 4680 participants across multiple countries, showed regional variability in sodium–BP associations and noted a modest mean increase in systolic BP of ~2.1 mm Hg per gram of sodium intake in multivariable models [27]. These findings underscore the complexity of sodium’s effect on BP and the influence of other dietary and environmental factors.

Cardiovascular events: cohort and meta-analytic evidence

Beyond BP reduction, the evidence linking sodium intake to CV events and mortality is less clear. Most of the evidence base derives from short-term BP-lowering trials rather than long-term randomized studies evaluating clinical endpoints such as myocardial infarction or mortality [5, 18]. Longitudinal follow-up from TOHP I and II reported a 25% to 30% lower risk of CVD events in participants assigned to sodium reduction interventions [39]. However, the TOHP trial follow-up did not address the effect of low sodium (<2.3 g/day) per se, since the trial did not achieve the low sodium intake target in the intervention group, with the group achieving a mean sodium intake of 3.1 g/day at 18 months and 3.2 g/day at 36 months despite intensive dietary counseling. Thus, sustained sodium intake below 2.3 g/day is difficult to maintain even in a controlled clinical trial setting. Nationally, average sodium intake in the US ranges from 3.0 to 3.5 g/day in women and 4.1 to 4.4 g/day in men—levels that fall within or near the “optimal” risk range identified in population-based studies [5]. Additionally, the TOHP trial lost 23% of participants to follow-up for CV outcomes, but the follow-up was complete for mortality, with no observed difference in events [45]. More recently, the SODIUM-HF trial, testing less than 1500 mg/day of sodium in patients with heart failure, found no reduction in death or hospitalizations compared with usual care (HR 0.89; p = .53) at 12 months [46].

Globally, the PURE study, a large international cohort that assessed sodium intake in over 100 000 adults using estimated 24-hour urinary sodium excretion, found a J-shaped association between sodium intake and CV outcomes. CV event rates and total mortality were lowest among individuals consuming 3 to 5 g/day of sodium, while both higher (>6 g/day) and lower (<3 g/day) intakes were associated with increased risk [14, 47, 48]. These findings were particularly pronounced in individuals with hypertension or low potassium intake [14]. Notably, excess risk from high intake (>5 g/day) was mostly seen in regions with very high baseline consumption, such as China, suggesting that broad sodium reduction may be most relevant in such settings [48].

Similar findings were observed in the ONTARGET and TRANSCEND studies, which included high-risk individuals with CVD or diabetes. These cohorts found increased CV mortality at both extremes of sodium intake, with higher risk at less than 3 g/day and at greater than 6 g/day, compared with a moderate intake of 3 to 6 g/day [49]. Further, a similar J-shaped pattern was found in men and women in the large UK Biobank study [50, 51]. Such findings have challenged the assumption that “lower is better” and raised questions about whether current guideline targets (<2.3 g/day) are optimal or feasible for all populations.

Some have criticized the PURE study and other similar studies, citing methodological limitations, including reliance on spot urine collections and potential reverse causality [26]. However, other cohort studies that used single or repeat 24-hour urine collections and dietary assessments have reported similar risk patterns, suggesting the J-shaped curve is not merely an artifact of how sodium intake is measured [13, 21]. Additionally, a 2020 BMJ meta-analysis found increased mortality associated with sodium intakes below 1.5 g/day, and the ONTARGET trial observed elevated CV risk in high-risk patients consuming less than 2.3 g/day [13, 21].

Overall, there is consistency in the results from studies using 24-hour urine collections (the reference method) and single urine voids. In fact, from the 9 cohort studies which employed 24-hour urine collections, 4 reported an inverse association of sodium intake with CVD outcomes, 2 reported no association, 2 reported a J-shaped association, and 2 reported a positive association with CVD risk [52-61]. Of those reporting a positive association, none reported a significantly lower risk with low (<2.5 g/day) versus moderate sodium intake. Therefore, there is no scientific basis behind the claim that reducing sodium intake from average levels (3–5 g/day) to low levels (<2.3 g/day) will reduce CVD events or mortality. This has been echoed in 2 recent reviews by a group of experts with a range of views and backgrounds and in 2 editorials published in The Lancet [5, 18, 62, 63].

Conversely, modeling studies using BP to project sodium effects on health show a different conclusion. The NutriCode model, a widely cited global analysis, estimated that 1.65 million CV deaths annually could be attributed to a sodium intake of greater than 2 g/day [64]. However, this model relies on assumed linear relationships between sodium intake and CV risk and the notion that sodium impacts only BP and no other biological systems in the body, an unprecedented assumption for an essential nutrient such as sodium which is needed for normal physiological function. As such, modeling exercises such as NutriCode may incorrectly characterize the effects of sodium intake on health outcomes as seen in cohort studies [65]. More recently, umbrella reviews and meta-analyses have reported mixed results. While some show reduced CV and all-cause mortality with sodium reduction—particularly in hypertensive populations—others note limited or no significant benefit at intakes below 2.3 g/day [37, 66, 67].

Importantly, several US-based cohort studies, including analyses of National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data and the Scottish Heart Study, have failed to demonstrate consistent associations between sodium intake and CV outcomes in individuals consuming 2 to 4 g/day [26, 68]. These null findings in moderate intake ranges further support the hypothesis of a nonlinear relationship and suggest that sodium restriction to currently recommended levels may not provide additional benefit compared with average intakes in general populations.

In the Salt Substitute and Stroke Study (SSaSS), potassium-enriched salt (containing 75% sodium chloride and 25% potassium chloride) resulted in reduced stroke and CVD events compared with regular salt in rural villages in China. However, the study population was characterized by a markedly higher sodium intake (4.3 g/day) and lower potassium intake (1.4 g/day) than typically found in the US population (3.5 g/day sodium and 2.1 g/day potassium), which limits generalizability to Western populations or those using sodium-containing medications [69, 70]. Further, the reduction in sodium intake in the intervention group was only 8%, while the increase in potassium intake was 57% [71]. Therefore, it is likely that most or all of the benefits observed in the SSaSS trial were due to the increase in potassium with the salt substitute.

Interpreting the evidence for clinical practice

Sodium reduction is a proven strategy for lowering BP, but its impact on CV outcomes is less definitive. Trials and meta-analyses consistently show that reduced sodium intake lowers systolic BP across individuals both with and without hypertension [26, 37]. However, the evidence linking very low intake (<2.3 g/day) to reduced CV morbidity or mortality, compared with moderate or average levels of intake (3–5 g/day), is practically nonexistent [5, 18]. Numerous cohort studies using different methods to estimate sodium intake in various populations (generally healthy people, individuals with or without hypertension, with or without diabetes, and with or without vascular disease) show a J-shaped association between sodium intake and CVD or mortality. Observational studies like PURE, ONTARGET, and the UK Biobank associate both high (>6 g/day) and low (<3 g/day) sodium intake with increased CV risk, with the lowest event rates found at ~3 to 5 g/day [37, 72].

Clinically, this moderate range (~3–5 g/day) may offer the best balance—lowering BP without activating compensatory mechanisms such as RAAS, which can undermine CV benefit at very low intake levels [28, 37]. Thus, aiming for moderate sodium intake while personalizing dietary advice based on comorbidities and total sodium burden may be the most effective approach.

Pharmacologic sodium load: an overlooked contributor

Current guidelines typically do not account for sodium intake from medications, a gap with real clinical implications. SO, used for treating narcolepsy, can contribute up to 1640 mg/day—nearly meeting or exceeding EFSA’s recommended total daily intake [19, 73]. Extended-release and low-sodium formulations reduce sodium content by 92%, making them preferable for patients with hypertension, CKD, or salt sensitivity [74]. Despite this, no existing dietary guidelines explicitly address a medication-based sodium burden, leaving clinicians without clear recommendations.

For patients with comorbidities, especially those on chronic sodium-containing therapies, clinicians should consider total sodium exposure—both dietary and pharmacologic—and monitor for BP or metabolic changes. Shared decision-making is essential to weigh symptom control against potential CV risks [73, 75].

Clinical heterogeneity in sodium sensitivity

Population-wide sodium guidelines (eg, <2.3 g/day) are grounded in strong BP–lowering data, but they do not fully account for individual variability in sodium sensitivity, feasibility of sustained restriction, or pharmacologic sodium exposure. Beyond measurement challenges, wide interindividual variability in physiological responses to sodium further undermines the utility of uniform intake targets. Salt sensitivity—a condition in which BP changes substantially in response to sodium intake—varies with age, sex, race, body mass index (BMI), kidney function, and genetic background [76]. For example, salt sensitivity is more prevalent among older adults, individuals of African ancestry, and those with certain genetic polymorphisms.

Despite its clinical relevance, salt sensitivity is not routinely tested, and there is currently no standardized method for diagnosis. Recent studies highlight that even in tightly controlled feeding trials, a subset of individuals with normal BP exhibits “inverse salt sensitivity,” wherein BP paradoxically increases with sodium restriction [77, 78]. These findings emphasize the need for a more individualized approach to sodium management that considers not only baseline CV and renal risk but also physiological response [79, 80].

Consequences of overgeneralization

Universal sodium restriction targets, though well intentioned, can have unintended consequences in clinical practice. Reducing sodium in processed foods may affect palatability and decrease adherence, particularly in patients with neurologic conditions or sleep disorders who already face dietary challenges. Potassium-based salt substitutes, often used in sodium-reduction strategies, may produce a bitter or metallic taste and are unsuitable for individuals with CKD [81]. Additionally, sodium restriction without careful nutritional planning may reduce the intake of other essential electrolytes like potassium and magnesium, especially if reformulated foods are not nutritionally rebalanced. These issues are rarely addressed in clinical guidelines. An added concern is the interaction between dietary sodium restriction and sodium-containing medications. For example, SO, used for narcolepsy, can contribute up to 1.6 g of sodium per night—nearly the full daily recommended limit. Yet no studies have evaluated how pharmacologic sodium exposure interacts with restricted diets, creating a gap in guidance for specific populations [82]. Without accounting for total sodium burden, clinicians may inadvertently exceed targets or impose unnecessary restrictions.

Recommendations for clinicians

For clinicians, navigating sodium intake recommendations requires a practical, patient-centered approach—particularly when managing individuals with comorbidities such as hypertension, CKD, or CV risk. While public health efforts have emphasized dietary sodium reduction, less attention has been given to the sodium content of medications, which can significantly affect total daily sodium exposure.

Assess total sodium exposure: diet and therapy

Recent European and international guidance highlights the need for comprehensive sodium assessment that includes both dietary and pharmacologic sources. Medications such as effervescent analgesics, dispersible tablets, and SO formulations can contribute substantially to daily sodium intake—sometimes exceeding dietary targets even when patients consume a “low-sodium” diet. This is especially relevant in patients with salt-sensitive conditions, where cumulative sodium exposure may exacerbate BP elevation or fluid retention [19, 73].

What does this mean for patients with narcolepsy taking sodium oxybate?

For patients with narcolepsy, the choice of SO formulation can significantly affect total sodium intake. Immediate-release SO (SXB) contributes ~1.6 to 1.9 g of sodium per night—often exceeding half of the recommended daily limit in a single therapy [20, 83]. A lower-sodium oxybate formulation, which contain 92% less sodium, is preferred in patients with hypertension, CVD, or CKD [20].

Additionally, extended-release formulations can reduce overall sodium burden and offer improved adherence by eliminating the need for nighttime dosing interruptions [74, 83].

Current sodium guidelines do not account for medication-derived sodium, leaving a gap in clinical assessment and counseling [2, 41]. Clinicians should evaluate cumulative sodium exposure and consider prescribing lower-sodium or extended-release SO formulations, particularly in patients with comorbidities [84, 85].

Monitor blood pressure, renal function, and weight

Initiation of sodium oxybate has been associated with increases in BP, even in individuals with normal BP. In 1 study, nearly 1 in 10 patients with narcolepsy developed new-onset hypertension during treatment, highlighting the importance of routine monitoring [75]. Clinicians should evaluate BP, renal function (eg, estimated glomerular filtration rate), and body weight at baseline and periodically during treatment with sodium-containing medications. Patients with preexisting hypertension or renal impairment may require more frequent assessments or dose adjustments.

In high-risk patients, regular monitoring of BP, serum sodium, and possibly 24-hour urinary sodium may be warranted. Shared decision-making is essential to ensure patients understand the trade-offs between symptom control and sodium burden and to guide personalized treatment decisions [83, 86].

Educate patients and engage in shared decision-making

Patients are often unaware of the sodium content of their medications. Clinicians should proactively educate patients about the unique contribution of pharmacologic sodium—particularly in the context of therapies like SO—and explain how this may influence CV or renal risk. These discussions are crucial when determining whether to use standard or lower-sodium formulations.

Shared decision-making should incorporate patient preferences, comorbidities, lifestyle factors, and long-term treatment goals. When framed within the context of broader CV risk management, informed treatment choices can help balance narcolepsy symptom control with optimized sodium exposure.
