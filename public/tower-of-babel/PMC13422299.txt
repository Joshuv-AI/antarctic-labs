A cross-sectional study on disaster adaptation and resilience among residents in Guangdong Province, China and analysis of its influencing factors
Authors: Hanxi Chen, Guandie Hong, Siyin Chen, Yingwen Huang, Xin Ma, Siyi Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn disaster-prone areas, residents’ disaster adaptation and resilience are important components of community resilience. This study aimed to assess the level of disaster adaptation and resilience among residents and to analyze its influencing factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Guangdong Province, China, from November 2023 to July 2024. A total of 3,217 residents were recruited using a purposive convenience sampling method with geographic quotas. Due to the overrepresentation of females in the sample, post-stratification weighting was applied to adjust for gender imbalance. Disaster adaptation and resilience levels were assessed using the Disaster Adaptation and Resilience Scale. The total scores were categorized into four levels based on quartiles (P25, P50, and P75). Multiple linear regression was used to identify associated factors, and a sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the results.ResultsA total of 3,181 valid questionnaires were collected in this survey, yielding an effective response rate of 98.88%. After weighting, the final sample size was 3,182. The total disaster adaptation and resilience score was 115.61 ± 36.36, and the item mean score was 2.57 ± 0.81, indicating a relatively upper-middle level based on the quartile classification (P50 = 111; P75 = 140). Among the five dimensions, optimism had the highest score (2.62 ± 0.89), whereas physical resources had the lowest score (2.49 ± 0.94). Weighted multiple linear regression analysis showed that health status (β = 0.140, p < 0.001), actively following disaster-related media (β = 0.099, p < 0.001), direct experience of disaster events (β = −0.075, p < 0.001), marital status (β = −0.072, p < 0.001), disaster preparedness (prepared: β = 0.043, p = 0.022; planned but not yet acted: β = −0.039, p = 0.034), and gender (β = 0.036, p = 0.040) were associated with residents’ disaster adaptation and resilience. Sensitivity analysis showed that the results for the main variables were generally consistent with the primary analysis, although slight differences were observed for some covariates.ConclusionsThis study provides scientific evidence for improving disaster adaptation and resilience among residents in disaster-prone coastal areas of China and for developing public health intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27919-8.Keywords: Residents, Disaster adaptation and resilience, Influencing factors, China

Introduction

Disasters have become a major global public health challenge. With the acceleration of globalization and the combined effects of climate change, urbanization, and industrialization, the frequency and intensity of various disasters have increased significantly, posing substantial threats to human health, social stability, and economic development [1]. Disasters not only result in direct physical injuries and economic losses but also lead to long-term psychological distress and social functioning impairments among affected populations [2]. According to data from the United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction, global disasters over the past two decades have caused annual direct economic losses of approximately USD 180–200 billion, with total losses exceeding USD 2.3 trillion [3].

China, as one of the most disaster-prone countries worldwide, experiences a wide variety of disasters with extensive geographic distribution, and the characteristics of disaster risks vary significantly across regions [4]. According to data released by the Office of the National Commission for Disaster Reduction and the Ministry of Emergency Management of China, more than 94.13 million people were affected by various natural disasters nationwide in 2024, resulting in direct economic losses of approximately CNY 401.11 billion [5]. Guangdong Province, located along the southeastern coast of China, is one of the country’s most populous and economically developed provinces [6], and is also highly vulnerable to natural disasters [7]. Reports indicate that during the 2023–2024 flood season, Guangdong experienced multiple rounds of heavy rainfall and typhoon landfalls. In 2023, floods and typhoons affected 4.5907 million people, causing direct economic losses of 16.537 billion RMB; in 2024, 13 numbered floods occurred across the Beijiang, Xijiang, Dongjiang, and Hanjiang rivers, with extreme rainfall and flood events continuing frequently, indicating high regional disaster exposure risk [8, 9]. Moreover, surveillance data from the Guangdong Provincial Health Commission show that, during multiple periods in 2023–2024, respiratory infectious diseases such as influenza consistently ranked among the top reported Class C notifiable diseases [10, 11]. These disaster-related events pose serious threats to local infrastructure, community functioning, and residents’ physical and mental health.

Against this backdrop, enhancing disaster resilience and adaptability at the individual level has become a key public health issue in reducing disaster risk and promoting post-disaster recovery. Previous research has shown that higher levels of resilience can mitigate the adverse effects of disasters on mental health, promote positive coping behaviours, and accelerate the post-disaster recovery process [12]. In disaster situations, ‘resilience’ and ‘adaptation’ are closely related, but conceptually distinct. Disaster resilience is typically defined as the ability of individuals, communities or societies to adapt effectively, respond appropriately and return to normal functioning when faced with disasters or situations of significant stress [13]. In comparison, adaptation is often regarded as a dynamic process, referring to the adjustments, cognitive strategies and resource allocation strategies that individuals may adopt in response to disaster risks and their potential impacts [14]. It emphasises an individual’s ability to utilise available resources, anticipate risks, respond appropriately and recover [15, 16]. Adaptation can therefore be understood as one of the key mechanisms through which resilience is manifested and realised. Resilience focuses on overall capacity and the outcomes of an individual’s recovery, whereas adaptation reflects the coping strategies and continuous adjustments an individual adopts throughout the course of a disaster; it is a concrete manifestation of resilience and a key pathway to achieving it.

To measure disaster resilience at the individual level, First et al. developed the Disaster Adaptation and Resilience Scale (DARS), which comprises five dimensions—physical resources, social resources, problem solving, distress regulation and optimism—and consists of 43 items [17]. In this context, physical resources refer to the tangible and economic resources that individuals possess or can access in a disaster situation, including their ability to secure income, housing security and basic necessities [17]. This dimension is primarily used to measure an individual’s level of preparedness prior to a disaster, their response during a disaster, and their reliance on and access to material support during the post-disaster recovery process. Social resources refer to the accessibility and availability of supportive social relationships that individuals can access, including support from family, friends and community networks [18]. This dimension primarily measures an individual’s ability and extent to which they receive emotional support, informational support and instrumental support (such as practical assistance and resource provision) in disaster situations. Problem solving refers to an individual’s cognitive and behavioural capacity to identify problems, analyse situations and take effective measures in response to disaster-related challenges [19]. This dimension primarily assesses an individual’s judgement, decision-making ability, adaptability and ability to execute tasks. Distress regulation refers to an individual’s ability to recognise, cope with and alleviate negative emotional reactions or psychological distress triggered by disaster-related stressors [17]. This dimension primarily measures an individual’s ability to actively regulate their emotional state and alleviate psychological distress in stressful disaster situations. Optimism refers to an individual’s tendency to hold positive hopes or a hopeful attitude towards the future [20]. This dimension primarily reflects an individual’s confidence and positive mindset when faced with the uncertainty of disasters; it is a key component of psychological resilience and helps to promote adaptive coping behaviours and the post-disaster recovery process [20]. Overall, these five dimensions encompass both external resources (physical and social resources) and internal capabilities (problem solving, distress regulation and optimism), providing a comprehensive framework for assessing disaster adaptation and resilience at the individual level.

A number of tools for assessing individual disaster resilience have been developed internationally [12, 21], among which the DARS and the Disaster Resilience Scale for Individuals (DRSi) have demonstrated good reliability and validity across different populations [17, 22]. In China, Chen et al. conducted a Chinese adaptation and reliability and validity testing of the DARS scale, providing a reliable tool for assessing disaster adaptation and resilience at the individual level among the Chinese population, and laying the foundation for the development of targeted public health interventions and the enhancement of residents’ disaster response capabilities [13].

The Social-Ecological Model (SEM) posits that individuals’ health behaviours and resilience are influenced by a combination of factors at multiple levels, including the interaction between individual characteristics, social support networks and broader environmental factors [23–25]. Within this theoretical framework, disaster adaptation and resilience are viewed as dynamic adaptive processes shaped by multi-level influences; they depend not only on individual psychological and demographic characteristics, but are also shaped by the combined effects of multidimensional factors such as social structures, access to information and availability of resources. Previous research has identified factors influencing disaster resilience from various perspectives. At the individual level, gender [26, 27], age [12], marital status [28], educational attainment [29], monthly income and health status [30] are all associated with resilience. At the societal level, factors such as living with family members [31] and the use of social media [32] are considered to enhance individuals’ coping abilities through the dissemination of information and the mobilisation of resources. Furthermore, there may be a complex relationship between exposure to disasters and resilience; research has found that exposure to disasters may increase mental health risks, whilst at the same time enhancing individual resilience through adaptive processes [33].

Although research on disaster adaptation and resilience has increased in recent years, there remain several key gaps in the existing literature. Firstly, previous research has primarily focused on building community resilience [34, 35]; studies at the individual level among the general population in developing countries, particularly in Asia, remain relatively limited, and there is a lack of empirical evidence based on standardised measurement tools. Secondly, although previous studies have explored the relationships between social and behavioral factors and disaster adaptation and resilience [12], relatively few have systematically integrated multilevel factors—such as sociodemographic, socioeconomic, and behavioral variables—within a theory-driven framework. This lack of theory-driven, multi-level analysis has, to some extent, limited our in-depth understanding of the mechanisms underlying disaster adaptation and resilience, and has also hindered the development of targeted public health intervention strategies. In addition, existing studies have primarily focused on the development and psychometric validation of disaster adaptation and resilience scales [13]. However, systematic analyses of the relationships between disaster adaptation and resilience scales and external variables such as sociodemographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, health, and behavioral factors remain limited. Moreover, there is a lack of multi-level mechanism exploration based on the social ecological model. Building on this gap, the present study, grounded in the social ecological model, systematically examines the associations between multiple dimensions—including sociodemographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, health status, disaster experience, access to disaster-related information, and disaster preparedness behaviors—and disaster adaptation and resilience. From a multilevel perspective, this study further enriches the theoretical framework explaining the mechanisms underlying disaster adaptation and resilience. Against this backdrop, Guangdong Province in China—a densely populated coastal region prone to frequent natural disasters—presents a typical disaster risk scenario; consequently, research into the disaster adaptation and resilience of the region’s residents holds significant practical and real-world importance.

Accordingly, this study focuses on residents of Guangdong Province, China, and employs a validated scale of disaster adaptation and resilience to conduct a large-scale survey. Within the framework of the socio-ecological model, it integrates multi-dimensional factors—including individual and societal dimensions—to systematically analyse their relationship with disaster adaptation and resilience. This study provides a comprehensive and theory-based analysis of resilience at the individual level. Furthermore, the study employed post-stratification weighting to improve the representativeness of the sample, which may help reduce sampling bias. This study not only adds to the empirical evidence at the individual level in disaster-prone coastal regions of China, but also provides a basis for conducting disaster adaptation and resilience assessments, as well as for formulating public health interventions and disaster management strategies in coastal areas of similar developing countries; furthermore, it contributes to a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying the development of disaster adaptation and resilience.

The primary objective of this study is to assess the levels of disaster adaptation and resilience among residents in Guangdong Province, China, and the factors influencing them. The specific objectives are as follows:

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted from November 2023 to July 2024 in four administrative regions of Guangdong Province, China: the Pearl River Delta, Eastern Guangdong, Western Guangdong , and Northern Guangdong. Purposive convenience sampling with geographic quotas was used to recruit participants, with the aim of assessing residents’ disaster adaptation and resilience levels and identifying associated factors. The inclusion criteria were as follows: residents with household registration in Guangdong Province; having lived in Guangdong Province for more than six months; aged 18 years or older; voluntarily participating in the study; and able to understand and complete the electronic survey. The exclusion criterion was: non-permanent residents of Guangdong Province.

Population and sample

According to the sample size estimation principle for multivariable studies, the sample size is generally set at 10–20 times the number of independent variables [36]. In this study, there were 10 independent variables. Considering the possibility of invalid questionnaires during the survey, the sample size was increased by 20%, resulting in a required sample of 120–240 participants. A total of 3,217 questionnaires were distributed, and 3,181 valid questionnaires were returned, yielding an effective response rate of 98.88%.

Variables and data collection procedure

Data were collected using a questionnaire consisting of two parts. The first part was a general information survey, self-designed by the researchers based on a review of relevant literature, comprising 10 items. These included gender, age, education level, monthly income, marital status, living situation, health status, direct experience of disaster events, exposure to disaster-related media, and disaster preparedness.

The second part employed the Disaster Adaptation and Resilience Scale (DARS), developed by First et al. in 2021 to measure the adaptation and resilience levels of individuals exposed to disasters [17]. Chen Hanxi et al. translated the scale into Chinese in 2024. The scale comprises five dimensions with a total of 45 items: physical resources (10 items), social resources (11 items), problem solving (8 items), distress regulation (9 items), and optimism (7 items). Detailed item descriptions are provided in the supplementary materials (Table S8 and S9). Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “note true at all” to “true nearly all of the time” and scored from 0 to 4, with a total score of 0–180; higher scores indicate better disaster adaptation and resilience. The Chinese version of the DARS demonstrated excellent reliability, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.978, and good validity [13]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.987. The total scores were categorized into four quartile-based levels [37].

Data were collected online using the “Wenjuanxing” platform. The survey link was distributed through multiple channels, including social media platforms (e.g., WeChat Moments), student management platforms of medical universities, and community service centers. Prior to participation, the purpose, content, and important instructions of the study were explained in the survey introduction, and participants were informed that participation was voluntary. To ensure questionnaire completeness and prevent duplicate responses, all questions were set as mandatory, incomplete responses could not proceed to the next section, and each IP address was allowed to submit only once. The questionnaire did not collect personal identifiers such as names or contact information. During data collection, questionnaires completed in less than one minute or showing obviously patterned responses were excluded.

Data processing and analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Given that the proportion of female participants in the sample (83.9%) was substantially higher than that reported in the Seventh National Population Census of Guangdong Province (46.93%) [38], post-stratification weighting was applied to correct for the sex distribution. Weights were calculated as the ratio of the population proportion to the sample proportion, resulting in weights of 3.29 for males and 0.56 for females. After weighting, the sex distribution of the sample (53.0% male and 47.0% female) closely approximated that of the Guangdong provincial population (53.07% male and 46.93% female), thereby improving the representativeness of the sample. All primary analyses were conducted using the weighted data. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, Q–Q plots, and skewness and kurtosis indices. Given the large sample size, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test is highly sensitive to deviations from normality; therefore, data distribution characteristics were primarily determined based on Q–Q plots and skewness and kurtosis values. Independent-samples t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for comparisons among multiple groups. When homogeneity of variances was violated, Welch’s correction was used. Multiple linear regression (Enter method) was performed to examine the associations between multiple independent variables and the dependent variable. Categorical variables were included in the model as dummy variables, with each multi-category variable represented by k − 1 dummy variables, using the reference category specified in the regression table as the baseline group. Model overall significance was assessed using the F-test, and model fit was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²) and adjusted R². The effects of individual predictors were interpreted using unstandardized coefficients (B), standardized coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals, and p-values. In addition, sensitivity analyses were conducted by repeating the regression analyses on unweighted data to assess the robustness of the findings. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a significance level of α = 0.05 was adopted.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No: KY-Z-2021-092-02) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent and voluntarily took part in the study.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

After weighting to adjust for gender distribution, the sample included 3,182 residents, of whom 53.0% were male and 47.0% were female. Most participants were aged 18–40 years, with 33.1% aged 18–25 years and 28.9% aged 31–40 years. Regarding education level, 51.9% had a college degree, and 44.8% had a bachelor’s degree or above. For monthly income, 36.3% earned > 10,000, 33.8% earned > 5,000 and ≤ 10,000, and 29.9% earned ≤ 5,000. Regarding marital status, 50.7% were unmarried/divorced/widowed and 49.3% were married. Most participants were living with family (64.6%) (Table 1).

Disaster adaptation and resilience of residents

The weighted analysis showed that the total disaster adaptation and resilience score among residents was 115.61 ± 36.36, with a mean item score of 2.57 ± 0.81. Based on quartile classification results (P25 = 90, P50 = 111; P75 = 140), the score fell within the P50–P75 range, indicating a relatively moderate-to-high level within the sample. Among the five dimensions, the mean item scores ranged from 2.49 ± 0.94 to 2.62 ± 0.89. Specifically, the optimism dimension had the highest mean item score (2.62 ± 0.89), followed by social resources (2.60 ± 0.87), distress regulation (2.58 ± 0.84), problem solving (2.57 ± 0.85), while physical resources had the lowest score (2.49 ± 0.94) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study examines the levels of disaster adaptation and resilience among residents in Guangdong Province, China, as well as the factors influencing them. This study found that the total score on the Community Disaster Adaptation and Resilience Scale was 115.61 ± 36.36, with an average item score of 2.57 ± 0.81, indicating a level slightly above average. The results of the multiple linear regression analysis indicate that health status, actively following disaster-related media, direct experience of disaster events, marital status, disaster preparedness, and gender are the primary factors influencing residents’ disaster adaptation and resilience. However, the R² value of the regression model was only 5.2%, suggesting that the included variables account for only a small proportion of the total variation in disaster adaptation and resilience. This may be attributed to the complexity of individual behaviour, which is influenced by a wide range of factors. Furthermore, the model’s limited explanatory power highlights the complexity of disaster adaptation and resilience, which are likely influenced by broader psychological, social and contextual factors not covered in this study. For example, research indicates that individual personality traits and psychological characteristics (such as self-efficacy and risk perception) influence coping behaviours and emotional responses to disasters [39, 40]; social capital (such as neighbourhood trust and mutual aid networks) may facilitate access to resources and emotional support during crises [41]; and trust in government and institutions (such as public health facilities and basic healthcare provision) may influence residents’ willingness to follow official guidance and participate in disaster preparedness activities [42]. Future research should consider incorporating these variables in order to gain a better understanding of the factors influencing disaster adaptation and resilience, and to provide a basis for developing more targeted interventions. Furthermore, disaster adaptation and resilience may involve underlying processes that are not fully captured by observable variables, which could result in a more limited explanation of the observed variability.

In this study, the total score on the Disaster Adaptation and Resilience Scale for residents of Guangdong Province, China, was 115.61 ± 36.36. Compared with the total score of 145.17 ± 15.03 reported by Susan F. Rockloff et al. for adult residents in Noosa, Queensland, Australia [12], the scores for residents of Guangdong Province, China, were lower. This may be due to the fact that the DARS scale is a translated version, which could introduce cultural response biases or subtle linguistic differences; these factors may influence the results of cross-national comparisons. Overall, this suggests that there is still room for improvement in the disaster adaptation and resilience capabilities of residents in Guangdong Province, China. The mean score for the scale was 2.57 ± 0.81. The mean scores for the five dimensions, from highest to lowest, were: Optimism 2.62 ± 0.89, Social resources 2.60 ± 0.87, Distress regulation 2.58 ± 0.84, Problem solving 2.57 ± 0.85, and Physical resources 2.49 ± 0.94. A comparison of the average scores across all dimensions reveals that the physical resources dimension scores lower than psychological resilience dimensions such as problem solving. This may be attributed to the fact that Guangdong Province in China, due to its climate and topography, is frequently affected by floods, landslides and typhoons [36], These disasters have had a severe impact on infrastructure, transport, agricultural production and the ecological environment. Residents face issues such as power, water and internet outages, fallen trees and blocked roads [43], and household emergency supplies are generally insufficient, meaning that residents are unable to obtain necessary material support in a timely manner when disasters strike. The findings of this study also indicate that, in Guangdong Province, China, the social resources dimension scores higher than the material resources dimension; this may be linked to the Guangdong Provincial Government’s increased investment in disaster prevention and control systems, strengthened public health infrastructure and improved social support systems in recent years [44]. It is suggested that, in future disaster management, specific supplies should be scientifically planned and stockpiled for different types of disasters; at the same time, the disaster resilience and recovery capabilities of key infrastructure—such as transport, water resources and communications—should be further enhanced, and social support systems strengthened. These measures may help to improve the public’s ability to adapt to and recover from disasters [45].

In terms of socio-demographic characteristics, the findings of this study indicate that both gender and marital status are associated with residents’ disaster adaptation and resilience. In particular, males scored higher than females on the total score for disaster adaptation and resilience (β = 0.036, p = 0.040). This finding may be linked to differences in how men and women respond to crisis situations. Previous research has shown that there are differences between the sexes in terms of behavioural and emotional responses: men tend to adopt problem-oriented or action-oriented coping strategies, whilst exhibiting relatively weaker emotional responses; women, on the other hand, are more likely to display higher levels of negative emotions and stronger preventive behavioural responses [46]. In terms of individual psychological characteristics, women tend to score higher on average on neuroticism and are also relatively more emotionally sensitive [47]. These personality traits may make women more prone to experiencing negative emotions when faced with disaster situations, thereby limiting their ability to respond effectively to disasters and regulate their emotional state, resulting in reduced levels of disaster adaptation and resilience. Furthermore, previous research has indicated [48] that women typically shoulder a greater share of caregiving responsibilities, and that their social capital relies primarily on intra-family networks. This may make it difficult to secure adequate external support, which in turn could affect disaster adaptation and resilience. This study found that married individuals exhibited lower levels of disaster adaptation and resilience than unmarried/divorced/widowed individuals (β = −0.072, p < 0.001); this finding is inconsistent with the conclusions of previous studies on post-disaster survivors [49]. This discrepancy may stem from differences in the study populations and contexts. This study is based on the general population, whereas previous studies have largely focused on post-disaster survivors. In actual post-disaster situations, marital relationships may serve a protective function by providing emotional support and facilitating the sharing of resources [50]; whereas in non-disaster situations or contexts of potential risk, the family responsibilities associated with marriage may increase an individual’s psychological burden [51], thereby hindering their ability to adapt to and recover from disasters. Furthermore, in times of disaster or stress, married individuals often have to shoulder greater responsibilities for family care and resource coordination within the household; this additional burden may further exacerbate stress levels [52].At the same time, research suggests that married people experience higher levels of stress and anxiety during disasters due to concerns about the safety and well-being of their families [53]. This difference may reflect the fact that marital status influences individuals’ disaster adaptation and resilience through the dual effects of family responsibility structures and social support systems. In summary, both gender and marital status influence an individual’s ability to adapt to disasters and their level of resilience through factors such as emotional response patterns, social support networks and the burden of family responsibilities. It is suggested that, in disaster management and response, tailored support strategies should be developed based on different socio-demographic characteristics. Particular attention should be paid to the specific needs of women and married individuals in terms of emotional regulation, access to social support and the coordination of household resources, with a view to enhancing their overall capacity for disaster adaptation and resilience.

This study further found that health status and whether individuals were direct victims of the disaster are factors influencing disaster adaptation and resilience. Residents in good health exhibited higher levels of disaster adaptation and resilience than those in poor or average health (β = 0.140, p < 0.001). This may be due to their superior physical functioning and psychological stability, which enable them to cope more effectively with the environmental changes and life challenges brought about by disasters. Previous research has shown that higher levels of psychological resilience and positive coping strategies are closely associated with better physical and mental health [54]. Conversely, people in poorer health are more prone to negative emotions such as anxiety and depression. Previous studies have shown that following major disasters such as tornadoes and the COVID-19 pandemic, individuals experience significantly higher levels of depression and post-traumatic stress disorder, which have a negative impact on their mental health [21]. Furthermore, compared with those without direct experience of disaster events, individuals with direct experience of disaster events exhibited lower levels of disaster adaptation and resilience (β = −0.075, p < 0.001). It may be that they experienced severe physical and psychological trauma, financial loss and a breakdown in family support during the disaster, leading to reduced levels of disaster adaptation and resilience. Previous studies have indicated that the higher the level of exposure to disasters, the greater the risk of individuals developing depressive and post-traumatic stress symptoms, and the more difficult it is for them to recover [33]. Taken together, health status and exposure to disasters influence disaster adaptation and resilience through two distinct channels: an individual’s internal health status and the extent of their exposure to external trauma, respectively. In light of this, disaster management practices should focus on high-risk groups, such as those in poor health and those directly affected by disasters, and further improve the post-disaster medical and psychological support services. The relevant authorities could step up the dissemination of disaster prevention and mitigation knowledge, make full use of digital technology to disseminate disaster warnings and practical safety skills, and optimise post-disaster emergency medical management to improve the efficiency of medical resource allocation. At the same time, communities can carry out tailored awareness-raising and health education campaigns for different groups, thereby improving residents’ ability to help themselves and one another, as well as their capacity to respond to disasters, and thus enhancing community resilience [55].

The results of this study indicate that, compared with residents who do not follow disaster-related media, those who actively follow such media exhibited higher levels of disaster adaptation and resilience (β = 0.099, p < 0.001). One possible explanation is that most disasters in Guangdong Province, China, are predictable, which has helped residents develop a strong awareness of risks and knowledge of disaster prevention, enabling them to respond effectively when disasters strike and facilitating a swift recovery afterwards. Relevant research indicates that community resilience is closely linked to the proactive acquisition of disaster information and the preparation of resources [56], which is consistent with the findings of this study. Furthermore, research shows that Chinese households rely primarily on the internet, information from friends and relatives, and community outreach for disaster preparedness information, with these sources accounting for a significantly higher proportion than in the United States [57].It is worth noting that this study found that, compared with residents who were not prepared, those who had planned but not yet acted exhibited lower levels of disaster adaptation and resilience (β = −0.039, p = 0.034), whereas those who were prepared showed higher levels (β = 0.043, p = 0.022). This finding suggests that disaster preparedness may not be a simple linear cumulative process, but rather involves a critical stage in the transition from risk perception and behavioural intentions to actual action. According to behavioural science theory, behavioural intentions do not necessarily translate into actual behaviour [58]. Previous research has indicated that individuals may be influenced by factors such as resource constraints, underestimation of risk or procrastination during this process, thereby giving rise to an ‘intention–behaviour gap’ [59]. In short, individuals who are at the planning stage but have not yet taken action, whilst possessing a certain level of risk awareness, have not yet developed the practical ability to respond. The mismatch between their awareness of risk and their lack of action may lead to a greater sense of uncertainty and psychological burden, resulting in a lower level of resilience. Research based on protection motivation theory indicates that self-efficacy is significantly associated with disaster preparedness behaviour, and that behavioural intentions do not always align with actual behaviour; an individual’s actual disaster preparedness behaviour is influenced by self-efficacy and perceived barriers [60]. In contrast, individuals who have taken practical preparatory measures typically enhance their ability to cope through means such as stockpiling supplies and acquiring emergency skills [61, 62]. This process helps facilitate the transition from cognition to action and may enhance their sense of self-efficacy, thereby improving their level of disaster adaptation and resilience to a certain extent. In light of these findings, relevant authorities could, in the context of disaster management, step up the dissemination of knowledge on disaster prevention and mitigation, and make full use of digital technology to disseminate disaster warnings and practical safety skills. At the same time, communities can organise training sessions on disaster emergency response and evacuation procedures, as well as campaigns promoting the stockpiling of household emergency supplies, for different groups of people, thereby encouraging residents to translate their awareness of risks into practical disaster preparedness actions. Individual residents can proactively keep abreast of disaster information, learn disaster-related emergency knowledge and skills, and improve their ability to help themselves and others, as well as their capacity to respond to disasters. This will strengthen community resilience and help enhance disaster adaptation and recovery capabilities [55].

Limitations

This study has a number of limitations. Firstly, the use of purposive convenience sampling with geographic quotas and the fact that the sample was drawn from Guangdong Province in China may limit the representativeness of the sample and, to some extent, affect the generalisability of the findings to non-coastal regions or areas with different disaster risk characteristics. Although the gender distribution was corrected using post-stratification weighting, multivariate weighting could not be applied because the grouping of age and education variables did not correspond to that of the Seventh National Population Census. Secondly, cross-sectional study designs can only reveal correlations between variables; they cannot establish causal relationships or reflect dynamic changes. Furthermore, as this study employed non-probability sampling and an online survey, it may have resulted in a disproportionately high proportion of respondents who are young, highly educated and digitally literate. This may, to some extent, overestimate levels of disaster adaptation and resilience, thereby affecting the generalisability of the findings. Consequently, older adults and those with lower digital literacy may have been underestimated, thereby overestimating resilience levels to some extent and affecting the generalisability of the results. At the same time, individuals with higher educational attainment typically possess greater access to information and use the internet more frequently, leading to a degree of structural co-occurrence in the distribution of socio-economic and information-related variables. In the multiple regression model, this structural co-occurrence among variables may enhance the predictive power of covariates for the dummy variable representing a bachelor’s degree or above, causing its variance inflation factor (VIF) to approach the critical threshold, suggesting the presence of a certain degree of multicollinearity. Finally, although this study incorporated a range of factors (such as socio-demographic characteristics and certain behavioural factors), the explanatory power of the model remains limited, suggesting that disaster adaptation and resilience may be influenced by a number of unmeasured factors (such as psychological traits, the social environment, public health infrastructure and basic healthcare provision).

Recommendations

The findings of this study offer important insights for disaster risk management and public health practice. Firstly, it is recommended that the implementation of health promotion measures in the public health sector be strengthened and that the healthcare service delivery system be improved, with a view to enhancing the general health and health literacy of the population. Secondly, it is recommended that channels for disseminating information on disaster incidents, as well as on disaster prevention, preparedness and response, be optimised—such as digital media and community communication systems—to improve the accessibility, effectiveness and practicality of such information, thereby enhancing residents’ awareness of disaster risks and their ability to respond to them. Thirdly, it is recommended that efforts be focused on encouraging community residents to move from the ‘intentional stage’ to the ‘action stage’ in their disaster preparedness, for example by promoting the stockpiling of household emergency supplies and regular participation in disaster response training. In addition, tailored support strategies should be developed for vulnerable groups (such as those in poor health and those directly affected by disasters), the rapid response capacity of public health departments should be strengthened, and resource allocation and the development of social support networks should be optimised at the community level.

Practical implications

This study reveals the multifactorial characteristics of disaster adaptation and resilience at the individual level, thereby providing empirical evidence for public health disaster management practices. The findings suggest that, in disaster risk management, it is necessary not only to raise awareness of disaster risks but also to ensure that disaster preparedness measures are effectively implemented. Based on data from disaster-prone coastal regions in China, this study provides an empirical basis from which regions with similar economic and geographical characteristics can draw upon when conducting disaster adaptation and resilience assessments and implementing interventions.

Conclusion

This study systematically assessed the levels of disaster adaptation and resilience among residents in Guangdong Province, China, as well as the factors influencing them. The results indicate that residents’ overall levels are slightly above average and are influenced by a range of factors, including health status, access to information and disaster preparedness behaviours. This study suggests that the transition from the ‘intentional stage’ to the ‘actual behaviour stage’ in disaster preparedness plays a significant role in enhancing disaster adaptation and resilience. Overall, this study provides a scientific basis for enhancing the disaster adaptation and resilience of residents in disaster-prone coastal areas of China, as well as for formulating relevant public health intervention strategies.
