Dynamical Fluctuation-Response Relations
Timur Aslyamov, Massimiliano Esposito

Abstract
We derive exact dynamical fluctuation-response relations (FRRs) for time-integrated observables of any nonautonomous Markov jump process. The finite-time covariance splits into an initial variability and an integral of response kernels along the driven dynamics. The identity sharpens the dynamical response thermodynamic and kinetic uncertainty relations and fluctuation-response inequalities (FRIs). It also recovers steady-state FRRs, fluctuation-dissipation theorem and Onsager reciprocity, identifies known autonomous FRIs as the zero-frequency mode.

In this Letter, we derive exact dynamical FRRs for arbitrary time-integrated state and current observables of nonautonomous Markov jump processes.
The central result is a finite-time covariance identity with two contributions.
The first is an integral over products of dynamical response kernels to delta-like perturbations of local transition parameters.
The second is an initial-variability term measuring how much the conditional mean of the observable depends on the initial state.
For variances, this term is nonnegative.
It vanishes for deterministic initial preparation, decays once the dynamics loses memory of the initial ensemble, and disappears in the asymptotic state of a periodically driven system.
Thus, the new term is genuinely dynamical, while the response integral reduces to the known steady-state FRRs in the long-time limit.

Setup—
We consider a system described by a nonautonomous Markov jump process over the discrete state space labeled by
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
The probability that the system occupies state
n
at time
t
is denoted by
\bm{p}(t)=(p_{1},\dots,p_{N})^{\intercal}
and satisfies the master equation
d_{t}\bm{p}(t)=W(t)\cdot\bm{p}(t)
,
where
\sum_{n}p_{n}=1
and where
\bm{p}(0)
is assumed to be known.
The rate matrix
W(t)
explicitly depends on time via a general driving protocol. Its off-diagonal elements are
W_{nm}(t)=\sum_{e}[W_{+e}(t)\delta_{s(+e)m}\delta_{t(+e)n}+W_{-e}(t)\delta_{s(+e)n}\delta_{t(+e)m}]
,
where
s(\pm e)
is the source of edge
\pm e
and
t(\pm e)
is its target; its diagonal elements are
W_{nn}=-\sum_{m\neq n}W_{mn}
.
The rate matrix can be decomposed as
W(t)=\Delta\cdot\Gamma(t)
in terms of the incidence matrix
\Delta_{ne}=\delta_{nt(+e)}-\delta_{ns(+e)}
and the weighted incidence matrix
\Gamma_{en}(t)=W_{+e}(t)\delta_{ns(+e)}-W_{-e}(t)\delta_{nt(+e)}
. The incidence vector is
\bm{\Delta}_{e}=(\dots,\Delta_{ne},\dots)^{\intercal}
such that
\bm{\Delta}_{e}\cdot\bm{f}=f_{t(+e)}-f_{s(+e)}
.

The stochastic trajectory of the system from time
0
to
T
is denoted by
\omega_{0:T}=\{n_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
. A generic integrated observable along it reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}[\omega_{0:T}]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int_{0}^{T}d\tau o_{n_{\tau}}(\tau)}_{\mathcal{O}[\omega_{0:T}]}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{e}x_{e}(\tau)dk_{e}(\tau)}_{\mathcal{J}[\omega_{0:T}]}\,.

(1)

Here
\mathcal{O}[\omega_{0:T}]
is a state observable part, with
o_{n_{\tau}}(\tau)
a function defined on the states of the trajectory, while
\mathcal{J}[\omega_{0:T}]
is a current observable part, with edge weights
x_{e}(\tau)
(which may explicitly depend on time) and a current counter
dk_{e}=dk_{+e}-dk_{-e}
such that
dk_{\pm e}(\tau)=1
if a transition
\pm e
occurs along the trajectory in
[\tau,\tau+d\tau]
and zero otherwise. An activity counter
dk_{+e}+dk_{-e}
may also be considered.

Introducing the trajectory probability density
\mathbb{P}[\omega_{0:T}]
, we define the mean of
Eq.

1
as

\displaystyle Q(0,T)\equiv\sum_{\omega}\mathcal{Q}[\omega_{0:T}]\mathbb{P}[\omega_{0:T}]\,.

(2)

It can be computed as

\displaystyle Q(0,T)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\sum_{n}o_{n}(\tau)p_{n}(\tau)}_{O(0,T)}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\sum_{e}x_{e}(\tau)j_{e}(\tau)}_{J(0,T)}\,,

(3)

where
O(0,T)
and
J(0,T)
are the means of the state and current observables, respectively, and
j_{e}(t)=j_{+e}-j_{-e}
, with
j_{\pm e}=W_{\pm e}(t)p_{s(\pm e)}(t)
, are the average edge currents. The average activity reads
a_{e}(t)=W_{+e}(t)p_{s(+e)}(t)+W_{-e}(t)p_{t(+e)}(t)
.
The average currents define the entropy production rate (EPR) as
\sigma(t)=\sum_{e}\sigma_{e}(t)
with
\sigma_{e}(t)=j_{e}(t)\log[j_{+e}(t)/j_{-e}(t)]
, while a combination of currents and activities defines the pseudo-EPR
\sigma^{\text{ps}}(t)=\sum_{e}j_{e}(t)^{2}/a_{e}(t)
such that
\sigma(t)\geq 2\sigma^{\text{ps}}(t)
.

Using the conditional probability density
\mathbb{P}[\omega_{\tau:T}|n_{\tau}=n]
for trajectories starting at time
\tau
from state
n
, we introduce the conditional mean:

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\tau,T)\equiv\sum_{\omega}\mathcal{Q}[\omega_{\tau:T}]\mathbb{P}[\omega_{\tau:T}|n_{\tau}=n]\,,

(4)

which defines
\bm{Q}(\tau,T)\equiv(Q_{1}(\tau,T),\dots,Q_{N}(\tau,T))^{\intercal}
.
Naturally,
Q(\tau,T)=\sum_{n}Q_{n}(\tau,T)p_{n}(\tau)
.
Let us note that a useful tool for computing
\bm{Q}(\tau,T)
is solving the backward Kolmogorov equation (derived in
Appendix

B
):

\displaystyle-\partial_{\tau}\bm{Q}(\tau,T)=W^{\intercal}(\tau)\cdot\bm{Q}(\tau,T)+\bm{o}(\tau)+\Gamma(\tau)^{\intercal}\cdot\bm{x}(\tau)\,,

(5)

with terminal condition
\bm{Q}(T,T)=0
.

Eq.

5
can be seen as a dynamical extension of the Poisson equation
29
.

Dynamical FRRs—
The first central object is the scaled covariance between the integrated observables
\mathcal{Q}[\omega_{0:T}]
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}[\omega_{0:T}]
corresponding to the pairs
\{x_{e},o_{n}\}
and
\{x^{\prime}_{e},o^{\prime}_{n}\}
:

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{T}\sum_{\omega}\Delta\mathcal{Q}[\omega_{0:T}]\Delta\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}[\omega_{0:T}]\mathbb{P}[\omega_{0:T}]\,,

(6)

where
\Delta\mathcal{Q}[\omega_{0:T}]\equiv\mathcal{Q}[\omega_{0:T}]-Q(0,T)
.

The second central object is the response kernel to a perturbation of the rates
W(t;\lambda(t))
by a parameter
\lambda(t)
:

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}(\tau,T)\equiv\frac{\delta Q(0,T)}{\delta\lambda(\tau)}=\frac{\delta Q(\tau,T)}{\delta\lambda(\tau)}\;,

(7)

defined as a functional derivative.
It describes the linear response of the observable
\delta Q(0,T)\equiv Q^{\mathrm{pert}}(0,T)-Q(0,T)
to a time-dependent perturbation

\lambda(t)\mapsto\lambda(t)+\delta\lambda(t)
:

\displaystyle\delta Q(0,T)=\int_{0}^{T}d\tau R_{\lambda}(\tau,T)\,\delta\lambda(\tau)+\mathcal{O}[(\delta\lambda)^{2}]\,.

(8)

We note that for a delta perturbation
\delta\lambda(t)=h\delta(t-\tau)
,
\delta Q(0,T)=hR_{\lambda}(\tau,T)+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
,
while for step perturbation
\delta\lambda(t)=h\Theta(t)
,
\delta Q(0,T)=hT\overline{R}_{\lambda}+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
, where
\overline{f}\equiv T^{-1}\int_{0}^{T}d\tau f(\tau)
.
We emphasize that the perturbation parameter
\lambda(t)
and the driving protocol may be distinct objects.

We show in
Appendix

A
that

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}(\tau,T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e}\varphi_{e}(\tau,T)\frac{\partial j_{e}(\tau)}{\partial\lambda(\tau)}\,,

(9a)

\displaystyle\varphi_{e}(\tau,T)

\displaystyle\equiv x_{e}(\tau)+\bm{\Delta}_{e}\cdot\bm{Q}(\tau,T)\,,

(9b)

where
\partial_{\lambda}j_{e}(\tau)=\sum_{n}p_{n}(\tau)\partial_{\lambda}\Gamma_{en}(\tau)
. The usefulness of
Eq.

9a
is that only derivatives in
\lambda
of the rate matrix now appear.
For single edge perturbations
\lambda_{e}(t)
such that
W_{\pm e}\equiv W_{\pm{e}}(t;\lambda_{e}(t))
,
Eq.

9a
simplifies to:

\displaystyle R_{\lambda_{e}}(\tau,T)=\varphi_{e}(\tau,T)\frac{\partial j_{e}}{\partial\lambda_{e}}\,.

(10)

There is an interesting side result of our response theory. Using
Eq.

10
with
\lambda_{e}(t)\in\{W_{+e}(t),W_{-e}(t)\}
, we find

\displaystyle\frac{R_{W_{+e}}(\tau,T)}{R_{W_{-e}}(\tau,T)}=-\frac{p_{s(+e)}(\tau)}{p_{t(+e)}(\tau)}\,.

(11)

This result reduces in steady state to the mutual linearity corollary from
12
.
In our derivations, we assume constant observable weights
\partial_{\lambda}x_{e}(\tau)=\partial_{\lambda}o_{n}(\tau)=0
. For general weights, the same formulas hold after replacing
R_{\lambda}(\tau,T)
by
R_{\lambda}(\tau,T)-\sum_{n}p_{n}(\tau)\partial_{\lambda}o_{n}(\tau)-\sum_{e}j_{e}(\tau)\partial_{\lambda}x_{e}(\tau)
.

Our central result is the dynamical Fluctuation-Response Relation (FRR):

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{dt}{T}\sum_{e}a_{e}(t)\varphi_{e}(t,T)\varphi^{\prime}_{e}(t,T)

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt=\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{dt}{T}\sum_{e}\frac{a_{e}(t)R_{\lambda_{e}}(t,T)R^{\prime}_{\lambda_{e}}(t,T)}{[\partial_{\lambda_{e}}j_{e}(t)]^{2}}\,,

(12)

which we derive in
Appendix

B
using the finite-time cumulant-generating functions
36
;
20
.
For variance, it reduces to

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\sum_{e}\frac{a_{e}(t)}{[\partial_{\lambda_{e}}j_{e}(t)]^{2}}R^{2}_{\lambda_{e}}(t,T)\,,

(13)

where
\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle\equiv\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}\rangle\!\rangle
.
The FRR shows that covariance and response are related via a third central quantity

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{T}\sum_{n}\Delta Q_{n}(0,T)\Delta Q^{\prime}_{n}(0,T)p_{n}(0)\,,

(14)

where
\Delta Q_{n}(t,T)\equiv Q_{n}(t,T)-Q(t,T)
are the excess observables measuring the difference between a conditional mean over the interval
[t,T]
given the state at time
t
and its unconditional average (generalizing the stationary excess observables from Ref.
4
).

\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}
measures the variability resulting from the conditioning on the initial states.
It vanishes when the initial state lacks variability, i.e. when
p_{n}(0)=\delta_{nk}
and thus
\Delta Q_{k}(0,T)=0
. It also vanishes over times longer than the memory time of the initial state,
\lim_{T\to\infty}\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}=0
, while the other two terms in
Dynamical Fluctuation-Response Relations
remain finite.
In particular, when considering a periodic driving protocol of period
T_{p}
, after sufficiently long time
t
, the system settles into a periodic state
\bm{p}(t)=\bm{p}(t+T_{p})

10
, and
Eq.

13
over such a period simplifies to

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle=\frac{1}{T_{p}}\int_{t}^{t+T_{p}}d\tau\sum_{e}\frac{a_{e}(\tau)}{[\partial_{\lambda_{e}}j_{e}(\tau)]^{2}}R^{2}_{\lambda_{e}}(\tau,t+T_{p})\,.

(15)

We emphasize that the FRR is exact. Although it is written in terms of response kernels, it is not obtained by linearizing the dynamics. The response kernels enter only as exact functional derivatives evaluated along the unperturbed evolution, which may be driven by a time-dependent protocol.

Specific local perturbations—
We parameterize the transition rates as

\displaystyle W_{\pm e}(t)=\exp\big[B_{e}(t;\varepsilon)\pm S_{e}(t;\eta)/2+E_{s(\pm e)}(t;\theta)\big]\,,

(16)

where
B_{e}
and
S_{e}
are the symmetric and antisymmetric edge parameters, respectively, and
E_{n}
are the vertex parameters.
In stochastic thermodynamics,
\ln W_{+e}/W_{-e}=S_{e}-\bm{\Delta}_{e}\cdot\bm{E}
is the entropy change in the reservoir resulting from the transition

, where
S_{e}
are the nonconservative contributions
and

are suitable energy potentials

;

.
In turn,

can be seen as kinetic barriers along

(controlled by enzymes in metabolic reactions

24
).
For later purposes, we also introduce specific types of parameters,
\{\varepsilon,\eta,\theta\}
, that act on several edges and vertices.

If we consider energy perturbation, using
Eq.

9a
for
\lambda(\tau)=E_{n}(\tau)
(see

\displaystyle R_{E_{n}}(\tau,T)=p_{n}(\tau)\sum_{e}\Gamma_{en}(\tau)\varphi_{e}(\tau,T)\,,

which is a dynamical generalization of the static energy responses

;

For perturbations in

\partial_{B_{e}}j_{e}(\tau)=j_{e}(\tau)
and
\partial_{S_{e}}j_{e}(\tau)=a_{e}(\tau)/2

\displaystyle R_{B_{e}}(\tau,T)=\varphi_{e}(\tau,T)j_{e}(\tau)\,,\,R_{S_{e}}(\tau,T)=\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{e}(\tau,T)a_{e}(\tau)\,.

(18)

These response kernels satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{R_{B_{e}}(\tau,T)}{R_{S_{e}}(\tau,T)}=\frac{2j_{e}(\tau)}{a_{e}(\tau)}\,,

(19)

which extends similar relations

;

to nonautonomous dynamics.
Inserting
Eq.

into
Dynamical Fluctuation-Response Relations

:

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\frac{dt}{T}\sum_{e}\frac{4R_{S_{e}}(t,T)R^{\prime}_{S_{e}}(t,T)}{a_{e}(t)}\,,

(20)

as well as the barriers-perturbations counterpart using the substitution
R_{S_{e}}=a_{e}R_{B_{e}}/(2j_{e})
[
Eq.

].
We note that there is no direct vertex-parameter analog of
Eq.

for perturbations in

Using

\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
and applying the Sedrakyan integral inequality, we find a local version of the

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}^{\text{ps}}_{e}(T)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\overline{R}^{2}_{B_{e}}(T)}{\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle-\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}}\,,\,\overline{a}_{e}(T)\geq\frac{4\overline{R}^{2}_{S_{e}}(T)}{\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle-\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}}\,.

(21)

Kinetic perturbations—
We now perturb the kinetic parameter
\varepsilon
. The resulting response is:

\displaystyle\frac{dQ(0,T)}{d\varepsilon}=\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\sum_{e}R_{B_{e}}(\tau,T)\partial_{\varepsilon}B_{e}(\tau)\;,

(22)

where the response kernel is calculated along the unperturbed driven evolution.
Using the FRRs and algebraic inequalities (see
Appendix

C
), we find the dynamical generalization of the static R-TUR
48
;
5
:

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}(T)\geq\frac{2}{T^{2}b^{2}_{\text{max}}}\frac{\big[d_{\varepsilon}Q(0,T)\big]^{2}}{\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle-\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}}\geq\frac{2\big[d_{\varepsilon}Q(0,T)\big]^{2}}{T^{2}b^{2}_{\text{max}}\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle}\,,

(23)

where
\overline{\sigma}(T)=\sum_{e}\overline{\sigma}_{e}(T)
is the time-averaged entropy production and
b_{\text{max}}=\max_{e,t}|\partial_{\varepsilon}B_{e}(t)|
is the model dependent parameter.

As a special case, we consider a “virtual” perturbation of all barriers,
B^{\varepsilon}_{e}=B_{e}+\log(1+\varepsilon)
, implying
b_{\text{max}}=1
and a shift
W^{\varepsilon}_{\pm e}=(1+\varepsilon)W_{\pm e}
as in Refs.
18
;
30
.
Introducing the speed of driving protocol

, we notice that the virtually perturbed system is equivalent to the unperturbed dynamics observed at rescaled time
t^{\varepsilon}=(1+\varepsilon)t
under the protocol with the rescaled speed
v^{\varepsilon}=v/(1+\varepsilon)
such that
W(v^{\varepsilon}t^{\varepsilon})=W(vt)
. Then using
O^{\varepsilon}(0,T;v)=(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}O(0,T^{\varepsilon};v^{\varepsilon})
and
J^{\varepsilon}(0,T;v)=J(0,T^{\varepsilon};v^{\varepsilon})
, we find the response

\displaystyle d_{\varepsilon}Q^{\varepsilon}(0,T)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}=-O(0,T)+(T\partial_{T}-v\partial_{v})Q(0,T)\,.

(24)

Here the observable weights are assumed to be constant.

Using
Eq.

23
for current observables,
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{J}
, and kinetic response (

) with
b_{\text{max}}=1
, we find

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}(T)\geq\frac{2}{T^{2}}\frac{\big[(T\partial_{T}-v\partial_{v})J(0,T)\big]^{2}}{\langle\!\langle\mathcal{J}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle-\langle\!\langle\mathcal{J}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}}\geq\frac{2\big[(T\partial_{T}-v\partial_{v})J(0,T)\big]^{2}}{T^{2}\langle\!\langle\mathcal{J}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle}\;,

(25)

where the last looser inequality is the time-dependent driving TUR

, which, for autonomous relaxation (
\partial_{v}J(0,T)=0
), further reduces to
\overline{\sigma}(T)\geq 2[\bm{x}(T)\cdot\bm{j}(T)]^{2}/\langle\!\langle\mathcal{J}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle
known from Ref.
37
. We see that the presence of the initial variability term makes our bounds always tighter.

Thermodynamic perturbations—
We now introduce the physically motivated decomposition of the nonconservative term as
S_{e}(t;\eta)=\sum_{\gamma}X_{e\gamma}(t)F_{\gamma}(t;\eta)
, where
F_{\gamma}
denotes a set of thermodynamic forces, while
X_{e\gamma}
specifies how force
\gamma
contributes to the nonconservative term on transition
e

53
.
Two standard choices are particularly useful. In cycle/co-cycle representation
53
,
F_{\gamma}
are the affinities of a chosen set of independent cycles, and
X_{e\gamma}
is the edge–cycle incidence matrix, encoding whether edge
e
belongs to cycle
\gamma
and with which orientation. In the thermodynamic-force representation,
F_{\gamma}
are the fundamental forces imposed by the reservoirs, and
X_{e\gamma}
maps those reservoir forces onto the transitions they drive
52
.
When all forces are set to zero,
F_{\gamma}=0
for all
\gamma
, the dynamics is said to be detailed balanced.

The fundamental current corresponding to the force
F_{\gamma}
is
\mathcal{J}^{\gamma}[\omega_{0:T}]=\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\sum_{e}X_{e\gamma}(\tau)dk_{e}(\tau)
[
Eq.

1
].
We consider a perturbation of the force
F_{\gamma^{\prime}}
and its response on the mean fundamental current,
J^{\gamma}(0,T)
, which reads
L_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(T)\equiv\int_{0}^{T}d\tau T^{-1}\delta J^{\gamma}(0,T)/\delta F_{\gamma^{\prime}}
.
For an unperturbed autonomous detailed-balance dynamics with respect to a time-independent equilibrium distribution
\pi
, we recover the standard FDT
\langle\!\langle\mathcal{J}^{\gamma},\mathcal{J}^{\gamma^{\prime}}\rangle\!\rangle=2L_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(T)
, as well as Onsager’s reciprocal relation
L_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}=L_{\gamma^{\prime}\gamma}
.
For unperturbed driven detailed-balance dynamics, the same calculation gives a symmetrized finite-time identity
\langle\!\langle\mathcal{J}^{\gamma},\mathcal{J}^{\gamma^{\prime}}\rangle\!\rangle=L_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(T)+L_{\gamma^{\prime}\gamma}(T)
. For details, see
Appendix

D
.
In addition, for general dynamics, we show how our theory provides a covariance expression for responses in line with the FDTs of Refs.
1
;
54
;
62
; see
Appendix

F
.

We now derive dynamical inequalities for perturbations in
\eta
. Using
Eq.

20
and Sedrakyan’s inequality (see

), we prove the dynamical R-KUR:

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle-\langle\!\langle\mathcal{Q}^{2}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}\geq\frac{4\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{d\tau}{T}\sum_{\gamma}R_{F_{\gamma}}(\tau,T)\partial_{\eta}F_{\gamma}(\tau)\Big]^{2}}{\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{d\tau}{T}\sum_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}A_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(\tau)\partial_{\eta}F_{\gamma}(\tau)\partial_{\eta}F_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\tau)}\,,

(26)

where
R_{F_{\gamma}}(\tau,T)\equiv\delta Q(0,T)/\delta F_{\gamma}(\tau)
are the force responses and where
A_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(\tau)=\sum_{e}a_{e}(\tau)X_{e\gamma}(\tau)X_{e\gamma^{\prime}}(\tau)
is the weighted activity matrix. The alternative derivation of the dynamical R-KUR

;
38
;
63
does not include the variability term, resulting in a looser bound.

A first special case of
Eq.

is the optimal R-KUR.
The bound (
26
) holds for an arbitrary
\partial_{\eta}F_{\gamma}(t)
. We find
\partial_{\eta}\bm{F}^{*}(t)
that maximizes the right-hand side of

26
. Solving quadratic optimization

\displaystyle\equiv\bm{p}(\tau)\cdot\bm{J}(\tau,T_{p}+\tau)=[\bm{p}(\tau)-\bm{p}(\tau+T_{p})]\cdot\bm{c}.

(56)

For a cyclic protocol,
E_{n}(\tau)=E_{n}(\tau+T_{p})
, and in the associated Floquet periodic state, one has
\bm{p}(\tau+T_{p})=\bm{p}(\tau)
, yielding

J(\tau,\tau+T_{p})=0
and thus proving the no-pumping theorem.
