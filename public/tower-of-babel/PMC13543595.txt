Preoperative topical 5% benzoyl peroxide reduces intraoperative deep cutibacterium acnes contamination in shoulder arthroscopy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xianzheng Jin, Libei Fan, Haifeng Zhou, Jing Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntraoperative deep contamination with Cutibacterium acnes (C. acnes) from hair follicles and sebaceous glands remains a microbiological risk in shoulder arthroscopy, and routine chlorhexidine–alcohol skin preparation has limited efficacy against this reservoir.ObjectiveTo evaluate the association between preoperative topical 5% benzoyl peroxide use and intraoperative deep C. acnes contamination, and to explore skin colonization load.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis was performed on patients undergoing shoulder arthroscopy. Deep specimens (synovial fluid, synovium, inner portal cannula wall) were obtained for anaerobic culture. The primary outcome was the patient-level deep contamination rate. Relative risks (RR), adjusted odds ratios (aOR), and incidence rate ratios (IRR) were estimated using multivariable logistic regression, Poisson regression, and non-parametric tests. Sensitivity analyses used a strict positivity definition.ResultsThe cohort included 162 patients (81 with BPO pretreatment, 81 with standard preparation alone). The BPO group had a lower deep contamination rate (16.05% vs. 34.57%; RR = 0.46, 95%CI 0.26–0.83, p = 0.007), with an adjusted aOR = 0.38 (95%CI 0.18–0.79). The number of positive deep specimens per patient was lower (IRR = 0.44, p = 0.004). Among positive cases, the time to culture positivity was delayed in the BPO group (p = 0.031). Preoperative skin colonization load was lower in the BPO group (p = 0.001). A higher skin score was independently associated with increased risk of deep contamination (aOR = 1.57 per 1-grade increase, 95%CI 1.19–2.07, p = 0.002). Sensitivity analyses showed consistent results. No significant between-group differences were found in short-term infection-related clinical events.ConclusionIn this retrospective analysis, preoperative topical 5% benzoyl peroxide was associated with a significant reduction in intraoperative deep C. acnes contamination and bacterial burden during shoulder arthroscopy, supporting the microbiological rationale for a source decolonization strategy.Clinical Trial RegistrationNot applicable.

Introduction

Shoulder arthroscopy has become an important minimally invasive treatment for various shoulder disorders, and the incidence of postoperative infection is low, but once it occurs it often leads to persistent pain, repeat surgery, prolonged hospitalization, and limited functional recovery [1, 2]. Cutibacterium acnes (C. acnes) is one of the most representative pathogens in shoulder surgery–related infections, colonizing long term in the deep portions of hair follicles and sebaceous glands, growing slowly and capable of forming a biofilm-associated phenotype, with clinical manifestations often insidious and delayed [3, 4]. The shoulder anatomical region is rich in sebaceous glands and adjacent to the axilla, and routine disinfection has limited coverage of the deep reservoir, making bacterial introduction during portal establishment, cannula insertion, and instrument exchanges a risk step that warrants attention [5].

For prevention strategies around this pathogen, in addition to focusing on clinical infection, it is more necessary to focus on the microbiological endpoint of intraoperative deep contamination, which is closer to the transmission pathway [6]. At present, chlorhexidine bathing and chlorhexidine–alcohol skin preparation combined with standard aseptic techniques are commonly used clinically to reduce the risk of contamination, but deep specimens in shoulder surgery can still detect C. acnes, suggesting that a single preoperative skin preparation is difficult to effectively reduce the follicular–sebaceous reservoir [6]. Benzoyl peroxide has oxidative bactericidal activity and can enter follicular channels, and is used in acne treatment to inhibit C. acnes colonization, with its site of action better matching the anatomical distribution of the shoulder reservoir [7, 8]. Previous studies have focused more on the positivity rate or load changes of skin cultures, and high-quality prospective evidence for the scenario of shoulder arthroscopy is still insufficient, and it is still unclear whether skin decolonization can translate into a decrease in intraoperative deep contamination, whether the intensity of contamination decreases synchronously, and whether culture behavior changes consistently [9]. Whether adherence and skin tolerability within real-world perioperative pathways are stably acceptable also needs to be clarified. The above gaps directly limit the standardized application and evidence-based recommendations of BPO in preoperative preparation for shoulder arthroscopy.

For prevention strategies targeting Cutibacterium acnes, increasing attention has been directed toward reducing the follicular–sebaceous bacterial reservoir before shoulder surgery. Routine chlorhexidine bathing and chlorhexidine–alcohol skin preparation remain standard approaches, but their penetration into deep hair follicles and sebaceous glands is limited, and C. acnes can still frequently be recovered during shoulder procedures [10]. Previous clinical studies have demonstrated that topical BPO, owing to its oxidative bactericidal activity and follicular penetration, can significantly reduce preoperative shoulder skin colonization. Dizay HH et al. [11] showed that combined topical BPO and clindamycin preparation reduced Propionibacterium acnes colonization during shoulder arthroscopy, while Kolakowski L et al. [12] demonstrated in a prospective randomized trial that BPO significantly decreases preoperative shoulder bacterial burden. Subsequent randomized and placebo-controlled studies further confirmed reductions in skin colonization following BPO application and highlighted the dynamic nature of recolonization over time [11, 13]. More recently, systematic reviews and network meta-analyses have supported BPO-based preparation strategies as among the most effective approaches for reducing C. acnes burden in shoulder surgery. In addition, perioperative implementation studies have demonstrated generally acceptable patient compliance and tolerability with topical BPO regimens [14, 15].

However, despite these advances, several important gaps remain. Much of the existing literature has focused primarily on superficial skin culture positivity or bacterial load reduction rather than standardized intraoperative deep contamination endpoints that are more closely related to surgical inoculation pathways. Furthermore, limited data are available regarding contamination burden, culture behavior, and the quantitative relationship between preoperative skin colonization load and deep intraoperative contamination risk during shoulder arthroscopy. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate whether preoperative topical 5% BPO pretreatment, when added to standard preparation, is associated with reduced intraoperative deep C. acnes contamination and contamination burden, while also examining the relationship between skin colonization load and deep contamination in a real-world perioperative setting.

To verify the role of preoperative topical 5% benzoyl peroxide (BPO) pretreatment in shoulder arthroscopy, a single-center, retrospective was conducted to compare its effect, when combined with standard skin preparation, versus standard skin preparation alone on intraoperative deep C. acnes contamination, and to simultaneously evaluate preoperative skin colonization load, the number of positive deep specimens, the earliest time to culture positivity, safety, and short-term infection-related events. The study provides evidence directly linking the decolonization strategy with the deep contamination endpoint, and supports skin load as an operable indicator for contamination risk stratification and intervention monitoring.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed as a retrospective cohort analysis using prospectively collected data derived from a standardized institutional infection-surveillance protocol for elective primary shoulder arthroscopy. Patients were identified retrospectively from the surgical database and categorized according to the perioperative preparation protocol received during routine clinical care. No prospective randomization, allocation concealment, or investigator-directed treatment assignment was performed. During the study period, intraoperative deep microbiological sampling for C. acnes was routinely performed in consecutive cases as part of quality monitoring and infection prevention assessment. Patients were identified from a surgical database and categorized into two groups based on their preoperative preparation: those who received topical 5% BPO gel pretreatment plus standard skin preparation (BPO group), and those who received standard skin preparation alone (Control group).

Patients were categorized according to the perioperative preparation protocol received during routine clinical care. Although microbiological surveillance and specimen collection were prospectively standardized within the institutional infection-monitoring pathway, no randomization or allocation concealment was performed because the study was designed as an observational retrospective cohort analysis rather than a prospective interventional trial. The choice of preoperative preparation reflected routine clinical implementation during the study period. No prospective randomization or allocation concealment was performed. The standard skin preparation involved chlorhexidine bathing and intraoperative application of chlorhexidine-alcohol solution. The BPO regimen consisted of topical application to the shoulder area for three consecutive evenings before surgery and once on the morning of surgery.

Inclusion criteria were age 18–75 years and primary shoulder arthroscopy. Exclusion criteria were previous ipsilateral shoulder surgery, active infection, known allergy to BPO or chlorhexidine, significant skin lesions, systemic immunosuppression, or recent antibiotic use. This study was designed as a retrospective cohort analysis. Patients were categorized according to the preoperative preparation protocol they received as part of routine clinical care. No prospective randomization or allocation concealment was performed.

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Zhe Jiang Jin Hua Guang Fu Tumor Hospital), and all participants signed written informed consent before any study procedures.

Although microbiological surveillance and specimen collection were prospectively standardized as part of the institutional infection-monitoring pathway, the present analysis itself was retrospective in nature. Therefore, the study should not be interpreted as a prospective interventional trial.

Study population

The study population comprised outpatients or inpatients scheduled for elective primary shoulder arthroscopy, screened consecutively. All candidates received study information at the preoperative clinic and signed written informed consent. Baseline information was collected by the research coordinator at admission via an electronic case report form, including age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, diabetes, ASA classification, preoperative skin condition, history of previous ipsilateral shoulder injection, planned surgical type, and estimated operative time.

Inclusion criteria were age 18–75 years; planned first ipsilateral shoulder arthroscopy (pure arthroscopic procedures such as rotator cuff repair, subacromial decompression, labral repair, or adhesiolysis); ASA classification I–III [16]; no systemic antibiotic therapy within two weeks preoperatively; ability to understand and comply with the BPO topical regimen and study follow-up; and signed written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were previous ipsilateral shoulder surgery; suspected or confirmed active infection of the ipsilateral shoulder, determined through preoperative clinical evaluation including local signs or symptoms suggestive of infection (persistent pain disproportionate to the underlying condition, erythema, warmth, swelling, drainage, or fever), laboratory inflammatory markers when clinically indicated, imaging findings suggestive of infection, and/or joint aspiration with microbiological analysis in cases where septic arthritis or deep infection was suspected; allergy to BPO, chlorhexidine, or alcohol; significant skin lesions in the operative field (active eczema, psoriasis, severe acne nodulocystica); systemic immunosuppression (oral prednisone ≥ 10 mg/d for ≥ 14 days, chemotherapy or biologic therapy within the past three months, immunosuppression after solid organ transplantation); glycated hemoglobin > 8.5%; pregnancy or lactation; planned arthroplasty, bone grafting, or procedures requiring conversion to open surgery; use of retinoids or other prescription topical anti-acne agents on the operative field within one month preoperatively; and those judged by the study team to have insufficient adherence or to be unlikely to complete culture sampling.

The primary outcome was the patient-level intraoperative deep C. acnes contamination rate. Based on previous literature in similar populations and a pilot survey at our center, the expected deep contamination rate was 35% in the control group and 15% in the BPO group [17]. A post hoc power estimation based on expected contamination rates reported in previous literature and institutional pilot observations indicated that a sample size of approximately 73 patients per group would provide 80% statistical power to detect the observed between-group difference in deep contamination rates. The final cohort size exceeded this threshold.

Data collection and variables

Data were extracted from electronic medical records, including demographics (age, sex, BMI), comorbidities (diabetes, smoking status), surgical details (procedure type, operative time, irrigation volume), and microbiological results.

The primary exposure was the use of preoperative topical 5% BPO. The primary outcome was patient-level intraoperative deep C. acnes contamination, defined as a positive anaerobic culture for C. acnes from at least one of three deep specimens: synovial fluid, synovial tissue, or the inner wall of the portal cannula.

Surgical procedures and operative standards

All surgeries were performed by two senior surgeons with annual surgical volumes ≥ 150 cases. Patients were placed in the beach-chair position; the initial portal was posterior, followed by establishment of anterosuperior and lateral portals. Arthroscopic pump pressure was maintained at 45–55 mmHg, and the total intraoperative irrigation volume was recorded. The intraoperative addition of other skin disinfectants was prohibited. The surgical procedure (rotator cuff repair, labral repair, subacromial decompression, or adhesiolysis) was performed according to the preoperative plan, and operative time was recorded. Both groups used a unified skin disinfection process and sterile draping, and iodine-impregnated adhesive drapes were not used, to avoid the impact of additional barrier materials on the skin microbiota and sampling paths. Portals were established only after draping was completed, and non-disinfected areas were not contacted before portal establishment.

Microbiological sampling and culture methods

Skin samples were collected before skin preparation of the operative field. A 4 cm² area centered on the planned anterosuperior portal was measured with a sterile ruler; a pre-moistened sterile flocked swab was used to wipe evenly 10 times horizontally and 10 times vertically, rotating the swab to ensure full surface contact; the swab was immediately placed into pre-reduced anaerobic transport medium and delivered to the laboratory within 60 min.

Three deep specimens were collected per case, with standardized sequence and methods. The first was the initial joint cavity fluid (10 mL), aspirated with a syringe immediately after insertion of a cannula through the posterior portal; 5 mL was inoculated into an anaerobic blood culture bottle, and 5 mL into thioglycollate enrichment broth. The second was approximately 5 mm synovial tissue from the rotator interval, obtained with an arthroscopic punch and then placed into an anaerobic transport tube. The third was a swab of the inner wall of the anterosuperior portal cannula obtained before the end of surgery, using a sterile flocked swab inserted 5 cm and rotated 10 times, then placed into transport medium. All sampling avoided contact with the skin edge; containers were affixed with uniform barcodes and the time of sampling was recorded.

Skin and deep swabs were inoculated under anaerobic conditions onto Brucella agar supplemented with blood, vitamin K, and hemin, and simultaneously into thioglycollate broth for enrichment; tissue samples were ground under sterile conditions and inoculated in the same manner; joint fluid was inoculated into anaerobic blood culture bottles and onto solid media simultaneously. All anaerobic cultures were incubated at 35–37℃ for 14 days in an incubator; morphology and colony counts were read on days 3, 7, 10, and 14. Suspicious colonies were identified by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry, and 16 S rRNA gene sequencing was performed when necessary for confirmation. Semi-quantitative assessment of swab and tissue plates used the four-quadrant streaking method: 0 for no growth, 1 + for sparse growth limited to the first quadrant, 2 + for growth in the first two quadrants, 3 + for growth in the first three quadrants, and 4 + for growth in all four quadrants [18]; for blood culture bottles, the number of days to first positive alarm was recorded as the ‘time to culture positivity’.

C. acnes culture positivity was defined as growth of C. acnes confirmed by identification in any of the above anaerobic culture systems. Patient-level ‘intraoperative deep contamination’ was defined as at least one of the three deep specimens positive for C. acnes. Sensitivity analysis used a stricter standard, defined as at least two of the three deep specimens positive for C. acnes. Other concomitantly growing aerobic or anaerobic skin commensals were recorded separately and were not included in the primary outcome. The study did not use clinical infection diagnosis as the primary endpoint; postoperative infection events were recorded as independent safety and exploratory outcomes.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was intraoperative deep C. acnes contamination, namely the patient-level deep contamination rate, with the denominator calculated as subjects who completed culture of at least two deep specimens; the number of C. acnes–positive specimens at the specimen level and the time to culture positivity were also recorded. Secondary outcome measures were skin C. acnes colonization load, assessed by semi-quantitative scoring (0–4+) using a skin swab taken before skin preparation of the operative field, and the number of days from inoculation to first visible colonies; the distribution and median differences of skin load between the two groups were compared in the analysis. Safety indicators included the incidence and severity of BPO-related skin reactions (grade 1 for mild erythema or dryness not affecting daily activities; grade 2 for moderate erythema or burning pain requiring shorter contact time or increased moisturizing; grade 3 for marked erythema with edema or pain requiring discontinuation and local management); adherence was calculated by reconciling medication logs and remaining drug amounts, with completion proportion ≥ 80% defined as adherent. The exploratory outcome was the correlation between skin bacterial burden and deep contamination, namely evaluating, at the individual level, the relationship between the semi-quantitative skin score and deep contamination, and using a multivariable model to estimate the independent association strength of skin load with deep contamination.

Adherence to chlorhexidine bathing was defined as completion of two documented preoperative baths. Intraoperative skin preparation adherence was defined as completion of the standardized chlorhexidine–alcohol disinfection protocol as recorded in the operative checklist.

Adverse event monitoring and safety assessment methods

After each of the three home topical applications, research nurses reviewed skin reactions by telephone or outpatient visit, and rechecked and recorded them before entering the operating room. At 24 h postoperatively, before discharge, and at 30-day and 90-day follow-up, patients were asked about wound healing, local redness, exudation, and fever. If a grade 3 skin adverse reaction or suspected postoperative infection occurred, the principal investigator was notified immediately for causality assessment and medical management, and ethics filing was completed within 24 h.

Statistical analysis methods

All eligible patients entering the operating room were included in the primary analysis. For the primary outcome, the absolute risk difference and relative risk of deep contamination rates between the two groups were calculated, with 95% confidence intervals; significance testing used a two-sided chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected frequencies were insufficient. Prespecified covariates (age, sex, body mass index, diabetes, smoking, operative time, irrigation volume) entered a multivariable Logistic regression model to estimate the adjusted odds ratio. The semi-quantitative skin score was compared using the Wilcoxon test; the number of positive specimens at the specimen level was analyzed using Poisson regression with testing for overdispersion, and negative binomial regression was used if overdispersion was present; among positive cases, time to culture positivity was compared using the Wilcoxon test. No multiplicity adjustment was performed for secondary and exploratory analyses, and their p-values were interpreted as descriptive. Two sensitivity analyses were performed for the primary outcome: first, a strict positivity definition (≥ 2 deep specimens positive); second, extreme-case handling for subjects with missing deep specimens (treating missing as positive and treating missing as negative) to verify robustness. Statistical software was R 4.3.2, with the significance level set at 0.05 (two-sided). Baseline and intraoperative characteristics were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. These comparisons were performed for descriptive purposes only and were not used to guide variable selection in the multivariable models. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed.

Results

Participant flow and baseline comparability

A total of 243 patients were assessed, of whom 69 were not included due to not meeting inclusion criteria or declining participation, and 174 patients met eligibility criteria and were allocated according to the preoperative preparation protocol implemented during the study period. Due to preoperative cancellation and specimen loss, 162 were included in the ITT analysis (81 per group). The two groups were well balanced with respect to baseline demographic and clinical characteristics (Table 1). There were no statistically significant differences between groups in age, sex, BMI, smoking status, diabetes, ASA classification, or history of previous ipsilateral shoulder injection (all p > 0.05). Similarly, intraoperative procedural characteristics, including operative time, irrigation volume, and distribution of surgical procedures, were comparable between the two groups (Table 2, all p > 0.05). Intraoperative process and intervention implementation

The distributions of operative time and irrigation volume were similar between the two groups. The composition proportions of rotator cuff repair, labral repair, subacromial decompression, and adhesiolysis were similar, and the incidence of conversion to open surgery was comparable. Overall intraoperative process characteristics and procedural consistency were good (Table 2). The overall execution rates of the planned skin preparation were high in both groups. In the BPO group, 72/81 (88.89%) patients completed ≥ 80% of the planned topical regimen. Adherence to chlorhexidine bathing, defined as completion of two preoperative chlorhexidine baths according to protocol, was high in both groups (95.06% in the BPO group and 97.53% in the control group). Adherence to chlorhexidine bathing, defined as completion of two documented preoperative chlorhexidine baths according to protocol, was high in both groups (77/81 [95.06%] in the BPO group vs. 79/81 [97.53%] in the control group).

Intraoperative deep Cutibacterium acnes contamination

Using the chi-square test and multivariable Logistic regression analysis, the intraoperative deep C. acnes contamination rate in the BPO group (16.05%) was significantly lower than that in the control group (34.57%), with RR = 0.46 (95% CI 0.26–0.83, p = 0.007). Relative risk reduction was 54%, corresponding to an absolute risk reduction of 18.5% (number needed to treat ≈ 5.4). After adjustment for age, sex, BMI, diabetes, smoking, operative time, and irrigation volume, the risk of deep contamination in the BPO group remained significantly reduced, with aOR = 0.38 (95% CI 0.18–0.79) (Table 4). Using Poisson regression analysis, compared with the control group, the number of positive deep specimens per patient was significantly reduced in the BPO group (IRR = 0.44, p = 0.004) (Fig. 2A). Using the Wilcoxon test, among patients with at least 1 positive deep specimen, the earliest time to culture positivity in the BPO group (7 days) was slightly later than in the control group (6 days), and the difference in distributions between the two groups was statistically significant (p = 0.031) (Fig. 2B).

Relationship between preoperative skin colonization and deep contamination

Using the Wilcoxon test, the overall preoperative skin C. acnes colonization load score in the BPO group was significantly lower than that in the control group (p = 0.001) (Fig. 3). Among subjects who completed both skin and deep sampling, multivariable Logistic regression was used to explore the relationship between preoperative skin C. acnes colonization load and intraoperative deep contamination. Treating the semi-quantitative skin score graded 0–4 as a continuous ordered variable, for each 1-grade increase in score, the risk of deep contamination increased (aOR = 1.57, 95% CI 1.19–2.07, p = 0.002), and this association remained after adjustment for group, age, sex, BMI, diabetes, smoking, operative time, and irrigation volume.

Sensitivity analyses

Using the chi-square test and multivariable Logistic regression to repeat the primary analysis under different sensitivity scenarios, the direction and magnitude of the reduced risk of deep contamination in the BPO group remained consistent whether a strict positivity definition was used or missing deep specimens were treated as positive or as negative (aOR approximately 0.38–0.41, all < 1). The differences remained statistically significant when missing was treated as positive or as negative (both p < 0.05), while a near-significant trend was observed under the strict positivity definition (p = 0.058), overall indicating good robustness of the primary outcome (Table 5).

Discussion

After preoperative topical 5% benzoyl peroxide, both the incidence of intraoperative deep C. acnes contamination and the contamination burden decreased, and the results remained consistent after adjusting for patient characteristics and perioperative factors, suggesting that the intervention effect does not depend on differences in procedure composition, operative time, or irrigation volume. A high proportion of patients completed the scheduled topical regimen and routine skin preparation; skin adverse reactions were mainly mild irritation and controllable, and no severe events requiring special management were observed, indicating that this regimen can be embedded in routine perioperative pathways without significantly increasing management burden. The microbiological explanation is more consistent with a reduction in the bacterial load of the hair follicle and sebaceous gland reservoir after repeated topical application, reducing the effective inoculum introduced into the joint cavity during portal establishment and repeated instrument exchanges, decreasing the probability of detection in joint fluid, synovium, and the inner wall of the cannula, and relatively delaying the time to culture positivity, thereby forming an evidence chain consistent with the primary outcome [12, 19]. Previous studies suggest that a single chlorhexidine or iodine preparation has limited penetration into the deep portions of hair follicles, and C. acnes culture positivity remains relatively common in shoulder surgery [20]; although BPO has been shown to reduce the burden on shoulder skin and specimens associated with sebaceous glands, evidence is insufficient for the endpoint of deep contamination during arthroscopy, which is closer to intraoperative risk [21]. The current results link skin-level decolonization with improvements in deep contamination, providing more direct microbiological support for using BPO as a preoperative pretreatment strategy in shoulder arthroscopy.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that preoperative benzoyl peroxide reduces Cutibacterium acnes colonization on the shoulder skin surface; however, most investigations focused primarily on superficial culture positivity rather than intraoperative deep contamination. Kolakowski L et al. [12] reported in a prospective randomized trial that BPO significantly decreased preoperative shoulder bacterial burden, while van Diek FM et al. [22] demonstrated reduced skin colonization after topical BPO use in a placebo-controlled randomized study. Similarly, Duvall G et al. [23] observed that the reduction in C. acnes burden after BPO treatment may be transient, emphasizing the dynamic nature of follicular recolonization. More recently, the systematic review by Green N et al. [17] concluded that preoperative topical BPO is effective in reducing C. acnes burden in shoulder surgery overall. However, evidence directly connecting skin decolonization with reduced intraoperative deep contamination during arthroscopy has remained limited. Compared with prior literature, the present study extends existing evidence by evaluating standardized deep intraoperative specimens, including synovial fluid, synovium, and portal cannula cultures, which are microbiologically closer to the pathway of surgical inoculation. In addition, this study evaluated contamination burden, time to culture positivity, and the independent relationship between preoperative skin colonization load and deep contamination risk. The observed association between reduced skin bacterial burden and lower deep contamination rates supports the concept that preoperative BPO functions not merely as a surface antiseptic adjunct but as a source-targeted decolonization strategy aimed at the follicular–sebaceous reservoir of C. acnes [14, 22].

Preoperative skin colonization load decreased overall after BPO pretreatment, and the higher the skin score, the greater the probability of intraoperative deep contamination; this association persisted even after controlling for group and key perioperative factors. This phenomenon suggests that the bacterial burden in the skin hair follicle reservoir is not merely an accompanying indicator but an intervenable factor closely linked to the risk of deep contamination. C. acnes reside deep in sebaceous glands and hair follicles; the shoulder has a high density of sebaceous glands and is adjacent to the axilla, making this bacterium more difficult to clear with a single skin preparation [24]. Routine preoperative scrubbing and chlorhexidine–alcohol skin preparation mainly act on the surface layer and are difficult to eliminate deep colonization in a short time; mechanical disturbance of hair follicles during incision and portal establishment can carry residual bacteria into the cannula and joint cavity [25]. Repeated topical BPO can, through sustained oxidative bactericidal activity, reduce the cultivable bacterial burden within hair follicles, thereby decreasing the introduced inoculum during portal establishment and instrument exchanges and weakening the chain of contamination from skin to deep tissues [26]. The semi-quantitative skin score shows a graded increase and is independently associated with deep contamination, and the model has included baseline patient status and indicators of intraoperative exposure intensity, supporting that the risk of contamination is not driven solely by differences in operative time or irrigation volume [27]. This chain of evidence also gives preoperative skin scoring value for risk stratification, which can be used to identify individuals with higher bacterial burden and to strengthen pretreatment adherence. Previous studies have mostly focused on endpoints such as the positivity rate of skin cultures or changes in bacterial load and have lacked analyses directly linking skin load to deep contamination [28]; this cohort study provides evidence of an independent association through a multivariable model, elevating the role of BPO from empirically intensifying disinfection to a source decolonization strategy.

The semi-quantitative score of preoperative skin swabs shifted downward overall in the BPO group, and skin colonization load and intraoperative deep contamination remained independently associated within a multivariable framework, suggesting that deep contamination is not a random event but is closely linked to the size of the skin bacterial reservoir at the operative site. This association also explains the marked individual differences that persist despite a highly standardized surgical process. C. acnes have hair follicles and sebaceous glands as its main niches, with a locally anaerobic environment and a propensity to form a biofilm-associated phenotype [29]. Short-duration chlorhexidine–alcohol skin preparation tends to clear surface flora and is difficult to adequately cover the deep portions of glands [30]. BPO is lipophilic and keratolytic, can enter follicular channels and release oxidative active products, alter the local anaerobic conditions, reduce the cultivable bacterial burden, and weaken at the source the risk of carry-in during portal establishment and instrument passage [31]. The graded association of the skin score with deep contamination provides quantitative support for this biological pathway and also provides clinical cues for risk stratification: patients with more active sebaceous glands, more hair, or higher skin burden may benefit more from targeted pretreatment. Previous literature comparing multiple preoperative preparation strategies suggests that single cleansing or intraoperative skin preparation has limited impact on the deep follicular bacterial reservoir, whereas repeated BPO pretreatment better matches its slow growth and deep colonization characteristics [32]. This finding provides a basis for evaluating preventive measures using microbiological surrogate endpoints and lays the foundation for subsequent multicenter validation and strategy optimization. The association remained after including skin load in the adjustment model, making the effect of BPO more consistent with the microbiological causal chain. Skin load can also serve as an operable indicator of the preoperative decolonization effect.

Deep microbiological sampling was conducted under a standardized institutional protocol rather than selectively for research purposes, reducing the likelihood of sampling bias. However, as a non-randomized cohort analysis, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded.

This study was a single-center design; although standardized processes help reduce operational variability, differences among institutions in skin preparation methods, choice of disinfectants, and details of instrument management may affect the generalizability of the effect. The primary outcome selected was intraoperative deep culture positivity, which is a surrogate indicator and did not directly assess clinical infection risk, and the 90-day follow-up period may be insufficient to capture delayed infection events. Culture results are affected by sampling, transport, and anaerobic culture conditions; molecular methods were not applied to all samples, which may have missed low-burden or non-cultivable strains; the deep sampling region was fixed, which may underestimate spatial heterogeneity. Although a small number of missing samples were addressed in sensitivity analyses, uncertainty may remain. The skin sampling area was fixed and a semi-quantitative scoring system was used, without involving other areas or absolute bacterial load measurements.

Although intraoperative deep contamination is a surrogate microbiological endpoint rather than a clinical infection outcome, it represents a proximal event in the pathogenic cascade leading to postoperative infection. Given the low incidence and delayed presentation of clinical C. acnes infection, large-scale trials with extended follow-up would be required to detect differences in clinical endpoints. Therefore, deep contamination serves as a mechanistically relevant and statistically feasible surrogate outcome in preventive strategy research. Future multicenter prospective trials with longer follow-up are warranted to determine whether the observed microbiological benefit translates into a reduction in clinically significant infections.

Patient adherence was estimated based on medication logs and remaining drug amount, which may be overestimated, and deviations in contact area and duration were not fully recorded. The study did not include patients who had recently received antibiotics or immunosuppressive therapy or those with marked skin lesions, limiting generalization to high-risk populations and to open or implant surgeries. The intervention was limited to the established BPO regimen, and different regimen lengths, combined pretreatment approaches, or cost-effectiveness were not further evaluated.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the retrospective cohort design limits the ability to establish causality. Although multivariable adjustment was performed to control for important demographic and intraoperative variables, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be fully excluded. Variables such as subtle differences in surgical technique, operating room traffic, individual skin microbiome variability, or environmental factors may have influenced contamination risk. Second, the primary outcome was intraoperative deep C. acnes culture positivity, which represents a microbiological surrogate endpoint rather than a clinical infection outcome. While deep contamination is mechanistically proximal to postoperative infection, it does not necessarily translate into clinically significant infection. The relatively short 90-day follow-up may not fully capture delayed C. acnes infections, which are known to present insidiously months after surgery. Third, although deep microbiological sampling was standardized and routinely performed under an institutional protocol, culture-based detection may underestimate bacterial burden due to the slow-growing and biofilm-associated nature of C. acnes. Molecular diagnostic techniques such as quantitative PCR were not systematically applied and may have identified additional low-burden or non-cultivable organisms. Fourth, the study was conducted at a single center with standardized perioperative protocols. While this reduces procedural variability and strengthens internal consistency, it may limit external generalizability to institutions using different antiseptic regimens, surgical workflows, or patient populations. Additionally, patients with recent antibiotic exposure, significant immunosuppression, or open/implant procedures were excluded, which may restrict applicability to higher-risk surgical settings. Finally, adherence to preoperative topical BPO was assessed through documentation and medication reconciliation, which may overestimate true compliance. Variability in application technique, contact duration, and skin coverage was not objectively measured. Because treatment allocation was not randomized, the possibility of selection bias and residual confounding cannot be completely excluded despite multivariable adjustment and standardized perioperative protocols.

As a non-randomized retrospective cohort study, this analysis is inherently subject to potential selection bias and residual confounding. Although multivariable adjustment was performed for major demographic and intraoperative variables and perioperative protocols were standardized, unmeasured confounders such as surgeon-specific technical variation, operating room environmental factors, or individual microbiome variability may still have influenced the observed associations. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as demonstrating an association rather than a causal effect of benzoyl peroxide pretreatment. These limitations further emphasize the need for adequately powered prospective randomized controlled trials with standardized microbiological sampling protocols to more definitively determine the causal impact of preoperative benzoyl peroxide decolonization on intraoperative contamination and postoperative infection risk. Although multivariable adjustment was performed for major demographic and intraoperative variables, unmeasured confounders such as surgeon-specific technical nuances, subtle skin microbiome variations, or environmental factors may persist. Therefore, causality should be interpreted cautiously. As a retrospective observational cohort study without randomization or blinding, the possibility of selection bias and residual confounding cannot be completely excluded, despite the use of standardized perioperative protocols and multivariable adjustment.

Future research should aim to validate these findings in multicenter prospective studies with longer-term follow-up to determine whether reductions in intraoperative deep contamination translate into clinically meaningful reductions in postoperative shoulder infections. Randomized controlled trials with standardized sampling protocols would provide higher-level evidence and clarify causal relationships. Incorporating molecular microbiological techniques may allow more precise quantification of bacterial burden and better characterization of the relationship between skin reservoir dynamics and intraoperative contamination. Furthermore, exploring optimized BPO regimens including duration, concentration, or combination with other decolonization strategies may help refine perioperative protocols. Stratified analyses in higher-risk populations, such as patients undergoing shoulder arthroplasty or implant-based procedures, could determine whether source-targeted decolonization strategies provide greater benefit in settings with increased susceptibility to biofilm-associated infections. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses and patient-centered tolerability assessments would help determine the practicality of routine BPO pretreatment in broader clinical practice.

Conclusion

Preoperative topical 5% benzoyl peroxide as a pretreatment before shoulder arthroscopy can reduce intraoperative deep C. acnes contamination and the contamination burden, and this effect remained stable after controlling for patient baseline characteristics and intraoperative exposure intensity. Skin colonization load was independently and positively associated with deep contamination, suggesting that the key target of perioperative decolonization is to reduce the hair follicle and sebaceous gland reservoir. This regimen is implementable within routine perioperative workflow, with acceptable adherence, and skin adverse reactions were mostly mild and controllable. Within the short follow-up period, no clear differences in infection-related clinical events were observed, and deep contamination can serve as a microbiological endpoint for evaluating preventive strategies.
