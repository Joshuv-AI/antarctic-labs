Overcoming methicillin resistance in Staphylococcus aureus via metal ion disruption of bacterial metabolism
Authors: Ella K Macbeth, Francesca Todd Rose, Chen-Yi Cheung, Nichaela Harbison-Price, David M P De Oliveira, Hayden G Whyte, Mark J Walker, Maytham Hussein, Tony Velkov, Stephanie L Neville, Christopher A McDevitt, Ian R Monk, Scott A Ferguson, Gregory M Cook
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMethicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a significant public health threat due to both extensive antimicrobial resistance and immune evasion capabilities, necessitating alternative therapeutic strategies. Disruption of bacterial metal ion homeostasis, a process already leveraged by host nutritional immunity, represents a promising therapeutic approach. The synthetic ionophore PBT2 delivers zinc (Zn) directly into the bacterial cytosol, where it can dysregulate cellular processes and restore the efficacy of conventional antibiotics. Here, we use PBT2 and Zn (PZ) to study the cellular response to metal dysbiosis in MRSA, identifying new metal-dependent molecular vulnerabilities. Integrated transcriptomics, metabolomics and molecular analyses revealed that the antibacterial and oxacillin-resensitisation action of PZ is driven by dual metal stress: intracellular Zn accumulation and manganese (Mn) depletion. PZ disrupted central carbon metabolism at multiple key nodes, impairing glycolysis, the TCA cycle and respiration, leading to NADH and ATP depletion and compromised peptidoglycan biosynthesis. PZ also altered the metal-dependent oxidative stress response, resulting in superoxide accumulation. Collectively, this work presents the dynamic interplay between bacterial metal ion homeostasis, central metabolism, and β-lactam resistance. Uncovering how PBT2 subverts the adaptive responses of MRSA to host-imposed stresses contributes to our understanding of host-pathogen interactions and offers a foundation for developing novel antimicrobials based on metal homeostasis disruption.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a critical global health crisis, causing an estimated 4.95 million associated deaths in 20191. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) infections are particularly alarming, with over 130,000 deaths directly attributed to this pathogen globally in 20212. The pathogenicity of S. aureus is underpinned by its ability to adapt and thrive in diverse host environments, evade the immune system, and acquire β-lactam resistance3.

Metal ions have been used for centuries to treat bacterial infections4, but recent investigations highlight their potential to combat AMR5. First-row transition metals, particularly zinc (Zn), manganese (Mn) and iron (Fe), are crucial for bacterial survival, growth and pathogenesis6,7. These elements serve as cofactors for proteins that contribute to various cellular processes, including oxidative stress resistance and central carbon metabolism8,9. Accordingly, bacteria such as S. aureus must tightly regulate their intracellular metal concentrations to avoid starvation and concurrently prevent excessive and potentially toxic accumulation10,11. The host exploits this reliance on metal ion homeostasis as a defence strategy against bacteria, employing nutritional immunity mechanisms to restrict essential metals and carbon sources, whilst also inducing intracellular metal toxicity12. For example, calprotectin, a host-derived protein, can sequester Mn ions away from S. aureus13, and phagocytic immune cells can release Zn stores to kill internalised bacteria14–16. However, MRSA and other successful pathogens have evolved sophisticated strategies to counteract these innate immune pressures, including high-affinity metal transporters11,17–19, metal-dependent antioxidants to combat oxidative stress20,21, and metabolic flexibility with alternative routes for energy generation22–26. Understanding how MRSA adapts to host-imposed stresses is crucial for developing innovative therapeutics that can counteract these adaptive mechanisms.

Numerous studies have explored the potential of metal-binding compounds as antibacterial agents and adjuvants alongside antibiotics to overcome AMR5. The Zn ionophore PBT2 is a promising candidate, which binds and translocates Zn ions across biological membranes27,28. Originally developed as an Alzheimer’s and Huntington’s Disease treatment candidate, PBT2 displayed favourable safety profiles in Phase I/II clinical trials, but it lacked efficacy and is no longer in active development for neurological indications29,30. PBT2, in combination with Zn (PZ), induces intracellular Zn accumulation in numerous bacterial pathogens31–36, often accompanied by Mn and/or Fe depletion31–33. While the antibiotic-potentiating effects of PZ are well documented across drug-resistant pathogens31,33,35,37 and characterised in detail for Streptococcus pneumoniae33, the molecular mechanisms in MRSA remain unclear.

Here, we demonstrate that dual metal stress—comprising intracellular Zn toxicity and Mn depletion—contributes to the PZ mechanism of action against MRSA and resensitisation to the β-lactam antibiotic oxacillin. PZ triggered widespread metabolic perturbations within central carbon metabolism and associated biosynthetic pathways, including cell wall, amino acid, lipid and nucleotide synthesis. These disruptions destabilised cellular redox balance and led to severe ATP depletion, exposing critical metabolic vulnerabilities in the response of MRSA to metal stress. We propose that PZ mimics aspects of nutritional immunity, such as phagocyte-driven Zn toxicity and calprotectin-mediated Mn sequestration, but it directly targets intracellular metal pools. Importantly, in S. aureus PBT2 circumvents the high-affinity metal transport systems, limits ROS detoxification via Mn-dependent antioxidants, and restricts metabolic flexibility, which can result in cell death. Our findings reveal the clinical potential of PZ as an adjuvant that boosts the efficacy of conventional antibiotics and as a standalone agent, highlighting the broader applicability of targeting bacterial metal ion homeostasis as a novel strategy to combat AMR.

Results

PBT2 + Zn (PZ) is bactericidal against MRSA, and supplementation with Mn rescues this effect

PBT2 is a well-characterised Zn ionophore that exhibits antimicrobial activity by disrupting intracellular metal ion homeostasis in several pathogenic bacteria. We focused on PBT2 and Zn (PZ) against the MRSA strain USA300-HOU-MR to determine the molecular determinants of antimicrobial action. To investigate the impact of PBT2 on metal ion homeostasis, we utilised ICP-MS to determine the total cellular content of specific metals. Hereafter, this is termed the ‘intracellular concentration’, but we acknowledge that it reflects the overall combination of the free, protein-associated and cell envelope-associated metals38. Subinhibitory PBT2 treatment (9 µM, MIC = 16 µM) alone was not sufficient to significantly impact intracellular levels of Zn or Mn, but it did significantly deplete intracellular Fe (5.8-fold) (Fig. 1A-C). Therefore, the labile fraction of the Zn content present in TSB medium (total 11 µM) was not adequate to facilitate substantial Zn ionophoric activity. Consistent with this observation, subinhibitory PBT2 increased intracellular Zn accumulation in a dose-dependent manner upon ZnSO4 (Zn) supplementation (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Treatment with Zn alone (50–400 µM) did not significantly alter intracellular Zn content (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 1A). In contrast, treatment with subinhibitory PZ (9 µM PBT2 + 50 µM ZnSO4) led to a significant increase in intracellular Zn (2.5-fold) and a corresponding significant decrease in intracellular Mn (5.1-fold) and Fe (12.9-fold), compared to untreated controls (Fig. 1A–C). These findings align with those of Bohlmann et al., in MRSA USA300 FPR3757, although their different experimental conditions (1 µM PBT2 + 100 µM Zn in THY) did not elicit Fe depletion31. Notably, intracellular potassium, copper, magnesium, and nickel were also depleted by PZ, albeit to differing extents (Supplementary Fig. 2).

To investigate the impact of metal depletion by PZ, we focused on Mn, as the intracellular concentration was specifically reduced by the PZ combination (Supplementary Fig. 1B), in contrast to intracellular Fe, which showed distinct responses to treatment by PBT2 and Zn (Fig. 1A–C). MRSA USA300-HOU-MR growth in TSB or supplemented with a supraphysiological concentration of MnSO4 (Mn, 5 mM), demonstrated synergy between PBT2 and Zn (FICIs <0.5) (Fig. 1D, E). However, the PBT2 MIC was increased fourfold in TSB + Mn (Fig. 1D, E). Checkerboard analysis also revealed 0.039–2.5 mM Mn increased the PBT2 MIC to 32 µM (twofold), 5 mM Mn increased the PBT2 MIC to 64 µM (fourfold), and 10 mM Mn increased the PBT2 MIC to >64 µM, suggesting a dose-dependent rescue (Fig. 1F). Treatment with PBT2 at 1×MIC (16 µM), alongside Zn (PZ) in TSB was bactericidal (≥3-log10 reduction of initial inoculum) against USA300-HOU-MR after 24 h (Fig. 1G). In contrast, PBT2 (16 µM) or Zn (50 µM) alone did not impact cell viability (Fig. 1G). The addition of Mn (5 mM) completely restored the viability of PZ-treated cells after 24 h (Fig. 1H). Taken together, these data suggest that the depletion of Mn contributes to PZ-induced metal dyshomeostasis in S. aureus. We assessed the impact of PZ + Mn (5 mM, PZM) on USA300 intracellular metal accumulation and observed a significant reduction in intracellular Zn relative to the PZ treatment (1.5-fold), but still 1.6-fold higher than the untreated control (Fig. 1A). Thus, PBT2 retains Zn ionophore function despite the 100-fold molar excess of Mn relative to Zn in TSB. Further, PZM treatment significantly increased intracellular Mn, compared to the untreated control (2.3-fold), to a level comparable to the Mn treatment (Fig. 1B). However, PZM treatment did not restore the intracellular concentration of Fe (Fig. 1C). Collectively, these results indicate that metal dyshomeostasis is central to the mechanism of action of PZ against S. aureus, which likely perturbs or abrogates crucial metal-dependent cellular processes.

Deletion of Zn detoxification systems confirm Zn intoxication as a component of the PBT2 mechanism of action

To identify genes and pathways impacted by PZ-mediated metal disruption, we generated markerless deletion mutants of genes implicated in Zn transport and regulation in strain USA300-HOU-MR (Fig. 2). Deletion of the czrAB operon (ΔczrAB), encoding a transcriptional regulator (czrA) and a Zn exporter (czrB) in USA300-HOU-MR, revealed no hypersensitivity to PZ (Fig. 2A, B and Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). However, strain USA300-HOU-MR (WT) contains a plasmid-borne (pUSA300HOUMR) cadmium export system, cadDX (cadD encodes the exporter and cadX the transcriptional regulator), which may compensate for Zn export, as homologues can export Zn, as well as cobalt, nickel and copper39–41. Deletion of ΔcadDX revealed no hypersensitivity to PZ (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). However, deletion of both operons in USA300-HOU-MR (ΔczrABΔcadDX) demonstrated hypersensitivity with decreased PBT2 and Zn MICs (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5), increased inhibition with combinations of PZ (FICI 0.09) (Fig. 2D), and enhanced bactericidal killing (Fig. 2F), compared to WT (Fig. 2E). ICP-MS of the ΔczrABΔcadDX mutant demonstrated significantly elevated intracellular Zn (1.7-fold) under untreated conditions, relative to WT (Fig. 2G). This was further increased in response to Zn (50 µM) (3.3-fold increase), to similar levels as the PZ treatment (1.7-fold increase), both compared to respective WT treatments (Fig. 2G). Intracellular Mn was not significantly reduced by 50 µM Zn treatment in ΔczrABΔcadDX (Fig. 2H), suggesting that PZ-induced Mn depletion is not a direct physiological impact arising from Zn accumulation in S. aureus. Our ΔczrABΔcadDX mutant provides clear molecular evidence that the antibacterial activity of PBT2 is enhanced by increased Zn intoxication, and that disruption of metal export systems renders S. aureus substantially more vulnerable to metal stress.

Mn depletion contributes to the mechanism of action of PBT2 against MRSA

To further understand the impact of PZ-induced metal dyshomeostasis, we focused on PBT2-mediated Mn depletion, given the requirement for Mn during S. aureus growth and pathogenesis and the restriction of Mn via nutritional immunity13,42,43. To examine this, we generated a USA300-HOU-MR mutant lacking the characterised Mn importers: the primary ATP-binding cassette (ABC) importer, mntABC, and the natural resistance-associated macrophage protein (NRAMP) transporter, mntH (ΔmntABCΔmntH)18,44. The ΔmntABCΔmntH mutant demonstrated hypersensitivity to PBT2 alone (fourfold lower MIC) (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 3, and Supplementary Tables 4 and 5) and reduced cell viability when exposed to PBT2 (8 or 16 µM) or combined with Zn (PZ) (Fig. 3B), compared to WT (Fig. 2D). Under untreated conditions, ΔmntABCΔmntH had significantly lower intracellular Mn (2.9-fold) compared to WT (Fig. 3C), consistent with the loss of primary Mn uptake systems. PZ treatment further decreased intracellular Mn (15-fold), relative to the PZ-treated WT strain (Fig. 3C). Taken together, further compromising Mn acquisition increased the potency of PZ against USA300-HOU-MR, supporting the inference that this impact contributes to the PBT2 mechanism of action against S. aureus.

Deletion of the sole characterised Mn exporter (ΔmntE) or the Mn importers (ΔmntABCΔmntH) still resulted in significant Mn depletion when treated with PZ, relative to untreated (Fig. 3D–F). Therefore, PBT2 causes depletion of intracellular Mn through a mechanism independent of the known Mn transporters in S. aureus, although the involvement of an unknown transporter in response to PBT2 cannot be excluded. The ability of subinhibitory PBT2 to increase the Mn MIC of ΔmntE 128-fold (Supplementary Table 6), and to increase ΔmntE cell viability in the presence of Mn (Fig. 3G, H), further suggests that PBT2 promotes Mn efflux. Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) demonstrated that PBT2 can indeed bind Mn2+ with a Kd = 42 µM at pH 7.7 and a Kd = 2 µM at pH 4 (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). In comparison, PBT2’s affinity for Zn2+ has a Kd = 2 µM at pH 7.7 and no binding affinity at pH 4 (Supplementary Fig. 4C, D). Therefore, while PBT2 binds stronger to Zn than Mn at physiological pH, these findings highlight the possibility of direct PBT2-Mn translocation. Together, this data indicates that Mn depletion occurs in response to PBT2 treatment, and not as a result of Zn accumulation alone, but whether this reflects direct Mn translocation by PBT2 or an indirect effect on alternative transporters remains to be determined.

PZ impacts the oxidative stress response and metabolic gene expression in MRSA

To determine the global impact of PZ-induced metal dyshomeostasis, we analysed the transcriptome of USA300-HOU-MR in response to subinhibitory PZ (4 µM PBT2 + 50 µM Zn) and with Mn supplementation (5 mM, PZM) (0.5, 1 and 2 h) in TSB. Validating previous results, metal ion transporter genes were differentially expressed (Fig. 4A), which was confirmed through RT-qPCR (Supplementary Table 7). PZ upregulated Zn exporter genes czrA, czrB and cadD (3.95, 3.4 and 3.01 log2-fold 0.5 h) and downregulated Zn importer genes adcA and cntB (−1.81 and −2.36 log2-fold, 1 h) (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Data 1), but did not impact Mn transporter expression (mntABC, mntH and mntE) (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Data 1). Moreover, Fe transport genes (fetB, sirA, sirB and sirC) were downregulated and Cu export genes (copA, copB and copZ) were upregulated in response to PZ (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Data 1). Notably, changes in gene expression extended beyond metal ion homeostasis, with 1029 genes differentially expressed (>1-log2-fold) by PZ (0.5 h) (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Analysis of significantly enriched KEGG pathways revealed widespread effects, particularly on metabolic pathways (Fig. 4C).

We detected changes in expression of ROS detoxification genes, with sodA and sodM upregulated (2.2- and 2.5 log2-fold, respectively) by PZ, while sodM was downregulated by PZM (−2.17 log2-fold 0.5 h) (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Data 1 and 2). Other ROS detoxification genes (katA, ahpC, ahpF, msrAB, trxAB and bcp) similarly were upregulated by PZ (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Data 1), suggesting elevated intracellular ROS or impaired enzyme function. We hypothesised that PZ-mediated Mn depletion could impact the ability to detoxify ROS due to the metal-dependent nature of superoxide dismutase enzymes, with the Mn-dependent SodA, and the Mn- or Fe-dependent SodM20,45. To investigate, we generated USA300-HOU-MR single and double mutants lacking specific ROS detoxification genes (ΔsodA, ΔsodM, ΔkatA and ΔahpC). Only ΔahpC and ΔsodAΔsodM demonstrated increased susceptibility to PBT2 (twofold decreased MIC) (Supplementary Table 8). However, in time-kill assays PZ was more bactericidal against ΔahpC, ΔsodA, ΔsodAΔsodM and ΔsodAΔkatA, compared to WT (Supplementary Fig. 6). We detected a significant increase in CellROX™ fluorescence, an indicator of superoxide46,47, by 24 h PZ treatment, comparable to the menadione positive control and PZ-treated ΔsodAΔsodM (Supplementary Fig. 6H). The Amplex Red™ assay for H2O2 detection showed no significant change in response to PZ but a significant increase in H2O2 by PZM (Supplementary Fig. 6I, J).

Transcriptomic analysis revealed differential expression of many genes involved in central carbon metabolism (Fig. 4B, C). The glycolytic genes pgi, fbaA, pgk, gpmA, gpmI and eno were upregulated in response to PZ treatment (0.5 h) (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Data 1), with gpmI and fbaA encoding Mn-dependent enzymes48,49. Conversely, PZM treatment (relative to PZ) reversed this effect, downregulating these genes (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Data 2). Downstream of glycolysis, genes encoding the Fe-S cluster-dependent TCA cycle enzymes, citZ, acnA and icd, were upregulated by PZ 0.5 h treatment (4.15, 4.13 and 3.73 log2-fold respectively), but were downregulated by PZM 0.5 h treatment (−5.73, −5.92 and −3.77 log2-fold respectively) (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Data 1 and 2). The most strongly downregulated gene by PZ was ldh1 (−9.43 log2-fold, 0.5 h) (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. 5A and Supplementary Data 1), encoding a NADH-dependent L-lactate dehydrogenase, important for redox homeostasis by regenerating NAD+ 50,51. Other genes in the fermentative network (srrAB, pflAB and adhPE) were downregulated by PZ, albeit to a lesser degree (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Data 1). Notably, ldh1 repression was reversed by Mn supplementation (PZM) with a 6.54 log2-fold upregulation relative to PZ (0.5 h) (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. 5B and Supplementary Data 2). Additionally, genes encoding subunits of the ATP synthase F1 sector (atpA, atpD, atpG, atpH and atpC) and Fo sector (atpB, atpF and atpE) were downregulated in response to PZ and PZM (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Data 1 and 2). These transcriptional analyses reveal a broad disruption of gene expression by PZ, with changes in the oxidative stress response and central carbon metabolism likely resulting from the metal-dependency of key enzymes, and potentially mitigated by Mn supplementation.

PZ-mediated metal dyshomeostasis perturbs central carbon metabolism, impacting redox homeostasis, energy metabolism and peptidoglycan synthesis in MRSA

To address the PZ-mediated changes in metabolic gene expression, we performed untargeted metabolomic analysis of MRSA USA300-HOU-MR under inhibitory PZ conditions (16 µM PBT2 + 100 µM Zn, 1, 3, 6 h) to observe significant changes in metabolic flux (Supplementary Fig. 7). After 6 h treatment, 447 metabolites were significantly changed (p value <0.05; log2-fold change (FC) threshold >1.0) in response to inhibitory PZ, 88 were significantly changed in response to PZM, and 210 were similarly altered across both treatments (Supplementary Fig. 8). Principal-components analysis demonstrated a significant separation of treatment groups from untreated across all time points (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Our data demonstrate that PZ disrupts MRSA central carbon metabolism at multiple key nodes, with the most pronounced changes occurring after 6 h treatment (Fig. 5). Glycolytic flux was impaired early, with glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) accumulation (log2-FC3h = 1.34, log2-FC6h = 7.54) and subsequent depletion of fructose-6-phosphate (F6P) (log2-FC1h = -1.17, log2-FC6h = -1.81) (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Data 4). The significant accumulation of the non-reducing sugar trehalose-6-phosphate (log2-FC3h = 7.76, log2-FC6h = 8.34) and pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) metabolites (ribulose-5-phosphate, ribose-5-phosphate and sedoheptulose-7-phosphate) suggest a complementary rerouting of metabolic flux to avoid glycolysis, potentially as an adaptive response to oxidative stress (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Data 4). At all time points pyruvate was significantly accumulated in response PZ (log2-FC1h = 1.36, log2-FC3h = 1.99, log2-FC6h = 2.05) while acetyl-CoA was reduced (log2-FC = −2.71, log2-FC = −1.47, log2-FC = −1.88) (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Data 4). Lactic acid was reduced after 6 h PZ treatment (log2-FC6h = −1.32), suggesting impaired flux from pyruvate to lactate via lactate dehydrogenases51, aligning with the strong downregulation of ldh1 (0.5 h, subinhibitory) (Fig. 4B).

Within the TCA cycle, citrate (log2-FC1h = 1.88, log2-FC3h = 6.62, log2-FC6h = 6.86) and cis-aconitate (log2-FC1h = 1.85, log2-FC3h = 6.31, log2-FC6h = 5.96), the intermediate metabolite formed by aconitase (AcnA), were both strongly increased by PZ after all time points, indicating potential disruption of the AcnA Fe-S cluster via oxidative damage52, Zn mismetallation53–55, or PZ-induced Fe depletion56 (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Data 4). Subsequently, we propose that AcnA disruption blocks TCA cycle flux, resulting in the depletion of succinyl-CoA (log2-FC6h = −3.79) and succinate (log2-FC6h = −1.15) (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Data 4). Oxaloacetate was significantly increased (log2-FC6h = 3.25), potentially due to increased flux through the PEP-pyruvate-oxaloacetate (PPO) node57. Consistent with disruptions in carbohydrate catabolism through glycolysis and the TCA cycle, the critical electron-carrying coenzyme, NADH, was significantly depleted by PZ treatment across all time points (log2-FC1h = −4.63, log2-FC3h = −4.85, log2-FC6h = −6.50), indicating a disruption of redox homeostasis (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Data 4). Significant ATP depletion by PZ (log2-FC3h = −2.68, log2-FC6h = −2.08), and PZM (log2-FC = −4.01 and −3.11 after 3 and 6 h) was observed (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Data 4).

Beyond impairing redox balance and energy production, PZ disrupted downstream biosynthetic pathways. Peptidoglycan synthesis branches from glycolysis via the diversion of F6P by the GlmSMU enzymes, generating UDP-N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc)58. Multiple early peptidoglycan precursor metabolites were significantly changed by PZ (GlcNAc-6-phosphate, UDP-GlcNAc, UDP-MurNAc) across all time points (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Data 4). Therefore, PZ may limit the availability of precursors required to synthesise lipid II, the essential rate-limiting step, affecting cell wall integrity59. Additionally, among the eleven amino acids that can be catabolised by S. aureus60, seven were significantly decreased by PZ (6 h), including proline, glutamine, citrulline, ornithine, aspartate, aspartic acid and alanine (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Data 4). Numerous metabolites involved in synthesising nucleotides and lipids (glycerophospholipids and fatty acids) were also reduced following PZ treatment (Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12 and Supplementary Data 4). Overall, these widespread metabolic disruptions highlight the multifaceted impact of PZ-induced metal stress on core bacterial physiology, likely driven by a combination of Zn mismetallation, Mn/Fe depletion affecting cofactor availability and Fe-S clusters, and oxidative damage.

PZ disrupts peptidoglycan biosynthesis and resensitises MRSA to bactericidal killing by oxacillin in vitro and in vivo

Previous studies have demonstrated PBT2’s ability to resensitise various bacterial pathogens to antibiotics rendered ineffective by resistance31,33,35,37, but the molecular mechanisms remain undefined in MRSA. Our transcriptomics revealed differential expression of genes involved in peptidoglycan biosynthesis (dltAB, mupG, murQ, mraY and murE) and β-lactam resistance (mecA, blaR1, blaI, pbp4 and ftsH) in response to PZ treatment (Supplementary Fig. 5G). Given this and the significant PZ disruption of peptidoglycan precursor metabolites (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 10), we further investigated the impact of PZ on the cell envelope and its capacity to restore susceptibility to β-lactam antibiotics in MRSA.

PBT2 (2 µM, ¼×MIC) and Zn (40 µM) in combination (PZ) sensitised USA300-HOU-MR to oxacillin (>512-fold MIC decrease) and ampicillin (64-fold MIC decrease) (below the CLSI oxacillin breakpoints61) and substantially reduced the MICs of other β-lactams and erythromycin in CAMHB (Supplementary Table 9). Similar reductions were observed for EMRSA-1562 and the laboratory-derived MRSA strain RA12063 (Supplementary Table 9). In TSB at ¼ MIC of PBT2 (4 µM) and Zn (50 µM) the oxacillin MIC was also reduced from 32 µg/mL to 0.5 µg/mL in USA300-HOU-MR (Supplementary Table 10). Time-kill assays in TSB with subinhibitory PBT2 (4 µM), Zn (50 µM) or oxacillin (16 µg/mL) did not impact USA300-HOU-MR cell viability after 24 h, whereas their combination (PZO) was strongly bactericidal (≥3-log10 reduction of the initial inoculum), demonstrating oxacillin resensitisation (Fig. 6A). Aligning with our previous data (Fig. 1A, B), Mn (5 mM) completely restored growth in the presence of PZO at 24 h (>4-log10-fold increase in CFU/mL) (Fig. 6B). Mn also impacted oxacillin treatment alone (16 µg/mL), where the 0–12 h lag in cell viability (Fig. 6A) was not observed with supplementary Mn (Fig. 6B). Consequently, loss of Mn import (ΔmntABCΔmntH) increased the bactericidal activity of PZO (Supplementary Fig. 13B), whereas loss of Mn export (ΔmntE) partially restored cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 13C), relative to WT (Supplementary Fig. 13A). Deletion of Mn transporters also altered oxacillin sensitivity, with a fourfold lower MIC against ΔmntABCΔmntH and ΔmntE (Supplementary Table 4), directly linking Mn dysregulation and β-lactam resistance in S. aureus, potentially as a consequence of disrupted central carbon metabolism. Notably, PBT2 further sensitised USA300-HOU-MR deletion strain ΔmecA to oxacillin (Supplementary Fig. 14), suggesting that the mechanism of PZ-mediated oxacillin resensitisation occurs independently of the PBP2a resistant determinant.

Treatment with subinhibitory PZ (9 µM + 50 µM) did not alter cell membrane permeability, in contrast to the nisin-positive control (Supplementary Fig. 15). Similarly, 4 h treatment with the same PZ concentration did not visibly affect the cell envelope through TEM and SEM, compared to untreated (Supplementary Fig. 16). Contrasting these observations, 24 h treatment with 32 µM PBT2 (2×MIC) combined with 100 µM Zn caused substantial cell envelope morphological changes compared to untreated cells, including a wavy cell wall-membrane interface, abnormal septum formation and some complete cell lysis (Supplementary Fig. 17). Notably, the addition of Mn (PZM) restored cell morphology comparable to untreated, with intact and defined cell wall structure (Supplementary Fig. 17). We additionally quantified muramic acid as an indicator of peptidoglycan abundance for subinhibitory PZ (4 µM + 50 µM) and inhibitory PZ (16 µM + 100 µM). Across all conditions, PZ treatment significantly decreased the peptidoglycan abundance of USA300-HOU-MR WT relative to untreated samples (Fig. 6C, D), confirming that in the absence of oxacillin, PZ impairs cell wall biosynthesis in USA300-HOU-MR. Notably, Mn supplementation (PZM) restored peptidoglycan abundance at most time points (Fig. 6C, D).

To ascertain the contribution of Zn and Mn disruptions to the mechanism of antibiotic resensitisation, we utilised ΔczrABΔcadDX, which experiences significant intracellular Zn accumulation from 50 µM Zn (Fig. 2F) and a quadruple deletion strain of the two Zn exporters (ΔczrABΔcadDX,) and the two Mn importers (ΔmntABCΔmntH), referred to as ΔCCΔMM (Supplementary Table 1). No change in oxacillin MIC was observed against ΔczrABΔcadDX in TSB or with additional Zn (50, 100, 200 or 400 µM) (Supplementary Fig. 18A). In contrast, treatment of ΔCCΔMM with 400 µM Zn resulted in a 128-fold lower oxacillin MIC (Supplementary Fig. 18A). After 24 h, we detected a 3-log10 decrease in ΔczrABΔcadDX viability treated with oxacillin + Zn (400 µM) relative to untreated, while the same combination against ΔCCΔMM caused a 7-log10 decrease with cell viability reaching the LOD (1 × 102 CFU/mL) (Fig. 6E). ICP-MS analysis of ΔCCΔMM confirmed significant dose-dependent Zn accumulation and Mn depletion in response to all Zn treatments, relative to WT (Supplementary Fig. 18B, C). Therefore, we propose that Zn toxicity alone is not sufficient for β-lactam resensitisation, with Mn depletion also contributing to this phenotype.

Finally, we evaluated whether PBT2 enhances oxacillin efficacy against USA300-HOU-MR in vivo using innate antimicrobial Zn64. In a 10-day intraperitoneal infection model, PBT2 + oxacillin treatment significantly improved mouse survival (80%) compared to monotherapy or saline (40%) (Fig. 6F). In a 24 h pulmonary infection model, PBT2 + oxacillin significantly reduced the USA300-HOU-MR burden in the lungs of mice compared to the saline control (2.2-log10 reduction), while individual treatments were not significant (Fig. 6G). ICP-MS analysis revealed a significant elevation of Zn and Mn in lung tissue in USA300-HOU-MR challenged and PBT2-treated mice, compared to unchallenged (Supplementary Fig. 19A, B). In contrast, in blood serum samples, both challenged, and PBT2-treated mice had significantly decreased Zn (and significantly decreased Mn with PBT2 treatment) compared to unchallenged mice (Supplementary Fig. 19C, D). Collectively, these data show the potential of PBT2 as an adjuvant to potentiate oxacillin within the host by using bioavailable antimicrobial Zn.

Discussion

In the face of rising AMR and a dwindling supply of effective antibiotics, there is renewed interest in harnessing metals as novel antibacterials5. We investigated the molecular impacts of metal dyshomeostasis in response to PBT2 and Zn (PZ) in MRSA, focusing on the dual metal stress of Zn toxicity and Mn depletion. Notably, the antibacterial activity of PZ was reversed by the addition of 5 mM Mn; a concentration that far exceeds physiological levels in host tissues and blood65. Excess Mn restored PZ-induced Mn depletion and partially mitigated Zn accumulation, which we propose restores the function of metal-dependent pathways, such as superoxide detoxification and glycolysis.

Previously, we have characterised PBT2 as a Zn2+/H+ exchanger using reconstituted proteoliposomes32; however, whether the same proton-coupled mechanism operates in the more complex intracellular environment of MRSA has not been investigated. The metal dyshomeostasis observed here, simultaneous Zn accumulation and Mn depletion, is consistent with PBT2 additionally mediating exchange of Zn2+ with Mn2+, in line with its affinity for both metals and the buffered intracellular pools of Zn and Mn in bacteria38. The depletion of intracellular Fe by PBT2 alone also raises the possibility that PBT2 functions as an Fe ionophore, given the known affinity of the 8-hydroxyquinoline scaffold for Fe2+66. However, unlike Mn depletion, Fe depletion was not specific to the PZ combination, and the underlying mechanism, whether direct Fe translocation, indirect effects on Fe homeostasis, or disruption of Fe-S cluster integrity, remains to be determined.

Metals are critical cofactors for bacterial central carbon metabolism, making these pathways susceptible to disruption of bacterial metal ion homeostasis26,67,68 and host-imposed metal stress22,69. Under PZ-induced dual metal stress, we observed disruption of central carbon metabolism at the transcriptomic and metabolic levels, and impaired aerobic respiration, demonstrating perturbation of metabolic flux in USA300-HOU-MR. Zn-mediated mismetallation of glycolytic enzymes potentially drives this central metabolic disruption, as observed in Streptococcus pyogenes with Zn-mediated inhibition of phosphofructokinase and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase enzymes67. In turn, multiple glycolytic enzymes (GpmI and FbaA) rely on Mn for function, which likely contributes to the disruptions observed within this pathway48,49. We propose that PZ inhibits the function of multiple metabolic enzymes, including aconitase, given that the enzyme’s [4Fe-4S] cluster is sensitive to oxidative damage52, Zn mismetallation53,70 and Fe depletion56, warranting further metalloproteomic investigation. Disruption of the TCA cycle at this step prevents the generation of reducing equivalents for respiration, and likely underlies the accumulation of upstream precursors, like pyruvate, that feed into the cycle. The concurrent collapse of glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and respiration by PZ, coupled with acetyl-CoA, NADH and ATP depletion, highlights a severe metabolic disruption. The increased abundance of PPP metabolites may demonstrate a compensatory metabolic rerouting in response to these perturbations. However, as the PPP contains Mg-dependent enzymes, certain steps may be compromised by PZ-mediated Mg depletion, potentially limiting the capacity of the PPP to sustain growth under PZ treatment. Targeting glucose metabolism is significant because S. aureus prefers glucose as its carbon source in the host environment and relies on glycolysis for virulence23,24,71. This is especially relevant to diabetic patients who experience more severe S. aureus infections due to elevated blood glucose levels25,72,73. Conversely, under glucose limitation S. aureus can alter metabolic flux to catabolise specific amino acids60,74, therefore, the PZ-induced depletion of these amino acids may additionally impair this metabolic adaptation.

Beyond central carbon metabolism, PZ-mediated metal dysregulation reduced S. aureus’s ability to respond to ROS stress. ROS detoxification systems require metal cofactors, such as Mn/Fe for SodA/SodM, and reducing equivalents, such as the NADH-dependent enzyme AhpC, for effective activity20,21,75. Therefore, we attributed the PZ-induced accumulation of ROS to the reduction of available Mn/Fe for SOD enzyme function and redox imbalance caused by NADH depletion.

Competition between the host and S. aureus for Mn and Zn is well established, where S. aureus utilises high-affinity transport systems to outcompete sequestering proteins such as calprotectin18. PBT2 overcomes both the Zn export and Mn import systems to maintain Zn toxicity and Mn depletion at similar levels observed when these systems are deleted. Furthermore, our murine pulmonary model revealed that USA300-HOU-MR infection elevated Zn and Mn levels in the lungs, and PBT2 further enhanced these levels. Host neutrophils express calprotectin, but the level of metal sequestration varies across infection sites76–78. Consequently, even in the absence of significant host-derived metal sequestration in the lungs, PBT2 + oxacillin demonstrated therapeutic efficacy against pulmonary MRSA infection. We propose that PZ simultaneously mimics key aspects of nutritional immunity, specifically the limitation of Mn bioavailability by calprotectin, and Zn intoxication within tissues and phagosomes64,69. While our findings establish PBT2 as an effective antibiotic adjuvant against MRSA in vivo, alongside studies in other high-priority pathogens31,33,35,36, key questions remain about its therapeutic potential. Specifically, it is unclear whether PBT2 retains activity within phagocytic immune cells, where bacteria are already subjected to metal dysregulation, oxidative stress and metabolic shifts51. Additionally, our metabolic findings highlight the importance of considering the potential impacts of PBT2 on host cell metabolism.

To summarise, despite the host employing innate antimicrobial mechanisms to control infections, which include nutritional immunity and oxidative stress, bacterial pathogens are able to overcome these factors via the use of high-affinity metal transporters17–19, antioxidants to combat oxidative stress20,21, and metabolic flexibility22–26. PBT2 disrupts these systems in USA300-HOU-MR by dysregulating metal ion homeostasis (CzrAB, CadDX, MntABC and MntH) (Fig. 7A), impairing oxidative defence systems (SodA, SodM and AhpC) (Fig. 7B), and restricting metabolic flux, thereby disrupting energy metabolism (Fig. 7C). We identified several factors underlying the potentiation of oxacillin activity by PBT2, including heightened cell wall stress, impaired synthesis of peptidoglycan precursors, reduced peptidoglycan abundance, redox imbalance, and ATP depletion (Fig. 7D). These global molecular disruptions of MRSA align with our inability to generate PBT2-resistant mutants and are consistent with findings in other bacteria31,32,35,79. Therefore, in comparison to current MRSA therapeutics (e.g. vancomycin and ceftaroline), which face growing resistance80,81, PBT2 or similar metal-targeting compounds may represent sustainable long-term treatment options for combatting antibiotic-resistant infections. Collectively, these findings establish a strong foundation for developing innovative metal-based therapies to combat AMR, informed directly by the vulnerabilities exposed by dual metal stress.

Methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

Bacterial strains utilised in this study are described in Supplementary Table 1. Staphylococcus aureus isolates (USA300-HOU-MR) were grown in either tryptic soy broth (TSB) or cation-adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth (CAMHB). Cultures were routinely grown at 37 °C with shaking (200 rpm) or on Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA, agar 1.5% [w/v]) for 16–24 h. All strains were maintained in cryo-culture (−80 °C) at a 1:1 ratio of culture to 87% glycerol. For large-volume experiments (30–250 mL), strains were grown in a 1:4 media:headspace ratio in glass Erlenmeyer flasks.

Antimicrobial stocks and chemical reagents

Unless otherwise stated, all antibiotics used were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (New Zealand). Antibiotics were dissolved in Milli-Q (mQ) water at 10 mg/mL and filter-sterilised except for erythromycin (Erm; 90% EtOH) and chloramphenicol (Cm; 90% EtOH). All antibiotic stocks were stored at −20 °C. Stocks of ZnSO4•7H2O (Zn) and MnSO4•H2O (Mn) were prepared in mQ water at 100 or 400 mM and filter-sterilised. PBT2 was synthesised as described31,32, and stocks were prepared at 2 mg/mL in 10% (v/v) DMSO and stored at 4 °C. The internal standards for metabolomics (3-[(3-cholamidopropyl)-dimethylammonio]-1-propanesulfonate (CHAPS), N-cyclohexyl-3-aminopropanesulfonic acid (CAPS), piperazine-N,N′-bis(2-ethanesulfonic acid) (PIPES), and tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (Tris)) were prepared in mQ water at 100 mM, adjusted to pH 7.5 and filter-sterilised.

Construction of markerless gene deletion mutants in MRSA USA300-HOU-MR

Markerless in-frame deletion mutants were generated in S. aureus USA300-HOU-MR using the allelic exchange plasmid, pIMAY-Z, as described by Monk and Stinear82 (Supplementary Table 2). Briefly, AF, BR, CF and DR primers for each gene deletion (Supplementary Table 3) were designed against the S. aureus USA300-HOU-MR (TCH1516) genome to generate the upstream (AB) and downstream (CD) flanks. A and D primers were tailed with a complementary sequence to the pIMAY-Z to facilitate recombination cloning. Regions flanking the gene to be deleted were amplified with Phusion™ High-Fidelity PCR (Thermo Scientific). The resulting AB and CD products were used as templates in spliced overlap extension PCR to generate the AD deletion construct. This was cloned into PCR amplified (primers IM1/IM2) pIMAY-Z and SLiCE cloned into E. coli IM08B with selection on Cm 10 µg/mL and 100 µg/ml of X-gal82. Following colony PCR to confirm the presence of the pIMAY-Z_gene construct, the purified plasmid was transformed into electrocompetent USA300-HOU-MR and allelic exchange was performed as described by Monk and Stinear82. Successful construction of gene deletion mutants was confirmed through colony PCR, followed by Illumina Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS), performed by SeqCenter (Pittsburgh, PA, USA).

Determining the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) and time-dependent cell-killing assays

The MIC of all compounds in this study were determined by broth microdilution following the CLSI guidelines61. To determine the MBC, 10-fold serial dilutions from MIC plates were made in sterile PBS, spotted (10 µL) on TSA and incubated for 16–20 h. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration preventing solid agar growth for all dilutions. All MIC/MBC assays were undertaken in biological triplicate. When required, media was supplemented with MnSO4 (Mn, 5 mM). For time-dependent cell-killing assays, overnight cultures of USA300-HOU-MR WT were used to inoculate TSB at OD600 0.1 and grown to mid-exponential phase (OD600 0.5, ~2 × 107 CFU/mL). Cultures were diluted 1:10 in 12-well plates to an OD600 0.05 (~2 × 106 CFU/mL) in TSB containing either PBT2 (16 μM), Zn (50 μM), PBT2 + Zn (PZ: PBT2, 16 µM; Zn, 50 μM) or remained untreated, and all treatments were also supplemented with Mn (5 mM). Cell viability (CFU/mL) was assessed at 0, 4, 8, 12 and 24 h post-treatment via serial dilution in PBS and spot plating (10 µL) on TSA. All experiments were performed in biological triplicate. For USA300-HOU-MR ΔczrABΔcadDX and ΔmntABCΔmntH gene deletion strains (Supplementary Table 1), the same methodology was used with treatments in TSB containing either PBT2 (4, 8 or 16 µM) ± Zn (50 μM). To investigate Mn sequestration, USA300-HOU-MR WT and ΔmntE were treated with PBT2 (4 μM) and Mn (5, 10 or 20 mM) ± PBT2 (4 µM). For oxacillin resensitisation assays, USA300-HOU-MR WT, ΔmntABCΔmntH and ΔmntE were treated with PBT2 (4 μM), Zn (50 μM), oxacillin (16 μg/mL), PZ (PBT2; 4 µM, Zn; 50 µM), PZ + oxacillin (PZO), remained untreated, or were additionally supplemented with Mn (5 mM).

Checkerboard broth microdilution assays

Checkerboard assays involved arraying concentrations of PBT2 (0–16 or 0–64 µM, final) and Zn (0–3200 µM, final) in 96-well microtiter master plates in compound diluent at 40 × concentration to account for subsequent dilutions. For each checkerboard, 2.5 µL of each well of the master plate was added to a 96-well microtiter assay plate for both compounds. Then, 45 µL of TSB was added to all wells. Finally, 50 µL of MRSA USA300-HOU-MR was prepared by direct colony suspension to a 0.5 MacFarland standard, diluting 1/150 in media yielding ~5 × 105 CFU/mL (final). Untreated cells and sterile media controls were included on each plate. After incubation overnight (16–24 h) at 37 °C, the OD600 was determined in a Varioskan Flash microplate reader (Thermo Scientific). For Mn supplementation checkerboards, the OD440 of bromothymol blue (BTB) dye was used to determine growth, due to precipitation. The fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) was determined as previously described83. Results of FICI analysis were interpreted according to previously described threshold values for two compounds in combination84. Synergism between two compounds is considered at an FICI of ≤0.5. Checkerboard assays were performed in biological triplicate.

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS)

ICP-MS was performed as described in ref. 32. Briefly, USA300-HOU-MR WT overnight cultures were used to inoculate TSB (250 mL) and grown to mid-exponential phase (OD600 0.5, ~2 × 107 CFU/mL). Then 10 mL aliquots were challenged for 4 h with either PBT2 (9 μM), Zn (50 μM), Mn (5 mM), PZ, PZM, or remained untreated. Metal contents of the samples were determined using an Agilent 7500ce ICP-MS (Centre of Trace Element Analysis, Department of Chemistry, University of Otago). The resulting metal ion concentrations (mg/L) of samples were converted to molar units as described in ref. 32. The OD600 of each sample and the USA300-HOU-MR protein content (94.62 mg protein/OD600/L) were used to determine the total metal ion concentration (which we term the ‘intracellular’ metal ion concentration) based on an intracellular volume of 5 μL/mg protein85. Protein content was determined by a BCA assay (Thermo Scientific) of cell lysates (lysed by 1 M NaOH, 95 °C, and neutralised with 1 M HCl) using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard. ICP-MS was conducted in technical duplicate for three biological replicates, and statistical significance was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-test.

RNA sequencing and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Overnight cultures of USA300-HOU-MR WT were used to inoculate (OD600 0.1) flasks containing 500 mL TSB and were grown to mid-exponential phase (OD600 0.5, ~2 × 107 CFU/mL) in biological quadruplicate. Cells were treated (125 mL in 500 mL flasks) for 0, 0.5, 1 or 2 h with either PZ (4 + 50 µM) or PZ + Mn (5 mM) (PZM) or remained untreated. At each time point, 15 mL aliquots were added to centrifuge bottles for quenching via 30 mL ice-cold glycerol:saline (3:2), as previously described86. Samples were centrifuged at 8000×g for 20 min at −20 °C, supernatants were removed, and the resulting pellets were resuspended in 1 mL ice-cold glycerol:saline (3:2), before being stored at −80 °C.

Cell pellets were thawed on ice and then centrifuged at 17,000×g for 3 min at 4 °C to remove the glycerol:saline. Bead-beating tubes (SSI Bio) were prepared with ~50 µL zirconium beads (0.1 mm, BioSpec Products) for RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated as previously described87, by TRIzol-chloroform extraction (1 mL per pellet). The RNA was purified using the RNA clean and concentratorTM-5 extraction kit (ZYMO Research) following the manufacturer’s instructions and eluted in DEPC-mQ (15 µL). The RNA was treated with DNase (TURBO DNA-free kit, Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA (ng/μL) was quantified using a Qubit (RNA BR Assay Kit), and RNA quality (RIN numbers) was determined using a Bioanalyzer. Sequencing and differential expression analysis of RNA samples was conducted by SeqCenter (Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Library preparation was performed using the Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep Ligation with Ribo-Zero Plus kit and 10 bp unique dual indices. Sequencing was completed on a NovaSeq X Plus, producing paired-end 150 bp reads. Quality control and adaptor trimming were performed with bcl-convert1 (4.1.5). Read mapping was performed with HISAT2 (2.2.0) to the S. aureus USA300-HOU-MR (TCH1516) genome (NC_010079.1). Read quantification was performed using Subread’s featureCounts (2.0.1) functionality. Read counts loaded into R were normalised using edgeR’s Trimmed Mean of M values algorithm. Subsequent values were then converted to counts per million (CPM). Differential expression analysis was performed using edgeR’s glmQLFTest.

RT-qPCR was utilised to validate RNA sequencing results for genes with known differential expression (mntE, mntA, mntB, mntC, and czrA). RNA samples underwent cDNA synthesis from 1 μg of the DNA-free RNA samples using a Superscript™ IV VILO Master Mix kit (Invitrogen). RT-qPCR primers were designed against the S. aureus USA300-HOU-MR (TCH1516) genome (Supplementary Table 3). All RT-qPCR were conducted with the PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix kit (Applied Biosystems), on a ViiA7 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Results were normalised to the gyrB and hu reference genes using the ΔΔCT method88 and presented as the log2-fold change in gene expression relative to the untreated control. Samples for RT-qPCR were in biological triplicate and technical duplicate.

Measurement of superoxide via the CellROX™ assay

The CellROX™ oxidative stress kit (Invitrogen) was used to measure superoxide (•O2−) production46,47, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Overnight cultures of USA300-HOU-MR WT or ΔsodAΔsodM were used to inoculate TSB and were grown to mid-exponential phase (OD600 0.5, ~2 × 107 CFU/mL). Cells were aliquoted (100 µL) into black-walled, clear-bottomed 96-well microtiter plates (Greiner Bio-One) and treated for 24 h with either: PZ (16 + 50 µM), Mn (5 mM), PZ + Mn (PZM), menadione (128 µM), or remained untreated. CellROX™ reagent was added to all wells at a final concentration of 5 µM, including TSB controls, and samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. CellROX™ fluorescence was measured using a Varioskan Flash microplate reader at λex and λem wavelengths of 485/520 nm. The OD600 of all samples was measured to normalise the fluorescence relative to OD600.

Measurement of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) via the Amplex Red™ assay

The Amplex Red™ Hydrogen Peroxide/Peroxidase Assay kit (Invitrogen) was used to measure H2O2 production according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Overnight cultures of USA300-HOU-MR WT or ΔsodAΔsodM were used to inoculate TSB and were grown to mid-exponential phase (OD600 0.5, ~2 × 107 CFU/mL). Cells were split into 1 mL aliquots in 12-well plates (Thermo Scientific) and treated for 4 h with either PZ (16 + 50 µM), Mn (5 mM), or PZ + Mn (PZM), or remained untreated. Cells were transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and harvested by centrifugation (13,000×g, 3 min, 4 °C), and washed twice in PBS. Subsequently, all samples were normalised to an OD600 1 (±0.05) by resuspension in 1×Reaction Buffer (from Amplex Red™ kit). For analysis, 50 µL of each normalised sample was transferred to a black-walled, clear-bottomed 96-well microtiter plate and 50 µL of Amplex Red™ reagent/HRP working solution was added (final volume 100 µL). Additionally, H2O2 spike-in controls were added to untreated samples to assess fluorescence at varying H2O2 concentrations (0.001, 0.01, 0.1 or 1 mM). Amplex Red fluorescence was measured using a Varioskan Flash microplate reader at λex and λem wavelengths of 560/590 nm.

Untargeted metabolomics sample preparation

Overnight cultures of USA300-HOU-MR WT were used to inoculate TSB (250 mL) and grown to mid-exponential phase (OD600 0.5, ~2 × 107 CFU/mL) in biological quadruplicate. Aliquots (65 mL) of cells were transferred to sterile 250 mL flasks containing either PZ (16 + 100 µM), PZM (5 mM) or remained untreated and were grown at 37 °C with shaking (200 rpm). After 1-, 3- and 6 h of treatment, 10 mL samples were removed and transferred to 50 mL falcons. Samples were quenched in a dry ice-ethanol bath for 30 s (swirling to prevent freezing), repeated three times, and rested on ice between each repeat. The OD600 of each sample was measured for normalisation to OD600 0.5 (±0.05) in TSB. Normalised samples were transferred to 15 mL falcons and centrifuged (3220×g, at 4 °C, for 10 min), and supernatants were removed. Sample pellets were stored at −80 °C until further processing for metabolite extraction.

Intracellular metabolite extraction for metabolomics

Cell pellets were thawed on ice and washed with 1 mL cold saline (0.9%) and centrifuged (3220×g, at 4 °C, for 10 min), repeated twice, to remove residual extracellular metabolites and medium components. Samples were mixed with a cold extraction solvent (chloroform:methanol:water at 1:3:1, v/v) containing 1 µM each of the internal standards (CHAPS, CAPS, PIPES and Tris). The samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen, thawed on ice, and vortexed (repeated three times) to release the intracellular metabolites. Samples were transferred to microcentrifuge tubes and centrifuged (14,000×g, at 4 °C, for 10 min) to remove particulate matter. Finally, 200 µL of the resulting supernatant was removed and transferred into an injection vial with a 100 µL conical insertion (Phenomenex) and stored at −80 °C until sample processing. Samples were shipped on dry ice to the Pharmaceutical Sciences and Monash Proteomics and Metabolomics Platform (MPMP), MIPS node (Australia), for liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis, as described in ref. 89. A 10 µL aliquot of each sample was combined as a pooled quality control (QC) sample; containing all the analytes that will be encountered during the LC-MS analysis90.

Untargeted metabolomics data processing, bioinformatics and statistical analyses

Raw mass spectrometry data were processed using IDEOM, an Excel interface for LC-MS data analysis91. ProteoWizard was utilised to convert the raw LC-MS data into a .mzXML format. Mzmatch.R is a software package developed to extend the capabilities of XCMS to align samples and filter peaks using a minimum peak intensity threshold of 100,000 with a relative standard deviation (RSD) of < 0.5 (reproducibility)92. Noise and artefact peaks were removed using the default IDEOM parameters. While the loss of a proton was corrected in negative ESI mode, the gain of a proton was corrected in positive ESI mode, followed by identification of metabolites based on their exact mass within 2 ppm. The identification of each metabolite (Level 1 identification based on MSI standards) was confirmed using the retention rates of authentic standards. Other metabolites were identified (Level 2 identification based on MSI standards) using exact mass and predicted retention time based on the Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), MetaCyc, and LIPIDMAPS databases. In cases where isomers could not be differentiated by retention time, bacterial metabolites annotated in EcoCyc were preferentially selected. Statistical analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Putative metabolites with a median RSD ≤0.2 (20%) within the QC group and IDEOM confidence level ≥5 were incorporated into a table and then uploaded to MetaboAnalyst. Data with >50% missing values were removed, and the remaining missing values were replaced by half the minimum positive value in the original data93. The interquartile range was used to filter the data, followed by log2 transformation and auto-scaling to normalise the data. Two-sample t-tests (one-way, paired) were used to identify metabolites with significant changes (p value <0.05; log2-fold change threshold >1.0) between untreated controls and treatment groups (PZ and PZM). Metabolites were manually curated, and the significantly altered metabolic pathways were identified and described based on statistical analysis. Pathway information was sourced from available databases and included only those pathways with significant changes between untreated controls and treatment groups (PZ and PZM).

Cell membrane permeability assays

Membrane permeability of bacterial strains was measured using the Live/Dead BacLight bacterial viability kit (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. USA300-HOU-MR WT was grown in TSB to mid-exponential-phase (OD600 0.5, ~2 × 107 CFU/mL). Cells were harvested via centrifugation (4000×g, 10 min, 4 °C), then washed and resuspended to an OD600 of 0.6 in potassium phosphate buffer (100 mM, pH 7.5), containing MgCl2 (2 mM) and NaCl (10 mM). A 400 µL aliquot of the cell suspension was removed and maintained on ice as the 0 h sample. The remaining cell suspension was energised by a 10 min incubation with glucose (20 mM). Aliquots (5 mL) were either untreated or treated with PBT2 (9 µM), Zn (50 µM), oxacillin (8 µg/mL), Mn (5 mM), PBT2 + Zn (PZ), PZ + oxacillin (PZO), PZO + Mn (PZOM), nigericin (10 µM) as the negative control or nisin (50 µg/ml) as the positive control, for 1 h at 37 °C with shaking (200 rpm). Aliquots (100 µl) of the 0 and 1 h samples were resuspended with 2× Live/Dead BacLight staining reagent (100 µl) in a 96-well, flat-bottom microtiter plate. Following incubation in the dark for 15 min at RT, the fluorescence emission was measured using a Varioskan Flash microplate reader. Membrane permeability was measured as a relative fluorescence emission ratio (530/630 nm) of the fluorescent dyes SYTO9 and propidium iodide and expressed as the relative change in membrane integrity after 1 h of compound treatment. All experiments were conducted in biological triplicate and measured in technical triplicate, and statistical significance was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post-test.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Overnight cultures of USA300-HOU-MR WT were used to inoculate TSB (30 mL) at OD600 0.1 and were grown to mid-exponential phase (OD600 0.5, ~2 × 107 CFU/mL). Aliquots of 10 mL were treated for 24 h with either PBT2 (32 µM, 2×MIC), Zn (100 µM), PZ (32 + 100 µM), PZ + Mn (5 mM) (PZM), or remained untreated. TEM cultures were pelleted by centrifugation (3220×g, 10 min, 4 °C) and then immersed in a primary fixative solution (2.5% v/v glutaraldehyde in 0.161 M cacodylate buffer, pH 7.2) for 1 h. Samples were washed three times in 0.161 M cacodylate buffer via centrifugation (3220×g, 10 min, RT) and with gentle mixing between washes (10 min shaker, RT). After discarding the supernatant, cells were post-fixed with 1% (w/v) osmium tetroxide in 0.161 M cacodylate buffer for 1 h in the dark with gentle mixing at RT. Cells were rewashed three times in 0.161 M cacodylate buffer before being enrobed in 3% (w/v) agarose (prepared in 0.161 M cacodylate buffer) and allowed to solidify at 4 °C for 15 min. Cells were rinsed twice in ddH2O, serially dehydrated in EtOH (10 min each), and then infiltrated twice with propylene oxide. Samples were embedded with resin (Embed812) in silicone moulds and cured for 48 h at 60 °C. Sections were viewed with a Philips CM 100 transmission electron microscope.

SEM samples were filtered through a 0.45-μm membrane filter (Merck) in a 13 mm Swinnex filter holder (Merck), then transferred to a primary fixative solution (2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer, pH 7.2) for 4 h. Samples were washed three times in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (centrifugation 3220×g, 10 min, RT) before being transferred to 1% (w/v) osmium tetroxide for 1 h. Samples were again washed three times (3220×g for 10 min at RT) in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer before undergoing serial dehydration in EtOH (10 min each). Samples were critical point dried in a Bal-Tec CPD-030 critical point dryer (Bal-Tec AG, Balzers, Liechtenstein) with liquid carbon dioxide and absolute EtOH as the intermediate fluid. When dried, the samples were mounted on aluminium stubs using double-sided carbon tape. All samples were then coated with ~10 nm of gold/palladium (80:20) using a Quorum Q150V ES PLUS coater (Quorum Technologies, East Sussex, UK) before being imaged in a Zeiss Sigma 300VP field emission scanning electron microscope (Carl Zeiss Inc, Oberkochen, Germany) operating at high vacuum, with an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. Sample processing and microscopy were performed at the Otago Micro and Nanoscale Imaging (OMNI) Electron Microscopy Centre at the University of Otago.

Peptidoglycan detection assay

Detection of peptidoglycan abundance was conducted via muramic acid quantification based on the protocol of ref. 33. USA300-HOU-MR WT was grown to mid-exponential phase (OD600 0.5) in TSB and treated under the same conditions as the RNA sequencing samples (PZ; 4 µM + 50 µM) and metabolomics samples (PZ; 16 µM + 100 µM). At the indicated time points (0.5, 1, 2 h or 1, 3, 6 h), 1 mL aliquots were removed and pelleted via centrifugation (13,000×g, 4 min, 4 °C), washed once in sterile PBS, and 20 µL aliquots removed for serial dilution in PBS (to 10−7) and spot plated (10 μL) on TSA. Cell viability (CFU/mL) was determined after 24 h incubation. Pellets were stored at −20 °C until shipment on dry ice to the University of Melbourne for processing. Defrosted pellets were resuspended in 100 µL NaOH (1 M) and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, 100 µL H2SO4 (0.5 M) and 1 mL concentrated H2SO4 (~18 M) were added to samples before heating at 96 °C for 7 min. Samples were cooled on ice before adding 10 µL CuSO4 (4% w/v) and 20 µL 4-phenylphenol in 96% EtOH (1.5% w/v) with immediate mixing. Finally, samples were incubated at 30 °C for 30 min before determining the OD560 in a clear flat-bottom 96-well plate using a CLARIOStar spectrophotometer (BGM Labtech). The OD560 measurement for each sample was corrected for its CFU/mL and normalised to the untreated control for each time point. All experiments were performed in biological triplicate, and statistical significance was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post-test.

Murine intraperitoneal and pulmonary infection models

For the preparation of USA300-HOU-MR WT frozen stocks for infection, cells were grown to mid-exponential phase (OD600 0.5) in TSB, harvested by centrifugation (7000×g, 20 min, 4 °C), washed twice in ice-cold TSB, resuspended in TSB + 15% (v/v) glycerol and then snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage at −80 °C. For peritonitis survival experiments, 6-week-old female BALB/c mice (n = 10) were infected with 5 × 108 CFU via intraperitoneal injection (100 μL). At 0.5, 6, 24, 30 and 48 h post-infection, mice were treated with either an intraperitoneal injection (100 μL) of saline, PBT2 (1.5 mg/kg), oxacillin (15 mg/kg) or PBT2 and oxacillin in combination. Infected mice were housed in individual cages based on treatment, and their survival and morbidity were monitored over 10 days. The log-rank test calculated statistical significance.

To analyse clearance of MRSA USA300-HOU-MR WT from the lungs, groups of 12-week-old BALB/c mice (n = 8/group, sex-matched) were infected intranasally with 25 µL of infection stock (2 × 109 CFU/mL stock, 5 × 107 CFU per mouse). Mice were treated at 0 and 6 h post-infection with either vehicle (saline [0.9% NaCl] via intraperitoneal injection, or SSV [0.9% NaCl, 0.5% (w/v) sodium-carboxymethylcellulose, 0.5% (w/v) benzyl alcohol, 0.4% (w/v) Tween 80] via oral gavage), PBT2 (40 mg/mL, oral gavage), oxacillin (40 mg/mL, intraperitoneal injection), PBT2 + oxacillin or minocycline (40 mg/mL, oral gavage) as a positive control. At 24 h, mice were culled by inhalation of CO2. Lung samples were surgically extracted, and blood samples were taken by cardiac puncture. Autopsied lungs were homogenised by lysing matrix F tubes using a FastPrep instrument (MP Biomedicals). Homogenised lung samples were serially diluted in PBS and plated onto TSA (4 µg/mL oxacillin) to determine the viable bacteria recovered. Statistical significance was calculated by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-test using log-transformed values relative to lung tissue weight (mg). Both blood and lung samples were additionally processed for ICP-MS analysis of Zn and Mn concentrations in these samples, following a method similar to that previously used.
