Emergent dual functionality of water and reverse coordination of s-block metal ions in weakly solvating media
Authors: Masashi Hojo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water exhibits a wide range of anomalous properties, and various models have been proposed to elucidate its nature. In this study, we reexamine the diversity of water's behavior through the lens of solvating power and fundamental chemical interactions in solution. Electroanalytical and spectroscopic investigations in nonaqueous media reveal that s-block metal ions (Li+, Na+, Mg2+, Ca2+, etc.) possess an intrinsic ability to form both coordination-type (metal-centered species such as [MXn]m−) and “reverse coordination” complexes (anion-centered species represented by [MnX]m+) with anions when the solvent's solvating power is sufficiently weak. These findings prompted a reconsideration of the role of trace water in nonaqueous systems, leading to a novel interpretation of the dual (or even multiple) nature of water. We propose that the cooperative hydrogen bond interactions within large-scale water assemblies enable bulk water to undergo reversible cleavage and regeneration of O–H bonds. The disruption of this hydrogen bond network—as observed in highly concentrated electrolyte solutions—markedly reduces the hydration power of water, giving rise to unusual chemical phenomena, such as a partial suppression of nitric acid dissociation and enhanced oxidizing ability.

Introduction

Water exhibits a variety of unusual physicochemical properties, and numerous studies have attempted to elucidate its fundamental nature. Models proposed for liquid water are generally classified into two categories: mixed (two-state) models and continuum models. In the mixed (two-state) model proposed by Frank and Wen,1 non-hydrogen-bonded monomers coexist with hydrogen-bonded clusters, and the equilibrium between these states varies with temperature and pressure. In contrast, continuum models pioneered by Pople2 assume that essentially all water molecules participate in a hydrogen bond network. Bernal and Fowler3 further developed this concept by considering the structural organization of hydrogen-bonded water molecules. More recently, Ohmine and Tanaka4 investigated structural fluctuations and the dynamical properties of hydrogen bond networks to clarify the origin of water's structural diversity. Gallo et al.5 examined the behavior of water from ambient conditions to the deeply supercooled region. The editorial article6 in the special issue of Chem. Rev., ‘Water – The Most Anomalous Liquid,’ provides an overview of the current state of research into water's anomalous properties and the unresolved questions in the field. In addition, Shi and Tanaka7 reported evidence suggesting the coexistence of two types of local structures in liquid water based on molecular simulations combined with analyses of X-ray scattering data.

Very recently,8,9 isolated water molecules have been observed both in highly concentrated salt solutions and in aqueous acetonitrile solutions. Lewis et al.8 studied the changes in the water hydrogen bonding network induced by the dissolution of lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in concentrations ranging from the dilute to the superconcentrated regimes. Using time-resolved two-dimensional infrared spectroscopy (2D IR), they observed the progressive disruption of the water–water hydrogen bond network and the appearance of isolated water molecules interacting only with ions. In aqueous acetonitrile solutions, Nagasaka9 has proposed that isolated water molecules are easily formed and that the electronic structures of the isolated water molecules can be analyzed using O K-edge XAS spectra, which separates the contributions of small water clusters.

The physicochemical properties were examined for concentrated lithium salt solutions, including LiCl, LiBr, LiNO2, and LiNO3, with methyl thiocyanate as an infrared vibrational probe molecule, using two-dimensional infrared spectroscopy (2D IR), NMR, and viscometry.10 In the concentrated solutions, LiNO2 and LiNO3 showed significantly weakened chemical interactions between the lithium cations and the probe when compared with the lithium halide salts. The hydration numbers and geometric structures of alkali metal ions in aqueous solutions have been studied by Mähler and Persson11 using large-angle X-ray scattering (LAXS) and EXAFS, suggesting a systematic increase in coordination number from 4 for Li+ to 5 for Na+, and 7 for K+. Although the ionic effects on water have traditionally been discussed in terms of “structure making” or “breaking,” Mancinelli et al.12 have demonstrated that these macroscopic concepts do not imply a fundamental alteration of the hydrogen-bonding network itself. Instead, they have revealed that the distinct behavior of these ions reflects the localized spatial and orientational disorder of water molecules within the immediate first hydration shell.

Although alkali metal cations are conventionally treated as inert background electrolytes, Daniele et al.13 emphasized that these ions actively participate in solution equilibria through weak interactions. This effect becomes highly pronounced under physiological or concentrated conditions, significantly altering the apparent equilibrium constants. Sodium ions generally exhibit a strong preference for hard oxygen-donor ligands, however, exceptional coordinating behaviors have been reported. For instance, Chattopadhyay et al.14 demonstrated the unique coordination chemistry of Na+, where it directly binds to the neutral nitrogen atoms of non-chelating hexamethylenetetramine to form a water-bridged dimeric core. Alkaline earth metal cations are traditionally considered to form purely ionic compounds due to their closed-shell electronic configurations. Nevertheless, recent advances have revealed their sophisticated coordination chemistry. As elegantly reviewed by Fromm,15 these s-block elements can exhibit transition-metal-like behaviors, forming diverse molecular complexes with significant covalent characteristics.

In general, s-block metal ions (Li+, Na+, Mg2+, Ca2+, etc.) are considered to have a limited ability to form coordination bonds compared with transition metal ions because they lack ligand-field stabilization involving d-orbitals. In aqueous solution, both metal cations and anions are strongly solvated by water molecules, and direct chemical interactions between ions are therefore largely screened by hydration. Consequently, ion–ion interactions in water are usually described primarily in terms of electrostatic forces.

Now, we propose that s-block metal ions possess a latent ability to form both conventional coordination complexes and so-called reverse coordination complexes involving electron-pair donation from anions to vacant orbitals of the metal ions (see Section 2.2). Such interactions are expected to become significant in weakly solvating media. Whereas conventional coordination complexes are metal-centered species such as [MXn]m−, reverse coordination complexes are anion-centered species represented by [MnX]m+. In these species, several lone pairs on a single anion can donate electron density to the vacant orbitals of multiple s-block metal ions. For example, in the Li+–Cl− system, conventional and reverse coordination complexes correspond to [LiCl2]− and [Li2Cl]+, respectively. In such species the bonding cannot be described solely by the neutral ion pair LiCl0 but involves a chemical bonding component beyond simple electrostatic attraction. Note that an chloride ion possesses four lone pairs capable of interacting with multiple metal ions. Even in aqueous solution, Buchner et al.16 reported evidence for the existence of a triple ion, Mg2SO42+(aq.), or possibly a more aggregated species, at high electrolyte concentrations (c > 1 M).

We have previously investigated chemical equilibria and reactions in nonaqueous solvents primarily using electroanalytical techniques, complemented by various spectroscopic methods. During studies addressing the presence of trace amounts of water in organic solvents, we were led to reconsider the fundamental nature of water itself. On the basis of these studies, the dual (or even multiple) nature of water can be interpreted in terms of the collective behavior of water molecules (see Section 2.1). When a vast number of isolated water molecules assemble, cooperative hydrogen bonding gives rise to emergent properties, including an ability to cleave and regenerate O–H bonds within water molecules themselves.

In aqueous solutions containing highly concentrated salts rather than dilute electrolytes, the hydrogen bond network of water becomes significantly disrupted. As a consequence, bulk water loses part of its typical solvent functionality and its solvation ability is markedly reduced. Under such conditions even nitric acid, which normally behaves as a strong electrolyte, may remain partially undissociated. Dilute nitric acid may therefore exhibit a strong oxidizing ability comparable to that of concentrated nitric acid (see Section 2.3.1). It is well known that pure gold dissolves in aqua regia, a mixture of concentrated hydrochloric acid and nitric acid. However, a variety of leaching processes have been developed for gold metallurgy. Chlorination processes based on the Cl2/Cl− system were already used industrially in the nineteenth century but were largely replaced by cyanidation after its introduction in 1887.17 Other halogen-based leaching systems, such as Br2/Br− and I2/I−, have also been investigated. Gold dissolves only in solutions possessing both strong complexing ability and strong oxidizing power. In the final part of this article (Section 2.3.2), the origin of the high oxidation potential of aqua regia is reexamined.

The purpose of this article is to demonstrate that weakly solvating media reveal previously overlooked coordination behavior of s-block metal ions and that these findings provide a unified framework for understanding the dual functionality of water.

Results and discussion

Continuous emergence of water: from isolated water molecules (“dihydrogen ether”) to bulk water with strong solvation power

We have pointed out that, in the following situations, water may more or less lose the normal properties of bulk water:18 (1) residual water in organic solvents (∼mmol dm−3); (2) aqueous solutions or organic solvent–water mixtures containing higher concentrations of salts; (3) nanoscale water droplets in nanotube or reversed micellar systems (number of water molecules, nw < ca. 107); (4) water on metal electrodes, ion-exchange resins, proteins, organic–solvent interfaces, and glass surfaces; and (5) supercritical (or high temperature) water. Under such conditions, water may be “reduced” to isolated H2O molecules, i.e., a poorly solvating form corresponding to “dihydrogen ether” (H)–O–(H).

Nonaqueous solvents such as acetonitrile inherently contain trace amounts of water (typically several mmol dm−3 or more), even after careful drying and dehydration. For this reason, many chemists may suspect that the properties of commonly used solvents differ substantially from those of completely dehydrated solvents. However, it is reasonable to assume that small amounts of water (residual water) in nonaqueous solvents do not significantly influence the chemical equilibria of solutes or reaction rates in the solvent.

Bulk water is highly structured through an extended hydrogen bond network (a “self-assembled” structure). The familiar properties of water are considered to originate from freely existing bulk water composed of an enormous number of molecules (nw ≫ 107). Bulk water behaves as a protic amphoteric solvent and can act toward solutes as a rather “strong” acid as well as a “strong” base. In contrast, an isolated water molecule is expected to exhibit properties markedly different from those of bulk water, because it does not interact with other water molecules and lacks the cooperative effects arising from the collective behavior of a vast number of molecules. Considering that aggregation of water molecules can occur even in the gas phase,19 completely isolated water molecules may exist only in the low-density gas phase at high temperatures. At the moment when an enormous number of H2O molecules condense to form water, proton dissociation becomes possible at any location. This phenomenon arises from the intermolecular hydrogen bond network of water and cannot be attributed merely to interactions among a relatively small number of molecules. Thus, the characteristic acid–base behavior of water appears to be an emergent property arising from the cooperative hydrogen bond network of a large ensemble of molecules.

The properties of nanoscale water droplets confined in reversed micelles differ from those of bulk water, and the number of molecules in such nanosized water clusters can be roughly estimated to be on the order of ∼107–109. Furthermore, considering that the self-dissociation constant of water is Kw = [H+][OH−] = 10−14, it appears that aggregates containing fewer than about 107 H2O molecules (nw < ∼107) cannot retain the intrinsic properties of water.

When H2O condenses from the gas phase to the liquid phase, it appears to acquire a qualitatively new capability arising from the hydrogen bond network: a collective ability to cleave the O–H bond readily and to regenerate it immediately. No significant difference is observed between “isolated” water (H2O) and diethyl ether (Et2O) with respect to their acidity or basicity as solvents. For example, the donor number of water dispersed (dilutely dissolved) in 1,2-dichloroethane has been evaluated as DN = 18,20 which is very close to (or even slightly lower than) that of diethyl ether (DN = 19.2). V. Gutmann20 noted that it is noteworthy that even water, which exhibits anomalous entropy effects as a bulk solvent, conforms to the linear correlation observed for other solvents when it is present in a diluted form in dichloroethane. These observations suggest that a single H2O molecule behaves essentially as a weakly basic ether-like solvent rather than as bulk water.

In the absence of intermolecular hydrogen bonding among a large number of water molecules, the intrinsic acidity and basicity of a single H2O molecule are therefore extremely weak compared with those expected for bulk water. It should also be noted that the O–H bond in a water molecule is not easily cleaved, since the bond enthalpy is estimated to be 463 kJ mol−1 per bond. This value is even larger than that of the C–H bond in methane (413 kJ mol−1).21

Fig. 1 shows the chemical shift values (vs. TMS) of the H2O proton signal in the NMR spectrum as a function of increasing water content in acetonitrile (MeCN). The chemical shift of residual water in dry acetonitrile was approximately 2.1 ppm. As the amount of water increased, the chemical shift also increased (downfield shift), approaching the value of bulk water (ca. 4.7 ppm). Alcohols possessing hydrogen-bonded structures, such as methanol and ethanol, exhibit behavior similar to that of water in their chemical shifts. Silverstein et al.22 pointed out the difference in chemical shifts between bulk water and isolated water as follows. The chemical shift of bulk water—for example, water films on the wall of an NMR tube containing CDCl3 contaminated with moisture, or water suspended in CDCl3—appears at δ ≈ 4.7. In contrast, “monomeric” water dissolved in CDCl3 appears at δ ≈ 1.5, which can interfere with important signals of organic compounds. Residual water or small amounts of water in solvents such as propylene carbonate (PC),23 acetone, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)24 also exhibit chemical shifts smaller than that of bulk water.

These observations indicate that the characteristic chemical shift of bulk water develops progressively with increasing water content, reflecting the formation of an extended hydrogen bond network. In other words, the spectroscopic behavior of water provides direct evidence that the familiar properties of water arise not from individual H2O molecules but from the collective interactions of a large ensemble of molecules.

As discussed above, the acidity and basicity of residual water in nonaqueous solvents should be extremely small, because such residual water cannot form a large hydrogen bond network. In other words, the influence of sufficiently low concentrations of water (<0.01%) in nonaqueous solvents on solutes should be comparable to that of diethyl ether. However, strong Lewis acids such as Al3+ may still react with “isolated” water molecules. Therefore, in studies of nonaqueous solutions, it is needless to say that great care must be taken to remove water from solvents, glassware, and reagents, as well as to minimize the influence of atmospheric moisture. In laboratories handling nonaqueous solvents, it is a routine procedure to remove surface water from glassware by heating in a drying oven at 120 °C. Attention should also be paid to the hysteresis of glass upon repeated heating of flasks.

Frank and Wen1 proposed a model for the hydration of salts, in which three distinct regions are formed around a cation: (A) a region where water molecules are immobilized; (B) a region where the structure of water is disrupted; and (C) structurally “normal” water, i.e., bulk water. Depending on the relative development of regions A and B, an ion is classified as either structure-making or structure-breaking. For example, Li+ develops region A beyond region B and is therefore classified as a structure-making ion. In contrast, the development of region A around Cs+ is small because its hydration is weak; thus, Cs+ behaves as a structure-breaking ion. This discussion, however, is limited to dilute salt solutions.

We propose that when salts are present in water at very high concentrations, region C (“ice-like” water) may decrease substantially or even disappear entirely.25 Under such extreme conditions, only region B (the structure-disrupted region) in the above model would remain capable of functioning as the solvent. Consequently, the properties of the aqueous solvent may change to those of so-called “dihydrogen ether” (H)–O–(H).25–27 Reichardt et al.28 briefly explained the concept of “dihydrogen ether.” At very high salt concentrations (c(salt) > 5 mol dm−3), region C in the Frank–Wen solvation model1 is expected to disappear, leaving only regions A and B. Under such conditions, the resulting aqueous solvent may behave as what has been termed “dihydrogen ether.” This idea was first proposed in 1998.25

Nanoscale water droplets in organic solvents, i.e., in reversed micellar systems, have already partially lost the properties of bulk water, and it has been clearly demonstrated that the addition of salts further enhances this loss of bulk water characteristics.29 In chloroform reversed micellar systems composed of [CHCl3/CTAC (or CTAB)/H2O (1.0 vol%)], where cetyltrimethylammonium chloride (CTAC) or cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) acts as the surfactant, the color of CoCl2 or CoBr2 dissolved in the nanosized water droplets changes from almost colorless to deep blue upon addition of LiClO4. The appearance of the blue color indicates the formation of the [CoX4]2− complex (X = Cl or Br) from [Co(H2O)6]2+, suggesting that this complex is favored under drier conditions, i.e., in a less hydrated environment. Bjerrum et al.30 reported that the overall formation constant β = [CoCl42−]/[Co2+][Cl−]4 in aqueous solution is 10−6.6 at zero ionic strength and 104 in 9 mol dm−3 HClO4 medium. Thus, the formation constant increases by as much as 4 × 1010 upon addition of 9 mol dm−3 HClO4. This large change has been attributed to an increase in the ionic activity coefficient, γ±, up to approximately 350. Such a huge apparent increase is suspicious or difficult to reconcile with simple hydration effects alone. Furthermore, we have observed that the oxidizing ability of dilute nitric acid (<2 mol dm−3) is markedly enhanced in nanoscale water droplets of reversed micellar systems31 (see Section 2.3). This phenomenon may not be explained solely by changes in ionic activity coefficients. Baruah et al.32 have also suggested that the properties of water in reversed micelles differ from those of bulk water even when W > 10 (W = [H2O]/[surfactant]).

In 1947, D. Grahame33 proposed, within the framework of the electric double-layer model, that the permittivity (εr) in the region inside the inner Helmholtz plane—within a few ångströms of a metal electrode—is much smaller than that of bulk water (≈80) and is approximately 6. Under such extreme interfacial conditions, water molecules cannot maintain the hydrogen bond network characteristic of bulk water and instead behave in a kind of “isolated state.” This situation is considered to be directly related to the drastic decrease in permittivity as well as to the characteristic behavior of specific adsorption of ions. As a result, they tend to behave as isolated molecules. The permittivity of such isolated water has been assumed to be approximately 6, a value close to those of organic ethers such as diethyl ether (εr = 4.2) and tetrahydrofuran (THF, εr = 7.5).34 Not only at metal electrodes but at any interface, water molecules in contact with the interface are prevented from forming a fully developed hydrogen bond network.

Postorino et al.35 have reported that the hydrogen bond structure of water disappears under supercritical conditions. However, because aggregates of water molecules can form even in the gas phase,19 it is reasonable to assume that some degree of hydrogen bond structure remains in the condensed phase of supercritical water. Nevertheless, the hydrogen bond network in supercritical water is severely disrupted, and many characteristic properties of “water” may be largely lost. It has also been suggested that higher ion associates are formed from NaCl under supercritical conditions.36

It should be noted that when the extensive hydrogen-bonded network of water molecules—a self-assembled structure—cannot be formed, the characteristic properties of bulk water would disappear. Only when such a large molecular network is established can water acquire the properties characteristic of bulk water. In this context, in 1972 the condensed matter physicist Philip W. Anderson37 emphasized in his seminal essay More Is Different (Science, 1972) that even a precise understanding of individual elements does not allow one to deduce the essential properties that emerge when their number increases. Knowledge of the laws governing elementary particles alone cannot fully explain the properties of life or complex materials. In other words, when “many” components (atoms or molecules) assemble, new and often unpredictable properties emerge. If bulk water is indeed an emergent product derived from isolated water molecules, the required number of water molecules (nw) must be extremely large.

In 1984, Y. Marcus38 reported that the donor number (DN) of bulk water is 40.3 or 42.3. These values are much larger than that of H2O measured in 1,2-dichloroethane (DN = 18),20 yet they correspond more closely to the intuitive image that chemists generally have of water. Thus, water exhibits an apparent dual character depending on the extent of molecular association: (a) bulk water (DN ≈ 40), in which innumerable water molecules form a natural hydrogen bond network and cooperate as a collective state, and (b) isolated water molecules (DN = 18), which exist as individual entities when dissolved at low concentration in organic solvents or confined within nanoscale spaces where hydrogen bonding between water molecules is disrupted. In this sense, the characteristic properties of bulk water may be regarded as emergent phenomena arising from the assembly of a vast number of hydrogen-bond-isolated “individual” water molecules.

The donor number (DN) and acceptor number (AN) are indices that represent the electron pair donating ability (donor property) and electron pair accepting ability (acceptor property) of solvents, respectively. Although the acceptor number of isolated water molecules has not been explicitly reported, if it is tentatively assumed to be approximately AN ≈ 20, the value for bulk water is AN = 54.8.20 In this case as well, an emergent difference from the “individual” to the “collective” state can be recognized, analogous to that observed for the donor number. Unless otherwise noted, the permittivities of solvents cited hereafter are mainly taken from ref. 34, whereas donor and acceptor numbers (DN and AN) are taken from ref. 20.

We have already investigated and discussed changes in the properties of bulk water with increasing amounts of salts and water-miscible organic solvents using NMR (Fig. 1)18 and Raman spectroscopy.27,39–43 Furthermore, in higher permittivity solvents (20 < εr < 65) with low solvation ability, strong chemical interactions exceeding simple electrostatic forces between ions have been observed (see Section 2.2). The observed interactions include unusual coordination phenomena between alkali metals (M+) or alkaline earth metals (M2+) and various anions,18,44–47 as well as hydrogen bonding between partially substituted ammonium cations and simple anions such as Cl− and Br−.18,48–50 Partially substituted ammonium cations refer to RnNH4−n+ (n = 1–3) and do not include R4N+.

Under conditions approaching the “dihydrogen ether” state, where the properties of bulk water are strongly modified by mixing with concentrated salts and organic solvents, specific chemical interactions (Fig. 2) have been proposed between high concentrations of alkali metal ions (M+) or alkaline earth metal ions (M2+) and the leaving-group anions (X−) generated from SN1 solvolysis substrates (RX).25–27,43,51,52 These observations further suggest that, in highly concentrated aqueous solutions rather than in dilute solutions, strong chemical interactions may occur between H+, NH4+, partially substituted ammonium ions, or s-block metal cations and various anions (except for ClO4−, PF6−, BF4−, etc.). Salts or acids containing these interacting anions, although they function as strong electrolytes (or strong acids) under dilute aqueous conditions, may exhibit reduced effective ionic dissociation under conditions of high ionic strength (see Section 2.3).

Coordination and “reverse coordination” ability of s-block metal ions in weakly solvating media

In 1925, Sidgwick and Brewer53 reported the synthesis of salicylaldehyde and β-diketone complexes of alkali metals. This work represents the first report on the coordination chemistry of alkali metals. In acetonitrile (MeCN, εr = 35.9, DN = 14.1, AN = 18.9), we have found, by means of the mercury anodic wave in polarography, that unusual chemical species with a metal-to-ligand ion ratio of 2 : 1 are formed when a large excess of LiClO4 coexists with carboxylate ions. These species were identified as CH3COO−(Li+)2 and C6H5COO−(Li+)2.54 The overall formation constants (Kf) of these “reverse coordination” complexes were 1 × 1011 and ca. 1 × 109 M−2, respectively. In contrast, the overall formation constants of the normal coordination complexes with an ion ratio of 1 : 2, [M(C6H5COO)2]− (M = Li, Na, and K), were obtained from the negative shift of the cathodic wave of the alkali metal ions, giving values of 1016.7, 108.4, and 104.2 M−2, respectively. However, upon addition of water, the apparent stability constant of [Li(C6H5COO)2]− decreased markedly. For example, at H2O contents of 0.5% and 2.0%, the stability constants decreased to 108.1 and 106.0 M−2, respectively, and were extrapolated to ≈0 in 100% H2O. Here, 1 M = 1 mol dm−3.

In 1933, Fuoss and Kraus55–58 proposed the concept of “symmetrical” triple ions based on electrostatic interactions between (spherical) ions in low permittivity media. The minima observed in conductivity curves (Λ–c) in such media (εr < 10) were interpreted in terms of the formation of these ionic species.59,60 However, some researchers attempted to explain the appearance of conductivity minima by alternative mechanisms.61,62 Although this controversy may not yet be completely resolved, it is clear that conductivity theory in high ionic fields has been extensively developed.63 In 1975, R. Fuoss64 improved the Fuoss–Hsia equation and related theoretical treatments and pointed out that even when the salt concentration (maximum) is less than 2 × 10−4 mol dm−3, the theory may not be applicable to solvents with permittivities of about 10 or less. This limitation arises because the coulombic force between ions becomes sufficiently strong to allow overlap among three or more cospheres. Petrucci and Eyring65 later developed a theory based on multibody interactions and assigned activity coefficients smaller than unity to ion pairs.

Fig. 3 shows the molar conductivities (Λ/S cm2 mol−1) of various lithium salts in THF (εr = 7.58, DN = 20.0, AN = 8.0) at 25 °C.66 For LiClO4, LiPic (picrate), LiNO3, and 2,4-(NO2)2C10H5OLi (2,4-dinitro-1-naphtholate), the salt concentrations at which the conductivity minima appear (cmin) were 2.11 × 10−2, 1.14 × 10−2, 6.30 × 10−4, and 1.73 × 10−4 mol dm−3, respectively. Analysis of the conductivity data yielded formation constants for ion pairs (Li+X−) and “symmetrical” triple ions [(Li+)2X− and Li+(X−)2], and in some systems even for quadrupoles [(Li+X−)2]. Similar measurements carried out in other low permittivity solvents (εr < 10) besides THF—such as 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, εr = 7.2, DN = 23.9, AN = 10.2), chloroform (CHCl3, εr = 4.8 at 20 °C, DN ≈ 0, AN = 23.1), and ethyl acetate (εr = 6.0, DN = 17.1, AN = 9.3)—showed that almost all of the alkali metal salts or quaternary ammonium salts investigated exhibited minima in their conductivity curves.

However, LiBr in THF (entry 20) and LiNO3 and 2,4-(NO2)2C6H3OLi in DME (entries 11 and 14) were exceptional cases. The large positive deviations observed for these three salts were attributed to the formation of significant amounts of quadrupoles in the respective solvents, namely LiBr (K41 = 50), LiNO3 (K41 = 35), and 2,4-(NO2)2C6H3OLi (K41 = 70). For other salts with relatively small quadrupole formation constants, such as n-Bu4NCl and n-Bu4NBr in THF, or n-Bu4NClO4 in DME, only small positive deviations were observed. Fuoss and Accascina67 had already theoretically derived a linear relationship between the triple ion formation constant and the minimum concentration in the form K = (λ0/Λ0)cmin. Here, K is the dissociation constant of the triple ion (assumed to be formed by electrostatic interactions), i.e., the reciprocal of the formation constant, while λ0 and Λ0 denote the limiting molar conductivities of the triple ion and the single ion, respectively.

Interestingly, in THF, LiBr showed a conductivity minimum at cmin = 4.86 × 10−3 mol dm−3, whereas LiCl did not exhibit any minimum over a wide concentration range (Δc = (0.4–18) × 10−3 mol dm−3). It was found that LiCl gave an exceptionally large quadrupole formation constant. Calculations have shown that even in systems where triple ion formation is sufficiently large to produce a conductivity minimum, the minimum disappears when the quadrupole formation constant exceeds a critical value.68 Studies based on vapor-pressure osmometry69 and NMR spectroscopy70 have demonstrated that LiCl associates strongly in THF.

Fig. 5 shows the conductivity curve (Λ–c1/2) of a partially substituted ammonium salt, diethylcyclohexylammonium chloride, in benzonitrile (PhCN, εr = 25.2, DN = 11.9, AN = 15.5), a solvent of relatively high permittivity but weak solvating ability, where a clear minimum appears.68 PhCN, like acetonitrile (MeCN), is classified as an aprotic and weakly protophilic (or protophobic) solvent, possessing only weak hydrogen bond donating and accepting abilities. From the values of the molar conductivities, the formation constants of the ion pair between R2R′NH+ and Cl−, the “symmetrical” triple ions, and the quadrupoles were estimated. Calculations show that when the ion pair and “symmetrical” triple ion formation constants are kept fixed while only the quadrupole formation constant is increased, the minimum in the conductivity curve disappears once the quadrupole formation constant exceeds a critical value. Similar conductivity minima have also been observed for other partially substituted ammonium salts, such as n-Bu3HNCl, in benzonitrile and in nitrobenzene (PhNO2, εr = 34.8, DN = 4.4, AN = 14.8).50 These results indicate that higher ion association in such aprotic solvents arises not only from the electrostatic interaction between –NH+ and Cl− but also from hydrogen bond interactions. Marcus and Hefter71 reviewed ion-pairing phenomena from the traditional viewpoint, critically commenting on ion association involving hydrogen bonding and electrostatic forces.

On the basis of these experimental and theoretical studies, the conditions required for the appearance of conductivity minima can be summarized as follows. For ionic association of a given salt, if triple ions are formed in addition to ion pairs, the conductivity in the higher concentration region becomes higher than the value predicted (calculated) by considering only ion pair (1 : 1) formation. When triple ion formation becomes sufficiently extensive, a minimum appears in the conductivity curve. In systems where ionic association is even stronger, quadrupole formation occurs in addition to triple ion formation. In such cases, the experimentally observed molar conductivity can become lower than the value calculated by considering only ion pair formation.72,73 When the quadrupole formation constant becomes extremely large, the minimum disappears even though triple ions are still formed. Another condition for the appearance of a minimum is that triple ion formation must be “symmetrical.” Even when triple ion formation is sufficiently large to produce a minimum, the minimum will not appear unless the system generates both triple cations, (M+)2X−, and triple anions, M+(X−)2. However, if the formation constants are sufficiently large, the formation constants of the triple cation and triple anion do not necessarily have to be exactly identical.

A minimum appears in the conductivity curve at around cs = 1.6 × 10−3 mol dm−3. Analysis of the molar conductivity data yielded formation constants for the ion pair, the “symmetrical” triple ions, and the quadrupole. In contrast, structurally related compounds, Li+PMTFP− (lithium 1-phenyl-3-methyl-4-trifluoroacetyl-5-pyrazolonate) and Li+PBI− (lithium 3-phenyl-4-benzoyl-5-isoxazolonate), did not exhibit a minimum, and their conductivity curves decreased monotonically. The possibility of observing reverse coordination in amphoteric alcohol solvents with stronger solvation ability was examined. As described above, in aprotic dipolar solvents with relatively low donor and acceptor abilities, such as MeCN (DN = 14.1, AN = 19.3) and acetone (DN = 17.0, AN = 12.5), chemical interactions operate between alkali metal cations (particularly Li+) and simple monovalent anions (except ClO4−, BF4−, etc.). Consequently, not only coordination-type complexes (LiL2−) and ion pairs (LiL), but also “reverse coordination” complexes (Li2L+) can be formed.45,77,78 However, such specific interactions between Li+ and monovalent anions have not been observed in relatively strong donor solvents such as MeOH (DN = 31.3,38 AN = 41.3), DMF (DN = 26.6, AN = 16.0), and DMSO (DN = 29.8, AN = 19.3). Furthermore, the chemical interaction between metal cations and anions is markedly suppressed when protic solvents such as H2O or MeOH are added to MeCN.46,54,78

Origin of the strong oxidizing power of aqua regia that dissolves pure gold

Discovery of the strong oxidizing ability of dilute nitric acid and its mechanism

Nitric acid (HNO3) behaves as a strong acid in dilute solutions, whereas concentrated nitric acid or 100% nitric acid acts as a powerful oxidizing agent. Owing to these two distinct functions, nitric acid is one of the best-known and most widely used oxidizing agents in chemistry. Although the oxidizing ability of nitric acid depends on its concentration in solution, it is well known that dilute nitric acid (<ca. 2 mol dm−3) has little oxidizing power.83 However, we have clarified the conditions under which dilute nitric acid can exhibit strong oxidizing ability,39 and, by applying this principle, we ultimately succeeded in dissolving noble metals such as pure gold in a mixed solution of dilute nitric acid and seawater.40 The discovery of the strong oxidizing ability of dilute nitric acid and the elucidation of its mechanism proceeded as follows.

Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) was used as a surfactant to construct a chloroform reversed micellar system, [CHCl3/CTAB/H2O (1.0 vol%)], and the oxidizing ability of dilute nitric acid within the micro-water droplets was examined at W = 2.0 ([H2O]/[CTAB]).31 The initially colorless reversed micelle solutions containing lower concentrations of nitric acid (1.0, 2.0, and 3.0 mol dm−3) gradually turned yellow over a period ranging from several tens of minutes to several hours at 25 °C (Fig. 12). This color change was attributed to the oxidation of the counter anion Br− of the surfactant CTAB to Br2 (or Br3−). The addition of LiClO4, LiBr, or Mg(ClO4)2 accelerated the reaction rate, and eventually nitric acid at concentrations of 0.25–2.5 mol dm−3 (in 1.0 vol% H2O) produced approximately half an equivalent amount of Br2 molecules. Based on the amount of product formed, the generation of Br2 (or Br3−) is presumed to follow the reaction shown in eqn (1):12HNO3 + 2Br− → Br2 + NO2− + NO3− + H2O

The oxidizing ability of dilute nitric acid was considered to arise from NO2+ (nitronium ion) generated within the reverse micelles, and therefore chemically synthesized NO2+ was examined. However, because NO2BF4 has low solubility in CHCl3, which is the solvent of the reversed micellar system, the oxidizing ability of NO2+ was instead investigated in MeCN as a solvent.

As shown in Fig. 13, the formation of Br2 (or Br3−) in a 0.1 mol dm−3 Et4NBr–MeCN solution increased in proportion to the concentration of the commercially available reagent NO2BF4 added, and the amount formed was nearly quantitative. For example, when the NO2BF4 concentrations were 2.0, 5.0, and 10 × 10−3 mol dm−3, the detected concentrations of Br2 were 1.6, 4.3, and 9.1 × 10−3 mol dm−3, respectively (NO2+ + 2Br− → Br2 + NO2−). From the reaction behavior of the chemical species NO2+ and Br− in MeCN, the NO2+ species was therefore proposed to be the reaction intermediate in the oxidation process of bromide by dilute nitric acid within the reverse micelles.

Incidentally, it is widely accepted that NO2+ is the active species in nitration reactions. Olah et al.84 stated that, in electrophilic aromatic nitration reactions, the active electrophile is the nitronium ion (NO2+), which is generated from nitric acid in the presence of strong acids. Thus, when aromatic nitration occurs, it indicates that NO2+ participates in the reaction system. To examine this possibility, we examined whether the NO2+ species is involved in the oxidation process of dilute nitric acid in the reversed micellar system. Nitration reactions of phenols were therefore attempted using dilute nitric acid (2.0 mol dm−3) in various reversed micellar systems. The quantitative conversion of phenols (e.g., 4-methylphenol) to nitrophenols was confirmed by UV and 1H NMR spectroscopy.39 With respect to the redox potentials of the highly oxidizing species NO2+ and NO+, Olah et al.84 reported values of 1.45 and 1.51 V, respectively; however, according to the original source,85 the correct values should be 1.51 and 1.45 V (vs. NHE), respectively.41

A further important issue is whether the NO2+ species is generated in the organic phase or in the microaqueous phase of the reversed micellar system. In contrast to the reversed micellar system, the oxidizing ability of dilute nitric acid was therefore examined in bulk aqueous solution containing no organic phase. At 35 °C, when 4.0 mol dm−3 LiCl (and 0.010 mol dm−3 LiBr) was added to a 2.0 mol dm−3 aqueous nitric acid solution and monitored by UV-visible absorption spectroscopy, the solution immediately changed from colorless to yellow.39 The absorption band around 371 nm gradually increased for up to 30 min, indicating that Br− had been oxidized to Br2. Thus, dilute nitric acid was found to exhibit oxidizing ability in bulk aqueous solutions containing high concentrations of salts, indicating that the reactive species was generated in the aqueous phase (concentrated saline solution). The bromine-formation profiles (Fig. 14) in dilute nitric acid aqueous solutions containing salts other than chloride (CF3SO3−, ClO4−, and NO3−) differed from those observed in chloride solutions in that they exhibited an induction period; nevertheless, Br− was similarly oxidized to Br2.

Subsequently, it was demonstrated that dilute nitric acid can oxidize not only Br− but even Cl− in bulk water containing highly concentrated salts. At 20–35 °C, in 2.0 mol dm−3 HNO3 containing 1.0 mol dm−3 AlCl3 (and 0.010 mol dm−3 LiBr), the oxidation of Br− proceeded rapidly. However, the yield of Br2 was not very high, and it was noticed that Cl2 gas was simultaneously evolved from the solution. The evolution of Cl2 was therefore examined using the o-tolidine method.86 Chlorine was detected in test solutions collected both from the gas phase and from the condensed phase (HNO3 solution). By the same procedure, the formation of Cl2 was confirmed when 4.0, 1.7, and 2.5 mol dm−3 LiCl, MgCl2, and CaCl2, respectively, were added to 2.0 mol dm−3 HNO3.

Thus, dilute nitric acid (≤2 mol dm−3) in bulk water containing highly concentrated salts was found to oxidize not only Br− but also Cl− to Cl2. These results strongly suggest that the active species for nitration or oxidation, NO2+, can be generated not only in the aqueous phase of reversed micellar systems but also in bulk water containing high concentrations of salts. Eqn (2) represents the generally accepted reaction for the formation of NO2+ in concentrated or 100% nitric acid; however, the same reaction should also be applicable to dilute nitric acid in reversed micelle microdroplets31,39 as well as in bulk water containing highly concentrated salts.39–4122HNO3 ⇆ NO2+ + NO3− + H2O

It follows that the promotion of molecular HNO3 (undissociated nitric acid) is essential for favorable formation of NO2+. In bulk aqueous solutions, however, diluted nitric acid is completely dissociated. How, then, can the ionization of nitric acid molecules into H+ and NO3− be suppressed? Reducing the permittivity of the medium (e.g., εr < 10) might be an effective approach. Nevertheless, it is not easy to reduce the permittivity of bulk aqueous solutions. In this context, the concept of transforming H2O into “dihydrogen ether” (see Section 2.1) provides a key to solving this problem. When water molecules within the large hydrogen-bonded network are separated into isolated molecules by the addition of large amounts of salts, the emergent “strong” acidity and basicity of bulk water are immediately lost, and nitric acid is transformed from a “strong” electrolyte into a “weak” electrolyte. A mechanism similar to that proposed for HNO3 can be applied to the enhanced oxidation ability of dilute chloric acid (HClO3) at higher ionic strength.87

The strong oxidizing ability of dilute nitric acid containing highly concentrated salts was thus discovered for the first time, and its mechanism was proposed. When chloride ions coexist in such systems, Cl2 is generated, which readily establishes the Cl2–Cl− leaching system for gold. This Cl2–Cl− system had been used for gold refining until the late nineteenth century but was replaced by the cyanide process around 1887.17 As an example, the dissolution rate constants of pure gold at 40 °C in dilute nitric acid containing highly concentrated salts are given as log(k/s−1).40 In solutions of 2 mol dm−3 HNO3 containing 2 mol dm−3 LiCl, NaCl, MgCl2, and CaCl2, the values of log(k/s−1) were −4.39, −4.62, −3.82, and −4.09, respectively. With the gold wire (99.95%, 0.25 mm diameter), the total dissolution periods as well as kinetics (monitoring the changes in the gold mass and the absorbance at 375 nm of AuCl4−) were measured in the presence of various salts. Furthermore, five pure gold wires (totally 0.10 g) were completely dissolved in 100 mL of a 1 : 1 mixed solution of seawater and 2.0 mol dm−3 dilute nitric acid. Complete dissolution occurred within 17 h at approximately 100 °C, with a dissolution rate constant of log(k/s−1) = −4.52. In addition to chloride salts, the enhancement of the oxidizing ability of dilute nitric acid aqueous solutions (0.10–2.0 mol dm−3) containing bromide and iodide salts was also examined on the basis of the dissolution rate of pure gold at 30–60 °C.41 The ability to dissolve Au in dilute nitric acid solutions was found to be more effective with bromide salts (Br2–Br− system) than with chloride salts. The stability constants for complex formation between Au3+ and halide ions (Au3+ + 4X− ⇆ AuX4−) are β4 = 1026, 1032, and 5 × 1047 for X = Cl−, Br−, and I−, respectively.17

Origin of the strong oxidizing power of aqua regia

When concentrated nitric acid is mixed with concentrated hydrochloric acid, the following reaction occurs, producing nitrosyl chloride (NOCl) and chlorine (Cl2), both of which possess strong oxidizing ability.883HNO3 + 3HCl → NOCl + Cl2 + 2H2O

Nitrosyl chloride and “nascent chlorine” generated by this reaction have been considered to play important roles in the attack on noble metals. Chloride ions originating from hydrochloric acid form complexes with Au+ and Au3+, thereby markedly lowering the oxidation potential of Au. In order for Cl2 to be generated from Cl−, a redox potential greater than E° = 1.36 V for the Cl2/Cl− couple is required.89 Calculating the activity (concentration) conditions of each chemical species necessary to achieve this potential gives (a(NO3−)·a(H+)4)/PNO ≈ 1020.3. Assuming a(NO3−) = 1 and PNO = 1 atm, a(H+) ≈ 105 is required. Such a hydrogen ion activity (or concentration), approximately 105, is clearly impossible to realize. In a solution prepared by mixing concentrated nitric acid (ca. 16 mol dm−3) and concentrated hydrochloric acid (12 mol dm−3) in a 1 : 3 ratio, the approximate concentrations of the components are [H+] = 13 mol dm−3, [NO3−] = 4 mol dm−3, and [Cl−] = 8 mol dm−3.

Thus, the evolution of Cl2 from aqua regia cannot be rationalized solely in terms of the redox potential associated with nitrate ions. Aqua regia should therefore be considered from a broader physicochemical perspective. This medium is essentially a highly concentrated mixture of nitric acid and chloride ions, in which the ionic strength becomes extremely high and the availability of bulk-like water is significantly reduced. Under such conditions, the solvation environment differs markedly from that of ordinary aqueous solutions. In highly concentrated nitric acid, particularly in the presence of concentrated hydrochloric acid, a substantial fraction of nitric acid remains undissociated. These considerations indicate that nitrate ions alone cannot account for chlorine evolution under the composition of aqua regia. Instead, highly oxidizing intermediates generated in the concentrated nitric acid/chloride medium should be considered. This environment is expected to facilitate the formation of highly reactive electrophilic species such as NO2+. It is therefore reasonable to consider that the evolution of chlorine from aqua regia involves oxidizing intermediates derived from nitric acid, including NO2+, which possesses a sufficiently high oxidation potential to convert Cl− into Cl2. In addition, the strong oxidizing character of nitrosyl chloride (NOCl) can be attributed to the presence of the nitrosonium ion NO+. Since the reduction potentials of NO2+ and NO+ are +1.51 V and +1.45 V (vs. NHE),41,84 both species are sufficiently strong oxidants to oxidize chloride ions to molecular chlorine.

This interpretation is also consistent with recent studies on highly concentrated aqueous electrolytes, such as those reported by Suo et al.,90 where the activity and reactivity of water are markedly modified when the number of available water molecules becomes limited. These observations support the view that the chemical behavior of water depends strongly on its collective state and solvation environment, as well as on the intrinsic (latent) properties of isolated H2O molecules. In this sense, the chemistry of aqua regia may also be regarded as an example in which the effective chemical role of water is significantly altered under conditions of extremely high ionic concentration.

Conclusions

In this study, the essential properties of water were examined from the perspective of two limiting states, namely the “isolated water molecule” and “bulk water”, and the changes in properties between these states were reinterpreted in terms of emergence. An isolated H2O molecule, lacking a hydrogen-bonded network with other water molecules, exhibits only weak acidity and basicity as a solvent, and its chemical character resembles that of an ether, showing weak donor and acceptor abilities. In contrast, when an enormous number of water molecules become self-assembled through intermolecular hydrogen bonding, water acquires the unique ability to undergo reversible O–H bond cleavage and behaves as a highly polar solvent with strong solvating power. Thus, the distinctive solvent properties of water cannot be derived directly from the properties of a single molecule but should instead be understood as a collective emergent phenomenon arising from the cooperative interaction of many molecules.

This viewpoint also provides important insight into ion–ion interactions in weakly solvating media. In aprotic solvents such as MeCN, solvation is sufficiently weak that specific chemical interactions beyond simple electrostatic interaction can arise between s-block metal ions and anions. As a consequence, not only conventional coordination complexes but also “reverse coordination” complexes centered on anions, can be formed. Electrochemical measurements and spectroscopic observations provide evidence for the formation of such species. These results suggest that phenomena previously treated simply as “ion association” may in fact involve clear coordination–chemical interactions. Recently, the author of this manuscript summarized his work in a book,91 which involves fundamental coordination ability of s-block metal ions.

Furthermore, this study focused on the destruction of the water structure in the presence of highly concentrated salts, which markedly reduces the solvating power of water. Under such conditions, nitric acid does not undergo complete dissociation and can exist as molecular HNO3. As a consequence, even dilute nitric acid, which normally exhibits little oxidizing ability, can function as a strong oxidizing agent in environments of high ionic strength. This phenomenon can be understood by considering that the disruption of the hydrogen bond network of water alters the solvent properties toward a “dihydrogen ether-like” state. Pure gold was successfully dissolved in dilute nitric acid (<2 mol dm−3) containing abundant halide salts.

The present work highlights that the chemical behavior of water is not a fixed molecular property but an emergent phenomenon governed by the collective organization of water molecules. The two-state model provides a structural description of bulk water, whereas the present emergence-based picture addresses the origin of the collective solvent properties themselves. Recently, a closely related study has proposed emergence as a conceptual framework for relating molecular properties to bulk behavior.92
