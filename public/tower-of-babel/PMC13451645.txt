Preserving Valued Activities in Life for Adults With Rheumatoid Arthritis: Development and Pre‐Testing Refinement of the PREVAIL Model of Care
Authors: Louise M. Thoma, Joel R. Thompson, Jason Sharpe, Beth Jonas, Patti Katz, Kelli Allen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveRehabilitation is rarely integrated into routine rheumatology care, despite high rates of functional limitation in adults with rheumatoid arthritis (RA). The Preserving Valued Activities in Life (PREVAIL) model of care proposes a function‐focused screening survey administered during routine rheumatology visits to guide referrals to rehabilitation and facilitate integration into standard RA care. This study aimed to assess the perceived feasibility and acceptability of the PREVAIL model and inform modifications for pilot testing.MethodsWe used a multimethod approach including (1) a cross‐sectional survey of 309 adults with RA to assess the feasibility and validity of a proposed screening tool to identify functional limitations for referral and (2) semistructured interviews with 24 adults with RA, 6 rheumatology clinicians, and 6 physical therapists to evaluate the perceived acceptability and feasibility of the PREVAIL model of care.ResultsThe proposed screening tool demonstrated moderate correlations with established measures of functional status and disease activity but identified 90% of participants for rehabilitation referral based on proposed disability risk criteria, necessitating refinement. Interview data revealed broad support for the PREVAIL model across key vested parties. Participants emphasized the value of proactively addressing function, identified patient and clinician knowledge gaps regarding rehabilitation, and highlighted the model's potential to enhance patient‐clinician communication. Modifications to improve feasibility included remote survey completion, educational materials for key vested parties, and an interim physical therapy consultation call to better prioritize referrals.ConclusionThe PREVAIL model of care was perceived to be both feasible and acceptable. Study findings directly inform refinements to support real‐world implementation in a pilot clinical trial.

INTRODUCTION

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, inflammatory autoimmune disease marked by joint inflammation, joint erosions, and functional limitations. 1 Control of disease activity can often be achieved with consistent medical management with disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs and treat‐to‐target regimens. 2 However, functional limitations remain common, even when disease activity is well‐controlled. 3 , 4 Exercise and rehabilitation (ie, physical therapy [PT] and occupational therapy [OT]) interventions are specifically indicated to improve functional status in individuals with RA. 5 , 6 , 7 Yet, exercise participation 8 , 9 and rehabilitation utilization 10 , 11 rates are low among adults with RA. Adults with RA may not consistently learn about exercise and rehabilitation from their rheumatologist. 12 Although adults with RA desire exercise recommendations from a trusted expert who understands RA, 13 they also lack confidence that rheumatology clinicians are knowledgeable about exercise guidance. 14 , 15 Existing treatment guidelines provide broad support for exercise and rehabilitation, but the lack of prescriptive guidance may leave rheumatologists uncertain on how to provide exercise recommendations or when to refer to rehabilitation. 12 , 16 Although measures of functional status are recommended in routine care, 17 there is currently no guidance on how to use functional status measures to determine need for rehabilitation in RA. Strategies to systematically integrate exercise recommendations and rehabilitation referrals into routine rheumatology care are needed to address functional limitations and maintain quality of life for adults with RA.

We developed a novel model of care to systematically integrate exercise and rehabilitation into routine RA care to help adults with RA preserve function and prevent disability (Preserving Valued Activities in Life [PREVAIL]). The PREVAIL model of care was designed to support rheumatology clinicians’ decision‐making by identifying functional decline or risk thereof, providing a structured framework to guide rehabilitation referral and equipping clinicians with RA‐specific exercise resources. PREVAIL was developed with the premise that early identification of functional decline is critical for timely intervention. 18 In previous studies, older adults and adults with RA who reported accommodation use (eg, extra time, assistance from another person or devices) without disability were more likely to develop a disability in the next two to five years. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 The progression of functional limitation to permanent disability from RA is gradual. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 Timely rehabilitation may preserve function and interrupt progression from mild functional limitation and accommodation use to disability. 5 , 6 , 7 Identifying these early signs of decline (eg, accommodation use) at the point of care presents an opportunity to guide rehabilitation referrals.

The PREVAIL model of care aims to expand the current paradigm of RA management by systematically integrating nonpharmacologic strategies into routine care. The initial model (Figure 1A) proposed using a brief patient‐reported outcome measures as a screening tool administered during routine rheumatology visits to determine disability risk, guide rehabilitation referral, and prompt the delivery of exercise guidance. For individuals identified as at risk or currently experiencing disability, the proposed PREVAIL model of care recommends a rehabilitation referral to maintain or restore function. For all patients, the model prompts rheumatology clinicians to provide exercise guidance using RA‐specific resources to support safe and effective exercise participation (Figure 1A). The guidance provided by this model will shift focus from disease activity alone to also include functional health and longer‐term quality of life. The purpose of this study was to determine the perceived feasibility and acceptability of the proposed PREVAIL model of care and to inform refinements to PREVAIL before pilot testing in a clinical trial.

METHODS

We used a multimethod approach to evaluate the proposed PREVAIL model of care. First, we conducted a survey to test the feasibility and validity of the proposed disability screening tool to categorize disability level among adults with RA. Then we conducted an interview study to assess the perceived feasibility and accessibility of the PREVAIL model of care concept and components from the perspectives of patients, rheumatology clinicians, and physical therapists (PTs). Using the results of the survey and interview studies, we iteratively sought feedback from key vested parties and modified the PREVAIL model of care and pilot trial design.

Survey study

Study design and sample

We conducted a cross‐sectional survey study from July 2022 to April 2023. The study was approved by the university institutional review board (IRB) (IRB 21‐2200). We recruited adults with RA who were receiving care from an academic rheumatology clinic. Potential participants were identified from the medical record if they had at least one visit to the rheumatology clinic with an International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) code for RA (M05.x, M06.x) in 2020 and/or 2021 and at least one additional visit with an ICD‐10 code for RA since 2019. Participants were eligible if they received rheumatology care for RA at the rheumatology clinic in 2020 or 2021 and were able to complete an electronic or verbal survey in English. Individuals were contacted through electronic medical record chart message, email, mail, and/or phone, as available.

Procedure

After providing informed consent electronically, participants completed an online survey that included sociodemographic characteristics, clinical history, and patient‐reported outcome measures, including the Short Valued Life Activities Questionnaire (S‐VLA), Routine Assessment of Patient Index Data 3 (RAPID3), and Patient‐Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Short Form for Physical Function (PROMIS PF 10a).

To facilitate identification of individuals with and at risk for disability, we sought a screening tool that could detect functional limitation and accommodation use. Although the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) recommends several functional status assessments (eg, MD‐HAQ, PROMIS‐PF10a) for routine care, 17 these tools do not assess accommodation use. We proposed categorizing using items from the S‐VLA for the screening tool. The S‐VLA is a 14‐item patient‐reported outcome measure that captures activity difficulty and accommodation use across common valued life activities. The S‐VLA was chosen because it has been used to evaluate subclinical disability in adults with RA (eg, activity completion requiring accommodation use but no reported difficulty). 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 28 Using the responses to the S‐VLA items, we categorized participants into disability levels (no disability, at risk for disability, current disability; detailed in Supplement A). 18 , 19 In general, no disability reflected those reporting no difficulty across items, at risk for disability reflected those reporting accommodation use in at least one activity but no difficulty across activities, and current disability reflected those reporting at least some difficulty on a daily activity or a lot of difficulty in a discretionary activity. Items that the participants reported as not personally relevant were not used.

To assess construct validity of the disability categories derived from the S‐VLA, we examined the association of the categories with established patient‐reported measures of disease activity and physical function. We selected the RAPID3 because it is endorsed as a disease activity measure by the ACR. 29 , 30 RAPID3 scores were categorized as remission, low severity, moderate disease severity, and high severity based on established cutoffs. 29 , 31 We selected the PROMIS PF‐10a because it is endorsed by the ACR as a functional status measure in RA. 17 , 32 , 33 T‐scores were calculated and functional status was categorized as normal (T‐score >45), mild limitation (40< T‐score ≤45), moderate limitation (30< T‐score ≤40), and severe limitation (T‐score ≤30). 33 After data collection, we discovered an inadvertent error in the PROMIS PF 10a response options. Evaluation of the error concluded that the impact on this analysis was minimal (see Supplement B).

Statistical analyses

We used descriptive statistics to describe the distribution of disability categories. To assess construct validity, we calculated the correlation of the S‐VLA disability categories with the categories of physical function (PROMIS categories) and disease activity (RAPID3 categories) using Spearman rank test. Correlations were interpreted as moderate if 0.40 ≤ ρ < 0.60 and strong if ρ ≥ 0.60.

Interview study

Study design and sample

The study was approved by the university IRB (IRB #22‐3194). This study used a thematic analysis qualitative design, and reporting was guided by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ). 34 Adults with RA were eligible if they indicated interest during the survey study. Using the survey data, purposive sampling was used to invite adults with RA with characteristics to maximize variation in disability category, physical function, age, gender, disease duration, and experience with rehabilitation. Rheumatology clinicians and PTs were recruited through a snowball approach, starting with the professional networks of the authors. A recruitment survey was used to gather information from interested clinicians and included questions about degree, years of clinical experience, type of experience (eg, practice type, patient panel), and typical practice regarding rehabilitation for RA (eg, percentage of patients referred to rehabilitation for rheumatology clinicians; number of patients with RA seen per year for PTs). Recruitment continued until discussion of acceptability and feasibility of PREVAIL reached information redundancy.

Procedure

Semistructured interviews were conducted between October 2023 and May 2024 by two research team members with previous interview experience. Interview guides were developed for each group (Supplement C) following a similar structure, using a pragmatic, objective‐driven approach consistent with rapid qualitative methods. 35 Guides were structured around key domains derived from the study aims, including experiences with exercise and rehabilitation in routine rheumatology care, perceived needs, and perspectives on the acceptability and feasibility of PREVAIL. Participants were also invited to share feedback and suggestions.

Interviews were conducted via phone or video call per participant preference. The audio was recorded with participant consent. Interviews with adults with RA typically took 60 to 90 minutes; interviews with clinicians were about 30 to 45 minutes. Participants were compensated for their time. Interviews were transcribed with an automated transcription software (NVIVO Transcription, Lumivero) then de‐identified, cleaned, and edited for accuracy by two reviewers. No participant follow‐up was performed.

Analysis

We used a rapid qualitative analysis approach to evaluate participant perspectives on the feasibility and acceptability of the PREVAIL model of care. This approach used structured extraction templates with predefined domains informed by the study objectives. 35 Separate templates were developed for each interview type. Analysis was conducted by three members (LMT, JS, JRT) of the research team. Consensus analysis was conducted on interviews of each template until consistency of domain identification was established across all team members. Once consistency was established, the remaining transcripts were divided among team members for analysis and domain identification. Weekly meetings were conducted for reflexive domain analysis. Domain extractions were summarized in a matrix to facilitate analysis across participants for each domain. Thematic analysis was used to summarize across‐domain trends regarding acceptability and feasibility of the PREVAIL model of care.

Feedback and refinement

Results of the survey and interview studies were discussed at weekly team meetings to generate potential modifications to optimize the feasibility and acceptability of the PREVAIL model before pilot testing. We presented the results and potential modifications to key vested parties (rheumatology clinicians; patients via the community advisory board; practicing PTs) to receive further feedback. The process was iterative throughout the data collection and analysis period. We proposed the final model and trial design to the participating clinicians for feedback then operationalized the implementation plan with clinical leaders.

Interview study

The sample included 24 adults with RA, 6 rheumatology clinicians, and 6 PTs with broad experiences (Supplemental Table 2). Patients, rheumatology clinicians, and PTs generally perceived the PREVAIL model of care to be a feasible and acceptable approach to systematically integrating exercise guidance and rehabilitation into routine care for RA. Four major themes emerged regarding feasibility and acceptability: (1) The PREVAIL model of care is acceptable and adds value; (2) knowledge gaps may hinder the PREVAIL model of care implementation; (3) the PREVAIL model of care may facilitate communication; and (4) modification will improve feasibility of the PREVAIL model of care. Each theme is further described subsequently. Insights from these themes led to modifications to optimize pilot testing for the PREVAIL model of care, directions for post‐trial study, and future considerations to enhance rheumatology care.

Theme 1: The PREVAIL Model of Care is acceptable and adds value to patients and clinicians

Patients and clinicians were primarily positive and excited about the PREVAIL model of care (Table 2). Patients desired more treatment options to complement pharmacological management and expressed strong interest in preventive or proactive rehabilitation options. Further, patients appreciated that the recommendations were delivered by their rheumatologists because they trusted and often relied on their guidance to manage their RA. Patients and rheumatology clinicians perceived the model to prompt conversations about patients’ functional levels, daily activities, and exercise participation. Patients described difficulty initiating exercise programs and cited the need for accessible and trustworthy resources on exercise and rehabilitation tailored to RA, which they were receptive to receiving from their rheumatologist and that the model could provide.

Theme 2: Knowledge gaps will be a barrier to PREVAIL Model of Care implementation

Patients and rheumatologists described limited knowledge about rehabilitation for RA, whereas PTs reported minimal exposure to rheumatology during training (Table 3). Some patients were unaware that rehabilitation was an option for RA management or how it could support daily function. PTs noted this uncertainty in clinical care that their patients with RA had difficulty establishing goals and objectives of PT care. Most previous rehabilitation experiences were limited to post‐operative care. Rheumatologists described a narrow perspective of rehabilitation, often prioritizing pharmacological management and reserving rehabilitation until severe joint damage or functional limitation were present. Rheumatologists described an understanding of reactive rehabilitation use, whereas patients regularly verbalized a desire for proactive preventive use of rehabilitation, indicating a critical practice gap and incongruence in current rheumatology practice between patient wishes and clinical care. Despite these gaps, patients, rheumatologists, and PTs expressed interest in further education and requested tailored materials to improve understanding of rehabilitation options. PTs in particular noted minimal formal training in PT education regarding RA and would feel more comfortable with additional PT specific resources to support RA care.

Theme 3: The PREVAIL Model of Care may facilitate communication

Patients, rheumatologists, and PTs all indicated that improved communication is necessary and should be considered for successful implementation of the PREVAIL model of care (Table 4). Patients identified communication gaps with their rheumatologists, including limited conversations regarding functional activities and daily challenges. Patients hoped that the PREVAIL model of care could bring these challenges to the forefront at routine visits, (specifically with the screening tool). Similarly, rheumatologists and PTs shared a need to better understand each other's treatment and work processes. Rheumatologists reported uncertainty regarding effective PT referrals (eg, therapy directions, diagnosis codes). PTs suggested that a helpful referral should include the functional concerns as an approach to communicate the intention of the referral.

Theme 4: Specific modifications will improve the feasibility of the PREVAIL model of care

The feasibility of the PREVAIL model of care can be improved by addressing potential logistical concerns. Patients and rheumatologists were not concerned with completing surveys to guide care. However, some patients and clinicians had concerns related to timing. Rheumatologists described limited time in routine visits and patients described concerns that a survey could prolong their visit. Some patients described often feeling that they did not have time to fill out surveys: “My experience with surveys at the time of visits are that I'm not given sufficient time to complete them” (Jennifer, patient). Both patients and clinicians described the importance of brevity for surveys. Remote completion of surveys before appointments was suggested as a strategy to improve feasibility: “We have them do the survey before they come to the visit; I don't have a whole lot of time as part of the visit” (Anna, rheumatologist).

Modifications to the PREVAIL Model of Care for pilot testing

The results of the survey and interview studies and iterative feedback from the study team and key vested parties identified areas to optimize before pilot testing (Table 5). The modifications related to improving feasibility (eg, sending surveys a week before the visit), enhancing acceptability (eg, aligning name of the screening tool with identified priorities of the patients, providing more opportunities for education to patients, rheumatology clinicians, and PTs), and refining outcome collection. Some findings did not lead directly to modifications for the pilot trial but rather clarified additional learning and resource needs to refine the model of care as a future scalable solution. These included more education for all parties, assessment in the pilot trial regarding reservations, and if the PREVAIL model of care provided the advantages that were perceived (eg, bringing more attention to daily activities). A major change to the model of care related to improving the screening tool. As identified in the survey study, the screening tool was valid but futile in prioritizing needs for rehabilitation because approximately 90% of patients would have been identified for referral.

We made changes to the pilot clinical trial to enable us to gather more information and further refine the screening tool to prioritize PT referrals for judicious use of health services. The primary modification was the addition of a structured PT consultation call for participants with current or at risk of disability as the primary method for assessing priority for rehabilitation. These consultation calls will allow for a more comprehensive assessment of functional status and rehabilitation needs. Although consultation calls are not feasible for clinical implementation due to increased resource utilization, the expert clinical assessment may serve as a criterion standard to assess the performance of other candidate outcome measures and optimize screening.

DISCUSSION

The findings supported the perceived feasibility and acceptability of the PREVAIL model of care and also provided critical insights to improve the model of care and pilot trial design (Figure 1B). From the survey study, we learned that the proposed disability categories from the screening tool had good construct validity with established measures of functional limitation and disease activity in adults with RA, but changes are needed to improve the prioritization of patients for referral to rehabilitation because the current tool identified 90% of participants for PT referral. In the interview study, patients and clinicians broadly endorsed the value and acceptability of the PREVAIL model of care but raised important considerations regarding knowledge gaps, process logistics, and communication needs. From the feedback sessions, we confirmed that our planned changes were addressing the feasibility challenges identified in studies and identified additional modifications to enhance patient acceptability in preparation for a pilot trial.

Although current guidelines for RA care recommend multidisciplinary, nonpharmacological care to address functional needs, 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 such approaches remain uncommon in the United States and may contribute to underutilization of rehabilitation. 10 , 11 , 12 The PREVAIL model of care offers a conceptual approach to systematically facilitate multidisciplinary care by using patient‐reported outcome measures to prompt referrals to other disciplines. Although PREVAIL focuses on rehabilitation and exercise guidance to improve function, this approach can be applied to other disciplines, including psychology, pain management, and social work. A recent study reported pilot clinical implementation of PROMIS surveys paired with treatment‐optimizing recommendations for those with suboptimal scores, including the recommendation to change medication or refer to PT for patients with functional limitations. 40 Although preliminary, the results indicated that the PROMIS results did not increase multidisciplinary referral. The PREVAIL model of care uses a similar approach, and findings from the current study were used to iteratively refine and optimize the model based on patient and clinician perspectives, with the goal of improving likelihood of success in pilot testing.

We need to refine the screening tool to better prioritize rehabilitation resources for those who need it most. Previous research using the S‐VLA indicated performing daily activities with modifications (eg, use of accommodation) was a predictor of disability; however, the S‐VLA has not been used previously for clinical application. Although the S‐VLA questions were chosen to specifically identify those at risk for disability, the proposed criteria classified most adults with current or at risk for disability. A gold standard is ideally used to improve screening tools, but there is no gold standard to identify the need for PT. In the absence of a gold standard, a criterion standard will enable refinement of the screening tool. The 2021 Royal Dutch Society for Physical Therapy (KNGF) clinical practice guidelines for PT for RA provided clinical indications for PT. 39 To develop a criterion standard, we will add a PT consultation call to the PREVAIL model of care for the pilot trial (Figure 1B) to apply the guidance from the KNGF guidelines and determine a priority for PT. Using the criterion standard and other candidate patient‐reported outcome measures for the screening tool (eg, PROMIS), we can determine if a screening tool can effectively prioritize need for PT so that resource‐intensive consultation calls are not needed in the future.

Patients reported limited understanding of the role of rehabilitation for RA, which is a critical knowledge gap that could threaten PREVAIL model of care implementation. Limited previous exposure and a narrow perspective of the role of rehabilitation are likely to contribute to the gap. Lack of experience with PT was previously associated with lower perceived access and utilization of PT. 41 Additionally, patient satisfaction of PT care and outcomes were lower when patient expectations were misaligned with recovery timelines and treatment practices. 42 , 43 In this study, PTs shared that some patients with RA had difficulties setting meaningful personal goals due to limited understanding of PT care, further reflecting this challenge. To improve understanding of rehabilitation, we will add and refine opportunities to educate patients about rehabilitation and exercise in the PREVAIL model of care. We will develop a website with curated evidence‐based exercise education and resources tailored for adults with RA and include information about seeking help from a PT. The PT consultation call offers an opportunity to identify further education on how rehabilitation may help. These resources will provide patients with a richer understanding of the role exercise and rehabilitation can play in routine management and promote patient satisfaction and participation with rehabilitation.

Clinician knowledge gaps and poor interdisciplinary communication were also identified as critical barriers to rehabilitation utilization. Although multidisciplinary care teams are recommended for RA, 38 rheumatologists and PTs shared that their clinical experiences offered little opportunities for collaboration, communication, multidisciplinary care, and patient education about rehabilitation. Insufficient training and lack of knowledge about nonpharmacological interventions in rheumatologists have been associated with lower integration of rehabilitation and associated multidisciplinary care. 44 Our findings reflect these barriers because rheumatologists described a similar lack of training and exposure to rehabilitation. Taken together, these findings indicate the need to support clinician knowledge, preparation, and communication with education, training, and communication supports (eg, template language) to improve the PREVAIL model of care in the pilot trial.

Patients identified improved patient‐clinician communication as a potential advantage of the PREVAIL model of care. Effective communication between patients and clinicians is consistently associated with better clinical outcomes, greater treatment satisfaction, and enhanced patient confidence in decision‐making. 45 , 46 The interviews revealed a clear disconnect because patients wanted to learn about PT and other nonpharmacological interventions early in care whereas rheumatology clinicians described reserving rehabilitation for advanced disease or regional musculoskeletal concerns. Likewise, patients sought more attention on how they were managing with their daily life. Rheumatologist appointments were often described as focused on disease stabilization and medication efficacy, leaving limited opportunity for open discussion about function or rehabilitation. Patient‐reported outcome surveys can be effective tools in enhancing and facilitating communication between clinician and patient. 47 The PREVAIL model of care, and particularly the survey focused on daily tasks, may bring increased attention to functional needs at the routine visit.

Strengths of this study include the use of a multimethod approach to comprehensively inform the development and refinement of the PREVAIL model of care. The sample also included a diverse group of participants reflective of real‐world care, which allowed us to incorporate a range of perspectives across experiences. However, several limitations should be noted. The qualitative component of the study was focused on informing model refinement and therefore was not designed to reach thematic saturation across clinicians. Although this was consistent with our goal of refining the model rather than generating a comprehensive theory, it potentially limits the breadth of perspectives captured. In addition, all patient participants were recruited from a single academic center using diagnostic codes and not classification criteria. which limits the generalizability of findings to other care settings, geographic areas, and strictly defined RA cohorts. Finally, survey responses may be influenced by recall or response bias, and interviews may reflect socially desirable perspectives. All interviews and analyses were performed by licensed PTs, which may have provided additional bias into the analysis of the data. Despite these limitations, the findings offer critical insights into model feasibility and acceptability and lay a strong foundation for further testing in a pilot clinical trial.

The PREVAIL model of care was both feasible and acceptable to patients and clinicians. PREVAIL may help address long‐standing gaps in rehabilitation integration for adults with RA. Iterative feedback from this study directly informed refinements to the PREVAIL model of care before pilot testing.
