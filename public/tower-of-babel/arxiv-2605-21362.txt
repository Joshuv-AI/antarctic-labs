Lash : Adaptive Semantic Hybridization for Black-Box Jailbreaking of Large Language Models
Abdullah Al Nomaan Nafi, Fnu Suya, Swarup Bhunia, Prabuddha Chakraborty

Abstract
Jailbreak attacks expose a persistent gap between the intended safety behavior of aligned large language models and their behavior under adversarial prompting. Existing automated methods are increasingly effective but each commits to a single attack family (e.g., one refinement loop, one tree search, one mutation space, or one strategy library) and no single family dominates: the best-performing method shifts across target models and harm categories, suggesting complementary strengths that per-prompt composition could exploit. We introduce Lash ( L LM A daptive S emantic H ybridization), a black-box framework that treats outputs from multiple base attacks as reusable seed prompts and adaptively composes them for each target request. Given a seed pool, Lash searches over seed subsets and softmax-normalized mixture weights; a composition module synthesizes a single candidate prompt, and a derivative-free genetic optimizer updates the weights using black-box target feedback and a two-stage fitness function combining keyword-based refusal detection with LLM-judge scoring. On JailbreakBench, which contains 100 harmful prompts across 10 categories, we evaluate Lash on six common target models. Lash achieves an average attack success rate of 84.5% under keyword-based evaluation and 74.5% under two-stage evaluation, where responses are first filtered for refusals and then scored by an LLM judge for whether they substantively fulfill the original harmful request. Lash outperforms five state-of-the-art baselines on both metrics with only 30 mean target queries. Lash also remains competitive under three defense mechanisms and induces more success-like internal representations. These results suggest that adaptive composition across heterogeneous jailbreak strategies is a promising direction for black-box red-teaming.

Introduction

Aligned LLMs refuse most harmful requests. Modern alignment techniques such as reinforcement learning from human feedback
[
25
]
and constitutional AI
[
2
]
have made these models substantially more reluctant to produce dangerous instructions, disinformation, or other content their safety guidelines explicitly prohibit than their untuned predecessors. Yet this reluctance is not a hard guarantee: a carefully reworded version of the same request can flip a model’s refusal into compliance
[
34
]
.

Jailbreaking research has progressed from hand-crafted manual attacks
[
34
,
30
]

to automated white-box methods such as GCG
[
41
]
and
AutoDAN
[
20
]
, which use model-internal signals such as gradients,
log-probabilities, or losses. More recent black-box attacks, including
PAIR
[
4
]
, TAP
[
23
]
,
GPTFuzzer
[
37
]
, AutoDAN-Turbo
[
19
]
,
and FlipAttack
[
21
]
, avoid access to target-model internals and
instead use the model response for refinement, fuzzing, strategy selection,
or transformation-based attack generation. Yet within this setting, each attack confines its search to a single family (one refinement loop, one tree, one mutation pool, one strategy library, one transformation), and which family wins varies sharply: among the four black-box baselines we evaluate, the strongest method shifts across our six target models (Tab.
2
) and across ten harm categories (Tab.
4
). The outputs of different black-box methods typically carry distinct, partial signals, such as framing that suppresses refusal triggers, transformations that evade keyword filters, or structural templates that the target model responds to. Combining these signals can therefore outperform any single attack family. This motivates a black-box meta-attack that combines existing methods per request, exploiting the complementarity that no single family delivers alone.

Manual and white-box attacks:

Manual prompt-engineering attacks
[

,

,

Black-box jailbreak attacks:

Automated black-box attacks fall into four overlapping subfamilies: iterative-refinement loops driven by an attacker LLM (PAIR
[
4
]
, TAP
[
23
]
); mutation- and strategy-based methods that evolve handcrafted templates or retrieve from learned libraries (GPTFuzzer
[

[

, RLbreaker

; and learned generators that amortize attack synthesis through dedicated trained models
[
18
,
26
,
14
,
6

. Despite this breadth, each method is tied to a single search space, such as one refinement loop, one tree search, one mutation pool, one strategy library, one transformation, or one frozen generator. As a result, these methods cannot easily draw on the strengths of other attack families when their chosen strategy is poorly matched to the input.
Lash
operates one level above the methods above: instead of designing another search procedure, it treats their outputs as seed prompts and optimizes how to combine them per request for improved attack success and greater stealth. A more detailed discussion of related work is provided in Appendix

3
Methodology

Threat model:

We assume only query access to the target model: an attacker can submit arbitrary prompts and observe the natural-language responses, but never observes weights, gradients, or hidden activations. This reflects the setting of proprietary, API-only deployments and is the threat model under which our entire pipeline operates. To preserve this assumption end to end,
Lash
composes only seed prompts from black-box base attacks, and the optimizer uses response-level feedback alone, with no white-box loss, surrogate models, or component fine-tuning.

Why hybridize black-box attacks:

Existing black-box methods are not uniformly effective: each commits to a single search space, and the family that succeeds depends strongly on the target model and harm category. Tab.
1
, distilled from Tab.
2
and Tab.
4
, summarizes where each of the four black-box baselines is the strongest (excluding
Lash
) under two-stage evaluation (
ASR_{2}

Every method wins on at least one target or category, but none wins more than eight of sixteen cases: PAIR wins one target and no categories, while TAP, FlipAttack, and AutoDAN-Turbo each carry a different operational regime. This complementarity, rather than any single method’s peak performance, is what
Lash
is designed to exploit.

At a high level,
Lash
operates through four stages (Fig.
1
). First, it constructs a seed prompt pool by running multiple base attack methods on the input prompt. Second, it selects candidate subsets from this pool and assigns continuous mixture weights to the selected blocks. Third, a composition block synthesizes a single candidate prompt from the weighted blocks. Finally, the candidate is queried against the target model, and the resulting score drives genetic optimization of the mixture weights. This separates the generation of seed blocks from the optimization of their composition, so
Lash
does not depend on any single seed method being consistently effective; instead, it searches for complementary combinations of blocks for each input prompt.

3.1
Problem Formulation and Seed Pool

Let
x
denote an input prompt and
T
the target model. The goal of
Lash
is to construct a final prompt
p^{\star}
such that the response
r=T(p^{\star})
receives a high score under an evaluator
J
. The evaluator assigns a scalar score
J(x,p,r)\in[0,10]
, with larger values indicating that the response is more strongly aligned with successful jailbreak behavior, and a prompt is considered successful if
J(x,p,T(p))\geq\tau
for a predefined threshold
\tau
.

Lash
then constructs a seed prompt pool for
x
. Let
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\}
denote a set of
n
black-box base attack methods, drawn from diverse families such as iterative refinement, tree search, and strategy-based attacks (and possibly multiple configurations of the same method). Each base method
A_{i}
generates one seed prompt
b_{i}=A_{i}(x)
, and the resulting pool is
\mathcal{B}(x)=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}
. Each seed captures a distinct attack strategy, phrasing pattern, or structural transformation. Rather than selecting one seed as the final attack,
Lash
treats the pool as a set of reusable components and searches for an effective combination of them for the current input.

3.2
Weighted Hybridization with Subset Search

Given the seed pool
\mathcal{B}(x)
,
Lash
searches over both subsets of seeds and their relative mixture weights. For a candidate subset
\mathcal{S}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(x)
,
Lash
assigns each selected seed a real-valued logit
z=[z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}]
, converted into normalized mixture weights via softmax:

w_{i}=\frac{\exp(z_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\exp(z_{j})}

(1)

A prompt-composition model
M
then takes the input prompt and the weighted seeds and returns a single synthesized candidate:

p=M\left(x,\{(b_{i},w_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}\right).

(2)

The mixture weights control how strongly each selected seed influences the candidate, and the composition block produces a coherent prompt that preserves the intent of the original input. Searching over subsets in addition to weights lets
Lash
suppress low-quality or conflicting seeds while retaining mutually reinforcing combinations: not all seeds are equally useful for a given input, and some may introduce noisy phrasing, incompatible framing, or weak transformations.
Lash
first considers larger subsets to encourage broad strategy fusion, then progressively evaluates smaller subsets to identify more selective combinations. Details on the composition block are given in Appendix
C

3.3
Genetic Optimization

For each candidate subset
\mathcal{S}
,
Lash
optimizes the mixture weights using a genetic algorithm. Each genome is a real-valued logit vector
z=[z_{1},\ldots,z_{|\mathcal{S}|}]
, with one logit per seed in
\mathcal{S}
. The initial population is sampled from a Gaussian:
z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Each genome is evaluated by converting its logits into mixture weights (Eq.
1
), passing the weighted seeds to the composition block to produce a candidate prompt (Eq.
2
), querying that prompt against the target, and scoring the response with the fitness function.

Let
\mathcal{P}^{(g)}
denote the population at generation
g
. After evaluation,
Lash
selects the top-performing fraction as elites
\mathcal{E}^{(g)}=\operatorname{TopK}(\mathcal{P}^{(g)})
, and offspring are generated by perturbing elites with Gaussian noise:

z^{\prime}=z+\epsilon,\qquad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{mut}}^{2}I)

(3)

where
\sigma_{\mathrm{mut}}
controls mutation strength. The elites and their offspring form the next population. This repeats until a candidate reaches the success threshold or the generation budget is exhausted. Because optimization is performed only over the mixture logits,
Lash
remains lightweight and fully black-box, requiring no gradients, hidden states, or weight access.

3.4
Two-Stage Fitness Function

Lash
evaluates each candidate using a two-stage fitness function. Given the input prompt
x
, the synthesized prompt
p
, and the response
r=T(p)
, the first stage applies a keyword-based refusal detector: if
r
contains a common refusal or non-compliance pattern, the candidate is assigned a score of zero,

J(x,p,r)=0\quad\text{if }r\text{ contains a refusal marker,}

(4)

avoiding unnecessary evaluator calls for clearly unsuccessful candidates. If the response passes the refusal filter, the second stage applies an LLM-based evaluator,
J(x,p,r)=\operatorname{JudgeLLM}(x,p,r)
, which scores how much the response satisfies the original target intent on a
[0,10]
scale. The full system prompt for judge LLM is given in Appendix
D
.

For input prompt
x
,
Lash
terminates when either a candidate reaches the threshold
\tau
or the search budget is exhausted, returning the highest-scoring prompt found:

p^{\star}=\arg\max_{p\in\mathcal{Q}(x)}J(x,p,T(p))

(5)

where
\mathcal{Q}(x)
is the set of candidates evaluated for
x
. Algorithm
1
(Appendix
B.2
) gives the full procedure.

Dataset:
We evaluate
Lash
on JailbreakBench
[
3
]
, a standardized benchmark of 100 harmful prompts across 10 behavior categories. Its broad category coverage and common use in jailbreak evaluation enable systematic red-teaming and comparison with established attack families.

Target models and baselines:
We select these six target models to provide a diverse and representative evaluation across model families, deployment settings, and capability levels. Specifically, Llama-2-7B-Chat, Llama-3-8B-Instruct, and Vicuna-7B represent widely used open-source models, while Gemini-2.0-Flash, GPT-4o-mini, and GPT-5.4-nano represent closed-source commercial models with strong instruction-following capabilities. This mixture allows us to evaluate whether the proposed method generalizes across both open and proprietary models rather than overfitting to a single architecture, model family, or provider. For all target models, we use deterministic decoding by setting the temperature to 0 and top-
p
to 1. This minimizes sampling variance and ensures that observed differences are primarily attributable to the attack method and target model behavior rather than randomness in generation. We compare
Lash
against five state-of-the-art jailbreak baselines: PAIR
[
4
]
, TAP
[
23
]
, AutoDAN
[
20
]
, AutoDAN-Turbo (AutoDAN-T)
[
19
]
, and FlipAttack
[
21
]
. For recording the performance of baseline methods, we have kept the standard settings according to their respective paper. For fair comparison, we tune the query-related parameters of query-based baselines to use the same maximum target-query budget of 160. This ensures that all query-based methods are evaluated under the same constraint.

Evaluation metrics:

We report two attack success rate (ASR) metrics.
ASR_{1}
(keyword-based ASR) considers a response successful if no refusal keyword is detected.
ASR_{2}
(two-stage LLM judge-based ASR) first applies keyword-based refusal filtering, then uses GPT-4o-mini as a judge LLM to score whether the response substantively fulfills the harmful request. Responses passing the refusal filter receive a judge score in
[0,10]
, and are considered successful if the score exceeds the threshold
\tau=9
. We also report Mean # Q for iterative query-based attacks, defined as the
average number of target queries used during attack optimization per input
prompt. For
Lash
, Mean # Q includes both
seed-generation queries and GA-search queries; the seed-generation budgets are
maximum budgets, so seed methods may terminate before using all allocated
queries.

We introduce
ASR_{2}
because keyword-only evaluation may overestimate success when models produce evasive or unhelpful responses that avoid refusal keywords
[
32
,
22
]
. While LLM judges have known limitations, recent work demonstrates that strong LLM judges achieve over 80% agreement with human evaluators, matching human-to-human agreement rates
[
40
]
.

Lash
configuration and seed generation:

Lash
constructs its seed prompt pool from three black-box base attack methods: PAIR, TAP, and AutoDAN-Turbo. To generate seed prompts, we run each base method with intentionally reduced maximum query budgets (may converge faster): 5 for PAIR, and TAP, and 10 for AutoDAN-Turbo. These low-budget seeds are intentionally weak, allowing us to test whether
Lash
’s semantic composition and weight optimization can improve beyond the raw seed prompts. The total seed generation cost is maximum of 20 queries per input prompt, leaving 140 queries for
Lash
’s genetic search within the 160 maximum query budget.

Lash
’s genetic algorithm uses population size
P=8
, mutation scale
\sigma_{\text{mut}}=0.6
, and
G=2
generations. We use GPT-4o-mini as both the composition LLM in composition block and the judge LLM (for two-stage fitness evaluation). This configuration maintains the black-box threat model throughout the pipeline, as all three seed methods operate without white-box access.

Lash
and all baselines under three advanced defense mechanisms. We evaluated against SmoothLLM

]

[

5
Conclusion and Discussion

We introduced
Lash
, a black-box meta-attack that lifts the unit of jailbreak optimization from individual prompts to mixtures over attack outputs. Rather than designing yet another search procedure,
Lash
treats the outputs of existing black-box attacks as composable seed prompts and adaptively re-weights them per request, with a derivative-free genetic algorithm mutating real-valued mixture logits and a composition LLM translating each weight vector into a single coherent candidate prompt. The entire pipeline runs under query access only, making the complementarity between black-box attack families directly available on every input rather than only on inputs that some single family happens to handle.

On JailbreakBench across six target models and ten harm categories,
Lash
outperforms five state-of-the-art baselines under both keyword-based and two-stage evaluation and remains competitive under three defense mechanisms. Ablations show that
Lash
does more than select the best seed or assign uniform weights, and mechanistic probing and activation patching link its gains to a shift in the target model’s intermediate representations rather than to surface wording alone.

Lash
is limited to single-turn prompt optimization and is not defense-aware, motivating multi-turn planning and safeguard-adaptive composition. For broader impact,
Lash
supports pre-deployment safety evaluation but is dual-use, requiring safeguarded release and clear usage guidelines.
Future versions of
Lash
can make the composition process defense-aware
and extend the method to multi-turn attack planning, where it optimizes sequences of composed prompts rather than a single final prompt.

Appendix A
Detailed Related Work

Manual and prompt-level jailbreaks:

Hand-crafted attacks remain useful for understanding how surface wording, instruction
structure, and persona framing influence the safety behavior of aligned language models.

34
traced model failures to two recurring causes, namely competing
objectives and mismatched generalization, and showed that even small format changes can
shift refusal behavior. In-the-wild prompt analyses such as
30

characterize DAN-style and persona-driven prompts collected from public communities,
while
28
and
9
formalize prompt injection
in its direct and indirect variants.
39
further studied persuasion-style
appeals as jailbreak vectors. Collectively, these studies show that aligned models are
sensitive not only to literal content, but also to framing, instruction hierarchy, and
presentation format. This motivates attack pipelines that search over natural-language
reformulations rather than only token-level perturbations.
Lash
does not rely on any
fixed manual template, but hand-crafted prompts can be inserted into its seed pool as
additional attack blocks alongside automatically generated ones.

White-box and token-level optimization:

A major family of automated jailbreak attacks operates under the assumption of white-box
access to the target model. GCG
[
41
]
exemplifies this approach by
constructing adversarial suffixes through greedy coordinate gradient-based optimization
over discrete token choices, using model-internal signals such as gradients and
next-token log-probabilities. This builds on earlier gradient-based prompt-optimization
methods such as AutoPrompt
[
31
]
and Hard Prompts Made
Easy
[
35
]
, which use model-internal signals to optimize discrete prompt
tokens. COLD-Attack
[
10
]
adapts energy-based constrained decoding with
Langevin dynamics to generate controllable adversarial prompts under constraints such as
fluency, stealthiness, sentiment, and contextual coherence. AutoDAN
[
20
]
uses a hierarchical genetic algorithm over
structured natural-language prompts to generate stealthy, semantically meaningful jailbreak
prompts. Unlike token-level suffix attacks such as GCG, AutoDAN is designed to preserve
readability and evade simple perplexity-based filtering. However, its original optimization
still relies on white-box model-internal objective signals, such as loss values, when scoring
candidate prompts. More recently, LARGO
[
15
]
revisits gradient-based
jailbreak optimization in continuous latent space. It first optimizes an adversarial embedding
vector and then uses self-reflective decoding to translate the optimized latent state into a
fluent natural-language suffix. LARGO therefore shows that white-box optimization can
produce readable and transferable jailbreak prompts when the search is performed over
latent representations rather than directly over discrete tokens. The shared limitation across
this family is the requirement for model internals, including weights, gradients, loss values,
or embedding-space access.
Lash
does not make this assumption. It works with
natural-language prompts, queries the target model only as a black box, and optimizes using
response-level feedback rather than token probabilities, loss values, or latent gradients.

Black-box LLM-based search:

PAIR
[
4
]
instantiates a black-box attacker-target loop in which
an attacker LLM iteratively rewrites candidate prompts based on feedback from the target
model’s responses. TAP
[
23
]
extends this idea into a branching tree
search, using evaluator-guided pruning to discard low-promise candidates and improve query
efficiency on closed-source models. The broader LLM-as-red-team paradigm proposed by

27
underlies these pipelines. However, each of these methods commits to
a single search trajectory, such as one attacker loop, one tree search process, or one
refinement policy for a given prompt. Rather than running one search process to generate
one candidate,
Lash
runs multiple base methods to populate a seed pool, then searches
over weighted combinations of those seeds. This allows
Lash
to exploit the complementary
strengths of different black-box search strategies rather than committing to a single
trajectory.

Genetic, fuzzing, and strategy-based attacks:

GPTFuzzer
[
37
]
evolves human-written jailbreak templates by applying
LLM-based mutations, such as expansion, shortening, rephrasing, and crossover, directly to
the prompt text. AutoDAN-Turbo
[
19
]
uses lifelong strategy
self-exploration to discover, maintain, and apply jailbreak strategies in a black-box setting,
and can also incorporate human-designed strategies. RLbreaker
[
5
]

uses a deep reinforcement learning agent to guide black-box jailbreak search, allowing the
attacker to adapt its search actions based on feedback from previous attempts. These methods search within one method-specific space: GPTFuzzer mutates a
template pool, AutoDAN-Turbo explores and applies strategies from its strategy space, and
RLbreaker learns which operators to use during search.
Lash
instead optimizes
real-valued weights over seeds from multiple heterogeneous methods. For example, a PAIR
output, a GPTFuzzer mutation, and an AutoDAN-Turbo strategy can coexist in one pool, and
the composition LLM can blend them according to learned weights.

Transformation-based attacks:

Transformation-based attacks alter how the harmful request is encoded, structured, or
presented, rather than changing its underlying semantic intent. ReNeLLM
[
7
]

first rewrites the harmful query to appear more benign, then embeds it inside a nested
scenario that encourages the model to respond. DeepInception
[
17
]

constructs nested fictional scenes and personas around the request to induce unsafe behavior.
DrAttack
[
16
]
decomposes a harmful instruction into less detectable
sub-prompts, uses benign in-context reassembly demonstrations, and searches for synonym
substitutions that preserve the original malicious intent. ArtPrompt
[
13
]

represents sensitive words using ASCII art, exploiting weaknesses in how models interpret
text-based visual patterns. CipherChat
[
38
]
encodes unsafe requests using
cipher-based prompts and demonstrations to test whether safety alignment generalizes beyond
natural-language inputs. FlipAttack
[
21
]
flips or reverses text and uses
guidance mechanisms that help the model recover and execute the hidden request.
Low-resource language attacks
[
36
]
translate unsafe prompts into
languages with weaker safety coverage, exposing cross-lingual gaps in alignment. No single transformation is uniformly effective across all models and defenses: ASCII-art,
cipher-based, character-level, and language-based transformations each exploit different
weaknesses, and their effectiveness can vary substantially across target models and safeguard
mechanisms.
Lash
can exploit this complementarity by running
multiple transformation methods on the same harmful prompt, collecting their outputs as seed
prompts, and using genetic optimization to find an effective weighted combination.

Learned jailbreak generators:

Instead of optimizing a new attack from scratch for each prompt, learned jailbreak generators
train a dedicated model once and then use it to generate attacks quickly. AmpleGCG
[
18
]

trains a generative model on adversarial suffixes collected from many GCG runs, allowing it
to produce similar suffixes without repeatedly running gradient optimization. AdvPrompter
[
26
]

trains an adversarial prompt generator through alternating optimization and low-rank
fine-tuning, producing human-readable adversarial suffixes quickly without requiring gradients
of the target LLM. JailPO
[
14
]
trains attacker models using a
preference-optimization-based objective so that they learn to generate covert jailbreak
prompts that are more likely to elicit unsafe responses. MASTERKEY
[
6
]

collects successful jailbreak examples and fine-tunes a generator to mimic their patterns,
while also studying how chatbot defenses can be reverse-engineered. The main advantage of
these approaches is fast attack generation after training. However, the generator is typically
fixed after training and may not easily incorporate new attack methods or strategies that
emerge later without additional data collection and retraining. Training such generators can
also require substantial computing resources, time, and attack data.

Lash
is training-free. It composes outputs from existing methods, so learned generators
can contribute seeds without requiring
Lash
itself to retrain.

B.1
Design Rationale

The design of
Lash
is motivated by the observation that seed prompts generated by different base attack methods often contain useful but incomplete strategies. Some seed prompts may contain strong framing, while others may contain better structure, wording, or transformation patterns. Instead of selecting only one seed prompt,
Lash
treats the seed prompt pool as a compositional search space.

Weighted prompt hybridization allows
Lash
to emphasize useful seed prompts and reduce the effect of weaker or conflicting ones. The softmax weights provide a simple way to assign different levels of influence to each selected seed prompt. Subset search further helps remove seed prompts that are noisy or incompatible for the current input prompt.

The resulting objective is difficult to optimize with gradient-based methods because the composition block, target model, and evaluator are all treated as black-box components. For this reason,
Lash
uses a derivative-free genetic algorithm. The genetic algorithm preserves strong candidates through elitism and explores nearby prompt mixtures through Gaussian mutation of the mixture logits. This keeps the search lightweight and fully black-box with respect to the target model.

B.2
Genetic Search over Mixed Prompts

Algorithm
1
describes the prompt-level genetic search used by
Lash
. The algorithm starts after the seed prompt pool
\mathcal{B}(x)
has already been constructed for the input prompt
x
. It then searches over nonempty subsets of the seed prompt pool in descending order of subset size. This means that
Lash
first tries to combine more seed prompts together, and then moves to smaller subsets if larger combinations are not successful.

For each subset
\mathcal{S}
, the algorithm initializes a population of random logit vectors. Each logit vector represents one possible weighting of the seed prompts in the subset. The logits are converted into normalized mixture weights using the softmax function. These weights, together with the selected seed prompts and the original input prompt, are passed to the composition block. The composition block then produces one synthesized prompt.

Each synthesized prompt is sent to the target model, and the target response is scored by the evaluator. During this process,
Lash
keeps track of the best prompt, response, and score found so far. If any candidate reaches the success threshold
\tau
, the algorithm stops early and returns the best candidate.

After evaluating the initial population,
Lash
applies genetic refinement. In each generation, the top-scoring genomes are selected as elites. These elites are copied into the next population. The remaining genomes are created by adding Gaussian noise to the elite logit vectors. This mutation changes the mixture weights, but it does not directly change the prompt text. Each mutated genome is then evaluated through the same composition, target-query, and scoring steps.

If no candidate reaches the success threshold,
Lash
continues until the generation budget is exhausted for all subsets. It then returns the highest-scoring candidate observed during the full search. Since the genetic algorithm only mutates continuous logits over seed prompts, the search remains derivative-free, lightweight, and black-box.

Appendix C
Details of Composition Block

The composition block receives three inputs: the original input prompt, a selected subset of seed prompt pool, and the logits associated with those seeds. The logits are first converted into normalized mixture weights using the softmax function. The original prompt, the selected seed prompts, and their corresponding weights are then passed to the composition LLM. Guided by these weights, the composition LLM synthesizes a single candidate prompt that integrates the selected seed prompts according to the mixing weights while preserving the intent of the original request. Appendix E
Details on Mechanistic Analysis

Lash
achieves higher attack success rates than baselines, but it is unclear whether this advantage comes from better prompt wording or from inducing different internal model behavior. To investigate this question, we conduct two complementary mechanistic analyses: layerwise diagnostic probing and causal activation patching.

Layer-wise success-likeliness probing:

We first examine whether successful jailbreak prompts create distinguishable internal representations that can be detected by analyzing the model’s hidden states. For each attack prompt, we extract the last-token hidden state at every transformer layer and train a separate logistic regression probe for each layer to predict whether the prompt will succeed
[
1
]
. The probe is trained on 75% of prompts and evaluated on the held-out 25%.

Figure
4
shows the held-out probe success probability
\sigma(g_{\ell})
, where
g_{\ell}
is the probe’s logit output at layer
\ell
and
\sigma
is the sigmoid function. The y-axis represents how "success-like" the hidden states appear to the probe, this correlates with the attack success rate. Higher values mean the probe classifies those hidden states as more similar to successful attacks.

On Llama2-7B,
Lash
produces the clearest separation from the baselines. Its success-likeness rises sharply after the embedding layer and remains high across most early and middle layers, while PAIR, AutoDAN-Turbo, AutoDAN, and FlipAttack stay substantially lower. TAP shows a moderate success-likeness signal, but remains clearly below
Lash
throughout the network. This suggests that, on Llama2-7B,
Lash
prompts induce a more consistently success-associated internal trajectory than the baseline attacks. On Llama3-8B, both
Lash
and TAP produce strong success-likeness signals.
Lash
reaches the highest values in early-to-middle layers and remains near the top across depth, while TAP stays close and is occasionally comparable at individual layers. AutoDAN-Turbo and FlipAttack show success-likeness in intermediate layers, whereas AutoDAN collapses to near-zero after the first few layers. Thus, the Llama3-8B results still favor
Lash
, but the separation from TAP is less pronounced than on Llama2-7B.

Overall, the probe analysis suggests that
Lash
’s higher attack success is reflected not only in final outputs but also in the model’s internal representations. The strongest success-likeness signal appears in early-to-middle or middle layers rather than only at the output layer, indicating that successful jailbreak behavior becomes linearly decodable before generation. However, because this is a diagnostic probing experiment, we interpret the result as representation-level evidence.
