Group Resonance Network: Learnable Prototypes and Multi-Subject Resonance for EEG Emotion Recognition
Renwei Meng

Abstract
Electroencephalography (EEG)-based emotion recognition remains challenging in cross-subject settings due to severe inter-subject variability. Existing methods mainly learn subject-invariant features, but often under-exploit stimulus-locked group regularities shared across subjects. To address this issue, we propose the Group Resonance Network (GRN) , which integrates individual EEG dynamics with offline group resonance modeling. GRN contains three components: an individual encoder for band-wise EEG features, a set of learnable group prototypes for prototype-induced resonance, and a multi-subject resonance branch that encodes PLV/coherence-based synchrony with a small reference set. A resonance-aware fusion module combines individual and group-level representations for final classification. Experiments on SEED and DEAP under both subject-dependent and leave-one-subject-out protocols show that GRN consistently outperforms competitive baselines, while ablation studies confirm the complementary benefits of prototype learning and multi-subject resonance modeling. Code is available at github.com/xiaolu-666113/Group-Resonance-Network .

3
Methodology

3.1
Overview

We propose the
Group Resonance Network (GRN)
for EEG-based emotion recognition under both subject-dependent and subject-independent settings.
GRN learns (i) an
individual representation
from EEG features, (ii) a
prototype-induced group resonance
via
learnable group prototypes
, and (iii) an explicit
multi-subject resonance representation
from
pairwise synchrony matrices
computed against a small reference set.
The three views are fused with difference/commonality interactions for classification.

Figure 1:
Overall GRN pipeline.

3.2
Input Representation and Individual Encoding

Given an EEG segment
X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T}
, we first extract band-wise features over
B
frequency bands (e.g.,
\delta,\theta,\alpha,\beta,\gamma
).
For each time window, we stack features across channels and bands, denoted as
X_{feat}
.
An encoder
\mathrm{Enc}(\cdot)
maps the input to an individual embedding:

F=\mathrm{Enc}(X_{feat})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

In practice,
\mathrm{Enc}(\cdot)
can be instantiated as a lightweight CNN/Transformer backbone operating on channel-band grids or tokenized channel features.

3.3
Learnable Group Prototypes for Prototype-Induced Resonance

Instead of fixing a single “prototype subject”, GRN maintains
M

learnable group prototypes
:

\mathcal{P}=\{p_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M},\quad p_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

optimized jointly with the rest of the model.
For a given individual embedding
F
, we compute similarity scores to all prototypes, then produce a soft weighting (attention) over prototypes.
The
prototype-induced group resonance
is a weighted combination:

R=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}p_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Intuitively,
R
summarizes which
group-level
pattern the current sample aligns with most.

3.4
Multi-Subject Resonance Tensor

To capture stimulus-locked group synchrony beyond prototypes, we construct an explicit
multi-subject resonance tensor
.
For each training fold, we sample a small reference set
\mathcal{S}=\{X^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K_{r}}
from training subjects only (to avoid leakage in LOSO).
For each reference
X^{(k)}
, we compute:

•

PLV matrix
(phase synchrony) between current sample and reference,

•

CoH matrix
(frequency-domain coupling), optionally averaged over frequency bands.

This yields a stacked tensor:

\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{r}\times C\times C\times 2},

where the last dimension corresponds to (PLV, CoH).
A lightweight resonance encoder
\mathrm{ResEnc}(\cdot)
(e.g., shallow 2D CNN + pooling) compresses
\mathbf{M}
into:

G=\mathrm{ResEnc}(\mathbf{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Here,
G
represents the
structural synchrony
between the current subject and a small cohort under the same stimulus alignment.

Figure 2:
Multi-subject resonance tensor construction

3.5
Resonance-Aware Fusion and Classification

GRN fuses three embeddings: individual
F
, prototype-induced resonance
R
, and multi-subject resonance
G
.
We form interaction terms that explicitly model:

•

Difference
(individual-specific components):
F-R
and
F-G
,

•

Commonality
(shared components): element-wise products
F\odot R
and
F\odot G
.

All terms are concatenated and fed to an MLP to obtain the final representation, followed by a softmax classifier.
This design encourages the model to simultaneously preserve subject-specific cues and leverage stimulus-locked group patterns.

3.6
Training Objective

We optimize the standard cross-entropy loss for classification.
Additionally, we apply a prototype regularizer to stabilize prototype learning by encouraging the embedding to be close to high-attention prototypes.
The final objective is:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{cls}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{proto}.

In all experiments,
\lambda
is tuned on the validation set.

Experiments

Datasets

We evaluate on two widely used EEG emotion benchmarks:

SEED

DEAP

We follow the official preprocessing and windowing protocols provided by each dataset.

Protocols

We report results under:

Subject-Dependent (SD)

•

Subject-Independent (SI)
: Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation.

Reference selection:
for SI, the resonance reference set
\mathcal{S}
is sampled strictly from training subjects in each fold, never using the held-out subject.

Implementation Details

Unless stated otherwise:

•

Embedding dimension
d=256
, prototypes
M=8
, references
K_{r}=3
.

•

Optimizer: Adam, learning rate
1\times 10^{-4}
, weight decay
1\times 10^{-4}
.

•

Batch size 64, up to 80 epochs, early stopping patience 10.

•

PLV computed via analytic phase (Hilbert transform); CoH via Welch PSD; CoH band-averaged.

5
Conclusion

We presented GRN, a dual-level group modeling framework for EEG-based emotion recognition.
GRN integrates (i) an individual representation learned from EEG features, (ii) prototype-induced group resonance via learnable group prototypes, and (iii) explicit multi-subject resonance captured by PLV/CoH-based synchrony tensors.
A resonance-aware fusion module jointly models commonality and difference among individual and group views, improving robustness to inter-subject variability.
Experiments on SEED and DEAP under subject-dependent and subject-independent protocols demonstrate consistent gains, with ablations confirming the complementary contributions of learnable prototypes and multi-subject resonance modeling.
