Toolkit for multistate disease progression simulation and treatment decision-making aid
Authors: Can Xie, Ruosha Li, Wael Saber, Jorge E. Cortes, Nicholas J. Short, Xuelin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal timing of a treatment within therapy lines can maximize its benefit while minimizing risks and side effects. Because patients differ in clinical and biological characteristics, personalized treatment planning can support physicians’ decision-making. However, multistate disease progression complicates the comparison of treatment strategies, motivating the development of a user-friendly web tool for multistate disease progression simulation and treatment decision support. Microsimulation is a natural approach for comparing prespecified treatment-timing strategies, where it generates disease trajectories for a large number of virtual patients matched to an individual patient’s observed data. In our framework, microsimulation is performed using a multistate model to capture state-to-state transition hazards.ResultsWe develop TxMicroSim, a web-based interface built with R Shiny, to compare treatment-timing strategies by estimating model-projected restricted mean survival times (RMSTs). The application consists of three main steps: defining the multistate structure, building the multistate model, and performing microsimulation. First, the user specifies the multistate structure, with the treatment of interest as one of the states and an absorbing state representing the time-to-event endpoint. The user also identifies transitions that lead into the treatment state, defining the strategy-driven transitions used to compare treatment-timing strategies. Next, to build clock-reset transition-specific flexible parametric proportional hazards models, the user can either upload data for model fitting or manually enter covariate hazard ratios and baseline hazard parameters. Two baseline hazard specifications are supported: a piecewise constant function and a natural cubic spline. Finally, the user enters patient-specific information, including covariates, the initial disease state at diagnosis, and the treatment start time for strategy-driven transitions. Microsimulation is used to compute RMST up to a prespecified time horizon under each strategy. Throughout the app, interactive visualizations are provided for the multistate structure, estimated hazard ratios and baseline hazards, and RMST comparisons across treatment strategies.ConclusionsTxMicroSim is a flexible web tool that compares treatment-timing strategies and quantifies survival impact using interpretable summaries and interactive visualizations.

Background

Optimal treatment sequencing has received increasing attention in recent years, driven by the growing number of therapeutics and the emphasis on personalized medicine. In oncology, where multiple effective options often exist across therapy lines, the key question is not only which treatment to use but also when to initiate or switch therapy. Different sequences can yield substantially different survival outcomes in individuals based on patient- and disease-related characteristics. The development of rigorous, user-friendly methods and tools for comparing sequencing strategies is motivated. More specifically, we focus on comparing the alternative timings of one treatment throughout the therapy lines in this paper.

Our work is motivated by chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) studies. CML is a relatively uncommon hematologic malignancy that originates in the bone marrow. The primary treatment inhibits the abnormal BCR::ABL1 oncoprotein via a tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI). With the introduction of TKIs, the rate of disease progression from chronic phase (CP) to more aggressive, advanced phases has declined substantially. Another treatment option for CML is allogeneic hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation (HSCT). Since the TKI era, HSCT has been reserved primarily for patients in the accelerated phase (AP) and blast phase (BP) of CML or those with CP who are resistant or intolerant to most TKIs [1].

Although HSCT has curative potential, it carries substantial risk. The timing of HSCT in patients with CML remains controversial [2]. In the comparison of a non-transplant cohort and a transplant cohort, Hu et al. [3] found that the HSCT shortened overall survival (OS) among patients in CML-CP but tended to increase OS for patients in CML-BP. The benefit of HSCT was not clear among patients in CML-AP. Paradoxically, although HSCT offers a relative survival advantage in BP, patients transplanted during BP have notably worse survival outcomes compared to those transplanted in earlier phases [4]. This paradox underscores the importance of comparing alternative HSCT-timing strategies to balance its potential benefits against its associated risks [5].

To compare treatment-timing strategies, microsimulation is used to assess the potential impact of proposed treatment before it is implemented in real-world settings. This is done by generating a large number of virtual individuals and their responses to the treatment, and using their aggregate data for evaluation. Microsimulation has also recently been applied to treatment-sequence evaluation of costs and effects in chronic disease settings [6]. When longitudinal disease processes exist (e.g., CP, AP, BP, and death), a multistate model can model the transitions, allowing patients to move from one state to another. Therefore, the microsimulation needs to rely on the state-to-state transition hazards. This approach has been applied in myelodysplastic syndromes to evaluate the optimal timing of HSCT [5, 7].

Several web tools have been developed under microsimulation. Mihaylova et al. [8] developed a Shiny app for a microsimulation policy model used for cardiovascular disease. It provides patients’ disease risk and the projected consequences with or without treatments. This application has not incorporated the multistate disease progression, same as the Shiny apps developed for microsimulation with uncertainty in baseline risk [9] and concurrency on human immunodeficiency virus transmission dynamics [10]. Peterson [11] developed a simulation designer for multistate model, but it cannot be used to evaluate the survival effect of a treatment along with multiple disease states. Zhou et al. [12] developed a discrete event simulation model for the economic evaluation of antipsychotics in schizophrenia. The framework combines microsimulation with multistate disease dynamics, but it is tailored to schizophrenia and is not readily generalizable to other diseases or alternative multistate structures. The SHINE Health Intervention Impact Calculator provided a web-based interface for exploring health and economic impacts of public health interventions using proportional multistate lifetable simulation modeling [13]. To the best of our knowledge, there is no existing Shiny app in which users can specify the multistate structure, define the treatment strategies, and conduct the comparison.

We develop an R Shiny app, TxMicroSim, that supports decision-making by identifying the optimal timing of treatment along a multistate disease course. The web tool integrates microsimulation with a multistate model via transition-specific hazard models for all possible state-to-state transitions. For each candidate treatment-timing strategy, we use microsimulation to generate patient trajectories and obtain the resulting OS distribution. The restricted mean survival time (RMST) [14], evaluated up to a prespecified follow-up time, is used to quantify the projected survival time of the prespecified strategy.

Microsimulation

The multistate structure is set up with the treatment as one of the states. With the estimated transition-specific models, the microsimulation is implemented to generate a large number of trajectories with states and times (e.g., 1,000 replicates) for one patient under each treatment strategy. Then we evaluate this patient’s RMSTs of OS at different treatment strategies.

In one microsimulation run, given the current state, candidate waiting times for all allowable outgoing transitions are computed by using their corresponding transition-specific hazard functions. This is implemented with a transition-specific clock-reset time scale, where each baseline hazard function depends on the time since entry into the current state rather than the absolute time from study entry. If the transition from the current state to the treatment state is also one of the potential transitions, its candidate time is set to the prespecified treatment start time since entry into the current state. By comparing all the clock-reset candidate waiting times, the transition is determined by the minimum time. The elapsed time is added to the total follow-up time. The process continues with the corresponding destination state as the new current state. This procedure is repeated until the accumulated follow-up time reaches the prespecified time horizon of the RMST or the process enters the absorbing time-to-event state, whichever occurs first.

We compute the RMST by averaging restricted survival times across all simulated trajectories. RMST is evaluated under an intention-to-treat principle: survival time up to the prespecified horizon is included for every simulated patient according to the assigned treatment-timing strategy, regardless of whether the trajectory successfully enters the treatment state when assigned to receive treatment. To account for parameter uncertainty in the multistate model, we implement a probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) using parametric bootstrapping with a large number of replicates (e.g., 1000). In each replicate, the estimated regression coefficients and the parameters associated with the baseline hazard functions are sampled from their asymptotic multivariate normal distributions, and the RMST is recomputed through microsimulation. From all the replicates, we propagate the parameter uncertainty into RMSTs and obtain the 95% confidence interval for the point estimate of RMST.

Implementation

The app TxMicroSim is developed in the R statistical software (version 4.5.2) [16]. Users access this web application via https://multistatemodel.shinyapps.io/txmicrosim/ or download the corresponding Shiny app code with its helper functions from the GitHub repository https://github.com/cxie19/TxMicroSim. The GitHub repository also contains the sample datasets which are simulated based on the real data discussed in Results section. These datasets are referenced in the Shiny app and can be downloaded directly from the app. The R package shiny [17] supplies the UI and server framework, along with reactive functions that implement interactive app behavior. The R packages shinyjs [18] and shinybusy [19] also support the development. The web application is structured into three parts: multistate structure, multistate modeling, and treatment strategies.

In the first part of the web application, Multistate Structure, the user can define the state names and transitions among the states for the multistate modeling, where the minimum number of states is 3. A terminal (absorbing) state is required to represent the endpoint. For example, with OS as the endpoint, the absorbing state is death from any cause. For progression-free survival, the absorbing state corresponds to the first occurrence of progression or death, whichever happens first.

The treatment under consideration should be explicitly represented as a state in the multistate model. Among the defined transitions, the user must also identify transitions leading into the treatment state, which are used to define and compare treatment-timing strategies. These transitions ending with the treatment are treated as deterministic or strategy-driven, and are therefore not modeled stochastically during model fitting and microsimulation. The deterministic transition is defined as a transition whose occurrence time is fixed by the prespecified treatment strategy rather than generated from an estimated transition-specific hazard function. The multistate model’s diagram is plotted by using the R package visNetwork [20], and the transition matrix is presented.

In the second part, Multistate Modeling, the user may upload one or multiple datasets in the required data format for selected transitions to fit the multistate model under the structure defined in the first part. If a transition is modeled using a single dataset, the transition-specific model can include this dataset’s unique covariates. If multiple datasets are used to model the same transition(s), only the shared covariate space can be set as the covariates in each transition-specific model. Among the imported datasets, the user needs to define the source data, which is set as the reference in the model fitting and microsimulation.

For data pooling in transition-specific modeling, we assume that the transition definition, time origin, event definition, censoring mechanism, and covariate coding are compatible across datasets. Pooling data for a transition-specific hazard model further assumes that, conditional on the covariates included in the model, the transition-specific hazard structure is comparable across datasets. When appropriate, a data source indicator may be included as an optional covariate in the transition-specific hazard model to account for systematic differences across contributing datasets, such as differences in patient selection, treatment practice patterns, follow-up procedures, or unmeasured prognostic factors. If a transition model is estimated from pooled datasets without a data source indicator, the transition-specific hazard function is comparable across contributing data sources conditional on the covariates included in the model.

Given the dataset(s), a table of the number of patients per transition is provided. The R package mstate (function msprep) [21] is used to transform the data for the multistate model. The R package DT (function datatable) [22] prints out the data set(s) with interactive features.

The user assigns the covariates for transitions not driven by the treatment. The selectable covariates are those available in the uploaded dataset(s), including the data source indicator if more than one dataset contributes to the model fitting of a transition and the time from the initial state (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{start}$$\end{document}). For the data source indicator, the source dataset is used as the reference category, and dummy variables are included for the auxiliary dataset(s). \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{start}$$\end{document} is defined as the accumulated time from the initial state to the beginning of the current transition. It can be considered as an optional covariate. For example, patients who enter AP or BP after a long duration in CP may differ prognostically from patients who progress rapidly. Including \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{start}$$\end{document} allows the model to account for the prior disease history in post-progression transitions and may help reduce duration-related prognostic bias. However, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{start}$$\end{document} is not required for all transition-specific models and can be omitted when it does not improve model fit or is not statistically significant. The covariate assignment can also be done without uploading any data. The user can manually write out the covariate names for all the transitions not affected by the treatment.

Given the uploaded data, the clock-reset flexible parametric PH models are fitted for all transitions not affected by the treatment. We provide two choices of baseline hazard functions. The default option is piecewise constant functions, whose corresponding R package is eha [23]. The default values of the maximum number of time intervals and number of events in each interval are both set to 20, and both can be adjusted in the interface. The other option is the natural cubic spline function for the logarithm of baseline cumulative hazard function proposed by Royston and Parmar [15], whose corresponding R package is flexsurv [24]. The best number of knots ranging from 1 to 10 is determined by the smallest BIC for each transition-specific model.

The hazard ratios of the covariates estimated from the flexible parametric PH models are illustrated by forest plots. In addition, the estimated baseline hazard functions are shown with the estimated baseline hazard rates at the time intervals from the piecewise constant functions, or estimated regression coefficients and knot values from the natural cubic spline functions. The app also provides an optional baseline-hazard comparison module, allowing users to compare the estimated baseline hazard functions from the selected model with those obtained from the alternative baseline-hazard specification.

Meanwhile, the multistate model can be established without uploading any dataset. The hazard ratios of covariates and regression coefficients in the baseline hazard functions can even be modified when the data exists. The R packages ggplot2 [25] and scales [26] are used for the plots of the estimated hazard ratios and baseline hazard functions.

The third part, Treatment Strategies, uses microsimulation to compute patient-specific, model-based RMST estimates up to a prespecified time horizon under different treatment strategies. Each treatment strategy is whether the treatment should be initiated at the prespecified state and time. The default number of microsimulation runs is 1000. For the prespecified patient, the user specifies covariate values, the initial disease state at diagnosis, and the treatment start time since the prespecified state for strategy. Shared inputs across treatment strategies can be entered once, and values unique to each strategy should be provided separately.

Under each treatment strategy, we compute RMSTs for two scenarios: receiving the treatment of interest at the prespecified state and time, and not receiving the treatment of interest throughout the disease progression. In the no-treatment scenario, patients are assumed to continue along the current care path without receiving the treatment of interest. To ensure reproducibility, the simulation randomness is controlled by fixing the seeds for both treatment and no-treatment scenarios. We create a helper function gen_trans_time to generate the trajectories and compute the RMSTs from the transition-specific PH models, whose details can be found at https://github.com/cxie19/TxMicroSim.

These RMSTs should be interpreted as model-based projections from the fitted multistate model, rather than causal estimates of optimal treatment timing since the treatment entry is strategy imposed. A larger RMST indicates a longer projected survival time within the prespecified time horizon under the specified treatment strategy. We recommend the time horizons of the RMSTs for patients aged < 45, 45-64, and ≥ 65 years as 20, 15, and 10 years, respectively.

RMSTs for different treatment strategies are displayed using bar plots. With fitted transition-specific hazard functions for the uploaded data, the bar plots illustrate the point estimates of the RMSTs with their 95% confidence intervals from the PSA. For PSA, 1000 parametric bootstrap replicates are generated by sampling the model parameter vectors from their estimated multivariate normal distributions using the R package mvtnorm [27]. If the user has modified the original parameter estimates, the updated values would replace the mean vector in the corresponding estimated multivariate normal distribution.

The RMST difference plots (yes minus no treatment) with 95% confidence intervals are also provided. If the 95% confidence interval crosses 0, there is no statistically significant RMST difference between the treatment and no-treatment strategies. If the entire confidence interval lies above 0, the specified treatment strategy yields a significantly higher projected RMST than the no-treatment strategy. Conversely, if the entire confidence interval lies below 0, the specified treatment strategy yields a significantly lower projected RMST than the no-treatment strategy.

On the other hand, when no data are provided, the error bars in the bar plots represent ± 1 standard deviation of RMSTs across microsimulation runs, which is truncated at 0 and the prespecified time horizon. The additional R packages used are dplyr [28], tidyr [29], doParallel [30], and patchwork [31].

Results

We demonstrated our developed Shiny app, TxMicroSim, using a real data application of patients diagnosed with CML. We aimed to compare model-based RMSTs of OS under scenarios of receiving versus not receiving HSCT at different times/states for an individual patient. The treatment strategies included receiving HSCT in CP, AP, or BP, and no HSCT.

Within this multistate framework, transitions from CP, AP, and BP to HSCT (3, 6, and 8) were modeled as deterministic (non-stochastic) events, shown in the rightmost panel of Fig. 1a, since HSCT was administered to patients meeting predefined transplant-eligibility criteria. For example, patients with resistance and/or intolerance to multiple TKIs or progression to AP or BP are more likely to receive HSCT. The timings of HSCT could be prespecified at transition 3, 6, and 8 in the microsimulation. All remaining transitions (1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, and 10) were modeled stochastically. Figure 1b displays the diagram of the multistate model and its corresponding transition matrix.

Data description

The original CML dataset included 2060 patients diagnosed with CML either at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (MDACC) or outside institutions, with all patients receiving subsequent care at MDACC between 2003 and 2025. There were 91 patients diagnosed before 2001, where the earliest diagnosis time was 1984 at outside institutions and 1991 at MDACC. We included patients diagnosed in 2001 or later only since the contemporary first-line choice of TKIs started in 2001, an era which is most relevant for current decision-making. We excluded patients with unknown OS outcomes (n=1) and aged < 18 years at diagnosis (n=13). The final dataset for analysis contained 1955 adults with CML. Among patients with CML-AP (n=371), 58.2% were newly diagnosed with CML in AP, and the remaining patients were progressed from CP. There were 25.4% of patients newly diagnosed with CML in BP among 283 patients with CML-BP.

To increase statistical power for modeling the HSCT-to-death transition, we also incorporated the data from the Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplantation Research (CIBMTR). The CIBMTR dataset consisted of 1070 patients diagnosed with CML between 2001 and 2013, all of whom underwent HSCT [3]. We included patients aged ≥ 18 years at diagnosis. Hence, 1059 patients receiving HSCT were used to support the establishment of the multistate model. The CIBMTR dataset classified the disease status prior to HSCT as first chronic phase (CP1, n=623), second or greater chronic phase (CP2+, n=192), hematologic complete remission with prior BP (n=38), AP (n=132), and BP (n=74). We classified the first three disease-status categories as CP to align with the states defined in the multistate model. Since the CIBMTR dataset did not provide the time of entering the disease statuses (CP, AP, or BP) before receiving HSCT, it could contribute to transition 10 only (HSCT → death) with the MDACC dataset. Multistate modeling

All non-treatment affected transitions (1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, and 10) were modeled stochastically using the transition-specific PH models with piecewise constant baseline hazard functions. To determine the cutoff time points for the baseline hazard function, we used the default values of the maximum number of intervals and maximum number of events in each interval. These transition-specific models incorporated sex (1 = female and 0 = male) and continuous age centered by 50 years as covariates. Treatment strategies

Based on the estimated multistate model, the goal was to compare the model-projected RMSTs under timing strategies of HSCT for an individual patient across the CP, AP, and BP states. Three clinical scenarios were considered in which the patient was assumed to be newly diagnosed with CML in CP, AP, and BP. When CP was the initial state, RMSTs were compared across three potential HSCT time points: CP, AP, and BP. When AP and BP were the initial states, two time points (AP and BP) and one time point (BP) were evaluated, respectively. We defined the no-HSCT therapy as the continuation of the current non-transplant care path. Subsequent changes in the no-HSCT therapy, such as switching to a third-generation TKI, were not explicitly modeled. Under the same scenario, the RMSTs of not receiving HSCT in all strategies were the same. Analyses below were conducted for patients aged 40, 50, and 65 years, and for both sexes. The time horizons of RMSTs for patients aged 40, 50, and 65 were 20, 15, and 10 years, respectively.

Suppose HSCT occurred three months after reaching the HSCT-eligible state, for all treatment strategies under the three scenarios, where we set the treatment state time since prior state equal to 0.25 years on the interface. Based on the defined covariates in the multistate model, the sex covariate was set to 1 for female and 0 for male, and the centered age values were -10, 0, and 15 for 45-, 50-, 65-year-old patients on the interface. The non-shared covariates needed to be specified for treatment strategies. The dummy variable of state AP prior to HSCT was set to 0, 1, and 0, respectively, when the HSCT was considered in CP, AP, and BP. Similarly, the dummy variable of state BP prior to HSCT was set to 0, 0, and 1, respectively, when the HSCT was considered in CP, AP, and BP.

We conducted an additional analysis using the richer covariate set available in the CIBMTR dataset, rather than restricting the analysis to the covariates shared with the MDACC dataset, to fit the transition-specific hazard function from HSCT to death (transition 10). We included the patients without missing KPS (90-100 and <90), donor type (HLA-identical sibling donor and well-matched unrelated donor), and conditioning regimen intensity (MAC and RIC/NMA) from the CIBMTR dataset (n=1059), yielding 965 patients with 397 deaths. These three variables, along with sex, centered age, and disease status prior to HSCT, were incorporated into the HSCT-to-death transition-specific hazard model. The MDACC dataset contributed to the multistate modeling of all transitions except HSCT to death with sex and centered age as covariates described above.

Discussion

Our developed web application TxMicroSim compares the RMSTs of treatment strategies by building the multistate model and simulating a large number of virtual patients with the same covariate values under different treatment strategies. When the user sets up the multistate structure, the treatment considered for strategy comparison should be included as one of the states, such as HSCT in CML. Among all the transitions, those ending with the treatment should be specified by the user, since they are not modeled stochastically by the multistate model and are treated as deterministic and strategy-driven. Also, the multistate model must include an absorbing state to reflect the time-to-event endpoint. For the deterministic transitions, the user can specify the start time of the treatment since the beginning state of the transitions, such as three months after entering CP, AP, and BP as examples in Results section.

Compared with prior microsimulation and decision-support tools, TxMicroSim addresses a specific methodological and practical gap: interactive comparison of user-defined treatment-timing strategies within a customizable multistate disease-progression framework. Recent microsimulation studies have evaluated treatment sequences in chronic disease settings [6] and transplantation timing in hematologic malignancies using multistate modeling and microsimulation, including HSCT timing in myelodysplastic syndromes [5, 7]. However, these studies were generally developed for specific clinical applications rather than as a generalizable, user-facing web tool. In parallel, several Shiny or web-based microsimulation tools have been developed for cardiovascular disease policy modeling [8], uncertainty in baseline risk [9], HIV transmission dynamics [10], multistate simulation design [11], schizophrenia economic evaluation [12], and public-health intervention impact assessment using proportional multistate lifetable simulation modeling [13]. These tools provide useful frameworks for their intended applications. In contrast, TxMicroSim allows users to define their own multistate disease structure, explicitly specify treatment-related deterministic transitions, fit or manually enter transition-specific hazard models, compare treatment-timing strategies through microsimulation, and summarize projected survival benefit using RMST. Thus, this study contributes a generalizable, web-based implementation that bridges multistate modeling, microsimulation, and RMST-based treatment-timing comparison in a form that can be used by clinical investigators without requiring them to develop disease-specific simulation code.

In addition, the multistate modeling in TxMicroSim is not restricted by the availability of data. The hazard ratios of covariates and regression parameters of the baseline hazard functions can be numerically inputted when no data is available for model fitting, or adjusted after the model fitting given the data. We also provide the option to upload more than one dataset to support the fitting of transition-specific hazard functions, which adds flexibility and helps increase the statistical power for specific transitions.

The real data application in Results section is an illustration of the Shiny app, which generates patient-specific, model-based RMST projections under prespecified treatment-timing strategies. In clinical practice, the best timing of HSCT for patients with CML remains debated. No single strategy is appropriate for all patients. The preferred timing of HSCT is guided by physicians’ clinical judgment and patient characteristics, such as age, disease stage, and performance status. Our Shiny app provides a personalized decision-support tool for comparing multiple treatment-timing strategies to identify the strategy associated with the largest projected RMST.

Limitations

The developed Shiny app TxMicroSim has several limitations. First, the RMST generated from the microsimulation based on the multistate model should be interpreted as model-based projections under user-specified treatment strategies, rather than causal estimates of the optimal treatment timing. In particular, HSCT entry is imposed by the specified strategy and is not estimated from the observed transitions. Causal interpretation would require additional assumptions, including adequate adjustment for treatment-selection factors, no unmeasured confounding, and correct specification of the transition-specific hazard models. Also, the projected RMSTs could be sensitive to user-specified modeling choices, including the multistate structure, the definition of strategy-driven treatment transitions, covariate selection, and the selected baseline hazard specification. In addition, when multiple datasets are pooled for transition-specific modeling, the validity of the projected RMSTs requires compatibility among the datasets. Last, when no datasets are uploaded, only the standard deviation of the RMST is displayed. To perform PSA, it would be a complex procedure for the user to input the covariance matrix of the regression parameters for the covariates and the baseline hazard functions.

In the CML application, some important confounders related to HSCT selection and post-HSCT outcomes were unavailable or incompletely captured. Therefore, the projected RMST differences should be interpreted with caution and should not be viewed as definitive evidence for modifying clinical practice or treatment guidelines. Rather, this application demonstrates how TxMicroSim can be used to compare prespecified strategies in a structured and interpretable way. Practice-changing conclusions would require up-to-date, high-quality datasets, adequate adjustment for treatment-selection factors and clinically relevant confounders, and external validation.

Future directions

Several directions merit future work. First, longitudinal biomarkers and time-dependent covariates could be incorporated to support dynamic prediction and update treatment-strategy comparison during follow-up. Second, the current data-pooling framework could be extended to improve robustness to differences in covariate distributions, covariate effects, and baseline hazard functions across data sources. For example, transfer-learning approaches could be implemented to facilitate data borrowing. Next, the PH assumption in the transition-specific hazard models for multistate modeling could be relaxed. Future work could incorporate transition-specific non-PH models and time-varying coefficients. Finally, our developed Shiny app can also be applied and extended to other disease settings involving aggressive but potentially curative treatments. As a web-based decision-support tool, it may help inform individualized treatment-timing decisions.

Conclusions

TxMicroSim is an R Shiny app that provides a flexible, user-friendly framework to help clinicians compare prespecified treatment-timing strategies in multistate disease progression using microsimulation based on multistate modeling. Treatment-timing strategies are compared using model-projected RMSTs.
