Psychotic episode concurrent with interaction with a large language model (llm): a case report
Authors: Ahmet Selim Başaran, Behcet Coşar
Published: 2026

Introduction

Large language model (LLM)-based chat systems generate responses with high fluency and contextual coherence, producing an interaction style that can feel “human-like.” Research on human-computer interaction (HCI) has established that people readily apply social rules to interactive systems. This tendency, originally described for desktop computers, appears to be intensified in conversational AI, where systems respond in real time, address the user directly, and sustain extended dialogue [1]. The theory of anthropomorphism predicts that when a system’s behavior appears meaningful and reciprocally interactive, the likelihood of attributing intention, affect, and agency to it increases [2].

In the phenomenology of psychotic disorders, persecutory delusions and ideas of reference are distinct but related phenomena. Persecutory delusions involve the conviction that one is being deliberately targeted, harmed, or conspired against [3]. Ideas of reference refer to the experience of perceiving neutral environmental events such as words, gestures, or coincidences as carrying special, personally directed meaning [3]. Both phenomena involve excessive personalization and threat attribution to environmental cues and are phenomenologically relevant to LLM interactions. Cognitive approaches highlight reasoning biases, notably jumping to conclusions (JTC), reduced belief flexibility, and attributional errors that sustain and amplify such meaning assignments [4, 5]. Meta-analytic evidence confirms that in psychosis, drawing confident inferences from limited data is reliably elevated [4]. This tendency is directly relevant to LLM interactions: fluent, authoritative-sounding text generated for a general audience may, in the presence of JTC, be rapidly recruited as confirmatory evidence for a pre-existing persecutory or referential schema. Early-episode samples also show interplay between JTC and social cognition [6]. Given that LLMs are designed to simulate socially responsive conversation, they may engage social-cognitive processing in ways that further amplify referential interpretation.

Kapur’s aberrant salience framework proposes that in psychosis, neutral stimuli acquire inappropriate significance, with dopaminergic dysregulation contributing to this misallocation of importance [7]. Subsequent work has reported heterogeneity in this association, yet evidence continues to accumulate that salience misattribution is a meaningful contributor to positive-symptom dimensions [8]. This framework is clinically applicable to text-centered interfaces: typographic emphasis, repeated headers, or lexically salient keywords such as “monitoring” or “permission” can be perceived as semantically important in ways that go beyond their intended neutral function, providing opportunities for delusional organization.

The relationship between digital media and psychotic content is not new: reports since the 1990s have described delusional themes linked to the internet and social networks [9–11], suggesting that the sociotechnical context can shape symptom content and enrich persecutory or referential themes. With the widespread adoption of generative artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots, this phenomenon has acquired a qualitatively new dimension. Recent commentaries have warned that the plausibility and dialogic continuity of such systems may trigger or exacerbate delusional content in individuals prone to psychosis [12, 13]. These concerns align with HCI and cognitive models: when anthropomorphic attributions amplify pre-existing JTC tendencies, a system designed to provide general guidance may be experienced as issuing personally directed instructions. A distinctive risk in this context is what may be termed “text-centered reading”—a pattern in which the individual selectively attends to specific words, phrases, or formatting features and interprets them as carrying a hidden personal meaning, distinct from the text’s intended general purpose.

Against this backdrop, detailed case narratives are needed to show how such reading patterns emerging in LLM interactions intersect with clinical psychopathology. Below we present a case in which neutral safety and privacy language within an LLM chat was incorporated into a pre-existing “being watched” schema, accelerating both referential and persecutory themes.

Case presentation

Patient history and baseline

A 33-year-old man presented to the Psychiatry Clinic of Ünye State Hospital. He had received a DSM-5 diagnosis of schizophrenia approximately ten years earlier. His first episode had featured prominent persecutory and referential themes alongside intermittent thought broadcasting, requiring two brief inpatient admissions. In subsequent years, the clinical picture had been dominated by negative symptoms, specifically social withdrawal and avolition, as well as attentional and working-memory difficulties that limited daily functioning. Over the preceding year, treatment with oral paliperidone 9 mg/day had produced partial remission of positive symptoms, with residual negative symptoms persisting. Pharmacy records and family reports confirmed consistent medication adherence. The patient described a persistent background sense of “being watched,” likened to “a low hum,” which was no longer markedly impairing his daily life. There had been no additional health-care contact in the six months prior to this presentation.

Note on data sources

The following account of ChatGPT interactions is based entirely on the patient’s retrospective self-report, obtained during the post-stabilization consent discussion. The characterization of the system’s responses as neutral or generic reflects the authors’ clinical judgment and is not based on independent analysis of chat logs, which were unavailable for privacy reasons.

LLM interactions and symptom escalation

Approximately twenty days before presentation, the patient began using ChatGPT to polish writing, summarize short texts, and conduct everyday information searches. Initial use was focused on practical and encyclopedic content; he described the experience as “practical and useful.” Over subsequent days he browsed the platform’s interface guides and privacy-security sections, initially without idiosyncratic interpretation. A shift occurred when his queries moved to security and surveillance topics.

In one session he asked, “How can I tell if there is a listening device at home?” The system responded with neutral, general advice: review microphone, camera, and location permissions on phone and apps; check Wi-Fi and network security; inspect for unknown devices; keep operating systems updated; use strong passwords. In a follow-up he asked, “Could my phone be monitored—how would I know?” and received similar template guidance, including limiting unfamiliar app permissions and seeking technical support if concerned. As described by the patient, and judged by the authors to be neutral guidance intended for a general audience, these statements were rapidly interpreted by him as personalized warnings: “The words ‘monitoring’ and ‘permission’ are there on purpose; that means my state file has been opened.” He perceived repetition of key terms and emphasized headers as “hidden signs” and described the texts as “secret instructions.”

Within days, ideas of reference widened beyond the chat interface. He began repeatedly inspecting small electronic devices and outlet and cable connections at home, and attempted to remove some outlet plates. He toggled microphone and location permissions on his phone multiple times. While walking outside, he grouped overheard words, particularly “tracking,” “record,” and “permission,” as evidence that “my file is being reviewed and my home is being bugged.” He perceived the system as an agent that was “indirectly telling me what to do,” interpreting general safety suggestions as warnings addressed specifically to him. Concurrently, he formed the belief that his medication “thickens my mind and makes me miss the signs,” and discontinued oral paliperidone. His family corroborated that he had stopped taking medication “to read the text more clearly” and “not miss the signs being sent.”

Day 0 assessment

The patient was alert, cooperative, and fully oriented. Mild psychomotor agitation was noted. Speech was fluent. Intermittent loosening of associations was present. Thought content was dominated by persecutory and referential themes: he was certain there was a listening device at home, that his phone was being monitored, and that chat texts contained “recorded confessions.” Auditory and visual hallucinations were denied, though he reported interpreting word repetitions as “secret signs.” Insight was markedly limited: “These messages are for me; otherwise the words wouldn’t line up so precisely.” The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) total score was 102 [14] and the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) score was 58 [15]. Vital signs were stable. Complete blood count, biochemistry panel, thyroid function, and C-reactive protein were all within normal limits. A brain MRI performed one year earlier had been normal; in the absence of new focal neurological signs, repeat imaging was deferred.

Management and follow-up

The initial consultation framed the patient’s current presentation explicitly within the context of his prior illness history. He was told that similar interpretations and beliefs had been part of previous relapses and that abrupt medication cessation had likely contributed to the current exacerbation. Brief structured reframing prompts were used throughout consultations, for example asking “Could this statement have been written for everyone?” to encourage him to consider the possibility that the texts were not personally directed. With good treatment adherence maintained through this collaborative approach, oral paliperidone was reinstated at the previously effective dose of 9 mg/day.

At Week 2, the intensity of persecutory interpretations had partially decreased and repetitive checking had abated; PANSS total 78, BPRS 46. At Week 4, improvement accelerated and he could articulate the possibility that “the texts may have been written for everyone”; PANSS total 58, BPRS 32. By the Week 8 visit, home checking loops had largely extinguished and daytime functioning had improved; PANSS total 50, BPRS 28. By Week 12, referential interpretations had become episodic and were more readily questioned by the patient himself; PANSS total 48, BPRS 26. Medication adherence remained good throughout follow-up; no dose adjustments were required due to adverse effects.

Ethics and reporting

After clinical stabilization, written informed consent for publication of anonymized details was obtained from the patient and his family. During the consent discussion, he recounted in detail the phrases he had read and the inferences he had drawn during the pre-presentation chat sessions; these accounts, together with medical records and time-stamped prescription entries, form the basis of this report. Institutional permission to use medical records for an anonymized case report was obtained.

Discussion

This case illustrates how neutral safety and privacy language within a highly fluent, second-person LLM chat may become incorporated into a pre-existing “being watched” schema, intensifying both referential and persecutory themes. Beginning with the patient’s own account and then situating it within established theoretical frameworks, we discuss the mechanisms that likely contributed to this process.

Research on human-computer interaction has consistently shown that people automatically apply social rules to interactive systems, without deliberate reflection [1]. Anthropomorphism theory further predicts increased attribution of intention and agency to coherent, responsive behavior [2]. Accordingly, a general suggestion such as “review permissions” was interpreted as “a warning about your file,” with the dialogue format, polite register, and second-person address reinforcing the patient’s sense that “this text is for me.” Social cognition research suggests that when an interactive system simulates the conversational features of a real person, including responsiveness, within-session memory, and an empathic tone, it may engage the same inferential processes used in interpreting human communicative intent [6]. This may explain why the patient represented the system as an agent with deliberate communicative purpose, a framing that accelerated referential interpretation.

In our patient, rapid extraction and aggregation of recurring lexical motifs such as “monitoring” and “permission” across multiple conversational turns, ambient speech, and home observations created a self-reinforcing cycle of confirmatory evidence. This pattern is consistent with a JTC-driven feedback loop [4, 5, 16]: each new occurrence of a salient keyword appeared to lower the evidential threshold further, while limited belief flexibility prevented disconfirmatory information from being incorporated. An additional factor specific to conversational AI systems, and one that distinguishes them from social media, television, or other digital media, is the architecture of the chat interface itself. ChatGPT and similar systems are designed to be persistently helpful, context-sensitive within a session, and to respond in natural, second-person language. When all interactions occur within the same chat thread, the system is implicitly cued to maintain topical consistency, returning repeatedly to themes of monitoring, security, and privacy when that is the established conversational context. This modus operandi creates an environment in which referentially salient content is continuously reinforced and presented with apparent communicative intent, a combination that is qualitatively different from passive consumption of digital media.

At the biology-phenomenology interface, Kapur’s aberrant salience model explains how neutral cues such as the words “monitoring” and “permission” can acquire inappropriate prominence and become the building blocks of delusional organization [7]. In this case, not only words but typographic and formatting features such as headers and bullet points were perceived as coded signals. General-population data on aberrant salience and psychotic experiences highlight heterogeneity and the role of underlying vulnerability dimensions [8]. When salience misattribution converges with misclassification of document purpose, that is, when general guidance is perceived as a personally addressed communication, the evidential standards required for delusional conviction may drop precipitously. Persecutory delusions and ideas of reference, while conceptually distinct, share this mechanism of excessive personalization [3]. In our patient, both phenomena co-occurred and mutually reinforced each other as the referential content of the LLM interactions became progressively incorporated into a broader persecutory framework.

The clinical course underscores the contribution of medication discontinuation. The temporal overlap between stopping paliperidone and the rise in positive symptoms suggests that deterioration likely reflects a convergence of digital triggers and pharmacological destabilization, rather than either factor alone. The stepwise reductions in PANSS and BPRS scores following reinstatement, alongside the progressive extinction of repetitive checking and the gradual restoration of belief flexibility, are consistent with re-established dopaminergic regulation facilitating more normative salience attribution. Historical scholarship has documented that core delusional themes persist across eras while their content adapts to the prevailing technological landscape [17, 18]; the present case represents a contemporary instance of this pattern.

Two practice-oriented implications follow. First, digital-use history, including whether patients use AI chatbots, for what purposes, and how they interpret the responses, should become a routine component of psychiatric assessment. Beyond classic probes about television- or radio-related referentiality, clinicians should ask specifically who patients believe the text is written by and for, and with what intent. This helps identify early tendencies to misclassify general guidance as personally directed messages in the context of highly fluent conversational interfaces. As illustrated in this case, such inquiry also provides a concrete foundation for subsequent reframing. Second, it is clinically useful to communicate explicitly and early in treatment that such mispersonalizations can be part of the illness’s relapse pattern and that abrupt medication discontinuation can amplify them. Brief structured reframing prompts used during consultations, for example “Could this statement be intended for everyone who uses this system?” can facilitate the patient’s own recognition that general-audience texts are not personally directed. These micro-interventions are low-cost, readily scalable, and consistent with evidence-based approaches targeting reasoning biases in psychosis.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This single-case design inherently limits causal inference: digital interaction content and timeline, treatment discontinuation, and any co-occurring psychosocial stressors were concurrent, making the separation of their respective contributions difficult. Full chat logs were unavailable; the account of LLM interactions relied on the patient’s retrospective self-report during a post-stabilization consent discussion. Nonetheless, the temporal sequence, the systematic misattribution of communicative intent and document purpose, the extraction of “hidden signs” from keywords and typographic features, the expansion of referential themes beyond the chat interface, the worsening following paliperidone cessation, and the stepwise improvement following reinstatement collectively provide strong phenomenological coherence. Future work should address several needs: development of assessment instruments specifically sensitive to AI-related referentiality, prospective longitudinal studies in psychosis-prone samples with systematic recording of digital media use, theoretical elaboration of the mechanisms by which conversational AI properties amplify salience misattribution, and clinical protocol development for structured LLM-use assessment in psychiatric practice.

In conclusion, this case illustrates how neutral safety and privacy statements generated by a large language model may, in the context of pre-existing psychotic vulnerability, be perceived as highly personalized and threatening communications, likely through the combined operation of anthropomorphic attributions, aberrant salience, and reasoning biases. LLMs differ from earlier forms of digital media in ways that may be particularly relevant to this risk: their persistent second-person address, context-sensitive session memory, always-available format, authoritative and empathic tone, and the capacity to repeatedly return to salient themes create an interactional environment distinct from passive media consumption. The patient’s symptom exacerbation was compounded by abrupt discontinuation of effective antipsychotic medication, and clinical recovery coincided with treatment reinstatement, psychoeducation, and structured reframing of the patient’s interpretations. These findings point to three practical priorities: routinely asking patients about AI chatbot use and how they interpret its output, explicitly linking such misinterpretations to the relapse cycle within the therapeutic dialogue, and ensuring uninterrupted access to effective antipsychotic treatment. Taken together, these steps may meaningfully reduce the risk of text-centered personalization and its contribution to relapse in psychosis.
