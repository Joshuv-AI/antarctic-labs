 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 Powder metallurgy hot isostatic pressing (PM-HIP) is a promising advanced
manufacturing technology for producing near-net-shape nuclear components.
However, despite tensile properties comparable to conventional wrought
materials, the reduced creep performance of PM-HIP materials remains a major
barrier to the qualification of the technology and materials for elevated-
temperature nuclear applications. This study investigated the microstructure,
tensile behavior, and creep performance of wrought and PM-HIP 316H subjected
to different post-HIP heat treatments to identify the root cause of the reduced
creep resistance. The results showed that PM-HIP 316H satisfied the ASME
tensile property requirements. In contrast, creep testing revealed significantly
shorter rupture life than wrought material. Microstructural characterization
identified retained powder-derived oxide particles along prior powder particle
boundaries as the primary microstructural feature responsible for the degraded
creep performance. Post-creep analysis demonstrated that these oxides promoted
creep cavity nucleation, cavity growth, and crack propagation, leading to
premature creep rupture. High-temperature solution annealing at 1300°C
promoted oxide coarsening and further reduced creep life, indicating that grain
growth alone is insufficient to improve creep resistance. By correlating the
microstructure characteristics to elevated-temperature mechanical properties, this
work identifies the root cause of reduced creep performance in PM-HIP 316H
and provides a mechanistic foundation for improving long-term performance
through powder conditioning and optimized post-HIP heat treatment. The results
established critical understanding of microstructural origins of creep behavior
that will support future code qualification of PM-HIP 316H for advanced nuclear
applications.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Powder metallurgy hot isostatic pressing (PM-HIP) is an advanced manufacturing process that
enables the fabrication of large, complex, near-net-shape metallic components for nuclear energy
applications [1-3]. Recognizing the potential of PM-HIP to transform the fabrication of nuclear
components, the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) has identified PM-HIP as one of the five
advanced manufacturing technologies that are the focus of ongoing regulatory and qualification efforts for
nuclear applications [4, 5], alongside laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), directed energy deposition (DED),
cold spray, and electron beam welding (EBW). Unlike conventional wrought processing, which relies on
multiple forging, rolling, machining, and welding operations, PM-HIP consolidates metal powders
directly into fully dense components through the simultaneous application of elevated temperature and
isostatic gas pressure. PM-HIP offers several manufacturing advantages, including high material
utilization, reduced fabrication steps, elimination of large weldments, shorter production lead times, and
the ability to manufacture geometrically complex components that are difficult or economically
impractical to fabricate using conventional methods. These advantages make PM-HIP particularly
attractive for advanced nuclear reactors. Although PM-HIP is a mature industrial manufacturing
technology that has been successfully applied in the aerospace, oil and gas, and power generation
industries, its application to nuclear structural components remains an active area of research [6, 7]. Over
the past decade, substantial international efforts have focused on evaluating PM-HIP materials for nuclear
applications [8-14]. Studies have demonstrated that PM-HIP can produce fully dense components with
homogeneous microstructures and mechanical properties that compare favorably with conventional
wrought products.
 Among the candidate structural materials for elevated-temperature nuclear systems, 316H stainless
steel is one of the most widely used and extensively qualified alloys. 316H has been selected for a variety
of structural components in advanced reactor concepts, including sodium-cooled fast reactors and molten
salt reactors. 316H is an American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) Code-qualified material for
elevated-temperature nuclear service, making it an ideal benchmark alloy for evaluating the capability of
PM-HIP technology. Demonstrating that PM-HIP 316H can achieve mechanical performance equivalent
to conventional wrought 316H is a critical step toward qualifying both the material and the manufacturing
process for nuclear applications.
 For 316H, previous investigations have demonstrated that PM-HIP 316H exhibited room-temperature
(RT) and elevated-temperature (ET) tensile strength higher than those of conventional wrought 316H due
to refined grains in PM-HIP 316H [8, 9]. Tensile testing demonstrated that PM-HIP 316H consistently
satisfied the minimum tensile property requirements specified by the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel
Code (BPVC). Similarly, low-cycle fatigue performance has been reported to be comparable between
PM-HIP and wrought 316H, indicating that the PM-HIP process does not adversely affect the material's
resistance to monotonic or low-cycle fatigue loading [9]. Despite these encouraging results, numerous
studies have consistently reported significantly reduced creep and creep-fatigue performance for PM-HIP
316H [12, 15, 16]. Under ET loading, PM-HIP 316H exhibited substantially shorter creep rupture life and
lower creep ductility than its wrought counterpart. Fractographic analyses of post-creep and post-creep-
fatigue specimens have revealed damage dominated by transgranular cracking [8], whereas low-cycle
fatigue specimens predominantly exhibited intergranular fracture, indicating that creep is the governing
damage mechanism during ET service. Because creep resistance governs the long-term structural integrity
and service life of high-temperature nuclear components, improving the creep performance of PM-HIP

 1

316H remains one of the primary challenges for the qualification of the material and the PM-HIP process
for nuclear applications.
 Previous studies have suggested that the degradation in creep performance is associated with
microstructural features unique to the powder metallurgy route, including prior particle boundaries
(PPBs) and retained oxide films [16]. During gas atomization, thin oxide films form naturally on the
surface of individual 316H powder particles. Because PM-HIP is a solid-state consolidation process, these
oxide films cannot be completely dissolved during HIP consolidation and instead become incorporated
into the consolidated material along PPBs. Although numerous studies have identified these oxide-
decorated PPBs as potential damage initiation sites during creep, the mechanisms by which these
microstructural features evolve during heat treatment and subsequently influence cavity nucleation, crack
propagation, and creep rupture remain incompletely understood. Furthermore, the interactions among
powder chemistry, post-HIP heat treatment, oxide morphology, grain structure, and long-term mechanical
performance especially creep behavior have not yet been systematically established. Addressing these
knowledge gaps is essential for developing robust qualification strategies for PM-HIP nuclear materials.
A mechanistic understanding of the relationships among powder characteristics, processing,
microstructure, and creep behavior is therefore essential for developing effective mitigation strategies and
supporting the code qualification of PM-HIP materials.
 The objective of the present study is therefore to understand the microstructural origins of reduced
creep properties of PM-HIP 316H to provide insights to improve the creep properties for code
qualification of the PM-HIP materials. This study investigated the microstructure, tensile properties, and
creep behavior of wrought 316H and PM-HIP 316H subjected to different post-HIP heat treatments.
Particular emphasis was placed on identifying the microstructural differences of 316H at different
conditions and correlating the microstructural characteristics to ET mechanical properties, particularly
creep performance. Creep behavior was investigated to reveal creep damage evolution from oxide
particles to cavity nucleation, cavity growth, and final fracture. Based on these observations, processing
strategies were proposed to mitigate oxide-assisted creep damage through powder conditioning and
optimized post-HIP heat treatment. The results provide a scientific foundation for developing powder-
process-microstructure-property relationships that will guide the development of next-generation PM-HIP
processing routes with improved long-term creep performance and support the qualification of PM-HIP
materials for high-temperature nuclear applications.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
 2.1. Powder Feedstock and HIP Parameters
 Commercially available 316H powder procured from Sandvik was used as the feedstock for the
fabrication of PM-HIP billets. The powder has a density of 7.91 g/cm3 and powder flow rate of 3.4 g/s.
Table 1 lists the chemical composition of the powder in comparison with the composition required by
ASME Section II, Part A for 316H stainless steel plate (S31609) [17], the composition for 316H required
by ASME BPVC Section III, Division 5 for high-temperature (>595°C) applications [18], and the
composition provided by the ASTM A988/A988M “Standard Specification for Hot Isostatically-Pressed
Stainless Steel Flanges, Fittings, Valves, and Parts for High Temperature Service” [19]. Compared to the
ASME requirements and powder used in previous PM-HIP studies[16], the Sandvik lot is characterized
by relatively high oxygen (0.028%) and nitrogen (0.027%) content. The high oxygen impurities in the
powder feedstock were expected to result in oxide inclusions in the final consolidated billets, thus
affecting the mechanical performance of the materials. Microstructural characterization of the as-received
powder revealed grain-boundary segregation of Cr, Mo, Mn, and Si, along with oxides and sulfide
precipitates enriched in Mo, Mn, and Si. Regions depleted in Ni and enriched in Cr and Mo were also
identified within powder particles, which is consistent with the presence of delta ferrite.

 The powder was loaded into a 316L stainless steel HIP can with a diameter of 65 mm and height of
100 mm. The powder was loaded into the HIP can with intermittent vibration to promote uniform packing
and minimize entrapped porosity. The HIP can was then evacuated to remove interstitial gas, outgassed
under vacuum to reduce moisture and adsorbed gas content, and seal-welded to produce a hermetic
assembly for HIP processing. The prepared HIP can was then processed in a PM-HIP furnace at 1160°C
and 103 MPa for 4 hours (As-HIPed condition), during which simultaneous heat and isostatic gas
pressure consolidated the powder into a fully dense billet. Figure 1a shows the temperature and pressure
history during HIP processing. The fabricated billet is shown in Figure 1b. The billet was scanned using a
3D scanner to obtain the STL model (Figure 1b). The billet had a shrinkage during HIP processing,
having a final diameter of ~62 mm and height of 95 mm.
 Besides the as-HIPed condition, two post-HIP solution annealing (SA) treatments were applied to
sectioned materials from the billet to evaluate the effect of annealing temperature on microstructure and
mechanical performance. SA1 was conducted at 1150°C for 4h followed by water quenching (WQ), while
SA2 was conducted at 1300°C for 4h followed by WQ. The intent of the two SA studies was to determine
whether evaluated solution annealing temperature could dissolve prior precipitates introduced during HIP
consolidation while balancing the associated grain growth.

 2.2. Mechanical Testing
 The as-fabricated PM-HIP billet was sectioned along its length into two halves as shown in Figure 2.
Two 2-mm thick slices at the center of the billet were reserved for microstructure characterization and
heat treatment study (Figure 2b). As shown in Figure 2b, ten cylindrical specimen blanks (numbered #1-
#10) were machined from the two halves to obtain materials for mechanical testing across the three
material conditions of interest: As-HIPed, SA1, and SA2. The allocation of specimens for tensile and
creep testing was denoted in Figure 2a. For solution annealed samples, the cylindrical blanks were heat
treated prior to machining of the mechanical testing specimens (Figures 3b and 3c). The tensile and creep
specimens have a gauge section diameter of 6.35 mm and a gauge length of 32 mm.

 Figure 2. (a) Specimen extraction plan for microstructure analysis and mechanical testing. (b) Image of
 the sectioned billet and the cylindrical blanks extracted from the billet.

 Tensile testing was performed at two temperatures to characterize both RT and ET (650°C) tensile
behaviors. Tensile testing was conducted using a 100 kN load cell with a controlled strain rage of 0.002/s
using an extensometer up to 20% of strain. After a 20% strain, the extensometer was removed and a
crosshead speed of 1.75 mm/min was used till the fracture of the specimens. Tensile properties (yield
strength (YS), ultimate tensile strength (UTS), and elongation) were evaluated and compared with the
ASME minimum RT tensile requirements for 316H (YS ≥205 MPa, UTS ≥ 515 MPa, and elongation ≥

40%). Creep testing was conducted at 650°C in air under an applied stress of 160 MPa. Prior to reaching
the target stress in creep testing, specimens were held under a preload of 15 MPa for an initial soak period
of 3 hours to allow thermal equilibration at the target temperature. Creep testing was conducted in
accordance with the ASTM E21 “Standard Test Methods for Elevated Temperature Tension Tests of
Metallic Materials” [20]. Creep specimens were run to rupture, with rupture life and creep strain recorded
for comparison across the As-HIPed, SA1, and SA2 conditions. The creep properties on PM-HIP 316H
obtained in this study were compared with those of prior PM-HIP billets [16], wrought 316H and EPRI
database [21]. The fracture surfaces and longitudinal sections were subsequently examined to identify the
dominant damage and failure mechanisms.

 2.3. Microstructure Characterization
 Microstructure examination was conducted on wrought 316H, as-HIPed and solution annealed PM-
HIP 316H, and specimens after tensile and creep testing. For microstructure characterization, the samples
were mounted, ground, and polished following standard metallographic procedures. Final polishing was
performed using 0.02 µm colloidal silica suspension in a vibratory polisher for ~4 hours. The prepared
samples were examined using an optical microscope (Keyence VTX 6000) and scanning electron
microscope (SEM, FEI Quanta 650). SEM in secondary and backscattered-electron modes was used to
image porosity, PPBs, and precipitate/oxide morphology and distribution, while energy-dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDS) was used to identify the composition of secondary phases/oxides, and to characterize
local elemental segregation and depletion. Grain size and grain boundary characters (fraction of high-
angle grain boundaries, HAGBs) were determined via electron-backscattered diffraction (EBSD). The
EBSD data were post-processed using Orientation Imaging Microscopy (OIM) Analysis software to
exclude datasets with an indexing confidence below 0.1. The samples after mechanical testing was etched
electrochemically using 10% oxalic acid at 3 V for ~30 seconds. The etched samples were examined
using an optical microscope to investigate crack propagation and fracture mode.

 3. RESULTS
 3.1. Microstructure
 Microstructural characterization was performed on wrought 316H and the PM-HIP billet to identify
the grain structure, precipitates, oxide inclusions, and residual porosity introduced during processing.
These microstructural features play a critical role in controlling the ET mechanical behavior of austenitic
stainless steels by influencing deformation mechanisms, grain boundary stability, and damage initiation
during long-term creep exposure. The primary objective of the microstructural characterization was
therefore to identify the distinguishing features between wrought and PM-HIP 316H that could explain
the reduced creep performance of PM-HIP material and provide guidance for developing mitigation
strategies. Figure 4 presents representative backscattered electron (BSE) images of wrought and PM-HIP
316H in the as-HIPed condition together with the two solution-annealed conditions (SA1 and SA2).
Significant differences in inclusion population, precipitate morphology, and grain boundary
characteristics were evident among the materials.
 The wrought 316H (Figure 4a) exhibited a clean, fully recrystallized equiaxed austenitic
microstructure with relatively uniform grain size and very limited second-phase particles. Only a small
number of isolated inclusions were observed throughout the matrix. Subsequent EDS characterization
identified these particles as isolated Mo/Mn-rich phases and Si/Mo-rich oxides. The absence of extensive
precipitation is consistent with conventional wrought processing, where melting and thermomechanical
processing effectively minimize oxide retention and promote chemical homogeneity. In contrast, the as-
HIPed material (Figure 4b) exhibited a substantially higher density of second-phase particles distributed
throughout the microstructure, with many decorating PPBs and grain boundaries. Besides the inclusions,
a small number of isolated pores were also observed. Compared with the wrought material, these
microstructural heterogeneities were significantly more numerous. To determine the chemistry of these
inclusions, EDS line scans and elemental mapping were performed, as shown in Figure 5. Two
representative inclusion types were identified. The smaller particles exhibited strong enrichment of Mn
and O (Figure 5b), indicating Mn-rich oxides. Larger inclusions showed simultaneous enrichment of Mn,
Mo, Si, and O (Figures 5c and 5d), indicating complex Mn-Mo-Si oxide particles. Elemental maps further
confirmed that oxygen is concentrated within these particles while the surrounding matrix remained
chemically uniform.
 The formation of these oxides originated from the atomized powder feedstock used during PM-HIP
processing. During gas atomization, each powder particle developed a thin oxide film on its surface due to
exposure to oxygen during production and handling. Because PM-HIP is a solid-state consolidation
process, these oxide films cannot be completely dissolved during HIP consolidation. Instead, the surface
oxides became trapped at the interfaces between neighboring powder particles, where they remained as
discrete oxide inclusions after consolidation. The relatively high oxygen content measured in the
feedstock powder (Table 1) further supported this mechanism. Similar oxide morphologies have been
reported in PM-HIP stainless steels and were generally recognized as an inherent consequence of
feedstock powder and HIP processing [16]. In addition to oxide inclusions, several isolated pores were
present within the as-HIPed material. These pores likely originated from incomplete elimination of
interparticle voids during HIP consolidation or localized gas entrapment between powder particles. Some
apparent pores may also result from pull-out of brittle oxide particles during metallographic polishing,
leaving cavities where inclusions were removed.
 The microstructure of 316H after SA1 (Figure 4c) remained similar to that of the as-HIPed condition.
Numerous oxide particles were still present throughout the microstructure, particularly along grain
boundaries, indicating that this heat treatment temperature was insufficient to substantially modify the
oxide population inherited from the powder feedstock. While some fine carbides formed during HIP
processing may partially dissolve during solution annealing, the oxide inclusions remained essentially
unchanged because of their high thermodynamic stability and extremely low solubility in the austenitic

 6

matrix. Consequently, SA1 produced only limited changes in inclusion morphology or distribution,
suggesting that conventional solution annealing temperatures were ineffective for eliminating the oxide-
related microstructural features associated with PM-HIP processing.
 316H after SA2 showed a different microstructure, as shown in Figure 4d. Most of the fine
precipitates observed in the as-HIPed and SA1 conditions were no longer visible, indicating that the
higher solution treatment temperature effectively dissolved secondary precipitates within the matrix.
However, despite the reduction in fine particles, several larger oxide particles remained. Compared with
the as-HIPed condition, the number density of oxide inclusions decreased, while their average size
increased. This observation suggested that the elevated solution annealing temperature promoted
diffusion-driven coarsening of the stable oxide particles through particle coalescence and Ostwald
ripening, resulting in fewer but significantly larger inclusions. Due to the strong atomic-number contrast
under BSE imaging, these oxide particles appeared as dark features against the surrounding matrix. EDS
analysis identified the remaining coarse particles as Si/Mn-rich oxides together with Fe/Cr-rich oxides,
indicating that although the high-temperature treatment effectively removed most matrix precipitates, the
thermodynamically stable oxide inclusions persisted and underwent significant coarsening rather than
dissolution. These observations established the microstructural basis for evaluating how oxide inclusions
influenced the subsequent high-temperature mechanical behavior of PM-HIP 316H.

 Figure 6 shows the EBSD inverse pole figure (IPF) orientation maps and corresponding grain size
distributions of the wrought 316H and PM-HIP 316H under different heat treatment conditions. The grain
boundary maps with quantified high-angle grain boundary (HAGB) fractions are presented in Figure 7.
The wrought 316H (Figures 6a1 and 6a2) exhibited a coarse and heterogeneous equiaxed grain structure
with an average grain size of 39.9 ± 38.1 μm (ASTM grain size number 6). This grain size is consistent
with the UNS S31609 316H specification, which typically requires a grain size of ASTM No. 7 or coarser
for high-temperature service applications. In contrast, the as-HIPed material exhibited a substantially
refined microstructure (Figures 6b1 and 6b2) with an average grain size of 10.8 ± 8.4 μm (ASTM grain
size number 10). This pronounced grain refinement is attributed to the powder metallurgy route, where
each powder particle served as an individual nucleation region during HIP consolidation, producing a fine
equiaxed grain structure after consolidation. Furthermore, the large population of undissolved carbides
and oxide inclusions inherited from the powder particle surfaces (Figure 4b) effectively restricted grain
boundary migration during HIP processing, further suppressing grain growth.
 Solution annealing at 1150°C for 4 h (SA1) resulted in only limited microstructural evolution
(Figures 6c1 and 6c2). The average grain size increased slightly to 12.6 ± 8.6 μm. This observation is
consistent with the SEM characterization (Figure 4c), which showed that a high density of oxide
inclusions and undissolved carbides remained after SA1. These thermally stable particles exerted a strong
pinning effect on migrating grain boundaries, thereby inhibiting grain boundary motion and preventing
significant grain coarsening. A markedly different grain structure developed after SA2 (Figure 6d1 and
6d2). The average grain size increased to 34.9 ± 22.2 μm, approaching that of the wrought material. This
significant grain coarsening is consistent with the microstructural observations presented in Figure 4d,
where most fine precipitates have dissolved following the higher-temperature solution treatment.

 Grain boundary characters also evolved with heat treatment, as shown in Figure 7. The wrought
material contained 89.1% HAGBs, whereas the HAGB fraction increased to 92.8% in the as-HIPed
condition, 93.9% after SA1, and 95.1% after SA2. Although the increase in HAGB fraction was relatively
modest, the trend indicates progressive transformation of low-angle boundaries into high-angle
boundaries during high-temperature solution annealing through recovery and recrystallization processes.
The combined grain size and grain boundary characteristics have important implications for the ET
behavior of PM-HIP 316H. The as-HIPed and SA1 conditions contained both a very fine grain structure
and a high fraction of HAGBs, resulting in a substantially greater grain boundary area per unit volume
than the wrought material. Since HAGBs serve as preferred pathways for diffusion-controlled
deformation, grain boundary sliding, and creep cavity nucleation [22, 23], the increased density of
HAGBs is expected to increase the susceptibility of the material to grain-boundary-controlled creep
damage. Although the SA2 treatment significantly reduced the total grain boundary area through grain
coarsening, the HAGB fraction remained higher than that of the wrought material. Consequently, while
high-temperature solution annealing partially restored the grain structure toward that of conventional
wrought 316H, important differences in grain boundary character remained. The grain boundary
characteristics and oxide inclusions established the foundation for understanding the differences in creep
deformation and rupture behavior observed in mechanical testing.

 3.2. Tensile Behavior

 RT and ET (650°C) tensile tests were performed to evaluate the influence of the PM-HIP process and
post-HIP heat treatment on tensile properties of 316H and to determine whether the PM-HIP materials
satisfy the tensile property requirements specified by the ASME Code. Representative engineering stress-
strain curves are presented in Figure 8, while the corresponding YS, UTS, and total elongation are
summarized in Figure 9. At RT, the as-HIPed 316H exhibited an YS of 284 MPa, compared with 311
MPa for the wrought material and 274 MPa for the SA1 condition (Figure 9a). The relatively high YS of
the as-HIPed condition is consistent with its refined grain structure (Figure 6), where grain boundary
strengthening contributed to increased resistance against dislocation motion. Following SA1, the YS
decreased slightly because of limited grain growth during heat treatment. Despite this reduction, the YS
remained above the minimum ASME requirement. The UTS was also comparable for all three materials,
ranging from 635 to 653 MPa (Figure 9a). All materials exhibited excellent tensile ductility, with
elongations exceeding 53% (Figure 9b). These results demonstrate that neither the oxide inclusions
introduced during powder consolidation nor the fine grain structure adversely affect the short-term tensile
deformation behavior.
 The ET tensile behavior at 650°C is shown by the dashed stress-strain curves in Figure 8, with the
corresponding mechanical properties summarized in Figures 9c and 9d. The PM-HIP materials exhibited
tensile properties comparable to those of the wrought 316H. The as-HIPed 316H exhibited an YS of 147
MPa and UTS of 387 MPa, while the SA1 material showed an YS of 151 MPa and UTS of 393 MPa. The
as-HIPed and SA1 316H revealed tensile strength closely matching the wrought material. All the
materials exhibited an elongation over 43%. The tensile results demonstrated that both the RT and ET
tensile properties were essentially equivalent to those of the wrought material.
 Representative fracture surfaces following RT tensile testing are shown in Figure 10. All three
materials exhibited fracture surface characteristics of ductile failure, consisting of equiaxed dimples
formed through microvoid nucleation, growth, and coalescence. The as-HIPed material (Figure 10b)
exhibited a ductile fracture surface. However, higher magnification examination revealed numerous
ridge-like features distributed across the fracture surface in the as-HIPed material, as indicated by the
arrows in Figure 10b. These ridges were absent from the wrought material and were believed to
correspond to preferential crack propagation along decorated PPBs. During HIP consolidation, stable
oxide inclusions inherited from the powder surfaces remained concentrated along portions of the PPBs.
Under tensile loading, these oxide-decorated interfaces locally reduced interparticle cohesion and
provided favorable paths for crack propagation. Consequently, fracture proceeded through a combination
of ductile microvoid coalescence and localized crack growth along decorated PPBs, producing the
characteristic ridge morphology while maintaining an overall ductile fracture appearance.
 Following SA1, the fracture surface became considerably more homogeneous (Figure 10c). The
ridge-like features observed in the as-HIPed condition were largely eliminated, and the fracture
morphology more closely resembled that of the wrought material. SA2 partially dissolved carbides and
resulted in limited grain boundary migration disrupting the continuity of decorated PPBs. As a result,
crack propagation became less constrained by the PPB network.
 The fracture behavior of as-HIPed and SA1 316H after testing at 650°C is shown in Figure 11.
Optical microscopy of longitudinal sections (Figures 11a and 11c) revealed extensive necking and large
plastic strains prior to fracture for both the as-HIPed and SA1 conditions. The fracture path remained
predominantly transgranular. SEM examination of the fracture surfaces (Figures 11b and 11d) showed
deep equiaxed dimples formed by extensive microvoid coalescence. The tensile fractography
demonstrated that both RT and ET failure of PM-HIP 316H was dominated by ductile microvoid
coalescence, despite the presence of oxide inclusions and decorated PPBs. Although the as-HIPed

condition exhibited localized crack propagation along PPBs, these microstructural features did not
significantly degrade the short-term tensile ductility.

 3.3. Creep Behavior
 Creep strain-time curves of the SA1 and SA2 PM-HIP 316H tested at 650°C and 160 MPa are shown
in Figure 12. The corresponding rupture life and total creep strain are summarized in Figure 13. The SA1
condition exhibited an enhanced creep performance compared to SA2 condition. A creep life of 436 h
was measured for SA1 specimen, whereas the SA2 specimen failed at 292 h, representing a reduction in
rupture life of 33%. In addition to the longer rupture life, SA1 accumulated substantially more plastic
strain during creep, reaching 29.4% total creep strain prior to failure, whereas SA2 fractured with a 14.9%
creep strain (Figure 13b). These results indicate that although the 1300°C solution treatment promoted
grain growth and dissolved most secondary precipitates, it did not improve the long-term creep resistance
of the PM-HIP material. Compared with previously published data for wrought 316H tested under
identical conditions, both SA1 and SA2 heat treatment conditions exhibited substantially shorter rupture
lives than conventional wrought 316H. Published rupture life for wrought 316H under 650°C and 160
MPa is approximately 1000~3,000 h [16, 21]. Wrought 316H also exhibited a rupture strain exceeding
40%. Compared with the literature data on PM-HIP materials [16] (Billets 2A and 2B shown in Figure
12), the SA1 specimen exhibited a slightly improved creep life and strain than these 2A and 2B billets.
Both billet 2A and 2B exhibited rupture life shorter than wrought 316H, demonstrating that the reduced
creep resistance is consistently observed across multiple PM-HIP 316H materials.
 These observations contrast sharply with the tensile results presented in the Section 3.2. While the
PM-HIP materials exhibited RT and ET tensile properties comparable to wrought 316H and fully satisfied
the ASME tensile requirements, their creep rupture lives were reduced significantly under identical creep
conditions. This discrepancy demonstrated that the microstructural features introduced during powder
consolidation have a relatively minor influence on short-term monotonic deformation but strongly affect
long-term creep damage accumulation.

 4. DISCUSSION

 4.1. Root Cause of Accelerated Creep Damage in PM-HIP 316H
 PM-HIP 316H exhibited tensile properties equivalent to wrought 316H at both RT and 650°C.
However, the creep rupture life of PM-HIP 316H was significantly reduced compared to wrought
material. The degradation in creep performance was not attributed to inadequate tensile strength or
ductility but originated from the unique microstructural features introduced during powder consolidation,
particularly the fine stable oxide particles inherited from the powder surfaces. The microstructural
observations presented in Figures 4–7 show that the PM-HIP 316H contained numerous Mn-Cr-rich and
Mn-Mo-Si-rich oxide particles that were absent in the wrought material. These oxide particles remained
after HIP consolidation because of the solid-state processing. The oxide films were trapped along PPBs,
where they formed a discontinuous network of brittle inclusions throughout the consolidated billet.
Unlike short-duration tensile loading, creep deformation proceeded through diffusion-assisted
deformation mechanisms. Consequently, the oxide particles became preferential sites for local stress
concentration and vacancy accumulation during long-term exposure. The observations shown in Figures
14 and 15 provide direct evidence of crack initiation at grain boundaries and PPBs.
 Figure 16 summarizes the evolution of creep damage in the PM-HIP material based on the
experimental observations. In the as-HIPed 316H (Figure 16a), the microstructure contained numerous
isolated oxide particles distributed along grain boundaries and PPBs. These oxide particles are
mechanically incompatible with the surrounding austenitic matrix. The as-HIPed material also contained
refined grains. A low heat treatment temperature (SA1) was insufficient to dissolve these oxide particles
and promote substantial grain growth. While a higher heat treatment temperature (SA2) was able to lead

 16

to sufficient grain growth, the fine oxide particles became accumulated (Figure 16b) due to grain
boundary motion. As creep deformation proceeded (Figure 16c), local stress concentrations developed
around the oxide particles because of the mismatch in deformation between the rigid oxides and the
surrounding matrix. Small cavities began to nucleate preferentially at the oxide/matrix interfaces. Higher
magnification observations shown in Figure 16c provide direct evidence for this damage process. Oxide
particles exhibited cracking and interfacial debonding from the surrounding matrix (Figure 16c).
Microvoids initiated adjacent to the fractured oxide particles, demonstrating that the oxide/matrix
interface served as a preferential nucleation site for creep cavities. With continued exposure, neighboring
cavities progressively linked together to form microcracks. Because many oxide particles remained along
PPBs, cavity coalescence followed these oxide-decorated paths, producing interconnected crack
networks. Eventually, these cracks propagated through the microstructure and linked with neighboring
damaged regions, leading to extensive intergranular cracking upon rupture (Figure 16d).
 SA2 specimen had a shorter creep life and strain compared to SA1 specimen, indicating heat
treatment significantly affected creep rate and damage accumulation. Following SA1, the microstructure
retained a large amount of fine oxide particles with fine grains. Although these oxide particles provided
numerous potential cavity nucleation sites, their relatively small size limited the local stress concentration
generated at individual particles. Consequently, cavity nucleation occurred more gradually, allowing the
specimen to accumulate 29.4% creep strain before rupture. In contrast, SA2 substantially altered the oxide
population and size. The thermodynamically stable oxides coarsened through particle accumulation and
growth. As a result, the remaining particles became significantly larger although the density of oxide
particles decreased. The larger oxide particles generated more severe local stress concentrations during
creep. Figure 15a clearly show oxide cracking, interfacial debonding, and cavity formation directly
adjacent to these coarse oxide particles. Once initiated, cavities rapidly propagated along the oxide-
decorated grain boundaries, causing earlier transition into tertiary creep and significantly reduced rupture
strain. This observation also explained why grain growth alone did not improve creep life. Although SA2
produced grain sizes approaching the grain size of wrought 316H, the detrimental effect of coarse oxide
particles dominated the creep response.

 4.2. Strategies for Improved Creep Performance
 The present results demonstrated that improving creep performance requires modification of the
oxide population rather than simply optimizing grain size through heat treatments. To improve creep
properties of PM-HIP 316H, powder quality control is an important mitigation strategy. Since the oxide
particles originated directly from the atomized powder surfaces, reducing the oxygen content of the
feedstock powder should proportionally decrease the amount of oxide retained after HIP consolidation.
This reduction may be achieved through improved powder atomization and atmosphere control during
powder production, or powder conditioning processes that reduce surface oxides prior to consolidation.
Optimization of powder conditioning together with HIP parameters offers the most promising route
toward producing PM-HIP 316H with a cleaner grain boundary structure. By minimizing oxide size,
reducing oxide density, and eliminating continuous oxide networks, the dominant creep damage
mechanism due to oxides at grain boundaries can be substantially suppressed, enabling PM-HIP materials
to achieve creep performance approaching that of wrought 316H. Additionally, controlling post-HIP heat
treatment to prevent oxide coarsening while promotes sufficient grain growth is also important to
enhancing creep performance. Future work will focus on integrating powder conditioning, PM-HIP
processing, and post-HIP heat treatment to establish powder-process-microstructure-property
relationships and develop PM-HIP 316H with creep resistance approaching that of wrought material,
thereby supporting its code qualification for nuclear applications.

 5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
 This study investigated the microstructure, tensile behavior, and creep performance of PM-HIP 316H
in comparison with conventional wrought 316H to identify the microstructural origins of the reduced
creep resistance commonly observed in PM-HIP materials. The following conclusions can be obtained
from this study.
1. The PM-HIP 316H exhibited tensile properties equivalent to wrought 316H. Both the as-HIPed and
 solution-annealed PM-HIP materials satisfied the ASME minimum tensile property requirements.
 Yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and elongation were comparable to those of wrought 316H.
2. The primary microstructural difference between PM-HIP and wrought 316H is the presence of oxide
 inclusions inherited from the powder feedstock. Microstructure characterization identified Mn-Cr-rich
 and Mn-Mo-Si-rich oxide particles distributed primarily along grain boundaries. These oxides
 originated from the surface oxide films present on the atomized powder and were retained during
 solid-state HIP consolidation.
3. Solution annealing significantly modified the grain structure but did not eliminate the oxide
 inclusions. Solution annealing at 1150°C produced only limited grain growth. Solution annealing at
 1300°C promoted substantial grain growth, but the stable oxide particles remained and underwent
 significant coarsening.
4. Despite their excellent tensile properties, PM-HIP materials exhibited substantially reduced creep
 resistance compared with wrought 316H. The reduced creep performance was governed by oxide-
 assisted creep damage. Post-creep microscopy demonstrated that creep damage initiated by oxide
 cracking and oxide/matrix interfacial debonding, followed by cavity nucleation, cavity growth, cavity
 coalescence, and eventual intergranular crack propagation. The retained oxide particles therefore
 acted as preferential sites for creep damage accumulation and are identified as the primary root cause
 of the reduced creep rupture life of PM-HIP 316H.
5. Solution annealing at 1300°C did not improve creep performance. Although this treatment produced
 grain sizes approaching those of wrought 316H, it promoted coarsening of the remaining oxide
 particles, resulting in earlier cavity formation and shorter creep life. These results demonstrate that

 18

 optimizing grain size alone cannot improve creep performance in PM-HIP 316H. The size,
 morphology, and distribution of retained oxide particles are the dominant factors governing long-term
 creep resistance.
Future work will focus on developing processing strategies that minimize oxide-related creep damage.
The primary research directions include powder conditioning and feedstock optimization. Powder
conditioning techniques will be investigated to reduce oxygen content and modify the surface oxide films
on the feedstock powder prior to HIP consolidation. Hydrogen treatment will be carried out on the
powder feedstock to reduce oxides in powders. The objective is to decrease the quantity, size, and
continuity of retained oxide particles in the consolidated material. Post-HIP heat treatment will also be
explored to control grain growth and precipitate evolution while minimizing oxide coarsening. Advanced
characterization techniques and fractographic analysis will be employed to quantify the evolution of grain
structure and oxide particles. Mechanical performance evaluation including tensile, creep, and creep-
fatigue testing will be conducted on materials produced under different powder treatment conditions and
heat treatment conditions. The mechanical testing results will be correlated with the corresponding
microstructural features to establish powder-process-microstructure-property relationships. Based on the
relationships, the ultimate objective of the research is to develop powder conditioning and PM-HIP
processing strategies that suppress oxide-assisted cavity nucleation and crack propagation, thereby
producing PM-HIP 316H with creep performance matching that of conventional wrought 316H while
retaining the manufacturing and near-net-shape advantages of the PM-HIP process for ASME code
qualification of PM-HIP technology.
