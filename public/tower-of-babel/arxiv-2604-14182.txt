Cellwise Outliers
Mia Hubert, Jakob Raymaekers, Peter J. Rousseeuw

Abstract
In statistics and machine learning, the traditional meaning of the terms ‘outlier’ and ‘anomaly’ is a case in the dataset that behaves differently from the bulk of the data. This raises suspicion that it may belong to a different population. But nowadays increasing attention is being paid to so-called cellwise outliers. These are individual values somewhere in the data matrix (or data tensor). Depending on the dimension, even a relatively small proportion of outlying cells can contaminate over half the cases, which is a problem for existing casewise methods. It turns out that detecting cellwise outliers as well as constructing cellwise robust methods requires techniques that are quite different from the casewise setting. For instance, one has to let go of some intuitive equivariance properties. The problem is difficult, but the past decade has seen substantial progress. For high-dimensional data the cellwise approach is becoming dominant, and typically can deal with missing values as well. We review developments in the estimation of location and covariance matrices as well as regression methods, principal component analysis, methods for tensor data, and various other settings.



Keywords:

Anomaly detection; Breakdown value;
Multivariate statistics; Robustness;
Tensor
data.

1
INTRODUCTION

Real-world data often contains elements
that do not follow the pattern
suggested by the majority of the data.
Such outliers may be gross errors with
the potential to severely distort a
statistical analysis, but they may also
be valuable pieces of information that
warrant further inspection. But whatever
their cause, we need to be able to detect
them.

The approach of robust statistics,
as formalized by e.g.
Huber 1964
and

Hampel et al. 1986
has been to first
obtain a robust fit, i.e. a model that fits
the majority of the data well, and then to
look for cases that deviate from that fit.
An important assumption underlying this
approach is that of casewise contamination,
in which a case is either an outlier or
free of any contamination.
(This is sometimes called rowwise
contamination, because cases are often
encoded as rows of the data matrix,
but the name casewise is more general.)
Different formalizations of casewise
contamination exist, but the most common
one is to assume that the observed data
was generated from
a clean distribution
F
with probability

1-\varepsilon>0.5
and from an arbitrary
distribution
H
with probability

\varepsilon<0.5
. This is known as
the
Tukey-Huber contamination model
.
A commonly used formulation is

X_{\varepsilon}\sim F_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq(1-\varepsilon)F+\varepsilon H
.
The goal is to estimate the characteristics
of
F
given that we only observe

F_{\varepsilon}
and, importantly,
we don’t assume anything about
H
.
The casewise contamination model can
equivalently be written as

X_{\varepsilon}=A^{\text{\scriptsize{case}}}\odot X+(\bm{1}_{d}-A^{\text{\scriptsize{case}}})\odot Z

(1)

where the Hadamard product
\odot
multiplies
vectors (and matrices) entry by entry, and

\bm{1}_{d}
is a column vector with all

d
components equal to
1
. We assume that

X
,
A^{\text{\scriptsize{case}}}
and
Z
are mutually
independent, with
X\sim F
and
Z\sim H
.
The
d
-variate variable
A^{\text{\scriptsize{case}}}
has
Bernoulli distributed marginals
A_{j}^{\text{\scriptsize{case}}}

for
j=1,\ldots,d
with success parameter

1-\varepsilon^{\text{\scriptsize{case}}}
, that are
fully
dependent
in the sense that

P(A_{1}^{\text{\scriptsize{case}}}=\ldots=A_{d}^{\text{\scriptsize{case}}})=1
.

Alqallaf et al. 2009
call this the

fully dependent contamination model
.
It implies that on average,

(1-\varepsilon^{\text{\scriptsize{case}}})100\%
of the cases are clean.
The left panel of Figure
1

visualizes this setting for a toy data set with
15 cases and 10 variables. Here 3 out of the 15
cases (20%) are casewise outliers.

Figure 1:
Illustration of the casewise, cellwise,
and mixed contamination models. Black means
outlying, and white cells are missing.

Casewise robust methods require that at least
half of the cases are entirely uncontaminated.
This assumption is often realistic in
low-dimensional datasets, but less so
in high dimensions because then there are many
variables in which something can go wrong.
Moreover, casewise robust methods work by
downweighting or deleting all variables of the
outlying cases, whereas their outlying behavior
might occur in only a few measurements. This
has motivated the study of
cellwise
outliers
. These are deviating measurements
(cells) that can occur anywhere in the data
matrix.

The
cellwise contamination model

assumes that the data are generated according to

X_{\varepsilon}=A^{\mbox{\scriptsize{cell}}}\odot X+(\bm{1}_{d}-A^{\mbox{\scriptsize{cell}}})\odot Z

(2)

with
X
and
Z
as in (
1
).
The
d
-variate variable
A^{\mbox{\scriptsize{cell}}}
has
Bernoulli components
A_{j}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{cell}}}
with
success probability
1-\varepsilon^{\mbox{\scriptsize{cell}}}_{j}
.
If the components of
A^{\mbox{\scriptsize{cell}}}
are

independent
, we obtain the

fully independent contamination
model
of
Alqallaf et al. 2009
.
Then on average each
variable has
(1-\varepsilon^{\mbox{\scriptsize{cell}}}_{j})100\%
clean
values, but even a relatively small proportion
of outlying cells can contaminate over half
the cases, which may cause casewise robust
methods to fail.
The middle panel of
Figure
1
illustrates
how a dataset with
22/150\approx 15\%
of
outlying cells yields only

4/15\approx 27\%
entirely uncontaminated
cases. It also illustrates that a case can
have zero, one, a few, or many outlying
cells. In general, the probability that a
case contains at least one outlying cell is

1-(1-\varepsilon^{\mbox{\scriptsize{cell}}})^{d}
which grows quickly
with the dimension
d
. For instance, when

\varepsilon^{\mbox{\scriptsize{cell}}}=0.05
and
d=14

this probability is 51%. It increases to
97% when
d=70
, thus impacting the vast
majority of cases.

In real data, both types of outliers can
occur simultaneously, and some values
might be missing. The

mixed contaminated and partially
observed model
of
Centofanti et al. 2026
says
that

X_{\varepsilon}=A\odot X+(\bm{1}_{d}-A)\odot Z

(3)

where
A=A^{\text{\scriptsize{case}}}\odot A^{\mbox{\scriptsize{cell}}}\odot A^{\mbox{\scriptsize{obs}}}
, the variable
A^{\text{\scriptsize{case}}}
is
defined as in formula (
1
), and

A^{\mbox{\scriptsize{cell}}}
as in (
2
).
The entries
A_{j}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{obs}}}
in
A^{\mbox{\scriptsize{obs}}}
are
binary variables with possible outcomes 1
and NA, and

P(A_{j}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{obs}}}=1)=1-\varepsilon^{\mbox{\scriptsize{obs}}}_{j}
.
The right panel of
Figure
1
shows a toy
example.

Different assumptions on the dependence
structure of
X
,
Z
,
A^{\text{\scriptsize{case}}}
,

A^{\mbox{\scriptsize{cell}}}
and
A^{\mbox{\scriptsize{obs}}}
lead to different
contamination models. For instance, when

A^{\mbox{\scriptsize{obs}}}
is independent of the other
variables, the values in the dataset are
missing completely at random.

Long before the notion of cellwise
outliers appeared in print in

Alqallaf et al. 2009
, it was
discussed informally by some.
One of us remembers clearly that the
topic was brought up in the early
eighties in seminar talks by Alfio
Marazzi and Werner Stahel at ETH
Zürich. It was presented as an open
problem, with the formula

1-(1-\varepsilon^{\mbox{\scriptsize{cell}}})^{d}
. At that
time the available tools appeared
insufficient to address this
challenge.

The remainder of the paper is organized
as follows. Section
2

gives an overview of methods to detect
outlying cells. Section
3

surveys the work on cellwise robust
estimation of multivariate location and
covariance matrices, and
Section
4
reviews the work
on regression. Section
5

looks at methods specifically designed for
high-dimensional data, and tensors are
treated in Section
6
.
Section
7
summarizes work
in other statistical settings, and
Section
8
concludes.

2
DETECTING CELLWISE OUTLIERS

The detection of cellwise outliers is
not easy. A natural approach is to
look at each individual variable, and
identify marginal outliers through,
for example, robust z-scores. A more
refined version of this principle is
the filter introduced by

Gervini and Yohai 2002
. When variables
are asymmetric, we can first apply the
robust transformation towards symmetry
constructed by

Raymaekers and Rousseeuw 2024b
.

Detecting cellwise outliers by only
looking at marginal distributions has
some evident drawbacks. One can easily
imagine cellwise outliers that need not
be marginally outlying, especially
in multivariate distributions with
substantial correlations between the
variables.
Consider for instance the TopGear dataset
from the R package
robustHD

(
Alfons 2022
)
. It contains technical
information of 297 cars such as their height,
weight, acceleration, fuel consumption,
horsepower, Miles Per Gallon (MPG) etc.
The Ssangyong Rodius was reported with an
acceleration time of 0 seconds, indicating
that it achieves 60 mph instantly. This was
obviously an error, and it can easily be
identified from the marginal distribution
as seen in the bottom left panel of
Figure
2
. On the
other hand, the Fiat 500 Abarth appears to
have a small width, but not marginally. The
small width only becomes apparent when
considering the car’s other features, such
as Top speed and Acceleration, as seen in
the panels on the left.

Figure 2:
Left: some variables of the Top
Gear data, illustrating that cellwise
outliers need not be marginally outlying.
Right: DDC cellmap of these data.

As a result, several proposals
have sought to incorporate multivariate
information into the detection of cellwise
outliers. For example,
Leung et al. 2017

extended the Gervini-Yohai filter to
a bivariate filter in the context of
estimating location and scatter.

Saraceno and Agostinelli 2021
further extended
the approach to more than two dimensions.

A popular approach for this purpose
is the Detect Deviating Cells (DDC)
algorithm of
Rousseeuw and Van den Bossche 2018

that is available in the
R

package
cellWise

(
Raymaekers and Rousseeuw 2022
)
. For each variable,
DDC fits robust simple linear regressions
on the other variables in the multivariate
data set. By combining the predictions
from these regressions, DDC obtains
predictions for each variable.
The difference between these predictions
and the observed values then yield
cellwise residuals, based on which
cellwise outliers can be identified.

The result can be visualized in a
cellmap as shown in the right panel of
Figure
2
,
displaying a part of the Top Gear
data. The blue cells have a negative
residual, that is, they have a lower
value than expected based on the
other variables. The red cells have a
positive residual. We see that the DDC
method does flag the width of the
Fiat 500 Abarth, even though it is not
marginally outlying. The DDC method has
been applied in epidemiology

(
Viviani et al. 2023
)
,
geochemistry
(
Grygar et al. 2024
)
,
archaeology
(
Santos 2020
;
Leggett 2021
)
,
and other fields. It also serves as a
starting value for many iterative
algorithms for cellwise robust estimation
of more complex models.

These early proposals of detecting
cellwise outliers can and have been
used as preprocessing before
applying traditional statistical
methods. Unfortunately, this makes
the process of identifying cellwise
outliers oblivious to the statistical
model that is being fit afterward.
This deviates
from the established approach in
traditional robust statistics of
detecting outliers with respect
to a robust fit.
As we will see, recent advances
in cellwise robust statistics are
moving in this direction,
where the detection of outliers
is naturally paired with the model
fitting. This typically
leads to more accurate detection
of harmful cellwise outliers, as
the influence of these outliers
depends on the model being fit.

3
MULTIVARIATE LOCATION AND
COVARIANCE MATRICES

Suppose we are given a data matrix
\bm{X}
with

n
observations in
d
dimensions. In this
section we consider the estimation of a location
vector and a covariance matrix when
d<n
.
In casewise robust statistics, the traditional
goal is to develop estimators for location and
covariance that are affine equivariant. More
precisely, consider any affine transformation

\bm{x}\mapsto\bm{v}+\bm{A}\bm{x}
where
\bm{x}
and

\bm{v}
are in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}
and

\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is nonsingular.
The location estimator
\bm{\widehat{\mu}}
and covariance
estimator
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}
are called affine
equivariant when

\displaystyle\bm{\widehat{\mu}}(\bm{1}_{n}\bm{v}^{\bm{\top}}+\bm{X}\bm{A})=

\displaystyle\bm{v}+\bm{A}\bm{\widehat{\mu}}(\bm{X})

(4)

\displaystyle\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}(\bm{1}_{n}\bm{v}^{\bm{\top}}+\bm{X}\bm{A})=

\displaystyle\bm{A}\,\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}(\bm{X})\bm{A}^{\bm{\top}},

where
\bm{1}_{n}
is the
n\times 1

vector of ones.
These equivariances say that the estimators
behave naturally under affine transformations.
Under this equivariance condition it turned
out one can construct highly casewise robust
estimators of location and covariance, in
the sense that almost 50% of the cases can
be replaced before the estimator becomes
completely uninformative.

In this section we consider the more general
contamination model of (
3
).
We assume that the clean data
\bm{X}
are
sampled independently from the model
F
,
say a multivariate normal distribution with mean

\bm{\mu}
and covariance
\bm{\Sigma}
. Afterward
the data can be contaminated by casewise and
cellwise outliers. Despite affine equivariance
being natural, we will see that it cannot be
reconciled with robustness under cellwise
contamination. This indicates that robustness
to cellwise outliers requires a paradigm shift
away from the casewise framework.

3.1
Breakdown of location and
covariance estimators

In order to measure the cellwise robustness of a
location estimator,
Alqallaf et al. 2009

considered an asymptotic cellwise breakdown value.
Here we will instead focus on a finite-sample
breakdown value in the spirit of
Donoho and Huber 1983

and
Lopuhaä and Rousseeuw 1991
.
Consider a location estimator
\bm{\widehat{\mu}}
and a data
matrix
\bm{X}
. Informally, we define the cellwise
breakdown value of
\bm{\widehat{\mu}}
as the minimum fraction
of cells that needs to be replaced in columns of

\bm{X}
so that the resulting estimate carries
no information on
\bm{\widehat{\mu}}(\bm{X})
, the estimate on
the original data. More precisely, denote by

\bm{X}^{m}
any corrupted data matrix obtained by
replacing at most
m
cells in each column of

\bm{X}
by arbitrary values. The

finite-sample cellwise breakdown value
of

\bm{\widehat{\mu}}
at
\bm{X}
is defined as

\varepsilon^{*}_{n}(\bm{\widehat{\mu}},\bm{X})=\min\left\{\frac{m}{n}:\;\sup_{\bm{X}^{m}}{\left|\left|\bm{\widehat{\mu}}(\bm{X}^{m})-\bm{\widehat{\mu}}(\bm{X})\right|\right|}=\infty\right\}.

(5)

Note that the casewise breakdown value of an
estimator is an upper bound on its cellwise
breakdown value. After all,
we can always concentrate all the contaminated
cells in a fraction of the rows, and thereby
replicate the casewise setting. Therefore,
we know that
\varepsilon^{*}_{n}(\bm{\widehat{\mu}},\bm{X})
can
be at most
\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor/n
for
any translation equivariant location
estimator due to Theorem 2.1 of

Lopuhaä and Rousseeuw 1991
. We will now
illustrate that some properties of
\bm{\widehat{\mu}}

that may seem desirable in fact prevent
a high cellwise breakdown value.

A first example of such a property is the
following:

\displaystyle\mbox{if all points of }\bm{X}\mbox{ lie in a lower-dimensional}

(6)

\displaystyle\mbox{ affine subspace, then }\bm{\widehat{\mu}}(\bm{X})\mbox{ lies in that subspace as well.}

Under this property, which appears very natural,
we obtain the following result.

Proposition 1
.

The cellwise breakdown value of any
location estimator
\bm{\widehat{\mu}}

satisfying (
6
) is
bounded by

\varepsilon^{*}_{n}(\bm{\widehat{\mu}},\bm{X})\leqslant\left\lceil\frac{n}{d}\right\rceil/n\,\approx\frac{1}{d}

(7)

at any dataset
\bm{X}
.

The proof relies on the fact that all the
data can be moved to a hyperplane of choice
by changing no more than
\lceil n/d\rceil

cells in each column of
\bm{X}
. This proof,
and those of the next results, can be found
in the appendix of
Raymaekers and Rousseeuw 2026a
.

Any estimator
\bm{\widehat{\mu}}
satisfying property
(
6
) thus has a cellwise
breakdown value of at most
1/d
.
Unfortunately, this property is shared
by many well-known estimators. In particular,
we can show that property (
6
)
holds for any orthogonal equivariant
estimator of location. This is a weaker form
of equivariance that only
requires (
4
)
for orthogonal matrices
\bm{A}
.

Corollary 1
.

Any orthogonally equivariant location
estimator satisfies
property (
6
),
and hence the upper bound of
Proposition
1
applies.

We conclude that orthogonal equivariance,
and the weaker property (
6
),
cannot be reconciled with cellwise
robustness. For the estimation of location,
there is a natural solution in using
a univariate robust location estimate for each
variable separately, and combining them into a
vector. Such an estimator does not satisfy
property (
6
), and inherits the
breakdown value of the univariate location
estimator.

Unfortunately, for estimating a covariance
matrix there is no coordinatewise trick. Note
that a covariance matrix estimator can break
down in two ways, namely through having an
arbitrarily large or an arbitrarily small
eigenvalue. More precisely, the

cellwise explosion
breakdown value
of the covariance
estimator
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}
is defined as

\varepsilon^{+}_{n}(\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}},\bm{X})=\min\left\{\frac{m}{n}:\;\sup_{\bm{X}^{m}}\lambda_{1}(\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}(\bm{X}^{m}))=+\infty\right\}

(8)

where
\lambda_{1}
denotes the largest
eigenvalue. The
cellwise
implosion breakdown value
of

\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}
is defined as

\varepsilon^{-}_{n}(\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}},\bm{X})=\min\left\{\frac{m}{n}:\;\inf_{\bm{X}^{m}}\lambda_{d}(\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}(\bm{X}^{m}))=0\right\}

(9)

where
\lambda_{d}
is the smallest
eigenvalue.
Implosion implies that the estimated
covariance matrix is singular.
A singular covariance matrix is often not
very useful, as it is not invertible and
therefore cannot be used to perform
standard statistical analyses such as
the computation of Mahalanobis distances
or discriminant analysis.

In the casewise setting, it is
known that affine equivariant
covariance estimators can have a
breakdown value of at most

\lfloor(n-d+1)/2\rfloor/n\approx 0.5
.
It turns out that obtaining a high
cellwise breakdown value for the
covariance matrix is impossible under
affine equivariance, and even under a
more general property analogous
to (
6
):

\displaystyle\mbox{if all points of }\bm{X}\mbox{ lie in a lower-dimensional}

(10)

\displaystyle\mbox{affine subspace, then }\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}(\bm{X})\mbox{ is singular.}

Under this property, we can show the following
disappointing bound on the cellwise
implosion breakdown value.

Proposition 2
.

The cellwise implosion breakdown value
of any covariance estimator
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}

satisfying
(
10
) is
bounded for any dataset
\bm{X}
by

\varepsilon^{-}_{n}(\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}},\bm{X})\leqslant\left\lceil\frac{n-1}{d}\right\rceil/n\,\approx\frac{1}{d}\;.

(11)

Note that most common estimators
satisfy property (
10
)
and thus have a cellwise implosion
breakdown value of approximately

1/d
. An interesting example is the
classical empirical covariance matrix,
that has a high casewise implosion
breakdown value of
(n-d)/n\approx 1
,
but satisfies property (
10
)
so its cellwise breakdown value is low.
More generally, any affine equivariant
covariance estimator satisfies
property (
10
),
as shown in Corollary
2
.

Corollary 2
.

Any affine equivariant covariance
estimator
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}
satisfies
property (
10
), and
hence the upper bound of
Proposition
2
applies.

While the above results may appear very
negative, we will see in the next section
that it is still possible to construct
estimators of covariance with a high
cellwise breakdown value.

3.2
Cellwise robust estimators of
location and covariance

While location can be estimated
coordinatewise, this strategy is no
longer viable for the estimation of
covariance matrices.
Van Aelst et al. 2011

proposed the first cellwise robust
covariance estimator. Their cellwise
Stahel-Donoho estimator is inspired
by the classical Stahel-Donoho estimator
for casewise robust covariance estimation

(
Stahel 1981
;
Donoho 1982
)
. The estimator
works quite well, but was later
surpassed in performance by the
two-step generalized S-estimator
(TSGS) of
(
Agostinelli et al. 2015
;
Leung et al. 2017
)
. TSGS first filters the data
for cellwise outliers, and puts
the flagged cells to missing. In a
second step, it applies the generalized
S-estimator (GSE) of
Danilov et al. 2012

for casewise robust covariance
estimation with missing data. This
combination of filtering and estimation
was the topic of an extensive comparative
study by
Danilov 2010
.
The conclusion was that filtering
based on partial Mahalanobis distances
is preferred, but computationally
burdensome. TSGS therefore incorporates
a bivariate filter to keep
the computation time manageable.
TSGS and GSE are implemented in
the
R
-package
GSE

(
Leung et al. 2019
)
.

TSGS was the state-of-the-art method for
estimating covariance matrices under cellwise
contamination for quite some time. More
recently,
Raymaekers and Rousseeuw 2024a
proposed the cellMCD
method that combines the identification of
suspicious cells and the estimation of the
covariance matrix, based on preliminary research
in
Raymaekers and Rousseeuw 2021b
. CellMCD is a cellwise
robust extension of the well-known casewise
robust Minimum Covariance Determinant estimator
of
Rousseeuw 1984
.

The casewise MCD estimator maximizes the
likelihood of a subset of cases. It is thus a
combinatorial problem with the aim of
identifying a subset of observations that
tightly fit together. On the other hand,
cellMCD maximizes the
likelihood of a subset of cells.
Given a set of reliable cells, it thus
needs to quantify how well these cells fit
together. The most natural candidate for this
is the observed Gaussian likelihood, that
is well-known in the missing value literature.
More precisely, cellMCD introduces a new
estimand
\bm{W}
. This
\bm{W}
is an unknown

n\times d
binary matrix in which

\bm{W}_{ij}=1
indicates that the cell

x_{ij}
is considered clean, and

\bm{W}_{ij}=0
flags it as contaminated.
Denoting the rows of the observed data
matrix
\bm{X}
as

\bm{x}_{1\bm{\cdot}},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n\bm{\cdot}}
,
cellMCD estimates
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}}
,

\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}}
, and
\bm{W}
by minimizing the
objective

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\big(\ln|\bm{\Sigma}^{(\bm{W}_{i\bm{\cdot}})}|+d(\bm{W}_{i\bm{\cdot}})\ln(2\pi)+\MD^{2}(\bm{x}_{i\bm{\cdot}},\bm{W}_{i\bm{\cdot}}\,,\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})\,\big)}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}||\bm{1}_{d}-\bm{W}_{\bm{\cdot}j}||_{0}

(12)

\displaystyle\mbox{ under the constraints }\lambda_{d}(\bm{\Sigma})\geqslant a\mbox{ and }||\bm{W}_{\bm{\cdot}j}||_{0}\geqslant h\mbox{ for all }j=1,\ldots,d

with respect to
\bm{\mu}
,
\bm{\Sigma}
, and
\bm{W}
.
Here
\bm{W}_{i\bm{\cdot}}
is the
i
-th row of

\bm{W}
, and
\bm{W}_{\bm{\cdot}j}
is its
j
-th
column. This objective consists of two sums.
The first sum is simply
minus twice the log likelihood of the clean
cells, in which the row
\bm{W}_{i\bm{\cdot}}

indicates that the log-determinant, the
dimension and the Mahalanobis distances are
calculated only on the variables
j
with

\bm{W}_{ij}=1
. The second sum is a penalty
on
\bm{W}
, ensuring that the number of flagged
cells is not too large. More precisely,

||\bm{1}_{d}-\bm{W}_{\bm{\cdot}j}||_{0}
simply
counts the number of nonzero entries in

\bm{1}_{d}-\bm{W}_{\bm{\cdot}j}
which is
the number of zero weights in column
j
of

\bm{W}
, so the number of flagged cells in
column
j
of
\bm{X}
. The penalty also
involves the constants

q_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,d
. These are
fixed before running the algorithm, and
determine the quantile at which cells are
considered outlying in the absence of
contamination. There are two constraints.
One bounds the smallest eigenvalue from below,
and the other ensures that we flag no more
than
h
cells per column.

The objective in Equation (
12
)
is minimized by block-coordinate descent.
Starting from a robust initial guess for

(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
, the algorithm iterates between
updating
\bm{W}
(i.e., flagging suspicious cells),
and re-estimating
(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
.
This iterative procedure is guaranteed to
lower the objective function, so that it
is guaranteed to converge. CellMCD has a
high breakdown value that is at most

(n-\lfloor n/2\rfloor)/n
, depending on
the choice of
h
.

CellMCD implicitly uses a hard-thresholding
rule. If a cell is flagged as outlying,
all information in that cell is discarded.
In some settings, it might be more
appropriate to give smoother weights
to the cells of a data matrix, and obtain
estimates based on these weights. To this
end
Rousseeuw 2026
proposed a method to
statistically analyze data with cellwise
weights in
[0,1]
. It provides a canonical
definition of cellwise weighted likelihood,
that can be used to estimate location and
covariance for given weights.

REGRESSION

We denote the linear regression model as

\bm{y}=\alpha+\bm{X}\bm{\beta}+\mbox{noise}

(13)

where
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
is the

n\times 1
column vector of responses,
and the
n\times p
matrix
\bm{X}
of
regressors has the rows
\bm{x}_{i}^{\bm{\top}}
.
We want to estimate the
(p+1)\times 1

parameter vector
\bm{\gamma}=(\alpha,\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p})^{\bm{\top}}
that combines
the intercept and the slopes.

In casewise robust linear regression,
an observed pair
(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})
is
considered to be either outlying or
entirely clean.
For cellwise robust regression the setup
is different.
It remains true that the response
y_{i}

is either outlying or clean because it
is univariate, so it has only one cell.
The main difference lies in the
contamination of the regressor variables.
Instead of working with either outlying
or entirely clean

\bm{x}_{i}^{\bm{\top}}=(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{ip})
,
we now allow for some of the cells

x_{ij}
to be contaminated while the
others are clean.
We would like to use the clean cells of

\bm{x}_{i}
in the estimation of
\alpha

and
\bm{\beta}
, but at the same time limit
the harmful influence of the bad cells
of
\bm{x}_{i}
.

4.1
Breakdown in regression

For convenience we combine
\bm{X}

and
\bm{y}
into
\bm{Z}=[\bm{X}\,;\,\bm{y}]
,
so a regression estimator can be written
as
\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}(\bm{Z})
.
We now contaminate the dataset

\bm{Z}
into some
\bm{Z}^{m}
,
obtained by replacing at most
m

cells in each column of
\bm{Z}

by arbitrary values.
We define the finite-sample cellwise
breakdown value of the regression
estimator
\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}
at the dataset

\bm{Z}
as

\varepsilon^{*}_{n}(\bm{\widehat{\gamma}},\bm{Z})=\min\left\{\frac{m}{n}:\;\sup_{\bm{X}^{m}}{\left|\left|\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}(\bm{Z}^{m})-\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}(\bm{Z})\right|\right|}=\infty\right\}.

(14)

Many existing regression methods

\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}
satisfy the following
intuitive property:

\displaystyle\mbox{if }y_{i}=[1\,;\,\bm{x}_{i}^{\bm{\top}}]\bm{\gamma}_{0}\mbox{ for all $i=1,\ldots,n$ and }\bm{X}\mbox{ is not}

(15)

\displaystyle\mbox{in a lower-dimensional}\mbox{ subspace, then }\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}=\bm{\gamma}_{0}\;.

The condition that
\bm{X}
does not lie
in a lower-dimensional subspace
serves to avoid multicollinearity,
which causes problems for many
regression methods.

Proposition 3
.

The cellwise breakdown value of any
regression estimator
\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}

satisfying (
15
)
is bounded at any dataset
\bm{Z}
by

\varepsilon^{*}_{n}(\bm{\widehat{\gamma}},\bm{Z})\leqslant\left\lceil\frac{n-1}{p+1}\right\rceil/n\,\approx\frac{1}{p+1}\;.

(16)

The weak condition (
15
)
holds under natural equivariance
properties. The first of these is

regression equivariance
, which
requires that for all
(p+1)\times 1

vectors
\bm{v}
it holds that

\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}(\bm{X},\bm{y}+[\bm{1}_{n}\,;\,\bm{X}]\bm{v})=\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}(\bm{X},\bm{y})+\bm{v}

(17)

for all datasets
(\bm{X},\bm{y})
. This
is similar to translation equivariance
for location estimators.
The second property is
scale
equivariance
, meaning that for any
constant
c
we have

\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}(\bm{X},c\,\bm{y})=c\,\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}(\bm{X},\bm{y})\;.

(18)

Corollary 3
.

Any estimator
\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}
that is
regression equivariant (
17
)
and scale equivariant (
18
)
satisfies property (
15
),
and hence the upper bound of
Proposition
3
applies.

So if we want a regression estimator with
a better cellwise breakdown value, we have
to let go of the intuitive
condition (
15
) and the
combination of equivariance
properties (
17
)
and (
18
).

4.2
Cellwise robust regression
methods

One of the earliest proposals for
cellwise robust regression was by

Leung et al. 2016
. Their model
assumes joint multivariate normality
of the pairs
(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})
before
the cells are contaminated.
They start by estimating the location

\bm{\widehat{\mu}}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}

of the joint distribution of the
pairs
(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})
by the 2SGS
estimator of
Agostinelli et al. 2015
,
with a modified filter in the first step.
Then the slope coefficients and the
intercept are estimated from
\bm{\widehat{\mu}}

and
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}
by the usual formulas

\bm{\widehat{\beta}}:=\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{xx}^{-1}\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{xy}\;\;\;\mbox{ followed by }\;\;\;\widehat{\alpha}=\widehat{\mu}_{y}-\bm{\widehat{\mu}}_{x}^{{\bm{\top}}}\bm{\widehat{\beta}}\;.

(19)

The robustness of this estimator stems
from the 2SGS method, but this approach
leans heavily on the assumption of joint
normality of the clean data.

An alternative proposal is the
shooting S-estimator
of
Öllerer et al. 2016
.
It uses the coordinate descent
algorithm
(
Bezdek et al. 1987
)
, also
called “shooting algorithm”.
A typical step is to fit only the

j
-th slope coefficient
\beta_{j}

while keeping the other slopes fixed
at their previous values:

y_{i}^{(j)}\sim\;\widehat{\alpha}_{j}^{\mbox{\tiny{new}}}+\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{\mbox{\tiny{new}}}x_{ij}\;\;\;\;\;\mbox{ where }\;\;\;\;\;y_{i}^{(j)}\coloneqq y_{i}-\bm{\widehat{\beta}}_{-j}^{\mbox{\tiny{old}}}\bm{x}_{i,-j}\;\;.

(20)

This can be seen as a simple regression
of a new response
\bm{y}^{(j)}
on the

j
-th predictor.
The shooting S-estimator makes two
changes to the coordinate descent
algorithm. The first is that in the
simple regression
of (
20
) the OLS
regression is replaced by a casewise
robust S-estimator.
The second is that the response

\bm{y}^{(j)}
is modified in each
iteration, to avoid that the cellwise
outliers propagate to the new
responses in the iteration steps.

Bottmer et al. 2022
recently
proposed a version of shooting S
regression for high dimensions which
uses hard thresholding, yielding
sparse estimates of the regression
coefficients.

The variable selection method of

Su et al. 2024
first computes a
robust covariance matrix of the joint
distribution of the pairs
(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})
.
For this they use a method based on
pairwise correlations, using those of

Gnanadesikan and Kettenring 1972
or the
Gaussian rank correlation studied by

Boudt et al. 2012
.
They then plug the square root of this
covariance matrix into the adaptive
lasso objective.

Toka et al. 2021
propose a
three-step procedure consisting of (i)
identifying marginal cellwise outliers
using robust z-scores and setting them
to missing, (ii) running the casewise
robust MCD estimates of location and
covariance on the complete cases followed
by imputing the NA’s as in the E-step
of the EM algorithm, and (iii) applying
a robust lasso regression to the
imputed data.

Saraceno et al. 2021
carry out
robust seemingly unrelated regressions
using several univariate MM-regressions,
and apply the 2SGS method of

Agostinelli et al. 2015
to the residual
matrix. Another proposal is the algorithm
by
Su et al. 2024
. It optimizes an
objective function inspired by
mean-shift penalized objectives for
casewise robust regression

(
She and Owen 2011
)
. Recently

Christidis et al. 2026
constructed a
cellwise robust ensemble regression
method.

The cellLTS method proposed by

Raymaekers and Rousseeuw 2026b
works in a different way.
Its first step is to apply the cellMCD
method of Section
3.2

to the regressor matrix
\bm{X}
.
Next, cellwise
outliers as well as NA’s are imputed,
yielding a cleaned matrix
\bm{\widehat{X}}
of
regressors. Finally, the casewise
least trimmed squares (LTS) method is
applied to regress
\bm{y}
on
\bm{\widehat{X}}
.
To avoid collinearity issues, the LTS
objective now includes a penalty term

\lambda||\bm{\beta}||_{2}^{2}
with a small

\lambda
, so it is a ridge version of
LTS, with a tailor made algorithm.
Moreover, cellLTS has an option to
symmetrize the data, for the situation
that some variables are rather skewed.
This is applied to
[\bm{X}\;;\;\bm{y}]
, and
then the location
\bm{\mu}
of
\bm{X}
and
the intercept
\alpha
of the regression
must be estimated afterward.

Figure 3:
Illustration of out-of-sample
prediction with the cellLTS method.

Another novelty of cellLTS is that it
is geared to making robust
out-of-sample predictions. Indeed,
when a new regressor vector
\bm{x}_{\mbox{\scriptsize{new}}}

arrives, it may have outlying cells.
Therefore we should not just use the
standard formula
\widehat{y}=\widehat{\alpha}+\bm{x}_{\mbox{\scriptsize{new}}}^{\bm{\top}}\widehat{\beta}
.
This is illustrated in
Figure
3
, where
\bm{y}
is
regressed on two variables. The black
points are the in-sample (training)
data. The majority of the bivariate
regressor vectors
(x_{i1},x_{i2})
lie
inside the blue ellipse.
At the bottom right is the new point

\bm{x}_{\mbox{\scriptsize{new}}}
, whose first cell is a
distant cellwise outlier. Simply
multiplying that cell by
\widehat{\beta}_{1}

gives it a very big effect: the
predicted value would be obtained by
following the red arrow upward until
it meets the regression plane.
Instead, the cellLTS prediction starts
by imputing the first cell of

\bm{x}_{\mbox{\scriptsize{new}}}
along the blue
horizontal arrow. For the prediction
we then go upward on the blue
vertical arrow, yielding a more
natural prediction.

The in-sample predictions by cellLTS
use the exact same mechanism, that
also works for missing cells. It was
verified by simulation that the cellLTS
predictions outperformed those of
earlier methods, and it was illustrated
on real data with outlying cells.

CellLTS has the advantage that its
cellwise breakdown value can be
derived. First a number
m^{*}
is
computed, that depends on whether
symmetrization is used. CellLTS
uses a value
h
like the cellMCD
and casewise LTS methods.

Proposition 4
.

If
\bm{X}
and
\bm{y}
are in general
position and
h>n-m^{*}
, then the
cellwise breakdown value of cellLTS is

\varepsilon_{n}^{*}(\bm{\widehat{\gamma}},\bm{Z})=(n-h+1)/n
.
This is also its casewise breakdown
value.

The proof is in
(
Raymaekers and Rousseeuw 2026b
)
.

5
HIGH-DIMENSIONAL DATA

5.1
Detecting outlying cells in
high dimensions

The methods discussed so far were primarily
designed to deal with low-dimensional data.
Most of them require
d<n
and have a strongly
deteriorating performance as the dimension
increases. However, the problem of cellwise
outliers becomes more pronounced and thus
increasingly relevant as the dimension of the
data increases. The high-dimensional setting
is thus a natural one for cellwise robustness,
and several proposals for detecting outliers,
dimension reduction and covariance estimation
have been made. While working componentwise is
usually very fast, it has the obvious limitation
that the outlyingness of a cell is determined
only by considering its marginal distribution.
This has motivated the development of many other
techniques, that have been shown to perform
better in challenging scenarios.

For detecting cellwise outliers,

Raymaekers and Rousseeuw 2021a
introduced FastDDC,
a computationally efficient version of the DDC
method of Section
2
. FastDDC
relies on a fast and robust screening technique
for detecting linearly dependent variables,
that are then used in an ensemble of simple
linear regressions to detect cellwise outliers.
For this purpose the variables are first
transformed with componentwise transformations
that limit the influence of outliers. Next,
these transformed variables are subjected
to a fast nearest-neighbor algorithm in dual
space.

In order to obtain a cellwise robust
correlation matrix in very high dimensions
the so-called
wrapping transformation

of
Raymaekers and Rousseeuw 2021a
can be used.
They gave an illustration where a dataset
with 921,600 dimensions was analyzed by
wrapping followed by a truncated SVD.

5.2
Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) is an
essential tool of multivariate statistics.
Several formulations of classical PCA
exist, e.g. based on the spectral
decomposition of the covariance matrix
or the singular value decomposition of
the centered data matrix.
One way of looking at it is that PCA
approximates the dataset
\bm{X}

with
d
columns by a new dataset

\bm{\widehat{X}}
that lies in an affine subspace of
dimension
k<d
such that the Frobenius
norm
||\bm{X}-\bm{\widehat{X}}||_{F}
is minimized.
Several proposals exist for casewise
robust PCA, including spherical
PCA
(
Locantore et al. 1999
)
, PCA
based on least trimmed squares orthogonal
regression
(
Maronna 2005
)
,
the PCAgrid projection pursuit method

(
Croux et al. 2007
)
, and the hybrid
ROBPCA method
(
Hubert et al. 2005
)
.
There are also approaches for casewise
robust sparse PCA, see

Croux et al. 2013
,

Hubert et al. 2016
,
and
Wang and Van Aelst 2020
.

For cellwise outliers, things are more
difficult. One complication stems from
the fact that classical PCA projects the
data orthogonally on a lower dimensional
subspace. Projecting a case with
cellwise outliers is problematic, as the
outlying cells can propagate over all
cells. Therefore, whenever the algorithm
makes a projection, the outlying cells
should be addressed.

5.3
Breakdown of PCA

Following
Centofanti et al. 2026
we formulate a
PCA method as an estimator of the

k
-dimensional
principal subspace

\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X})
of
\bm{X}
. This definition is
quite general and does not require to specify
principal directions inside the subspace.

For the breakdown value, we need an appropriate
definition of breakdown in the context of PCA.
We propose to define the finite-sample cellwise
breakdown value of
\bm{\widehat{P}}
at the dataset
\bm{X}

as the minimum fraction of contamination needed
to get a maxangle of
\pi/2
between the
resulting affine subspace and the original one:

\varepsilon^{*}_{n}(\bm{\widehat{P}},\bm{X})=\min\left\{\frac{m}{n}:\;\sup_{\bm{X}^{m}}\;\mbox{maxangle}\Big(\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X}^{m}),\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X})\Big)=\frac{\pi}{2}\right\}.

(21)

Here the maxangle between subspaces
\bm{A}
and

\bm{B}
is defined as follows. Shift the affine
subspaces so they pass through the origin,
making them linear subspaces
\bm{A}_{0}
and

\bm{B}_{0}
. Then

\mbox{maxangle}(\bm{A},\bm{B}):=\max_{\bm{a}\in\bm{A}_{0},\,||\bm{a}||=1}\Big(\min_{\bm{b}\in\bm{B}_{0},\,||\bm{b}||=1}\mbox{angle}(\bm{a},\bm{b})\Big).

The maxangle equals
\pi/2
iff there is a
vector in
\bm{A}_{0}
that is orthogonal to all
the vectors in
\bm{B}_{0}
(and vice versa).
It is provided by the
R

function
pracma::subspace()
.

Now consider the following seemingly
natural property:

\displaystyle\mbox{{if all points of} }\bm{X}\mbox{{ lie in an}}\mbox{ {affine subspace,}}

(22)

\displaystyle\mbox{then }\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X})\mbox{{ lies in that subspace as well.}}

Proposition 5
.

The cellwise breakdown value of a
PCA estimator
\bm{\widehat{P}}
satisfying
(
22
) at a dataset

\bm{X}
for which
\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X})
is not
parallel to any coordinate axis, is
bounded by

\varepsilon_{n}^{*}(\bm{\widehat{P}},\bm{X})\leqslant\left\lceil\frac{n-1}{d}\right\rceil/n\,\approx\frac{1}{d}\;.

Proof.

Take any vector
\bm{v}
in the principal
subspace
\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X})
and consider

\bm{P}_{0}=\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X})-\bm{v}
which contains the
origin. Construct
k
basis vectors

\bm{e}_{1},\ldots,\bm{e}_{k}
of
\bm{P}_{0}
.
Since
k<d
the orthogonal complement of

\bm{P}_{0}
is nonempty, and it has
a basis
\bm{e}_{k+1},\ldots,\bm{e}_{d}
. Now
consider the hyperplane
\bm{H}
spanned by

\bm{e}_{2},\ldots,\bm{e}_{d}
. It satisfies

\mbox{maxangle}\big(\bm{H},\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X})\big)=\pi/2
because
\bm{e}_{1}
is orthogonal
to all vectors in
\bm{H}
. We are
then again in the situation
of Proposition
1
, where
in each row of
\bm{X}
we can move one cell
to obtain a point on
\bm{H}
. Condition
(
22
) then implies that

\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X}^{m})\subset\bm{H}
, so

\mbox{maxangle}\big(\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X}^{m}),\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X})\big)=\pi/2
.
∎

Corollary 4
.

Any orthogonally equivariant PCA
method satisfies
property (
22
),
and hence the upper bound of
Proposition
5
applies.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can consider
subspaces through the origin (by shifting).
Suppose
\bm{\widehat{P}}
is an orthogonally equivariant
estimator, i.e.
\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X}\bm{R})=\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X})\bm{R}

for any orthogonal matrix
\bm{R}
and any
dataset
\bm{X}
.

Due to the orthogonal equivariance of
\bm{\widehat{P}}
,
we can assume
\bm{X}
lies in the subspace
spanned by the first
k
canonical basis
vectors
\bm{e}_{1},\ldots,\bm{e}_{k}
. If this is not
the case, we can transform
\bm{X}
by orthogonal
transformation so that
\bm{X}
lies in

\mbox{span}(\bm{e}_{1},\ldots,\bm{e}_{k})
. This means
that the last
d-k
columns of
\bm{X}
are all
zeroes. Now consider the diagonal matrix

\bm{D}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{k}&\bm{0}_{k\times(d-k)}\\
\bm{0}_{(d-k)\times k}&-\bm{I}_{(d-k)}\\
\end{bmatrix}
. This orthogonal matrix

\bm{D}
reflects the
last
d-k
entries of
\bm{X}
. Since these are
zero, we have
\bm{X}\bm{D}=\bm{X}
. Now, because

\bm{\widehat{P}}
is orthogonally equivariant, we have

\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X})=\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X}\bm{D})=\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X})\bm{D}
.
This implies that the last
n-k
components
of
\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X})
are zero, hence

\bm{\widehat{P}}(\bm{X})\subseteq\mbox{span}(\bm{e}_{1},\ldots,\bm{e}_{k})
.
∎

5.4
Cellwise robust PCA
methods

An early proposal for the related
problem of cellwise robust factor
analysis was made by
Croux et al. 2003
.
The first cellwise robust PCA method
was proposed by
De La Torre and Black 2003

and improved by
Maronna and Yohai 2008
.
Instead of minimizing the Frobenius norm
of
||\bm{X}-\bm{\widehat{X}}||_{F}
they minimize a
robust approximation error by applying
a bounded function
\rho
to each cell
of
\bm{X}-\bm{\widehat{X}}
and summing the
resulting errors. The minimization
is carried out by iteratively solving
weighted least squares problems.
The bounded
\rho
function guarantees
that a single cell
cannot dominate the approximation error.
The MacroPCA method proposed by

Hubert et al. 2019
was the first
all-in-one method capable of handling
missing values as well as being robust
to cellwise and casewise outliers.
It starts from DDC and
incorporates elements of ROBPCA.
It imputes
cells that caused data points to lie
far from the fitted subspace, and
produces cellwise residuals that can
be plotted.

The recent cellPCA method of
Centofanti et al. 2026

improves on MacroPCA by using a single
objective function that combines two robust
loss functions. It approximates

\bm{X}
by
\bm{\widehat{X}}:=\widehat{\bm{X}^{0}}+\bm{1}_{n}\bm{\widehat{\mu}}^{\bm{\top}}
with

\widehat{\bm{X}^{0}}
an
n\times d
matrix
of rank
k<d
, obtained by minimizing

\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\rho_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{\widehat{\sigma}_{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}m_{ij}\,\widehat{\sigma}_{1,j}^{2}\,\rho_{1}\!\left(\frac{x_{ij}-\widehat{x}_{ij}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{1,j}}\right)}\,\right)\,.

(23)

Here
m_{ij}
is 0 if
x_{ij}
is missing and 1
otherwise,
m_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}m_{ij}
,
and
m=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}
.
The idea is to limit the impact of
the standardized cellwise residuals
(x_{ij}-\widehat{x}_{ij})/\widehat{\sigma}_{1,j}
by a bounded loss
function
\rho_{1}
. Similarly,
\rho_{2}
mitigates
the effect of casewise outliers with a large
standardized total deviation defined as

\frac{t_{i}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{2}}:=\frac{1}{\widehat{\sigma}_{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}m_{ij}\,\widehat{\sigma}_{1,j}^{2}\,\rho_{1}\!\left(\frac{r_{ij}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{1,j}}\right)}\,.

The scales are M-scales, computed from the
residuals obtained via the MacroPCA fit.
All the
\rho
functions involved in computing
the scales and the subspace are of the
hyperbolic tangent type
(
Hampel et al. 1981
)
given by

\rho_{b,c}(z)=\begin{cases}z^{2}/2&\mbox{ if }0\leqslant|z|\leqslant b\\
a-(q_{1}/q_{2})\ln(\cosh(q_{2}(c-|z|)))&\mbox{ if }b\leqslant|z|\leqslant c\\
a&\mbox{ if }c\leqslant|z|\\
\end{cases}

(24)

where

a=(b^{2}/2)+(q1/q2)\ln(\cosh(q_{2}(c-b)))
, with
default values
b=1.5
,
c=4
,
q_{1}=1.54

and
q_{2}=0.86
.

The objective function is minimized by an
iteratively reweighted least squares algorithm.
For each case it yields a casewise weight

w^{\text{\scriptsize{case}}}_{i}
and cellwise weights

w^{\mbox{\scriptsize{cell}}}_{ij}
that transition smoothly from
1 for clear inliers to 0 for far outliers.

The cellPCA method also provides imputed cases

\bm{x}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{imp}}}_{i}
in which suspicious cells are cleaned
and missing cells are filled in. The modified
cells are defined such that
\bm{x}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{imp}}}_{i}
is
shrunk toward the PCA subspace, and that the
orthogonal projection of
\bm{x}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{imp}}}_{i}
coincides
with the fitted
\bm{\widehat{x}}_{i}
. This imputation is
illustrated in the top panel of
Figure
4
for a 1-dimensional
principal subspace in 3-dimensional
space. Most points are projected orthogonally on
the fitted subspace, but the first cell of point

a
is imputed before projecting it, and the
second cell of
b
, and the third cell of
c
.
None of these cells are marginally outlying.

Figure 4:
Upper panel: Illustration of cellPCA
imputation for
d=3
and
k=1
. The first cell
of point
a
was imputed before projecting it on
the principal subspace, the second cell of
b
,
and the third cell of
c
. Lower panel: Shaded
residual cellmap of the octane data.

After obtaining the PCA subspace, the algorithm
carries out an additional step by applying the
casewise MCD estimator to the raw
k
-variate
scores. This yields an orthogonal set of
k

loading vectors collected in the loadings matrix

\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
, as well as a
scores matrix
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}

such that

\bm{\widehat{X}}:=\widehat{\bm{U}}\widehat{\bm{V}}^{\bm{\top}}+\bm{1}_{n}\bm{\widehat{\mu}}^{\bm{\top}}
.

Based on the cellPCA results, a
shaded
residual cellmap
can be drawn

(
Hirari et al. 2025
)
.
Figure
4
is the map of the
octane data of
Esbensen et al. 1996
that
contains near infrared (NIR) absorbance spectra of

n=39
gasoline samples over
d=226>n

wavelengths. First the cells are colored as in
Figure
2
according to their
standardized cellwise residual. Next, each case
with high standardized casewise total deviation is
represented by a shade of grey overlaid on its
row. Note the resemblance with the right panel
of Figure

.

Hirari et al. 2025
also construct other
graphical displays.
Pfeiffer et al. 2025

propose a cellwise robust PCA method that yields
sparse loadings.

The casewise influence function (IF) is a
fundamental tool in robust statistics

(
Hampel et al. 1986
)
. It measures an
estimator’s sensitivity to a small amount of
contamination in different positions in the data
space.
Alqallaf et al. 2009
proposed a cellwise
version, and computed it for a multivariate
estimator of location. The cellwise IF of
cellPCA is derived in
Centofanti et al. 2026
. It is
bounded and redescending.

5.5
Regularized covariance matrices

The covariance estimators discussed in
Section
3
require that
n>d
, and
most are computationally feasible in up to about
20 dimensions. In high-dimensional settings,
nonrobust regularized covariance estimators have
been constructed to avoid ill-conditioned or
singular covariance estimates.
Centofanti et al. 2025

proposed a robust version called Cellwise
Regularized Covariance (cellRCov) that works well
in the presence of casewise and cellwise outliers.
It decomposes the covariance matrix into two
components. Given a
d
-dimensional random
variable
X
with covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
,
we can express it as the sum of a component
lying in the
k
-dimensional subspace of

\mathbb{R}^{d}
spanned by the first

k
eigenvectors of
\bm{\Sigma}
, and another in
its orthogonal complement:

X=X^{k}+X^{\perp}\,.

For a given data set of size
n
, we first
standardize its variables robustly, yielding
the dataset
\bm{Z}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
.
The cellRCov estimate of
\bm{Z}
is then defined
as

\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}(\bm{Z}):=\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Z}^{k}}+\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Z}^{\perp}}\;.

Here the first covariance estimate

\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Z}^{k}}
is obtained from cellPCA
applied to
\bm{Z}
, whereas

\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Z}^{\perp}}
is computed as a
regularized weighted covariance matrix of
the residuals
\bm{Z}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{imp}}}-\widehat{\bm{Z}}

of the imputed data. The resulting covariance
matrix
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}(\bm{Z})
is then transformed
back to the unstandardized data.

Making use of cellRCov, a cellwise and casewise
robust method for canonical correlation analysis
was constructed also.

5.6
Estimating a precision matrix

In many applications it is the inverse
of the covariance matrix, also known as
the precision matrix, that is of main
interest.
In low dimensions, it is of course
possible to estimate a covariance matrix
and then to invert the estimate, but
this is no longer the case in
high dimensions because the estimated
covariance matrix is often singular.
Rather than regularizing the covariance
matrix, one can then estimate the
precision matrix and regularize it
at the same time.
This induces zeroes in the precision
matrix, which correspond to partial
correlations of zero which in turn
correspond to conditional independence
in the Gaussian graphical model.
A popular estimator of a high-dimensional
precision matrix is the graphical lasso
of
Friedman et al. 2008
.
It requires the input of an estimate
of the covariance matrix
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}
,
for which the classical empirical
covariance is often used. The precision
matrix
\bm{\Theta}
is then estimated as

\bm{\widehat{\Theta}}=\argmin_{\bm{\Theta}\succcurlyeq 0}\left(\tr\left(\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}\bm{\Theta}\right)-\log(\det(\bm{\Theta}))+\lambda\sum_{j\neq\ell}|\bm{\Theta}_{j\ell}|\right)\;.

(25)

A natural question is whether a robust
version of
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}
would result in a
robust precision matrix estimator, and
this is indeed the case.
This idea has been studied
empirically and theoretically by

Öllerer and Croux 2015
,

Croux and Öllerer 2016
,

Tarr et al. 2016
,
Loh and Tan 2018
,
and
Katayama et al. 2018
, using
different
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}
based on robust
pairwise correlations.
The
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}
in these studies
are typically of the form

\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{j\ell}=\mbox{s}\!\left(\bm{X}_{\bm{\cdot}j}\right)\mbox{s}\!\left(\bm{X}_{.\ell}\right)\mbox{r}\!\left(\bm{X}_{\bm{\cdot}j},\bm{X}_{.\ell}\right)

(26)

where
\mbox{s}(\cdot)
denotes a robust
scale estimator, often the
Q_{n}
estimator
of
Rousseeuw and Croux 1993
, and

\mbox{r}(\cdot)
denotes a robust
correlation estimator such as Spearman’s
rank correlation.
Note that
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}
needs to be positive
semidefinite for (
25
) to
work. When
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}
is not positive
semidefinite it is first modified, e.g. by adding a small multiple of the
identity matrix.
The resulting precision matrix
estimators perform well under
non-adversarial cellwise contamination,
are fast to compute, and can be analyzed
theoretically due to the well-defined
objective function (
25
).
But since they focus on pairwise
correlations, their performance is
likely to suffer under more adversarial
cellwise outliers.

6
TENSOR DATA

Tensors extend two-way matrices into
higher-dimensional arrays, and contain many
cells, some of which may be corrupted.
Casewise robust methods for tensor data
discard or downweight complete cases
(tensors), see e.g.
(
Pravdova et al. 2001
;
Engelen and Hubert 2011
;
Hubert et al. 2012
;
Mayrhofer et al. 2025
)
. This however leads to
a large loss of efficiency when cases contain
only a few outlying cells.

Similarly to PCA, tensor decompositions aim to
reduce the dimensionality of the data. Given a
set of
n
independent tensors

\mathcal{X}_{i}=\left[x_{i,p_{1}\ldots p_{L}}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{P_{1}\times\cdots\times P_{L}}
(for

i=1,\ldots,n)
, the objective of Multilinear
Principal Component Analysis (MPCA) is to find
a collection of orthogonal matrices

\bm{V}^{(\ell)}=\left[v_{p_{\ell}k_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{P_{\ell}\times K_{\ell}}
of rank

K_{\ell}\leqslant P_{\ell}
, together with a center

\mathcal{C}=\left[c_{p_{1}\ldots p_{L}}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{P_{1}\times\cdots\times P_{L}}
and
core tensors

\mathcal{U}_{i}=[u_{i,k_{1}\ldots k_{L}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{L}}
, such
that the reconstructed tensors

\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{i}
with entries

\widehat{x}_{i,{p_{1}\ldots p_{L}}}=c_{{p_{1}\ldots p_{L}}}+\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{K_{1}}\sum_{k_{2}=1}^{K_{2}}\cdots\sum_{k_{L}=1}^{K_{L}}u_{i,k_{1}\ldots k_{L}}v_{p_{1}k_{1}}^{(1)}\dots v_{p_{L}k_{L}}^{(L)}

(27)

are good approximations of the original tensors

\mathcal{X}_{i}

(
Lu et al. 2008
)
. The decomposition
in (
27
) is called a Tucker
decomposition.
When all
K_{\ell}
are equal and the core tensors
are required to be superdiagonal (which means that
the only non-zero elements are those where all
indices are identical), a CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP)
decomposition is obtained

(
Ballard and Kolda 2025
;
Bi et al. 2021
)
.

Inoue et al. 2009
introduced robust adaptations
of MPCA that can either deal with casewise or
cellwise outliers in the data, but not
simultaneously. They either apply a bounded
\rho

function to the Frobenius norm of the residual
tensor
\mathcal{X}-\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
, or to
its entries.

The ROMPCA method of
Hirari et al. 2026a
can
cope with both types of outliers simultaneously,
and allows for missing values in the tensors as
well. It generalizes ideas from cellPCA to the
tensor setting by combining two bounded
\rho

functions that act at the casewise and at the
cellwise level. For each case it also provides
an imputed tensor

\mathcal{X}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{imp}}}_{i}
and a weight

w^{x}_{i}
that is 0 when either

\|\mathcal{X}_{i}-\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{i}\|_{F}

is too large, or when its estimated core tensor
deviates highly from the regular core tensors.

Residual cellmaps can be drawn for the unfolded
tensors, or for some slices. Consider for example
the Dog Walker dataset which is a surveillance
video of a man walking his dog. The video was
filmed using a static camera and contains 54 color
frames. Each case is a frame with
72\times 90

pixels stored using the RGB (Red, Green, Blue)
color model, and thus corresponds with a 3rd order
tensor. The top row of Figure
5

contains some frames, and the second row shows the
corresponding ROMPCA residual maps of the blue
color. The residual cellmap in the 3rd row is
obtained by vectorizing each frame and stacking
the vectors rowwise. Adjacent
columns are aggregated, and their average
color is displayed. The last panel depicts the
cellmap as a 3D array. We clearly see that most of
the outlying cells correspond to the man walking
his dog. He is successfully flagged as outlying,
as this is the only part that significantly changes
in the successive frames. The first and last frames
also show numerous deviating pixels in the
background. They mostly result from tree leaves
moving in the wind.

Figure 5:
Some frames of the Dog Walker data, and
the corresponding ROMPCA cellmaps. The third row
shows the residual cellmap of the unfolded video
frames and a 3D cellmap.

Hubert and Hirari 2024
introduced a casewise
and cellwise robust method for CP decomposition.
Their MacroPARAFAC method starts by applying
FastDDC to the unfolded tensors, and then follows
several principles underlying MacroPCA. It
estimates loadings and the core vectors (the
entries on the superdiagonal of the core tensor)
iteratively by an alternating least squares
PARAFAC algorithm for incomplete data, thereby
updating the imputations for the cellwise outliers
and missing values.

The linear regression model in (
13
)
can be generalized to tensor-on-tensor (TOT)
regression where both the predictor

\mathcal{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{P_{1}\times\cdots\times P_{L}}
and the response
\mathcal{Y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{1}\times\cdots\times Q_{M}}

are tensors. The TOT regression model of

Lock 2018
assumes that

y_{i,q_{1}\cdots q_{M}}=\beta_{0,q_{1}\cdots q_{M}}+\sum_{p_{1}=1}^{P_{1}}\cdots\sum_{p_{L}=1}^{P_{L}}x_{i,p_{1}\cdots p_{L}}\beta_{p_{1}\cdots p_{L}q_{1}\cdots q_{M}}+\varepsilon_{i,p_{1}\cdots p_{L}q_{1}\cdots q_{M}}

where
\mathcal{B}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{1}\times\cdots\times Q_{M}}
is the intercept,

\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{P_{1}\times\cdots\times P_{L}\times Q_{1}\times\cdots\times Q_{M}}

is the slope tensor, and
\mathcal{E}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{1}\times\cdots\times Q_{M}}
is
the error tensor. Moreover, it is assumed that

\mathcal{B}
can be represented by a CP
decomposition of rank
R
.
Lee et al. 2024

introduced a robust adaptation of TOT regression
that can handle cellwise outliers in the response
tensor.
Hirari et al. 2026b
proposed the ROTOT
method that can also cope with casewise and
cellwise outliers in the predictors. It starts
by replacing the predictors with their ROMPCA
imputed tensors, and then applies a weighted
ridge M-regression to the cases whose ROMPCA
weight
w_{i}^{x}
is different from 0.
Out-of-sample predictions are obtained as in
Figure
3
, based on the ROMPCA
imputations of the predictors.

7
OTHER MODELS

Apart from the estimation of location and
covariance matrices, regression, PCA,
and tensor analysis covered in the preceding
sections, the cellwise paradigm has made
inroads in many other statistical settings.
We will give a brief summary.

Detecting outlying
cells in other settings.

The DDC method described in
Section
2
operates on
numeric data, and has been extended to
some other data types.

Walach et al. 2020
used a
similar approach for metabolomics
data by aggregating outlyingness over
pairwise log-ratios.
Liu et al. 2025

extended DDC to contingency tables, in
the context of detecting side effects of
medical products.

Cellwise robust
discriminant analysis.

Aerts and Wilms 2017
construct
cellwise robust versions of linear
and quadratic discriminant analysis.
To this end they estimate a cellwise
robust precision matrix of the type of

Croux and Öllerer 2016
for each class,
and plug these into the assignment rule.

Cellwise robust cluster
analysis.

Several methods for clustering data with
cellwise outliers have been proposed.
The earliest was
Farcomeni 2014
,
who used a mixture model with an
additional
n\times d
parameter
matrix of zeroes and ones, indicating
which cells should be flagged and set
to missing.
The goal was to maximize the observed
likelihood of the unflagged cells.
The method was implemented in the

R
package
snipEM

(
Farcomeni and Leung 2019
)
that is no longer
maintained.

García-Escudero et al. 2021
consider a
model in which clusters are linear,
i.e. they lie near lower-dimensional
subspaces. Each cluster is then fitted
by a cellwise robust PCA in the style
of
Maronna and Yohai 2008
.
They minimize the robust scales
of the coordinatewise residuals as in
least trimmed squares regression.
The algorithm iterates the following
three steps: (i) updating the subspace
parameters by robust PCA, (ii) updating
the weights indicating which cells are
deemed outlying, and (iii) updating
the group memberships as in the usual

k
-means clustering algorithm.

The cellGMM method of

Zaccaria et al. 2025

is aimed at finding ellipsoidal clusters
instead. The authors assume a Gaussian
mixture model (GMM), in which they use
the cellMCD method described in
Subsection
3.2
to fit
centers and covariance matrices and to
impute outlying cells. This is alternated
with EM-type updates of the cluster
memberships by constrained optimization of
the cellGMM objective function.

Zaccaria et al. 2026
extend this work
to the cellFCLUST method for fuzzy
clustering.

Time series.

An application of cellwise robust methods
to autoregressive time series was described
in
Raymaekers and Rousseeuw 2026a
.

Centofanti et al. 2025
developed a cellwise robust
version of singular spectrum analysis
called RODESSA, using a kind of dimension
reduction.

Functional data.

In functional data analysis, a case
is not a row of a matrix but a function
such as a curve.
In that setting, cellwise outliers
correspond to local deviations (like
spikes) in a curve, rather than global
outlyingness of the entire curve.
In the taxonomy of
Hubert et al. 2015

of functional outliers these are
called
isolated outliers
, and
the authors constructed methods to detect
them.
García-Escudero et al. 2021
apply
cellwise robust methods to functional
data.

Compositional data.

For regression with compositional
covariates,
Štefelová et al. 2021

propose a four-step procedure:
(i) detecting outlying cells by DDC,
(ii) imputing the outlying cells that
are not in outlying cases,
(iii) running robust regression
on the imputed data, and
(iv) performing inference based on multiple
imputation.

8
CONCLUSION

Cellwise outliers form a new paradigm in
statistics and machine learning. We have
seen that addressing them requires
abandoning several cherished technical
properties, including some forms of
equivariance. But even though the problem
was tough, the community has stepped up
and progress has been made. Here we
surveyed developments in the
detection of cellwise outliers as well as
robust estimation of covariance matrices,
linear regression, dimension reduction,
tensor data, and other settings. Much work
remains to be done on other frameworks,
algorithms, and graphical displays to
facilitate interpretation and
communicating results.

Software availability.

The code for several cellwise robust
methods described here is available in
the
R
package
cellWise

(
Raymaekers and Rousseeuw 2022
)
. Other software is
listed in the text or referenced in
the cited papers.
