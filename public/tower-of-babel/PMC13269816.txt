Use of Mendelian Randomization to Unveil Metabolic Markers in Helicobacter pylori Infection
Authors: Tung Hoang, Jeongseon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnzymes encoded by Helicobacter pylori are involved in various metabolic processes. Using a Mendelian randomization (MR) framework, we investigated the metabolites associated with six antibodies against H. pylori and explored the potential underlying biological pathways.MethodsA meta-analysis of 65 genome-wide association studies was conducted to assess the genetic predisposition to approximately 3,550 metabolites. Summary-level data for > 10 million genetic variants associated with H. pylori antibodies were extracted from the UK Biobank. MR analysis was performed using inverse-variance weighting, weighted median, and Egger regression to ensure robust findings. Significant metabolites were further analyzed using enrichment analysis to identify the relevant biological pathways.ResultsA total of 100 metabolites were positively associated with H. pylori antibodies (cytotoxin-associated gene A [CagA], 16; catalase, 25; GroEL, 16; outer membrane protein [OMP], 22; urease [UREA], 15; and vacuolating cytotoxin [VacA], 6). These metabolites were linked to pathways involving the metabolism of alanine, aspartate, glutamate, glycine, serine, threonine, purine, and tryptophan in four antibodies. Additionally, 67 metabolites were negatively associated with H. pylori antibodies (CagA, 11; catalase, 6; GroEL, 13; OMP, 11; UREA, 11; and VacA, 15). These metabolites were primarily involved in pyrimidine metabolism in the three antibodies.ConclusionOur study identified numerous metabolites linked to H. pylori antibody levels, indicating that metabolic alterations are associated with infection. These changes were particularly enriched in pathways involved in amino acid, nucleotide, and coenzyme metabolism and biosynthesis. These findings highlight the systemic metabolic impact of H. pylori infection and offer insights into the biological mechanisms underlying host-pathogen interactions.

INTRODUCTION

Helicobacter pylori is a spiral-shaped, gram-negative, microaerophilic bacterium that primarily colonizes the stomach and duodenum, contributing to various gastrointestinal diseases, such as gastritis, peptic ulcers, and neoplastic conditions.1 It possesses virulence factors that facilitate adhesion, translocation, inflammation, and infection in host epithelial cells, and genetic heterogeneity and polymorphisms influence its pathogenicity.23

Since 1994, the World Health Organization’s International Agency for Research on Cancer has classified H. pylori as a class I bacterial carcinogen, although only about 20% of infected individuals develop disease symptoms.4 The current prevalence of H. pylori infection is estimated to be 43.9% in adults and 35.1% in children and adolescents,5 although the rates in children may vary depending on the diagnostic method, with 28.6% detected through serology and 35.9% through urea breath tests or stool antigen tests.6 Several proteins have been reported to contribute to H. pylori infection, including cytotoxin-associated gene A (CagA), catalase, GroEL, outer membrane protein (OMP), urease (UREA), and vacuolating cytotoxin (VacA).478

Identifying circulating metabolic profiles is promising for detecting high-risk populations susceptible to diseases caused by H. pylori infection. Previous studies have demonstrated the impact of H. pylori on human metabolism, highlighting significant metabolites and disrupted metabolic pathways associated with infection, while suggesting a link between H. pylori and gastric cancer.9101112 However, limitations of observational study designs, including confounding factors and reverse causation, pose challenges in establishing causal relationships. Thus, further in-depth identification of metabolomic markers and evaluation of the etiology of H. pylori infection are crucial for informing high-risk populations of H. pylori-related conditions.

As genome-wide association studies (GWAS) continue to expand, the application of the Mendelian randomization (MR) framework has been shown to overcome the shortcomings of observational study designs, such as confounding, reverse causation, and measurement error.13 Despite evidence linking H. pylori to metabolic alterations,14 no study has comprehensively assessed the metabolic profiles associated with H. pylori infection using an MR approach. Therefore, in this study, we used MR to systematically identify significant metabolites related to H. pylori antibodies and further explore the underlying biological pathways.

METHODS

Study design

In this study, we investigated the genetic association between metabolic markers and H. pylori infection by using a two-sample MR framework with publicly available summary-level data. Genetic instruments for metabolite levels and estimates of associations between genetic variants and H. pylori antibody levels were obtained from large-scale GWAS.

To ensure the validity of our findings, we adhered to three fundamental MR assumptions: 1) the relevance assumption, where the selected single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are strongly associated with the metabolic markers; 2) the independence assumption, which ensures that the SNPs are not linked to confounding factors; and 3) the exclusion restriction assumption, which requires that the SNPs influence H. pylori infection only through their effect on the metabolic markers.15 To specifically address the relevance assumption, we calculated F-statistics to confirm the strength of the selected instrumental variables (IVs) using the square of the ratio between the SNP and exposure effect estimate (β) and its standard error (SE).1617

Data sources

Significant genetic variants for H. pylori antibodies

Summary-level data for H. pylori infection were obtained from the GWAS Catalog website (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/), based on a previous genome-wide human leukocyte antigen association study conducted using UK Biobank data of more than 10 million SNPs.18 In particular, we extracted summary statistics for six anti-H. pylori seropositivity antibodies, including CagA (985 samples), catalase (1,558 samples), GroEL (2,716 samples), OMP (2,640 samples), UREA (2,251 samples), and VacA (1,571 samples).18

Significant genetic variants for metabolites

To select the genetic instruments of the MR framework, we utilized summary statistics from the previous 65 GWAS of metabolomic traits (Table 1).1819202122232425262728293031323334353637383940414243444546474849505152535455565758596061626364656667686970717273747576777879808182 Overall, 353,753 significant SNPs linked to metabolites were identified in 65 previous GWAS. Of these, 74,145 SNPs were excluded due to missing beta coefficients and/or allele information, whereas 78,450 SNPs were removed due to missing SE values. Of the remaining 201,158 SNPs, the effect estimates of duplicated SNPs were pooled using random-effect models, ultimately yielding summary statistics for 24,960 SNPs. After excluding weak instruments with F-statistics ≤ 10 and then harmonizing with the summary statistics of H. pylori antibodies, more than 21,000 SNPs remained for MR analyses (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Fig. 2 summarizes the different approaches used for MR analysis. In the primary analysis, we applied an inverse-variance weighted (IVW) meta-analysis with multiplicative random effects, assuming balanced pleiotropy across instruments.84 This method utilized SNP-specific Wald estimates derived by dividing the SNP-outcome association by the SNP-exposure association. These estimates were then combined using inverse-variance weighting, thus providing an efficient causal estimate under the assumption that all the instruments are valid.

Conceptual framework of different approaches for MR analysis.

SNP = single nucleotide polymorphism, IVW = inverse-variance weighted, WM = weighted median, MR = Mendelian randomization.

To assess the robustness of our findings and account for potential violations of MR assumptions, we performed sensitivity analyses using the weighted median method85 and the MR-Egger regression.86 The weighted median method provides an estimate of whether at least 50% of the weight comes from valid instruments and offers robustness against horizontal pleiotropy. MR-Egger regression, which includes an intercept term, allows for the detection and correction of directional pleiotropy but is less statistically efficient than IVW.848586 All MR analyses were performed using R statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).87

To further investigate the mechanisms linking metabolites and H. pylori antibodies, we performed enrichment analyses of H. pylori antibody-associated metabolic pathways. Using the list of significant metabolites, these analyses were conducted using the web-based tool MetaboAnalyst (version 6.0).88 Overrepresentation analysis was performed against 80 curated KEGG human metabolic pathways (updated December 2023).88 The analysis employed a hypergeometric test to evaluate whether specific pathways were enriched with input metabolites more than would be expected by chance, based on the background of all known human metabolites.88

RESULTS

Genetically predicted metabolites and H. pylori antibody levels

Table 2 presents the results of the two-sample MR analysis evaluating the relationships between metabolites and H. pylori antibody levels. In total, the associations between approximately 3,550 metabolites and six H. pylori antibodies were examined (Supplementary Tables 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6). Using the IVW method, we identified 135 metabolites linked to CagA (135 metabolites), catalase (105 metabolites), GroEL (107 metabolites), OMP (123 metabolites), UREA (105 metabolites), and VacA (115 metabolites).

KEGG pathways linking metabolites and H. pylori antibodies

Several biological pathways were identified through enrichment analysis using significant metabolites (Tables 3 and 4). In Particularly, metabolites positively associated with H. pylori antibodies were linked to several significant KEGG pathways, including arginine biosynthesis for CagA (P = 0.036), OMP (P = 0.045), and UREA (P = 0.036) antibody levels; fructose and mannose metabolism (P = 0.013); galactose metabolism (P = 0.018); and amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (P = 0.027) for VacA antibody levels (Table 3). In addition, metabolites negatively associated with H. pylori antibodies were linked to various significant KEGG pathways, including pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis for catalase (P = 0.013) and UREA (P = 0.026) antibody levels; β-alanine metabolism for catalase (P = 0.014) and UREA (P = 0.027) antibody levels; pyrimidine metabolism for catalase (P = 0.025), GroEL (P = 0.050), and UREA (P = 0.050) antibody levels; fructose and mannose metabolism for OMP (P = 0.039) and UREA (P = 0.003) antibody levels; and galactose metabolism for UREA levels (P = 0.035) (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Although evidence indicates that metabolomic signatures are associated with H. pylori infection, the exact cause and underlying mechanisms remain unknown. Here, we investigated genetically predicted metabolites and six H. pylori antibodies using MR. The results showed 100 positive and 67 negative associations between the metabolites and six H. pylori antibodies. Functional analyses further showed that these metabolites were significantly enriched in the biosynthesis and metabolism of amino acids (e.g., glycine, serine, threonine, and arginine), nucleotides (e.g., purine and pyrimidine), and coenzyme-related pathways (e.g., pantothenate and CoA).

A previous study detected 26 metabolites as independent predictors of H. pylori infection, including 13 amino acids, 10 lipids, 1 carbohydrate, 1 partially characterized molecule, and 1 nucleotide.10 Among these, two metabolites were replicated in our current study, isobutyrylcarnitine (UREA) and etiocholanolone glucuronide (for VacA). First, the MR approach, compared with observational study designs, reduces bias and minimizes the risk of reverse causation. Observational studies are prone to confounding and cannot establish causality as effectively as MR. In addition, our study assessed various H. pylori antibodies rather than relying solely on IgG levels, thus providing a more comprehensive measure of bacterial infection. Moreover, our study screened a much larger set of 3,550 metabolites across six different H. pylori antibodies than the 615 metabolites analyzed in the previous untargeted approach. This broad analysis identified hundreds of significant metabolites, demonstrating the enhanced detection power of our approach.

Metabolites linked to H. pylori may play a mediating role in the association between H. pylori infection and related conditions, particularly gastric cancer.10 A previous study highlighted adenine as a key mediator given its role in abnormal cell proliferation, while cancer cells require substantial amounts of adenine for ATP production and nucleic acid synthesis.1089 Given that adenine may be identified as a primary carcinogenic factor in gastric cancer, it can influence metabolic pathways in response to inflammation and promote precancerous lesions.1090 However, we were unable to confirm the genetically predicted adenine and H. pylori antibody levels owing to the lack of IVs.

H. pylori has been classified as a group 1 carcinogen for the majority of non-cardia gastric cancers.91 Thus, identifying metabolic markers for H. pylori could provide potential biomarkers for gastric cancer, as H. pylori infection is known to influence metabolic changes. Other studies have investigated the association between metabolites and H. pylori infection.929394 Wu et al.92 found that H. pylori eradication was linked to changes in the pretreatment levels of three metabolites. In another study, the H. pylori eradication group had lower citrulline but higher serine and alanine concentrations than the non-eradication group.93 Additionally, Dai et al.94 identified several metabolites correlated with Helicobacter abundance, including 16 fatty acids, 24 glycerophospholipids, 15 amino acids, 10 nucleosides and nucleotides, and 9 carbohydrates.

Six metabolites, including CagA, catalase, GroEL, OMP, UREA, and VacA, play distinct roles in H. pylori infection and pathogenicity. CagA, a well-known virulence factor, is injected into host cells, disrupting signaling pathways and contributing to gastric carcinogenesis.95 Catalase helps H. pylori evade host immune responses by neutralizing reactive oxygen species.96 GroEL, a heat shock protein, aids bacterial survival under stress conditions and may trigger immune responses. OMPs facilitate bacterial adhesion to gastric epithelial cells, thereby promoting colonization and persistence.97 UREA is crucial for H. pylori survival in the acidic stomach environment because it hydrolyzes urea into ammonia, which neutralizes gastric acid. Finally, VacA induces host cell vacuolation, disrupts immune responses, and contributes to tissue damage. Together, these metabolites interact in complex ways, influencing H. pylori infection, host immune response, and disease progression. Given the distinct characteristics of H. pylori antibodies, none of the metabolites in our study was associated with all six antibody levels. This suggests that further studies are needed to explore metabolite-antibody specificity and confirm metabolic signatures for H. pylori infection across different antibody responses.

Regarding the underlying biological pathways linking metabolomic markers with H. pylori infection, our study is in line with previous studies on functional impacts, including glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism, and phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis.10 Serine-glycine-one-carbon metabolism is a critical metabolic pathway in cancer, providing one-carbon units for nucleotide synthesis, methylation reactions, and redox balance.9899100 Its dysregulation supports tumor growth by enhancing DNA replication, epigenetic modifications, and antioxidant defense, making it a potential target for cancer therapy.9899100 In addition, tyrosine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan metabolism have been implicated in gastroesophageal cancer, with alterations in these aromatic amino acids serving as potential biomarkers.101

Despite its strengths, this study had several limitations that need to be addressed. In this study, although the findings were derived using different MR analysis methods, none of the metabolites were consistently associated with all six antibodies examined. Future studies should aim to identify more robust metabolic markers by integrating larger datasets and refining the analytical approaches. Moreover, owing to the lack of full summary statistics for all metabolites, we were unable to confirm the effect of genetically predicted H. pylori infection on metabolites, which limits the interpretation of bidirectional MR associations. Future research should focus on specific candidate metabolites to establish stronger causal relationships. Furthermore, there is a potential limitation in applying our findings to populations with higher H. pylori prevalence, such as those in East Asia,102103 as our MR analyses were based primarily on European ancestry summary statistics. In particular, global variation in H. pylori prevalence arises from methodological heterogeneity, including differences in diagnostic tests, sampling strategies, definitions of infection status, and study populations,5 which may influence SNP-H. pylori associations. In addition, population-specific factors, such as diet, hygiene, and antibiotic resistance, further complicate comparability.104 In East Asia, resistance to metronidazole (≤ 51%) and clarithromycin (≤ 37%) is notably high, potentially affecting host-pathogen interactions and metabolomic responses.105106 Another contributing factor is that genetic susceptibility to H. pylori infection differs across ancestries. For example, variants in TLR1 have been suggested to be associated with infection risk in Europeans, but these associations may not replicate in other populations due to distinct genetic architectures.107 These considerations highlight a key limitation of our study and underscore the need for future MR studies that leverage ancestry-specific GWAS datasets, particularly from high-burden regions, to validate and extend the generalizability of our findings.

In summary, we screened a large number of metabolites and identified hundreds of significant associations with H. pylori antibody levels. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these metabolites are involved in biologically relevant pathways, such as amino acid and nucleotide metabolism and coenzyme-related pathways. These findings highlight the potential role of metabolic regulation in host response to H. pylori infection.
