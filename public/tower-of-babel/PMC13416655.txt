Long-term outcome of Sorbie distal humerus hemiarthroplasty: 15-year follow-up case report
Authors: Clay Garrison, Joel Bird, Srinath Kamineni
Published: 2026

Introduction

Distal humeral fractures can be complex injuries that pose significant challenges when considering treatment options, particularly in cases of severe comminution or poor bone quality. These fractures can vary greatly from patient to patient and require careful consideration when selecting the most appropriate treatment modality. Traditional surgical approaches, including open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) and total elbow arthroplasty (TEA), have demonstrated effectiveness in specific patient populations; however, each comes with inherent limitations.

ORIF remains the preferred treatment for many distal humeral fractures, as it aims to restore the patient's native anatomy and joint function. However, in cases of severe comminution or poor bone quality, achieving predictable stable fixation may be difficult, increasing the risk of hardware failure or nonunion.3,6,16 In contrast, TEA provides a reliable alternative for patients with extensive joint damage or fractures that cannot be internally fixated. Despite its advantages, TEA has significant drawbacks, including restricted life-long weight-bearing capacity, the risk of implant loosening, and complications related to the component fatigue failure.1,9,10,12,16

Distal humeral hemiarthroplasty (DHH) has emerged as a promising alternative for cases in which ORIF is not feasible, offering advantages over TEA by preserving the native ulnar and radial articulations while allowing for greater post-operative function. DHH is more recently considered the best treatment for acute comminuted intra-articular fractures, salvage of nonoperative management, and failed internal fixation.1,2,4,6,8,9,11,15,16 While existing literature has shown that DHH can lead to favorable outcomes, long-term follow-up studies remain limited.1,2,4,12,15 The Sorbie-Questor implant is an unlinked elbow arthroplasty with a geometrically anatomic humeral component with ulna and radial components. The humeral component has been utilized as a DHH since it is able to restore the distal humeral articular surface while preserving the native ulnohumeral and radiocapitellar articulations. The implant is dependent on restoration of collateral ligament stability, as elbow stability following hemiarthroplasty requires competent soft tissue balancing. It was designed to maintain more physiological elbow kinematics compared to linked TEA systems. Although no longer commercially available or in widespread use, it represents an important historical design in the evolution of elbow arthroplasty. Case presentation

The patient originally presented as a 52 y/o female who suffered a comminuted (c3) intra-articular distal humerus fracture with posterior displacement after a fall from standing (Fig. 2). The patient had a past medical history of ORIF of bilateral ulnas years prior. It was determined that the new fracture was not suitable for an ORIF. The patient's condition was discussed, and the surgical plan was to proceed with a distal humerus hemiarthroplasty with Sorbie implant, in addition to robust collateral ligament repair.Figure 2Radiographs of anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) view at the time of initial injury.

Radiographs of anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) view at the time of initial injury.

The posterior (Triple Window7) was utilized without disrupting the triceps. The triceps was mobilized on either side with the ulnar nerve first having been mobilized and kept safe. The ulnar nerve was not transposed at this point but at the end of the procedure was transposed into a subcutaneous pocket over the flexor pronator mass. A radical circumferential capsulectomy was performed for the stiffness. The distal humeral fracture fragments were denuded of muscular and tendinous attachment and excised. The distal humeral fracture was noted to be comminuted, devascularized, and displaced. The humerus was then dislocated laterally through the triceps and extensor mechanism window. It was prepared with a standard manner with reaming and a Sorbie DHH was cemented into place. A small size hemiarthroplasty was stopped. The extensor and flexor masses were then repaired on to the epicondylar region of the implant. A good reduction was achieved and close to normal range of motion was achieved on the table. The wound was closed and dressed. The elbow was maintained at 90 degrees of flexion in a broad arm sling (Fig. 3).Figure 3Anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) radiographs immediately post-operatively.

Anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) radiographs immediately post-operatively.

Key technical considerations included preservation of the triceps mechanism through the Triple Window approach, selection of hemiarthroplasty rather than TEA due to preserved ulnar cartilage, good bone stock, meticulous third-generation cementation technique, and robust reconstruction of the medial and lateral soft tissue stabilizers. Restoration of collateral ligament stability was considered critical to optimize varus-valgus stability and long-term functional outcomes. Because of preservation of the extensor mechanism, stable cement fixation, and ligament reconstruction, the patient was ultimately not maintained with long-term weight-bearing restrictions.

The patient was seen two weeks post-operatively where her wounds were clean and sutures were removed. At that time, the patient was doing well and was instructed to continue range of motion as tolerated.

Four weeks post-operatively, the patient continued to do well. At that time, her range of motion of the elbow was from about 10° to 110 degrees of flexion. Her X-rays showed her hemiarthroplasty prosthesis was in correct position with no displacement (Fig. 4). The patient was instructed to continue with range of motion exercises. The ligamentous repair was healing but not yet complete, so the patient was continued with no weight bearing of the upper extremity.Figure 4Anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) radiographs 4 weeks post-operatively.

Anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) radiographs 4 weeks post-operatively.

Three months post-operatively, the patient continued to progress without difficulties. The patient had active range of motion with full extension to 130 degrees of flexion. The joint was very stable in varus-valgus testing. Imaging confirmed that the joint remained in perfect position (Fig. 5). The patient was encouraged to continue with her gentle active rehabilitation protocols.Figure 5Anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) radiographs 3 months post-operatively.

Anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) radiographs 3 months post-operatively.

Six months post-operatively, the patient was doing well. The examination showed active range of motion including a 5° extension deficit to 130 degrees of flexion. The patient did not have any pain or difficulties at this time. The X-rays confirmed epicondylar healing on medial and lateral aspects with good position of the joint (Fig. 6). The patient was doing very well at this time, and this was the last time the patient was seen in clinic for several years.Figure 6Anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) radiographs 6 months post-operatively.

Anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) radiographs 6 months post-operatively.

Fifteen years later, the patient returned to clinic because of a fall onto the elbow with the hemiarthroplasty. Due to lower limb issues, she had been load bearing with a walking frame for the past 8 years. The patient only had minor pain associated with the fall and only came to the clinic seeking reassurance regarding implant integrity. At that time, the patient had excellent range of motion with extension of the elbow to 0° and flexion to 150° with unrestricted pronation and supination (Fig. 7). The patient did have a nontender mass over the elbow, which was likely due to remodeling of bone. She was still able to bear weight with her walking frame. The X-rays and computed tomography showed there was no hardware failure or loosening (Fig. 8). There was heterotrophic ossification to the elbow joint that was not painful.Figure 7Patient examination at 15 years post-operatively shows extension of the elbow to 0° (A) and flexion of the elbow to 150° (B).Figure 8Anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) radiographs 15 years post-operatively.

Fifteen years after undergoing a DHH with a Sorbie implant, the patient continued to have good functional outcomes without the need for revision surgery or interventions. The patient has some heterotrophic ossification to the joint but remains asymptomatic. The overall clinical course highlighted the durability and stability of the Sorbie implant over the extended follow-up period, with no significant complications or need for additional surgical management.

Discussion

Complex comminuted distal humeral fractures present a significant treatment challenge. Historically, the treatment option was conservative treatment with the ‘bag of bones’ technique, where the position of the fragments was accepted and early movement was encouraged, but this is now rarely considered due to poor functional outcomes.16 ORIF is the preferred approach in many cases, but it is not always feasible due to extensive bone loss, poor bone quality, or complex fracture patterns.3,5,6,16 Historically, TEA has been the alternative treatment in more severe cases. However, it also has complications, including life-long restricted weight-bearing, risk of implant loosening, and potential complications related to the components.9,10,16 DHH has emerged as a promising middle ground, offering anatomic joint preservation while mitigating some of the complications associated with TEA.

Alternative treatment options for complex distal humerus fractures include nonoperative management with the limitations of loss of motion and functional impairment, ORIF, and TEA. ORIF remains the preferred option when stable fixation can be achieved, particularly in younger patients, with good bone stock. However, outcomes are less predictable in cases of severe comminution or poor bone quality. TEA provides reliable pain relief and stability but is associated with lifelong activity restrictions and risk of mechanical failure and secondary bone loss consequent to loosening failure. DHH offers an intermediate solution by preserving native joint articulation while avoiding the functional limitations associated with TEA, particularly in more active patients.

Over the past decade, DHH has gained increased attention as an effective treatment for acute complex distal humeral fractures, particularly in younger or more active patients for whom TEA is not an ideal option.11 Studies have reported favorable short to midterm outcomes, with patients demonstrating good elbow stability, functional range of motion, and low complication rates in comparison to ORIF and TEA.1,2,4,14, 15, 16 Many of the complications associated with DHH are adverse events that are minor, asymptomatic, or have very little clinical consequences.16 Unlike TEA, which requires lifelong activity modifications to prevent implant failure, DHH allows for a more natural joint articulation and greater load-bearing capacity. Although DHH provides reliable pain relief and functional restoration, with survival and maintenance of motion in most cases, there is still a need for evaluation of long-term outcomes. One study evaluated the long-term outcomes of DHH that showed no radiographic difference compared to short-term and midterm studies.12 Another case was reported to have a twenty year follow-up of an elbow hemiarthroplasty using an older custom Vitallium model with good outcomes.13

Contraindications to DHH include severe osteopenia or osteoporosis resulting in proximal ulnar or radial bone stock structural support integrity, as metallic hemiarthroplasty articulation may accelerate erosion in poor quality bone. Additional contraindications include incongruity or poor cartilage quality of the native ulna greater sigmoid notch and radial head articular surfaces, as well as inability to restore adequate soft tissue stability due to poor quality collateral ligament tissues. Patient noncompliance with post-operative rehabilitation may also negatively impact outcomes.

Despite these promising findings, long-term follow-up data on DHH remains limited, and its durability beyond 10 years is not well documented. While short-term and midterm studies on DHH have demonstrated favorable functional outcomes and implant survival, long-term data remain scarce. This case provides one of the longest documented follow-ups of a Sorbie implant, reinforcing the potential for DHH to provide good joint stability and function over an extended period, despite significant load bearing activities. The absence of implant failure, loosening, or significant functional decline suggests that, in select patients, DHH can offer sustained benefits comparable to TEA in similar cases.

Several DHH implant designs have been developed over the years to optimize outcomes. The three primary models used in modern elbow hemiarthroplasty include the Kudo, Sorbie-Questor, and Latitude implants. The Kudo implant was originally designed as a TEA system and the implant has been used occasionally as a hemiarthroplasty. Midterm follow-up results were acceptable in elderly osteopenic women, but radiographic signs of attrition indicate that this design may not be ideal for long term outcomes.2 Studies on the Latitude implant have demonstrated encouraging short-term and midterm outcomes with good pain relief and preservation of functional range of motion.1,2,4,14, 15, 16 One study even demonstrated that this design has acceptable long-term outcomes.12

The Sorbie implant was designed as an anatomic, unlinked elbow arthroplasty system with the goal of preserving native joint articulation and improving range of motion. It featured a humeral component with a bone-preserving stem and an anatomic articulation. Despite these advantages, the implant had limitations related to its unlinked design, including reliance on soft tissue stability and the absence of an anterior flange for additional fixation.9 Studies have shown that designs with the absence of an anterior flange leads to higher complications including implant loosening and revision surgeries.14,15

Although concerns regarding implant loosening and revision have been noted in the literature, this patient's 15-year follow-up demonstrates that, in certain cases, the Sorbie implant can provide excellent functional outcomes and implant longevity. This case report details the long-term clinical course of a patient who underwent DHH with a Sorbie implant, possibly due to implant geometry/articular congruence, cementation technique, and robust collateral ligament repair. The patient remained asymptomatic for 15 years with no signs of implant failure, loosening, or significant functional decline. The patient maintained a full range of motion, including full extension and 150 degrees of flexion, with unrestricted pronation and supination.

While this case highlights a successful long-term outcome, it is important to acknowledge limitations. First, this is a single case report, and results may not be generalizable to all patients undergoing DHH. Additionally, while the patient remained asymptomatic, imaging did reveal heterotopic ossification, which could have led to functional impairment in other cases. Finally, as the Sorbie implant is no longer in production, its direct applicability to current clinical practice is limited, though lessons from this case may inform the use and evaluation of modern DHH designs.

Conclusion

This case report highlights the successful long-term outcome of a patient who underwent DHH using the Sorbie implant, with 15 years of maintained joint function and stability. Despite concerns in the literature regarding implant loosening and revision associated with this design, the patient remained asymptomatic with full range of motion and no evidence of mechanical failure. This case reinforces the potential durability of DHH, even with earlier implant designs that are no longer in use. While modern implants have addressed some of the limitations of previous designs, this case emphasizes the importance of proper patient selection and surgical technique in achieving favorable long-term outcomes. Given the limited availability of long-term follow-up data on DHH, this report contributes valuable insight into the durability and functional success of DHH in cases where ORIF is not feasible. Future studies should continue to evaluate the long-term performance of DHH implants to further refine indications and optimize patient outcomes.
