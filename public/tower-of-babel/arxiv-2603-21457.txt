Local linear stability of dual-pairing summation-by-parts methods for nonlinear conservation laws
Dougal Stewart, Kenneth Duru

Abstract
A recent study by Gassner et al. [J. Sci. Comput. 90:79 (2022)] demonstrates that local energy stability—that is, ensuring the asymptotic numerical growth rate does not exceed the continuous growth rate—is crucial for achieving accurate numerical simulations of nonlinear conservation laws. While nonlinear entropy stability is necessary for numerical stability (i.e., ensuring the boundedness of nonlinear numerical solutions), local energy stability is essential to prevent unresolved high-frequency wave modes from dominating the simulation. Currently, it remains an open question whether high-order numerical methods for nonlinear conservation laws can be simultaneously entropy-stable and locally energy-stable. In this work, we examine the local energy-stability properties of recently developed entropy-stable, high-order accurate dual-pairing (DP) SBP methods, as introduced by Duru et al. [arXiv: 2411.06629], for nonlinear conservation laws. Our analysis indicates that the entropy-stable volume upwind filter inherent in these methods can ensure local energy stability. This approach offers a novel numerical strategy for designing reliable high-order methods for nonlinear conservation laws that are provably entropy-stable and locally energy-stable. The theoretical findings are supported by numerical experiments involving the inviscid Burgers’ equation and nonlinear shallow water equations, in one and two space (2D) dimensions. Furthermore, we present accurate numerical simulations of 2D barotropic shear instability, with fully developed turbulence, demonstrating the efficiency of the DP SBP method in resolving turbulent scales.

Introduction

Enhancing the robustness of high-order accurate numerical methods for nonlinear conservation laws remains a fundamental challenge. Historically, many studies have promoted schemes with provable linear stability, leveraging assumptions of solution smoothness to approximate nonlinear problems
[
21
,
22
,
17
,
28
]
. However, linear stability alone often proves insufficient for capturing complex nonlinear phenomena such as turbulence and shock formation, where nonlinear effects dominate. Empirical evidence from numerous numerical simulations
[
42
,
11
,
7
,
3
]
demonstrates that ensuring nonlinear numerical stability is crucial for robustness of high-order numerical methods for nonlinear conservation laws.

For entropy-stable partial differential equations (PDEs), constructing provably nonlinearly stable numerical methods can be achieved by replicating continuous entropy stability properties at the discrete level. A key tool in this endeavor is the summation-by-parts (SBP) principle
[
20
,
39
,
6
,
41
,
10
,
43
]
, which possesses a mimetic structure facilitating the design of stable semi-discrete schemes, provided boundary conditions are carefully managed. At the continuous level, entropy stability analysis uses integration by parts, along with the chain rule and product rule. Although SBP operators discretely mimic integration by parts, replicating the chain and product rules remains challenging. To mirror continuous analysis, nonlinear conservation laws are often reformulated into skew-symmetric or entropy-conserving split-forms, circumventing direct application of these rules at the discrete level and enabling stability proofs, see
[
36
,
14
,
7
,
38
,
35
,
33
,
32
,
3
,
13
,
27
]
.

Once an appropriate entropy functional and an entropy-conserving reformulation of the PDE system are established, the system can be discretized using SBP operators to produce entropy-conserving schemes. Although this approach guarantees nonlinear stability in principle, traditional SBP finite difference (FD) methods
[
20
,
39
,
6
,
41
]
and collocated discontinuous Galerkin spectral element methods (DGSEM)
[
10
,
43
,
12
]
are often prone to generating spurious unresolved wave modes. These artifacts can degrade solution accuracy or even cause numerical instabilities. Recent research
[
11
]
emphasizes that local linear stability of high-order methods is vital for reliable numerical simulations of conservation law. Unfortunately, many existing high-order entropy-stable schemes—whether SBP FD, DGSEM, or spectral collocation methods—implemented in skew-symmetric split form, typically lack this local linear stability.

The dual-pairing (DP) SBP framework
[
4
,
26
,
44
]
has recently been developed to improve the accuracy of high-order methods for wave propagation and conservation laws. This approach has been extended to discontinuous Galerkin methods
[
15
]
, enabling more flexible and robust treatment of complex geometries. DP-SBP operators consist of a dual pair of high-order discrete operators that satisfy the SBP property, ensuring stability and consistency. These operators include additional degrees of freedom, which can be optimized to reduce dispersion errors
[
44
]
.

The DP-SBP method for nonlinear conservation laws was recently introduced in
[
5
]
. It integrates three key components: 1) the DP SBP DG/FD operators, 2) skew-symmetric reformulation, and 3) upwind flux splitting. This method is conservative, entropy consistent (correctly capturing entropy loss at shocks), provably entropy-stable and naturally incorporates intrinsic high-order accurate filters at shocks and discontinuities. In this work, we analyze the local linear stability properties of the DP-SBP method
[
5
]
for nonlinear conservation laws. Our analysis indicates that the entropy-stable volume upwind filter embedded within this approach can ensure local linear stability.

This framework offers a novel numerical strategy for designing reliable high-order methods for nonlinear conservation laws that are both provably entropy-stable and locally linear stable. The theoretical insights are supported by numerical experiments involving the inviscid Burgers’ equation and nonlinear shallow water equations (SWEs) in one and two spatial dimensions. Additionally, we present high-fidelity simulations of 2D barotropic shear instability with fully developed turbulence, demonstrating the DP SBP method’s effectiveness in resolving turbulent scales.

4
. Section
5

6

2
Entropy stability and local linear stability

As a model problem we consider the scalar hyperbolic nonlinear conservation law in one space dimension given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}f(u)=0,\quad x\in[0,L],\quad t\in[0,T],

(1)

where
L>0
is the width of the spatial domain and
T>0
is the final time. Here
u:[0,L]\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is the unknown solution and the continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the PDE flux.
We augment the conservation law (
1
) with the smooth initial condition
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)
for
x\in[0,L]
and periodic boundary conditions
u(0,t)=u(L,t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

2.1
Continuous analysis

Without loss of generality we will assume that the nonlinear conservation law (
1
) admits square entropy, that is
e=u^{2}
(see for e.g. the nonlinear Burgers equation discussed in the next section), such that for sufficiently smooth solutions we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\derivative{t}\int_{0}^{L}edx=\frac{1}{2}\derivative{t}\norm{u}^{2}

\displaystyle=\text{boundary terms},\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T],

(2)

where the weighted
L^{2}([0,L])
scalar product and norm are defined by

\displaystyle(u,v)_{\rho}=\int_{0}^{L}uv\rho dx,\quad\rho>0,\quad\norm{u}^{2}_{\rho}=(u,u)_{\rho}.

(3)

If
\rho\equiv 1
we get the standard
L^{2}([0,L])
scalar product and norm, we omit the subscript
{\rho}
, and we have
\norm{u}^{2}=(u,u)
.
The boundary terms in (
2
) vanish because of the periodic boundary conditions, thus after time integration we have the conservation of total entropy

\displaystyle\norm{u(\cdot, T)}^{2}=\norm{u_0}^{2}.

(4)

The conservation of of total entropy implies
L_{2}
-stability for the solutions of the nonlinear conservation law, and can be targeted by high-order numerical methods yielding entropy conserving/stable schemes
[
3
,
5
]
. However, an entropy/nonlinearly stable numerical can support high-frequency unresolved wave modes with growing amplitude which can destroy the accuracy of numerical solutions
[
11
,
5
]
.

In turbulence research (see, for example,
[
24
]
), linear stability theory is a key mechanism that explains how laminar flow can transition to nonlinear turbulent flow. This approach involves linearizing the nonlinear system of conservation laws around a chosen base-flow state. The resulting linearized equations indicate whether the base-flow is locally stable in both space and time–meaning the flow remains laminar–or if it permits growth of disturbances, implying local instability and the potential transition to turbulence through nonlinear interactions.

This concept is closely related to linear stability in the sense defined by Strang
[
40
]
. It is reasonable to expect that a numerical scheme should exhibit the same local behavior as the underlying equations. For linear PDEs, it is well-known that energy-stable schemes possess this property, as they prevent non-physical growth—the growth of the numerical solution does not exceed that of the continuous problem. In contrast, Lax-stable
[
23
]
schemes may potentially violate this principle.

We assume the existence of a smooth, periodic base-flow
U(x,t)
defined on
[0,L]
that solves the nonlinear conservation law (
1
). Our primary interest lies in examining the dynamics of a sufficiently small perturbation
\delta u(x,t)
around this base-flow, where
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
and
|\epsilon\delta u(x,t)|\ll|U(x,t)|
for
(x,t)\in[0,L]\times[0,T]
.
To analyze these dynamics, we linearize the nonlinear conservation law (
1
) around the smooth solution
U(x,t)
. Specifically, we consider a perturbed solution
u=U+\epsilon\delta u
, with
0<|\epsilon|\ll 1
, and neglect terms of order
O(\epsilon^{2})
. Expanding
f(u)
around
U
, we obtain:

f(u)\approx f(U)+\epsilon\partial_{u}f|_{u=U}\delta u,

which leads to the linearized conservation law governing the evolution of the perturbation
\delta u(x,t)
.

\partial_{t}\delta u+\partial_{x}(a\delta u)=0,\quad x\in[0,L],\quad t\in[0,T],

(5)

where
a=\partial_{u}f|_{u=U}
is the Jacobian of the flux, with the initial condition
\delta u(x,0)=\delta u_{0}(x)
and periodic boundary condition
\delta u(0,t)=\delta u(L,t)
.

To estimate the growth/decay of the
L_{2}
-norm of the perturbation, we multiply the linear conservation law (
5
) by
\delta u
and integrate the product over the spatial domain
[0,L]
. By applying the product rule
\partial_{x}(a\delta u)=a\partial_{x}\delta u+\delta u\partial_{x}a
and integration-by-parts, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\derivative{t}\norm{\up(\cdot, t)}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta u\partial_{x}(a\delta u)+\delta ua\partial_{x}\delta u+\delta u^{2}\partial_{x}a\right)\differential{x}

(6)

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{1}{2}\delta u^{2}\partial_{x}a\differential{x}\underbrace{+a\delta u^{2}|_{x=0}-a\delta u^{2}|_{x=L}}_{\text{boundary terms}}.

Again the boundary terms will vanish because of the periodic boundary conditions, and after time integration yields

\displaystyle\norm{\up(\cdot, T)}^{2}\leq e^{\eta_{c}T}\norm{\up_0}^{2},\quad\eta_{c}=-\min_{x,t}\partial_{x}a(x,t).

(7)

The above estimate (
7
) is nearly optimal for a general base-flow
U(x,t)
. However, if we have further information about the base-flow
U(x,t)
, a sharper and more optimal estimate can be derived. For example, if the base-flow is defined such that
a(x,t)\neq 0
does not change sign within the domain, that is
a=a_{-}<0
for all
(x,t)\in[0,L]\times[0,T]
or
a=a_{+}>0
for all
(x,t)\in[0,L]\times[0,T]
, we have

\partial_{t}\delta u\pm\partial_{x}(|a_{\pm}|\delta u)=0,\quad x\in[0,L],\quad t\in[0,T],

(8)

where
|a|=|a_{+}|=a_{+}>0
and
|a|=|a_{-}|=-a_{-}>0
. Multiplying the linear conservation law (
8
) by
\delta u|a|
and integrating the product over the spatial domain
[0,L]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\derivative{t}\norm{\up(\cdot, t)}^{2}_{|a|}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{1}{2}\delta u^{2}\partial_{t}|a|\differential{x}\underbrace{\pm|a_{\pm}|^{2}\delta u^{2}|_{x=0}\mp|a_{\pm}|^{2}\delta u^{2}|_{x=L}}_{\text{boundary terms}}.

(9)

As before, the boundary terms in (
9
) will vanish because of the periodic boundary conditions, giving

\displaystyle\norm{\up(\cdot, T)}^{2}_{|a|}\leq e^{\eta_{c}T}\norm{\up_0}^{2}_{|a|},\quad\eta_{c}=\max_{x,t}\frac{\partial_{t}|a(x,t)|}{|a(x,t)|}.

(10)

The estimate (
10
) is more optimal.
Note that if the base-flow is time-invariant, that is
\partial_{t}U=0\implies\partial_{t}a(x,t)=0
and
\eta_{c}=0
, then we have

\displaystyle\norm{\up(\cdot, T)}^{2}_{|a|}=\norm{\up_0}^{2}_{|a|}.

(11)

Using the equivalence of norms, the estimate (
11
) in the weighted
L_{2}
-norm also holds for standard
L_{2}
-norm, that is

\displaystyle\norm{\up(\cdot, t)}^{2}\leq K\norm{\up_0}^{2},\quad K=\frac{\max_{x}|a(x)|}{\min_{x}|a(x)|}>0.

(12)

Therefore, if the Jacobian
a
does not change sign within the domain then for a constant or time-independent base-flow (
U(x,t)=\emph{const.}
or
U(x,t)=U(x)
) the
L_{2}
-norm of the perturbation can never grow in time. A robust numerical method should, as much as possible, replicate the energy estimates of the continuous problem. Specifically, if the continuous system does not exhibit growth, the numerical method should likewise prevent it.

2.2
Semi-discrete analysis

Now consider the discretization of the spatial domain
0\leq x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{N}\leq L
. Let
\mathbf{u}:=[u_{1}(t),u_{2}(t),\cdots,u_{N}(t)]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
approximate the solution on the grid
\mathbf{x}:=[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{N}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, that is
u_{j}(t)\approx u(x_{j},t)
. We denote the maximum grid-size
\Delta x=\max_{j\in\{1,2,\cdots,N-1\}}(x_{j+1}-x_{j})
and define the discrete
l_{2}([0,L])
scalar product and norm by

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}v_{j}h_{j},\quad h_{j}>0,\quad\norm{\vb{u}}^{2}_{h}:=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}\rangle>0,\,\forall\,\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}/\{\mathbf{0}\},

(13)

where
h_{j}>0
are weights of a composite quadrature rule.

We denote the semi-discrete approximation of the nonlinear conservation law (
1
) by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{f},D)=0,\quad\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad t\in[0,T],

(14)

where
D
is a consistent approximation of the first derivative on the grid, that is for a smooth
u
we have
(D\mathbf{u})_{j}=\partial_{x}u|_{x=x_{j}}+O(\Delta x^{r})
with
r>0
. The discrete differential operator
\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{f},D)
denotes a consistent discrete approximation of the divergence of the PDE flux
\partial_{x}f
including the periodic boundary conditions. We introduce the notion of entropy stability for the consistent semi-discrete approximation (
14
).

Definition 2.1
.

A consistent semi-discrete approximation of the nonlinear conservation law (
1
) is called entropy stable (entropy conservative) if
\norm{\vb{u}(T)}^{2}_{h}\leq K_{n}\norm{\vb{u}_0}^{2}_{h}
(
\norm{\vb{u}(T)}^{2}_{h}=\norm{\vb{u}_0}^{2}_{h}
) for some moderate and real constant
K_{n}>0
independent of the mesh parameters.

High-order accurate and entropy-stable methods for nonlinear conservation laws can often be developed by combining SBP discrete derivative operators with a skew-symmetric (split-form) reformulation of the conservation laws. This approach eliminates the need to invoke the chain rule and product rule when establishing entropy stability. However, it is crucial that the linearization of the semi-discrete approximation given by (
14
) is strictly stable, see Definition
2.3
below.

Let
A=\operatorname{diag}{([a(x_{1},t),a(x_{2},t),\cdots,a(x_{N},t)]})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and
Q(A,D)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, with
\mathbf{\delta u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
denoting the approximation of the perturbation on the grid and
Q(A,D)\mathbf{\delta u}
denotes the linearization of the nonlinear semi-discrete differential operator
\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{f},D)
such that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{\delta u}=Q(A,D)\mathbf{\delta u},\quad\mathbf{\delta u}(0)=\mathbf{\delta u}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad t\in[0,T].

(15)

Further, we assume that the linearized semi-discrete equation (
15
) is a consistent approximation of the linearized conservation law (
5
). The following definitions are significant for the current study.

Definition 2.2
.

A consistent semi-discrete approximation of the linearized conservation law (
5
) is called stable if there exist constants
K_{n},\eta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
with
K_{n}>0
such that the estimate
\norm{\vb{\up}(T)}^{2}_{h}\leq K_{n}e^{\eta_{n}t}\norm{\vb{\up}_0}^{2}_{h}
holds.

If the numerical growth rate is an arbitrary real number,
\eta_{n}=\eta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, this corresponds to Lax stability
[
23
]
. As is well known, a Lax-stable numerical method can allow non-physical numerical growth that does not occur in the continuous problem, which may require excessively fine grid resolutions to obtain accurate simulations. To be effective, the numerical growth rate
\eta_{n}
should not exceed the continuous growth rate
\eta_{c}
. In particular, if the continuous problem does not support any growth, the numerical method should also exhibit no growth. This property is often referred to as strict stability; see
[
16
]
for further details. We now provide the formal definition.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\eta_{c}\in\mathbb{R}
denote the continuous growth rate of the solutions of the linear conservation law (
5
). A consistent semi-discrete approximation of (
5
) is called strictly stable if there exist constants
K_{n},\eta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
with
K_{n}>0
and
\eta_{n}\leq\eta_{c}+O(\Delta x)
, such that the estimate
\norm{\vb{\up}(T)}^{2}_{h}\leq K_{n}e^{\eta_{n}T}\norm{\vb{\up}_0}^{2}_{h}
holds.

A strictly stable numerical method guarantees that the asymptotic numerical growth rate does not surpass the continuous growth rate. This is crucial for maintaining the accuracy of numerical solutions, especially in long-term simulations. For nonlinear conservation laws, strict linear stability is essential to prevent unresolved high-frequency wave modes from dominating the simulation. Consequently, the following definition is fundamental to the present study.

Definition 2.4
.

Consider the semi-discrete approximation (
14
) of the nonlinear conservation law (
1
) and the linearized semi-discrete equation (
15
). The nonlinear semi-discrete approximation (
14
) is called locally energy stable if the linearized semi-discrete equation (
15
) is strictly stable.

The local energy stability properties of the nonlinear semi-discrete approximation (
14
) are closely related to the eigenvalues of the discrete differential operator
Q(A,D)
defined in (
15
). Specifically, let
\lambda_{\max}:=\max_{j}\real(\lambda_{j}(Q))
denote the largest real part among the eigenvalues of
Q(A,D)
. A necessary condition for ensuring local energy stability is that
\lambda_{\max}\leq\eta_{c}+O(\Delta x)
.
Furthermore, if the discrete differential operator
Q(A,D)
is diagonalizable, then the estimate

\norm{\vb{\up}(T)}_{h}^{2}\leq K_{n}e^{\lambda_{\max}T}\norm{\vb{\up}_0}_{h}^{2}

holds. The subsequent sections will provide more specific applications of these concepts, particularly in the context of the DP FD/DG SBP-SAT approximation of the inviscid Burgers’ equation and the nonlinear SWEs.

3
A DP SBP approximation for the scalar conservation law

In this section, we will give a quick introduction to entropy stable DP SBP approximation for the scalar nonlinear conservation law (
1
). Detailed analysis of local linear/energy stability for the approximation will be presented, focusing on the Burgers’ equation. For systems of conservation laws and more elaborate discuss, we refer the reader to the recent work
[
5
]
.

3.1
Skew-symmetric split-form

Entropy stable schemes for nonlinear conservation laws typically begins at the continuous level by deriving the skew-symmetric form.
The skew-symmetric form is often found by splitting the divergence of the flux as a convex combination of the nonlinear conservation laws in flux/conservative form and the quasi-linear form so that the energy method can be applied without the chain rule and/or the product rule. For a scalar PDE flux
f(u)
we have

\partial_{x}f(u)\equiv F(u,f,\partial_{x}):=\alpha\partial_{x}f+(1-\alpha)\partial_{u}f\partial_{x}u,\quad\alpha\in[0,1],

which is a convex combination of the flux/conservative form and the advective form. For Burgers’ equation with
f(u)=u^{2}/2
we have
\alpha=2/3
and

F(u,f,\partial_{x}):=1/3\partial_{x}(u^{2})+1/3u\partial_{x}u.

It is significantly noteworthy that if
\alpha=1
we regain the flux/conservative form, that is
F(u,f,\partial_{x})=\partial_{x}f
.

3.2
The DP SBP operator for
d/dx

Recently, the DP SBP FD framework
[
26
,
4
,
44
]
was introduced to improve the accuracy and flexibility of numerical approximations of PDEs. The DP SBP operators are a pair of forward and backward finite difference stencils that together obey the SBP property. The DP upwind SBP framework for the first derivative
d/dx

[
26
,
4
]
can be expressed through the following assumptions:

A.1

There exists
H:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
which defines a positive discrete measure

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}\rangle_{H}=\mathbf{g}^{T}{H}\mathbf{g}>0,\quad\forall\,\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad\langle\mathbf{1},\mathbf{g}\rangle_{H}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}h_{j}g(x_{j})\to\int_{0}^{L}g(x)\differential{x},

for all
g\in C^{0}([0,L])
.

A.2

There exists a pair of linear operators
D_{\pm}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
with

\left(D_{\pm}\mathbf{f}\right)_{j}=\derivative{f}{x}|_{x=x_{j}}
,
for all
j\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
and
f\in V^{p}
where
V^{p}
is a polynomial space of at most degree
p\geq 0
.

A.3

The linear operators
D_{\pm}
together obey
\langle D_{+}\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\rangle_{H}+\langle\mathbf{f},D_{-}\mathbf{g}\rangle_{H}=f_{N}g_{N}-f_{1}g_{1}
for all
\mathbf{f},\,\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

A.4

The linear operators
D_{\pm}
together obey

\langle\mathbf{f},\left(D_{+}-D_{-}\right)\mathbf{f}\rangle_{H}\leq 0
for all
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Remark 1
.

The DP SBP DG framework of
[
15
]
satisfies Assumptions (
\mathbf{A}
.1)–(
\mathbf{A}
.4) with
x_{j}
,
w_{j}>0
being the nodes and weights of a Legendre-Gauss-Lobatto (LGL) numerical quadrature.

Remark 2
.

The traditional SBP operator
(D,H)
–based on central FD
[
20
,
39
,
6
,
41
]
–or collocated DGSEM
[
10
,
43
,
12
]
–also satisfies the DP-SBP framework, that is Assumptions (
\mathbf{A}
.1)–(
\mathbf{A}
.4), with
D_{-}=D_{+}=D
and
\langle\mathbf{f},\left(D_{+}-D_{-}\right)\mathbf{f}\rangle_{H}=0
.
Similarly, given the upwind DP-SBP operator
(D_{-},D_{+},H)
which satisfies Assumptions (
\mathbf{A}
.1)–(
\mathbf{A}
.4), the averaged operator

D:=\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{+}+D_{-}\right)

is a discrete derivative operator which satisfies the traditional SBP framework
(D,H)
.

The SBP operators alone do not guarantee numerical stability; they must be applied carefully with appropriate boundary treatments. The DP-SBP framework offers advantages over traditional SBP schemes by providing pairs of derivative operators and increased flexibility, enabling the design of schemes with desirable properties. For conservative PDEs, energy/entropy conservation can be achieved due to the DP-SBP property (Assumption
\mathbf{A}.3
), where operators
\mathcal{Q}_{\pm}:=H{D}_{\pm}-\frac{1}{2}B
, with
B=\diag([-1,0,\cdots,0,1])
, satisfy
\mathcal{Q}_{-}+\mathcal{Q}_{+}^{T}=0.
Additionally, entropy or energy dissipation schemes can be constructed, useful for upwinding or damping unresolved modes, primarily enabled by the upwind DP-SBP (Assumption
\mathbf{A}.4
).
Using penalties we will extend the DP SBP operators to multi-block operators and implement the periodic boundary conditions weakly.

For simplicity, we split the spatial domain into two elements, with
K=2
and

\Omega=\Omega_{k}\cup\Omega_{k+1}
. The method and analysis extend to many more elements. For each element
\Omega_{k}
,
k\in\{1,2\}
, we discretize the domain into
N
grid points and sample the solutions on the grids. For the multi-block FD method the grids are equidistant nodes and for the DG method the grids are given by the LGL nodes. The semi-discrete solution is denoted
\mathbf{u}^{k}=({u}_{1}^{k},{u}_{2}^{k},\cdots,{u}_{N}^{k})^{T}
. The two elements are connected at the interface
x=x_{k}
with the interface conditions

\displaystyle[\![{\mathbf{u}}]\!]:={u}_{1}^{k+1}-{u}_{N}^{k}=0,\quad[\![{\mathbf{f}}]\!]:={f}_{1}^{k+1}-{f}_{N}^{k}=0.

For the global solutions in the two elements, we introduce the augmented discrete solution vector and discrete flux vector, given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\begin{bmatrix}{u}_{1}^{k},\cdots,{u}_{N}^{k},{u}_{1}^{k+1},\cdots,{u}_{N}^{k+1}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N},\quad\mathbf{f}=\begin{bmatrix}{f}_{1}^{k},\cdots,{f}_{N}^{k},{f}_{1}^{k+1},\cdots,\mathbf{f}_{N}^{k+1}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}.

To implement the interface conditions and the periodic boundary conditions we also define the boundary and interface penalty matrices

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{I}=\begin{pmatrix}-\mathbf{e}_{N}\mathbf{e}_{N}^{T}&\mathbf{e}_{N}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T}\\
-\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{N}^{T}&\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T}\end{pmatrix},\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{I}=\begin{pmatrix}-\mathbf{e}_{N}\mathbf{e}_{N}^{T}&\mathbf{e}_{N}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T}\\
\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{n}^{T}&-\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{B}_{N}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T}&-\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{N}^{T}\\
\mathbf{e}_{N}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T}&-\mathbf{e}_{N}\mathbf{e}_{N}^{T}\end{pmatrix},\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{N}=\begin{pmatrix}-\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T}&\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{N}^{T}\\
\mathbf{e}_{N}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T}&-\mathbf{e}_{N}\mathbf{e}_{N}^{T}\end{pmatrix},

(16)

where
\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{u}=u(x_{1})
and
\mathbf{e}_{N}^{T}\mathbf{u}=u(x_{N})
.
We have

\mathbf{B}_{I}\mathbf{u}=\left(0,\cdots,0,[\![{\mathbf{u}}]\!],[\![{\mathbf{u}}]\!],0,\cdots,0\right)^{T},\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{I}\mathbf{u}=\left(0,\cdots,0,[\![{\mathbf{u}}]\!],-[\![{\mathbf{u}}]\!],0,\cdots,0\right)^{T},

The interface matrices
\mathbf{B}_{I}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{I}
will be used to weakly couple the elements together to the global domain and the boundary matrices
\mathbf{B}_{N}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{N}
will weakly implement the period boundary conditions. Note also that the matrices
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{I}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{N}
are symmetric and negative semi-definite, that is
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{I}=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{I}^{T}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{N}=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{N}^{T}
and

\mathbf{u}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{I}\mathbf{u}=-[\![{\mathbf{u}}]\!]^{2}=-(u_{1}^{k+1}-u_{N}^{k})^{2}\leq 0,

\mathbf{u}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{N}\mathbf{u}=-(u_{1}^{1}-u_{N}^{K})^{2}\leq 0.

We introduce the multi-block operators

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=\begin{pmatrix}H&0\\
0&H\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{D}=\begin{pmatrix}D&0\\
0&D\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{D}_{\pm}=\begin{pmatrix}D_{\pm}&0\\
0&D_{\pm}\end{pmatrix},\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{\pm}=\mathbf{D}_{\pm}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{B}_{N}+\mathbf{B}_{I}\right),

(17)

where the penalty terms weakly implement the interface and the periodic boundary conditions. The penalized multi-block operators
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{\pm}
also satisfy the identities

\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{+}\mathbf{f},\,\mathbf{g}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{H}}+\left\langle\mathbf{f},\,\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{-}\mathbf{g}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{H}}=0,\,\left\langle\mathbf{f},\,\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{+}-\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{-}\right)\mathbf{f}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{H}}=\left\langle\mathbf{f},\,\left(\mathbf{D}_{+}-\mathbf{D}_{-}\right)\mathbf{f}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{H}}\leq 0,\quad\forall\,\mathbf{f},\,\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}.

(18)

For simplicity, we will ignore the
\sim
in the penalized SBP operators and write
\mathbf{D}_{\pm}=\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{\pm}
where
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{\pm}
are multi-block and periodic operators implemented using penalties as in (
17
).

3.3
An entropy stable DP SBP approximation

A skew-symmetric SBP approximation of the divergence of the PDE flux including the periodic boundary condition is

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{f},\mathbf{D}):=\alpha\mathbf{D}\mathbf{f}+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{\partial_{u}f}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{u},\quad\mathbf{D}=\frac{\mathbf{D}_{+}+\mathbf{D}_{-}}{2}.

(19)

For Burgers’ equation we have

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{f},\mathbf{D}):=1/3\mathbf{D}\left(\mathbf{u^{2}}\right)+1/3\mathbf{u}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}.

A multi-block entropy stable DP SBP semi-discrete approximation of the systems of 1D conservation laws (
1
) with volume upwinding reads

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{f},\mathbf{D})=0,\quad\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{f},\mathbf{D}):=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{D})-\gamma\mathbf{\mathcal{D}^{s}}\mathbf{g},\quad\mathbf{\mathcal{D}^{s}}=\frac{\mathbf{D}_{+}-\mathbf{D}_{-}}{2},

(20)

where
\gamma>0
is the volume upwind parameter and
\mathbf{g}
is the entropy variable. If the continuous problem (
1
) admits a square entropy and
\mathbf{D}_{+},\mathbf{D}_{-}
satisfy the DP SBP properties then it follows that the nonlinear discrete energy/entropy estimate holds, that is

\derivative{t}{\norm{\vb{u}(t)}^{2}_{h}}=\gamma\mathbf{g}^{\top}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{\mathcal{D}^{s}})\mathbf{g}\leq 0\implies\norm{\vb{u}(T)}^{2}_{h}\leq\norm{\vb{u}(0)}^{2}_{h}.

(21)

In the absence of volume DP upwinding, that is
\gamma=0
or
\mathbf{\mathcal{D}^{s}}=0
, the total energy/entropy is conserved,
\norm{\vb{u}(T)}^{2}_{h}=\norm{\vb{u}(0)}^{2}_{h}
. An entropy/nonlinearly stable numerical method can ensure robustness and the boundedness of the numerical solution for nonlinear problem. We refer the reader to
[
5
]
for more elaborate discussions. However, as shown in
[
11
]
, a numerical method can be nonlinearly stable and at the same allow the growth of unresolved nonlinear numerical wave modes which can corrupt the results of numerical simulations of nonlinear conservation laws.

3.4
Local linear/energy stability

Our primary goal in this study is the investigation of the linear stability properties of the recent paper
[
5
]
. As a model problem we will consider the model problem (
1
) and the corresponding DP-SBP entropy stable approximation (
20
).

Next, consider a smooth base-flow
\mathbf{U}
and linearize the nonlinear semi-discrete approximation (
20
)

\derivative{t}\mathbf{\delta u}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\delta u},\quad\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\delta u}=-\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\delta u})-\underbrace{(1-\alpha)\left(\left(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{D}_{U}\mathbf{\delta u}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{\delta u}\right)-\mathbf{D}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\delta u}\right)\right)}_{\text{destablizing term}}+\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}^{s}}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{\delta u}).

(22)

Here
\mathbf{D}_{U}=\operatorname{diag}{([(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{U})_{1},(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{U})_{2},\cdots(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{U})_{2N}])}
, and
A,B,C

are diagonal matrices, with
A_{ii}=a(x_{i},t)
,
B_{ii}=b(x_{i},t)
,
C_{ii}=c(x_{i},t)
, where

a=\partial_{u}f|_{u=U}
is the Jacobian of the flux,
b=\partial_{U}a
is the Hessian of the flux, and
c=\partial_{u}g|_{u=U}
is the Jacobian of the entropy variable with respect to the conserved variable. For Burgers’ equation
a=U
,
b=1
, and
c=1
, yielding

\derivative{t}\mathbf{\delta u}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\delta u},\quad\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{D}\mathbf{A}-\underbrace{(1-\alpha)\left(\mathbf{D}_{U}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{D}\mathbf{A}\right)}_{\text{destabilizing term}}+\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}^{s}},

(23)

In (
22
) and (
22
), the "destabilizing" terms inside the bracket with the factor
(1-\alpha)
are a residual of the chain/product rule. That is

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\mathbf{D}_{U}\mathbf{\delta u}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{\delta u}-\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\delta u})

\displaystyle\approx(\partial_{U}a)(\partial_{x}U)\delta u+a(\partial_{x}\delta u)-\partial_{x}(a\delta u)

\displaystyle=(\partial_{x}a)\delta u+a(\partial_{x}\delta u)-\partial_{x}(a\delta u)\equiv 0.

Note that for Burgers’ equation
a=U
.
The "destabilizing" terms will vanish if
\alpha=1
or if the discrete derivative operator
\mathbf{D}
satisfies both the chain rule and the product rule.
Note in particular that
\alpha=1
and
\gamma>0
will correspond to the upwind method of
[
15
,
30
]
, which are well-known to be linearly stable. However, these schemes are not nonlinearly stable and may lack the robustness necessary for simulating nonlinear phenomena such as turbulence and shocks, see
[
5
]
.
When
\alpha\neq 1
and
\gamma=0
, to ensure local linear stability the discrete derivative operator
\mathbf{D}
must satisfy both the chain rule and the product rule. However,
\mathbf{D}
will only satisfy these rules in the trivial case of a constant background flow
\mathbf{U}=\text{const.}
It appears that the skew-symmetric flux splitting, which ensures nonlinear and entropy stability, can actually destabilize the linearized semi-discrete approximation and amplify grid-scale errors. In the context of finite element discretization
[
37
,
25
]
, efforts such as over-integration have been employed to reduce aliasing errors. Nonetheless, these approaches often do not yield satisfactory results, as they can introduce other stability issues.

In the following analysis,
as in the continuous setting, we will consider the background flow such that the coefficient
a(x,t)
does not change sign within the domain, and the linearize continuous problem does not support growth. Thus if
\alpha=1
and
\gamma=0
, we have

\displaystyle\norm{\vb{\up}(\cdot, T)}^{2}_{|AH|}\leq e^{\eta_{d}T}\norm{\vb{\up}_0}^{2}_{|AH|},\quad\eta_{d}=\max_{j,t}\frac{\partial_{t}|a(x_{j},t)|}{|a(x_{j},t)|}.

(24)

Therefore, if
\alpha=1
and
\gamma=0
and in addition the base-flow is time-invariant that is
\partial_{t}U=0\implies\partial_{t}a(x,t)=0\implies\eta_{d}=0
, then we have

\displaystyle\norm{\vb{\up}(\cdot, T)}^{2}_{H}\leq K\norm{\vb{\up}_0}^{2}_{H},\quad K=\frac{\max_{j}|a(x_{j})|}{\min_{j}|a(x_{j})|}>0.

(25)

Note that in (
24
) and (
25
), the numerical growth rate is bounded by the continuous growth rate, i.e.,
\eta_{d}\leq\eta_{c}
, thus yield local linear energy stability fot the discretization. However, setting
\alpha=1
does not yield an entropy-stable scheme for the nonlinear problem. Furthermore, detailed numerical experiments conducted in
[
5
]
demonstrate that the linearly stable scheme with
\alpha=1
is not robust; when nonlinear phenomena are dominant it crashes before reaching the final time, even with a non-vanishing upwind parameter (
\gamma>0
). Our goal is to show that the DP FD/DG SBP method (
20
) can simultaneously preserve both nonlinear/entropy stability and local linear stability. Achieving this ensures robustness and prevents grid-scale errors from contaminating the numerical simulations.

For the Burgers’ equation, discretized using the DP FD/DG SBP method (
20
), we will demonstrate that, for
\alpha={2}/{3}
, it is possible to select a positive volume upwinding parameter
\gamma>0
such that the scheme is both entropy stable and locally linear energy stable. This result will ensure both entropy/nonlinear stability and local linear/energy stability, thereby improving the accuracy of numerical simulations and enabling the proof of convergence for sufficiently smooth solutions.
As discussed in the previous section, the local linear and energy stability of the semi-discrete approximation (
20
) for the nonlinear conservation law is determined by the eigenvalues of the linear operator
\mathbf{Q}
, defined in (
23
). In particular, if the base-flow is sufficiently smooth and does not change sign within the domain, then we must have
\Re{\lambda(\vb{Q})}\leq 0
.

3.5
A finite volume reformulation for the Burgers’ equation

Here, we will consider specifically the Burgers’ equation and the semi-discrete DP SBP method (
22
) in a single element and reformulate the reformulate as finite volume method by introducing numerical fluxes
\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{N}
. For the Burgers’ equation, with the PDE flux
f(u)=u^{2}/2
, the finite volume reformulation of the DP FD SBP semi-discrete approximation (
20
) is

\displaystyle\derivative{t}{\mathbf{u}_{i}}+\frac{\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{N}-\mathbf{f}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{N}}{\Delta{x}}=0.

(26)

The numerical fluxes
\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{N}
decompose into

\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{N}=\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{C}+\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{R}+\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{U},

where
\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{C}
is the centered flux,
\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{R}
is the residual flux,
\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{U}
is the upwind flux, and are defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{C}

\displaystyle=\Delta{x}\sum_{j>i}D_{ij}\mathbf{f}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{f}_{i},\quad\mathbf{f}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{C}=-\Delta{x}\sum_{j<i}D_{ij}\mathbf{f}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{f}_{i},

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{R}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\Delta{x}\sum_{j>i}D_{ij}(\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{j})^{2},\quad\mathbf{f}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{R}=\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\Delta{x}\sum_{j<i}D_{ij}(\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{j})^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{U}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\Delta{x}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{s}_{ii}\mathbf{u}_{i}+\sum_{j>i}\mathcal{D}^{s}_{ij}\mathbf{u}_{j}\right),\quad\mathbf{f}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{U}=\gamma\Delta{x}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{s}_{ii}\mathbf{u}_{i}+\sum_{j<i}\mathcal{D}^{s}_{ij}\mathbf{u}_{j}\right).

Note that when
\alpha=1
and
\gamma=0
we get
\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{R}=0,\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{U}=0
which yields the SBP scheme applied to the flux/conservative form. With
\alpha=2/3
and
\gamma=0
or
\mathbf{\mathcal{D}^{s}}=0
we obtain
\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{U}=0
which corresponds to the skew-symmetric and entropy conserving SBP scheme without volume upwinding. However,
\alpha=2/3
and
\gamma>0
yields the skew-symmetric and entropy stable DP-SBP scheme with volume upwinding.

Next we linearized the semi-discrete approximation (
26
) of the Burgers’ equation around a smooth base-flow,
\mathbf{U}_{i}=U(x_{i},t)
, we have

\displaystyle\derivative{t}{\delta\mathbf{u}_{i}}+\frac{\delta\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{N}-\delta\mathbf{f}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{N}}{\Delta{x}}=0.

(27)

Again, the linear numerical fluxes
\delta\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{N}
decompose into

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{N}=\delta\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{C}+\delta\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{R}+\delta\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{U},

(28)

where
\delta\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{C}
is the centered flux,
\delta\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{R}
is the residual flux,
\delta\mathbf{f}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{U}
is the upwind flux, and are defined by

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{C}

\displaystyle=\Delta{x}\sum_{j>i}D_{ij}\mathbf{\delta u}_{j}\mathbf{U}_{j}+\mathbf{\delta u}_{i}\mathbf{U}_{i},\quad\delta\mathbf{f}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{C}=-\Delta{x}\sum_{j<i}D_{ij}\mathbf{\delta u}_{j}\mathbf{U}_{j}+\mathbf{\delta u}_{i}\mathbf{U}_{i},

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{R}

\displaystyle=-\left({1-\alpha}\right)\Delta{x}\sum_{j>i}D_{ij}(\delta\mathbf{u}_{i}-\delta\mathbf{u}_{j})(\mathbf{U}_{i}-\mathbf{U}_{j}),\quad\delta\mathbf{f}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{R}=\left({1-\alpha}\right)\Delta{x}\sum_{j<i}D_{ij}(\delta\mathbf{u}_{i}-\delta\mathbf{u}_{j})(\mathbf{U}_{i}-\mathbf{U}_{j}),

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{U}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\Delta{x}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{s}_{ii}\delta\mathbf{u}_{i}+\sum_{j>i}\mathcal{D}^{s}_{ij}\delta\mathbf{u}_{j}\right),\quad\delta\mathbf{f}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{U}=\gamma\Delta{x}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{s}_{ii}\delta\mathbf{u}_{i}+\sum_{j<i}\mathcal{D}^{s}_{ij}\delta\mathbf{u}_{j}\right).

Recall that the linear centered flux
\delta\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{C}
is energy conservative. In contrast, the linear residual flux
\delta\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{R}
is not sign definite and can potentially lead to energy growth when
\alpha\neq 1
. By construction, the linear upwind flux
\delta\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{U}
is energy dissipative.
Our goal is to select the upwind parameter
\gamma>0
– sufficiently large and optimally chosen – so that the linear residual flux
\delta\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{R}
is effectively "absorbed" by the linear upwind flux. In other words, we want:

\delta\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{N}=\delta\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{C}+\bar{\gamma}\delta\mathbf{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{U},

for some
\bar{\gamma}>0
.
The choice of
\gamma>0
or
\bar{\gamma}>0
depends on the order of accuracy of the specific DP-SBP operator employed. For first-order accurate DP-SBP operators
D_{\pm}
, the interior stencils are

\displaystyle\Delta{x}D

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[-1,0,1\right],\quad\Delta{x}\mathcal{D}^{s}=\frac{1}{2}\left[1,-2,1\right],

(29)

and the linear flux

\displaystyle 2\mathbf{\delta f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{N}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(\delta u_{i+1}U_{i+1}+\delta u_{i}U_{i}\right)}_{2\mathbf{\delta f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{C}}+\bar{\gamma}\underbrace{\left(\delta u_{i}-\delta u_{i+1}\right)}_{2\mathbf{\delta f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{U}},

(30)

where
\bar{\gamma}=(1-\alpha)(U_{i+1}-U_{i})+\gamma
. Thus if

\gamma\geq\gamma_{opt}=(1-\alpha)\max_{i}|(U_{i+1}-U_{i})|\approx(1-\alpha)\max_{i}|\Delta x\partial_{x}U(x_{i},t)|
then we must have
\bar{\gamma}>0
. Note that for
\alpha=2/3
, we have
\gamma_{\mathrm{opt}}=1/3\max_{i}|\Delta x\partial_{x}U(x_{i},t)|\leq 2/3\max_{i}|U_{i}|<\gamma_{\mathrm{LF}}=\max_{i}|U_{i}|
where
\gamma_{\mathrm{LF}}
denotes the upwind parameter given by the global Lax-Friederichs’ scheme
[
8
]
. For a sufficiently smooth base-flow, we have
\gamma_{\mathrm{opt}}=1/3\max_{i}|\Delta x\partial_{x}U(x_{i},t)|\to 0
as
\Delta{x}\to 0
.

For the third order accurate DP-SBP operators
D_{\pm}
we have the interior stencils

\displaystyle\Delta{x}D

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}\left[1,-8,0,8,-1\right],\quad\Delta{x}\mathcal{D}^{s}=\frac{1}{12}\left[-1,4,-6,4,-1\right],

(31)

and the linear flux

\displaystyle 12\mathbf{\delta f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(-\delta u_{i+2}U_{i+2}+8\delta u_{i+1}U_{i+1}+5\delta u_{i}U_{i}\right)

\displaystyle+(1-\alpha)\left(8(U_{i+1}-U_{i})(\delta u_{i}-\delta u_{i+1})-(U_{i+2}-U_{i})(\delta u_{i}-\delta u_{i+2})\right)

\displaystyle+\gamma\left(4\left(\delta u_{i}-\delta u_{i+1}\right)-\left(\delta u_{i}-\delta u_{i+2}\right)\right).

Thus for a smooth base-flow
U
, with
U_{i+1}-U_{i}\approx\Delta x\partial_{x}U(x_{i},t)
and
U_{i+2}-U_{i}\approx 2\Delta x\partial_{x}U(x_{i},t)
, we have

\displaystyle 12\mathbf{\delta f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(-\delta u_{i+2}U_{i+2}+8\delta u_{i+1}U_{i+1}+5\delta u_{i}U_{i}\right)}_{12\mathbf{\delta f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{C}}+\bar{\gamma}\underbrace{\left(4\left(\delta u_{i}-\delta u_{i+1}\right)-\left(\delta u_{i}-\delta u_{i+2}\right)\right)}_{12\mathbf{\delta f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{U}},

where
\bar{\gamma}=2(1-\alpha)\Delta x\partial_{x}U(x_{i},t)+\gamma
. Thus if

\gamma\geq\gamma_{opt}=(1-\alpha)\max_{i}|2\Delta x\partial_{x}U(x_{i},t)|
then we must have
\bar{\gamma}\geq 0
. Note again that for a sufficiently smooth base-flow, we have
\gamma_{\mathrm{opt}}=(1-\alpha)\max_{i}|2\Delta x\partial_{x}U(x_{i})|\to 0
as
\Delta{x}\to 0
.

4
Some instructive numerical experiments: Burgers’ equation

We will now conduct some instructive numerical experiments that illustrate and fully align with the analyses presented in the previous section. Specifically, we consider the Burgers equation on the unit interval
x\in[0,1]
, with the initial condition

u(x,0)=2+\sin(\pi(x - 0.7)),

and periodic boundary conditions,
u(0,t)=u(1,t)
for all
t\geq 0
. Note that the initial condition is strictly positive for all
x\in[0,1]
, i.e.,
u(x,0)>0
, and does not change sign within the domain
x\in[0,1]
. Consequently, at the continuous level, the linearization of Burgers’ equation around this initial condition,
U(x)=u(x,0)
, does not support the growth of perturbations. A good numerical method, when linearized around the base-flow, should not support growth of perturbations.

We have linearized the the DP SBP approximation (
20
) of the nonlinear Burgers’ equation around the initial condition
U(x)=u(x,0)
. Subsequently, we computed the eigenvalues of the resulting linear operator
\mathbf{Q}
. The eigenvalue spectra of
\mathbf{Q}
are presented in Figures
2(a)
and
3(a)
for various DP SBP operators, including the recently developed DP DRP FD SBP operators
[
44
]
and the DP DG SBP operators introduced in
[
15
]
.
To guarantee nonlinear stability, we employed the skew-symmetric form with the parameter
\alpha=2/3
. We conducted stability tests for different volume upwind parameters
\gamma
, specifically the case
\gamma=0
(no volume upwinding) and the case
\gamma=\gamma_{\text{opt}}>0
(non-vanishing volume upwinding). The eigenvalue analyses indicate that choosing
\gamma=\gamma_{\text{opt}}>0
prevents the emergence of eigenvalues with positive real parts, that is
\Re{\lambda(\mathbf{Q})}\leq 0
, which is a necessary condition for the linear stability of the entropy-stable DP SBP approximation (
20
) with
\alpha=2/3
.
Conversely, when
\alpha=2/3
and the volume upwind parameter is set to zero (
\gamma=0
), the eigen spectra are evenly split between the negative complex plane (
\Re{\lambda(\mathbf{Q})}<0
) and the positive complex plane (
\Re{\lambda(\mathbf{Q})}>0
). Majority of eigenvalue spectra that exhibit positive real eigenvalues (
\Re{\lambda(\mathbf{Q})}>0
) correspond to numerical modes with exponentially growing amplitudes. This is indicative of unstable and non-physical solutions. Such growing modes are primarily associated with unresolved high-frequency numerical oscillations, which can compromise the accuracy and stability of the numerical simulations.

Despite this stabilization, the linear instability has effectively amplified the initial error, resulting in widespread solution contamination.
Finally, for the case

5
Systems of nonlinear conservation laws: SWEs

Next, we extend the linear stability analysis to systems of nonlinear conservation laws. Specifically, we focus on the numerical approximation of the nonlinear SWEs in both flux/conservative form and skew-symmetric form. In 1D, the unknown vector is
\mathbf{q}=[h,uh]^{T}
, where
h>0
denotes the water height,
u
is the flow velocity, and
g>0
represents the constant acceleration due to gravity. The PDE flux function is given by
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})=[uh,hu^{2}+\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}]^{T}
, and the entropy is the total mechanical energy, expressed as
e(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{2}(hu^{2}+gh^{2})
>0.
As seen in the previous section, one way to ensure local linear/energy is to apply the DP FD/DG SBP operators directly to the flux, see for examples
[
30
,
15
]
. However, while the resulting method is locally linear/energy stable, detailed numerical experiments presented in
[
5
,
30
,
15
]
show that method lacks the robustness that is necessary for simulating nonlinear phenomena such as turbulence and shocks. The DP SBP entropy stable scheme derived in
[
5
]
was shown to be robust. Here we will investigate the local linear/energy stability properties of the method for systems of nonlinear SWEs.

An entropy-stable, multi-block upwind DP SBP method for the nonlinear SWEs–applied to the conserved variables–was developed in
[
5
]
. This method combines the skew-symmetric form with entropy-stable upwind splitting, and employs DP SBP operators to produce a semi-discrete approximation of the one-dimensional shallow water system that preserves key stability properties.
The entropy stable DP FD/DG SBP approximation reads

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}),\mathbf{D}_{x})=0,

(32)

where skew-symmetric SBP approximation of the divergence of the PDE flux is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}),\mathbf{D}_{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{D}({\mathbf{h}\mathbf{u}})\\
\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{D}\left({\mathbf{h}\mathbf{u}}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{D}\left({\mathbf{h}\mathbf{u}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}{\left(\mathbf{h}\mathbf{u}\right)}\mathbf{D}{\mathbf{u}}+{g\mathbf{h}}\mathbf{D}{\mathbf{h}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{g}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}\\
g\mathbf{h}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}^{2}\end{bmatrix},

and spatial discrete operator with interface and volume upwinding is

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}),\mathbf{D}_{x})=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}),\mathbf{D}_{x})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}_{x}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}\otimes\left(\mathbf{B}_{Ix}+\mathbf{B}_{nx}\right)\right)\mathbf{g}-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma\left(I_{m}\otimes\left(\mathbf{D}_{+}-\mathbf{D}_{-}\right)\right)\mathbf{g}.

Here, for the
k
th element,
\Upsilon^{k}=\mathinner{\diag\mathopen{\left([\upsilon_{1}^{k}+\upsilon_{1}^{k+1},\upsilon_{2}^{k}+\upsilon_{2}^{k+1}]\right)}}
is the interface upwind parameter matrix and

\Gamma^{k}=\mathinner{\diag\mathopen{\left([\gamma_{1}^{k},\gamma_{2}^{k}]\right)}}
is the volume upwind parameter matrix with, e.g.,
\upsilon_{1}^{k}=\gamma_{1}^{k}=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}h^{k}_{j}(|u^{k}|+\sqrt{gh^{k}_{j}})/(h^{k}_{j}g+(u^{k}_{j})^{2})\geq 0
,
\upsilon_{2}^{k}=\gamma_{2}^{k}=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}|h_{j}^{k}u_{j}^{k}|\geq 0
.
We define the total semi-discrete entropy
E_{h}(t):=\langle\mathbf{1},\mathbf{e}\rangle_{H}=\mathbf{1}^{T}{H}\mathbf{e}
. Then it follows that

\displaystyle\derivative{t}E_{h}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{\upsilon_{i}^{k}+\upsilon_{i}^{k+1}}{2}[\![{\mathbf{g}_{i}^{k}}]\!]^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{2}{\gamma_{i}^{k}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{i}^{k},\,\left(D_{-}-D_{+}\right)\mathbf{g}_{i}^{k}\right\rangle_{H}}\leq 0.

The numerical method (
32
) is entropy stable for all
\upsilon_{i}^{k}\geq 0
,
\gamma_{i}^{k}\geq 0
. We refer the reader to
[
5
]
for proofs and more elaborate discussion. It is significant to note that the skew-symmetric reformulation can support linearly unstable numerical modes which will corrupt numerical simulations everywhere. Thus, our main objective here is to investigate the local linear stability properties of the nonlinear semi-discrete approximation (
32
).

5.2
Local linear stability analysis for the 2D SWE

We extend the local linear stability analysis to the 2D SWEs. In 2D, the unknown vector field is
\mathbf{q}=[h,uh,vh]^{T}
, where
h>0
denotes the water height,
[u,v]^{T}

g>0
represents the constant acceleration due to gravity. The PDE flux function is given by
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})=[\mathbf{f}_{x},\mathbf{f}_{y}]

e(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{2}(hu^{2}+hv^{2}+gh^{2})
>0. We consider the corresponding 2D skew-symmetric form and extend the entropy stable 1D semi-discrete approximation (

[

\mathbf{Q}

5.3
Barotropic shear instability with fully developed turbulence

Finally, we assess the accuracy of the DP DG/FD SBP numerical framework in capturing turbulent scales. Specifically, we examine the barotropic shear instability
[
18
,
9
,
29
]
, characterized by fully developed turbulence modeled through the 2D nonlinear SWEs. This scenario, also known as the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability, involves initiating a barotropic shear within zonal jets by introducing a sharp fluid discontinuity, augmented with small Gaussian perturbations.

The computational domain is defined as
\Omega=[0,L]^{2}
, where
L=2\pi\times$6371.22\text{\,}\mathrm{km}$

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle=\bar{u}_{0}\left(\operatorname{sech}\frac{y-y_{+}}{10^{6}}-\operatorname{sech}\frac{y-y_{-}}{10^{6}}\right),\quad v_{0}=0,\quad h_{0}=H-\frac{f}{g}\int_{0}^{y}u(x,s)\,ds+\tilde{h}(x,y),

\displaystyle\tilde{h}(x,y)=\bar{h}_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\exp\left(-k\,d_{i}(x,y)\right),\quad d_{i}(x,y)=\frac{(x-x_{i})^{2}}{L^{2}}+\frac{(y-y_{i})^{2}}{L^{2}},

with:

\bar{h}_{0}=0.01H
,

(x_{1},y_{1})=(0.15L,y_{+})
,

(x_{2},y_{2})=(0.85L,y_{-})

,

,

,

,

,

,
and

To demonstrate the effectiveness of the DP-DG framework in resolving turbulent scales, we analyze the shell-integrated kinetic energy and enstrophy spectra in our simulations (see Figure

\displaystyle E_{n}=\sum_{n\leq|\mathbf{k}|<n+1}E_{\mathbf{k}},\quad\text{where}\quad E_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{u}(\mathbf{k})\hat{u}^{*}(\mathbf{k})+\hat{v}(\mathbf{k})\hat{v}^{*}(\mathbf{k})\right),

with
(\hat{u}(\mathbf{k}),\hat{v}(\mathbf{k}))
representing the Fourier-transformed velocity components,
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},k_{2})
denoting a wavenumber vector, and

indicating complex conjugation. Here,
|\mathbf{k}|=\sqrt{k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}}
is the magnitude of the wavenumber, and
E_{n}
reflects the spectral density corresponding to wavenumber

L/n

L
being the domain size.
The enstrophy spectrum, assuming isotropy, is defined as
E_{\omega}(k)=k^{2}E_{k}
, consistent with the expected power-law cascades observed in two-dimensional hydrodynamic turbulence
[
1

For the DGSEM without volume upwinding (
\Gamma=0

In contrast, for the DP DG scheme with volume upwinding (
\Gamma>0

k^{-3.5}
power law
[
19
]

[
2
]
).
Similarly, the enstrophy spectra (Figure

k^{-1.5}

6
Conclusions

Three desirable properties of numerical methods for nonlinear conservation laws are
[
30
]
:
1) High-order accuracy,
2) Nonlinear (entropy) stability,
3) Local linear-energy stability.
An open question remains whether higher-order methods can simultaneously possess these two stability properties. Nonlinear or entropy stability enhances robustness, allowing simulations to run without crashing
[
5
]
. However, because of the amplification of grid-scale errors from unresolved nonlinear wave modes, numerical simulations might though be stable but still remain inaccurate or fail to converge, even for smooth solutions. Meanwhile, local linear-energy stability ensures that grid-scale errors do not dominate the numerical solution.
As demonstrated in
[
11
]
, many split-form, entropy-stable high-order schemes fail to achieve local linear-energy stability and thus lack this critical property.

In this work, we have investigated the local linear stability of the DP DG/FD SBP schemes recently introduce in
[
5
]
within the split-form framework. Our analysis suggests that the entropy-stable volume upwind filter inherent in these methods can guarantee local linear stability. The DP DG/FD SBP framework introduces a novel numerical approach for designing reliable high-order methods for nonlinear conservation laws that are both provably entropy-stable and locally energy-stable. These theoretical insights are supported by numerical experiments involving the inviscid Burgers’ equation and nonlinear SWEs in both 1D and 2D. Furthermore, we present accurate simulations of 2D barotropic shear instability with fully developed turbulence, demonstrating the effectiveness of the DP SBP method in capturing turbulent scales efficiently.
