Rare Nocardia cyriacigeorgica osteomyelitis in an immunocompromised elderly patient: A case highlighting treatment challenges
Authors: Vaishnavi M Rathod, Nipa Patel, Darshankumar M Raval, Rohit A Chitale, Ravindra Durvasula, Bala Munipalli, Justin M Oring
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNocardia cyriacigeorgica, an emerging pathogen, poses significant risks to immunocompromised patients. We report a rare case of N.cyriacigeorgica-induced osteomyelitis in an 82-year-old woman undergoing chemotherapy for relapsed follicular lymphoma. Her complex medical background included chronic kidney disease, heart failure, and recent skin infections. She presented with severe pain and swelling in a surgically replaced right knee. Initial evaluations, complicated by neutropenia and a necrotic lung lesion, suggested atypical infections. Despite broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, the infection persisted. Synovial fluid analysis confirmed N.cyriacigeorgica, prompting surgical debridement and a modified antibiotic regimen. However, resistance issues and severe side effects complicated treatment, leading to a progressive decline in her condition. This case underscores the challenges of diagnosing and treating N.cyriacigeorgica infections, highlighting the need for enhanced diagnostic methods and personalized treatment approaches to manage resistance and complex patient profiles effectively.Keywords: Nocardia cyriacigeorgica, Osteomyelitis, Immunosuppression, Nocardiosis, Treatment challenges, Antibiotic resistance

Introduction

Nocardiae are aerobic actinomycetes commonly found in soil and water, with approximately 50 recognized species, of which nearly 30 are implicated in human disease. Advances in molecular and proteomic diagnostic techniques, including gene sequencing and Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry, have led to increased recognition of Nocardia cyriacigeorgica (N.cyriacigeorgica) as an emerging pathogen, particularly among immunocompromised hosts such as patients with HIV infection or those receiving immunosuppressive therapies. In addition to its increasing detection, N.cyriacigeorgica is notable for its distinct and often variable antimicrobial resistance profile compared with other Nocardia species, which can complicate therapeutic decision-making and underscores the importance of species-level identification and susceptibility testing. However, detailed clinical reports of N.cyriacigeorgica infections remain scarce [1]. Here, we present a rare case of N.cyriacigeorgica–associated osteomyelitis in an 82-year-old female undergoing chemotherapy. This case provides a unique opportunity to discuss the challenges faced in treating N.cyriacigeorgica in an elderly immunocompromised patient, and the therapeutic approaches employed to manage this patient.

Case presentation

An 82-year-old female with relapsed and refractory follicular lymphoma, treated with mosunetuzumab, copanlisib, R-CHOP, and obinutuzumab since 2017, presented with acute right knee pain, swelling, and erythema. Her medical history was significant for chronic kidney disease stage 2, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (EF 60%), bilateral total knee arthroplasty, stasis dermatitis, incidental pulmonary embolism, and unprovoked seizures, for which she was receiving apixaban and levetiracetam.

At presentation, she exhibited lower-extremity lymphedema and reported worsening right knee pain that had become acutely painful, swollen, and erythematous. She also described a prior severe skin infection involving her arm several months before admission that did not require antibiotic therapy. She denied fever, chills, or night sweats.

Initial evaluation revealed chronic neutropenia and a necrotic right upper lobe lung lesion, raising concern for atypical infection. Infectious Diseases was consulted for suspected prosthetic joint infection and possible pulmonary involvement. Synovial fluid aspirated from the right knee on the day of admission, prior to antibiotic initiation, was positive for alpha defensin with a total nucleated cell count of 34,145 and grew one colony of N.cyriacigeorgica. A repeat synovial fluid aspiration performed approximately one week later again yielded N.cyriacigeorgica. Within the first week of admission, given evidence of prosthetic joint involvement, the patient underwent right total knee arthroplasty irrigation and debridement with polyethylene exchange. Intraoperative findings included cloudy synovial fluid and acute synovitis, though intraoperative cultures demonstrated no growth.

Initial laboratory studies were notable for a white blood cell count (WBC) of 0.9 × 10⁹/L, absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of 0.03 × 10⁹/L, hemoglobin of 8.5 g/dL, and platelet count of 59 × 10⁹/L. Although a staphylococcal prosthetic joint infection (PJI) was initially considered given the acute presentation and prior arthroplasty, her immunocompromised state and pancytopenia broadened the differential to include atypical bacterial and fungal pathogens.

Despite the presence of a necrotic pulmonary lesion, the patient denied respiratory symptoms, including cough, hemoptysis, or night sweats, and reported no known exposure to birds, soil, caves, deserts, or farm animals. The lung lesion was evaluated as a possible occult infectious focus, including bacterial or fungal etiologies; however, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) cultures were negative. A cryptic pulmonary source could not be definitively excluded.

At the time of admission, empiric broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy with imipenem/cilastatin was initiated due to concern for severe bacterial infection in an immunocompromised host, pending definitive microbiologic identification. Following culture confirmation of N.cyriacigeorgica from synovial fluid obtained at admission, antimicrobial therapy was narrowed based on clinical response and evolving hematologic tolerance. Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX), the cornerstone therapy for nocardiosis, was avoided due to baseline cytopenias and the high risk of exacerbating pancytopenia in the setting of ongoing chemotherapy.

Subsequently, the patient was treated with a combination of linezolid and ceftriaxone to balance antimicrobial coverage against Nocardia species while attempting to minimize hematologic toxicity. However, approximately one month after admission, following readmission from rehabilitation for worsening anemia and fatigue, linezolid was discontinued due to progressive pancytopenia.

Antimicrobial therapy was then transitioned to high-dose ceftriaxone (2 g intravenously every 12 h) in combination with doxycycline (100 mg orally twice daily). This regimen was selected as a toxicity-driven, temporizing strategy in the context of limited alternative agents. Although doxycycline demonstrated only intermediate susceptibility (minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 4 µg/mL), its use reflected tolerability considerations rather than first-line preference, particularly given the patient’s inability to tolerate TMP-SMX or prolonged linezolid therapy. This decision was further influenced by the absence of safer fully susceptible oral alternatives in the setting of worsening bone marrow suppression.

Throughout the hospital course, the patient demonstrated persistent cytopenias, with leukocyte counts as low as 0.4 × 10⁹/L, hemoglobin 8.5 g/dL, and platelet count 174 × 10⁹/L. Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, despite in vitro susceptibility, was avoided due to baseline and worsening pancytopenia and was deemed unsafe both for treatment of nocardiosis and for Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis. Antimicrobial therapy with ceftriaxone and doxycycline was continued for approximately 12 weeks; however, subsequent resistance to doxycycline prompted a brief trial of minocycline before initiation of imipenem and amikacin. During this period, the patient was admitted with febrile neutropenia and acute diverticulitis, treated with meropenem, and discharged on oral moxifloxacin.

Despite multiple antimicrobial regimens and supportive interventions, the patient experienced progressive clinical decline with recurrent hospitalizations, persistent cytopenias, and limited remaining therapeutic options. She was deemed not to be a candidate for further surgical intervention due to frailty and medical comorbidities. After multidisciplinary discussions and in alignment with the patient’s and family’s goals of care, a decision was made to transition to comfort-focused management. She was discharged to inpatient hospice, where she received palliative care until her passing.

Discussion

This case represents one of the very few reported instances of N.cyriacigeorgica osteomyelitis and, to our knowledge, the first involving a prosthetic joint with prolonged, toxicity-driven antimicrobial modifications and evolving resistance patterns. The only previously reported comparable case by Raszka et al. described tibial shaft osteomyelitis secondary to pulmonary nocardiosis in an elderly patient managed conservatively with prolonged trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole therapy [2]. In contrast, our case expands the clinical spectrum by highlighting prosthetic joint involvement, profound treatment-limiting cytopenias, sequential antimicrobial intolerance, and the necessity for repeated regimen changes in an immunocompromised host.

Nocardia species are opportunistic bacterial pathogens capable of affecting various organ systems, most commonly the skin, lungs, and brain, particularly in patients with impaired cell-mediated immunity. The infection can be treated with widely available antibiotics; however, it remains potentially fatal in up to 40% of cases. [3] Although nocardiosis is estimated to account for approximately 500–1000 cases annually in the United States, musculoskeletal involvement—and particularly infection due to N.cyriacigeorgica—remains rare and underreported. Improved molecular diagnostics, including MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry and gene sequencing, have increasingly identified N.cyriacigeorgica as a distinct and emerging pathogen with a susceptibility profile that differs from other Nocardia species [1].

The emerging nature of N.cyriacigeorgica has been described across diverse organ systems. Schlaberg et al. first characterized it as a clinically significant species in immunocompromised patients, including those with AIDS and solid organ transplantation, documenting seven cases at a New York City medical center. Notably, this study reported the first N.cyriacigeorgica infections in heart transplant patients [1]. More recently, Altan et al. reported a primary brain abscess caused by N.cyriacigeorgica in a renal transplant recipient, emphasizing its neurotropism and variable antimicrobial response [4]. Together with our case, these reports underscore the organism’s capacity for invasive disease across multiple organ systems and reinforce the importance of early species-level identification to guide therapy.

Raszka et al. reported an 81-year-old female with pulmonary Nocardia who developed osteomyelitis as a secondary complication [2]. In contrast, the source of infection in our patient remains uncertain. She had a history of a severe skin infection involving the arm several months prior to admission, raising the possibility of cutaneous inoculation with subsequent hematogenous spread. Concurrently, imaging revealed a necrotic right upper lobe pulmonary lesion. Although bronchoalveolar lavage was negative and the pulmonary lesion was not biopsied, a primary pulmonary focus with secondary seeding of the knee prosthesis cannot be definitively excluded. This diagnostic ambiguity highlights an important clinical principle: in immunocompromised hosts, Nocardia infection may arise from subtle or clinically silent portals of entry, necessitating heightened diagnostic vigilance even in the absence of classic respiratory symptoms.

A prior report of mandibular osteomyelitis due to N.cyriacigeorgica in a cat prompted evaluation for zoonotic exposure in our patient. Although no pet exposure or animal bites were identified, this consideration informed the diagnostic process and ultimately supported an environmental rather than zoonotic source of infection [5]. This reinforces the environmental ubiquity of Nocardia species and the importance of exposure history in guiding—but not limiting—diagnostic evaluation.

The treatment of Nocardia osteomyelitis involves surgical debridement and prolonged antibiotic therapy, typically starting with intravenous administration [2]. TMP–SMX remains the cornerstone of treatment, often combined with amikacin or a β-lactam for severe disease. However, therapy must be individualized based on species-level susceptibility and patient-specific factors. In our case, TMP-SMX was avoided due to profound baseline pancytopenia and ongoing chemotherapy-related marrow suppression, rendering its use unsafe.

Antimicrobial selection was further complicated by drug-related toxicities and evolving resistance. Initial empiric therapy with imipenem/cilastatin was chosen for its reliable activity against Nocardia species but was discontinued due to seizure risk. Linezolid, despite excellent in vitro susceptibility, led to worsening cytopenias. The subsequent use of ceftriaxone combined with doxycycline represented a toxicity-driven, temporizing strategy rather than a preferred regimen, particularly given the intermediate doxycycline susceptibility and known resistance rates reported by Yang et al., who observed doxycycline resistance in nearly half of Nocardia isolates. He reported on 130 Nocardia isolates collected from 2015 to 2021. The most common species were N.cyriacigeorgica (37.7%) and N.farcinica (20.8%). All isolates were susceptible to linezolid and amikacin, with 97.7% susceptibility to TMP-SMX. However, resistance rates to doxycycline and minocycline were high (46.9% and 34.6%, respectively). Imipenem, ceftriaxone, and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid showed significant non-susceptibility rates of 56.1%, 51.5%, and 64.6%, respectively [6]. Jonathan Pham et al. assessed omadacycline against 300 Nocardia isolates, including N.cyriacigeorgica (21%). Omadacycline showed MIC of 0.06–8 µg/mL, with an MIC50 of 2 µg/mL and MIC90 of 4 µg/mL, demonstrating variable effectiveness among species and suggesting the need for further research on its clinical application [7].

In our patient, sequential resistance ultimately necessitated further regimen escalation, underscoring the challenges of managing nocardiosis in patients with limited therapeutic tolerance. This case also illustrates the ethical and clinical complexity of care in advanced immunocompromised patients. Despite prolonged antimicrobial therapy, repeated hospitalizations, and multidisciplinary management, the patient experienced progressive decline with persistent cytopenias, recurrent infections, and no viable surgical or antimicrobial options. Following comprehensive discussions with the patient and family, care was transitioned to a comfort-focused approach, aligning treatment with goals of care and highlighting the real-world limitations of aggressive therapy in such contexts.

This case contributes novel insight by documenting N.cyriacigeorgica prosthetic joint osteomyelitis complicated by sequential antimicrobial intolerance and resistance, emphasizing the importance of early species-level identification, individualized susceptibility-guided therapy, and realistic goal setting in immunocompromised patients. As N.cyriacigeorgica continues to emerge as a significant cause of nocardiosis across diverse geographic regions, improved diagnostic capacity and expanded therapeutic options are urgently needed.

Conclusion

This case, the second reported case of N.cyriacigeorgica osteomyelitis, highlights significant clinical challenges. The patient’s treatment was complicated by evolving resistance patterns and severe side effects, necessitating chronic suppression therapy due to the inability to remove the prosthetic joint. The complex interplay of species-specific resistance and the patient's existing comorbidities made effective oral chronic suppression therapy particularly challenging. This underscores the urgent need for newer antibiotics and improved laboratory protocols to enhance detection and management. Identifying N.cyriacigeorgica as a prominent cause of nocardiosis across various regions indicates its broader distribution than previously recognized. Accurate species-level identification and combination therapy guided by susceptibility testing are crucial for managing severe nocardiosis, highlighting the need for vigilance and adaptability in treating this challenging pathogen.
