Transplant versus no transplant outcome in adolescents and adults with sickle cell disease eligible for allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation

ABSTRACT
AbstractHematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains the only curative option for sickle cell disease (SCD), but its use in adolescents and adults is limited by toxicity concerns. We evaluated the risk–benefit profile of HSCT in this population through a comparative analysis of transplant versus nontransplant strategies. HSCT was analyzed as a time‐dependent variable in consecutive adolescents and adults with an indication for transplantation who were referred for HSCT counseling. At the last follow‐up, patients had either undergone HSCT or not. HSCT procedures involved matched related donor (MRD) transplants after myeloablative or non‐myeloablative conditioning, and haploidentical transplants after reduced‐intensity conditioning. The primary endpoints were overall survival (OS) and composite event‐free survival (cEFS), calculated from the initial consultation. Events included death, grade III–IV acute or moderate‐to‐severe chronic graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD), graft rejection, overt stroke, third hospitalized painful crises within two years, acute chest syndrome (ACS), delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction, or initiation of new SCD modifying therapy. Ninety‐four patients were included (median follow‐up: 2.9 years), of whom 58 underwent HSCT. The 2‐year OS was 98% with HSCT versus (vs.) 97% without HSCT (P = 0.55). cEFS was significantly higher post‐transplant (72% vs. 28%, P < 0.0001), with marked reduction in painful crises and ACS. Among patients who underwent transplantation, the 2‐year rejection‐free survival from the time of transplant was 91% for MRD and 82% for haploidentical HSCT (P = 0.40). HSCT substantially improves cEFS without excess short‐term mortality, supporting its broader use in adolescents and adults with SCD, particularly when an MRD is available.

INTRODUCTION

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is the most common genetic disorder worldwide and is associated with significant morbidity and early mortality. 1 Despite advances in management over recent decades, SCD continues to cause progressive organ damage, ultimately leading to reduced quality of life and premature death in adults. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the only curative option. Human leukocyte antigen (HLA)‐matched related transplant with myeloablative conditioning (MAC) has been associated with excellent outcomes in children, with a sickle cell‐free survival rate exceeding 95% and a low incidence of chronic graft‐versus‐host disease (cGVHD). 10 In adolescents and adult patients, HSCT is associated with higher rates of GVHD and transplant‐related mortality (TRM). 11 , 12 Reduced‐intensity conditioning (RIC) regimens have been developed to limit toxicity. The key challenge of RIC is to ensure long‐term engraftment with sufficient donor chimerism to prevent the recurrence of SCD. A nonmyeloablative regimen using low‐dose total body irradiation (TBI) and alemtuzumab has shown promising results in adult patients with a matched related donor (MRD). This approach results in long‐term mixed chimerism and immune tolerance, leading to cure in approximately 90% of patients. An important finding is the absence of GVHD, which is, however, accompanied by an increased incidence of graft failure. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17

In patients who lack a matched related donor, haploidentical HSCT with reduced‐intensity conditioning and post‐transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy) may offer a viable alternative. Since the initial experience, which was associated with high graft rejection rates, intensification of the conditioning regimen has led to major improvement in rejection‐free survival, with an acceptable incidence of GVHD. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21

The decision to transplant presents a complex trade‐off: a low, but tangible, short‐term risk of mortality for a high probability of cure versus a higher long‐term risk of mortality from SCD. Additional considerations include the impact of SCD on quality of life and the potential long‐term consequences of transplantation, such as cGVHD and fertility issues. The objective of this study was to assess the risk–benefit ratio of HSCT in adolescents and adults with severe SCD. HSCT was analyzed as a time‐dependent variable in a cohort of consecutive patients referred for HSCT counseling, and clinical outcomes were compared between patients who underwent transplantation and patients who did not undergo transplantation.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study design

This retrospective study was conducted on SCD patients consulting a transplant specialist in the Adolescents and Young Adults (AYA) hematology unit in St Louis hospital, Paris, France, between January 2010 and July 2022. Patients were eligible if they were aged ≥14 years and ≤40 years when considered for haploidentical transplantation, or ≤45 years when considered for matched related transplantation (these age limits reflect institutional practice for each approach). HSCT indications were defined according to the ASH guidelines and EBMT recommendations, and followed the criteria used in the Drep‐Haplo trial for haploidentical transplantation. 19 , 22 , 23 All indications were reviewed and validated by the national SCD‐dedicated transplant review board (Table 1). Patients with a history of solid organ transplantation were excluded. The date of inclusion in the study was the date of the first consultation in the AYA unit. In most cases, HLA‐identical familial donor status was known at the initial consultation, though in some patients, sibling HLA typing was performed afterwards. At the last follow‐up, some patients have undergone transplantation, while some have not. Data from patients who had not undergone transplantation were collected retrospectively from their referral centers; medical records were reviewed to obtain clinical data and information about treatment. Patients or their guardians provided informed consent for data analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the IRB (Avicenne hospital, APHP, CLEA‐2024‐418).

Endpoints

The primary endpoints were overall survival (OS) and composite event‐free survival (cEFS), compared between patients with and without transplant. The secondary endpoints included comparison of the incidence of painful crises and acute chest syndrome (ACS) between the two groups, assessment of target organ involvement in SCD, and an evaluation of post‐transplant outcomes such as graft rejection, SCD‐free survival (survival without rejection), GVHD, and chimerism.

OS and cEFS estimation were defined from the date of the first consultation to the date of death or to the date of the first defined event, respectively. Events considered for cEFS were death, acute GVHD (aGVHD) grade III–IV, moderate‐to‐severe cGVHD, graft failure, overt stroke, third hospitalized painful crises in a period of 2 years, ACS, delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction (DHTR), or need for new disease‐modifying therapy for SCD (second HSCT, hydroxycarbamide, chronic transfusion program, and crizanlizumab). SCD‐free survival was defined from the date of transplant to the date of death or graft rejection. Whole‐blood chimerism was categorized as full donor chimerism (>95% donor cells), rejection (<5% total blood donor cells), low mixed (5%–50% donor cells), and high mixed (50%–95%). Acute and chronic GVHD were evaluated according to the International Bone Marrow Transplant Research and National Institutes of Health consensus. 24 , 25 Progression of organ dysfunction was evaluated as separate events during follow‐up. Significant renal impairment was defined by the introduction of angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE inhibitors) or angiotensin II receptor blockers for proteinuria. Significant cardiac dysfunction was defined by the initiation of ACE inhibitors and/or beta blockers. Assessments of cerebral vasculopathy and pulmonary function were restricted to patients in whom cerebral magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) and pulmonary function tests had been performed both at baseline and during follow‐up.

Transplant modalities

Patients could receive one of three transplant modalities: those with an MRD received either a myeloablative or a non‐myeloablative (NMA) conditioning regimen, while those without a matched related donor underwent a reduced‐intensity haploidentical transplant. Before 2017, MRD transplants were performed using MAC. After 2017, with the development of NMA approaches in adolescents and adults with SCD, NMA regimens became the preferred strategy. The myeloablative approach consisted of a conditioning regimen with intravenous busulfan (12.8 mg/kg), cyclophosphamide (200 mg/kg), and anti‐thymocyte globulin (ATG) (20 mg/kg). The stem cell source was the bone marrow (BM) or cord blood, and GVHD prophylaxis consisted of cyclosporine and a short course of methotrexate. 26 Non‐myeloablative conditioning consisted of alemtuzumab (1 mg/kg) and 3 Gy TBI. The stem cell source was high‐dose peripheral blood stem cells, and sirolimus was used as GVHD prophylaxis. 13 Reduced‐intensity conditioning for haploidentical transplant consisted of ATG (4.5 mg/kg), cyclophosphamide (29 mg/kg), fludarabine (120 mg/m2), and 2 Gy TBI with the addition of thiotepa (10 mg/kg) in the more recent period. The stem cell source was unmanipulated BM (primed by filgrastim in earlier patients), and GVHD prophylaxis was based on high‐dose PTCy, followed by sirolimus and mycophenolate mofetil. 27

Statistical analysis

Qualitative variables are described using their number and frequencies, and compared using Fisher's test. Quantitative variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney test.

As transplantation can be performed at highly variable time points after the first consultation, HSCT was analyzed as a time‐dependent variable to compare transplant versus no‐transplant outcomes in survival analyses. This method avoids immortal time bias, the artificial survival advantage introduced when patients must survive a certain period (until HSCT) to be included; and ensures that all relevant events, including those occurring before HSCT, are properly captured in the analysis. Using this methodology, patients who eventually underwent transplantation were analyzed within the non‐transplant arm until the time of transplant and switched to the transplant arm after transplantation. Thus, for cEFS estimation, analyzed from the first consultation, some patients who had undergone transplantation were analyzed only in the non‐transplanted arm if the first event occurred before transplant. OS and cEFS according to HSCT were represented using the Simon–Makuch method and compared using a Mantel–Byar test. 28 , 29 The impact of HSCT on recurrent events (painful crises and ACS) was tested using a Prentice, Williams, and Peterson model. 30 The probabilities of experiencing or not one of the defined events before and after HSCT were estimated using a non‐parametric time‐inhomogeneous multistate model. 31 In patients who underwent transplantation, the OS and survival with graft rejection were evaluated from the time of HSCT using the Kaplan–Meier estimator. 32 Statistical analyses were performed on R v4.2.2 and Graphpad Prism v10.0.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Ninety‐four patients (47 females and 47 males, median age: 18.8 years [IQR 16.9–26.0]) were included. At the last follow‐up, 58 patients had undergone transplantation and 36 patients had not undergone transplantation. The characteristics of patients who underwent transplantation and patients who did not undergo transplantation are described in Table 1. There was no difference in disease characteristics or treatment at baseline between the two groups; however, patients who did not undergo transplantation were older and had a shorter follow‐up. Most patients presented with severe disease, with a median of 4 (IQR 1–7) hospitalized vaso‐occlusive crises (VOC) in the previous 2 years and a median of 2 (IQR 1–4) ACS in their history. Thirty‐four percent of patients had a history of cerebral vasculopathy, 37.2% had SCD‐related nephropathy, and 9.5% had a history of DHTR. At inclusion, 46.8% of the patients were treated with hydroxycarbamide and 44.7% with chronic transfusions; as expected, cerebral vasculopathy was more frequent in those receiving chronic transfusions (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). Fifty‐five patients were considered for matched related transplant and 39 patients were considered for haploidentical transplant. The characteristics of patients referred for matched related and haploidentical transplant are detailed in Supporting Information S1: Table 2. The main differences were a higher prevalence of a history of abnormal transcranial Doppler (velocity ≥2 m/s) and of DHTR in patients referred for haploidentical transplantation. These patients were also more frequently treated by chronic transfusion programs.

For the 58 patients who underwent transplantation, 59% (n = 34) received a matched related transplant, with 24% (n = 14) after a MAC regimen and 35% (n = 20) after a nonmyeloablative regimen, and 41% (n = 24) received a RIC haploidentical transplant. The median time between inclusion and transplantation was 6 months (IQR 3–9). Among the 36 patients who did not undergo transplantation, the primary reason was patient or donor refusal (n = 20, with n = 13 for patients considered for matched related transplant and n = 7 for haploidentical transplant). No statistical difference was found in baseline characteristics between patients who did not undergo transplantation due to patient or donor refusal and those who did not undergo transplantation for other reasons (Supporting Information S1: Table 3). Transplantation was not conducted in nine patients due to the lack of an available donor: matched sibling in 4 patients and presence of donor‐specific anti‐HLA antibodies in 5 patients with a haploidentical transplant indication (Supporting Information S1: Table 4).

Transplant versus no transplant comparison

OS and composite EFS

In the total cohort, the median follow‐up was 2.9 years (IQR 1.8–4.3), with a follow‐up of 2.7 years posttransplant (IQR 1.3–4.3). Considering HSCT as a time‐dependent variable, the 2‐year OS was estimated to 97% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 92%–100%) in patients without transplantation and 98% (95% CI: 94%–100%) in patients who had undergone transplantation (P = 0.55) (Figure 1A). Four patients died: 2 patients who had not undergone transplantation (1 from cerebral aneurysm rupture and 1 sudden death of undetermined etiology) and 2 after a haploidentical transplant (1 patient with pretransplant ulcerative colitis and pretransplant sclerosing cholangitis died due to gastrointestinal aGVHD on Day 49 posttransplant and 1 patient died due to late pneumococcal sepsis a year and a half after transplantation). The composite EFS was significantly better in patients who underwent transplantation: the 2‐year cEFS was 28% (95% CI: 15%–50%) without transplantation vs. 72% (95% CI: 58%–89%) with transplantation (P < 0.0001) (Figure 1B). Considering the criteria used for cEFS, 25 of the 36 patients who did not undergo transplantation experienced at least one event. Among the 58 patients who underwent transplantation, 20 experienced an event, including 11 that occurred prior to transplantation. These 11 patients were therefore analyzed in the nontransplanted group for the cEFS estimation. Three patients who underwent transplantation continued to report painful episodes, which were considered for cEFS, despite successful engraftment and normalization of hemolytic markers, likely due to residual pains. All severe events, which could be multiple for a single patient, are reported in Table 2: 124 events occurred in patients who did not undergo transplantation or prior to transplantation, and 20 occurred posttransplantation (10 after matched related transplant and 10 after haploidentical transplant). The main events observed in patients who did not undergo transplantation or had not yet undergone transplantation were the occurrence of recurrent painful crises and ACS.

When evaluating the impact of transplantation separately according to donor type, no significant difference in the OS was observed. Among patients referred for an MRD transplant (n = 55), the 2‐year OS was 100% (95% CI noncomputable) with transplantation and 95% (95% CI: 87%–100%) without transplantation (P = 0.24). Among those referred for haploidentical transplantation (n = 39), the 2‐year OS was 95% (95% CI: 85%–100%) with transplantation and 100% (95% CI non computable) without transplantation (P = 0.92) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1A,B). The composite EFS was significantly improved by transplantation in patients referred for MRD transplant, with a 2‐year cEFS of 81% (95% CI: 66%–100%) versus 15% (95% CI: 4%–50%) without transplantation (P < 0.0001). In patients referred for haploidentical transplantation, there was a nonsignificant trend toward improved cEFS with transplantation: the 2‐year cEFS was 54% (95% CI: 33%–89%) versus 44% (95% CI: 26%–76%) without transplantation (P = 0.21) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1C,D).

A nonparametric time‐inhomogeneous multistate model was used to capture patient transitions over time, allowing a comprehensive assessment of events occurring both before and after transplantation, particularly those arising prior to HSCT. In this model, the 2‐year probability of having received HSCT was 60% (95% CI: 49%–70%). At 2 years, 11% (95% CI: 6%–21%) of patients were alive without transplantation and without event, 29% (95% CI: 20%–40%) had not undergone transplantation and had experienced an event, 48% (95% CI: 38%–59%) had undergone transplantation and were event‐free after transplant, and 11% (95% CI: 6%–20%) had undergone transplantation and had experienced an event after transplant (Figure 2).

Painful crises and ACS

The median annual incidence of hospitalized painful crises was 1.59/year per patient (IQR 0.52–2.63) in patients who did not undergo transplantation (median follow‐up of 2.2 years [IQR 1.4–3.4]). In patients who underwent transplantation, the median annual incidence of hospitalized painful crises was 0.0/year per patient (IQR 0.0–1.26) before transplantation and 0.0/year per patient (IQR 0.0–0.0) after transplantation.

Overall, 33 ACS events were recorded involving 18 individuals, with 32 occurring before or without transplantation and 1 after transplantation in a patient with graft failure (Figure 3). Considering HSCT as a time‐dependent variable, HSCT significantly reduced the risk of hospitalization for painful crises (HR = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.08–0.34, P < 0.0001) and for ACS (HR = 0.03, 95% CI: 0.004–0.19, P = 0.0002).

Progression of organ dysfunction during follow‐up

Despite the relatively short follow‐up, 4 patients developed worsening cerebral vasculopathy on MRA: 1 in the group that underwent transplantation due to aneurysm rupture during transplant conditioning and 3 in the group that did not undergo transplantation, including one aneurysm rupture and two cases of progression of pre‐existing stenosis. The occurrence of pulmonary dysfunction was often reported in patients who did not undergo transplantation. In contrast, no patient developed proteinuria requiring treatment (Table 3).

Hemolysis markers

At the last follow‐up, 49 of the 58 patients who underwent transplantation showed normalization of hemolysis parameters, with a median hemoglobin level of 124 g/L (IQR 114–137), reticulocyte count of 78 × 109/L (IQR 59.5–106.9), LDH of 230 U/L (IQR 184–270), and total bilirubin of 6.5 µmol/L (IQR 5.1–10). In these patients, the median HbS level was 38% (IQR 28–41) for patients with an AS donor and 0% (IQR 0–0) for patients with an AA donor. Nine patients who had undergone transplantation continued to display mild hemolysis at the last follow‐up: 4 patients who experienced graft rejection without subsequent re‐transplantation and 5 patients with mixed chimerism (whole‐blood median donor chimerism of 40% [IQR 20–70] at the last‐follow‐up). In these 5 patients, the median hemoglobin level was 123 g/L (IQR 109–132), the median reticulocyte count was 123 × 109/L (IQR 108–155), the median LDH was 259 U/L (IQR 182–272), and the median bilirubin was 12 µmol/L (IQR 10–18). HbS levels were 42%, 30%, and 38% in the three patients with an AS donor, and 44% and 4% in the 2 patients with an AA donor. Four of these 5 patients had received a non‐myeloablative HLA matched transplant and one patient had received a haploidentical transplant.

In the patients who did not undergo transplantation, we observed persistent hemolytic anemia (median 95 g/L, IQR 82–100), higher reticulocyte counts (210 × 109/L, IQR 154–387), elevated LDH (383 U/L, IQR 289–623), and markedly increased bilirubin (41 µmol/L, IQR 24–70).

Posttransplant outcomes

Patient characteristics and outcomes by transplant modality are presented in Table 4. The 2‐year OS from the time of transplant was 100% (95% CI: 100%–100%) after the 34 matched related transplants and 89% (95% CI: 76%–100%) after the 24 haploidentical transplants (P = 0.08) (Figure 4). The 2‐year SCD‐free survival (survival without graft rejection) was 91% (95% CI: 82%–100%) for matched related transplants and 82% (95% CI: 67%–100%) for haploidentical transplants (P = 0.40). Rejection was observed in five patients (8.6%): one after a MAC‐matched related transplant, two after an NMA‐matched related transplant, and two after a RIC haploidentical transplant. One of the three patients who showed rejection after a matched related transplant successfully underwent re‐transplantation without GVHD. No case of moderate to severe cGVHD was reported after matched related transplant and two cases (8.3%) were reported after haploidentical transplant. Among patients who did not show rejection, mixed chimerism was observed in 46% of patients following MAC‐matched related HSCT and in 89% after NMA‐matched related HSCT. After haploidentical HSCT, 9% of patients had mixed chimerism at the last follow‐up. Only four patients had donor chimerism levels below 50% at the last follow‐up (median 3.2 years), all after NMA transplant, including one patient with levels below 20%. Their median level of hemoglobin was 124 g/L (IQR 123–137) with moderate hemolysis: median reticulocyte count at 109 × 109/L (IQR 102–123), LDH at 259 U/L (IQR 138–272), and bilirubin at 12 µmol/L (IQR 11–22). Painful crises after HSCT were observed only in the patient with donor chimerism at 11%, although the HbS level did not exceed 20%.

DISCUSSION

Over the past decades, significant advances have been made in the field of SCD transplantation, including the development of new approaches. Reduced‐intensity conditioning transplant from MRD and haploidentical transplantation have expanded the potential pool of transplant candidates, including patients lacking an HLA‐identical related donor. Despite this, the number of adults undergoing HSCT in high‐income countries remains low compared with the prevalence of SCD. Key barriers include the limited availability of HLA‐matched donors and the restricted availability of haploidentical transplantation to highly specialized centers. Concerns also persist regarding HSCT‐related toxicity, particularly the morbidity associated with chronic GVHD. Nevertheless, it is essential to underscore the severity of SCD in adults. Even in cohorts with potential access to hydroxycarbamide, the median survival ranged from 36 to 53 years. 3 , 4 , 5 , 7 , 8 Thus, a rigorous assessment of the risks and benefits of transplantation, compared to those of the disease treated with standard medical care, is needed.

To compare outcomes between patients who underwent transplantation and patients who did not undergo transplantation, we included consecutive adolescents and adults with a validated indication for HSCT, established during a national SCD‐dedicated transplant review board, who were referred to our center for pretransplant consultation. The main reasons for non‐transplantation were patient's refusal and the lack of a suitable donor, factors unlikely to bias key clinical outcomes. To evaluate the impact of HSCT on OS and composite EFS, transplantation was modeled as a time‐dependent variable: patients were included in the non‐transplanted group until HSCT and in the transplanted group thereafter. This method ensures that all relevant events, including those occurring before HSCT, are properly captured in the analysis. Therefore, patients experiencing events prior to transplantation were classified in the nontransplanted group for cEFS. To better account for this limitation, we additionally applied a nonparametric time‐inhomogeneous multistate model, enabling the capture of events occurring both before and after HSCT.

One of the main objectives was to assess whether HSCT carries an excess risk of mortality compared to standard care. Transplant‐related mortality is primarily early, driven by conditioning toxicity, infections, and acute GVHD, with limited late mortality mainly related to chronic GVHD. In contrast, SCD‐related mortality typically occurs in the medium to long term. Importantly, no excess early mortality was observed in patients who underwent transplantation, and this remained true when specifically analyzing the impact of transplantation in patients referred for haploidentical HSCT, which is traditionally associated with higher mortality. The limited follow‐up does not allow evaluation of long‐term outcomes after transplantation; however, the low incidence of moderate to severe chronic GVHD suggests a minimal contribution of this complication to late mortality, supporting the likelihood of a survival advantage for patients who underwent transplantation in the medium to long term. 33

The second key objective was to compare the composite EFS by incorporating death, acute disease‐related complications, and severe treatment‐related toxicities. We acknowledge that the selection of events included in this composite endpoint may be subject to debate, particularly because not all events can occur in both treatment groups. Moreover, these events differ in their severity and in their clinical significance, as some are transient, whereas others may have long‐term consequences. However, this composite endpoint was designed to capture an integrated assessment of overall morbidity according to treatment strategy. We exhaustively recorded hospitalizations for painful crises and ACS. An event was defined not as a single painful crisis, but as the recurrence of ≥3 painful crises within two years, as repeated vaso‐occlusive episodes are a well‐established marker of SCD severity and are associated with increased mild‐term mortality. 2 , 34 When comparing cEFS with and without transplant using a time‐dependent approach, HSCT markedly improved cEFS. While the difference in cEFS between patients who underwent transplantation and patients who did not undergo transplantation is substantial among those referred for MRD transplantation, it does not reach statistical significance in the smaller group referred for haploidentical transplantation. Notably, all posttransplant deaths and cases of severe GVHD occurred after haploidentical transplantation, suggesting that, despite recent advances, this approach remains associated with higher toxicity compared to MRD transplantation. In the absence of transplantation, most events were recurrent painful crises and ACS, with ACS occurring at a particularly high frequency. In the transplant group, three patients with successful engraftment and normalization of hemolytic markers experienced recurrent pain episodes mimicking VOC during the first months after transplantation. These episodes are commonly reported in adults with a heavy history of VOC who have undergone transplantation and tend to resolve over time. 35 These events may have slightly underestimated cEFS in the transplant arm. Patients who underwent transplantation had few VOC between enrollment and transplantation, likely due to the systematic red cell exchange program implemented for at least 3 months before HSCT. One patient experienced a DHTR after study inclusion, and transplantation was not conducted. Conversely, six patients with a history of DHTR, a condition usually considered a contraindication to transfusion, underwent successful transplantation. These patients received rituximab infusions with extended matching for Duffy, Kidd, and MNS antigens, and systematic RH genotyping to detect partial Rh variants. 36

When focusing on transplants actually performed, outcomes following both matched related and haploidentical HSCT were consistent with previously published data. 10 , 11 , 12 , 27 , 37 In particular, outcomes were excellent after matched related transplantation, with the 3‐year OS and SCD‐free survival reaching 100% (95% CI: 100%–100%) and 91% (95% CI: 82%–100%), respectively, without severe chronic GVHD. Importantly, one patient who experienced graft rejection following an NMA approach subsequently underwent successful retransplantation after the end of the study. In this group of adolescents and young adults, both MAC and NMA transplants seem to be valid options that should be more widely discussed. Haploidentical transplantation was historically associated with poorer outcomes, but thiotepa‐intensified reduced conditioning regimens yield markedly improved SCD‐free survival. Our results are consistent with major series, reporting SCD‐free survival approaching 80% with acceptable rates of GVHD. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21

This study has several limitations. First, this is a retrospective comparison including patients undergoing different transplant modalities, and patients had heterogeneous reasons for not undergoing transplantation. Although a prospective randomized trial would represent the gold standard, no such study has been completed to date in adults. The prospective STRIDE‐2 study, designed to compare patients with an available HLA‐matched donor to those without one, was terminated early due to poor accrual and limited donor availability. 38 Another limitation of this study is referral bias related to donor availability. Preliminary assessment by referring physicians may have led to under‐referral of patients without an HLA‐matched related donor, potentially resulting in underrepresentation of patients eligible for haploidentical HSCT. Despite these limitations, our study reflects real‐world clinical practice and allows comparison between transplantation and non‐transplantation in a cohort of 94 adults. Another limitation is the lack of comprehensive longitudinal data on SCD‐related target organ involvement, due to the absence of systematic evaluations and the relatively short and heterogeneous follow‐up. Robust evidence based on systematic medium‐ and long‐term assessments of organ involvement remains limited; however, normalization of hemolytic parameters may prevent further organ deterioration after transplantation, as suggested by emerging data. 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 Notably, a recent prospective study reported reversal of hyperfiltration following HSCT, supporting a potential role in preventing long‐term renal morbidity. 40 Finally, patient‐reported health‐related quality‐of‐life outcomes were not collected but would have been an important component for comparing the morbidity associated with transplantation versus that of the disease.

In conclusion, among the 94 patients included in our study, the 58 who underwent transplantation experienced no excess mortality, even though a substantial proportion received haploidentical grafts. HSCT demonstrated superior mid‐term cEFS, with outcomes likely to be sustained long term, given the low incidence of chronic GVHD. This study also highlights the high burden of painful crises and ACS observed in the absence of transplantation, complications for which recurrence is known to be associated with increased future mortality. These findings support expanding transplantation indications, particularly for matched related transplant enabling earlier intervention. The results of both ongoing prospective comparative studies, DREPA‐RIC (NCT04046705) and the randomized REDRESS trial (NCT05392894), will be critical to better define the respective roles of MRD and haploidentical transplantation compared with the standard of care in adolescents and adults with severe disease.
