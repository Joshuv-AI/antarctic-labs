Mining the Galium aparine L. microbiome: genome-guided discovery and experimental validation of metallophore-producing endophytic bacteria
Authors: Natalia Rutkowska, Bartosz Sekuła, Olga Marchut-Mikołajczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnvironmental pollution resulting from heavy metals constitutes a critical global issue. Remediation technologies offer potential solutions, particularly through the innovative use of endophytic microbes, either independently or in conjunction with plants. This solution is based on the ability of certain endophytic bacteria to produce metallophores, which are low-molecular-weight compounds capable of chelating various heavy metals.MethodsThis study investigates ten bacterial endophytes isolated from the medicinal plant Galium aparine L. belonging to the Bacillus, Priestia, and Peribacillus genera. We tested different media to efficiently induce their production and assessed their ability to chelate various heavy metals, including highly toxic Pb2+, Cd2+ and Hg2+. Moreover, we examined in detail of their metallophore gene clusters, their organization, diversity and prevalence, by broad homology search.ResultsAll strains exhibited moderate to high metallophore production ability, with few strains capable of chelating more metals than iron. Among them, Priestia sp. GS2 was identified as promising producer, reaching up to 60% SU, with binding activity also towards Co2+, Mn2+, Zn2+, Ni2+ or Cu2+. Also, Peribacillus frigoritolerans GR2 exhibits a remarkable ability to chelate Pb2+, Hg2+ and Cd2+. An in-depth analysis of the biosynthetic gene clusters and enzymes involved in metallophore biosynthesis revealed homologous clusters within previously deposited genomes, highlighting their distribution and potential evolutionary conservation.ConclusionsThe strains demonstrated capacity for metallophore production and heavy metal chelation, which makes them promising candidates for the development of advanced microbial solutions. A genome-guided selection approach can guide the selection of strains for agricultural applications, where they enhance plant nutrient uptake, suppress soil pathogens, and support sustainable fertilization strategies beyond sequestering crucial metals. Apart from agriculture, purified metallophores can aid bioremediation and mobilization of heavy metals from various environments and matrices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13037-7.

Introduction

One of the most pressing environmental challenges today is pollution caused by heavy metals (HMs), a consequence of increasing industrialization and the disruption of natural biogeochemical cycles [1]. HMs originate from both natural sources—such as mineral weathering and volcanic activity—and anthropogenic activities, including mining, extensive use of pesticides and fertilizers, industrial discharge, and sewage sludge disposal [1, 2]. These metals are non-biodegradable and cannot be metabolized by living organisms, making them extremely harmful to humans, animals, and plants. Once released into the environment, they tend to accumulate in the tissues of organisms through bioaccumulation, disrupting physiological functions and leading to various health disorders. In soils, heavy metals significantly reduce microbial populations and biodiversity, negatively affecting soil fertility and ecosystem balance [1]. Some HMs, such as iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), copper (Cu), cobalt (Co), chromium (Cr), zinc (Zn), and nickel (Ni), are essential micronutrients required in trace amounts for proper biological functioning. However, others—namely arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), and mercury (Hg)—are highly toxic, even at low concentrations [3, 4]. Contamination of agricultural soils and water bodies with these toxic metals poses a serious threat to ecosystem health and food safety. Due to their persistence and potential to enter the food chain, heavy metals pollution has become an increasing global concern.

Conventional approaches for the removal of heavy metals from contaminated soils mostly rely on physical and chemical methods. These techniques are often expensive, labor-intensive, and can further harm the environment, rendering the treated soil unsuitable for agricultural use. Therefore, biological methods have gained attention as more cost-effective and sustainable alternatives. Among these, phytoextraction, the uptake and accumulation of pollutants in harvestable plant biomass, has emerged as a promising strategy [5]. However, the success of this method depends on various factors, such as soil properties and plant species characteristics. Ideal candidates for phytoextraction are fast-growing, easy to cultivate, possess a well-developed root system, and can efficiently translocate HMs from roots to shoots [6]. Such plants are known as hyperaccumulators and include species such as Alyssum serpyllifolium, Arabidopsis halleri, Phytolacca americana, and Thlaspi caerulescens. These plants can typically accumulate between 1,000 and 10,000 mg/kg of HMs in their dry biomass [7, 8].

In addition to plants, microorganisms play a significant role in bioremediation. Endophytic bacteria, which live within plant tissues without harming their hosts, can establish synergistic interactions with plants and enhance HMs phytoremediation [8, 9]. These microbes support plant growth through various mechanisms, one of the most critical being the production of metallophores - low-molecular-weight (500–1500 Da) organic ligands that bind and sequester HMs from the environment.

The best characterized metallophores are those called ‘siderophores’, which have strong affinity for Fe3+ (Kf > 1030), thus promoting Fe uptake by plants [10, 11]. Iron is a crucial element for the proper functioning of all organisms, including plants. Low iron levels impede photosynthesis and the synthesis of plant hormones, leading to reduced plant growth rates. Fe2+ ions readily oxidize to Fe3+, which is less soluble in soil and cannot be easily uptaken by plants; thus, siderophores produced by endophytes increase iron bioavailability directly impacting plant health [11]. The metal binding abilities of metallophores are not limited only to iron. In fact, they can exhibit even higher affinities to other metal ions, including Zn2+, Cu2+, Ni2+, Co2+, Mn2+, Cr3+, As3+, As5+, Pb2+, Cd2+ and Hg2+ [12–14]. Therefore, the broader term ‘metallophore’ is often preferred to reflect this versatility. Additionally, more specific names are in use, such as ‘zincophores’ (zinc-binding) or ‘chalkophores’ (copper-binding) [15, 16]. The microbial production of metallophores can influence both the mobility and solubility of toxic metals in the environment, highlighting their potential role in bioremediation of contaminated ecosystems.

Galium aparine L., commonly known as cleavers, is a worldwide-occurring annual herbaceous plant. Herbaceous plants hold significant economic value owing to their ability to produce a wide range of bioactive compounds with pharmaceutical applications [17]. Galium aparine L. has been traditionally used for its beneficial properties on the kidneys, skin disorders, wounds, high blood pressure, and insomnia [18, 19]. In 2024, we isolated ten bacterial strains from roots, stems, and leaves of G. aparine L. plants growing in central Poland [20]. The strains are classified within the genera Bacillus (B. pretiosus GR1, B. cereus GR3, B. thuringiensis GS1, B. cereus GS3, B. sp. GL1, B. cereus GL2, B. wiedmannii GL3), Priestia (sp. GS2, megaterium GR4), and Peribacillus (P. frigoritolerans GR2). Their genomes were analyzed in detail for the presence of genes involved in plant growth, enzyme metabolism, and the secondary metabolites production associated with metallophore synthesis, prompting further studies in this area.

Given the growing concern over soil contamination and the promising role of metallophores in heavy metal remediation, the present study was designed to: (1) evaluate the metallophore production of ten G. aparine-associated bacterial endophytes based on the genomic evidence; (2) characterize their ability to chelate various heavy metals; (3) identify the most efficient producers; and (4) conduct an in-depth analysis of biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) and enzymes involved in the metallophore biosynthesis.

Results and discussion

Bacterial endophytes from Galium aparine L. possess only a few types of metallophore gene clusters (Fig. 1) [20]. Based on that, strains can be easily grouped. All Bacillus cereus group strains possess both bacillibactin and petrobactin BGCs (marked as ‘group B + P’). Additionally, two strains B. cereus GS3 and B. cereus GL2, contain schizokinen-like BGC (‘group B + P+S’). Three other strains also contain a schizokinen biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC). Peribacillus frigoritolerans GR2 and Priestia megaterium GR4 possess that cluster independently (‘group S’), whereas Priestia sp. GS2 has it alongside the opine metallophore BGC (‘group S + O’).

Metallophore synthesis and iron-binding activity

Initial screening was conducted using different types of media to trigger metallophore production. Metallophore production was assessed by measuring iron-binding activity (siderophore activity) using CAS reagent; thus, it is demonstrated as a percentage of siderophore units (SU). All of the isolates demonstrated production of metallophores on standard LB medium extracted with 3% 8-hydroxyquinoline (Fig. 2, Supplementary File 1). The extraction was conducted to remove iron from the media, which should induce the metallophore production [21]. In the extracted LB medium, they all demonstrated similar values of siderophore units (SU), ranging mostly from 20 to 30% after 48 h. Cultivation on extracted LB does not significantly differentiate metallophore production in tested strains.

The absence of metallophore production in the extracted LB medium is not unexpected, as metallophore biosynthesis is typically induced under conditions of metal limitation. For the metallophore-specific production, we tested a modified minimal “A” medium, increasing the concentration of glucose and adding succinic acid, which is commonly recognized as a metallophore precursor [22, 23]. We used both media without extraction with 3-hydroxyquinoline, and after such extraction. The results of extracted medium (Fig. 2) showed greater variability, as reflected in fluctuations in the percentage of SU. This variability may be associated with trace residual amounts of 8-hydroxyquinoline or chloroform remaining after the extraction procedure, which could have influenced metallophore production. Nevertheless, all strains demonstrated production after 96 h, and some starting after 48 h. The extraction of MSM is not needed, as the results without it differentiate metallophore production in those bacteria even better (Fig. 2). Thus, we suggest that our adjusted medium can be a good fit for the initial testing of metallophore production abilities. After 72 h, strains from group B + P oscillate on app. 25–40% of SU. The presence of additional clusters seemingly enhances metallophore production, at least for 3 (GL2, GR2, GS2) out of 5 strains from other groups. Priestia sp. GS2 exhibited the highest SU content of all other strains after 72 h, reaching up to 55%. Furthermore, Bacillus cereus GL2 and Peribacillus frigoritolerans GR2 demonstrated stable production up to 58% after 72 h. Metallophore accumulation in the culture medium increased over time in nearly all cultivations of the tested strains as anticipated. As the bacterial population grows, the overall iron demand also increases, stimulating greater metallophore production. However, the production of metallophores eventually stops when the necessary primary metabolites are depleted. Endophytic and rhizospheric bacteria are frequently identified as metallo- and siderophore producers, often simultaneously exhibiting other additional key plant growth-promoting traits (PGPT) [24–28]. For instance, out of 50 endophytic bacteria isolated from roots and stems of Helianthus annuus (sunflower), 20 strains were selected for further in-depth screening of their PGP characteristics [27]. Among Pseudomonas and Stenotrophomonas bacteria, Bacillus species were the most dominant. The highest production (87.73 ± 0.05% SU) was observed in Bacillus cereus T4S, with several other Bacillus strains producing approximately 70–80% SU [27].

In general, inoculation with siderophore-producing bacteria or the application of extracted siderophore enhances plant yield and nutrient uptake, improves iron content, and thus, alleviates iron deficiency-acquired chlorosis [29, 30]. These benefits are particularly significant in the light of abiotic stress such as salinity, drought and heavy metal contamination [31, 32]. For instance, the multi-tolerant endophyte Priestia aryabhattai BPR-9 demonstrated antagonistic activity against several major fungal pathogens (e.g. Alternaria solani, Fusarium oxysporum) and promoted growth of wheat seedlings in high salinity conditions [31]. Similarly, Priestia aryabhattai MS3 from the root area of salt-prone rice fields exhibited the highest siderophore production, estimated at 60% and 43% SU under non-saline and saline (200 mM NaCl) conditions, respectively [32]. Bacillus cereus strains C9 and C27, producing nearly 58 and 46% SU, respectively, were found to remediate cadmium-contaminated saline soils, as well as augment their fertility due to ammonia production [33]. Additionally, Bacillus sp. PZ-1, a heavy metal-resistant bacterium isolated from a lead-zinc mine, showed strong siderophore production and lead adsorption in aqueous solutions, yielding almost 91% SU after optimization [34].

Chelation of other heavy metals

As stated before, most of the research concentrates solely on siderophore activity, without any examination of the chelation capabilities of other essential metals. Thus, after testing isolates for iron scavenging in liquid medium, the ability of the produced metallophores to bind other heavy metal ions was further evaluated on solid agar plates using the O-CAS method (Figs. 3 and 4) [35]. The chelation of Fe3+ was used as a comparison.

The presence of metallophores capable of binding to Fe3+ (possessing siderophore binding activity) was also confirmed using the O-CAS method. The results are mostly consistent with those obtained from testing supernatants of liquid culture media after 96 h. However, the strains Bacillus sp. GL1 and Bacillus cereus GS3 did not produce metallophores with siderophore activity in agar medium, while they demonstrated moderate activity in liquid media. The results from liquid media are more accurate, as halos are not always visible on agar media or activity does not happen due to diffusion or the effect of the solid substrate. Strains with the best siderophore activity in liquid media, Peribacillus frigoritolerans GR2 and Priestia sp. GS2, also exhibited the highest activity in agar medium. Seventeen other metal ions were tested similarly by replacing Fe3+ with the respective ions in the CAS reagent. None of the strains were able to chelate all tested metals; however, Priestia strains GR4 and GS2, and Peribacillus frigoritolerans GR2 demonstrated chelation activity across almost the full range of evaluated metals. These strains were classified to the group S (GR2 and GR4) and S + O (GS2). Their chelating properties differ. Priestia strains showed moderate to strong activity towards common essential metals such as Zn2+ or Co2+, while exhibiting only slight activity towards those more toxic. In contrast, Peribacillus frigoritolerans GR2 was more active towards toxic heavy metals such as Pb2+, Hg2+, and Cd2+, thus indicating its bioremediation potential. The rest of the strains, belonging to the Bacillus cereus group (group B + P and group B + P+S), displayed weak activity towards the tested metals. Out of them, Bacillus wiedmannii GL3 displayed moderate activity towards most metals, even Pb2+, Hg2+, and Cd2+. Notably, in all tested variants of O-CAS halo around colonies were pinkish to purple, not yellow (Fig. 4). Usually, color depends on the type of metallophore. The color change from blue to orange suggests the presence of hydroxamate type (f.e. petrobactin), while to purple - catechol type (f.e. bacillibactin) [35].

Several studies have investigated the ability of microorganisms to bind metals and metalloids beyond Fe3+. Ortuzar et al. (2020) examined the chelation abilities of Micromonospora strains, isolated from legume tissues and rhizosphere, using CAS agar plates supplemented with 10 metals/metalloids (As5+, Co2+, Cu+, Cr6+, Fe3+, Hg2+, Mo6+, Ni2+, V5+ and Zn2+) [36]. Among these, vanadium (V) and zinc (Zn) were the least commonly bound, while copper (Cu+, 40% of strains), chromium (Cr6+, 35%) and nickel (Ni2+, 30%) showed the highest binding frequencies. No clear correlation was observed between the metal-binding capacity and source of microorganisms. Under heavy metal stress, Arabidopsis thaliana plants inoculated with Micromonospora saelicesensis GAR05 exhibited significantly enhanced growth compared to uninoculated control plants, indicating plant growth promotion via increased metal tolerance. A new bacterial structure named a ‘rootbead’ was observed on the roots of inoculated A. thaliana suggesting a strong interaction between those two organisms [36]. Metallophore profiles were determined for other ubiquitous nitrogen-fixing bacteria through metal isotope-coded profiling (MICP) using Fe, Cu, Zn and Mo isotopes [37]. Frankia strains produced specific metallophores with a preference towards Fe, Cu, or less often, Cu. No Mo-binding ligand was identified, although, it is also an essential element for some nitrogenases. Notably, Frankia sp. CH37 was identified as ‘a metallophore hyper-producer’, whereas other strains released only minimal amounts or even no metallophores into the growth medium.

Maldonado-Hernandes et al. (2022) isolated a plethora of heavy metal-resistant bacteria from soils and sediments of the Lerma-Chapala basin (Mexico). These isolates produced metallophores able to bind all tested metal ions (As5+, As3+, Co2+, Cr6+, Cu2+, Ni2+, Pb2+, and Zn2+), with strains belonging to the Delftia and Pseudomonas genera showing the highest siderophore level (up to 95% SU). These findings suggested that microbial communities may evolve metallophore production ability as a survival mechanism [14]. Other ecological niches (marine ecosystems, freshwater and meromictic lakes, hypersaline springs, soil crusts) were also examined for the presence of isolates producing metallophores binding with Fe3+ and other metal ions (Mg2+, V3+, Mn2+, Co2+, Ni2+, Cu2+, Zn2+, Se4+ and Te2+) [16]. Iron and zinc were the most commonly chelated elements, reflecting their biological importance and often limited bioavailability. A small number of strains showed preference for other metal(loid)s. Interestingly, halotolerant and halophilic isolates typically produced fewer or less potent metallophores than non-halophilic bacteria. This phenomenon is likely due to specialized cation pumps in their cells, that mitigate intracellular metal toxicity without relying on extracellular chelation [16]. Several Bacillus and Priestia strains were isolated from tissues and rhizosphere of pioneer plants from mine tailings [38]. All the 17 strains of Priestia megaterium and 3 of Bacillus mojavensis demonstrated the ability to chelate Fe3+, but showed no capacity to bind Cd2+, Co2+, Cu2+, Ni2+, and Zn2+. Bacillus subtilis strains failed to chelate any of the evaluated metals [38]. The available data on metallophore production are limited; however, they show the influence of occupied environmental niches on bacteria and the rate of community adaptation. It also provides guidance on where to search for plant growth-beneficial bacteria with such chelating properties.

Homology of metallophore gene clusters

Biosynthesis of the majority of metallophores is conducted via nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS)-dependent or NRPS-independent (NI) siderophore synthetase [22]. The most prevalent clusters for metallophore production within the Bacillus genus are associated with bacillibactin and petrobactin production. Bacillibactin and petrobactin are both catecholate-type of siderophores; however, their synthesis route differs. Bacillibactin is synthesized via a NRPS system encoded by the dhb operon, which assembles a trilactone scaffold of 2,3-dihydroxybenzoate and glycine-threonine units [40]. Petrobactin synthesis involves the asb gene cluster, which directs assembly through NI siderophore synthetases [41, 42]. These two clusters are conserved across both Bacillus cereus and Bacillus subtilis groups, without many differences in gene organization (Supplementary File 2). Our search for homologous gene clusters to bacillibactin and petrobactin resulted in 4,842 and 6,438 hits, respectively, all within Bacillus genus (with some misidentifications, which were manually verified). Bacillus spp. members are probably the most common endophytes isolates from various plants, with many of their genomes sequenced and analyzed thoroughly [20, 43–46]. Interestingly, only three out of ten isolates were identified as non-Bacillus species, and those strains harbored other gene clusters involved in the metallophore biosynthesis compared to the well-characterized Bacillus-associated bacillibactin and petrobactin.

Opine metallophore gene cluster in Priestia sp. GS2

Priestia sp. GS2 strain is a promising candidate for metallophore production, as indicated by genome mining and in vitro assessments of iron binding and metal-chelating activities. It demonstrates the highest amounts of SU after 72 h, reaching up to 63%. Its genome contains both schizokinen-like and opine metallophore gene clusters.

A search for homologous gene clusters to the opine metallophore from Priestia sp. GS2 yielded 649 hits with the highest score for clusters of Priestia flexa and Priestia aryabhattai strains (Fig. 6A). Notably, Bacillus sp. CBEL-1, which has been proposed to belong to the same species as Priestia sp. GS2 [20], shares over 98% sequence identity for all nine genes of this BGC with 100% coverage each. Found clusters were divided into five groups based on score values (Fig. 6B). The group with the highest score (17.62–17.39) consisted of Priestia spp. species (11%), but above all of Paenibacillus spp. (43%) and Brevibacillus spp. (14%) (Fig. 6C). In that group, core genes (cntL, cntM) from Priestia flexa strains (phylogenetically the closest to GS2) share more than 90–98% identity of core genes and remaining genes also exceed 90% with a score of 17.62. Both Paenibacillus spp. and Brevibacillus spp. from this group possess a cluster with all nine genes with 58–62% identity of core genes with > 98% coverage, resulting in a score of 17.39. Priestia spp. species are limited mostly to that group, while Paenibacillus spp. and Brevibacillus spp. are also present in groups with a lower score (IV-V, Fig. 6C). Lower score can be the result of e.g. the absence of some additional biosynthetic genes in these bacteria, their lower similarity, which suggests high diversity of that cluster across these species.

To date, four examples of bacterial opine metallophores produced via the Cnt biosynthetic system have been described and deposited in the MiBIG database (Fig. 6D). These include human pathogens Staphylococcus aureus (staphylopine), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (pseudopaline), and Yersinia pestis (yersinopine), as well as the soil-associated bacterium Paenibacillus mucilaginosus (bacillopaline) [47, 48]. These compounds have been primarily described as zincophores, referring to their importance in Zn acquisition. A comprehensive genome neighborhood network analysis of opine metallophore BGCs conducted by Morey and Kehl-Fie (2020) identified 250 potential producers [48]. This indicates a significant diversity of metallophores and their crucial function for adaptation to various ecological niches beyond their involvement in pathogenicity. In the context of endophytic bacteria, production of broad-spectrum metallophores offers a competitive advantage. The rhizosphere is a highly contested niche, and the ability to acquire essential metals benefits both endophytic bacteria and their plant host. By facilitating metal uptake, these beneficial bacteria may help plants meet their nutritional needs while simultaneously restricting metal availability to potential pathogens, thereby contributing to plant defense. Notably, plants also synthesize their own metallophore, nicotianamine, via a pathway involving NAS synthase, an enzyme functionally and structurally analogous to that involved in the biosynthesis of bacterial opine metallophores [49]. This similarity raises intriguing possibilities for functional interactions or shared metal acquisition strategies between plants and their associated microbiota.

The biosynthetic pathway for opine metallophore synthesis is dependent on a set of two or three enzymes. The first enzyme in the pathway is histidine racemase (CntK) for S. aureus and P. mucilaginosus, which catalyzes conversion of L-histidine (L-His) to D-histidine (D-His). Presence or absence of CntK in this pathway is the first determinant of stereochemistry of metallophore. Then, the second enzyme, which is nicotianamine synthetase (NAS synthetase, CntL) attaches a γ-butyric acid moiety from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) onto D-His to form intermediate xNA (S. aureus, P. mucilaginosus) or onto L-His to form yNA (P. aeruginosa, Y. pestis), alternative intermediates [50]. The third enzyme, opine dehydrogenase (ODH, CntM) catalyzes reductive condensation of xNA or yNA with α-ketoacid. CntM originating from P. mucilaginousus and P. aeruginosa utilizes α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), while CntM from S. aureus and Y. pestis – pyruvate [51]. Organization of opine metallophore biosynthesis in Priestia sp. GS2 is more similar to that in S. aureus and P. mucilaginosus, suggesting that it may produce similar metallophore with plant growth promoting properties, not associated with virulence as in three other types. Recognizing the mechanisms behind their production has great value for agricultural studies, enabling to obtain an efficient production system. Microbial biostimulants are promising alternatives to hazardous chemical pesticides, dangerous for the environment [52]. In addition, it has been suggested that metallophores could serve in food industry as food-grade antioxidants for food preservation and prolonged-shelf life or as an additive to functional foods; however, not much attention was put there [53]. While the enzymes involved in biosynthetic pathway of staphylopine from S. aureus have been described, structural data for pseudopaline from P. aeruginosa and yersinopine from Y. pestis are limited only to the opine dehydrogenase (ODH, CntM) (Table 1) [54, 55].

The schizokinen-like clusters identified in Priestia megaterium GR4, Peribacillus frigoritolerans GR2, Priestia sp. GS2, and Bacillus cereus GL2 strains were further analyzed for the presence of homologous gene clusters in other bacterial genomes. For each strain, homologs were grouped according to their overall similarity scores. The relative contribution and diversity of these homologous cluster groups across different bacterial taxa are summarized in Fig. 7.

Among all analyzed clusters, Group I, comprising those most closely resembling schizokinen-like clusters found in our strains, was the least abundant in Priestia sp. For GS2 strain, the Group I consisted of Priestia megaterium, Priestia flexa and Priestia aryabhattai strains. For GL2 – mostly Aneurinibacillus migulans and other members of Bacillus spp. The scarcity of highly similar or identical clusters indicate that those clusters are uncommon among these taxa, suggesting that this BGCs is relatively rare among them. However, in GS2 dataset, Priestia spp. members were also abundant in two groups of clusters with lower similarity (Group II and III). It indicate greater diversity of that cluster within that genus. Overall, schizokinen clusters appear to be conserved within Priestia spp., as their distribution patterns in GS2 and GR4 show strong similarity. In both GS2 and GR4 datasets, clusters from Peribacillus spp. were placed in the least similar group (Group III). Additionally, several Bacillus spp. (e.g., B. smithii) were represented across their results; however, they were less abundant and did not dominate the overall composition. In GR2 dataset, Group I was relatively rich, consisting predominantly of Peribacillus spp. members (P. frigoritolerans, P. butanolivorans, P. simplex). Subsequent groups (II and III) revealed close relationships to Bacillus spp. strains, including B. licheniformis, B. paralicheniformis, and B. haynesii. Collectively, these findings suggest that schizokinen-like biosynthetic clusters are conserved within genera closely related to Bacillus (e.g., Peribacillus, Priestia, Domibacillus, Metabacillus, Mesobacillus) with the same gene organization (Fig. 8). However, their detection in more distantly related taxa, such as Thermoactinomyces spp. and Halalkalibacterium spp., points out the extensive diversity of this secondary metabolite pathway across bacteria.

As shown in Fig. 8, representative homologous clusters can be classified based on the sequence identity of the two core genes encoding IucA/IucC synthases (indicated by red and green arrows). Within Priestia spp., two subgroups were distinguished: P. megaterium and P. aryabhattai shared ≥ 93% sequence identity, the same for Priestia sp. GS2 and P. flexa NBRC 15,715, but, in contrast, sequence identity between Priestia sp. GS2 and P. megaterium GR4 was substantially lower (67% and 74%). Even lower values (47% and 57%) were observed between P. flexa NBRC 15,715 and Peribacillus frigoritolerans GR2. Therefore, Peribacillus spp. strains formed a distinct group. In Bacillus spp., sequence diversity was higher overall; nevertheless, certain strains carried clusters with relatively close similarity. Conversely, the three deposited reference clusters exhibited low similarity to the groups analyzed, which is expected given the greater phylogenetic distance of their source organisms (Leptolyngbya, Nostoc, Synechococcus) from Bacillus, Peribacillus, and Priestia spp. This observation further highlights the absence of schizokinen-like clusters in the current MIBiG database [56]. Schizokinen from Priestia megaterium has been reported to exhibit a higher affinity to Fe3+ than desferrioxamine B from Streptomyces pilosus, a therapeutic agent used for iron poisoning and hemochromatosis [57]. Also, Gallium-68-labeled schizokinen ([68Ga]Ga-SKN) has been reported as a promising new radiotracer for imaging bacterial infections [58]. Even though schizokinen production in Priestia megaterium is relatively well-described in the literature, there is no gene cluster deposition in the MIBiG database.

Both genes ‘a’ (colored in red in Fig. 8) and ‘b’ (green) encode IucA/IucC family synthases. IucA and IucC are NI synthases catalyzing ATP-dependent condensation reactions in hydroxamate-type metallophore production, such as aerobactin, petrobactin and related, such as schizokinen. The functional distinction between IucA- and IucC-type enzymes was first elucidated in the aerobactin pathway of E. coli, where IucA catalyzes the initial condensation of a hydroxamate donor with citrate, and IucC performs the second condensation step to complete siderophore assembly [59, 60]. Homologs in schizokinen-like clusters of Bacillus, Priestia, and Peribacillus are predicted to fulfill similar roles.

Amino acid sequence of synthase ‘a’ has higher coverage with Klebsiella pneumoniae IucA synthase, while synthase ‘b’ with its IucC synthase. Those sequences of the analyzed proteins share mostly only 20–25% identity with the most similar protein structures deposited in the PDB database. In addition, several homologs with 30–37% sequence identity to deposited PDB proteins were identified. Sequence identity in this range provides stronger indication of evolutionary relatedness and suggests at least partial conservation of protein structure or function, although experimental validation or structural modeling would still be required to confirm such inferences.

Pulcherriminic acid-like gene cluster in Bacillus wiedmannii GL3

Pulcherriminic acid chelates iron to form red pigment known as pulcherrimin [61]. Despite this iron-binding activity, it cannot be considered as a siderophore, as it neither transports iron into the cell nor renders its bioavailability. Instead, it sequesters iron, thereby preventing its utilization by competing microorganisms, which stands behind its biocontrol activity. Pulcherriminic acid is produced mostly by yeast, particularly from Metschnikowia, Candida and Kluyveromyces genera, but also by certain Bacillus species [62, 63]. To date, only three biosynthetic gene clusters related to pulcherriminic acid have been deposited in MIBiG database, from Metschnikowia aff. pulcherrima (BGC0002321), Kluyveromyces lactis (BGC0001989) and Bacillus subtilis subsp. subtilis str. 168 (BGC0002103). Leucine (Leu) serves as the primary precursor of pulcherriminic acid. Leucine is activated by leucyl-tRNA synthetase, condensed by YvmC enzyme (encoded by yvmC or pchC gene) into cyclo(L-Leu-L-Leu), oxidized by CypX enzyme (cypX gene) to pulcherriminic acid, and exported by YvmA, where it chelates Fe³⁺ to form pulcherrimin [65].

A homologous identified in Bacillus wiedmannii GL3 genome is conserved across numerous Bacillus species (Fig. 9).

In total, 1,774 clusters possessing homologs of cypX and pchC (> 50% identity) were identified and grouped based on their scores. The GL3 cluster showed greater similarity to clusters from Bacillus cereus group members (group I, score 9.27–9.25) than to those from the Bacillus subtilis group or Priestia spp. members (group II, score 8.17–6.15). GL3’s BGC differs in gene organization in comparison with the reference cluster from Bacillus subtilis subsp. subtilis str. 168 (BGC0002103). Sequence comparison of the key biosynthetic genes revealed that in Bacillus subtilis DSM 13,019, cypX and pchC share 62 and 73% identity, respectively, with the GL3 cluster (> 99% coverage, score 6.16). However, there is no reference cluster from species within Bacillus cereus group. As the results of chelation for various heavy metals were slightly better for the GL3 strain with pulcherriminic acid cluster (Fig. 3), it is possible that it can also mediate binding of other metals than iron.

Conclusions and future perspectives

This research demonstrates that endophytic bacteria exhibit strong potential for metallophore production, which can be significantly triggered by the right medium composition to high levels. Genome mining has proven to be an efficient strategy for assessing biosynthetic potential, enabling the identification of strains likely to produce functional metallophores through the analysis of homologous gene clusters. Comparing the presence, sequence similarity, and organization of these clusters across strains and genera provides insights into their conservation and diversity, and highlights candidates capable of producing unique or previously undescribed compounds. When combined with functional assays measuring iron-binding activity and chelation of other heavy metals, this approach identified two particularly promising strains, Peribacillus frigoritolerans GR2 and Priestia sp. GS2.

Peribacillus frigoritolerans GR2 exhibited strong chelation of highly toxic metals such as Pb²⁺, Cd²⁺, and Hg²⁺, presumably via a schizokinen-like metallophore, suggesting potential for bioremediation applications. Optimization of production, followed by purification and structural characterization, is required to exploit its potential fully. The second strain, Priestia sp. GS2, showed the highest metallophore production and a broad chelation spectrum, including Mn²⁺, Co²⁺, Ni²⁺, Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺, Al³⁺, and Mg³⁺. The analysis of its opine-type metallophore biosynthetic gene cluster revealed unique features, with homologs present in only a few other species. Most current knowledge of the enzymes involved in the pathway of opine metallophore biosynthesis comes from studies on human pathogens, leaving a significant gap in understanding pathways in plant-associated bacteria, thus, the ecological roles of endophytes in plant symbiosis.

While computational predictions provide valuable insights, experimental determination of protein structures remains essential for enzymes. High-resolution models precisely define active sites, substrate-binding pockets, and domain dynamics, which are critical for understanding catalytic mechanisms and evolutionary relationships. Structural knowledge also enables engineering of enzymes to produce derivatives with enhanced binding affinity, broadened metal specificity, or improved stability Integrating genome mining, functional assays, and structural biology provides a powerful framework for discovering, characterizing, and harnessing metallophore-producing bacteria. These findings expand the current knowledge on metallophore-producing endophytes and highlight their potential for future applications following further experimental validation.

Materials and methods

Biological material

The bacterial endophytes were isolated from surface-sterilized healthy tissues of medicinal plant Galium aparine L. and preserved as pure cultures in 25% glycerol (v/v) at -80 °C during the previous stage of our research [20].

Siderophore activity

All of used glassware was soaked in 6 M HCl overnight, washed with deionized water and autoclaved before use [21]. For the first screening of iron binding abilities, isolates were inoculated onto LB medium extracted with 3% 8-hydroxyquinoline and incubated at 30 °C for 96 h in 180 rpm with testing every 24 h. Extraction with 3% 8-hydroxyquinoline was used to remove iron from the media to trigger metallophore production [21]. Original metallophore-specific medium was created (g/L: glucose, 5; succinic acid, 1; yeast extract, 2; disodium phosphate, 1.5; ammonium chloride, 2.5; pH 7.0 ± 0.2) and tested in conditions as above with and without 8-hydroxyquinoline extraction. CAS reagent was prepared (Chrome Azurol S, 60.5 mg; HDTMA, 73 mg; 1 mM of FeCl3 in 10 mM HCl, 10 ml; dH2O, 100 mL) [64], and siderophore activity estimation assay was taken each 24 h. Following incubation, the fermented broth was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min and cell-free supernatant was mixed with CAS reagent (ratio 1:1). It was incubated for 45 min in the dark, and its absorbance at 630 nm was measured. All experiments were conducted in triplicate. Metallophore content was calculated by using following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\%\;\left(\mathrm{SU}\right)\;\text{siderophore units}\;=\frac{A_{r}-A_{s}}{A_{r}}\cdot{100\%}$$\end{document}

where: Ar = absorbance of reference (uninoculated broth with CAS reagent); As = absorbance of sample.

Statistical analysis was conducted using R v4.5.1 and RStudio [65, 66], and comparisons were done using ANOVA with further Tukey HSD method.

Heavy metal chelation

The ability to chelate different metal ions by produced metallophore was tested using the O-CAS agar plate method modified by replacing Fe3+ with other heavy metal ions. The concentration of all metal ions was kept to 1 mM metal salt stock in 10 mM HCl. Salts included FeCl3·6H2O, MnCl2·4H2O, CoCl2·6H2O, NiCl2·6H2O, CuCl2, ZnCl2, AlCl3, MgCl2, CrCl3·6H2O, PbCl2, HgCl2, CdCl2·2.5H2O, BaCl2, BiCl3, LiCl, CsCl, SrCl2·6H2O and SbCl3. The plates were prepared the same as standard O-CAS [35]. Bacteria were grown on our metallophore-specific medium (MSM) with addition of 2% agar for 96 h in 30 °C, then flooded with CAS medium overlay (per 1 L: PIPES, 30.24 g; agar, 10 g; CAS reagent, 100 ml). After 1 h, plates were assessed for a color change around the bacterial growth area, which indicates the removal of metal from the CAS reagent as a result of metallophore activity. All experiments were performed in triplicates.

Genomic analysis of metallophore gene clusters

Genomic data of the studied strains was obtained in our previous research [20]. The genome sequences are deposited in National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, USA) database under the BioProject number PRJNA1068863. Visualization of metallophore and siderophore gene clusters in chosen strains was conducted using antiSMASH v8.0.1 [67]. Metallophore BGC were analyzed in details using CAGECAT online tool [68]. Cblaster was employed to detect homologous gene clusters in genomes deposited in NCBI database using query genes with crucial required sentences for each cluster (Table 3) (max e-value 0.01, min. sequence identity 50%, min. query coverage 50%) [69]. Clinker generated comparison figures query clusters and chosen examples [69]. Comparison of the enzymes involved in metallophore biosynthesis with the enzymes from other bacteria with known structures deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) were obtained by BlastP search [70, 71].
