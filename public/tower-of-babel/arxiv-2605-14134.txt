Stochastic Mackey–Glass Equations and Other Negative Feedback Systems: Existence of Invariant Measures
M. van den Bosch, O. W. van Gaans, S. M. Verduyn Lunel

Abstract
We study equations like the Mackey–Glass equations and Nicholson’s blowflies equation, each perturbed by a (small) multiplicative noise term. Solutions to these stochastic negative feedback systems persist globally and are bounded above in probability under mild assumptions. A non-trivial invariant measure is proved to exist if and only if there is at least one initial condition for which the solution remains bounded away from zero in probability. The noise driving the dynamical system is allowed to be a square integrable Lévy process with finite intensity. Existence of invariant measures is obtained via the Krylov–Bogoliubov method. In addition to our theoretical results, we present numerical simulations identifying the invariant measures obtained via the Krylov–Bogoliubov method and illustrating their connection to the system’s long-term behaviour.

Types of Lévy noise

Throughout this paper, we consider SDDEs driven by what call “regulated Lévy processes”. To this end, we first introduce the following class of Lévy processes and introduce some notation that is also used in §
4
–§
5
.

(HJudi)

The process
L=(L(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a square integrable Lévy process that is of finite intensity, i.e.,
\nu(\mathbb{R})<\infty
, where
\nu
is the associated Lévy measure.

The finite intensity property above implies that
L
has a finite number of jumps on any compact time interval
[
76
, Thm. 21.3]
. Either
[
2
, Thm. 2.3.9]
or
[
51
, Lem. 2.8]
subsequently tells us that any
L
of type (HJudi) is a
jump  diffusion  process
. That is, a sum of two independent processes: a Brownian motion
W=(W(t))_{t\geq 0}
which is scaled with the dispersion coefficient
\sigma^{2}
and includes a drift; and a compound Poisson process
Z=(Z(t))_{t\geq 0}
with jump measure
\frac{1}{\nu(\mathbb{R})}\nu.

Indeed, we have

L(t)=\left(\gamma-\int_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}x\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\right)t+\sigma W(t)+Z(t),\quad Z(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N(t)}Z_{k},\quad t\geq 0.

(3.7)

We say
N=(N(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a Poisson process associated to the Lévy process
L
. Rewriting yields

L(t)=\left(\gamma+\int_{|x|>1}x\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\right)t+\sigma W(t)+\big[Z(t)-\lambda_{N}\mathbb{E}Z_{1}t\big],\quad t\geq 0.

(3.8)

In particular, we have
\lambda_{N}\mathbb{E}Z_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\nu(\mathrm{d}x).
A Lévy process of finite intensity is a martingale if and only if
\gamma=-\int_{\{|x|>1\}}x\nu(\mathrm{d}x).
Suppose
Z_{1}
is centred, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}Z_{1}=0,
then we have
\gamma=\int_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}x\nu(\mathrm{d}x)
and
Z
is a compound Poisson process.

Note that
\mathbb{E}Z_{1}^{2}<\infty
holds if and only if
L
is of class (HJudi), and then the quadratic variation and the predictable quadratic variation (whether
L
is a martingale or not) equal

[L](t)=\sigma^{2}t+\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta Z(t))^{2}=\sigma^{2}t+\sum_{k=1}^{N(t)}Z_{k}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\langle L\rangle(t)=\lambda t,\quad t\geq 0,

(3.9)

respectively, where
\lambda=\sigma^{2}+\lambda_{N}\mathbb{E}Z_{1}^{2}
and
\lambda_{N}=\mathbb{E}[N(1)]
is the intensity of the Poisson process
N
.

(HReg)

The process
L=(L(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a Lévy process of class (HJudi) and satisfies the following two additional properties:

(P1)

the process experiences no continuous drift, i.e.,
\gamma=\int_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}x\nu(\mathrm{d}x);

(P2)

jumps are
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. uniformly bounded by some
\zeta\geq 0,
i.e., we have

\mathbb{P}
-a.s., where

The process

Property (P2) implies that

Finally, note that (HRegM) implies

\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{P}(X(t)>R)\leq\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{t\geq 0}R^{-1}{\Xi(t)}=0,

(3.15)

since
\sup_{t\geq 0}\Xi(t)\leq\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Xi(t)+\limsup_{t\to 0}\Xi(t)\leq K+\tilde{r}M/\tilde{\gamma},

for some
T>0
sufficiently large and constant
K\geq 0.
The latter exploits continuity of
t\mapsto\Xi(t).

∎

Note that the Brownian motion
W
in Proposition
3.5
can be replaced by any (local) martingale
M
, as the proof remains valid (after additional stopping time arguments). Moreover, setting
g(\varphi)=\varphi(0)c(\varphi),

\varphi\in C[-\tau,0]
, gives rise to model (
1.13
).

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. almost) everywhere, and that this process has continuous sample paths whenever the integrator does not admit jump events.

The ultimate purpose

4.1
The Brownian motion case

Let
W=(W(t))_{t\geq 0}
denote a standard Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
and consider an Itô-process with negative drift:

Y(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}{a(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}W(s),\quad t\geq 0,

(4.4)

where
(a(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(b(t))_{t\geq 0}
are measurable and adapted processes such that

\int_{0}^{t}{|a(s)|}\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{0}^{t}{|b(s)|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},

(4.5)

for all
t\geq 0
. In this setting, we observe that the stochastic integral in (
4.4
) is a well-defined local martingale. Assuming

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}{|b(s)|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty,\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0,

(4.6)

ensures us that the second term is a (true) square integrable martingale. We refer to §
4.3
for more information regarding the conditions on the integrands.

Finding suitable estimates for the reverse time supremum
is the subject of the next two lemmas. For the first lemma we note it is necessary to have a negative drift—no matter how small—to ensure an upper bound as in (
4.8
). The right hand side tends to infinity as
\alpha\searrow 0.
An important feature of this estimate is that
it does not depend on the length of the interval
[0,l]
on which the supremum is
taken. In particular, we could thus take
l\to\infty
. This result below is obtained by a time-change argument using the Dambis–Dubins–Schwarz theorem for local martingales
[
43
, Thm. 4.6]
.

Lemma 4.2

(Lem. 3.4 of
[
16
]
)
.

Let
Y=(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a stochastic process given by (
4.4
).
Assume that there exist positive constants
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
such that

a(s)\geq\alpha\quad\text{ and }\quad b(s)^{2}\leq\beta^{2}\quad\mathbb{P}\textnormal{-a.s.}\,\,for\,\,all\,\,s\geq 0.

(4.7)

Then for every
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
R\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}(Y(l)-Y(\theta))\geq R\right)\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{64\beta^{2}}\right)+\frac{4\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha R}{64\beta^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{128\beta^{2}}\right)}.

(4.8)

The second lemma is a Gaussian tail estimate of the supremum of a stochastic integral over an interval with a fixed length
T>0
. This result is obtained in
[
16
, Lem. 3.5]
again by a time-change argument. Note that the value 16 in (
4.10
) is not necessarily optimal. This bound is strongly related to sub-Gaussian variables, asymptotic
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{p})
-dependence as
p\to\infty
of the upper universal constant
\sqrt[\scalebox{.7}{$p$}]{C_{p}}
in the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy (BDG) inequality, and Dudley’s theorem
[
1
,
19
,
50
,
74
,
68
,
78
,
80
]
. For illustrative purposes, we provide an alternative proof of Lemma
4.3
that does not exploit the time-change theorem nor the BDG-inequality (see the discussion succeeding this lemma).

Lemma 4.3

(Lem. 3.5 of
[
16
]
)
.

Let
W=(W(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a standard Brownian motion. Assume there exists a
\beta>0
such that

b(s)^{2}\leq\beta^{2}\quad\mathbb{P}\textnormal{-a.s.}\text{ for all $s\geq 0$}.

(4.9)

Let
t_{0}\geq 0
and
T>0
be fixed. Then for every
R\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+T}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq R\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{16\beta^{2}T}\right).

(4.10)

Proof.

Let
I=[t_{0},t_{0}+T]
be the interval on which we consider the martingale
M(t)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}W(s),

t\in I,
and define
X_{T}=\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+T}M(t)
. We deduce from the Borell-TIS theorem
[
1
, Thm. 2.1.1]
that
X_{T}-\mathbb{E}X_{T}
is sub-Gaussian
[
80
, Def. 2.5.6]
, hence the moment generating function for
X_{T}
as function of
\lambda
is bounded by

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\lambda X_{T}}\big]\leq e^{\lambda\mathbb{E}X_{T}+\delta\lambda^{2}\sup_{t\in I}\mathbb{E}[M(t)^{2}]},

(4.11)

for some
\delta>0

[
74
, Lem. 1.5]
.

There exists a
\rho>0
such that
x\leq\ln 2+x^{2}/\rho
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In addition, we have
(\mathbb{E}X_{T})^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}X_{T}^{2}\leq 4\mathbb{E}[M({t_{0}+T})^{2}]
due to Doob’s maximal inequality
[
43
, Thm. 1.3.8]
. This gives us

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X_{T}}]\leq 2e^{c\lambda^{2}\sup_{t\in I}\mathbb{E}[M(t)^{2}]},

(4.12)

where
c=4\rho^{-1}+\delta.
We infer that
X_{T}
is also sub-Gaussian; in particular, by Markov’s inequality we find the so-called Chernoff bound which satisfies

\mathbb{P}(X_{T}\geq R)\leq\inf_{\lambda>0}\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\lambda X_{T}}\big]}{e^{\lambda R}}\leq\inf_{\lambda>0}2e^{-\lambda R+c\lambda^{2}\sup_{t\in I}\mathbb{E}[M(t)^{2}]}=2e^{-\tfrac{R^{2}}{4c\sup_{t\in I}\mathbb{E}[M(t)^{2}]}},

(4.13)

as the infimum is attained at
\lambda={R}/({2c\sup_{t\in I}\mathbb{E}[M(t)^{2}]})
.

Finally, we have
\mathbb{E}[M(t)]^{2}=\mathbb{E}\langle M\rangle(t)=\mathbb{E}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}b(s)^{2}\mathrm{d}s\leq\beta^{2}T
, for
t\in I
. Setting
\rho=1
and
\delta=4
as in
[
74
, Lem. 1.5]
results into (
4.10
) with 16 replaced by 32. Careful inspection shows that we may also consider
\rho=2.77
and
\delta=2.552
, implying
c=4\rho^{-1}+\delta\leq 4
.
∎

Note that the estimate above—with constants different from 2 and 16—can also be achieved by exploiting the BDG-inequality. Recall the notation
X_{T}
and
M
above. We have the moment bounds

\mathbb{E}X_{T}^{p}\leq C_{p}\mathbb{E}[\langle M\rangle^{p/2}(t_{0}+T)]\leq K^{p}p^{p/2}(\beta^{2}T)^{p/2},

(4.14)

for some
K>0
and all
p\geq 2;
we can take
C_{p}=10^{p}p^{p/2}
,
K=10,
according to
[
68
, Thm. 5.2]
; see also
[
26
]
. Similar estimates to (
4.10
) readily follow from, e.g.,

[
36
, Lem. 2.2]
or
[
68
, Lem. 4.3]
. Recall that the standard proof of the BDG-inequality that involves Itô’s formula only results into asymptotic
\mathcal{O}(p)
-dependence. Indeed, from
[
73
, Prop. IV.4.3]
, we can merely deduce
C_{p}=2^{p}p^{p}
, but this does not prevent us from deducing Gaussian tail estimates
[
17
, Lem. B.1]
.

4.2
Extensions to regulated Lévy processes

In this section we derive estimates similar to those in §
4.1
for Lévy-driven processes with negative drift. In particular, we consider a process

Y(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}{a(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}L(s),\quad t\geq 0,

(4.15)

where
L=(L(t))_{t\geq 0}
is of class (HReg) and with
(a(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(b(t))_{t\geq 0}
being processes in
\mathbb{L}[0,\infty),
i.e., the space of adapted processes with càglàd sample paths (
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. almost) everywhere. Again, we refer to §
4.3
for a discussion on the conditions imposed upon the integrands.

The following lemma shows that an estimate comparable to (
4.8
) holds true as well in certain cases with jumps.
It seems that we cannot take
\alpha
arbitrarily small now, as opposed to the Brownian noise case.
This reflects the fact that all jumps need to be compensated by the negative drift. By increasing the jump height
\zeta
one might speculate the overall control to eventually break, yet the proof below makes no use of martingale properties whatsoever. We will leave it as an open problem whether
\alpha
needs to be bounded away from zero.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
Y=(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}
be given by (
4.15
) with
L=(L(t))_{t\geq 0}
of class
(HReg)
with jumps uniformly bounded by some
\zeta\geq 0.
Assume there exist constants
\alpha,\beta>0
such that

a(s)\geq\alpha>2\lambda_{N}\zeta\beta\quad\text{and}\quad b(s)^{2}\leq\beta^{2}\quad\mathbb{P}\textnormal{-a.s.}\text{ for all $s\geq 0$,}

(4.16)

where
\lambda_{N}\geq 0
is the rate of the Poisson process
N
associated to
L
.
Then there exists a
\kappa_{1}>0
such that for every integer
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
R\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}(Y(l)-Y(\theta))\geq R\right)

(4.17)

\displaystyle\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{256\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right)+\frac{4\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha R}{256\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{512\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right)}+\exp\big(-\kappa_{1}R\big).

Proof.

Assume
\zeta>0
without loss of generality. Observe we have the inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}(Y(l)-Y(\theta))\geq R\right)

(4.18)

\displaystyle\leq\,\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\theta}^{l}{a(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\theta}^{l}{b(s)\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq\frac{R}{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\theta}^{l}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{(\theta,l]}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}Z(s)\geq\frac{R}{2}\right).

Thanks to Lemma
4.2
, where basically one replaces
\alpha,\beta,
and
R
by
\alpha/2,\beta\sigma,
and
R/2
, respectively, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\theta}^{l}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\theta}^{l}b(s)\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq\frac{R}{2}\right)

(4.19)

\displaystyle\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{256\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right)+\frac{4\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha R}{256\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{512\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right)}.

For the remaining part, note that

\int_{0}^{l}|b(s)|\,\mathrm{d}|Z|(s)\leq\beta\zeta N(l)
holds, hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\theta}^{l}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{(\theta,l]}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}Z(s)\geq\frac{R}{2}\right)\hskip-142.26378pt

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}\int_{(\theta,l]}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}Z(s)\geq\frac{R}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}l\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}\int_{(\theta,l]}|b(s)|\,\mathrm{d}|Z|(s)\geq\frac{R}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}l\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{0}^{l}|b(s)|\,\mathrm{d}|Z|(s)\geq\frac{R}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}l\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(N(l)\geq\frac{1}{2\zeta\beta}(R+\alpha l)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{c}{2\zeta\beta}(R+\alpha l)\right)\mathbb{E}\exp(cN(l)),

(4.20)

where Markov’s inequality has been applied in the ultimate line with convex function
x\mapsto\exp(cx),
for some unspecified constant
c>0
. Since the variable
N(l)
is Poisson distributed with parameter
\lambda_{N}l
, the moment generating function for
N(l)
as function of
c
is given by

\mathbb{E}\exp(cN(l))=\exp(\lambda_{N}l(\exp(c)-1)).

(4.21)

Subsequently, let us introduce the function

f(\kappa_{1})=\frac{\lambda_{N}}{\kappa_{1}}(\exp(2\kappa_{1}\zeta\beta)-1),\quad\kappa_{1}>0.

(4.22)

An elementary computation shows that
f
is increasing and that we have

\lim_{\kappa_{1}\searrow 0}\frac{\lambda_{N}}{\kappa_{1}}(\exp(2\kappa_{1}\zeta\beta)-1)=2\lambda_{N}\zeta\beta.

(4.23)

So, there exists a
\kappa_{1}>0
with
\alpha\geq f(\kappa_{1})>2\lambda_{N}\zeta\beta.
Now take such a
\kappa_{1}
, set
c=2\kappa_{1}\zeta\beta
, and let us introduce the constant
\mu=\lambda_{N}(\exp(c)-1)
. This yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\theta}^{l}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{(\theta,l]}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}Z(s)\geq\frac{R}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\big(-\kappa_{1}(R+\alpha l)\big)\exp(\mu l)

(4.24)

\displaystyle=\exp(-\kappa_{1}R)\exp\big(l(\mu-\kappa_{1}\alpha)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\kappa_{1}R),

with the last inequality being immediate due to the fact that
\mu=\kappa_{1}f(\kappa_{1})\leq\kappa_{1}\alpha
holds.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

The restriction
\alpha>2\lambda_{N}\zeta\beta
in equation (
4.16
) is however stronger than necessary. Let us reconsider the split up

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}(Y(l)-Y(\theta))\geq R\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\,\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}-(1-q)\int_{\theta}^{l}{a(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\theta}^{l}{b(s)\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq(1-p)R\right)

(4.25)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\quad\quad+\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq l}-q\int_{\theta}^{l}a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{(\theta,l]}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}Z(s)\geq pR\right),

for any reals

, where

is set in the proof of Lemma

. Following the exact same steps, the function in (

for any
p
and

Letting

yields that

tends to

. It seems the

is a sufficient condition after finding appropriate values for

as

.

4.3
extends to regulated Lévy processes without additional constraints. We would like to point out that an exponential tail estimate like (

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
Y=(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}
be given by (

L=(L(t))_{t\geq 0}

(HReg)
with jumps uniformly bounded by some

Assume there exists a constant

(4.28)

Let
t_{0}\geq 0
and
T>0
be fixed. Also, take
\kappa_{2}>0
to be an arbitrary value. Then there exists a constant
R_{0}\geq 0
such that for every

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+T}\int_{(t_{0},t]}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}L(s)\geq R\right)

(4.29)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{64\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}T}\right)+C\exp\big(-\kappa_{2}R\big)+\mathbf{1}_{\{R<R_{0}\}},

where

is a constant not depending on

and
t_{0}
, and
\lambda_{N}\geq 0
the rate of the Poisson process

associated to
L
. If
L
is of class
(HRegM)
, then we may set
R_{0}=0.

Proof.

As before, consider the inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+T}\int_{(t_{0},t]}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}L(s)\geq R\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+T}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}b(s)\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq\frac{R}{2}\right)

(4.30)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+T}\int_{(t_{0},t]}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}Z(s)\geq\frac{R}{2}\right).

Appealing to Lemma
4.3
with
R
and
\beta
replaced by
R/2
and
\beta\sigma
, respectively, gives us

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+T}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}b(s)\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\geq\frac{R}{2}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{64\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}T}\right).

(4.31)

Let
M=(M(t))_{t\geq 0}
be the martingale part of
Z=(Z(t))_{t\geq 0}
, i.e.,

M(t)=Z(t)-\lambda_{N}\mathbb{E}Z_{1}t,\quad t\geq 0.

(4.32)

We obtain the inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+T}\int_{(t_{0},t]}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}Z(s)\geq\frac{R}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+T}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}M(s)\geq\frac{R}{4}\right)+\mathbf{1}_{\{R<R_{0}\}},

(4.33)

for some

sufficiently large. Note that this splitting is not needed when

, thus when

is a martingale. In that case, this allow us to disregard the indicator function, or differently put, we then may set
R_{0}=0.

Subsequently, introduce the process
X=(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
, defined by

X(t)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{(t_{0},t]}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}M(s),&t>t_{0},\\
0,&0\leq t\leq t_{0},\end{cases}

(4.34)

and observe that
X
is a square integrable martingale. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X(t)|\mathcal{F}_{s}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}M(s)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t_{0}}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}M(s)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]

(4.35)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}M(s)-\int_{0}^{t_{0}\wedge s}{b(s)}\,\mathrm{d}M(s)

\displaystyle=X(s),

for
t\geq s\geq 0
. Doob’s supremal inequality (combine
[
43
, Prop. 1.3.6]

Delay equations with stochastic negative feedback

This section is dedicated to proving Theorem

The following method is in line with the proof strategy of the companion paper on the stochastic Wright’s equation
