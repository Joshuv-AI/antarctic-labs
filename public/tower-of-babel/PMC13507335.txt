Toxic effects of Annona squamosa seed extracts against Tuta absoluta (Meyrick) (Lepidoptera: Gelechiidae) and their enzymatic response
Authors: Kannan Swathy, Perumal Vivekanandhan, Sunanta Piyachat, Siripan Thanandon, Pittarate Sarayut, Supamit Mekchay, Peng Han, Patcharin Krutmuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tomato leaf miner, Tuta absoluta, is a major pest responsible for significant yield losses in tomato cultivation worldwide. Increasing resistance to chemical insecticides and growing environmental concerns have underscored the need for eco-friendly pest management alternatives aligned with sustainable development Goals (SDGs) such as zero hunger and responsible consumption and production. This study evaluated the insecticidal potential of Annona squamosa seed extracts prepared using five different solvents: acetone, ethyl acetate, ethanol, methanol, and hexane. Their toxicity was tested against T. absoluta eggs and larvae. Bioassays revealed that the hexane extract, at the highest concentration (250 ppm), completely suppressed egg hatchability (0%) up to 120 h. The same concentration exhibited larvicidal activity, causing 46.66% at 24 h and 73.33% mortality at 48 h post-treatment. The lowest LC₅₀ and LC₉₀ values were observed in the hexane extract: 155.04 and 344.06 ppm at 24 h, and 85.49 and 169.43 ppm at 48 h, respectively. Enzymatic assays indicated a decrease in catalase (CAT) activity and an increase in glutathione S-transferase (GST) activity in T. absoluta larvae 24 h after treatment with the hexane extract. A non-target assay on Eudrilus eugeniae showed 5.33% minimal mortality at the highest concentration, compared to 96.66% mortality in Imidacloprid treatment and 0% in control. GC-MS analysis identified two major compounds, E-11-hexadecenoic acid ethyl ester (25.18%) and 9,12-octadecadienoyl chloride (Z, Z) (15.23%), which may be involved in the insecticidal activity. Molecular docking studies demonstrated strong binding affinities of E-11-hexadecenoic acid ethyl ester and 9,12-octadecadienoyl chloride (Z, Z) with the target insect enzyme acetylcholinesterase (AChE), suggesting their potential mode of action. These findings indicate that the hexane extract of A. squamosa seeds contains promising bioactive molecules with significant insecticidal potential. This research highlights the potential of plant-derived insecticides for environmentally safe and sustainable management of T. absoluta, reducing dependence on synthetic chemicals and promoting sustainable tomato production, good health and well-being and responsible consumption and production.

Introduction

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) is one of the most widely cultivated and economically important horticultural crops worldwide1. It serves not only as a dietary staple but also as a vital source of essential nutrients, including vitamins A and C, lycopene, and various antioxidants, all of which play a crucial role in promoting human health and nutrition2. In many developing countries, tomato production supports smallholder farming systems, significantly contributing to both livelihoods and food security3. Although global demand for tomatoes continues to rise, driving the expansion of cultivation areas, production remains heavily constrained by various biotic stresses—chief among them are insect pests4. Tuta absoluta (Meyrick), commonly known as the tomato leaf miner, is one of the most destructive pests threatening tomato cultivation worldwide5,6. This insect, a member of the family Gelechiidae, was originally endemic to South America but has emerged as an invasive species of global concern due to its rapid spread across Europe, Africa, and Asia in recent decades7. Its expansion has been facilitated by increased international trade, favourable climatic conditions, and remarkable adaptability to diverse agro-ecological environments8. The larvae of T. absoluta are particularly destructive, feeding internally on leaves, stems, flowers, and fruits. Their mining activity disrupts photosynthesis, stunts plant growth, and causes fruit deformities. Such damage leads to substantial yield losses and reduces fruit quality, negatively impacting both marketability and farmers’ income9.

Yield losses caused by T. absoluta can be catastrophic, with damage reaching up to 100% in the absence of effective control measures7,10. The pest’s biology significantly complicates its management: it has a short life cycle, produces multiple generations per year, exhibits high reproductive potential, and engages in cryptic feeding habits that protect larvae from contact insecticides11,12. Moreover, T. absoluta has demonstrated a remarkable capacity to develop resistance to various classes of chemical insecticides, including organophosphates, pyrethroids, and neonicotinoids, severely limiting the effectiveness of conventional control strategies13. The heavy reliance on synthetic insecticides has raised serious concerns beyond resistance development14,15. Chemical control contributes to environmental pollution through pesticide runoff and residue accumulation in soil and water bodies16. Additionally, non-target organisms such as pollinators, natural enemies, and beneficial soil fauna are adversely affected, disrupting ecological balance within agroecosystems. Eudrilus eugeniae, commonly known as the African nightcrawler, is a significant earthworm species widely used in vermicomposting because of its efficiency in decomposing organic waste and enhancing soil fertility17,18. As a beneficial soil organism, it plays a crucial role in maintaining soil health and nutrient cycling16. Assessing the impact of pesticides on E. eugeniae is essential to ensure environmental safety in pest management practices. Human health risks associated with pesticide residues on food products and occupational exposure during pesticide application also demand urgent attention19. These pressing issues underscore the urgent need for sustainable, eco-friendly pest management alternatives that ensure environmental safety, protect human health, and support the long-term viability of agricultural systems.

Botanical insecticides natural products derived from plants have garnered increasing interest as promising alternatives to synthetic chemicals20–25. These insecticides typically consist of complex mixtures of bioactive compounds that act through multiple modes of action on target pests, thereby reducing the likelihood of resistance development26. Their biodegradability and lower toxicity to non-target organisms make them ideal components of integrated pest management (IPM) programs aimed at minimizing chemical inputs. Furthermore, the use of locally available plant materials can reduce costs and improve accessibility for resource-poor farmers, promoting equitable and sustainable agricultural practices27. Annona squamosa L., commonly known as custard apple or sugar apple, is a tropical fruit-bearing tree belonging to the Annonaceae family. It is widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions for its sweet and nutritious fruits28,29. Beyond its economic importance, A. squamosa has traditionally been valued for various therapeutic properties. Recent research has increasingly focused on its potential as a source of bioactive compounds with pesticidal activity29,30. The seeds of A. squamosa are particularly rich in toxic secondary metabolites, including acetogenins, alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, phenolic compounds, coumarins, saponins, quinones, and terpenoids31. These phytochemicals suggest a multi-targeted insecticidal mechanism that enhances efficacy and minimizes the risk of resistance development32.

Annona squamosa extracts have demonstrated significant insecticidal activity against a wide range of medical and agricultural insect pests29. Both seed and leaf extracts, including their nanoparticle formulations, have exhibited strong larvicidal effects against mosquito vectors such as Aedes aegypti, Anopheles stephensi, and Aedes albopictus29,33. Toxicity, growth inhibition, and antifeedant activities have also been reported against lepidopteran pests like Spodoptera litura and Spodoptera frugiperda29,34. The crude extracts of A. squamosa have proven effective against storage pests such as Tribolium castaneum and Callosobruchus chinensis, inducing repellency and mortality35. Additionally, aphid species including Aphis gossypii and Aphis glycines have shown susceptibility to seed oil and ethanolic extracts. Other economically important crop pests, such as Chilo partellus, Nilaparvata lugens, Helicoverpa armigera, and Spilosoma obliqua, have also been successfully managed using seed extracts36. The insecticidal efficacy of A. squamosa extracts strongly depends on solvent polarity: non-polar solvents like hexane and petroleum ether favor the extraction of fatty acids and acetogenins, whereas polar solvents such as methanol and water predominantly extract phenolics and flavonoids37. Despite these promising findings, reports on the effects of A. squamosa extracts against Tuta absoluta remain limited, underscoring the need for further investigation into their potential as eco-friendly alternatives for managing this devastating pest.

Glutathione S-transferase (GST) is a detoxification enzyme in insects that catalyses the conjugation of glutathione to toxic compounds, facilitating their breakdown and excretion. It plays a crucial role in insecticide resistance by neutralizing harmful chemicals, thereby protecting insect cells from oxidative damage and enhancing survival during pesticide exposure38. Catalase (CAT) is an antioxidant enzyme that protects insect cells by decomposing hydrogen peroxide, a harmful reactive oxygen species, into water and oxygen. This detoxification process reduces oxidative stress caused by metabolic activities or chemical exposure, thereby maintaining cellular health and enhancing insect tolerance to environmental and chemical stresses38.

The present study was designed to evaluate the insecticidal potential of chemical compounds derived from A. squamosa seeds, extracted using five different solvents: acetone, ethyl acetate, ethanol, hexane, and methanol. The primary objective was to assess the biological activity of these compounds against both the larval and egg stages of T. absoluta, focusing on larval mortality and egg hatch inhibition. To further elucidate the mode of action, enzymatic assays were conducted to evaluate antioxidant and detoxification responses in T. absoluta larvae, specifically measuring the activities of glutathione S-transferase (GST) and catalase (CAT). Additionally, molecular docking studies were performed to predict the interactions between key bioactive compounds—E-11-hexadecenoic acid, ethyl ester, and 9,12-octadecadienoyl chloride (Z, Z)—and the target enzyme acetylcholinesterase (AChE). This integrated approach provides a comprehensive understanding of the insecticidal mechanisms involved and supports the development of sustainable, plant-based insecticides aligned with global objectives for safe food production and environmental protection.

Materials and methods

Rearing of Tuta absoluta

Tuta absoluta was continuously maintained in the Insect Pathology Laboratory, Department of Entomology and Plant Pathology, Chiang Mai University, Thailand. The ethical approval No. AG04001/2568. The larvae were reared on tomato (S. lycopersicium) and other solanaceous host plants under controlled greenhouse conditions. Environmental parameters were maintained at a temperature of 28 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 65 ± 5%, and a photo period of 12:12 h (light: dark), following the protocol of Vivekanandhan et al.7. Adult moths were provided with a 10% (w/v) honey solution every two days as a nutritional supplement. Potted tomato plants were placed inside rearing cages measuring 60 cm (width) × 90 cm (length) × 60 cm (height) to serve as oviposition substrates. Eggs and larvae that were pesticide-free and morphologically healthy were selected for subsequent experiments.

Preparation of extract

The fruits of A. squamosa were collected from a local market in Chiang Mai, Thailand (18.7883° N, 98.9853° E). Seeds were manually squeezed from the fruits, thoroughly washed with distilled water, and dried under controlled laboratory conditions at a temperature of 27 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 60 ± 2%, and a 12:12 h (light: dark) photo period. Once completely dried, the seeds were ground into a fine powder using an electric stainless-steel grinder, following the method described by Vivekanandhan et al.39,40. For crude extraction, 100 g powdered seed material was macerated using five different solvents of 500 mL—acetone, ethanol, ethyl acetate, hexane, and methanol—at a ratio of 1:5 (w/v), according to the procedure outlined by Pratheeba et al.20. The plant powder–solvent mixtures were stored in sealed glass containers at room temperature for seven days. After the maceration period, the mixtures were filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The filtrates were then concentrated by evaporating the solvents under reduced pressure at 60 °C using a rotary evaporator set at 150 mmHg, following a modified procedure from Fawole et al.41. The crude extraction yields obtained for each solvent were as follows: acetone (21.45 g), ethanol (5.70 g), ethyl acetate (19.71 g), hexane (14.84 g), and methanol (6.29 g). All crude extracts were stored at 4 °C for further experiments.

Insecticidal bioassay

Two distinct bioassays were conducted to evaluate the insecticidal efficacy of extract derived from A. squamosa seeds: an egg hatchability assay and a larval mortality assay. Each assay used five concentrations of crude extracts such as, 50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 ppm—with five different solvents extracts: acetone, ethanol, ethyl acetate, hexane, and methanol, using 0.5% DMSO. All experiments were performed under controlled laboratory conditions, maintained at 25 ± 2 °C, relative humidity 65 ± 5%, and humidity of photoperiod of 16:8 h (light: dark). Each treatment was replicated thrice.

Egg hatchability bioassay

Two-day-old T. absoluta eggs were used to assess egg hatchability following treatment with crude extracts derived from A. squamosa seeds using five different solvents: acetone, ethanol, ethyl acetate, hexane, and methanol. Tomato plant branches bearing eggs on the leaf surfaces were placed in plastic containers filled with water to prevent dehydration. The egg-laden tomato branches were uniformly sprayed with 1 mL of test solutions at five concentrations: 50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 ppm. Test solutions were prepared using 0.5% DMSO (Dimethyl sulfoxide). The treated branches were allowed to dry air for 1 h under ambient laboratory conditions and then transferred into bioassay containers. Each treatment had 20 eggs per replicate. Egg hatchability was recorded at 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h post-treatment. The percentage of egg hatchability was calculated for each time point and compared to the control group, which was treated with distilled water. This assay followed a modified procedure based on Kona et al.42.

Larvicidal assay

Third-instar larvae of T. absoluta were used to evaluate the larvicidal activity of crude extract derived from A. squamosa seeds, following the method described by Nilahyane et al.43 with slight modifications. Test solutions were prepared at five concentrations using 0.5% DMSO—50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 ppm—using extracts obtained from five different solvents: acetone, ethanol, ethyl acetate, hexane, and methanol. Fresh tomato leaf branches were uniformly treated with the respective test solutions and allowed to dry air under laboratory conditions (27 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 65 ± 5%, and photoperiod of 16:8 h light: dark). The treated tomato branches were then placed in plastic containers with their cut ends submerged in water to prevent dehydration. Each treatment had 20 larvae per replicate). The containers were covered with lids perforated with small holes to allow proper aeration. Larval mortality was recorded at 24- and 48-hours post-treatment. Larvae were considered dead if they exhibited no movement upon gentle probing with a fine brush. The percentage of larval mortality was calculated and compared with the control group, which was treated with 0.5% DMSO.

Homogenate preparation

After 24 h of larvicidal treatment, surviving larvae from both the control group and those treated with A. squamosa seed-derived hexane extract were collected to assess the activity of detoxification and antioxidant enzymes—catalase (CAT) and glutathione S-transferase (GST). The midguts of third-instar T. absoluta larvae (three from each group) were dissected under cold conditions. The dissected midgut tissues were pooled and homogenized in 2 mL of ice-cold 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) using a pre-chilled glass homogenizer. The homogenates were then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected and used for subsequent enzymatic assays, following the method described by Shahbaz et al.44.

Enzyme assay

Catalase (CAT) assay

Catalase (CAT) activity was measured by monitoring the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide, following the protocol described by Wang et al.45. Briefly, 0.1 mL of enzyme extract was added to a reaction mixture containing 2.9 mL of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 0.03 mL of 0.036% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). The decrease in absorbance at 240 nm, corresponding to the breakdown of H₂O₂, was continuously recorded for 2 to 3 min using a spectrophotometer. One unit of catalase activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to decompose 1 µmol of hydrogen peroxide per minute at 25 °C.

Glutathione S-Transferase (GST) assay

The activity of glutathione S-transferase (GST) was determined using the method originally described by Habig et al.46, with minor modifications. The reaction mixture (1 mL) contained 1 mM reduced glutathione, 1 mM 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (CDNB), and 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.5). The reaction was initiated by adding 20 µL of crude enzyme extract, ratio of 49:1 (V/V). The formation of the glutathione-CDNB conjugate was monitored by measuring the increase in absorbance at 340 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer. One unit of GST activity was defined as the amount of enzyme catalyzing the conjugation of 10 nmol of CDNB with glutathione per minute under the specified assay conditions.

Eudrilus eugeniae

The soil bioindicator species, E. eugeniae, was maintained under controlled laboratory conditions in the Insect Pathology Laboratory. Environmental parameters were regulated at 28 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 65 ± 5%, and a photoperiod of 12:12 h (light: dark). Earthworms were reared in a substrate composed of cow dung, decomposed plant leaf matter, and cattle manure, which served as their primary food source47,48. Only healthy, mature earthworms were selected for subsequent assays assessing the non-target toxicity effects to assess the treatments.

Artificial soil toxicity assay

The artificial soil toxicity assay was conducted following the method described by Kühnel and Nickel49. The artificial soil consisted of 69% fine sand, 12% ground sphagnum peat, and 19% kaolinite clay. For each treatment, 1 kg of fine soil was mixed with one of the following: imidacloprid (100 ppm), 0.5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as a control, and the hexane crude extract of A. squamosa seeds at a concentration of 250 ppm/kg. The treated soils were thoroughly homogenized and transferred into glass containers with a capacity glass capacity, loosely covered with perforated polypropylene lids to ensure adequate ventilation. All containers were maintained under controlled conditions at 27 ± 2 °C and 85–90% relative humidity. Earthworm (E. eugeniae) mortality was assessed at 24- and 48-hours post-exposure. Earthworms were considered dead if they exhibited no movement upon gentle mechanical stimulation, in accordance with the criteria described by Vivekanandhan et al.25.

Phytochemical screening

A preliminary qualitative phytochemical screening was performed to identify major classes of secondary metabolites present in the crude seed extracts of A. squamosa using standard protocols with minor modifications following Vivekanandhan22. The extracts were tested for alkaloids (Mayer’s test), flavonoids (aluminum chloride colorimetric assay), phenolic compounds (ferric chloride test), saponins (froth formation test), quinones (hydrochloric acid test), tannins (lead subacetate test), and terpenoids (Salkowski test). The presence of these phytochemical groups was confirmed based on characteristic color changes or precipitate formation as described in previous studies22,50–53.

GC–MS analysis

The more potent hexane crude extract of A. squamosa seeds were analyzed using a Shimadzu GC–MS system (Model GCMS-QP2020) equipped with an electron ionization (EI) source. Separation was performed on a silica capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness; Elite-5MS, 5% biphenyl/95% dimethylpolysiloxane). Helium served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. A 1 µL aliquot of the extract was injected in split mode into the injector port maintained at 260 °C. The oven temperature program was as follows: initial hold at 60 °C for follows: a minute, followed by a temperature ramp of 10 °C/min to 300 °C, with a final hold at 300 °C for 6 min. The mass spectrometer conditions included an ion source temperature of 240 °C, electron ionization at 70 eV, and data acquisition over an m/z range of 40–600. The scan speed was set to 0.2 s per scan with a scan interval of 0.1 s54.

Molecular docking studies

Molecular docking simulations were performed using the CB-Dock2 web server to investigate the interactions between two major chemical molecules from the more potent hexane crude extract—E-11-Hexadecenoic acid, ethyl ester (PubChem CID: 445639) and 9,12-Octadecadienoyl chloride (Z, Z) (PubChem CID: 9817754)—and the insect acetylcholinesterase (AChE) enzyme. The crystal structure of AChE (PDB ID: 6XYU) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank and prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard. Preparation involved adding missing hydrogen atoms, optimizing protonation states, and neutralizing surface residues distant from the active site that were not involved in salt bridge formation to enhance structural accuracy. The binding site of AChE was identified using the Sitemap tool, which evaluates potential ligand-binding pockets based on geometric and energetic criteria. Multiple putative binding regions were detected on the protein surface, and an appropriate docking grid was generated to encompass the selected active site region. Ligand preparation included generating all relevant molecular conformations, such as stereoisomers, ionization states, and tautomeric forms. Energy minimization of the ligands was performed using the OPLS-2005 force field with default parameters to ensure optimal geometry prior to docking.

Data analysis

Egg hatchability and larval mortality of T. absoluta were calculated using Abbott’s formula55 to correct for natural mortality in the control group. Median lethal concentration (LC₅₀) and 90% lethal concentration (LC₉₀) values for larval mortality were estimated using probit analysis in IBM SPSS Statistics (version 20; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Catalase (CAT) and glutathione S-transferase (GST) enzyme activity data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Egg hatchability and larval mortality data were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to determine statistically significant differences among treatments and exposure times. Egg hatchability percentages were arcsine square-root transformed prior to analysis to stabilize variances and meet ANOVA assumptions. Multiple comparisons among means were performed using the Bonferroni post hoc test. However, non-transformed means ± SE are presented in table for clarity of biological interpretation. Statistical analyses were conducted using both IBM SPSS Statistics (version 20) and GraphPad Prism (version 5.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Effect on egg hatchability bioassay

The crude seed extracts of A. squamosa significantly reduced the egg hatchability of T. absoluta at 72, 96, and 120 h post-treatment. Statistical analysis revealed a highly significant effect of solvent type on egg hatchability (P < 0.0001). At the highest tested concentration (250 ppm) and 120 h after treatment, egg hatchability was markedly reduced to 1.66% with acetone, 16.66% with ethanol, 6.66% with ethyl acetate, 0% with hexane, and 1.66% with methanol. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) demonstrated significant differences in egg hatchability among the five solvent extracts at each exposure interval: 72 h (df = 25, 72; P < 0.0001), 96 h (df = 25, 72; P < 0.0001), and 120 h (df = 25, 72; P < 0.0001), indicating a strong treatment-dependent response over time. Notably, the hexane, methanol, and acetone extracts exhibited the greatest ovicidal efficacy, achieving near-complete inhibition of egg hatchability across all evaluated time points, thereby highlighting their strong insecticidal potential against T. absoluta (Table 1).

Larvicidal bioassay

The larvicidal bioassay of A. squamosa seed extracts against third-instar larvae of T. absoluta, evaluated at 24 and 48 h post-treatment, revealed highly significant differences among the solvent extracts (P < 0.0001). At 48 h, the highest larval mortality was recorded with the hexane extract (73.33%), followed by methanol (63.33%), acetone (60.00%), ethyl acetate (46.66%), and ethanol (41.66%). Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed that both extract type and concentration significantly influenced larval mortality at both exposure periods. At 24 h, mortality differed significantly among treatments (df = 25, 72; P < 0.0001), and a similarly significant variation was observed at 48 h (df = 25, 72; P < 0.0001), indicating a strong treatment-dependent response over time. Among the tested solvent extracts, hexane exhibited the highest larvicidal efficacy, producing the greatest mortality at both 24 and 48 h post-treatment, thereby highlighting its strong insecticidal potential against T. absoluta (Table 2).

Enzyme assay

Catalase (CAT) activity

The effect of A. squamosa hexane seed extract on catalase activity in T. absoluta larvae was evaluated 24 h post-treatment. A significant, concentration-dependent decline in catalase activity was observed, with the greatest suppression recorded at 250 ppm (Fig. 1a). Compared to the untreated control group, larvae treated with the seed extracts exhibited a marked reduction in catalase levels, with enzyme activity decreasing to 0.3 µmol/Min/Mg protein at 250 ppm. This reduction was statistically significant (F = 37.081; df = 5, 12; P < 0.01). In contrast, catalase activity in control larvae remained stable and within the normal physiological range. The data clearly demonstrate a dose-dependent inhibitory effect of the hexane extract on catalase activity, indicating that increasing concentrations of the extract progressively impair the antioxidant defence system of T. absoluta larvae (Fig. 1a).

Glutathione-S-transferase (GST) activity

The effect of A. squamosa seed extract on glutathione-S-transferase (GST) activity in T. absoluta larvae was evaluated to be 24 h post-treatment. A significant, concentration-dependent increase in GST activity was observed, with the highest induction recorded at 250 ppm (Fig. 1b). Larvae exposed to the seed extract exhibited a marked elevation in GST enzyme levels compared to the untreated control group, with maximum activity reaching 9.0 µmol/Min/Mg protein. This increase was statistically significant (F = 46.609; df = 5,12; P ≤ 0.01). The enhancement of GST activity followed a dose-dependent trend, suggesting that the larvae activated detoxification mechanisms in response to the phytochemical stress imposed by the seed extract. These findings indicate that the seed-derived extracts of A. squamosa may induce oxidative stress, triggering an upregulation of GST as part of the insect’s biochemical defense response (Fig. 1b).

Toxicity to non-target earthworms

The non-target toxicity assessment revealed that imidacloprid (100 ppm) exhibited significantly higher toxicity to E. eugeniae compared to the hexane seed extract of A. squamosa at its highest tested concentration (250 ppm). While Imidacloprid caused severe adverse effects, the A. squamosa hexane seed extract showed minimal impact on earthworms at both 24 and 48 h post-treatment. Statistical analysis indicated no significant difference in mortality between the A. squamosa extract and the untreated control group (F = 1.0815; df = 2, 6; P > 0.01), confirming the extract’s safety for non-target organisms. In contrast, Imidacloprid treatment caused substantial mortality in E. eugeniae, with death rates reaching up to 98%, a result that was highly significant (F = 1012.333; df = 2, 6; P < 0.01) (Fig. 2). Notably, earthworms exposed to the A. squamosa seed extract remained healthy and active throughout the observation period, highlighting the extract’s selective toxicity and environmental safety compared to synthetic chemical insecticides.

Phytochemical assay

Qualitative phytochemical screening of the crude seed extracts of A. squamosa revealed the presence of several major classes of secondary metabolites associated with insecticidal activity (Table 3). Alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, saponins, terpenoids, steroids, glycosides, phenolic compounds, and quinones were detected, with their distribution varying among the different solvent extracts. Non-polar extracts, particularly hexane, showed a strong presence of terpenoids and flavonoids, whereas polar extracts such as methanol and ethanol were richer in alkaloids, flavonoids, phenolic compounds, tannins, saponins, terpenoids, and quinones. Acetone and ethyl acetate extracts exhibited an intermediate phytochemical profile, containing both polar and moderate non-polar compounds. The observed variation in phytochemical composition among the extracts suggests that the insecticidal activity of A. squamosa seeds may result from the combined or synergistic effects of multiple chemical groups present in different solvent fractions.

Molecular docking studies

Molecular docking analysis was performed for two fatty acid derivatives—E-11-Hexadecenoic acid, ethyl ester (PubChem CID: 445639) and 9,12-Octadecadienoyl chloride (Z, Z) (PubChem CID: 9817754) (Figs. 3 and 4)—against acetylcholinesterase (AChE) from Torpedo californica (PDB ID: 6XYU). The study identified five potential binding pockets (C1–C5) for each ligand on the AChE enzyme. Both compounds exhibited their strongest binding affinity at Pocket C1, indicating that this site is the most favourable for interaction with AChE. For E-11-Hexadecenoic acid, ethyl ester, Pocket C1 yielded a docking score of − 6.4 kcal/mol (Fig. 3; Table 5), while 9,12-Octadecadienoyl chloride (Z, Z) showed a stronger interaction with a score of − 7.4 kcal/mol (Fig. 4; Table 5). Pocket C1, the largest cavity identified (volume: 3002 Å³), encompasses both the catalytic active site (CAS) and the peripheral anionic site (PAS) of Chain A. Key residues involved in ligand interactions at this site include Glu69, Tyr71, Tyr73, Trp83, Tyr148, Phe330, Tyr370, Phe371, and Trp472. These interactions suggest that both compounds may exert dual-site inhibition by simultaneously engaging the CAS and PAS regions, a mechanism known to enhance acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitory efficacy. Other identified pockets (C2–C5) demonstrated moderate to low binding affinities, with docking scores ranging from − 4.1 to − 5.2 kcal/mol.

Molecular docking was conducted to evaluate the binding affinity and interaction profile of 9,12-Octadecadienoyl chloride (PubChem CID: 9817754) with acetylcholinesterase (AChE; PDB ID: 6XYU). Using the submitted structures (protein: 6XYU.pdb; ligand: 9817754), the docking workflow identified five distinct binding pockets (C1–C5) within the receptor (Fig. 4; Table 5). These pockets differed in their cavity volumes, spatial locations, and the flexibility of their surrounding residues. The docking configurations are illustrated in Fig. 4, where Panels A–F highlight the ligand orientation within each pocket, visualized using distinct color schemes.

Pocket C1: Highest-affinity binding site

Pocket C1 exhibited the strongest binding affinity with a vina score of − 8.0 and the largest cavity volume (3603 Å³). The docking box was centered at coordinates (36, 66, 9) with dimensions 26 × 26 × 26 Å.

The ligand demonstrated extensive interactions with critical AChE residues, including GLN68, TYR71, TYR73, GLY79, GLU80, TRP83, ASN84, and residues near the catalytic gorge such as GLY149–GLY151, THR154, GLY155, LEU159, TYR162. Additional stabilization was provided by contacts with GLU237, SER238, ALA239, TRP271, TRP321, TYR324, LEU328, PHE330, TYR370, PHE371, TYR374, ASP375, and residues near the peripheral anionic site (PHE440, TRP472, LEU479, HIS480, GLY481, ILE484) (Fig. 4; Table 5). The dense network of hydrophobic and polar interactions supports C1 as the primary and most favorable binding region for the ligand.

Pocket C2: Moderate-affinity cavity

Pocket C2 displayed a vina score of − 4.9 with a cavity volume of 1167 Å³, centered at (20, 77, 20). Key interacting residues included ASN268, ALA269, PRO270, TRP271, HIS273, LYS278, ILE327, LEU328, SER329, and ASP344. Additional contacts involved MET346, ILE400, PHE401, GLY402, LYS403, ALA404, GLU408, ILE412, and several residues toward the protein surface (ASP437, HIS438, PHE439, CYS442, PRO443, GLU446, TRP563, ASN564, LEU567, ARG571) (Fig. 4; Table 5). This pocket offers a narrower and more hydrophilic cavity than C1, resulting in reduced affinity.

Pocket C3: Stable secondary binding region

Pocket C3 yielded a vina score of − 5.0 and a cavity volume of 591 Å³, centered at (38, 56, 3). The ligand interacted with residues near the upper channel of AChE, including PHE77, SER78, GLY79, ILE82, TRP83, and additional contacts with ASP160, ILE161, ASN163, ILE166. Deeper interactions involved THR467, THR469, SER470, LEU471, TRP472, LEU479, GLY481, ASP482, GLU485, and residues near the exit channel (GLN490, ASN493, LEU496, GLN497, TYR498) (Fig. 4; Table 5). Although smaller than C1, pocket C3 supports stable hydrophobic interactions reflected by its moderate docking score.

Pocket C4: Small peripheral cavity

Pocket C4 showed a vina score of − 4.8 and a cavity volume of 232 Å³, with a center at (39, 73, 37). Interacting residues primarily lined the protein periphery, including LEU388, PRO389, ARG390, LEU394, ASN398, and GLN406–GLU410. Additional contacts extended to ILE413, PHE414, GLN415, TYR416, THR417, SER418, TRP419, GLU420, GLY421, PRO423, GLY424, and several polar residues such as GLN426, ASN427, GLN430, ARG433, ASP437, PRO555, ALA558, ARG559 (Fig. 4; Table 5). The compact nature of this cavity contributed to a weaker binding affinity relative to C1–C3.

Pocket C5: Smallest cavity with lowest affinity

Pocket C5 recorded the lowest affinity with a vina score of − 3.9, and a cavity volume of 208 Å³, centered at (17, 59, 30). Interactions included MET346, MET349, LYS350, ASN445, GLU446, GLN449, ALA450, GLU453, ARG454, and several surface-exposed residues such as LYS534, ALA558, SER561, PHE562, ASN564, ASP565, TYR566 (Fig. 4; Table 5). The limited interaction area and shallow pocket geometry likely account for its reduced binding strength. Among the five pockets, C1 emerged as the most favorable binding site due to its larger cavity volume, extensive contact network, and the most negative docking score. The results collectively indicate that 9,12-Octadecadienoyl chloride is capable of occupying multiple regions of AChE, but its highest binding stability occurs within Pocket C1, suggesting this region as the potential primary interaction site.

Discussion

The tomato leaf miner, T. absoluta (Lepidoptera: Gelechiidae), has emerged as one of the most destructive pests of tomato cultivation worldwide due to its cryptic feeding behavior, high fecundity, and rapid development of resistance to conventional insecticides, making its control particularly challenging56. In response to these challenges, interest has increasingly shifted toward the use of botanical insecticides, which are generally considered eco-friendly, biodegradable, and compatible with integrated pest management (IPM) strategies20,22–25,57. Among these, A. squamosa (custard apple) has gained considerable attention due to its diverse array of secondary metabolites with established insecticidal activity29. The seeds of A. squamosa are particularly rich in acetogenins, alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, and phenolic compounds, which are known to act through multiple biochemical and physiological mechanisms31. In the present study, seed extract of A. squamosa prepared using various solvents—acetone, ethanol, ethyl acetate, hexane, and methanol—were evaluated for their egg hatchability and larvicidal efficacy against T. absoluta. The hexane extract at 250 ppm exhibited the highest activity, completely inhibiting egg hatchability (0%) by 120 h post-treatment, followed by methanol and acetone extracts, both reducing hatchability to 1.66%. Similarly, larvicidal assays showed significant mortality at 48 h post-treatment, with the hexane extract achieving 73.33% mortality, followed by methanol (63.33%) and acetone (60.00%). These findings are consistent with previous studies on lepidopteran pests such as S. litura and Helicoverpa armigera, where A. squamosa seed derived crude extracts prepared in non-polar and semi-polar solvents demonstrated strong toxic effects58,59.

Like the present findings, crude seed extracts of A. squamosa have previously demonstrated significant insecticidal and antifeedant activities against key lepidopteran pests, including the diamondback moth (Plutella xylostella L.) and the cabbage looper (Trichoplusia ni Hübner)60. Moreover, these crude extracts exhibited comparatively lower toxicity toward beneficial arthropods such as Chrysoperla carnea (Stephens) (Neuroptera: Chrysopidae) and Orius insidiosus (Say) (Hemiptera: Anthocoridae)61. Among these, C. carnea larvae were found to be less susceptible to the extracts than adult O. insidiosus, suggesting selective toxicity that supports the conservation of natural enemies in agroecosystems60,61. Similarly, seed extracts of A. squamosa and A. muricata (Annonaceae) have demonstrated high toxicity against both larvae and adults of Aedes albopictus and Culex quinquefasciatus (Culicidae), highlighting their potential as botanical alternatives for vector control62.

The superior activity of the hexane extract can be attributed to its efficiency in extracting lipophilic bioactives such as acetogenins, terpenoids, and fatty acids. Acetogenins are known to inhibit mitochondrial electron transport, thereby disrupting ATP synthesis and causing cellular energy depletion, which leads to mortality63. Fatty acid derivatives exert their effects through multiple pathways, including membrane disruption, enzyme inhibition, ovicidal suffocation, and interference with embryogenesis via chorion penetration64. Meanwhile, flavonoids and phenolics, which are more abundant in polar extracts like methanol and acetone, are known to induce oxidative stress and disrupt normal metabolic and hormonal functions, thereby contributing to larval mortality. The significant ovicidal activity observed is particularly noteworthy, as it directly impacts the pest’s reproductive potential and population dynamics. The reduction in egg hatchability may be attributed to the ability of fatty acid derivatives and flavonoids to penetrate the chorion, causing embryonic developmental arrest. Additionally, phenolic compounds may compromise eggshell permeability, leading to desiccation or failure of embryogenesis. Similar effects were reported by Kumar et al.30, who found that A. squamosa seed extracts interfered with embryonic development in other lepidopteran species. Therefore, the combined ovicidal and larvicidal actions observed in this study reinforce the potential of A. squamosa extracts as effective botanical insecticides against T. absoluta. Similarly, extract derived from A. squamosa have demonstrated significant larvicidal activity against the larvae of the diamondback moth, P. xylostella L65. Consistent with these findings, aqueous extracts of A. squamosa and its bioactive compounds have also exhibited acaricidal, insecticidal, and larvicidal efficacy against a variety of blood-feeding parasites, including Haemaphysalis bispinosa, the hematophagous fly Hippobosca maculata, as well as larvicidal activity against the cattle tick Rhipicephalus spp., Anopheles subpictus, and C. quinquefasciatus66.

Biochemical enzyme assays further elucidated the mode of action of hexane extract. A significant inhibition of catalase (CAT) activity and a corresponding increase in glutathione-S-transferase (GST) levels were observed in larvae exposed to the hexane extract. GST is essential for detoxifying xenobiotic compounds through glutathione conjugation, while catalase plays a key role in mitigating oxidative damage by neutralizing hydrogen peroxide67. Suppression of these enzymes indicates that the phytochemicals in the extract compromise the insect’s antioxidant defense system, leading to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species and subsequent cellular damage. The elevated lipid peroxidation observed supports this oxidative stress mechanism. Similar oxidative disruptions have been documented in S. litura following exposure to neem and other botanical insecticides17, as well as in H. armigera treated with phenolic-rich plant extracts68. These findings reinforce that oxidative imbalance is a key physiological target of plant-derived insecticidal agents. In line with the present study, extracts derived from Melaleuca cajuputi leaf essential oils have been shown to inhibit key detoxification and neural enzymes, including acetylcholinesterase, α-carboxylesterase, and β-carboxylesterase, in the larvae of Anopheles stephensi25. Additionally, a related study reported Acacia nilotica derived essential oils caused significant changes in the enzymatic profile of C. quinquefasciatus, characterized by a marked decrease in acetylcholinesterase activity and a concurrent increase in glutathione S-transferase levels compared to the untreated control group57.

The non-target toxicity assessment using E. eugeniae revealed that the A. squamosa hexane extract caused no observable adverse effects, with survival rates comparable to untreated controls. In contrast, Imidacloprid treatment resulted in nearly 99% mortality within 48 h. These findings align with Leatemia65, who reported that A. squamosa seed extracts exhibited no toxicity to earthworms, whereas chemical insecticides caused significant mortality. Isman and Seffrin69 also emphasized the safety and selective toxicity of botanical insecticides, highlighting their biodegradability and minimal impact on beneficial soil organisms compared to synthetic insecticides such as organophosphates and carbamates. Consistent with the present findings, essential oil molecules derived from M. cajuputi exhibited no toxic effects on the non-target soil invertebrate E. eugeniae25. Similarly, essential oils from Acacia nilotica were found to be non-toxic to both E. eugeniae and the aquatic indicator species Artemia salina, further reinforcing the environmental safety and selectivity of botanical insecticides toward non-target organisms57.

Phytochemical screening revealed that the choice of solvent for extraction significantly influenced the qualitative profile of secondary metabolites. Methanol and ethanol extracts exhibited a strong presence of alkaloids, flavonoids, phenolics, tannins, saponins, quinones, and terpenoids, reflecting the broad solubilizing capacity of polar solvents. Acetone extracts were rich in flavonoids and phenolics but lacked tannins and saponins. Ethyl acetate extracts showed moderate levels of flavonoids, alkaloids, and quinones. Hexane extracts primarily contained lipophilic compounds such as acetogenins, alkaloids, and terpenoids but lacked polar compounds like saponins. These findings are consistent with previous research by Dey et al.33, which emphasized the critical role of solvent polarity in determining both the chemical composition and biological activity of A. squamosa seed extracts. This variation highlights the importance of optimizing extraction protocols to enrich bioactive fractions with maximal insecticidal potential.

GC–MS analysis of the hexane extract from A. squamosa seeds identified ten major compounds, with E-11-hexadecenoic acid, ethyl ester (25.18%) and 9,12-octadecadienoyl chloride (Z, Z) (15.23%) being the most abundant (Table 3). These fatty acid derivatives are known for their insecticidal and antimicrobial properties, likely contributing to the extract’s bioactivity. Other components, such as hexadecanoic acid, ethyl ester and halogenated or heterocyclic compounds, including thiadiazole and brominated aromatic derivatives—may also enhance insecticidal effects through synergistic or multi-target mechanisms. The presence of diverse chemical classes suggests a complex mode of action, potentially increasing the extract’s efficacy and reducing the risk of resistance development in pests. Similar to the findings of the present study, the essential oil of Gardenia jasminoides demonstrated strong repellent, insecticidal, and anti-oviposition activities when extracted with ethanol and tested against Bemisia tabaci and Tetranychus urticae at both adult and nymph stages under laboratory and greenhouse conditions70. Similarly, Capsicum annuum L. (Cayenne oil), combined with gamma irradiation, was shown to enhance the efficacy of Bacillus thuringiensis (Kurst.) in controlling Pectinophora gossypiella (Saund.)71. Overall, the insecticidal activity of the A. squamosa seed extract is likely driven by a combination of abundant fatty acid esters and bioactive minor molecules. Further studies are recommended to isolate active compounds and clarify their mechanisms.

To complement the biochemical findings, molecular docking studies provided a mechanistic basis for the observed insecticidal effects. Two major compounds identified by GC-MS—E-11-hexadecenoic acid ethyl ester and 9,12-octadecadienoyl chloride (Z, Z)—demonstrated strong binding affinities toward acetylcholinesterase (AChE), particularly at the enzyme’s catalytic and peripheral anionic sites. AChE is essential for synaptic signal termination, and its inhibition results in neuromuscular dysfunction, leading to paralysis and eventual death72. Therefore, the docking results support a neurotoxic mode of action, in addition to oxidative and metabolic disruption. The multi-targeted mechanisms of these compounds underscore the complexity and effectiveness of A. squamosa seed extracts as botanical insecticides.

Altogether, this study provides comprehensive evidence that A. squamosa seed extracts, particularly the hexane fraction, exhibit significant ovicidal and larvicidal activities against T. absoluta. These effects are mediated through a combination of biochemical, physiological, and molecular mechanisms. The integration of phytochemical analysis, bioassays, enzymatic assays, and molecular docking offers a robust understanding of the insecticidal mode of action. The consistency of these findings with prior studies on major insect pests such as H. armigera, T. absoluta, S. litura, and A. aegypti65,73, further supports the broad-spectrum potential of A. squamosa as a botanical insecticide. Nonetheless, the marked differences in efficacy across solvent types highlight the need for further refinement of extraction methods to maximize the yield of active molecules. The strong bioactivity, coupled with high selectivity and environmental safety demonstrated by the A. squamosa hexane extract, positions it as a promising candidate for inclusion in sustainable pest management strategies, potentially reducing reliance on hazardous synthetic insecticides and contributing to safer agricultural practices.

Conclusion

The present study provides compelling evidence of the insecticidal efficacy of A. squamosa seed extracts, particularly the hexane-derived crude extract, against the tomato leaf miner, T. absoluta. The hexane extract demonstrated significant ovicidal and larvicidal activity, completely inhibiting egg hatchability and achieving over 73% larval mortality 48 h post-treatment. The low LC₅₀ and LC₉₀ values further confirm the potency of this botanical extract. Biochemical assays revealed a notable reduction in catalase activity alongside a corresponding increase in glutathione-S-transferase levels in treated larvae, suggesting that the extract induces oxidative stress and activates detoxification pathways. Importantly, non-target toxicity tests on E. eugeniae showed no significant mortality, highlighting the extract’s safety and selectivity compared to the chemical insecticide Imidacloprid, which caused near-total mortality. Chemical profiling via GC-MS analysis identified E-11-hexadecenoic acid, ethyl ester and 9,12-octadecadienoyl chloride (Z, Z) as the major molecules of the hexane extract.

Molecular docking studies demonstrated that both compounds exhibit strong binding affinities to acetylcholinesterase (AChE), particularly at the enzyme’s catalytic and peripheral anionic sites. These interactions suggest that the insecticidal activity of the extract may involve neural inhibition through AChE disruption. Collectively, the bioassay, enzymatic, chemical, and computational results support the conclusion that A. squamosa seed extract—especially the hexane extract—contain bioactive molecules with potent insecticidal properties and a favorable environmental safety profile. These findings highlight the potential of A. squamosa seed extract as a promising botanical alternative to synthetic insecticides for managing T. absoluta. Integrating such plant-derived products into pest control strategies could reduce reliance on chemical pesticides, mitigate environmental risks, and promote more sustainable tomato production systems. Future research focusing on the isolation and structural elucidation of active compounds, formulation development, field validation, and comprehensive ecotoxicological assessments will be essential to advance these extracts toward commercial application as effective, eco-friendly biopesticides.
