When size misleads: CT attenuation and enhancement characteristics predict benignity in a giant ovarian mucinous cystadenoma
Authors: Srikanth Nalla, Pravin Sudhakar Patil, Sireesha TV
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Giant ovarian cysts are uncommon due to widespread early imaging detection; however, when encountered—particularly in perimenopausal women—they raise significant concern for malignancy. We report a 53-year-old woman presenting with progressive abdominal distension and 4 months of amenorrhea. Ultrasound demonstrated a 27 cm cystic adnexal mass with internal debris, categorized as O-RADS US 3. Contrast-enhanced CT showed a well-circumscribed cyst with homogeneous near-water attenuation (0-4 Hounsfield units), no enhancement, and no solid components, ascites, or lymphadenopathy—features demonstrating benign imaging characteristics analogous to O-RADS 2. Serum tumor markers (CA-125 15.9 U/mL, CEA 0.999 ng/mL, AFP 1.21 ng/mL, β-hCG < 0.2 mIU/mL, and LDH 215 U/L) were overall within or near normal limits. The patient underwent staging laparotomy, and histopathology confirmed benign mucinous cystadenoma with reactive changes in lymph nodes, omentum, and peritoneal biopsies. This case highlights that near-water CT attenuation and absence of enhancement are strong predictors of benignity even in extremely large adnexal masses, and that lesion size alone should not be considered a surrogate marker for malignancy.

Introduction

Ovarian cystic lesions are common, but truly ``giant'' ovarian cysts—typically defined as lesions exceeding 10-15 cm—have become infrequent in the era of routine ultrasonography and cross-sectional imaging [1,2]. When such lesions present in perimenopausal or postmenopausal women, the clinical concern for malignancy is high and often drives aggressive surgical management [3]. Historically, large size itself was considered worrisome; however, contemporary practice recognizes that internal architecture and enhancement characteristics are more informative predictors of malignant potential than size alone [4,5].

Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) complement ultrasound by clarifying organ of origin, tissue composition, and disease extent [6]. Among CT features, attenuation values and contrast enhancement are particularly useful in distinguishing simple or benign cystic lesions from solid or complex neoplasms [7]. We present a perimenopausal patient with a massive adnexal cyst in whom CT demonstrated near-water attenuation and no enhancement, enabling a confident preoperative impression of benignity that was subsequently confirmed by comprehensive histopathology.

Case presentation

Patient information and history

A 53-year-old perimenopausal woman (gravida 2, para 2) presented with progressive abdominal distension, abdominal bloating, and amenorrhea of 4 months duration. She denied abdominal pain, weight loss, nausea, vomiting, or altered bowel habits, but reported early satiety. Her past medical history was significant only for hypothyroidism, well-controlled on thyroxine daily. She had no prior abdominal or pelvic surgeries, and family history was negative for breast, ovarian, or colorectal malignancy.

Physical examination

On admission, she was conscious, afebrile, and in fair general condition. The abdomen was distended with full flanks, soft and non-tender, with normal bowel sounds. No clinical ascites was detected. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable.

Laboratory investigations

Complete blood count, renal function tests, and liver function tests were within normal limits. Serum tumor markers demonstrated CA-125 of 15.9 U/mL (normal < 35 U/mL), CEA 0.999 ng/mL (normal < 3 ng/mL in non-smokers), AFP 1.21 ng/mL (normal < 8 ng/mL), β-hCG < 0.2 mIU/mL (normal < 5.3 mIU/mL in non-pregnant women), and LDH 215 U/L (normal 135-214 U/L), representing only a minimal borderline elevation. Overall, the tumor marker profile supported a low likelihood of malignancy.

Imaging

Ultrasound

Transabdominal ultrasonography revealed a large, well-defined, predominantly cystic abdominopelvic lesion measuring approximately 27 × 25 × 24 cm, likely arising from the right adnexa. Internal echogenic debris was present. No definite septations, mural nodules, or solid components were identified. The mass caused significant mass effect on adjacent organs. No ascites or lymphadenopathy was seen. Contrast-enhanced CT

CECT of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated a well-circumscribed, massive cystic lesion occupying the abdominopelvic cavity with homogeneous near-water attenuation measuring approximately 0-3 HU on non-contrast images and 4 HU post-contrast (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4). The negligible change in attenuation further supported the absence of enhancing soft tissue. No solid components, enhancing septations, ascites, omental caking, peritoneal deposits, or lymphadenopathy were identified. MRI

MRI was not performed because the CT findings (near-water attenuation, smooth walls, and absence of enhancement) were considered diagnostic of a benign cystic lesion, and the patient’s marked abdominal distension made prolonged supine positioning impractical. CT also adequately delineated disease extent for surgical planning.

Management and surgical approach

A surgical oncology consultation was obtained. The patient was counseled regarding the need for staging laparotomy and the risks of bleeding, infection, blood transfusion, prolonged ICU stay, and ventilatory support. Written informed consent was obtained.

Surgical procedure

Patient underwent staging laparotomy under combined general and epidural anesthesia. Intraoperatively, a 25 × 15 cm cystic lesion arising from the right ovary with minimal focal wall thickening and no definite solid nodules was identified and excised. Total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, peritoneal and lymph node sampling, and infracolic omentectomy were performed. Minimal ascites (< 50 mL) was noted, without liver or subdiaphragmatic deposits. The procedure was uneventful.

Postoperative course and follow-up

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient remained afebrile, was ambulated early, and discharged on postoperative day 5 with the drain in situ. At 2-day follow-up, the drain was removed and wound healing was satisfactory. At 4-month follow-up, she remained asymptomatic with no evidence of recurrence on clinical or sonographic evaluation.

Histopathology and cytology

Gross examination revealed a large unilocular ovarian cyst (∼ 23 × 23 cm) with a thin wall containing grey-brown fluid and no solid or papillary areas (Fig. 5). Microscopy showed mucin-secreting columnar epithelium lining a fibrocollagenous stroma, consistent with benign mucinous cystadenoma. Pelvic lymph nodes, peritoneal and omental biopsies showed only reactive/inflammatory changes without malignancy. Peritoneal fluid cytology demonstrated reactive epithelial cells with mild atypia but no malignant cells. Discussion

Giant ovarian cysts are now uncommon because most adnexal lesions are detected earlier [1,2]. When they do occur—particularly in perimenopausal or postmenopausal patients—they pose a diagnostic challenge, as malignancy must be carefully excluded [3,4]. While size historically raised suspicion, modern imaging emphasizes morphology, tissue composition, and enhancement characteristics over size alone [[5], [6], [7]]. The negative tumor marker profile (CA-125, CEA, AFP, β-hCG, and LDH with only borderline elevation) provided supportive—though not definitive—evidence of low malignant potential when interpreted alongside the benign imaging morphology [4,8]. An instructive aspect of this case is the mild atypia on cytology despite benign final histopathology, highlighting the limitations of cytology in isolation and the importance of correlation with cross-sectional imaging and surgical pathology.

Many published reports of giant ovarian cysts focus on surgical challenges, torsion, or diagnostic uncertainty regarding organ of origin. In contrast, the present case highlights a radiology-centered teaching point: objective CT tissue characterization can allow confident preoperative prediction of benignity even in extremely large adnexal masses. In this patient, the lesion demonstrated homogeneous near-water attenuation (∼ 0-4 HU) and absence of post-contrast enhancement, strongly arguing against clinically significant solid tumor components. The absence of ascites, peritoneal disease, or lymphadenopathy further reduced the likelihood of malignancy.

Comparison with recently published giant ovarian mucinous cystadenomas

To our knowledge, few published reports have emphasized quantitative CT attenuation measurements together with structured O-RADS risk stratification in giant mucinous cystadenomas. Six recent case reports have described giant mucinous cystadenomas ranging from 22 to 40 cm [8,[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. These reports primarily highlighted surgical challenges, diagnostic confusion, or elevated CA-125 levels in benign disease. However, none provided quantitative CT attenuation values (Hounsfield units), and few incorporated structured low-risk O-RADS assessment in giant cystic lesions. Our case is unusual in demonstrating that a giant (27 cm) ovarian cyst can show imaging features strongly favoring benignity—unilocular morphology, near-water attenuation (0-4 HU), and absence of enhancement—despite its massive size. Ultrasound categorized the lesion as O-RADS US 3, while CT demonstrated benign imaging features analogous to an O-RADS 2 lesion, increasing diagnostic confidence. Normal tumor markers, negative staging biopsies, and 4-month follow-up further supported the benign nature of the lesion.

Key radiological lesson and limitations

Size alone should not outweigh objective CT tissue characterization in assessing adnexal masses. In giant cystic ovarian lesions, near-water attenuation (< 15 HU), absence of enhancement, and lack of solid components strongly favor benign pathology and can improve preoperative diagnostic confidence. Although histopathology remains the gold standard, this case highlights the value of quantitative CT assessment in risk stratification of giant adnexal cysts.

Conclusion

This case illustrates that even extremely large adnexal cysts may be benign when imaging demonstrates homogeneous near-water attenuation, absence of enhancement, and no associated peritoneal disease. Quantitative CT assessment provides valuable objective criteria for preoperative characterization and should be considered alongside morphology rather than lesion size alone.
