Distribution-free root cause analysis
Rohan Hore, Aaditya Ramdas

Abstract
We study distribution-free root cause analysis in multi-stream data, where an evolving underlying system is observed through multiple data streams that may each undergo distributionaleach stream may undergo a changes at an unknown timepoints. In such settings, and the goal is to identify the stream exhibitingwith the earliest change provides a natural starting point for investigating the underlying cause, which we refer to as the , which we call the root-cause index . Leveraging conformal p p -values, we propose a novel framework, Conformal Root Cause Analysis (CROC), which constructs finite-sample valid confidence sets for the root-cause index under minimal assumptions: the data streams are independent, and within each stream the pre- and post-change observations are sampled exchangeably from arbitrary and unknown distributions. We further establish a universality property, showing that any distribution-free method for root cause localization can be represented within the CROC framework. In addition, under mild regularity conditions and principled score design, our method yields asymptotically sharp confidence sets that efficiently isolate the root cause. We further extend CROC to efficiently handle cross-stream dependence when present. Extensive simulations demonstrate accurate localization of the root stream, supporting our theoretical guarantees.

Introduction

In this paper, we study root-cause analysis in multi-stream data, where observations arise from multiple interacting sources evolving over time and undergoing distributional changes. In many applications, these streams serve as downstream monitors of an underlying system that is not directly observed, and changes in the data reflect latent system-level shifts—for instance, changes in restaurant operations may be reflected in later variations in customer feedback, while policy changes may result in later shifts in financial indicators. Root-cause analysis aims to identify the mechanism driving such changes
(
20
;
2
;
14
)
. However, modeling the relationship between observed streams and the underlying system typically requires strong structural or domain assumptions, which may be unreliable in practice.
In most such settings, temporal ordering provides a natural signal: when changes propagate across many streams, the stream with the earliest change offers a principled starting point for identifying the source of the disruption. Accordingly, in this work, we define the
root-cause index
as the stream whose distribution changes before the others. This serves as an actionable proxy for the underlying cause, particularly in systems where effects propagate downstream over time.

Formally, let
K\in\mathbb{N}
denote the number of data streams, and write
[m]=\{1,\ldots,m\}
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. For each
k\in[K]
, we observe a sequence of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
-valued random variables
(X_{1,k},\ldots,X_{n,k})
, where the spaces
\mathcal{X}_{k}
may differ across streams (e.g., images in one stream and textual descriptions in another). We assume that each stream may undergo a distributional change at an unknown location. Specifically, there exists an unknown vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}:=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{K})\in[n]^{K}
such that, for each
k\in[K]
,

(X_{1,k},\ldots,X_{\xi_{k},k})\sim\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{0,\xi_{k}},\qquad(X_{\xi_{k}+1,k},\ldots,X_{n,k})\sim\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{1,\xi_{k}},

where
\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{0,\xi_{k}}
and
\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{1,\xi_{k}}
denote the pre- and post-change distributions, respectively. Here
\xi_{k}=n
indicates that no changepoint occurs in stream
k
. We assume that at least one coordinate of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is strictly less than
n
, ensuring the presence of a non-trivial change.

In many applications, additional information is available regarding the joint configuration of changepoints across streams. We encode this via a known constraint set
\mathcal{R}\subseteq[n]^{K}
with
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{R}
. The set
\mathcal{R}
captures structural relationships among the changepoints. For instance,
\mathcal{R}=[n]^{K}
allows arbitrary changepoint configurations, while
\mathcal{R}=\{(t,\ldots,t):t\in[n]\}
corresponds to a fully synchronized setting where all streams share a common changepoint.

1.1
Distribution-free root cause analysis

Given
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{R}
, we define the root-cause index as
k_{\star}:=\arg\min_{k\in[K]}\xi_{k}

and assume throughout that the minimizer is unique. Our goal is to identify
k_{\star}
in a distribution-free manner. To this end, we do not assume knowledge of the data-generating distribution beyond the following condition.

Assumption 1
.

The data streams are mutually independent across
k\in[K]
. Moreover, for each
k\in[K]
, the pre- and post-change distributions are independent and individually exchangeable, i.e.,
\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{0,\xi_{k}}\perp\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{1,\xi_{k}}
, and for any permutations
\pi:[\xi_{k}]\to[\xi_{k}]
and
\pi^{\prime}:[n]\setminus[\xi_{k}]\to[n]\setminus[\xi_{k}]
,

(X_{1,k},\ldots,X_{\xi_{k},k})\overset{d}{=}(X_{\pi(1),k},\ldots,X_{\pi(\xi_{k}),k}),\,\,\,(X_{\xi_{k}+1,k},\ldots,X_{n,k})\overset{d}{=}(X_{\pi^{\prime}(\xi_{k}+1),k},\ldots,X_{\pi^{\prime}(n),k}).

We write
\mathbf{X}:=(X_{i,k})_{i\in[n],\,k\in[K]}
for the full data array. For any
k\in[K]
, let
\mathcal{H}_{0,k}
denote the hypothesis that
k_{\star}=k
. We write
\mathbb{P}_{k}
and
\mathbb{E}_{k}
for probability and expectation under
\mathcal{H}_{0,k}
. We also write
2^{[K]}
denotes the power set of
[K]
. Under this setup, we define a distribution-free confidence set for the root-cause index as follows.

Definition 1
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
. A mapping
\mathcal{K}_{1-\alpha}:\mathcal{X}^{n\times K}\to 2^{[K]}
is called a
distribution-free confidence set for the root-cause index
at level
1-\alpha
if
\mathbb{P}_{k_{\star}}\bigl(k_{\star}\in\mathcal{K}_{1-\alpha}(\mathbf{X})\bigr)\;\geq\;1-\alpha

for all
\mathcal{P}
satisfying
1
.

We note that
1
is quite mild: it requires only independence across streams and segment-wise exchangeability within each stream, without any further knowledge of the pre- and post-change distributions. This stands in contrast to much of the existing literature on root-cause analysis, which typically relies on structural assumptions or domain knowledge
(
17
;
22
;
1
)
.

A natural approach to this problem is to first localize changepoints in each stream and then subsequently identify the root cause. However, even the simpler task of changepoint localization remains underexplored in a fully distribution-free setting. Broadly, existing approaches of changepoint localization fall into three categories. Parametric methods
(
12
;
15
)
often require a separation between pre- and post-change distributions. Nonparametric methods
(
9
;
18
)
typically provide only asymptotic guarantees and may involve difficult-to-calibrate constants. Resampling-based approaches
(
6
)
are widely used in practice but generally lack rigorous finite-sample guarantees.

More recently,
(
7
;
10
)
take initial steps toward distribution-free changepoint localization, and these developments serve as key building blocks for our framework.

Methodology

In this section, we develop a general framework for distribution-free root cause analysis, based on conformal
p
-value machinery. Since introduction in
19
, conformal
p
-values have been extensively employed in several other applications
(
3
;
21
;
11
)
. Apart from them, the conformal changepoint localization (
CONCH
), introduced in
10
, provides a distribution-free changepoint localization in the single-stream setting. Motivated by the
CONCH

Conformal Root Cause Analysis
(CROC).

2.1
Background: changepoint localization in single data-stream

We begin by reviewing the
CONCH
procedure, which provides a distribution-free confidence set for the true changepoint when there is a single data stream. Beyond serving as background, the purpose of this section is to also highlight the key principle that will guide the construction of
CROC
.

To that end, consider the case
K=1
, where we observe a sequence
\mathbf{X}:=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
with an unknown changepoint
\xi\in[n-1]
, such that
1
holds.
The implementation of
CONCH
requires specifying two key components:

(i)

ChangePoint Plausibility (CPP) score.
This is any mapping
S:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. While there is no restriction on the choice of
S
to implement the algorithm, the guiding philosophy is that the
t
-th coordinate
S_{t}
should quantify the plausibility of
t
being a changepoint, with larger values indicating stronger evidence.

(ii)

Split-permutation group.
For each
t\in[n-1]
, define

\Pi_{t}:=\Bigl\{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}:\pi(i)\leq t\ \text{for all }i\leq t,\ \ \pi(i)>t\ \text{for all }i>t\Bigr\},

i.e., the set of permutations that act separately on the pre- and post-change segments.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,t}
denote the hypothesis that
\xi=t
. Under
1
, if
t
is the true changepoint, then permutations in
\Pi_{t}
preserve the pre- and post-change exchangeability, and hence leave the distribution of
S_{t}(\mathbf{X})
invariant under
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,t}
.
This invariance then leads to the conformal
p
-value

p_{t}:=\frac{1}{|\Pi_{t}|}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{t}}{\mathbbm{1}}\left\{{S_{t}(\pi(\mathbf{X}))\leq S_{t}(\mathbf{X})}\right\},

(2.1)

which is super-uniform under
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,t}
. Finally, the corresponding confidence set is obtained by thresholding these
p
-values appropriately, and defining
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{CONCH}}_{1-\alpha}:=\{t\in[n-1]:p_{t}>\alpha\}
.

This construction is not only distribution-free valid, but also admits a natural interpretation: building a confidence set for
\xi
is equivalent to testing the hypotheses
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,t}
for all candidate changepoint locations
t\in[n-1]
.

We now extend the correspondence between hypothesis testing and confidence sets to the multi-stream setting for root-cause analysis. Building on this philosophy, for this task, it suffices to test the null hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{0,k}

k\in[K]

Under the null
\mathcal{H}_{0,k}

\boldsymbol{\xi}

Equivalently, we may write

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\mathbf{t}}

\boldsymbol{\xi}

\mathbf{t}

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\mathbf{t}}

\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{R}

(i)

3
Finite-sample validity of
CROC

In this section, we establish the finite-sample coverage of
\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{CROC}}_{1-\alpha}
. Recall that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\mathbf{t}}
denotes distributions satisfying
1
with changepoint configuration
\mathbf{t}
, while
\mathcal{H}_{0,k}
denotes those satisfying
1
with root index
k
.

Theorem 3.1

(Validity)
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For each
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{R}
, the
p
-value
p_{\mathbf{t}}
in (
2.2
) satisfies
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\mathbf{t}}}(p_{\mathbf{t}}\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha
. Thus,
\mathbb{P}_{k}(p_{(k)}\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha
, and
\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{CROC}}_{1-\alpha}
is a distribution-free confidence set for
k_{\star}
.

This is a distribution-free result, meaning that the finite-sample validity of
\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{CROC}}_{1-\alpha}
holds for any distribution
\mathcal{P}
satisfying
1
. Further, this validity holds for any CPP score function
S
, and thus it offers great flexibility in the choice of CPP score for
CROC
; in particular, one may learn the score from the observed data
\mathbf{X}
itself.

Note that the
p
-values defined in (
2.2
) require evaluation of
S(\cdot,\mathbf{t})
for all permutations
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathbf{t}}
. However, as the sample size in each stream,
n
, increases, even for a moderate number of streams,
K
, the size of
\Pi_{\mathbf{t}}
becomes large, and computing the
p
-value becomes expensive. A natural remedy is to use a Monte Carlo approximation, i.e., sample
\pi^{(1)},\ldots,\pi^{(M)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\text{Unif}(\Pi_{\mathbf{t}})
, and then calculate:

\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t}}:=\frac{1+\sum_{m=1}^{M}{\mathbbm{1}}\left\{{S(\pi^{(m)}(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{t})\leq S(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})}\right\}}{1+M}.

(3.1)

We then define
\tilde{p}_{(k)}:=\max_{\mathbf{t}\in I_{k}}\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t}}
. Importantly, for any permutation in
\Pi_{\mathbf{t}}
, under the null
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\mathbf{t}}
, the joint distribution of
\mathbf{X}
is preserved. Hence, even with this randomized construction,
\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t}}
is a valid
p
-value under
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\mathbf{t}}
. Consequently, the randomized confidence set
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}^{\mathrm{CROC}}_{1-\alpha}:=\{k\in[K]:\tilde{p}_{(k)}>\alpha\}
is a valid confidence set for
k_{\star}
. We record this in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For each
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{R}
,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\mathbf{t}}}(\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t}}\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha
. Thus,
\mathbb{P}_{k}(\tilde{p}_{(k)}\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha
, and
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}^{\mathrm{CROC}}_{1-\alpha}
is a distribution-free confidence set for
k_{\star}
.

4
Universality of the
CROC
algorithm

While, through the choice of CPP score
S
, the
CROC
framework captures a broad class of methods for constructing distribution-free confidence sets for
k_{\star}
, a natural question is whether any alternative distribution-free procedures for root-cause analysis may exist. In this section, we give a definitive answer: any distribution-free procedure for root-cause analysis must be an instance of the
CROC
framework with an appropriate choice of CPP score.

Theorem 4.1

(Universality of
CROC
)
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Let
C
be any procedure that maps
\mathbf{X}\in\prod_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{X}_{k}^{n}
to a set
C(\mathbf{X})\subseteq\mathcal{R}
such that
\mathbb{P}_{k}(k\in C(\mathbf{X}))\geq 1-\alpha
. Then
C
coincides exactly with the set
\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{CROC}}_{1-\alpha}
constructed using the CPP score
S(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})=\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\argmin_{k\in[K]}t_{k}\in C(\mathbf{x})\bigr\}
.

Therefore,
CROC
captures the entire class of distribution-free root-cause analysis methods. Beyond its conceptual significance, it naturally provides a principled way to calibrate any existing root-cause localization method that may be only valid asymptotically or under stringent model assumptions. As mentioned before, a common approach is to first perform changepoint localization in each data stream, and then develop a method for root-cause analysis. Such approaches, often built under parametric model assumptions, are very sensitive to model misspecification. Therefore, we may wrap such existing methods within the
CROC
framework via a CPP score designed from these localization methods, and restore validity. If the original procedure
C
is already distribution-free valid, this calibration step leaves it unchanged and preserves its structural properties; otherwise, it refines
C
into a distribution-free valid confidence set.

5
Role of CPP score

While validity holds for any choice of CPP score, an equally important consideration in practice is the sharpness of the resulting
CROC
confidence sets. In particular, appropriately tailored score functions can lead to substantially narrower confidence sets. To start with, the following proposition sets up some basic guidelines regarding the choice od
S
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

(i)

(Symmetry yields power loss).
Fix
t\in\mathcal{R}
. If
S
satisfies
S(\cdot,\mathbf{t})=S(\pi(\cdot),\mathbf{t})
for all
\pi\in\Pi_{t}
, then the
p
-value
p_{t}
in (
2.2
) equals
1
, and thus,
\mathbb{P}(\argmin_{k\in[K]}t_{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{CROC}}_{1-\alpha})=1
.

(ii)

(Conformal data-processing inequality).
Let
C_{1}
be the
CROC
set based on score
S
. For any non-decreasing
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, let
C_{2}
be the corresponding set based on
f(S)
. Then
C_{1}\subseteq C_{2}
.

To put in words, the first part suggests that using the observed data
\mathbf{X}
in symmetric way yields trivial
p
-values and hence leads to large
CROC
confidence sets. Such scores should be avoided in practice. Second part is a monotonicity property of
CROC
: applying any non-decreasing transformation to the CPP score can only enlarge the resulting set, and any
strictly
increasing transformation leaves it unchanged.

5.1
Optimal CPP score

In order to characterize an expression for the optimal choice of score, we consider a canonical setting, namely the i.i.d. changepoint model. Suppose
\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{0,\xi_{k}}=(\mathcal{P}_{0,k})^{\xi_{k}}
and
\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{1,\xi_{k}}=(\mathcal{P}_{1,k})^{n-\xi_{k}}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{0,k}
and
\mathcal{P}_{1,k}
admit densities
f_{0,k}
and
f_{1,k}
with respect to a common dominating measure
\mu
. That is, within each stream
k
, pre-change observations are i.i.d. from
f_{0,k}
and post-change observations are i.i.d. from
f_{1,k}
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{IID}}
denote the class of distributions consistent with this model and satisfying
1
.

Now, we observe that the task of building a narrow
CROC
confidence set is tied to the task of constructing tests that have greater power against the hypothesis
\{\mathcal{H}_{0,k}\}_{k\neq k_{\star}}
, or equivalently constructing tests that have greater power against the hypothesis
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\mathbf{t}}
for any
\mathbf{t}\neq\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
Hence, the optimal choice of CPP score can be viewed through the lens of testing the null
\mathcal{H}_{0,\boldsymbol{\xi}}
against alternative
\mathcal{H}_{0,\mathbf{t}}
, with the goal of maximizing power. That said, within the framework of
CROC
, we are still limited to this testing problem only via conformal
p
-values. However, a non-trivial formulation of the testing problem conditional on suitable multisets, and by applying the classical Neyman–Pearson Lemma, we get the following result.

Theorem 5.2
.

Any strictly increasing transformation of the CPP score
S^{\mathrm{OPT}}
defined as

S^{\mathrm{OPT}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\left(\frac{\prod_{i\leq t_{k}}f_{0,k}(x_{i,k})\prod_{i>t_{k}}f_{1,k}(x_{i,k})}{\prod_{i\leq\xi_{k}}f_{0,k}(x_{i,k})\prod_{i>\xi_{k}}f_{1,k}(x_{i,k})}\right).

(5.1)

gives the optimal
minimizes the expected size of the
CROC
confidence set. In particular, for any
k\in[K]
and any CPP score
S:(\prod_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{X}_{k}^{n})\times\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, it holds that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}_{0,k}\,\cap\,\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{IID}}}\!\big[\,|\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{CROC}}_{1-\alpha}(S)|\,\big]\;\geq\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}_{0,k}\,\cap\,\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{IID}}}\!\big[\,|\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{CROC}}_{1-\alpha}(S^{\mathrm{OPT}})|\,\big]
.

5.2
Practical CPP scores

The optimal score
S^{\mathrm{OPT}}
depends on the unknown quantities
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\{f_{0,k},f_{1,k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, and is therefore not directly implementable. In practice, we construct a data-driven approximation. Specifically, we first obtain estimates
\{\hat{f}_{0,k},\hat{f}_{1,k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, and then replace
\boldsymbol{\xi}
by a maximum likelihood estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=(\hat{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\xi}_{K})
, where

\hat{\xi}_{k}:=\arg\max_{t\in[n]}\prod_{i\leq t}\hat{f}_{0,k}(x_{i,k})\prod_{i>t}\hat{f}_{1,k}(x_{i,k}).

(5.2)

We then define the CPP score, referred to as the
learned CPP score
,

S(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left(\frac{\prod_{i\leq t_{k}}\hat{f}_{0,k}(x_{i,k})\prod_{i>t_{k}}\hat{f}_{1,k}(x_{i,k})}{\prod_{i\leq\hat{\xi}_{k}}\hat{f}_{0,k}(x_{i,k})\prod_{i>\hat{\xi}_{k}}\hat{f}_{1,k}(x_{i,k})}\right).

(5.3)

In some settings, the true pre- and post-change densities
\{f_{0,k},f_{1,k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
may be known. In this case, we take
\hat{f}_{0,k}=f_{0,k}
and
\hat{f}_{1,k}=f_{1,k}
for each
k\in[K]
, and refer to the corresponding version of (
5.3
) as the
oracle CPP score
.

More generally, the densities can be learned from
\mathbf{X}
independently within each data stream. For instance, for the
k
-th stream, we may treat the observations
\{X_{1,k},\ldots,X_{n,k}\}
as an unordered collection, apply an unsupervised clustering method to partition the data into two groups, and then use these groups to estimate the pre- and post-change densities separately.

In particular, in high-dimensional settings where direct density estimation is challenging, one may instead train a classifier to distinguish between pre- and post-change samples, and use the resulting logits as a proxy for the log-likelihood ratio. These logits can then be plugged directly into (
5.3
), yielding a computationally efficient and scalable CPP score. This approach is especially useful in settings such as high-dimensional text or image data.

5.3
Asymptotic sharpness of
CROC
confidence sets

We now show that, under mild conditions, the
CROC
confidence sets concentrate around the true root-cause index as the sample size grows.

Consider a sequence of root-cause analysis problems with
K_{n}\to\infty
and
K_{n}/n\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. For each
n
, we observe
\mathbf{X}=\bigl((X_{i,j,n})\bigr)_{i\in[n],\,j\in[K_{n}]}
, where stream
j
has a changepoint at
\xi_{j,n}\in[n-1]
and follows an i.i.d. changepoint model with pre- and post-change distributions
P_{0,j}
and
P_{1,j}
admitting densities
f_{0,j},f_{1,j}
. Let
(p_{(k),n})_{k\in[K]}
denote the resulting
CROC

p
-values and
\mathcal{K}^{{\textsc{CROC}}}_{n,1-\alpha}
the corresponding confidence set.

We analyze the oracle log-likelihood ratio (LLR) score
\ell_{j}(x)=\log\!\bigl(f_{0,j}(x)/f_{1,j}(x)\bigr)
, and impose a set of mild and interpretable conditions. First, we assume non-degenerate changepoints, in the sense that they lie away from the boundaries, i.e.,
\min_{j}\min(\xi_{j,n},\,n-\xi_{j,n})/n\geq\tau
for some
\tau\in(0,1/2)
. Second, we assume that in each stream, the pre- and post-change distributions are distinguishable,
\mathrm{KL}(P_{0,j}\|P_{1,j}),\,\mathrm{KL}(P_{1,j}\|P_{0,j})>0
, and the LLRs have uniformly bounded variance,
\sup_{j}\max\{\mathrm{Var}_{P_{0,j}}(\ell_{j}),\,\mathrm{Var}_{P_{1,j}}(\ell_{j})\}<\infty
. Finally, we require a separation condition ensuring that the root changepoint appears sufficiently earlier than the others, namely
\min_{j\neq k_{\star}}(\xi_{n,j}-\xi_{n,k_{\star}})/(K_{n}\log K_{n})\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Under the above conditions,
\max_{k\neq k_{\star}}p_{(k),n}\xrightarrow{P}0
.
Consequently, we have that

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathcal{K}^{{\textsc{CROC}}}_{n,1-\alpha}=\{k_{\star}\}\bigr)\geq 1-\alpha-\mathrm{o}(1).

The proof is deferred to Appendix
C.6
. This result shows that, under mild and interpretable conditions, the oracle LLR score yields asymptotically sharp localization of the root-cause index.

8
Discussion

We proposed
CROC
, a conformal framework that constructs finite-sample valid confidence sets for the root-cause index in a multi-stream setting under minimal exchangeability assumptions. Beyond validity, we established a universality property, showing that any distribution-free procedure can be represented within this framework via an appropriate choice of score function. We also characterized optimal score design under a canonical model and showed that practical approximations yield asymptotically sharp localization. Empirical results demonstrate that
CROC
performs effectively across a range of settings, including challenging regimes where the change in the root stream is difficult to detect. While we showed that our approach extends to dependent streams, improving efficiency under structured dependence and extending the framework to handle within-stream dependence remain important directions for future work.

C.4.2
Proving optimal form of CPP score

We recall that the

Since

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\mathbf{t}}
against
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\boldsymbol{\xi}}
conditional on the relevant multisets. Specifically, fix any
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{R}

\mathcal{X}_{L,\mathbf{t},j}:=\{X_{1,j},\ldots,X_{t_{j},j}\},\quad\mathcal{X}_{R,\mathbf{t},j}:=\{X_{t_{j}+1,j},\ldots,X_{n,j}\},

as the (unordered) left and right multisets for the
j

\mathbf{r}\in\mathcal{R}
, let
\mathcal{P}^{(\mathbf{r})}
denote the conditional distribution of
\mathbf{X}
given
(\mathcal{X}_{L,\mathbf{t},1},\mathcal{X}_{R,\mathbf{t},1},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{L,\mathbf{t},K},\mathcal{X}_{R,\mathbf{t},K})

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\mathbf{r}}

Restricting attention to conformal
p

CROC

S:(\prod_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{X}_{k}^{n})\times\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{R}

denote the permutation set, the randomized conformal

p_{\mathbf{t}}(s)=\frac{1}{|\Pi_{\mathbf{t}}|}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathbf{t}}}{\mathbbm{1}}\left\{{s(\pi(\mathbf{X}))<s(\mathbf{X})}\right\}\ +\ U\cdot\frac{1}{|\Pi_{\mathbf{t}}|}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathbf{t}}}{\mathbbm{1}}\left\{{s(\pi(\mathbf{X}))=s(\mathbf{X})}\right\},

The analysis in Section

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0}:\mathbf{X}\mid(\mathcal{X}_{L,\mathbf{t}_{1},1},\mathcal{X}_{R,\mathbf{t}_{1},1},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{L,\mathbf{t}_{1},K},\mathcal{X}_{R,\mathbf{t}_{1},K})\sim\mathcal{P}^{(\mathbf{t}_{1})}\quad\text{vs.}

\phi(\mathbf{X})

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}

\phi(\mathbf{X})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }{s^{\star}(\mathbf{X})}^{-1}>\tau_{\alpha},\\[4.0pt]

for some threshold
\tau_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
against
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\prime}

From Section

Let

C.5

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\,\text{there exists $k\in[K]$ such that}\,\,\hat{\xi}_{k,n}\leq\xi_{k,n}-M)\leq K_{n},C^{\prime}_{0}\,{C^{\prime}_{1}}^{M},

Together, this yields
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}\|_{\infty}=\mathrm{O}_{P}(\log K_{n})
, as required.
∎

C.6
Proving asymptotic sharpness of
CROC

Without loss of generality, we may assume
k_{\star}=1
. Recalling that for each
k\in[K_{n}]
, the
CROC

p
-values are defined as
p_{(k),n}=\max_{\mathbf{t}\in I_{k,n}}p_{\mathbf{t},n}
, it suffices to show that

\max_{k\neq 1}\max_{\mathbf{t}\in I_{k,n}}p_{\mathbf{t},n}\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}0.

Throughout this proof, we write

\Delta_{n}=\min_{j\in[K_{n}]:j\neq 1}(\xi_{j,n}-\xi_{1,n}),

which denotes the separation between the changepoint of the root-cause stream and that of the remaining streams.

The proof from here is split into four key steps.

Step 1: Reduction to a high probability event, and frozen-estimator p-values.

First, we note that by Lemma
C.3
, there exists
C>0
such that, writing
r_{n}:=C\log(K_{n})
and defining
\mathcal{E}_{n}:=\{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq r_{n}\}
, we have
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. Moreover, on the event
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, for every
k\neq 1
,

\hat{\xi}_{k,n}-\hat{\xi}_{1,n}\geq\xi_{k,n}-\xi_{1,n}-2r_{n}\geq\Delta_{n}-2r_{n}.

Now fix any
k\neq 1
and any
\mathbf{t}\in I_{k,n}
. Since by definition,
t_{k}<t_{1}
, by triangle inequality,

|t_{1}-\hat{\xi}_{1,n}|+|t_{k}-\hat{\xi}_{k,n}|\geq|(\hat{\xi}_{k,n}-\hat{\xi}_{1,n})+(t_{1}-t_{k})|\geq\hat{\xi}_{k,n}-\hat{\xi}_{1,n}.

Thus, it follows that

|t_{1}-\hat{\xi}_{1,n}|+|t_{k}-\hat{\xi}_{k,n}|\geq\Delta_{n}-2r_{n}.

Therefore, writing
M(\mathbf{t}):=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}}|t_{j}-\hat{\xi}_{j,n}|
,
we have, uniformly over all wrong-root configurations,

\inf_{k\neq 1}\inf_{\mathbf{t}\in I_{k,n}}M(\mathbf{t})\geq\Delta_{n}-2r_{n}.

Since by the theorem statement,
\Delta_{n}/r_{n}\to\infty
, on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, for all sufficiently large
n
,
M(\mathbf{t})\geq\frac{\Delta_{n}}{2}
.

Let
\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t},n}
denote the frozen-estimator p-value, defined in (
C.6
). By Lemma
C.6
, deterministically,
p_{\mathbf{t},n}\leq\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t},n}
for all
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{R}
.
Therefore, it suffices to prove that

\sup_{k\neq 1}\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in I_{k,n}}\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t},n}\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}0.

Step 2: Deterministic upper bound on frozen-estimator
p
-values.

First, note that
\mathrm{Var}_{X\sim P}(\ell_{j}(X))\leq\sigma_{\star}^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}(\ell_{j}(X))\leq J_{\max}
for both
P=P_{0,j}
and
P=P_{1,j}
. Therefore, it follows that

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{0,j}}\ell_{j}^{2}(X),\quad\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{1,j}}\ell_{j}^{2}(X)\leq\sigma_{\star}^{2}+J_{\max}^{2}.

Furthermore, for any nonnegative weights
(a_{1},\dots,a_{K_{n}})
with
\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}}a_{j}=1
,

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}}a_{j}\hat{v}_{n,j}\Big]\leq\sigma_{\star}^{2}+J_{\max}^{2}.

Fix
\eta\in(0,1)
.
Applying Markov’s inequality yields that, with probability at least
1-\eta
,

\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}}a_{j}\hat{v}_{n,j}\leq\frac{\sigma_{\star}^{2}+J_{\max}^{2}}{\eta}.

Choosing
a_{j}=\frac{d_{j}(\mathbf{t})}{M(\mathbf{t})}
for
j\in[K_{n}]
, we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}}d_{j}(\mathbf{t})\hat{v}_{n,j}\leq\frac{\sigma_{\star}^{2}+J_{\max}^{2}}{\eta}M(\mathbf{t}).

Since the changepoints
(\xi_{n,j})
lie in the interior, we also have for each
j\in[K_{n}]
,

\frac{n}{\min(\hat{\xi}_{n,j},n-\hat{\xi}_{n,j})}\leq\frac{1}{\tau}.

Hence, by Lemma
C.5
, we conclude that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{k\neq 1}\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in I_{k,n}}\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t},n}\leq\sup_{k\neq 1}\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in I_{k,n}}\left\{\frac{\left(\frac{\sigma_{\star}^{2}+J_{\max}^{2}}{\eta\tau}\right)M(\mathbf{t})}{\{S^{(n)}(X,\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})\}^{2}}+\mathbf{1}\{S^{(n)}(X,\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})\geq 0\}\right\}\right)\geq 1-\eta.

(C.7)

Next, we prove a uniform negative drift bound for
S^{(n)}(X,\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})
over all wrong-root configurations.

Step 3: Uniform negative drift of
S^{(n)}(X,\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})
.

We now establish a uniform negative drift for
S^{(n)}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})
. In particular, we show that there exists
c_{0}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\{\sup_{k\neq 1}\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in I_{k,n}}\frac{S^{(n)}(X,\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})}{M(\mathbf{t})}\leq-c_{0}\right\}\cap\mathcal{E}_{n}\right)\geq 1-\eta+\mathrm{o}(1).

(C.8)

For each
j\in[K_{n}]
, we define
d_{j}(\mathbf{t}):=|t_{j}-\hat{\xi}_{j,n}|
and note that

S^{(n)}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}}D_{j}(t_{j},\hat{\xi}_{j,n}),

where we let

D_{j}(t_{j},\hat{\xi}_{j,n}):=\begin{cases}\sum_{i=\hat{\xi}_{j,n}+1}^{t_{j}}\ell_{j}(X_{i,j,n})-\frac{d_{j}(\mathbf{t})}{t_{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{t_{j}}\ell_{j}(X_{i,j,n}),&t_{j}>\hat{\xi}_{j,n},\\[8.61108pt]
\sum_{i=t_{j}+1}^{\hat{\xi}_{j,n}}(\ell_{j}(X_{i,j,n}))+\frac{d_{j}(\mathbf{t})}{n-t_{j}}\sum_{i=t_{j}+1}^{n}\ell_{j}(X_{i,j,n}),&t_{j}<\hat{\xi}_{j,n},\\[8.61108pt]
0,&t_{j}=\hat{\xi}_{j,n}.\end{cases}

Now, for each
j\in[K_{n}]
, we derive a upper bound on each
D_{j}(t_{j},\hat{\xi}_{j,n})
that holds uniformly over all choices of
t_{j}\in[n-1]
. Consider first the case
t_{j}>\hat{\xi}_{j,n}
. On the event
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, a direct decomposition gives
D_{j}(t_{j},\hat{\xi}_{j,n})\leq T_{1j}+T_{2j}+T_{3j}
,
where we define

\displaystyle T_{1j}:=-\frac{d_{j}(\mathbf{t})}{t_{j}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\xi_{j,n}-r_{n}}\ell_{j}(X_{i,j,n}),\qquad T_{2j}:=\sum_{|i-\xi_{j,n}|\leq r_{n}}|\ell_{j}(X_{i,j,n})|,

\displaystyle T_{3j}:=\frac{\hat{\xi}_{j,n}}{t_{j}}\sum_{i=\xi_{j,n}+r_{n}+1}^{t_{j}}\ell_{j}(X_{i,j,n}).

Here
T_{3j}
is interpreted as zero if
\xi_{j,n}+r_{n}+1>t_{j}
. We control each of these terms one by one. First, since

X_{1,j,n},\ldots,X_{\xi_{j,n}-r_{n},j,n}
are i.i.d. from
P_{0,j}
,
Lemma
C.4
gives that, for each
j
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{1}{\xi_{n,j}-r_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\xi_{n,j}-r_{n}}\ell_{j}(X_{i,j})\geq cJ_{\min}\right)\geq 1-e^{-\gamma(\xi_{n,j}-r_{n})}.

We now use the interiority condition to lower bound the ratio
(\xi_{j,n}-r_{n})/t_{j}
. Since
t_{j}\leq n
and
\xi_{j,n}\geq\tau n
for all
j\in[K_{n}]
,

\frac{\xi_{j,n}-r_{n}}{t_{j}}\geq\frac{\xi_{j,n}-r_{n}}{n}\geq\tau-\frac{r_{n}}{n}.

As
r_{n}=\mathrm{O}(\log K_{n})=\mathrm{o}(n)
, for all sufficiently large
n
, uniformly over
j
,
\frac{\xi_{j,n}-r_{n}}{t_{j}}\geq\frac{\tau}{2}
.
Consequently, setting
c_{1}:=\frac{c\tau J_{\min}}{2}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(T_{1j}\leq-c_{1}d_{j}(\mathbf{t})~~\textnormal{for all}~~t_{j}\in[n-1]\right)\geq 1-e^{-\gamma(\xi_{j,n}-r_{n})}

Next, we control
T_{3j}
uniformly over
t_{j}
. Since, in the particular case of consideration,
\hat{\xi}_{j,n}/t_{j}\leq 1
, we further have

T_{3j}\leq\sup_{s\geq\xi_{j,n}+r_{n}+1}\sum_{i=\xi_{j,n}+r_{n}+1}^{s}\ell_{j}(X_{i,j,n}).

For
s>\xi_{j,n}+r_{n}
, we observe that
X_{\xi_{j,n}+r_{n}+1,j,n},\ldots,X_{s,j,n}
are drawn i.i.d. from the distribution
P_{1,j}
.
By Lemma
C.4
, we obtain that there exists
c^{\prime}>0
and
\gamma^{\prime}>0

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{s\geq\xi_{j,n}+r_{n}+1}\sum_{i=\xi_{j,n}+r_{n}+1}^{s}\ell_{j}(X_{i,j,n})>c^{\prime}r_{n}\right)\leq e^{-\gamma^{\prime}r_{n}}.

A union bound over
j\in[K_{n}]
gives

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{j\in[K_{n}]}\sup_{s\geq\xi_{j,n}+r_{n}+1}\sum_{i=\xi_{j,n}+r_{n}+1}^{s}\ell_{j}(X_{i,j,n})>c^{\prime}r_{n}\right)\leq K_{n}e^{-\gamma^{\prime}r_{n}}.

If instead,
t_{j}<\hat{\xi}_{j,n}
we follow an analogous argument to bound
D_{j}(t_{j},\hat{\xi}_{j,n})
. Specifically, on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, we write
D_{j}(t_{j},\hat{\xi}_{j,n})\leq T^{\prime}_{1j}+T_{2j}+T^{\prime}_{3j}
,
where
T_{2j}
is unchanged and

T^{\prime}_{1j}:=\frac{d_{j}(\mathbf{t})}{n-t_{j}}\sum_{i=\xi_{j,n}+r_{n}+1}^{n}\ell_{j}(X_{i,j,n}),\qquad T^{\prime}_{3j}:=-\frac{n-\hat{\xi}_{j,n}}{n-t_{j}}\sum_{i=t_{j}+1}^{\xi_{j,n}-r_{n}}\ell_{j}(X_{i,j,n}).

We can follow the argument from above and derive similar upper bounds on
T^{\prime}_{1j}
uniformly for all
t_{j}\in[n-1]
, and on
T^{\prime}_{3j}
uniformly for all
t_{j}\in[n-1]
and
j\in[K_{n}]
. Combiningn both cases, we get the following: define the event

\mathcal{A}_{n}:=\left\{D_{j}(t_{j},\hat{\xi}_{j,n})\leq-c_{1}d_{j}(\mathbf{t})+T_{2j}+c^{\prime}r_{n}\ \text{for all }j\in[K_{n}]\text{ and all }t_{j}\in[n-1]\right\}\cap\mathcal{E}_{n}.

The preceding bounds imply

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{n}^{c})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}}\left\{e^{-\gamma(\xi_{j,n}-r_{n})}+e^{-\gamma(n-\xi_{j,n}-r_{n})}\right\}+2K_{n}e^{-\gamma_{3}r_{n}}.

By the interiority condition, we have

\xi_{j,n}\wedge(n-\xi_{j,n})\geq\frac{\tau n}{2}\qquad\text{for all }j\in[K_{n}].

Therefore,

\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}}\left\{e^{-\gamma(\xi_{j,n}-r_{n})}+e^{-\gamma(n-\xi_{j,n}-r_{n})}\right\}\leq 2K_{n}e^{-\gamma(\tau n/2-r_{n})}=o(1).

Also, since
r_{n}=C\log K_{n}
, choosing
C
large enough,
2K_{n}e^{-\gamma^{\prime}r_{n}}=o(1)
. Thus,
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{n})\to 1
.
On
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, summing over
j\in[K_{n}]
gives, uniformly over
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{R}
,

S^{(n)}(X,\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})\leq-c_{1}M(\mathbf{t})+\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}}T_{2j}+c^{\prime}K_{n}r_{n}.

It remains to control the middle term. Define

\mathcal{B}_{n}(\eta):=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}}T_{2j}\leq\frac{2K_{n}r_{n}(\sigma_{\star}^{2}+J_{\max}^{2})^{1/2}}{\eta}\right\}.

By Markov’s inequality and Cauchy–Schwarz,
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{B}_{n}(\eta))\geq 1-\eta.

Consequently, on
\mathcal{A}_{n}\cap\mathcal{B}_{n}(\eta)
, uniformly over
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{R}
,

S^{(n)}(X,\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})\leq-c_{1}M(\mathbf{t})+C_{\eta}K_{n}r_{n},

where we let

C_{\eta}:=c^{\prime}+\frac{2(\sigma_{\star}^{2}+J_{\max}^{2})^{1/2}}{\eta}.

Finally, in Step 1, we proved that on the event
\mathcal{E}_{n}
,

\inf_{k\neq 1}\inf_{\mathbf{t}\in I_{k,n}}M(\mathbf{t})\geq\Delta_{n}-2r_{n}.

Therefore, on
\mathcal{A}_{n}\cap\mathcal{B}_{n}(\eta)
,

\sup_{k\neq 1}\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in I_{k,n}}\frac{S^{(n)}(X,\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})}{M(\mathbf{t})}\leq-c_{1}+\frac{C_{\eta}K_{n}r_{n}}{\Delta_{n}-2r_{n}}.

Since
r_{n}=C\log K_{n}
and
\Delta_{n}\gg K_{n}\log K_{n}
, the second term is
o(1)
. Hence, for all sufficiently large
n
, on
\mathcal{A}_{n}\cap\mathcal{B}_{n}(\eta)
,

\sup_{k\neq 1}\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in I_{k,n}}\frac{S^{(n)}(X,\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})}{M(\mathbf{t})}\leq-\frac{c_{1}}{2}.

Therefore, (
C.8
) holds with
c_{0}=c_{1}/2
.

Step 4: Completing the proof.

By the conclusion of Step 2, and Step 3, that is (
C.7
) and (
C.8
), we have that except on an event with probability at most
1-2\eta
, for every

\mathbf{t}\in\bigcup_{k\neq 1}I_{k}
,

\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t},n}\leq\frac{\left(\frac{\sigma_{\star}^{2}+J_{\max}^{2}}{\eta\tau}\right)M(\mathbf{t})}{c_{0}^{2}M(\mathbf{t})^{2}}=\frac{\sigma_{\star}^{2}+J_{\max}^{2}}{\eta\tau c_{0}^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{M(\mathbf{t})},

and also that for all sufficiently large
n
,

M(\mathbf{t})\geq\frac{\Delta_{n}}{2}.

Hence, on this event,

\sup_{k\neq 1}\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in I_{k}}\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t},n}\leq\frac{2(\sigma_{\star}^{2}+J_{\max}^{2})}{\eta\tau c_{0}^{2}\Delta_{n}}\to 0

Since
\eta>0
is arbitrary to start with,

\max_{k\neq 1}\max_{\mathbf{t}\in I_{k}}\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t},n}\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}0.

This proves the first part. Finally, for any fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
, note that

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists k\neq 1:k\in\mathcal{K}^{{\textsc{CROC}}}_{n,1-\alpha}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{k\neq 1}p_{(k),n}>\alpha\right)\to 0.

Thus,
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{K}^{{\textsc{CROC}}}_{n,1-\alpha}\subseteq\{1\}\right)\to 1
.
By Theorem
3.1
, we further have

\mathbb{P}\left(1\in\mathcal{K}^{{\textsc{CROC}}}_{n,1-\alpha}\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{K}^{{\textsc{CROC}}}_{n,1-\alpha}=\{1\}\right)\geq 1-\alpha-o(1).

This proves the second part.

C.6.1
Auxiliary lemmas

Lemma C.4
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}P_{1}
and

Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}P_{0}
, such that
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
admit densities
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
w.r.t. a common dominating measure. We write
\ell(x)=\log(f_{0}(x)/f_{1}(x))
and further, assume that

0<\mathrm{KL}(P_{0}\|P_{1}),\ \mathrm{KL}(P_{1}\|P_{0})<\infty
.
Then there exist constants
c_{0}>0
and
\gamma_{0}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\biggl(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ell(X_{i})\geq-c_{0}J(P_{0},P_{1})\biggr)\leq e^{-\gamma_{0}k},\qquad\mathbb{P}\biggl(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ell(Y_{i})\leq c_{0}J(P_{0},P_{1})\biggr)\leq e^{-\gamma_{0}k}.

Moreover, there exist constants
c_{1},\gamma_{1}>0
such that, for every
x>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{m\geq 1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell(X_{i})>x\right)\leq c_{1}e^{-\gamma_{1}x},\qquad\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{m\geq 1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}-\ell(Y_{i})>x\right)\leq c_{1}e^{-\gamma_{1}x}.

Proof.

For a proof of the first part, we refer the reader to Lemma
B.4
in
10
.
For the second part, observe that for any
\theta\in(0,1)
, by Hölder’s inequality,

M(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{1}}[e^{\theta\ell(X)}]=\int f_{0}^{\theta}(x)f_{1}^{1-\theta}(x)\,\mathsf{d}x\leq\left(\int f_{0}(x)\,\mathsf{d}x\right)^{\theta}\left(\int f_{1}(x)\,\mathsf{d}x\right)^{1-\theta}\leq 1.

Moreover,
M(0)=1
and

\lim_{\theta\to 0^{+}}M^{\prime}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{1}}[\ell(X)]=-\mathrm{KL}(P_{1}\|P_{0})<0.

Hence there exists
\theta_{\star}\in(0,1)
such that

\rho:=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{1}}[e^{\theta_{\star}\ell(X)}]=M(\theta_{\star})<1.

Next, note that for any
x>0
, by a union bound and Chernoff’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{m\geq 1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell(X_{i})>x\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell(X_{i})>x\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}e^{-\theta_{\star}x}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\theta_{\star}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell(X_{i})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=e^{-\theta_{\star}x}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\rho^{m}=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}e^{-\theta_{\star}x}.

Thus the first maximal bound holds with

c_{1}=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho},\qquad\gamma_{1}=\theta_{\star}.

The second maximal bound follows by a similar argument.

∎

Lemma C.5
.

For any
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{R}
, we have deterministically

\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t},n}\leq\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\frac{n}{\min(\hat{\xi}_{k,n},n-\hat{\xi}_{k,n})}\cdot|t_{k}-\hat{\xi}_{k,n}|\cdot\hat{v}_{n,k}\right)}{(S^{(n)}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n}))^{2}}+{\mathbbm{1}}_{{S^{(n)}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n})\geq 0}}.

(C.9)

Proof.

Fix
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{R}
and write
d_{k}(\mathbf{t})=|t_{k}-\hat{\xi}_{k,n}|
We start by writing

S^{(n)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\rm sgn}(t_{k}-\hat{\xi}_{k,n})\cdot\biggl\{\sum_{i=t_{k}\wedge\hat{\xi}_{k,n}(\mathbf{x})+1}^{t_{k}\vee\hat{\xi}_{k,n}}\ell_{k}(x_{i,k})\biggr\},

i.e., the inner sum is a sum of
\ell_{k}
computed over a block of
d_{k}(\mathbf{t})
observations. Thereby, the permutation
p
-value (
C.6
) can be equivalently expressed as

\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t},n}:=\mathbb{P}_{\pi\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Pi_{\mathbf{t}})}\!\left(S^{(n)}(\pi(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{t})\leq S^{(n)}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})\ \middle|\ \mathbf{X}\right).

Let for each
k\in[K]
,

\mathcal{X}_{k}=\begin{cases}\{X_{1,k,n},\ldots,X_{t_{k},k,n}\}&\text{if}~~t_{k}>\hat{\xi}_{k,n},\\
\{X_{t_{k}+1,k,n},\ldots,X_{n,k,n}\}&\text{if}~~t_{k}<\hat{\xi}_{k,n},\end{cases}.

and suppose for each
k\in[K]
,
\tilde{X}_{1,k,n},\ldots,\tilde{X}_{d_{k}(\mathbf{t}),k,n}
are drawn WOR from
\mathcal{X}_{k}
. Therefore, we can equivalently express
\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t},n}
as

\tilde{p}_{k,n}=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}(\mathbf{t})}\ell_{k}(\tilde{X}_{j,k,n})\leq S^{(n)}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})\ \middle|\ \mathbf{X}\right).

Furthermore, observe that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}(\mathbf{t})}\ell_{k}(\tilde{X}_{j,k,n})\ \middle|\ \mathbf{X}\right]=\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})

and that

\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}(\mathbf{t})}\ell_{k}(\tilde{X}_{j,k,n})\ \middle|\ \mathbf{X}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}(\mathbf{t})}\ell_{k}(\tilde{X}_{j,k,n})\ \middle|\ \mathbf{X}\right).

Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}(\mathbf{t})}\ell_{k}(\tilde{X}_{j,k,n})\ \middle|\ \mathbf{X}\right)

\displaystyle\leq d_{k}(\mathbf{t})\cdot\mathrm{Var}(\ell_{k}(\tilde{X}_{j,k,n})\ |\ \mathbf{X})

\displaystyle\leq d_{k}(\mathbf{t})\cdot\begin{cases}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{t_{k}}\ell^{2}_{k}(X_{i,k,n})&\text{if }t_{k}>\hat{\xi}_{k,n}\\
\frac{1}{n-t_{k}}\sum_{i=t_{k}+1}^{n}\ell^{2}_{k}(X_{i,k,n})&\text{if }t_{k}<\hat{\xi}_{k,n}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq d_{k}(\mathbf{t})\cdot\frac{n}{\min(\hat{\xi}_{k,n},n-\hat{\xi}_{k,n})}\,\hat{v}_{n,k}

where recall
\hat{v}_{n,k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\ell_{k}}^{\,2}(X_{i,k,n})
.
Hence, writing

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}(\mathbf{t})}\ell_{k}(\tilde{X}_{j,k,n})\leq S^{(n)}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})\ \middle|\ \mathbf{X}\right)\\
\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}(\mathbf{t})}\ell_{k}(\tilde{X}_{j,k,n})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})\leq S^{(n)}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})\ \middle|\ \mathbf{X}\right).

Observe that whenever
S^{(n)}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})<0
, then by Chebyshev’s inequality,

\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t},n}\leq\,\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n}{\min(\hat{\xi}_{n,k},n-\hat{\xi}_{n,k})}\cdot d_{k}(\mathbf{t})\hat{v}_{n,k}}{(S^{(n)}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n}))^{2}}.

On the other hand, if
S^{(n)}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})-\Gamma(\mathbf{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n})\geq 0
, then
\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t},n}\leq 1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma C.6
.

Let
\{p_{\mathbf{t},n}\}
be the
CROC

p
-values computed based on the oracle CPP score. Then, it holds that deterministically,

p_{t,n}\ \leq\ \tilde{p}_{t,n},\qquad\text{for}~~t\in\mathcal{R}.

Proof.

We start with observing that the original score
S(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})
and the frozen score
S^{(n)}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})
can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle S(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t_{k}}\ell_{k}(x_{i,k,n})-\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{\xi}_{k,n}(\mathbf{x})}\ell_{k}(x_{i,k,n})\right),

\displaystyle S^{(n)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t_{k}}\ell_{k}(x_{i,k,n})-\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{\xi}_{k,n}}\ell_{k}(x_{i,k,n})\right),

where
(\hat{\xi}_{1,n},\ldots,\hat{\xi}_{K,n})=\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n}(\mathbf{X})
.
Recall that by (
5.2
), for any
\mathbf{x}\in\prod_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{X}_{k}^{n}
,

\hat{\xi}_{k,n}(\mathbf{x})\in\argmax_{s\in[n-1]}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\ell_{k}(x_{i,k,n}).

Hence, for each
k\in[K]
, and for any permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, we have that

\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{\xi}_{k,n}(\pi(\mathbf{x}))}\ell_{k}((\pi(\mathbf{x}))_{i,k,n})\ \geq\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{\xi}_{k,n}(\mathbf{x})}\ell_{k}((\pi(\mathbf{x}))_{i,k,n}).

Consequently, for any
t\in\mathcal{R}
and any permutation
\pi\in\Pi_{t}
,

S(\pi(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{t})\leq S^{(n)}(\pi(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{t}),\qquad S(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t})=S^{(n)}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t}).

Hence, for all
t\in\mathcal{R}
, deterministically,
p_{\mathbf{t},n}\leq\tilde{p}_{\mathbf{t},n}
as required.
∎
