Anesthesia for tracheoplasty in a patient with tracheal stenosis: a case report
Authors: Cuiwen Li, Shangting Zhou, Huiwen Wang, Yanli Zhu, Yuxiong Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTracheal stenosis is a serious complication often following prolonged endotracheal intubation. Surgical tracheoplasty is definitive treatment but poses significant airway management challenges during anesthesia, especially in patients with severe stenosis and comorbidities like coronary artery disease.Case presentationWe present a 28-year-old male patient scheduled for tracheoplasty due to severe upper tracheal stenosis (narrowest diameter ~ 4 mm) following cardiac arrest and prolonged intubation. He also had pre-existing coronary artery disease (moderate stenosis). Traditional airway management (endotracheal tube, laryngeal mask, tracheostomy) was deemed unsafe. Anesthesia was achieved using bilateral superficial cervical plexus blocks and bilateral vagus nerve blocks under ultrasound guidance, supplemented with dexmedetomidine sedation and minimal sufentanil analgesia. Spontaneous respiration was preserved throughout the procedure. Proximal tracheotomy allowed local oxygen insufflation during resection and anastomosis. The surgery was completed successfully without extracorporeal circulation support.ConclusionsFor patients undergoing tracheoplasty with critical stenosis and comorbidities, an awake, spontaneously breathing technique utilizing regional anesthesia blocks (superficial cervical plexus and vagus nerve) combined with targeted sedation/analgesia can provide a relatively safe and effective alternative to general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation or extracorporeal support. Introduction

Tracheal stenosis, often a consequence of prolonged endotracheal intubation, can lead to progressive dyspnea and life-threatening airway obstruction [1]. Surgical resection and reconstruction (tracheoplasty) offer a curative solution but present formidable anesthetic challenges, primarily concerning airway management and oxygenation during the critical phases of tracheal transection and anastomosis [2–4]. Conventional approaches include general anesthesia with various airway techniques (e.g., intubation across the stenosis, jet ventilation, laryngeal mask airway) or extracorporeal support (ECMO or cardiopulmonary bypass) [5]. However, these methods carry inherent risks, particularly in patients with critical stenosis and comorbidities such as coronary artery disease [6, 7]. We report a case of successful tracheoplasty in a patient with severe tracheal stenosis and coronary artery disease managed with an awake, spontaneously breathing regional anesthesia technique.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old male (height 170 cm, weight 71.5 kg) presented with a 6-month history of intermittent shortness of breath and wheezing, Significantly worsening over the previous day. His history included cardiac arrest 6 months prior requiring prolonged (8 days) intubation and ICU care, complicated by multi-organ failure. Organ function recovery was complete at the time of surgery, as confirmed by preoperative evaluations.Subsequent symptoms suggested post-intubation tracheal stenosis. Preoperative cervicothoracic CT with 3D reconstruction confirmed severe stenosis at the upper trachea, with a minimal luminal diameter of approximately 4 mm and an estimated stenosis of 67–78% over a 1.0 cm segment (Figs. 1 and 2). Preoperative tracheoscopy showed the stenosis was impassable with a 4.9 mm scope (Fig. 3). Coronary CT angiography ,routinely performed as part of the preanesthetic assessment for patients with cardiac history,revealed moderate stenosis in the left circumflex artery and a myocardial bridge in the left anterior descending artery. Preoperative metabolic equivalents (METS) were ≤ 4.This low METS value was likely attributable to the patient’s limited activity due to dyspnea from tracheal stenosis rather than solely cardiac dysfunction. Arterial blood gas analysis was normal.

Anesthetic management

Given the severity of stenosis (4 mm lumen), low location (below cricoid cartilage), length of the stenotic segment, and coexisting coronary artery disease, traditional airway management (endotracheal tube, laryngeal mask, or emergent tracheostomy) was considered unsafe due to the inability to secure the airway below the cords or the risk of precipitating cardiac ischemia. The patient exhibited severe anxiety due to dyspnea, significant insomnia, and even suicidal thoughts. During preoperative communication, the patient refused to undergo a conscious tracheotomy. A comprehensive plan was formulated:

Primary Plan: Ultrasound-guided bilateral superficial cervical plexus blocks and bilateral vagus nerve blocks, supplemented by dexmedetomidine sedation and minimal sufentanil analgesia, preserving spontaneous respiration. Following bilateral superficial cervical plexus block (10 ml of 0.375% ropivacaine in each side) + bilateral vagus nerve block (3 ml of 0.375% ropivacaine each). We thoroughly assessed the extent of the block, confirming normal bilateral chest wall movement and symmetrical breath sounds in both lungs. The patient reported no chest tightness, shortness of breath, or dyspnea. There was no hoarseness, weak voice, or aphonia. Vital signs were continuously monitored with no significant changes before or after the block. During surgery, dexmedetomidine was administered for sedation to alleviate anxiety and fear. The patient was regularly queried for discomfort, reporting no chest tightness, shortness of breath, or dyspnea throughout anesthesia and surgery. To mitigate risks associated with vagal nerve block, the following measures were implemented: 1) Strict adherence to preoperative fasting. 2) Employed low-concentration ropivacaine (0.375%) in minimal doses (3 mL per side) for the block to minimize impact on the recurrent laryngeal nerves while effectively suppressing the tracheal cough reflex. 3) Assessed the patient’s vocal ability post-blockade, confirming hoarseness as the anticipated outcome. 4) Vagus nerve block puncture site: Ultrasound-guided C6-C7 level, within the carotid sheath, with a relatively low puncture position.

Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring (ECG, SpO2, NIBP) was initiated. An arterial catheter was placed in the radial artery under local anesthesia. Depth of anesthesia was monitored using Narcotrend (A99). Preoxygenation was administered via facemask (5 L/min). Dexmedetomidine infusion was started (loading dose 0.7 µg/kg/min over 10 min, then maintenance 0.3 µg/kg/h). Using a high-frequency linear ultrasound probe at the C4 level, bilateral superficial cervical plexus blocks (10 ml of 0.375% ropivacaine per side) and bilateral vagus nerve blocks (3 ml of 0.375% ropivacaine per side) were performed using an in-plane technique. Sensory blockade was confirmed within 5 min (submandibular to subclavicular regions) [8, 9]. The patient remained cooperative and breathed comfortably.

Surgical procedure

The patient was positioned supine with neck extension (thyroid position). A 6 cm transverse incision was made at the level of the cricoid cartilage. Dissection proceeded through skin, subcutaneous tissue, and muscle to expose the thyroid isthmus, which was retracted superiorly to reveal the trachea. A longitudinal incision (approx. 4 cm) was made in the trachea 0.5 cm below the cricoid cartilage. Exploration confirmed a thickened, scarred stenotic segment approximately 4 cm long, with adhesions between the membranous trachea and esophagus (Fig. 4). The stenotic segment was sharply dissected, resected, and an end-to-end anastomosis was performed. High-concentration oxygen was blown locally into the proximal tracheal opening after resection to maintain oxygenation and a clear surgical field (Fig. 5). The patient remained awake and cooperative during the anastomosis, actively maintaining a head-flexed position. Intraoperative sedation was maintained (Ramsay score 2–4, Narcotrend B1-C2). A single intravenous dose of sufentanil (5 µg) was administered for mild movement during posterior tracheal manipulation, effectively suppressing discomfort. Tracheoscopy via the nose confirmed a patent anastomosis (Fig. 6). Spontaneous respiration remained adequate (RR normal), and vital signs were stable (HR 78–102 bpm, BP 124–158/74–87 mmHg, SpO2 93%-100%).

Outcome

The surgery (tracheal resection, end-to-end anastomosis) was completed in 180 min (anesthesia time 210 min) without requiring extracorporeal support. Intraoperative fluid intake was 700 ml, with estimated blood loss of 50 ml. The patient was fully awake, comfortable, and transferred to the ward ambulatory, maintaining a head-flexed position with bilateral mandibular suspension wires. He recovered well and was discharged successfully.

Discussion

Post-intubation tracheal stenosis presents a significant anesthetic dilemma.To precisely characterize the severity of critical airway stenosis, we report both the absolute tracheal diameter, the percentage of luminal narrowing, and the Myer-Cotton grade. The Myer-Cotton grading system categorizes subglottic and tracheal stenosis as follows: Grade I (< 50% luminal narrowing), Grade II (51%–70%), Grade III (71%–99%), and Grade IV (complete occlusion). The normal adult tracheal diameter ranges from 15 to 25 mm. In our patient, preoperative 3D-reconstructed CT demonstrated a minimal luminal diameter of approximately 4 mm, corresponding to an estimated stenosis of 67%–78% over a 1.0 cm segment. According to the Myer-Cotton classification, this degree of narrowing (71%–99% lost lumen diameter) constitutes Grade III stenosis — a category considered severe, where even minor additional insults (e.g., secretions, edema, or positional changes) may precipitate complete airway obstruction. The 4.9 mm outer diameter of the bronchoscope used in preoperative assessment (the fiberoptic bronchoscope could not pass through the stricture) precluded conventional tracheal intubation or ventilation. Forced attempts risked trauma, edema, or worsening obstruction. The patient’s preoperative presentation of progressively worsening dyspnea and wheezing indicated that the stenosis had already caused symptoms and significant functional impairment, clearly placing the patient in the highest risk category for airway management. This decision was further influenced by the patient’s comorbidities (coronary artery disease), necessitating avoidance of hypoxia, carbon dioxide retention, or stress responses. A 4-millimeter lumen leaves no margin for error, fully justifying the use of regional anesthesia techniques that preserve spontaneous breathing as both reasonable and necessary.Surgical correction necessitates a secure airway and adequate gas exchange during tracheal transection, where traditional intubation is impossible or hazardous [1–4]. While techniques like ECMO or cardiopulmonary bypass provide oxygenation security [6, 7], they are invasive, resource-intensive, and carry risks of complications (coagulopathy, DVT, limb ischemia). Awake techniques preserving spontaneous ventilation offer a compelling alternative, relying on the patient’s intrinsic ability to maintain their own airway and ventilation until the surgeon establishes a proximal airway opening [10, 11].

The surgical scope primarily involved the area from the cricoid cartilage plane to the suprasternal notch. The cervical plexus originates from the C1-C4 spinal nerves and is divided into superficial and deep branches. The superficial branches innervate the neck skin, while the deep branches primarily supply the deep neck muscles and portions of the diaphragm [9]. The recurrent laryngeal nerve is a branch of the vagus nerve: the right recurrent laryngeal nerve branches off the vagus nerve at the level of the right subclavian artery (at the entrance to the thoracic cavity), while the left recurrent laryngeal nerve branches off at the level of the aortic arch (within the thoracic cavity); After branching off, both recurrent laryngeal nerves travel upward along the tracheoesophageal groove to the larynx; there are no branches of the recurrent laryngeal nerve at the C6-C7 cervical vertebral segments (Gray’s Anatomy, 42nd Edition). Ultrasound-guided puncture at the C6–C7 segment targets the main trunk of the vagus nerve within the carotid sheath; the local anesthetic is confined to the area surrounding the main trunk of the vagus nerve and does not diffuse to the recurrent laryngeal nerves located within the thoracic cavity or the tracheoesophageal groove at this segment, thereby fundamentally reducing the risk of recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy.Bilateral vagal nerve blockade effectively prevents vagal reflexes and coughing during tracheal exploration and resection.After a thorough risk-benefit assessment, we decided to perform bilateral cervical superficial plexus vagus nerve block. Safety considerations of bilateral vagus nerve block within the carotid sheathThe bilateral vagus nerve block described in this report involves injection of local anesthetic into the bilateral carotid sheaths at the C6-C7 level [12, 13]. We acknowledge that this technique is highly unconventional and carries significant potential risks. To our knowledge, there are very few clinical reports describing intentional bilateral local anesthetic injection into the carotid sheaths [14]. Bilateral vagus nerve or recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy may lead to life-threatening airway complications, including bilateral vocal cord paralysis, aspiration, and respiratory compromise. Furthermore, local anesthetic infiltration into the bilateral paracarotid region has been reported to potentially cause fatal complications, including local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST), hematoma, and inadvertent intra-arterial injection with cerebral sequelae .For achieving bilateral airway sensory blockade to suppress the cough reflex during tracheal surgery, more commonly used techniques include bilateral superior laryngeal nerve blocks and transtracheal (through-the-cricothyroid-membrane) blocks. These techniques have a longer track record and a more favorable safety profile. However, in our patient, the stenosis was located immediately below the cricoid cartilage, and the surgical resection involved a 4-cm segment extending into the upper thoracic trachea. Superior laryngeal nerve blocks anesthetize the supraglottic and glottic regions but provide incomplete blockade of the infraglottic trachea and carina, which are innervated by the recurrent laryngeal nerves and vagal afferents. Transtracheal block, while effective for the tracheal mucosa, carries risks of bleeding, coughing, and potential injury to the posterior tracheal wall, and it does not block vagal reflexes from deeper manipulation. Given the need for complete suppression of coughing and vagal reflexes during extensive tracheal mobilization and anastomosis, we opted for bilateral vagus nerve blocks despite their higher risk profile.We do not advocate this technique as a routine or first-line approach. Its use in this case was justified only by the convergence of several exceptional circumstances: (1) the patient’s explicit refusal of awake tracheostomy under local infiltration; (2) the critical 4 mm lumen, which made conventional endotracheal intubation, laryngeal mask placement, or high-frequency jet ventilation unsafe or impossible; (3) the presence of coronary artery disease, requiring avoidance of hypoxia, hypercapnia, and stress responses; and (4) the patient’s willingness to cooperate and accept the risks after detailed informed consent. The “safety” of this technique in our report is therefore relative — it was considered safer than the available alternatives (e.g., attempting intubation with likely fatal hypoxia, or proceeding with awake tracheostomy against the patient’s will).

To mitigate risks, we implemented several measures: low-concentration (0.375%) and low-volume (3 mL per side) ropivacaine; ultrasound-guided injection within the carotid sheath at the C6-C7 level (below the origin of the superior laryngeal nerve and below the cricoid cartilage); confirmation of block effects without respiratory compromise; continuous monitoring of voice and respiratory effort; and immediate availability of ECMO, high-frequency jet ventilation, and a surgical airway team as a backup plan. Despite these precautions, we emphasize that this technique should only be considered in highly selected cases by experienced teams with robust contingency plans.

We fully considered the concerns regarding vocal cord paralysis, risk of aspiration, and difficulties in clearing secretions or controlling bleeding. In this case, the primary threat to patient safety was that when the degree of airway narrowing exceeded the critical value (4 mm), airway stabilization procedures could lead to complete airway obstruction. Traditional methods such as endotracheal intubation or laryngeal mask airway placement carry risks of tissue edema, displacement, or trauma, which could further obstruct the already narrow airway, and for patients with coronary heart disease, this could easily result in fatal hypoxia. In addition, strong cough reflexes in awake patients during tracheal manipulation could cause sudden movements, endangering the stability of the anastomosis. The main advantage of this approach is avoiding general anesthesia and preserving spontaneous ventilation before controlling the proximal airway, which is considered the safest route during tracheostomy [15]. While extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) can provide ultimate oxygen support, it is highly invasive and carries risks such as coagulopathy, limb ischemia, and significant cost, so it is reserved as a backup plan in case of pre-tracheostomy ventilation failure. An experienced team concluded that the combination of regional anesthesia and close monitoring provided a good risk assessment for this cooperative patient.

Regional anesthesia strategy

Our approach utilized bilateral superficial cervical plexus blocks to anesthetize the surgical field (skin, subcutaneous tissue) and bilateral vagus nerve blocks to suppress the tracheal cough reflex during manipulation [12]. Avoiding deep cervical plexus blocks was crucial to prevent phrenic nerve blockade and respiratory compromise in this patient with pre-existing dyspnea. Dexmedetomidine provided excellent cooperative sedation, hemodynamic stability, and minimal respiratory depression. Sufentanil, administered in minimal doses (5 µg), provided supplemental analgesia without significant respiratory or circulatory compromise. This combination allowed the patient to tolerate the prolonged thyroid position and surgical manipulations while maintaining spontaneous ventilation and airway reflexes.

Considerations and limitations

This technique demands meticulous preoperative planning, expertise in advanced regional anesthesia (ultrasound-guided blocks), and airway management. Continuous communication between the surgeon and anesthesiologist is paramount. Potential limitations include:

Incomplete Blockade: Surgical traction or deep dissection may cause discomfort, requiring supplemental analgesia (as used in this case).

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that awake tracheoplasty under regional anesthesia (superficial cervical plexus and vagus nerve blocks) with dexmedetomidine sedation and minimal opioid supplementation is a viable and relatively safe strategy for patients with severe tracheal stenosis, particularly those with comorbidities like coronary artery disease where general anesthesia or extracorporeal support poses significant risks. This technique leverages spontaneous ventilation and avoids the complexities of ECMO/CPB. Success hinges on thorough preoperative assessment, precise regional anesthesia technique, careful sedation management, a detailed contingency plan, and excellent teamwork.
