CRIM: A Natural Game on Integer Partitions
Ina Bašić, Eric Gottlieb, Matjaž Krnc

Abstract
We analyze Column-Row Impartial Merge (CRIM), an impartial combinatorial game played on integer partitions. A move in CRIM consists of removing an arbitrary row or column from the corresponding Young diagram, with the remaining parts reattaching to form a single partition. We define rectairs — a common generalization of rectangles and staircases — and characterize their 𝒫 / 𝒩 \mathcal{P}/\mathcal{N} -status. We define the meld operation on partitions and show that the meld of losing rectairs is losing. We introduce Odds-Are-Even (OAE) and Evens-Are-Odd (EAO) partitions, proving that all OAE partitions are 𝒫 \mathcal{P} -positions and characterizing the losing positions within EAO partitions. We determine the 𝒫 / 𝒩 \mathcal{P}/\mathcal{N} -status for staircases and for 2 2 - and 3 3 -part partitions. We evaluate CRIM and its restrictions to certain partition families within the Conway-Gurvich-Ho classification scheme, establishing that CRIM is neither returnable nor domestic. We conjecture that every losing partition has even rank.

2.3
Combinatorial games and their classification

In this paper we consider finite (short) impartial games, that is, players have the same moves available to them throughout a game, and after finitely many steps the games concludes with a uniquely defined winner.
Throughout the paper all positions in the games are partitions.

A
winning position
, also known as an
\mathcal{N}
-position, is a position in which the next player can guarantee a win.
Conversely, a
losing position
, equivalently a
\mathcal{P}
-position, is a position from which the previous player can guarantee a win. Whenever a move leads to a
\mathcal{P}
position, we also say that such a move
wins.

We call a position
terminal
if no further moves can be made from it.

There are two ways to play a combinatorial game, depending on the winning condition. In
normal play
, a player who reaches a terminal position loses. In
misère play
, a player who reaches a terminal position wins.

Given an impartial game
G
, let
\lambda
be one of its positions.
For normal-play impartial games, Sprague
31
and Grundy
21
independently found a way to generalize winning and losing positions, assigning to each position in an impartial combinatorial game a nonnegative integer value, called the
Sprague-Grundy value
of the position. We will denote the Sprague-Grundy value of a position
\lambda
by
\mathcal{G}_{G}(\lambda)

and the related
minimal excluded value
of a finite set of nonnegative integers
S
by
\mathrm{mex}(S)
. For an in-depth treatment of the concepts mentioned in this subsection we refer the reader to
30
.

The
misère Grundy numbers

\mathcal{G}_{G}^{-}
are defined the same way as the ordinary Sprague-Grundy values, where the recursion end with terminal positions having the misère Grundy value equal to one.
Let
\mathcal{G}_{G}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{G}_{G}^{-}(\lambda)
be its Sprague-Grundy and misère Grundy values, respectively.
Then, we define the
Conway pair
of
\lambda

\mathrm{CP}_{G}(\lambda)=(\mathcal{G}_{G}(\lambda),\mathcal{G}_{G}^{-}(\lambda))

We omit the subscript when

Proposition 10
.

A partition
\lambda=[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]\in\mathcal{P}
if and only if
\lambda_{1}
is even and
\lambda_{2}>0
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
\lambda_{1}

\lambda_{1}
is even.
For the base step, recall that
[2,1]\in\mathcal{P}
and
[2,2]\in\mathcal{P}
.
For the induction step, suppose
\lambda_{1}>2
. Note that either row move allows for a winning parallel response, so suppose the first player removes a column.
If the column move intersects only the first row, then the removal of rightmost column is a winning response.
If the column move intersects the second row and
\lambda_{2}=1
then removing the sole remaining row wins.
Finally, if the column move intersects the second row and
\lambda_{2}>1
then any column move is a winning response.

Now suppose
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}
.
If
\lambda_{1}
is odd and at least 3, then the removal of a column other than the first one leaves a partition
\bar{\lambda}=[\bar{\lambda}_{1},\bar{\lambda}_{2}]
where
\bar{\lambda}_{1}
is even.
By induction,
\bar{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}
, a contradiction. So
\lambda_{1}
is even.
∎

\mathrm{CP}([c^{r}])=\begin{cases}(1,0)&\mbox{ if $r=1$ and $c$ is odd or $r$ is odd and $c=1$}\\
(2,2)&\mbox{ if $r=1$ and $c$ is even or $r$ is even and $c=1$}\\
(0,0)&\mbox{ if $r>1$ and $c>1$ have the same parity}\\
(2,1)&\mbox{ if $r=2$ and $c>1$ is odd or $r>1$ is odd and $c=2$}\\
(1,1)&\mbox{ if $r>2$ and $c>2$ have different parity}.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
r=1

c=1

17
. Note that
\mathrm{CP}([2,2])=(0,0)
. If
c>2
then the partitions reachable in one move from

[c]

[(c-1)^{2}]

\displaystyle\mathrm{CP}([c^{2}])

\displaystyle=\rm{mex}(\mathrm{CP}([c]),\mathrm{CP}([(c-1)^{2}]))

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\rm{mex}((2,2),(2,1))=(0,0)&\mbox{ if $c$ is even}\\
\rm{mex}((1,0),(0,0))=(2,1)&\mbox{ if $c$ is odd.}\end{cases}

The result for

Now suppose

c>2
. The partitions reachable from
[c^{r}]

[(c-1)^{r}]

\displaystyle\mathrm{CP}([c^{r}])

\displaystyle=\rm{mex}(\mathrm{CP}([c^{r-1}]),\mathrm{CP}([(c-1)^{r}])

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\rm{mex}((1,1),(1,1))=(0,0)&\mbox{ if $r$ and $c$ have the same parity}\\
\rm{mex}((0,0),(0,0))=(1,1)&\mbox{ if $r$ and $c$ have different parities.}\end{cases}
