A scientific note on Aethina sp. in wild stingless bee nests

Michael N. K. Muturi, Seunghyun Lee, Seunghwan Lee, Rebecca Denes, H. Michael G. Lattorff, Kiatoko Nkoba, Peter Neumann, Alexis Beaurepaire

A few beetle species can be found in bee nests, ranging from occasional associates to harmful common parasites (Neumann and Ritter ; Krishnan et al. ; Camenzind et al. ; Wambua et al. ). In general, the host ranges of these beetles remain poorly understood, with the notable exception of small hive beetle (SHB), Aethina tumida, which is a well-known parasite of both wild and managed honey bee colonies as well as managed stingless bees and bumblebees (Neumann et al. ). However, whether any Aethina species are associated with wild stingless bee colonies remains unknown. Here, we report for the first time the association of a putative new species of Aethina with wild stingless bee colonies.

In February 2024 (local dry season), four underground nests of Meliponula lendliana were identified in Kakamega Forest, Kenya (Table ). The nests were carefully excavated and inspected. Adult beetles (N = 71), found in all four nests, were sampled and sexed following standard methods (Neumann et al. ). No beetle larvae or eggs were found in any of the stingless bee nests. Samples were preserved in 90% ethanol and stored in a −20 °C freezer until further molecular analysis. The samples were later shipped to the Institute of Bee Health at the University of Bern, Bern, Switzerland. A subset of the samples (three males and three females) was shipped to the Laboratory of Insect Biosystematics, Division of Entomology, Seoul National University, Republic of Korea, for morphological identification.

To conduct the morphological identification, specimens were examined using an Olympus SZ61 stereomicroscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Photographs were taken with a Canon EOS 70D digital camera equipped with a Canon MP-E 65 mm f/2.8 1–5× macro lens and controlled using a Cognisys StackShot system. To examine the male and female genitalia and pygidium, specimens were relaxed in distilled water at room temperature for 2 to 4 h. The genitalia were then carefully dissected from the last abdominal segment using hooked pins or fine forceps, while leaving the abdomen intact. The dissected genitalia were macerated in 10% KOH at room temperature for 4 to 6 h and subsequently photographed using a Samsung Galaxy Note S24 Ultra. The body length and thoracic width measurements for the male and female stingless bee beetles were also recorded.

In parallel with the morphological identification, total DNA was extracted from 44 stingless bee beetles using a NucleoSpin® Tissue kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany). Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) was then amplified (n = 18 stingless bee beetles) with SHB COI barcoding primers (Evans et al. , Table ) under optimal thermal conditions (Table ) and PCR volumes (Table ). The quality of the PCR products was assessed in a 2% (w/v) agarose gel, cleaned up using the NucleoSpin® Gel & PCR Clean-up kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany), and sent to the Fasteris Company-Genesupport SA Life Sciences (Plan-les-Ouates, Switzerland) for unidirectional sequencing using the forward primer. To complement the mtDNA data and assess the fine-scale population structure of the beetles, nuclear DNA (nuDNA) from 44 stingless bee beetles was amplified using seven microsatellite primers (Evans et al. ; Papach et al. Table ) using optimized PCR thermal conditions and reaction volumes (Tables , Table ). PCR products were sent to Microsynth AG (Balgach, Switzerland) for genotyping.

The mtDNA sequences of the stingless bee beetles were first identified using the BLAST software (Altschul et al. ). After removing redundant sequences, a final dataset of 63 sequences spanning 985 bp was assembled, consisting of stingless bee beetles, A. tumida specimen obtained from nine neighbouring African-derived honey bee colonies in another study (Muturi et al. unpubl. data), and 31 non-Aethina nitidulid specimens (Table ). A second sequence dataset was also constructed using 65 sequences (709 bp long, i.e. shorter than the first dataset) to supplement the first dataset by including two additional Aethina sequences: Aethina aeneipennis and Aethina argus (Table ). A phylogenetic tree was rooted using Carpophilinae (Table ) as the outgroup, following the topology of Lee et al. () and using the maximum likelihood method implemented in IQ-TREE (Trifinopoulos et al. ). The optimal substitution model (GTR + F + I + G4) was selected using ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. ) under the Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Nodal support was assessed using ultrafast bootstrap with 1000 replicates. To determine whether the stingless bee beetles were genetically similar to or distinct from the honey bee beetles, the mtDNA-based genetic distance (p-distance) between the two groups was calculated in MEGA 11 (Tamura et al. ). To determine the levels of nuDNA differentiation between the two groups and investigate potential admixture between the beetles, FST values were calculated. As the microsatellite primers used are located on the X chromosome (Papach et al. ), the latter analysis was done separately for females (35 stingless bee beetles and 28 honey bee beetles) and males (9 stingless bee beetles and 26 honey bee beetles) in GenAlEx software version 6.5 (Peakall and Smouse ). Furthermore, to visualize the genetic distance in the nuDNA between the two beetles, a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using R version 4.4.2 and the adegenet package (R Core Team ; Jombart ).

Detailed morphology revealed that the stingless bee beetles resemble SHB in overall habitus, having an oval, slightly convex body with pale dorsal pubescence and truncated elytra exposing the pygidium. The femora and tarsi are strongly expanded (Figure ). They can be assigned to the Aethina-complex based on the combination of general habitus, a relatively wide distance between the hind coxae, broadly lobed tarsi, and the structure of the genitalia (Lee et al. ). However, they differ from A. tumida in being smaller, more elongated, and distinctly more parallel-sided (Figure ). The male genitalia are similar in general structure but differ in having a more parallel-sided tegmen lacking a median constriction compared to that of SHB (Figure ). Most notably, the new species lacks the characteristic arch-like depression on the pygidium, which is present in Aethina s.s. (Figure -, Kirejtshuk and Lawrence ; WOAH (); Lee et al. ). In both the 985 bp and 709 bp datasets, the A. tumida sequences from samples collected in honey bee colonies and sequences from the stingless bee beetles were consistently recovered as sister taxa with an 85%–88% similarity to each other (Figure , Figure ). Both sequence groups differed by 12.4 ± 0.011%, and the same haplotypes were found in males and females. While stingless bee and honey bee beetles were strongly supported as monophyletic, the relationship between the two was only marginally supported. It would be expected that the two available Aethina sequences, Aethina aeneipennis and Aethina argus, would cluster closely to A. tumida sequences in the phylogenetic tree as they belong to the same genus. However, this was not the case as the two were distantly separate from A. tumida, rendering Aethina non-monophyletic. Thus, we report a second COI dataset of 709 bp (Table , Figure ). However, given that Aethina was recovered as monophyletic in Lee et al. (), this discrepancy in the two datasets is likely attributable to limitations of the COI-only approach used here. Altogether, the combined morphology and genetics data suggest that the beetles sampled in wild stingless bee colonies are a novel species of the genus Aethina, proposed here as Aethina sp. with its common name, being the underground nest beetle (UNB), a name that has not been previously used. The comparatively large numbers of UNB found in all investigated M. lendliana nests indicate a more common association than that observed with other species (e.g., Cychramus luteus with Apis mellifera; Neumann and Ritter ). However, the absence of any beetle eggs or larvae in M. lendliana nests needs further investigation to establish the nature of interactions between UNB and its stingless bee host. Notably, it is known that A. tumida does not always reproduce in association with A. mellifera nests (Neumann et al. ), and it is possible that UNB exhibits a similar behaviour. Thus, further investigations are required into the ecology of UNB, its host range across both underground and tree-nesting meliponine species, and its potential impact on its hosts’ health. Further description of UNB is ongoing to prepare for its approval as a new species within the Aethina genus.
