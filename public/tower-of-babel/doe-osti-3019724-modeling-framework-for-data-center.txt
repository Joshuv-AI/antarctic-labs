Executive Summary
This report highlights the critical need for advanced modeling of data centers due to their rapidly
increasing energy consumption and their impacts on power grid reliability. Driven by the
demand for artificial intelligence (AI) applications, data centers are projected to consume a
significant portion of U.S. energy by 2028, stressing an already challenged grid. This paper
emphasizes the importance of “fast” timescale models to understand the dynamic interactions
between data centers and the grid, especially given the rapid power fluctuations of AI workloads.
It outlines a modeling framework that includes both offline and real-time electromagnetic
transient domain simulations, detailing the necessary representations for various components,
such as utility interfaces, transformers, information technology loads, uninterruptible power
supply, cooling loads, battery energy storage systems, generators, protection systems, and
higher-level control systems.

Although standard simulation tools like PSCAD offer basic models, custom development is often
required to accurately capture the unique and fast-changing behaviors of modern data centers.
The paper also discusses key metrics and test cases for validating these models, focusing on
transient load responses, protection relay coordination, and demand flexibility.

Finally, it addresses the challenges of modeling large-scale data centers, such as computational
complexity and the trade-off between model fidelity and practicality, suggesting hybrid modeling
approaches as a solution. The overarching goal is to create a robust framework that helps assess
data center impacts on grid stability, identify vulnerabilities, and inform the development of
standards for these reliable integration of these large loads into the bulk power system.

Introduction
There is tremendous activity in data center interconnection requests throughout the United
States, with some utilities having queues exceeding their total current operational capacity by a
few multiples. This is fueled by a significant demand for data center computing capacity for
artificial intelligence (AI) applications. A recent report from Lawrence Berkeley National
Laboratory (Shehabi et al. 2024) projects data center energy consumption to be 6.7−12% of the
total U.S. energy consumption by 2028, compared to 4.4% of the total U.S. energy consumption
in 2025. The Long-Term Reliability Assessment report from the North American Electric
Reliability Corporation (NERC) (2024) identifies that most of the North American bulk power
system faces significant resource adequacy challenges in the next 10 years due to surging energy
demand, the retirement of thermal generators, and the time to completion of new power plant
projects. In most locations, power grids have limited available capacity to support data center
campuses ranging from several hundred megawatts to the gigawatt scale (or, as NVIDIA refers
to them, AI factories).

To address these capacity constraints in the near term, there are significant conversations around
data center load flexibility. A 2025 report by Duke University’s Nicholas Institute estimates that
modest curtailment of only 0.25–1% of annual consumption during critical hours could enable
the grid to absorb up to 100 GW of new load without the need for new generation or other major
upgrades (Norris et al. 2025). To mitigate the shortage of grid power, certain data center
campuses are colocating energy generation capabilities, with the possibility of selling excess
generation back to the grid (Bloomberg 2025). Some hyperscalers are demonstrating their load
flexibility capabilities through workload scheduling (Terrell 2025) or through virtual power plant
contracts (Voltus 2025). A recent PJM stakeholder review called out the need for load flexibility,
the use of backup power generation in data center campuses, and load curtailment as options for
assisting the grid (PJM 2025).

In summary, the rapid demand for data centers is already stressing the grid and poses questions
about how to continue to operate the grid reliably. The interaction between grid-side events and
data center power systems at various timescales needs to be better understood, as data centers are
on track to be a significant load on the grid. This understanding would help inform
interconnection studies, policies, and regulations to assist with grid reliability. There are already
new regulations and interconnection policies; a notable one is SB6 in the Electric Reliability
Council of Texas (ERCOT) territory, which can cause data centers to be shut down during grid
emergency periods.

Data center load has significantly evolved from being central processing unit-heavy uncorrelated
workloads to being graphics processing unit-dominated and coordinated workloads. These
workloads can go from an idle power state (10–20% of rated power) to a peak power state (80–
100% of rated power) within 10–100 milliseconds, exposing an oscillatory point of
interconnection (POI) demand signal to the grid. AI workload frequencies fall into the
subsynchronous regime. These can excite resonant modes in transmission networks, potentially
leading to subsynchronous resonance or interarea oscillations. Further, rapid, cyclical power
swings can lead to voltage flicker that is visible on lighting systems and frequency modulations
that impact grid frequency regulation, particularly in isolated or constrained regions. A recent

 1

NERC incident review (NERC 2025) reports approximately 1,500 MW of data center loads
disconnecting from the grid due to short-duration grid faults (solely due to data center protection
schemes) causing overvoltages (to 1.07 per unit [p.u.]) and overfrequencies (60.047 Hz), thus
exposing risks of wide-area outages. With significant expansion on the demand and supply side,
including behind-the-meter generation or energy storage capabilities to come, along with
dynamic workloads, it is important to view this challenge from the lens of grid reliability.

There is a clear need to put together a framework to specify ramp rates, acceptable oscillations,
and allowable protection schemes, etc., that would assist in sustaining reliable grid operation and
not exacerbate grid outage risks. This necessitates the need for power system studies that can
model grid and data center dynamics/interactions in a unified framework. To enable the study of
data center and grid interactions, a high-fidelity power system model of the data center needs to
be developed, particularly one that can cater to the fast-timescale needs of dynamic AI loads and
fast-timescale grid instability events (such as those reported in the NERC incident review
[NERC 2025]). Two types of modeling frameworks are necessary to evaluate the impacts of data
centers on grid stability:

 1. Offline electromagnetic transient (EMT) domain simulations (Badrzadeh n.d.) (using
 tools such as PSCAD and EMTP) of the data center power system with appropriate
 representations of the grid
 2. Real-time EMT domain simulations (in a controller-hardware-in-the-loop [CHIL]
 framework [IEEE 2025]) with the inclusion of the data center and grid-side controls.
In this regard, a technical report from the National Laboratory of the Rockies performed EMT
domain CHIL simulations for the 70-MW data center design of Verrus (Vaidhynathan et al.
2025).

1 Design/Operational Philosophy of Data Centers
Data center designs are entirely focused on providing a high uptime for computing/information
technology (IT) (Uptime Institute n.d.) and void any consideration for demand flexibility and
grid resilience. A typical data center uptime requirement can range from 99% to 99.999%
(leading to appropriate tier categories) (Uptime Institute n.d.), presenting a very short window of
acceptable downtime, outside of which means significant losses for businesses. Figure 1 shows
the power system architecture of various entities.

Based on the representation shown in Figure 1, next, we discuss the common attributes across
these designs:

 • Utility/grid: These data centers are powered by the utility/grid as the primary source of
 energy. The data centers are typically fed by a medium-voltage (13.8-kv–34.5-kV) supply
 from a utility substation, which is then stepped down to low-voltage (~400-V) supply for
 the IT and cooling loads in the data center.
 • Transfer switches: Depending on the uptime requirements, appropriate redundancies are
 implemented in the power distribution system feeding the IT and cooling loads. These
 redundancies facilitate, e.g., concurrent maintainability and automatic failover to an
 alternate power feed to deliver to the uptime requirements. The presence of the
 redundancy is managed through appropriate automatic or static transfer switches, as
 shown in Figure 1 (a), (e).
 • IT load: Data center IT hardware (e.g., computing servers, network servers, storage
 servers) are voltage-sensitive equipment, and their power quality requirements are
 governed by the Information Technology Industry Council (ITIC) curve (Elphick and

 5

 Smith 2010). The ITIC curve is published by ITIC (formerly known as the Computer and
 Business Equipment Manufacturers Association [CBEMA]), and it provides an AC
 voltage boundary that most IT equipment can tolerate or ride through without
 experiencing unexpected shutdowns or malfunctions. Figure 2 shows the ITIC curve: It
 shows that the input AC voltage below 0.7 p.u. is considered a dropout event, and a 1.2-
 p.u. overvoltage is acceptable for up to 3 milliseconds. The typical IT load is
 approximately 400 V, so the appropriate transformation of the medium-voltage supply
 from the substation needs to be incorporated into the design.
  • UPS: To mitigate AC supply voltage fluctuations outside the allowable region within the
 ITIC curve, most data center power systems have UPS units. The UPS has batteries and
 can support the IT load for a few minutes.

  • Backup power (generators): To meet the (tier-specific) uptime requirements, data
 centers need backup energy sources to mitigate risks of losses due to grid outages causing
 downtime. Most data centers rely on generators (diesel, natural gas, hydrotreated
 vegetable oil) as a backup energy source (Uptime Institute. n.d) (Figure 1 (a) to (e)), and
 some newer designs are relying on BESS (Figure 1 (f)). In particular, the generators are
 to be used only when the utility is not available, and the transition is managed through
 automatic transfer switches (see Figure 1 (a), (b), (c), (d), (e)). The backup energy source
 (generators and/or BESS) are connected at a low voltage in some designs (such as in
 Figure 1 (a), (b), (c), (e)) and at the medium-voltage level in others (Figure 1 (d) and (f)).
 The UPS helps keep the IT hardware powered when the data center transitions from
 utility power to a backup source (generator power) during a utility outage. A recent PJM
 event called on dispatching on-site generators in hyperscale data centers to serve the grid.
 Most data centers are designed to operate on generators only when the utility is not
 available (Figure 1 (a) to (e)) and not with the intent to parallel generators with the grid

 6

 for demand response. Such changes in operational requirements require modifications at
 the physical hardware level (possibly higher ratings of switchgear) as well as
 modifications of the controls software. In short, this is riskier to implement in brownfield
 sites.
 • BESS: Recently, owing to grid capacity constraints, data centers are increasingly being
 considered for grid services such as demand response. A recent report from EPRI (2025)
 compares the different approaches for data center load flexibility; in the report, BESS are
 particularly suitable compared to generators because of both reliability and emission
 constraints. In Figure 1 (f), the system design from Verrus accounts for grid paralleling of
 the BESS to respond to demand response requests (Vaidhynathan et al. 2025) through
 appropriate control systems.
 • Cooling load: The cooling system in data centers comprises pumps, chillers, air handling
 units, and coolant distribution units, which are low-voltage loads (~400–480 V). Many of
 these have variable-frequency drives. In most data centers, the IT and cooling loads are
 separate power distribution systems (see Figure 1 (a) to (e); cooling systems are not
 included), unlike the design from Verrus shown in Figure 1 (f), where cooling loads and
 IT loads are fed from the common medium-voltage bus. Appropriate backup power
 systems are put in place for cooling loads to meet the uptime requirements of the data
 center. In recent times, cooling systems in data centers are being considered for load
 flexibility (EPRI 2025) and power usage effectiveness optimization (Lazic et al. 2018).
Most traditional data center design and operational philosophies cater only to the uptime,
inherently leading to data centers being a passive participant on the grid. The operations must
keep the IT hardware operating 24-7, which can sometimes exacerbate grid instability during
voltage events (as described in the NERC incident review [NERC 2025]) based on the protection
schemes implemented on the load side. The NERC incident review found almost 1,500 MW of
data center load disconnecting from the grid during approximate 10-millisecond-scale grid-side
faults, causing overvoltages and overfrequencies on the grid, with the possibility of causing a
wide-area outage. Given the current growth of data centers and the grid constraints, it is very
important to understand how data center design and operations interact with the grid and what
types of grid disturbances/events a data center can “ride-through” and assist the grid during
instability events.

In recent times, there is a lot of attention on developing data centers as a flexible load on the grid
using a variety of approaches, such as on-site generation, on-site energy storage, and compute
job scheduling, and by dispatching cooling systems. As noted, every system architecture shown
inFigure 1 has generators except that from Verrus, which also has BESS (Figure 1 (f)). A
comparison of the effectiveness of these different approaches for data center load flexibility is
discussed in a recent report from EPRI (2025). From the grid operator’s perspective, it is
important to be able to better understand how such components of the data center power system
could be leveraged to improve grid reliability. The next section describes a model of individual
components that need to be integrated to develop an EMT domain model of any of the data
center power system architectures shown in Figure 1.

2 Modeling Framework Overview
The modeling framework builds on high-fidelity EMT representations developed through offline
simulations and validated in real-time CHIL environments. To ensure the reliable and scalable
integration of data centers into the grid, a flexible and detailed modeling approach is required.
The framework should support:

 • Offline EMT simulations—comprehensive time domain models using tools like PSCAD
 for detailed disturbance studies
 • Real-time EMT simulations—CHIL environments enabling closed-loop control and
 dynamic studies, including actual devices and controllers.

3 System Modeling Layers
3.1 Utility Interface and Medium-Voltage Bus
An accurate model of the utility interface must:

 • Model the data center POI as a dynamic, voltage-/frequency-sensitive node,
 parameterized with a realistic grid impedance (resistance and reactance) to simulate both
 strong and weak utility grid (e.g., N-1 contingency) scenarios.
 • Incorporate medium-voltage protection relays and utility coordination schemes to ensure
 reliable fault detection and grid compliance.
 • Simulate voltage sag/swells, frequency excursions, and POI oscillations, including those
 driven by rapid changes in AI loads.

3.1.1 Standard PSCAD Models Available
The PSCAD standard library includes:

 • Three-phase medium-voltage bus and basic node models
 • Standard relays (overcurrent/undervoltage)
 • Circuit breakers and line elements
 • Modules for simulating common voltage/frequency disturbances.

3.1.2 Additional Model Requirements
Custom or advanced model enhancements might be required to:

 • Parameterize the grid impedance at the POI to reflect the dynamic strength of the grid,
 enabling accurate representation of strong (high short-circuit ratio) and weak (low short-
 circuit ratio) grid behaviors and their effects on system stability and control interactions
        (Shah 2024).
 • Implement high-fidelity POI models capturing fast transient effects, relay coordination,
 and voltage/frequency dynamics unique to large data center and inverter-dominated
 systems.
 • Integrate programmable protection schemes aligned with utility standards and
 coordination routines.
 • Design and execute test routines for rapid load changes, harmonic distortion analysis, and
 oscillatory impacts that are not present in default PSCAD models.
 • Use impedance scan and system strength tools for frequency domain validation, and
 support advanced simulations for black-start, ride-through, and grid support controller
 performance.

3.2 Transformers
To accurately represent transformers in data center interconnections, models must capture:

 • Fast transient phenomena, such as inrush current and saturation during energization or
 reclosing
 • Detailed tap changer control behavior under varying load and voltage conditions

 9

 • Protective relaying schemes for transformer overcurrent, differential protection, and
 under-/overvoltage trips
 • Transformer interaction with dynamic, fast-varying data center loads and grid
 disturbances, including fault scenarios and overvoltage risk.

3.2.1 Standard PSCAD Models Available
The PSCAD library provides comprehensive transformer models, including three-phase power
transformers with magnetization characteristics:

 • Saturation modeling
 • Tap changer functionality
 • Protection elements.
These components support dynamic simulations of steady-state and transient conditions, such as
load changes and switching events.

3.2.2 Additional Model Requirements
Given the diverse data center power system architectures, additional custom model development
might be required for:

 • Transformers in medium-voltage power plane architectures, where the integration of
 BESS and pooled IT and cooling loads creates complex interactions
 • Specialized transformer configurations found in distributed redundant 4N/3 power
 distribution or designs involving medium-voltage-level backup power connections
 • Transformer protections tailored for rapid fault response and load transfer schemes
 unique to data center power redundancy approaches
 • Integrating transformer models with complex transfer switch logic and protection
 schemes, as seen in modern hyperscale data centers
 • Capturing the impact of combined IT and cooling load feeding from a common medium-
 voltage bus, which requires coordinated transformer behavior modeling.
Custom models should ensure compatibility with dynamic load changes from AI workloads, on-
site generation, and demand flexibility strategies prevalent in evolving data center designs.

3.3 Information Technology Loads
IT load models need to represent typical power consumption dynamics of the server and network
hardware, including:

 • Time-varying load transitions from idle to peak demand with rapid ramp capabilities
 • Load sensitivity to voltage and frequency variations.

3.3.1 Standard PSCAD Models Available
PSCAD offers generic composite and resistive load components that can approximate aggregated
IT loads. These models capture basic steady-state and dynamic load characteristics.

3.3.2 Additional Model Requirements
For data center applications, custom models should address:

 10

 • Fast transient behavior associated with AI-driven workload fluctuations
 • Power demand diversity reflecting heterogeneous server types (central processing units,
 graphics processing units)
 • Interaction effects with cooling and environmental systems to simulate coupled demand
 changes
 • Support for real-time adaptive load control mechanisms for grid support functions.

3.4 Uninterruptible Power Supply
UPS models must accurately capture:

 • Dual-mode operation—grid-following during normal operation and grid-forming during
 islanded or backup modes
 • Transition logic for seamless switching between utility power and battery backup in
 response to faults or power quality events
 • Compliance with ride-through requirements during voltage disturbances.

3.4.1 Standard PSCAD Models Available
PSCAD does not include specific UPS library components. Common practice is to represent
UPS units either as ideal voltage sources or by building custom inverter models with integrated
battery systems.

3.4.2 Additional Model Requirements
Custom UPS inverter and battery management system models are required to emulate real UPS
behavior during dynamic load changes, battery charge/discharge cycles, and power quality
events. Integration with data center energy management systems and coordination with BESS are
necessary for the accurate simulation of backup power architectures in data centers.

3.5 Cooling Loads
Cooling load models must encompass:

 • Voltage and frequency sensitivity of motor-driven components, such as pumps and fans
 • Control logic for staged startup, load shedding, and curtailment during grid events or
 demand response
 • Thermal inertia effects influencing cooling system recovery times after disturbances
 • Coordination with facility management systems to reflect realistic operational sequences.

3.5.1 Standard PSCAD Models Available
PSCAD includes generic motor load components that can be used to model variable-frequency
drive-driven pumps, chillers, and fans commonly found in cooling systems.

3.5.2 Additional Model Requirements
Customized models should be developed to:

 • Capture detailed variable-frequency drive control schemes and their dynamic response to
 grid disturbances.

 11

 • Model interactions between cooling loads and IT loads, including common medium-
 voltage bus influences.
 • Represent emergency backup operation modes powered by UPS or generators.
 • Support integration with data center energy management and supervisory control systems
 for demand flexibility and resilience strategies.

3.6 Battery Energy Storage System
BESS models must comprehensively capture:

 • Fast transient response and dynamic control during grid events, including seamless
 operation in both grid-connected and island modes
 • Performance criteria outlined in the Institute for Electrical and Electronics Engineers
 (IEEE) Std 1547 (IEEE 2018), such as autonomous frequency response, voltage support,
 and disturbance ride-through
 • Accurate battery state-of-charge management and interactions with site controllers,
 especially under rapid load changes.

3.6.1 Standard PSCAD Models Available
PSCAD includes generic battery storage models and inverter-based templates that simulate
charge/discharge cycles, grid-following and grid-forming controls, and interactions with AC/DC
systems. Increasingly, most original equipment manufacturers now provide factory-tested, IEEE
Std 1547-compliant BESS models that are ready for integration with simulation platforms. These
models reflect current grid support functions—including ride-through, voltage and frequency
regulation, and advanced controls—ensuring alignment with utility interconnection
requirements.

3.6.2 Additional Model Requirements
Custom development might still be required to:

 • Implement advanced control strategies tailored for data center microgrids and integrate
 with other backup assets, such as UPS and generators.
 • Support parallel operation and coordinated dispatch, unique to large-scale deployments.
 • Enable hardware-in-the-loop or real-time simulation for the comprehensive validation of
 BESS performance within complex, fast-varying data center loads.

3.7 Generator Models
Generator models should incorporate the following key elements:

 • Startup delays and ramp rates—reflecting the response times of diesel, natural gas, or
 other backup generation asset
 • Load acceptance dynamics—capturing realistic backup power connection times during
 transitions
 • Protective relays—including overcurrent, undervoltage, overvoltage, and frequency
 deviation protections

 12

 • Synchronization and reconnection mechanisms—for smooth transitions back to grid
 operation during utility restoration
 • Automatic generation control—for continuous regulation of generator output to maintain
 system frequency and overall power balance across multiple generators
 • Automatic voltage regulation—to control excitation and regulate voltage stability and
 reactive power output at the POI
 • Control modes:
 o Isochronous control for maintaining constant frequency/speed, critical during
 islanded or isolated operation
 o Droop control for proportional load sharing among multiple parallel generators in
 response to frequency changes
 o Load-sharing schemes coordinating power dispatch among generators based on
 capacity and system demands.

3.7.1 Standard PSCAD Models Available
PSCAD offers detailed synchronous and asynchronous generator models, including excitation
systems, governors, and protection schemes; however, these models are often challenging to
integrate because the detailed dynamic characteristics and control parameters are typically not
provided by manufacturers, making it difficult to accurately represent actual generator behavior.

3.7.2 Additional Model Requirements
Custom development is needed to:

 • Capture real-world generator dynamics reflecting site-specific configurations and unique
 backup power operations.
 • Integrate tightly with UPS and BESS models for coordinated backup power responses
 under fault and transient conditions.
 • Include advanced control schemes allowing smooth switching between control modes
 (e.g., from droop to isochronous) depending on the operational scenarios.
 • Model fault ride-through capabilities and grid code compliance for reliable operation
 during disturbances.
 • Enable the simulation of coordinated load sharing and fault response among multiple
 generators to avoid overload and ensure stable power delivery.

3.8 Protection Systems
Every breaker in the modeled system is assumed to have its own programmable thresholds.
These thresholds define whether a specific subsystem remains connected or is isolated during an
abnormal condition. Accurate protection system modeling requires:

 • Implementation of protective relay logic with customizable thresholds for voltage,
 current, and frequency
 • Circuit breaker models supporting single-pole and three-pole operations, with realistic
 opening/closing time delays
 • Simulation of relay coordination and cascading trip sequences under various fault
 scenarios

 13

 • Integration of current transformer and voltage transformer saturation effects and
 harmonic filtering for refined fault discrimination.

3.8.1 Standard PSCAD Models Available
PSCAD offers a range of protection system modeling elements, including circuit breakers,
relays, and protective devices that can replicate fundamental protection functions, such as
overcurrent, undervoltage, and distance protection. But it does so primarily through a
combination of predefined components and user-defined or custom-built logic.

3.8.2 Additional Model Requirements
Custom development is needed to incorporate:

 • Advanced relay functions, such as relay coordination—including timing, multizone
 protection, and cascading trip sequences—which are often implemented through user-
 developed logic or scripting
 • Communication-assisted protection features, such as breaker failure detection and
 interlocking
 • Breaker control and reclose sequences tailored to the operational requirements of data
 centers.

3.9 Higher-Level Control Systems
Higher-level control systems in data centers include supervisory control and data acquisition
(SCADA) systems, building management systems, energy management systems, paralleling
switchgear controllers, and microgrid controllers, among others. These systems coordinate
breaker operations and control distributed energy resources (DERs), loads, and backup
generation to ensure reliable, efficient, and flexible operation. Incorporating supervisory control
logic allows the assessment of how coordinated dispatch and automated restoration can mitigate
widespread disconnections observed in past incidents. These control systems implement a
complex sequence of operations to facilitate automated operation of the data center.

Modeling of higher-level controls requires:

 • Implementation of custom control sequences for the operation of breakers, load/DER
 dispatch, and demand response
 • Real-time communication and monitoring interfaces between control layers and power
 equipment
 • Integration of fault detection, system isolation, and black-start control strategies.

3.9.1 Standard PSCAD Models Available
PSCAD does not provide ready-made library models for higher-level data center control
systems, such as microgrid controllers, SCADA systems, building automation systems, and
energy management systems. Instead, models must be developed using custom logic and control
blocks tailored to specific project requirements. For reference frameworks, it is worthwhile to
consider established standards:

 14

 • IEEE Std 2030.7 (IEEE 2017): defines functional specifications and interoperability
 requirements for microgrid controllers, covering dispatch, transitions between grid-
 connected and islanded states, and cyber-physical system integration
 • ASHRAE Guideline 13 (ASHRAE 2024): standardizes the design, documentation,
 specification, and integration of building automation systems, ensuring support for
 system architecture, commissioning, performance monitoring, and communication
 between heating, ventilating, and air-conditioning/mechanical and electrical systems.

3.9.2 Additional Model Requirements
Custom development is generally needed to:

 • Build site-specific supervisory controllers with interfaces conforming to IEEE Std 2030.7
 and ASHRAE Guideline 13, ensuring robust communications and fail-safe operation.
 • Integrate with third-party building automation systems or energy management system
 solutions to support holistic facility management.
 • Validate controller performance using advanced simulation environments, such as CHIL,
 which enables real-time interaction and robust predeployment testing of the control
 hardware under realistic operational conditions.

3.10 Automatic Transfer Switches/Static Transfer Switches
Automatic transfer switch and static transfer switch models should capture:

 • Fast transfers between independent utility and backup sources (utility, generator, UPS)
 • Sensing and logic-driven transfer initiation (utility loss, voltage/frequency threshold
 crossing)
 • Transfer time, transient response, and interruption characteristics unique to automatic
 transfer switch (mechanical) and static transfer switch (solid-state, subcycle response)
 • Voltage synchronization, inrush control, and load acceptance routines

3.10.1 Standard PSCAD Models Available
PSCAD provides standard switch components and logic blocks that can emulate basic automatic
transfer switch/static transfer switch operation. Users can deploy programmable breakers and
switching elements to represent both manual and automatic transfer events. The standard library
includes:

 • Basic three-phase switch models for utility-to-backup source transitions
 • Logic blocks for emulating status sensing and transfer decision processes
 • Time delay and sequential switching features.

3.10.2 Additional Model Requirements
Custom development might be required to:

 • Accurately represent the transient switching dynamics, including the difference between
 a mechanical automatic transfer switch (typical transfer time in seconds) and solid-state
 static transfer switch (typical transfer time in milliseconds).

 15

  • Integrate programmable protection coordination to prevent back-feed, ensure source
 synchronization, and comply with utility interlock requirements.
  • Implement fault ride-through and seamless load transition during critical events,
 particularly important for Tier IV and hyperscale data centers with stringent uptime
 demands.
  • Design and run test cases for switching under abnormal conditions, such as voltage sags,
 swells, and faulted source transfer.

5 Challenges and Considerations
Modeling large-scale data centers poses significant challenges due to the sheer number of
electrical components involved.

5.1 Computational Complexity
Simulating this scale with detailed component-level models significantly increases
computational time and resource requirements. Each individual device, such as an inverter or a
breaker, introduces additional states and equations into the EMT simulation, potentially leading
to long run times and memory constraints.

5.2 Model Fidelity vs. Practicality
 • Detailed modeling involves representing each device with high fidelity. This offers
 precise insight into fast transients (e.g., inrush currents, dynamic load ramping) and
 control system interactions, which is critical for the robust evaluation of reliability and
 protection coordination.
 • Aggregate modeling simplifies groups of similar devices (e.g., IT loads, BESS, or
 generators) into lumped equivalent models, reducing the computational burden but
 potentially losing individual device behaviors and interacting dynamics, which are crucial
 to assessing certain fast-timescale phenomena.

5.3 Trade-Off Decisions
 • The need for model accuracy must be balanced with simulation efficiency and the
 purpose of the study. For system-level studies assessing the overall load impacts or grid
 interaction, aggregate models might suffice.
 • Detailed models should be reserved for critical components or subsystems where
 individual device behavior strongly influences outcomes (e.g., specific IT load or UPS
 system dynamics affecting grid event studies).
 • Hybrid approaches combining detailed and aggregated models can effectively target
 computational resources while retaining sufficient fidelity for key metrics, such as inrush
 current, load ramp-up profiles, and protection system coordination.
Hybrid modeling approaches that combine detailed subsystem representations with aggregated
load equivalents provide a practical balance between fidelity and scalability. For example, Figure
1 illustrates the power system architecture of a 70-MW data center featuring medium-voltage
power planes, distributed redundant power sources, coordinated IT and cooling loads, BESS,
multiple UPS units, and utility feeds.

 • Detailed modeling for this 70-MW system provides high-fidelity insight into fast
 transient events, such as inrush currents during startup, load ramping linked to AI
 workload cycles, detailed UPS transition behaviors, and precise relay coordination. This
 is crucial for accurate studies on grid stability, fault response, and protection setting
 validation; however, the computational burden is substantial, as simulating a 70-MW
 system (with 22 IT load domains and 8 cooling domains), each with unique electrical
 devices (with controls and measurements), requires significant central processing unit
 time, memory, and data management efforts.

 21

  • Aggregate modeling offers a practical alternative by reducing the model complexity
 through lumping similar devices, enabling more rapid simulations that are suitable for
 high-level scenario planning or grid impact studies—for example, lumping the IT racks
 with UPS, which are spread over several rows, into a single IT load with a large inline
 UPS.
  • A hybrid approach is often effective, employing detailed modeling for critical
 components (e.g., BESS, UPS, critical transformers) while aggregating less critical or
 more uniform loads (e.g., bulk IT and cooling loads) to optimize fidelity and efficiency
 trade-offs.

6 Conclusion
This framework provides a structured, modular approach for representing data center power
systems within EMT platforms such as PSCAD or a digital real-time simulator. By integrating
component-level dynamics, protective coordination, and high-level control logic, it captures both
transient and steady-state behavior under abnormal grid events. The inclusion of grid-following,
grid-forming, and black-start capabilities ensures applicability across evolving data center
architectures. Aligning with recent reliability observations, the framework enables NERC and
utilities to assess aggregate load behavior at the POI, identify vulnerability thresholds, and
support the development of standards for reliable large load integration.
