Protocol for programming DNA crystal morphology using concentration-modulated W/O/W double-emulsion microfluidic droplets
Authors: Jia Liu, Xugen Chen, Lebing Wang, Feng Zhou, Jiantao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for generating concentration-modulated water-in-oil-in-water (W/O/W) double-emulsion droplets (DEDs) to program DNA crystal morphology. We describe assembly of a dual-inner-phase glass capillary microfluidic device, flow-rate-controlled co-encapsulation, osmotic shrinkage, and image-based morphology analysis. This protocol validates encapsulation predictability using methylene blue and enables aspect-ratio control of 2T crystals and directional morphology regulation in 13-mer systems.For complete information on the generation and use of this protocol, please refer to Chen et al.1Subject areas: Biotechnology and bioengineering, Material sciences, Microscopy, Physics

Before you begin

This protocol enables co-encapsulation of DNA crystallization motifs and modifiers within dual-inner-phase W/O/W DEDs, followed by osmotic concentration to induce confined crystal growth and morphology control. The protocol is primarily demonstrated using the 2T DNA system, which provides a broader operating window for droplet generation and crystallization analysis, and can subsequently be extended to 13-mer DNA crystals.

For initial implementation, we recommend reproducing the 2T system, which provides a more robust operating window for droplet generation and crystallization, as well as more reliable identification of single-droplet–single-crystal structures and geometric readouts.

While maintaining a constant total inner-phase flow rate, the encapsulated composition within individual droplets is tuned by adjusting the flow-rate ratio between W1 and W2, without interrupting droplet generation.

In this protocol, crystallization motifs refer to the DNA building blocks that assemble into ordered crystals, whereas modifiers refer to additional DNA strands that regulate the crystallization pathway or final crystal morphology. In the 2T system, the 2T motif provides the crystal-forming component, and the ΔH strand is used as a morphology-regulating modifier. The workflow first fabricates and assembles a dual–inner-phase glass capillary microfluidic device, then prepares W1, W2, O, and WO phases for droplet generation. W1 and W2 are co-encapsulated into the inner aqueous core of individual W/O/W DEDs, while the outer aqueous phase provides a hypertonic environment for osmotic shrinkage. During incubation, water efflux from the inner aqueous core concentrates the encapsulated DNA components and induces DNA crystal growth inside individual droplets. The resulting crystal formation and morphology are then quantified by microscopy and image analysis.

Innovation

This protocol integrates a dual–inner-phase glass capillary microfluidic device with osmotic shrinkage of DEDs to establish a platform for programmable control of droplet composition and confined crystallization. Compared with conventional hanging-drop or bulk crystallization methods, this approach generates droplets with distinct compositions under a constant total inner-phase flow rate, where composition is tuned by adjusting the flow-rate ratio between two aqueous streams.

This design enables predictable control over the concentration of crystallization modifiers within individual droplets and improves reproducibility across a wide range of conditions. In the associated study, the protocol allows precise regulation of the aspect ratio of 2T DNA crystals and directional morphology remodeling in 13-mer systems.

Expected outcomes

Successful implementation of this protocol should first yield stable and size-uniform dual–inner-phase W/O/W DEDs. Under bright-field microscopy, the droplets should display a clear core–shell structure, with an inner aqueous core and a relatively uniform oil layer.

In the methylene blue validation assay, the blue intensity in the droplet core should increase monotonically as QW2 increases. This trend should be consistent with the theoretical W2 volume fraction, supporting predictable W1/W2 co-encapsulation.

For the 2T DNA crystal system, incubation of droplets in the 2T WO outer aqueous phase/collection solution containing 20× 2T crystallization buffer should lead to the formation of clearly defined DNA crystals within individual droplets. As the encapsulated ΔH fraction increases, the proportion of single-crystal droplets typically increases, whereas the crystal dimension along the X direction and the X/Y aspect ratio typically decrease. At high ΔH fractions, the overall percentage of crystal-containing droplets may decrease. Expected outcomes of 2T DNA crystal morphology control are shown in Figure 11.

Quantification and statistical analysis

In the methylene blue encapsulation assay, the blue intensity measured from the central ROI of each droplet is used as the quantitative readout. For each flow-rate condition, intensity values from multiple droplets are averaged and compared with the theoretical W2 volume fraction.

For the 2T DNA crystal system, each droplet is treated as one statistical unit. The percentage of crystal-containing droplets is calculated as:

Percentage of crystal-containing droplets (%) = number of droplets containing crystals/total number of droplets × 100%

The percentage of single-crystal droplets is calculated as:

Percentage of single-crystal droplets (%) = number of single-crystal droplets/number of crystal-containing droplets× 100%

For droplets classified as single-crystal droplets, the crystal dimensions along the X and Y directions are measured using ImageJ, and the X/Y aspect ratio is calculated. X, Y, and X/Y values are reported as mean ± SD, n=20.

For the 13-mer system, quantification should focus on the top-face side length or other predefined geometric parameters. The Δ3′ and Δ5′ branches should be analyzed and presented separately.

Limitations

This protocol has been validated in two DNA crystal systems, namely the 2T and 13-mer systems. However, when applying the workflow to other crystallization systems, the W1/W2 compositions, modifier concentrations, outer-phase buffer strength, and incubation time will likely require re-optimization. Therefore, this protocol should be considered as a transferable workflow rather than a fixed formulation that can be directly applied without modification.

Reproducibility depends strongly on device quality and interfacial stability. Coaxial alignment of the glass capillaries, capillary tip dimensions, junction sealing, and surface wettability all affect droplet architecture and co-encapsulation consistency. Therefore, successful implementation requires basic experience with glass capillary microfluidics.

Although this platform enables precise tuning of droplet composition, changes in modifier ratio do not necessarily translate into proportional changes in crystallization efficiency. In addition, appropriate morphology readouts may differ among crystal systems and should be defined before quantitative analysis.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Stable core-shell W/O/W DEDs cannot be generated (related to step 13b).

Potential solution

Confirm that W1, W2, O, and WO all enter the device smoothly. Remove residual air bubbles from the channels and restore the flow rates to the validated conditions: QW1 + QW2 = 2000 μL/h, QO = 3000 μL/h, and QWO = 28000 μL/h. If droplet generation remains unstable, check whether the device has leakage at the junctions, whether the oil phase has been sufficiently degassed, and whether any channel is blocked.

Problem 2

Methylene blue intensity does not increase monotonically with QW2 (related to step 22b).

Potential solution

Use identical exposure time, illumination intensity, focus plane, and white balance for all conditions. Analyze only structurally intact droplets with clear boundaries and use the same ROI size and position for all droplets. If droplet size fluctuates substantially, optimize droplet generation before performing intensity analysis.

Problem 3

Droplets rupture, drift, or aggregate after collection (related to steps 16 and 25–26, and step 40 for the 13-mer system).

Potential solution

Add the outer collection phase to the well before droplet collection. Collect droplets gently and add additional outer phase slowly along the well wall. Avoid pipetting directly onto droplets. If droplet rupture is widespread, verify the PVA concentration and crystallization buffer strength in the outer phase.

Problem 4

Morphological changes are weak or unclear in the 13-mer system (related to step 42).

Potential solution

Analyze the Δ3′ and Δ5′ branches as independent experimental series. For the Δ3′ branch, focus on the evolution from truncated hexagonal bipyramidal crystals toward column-like structures. For the Δ5′ branch, focus on changes in the lateral dimension of the top hexagonal face. Use a predefined geometric parameter for quantification.

Problem 5

Droplet size or crystal size varies substantially between independent replicates (related to step 31 and step 41).

Potential solution

Whenever possible, perform replicate experiments using devices with comparable alignment and wettability, identical flow-rate settings, and consistent imaging parameters. Prepare W1 and W2 freshly after annealing. Apply the same inclusion and exclusion criteria across all replicates.
