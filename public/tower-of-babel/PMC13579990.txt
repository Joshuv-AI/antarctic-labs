Effect of social determinants of health care on outcomes for well-differentiated thyroid cancer at an urban safety-net hospital
Authors: Patrick Weldon, Regan Dunn, Ryan Hoefferle, Kim Dyer, Heather Klepacz, Michael Moncure, Betty Drees
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThere are multiple studies reporting the impact of social determinants of health on survival of well differentiated thyroid cancer. Many studies utilized national databases, but few have examined the effect at an institutional level. We hypothesized that assessing survival at our urban safety-net hospital would identify disparities among gender, race, and insurance status.MethodsWe examined records from our institutional cancer registry for patients diagnosed with thyroid cancer from 2000 to 2023. We obtained data regarding their demographics and cancer stage at diagnosis. A survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier estimates and Cox proportional hazards regression models.ResultsA total of 336 patients were diagnosed during this time. Most patients were female 285 (84%) and 215 (63%) under age 55. We compared survival among our White 155 (54%) and Black populations 127 (45%). Insurance status was stratified into non-insured/VA insurance/other 116 (34%), Medicaid 107 (32%), private insurance 64 (19%), and Medicare 49 (15%). Five-year survival was worse in those age > 55 (p < .001), White individuals (p=.019), and Medicaid beneficiaries (p=.003). Mortality risk was higher in males (p=.012, confidence interval (CI): 0.13–0.78), age > 55 (p<.001, CI: 0.13–0.78), Whites (p=.026, CI: 0.21–0.91), and Medicaid insured (p=.002, CI: 0.08–0.56).ConclusionsOur study demonstrates there are significant factors associated with disparities in survival for thyroid cancer. Interestingly, our White population is at increased risk which is contrary to other studies at either a national or institutional level. Further investigation is required to identify how to target this group to alleviate any modifiable factors impacting outcomes.Keywords: Well-differentiated thyroid cancer, Social determinants of health, Health disparities, Insurance status, Race, Gender

Introduction/background

Thyroid cancer incidence saw a significant increase in the early 2000s, with rates peaking at 23.0 per 100,000 for women and 8.0 per 100,000 in men in 2015 [1]. This is attributed to increased detection of smaller thyroid cancers with advances in imaging [2]. Despite the increased incidence, mortality rates have remained unchanged overall at 0.5 per 100,000 [1]. As with any disease process, there is a recent focus on disparities in incidence, mortality, and health care outcomes related to socioeconomic factors, including race, gender, and insurance status to name a few. Several studies evaluate disparities in thyroid cancer outcomes [2–10]. Many of these studies have examined disparities in thyroid cancer using publicly available national or state-based databases. While this information is useful in identifying systemic issues, there may be institution specific disparities that can present more actionable opportunities for improvement than those observed nationally.

Multiple studies conducted using data from both the National Cancer Institute’s Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results Program (SEER) and California Cancer Registry identified that Black individuals in the United States have worse overall survival and are more likely to present with more advanced disease, including regional and distant metastases [3–5]. Further research utilizing the National Cancer Database highlights disparities in the extent of treatment offered, acknowledging individuals at risk for under-treatment being Black, uninsured, or on Medicaid, and those treated in community cancer centers [6,7]. Additionally, these inequities are seen in the Pediatric thyroid cancer population as well. A review of data from the SEER database on pediatric thyroid cancer revealed worse overall survival among males, non-White patients, and individuals experiencing poverty or facing language barriers [8]. Aside from the aforementioned racial and socioeconomic factors, SEER data also shows a negative association with prognosis related to increasing age and presence of more advanced disease at the time of diagnosis [11].

Given the paucity of institutional-level data evaluating disparities in thyroid cancer outcomes, we sought to evaluate the association between demographic and socioeconomic factors and overall survival among patients with thyroid cancer treated at an urban safety-net hospital. We hypothesized that Black patients, Medicaid or uninsured patients, and those aged ≥ 55 years would demonstrate worse overall survival.

Methods

This study utilized the Cancer Registry Database at University Health Hospital in Kansas City, Missouri. De-identified data were collected for all patients diagnosed with thyroid cancer at our institution between 2000 and 2023, including both surgically and non-surgically managed patients. ICD-10 codes for cancer of the thyroid gland (C73) and histologic codes for papillary thyroid carcinoma (8050, 8260, 8340–8344, 8350, 8450–8460) and follicular carcinoma (8290, 8330–8335) were used. The project was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board and determined to be non-human subject research. The study group consisted of 336 patients diagnosed with thyroid cancer during this period. Patients with unknown/incomplete staging or other missing values were excluded.

The primary variables of interest included age, sex, race, insurance status, stage at diagnosis, marital status and histologic subtype. Age was categorized into two levels (< 55 years and ≥ 55 years) based on the 8th edition American Joint Committee on Cancer staging for differentiated thyroid cancer. Sex was categorized as male or female. Race was categorized as White or Black, with other racial groups being excluded due to insufficient sample size for meaningful analysis. Insurance status was categorized as Medicaid, Medicare, private insurance and uninsured/VA/other coverage. Due to limited sample sizes within several insurance subgroups, uninsured patients, Veterans Affairs (VA) coverage, and other miscellaneous insurance categories were combined into a single analytic group to maintain statistical power. Marital Status was categorized as single, married/domestic partner, divorced/separated/widowed. Diagnosis type was categorized as follicular, papillary, and other.

Histograms of each categorical variable were created to show the individual distributions. A survival analysis was performed to assess the differences in survival among patients within the study group. A Kaplan-Meier curve was created for overall survival for each of the variables of interest. The log-rank test was used to evaluate significant differences in the survival curves for each categorical variable. Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed for each covariate of interest, including age, sex, race, insurance status, stage at diagnosis, marital status, and histologic subtype. None of the variables violated the proportional hazards assumption and Schoenfeld Residual plots were created for each variable. An additional survival analysis was performed for insurance status while controlling for race. Kaplan-Meier curves were created for insurance status for White and Black. All analysis of the data was performed using R version 4.3.1.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 336 patients were diagnosed with thyroid cancer at University Health Hospital located in Kansas City, Missouri between 2000 and 2023. Of the 336 patients included in the review, 49 died during the study period with the overall 5-year survival rate of 93%.

The majority of patients were younger than 55 years (n = 215, 64.0%), while 121 (36.0%) were 55 years or older. Most patients were female (n = 215, 64.0%), while the male population accounted for 121 (36.0%) patients. In terms of racial distribution, 155 patients (55.0%) identified as White and 127 (45.0%) as Black. Cancer stage at the time of diagnosis was predominantly early, with 241 patients (78.8%) presenting with stage I thyroid cancer. Fewer patients were diagnosed at more advanced stages with 42 (13.7%), 17 (5.6%), and 6 (2.0%) patients being diagnosed with Stage II, Stage III, and Stage IV thyroid cancer respectively. Insurance status varied across the population. The largest groups were uninsured/VA/other coverage (n = 116, 34.5%) and Medicaid beneficiaries (n = 107, 31.8%). Private insurance covered 64 patients (19.0%), while 49 patients (14.6%) were insured through Medicare. The patients’ demographics and tumor staging are summarized in Table 1. A summary of patient survival status stratified by demographic, clinical, and socioeconomic variables is presented in Table 2, including age, sex, race, insurance type, cancer stage, diagnosis subtype, and marital status.

We next evaluated survival status within the population to identify patterns associated with patient characteristics. Patients aged ≥ 55 years had a lower survival rate, with only 71.9% (87/121) alive at follow-up, compared to 93.0% (200/215) in those < 55 years. Males demonstrated slightly lower survival (82.4%, 42/51) than females (86.0%, 245/285). Organized by race, 81.3% (126/155) of White patients were alive at follow-up, compared to 86.6% (110/127) of Black patients. Additionally, insurance status was strongly associated with survival outcomes. Patients with private insurance and those in the uninsured/VA/other category had the highest survival rates (95.3% and 92.2%, respectively). In contrast, Medicaid and Medicare beneficiaries had lower survival rates (78.5% and 71.4%, respectively), with Medicare patients exhibiting the highest mortality rate at 28.6%.

From a diagnostic perspective, disease stage at diagnosis also correlated with survival rates. Patients with Stage I disease had the highest survival rates (90.5%, 218/241), while those with Stage III and IV disease had lower survival rates (58.8% and 50.0%, respectively). Mortality was highest among those with Stage IV disease (50.0%). Among histologic subtypes, patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma had a survival rate of 86.0% (246/286), compared to 91.3% (21/23) for follicular carcinoma and 74.1% (20/27) for other subtypes.

Outcome analysis

Survival outcomes were assessed using Kaplan-Meier curves, with log-rank tests evaluating differences between categorical variables. Statistically significant differences in survival were observed for age group (p<.001), race (p=.019), insurance status (p=.003), stage (p<.001), and diagnosis type (p=.040). Cox proportional hazards regression analysis demonstrated that female sex was associated with a higher 5-year survival rate compared to males (HR: 0.32, CI: 0.13–0.78, p=.012). Patients < 55 years had a lower survival rate compared to those aged 55 and older (HR: 0.15, CI: 0.07–0.33, p<.001). White patients had worse survival outcomes compared to Black patients (HR: 0.43, CI: 0.21–0.91, p=.026), as shown in Fig. 1. Insurance status also influenced survival, with Non-Insured/VA/Other (HR: 0.21, CI: 0.08–0.56, p=.002) and Medicare patients (HR: 0.31, CI: 0.13–0.76, p=.011) demonstrating improved outcomes compared to those with Medicaid, as displayed in Fig. 2. Lastly, later stage at diagnosis was associated with significantly increased risk of mortality. Discussion

Data regarding the epidemiologic factors influencing the incidence and distribution of thyroid cancer are presented in prior studies. However, our institutional data reveals notable deviations from these established conclusions. Previous research shows worse overall survival among males, non-White patients, and individuals experiencing socioeconomic disadvantages such as poverty or language barriers [8]. Similarly to the SEER data published by Chen et al. our results showed patients aged 55 years or older had decreased survival [11]. In contrast, our analysis showed that White patients and individuals insured through Medicaid or Medicare experienced notably worse outcomes. These findings suggest that, at our institution, demographic, socioeconomic, and healthcare access factors may influence survival differently than what is observed in national datasets.

Several factors may account for these unexpected disparities. As an urban safety-net hospital, our institution serves a diverse patient population with unique socioeconomic challenges that may not be fully represented by national databases. The observed disparity in survival among White patients may reflect unmeasured socioeconomic, healthcare access, comorbidity, or referral-patter differences that were not captured within our dataset. Similarly, patients insured through Medicaid or Medicare may face delays in diagnosis or even reduced access to specialized care which are both barriers to consistent follow-up and adequate cancer surveillance. These factors may adversely impact survival, accounting for the higher mortality among these groups of patients. Patients aged 55 years and older have historically been associated with worse thyroid cancer outcomes. This could be due to the more aggressive tumor biology at the time of diagnosis and higher rates of concurrent medical conditions.

To our knowledge this is the first institutional based study of disparities in thyroid cancer at a safety-net hospital. We were able to perform a systematic collection of demographics, stage and histological subtype highlighting the unique population at our single institution which differs from previous national and state-based studies. Further investigations at this level may highlight inequities that are masked when looking at a broader population.

This study has several limitations. First, additional tumor-specific pathologic variables including extrathyroidal extension, lymphovascular invasion, margin status, and nodal burden were not consistently available within the institutional cancer registry and therefore could not be incorporated into the survival analysis. Second, since our data were de-identified we were unable to include certain aspects of socioeconomic status such as annual income, geographic location, or highest level of education. Additionally, we did not have any information on specific treatments beyond surgical intervention such as radioactive iodine, thyroid stimulating hormone suppression, etc. which can impact rates of recurrence and potentially overall survival. Finally, we were unable to obtain disease-specific survival to accurately measure thyroid cancer related survival. However, with that said our overall survival did align with previous studies.

Future institutional studies incorporating granular pathologic and treatment level data may further clarify the mechanisms underlying the disparities observed in this cohort. Additionally, integrating additional socioeconomic variables such as income, educational attainment, geographic location, and area-level deprivation indices may help explain these findings.

Conclusions

Our study highlights multiple factors that may contribute to disparities in thyroid cancer survival. Notably, we found that our White patient population faces an increased risk, an observation that contrasts with findings from national datasets. This unexpected trend warrants further investigation. By examining local trends and disparities, we can hope to tailor individual and system-based changes to address social determinants of health and improve health equity in our population.
