 Executive Summary
 This technical report documents the development of a model-based workflow to support sensor-
placement optimization at an integrated nuclear-industrial thermal interface. The thermal interface
considered in this activity is based on the FY25 optimized configuration, which couples the Rankine-cycle
portion of the balance of plant (BOP) with a high-temperature vapor-compression heat pump and an
industrial-process heat-delivery connection (Figure ES-1). The objective of the present work is to establish
a consistent diagnostic basis for identifying the measurements needed to detect and, when possible, isolate
selected degradation mechanisms affecting the thermal-interface components.

Figure ES-1. Reference FY25 optimized thermal-interface configuration used as the basis for the PRO-AID
sensor-placement study. Red highlights indicate components modified by the degradation-mitigation strategies
adopted in the FY25 optimized configuration.

 The first part of the report describes the system-level modeling activity. The reference thermal-
interface configuration was represented using complementary modeling tools. Ebsilon was used to
describe the steady-state thermodynamic behavior of the optimized configuration, while Dymola was used
to develop a dynamic representation of the integrated BOP architecture. The Dymola model was extended
to represent selected degradation mechanisms identified through the fault-selection process based on
Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA), including turbomachinery degradation, heat-exchanger
fouling, moisture-separator efficiency degradation, leakage, and heat-loss mechanisms. Representative
dynamic fault-injection simulations were then performed to demonstrate the ability of the model to
reproduce physically consistent system responses under degraded operating conditions. These simulations

provide an enabling capability for future validation of PRO-AID-optimized sensor configurations against
simulated single- and multi-fault scenarios.

 The second part of the report focuses on the development of component-level diagnostic models for
PRO-AID. Dedicated residual-based models were developed for the steam turbine, compressor, deaerator,
and moisture separator. For each component, the governing relationships, measurable quantities,
diagnostic residuals, and expected fault signatures were defined. These models provide the analytical
structure needed to connect physical degradation mechanisms to measurable deviations in process
variables and to determine which measurements are required to support fault detectability and, when
possible, fault isolation. The sensor-assignment problem was formulated as a minimum-cost sensor-
placement optimization constrained by diagnostic requirements. Candidate measurements were mapped
to the residuals required by the PRO-AID component models and evaluated according to their ability to
support residual generation, fault detectability, and fault isolation. The optimization was first performed
using a partitioned approach, in which the BOP was divided into two interconnected domains that were
optimized separately and then recombined. This approach reduced the computational burden and allowed
the selected configurations to be reviewed on systems of manageable complexity.

Figure ES-2. Representative result of the whole-BOP sensor-placement optimization. Candidate measurements
provided to PRO-AID are shown in gray, while the 84 sensors selected by the optimization are highlighted in
green.

 As a final assessment, the optimization was repeated by treating the complete BOP as a single
optimization domain (Figure ES-2). This analysis verified the results of the partitioned approach and
evaluated whether additional fault-isolation capability could be obtained by considering measurement
correlations across the full system. The whole-BOP optimization retained 84 sensors out of 291 potential
sensor locations, corresponding to 29% of the original sensor set. The resulting configuration preserved

detectability for all but one of the considered degradation scenarios and improved fault isolation relative
to the partitioned optimization by exploiting analytical redundancy across subsystem boundaries.

 Overall, this activity fulfilled the milestone objective by adapting a physics-based PRO-AID capability
to support the design of a sensor set that meets diagnostic needs for the thermal interface. The developed
workflow links system-level thermal-interface modeling, FMEA-based fault selection, component-level
diagnostic-model development, and sensor-placement optimization. Future work should use the Dymola
model to generate additional nominal and degraded simulation data, including multi-fault scenarios, and
validate the optimized sensor configurations against those datasets. Additional efforts should also focus
on refining selected fault implementations, expanding the component-model library, quantifying
diagnostic performance under uncertainty, and improving fault isolation where residual-signature
ambiguity remains.

1. Introduction
 Advanced nuclear energy systems are increasingly being considered not only for electricity generation,
but also as sources of high-temperature thermal energy for industrial processes. These systems can support
flexible energy production while reducing the reliance of energy-intensive industries on fossil-fuel
combustion. Such configurations, however, introduce additional operational complexity because the
performance of the nuclear power-conversion system, the thermal interface, and the industrial process
becomes closely coupled. Degradation or failure of a single balance of plant (BOP) component may
therefore affect both electrical generation and process-heat delivery.
A reference architecture for this application was developed in the milestone completed in September 2025
[1]. That work considered a Rankine-cycle BOP coupled to a high-temperature vapor-compression heat
pump for the delivery of high-quality heat. The design was subsequently optimized from a degradation-
aware perspective by evaluating alternative mitigation strategies intended to preserve production capacity
during long-term component aging. The selected configuration incorporated provisions such as component
oversizing, turbine-protection measures, and redundancy in selected auxiliary systems to improve
operational resilience while maintaining the fundamental thermodynamic structure of the reference cycle.

 The present activity builds upon the optimized design by developing the monitoring and diagnostic
capabilities needed to maximize continuity of service and enable the early detection of performance
degradation before it significantly reduces plant power capacity or disrupts operation. In particular, the
work focuses on the development of a model-based framework for generating representative failure data,
defining component-level diagnostic models, and identifying the measurements needed to detect and
distinguish selected degradation mechanisms. The objective is to establish a consistent connection between
system-level simulation, physics-based residual generation, fault-signature analysis, and sensor
assignment.
In the September 2025 study, the steady-state Ebsilon model of the thermal interface was presented, and
the component failure modes of greatest interest were identified through a Failure Modes and Effects
Analysis (FMEA) [1]. These results provided both the system architecture and the degradation scenarios
to be examined in the present activity. Building on that foundation, a dynamic model of the Ebsilon-
optimized configuration was developed in Dymola, a Modelica-based environment for system model
creation, simulation, and post-processing [2]. The Dymola model was developed to reproduce the time-
dependent response of the integrated BOP and to simulate the occurrence of selected degradation and
failure scenarios by modifying relevant component parameters, performance characteristics, and flow
paths. The resulting simulations provide controlled, repeatable, and time-dependent datasets under
nominal and degraded operating conditions, which can be used to support future evaluation of the proposed
diagnostic methods and the adequacy of the selected sensor set.

 To this aim, dedicated component models are developed for the Parameter-Free Reasoning Operator
for Automated Identification and Diagnosis (PRO-AID), a model-based diagnostic framework that uses
process measurements and analytical redundancy among component equations to detect inconsistencies
between expected and observed system behavior. These models support fault detection and, when

sufficient analytical redundancy is available, fault isolation [3]. Steam turbines, compressors, deaerators,
and moisture separators are considered because of their relevance to the operation and long-term
performance of the thermal interface. For each component, the analysis identifies the governing
relationships, candidate sensors, residual definitions, and expected fault signatures. The component
models are then used to formulate the sensor-assignment problem. The purpose of this step is to determine
whether the available measurements provide sufficient analytical redundancy to detect the selected faults
and discriminate among competing fault hypotheses. The resulting sensor set is assessed through the PRO-
AID sensor-placement optimization, while the Dymola model provides a dynamic simulation capability
for future validation against representative nominal and degraded operating scenarios.

 The remainder of this report is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the reference thermal-
interface configuration, the system-level Ebsilon and Dymola models, the FMEA-based selection of
failure modes, and their implementation in the dynamic model. Section 3 presents the PRO-AID
component models and associated fault signatures. Section 4 assesses the selected sensor set using the
generated failure data. Section 5 summarizes the main conclusions and identifies directions for future
development.

2. System-Level Modeling of the Thermal Interface for Failure-Scenario
 Data Generation
2.1. Reference Configuration of the Thermal Interface
 This section summarizes the main features of the reference thermal-interface design developed and
optimized in [1]. The selected configuration provides the basis for the dynamic modeling and failure-
scenario analyses presented in this work. The system couples a power-conversion and industrial heat-
delivery interface to the GE Hitachi PRISM sodium-cooled fast reactor (SFR). The reference nuclear plant
comprises two PRISM Mod-A reactor modules, each rated at 425 MW th, for a total reactor thermal power
of 850 MWth. PRISM is a pool-type reactor that uses liquid sodium as the primary coolant. Sodium enters
the core at approximately 360 °C and exits at approximately 499 °C. Heat is transferred from the
radioactive primary sodium to the power-conversion system through two intermediate sodium loops,
thereby providing an additional safety barrier. Each loop circulates approximately 2,200 kg/s of sodium
and supplies heat to a steam generator (SG) while maintaining hot- and cold-leg temperatures of
approximately 477 °C and 326 °C, respectively. In addition to isolating the secondary system from the
primary coolant, the intermediate loops reduce the direct coupling between reactor operation and
secondary-side transients.

Figure 2-1. Best-performing mitigated BOP architecture. Red highlights indicate components modified by the
applied mitigation strategies.

 The BOP is based on a superheated Rankine cycle derived from the PRISM reference design.
Feedwater at approximately 216 °C is supplied to two helical-coil SGs, where it is heated and superheated
to approximately 452 °C at 147 bar. The steam then expands through a multistage turbine train comprising
high-pressure (HP), intermediate-pressure (IP), and low-pressure (LP) sections. A moisture separator-
reheater (MSR) is located between the HP and IP sections to maintain adequate steam quality and protect
downstream turbine components from excessive moisture. After expansion, the steam is condensed and
returned to the SGs through a regenerative feedwater system that includes low- and high-pressure
feedwater heaters (FWHs) and a deaerator. In the integrated energy-system configuration, the conventional
Rankine cycle is coupled to a high-temperature vapor-compression heat pump (VCHP). Steam extracted
downstream of the initial turbine expansion is compressed to supercritical conditions and heated to
approximately 660 °C to meet the requirements of an external industrial process. This arrangement enables
the delivery of more than 350 MWth of high-quality process heat while supplying the heat-pump power
demand and retaining a limited but valuable electrical output from the power cycle. The VCHP thus
provides the thermal link between the nuclear plant and high-temperature industrial applications that
would otherwise rely on fossil-fuel combustion. The resulting BOP layout is shown in Figure 2-1.

2.2. Steady-State System-Level Model of the Thermal Interface in Ebsilon
 A steady-state model of the reference thermal-interface configuration described in Section 2.1 was
developed in Ebsilon Professional. To reduce model complexity while preserving the overall
thermodynamic behavior, the two reactor modules and their associated intermediate sodium loops were
represented by a single equivalent heat source coupled to an equivalent steam generator. The model was
sized and calibrated using the nominal design conditions summarized in Table 2-1.

 The reactor thermal power was fixed at 850 MWth (2 × 425 MWth modules), while the steam cycle was
designed for a turbine inlet condition of 452.0 °C and 147 bar, with a total steam flow rate of 380 kg/s and
a feedwater temperature of 216.0 °C at the steam-generator inlet. These conditions were maintained as
boundary conditions during the steady-state model design. The industrial process was modeled as an
equivalent heat sink. The process itself was not represented explicitly; instead, its interaction with the
thermal interface was captured through prescribed outlet conditions, namely saturated steam at a fixed
pressure of 200 bar. Heat exchangers were modeled using prescribed thermal and hydraulic design

constraints. The steam generator was characterized by a cold-side pressure drop of 4 bar. Feedwater heaters
and reheaters were modeled with an upper terminal temperature difference (UTTD) of 5 °C, a hot-side
pressure drop of 0.1 bar, and a cold-side pressure drop of 1 bar. Other component performance parameters
adopted in the model are summarized in Table 2-2. The model incorporates several operational constraints
to ensure consistency with equipment limitations and established steam-cycle design practices. These
include a maximum deaerator pressure of 20 bar, a minimum condenser pressure of 0.07 bar, minimum
steam-quality limits of 0.90 at the HP and IP turbine outlets and 0.85 at the LP turbine outlet, and a
maximum VCHP discharge pressure of 345 bar (Table 2-3).

 Particular attention was given to moisture control within the turbine train. Because the VCHP returns
steam from the industrial process at approximately 200 bar, direct expansion through the turbine train
would drive the flow rapidly toward the saturation region and produce excessive moisture in the
downstream stages. This would reduce turbine efficiency and increase the risk of blade erosion and
mechanical damage. To mitigate these effects, the BOP incorporates multiple moisture-separation and
reheating stages. The separators remove liquid droplets formed during expansion, while the reheaters
restore the steam temperature before admission to the subsequent turbine sections. These components play
a more critical role than in a conventional Rankine cycle because they enable the high-pressure return
stream from the integrated heat pump to be expanded while maintaining acceptable steam quality. As
illustrated in Figure 2-2, direct expansion of the 200-bar saturated-steam return without intermediate
moisture separation and reheating would penetrate deeply into the two-phase region, whereas the staged
configuration limits moisture formation in the downstream turbine sections.

Figure 2-2. Qualitative T–s diagram of the staged turbine expansion with moisture separation and reheating.

 The resulting arrangement provides a practical balance between thermodynamic performance and
equipment protection, enabling operation at the pressure and temperature levels required for industrial heat
delivery while maintaining acceptable steam quality throughout the expansion process. The associated
control strategy was designed to preserve these operating conditions across the thermal interface.
Feedwater temperature upstream of the SG is regulated through a live-steam extraction line, while a
dedicated reheating-steam flow maintains the MSR outlet temperature near its nominal value and ensures
suitable steam conditions at the turbine inlets. Together, these control actions maintain compliance with
the thermal-hydraulic limits of the steam generator and turbine train while supporting the simultaneous
production of electricity and high-temperature process heat. These moisture-management provisions,
control strategies, boundary conditions, and operational constraints formed the basis for evaluating
alternative thermal-interface configurations. The candidate designs incorporated different mitigation
measures intended to reduce the effects of component aging and performance degradation and were
assessed using the PRIORITY methodology. This approach combines degradation-aware performance
modeling with resilience-based ranking metrics to identify the most robust system architecture.

2.3. Dynamic System-Level Model of the Thermal Interface in Dymola
 One of the principal capabilities of PRO-AID is real-time health monitoring in support of condition-
based maintenance and asset-management decisions. Starting from Piping and Instrumentation Diagram
(P&ID) of the system, PRO-AID constructs physics-based diagnostic models and evaluates the
consistency of measurements acquired from physical and virtual sensors [3]. Because the thermal-interface
configuration considered in this study is still in the design phase, no experimental data are available to
assess its monitoring and diagnostic performance. Representative sensor data must therefore be generated
numerically under both healthy and degraded operating conditions. Many of the degradation mechanisms
considered in this work evolve slowly and can be approximated as sequences of quasi-steady operating
states. For mechanisms such as component-efficiency loss or heat-exchanger fouling, a steady-state

simulator such as Ebsilon can provide suitable synthetic datasets. Other faults, however, introduce
unmeasured mass or energy transfers, as in the case of fluid leakage and distributed heat loss. Simulating
these scenarios while maintaining physically consistent transient responses requires a more flexible
modeling environment. A dynamic model of the selected BOP configuration was therefore developed in
Dymola to reproduce the plant response under nominal and degraded conditions and generate the synthetic
sensor data required for the PRO-AID assessment.

 The Dymola model was developed using components from the Modelica Standard Library and the
ThermoPower library, with selected models modified to represent the degradation mechanisms considered
in this study. The resulting model reproduces the overall BOP architecture implemented in Ebsilon and
was parameterized to match the nominal operating conditions defined in Section 2.2. The main differences
between the two tools concern the representation of the heat exchangers, valves, and heat-pump
compressor. These differences are minor and do not affect the intended use of the Dymola model, which
is to generate physically consistent synthetic sensor data representing the system response under fault
conditions. In Dymola, the heat exchangers are represented using one-dimensional distributed-parameter
models sized to reproduce the nominal design conditions. Single-phase heat transfer is evaluated using the

Dittus–Boelter correlation, while a constant heat-transfer coefficient is assumed under two-phase
conditions. Pressure losses are modeled through a quadratic hydraulic-resistance relation.
All heat exchangers are modeled as shell-and-tube units using the common geometric parameters
summarized in Table 2-4. The two helical-coil steam generators are modeled as a single equivalent heat
exchanger supplied by a linearly distributed temperature source representing the thermal power transferred
from the intermediate sodium loops. The industrial-process heat exchanger is represented using the same
heat-exchanger formulation. The remaining BOP components are modeled using standard or modified
ThermoPower components, as appropriate for the present analysis. The main assumptions adopted for the
valves, pumps, deaerator, condenser, steam turbines, heat-pump compressor, moisture separator, and
electric generator are summarized in Table 2-5. A representation of the BOP graphic interface
implemented in Dymola is shown in Figure 2-3.

 A limited set of control systems was implemented to reproduce the principal operating logic of the
thermal interface and the mitigation strategies considered in this study. The control architecture relies
primarily on proportional (P) and proportional–integral (PI) controllers. A proportional controller
regulates the thermal power delivered to the industrial process by adjusting the heat extracted from the
high-temperature steam at the heat-pump outlet. This control action maintains the steam returning to the
power-conversion system near the prescribed saturated condition. Several PI control loops were also
implemented to maintain the principal operating conditions of the integrated BOP. The turbine inlet
pressure is regulated by manipulating the opening of the turbine admission valve, while the deaerator
pressure is controlled by varying the rotational speed of the low-pressure feedwater pump. The thermal
power extracted from the steam generator is regulated by adjusting the feedwater-flow-rate setpoint of the
redundant high-pressure feedwater pumps. A lower-level PI controller adjusts the rotational speed of each
pump to satisfy the prescribed flow demand, whereas a supervisory PI controller modifies the individual
pump setpoints according to their estimated health condition. In this way, the hydraulic load is
redistributed from degraded units while preserving both the required feedwater flow rate and the desired

2.5. Modeling of Degradation Mechanisms in the Dynamic System-Level Simulator
 To generate synthetic datasets for assessing PRO-AID health-monitoring capabilities, the Dymola
model was extended to represent the degradation mechanisms introduced in Section 2.4. Because the
standard Modelica and ThermoPower component models do not include built-in fault or degradation
formulations, selected component models were modified accordingly. For each selected failure mode, one
or more component health or performance parameters, whose progressive variation provides a
representative description of the underlying physical degradation mechanism, were identified. Examples
include mechanical and isentropic efficiencies for rotating machinery and overall heat-transfer coefficients
for heat exchangers. Each scenario was generated by activating the selected degradation mechanism at a
predefined simulation time and prescribing a gradual variation of the corresponding health or performance
parameter from its nominal value to the degraded value summarized in Table 2-7. The variation was
implemented through an accelerated ramp profile. The ramp duration was selected to be slow relative to
the characteristic dynamics of the plant, so that the simulated response represents a progressive loss of
component health rather than an abrupt failure. However, the adopted ramp durations are not intended to
reproduce the actual timescales of component aging, fouling, wear, or erosion, which would typically
occur over much longer operating periods. Instead, the ramps are accelerated to make the system response
observable within a practical simulation time while preserving a gradual transition between nominal and
degraded conditions. Some degradation mechanisms, such as external leakage and distributed heat loss,
required dedicated implementations because they directly modify the mass or energy balances of the
affected components. For these cases, the corresponding Modelica equations were extended to account for
the additional mass or energy exchange introduced by the fault.
 Dedicated fault implementations were also developed for selected components whose degradation
could not be represented by a simple parameter ramp. For the vapor-compression heat-pump compressor,
the ThermoPower model calculates isentropic efficiency and pressure ratio from characteristic maps as
functions of corrected shaft speed and corrected mass flow rate. To represent aerodynamic degradation
while preserving the compressor off-design behavior, multiplicative penalty factors were applied to the
map-based efficiency and pressure-ratio values. These factors progressively reduce the predicted
performance without modifying the underlying map formulation. The feedwater-pump blockage scenario
was treated similarly as a special case. Rather than degrading the pump model directly, the fault was
simulated by progressively reducing the opening of the valve located immediately downstream of the
pump. This increases the hydraulic resistance and reproduces the pressure losses associated with a partial
flow obstruction while preserving the pump characteristics. The valve opening was adjusted using the
same accelerated ramp formulation adopted for the other fault scenarios.

3. Development of PRO-AID Component Models
 The simulation data corresponding to the selected failure scenarios at the thermal interface are
generated with the Dymola model described in Section 2.3. Their diagnostic interpretation requires
component-level PRO-AID models that relate measured process variables to fault indicators through
governing equations, constitutive or performance relationships, and residuals. The following subsections
describe the PRO-AID models developed for the steam turbine, compressor, deaerator, and moisture
separator. For each component, governing equations representing its physical behavior are identified and
used to construct residuals that quantify violations of the underlying constitutive relationships. For each
performance parameter of interest, two independent estimates are calculated: a measurement-based
estimate, reconstructed from the sensor data and the governing equations (𝑝 ), and a model-based
estimate, obtained without directly using the same set of measurements (𝑝 ). The residual is defined
as the difference between these two estimates (Eq.(1)).

 𝑟= 𝑝 − 𝑝 (1)

The analytical redundancy required for residual generation depends on the component and parameter
considered. The governing equations may be sufficient to reconstruct the parameter from available
measurements, but they do not always provide an independent prediction. If the parameter is
approximately constant over the operating range, its nominal value can be used as the predicted estimate;
if it varies significantly with operating conditions, an additional predictive model is required. In that case,
the parameter is expressed as a function of measurable process variables, and the model coefficients are
identified from data collected under healthy conditions. The calibrated model then provides the expected
parameter value for comparison with the measurement-based estimate. Deviations of the generated
residuals from their nominal values define patterns of active and inactive residuals, which constitute the
fault signatures used for detection, discrimination, and isolation of component faults.
Some components were represented using simplified PRO-AID models rather than dedicated physics-
based diagnostic models. In particular, the steam generator and reheaters were modeled as ideal heaters
with a prescribed heat input. Although PRO-AID provides a generic counter-current shell-and-tube heat
exchanger model, this representation was not considered sufficiently detailed to capture the thermal
behavior of these components. The adopted simplified models therefore allow the generation of mass-
balance residuals for leakage detection but do not explicitly represent heat-transfer degradation
mechanisms. By contrast, no dedicated PRO-AID model was developed for the condenser. Owing to the
complexity of the condensation process, the condenser was instead represented as a process sink providing
the boundary conditions for the downstream condensate line. Consequently, no condenser-specific
residuals were generated, and condenser faults were not considered in the diagnostic analysis presented in
this work. To support the interpretation of the component diagrams and residual equations presented in

3.1. Steam Turbine Model
3.1.1. Modeling Assumptions
 The steam turbine is assumed to belong to a single-shaft, multistage train that may include HP, IP, and
LP sections. For diagnostic purposes, however, these physical sections cannot always be represented as
single lumped components. Steam extractions, leakages, or other intermediate flow paths may cause the
mass flow rate to vary between the inlet and outlet of a section, making its boundaries unsuitable for a
simple component-level mass and energy balance. The turbine train is therefore divided into diagnostic
segments, each defined as a control volume with one identifiable inlet, one identifiable outlet, and a
nominally continuous mass-flow path. Depending on the turbine layout and available instrumentation, a
PRO-AID segment may coincide with an entire turbine section or represent only a portion of it. Within
this diagnostic control volume, the nominal design mass flow rate is assumed to be constant, while
deviations from the expected mass-flow, pressure-flow, or enthalpy-drop relationships are interpreted as
possible fault indicators. The model also includes the mechanical connection to the turbine shaft. Multiple
turbine segments may be mounted on the same shaft and therefore share the same rotational speed. Shaft
speed and power are assumed to be available only at the turbine-train or generator level and are therefore
not used for segment-level diagnostics.

3.1.2. Residual Definitions
Three main parameters can be adopted for the residual generation of the steam turbine model:

 Turbine flow coefficient, 𝑲𝒕 : it combines the effects of nozzle area, blade-passage area, discharge
 coefficient, and flow-path geometry. It can therefore be treated primarily as a function of turbine-stage
 geometry, which remains fixed under healthy conditions. Although 𝐾 also exhibits some dependence
 on fluid properties, Reynolds number, and moisture content, these effects are generally small and
 become more relevant only at low load. The underlying assumption is that fault-induced changes in
 flow capacity are significantly larger than the variations observed during steady operation at a fixed
 power level.
 Isentropic efficiency, 𝜼𝒊𝒔𝒐 : it is a performance parameter that accounts for irreversibilities during the
 expansion process. Under steady, constant-load conditions, it reflects the combined effect of dominant
 loss mechanisms, including profile losses, tip-leakage losses, disc-friction and windage losses, and
 moisture-related losses. These mechanisms reduce the available shaft work and are present even under
 healthy operating conditions. Although their relative contribution may vary with load and
 environmental conditions, such variations are assumed to be small compared with those produced by
 component faults when the turbine operates at approximately constant load.
 Mechanical efficiency, 𝜼𝒎𝒆𝒄𝒉 : it accounts for losses occurring after the steam has transferred energy
 to the rotor. It depends mainly on shaft speed and on the condition of bearings, seals, lubrication
 systems, and mechanical transmission components. When rotational speed is constant or varies only
 slightly, changes in 𝜂 are assumed to be primarily fault-induced.

coefficient and the turbine efficiencies may vary with load in practice, neglecting this dependence provides

3.2. Compressor Model
3.2.1. Modeling Assumptions

diagnostic segments. Each segment is represented as a lumped control volume with one flow inlet and one

aggregate quantities such as mass flow rate, pressure and enthalpy rise, efficiency, and shaft power. The

pressure ratio (𝑃𝑅) and isentropic efficiency (𝜂 ) are represented as functions of corrected rotational speed
and corrected mass flow rate using interpolating functions calibrated from healthy operating data. Multiple
compressor segments may share the same shaft and rotational speed. Because shaft speed and motor power
are generally measured only for the complete compressor train, these quantities are not directly available
for segment-level diagnostics.

as vibration, blade-passing-frequency, or acoustic-emission data.

These binary signatures represent the dominant residual response associated with each fault family. They

3.4.1. Modeling Assumptions
 The moisture separator is represented as a lumped control volume with uniform thermodynamic
conditions whose behavior is described through the inlet, steam-outlet, and drain conditions (Figure 3-4).

internal flow field nor local separation phenomena are resolved.

The model also assumes steady-state operation, so no mass or energy accumulation occurs within the

4.1. Sensor-Placement Objective Function and Diagnostic-Value Weights
 The sensor-assignment problem is formulated as a mixed-integer optimization problem in which
binary decision variables represent the presence or absence of sensors at candidate locations. The objective
is to identify the sensor configuration that satisfies the specified diagnostic requirements while minimizing
the total cost. In principle, the objective function should account for both the cost of the selected sensors
and the diagnostic value they provide, including fault detectability, localization capability, and sensitivity.
In the present work, a simplified formulation is adopted (Eq.(23)),

4.2. Sensor-Placement Optimization Procedure and Results
 From the FMEA, the faults of primary interest for the system under consideration are identified [1].
These faults define the diagnostic objectives of the sensor-placement optimization. The problem is then
posed in inverse form:

 Given a set of faults considered important for ensuring continuity of service, the objective is to identify
 the sensor configuration that best balances diagnostic performance against sensor procurement and
 installation costs.

The sensor-placement procedure follows the PRO-AID sensor-assignment framework, in which candidate
sensors are evaluated according to their ability to support residual generation, fault detectability, and fault
isolation. Accordingly, the problem is treated as a constrained optimization task that seeks the sensor
configuration providing the best balance between diagnostic value and sensor cost. Because the number
of possible sensor combinations grows rapidly with the number of candidate measurements, exhaustive
evaluation becomes impractical. In the present work, PRO-AID is used to construct and evaluate the
diagnostic relationships associated with each candidate sensor set, and the resulting sensor-placement
problem is solved using the Genetic Algorithm (GA) implemented in PRO-AID [7][8].
In this work, the sensor-placement optimization was carried out using two complementary
implementations. First, the BOP was divided into two interconnected optimization domains, which were
optimized separately and then recombined. This partitioned implementation reduced the computational
burden and allowed the selected sensor configurations to be reviewed on systems of manageable
complexity. Second, the optimization was repeated by treating the complete BOP as a single optimization
domain. This whole-system implementation was used to verify the results of the partitioned approach and
to evaluate whether additional fault-isolation capability could be obtained by considering measurement
correlations across the full system.
In the partitioned implementation, the BOP was divided into the following two domains:

  First BOP domain: this domain covers the high-pressure and industrial-process portion of the
 thermal interface, extending from the deaerator outlet through the high-pressure feedwater pumps

 (HP-FWP), high-pressure feedwater heater (HP-FWH), steam generator (SG), high-pressure
 turbine (HP-ST) train, vapor-compression heat pump (HP-COMP), industrial-process heat
 exchanger (IP-HX), and the downstream interstage moisture separators (IMS), before rejoining the
 Rankine cycle at the inlet of the reheating section. The redundant turbine admission valve was
 excluded from the optimization domain because control-valve faults were outside the diagnostic
 objectives of the present analysis.
  Second BOP domain: this domain covers the downstream Rankine-cycle portion of the thermal
 interface, extending from the reheating (RH) section through the low-pressure turbine (LP-ST)
 train, condenser outlet, low-pressure feedwater pump (LP-FWP), low-pressure feedwater heater
 (LP-FWH), and deaerator (DEA).

This partitioned approach reduced the computational burden while allowing the optimization results to be
reviewed on systems of manageable complexity and checked for consistency at the interface between the
two domains. For each optimization domain, the fault list was limited to the degradation mechanisms
relevant to the included components and previously identified as important contributors to BOP
performance degradation [1]. The same workflow was followed for both optimization domains. First, the
P&ID representation of each subsystem was used to define the plant topology, the diagnostic loops
available to PRO-AID, and the initial pool of potential sensor locations. This initial pool included all
measurements that could realistically be installed on the process equipment.

 For the partitioned optimization presented here, the initial pool was then pre-screened before execution
of the GA to reduce the computational burden associated with the combinatorial search. This step was
introduced as a practical measure to limit runtime and computational-resource requirements, not to impose
diagnostic preferences on the solution. Measurements were excluded from the candidate pool only when
they were outside the fault scope considered in the corresponding domain or when they were expected to
provide equivalent diagnostic information to nearby measurements for the purposes of the present analysis.
Before executing the optimization, fault detectability with the pre-screened candidate sensor set was
verified using the PRO-AID sensor-analysis tool. This verification was performed one fault at a time to
confirm that each considered degradation mechanism could be detected with the available measurements.
Sensor-placement optimization was carried out only after the complete fault set had been confirmed to be
detectable. For each BOP partition, several GA runs were performed with progressively increasing
population sizes and numbers of generations until convergence was achieved. Convergence was assessed
by monitoring the objective function and was considered reached when the minimum cost remained
unchanged over successive generations. The GA parameters used for both the partitioned-domain
optimizations and the whole-BOP optimization are reported in Table 4-4.
After optimization, the selected sensor sets were reviewed to identify any measurements retained by the
solver that did not provide additional diagnostic value for the fault set considered in the corresponding
domain. In this application, this post-processing step was used as a practical refinement of the partitioned
optimization results, not as a substitute for convergence of the GA search. In general, when the candidate
sensor set accurately reflects the intended diagnostic scope and the GA search is allowed to converge fully,
manual post-processing should not be necessary.

For the first BOP domain, the considered fault set included the following mechanisms:

  Fouling of the HP FWH on both the hot and cold sides;
  Leakage, blade fouling and erosion, blade degradation, and moisture-related losses in each HP
 turbine stage;
  Blade fouling and blade damage in the vapor-compression heat pump;
  Efficiency degradation of the moisture separators.

In this domain, 200 potential sensor locations distributed among the instrumented components are
considered. After preliminary screening, candidate measurements that were immediately adjacent to other
measurements or associated with fault mechanisms not considered in the present study were removed.
This reduced the candidate set to 93 measurements. From this pool, the GA initially selected 54 sensors.
A subsequent post-processing review removed five selected measurements that did not provide additional
diagnostic value for the identified set of faults:

  the temperature and pressure measurements at the SG inlet, whose information was already
 available from the corresponding measurements at the HP-FWH outlet;
  the current measurement associated with HP-FWP-2, because pump faults were not included in
 this optimization domain;
  the flow measurement at the HP-ST-4 inlet, whose information was already captured by the
 corresponding measurement at IMS-2;
  the mechanical power sensor at the HP-COMP shaft, because no power-related faults were
 included in the analysis.

After this post-processing step, the final sensor set for the first BOP portion consists of 49 sensors (Figure
4-1). The final set detects 32 of the 33 degradation scenarios considered in this domain. Among them, only
the leakage in the first HP steam turbine can be uniquely isolated, whereas the remaining 31 faults are
detectable but not uniquely distinguishable because they produce identical residual-activation patterns
under the adopted component diagnostic models. The only undetected fault is the efficiency degradation
of the fifth moisture separator, identified as IMS-5 in Figure 4-1. This result is primarily explained by the

economic weighting adopted in the objective function: detecting this fault would require additional vapor-
quality instrumentation, with each sensor costing $20,000, whereas the diagnostic benefit assigned to the
fault is limited to $17,500. Consequently, the GA favors a lower-cost sensor configuration that does not
detect this single, relatively low-value fault while preserving coverage of the remaining degradation
scenarios.

 The post-processing review also supported interpretation of the selected configuration in terms of the
fault signatures defined in the PRO-AID component models. For the HP-FWP branches, the optimizer
retained only a limited subset of branch measurements, primarily temperature and selected flow
measurements, rather than fully instrumenting each pump. This is consistent with the hydraulic
connectivity of the parallel branches, where several flow and pressure quantities can be inferred from
neighboring measurements and mass-conservation relationships. Similar patterns are observed for the HP-
FWH, turbine train, vapor-compression heat pump, and moisture separators. For the HP-FWH, the retained
measurements are those most directly related to heat-transfer degradation. For the HP turbine stages, the
selected configuration emphasizes inlet and outlet pressure measurements, together with steam-quality
measurements where required by the two-phase formulation, because these quantities directly enter the
Stodola-flow and isentropic-efficiency residuals. For the vapor-compression heat pump, pressure,
temperature, and rotational-speed measurements are retained because of their direct sensitivity to
compressor degradation. For the moisture separators, the algorithm preferentially retains steam-quality
measurements, which are directly affected by separation-efficiency degradation, together with selected
condensate-flow measurements, resulting in a non-uniform instrumentation pattern that reflects the
contribution of each measurement to diagnostic coverage.

  Leakage and heat-loss faults in the Deaerator;
  Fouling of the LP FWH on both the shell and tube sides;
  Blade fouling and erosion, blade degradation, and moisture-related losses in each LP turbine stage.

The second domain contains a total of 91 potential sensor locations distributed among the instrumented
components. Following the same preliminary screening procedure adopted for the first domain, candidate

from the present study were removed from the optimization problem. The number of candidate
measurements was then reduced to 62, from which the optimization algorithm selected 28 sensors. A
subsequent post-processing review identified three additional sensors associated with the reheating stage
that did not contribute to the diagnostic objectives of the present study:

  The temperature sensor at the RH-2 cold-side inlet.
  The temperature sensors at the RH-1 hot side inlet and outlet.

The final optimized sensor set consists of 25 sensors (Figure 4-2). The sensor set provides detection of all
16 degradation scenarios considered in this domain. None of the faults can be uniquely isolated and, as in
the first domain, this indicates that the limiting factor is not the number of retained sensors, but the
diagnostic similarity among residual signatures generated by the selected fault mechanisms. The optimized
sensor set is also consistent with the physical behavior of the monitored components. Temperature
measurements are retained only at selected turbine inlets, while most flow measurements were not selected
because they can be reconstructed through mass conservation and the continuity of the steam path.

 As for the HP turbine train discussed previously, the retained pressure measurements provide the
information required by the Stodola flow relationship and the isentropic-efficiency residuals, whereas
additional flow and temperature measurements offer limited independent diagnostic information. A similar
trend is observed for the LP feedwater heater. Since the considered degradation mechanisms are associated
with heat-transfer performance, the selected temperature and flow measurements provide sufficient
information to monitor the component without requiring complete instrumentation of both the shell and
tube sides. Only a limited number of measurements are retained within the condensate line, because the
remaining thermodynamic variables can be inferred from adjacent instrumentation. The Deaerator
represents the only component for which the optimization retains an extensive instrumentation set.
Temperature, pressure, flow, and liquid-level measurements are all selected because they directly support

the mass- and energy-balance residuals required to detect both leakage and heat-loss faults. Unlike the
remaining components, the Deaerator diagnostic model relies on the simultaneous reconstruction of
inventory and energy balance, making these measurements essential for maintaining complete fault
detectability.

 As a final step, the optimization was repeated with the complete BOP treated as a single optimization
domain. This whole-system optimization was performed (1) to verify whether the partitioned approach
affected the resulting sensor configuration and (2) to evaluate whether additional fault-isolation capability
could be obtained when measurement correlations across the entire BOP were considered simultaneously.
Because of the substantially larger search space, the candidate set was reduced from the initial pool of 291
potential sensor locations to 166 candidate measurements. To accommodate the larger optimization
problem and improve GA convergence, the number of generations was increased to 35, along with the
tournament size and crossover probability. The considered set of faults in the whole-BOP optimization
includes the following degradation mechanisms:

  Leakage and heat-loss faults in the Deaerator;
  Fouling of the LP- and HP feedwater heaters on both the shell and tube sides;
  Leakage, blade fouling and erosion, blade degradation and moisture-related losses in each HP
 steam turbine stage;
  Blade fouling and erosion, blade degradation, and moisture-related losses in each LP steam turbine
 stage;
  Blade fouling and blade damage in the heat pump compressor;
  Efficiency degradation in each intermediate moisture separator.

Under these conditions, the optimization selected 84 sensors, as shown in Figure 4-3. This configuration
preserves the same overall fault detectability obtained with the partitioned approach. As in the previous
analyses, the only degradation mechanism that remains undetected is the efficiency degradation of the fifth
moisture separator, confirming that its exclusion is a consequence of the adopted cost-benefit weighting
rather than of the domain partitioning. No additional sensors were removed from the whole-BOP
configuration during post-processing.
The selected set of sensors also confirms the measurement-selection rationale observed in the two
subsystem optimizations. Measurements directly related to the governing diagnostic equations are
systematically retained, whereas measurements whose information can be reconstructed from mass and
energy balances or inferred from neighboring components are generally not selected. Pressure
measurements remain dominant throughout both the HP and LP turbine trains because of their direct role
in the Stodola-flow relationship and the isentropic-efficiency residuals. Similarly, the compressor retains
pressure, temperature, flow, and rotational-speed measurements, whereas the selected heat-exchanger
instrumentation is limited to the measurements required to capture fouling-induced thermal-performance
degradation. As in the partitioned optimization, steam-quality measurements are preferentially retained
for the intermediate moisture separators, reflecting their high diagnostic sensitivity to separator-efficiency
degradation. Compared with the partitioned optimization, the whole-BOP optimization provides a

significant improvement in fault isolation. In addition to uniquely identifying leakage in the first high-
pressure turbine stage, the complete configuration uniquely isolates leakage faults affecting the remaining
high-pressure turbine stages, fouling of both high-pressure feedwater heaters, and the heat-loss fault in the
deaerator. This improvement occurs because the integrated optimization simultaneously considers
measurements distributed throughout the entire plant, thereby exploiting additional analytical redundancy
across subsystem boundaries. These results confirm the value of the proposed partitioning methodology:
dividing the BOP into two optimization domains preserves overall fault detectability while substantially
reducing optimization complexity, whereas the whole-system optimization demonstrates that additional
fault-isolation capability can be achieved when cross-domain measurement correlations are available.

Conclusions
 This technical report presented a model-based workflow for sensor-placement optimization in support
of PRO-AID-based health diagnostics for the thermal-interface configuration studied and optimized in
FY25. That configuration couples the Rankine-cycle portion of the BOP with a high-temperature heat
pump and an industrial-process heat-delivery connection. Building on that configuration, the present
activity applied the PRO-AID diagnostic framework to identify the measurements needed to detect and
diagnose the degradation mechanisms of primary interest. The reference thermal-interface configuration
was implemented in Dymola to provide a dynamic representation of the BOP architecture and to create a
simulation environment in which the selected degradation mechanisms can be represented explicitly. The
model incorporates the component failures identified through the FMEA-based selection process, thereby
establishing the infrastructure needed to generate representative nominal and degraded operating
scenarios. Although the optimized sensor configurations were not validated against Dymola-generated
data in the present activity, the model provides an enabling capability for future assessment against
simulated single- and multi-fault scenarios.

 Component-level diagnostic models were developed for PRO-AID for the steam turbine, compressor,
deaerator, and moisture separator. For each component, the governing relationships, measurable
quantities, diagnostic residuals, and expected fault signatures were defined. These models provided the
analytical structure needed to formulate the sensor-assignment problem as a minimum-cost optimization
constrained by diagnostic requirements. The sensor-placement optimization was first performed using two
interconnected BOP domains, which were optimized separately and then recombined. This partitioned
approach reduced the computational burden while preserving detectability for all but one of the considered
degradation scenarios. The only undetected mechanism was the efficiency degradation of the fifth
moisture separator, for which the assigned diagnostic benefit was lower than the cost of the additional
vapor-quality instrumentation required for detection. A subsequent whole-BOP optimization confirmed
the same detectability result, retained 84 sensors out of 291 potential sensor locations, and improved fault
isolation by exploiting measurement correlations across subsystem boundaries.

 Overall, the work fulfilled the milestone objective by adapting a physics-based PRO-AID capability
to support the design of a sensor set that meets diagnostic needs for the thermal interface. The developed
workflow connects FMEA-based fault selection, component-level diagnostic modeling, sensor-placement
optimization, and whole-system diagnostic assessment. Future work should use the Dymola model to
generate nominal and degraded simulation data, including multi-fault scenarios, and validate the optimized
sensor configurations against those datasets. Additional efforts should focus on refining selected fault
implementations, expanding the component-model library, quantifying diagnostic performance under
uncertainty, and improving fault isolation where residual-signature ambiguity remains.
