Biological Determinants and non-pharmacological management of orthodontic pain
Authors: Yifan Wang, Shaoling Yu, Yubing Zhang, Xinlong Yang, Ting OuYang, Gaiping Shi, Lili Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOrthodontic pain remains a significant barrier to patient compliance, affecting up to 95% of individuals. This review provides a comprehensive analysis of the multifactorial nature of this pain, encompassing demographic, systemic, intraoral, and treatment-related variables. Furthermore, the paper critically evaluates a spectrum of management strategies beyond traditional pharmacotherapy, assessing their clinical efficacy and quality of evidence. Based on the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework, non-pharmacological alternatives show varying levels of support: low-level laser therapy (LLLT) and chewing therapy are backed by moderate-certainty evidence, whereas vibration and emerging psychological interventions currently rely on low-certainty evidence. By identifying key research gaps in optimizing these modern therapies, the review emphasizes the urgent need for larger, highly standardized clinical trials to develop personalized, evidence-based pain management protocols and improve the patient experience.Subject areas: medical science, dentistry, pain management in health technology

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization, malocclusion is the third most common oral health problem worldwide, following dental caries and periodontal diseas.1 However, orthodontic treatment, the primary method for correcting malocclusions, often diminishes patient comfort—affecting satisfaction, quality of life, and adherence to treatment plans. Studies show that 70–95% of orthodontic patients experience some degree of pain during treatment. 2,3,4 This discomfort can provoke negative emotions that, in turn, intensify the pai.5 Pain remains one of the leading causes of treatment discontinuatio,6 making research on orthodontic pain both critical and urgen.7,8

Orthodontic pain is broadly defined as any discomfort caused by orthodontic appliances, such as mucosal ulcers, tongue irritation, or gingival lesions. However, this review focuses on a narrower definition: dental pain resulting from orthodontic tooth movement (OTM). The intensity of this pain varies among individuals and is influenced by factors such as gender, age, and psychological condition.

When orthodontic forces are applied—such as during initial archwire placement, separator insertion, or banding—sensory receptors in the periodontal tissues are stimulated, triggering a cascade of nociceptive processes involving both the peripheral and central nervous systems. Pain typically begins soon after force application, peaks within 24 h, and gradually subsides within seven day.9,10 Effective pain management should therefore focus on this period. Current strategies include pharmacologic therapy, low-level laser therapy (LLLT), chewing exercises, vibration therapy, and psychological interventions.

In this review, distinct from previous reviews that address orthodontic pain (excluding appliance-related mucosal trauma or ulcerations) mechanisms or treatments in isolation, this comprehensive update (through 2026) provides a novel mechanistic integration of etiology, susceptibility factors, and therapeutic interventions. Our unique contribution lies in translating these complex biological interactions into actionable clinical practice: We synthesize individual variables to define clinically identifiable “high-risk phenotypes,” and propose a novel time-dependent, hierarchical management protocol driven by GRADE-appraised evidence. Ultimately, this review bridges foundational science with targeted clinical applications to shift pain management toward personalized care.

To support this framework, a comprehensive literature search and subsequent GRADE evidence appraisals were conducted. Detailed methodological procedures—including the search strategy, strict eligibility criteria, and the full evidence profile (which refined an initial 1,077 records to 225 included articles)—are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Factors related to orthodontic pain

Orthodontic pain results from the mechanical forces applied to teeth, which trigger inflammatory responses in the periodontal tissues, leading to tissue damage.5,6,11,12 Historically, pain intensity was thought to correlate exclusively with the extent of tissue damage. However, this explanation fails to account for the significant variability in pain perception across individuals. It is now widely recognized that pain perception is not only related to tissue damage but also to demographic characteristics, systemic factors, intraoral conditions, and treatment-related variables (see Figure 1).13,14,15

Demographic characteristics

Age

Clinical observations indicate that orthodontic pain displays distinct age-related characteristics: Adolescents (12–18 years) report the highest intensity, while adults (>18 years) experience intermediate but more protracted discomfort compared to children.16,17 Longitudinal data indicate a 3.23-point visual analog scale (VAS) increase per year of age in patients aged 8–1.18 Moreover, younger patients adapt rapidly as inflammation subsides, whereas adults often experience persistent, lower-intensity pain.19,20

To explain this, preclinical rodent models reveal that adolescent hypersensitivity can be attributed to transient peaks in periodontal sensory-nerve density and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) release, which lowers the mechanical stimuli threshol.21

Mechanistically, orthodontic forces induce a state resembling nerve injury in the trigeminal ganglion (TG)—characterized by the downregulation of potassium channels (Kcna2, Kcnj10) and upregulation of injury markers (Atf3). Atf3, in turn, significantly upregulates the expression of xanthine dehydrogenase (Xdh), thereby amplifying pain signals through the oxidative stress pathway.22,23 This burst of peripheral oxidative stress sensitization is the key mechanism underlying why young patients, compared to older patients, are more prone to severe pain and hyperalgesia during the pain peak.24

Conversely, the protracted, dull aching frequently observed in adult orthodontic patients is driven by a failure in post-injury tissue recovery rather than extreme initial sensitization. The age-related decline in brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)—alongside the molecular aging and functional decline of mesenchymal stem cells—delay the healing of compressed nerve fibers, severely prolonging nociceptive firin.25,26

Centrally, human neuroimaging (fMRI) confirms that these age-divergences profoundly shape clinical perception. Adolescents exhibit heightened connectivity between the neural pain signature (NPS) and the amygdala, clinically manifesting as amplified acute pain and greater vulnerability to dental anxiety (DA).27 Conversely, aging adults exhibit reduced connectivity between the periaqueductal gray (PAG) and insula/anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), compromising endogenous pain control.28

Gender

Translational evidence suggests that sex-based differences in orthodontic pain involve the hormonal modulation of nociceptive pathways. Estrogen enhances pain sensitivity by upregulating transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) and N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors in sensory neurons, facilitating both peripheral and central sensitization.29,30 Specifically, preclinical models indicate that estrogen increases CGRP expression in the TG through gene regulation, along with the synthesis of its receptor activity-modifying protein 1 - a key functional protein of the CGRP receptor to lower activation threshold.31,32 Conversely, androgens exert antinociceptive effects, potentially by downregulating TRPV,33 suppressing proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β), promoting M2 macrophage polarization34,35 and enhancing anti-inflammatory IL-10 expression.36

Despite these biological distinctions, clinical consensus is lacking: Some studies report that females experience more pain than male,16,37,38,39,40,41,42 while others find no significant difference.17,18,43,44 A recent meta-analysis by Sangalli et al. reviewed 40 clinical studies with nearly 3,000 participants and has provided a more comprehensive perspective on these conflicting findings. The authors emphasized that many previous studies lacked adequate sex-stratified analyses or statistical power to detect subtle differences, leading to low-to-moderate certainty of evidence.45 Thus, while hormonal mechanisms may influence nociception, the isolated clinical impact of sex appears minimal when controlling for confounders such as age and psychosocial factors, necessitating future multivariate investigations.

Interestingly, refined investigations suggest sex-age interactions better elucidate variability. Significant sex differences appear in late (14–17 years) rather than early (11–14 years) puberty,46 with pain intensity peaking in late-pubertal females and lowest in early-pubertal male.47

Furthermore, menstrual fluctuations also contribute.48 Elevated progesterone during the luteal phase amplifies inflammatory responses and sensitivity.49 Long et al. confirmed greater pain during the luteal versus the follicular phase,50 suggesting practitioners schedule initial procedures during the follicular phase to minimize discomfort.

Intraoral factors

Preclinical and in vitro models elucidate that poor oral hygiene initiates periodontal inflammation involving cytokine pathways distinct from orthodontic nociception.51,52 Bacterial endotoxin in dental plaque binds to Toll-like receptor 4 on the surface of immune cells, activating the NF-κB signaling pathway, initiating the transcription of genes including pro-IL-1β and the NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) itself. In periodontal tissues of patients with poor oral hygiene, substantial amounts of pro-IL-1β have already accumulated in the cytoplasm. Once triggered (by orthodontic forces), hypoxia and stress induce mitochondria to produce large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), while unstable lysosomes release cathepsins, promoting conformational changes in NLRP3. This recruits Pro-Caspase-1 to a massive multiprotein complex—the inflammasome, which rapidly cleaves accumulated pro-IL-1β into mature IL-1β for release, triggering an inflammatory storm.53,54

Regarding ion channels, inflammation-induced prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) activates protein kinase A (PKA), which phosphorylates TRPV1 serine residues (e.g., Ser116). This alters the intracellular conformation of TRPV1, preventing the channel desensitization. Moreover, cAMP-dependent PKA activation promotes the intracellular transport of TRPV1 to the membrane, directly promoting membrane trafficking, enhancing channel density, and open probability.55,56 TRPV1 is further sensitized by inflammatory RO,57 while bradykinin enhances Piezo-type mechanosensitive ion channel component 2 (Piezo2) mechanosensitivity, increasing susceptibility to mechanical force.58 Additionally, inflammation upregulates substance P (SP) synthesis and transport, leading to excessive neuropeptide release upon stimulatio.58

Transitioning to human cohorts, clinical observations confirm that compared to plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI) remains a superior predictive tool for initial orthodontic pain. Sum et al. demonstrated that baseline GI—but not PI—significantly correlates with elevated pain intensity, duration, and inflammatory markers post-activation. This distinction arises because GI captures persistent tissue sensitization rather than modifiable plaque accumulation.59 Emerging non-invasive salivary biomarkers, such as extracellular vesicle-associated bacterial DNA, may further aid in profiling this baseline periodontal status.60

Treatment factors

Clinical studies generally indicate fixed appliances (FAs) correlate with pain more than clear aligners (Cas).61,62,63,64 Preclinical and materials science models explain this difference: FA utilizes nickel-titanium (NiTi) archwires, which exhibit unique superelasticity. Their stress-strain curve features a long, flat unloading plateau. This sustained high stress level causes periodontal ischemia and hyalinization.65 Conversely, CA exhibit viscoelastic properties, simultaneously demonstrating characteristics of elastic solids (energy storage) and viscous fluids (energy dissipation) when deformed under force. When a patient first wears a new orthodontic appliance, the polymer chains within the material undergo instantaneous stretching, generating an initial peak stress. However, due to the effects of creep and stress relaxation, the material’s microstructure gradually rearranges to accommodate this deformation, causing the macroscopic force applied to the teeth to decrease rapidly.66,67

Returning to clinical observations, daily CA removal facilitates periodontal reperfusion (orthodontic pain is related to oral et al., 2019). The restored blood flow flushes algogenic mediators (e.g., PGE, IL-1β, and SP,68 which hinders central sensitization.69 As modern clinical practice and treatment planning are increasingly shaped by artificial intelligence and advanced data acquisition,70 CA can achieve highly precise sequential force delivery (sequential trays,71 which, along with their anxiety-reducing aesthetics, collectively mitigate discomfort.72

Regarding brackets, biomechanical theory suggests friction varies by ligation (traditional > active self-ligating > passive self-ligating). Traditional brackets use elastomeric ligatures or ligature wires to force the archwire tightly into the bracket groove. This generates a significant normal force, resulting in extremely high sliding friction resistance, necessitating greater initial force application. In contrast, passive self-ligating brackets (e.g., Damon): Their sliding cover design forms a rigid tube, exerting no active pressure on the archwire. Active self-locking brackets (e.g., SmartClip): Spring clips exert pressure on thicker archwires, resulting in friction levels intermediate between passive and traditional bracket.73,74,75

However, clinical studies report equivalent pain level.76,77,78,79 Because in clinical practice, teeth are often tilted or rotated. Once the archwire contacts the edge of the bracket groove (forming a contact angle), the resulting “binding force” increases exponentially. Burrow’s research indicates that binding accounts for at least 80% of the total resistanc,80 which depends on archwire stiffness and contact angle instead of ligation method. The actual resistance of self-ligating brackets in crowded arches is nearly identical to that of conventional bracket.80,81 Molecular analyses confirm this, showing no difference in SP expression between bracket type.82

For FA, pain intensity does not significantly vary between archwire materials in the clinical setting.83,84,85 Attempts to shorten treatment should not rely on excessive force. Time wearing braces was not significantly associated with pain intensity, suggesting that discomfort is driven more by individual sensitivity and activation-related force application/adjustments than by elapsed time in treatment.71

Systemic factors

Psychology

Extensive clinical and psychological evidence demonstrates that psychological factors significantly correlate with orthodontic pain. Before the treatment, patients with high treatment expectations (TEs) report more orthodontic pai,86 as anticipatory thoughts can amplify cortical responses to noxious stimuli.87 Treatment motivation (TM) also plays a crucial role: Patients experiencing persistent pain often report lower T,39,88 while those with strong TM and realistic TE tend to experience less pai.89 In general, low TM combined with high TE is predictive of greater pain.

During treatment, individuals with high levels of DA and pain catastrophizing (PC)—a negative cognitive-affective response to anticipated or actual pai90—are more likely to feel pain than those with low level.14,50,91,92 Moreover, high levels of anxiety can accelerate the pain trajectory, leading to increased discomfort more quickl.91,93

Human neuroimaging studies elucidate these phenomena, revealing that emotional states affect baseline resting-state functional connectivity between brain regions involved in pain processing, which in turn influences pain responses to trigeminal nociceptive input.94

In the clinical setting, a patient’s positive expectations (e.g., high TM and low TE) engage the endogenous opioid system, wherein the prefrontal cortex (PFC) activates the midbrain PAG to promote endorphin release. Therapeutically, this process produces clinical analgesia not only by blocking ascending pain signals from the spinal cord but also by suppressing activity in the insula and ACC—key regions dictating the patient’s subjective pain perception.95,96 Conversely, negative expectations (e.g., low TM and high TE) trigger an amygdala-driven amplification pathway. By transmitting excitatory signals to the PAG and dorsal ACC while stimulating cortisol release, this pathway collectively exacerbates the patient’s clinical pain experience.97,98

Physical activity

Clinical and epidemiological evidence suggests that regular physical activity (PA), in the form of exercise, can reduce pain perception by inducing exercise-related physiologic hypoalgesia99,100,101,102 and positively impact mental health.100,103,104 Orthodontic research indicates that higher levels of PA are associated with lower orthodontic pain and reduced analgesic consumption in patient.105,106 In patients with orofacial pain, aerobic exercise may reduce pain and disability, particularly in the short to medium term. 107

Preclinical animal models perfectly elucidate the mechanisms of this exercise-induced hypoalgesia (EIH). These studies show that EIH is primarily mediated by endogenous analgesic mechanisms involving endogenous analgesic substances and hormone.100,101 The reward/emotion system, with dopamine as the key neurotransmitter, plays a central role in neuropsychological integration during EIH. This system integrates pain perception through the mesocorticolimbic network (ventral tegmental area → nucleus accumbens → PFC), thereby modulating the emotional interpretation of pain and reducing subjective discomfort. Additionally, PA activates the PAG-rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM)-spinal cord pathway: it stimulates the PAG (initiating station), enhances the inhibition of spinal nociceptive input through the RVM (relay station), and relies on the synergistic activation of opioid, serotonin (5-HT), and norepinephrine system.108 PA also promotes the release of endogenous analgesic substances (opioids and endocannabinoids) from the pituitary and peripheral tissues, further contributing to pain relief.109

Translating these findings to clinical demographics, adolescent males exhibit higher sports participation rates than females, which may partially explain why females report greater orthodontic pain sensitivity. 101,104

Moreover, since exercise habits are closely linked to body weight management, maintaining a healthy body mass index through regular PA can help reduce orthodontic discomfort. Research has shown that obese adolescents experience greater pain 24 h after orthodontic treatment and exhibit higher levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β compared to non-obese adolescent control.110

Pain management methods

Based on the demographic distribution of orthodontic pain described above, pain management strategies can target systemic, intraoral, and treatment-related factors. Although gender and age are non-modifiable factors, they are influenced by the body’s response to pain-related cytokines.

Preclinical animal models have extensively mapped the peripheral mechanisms of this discomfort. During orthodontic treatment, forces applied to the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone cause local hemodynamic disturbances, alterations in ion channels, and the release of inflammatory cytokines and neuropeptides. These processes result in aseptic inflammation, which leads to the increased expression of prostaglandins, TRPV1, and CGRP in the nerve endings of trigeminal neuron.4,111,112,113,114

Following this peripheral transmission, human neuroimaging and clinical studies reveal how nociceptive inputs are subjected to central modulation. This occurs through functional connectivity between brain regions such as the amygdala, PFC, insula, and AC.27,94 This central processing is heavily mediated by key neurochemicals—including endogenous opioids (endorphins), 5-HT, endocannabinoids, and stress hormones such as cortisol—which dynamically inhibit or amplify the ascending nociceptive signal.95,96,97,98,109,110 Ultimately, these cognitive and emotional networks dictate the patient’s subjective perceived pain. Current therapeutic approaches focus on intervening in these processes to alleviate pain during orthodontic procedures (see Figure 2).

However, most practitioners underestimate the actual pain levels patients experience during treatment.6 While patients recognize the significant health benefits of orthodontic,115 negative expressions and posts on social media still account for 36% of their treatment experience.116 The level of care provided by doctors throughout the entire process is a key factor in patient satisfaction and can significantly impact patient compliance, which in turn affects the effectiveness of orthodontic treatment.117,118,119

Pharmacotherapy

Clinical and pharmacological evidence indicates that non-selective non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (e.g., ibuprofen and aspirin) manage orthodontic pain by inhibiting cyclooxygenase (COX) enzyme,120 though COX-1 blockade causes gastrointestinal adverse effect.121 Conversely, selective COX-2 inhibitors preserve anti-inflammatory benefits with minimized toxicity.122 However, preclinical animal studies raise concerns that NSAIDs could slow OTM, due to their potential to inhibit prostaglandin involvement in osteoclast maturation. 123,124,125,126

Unlike traditional NSAIDs, acetaminophen avoids this mechanism due to negligible peripheral anti-inflammatory activity in oxidative environments. By failing to inhibit COX in oxidative inflammatory environments (e.g., compressed periodontal tissues), it preserves local PGE2 synthesis and RANKL/OPG signaling, thereby maintaining normal osteoclast function and OT.127

Acetaminophen’s analgesic mechanism: Being metabolized into para-aminophenol (crossing the blood-brain barrier), then converted into N-arachidonoylphenolamine (AM404), which mildly activates TRPV1 receptors to induce pain desensitization. AM404 also inhibits the reuptake and degradation of endogenous cannabinoids, increasing their concentration in the synaptic cleft, producing analgesic and anxiolytic effects.127,128 Interestingly, returning to clinical practice, human trials confirm that acetaminophen exhibits enhanced analgesic efficacy, particularly in patients with elevated anxiety.12

Other adjunctive therapies include Danggui-Shaoyao-San, which alleviates pain via neuronal inhibition,129 and topical formulations that offer effective analgesia with minimized systemic exposure.130,131

Pharmacotherapy for Orthodontic pain

In conclusion, it is essential to recognize the dual role of PGE2 in both “pain” and “OTM.” In clinical practice, acetaminophen is recommended as the first-line pharmacotherapy, particularly for anxious patients, while NSAIDs should be reserved for short-term (<3 days) management of severe pain. Novel COX-2 inhibitors (tenoxicam and etoricoxib) offer a bone-sparing alternative.123,138,143,144 Standardizing pre-emptive analgesia protocols is advised to enhance clinical outcomes.

Low-level laser therapy

LLLT has been shown to effectively relieve orthodontic pain in numerous clinical trial,11,145,146,147,148,149,150,151,152,153,154 demonstrating comparable efficacy to NSAID13,155—the gold standard for pain management,156 with minimal systemic side effect.157 Randomized evidence directly comparing LLLT with pharmacologic analgesia demonstrates comparable short-term pain reduction but highlights heterogeneity in laser parameters and follow-up duration.158 Beyond isolated analgesia, contemporary clinical trials emphasize LLLT’s dual benefit in accelerating OTM via photobiomodulation.145 Furthermore, when LLLT is combined with minimally invasive surgical acceleration techniques (e.g., piezocision), clinical evaluations report nuanced effects on both pain modulation and retraction velocity, underscoring the need to standardize protocols for these multimodal approaches.159

The underlying mechanisms of this analgesia are well-elucidated in preclinical and in vitro models. LLLT can penetrate the gingiva to reach the periodontal ligament and superficial alveolar bone at a depth of 2–5 mm, targeting areas of pain in the periodontal tissues. Absorbed by mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase (CCO) of periodontal cells (e.g., fibroblasts and nerve endings), it improves the efficiency of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production.160 This upregulation facilitates tissue repair and elevates nerve excitability thresholds, thereby desensitizing pain receptor.161

Concurrently, LLLT promotes an anti-inflammatory M1-to-M2 macrophage shift, reducing cytokine secretion,162 and inhibits nociceptive transmission by modulating voltage-gated sodium channels and neuropeptides such as S.160,163,164

Regarding the clearance of inflammatory mediators, LLLT stimulates nitric oxide release, improving microcirculation and accelerating metabolic byproduct clearance while promoting the delivery of reparative nutrient.165

Low-level laser therapy for orthodontic pain

In orthodontics, energy densities of 2–8 J/cm2 are optima11,13,147,150,152,168; lower doses (<2 J/cm2) fail to stimulate adequate AT155,174; whereas excessive doses (>8 J/cm2) may induce excessive ROS accumulation, leading to cellular oxidative stress.13,175

GaAlAs lasers are most commonly used. Low-level laser treatments are typically performed with power levels between 100 and 500 m.176 Most studies employ near-infrared light with a wavelength of around 810 nm for irradiation. Experiments on human tissue penetration have demonstrated that this wavelength exhibits the best tissue penetration effec.177,178

For optimal clinical efficacy, GaAlAs lasers (∼810 nm, 100–500 mW) delivering energy densities of 2–8 J/cm2 are recommended. Irradiation should be performed immediately post-treatment, with an optional follow-up at 24–48 h to manage peak pai.169 Application should utilize vertical contact (10–30s/point) at apical and root zones on both buccal and lingual surfaces.

High equipment costs limit LLLT access to well-equipped clinics. The minimum workable schedule is a single, immediate chairside application post-adjustment; requiring 24-48 h return visits drastically reduces compliance. When integrated seamlessly without extra fees, patient acceptance is exceptionally high.

Chewing therapy

Clinical trials and meta-analyses consistently demonstrate that chewing gum or bite wafers significantly alleviates orthodontic pai,179,180,181 with efficacy comparable to that of NSAID.9,10,156,182,183 Most studies that observed effectiveness using chewing gum or bite wafers for 5–20 min immediately after treatment and every 6–8 h on pain days.

Human physiological studies clearly elucidate the local mechanisms of this relief: chewing temporarily displaces the teeth, the intermittent compression and rebound facilitate periodontal reperfusion, alleviating tissue hypoxia, and nerve edema.179 Doppler flowmetry revealed that mechanical stimulation significantly increased blood flow in gingival papilla.184

Furthermore, translational and human neuroimaging studies reveal central top-down modulation. Neurologically, chewing activates the ventral PFC, upregulates 5-HT in the dorsal nucleus of the median raphe, inhibiting nociceptio.185,186 Electroencephalogram (EEG) findings indicate that chewing gum during stressful situations enhances alpha wave power, reflecting a state of “relaxed alertness,” making the VAS scale decrease by 8–15 point,185 lowering analgesic requirements even in sensitive patient.187

Since the brain’s resources for processing sensory information are limited, chewing creates a “distraction effect” that competitively occupies attentional resource.179 The gating theory of nerve fibers operates on a similar principle. Detailed descriptions can be found in the chapter on vibration.

However, some studies report null result.188,189 These discrepancies may be due to factors such as allowing the use of analgesics by subject188 or an excessively long chewing interval (24 h.189

Concerns about chewing mainly involve potential damage to the orthodontic appliance. However, several studies have shown that chewing does not increase appliance damage rate.9,10,179,182 Additionally, chewing stimulates salivation, improving oral hygiene, thereby reducing orthodontic pain190,191 and reducing caries risk.192

In summary, patients are advised to initiate chewing immediately post-treatment, thrice daily or as needed, for 10–20 min to optimize periodontal blood flow without inducing masticatory muscle fatigue. Gentle, generalized mastication using low-viscosity xylitol gum is recommended; however, Cas must be removed prior to use to prevent appliance deformation or hygiene compromise. Alternatively, viscoelastic bite blocks may be utilized for patients averse to gum, progressing from soft (days 1–(2) to harder consistencies (days 2–(3) as inflammation subsides.

Patient education regarding transient “rebound pain” is essential to ensure therapeutic adherence. Adjunctive pharmacotherapy is indicated for patients with low pain thresholds, while those with temporomandibular disorders warrant strict caution and controlled duration.193

Chewing is a highly accessible, zero-cost option for most (excluding TMJ patients). The minimum schedule is 10 min post-adjustment, followed by “as-needed” use during peak pain, maximizing long-term compliance. While adolescents readily chew gum, adults or aligner patients prefer bite wafers (chewies) integrated into routine seating exercises.

Vibration therapy

Based on established neurophysiological models, vibratory stimuli are transmitted via large-diameter Aβ afferent fibers to the central nervous system. According to the gate control theory, this non-noxious input competes with noxious signals from Aδ fibers (sharp pain) and C fibers (persistent dull pain) for access to central pathways. Because Aβ fibers possess significantly higher conduction velocities than Aδ and C fibers, non-nociceptive signals reach the central nervous system first, blocking ascending pain transmission and attenuating pain perception.194,195

Preclinical and in vitro models further validate these analgesic effects at the cellular level. The subtle mechanical deformations (micro-deformations) induced by vibration exhibit effects similar to chewing in promoting microcirculation within periodontal tissues. Analysis of gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) indicates that certain pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., PGE2 and IL-1β) peak earlier and decline more rapidly following vibration therapy. Concurrently, this treatment accelerated the turnover of cytokines essential for bone remodeling, such as RANK,196 while downregulating CGRP and TRPV1 expression in the TG.197 Furthermore, vibration may provide analgesia by reducing the release of harmful neuropeptide.198

Transitioning to human clinical evidence, high-frequency vibration (>100 Hz) demonstrates more robust analgesic efficacy,197,199,200,201 although efficacy at low frequencies (<30 Hz) has also been reported.202,203 Conversely, other studies with similar vibration parameters (both high and low frequency) observed only marginal pain relief without statistical significance.189,204,205,206,207

Given these discordant findings, caution is advised regarding vibration therapy. Nevertheless, a regimen of high-frequency stimulation for 20 min daily is recommended to optimize Pacinian corpuscle activation,208 thereby facilitating pain inhibition via the gate control mechanism.

High device costs restrict vibration therapy to motivated, affluent patients. To combat “device fatigue” from daily 20-min recommendations, the minimum workable schedule targets only during the peak-pain days post-adjustment. Initial acceptance is high among aligner users, though some eventually abandon the ticklish, time-consuming sensation.

Psychological therapy

Extensive clinical and human neuroimaging research supports the use of psychological therapies, including music therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), and placebo. Unlike physical interventions that directly block nociceptive signals, these therapies target the multidimensional nature of pain. They modulate the patient’s psychological state by engaging complex cognitive, emotional, and pain-processing brain networks.

Cognitively, psychological therapy helps correct irrational beliefs, thereby reducing anxiety that can amplify orthodontic discomfort. Techniques such as attention diversion minimize the focus on tooth sensitivity and chewing discomfort. Behaviorally, therapy encourages gradual dietary transitions (from soft foods to regular meals) and alleviates maxillofacial muscle tension through relaxation training. Neurophysiologically, it helps regulate the autonomic nervous system and promotes endorphin release. By leveraging neuroplasticity, it also remodels functional connectivity in pain-related brain regions, such as the dorsolateral PFC and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC). Ultimately, these therapies reduce subjective pain perception and improve dietary habits during treatment by breaking the cycle of pain-negative cognition-maladaptive behavior.209

Transitioning to orthodontic clinical practice, these psychological treatments have been shown to yield excellent clinical outcome.210,211 Even when used as adjuncts, they can effectively complement other treatments by reducing the need for analgesic medication.212

Traditional music therapy uses specific pitch or rhythmic characteristics to influence brain function and relieve pain. A newer form of music therapy, brainwave music (BWM), is composed of personalized EEG signals and frequency bands, which may have a more pronounced effect on brain activity.213

CBT is particularly important in today’s information-explosive era, as it can reduce the psychological impact of patients’ dependence on false information leading to pain reductio,210,214 with efficacy even comparable to that of ibuprofen.214

While formal CBT is effective, it involves high costs and stigmatizing referrals that patients often reject. Therefore, it is more practical for the orthodontic team to deliver modified interventions. The most efficient approach is a brief 3–5 min chairside discussion regarding expectation management pre-bonding, supplemented by background music. This zero-cost method ensures high rates of patient acceptance and improved compliance.

Other orthodontic pain control therapy

Emerging clinical trials and translational research highlight several promising adjunctive therapies for orthodontic pain management, summarized briefly later in discussion:

Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) has demonstrated efficacy in a human orthodontic trial.146 Preclinical models indicate this analgesia is achieved by eliminating inflammation and preventing the cellular secretion of inflammatory factors with minimal side effect.216,217

Pulsed electromagnetic field (PEMF) therapy relieves orthodontic pain in clinical cohorts by enhancing the binding rate of calcium ions to calcium-regulated proteins, stimulating nitric oxide production, and improving local blood and lymphatic flo.218

Furthermore, robust clinical evidence supports acupoint therapies, including acupressur,219 transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation (TEAS),220 and acupuncture,221 which have been shown to control orthodontic pain. Specific points, such as those between the thumb and forefinger, and ear acupoints, can provide pain relief comparable to that of painkillers.

Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) applies electrical impulses to the injury site and is commonly used in dental pain managemen.222 Current studies suggest that TENS is equally effective for pain relief in orthodontic.223,224

Conclusion and prospects

Orthodontic pain is a multidimensional experience driven by a cascade of mechanical, ischemic, and inflammatory events. The application of orthodontic force disrupts local microvascular hemodynamics, triggering a sterile inflammatory response characterized by the release of cytokines (IL-1β), prostaglandins, and neuropeptides (CGRP, SP). These mediators lower the activation threshold of peripheral nociceptors (e.g., TRPV1) and subsequently induce central sensitization via the trigeminal-amygdala pathway. Conversely, current pain management strategies effectively counter these pathways at multiple levels: Psychological therapies and chewing exercises activate the endogenous descending inhibitory system and promote tissue reperfusion; non-invasive physical therapies (e.g., LLLT and vibration) facilitate ATP-driven tissue repair, shift macrophage polarization, and block nociceptive transmission via the gate control theory; while pharmacotherapy directly inhibits the inflammatory enzymatic cascades (COX pathways) and central pain perception.

Limitations of the study

While providing a comprehensive framework, this review has several limitations. Included clinical trials exhibit considerable heterogeneity in force protocols and patient demographics. Additionally, pain assessment relies heavily on subjective tools (e.g., visual analog scale). Finally, evidence for certain non-pharmacological interventions remains low-certainty due to small sample sizes and inherent blinding challenges.
