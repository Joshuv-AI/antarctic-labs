The Arrow and the Atlas—A Narrow Escape in the Cervical Spine: A Case Report About Penetrating Injury With an Arrow Impacted in the Neck
Authors: Sameer Lamichhane, Santosh Khadka, Aayush Aryal, Sushil Paudel, Dinesh Kafle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPenetrating neck injuries (PNI) are a rare presentation of all trauma cases. A rational strategy and prompt patient assessment are necessary for management. Selective treatment has replaced mandatory exploration in the treatment of penetrating neck trauma. A 41‐year‐old male patient was admitted to the emergency department with an arrow penetrating the neck, without signs of neurological deficit. He had an arrow impacted into the right posterolateral aspect of his neck just posterior to the sternocleidomastoid muscle, corresponding to the axial level of the C1‐C2 vertebra. The arrow was dislodged after extending the entry wound and removed with a gentle maneuver in the operating room with an uneventful postoperative period and follow‐ups. Although PNIs are rare, a thorough understanding of the anatomy in this area is essential. Direct withdrawal in a controlled setting may be an excellent option for penetrating cervical and posterior neck injuries by a foreign body that is away from the neurovascular and aerodigestive tracts and in a hemodynamically stable patient.

Introduction

Penetrating neck injuries (PNI) occur in 5%–10% of trauma cases [1]. PNI can be caused by a variety of objects, including knives, bullets, metallic rods, bamboo sticks, arrows, and more. Such injuries have the potential to endanger life by harming critical neck tissues such as the pharynx, esophagus, major arteries (vertebral and carotid arteries), and nerves [2]. Before surgical intervention, management requires a rational strategy and prompt evaluation of the airway and sufficiency of circulation, as well as the potential existence of any skeletal or neurological injury [3]. The approach to treating penetrating neck trauma has changed from requiring exploration to targeted management. For patients with PNI, doctors are now widely favoring selective operative management based on selective diagnostic investigations and physical examination. Based on physical examination and auxiliary examination, selective management is safe and practical for hemodynamically stable patients who show no evidence of major structural damage [4, 5]. We hereby report a case of a 41‐year‐old male who presented to us with an arrow lodged into the right posterolateral part of his neck, which was managed successfully with direct extraction under controlled conditions.

Case History/Examination

A 41‐year‐old male from Nepal presented to the emergency department of our tertiary care center with a penetrating arrow lodged in his neck from an accidental shot during archery by his friend 2 h prior, with no other injury reported (Figure 1). Upon examination, his vital signs were stable, he was alert and focused, not pale or cyanosed, and there were no signs of neurological deficit, mouth or nose bleeding, hoarseness in speech, stridor, respiratory distress, or active bleeding from the puncture site. With carotid pulses perceptible and the right upper limb's range of motion and strength intact, there was soreness around the damage site upon palpation, but no crepitus or emphysema was detected. The remaining systemic examinations were normal. He had an arrow impacted into the right posterolateral aspect of his neck (Figure 2). The entry point was located 4 cm lateral to the midline, just posterior to the posterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, corresponding to the axial plane of the C1‐C2 vertebrae (Figure 3).

Investigations and Treatment

Radiographic and clinical assessment confirmed a posterolateral trajectory in the upper part of the neck, staying strictly posterior to the carotid artery region. Importantly, the arrowhead was lodged within the posterior soft tissues, away from the vertebral artery's course, and without penetrating the atlanto‐occipital or atlanto‐axial joint spaces or contacting the bony arch of the atlas.

He was taken to the operating room and underwent neck exploration under total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA). He was placed in a lateral position with the head supported. Although the patient was hemodynamically stable, the surgical team remained prepared for potential vascular compromise upon foreign body dislodgement; a vascular tray was open, and proximal/distal control strategies were reviewed. The entry point was extended by 2 cm along the trajectory. Dissection was carried out meticulously through the posterior paraspinal muscles in a stepwise fashion, staying strictly within the posterolateral musculature compartment throughout. The arrow—a carbon‐fiber shaft with a smooth and compact stainless steel field point—was visualized. It was found to be impacted into the paraspinal musculature, lateral to the vertebral artery's expected course, without ligamentous or bony injury. The surrounding tissue showed no evidence of hematoma formation or vascular injury. At no point did the dissection approach the carotid sheath anteriorly or the spinal canal medially. The arrow was extracted under direct vision with a steady, controlled maneuver to prevent any secondary “swing” injury to deeper structures. Throughout the procedure, the surgical field remained bloodless, without any arterial or venous bleeding and minimal normal tissue ooze. Complete removal of the arrow was confirmed by the use of fluoroscopy, and the tract of the wound was debrided, washed, and sutured Figures 4 and 5.

After surgery, he was administered intravenous antibiotics (Inj. Cefuroxime 1.5 g IV BD and Inj. Amikacin 500 mg BD), analgesics, and observation as per postoperative protocol. On the first day after the operation, he only felt mild pain at the site of surgical intervention, but could eat food and converse, and there were no signs of difficulty speaking. Physical examination showed that his vitals were stable, there were no signs of central nervous system involvement, and the function of his right arm was intact. After 24 more hours without any complications, he was discharged from the hospital with a soft cervical collar for 1 week. Clinical follow‐up at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, and 3 months demonstrated complete healing of the surgical site. There were no delayed vascular complications, no signs of deep space neck infection, and a full, painless range of motion of the cervical spine was achieved by the 6‐week mark, at which point he returned to all pre‐injury activities (Table 1).

Conclusion and Results

Despite the rarity of penetrating neck injuries, they provide a critical test of a surgeon's anatomical knowledge and clinical judgment. This case demonstrates that mandatory surgical exploration and extensive vascular imaging (CTA) can be safely replaced by a selective, anatomy‐guided management strategy in hemodynamically stable patients.

The success of this “narrow escape” relied on a precise three‐dimensional understanding of the C1‐C2 region. By identifying a trajectory strictly posterior to the sternocleidomastoid muscle and the carotid sheath, we were able to proceed with a controlled operative extraction that minimized tissue trauma while ensuring immediate readiness for vascular complications.

Ultimately, this report reinforces that in resource‐limited settings or specific low‐risk trajectories, meticulous clinical examination and plain radiography remain powerful tools for surgical triage. A cautious decision of direct extraction under controlled operative conditions can be a safe and effective option for posterior cervical penetrations that are clinically and radiologically confirmed to be distant from the vital neurovascular and aerodigestive pathways.

Discussion

As the neck contains vital structures, all penetrating neck wounds have the potential to be serious and need immediate medical attention [6]. Structures are more susceptible to damage from any penetration injury of the neck that could have major and fatal repercussions since they are concentrated in a limited volume [2]. The vital structures are, however, sometimes covered by a hard, bony shield, as well as muscles, whereas in other locations they are more superficial and may become exposed. This arrangement's diversity forces the clinician to use a separate management approach for various neck locations [1]. In a stable patient with no need for urgent surgery, a CT angiography has emerged as the preferred test for PNI triage and subsequent care, regardless of the area of injury [7]. Moreover, physical examination findings have been noted to exclude injuries in more than 99% of patients and are good predictors for vascular injury in penetrating neck wounds. The physical signs for vascular injuries, like active hemorrhage, enlarging hematoma, bruit, pulse deficit, and central neurological deficit, can reliably diagnose vascular injuries with a missed injury rate of 0.7%, equivalent to arteriography in accuracy but cheaper and non‐invasive, based on a study by Sekharan et al. [8] involving 145 cases of zone II penetrating neck injuries. Management of PNI has undergone a paradigm shift from mandatory surgical exploration to a more nuanced, selective approach, with traditional management dictated by anatomical zones (I, II, and III) and modern practice increasingly adopting a “no‐zone” approach. Current “no‐zone” management algorithms for PNI also prioritize CT angiography (CTA) as the gold standard for stable patients [5, 9]. However, in this specific scenario, advanced vascular imaging was deferred based on a combination of factors: the clearly superficial and posterolateral trajectory (Zone II posterior), the complete absence of hard signs (expanding hematoma, bruit, or pulse deficit) of vascular or aerodigestive injury, and the clarity of plain films in localizing the foreign body away from the “danger zone” of the carotid and vertebral arteries. Furthermore, existing research on penetrating craniocervical injuries supports a selective imaging approach in clinically stable patients with injuries clearly distant from major vascular structures [10].

A critical principle in managing impacted foreign bodies is the avoidance of “blind” removal, as foreign bodies can occasionally tamponade significant vessel damage; consequently, blind removal of the foreign body could result in potentially fatal bleeding. It is always best to remove such foreign bodies through examination in a suitable, controlled setup. Many surgeons believe that neck exploration is required, but others believe that a selective approach is more suitable, given the morbidity and mortality linked to surgical investigation [6]. In our case, as the chances of major vessel and organ injury were very low according to the site of penetration, we removed the foreign body in a controlled setup utilizing a selective “no‐zone” approach, which has been shown to safely reduce non‐therapeutic, unnecessary explorations while maintaining safety in stable patients [5, 11]. We reasoned that a controlled operative exploration provided both diagnostic confirmation and therapeutic resolution without the delay of further imaging, given the patient's low‐risk anatomical path.

Similar to our case, in the Tanzania case by Ismail et al. [12], where the arrow injury was in the posterior cervical region, and the patient was hemodynamically stable, we both managed the case with direct controlled extraction without much exploration and had uneventful recoveries with prophylactic antibiotics. But our case was specifically around the C1‐C2 region, which is anatomically more complex due to proximity to major arteries, yet it was safely managed without CTA, which makes our case particularly noteworthy. In contrast, the study by Joseph et al. [7], whereby CTA guided the surgical approach, our case has a distinctly superficial and posterolateral trajectory on a plain radiograph with a complete absence of any hard signs, giving us sufficient anatomical reassurance to proceed without advance vascular imaging—a decision further justified by the resource‐limited settings in institutions like ours.

Nevertheless, it has to be noted that the comparison has been conducted on the basis of a small number of published cases. It is not possible to draw any firm conclusion about the non‐use of CTA in arrow wounds involving the C1‐C2 region based on these few cases, and the decision has to be taken with caution; further case studies would have to be conducted for this method to be validated. Additionally, another obvious limitation in our case report is the lack of postoperative vascular imaging, such as CTA and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), at follow‐up. Although the patient did not present any symptoms and there was no clinical evidence of vascular compromise during the three‐month follow‐up, it is difficult to rule out a possible vascular injury, such as a pseudoaneurysm or irregularity in the internal lining of an artery due to its anatomical proximity to the vertebral artery.
