Dataset on the genome sequence and biological characteristics of bacteriophage vB_RsoP_PBa1 infecting Ralstonia pseudosolanacearum GMI1000
Authors: Qingshan Wu, Xiaoli Yu, Wenjing Liu, Yuanfeng Yang, Zhengmin Li, Zheng Fang, Lan Xiang, Qingbei Weng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This dataset reports the isolation and genomic characterization of vB_RsoP_PBa1, a novel Caudoviricetes phage that infects Ralstonia pseudosolanacearum GMI1000. The phage was isolated and enriched using double-layer agar plaque assays. Genomic analysis revealed that vB_RsoP_PBa1 possesses a linear double-stranded DNA genome of 42,068 bp with a G+C content of 62.44%, encoding 53 predicted coding sequences, of which 18 were assigned putative functions. The annotated open reading frames include functional modules for genome packaging, virion assembly, host lysis (holin, lysozyme, and a helix-turn-helix domain-containing protein), host infection, genome replication, and phage integration. Phylogenetic analyses based on the proteomic and the terminase large subunit amino acid sequences demonstrated that vB_RsoP_PBa1 is most closely related to Ralstonia phage DU_RP_II (MF150911.1), with which it shares 78.8% average nucleotide identity. Overall, this dataset provides a valuable genomic resource for comparative phage genomics and supports the development of phage-based biocontrol strategies against bacterial wilt caused by R. pseudosolanacearum.

Background

The R. pseudosolanacearum species complex is a dominant soil-borne phytopathogen that causes severe economic losses in global agriculture by inducing bacterial wilt in >200 plant species (e.g., potato, tomato, and banana), with the disease being particularly destructive in tropical and subtropical regions [1,2]. A review by Toyota summarized diverse control strategies against plant pathogens such as R. pseudosolanacearum, including biological, physical, chemical, cultural, and integrated approaches, and highlighted the critical role of integrated pest management with a focus on biological control [3]. In contrast to physical and chemical interventions—such as fumigants and copper-based compounds, which often show inconsistent efficacy, cause environmental risks, and may promote bacterial resistance—lytic bacteriophages (phages) represent a promising and eco-friendly alternative for R. pseudosolanacearum management via host-specific bacteriolytic activity [4,5]. However, the narrow host range of individual phages, the emergence of phage-resistant bacterial mutants, and the poor persistence of phages under field conditions collectively highlight the urgent need to continuously isolate and characterize novel phages with broad lytic host ranges and improved environmental stability [6]. Therefore, the isolation, propagation, and whole-genome sequencing of a newly identified phage infecting R. pseudosolanacearum GMI1000—designated vB_RsoP_PBa1, a member of the class Caudoviricetes—represent a key step toward developing effective phage-based biocontrol strategies.

Data Description

This study reports a novel lytic phage infecting R. pseudosolanacearum GMI1000, designated vB_RsoP_PBa1, which was isolated from soil samples collected in Guizhou, China. Phage vB_RsoP_PBa1 produced small, clear, circular plaques approximately 1 mm in diameter on R. pseudosolanacearum GMI1000 lawns after incubation at 30 °C (Fig. 1A). Morphological observations revealed an icosahedral head (54.04 ± 1.12 nm in diameter) and a short tail (20.03 ± 1.85 nm in length) (Fig. 1B). The phage harbors a linear double-stranded DNA genome of 42,068 bp with a G+C content of 62.44% (Table 1). Comparative nucleotide analysis using BLASTn revealed high sequence similarity between vB_RsoP_PBa1 and three previously reported Ralstonia phages: Ralstonia phage RpY1 (MN996301.1; 73% genome coverage, 92.34% identity), DU_RP_II (MF150911.1; 84% coverage, 91.72% identity), and C22 (OR538033.2; 74% coverage, 92.49% identity). The genome of phage vB_RsoP_PBa1 shows a distinct strand-specific distribution of coding sequences (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S1). Both the lysis module—comprising ORF22 (holin), ORF23 (lysozyme), and ORF28 (helix-turn-helix domain-containing protein)—and the packaging module, which consists of ORF1 and ORF53 (terminase large subunit and terminase small subunits), reside on the positive strand and are associated with a positive G+C skew. In contrast, more than half of the ORFs classified as ``unsorted'' are located on the negative strand. Proteome phylogenetic tree analysis revealed that phage vB_RsoP_PBa1 clusters first with Ralstonia phages DU_RP_II (MF150911.1) and RpY1 (MN996301.1), and then with Ralstonia phage C22 (OR538033.2) (Fig. 3A). Phylogenetic analysis based on terminase large subunit (TerL) amino acid sequences further supported these relationships, although the TerL of vB_RsoP_PBa1 first clustered with that of Ralstonia phage C22 (Fig. 3B). Its closest relative is Ralstonia phage DU_RP_II (MF150911.1), with which vB_RsoP_PBa1 shares a 78.8% two-way average nucleotide identity (ANI) (Fig. 3C). Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The host strain R. pseudosolanacearum GMI1000 was routinely cultured in CPG liquid medium (comprising 10 g/L casamino acids, 10 g/L glucose, and 1 g/L K2HPO4, pH 7.0) at 30 °C with 180 rpm shaking overnight. Cells were harvested at the exponential phase (approximately 2 × 107 colony-forming units per milliliter) for subsequent phage isolation and plaque assays.

The phage vB_RsoP_PBa1 was isolated from soil samples collected in July 2025 from a long-term continuous monoculture tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) field in Guiyang, China. This field has been consecutively cropped with tobacco for multiple years and remained free from bacterial wilt disease. Soil samples were homogenized and soaked in 10% (w/v) SM buffer, incubated with shaking at 30 °C and 180 rpm for 12 h, and filtered through a 0.22-µm filter [7]. A 9-mL aliquot of the filtrate was mixed with 1 mL of the exponential-phase host culture, incubated for 10 min, added to 20 mL of Nutrient Broth, and cultured overnight (16–18 h) at 30 °C with 180 rpm shaking [8]. After centrifugation and filtration, the crude phage lysate was collected.

For phage isolation, 1 mL of lysate was mixed with 1 mL of host culture, incubated for 20 min, and plated using the double-layer agar method with CPG-based media (bottom layer: 1.5% (w/v) agar; top semi-solid overlay: 0.6% (w/v) agar) [9]. The plates were then incubated at 30 °C for 24 h until distinct plaques appeared. Phage purification was performed by picking individual plaques, suspending them in 1 mL of SM buffer, and serially diluting the suspension to 10−4 [10]. For each dilution, 1 mL of diluted phage was mixed with 1 mL of host bacterial culture, incubated for 20 min, and overlaid with the same CPG semi-solid agar (0.6% agar) using the double-layer agar technique [11]. Plates were again incubated at 30 °C for 24 h. This procedure was repeated 3–5 times until uniform plaque morphology was obtained.

For phage enrichment, a single purified plaque was resuspended in 1 mL of SM buffer, mixed with 1 mL of host culture, and poured onto a double-layer agar plate. After overnight incubation at 30 °C (16–18 h), confluent lysis (a “clear plate”) was observed, and 5 mL of SM buffer was added to the semi-solid agar layer for overnight elution at 4 °C. The eluate was collected and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant containing the enriched phage was stored at 4 °C for further use.

A 10-µL aliquot of the phage concentrate was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) after negative staining with 2% (w/v) uranyl acetate [12].

Phage genomic DNA was extracted using a viral DNA extraction kit (Takara MiniBEST Viral DNA/RNA Extraction Kit Ver.5.0) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, a 200-µL aliquot of the purified lysate (107 plaque-forming units per milliliter) was subjected to proteolytic digestion with proteinase K (20 µL) at 56 °C for 10 min. Following the addition of an equal volume of absolute ethanol, the mixture was loaded onto the silica membrane column, and the DNA was allowed to bind during a 2-min centrifugation step at 12,000 × g. After two sequential washes with 500 µL of supplied buffer (with 1-min centrifugation at the same speed each time), the purified genomic DNA was recovered by eluting with 50 µL of elution buffer. The final DNA preparations were preserved at −20 °C for downstream use. Paired-end (2 × 150 bp) sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). Clean reads were assembled using SPAdes v3.15.0 with default parameters [13].

ORFs were predicted by Prodigal v2.6.3 within the PhageScope web server, followed by functional annotation using BLASTP against the NCBI nr database [14]. To identify genetically related phages, a protein-sequence-based phylogenetic tree was first constructed using the ViPTree web server (https://www.genome.jp/viptree/) [15], and reference genomes were selected accordingly. Pairwise average nucleotide identity (ANI) between the newly sequenced phage and each selected reference phage was calculated using the VIRIDIC web server (https://viridic.icbm.de/) [16]. The terminase large subunit (TerL) amino acid sequences of these reference phages were retrieved from NCBI, aligned with ClustalW, and used to construct a Maximum Likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree with 1000 bootstrap replicates in MEGA 12 [17]. Finally, a circular genome map of the phage was generated using Proksee [18].
