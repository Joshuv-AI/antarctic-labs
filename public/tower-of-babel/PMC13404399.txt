Growing up digital: respecting children’s evolving capacities in times of change
Authors: Ramya Subrahmanian, Cécile Aptel, Maria Herczog, Ratna Jhaveri, Gerison Lansdown, Raúl Mercer, Benyam Dawit Mezmur, Vikram Patel, Vanessa Sedletzki, Camila Teixeira
Published: 2026

Digital technology has transformed childhood in unprecedented ways. Adolescents and young people are the most connected group globally: 71% of 15–24-year-olds are online.1 A UNICEF–Gallup poll in 55 countries found that 60% of them rely mainly on social media for news, while 75% of adults 65 or older still turn to TV, radio or print.2 For children today, the digital environment is a basic infrastructure of life.

This transformation presents an urgent and unresolved challenge notably for policymakers, platform designers, parents and caregivers: how to balance protection against the risks of the digital environment with recognition of children’s growing agency and competence. Getting this balance right matters greatly. Indeed, the decisions made today about how children engage with digital technology will shape their development, their rights and their futures.

The concept of evolving capacities offers a coherent framework for navigating these changes. Evolving capacities recognise that, throughout childhood, children progressively develop the capacities to understand, decide and act while becoming more independent. This concept was first articulated in the Convention on the Rights of the Child which was adopted in 1989. A landmark 2005 UNICEF report elaborated on the concept and proposed a framework to further understanding of its key components. This framework has now been updated two decades on in a new publication, Growing with Rights.3 The report draws on advances in neuroscience, developmental science and the social sciences, examining how changes in children’ lives and environments—including the rapid expansion of the digital world—shape the exercise of rights across childhood.

Understanding how to support children through the rapid changes impacting the world requires looking more carefully at what capacities actually mean in practice. The framework presented in Growing with Rights identifies three interdependent dimensions that together offer a holistic basis for thinking about children’s rights across any sector or setting—from education and health to justice and family life.3 First, developmental investment must ensure that environments surrounding children—families, schools, platforms, public spaces—actively build, not hinder, the cognitive, social and emotional capacities children need. Second, emancipatory recognition must acknowledge children’s growing agency and competence, supporting rather than substituting for their decision-making as capacities mature. And third, protective frameworks must be calibrated to different developmental stages, shifting in balance as children develop rather than applying uniform restrictions. These three dimensions must be pursued together: taken in isolation, each can produce its own failure.

Examining these dimensions is especially urgent in relation to the digital world, where the stakes for healthy development are particularly high. During adolescence, in particular, capacities for reasoning judgement grow rapidly alongside capacities for self-regulation. This phase of development creates a specific window of both opportunity and vulnerability. Neuroplasticity means that new influences—including sustained engagement with social media—can significantly shape cognitive, emotional and prosocial development in ways that are not yet fully understood.

Scholars studying the digital environment have also contributed significantly to understanding the dynamic digital ecology surrounding children, offering ways of framing risks and opportunities in the context of their well-being. For instance, a Global Kids Online framework elaborates how individual characteristics interact with family, community and country-level factors.4 It treats capacity as dynamic, relational and context-dependent. The risk landscape that children engage with as their capacities evolve is also dynamic, spanning their interaction with online content, contact and conduct, and transactional aspects associated with joining digital platforms.5 Finally, focusing on the opportunities associated with digital engagement also helps move the debates away from a deficit model towards one that balances risks and opportunities, and brings in the wider ecosystem of stakeholders to promote children's rights and build a preventive approach to harm.6

Evidence also continues to grow on the interactions between the digital environment and children’s health and well-being, with the digital determinants of health—digital access, use and experiences—becoming a distinct field of analysis.7 Research suggests that what children do online matters more than how long they spend there.8 9 The picture is mixed; however, digital engagement can support learning, connection and creativity. At the same time, documented harms include impacts on sleep, attention and verbal development.3 10 Different types of risk also emerge in association with online gaming,11 digital gambling12 and exposure to harassment and sexual abuse.13 As internet access to children younger than 15 continues to increase, potentially more harmful effects may emerge given their still-forming capacities to understand and judge content.

Children themselves are very aware of what is at stake. In their own accounts, they describe the internet as a space to access health information, peer support and connection. At the same time, children also recognise that excessive online engagement can disrupt physical activity and direct social contact on which healthy development depends.3 While children value AI embedded in digital technologies for speed, homework support and convenience, many also worry about misinformation, over-dependence and the erosion of their own skills and confidence. Their views also point to the need for balance and nuance in determining safeguards 'that factor in use and impact informed by age and socioeconomic status.14

Mediating the harms and the benefits of digital technology has put parents and caregivers in a difficult position. Ensuring children’s safety online requires new forms of parent–child connection based on trust and openness, and the ability to balance screen usage with respect for privacy.15 This can be hard for parents given the fast-paced nature of the digital transformation. In addition, and as in other contexts, when engaging online children make multiple decisions on their own. A simple click can have huge repercussions depending on the child’s understanding, the protection they enjoy, as well as other influences and constraints.

Underlying all of this is a more fundamental problem: the internet and related tools have been designed without sufficient consideration to children’s rights and capacities. When applications are used in health, education or social policy, they may fail to uphold children’s rights and possibly cause harm. Corporations have launched advertising and marketing strategies, digital platforms and algorithms that exploit children’s vulnerabilities. Recent court decisions have questioned whether private sector tactics for online engagement deprive children of opportunities to exercise judgement.16 By primarily serving commercial interests, the technology industry has often failed to fulfil the responsibility to place the best interests of the child as a primary consideration in matters affecting them.

Public policy responses have also failed to match the scale of the challenge. Governments have oscillated between restriction and inaction, without sufficiently considering children’s perspectives. Fixed age thresholds can be justified where risks of harm are severe and enduring—prosecution under adult criminal law and child marriage being the clearest examples. Yet evidence for blanket restrictions on mobile phones or online platforms is far less clear-cut, and these policies shift the attention away from poorly regulated underlying technology.17

An urgent revisit of how legislation, policies and practices are designed and implemented is therefore needed, in line with the three interdependent dimensions in the evolving capacities framework. First, developmentally, platforms must be designed around how children actually learn and grow, not retrofitted for them after the fact, and they must be regulated to enforce safety standards.18 Second, in fulfilling technology’s emancipatory potential, children’s expertise as digital users must be treated as a genuine input into regulatory and design decisions, not an afterthought. And third, protectively, the goal is not to shield all children from all digital risk, but to ensure that protections match children’s actual capacities at different stages, tightening where vulnerability is greatest and loosening as competence grows.

We should all act decisively to build a world in which children can safely develop their capacities and autonomy. Children are neither passive victims to be shielded from the world nor fully autonomous adults equipped to navigate it alone. Binary policy responses—total restriction or no intervention—fail them on both counts. An effective response requires an interdisciplinary approach beyond healthcare, engaging education, childcare and scientific societies to support safe technology use and reduce conflicts of interest. Health professionals should advocate for children’s rights across family, community and policy domains, while promoting lifelong digital health literacy and addressing early digital determinants. In practice, this includes routine assessment of children’s digital interactions, age-appropriate guidance on risks and mitigation, and vigilance on safeguarding, especially for vulnerable children. Addressing technology-facilitated sexual abuse requires strong health-sector leadership, with tailored tools integrated into policy and practice to support prevention, identification and recovery.

The deep transformations affecting contemporary childhood are not waiting for governance to catch up. Delays and omission can cause real harm. Policymakers, industry leaders, politicians, clinicians and caregivers must respond to the existing evidence and adapt their actions as children’s capacities and the world around them evolve. More investment is also needed in evidence generation to address gaps in knowledge and understanding. The evolving capacities framework offers a valuable guide for doing so.
