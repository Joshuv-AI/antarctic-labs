Mechanistic success and clinical neutrality: lessons from the OXICARD trial
Authors: Ioana Cozma, Emilie P Belley-Côté, Jessica Spence
Published: 2026

Cardiac surgery remains among the most physiologically disruptive interventions in modern medicine (1). Despite major advances in surgical technique, myocardial protection, and perioperative critical care, postoperative morbidity following cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) continues to be driven by systemic biological responses that extend well beyond the operating field (2,3). Central among these is a robust and often dysregulated inflammatory cascade, triggered by contact of blood with the non-endothelial surface of the extracorporeal circuit, ischemia-reperfusion injury, endotoxemia, and surgical trauma (4,5). While inflammation is an essential and tightly regulated component of host defense and tissue repair (6), sustained or excessive activation in the perioperative setting becomes maladaptive, contributing to the development of clinical complications including vasoplegia (7), acute kidney injury (AKI) (8), myocardial dysfunction (9), coagulopathy (10), delirium (11), prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stay (12), and increased mortality (12).

Over the past several decades, numerous pharmacological strategies have been evaluated for their ability to attenuate this dysregulated inflammatory response, including corticosteroids (13), antifibrinolytics (14), leukocyte filtration (15,16), and targeted cytokine blockade (17). Despite strong biological plausibility, none have consistently produced definitive and reproducible effects on clinically meaningful outcomes (18,19). This unfulfilled hypothesis has subsequently maintained research interest in extracorporeal blood purification technologies, particularly cytokine hemoadsorption, as a non-pharmacological approach to immune modulation.

In this regard, the OXICARD study entitled “Cytokine Hemoadsorption versus Standard Care in Cardiac Surgery Using the Oxiris Membrane: The OXICARD Single-center Randomized Trial” represents a timely and important contribution to the field. By rigorously evaluating hemoadsorption during cardiac surgery, the investigators provide insight into the feasibility and biological effects of this intervention (20). The results of OXICARD highlight the persistent gap between mechanistic efficacy and clinical effectiveness in perioperative therapeutics, and the challenges that limit the clinical translation of interventions with demonstrated benefits in vitro (20).

The OXICARD trial: design and principal findings

The OXICARD trial was a single-center randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted at the Amiens University Hospital in Amiens, France. The trial evaluated the effects of intraoperative cytokine hemoadsorption using the Oxiris membrane on postoperative outcomes in adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery with anticipated prolonged CPB time (>90 minutes). Patients were randomized to standard CPB or CPB integrated with the Oxiris hemoadsorptive membrane.

The trial adopted an ambitious multimodal research design spanning multiple levels of the translational research continuum, and include: (I) an RCT evaluating postoperative clinical outcomes; (II) a mechanistic substudy measuring the effect of the intervention on circulating cytokines and inflammatory biomarkers; and (III) a hybrid ex vivo/in vitro substudy assessing the effects of study subject plasma on the viability of in vitro cultured endothelial cells. This multitargeted integrative approach offers methodological strength, and offers an innovative approach to facilitating translational research by combining clinical trials with experimental basic science.

The primary outcome studied was the sublingual microcirculatory flow index on postoperative day 1, which did not demonstrate statistical significance between the two groups studied. The secondary outcomes measured include clinical outcomes (organ dysfunction scores, cumulative vasopressor requirements, length of stay in ICU, incidence of adverse events over a 30-day period postoperatively), mechanistic outcomes (measured cytokine and biomarkers levels), and ex vivo basic science outcomes (cell viability measured through optical density). Mechanistically, the intervention did not show statistically significant differences in circulating cytokines and biomarkers and, in fact, demonstrated a paradoxical increase in circulating interleukin-6 (IL-6) concentrations across multiple time points in the Oxiris arm of the trial. Conversely, the ratio of biomarkers suggestive of microcirculatory dysregulation appeared to favour the Oxiris group, suggesting mechanistic/biologic efficacy. Ultimately, the biomarker and cytokine alterations did not translate into statistically significant improvements in endothelial cell viability or the secondary clinical outcomes studied.

Consequently, OXICARD joins a growing body of hemoadsorption studies that demonstrate mechanistic efficacy without demonstrable clinical benefit (18). The critical question that must be addressed is whether cytokine removal, as currently conceptualized and deployed, is sufficient and well suited to meaningfully improve patient outcomes.

Methodological strengths and limitations

The strengths of OXICARD include its randomized design with microcirculation assessor blinding, robust primary and secondary outcome measurement, demonstration of implementation feasibility, incorporation of biomarker quantification, and an innovative ex vivo experimental component to evaluate isolated intervention effects on endothelial integrity, providing mechanistic insight alongside clinical data. Concurrently, several limitations merit careful consideration. First, the single-center design limits generalizability. Clinical outcomes following cardiac surgery are highly sensitive to institutional practices, including perfusion protocols, ICU management strategies, and clinician to clinician practice variability, such that feasible or promising solutions in one expert center may not translate across systems.

Second, the trial was underpowered to detect rare but critically relevant outcomes such as mortality, severe AKI, stroke, or need for prolonged mechanical ventilation. From a statistical perspective, OXICARD functions more convincingly as a feasibility and mechanistic study than as a definitive outcomes trial. Third, heterogeneity likely diluted treatment effects. The category of “high-risk cardiac surgery” encompasses a wide spectrum of procedures with distinct inflammatory profiles. Without biological stratification or responder analysis, meaningful subgroup effects may remain obscured.

Fourth, the primary outcome of the RCT employs a physiological proxy to approximate a derangement in end-organ perfusion. If the primary outcome tracked is the direct biologic effect on end-organ perfusion, it can be argued that directly measuring cerebral or renal perfusion would have strengthened causal inference. Similarly, the mechanistic and ex vivo substudies also highlight a recurrent methodological concern. By collecting blood samples from the patient rather than the CPB circuitry immediately distal to the Oxiris membrane, the isolated effect of the membrane on circulating inflammatory cytokines and biomarkers is inevitably diluted. Conversely, measuring cytokine and biomarker concentrations immediately proximal and distal to the Oxiris membrane during CPB would have contributed additional insight into the mechanistic efficacy of the intervention by enabling direct quantification of the effect of the Oxiris membrane at the molecular level. As the study currently stands, the biological signal is confounded by systemic dilution of effect.

Fifth, the ex-vivo endothelial cell viability study also requires certain physiological assumptions. Replacing the cell culture medium used to develop the endothelial cell line with patient plasma may itself affect cell viability, given the notable differences in their biochemical components (21,22). Furthermore, measurement of the isolated effect of the intervention on endothelial cell viability was compromised by the location of blood sampling (patient versus CPB circuit, once again diluting the effect of the intervention). Supplementing rather than replacing the cell culture medium with plasma samples obtained from the circuit immediately distal to the Oxiris membrane, or spiking the medium with cytokine concentrations matched to the effect of the intervention on circulating inflammatory mediators, would have yielded a more robust mechanistic model with a stronger causal relationship inference.

Collectively, these limitations illustrate a central epistemic principle: the greater the number of physiological and statistical assumptions required to link an observed signal to a clinical conclusion, the weaker the evidentiary strength of that conclusion. OXICARD is rich in biological signal, but the inferential distance between those signals and patient outcomes remains substantial.

Design considerations: principles of hemoadsorption and why current hemoadsorption devices fall short

If hemoadsorption is to function as a precision intervention rather than a generic anti-inflammatory adjunct, the limitations of current device design become immediately apparent. Existing hemoadsorption technologies are, by necessity, blunt instruments, relying predominantly on non-specific physicochemical properties such as molecular size, charge, and hydrophobicity (17,23). For example, CytoSorb removes molecules in the 5–60 kDa range (24,25), while Oxiris binds endotoxin and cytokines primarily through electrostatic interactions (26). These architectures inherently prioritize breadth over selectivity. Although such broad-spectrum approaches are appealing in theory, they generate several fundamental problems in practice. First, non-specificity raises the risk that beneficial mediators, including anti-inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, and regulatory proteins, are removed alongside pathogenic signals, potentially disrupting protective or reparative pathways. Second, the absolute quantity of cytokine removed may constitute only a negligible fraction of the total systemic burden, leading to a dilutional effect that limits its biological and clinical impact. Third, extracorporeal removal of circulating mediators may trigger compensatory upregulation through homeostatic feedback loops, paradoxically amplifying inflammatory signaling rather than attenuating it. This rationale may account for the observed effect of the Oxiris membrane on circulating concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the Oxiris group at multiple later time points compared to the standard group. This is further suggestive of a compensatory biological response, whereby acute extracorporeal cytokine removal disrupts endogenous feedback control and triggers a reactive upregulation of pro-inflammatory signaling, potentially attenuating or causing a deleterious effect on the intended immunomodulatory effect of hemoadsorption.

Finally, there appears to be a temporal mismatch between cytokine dynamics and device deployment as demonstrated by peak inflammatory cytokine concentrations in both groups arising later in the disease or perioperative trajectory, such that hemoadsorption is initiated before the critical point of maximal potential effect.

Mechanistic efficacy versus clinical effectiveness

At the heart of the OXICARD trial lies a familiar translational dilemma: mechanistic efficacy does not guarantee clinical effectiveness. Despite hemoadsorption devices reducing circulating cytokines, these effects do not reliably correlate with patients requiring fewer vasopressors, avoiding dialysis, or leaving the ICU sooner (13). This may be attributed to the complexity of the inflammatory cascade and its dynamic, multi-layered network with feedback loops, tissue compartmentalization, and context-dependent effects (13). Furthermore, cytokines are not inherently pathological and their indiscriminate chelation may attenuate protective anti-inflammatory signals, or cause further dysregulation through activation of negative feedback loops causing an even more exaggerated effect (56,57), as evidenced by increased pro-inflammatory IL-6 concentrations in the Oxiris arm (20). This observation is consistent with minimal beneficial alterations of the downstream processes that drive organ dysfunction (20).

This disconnect between mechanistic efficacy and clinical effectiveness is not unique to hemoadsorption. Perioperative medicine has encountered similar patterns studying the benefits of tight glycemic control (58), supraphysiologic oxygen delivery (59), and early goal-directed therapy in septic shock (60). These interventions improved physiological or biochemical targets without improving survival, reflecting the same underlying lesson: biomarkers are not outcomes, and physicians should continue to treat patients rather than numerical values (58-60).

Consequently, the most enduring lesson from the OXICARD trial is a clinical one. By collecting both biochemical and clinical data, the trial highlights a recurring temptation in translational research: to equate biomarker movement with therapeutic success. However, unless biomarker changes are convincingly and causally linked to patient-centered outcomes, they remain descriptive rather than therapeutic. Biomarkers may confirm that an intervention interacts with/disrupts biology, but they cannot substitute for clinical endpoints. In complex syndromes such as perioperative inflammation, biomarker reduction is neither necessary nor sufficient evidence of clinical benefit.

Redefining “high risk”: the problem of heterogeneity

Another recurring theme in perioperative medicine clinical trials is the heterogeneity of treatment effect. Labeling patients as “high risk” often obscures meaningful biological differences. For example, a re-do mitral valve replacement and an emergent type A aortic dissection repair both qualify under the umbrella of high risk cardiac surgery (61,62), yet vary considerably in their inflammatory pathways and trajectories (63,64). Consequently, it can be argued that the future of hemoadsorption lies not in its universal implementation, but in identifying the subset of patients whose inflammatory phenotype makes them likely responders or beneficiaries. To do so, however, would require pivoting from demographic risk scores toward biomarker-driven patient stratification, highlighting biomarkers’ potential to function as precision medicine tools (65).

Future directions: toward precision perioperative immunomodulation

Operationalizing a precision medicine framework in the context of hemoadsorption will require a fundamental shift in both trial design and technological development. Rather than evaluating these devices as universally applicable anti-inflammatory interventions, future research should focus on biologically informed, patient-specific strategies. Future trials should integrate comprehensive immune phenotyping using multi-omic approaches, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, to identify discrete inflammatory endotypes and treatment-responsive subgroups. Adaptive trial designs may further enhance therapeutic signal by enabling dynamic allocation of interventions based on real-time biomarker trajectories, while multicenter platform trials would improve generalizability, statistical power, and external validity.

Separate from these methodological advances, several fundamental mechanistic questions remain unresolved. For instance, it is unclear what magnitude of cytokine removal is required to meaningfully perturb systemic inflammatory networks. Furthermore, the extent to which cytokine hemoadsorption induces compensatory upregulation, thereby paradoxically amplifying inflammatory signaling through homeostatic feedback loops has yet to be elucidated.

From an engineering and translational standpoint, next-generation hemoadsorption technologies may benefit from incorporating molecular selectivity through aptamer-based or ligand-directed platforms. Such approaches could enable targeted removal of pathogenic mediators while preserving protective and regulatory signaling molecules. These hybrid biologic-synthetic systems offer a conceptual pathway to bridge the gap between non-specific bulk adsorption and precision immunomodulation, aligning device design with the broader objectives of precision perioperative medicine.

Aptamers: a path toward precision hemoadsorption

If the fundamental limitation of current hemoadsorption devices lies in their lack of molecular selectivity, the next logical step dictates pivoting toward platforms that can discriminate between pathogenic and protective mediators at the level of specific biological targets. In this regard, functional nucleic acids (notably aptamers) offer a particularly attractive technological solution. Unlike antibodies, aptamers are synthetically produced, chemically stable, non-immunogenic, readily modifiable, and less costly to produce (66,67). They can be engineered to bind with high affinity to virtually any protein, cytokine, receptor, or toxin (68). Within the context of hemoadsorption, aptamers can be immobilized on membrane surfaces or polymer beads (69), enabling the development of a new generation of selective adsorbers. These devices can facilitate the transition from indiscriminate chelation governed only by size or molecular charge, to targeting specific pro-inflammatory mediators like IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), endotoxin, or high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), while sparing protective or regulatory signaling molecules.

Aptamer nanotechnology is readily compatible with the principles of precision medicine by virtue of their ease of customization. For instance, modular aptamer panels could facilitate device personalization based on biomarker-driven patient stratification, with configurations tailored to individual inflammatory phenotypes. Furthermore, dynamic adaptability becomes possible in real time, as adsorber cartridges could be exchanged or combined in response to monitoring a patient’s evolving biomarker profile over time. Consequently, aptamer-based hemoadsorption represents a convergence of molecular biology, biomedical engineering, and precision medicine and has the potential to reframe hemoadsorption from a blunt chelation strategy into a programmable, target-specific immunomodulatory platform that is biologically informed and adaptable to evolving perioperative and critical care demands.

Conclusion: from biological signals to clinical strategy

The OXICARD study represents an important contribution to the evolving literature on perioperative immunomodulation. It demonstrates that cytokine hemoadsorption with the Oxiris membrane is feasible, safe, and biologically active in patients undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB. Yet it simultaneously delivers an important reminder that measurable biological effects do not automatically translate into improved clinical outcomes, highlighting a recurring temptation in translational research to equate biomarker alteration with therapeutic success. It must therefore be stressed that unless biomarker changes are convincingly and causally linked to patient-centered outcomes, they remain descriptive rather than therapeutic and cannot substitute for clinical endpoints. Consequently, in complex physiological derangements such as perioperative inflammation, biomarker reduction currently lacks sufficient evidence of clinical benefit.

Ultimately, the contribution of OXICARD lies not in demonstrating that cytokines can be chelated, but in clarifying the limits of its effects in routine practice. For clinicians caring for patients undergoing cardiac surgery, the lesson is a familiar one: interventions must be judged not by what they change in the laboratory, but by what they change for the patient. In this respect, OXICARD does not close the chapter on hemoadsorption, but it does sharpen the questions that future trials must answer, specifically identifying the timing, technological mechanism, and the patients in whom extracorporeal immunomodulation can meaningfully improve outcomes that truly matter.
