High-affinity inclusion of sitagliptin by cucurbit[7]uril: structural insights and fluorescence-based sensing
Authors: Mahmoud K. Al-joumhawy, Khaleel I. Assaf, Mohammad A. Alnajjar, Roaa Z. Sukkar, Abdel Monem M. Rawashdeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The host–guest complexation between sitagliptin and cucurbit[7]uril (CB7) was systematically investigated using a combination of spectroscopic, calorimetric, and computational techniques. 1H and 19F NMR studies revealed significant complexation-induced chemical shifts consistent with the inclusion of the hydrophobic moiety of sitagliptin inside the CB7 cavity, while the polar triazolopiperazine residue remained near the portal. UV-visible absorption titration indicated complex formation. A competitive fluorescence displacement assay based on berberine chloride as a fluorescent dye enabled reliable determination of the binding constant, yielding Ka of 1.41 × 107 M−1. Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) further confirmed strong binding with Ka ∼ 106 M−1 and revealed an enthalpy-driven interaction with favorable entropy contributions. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations supported the formation of an inclusion complex. In addition, the CB7·berberine reporter pair demonstrated a sensitive fluorescence response toward sitagliptin, with low limits of detection and quantification, indicating potential for sensing applications.

Introduction

Supramolecular host–guest chemistry has become a cornerstone of modern chemical research, offering versatile strategies for controlling molecular recognition, reactivity, and function in solution. By exploiting noncovalent interactions such as hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic effects, electrostatic forces, and dispersion interactions, host–guest systems enable the formation of well-defined complexes with tunable properties. These systems have found applications in areas ranging from drug delivery and chemical sensing to catalysis and environmental remediation.1–4 Among the diverse classes of macrocyclic receptors, cucurbiturils,5–7 cyclodextrins,8–10 calixarenes11–13 are attractive hosts due to their unique structural and physicochemical characteristics.

Cucurbit[n]urils (CBn, Fig. 1) are rigid, pumpkin-shaped molecules composed of glycoluril units linked by methylene bridges, which have hydrophobic cavities and electronegative carbonyl-lined portals that allow strong hydrophobic and ion–dipole interactions with guest molecules.5–7 In particular, cucurbit[7]uril (CB7) is water-soluble, with a suitable cavity size, and can form high affinity 1 : 1 inclusion complexes with a wide variety of biologically relevant guests.14–17 The host–guest chemistry of CB7 has been intensively investigated for small molecules,18,19 drug compounds,20–23 and biologically active guests.24–26 For instance, dopamine forms a 1 : 1 complex with CB7 with binding constant (Ka of 5.3 × 105 M−1), higher than its affinity for β-cyclodextrin (β-CD), which reflects the preference of CB7 for protonated amines.27 The binding of phenylephrine is even stronger (Ka = 4.0 × 106 M−1) and is the result of cooperative ion–dipole and hydrophobic interactions.28 In the pharmaceutical field, inclusion of piroxicam by CB7 results in an affinity that is 70 times greater than that for β-CD complex, which would facilitate enhanced dissolution and bioavailability.29

Other drug classes such as local anaesthetics and fluoroquinolone antibiotics also exhibit significant binding to CB7 using a variety of analytical techniques.23,30 These examples illustrate the versatility and high affinity of CB7 host–guest interactions, and the potential use of CB7 in drug complexation and formulation enhancement,31 particularly due to its low toxicity and high biocompatibility.32,33

Sitagliptin (Fig. 1) is a well-known medication used to treat type 2 diabetes mellitus. It is characterized by a strong and specific dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP 4) inhibitor that increases incretin hormones, thereby increasing glucose-dependent insulin secretion and suppressing inappropriate glucagon release.34,35 In addition to its glycaemic effects, sitagliptin is associated with anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective properties; studies suggest that sitagliptin may be associated with modulation of cytokine pathways.36,37 Despite its pharmaceutical importance, detailed studies on the supramolecular interactions of sitagliptin with macrocyclic receptors remain limited, particularly with regard to binding mode, thermodynamics, and potential applications. Sohajda et al. reported on the binding of sitagliptin with different cyclodextrins (CDs) by capillary electrophoresis and NMR spectroscopy, establishing 1 : 1 complexes, however, with poor binding affinity.38 Recently, carboxymethylated β-CD was magnetized with Fe3O4 to produce an active nanocarrier for encapsulating sitagliptin for targeted diabetes therapy.39 Two novel sitagliptin electrodes, in a polymeric matrix of polyvinyl chloride, using di-octyl phthalate as plasticizer were developed using β-CD and calix[8]arene (CX8) macrocycles.40 Both sensors showed excellent sensitivity and selectivity in bulk, pharmaceutical formulations, plasma, and urine.40 Sitagliptin is known to exhibit limited chemical stability under a variety of stress conditions, which can present challenges during formulation development and storage. Previous studies have demonstrated that sitagliptin is particularly susceptible to degradation in acidic and alkaline environments, in the presence of oxidizing agents, and may form multiple degradation products under thermal and light.41 These stability concerns highlight the need for effective stabilization strategies, such as supramolecular complexation.

Structurally, sitagliptin contains a trifluorophenyl moiety, an aliphatic linker, and a nitrogen-rich triazolopiperazine fragment with protonatable amine group (Fig. 1). This combination of hydrophobic and cationic characteristics makes sitagliptin an ideal candidate for host–guest complexation with CB7, where both inclusion inside the hydrophobic cavity and electrostatic stabilization at the portal can be expected to contribute significantly to binding. In this study, we report the complexation of sitagliptin with CB7 in aqueous solution, including characterization of binding affinity and structural features of the host–guest assembly by spectroscopic, calorimetric, and computational techniques.

Experimental section

Materials

Sitagliptin (CAS no. 486460-32-6) and berberine chloride (BE, CAS no. 141433-60-5) were purchased from TCI Chemicals. CB7 (80% content) was synthesized and purified according to the previous report.42

Instrumentation and sample preparation

Milli-Q water was used for solution preparation. Proton and fluorine nuclear magnetic resonance (1H and 19F NMR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker DRX-400 spectrometer. The 1H NMR spectra of free sitagliptin (2 mM), CB7 (2 mM) and CB7·sitagliptin (2 mM of each) were measured in neat D2O. UV-visible absorption and fluorescence spectra were recorded on Shimadzu UV-2450 and Varian Cary Eclipse, respectively. All measurements were performed at ambient temperature (unless otherwise stated), at approximately pH 7, using rectangular SUPRASIL® quartz glass cuvettes (Hellma Analytics) with 1 cm path length. The 1 : 1 binding constant of berberine chloride (BE, structure is provided in the SI) to CB7 was determined using fluorescence titration using 2 µM BE and varying concentrations of CB7 (Fig. S1, SI). For dye-displacement experiment, a preformed complex containing a mixture of BE (2 µM) and CB7 (1 µM) was titrated with increasing concentrations of sitagliptin, while maintaining the host·dye complex concentration constant. Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) measurements were performed using a VIP-ITC from Microcal Inc. at 25 °C. Solutions of the host (600 µM, loaded in the syringe) and guest (50 µM, placed in the sample cell) were thermostated and degassed using a ThermoVac accessory prior to each experiment. Data were analyzed using Origin 7.0 software by applying a one-set-of-sites binding model.

Computational details

Quantum-chemical calculations were carried out using Gaussian 16 program, Revision C.02.43 To account for different possible binding modes, multiple starting geometries were generated based on molecular docking. AutoDockVina was used for the guest docking and generating initial host–guest complexes (Fig. S3), applying a simulation box (50 × 50 × 50 Å).44 The exhaustiveness parameter was set to 100. Other plausible host–guest orientations were considered by manually placing the guest molecule inside and outside the cavity of the host. Geometry optimizations and energy calculations were performed within the framework of density functional theory (DFT) employing the B3LYP functional with Grimme's D3 dispersion correction,45 and wB97XD functional46 in conjunction with the 6-311++G(d,p) basis set. Solvent effects were incorporated using the Polarizable Continuum Model (PCM).47 All geometries (in both protonated and deprotonated forms) were fully optimized without any constraints. Each structure was subsequently optimized, and the lowest-energy conformers were identified and are presented in Fig. 7, S4 and S5. The non-covalent interactions (NCI) have been performed by using MULTIWFN v3.7.48

Results and discussion

The UV-visible absorption spectra of sitagliptin at pH 7 (sitagliptin is protonated, pKa ∼ 9.2)49 recorded upon incremental addition of CB7 showed systematic spectral changes, indicating host–guest interaction (Fig. 2). In particular, a gradual decrease in absorbance at ∼267 nm was observed with increasing CB7 concentration (Fig. 2). Such spectral changes are consistent with the formation of a ground-state inclusion complex between sitagliptin and CB7. The decrease in absorbance, as highlighted in the inset (Fig. 2), supports binding; however, the absence of a clear isosbestic point suggested either overlapping contributions from multiple species or interference from the host absorption. Notably, at higher CB7 concentrations, the intrinsic absorption of CB7 in the same spectral region becomes significant, leading to overlap with the sitagliptin absorption band (inset, Fig. 2). This overlap complicates reliable deconvolution of the spectra and prevents accurate fitting of the titration data using standard binding models. Although the UV-visible titration qualitatively confirmed complex formation, it is not suitable for quantitative determination of the binding constant under these conditions. Alternative techniques such as fluorescence-based assays (e.g., dye-displacement)50–53 and isothermal titration calorimetry are therefore more appropriate for determining binding affinity of the host–guest complexation.

UV-visible absorption spectra of sitagliptin (10 µM) with increasing CB7 concentration in aqueous solution.

The binding constant of sitagliptin with CB7 was quantitatively estimated using a competitive fluorescence displacement assay based on the BE dye. BE exhibits weak fluorescence in aqueous solution but becomes strongly emissive upon encapsulation within CB7 due to restriction of non-radiative decay pathways,54–56 which has been previously used for fluorescence displacement assay.28 The binding constant of BE was determined as 1.2 × 107 M−1, in a good agreement with reported values (Fig. S2, SI).54–57 Upon gradual addition of sitagliptin to a preformed CB7·BE complex (1.0 µM CB7 and 2.0 µM BE), a pronounced decrease in fluorescence intensity centered at ∼497 nm is observed (Fig. 3).

Competitive fluorescence titration of sitagliptin using CB7·BE (1.0 µM CB7 and 2.0 µM BE; λex = 425 nm). Inset indicated the fluorescence response of CB7·BE reporter pair upon the addition of sitagliptin in aqueous solution.

This fluorescence quenching indicates the displacement of BE from the CB7 cavity by sitagliptin. The progressive reduction in emission intensity reflects the competitive binding equilibrium between sitagliptin and BE for the CB7 cavity. The inset plot of fluorescence intensity at 497 nm as a function of sitagliptin concentration exhibits a typical displacement profile, which was fitted to an appropriate competitive binding model (inset, Fig. 3). From this analysis, the Ka for the CB7·sitagliptin complex was determined to be 1.41 × 107 M−1, indicating strong host–guest interaction. The high binding affinity can be attributed to the complementary fit of sitagliptin within the hydrophobic cavity of CB7, combined with favorable ion–dipole interactions between the protonated amine group of sitagliptin and the carbonyl-lined portal of CB7. Overall, the competitive fluorescence assay provides robust quantitative evidence of strong inclusion complex formation. Although, other methods have been described for the determination of sitagliptin in aqueous solution including spectrophotometry58,59 and HPLC,60 the sensitive fluorescence response of CB7·BE reporter pair toward sitagliptin highlights its potential for sensing applications in aqueous media. A linear fluorescence response was observed over the concentration range of 0–3 µM sitagliptin, enabling quantitative analysis. Based on this calibration, the limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were determined to be 1.0 and 2.9 ng L−1, respectively. These results demonstrate a highly sensitive fluorescence-based method for the detection of sitagliptin.

The thermodynamic data for the complexation between sitagliptin and CB7 was quantified by isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). The raw thermogram shows a series of pronounced exothermic injection peaks that gradually decrease in magnitude upon successive additions of the host, indicating saturation of the binding sites (Fig. 4). Integration of the heat signals followed by fitting to a one-set-of-sites model afforded a binding stoichiometry (n) of 1.04, suggesting a 1 : 1 host–guest complex. The Ka was determined to be 1.71 × 106 M−1, confirming strong binding between sitagliptin and CB7, albeit somewhat lower than the value obtained from fluorescence displacement measurements. The binding process is characterized by a negative enthalpy change (ΔH = −6.22 kcal mol−1), indicating that the interaction is enthalpically driven, consistent with favorable ion–dipole interactions between the protonated amine groups of sitagliptin and the carbonyl-lined portal of CB7, as well as hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions within the cavity. In addition, a positive entropy contribution (TΔS = +2.29 kcal mol−1) is observed, suggesting that the binding is also entropically favorable. This entropy gain can be attributed to the release of high-energy water molecules from the hydrophobic cavity of CB7 and partial desolvation of the guest upon complexation.61 Overall, the ITC results indicate that the complexation of sitagliptin with CB7 is both enthalpically and entropically favorable, with the former being the dominant driving force.

Microcalorimetric titration in water; top: raw ITC data for sequential injections of 600 µM CB7 into a sitagliptin solution (50 µM) and bottom: apparent reaction heats resulted from the integration of the calorimetric traces.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was employed to elucidate the binding mode and structural features of the complex formed between sitagliptin and CB7 in solution. The 1H NMR spectra of host–guest complexes are highly informative and are one of the primary tools used to confirm inclusion, binding mode, and dynamics. The 1H NMR spectrum of sitagliptin recorded in D2O displays eight distinct resonances attributable to the aliphatic spacer, the triazolopiperazine core, and the trifluorophenyl moiety (Fig. 5).

1H NMR spectra of sitagliptin (bottom, 2 mM), CB7 (middle, 2 mM), and CB7·sitagliptin mixture (top); measured in D2O at 25 °C.

Signals corresponding to the aliphatic spacer (H1–H3) appear in the range 2.6–3.0 ppm, whereas those assigned to the triazolopiperazine core (H4–H6) are observed at 3.8–4.3 ppm.62,63 The aromatic protons (H7 and H8) of the trifluorophenyl group resonate in the region 6.9–7.2 ppm. The 1H NMR of CB7 shows three signals that correspond to the methylene bridge protons at ∼4.1 and ∼5.7 ppm and the glycoluril methine protons at ∼5.4 ppm. The 1H NMR spectrum of the complex formed between sitagliptin and CB7 in D2O exhibited clear spectral changes consistent with host–guest complexation. Upon complexation, the aromatic protons of the trifluorophenyl moiety undergo noticeable upfield shifts (Δδ ≈ 0.5–1.1 ppm), indicating their encapsulation within the hydrophobic cavity of CB7. Similarly, the aliphatic spacer protons (H2 and H3) displayed upfield shifts, suggesting their penetration into the cavity of CB7. In contrast, the signals corresponding to the triazolopiperazine core (H4–H6) showed only minor downfield shifts, consistent with their location near the carbonyl-lined portal of the host. The methylene bridge protons of CB7 exhibited small but noticeable chemical shift and splitting, further supporting complex formation. The absence of separate resonances for free and bound species indicates fast exchange on the NMR timescale under the experimental conditions. These spectral features confirmed the formation of an inclusion complex, with the hydrophobic regions of sitagliptin preferentially residing inside the CB7 cavity while the more polar moiety remains solvent-exposed near the portal (see DFT results).

The 19F NMR spectra of sitagliptin provide complementary and highly sensitive insight into its host–guest complexation with CB7. In the free state, sitagliptin exhibits well-resolved fluorine signals corresponding to the trifluorophenyl moiety (A–C) and the trifluoromethyl group (D) (Fig. 6).62,63 Upon complexation with CB7, significant changes in the 19F NMR spectrum are observed (Fig. 6). Notably, the fluorine signals associated with the aromatic ring (A–C) exhibited downfield shifts, consistent with their partial or complete inclusion inside the hydrophobic cavity of CB7. In contrast, the CF3 signal (D) showed no shift, suggesting that trifluoromethyl group remained outside the cavity. Such complexation-induced chemical shifts for the 19F NMR are consistence with the complexation of perfluorinated guests with CBn.18

19F NMR spectra of sitagliptin (bottom, 2 mM) and CB7·sitagliptin mixture (top); measured in D2O at 25 °C.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations provide detailed molecular-level insight into the binding mode and energetics of the complex formed between sitagliptin and CB7. The initial host–guest complexes were generated using molecular docking, yielding nine representative inclusion complexes (Fig. S3, SI). These structures can be classified into two main groups: (i) those in which the aliphatic linker is positioned within the CB7 cavity, and (ii) those in which the trifluorophenyl moiety is encapsulated inside the cavity. In addition, alternative starting geometries, including the complexation through the triazolopiperazine residue as well as exclusion complexes, were also considered. All starting host–guest geometries were fully optimized at B3LYB-D3/6-311++G(d,p) and wB97XD/6-311++G(d,p), see Fig. 7 and S4, respectively. The calculated binding energies (ΔE) are list in Table 1. The optimized structures reveal that binding affinity strongly depends on both the protonation state of the guest and its orientation inside the CB7 cavity. The protonated form of sitagliptin consistently exhibits more favorable interaction energies (ΔΔE > 10 kcal mol−1) compared to the deprotonated form, highlighting the critical role of electrostatic and ion–dipole interactions between the positively charged ammonium group and the carbonyl portal of CB7 (Fig. 7 and Table 1). The binding through the trifluorophenyl moiety (mode 1) or through the triazolopiperazine residue (mode 2) were energetically similar. In contrast, the orientation in which the spacer is centered inside the CB7 cavity (mode 3) was associated with higher binding energies. In addition, the possibility of forming exclusion complexes between CB7 and the protonated form of sitagliptin was investigated (Fig. S5). The DFT results revealed that these exclusion complexes are energetically less favorable than the corresponding inclusion complexes supporting the preferential formation of the inclusion complex in solution, as revealed by the complexation-induced chemical shifts observed in the 1H and 19F NMR spectra (Fig. 5 and 6). It should be noted that the formation of exclusion complexes between CBn and organic (e.g. alkyammonium) guests are generally rare and have primarily been reported in gas-phase64,65 and solid-state.66 In aqueous solution, protons located inside the hydrophobic cavity of CBn experience a different magnetic environment than those exposed to bulk solvent, resulting in increased magnetic shielding and consequently characteristic upfield shifts in the 1H NMR spectrum.67 This behavior is well documented for inclusion complexes of CB7 and related CBn.19,28 In contrast, if the guest is associated only externally (non-encapsulated or exclusion complex), the protons remain exposed to the aqueous environment and are expected to exhibit little or no change in chemical shift, typically only a few hundredths to a few tenths of a ppm due to weak external interactions. The substantial upfield shifts observed for the trifluorophenyl protons (Δδ ∼ 0.5–1.1 ppm) are therefore consistent with encapsulation of this moiety within the CB7 cavity rather than external association (exclusion complex). Overall, the DFT results confirm that both hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions are key driving forces governing the strong host–guest inclusion association.

Noncovalent interaction (NCI) analysis was performed using the reduced density gradient (RDG) approach as implemented in Multiwfn48 to elucidate the nature of intermolecular interactions governing the complexation between sitagliptin and CB7 (Fig. 8). The RDG versus sign(λ2)ρ scatter plots of the different binding modes displayed characteristic features corresponding to different types of noncovalent interactions. Pronounced spikes in the negative sign(λ2)ρ region indicated the presence of attractive interactions, including hydrogen bonding and ion–dipole interactions between the protonated amine group of sitagliptin and the carbonyl portal of CB7 (Fig. 8A–C). In addition, a broad distribution of low-RDG points around sign(λ2)ρ ≈ 0 suggested significant van der Waals interactions. Conversely, the positive sign(λ2)ρ region reflects weak steric repulsion arising from close contacts within the confined host cavity. The corresponding NCI isosurface further supported these observations (Fig. 8D–F). The presence of blue-colored regions indicates strong attractive interactions, primarily localized near the portal where electrostatic interactions dominate. Extensive green isosurfaces are observed around the encapsulated residues of sitagliptin, highlighting the prevalence of dispersive van der Waals interactions inside the cavity.

Noncovalent interactions of CB7·sitagliptin complex. 2D scatter plots (A–C) and color-filled RDG isosurfaces (D–F). Arrows indicate hydrogen bonding interactions.

Overall, the strong and well-defined interaction between sitagliptin and CB7, supported by complementary spectroscopic and calorimetric evidence, highlights the potential of this system for practical applications. The high binding affinity, combined with the sensitive fluorescence response of the reporter displacement assay, enables the development of a robust and selective sensing platform for sitagliptin in aqueous media. Beyond its sensing applications, the formation of a stable CB7·sitagliptin inclusion complex provides a promising platform for future investigations aimed at evaluating the potential of CB7 encapsulation to improve the physicochemical and pharmaceutical properties of sitagliptin, including enhanced stability, controlled release, and optimized drug delivery.

Conclusions

In summary, the present study demonstrates the formation of a stable inclusion complex between sitagliptin and cucurbit[7]uril (CB7) in aqueous solution. Spectroscopic investigations, including NMR and fluorescence displacement assays, along with isothermal titration calorimetry, confirm strong host–guest complexation with high binding affinity. The binding process is primarily enthalpy-driven, with additional contribution from favorable entropy, in which the hydrophobic moiety of sitagliptin is encapsulated within the CB7 cavity and the polar moiety remains near the portal. Importantly, the CB7-berberine fluorescence displacement system enables highly sensitive detection of sitagliptin, highlighting its applicability as a sensing platform. Overall, this work provides detailed molecular-level insight into the interaction of sitagliptin with cucurbiturils and establishes a foundation for developing supramolecular strategies for drug sensing and functional modulation in aqueous environments.
