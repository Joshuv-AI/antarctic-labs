Complex Weyl correspondence and harmonic representation of S ​ U ​ ( p , q ) SU(p,q)
Benjamin Cahen

Abstract
. We study the harmonic representation of S ​ U ​ ( p , q ) SU(p,q) in connection to the complex Weyl correspondence on the Fock space. In particular, we give explicit formulas for the complex Weyl symbols of the harmonic representation operators. Similar results are also obtained for the extended harmonic representation of the semi-direct product of the ( 2 ​ p + 2 ​ q + 1 ) (2p+2q+1) -dimensional Heisenberg group by S ​ U ​ ( p , q ) SU(p,q) .

1.
Introduction

Let
p
and
q
be positive integers and let
n=p+q
.
Let
H_{n}
be the
(2n+1)
-dimensional Heisenberg group. Then

SU(p,q)
naturally acts on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, hence on
H_{n}
. We denote by
k\cdot h
the action of
k\in SU(p,q)
on
h\in H_{n}
. Also, for each unitary irreducible representation
\rho
of
H_{n}
and each
k\in SU(p,q)
, we denote by
\rho_{k}
the representation of
H_{n}
defined
by
\rho_{k}(h)=\rho(k\cdot h)
for each
h\in H_{n}
.

Now, we fix a non degenerate irreducible unitary representation
\rho
of
H_{n}
on the Fock space
\mathcal{F}
. Then, for each
k\in SU(p,q)
,
\rho_{k}
and
\rho

have the same central character hence are unitarily equivalent.
Thus there exists a family
\sigma(k)
,
k\in SU(p,q)
of unitary operators on
\mathcal{F}

such that

\rho_{k}(h)\sigma(k)=\sigma(k)\rho(h)

for each
k\in SU(p,q)
and
h\in H_{n}
.
Moreover, one can choose the operators
\sigma(k)
in such a way that
\sigma

is a unitary representation of
SU(p,q)
, see
[
10
,
15
]
. Then
\sigma

is called the
harmonic representation
of
SU(p,q)
and its irreducible components
called the
ladder representations
are of some importance both in Mathematics

[
21
]
and in Physics
[
13
]
. Note that
\sigma
is analogous to the so-called
metaplectic representation of
Sp(n,{\mathbb{R}})
which was intensively studied, see for
instance
[
16
]
and
[
21
]
. More precisely,
SU(p,q)
can be embedded in

Sp(n,{\mathbb{R}})
and then
\sigma
can also be introduced as a restriction of the
metaplectic representation. Consequently, it is possible to study
\sigma
starting from
known results on the metaplectic representation, see in particular
[
21
]
. Here, however,
we prefer to consider
\sigma
independently of the metaplectic representation as in
[
15
]

and
[
10
]
.

The present paper can be considered as a continuation of
[
10
]
. In
[
10
]
,
we follow the method indicated in
[
24
, p. 533]
and obtain an explicit expression for the Berezin symbol or, equivalently, for the integral kernel of
\sigma(k)
for
k\in SU(p,q)
, by using the highest weight representations of
H_{n}\rtimes SU(p,q)
. Then we get formulas which are close to those given in
[
15
]
and
[
23
]
.

Here, we give explicit formulas for the complex Weyl symbol of
\sigma(k)
,
k\in SU(p,q)
.
The complex Weyl calculus
W
can be defined as the correspondence between operators on
\mathcal{F}
and functions on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
obtained by translating the usual Weyl correspondence by means of the Bargmann transform, see
[
16
]
.

Our computation of
W(\sigma(k))
is mainly based on the results of
[
10
]
and on basic Gaussian calculus. We also give a formula for
W(d\sigma(X))
,
X\in su(p,q)
. Note that similar
results on Weyl symbols of metaplectic representation operators were obtained by various methods, see
[
11
,
13
,
14
,
19
]
. Such formulas for Weyl symbols are useful to study the quantum unitary propagators for quadratic Hamiltonians
which are important in quantum mechanics, see
[
13
, Chapter 3]
.

The extended harmonic representation is the unitary representation
\pi
of
G:=H_{n}\rtimes SU(p,q)
defined by
\pi(h,k)=\rho(h)\sigma(k)
for each
h\in H_{n}
and
k\in SU(p,q)
.
Note that
\pi
is the exact analogue of the extended metaplectic
representation
[
16
, p. 196]
.
In this paper, we deduce a formula for
W(\pi(g))
,
g\in G
, from the previously established formula
for
W(\sigma(k))
,
k\in SU(p,q)
by exploiting the covariance of
W
with respect to

\rho
, see
[
7
,
9
]
.

This paper is organized as follows. We first introduce the Heisenberg group, its unitary irreducible representations on
\mathcal{F}
and the Berezin correspondence on
\mathcal{F}
(Section
2
). Then, we recall the construction of the complex Weyl correspondence
W

from a Stratonovich-Weyl quantizer defined by using
\rho

(Section
3
). In Section
4
, we establish the first main result of the paper,
namely some formulas for
W(\sigma(k))
(Theorem
4.5
). In Section
5
, we introduce
the extended harmonic representation
\pi
and give a formula for
W(\pi(g))
. This is the second main result of the paper (Theorem
5.3
). We also give a formula for
W(d\pi(X))
for

X
in the Lie algebra of
G
. See also
[
12
]
, where similar results for the extended
metaplectic representation are established by direct, but tedious, computations.

2.
Berezin quantization on the Fock space

We first introduce the Heisenberg group.
Let
p
and
q
be positive integers and let
n=p+q
. For
z=(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}e_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and
w=(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we write
zw:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}w_{j}
for simplicity. For
A\in M_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
, we define
Az:=zA^{t}
where the subscript
t
denotes transposition.

Let
J_{0}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}I_{p}&0\\
0&-I_{q}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and let
\omega
be the symplectic form on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
defined by

\omega((z,w),(z^{\prime},w^{\prime}))=\tfrac{i}{2}(z(J_{0}w^{\prime})-z^{\prime}(J_{0}w)).

for
z,w,z^{\prime},w^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

The
(2n+1)
-dimensional (real) Heisenberg group is

H_{n}:=\{((z,{\bar{z}}),c)\,:\,z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n},c\in{\mathbb{R}}\}

equipped with the multiplication defined by

((z,{\bar{z}}),c)\cdot((z^{\prime},{\bar{z}^{\prime}}),c^{\prime})=((z+z^{\prime},{\bar{z}}+{\bar{z}^{\prime}}),c+c^{\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}\omega((z,{\bar{z}}),(z^{\prime},{\bar{z}^{\prime}}))).

For
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we write
z=(z^{+},z^{-})
where
z^{+}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{p}
and

z^{-}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{q}
. Let
s:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be the involution defined by
s(z)=(z^{+},\bar{z}^{-})
.

The center of
H_{n}
is
C(H_{n}):=\{((0,0),c)\,:\,c\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
.
Let
\lambda>0
. By the Stone-von Neumann
theorem, there exists a (unique, up to unitary equivalence) unitary
irreducible representation
\rho_{\lambda}
of
H_{n}
whose restriction to
C(H_{n})
is the character
\chi_{\lambda}:((0,0),c)\rightarrow e^{i\lambda c}
,
see
[
16
,
29
]
. Then
\rho_{\lambda}
is called a
generic
representation of
H_{n}
. The general method for constructing holomorphic representations of Hermitian Lie groups developped in
[
24
]
when applied to
H_{n}
leads to introduce the Fock space, that is,
the Hilbert space

{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
consisting of all holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such
that

\|f\|^{2}:=\left(\tfrac{\lambda}{2\pi}\right)^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}\,|f(z)|^{2}\,e^{-\lambda|z|^{2}/2}\,dm(z)<+\infty

where
dm(z):=dx\,dy
is the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
(
z=x+iy
,
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
) and the realization of
\rho_{\lambda}
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}

given by

(\rho_{\lambda}(g)f)(z)=e^{i\lambda c_{0}}\,\exp\lambda\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{s(z_{0})}z-\tfrac{1}{4}|z_{0}|^{2}\right)\,f(z-s(z_{0}))

for
g=((z_{0},{\bar{z}_{0}}),c_{0})\in H_{n}
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. See
[
10
]
for details.

Recall that
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. More precisely, for each
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, let
e_{z}
be the function of
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
defined by

e_{z}(w)=\exp(\lambda{\bar{z}}w/2)
. Then we have the reproducing property

f(z)=\langle f,e_{z}\rangle
for each
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
.

Now, we introduce the Berezin
calculus on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}

[
3
,
4
,
7
]
.
The Berezin (covariant) symbol of the operator
A
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
is the
function
S(A)
defined on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
by

S(A)(z):=\frac{\langle A\,e_{z}\,,\,e_{z}\rangle}{\langle e_{z}\,,\,e_{z}\rangle}

and the double Berezin symbol of
A
is the function
S^{\prime}(A)
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
defined by

S^{\prime}(A)(z,w):=\frac{\langle A\,e_{w}\,,\,e_{z}\rangle}{\langle e_{w}\,,\,e_{z}\rangle}.

On the one hand, by the reproducing property, for each operator
A
on

{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
,
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
,
we have

(Af)(z)=\langle Af,e_{z}\rangle=\langle f,A^{\ast}e_{z}\rangle.

This shows that the kernel
k_{A}(z,w)
of
A
is given by

k_{A}(z,w)=\overline{(A^{\ast}e_{z})(w)}=\langle Ae_{w},e_{z}\rangle=S^{\prime}(A)(z,w)\langle e_{w},e_{z}\rangle.

On the other hand,
S^{\prime}(A)(z,w)
is holomorphic in the variable
z
and anti-holomorphic in the variable
w
, then
S^{\prime}(A)
is determined by its restriction to the diagonal of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, that is, by
S(A)
. Consequently,
A
is determined by
S(A)
.
We have the following result, see
[
3
,
7
,
9
,
30
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

(1)

For each operator
A
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
and each
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we
have
S(A^{\ast})(z)=\overline{S(A)(z)}
;

(2)

Consider the action of
H_{n}
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
defined by

h\cdot z=z+s(z_{0})
for
h=((z_{0},\bar{z_{0}}),c_{0})\in H_{n}
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Then, for each operator
A
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
,
h\in H_{n}
and

z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we have

S(A)(h\cdot z)=S(\rho_{\lambda}(h)^{-1}A\rho_{\lambda}(h))(z);

(3)

Denote by
{\mathcal{L}}_{2}({{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}})
the space of all Hilbert-Schmidt operators on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
(endowed with the
Hilbert-Schmidt norm). Then
S
is a bounded operator from

{\mathcal{L}}_{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda})
to
L^{2}({\mathbb{C}}^{n},\mu_{\lambda})
which
is one-to-one and has dense range.

Note that the second assertion of the proposition immediatly follows from the following
relation. For each
h=((z_{0},\bar{z_{0}}),c_{0})\in H_{n}
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we have

(2.1)

\rho_{\lambda}(h)e_{z}=\exp\left(i\lambda c_{0}-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}s(z_{0}){\bar{z}}-\tfrac{\lambda}{4}|z_{0}|^{2}\right)\,e_{h\cdot z}.

Let us also recall the Berezin transform.
Let
S^{\ast}
be the adjoint operator of
S:{\mathcal{L}}_{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda})\rightarrow L^{2}({\mathbb{C}}^{n},m_{\lambda})
where
m_{\lambda}
is the measure on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
defined by
dm_{\lambda}(z)=({2\pi})^{-n}{\lambda}^{n}dm(z)
. Then
the Berezin transform is the operator
B

on
L^{2}({\mathbb{C}}^{n},m_{\lambda})
defined by
B:=SS^{\ast}
.
Hence we have
the integral formula

(Bf)(z)=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}\,f(w)\,e^{-\lambda|z-w|^{2}/2}\,dm_{\lambda}(w),

see
[
3
,
4
,
30
]
.
It is also known that
B=\exp(\Delta/2\lambda)
where

\Delta=4\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial^{2}/\partial z_{k}\partial{\bar{z}}_{k}
,
see
[
22
,
30
]
.

3.
Complex Weyl correspondence on the Fock space

As the classical Weyl correspondence, the complex Weyl correspondence can be constructed from a
Stratonovich-Weyl quantizer
, see
[
1
,
17
]
. Here we follow more or less the exposition of
[
9
]
, see also
[
8
]
.

We start from the parity operator
R
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
defined by

(Rf)(z)=2^{n}f(-z).

The map
z\rightarrow h_{z}:=((s(z),\overline{s(z)}),0)
is a section
for the action of
H_{n}
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, that is, we have
h_{z}\cdot 0=z

for each
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Let us define, for each
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
,

\Omega(z):=\rho_{\lambda}(h_{z})R\rho_{\lambda}(h_{z})^{-1}.

By an easy computation, we get

(\Omega(z)f)(w)=2^{n}\exp\left(\lambda({\bar{z}}w-|z|^{2})\right)f(2z-w)

for each
z,w\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
The map
\Omega
is called a
Stratonovich-Weyl quantizer
and satisfies the covariance property

\Omega(h\cdot z)=\rho_{\lambda}(h)\Omega(z)\rho_{\lambda}(h)^{-1}

for each
h\in H_{n}
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

For each trace-class operator
A
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
, we define the function
W(A)
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
by

W(A)(z):=\Tr(A\Omega(z)).

Proposition 3.1
.

[
8
]
For each trace-class operator
A
on

{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}

and each
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we have

(3.1)

W(A)(z)=2^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}k_{A}(z+w,z-w)\exp\left(\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\left(-z{\bar{z}}-w{\bar{w}}+z{\bar{w}}-{\bar{z}}w\right)\right)dm_{\lambda}(w).

We can use this integral formula in order to define
W(A)
for more general operators
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
as for instance Hilbert-Schmidt operators or even differential
operators with polynomial coefficients, see
[
1
]
,
[
8
, Proposition 4]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The map
W:{\mathcal{L}}_{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda})\rightarrow L^{2}({\mathbb{C}}^{n},m_{\lambda})
is the unitary part in the polar decomposition of
S
, that is we have
S=(SS^{\ast})^{1/2}W=B^{1/2}W
.

Various proofs of this proposition can be found in
[
25
,
22
,
7
]
and
[
9
]
.
Note that, in
[
25
]
, the space
{\mathcal{L}}_{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda})
is identified
with the tensor product
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}\otimes\overline{{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}}

of
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
with its conjugate. Then
S
can be seen as the restriction map

f(z,w)\rightarrow f(z,z)\exp(-\lambda|z|^{2}/2).

Note that the Weyl correspondence introduced in
[
25
]
is the inverse map of
W
.

An alternative method is to define
W
(on Hilbert-Schmidt operators) as
the unitary part in the polar decomposition of
S:{\mathcal{L}}_{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda})\rightarrow L^{2}({\mathbb{C}}^{n},m_{\lambda})
and then to obtain Eq.
3.1

by using the properties of
B
, see Section
2
.

Now, we can easily verify the following proposition which will be used later.

Proposition 3.3
.

[
8
]

The map
W
is covariant with respect to
\rho_{\lambda}
, that is,
for each operator
A
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
,
h\in H_{n}
and

z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we have

W(A)(h\cdot z)=W(\rho_{\lambda}(h)^{-1}A\rho_{\lambda}(h))(z).

Various generalizations of the complex Weyl correspondence can be found in
[
1
,
2
,
17
]
.

4.
Complex Weyl symbols of harmonic representation operators

Here we retain the notation of Sect.
2
. We write the elements
g
of
SU(p,q)

as block matrices
g=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)
where
A,B,C,D
are
p\times p
,
p\times q
,
q\times p
,
q\times q

matrices satisfying the relations

\displaystyle AA^{\star}-BB^{\star}=I_{p},\,\,CC^{\star}-DD^{\star}=-I_{q},

\displaystyle A^{\star}A-C^{\star}C=I_{p},\,\,B^{\star}B-D^{\star}D=-I_{q},

\displaystyle A^{\star}B=C^{\star}D,\,\,BD^{\star}=AC^{\star}.

Here the subscript
\star
denotes the conjugate transpose.

The group
SU(p,q)
naturally acts on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
hence acts on
H_{n}
by

k\cdot((z,\bar{z}),c)=((kz,\overline{kz}),c).

We fix
\lambda>0
. For simplicity, we denote
\rho:=\rho_{\lambda}
and
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
.

For each
k\in SU(p,q)
, we define
\rho_{k}
by
\rho_{k}(h):=\rho(k\cdot h)
. Then, since
\omega
is
SU(p,q)
-invariant, that is, we have

\omega(k(z,\bar{z}),k(z^{\prime},\bar{z^{\prime}}))=\omega((z,\bar{z}),(z^{\prime},\bar{z^{\prime}}))

for each
z,z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we can verify that
\rho_{k}

is also a generic representation of
H_{n}
, which has the same central character as
\rho
.

In
[
10
]
, we presented a proof of the following result which gives explicit formulas for the kernels of the harmonic representation operators. This theorem, together with Proposition

3.1
, is the main tool for the computations of the complex Weyl symbols of the
harmonic representation operators.

Theorem 4.1
.

For each
k=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in SU(p,q)
, let
\sigma(k)
be the operator on
\mathcal{F}
with kernel

b_{k}(z,w)=(\Det A)^{-1}\exp\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\left(-({\overline{D}}^{-1}{\overline{C}}\bar{w_{1}})\bar{w_{2}}+({\overline{D}}^{-1}z_{2})\bar{w_{2}}+(A^{-1}z_{1})\bar{w_{1}}+z_{1}({\overline{B}}({\overline{D}})^{-1}z_{2})\right)

where
z=(z_{1},z_{2}),w=(w_{1},w_{2})
with
z_{1},w_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{p}
and

z_{2},w_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{q}
. Then

(1)

\sigma
is a unitary representation of
SU(p,q)
;

(2)

For each
h\in H_{n}
and each
k\in SU(p,q)
, we have
\rho_{k}(h)\sigma(k)=\sigma(k)\rho(h)
;

(3)

For each
k=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in SU(p,q)

and each
z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{p}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{q}
, we have

\displaystyle S(\sigma(k))

\displaystyle(z)=(\Det A)^{-1}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\left(-|z|^{2}-({\overline{D}}^{-1}{\overline{C}}\bar{z_{1}})\bar{z_{2}}+({\overline{D}}^{-1}z_{2})\bar{z_{2}}+(A^{-1}z_{1})\bar{z_{1}}+z_{1}({\overline{B}}({\overline{D}})^{-1}z_{2})\right).

(4)

Let
X=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in su(p,q)
.
Then, for each
z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{p}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{q}
, we have

S(d\sigma(X))(z)=-\Tr(A)-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\bigl(({\overline{C}}\bar{z_{1}})\bar{z_{2}}+({\overline{D}}z_{2})\bar{z_{2}}+(Az_{1})\bar{z_{1}}-z_{1}({\overline{B}}z_{2})\bigr).

In fact, we can also derive Theorem
4.1
from results on the metaplectic representation.
In particular, we can recover (up to normalization) Theorem
4.1
, (1) from
[
16
, Eq. (4.38)]
by using
the embedding

k=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\rightarrow{\tilde{k}}=\begin{pmatrix}A&0&0&B\\
0&{\overline{D}}&{\overline{C}}&0\\
0&{\overline{B}}&{\overline{A}}&0\\
C&0&0&D\\
\end{pmatrix}

from
SU(p,q)
to a subgroup of
SU(n,n)
which is isomorphic to
Sp(n,{\mathbb{R}})
.

Note that there are many ways to construct the metaplectic representation. Let us mention some of them.

In
[
28
]
, Sternberg and Wolf used representations of Lie superalgebras to construct the metaplectic representation of
Sp(n,{\mathbb{R}})
and also studied its restriction
to
U(p,q)
,
p+q=n
. Formulas (4.11) and (4.12) in
[
28
]
then correspond to
Theorem
4.1
, (4).

In
[
26
]
, in order to understand some invariance properties of theta functions, Peetre
introduced a new object called the
Fock bundle
which is a Hilbert bundle over the
Siegel’s generalized upper halfplane whose fibers are Fock spaces. This also led to a construction of the metaplectic representation, see the last formulas in
[
26
, Sect. 5]
.
See also
[
27
]
for direct computations in the case
n=2
.

Blattner and Rawnsley
[
5
]
used geometrical quantization techniques to introduce and
study the harmonic representation.

In
[
15
]
, a formula for the kernels of the harmonic representation operators
was obtained by group-theorical methods. This formula is similar to that of
Theorem
4.1
, (1).

More generally, Howe
[
20
]
took a higher point of view by introducing and studying
the
oscillator semigroup
which is a semigroup of contractions whose closure contains
the metaplectic representation operators.

Now we aim to compute
W(\sigma(k))
for
k\in SU(p,q)
. This computation is based on the following lemmas.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
R,S,T\in M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
such that
R
and
T

are symmetric. Assume that
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}R&S\\
S^{t}&T\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
is invertible and denote by
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{t}&\gamma\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
its inverse. Then we have

\begin{pmatrix}-R&2I_{n}-S\\
S^{t}-2I_{n}&T\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{t}&\gamma\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-R&S-2I_{n}\\
2I_{n}-S^{t}&T\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}R+4\gamma&4I_{n}-S-4\beta^{t}\\
4I_{n}-S^{t}-4\beta&T+4\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

By writing

\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{t}&\gamma\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}R&S\\
S^{t}&T\end{pmatrix}=I_{2n}

z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Assume,
moreover, that
k-I_{n}
is also invertible and define
z_{1}:=(k-I_{n})^{-1}z_{0}
and

h:=((z_{1},{\bar{z}_{1}}),0)
. Then we have

\displaystyle h^{-1}(k\cdot h)=

\displaystyle((-z_{1},-{\bar{z}_{1}}),0)((kz_{1},\overline{kz_{1}}),0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((z_{0},{\bar{z}_{0}}),-\tfrac{1}{2}\omega((z_{1},{\bar{z}_{1}}),(kz_{1},\overline{kz_{1}})).

Let us denote
c:=-\tfrac{1}{2}\omega((z_{1},{\bar{z}_{1}}),(kz_{1},\overline{kz_{1}})
.
Then we have

W(\rho((z_{0},{\bar{z}_{0}}),0)\sigma(k))(z)=e^{-i\lambda c}W(\sigma(k))(h\cdot z).

By Theorem
4.5
, we get

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle(\rho((z_{0},{\bar{z}_{0}}),0)\sigma(k))(z)=2^{n}|\Det(k+I_{n})|^{-1}e^{-i\lambda c}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\begin{pmatrix}z+s(z_{1})&\overline{z+s(z_{1})}\end{pmatrix}J({\tilde{k}}+I_{2n})^{-1}({\tilde{k}}-I_{2n})\begin{pmatrix}z+s(z_{1})\\
\overline{z+s(z_{1})}\end{pmatrix}\right).

We are led to consider the following functions

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(z,k):=

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\begin{pmatrix}z&{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix}J({\tilde{k}}+I_{2n})^{-1}({\tilde{k}}-I_{2n})\begin{pmatrix}z\\
{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix};

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}(z,k):=

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{1})&\overline{s(z_{1})}\end{pmatrix}J({\tilde{k}}+I_{2n})^{-1}({\tilde{k}}-I_{2n})\begin{pmatrix}z\\
{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix};

\displaystyle\varphi_{3}(z,k):=

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\begin{pmatrix}z&{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix}J({\tilde{k}}+I_{2n})^{-1}({\tilde{k}}-I_{2n})\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{1})\\
\overline{s(z_{1})}\end{pmatrix};

\displaystyle\varphi_{4}(z,k):=

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{1})&\overline{s(z_{1})}\end{pmatrix}J({\tilde{k}}+I_{2n})^{-1}({\tilde{k}}-I_{2n})\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{1})\\
\overline{s(z_{1})}\end{pmatrix};

\displaystyle\varphi_{5}(z,k):=

\displaystyle-i\lambda c=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}i\omega((z_{1},{\bar{z}_{1}}),(kz_{1},\overline{kz_{1}})).

Note that

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}(z,k)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\begin{pmatrix}z&{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix}({\tilde{k}}^{t}-I_{2n})({\tilde{k}}^{t}+I_{2n})^{-1}J^{t}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{1})\\
\overline{s(z_{1})}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\begin{pmatrix}z&{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix}J({\tilde{k}}^{-1}-I_{2n})({\tilde{k}}^{-1}+I_{2n})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{1})\\
\overline{s(z_{1})}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\begin{pmatrix}z&{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix}J(I_{2n}-{\tilde{k}})(I_{2n}+{\tilde{k}})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{1})\\
\overline{s(z_{1})}\end{pmatrix}

by Lemma
5.2
. This implies that

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}(z,k)+\varphi_{3}(z,k)=

\displaystyle\lambda\begin{pmatrix}z&{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix}J({\tilde{k}}+I_{2n})^{-1}({\tilde{k}}-I_{2n})\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{1})\\
\overline{s(z_{1})}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\begin{pmatrix}z&{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix}J({\tilde{k}}+I_{2n})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{0})\\
\overline{s(z_{0})}\end{pmatrix}

by Lemma
5.2
again.

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{4}(z,k)=

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{0})&\overline{s(z_{0})}\end{pmatrix}({\tilde{k}}^{t}-I_{2n})^{-1}J({\tilde{k}}+I_{2n})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{0})\\
\overline{s(z_{0})}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{0})&\overline{s(z_{0})}\end{pmatrix}J{\tilde{k}}(I_{2n}-{\tilde{k}})^{-1}({\tilde{k}}+I_{2n})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{0})\\
\overline{s(z_{0})}\end{pmatrix}

and also

\displaystyle\varphi_{5}(z,k)=

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{2}i\omega((z_{1},{\bar{z}_{1}}),(kz_{1},\overline{kz_{1}})-(z_{1},{\bar{z}_{1}}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{2}i\omega((z_{1},{\bar{z}_{1}}),(z_{0},{\bar{z}_{0}}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{\lambda}{4}(z_{1}^{+}{\bar{z}_{0}}^{+}-z_{1}^{-}{\bar{z}_{0}}^{-}-z_{0}^{+}{\bar{z}_{1}}^{+}+z_{0}^{-}{\bar{z}_{1}}^{-})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{4}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{0})&\overline{s(z_{0})}\end{pmatrix}J\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{1})\\
\overline{s(z_{1})}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{4}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{0})&\overline{s(z_{0})}\end{pmatrix}J({\tilde{k}}-I_{2n})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{0})\\
\overline{s(z_{0})}\end{pmatrix}.

Hence we get

\displaystyle\varphi_{4}(z,k)

\displaystyle+\varphi_{5}(z,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{4}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{0})&\overline{s(z_{0})}\end{pmatrix}J(I_{2n}-2{\tilde{k}}({\tilde{k}}+I_{2n})^{-1})({\tilde{k}}-I_{2n})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{0})\\
\overline{s(z_{0})}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{\lambda}{4}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{0})&\overline{s(z_{0})}\end{pmatrix}J({\tilde{k}}+I_{2n})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}s(z_{0})\\
\overline{s(z_{0})}\end{pmatrix}.

By putting all these relations together and observing that

W(\rho((z_{0},{\bar{z}_{0}}),0)\sigma(k))(z)=2^{n}|\Det(k+I_{n})|^{-1}\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{j=5}\varphi_{j}(z,k)\right),

we obtain the result.
∎

Example.
We take
p=q=1
. Let

.
Let

where

,

.
Then we have
{\tilde{k}}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}e^{it}I_{2}&0\\

gives

\displaystyle(\pi(g))(z)=2(1+\cos(t))^{-1}e^{i\lambda c_{0}}\exp\left(-i\lambda|z|^{2}\tan(\tfrac{1}{2}t)\right)

\displaystyle\times

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
Y=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in su(p,q)
,
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
,
c_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Let

. Then, for each

, we have

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle(d\pi(X))(z)=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(\overline{s(z_{0})}z-{\bar{z}}s(z_{0}))+i\lambda c_{0}-\Tr(A)

\displaystyle-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\bigl(({\overline{C}}\bar{z_{1}})\bar{z_{2}}+({\overline{D}}z_{2})\bar{z_{2}}+(Az_{1})\bar{z_{1}}-z_{1}({\overline{B}}z_{2})\bigr).

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle(d\pi(X))(z)=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(\overline{s(z_{0})}z-{\bar{z}}s(z_{0}))+i\lambda c_{0}

\displaystyle-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\bigl(({\overline{C}}\bar{z_{1}})\bar{z_{2}}+({\overline{D}}z_{2})\bar{z_{2}}+(Az_{1})\bar{z_{1}}-z_{1}({\overline{B}}z_{2})\bigr).
