Kinematic Alignment in Total Knee Arthroplasty: Unresolved Challenges and Barriers to Overcome for Universal Application
Authors: Gio Song, Cheol Hee Park, Sang Jun Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Restoring patient-specific alignment in total knee arthroplasty has emerged as a promising approach to improve joint kinematics and reduce the need for soft-tissue release. Kinematic alignment (KA) and its variants—including restricted, inverse, and functional alignment—seek to replicate the patient’s native joint line obliquity (JLO) and pre-arthritic alignment, often guided by classification systems such as the Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK). However, despite the theoretical appeal, the universal adoption of KA remains controversial. This review highlights several concerns, including the risk of early loosening in varus-positioned components, the limited applicability of KA in advanced osteoarthritic knees, the difficulty of applying KA in severe valgus deformity, and the uncertainty in aligning the femoral cylindrical axis with the tibial rotational axis. Moreover, CPAK classification oversimplifies the spectrum of alignment phenotypes, even though JLO exhibits substantial variability in its distribution and its functional significance remains uncertain. Static measurements of JLO may not accurately reflect the dynamic behavior of the joint during gait, especially given compensatory mechanisms in adjacent joints. Evidence also shows poor correlation between 2-dimensional joint line parameters and 3-dimensional joint surface orientation. While restoring native alignment and JLO might remain a worthwhile goal, the limitations of existing classification systems and the paucity of supporting evidence should be acknowledged. KA and its variants offer the potential to restore patient-specific alignment and JLO while minimizing soft-tissue releases, but widespread adoption is limited by unresolved issues such as variability in JLO, unequal CPAK boundaries, and uncertain functional relevance. Until high-quality, long-term studies address these concerns, KA strategies should be applied selectively within safe limits.

Proper alignment in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) has long been recognized as a critical determinant of postoperative function, patient satisfaction, and implant longevity.123) Among the various parameters influencing coronal, sagittal, and rotational alignment, the coronal orientation of the knee joint line—commonly referred to as joint line obliquity (JLO) or joint line orientation (JLOri)—has received increasing attention in TKA.456)

A landmark study by Bellemans et al.7) highlighted the prevalence and clinical importance of constitutional varus. In a normal, healthy young Western population, 32% of men and 17% of women exhibited a natural varus of 3° or more. This was associated with a lower-thanaverage medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA), resulting in a mechanical axis (MA) that passed slightly medial to the knee center. This challenges the traditional TKA goal of achieving a neutral MA and underscores the need to consider constitutional varus—particularly in personalized (individualized) alignment strategies such as kinematic alignment (KA).478) KA-TKA, introduced by Howell et al. in 2008,9) customizes component placement based on individual anatomy. Bone resections are guided by the patient’s native JLO on the distal femur, posterior femur, and proximal tibia, with adjustments for cartilage loss.9)

In addition, efforts to restore knee joint line parallelism have gained attention, driven by the hypothesis that a more vertical ground reaction force may reduce shear stress and knee adduction moment.10) Such orientation is thought to enhance load distribution during both single- and double-leg stance phases,1112) contributing to a more natural joint sensation.13) Among existing alignment techniques, KA appears most effective in achieving joint line parallelism after TKA.811121415)

However, conflicting evidence exists: many reports suggest that changes in JLOri have no significant impact on clinical outcomes,1617) and some even demonstrate superior outcomes in patients with laterally tilted joint lines following mechanically aligned TKA (MA-TKA).513)

This conflicting evidence contributes to the hesitation of many surgeons to fully embrace the concepts of KA-TKA and the clinical importance of JLO, given their long-standing experience, accumulated knowledge, and favorable outcomes associated with MA-TKA. Therefore, through a comprehensive review of the literature and clinical insights, the authors aim to identify which aspects of KA-TKA research should be prioritized, which challenges must be addressed, and which concepts require further clarification to achieve universal acceptance of the KA strategy.

CORONAL PLANE ALIGNMENT OF THE KNEE

In arthritic patients, the MA or mechanical hip-knee-ankle angle (HKA) often becomes unreliable for estimating pre-arthritic alignment due to deformities from asymmetric joint space narrowing.4) To approximate pre-arthritic alignment, the arithmetic HKA (aHKA) can be used, calculated as MPTA minus lateral distal femoral angle (LDFA) (MPTA – LDFA).4) This is particularly useful prior to the onset of bone erosion. JLO reflects the joint line’s inclination in double-leg stance and is calculated as MPTA plus LDFA (MPTA + LDFA).4)

Although most knees demonstrate an apex distal JLO relative to the weight-bearing axis, the shift of the body’s center of mass during gait causes the JLO to become more horizontal or neutral.4) The Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) classification utilizes aHKA and JLO—both of which remain stable despite arthritic deformity—to categorize knee phenotypes.4) In this system, an aHKA less than –2° indicates varus alignment, between –2° and 2° is considered neutral, and greater than 2° indicates valgus. For JLO, a value less than 177° indicates apex distal alignment, 177°–183° is neutral, and more than 183° is apex proximal. These metrics are useful not only for preoperative planning in KA-TKA but also for postoperative assessment of alignment outcomes.4) In this context, JLO refers to the arithmetically calculated joint line inclination based on the femoral and tibial mechanical axes, and should be distinguished from the JLOri relative to the ground or floor (Fig. 1).6)

In this manuscript, pre-arthritic alignment refers to the estimated limb alignment prior to the onset of degenerative changes. Native alignment is used to describe patient-specific anatomical characteristics of the knee, including coronal limb alignment and JLO. Constitutional alignment denotes a non-neutral varus or valgus phenotype inherent to the native, pre-arthritic limb alignment. These terms are used consistently throughout the manuscript to avoid conceptual overlap.

VARIOUS CORONAL ALIGNMENT CONCEPTS IN PRIMARY TKA

In primary TKA, various coronal alignment concepts are employed to ensure optimal positioning of the femoral and tibial components, restore proper limb alignment, distribute loads evenly, and mimic the natural kinematics of the knee.181920212223) These strategies are generally categorized into fixed alignment and personalized (or individualized) alignment approaches (Table 1).24)

Fixed Alignment

Fixed alignment approaches include mechanical and anatomic alignment (AA). Mechanical alignment is the traditional and widely accepted method. In this approach, the components are placed perpendicular to the MAs of the femur and tibia, aiming to achieve a neutral overall alignment.2526) This strategy is designed to ensure even load distribution across the components and theoretically prolong implant survivorship.2526)

AA, first introduced by Hungerford in 1982,27) incorporates specific angular adjustments to the femoral and tibial resections in an effort to replicate the native JLO (MPTA and LDFA). In this method, the tibial cut is made with 3° of mechanical varus and the distal femoral cut with 3° of mechanical valgus, producing a neutral MA and an HKA angle of 0°. Thus, while the overall limb alignment resembles that of mechanical alignment, the JLO differs. Notably, AA utilizes the posterior condylar axis instead of the transepicondylar axis (TEA) for rotational positioning of the femoral component, which necessitates internal rotation of the tibial component compared to mechanical alignment.

Despite its physiological rationale, AA was eventually abandoned due to the technical difficulties of achieving accurate 3° varus tibial resections using the manual instruments available at the time, along with concerns about tibial collapse in varus.24) Furthermore, this technique required significant resection of the lateral distal and posterior femoral condyles, increasing the risk of injury to the lateral collateral ligament or the popliteal tendon.24)

Personalized or Individualized Alignment

Personalized or individualized alignment approaches are designed to tailor implant positioning to each patient’s unique anatomy, including JLO and pre-arthritic alignment. KA aims to restore the patient’s pre-arthritic alignment, including constitutional varus or valgus, by resurfacing the joint lines. This contrasts with AA, which ultimately positions the components perpendicular to the MA.

By resurfacing the native femoral joint line, KA inherently restores a single functional flexion axis, approximated by the cylindrical axis. This also differs from AA, which uses the posterior condylar axis as the rotational reference. The cylindrical axis is generally located more posterior and distal relative to the TEA in the distal femoral condyles.828) In varus deformity, KA typically results in the femoral component being placed in more valgus and internal rotation, and the tibial component in more varus and internal rotation, compared to mechanical alignment.

Unrestricted KA does not impose strict constraints on final component alignment, which may lead to extreme outliers if not executed precisely.29) To address this, restricted KA introduces boundaries to limit excessive deviation, often utilizing computer-assisted techniques to control alignment and optimize soft-tissue balance.1130)

In inverse (or reverse) restricted KA, the native tibial JLO is restored first through tibial resurfacing, with the femoral component subsequently adjusted to achieve balance.31) In contrast, conventional restricted KA prioritizes femoral resurfacing to restore the native flexion axis, after which the tibial resection is modified within defined alignment boundaries.

Modified (or adjusted) mechanical alignment can also be considered a form of personalized alignment. The tibial resection is made perpendicular to the tibial MA. Subsequently, the femoral component’s coronal and rotational alignment is adjusted using a gap-balancing technique, allowing minor deviations (typically within ± 3°) from the MA. This approach aims to restore near-neutral overall alignment while accommodating individual anatomical variations and minimizing the need for soft-tissue releases.224)

Functional alignment (FA) seeks to place components in a way that minimizes soft-tissue releases while maintaining overall limb alignment within safe limits.3233) It typically relies on navigation- or robot-assisted systems to assess gaps or soft-tissue laxity prior to bone resection. FA can operate within the broader boundaries of restricted KA or the narrower margins of modified mechanical alignment.24) It may also be regarded as a form of gap-balancing technique, enhanced by computer-assisted technology that provides real-time gap assessment before bone cuts.

LITERATURE SUPPORTING PERSONALIZED ALIGNMENT

Personalized alignment strategies in TKA—including unrestricted KA, restricted KA, inverse KA, and FA—have gained momentum as evidence accumulates that many knees do not conform to a single “neutral” target and that respecting native JLO and soft-tissue balance can yield outcomes at least comparable to, and sometimes better than, traditional mechanical alignment.34) Contemporary classifications (e.g., CPAK) and phenotype work highlight wide variability in native coronal alignment and JLO, challenging a one-size-fits-all mechanical alignment goal; there is mounting evidence that maintaining native JLO is associated with improved outcomes after TKA.3435) These insights, coupled with long-standing dissatisfaction rates after conventional MA-TKA, have driven interest in individualized strategies.

In the 2024 American Association of Hip and Knee Surgeons membership poll, respondents preferring nonmechanical targets (KA/AA/FA) rose to 61%, while strict mechanical/neutral targets fell to 39%, reflecting realworld adoption of personalized concepts.36) Randomized evidence shows KA-TKA provides at least noninferior, and sometimes superior, outcomes versus MA-TKA. Dossett et al.3738) reported better 2-year functional results with KA and a 13-year follow-up confirming noninferiority for revisions and major reoperations (with higher satisfaction). Beyond average-risk cohorts, KA has also been reported as effective in severe varus deformities, supporting applicability across deformity spectra.39) To mitigate concerns about extreme outliers of component positions, restricted KA applies pragmatic boundaries (e.g., HKA ± 3°, femoral/tibial varus–valgus within ~5°), often aided by computer assistance. A “safe-zone” concept has been advocated to facilitate stepwise adoption while minimizing risk.34)

FA leverages navigation/robotics to position components in 3 dimensions (3D) according to quantified soft-tissue tension across the range of motion (ROM), typically within conservative alignment bounds. Early clinical data—including varus-phenotype cohorts—show improved Forgotten Joint Scores and ROM versus mechanical alignment, supporting FA as a patient-specific, balance-oriented approach.15) Robotics/navigation improve alignment precision and make individualized strategies feasible. Importantly, multiple studies indicate that small deviations (± 3°) from neutral MA are not associated with worse long-term survivorship, providing latitude for individualized targets.834)

Recent perspectives advocate expanding from purely static radiographic targets toward kinetic alignment, integrating dynamic measures (e.g., dynamic HKA, knee adduction moment, ground-reaction and muscle forces) to better reflect real-world loading and to further individualize planning.40) Despite the promising results and growing acceptance of personalized alignment strategies, universal adoption of unrestricted KA remains limited. If these unresolved issues and clinical concerns can be effectively addressed, they may pave the way for the widespread implementation of KA in TKA.

PRACTICAL SURGICAL CONSIDERATIONS AND LIMITATIONS OF KA

From a surgical perspective, unrestricted KA may introduce challenges in managing flexion–extension gap asymmetry, particularly in knees with preexisting ligament imbalance. Preservation of native JLO does not necessarily guarantee symmetric gaps, and selective adjustment of component position or limited soft-tissue release may still be required.4142)

Challenges related to posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) tension are not limited to MA-TKA and are also encountered in KA-TKA. In KA-based strategies, however, optimization of PCL tension may be more complex, as modification of tibial slope to compensate for degenerative PCL laxity or alteration of femoral sizing to address flexion imbalance may be limited by the intent to preserve native JLOri and anatomy.84243)

Implant-related considerations also arise during KA, as rotational adjustment of the femoral and tibial components may complicate cut surface coverage and component size selection. Finally, although robotic assistance can improve the precision of KA execution, reproducibility remains influenced by preoperative planning, registration accuracy, and surgeon-defined alignment boundaries.

These factors underscore the importance of combining KA principles with pragmatic intraoperative judgment. Therefore, long-term follow-up studies are required to determine whether KA provides superior functional outcomes without compromising patellofemoral tracking or component longevity compared with traditional mechanical alignment.

BARRIERS TO THE UNIVERSAL ADOPTION OF KA AND ITS VARIANTS: UNRESOLVED ISSUES AND CLINICAL CONCERNS

Early Loosening Risk Associated with Coronal Alignment Outliers

Many surgeons have expressed concerns about the universal application of KA in all TKA cases, as early loosening has occasionally been observed in patients with even moderately varus component positioning (Fig. 2). These experiences make it difficult to fully adopt KA. Mechanical alignment, supported by robust long-term data, provides balanced load distribution and reliability, making it unwise to disregard this approach in TKA.544)

Limited Applicability of KA in Advanced Osteoarthritis

The applicability of KA in patients with advanced osteoarthritis remains questionable, as these patients often present with severe cartilage loss, bone defects, soft-tissue contractures, and instability—particularly in geographic regions where TKA is typically performed at more advanced stages of disease than in Western populations. It is often difficult to accurately estimate pre-arthritic alignment and JLO in advanced arthritic knees. The feasibility of applying CPAK phenotyping in such cases therefore remains unclear, and both intraoperative assumptions regarding native condylar surfaces and the preservation of pathologically contracted ligaments may be unreliable guides for component positioning (Fig. 3).45)

Caution in Applying KA to Severe Valgus Deformities

Applying KA in severe valgus deformities, such as CPAK types III, VI, and IX, raises concern.46) These deformities are frequently associated with medial soft-tissue laxity and patellofemoral dysfunction, which may predispose to recurrence of the deformity or postoperative patellofemoral complications. Thus, the strict application of KA in such cases should be approached with caution (Fig. 4).

Oversimplification of Knee Kinematics Focusing only on Coronal Alignment

Knee kinematics after TKA should not be oversimplified as a matter of coronal alignment alone. Surgical outcomes are influenced by multiple principles, including sagittal and rotational alignment, component sizing, mediolateral positioning, joint line height restoration, and soft-tissue balancing. Therefore, the results of TKA cannot be attributed solely to coronal alignment. In addition, whether the cylindrical axis can be consistently achieved intraoperatively through condylar resurfacing remains uncertain, while the diversity of KA techniques complicates standardization of femoral and tibial rotational axes.

Potential Patellofemoral Complications Due to Design–Concept Mismatch between Mechanical Alignment Implants and KA-TKA

Most implants have been designed with mechanical alignment principles and may present technical challenges when applied to KA.47) For example, resurfacing the distal femur according to KA principles may result in internal rotation of the femoral component relative to the surgical TEA and elevation of the lateral femoral flange, potentially leading to patellofemoral maltracking.48)

Geometric and Structural Limitations of the CPAK Classification

The CPAK classification categorizes knees into 9 phenotypes, with types I and II reported as the most common.449) In contrast, type V—the target phenotype for MA-TKA—is found in only about 15% of cases.4) However, our recent research (prior to publication) on healthy populations shows that the distribution of JLO spans a wide range from 166° to 184°, suggesting that the aHKA and JLO boundaries used in the CPAK classification are unequally divided and do not accurately reflect the continuous nature of their values in healthy populations (Table 2, Fig. 5). Mathematically, the dividing area of type V (aHKA range of 4° × JLO range of 6° = 24) is considerably smaller than that of types I, II, and III, which occupy approximately 4, 2, and 4 times larger areas, respectively (Fig. 5). Given the disproportion in dividing areas, differences in the reported proportions of CPAK types across studies may partly reflect classification geometry rather than true population differences, suggesting that preferentially targeting a specific CPAK type should be interpreted with caution.14505152)

Questioning the Significance of JLO Restoration and Type Classification

Although JLO restoration is often emphasized, the wide variability in JLO and the arbitrary division of CPAK types call into question the full justification for this focus. Rather than focusing solely on restoring a specific CPAK phenotype, it may be more meaningful to consider the magnitude of change in both JLO and aHKA—quantified as ΔaHKA2+ΔJLO2 as a composite metric of deviation from the native alignment.53) As illustrated in Fig. 5, can we truly say that Patient A has achieved a better restoration of their CPAK phenotype compared to Patient B, simply based on type classification? This remains an open question worthy of further consideration.

Inconsistency between JLO and JLOri and Compensatory Effects of Adjacent Joints

There is the issue of inconsistency between JLO measured relative to the MA and JLOri measured relative to the floor, as well as the influence of adjacent joint compensation on JLOri (Fig. 1).6) One study reported that the tibial joint line was nearly parallel to the floor in healthy individuals (JLOri, 0.3° ± 1.9°), including those with constitutional varus (JLOri, 0.2° ± 2.2°). In contrast, patients with symptomatic varus knee osteoarthritis showed a significantly greater lateral inclination of the joint line (JLOri, 1.9° ± 3.5°), likely due to bone loss of the medial femoral condyle.6) In our recent analysis of healthy adults, JLO exhibited a wide distribution, and the corresponding JLOri often differed from JLO (Table 2). This raises the question of whether a meaningful or consistent “target” value can be defined when JLO alone is used as a reference.

Functionally, what ultimately matters is whether the joint line remains approximately parallel to the floor during daily activities—a condition that may often be achieved through compensatory mechanisms of adjacent joints.5258) Many studies have reported that the change in JLOri after high tibial osteotomy (HTO) is less than half of the change in MPTA (Table 3).5456575960) The magnitude of JLOri change does not necessarily increase linearly with the amount of angular correction. This discrepancy is likely due to compensatory mechanisms in adjacent joints and periarticular constraints.545556) Such compensatory mechanisms of adjacent joints are observed not only in HTO, but also in TKA. A study demonstrated that while the postoperative MPTA showed a wide and slightly varus-skewed distribution, the JLOri had a narrower, more normally distributed pattern centered near 1.0° varus.11) The significant difference between MPTA and JLOri (mean 2°, p < 0.001) suggests that despite variation in tibial component alignment, the functional joint line remains consistently near-horizontal, resembling the orientation seen in normal, asymptomatic knees.11)

Static Versus Dynamic Evaluation of the Joint Line

The inherently dynamic nature of the joint surface raises questions about the validity of evaluating JLO based solely on static imaging. While it is commonly assumed that the joint line of a normal knee remains parallel to the ground during walking,61) this assumption may not fully capture the complexity of dynamic knee biomechanics. The JLO or JLOri observed in a particular knee or foot position may not accurately represent its behavior during dynamic activities such as gait. Trepczynski et al.58) demonstrated that dynamic JLOri during walking showed little predictive value for medial-lateral tibiofemoral load distribution. This finding suggests that static radiographic assessments have limited ability to capture the joint’s true functional biomechanics. Similarly, Sasaki et al.52) found no correlation between 3D joint surface orientation and 2-dimensional (2D) coronal JLOri, and no relationship with CPAK phenotype. These results indicate that the static evaluation of JLOri may not accurately reflect the joint’s actual spatial alignment, highlighting a potential limitation of relying solely on phenotype-based classification.52586263) Appropriate research and a deeper understanding of the 9 key aspects concerning KA-TKA will help facilitate broader consensus and ultimately promote the universal acceptance of the KA strategy.

SUMMARY

KA and its variants offer the potential to restore patient-specific aHKA and JLO while minimizing soft-tissue releases, but widespread adoption is limited by unresolved issues such as variability in JLO, unequal CPAK boundaries, and uncertain functional relevance. Until high-quality, long-term studies address these concerns, KA strategies should be applied selectively within safe limits.
