Conformal Selective Prediction with General Risk Control
Tian Bai, Ying Jin

Abstract
In deploying artificial intelligence (AI) models, selective prediction offers the option to abstain from making a prediction when uncertain about model quality. To fulfill its promise, it is crucial to enforce strict and precise error control over cases where the model is trusted. We propose Selective Conformal Risk control with E-values (SCoRE), a new framework for deriving such decisions for any trained model and any user-defined, bounded and continuously-valued risk. SCoRE offers two types of guarantees on the risk among “positive” cases in which the system opts to trust the model. Built upon conformal inference and hypothesis testing ideas, SCoRE first constructs a class of (generalized) e-values, which are non-negative random variables whose product with the unknown risk has expectation no greater than one. Such a property is ensured by data exchangeability without requiring any modeling assumptions. Passing these e-values on to hypothesis testing procedures, we yield the binary trust decisions with finite-sample error control. SCoRE avoids the need of uniform concentration, and can be readily extended to settings with distribution shifts. We evaluate the proposed methods with simulations and demonstrate their efficacy through applications to error management in drug discovery, health risk prediction, and large language models.



Keywords:
Selective prediction; Conformal inference; Hypothesis testing; Multiple testing; Trustworthy AI.

Introduction

Limiting errors when deploying AI models is an indispensable component of their life cycle
(
76
;
44
)
.
As model prediction errors are inevitable—arising from inadequate modeling, sampling uncertainty, and randomness in training—post-training mechanisms that manage errors at deployment are especially important.
A prominent approach is to deploy a model with an
abstention
(or
rejection
) option: a model is used only when it appears reliable and is withheld otherwise
(
13
;
15
)
.
This paradigm, often called
selective prediction
, aims to control errors precisely among the predictions we choose to deploy while maintaining high coverage, i.e., deploying as often as possible
(
22
)
.
This leads to the general question:

Given a black-box model
f
, labeled data
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and a new instance
X_{n+1}
, can we derive a trust decision
\psi_{n+1}\in\{0,1\}
that controls an unknown risk
L_{n+1}
among those with
\psi_{n+1}=1
?

Most prior work addresses this problem for classifiers
f
with binary risks
L_{n+1}\in\{0,1\}
, typically offering either asymptotic control of a selective error rate or finite-sample bound based on uniform concentration of empirical classification errors.
Recent extensions of conformal prediction provide finite-sample, distribution-free guarantees for selective tasks with binary risks
(
71
;
38
;
37
)
, and have been used to identify trustworthy AI outputs in applications such as compound screening
(
4
)
,large language models
(
25
;
43
)
, and medical foundation models
(
39
)
.
However, many high-stakes applications demand control of
continuously-valued
risks, where a principled and powerful “trust” mechanism remains underdeveloped:

•

In
drug discovery
, the early screening phase uses AI models to identify drug candidates with high binding affinities for follow-up experiments. False leads waste resources, and a natural quantitative risk is a (continuous) development cost incurred by pursuing an inactive candidate
(
38
;
4
)
, e.g.,
L_{n+1}=\text{cost}\cdot\ind\{Y_{n+1}\leq c\}
for the unknown affinity
Y_{n+1}
and a known threshold
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

In
radiology report generation
, an AI-generated report is useful only when it is sufficiently close to expert references
(
25
)
.
Here, the risk can be naturally continuous, such as a semantic distance between the model output
f(X_{n+1})
and the (unknown) expert-level reference report
Y_{n+1}
.

•

In
healthcare management
, hospitals routinely use predictions of continuous outcomes, such as ICU length of stay, to support downstream planning and interventions
(
11
;
57
;
30
)
. Practitioners may seek to deploy only highly accurate predictions
(
39
)
, where the risk can often be a continuous metric such as the squared prediction error.

Besides the focus on continuous risks, these settings also call for different
notions
of risk control tied to downstream objectives: one may seek to bound the expected total risk accumulated over deployed instances, while another may prioritize the expected risk per deployed instance. As we shall see, these considerations reflect distinct error notions. Ideally, after all, such guarantees should be finite-sample and distribution-free, applying to any black-box model under mild exchangeability assumptions.

1.1
Our contributions

We introduce Selective Conformal Risk control with E-values (SCoRE), a new framework that provides
finite-sample, distribution-free control of bounded, continuously-valued risks in selectively trusting any model.
Viewing trust as a binary decision for each test instance, we formalize two criteria:

(i)

Marginal deployment risk (MDR):
\mathbb{E}[L_{n+1}\psi_{n+1}]
, the expected risk incurred by deployed instances;

(ii)

Selective deployment risk (SDR):
\mathbb{E}[(\sum_{j}L_{n+j}\psi_{n+j})/(1\vee\sum_{j}\psi_{n+j})]
when given multiple test instances
\{X_{n+j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, which quantifies the average risk
per
deployed instance.

See Section
2.1
for formal definitions.
Both metrics target “positive” deployed cases and conceptually parallel type-I error metrics in hypothesis testing. The SDR, which requires an intrinsically “selective” treatment, extends the selective prediction literature beyond binary risks
(
13
;
15
;
22
)
, while the MDR offers a complementary perspective within our unified framework.

Figure
1
previews two representative applications.
In a drug discovery task (panel (a)), our SDR-control procedure selects compounds while controlling the average cost wasted on false leads. In a clinical prediction task (panel (b)), our MDR-control procedure identifies highly accurate predictions and tightly controls the total prediction error accumulated across daily batches (lower right, divided by 50).
Achieving such guarantees is nontrivial because the MDR and SDR concern the unknown risk on a data-dependent subset of test instances: we must decide which test instances to deploy using calibration data (and predictions for the risk), yet the deployment risk of each selected instance depends on an unseen outcome.

Methodologically, SCoRE connects selective deployment to hypothesis testing with e-values
(
72
)
.
The key idea is to connect a deploy decision with a reject decision in hypothesis testing.
We show that applying standard hypothesis testing procedures that threshold a class of (risk-adjusted) e-values obeying
E_{n+j}\geq 0
and
E[L_{n+j}E_{n+j}]\leq 1
leads to finite-sample MDR and SDR control.
For each test point, we use a set of labeled calibration data to construct such an e-value under standard exchangeability conditions, which then leads to risk control. Figure
2
summarizes the workflow.
While e-values have been used to test (deterministic) hypotheses
(
72
;
61
)
, their expectation-based validity is a natural match for controlling the expectation of unknown risks.
Notably, our guarantees only require the exchangeability among data. It avoids uniform concentration arguments common in selective prediction
(
22
)
, accommodates dependence among data (e.g., predictions from graphs)
(
34
)
, and extends naturally to covariate shift settings
(
70
)
.

Finally, risk control should be balanced with utility
(
22
)
:
a mechanism that abstains too often limits the model power.
We analyze the power through any user-specified reward of deployment,
leading to a Neyman–Pearson-type characterization of the asymptotically optimal scores that guide selection.
We also develop practical strategies to achieve this when the risk is consistently estimated.

Problem setup

2.1
Defining deployment risk

We begin by introducing the setup and our notions of deployment risk. Assume access to a set of labeled (calibration) data
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{calib}}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
and a set of unlabeled (test) data
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}=\{X_{n+j}\}_{j=1}^{m}

whose labels
\{Y_{n+j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
are unobserved.
For now, we assume that
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m+n}
are exchangeable across
i\in[m+n]
; we relax this in Section
6
to covariate shift settings.
Here
X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
is the feature and
Y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
is the label.
We are interested in deploying a model
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
.
It may be a regression model with
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
, or a classification model with
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,\dots,K\}
, or a language model where
\mathcal{Y}
is the space of natural language.

We quantify the consequence of erroneously deploying
f
on a new instance
X
with unknown outcome
Y
by a numerical
risk

\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, where
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
is a known mapping.
Throughout, we work with a bounded risk, and without loss of generality, assume
\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y)\in[0,1]
.
Concrete examples of risks are discussed in Section
2.2
.
The risk for the
j
-th test point is denoted as
L_{n+j}=\mathcal{L}(f,X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})
, which is
unknown
since the label
Y_{n+j}
is not observed.
To formalize optimality of deployment outcomes, we allow a user-specific
reward
for deployment, represented by a random variable
r(f,X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, where
r(\cdot)
is a known mapping. Intuitively,
r
captures the utility of deploying a model on a test instance, such as the scientific value, operational benefit, or downstream savings in resources.

We will use a pre-trained score function
s\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
to calibrate the deployment decisions, and our procedure prioritizes instances with smaller scores
s(X)
(so smaller scores shall indicate preliminary evidence for safer instances). The validity of our procedures does not rely on the choice of of
s
.
We assume for convenience that both
f(\cdot)
and
s(\cdot)
are trained independently of
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{calib}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}
. More generally, our results apply as long as the triplets
(s(X_{i}),f(X_{i}),Y_{i})
’s are exchangeable across
i\in[n+m]
, such as with graph neural networks trained over an entire graph with a separate labeled training data and all features of
i\in[n+m]

(
34
)
.
A natural idea is to set
s(X)
as a prediction for
\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y)
; we discuss optimal score choice later.

Our goal is to construct binary decisions
\hat{\psi}_{n+j}\in\{0,1\}
for all
j\in[m]
, which may depend on both
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{calib}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}
.
Here,
\hat{\psi}_{n+j}=1
means to deploy/trust the model for
X_{n+j}
, and
\hat{\psi}_{n+j}=0
means abstention.
What
deploying
a model means depends on the context (e.g., sending a compound to wet-lab follow-up, accepting an automated clinical prediction, or releasing an LLM-generated report).
Following
22
, we emphasize risk control over the trusted cases and consider two error metrics.

Marginal deployment risk (MDR).

The first error metric concerns the overall (expected) risk. Given a user-specified error rate
\alpha
, we aim to develop
\hat{\psi}_{n+j}\in\{0,1\}
such that

\displaystyle{\textnormal{MDR}}:=\mathbb{E}[L_{n+1}\cdot\hat{\psi}_{n+1}]

(2.1)

is controlled below
\alpha
.
This is an analogue for classical type-I error control
(
52
)
for a random and non-binary risk.
It is useful to interpret MDR with multiple test points, in which controlling (
2.1
) at
\alpha
implies control over the
total deployment risk
(TDR):

\displaystyle{\textnormal{TDR}}:=\mathbb{E}\big[\textstyle{\sum_{j=1}^{m}}L_{n+j}\hat{\psi}_{n+j}\big]\leq\alpha m.

(2.2)

That is, the total risk accumulated by the deployed instances
\mathcal{R}=\{j\colon\hat{\psi}_{n+j}=1\}
is controlled.

Selective deployment risk (SDR).

The second type of error we study measures the average risk per deployed unit. Formally, letting
\mathcal{R}=\{j\in[m]\colon\hat{\psi}_{n+j}=1\}
be the set of deployed units, we define

\displaystyle{\textnormal{SDR}}:=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{n+j}\cdot\ind\{j\in\mathcal{R}\}}{1\vee|\mathcal{R}|}\Bigg].

(2.3)

The SDR is motivated by, and generalizes, the false discovery rate (FDR) in classical hypothesis testing
(
9
)
.
In particular, if we set
L_{n+j}=\ind\{H_{0,j}\text{ is true}\}
for a set of deterministic null hypotheses
\{H_{0,j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, then SDR reduces to the usual FDR.
In prediction problems, SDR connects to the model-free selective inference problem
(
38
;
37
;
25
)
when
L_{n+j}=\ind\{Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}
represents a binary “bad event” (e.g., the outcome is not sufficiently large relative to a cutoff
c_{n+j}\in\mathbb{R}
), and our SDR-control procedure (
2.3
) reduces to the methods studied there. When
m\to\infty
, by the law of large numbers, the SDR is close to the risk conditional on deployment
(
22
)

\mathbb{E}[L_{n+1}{\,|\,}\psi_{n+1}=1]
, and more broadly, to marginal FDR-type notions
(
67
)
. However, our formulation allows the development of effective solutions, whereas those criteria can be difficult to control in finite sample in a model-free fashion.

When to use which?

The two metrics serve different goals. MDR is natural when there is a fixed risk budget and does not require the risk to scale with the number of deployments: a procedure may deploy few but comparatively risky cases yet still controlling the MDR.
On the other hand, SDR is suitable when one requires that
only low-risk cases are deployed
, so the incurred risks scale with the number of deployed cases. Such distinctions mirror those between the type-I error and FDR, which have been extensively discussed in the statistics literature (see, e.g.,
36
;
9
).

2.2
Examples of application scenarios

To further contextualize the discussion, we now give several concrete examples of the deployment risks and how the MDR/SDR translate into practical guarantees in four representative applications. Readers interested in methodology may skip the rest of the section without missing key information.

Drug discovery with low risk.

Early stages of drug discovery aims to select promising drug candidates from a large library. While traditional approaches rely on exhaustive physical screening to evaluate their properties
(
69
;
56
)
, it is increasingly popular to rely on AI predictions to shortlist drug candidates
(
12
;
14
)
. In this case,
X
is the physical/chemical structure of a drug candidate, and a model
f
generates an imperfect prediction
f(X)
for the unknown property of interest
Y
. Here, a decision to trust
f
for a test instance
X_{n+j}
means selecting it for future development, where a false positive may incur a waste of subsequent cost
\ell(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})\in[0,1]
. In
38
;
4
, the risk is binary
\ell(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})=\ind\{Y_{n+j}\leq c\}
where
c
is a known threshold.
Controlling the TDR (
2.2
) limits the total expected cost of false leads.
Controlling the SDR (
2.3
) implies that the average cost per selected compound is limited.

Finding small-error predictions.

For a regression model
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, practitioners may rely on its predictions only when sufficiently accurate, for tasks such as auto-labeling and decision support. In this case,
a natural risk is
\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y)=\ind\{|Y-f(X)|>c\}
for a fixed tolerance
c>0
, or
\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y)=|Y-f(X)|^{2}
for mean squared error (MSE).
In the former case, controlling the MDR (
2.1
) limits the probability of deploying a high-error case, while controlling the SDR (
2.3
) limits the fraction of high-error cases among deployed ones.
With the MSE risk, limiting the SDR (
2.3
) controls the average MSE among the deployed units.

Deploying LLMs with low semantic error.

In using LLMs for radiology report generation, the input
X
is a medical image, and the output
f(X)
is a natural-language report describing the findings from the image. Since the report will be handed to clinicians to make medical decisions, it is useful to control risks in cases where LLM reports are adopted. In
25
, the unknown label
Y
is a human-expert report, and
\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y)
is a binary risk which equals
1
if
f(X)
differs from
Y
based on CheXbert labels
(
64
)
. More generally,
\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y)
may measure semantic distances or number of deviations in key findings between the reports.
Here, controlling the SDR (
2.3
) below a expert-defined error rate would be useful for ensuring that the LLM models are deployed only when they are comparable to experts.

Selecting accurate diagnosis with few follow-ups.

For multi-class diagnosis such as a disease subtype
Y\in[K]
, a foundation model
f
produces probability estimates
f(X,k)
for each label
k
, leading to a ranking of labels
f(X,[1])\geq f(X,[2])\geq\dots\geq f(X,[K])
, where
([1],[2],\dots,[K])
is a permutation of
(1,\dots,K)
.
Clinical workflows may proceed down this list with confirmatory tests until the true label is reached.
To expedite the process, it is useful to only use high-quality predictions where one does not need to go too far down the list to reach the correct label (an extreme case is when the top-1 prediction is correct).
One may define
\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\ind\{f(X,k)\leq f(X,Y)\}
, the number of steps needed before reaching the true label. Then, controlling the TDR (
2.2
) finds units needing fewer than
\alpha K\cdot m
follow-up steps in total, while controlling the SDR (
2.3
) means
each
deployed unit needs
\alpha K
follow-up steps. Arguably, the SDR is more sensible for AI integration: we trust AI only when it improves efficiency upon traditional human inspections.

2.3
Related work

Selective prediction.

This paper is motivated by the philosophy of selective prediction, that is, we only deploy a model when confident and control errors on the deployed cases
(
13
;
15
;
22
;
58
)
. Much of this literature addresses classification settings with asymptotic guarantees for selective risk. This is related to, and expanded by our SDR notion (see
25
for a discussion on the distinctions for binary risks). We contribute to this literature from the conformal inference perspective. Our methods provide both selective and marginal guarantees, work in finite sample, and address general, continuously-valued risks.

Selective conformal inference.

Methodologically, SCoRE is closest to the work on selective inference and multiple testing in prediction problems via conformal inference
(
38
;
37
;
3
;
35
;
51
;
59
;
24
;
21
;
55
;
31
)
. As we shall discuss in Section
3.1
, this literature builds on conformal p-values to control a binary risk, adapts them to selective settings. The key technical distinction is that we target continuous risks with e-values instead of p-values.
Other works using conformal prediction to address selective prediction include
17
;
65
, yet they focus on distinct aspects like calibration or directly using prediction sets, instead of valid error control among selected cases. Finally, our work connects to the line of work on conformal risk control (CRC)
(
2
)
and learn-then-test (LLT)
(
1
)
. These methods address related marginal or selective risk notions, primarily for binary risks. Our setting differs in targeting continuous risks via exact calibration with e-values. In particular, our SDR variant targets a selective criterion that avoids uniform concentration (over a grid) needed there, while our MDR variant provides an e-value perspective that connects to CRC and enables a unified analysis of finite-sample validity, covariate shift, and asymptotic optimality (see more detailed discussion later).

Conformal inference with e-values.

The e-values, as a parallel to p-values, have attracted recent interest in hypothesis testing and related tasks due to advantages such as compatibility with dependence
(
72
;
74
;
75
;
61
)
. E-values in conformal prediction date back to
73
, and have attracted recent attention
(
5
;
46
;
20
;
45
;
19
)
. Distinct from other works that harness advantages of e-values like any-time validity, we leverage e-values is to control the expectation of unknown risks (though our construction of risk-adjusted e-values is related to the soft-rank e-values
(
19
)
; see discussion in Section
4.1
). Finally, this work generalizes conformal selection methods that can be interpreted via e-values, yet with a different goal of controlling risks (see Section
3.2
for detailed discussion).

Statistical hypothesis testing.

This work is deeply connected to classical statistical hypothesis testing. While most of the works focus on binary type-I error control of rejecting a null hypothesis, there are methods that incorporate “weights” for the hypotheses in defining the type-I error
(
10
;
63
;
6
;
8
)
. Our risks
L_{n+j}
in both error metrics can be viewed as unknown, random weights, and we provide a solution with e-values, which might be useful for other problems where a similar structure is present. While the connection is not straightforward, this relates to
23
which uses e-values to control the downstream costs in distinct test decisions.
Another related line of work considers selecting multiple families of hypotheses, so that the average risk (such as the FDP in the family) is controlled among the selected families
(
29
;
68
;
7
)
; while we use quite different techniques, our methods may be applicable in their setting if knowledge of the risk is available in some “calibration” families.

3
General strategy: testing with risk-adjusted e-values

This section presents the high-level strategy for controlling the two metrics. Section
3.1
warms up via an existing framework with binary risk control. Section
3.2
introduces the concept of risk-adjusted e-values, and Section
3.3
shows how any risk-adjusted e-values yield MDR and SDR control.

3.1
Warm-up: conformal p-value for binary risk

We briefly review the binary-risk setting to motivate our framework.
Conformal selection methods (e.g.,
38
;
37
;
3
and references therein) address the problem of identifying sufficiently large outcomes
Y_{n+j}>c
for a pre-specified constant
c>0
while controlling a binary error
\ind\{Y_{n+j}\leq c\}
.

38
formalizes this problem as testing a random hypothesis
H_{j}\colon Y_{n+j}\leq c
, where rejecting
H_{j}
implies declaring a large outcome.
They leverage conformal prediction
(
71
)
to construct conformal p-values
\{p_{j}\}
obeying

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+j}\leq c,~p_{j}\leq t)\leq t,\quad\text{for all}~t\in[0,1].

This resembles the null property of valid p-values in classical hypothesis testing.
However, the null event is random and is not conditioned upon; instead, it appears jointly with the p-value in the probability statement.
With this in hand, rejecting
H_{j}
when
p_{j}\leq\alpha
naturally leads to the control of the binary MDR, as
\mathbb{E}[\ind\{Y_{n+j}\leq c\}\ind\{p_{j}\leq\alpha\}]\leq\alpha
.
In addition,
38
show that, when the calibration and test samples are exchangeable, passing multiple p-values
\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
to the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure
(
9
)
at level
\alpha\in(0,1)
produces a selection set
\mathcal{R}
with FDR control:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{Y_{n+j}\leq c\}\ind\{j\in\mathcal{R}\}}{1\vee|\mathcal{R}|}\Bigg]\leq\alpha,

which coincides with (
2.3
) when taking
L_{n+j}=\ind\{Y_{n+j}\leq c\}
.

Conformal selection draws upon classical hypothesis testing to control the expectation of a binary risk by the tail probability of a uniformly distributed p-value.
However, tail probability is not a natural instrument for quantifying and controlling the expectation of continuous risks.
This motivates the use of e-values whose validity is defined through expectation
(
72
)
.
The remaining challenge is then to construct e-values that remain valid when the “null” is an unknown random risk.

3.2
Risk-adjusted e-values

We now introduce our key technical tool inspired by e-values
(
72
)
. Specifically, for each test unit, we construct a non-negative random variable obeying the following definition.

Definition 3.1

(Risk-adjusted e-value)
.

For the random risk
L_{n+j}=\mathcal{L}(f,X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})
, we say a random variable
E_{n+j}
is a
risk-adjusted e-value
if
E_{n+j}\geq 0
almost surely and
\mathbb{E}[E_{n+j}L_{n+j}]\leq 1
.

The concrete constructions of risk-adjusted e-values based on the scores
s(X_{i})
and observed risks
\mathcal{L}(f,X_{i},Y_{i})
will be tailored to each error metric and introduced later.

Similar to the null property of conformal p-values, the defining property of risk-adjusted e-values characterizes the joint behavior of risks and e-values.
This joint control naturally allows these e-values to be combined with hypothesis testing procedures to produce binary trust decisions
\{\hat{\psi}_{n+j}\}
.
Intuitively, a large value of
E_{n+j}
provides evidence that the risk
L_{n+j}
is small due to the validity condition
\mathbb{E}[L_{n+j}E_{n+j}]\leq 1
. Definition
3.1
generalizes the notion of e-values in statistical hypothesis testing: when testing a deterministic null hypothesis
H_{0}
, a random variable
E\geq 0
is an e-value if
\mathbb{E}[E]\leq 1
under
H_{0}
(that is, the risk being
\ind\{H_{0}\text{ is true}\}
), so that a large value of
E
suggests evidence against the null
(
61
)
.

Even closer to us is the e-value perspective of conformal selection
(
38
)
.
While the original method relies on p-values, several works construct e-values
e_{n+j}
obeying
\mathbb{E}[e_{n+j}\ind\{Y_{n+j}\leq c\}]\leq 1
for controlling the FDR in online selection
(
77
)
, promoting selection diversity
(
59
)
, and addressing hierarchical data
(
51
)
.
Other works that address other issues in conformal p-values, including covariate shift in
37
and the model optimization in
3
, can also be interpreted as implicitly using certain e-values obeying such property.

3.3
General strategies for MDR and SDR control

Once any risk-adjusted e-values are available, Theorem
3.2
offers a general strategy for deriving trust decisions that control the MDR (
2.1
) in finite samples, and Theorem
3.3
provides such a strategy for SDR (
2.3
).

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
E_{n+j}
obeys Definition
3.1
. Setting the trust decision as
\hat{\psi}_{n+j}=\ind\{E_{n+j}\geq 1/\alpha\}
yields the marginal risk control:
\mathbb{E}[L_{n+j}\cdot\hat{\psi}_{n+j}]\leq\alpha
.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Since
L_{n+j}\geq 0
and
E_{n+j}\geq 0
, we have
L_{n+j}\hat{\psi}_{n+j}=L_{n+j}\ind\{E_{n+j}\geq 1/\alpha\}\leq L_{n+j}E_{n+j}\cdot\alpha
.
Taking the expectation gives
{\textnormal{MDR}}=\mathbb{E}[L_{n+j}\hat{\psi}_{n+j}]\leq\alpha
due to Definition
3.1
.
∎

We apply the e-BH procedure
(
74
)
to risk-adjusted e-values to control the SDR.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
\{E_{n+j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
obey Definition
3.1
. Let
\hat{\psi}_{n+j}=1
if and only if
j
is selected by the e-BH procedure applied to
\{E_{n+j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
at level
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
That is,
\hat{\psi}_{n+j}=\ind\{E_{n+j}\geq m/(\alpha\hat{\tau})\}
, where
\hat{\tau}=\max\{\tau\colon\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{E_{n+j}\geq m/(\alpha\tau)\}\geq\tau\}
.
Then, it holds that
\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{n+j}\cdot\hat{\psi}_{n+j}/(1\vee\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{\psi}_{n+j})\big]\leq\alpha
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

By the definition of
\hat{\tau}
, we have

\displaystyle{\textnormal{SDR}}=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{n+j}\ind\{E_{n+j}\geq m/(\alpha\hat{\tau})\}}{1\vee\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{E_{n+j}\geq m/(\alpha\hat{\tau})\}}\ind\{\hat{\tau}>0\}\Bigg]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{E_{n+j}\geq m/(\alpha\hat{\tau})\}}{\hat{\tau}}\bigg].

Since
L_{n+j}\geq 0
and
E_{n+j}\geq 0
, we have
L_{n+j}\ind\{E_{n+j}\geq m/(\alpha\hat{\tau})\}\leq L_{n+j}E_{n+j}\cdot\alpha\hat{\tau}/m
. Therefore,

\displaystyle{\textnormal{SDR}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{L_{n+j}E_{n+j}\cdot\alpha\hat{\tau}}{\hat{\tau}\cdot m}\bigg]\leq\frac{\alpha}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}[L_{n+j}E_{n+j}]\leq\alpha,

since
E_{n+j}
obeys Definition (
3.1
).
∎

The remaining task then reduces to constructing valid risk-adjusted e-values; the MDR and SDR control then follow automatically by Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
.

The strategy in Theorem
3.2
for controlling the MDR is related to
23
, though the connection is not straightforward. There, e-values are used to control risk in classical hypothesis testing where scientists are allowed to derive rules of taking multiple actions—more than just reject or not, each with a known risk.
In contrast, we use e-values to control unobserved risks in prediction problems.
Our e-values are also compatible with other techniques for e-values such as multiple testing, and can lead to control of interpretable metrics like the SDR in predictive inference settings.

4
Marginal risk control with conformal e-values

While Section
3
shows that any collection of risk-adjusted e-values can lead to valid, finite-sample control of the MDR or SDR, the power or utility of the procedure depends critically on the quality of the e-values. Poorly designed e-values can result in an excessively large number of unnecessary abstentions.

In this section, we study the concrete construction of risk-adjusted e-values tailored for MDR control based on conformal inference and data exchangeability, thereby completes the strategy in Theorem
3.2
. Owing to the distinct testing structure, the corresponding e-value construction for SDR control differs and is presented in Section
5
.
We then discuss an efficient computation shortcut that produces trust decisions directly, bypassing the explicit numerical search for e-values. Finally, we derive optimal choices within the proposed family of e-values.

4.1
Constructing e-values

Recall that we have a pre-trained score function
s\colon\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
that predicts
\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y)
or a related notion of uncertainty.
The construction below produces an e-value—and hence the deploy/abstain decision—based on the magnitude of score
s(X_{n+j})
.
Let the observed calibration risks be
L_{i}=\mathcal{L}(f,X_{i},Y_{i})
for
i\in[n]
.

Fix any constant
\gamma\in(0,1)
. We define

\displaystyle E_{\gamma,n+1}=\inf_{\ell\in[0,1]}~\Bigg\{\frac{(n+1)\cdot\ind\{s(X_{n+1})\leq t_{\gamma}(\ell)\}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t_{\gamma}(\ell)\}+\ell\ind\{s(X_{n+1})\leq t_{\gamma}(\ell)\}}\Bigg\}.

(4.1)

Here
\ell\in[0,1]
is a candidate value of the unknown risk
L_{n+1}
, and
t_{\gamma}(\ell)
is a data-dependent threshold chosen so that an empirical risk estimate does not exceed
\gamma
. Concretely,

\displaystyle t_{\gamma}(\ell)=\max\big\{t\in\mathcal{M}\colon\textrm{F}(t;\ell)\leq\gamma\big\},\quad\textrm{F}(t;\ell)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}+\ell\ind\{s(X_{n+1})\leq t\}}{n+1}.

(4.2)

Here we define
\mathcal{M}:=\{s(X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
. By convention,
\max\varnothing=-\infty
, and
E_{\gamma,n+1}=0
when
\inf_{\ell\in[0,1]}t_{\gamma}(\ell)=-\infty
.
Put differently,
E_{\gamma,n+1}=\inf_{\ell\in[0,1]}\{\ind\{s(X_{n+1})\leq t_{\gamma}(\ell)\}/\textrm{F}(t_{\gamma}(\ell);\ell)\}
.

Remark 4.1
.

In (
4.1
), we take the infimum over the entire range
[0,1]
. In principle, this search domain can be reduced to the values that can be attained, i.e.,
\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\cap\{\mathcal{L}(X_{n+1},y)\colon y\in\mathcal{Y}\}
. While our computation strategies and numerical experiments are tied to (
4.1
), such a replacement may lead to larger e-values and faster computation. For binary risk, this reduction allows computing
E_{\gamma,n+1}
by simply plugging in
\ell=1
.

The SCoRE procedure for MDR control is summarized in Algorithm
1
. (In practice, we recommend setting
\gamma=\alpha
; see Section
4.2
.)

Algorithm 1

SCoRE-MDR

0:

Labeled data
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, test data
X_{n+1}
, pre-trained score function
s(\cdot)
, MDR target
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

1:

Compute calibration risks
L_{i}=\mathcal{L}(f,X_{i},Y_{i})
for

.

2:

Obtain the scores
\mathcal{M}:=\{s(X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
.

3:

Compute
E_{\alpha,n+1}
as in (

).

Compute
\hat{\psi}_{n+1}=\ind\{E_{\alpha,n+1}\geq 1/\alpha\}
.

Deployment decision

.

Theorem
4.2
confirms that
E_{\gamma,n+1}
obeys Definition
3.1
, whose proof is in Appendix
B.1
. Consequently, Algorithm
1
controls the MDR below
\alpha
in finite samples. Importantly, Theorem
4.2
only relies on exchangeability among data, without requiring the score function

to accurately predict the risk.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
are exchangeable. Then,
\mathbb{E}[L_{n+1}E_{\gamma,n+1}]\leq 1
for any fixed
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

The intuition of (
4.1
) is as follows.
Should
L_{n+1}
be known, any random variable of the form

\displaystyle\frac{(n+1)\cdot L_{n+1}A_{n+1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}A_{i}+L_{n+1}A_{n+1}},

has expectation equal to
1
if
\{(L_{i},A_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
are exchangeable.
Thus, we can define
E_{n+1}:=\frac{(n+1)\cdot A_{n+1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}A_{i}+L_{n+1}A_{n+1}}
for some
\{(L_{i},A_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
that are exchangeable, which is a risk-adjusted e-value obeying
\mathbb{E}[E_{n+1}L_{n+1}]\leq 1
.

While the choice of
\{A_{i}\}
can be quite flexible, we set
A_{i}=\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq T\}
, where
T
is a random variable that is permutation invariant to
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
.
This is because in applying Theorem
3.2
to obtain MDR control,
a crucial inequality is
\ind\{E_{n+1}\geq 1/\alpha\}\leq\alpha\cdot E_{n+1}
, which is tight only if
E_{n+1}
takes value in
\{0,1/\alpha\}
. This motivates the “one-hot” form of the e-value.
Since
L_{n+1}
is unobserved,
we construct a conservative e-value by taking the smallest value over all the possible values of
L_{n+1}
via
\ell\in[0,1]
.
Finally, the
t_{\gamma}(\ell)
in (
4.2
) can be viewed as an empirical calibration: we note that
\textrm{F}(t;L_{n+1})
estimates
\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}]
in a way that preserves exchangeability.

Remark 4.3
.

We may generalize
s(x)
to any label-dependent scores

V\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
. Define

\displaystyle E_{\gamma,n+1}^{\textnormal{general}}=\inf_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}~\Bigg\{\frac{(n+1)\cdot\ind\{V(X_{n+1},y)\leq t_{\gamma}(y)\}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{V(X_{i},Y_{i})\leq t_{\gamma}(y)\}+\mathcal{L}(X_{n+1},y)\ind\{V(X_{n+1},y)\leq t_{\gamma}(y)\}}\Bigg\},

(4.3)

where
t_{\gamma}(y)=\max\big\{t\in\mathcal{M}\colon{\mathrm{F}}^{\textnormal{general}}(t;y)\leq\gamma\big\}
, and

\displaystyle{\mathrm{F}}^{\textnormal{general}}(t;y)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{V(X_{i},Y_{i})\leq t\}+\mathcal{L}(X_{n+1},y)\ind\{V(X_{n+1},y)\leq t\}}{n+1}.

(4.4)

Then, the definition in (
4.1
) is a special case with
V(x,y)=s(x)
. One can still follow the proof idea of Theorem
4.2
outlined above to show that
\mathbb{E}[E_{\gamma,n+1}^{\textnormal{general}}L_{n+1}]\leq 1
under exchangeability. However,
E_{\gamma,n+1}^{\textnormal{general}}\geq 1/\alpha
requires
V(X_{n+1},y)\leq t_{\gamma}(y)
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, which might be harder to satisfy in general. The computational and statistical benefits of this definition are beyond the scope of the current work.

4.2
Efficient computation

The definition of
E_{\gamma,n+1}
in (
4.1
) involves an infimum over a continuous variable
\ell\in[0,1]
.
Fortunately, for MDR control we only need the thresholding decision
\ind\{E_{\gamma,n+1}\geq 1/\alpha\}
, not the exact value of
E_{\gamma,n+1}
.
The next proposition shows how to streamline the computation, whose proof is in Appendix
B.2
.

Proposition 4.4
.

For
\gamma\leq\alpha
, we have

\displaystyle\ind\{E_{\gamma,n+1}\geq 1/\alpha\}=\ind\bigg\{\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind{\{s(X_{i})\leq s(X_{n+1})\}}}{n+1}\leq\gamma\bigg\}.

For
\gamma>\alpha
, we have

\displaystyle\ind\{E_{\gamma,n+1}\geq 1/\alpha\}=\ind\bigg\{

\displaystyle\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind{\{s(X_{i})\leq s(X_{n+1})\}}}{n+1}\leq\gamma,~

\displaystyle\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\ell+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}}{n+1}\notin(\alpha,\gamma],~~\forall t\in\mathcal{M},\ell\in[0,1]\bigg\},

where
\mathcal{M}=\{s(X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
is the set of all calibration and test scores.

Remark 4.5
.

Proposition
4.4
justifies setting the parameter
\gamma
equal to the nominal level
\alpha
. When
\gamma<\alpha
, the proposition implies
\ind\{E_{\gamma,n+1}\geq 1/\alpha\}\leq\ind\{E_{\alpha,n+1}\geq 1/\alpha\}
, and SCoRE always selects less frequently than
\gamma=\alpha
.
On the other hand, if
\gamma>\alpha
, one must impose an extra thresholding condition that almost always fails in practice, yielding asymptotically zero power (Theorem
4.6
) under standard regularity conditions.

Proposition
4.4
allows us to connect the MDR control instantiation of SCoRE with existing works in conformal inference and selective inference.
First, SCoRE with binary risks reduces to the conformal selection framework
(
38
)
discussed in Section
3.1
.
To select test instances with responses exceeding a specific threshold
Y_{n+1}>c
, it constructs p-values

\displaystyle p_{n+1}=\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\{V(X_{i},Y_{i})\leq V(X_{n+1},c)\}}{n+1},

(4.5)

where
V:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
,
V(x,y)=\infty\ind\{y>c\}+s(x)
is the clipped nonconformity score
1
1

1

We flipped the sign of the scores in
38
to be consistent with the current setup.
.
One can check that defining the risks as
L_{i}=\ind\{Y_{i}\leq c\}
, Proposition
4.4
yields
\ind\{E_{\alpha,n+1}\geq 1/\alpha\}=\ind\{p_{n+1}\leq\alpha\}
.
Thus, SCoRE is procedurally equivalent to conformal selection for one hypothesis with type-I error control.

Furthermore, our e-value is related to conformal risk control
(
2
)
with risk functions
L_{i}(\lambda):=L_{i}\ind\{-s(X_{i})\geq\lambda\}
, and
\lambda\in\Lambda=[-1,0]
. Given any bounded, non-increasing risk function, conformal risk control determines a parameter
\hat{\lambda}\in\Lambda
so that the test risk
\mathbb{E}[L_{n+1}(\hat{\lambda})]
is controlled. With this risk, it yields

\hat{\lambda}=\inf\big\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}(\lambda)}{n+1}\leq\alpha\big\},

We observe that
\hat{\psi}_{n+1}=1
given by Theorem
3.2
(with
\gamma=\alpha
) is equivalent to
-s(X_{n+1})\geq\hat{\lambda}
. That is, SCoRE opts to deploy a unit if and only if it is a risk-controlled decision. We defer the detailed explanation of this fact to Appendix
A.1
.

4.3
Asymptotics and optimality

While the MDR control holds regardless of the score function
s(\cdot)
,
the usefulness of the procedure depends on the average number of deployable instances, and more generally, on the downstream reward from deploying the model.
To navigate this choice, we define a general notion of power:

\displaystyle\text{Power}:=\mathbb{E}\big[r(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})\hat{\psi}_{n+1}\big],

(4.6)

where
r\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
encodes a bounded “reward” of deploying the model on a test instance which may depend on the unknown label.
It may also depend on the model
f
but we omit this for simplicity.
When
r(x,y)\equiv 1
, the power is the probability of deployment.
This flexibility allows practitioners to prioritize deployment on more valuable instances. For example, drug discovery scientists may assign a high reward to “novel” instances and maximize the reward in the selected candidates while controlling the total wastage.

Theorem
4.6
establishes a “Neyman-Pearson lemma”-like rule
(
52
)
for asymptotically optimal scoring functions that maximizes (
4.6
) subject to MDR control. Its proof is in Appendix
B.3
. Throughout, we treat
f(\cdot)
and
s(\cdot)
as fixed while taking the calibration sample size
n
to infinity.

Theorem 4.6
.

Suppose
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
are i.i.d. from some unknown distribution
P
. Define
\mathrm{F}^{*}(t):=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y)\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}]
for an independent copy
(X,Y)\sim P
, and
f,s
are viewed as fixed. Define
t^{*}:=\sup\{t\in[0,1]\colon\mathrm{F}^{*}(t)\leq\gamma\}
.
Suppose the distribution of
s(X)
is non-atomic, and
\mathrm{F}^{*}(t)
is strictly increasing at
t^{*}
.
Then the following holds:

(i).

As
n\to\infty
,
\sup_{\ell\in[0,1]}|t_{\gamma}(\ell)-t^{*}|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0
.

(ii).

\lim_{n\to\infty}\textnormal{Power}=\mathbb{E}[r(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})\ind\{s(X_{n+1})\leq t^{*}\}]
if
\gamma\leq\alpha
, and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\textnormal{Power}=0
if
\gamma>\alpha
. Furthermore, for a fixed
s(\cdot)
, the asymptotic power is optimized at
\gamma=\alpha
.

(iii).

Fix
\gamma=\alpha
. Define
l(x):=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y){\,|\,}X=x]
and
r(x):=\mathbb{E}[r(X,Y){\,|\,}X=x]
. Suppose
r(X)>0
a.s., and the distribution of
l(X)/r(X)
is non-atomic. Then, the asymptotic power is optimized at any
s(x)
that is strictly increasing in
l(x)/r(x)
.

With a constant reward, Theorem
4.6
suggests using standard estimators of the conditional prediction error. For example, in multi-class classification, one may be interested in whether the top-1 prediction (i.e., the label with the highest predicted probability) equals the true class, thereby defining
\mathcal{L}(f,x,y)=\ind\{y\neq\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{y^{\prime}}f(x,y^{\prime})\}
where
f(x,y)
is the predicted probability of label
y
. Letting
\hat{y}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{y^{\prime}}f(x,y^{\prime})
, a natural estimator for
l(x)
is then
\sum_{y^{\prime}\neq\hat{y}}f(x,y^{\prime})=1-f(x,\hat{y})
. In regression tasks with point prediction
f(x)
, it is natural to consider the mean squared error (MSE)
\mathcal{L}(f,x,y)=(y-f(x))^{2}
, in which case
s(x)
should estimate the conditional MSE
\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(x))^{2}{\,|\,}X=x]
.

When the reward is non-constant, Theorem
4.6
implies that the score function
s(X)
should aim to preserve the ranking of the risk-to-reward ratio
l(x)/r(x)
. It changes the choice of the optimal score (compared with that for a constant reward) only when dividing by
r(x)
substantially changes the ranking of
l(x)
alone. We shall see that this seems to rarely happen in real datasets, but our simulations do find some settings where the optimal scores under constant/non-constant rewards make a difference in the final decisions.

5
Selective risk control with conformal e-values

This section provides a construction of risk-adjusted e-values tailored to SDR control, which completes the procedure in Theorem
3.3
.
The key distinction from MDR control is that SDR concerns the average risk among selected instances (this notion is closer to the standard ideas in selective prediction
(
22
)
). Accordingly, the e-values are designed to integrate effectively with the e-BH filter. Section
5.1
presents the construction, along with an efficient algorithm for e-value computation that avoids grid search and runs in quadratic time. For multiple testing with the e-BH filter, Section
5.2
introduces a boosting strategy. Finally, Section
5.3
characterizes the asymptotically optimal choice of score.

5.1
Construction of e-values

We construct e-values for SDR control using the same exchangeability idea as in Section
4
, but with a thresholding rule calibrated to approximate the SDR incurred by selecting low-score test points.

As before, let the calibration risks be
L_{i}=\mathcal{L}(f,X_{i},Y_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,n
, and let
s\colon\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
be any pre-trained score. Fixing any constant
\gamma>0
, we define

\displaystyle E_{\gamma,n+j}=\inf_{\ell\in[0,1]}\bigg\{\frac{(n+1)\cdot\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(\ell)\}}{\ell\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(\ell)\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(\ell)\}}\bigg\}.

(5.1)

The threshold
t_{\gamma,n+j}(\ell)=\max\{t\in\mathcal{M}\colon{\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(t;\ell)\leq\gamma\}
is chosen as the largest score cutoff such that a plug-in estimate of the SDR does not exceed
\gamma
, and

\displaystyle{\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(t;\ell)=\frac{\ell\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}}{1+\sum_{k\neq j}\ind\{s(X_{n+k})\leq t\}}\cdot\frac{m}{n+1}.

Here
\mathcal{M}=\{s(X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m+n}
is the empirical calibration and test scores,
\max\varnothing=-\infty
, and we set
E_{\gamma,n+j}=0
when
\inf_{\ell\in[0,1]}t_{\gamma,n+j}(\ell)=-\infty
.
A slightly more conservative yet computationally efficient version is discussed in Appendix
A.2
.
We summarize the entire procedure in Algorithm
2
.

Algorithm 2

SCoRE-SDR

0:

\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}

\{X_{n+j}\}_{j=1}^{m}

s
, SDR target
\alpha\in(0,1)

\gamma>0
.

Compute calibration risks
L_{i}=\mathcal{L}(f,X_{i},Y_{i})
for

.

Obtain the scores

.

as in (

) (or the conservative version in Appendix

) for
j=1,\dots,m
.

\mathcal{R}
as the selection set of the eBH procedure applied to

5.1
establishes the validity of
E_{\gamma,n+j}
as a risk-adjusted e-value, whose proof is in Appendix
B.4
. As a consequence, the output of Algorithm

3.3
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n+m}
are exchangeable. Then
E_{\gamma,n+j}
defined in (
5.1
) obeys
\mathbb{E}[L_{n+j}E_{\gamma,n+j}]\leq 1
for any fixed
\gamma>0
.

Similar to Remark
4.1
, the infimum over
\ell\in[0,1]
in (
5.1
) can be restricted to attainable risk values, i.e.,
\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\cap\{\mathcal{L}(X_{n+j},y)\colon y\in\mathcal{Y}\}
, leading to sharper e-values when the range of risk is narrower. However, for unified statements, we keep the current definition throughout.

The high-level intuitions of (
5.1
) are as follows.
E_{\gamma,n+j}
conservatively approximates
\frac{(n+1)A_{n+j}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}L_{i}+A_{n+j}L_{n+j}}
where
A_{i}=\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq T\}
are random variables such that the
(A_{i},L_{i})
’s are exchangeable.
Here the “stopping time”
T
is approximated by
t_{\gamma,n+j}(\ell)
, which is carefully designed to align with the e-BH filter. This choice is inspired by the stopping-time interpretation of the BH procedure
(
9
;
67
)
as inverting an empirical-process estimate of the false discovery proportion (FDP). In our context,
{\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(t;\ell)
estimates the SDR when selecting test units with
s(X_{n+j})\leq t

5.2

.

Suppose the e-values

Remark 5.6
.

For a set of standard e-values in classical hypothesis testing, the boosting strategy described above remains valid. Specifically, given e-values

and independent boosting factors

Remark 5.7
.

Since MDR coincides with SDR when

shows boosting brings little benefit. Let

5.3
Asymptotics and optimality

To complete the picture, we now study the asymptotic behavior of our SDR-controlling procedure to gain insights on the choice of
s(\cdot)
. Again, we view the model
f
and the score function
s
as fixed.
We define

\displaystyle\text{Power}:=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}r(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})\hat{\psi}_{n+j}\bigg],

(5.2)

where
r\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
is a user-specified reward function. Intuitively, this notion of the power captures the total reward in the selectively deployed units (scaled by
1/m
), such as the expected rewards in investing in promising drugs.
The asymptotic behavior of our SDR-controlling procedure, as well as the optimal choice of the score function, are characterized in Theorem
5.8
, whose proof is in Appendix
B.9
.

Theorem 5.8
.

Assume the distribution of
s(X)
has no point mass. Define

\displaystyle{\textnormal{FR}}(t)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}]}{\mathbb{P}(s(X)\leq t)},\quad\text{and}\quad t_{\gamma}^{*}=\max\{t\colon{\textnormal{FR}}(t)\leq\gamma\}.

We further assume that for any sufficiently small
\delta>0
, we have
{\textnormal{FR}}(t)<\gamma
for
t\in(t_{\gamma}^{*}-\delta,t_{\gamma}^{*})
. Then the following statements hold:

(i).

As
n,m\to\infty
,
\sup_{1\leq j\leq m}\sup_{\ell\in[0,1]}\big|t_{\gamma,n+j}(\ell)-t_{\gamma}^{*}\big|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0
.

(ii).

\lim_{n,m\to\infty}\textnormal{Power}=\mathbb{E}[r(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})\ind\{s(X_{n+1})\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}]
if
\gamma<\alpha
, and
\lim_{n,m\to\infty}\textnormal{Power}=0
if
\gamma>\alpha
. Thus, for a fixed score function
s(\cdot)
, the asymptotic power is optimized as
\gamma\uparrow\alpha
.

(iii).

Let
r(x):=\mathbb{E}[r(X,Y){\,|\,}X=x]
and
l(x):=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y){\,|\,}X=x]
be the conditional expectation of the reward and risk, and suppose
r(X)>0
almost surely, and the distribution of
(l(X)-\alpha)_{+}/r(X)
has no point mass. Let
\gamma\uparrow\alpha
, then
\lim_{n,m\to\infty}\textnormal{Power}
is optimized at any score function
s(\cdot)
such that
s(x)
is monotone in
(l(x)-\alpha)/r(x)
.

The conditions in Theorem
5.8
resemble the standard mild assumptions in
66
to obtain meaningful asymptotic analysis of the FDR.

Theorem
5.8
demonstrates that the score
s(X)
should aim to rank test instances by their
excess risk per unit reward

(l(x)-\alpha)/r(x)
.
This is distinct from the optimality result for MDR control (Theorem
4.6
).
Intuitively, the optimal procedure explores the cost–benefit tradeoff: it prioritizes instances that achieves high reward per unit of risk.
Compared with the intuitive choice in which
s(x)
estimates
l(x)
, this makes a substantial difference only when dividing by
r(x)
drastically changes the ranking, such as when
l(x)-\alpha
and
r(x)
are very positively correlated. Finally, an intuitive way to implement this is to plug in estimators for the two functions and set
s(x)=(\hat{l}(x)-\alpha)/\hat{r}(x)
.

6
Extension: SCoRE under distribution shift

The techniques of constructing risk-adjusted e-values based on exchangeability enable broader methodology.
Here, we present a natural extension of SCoRE to scenarios where the calibration and test data are only weighted exchangeable, referred to as the covariate shift setting
(
70
)
. Such settings are particularly useful in applications like drug discovery where there is often differences between labeled and unlabeled data
(
47
;
37
;
50
;
49
)
.

Assumption 6.1
.

The labeled data follow
(X_{i},Y_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}P
while the test data follow
(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}Q
, and the two distributions obey
{\,\mathrm{d}}Q/{\,\mathrm{d}}P(x,y)=w(x)
for a known or estimable weight function
w\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

The key strategy is to construct risk-adjusted e-values obeying
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L_{n+j}E_{n+j}]\leq 1
under the
test
distribution. The MDR and SDR control then follow by the same testing arguments in Section
3
.
We first address the case where
w(\cdot)
is known, in which case an extension of SCoRE provides finite-sample MDR/SDR control. We then briefly discuss robustness properties with estimated weights where the guarantees become asymptotic to accommodate estimation errors.

6.1
Marginal risk control under covariate shift

We use the same thresholding rule

\hat{\psi}_{n+1}=\ind\{E_{\gamma,n+1}\geq 1/\alpha\}
, where the
weighted
e-value is defined as

\displaystyle E_{\gamma,n+1}=\inf_{\ell\in[0,1]}~\Bigg\{\frac{\ind\{s(X_{n+1})\leq t_{\gamma}(\ell)\}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\cdot L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t_{\gamma}(\ell)\}+w_{n+1}\cdot\ell\ind\{s(X_{n+1})\leq t_{\gamma}(\ell)\}}\Bigg\}.

(6.1)

Here we set
w_{i}=w(X_{i})
for
i\in[n+1]
,
t_{\gamma}(\ell)=\max\big\{t\in\mathcal{M}\colon\textrm{F}(t;\ell)\leq\gamma\big\}
, and

\displaystyle\textrm{F}(t;\ell)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}+w_{n+1}\cdot\ell\ind\{s(X_{n+1})\leq t\}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}}.

Here we define
\mathcal{M}:=\{s(X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
, and again set
E_{\gamma,n+1}=0
when
\inf_{\ell\in[0,1]}t_{\gamma}(\ell)=-\infty
.
The following theorem, whose proof is in Appendix
B.10
, demonstrates the validity of the weighted SCoRE e-value.

Theorem 6.2
.

Under Assumption
6.1
,
for any fixed constant
\gamma\in(0,1)
, it holds that
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L_{n+1}E_{\gamma,n+1}]\leq 1
.

Extending our discussion below Theorem
4.2
, the main idea of (
6.1
) is based on the observation that, should
L_{n+1}
be known, any random variable of the form

\displaystyle\frac{w_{n+1}\cdot L_{n+1}A_{n+1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\cdot L_{i}A_{i}+w_{n+1}\cdot L_{n+1}A_{n+1}},

has expectation equal to
1
under the covariate shift assumption
(
70
)
.

As in the unweighted case, one can often avoid computing the infimum in (
6.1
) explicitly. Proposition
4.4
in Appendix
A.3
presents an equivalent shortcut.

6.2

For the SDR control, we define the
weighted
e-values as

\displaystyle E_{\gamma,n+j}=\inf_{\ell\in[0,1]}\bigg\{\frac{\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(\ell)\}\cdot(w_{n+j}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i})}{w_{n+j}\cdot\ell\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(\ell)\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\cdot L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(\ell)\}}\bigg\},\qquad

(6.2)

where
w_{i}=w(X_{i})
for

, and

\displaystyle{\textrm{FR}_{n+j}}(t;\ell)=\frac{w_{n+j}\cdot\ell\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\cdot L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}}{1+\sum_{k\neq j}\ind\{s(X_{n+k})\leq t\}}\cdot\frac{m}{w_{n+j}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}}.

Here
\max\varnothing=-\infty
, and set

Theorem 6.3
.

Under Assumption
6.1
,

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L_{n+j}E_{\gamma,n+j}]\leq 1
for any fixed

and

6.3
Robustness to estimated weights

When the weight function

.

Under mild assumptions, the SCoRE procedure exhibits a

Remark 6.6

(Doubly robust calibration)
.

A series of research has shown that conformal prediction and selection under covariate shift enjoy “double robustness” properties
(
53
;
78
;
37
)
in the sense that they achieve the desired guarantee (coverage or FDR control) when either (i) the estimated weights are consistent or (ii) certain score function converges to an ideal score (conditional quantiles in
53
or conditional distribution functions in
37
). We remark that, with the threshold-based decisions and the expected risk control target, it is nontrivial to prove analogous double robustness results for SCoRE when only plug-in weights are used and no bias-adjustment terms like
78
. Nevertheless, it is possible to achieve so by calibrating the weights to a finite-sample balancing condition
(
27
;
79
;
40
)
. As the development of this approach is somewhat technical, we defer the statements and theory of MDR (resp. SDR) control to Appendix
A.4
(resp.
A.5
). In a nutshell, our results show that, if the estimated weights additionally satisfy a finite-sample balancing condition based on an estimated conditional risk
\hat{l}(x)
for
l(x)=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y){\,|\,}X=x]
, then SCoRE achieves (asymptotic) MDR/SDR control if either (i) the weights are consistent, or (ii) the conditional risk model is consistent.

Real data applications

We apply SCoRE to three applications that require selective deployment with continuous, task-specific risks: drug discovery under covariate shift (Section
7.1

7.2

).
Each application specifies a distinct risk function
\mathcal{L}
and (optionally) a reward function

7.1
Application to drug discovery

We first apply SCoRE to drug discovery to select promising drug candidates while controlling the wasted resources.
Since wet-lab essays for drug properties (e.g., activity against a disease target) are expensive
(
56
)
, ML models are often used to prioritize candidates for follow-up experiments. Existing conformal selection methods in this area typically control the fraction of false leads
(
4
;
3
;
24
;
35
)
, which is appropriate when each false lead incurs a similar downstream cost. In practice, however, follow-up costs can vary substantially across molecules, and one may also want to encourage secondary objectives such as diversity
(
59
)
.

Risk and reward functions.

Each sample is a drug candidate with features
X\in\mathcal{X}
and a biological property
Y\in\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
We aim to control the expected
wasted resources
among
false leads
. Consider a pre-determined threshold
c\in\mathbb{R}
, and a general cost of development
L(X)\in\mathbb{R}
. We define the risk
\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y)=L(X)\cdot\ind\{Y\leq c\}
.
Here we use the synthetic accessibility (SA) score
(
16
)
, denoted as
\text{SA}(x)
, as a proxy for cost (difficulty of development), which is fully determined by its chemical structure.
Here, MDR control implies limited total wastage of resources, while SDR control implies limited average wastage among selected candidates, which is more appropriate when wastage is allowed to scale with the number of follow-ups.
Throughout, we run SCoRE after normalizing the risks to
[0,1]
and report results on the original scale.
To reflect secondary factors, we consider three rewards:

(a)

Diversity.
To encourage the selection of diverse molecules by setting a reward function as the dissimilarity to a hold-out reference set. Here, we use
r_{1}(X,Y)=1-\mathrm{AvgTanimoto}(X)
where
\mathrm{AvgTanimoto}(X)

9
Discussion

In this paper, we present SCoRE, a framework based on conformal inference and e-values to derive a selective trust mechanism for any prediction model with precise control of risks among trusted instances. We propose two complementary risk metrics, and show how each can be controlled by applying standard testing procedures to any “risk-adjusted e-values”. We then propose concrete constructions of the e-values for each metric and analyze the optimal choice of scoring functions in these e-values. SCoRE’s principles can be readily extended to settings with covariate shift.
We demonstrate the utility of SCoRE in several real applications with diverse risk metrics, and conduct simulations to investigate factors that affect its performance.

Several interesting directions remain open. First, while the asymptotic analysis offers guidance on the choice of score functions that determines which instances may be more trustworthy, it is naturally desirable to use data to optimize the scores. It is thus interesting to develop methods that allow rigorous risk control with data-driven score choices
(
3
)
. However, compared with the binary setting, maintaining validity with an unknown continuous test risk is substantially more challenging.
In addition, the ideas of SCoRE may extend to richer scenarios such as online settings where test instances arrive sequentially and real-time decisions need to be made, where the e-values might be a useful tool
(
77
)
. It would also be interesting to apply SCoRE to selectively automate workflows with tailored risks.

Appendix A

A.1
Connection between SCoRE and conformal risk control

Here we continue the discussion on the equivalence of SCoRE and conformal risk control in Section
4.2
.
To see this, note that if SCoRE deploy the test instance, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind{\{s(X_{i})\leq s(X_{n+1})\}}}{n+1}=\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}(-s(X_{n+1}))}{n+1}\leq\alpha,

so
-s(X_{n+1})\geq\hat{\lambda}
by definition of
\hat{\lambda}
. Conversely,
-s(X_{n+1})\geq\hat{\lambda}
implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}(-s(X_{n+1}))}{n+1}\leq\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}(\hat{\lambda})}{n+1}\leq\alpha

by monotonicity of
L_{i}
, and the test instance is deployed by SCoRE.

A.2
A simpler construction of e-values

Following conformal inference ideas,
we will use the exchangeability among data to construct the e-values. At the same time, we use the estimation idea in the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to ensure tight selective risk control. The idea is to set

\displaystyle e_{j}=\frac{\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq\hat{t}_{j}\}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq\tilde{t}_{j}\}}\cdot\frac{m}{\alpha}.

where
\hat{t}_{j}
and
\tilde{t}_{j}
are stopping times chosen such that
\mathbb{E}[L_{n+j}e_{j}]\leq 1
.

Specifically, we set
\hat{t}_{j}\leq t_{j}(L_{n+j})\leq\tilde{t}_{j}
for some function
t_{j}(y)
obeying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t_{j}(L_{n+j})\}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq t_{j}(L_{n+j})\}}\Bigg]\leq\frac{\alpha}{m}.

Specifically, we set

\displaystyle\hat{t}_{j}=\max\Big\{t\colon\hat{\textrm{FR}}(t)\leq\alpha\Big\},\quad\textrm{where}\quad\hat{\textrm{FR}}(t)=\frac{\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq t\}}\cdot\frac{m}{n+1}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{j}=\max\Big\{t\colon\tilde{\textrm{FR}}(t)\leq\alpha\Big\},\quad\textrm{where}\quad\tilde{\textrm{FR}}(t)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq t\}}\cdot\frac{m}{n+1}.

For the purpose of proof, we define

\displaystyle t_{j}(\ell)=\max\Big\{t\colon{\textrm{FR}}(t;\ell)\leq\alpha\Big\},\quad\textrm{where}\quad{\textrm{FR}}(t;\ell)=\frac{\ell\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq t\}}\cdot\frac{m}{n+1}.

By definition, we know that for any
\ell\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\hat{\textrm{FR}}(t)\geq{\textrm{FR}}(t;\ell)\geq\tilde{\textrm{FR}}(t),

and hence
\hat{t}_{j}\leq t_{j}(\ell)\leq\tilde{t}_{j}
.
Therefore, writing
T_{j}=t_{j}(L_{n+j})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L_{n+j}e_{j}]

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{\alpha}\cdot\mathbb{E}\Bigg[L_{n+j}\frac{\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq\hat{t}_{j}\}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq\tilde{t}_{j}\}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m}{\alpha}\cdot\mathbb{E}\Bigg[L_{n+j}\frac{\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq{t}_{j}(L_{n+j})\}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq{t}_{j}(L_{n+j})\}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m}{\alpha}\cdot\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq T_{j}\}}{L_{n+j}\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq T_{j}\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq T_{j}\}}\Bigg]\cdot\frac{\alpha(n+1)}{m}.

Note that
T_{j}
is permutation invariant to
(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n},Z_{n+j})
where
Z_{n+j}=(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})
. Thus, by exchangeability, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L_{n+j}e_{j}]=\frac{m}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{n+1}\cdot\frac{\alpha(n+1)}{m}\leq 1.

A.3
Computation shortcuts of SCoRE under distribution shift

Proposition
A.1
presents the computation shortcuts for MDR control in SCoRE under covariate shift, parallel to Proposition
4.4
.

Proposition A.1
.

For
\gamma\leq\alpha
, we have

A.4
Doubly robust calibration of MDR under covariate shift

In this part, we present a general approach to achieve double robustness in MDR control under unknown covariate shift when multiple samples from the test distribution
Q
are available.
The key idea is to use an estimate
\hat{l}(x)
for the conditional risk
l(x):=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y){\,|\,}X=x]
and calibrate the weights to satisfy finite-sample balance.

The following assumption posits that the estimated weights must enforce a finite-sample balance condition in the thresholded, estimated loss, serving to protect against weight misspecification.

Assumption A.3
.

\{\hat{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n+m}
obey
the following approximate balancing condition:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}\hat{l}(X_{i})\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq\hat{t}\}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{l}(X_{n+j})\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq\hat{t}\}+o_{P}(1),\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}=1+o_{P}(1),

where
\hat{t}=\sup\{t\colon\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{l}(X_{n+j})\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq{t}\}\leq\alpha\}
.

Intuitively, the “correct” weights
w(X_{i})
should balance the empirical mean of any function across the two groups. While this might be difficult to achieve especially with misspecified weights, Assumption
A.3
enforces this balance at a specific function: the estimated weights to have an equal (reweighted) mean between two groups at the estimate cutoff
\hat{t}
. Asymptotically, this ensures the unknown MDR under the test distribution is well approximated by the reweighted calibration data even though the weights may be misspecified.

Given certain preliminary estimators
\hat{w}(\cdot)
and
\hat{l}(\cdot)
, Assumption
A.3
can be fulfilled by weight calibration via an efficient covariate balancing procedure, see, e.g.,
79
;
40
.

Theorem
A.4
ensures the double robustness in MDR control, in the sense that as long as either the weight or the conditional risk model is correct, we obtain asymptotic MDR control. Its proof is in Appendix
C.3
.

Theorem A.4
.

Take
\gamma=\alpha
, and suppose
\hat{l}(\cdot)
is trained independent of the calibration and test data, and
s(X)
has no point mass. Suppose Assumption

holds, and assume

A.5
Doubly robust calibration of SDR under covariate shift

In this part, we present a strategy for doubly robust calibration of SDR control. To protect against weight misspecification, we impose the following balancing condition on the estimated weights. Compared with the MDR version, here we enforce balance at a distinct cutoff
\hat{t}
that is relevant to the SDR.

Assumption A.5
.

\{\hat{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n+m}
obey the following approximate balancing condition:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}\hat{l}(X_{i})\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq\hat{t}\}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{l}(X_{n+j})\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq\hat{t}\}+o_{P}(1),\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}=1+o_{P}(1),

\gamma=\alpha

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[r(X,Y)\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}\big]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y)\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}\big]\leq\alpha.

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{maximize}}_{b\colon\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}}\quad

is nonnegative. As a result,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(l(X)-c_{0}\cdot r(X))(b^{*}(X)-b(X))\big]\leq 0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle c_{0}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[r(X)b^{*}(X)-r(X)b(X)\big]\geq\mathbb{E}[l(X)(b^{*}(X)-b(X))]=\alpha-\mathbb{E}[l(X)b(X)]\geq 0,

where the last inequality is due to the constraint for

Therefore, the original problem is optimized at any

such that

\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}=\ind\{l(x)/r(x)\leq c_{0}\},

for which a sufficient condition is that

is strictly increasing in

.

s(X_{n+j})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)
.

With Lemma
B.1
in hand, for any
j
, we have

\displaystyle E_{\gamma,n+j}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+1)\cdot\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)\}}{\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{{\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(t_{\gamma,n+j}(1),1)}\cdot\frac{\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)\}}{1+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)\}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)\}}{1+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)\}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{\ind\{p_{j}\leq\alpha|{\mathcal{S}}^{\text{cs}}|/m\}}{|{\mathcal{S}}^{\text{cs}}|}=e_{j},

where the first inequality is due to the definition of
{\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(\cdot,\cdot)
, and the second inequality follows from Lemma
B.1
. Finally, if
e_{j}=0
, then
j\notin{\mathcal{S}}^{\text{CS}}
. By Lemma
B.1
, this implies
s(X_{n+j})>t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)
, and hence
E_{\gamma,n+j}=0
. We thus conclude the proof of Proposition
5.3
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
B.1
.

Fix any
j\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\text{CS}}
throughout. We prove the two facts separately.

Proof of (i).

Similar to the proof of Theorem 2.6 in
38
, we note that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{\text{CS}}={\mathcal{S}}^{\text{p}}(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})={\mathcal{S}}^{\text{p}}(p_{1}^{(j)},\dots,p_{m}^{(j)})

for any
j\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\text{CS}}
, where the “proxy” p-values
\{p_{\ell}^{(j)}\}^{m}_{\ell=1}
are given by

\displaystyle p_{\ell}^{(j)}=\frac{1}{n+1}\Big[\ind\{\widehat{V}_{n+j}\leq\widehat{V}_{n+\ell}\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\{V_{i}\leq\widehat{V}_{n+\ell}\}\Big].

To see this equivalence, comparing the pairs of p-values
p_{\ell}
and
p_{\ell}^{(j)}
, we observe that when
p_{j}\leq p_{\ell}
, we have
\widehat{V}_{n+j}\leq\widehat{V}_{n+\ell}
, hence
p_{\ell}^{(j)}=p_{\ell}
. On the other hand, if
p_{j}>p_{\ell}
, we have
\widehat{V}_{n+j}>\widehat{V}_{n+\ell}
and thus
p_{\ell}^{(j)}\leq p_{\ell}\leq p_{j}
.
In both cases, the ordering of each
p_{\ell}
relative to
p_{j}=p_{j}^{(j)}
is preserved when replacing
p_{\ell}
with
p_{\ell}^{(j)}
. By the step‑up property of the BH procedure, this implies that the BH selection set remains unchanged when replacing
(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})
with
(p_{1}^{(j)},\dots,p_{m}^{(j)})
.

We now rank the proxy conformal p-values to obtain
p_{(1)}^{(j)}\leq\dots\leq p_{(m)}^{(j)}
. In addition, since each p-value increases with the predicted risk
s(X_{i})
by our definition, with a slight abuse of notation we also denote
s(X_{n+(1)})\leq\dots\leq s(X_{n+(m)})
, where
s(X_{n+(1)})
corresponds to
p_{(1)}^{(j)}
, and so on.

By the property of BH procedure, we know
p_{(k^{*})}^{(j)}\leq\alpha k^{*}/m
where
k^{*}=|{\mathcal{S}}^{{\textnormal{CS}}}|
since
j\in{\mathcal{S}}^{{\textnormal{CS}}}
. Define
\ell^{*}=|\{\ell\in[m]:s(X_{n+\ell})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)\}|
, so that there are
\ell^{*}
-many predicted test scores below
t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)
. We then have

\displaystyle{\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}

\displaystyle(s(X_{n+(k^{*})}),1)=\frac{\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq s(X_{n+(k^{*})})\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq s(X_{n+(k^{*})})\}}{1+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq s(X_{n+(k^{*})})\}}\cdot\frac{m}{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq s(X_{n+(k^{*})})\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\{L_{i}\neq 0\}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq s(X_{n+(k^{*})})\}}{1+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq s(X_{n+(k^{*})})\}}\cdot\frac{m}{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ind\{\widehat{V}_{n+j}\leq\widehat{V}_{n+(k^{*})}\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\{V_{i}\leq\widehat{V}_{n+(k^{*})}\}}{1+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq s(X_{n+(k^{*})})\}}\cdot\frac{m}{n+1}

\displaystyle=p_{(k^{*})}^{(j)}\cdot\frac{m}{k^{*}}\leq\frac{\alpha k^{*}}{m}\frac{m}{k^{*}}=\alpha,

by the construction of the risk function and scores.
Consequently, we know
s(X_{n+(k^{*})})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)
by the definition of
t_{\gamma,n+j}(\cdot)
. This implies
s(X_{n+\ell})\leq s(X_{n+(k^{*})})
, hence
s(X_{n+\ell})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)
, for any
\ell\in{\mathcal{S}}^{{\textnormal{CS}}}
. We thus establish the direction
{\mathcal{S}}^{{\textnormal{CS}}}\subseteq\{\ell\in[m]:s(X_{n+\ell})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)\}
.

For the converse, we see that

\displaystyle p_{(\ell^{*})}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ind\{\widehat{V}_{n+j}\leq\widehat{V}_{n+(\ell^{*})}\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\{V_{i}\leq\widehat{V}_{n+(\ell^{*})}\}}{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ind\{\widehat{V}_{n+j}\leq\widehat{V}_{n+(\ell^{*})}\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\{V_{i}\leq\widehat{V}_{n+(\ell^{*})}\}}{1+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})})\}}\cdot\frac{m}{n+1}\cdot\frac{\ell^{*}}{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})})\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})})\}}{1+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})})\}}\cdot\frac{m}{n+1}\cdot\frac{\ell^{*}}{m}

\displaystyle={\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})}),1)\cdot\frac{\ell^{*}}{m},

where we used the fact that
1+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})})\}=\ell^{*}
. From here, an important observation is that
s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})})
is the largest test prediction no greater than
t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)
.
When
t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)
corresponds to a test point, we must have
s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})})=t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)
, and thus
{\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})}),1)={\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(t_{\gamma,n+j}(1),1)
. Otherwise if
t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)
corresponds to a calibration point, we notice that the function
{\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(\cdot,1)
is monotonically increasing in the range
[s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})}),t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)]
, since the nominator is increasing and the denominator is constant across this range. As such, we must have
{\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})}),1)\leq{\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(t_{\gamma,n+j}(1),1)
.
Combining the two cases, we have

\displaystyle p_{(\ell^{*})}^{(j)}={\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})}),1)\cdot\frac{\ell^{*}}{m}\leq{\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(t_{\gamma,n+j}(1),1)\cdot\frac{\ell^{*}}{m}\leq\gamma\ell^{*}/m=\alpha\ell^{*}/m,

so we must have
\ell^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{{\textnormal{CS}}}
by the nature of the BH procedure. Therefore, for any
\ell\in[m]
with
s(X_{n+\ell})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)
, we know
s(X_{n+\ell})\leq s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})})
and
p_{\ell}^{(j)}\leq p_{(\ell^{*})}^{(j)}
, so we must also have
\ell\in{\mathcal{S}}^{{\textnormal{CS}}}
. We thus establish the other direction, which, together with the preceding part, proves (i).

Proof of (ii).

If
j\in{\mathcal{S}}^{{\textnormal{CS}}}
, we know
j\in\{\ell\in[m]:s(X_{n+\ell})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)\}
by (i), and thus
s(X_{n+j})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)
. For the other direction, suppose
s(X_{n+j})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)
. Then by definition of
\ell^{*}
(recall its definition in our proof of (i)), we have
s(X_{n+j})\leq s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})})\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(1)
, which implies
p_{(\ell^{*})}^{(j)}=p_{(\ell^{*})}
. By the same arguments as in the proof of (i), we know
{\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(s(X_{n+(\ell^{*})}),1)\leq{\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(t_{\gamma,n+j}(1),1)\leq\alpha
, hence
p_{(\ell^{*})}^{(j)}=p_{(\ell^{*})}\leq\alpha\ell^{*}/m
, and thus
\ell^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{{\textnormal{CS}}}
. As a result, we must have
j\in{\mathcal{S}}^{{\textnormal{CS}}}
since
p_{j}\leq p_{(\ell^{*})}
. This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

B.8
Proof of Theorem
5.5

Proof of Theorem
5.5
.

In this proof, we define the notation
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}
to denote expectation or probability conditional on the base e-values
\bm{E}:=(E_{n+1},\dots,E_{n+m})
and the risks
\bm{L}:=(L_{n+1},\dots,L_{n+m})
. Our proof strategy is to analyze the impact of boosting coefficients
\xi_{n+j}
on the selection set, after fixing a certain set of e-values and risks.

First, by the law of total expectation, we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{SDR}=\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{SDR}(\bm{E})]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{SDR}(\bm{E},j)],

where we define

\displaystyle\textnormal{SDR}(\bm{E},\bm{L}):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}\Bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{n+j}\ind\{j\in\mathcal{R}\}}{|\mathcal{R}|}\Bigg],\quad\text{and}\quad\textnormal{SDR}(\bm{E},\bm{L},j):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}\Bigg[\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{j\in\mathcal{R}\}}{|\mathcal{R}|}\Bigg].

We note that the randomness of
\textnormal{SDR}(\bm{E},\bm{L})
and
\textnormal{SDR}(\bm{E},\bm{L},j)
only lies in the boosting coefficients
\xi_{j}
. By properties of the eBH procedure, we can then write

\displaystyle\textnormal{SDR}(\bm{E},\bm{L},j)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}\Bigg[\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{E_{n+j}/\xi_{n+j}\geq m/\alpha|\mathcal{R}|\}}{|\mathcal{R}|}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}\Bigg[\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{E_{n+j}/\xi_{n+j}\geq m/\alpha k\}}{k}\ind\{|\mathcal{R}|=k\}\Bigg].

From here, we consider the case of heterogeneous and homogeneous boosting separately.

Heterogeneous Boosting
. We employ a similar leave-one-out argument as in
37
;
3
. Define
\mathcal{R}_{j\to\infty}
as the rejection index set of eBH (at level
\alpha
) applied to the set
\{E_{n+1}/\xi_{n+1},\dots,E_{n+j-1}/\xi_{n+j-1},\infty,E_{n+j+1}/\xi_{n+j+1},\dots,E_{n+m}/\xi_{n+m}\}
. Then, clearly
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{j\to\infty}
, and when the
j
-th sample is already rejected, i.e.
E_{n+j}/\xi_{n+j}\geq m/\alpha k
, we know
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{j\to\infty}
. This is due to the step-up nature of eBH. Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}\Bigg[\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{E_{n+j}/\xi_{n+j}\geq m/\alpha k\}}{k}\ind\{|\mathcal{R}|=k\}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}\Bigg[\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{E_{n+j}/\xi_{n+j}\geq m/\alpha k\}}{k}\ind\{|\mathcal{R}_{j\to\infty}|=k\}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{n+j}}{k}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}\Big[\ind\{|\mathcal{R}_{j\to\infty}|=k\}\Big]\cdot\mathbb{P}(\xi_{n+j}\leq E_{n+j}\alpha k/m)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}\Big[\ind\{|\mathcal{R}_{j\to\infty}|=k\}\Big]\cdot L_{n+j}E_{n+j}\alpha/m

where the first equality is due to independence between
\mathcal{R}_{j\to\infty}
and
\xi_{j}
, and the second equality is due to the uniformity of
\xi_{j}
. Then, we know

\displaystyle\textnormal{SDR}(\bm{E},\bm{L})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}[\ind\{|\mathcal{R}_{j\to\infty}|=k\}]\cdot L_{n+j}E_{n+j}\alpha/m=\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{n+j}E_{n+j}\alpha/m.

Finally, taking expectation over
(\bm{E},\bm{L})
yields
\textnormal{SDR}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\alpha}{m}\mathbb{E}[L_{n+j}E_{n+j}]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha/m=\alpha
.

Homogeneous Boosting
.
To prove this case, we first further decompose the SDR. We have

\displaystyle\textnormal{SDR}(\bm{E},\bm{L},j)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}\Bigg[\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{E_{n+j}/\xi\geq m/\alpha k\}}{k}(\ind\{|\mathcal{R}|\leq k\}-\ind\{|\mathcal{R}|\leq k-1\})\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}\Big[\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{E_{n+j}/\xi\geq\alpha\}}{m}\Big]+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}\Big[\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{E_{n+j}/\xi\geq m/\alpha k\}}{k}\ind\{|\mathcal{R}|\leq k\}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}\Big[\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{E_{n+j}/\xi\geq m/\alpha(k+1)\}}{k+1}\ind\{|\mathcal{R}|\leq k\}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{n+j}}{m}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(\xi\leq\alpha E_{n+j})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{L_{n+j}}{k}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(\xi/E_{n+j}\leq\alpha k/m,|\mathcal{R}|\leq k)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{L_{n+j}}{k+1}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(\xi/E_{n+j}\leq\alpha(k+1)/m,|\mathcal{R}|\leq k)

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{n+j}}{m}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(\xi\leq\alpha E_{n+j})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{L_{n+j}}{k}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(|\mathcal{R}|\leq k\mid\xi/E_{n+j}\leq\alpha k/m)\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(\xi/E_{n+j}\leq\alpha k/m)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{L_{n+j}}{k+1}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(|\mathcal{R}|\leq k\mid\xi/E_{n+j}\leq\alpha(k+1)/m)\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(\xi/E_{n+j}\leq\alpha(k+1)/m).

To proceed, we relies on the PRDS property of the boosted e-values
(\xi/E_{n+1},\dots,\xi/E_{n+m})
, if a common boosting factor is used.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be non-negative, fixed constants, and let
\xi\sim\textnormal{Unif}(0,1)
. Then, the random variables
(a_{1}\xi,\dots,a_{m}\xi)
are PRDS on the index set
\{j:a_{j}\neq\infty\}
.

The proof of above lemma can be easily adapted from that of Lemma C.1 in
37
by additionally considering the case
a_{j}=\infty
. Setting
a_{j}=1/E_{n+j}
in this lemma, we know that
(\xi/E_{n+1},\dots,\xi/E_{n+m})
is PRDS on
\{j:E_{n+j}\neq 0\}
, conditional on
(\bm{E},\bm{L})
. As a result,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(|\mathcal{R}|\leq k\mid\xi/E_{n+j}\leq\alpha k/m)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(|\mathcal{R}|\leq k\mid\xi/E_{n+j}\leq\alpha(k+1)/m)

for
j
with
e_{j}<\infty
, since
|\mathcal{R}|\leq k
is an increasing set in the boosted e-values. Due to the independence of
\xi
from every other variable, we obtain

\displaystyle\textnormal{SDR}(\bm{E},\bm{L},j)\leq\frac{L_{n+j}}{m}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(\xi\leq\alpha E_{n+j})+\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}L_{n+j}\Big\{\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(\xi/E_{n+j}\leq\alpha k/m)-\phantom{a}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\frac{1}{k+1}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(\xi/E_{n+j}\leq\alpha(k+1)/m)\Big\}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(|\mathcal{R}|\leq k\mid\xi/E_{n+j}\leq\alpha k/m)

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{n+j}\min\{\alpha E_{n+j},1\}}{m}+L_{n+j}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\Big\{\frac{\min\{1,\alpha kE_{n+j}/m\}}{k}-\frac{\min\{1,\alpha(k+1)E_{n+j}/m\}}{k+1}\Big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(|\mathcal{R}|\leq k\mid\xi/E_{n+j}\leq\alpha k/m).

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{n+j}\min\{\alpha E_{n+j},1\}}{m}+L_{n+j}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\Big\{\min\{1/k,\alpha E_{n+j}/m\}-\min\{1/(k+1),\alpha E_{n+j}/m\}\Big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(|\mathcal{R}|\leq k\mid\xi/E_{n+j}\leq\alpha k/m).

(
*
)

Now, if
\alpha E_{n+j}\leq 1,
then both minimum term in the summation evaluated to
\alpha E_{n+j}/m
for any
k
, and we have
\textnormal{SDR}(\bm{E},\bm{L},j)\leq L_{n+j}\min\{\alpha E_{n+j},1\}/m=\alpha L_{n+j}E_{n+j}/m
. Otherwise, we let
k^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
to be the unique integer with
\frac{1}{k^{*}+1}\leq\alpha E_{n+j}/m\leq\frac{1}{k^{*}}
, and we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{SDR}(\bm{E},\bm{L},j)

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{n+j}}{m}+L_{n+j}\Bigg\{\frac{\alpha E_{n+j}}{m}-\frac{1}{k^{*}+1}+\sum_{k=k^{*}+1}^{m-1}(\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k+1})\Bigg\}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{E},\bm{L}}(|\mathcal{R}|\leq k\mid\xi/E_{n+j}\leq\alpha k/m)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{n+j}}{m}+L_{n+j}\Big(\frac{\alpha E_{n+j}}{m}-\frac{1}{m}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha L_{n+j}E_{n+j}}{m}.

Putting above bounds together, we know

\displaystyle\textnormal{SDR}(\bm{E},\bm{L})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{n+j}E_{n+j}\alpha/m,

and we conclude the proof by taking the expectation over
(\bm{E},\bm{L})
.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Proof of Theorem

.

Throughout, we view
s(\cdot)
as fixed.
Note that by definition, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}}{1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq t\}}\cdot\frac{m}{n+1}\leq{\textnormal{FR}}_{n+j}(t;\ell)\leq\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}}{1\vee\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq t\}}\cdot\frac{m}{n+1}.

{\textnormal{FR}}(t)<\alpha
for

for any sufficiently small

, we know that

\displaystyle\sup_{1\leq j\leq m}\sup_{\ell\in[0,1]}\big|t_{\gamma,n+j}(\ell)-t_{\gamma}^{*}\big|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0.

(B.4)

Also, due to the continuity of the distribution of
s(X)
, we have

\displaystyle{\textnormal{FR}}(t_{\gamma}^{*})=\frac{\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}]}{\mathbb{P}(s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*})}=\gamma.

(B.5)

For simplicity, we write
s_{i}=s(X_{i})
for
i\in[n+m]
. We define

\displaystyle\hat{F}(\eta)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{E_{\gamma,n+j}\geq\eta\},\quad\eta>0.

By Proposition
5.2
, we know that
t_{\gamma,n+j}(\ell)

, and therefore

\displaystyle\hat{F}(\eta)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{s_{n+j}\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(0)\}\ind\Big\{\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s_{i}\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(0)\}}{n+1}\leq 1/\eta\Big\}.

By (

) and the uniform law of large numbers applied to
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s_{i}\leq t\}
, as well as the continuity of the distribution of
s(X)
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{1\leq j\leq m}\bigg|\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s_{i}\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(0)\}}{n+1}-\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}]\bigg|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0.

As such, for any
\eta<1/\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}]
, by (
B.4
) we have

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq j\leq m}\ind\bigg\{\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s_{i}\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(0)\}}{n+1}\leq 1/\eta\bigg\}~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~1,

whereas for any
\eta>1/\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}]
, by (
B.4
) we have

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq m}\ind\bigg\{\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s_{i}\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(0)\}}{n+1}\leq 1/\eta\bigg\}~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0.

Therefore, the uniform law of large numbers and the continuity of the distribution of
s_{n+j}
’s imply

\displaystyle\sup_{\eta>0,~\eta\neq 1/\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}]}\big|\hat{F}(\eta)-F^{*}(\eta)\big|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0,

(B.6)

where

\displaystyle F^{*}(\eta)=\mathbb{P}\big(s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\big)\ind\Big\{\eta\leq 1/\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}]\Big\}.

Note that the e-BH procedure can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{n+j}=\ind\{E_{\gamma,n+j}\geq\hat{\eta}\},\quad\hat{\eta}=\inf\bigg\{\eta\colon\frac{m}{\eta\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{E_{\gamma,n+j}\geq\eta\}}\leq\alpha\bigg\}.

Put differently, we have
\hat{\eta}=\inf\{\eta\colon\eta\hat{F}(\eta)\geq 1/\alpha\}
. Due to (
B.6
), we know that
\eta\hat{F}(\eta)~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0
uniformly over
\eta>1/\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}]
, whereas

\displaystyle\sup_{\eta<1/\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}]}\big|\eta\hat{F}(\eta)-\eta F^{*}(\eta)\big|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0.

(B.7)

Writing
\eta^{*}=1/\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}]
, we have

\displaystyle\eta^{*}F^{*}(\eta^{*})=\frac{\mathbb{P}(s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*})}{\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}]}={\textnormal{FR}}(t_{\gamma}^{*})^{-1}=1/\gamma,

and
\eta F(\eta)=0
for all
\eta>\eta^{*}
. On the other hand, for any
\eta<\eta^{*}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\eta F^{*}(\eta)=\eta\mathbb{P}(s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*})<\frac{\mathbb{P}(s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*})}{\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}]}=1/\gamma,\quad\text{for all }\eta<\eta^{*},

where the inequality uses
\eta<\eta^{*}
and (
B.5
).
Therefore, for any
\gamma>\alpha
, we have
\mathbb{P}(\hat{\eta}=+\infty)\to 1
and thus
\mathbb{P}(\hat{\psi}_{n+j}=1,~\forall j)\to 0
, i.e., the procedure is powerless. On the other hand, for any
\gamma\leq\alpha
, due to the uniform convergence (
B.7
) and the linearity of the limiting function
\eta F^{*}(\eta)
, we see that

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~\eta_{\gamma}^{*},\quad\text{where}\quad\eta_{\gamma}^{*}=\frac{1}{\alpha\cdot\mathbb{P}(s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*})}=\inf\{\eta\colon\eta F^{*}(\eta)\geq 1/\alpha\}<\eta^{*}.

Recalling the definition of power and writing
R_{n+j}=r(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})
for simplicity, for any
\eta<\alpha
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}r(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})\hat{\psi}_{n+j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}R_{n+j}\ind\{E_{\gamma,n+j}\geq\hat{\eta}\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}R_{n+j}\ind\{s_{n+j}\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(0)\}\ind\Big\{\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s_{i}\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(0)\}}{n+1}\leq 1/\hat{\eta}\Big\}

\displaystyle{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~\mathbb{E}\big[r(X,Y)\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}\big]\cdot\ind\big\{\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}]\leq 1/\eta_{\gamma}^{*}\big\},

(B.8)

where the last a.s. convergence uses the uniform law of large numbers for
\{s_{n+j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
and
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and the convergence of
1/\hat{\eta}~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~1/\eta_{\gamma}^{*}>1/\eta^{*}=\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}]
.
We thus have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}r(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})\hat{\psi}_{n+j}~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~\mathbb{E}\big[r(X,Y)\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}\big],\quad\text{for all }\gamma<\alpha.

By the dominated convergence theorem, this also implies the converges of the expectation since every
r(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})
is bounded.
We also remark that the limiting behavior at the critical point
\gamma=\alpha
is unclear, since in the indicator function
\ind\big\{\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s_{i}\leq t_{\gamma,n+j}(0)\}}{n+1}\leq 1/\hat{\eta}\big\}
, both sides converge to the same limit. We thus focus our discussion on
\gamma\uparrow\alpha
in the next.

Now let
\gamma\uparrow\alpha
. The problem of optimizing the asymptotic power subject to SDR constraint reduces to maximizing
\mathbb{E}\big[r(X,Y)\ind\{s(X)\leq t_{\gamma}^{*}\}\big]
where
\gamma\uparrow\alpha
, which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{maximize}}_{s(\cdot),t\in\mathbb{R}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[r(X,Y)\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}\big]

subject to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}]}{\mathbb{P}(s(X)\leq t)}\leq\alpha.

Using the equivalent representation with the binary function
b(X)=\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}
as the decision variable, the above optimization program is further equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{maximize}}_{b(\cdot)}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[r(X,Y)b(X)\big]

subject to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Lb(X)]\leq\alpha\mathbb{E}[b(X)].

Now letting
r(X)=\mathbb{E}[r(X,Y){\,|\,}X]
and
l(X)=\mathbb{E}[L{\,|\,}X]
, it is further equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{maximize}}_{b(\cdot)}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[r(X)b(X)\big]

subject to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)b(X)]\leq 0.

It is clear that the optimal
b^{*}(X)
must take the value of
1
whenever
l(X)-\alpha\leq 0
, as it increase the objective without increasing
\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)b(X)]
. As such, the optimal solution and objective of the above program is further equivalent to those of

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{maximize}}_{b(\cdot)}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[r(X)b(X)\big]

subject to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)_{+}b(X)]\leq\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)_{-}],

where we denote
x_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
and
x_{-}=\max\{-x,0\}
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

We now define
\rho(x)=(l(x)-\alpha)_{+}/r(x)
, and
b^{*}(x):=\ind\{\rho(x)\leq c_{0}\}
where
c_{0}=\sup\{c\colon\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)_{+}\ind\{\rho(X)\leq c\}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)_{-}]\}
, and show the optimality of
b^{*}(X)
with similar ideas as the Neyman-Pearson Lemma. Due to the continuity of the distribution of
\rho(X)
, we know
\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)_{+}b^{*}(X)]=\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)_{-}]
.
Let
b(\cdot)
be any binary function that obeys
\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)_{+}b(X)]\leq\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)_{-}]
. Since
b(x)\in\{0,1\}
, we have
b^{*}(x)-b(x)\geq 0
whenever
\rho(x)\leq c_{0}
and
b^{*}(x)-b(x)\leq 0
whenever
\rho(x)>c_{0}
. This implies
(\rho(X)-c_{0})(b(X)-b^{*}(X))\geq 0
almost surely. Multiplying both sides by
r(X)
and taking expectation, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\{(l(X)-\alpha)_{+}-c_{0}r(X)\big\}\cdot\big\{b(X)-b^{*}(X)\big\}\Big]\geq 0.

Re-organizing terms, we have

\displaystyle c_{0}\mathbb{E}\big[r(X)b(X)\big]

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\mathbb{E}\big[r(X)b^{*}(X)\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[(l(X)-\alpha)_{+}\cdot\{b(X)-b^{*}(X)\}\big]

\displaystyle=c_{0}\mathbb{E}\big[r(X)b^{*}(X)\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[(l(X)-\alpha)_{+}b(X)\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[(l(X)-\alpha)_{+}b^{*}(X)\big]

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\mathbb{E}\big[r(X)b^{*}(X)\big],

where the last inequality uses the fact that
\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)_{+}b(X)]\leq\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)_{-}]
and
\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)_{+}b^{*}(X)]=\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)_{-}]
. Dividing both sizes by
c_{0}
, we then have
\mathbb{E}[r(X)b(X)]\leq\mathbb{E}[r(X)b^{*}(X)]
, confirming the optimality of
b^{*}(\cdot)
.
Recalling that
b^{*}(x)=1
whenever
l(x)\leq\alpha
, it can be equivalently written as
b^{*}(x)=\ind\{(l(x)-\alpha)/r(x)\leq c_{0}\}
, where
c_{0}=\sup\{c\colon\mathbb{E}[(l(X)-\alpha)\ind\{\rho(X)\leq c\}]\leq 0\}
.

So far, we have shown that the asymptotic power (as
\gamma\uparrow\alpha
) is optimized for any function
s(\cdot)
such that
b^{*}(X)=\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}
for the critical value of
t
that obeys the constraint
\frac{\mathbb{E}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}]}{\mathbb{P}(s(X)\leq t)}\leq\alpha
, where
b^{*}(x)=\ind\{(l(x)-\alpha)/r(x)\leq c_{0}\}
. Noting the equivalent constraint
\mathbb{E}[l(X)\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}]\leq\alpha\mathbb{P}(s(X)\leq t)
, we see that this is true for any
s(x)
that is monotone in
(l(x)-\alpha)/r(x)
, thereby completing the proof of the last statement.
∎

Proof of Proposition

We proceed by mimicking the proof of Proposition

. In the weighted case, the following equivalence continues to hold:

\displaystyle E_{\gamma,n+1}\geq 1/\alpha\iff s(X_{n+1})\leq t_{\gamma}(\ell),\text{ and }\textrm{F}(t_{\gamma}(\ell);\ell)\leq\alpha\text{ for any }\ell\in[0,1].

\textrm{F}(t_{\gamma}(\ell);\ell)>\alpha

The proof is complete after combining all the demonstrated equivalences.

\displaystyle\bar{\textrm{F}}_{n+j}(t):=\frac{\hat{w}_{n+j}\cdot L_{n+j}\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}\cdot L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}}{1+\sum_{k\neq j}\ind\{s(X_{n+k})\leq t\}}\cdot\frac{m}{\hat{w}_{n+j}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}}.

By construction,

\displaystyle\bar{E}_{n+j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq\hat{t}_{n+j}\}}{\bar{F}_{n+j}(\hat{t}_{n+j})}\cdot\frac{m}{1+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq\hat{t}_{n+j}\}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq\hat{t}_{n+j}\}}{1+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\ind\{s(X_{n+\ell})\leq\hat{t}_{n+j}\}}\cdot\frac{m}{\alpha}.

(C.10)

Here the second inequality holds because
\bar{F}_{n+j}(\hat{t}_{n+j})\leq\alpha
since
\hat{t}_{n+j}
searches over the finite set
\mathcal{M}
.

By construction, and since
\sup_{i}|\hat{w}_{i}|\leq M
, it holds deterministically and uniformly over all

that

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}\cdot L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}}{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\ind\{s(X_{n+k})\leq t\}+\frac{1}{m}}\cdot\frac{1}{M+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}}.\leq\bar{\textrm{F}}_{n+j}(t)\leq\frac{M+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}\cdot L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}}{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\ind\{s(X_{n+k})\leq t\}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}}.

(C.11)

Now define

\displaystyle G(t):=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{w}(X)L\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}],\quad H_{Q}(t)=\mathbb{P}_{Q}(s(X)\leq t)=\mathbb{P}(s(X_{n+j})\leq t),

\displaystyle\bar{F}(t)=\frac{G(t)}{H_{Q}(t)\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{w}(X)]},\quad t^{*}=\sup\{t\colon\bar{F}(t)\leq\alpha\}.

The given convergence conditions imply

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}\cdot L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{w}(X_{i})\cdot L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}\bigg|\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{w}_{i}-\bar{w}(X_{i}))^{2}}=o_{P}(1)

and similarly
|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{w}(X)]|=o_{P}(1)
.
In addition,
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\ind\{s(X_{n+k})\leq t\}-H_{Q}(t)|=o_{P}(1)
due to the uniform law of large numbers or Lemma
E.1
. Therefore, combining these results with (
C.11
), and since
H_{Q}(t^{*})>0
, there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that for any
\epsilon\in(0,\delta)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq t^{*}-\epsilon,j\in[m]}\big|\bar{\textrm{F}}_{n+j}(t)-\bar{F}(t)\big|=o_{P}(1).

(C.12)

Recall that
\bar{F}(t)
is continuous at

, and for any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, there exists some

such that
\bar{F}(t_{\epsilon})<\alpha
. Thus, by (

) we know

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\inf_{j\in[m]}\hat{t}_{n+j}\geq t_{\epsilon}\Big)\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{j\in[m]}\bar{\mathrm{F}}_{n+j}(t_{\epsilon})\leq(\alpha+\bar{F}(t_{\epsilon}))/2\Big)\to 1

as

.
On the other hand, by the definition of

and the right-continuity of

, for any
\epsilon>0

\bar{\mathrm{F}}(t)>\alpha+\delta

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{j\in[m]}\hat{t}_{n+j}\leq t+\epsilon\Big)\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(\inf_{j\in[m]}\inf_{t^{\prime}\geq t+\epsilon}\bar{\mathrm{F}}_{n+j}(t+\epsilon)\geq\alpha+\delta/2\Big)\to 1

as
n,m\to\infty

\epsilon>0
, we know

\displaystyle\sup_{j\in[m]}|\hat{t}_{n+j}-t^{*}|=o_{P}(1).

(C.13)

For any
\epsilon>0
, we define the event

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}=\bigg\{\sup_{j\in[m]}|\hat{t}_{n+j}-t^{*}|>\epsilon\bigg\}

\displaystyle\cup\bigg\{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{n+j}\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t\}-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}]\Big|>\epsilon\bigg\}

\displaystyle\cup\bigg\{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t\}-\mathbb{P}_{Q}(s(X)\leq t)\Big|>\epsilon\bigg\}.

which satisfies

for any fixed

as

by (

.

By the definition of the eBH procedure (Theorem

), we know that
\mathcal{R}=\{j\in[m]\colon E_{n+j}\geq m/(\alpha\hat{\tau})\}
for

. Thus the SDR can be bounded as

\displaystyle{\textnormal{SDR}}_{n,m}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{n+j}\ind\{j\in\mathcal{R}\}}{1\vee\hat{\tau}}\ind_{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}\bigg]+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{n+j}\ind\{j\in\mathcal{R}\}}{1\vee\hat{\tau}}\ind_{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}^{c}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{n+j}\ind\{E_{n+j}\geq m/(\alpha\hat{\tau})\}}{1\vee\hat{\tau}}\ind_{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}^{c}}\bigg]+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{n+j}\ind\{\bar{E}_{n+j}\geq m/(\alpha\hat{\tau})\}}{1\vee\hat{\tau}}\ind_{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}^{c}}\bigg]+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[L_{n+j}\frac{\ind\{\frac{\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq\hat{t}_{n+j}\}}{1+\sum_{k\neq j}\ind\{s(X_{n+k})\leq\hat{t}_{n+j}\}}\geq\frac{1}{\hat{\tau}}\}}{1\vee\hat{\tau}}\ind_{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}^{c}}\bigg]+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq\hat{t}_{n+j}\}}{1\vee\sum_{k=1}^{m}\ind\big\{s(X_{n+k})\leq\hat{t}_{n+j}\}}\ind_{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}^{c}}\bigg]+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}),

where the second inequality uses

, the third inequality uses (

\displaystyle\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t^{*}-\epsilon\}}{1\vee\sum_{k=1}^{m}\ind\big\{s(X_{n+k})\leq t^{*}+\epsilon\}}\leq\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq\hat{t}_{n+j}\}}{1\vee\sum_{k=1}^{m}\ind\big\{s(X_{n+k})\leq\hat{t}_{n+j}\}}\leq\frac{L_{n+j}\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq t^{*}+\epsilon\}}{1\vee\sum_{k=1}^{m}\ind\big\{s(X_{n+k})\leq t^{*}-\epsilon\}},

(C.15)

With the similar arguments as in the proof of (

‣
C.3
) in Theorem

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{l}(X_{n+j})\ind\{s(X_{n+j})\leq{t}\}-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\bar{l}(X)\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}]\bigg|=o_{P}(1).

(C.16)

which, together with (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{w}(X)l(X)\ind\{s(X)\leq\hat{t}\}]=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[l(X)\ind\{s(X)\leq\hat{t}\}]+o_{P}(1),\quad\mathbb{E}[\bar{w}(X)]=1,

\epsilon_{n}=\sqrt{\log(2|\mathcal{G}|/\delta)/2n}
as the slack determined by Hoeffding’s inequality. As such,
\widehat{{\textnormal{MDR}}}(t)+\epsilon_{n}
is a uniform upper bound on
{\textnormal{MDR}}(t)
over all
t\in\mathcal{G}
with probability at least
1-\delta
. With
\hat{t}=\max\{t\in\mathcal{G}\colon\widehat{{\textnormal{MDR}}}(t)+\epsilon_{n}\leq\alpha\}
, we have the PAC-type guarantee:

\displaystyle{\textnormal{MDR}}(\hat{t})\leq\alpha\quad\text{with probability}\geq 1-\delta.

Similarly, the
Rademacher
approach bounds
\widehat{{\textnormal{MDR}}}(t)
over all
t\in[0,1]
by
\widehat{{\textnormal{MDR}}}(t)+2\widehat{\text{Rad}}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{calib}})+3\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)/2n}
. Here,
\widehat{\text{Rad}}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{calib}})
denotes the empirical Rademacher complexity of the function class
\{t\mapsto L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}\}
for all
(X_{i},L_{i})\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{calib}}
; it is evaluated by empirically sampling
k=100
Rademacher random variables. The slack term
3\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)/2n}
is added once to account for the estimation of the empirical MDR and twice for that of the empirical Rademacher complexity. With this uniform upper bound on
t\in[0,1]
, we set the grid to all predicted values
\mathcal{G}=\{s(X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
for tightness. It is straightforward to see that such approach also ensures above PAC-type guarantee.

For SDR control, the two variants are constructed similarly. Both variants bound the numerator
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(f,X,Y)\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}]
and denominator
\mathbb{P}(s(X)\leq t)
separately. For the
Hoeffding
variant, the numerator is upper bounded by

\displaystyle A_{h}(t):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}\log(4|\mathcal{G}|/\delta)}

and the denominator is lower bounded by

\displaystyle B_{h}(t):=\frac{1}{n}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}\log(4|\mathcal{G}|/\delta)}.

Setting
\mathcal{G}
to be a fixed evenly-spaced grid of size
|\mathcal{G}|=100
, above bounds hold uniformly over
t\in\mathcal{G}
with probability at least
1-\delta/2
. Therefore, with probability at least
1-\delta
,
\widehat{{\textnormal{SDR}}}^{+}(t):=A_{h}(t)/B_{h}(t)
if
B_{h}(t)>0
and
\infty
otherwise is an uniform upper bound on
{\textnormal{SDR}}^{*}(t)
. Now, for the
Rademacher
approach, we set
\mathcal{G}=\{s(X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and the upper and lower bounds are

\displaystyle A_{r}(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}+2\widehat{\text{Rad}}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{calib}})+3\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}\log(4/\delta)},

\displaystyle B_{r}(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}-2\widetilde{\text{Rad}}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{calib}})+3\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}\log(4/\delta)}

where
\widetilde{\text{Rad}}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{calib}})
denotes the empirical Rademacher complexity of the function class
\{t\mapsto\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}\}
. Again,
\widehat{{\textnormal{SDR}}}^{+}(t):=A_{r}(t)/B_{r}(t)
if
B_{r}(t)>0
and
\infty
otherwise would be a valid uniform upper bound on
{\textnormal{SDR}}^{*}(t)
. As constructed,
Hoeffding
and
Rademacher
variants guarantees
{\textnormal{SDR}}^{*}(\hat{t})\leq\alpha
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Appendix E
Auxiliary lemmas

Lemma E.1
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to[0,M]
be any fixed, bounded function, and
s\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a fixed function so that
s(X)
has no point mass for
X\sim Q
. Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be i.i.d. samples from
Q
and independent of
f
and
s
. Then there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}f(X_{i})\ind\{s(X_{i})\leq t\}-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f(X)\ind\{s(X)\leq t\}]\bigg|\Bigg]\leq\frac{CM}{\sqrt{m}}.

Proof of Lemma
E.1
.

Define

\displaystyle f_{t}(x):=f(x)\mathbf{1}\{s(x)\leq t\},\qquad\mathcal{F}:=\{f_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Consider the function class
\mathcal{H}:=\{h_{t}(x)=\mathbf{1}\{s(x)\leq t\}:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
which is well-known to be a VC class. Hence there exist constants
A,v<\infty
(e.g.,
A=\sqrt{2}
,
v=2
) such that the covering number obeys

N\big(\varepsilon,\mathcal{H},L_{2}(\mathbb{P})\big)\leq\Big(\frac{A}{\varepsilon}\Big)^{v},\qquad 0<\varepsilon<1.

Due to the boundedness of
f(\cdot)
, it is straightforward to see that

N\big(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},L_{2}(\mathbb{P})\big)\leq\Big(\frac{AM}{\varepsilon}\Big)^{v},\qquad 0<\varepsilon<1,

so
\mathcal{F}
is a VC-type class with envelope
F(x)\equiv M
.
By a standard maximal inequality for VC-type classes we obtain, for a universal constant
C_{0}>0
, that

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{F}}\big|\sqrt{m}(P_{m}-P)f\big|\Big]\leq C_{0}\|F\|_{L_{2}(Q)}=C_{0}M,

where
P_{m}(\tilde{f})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{f}(X_{i})
and
P(\tilde{f})=\mathbb{E}[f(X)]
.
Dividing by
\sqrt{m}
yields the displayed expectation bound.
∎
