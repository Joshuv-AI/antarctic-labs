Azithromycin versus doxycycline in hospitalised adult patients with community-acquired pneumonia treated with beta-lactams: protocol for a pragmatic randomised clinical trial
Authors: Aysun Tekin, Amos Lal, Autumn Brogan, Fernanda Bellolio, John C. O’Horo, Hemang Yadav, Phillip Schulte, Ognjen Gajic, Erin F. Barreto, Yewande E. Odeyemi, Amy L. Amsbaugh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCommunity-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is common and remains the leading infectious cause of death worldwide. Antibiotics remain the mainstay of treatment, but organisms are often not identified, necessitating empirical treatment. Current treatment guidelines recommend combination therapy with beta-lactams and macrolides or monotherapy with fluoroquinolones for non-severe CAP. However, rising antibiotic resistance and safety concerns have led to increased doxycycline use, despite limited evidence supporting its efficacy. Our goal is to conduct a pragmatic, open-label, randomised clinical trial comparing the effectiveness of azithromycin with that of doxycycline in combination with beta-lactam therapy in adult patients hospitalised for CAP.Methods and analysisPatients presenting to the emergency department or directly referred to the hospital with a clinical presentation concerning for CAP and need for hospitalisation are eligible. We plan to enrol 1120 adults hospitalised at six hospitals across four states, including rural, suburban and urban settings and academic and community hospitals. Randomisation will be 1:1 using an electronic health record (EHR)-embedded screening and randomisation tool triggered by the treating clinician’s orders for azithromycin or doxycycline. Outcomes will be ascertained pragmatically via EHR-based data retrieval and will not require additional study visits. Our primary outcome is days alive and out of the hospital through day 28. Secondary outcomes include oxygen-free days; a composite of a need for advanced respiratory support or in-hospital mortality with analyses of each component separately; an ordinal measure of clinical deterioration defined by a prespecified severity scale; and 60-day and 180-day mortality. Safety outcomes are the incidence of Clostridioides difficile infection and QT prolongation. Analyses will follow an intention-to-treat framework, with per-protocol analyses for sensitivity. The primary endpoint will be compared between groups using a stratified van Elteren test, with supportive analyses using proportional odds regression and analysis of covariance adjusted for key covariates.Ethics and disseminationThe Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board reviewed and approved this protocol with a waiver of written consent, requiring verbal consent to be obtained by the clinician initiating the antibiotics (IRB# 24–0 06 503). Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and international conference presentations.Trial registration numberNCT07164131.

Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is common,1–7 leading to approximately 1.3 million emergency visits and over US$10 billion in annual healthcare costs in the USA.8 9 It remains the leading infectious cause of death worldwide, with mortality ranging from 3% in outpatients to over 50% in patients admitted in intensive care units (ICUs).2–5 10–13 Early antibiotic administration remains the mainstay of treatment for bacterial pneumonia and sepsis from any source in hospitalised and critically ill patients, with proven mortality benefit.14 15

For patients hospitalised for CAP without risk factors for resistant pathogens, clinicians have traditionally relied on combination therapy with beta-lactam and a macrolide or fluoroquinolone monotherapy. However, concerns about fluoroquinolone safety and emerging resistance16 17 have reduced their use, leaving beta-lactam-based combinations as the mainstay.18 In this setting, beta-lactam plus doxycycline combination has gained popularity, despite the lack of randomised clinical trials comparing any doxycycline-based regimen. As a result, doxycycline is only conditionally recommended by the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) and American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines, particularly for patients with non-severe CAP,19 20 whereas recommendations from other international guidelines diverge.21

A systematic review of randomised controlled trials highlighted the evidence gap: most randomised trials comparing doxycycline with macrolides were conducted in the 1980s and 1990s, involved small sample sizes, and tested agents such as roxithromycin, spiramycin and erythromycin, which are not in routine use today; and were further limited by significant heterogeneity in antibiotic selection within the control group.22 More recent observational studies with direct comparison of azithromycin and doxycycline reported no clear differences in efficacy and safety outcomes23 24 but were subject to bias and residual confounding.

Scientific premise for the planned trial

The potential superiority of azithromycin over doxycycline in CAP treatment rests on several biologically plausible mechanisms. Although both antibiotics target ‘atypical’ organisms, azithromycin may provide superior coverage against Legionella spp.25 26 and exert immunomodulatory effects that could lead to improved outcomes.27–30 Conversely, doxycycline may reduce Clostridioides difficile toxin production, and limited data suggest lower frequency of Clostridioides difficile infection in those on doxycycline than in those on azithromycin.31

These biologically plausible differences suggest that azithromycin may confer advantages in pathogen coverage and host response modulation that could favour improved clinical recovery in patients hospitalised for CAP. Nonetheless, the extent to which these effects influence patient-centred outcomes remain unknown, and true clinical equipoise exists.

Preliminary data

To better understand prescribing practices, we reviewed 64 200 hospitalisations for CAP between 2015 and 2023 across six Mayo Clinic campuses, including both academic and community hospitals. Along with declining fluoroquinolone use, there was an increase in the proportion of patients receiving azithromycin (11.8% increase per year; 95% CI 11.3% to 12.4%, p<0.001) and doxycycline (19.6% increase per year; 95% CI 18.8% to 20.4%, p<0.001) prescriptions, with over 50% of admissions in recent years receiving one of those two antibiotics (figure 1).

Using a subset of this cohort, we compared outcomes of adult patients hospitalised for CAP who received either azithromycin or doxycycline for its treatment. After direct matching on the presence of congestive heart failure, coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, HIV infection, recent immunosuppressant use and pneumonia severity index class to minimise confounding (2671 pairs), results revealed that azithromycin was associated with lower mortality and more hospital-free days compared with doxycycline. Full results and methodology of this study are described elsewhere.32 In a complementary evaluation of the same primary cohort restricted to patients with non-severe CAP treated with azithromycin or doxycycline (n=2631), in-hospital mortality was approximately 1.9% (unpublished data). However, given the marginal effect size and the inherent limitations of an observational study design with regard to unmeasured confounders, substantial uncertainty remains. These data highlight that the optimal empirical treatment strategy for CAP is unknown, but it is possible that one treatment option is superior. If our findings demonstrate that the two medications are equivalent, they could help inform prescribing practices and contribute to clinical practice guidelines beyond the current considerations of local resistance patterns or cases in which macrolides are contraindicated.

Mayo Clinic Acute Care Clinical Trials Platform

The Mayo Clinic Acute Care Clinical Platform has been developed as an electronic surveillance system to enhance the enrolment of eligible patients and eliminate disparities in clinical trials across Mayo Clinic hospitals located in four US states: Arizona, Florida, Minnesota and Wisconsin. The trial-specific tailored screening mechanism is adapted from the Acute Care Multi Patient viewer (AMP) and electronic health record (EHR) infrastructure, augmenting time-sensitive trials. In addition to automated EHR screening, virtual e-consenting and automated data download to Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) features enable a more efficient clinical trial workflow. Objectives

The objective of this study is to conduct a pragmatic, open-label, randomised clinical trial evaluating the effectiveness of azithromycin versus doxycycline in combination with beta-lactam therapy in patients hospitalised for CAP. We hypothesise that azithromycin is more effective in improving patient-centred outcomes than doxycycline.

Study design and setting

This pragmatic, randomised, open-label, parallel-group, superiority clinical trial will evaluate the effectiveness of azithromycin versus doxycycline in combination with beta-lactam therapy in patients hospitalised for CAP. Participants will be randomised 1:1 (figure 2). Enrolment began on 15 September 2025 at the Rochester Hospital only for the initial feasibility assessment. The study is expected to be completed by September 2031.

We will enrol patients hospitalised with CAP from six Mayo Clinic hospitals across four states (Rochester, MN; Jacksonville, FL; Phoenix, AZ; Austin, MN; Mankato, MN; La Crosse, WI), enhancing the generalisability of the findings.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and community representatives, engaged through the Mayo Clinic Centre of Health Equity and Community Engagement Research and its advisory board, contributed to shaping the research question and trial design. Input from survivors of critical illness and patient advocates informed outcome selection and ensured relevance to patient priorities. During the trial, they will remain involved in communication strategies, recruitment support, and project updates. Following study completion, they will help guide dissemination of findings to patients, families and community groups.

Study population

Patients admitted to the hospital with CAP via the emergency department (ED) or as a direct transfer will be eligible for the study at the time of the decision to initiate an antibiotic therapy regimen that includes either azithromycin or doxycycline within 12 hours of admission. CAP will be diagnosed clinically by the treating teams as part of the routine care. The proposed eligibility time would help exclude antibiotic coverage for hospital-acquired pneumonia.

In keeping with a pragmatic clinical trial design based on the PRagmatic-Explanatory Continuum Indicator Summary (PRECIS) 2 tool, inclusion criteria have been minimised and represent typical patients admitted for treatment of CAP.33

Informed consent

Verbal informed consent, under a waiver of written documentation, will be obtained by the treating ED clinician by using an IRB-approved, standardised oral consent script (online supplemental file 3). Oral consent will be documented using the OurPractice Advisory tool, and participants will be informed by the treating clinician prior to randomisation that participation is voluntary and will be given the option to opt out. Additionally, participants will be provided with a trial information sheet or brochure by a study team member. Within 72 hours (goal within 12 hours), the research team will re-approach participants to reconfirm consent and provide the opportunity to withdraw and continue routine care. Patients who decline participation will be documented within the enrolment workflow.

Screening and enrolment

Screening and enrolment will be performed by utilising a pop-up alert within the EHR triggered by an order for either doxycycline or azithromycin. Treating clinicians will have the option to defer the alert or proceed with acknowledgements. The alerting criteria will include adult patients admitted from the ED within 12 hours who have no documented allergies to either doxycycline or azithromycin and are not taking either medication at home. Treating clinicians will confirm key eligibility and exclusion criteria and will be prompted to order the assigned randomised intervention. Duration of antibiotics will remain at clinician’s discretion (5–7 days per guidelines). Figure 3 and 4 show examples of patients randomised to receive doxycycline and azithromycin with order prompts, respectively. Following enrolment, a second pop-up alert will appear with any subsequent order for azithromycin or doxycycline, notifying clinicians of the assigned intervention. This is particularly important during transitions of care, including transfers from the ED to inpatient units or between hospitals within the health system (figure 5). A report for the alerts will be monitored daily by the principal investigator and study coordinators at each participating hospital to ensure appropriate enrolment and randomisation. This process is supported by the Mayo Clinic Acute Care Clinical Trials Platform, and the integrated informatics infrastructure is expected to ensure timely recruitment and attainment of the target sample size.

Randomisation

Study group assignments will be generated by computerised randomisation within the EHR in a 1:1 ratio of azithromycin to doxycycline. Patients, clinicians, pharmacists and investigators are unblinded to study group assignments.

Study interventions

We will evaluate the effectiveness of azithromycin versus doxycycline in combination with beta-lactam therapy in patients hospitalised for CAP. Either parenteral or enteral formulations of both antibiotics are acceptable at the discretion of the treating physician.

Concomitant care

All concomitant care and co-interventions, including administration of corticosteroids, will be at the treating clinical team’s discretion and will be recorded. No treatments other than the randomised study antibiotic are restricted or prohibited. Co-enrolment in other critical care trials will be allowed unless deemed clinically unwarranted.

Outcomes

The outcomes of interest are outlined in table 2. The primary outcome is days alive and out of hospital in the 28 days following randomisation. Days alive and out of hospital has been shown to be a meaningful and pragmatic patient-centred outcome for clinical trials in critically ill patients.36 37 Based on input from patient and caregiver stakeholders, this measure reflects a desire to avoid emergency room visits and hospitalisations. This outcome has been described as responsive to individual patient preferences and valued by patients and family, as most patients would prefer spending more days outside of the hospital and potentially increase the ability to return to familiar environment and loved ones. Importantly, even a 1-day difference between intervention groups is considered clinically meaningful by our patient and family partners. They emphasised that a single additional day at home represents a real improvement in well-being and reduced burden. From a healthcare perspective, 1 day less in the hospital translates into measurable cost savings and improved capacity, making this threshold both patient-centred and pragmatically relevant. Further, it is easily measured as an outcome and collected as part of routine care. Participants who die at any time within 28 days are assigned a value of zero. Secondary outcomes include oxygen-free days, a composite of the need for advanced respiratory support or in-hospital mortality (with each component analysed separately), an ordinal measure of clinical deterioration defined by a prespecified severity scale and 60-day and 180-day mortality. All outcomes will be ascertained pragmatically via EHR-based data retrieval after study completion and will not require additional study visits. All clinical end points will be adjudicated by research coordinators blinded to randomised treatment assignment.

Analysis plan

The detailed analysis plan is reported in online supplemental file 2.

Statistical analysis

Analyses will follow both intention-to-treat (ITT, ie, all randomised participants included and analysed according to randomised arm, even if the randomisation-determined medication order is deferred or not placed) and per-protocol frameworks (ie, only participants who completed the study without major protocol deviation).

Days alive and out of hospital will be compared between randomised treatment arms using a van Elteren test with stratification by hospital. The secondary outcome of oxygen-free days will be analysed similarly. Other secondary endpoints, which include a composite of the need for advanced respiratory support along with its components and/or in-hospital mortality and 180-day mortality, will be evaluated using a Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test with stratification by hospital. Secondary outcomes will be tested without adjustment for multiple testing.

Pre-specified subgroup and sensitivity analyses are detailed in the statistical analysis plan (online supplemental file 2).

Sample size and power

This study is powered to detect a clinically meaningful difference of +1 day increase in median days alive and out of hospital through day 28 after randomisation. As such, a total sample size of 1120 participants (560 per arm) provides 90% power to detect this difference attributable to azithromycin as compared with doxycycline based on our simulation settings using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test with a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. Power and sample size were determined by simulating the outcome under a mixture distribution for each arm, with the combined distribution approximating historical data on patients admitted for CAP. Figure 6 shows the assumed distribution under each arm for our simulation, combining mortality with binary point mass at 0 days and days simulated under a truncated normal distribution. Given the inpatient population at randomisation and pragmatic approach to outcome ascertainment, we anticipate no missing data for evaluation of days alive and out of hospital. Conservatively, withdrawal of consent of 5% retains 89% power.

Simulation settings for power and sample size calculation.

For the secondary outcome, the composite need for advanced respiratory support or in-hospital mortality, we anticipate that up to 30% of patients will experience the event under doxycycline. The sample size of 560 per arm provides 93% power to detect a relative risk 0.70 (21% among azithromycin) using a test of proportions with a two-sided alpha level of 0.05.

Missing data

The primary analysis assumes missing data are missing completely at random, though expected to be very rare occurring only when there is withdrawal of consent and will use complete case analysis. If proportions of missing data are >5%, sensitivity analyses may include a worst-case imputation, multiple imputation under a missing at random assumption, and multiple imputation pattern mixture models under missing not at random assumption.

Interim analysis

Pre-planned interim analysis will occur at 25% and 50% completion of the primary outcome, days alive and out of hospital. The primary purpose of the interim analysis is to evaluate outcome (days alive and out of hospital, oxygen-free days, advanced respiratory support and mortality), data collection and data transfer procedures. The study will not be stopped or halted early for efficacy reasons. A Haybittle–Peto alpha spending boundary will be used, with negligible impact to the power calculation given previously.

Intercurrent events

Cross-over and treatment non-compliance will be recorded in the randomisation tool embedded in the EHR. Other intercurrent events will be captured in the EHR. Estimands reflecting analysis in the presence of intercurrent events, including those under per-protocol and as-treated principles, will be specified prior to completion of study enrolment.

Data management

Planned automated data extraction from the EHR into a REDCap database supported by the Mayo Clinic will occur for interim analyses and at the end of the study. Personal information about potential and enrolled participants will be obtained only as necessary for eligibility screening, enrolment and follow-up. Identifiable information will be stored securely within REDCap, a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act−compliant, web-based application designed exclusively to support data capture for research studies and institutional projects.38 Access will be restricted to authorised study personnel through role-based permissions. Data will be de-identified prior to analysis and dissemination, and no personal identifiers will be shared outside the clinical care or study team. Confidentiality safeguards, including secure data transfer procedures and audit trails, will be maintained throughout the study and after its completion.

Ethics and dissemination

This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board, with a waiver of written informed consent and the requirement of verbal consent by the ED or hospital clinician (IRB# 24–0 06 503). Timely initiation of antibiotics is essential in CAP, and written consent was deemed impractical because it could introduce delays that worsen outcomes. In our retrospective data, the median time from admission to antibiotic administration was 5 hours (IQR 2–14). Administration of either doxycycline or azithromycin within 12 hours was associated with a 36% reduction in odds of mortality (OR 0.64; 95% CI 0.53 to 0.78; p<0.001), highlighting the clinical importance of avoiding even moderate delays.

The Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board therefore approved a two-stage verbal consent process. Initial consent is obtained and documented by the ED clinician, or hospital clinician if directly admitted to the medical floor or critical care unit, using the pop-up alert system prior to randomisation, followed by reconfirmation by the research team within 72 hours.

As a requirement for enrolment, the treating clinician must have independently decided that either azithromycin or doxycycline would be a safe and reasonable option for the patient as part of routine care. If this criterion is not met, the patient is excluded. Therefore, randomisation between the two regimens introduces no more than minimal incremental risk, since both are widely used and clinically appropriate. This strategy maintains ethical oversight, protects patient safety and ensures that antibiotics are not delayed—consistent with best practices for minimal-risk pragmatic trials in emergency settings.

Any important protocol modifications (eg, changes to eligibility criteria, outcomes or analyses) will be submitted for approval by the Institutional Review Board prior to implementation, updated in the trial registry and communicated to investigators, study staff and participants as appropriate.

Because this is a minimal-risk pragmatic trial, a formal independent Data Safety Monitoring Board is not required. Instead, a Data Safety Monitoring Plan has been instituted. Safety oversight will be carried out by investigators and study coordinators at each participating hospital, who will be readily available to the clinical team via pager, phone, email or EHR chat features for any questions or adverse event concerns. Trial conduct and adverse events will be monitored continuously, with formal interim safety analyses as described above. Escalation or change in antibiotic regimen remains at the discretion of the treating clinicians, and such events will be tracked closely by the study team as part of ongoing monitoring procedures.

Findings will be disseminated through publications in peer-reviewed journals and presentations at international and relevant society conferences. De-identified individual participant data and the accompanying data dictionary will be made available in a secure, controlled-access data repository within 1 year of publication. Access will be granted to qualified investigators under a data use agreement. Statistical code (R scripts) and supporting documentation will be shared via GitHub to enable transparency and reproducibility.
