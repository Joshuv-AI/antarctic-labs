Throughput-Optimal Multiresource-Job Scheduling with Continuous Requirement Distribution
Heyuan Yao \orcidlogo, Willow Kowalik, Izzy Grosof \orcidlogo

Abstract
Modern computing systems process jobs with resource requirements such as CPU and memory, which are described by multiresource jobs (MRJ) queueing models. In practice, job resource requirements are spread out over so many values, that it is rare to see the same value twice. This pattern is best modeled by a continuous distribution of requirement values. However, the existing theoretical work on stability or throughput-optimality focuses on queueing models with class-based resource requirements. In class-based models, the number of distinct resource requirements must be small to demonstrate strong empirical performance, making them a poor match for these practical systems. We introduce the first throughput-optimal family of scheduling policies for the continuous MRJ model, with both preemptive and nonpreemptive variants. We further introduce several efficient policy families, which remain throughput-optimal while considerably improving computational efficiency, under some distributional assumptions. We use a discretization approach, where we choose the discretization granularity based on the system load and the distribution of resource requirements. We validate the real-world applicability of our policies by comparing them against existing index-based policies on parametrized distributions and on datacenter trace data from the Google Borg scheduler, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance.

Introduction

Modern computing systems, such as cloud platforms, datacenters, and personal machines, typically possess multiple kinds of computational resources (e.g., CPU, GPU, memory). These systems can be modeled as queues where jobs require certain amounts of these resources, in order to be served. We refer to these jobs as multiresource jobs.

For instance, MaxWeight weights jobs by how many jobs of identical resource requirements are present. The larger the number of job classes, the less the empirical performance corresponds to the theoretical results. In particular, the number of jobs present must be of at least the order of the number of job classes for empirical performance to correspond to the theoretical results. These queue lengths are too large for practical purposes. In datacenter scheduling traces, hundreds of thousands of unique resource requirements are commonly seen

In real-world scheduling environments, for example, the first million jobs of the Borg trace

, in which the distribution of the resource requirement is continuous. While real systems do not literally have continuous resource requirements, the continuous MRJ model captures the behavior of rarely repeating resource requirements. In this model, we ask a question:

Does there exist a throughput-optimal family of scheduling policies in the continuous MRJ setting?

We resolve this question by developing the first throughput-optimal family of scheduling policies, the
{\vec{K}}
-discretized MaxWeight (
{\vec{K}}
-MW) family.
{\vec{K}}

Many applications involve nonpreemptible jobs or require substantial preemption overhead. We therefore introduce the first nonpreemptive throughput-optimal family of scheduling policies:
{\vec{K}}

In addition, computational efficiency is essential for both the online optimization in MaxWeight-type policies and the offline precomputation in nMSR-type policies. For instance, when using standard class-based MaxWeight in systems with numerous classes and small expected service durations, the exponentially large number of possible service options may cause the system to spend more time updating service options than actually serving jobs. To achieve computational efficiency, we must avoid handling an extremely large candidate set. We introduce a new group of policies that restrict attention to smaller candidate sets, where we only consider a small fraction of the possible service options, during MaxWeight and nMSR’s optimization steps. We call these smaller candidate sets “efficient sets”.

We start by considering only the service options that form extreme vertices of the convex hull of the set of all service options. By doing so, for example, we reduce the candidate set by around
200\times
when
K=60
in the single-resource setting. Next, in the settings with symmetrical resource requirement distribution, our 2-Job efficient set reduces the number of service options to a quantity proportional to the number of discretization classes. In the single-resource setting with decreasing requirement density, our 2-Bucket efficient set likewise considers a number of service options proportional to the number of discretization classes. Our policies built on these three efficient sets are each throughput-optimal under corresponding distributional assumptions on the resource requirements.

We use stimulation to extensively verify the performance, both throughput and mean response time, in both parameterized settings and a public Borg trace. Our simulation results verify the throughput optimality of the
K

K

Moreover, both on these distributions and in experiments based on job requirements extracted from the Google Borg trace, our theoretically-motivated policies outperform in mean response time existing index-based policies: FCFS, First-Fit, Best-Fit, and Least-Server-First (LSF). Note that LSF is equivalent to naive MaxWeight in the continuous MSJ setting, because every job in its own class has equal weight. Naive MaxWeight then always serves most jobs, and packs the jobs in increasing order of their resource requirement.

Prior Work

In this section, we review real-world scheduling practices in datacenters and supercomputers, as well as the theoretical development of scheduling policies for multiresource-job systems.

2.1
Scheduling in Large-Scale Computing Settings

Traditional one-server-per-job queueing systems are not suitable for modeling many modern computing environments. A more accurate representation is provided by the multiserver-job (MSJ) system, where a job requires some amount of a single resource, and the multiresource-job (MRJ) system, where a job requires some amount of multiple resources. LLM handling infrastructure, such as within Microsoft Azure
Patel et al. 2024
, handles inference tasks, which vary by having a different number of tokens in different requests, corresponding to an MSJ model. The number of tokens implicitly corresponds to GPU and memory requirements.

Similarly, traditional datacenters process jobs that use varying amounts of resources in multiple categories, such as CPU, memory, and more, corresponding to an MRJ model. These multiresource requirements are present at the Google Borg
Reiss et al. 2012
;
Verma et al. 2015
;
Tirmazi et al. 2020
;
Wilkes 2020a
;
Wilkes 2020b
;
Wilkes 2011
;

and Google Omega
Schwarzkopf et al. 2013

Guo et al. 2019
;
Li and Hu 2019
;
Wu et al. 2019
. Finally, high-performance computing jobs submitted to supercomputers require a pre-specified number of nodes, for instance, at Argonne’s Aurora
Allen et al. 2025
and Oak Ridge’s Frontier
Budiardja et al. 2023
;
Atchley et al. 2023
supercomputers, corresponding to an MSJ model.

Furthermore, the distribution of these resource requirements has extremely large support, subdivided smaller than any useful measure of precision. For instance, the traces available from Google Borg
Wilkes 2020a
;
Wilkes 2020b
;
Tirmazi et al. 2020
demonstrate that CPU and memory requests are specified as floats with at least 13 digits of precision, and have nearly no repeated pairs: around 95% unique pairs over the first 1 million requirements. The RAM usage in Google Omega is recorded in bytes and is therefore specified down to the individual byte, e.g., 1074000000, 82587)
Schwarzkopf et al. 2013

2339

623

In all of these scenarios, class-based scheduling, where the “class” refers to a set of jobs with identical resource requirements, becomes unsuitable, as two jobs with only minor differences in some resource requirements would be assigned to distinct classes. This observation motivates the need to consider scheduling policies under the settings where resource requirements are continuous or of large support.

2.2
Multiserver-job and Multiresource-job Scheduling

We now review prior work on scheduling policies for the MSJ and MRJ settings. Some policies are designed primarily for the MSJ system and do not extend to the MRJ system.

The policies proposed in prior work can be broadly divided into two categories: index-based policies and class-based policies. Specifically, a job class refers to a set of jobs with identical resource requirements. Class-based policies schedule based on the number of jobs in each class that are present in the system at a given time. In other words, class-based policies rely on job-type information when making scheduling decisions. This implicitly assumes that job classes are discrete and finite, and that the number of possible classes is smaller than the number of jobs typically present in the system, so that the queue-length information for each class is meaningful. In contrast, index-based policies do not classify jobs into finitely many types. Instead, an index-based policy orders the jobs in the system according to a certain rule, iterates over all jobs or a subset of jobs, and decides whether to serve each job in turn.

We now introduce some classical index-based policies. As a representative index-based policy, First-Come-First-Served (FCFS) has been studied since the 1980s. Stability and mean response time analyses in the two-server setting were conducted in
Green 1980
;
Brill and Green 1984
. In the integer-valued requirement setting, the stability region of FCFS was characterized in
Rumyantsev and Morozov 2017
, and the first mean response time analysis was conducted in
Grosof et al. 2023
. Other well-known index-based policies include First-Fit, Best-Fit, and Least-Server-First (LSF), each of which uses Backfilling to greedily pack jobs according to some scanning order. We elaborate on the definitions of these three policies in Appendix
9
. First-Fit and Best-Fit are throughput-optimal if the requirement distribution is symmetric
Coffman and Stolyar 2001
. However, they generally fail to be throughput-optimal in more general settings
Maguluri et al. 2012
.
Hong and Wang 2022
studied the mean response time of LSF in a low-load scaling regime, showing that LSF achieves the same asymptotic growth rate as the optimal mean response time bound. However, LSF is generally not throughput-optimal because it prioritizes small jobs and fails to fully utilize system capacity.
ServerFilling
Grosof et al. 2022a
and ServerFilling-SRPT
Grosof et al. 2022b
are recently introduced near-index policies, with strong mean response bounds in the power-of-two MSJ requirement setting. Although most of these index-based policies can naturally be applied to the continuous MSJ or MRJ framework, their theoretical analysis has focused only on discrete settings, where job requirements have finite support. Moreover, none of them is throughput-optimal in the general resource requirement distribution setting.

We next move to some well-known class-based policies. One of the earliest preemptive class-based policies in the literature is MaxWeight
Tassiulas and Ephremides 1993
;
Stolyar 2004
, which is also the earliest policy proven to be throughput-optimal in the MRJ setting
Maguluri et al. 2012
. Despite its throughput-optimality and low empirical mean response time, MaxWeight repeatedly solves an online optimization problem and chooses service options whenever the system state changes, leading to frequent preemption and computational inefficiency. The first nonpreemptive policy proven throughput-optimal in the MRJ setting, Randomized Multi-Resource Scheduling (RMS, also known as Randomized-Timer)
Psychas and Ghaderi 2018
, addresses these issues but often results in a higher empirical mean response time than MaxWeight. A family of policies called Markovian Service Rate (MSR) was recently introduced in
Chen et al. 2025
. MSR can accommodate both preemptive and nonpreemptive settings and is throughput-optimal with bounded mean response time.
Chen et al. 2025
showed that nonpreemptive MSR (nMSR) achieves better empirical mean response times than RMS, especially under high load. However, nMSR has an issue: it ignores the queue-length state when switching service options, resulting in still subpar mean response time. To reduce the mean response time of nMSR in the MSJ setting,
Chen et al. 2026
proposed Most Servers First with Quickswap (MSFQ). MSFQ is throughput-optimal in the one-or-all case and empirically achieves better mean response time and weighted mean response time than nMSR in the general integer-valued resource requirement case. Many class-based policies achieve throughput-optimality, in contrast to index-based policies.

Class-based policies are not immediately applicable to the continuous MSJ or MRJ settings, because job requirements are continuously distributed, and the system almost never contains multiple jobs with exactly identical requirements at any given time. We will build on existing class-based policies (MaxWeight and nMSR) by using a discretization scheme, to achieve throughput-optimality in the continuous setting. For a boost to empirical performance, we also augment our discretization-based policies with an index-based Backfilling approach.

3.2
Multiresource-Job Queueing System with Continuous Resource Requirements

We study a model where a server has
d
distinct resources available, and each job requires some amount of each resource. Without loss of generality, we normalize the server’s available capacity for each resource to be
1
. A set of jobs can be served simultaneously whenever, for each resource type, the total requirement of these jobs does not exceed the capacity
1
.

Job arrivals follow a Poisson arrival process, with arrival rate
\lambda
. Resource requirements are sampled i.i.d. from the underlying distribution with random variable
V
, which takes values in
(0,1]^{d}
. In addition to a resource requirement, each job has a duration demand, sampled i.i.d. from a distribution with random variable
D
. The states of the system are lists of (requirement, duration) pairs, ordered according to their arrival times. A scheduling policy selects a set of jobs to be served simultaneously, and is assumed to be a Markovian policy, which depends only on the current state. We consider both the preempt-resume and nonpreemptive settings. When we refer to a queueing system as stable, formally we mean that the empty state is positive recurrent.

In this paper, we do not assume the resource is pre-discretized. This is in contrast to prior work, which typically assumes the job resource requirements distribution is discrete with finite support. Our assumption is more useful for real-world cloud and cluster scheduling. Specifically, resource requirement distribution
V
is characterized by the probability density function (p.d.f.)
f_{V}:(0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. For simplicity, we focus on the case where the duration
D
follows the exponential distribution
Exp(1)
, and the scheduling policy does not observe the remaining duration demand. However, we believe our policy should perform well under the general duration settings.

Under these assumptions, the state space simplifies to the set of current resource requirements, denoted by

\mathcal{S}:=\left\{x=\left(\vec{v}_{n}\right)_{1\leq n\leq N}\subseteq(0,1]^{d}:N\in\mathbb{N}\right\},

where the smaller index
n
means an earlier arrival time. A Markovian policy can then be defined as follows.

In the single-resource setting, with a chosen discretization parameter

One of our main focuses is to show that the

-MW can also stabilize the system.

3.3.4
{\vec{K}}
-Discretized Nonpreemptive Markovian Service Rate

In addition to the above preemptive policies, we provide nonpreemptive policies to schedule jobs with continuous resource requirements. This family of policies is built from the nonpreemptive Markovian Service Rate (nMSR) policy

as a modulating process. The state space of

Chen et al. 2025

In our setting, where the resource requirement distribution is continuous, we again use
{\vec{K}}
-discretization as introduced in
Section

3.3.1
, and consider the set of all schedulable discretized service options

|\Gamma_{\vec{K}}|
service options in
C_{\vec{K}}
to form the state space of the modulating process
\{M(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
. Then nMSR can be generalized to the continuous setting, called
{\vec{K}}
-discretized nMSR
(
{\vec{K}}
-nMSR). We will show in
Theorem

2
that
{\vec{K}}
-nMSR is throughput-optimal, given that the p.d.f. of the resource requirement is Lipschitz continuous.

To reduce the complexity of the offline computation in selecting the modulating process, the efficient set setting can be extended to the nonpreemptive case. Specifically, after

3.4
Discretization-Based Policies with Backfilling

To fully utilize the available capacity, Backfilling can be applied for each preemptive discretization-based policy. Backfilling works the same way as the filling rule of First-Fit. Once the underlying policy selects a candidate service option, the remaining unused resources may still allow some jobs in the system to be packed. The server then scans the queue in arrival order and packs jobs by the following greedy rule. The server packs a scanned job and serves it together with the jobs already selected, whenever the resource requirements of the scanned job are at most the remaining resources of the server. For brevity, we refer to
{\vec{K}}
-discretized MaxWeight with Backfilling as
{\vec{K}}
-MW-B. We note that
{\vec{K}}
-MW-B or
{\vec{K}}
-EMW-B are not purely discretization-based policies.

Lemma

3
states that the same type of proof of stability for
{\vec{K}}
-MW (or
{\vec{K}}
-EMW) also guarantees the stability of
{\vec{K}}
-MW-B (or
{\vec{K}}
-EMW-B). In addition, adopting Backfilling improves performance without significantly worsening computation efficiency, which is verified in
Section

6
.

Lemma 1
.

In the continuous MRJ setting, for a fixed
{\vec{K}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, the
{\vec{K}}
-MW policy is stable if there exists a service option distribution
\beta_{\vec{K}}
such that its corresponding service measure
\eta_{\vec{K}}^{S}
(see
Definition

5
), satisfies the following discrete measure dominance condition

\eta_{\vec{K}}^{S}(\vec{i})>\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}(\vec{i}),\quad\forall\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}.

(17)

Moreover, the
{\vec{K}}
-EMW policy with efficient set
E_{\vec{K}}:=\{M_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
is stable if the condition (
17
) holds, and the support of
\beta_{\vec{K}}
is a subset of
E_{\vec{K}}
.

Proof.

Given that the measure dominance condition (
17
) holds, we will use the Foster-Lyapunov Theorem
Meyn and Tweedie 1993a
to prove the stability of
\{q(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
, the CTMC defining the
{\vec{K}}
-discretized system in
Section

3.3.1
. In this context, we define the Lyapunov function
L:\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

L(q)=\left\|q\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}q_{\vec{i}}^{2}.

(18)

We let
\Delta_{M}\circ L(q)
denote the drift (
4
) of
L(q)
under our
K
-EMW policy for some efficient set, which also applies to the full
K
-MW. The Foster-Lyapunov Theorem states that the CTMC
\{q(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is positive recurrent if there exist constants
c>0
,
b>0
and a finite set
C\subset\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}
such that

\Delta_{M}\circ L(q)\leq-c+b\mathds{1}{\{q\in C\}},\quad\text{for all }q\in\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}.

(19)

We define the random vector
A(t):=(A_{\vec{i}}(t))\in\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}
, where for each job type
{\vec{i}}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}
, the entry
A_{\vec{i}}(t)
denotes the numbers of type-
\vec{i}
jobs arriving in the time period
(0,t]
. We define the potential completion
S(t)\in\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}
, and the realized completion
S(t)\in\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}
similarly, such that
\tilde{S}_{\vec{i}}(t)
and
{S}_{\vec{i}}(t)
denote the number of type-
{\vec{i}}
jobs our policy attempts to complete and actually completes, respectively.

We now clarify the distinction between
\tilde{S}_{\vec{i}}(t)
and
S_{\vec{i}}(t)
. During a time period with service option
M
that attempts to serve
M^{({\vec{i}})}
type-
{\vec{i}}
jobs, the potential completion process of job type
{\vec{i}}
is a Poisson process
PP(M^{({\vec{i}})})
. However, the system may have less than
M^{({\vec{i}})}
type-
{\vec{i}}
jobs during this period, resulting in the realized completions
\tilde{S}_{\vec{i}}(t)
being less than the potential completions
S_{\vec{i}}(t)
. Hence, we note that
\tilde{S}_{\vec{i}}(t)\leq S_{\vec{i}}(t)
because some potential service may go unused.

We now upper bound the drift
\Delta_{M}\circ L
of our Lyapunov function. Given any
q\in\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{M}\circ L(q)=\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}\left[V(q(t))-V(q(0))\,|\,q(0)=q\right]

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\|q(0)+A(t)-\tilde{S}(t)\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|q(0)\right\|_{2}^{2}\,\bigg|\,q(0)=q\Big]

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}{\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\|A(t)-\tilde{S}(t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\,\Big|\,q(0)=q\Big]}+2\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}{\mathbb{E}\Big[\langle q(0),A(t)-\tilde{S}(t)\rangle\,\Big|\,q(0)=q\Big]}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\bigg\{\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\|A(t)-\tilde{S}(t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\,\Big|\,q(0)=q\Big]}_{(I)}+2\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\Big[\langle q(0),A(t)-S(t)\rangle\,\Big|\,q(0)=q\Big]}_{(II)}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+2\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\Big[\langle q(0),S(t)-\tilde{S}(t)\rangle\,\Big|\,q(0)=q\Big]}_{(III)}\bigg\}

(20)

We now upper bound
(I)
,
(II)
, and
(III)
. For
(I)
, by letting
K_{m}:=\min\{K_{1},...,K_{d}\}
, we have that

\displaystyle(I)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum\limits_{\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}\Big(A_{\vec{i}}(t)-\tilde{S}_{\vec{i}}(t)\Big)^{2}\,\Big|\,q(0)=q\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}\mathbb{E}A_{\vec{i}}^{2}(t)+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}\tilde{S}_{\vec{i}}^{2}(t)\Big|\,q(0)=q\Big]-2\sum_{\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}\mathbb{E}A_{\vec{i}}(t)\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\tilde{S}_{\vec{i}}(t)\Big|\,q(0)=q\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}A_{\vec{i}}(t)\Big)^{2}\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}\tilde{S}_{\vec{i}}(t)\Big)^{2}\Big|\,q(0)=q\Big]

(21)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}A_{\vec{i}}(t)\Big)^{2}\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}S_{\vec{i}}(t)\Big)^{2}\Big|\,q(0)=q\Big]

(22)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda t+\lambda^{2}t^{2}+K_{m}t+K_{m}^{2}t^{2}.

(23)

where

•

(
21
) is due to
A_{\vec{i}}(t)
and
\tilde{S}_{\vec{i}}(t)
are nonnegative for all
\vec{i}
,

•

(
22
) is due to
\tilde{S}(t)\preceq S(t)
, and

•

(
23
) is due to
\sum_{\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}A_{\vec{i}}(t)\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda t)
and
\sum_{\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}S_{\vec{i}}(t)
is stochastically dominated by
\text{Poisson}(K_{m}t)
, because the server can serve at most
K_{m}
jobs simultaneously, which in turn holds because the minimum resource
m
requirement is
1/K_{m}
.

Hence,
\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{(I)}{t}\leq\lambda+K_{m}
. Note that this bound holds for any
{\vec{K}}
-discretized policy.

For
(II)
, we primarily focus on
\vec{K}
-EMW policy (
13
) with some efficient set
E_{\vec{K}}
.
{\vec{K}}
-MW can be treated as a special case where the efficient set
E_{\vec{K}}=C_{\vec{K}}
.

The corresponding service process is approximated by an entry-wise independent Poisson distribution over short time intervals, such that
S^{EMW}(t)\sim\text{Poisson}(M^{MW\text{-}E_{\vec{K}}}(0)t)+\epsilon_{t}
, where
\epsilon_{t}
satisfies
\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\mathbb{P}(\left\|\frac{\epsilon_{t}}{t}\right\|_{2}>\delta)=0
for any
\delta>0
. In addition, the discrete measure dominance condition (
17
) means that there exist some positive
\delta
such that for all
{\vec{i}}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}
,

\eta^{S}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})=\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\beta_{j}M_{j}^{(\vec{i})}\geq(1+\delta)\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}=(1+\delta)\Lambda_{\vec{K}}(\vec{i}).

(24)

Therefore, term (II) has the following upper bound:

\displaystyle(II)

\displaystyle=\langle q\,,\,\Lambda_{\vec{K}}t-M^{MW\text{-}E_{\vec{K}}}(0)t+o(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle q\,,\,\Lambda_{\vec{K}}t+o(t)\rangle-\langle q\,,\,t\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}\beta_{j}M^{MW\text{-}E_{\vec{K}}}(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle q\,,\,\Lambda_{\vec{K}}t+o(t)\rangle-t\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}\beta_{j}\langle q\,,\,M^{MW\text{-}E_{\vec{K}}}(0)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\langle q\,,\,\Lambda_{\vec{K}}t+o(t)\rangle-t\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}\beta_{j}\langle q\,,\,M_{j}\rangle

(25)

\displaystyle\leq\langle q\,,\,\Lambda_{\vec{K}}t+o(t)\rangle-\langle q\,,\,(1+\delta)\Lambda_{\vec{K}}t\rangle

(26)

\displaystyle=\langle q\,,\,-\delta\Lambda_{\vec{K}}t+o(t)\rangle,

where inequality (
25
) is due to the definition of
\vec{K}
-EMW, and inequality (
26
) is due to (
24
).
As a result,

\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}(II)/t\leq-\delta\langle q\,,\,\Lambda_{\vec{K}}\rangle\leq\delta\lambda_{min}\left\|q\right\|_{1},

where
\lambda_{min}=\min\{\mathbb{P}(V\in I_{\vec{i}}):\mathbb{P}(V\in I_{\vec{i}})>0,{\vec{i}}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}\}
is the smallest positive arrival rate for any job type
{\vec{i}}
. Here we disregard the buckets
I_{\vec{i}}
with
\mathbb{P}(V\in I_{\vec{i}})=0
because it is almost never that
q(\vec{i})>0
occurs for such buckets.

Finally, for item
(III)
we again consider
{\vec{K}}
-MW as a special case of
{\vec{K}}
-EMW. We have
(III)=\sum_{{\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}}\mathbb{E}[q_{\vec{i}}(S_{\vec{i}}(t)-\tilde{S}_{\vec{i}}(t)|q(0)=q]
. For each job type
{\vec{i}}
, notice that
\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}(S_{\vec{i}}(t)-\tilde{S}_{\vec{i}}(t)>0)}{t}>0
only if the queue length for type-
{\vec{i}}
jobs is smaller than the number of type-
{\vec{i}}
jobs that
M^{MW\text{-}E_{\vec{K}}}
attempts to serve, i.e.,
q_{\vec{i}}<M^{MW\text{-}E_{\vec{K}}}_{\vec{i}}(0)
. Therefore, the upper bound for
\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}(III)/t
is as follows:

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}(III)

\displaystyle\leq\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle q(0),S(t)\rangle\,\Big|\,q(0)=q,\,q\preceq M^{MW\text{-}E_{\vec{K}}}(0)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\sum\limits_{{\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}}\mathbb{E}\left[M^{MW\text{-}E_{\vec{K}}}_{\vec{i}}(0)\cdot S_{\vec{i}}(t)\,\Big|\,q(0)=q,\,q\preceq M^{MW\text{-}E_{\vec{K}}}(0)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\sum\limits_{{\vec{i}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}}(M^{MW\text{-}E_{\vec{K}}}_{\vec{i}}(0))^{2}t

\displaystyle=\left\|M^{MW\text{-}E_{\vec{K}}}(0)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq K_{m}^{2},

where the last inequality is due to
\left\|M^{MW\text{-}E_{\vec{K}}}(0)\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left\|M^{MW\text{-}E_{\vec{K}}}(0)\right\|_{1}^{2}\leq K_{m}^{2}
.

By adding the above three limits for
(I)
,
(II)
, and
(III)
in (
20
), we have shown that
\Delta_{M}\circ L(q)\leq K_{m}+2K_{m}^{2}+\lambda-2\delta\lambda_{min}\left\|q\right\|_{1}
. Hence, for any
c>0
, when

\left\|q\right\|_{1}\geq\frac{K_{m}+2K_{m}^{2}+\lambda+c}{2\lambda_{min}\delta},

(27)

we can guarantee the drift
\Delta L(q)<-c
. Therefore, we can define the finite set
C
to contain all
q
for which (
27
) does not hold such that the Foster-Lyapunov condition is satisfied, and the CMTC
\{q(t)\}
is positive recurrent. With the positive recurrence of the CTMC, we have proven that the empty state of the discretized system is positive recurrent. As a result, the empty state of the original continuous MRJ system is also positive recurrent. This concludes our stability proof.
∎

(Convex Hull Condition)

In the continuous MRJ setting, for a fixed

Under the same assumption of

{\vec{K}}
-EMW. Note that

can move to the empty state in bounded time with uniformly lower-bounded probability, demonstrating positive recurrence to the empty state.

In the continuous MRJ setting, assume that the job resource requirements random variable
V
has a Lipschitz continuous density
f_{V}
, and assume that given some
\lambda>0
, there exists a policy
\pi
that stabilizes the queueing system. Then there exists some
{\vec{K}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and a distribution
\beta_{\vec{K}}
over
C_{\vec{K}}
, such that the discretized service measure
\eta^{S}_{\vec{K}}
satisfies the measure dominance (
17
).

Proof.

We give the proof in the multi-resource (MRJ) setting, and the single-resource setting (MSJ) can be shown by setting
d=1
.

Suppose that for a given
\lambda>0
and
V
, there exists some policy
\pi
(see
Definition

3
) that leads to a stable system. At the steady-state of this Markov process under
\pi
, we obtain a stationary measure
\beta^{\pi}
over
\pi(\mathcal{S})
, the set of service options used by
\pi
. The stability of
\pi
implies that
\eta^{A}=\eta^{\pi}
, where

\eta^{\pi}(dx)=\int_{\mathscr{S}}\sum_{r\in\pi(s)}\mathds{1}{\{r\in dx\}}\;d\beta^{\pi}(s),

so that the rate of job arrivals with resource requirements in
I
equals the completion rate of jobs served with resource requirements in
I
, where
I
is any subset of
(0,1]^{d}
.

In the steady state, because the empty state is positively recurrent, the Markov process assigns positive probability mass
\epsilon_{0}>0
to the empty job state
\emptyset\in\mathcal{S}
. At the empty job state, the only service option is
\emptyset\in\mathscr{S}:=\pi(\mathcal{S})
. We consider the conditional measure over
(0,1]^{d}

\eta^{S}=\mathcal{L}(\eta^{\pi}|\pi\neq\emptyset)=\frac{1}{1-\epsilon_{0}}\eta^{\pi},

and call
\eta^{S}
the
service measure
. The service measure is induced by
\{M_{\alpha}^{\pi}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}=\pi(\mathcal{S})\setminus\emptyset
, namely the family of continuous service options
\pi
uses. Note that the index set of these service options
\mathcal{A}
is uncountable. With a probability measure
\mathcal{L}(\pi|\eta\neq\emptyset)
over
\pi(\mathcal{S})
, we can obtain a probability measure
\zeta
over
\mathcal{A}
, which we call the reference distribution, and express the service measure as

\eta^{S}(dx)=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\sum_{r\in M_{\alpha}^{\pi}}\mathds{1}{\{r\in dx\}}\;d\zeta(\alpha).

(30)

The above construction guarantees that
\eta^{A}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\eta^{S}
, and that

\frac{d\eta^{S}}{d\eta^{A}}(x)\geq 1+\epsilon,\quad\forall x\in int(supp(\eta^{A})),

(31)

where
\epsilon=\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{1-\epsilon_{0}}
. We call this property “continuous measure dominance”.

Now our goal is to find some
{\vec{K}}
such that
{\vec{K}}
-MW stabilizes the system as well, by applying (
17
), with a service measure
\beta_{\vec{K}}
over
C_{\vec{K}}
chosen based on
\beta^{\pi}
. To be specific, we let
{\vec{K}}=(K,...,K)
for some
K
to be determined later, and aim to build a
{\vec{K}}
-discretized service measure
\eta^{S}_{{\vec{K}}}
based on some distribution
\beta_{\vec{K}}
over
C_{\vec{K}}
, such that there exists some
\delta>0
satisfying

\frac{\eta^{S}_{{\vec{K}}}({\vec{i}})}{\eta^{A}_{{\vec{K}}}({\vec{i}})}\geq 1+\delta,\quad\text{ for all }{\vec{i}}\in\Gamma_{{\vec{K}}}\text{, for which }\eta^{A}_{{\vec{K}}}({\vec{i}})>0,

(32)

where recall that for each job type
{\vec{i}}
,
\eta^{A}_{{\vec{K}}}({\vec{i}}):=\eta^{A}(I_{{\vec{i}}})
.

In our construction of
\beta_{\vec{K}}
, we start by classifying each discretized job type
{\vec{i}}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}
in either the default set
\mathcal{D}^{\vec{K}}
or the exceptional set
\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}
. For any default index
{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{D}^{\vec{K}}
, we rely on the continuous service options
\{M_{\alpha}^{\pi}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
to propose corresponding
{\vec{K}}
-discretized service options to serve type-
{\vec{i}}
jobs, which we then modify. We will discuss the modification after we have defined the exceptional set. For any exceptional index
{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}
, we include the service option that serves a single type-
{\vec{i}}
job. Specifically, we assign a total probability mass
\xi_{\vec{K}}
of
\beta^{\vec{K}}
to each service option
\{{\vec{i}}\}
for
{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}
, such that

\beta_{{\vec{K}}}(\{{\vec{i}}\})=(1+\delta)\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}(\{{\vec{i}}\}),\quad\text{for all }{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}.

(33)

We will then assign the remaining mass to the discrete service option family
\{M\}
induced by the continuous service option family
\{M^{\pi}_{\alpha}\}
, where the probability will be given by (
34
) to control the default set
\mathcal{D}^{\vec{K}}
. We will show that as
K
becomes large, the arrival measure on
\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}
vanishes, and for any job type in the set
\mathcal{D}^{\vec{K}}
, the gap between our service measure
\eta^{S}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})
and
\eta^{\pi}(I_{\vec{i}})
vanishes. This then allows us to prove discrete measure dominance for large enough
{\vec{K}}
.

We now introduce the exception set
\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}
, which is the union of three different subsets, namely the boundary set
\mathcal{B}^{\vec{K}}
, the empty-round-up set
\mathcal{I}^{\vec{K}}
, and the low-probability set
\mathcal{J}^{{\vec{K}}}(c_{1},c_{2})
, with parameters
c_{1}>0
and
0<c_{2}<1
. Then we guarantee discrete measure dominance for all
{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}
, by including in
\beta_{\vec{K}}
the corresponding service option
\{{\vec{i}}\}
and with mass
\beta_{\vec{K}}(\{{\vec{i}}\})
given in (
33
). To bound the probability assigned to the service options
\{{\vec{i}}\}
,
{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}
, we need to upper bound the total arrival rate of each subset of
\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}
, and further show that the upper bound converges to zero. Specifically, we will show that
\sum_{{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}}\beta_{\vec{K}}(\{{\vec{i}}\})=(1+\delta)\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}(\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}})=O(K^{-c_{2}})
. We note that
\delta
can be arbitrarily chosen in
(0,\epsilon)
, which does not affect the existence of
{\vec{K}}
.

The first subset of
\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}
is the boundary set
\mathcal{B}^{\vec{K}}
, which includes all boundary job types defined in (
15
), namely any job type that uses the entirety of any of the resources. Using the Lipschitz condition, we provide in
Lemma

8
an upper bound on the maximum density
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}
, such that
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\leq L:=L(C,d)<\infty
. The value of
L(C,d)
is given by (
46
), depending only on the Lipschitz constant
C
and the dimension
d
. We defer the proof to Appendix
10
. Therefore, we have that
\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}(\mathcal{B}^{\vec{K}})\leq\frac{L}{K^{d}}dK^{d-1}\leq LdK^{-1}
, because there are at most
dK^{d-1}
job types in
\mathcal{B}^{\vec{K}}
, and each type has arrival rate upper bounded by
\frac{L}{K^{d}}.
This completes our bound for
\mathcal{B}^{\vec{K}}
.

The next subset of
\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}
is defined as the empty-round-up set
\mathcal{I}^{\vec{K}}
, which contains all non-boundary job types
{\vec{i}}
with
\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}(I_{\vec{i}})>0
and
\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}(I_{{\vec{i}}+\vec{1}})=0
. To bound the arrival measure on
\mathcal{I}^{\vec{K}}
, we consider the zero set
\mathcal{Z}^{\vec{K}}:=\{{\vec{i}}\in\Gamma_{{\vec{K}}}:\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})=0\}
of all job types, the corresponding buckets of which have zero arrival rate. Then the following inclusion holds:

\mathcal{I}^{\vec{K}}\subseteq\bigcup\limits_{{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\vec{K}}}\{{\vec{i}}-\vec{1}\}.

For any
{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{I}^{\vec{K}}
,
f_{V}(\frac{{\vec{i}}+\vec{1}}{K})=0
. Because
f_{V}
is Lipschitz with constant
C
,
\sup_{v\in I_{\vec{i}}}f(v)\leq C\sqrt{d}/K
for all
{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\vec{K}}
. Therefore,
\eta^{A}_{{\vec{K}}}(\{{\vec{i}}-\vec{1}\})\leq C\sqrt{d}K^{-D-1}
for any
{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\vec{K}}
, and consequently,

\eta^{A}_{{\vec{K}}}(\mathcal{I}^{\vec{K}})\leq C\sqrt{d}K^{-D-1}K^{D}=C\sqrt{d}K^{-1},

where we use the total number of job types to bound the number of job types in
\mathcal{I}^{\vec{K}}
, i.e.,
|\mathcal{I}^{\vec{K}}|\leq|\mathcal{Z}^{\vec{K}}|\leq|\Gamma_{\vec{K}}|=K^{D}
. This completes our bound for
\mathcal{I}^{\vec{K}}
.

The final subset of
\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}
is the low-probability set
\mathcal{J}^{\vec{K}}(c_{1},{c_{2}})
for some constants
c_{1}>0
,
0<{c_{2}}<1
, such that

\mathcal{J}^{\vec{K}}(c_{1},{c_{2}})=\{{\vec{i}}\in\Gamma_{\vec{K}}:\eta^{A}_{{\vec{K}}}({\vec{i}})\leq c_{1}K^{-(D+{c_{2}})}\}.

The choice of the pair
(c_{1},c_{2})
does not affect the existence of
{\vec{K}}
, but affects the final value of
{\vec{K}}
. In this paper, we do not attempt to optimize
{\vec{K}}
. If concrete values of
c_{1},c_{2}
are needed,
c_{1}=1
and
c_{2}=1/2
suffice. The arrival measure on the set
\mathcal{J}^{{\vec{K}}}
is then upper bounded by

c_{1}K^{-{c_{2}-D}}\,|\mathcal{J}^{{\vec{K}}}(c_{1},c_{2})|\leq c_{1}K^{-{c_{2}}-D}|\Gamma_{\vec{K}}|=c_{1}K^{-{c_{2}}}.

This completes our bound for
\mathcal{J}^{\vec{K}}(c_{1},{c_{2}})
.

As mentioned before, for each exceptional job type
{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}:=\mathcal{I}^{\vec{K}}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\vec{K}}\cup\mathcal{J}^{\vec{K}}(c_{1},{c_{2}})
, we include in
\beta_{\vec{K}}
the service option
\{{\vec{i}}\}
, which only serves one type-
{\vec{i}}
job, and let
\beta_{\vec{K}}(\{{\vec{i}}\})=(1+\delta)\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})
. This guarantees the discrete measure dominance holds for all
{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}
, i.e.,
\eta^{S}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})\geq(1+\delta)\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})
. We also let
\xi_{\vec{K}}:=\sum_{{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}}\beta_{\vec{K}}(\{{\vec{i}}\})=(1+\delta)\eta^{S}_{\vec{K}}(\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}})
, which denotes the the summed probability mass
\beta_{\vec{K}}
assigns to these options. The above discussion implies that

\displaystyle\xi_{\vec{K}}

\displaystyle=(1+\delta)\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}(\mathcal{I}^{\vec{K}}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\vec{K}}\cup\mathcal{J}^{\vec{K}}(c_{1},{c_{2}}))

\displaystyle\leq(1+\delta)\left[LdK^{-1}+C\sqrt{d}K^{-1}+c_{1}K^{-c_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1+\delta)(Ld+C\sqrt{d}+c_{1})K^{-{c_{2}}},

and thus
\lim\limits_{K\uparrow\infty}\xi_{\vec{K}}=0
.

We now switch our focus to constructing
\beta_{\vec{K}}
to achieve the discrete measure dominance for the default job types
{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{D}^{\vec{K}}:=\Gamma_{\vec{K}}\setminus\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}
. We consider the family of service options
\{M_{\alpha}^{\pi}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
used by the continuous policy
\pi
. We will take advantage of
\{M_{\alpha}^{\pi}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
to construct the discretized service options that we will use for
\beta_{\vec{K}}
.

We create a mapping from
M_{\alpha}^{\pi}
to the discretized service options
M_{\alpha}^{\vec{K}}
. This mapping entry-wise rounds down each resource requirement
r
to the nearest smaller discretized bucket (job type). Formally speaking, given any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
,
M_{\alpha}^{\pi}
contributes a density
\zeta^{{\vec{K}}}(\alpha)=(1-\xi_{{\vec{K}}})\zeta(\alpha)
to
M^{{\vec{K}}}_{\alpha}:=\left\{\lfloor Kr\rfloor\right\}_{r\in M_{\alpha}^{\pi}}
. By using smaller job types, any valid
M_{\alpha}^{\pi}
is mapped to a valid
M_{\alpha}^{\vec{K}}
that does not exceed any resource capacity constraint. Then for any service option
M\in\{M_{\alpha}^{\vec{K}}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
,

\beta_{\vec{K}}(M)=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\mathds{1}{\left\{M=\left\{\lfloor Kr\rfloor\right\}_{r\in M_{\alpha}^{\pi}}\right\}}d\zeta^{\vec{K}}(\alpha),

(34)

and we obtain a valid probability distribution
\beta_{\vec{K}}
, where the remaining
\mathcal{E}^{\vec{K}}
indices were defined in (
33
).

The above construction of
\beta_{\vec{K}}
means that, for each
{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{D}^{\vec{K}}
,
\eta^{S}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})
is given by

\eta^{S}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})=\sum\limits_{M\in C_{\vec{K}}}M^{({\vec{i}})}\beta_{\vec{K}}(M)=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{\left\|M_{\alpha}^{\pi}\right\|_{0}}\mathds{1}{\{M_{\alpha}^{\pi}(l)\in I_{{\vec{i}}+\vec{1}}\}}d\zeta^{\vec{K}}(\alpha),

which can be lower bounded, due to (
31
) and the Lipschitz assumption
\left\|f\right\|_{Lip}=C
, as follows:

\displaystyle\eta^{S}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{\left\|M_{\alpha}^{\pi}\right\|_{0}}\mathds{1}{\{M_{\alpha}^{\pi}(l)\in I_{{\vec{i}}+\vec{1}}\}}(1-\xi_{\vec{K}})d\zeta(\alpha)

\displaystyle=(1-\xi_{\vec{K}})\eta^{S}(I_{{\vec{i}}+\vec{1}})

\displaystyle\geq(1+\epsilon)(1-\xi_{\vec{K}})\eta^{A}(I_{{\vec{i}}+\vec{1}})

\displaystyle=(1+\epsilon)(1-\xi_{\vec{K}})\int_{I_{{\vec{i}}+\vec{1}}}\lambda f(x)dx

\displaystyle\geq(1+\epsilon)(1-\xi_{\vec{K}})\int_{I_{{\vec{i}}+\vec{1}}}\lambda(f(x-\frac{\vec{1}}{K})-\frac{C\sqrt{d}}{K})dx

\displaystyle=(1+\epsilon)(1-\xi_{\vec{K}})\left[\eta^{A}({\vec{i}})-\frac{\lambda C\sqrt{d}}{K^{d+1}}\right].

To conclude that
\eta^{S}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})\geq(1+\delta)\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})
, we need to show for any
{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{D}^{\vec{K}}
,

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)(1-\xi_{\vec{K}})\left[\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})-\frac{\lambda C\sqrt{d}}{K^{d+1}}\right]\geq(1+\delta)\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}}),

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left[(1+\epsilon)(1-\xi_{\vec{K}})-(1+\delta)\right]\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})\geq(1+\epsilon)(1-\xi_{\vec{K}})\frac{\lambda C\sqrt{d}}{{K}^{d+1}}.

Because
{\vec{i}}\notin\mathcal{J}^{\vec{K}}(c_{1},{c_{2}})
,
\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})\geq c_{1}K^{-(D+{c_{2}})}
. It is enough to let
K
satisfy that

\displaystyle\left[(1+\epsilon)(1-\xi_{\vec{K}})-(1+\delta)\right]c_{1}{K}^{-(d+{c_{2}})}\geq(1+\epsilon)(1-\xi_{\vec{K}})\frac{\lambda C\sqrt{d}}{K^{d+1}},

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left[(1+\epsilon)(1-\xi_{\vec{K}})-(1+\delta)\right]c_{1}\geq(1+\epsilon)(1-\xi_{\vec{K}})\lambda C\sqrt{d}K^{{c_{2}}-1}.

(35)

When
K
goes to infinity, the limit of the L.H.S. of (
35
) is
c_{1}(\epsilon-\delta)
, and the limit of the R.H.S. of (
35
) is
0
, because
c_{2}
is chosen in
(0,1)
. Therefore, the discrete measure dominance holds also for
{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{D}^{\vec{K}}
when
K
is large enough. In particular, if we choose
c_{1}=1
and
c_{2}=\frac{1}{2}
, then it suffices to let

K\geq\left(\frac{(1+\delta)(1+\epsilon)(Ld+\frac{2+\delta}{1+\delta}C\sqrt{d}+1)}{\epsilon-\delta}\right)^{2}.

Because
\delta
can be arbitrarily small, we can let

K=\epsilon^{-2}[(1+\epsilon)(Ld+2C\sqrt{d}+1)]^{2}=\Theta(\epsilon^{-2}).

∎

From the above two lemmas,
Theorem

1
follows immediately that the
{\vec{K}}
-MW family is throughput-optimal for any resource requirement
V
with Lipschitz continuous p.d.f.
f_{V}
.

Theorem
1

(Throughput-optimality of
{\vec{K}}
-MW)
.

In the continuous MRJ setting with exponential durations, for any resource requirement distribution
V
with Lipschitz continuous probability density function
f_{V}
over
(0,1]^{d}
, the family of
{\vec{K}}
-MW policies is throughput-optimal. That is, if the continuous MRJ system is stable under some policy
\pi
, then there exists some
{\vec{K}}
such that
{\vec{K}}
-MW stabilizes the system.

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

For any system that can be stabilized, with Lipschitz continuous
f_{V}
,
Lemma

4
shows the existence of
{\vec{K}}=K\vec{1}
and some service option distribution
\beta_{\vec{K}}
, such that the corresponding discretized service measure satisfies discrete measure dominance (
17
). This is a sufficient condition for the stability of
{\vec{K}}
-MW, as proven in
Lemma

1
.
∎

5.4
Proof of
Theorem

3
: Throughput-Optimality of the Extreme-vertices
{\vec{K}}
-EMW and
{\vec{K}}
-EnMSR Families

Before we prove
Theorem

3
, we provide the following auxiliary lemma, which gives a necessary condition for a stable
{\vec{K}}
-discretized policy.

Lemma 6

(Necessary Condition for Stable
{\vec{K}}
-discretized Policy)
.

In the continuous MRJ setting, for a fixed
{\vec{K}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, if there exists any
{\vec{K}}
-discretized policy that stabilizes the system, then there exists a service option distribution
\beta_{\vec{K}}
over
C_{\vec{K}}
such that its corresponding service measure satisfies the discrete measure dominance (
17
).

Proof.

Assume that the queueing system is stable under some
{\vec{K}}
-discretized policy
\pi
, i.e., the empty state of the CTMP
\emptyset\in\mathcal{S}
is positive recurrent. Then considering the CTMC
\{q(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
, we have that the empty state
\vec{0}:=(0)_{\Gamma_{{\vec{K}}}}
is also positively recurrent. Because the CTMC is irreducible, it is positive recurrent and thus ergodic. Hence, an ergodic distribution over the state space
\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma_{\vec{K}}}
induces a stationary distribution
\beta^{\prime}_{\vec{K}}
over the set of all schedulable service options
\pi(\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma_{\vec{K}}})\subset C_{\vec{K}}
, such that

1.

The probability
\epsilon
that the server serves no job is identical to the ergodic measure of the empty state
\vec{0}
, which is positive:
\epsilon=\beta^{\prime}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{0}})>0.

2.

The service rate vector under
\beta^{\prime}_{\vec{K}}
matches the arrival rate vector such that the equilibrium is achieved:
\mathbb{E}^{\beta^{\prime}_{\vec{K}}}M=\Lambda_{\vec{K}}
.

Therefore, when we let
\beta_{\vec{K}}
be the distribution of service options over
C_{\vec{K}}
such that
\beta(M)=\frac{\beta^{\prime}_{\vec{K}}(M)}{1-\epsilon}
for all
M\neq\vec{0}
, the measure dominance (
17
) holds via the following inequality:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{M\in C_{\vec{K}}}\beta^{\prime}_{\vec{K}}(M)M=\sum\limits_{M\in C_{\vec{K}}\setminus\vec{0}}\beta^{\prime}_{\vec{K}}(M)M=(1-\epsilon)\sum\limits_{M\in C_{\vec{K}}\setminus\vec{0}}\frac{\beta^{\prime}_{\vec{K}}(M)}{1-\epsilon}M=\Lambda_{\vec{K}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{M\in C_{\vec{K}}\setminus\vec{0}}\beta_{\vec{K}}(M)M=\sum\limits_{M\in C_{\vec{K}}\setminus\vec{0}}\frac{\nu(M)}{1-\epsilon}M=\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\Lambda_{\vec{K}}\succ\Lambda_{\vec{K}}.\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\qed

(Sufficient and Necessary Condition for
{\vec{K}}
-MW)
.

Lemma

1
shows that the discrete measure dominance condition (

) is sufficient for the stability of
{\vec{K}}
-MW.

shows that the convex hull condition (

) is an equivalent condition of (

), for the stability of
{\vec{K}}
-MW.
Lemma

shows either conditions (

) and (

) are also necessary.

3
, the throughput-optimality of the X
{\vec{K}}
-EMW and X

,

,

, and

.

Theorem
3

(Throughput-optimality of X
{\vec{K}}
-EMW and X
{\vec{K}}
-EnMSR)
.

In the continuous MRJ setting, given the same conditions as in
Theorem

1
, X
{\vec{K}}
-EMW and X
{\vec{K}}
-EnMSR are both throughput-optimal. Moreover, for any
{\vec{K}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
fixed,
{\vec{K}}
-MW, X
{\vec{K}}
-EMW,
{\vec{K}}
-nMSR, and X
{\vec{K}}

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

Recall that
E^{X}_{\vec{K}}
is the set of extreme vertices of
C_{\vec{K}}
. Therefore,
E^{X}_{\vec{K}}
is the set of the extreme points of the polyhedron
ConvH(C_{\vec{K}})
, and
ConvH(C_{\vec{K}})=ConvH(E^{X}_{\vec{K}})
.

By the convex hull condition (
29
) provided in
Lemma

2
for the stability of the system, we conclude the sufficient and necessary condition for the stability of X
{\vec{K}}
-EMW coincides with (
17
). Hence, X
{\vec{K}}

5.5
Necessary Stability Condition for an MRJ System

The following lemma provides an upper bound for the stability region of any policy. We will use this lemma to prove
Theorems

4
and
5
in the
Sections

5.6
and
5.7
.

Lemma 7
.

Consider an MRJ system with resource requirement distribution
V=(V_{1},...,V_{d})
. We let
m
be the resource type that has the largest mean requirement, i.e.,
m:=\arg\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq d}\mathbb{E}V_{j}
.
Then the stability region for any policy is a subset of

\{\lambda>0:\lambda\mathbb{E}V_{m}<1\}.

(37)

Proof.

We choose the test function
w_{m}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+,0}
to be the total requirement of the type-
m
resource in the system, i.e., workload of type-
m
resource. Formally, when the state is given by
x=(\vec{v}_{n})_{1\leq n\leq N}
,
\phi(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\vec{v}_{n}(m)
. Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that when
\lambda\geq\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}V_{m}}
, there exists a policy
\pi
stabilizing the system and resulting in the steady state distribution
X
. Then the ergodic probability at the empty state
\mathbb{P}(X=\emptyset)>0
.

According to
Glynn and Zeevi 2008
, at the steady state
X
, we have zero expected drift of
w_{m}(X)
under the policy
\pi
:

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{\pi}\circ w_{m}(X)]=0.

(38)

Let
H
denote the stationary total resource requirement in service. As a result, the rate of decrease of
w_{m}(x)
is
H
, where we recall that jobs have duration distribution
\text{Exp}(1)
. On the other hand, the rate of increase of
\phi
due to arrivals is
\lambda\mathbb{E}V_{m}
. Therefore, we can express the expected drift explicitly, such that

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{\pi}\circ w_{m}(X)]=\lambda\mathbb{E}V_{m}-\mathbb{E}[H|X\neq\emptyset]\mathbb{P}(X=\emptyset).

Because
H\leq 1
,

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{\pi}\circ w_{m}(X)]>\lambda\mathbb{E}V_{m}-1\geq 0,

which contradicts the fact of zero expected drift (
38
). Therefore, we conclude that the stability region of any policy is a subset of
\{\lambda:\lambda\mathbb{E}V_{m}<1\}
.
∎

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\int_{I_{\vec{i}}}\left|f_{V}(x)-f_{V}(x+K^{-1}\vec{1})\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\int_{I_{\vec{i}}}C\sqrt{d}K^{-1}dx

\displaystyle=\lambda C\sqrt{d}K^{-d-1},

As a result, we have
\beta_{\vec{i}}\leq(1+\epsilon)[\eta^{A}_{\vec{K}}({\vec{i}})+C\sqrt{d}K^{-d-1}]
. Therefore, because the number of non-boundary job types
|\mathcal{H}^{\vec{K}}|=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{K-1}{K})^{d}
, the summed mass is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\vec{K}}\cup\mathcal{H}^{\vec{K}}}\beta_{\vec{i}}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)\lambda\left[LdK^{-1}+\sum_{{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{H}^{\vec{K}}}(\frac{1}{\lambda}\eta^{A}_{{\vec{K}}}({\vec{i}})+C\sqrt{d}K^{-d-1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)\lambda\left[(Ld+\frac{C\sqrt{d}}{2}(\frac{K-1}{K})^{d})K^{-1}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{H}^{\vec{K}}}\eta^{A}_{{\vec{K}}}({\vec{i}})\right]

\displaystyle<(1+\epsilon)\lambda\left[(Ld+\frac{C\sqrt{d}}{2})K^{-1}+\frac{1}{2}\right].

(45)

Here, the last inequality (
45
) holds because the two disjoint sets
\mathcal{H}^{\vec{K}}
and
{\vec{K}}-\mathcal{H}^{\vec{K}}
have identical arrival measure, and their union is a subset of
\Gamma_{\vec{K}}
, which has total arrival measure at most
\lambda
. As a result,
\sum_{{\vec{i}}\in\mathcal{H}^{\vec{K}}}\eta^{A}_{{\vec{K}}}({\vec{i}})\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

Using the upper bound (
45
), we choose
K
to be such that

\displaystyle\quad(1+\epsilon)\lambda\left[(Ld+\frac{C\sqrt{d}}{2})K^{-1}+\frac{1}{2}\right]\leq 1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow K\geq\Big(\frac{1}{(1+\epsilon)\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)^{-1}\Big(Ld+\frac{C\sqrt{d}}{2}\Big).

This completes the general centrally symmetric case. We now discuss a few special-case results. For the multi-resource uniform setting,
L=1
and
C=0
. Thus it suffices to let
K\geq\lceil\frac{2d\lambda(1+\epsilon)}{2-(1+\epsilon)\lambda}\rceil
. By letting
\epsilon
be small enough, we note that
K=\lfloor\frac{2\lambda d}{2-\lambda}\rfloor+1
enables discrete measure dominance (
17
) to hold.

We then show stability in the uniform single-resource (MSJ) setting. With
M_{K}=\{K\},M_{1}=\{1,K-1\},...,M_{\frac{K-1}{2}}:=\{\frac{K-1}{2},\frac{K+1}{2}\}
, we let
\beta_{K}=\beta_{1}=...,\beta_{\frac{K-1}{2}}=(1+\epsilon)\lambda/K
for some
\epsilon>0
, so that discrete measure dominance (
17
) can be established. It remains to ensure

\beta_{K}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\frac{K-1}{2}}\beta_{j}=(1+\epsilon)\lambda\frac{K+1}{2K}\leq 1\Rightarrow K\geq\lceil\frac{(1+\epsilon)\lambda}{2-(1+\epsilon)\lambda}\rceil.

Because
\epsilon
can be arbitrarily small, it is enough to let
K=\lfloor\frac{\lambda}{2-\lambda}\rfloor+1
.

Finally, by applying
Lemmas

1
and
5
, the stability of the system is achieved for any
\lambda<2
, respectively under 2J
{\vec{K}}
-EMW and 2J
{\vec{K}}
-EnMSR.
∎

6
Empirical Results

In this section, we use simulations to validate our main results for both parametric distributions and Google Borg trace data
Tirmazi et al. 2020
.
We compare our policies with several index-based benchmarks: FCFS, First-Fit, Best-Fit, and Least-Server-First (LSF). These index-based policies can also be naturally extended to the continuous-resource setting. The detailed definitions of First-Fit, Best-Fit, and LSF are given in Appendix
9
. In particular, we note that LSF is equivalent to naive MaxWeight in the continuous MSJ setting, and very similar in the Google Borg setting. When job resource requirements are continuous and almost surely distinct, each “job type” contains at most one job. As a result, naive MaxWeight always selects the feasible set containing the largest number of jobs, which is equivalent to batching jobs in increasing order of their resource requirements.

In
Section

6.1
, we simulate MSJ systems with i.i.d. parametric continuous resource requirements, to verify the throughput-optimality of our theoretically-motivated policies. In addition, we demonstrate that with a large discretization parameter
K
, the mean response time of
K
-EMW-B policies is consistently better than all prior index-based policies.

In
Section

6.2
, we compare our policy with the prior index-based and class-based policies, using resource requirement demand from a Google Borg trace. In this practical setting, although the requirements are not continuous or i.i.d., the performance advantages of our policies generalize beyond our model.

All data and code in this section can be found in the following repository:
https://github.com/isaacg1/buckets
.

6.1.4
General Lipschitz: Triangular Resource Requirement

We next consider a resource requirement distribution, the p.d.f. of which is Lipschitz continuous but neither centrally symmetric nor weakly decreasing, to empirically show that First-Fit is not always throughput-optimal, while our theoretically-motivated policies can achieve stability under higher load. We recall from
Theorem

We choose the symmetric triangular distribution

as our requirement distrbution, whose density is supported in

Because the server can always serve at least two jobs simultaneously,

6.2
Our Theoretically-motivated Policies v.s. index-based Policies: Borg Traces

We next evaluate the practical performance of our policy using the CPU and RAM requirements from a Google Borg trace
Yildiz and Baiocchi 2024
2
2

2

Data available at the following repository:
https://github.com/MertYILDIZ19/Google_cluster_usage_traces_v3_single_cell
, and show the state-of-the-art performance of our theoretically-motivated policies.

In our first simulation experiment, we consider an MSJ model in which the job arrival order follows the order observed in the Google Borg traces, and the resource requirement of each job is given by its rescaled CPU demand. We rescale the CPU requirements because the system capacity is unknown and the empirical distribution is highly skewed: Only a very small fraction of jobs request relatively large amounts of CPU, whereas the majority require extremely small amounts. As a result, directly using the raw data would require an excessively fine discretization, with most buckets receiving essentially zero arrivals. To address these issues, we discard the largest
10\%
of the observations and normalize the remaining requirements. In the second simulation experiment, we rescale the RAM requirements in the same way.

We assign each job an independent
Exp(1)
service time. Arrival times are modeled by a Poisson process
PP(\lambda)
. However, because the resource requirements are not i.i.d. and the resulting system is non-stationary, the stability threshold
\lambda_{V}^{*}
does not apply, and hence we do not define the load in this section.

Because the two requirement distributions are approximately weakly decreasing, the 2B
K
-EMW-B policy is a natural fit. We set
K=64
and compare 2B
64
-EMW-B with index-based policies. In addition, the inherently discrete value of requirements in the trace motivates us to include the class-based policy in our comparison. However, in these 1 million data points, the number of unique RAM requirements and unique CPU requirements are 3,668 and 536,229, respectively. This large number of classes makes it infeasible to run naive MaxWeight in our simulation. Instead, we propose the following class-based policy to approximate naive MaxWeight, called Pseudo MaxWeight (Pseudo-MW). Recall that naive MaxWeight performs identically to LSF when the resource requirements are continuously distributed. Similar to LSF, Pseudo-MW packs the jobs in increasing order of the ratio of resource requirement and the current number of jobs of that type. In addition to using Pseudo-MW to approximate naive MaxWeight, we further examine whether this policy achieves a similar performance to LSF.

The simulation results in Figures
Figs.

7(a)
and
7(b)
both show that 2B
64
-EMW-B significantly outperforms the index-based policies and Pseudo-MW, achieving substantially smaller mean response times. In addition, Pseudo-MW performs almost identically to LSF, which implies that when the class size is extremely large, class-based policy is not distinguishable from the index-based policies. These results show that our continuously-motivated policies can still exhibit superior performance in more realistic settings, where the resource requirements are not i.i.d., and are discrete with large support.

Conclusion

We introduce the continuous-resource multiresource-job (MRJ) system.
We show the first throughput-optimality results in this setting, for the
{\vec{K}}
-discretized MaxWeight family, and a related nonpreemptive policy family.
Moreover, we introduce three efficient variants, which achieve the same throughput optimality with more efficient computation, under distributional assumptions.

We demonstrate via simulation the advantages of our policies when combined with Backfilling, relative to existing index-based policies. Most importantly, we evaluate our policies using Google Borg traces as job requirements. Despite the fact that these traces are not independently distributed, our simulation shows that our 2-Bucket Efficient MaxWeight achieves the best mean response time uniformly over all arrival rates.

A promising direction for future work lies in analyzing a MaxWeight variant where the discretization parameter is chosen dynamically based on the queue length, as well as nonuniform discretization. Another future direction in the single-resource setting is to prove that the throughput-optimality of First-Fit and Best-Fit, when the resource requirement distribution has symmetric or decreasing density
Coffman and Stolyar 2001
.

Definitions of Some Index-Based Policies

In this section, we define First-Fit, Best-Fit, and Least-Server-First (LSF), which are the index-based policies used in
Section

6
as simulation benchmarks for our policies.

We first describe First-Fit in the MSJ setting. Whenever the system state changes, First-Fit scans the jobs in the queue according to their arrival order and greedily constructs a feasible set of jobs for joint service. To be specific, suppose that the policy has scanned the first
i
jobs in this order, and let
x
denote the total resource requirement of the jobs that have already been selected for service. Because the server has total capacity
1
, the remaining available capacity is
1-x
. If the
i+1
-th scanned job has a requirement
r\leq 1-x
, then this job is selected for service, the total requirement is updated to
x+r
, and the policy moves to scan the next job. Otherwise, the job is skipped, and the policy proceeds to scan the next job without changing the selected set. First-Fit continues this procedure until all jobs in the queue have been scanned. In addition, First-Fit can be naturally extended to the MRJ setting. In this case, each job has a resource-requirement vector, and the server has unit capacity for each resource type. A scanned job is selected for service whenever its requirement for every resource type does not exceed the corresponding remaining capacity.

Likewise, in the MSJ setting, Best-Fit and LSF follow the same greedy packing rule but use different scanning orders. Best-Fit attempts to pack larger jobs first, while LSF prioritizes smaller jobs. Formally speaking, Best-Fit scans the jobs in decreasing order of their resource requirements, whereas LSF scans the jobs in increasing order of their resource requirements. We note that LSF does not need to scan the entire queue. Once the remaining capacity is smaller than the requirement of the current scanned job, any subsequently scanned job cannot fit into the remaining capacity. Hence, LSF can terminate the scan at the first time when a job fails to fit.

Upper bound for the Supremum of Lipschitz Density Function

In this section, we derive an upper bound
L(C,d)
on the probability density function
f
on
[0,1]^{d}
where we assume the Lipschitz constant of
f
is
C
. Formally speaking, we bound

\sup\left\{\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}:f:[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+},\;\left\|f\right\|_{Lip}\leq C,\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f=1\right\}.

Lemma 8

(Supremum Bound of Lipschitz Continuous p.d.f. on Unit Cube)
.

For any probability density function
f
defined on
[0,1]^{d}
, if
f
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
C
, then
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\leq L(C,d)
, where

L(C,d):=\left[\frac{(d+1)\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}(\max\{C,C^{*}\})^{d}}{2^{d}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\right]^{\frac{1}{d+1}},\quad\text{ where }C^{*}=\frac{(d+1)\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{2^{d}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}.

(46)

Proof.

For any dimension
d
, when
C
is large enough, the upper bound of
\sup\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}
is achieved by a function with maximum at
\vec{0}
and directional derivative
-C
in all directions.

In the single-resource setting, the integral of
f
is the area of a right triangle with side lengths
L
and
L/C
, i.e.,
\frac{L^{2}}{2C}=1\Rightarrow L=(2C)^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Thus
L(C,1)\leq\sqrt{2C}
, given large enough
C
. Specifically, the above holds when
L/C\leq 1
, i.e,
C\geq 2
. Thus
L(C,1)\leq\max(\sqrt{2C},2)
.

In
d
-dimensional case, the integral of
f
is the same as
2^{-d}
the volume of a
d+1
-dimensional circular cone with radius
L/C
and height
L
, when
d\geq 2
. By applying the surface area formula in

d
-dimensional space,
A(d,r)=\frac{d\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}r^{d-1}
, where
r
denotes the radius and
\Gamma(\cdot)
denotes the Gamma function, we have that

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L/C}\frac{d\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{2^{d}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}r^{d-1}(L-Cr)dr=\frac{d\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{2^{d}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\frac{L^{d+1}}{d(d+1)C^{d}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow L=\left[\frac{(d+1)\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}C^{d}}{2^{d}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\right]^{\frac{1}{d+1}}.

Note that therefore the discussion above requires
C\geq\frac{(d+1)\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{2^{d}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}=:C^{*}
. Note that
L(C^{*},d)=C^{*}
.

We conclude that
L(C,d)
can be chosen as
\left[\frac{(d+1)\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}C^{d}}{2^{d}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\right]^{\frac{1}{d+1}}
, when
C\geq C^{*}
. When
C<C^{*}
, we can trivially choose the upper bound
L(C,d)=L(C^{*},d)=C^{*}
.
