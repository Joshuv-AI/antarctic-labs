Integrated physiological, biochemical, and gene expression responses of tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) to time-dependent salinity stress
Authors: Merve Dilek Karataş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSalinity is a major abiotic stress factor limiting tomato production by affecting plant growth, physiology, and metabolism. This study investigated the time-dependent physiological, biochemical, and gene expression responses of tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) cultivar H2274 under different salinity levels (control, 1.5, 3.0, and 4.5 dS m⁻¹). Plants were sampled on the 15th and 45th days of stress exposure. Physiological parameters, including chlorophyll content and leaf color, biochemical responses associated with antioxidant activity and lipid peroxidation, and the expression of salt stress-related genes were evaluated.ResultsIncreasing salinity levels significantly altered chlorophyll content, leaf color parameters, antioxidant enzyme activities, and malondialdehyde (MDA) accumulation, with responses varying depending on stress intensity and duration. Gene expression analyses revealed differential regulation of genes involved in antioxidant defense, osmotic regulation, ion homeostasis, stress signaling, chlorophyll catabolism, and DNA repair mechanisms under salinity stress. Hierarchical clustering and heatmap analyses further demonstrated distinct expression patterns among the investigated genes under different salinity conditions and sampling times.ConclusionsThe findings provide an integrated overview of the physiological, biochemical, and molecular responses of tomato plants under salinity stress. Overall, prolonged and high-intensity salinity stress appeared to weaken several physiological, biochemical, and transcriptional responses in H2274 tomato plants. These findings may contribute to future studies on salinity stress physiology and stress-response regulation in tomato plants.Keywords: Solanum lycopersicum, Salinity stress, Stress-responsive genes, Antioxidant defense, Ion homeostasis, Abiotic stress

Introduction

Salinity is considered one of the major abiotic constraints limiting global agricultural productivity. Soil salinity affects approximately 20–25% of agricultural lands worldwide [1]. Increasing soil salinity caused by climate change, improper irrigation practices, and inadequate drainage systems poses a substantial threat to sustainable agricultural production, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions [2, 3]. When the electrical conductivity of the soil solution reaches 4 dS m⁻¹ (equivalent to 40 mM NaCl), the soil is considered saline, and these conditions cause physiological and metabolic disturbances in many plant species [4].

Salt stress in plants is generally characterized by a two-phase process consisting of a rapid osmotic stress phase that inhibits growth in young leaves and a slower ionic toxicity phase that accelerates senescence in mature leaves [5]. These two components are temporally and tissue-specific; while osmotic stress leads to the loss of cellular turgor during the early phase, ionic toxicity disrupts the Na⁺/K⁺ balance, thereby negatively affecting metabolic processes [6]. Reduced photosynthetic capacity, disruption of membrane integrity, and excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) represent the major types of damage induced by salt stress [7–9]. In response to these stress conditions, plants activate diverse physiological and molecular response mechanisms, including antioxidant defense systems, osmotic adjustment, ion transport regulation, and stress-responsive gene networks [5, 6]. Understanding the molecular basis of these stress-associated responses is important for elucidating salinity-induced physiological and molecular changes in plants [3, 10].

The molecular responses of plants to salt stress involve changes in the expression of genes associated with antioxidant defense, osmotic adjustment, ion homeostasis, hormonal signaling, chlorophyll metabolism, and genome stability. Antioxidant-related genes such as SOD, CAT, and APX contribute to ROS detoxification under stress conditions [8, 9], whereas P5CS and NHX1 play important roles in osmotic regulation and ion homeostasis [11, 12]. Stress signaling pathways involving NCED1 and DREB2A are also important in regulating stress-associated responses to salinity [13–15]. In addition, genes associated with chlorophyll catabolism (PAO and SGR1), cell wall remodeling (EXP1), and DNA repair mechanisms (LYKU70, PARP1, and RAD51) have been reported to participate in plant stress responses under adverse environmental conditions [16–22].

Although studies on salinity stress in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.), one of the most economically and nutritionally important vegetable crops worldwide, have provided substantial physiological and molecular information [3], most studies have generally focused on a single physiological process or a limited number of stress-responsive genes. Tomato is considered moderately sensitive to salinity, with a threshold value of approximately 2.5 dS m⁻¹ [3], and salinity stress negatively affects plant growth, photosynthetic capacity, membrane stability, fruit quality, and yield [2]. However, plant adaptation to salinity results from the interaction of multiple physiological, biochemical, and molecular responses. Therefore, integrative approaches evaluating these responses collectively may provide a broader understanding of salinity-associated stress responses in tomato plants. Furthermore, evaluating plant responses under different salinity intensities and exposure durations may provide further insight into the temporal dynamics of stress adaptation.

In this context, the present study hypothesized that increasing salinity levels and stress duration would induce physiological, biochemical, and gene expression changes in tomato plants. Therefore, the time-dependent effects of different salinity levels on tomato plants were investigated. Within this scope, chlorophyll content, leaf color parameters, antioxidant enzyme activities, lipid peroxidation, and the expression profiles of stress-related genes associated with antioxidant defense, osmotic regulation, ion homeostasis, hormonal signaling, chlorophyll catabolism, and DNA repair mechanisms were evaluated. The findings obtained may contribute to a better understanding of the physiological, biochemical, and molecular mechanisms underlying salinity stress responses in tomato plants and provide useful insights for future studies on stress physiology and salinity management strategies.

Materials and methods

Materials and experimental design

The experiment was conducted under controlled conditions in the climate chambers of the Faculty of Agriculture, Van Yüzüncü Yıl University. The climate chamber conditions were set as follows: ambient temperature of 25 ± 2 °C during the day and 18 ± 2 °C at night, relative humidity of 60–70%, and a photoperiod of 16 h light / 8 h dark. Solanum lycopersicum L. cv. H2274, a commercially cultivated processing tomato genotype, was used as the plant material in the study. Seeds of this cultivar were obtained from Bursa Tohumculuk Ziraat ve Ticaret A.Ş. (a commercial seed company, Bursa, Türkiye). Uniformly developed plants at the 3–4 true leaf stage were selected and transplanted into 1.5 L plastic pots (diameter: 130 mm, height: 110 mm). Saline treatments were initiated immediately after transplantation at the 3–4 true leaf stage and continued for 45 days until the final sampling period. Plants were grown in sterilized soil as the growth medium (pH: 8.75, electrical conductivity: 0.63 dS m⁻¹, organic matter content: 1.91%). Although the soil exhibited alkaline characteristics, all experimental groups were grown under the same soil conditions; therefore, the observed differences among groups were primarily attributed to saline conditions. The soil used was sterilized in an autoclave at 121 °C for 20 min prior to the experiment to eliminate potential pathogenic and contaminating microorganisms. The experiment was established according to a completely randomized design. Pots were randomly positioned in the climate chamber and relocated on a weekly basis to minimize positional effects. Four different salinity conditions were evaluated in the study. The selected EC levels were designed to represent a gradient ranging from non-saline conditions to low, moderate, and relatively high salinity exposure. Since tomato is generally considered moderately sensitive to salinity, with a reported threshold of approximately 2.5 dS m⁻¹, the 1.5 dS m⁻¹ treatment was included as a sub-threshold salinity level, whereas 3.0 and 4.5 dS m⁻¹ were selected to evaluate plant responses under elevated EC conditions. Saline solutions were prepared on the basis of electrical conductivity (EC) values, and the treatment groups were established as follows: control (0 dS m⁻¹, irrigation water without salt addition), low salinity (1.5 dS m⁻¹), moderate salinity (3.0 dS m⁻¹), and high salinity (4.5 dS m⁻¹). Sodium chloride (NaCl) was used to establish saline conditions, and the required salt concentrations were dissolved in distilled water and adjusted to the target EC values. Approximately 0.96, 1.92, and 2.88 g NaCl L⁻¹ were used to obtain EC values of 1.5, 3.0, and 4.5 dS m⁻¹, respectively. The final EC values were verified using a conductivity meter prior to each application. The prepared salt solutions were applied to the plants as irrigation water, while the plants in the control group were irrigated with water without salt addition. Photographs of tomato plants subjected to different salinity treatments under controlled growth conditions are presented in Fig. 1. Each treatment was conducted with three replicates. Pots were used as the experimental unit, with four plants growing in each pot. The plants were irrigated every three days, and salt treatments were applied directly at the target EC levels immediately following transplantation, without gradual increments. To prevent growth differences from nutrient deficiency and to ensure that the observed effects were attributable solely to salinity treatments, all the plants were supplied with half-strength Hoagland nutrient solution once per week. Sampling was performed on the 15th and 45th days of stress application. The 15th day was selected to evaluate the early physiological and molecular responses to salinity, whereas the 45th day was selected to assess longer-term stress adaptation and chronic stress-related responses under prolonged salinity exposure. Chlorophyll content and leaf color parameters were measured in fresh leaves. In addition, leaf samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for biochemical and molecular analyses. Three biological replicates were used for each treatment and sampling time point. Each biological replicate was composed of composite leaf samples obtained from four plants grown in the same pot.

Chlorophyll and color analysis

Chlorophyll and color measurements were performed on the third fully expanded leaf from the apical meristem of each plant. Measurements were conducted between 09:00 and 11:00 to minimize the effect of diurnal variation.

Chlorophyll content was determined using a portable chlorophyll meter (SPAD-502, Minolta, Japan). A total of 12 measurements were obtained from the plants within each pot, and the mean of the SPAD value was used for statistical analyses.

Leaf color parameters were determined via a chromameter (Minolta CR-400, Osaka, Japan). The color parameters L* (lightness), a* (red-green axis), C* (chroma), and h° (hue angle) were recorded. For each replicate, 12 different leaves were randomly selected for measurement.

Antioxidant enzyme activities and lipid peroxidation (MDA) analysis

A 0.5 g leaf sample was collected for the determination of antioxidant enzyme activities and lipid peroxidation levels. The samples were homogenized on ice in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.6). The homogenates were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 0 °C, and the resulting supernatants were used for subsequent analyses. CAT activity was determined by monitoring the decrease in absorbance at 240 nm associated with H₂O₂ decomposition and expressed as µmol H₂O₂ reduced min⁻¹ g⁻¹ fresh weight (FW). APX activity was determined by measuring the change in absorbance at 290 nm associated with ascorbate oxidation and expressed as µmol ascorbate oxidized min⁻¹ g⁻¹ FW. The MDA content, used as an indicator of lipid peroxidation, was calculated via absorbance measurements at 532 and 600 nm on the basis of the thiobarbituric acid reaction and expressed as nmol g⁻¹ FW. All analyses were performed according to the methods described by [23, 24].

RNA isolation

Total RNA was extracted from the plant samples using the DiaRex® Total RNA Extraction Kit (TR-0877-100, Diagen, Ankara, Turkey) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 600 µl of LBD solution was added to 25 mg of tissue, followed by homogenization using a TissueLyser LT (Qiagen). After homogenization, the samples were centrifuged twice at 3,000 rpm for 30 s each time and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The resulting supernatant was transferred to a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube, mixed with 600 µL of 99% ethanol, and centrifuged at 8,000 × g (10,000 rpm) for 2 min. The supernatant was then passed through a spin column and centrifuged again at 8,000 × g (10,000 rpm) for 2 min. Washing steps were performed sequentially with 500 µL of WBD-1 and WBD-2 solutions, each followed by centrifugation at 8,000 × g (10,000 rpm) for 2 min to complete the washing steps. Finally, RNA was eluted with 40 µL of EBD solution by centrifugation at 8,000 × g for 2 min. RNA quantity and purity were assessed by measuring absorbance at 260 and 280 nm using a nanospectrophotometer (QIAxpert, Qiagen). RNA purity was evaluated based on the A260/A280 ratio for each sample. RNA integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, with distinct visualization of 28 S and 18 S rRNA bands accepted as indicators of RNA quality. To eliminate possible genomic DNA contamination, RNA samples were treated with DNase I according to the manufacturer’s protocol, followed by enzyme inactivation. The RNA samples were stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

cDNA synthesis

All steps of cDNA synthesis were performed on ice using the OneScript Plus cDNA Synthesis Kit (catalog No: G236). The RNA samples were standardized to a concentration of 100 ng per microliter, and a total of 700 ng of RNA was used in each reaction. For cDNA synthesis, 7 µL of RNA, 7 µL of 5× RT mix, and 6 µL of DNase/RNase-free water were combined to obtain a final reaction volume of 20 µL. The reaction mixture was incubated at 55 °C for 50 min, followed by 10 min at 25 °C in a thermal cycler (Rotor-Gene Q 9000). To confirm the absence of genomic DNA contamination, No-RT (reverse transcription control) reactions were prepared for each sample without reverse transcriptase enzyme. No amplification was observed in these reactions.

Gene expression analysis

The selected target genes were chosen based on their reported involvement in major salinity stress-response pathways. Copper/zinc superoxide dismutase (CuZnSOD), catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) were selected as antioxidant defense-related genes; pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthetase (P5CS) was selected as an osmotic adjustment-related gene; Na+/H+ antiporter 1 (NHX1) was selected as an ion homeostasis-related gene; 9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase (NCED1) and dehydration-responsive element binding protein 2 (DREB2A) were selected as ABA biosynthesis and stress signaling-related genes; pheophorbide a oxygenase (PAO) and stay-green protein 1 (SGR1) were selected as chlorophyll degradation and senescence-related genes; expansin 1 (EXP1) was selected as a cell wall remodeling-related gene; and ATP-dependent DNA helicase/Ku70 homolog (LYKU70), poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1), and RAD51 homologous recombination repair protein (RAD51) were selected as DNA damage response and repair-related genes. These genes were included to provide an integrative evaluation of physiological, biochemical, and molecular responses of tomato plants to salinity stress.

cDNA products were analyzed by real-time PCR using 2× BlasTaq qPCR Master Mix (Applied Biological Materials, ABM) in a thermal cycler. EF1α (Elongation Factor 1-alpha) and Ubi3 (Ubiquitin) were used as reference genes for normalization of gene expression data. The target genes included CuZnSOD, CAT, APX, P5CS, NHX1, NCED1, DREB2A, PAO, SGR1, EXP1, LYKU70, PARP1, and RAD51. The primers used in the study are listed in Table 1.

The PCR mixture consisted of 10 µL of 2× BlasTaq qPCR Master Mix, 3 µL each of forward and reverse primers, and 5 µL of H₂O, resulting in a total volume of 21 µL. An additional 4 µL of cDNA was added to bring the final volume to 25 µL. The PCR amplification cycle parameters were set as follows: an initial predenaturation step at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 45 s and annealing at 60 °C for 45 s. qPCR was performed in two technical replicates for all samples to minimize experimental error. Each treatment was analyzed across three independent biological replicates (n = 3). No-template control (NTC) reactions were prepared for each primer pair to monitor possible contamination and primer-dimer formation, and no amplification was observed in these reactions. Primer efficiencies were evaluated on the basis of literature validation and standard curve analyses, and the amplification efficiencies of the qPCRs were confirmed to fall within the range of 90–110%, with correlation coefficients (R²) exceeding 0.99. Amplification specificity was assessed by melt curve analysis performed for each gene, and the presence of a single peak was accepted as an indicator of specific amplification. The reference genes EF1α and Ubi3 were identified as stably expressed genes by examining the coefficients of variation in Ct values across treatment groups and sampling time points, and were used for normalization of target gene expression levels. Relative gene expression levels were calculated via the comparative threshold cycle method (2^−ΔΔCt) [25]. To visualize gene expression patterns, a heatmap was constructed via log2 transformation of the relative expression values obtained via the 2^−ΔΔCt method, and hierarchical clustering analysis based on Euclidean distance was applied to determine similarities among genes.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were analyzed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) depending on the evaluated parameter and experimental structure. For parameters evaluated simultaneously across different salinity treatments and sampling times, two-way ANOVA was performed to determine the effects of salinity, stress duration, and their interaction. Tukey’s post hoc test was applied to identify statistically significant differences among groups. All statistical evaluations were performed at a significance level of α = 0.05, and the results are presented as means ± standard errors (means ± SE). Prior to analysis, the normality assumption of the data was assessed using skewness and kurtosis statistics, and homogeneity of variance was verified using Levene’s test. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics. In addition, Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships among physiological, biochemical, and gene expression parameters under different salinity treatments and sampling times. Correlation coefficients were visualized using a correlation heatmap generated from the mean values of all measured parameters.

Results

Chlorophyll content (SPAD)

The effects of different salinity levels on the chlorophyll content (SPAD) of tomato plants were statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2). On day 15, the control (25.03 ± 0.85) and 1.5 dS m⁻¹ (24.31 ± 0.52) treatments were placed in the same statistical group, and no significant difference was detected between these treatments. In contrast, the SPAD values decreased significantly in the 3.0 dS m⁻¹ (21.48 ± 0.38) and 4.5 dS m⁻¹ (20.22 ± 0.57) treatments, and these two treatments were significantly different from the control and 1.5 dS m⁻¹ treatments (p < 0.05).

Effects of salinity level on SPAD chlorophyll values at different sampling times. The values are the means ± SE. Different letters indicate significant differences (p˂0.05)

On day 45, the highest SPAD value was recorded in the control group (30.31 ± 1.08), which was significantly different from all the other treatments (p < 0.05). A gradual decrease in SPAD values was observed with increasing salinity; a value of 23.61 ± 0.56 was obtained in the 1.5 dS m⁻¹ treatment, whereas lower SPAD values were recorded in the 3.0 dS m⁻¹ (19.31 ± 1.24) and 4.5 dS m⁻¹ (17.49 ± 0.85) treatments. These two treatments were placed in the same statistical group (p < 0.05). Compared with the control, SPAD values decreased by approximately 36% and 42% in the 3.0 and 4.5 dS m⁻¹ treatments, respectively, on day 45.

Leaf color parameters

The effects of different salinity levels on the leaf color parameters of tomato plants were statistically evaluated (Tables 2 and 3). On day 15, the L* parameter was significantly affected by the salinity treatment (p < 0.05). The highest L* value was recorded in the 3.0 dS m⁻¹ treatment (45.78 ± 0.02), whereas the lowest value was detected in the 1.5 dS m⁻¹ treatment (42.86 ± 0.55). The control (44.99 ± 0.88) and 4.5 dS m⁻¹ (44.67 ± 0.56) treatments were placed in an intermediate group. In contrast, no statistically significant difference was detected among the treatments with respect to the a*, b*, Ch, and H°ab parameters (p > 0.05).

Antioxidant enzyme activities and lipid peroxidation (MDA)

When changes in CAT enzyme activity were examined, all salinity treatments increased CAT activity on day 15, whereas on day 45, all the salt treatments decreased the CAT activity compared with that of the control group. The differences among the treatments were significant on day 15, whereas the differences on day 45 were not statistically significant (p < 0.05). When differences between sampling days were examined, the difference between days was found to be significant in the control and 1.5 dS m⁻¹ treatments, whereas differences in the 3.0 and 4.5 dS m⁻¹ treatments were not statistically significant (p < 0.05). The highest CAT activity on day 15 was detected in the 3.0 and 1.5 dS m⁻¹ treatment groups, showing approximately 362% and 361% increases relative to the control group, respectively, whereas on day 45, the highest CAT activity was recorded in the control group (Table 4).

APX enzyme activity was decreased in all the treatment groups compared with the control group at both days 15 and 45. Discussion

Chlorophyll degradation and leaf color responses

Salinity leads to a reduction in the functionality of chlorophyll by causing clustering and a decrease in the number of chloroplasts. In particular, disruption of thylakoid membrane organization, a reduction in photosystem II photochemical efficiency, and impairment of the electron transport chain under high salinity conditions adversely affect photosynthetic pigment stability [7]. The decrease in chlorophyll content observed in parallel with increasing salinity in the present study indicates that photosynthetic pigments in the H2274 tomato cultivar are sensitive to salt stress and that pigment stability weakens as stress intensity increases. In parallel, the changes in b* and chroma (Ch) values indicate that leaves tend to shift toward yellow tones as the level of salt stress increases. It has been reported that salt stress suppresses chlorophyll biosynthesis and may affect processes associated with chlorophyll degradation, thereby leading to deterioration in the stability of pigment-protein complexes [26]. These visible phenotypic changes may reflect combined effects of chlorophyll loss, stress-associated senescence, and altered leaf physiological status under salinity conditions.

The response of the PAO and SGR1 genes to salt stress markedly differed depending on the sampling time and stress intensity. PAO is a key enzyme involved in chlorophyll catabolism through the conversion of pheophorbide a into red chlorophyll catabolite (RCC), whereas SGR1 contributes to the initiation of chlorophyll degradation at the thylakoid membrane [17, 18, 27, 28].

In the present study, the significant increase in PAO expression exclusively in the 3.0 dS m⁻¹ treatment at day 15 suggested that moderate salt stress can activate the chlorophyll degradation pathway at an early stage. Recent tomato studies have highlighted the association between chlorophyll degradation-related pathways and stress responses under salinity conditions [28], which is consistent with earlier reports showing that PAO expression can be upregulated under osmotic stress conditions [17]. In contrast, the absence of a marked increase in PAO expression at the 4.5 dS m⁻¹ dose suggests that high ionic stress may suppress the transcriptional regulation of chlorophyll catabolism. Sakuraba et al. [18] demonstrated that PAO and SGR1 function together during chlorophyll degradation. The suppression of SGR1 expression at high salinity in the present study suggests that the integration of PAO into the functional catabolic complex may have been limited.

On day 45, PAO expression decreased markedly at all salinity doses. However, notably the most pronounced decline in SPAD values and the most intense yellowing pattern in color parameters were observed during the same period. The continued chlorophyll loss despite reduced PAO and SGR1 expression suggests that additional regulatory processes may contribute to chlorophyll degradation under prolonged stress conditions. Since biochemical intermediates of chlorophyll degradation and protein-level changes were not examined, this interpretation should be considered cautiously, and further biochemical and proteomic analyses are required to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

SGR1 is a senescence-associated regulatory protein involved in chlorophyll degradation [18, 29, 30]. SGR1 expression was suppressed at all salinity levels on day 15, suggesting that senescence-related chlorophyll degradation was not transcriptionally activated during the early phase of salt stress. Therefore, the suppression of this gene in the early phase of salt stress may indicate that chlorophyll loss under early salinity stress is not solely associated with the classical senescence-related pathway. On day 45, both SGR1 and PAO expression decreased with increasing salinity, suggesting that chlorophyll catabolism was not primarily regulated through these genes under prolonged stress conditions. Previous studies have shown that suppression of SGR1 function can delay chlorophyll degradation and stabilize LHCII complexes [29, 30]. The continued decline in chlorophyll content despite SGR1 suppression may reflect additional stress-associated regulatory processes affecting chlorophyll stability under prolonged salinity conditions.

Oxidative stress responses

Salt stress disrupts the balance between ROS production and detoxification, leading to an increase in intracellular ROS levels and the resulting oxidative stress [31]. Recent studies have emphasized the importance of enzymatic and nonenzymatic antioxidant defense systems in regulating oxidative stress responses under salinity conditions [9], consistent with earlier ROS-related findings [8]. APX is an important component of the ascorbate-glutathione (AsA-GSH) cycle involved in H₂O₂ detoxification [8, 32]. The decrease in APX activity observed in the present study indicates that the accumulation of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ may limit the efficiency of the AsA-GSH cycle and that ionic stress may negatively affect enzyme function and cellular redox regulation [33]. Recent studies on tomato salinity stress responses have similarly reported that APX and CAT activities may decrease in salt-sensitive genotypes under prolonged salinity stress, accompanied by increased oxidative damage [3]. Earlier studies conducted in wild salt-tolerant tomato species also demonstrated that antioxidant enzyme activities may be maintained or increased under salinity conditions [34]. Despite this decrease in APX activity, the increase in CAT activity observed at the early time point (day 15) is noteworthy. The fact that the CAT activity was greater than that in the control group under all the salt treatments suggests that the plants may have activated alternative enzymatic mechanisms to sustain H₂O₂ detoxification. However, the decrease in CAT activity below the control level in the salt treatments on day 45, along with the loss of statistical significance among the treatments, is noteworthy. This pattern suggests that ROS production may exceed enzymatic defense capacity under prolonged stress, reducing the sustainability of antioxidant responses over time [8, 33]. The MDA content, a biochemical indicator of lipid peroxidation, exhibited a distinct dose- and time-dependent pattern. Consistent with previous reports, salt stress has been shown to increase H₂O₂ and MDA levels, indicating enhanced oxidative damage and membrane lipid peroxidation, whereas variations in CAT and APX activities reflect the dynamic regulation of the antioxidant system under stress conditions [35]. The increase in MDA levels in the 3.0 dS m⁻¹ treatment group at the early time point (day 15) demonstrated that moderate salt stress triggers oxidative damage to membrane lipids. In contrast, the relative decrease in the 1.5 dS m⁻¹ treatment group suggested that a low salinity dose may have suppressed lipid peroxidation by stimulating antioxidant defense. Similar findings indicating that low-intensity stress factors can trigger antioxidant capacity have also been reported in the literature [36]. On the other hand, the decrease in the MDA content below that of the control group in all the salt treatments on day 45, particularly the statistically significant interday difference in the 3.0 and 4.5 dS m⁻¹ treatments (p < 0.05), may reflect a time-dependent change in lipid peroxidation status rather than a direct indication of improved stress tolerance. One possible explanation for this observation is that the observed pattern may be associated with changes in oxidative metabolism, adaptive modifications in membrane lipid composition, or reduced metabolic activity under prolonged stress conditions [37]. However, these possibilities require further biochemical and lipidomic confirmation.

Overall, the findings suggest that antioxidant responses to salinity stress were strongly dependent on both stress intensity and duration. Early CAT activation may reflect a transient compensatory antioxidant response, whereas prolonged stress appeared to reduce the sustainability of enzymatic defense mechanisms.

Antioxidant gene expression responses

Salt stress can trigger ROS production by increasing electron leakage in chloroplasts and mitochondria through ionic and osmotic imbalance [33]. The increase in CuZnSOD gene expression at moderate salinity on day 15 in the present study suggests that ROS may function as signaling molecules in the early phase. Recent studies have suggested that controlled H₂O₂ accumulation may function as an important redox signal involved in stress-responsive gene regulation under salinity conditions [9, 38]. Previous studies have suggested that miR398-mediated posttranscriptional regulation may contribute to changes in CuZnSOD expression under stress conditions [39]. In contrast, the decrease in CuZnSOD expression and CAT/APX activity on day 45, particularly at high salt concentrations, suggests that the defense system may become less sustainable under chronic stress conditions. mRNA processing, stability, and translation are also important regulatory steps under abiotic stress conditions in plants [40]. Overall, the findings suggest a time-dependent biphasic antioxidant response to salinity stress. In the early phase, increased CuZnSOD expression and CAT activity corresponded with maintenance of redox balance under moderate salinity stress. In the later phase, suppression of antioxidant defense components under high salinity conditions may reflect reduced sustainability of stress-related regulatory responses. Pearson correlation analysis further indicated relationships between antioxidant-related molecular and biochemical responses, including antioxidant gene expression patterns and CAT/APX enzyme activities. These correlations provide an integrative basis for interpreting the antioxidant response, although they do not imply direct causal regulation. These findings suggest that antioxidant responses were influenced not only by transcriptional regulation but also by additional posttranscriptional processes [40]. Overall, H2274 tomato plants exhibited a transient antioxidant response under low to moderate salinity conditions; however, this response appeared to decline under prolonged and high-intensity stress.

Osmotic and ionic stress responses (P5CS and NHX1)

Proline is an important osmotic adjustment compound that accumulates under salt stress, and P5CS, the rate-limiting enzyme of proline biosynthesis, is widely used as a molecular indicator of osmotic adaptation [11, 41, 42]. The increasing trend in P5CS expression at the 3.0 dS m⁻¹ treatment on day 15 in the present study indicates that moderate salt stress can activate proline biosynthesis; however, the suppression of P5CS expression at the 4.5 dS m⁻¹ dose suggests that this transcriptional response appeared to become more limited as stress intensity increased. Similarly, Parida and Das [7] reported that high salt concentrations may suppress metabolic processes and reduce the sustainability of stress response mechanisms. On day 45, P5CS expression peaked in the 1.5 dS m⁻¹ treatment, suggesting sustained osmotic adjustment under low to moderate chronic stress. Recent studies investigating salinity-associated osmotic responses in tomato plants have similarly reported that proline accumulation was associated with stress-associated physiological regulation under prolonged stress conditions [3], consistent with earlier findings [43]. In contrast, the low expression levels at the 3.0 and 4.5 dS m⁻¹ doses on day 45 suggest that high-intensity chronic stress may diminish the transcriptional response associated with proline biosynthesis. Overall, P5CS expression showed a time- and dose-dependent response, with reduced expression under prolonged and severe stress suggesting limited sustainability of osmotic adjustment-related transcriptional responses.

Salt tolerance depends not only on osmotic adjustment mechanisms but also on the maintenance of cellular ion homeostasis. NHX1 is a Na⁺/H⁺ antiporter protein localized at the tonoplast that contributes to the maintenance of cellular ion homeostasis by facilitating the sequestration of cytoplasmic Na⁺ ions into the vacuole [44]. This mechanism contributes to cellular ion homeostasis under salt stress by limiting the accumulation of cytotoxic Na⁺. Apse et al. [12] demonstrated that the overexpression of AtNHX1 in Arabidopsis confers tolerance to salt stress, and that this tolerance is associated with high NHX1 transcript and protein levels. Similarly, Yamaguchi and Blumwald [45] emphasized that the continuity of vacuolar Na⁺ transport systems is important for maintaining ion homeostasis under salinity stress.

In the present study, NHX1 gene expression increased in a dose-dependent manner at day 15, reaching its highest level on the 3.0 dS m⁻¹ treatment. These findings suggest that NHX1 expression was associated with transcriptional responses related to vacuolar Na⁺ sequestration during the early stress period. However, the marked decrease in NHX1 expression at all salinity levels on day 45 suggests reduced transcriptional support for Na⁺ compartmentalization under prolonged stress conditions. Taken together with the P5CS findings, the simultaneous suppression of molecular responses related to osmotic adjustment and ion transport suggests reduced sustainability of transcriptional responses associated with ion homeostasis under prolonged and high-intensity salinity stress.

ABA-related stress signaling (NCED1 and DREB2A)

NCED1 encodes the rate-limiting enzyme in ABA biosynthesis and plays an important role in ABA accumulation under abiotic stress conditions [13]. ABA accumulation is closely associated with physiological stress responses, including stomatal regulation and stress-responsive gene activation [46, 47], and the elevated NCED1 expression observed under 1.5 dS m⁻¹ salinity on day 15 may reflect transcriptional activation of ABA-related signaling. These findings suggest that low-intensity salt stress can stimulate the transcriptional response associated with ABA biosynthesis. However, the absence of a similar increase at higher salinity levels suggests that this transcriptional response may become limited as stress intensity increases. This pattern is consistent with reports that high salinity may suppress metabolic processes and that stress responses can vary in a dose-dependent manner [7]. On day 45, the decrease in NCED1 expression with increasing salinity suggests that the transcriptional response associated with ABA biosynthesis appeared to weaken under prolonged stress, although NCED genes are known to play a critical role in ABA homeostasis [46]. The present results indicate that this regulatory mechanism may be suppressed under high and prolonged salt stress; however, since ABA levels were not directly measured, this interpretation is limited to the transcriptional level.

DREB2A is an important transcription factor that regulates the expression of numerous target genes under stress conditions by binding to dehydration-responsive elements [14]. Recent studies on salinity-associated transcriptional regulation in tomato have highlighted the importance of DREB-related signaling pathways under stress conditions [3], supporting earlier findings reported for DREB2A [15]. In the present study, the relatively high DREB2A expression observed under 1.5 dS m⁻¹ salinity suggests that stress-related transcriptional regulation was more pronounced under low salinity, whereas its suppression at higher salinity levels was associated with reduced sustainability of this response under stronger ionic stress.

Together, the NCED1 and DREB2A expression patterns suggest that transcriptional stress signaling responses were more pronounced under low salinity conditions, whereas prolonged and high-intensity stress reduced the sustainability of these responses.

Cell wall remodeling responses (EXP1)

EXP1, a member of the α-expansin family, is associated with cell wall loosening and growth-related processes in plants [16, 48, 49]. Previous studies have reported that expansin-related genes may participate in cell wall remodeling responses under salinity stress conditions [50, 51].

The expression profile on day 15 in the present study did not exhibit a linear response. The increase in gene expression at the 1.5 and 4.5 dS m⁻¹ treatments compared with the control, alongside the decrease to the lowest level in the 3.0 dS m⁻¹ treatment, suggested that the effects of different salinity levels on cell wall remodeling cannot be explained by a simple dose-response relationship. The maintenance of EXP1 expression at low salinity doses suggests an early growth-related transcriptional response associated with cell wall remodeling under stress conditions [21, 22]. Chen et al. [52] reported that an increase in expansin gene expression was associated with the preservation of cell wall flexibility and the maintenance of growth under salt stress. In contrast, the suppression observed at the 3.0 dS m⁻¹ dose suggests that stress-related effects on cell wall remodeling appeared at this salinity level. With increasing stress duration, a systematic dose-dependent trend emerged. EXP1 expression peaked in the 1.5 dS m⁻¹ treatment, suggesting sustained transcriptional responses related to cell wall remodeling under low-level chronic salinity. In contrast, EXP1 expression declined to its lowest level under chronic high-salinity conditions. Geilfus et al. [50] reported that the decrease in β-expansin protein levels associated with high salt concentrations in salt-sensitive maize plants was related to the decline in leaf growth. Similarly, Zorb et al. [53] demonstrated that the decrease in expansin protein levels under high salinity conditions may be associated with the limitation of cellular growth capacity. Considering the osmotic and ionic effects of salt stress, the suppression of EXP1 expression may indicate that cell wall remodeling processes were negatively affected under prolonged salinity conditions [26]. Overall, the EXP1 expression pattern suggests that cell wall remodeling-related responses varied depending on salinity intensity and stress duration.

DNA repair responses (LYKU70, PARP1, and RAD51)

Salt stress can compromise genomic integrity through ROS-induced DNA damage, thereby activating DNA repair pathways [8]. LYKU70, PARP1, and RAD51 are involved in DNA repair pathways associated with genome stability under stress conditions [19, 20, 54]. The highest expression levels of LYKU70 and RAD51 were detected in the 1.5 dS m⁻¹ salt treatment on day 15 of the present study, suggesting activation of transcriptional responses associated with genome stability during the early stress period. Mahapatra and Roy [20] suggested that salt stress can activate DNA repair-related genes in response to DNA damage. In contrast, the decrease in LYKU70 expression at the 3.0 dS m⁻¹ treatment suggests reduced transcriptional regulation associated with the NHEJ pathway. The decrease in PARP1 expression with increasing salinity suggests reduced transcriptional responses associated with single-strand break repair under salt stress. As the duration of stress increased, the highest expression of all three genes was observed in the control group. The simultaneous decrease in LYKU70, PARP1, and RAD51 expression under prolonged salinity is consistent with previous reports suggesting reduced DNA repair-related responses and compromised genome stability under stress conditions [19]. Taken together, the reduction in both antioxidant defense-related responses and DNA repair gene expression under prolonged salinity conditions may indicate decreased sustainability of stress-associated cellular protection mechanisms.

Ingrated molecular and physiological responses

The heatmap and hierarchical clustering analysis based on log₂-transformed fold change values revealed distinct expression patterns among genes under different salinity treatments and sampling time points.

The NHX1, CAT, RAD51, and NCED1 clustered together and generally exhibited increased expression under low salinity at day 15, followed by decreased expression with increasing salinity and stress duration. The early increase in NCED1 and RAD51 expression may reflect ABA-related signaling and DNA repair-associated responses [8, 13, 19, 55].

The clustering of the EXP1, DREB2A, P5CS, and APX formed a separate cluster and generally showed higher expression under low to moderate salinity during the early stress period. These genes are associated with osmotic adjustment, stress signaling, antioxidant defense, and cell wall remodeling [11, 31, 38, 43, 56].

CuZnSOD, SGR1, and PAO clustered together and exhibited similar expression patterns under salinity stress. The marked decrease in PAO and SGR1 expression under prolonged high salinity (4.5 dS m⁻¹, day 45) suggests altered transcriptional regulation of chlorophyll metabolism under chronic stress conditions [17, 18].

The separation of LYKU70 and PARP1 from other gene groups and their clustering at distinct positions may indicate distinct transcriptional patterns compared with other stress-related genes. The decreasing trend in LYKU70 expression under high-salinity conditions suggests reduced transcriptional responses associated with DNA repair under prolonged salinity stress [19]. PARP1 exhibited comparatively limited variation across treatments.

Pearson correlation analysis provided an integrative view of the relationships among physiological, biochemical, and gene expression parameters under salinity stress. The observed correlations among SPAD, MDA, antioxidant enzyme activities, and stress-responsive genes suggest associations between pigment stability, oxidative status, and molecular stress responses. In particular, antioxidant-related genes were associated with CAT and APX activities, indicating similar transcriptional and biochemical response patterns. However, these relationships should be interpreted as associative rather than causal, and further validation is required to confirm the underlying mechanisms.

Taken together, these findings suggest that tomato responses to salinity followed a time- and stress intensity-dependent biphasic pattern. Early stress exposure was associated with hormonal signaling, transcription factor activation, osmotic adjustment, antioxidant defense, and DNA repair-related responses, whereas prolonged high salinity reduced the sustainability of many of these transcriptional responses.

Conclusion

In the present study, the physiological, biochemical, and molecular responses of the H2274 tomato cultivar to different salinity levels were comprehensively evaluated. The results suggested changes in antioxidant defense, osmotic adjustment, ion homeostasis, ABA-related signaling, cell wall remodeling, and DNA repair-related responses under low to moderate salinity conditions. Biochemical analyses revealed that antioxidant enzyme activities and MDA levels, an indicator of lipid peroxidation, varied depending on stress intensity and duration. However, with increasing salinity level and prolonged stress exposure, the sustainability of many of these responses appeared to decline at both the transcriptional and biochemical levels.

The continued chlorophyll degradation despite the suppression of PAO and SGR1 expression suggested that chlorophyll loss under prolonged salinity stress may not be explained solely by the transcriptional regulation of these genes. However, since biochemical intermediates and protein-level changes associated with chlorophyll degradation were not examined, this interpretation should be considered cautiously. Overall, prolonged and high-intensity salinity appeared to reduce the sustainability of several physiological, biochemical, and transcriptional stress responses in H2274 tomato plants.

It should also be noted that the alkaline soil conditions used in the present study may have influenced micronutrient availability, which could potentially interact with plant responses to salinity stress. In addition, the present study was conducted using a single tomato genotype under controlled environmental conditions, and several important physiological and biochemical parameters directly associated with salinity tolerance, including Na⁺ and K⁺ accumulation, proline content, and relative water content, were not evaluated. Furthermore, early stress-response time points were not included in the sampling design. The physiological and transcriptional findings were also not supported by proteomic, metabolomic, or ionomic analyses. Therefore, future studies integrating these measurements with multi-omics approaches and comparative evaluations across different tomato genotypes would contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of salinity stress responses and their temporal regulation.
