1 Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G) Systems
Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) technology extends the role of Plug-in Hybrid Vehicle’s (PHEV) and
Battery Electric Vehicle’s (BEV) batteries beyond transportation by enabling bidirectional power
transfer between the vehicle's onboard energy storage and the utility grid. Modern battery-powered
vehicles are equipped with high-capacity battery packs that remain stationary and underutilized
for the majority of their operational life. A vehicle used for daily commuting may be parked and
connected to charging infrastructure for 18–20 hours per day, representing a significant untapped
energy resource. When considered at scale, the aggregated storage capacity of a modest fleet of
10–15 vehicles within a single parking facility can reach the MWh range, sufficient to partially
offset peak demand for a mid-size commercial building or contribute meaningful ancillary services
to the local grid.

The ability to charge during off-peak periods when electricity prices are low and discharge during
peak demand when prices are high positions EV batteries as distributed energy arbitrage assets,
with the potential to offset vehicle ownership and charging costs. Beyond energy arbitrage, V2G-
capable assets can provide ancillary grid services through active and reactive power injections.
Active power supports grid frequency regulation, while reactive power supports local voltage
regulation. Together, these capabilities establish V2G as a technically promising and economically
relevant pathway toward greater integration of battery-powered vehicles into the broader energy
system.

1.1 DC vs AC V2G Systems
V2G systems can be broadly classified into two configurations based on the location of the power
conversion stage, as shown in Figure 1. In AC V2G systems, a vehicle is equipped with an onboard
bidirectional converter that rectifies AC grid power to DC during charging. It inverts DC battery
output back to AC during discharge, supplying power directly to residential loads or the grid. In
DC V2G systems, power conversion is performed externally by a bidirectional charger unit.
During charging, grid AC power is rectified within the charger and delivered directly to the vehicle
battery as DC power. During discharge, DC power drawn from the vehicle battery is inverted to
AC by the same charger unit before being supplied to the grid or local loads.

The use of external power conversion hardware in DC V2G systems introduces additional
component and installation costs; however, it also offers greater flexibility in converter design and
can support improved conversion efficiency compared to onboard solutions constrained by vehicle
space and weight requirements. The V2G system evaluated in this report employs a DC V2G
architecture, with all power conversion performed externally by the InCharge™ ICE-66 V2X
bidirectional DC fast charger.

1.2 Current State of V2G Technologies
V2G technology has matured to Technology Readiness Levels (TRL) 7–9, with bidirectional DC
fast chargers commercially available from multiple manufacturers at power levels exceeding
500kW [1], and initial OEMs beginning to release production vehicles with native V2G
functionality. This progression reflects both the technical viability of the concept and increasing
industry confidence in its commercial potential.

Standardization has been central to enabling this progression. The first DCFC communication
protocols to be adopted, DIN 70121 and ISO 15118-2, were developed exclusively for
unidirectional charging and carry no native support for bidirectional power flow. Where
bidirectional operation has been demonstrated using these protocols, it typically reflects either a
proprietary modification of the standard or an exploitation of implementation gaps in the vehicle,
such as the absence of a charging performance check for low or negative current setpoints, or the
deliberate condition where EVSE output voltage is held below the vehicle's battery voltage to
induce reverse current flow.

ISO 15118-20 addresses this limitation directly by defining a communication framework for
bidirectional power and energy negotiation between the vehicle and the EVSE. Alongside it, IEEE
1547-2018 establishes grid interconnection requirements for distributed energy resources,
including V2G systems, while OCPP 2.1 standardizes backend communication between EVSEs
and charging station management systems (CSMS), enabling remote session control and smart
bidirectional charging profile delivery. Collectively, these standards form the interoperability
foundation upon which scalable, certified V2G deployment depends.

Active research areas include smart charge management algorithms that optimize dispatch against
dynamic price signals and battery state-of-health constraints, extension of V2G to heavy-duty
vehicles with larger battery packs and higher power levels, and EV aggregation platforms capable
of providing bulk ancillary services at MV distribution scale.

Despite demonstrated feasibility across global pilot programs, widespread deployment continues
to face meaningful barriers, including limited grid infrastructure for bidirectional power flow,
incomplete standards adoption across vehicle and EVSE platforms, and the absence of harmonized
regulatory frameworks. Addressing these challenges will be essential to transitioning V2G from
demonstrated capability to a dependable and scalable grid resource.

1.3 Need for Performance Characterization
To address this gap, the Charging Technologies group at Argonne National Laboratory (ANL)
procured and commissioned the InCharge™ ICE-66 V2X in March 2025, a 66kW bidirectional
DC fast charger and among the first of its power class to become commercially available in North
America. Following the availability of compatible V2G-capable vehicles in late 2025, a structured
characterization test campaign was conducted under the “V2X Connect” Project, funded by the
U.S. Department of Energy's Transportation Technologies Office (TTO). The findings presented
in this report provide a practical assessment of the ICE-66 V2X's operational capabilities,
standards conformance, and performance boundaries. System specifications and test results are
presented in the following section.

2.1 System Information
The InCharge™ ICE-66 V2X is a 66kW bidirectional DC fast charger supporting V2G, vehicle-
to-vehicle (V2V), and other vehicle energy export applications [2]. The charger employs a modular
power electronics architecture comprising three independent 22kW bidirectional converter
modules. Each module can gradually adjust its power output from 0 to 22 kW, allowing the system
to reach its full rating of 66 kW in discrete increments. Two CCS Combo 1 DC output handles, as
seen in Figure 2 (a primary and a secondary port), share the aggregate 66kW power budget, with
per-port allocation configurable through the local interface. The unit supports ISO 15118-20, ISO
15118-2, and DIN 70121 for vehicle-to-EVSE communication, and OCPP 1.6j for backend
connectivity with OCPP 2.0.1 and OCPP 2.1 support planned in the future.

 Figure 2. InCharge™ ICE-66 V2X System at ANL

Detailed technical specifications, including electrical input and output parameters and full system
capabilities, are available in the manufacturer's product documentation.[2][3] The InCharge™
ICE-66 V2X is referred to as “EVSE” throughout the rest of the report.

2.2 Tests Description and Results
A suite of performance characterization tests was conducted on the EVSE using multiple vehicles
to assess interoperability and operational behavior across charging and discharging modes.
Vehicle-to-EVSE high-level communication (HLC) was captured via a CCS listener [4], recording
power line communication (PLC) signal traffic including negotiated current, voltage, and power
setpoints, along with real-time state-of-charge (SOC) updates, as illustrated in Figure 3. An
inductive coupler was positioned around the DC cable segment between the vehicle charging port
and the EVSE, enabling non-intrusive acquisition of the HLC signal during active sessions. Grid
side power flow was monitored using revenue-grade Measurlogic DTS 310 [5] energy submeters,
with OCPP-reported meter values logged in parallel at CSMS for cross-validation purposes.

 Figure 3. InCharge ™ ICE-66 V2X Performance Characterization Test Setup

The individual performance characterization tests and associated results are described in the
following sections.

The vehicle battery voltage at the start of each session was extracted from the PreChargeReq
message using the EVTargetVoltage parameter and compared against the EVSEPresentVoltage
reported in CurrentDemandRes messages throughout the session. EVSEPresentVoltage was
observed to remain consistently below both EVTargetVoltage and EV Battery Voltage throughout
discharging sessions, as seen in Figure 5. Thus, by maintaining the EVSE output voltage below
the vehicle battery voltage, a reverse voltage gradient is established that drives power flow
from the vehicle battery to the EVSE.

A closer examination of the protocol messages exchanged during discharging sessions reveals that
the EVSE does not reflect discharge mode in its communication stack. The

EVSEMaxCurrentLimit and EVSEMaxPowerLimit parameters reported in both
ChargeParameterDiscoveryRes and CurrentDemandRes messages, as well as the
EVSEPresentCurrent in CurrentDemandRes, were reported exclusively as positive values during
discharging, identical in sign convention to the charging operation. This indicates that the EVSE
relies on an implementation that departs from DIN 70121:2012 and ISO 15118-2:2018,
neither of which defines any standardized support for bidirectional charging. Reverse power flow
is indeed achieved entirely through a voltage-gradient mechanism once discharging mode is
selected from the EVSE interface, with no corresponding change reflected in the protocol message
fields. As a result, the vehicle receives no explicit indication at the communication layer that
a discharge session is underway.

This limitation was evident in the behavior of the Tesla Cybertruck and Polestar 3, both of which
continuously extended their “estimated time to full charge” during active discharge sessions. This
response suggests that these vehicle platforms do not differentiate the EVSE-defined “V2G
discharge” mode from a standard charging session at the battery management and communication
stack level, likely interpreting the reduced terminal voltage as a low-rate charge condition rather
than an intentional discharge event. This behavior points to a gap in vehicle-side charge
performance monitoring for detecting reverse power flow that may warrant attention in future
vehicle firmware and communication stack development.

Vehicles tested that did not allow for bidirectional charging with the EVSE exhibited a distinctly
different response when the EVSE was set to discharge mode. These vehicles issued a
PowerDeliveryReq with the DCEVStatus→ EVErrorCode field set to “EVRESSMalfunction”,
triggering immediate session termination. This indicates that these vehicles interpret the reverse
power flow condition as a battery system fault rather than responding with a protocol-level
rejection of the discharge request, an ungraceful termination mode that underscores the importance
of robust discharge capability detection and handling in both EVSE firmware and vehicle
communication stacks.

For the remainder of the tests, a 2025 Polestar 3 BEV was used as the test vehicle.

This sequence indicates that the EVSE implements stop conditions unilaterally at the power
electronics control level without communicating session termination intent to the vehicle through
the protocol stack, placing the burden of session closure on the vehicle.

For the fixed-duration stop condition, the same communication pattern was observed. Upon
expiration of the configured 5-minute charging or discharging period, the EVSE ceased current
output without prior notification to the vehicle, and the identical downstream message sequence,
PowerDeliveryReq, WeldingDetectionReq, and eventual SessionStopReq from the vehicle,
followed in both modes.

For the SOC-based stop condition during charging, the EVSE monitored the vehicle battery SOC
reported in successive CurrentDemandReq messages and terminated power delivery upon
detecting a 5% rise from the initial SOC value. The resulting communication behavior for session
termination was consistent with the pattern described previously.

For the SOC-based stop condition during discharging, the test revealed a significant functional
deficiency: the EVSE failed to initiate discharge entirely when a 5% SOC reduction cutoff was
pre-configured. Following successful authorization, no discharge current was delivered to the grid,
and the session did not progress to active energy transfer. This represents a notable limitation of
the ICE-66 V2X firmware, wherein the SOC-referenced discharge cutoff parameter prevents
session initiation rather than governing session termination as intended.

For the OCPP smart charging profile-based stop method, a profile specifying a 1-minute initial
delay followed by a 1-minute active charge or discharge interval was delivered via the
SetChargingProfile message. The initial delay was enforced by the EVSE as an authorization hold
period, after which energy transfer commenced and was subsequently terminated at the profile's
scheduled endpoint. As with the other stop methods, the vehicle received no explicit session
termination signal through the ISO 15118-2 protocol; the EVSE-side stop was unexpected to the
vehicle, and session closure followed the same unilateral termination sequence described above.
The OCPP profile commands operated exclusively within the EVSE's internal control logic and
were not propagated to the vehicle through the vehicle-to-EVSE communication layer.

2.2.3 Remote Start/Stop via OCPP:

The ICE-66 V2X was authenticated with CIP.io-Link, an OCPP CSMS Backend at ANL, by
providing the EVSE Charger box ID and pre-configured access credentials, establishing a
persistent OCPP 1.6j connection over the facility network. OCPP 1.6j messages were exchanged
as WebSocket frames over this connection, enabling remote session control, smart charging profile
delivery, and meter value retrieval.

Remote session initiation and termination were validated for both charging and discharging modes.
Before issuing any remote command, the EVSE connector handle was physically connected to the
test vehicle. The remote start and stop sequence proceeded as follows.

Remote Session Start: The CSMS server initiated the session by issuing a
RemoteStartTransaction.req to the EVSE containing the connector ID and authorization ID tag, to
which the EVSE responded with RemoteStartTransaction.conf (Accepted). EVSE then completed
the Vehicle-EVSE protocol negotiation, progressing through SDP, SupportedAppProtocol,
Authorization, and ChargeParameterDiscovery sequences. Upon successful session establishment,
the EVSE reported the session initiation to CSMS via StartTransaction.req, receiving a transaction
ID in the StartTransaction.conf response. Meter values were subsequently reported to the server
every 60 seconds via periodic MeterValues.req messages throughout the active session.

OCPP 1.6j does not provide a native mechanism to initiate a V2G discharge session as a distinct
transaction type. The protocol was developed exclusively around charging operations, and
consequently, RemoteStartTransaction.req carries no parameter to specify discharge mode or
reverse power flow intent. To address this limitation, a custom OCPP 1.6j message
“DataTransfer.req” was issued instead, leveraging functionality unique to InCharge™ ICE-66
V2X OCPP implementation. The DataTransfer message is intended as a means for OEMs to
implement custom commands outside the OCPP 1.6j standard. This message carries
a “RemoteStartV2X” custom command specific to EVSE to initiate a discharge session remotely.
The session was subsequently terminated using the standard RemoteStopTransaction.req
command.
2.2.4 Smart Charge Management via OCPP:

Time-varying power profiles were delivered to the EVSE via SetChargingProfile commands
through the CSMS. As established in the remote start/stop tests, charging and discharge sessions
were initiated remotely via RemoteStartTransaction.req and DataTransfer.req (remoteStartV2X)
commands, respectively. Three distinct profiles were evaluated to characterize EVSE tracking
accuracy, profile transition response, and the behavior of signed power limit values under both
charging and discharging modes.

Profile 1 - Charging with Power Curtailment: A remote charge session was started with the
EVSE operating at full rated output (66kW) for 2 minutes, followed by a curtailment period
specifying a power limit of 10kW for 5 minutes, after which the limit was removed and full power
resumed for a further 2 minutes. The EVSE tracked the delivered profile accurately, reducing
output to the commanded 10kW during the curtailment window and restoring full power at the
scheduled transition, as shown in Figure 8. This result confirms that the EVSE correctly interprets
and executes stepwise SetChargingProfile schedules during charging operation.

The three profiles collectively demonstrate that the EVSE determines power flow direction based
on the “charging or V2G discharge” operating mode established at session initiation, and not from
the sign of the power limit value specified in the SetChargingProfile command. Positive power
limit values are applied as expected within the operating mode; negative values, however, produce
unpredictable behavior. While OCPP 1.6j does not natively support bidirectional session control,
the ICE-66 V2X implementation achieves this through the proprietary DataTransfer.req message
carrying the remoteStartV2X command for discharge session initiation, with standard OCPP 1.6j
SetChargingProfile commands applied thereafter for power dispatch control in both modes.

It should be noted that the ICE-66 V2X does not support mid-transaction mode switching. There
is no mechanism within a single active transaction to transition from charging to discharging or
vice versa via a smart charging profile. To change power flow direction, the active transaction
must be fully terminated via RemoteStopTransaction.req, after which a new session is initiated
using either RemoteStartTransaction.req for charging or DataTransfer.req (remoteStartV2X) for
discharge, without requiring physical disconnection of the vehicle. This approach, however, results
in multiple discrete transactions and necessitates a complete session stop before the mode
transition can be executed, which has implications for applications requiring seamless bidirectional
dispatch within a single session.

2.2.5 Simultaneous Dual-Port Operation:

For these tests, both output handles of the EVSE were occupied concurrently under three
configurations: both ports charging, one port charging while the other discharging, and both ports
discharging. These tests examined inter-port power interaction, EVSE stability under split-power
conditions, and vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) power transfer capability.

Both Ports Charging: With two vehicles connected and charging simultaneously, the EVSE
operated at its full rated capacity of 66kW. Power allocation between the primary port (Port 1) and
the secondary port (Port 2) is user-configurable; however, due to the modular 22kW converter
architecture, allocation is constrained to discrete steps as seen in Figure 11. The available split
configurations are 44kW / 22kW or 22kW / 22kW between Port 1 and Port 2, respectively,
precluding an equal 33kW / 33kW distribution.

 Figure 11. Simultaneous Dural Port Operation: Both Ports Charging

One Port Charging, Another Port Discharging: When one port operated in discharge mode
while the other charged, the power discharged from the discharging vehicle was consumed directly
by the charging vehicle at the adjacent port, effectively enabling V2V power transfer through the
EVSE. When the power discharged at one port was less than the charging demand at the other, the
deficit was supplemented by importing power from the grid, as seen in Figure 12. Conversely,
when the discharged power exceeded the charging demand at the second port, the surplus was
exported to the grid. This behavior confirms that the ICE-66 V2X supports simultaneous
bidirectional port operation and is capable of dynamically routing excess discharge power to the
grid when the on-site load is insufficient to absorb it.

2.2.6 ISO15118-20 Communication Capability Test

Tests conducted in Section 2.2.1 established that the EVSE supports ISO 15118-20 for V2G
communication. A dedicated test was performed to confirm this capability. Since no vehicle with
a native ISO 15118-20 HLC implementation was available at the time of testing, an Electric
Vehicle Communication Controller (EVCC) was emulated using the SpEC II communication
module [6] to exercise this capability directly.

In the test, the emulated EVCC transmitted a SupportedAppProtocolReq message listing multiple
application protocols, with ISO 15118-20 assigned as the highest priority. The Supply Equipment
Communication Controller (SECC) within the EVSE responded with a SupportedAppProtocolRes
selecting ISO 15118-20, consistent with the priority ordering supplied by the EVCC, as shown in
Figure 14.

 Figure 14. ISO 15118-20: SupportedAPpProtocolReq and SupportedAppProtocolRes

However, ISO 15118-20 mandates TLS 1.3 as the required transport security layer, which was not
available on the EVSE side at the time of testing. The successful protocol negotiation nonetheless
confirms that the EVSE correctly implements the application protocol selection sequence per ISO
15118-20 and is expected to achieve full interoperability with conformant vehicles once TLS 1.3
support is incorporated into the EVSE firmware.

Summary & Next Steps
A structured performance characterization campaign was conducted on the InCharge™ ICE-66
V2X 66kW bidirectional DC fast charger at ANL, evaluating Vehicle-to-EVSE communication
behavior, session control, OCPP smart charge management, and simultaneous dual-port operation
across multiple V2G-capable battery-operated vehicle platforms.

The EVSE successfully established charging sessions with all tested vehicles, demonstrating
baseline interoperability across DIN 70121 and ISO 15118-2 protocol implementations. Session
termination under energy threshold, duration, and SOC-based stop conditions was executed
reliably during charging; however, the EVSE terminated sessions unilaterally at the power
electronics control level without communicating stop intent through the protocol layer, resulting
in ungraceful session closure from the vehicle's perspective in all tested stop methods.

A significant functional deficiency was identified in the SOC-based discharge stop condition,
wherein pre-configuring a discharge SOC cutoff prevented session initiation entirely rather than
governing termination during an active session. This represents a EVSE firmware-level limitation
requiring manufacturer resolution. The manufacturer has confirmed that this issue will be
addressed in an upcoming firmware update.

Examination of protocol message content during discharging revealed that the EVSE relies on an
implementation that departs from DIN 70121:2012 and ISO 15118-2:2018, neither of which
defines any standardized support for bidirectional charging, with reverse power flow achieved
solely through the established voltage gradient between the EVSE and the vehicle battery.
Therefore, tested vehicle platforms were unable to identify active discharge mode, exhibiting
charging behavior throughout V2G sessions. Vehicles that couldn’t initiate a discharge session
responded to discharge mode selection with an EVRESSMalfunction error, triggering immediate
ungraceful session termination.

Smart charge management testing confirmed that the EVSE accurately tracks stepwise power
dispatch profiles. OCPP 1.6j does not natively support bidirectional session control; however, the
EVSE addresses this through a proprietary DataTransfer.req message carrying the
“remoteStartV2X” command for remote discharge session initiation. SetChargingProfile power
limits were applied within the pre-selected operating mode, and mid-transaction mode switching
between charging and discharging was found to be unsupported, requiring a full session stop and
re-initiation to change power flow direction.

Simultaneous dual-port operation was successfully demonstrated under all three configurations,
dual charging, dual discharging, and mixed charging-discharging, confirming V2V power transfer
capability and grid-following behavior under concurrent bidirectional port operation.

The current characterization campaign evaluated the ICE-66 V2X against its DIN 70121 and ISO
15118-2 HLC stack, ISO 15118-20 basic communication capabilities and OCPP 1.6j backend
interface. Several areas warrant further investigation as firmware and protocol support
mature.

ISO 15118-20 Validation: The EVSE is specified to support ISO 15118-20, which defines a
dedicated bidirectional power transfer framework including explicit discharge mode signaling and
V2G-specific session parameters absent in ISO 15118-2. Validation of bidirectional power transfer
setup, discharge parameter negotiation, and schedule exchange under ISO 15118-20 is a critical
next step given the discharge signaling and TLS 1.3 deficiencies identified in the current campaign.

OCPP 2.0.1 and 2.1 Validation: OCPP 2.1 introduces explicit V2G transaction types, discharge
scheduling, and signed meter values for exported energy, capabilities absent in OCPP 1.6j.
Validation against both OCPP 2.0.1 and 2.1 would establish whether the bidirectional control
limitations observed in the current campaign are resolved through the upgraded protocol stack.
