Interaction driven artificial magnetic conductor and defected ground structure integrated microstrip antenna for subsurface communication
Authors: Souvik Halder, Abhijyoti Ghosh, Sushanta Bordoloi, Sudipta Chattopadhyay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Designing compact, high-gain antennas at 400 MHz is challenging due to large size, narrow bandwidth, and low efficiency. This work proposes an interaction-driven Artificial Magnetic Conductor-Defected Ground Structure (AMC-DGS) microstrip antenna for subsurface communication. The design exploits electromagnetic interaction between the slot-type DGS, radiating patch, and AMC surface with spacer layer for performance enhancement. The DGS functions not only for impedance tuning or to attain polarization purity but also as an active radiator, enabling current redistribution and higher order mode perturbation. This interaction, combined with composite superposed mode (CSM) excitation and orthogonal slot radiation, achieves a 400 MHz band (379–419 MHz) with 10.2% bandwidth and 6.5 dBi peak gain. Additionally, a higher-order mode generates a second band at 700 MHz (700–714 MHz) with 2% bandwidth and 6.1 dBi gain. The antenna maintains stable broadside radiation with efficiencies of 98% and 70% at 400 MHz and 700 MHz, respectively. The proposed AMC-DGS antenna provides a compact, dual-band solution for subsurface sensing, Wireless Underground Sensor Networks (WUSN), Internet of Underground Things (IoUT), Ground Penetrating Radar (GPR), and other low-frequency communication systems.

Introduction

With the ubiquitous development of cutting edge wireless technologies, such as IoUT and WUSN, dual band GPR, or supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) and unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV), LoRa applications, low frequency antennas (˂1000 MHz) design with improved impedance bandwidth and high gain performance is a crucial challenge to researchers and scientific communities as they suffers from large size, very poor gain (almost negative) and bandwidths (around 1%)1–7. Yet, for present era of high-speed reliable data communication technologies, the need for broader bandwidth and high gain is imperative. Thus, it demands keen research attention, posing an open challenge to develop low-frequency antennas (< 1000 MHz) that encompass the 400 MHz band; apt for WUSN, IoUT, LoRa, or SCADA applications.

It is very important to note that, the 400 MHz band, apt for long-range reach and deep penetration, is crucial for mission-critical communication, wide-area and next generation IoT, Artificial Intelligence (AI) driven network management. Therefore, the 400 MHz band holds not only profound legacy but also poses boundless future. Unfortunately, till date, hardly the literature is found that yield relatively higher gain (≥ 4dBi) and wider bandwidth performance (≥ 4%) form the planar lowprofile antennas which is thought-provoking to attain high speed data at 400 MHz.

Despite considerable efforts8–15 to develop planar antennas for advanced state-of-the-art applications in the 400 MHz band, recent advancements have only improved the gain from a gain of -13 dBi8 to 3.45 dBi9 with a maximum bandwidth of 3.7%10, still remain inadequate for reliable high-speed communication at present date. Amongst them, exploitation of capacitive strips using inter-digital slots and meander lines achieve 1.9 dBi gain (bandwidth ~ 0.9%)11, while an array of printed folded dipoles with differential feeding yields only − 1 dBi (0.6%)12. Folded dipoles with shorted patches offer 0.4 dBi (bandwidth ~ 3.7%)10 and 1.4 dBi (bandwidth ~ 3.5%)13, whereas meandered designs reach 1.9 dBi (bandwidth ~ 2.1%)14 and 3.1 dBi (bandwidth ~ 2.5%)15. Amid all, the dual probe-fed patch antenna, in both transparent and non-transparent forms, demonstrated relatively better performance with 3.45 dBi gain and 2.3% bandwidth9. Therefore, greater attention from researchers and the scientific community is needed to further explore the 400 MHz band for enabling high-speed data transmission and supporting multichannel full duplex communication for WUSN, IoUT or LoRa applications or in UAV based surveillance.

In addition to relatively wider band coverage of the 400 MHz band, the ability of an antenna to excite another band below 800 MHz offers a significant advantage, as modern Cognitive Radio (CR) systems operating in this range demand both wideband sensing and multiband operation for dynamic channel and spectrum access16. However, the existing literature reveals a persistent gap in achieving improved gain and bandwidth for low-frequency antennas (< 1000 MHz). A configuration employing two U-shaped strip-lines with differential feeding and a quarter-wavelength metallic stub exhibits a single resonance at 924 MHz, achieving a gain of -0.5 dBi with 1.30% bandwidth3. A conventional top-loaded wideband monopole with an L-type slot operates at 800 MHz, yielding 1 dBi gain but suffers from extremely poor impedance matching (0.01% bandwidth)6. A slotted printed monopole with partial ground plane reports a resonance at 743 MHz with 1.3 dBi gain and 1% bandwidth5. A non-resonating ring loaded with dipolar patches shows a resonance near 922 MHz, but with a significantly low gain of − 5.7 dBi and 1.40% bandwidth4. Yet again, the exploitation of concrete-backed ground plane with concrete superstrate loaded rectangular microstrip antenna (RMA)7 reveal very poor gain of − 3 dBi with around 1% bandwidth.

Recent advancements incorporate metamaterial technologies such as artificial magnetic conductors (AMC), offering improved performance; achieving multi-band operation (< 1000 MHz) with maximum 7 dBi gain at 937 MHz17 and 3 dBi at 413 MHz18, both with 1% bandwidth. Furthermore, TE-mode excitation19 in patch-based structures has been explored, demonstrating dual band operation with maximum 4.5 and 6 dBi gain with an expanded bandwidth of 12% at 572 MHz and 768 MHz respectively, albeit with increased structural complexity. In20, a single-band design integrates a AMC array with vias and a partial ground plane into a two-layer meandered-line RMA, achieving 2 dBi gain and 2% bandwidth. Furthermore, some high frequency design techniques21–23 is not applicable in sub-GHz antenna design for achieving higher bandwidth or gain.

To bridge the long-standing gaps in sub-GHz antenna technology; particularly the need for superior performance in the 400 MHz band along with an auxiliary sub-800 MHz resonance; a slot-type DGS integrated RMA24, literature on cross pole minimization of RMA has been revisited. The design elegantly realizes dual resonances at 395 MHz and 707 MHz, achieving bandwidths of 10.2% and 2% with corresponding gains of 6.52dBi and 6.1dBi, while preserving polarization purity exceeding 22dB. The meticulous placement of slot-DGS features, synergistically coupled with the AMC backing, proves pivotal in elevating low-frequency performance. The novelties of the present studies are:

Antenna structure

A 174 mm × 174 mm patch antenna was fabricated on FR4 (εr = 4.4, h = 6 mm) and backed by a 228 mm× 228 mm slotted DGS ground to target 405 MHz. Two longitudinal DGS slots (~ 175 mm × 70 mm) with a g = 40 mm gap was introduced (Fig. 1a), and the antenna is fed 42 mm off-centre for optimal matching. The unit is further backed by a 2 × 2 square-type AMC array, each cell being a 154 mm × 154 mm patch on a 158 mm × 158 mm FR4 substrate tuned to 407 MHz (Fig. 2). A 20 mm air spacer below the ground enables the AMC layer to act as the inner wall of antenna’s back-cover.

Influence of slot-type defected ground structure on the performance of the DGS-integrated RMA without AMC

In conventional microstrip patch antennas, the Defected Ground Structure (DGS) is primarily employed to introduce equivalent inductive and capacitive effects, thereby modifying the current distribution and input impedance characteristics. This enables improved impedance matching, and resonance tuning within the desired frequency range. Additionally, DGS slots are effective in improving polarization purity by altering the non-radiating boundary conditions of the antenna structure.

In contrast, in the proposed work, the DGS is not only utilized for the aforementioned functionalities but also intentionally employed as an active slot radiator, ground plane current perturber. It in fact, also contributes to the formation of a composite superposed mode (CSM), enabling effective radiation pattern reconfiguration depending on frequency of operation.

Excitation and widening of 400 MHz band (f1) using DGS

Initially, DGS slots (Ls × Ws) were placed at the non-radiating edges for polarization purity, as in27. However, their positioning has a notable impact, leading to their lateral shift into the cavity through the ground plane, gradually perturbing the antenna width. The S11 profiles at the excitation frequencies with varying gap g is illustrated in Fig. 3a. With deeper slot penetration (laterally) from the non-radiating edges, a tendency of an additional excitation at 446 MHz alongside the fundamental TM10 mode at f1′ = 405 MHz is noted. Eventually, the slot meets nearly half wave resonant length dimension (along x) and reveals good matching at f1″ =440 MHz (-16 dB) at g = 80 mm. As slots penetrates more, the new excitation f1" (slot resonance) shifts downward from 446 MHz to 425 MHz (where slot resonance shows excellent matching at g = 68 mm), while f1′ slightly increases from 408 MHz to 412 MHz due to the effective reduction of aspect ratio (W/L)25,27. However, these two resonances merge each other, and the eventual excitation frequency becomes 408 MHz (397 MHz-415 MHz) with 4.4% bandwidth. The above discussion is corroborated by the parametric study over multiple g values, with the resulting S11 characteristics and the Smith chart presented in Fig. 3.

Impact of artificial magnetic conductor (AMC) on slot type DGS integrated RMA.

Unlike a balanced printed dipole that uses an AMC as a reflector, an unbalanced microstrip antenna requires an inherent ground plane, typically realized using a PEC or a PEC-backed FSS (AMC). In this work, the AMC is placed beneath the antenna’s PEC ground as an inner surface of the back cover of complete antenna module. While an AMC placed beneath a simple PEC ground plane is generally ineffective, the introduction of slot-type defects creates Huygens-type apertures that facilitate electromagnetic field coupling. With appropriate placement, the AMC interacts with these apertures to direct radiation toward the forward hemisphere, thereby enabling efficient operation across both the 400 MHz and 700 MHz bands.

Augmentation of input and radiation characteristics of 400 MHz band

When an artificial magnetic conductor (AMC) is positioned beneath the defected ground structure (DGS) of the antenna with an intervening air gap (ga), the region between the antenna module and the AMC can be modelled as an effective transmission line. This transmission line behaves as an impedance-transforming section (indeed a resonating element) terminated by a resonant load represented by the AMC. For very small gap values (ga ≈ 1 mm), the electrical length of this transmission line becomes negligible, thereby limiting its impedance transformation capability. In this regime, the AMC, characterized by its inherently inductive behaviour near resonance, dominates the response, resulting in strong magnetic coupling and hence, a highly inductive input impedance is seen by the input port of overall structure. Now, as the gap ga increases, the electrical length becomes significant, enabling the transmission line to effectively participate in impedance transformation. Consequently, the impedance converter (Transmission line; resonating element), when terminated with a resonant load, begins to interact with and influence the overall antenna behaviour. Subsequently, the antenna input impedance decreases and shifts toward a less inductive, more resistive region, as observed from the movement of the locus on the Smith chart toward the resistive axis. At an optimal gap, the partially resonant transmission line and the AMC resonance interact constructively, facilitating the transformation of high impedance to lower impedance. This combined resonance aligns with the inherent antenna resonance, leading to an overall broadened impedance bandwidth and yielding a predominantly resistive input at resonance.

At 400 MHz, the radiating patch, inherently supports broadside radiation but with poor gain. However, it is important to note that the slots in the defected ground also resonates at this frequency and contributes to radiation which is coherent with patch radiation in terms of polarization and planes as described in “Current reconfiguration for polarization-coherent radiation and gain enhancementof 400 MHz band (f1) with help of DGS” section. However, the radiation from the slot is predominantly directed toward the backside of the antenna as the slot radiator is in fact the complimentary dipole antenna. Therefore, to utilize this backward radiation constructively, an artificial magnetic conductor (AMC) layer is employed beneath the DGS. At 400 MHz, the AMC operates close to its inherent resonance and provides in phase reflection. As a result, the backward radiation from the resonant DGS slots and leaked radiation from patch through the defected slots are redirected toward the broadside direction and constructively combines with the patch radiation. Hence, at this frequency, both the patch and the resonant DGS contribute to radiation, while the AMC enhances the broadside gain by suppressing backward radiation.

Evolution of dual AMC (enabled by AMC–DGS–spacer interaction) and its role on the performance improvement at f2 (700 MHz band)

In contrast to 400 MHz (described in previous section), at 700 MHz, the radiation mechanism differs significantly. In this case, the patch is the only the effective radiating element, as the slots in the defected ground does not resonate at this frequency. However, the DGS slots are positioned in a judicious way to evolve CSM mode which can radiate effectively in broadside direction. It has been elaborated in “Radiation pattern engineering enabled by composite mode excitation of f2 (713 MHz-723 MHz band) using DGS” section.

The PVC-enclosed antenna exhibits performance comparable to that of the uncovered configuration. The measured reflection coefficient (S11) and realized gain closely match the uncovered antenna configuration, achieving peak gains of 6.1dBi at 395 MHz and 6.3dBi at 707 MHz, with corresponding impedance bandwidths of 10% and 1.7%, respectively (Fig. 14a). The polarization purity exceeds 23 dB without the enclosure and improves to more than 30 dB with the PVC cover.

For subsurface operation, the simulated S₁₁ characteristics remain consistent with the free-space response, although the upper resonance shifts from 707 MHz to 722 MHz. As expected, gain and radiation characteristics degrade with increasing soil volumetric water content (VWC). In dry soil (VWC ≤ 5%), the gains are 4.8dBi and 3.5dBi at 400 MHz and 700 MHz, respectively. For moist soil (VWC = 10%), the gains decrease to 3.11dBi and 0.7dBi, while in wet soil (VWC = 25%), they further reduce to 1.16dBi and − 3.4dBi for the respective bands (Fig. 14).

Conclusion

This work presents a dual-band low-frequency antenna operating at 400 MHz and 707 MHz, designed to address the longstanding challenges of achieving high gain and wide bandwidth in sub-GHz regimes. The proposed antenna demonstrates a significant performance enhancement, achieving a bandwidth of 10.2% with 6.5 dBi gain and 98% efficiency at 400 MHz, and 2% bandwidth with 6.1 dBi gain and 70% efficiency at 707 MHz. The antenna also maintains stable performance when enclosed with a PVC cover, confirming its robustness for practical deployments. Subsurface analysis shows that the antenna retains impedance characteristics similar to free space, with a slight shift of the upper resonance from 707 MHz to 722 MHz. While gain decreases with increasing soil volumetric water content (VWC), acceptable performance is maintained: 4.8 dBi and 3.5 dBi in dry soil, 3.11 dBi/0.7 dBi at moist soil, and 1.16 dBi/−3.4 dBi at wet soil. Overall, the proposed design demonstrates strong potential for reliable free-space and subsurface communication, making it a promising candidate for applications such as WUSN, IoUT, and underground sensing systems.
