En Route to a Standard 𝖰𝖬𝖠 𝟣 \mathsf{QMA_{1}} vs. 𝖰𝖢𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QCMA} Oracle Separation
David Miloschewsky, Supartha Podder, Dorian Rudolph

Abstract
We study the power of quantum witnesses under perfect completeness. We construct a classical oracle relative to which a language lies in 𝖰𝖬𝖠 𝟣 \mathsf{QMA_{1}} but not in 𝖰𝖢𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QCMA} when the 𝖰𝖢𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QCMA} verifier is only allowed polynomially many adaptive rounds and exponentially many parallel queries per round. Additionally, we derandomize the permutation-oracle separation of Fefferman and Kimmel, obtaining an in-place oracle separation between 𝖰𝖬𝖠 𝟣 \mathsf{QMA_{1}} and 𝖰𝖢𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QCMA} . Furthermore, we focus on 𝖰𝖢𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QCMA} and 𝖰𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QMA} with an exponentially small gap, where we show a separation assuming the gap is fixed, but not when it may be arbitrarily small. Finally, we derive consequences for approximate ground-state preparation from sparse Hamiltonian oracle access, including a bounded adaptivity frustration-free variant.

Introduction

The complexity class
\mathsf{QMA}
(Quantum Merlin-Arthur, a quantum analogue of
\mathsf{NP}
) was formally introduced by Kitaev
[
32
]
as the class of languages for which a polynomial-time quantum verifier can be convinced to accept yes-instances with high probability using a polynomial-size quantum witness, while rejecting no-instances regardless of the witness supplied. A natural variant of
\mathsf{QMA}
, introduced by Aharonov and Naveh
[
4
]
, is
\mathsf{QCMA}
, where the verifier is quantum but the witness is restricted to be classical.

It is immediate that
\mathsf{QCMA}\subseteq\mathsf{QMA}
. A central question in quantum complexity theory is whether this inclusion is strict, i.e., whether quantum witnesses provide additional computational power over classical ones in the non-interactive setting. Despite significant effort, the status of this question in the unrelativized world remains open. Early intuition by Aharonov and Naveh
[
4
]
suggested that quantum witnesses might not be inherently more powerful, for instance, if ground states of local Hamiltonians were to admit efficient classical descriptions, then
\mathsf{QMA}=\mathsf{QCMA}
.

Considerable progress has been made in understanding this question in standard and relativized worlds.
Watrous
[
53
]
showed that the Group Non-Membership problem separates
\mathsf{QMA}
from
\mathsf{MA}
and was one of the first candidates for understanding the power of classical and quantum witnesses
[
1
,
23
]
. Aaronson and Kuperberg
[
1
]
gave the first separation between
\mathsf{QMA}
and
\mathsf{QCMA}
, albeit relative to a quantum oracle. Fefferman and Kimmel
[
20
]
later obtained separations using randomized in-place permutation oracles, and several subsequent works proposed alternative oracle constructions and candidate problems aimed at clarifying the source of quantum advantage
[
40
,
37
,
43
]
. More recent efforts pursued separations in increasingly standard oracle models
[
38
,
46
,
8
,
55
,
39
]
. In parallel, a line of work has investigated this question through the lens of uncloneability
[
47
,
16
,
15
,
18
]
, structural properties of
\mathsf{QMA}
and its variants
[
26
,
19
,
44
]
, as well as in communication complexity
[
49
,
33
,
34
]
. The longstanding question of whether
\mathsf{QMA}
and
\mathsf{QCMA}
can be separated relative to a standard classical oracle was ultimately resolved by
[
12
,
13
]
.

Thus a classical counterpart of the result of Aaronson and Kuperberg
[
1
]
was finally found. However, as noted by
[
12
]
(and also not achieved by
[
13
]
), neither of these classical oracle separations achieve perfect completeness. In contrast,
[
1
]
actually separates the class
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}
from
\mathsf{QCMA}
with respect to a quantum oracle.

\mathsf{QMA}
with perfect completeness.

\mathsf{QMA_{1}}
is the variant of
\mathsf{QMA}
in which the verifier must accept every YES instance with probability
exactly

1
(while still accepting NO instances with probability at most
1/2
). The class was formalized in
[
30
]
and gained prominence through the
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}
-completeness of Quantum
k
-SAT
[
14
,
25
]
. Whether
\mathsf{QMA}=\mathsf{QMA_{1}}
remains open; Aaronson
[
3
]
gave a quantum oracle separation, and the answer is known to depend delicately on the verifier’s gate set. Amplification and completeness techniques for one-sided-error
\mathsf{QMA}
have been developed in
[
45
,
35
]
. In contrast, the classical-witness analogue satisfies
\mathsf{QCMA}=\mathsf{QCMA}_{1}
under standard gate sets
[
36
]
. This leaves open the following natural question.

Question 1.1
.

Is there a
standard classical
oracle separation between
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}
and
\mathsf{QCMA}
?

As our first result, we give a bounded adaptivity analog of
1.1
.

Result 1.2

(
Theorem

3.12
)
.

For every polynomial
R
, there exists a classical oracle relative to which

\mathsf{QMA_{1}}\not\subseteq\mathsf{QCMA}
, assuming the
\mathsf{QCMA}
verifier is restricted to
R
rounds of adaptive queries. Additionally, the language is in
\mathsf{coRP}
with
R+1
adaptive one-query rounds.

By bounded adaptivity, we mean that the oracle calls are grouped into rounds. Each round of queries is performed in parallel and may only depend on the results of previous queries. Therefore, the restriction is on the depth of the oracle dependence, not on the total number of queries. In
Result

1.2
, the
\mathsf{QCMA}
lower bound applies even when each round makes exponentially many queries. The regime described above is the same as in
[
8
]
, who separated
\mathsf{QCMA}
from
\mathsf{QMA}
(but not
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}
) with
o(\log n/\log\log n)
adaptive rounds. In contrast,
Theorem

3.12
separates
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}
from
\mathsf{QCMA}
bounded to
R
rounds, where
R
is any fixed polynomial. On the other hand, the bounded round restriction is necessary in our problem as a
\mathsf{coRP}
algorithm solves the problem with
R+1
rounds, while the problem in
[
8
]
was used by
[
13
]
for the classical oracle separation.

In-place oracle separation.

As discussed before, in the permutation oracle model, Fefferman and Kimmel
[
20
]
separated
\mathsf{QMA}
from
\mathsf{QCMA}
using
randomized
in-place permutation oracles. Despite the extensive line of work on the
\mathsf{QMA}
versus
\mathsf{QCMA}
question that followed
[
38
,
46
,
8
,
55
,
39
,
12
,
13
]
, derandomizing the
[
20
]
construction has remained elusive, and none of these works achieve perfect completeness in the in-place model. Furthermore, the in-place oracle model has been studied in other contexts as well
[
2
,
29
,
7
,
28
]
. This motivates the following natural question.

Question 1.3
.

Can randomization be removed from the in-place oracle separation of Fefferman and Kimmel
[
20
]
, and can the separation be strengthened to
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}
versus
\mathsf{QCMA}
?

We answer both parts affirmatively.

Result 1.4

(
Theorem

3.9
)
.

There exists a deterministic in-place permutation oracle relative to which
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}\not\subseteq\mathsf{QCMA}
, even when the
\mathsf{QCMA}
verifier is allowed
2^{o(n)}
rounds of adaptivity and exponentially many queries per round.

Viewed as a query problem,
Result

1.4
is a set-preimage sampling lower bound. Formally defined in
3.1
, the problem is to estimate the parity bias of the preimage set
S_{b}=\pi^{-1}([N])
where
\pi
is the given permutation. The
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}
witness is simply
\ket{S_b}
. Without a witness, one may sample
S_{b}
by drawing some
j\in[N]
and applying
\pi
for
b-1
steps. This differs from the standard permutation inversion problem, where given some
y
, one must find
\pi^{-1}(y)
, which has query complexity
\Theta(\sqrt{N})
both in the standard XOR and in-place oracle models
[
27
,
9
,
20
,
28
]
. Thus
Result

1.4
shows that even when given a
classical witness
, the simple
b-1
round sampling algorithm is optimal.

Witness verification with an exponentially small gap.

A natural question in the study of verification classes with error is how they behave under various completeness-soundness gaps. For
\mathsf{QMA}
and
\mathsf{QCMA}
, standard error-reduction
[
41
]
shows that any inverse-polynomial gap can be amplified to a constant, meaning the specific choice of parameters is irrelevant. However, when the gap is allowed to shrink to
2^{-\mathrm{poly}(n)}
, no error-reduction techniques are known. The resulting classes
\mathsf{PreciseQMA}
and
\mathsf{PreciseQCMA}
have been characterized as
\mathsf{PSPACE}

[
21
]
and
\mathsf{NP}^{\mathsf{PP}}

[
42
,
24
]
, respectively.

Question 1.5
.

Is there a classical oracle separating
\mathsf{QMA}
from
\mathsf{QCMA}
when the completeness-soundness gap is exponentially small?

We partially answer this question. Specifically, by tweaking the original proof technique of
[
13
]
, we show that when the gap is fixed to
O(2^{-n})
, the classes may be separated. However,
\mathsf{PreciseQCMA}
, which considers the union over all
2^{-\mathrm{poly}(n)}
gaps, solves the problem.

Result 1.6

(
Theorem

3.20
)
.

There exists a classical oracle relative to which a problem lies in
\mathsf{QMA}(2^{-n})
and
\mathsf{PreciseQCMA}
, but not in
\mathsf{QCMA}(2^{-n})
.

The
\mathsf{PreciseQCMA}
inclusion holds due to the fact that the problem is in
\mathsf{\Pi_{2}^{P}}
, which is in
\mathsf{NP}^{\mathsf{PP}}
due to Toda’s theorem
[
52
]
. In particular, this provides evidence against an efficient gap amplification procedure for
\mathsf{PreciseQMA}
and
\mathsf{PreciseQCMA}
.

Circuit complexity of sparse Hamiltonian ground states.

The No Low-Energy Trivial States (NLTS) theorem of Anshu, Breuckmann, and Nirkhe
[
5
]
, resolving a conjecture of Freedman and Hastings
[
22
]
, establishes that there exist families of local Hamiltonians whose low-energy states cannot be prepared by constant-depth quantum circuits. In an oracle setting, one can ask whether sparse Hamiltonians given via Hamiltonian query access
O_{H}^{\mathrm{rc}}
have approximate ground states that provably require large circuits to prepare.

Question 1.7
.

Are there families of sparse Hamiltonians, given via oracle access, whose approximate ground states provably require large circuits to prepare?

A natural question in a similar spirit is whether the oracle separations between
\mathsf{QMA}
and
\mathsf{QCMA}
yield circuit complexity lower bounds for ground states of sparse Hamiltonians given via Hamiltonian query access
O_{H}^{\mathrm{rc}}
. To the best of our knowledge, this connection has not been made explicitly in prior work, although it follows relatively directly from applying the circuit-to-Hamiltonian construction
[
30
]
to the
\mathsf{QMA}
verifiers in
[
12
,
13
]
.

Result 1.8

(
Theorem

3.22
)
.

There exists a family of
O(1)
-sparse Hamiltonians
\{H_{i}\}
on
n_{i}
qubits such that any state preparable by a circuit of size
2^{o(n_{i}^{1/3}/\log n_{i})}
which has access to
H
via the oracle
O_{H_{i}}^{\mathrm{rc}}
has energy at least
\lambda_{0}(H_{i})+1/\mathrm{poly}(n_{i})
.

Furthermore, using
Result

1.2
, we are able to extend this to frustration-free Hamiltonians, except the circuit is restricted in adaptivity (see
Theorem

3.24
). Note that extending this without the restriction would resolve
1.1
.

Techniques.

The problem used to obtain
Result

1.4
is a pointer-chasing permutation oracle instance. The permutation consists of disjoint chains through buckets
I_{1},\dots I_{b}
with the first bucket being
[N]
and the last bucket mapping to
[N]
. The problem is to decide whether parity of the items in
I_{b}
which map to
[N]
is
0
or
1
, given the promise that it is either
0
in all items, or only over half of them (for a formal definition, see
3.1
). In a YES instance, the
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}
witness is the uniform state on
S_{b}=\pi^{-1}([N])
, which is used to test the parity of
S_{b}
and whether it maps back to
[N]
, both of which are checked with perfect completeness.

The
\mathsf{QCMA}
lower bound is as follows. Given a fixed verifier and classical witness, we record a transcript
\mathcal{T}^{(r)}
of all the points of the oracle which the algorithm “knows” after
r
adaptive rounds of queries. Each round,
\mathcal{T}^{(r)}
may only add points by either querying them with heavy weight or from the previous step in the chain. We describe a canonical permutation
\pi^{\prime}
based on
\mathcal{T}^{(r)}
and show that the verifier’s state using
\pi
is close to that with
\pi^{\prime}
. Assuming
\mathcal{T}^{(r)}
satisfies several conditions which we call “good”, which occurs with high probability, each YES instance may be transformed into a NO instance with the same canonical permutation by altering the last bucket
I_{b}
. A union bound over witnesses and diagonalization finishes the lower bound.

Result

1.2
uses the tools developed above. First, the
\mathsf{QCMA}
lower bound applies using the standard XOR oracle. Second, the
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}
algorithm is obtained by considering the algorithm which uses
R+1
rounds of queries and parallelizing it using the Feynman-Kitaev history state. As we show, any
R
-round
\mathsf{QMA}
(and
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}
) algorithm may be parallelized to a single-round verifier with
O(R^{4})
parallel oracle queries.

We note that the oracle separations underlying
Results

1.2
and
1.4
were developed independently of the two recent classical-oracle separations between
\mathsf{QMA}
and

\mathsf{QCMA}

[
12
,
13
]
. On the other hand, the results in
Section

3.3
directly use the machinery developed in
[
13
]
. In
Result

1.6
, we solely modify the bias parameter. Furthermore,
Result

1.8
is obtained by applying the circuit-to-Hamiltonian construction
[
30
]
to the
\mathsf{QMA}
verifier in
[
13
]
and using sparse-Hamiltonian energy estimation. In the frustration-free case, we use the
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}
verifier for
3.1
.

Preliminaries

We assume the reader is familiar with standard quantum computing and complexity theory background. For introductions, see
[
48
,
6
]
.
We use the following notation. We use
[a]
to denote the set
\{1,\dots a\}
and
[a,b]
to denote
\{a,a+1,\dots,b\}
. Given a set of integers
S
, let
S^{\textup{even}}
and
S^{\textup{odd}}
denote the subset of
S
which only contains its even or odd elements, respectively. For any finite non-empty set
S
, we let

\displaystyle\ket{S}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\absolutevalue{S}}}\sum_{s\in S}\ket{s}.

Given a matrix
H
, we let
\lambda_{0}(H)
denote the smallest eigenvalue of
H
.
Let us describe the oracle models we will use. For a string
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, the standard XOR oracle is,

\displaystyle O_{x}\ket{i,b}\coloneq\ket{i, b\oplus x_i},

where
i\in[n]
and
b\in\{0,1\}
. We call this a
classical oracle
as, when
x
represents a function
f:\{0,1\}^{\log n}\to\{0,1\}
,
O_{x}
is the associated unitary applied.
A
forward in-place oracle
for a permutation
\pi
is the oracle
O_{\pi}
defined as,

\displaystyle O_{\pi}\ket{x}=\ket{\pi(x)}.

We emphasize that we do not provide access to the inverse of
O_{\pi}
. Lastly, we will use the Hamiltonian query model. We say a Hamiltonian
H
is
d
-
sparse
if for each row, there are at most
d
non-zero terms, while
H=\sum_{i}H_{i}
is
frustration-free
if
H_{i}\succeq 0
and
\lambda_{0}(H)=0
.
Consider a
d
-sparse Hamiltonian
H\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
. Let
i,j\in[M]
,
k\in[d]
and
f(i,k)
be the
k
th column index of a non-zero term in row
i
of
H
. The
O_{H}
and
O_{f}
oracles are defined as,

\displaystyle O_{H}\ket{i,j,z}

\displaystyle\coloneq\ket{i,j,z\oplus H_{i,j}}

\displaystyle O_{f}\ket{i,k}

\displaystyle\coloneq\ket{i,f(i,k)}.

We call the pair
O_{H}
and
O_{f}
the row-column oracle gates
O_{H}^{\mathrm{rc}}
.

An algorithm
A
with
R
adaptive rounds of
q
queries to an arbitrary oracle
O
is a sequence of unitaries
A=(U_{0},U_{1},\dots,U_{R})
interlaced with parallel calls to
O
. On some input
\ket{\psi}
it behaves as,

\displaystyle A\ket{\psi}\coloneq U_{R}O^{\otimes q}U_{R-1}O^{\otimes q}\dots U_{1}O^{\otimes q}U_{0}.

2.1
Complexity Classes

Definition 2.1

(Variants of
\mathsf{BQP}
)
.

A language
L\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}
is in
\mathsf{BQP}(c,s)
where
c\geq s
if there exists a uniform polynomial-time family of quantum circuits
Q=\{Q_{n}\}
such that,

\displaystyle x\in L

\displaystyle\implies\Pr[Q(x)=1]\geq c

\displaystyle x\not\in L

\displaystyle\implies\Pr[Q(x)=1]\leq s.

We define
\mathsf{BQP}=\mathsf{BQP}(\tfrac{2}{3},\tfrac{1}{3})
,
\mathsf{RQP}=\mathsf{BQP}(\tfrac{1}{2},0)
and
\mathsf{ZQP}=\mathsf{RQP}\cap\mathsf{coRQP}
.

Equivalently,
\mathsf{ZQP}
is the class of zero-error quantum algorithms which may output
1,0
or “maybe”, where the probability of “maybe” is at most
\tfrac{1}{2}
and the algorithm never answers incorrectly.
Lastly,
\mathsf{RP}
is defined analogously to
\mathsf{RQP}
, with the quantum circuit replaced by a probabilistic Turing machine.

Definition 2.2

(Witness classes)
.

A language
L\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}
is in
\mathsf{QMA}(c,s)
where
c\geq s
if there exist a uniform polynomial-time quantum verifier
V
and a polynomial
p
such that for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{*}
,

\displaystyle x\in L

\displaystyle\implies\exists\ket{\psi}\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes p(\absolutevalue{x})}\colon\Pr\left[V(x,\ket{\psi})=1\right]\geq c

\displaystyle x\notin L

\displaystyle\implies\forall\ket{\psi}\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes p(\absolutevalue{x})}\colon\Pr\left[V(x,\ket{\psi})=1\right]\leq s.

\mathsf{QCMA}(c,s)
is the restriction of
\mathsf{QMA}(c,s)
in which the witness is a classical string
y\in\{0,1\}^{p(\absolutevalue{x})}
.
We define the following classes,

\displaystyle\mathsf{QMA}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{c-s\geq\tfrac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)}}\mathsf{QMA}(c,s),

\displaystyle\mathsf{QCMA}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{c-s\geq\tfrac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)}}\mathsf{QCMA}(c,s),

\displaystyle\mathsf{QMA_{1}}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{s\leq 1-\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)}}\mathsf{QMA}(1,s),

\displaystyle\mathsf{PreciseQMA}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{c-s\geq 2^{-\mathrm{poly}(n)}}\mathsf{QMA}(c,s),

\displaystyle\mathsf{PreciseQCMA}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{c-s\geq 2^{-\mathrm{poly}(n)}}\mathsf{QCMA}(c,s),

where the parameters
c,s
are functions of the input length.

By standard completion and soundness gap amplification techniques
[
41
]
,
\mathsf{QMA}=\mathsf{QMA}(\tfrac{2}{3},\tfrac{1}{3})
,
\mathsf{QCMA}=\mathsf{QCMA}(\tfrac{2}{3},\tfrac{1}{3})
and
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}=\mathsf{QMA}(1,\tfrac{1}{2})
. However, the same techniques are not known to apply to
\mathsf{PreciseQMA}
and
\mathsf{PreciseQCMA}
.

Definition 2.3

(Bounded adaptivity oracle classes)
.

Let
O=\{O_{n}\}_{n}
be a family of oracles and
R,q\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be query bounds. A language
L\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}
is in
\mathsf{QMA}^{O}[R,q]
if there exist a uniform quantum verifier
V
and a polynomial
p
such that
V
makes at most
R(\absolutevalue{x})
adaptive rounds of
q(\absolutevalue{x})
parallel queries to
O
and for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{*}
,

\displaystyle x\in L

\displaystyle\implies\exists\ket{\psi}\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes p(\absolutevalue{x})}\colon\Pr\left[V^{O}(x,\ket{\psi})=1\right]\geq\frac{2}{3},

\displaystyle x\notin L

\displaystyle\implies\forall\ket{\psi}\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes p(\absolutevalue{x})}\colon\Pr\left[V^{O}(x,\ket{\psi})=1\right]\leq\frac{1}{3}.

The classes
\mathsf{QCMA}^{O}[R,q]
and
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}^{O}[R,q]
are defined analogously.

Definition 2.4

(Advice classes)
.

A language
L\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}
is in
\mathsf{BQP}_{\!\!/\mathsf{qpoly}}
if there is a polynomial-time family of quantum circuits
Q=\{Q_{n}\}
, a polynomial
p(n)
and a family of quantum states
\{\ket{\psi_n}\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes p(n)}\}
such that,

\displaystyle x\in L

\displaystyle\implies\Pr[Q(x,\ket{\psi_{\abs{x}}})=1]\geq\frac{2}{3},

\displaystyle x\notin L

\displaystyle\implies\Pr[Q(x,\ket{\psi_{\abs{x}}})=1]\leq\frac{1}{3}.

\mathsf{BQP}_{\!\!/\mathsf{poly}}
is defined analogously, except the advice states
\ket{\psi_n}
are replaced by classical strings
y\in\{0,1\}^{p(n)}
. Similarly,
\mathsf{ZQP}_{\!\!/\mathsf{qpoly}}
is defined as
\mathsf{BQP}_{\!\!/\mathsf{qpoly}}
with the zero-error semantics of
\mathsf{ZQP}
.

We will use a parallel-query hybrid method

Lemma 2.5

(Parallel-query hybrid method)
.

Let
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
be permutations over
[M]
,
\ket{\phi}
an arbitrary state over
q
query registers,
\Pi
be projector
\Pi\coloneq\sum_{x:\pi(x)\neq\pi^{\prime}(x)}\outerproduct{x}{x}
and
\Pi_{j}
be
\Pi
over the query register
j\in[q]
. Furthermore, define
p_{j}=\bra{\phi}\Pi_{j}\ket{\phi}
. Then,

\displaystyle\norm{\left(O_\pi^{\otimes q} - O_{\pi^\prime}^{\otimes q}\right)\ket{\phi}}\leq 2\sqrt{\sum_{j\in[q]}p_{j}}.

Proof.

Let
\Pi^{\prime}\coloneq I-\prod_{j=1}^{q}(I-\Pi_{j})
. A basis state lies in
\ker(\Pi^{\prime})
if and only if all query inputs agree between
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left(O_{\pi}^{\otimes q}-O_{\pi^{\prime}}^{\otimes q}\right)\ket{\phi}=\left(O_{\pi}^{\otimes q}-O_{\pi^{\prime}}^{\otimes q}\right)(\Pi^{\prime}+(I-\Pi^{\prime}))\ket{\phi}=\left(O_{\pi}^{\otimes q}-O_{\pi^{\prime}}^{\otimes q}\right)\Pi^{\prime}\ket{\phi},

as
(I-\Pi^{\prime})\ket{\phi}
lies in
\ker(\Pi^{\prime})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\norm{\left(O_\pi^{\otimes q} - O_{\pi^\prime}^{\otimes q}\right)\ket{\phi}}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{O_\pi^{\otimes q} - O_{\pi^\prime}^{\otimes q}}\norm{\Pi^\prime\ket{\phi}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\bra{\phi}\Pi^{\prime}\ket{\phi}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\sum_{j\in[q]}p_{j}},

where the last inequality is an application of the union bound.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
U
be a finite set such that
\absolutevalue{U}=B
and let
S\subset U
be uniformly random subset of size
N
. Letting
\mu
be an arbitrary distribution on
U
and
H=\{x\in U:\mu(x)\geq\epsilon/N\}

\displaystyle\coloneq D_{i}^{(r-1)}\cup G_{i}^{(r)}\cup P_{i}^{(r)}\cup\pi\!\bigl(Q_{i-1}^{(r)}\bigr)

(9)

\displaystyle U_{i}^{(r)}

\displaystyle\coloneq U_{i}^{(r-1)}\setminus\bigl(H_{i}^{(r)}\cup D_{i}^{(r)}\bigr).

(10)

Note that for

.
One may see that the condition in

.
In summary, the order of operations is as follows. Given some canonical state
\ket{\psi_{r-1}^\prime}
, we obtain the update probabilities. In turn, this updates the transcript

, whose commited values in

are based on the original permutation

Next, we will show that two oracles with the same transcript up to the step

are difficult to distinguish.

Claim 3.6

(Transcript indistinguishability)
.

For any pair of oracles
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
consistent with the same transcript history
\mathcal{T}^{(t)}
for
t\in[r]
,

\displaystyle\norm{\ket{\psi_r^{\pi_1}} - \ket{\psi_r^{\pi_2}}}\leq 4r\sqrt{q\epsilon}.

Proof.

To show the statement, it suffices to show that for any permutation
\sigma
consistent with
\mathcal{T}^{(r)}
,

\displaystyle\norm{\ket{\psi_r^{\sigma}} - \ket{\psi_r^{\prime}}}\leq 2r\sqrt{q\epsilon},

(11)

as applying
Equation

11
to
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
and using the triangle inequality gives the claim. We prove
Equation

11
by induction on
t\in[0,r]
. The base case

is immediate since the witness and verifier are fixed. For the inductive step,

\displaystyle\norm{\ket{\psi_t^{\sigma}} - \ket{\psi_t^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\norm{O_{\sigma}^{\otimes q} \ket{\psi_{t-1}^{\sigma}} - O_{\tilde{\pi}_t}^{\otimes q}\ket{\psi_{t-1}^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\ket{\psi_{t-1}^{\sigma}} - \ket{\psi_{t-1}^{\prime}}}+\norm{(O_{\sigma}^{\otimes q} - O_{\tilde{\pi}_t}^{\otimes q}) \ket{\psi_{t-1}^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2(t-1)\sqrt{q\epsilon}+\norm{(O_{\sigma}^{\otimes q} - O_{\tilde{\pi}_t}^{\otimes q}) \ket{\psi_{t-1}^{\prime}}},

(12)

where the equality follows due to unitarity of
U_{t}
and the inequality is an application of the triangle inequality and inserting
O_{\sigma}^{\otimes q}\ket{\psi_{t-1}^\prime}
.

We claim that
\sigma
and
\tilde{\pi}_{t}
may disagree only on
\bigcup_{i}(S_{i}^{(t)}\setminus H_{i}^{(t)})
. This can be shown case by case. First, if

, both oracles are the identity. Let us check each part of the partition of

in
Equation

6
,

1.

If

, then by

7
both
\sigma
and

agree with

.

2.

If

, then

. This is due to the fact that since

.

3.

If

, then
\sigma
and
\tilde{\pi}_{t}
may only disagree on

as all other points are self-loops. As

,

and

If

\pi(x)=x=\tilde{\pi}_{r}(x)
.

In all cases, potential disagreement is contained in

be the probability that the

and using the fact that each

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{(t)}(S_{i}^{(t)}\setminus H_{i}^{(t)})\leq\absolutevalue{S_i^{(t)}}\cdot\frac{\epsilon}{N}\leq\epsilon,

we have that,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[q]}p_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[b]}p_{i}^{(t)}\mu_{i}^{(t)}(S_{i}^{(t)}\setminus H_{i}^{(t)})

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\sum_{i\in[b]}p_{i}^{(t)}\leq\epsilon q.

By

2.5

is bounded by

∎

Let

\displaystyle\forall i\in[r+2,b]:\absolutevalue*{D_i^{(r)}}\leq 4rb\delta N

Claim 3.7

(Probability of a bad transcript)

Assume

(13)

Proof.

We prove this by induction on

. The base case holds as, except for

,

. For the inductive step, assume that

is good and fix some

of size

even elements and at most

total have been removed,

(as the verifier and witness are fixed),

is stochastically dominated by a uniformly-chosen set of size

from a universe of size at least

. By

,

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\absolutevalue{G_i^{(r)}}\geq\delta N\mid\mathcal{T}^{(r-1)}\right]\leq\left(\frac{4eN}{\delta\epsilon B}\right)^{\delta N}.

(14)

Let

denote the event that for all

and

\absolutevalue{G_i^{(t)}}<\delta N
.
Next, we show that if

is good. Let,

\displaystyle J^{(r)}\coloneq\{j\in[N]:\exists t\leq r,k>t\text{ s.t. }f_{k}(j)\in G_{k}^{(t)}\},

denote the indices of

which were guessed earlier than expected. Under the assumption on

, we have that
\absolutevalue{J^{(r)}}\leq rb\delta N
. We claim that every point in

with

and
Q_{i}^{(r)}
with

has a label in

. We prove this by induction on

. The claim is immediate for

. For the inductive step, consider

with

\displaystyle Q_{i}^{(r)}\subseteq Q_{i}^{(r-1)}\cup D_{i}^{(r-1)}\cup G_{i}^{(r)}\cup P_{i}^{(r)}.

By the inductive hypothesis, the terms

and

have labels in

. The terms

have labels in

and

All that remains is bounding the probability of event

\displaystyle\Pr_{\pi\sim D_{n}^{\textup{YES}}}\left[\mathcal{T}^{(r)}\text{ is bad}\right]\leq rb\left(\frac{4eN}{\delta\epsilon B}\right)^{\delta N}.

Lastly, let us show that for any YES instance, we may create a NO instance whose state is extremely close to that of the YES instance.

Claim 3.8

(NO-instance transformation)

Suppose that for all

\mathcal{T}^{(r)}
is good for
\pi\in\mathrm{supp}(D_{n}^{\textup{YES}})

We may entirely describe the permutation
\pi
using

. Notice that by definition,

\pi\in\mathrm{supp}(D_{n}^{\textup{YES}})

As

\absolutevalue{J_{\textup{fix}}}\leq 8Rb\delta N\leq\tfrac{N}{4}

or

Choose

and bijectively assigning the remaining indices to

(feasible since

). We define
\pi_{\textup{NO}}

.

We claim that

generates the identical transcript to

through every round

. Since

and

Lemma 3.11

For any unitary

,
\mathsf{QMA}^{O}=\mathsf{QMA}^{\|O}
and
\mathsf{QMA_{1}}^{O}=\mathsf{QMA_{1}}^{\|O}
. In terms of the number of rounds,
\mathsf{QMA}^{O}[R,1]\subseteq\mathsf{QMA}^{O}[1,O(R^{4})]

\mathsf{QMA_{1}}^{O}[R,1]\subseteq\mathsf{QMA_{1}}^{O}[1,O(R^{4})]

Proof.

Let us first consider statement for
\mathsf{QMA}
. Consider some language
L\in\mathsf{QMA}^{O}[R,1]
and for any
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, let
V
be the verification circuit,

\displaystyle V=U_{R+1}OU_{R}\dots OU_{1}\ket{\phi_x}\ket{0}=U_{L}U_{L-1}\dots U_{2}U_{1}\ket{\phi_x}\ket{0},

where
L=2R+1
, for each even
i\in[L]

U_{i}
is an oracle call and
\ket{\phi_x}
is the witness.
Recalling Kitaev’s circuit-to-Hamiltonian construction
[
30
]
, we may map
V
to some Hamiltonian
H=H_{\mathrm{in}}+H_{\mathrm{prop}}+H_{\mathrm{out}}
whose ground states are of the form,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L+1}}\sum_{t\in[0,L]}\left(U_{t}\dots U_{0}\ket{\phi_x}\ket{0}\right)\otimes\ket{t}.

Notice that the
H_{\mathrm{in}}
term only checks whether the ancilla register is
\ket{0}
, but not the witness
\ket{\phi_x}
and this instance of
H
is no longer local. Nonetheless, this does not matter as with access to
O
, we may approximate its ground state energy. The main difference from
[
30
]
is in the following term,

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{prop}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{L}H_{t}

(19)

\displaystyle 2H_{t}

\displaystyle=I\otimes\bigl(\outerproduct{t}{t}+\outerproduct{t-1}{t-1}\bigr)-U_{t}\otimes\outerproduct{t}{t-1}-U_{t}^{\dagger}\outerproduct{t-1}{t}.

(20)

We note that
H_{t}
is a projector on the span of states
\ket{\psi}\ket{t-1}-(U_{t}\ket{\psi})\ket{t}
. Therefore, for a
d
-dimensional
U
,

\displaystyle H_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\bigl(\ket{j}\ket{t-1}-(U_{t}\ket{j})\ket{t}\bigr)\bigl(\bra{j}\bra{t-1}-(\bra{j}U_{t}^{\dagger})\bra{t}\bigr).

To measure with regard to projector
H_{t}
, we may perform the following change of basis,

\displaystyle U_{t}^{\prime}=U_{t}\otimes\outerproduct{t-1}{t-1}+I\otimes\outerproduct{t}{t},

(21)

as then,

\displaystyle 2U_{t}^{\prime}H_{t}(U_{t}^{\prime})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\bigl((U_{t}\ket{j})\ket{t-1}-(U_{t}\ket{j})\ket{t}\bigr)\bigl((\bra{j}U_{t}^{\dagger})\bra{t-1}-(\bra{j}U_{t}^{\dagger})\bra{t}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}U_{t}\outerproduct{j}{j}U_{t}^{\dagger}\otimes\bigl(\ket{t-1}-\ket{t}\bigr)\bigl(\bra{t-1}-\bra{t}\bigr)

\displaystyle=I\otimes\bigl(\ket{t-1}-\ket{t}\bigr)\bigl(\bra{t-1}-\bra{t}\bigr),

which is an
X
-basis measurement on the
\{\ket{t-1},\ket{t}\}
subspace of the clock register. By

2.7
and
2.8
,
U_{t}^{\prime}
may be applied using a single application of
U_{t}
.

The remainder of the analysis is standard. Letting
\tau
be the test where we check a term of
H_{t}
uniformly at random, we have that,

\displaystyle\Pr[\tau\text{ accepts}|x\in L]\geq 1-\frac{\tfrac{1}{3}}{(L+2)^{2}}

(22)

\displaystyle\Pr[\tau\text{ accepts}|x\notin L]\leq 1-\frac{c}{(L+2)^{4}},

where
