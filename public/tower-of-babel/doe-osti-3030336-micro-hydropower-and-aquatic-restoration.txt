 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Rural and Tribal communities in Southern Appalachia face persistent challenges in energy access owing
to rugged terrain, dense forest cover, and limited infrastructure. The Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians
(EBCI) is a federally recognized Tribe whose ancestral homelands include much of Southern Appalachia.
The Tribe’s present-day homeland, termed the Qualla Boundary, lies in the heart of the region, where the
extreme ruggedness of the Blue Ridge Mountains exacerbates challenges to energy access. For the EBCI,
these challenges also intersect with a deep cultural commitment to stewardship of land and water. The
Tribe has explored multiple energy solutions, with hydropower serving as an existing and realized source
of energy for the region’s communities. However, the EBCI’s commitment to protecting aquatic systems
has led not only to a historical lack of endorsement of new conventional hydropower on Tribal lands but
also to ongoing efforts to remove the area’s only existing hydropower facility at Ela Dam.

The Oconaluftee River Basin sustains ecological diversity and is deeply tied to cultural expression and
spiritual well-being for the Tribe. To address energy access while preserving the integrity of the Long
Person, or river system, the EBCI and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) partnered in 2022 to
evaluate “fish-friendly” micro-hydropower options (≤100 kW) that align with Tribal priorities for
restoration and energy sovereignty. The effort, funded by the U.S. Department of Energy’s Water Power
Technologies Office (WPTO), was aimed at assessing the feasibility of micro-hydropower throughout the
current homelands of the EBCI and optimizing generation potential relative to aquatic connectivity and
cultural values.

Southern Appalachia’s high-gradient streams are well-suited to distributed micro-hydropower. Micro-
hydropower generators are typically defined as generating < 100 kW and are available as both engineered
designs and as commercially available, modular systems with a small environmental footprint. These
systems can enhance energy reliability and resilience for remote communities by providing simpler
installation and maintenance costs and by remaining compatible with watershed restoration goals. Such
projects typically fall within the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s 10-MW exemption pathway
for small and low-impact hydropower and, when implemented as damless, run-of-the-river designs, can
minimize ecological impacts while preserving aquatic connectivity and cultural values.

Brook trout (Salvelinus fontinalis) was selected as a focal species for evaluating interactions between
micro-hydropower siting, watershed-level connectivity, and Tribal restoration priorities, owing to its
economic, ecological, cultural importance to the Tribe. The species is representative of Southern
Appalachia’s high-elevation headwater streams, where micro-hydropower development might be most
valuable to remote mountain communities and individual households. At the same time, the species is
broadly distributed throughout the entire Qualla Boundary, which permitted an assessment of connectivity
patterns across the Oconaluftee River Basin. High-resolution hydrologic and riparian habitat datasets
were developed from LiDAR data and remote sensing and informed partly by existing open-source
hydrological data and models for the region. All perennial and ephemeral streams within the Oconaluftee
River Basin were mapped at < 1 m resolution, and hydropower potential and barrier strength was assessed
at over 1,500 potential barriers locations. Barriers were assigned a strength score according to several
criteria expected to influence brook trout passage, such as presence and type of a physical structure,
degree of perch or drop for culvert barriers, flow velocity, substrate continuity, and surrounding riparian
conditions that affect temperature and shade. Ultimately, 533 barriers were evaluated for both
hydropower potential and restoration value, defined as the potential increase in watershed-level
connectivity if a given barrier were restored or replaced by a damless, micro-hydropower system.

A combined electricity generation potential of 22.6 MW was estimated across all identified barriers.
These ranged from < 1 kW at small headwater sites to > 1 MW at Ela Dam—the only powered facility
associated with the Qualla and a major barrier to aquatic passage into the upper Oconaluftee River Basin.

Connectivity simulations further demonstrated that removal or conversion of select barriers could
substantially enhance watershed-scale restoration outcomes while also providing distributed, low-impact
energy for remote households and facilities, even at high-elevation headwater sites where generation
potentials sometimes exceeded the defined limits of micro-hydropower (100 kW). Multiple locations
associated with the Tribal [Natural] Reserve were identified as high-priority sites where future restoration
efforts could help restore brook trout (and additional aquatic fauna) to historically occupied tributaries,
thereby enhancing ecological value and opportunities for recreation and cultural expression for the Tribe.

Through an objective-setting framework co-developed with the Tribe, this work ensured that all project
aims, datasets, and analyses were shaped by Tribal input and conducted under principles that protect
Tribal knowledge, data sensitivity, and decision-making authority. The framework establishes a replicable
model for integrating hydropower feasibility, ecological restoration, and cultural stewardship in Tribal
and mountain communities while respecting local knowledge and data sensitivities. The EBCI–ORNL
partnership now provides a pathway to advance site-specific validation and adaptive modeling under
future scenarios. The EBCI has been provided all data products to assist in future development of fish-
friendly micro-hydropower systems tailored to Southern Appalachia’s diverse landscape. Overall, the
work offers a practical path toward fish-friendly hydropower development that supports Tribal energy
independence while enhancing watershed connectivity and ecosystem health.

 1. BACKGROUND

1.1 ENERGY ACCESS CHALLENGES IN SOUTHERN APPALACHIA

Rural and Tribal communities in the Appalachian Mountains face growing challenges in securing reliable,
affordable energy. Rugged terrain, dense forest cover, and limited infrastructure access make energy
distribution difficult to establish and maintain. Where grid access does exist, power outages tend to be
more frequent, prolonged, and unevenly restored (Sieber et al. 2020, Do et al. 2023, Sovacool et al. 2024).
Opportunities to develop more resilient wind and solar facilities remain (Mainzer et al. 2019, Browder et
al. 2024), but they are especially limited in Southern Appalachia, where the Blue Ridge Mountains
impose severe constraints owing to topography and forest cover (Figure 1). Still, targeted deployment of
distributed systems—such as community and small-scale renewables—holds potential to fill critical gaps
in energy access throughout Appalachia (Mainzer et al. 2019, Browder et al. 2024).

 United States based on terrain and land use features. Higher terrain index indicates higher elevational variance,

 extreme landscape.

In many parts of central and northern Appalachia, dependence on coal as an economic driver has shaped
community identity and complicated the transition to renewable energy (Greenberg 2023, Gamper-
Rabindran and Ash 2024). In contrast, the rural and tribal communities of Southern Appalachia have
historically relied very little on coal. Their livelihoods are more deeply rooted in forest and stream-based
resources (Yarnell 1998, Gragson and Bolstad 2006). This same connection to the land and water has also
led the peoples of Southern Appalachia to place high value on protecting natural resources—whether for

 9

their ecological significance or their cultural role in sustaining identity, tradition, and subsistence (Welch-
Devine et al. 2022). As a result, even when communities are more receptive to coal alternatives, they can
be cautious about new energy infrastructure. The environmental footprint, altered scenic value, and
interference with recreational and subsistence practices such as hunting and fishing can compromise both
cultural identity and local economies and, in many cases, has drawn direct opposition from the
communities most affected (e.g., Grady and Inn 2002, EBCI 2009). Solutions for Southern Appalachia
are more likely to involve smaller-scale, microgrid, and off-grid energy systems that align with cultural
values and minimize landscape impacts.

1.2 HYDROPOWER IN SOUTHERN APPALACHIA

Hydropower is one renewable energy option with promise for Southern Appalachian communities.
Although the region’s streams are relatively small, they are ubiquitous, high-gradient, and fed by
abundant rainfall. Together, these conditions likely create greater potential for small-scale, distributed
hydropower than is found in adjacent regions. The steep terrain and rapid elevation changes allow
systems to achieve adequate head over shorter distances, which can simplify infrastructure and reduce
project footprints. In addition to these physical advantages, “micro-hydropower” systems (≤100 kW) are
well-suited to the region’s social and ecological context. Smaller or modular systems make installation
and maintenance feasible for small communities and individual households, and their low output typically
falls within the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s 10-MW exemption pathway for small and low-
impact hydropower (18 CFR Part 4, Subpart K), which reduces permitting complexity relative to larger
facilities. In parallel, damless, run-of-the-river designs minimize ecological disruption, may eliminate the
need for fish passage and protection infrastructure, and help preserve the scenic and recreational values
vital to local culture and economies. These designs exemplify a “fish-friendly” approach to hydropower
in which systems are designed to minimize barriers and disruptions to natural flow regimes and allow safe
passage of aquatic organisms (Permana and Potipituk 2024). Notably, technological advances in recent
years—such as high-efficiency vortex generators and commercially available microturbine systems
(Figure 2)—are both compatible with fish-friendly designs and capable of harnessing energy even from
remote, high-elevation headwater streams.

Here, we consider the potential for lower environmental and scenic impact, damless micro-hydropower
systems in a Southern Appalachian landscape. Our primary goal was to demonstrate that these streams
hold significant, untapped energy potential for rural mountain communities where energy access remains,
at best, unreliable, and that this could be achieved in a manner that minimizes harm to—or, in some cases,
improves—natural and cultural resource value. To illustrate this potential in a real-world context where
natural and cultural concerns are paramount, we focused on the Oconaluftee River Basin, located
primarily on lands of the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians (EBCI) in the Blue Ridge Mountains of
western North Carolina, USA (see Figure 1 inset).

1.3 THE QUALLA BOUNDARY AS A SOUTHERN APPALACHIAN MICROCOSM

The Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians (EBCI) is a federally recognized Tribe whose present-day and
ancestral homelands lie in the heart of Southern Appalachia, within the Qualla Boundary. The Qualla is
an approximately 230 km² tract of predominantly forested mountain terrain centered around the city of
Cherokee, North Carolina and the Oconaluftee River Basin. The area encompasses many of the extremes
found across Southern Appalachia—from high-elevation headwater streams and remote households to
lower-elevation rivers, valleys, and developed areas (EBCI 2013). In this way, the Qualla serves as a
microcosm of the broader region and a representation of the region’s extreme landscape (Figure 1). It
captures the distinct cultural, ecological, and infrastructural breadth of Appalachia. Tribal sovereignty and

 11

Cherokee stewardship are central to this landscape and shape decisions about energy, development, and
resource management in ways that reflect both broader Appalachian realities and the Tribe’s unique
responsibilities to people and place. Like other parts of Appalachia, the Qualla faces persistent challenges
related to energy access, infrastructure resilience, and balancing development with the protection of
natural and cultural resources. Here, tribal sovereignty ensures that Indigenous values—relevant across all
of Appalachia—are central to decision-making.

The EBCI has long prioritized energy sovereignty, ecological restoration, and preservation of cultural
values in its long-term planning and decision-making (EBCI 2009, 2013). Building on both Western
science and traditional knowledge systems, the EBCI constantly seeks to develop technologies and
solutions that meet the needs of its own people and those communities that face similar challenges. The
Tribe has considered alternative forms of energy production, including solar and hydropower. For
instance, a 705-kW solar facility was completed in 2020 to power the Cherokee Valley River Casino,
adjacent hotel, and administrative buildings near Murphy, NC. And, in 2009, four sites in the Oconaluftee
River Basin were considered for hydropower development. The locations were based on results from the
open-source Virtual Hydropower Prospector tool (Hall 2007). However, owing to its limited
consideration of Tribal values and resources, a combination of economic, ecological, and cultural
concerns ultimately contributed to a lack of endorsement of standard hydropower systems within the
Qualla (EBCI 2009). Although no powered dams currently exist within the Qualla, Ela Dam—located on
the Oconaluftee River immediately downstream of the Qualla (see Section 3.3)—remains closely
associated with it and serves as a primary barrier to aquatic passage into the Qualla. While the dam and
adjacent lands are not owned by the EBCI, the Tribe’s ongoing commitment to restoring the Oconaluftee
River Basin has prompted consideration of acquiring the facility to remove it or to retrofit the existing
infrastructure into a fish-friendly micro-hydropower system.

 2. PROJECT AIMS

In 2022, the EBCI began collaborations with Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) to explore the
potential for smaller-scale, micro-hydropower options within the Qualla. This strategic collaboration,
funded by the U.S. Department of Energy’s Water Power Technologies Office (WPTO), built upon
ongoing partnerships between the EBCI and the ORNL Natural Resources Management Program. The
effort was intended specifically to evaluate the feasibility of hydropower systems throughout the Qualla
and to optimize hydropower potential against aquatic connectivity, guided by direct input from the EBCI
regarding natural and cultural resources. Key aims for the project were as follows:
 Aim 1: Explore potential for fish-friendly hydropower options throughout the Oconaluftee River
 Basin and Qualla Boundary.
 Aim 2: Define all supporting objectives collaboratively with the EBCI.
 Aim 3: Engage the EBCI in all tasks to fulfill the project objectives.
 Aim 4: Deliver all data products to the EBCI.
 Aim 5: Provide the EBCI with a final list of candidate hydropower options that meet all objectives set
 forth.

 3. SETTING OBJECTIVES

To fulfill these aims, we established a set of technical and supporting objectives through sustained
collaboration between ORNL and EBCI resource managers and scientists. These discussions were aimed
at assessing the feasibility of different hydropower systems within the Oconaluftee River Basin,
identifying priorities for restoration and management, and developing a data-sharing framework that
would protect Tribal resources. The following sections outline the needs identified through this process.

3.1 NATURAL AND CULTURAL RESOURCE CONSIDERATIONS

First, any hydropower development within the Oconaluftee River Basin would need to balance energy
potential with Tribal concerns about aquatic connectivity, preservation of natural and scenic values, and
availability of fishing opportunities that affect subsistence, recreation, and cultural expression.

The EBCI has the most enduring history of dependence on—and appreciation for—the natural features of
Southern Appalachia’s landscape. For the Cherokee people, land and water are not merely resources but
are viewed as relatives. The Oconaluftee River and its tributaries have supported Cherokee communities
for generations. The river system, known as the Long Person or Ganvhidv Asgaya, is understood as a
living being—a conscious and sacred presence that carries memory, offers healing, and connects people
to place. The Long Person is central not only to subsistence and recreation, but to traditional knowledge,
spiritual practice, and individual identity. Its health reflects that of the people and the land that surrounds
it. Cherokee teachings also remind the present generation of its responsibility to ensure that future
generations—seven forward—inherit healthy lands and waters. This view informs contemporary
stewardship of aquatic and terrestrial systems and emphasizes the need to approach any change to the
landscape in a way that honors these relationships (Smithers 2025).

Importantly, we acknowledged during objective-setting that there is not a one-size-fits-all approach to
understanding and integrating knowledge systems. Traditional and local knowledge, being borne out of
enduring experience and relationships with the environment, can provide insights beyond the analytical
approaches of Western science. However, the cultural basis of traditional knowledge and the diverse ways
that it can be expressed also mean that it does not conform to a singular form or source that can be readily
mobilized as data. Approaches to bridge the gap between traditional knowledge and Western science must
respect and value this diversity without diluting it. Indigenous communities themselves should thus
determine how and when their knowledge and belief systems can be used, and they should oversee its
interpretations.

Takeaway: Based on the above considerations, the EBCI would not only provide insights from tribal
knowledge about ecological and cultural considerations but would be entrusted as decision-makers
without attempting to convert knowledge to discrete data points.

3.2 AQUATIC CONNECTIVITY

Both Western science and traditional knowledge point to existing ecological barriers that disrupt natural
patterns and relationships within aquatic systems. In the smaller, climate-sensitive waterways of Southern
Appalachia, culverts and other manmade structures are common at road and trail crossings. Although
these structures are not always absolute barriers, their cumulative effects, alongside natural barriers
(Figure 3), often impose significant costs on upstream and downstream movement by aquatic and
semiaquatic fauna. For example, culverts are frequently perched above the base flow level, especially
during dry seasons, and their smooth interiors can make upstream passage difficult or impossible for
weaker swimmers and early life stages (Northcote 1998, Olsen and Tullis 2013). Species that undertake

 13

breeding migrations, such as the endemic and culturally important sicklefin redhorse (Moxostoma
ugidatli), may also deplete energy reserves needed for successful reproduction as they navigate difficult
but not entirely impassable stream reaches (Northcote 1998). Additionally, canopy removal and
disturbance to riparian vegetation can raise water temperatures and increase erosion, which can degrade
habitat quality even where flow itself is unobstructed. This is particularly relevant to several Southern
Appalachian endemics with deep cultural importance to the tribe, such as brook trout (Salvelinus
fontinalis) and numerous salamander species, which require streams with relatively cool water and
adequate shade.

Takeaways: Given the above considerations, connectivity would be assessed for carefully selected
priority species, and this selection would be at the sole discretion of the Tribe. Additionally, estimating
the strength of barriers to aquatic movement would require high-resolution stream and riparian data most
relevant to focal species. Likewise, mapping and modeling of focal species habitat would be necessary at
higher resolution than currently available. Finally, models that describe ecological relationships (e.g.,
habitat value) must be capable of incorporating traditional knowledge from the Tribe.

3.3 FOCAL SPECIES AND LOCATIONS

As an extension of the above considerations, both brook trout (Salvelinus fontinalis) and sicklefin
redhorse (Moxostoma ugidatli) emerged as strong candidates for focal species consideration (Figure 4).

Sicklefin redhorse offered a clear opportunity to evaluate both ecological and hydropower objectives. The
conversion of Ela Dam—located near the base of the Oconaluftee River Basin—to a more efficient,
damless, run-of-the-river system would have broad implications for the entire watershed. Sicklefin
redhorse is a migratory species of deep cultural significance to the Cherokee and depends on mid-
elevation riverine habitats for spawning. Ela Dam presents a substantial barrier to these seasonal
movements and has had a disproportionate effect on the species' reproduction (Figure 5). In response, the
EBCI has previously facilitated translocation efforts to move individuals upstream of the dam.

Compared to sicklefin redhorse, brook trout occupy a broader extent of the watershed and inhabit the
highest-elevation headwater streams. These upper elevation areas are where hydropower has been least
explored but may be most applicable to rural mountain communities. As a coldwater species native to
Southern Appalachia, brook trout are deeply tied to both the ecological heritage and cultural traditions of
the region. In addition to their importance for subsistence, recreation, and cultural expression, they are
widely recognized as indicators of stream health and, more specifically for the Cherokee, the health of the
Long Person. As such, they can serve as a useful proxy to evaluate habitat conditions relevant to both
restoration and micro-hydropower planning.

further enhance natural value and ecological integrity of the reserve. Although hydropower development

3.4 HYDROLOGICAL AND RIPARIAN HABITAT DATA FOR THE OCONALUFTEE
 RIVER BASIN:

The EBCI previously developed custom spatial flowline and streamline features for the Oconaluftee River
Basin using a combination of existing stream maps and a high-resolution digital elevation model (DEM)
generated from light detection and ranging (LiDAR) data (Figure 7). Although these flowlines and stream
layers lacked flow rate estimates or other hydrologic attributes, they did encompass all perennial and
ephemeral streams within the Qualla. These ranged from major rivers to headwater channels and wet-
weather conveyances. At the same time, the open-source High Resolution National Hydrography Dataset
(NHDPlus HR) includes modeled flow metrics necessary to estimate hydropower potential (USGS 2023),
but it is constrained to a 10-meter resolution and omits many smaller tributaries that could feasibly

 17

support micro-hydropower and/or provide core habitat for brook trout. Additionally, neither dataset
included data relevant to terrestrial features that affect habitat quality and barrier strength.

Takeaways: Based on the above considerations, stream flow data would be needed at higher resolution
than currently available to identify additional opportunities for microturbine systems, particularly in steep,
high-elevation streams. These hydrologic data would also need to be paired with information on adjacent
landcover and riparian conditions to adequately assess habitat quality and evaluate the severity of partial
barriers for focal species.

3.5 METHODOLOGICAL FRAMEWORK

In response to the interconnected challenges of unreliable energy access, aquatic fragmentation, and the
need to protect culturally important resources, we established a framework to evaluate potential micro-
hydropower deployment while simultaneously advancing the EBCI’s aquatic restoration goals. Figure 8
outlines this general framework. Our goal was to classify barriers into two management pathways: (1)
barrier conversion to micro-hydropower, for structures that impede movement but do not significantly
affect watershed-scale connectivity and could support low-impact hydropower; and (2) barrier
removal/restoration, for structures whose elimination would substantially improve aquatic connectivity.
Two damless hydropower technologies were considered for conversion scenarios based on prior
discussions with the EBCI: vortex generators (40–100 kW), which could address energy needs for small
communities or larger facilities, and standalone microturbines (≥1 kW), suited for off-grid rural

 18

applications, especially in steep mountain terrain where infrastructure access is limited. Figure 2 (above)
provides an illustration of each of these generator scenarios.

Most importantly, final review and down-selection of candidate sites would be conducted by the EBCI
Natural Resources Department and Tribal Historic Preservation Office, with full respect for cultural
knowledge and data sensitivity. Detailed methods and results for each component of the framework are
presented in subsequent sections.

 4. METHODS AND RESULTS

4.1 FOCAL SPECIES AND RESTORATION PRIORITIES

Focal species and restoration priorities were concentrated in areas associated with the Tribal Reserve and
in headwater streams that support brook trout or could do so following restoration or barrier removal
(Figure 6). Less attention was eventually given to sicklefin redhorse and Ela Dam owing to ongoing
uncertainties in the fate of Ela Dam. Additionally, there was little doubt that, given the downstream
location of Ela Dam, its removal would have the greatest single effect on restoring connectivity to the
broader Oconaluftee River Basin. And, as a single site, it would provide the greatest hydropower potential
associated with the Qualla. Moreover, as the entryway to the Oconaluftee River Basin within the Qualla,
baseline models of barrier effects within the Qualla would not be significantly affected.

4.2 HYDROLOGIC AND RIPARIAN ZONE MAPPING OF THE OCONALUFTEE RIVER
 BASIN

We developed a new flowline layer and vegetation structure layers for the Oconaluftee River Basin using
LiDAR data based on the 2017 Phase 5 Acquisition by North Carolina Emergency Management (NCEM
2017). Flowlines were generated using a derived digital elevation model (DEM) alongside standard
hydrological modeling tools in R (v 4.2.3; R Core Team, 2024) and ArcGIS Pro (v 3.4.1; Esri, 2024). We
used the streamline features originally developed by EBCI to create burnlines, which were burned into the
DEM to account for stream crossing structures such as bridges and culverts. We then generated new
flowlines with flow accumulation values and imputed flow metrics from the NHDPlus HR dataset by
matching those values. This process yielded a complete NHDPlus HR–equivalent dataset for all flowlines
within the Oconaluftee River Basin (Figure 9–10). We likewise used the aforementioned LiDAR point
clouds to estimate overstory canopy density within a 20-m buffer around all stream centerlines. All layers
were produced as spatial rasters and at different resolutions ranging from 1–20 m. These products provide
flexibility for future modeling efforts, including more refined occupancy models or different spatial scales
of effect. They also equip the EBCI with multiple resolution options to match specific analytical or
computational needs.

4.3 IDENTIFYING BARRIERS AND ASSESSING HYDROPOWER POTENTIAL

We identified barriers throughout the Oconaluftee River Basin using a combination of the EBCI’s stream
crossing data (Figure 7) and remote sensing of stream crossing locations in ArcGIS Pro (Figure 11).
Every potential barrier identified was subject to manual review using satellite imagery, and over 100
barriers were field validated with EBCI during summer 2024. We prioritized stream crossing locations,
because these barriers were associated with infrastructure access (e.g., road crossings). This increased the
feasibility of hydropower development and represented the most apparent departures from natural
conditions (i.e., restoration need). In total, we identified 1,533 barriers and ultimately assessed
hydropower potential at 533 barriers (through Tribal input) throughout the Qualla (Figure 11).

We estimated hydropower potential at each barrier location using a simplified equation:
                                          W = H × Q / 10, where:
 W is power in watts, H is net head (feet), and Q is flow rate (gallons per minute).
This rule-of-thumb equation, based on U.S. Department of Energy guidance, assumes a typical minimum
system efficiency of 50% and is suitable for preliminary micro-hydropower screening (e.g.,
https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/planning-microhydropower-system). For this preliminary screening,
we assumed a minimum intake-to-outlet distance of 15 m. Head was calculated as the difference in
elevation between these two points in the stream. This conservative distance ensured that estimates
reflected the lower bound of feasibility while maintaining a minimal environmental footprint.

Estimated hydropower potential is shown in Figure 12, with a combined output of 22.6 MW across all
identified barriers. Individually, these ranged from < 1-kW in smaller tributaries to ~ 1-MW for sites such
as Ela Dam. Should the project advance beyond general watershed-level planning, more refined estimates
would be developed following final review of candidate hydropower locations by the EBCI.

4.6 REVIEWING NOTABLE PATTERNS

Following review of hydropower potential and barrier strength, several patterns were further investigated
to inform the final optimization of hydropower and restoration locations. Although brook trout habitat
(Figure 13) is concentrated in smaller, higher-elevation headwater streams, these areas presented
substantial opportunities for micro-hydropower owing to steep gradients and high flow velocities. Several
sites with generation potential exceeding 100 kW were identified even at extreme elevations (Figure 15).
Across all barriers with potential for hydropower development, there was a clear positive correlation
between brook trout habitat quality and power generation potential (Figure 16). The sole outlier in the
>100-kW category was Ela Dam. Interestingly, barrier strength was weakly correlated with generation
potential, which suggested the need for further optimization approaches. Notably, however, single-home
microturbine systems (2–10 kW) represented the most common development scenario, with 366 potential
sites that spanned the full range of barrier strengths (Figure 17) and elevations (Figure 15). This is
encouraging from a deployment perspective, as this generator class is well suited to both grid-connected
applications and remote, off-grid households in the rugged terrain of southern Appalachia.

4.7 OPTIMIZING HYDROPOWER AND RESTORATION LOCATIONS

4.7.1 Brook Trout Connectivity and Restoration Gains

We modeled connectivity using a graph representation of our new high-resolution stream network in
ArcGIS Pro 3.4.2. The polyline network contained fields for brook trout habitat suitability and barrier
strength (as calculated previously). For consistency, both metrics were rescaled to [0,1], where higher
values represented higher habitat suitability and higher resistance to movement, respectively. Isolated
components of the stream network were removed, which eliminated peripheral reaches that were not
hydrologically connected to the mainstem of the Oconaluftee River.

We then constructed a topological graph representation of the stream network, where each polyline
segment (edge) was represented as a pair of nodes (endpoints) with associated segment length, habitat
suitability, and normalized resistance value. Because hydropower potential was calculated with a
minimum 50-ft linear footprint, point barrier locations that occurred on the same stream segment and
within this distance of one another were merged into a single barrier group with summed barrier strength.

Upstream endpoints were defined as sources of movement in the network. To reduce spurious headwater
bias and improve computational efficiency, contiguous headwater reaches with Strahler stream order ≥ 2
were collapsed. Source strength (W) was set proportional to the sum of habitat values within each
collapsed upstream branch. As such, longer, high-quality upstream networks acted as stronger sources of
connectivity. This ensured that sources of movement were more likely to represent true source
populations, or core habitat areas, for brook trout.

We defined connectivity (C) as the modeled potential for exchange (movement) between nodes:

 𝐶𝑖𝑗 = 𝑊𝑖𝑊𝑗 exp ( ― λ𝑑𝑖𝑗), where:
  𝑊𝑖 and 𝑊𝑗 are the source weights for terminal points i and j, proportional to sum of upstream habitat
 value; 𝑑𝑖𝑗 is the least-cost distance between i and j in the barrier-adjusted stream network (higher
resistance = longer effective distance); and λ is a decay parameter that controls how quickly connectivity
 declines with increasing distance. Here λ was set as the inverse of the median terminal pair distance to
 match the scale of the network.

Total watershed connectivity (C) was computed as the sum of all pairwise connectivity values, and
restoration gain for each barrier was then calculated as the proportional increase in C after removing that
barrier (Wade et al. 2023). Removal was simulated by replacing the resistance value of the barrier-
affected segment with the nearest non-barrier resistance value in the same reach. All processing steps
were conducted in ArcGIS Pro/ArcPy, with versioned geodatabase outputs to support future reruns as
new field data or knowledge become available.

As expected, the greatest restoration gains would be realized for removal of barriers associated with Soco
Creek and Jenkins Creek, which flow southwest out of the Tribal Reserve, and large gains would also be
realized for several barriers associated with Bunches Creek to the north (Figure 18). Additional moderate
restoration gains might occur at lower elevation locations, but these values were likely driven by high
barrier strengths that are more attributable to poor habitat (high temperature, low canopy) rather than the
presence of strong physical barriers. Note that Ela Dam is not considered an important barrier to brook
trout (Figure 18). This was expected for brook trout given that Ela Dam is the most downstream point in
the network. Determination of the restoration potential for Ela Dam would need to incorporate
information well beyond the Qualla Boundary; although, a more targeted analysis could focus specifically
on sicklefin redhorse and access to upstream spawning habitats following dam removal.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

We demonstrated that the streams throughout the Qualla could support a wide range of hydropower
options, with a combined output potential of 22.6 MW across 533 identified barriers. These ranged from
~ 1 kW in smaller tributaries to potential > 1 MW for Ela Dam. Although some refinements to
hydropower estimates will be needed prior to final selection of sites, we found that several high-elevation
locations above 1,200 m (> 3,900 ft) could clearly support generation potentials that exceed the defined
limits of micro-hydropower (100 kW) (Figure 15). Additionally, microturbine generators over 2 kW were
possible across all elevations and habitat conditions with a minimum of 366 suitable locations (501
locations when including all sites ≥ 2 kW). This is an encouraging result for remote Appalachian homes
and businesses, particularly when one considers that the Qualla Boundary spans the full range of terrain
characteristics, land use patterns, economic statuses, and challenges to energy access as found throughout
broader Appalachia.

High-resolution hydrologic and riparian datasets developed in this study provide the EBCI with flexible
tools for modeling habitat, refining occupancy analyses, and evaluating tradeoffs between restoration and
hydropower implementation (Table 1). These products support testing of alternative scenarios and at
multiple spatial scales. Importantly, such high-resolution spatial data provide enhanced ability for natural,
cultural, and other land use planning efforts by the Tribe, which extend well beyond the scope of
hydropower development.

We focused on guiding hydropower implementation through simultaneous consideration of connectivity
and restoration potential for brook trout. Brook trout were selected as a focal species according to Tribal
wants as they pertained to both ecological and cultural considerations. The species can serve as an
effective proxy for stream health across the broadest elevational gradients but clearly biases outputs
toward headwater streams. This was indeed a goal. However, restoration of connectivity to the
Oconaluftee River Basin has equal significance to restoration within it. Ela Dam remains a critical barrier
to numerous species, including the sicklefin redhorse, a species long respected by the Cherokee and now
of broader conservation concern (Favrot and Kwak 2024, Jenkins et al. 2025). This represents a
disproportionate decision point for the entire Oconaluftee River Basin and health of Long Person. Thus,
the EBCI will continue to prioritize removal of Ela Dam and consider its conversion to a lower-impact,
damless system.

Energy independence and stewardship of Ganvhidv Asgaya remain inseparable in the Cherokee
homeland. The Qualla encompasses both remote mountain households and more developed centers,
meaning no single energy solution will suffice. The Tribe has already expressed interest in a portfolio
approach that combines ecological restoration with stand-alone microturbines, small vortex systems, and
additional energy sources such as solar, where feasible. Small-scale hydropower currently appears to offer
the clearest path to energy independence for the EBCI and adjacent communities of Southern Appalachia.

5.1 LOOKING AHEAD

Through this work, we have established a robust foundation to guide hydropower implementation for the
Tribe, but additional research is needed to translate this work into on-the-ground outcomes. Future work
could advance in four key areas:

1. Site-Specific Validation and Restoration Science
Detailed field assessments of candidate sites are needed to refine generation estimates, account for
hydrological seasonality, and evaluate geology. Surveys for brook trout, sicklefin redhorse, and/or
additional culturally significant species such as aquatic salamanders could be used to validate models and
enhance their value, and to further guide restoration priorities. From this process, a shortlist of pilot sites
can be selected for future demonstration projects. The EBCI has already expressed interest in up to three
hydropower demonstration sites. However, the EBCI Natural Resources Department expressed specific
interest in first conducting field sampling of brook trout and aquatic salamander populations to better
inform restoration planning. As such, we have co-developed a sampling plan based on current
uncertainties and future needs. As before, the effort would focus on streams associated with the Tribal
Reserve, beginning with Big Witch Creek, Jenkins Creek and upper Soco Creek (Figure 21–22).

2. Hydrological Scenario Modeling
Future modeling should incorporate downscaled environmental projections and hydrological extremes,
such as drought and flooding, which have disproportionately affected Southern Appalachia in recent
years. This approach would ensure that hydropower systems are designed for resilience under future
conditions, while also identifying where restoration can buffer generation risks and sustain long-term
energy output.

3. Technology and Policy Development
Over longer timeframes, collaborative work with DOE and Tribal partners could continue to adapt
technologies to steep mountain terrain and remote deployment. This could emphasize modular,
transportable, and low-maintenance systems. Such project outcomes can simultaneously inform DOE
policy and Tribal energy initiatives by providing case studies of distributed hydropower in ecologically
and culturally sensitive landscapes.

4. Enhancing the Integration of Traditional Knowledge
As intended, the data products and tools developed here can serve as a model for other Tribal nations and
rural mountain communities. Future work could expand and formalize data-sharing protocols and
programs that effectively integrate traditional knowledge with Western science.

 38

The EBCI–ORNL partnership now has the foundation to advance each of these areas. For the Tribe, the
most pressing need is to move from watershed-level planning to practical implementation of restoration
and rigorous, site-specific impact assessments for hydropower deployment. With continued support, the
next phase could deliver restored aquatic connectivity for culturally significant species and establish a
scalable model of Tribal-led, ecologically integrated renewable energy for Southern Appalachia and
beyond. Success should be measured not only in megawatts produced but also in restored aquatic
connectivity, species responses, and cultural outcomes.
