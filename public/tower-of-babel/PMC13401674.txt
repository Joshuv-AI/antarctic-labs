Delivery of Dietary Advice for Type 2 Diabetes in Dental Settings and Its Relevance to Dental Practitioners: A Scoping Review
Authors: Kailey Paterson, Kay Franks, Janet Wallace, Dileep Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDiabetes and periodontal disease are inflammatory conditions where lifestyle interventions such as dietary messages could improve outcomes of both chronic conditions. Dental practitioners could support this behavioral intervention during periodontal treatment and maintenance in people with diabetes and periodontal diseases. However, studies that assess dietary interventions in type 2 diabetes patients within dental context setting are limited.MethodsThis scoping review was completed based on the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews. An initial comprehensive search on MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, and Scopus databases was conducted in May 2024, and again in June 2025. Only English language texts were considered for this review. The target question for the search on available literature was “What evidence is available on dietary counseling of type 2 diabetes patients in dental settings relevant to delivery by dental practitioners?”ResultsInitial search resulted in 394 studies with seven studies eligible for inclusion in this scoping review retrieved. Two studies focused on dental treatment along with dietary advice, four studies developed and/or implemented diabetes‐periodontitis educational programs, while one study explored the self‐reported capabilities of a dental practitioner in delivering education, including dietary advice.ConclusionWhile all included studies acknowledged the opportunities for dietary advice in a dental setting, the lack of comparative studies and minimal evidence of delivery by dental practitioners limits the ability to draw strong conclusions. These findings underpin the need for designing a communication tool for dental practitioners in advising type 2 diabetes patients about dietary interventions.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is predicted to affect 1.3 billion people worldwide by 2050 [1]. Specifically, Type 2 diabetes (T2DM) accounts for most of the current disease burden and predicted growth worldwide [1]. Traditionally, management of T2DM involves lifestyle modification, including a healthy diet, to minimize or augment medications and reduce associated health risks. Lifestyle factor and behavioral changes generally aim to lower the elevated blood glucose levels (BGL) and induce weight loss for management of health issues associated with the inflammatory burden of T2DM [2]. Links between T2DM and other chronic inflammatory diseases including obesity and periodontal disease (PD) are widely acknowledged and interventions targeting lifestyle factors may limit this inflammatory component across these conditions.

Diagnosis of T2DM warrants patients' lifelong commitment in making lifestyle and dietary choices on a daily basis [3]. This is generally led by the medical practitioner and dietician through formulation of the desired behavioral change. Modifying risk factors for T2DM includes reducing obesity, increasing physical activity, and addressing unhealthy eating habits. Obesity and T2DM are closely linked, and by reducing waist circumference in patients diagnosed with T2DM, the risk for complications is also reduced [4]. A widely used tool for T2DM and obesity management is dietary counseling. According to Diabetes Australia [5], there is no universal “diabetic diet” however healthy dietary choices are recommended, similar to those for the general population. A healthy diet includes eating a variety of food from all five food groups (grains, vegetables or legumes, fruit, dairy, and lean proteins) and limiting foods that are high in kilojoules, saturated fat, and added sugars or salt [5, 6]. Goals for dietary intervention focus on achieving body weight goals and reducing the glycaemic load with self‐management, to minimize inflammatory cascade commonly implicated in other systemic diseases, including T2DM, obesity, and PD [7]. As such, promoting healthy eating is considered the first‐line intervention for T2DM cases however, it is also recognized that diet is one of the most difficult factors to change [8].

Behavioral change is significantly reliant on consistent messages to self‐manage the disease over the lifespan. Self‐management evaluations have identified challenges in adhering to lifestyle changes in longer term unless repetitive educational resources are implemented. Therefore, dietary reinforcement through a multidisciplinary team of healthcare practitioners is essential to adequately support self‐management of T2DM through nutritional behaviors [9]. Whilst challenging to achieve, a greater dietary knowledge is associated with optimal self‐management of dietary habits [10]. Al‐Khawaldeh et al. [11] found that whilst some behaviors, such as timely medication had strong self‐efficacy, the confidence to comply with dietary changes was lower, especially in times of stress. Interestingly, those with better dietary self‐efficacy have been noted to have better glycaemic control, however a single exposure to education is likely insufficient to build skills needed to overcome dietary challenges [11]. Although specialized dietetic consultation is recommended for diabetics, dieticians have reported that the number of attended consultations is inadequate [12]. This indicates that barriers to optimal consistency of dietary counseling exist, therefore additional sources of support for dietary messages by healthcare professionals, including dental practitioners is beneficial.

PD includes gingivitis and periodontitis, inflammatory conditions initiated by plaque microbes (polymicrobial biofilm), in the presence of behavioral and biological risk factors [13]. Initial management of PD is generally provided by dental practitioners. In this review, the term “dental practitioners” encompasses dentists (including dental specialists), dental hygienists, oral health therapists, and dental therapists.

Elevated BGL or hyperglycemia is one of the well‐researched risk factors leading to increased inflammatory load thereby increasing prevalence and severity of PD [14, 15]. Additionally, in early stages of PD, higher plaque levels, gingival inflammation and bleeding is more prevalent in T2DM individuals [16, 17]. This inflammatory cascade triggers an overreactive immune response resulting in destruction of supporting bone and tooth loss in advanced PD stage known as periodontitis [2, 13, 14, 18]. While this risk factor is generally acknowledged by dental practitioners, the bi‐directional relationship and negative effect of severe PD on BGL control is widely supported acknowledged [2, 19].

There are conflicting reports on the role of diet relating to PD [20], however there is some evidence that optimal diet including Omega−3 fatty acids and a Mediterranean diet reduces inflammatory load, regardless of physical activity [21, 22]. Although these studies do not focus on T2DM populations, providing appropriate dietary messages to maintain healthy weight range and target BGLs, would conceivably impact prevalence and severity of PD based on the well‐established links. Whilst the bi‐directional link with PD is discussed by non‐dental practitioners, there is little evidence on the routine involvement of dental practitioners in lifestyle counseling whilst managing T2DM patients [7]. Dental practitioners are trained to provide dietary advice, but this is generally focused on prevention of dental issues, such as dental caries and erosion [23, 24]. Additionally, the Australian guidelines for preventive dental services states that dietary advice given to manage factors that may influence oral disease is within a dental practitioner's scope of practice [25]. This indicates there is scope to provide targeted dietary advice and influence health outcomes, beyond caries and erosion, whilst holistically supporting diabetes and periodontal outcomes in this population.

Dental practitioners are routinely involved in the management of dental conditions thereby have access to patients that may need additional support in making lifestyle changes to manage diabetes. Current literature recognizes the dental practitioners' role in diabetes management, including detection and referral [26, 27]. Periodontitis has been suggested as an early marker for diabetes in a longitudinal study on “non‐diabetic” patients with PD over a 5‐year period, and noted an increasing BGL [28]. Additionally, treating PD has shown to improve BGL and reduce the risk of T2DM complications [29]. This was further supported by a systematic review, which suggested that dental practitioners may be able to identify undiagnosed T2DM cases, therefore highlighting that management of each of these diseases could assist in reducing the prevalence of the other [18]. PD is believed to contribute to a low‐grade inflammation that impact BGLs and promotes inflammatory conditions such as T2DM and obesity [15]. Despite this, minimal evidence is available on the role of dental practitioners in supporting T2DM patients with dietary advice.

Considering the bidirectional relationship between T2DM and PD, the access by dental practitioners to T2DM patients during PD treatment and the underutilised dietary component of dental practice, opportunities in capacity building exist. Combining dietary counseling with management of PD for diabetic and pre‐diabetic patients has the potential to support overall health, as well reducing the prevalence and severity of PD. However, minimal research on dietary advice provided by dental practitioners to T2DM patients is currently available. Hence, a scoping review was conducted to achieve an understanding of the characteristics of dietary advice in a dental setting and provide relevance to the role of dental practitioner in preventive care.

Methods

A scoping review was chosen to map the evidence for dietary reform for the patient cohort by identifying methods of intervention and outcomes in populations with both PD and T2DM. A scoping review priori protocol was developed to limit reporting bias and provide a transparent plan of review [30]. The JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis and the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) [31] are considered to be the current benchmark to original frameworks such as Arksey and O'Malley [32] for scoping reviews.

This review was conducted by utilizing the guidelines provided within the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis [30] and the PRISMA framework (2018) [31]. The detailed protocol utilized in this scoping review has been published recently [33]. The search parameters for context were modified slightly to include studies that involve any health professional providing dietary advice in reference to oral health or within a dental setting. The initial scope of the study was too restrictive in terms of dental practitioners' delivery of dietary advice. Expanding the search parameters beyond dental practitioners to include other health personnel inevitably increases heterogeneity in the included studies. However, this broader scope is necessary to capture the full range of evidence on dietary advice delivered in a dental context to this population group. By broadening the provider group in this way, the review incorporates conceptually aligned research where the population and setting are appropriate, even when dental practitioners are not the exclusive providers. This strategy balances coherence with sufficient breadth to map the topic area adequately, ensuring that the scoping review meets its objective of describing the full landscape of dietary advice in dental settings, while still allowing clear implications for dental professionals to be drawn. To ensure methodological transparency and reflect the expanded search parameters, the review question was refined from the published protocol [33].

Findings of this review will assist with exploring barriers to chairside dietary interventions for T2DM patients and review perceptions of health behavior models that may impact at the individual, community, and population level in relation to T2DM and PD. Additionally, the findings will provide a basis for the development of a chairside intervention to be delivered by dental practitioners. This scoping review aims to explore the following research question: “What evidence is available on dietary counseling of T2DM patients in dental settings and relevant to delivery by dental practitioners?”

Search Strategy

A three‐step search strategy was used to identify relevant studies based on the JBI guidelines [30]. The initial pilot search was conducted on MEDLINE and CINAHL for Medical subject heading (MeSH) and keywords associated with the concept. Articles were searched to identify common index terms and keywords with specific population/concept/context (PPC) of the research question and adapted to each database search. The second step included a complete search using the identified keywords and index terms across MEDLINE, Scopus, CINAHL, and EMBASE databases on May 20, 2024, with the search repeated on June 6, 2025. After screening, a hand search of reference lists from the included papers was conducted to explore additional resources on this topic. Only peer‐reviewed and English language studies were considered for inclusion.

Endnote (Version 21, Clarivate Analytics (Australia) Pty Limited) was used to organize the search results and remove duplicates. Results were uploaded to Covidence (www.covidence.org Veritas Health Innovation Ltd) to assist in collating studies for screening. The inclusion criteria focused on population with T2DM, with intervention or assessment of dietary behaviors in a dental setting by dental practitioners. Studies that included the umbrella term of “Diabetes Mellitus” or “Diabetes” were screened and excluded if they focused primarily on T1DM. To retrieve the comprehensive information on the population, concept and context studied, Boolean operators and truncation were used. This scoping review excluded gray literature to focus on sources that have undergone formal peer review. While gray literature may offer valuable insights, the objective of this review, which was to map the current evidence, prioritized peer‐reviewed sources to ensure consistent standards of methodological transparency.

Results

The literature search across databases yielded a total of 394 articles that were screened. After exclusion of reviews, conference abstracts and duplicates, 145 studies were screened by abstract, and title and 28 full text articles were retrieved. The full text articles were compared against the inclusion criteria to identify seven eligible studies. Figure 1 illustrates the process of identification, screening and inclusion of relevant studies using a PRISMA flow diagram [31]. During full text screening, 16 articles were excluded as they did not deliver dietary advice, and five studies were excluded as they did not target or include T2DM in their delivery of dietary advice.

PRISMA flow diagram outlining identification, screening and inclusion of relevant studies.

Three overarching themes were identified: dietary advice within other dental treatment interventions, diabetes–periodontitis educational programs, and health practitioner perspectives. Of the included studies, two studies measured an intervention directly related to dietary advice to T2DM participants, four studies explored the development of a program aimed at this group and one paper assessed the experiences of dental practitioner participants in providing advice to T2DM patients in current practice. Although each study aligned with one primary theme, several addressed more than one theme; reflecting conceptual overlap across these domains. For example, Saengtipbovorn and Taneepanichskul [35, 36] primarily explored the success of a diabetes‐periodontitis educational program, but the study also incorporated dietary intervention, dental care, and stakeholder engagement in the development, including practitioner perspectives. Treatment Intervention

Doke et al. [34] conducted an RCT that involved 112 Japanese participants with metabolic syndrome (MetS). The aim of the study was to measure the effect of periodontal and prosthodontic treatment combined with dietary and exercise guidance on MetS related factors, including but not limited to diabetes. The study measured the effects of dietary information in conjunction with periodontal treatment; however, the study focused on MetS as a larger group and ascertained that T2DM is a related factor amongst participants. The study assessed BGLs as a secondary outcome to waist circumference.

Comparably, Brigido et al. [40] focused on T2DM participants who required prosthodontic rehabilitation with removable dentures and diet as an observational study. In both studies, the dietary advice was limited and BGL change was not statistically significant, however there was an improvement in nutritional status and waist circumference [34]. Brigido et al. [40] delivered 20 min of dietary counseling by the dentist and nutrition pamphlets based on recommendations by the Brazilian Diabetes Society utilized a 60 min video on diet and exercise. Doke et al. [34] highlighted that the lack of hyperglycaemic change within the study could be due to minimal deviation in normoglycemic levels, further acknowledging that diabetes was considered a risk for the participants but not necessarily a targeted characteristic of the study. Brigido et al. [40] reasoned that the delivery of more individualized advice may have more impact on BGL.

Diabetes and Periodontitis Educational Programs

Three studies were found relating to a needs assessment, design and implementation of an integrated diabetes‐periodontitis and nutrition health module in Malaysia [38, 39, 40]. Two of the studies developed a module based on interviews with selected healthcare professionals, including a dietitian, dentist, dental specialist, dental nurse, diabetic educator, endocrinologist, pharmacist, and health psychologist [38, 39]. Health professionals participated in interviews to identify the perceived barriers for patients and barriers within current practice that challenged the ability to meet the needs of patients in relation to T2DM and periodontitis. Jamil et al. [38] referred to the aforementioned study, then built upon those recommendations to develop the module, then evaluated the module for understandability and accessibility. The panel of multidisciplinary healthcare professionals evaluated the module highly based on those criteria.

Rusly et al. [39] aimed to demonstrate the feasibility of the diabetes‐periodontitis and nutrition module, however this was moved to an online wellness program due to COVID‐19 restrictions. The authors used purposive sampling to select patients with T2DM and periodontitis to participate, the number of participants was severely impacted by movement to online module, rather than in‐clinic as was initially intended. All participants answered an online questionnaire, a OHQoL assessment and had a videocall for limited dietary assessment and food frequency questionnaire. The intervention group received three individualized consultations with a dietitian and one with a dentist during the 12‐week period, were directed to watch a weekly educational video and instructed to take their own measurements. The authors reported the intervention group improved their diet and weight, but no change was found in waist circumference. They identified barriers including a lack of self‐discipline and the short program duration, as well as a lack of support. HbA1c screening was completed at baseline but was not mentioned in the results of the study.

Comparatively, two papers assessed the effectiveness of a lifestyle and oral health education program in affecting dietary choices of T2DM participants, among other lifestyle and oral health factors [35, 36]. The program and materials were developed by stakeholders (healthcare professionals and a representative diabetic person) based on a health belief model for behavior change and validated by an expert panel which included an educator, a dental practitioner and an endocrinologist. They utilized a nurse practitioner and dental assistant to provide individual dietary and lifestyle advice, and oral health education; respectively. Both had training in motivational interviewing (MI) techniques which were utilized at 20 min individual counseling sessions at baseline, 3 months and 6 months. Additionally, 15 min educational videos were delivered at 1, 2, 4, and 5 months. The study was designed as a single blind (to participants) quasi‐experimental study. The authors reported 92.3% of the intervention group modified their diet and had sustained improvement of BGL and PD status [35], and this was statistically significant at 3 and 6 months. Additionally, the program found to have improved knowledge and attitudes towards oral health and T2DM [36].

Health Practitioner Experiences

In the study by Yuen [37] 995 dental hygienists in the South Carolina were surveyed to assess the experiences associated with educating diabetic patients in clinical practice. This study was a cross‐sectional survey and relied on the self‐reported assessment of their individual experiences treating diabetic patient. Approximately 41% of respondents reported that they spend more time educating diabetic patients and self‐assessed that they adequately covered nutrition and dietary counseling in this population. Shorter recall intervals and more regular exposure to diabetic patients were associated with those who spent additional time educating patients on factors affecting diabetes such as diet and lifestyle. This study reported that time spent on dietary and nutritional priority was considered tertiary to plaque control and periodontitis‐diabetes relationship discussion, and dependant on time constraints in appointments [37].

Discussion

This review evaluated the current literature relating to the role of educating T2DM patients about diet and nutrition in dental settings. The included studies have considered different aspects around diabetes dietary education in relation to dental, dietary advice with dental treatment, diabetes‐periodontitis educational programs, and health practitioner perspectives. Although varied, these studies provide limited evidence of the impact of dietary messages delivered to T2DM patients in a dental setting. The scarcity of available studies pertaining to dietary advice provided by dental practitioners indicates that this preventive skillset is likely not well utilized within this group. However, this lack of evidence reflects limited evaluation of implementation rather than a lack of relevance.

Dietary advice delivered within the scope of dental care, particularly where it supports oral and periodontal health, remains critical for holistic patient care. Considering the evidence available on BGLs and T2DM impacting PD and the bi‐directional relationship between the two inflammatory conditions, more research would be helpful in influencing evidence‐based practice, training, and skills of dental practitioners to support T2DM patients. Importantly, emerging evidence identifies dietary inflammation as associated with an increased risk and prevalence of periodontitis [42]. Accordingly, Shi et al. [42] suggest that assessment of dietary habits should be incorporated into periodontal risk screening. This is particularly relevant for T2DM, where diet contributes to systemic inflammatory burden and periodontal risk.

While this review focused on dietary advice, other modifiable behaviors are routinely addressed in dental practice, including smoking cessation, alcohol consumption, and oral hygiene behaviors [43, 44]. There is stronger evidence supporting dental practitioners' role in these areas, particularly for smoking cessation interventions [45]. In comparison, dietary advice for T2DM within dental settings remains minimally defined and underresearched. This contrast further emphasizes that the potential scope of practice may exceed what is currently demonstrated in the literature.

Multiple studies provide evidence that periodontal treatment can reduce BGLs in the T2DM population [29, 46]. However, very few evaluate the importance of delivery of periodontal interventions along with dietary advice to address the bidirectional relationship between these two conditions. Although Doke et al. [34] used periodontal treatment as the primary intervention in combination with dietary and lifestyle advice, the burden of inflammatory load was shown to be reduced in those participants receiving both, when compared to just dietary and lifestyle advice. This finding highlights a potential role for dental practitioners integrating dietary advice alongside periodontal treatment in T2DM patients, rather than exclusively. Additionally, an RCT confirmed that a program which combined lifestyle advice and oral hygiene instructions statistically significant improvement in BGL and PD indices in participants with T2DM [35]. Although the dietary and lifestyle advice was delivered by a nurse‐practitioner and oral hygiene instructions by a dental assistant, it was developed as a combined program. Saengtipbovorn and Taneepanichskul [35] suggest that the inclusion of periodontal education is an important distinguishing feature compared with other studies. These findings indicate the potential for integrating dietary advice with routine dental services for T2DM patients to improve the outcomes of both diabetes and oral health. However, this has not been directly evaluated when delivered by dental practitioners. Unfortunately, no other studies directly comparing periodontal treatment with and without dietary advice in T2DM populations were identified.

Few studies aimed to compare prosthodontic treatment with and without dietary advice on the nutritional status of healthy edentulous patients [47, 48]. Of interest for this scoping review, one study draws attention to the need for preventive intervention within the T2DM community with their observational study [41]. Although unable to make a significant conclusion on the reduction of BGLs, the improvement of nutritional status among elderly T2DM patients with loss of dentition and requiring partial prosthesis was a significant finding. A holistic approach to treating T2DM patients at risk for tooth loss would conceivably decrease the probability of malnutrition due to tooth loss in the geriatric phase of life.

A series of studies on the need for, and development of a nutrition and health module for targeting diabetes and PD explored consultation with a multidisciplinary team to develop a program [38, 39, 40]. However, the implementation of the program was impacted by COVID‐19, and this resulted in the program being redesigned as an online intervention, which likely impacted the participation level [40]. The series of studies highlights a common theme that regular dietary messages are important for success, but also that health professionals wanted more consistent messages across the multidisciplinary team. Munakata et al. [49] demonstrated that frequent opportunities to counsel T2DM participants in lifestyle factors including dietary, resulted in reduced BGLs and waist circumference. Saengtipbovorn and Taneepanichskul [35, 36] utilized motivational interviewing techniques and health belief model of behavior change, as well as stakeholder input in the development of their program; however, similar theory‐informed intervention development was rarely reported in the broader dental context literature. Regular and individual consultation with the participants was identified as positively influencing the success of the program on dietary modification. These findings highlight an underutilization of theory‐based approaches to providing dietary advice to individuals with T2DM in dental settings.

The potential for dental practitioners to better support pre‐diabetes and T2DM patients within the dental practice is more evident in other areas of the literature. Screening for chronic diseases such as T2DM in dental clinics has shown positive results, both in relation to identifying individuals not on frequent visits to a medical service [50], and in the patients' acceptance of screening in dental setting [51]. Estrich et al. [26] identified a dental visit as being potentially the only point of contact with healthcare professionals in many patients. Interestingly, Strauss et al. [52] explored the BGL monitoring using bleeding from gingiva compared to finger prick in detecting pre‐diabetes and diabetes within a dental setting, offering a less invasive opportunity for professional glucose monitoring. Marruganti et al. [7] also conducted a review relating to the interconnection between inflammatory conditions of obesity, diabetes, and PD and the hypothesized approaches that dental practitioners could use to influence the associated inflammatory load. They suggested existing tools, such as the “Making Every Contact Count” project, to implement lifestyle support within a dental setting, as well as an integrated medical‐dental approach for holistic care of these patients.

A lack of training and resources aimed at dental practitioners appears to be a significant barrier in providing dietary advice in dental setting [53]. A study involving dental hygienists assessed the capabilities and barriers to providing education to T2DM patients [37]. However, despite the evidence of oral health affecting chronic diseases such as T2DM, medical and dental professions still lack optimal interdisciplinary interactions [54]. There is a need to integrate dental services and dental practitioners into the patient‐centered healthcare. While limited, evidence of dietary interventions is more prevalent than evidence of appropriate dietary assessment [55]. Since assessment is fundamental to the delivery of dietary advice within a dietetic practice, this should be considered in relation to the training needs of dental practitioners. Overall, acknowledging the supporting role of dental practitioners in T2DM intervention via management of PD provides the opportunity for sharing information and educational resources to achieve cohesiveness between allied health, medical, and dental services.

Yuen [37] also identified time constraints were a challenge to providing optimal support for T2DM patients, and that dietary counseling often fell down the list of priorities. This is supported by Kelly and Moynihan [53], who found that although most dental practitioners considered diet to be an important aspect of PD treatment, it was not prioritized, when compared to other risk factors with higher comparative evidence. A systematic review of nutrition care practices in oral health settings highlighted that dietary advice was often general and inconsistently documented, alongside the recurrent identification of time constraints and insufficient remuneration as barriers [55]. More regular contact improved dietary advice delivery rates [37], and it's conceivable that as T2DM patients warrant closer oral health monitoring, this may provide better opportunities for discussing diet. However, in the Australian context, this idealistic monitoring is not generally supported by private health funds or Medicare, thus increasing the out‐of‐pocket costs for patients and decreasing the incentives for practitioners to be proactive about discussing diet.

There are some limitations of this scoping review. The number of eligible studies that involved dental practitioners delivering dietary information to T2DM patients were very limited. Although the search parameters were expanded to include all health personnel delivering dietary messages in a dental setting, this broader approach limited the extent to which firm conclusions could be drawn about the specific role of dental practitioners. Of the available studies several methodological limitations should be considered including small sample sizes, self‐reported data, COVID‐19 related disruptions and the lack of control groups. Furthermore, no formal critical appraisal was undertaken, consistent with the standard of scoping review methodology; therefore, this review presents a map of the available evidence rather than an effectiveness of dietary intervention. This review supports the need for further research on dental practitioners' role in dietary counseling in T2DM patients.

Conclusions

This scoping review sought to evaluate the available evidence on dietary counseling of T2DM patients in a dental setting, with a view to informing dental practitioners' contribution to dietary counseling. Minimal evidence of implementation of a dietary intervention was available, based on the inclusion criteria and the objectives of the scoping review. Of those included studies, there appears to be agreement that the dental team have a role to play in delivery of dietary advice to T2DM patients. However, the evidence base remains limited with a lack of strong comparative studies of dietary intervention in dental settings and a low involvement of dental practitioners in counseling T2DM within the included evidence. This likely reflects on the constraints in available training and resources that make it challenging for dental practitioners to be involved in this aspect of preventive treatment, despite the plausibility of their involvement. The findings of this research can inform the development of a chairside communication intervention for dental practitioners to participate in dietary counseling of patients with T2DM. Furthermore, the limited use of theory‐informed behavioral approaches highlights an opportunity to strengthen future training resources to better support effective dietary advice delivery in dental practice.
