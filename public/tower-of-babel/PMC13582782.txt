Reprogramming parasitic signatures into anticancer peptide candidates: in silico discovery of Leishmania major-derived peptides
Authors: Abbas Dehghanian, Mohammad Izadi, Zahra Minaei, Mostafa Cheshrokh, Fatemeh Fathi Tadavani, Anahita Masoudi, Mohammad Aref Bagherzadeh, Mirza Ali Mofazzal Jahromi, Majid Pirestani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to computationally identify and prioritize anticancer peptide (ACP) candidate sequences derived from the Leishmania major proteins KMP11 and GP63 using an integrated bioinformatics framework that incorporates peptide design, safety assessment, multi-criteria decision-making, and membrane-oriented evaluation. Amino acid sequences of KMP11 and GP63 were obtained from the NCBI database. Peptide fragments ranging from 5 to 25 residues were computationally generated and initially screened for anticancer potential using AntiCP 2.0 and MLACP prediction tools. Candidate peptides were subsequently subjected to systematic amino acid substitutions followed by iterative re-evaluation to optimize predicted anticancer properties. Toxicity, allergenicity, and antigenicity were assessed using TOXINPRED2, ALGPRED2, and VAXIJEN2, respectively. Peptides meeting safety and functional criteria were prioritized using the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS). Structural modeling, exploratory molecular docking, and membrane interaction analyses were then performed to provide comparative mechanistic insights into membrane association and potential receptor accessibility, rather than to predict specific target binding. Reference datasets of experimentally validated ACPs and non-ACPs were compiled, and motif analysis using MERCI identified sequence patterns associated with anticancer activity, with enriched motifs most frequently observed in the 12–13 residue range. Following iterative screening and safety evaluation, subsets of peptides derived from KMP11 and GP63 were identified as non-toxic and non-allergenic according to in silico prediction tools. These peptides were subsequently prioritized using the TOPSIS multi-criteria decision-making model. The top-ranked candidates were further subjected to exploratory molecular docking against selected cancer-associated receptors and coarse-grained membrane interaction analysis to provide comparative mechanistic context regarding membrane interaction propensity and potential receptor accessibility. Based on integrated computational scoring, ten peptides were prioritized as candidate sequences for further experimental validation. This study demonstrates the feasibility of computationally deriving and prioritizing anticancer peptide candidates from L. major proteins KMP11 and GP63. The proposed framework provides a structured, hypothesis-generating strategy for ACP candidate prioritization, emphasizing comparative evaluation rather than direct prediction of therapeutic efficacy or specific molecular targets. By leveraging evolutionary and physicochemical features associated with host–pathogen interactions, this approach enables systematic exploration of parasite-derived peptide sequence space. Experimental validation will be essential to determine the biological activity, selectivity, and translational relevance of the identified candidates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00726-026-03540-2.

Introduction

Cancer remains the second leading cause of death worldwide, with its global burden projected to increase substantially in the coming decades. According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer, cancer-related deaths are expected to rise from approximately 9.6 million in 2018 to 13.1 million by 2030 if current trends persist (Bray et al. 2024, Siegel et al. 2024). This growing burden underscores the urgent need for innovative strategies in cancer treatment, diagnosis, and prevention (Sung et al. 2021, Farhood et al. 2018).

Among emerging therapeutic approaches, bioactive peptides—particularly anticancer peptides (ACPs)—have attracted significant attention due to their tumor-selective cytotoxicity, relatively low immunogenicity, and diverse mechanisms of action. These mechanisms, largely derived from antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), include membrane disruption, apoptosis induction, angiogenesis inhibition, and immune modulation (Chinnadurai et al. 2023, Norouzi et al. 2022, Sorolla et al. 2020, Foulds 1954, Abbas et al. 2014). Such properties make ACPs promising candidates for overcoming key limitations of conventional cancer therapies, including treatment resistance and off-target toxicity (Afonin et al. 2021, Daneshpour et al. 2019, Daneshpour et al. 2016, Okasha et al. 2021, Berriel et al. 2013), consistent with physicochemical- and evolution-guided peptide design strategies (Tornesello et al. 2020, Zare-Zardini et al. 2024).

ACPs have been identified from a wide range of natural and synthetic sources, including plants (Prabhu et al. 2013), animal venoms (Adade and Souto-Padrón 2015), marine organisms (Kang et al. 2018), and synthetic libraries (Hilchie et al. 2019). However, these sources often present challenges such as limited bioavailability, potential toxicity, or lack of evolutionary optimization for host interaction (Lath et al. 2023, Liscano et al. 2020). In contrast, parasite-derived peptides represent a unique and underexplored resource (Ruenchit et al. 2022). Due to their evolutionary adaptation for host interaction, immune evasion, and modulation of host cellular pathways, these molecules may offer advantageous structural and functional properties that can be repurposed for selective targeting of cancer cells (Eissa et al. 2024, Noya et al. 2013, Yousefi et al. 2023, Asghari et al. 2022).

The protozoan L. major expresses proteins with potential relevance for ACP design, particularly Kinetoplastid Membrane Protein-11 (KMP11) and Glycoprotein 63 (GP63). GP63 is a zinc-dependent surface metalloprotease that plays a central role in parasite–host interactions through proteolytic modulation of host signaling pathways, including cytokine regulation and kinase/phosphatase-mediated processes (Bagherzadeh et al. 2022, Shakibapour et al. 2021). In parallel, KMP11 is a small amphipathic membrane-associated protein involved in host–membrane interactions, including cholesterol-dependent modulation of membrane fluidity (Agrawal et al. 2021, Barati et al. 2022, Hallé et al. 2009). These functional characteristics are conceptually aligned with mechanisms exploited by many anticancer peptides, particularly membrane interaction and signaling modulation. Collectively, these properties provide a biologically plausible rationale for exploring GP63- and KMP11-derived peptide fragments as templates for computational ACP discovery.

It is important to note that the level of direct experimental evidence differs between the two proteins. GP63 has been more extensively studied due to its surface localization and its ability to modulate host signaling pathways overlapping with cancer-related processes, and GP63-derived sequences have also been explored in computational ACP screening studies (Hallé et al. 2009). In contrast, direct evidence supporting anticancer relevance of KMP11 is limited. However, its amphipathic structure and strong lipid bilayer interaction properties provide a mechanistically grounded basis for considering it as a source of membrane-active peptide fragments, consistent with the physicochemical principles underlying many ACPs.

Despite these promising characteristics, the anticancer potential of GP63- and KMP11-derived peptides has not been comprehensively explored through integrative computational approaches. In this study, we propose a systematic in silico framework incorporating motif analysis, physicochemical profiling, safety assessment (toxicity, allergenicity, and antigenicity), and controlled sequence optimization to identify high-confidence ACP candidates. The aim is to establish a rational and reproducible pipeline for the design and prioritization of parasite-derived peptides with potential anticancer activity and favorable computational profiles.

Methods

Extraction of reference sequence

The amino acid reference sequences of the target proteins GP63 (Protein ID: XP_001684335) and KMP11 (Protein ID: XP_003722616) were obtained in FASTA format from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) protein database (Sup Table 1). Sequence validity was confirmed by cross-checking their lengths and annotations with the corresponding GenBank records. Before further analysis, FASTA headers were standardized and quality control was performed to verify the absence of truncations or redundancies. The finalized sequences were then stored locally to maintain integrity during subsequent computational analyses.

Protein scanning for potential ACPs

Peptide fragments ranging from 5 to 25 amino acids were computationally generated from the FASTA sequences of GP63 and KMP11 using a custom Python script. All possible peptides were retained and screened for anticancer potential through the Protein Scanning module of the AntiCP 2.0 server (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/AntiCP2.0/) (Agrawal et al. 2021). AntiCP 2.0 was selected as the primary prediction platform due to its curated experimental training dataset, transparent classification strategy, and widespread use in ACP-related studies based on physicochemical descriptor–driven ACP discovery frameworks. Given the design-oriented objective of this work, which emphasizes peptide optimization and feature-driven ranking rather than classifier benchmarking, AntiCP 2.0 provided a suitable and reproducible foundation for initial screening. To reduce potential prediction bias associated with reliance on a single platform, peptide sequences were additionally evaluated using MLACP, an independent machine-learning–based anticancer peptide prediction tool. Unlike AntiCP 2.0, which was used for primary screening and mutation-guided optimization, MLACP served as an external validation model with a distinct prediction architecture and independent training characteristics. This complementary validation step enabled cross-confirmation of ACP potential and reduced overdependence on a single classifier. MLACP prediction scores were incorporated as an external validation criterion within the TOPSIS framework, rather than as a primary screening filter. We further attempted cross-validation using the iACP-FL server; however, the server was not accessible at the time of analysis, precluding reproducible evaluation. The AntiCP 2.0 Protein Scanning module applies an SVM-based classification model trained on curated experimental datasets to differentiate ACPs from non-ACPs. Scanning was performed with model 1, using the default support vector machine (SVM) threshold of 0.5. Consistent with the initial parameters, peptide lengths between 5 and 25 residues were analyzed. Fragments exceeding the 0.5 threshold were considered putative ACP candidates and selected for subsequent evaluation. Generation of peptide mutants

Mutated variants of the peptides identified in the previous phase were generated using a custom Python script. The number of mutations per peptide was defined by the formula β = ⌈α⌉, where α = (peptide length)/2; the number of mutations was then set as β − 1. This mutation scheme was designed to provide a controlled, length-scaled perturbation of each peptide sequence rather than unrestricted random mutagenesis. By scaling the number of substitutions relative to peptide length, the approach preserves core sequence identity while enabling local exploration of sequence space. All mutations were implemented as single-residue substitutions (sense mutations), ensuring structural continuity and preventing insertions or deletions that could lead to unrealistic peptide conformations. To avoid generation of nonfunctional variants, each mutant peptide was iteratively evaluated using AntiCP 2.0, and only variants exhibiting improved prediction scores relative to their parent sequences were retained as candidate sequences for subsequent evaluation, thereby imposing a performance-based selection constraint on the mutation process. This ensured that each peptide underwent multiple mutation cycles consistent with fragment length and optimization constraints. Each mutant was subsequently re-assessed using the AntiCP 2.0 server. Variants with prediction scores above 0.8 were iteratively subjected to further mutations as an operational threshold for sequence refinement until the maximum limit (β − 1) was reached. Peptide motif analysis

To identify sequence motifs associated with experimentally reported anticancer peptides, the MERCI software, a command-line motif discovery tool, was utilized. Positive and negative datasets (derived from the AntiCP 2.0 training and validation sets; see Methods Sect. 5) were analyzed under default parameters, with two adjustments: the motif count was set to “all” and the frequency threshold was lowered to 10. Identified motifs were then mapped onto both the original and mutated peptide sequences. Peptides containing at least one anticancer-associated (positive) motif were retained as candidates for downstream analysis, whereas sequences harboring only non-ACP motifs were excluded (Fig. 4). Motif presence was interpreted as a comparative indicator of similarity to experimentally reported ACP sequence patterns rather than as direct evidence of functional or structural anticancer activity. Because many ACP-associated motifs reflect broader physicochemical characteristics such as cationicity, amphipathicity, and membrane interaction propensity rather than receptor-specific domains, motif retention was used as one component of multi-criteria prioritization and not as an independent mechanistic validation step.Fig. 4Identification of motifs and prediction of physicochemical properties for generated peptides. Compilation of anticancer and non-anticancer datasets

Training and validation datasets from AntiCP 2.0 were merged to construct unified positive (anticancer) and negative (antimicrobial/non-ACP) peptide sets. The datasets were downloaded from the AntiCP 2.0 server (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/AntiCP2.0/download.php) and processed using a custom Python script. Positive entries from both training and testing sets were consolidated, as were the corresponding negative entries. This unified dataset served as the basis for downstream analyses of peptide physicochemical properties and biological activities, providing reference distributions for comparative physicochemical analysis (Sup Fig. 2).

Predicting physicochemical properties

The physicochemical properties of peptides from both the positive (anticancer) and negative (non-anticancer) datasets were systematically characterized using the AntiCP 2.0 web server (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/AntiCP2.0/). Twelve molecular descriptors were calculated for each peptide via the prediction module, including hydrophobicity (GRAVY index), steric hindrance (molar refractivity), side chain bulkiness, hydropathicity (Kyte–Doolittle scale), amphipathicity (hydrophobic moment), which was subsequently used for relative categorization of amphipathic properties, hydrophilicity (Hopp–Woods scale), net hydrogen count, formal charge at physiological pH, isoelectric point (pI), and molecular weight. These descriptors were chosen due to their reported roles in modulating peptide–membrane interactions and biological activity. The resulting property matrix was exported and archived for downstream statistical analyses (Fig. 4), providing a quantitative framework for differentiating ACPs from non-ACPs. These physicochemical descriptors were subsequently leveraged as independent, model-agnostic features in downstream analyses, thereby reducing reliance on AntiCP-derived scores alone and mitigating potential prediction bias.

Analyzing physicochemical differences

After predicting physicochemical properties for both positive (anticancer) and negative (non-anticancer) peptide datasets, quantitative comparative analyses were performed to examine differences across twelve molecular descriptors, including hydrophobicity, charge-related features, and molecular weight. The analysis was conducted using merged reference datasets derived from the AntiCP 2.0 training and validation sets, forming unified anticancer and non-anticancer peptide groups (Sup Fig. 2). For each descriptor, mean values, standard deviations, and corresponding p-values were calculated to assess the extent of separation between the two peptide classes (Sup Table 2). Several properties, most notably amphipathicity-related features (including hydrophobic moment), exhibited pronounced differences between anticancer and non-anticancer peptides, consistent with their established roles in peptide–membrane interactions. Rather than serving as a formal hypothesis-testing framework, this exploratory analysis provides a reference-based quantitative context, confirming that the selected physicochemical descriptors capture biologically relevant differences between anticancer and non-anticancer peptides. These findings support the suitability of the selected features for downstream multi-criteria ranking and comparative benchmarking analyses. In addition to p-values, effect sizes (Cohen’s d) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for each descriptor to quantify both the magnitude and precision of differences between ACP and non-ACP groups (Sup Table 2).

Toxicity, allergenicity, and antigenicity assessment

To assess potential toxicity, allergenicity, and antigenicity, three specialized computational tools were utilized: TOXINPRED2 (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/toxinpred2/), ALGPRED 2.0 (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/algpred2/), and VAXIJEN 2.0 (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html). All analyses were performed using default parameters to ensure consistency and reproducibility. VaxiJen evaluations employed the parasite-specific model to reflect the biological source of the peptides. Instead of applying fixed cutoffs, raw prediction scores (particularly for antigenicity) were retained and incorporated into the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) framework for objective, multi-criteria ranking, while toxicity and allergenicity predictions were reported as categorical outcomes (Bagherzadeh et al. 2022). Peptides flagged with unfavorable predictions in any category were not excluded but carried forward for comparative evaluation in subsequent analyses. These tools were chosen for their validated accuracy, methodological robustness, and relevance to the study’s objectives. TOXINPRED2 combines machine learning and motif-based strategies for accurate toxicity prediction, ALGPRED2 integrates SVM classification with IgE epitope mapping and allergen databases for allergenicity assessment, and VAXIJEN 2.0 estimates antigenicity from physicochemical properties, enhancing generalizability via its parasite-specific model. While these tools provide robust in silico screening, experimental validation remains essential to confirm safety and efficacy in complex biological contexts.

Implementation of decision model

Peptides were prioritized using the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) (Bagherzadeh et al. 2022), a multi-criteria decision-making framework that enables integrative ranking based on multiple heterogeneous attributes. Descriptor selection was guided by physicochemical and biological features commonly associated with anticancer peptide activity, including hydrophobicity-related properties, steric hindrance, charge, motif occurrence, and safety-related parameters. Prior to ranking, all criteria were normalized using vector normalization to ensure comparability across descriptors with different numerical scales. Equal weights were intentionally assigned to all criteria to minimize subjective bias, as no experimentally validated weighting scheme exists that reliably quantifies the relative biological importance of individual descriptors across diverse anticancer peptides. This strategy is commonly adopted in exploratory multi-criteria decision-making studies when empirical weighting schemes are unavailable. This choice reflects a conservative and transparent assumption suitable for exploratory peptide prioritization. The TOPSIS-based prioritization integrated multiple independent criteria, including physicochemical descriptors, motif counts, toxicity and allergenicity predictions, as well as prediction scores derived from AntiCP 2.0 and MLACP. This consensus-driven approach reduced dependence on any single predictive model and resulted in prioritized peptide rankings (Sup Fig. 3). The complete algorithmic workflow is provided in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Algorithm), ensuring transparency and reproducibility.

Benchmarking and framework validation

To evaluate the robustness and comparative performance of the proposed computational framework, a benchmarking analysis was conducted using the unified positive (anticancer) and negative (non-anticancer) peptide datasets described in Sect. 5. Physicochemical features predicted for these reference peptides (Sect. 6) were used to assess the discriminative behavior of the selected descriptors and to contextualize the TOPSIS-based ranking strategy relative to established ACP prediction tools.

Performance metrics derived from the reference datasets were qualitatively compared against reported outputs from existing ACP classifiers, enabling an indirect but informative benchmarking of the framework. This approach emphasizes interpretability and feature-level transparency rather than solely classifier accuracy. In contrast to conventional ACP pipelines that rely primarily on single-model classification outputs, the present framework integrates motif-guided sequence refinement, independent ACP validation using MLACP, membrane interaction propensity analysis, and exploratory docking-based receptor accessibility assessment to improve biological plausibility and ranking stability.

Rather than performing direct one-to-one comparisons with individual ACP predictors, the analysis focuses on whether the prioritized candidates occupy physicochemical and functional spaces commonly observed among experimentally validated ACPs. This strategy provides contextual benchmarking while maintaining a conservative interpretation of computational predictions.

3D structure prediction of peptides using Alphafold2

The five highest-ranked peptide sequences from each target protein, as identified by the TOPSIS decision-making model, were subjected to three-dimensional structure prediction using AlphaFold2 (version 2.3.2). The predicted models were generated primarily for comparative structural visualization and preparation of representative input conformations for exploratory downstream docking analyses rather than for definitive functional inference. Given the known limitations of AlphaFold2 for short peptide sequences (< 25 residues), which often exhibit intrinsic conformational flexibility and lack stable tertiary folds, these models were not interpreted as definitive folded structures. Prediction confidence was assessed using per-residue pLDDT scores, which are explicitly provided in the Supplementary Material and were used for qualitative assessment of model reliability; however, due to the flexible nature of short peptides, pLDDT values were interpreted cautiously and were not used as strict exclusion criteria. Structural assessment focused instead on general conformational features relevant to ACP behavior, including compactness, α-helical propensity, surface exposure of charged residues, hydrophobic residue distribution, and amphipathic organization, which are commonly associated with membrane-active anticancer peptides. Accordingly, AlphaFold2-derived structures were used solely as computational proxies and not as evidence of experimentally validated peptide conformations.

Molecular docking analysis using LightDock

Molecular docking analyses were performed using the LightDock software, a swarm-based docking framework designed for protein–protein and protein–peptide interactions. LightDock employs a flexible docking strategy that allows limited conformational sampling of peptide ligands while maintaining receptor rigidity, making it suitable for short peptide docking scenarios. The top-ranked peptides identified by the TOPSIS framework were docked against three cancer-associated receptors: epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), mouse double minute 2 homolog (MDM2), and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2), selected as representative cancer-related proteins to provide comparative structural context rather than as confirmed biological targets of ACP activity. Receptor structures were obtained from the Protein Data Bank, and peptide structures predicted by AlphaFold2 were used solely as initial input conformations. Docking simulations were performed using default LightDock parameters, and the resulting docking scores were used as relative indicators of receptor accessibility and interaction propensity in a comparative structural context. Docking outcomes were interpreted in a comparative and exploratory manner rather than to infer definitive binding mechanisms. Detailed docking scores are provided in Supplementary material. Docking results were interpreted alongside membrane interaction analyses to provide complementary mechanistic context without assuming receptor-specific modes of action. Docking simulations were designed as exploratory structural analyses to assess comparative receptor accessibility rather than to predict definitive biological targets or therapeutic efficacy.

Coarse-grained membrane interaction assessment

To assess peptide–membrane interaction propensity, a coarse-grained (CG) membrane interaction analysis was performed using a simplified slab-based energy model. Given that many anticancer peptides exert activity primarily through membrane disruption or selective membrane interaction rather than receptor-specific binding, membrane affinity analysis was considered a central mechanistic component of the framework. This approach was selected as a computationally efficient and mechanistically relevant alternative to full atomistic molecular dynamics simulations, particularly suited for short peptides where membrane affinity plays a central role in anticancer activity. In this model, a hydrophobic membrane core was represented as a planar slab centered at the membrane midplane, and residue-specific interaction energies were assigned based on amino acid physicochemical properties. Hydrophobic residues were assigned favorable interaction energies within the membrane core, whereas positively charged residues incurred energetic penalties upon membrane insertion. Peptides were systematically translated along the membrane normal (z-axis), and the total interaction energy was computed as the sum of residue-specific contributions at each insertion depth. The minimum interaction energy and corresponding preferred insertion depth were recorded for each peptide. This analysis enabled comparative evaluation of membrane affinity across peptide candidates while avoiding overinterpretation of detailed atomic interactions. The membrane interaction assessment was used exclusively as a comparative, hypothesis-generating tool and not as a substitute for experimentally validated membrane insertion or full-scale molecular dynamics simulations. The resulting membrane interaction metrics were incorporated as comparative indicators within the multi-criteria evaluation framework to support biologically informed prioritization of candidate peptides.

Results

Candidate peptide generation and anticancer profiling

The amino acid sequences of GP63 and KMP11 were retrieved from the NCBI database and validated for completeness and accuracy (Fig. 1; full sequences in Sup Table 2). Overlapping peptide fragments of 5–25 amino acids were computationally generated using a custom Python script, resulting in 11,592 peptides derived from GP63 and 1,638 from KMP11.

To assess their anticancer potential, we applied the AntiCP 2.0 server (model 1), which employs an SVM classifier trained on experimentally validated anticancer and antimicrobial peptides. At the default SVM threshold of 0.5, 4,647 peptides from GP63 and 680 from KMP11 were predicted to possess anticancer properties (GP63 results in Sup File 1; KMP11 results in Sup File 2).

Interestingly, both GP63- and KMP11-derived peptides showed a higher proportion of predicted ACPs within the shorter length range, where predicted ACPs outnumbered non-ACPs. Shorter peptides tended to be enriched in ACPs (e.g., 62 ACPs vs. 26 non-ACPs), whereas longer peptides were more frequently classified as non-ACPs (Fig. 5A, B). Refined peptide selection via rigorous mutation and score-based criteria

To further optimize candidate peptides within the computational framework, we implemented a systematic mutation strategy coupled with rigorous selection criteria. Using the AntiCP 2.0 server with an elevated prediction score threshold of 0.8, we refined the initial peptide pool to identify high-scoring variants. This process yielded 547 optimized peptides derived from GP63 and 99 from KMP11 (GP63 results in Sup File 3; KMP11 results in Sup File 4). This stringent, iterative filtering approach enriched the dataset with peptide variants exhibiting improved prediction scores under the applied selection criteria, thereby prioritizing candidates with favorable computational anticancer profiles for subsequent analyses.

Dataset assembly and motif profiling

A comprehensive dataset of anticancer (positive) and non-anticancer (negative) peptides was constructed by integrating the internal training and external validation sets of AntiCP 2.0, yielding a curated collection of 1,247 sequences comprising 832 ACPs and 415 non-ACPs (Fig. 4). Motif analysis using MERCI software revealed distinct sequence signatures between the two groups. Positive-class peptides showed significant enrichment in 12–13 amino acid motifs based on motif occurrence frequency (p < 0.001, Fisher’s exact test), with a prevalence of 23.7%. This motif length range is frequently reported among membrane-active regions of experimentally characterized anticancer peptides. In contrast, the negative dataset lacked short motifs (3–5 residues) and exhibited lower motif occurrence frequency in longer motifs (6–50 residues), with only 8.2% of sequences containing motifs longer than 15 residues (Fig. 5C, D).

Predicting physicochemical properties

Using a comparative physicochemical analysis approach similar to that described by Bagherzadeh et al. (Bagherzadeh et al. 2026), we characterized the physicochemical features distinguishing ACPs from non-ACPs by analyzing a dataset comprising key molecular descriptors. Descriptive statistics were used to quantitatively compare physicochemical properties between anticancer and non-anticancer peptide groups. Statistical comparisons and corresponding p-values derived from the reference datasets are reported in Sup Tables 2 to provide contextual, reference-based differentiation rather than formal hypothesis testing. On average, ACPs exhibited lower hydrophobicity (mean = −0.091 vs. −0.128), steric hindrance, side-chain bulkiness (both 0.602 vs. 0.617), and molecular weight (2313.97 Da vs. 2975.56 Da) compared to non-ACPs (p < 0.001 for all), indicating a trend toward reduced steric constraints and lower molecular size among ACPs within the reference dataset. These quantitative differences highlight structural trends distinguishing ACPs from non-ACPs within the reference datasets. Additionally, ACPs showed higher amphipathicity (0.784 vs. 0.656) and hydropathicity (0.176 vs. 0.006), indicating a more balanced hydrophilic-hydrophobic profile commonly associated with membrane-interacting bioactive peptides. The isoelectric point (pI) was significantly elevated in ACPs (9.41 vs. 9.16; p = 0.008), while net hydrogen content was slightly reduced (0.741 vs. 0.808; p < 0.001). Hydrophilicity was modestly lower in ACPs (−0.121 vs. −0.074; p = 0.034). Although ACPs carried a higher net charge on average (3.55 vs. 3.29), this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.061) (Sup Tables 2 and Sup Fig. 4).

These trends were further supported by violin plots, which demonstrated narrower distributions for steric hindrance and side-chain bulk, alongside broader distributions for amphipathicity in ACPs, reflecting greater structural diversity. Overall, these findings describe a physicochemical profile frequently reported in studies of experimentally characterized anticancer peptides, with amphipathicity, hydropathicity, steric profile, and molecular weight emerging as informative features. Therefore, these features provide a quantitative basis for downstream multi-criteria ranking and comparative benchmarking within our computational framework (GP63 = Sup File 5; KMP11 = Sup File 6).

Analyzing physicochemical differences

Comparative analysis of physicochemical properties between anticancer peptides (ACPs) and non-anticancer peptides was performed using merged AntiCP 2.0 reference datasets. Descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation) and quantitative group-wise comparisons (p-values reported in Sup Table 2) indicate that ACPs are characterized by significantly higher amphipathicity and hydropathicity, rather than globally increased hydrophobicity, compared with non-ACPs. While average GRAVY hydrophobicity values were comparable or slightly lower in ACPs, the enhanced amphipathic balance and hydrophobic moment are consistent with physicochemical profiles commonly associated with membrane-interacting peptides without excessive nonspecific hydrophobicity. These properties are well established as frequently reported contributors to selective peptide–membrane interactions observed in many experimentally validated anticancer peptides. This analysis is presented as reference-based comparative evidence to contextualize feature selection, rather than as definitive mechanistic proof. These differences are supported by effect size analysis, which indicates that amphipathicity and molecular weight contribute most strongly to the separation between ACP and non-ACP groups (Sup Table 2).

In contrast, non-ACPs showed greater hydrophilicity, slightly higher hydrogen content, and increased molecular weight, suggesting a preference for aqueous-phase interactions and reduced membrane affinity. Although ACPs displayed a marginally higher net charge, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.061). In contrast, steric hindrance, side-chain bulkiness, and pI showed statistically significant—but quantitatively modest—differences between groups (Sup Table 2), suggesting that the most discriminative separation is driven primarily by amphipathicity- and hydropathicity-related features rather than by large shifts in steric or ionization properties. This observation is consistent with the notion that electrostatic interactions in ACPs may depend more on localized charge distribution than on global pI alone. These findings provide guidance for computational peptide design, particularly for optimizing amphipathic balance and molecular size in membrane-active anticancer peptides. The hydrophobic–amphipathic profile of ACPs supports the rational development of synthetic peptides optimized for membrane penetration, whereas the hydrophilic character and larger size of non-ACPs suggests that non-ACPs may be comparatively more compatible with aqueous-phase contexts, underscoring mechanistic differences relevant to peptide behavior. Although steric hindrance and pI showed statistically significant differences between groups, the generally small to moderate effect sizes (Sup Table 2), with amphipathicity and molecular weight showing the most pronounced differences, suggest that functional divergence is driven primarily by amphipathicity- and hydropathicity-related features rather than large shifts in steric or ionization properties. These findings are summarized in Sup Fig. 6.

Importantly, the candidate peptides derived from GP63 and KMP11 exhibited physicochemical features comparable to those commonly reported for known ACPs, including lower molecular weight, enhanced amphipathicity, and balanced hydropathicity. Motif analysis also revealed several novel yet functionally relevant sequence signatures enriched among ACPs, resembling established membrane-interacting domains. These observations support the internal consistency of the computational pipeline and provide reference-based context for prioritizing the optimized candidates.

Toxicity, allergenicity, and antigenicity assessment

Using computational prediction platforms TOXINPRED2, ALGPRED2, and VAXIJEN2, we systematically assessed the toxicity, allergenicity, and antigenicity profiles of the candidate peptides. Among the GP63 derived peptides, a total of 309 were predicted to be non-allergenic, and 536 were identified as potentially non-toxic. Among KMP11-derived peptides, 98 sequences classified as non-toxic; however, only 51 were predicted to be non-allergenic. These predictions indicate that a substantial subset of peptides from both proteins were predicted as non-toxic by the applied computational tools, while allergenicity remains a more restrictive filter for KMP11-derived candidates (Sup Files 5 and 6). These safety-related predictions were incorporated as comparative criteria within the multi-parameter prioritization framework rather than used as absolute exclusion filters.

Implementation of the decision model TOPSIS and 3D structure prediction of peptides

To refine candidate selection, we applied the TOPSIS model, which integrates multiple physicochemical and functional parameters into a unified ranking framework. This approach enabled the systematic evaluation of peptide attributes and facilitated the prioritization of sequences with the most favorable computational profiles under the selected criteria. Based on the aggregated scoring criteria, the top 10 peptides with the highest TOPSIS scores were identified as the most promising ACP candidates. Molecular docking analysis

To provide structural context for the prioritized peptides, molecular docking analyses were performed using LightDock against three cancer-associated receptors (EGFR, MDM2, and VEGFR2). Docking results were interpreted exclusively in a comparative and exploratory manner, aiming to assess potential receptor accessibility rather than to infer specific binding mechanisms or target engagement. Consistent with the known limitations of peptide docking, docking scores were not compared to native ligands and were used solely as relative indicators of interaction propensity among the selected peptides. Docking behavior varied across receptors and peptide origins, suggesting differential interaction tendencies under the applied computational conditions. Docking scores for the prioritized peptides are summarized in Table 1, while complete docking outputs are provided in Sup File 11.

Membrane interaction analysis

The coarse-grained membrane interaction analysis revealed that the top-ranked peptides exhibited favorable interaction energies within the hydrophobic membrane slab, consistent with physicochemical trends frequently reported for experimentally validated anticancer peptides. Notably, peptides derived from KMP11 and GP63 displayed distinct preferred insertion depths, reflecting differences in amphipathic residue distribution, hydrophobic moment, and charge distribution. Despite variations in sequence origin, all selected peptides demonstrated comparable ranges of minimum interaction energies, supporting their potential for membrane association under the applied computational model. These results provide complementary, mechanistically consistent computational context for peptide–membrane affinity and reinforce the biological plausibility of the selected candidates without implying definitive membrane insertion or cytotoxic activity. Discussion

The therapeutic application of peptides in oncology has gained considerable momentum in recent years, with ACPs emerging as a widely investigated class of biologics due to their selectivity, potential functional activity, and diverse mechanisms of action (Tyagi et al. 2013). In this study, we focused on the parasite L. major, investigating two of its key proteins, GP63 and KMP11, as potential templates for the development of parasite-derived ACPs through a comprehensive in silico approach. Previous research has demonstrated the therapeutic relevance of parasite-derived peptides in cancer models (Shakibapour et al. 2021, Barati et al. 2022). For example, Dos Santos et al. showed that administration of L. braziliensis demonstrated strong antitumor effects, significantly slowing tumor progression and extending survival in mice with non-Hodgkin lymphoma (Dos Santos et al. 2024), while Caner et al. reported that live-attenuated Leishmania strains were able to regress 4T1 breast tumors in mice without causing pathogenic effects and elicited a robust immune response against the cancer (Caner et al. 2020). These findings collectively underscore the potential of parasite-derived molecules as a source of bioactive components for anticancer peptide discovery, rather than as direct therapeutic agents.

In our investigation, GP63 and KMP11 were analyzed at the peptide level to identify sequences with potential anticancer activity. Using the AntiCP 2.0 platform (Agrawal et al. 2021), this enabled the identification of conserved sequence patterns predicted to be associated with anticancer activity. The physicochemical properties of these motifs and peptides were then compared with known ACP datasets to assess their structural and physicochemical relevance. Notably, effect size analysis indicated that amphipathicity and molecular weight contribute most strongly to the separation between ACP and non-ACP groups, while other descriptors exhibited comparatively smaller effects, supporting a feature hierarchy consistent with membrane-active peptide behavior.

Importantly, our choice of GP63 and KMP-11 as source proteins was guided by prior evidence that Leishmania virulence determinants can reprogram host-cell signaling and membrane-associated processes. GP63 has been reported to modulate host signaling via proteolytic targeting of intracellular host proteins, including components involved in major regulatory pathways, while KMP-11 has been implicated in membrane interaction and membrane remodeling during host–parasite engagement. These features motivated our hypothesis that short, optimized fragments derived from these proteins could display membrane affinity consistent with membrane-active anticancer peptides, while allowing exploratory evaluation of potential receptor accessibility in a comparative computational context, supporting the conceptual basis of the current in silico discovery workflow. We emphasize that these analyses provide comparative and hypothesis-generating support and do not replace experimental validation of mechanism or in-cell activity.

For GP63, substantial evidence supports its involvement in host–cell signaling reprogramming through proteolytic targeting of host substrates, consistent with its role as a surface metalloprotease central to parasite–host interactions. Recent in silico work has also explored GP63-derived sequences in the context of anticancer peptide discovery, providing additional rationale for its use as a template protein in computational exploration and ACP candidate screening (Macdonald et al. 1995).

In contrast, we did not find direct reports describing KMP11 as an anticancer protein. However, KMP11 is a small amphipathic, membrane-associated virulence factor with strong lipid-bilayer activity. Experimental and biophysical studies indicate that KMP11 interacts strongly with lipid bilayers, perturbs membrane organization, and exhibits sterol-dependent membrane behavior. These properties provide a mechanistically plausible rationale for including KMP11 as a peptide source when the downstream evaluation explicitly includes membrane-interaction propensity, a key determinant of many ACP mechanisms (Mendonca et al. 2015).

Safety and immunogenicity assessments were integrated into the screening pipeline, evaluating toxicity, allergenicity, and antigenicity to identify peptides with favorable predicted safety profiles. Finally, we employed the TOPSIS multi-criteria decision-making framework to systematically rank the candidates based on their predicted properties. The top ten peptides identified through this approach represent computationally prioritized candidates that may serve as a starting point for subsequent experimental validation.

The TOPSIS-based prioritization strategy applied in this study should be interpreted as an exploratory integrative framework rather than a definitive predictor of biological efficacy. Accordingly, the final ranking represents a consensus-based, hypothesis-generating framework derived from multiple independent evaluation layers rather than a single-model predictive output. This consensus-driven prioritization reduces dependence on individual predictors and improves the robustness of candidate selection. The assignment of equal weights across all descriptors was an intentional and conservative modeling choice, reflecting the absence of experimentally validated evidence defining the relative biological importance of individual physicochemical, safety-related, or predictive features for anticancer peptides. Importantly, the robustness of the resulting peptide ranking is supported by convergent evidence from multiple independent analyses. Top-ranked peptides consistently exhibited favorable profiles across orthogonal evaluation layers, including independent ACP prediction tools, membrane-interaction propensity analyses, and exploratory molecular docking assessments performed to evaluate receptor accessibility in a comparative context. This convergence reduces the likelihood that peptide prioritization was driven by any single descriptor or weighting assumption, thereby supporting the stability and reproducibility of the ranking outcome.

Although equal weighting may not fully reflect the true biological contribution of individual descriptors, no experimentally validated evidence currently supports a universally applicable weighting scheme for ACP prioritization across structurally diverse peptides. Therefore, equal weighting was intentionally adopted as a conservative strategy to minimize subjective bias and preserve reproducibility. Formal sensitivity analyses using alternative weighting strategies such as entropy-based weighting, the analytic hierarchy process (AHP), or comparative evaluation using alternative multi-criteria decision-making frameworks were beyond the scope of the present study but represent important directions for future work to quantitatively assess ranking stability and descriptor influence.

Although AntiCP 2.0 served as the primary screening engine in this study, final candidate selection was not based solely on a single classifier output. Independent validation using MLACP provided orthogonal predictive support and reduced model-specific bias. Furthermore, motif preservation, physicochemical constraints, safety profiling, docking analysis, membrane interaction assessment, and TOPSIS-based multi-criteria ranking collectively contributed to candidate prioritization. This multi-layer decision framework improves robustness and reduces the risk of overfitting to any single ACP prediction model.

The MERCI-based motif analysis should also be interpreted conservatively. Identified motifs were used primarily to assess whether candidate peptides retained sequence patterns commonly observed among experimentally validated ACPs rather than to infer definitive functional domains or receptor-specific mechanisms. Importantly, these motifs are consistent with well-established physicochemical patterns underlying membrane-active anticancer peptide mechanisms, including amphipathic organization, cationic charge distribution, and membrane interaction propensity. Therefore, motif retention was considered supportive comparative evidence within the multi-layer prioritization framework rather than standalone evidence of direct anticancer activity or specific molecular mechanism. Experimental validation through structure-guided mutagenesis, molecular dynamics simulations, or functional cytotoxicity assays will be necessary to confirm the biological relevance of these motifs.

With respect to benchmarking against previously reported anticancer peptide discovery pipelines, the present study differs from conventional ACP screening approaches by combining biologically informed source selection with multi-layer prioritization rather than relying solely on classifier-based prediction. Many existing pipelines primarily depend on sequence classification tools such as AntiCP, iACP, or CancerPPD-based similarity screening. In contrast, our framework integrates controlled mutagenesis, motif preservation, independent ACP validation using MLACP, membrane interaction propensity analysis, and exploratory receptor accessibility evaluation through docking. Importantly, reference datasets curated within AntiCP 2.0, which incorporate experimentally validated peptides from established resources such as CancerPPD, were used to provide quantitative context for feature selection and candidate prioritization (Tyagi et al. 2015). This approach is consistent with previously reported physicochemical descriptor– and evolution-informed ACP discovery strategies, where structural and compositional properties guide candidate selection rather than direct efficacy prediction. Thus, the novelty of the present work lies in the structured integration of these orthogonal decision layers within a parasite-derived peptide discovery framework rather than in proposing a new standalone classifier.

We emphasize that LightDock-based docking was employed as a comparative and exploratory tool to assess receptor accessibility and does not constitute definitive evidence of stable binding or therapeutic efficacy. The coarse-grained membrane interaction analysis presented here is intended to provide comparative insight into membrane affinity, a key determinant of anticancer peptide behavior, and should not be interpreted as a substitute for detailed atomistic simulations or experimental membrane assays.

The AlphaFold2-based structural analysis should also be interpreted cautiously. Structural prediction was used primarily for comparative visualization of candidate peptide conformations rather than as definitive evidence of functional activity or experimentally validated receptor-binding mechanisms. Because many short peptides remain conformationally flexible in solution and may not adopt stable tertiary structures, residue-level confidence metrics such as pLDDT scores are often less informative than broader structural tendencies. In the context of anticancer peptides, secondary structure propensity—particularly α-helical tendency and amphipathic organization—together with charge distribution and membrane affinity are often more biologically relevant than stable globular folding. These structural features are consistent with known mechanisms of membrane-active anticancer peptides, where amphipathic α-helical organization and charge distribution play a central role in membrane interaction. Accordingly, structural observations were interpreted alongside physicochemical descriptors and membrane interaction analyses rather than as standalone predictors of activity. More detailed validation using molecular dynamics simulations and experimental biophysical characterization will be necessary in future studies.

The investigation of Leishmania-derived ACPs represents a promising yet underexplored direction in anticancer peptide research, capitalizing on the parasite’s evolutionary adaptations for host-pathogen interaction, immune evasion, and modulation of host signaling pathways that overlap with cancer-related processes (Chinnadurai et al. 2023, Lath et al. 2023). Despite their potential, several challenges must be overcome before clinical translation, including structural refinement to enhance tumor specificity, improved stability in systemic circulation, and scalable production strategies that minimize immune responses. Advances in computational peptide design and engineering offer potential solutions to improve pharmacokinetic properties, while high-throughput screening can facilitate the identification of minimal bioactive domains with improved biophysical and functional properties. Additionally, the incorporation of targeted delivery systems such as nanoparticles and liposomes may help reduce peptide degradation and improve tumor accumulation (Zhang et al. 2023, Bakare et al. 2021, Karami Fath et al. 2022). However, a critical barrier remains the risk of cross-reactivity with normal cells due to shared signaling pathways, highlighting the need for rigorous selectivity profiling. Nevertheless, the potential biocompatibility and multifunctional properties suggested by parasite-derived peptides underscore their potential as a complementary source of ACPs. Addressing these challenges will require interdisciplinary strategies that integrate bioinformatics, synthetic biology, advanced drug delivery platforms, and precision oncology approaches (Chen et al. 2022, Gaurav et al. 2021, Chunarkar-Patil et al. 2024).

Mechanistically, the combined docking and membrane interaction analyses suggest that the prioritized peptides may potentially interact with cancer-related targets through two complementary interaction modes in a comparative computational context. LightDock-based docking indicates that several top-ranked peptides can access surface-exposed regions of cancer-associated receptors, suggesting potential receptor accessibility in a comparative computational context rather than stable or high-affinity binding. In parallel, the coarse-grained membrane interaction analysis highlights favorable membrane association profiles, consistent with physicochemical features commonly observed in anticancer peptides. Collectively, these findings are consistent with a membrane-accessibility and receptor-proximity model, rather than implying definitive receptor inhibition or direct cytotoxic mechanisms. Importantly, all findings presented in this study are derived from computational analyses and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of in vitro or in vivo anticancer activity.

Overall, our study presents a comprehensive computational framework for the identification and prioritization of parasite-derived anticancer peptide candidates, integrating predictive modeling, motif-based screening, safety profiling, and multi-criteria optimization. The findings provide a structured basis for hypothesis generation and comparative mechanistic evaluation rather than direct evidence of therapeutic efficacy. The identified peptides represent computationally prioritized candidates for future experimental validation, where their biological activity, selectivity, and translational relevance should be systematically assessed. Although cross-validation with MLACP strengthened the methodological robustness of the framework, the unavailability of certain external servers (e.g., iACP-FL) limited additional cross-platform validation at the time of analysis, and future studies will further expand this framework as such tools become accessible.

The mutagenesis strategy employed here does not explicitly incorporate evolutionary conservation or property-based design matrices; however, the objective of the current work was to perform controlled sequence-space exploration under multiple independent selection constraints. Compared with unrestricted random mutagenesis, the length-scaled substitution framework used in this study preserves biologically relevant sequence identity while enabling local optimization of ACP-related features. This approach reduces excessive sequence drift and helps maintain motif continuity inherited from the parent parasite-derived peptides. Formal benchmarking against fully random mutation strategies and alternative rational design schemes was beyond the scope of the present study, but represents an important direction for future work to quantitatively assess optimization efficiency and ranking robustness. Integration of evolutionary or structure-guided mutation strategies therefore represents an important future extension of the framework. Importantly, the present framework is not intended to replace experimental discovery pipelines but rather to refine the hypothesis space by reducing sequence complexity and prioritizing biologically plausible candidates, thereby enabling more efficient downstream experimental validation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study describes an integrative computational framework for the identification and prioritization of parasite-derived anticancer peptide candidates. By combining predictive modeling, motif-based screening, safety profiling, molecular docking, and membrane interaction assessment, the proposed workflow facilitates systematic candidate selection while maintaining a conservative, hypothesis-generating perspective. The findings provide comparative mechanistic context regarding membrane affinity and receptor accessibility rather than direct evidence of therapeutic efficacy or clinical applicability. Experimental validation will be essential to determine the biological activity, selectivity, and translational potential of the proposed peptide candidates.

Limitations

While our in silico strategy offers a comprehensive and scalable computational framework for anticancer peptide discovery, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the motif analysis conducted using MERCI, although informative, is inherently dependent on the quality, diversity, and size of the input datasets, which may restrict the identification of rare or context-specific motifs. Future studies could address this limitation by integrating multiple motif discovery tools and incorporating larger experimentally validated datasets to improve both sensitivity and generalizability. Second, the present study is entirely computational and lacks experimental validation. In vitro and in vivo experiments will be essential to confirm the predicted peptides’ anticancer activity, selectivity, stability, and safety under physiological conditions. Third, although molecular docking and coarse-grained membrane interaction analyses were incorporated to provide comparative mechanistic insight, these approaches were applied in an explicitly exploratory manner. Docking simulations were used to assess relative receptor accessibility rather than to establish definitive binding affinities or functional inhibition, while membrane interaction analysis provides qualitative and comparative insight into membrane affinity rather than atomistic or quantitative detail. Future studies integrating high-resolution atomistic molecular dynamics simulations and experimental binding assays will be required to refine mechanistic interpretations. Lastly, due to the qualitative nature of some advanced allergenicity prediction tools, quantitative scores derived from AlgPred were employed to ensure compatibility with the TOPSIS decision-making framework. Future approaches may explore hybrid scoring strategies or alternative multi-criteria models capable of integrating qualitative and quantitative safety descriptors more effectively. Collectively, these limitations underscore the importance of combining advanced computational pipelines with rigorous experimental validation to fully realize the translational potential of peptide-based anticancer therapeutics.
