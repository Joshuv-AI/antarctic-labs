A systematic review and meta-analysis on labour pain management methods utilization and its associated factors among obstetric caregivers in Ethiopia
Authors: Keralem Anteneh Bishaw, Bewket Yeserah Aynalem, Demewoze Kefale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite growing attention to maternal health, labour pain management remains a neglected aspect of obstetric care in Ethiopia. Existing studies in Ethiopia on labour pain management among obstetric caregivers (OCGs) provide some insights; their findings are often inconsistent, geographically limited, and lack national representativeness. We therefore conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to examine the utilization of labour pain management methods and associated factors among obstetric caregivers in Ethiopia.MethodsThis review included all observational, full-text, English-language, published, and gray studies that reported on labour pain management practices or associated factors among obstetric caregivers in Ethiopia, searched from July 1-September 12/2025. Studies were identified via a comprehensive search of MEDLINE, PUBMED, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, Google Scholar, CINAHL, SCOPUS, and a direct Google search of international databases. Methodological quality and heterogeneity among the included studies was assessed using the Hoy D et al. risk of bias tool & I² statistic, respectively. Publication bias was checked using Egger’s and funnel plots. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 17.0.ResultsHalf of the included studies were rated as high quality. About 45.18% of OCGs utilized labour pain management methods (95% CI: 39.87–50.48). Utilization was higher among female OCGs (Adjusted Odd Ratio [AOR] = 1.90; 95% CI: 1.24–2.93), midwives (AOR = 1.50; 95% CI: 1.15–1.94), having adequate knowledge of labour pain (AOR = 2.61; 95% CI: 1.51–4.53), having received training on labour pain management (AOR = 3.05; 95% CI: 2.17–4.30), working in facilities with adequate supplies (AOR = 5.43; 95% CI: 2.90-10.14), the presence of labour pain management protocol (AOR = 2.06; 95% CI: 1.46–2.90), and perceiving of labour pain as moderate to severe (AOR = 2.21; 95% CI: 1.40–3.49).ConclusionFewer than one in two OCGs utilized labour pain management methods. Individual caregiver characteristics (sex, profession, knowledge) and institutional factors (protocols, supplies, training) were associated with labour pain relief utilization among obstetric caregivers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09496-1.

Background

Labour pain management methods include pharmacological (spinal techniques, nitrous oxide, and systemic opioids like pethidine) and non-pharmacological (transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, acupressure, acupuncture, aromatherapy, and breathing exercises) [1]. Effective management of labour pain is a fundamental component of respectful and high-quality intrapartum care. It is not merely a humanitarian concern but a critical element for improving maternal and neonatal outcomes. Unrelieved severe labour pain can lead to a cascade of physiological and psychological sequelae, including hyperventilation, reduced uterine blood flow, and prolonged labour, which subsequently increase the risk of fetal distress and operative interventions [2, 3]. Furthermore, a traumatic birth experience due to unmanaged labour pain is strongly associated with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), which negatively affects mother-infant bonding [4]. When analgesia is offered to labouring women, there may be greater satisfaction with their overall childbirth experience [5].

Managing labour pain is a complex and multifaceted process demanding a comprehensive approach [6]. Despite the existence of numerous labour pain relief options, utilization is largely limited to developed countries [7] compared to sub-Saharan Africa [8]. Studies in Ethiopia have identified multiple factors associated with the utilization of labour pain management methods among OCGs. Individual factors include age [9], sex [10–12], profession [10, 12, 13], years of experience [10, 13–17], educational level [10, 14, 15, 18–20], knowledge regarding labour pain management [9, 14, 16–24], attitude towards labour pain [9, 10, 12–16, 18, 24, 25], expectation of labour pain severity among OCGs [12, 16, 25], receiving training on labour pain management [12, 19, 25], perception side effect of pharmacologic obstetric analgesia [20] and having information about WHO pain ladder [12]. Institutional factors include allowing a companion during labour and delivery [22], availability of labour pain management protocol [9, 16], working in satisfactory delivery rooms [13], attending deliveries at private health facilities [23], and having the option to select preferred labour pain management methods within the facility [12].

In Ethiopian health facilities, OCGs consist of trained professionals such as midwives, nurses, health officers, medical doctors, obstetrician-gynecologists, and integrated emergency surgical officers (IESOs) who provide care for the woman during labour and delivery [26]. Previous reviews [27, 28] included 17 and 19 studies, respectively, and yielded fragmented and non-representative evidence on labour pain management in Ethiopia. In contrast, the current review included 24 published and gray literature and several relevant factors not comprehensively considered in earlier reviews, including OCG profession, labour pain management training, adequate facility supplies, availability of pain management protocols, and perceptions of labour pain severity. Therefore, this study aimed to fill this critical evidence gap by generating updated national level evidence on the pooled prevalence of labour pain management methods utilization and associated factors among OCGs. The findings are expected to provide actionable evidence for the development of targeted interventions, national guidelines, and training programs, thereby improving the quality of obstetric care and labouring women’s childbirth experiences.

Methods

Systematic review registration, study design and searching strategies

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the utilization of labour pain management methods as the primary outcome and factors affecting its utilization as the secondary outcome in Ethiopia. We registered the protocol with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), University of York, National Institute for Health Research (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/), with a registration number CRD42020172433. All published and gray articles were searched in the major international databases such as MEDLINE, PUBMED, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, Google Scholar, CINAHL, SCOPUS, and a direct Google search. Databases were searched from July 1-September 12, 2025. Additionally, reviewing the reference list of related articles and a desk review of the doctoral dissertations were used to find out related articles available at Ethiopian university libraries. Different Boolean operators AND, &, and OR were used in the database search. The searching strategy applied was obstetric caregivers OR/AND Utilization AND /OR labour pain management methods OR associated factors OR Ethiopia. Study selection and eligibility criteria

All observational full-text, English-language published, and gray studies reporting labour pain management prevalence or the associated factors up to September 12/2025, among obstetric caregivers in Ethiopia were included in the review. However, articles without full access and did not report the outcome of the interest were excluded. Initially, articles were assessed independently for inclusion through a title and abstract. In case of duplicated data, the full-text published article was retained. Reviewing of the title, abstract and the whole paper was done by two reviewers (Bishaw, KA and Aynalem, BY). Any disagreement with the two reviewers was solved by consensus through reviewers of DK. Finally, only full published articles and doctoral dissertations was retained when duplication of data occurred.

Quality assessment and data extraction

The Hoy D et al. risk of bias tool a validated, standardized tool specifically designed to assess the methodological quality of observational and prevalence studies. We used this tool to assess the included studies with two reviewers (Bishaw, KA and Aynalem, BY) [30]. Data were extracted using a pre-piloted data extraction format by Microsoft excel spreadsheet. Data extraction format was prepared by Bishaw, KA. It included name author, year of publication /study, study area, study design, sample size, prevalence of labour pain management methods utilization, and AOR (adjusted odds ratio) of associated factors. Primary data extraction was done by Bishaw, KA and Aynalem, BY, and then DK examined the extracted data independently. Any inconsistencies and disagreement were resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

After data extracted, it exported to stata version 17.0 software for analysis. I2 test statistic was used to assess heterogeneity among studies. Random effect meta-analysis model was used to determine the pooled prevalence of utilization of labour pain relief methods [31]. Meta-regression [32] and subgroup analysis [33] were also computed to investigate the source of heterogeneity among studies. The publication bias was assessed using Egger [34] and Begg [35] tests with a p-value less than 0.05. The effect of selected associated variables was analyzed using separate categories of meta-analysis [36]. The findings of the meta-analysis were presented using a forest plot and pooled odds ratio (OR) with its 95% confidence interval (CI). We conducted a sensitivity analysis to assess whether the pooled prevalence estimates were influenced by individual studies.

Results

Article selection & characteristics of the included studies

A total of 1667 published articles and 5 Gy literatures were identified in the review. Of these, 124 duplicated and 1519 articles were excluded after reviewing of the title and abstracts. The remaining 190 items were screened title and abstract for eligibility. Then the remaining twenty-seven records were assessed for eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. In addition, three articles were excluded because it does not report the outcome of interest [37–39]. Finally, a total of 24 studies included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Quality assessment and risk of bias

We assessed the quality of the included studies using Hoy et al.‘s risk of bias tool. This tool has 10 items, each scored as “yes” (low risk = 1) or “no” (high risk = 0), to evaluate representativeness, internal validity, measurement accuracy, and confounding [30]. Half of the studies, (50%, n = 12) were rated as high quality (≥ 8 points), while the half (50%, n = 12) medium quality (5–7 points). Regarding sample size, 20.8% of studies (n = 5) had sample size less 200 [20, 41, 43, 44, 47] and only one study had a sample size exceeding 500 participants [9]. Some the studies used a pretested and validated data collection tool [21, 25, 46] (Table 1).

Utilization of labour pain management methods

The highest (69.7%) utilization of labour pain management methods reported in Harar [10] and lowest (13.8%) in SNNP [15]. The pooled utilization labour pain management method among OCGs was 45.18% with a 95% CI: (39.87–50.48) with a significant heterogeneity between studies (I2 =95.65, p value = 0.000). Therefore, we performed subgroup analysis using region, sample size and year of study to investigate the source heterogeneity across studies (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis revealed significant regional variation, with the highest prevalence in Harar (59.82) and the lowest in SNNPR (32.940). Substantial and unexplained heterogeneity (I²≥91%, p < 0.001) was evident across nearly all subgroups by region, year of publication, and sample size, except for the “other” subgroup category by region (I²=0.00%, p = 0.63), which affected the reliability of the pooled prevalence. Importantly, neither the publication year nor the sample size explained the observed between study variation (Table 2).

Factors associated with utilization of labour pain relief methods among obstetric caregivers

Several factors including, sex [10, 25], profession [10, 12, 13, 15], knowledge regarding labour pain management [9, 14, 16–24], attitude towards labour pain management [9, 12–16, 18, 24, 25], availability of analgesics and supplies [17, 19, 22, 24], educational level [9, 10, 15, 18–21], work experience [13, 14, 16, 17], allowing a companion during labour and delivery [22], availability of labour pain management protocol [9, 16], labour pain expectation among OCGs [12, 16, 25], having training on labour pain management [12, 19, 25], perceiving side effect of pharmacologic obstetric analgesia [20], working in satisfactory delivery rooms [13], attending delivery at private health facility [23], having information about WHO pain ladder [12], presence of a chance for mothers to prefer labour pain management methods in the facility [12] were factors reported associated with utilization of labour pain management methods among obstetric caregivers. For this meta-analysis, we considered only those factors cited in more than two studies.

According to this meta-analysis, several factors significantly affect the utilization of labour pain management among OCGs. Regarding individual characteristics, female OCGs were 1.90 times more likely to utilize these methods than male OCGs (POR 95% CI: 1.24–2.93), and midwives were 1.50 times more likely to provide these methods than other professional groups of OCGs (POR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.15–1.94). OCGs with adequate knowledge were 2.61 times more likely to utilize than those with inadequate knowledge (POR = 2.61, 95% CI: 1.51–4.53). Furthermore, trained OCGs were 3.05 times more likely to provide labour pain management methods compared to their counterparts (POR = 3.05, 95% CI: 2.17–4.30).

The working health facilities are also a critical determinant of labour pain management practice. OCGs working in health facilities with available obstetric analgesics and supplies were 5.43 times more likely to manage labour pain (POR = 5.43, 95% 2.90-10.14), while the presence of a labour pain management protocol made them 2.06 times more likely to utilize labour management methods (POR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.46–2.90). Finally, this study revealed OCGs who perceived labour pain as moderate to severe were 2.21 times more likely to utilize labour pain management than those who perceived it as mild (POR = 2.21, 95% CI: 1.40–3.49). Despite the statistically significant associations of knowledge regarding labour pain management and availability of drugs and supplies with labour pain management utilization of among OCGs, substantial heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I² = 92.1% and 80.1%, respectively). This variability limits the precision of the estimates and warrants cautious interpretation (Table 4).

Discussion

Despite the intense nature of labour pain [53], access to pain relief is often neglected in low-income countries [54]. Subgroup analysis identified significant regional variation in the utilization of labour pain management methods in Ethiopia, with prevalence ranging from 32.94% (95% CI: 21.72–44.16) in SNNPR to 59.82% (95% CI: 49.81–69.82) in Harar. Differences in obstetric care providers’ specialization level, years of experience, attitude, and knowledge of labour pain may account for this variation. Additionally, disparities in the availability of protocols, guidelines, equipment, and analgesics across different healthcare settings, from public health centers to specialized hospitals, may further explain these differences.

The average pooled utilization of labour pain relief methods among OCGs was 45.18% (95% CI: 39.87–50.48). Substantial heterogeneity was identified across studies (I² = 95.65%, p < 0.001). Differential misclassification bias, which affects the quality of primary study, was present in the included studies. Variations in study populations, lack of standardized outcome measurement, and differences in study settings (public health centers, primary hospitals, general hospitals, and comprehensive specialized hospitals) may have influenced the pooled prevalence, potentially distorting it from the true value. Consequently, this pooled prevalence should be interpreted with caution.

This finding is consistent with studies from Ethiopia (45.73%) [28], the United States (BroMenn Regional Medical Center, 42.2%) [55], Australia (42.5%) [56], and Norway [57]. However, higher utilization of labour pain management methods were reported in studies conducted in the United States, 80.1% [58] and 60.1% [59], Iran 54.5% [60], 58.6% Bangladesh [61], 55% Tamil Nadu [62], and 55% Poland [63] compared to this finding. This discrepancy may reflect differences in the quality of intrapartum maternal health services, including the presence of well-organized and equipped health facilities in developed countries, the availability of labour relief methods, the socio-demographic characteristics of study participants, and the hospital’s labour pain management protocol. Disparities in socioeconomic, educational level and cultural factors of labouring women may also contribute to this discrepancy. Labouring women with a high education level and, better socioeconomic backgrounds are more likely to request labour pain relief, which may lead to increased utilization of labour relief methods by obstetric caregivers, and, since about 75% obstetricians administer labour pain relief methods upon request [62], this leads to higher utilization. Conversely, in low-income settings, a shortage of trained OCGs [62, 64] and their perception pose a significant barrier. This is supported by one study where only 6.2% OCGs believed a woman in labour should receive an analgesic [61].

On the other hand, this finding reported higher utilization compared to studies in Ethiopia 28% [65], the United Kingdom (24%) [66], Pakistan 19% [67], China (15%) [68], and Israel (29.3%) [64]. This difference could be due to methodological variations, such as study setting and measurement tool. Furthermore, ongoing improvement in the quality of intrapartum care through time may lead to high utilization relative to previous estimates. Subgroup analysis revealed that the highest prevalence of labour pain relief methods utilization was reported among studies conducted after 2020 (48.75%) compared to studies conducted at 2020 and before (41.55%). Progress in OCGs training, labour pain management protocol implementation, growing awareness of OCGs and women, and the availability of methods all contribute to more extensive and effective labor pain management over time.

Regarding associated factors, this meta-analysis revealed that female OCGs were 1.90 times more likely to utilize labour pain relief methods than male OCGs. This may be because biological shared characteristics of childbirth enable female OCGs to have a better understanding of the nature of labour pain and the vulnerability of labouring women. This experiential knowledge may lead them to higher utilization of labour pain management methods than male OCGs. Female OCGs also tend to have a more favorable attitude toward labor pain management and perceive labor pain as severe, which motivates them to provide pain relief more frequently. Midwives were more likely to utilize labour pain relief methods. This is because the midwifery model of care, a predominantly female profession, focuses on continuous labour support and uses non-pharmacological pain relief, which directly results in the higher utilizations observed among this group of OCGs [69, 70].

In contrast to studies in Tamil Nadu and Western Australia [62, 71], this meta-analysis revealed that OCGs with adequate knowledge regarding labour pain were 2.61 times more likely to utilize these than those with inadequate knowledge. Studies in Ethiopia [28] and Nigeria [72] supported this evidence. Adequate knowledge equips OCGs to use labour pain relief methods by improving their understanding of the nature of labour pain, shaping positive perceptions and building the confidence needed to manage labour pain effectively. Furthermore, OCGs who had received training regarding labour pain were more likely to utilize labour pain management methods. This is because training equips OCGS with necessary practical skills and knowledge, thereby increasing their competency and confidence in effectively utilizing of labour relief methods [69, 73].

OCGs in health facilities with available obstetric analgesics and supplies were 5.43 times more likely to utilize labour pain management methods. In this study, the presence of a labour pain management protocol made OCGs 2.06 times more likely to utilize labour management methods. This is because protocol is a critical institutional tool that standardizes practice. This enables OCGs to practice labour pain management as a standard routine care of labouring women.

On an individual level, this meta-analysis revealed that the OCG’s perception of labour pain severity was a significant independent factor. Those who perceived labour pain as moderate to severe were 2.21 times more likely to utilize management methods. This can be explained by the fact that an individual’s perception of the severity of a health problem serves as a crucial motivator for taking recommended actions. When OCGs recognize labour pain as significantly impactful, they are more compelled to intervene and provide appropriate labour pain relief methods.

Strength and limitations

To enhance representativeness, we included studies from all regions. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design of the included studies and the absence of standardized outcome definitions across studies limit the comparability of results. We also identified substantial unexplained heterogeneity. Additionally, the facility-based nature of the included studies may limit the generalizability of our findings to community settings.

Conclusion

This review found that fewer than half of OCGs administer labour pain management methods. Utilization rates were higher among female OCGs, midwives, and those knowledgeable about labour pain management. Key predictors of higher practice included training in labour pain management, availability of adequate supplies, and the presence of labour pain management protocols within health facilities. OCGs who perceived labour pain as moderate to severe were also more likely to administer labour pain management methods. Significant regional variation in the utilization of labour pain management methods was observed. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to limitations such as the lack of standardized outcome definitions and substantial unexplained heterogeneity. Despite these limitations, the results highlight the urgent need to strengthen training programs, improve facility resources, institutionalize labour pain management protocols, and implement region-specific interventions to increase the utilization of effective labour pain management methods.

Clinical and policy implications

From a clinical perspective, this systematic review highlights a significant gap in maternal care standards, as over half of obstetric caregivers didn’t administer labour pain management methods. This finding highlighted the need to improve the quality of intrapartum care by prioritizing effective labour pain management. Obstetric caregivers must acknowledge the intensity of labour pain and continuously strengthen their competencies through updated, evidence-based training. Such efforts will ensure that women receive appropriate pharmacological and non-pharmacological labour pain management methods, reducing maternal stress and preventing negative birth experiences.

From a policy perspective, it is essential to equip health facilities with reliable and sufficient labour pain management medications and equipment. Policymakers should also establish regular, mandatory training programs for all obstetric caregivers focused on labour pain assessment and management to bridge the existing knowledge–practice gap. Furthermore, health systems should formally integrate labour pain management as a core quality indicator within maternity services.
