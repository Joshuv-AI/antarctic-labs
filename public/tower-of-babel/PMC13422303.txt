A decade of SEP-1 compliance and sepsis mortality in the United States
Authors: James S. Ford, Suresh K. Pavuluri, Donald M. Yealy, Larissa May, Arjun K. Venkatesh
Published: 2026

Sepsis, a dysregulated host response to infection, accounts for 1 in 3 deaths in United States hospitals [1]. In 2015, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) released the Severe Sepsis and Septic Shock Management Bundle (SEP-1) as a pay-for-reporting quality measure, meaning that hospitals had to report compliance with SEP-1 elements, or risk payment penalties (Supplement, eTable 1). In 2023, CMS announced it would include SEP-1 in the Hospital Value-Based Purchasing (VBP) Program, transforming it into a pay-for-performance quality measure beginning fiscal year 2026. This transition has intensified debate regarding whether increasing compliance with SEP-1 has translated into improved patient outcomes. Notably, a recent systematic review found no moderate- or high-certainty evidence that patient-level SEP-1 compliance or system-level implementation reduces mortality, suggesting that inclusion of SEP-1 as a pay-for-performance metric was not supported by current scientific evidence [2].

To inform policy discussions about the future of federal sepsis quality measurement, we examined national SEP-1 compliance and sepsis mortality during the first decade of SEP-1 implementation. We analyzed publicly available data from CMS and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). National rates of hospital-level compliance with the SEP-1 measure were obtained from CMS Hospital Compare Data Archive’s “Timely and Effective Care” datasets (Supplement, eTable 2). Though introduced in October 2015, SEP-1 compliance data were first published publicly in January 2017, making data from 2015 to 2016 unavailable. We used United States national sepsis mortality data from the CDC National Center for Health Statistics’ Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) website (Supplement, eTable 3) and identified sepsis-related deaths using ICD-10 codes (Supplement, eTable 4). Age-adjusted mortality was standardized to the 2000 U.S. standard population. We performed a time series analysis of age-adjusted sepsis mortality using Prais-Winsten regression, which adjusts for autocorrelation of mortality estimates across sequential years. To account for heteroskedasticity in mortality estimates introduced by the COVID-19 pandemic, we used robust standard errors in our models. Finally, to account for the aberrative effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on mortality estimates, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding pandemic years 2020–2022. All statistical analyses used Stata 17 SE (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Overall SEP-1 compliance increased between 2017 (50%) and 2024 (64%) (absolute annual increase 1.5% [95% CI 0.4% to 2.6%], p = 0.018). Age-adjusted national sepsis mortality did not change from 2015 to 2024, with rates of 52.5 and 52.4 deaths per 100,000 population, respectively (annual change, 0.33 deaths per 100,000; 95% CI, -0.69 to 1.36; p = 0.47) (Fig. 1). In our sensitivity analysis, excluding COVID-19 pandemic years (2020–2022), national sepsis-associated mortality remained unchanged over time (absolute annual change, 0.05 [95% CI -0.38 to 0.48] deaths per 100,000, p = 0.78). In our time series model, for every 1% increase in SEP-1 compliance, there was no change in deaths per 100,000 population [95% CI -2.6 to 0.6 deaths per 100,000 population, p = 0.18].

These data highlight a persistent disconnect between sepsis process measure efforts and patient-centered outcomes. Despite increases in SEP-1 compliance over the last decade, national and sepsis mortality was largely unchanged over this period. Our model examining the effect of SEP-1 bundle compliance on sepsis mortality found no association with national changes in sepsis mortality. This trend suggests that the effect of SEP-1 compliance may be plateauing, with only small incremental gains in patient-centered outcomes. These population-level findings are consistent with a broader evidence base showing mixed or absent mortality benefit from patient-level SEP-1 compliance, system-level SEP-1 implementation, and standardized sepsis care protocols [2–5]. Taken together, these data support reconsidering (1) whether SEP-1 is the optimal federal policy for the evaluation of sepsis quality, and (2) whether it merits inclusion in the hospital VBP program. Rather than relying solely on narrowly defined process measures, future sepsis quality measures should evaluate patient-centered outcomes such as risk-adjusted mortality or sepsis 30-day readmissions. If SEP-1 remains in federal payment programs, revisions should prioritize components with the strongest evidence and allow greater flexibility for physiology-informed resuscitation.

This study is limited by its ecological design, which precludes causal inference and does not permit conclusions about the effect of SEP-1 compliance on outcomes for individual patients or hospitals. SEP-1 compliance data were unavailable before 2017, which prevented us from evaluating the earliest period after SEP-1 implementation. Finally, because this analysis used aggregate national mortality data and hospital-level SEP-1 compliance data, we did not assess patient-level effects of bundle compliance.

In summary, in the first decade following SEP-1 implementation, national SEP-1 compliance improved without a corresponding reduction in national sepsis mortality, underscoring the limited evidence of population-level benefit from mandates for uniform sepsis care pathways. Health care policy and payment approaches that empower clinicians to navigate the nuanced landscape of sepsis care may provide a way to achieve the improvement in patient outcomes that have remained elusive over the last decade.
