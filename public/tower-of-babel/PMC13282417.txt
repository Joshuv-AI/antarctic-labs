Genetic variation of CBS (844ins68) and MTHFR A1298C among aluminum-exposed workers with emphasis on the role of oxidative stress
Authors: Nermeen S. A. Aziz, Eman M. Shahy, Mona M. Taha, Heba Mahdy-Abdallah, Khadiga S. Ibrahim, Gehan M. Mahdy, Lamia S. Ellaithy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAluminum (Al) is the most abundant element in the Earth’s crust. Occupational exposure to aluminum can lead to serious adverse health effects for workers in the aluminum industry. Genetic variations could be considered as contributing internal factors for the susceptibility of individuals to toxicities related to Al exposure. Different experiments have investigated genetic polymorphism variations in metabolic pathways, which could significantly modulate an individual’s susceptibility to aluminum-induced toxicity. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of aluminum exposure on oxidant-antioxidant status, parathyroid hormone (PTH), and free testosterone levels. Also, to show whether an association exists between MTHFR A1298C (rs1801131) and CBS (844ins68) gene polymorphisms with the aforementioned biochemical parameters among occupationally exposed workers to Al.MethodsUrinary heavy metals (Al, Cd, Ni, Cr, Pb) were measured through ICP-MS in 95 workers in the aluminum industry and 90 control individuals. We also analyzed serum MT-1, MDA, PTH, free testosterone, and TAC using ELISA methods. CBS (844ins68) and MTHFR A1298C gene polymorphisms were performed by (PCR-RFLP).ResultsRevealed a significant rise in serum MT-1, MDA, and urinary heavy metals levels with reductions in TAC, Free testosterone, and PTH levels. A negative relation was found between serum TAC, duration of exposure, and between age and serum free testosterone levels. In workers, the association of the wild type of MTHFR A1298C genotype with a rise in serum MT-1 and MDA levels and a decrease in TAC was observed compared to the control, while the heterozygote genotype AC was associated with reduced serum free testosterone, TAC, and PTH levels among the workers group. Moreover, serum MT-1 showed a significant rise in NN genotype of the CBS gene with a decline in serum TAC and PTH levels, while Heterozygote NI recorded reduced TAC levels among the worker group compared to the control. Significant reduction in serum TAC was shown in the heterozygote NI among the worker group compared to the control.ConclusionAssociation of wild-type and heterozygote genotype of both MTHFR A1298C (rs1801131) (AA and AC) and CBS (844ins68) (NN and NI) with reduced TAC levels among the worker group. Moreover, the AC genotype of MTHFR was associated with a decrease in the PTH and free testosterone levels among Al industry workers.

Introduction

Aluminum (Al) is the most abundant metallic element in nature [1]. Occupational exposure to Al is considered a serious health problem due to the high bioavailability of inhaled fine particles during exposure [2].

Occupational exposures to Al were shown with processing and mining of ore, recycling of scrap metal, deploying as well as using Al-containing products and compounds. Also, exposure could be during engagement in aluminum sawing, cutting, filing, and welding. Humans and animals who are living in these highly contaminated environments with industrial wastes might be exposed to high levels of Al [3].

Metallothioneins (MTs) represent low molecular-weight protein that are rich in cysteine and metal-binding. Their biological role was not understood precisely. Existing evidence suggested involvement of MTs in antioxidant defense, metal ion homeostasis, and heavy metal detoxification (e.g., Al). Moreover, MTs are categorized as protective effectors that contribute in cellular homeostasis and survival [4, 5]. MTs are localized in the Golgi apparatus membrane. MTs can bind both physiological essential elements (like selenium, copper, zinc) and xenobiotics (e.g., arsenic, mercury, cadmium, silver) through the thiol group found in their cysteine residues that constitute about 30% of their amino acid residues [6]. Metallothionein-1 seemed to be a metalloprotein that primarily can balance metals to mitigate poisoning due to heavy metals and share in reducing stress responses over the last few decades [7].

Accumulated aluminum (Al) in the human body can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), which directly damage proteins, lipids, and DNA. Alterations in biochemical properties may also lead to DNA cross-linking [8]. Malondialdehyde, an indirect mediator of oxidative stress, is produced through lipid peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids such as triglycerides, cholesterol, and phospholipids. Due to its high reactivity, malondialdehyde readily modifies the imidazole groups of histidine residues and the sulfhydryl groups of cysteine residues, ultimately leading to protein oxidation [9].

Previous studies have demonstrated an association between occupational aluminum exposure and various disorders, including neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease [10], as well as respiratory health hazards, including reduced lung function and pulmonary fibrosis. Chronic exposure to high levels of aluminum has also been linked to kidney damage, bone disorders, hormonal imbalances, and cancer [11]. This occupational exposure is associated with increased ROS formation, including hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and superoxide radicals (O₂⁻) [12]. Aluminum toxicity primarily arises from its pro-oxidant activity, which promotes free radical generation, oxidative stress, and oxidation of cellular lipids and proteins [13]. ROS play a central role in multiple pathological processes, including inflammation, endocrine disruption, and toxicity induced by environmental chemicals. Although ROS can be neutralized by antioxidant systems in various organs, their damaging effects on cellular macromolecules ultimately impair normal cellular function [14].

Moreover, the accumulation of aluminum in endocrine glands can induce oxidative stress–mediated damage, leading to decreased hormone secretion into the bloodstream and reduced activity at target organs, resulting in glandular hypofunction [3]. Aluminum has also been reported to disrupt the parathyroid hormone–calcium axis through several mechanisms, including a diminished parathyroid response to hypocalcemia, which impairs parathyroid hormone (PTH) synthesis and release [15, 16].

In addition, aluminum exposure may cause male reproductive dysfunction, particularly testicular impairment, through the induction of oxidative stress in testicular tissue. This is associated with decreased androgen levels, reduced androgen receptor activity, and disruption of reproductive hormones, including testosterone, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and luteinizing hormone (LH) [17]. Consequently, reduced testosterone levels can impair spermatogenesis, disrupt testicular development, and alter androgenic function. Furthermore, oxidative stress induced by aluminum can damage testicular cell membranes, potentially affecting hormone transport across the blood–testis barrier [18].

The human MTHFR gene is located on the short arm of chromosome 1 (1p36.3) and encodes a key enzyme involved in folate and methionine metabolism [19]. This enzyme catalyzes the conversion of 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate to 5-methyltetrahydrofolate [20]. Polymorphisms in the MTHFR gene can increase thermolability and reduce enzymatic activity [19]. Two common variants have been identified as major contributors to impaired enzyme function. The most prevalent is the 677 C > T (rs1801133) polymorphism, in which a cytosine-to-thymine substitution at nucleotide 677 results in a thermolabile enzyme with reduced activity at elevated temperatures [20]. Another variant, A1298C (rs1801131), occurs in exon 7 at codon 429 and involves a glutamate-to-alanine substitution [21].

Cystathionine-β-synthase (CBS) is the first and rate-limiting enzyme in the transsulfuration pathway and is an important mammalian enzyme involved in both health and disease [22]. In humans, the CBS gene is located in the subtelomeric region of chromosome 21 (21q22.3). It consists of 23 exons, 15 of which encode the CBS polypeptide involved in the folate pathway [23]. A known polymorphism in the CBS gene occurs in exon 8 at nucleotide 844, where a 68-base pair insertion (CBS 844ins68) has been identified [24].

In normal individuals and non-exposed populations, the CBS 844ins68 variant is considered a common polymorphism. It consists of an exact duplication at the intron–exon boundary of exon 8. This mutation creates a new splice site, potentially allowing alternative splicing that still produces the wild-type CBS sequence. As a result, mRNA size remains unchanged, and no significant effects on transcription, translation, or CBS function have been observed [25].

Our study aims to evaluate the effects of aluminum exposure on oxidant-antioxidant status, PTH, and free testosterone levels, and to show whether there is an association with MTHFR A1298C (rs1801131) and CBS (844ins68) gene polymorphisms among aluminum-exposed workers.

Methods

Study design and subjects

A cross-sectional study was conducted from October 2023 to May 2024 in 2 aluminum factories in Cairo, Egypt (one in Helwan and the other in El-Tebbin area). In these factories, the manufacture of Al was shown to occur through a secondary process in which aluminum scrap was recycled into aluminum sheets for reuse. In the secondary process, the collection of aluminum scrap followed by its isolation was processed and finally placed into a melting furnace at high temperatures that range from 1300 to 1400 °F for melting. Then, secondary Al was shown to be used in several applications that use primary aluminum.

The current study included 95 male workers from two selected factories mentioned above (exposed workers group), in addition to 90 non-exposed workers from the administrative departments of the two factories (non-exposed control group). Inclusion criteria were workers whose working duration was more than 5 years and working hours of 8 h/day. The workers in the exposed groups and the control group were matched in their age, BMI, sociodemographic status, and nutritional status. Nutritional status, based on body mass index, was determined according to World Health Organization (WHO) Asia-Pacific criteria (normal, overweight, obese I, obese II, and obese III) [26]. Before the survey, written informed consent was signed by each participant. According to the Declaration of Helsinki, the present study was conducted after approval by the ethical committee at the National Research Centre. Then, all participants filled a personal questionnaire including detailed information about age, smoking habits, socioeconomic status, dietary habits, duration of occupational exposure (designated in years), shift duration (hours), as well as the type of exposure.

Samples collection

Six ml of blood sample was withdrawn from each participant and divided into 4 ml in a plain tube and left at room temperature to separate serum through centrifugation at 1500 rpm for about 10 min. Separated sera were transferred to microtubes and kept at − 70 °C for biochemical analysis. Another 2 ml of blood was added to an EDTA tube (with gentle mixing to avoid blood clotting) for gene analysis of CβS (844ins68) and MTHFR A1298C (rs1801131) gene polymorphisms.

Moreover, for heavy metals analysis, a urine sample was collected from each participant. The cups were washed with 15% nitric acid and rinsed with ultrapure water before sampling. Urine samples were stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Primer sequence used for amplification of the CBS (844ins68) gene polymorphism in the exon 8 and flanking intron 7 were: sense, 5’-GTTGTTAACGGCGGTATTGG-3’, and antisense, 5’- GTTGTCTGCTCCGTCTGGTT-3’. PCR amplification program was performed in a 20 µL reaction volume, which contained 75–100 ng of genomic DNA, 1.0 U Taq polymerase, 20 pmol of each primer, 200 µM dNTP mix, 0.001% gelatin, and 10 mM Tris buffer with 50 mM KCl and 1.0 mM MgCl2. The amplification program used was initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 45 s, annealing at 58 °C for 40 s, and extension at 72 °C for 40 s, and a final extension step for 5 min at 72 °C. Amplification products were separated by 12% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, stained with ethidium bromide, and visualized under UV light. The wild-type allele was seen at the 171 bp fragment (N), and the insertion variant was seen at the 239 bp fragment (I).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was done using the SPSS program 18. Mean ± SD was used for data expression. An independent t-test was used to compare differences between 2 groups. The Fisher-Exact test was used for the analysis of frequency in gene distribution. The Mann–Whitney test was used for non-parametric analysis of variables that were not normally distributed. test. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages, and comparisons between groups were conducted using the chi-square (χ2) test. Multivariable logistic regression analysis of factors associated with occupational exposure in the worker group versus the control group. The relation between variables was studied by Pearson’s correlation. P < 0.05 was set as significant.

Discussion

Aluminum is a naturally occurring element and the third most abundant metal in the Earth’s crust. It is widely used in several industries. People living near manufacturing facilities and/or working in aluminum-related industries may be more vulnerable to inhaling aluminum particles or fumes [11]. Exposure to aluminum has been reported in scrap metal recycling, ore mining, aluminum cutting, the production of aluminum-containing goods and chemicals, as well as in areas contaminated with industrial effluents [30].

Our study revealed a highly significant increase in the levels in serum MT-1, MDA, and urinary Al with a reduction in serum TAC, F. testosterone, and PTH levels in workers compared to the control group. Regarding MT-1 levels, previous studies have indicated that several factors, including heavy metals, glucocorticoids, alkylating agents, antioxidants, lipopolysaccharides, and cytokines, can trigger MT1 expression, leading to a series of immunomodulatory effects [31]. Singh et al. [32] demonstrated that polymorphism in the MT1A gene is associated with increased metal accumulation, which may consequently enhance its toxic effects in exposed individuals.

Concerning malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, Jadoon et al. [8] reported that the antioxidant system is weakened due to excessive free radical formation, causing oxidative stress. This, in turn, increases lipid peroxidation, MDA levels, and DNA adduct formation [8].

For total antioxidant capacity (TAC), a recent study confirmed that aluminum contributes to impairment of intracellular antioxidant defense systems by enhancing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which may disrupt metabolic homeostasis [33].

Furthermore, reduced levels of parathyroid hormone (PTH) among workers may be attributed to aluminum accumulation in the parathyroid glands, which reduces the gland’s response to hypocalcemia and consequently inhibits the release and synthesis of PTH [15]. An experimental study also showed that rats overloaded with aluminum exhibited reduced pre-proPTH messenger RNA (mRNA) levels [34].

Recent studies have shown that occupational exposure to aluminum occurs during handling of aluminum powders, welding, and in aluminum smelters [35, 36]. Moreover, emissions of air pollutants at various stages of aluminum production contribute to workplace contamination with heavy metals, particularly aluminum [36]. Rahimzadeh et al. [15] attributed increased urinary aluminum levels to exposure to airborne dust and fumes, which significantly elevate urinary aluminum concentrations [37]. Urinary aluminum is considered a sensitive, non-invasive biomarker widely used for monitoring recent occupational exposure [38].

Our study revealed significant genotype frequency distribution in the CBS 844ins68 polymorphism among workers and control groups. Previous study indicated that insertion of 68 bp in the CBS 844ins68 gene generates a splice site that may eliminate the inserted region, allowing formation of a normal mRNA transcript and resulting in a fully functional CBS enzyme [24].

In the present study, in the worker group, TAC was shown to be negatively related to duration of exposure. Our result was in line with a previous study [12], which found that chronic occupational exposure has been associated with a reduced rate of aluminum clearance from the body, likely due to systemic absorption and accumulation in compartments with prolonged turnover rates.

Furthermore, a negative relation was found between age and serum-free testosterone among the worker group. In agreement with Ahren and Wu [39] reported that aging increases the negative feedback of testosterone on the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis. Decline in testicular function and hypothalamic dysregulation may explain the age-related decrease in testosterone levels.

The current study mentioned an association of the wild type of MTHFR A1298C (rs1801131) genotype with a significant rise in serum MT-1 and MDA levels, with a decrease in levels of serum TAC among the worker group compared to the control. The heterozygote genotype AC was associated with a decrease in serum TAC, free testosterone, and PTH levels among the worker group compared with the control. A recent study also showed that the A1298C (rs1801131) polymorphism is associated with reduced allele frequency and up to a 33% decrease in enzyme activity. Carriers of homozygous or heterozygous variants may be at higher risk of toxic metal accumulation and electrophilic stress damage, along with a reduced ability to eliminate these metals. These individuals may also experience persistent oxidative stress affecting cellular function [40].

The present study found an association of the NN genotype of the CBS (844ins68) gene with significant elevation in serum MT-1 levels, with a decline in both serum TAC and PTH levels. Finally, a significant reduction in serum TAC was shown in heterozygote NI among the worker group compared to the control. Tsai et al. [41] suggested an association between the 68-bp insertion and increased CBS enzyme activity. However, the exact mechanism by which the 844ins68 allele affects CBS function remains unclear. It may increase CBS activity through linkage disequilibrium with other functional polymorphisms or via upregulation of CBS mRNA [42]. Another study indicated that the 844ins68 polymorphism in the CBS gene may generate an alternative splicing site, potentially disrupting the protein and reducing CBS functional activity [43].

The rare mutant form of the CBS (844ins68) gene, known as (II), was not compared between workers and controls because in the control group, 0% was found where no subject was recorded.

The multivariable logistic regression demonstrated that biochemical exposure-related markers were stronger predictors of occupational status than genetic polymorphisms, suggesting that environmental exposure plays a more significant role than inherited factors. The lack of significant genetic association may also be due to small subgroup sizes.

Limitations of the study

A limitation of the present study was shown in small sample size in both the worker and control groups. Small subgroup sizes lead to reduced statistical power and wide confidence intervals. Further studies, including a larger sample size in both groups, must be conducted to achieve definite conclusions.

Conclusions

The current study confirmed that periodic medical examination must be carried out for workers exposed to aluminum, including clinical examination with determination of PTH levels among workers in the aluminum industry. Analysis of free testosterone, with increasing age, must be achieved in workers occupationally exposed to aluminum. Determination of urinary Al levels is required as a sensitive non-invasive biomarker for recent occupational Al exposure among those workers. Dietary habits should be rich in antioxidants, or supplementation with antioxidant properties is necessary for reducing the toxic effects of prolonged exposure on working days.

Association of wild type and heterozygote genotype in both MTHFR A1298C (rs1801131) (AA and AC) and CBS (844ins68) (NN and NI) with reduced TAC levels among the worker group. Moreover, individuals carrying the heterozygote genotype AC of the MTHFR A1298C (rs1801131) gene were associated with lower levels of free testosterone and PTH among workers. Further studies with larger sample sizes are required for more understanding of this polymorphism and to confirm these findings.
