ASPSCR1: TFE3 Fusion‐Positive Epithelioid Angiomyolipoma/PEComa Lacking Melanocytic Markers: A Case Report

Introduction

Epithelioid angiomyolipoma (EAML) is a distinctive mesenchymal neoplasm belonging to the perivascular epithelioid cell tumor (PEComa) family. Although most are benign, EAML is recognized for its malignant potential [1]. EAML is a well‐known pitfall because its epithelioid morphology mimics renal cell carcinoma (RCC). The molecular pathogenesis of EAML commonly involves TSC 1 or TSC 2 expression. However, a small subset harbors TFE3 gene rearrangements, which are mutually exclusive of TSC mutations [2]. TFE3‐rearranged tumors often occur in young patients without tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) [3]. Differentiating TFE3‐rearranged EAML from TFE3‐rearranged RCC is challenging because both entities exhibit an epithelioid morphology and diffuse TFE3 immunoreactivity [4]. Recent studies have identified “PEComa‐like neoplasms” characterized by ASPSCR1::TFE3 fusion without melanocytic marker positivity, further complicating the diagnostic landscape [5]. We report a case of ASPSCR1::TFE3 fusion‐positive EAML that lacked melanocytic markers, which highlights the importance of PAX8 immunohistochemistry.

Case Presentation

A 25‐year‐old woman presented with incomplete bladder emptying. She had no medical or family history. Computed tomography (CT) revealed a 4.5 cm mass with an infiltrative margin, calcification, and marked contrast enhancement in the lower pole of the right kidney (Figure 1a,b), strongly suggesting RCC. However, given the patient's age and the atypical imaging features of the tumor, alternative diagnoses could not be excluded. Therefore, a CT‐guided biopsy was performed to establish a definitive diagnosis.

Computed tomography (CT) findings of the renal tumor. (a) Non‐contrast CT image showing a 4.5‐cm mass in the lower pole of the right kidney with an irregular outer margin accompanied by calcification (arrow). (b) Contrast‐enhanced CT (early phase) showing a hypervascular mass with early enhancement (arrow). The tumor also exhibited late‐phase washout (data not shown).

The tumor showed a solid growth pattern with nuclear pleomorphism and clear eosinophilic cytoplasm. The differential diagnoses included clear cell RCC, chromophobe RCC, and EAML. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) showed that the tumor cells were negative for CK (AE1/AE3), CK7, c‐kit, CAIX, and HMB45, diffusely positive for TFE3, leading to an initial diagnosis of TFE3‐rearranged RCC. Radical nephrectomy revealed a yellowish, solid tumor measuring 6.0 × 4.0 × 3.5 cm with hemorrhage, cystic change, and focal capsular invasion (Figure 2a,b). Histologically, the tumor was divided by fibrous septa and showed epithelioid growth patterns (Figure 2c). The tumor cells were round to polygonal with variable nuclear morphology and clear, eosinophilic, or vacuolated cytoplasm (Figure 2d,e). The results of IHC were the same as those of the initial biopsy specimen, and melan‐A was negative, thus maintaining the suspicion of TFE3‐rearranged RCC (Figure 3a). TFE3 gene rearrangement was detected using break‐apart FISH (Figure 4a). However, additional IHC revealed that the tumor was negative for PAX8 and AMACR, but positive for cathepsin K with focal positivity for smooth muscle actin (Figure 3b,c). PAX8 negativity was also retrospectively confirmed in the initial biopsy specimen. Accordingly, the diagnosis was revised to TFE3‐rearranged EAML.

Macroscopic and microscopic findings. (a) The resected specimen showed a yellowish solid tumor. (b) The cut surface of the tumor exhibited hemorrhage, cystic changes, and focal capsular invasion. (c) Histologically, the tumor demonstrated a solid proliferation of epithelioid cells divided by delicate fibrous septa (H&E, ×200). (d,e) High‐power view revealed atypical polygonal cells with abundant clear to eosinophilic cytoplasm and prominent nucleoli (H&E, ×400).

Immunohistochemical profile. The tumor cells exhibited (a) a diffuse and strong nuclear expression of TFE3 (×400). In contrast, the tumor cells were completely negative for (b) PAX8 (×400). The cells showed (c) focal positivity for smooth muscle Actin (×400).

A molecular genetic analysis using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). Bottom panels show schematic diagrams of the probe designs. (a) TFE3 break‐apart FISH using a break‐apart probe flanking the TFE3 gene (red: 5′ end, green: 3′ end). Separated red and green signals (arrows) indicate TFE3 gene rearrangement. (b) Dual‐color fusion FISH for ASPSCR1::TFE3. Probes are designed to target the ASPSCR1 locus (red) and the TFE3 locus (green). An overlapping yellow signal confirmed the presence of the fusion gene.

Thereafter, a FISH analysis identified the ASPSCR1::TFE3 fusion (Figure 4b). As the tumor completely lacked any melanocytic markers despite its EAML‐like morphology, this finding was consistent with the recently proposed category of “PEComa‐like neoplasm.” The postoperative course was uneventful for 16 months. No clinical signs of TSC were identified.

Discussion

TFE3‐rearranged EAML represents a rare subset of PEComas, typically occurring in younger patients and showing no association with TSC [3]. Unlike classic angiomyolipoma, EAML is characterized by the proliferation of atypical epithelioid cells and a malignant potential. The major clinical challenge is distinguishing TFE3‐rearranged EAML from TFE3‐rearranged RCC due to overlapping pathological and radiological features [4]. Radiologically, fat‐poor EAML mimics RCC [6]; even marginal (“eggshell”) calcification, often considered benign [7], occurs in TFE3‐rearranged neoplasms, such as Xp11.2 RCC and renal alveolar soft part sarcoma (ASPS) [8, 9]. Notably, ASPSCR1::TFE3 tumors may exhibit stromal hyalinization or ossification [5], although any possible fusion‐specific correlations remain unclear.

To resolve these diagnostic challenges, PAX8 and cathepsin K are vital. PAX8 was negative in TFE3‐rearranged PEComas (0/88), but it was positive in 91% (216/237) of TFE3‐rearranged RCCs [4]. While cathepsin K is highly sensitive for TFE3‐rearranged PEComas (97%; 86/89), its specificity is limited by frequent positivity in TFE3‐rearranged RCCs (43%; 111/256), particularly those with PRCC or SFPQ fusions [10]. We therefore propose a diagnostic algorithm prioritizing PAX8 and cathepsin K as mandatory components; namely, PAX8 negativity reliably excludes RCC even in TFE3‐positive cases, melanocytic‐marker‐negative tumors where lineage determination is most challenging.

In the present case, the initial suspicion of RCC highlighted the limitations of TFE3 IHC as a stand‐alone diagnostic marker. A key finding against RCC was PAX8 negativity [10]. In addition, TFE3 IHC can yield false‐positive results depending on the fixation and staining conditions [11]. Therefore, confirmatory FISH is essential to verify the presence of a true TFE3 rearrangement.

The complete absence of immunoreactivity for melanocytic markers further complicates the diagnosis. Argani et al. recently described a series of tumors termed “PEComa‐like neoplasms” harboring ASPSCR1::TFE 3 fusion, which is classically associated with ASPS [5, 12]. These neoplasms morphologically resemble EAML, but lack melanocytic differentiation. Our case is consistent with this emerging entity. The phenotypic variability associated with the same gene fusion likely reflects the “cell of origin” and the molecular strength of the fusion partner. PEComas may arise from neural crest precursors capable of melanocytic differentiation, while ASPS derives from mesenchymal stem cells lacking that potential [5]. Furthermore, ASPSCR1::TFE3 might drive MiTF targets less effectively than the SFPQ partner [13]. However, a rare melan‐A‐positive ASPSCR1::TFE3 case suggests a dynamic interplay between the cellular context and the fusion protein expression levels [14].

The pathological classification is clinically important because TFE3‐rearranged PEComas are thought to have a more aggressive clinical course than conventional EAML. Among the 42 reported cases with a follow‐up of more than 1 year, 13 (31%) developed systemic metastasis, and five (12%) died of the disease [12]. Recurrences and metastases typically occur within 2 years of the initial diagnosis, commonly involving the lungs, abdominal lymph nodes, and livers [4, 12]. Therefore, regular postoperative cross‐sectional imaging is essential in the follow‐up of such cases. The high‐risk features proposed by Argani et al. include a tumor size ≥ 5 cm, necrosis, and a mitotic count ≥ 1/10 high‐power fields. However, late‐onset metastasis has been reported as late as 7 years after resection, even in the absence of these features, thereby underscoring the need for long‐term oncological surveillance.

Thus, these molecular drivers have significant therapeutic implications. TFE3 rearrangements and TSC 1/2 mutations were mutually exclusive [2]. Unlike TSC‐mutated PEComas, which may respond to mTOR inhibitors, TFE3‐rearranged PEComas appear to show limited sensitivity to these agents [12]. Therefore, identifying these tumors is essential for tailoring the postoperative management. Although Argani et al. proposed the term “TFE3‐rearranged PEComa‐like neoplasms” to emphasize their distinctive molecular background and limited sensitivity to mTOR inhibitors, this nomenclature has yet to be fully standardized in the current classifications. Given the rarity and phenotypic variability of “TFE3‐rearranged PEComa‐like neoplasms,” further accumulation of cases is therefore required.

Conclusion

TFE3‐rearranged EAML/PEComa may lack melanocytic markers. PAX8 negativity and FISH exclude TFE3‐rearranged RCC and conventional TSC‐mutated PEComa. It is essential to ensure appropriate long‐term surveillance to support the optimal selection of systemic therapy.
