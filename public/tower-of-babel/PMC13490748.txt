Effect of dual hepatic and portal vein embolization on future liver remnant function and volume prior to major hepatectomy
Authors: Ananya Nayak, Anitha Muthusami, Michael Wilson, Salil Karkhanis, Homoyon Mehrzad, Khalid Hussain, Ramanivas Sundareyan, Robert Peter Sutcliffe, Ravi Marudanayagam, Keith John Roberts, Nikolaos Chatzizacharias, David Christopher Bartlett, Alicja Psica, Syed Soulat Raza, Bobby V M Dasari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgrounds/AimsDual hepatic and portal vein embolization, also known as dual venous embolization (DVE), is a potentially superior alternative to portal vein embolization for enhancing liver regeneration prior to major hepatectomy. This study evaluates the impact of DVE on the kinetic growth rate (KGR), volume increase (VI), functional increase (FI), and the correlation between volume and functional changes in the future liver remnant (FLR).MethodsPatients who underwent DVE with pre- and post-procedure volume and functional assessments were included. Growth rates of FLR volume and function were assessed using Tc99-mebrofenate single-photon emission computed tomography-computed tomography (Tc99-m SPECT-CT) scans. Correlation coefficients (R) were calculated to evaluate the relationship between VI and FI.ResultsAmong the 37 patients included, 27 proceeded to definitive surgery. The median time between DVE and the functional scan was 33 days (interquartile range [IQR], 13–69). The median increase in FLR volume was 30% (IQR, 14–50), and FLR function increased by 75% (IQR, 25–122) (p = 0.004). Median KGR (volume) was 6% (IQR, 3%–12%), and KGR (function) was 15% (IQR, 7%–23%) (p = 0.001). Pearson’s correlation coefficient between VI and FI was 0.179 (confidence interval, 0.15–0.48). Functional resectability was achieved in 35 patients.ConclusionsThe increase in FLR function following DVE is significantly greater than the increase in volume. Earlier SPECT-CT scans may reduce delays to surgery and warrant further investigation.Keywords: Surgery, Liver, Dual venous embolization, Neoplasm, Hepatectomy

INTRODUCTION

The liver is the most common site of metastatic spread in colorectal cancer, with approximately 50%–60% of patients developing liver lesions during their lifetime [1]. Despite the potential curative benefits of surgical resection, only 15% to 25% of patients are eligible for the procedure at the time of diagnosis [2].

Extended liver resections, even in the context of underlying chemotherapy-induced parenchymal disease, have expanded operative options and may offer greater long-term survival. Other common indications for these resections include hilar cholangiocarcinoma and primary and secondary liver tumors, depending on disease distribution. However, while these surgical strategies aim for tumor clearance, they can also increase the risk of post-hepatectomy liver failure (PHLF) [3-7], with associated morbidity and mortality, due inadequate liver remnant function.

Future liver remnant (FLR) volume and function can be modified preoperatively to reduce the risk of PHLF. In patients with a suboptimal FLR, several strategies are employed to stimulate hypertrophy and enable potentially curative resection. Established procedures for modulating FLR include portal vein embolization (PVE) and, more recently, combined portal and hepatic vein liver venous deprivation, also known as dual venous embolization (DVE). DVE is reported to promote faster and more pronounced hypertrophy of the FLR compared to PVE alone [8]. Comparative studies have demonstrated that DVE nearly halved the risk of PHLF (odds ratio 0.45; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.22–0.91; p = 0.03), achieved a significantly higher resection rate (92% versus 68%; p = 0.007), and is associated with superior long-term survival [9-11]. However, a significant proportion of patients do not proceed to surgery due to insufficient liver remnant and the time required to achieve satisfactory growth of the remnant liver. In clinical practice, the pathway from the decision to perform a DVE to surgery takes over 7 to 8 weeks. Disease progression during this period is a major reason for the failure to advance to resection surgery. Consequently, the failure to progress to completion surgery can be as high as 40% [12]. Whilst volumetric measurements have traditionally been used, it is now well recognized that volume itself does not reflect the true functional reserve of the liver. Radiological tools for pre-operative evaluation of liver function (in addition to volume) have been promising. Tc99-mebrofenate single-photon emission computed tomography-computed tomography (Tc99-m SPECT-CT) scan offers assessment of functional remnant, providing quantitative information on both total and regional hepatic functional capacity (FLR-Function) in addition to volume (FLR-Volume) [13].

Studies have shown a significant discrepancy between anatomical and functional volumes in patients scheduled for liver resection [14]. It is important to investigate whether this discrepancy also persists after venous embolization procedures. The current study aims to compare changes in remnant liver function and remnant liver volume following DVE, as assessed through Tc99-m SPECT-CT scans before and after DVE.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was conducted at the Queen Elizabeth Hospital, Birmingham and was approved by the institutional board (CARMS Number: 18,338). The retrospective analysis included all patients who underwent DVE at a tertiary hepatopancreatobiliary (HPB) center between July 2021 and June 2025. Since 2019, DVE has been the preferred method at our center and is performed on all patients with appropriate indications. A combination of embolic agents (plugs, coils, and glue) was used based on operator preference and anatomical considerations on a case-by-case basis. Similarly, the specific hepatic veins embolized varied depending on the planned resection. For the majority of cases (26 out of 37), the right hepatic vein and right portal vein were embolized.

Patients who underwent DVE were followed up through a prospectively maintained departmental database, which included demographic data, timings of pre- and post-Tc99-m SPECT-CT scans, rates of completion surgery, and the occurrence of PHLF. PHLF was defined according to the the International Study Group of Liver Surgery criteria. Volumetric analysis of FLR was performed using CT acquisitions from the Liver or Hermes software (with like-for-like modality pre- and post-embolization). Post-DVE scans were aimed to be performed four weeks post-procedure.

Resectability was assessed based on both volumetric and functional parameters. Cases were considered volume-resectable if the FLR volume was ≥ 40%, or if the FLR volume was between 30% and 39% in the absence of additional risk factors such as age ≥ 80 years, a body mass index (BMI) > 38, or the need for vascular reconstruction. Functional resectability was defined as the presence of an adequate functional FLR (FLR-Function) to permit safe hepatic resection, in accordance with Amsterdam criteria (2.7/min/sqm) [14]. The functional threshold was not changed based on the background liver disease.

This study evaluates the impact of DVE on kinetic growth rate (KGR), volumetric increase (VI), and functional increase (FI) in the FLR. Additionally, it examines the relationship between volumetric and functional changes following DVE. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess the correlation between these volumetric and functional changes.

RESULTS

A total of 37 patients were included, with a median age of 67.5 years. The majority were male (n = 25). Hilar cholangiocarcinoma constituted the largest proportion of cases (n = 17), followed by colorectal liver metastases (n = 10) (Table 1).

Median time between DVE and repeat volumetry/functional scan was 33 days (interquartile range [IQR], 13–69). The median duration from DVE to surgery was 76 days (IQR, 57–118). The median duration from post-DVE scan to surgery was 43.5 days (IQR, 27–78). Functional resectability was achieved in 35 patients. Volumetric resectability was achieved in 34 patients. The median KGR was 6%/week (IQR, 3%–12%) for volume and significantly higher at 15%/week (IQR, 7%–23%) for function (p = 0.001).

Volumetric growth vs. functional growth

Median pre-DVE FLR-Volume was 550 mL (IQR, 451–678), significantly increasing to 766 mL (IQR, 692–982) post-DVE. This represents a 30% median volume increase (IQR, 14%–50%) (Table 2). In addition to volumetric changes, functional improvements were even more pronounced (Fig. 2): the median pre-DVE FLR-Function was 2.05%/min/m2 (IQR, 1.54–2.44), which rose to 3.26%/min/m2 (IQR, 2.71–4.30) post-DVE, indicating a 75% median increase (IQR, 25%–122%; p = 0.003) (Table 2).

Failure to progress to definitive surgery

Of the 37 patients, 27 underwent definitive surgery, while 10 did not. 35 patients had either adequate FRL-Volume or FRL-Function. Reasons for not progressing included insufficient hypertrophy (n = 2) and disease progression (n = 8), with six of these patients diagnosed intraoperatively with peritoneal or local disease progression.

Complications

Postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo [15] ≥ grade III) occurred in 13 of 27 patients. PHLF developed in 4 patients: 3 with type C and 1 with type A (Table 3). In one patient, type C PHLF was secondary to hepatic artery thrombosis. The 90-day mortality rate was 5 of 27. Of the five patients who died postoperatively, two deaths were attributable to multi-organ failure secondary to postoperative sepsis, two to multi-organ failure secondary to PHLF, and one to cancer cachexia.

DISCUSSION

DVE may enhance parenchymal hypertrophy by altering hepatic venous outflow dynamics, which facilitates increased arterialization and regenerative signaling pathways. Guiu et al. [8] speculate that, in the absence of significant histological damage in the embolized liver, the suppression of ongoing low-grade portal inflow and the decrease in porto-portal collateral development are the primary mechanisms explaining the superior effects of DVE. Despite technical advances in the delivery of DVE, the incidence of PHLF following DVE is still reported to be around 13% in some studies [9]. The discrepancy between anatomical and functional regeneration is increasingly recognized, and recent studies have recommended using functional assessment of the liver remnant to prevent PHLF [16-18]. Cusumano et al. [19] reported that the functional assessment of patients with colorectal liver metastases has enabled major hepatectomy in 20% more patients who would not have been considered for surgery based solely on volumetric assessments. The presence of cirrhosis or other underlying liver disease such as fibrosis, steatosis or drug induced liver injury can also challenge long adopted volumetric cutoffs for resectability due to increased PHLF rates [20]. In addition, hepatobiliary scintigraphy has emerged as a valuable tool for selecting patients who have received extensive treatment with chemotherapeutic agents [21]. The E-AHPBA-ESSO-ESSR consensus guidelines for preoperative liver function assessment before hepatectomy recommend that functional assessments should complement FLR volumetry in all the scenarios mentioned above [22]. Rassam et al. [23] provide a comprehensive overview and guidance on using HBS for the quantitative assessment of liver function.

The current study demonstrates the additional benefits of functional assessment in patients undergoing venous embolization procedures. The functional gain following DVE is disproportionately greater than the volumetric gain. While DVE achieved a median 30% increase in FLR volume, the functional improvement reached a median of 75%, highlighting the importance of functional imaging. These findings are consistent with those of other single-center studies [12,24]. Additionally, there is a weak correlation between volume and function (R = 0.179; 95% CI, –0.15 to 0.48), which may support the prioritization of functional parameters in preoperative planning. The greater FI observed in this study, compared to volume gain, suggests that DVE induces rapid perfusional and metabolic adaptation in the FLR. Clinically, this has important implications, as the functional evaluation of the FLR (FLR-F) using Tc99-m SPECT-CT may offer superior guidance for surgical decision-making compared to volumetric assessment alone. Our findings suggest that DVE may lead to a greater FI compared to other hypertrophy-inducing techniques, such as associating liver partition with portal vein ligation for staged hepatectomy (ALPPS) and PVE. Kang and Schadde [25] reported that the FI following ALPPS is approximately 50% of the corresponding VI. de Graaf et al. [26] demonstrated comparable increases in volume and function following PVE (35.8% and 35.7%, respectively). In contrast, our study demonstrated a median FI of 75% despite a 30% increase in volume, suggesting a greater functional gain with DVE.

The interval between DVE, functional imaging, and surgery likely reflects logistical factors, including waiting list pressures within a publicly funded healthcare system. Notably, in patients who experienced disease progression, the median time from DVE to functional imaging was 29 days (IQR, 23–69), compared to 33 days (IQR, 13–64) in the other cohort, suggesting that delay alone may not fully explain disease progression.

Additionally, 21.6% (n = 8) of patients in our cohort did not proceed to resection due to disease progression. This highlights the importance of optimizing the timing of reassessment following DVE. A significantly greater improvement in FLR-Function compared to FLR-Volume growth in this study suggests that earlier intervention may be feasible based on FLR-Function cut-offs. In the current study, the median interval between DVE and Tc99-m SPECT-CT functional scanning was only 33 days, with a 75% increase in the FLR-F. If future prospective studies demonstrate that FLR-Function cut-offs can be achieved on earlier follow-up scans, it could allow for earlier resections, thereby reducing the risk of disease progression. Nevertheless, tumor biology remains unchanged, and a subset of patients may not benefit from this approach. Future research should explore time-to-surgery dynamics, with particular emphasis on expedited interventions that do not increase the risk of PHLF. This study has several limitations, primarily the small sample size. Although the data is reported retrospectively, patient pathway selection was based on a departmental protocol, which reduces the risk of selection bias. Future studies with larger cohorts and standardized imaging intervals are needed to validate these findings. However, the results of this study underscore the importance of incorporating functional imaging into preoperative assessments, as conventional volumetric measurements alone may underestimate the regenerative potential of the FLR, as previously reported [26].

In conclusion, DVE significantly enhances both FLR volume and FLR function, with a notably greater increase in function as assessed by Tc99-m SPECT-CT. These results support the routine incorporation of functional imaging into DVE protocols. Further prospective studies are needed to evaluate whether earlier post-DVE functional assessment using Tc99-m SPECT-CT may reduce dropout rates from the surgical pathway.
