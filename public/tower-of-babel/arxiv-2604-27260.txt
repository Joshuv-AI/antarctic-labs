Exact flatness constant for one-point convex bodies and the discrete isominwidth problem: the planar case Thanks: All of the authors are funded by the Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft (DFG, German Research Foundation) - 539867386.
Gennadiy Averkov, Giulia Codenotti, Ansgar Freyer, Kyle Huang

Abstract
. A variant of the flatness problem from integer programming is studied, in which one considers convex bodies in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} with at most k k interior lattice points. The maximum lattice width of such a body is denoted by Flt ​ ( d , k ) \hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k) and it is related to the classical flatness constant as well as a conjectural dual version of Minkowski’s convex body theorem due to Makai. Moreover, it is shown that Flt ​ ( 2 , 1 ) = 3 \hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(2,1)=3 , i.e., any planar convex body with at most one interior point has lattice width at most three. This leads to an isominwidth inequality for the lattice point enumerator of planar convex bodies.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Setting and main results

We investigate flatness-type problems from integer programming and geometry of numbers. The prototypical problem motivating our study is the classical flatness problem whose objective is maximizing the lattice width among all hollow convex bodies
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
in a given dimension
d
. Here, a
convex body
is a non-empty compact convex set. The set of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted by
\mathcal{K}^{d}
. The body
K
being
hollow
means that the interior of
K
contains no lattice points. The
lattice width
is defined as the infimum of the width function

\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K,{\boldsymbol{u}}):=\max_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in K}\langle{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle-\min_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in K}\langle{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle

of the underlying body
K
over non-zero lattice vectors:

\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K):=\inf_{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K,{\boldsymbol{u}})

If
K
has non-empty interior the infimum in the definition of
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)
is actually a minimum and is a positive number. Otherwise, the infimum is zero and it is not necessarily attained (cf. Remark
2.5
).
We also use
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K;X):=\inf_{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in X}\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K,{\boldsymbol{u}})
for some set of non-zero vectors
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular,
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)=\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K;\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\})
.
The lattice width is invariant with respect to
unimodular transformations
, i.e., affine maps of the form
{\boldsymbol{x}}\mapsto U{\boldsymbol{x}}+{\boldsymbol{t}}
, where
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
and
{\boldsymbol{t}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Here
\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
denotes the group of matrices
U\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times d}
for which
U^{-1}
exists in
\mathbb{Z}^{d\times d}
. We say that two sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are
unimodularly equivalent
, if there exists a unimodular transformation
g
such that
A=g(B)
.

In generalized flatness-type problems, one still maximizes the lattice width but replaces the hollowness constraint by another condition. The natural more general condition, which was already addressed in the seminal paper by Kannan and Lovász
[
coveringminima
, (4.1)]
, is having a fixed number
k
of interior lattice points. The case
k=0
corresponds to the classical setting (see Section
1.2.1
below). We call the respective convex bodies
k
-point convex bodies
and we study

(1.1)

\mathrm{Flt}(d,k):=\sup\{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K):K\in\mathcal{K}^{d},~\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}\leq k\},

where
\hypertarget{def:Gcirc}{\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}
denotes the number of interior lattice points of
K
.

We establish structural results for
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
in arbitrary dimension (Section
2
). One of our general insights is the algebraicity of the number
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
for any choice of
d
and
k
. This makes the problem of determination of the exact value of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
for given
d
and
k
a well-defined task. The problem of determining
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is non-trivial already in the case
d=2,k=0
. For this case, Hurkens determined that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(2,0)=1+\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}

[
hurkens
]
(cf. Theorem
A
).
When
d=2
and
k
is getting larger, the complexity of the problem seems to increase substantially. Already for
d=2,k=1
determination of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(2,1)
is very challenging, in particular, because the number of combinatorial situations to be considered grows dramatically and the cases themselves are more complex (with more degrees of freedom involved). Our main theorem states that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(2,1)=3
and that, up to unimodular equivalence, the standard triangle scaled by factor
3
, is the unique maximizer of the flatness problem among planar
1
-point convex bodies. We write
\Delta_{2}
for the
standard lattice triangle
, that is, the triangle with vertices
(0,0)
,
(0,1)
and
(1,0)
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex body with
\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}\leq 1
. Then,
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\leq 3
. Equality holds if and only if
K
is unimodularly equivalent to
3\Delta_{2}
.

Our main result implies two geometric inequalities, which we find remarkable. We discuss one here and postpone the other to Section
1.2.3
.

A central geometric inequality in metric geometry is the isoperimetric inequality in Euclidean geometry, which relates volume to surface area. A related version of this result is the isodiametric inequality. It relates volume to diameter and states that, among convex bodies of a given diameter, Euclidean balls maximize the volume.

Our main result is intimately related to the lattice version of the dual counterpart to the isodiametric inequality, namely the isominwidth inequality. While in the isodiametric case one minimizes the length of a longest segment at fixed volume, in the isominwidth setting one maximizes the width of a smallest enclosing strip at fixed volume. Note that segments and strips are dual objects.

Analogous problems arise in lattice geometry when the Euclidean minimum width is replaced by the lattice width. One may either keep a continuous volume or replace it by a discrete analogue (lattice point enumerator). Isominwidth problems, both in the Euclidean and in the lattice setting, are notoriously difficult. Exact solutions are typically known only in low dimensions, if any:
The exact solution to the Euclidean isominwidth problem is only known for dimension two, which is a result of Pál
[
pal
]
stating that for every
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\vol(K)>0
one has

(1.2)

\frac{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K;\mathbb{S}^{1})}{\vol(K)^{1/2}}\leq\frac{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(T_{\mathrm{reg}};\mathbb{S}^{1})}{\vol(T_{\mathrm{reg}})^{1/2}}.

Here,
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is the Euclidean unit circle and

T_{\mathrm{reg}}
is a regular triangle. Generalizing Pál’s inequality to higher dimensional convex bodies is a longstanding open problem.
The reader should be warned that the regular simplex is known to be
not
an optimal solution in dimensions
d>2
. A conjectural solution for
d=3
is described in
[
horvath
]
.

The version of the isominwidth problem for lattice width and volume was solved Fejes-Tóth and Makai
[
FejMak
,
Makai
]
in the plane. The solution is the inequality

(1.3)

\frac{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)}{\vol(K)^{1/2}}\leq\frac{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(T_{0})}{\vol(T_{0})^{1/2}}=\sqrt{\frac{8}{3}},

with the
terminal triangle

T_{0}=\mathrm{conv}\{(-1,-1),(0,1),(1,0)\}
being the unique equality case up to unimodular equivalence and dilation.
The corresponding inequality in three dimensional space was recently proven by Aliev in
[
Aliev
]
.

Here we complement the picture by providing what we call
an inhomogeneous isominwidth inequality, in which the lattice point enumerator takes the role of the volume.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex body with
\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}>0
. Then,

\frac{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)}{\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}^{1/2}}\leq\frac{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(3\Delta_{2})}{\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(3\Delta_{2})}^{1/2}}

and equality holds if and only if
K
is unimodularly equivalent to
3\Delta_{2}
.

Outline

In the remainder of this section, we discuss the role of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
in several branches of mathematics. In Section
2
, we prove results on
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
in arbitrary dimension.
Section
3
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
, which is carried out by a classification of inclusion maximal 1-point bodies. In Section
we apply the main theorem to prove Corollary
1.2
and we obtain explicit bounds in two transference theorems. We conclude with open questions in Section
.

1.2.
Motivation and applications

The flatness problem plays a fundamental role in many research areas, including integer programming, algebra, and algebraic geometry. In this section we explain the connection of flatness to these other topics, review results from these areas, many of which are also needed for our arguments, and present some consequences of our main results.

1.2.1.
The classical flatness problem

In recent years, substantial progress has been made in understanding the asymptotic behavior of the optimal value attained in the flatness problem – known as the
flatness constant
– as a function of the dimension
d
, see the recent breakthrough by Reis and Rothvoss
[
RR23
]
. One motivation for this is the study of asymptotic efficiency of algorithms for solving integer linear optimization problems, when the number
d
of integer variables goes to infinity. The first results of this type were given by Lenstra
[
lenstra
]
, with Reis and Rothvoss
[
RR23
]
providing a recent update on the asymptotic complexity of integer programming in dimension
d
.

Complementary to the asymptotic studies, determining the flatness constant
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
exactly for concrete values of
d
is a natural problem, in particular, because there is not a canonical candidate for an extremal body.
In fact, knowledge of values of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
is very limited. Trivially, one has
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(1,0)=1
while for
d=2
Hurkens obtained the following result:

Theorem A

(Hurkens,
[
hurkens
]
)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex body with
\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}=0
. Then,
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\leq 1+\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}
and the inequality is tight.

An alternative proof of Hurkens’ result can be found in
[
codenottifreyer
]
, significantly reducing the complexity of the resulting optimization problem. The proof of Theorem
1.1
borrows some ideas from this proof (cf. Section
).

Another way to phrase Theorem
A
is to say that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(2,0)=1+\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}
. Beyond the case
d=2
, no exact value of the flatness constant has been determined so far. Based on a symmetry argument, it has been conjectured in
[
acms_local_3d_2021
]
that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(3,0)=2+\sqrt{2}
. So, the non-asymptotic version of the classical flatness problem remains a challenge even in the dimension
d=3
. Mayrhofer, Schade and Weltge have provided conjectural realizers of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(4,0)
and
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(5,0)

[
MSW
]
, following the ideas from
[
acms_local_3d_2021
]
.

While the flatness problem in the fixed-dimensional setting is less well known than its asymptotic counterpart, it is nonetheless of significant importance.
Cutting plane theory, within integer optimization, is one context where this plays a key role.
One looks for additional linear inequalities in order to get closer to an integer solution. The new linear inequality is called a
cutting plane
.
In
[
balas_cuts_1972
]
, Balas established a general way to derive cutting planes from general hollow bodies. The respective cutting planes are known as
intersection cuts
(see
[
balas2018disjunctive
]
and
[
conforti2014integer
]
).

Cutting planes are derived from hollow bodies in fixed small dimensions.
For example, mixed-integer rounding cuts, which are omnipresent in integer optimization solvers,
are intersection cuts from the
1
-dimensional hollow segments
[a,a+1]
with
a\in\mathbb{Z}
. But it turns out that by making use of hollow bodies of higher dimensions, one can generate cuts that are significantly stronger; see Theorem 2 in
[
ave_basu_paat_2018
]
.

Understanding the strength of the cutting planes
depends crucially on the value of the flatness constant, and the values in fixed small dimensions are of particular importance. For instance, Dash et al.
[
dash2014lattice
]

show that so-called
21
-branch disjunctions are enough to derive any valid inequality for mixed integer models with three integer variables
by making use of the upper bounds on
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(3,0)
.

Computing the flatness constants in fixed dimensions also arises in toric geometry. Here, the study of the singularities corresponds to the analysis and classification of the so-called empty simplices–hollow lattice simplices whose only lattice points are their vertices. For
d=3
, White’s theorem says that all empty simplices have lattice width exactly one. But for
d=4
, the situation is much more complicated. The classification project started by Mori, Morrison and Morrison
[
mmm1988four
]
, and has been completed only recently by Iglesias-Valiño and Santos
[
iglesias_santos
]
, making use of the classification of maximal hollow lattice tetrahedra from
[
aww2011maximal
]
and
[
akw2017notions
]
and, in turn, the classification result in
[
akw2017notions
]
relies on the knowledge of bounds for the flatness constant in dimension
3
.

1.2.2.
Flatness problem for
k
-point convex bodies

We now discuss what is known about the
k
-point body version of the flatness problem, first addressed by Kannan and Lovász in
[
coveringminima
]
. Theorem (4.1) from
[
coveringminima
]
states that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)\leq\mathcal{O}(d^{2}(k+1)^{1/d})
.
This was subsequently improved to
\mathcal{O}(d(d^{1/2}+k^{1/d}))
by Banaszczyk, Litvak, Pajor and Szarek in
[
banalitvak
]
. As we will prove in Theorem
2.3
, an improvement on the asymptotic order of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
also gives improved bounds on the order of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
. By the aforementioned bound of Reis and Rothvoss, we obtain
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)\leq\mathcal{O}(d(\log(d)^{3}+k^{1/d}))
(cf. Corollary
2.4
).

In integer programming,
k
-point convex bodies can be used to generate cutting planes, as suggested by Averkov, González Merino, Paschke, Schymura, and Weltge
[
dhelly
, Section 3]
.
At the same time, in toric geometry, rational polygons with a single interior point encode so-called log canonical del Pezzo surfaces, see
[
bohnert_springer_classifying_2024
,
hhs_classifying_2025
]
.

Determining
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(2,2)
follows easily from work of Codenotti, Hofscheier, and Hall
[
gen_flatness_chh21
]
. A convex body in the plane which contains at most
2
interior lattice points is in particular a
\mathbb{Z}
-
\Delta_{2}
-free convex body, as defined by Averkov, Hofscheier and Nill in

[
AHN
]
: these are convex bodies in the plane whose interiors do not contain a unimodular triangle. Since the maximum width of such
\mathbb{Z}
-
\Delta_{2}
-free bodies was determined by Codenotti, Hall and Hofscheier in
[
gen_flatness_chh21
]
, this gives an upper bound for
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(2,2)
. To show that this bound is equal to
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(2,2)
, it is sufficient to observe that the unique maximizer of the width of
\mathbb{Z}
-
\Delta_{2}
-free (see Figure 9 in
[
gen_flatness_chh21
]
) contains exactly
2
interior lattice points. Thus, with
T_{0}=\mathrm{conv}\{(-1,-1),(0,1),(1,0)\}
, we have the following.

Theorem B

(Codenotti, Hofscheier, Hall,
[
gen_flatness_chh21
]
)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex body with
\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}\leq 2
. Then,
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\leq\tfrac{10}{3}
. Equality holds if and only if
K
is unimodularly equivalent to
\tfrac{5}{3}T_{0}+\tfrac{1}{3}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}
.

Our proof of Theorem
1.1
extends the methods of
[
gen_flatness_chh21
]
, although our casework is notably more involved, as the maximizer
3\Delta_{2}
arises as a limit of every parameter space requiring us to prove several sharp bounds, see Section
3
.

1.2.3.
Transference theorems

For a convex body
K
with
{\boldsymbol{o}}\in\mathrm{int}(K)
, one defines its
i
th successive minimum as

(1.4)

\lambda_{i}(K)=\min\{\lambda\geq 0:\dim(\lambda K\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d})\geq i\}

so that, in particular,

\lambda_{1}(K)=\min\{\lambda\geq 0:\lambda K\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}\neq\{{\boldsymbol{o}}\}\}.

The successive minima are critical parameters in the geometry of numbers as they give a good idea of the scale of the body
K
relative to the lattice.
It is easy to verify that
\lambda_{1}(K)\geq 1
holds if and only if
\mathrm{int}K\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\{{\boldsymbol{o}}\}
. Moreover, we have
\lambda_{1}((K-K)^{*})=\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)
, where
X^{*}=\{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle\leq 1,~\forall{\boldsymbol{x}}\in X\}
is the polar set and
K-K=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\colon{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in K\}
is the so-called difference body.
In this setting, an equivalent formulation of Theorem
1.1
is as follows (simply by applying the theorem to
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(K)}K
).

Corollary 1.3
.

For any convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
{\boldsymbol{o}}\in\mathrm{int}(K)
, we have

\lambda_{1}(K)\,\lambda_{1}((K-K)^{*})\leq 3,

and equality holds, if and only if

K=\lambda U\cdot\mathrm{conv}\{(-1,-1),(-1,2),(2,-1)\},

for some
\lambda>0
and
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

This inequality is an example of a “transference theorem”, by which one means an inequality relating a lattice quantity of
K
to its polar.
Another example is the inequality

(1.5)

\mu(K)\lambda_{1}((K-K)^{*})\leq 1+\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}},

where
\mu(K)
is the
covering radius
of
K
(cf. Section
2
).
This is simply a reformulation of Hurkens’ Theorem (cf. Theorem
A
) using the alternative definition of the flatness constant in Equation (
2.1
).

Interest in such transference theorems also stems from the interpretation of a convex body as the unit ball of a certain norm. Since this is most natural if
K
is origin symmetric, i.e.,
-K=K
holds, it is common to work in this setting.
Using bounds on the so-called Mahler volume one can show that

(1.6)

\lambda_{1}(K)\lambda_{1}(K^{*})\leq cd

holds for any origin symmetric convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}^{d}
with non-empty interior and some universal constant
c>0
. However, the constant
c
that is obtained this way is not optimal.

In
[
baranyfuredi
, Theorem 3]
, Barany and Füredi sketch an argument that shows that the quotient of lattice width and “lattice diameter” of a planar body is bounded by
\tfrac{4}{3}
.
From this one obtains an explicit upper bound of
\tfrac{4}{3}
in (
1.6
) via a simple limit argument. As a byproduct of our investigation we obtain a different proof of this fact, which additionally characterizes the equality case.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an origin symmetric convex body with
{\boldsymbol{o}}\in\mathrm{int}(K)
. Then,

(1.7)

\lambda_{1}(K)\,\lambda_{1}(K^{*})\leq\tfrac{4}{3}.

Moreover, equality holds if and only if

K=\lambda U\cdot\mathrm{conv}\{\pm(2,1),\pm(1,2),\pm(-1,1)\}

for some
\lambda>0
and
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

A similar theorem for the product of the first and second minimum of a planar convex body and its polar was proven by Ambro and Ito in
[
ambroito
]
.
On the asymptotic side, strong transference theorems have been obtained by Banaszczyk
[
banas1
,
banas2
,
banas3
]
, who obtained, for instance,

(1.8)

\lambda_{1}((K-K)^{*})\lambda_{d}(K-K)\leq c\,d\log(d)

for an absolute constant
c>0
and any
K\in\mathcal{K}^{d}
.

1.2.4.
Discretization of convexity

Our Corollary
1.2
can be seen as a “discretization” of the isominwidth inequality, where the area is replaced by the lattice point enumerator. This is inspired by the interpretation of volume as the lattice point count with respect to an infinitesimally fine lattice. More precisely, the volume of a compact set can be computed as the Riemann integral of its indicator.

The question that arises naturally is which theorems concerning the volume functional already hold for the lattice point enumerator? In recent years this question was studied for many classical theorem in convex geometry, such as the Brunn-Minkowski inequality
[
GardnerGronchi
,
Halikiasetal
,
Iglesiasetal
]
the Rogers-Shephard inequality
[
alonsoetal
]
as well as several results from discrete tomography
[
ahz
,
ggz
,
dcgpaper
,
freyerhenk
]
.

2.
Results in arbitrary dimension

2.1.
Asymptotic results and general inequalities

We give a summary of inequalities that link
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
for different choices of
(d,k)
and asymptotic results following from these inequalities. These inequalities and asymptotic statements are either directly available in the literature or follow directly from known results and techniques in the literature. While it is not our primary purpose to provide a survey, since results for
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
are scattered in the literature, we find providing an overview helpful. Further, we need some of the summarized results in our subsequent discussion.

Codenotti and Santos
[
codenottisantos
]
established that the sequence of flatness constants
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
for hollow convex bodies grows strictly with the dimension
d
.

Theorem 2.1

(Codenotti & Santos)
.

One has

\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(m+n,0)\geq\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(m,0)+\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(n,0)

for all
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
In particular,
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d+1,0)\geq\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)+1
for all

d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

In
[
acddf_chvatal_gomory_2013
]
, the generalized flatness constant for
k=1
appears in the context of the cutting plane theory of integer optimization problems; this paper studies the so-called reverse
Chvátal-Gomory rank
. As an auxiliary result, Corollary 5 in
[
acddf_chvatal_gomory_2013
]
gives an equality that can be interpreted as
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,1)\leq 2\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
. Based on the same idea, we can generalize this inequality as follows.

Proposition 2.2
.

For any
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
k,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, one has

\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)\leq m\cdot\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\lfloor\tfrac{k}{m^{d}}\rfloor).

In particular,

\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)\leq\left\lceil(k+1)^{1/d}\right\rceil\cdot\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0).

Proof.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
k
-point body. By the pigeonhole principle, there is a coset of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}/m\mathbb{Z}^{d}
that contains at most
\ell=\lfloor\tfrac{k}{m^{d}}\rfloor
lattice points of
\mathrm{int}(K)
. After applying a lattice translation, we may assume that this coset is the zero coset. Hence,
K
is an
\ell
-point body with respect to
m\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Thus,

\tfrac{1}{m}\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)=\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K;m\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{o}}\})\leq\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\ell),

which proves the first part of the claim. The second part follows from the first one by taking
m=\left\lceil(k+1)^{1/d}\right\rceil
.
∎

An alternative access to the growth of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is through volume approximation. To this end, we consider the constant

\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)=\sup\{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K):K\in\mathcal{K}^{d},~\vol(K)\leq 1\}.

It was conjectured by Makai that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)
is realized by the simplex
\mathrm{conv}\{-{\boldsymbol{1}},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{d}\}

[
Makai
]
. The conjecture is proven to be true for
d\leq 3

[
FejMak
,
Aliev
]
(cf. (
1.3
))

Intuitively, as
k
grows, the value
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)/k^{1/d}
should approach
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)
, since the latter quantity “counts lattice points in an infinitesimally fine lattice”.
We can verify and quantify this convergence:

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
d,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\geq 1
. Then,

\left|\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)}{k^{1/d}}-\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)\right|\leq\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}{k^{1/d}}.

In particular, we have
\lim k^{-1/d}\,\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)=\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)
as
k\to\infty
.

For the proof we use bounds on the lattice point enumerator in terms of volume and covering radius.
Let
\mu(K)
denote the covering radius of a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with respect to the integer lattice, i.e.,

\mu(K)=\min\{\mu\geq 0:\mu K+\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\}.

An alternative description of the covering radius is as the maximum dilate that admits a hollow translate, i.e.,

\mu(K)=\max\{\mu\geq 0:\exists{\boldsymbol{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}~\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(\mu K-{\boldsymbol{t}})}=0\}.

Therefore, the classical flatness constant may be expressed as

(2.1)

\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)=\max\{\mu(K)\cdot\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K):K\in\mathcal{K}^{d}\}.

In
[
dadush
,
mofmpaper
]
it was shown that

\vol(K)^{1/d}(1-\mu(K))\leq\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}^{1/d}\leq\mathrm{G}(K)^{1/d}\leq\vol(K)^{1/d}(1+\mu(K)),

where for the lower bound
\mu(K)\leq 1
is necessary, i.e.,
K+\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Combining this with (
2.1
) yields

(2.2)

\vol(K)^{1/d}\big(1-\tfrac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)}\big)\leq\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}^{1/d}\leq\mathrm{G}(K)^{1/d}\leq\vol(K)^{1/d}\big(1+\tfrac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)}\big).

We are now ready for the proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
K_{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{d}
be such that
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{k})\geq\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)-\varepsilon
, for
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.
First, we will show that

(2.3)

k^{1/d}\geq\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)-\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)}.

For this, we may assume that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)>\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
. Otherwise, the inequality becomes trivial. For
1\leq k<\infty
we have
\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K_{k})}\leq k
.
We apply the lower bound of (
2.2
) and obtain

k^{1/d}\geq\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K_{k})}^{1/d}\geq\vol(K_{k})^{1/d}\left(1-\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{k})}\right).

By the homogeneity of width and volume and the definition of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)
, we have

k^{1/d}\geq\frac{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{k})}{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)}\left(1-\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{k})}\right)\geq\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)-\varepsilon-\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)}.

We obtain Equation (
2.3
) by letting
\varepsilon\to 0
.
For the reverse direction we want to prove

(2.4)

k^{1/d}\leq\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)+\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)}.

After applying a dilation, we may assume that
\vol(K_{\infty})=1
. We choose
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
to be maximal such that
\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(\lambda K_{\infty})}\leq k
and, consequently,
k<\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}((\lambda+\varepsilon)K_{\infty})}
.
The upper bound from (
2.2
) applied to
(\lambda+\varepsilon)K_{\infty}
gives

\begin{split}k^{1/d}&<\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}((\lambda+\varepsilon)K_{\infty})}^{1/d}\leq(\lambda+\varepsilon)\left(1+\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}((\lambda+\varepsilon)K_{\infty})}\right)\\
&=\left(\lambda+\varepsilon+\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{\infty})}\right)\leq\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)+\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)-\varepsilon}+\varepsilon,\end{split}

where we used

\lambda\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{\infty})=\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(\lambda K_{\infty})\leq\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)

for the last inequality. Equation (
2.4
) follows from letting
\varepsilon\to 0
.
∎

From Theorem
2.3
we obtain the upper bound

(2.5)

\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)\leq\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)+\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)k^{1/d}.

While neither
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
, nor
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)
are determined explicitly, there are very good asymptotic bounds on these quantities. According to Reis and Rothvoss
[
RR23
]
we have
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)\leq O(d\log(d)^{3})
, whereas an application of the best-known bounds on the so-called Mahler volume quickly yields

(2.6)

\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)\leq\tfrac{8}{\pi}(d!)^{1/d}\leq\left(\tfrac{8}{e\pi}+o(1)\right)d.

We refer to
[
HenkXue
, page 3]
for details. As a consequence, we have

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
d,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\geq 1
. Then,

(2.7)

\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)\leq\mathcal{O}(d(\log(d)^{3}+k^{1/d})).

Remark 2.5
.

As a byproduct of Equation (
2.6
), we see that
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)=0
when
K\in\mathcal{K}^{d}
is not full-dimensional:
Since width and volume are homogeneous functionals, we can read (
2.6
) as

(2.8)

\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\leq(c_{d}\vol(K))^{1/d},

where
c_{d}=(\tfrac{8}{\pi})^{d}\,d!
, provided that
\vol(K)>0
. For
\vol(K)=0
we have

\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\leq\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K+\varepsilon B_{d})\leq(c_{d}\vol(K+\varepsilon B_{d}))^{1/d},

where
B_{d}
denotes the Euclidean unit ball and
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary. This shows that (
2.8
) holds for all convex bodies
K\in\mathcal{K}^{d}
.

This insight is not entirely trivial, as one may see by considering
K
being the irrational segment
K=\mathrm{conv}\{(0,0),(\sqrt{2},1)\}
. With this choice of
K
, the infimum in the definition of
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)
is not attained, which suggests that there is a need for justification why the infimum is in fact
0
. Diophantine approximation could be one line of justification for this particular
K
. This suggests that the equality
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)=0
for non-full-dimensional
K
is a disguised qualitative version of the Diophantine approximation.

From the asymptotic perspective, it is natural to ask for the ratio of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,1)/\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
as
d
grows large.
From (
2.5
) we obtain an upper bound:

(2.9)

1\leq\limsup_{d\to\infty}\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,1)}{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}\leq 1+\lim\sup_{d\to\infty}\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)}{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}\leq 1+\tfrac{4}{e\pi}\approx 1.468,

where we used a result of Mayrhofer, Schade and Weltge stating that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)\geq 2d-o(1)

[
MSW
]
in combination with (
2.6
) to obtain the last inequality.

Most likely, the above estimate of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,1)
through
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
is not sharp asymptotically, even if Makai’s conjecture was confirmed (in which case the upper bound would improve to
1+\tfrac{1}{e}
).

Restricting ourselves to simplices, the following proposition shows that the ratio converges to
1
as
d\to\infty
. Let
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}_{s}(d,k)
the maximum width of
k
-point simplices in dimension
d
. Then we have

Proposition 2.6
.

\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}_{s}(d,1)\leq(1+\frac{1}{d})\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}_{s}(d,0)
. In particular,

\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}_{s}(d,1)}{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}_{s}(d,0)}=1.

Proof.

In dimension
d
, consider the
1
-point simplex
S
of maximum lattice width. Let
{\boldsymbol{c}}
be the unique interior lattice point of
S
and let
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{d}
be the vertices of
S
. Then
{\boldsymbol{c}}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\lambda_{i}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}
with
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i=0}^{d}\lambda_{i}=1
. Assume that
\lambda_{0}
is the smallest barycentric coordinate. Then we have
\lambda_{0}\leq\frac{1}{d+1}
. After translation we can also assume that
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}=0
. It follows that
\frac{d}{d+1}S
is a hollow
d
-dimensional simplex. Consequently,
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}_{s}(d,0)\geq\frac{d}{d+1}\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}_{s}(d,1)
and the assertion follows.
∎

It would be interesting to understand the relation of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}_{s}(d,k)
and
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
. It seems plausible that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}_{s}(d,0)=\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
. For larger
k
, it might be that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}_{s}(d,k)
and
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
are close to each other or even coinciding. Indeed, Theorems
A
,
1.1
and
B
as well as Equation (
1.3
) seem to support this conjecture.

2.2.
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is attained

We clarify why the supremum in the definition of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is a maximum attained on a convex body.

A part of our attainability argument is topological, as we show that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is the problem of maximization of a continuous functional on a closed set.
Let
B_{d}
be the unit Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
centered in
{\boldsymbol{o}}
. The
Hausdorff distance
of convex bodies
K,L\in\mathcal{K}^{d}
is the minimum
\rho\geq 0
such that
K\subseteq L+\rho B_{d}
and
L\subseteq K+\rho B_{d}
.
The Hausdorff distance is a metric on
\mathcal{K}^{d}
so that it is also called the Hausdorff metric. It is well-known that the family
\mathcal{K}^{d}
of convex bodies endowed with the Hausdorff metric is a complete metric space.
For more details, see
[
schneider
]
.

In order to keep the paper self-contained, we want to give a rigorous proof of the continuity of the lattice width.

Lemma 2.7
.

The lattice width functional on
\mathcal{K}^{d}
is continuous with respect to the Hausdorff metric.

Proof.

Consider a sequence
(K_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}
of convex bodies in
\mathcal{K}^{d}
that converges to a convex body
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}^{d}
in the Hausdorff metric. It is known that the volume is continuous in the Hausdorff metric. Thus, the limit of
\vol(K_{t})
, as
t\to\infty
, is
\vol(K^{\prime})
. If
\vol(K^{\prime})=0
, then by Equation (
2.8
),
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{t})
is upper bounded by
(c_{d}\vol(K_{t}))^{1/d}
, a value converging to
0
. So,
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{t})\to 0
. Again by Equation (
2.8
),
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K^{\prime})=0
, as
K^{\prime}
is not full-dimensional. Consequently,
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{t})\to\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K^{\prime})
if
K^{\prime}
is not full-dimensional.

Consider the case of a full-dimensional limit
K^{\prime}
. Pick a ball
\rho B_{d}+{\boldsymbol{c}}
of a radius
\rho>0
, which is a subset of
K^{\prime}
. When
t
is large enough, we have
K^{\prime}\subseteq K_{t}+\frac{\rho}{2}B_{d}
and
K_{t}\subseteq K^{\prime}+\frac{\rho}{2}B_{d}
. Taking into account
\rho B_{d}+{\boldsymbol{c}}\subseteq K^{\prime}
, we obtain
\rho B_{d}+{\boldsymbol{c}}\subseteq K_{t}+\frac{\rho}{2}B_{d}
. Applying the cancellation law for convex bodies, we obtain
\frac{\rho}{2}B_{d}+{\boldsymbol{c}}\subseteq K_{t}
for all sufficiently large
t
. The latter implies that
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K,{\boldsymbol{u}})\geq\rho\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{2}
for
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{2}
is the Euclidean length of
{\boldsymbol{u}}
. On the other hand, in view of
K_{t}\subseteq K^{\prime}+\frac{\rho}{2}B_{d}
, one has
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{t})\leq\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K^{\prime}+\frac{\rho}{2}B_{d})=:\gamma
. Hence, if
\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{2}>\frac{\gamma}{\rho}
, then
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K,u)>\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)
. This allows us to determine
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{t})
as a minimum
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{t})=\min_{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in U}\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{t},{\boldsymbol{u}})
on the finite set
U
of all
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
that satisfy
\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\gamma}{\rho}
. As the width function is continuous in the Hausdorff metric for each fixed choice of direction
{\boldsymbol{u}}
, we conclude that
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{t})\to\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K^{\prime})
, as
t\to\infty
.
∎

In the appendix of
[
codenottifreyer
]
it is shown that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
is attained on some convex body. Our aim is to provide a somewhat more general statement for generic flatness-type problems.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a non-empty subfamily of
\mathcal{K}^{d}
, with the following properties:

i)

All convex bodies in
\mathcal{X}
are full-dimensional.

ii)

\mathcal{X}
is closed with respect to the Hausdorff metric.

iii)

Every convex body unimodularly equivalent to a convex body in
\mathcal{X}
belongs to
\mathcal{X}
.

iv)

\epsilon(\mathcal{X}):=\inf\{\lambda_{1}(K-K)\colon K\in\mathcal{X}\}>0
.

Then, whenever
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(\mathcal{X}):=\sup\{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\colon K\in\mathcal{X}\}
is finite, it is attained on some
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

By Equation (
1.8
),

\lambda_{d}((K-K)^{\ast})\lambda_{1}(K-K)\leq c\,d\log(d),

where
c>0
is an absolute constant.
This yields

\lambda_{d}((K-K)^{\ast})\leq\frac{c\,d\log(d)}{\epsilon(\mathcal{X})}

for
K\in\mathcal{X}
.
It is known that if
C
is a
{\boldsymbol{o}}
-symmetric convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
d\cdot\lambda_{d}(C)\cdot C
contains a basis of the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, see
[
geometryofnumbers
, equation (11) on page 68]
. Thus, for

\gamma_{d}(\mathcal{X}):=\frac{c\,d^{2}\log(d)}{\epsilon(\mathcal{X})}

we have that
every convex body
K\in\mathcal{X}
has the property that
\gamma_{d}(\mathcal{X})\cdot(K-K)^{\ast}
contains a basis of the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Since
(K-K)^{\ast}=\{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K,{\boldsymbol{u}})\leq 1\}
,
this means that there exists a basis
{\boldsymbol{b}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{d}
of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{i})\leq\gamma_{d}(\mathcal{X})
for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. Applying a unimodular transformation to
K
, we thus may assume that a translation of
K
is contained in the box
[0,\gamma_{d}(\mathcal{X})]^{d}
. But then, using translations by lattice vectors, we can assume that
K
is in the slightly expanded box
[0,\gamma_{d}(\mathcal{X})+1]^{d}
. Consequently,
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(\mathcal{X})
is the limit

\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(\mathcal{X})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{t})

for a sequence

(K_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}

of convex bodies

K_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
satisfying

K_{t}\subseteq[0,\gamma_{d}(\mathcal{X})+1]^{d}
. By Blaschke’s selection theorem (see
[
schneider
]
), we can pass to a subsequence convergent in the Hausdorff metric. Let
K^{\prime}
be a compact convex set being the limit of
K_{t}
in the Hausdorff metric, as
t\to\infty
. As
\mathcal{X}
is closed in the Hausdorff metric, we have
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
. Thus, since the lattice-width functional on
\mathcal{K}^{d}
is continuous in the Hausdorff metric, we have
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K^{\prime})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K_{t})=\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(\mathcal{X})
.
∎

In order to apply Lemma
2.8
to show that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is attained, we need to suggest a family
\mathcal{X}
of convex bodies with at most
k
interior lattice points that satisfies
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)=\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(\mathcal{X})
and the assumptions of Lemma
2.8
.

Lemma 2.9
.

For every
d
-dimensional convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}^{d}
with at most
k
interior lattice points, one has

\lambda_{1}(K-K)\geq\min\left\{\frac{1}{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)}\,,\,\frac{1}{k+1}\left(1-\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d-1,0)}{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)}\right)\right\}.

Proof.

We assume that
\lambda_{1}(K-K)<\frac{1}{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)}
and
1-\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d-1,0)}{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)}>0
, as otherwise the assertion is satisfied. Assume, without loss of generality, that
\lambda_{1}(K-K)
is attained in direction
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{d}
, so that
\lambda\cdot({\boldsymbol{p}}-{\boldsymbol{q}})={\boldsymbol{e}}_{d}
for some
{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{q}}\in K
. As
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)<\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(K-K)}
, the width of
K
is necessarily attained in a direction orthogonal to
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{d}
, because the points
{\boldsymbol{p}}
and
{\boldsymbol{q}}
ensure that the width function of
K
in directions not orthogonal to
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{d}
is at least
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(K-K)}
.

We consider the coordinate projection
\pi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}):=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1})
. Let
{\boldsymbol{r}}=\pi({\boldsymbol{p}})=\pi({\boldsymbol{q}})
. We scale
K
down to a smaller convex body
L=(1-\alpha)K+\alpha{\boldsymbol{p}}
, where
0<\alpha<1
. If
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(L)>\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d-1,0)
, then
\pi(L)
is not hollow and so it contains an interior lattice point, which can be given as
{\boldsymbol{z}}=\pi((1-\alpha){\boldsymbol{v}}+\alpha{\boldsymbol{p}})
with
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathrm{int}(K)
. It follows that
I:=(1-\alpha){\boldsymbol{v}}+\alpha\cdot\mathrm{conv}\{{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{q}}\}
is a segment whose relative interior is a subset of
\mathrm{int}(K)
of length
\frac{\alpha}{\lambda_{1}(K-K)}
. The segment
I
gets projected by
\pi
onto the lattice
{\boldsymbol{z}}
. As
K
has at most
k
interior lattice points, the length of segment
I
is at most
k+1
. We conclude that
\frac{\alpha}{\lambda_{1}(K-K)}\leq k+1
, whenever
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(L)>\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d-1,0)
. But

\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(L)=(1-\alpha)\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(\pi(K))=(1-\alpha)\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)
.
So, we see that
(1-\alpha)\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)>\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d-1,0)
implies
\alpha\leq(k+1)\lambda_{1}(K-K)
. Taking
\alpha=1-\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d-1,0)/\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)-\epsilon
, where
\epsilon>0
is small, we have

1-\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d-1,0)/\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)-\epsilon\leq(k+1)\lambda_{1}(K-K).

Taking the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
, we obtain
1-\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d-1,0)/\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\leq(k+1)\lambda_{1}(K-K)
.
∎

Theorem 2.10
.

For every
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
the generalized flatness constant
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is attained on a compact convex set
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}^{d}
with
\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K^{\prime})}\leq k
. Furthermore, every such
K^{\prime}
satisfies
\lambda_{1}(K^{\prime}-K^{\prime})\geq\frac{1}{(1+k)\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)}
.

Proof.

It is known that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is a finite positive number and from the definition of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
it can be seen that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)\geq\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
. So, let
\mathcal{X}
be the family of all convex bodies
K
with at most
k
interior lattice points that satisfy
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)\leq\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)
. We will use Lemma
2.8
to verify the assertion. For our choice of
\mathcal{X}
, we have
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(\mathcal{X})=\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
.
Lemma
2.9
yields

\lambda_{1}(K-K)\geq\min\left\{\frac{1}{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)},\frac{1}{k+1}\left(1-\frac{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d-1,0)}{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}\right)\right\}

for every
K\in\mathcal{X}
.
By Lemma
2.1
,
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d-1,0)\leq\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)-1
, which implies

\lambda_{1}(K-K)\geq\min\left\{\frac{1}{\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)},\frac{1}{(k+1)\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)}\right\}.

In view of Proposition
2.2
,
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)\leq\left\lceil(k+1)^{1/d}\right\rceil\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)\leq(k+1)\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
.

This implies that
\inf\{\lambda_{1}(K-K)\colon K\in\mathcal{X}\}\geq\frac{1}{(k+1)\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)}
for every
K\in\mathcal{X}
. Thus, the assumption
\epsilon(\mathcal{X})>0
in Lemma
2.8
is satisfied.

So, by Lemma
2.8
,
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(\mathcal{X})=\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is attained on some
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and that every such
K^{\prime}
satisfies
\lambda_{1}(K^{\prime}-K^{\prime})\geq\frac{1}{(k+1)\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)}
.
∎

We can also derive an analogous statement for
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)
.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
Then
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)=\sup\{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\colon K\in\mathcal{K}^{d},\ \vol(K)\leq 1\}
is attained on some
K^{\prime}\in\{K\in\mathcal{K}^{d}\colon\vol(K)\leq 1\}
.

Proof.

We recall that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)
is the supremum of the lattice width functional over convex bodies
K\in\mathcal{K}^{d}
that satisfy
\vol(K)\leq 1
.
When calculating
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)
we can restrict attention to convex bodies with
\vol(K)=1
, because the lattice width of convex bodies
K
with
\vol(K)=0
is zero and each convex body with the volume
0<\vol(K)<1
can be re-scaled to a larger convex body with the volume
1
. Such a re-scaling makes the lattice width larger. As the lattice width of the unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
is
1
and its volume is also
1
, we can restrict attention to convex bodies of the lattice width at least
1
.

We thus can define
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)
as
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)=\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(\mathcal{X})
, where
\mathcal{X}=\{K\in\mathcal{K}^{d}\colon\vol(K)=1,\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\geq 1\}
. This family satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
2.8
. The only assumption whose verification is not straightforward is
\epsilon(\mathcal{X})>0
.
Using Banaszczyk’s transference theorem (
1.8
) as well as the lower bound in Minkowski’s second theorem on successive minima

\frac{2^{d}}{d!}\leq\vol(K-K)\lambda_{1}(K-K)\cdots\lambda_{d}(K-K),

the Rogers-Shephard inequality for the difference body

\vol(K-K)\leq\binom{2d}{d}\vol(K),

and our assumption
\vol(K)=1
, we eventually obtain
\lambda_{1}(K-K)\geq\frac{2^{d}}{d!\cdot\binom{2d}{d}(cd\log(d))^{d-1}}
. Thus, one has
\epsilon(\mathcal{X})>0
.
Theorem
2.8
implies that
\mathcal{X}
is attained on some
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}^{d}
.
∎

2.3.
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is attained on polytopes

It turns out that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is attained on polyhedra and, for
k<\infty
, there are two classes of polyhedra that can be shown to contain optimizers of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
: lattice reduced bodies and
k
-maximal bodies. Lattice reduced bodies were introduced in
[
codenottifreyer
]
.

Definition 2.12
.

A
d
-dimensional convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}^{d}
is called lattice reduced if for every convex body
L
that is a proper subset of
K
one has
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(L)<\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)
.

In
[
codenottifreyer
]
, the following has been shown.

Theorem 2.13
.

Every
d
-dimensional convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}^{d}
contains a reduced convex body of the same lattice width. Every
d
-dimensional reduced convex body is a polytope with at most
2^{d+1}-2
vertices.

As an immediate corollary of this result, we obtain.

Theorem 2.14
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Then
every optimizer of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)
is a polytope with at most
2^{d+1}-2
vertices and, for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, there exists an optimizer of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
that is a polytope with at most
2^{d+1}-2
vertices.

Proof.

Consider an optimizer
K^{\prime}
of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\infty)
. If
K^{\prime}
is not reduced, by Theorem
2.13
, there would exist a reduced convex body
L^{\prime}
properly contained in
K^{\prime}
with the same lattice width. But then
\frac{1}{\vol(L^{\prime})^{1/d}}L^{\prime}
is a convex body of volume one and with a larger lattice width than
K^{\prime}
, which contradicts the choice of
K^{\prime}
. So,
K^{\prime}
is necessarily reduced and by Theorem
2.13
,
K^{\prime}
is a polytope with at most
2^{d+1}-2
vertices.

For
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
the argument is similar. Consider an optimizer
K^{\prime}
of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
. Then a reduced convex body
L^{\prime}
being a subset of
K^{\prime}
and having the same lattice width is also an optimizer of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
and, by Theorem
2.13
,
L^{\prime}
is a polytope with at most
2^{d+1}-2
vertices.
∎

While lattice reduced optimizers of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
arise from the incentive to make an optimizer of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
inclusion-minimal, we may have the opposite incentive of making a given optimizer of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
inclusion-maximal. For such inclusion-maximal sets, it will be convenient to distinguish the exact numbers of lattice points, which is why we use the following definition.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be closed convex set and let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. We say that
K
is
k
-maximal, if
\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}=k
and for any closed convex set
L\supseteq K
with
\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(L)}=k
, we have
L=K
.

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Then every closed convex set
K
with
k
interior lattice points is a subset of a
k
-maximal closed convex set.

Proof.

Let
Z=K\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and consider the partially ordered set consisting of all open convex set
U
satisfying

U\subseteq\mathrm{int}(K)
. Zorn’s lemma guarantees that this poset has a maximal element
U^{\prime}
. The topological closure of
U^{\prime}
is a
k
-maximal closed convex set.
∎

A 0-maximal closed convex set is also known as an “inclusion maximal hollow” body in the literature. Lovász gave a characterization of 0-maximal closed convex sets (see also
[
Av
]
for a short proof).

Theorem 2.17

(Lovász,
[
lovasz_2d
]
)
.

The following statements are true.

i)

A subset
K
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
0
-maximal if and only
K
is a polyhedron with no interior lattice points and such that the relative interior of each facet of
K
contains a lattice point.

ii)

A unbounded subset
K
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
0
-maximal if and only if it is unimodularly equivalent to
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times K^{\prime}
, where
m\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
and
K
is a bounded
0
-maximal set in
\mathbb{R}^{d-m}
.

iii)

Every
0
-maximal subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a polyhedron with at most
2^{d}
facets. Furthermore, there exist polytopes with
2^{d}
facets that are
0
-maximal.

For
k=0
, we thus have the following.

Proposition 2.18
.

For each
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, the flatness constant
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
is attained on a
0
-maximal polytope with at most
2^{d}
facets.

Proof.

Let
K^{\prime}
be an arbitrary
0
-point convex body with
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K^{\prime})=\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
and a
0
-maximal closed convex set
L^{\prime}
that contains
K^{\prime}
as a subset. Such a set exists by Proposition
2.16
. By Theorem
2.17
,
L^{\prime}
is a polyhedron. This polyhedron is bounded. In fact, if
L^{\prime}
were unbounded, then up to unimodular transformation it would have the form
L^{\prime}=\mathbb{R}^{m}\times M^{\prime}
, where
m\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
and
M^{\prime}
is a bounded
0
-maximal set in dimension
d-m
. But in view of Theorem
2.1
,
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K^{\prime})=\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(L^{\prime})\leq\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(M^{\prime})\leq\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d-m,0)<\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
, which is a contradiction to the choice of
K^{\prime}
. Hence,
L^{\prime}
is a bounded
0
-maximal set on which
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
is attained. Theorem
2.17
gives bound
2^{d}
on the number of facets of
L^{\prime}
.
∎

In the case
k>0
,
k
-maximal sets are necessarily bounded, but similarly to the case
k=0
, we can characterize
k
-maximal bodies as polytopes with exactly
k
interior points and at least one lattice point inside each facet.

Theorem 2.19

(see
[
dhelly
]
)
.

Let
d,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Then a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
k
-maximal if and only if it is a polytope with
k
interior lattice points and the relative interior of each facet containing a lattice point. Furthermore, there exists a finite upper bound
h(d,k)
on the number of facets of
k
-maximal polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We refer to the lattice points in the relative interior of facets of
K
as
blocking points
.

As a direct consequence of Proposition
2.16
and Theorem
2.19
, we obtain

Proposition 2.20
.

For all
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is attained on an
\ell
-maximal polytope with
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
. Furthermore, if
\ell<k
, then
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\ell)=\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,\ell+1)=\cdots=\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
.

If we knew that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is strictly increasing in
k
, we could strengthen the assertion of the above proposition by setting
\ell=k
. The following proposition implies that we can define
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
as
\sup\{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\colon K\in\mathcal{K}^{d},\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}=k\}
, using convex bodies satisfying
\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}=k

rather than the bodies satisfying
\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}\leq k
, but in such a definition it is not clear whether the supremum is attained.

We use it to prove that the
\leq
sign in the definition of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
can (almost) be replaced by an
=
sign.

Proposition 2.21
.

For any
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
with
k\leq\ell
we have

\sup\{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\colon K\in\mathcal{K}^{d},~\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}=k\}\leq\sup\{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\colon K\in\mathcal{K}^{d},~\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}=\ell\}.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the assertion for
\ell=k+1
. If
k=0
, fix a
0
-maximal convex body
K
that attains
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
. As we have observed, such a body
K
is a polytope. In the case
k=1
, take
K
to be an
k
-maximal polytope.
Consider a facet
F\subset K
and let
Z_{F}=\mathrm{relint}(F)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Moreover, let
{\boldsymbol{z}}
be a vertex of
\mathrm{conv}(Z_{F})
. We choose a
(d-2)
-plane
A\subset\mathrm{aff}(F)
that separates
{\boldsymbol{z}}
from
\mathrm{conv}(Z_{F}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\})
. Rotating
F
around
A
slightly yields a new polytope
K^{\prime}
with
\mathrm{int}(K^{\prime})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=(\mathrm{int}(K)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d})\cup\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\}
whose width is arbitrarily close to the width of
K
. This shows that

\sup\{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\colon K\in\mathcal{K}^{d},~\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}=k\}\leq\sup\{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\colon K\in\mathcal{K}^{d},~\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}=k+1\}.\qed

As a consequence, we see that

(2.10)

\begin{split}\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)&=\sup\{\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K):K\in\mathcal{K}^{d},~\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}=k\}.\end{split}

Unlike the maximum in (
1.1
) it is not clear that the supremum in (
2.10
) is attained. The main difference is that the set of convex bodies that satisfies
\hyperlink{def:Gcirc}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{G}^{\circ}(K)}=k
is not closed for
k>0
, which is why the usual compactness arguments don’t apply.

2.4.
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is an algebraic number

We show that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is algebraic for all
d,k
. Previously, it was not known if even
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,0)
is always an algebraic number.

Theorem 2.22
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Then
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is an algebraic number.

Proof.

Above we have shown that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is attained on a polytope. So let
P
be a polytope on which

\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is attained, let

Z=\mathrm{int}(P)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and let
m
be the number of vertices of
P
. Let us sandwich
P
between two rational polytopes
A
and
B
with the property that
A\subseteq\mathrm{int}(P)\subseteq P\subseteq\mathrm{int}(B)
and

A\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=Z
. Let
W=B\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Consider polytopes of the form
Q=\mathrm{conv}({\boldsymbol{q}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{q}}_{m})
with the property that
{\boldsymbol{q}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{q}}_{m}\in B
,
Q\supseteq A
and
Q\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=Z
.
We show that the condition

C(t,Q):=\quad\bigl(t=\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(Q)\bigr)\wedge\bigl(A\subseteq Q\subseteq B\bigr)\wedge\bigl(Q\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=Z\bigr)

can be formulated as a first-order formula over reals. We note that
Q
is given by
{\boldsymbol{q}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{q}}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so that this condition is a constraint involving in
1+dm
variables
(t,q_{1},\ldots,q_{m})
.
For the condition
t=\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(Q)
to be phrased as a first-order formula, we observe that the fact
A\subseteq Q\subseteq B
allows us to formulate
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(Q)
as a finite mini-max
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(Q)=\min_{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in U}\max_{i,j=1,\ldots,m}\langle{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{q}}_{j},{\boldsymbol{u}}\rangle
, where
U\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{o}}\}
is the set of all vectors
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{o}}\}
satisfying
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(A,{\boldsymbol{u}})\leq\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(B)
. This shows that
t=\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(Q)
can be formulated via a first-order formula over reals. The condition
A\subseteq Q
means that for every vertex
{\boldsymbol{a}}
of
A
one has
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in Q
, where
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in Q
can be phrased as the existence of a representation as a convex combination
{\boldsymbol{a}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}
, with
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}=1
. This can be described via first order formula, too. The condition
Q\subseteq B
can be phrased as
{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}\in B
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
and so it can be formulated as a system of linear inequalities with rational coefficients using the inequality description of
B
. Finally, the condition
Q\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=Z
can be formulated as the fact that among points of
W
, the points of
Z
belong to
Q
, while the points of
W\setminus Q
do not belong to
Q
. Again, this can be phrased as the existence of a convex combination
{\boldsymbol{z}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}
for the points in
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in Z
and as a negation of the existence of a convex combination
{\boldsymbol{w}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}
for the points
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in W\setminus Z
.

It follows that the condition
D(t)
describing that
t
is the lattice width of some polytope
Q
of the form
Q=\mathrm{conv}({\boldsymbol{q}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{q}}_{m})
that satisfies

A\subseteq Q\subseteq B
and

Q\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=Z
is a first-order formula over reals that is written using coefficients in the field
\mathbb{Q}
.

By quantifier elimination algorithm from real algebra, we can re-formulate the formula
D(t)
using a quantifier-free formula with the coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}
. Such a quantifier-free reformulation of
D(t)
is a Boolean combination

\Phi(g_{1}(t)\geq 0,\ldots,g_{N}(t)\geq 0)
of polynomial inequalities with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}
. That is,
\Phi
is a Boolean formula and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{N}
are polynomials in
\mathbb{Q}[t]
. The formula
D(t)
describes the set

T:=\{t\in\mathbb{R}\colon D(t)\ \text{is satisfied}\}
. By construction, the every element of
T
is the lattice-width of some polytope
Q
that contains at most
k
lattice points. Hence,
\sup T\leq\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
.
On the other hand, by taking
{\boldsymbol{q}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{q}}_{m}
arbitrarily close to the
m
vertices of
P
, the polytope
Q=\mathrm{conv}(q_{1},\ldots,q_{m})
gets arbitrarily close to the maximizer
P
of
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
. Even more specifically, we can pick
Q\subseteq P
that is a slightly smaller homothetical copy of
P
.
Consequently,
\sup T=\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
and
\sup T-\epsilon\in T
for every sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
. This shows that in a small neighborhood of
\sup T
as
t
increases and passes through
\sup T
, the truth value of the Boolean formula
\Phi(g_{1}(t)\geq 0,\ldots,g_{N}(t)\geq 0)
gets flipped from true to false. This is only possible if some of the polynomials
g_{1},\ldots,g_{N}
is zero at
\sup T
. In fact, if none of these polynomials were zero at
\sup T
, their sign would not change in a small neighborhood of
\sup T
, so that the truth value of the formula for
t
would remain the same, which is a contradiction. It follows that
\sup T=\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is a zero of a polynomial from
\mathbb{Q}[t]
. Hence,
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(d,k)
is an algebraic number.
∎

and

Remark 2.24

Remark 2.25

3.
Planar convex bodies with at most one interior point

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
, that is, we show that
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(2,1)=3
.

3.1.
1-maximal polygons and their vertices

In
[
hurkens
]
, Hurkens handled the case of convex bodies with no interior lattice points, i.e., he computes
\hyperlink{target:flt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Flt}}(2,0)
, so we now have to treat convex bodies with exactly one interior lattice point. We can further reduce to treating only
1
-maximal convex bodies (cf. Definition
2.15
). That is, it suffices to show the following statement.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
K
be a
1
-maximal convex body. Then
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(K)\leq 3
and equality holds if and only if
K
is unimodularly equivalent to
3\Delta_{2}
.

The notion of blocking points (cf. Section
2.3
) allows us to associate to any
1
-maximal polygon a lattice polygon that we call
blocking polygon
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a
1
-maximal polygon. Its
blocking polygon

B

P

is the convex hull of the blocking points of
P
, that is,

\hyperref@@ii[def:blocking-polytope]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B_{P}}=\mathrm{conv}\{\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathrm{relint}(e)\text{ for some edge }e\subset P\}.

We sometimes refer to
P
as a circumscriber of
B

P

.

Our first goal is to show that the vertices of the circumscriber
P
in the above definition correspond in a weak sense to the edges of its blocking polygon
B

P

and that they necessarily lie in specific regions that depend on the lattice point structure of
B

P

.

We let
B=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\leq b_{i},~0\leq i\leq m\}
be a lattice polygon. For an edge
e=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in B:\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle=b_{j}\}
,
0\leq j\leq m
, of
B
we define the polyhedron
S_{e}
obtained from
B
by reversing the inequality constraint responsible for
e
, i.e.,

(3.1)

S_{e}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\geq b_{j},~\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\leq b_{i},~i\neq j\}.

Moreover, for
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathrm{int}(B)
, we consider the face cone

F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},e)=\mathrm{pos}(e-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})+{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},

where
\mathrm{pos}(X)=\{\lambda{\boldsymbol{x}}:{\boldsymbol{x}}\in X,~\lambda\geq 0\}
.
These polyhedra are related as follows.

Lemma 3.3
.

In the above setting, we have

S_{e}=\bigcap_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in B\setminus e}F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},e).

Proof.

Let us write
B=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\leq b_{i},~0\leq i\leq m\}
, where
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We may assume that the inequality description is irredundant. Without loss of generality, let
e
be the edge defined by the outer normal vector
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}
. Then,

(3.2)

S_{e}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\geq b_{0},~\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\leq b_{i},~1\leq i\leq m\},

and we define
\overline{S}_{e}=B\cup S_{e}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\leq b_{i},~1\leq i\leq m\}
.

First, let
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in S_{e}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in B\setminus e
. The segment
[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{y}}]
intersects
S_{e}\cap\{\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle=b_{0}\}=e
in a point
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}\neq{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
. It follows that there exists some
\lambda>0
such that
{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+\lambda({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\in F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},e)
.

For the reverse inclusion, consider
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus S_{e}
. Then,
{\boldsymbol{y}}
violates one of the inequalities defining
S_{e}
(cf. (
3.2
)). First, we assume that
\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle<b_{0}
. There exists
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
in
B\setminus e
such that
\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle<\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\rangle
and, thus,
\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle>\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle
holds for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},e)
.

Finally, assume that
\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle>b_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq m
. There exists
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in B\setminus e
with
\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\rangle=b_{i}
. Since
e\subset B\subset\{\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\leq b_{i}\}
we have
\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\leq b_{i}
for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},e)
. Thus, in both cases, there exists
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in B\setminus e
with
{\boldsymbol{y}}\not\in F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},e)
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
B
be a lattice polygon with edge set
E(B)
and let
P
be a 1-maximal polygon with
\hyperref@@ii[def:blocking-polytope]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B_{P}}=B
. For
e\in E(B)
let
S_{e}
be the region described in Equation (
3.1
). Then for each
e\in E(B)
there exists a point
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{e}\in S_{e}
such that
P=\mathrm{conv}\{{\boldsymbol{p}}_{e}:e\in E(B)\}
.

Proof.

Fix an edge
e\in E(B)
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in B\setminus e
. Since the cones
(F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},e))_{e\in E(P)}
form a fan, each vertex
{\boldsymbol{p}}
of
P
is contained in some
F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},e)
. Within
F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},e)
there can be at most one vertex of
P
. Otherwise, there exists an edge of
P
that is separated from
B
by the affine hull of
e
. This would contradict
\hyperref@@ii[def:blocking-polytope]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B_{P}}=B
.

If
\mathrm{int}(F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},e))
does contain a vertex of
P
we
denote it by
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{e}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
. Note that
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{e}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\notin B
, since it is a vertex of
P
and
P
circumscribes
B
. Moreover,
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{e}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
is contained in two edges of
P
that support
B
at the endpoints of
e
. Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}
be a point in
B\setminus e\subset P
.
Then the segment
[{\boldsymbol{p}}_{e}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}),{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}]
is contained in
P
and, thus, crosses
e
. Hence,
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{e}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\in F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},e)
and we may set
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{e}={\boldsymbol{p}}_{e}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
.
If there exists no
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in B\setminus e
such that
F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},e)
contains a vertex of
P
, we define
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{e}
to be some point in the relative interior of
e
. In either case, we have
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{e}\in S_{e}
(cf. Lemma
3.3
).

By the fan property of
(F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},e))_{e\in E(B)}
, the vertices of
P
are covered by the points
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{e}
.
∎

We observe that the interiors of the regions
S_{e}
are pairwise disjoint. Moreover, we can further restrict the regions where the vertices of
P
can lie by taking into account the lattice point structure of
P
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
P
be a 1-maximal polygon with interior lattice point
{\boldsymbol{p}}
. Then, for any
{\boldsymbol{q}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{p}}\}
we have

P\cap(-\mathrm{int}((\mathrm{pos}(\hyperref@@ii[def:blocking-polytope]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B_{P}}-{\boldsymbol{q}}))+{\boldsymbol{q}})=\emptyset.

In what follows we write
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{q}}=-\mathrm{int}(\mathrm{pos}(\hyperref@@ii[def:blocking-polytope]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B_{P}}-{\boldsymbol{q}}))+{\boldsymbol{q}}
.

Proof.

Since
B

P

is 2-dimensional, we have
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{q}}=-\mathrm{pos}(\mathrm{int}(B)-{\boldsymbol{q}})+{\boldsymbol{q}}
. Towards a contradiction, let
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in P\cap\sigma_{\boldsymbol{q}}
. By definition of
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{q}}
, for some
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathrm{int}(B)
we have
{\boldsymbol{q}}\in[{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}]
. Since
B\subset P
, we have
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathrm{int}(P)
and, thus,
{\boldsymbol{q}}\in\mathrm{int}(P)
. This contradicts the fact that
{\boldsymbol{p}}
is the unique interior lattice point of
P
.
∎

In summary, Lemmas
3.4
and
3.5
give us a good idea of the vertices of a 1-maximal polygon
P
with
B_{P}=P
; First, we obtain from Lemma
3.4
that we have (at most) one vertex
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{e}
per edge
e
and that it is contained in the polyhedron
S_{e}
. In some cases, all points in the interior of a region
S_{e}
are possible as vertices of
P
, for instance if
B
is a hexagon (cf. Section
). In other cases, we can further reduce the set
S_{e}
with the help of Lemma
3.5
which gives

(3.3)

{\boldsymbol{p}}_{e}\in S_{e}\setminus\bigcup_{{\boldsymbol{q}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{p}}\}}\sigma_{q}.

This yields a proper subset of
S_{e}
if, for instance,
B
is a symmetric quadrilateral (cf. Section
).

3.2.
Classifying the blocking polygons

In this section we list (up to unimodular equivalence) the possible blocking polygons that we can see for a 1-maximal polygon.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
P
be a
1
-maximal polygon. Then its blocking polytope
B_{P}
is a 2-dimensional lattice polygon. Moreover exactly one of the following holds:

(i)

B

P

is an empty polygon (either the standard triangle or the unit square),

(ii)

B

P

is a hollow polygon with lattice points in the relative interior of some of its edges,

(iii)

B

P

is a polygon with exactly one interior lattice point.

Proof.

We first show that
B

P

is 2-dimensional. By Theorem
2.19
, each edge of
P
contains at least one blocking point. We need to show that three blocking points in distinct edges cannot be colinear. Indeed, take two such blocking points. Since they each lie in the relative interior of a different edge, the open segment connecting them is in the interior of
P
, and therefore cannot contain any blocking point.

Since an interior lattice point of
B

P

is also an interior lattice point of
P
, its clear that
B

P

cannot have more than one interior lattice point, otherwise
P
would not be
1
-maximal.
∎

We require the following lemma in order to reduce the sets of blocking polygons to a finite list.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
P
be a
1
-maximal polygon, with three colinear lattice points on an edge of
P
. Then
\hyperlink{def:wdt}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{w}}(P)\leq 3
with equality attained exactly if
P
is unimodularly equivalent to
3\Delta_{2}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
P
contains an edge
f_{0}
with three colinear points. After a unimodular transformation, we can assume that

(3.4)

(-1,-1),(0,-1),(1,-1)\in f_{0}.

and that
P\subset\{y\geq-1\}
. Let
{\boldsymbol{p}}
be the interior point of
P
. We have
p_{y}=0
; Otherwise, the triangle
\mathrm{conv}\{(0,-1),(1,-1),{\boldsymbol{p}}\}
contains an integer point which is not one of its vertices. Such a point would be an additional interior point of
P
.
After applying a shear, we can, thus, assume that
{\boldsymbol{p}}=(0,0)
.

Let
\ell_{0}
denote the length of
f_{0}
