Gut bacterial characteristics in children with autism spectrum disorder according to symptom severity: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Aliye Aili, Zumureti Yimin, Nadire Maiming, Yilijiang Tuhongjiang, jiazila Paerhati, Xayida Aini, Asimuguli Kelimu, Rena Maimaiti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is associated with alterations in gut microbiota. The gut–brain axis, which involves bidirectional communication between the gastrointestinal tract and the central nervous system, has been implicated in the pathophysiology of ASD. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between gut microbiota characteristics and symptom severity in children with ASD.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled a total of 81 children with ASD aged 3–12 years, who were divided into mild-to-moderate (n = 35) and severe (n = 46) groups based on Childhood Autism Rating Scale scores. Fecal samples were collected for 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Alpha diversity, beta diversity, and species composition were analyzed using R software. Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) with a threshold of LDA score ≥ 2.0 and P < 0.05 was used to identify differentially abundant taxa.ResultsThe severe ASD group showed significantly lower Shannon index [4.04 (3.74–4.33) vs. 3.82 (3.40–4.23); P = 0.047] and Simpson index [0.95 (0.93–0.96) vs. 0.92 (0.90–0.96); P = 0.012] compared with the mild-to-moderate ASD group. Beta diversity analysis revealed significant separation between groups (PERMANOVA, R² = 0.048, P = 0.003). At the phylum level, the severe ASD group showed decreased Firmicutes (56.92%→47.46%) and increased Proteobacteria (5.92%→14.46%). At the family level, Enterobacteriaceae was increased (4.64%→13.08%), while Selenomonadaceae (2.64%→0.80%), Veillonellaceae (3.99%→2.70%), and Coriobacteriaceae (3.90%→2.36%) were decreased. At the genus level, Klebsiella showed a 2.8-fold increase (1.81%→5.06%), while Faecalibacterium (14.81%→11.84%) and Dialister (2.85%→1.95%) were decreased. LEfSe identified 11 differentially abundant taxa (LDA score ≥ 2.0, P < 0.05), with 10 taxa enriched in the mild-to-moderate ASD group, including Firmicutes (LDA = 4.69, P = 0.003), Coriobacteriia (LDA = 3.95, P = 0.026), Coriobacteriales (LDA = 3.95, P = 0.026), Bacteroides ovatus (LDA = 3.10, P = 0.048), and Sutterella (LDA = 2.65, P = 0.030), and several uncultured taxa. Only Fusobacteriaceae (family) was enriched in the severe ASD group (LDA = 3.40, P = 0.043).ConclusionsGut bacterial dysbiosis in ASD children is characterized by reduced diversity and altered composition, with more pronounced abnormalities in the severe ASD group. However, this study only evaluated bacterial communities (not fungi or viruses), and the cross-sectional design does not support causal inferences. These findings suggest that microbiota modulation may represent a potential therapeutic approach for ASD, though causal relationships remain to be established in longitudinal studies.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder, Gut microbiota, 16S rRNA sequencing, Symptom severity, Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS), Gut-brain axis

Background

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a group of neurodevelopmental disorders characterized by impairments in social communication and interaction, restricted interests, and repetitive behaviors [1]. The incidence of ASD has been gradually increasing. World Health Organization (WHO) data show that the global prevalence of ASD in children is approximately 1 in 100 [2]. According to the latest data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) published in April 2025, the prevalence of ASD in 8-year-old children has risen to 1 in 31 (3.2%) [3]. The latest industry report in 2024 shows that the prevalence of ASD in China has approached 1% [4]. The rapid increase in ASD prevalence not only poses challenges for children with ASD but also imposes a heavy burden on families and society [5]. ASD is a multifactorial neurodevelopmental disorder, and its etiology is not fully understood [6]; however, studies suggest that it may be caused by the combined effects of genetic and environmental factors [7]. Currently, there is no effective method for early diagnosis and treatment of ASD [8], and the efficacy of rehabilitation training is limited [9], which represents a long-term and arduous challenge for children with ASD and their families [10]. Because the clinical symptoms of ASD are diverse [11], in addition to core symptoms [12], other comorbidities such as gastrointestinal (GI) complications and alterations in gut microbiota can be observed in some children with ASD [13], which not only affect the growth and development of children with ASD [14], but may also exacerbate the core symptoms of ASD, affecting the physical and mental health of children with ASD and the lives of their parents to varying degrees [15].

Recent high-impact studies have underscored the pivotal role of the gut–brain axis in ASD pathophysiology. For instance, a landmark study published in Cell demonstrated that transplantation of gut microbiota from ASD donors into germ-free mice induced ASD-like behaviors, suggesting that gut-brain connections contribute to the pathophysiology of ASD [16]. Another large-scale metagenomic study in Nature Microbiology identified specific microbial and metabolic signatures associated with ASD, highlighting the potential for microbiota-based stratification [17]. Animal experiments have shown that transplanting gut microbiota from healthy donors into ASD model mice improves their social behavior and restores intestinal barrier function [18], and clinical trials have also found that fecal microbiota transplantation can alleviate gastrointestinal symptoms and some core symptoms in children with ASD [19].

Despite growing evidence linking gut microbiota to ASD, most studies have focused on comparing ASD patients with typically developing controls, overlooking the substantial heterogeneity within the ASD population. Specifically, whether gut microbiota profiles differ across subgroups of children with varying symptom severity remains poorly characterized [20]. Addressing this gap is critical for identifying severity-associated microbial biomarkers and for developing precision intervention strategies. Furthermore, no studies have reported gut microbiota characteristics in children with ASD in the Xinjiang region of China, leaving a significant geographical gap in the understanding of ASD-associated microbiota in this understudied population.

This study, therefore, aims to investigate the gut microbiota characteristics of children with ASD stratified by symptom severity using 16 S rRNA sequencing. We hypothesize that children with severe ASD harbor a distinct gut bacterial community structure compared to those with mild-to-moderate ASD, characterized by reduced diversity, a reduction in beneficial bacteria, and enrichment of potential pathogens, and that these changes correlate with disease severity. By integrating clinical severity assessment with microbial profiling, this study seeks to establish a localized microbiota database and provide a foundation for more precise diagnosis and targeted therapeutic strategies for ASD.

The novelty of this study is threefold. First, it focuses on symptom severity stratification within the ASD population rather than the well-established ASD-healthy comparison, addressing a critical gap in understanding within-group heterogeneity. Second, to our knowledge, this is the first study to characterize the gut microbiota of children with ASD in the Xinjiang region of China, providing new data that fill a geographical gap in ASD microbiota research. Third, by considering both symptom severity and regional dietary and lifestyle factors, this study offers a more comprehensive and regionally relevant perspective compared to previous studies. These findings may contribute to the development of personalized microbiota-based interventions tailored to specific ASD subgroups and regional populations.

Methods

Study design and participants

In this study, a convenience sampling method was used. A total of 81 children with ASD aged 3–12 years were recruited from the Department of Child Health Care, The First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University, Urumqi, Xinjiang, China, between July 2024 and June 2025. The American DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for autism spectrum disorder in children were used as the gold standard for evaluating children with ASD. All children were diagnosed by two pediatricians with more than 3 years of work experience.

The calculated sample size was n=61 cases. Considering a 5% loss to follow-up, at least 64 cases were required. A total of 81 children with ASD were enrolled in this study; the sample size meets the requirements of the study.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Aged 3–12 years; (2) Diagnosed by a professional physician and meeting the diagnostic criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5); CARS score ≥ 30, ABC score ≥ 31; (3) No schizophrenia, simple mental retardation, brain tumor, cerebral palsy, metabolic diseases, or intellectual disability caused by other diseases; (4) No use of antibiotics, probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, or any drugs or foods with immunomodulatory effects within the past 3 months; no special diets (e.g., gluten-free, casein-free, ketogenic diet) during the same period; (5) Willingness to cooperate with relevant questionnaire surveys and developmental assessments.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Presence of other neurodevelopmental disorders; (2) Presence of organic brain diseases; (3) History of using immunosuppressants, probiotics, antibiotics, or other drugs within the past 3 months; (4) Presence of gastrointestinal diseases such as gastritis, appendicitis, colitis, or peptic ulcer; (5) Receipt of behavioral therapies (e.g., applied behavior analysis) or dietary interventions specifically targeting gut microbiota within the past 3 months.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (Approval No.: K202407-23). Written informed consent was obtained from all parents or guardians of the participating children. The reporting of this study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Clinical data collection

General information survey

A self-designed questionnaire was used, distributed uniformly through Wenjuanxing (a Chinese online survey platform). The main variables included: child’s gender, age, BMI, parents’ education level, and economic status. Members of the research team distributed QR codes for the questionnaire, used standardized instructions, and under the cooperation of hospital physicians, uniformly trained survey personnel guided parents to complete the questionnaire within the specified time.

BMI classification

BMI was classified according to the World Health Organization (WHO) Child Growth Standards [24] using age- and sex-specific BMI Z-scores: underweight (Z-score <-2), normal weight (-2 ≤ Z-score ≤ 1), and obese (Z-score > 1).

ASD symptom assessment

The Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS) [25] was used to assess the severity of ASD symptoms. A CARS total score of 30–36 with fewer than 5 items scoring below 3 was classified as mild-to-moderate ASD; a total score above 36 with at least 5 items scoring above 3 was classified as severe ASD. Based on this classification, subjects were divided into two groups: mild-to-moderate ASD and severe ASD (hereinafter referred to as the two groups), and differential analysis of gut microbiota characteristics was performed between the two groups of children with ASD.

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Sample collection

After obtaining informed consent from the parents or guardians, a single fecal sample was collected from each child with ASD. Participants were instructed to empty their bladder before defecation to prevent urine contamination, which could affect the accuracy of the results. Approximately 2 g of fresh feces was collected using a sterile, dedicated fecal collection container, and each sample was labeled with a unique identifier, participant name, and collection date. Samples were stored in a cooler at − 18 °C within 24 h of collection and transported to the research facility, where they were subsequently frozen at − 80 °C until further processing.

Key technology (16 S rRNA gene sequencing)

16 S rRNA gene amplification and sequencing were performed by Beijing Kangsheng Medical Laboratory Co., Ltd. The detailed procedures were as follows:

① Genomic DNA Extraction: Total genomic DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the QIAamp® DNA Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, employing a cetyltrimethylammonium bromide/sodium dodecyl sulfate (CTAB/SDS) method. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a OneDrop spectrophotometer and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The DNA was then diluted to 1 ng/µL with sterile water.

DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a OneDrop spectrophotometer and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The DNA was then diluted to 1 ng/µL with sterile water.

② PCR Amplification: PCR amplification was performed using 2.5 ng of diluted genomic DNA as a template. Bioinformatics analysis

Raw sequencing data were demultiplexed according to sample barcodes, and primer sequences were removed. Cutadapt (version 4.0) software was used for adapter trimming, and FLASH (version 1.2.11) was applied to merge paired-end reads based on overlapping regions. The merged sequences were imported into the QIIME 2 platform (version 2022.2). Quality filtering and feature construction were performed using the DADA2 plugin, including filtering of low-quality reads (Q-score < 20), dereplication, denoising, and chimera removal, resulting in an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) table and representative sequences. Taxonomic assignment was performed against the Silva 138.1 reference database using a naive Bayes classifier. Subsequent analyses were conducted using R software (version4.4.3) with the following packages: ① Alpha diversity indices (Observed species, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson) were calculated using the microeco package; ② Beta diversity was assessed using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray–Curtis distances, with significance tested using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) via the adonis function; ③ Differential abundance analysis was performed using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) with a threshold of LDA score ≥ 2.0; ④ Species composition analysis: The trans_abund function in the microeco package was used to calculate the mean relative abundance of the top 10 most abundant taxa at each taxonomic level (phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species) for each group. Statistical analysis

Data were entered using Excel, with a double-entry data-entry interface established. Logical data verification was performed after all entries were completed. SPSS 26.0 and R software (version 4.4.3) were used for analysis. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used for normality testing. Normally distributed measurement data were described as mean ± standard deviation (±s), and t-tests or ANOVA were used for intergroup comparisons. Non-normally distributed data were described using median and interquartile range, and rank-sum tests were used for intergroup comparisons. Count data were described using percentages, and χ² tests were used for comparisons.

For comparisons of microbial abundance at different taxonomic levels, the Mann-Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) was used. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the limited sample size, no adjustment for multiple comparisons (e.g., FDR or Benjamini-Hochberg) was applied. The results are presented as descriptive trends to show compositional patterns. For LEfSe analysis, taxa with an LDA score ≥ 2.0 and P < 0.05 were considered significantly different, with the LDA score serving as an effect size filter. No additional multiple comparison correction was applied to the LEfSe results, consistent with standard practice.

Alpha diversity

Alpha diversity analysis revealed that compared with the mild-to-moderate ASD group, the Shannon index was significantly lower in the severe ASD group [4.04 (3.74–4.33) vs. 3.82 (3.40–4.23); -0.22 (-0.44, -0.01); Z = -1.987, P = 0.047], indicating reduced community diversity in children with severe ASD. The Simpson index was also significantly lower in the severe ASD group [0.95 (0.93–0.96) vs. 0.92 (0.90–0.96); -0.03 (-0.05, -0.01); Z = -2.503, P = 0.012], suggesting decreased community evenness.

No significant differences were observed for Observed species or the Chao1 index [583 (414–788) vs. 555 (320–697); -28 (-98, 42); Z = -1.177, P = 0.236], although a trend toward reduced species richness was noted in the severe ASD group (Fig. 1).

Beta diversity

Beta diversity was assessed to evaluate differences in gut microbial community structure between the mild-to-moderate and severe ASD groups. Bray-Curtis distance was used as the dissimilarity metric. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) were performed to visualize the community structure.

The results showed that the gut microbiota structure of the severe ASD group was significantly separated from that of the mild-to-moderate ASD group, regardless of the method used. In the NMDS analysis, the severe group was located in the positive MDS1 region, while in the PCoA analysis, it was located in the negative PCO1 region. These findings consistently suggest that gut microbial community composition shifts toward a distinct direction with increasing symptom severity.

PERMANOVA results revealed a statistically significant difference in microbial community structure between the two groups (R² = 0.048, P = 0.003). The stress value for the NMDS analysis was 0.211, indicating acceptable representation of the distance matrix (Fig. 2).

The differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A).

At the family level, compared with the mild-to-moderate ASD group, the severe ASD group showed a decreasing trend in Selenomonadaceae (2.64%→0.80%), Veillonellaceae (3.99%→2.70%), and Coriobacteriaceae (3.90%→2.36%); and increasing trends in Bifidobacteriaceae (8.90%→11.75%) and Enterobacteriaceae (4.64%→13.08%). The differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3C).

At the order level, compared with the mild-to-moderate ASD group, the severe ASD group showed a decreasing trend in Veillonellales-Selenomonadales (6.64%→3.50%), an increasing trend in Bifidobacteriales (8.90%→11.75%), an increasing trend in Enterobacterales (5.08%→13.63%), and a decreasing trend in Coriobacteriales (4.65%→2.73%). The differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3D).

At the phylum level, compared with the mild-to-moderate ASD group, the severe ASD group showed a decreasing trend in Firmicutes (56.92%→47.46%) and an increasing trend in Proteobacteria (5.92%→14.46%). The differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3E).

At the species level, compared with the mild-to-moderate ASD group, the severe ASD group showed a decreasing trend in Streptococcus salivarius (0.97%→0.56%) and an increasing trend in Bacteroides uniformis (1.15%→1.78%). However, these differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3F).

LEfSe analysis

LEfSe analysis identified 11 differentially abundant bacterial taxa between the two groups (LDA ≥ 2.0, P < 0.05). Among these, 10 taxa were enriched in the mild-to-moderate ASD group, including Firmicutes (phylum, LDA = 4.69, P = 0.003), Coriobacteriia (class, LDA = 3.95, P = 0.026), Coriobacteriales (order, LDA = 3.95, P = 0.026), Bacteroides ovatus (species, LDA = 3.10, P = 0.048), Sutterella (genus, LDA = 2.65, P = 0.030), and several uncultured taxa. Only Fusobacteriaceae (family, LDA = 3.40, P = 0.043) was enriched in the severe ASD group (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Gut microbiota diversity

Microbiota diversity indices are comprehensive analysis indices of the types and quantities of gut microbiota, used to measure the richness and evenness of gut microbiota, reflecting the complexity and stability of the intestinal microecosystem [26].

Global studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding alpha-diversity in ASD. While some studies observed reduced microbial diversity in children with ASD compared to neurotypical controls [27], others found no significant differences [28]. These discrepancies may be attributed to variations in geographic regions, sample collection methods, and sequencing technologies across studies. In this study, α and β diversity indices showed certain differences between the two groups of children with ASD, indicating that gut microbiota diversity in children with ASD is generally affected, with severe ASD children showing lower gut microbiota diversity than mild-to-moderate ASD children, suggesting poorer complexity and stability of their gut microbiota. This is consistent with the research results of Zhao Yingxin [29], Morton [30], and others. The observation of reduced diversity with increasing symptom severity aligns with findings from Kang et al. (2017), who reported that Microbiota Transfer Therapy increased bacterial diversity and improved both GI and ASD symptoms in children with ASD [31, 32]. Decreased microbiota diversity may disrupt intestinal barrier function, leading to neuroimmune abnormalities and subsequently affecting ASD symptoms [33]. However, given the cross-sectional design of this study, we cannot establish causal relationships between reduced diversity and ASD severity, and these findings require validation in longitudinal studies.

Specific microbiota changes

Based on different physiological functions in the intestine, bacteria are classified into three categories: probiotics, neutral bacteria, and pathogenic bacteria [34]. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have shown significant differences in gut microbiota between children with ASD and healthy children, specifically a decrease in beneficial bacteria and an increase in harmful bacteria. This difference may be closely related to the pathophysiological mechanisms and symptom manifestations of ASD [35]. However, alterations in gut microbiota have shown high heterogeneity across different studies [28]. In this study, compared with the mild-to-moderate ASD group, multiple beneficial bacterial groups showed a declining trend in the severe ASD group. At the phylum level, Firmicutes; at the class level, Clostridia; and at the genus level, Faecalibacterium and Dialister all showed decreasing trends. It is important to note that findings regarding Firmicutes in ASD have been inconsistent across global studies. While some studies reported decreased Firmicutes in ASD patients [36, 37], others observed increased abundance [38]. These discrepancies may reflect methodological variations, geographic differences, or symptom characteristics across study populations.

Mechanistically, Firmicutes and Clostridia contain numerous butyrate-producing bacteria, and butyrate is a key metabolite for maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and regulating immune responses [39, 40]. This study observed that these beneficial bacterial groups decreased with increasing ASD severity, suggesting that the reduction of beneficial bacteria in the intestines of children with ASD may lead to immune dysregulation and neuroinflammation, thereby exacerbating ASD symptoms [41], though this remains a hypothesis requiring further investigation.

Notably, Bifidobacteriaceae and Bifidobacterium showed an increasing trend in this study, which is inconsistent with some previous studies [42]. This inconsistency reflects broader discrepancies in the literature regarding Bifidobacterium abundance in ASD. Several previous studies have reported conflicting findings: some studies found decreased Bifidobacterium in ASD patients [43–45], while others increased levels [46].

These conflicting results may be attributed to geographic variations, dietary habits, age differences, methodological variations in DNA extraction and sequencing protocols, or the significant heterogeneity of ASD itself. Bifidobacterium is generally considered a probiotic, but its role in ASD may be more complex and requires further research to elucidate.

Enrichment of potentially pathogenic bacteria

In this study, compared with the mild-to-moderate ASD group, multiple potentially pathogenic bacterial groups showed a significant enrichment trend in the severe ASD group. At the phylum level, Proteobacteria; at the class level, Gammaproteobacteria and Fusobacteriia were significantly enriched in the severe ASD group; at the family level, Enterobacteriaceae; and at the genus level, Klebsiella increased 2.8-fold. The enrichment of Proteobacteria observed in this study aligns with findings from several global studies. For example, a systematic review reported that Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Sutterella were significantly enriched in ASD patients compared to healthy controls [47]. Similarly, Julio Plaza-Díaz et al. noted that Proteobacteria was among the most consistently reported phylum-level alterations in ASD [48]. Enterobacteriaceae contains numerous Gram-negative pathogenic bacteria, and their overgrowth is associated with various intestinal infections and inflammatory diseases [49, 50]. Klebsiella is an important opportunistic pathogen in Enterobacteriaceae that can produce various virulence factors, induce inflammatory responses, and may affect central nervous system function by producing neurotoxins or inflammatory mediators [33]. The finding of a 2.8-fold increase in Klebsiella in this study is particularly noteworthy, suggesting that this genus may be an important biomarker for ASD severity. The enrichment of Fusobacteriia (particularly Fusobacteriaceae) in this study is another important finding. Studies [51] have also shown that Fusobacteriota is significantly enriched in patients with ASD and is closely related to intestinal inflammation and constipation symptoms.

Changes in neutral bacteria

This study also observed that multiple neutral bacterial groups showed a declining trend in the severe ASD group compared to the mild-to-moderate ASD group. At the class level, Bacilli, Coriobacteriia, and Negativicutes showed decreasing trends; at the family level, both Veillonellaceae and Coriobacteriaceae showed decreasing trends; and at the genus level, Collinsella showed a decreasing trend. Although the changes in these bacterial groups do not have as clear pathological significance as beneficial and pathogenic bacteria, they may reflect the disruption of the overall balance of the intestinal microecosystem. Coriobacteriaceae and Collinsella are related to bile acid metabolism and lipid metabolism, and their changes may affect host metabolic homeostasis [52]. Veillonellaceae plays an important role in carbohydrate fermentation and lactic acid metabolism, and its decrease may affect the pH value of the intestinal environment and microbial interactions [53].

Biomarkers identified by LEfSe analysis

This study identified a total of 11 differentially abundant taxa related to ASD disease severity through LEfSe analysis (LDA ≥ 2.0, P < 0.05). Notably, 10 taxa were significantly enriched in the mild-to-moderate ASD group, while only Fusobacteriaceae (family, LDA = 3.40, P = 0.043) was enriched in the severe ASD group.

In the mild-to-moderate ASD group, Firmicutes represented the strongest biomarker, followed by Coriobacteriia, and Coriobacteriales. The presence of multiple Coriobacteriaceae-related taxa (Coriobacteriia, Coriobacteriales) as biomarkers for mild-to-moderate ASD is consistent with our observation of declining trends for these taxa in severe ASD, suggesting that maintenance of these neutral/beneficial bacteria may characterize less severe disease states. The enrichment of Firmicutes in mild-to-moderate ASD contrasts with its reported depletion in severe ASD in our diversity analysis, suggesting complex, non-linear relationships between this phylum and ASD severity. This finding aligns with the inconsistent reports of Firmicutes in global ASD literature: While some studies reported decreased Firmicutes in ASD patients [36], others observed increased abundance [37, 38]. Bacteroides ovatus and Sutterella were also significantly enriched in the mild-to-moderate ASD group. This finding regarding Sutterella partially aligns with some previous studies reporting increased abundance in ASD [54], though others found no significant differences [55]. However, it contrasts with our initial observation of Sutterella enrichment in severe ASD in the earlier draft, highlighting the importance of rigorous statistical validation through LEfSe analysis. Sutterella may participate in the pathological process of ASD by affecting intestinal barrier function or immune regulation [56]. Bacteroides ovatus, as a member of the Bacteroides genus, is generally considered a commensal bacterium, but its overgrowth may produce excessive propionic acid and other short-chain fatty acids. Propionic acid has been confirmed to induce ASD-like behaviors in animal models [57], and its metabolic abnormalities may be one of the important mechanisms in the pathogenesis of ASD. The microbiota-gut-brain axis provides a plausible mechanism for this association: gut bacteria produce metabolites that can cross the blood-brain barrier and influence neurodevelopment and behavior [58]. However, it is important to emphasize that the current evidence is largely correlational, and the specific causal mechanisms linking particular bacterial taxa or metabolites to ASD symptoms remain to be established through longitudinal and interventional studies.

The sole biomarker for severe ASD was Fusobacteriaceae, representing the strongest discriminator for this group. This finding is consistent with our observation of significant Fusobacteriia enrichment in severe ASD and aligns with previous studies showing that Fusobacteriota is significantly enriched in patients with ASD and is closely related to intestinal inflammation and constipation symptoms [51]. The dominance of Fusobacteriaceae as the only severe ASD biomarker, contrasted with the diverse biomarker profile of mild-to-moderate ASD, suggests that severe ASD may be characterized by the selective expansion of specific potentially pathogenic taxa rather than generalized dysbiosis.

Notably, this study also found significant differences between groups in several uncultured or metagenome-defined species, suggesting that there are still numerous unknown intestinal microorganisms that may participate in the pathological process of ASD, requiring in-depth exploration using more advanced metagenomic sequencing technologies.

Clinical implications and microbiota-targeted therapies

The findings of this study have potential implications for microbiota-targeted interventions, though causal relationships cannot be established due to the cross-sectional design and lack of healthy controls. Several intervention strategies warrant investigation in future randomized controlled trials:

Probiotic supplementation: Given the reduction of beneficial butyrate-producing bacteria (Faecalibacterium, Clostridia) in severe ASD, probiotic strains that produce short-chain fatty acids (e.g., Faecalibacterium, Clostridium) may be candidates for intervention [59]. However, strain selection should be guided by functional capacity rather than taxonomic identity alone, and efficacy must be validated in RCTs.

Dietary modification: The observed increase in Bifidobacterium in severe ASD, while unexpected, highlights the complex role of diet in shaping gut microbiota. Dietary interventions such as gluten-free/casein-free diets have shown mixed results in ASD studies [61], emphasizing the need for personalized approaches based on individual microbiota profiles.

It is important to emphasize that these implications are purely speculative based on correlational findings from a cross-sectional study without healthy controls. The heterogeneity of ASD and individual differences in gut microbiota composition suggest that personalized approaches may be necessary, but rigorous randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up are essential to establish the efficacy and safety of any microbiota-targeted interventions in ASD.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. (1) Small sample size and single-center design: The study was conducted with a relatively small sample size (n = 81) at a single hospital, which may introduce regional sampling bias and limit generalizability to ASD populations in other geographic regions. (2) Lack of healthy control group: This design prioritized within-ASD comparisons while avoiding potential confounding variables (e.g., diet, childcare arrangements) from healthy children. Consequently, we cannot determine whether the observed microbial differences are severity-specific or reflect general ASD-related dysbiosis. The identified microbial signatures should therefore be interpreted as severity-associated variations within ASD, not as confirmed ASD-specific pathogenic markers. (3) Cross-sectional design: We could not establish causal relationships between gut microbiota and ASD symptoms. (4) Lack of fungal and viral evaluation: Only bacterial communities were analyzed. (5) No multiple comparison correction: Findings are exploratory and require validation. Future multi-center studies with larger sample sizes, healthy controls, comprehensive dietary assessments, and multi-kingdom analyses are needed to validate our findings.

Future directions

Several directions should be pursued in future research. First, multi-omics approaches (metagenomics and metabolomics) are needed to elucidate functional mechanisms. Second, longitudinal cohort studies are essential to establish causal relationships. Third, randomized controlled trials of microbiota-targeted interventions are warranted. Fourth, integration with clinical and behavioral data is necessary to control for confounders. Finally, multi-center studies are needed to validate our findings and address regional sampling bias.

Conclusion

This study found that gut microbiota dysbiosis in children with ASD is closely related to symptoms, characterized by reduced microbiota diversity, decreased beneficial bacteria, and increased harmful bacteria. However, it is important to note that this study only evaluated bacterial communities and did not assess the entire microbiota (including fungi and viruses). Gut microbiota dysbiosis is relatively less severe in mild-to-moderate ASD children compared to severe ASD. While the observed associations between bacterial composition and ASD severity are intriguing, we acknowledge that our cross-sectional design does not support causal inferences. Therefore, we refrain from concluding that “the imbalance between beneficial and harmful bacteria may play an important role in the pathophysiological mechanisms of ASD,” as our results demonstrate association rather than causation. Regulating the balance between beneficial and harmful bacteria may improve behavioral symptoms in children with ASD. However, due to the heterogeneity of ASD, future research and treatment strategies need to pay more attention to personalized interventions to achieve better clinical outcomes.
