Regulation of osteoimmune microenvironment and endogenous bone regeneration by versatile injectable nanocomposite hydrogel
Authors: Haoran Zhang, Xinwei Wang, Bingxu Li, Yiquan Zheng, Jianguo Zhang, Shengru Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIrregular-shaped bone defects are commonly encountered in clinical practice, and various biomaterials have been developed to address these issues. Injectable in situ forming hydrogels not only effectively fill irregular-shaped defects but also provide a suitable growth environment for host cells. In this study, we developed an injectable nanocomposite hydrogel, which includes chitosan oligosaccharides (COS), DL-α-Glycerol phosphate magnesium salt hydrate (Mg-GP), 4-arm PEG-Succinimidyl Carbonate (4-arm-PEG-SC), and mesoporous polydopamine (MPDA) nanoparticles loaded with bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2). The injectable hydrogel demonstrates excellent physical properties, including injectability, self-healing, photothermal property, appropriate swelling (182%) and degradation rates (59%), sufficient mechanical strength (280 kPa) and adhesion (10 kPa). Additionally, the injectable hydrogel exhibits multiple biological effects, including controlled release of BMP-2 (59.1%) and magnesium ions (55.1%), antioxidant and antibacterial properties, promotion of cell proliferation and migration, regulation of immune microenvironment, and stimulation of osteogenesis and angiogenesis. In summary, both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrate that the injectable nanocomposite hydrogel may represent a promising minimally invasive solution for endogenous bone regeneration.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04688-5.Keywords: Injectable hydrogel, Bone regeneration, Bone defect, Osteoimmune microenvironment

Introduction

In recent years, injectable in situ forming hydrogels have been extensively studied in the field of bone tissue engineering and regenerative medicine [1, 2]. These hydrogels not only effectively fill irregularly shaped damage sites but also provide the suitable growth environment for cells. Additionally, injectable in situ forming hydrogels are particularly well-suited for minimally invasive procedures and confined spaces, and hydrogels can be delivered via minimally invasive endoscopy or injection.

Injectable hydrogels have progressed from experimental biomaterials to clinically used healthcare products, particularly where minimally invasive delivery, local retention, mechanical support, or sustained therapeutic release are required. A review of hydrogels in clinical use reported that injectable hydrogels had already entered both approved-product and clinical-trial pipelines, with examples spanning ophthalmology, orthopaedics, oncology-supportive care, urology, and regenerative medicine [3]. Clinically marketed examples include hyaluronic acid or hylan viscosupplements for knee osteoarthritis, such as Synvisc/Synvisc-One, which are indicated for knee osteoarthritis pain in patients who have not responded adequately to conservative non-pharmacological therapy and simple analgesics. Other examples include SpaceOAR, a PEG-based absorbable hydrogel spacer that is injected between the prostate and anterior rectal wall to reduce rectal radiation exposure during prostate radiotherapy, and Bulkamid, a non-resorbable injectable polyacrylamide hydrogel used as a urethral bulking agent for stress urinary incontinence [3].

To date, numerous natural polymers, such as gelatin, collagen, and hyaluronic acid, have been used to create injectable hydrogels, owing to their excellent biocompatibility and ability to mimic the microstructure of the extracellular matrix. Among these polymers, chitosan is commonly used, and injectable hydrogels based on chitosan and glycerophosphate have been commercialized for wound healing applications [4]. Chitosan is derived from the deacetylation of chitin, the main component of the exoskeleton of crustaceans. Previous literature shows that chitosan has the inhibitory effects on various bacteria [5]. However, chitosan/glycerophosphate-based hydrogels exhibit poor mechanical properties, making them inadequate for the mechanical demands required for bone regeneration.

Some strategies have been proposed to optimize the mechanical properties, such as incorporating covalent cross-linking. Aldehydes (formaldehyde, glutaraldehyde, and glyoxal) have been used as cross-linking agents, reacting with the amino groups on chitosan via Schiff-base reactions to form covalent bonds. However, the potential toxicity and poor biocompatibility of aldehydes raise concerns [6]. Another commonly used cross-linking agent, genipin, offers good biocompatibility, but high-cost limits large-scale production [7]. Additionally, these chitosan hydrogels have relatively long curing times (over 30 min), which is unsuitable for surgical applications. Prolonged curing times can extend the duration of surgery and complicate surgical procedures. Therefore, alternative cross-linking agents are needed. The active functional groups of polyethylene glycol (PEG) can assemble polymer chains into an interwoven network, thereby enhancing the cross-linking density and mechanical properties of the hydrogels [8]. Additionally, small molecule drugs and bioactive factors can also be introduced into the hydrogel through 4-armed PEG [9]. Compared with natural materials, PEG also has the advantage that PEG does not require donor selection and therefore has a lower risk of disease transmission. More importantly, the properties of synthetic materials can be easily adjusted according to actual needs, and the preparation process is more standardized.

Bone morphogenetic proteins are the largest subclass of the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) superfamily, comprising 18 types that participate in various physiological processes. Among them, bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2) has been shown to possess strong osteoinductive potentials and pro-angiogenic capabilities [10, 11], facilitating the differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) into osteoblasts, thereby accelerating bone matrix deposition and repair. It is noteworthy, however, that excessively high local concentrations of BMP-2 may lead to complications such as inflammatory reactions, hematoma, infection, and ectopic bone formation [12]. Due to limited penetrating ability, most injectable hydrogels perform poorly in terms of adsorption and sustained release of small molecule drugs or bioactive factors [13]. Polydopamine (PDA) is a polymer with excellent biocompatibility and multiple advantageous properties, including simple preparation, antioxidant activity, antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects, regulation of immune microenvironment, strong adhesion, and photothermal property [14–16]. Additionally, PDA exhibits strong adsorption capabilities for small molecule drugs and bioactive factors [17]. Porous microcarriers based on PDA can be organically integrated with hydrogel systems, offering potential for the sustained release of growth factors in bone regeneration. PDA degrades into monomers without significant pharmacological effects of dopamine, making it suitable for in vivo applications.

The ideal solution is to use a simple procedure to integrate multiple functionalities into a single biomaterial. In this study, we developed a novel injectable in situ forming hydrogel by combining non-covalent and covalent interactions to meet the requirements for mechanical performance, biocompatibility, injectability, and bone regeneration capability. Firstly, we selected chitosan oligosaccharides (COS) instead of chitosan, because COS offers better water solubility and free amino groups. Next, we introduced DL-α-Glycerol phosphate magnesium salt hydrate (Mg-GP), which not only provides the non-covalent bonds but also enhances osteogenic function through magnesium ion (Mg2+). Mg2+ has been shown to play a significant role in promoting bone regeneration as a therapeutic metal ion [18]. Subsequently, we incorporated 4-arm PEG-Succinimidyl Carbonate (4-arm-PEG-SC) to form amide bonds with COS, thereby improving the mechanical strength of the hydrogel. Finally, we incorporated mesoporous polydopamine (MPDA) nanoparticles loaded with BMP-2 into the hydrogel. This not only imparts the inherent immunomodulatory and photothermal properties of PDA to the hydrogel but also ensures the controlled release of BMP-2 at effective concentrations. Our study demonstrates that this novel smart delivery system addresses multiple requirements for bone regeneration, providing a simple yet effective strategy for the development of advanced bone-associated biomaterials (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Materials

In this study, Pluronic F127 along with 1,3,5-Trimethylbenzene (TMB) were procured from Beijing InnoChem Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), while dopamine hydrochloride was supplied by Aladdin BioChem Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). For the polymer and inorganic components, COS, 4-arm-PEG-SC, and Mg-GP were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Cellular models, specifically BMSCs and RAW264.7, were obtained through ScienCell Research Laboratories (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Reagents essential for cell culture and molecular analysis, including the total RNA extraction kit, Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), and fetal bovine serum (FBS), were sourced from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Madison, USA). Furthermore, primary antibodies targeting osteopontin (OPN), osteocalcin (OCN), runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), collagen I (COL I), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) were provided by Proteintech (Wuhan, China). Standardized staining reagents, specifically Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and Alizarin Red S solutions, were purchased from Beyotime Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation and characterization of MPDA

MPDA nanoparticles were synthesized via an emulsion-induced interface polymerization strategy. The underlying mechanism of PDA formation replicates the melanin biosynthesis observed in biological systems, fundamentally involving a covalent oxidative polymerization pathway. Initially, Pluronic F127 (1 g) and dopamine hydrochloride (0.5 g) were integrated into a binary solvent system (ethanol/water, 1:1, v/v, 100 mL). This mixture was subjected to continuous stirring at ambient temperature until a completely homogeneous and transparent phase was achieved. Subsequently, 2 mL of TMB was introduced into the system and agitated for 30 min, resulting in the formation of a stabilized milky white emulsion. To initiate the polymerization, 5 mL of ammonia was added dropwise; the reaction proceeded under stirring for 2.5 h, during which the solution transitioned to a characteristic deep black color. The deionized water, ammonia, and ethanol used were all purchased from Aladdin BioChem Technology Co., Ltd.

To isolate the nanoparticles, the resulting suspension underwent centrifugation at 13,500 rpm for 15 min (utilizing a 3-18R centrifuge, Shanghai Lu Xiangyi Instrument Co., Ltd.), effectively eliminating the template, residual dopamine, and medium. The collected precipitates were purified through multiple washing cycles with a water/ethanol mixture (1:1, v/v) and were eventually redispersed in deionized water for room-temperature storage. For the preparation of BMP-2 loaded mesoporous polydopamine (BMPDA), BMP-2 was first dissolved in ultrapure water, followed by the addition of MPDA (1 mL, 1 mg/mL). This complex was stirred for a 12-hour duration at room temperature to ensure adequate adsorption, followed by a final centrifugation step (13500 rpm, 10 min) to harvest the BMPDA nanoparticles.

To investigate the molecular interactions and successful binding between the BMP-2 protein and the MPDA framework, Fourier Transform Infrared spectrometry (FTIR, Nicolet iS10, Thermo Fisher, USA) was employed. The hydrodynamic diameters and zeta potentials of both MPDA and BMPDA were quantified using a Zetasizer Nano ZS-90 (Malvern, UK). Specifically, the particle size distribution was analyzed via dynamic light scattering (DLS) at a fixed scattering angle of 90°, while surface charge (zeta potential) was determined through laser Doppler microelectrophoresis.

The internal architecture and topography of the nanostructures were further elucidated. Following a negative staining procedure with 2% sodium phosphotungstate, the morphological features of both MPDA and BMPDA were visualized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN, FEI, USA).

The efficiency of protein sequestration was rigorously assessed. BMPDA was thoroughly homogenized in ultrapure water via ultrasonication, followed by centrifugation (13500 rpm, 10 min) to isolate the free BMP-2. The concentration of the growth factor in the supernatant was quantified using specialized ELISA kits (CSB-E04508r, Cusabio Technology Co., Ltd.). The loading efficiency and encapsulation efficiency were calculated based on the following mathematical models: loading efficiency (%) = WBMP−2/(WBMP−2+Wnanoparticles)×100%; encapsulation efficiency (%) = WBMP−2 in nanoparticles/WBMP−2 added×100%. WBMP−2 represents the mass of BMP-2, Wnanoparticles represents the mass of the blank nanoparticles used for BMP-2 loading, WBMP−2 in nanoparticles represents the mass of BMP-2 loaded into the nanoparticles, WBMP−2 added represents the initial mass of BMP-2 added during the loading process.

Controllable release of BMP-2 from BMPDA

The release kinetics of the therapeutic growth factor from the BMPDA nanocarriers were systematically evaluated under simulated physiological conditions. Specifically, BMPDA was submerged in 50 mL of Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS, 0.01 M, pH 7.4) contained within a sterilized Erlenmeyer flask. To simulate the dynamic biological environment, the assembly was transferred to an orbital shaker maintained at a constant physiological temperature of 37 °C, with a continuous agitation rate of 100 rpm for a total duration of 24 h.

At pre-established chronological intervals, 2 mL aliquots of the supernatant were meticulously harvested for analysis. To ensure a constant sink volume, each withdrawn sample was immediately reconstituted with an equivalent volume of fresh, pre-warmed PBS. To eliminate the potential interference of suspended particulate matter, the collected supernatant samples were subjected to high-speed centrifugation (13500 rpm for 10 min). The concentration of liberated BMP-2 within the clarified supernatant was subsequently quantified using ELISA kits (CSB-E04508r, Cusabio Technology Co., Ltd.), strictly adhering to the protocols provided by the manufacturer. Based on these quantitative measurements, the cumulative release profile was generated to characterize the delivery efficiency of the system.

Fabrication and characterization of hydrogels

To develop the injectable, in situ cross-linking hydrogel system, separate precursor solutions were prepared: COS (3% w/v) and 4-arm-PEG-SC (10% w/v) were individually reconstituted in PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4), while the Mg-GP component was dissolved at a concentration of 200 mg/mL in a 0.1 M HCl acidic medium. The baseline COS/PEG/Mg-GP hydrogel (CPMH) was synthesized by integrating COS, 4-arm-PEG-SC, and Mg-GP in a strictly defined volumetric ratio of 8:1:1 at a physiological temperature of 37 °C.

For the fabrication of bioactive composite scaffolds, namely BMP-2@CPMH and BMPDA@CPMH, the therapeutic agents (BMP-2 or BMPDA nanoparticles) were initially homogenized within the 4-arm-PEG-SC phase. Subsequently, the COS solution, the modified 4-arm-PEG-SC mixture, and the Mg-GP stabilizer were gently combined using the same 8:1:1 volume ratio at 37 °C to facilitate gelation and encapsulate the bioactive cargos.

The chemical fingerprints and cross-linking interactions of the resultant hydrogels were elucidated via FTIR (Nicolet iS10, Thermo Fisher, Madison, USA). Lyophilized hydrogel specimens (approximately 10 mg) were positioned between two transparent glass slides and compressed into thin, uniform tablets suitable for transmission analysis. The infrared absorption spectra were recorded within a controlled anhydrous environment across a wavenumber range of 4000 to 500 cm− 1, utilizing a high-precision spectral resolution of 4 cm− 1 to identify characteristic functional groups.

To elucidate the internal micro-architecture and porosity of the hydrogel scaffolds, the specimens were partitioned into uniform square sections. These gel blocks underwent cryogenic treatment by immersion in liquid nitrogen for a 30-minute duration, followed by immediate lyophilization for 24 h to preserve their native structural integrity. The resulting anhydrous samples were securely attached to conductive copper stubs and subjected to a gold sputter-coating process for 45 s to enhance surface conductivity. Subsequently, the topographic features and cross-sectional morphologies were visualized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Sigma 500, Zeiss, Jena, Germany) operating at an acceleration voltage of 3 kV.

The mechanical and flow properties of the hydrogel systems were rigorously analyzed using a Haake Mars 60 rheometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Initially, the prepolymer solutions were cast into standardized molds to produce cylindrical hydrogel specimens with a diameter of 20 mm and a thickness of 2 mm, which were conditioned at 4 ℃. During testing, the peripheral edges of the samples were sealed with silicone oil to mitigate potential evaporative moisture loss.

To investigate the strain-dependent behavior of the storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G’’), dynamic oscillation measurements were executed at a consistent frequency of 1 Hz and a controlled temperature of 25 ℃ across a predefined strain sweep. Furthermore, the shear-thinning characteristics were assessed by recording the viscosity as a function of the shear rate (ranging from 1 to 1000 s− 1), while maintaining a constant temperature (25 ℃) and strain (1%). Finally, the intrinsic self-healing capabilities were quantified through cyclic step-strain tests, alternating between low (1%) and high (500%) strain amplitudes at a frequency of 1 Hz and a temperature of 25 ℃.

The absorptive capacity of the hydrogel scaffolds was systematically quantified. Initially, the lyophilized hydrogel specimens were accurately weighed to establish their dry baseline mass (W0). Subsequently, the samples were submerged in PBS buffer (0.01 M, pH 7.4) to initiate the hydration process. At designated chronological intervals, the hydrogels were retrieved from the aqueous medium, and any interstitial surface moisture was carefully eliminated using high-absorption filter paper. The swollen mass (Wt) was recorded periodically until the matrix reached a state of swelling equilibrium. The swelling ratio was then determined using the following expression: Swelling ratio (%) = Wt/W0 × 100%.

The biodegradation kinetics were assessed under simulated physiological conditions. The initial mass of the freeze-dried hydrogel matrix was recorded as W0. To mimic the in vivo metabolic environment, the scaffolds were immersed in a PBS solution (0.01 M, pH 7.4) supplemented with 1 U/mL lysozyme. To ensure the sustained catalytic activity of the enzyme, the degradation medium was replenished with freshly prepared lysozyme solution every 72 h. At predetermined time points, the remaining hydrogel fragments were harvested from the incubation medium, subjected to a secondary lyophilization process, and weighed to obtain the residual mass (Wt). The degradation rate, representing the cumulative mass loss, was calculated according to the following formula: Degradation rate (%) = (W0-Wt)/W0 × 100%.

Controllable release of Mg2+ and BMP-2 from hydrogels

To evaluate the spatiotemporal release profiles of divalent magnesium ions and growth factors, the hydrogel scaffolds were submerged in PBS buffer (0.01 M, pH 7.4) and incubated within a thermostatic environment maintained at 37 ℃. At each predefined chronological interval, 1 mL aliquots of the incubation medium were harvested and subsequently replenished with an identical volume of fresh, pre-warmed buffer to maintain sink conditions.

For the quantification of magnesium (Mg2+) concentration, a series of standardized Mg2+ solutions were prepared to establish linear calibration curves, ensuring the accuracy of the concentration measurements across the experimental range. The actual concentration of the released ions within the samples was determined using specialized colorimetric assay kits (BC8335, Cusabio Technology Co., Ltd.), strictly following the manufacturer analytical protocols.

In a parallel experimental design, the liberation of BMP-2 from the hydrogel matrix was quantified. The concentration of the growth factor in the sampled buffer was measured using high-sensitivity ELISA kits (CSB-E04508r, Cusabio Technology Co., Ltd.). The resulting data were then utilized to construct cumulative release profiles, providing a comprehensive overview of the protein delivery kinetics from the composite system.

Photothermal performance

To investigate the photo-responsiveness of the composite hydrogels, their thermal behavior under near-infrared (NIR) laser irradiation was systematically evaluated. The real-time temperature fluctuations and thermal distribution were recorded using a high-resolution infrared thermographic camera (FLIR E50, FLIR Systems Inc., Wilsonville, OR, USA).

For the experimental procedure, the hydrogel specimens were localized within standardized tubes and subjected to continuous monochromatic NIR irradiation at a wavelength of 808 nm, with a calibrated power density of 2 W/cm2. During a 5-minute exposure period, the thermal flux and cumulative temperature elevation were continuously monitored. This enabled the construction of photothermal heating curves, which characterize the efficiency of the hydrogel matrix in converting light energy into localized hyperthermia.

Antibacterial and antioxidant properties

The bactericidal potential of the hydrogels was rigorously tested against representative Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains, specifically Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli. Bacterial suspensions were standardized to a concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/mL, and 100 uL aliquots were thoroughly integrated with the respective hydrogel formulations. The experimental cohorts were categorized into: PBS control, BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel, and BMPDA@CPMH + NIR laser irradiation.

For the photothermal treatment group, the hydrogel-bacteria complex was subjected to 808 nm NIR laser exposure at a power density of 2 W/cm2 for a duration of 5 min to induce localized thermal ablation. Following the treatment, the bacterial suspensions were serially diluted and uniformly inoculated onto Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates. After an incubation period of 24 h at 37 °C, the viability of the microorganisms was quantified by manual colony-forming unit (CFU) counting, and the comparative antibacterial efficiency was analyzed.

The antioxidant performance of the hydrogel systems was determined utilizing a DPPH free radical scavenging assay (BC4755, Solarbio Science and Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). In strict accordance with the manufacturer established protocol, the hydrogel extracts and corresponding working reagents were prepared.

The OD of the reaction mixtures was quantified at a characteristic wavelength of 517 nm using a high-precision UV-Vis spectrophotometer. The concentration of the residual DPPH radicals was then interpolated from a linear standard curve. This allowed for the calculation of the scavenging efficiency, providing a quantitative measure of the hydrogel’s ability to mitigate oxidative stress within the bone microenvironment. DPPH scavenging efficiency (%) = [1 − (As − Ab) / Ac] × 100%. As is the absorbance of the reaction mixture containing DPPH solution and hydrogel extract, Ab is the absorbance of the hydrogel extract without DPPH solution, and Ac is the absorbance of the control group containing DPPH solution without hydrogel extract.

Mechanical and adhesive strength

The compressive mechanical properties of the hydrogel scaffolds were rigorously evaluated using a universal biomaterial testing system (WDW-10, Jinan Chuanbai Instrument Co., Ltd., Shandong, China). For this assessment, hydrogel specimens were fabricated into standardized blocks with dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 3 mm. These samples were subjected to uniaxial compression at a constant crosshead speed of 1 mm/min. The resultant stress-strain profiles were recorded in real-time, and the compressive modulus was calculated from the linear region of the stress-strain curve to characterize the structural stiffness of the matrix.

The interfacial adhesive capacity of the hydrogel formulations was quantified via a lap-shear test, adhering to the ASTM F2255-05 standardized protocol. To simulate a bonding substrate, two dry glass slides were pre-functionalized with a 20% (w/v) gelatin coating and allowed to undergo ambient air-drying. Subsequently, a 500 uL aliquot of the hydrogel was introduced into the defined overlapping region (3 cm × 1 cm) of the slides. The assembly was maintained under controlled humid conditions for a 60-minute incubation period to facilitate stable bond formation.

Mechanical separation was executed using the universal testing machine in tensile mode at a displacement rate of 2 mm/min until the adhesive failure occurred. Furthermore, to validate the bio-adhesive potential of the system, freshly harvested rat organs (equilibrated in PBS to preserve physiological hydration) were utilized. The hydrogel-tissue interface was established and monitored, with macroscopic images captured after 60 min to visually assess the integration and tissue-mimetic adhesion stability.

Cytocompatibility evaluation

Prior to biological evaluation, all hydrogel specimens were subjected to ethylene oxide sterilization to ensure a sterile environment for cellular interaction. To assess the indirect effects of the scaffolds, leachates (extracts) were prepared by incubating the hydrogels in DMEM culture medium for a 24-hour period, in strict accordance with the ISO 10993-12 protocol. BMSCs served as the cellular model for evaluating cytocompatibility.

The metabolic activity and proliferation kinetics of the BMSCs were quantified via the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay at specific time intervals of 1, 3, 5, and 7 days post-incubation. The OD at a wavelength of 450 nm was determined using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Complementarily, the spatial distribution of viable cells was visualized through Live/Dead fluorescence staining, with the resulting fluorescent signals captured and recorded using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, LSM800, Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

To elucidate the influence of the hydrogel environment on cellular topography, the cytoskeletal organization and spreading patterns were analyzed. Following a standard 4% paraformaldehyde fixation process, the cell membranes were permeabilized using Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich). Subsequently, the F-actin filaments were specifically labeled with Rhodamine-Phalloidin for a 20-minute duration. The morphological architecture of the BMSCs was then investigated under confocal laser scanning microscopy, focusing on the formation of stress fibers and focal adhesions.

A wound-healing (cell scratch) assay was implemented to quantify the chemotactic migratory response of BMSCs induced by the hydrogels. BMSCs were inoculated into multi-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well and allowed to reach confluence under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). A standardized linear was introduced into the monolayer using a sterile micropipette tip. After removing non-adherent cellular debris through PBS rinsing, the cells were replenished with the respective hydrogel extracts. The spatial closure of the denuded area was monitored at 0, 12, and 24 h using a fluorescence inverted microscope. The dynamic migration rate was calculated based on the reduction of the scratch area over time.

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were seeded onto Matrigel-coated 96-well plates and cultured with different hydrogel extracts. After incubation for 6 h at 37 °C, tube formation was observed under an inverted microscope.

In vitro osteogenic performance

The potential of the composite hydrogels to facilitate the osteoblastic commitment of BMSCs was rigorously evaluated using alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining assays. These markers were utilized to monitor the early-stage enzymatic activity and the late-stage formation of calcium nodules, respectively.

Following the incubation of BMSCs with the various hydrogel extracts for designated maturation periods of 7, 14, and 21 days, the cell monolayers were immobilized using a standard formalin fixative. To visualize the differentiation markers, the specimens were treated with either an ALP staining solution or an Alizarin Red S reagent for a 30-minute duration. The extent of enzymatic expression and the degree of extracellular matrix (ECM) mineralization were subsequently determined by recording the OD using a multispectral microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madison, USA).

qRT-PCR and western blot assay

To evaluate the transcriptional profiles induced by the hydrogel scaffolds, total RNA was isolated from the BMSCs after a 7-day co-cultivation period using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The harvested RNA was subsequently converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) via reverse transcription utilizing the Revert Aid first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan). The relative mRNA abundance of the target osteogenic markers was quantified using the TB Green qRT-PCR assay kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan) on a high-precision thermal cycler. The specific oligonucleotide primer sequences employed in this study are detailed in Table 1. To normalize the data and determine the fold-change in gene expression, the ∆∆Ct method was applied, with the results being expressed relative to the internal housekeeping gene control.

The translational expression of osteogenic-related proteins was further corroborated through Western blot analysis. Following a 7-day incubation with the hydrogels, cells were harvested and subjected to chemical lysis using a comprehensive protein extraction kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Jiangsu, China). The resulting protein lysates were resolved through SDS-PAGE (sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis) based on their molecular weight and subsequently electro-transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes.

To prevent non-specific binding, the membranes were blocked for 1 h using a solution of 5% nonfat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST). The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies targeting the osteogenic markers. Following multiple washing steps, the membranes were treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for a duration of 1 h. For semi-quantitative analysis, the densitometric intensity of each band was measured and normalized to the loading control using image analysis software.

Macrophage polarization and cytokines secretion

The osteoimmune regulatory effect of the hydrogel was evaluated by analyzing macrophage polarization markers under defined inflammatory stimulation conditions. These conditions were selected to mimic the early inflammatory microenvironment that occurs after bone injury and biomaterial implantation, during which macrophages are rapidly recruited and activated. In this phase, excessive or prolonged M1-like polarization may impair tissue repair, whereas timely transition toward an M2-like reparative phenotype is considered beneficial for angiogenesis, matrix remodeling, and subsequent bone regeneration.

The distinct polarization states of macrophages are characterized by the differential expression of specific surface markers, namely CD86 for the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and CD206 for the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. To visualize these markers, RAW264.7 cells were subjected to immunofluorescence analysis. Following ethylene oxide sterilization, the hydrogel specimens were positioned within 12-well culture plates. Subsequently, cells were inoculated at a density of 4 × 105 cells per well and allowed to undergo overnight attachment. To induce a baseline inflammatory response, the culture medium was substituted with fresh medium supplemented with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for a 12-hour incubation period.

Following the stimulation, the cell monolayers were immobilized with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized using Triton X-100. To prevent non-specific antibody binding, the cells were treated with a 5% goat serum blocking buffer for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific to CD86 and CD206. The nuclei were subsequently counterstained using DAPI. The spatial distribution and expression intensity of these phenotypic markers were visualized and recorded via fluorescence microscopy.

To further quantify the distribution of macrophage subpopulations under identical culture conditions, flow cytometry was performed. The cell populations were harvested and labeled with fluorescently conjugated antibodies to determine the precise percentages of M1 and M2 macrophages. This dual-modal approach (combining qualitative imaging with quantitative cytometric analysis) provided a robust assessment of the hydrogel capacity to modulate the immune microenvironment toward a pro-regenerative state.

Additionally, qRT-PCR and ELISA assay were employed to identify and analyze relevant pro-inflammatory (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (Arg-1, IL-10, and TGF-β). The primer sequences were listed in Table 1.

Cranial bone defect healing

The osteoregenerative potential of the composite hydrogels was investigated in vivo utilizing a critical-size calvarial defect model established in 6-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats. All animal housing conditions and surgical interventions were strictly conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The experimental protocols received formal approval and oversight from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Peking Union Medical College Hospital.

To initiate the modeling process, the rats were anesthetized, and a circular full-thickness bone defect (4 mm in diameter) was precision-drilled into the parietal bone of the cranium, ensuring no damage to the underlying dura mater. This defect size was selected as it exceeds the threshold for spontaneous healing in rats. Subsequently, the prepared hydrogel scaffolds were carefully implanted into the site of the injury. Following the procedure, the periosteum and skin were sutured in layers to ensure wound stability. At chronological intervals of 4 and 8 weeks post-implantation, the animals were euthanized, and the cranial specimens were harvested for comprehensive micro-CT imaging and histological analysis to quantify the volume and quality of the newly formed bone tissue.

Bone regeneration was evaluated at 4 and 8 weeks after treatment to assess the dynamic progression of bone repair. The 4-week time point was chosen to represent an early-to-intermediate stage of bone regeneration, during which newly formed bone tissue, matrix deposition, mineralization, and initial trabecular reconstruction can be detected. The 8-week time point was selected to evaluate the later stage of bone healing, characterized by further mineral deposition, maturation of regenerated bone tissue, and structural remodeling. By comparing bone regeneration at 4 and 8 weeks, we aimed to determine whether the hydrogel could not only initiate early bone formation but also sustain and enhance bone maturation during the repair process.

Micro-CT analysis

Following the specified recovery periods of 4 and 8 weeks post-operation, the harvested cranial specimens were subjected to an immobilization process in a 4% paraformaldehyde fixative to maintain structural integrity. Subsequent to the removal of redundant peripheral soft tissues, the region of interest (ROI) centered on the calvarial defect was analyzed via high-resolution micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) using NEMO Micro CT (NMC-200). Voltage was 80 kV, current was 0.06 mA, image reconstruction software was Recon, and image display and application software was Avatar.

Based on the volumetric data and radiographic reconstructions, a quantitative morphometric analysis was executed to evaluate the quality and architecture of the regenerated osseous tissue. Based on the micro-CT data, the bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume/total defect volume (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N) and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) of the newly formed bone tissue were quantitatively calculated.

Histological staining and immunohistochemistry

To evaluate the microscopic quality of the regenerated bone, cranial tissue specimens were harvested at 4 and 8 weeks post-operation. The specimens were subjected to a rigorous histological preparation sequence, which included immobilization in a formaldehyde fixative, followed by thorough decalcification in an EDTA solution. Subsequently, the tissues underwent a series of dehydration, clearing, and paraffin infiltration steps to facilitate sectioning. Standard Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess the general tissue architecture and inflammatory response, while Masson trichrome staining was utilized to visualize the maturation and organization of the newly synthesized collagen fibers.

To further characterize the osteoinductive environment, the expression of protein markers critical to osteogenesis and angiogenesis was analyzed. Immunohistochemical labeling was performed using monoclonal antibodies targeting Osteopontin (OPN), Osteocalcin (OCN), Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), Collagen I (COL I), and Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF). Post-staining, the histopathological sections were visualized and documented using an Olympus BX53 optical microscope (Shinjuku, Tokyo, Japan). To ensure objectivity, a quantitative assessment was performed using the average OD method, providing a numerical evaluation of the protein expression levels across the different treatment groups.

To elucidate the immunomodulatory role of the hydrogel in vivo, the phenotypic transition of macrophages was investigated. Immunofluorescence staining was executed using specific antibodies against CD86 (M1-type marker) and CD206 (M2-type marker) to identify the prevailing macrophage subtypes within the defect site. Additionally, an immunohistochemical assay was conducted to profile the local inflammatory microenvironment. This involved quantifying the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) as well as anti-inflammatory proteins (Arg-1 and IL-10). This comprehensive analysis provided a molecular snapshot of the hydrogel capacity to shift the immune response toward a pro-regenerative and anti-inflammatory phenotype.

Transcriptome RNA sequencing

To elucidate the intracellular signaling cascades and underlying molecular mechanisms triggered by the hydrogels, high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was conducted on BMSCs. The experimental groups were categorized into BMPDA@CPMH and CPMH hydrogels.

Initially, total RNA was extracted, and the mRNA subpopulation was selectively enriched utilizing Oligo(dT)-coupled magnetic beads (poly-A enrichment). The isolated mRNA was subsequently subjected to thermal fragmentation, yielding short segments suitable for library construction. Utilizing these fragments as templates, first-strand cDNA was synthesized via six-base random hexamer priming, followed by the synthesis of second-strand cDNA to generate stable double-stranded (ds) DNA molecules.

Following the synthesis, the ds-cDNA was refined through end-repair and adapter ligation. PCR amplification was subsequently executed to enrich for cDNA fragments within a targeted size range. The quality, concentration, and integrity of the resulting cDNA library were rigorously assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer. Finally, high-depth sequencing was performed on the Illumina sequencing-by-synthesis platform. The raw sequence data were then processed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and enriched signaling pathways associated with osteogenesis.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data derived from the experiments were expressed as the mean and standard deviation (SD), representing continuous variables. Conversely, categorical data were summarized as frequencies and corresponding percentages. To evaluate the statistical significance of differences between multiple experimental cohorts for continuous variables, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed. Where significant differences were detected, post hoc comparisons were executed using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test to identify specific group disparities (N = 3). For the analysis of categorical variables, the Pearson Chi-square test was utilized. A threshold for statistical significance was established at a P value < 0.05. All computational analyses and data processing were conducted using SPSS software (Version 27.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results and discussion

Characteristics of MPDA and BMPDA

Mesoporous nanomaterials, due to their specific pore size and volume, as well as high specific surface area, are widely used as microcarriers for drugs and proteins. The extensive internal space and numerous surface pores of mesoporous nanomaterials provide a foundation for the encapsulation and loading of drugs, resulting in a high drug loading efficiency. Previous studies have demonstrated that the incorporation of mesoporous bioactive nanoparticles into hydrogels enhances the crosslinking strength and shortens the gelation time, further promoting osteogenic effects in vitro and in vivo [19]. In the synthesis of PDA, Pluronic F127 and TMB were used as the template and medium, respectively. Dopamine self-assembled at the emulsion-template interface through π-π stacking and hydrogen bonding interactions and polymerized into PDA under alkaline conditions. The template and medium were removed by centrifugation and washing, resulting in the mesoporous nanostructure. We used FTIR to verify the chemical structures of MPDA, BMP-2, and BMPDA (Fig. 2A). MPDA exhibited characteristic stretching vibration signals for C-C, C-N, and indole rings. After loading BMP-2, composite absorption peaks corresponding to the MPDA C-N and BMP-2 amide bonds were observed, confirming the successful synthesis of BMPDA.

The particle size distribution of MPDA and BMPDA showed little variation, with average diameters of 234.5 ± 6.2 nm and 235.9 ± 3.7 nm, respectively (Fig. 2B). This suggests that the incorporation of BMP-2 did not alter the particle size. The surface area of the MPDA nanoparticles was 593 m2/g (Fig. S1). Dynamic light scattering analysis showed that the surface zeta potentials of MPDA and BMPDA nanoparticles were − 25.4 mV and − 22.4 mV, respectively, demonstrating the stability of the nanoparticles (Fig. 2C). Literature reports that nanoparticles with a zeta potential higher than − 30 mV exhibit good stability [20]. The electrostatic repulsive forces between nanoparticles that meet these criteria can counteract the attractive forces, thereby increasing the distance and spacing between the nanoparticles, which ultimately reduces the risk of particle aggregation and sedimentation [21].

TEM was used to observe the microscopic morphology of the nanoparticles, showing that both MPDA and BMPDA exhibited uniform spherical shapes with clearly visible mesopores (Fig. 2D). The BMPDA exhibited uniformly distributed protrusions, indicating the presence of BMP-2 on both the surface and within the mesopores. This also proved the effective combination of MPDA and BMP-2.

A major drawback of current nanotechnology-based delivery platforms is their relatively low drug loading efficiency, with some nanomaterials exhibiting drug loading efficiencies below 20% [22]. Additionally, even for materials with high loading efficiency, the drug loading content is still limited due to the restricted internal space. In our study, the drug loading efficiency and encapsulation efficiency of BMPDA were 31.9% and 93.7%, respectively, which was mainly attributed to the mesoporous structure and high specific surface area of MPDA.

Mesoporous nanomaterials can provide the controllable drug release rate, thereby maintaining stability of the drug concentration over a relatively long period. By controlling the release rate, the risks of complications and drug-related side effects caused by burst release of drugs or growth factors could be minimized [23]. The release curve indicates that BMP-2 is continuously and steadily released from BMPDA, with a cumulative release of approximately 66.1% within 24 h. The curve shows that BMP-2 is released rapidly in the first 4 h, followed by a slower and sustained release phase (Fig. 2E). This biphasic release profile ensures that BMP-2 rapidly reaches the effective concentration and matches the regeneration rate of bone tissue.

Verification and characterization of hydrogels

We chose COS as the backbone of the injectable in situ forming hydrogels because it has additional advantages over chitosan, such as better biocompatibility, water solubility, abundant availability, and the ability to promote cell differentiation and transduction [24]. In addition, COS exhibits strong adhesion to various mucosal surfaces and has low allergenicity, indicating that this material is suitable for application in various biological interfaces both on the surface and within the body [25]. Multi-arm PEG-based crosslinkers have been extensively studied, as the reactive groups of PEG could assemble polymer chains into interpenetrating networks [26]. This provides hydrogels with tunable mechanical properties and additional chemical binding sites [27]. These characteristics have led to the increasing use of PEG as the matrix material for surgical sealants or tissue adhesives. Although several PEG-based sealants have already been introduced into clinical applications, these commercially available products generally suffer from poor cell compatibility and lack of tissue regeneration potential [28]. Therefore, we added COS and Mg-GP to the system to improve biocompatibility. Moreover, the electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonds between COS and Mg-GP further contribute to enhancing the mechanical performances of the hydrogels [29]. In the hydrogels, the free amino groups in the COS interact with the phosphate groups of Mg-GP, facilitating the exposure of glycerine. This process further promoted the click reactions between the amine groups and the succinimide ester groups at the arms of 4-arm-PEG-SC.

The chemical structures of hydrogels were characterized by FTIR (Fig. 2F). The absorption peak at 3450 cm− 1 for COS attributed to the stretching vibration of -OH group. The peak near 1654 cm− 1 was attributed to the bending vibration of amide groups, while the peak around 1420 cm− 1 was associated with the methyl groups. The absorption peak at 1079 cm− 1 was assigned to the bending vibration of -CO group. For PEG, the absorption peak at 1741 cm− 1 represented the -C = O- stretching vibration, and characteristic peaks of the PEG appeared at 1345 cm− 1, 1281 cm− 1, 1243 cm− 1, 1112 cm− 1, 951 cm− 1, and 843 cm− 1. Mg-GP showed characteristic peaks at 1138 cm− 1, 1077 cm− 1, and 1030 cm− 1, which were ascribed to the stretching vibrations of P-O/P = O and P-OH. The absorption peak of the CPMH hydrogel around 1607 cm− 1 corresponded to the amide bond vibration, while peaks at 1417 cm− 1, 1343 cm− 1, 1096 cm− 1, 992 cm− 1, and 842 cm− 1 were attributed to the characteristic peaks of COS, PEG, and Mg-GP. The interaction between COS, PEG, and Mg-GP caused a shift in the peak positions. These results indicated the successful preparation of hydrogels.

Hydrogels with uniform and interconnected pore structures facilitate host cell adhesion, proliferation, and the ingrowth of blood vessels and nerves, which are critical for hydrogel degradation and remodeling [30]. To investigate the microstructure of the hydrogels, we employed SEM. As shown in Fig. 2G, both CPMH and BMP-2@CPMH hydrogels exhibited interconnected three-dimensional network structures. The pore sizes in CPMH and BMP-2@CPMH hydrogels were relatively large and unevenly distributed, whereas the BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel displayed a denser, honeycomb-like porous structure. Previous literature has shown that porous biomaterials with pore sizes ranging from 100 to 300 μm facilitate the transport of oxygen and nutrients, promote cell adhesion and infiltration, as well as the synthesis of the extracellular matrix [31]. Therefore, the pore size of BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel is advantageous. Furthermore, the BMPDA nanoparticles were observed within the pores of the BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel, confirming the loading of nanoparticles into the hydrogels and indicating the successful synthesis of BMPDA@CPMH.

We further determined the rheological properties of the hydrogels. Maintaining a constant frequency, the relationship between the modulus and strain was observed. The results indicated that for all hydrogels, the storage modulus G’ was greater than the loss modulus G’’ up to a strain of 100%. When G’ > G’’, rheological test suggests that the formation of a stable three-dimensional network structure within the hydrogels (Fig. 2H). The higher G’ in the BMP-2@CPMH and BMPDA@CPMH indicates enhanced tensile strain and compressive performance. This is crucial for applications in bone tissue engineering, as it ensures that hydrogel maintains its shape and structural integrity under external forces [32].

The shear thinning properties of the hydrogels confirmed the injectability (Fig. 2I and Fig. S2). By observing the relationship between viscosity and shear rates, we found that the hydrogels showed a decrease in viscosity with increasing shear rates, indicating that the hydrogels were injectable. The shear thinning properties could ensure the successful local injection of the hydrogels at the bone defect sites [33]. The step-strain rheological tests revealed that all hydrogels remained stable at low strains. When the strain exceeded the critical value (G’ < G’’), the hydrogels became softer and more susceptible to deformation. This change is attributed to the breaking of chemical bonds under high shear strain. However, when the strain decreased back to below the critical value (G’ > G’’), the hydrogels were able to recover, demonstrating good self-healing properties (Fig. 2J). The shear-thinning and self-healing properties of the hydrogel allow it to be injected and molded into various shapes required for clinical applications. Additionally, it can self-repair damaged structures when broken or compressed [34]. These characteristics are especially crucial for surgical procedures, particularly minimally invasive orthopedic surgeries, as the hydrogel samples must be able to pass through various narrow musculoskeletal gaps and fit through a range of minimally invasive endoscopes or surgical sheaths [35, 36].

Hydrogels with lower swelling ratios are more suitable for regenerating biological tissue defects, as these hydrogels typically exhibit better mechanical properties and are less likely to adversely affect surrounding tissues [37]. We tested the swelling properties of the hydrogels, and the results showed that the swelling ratios of CPMH, BMP-2@CPMH, and BMPDA@CPMH were 250%, 239%, and 182%, respectively (Fig. 2K). These results suggest that the incorporation of drug-loaded nanoparticles affects the swelling behavior of the hydrogels. The results of swelling test align with the changes in microstructure observed in the SEM. This is because the swelling ratio largely depends on the density and porosity of the hydrogels. The swelling ratio is an equilibrium state determined by the balance between the osmotic driving force, which drives the rehydration of the hydrophilic polymer chains, and the entropy penalty associated with the stretching of the polymer chains during water absorption [38]. The denser and smaller pore microstructure in BMPDA@CPMH can limit water absorption and reduce the swelling ratio.

The degradation rate of hydrogels is a crucial indicator, as it determines whether the hydrogel can match the rate of new bone regeneration [39]. Considering the generally fast degradation of hydrogels, the newly designed hydrogel needs to degrade slowly, providing initial mechanical support for the regeneration of bone tissue. The degradation rates of the hydrogels are shown in Fig. 2L, with all hydrogels demonstrating a gradual degradation over time. At day 20, the degradation rates of CPMH, BMP-2@CPMH, and BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel were 98.1 ± 0.5%, 95.2 ± 0.9%, and 59.0 ± 2.2%, respectively. The slower degradation rate of the BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel may be related to its denser microstructure and higher crosslinking density, which is consistent with the trend observed in the swelling ratios. Additionally, the incorporation of nanoparticles also reduces the degradation rates of biomaterials [40]. These results demonstrate that the BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel can match the regeneration rate of bone tissue.

Previous literature has shown that therapeutic metal ions and growth factors are essential factors for bone regeneration [41]. Among them, Mg2+ can promote the formation of bone tissue and mineral deposition, and BMP-2 can promote the recruitment and osteogenic differentiation of stem cells. However, the high concentrations or burst release kinetics of Mg2+ or BMP-2 could lead to poor bone regeneration and osteolysis [42]. We measured the release kinetics of Mg2+ in hydrogels. As shown in Fig. 3A, the hydrolysis of the Mg-GP phosphate monoester bond in the hydrogels resulted in a sustained release, with a cumulative release of about 55.1% after 30 days. The release pattern of BMP-2 was similar, with a cumulative release of approximately 59.1% after 35 days (Fig. 3B). Due to the presence of mesoporous nanoparticles, the BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel showed a sustained and controllable release, which helps to reduce the possible complications caused by the burst accumulation of BMP-2. In general, the hydrogel could achieve the controllable release of Mg2+ and BMP-2.

Photothermal and mechanical properties

The photothermal conversion properties endow the hydrogels with multiple biological functions, including anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and bone regeneration-promoting effects [43]. Dopamine exhibits excellent photothermal conversion capabilities, enabling the conversion of light into thermal energy [44]. We assessed the photothermal performance of the hydrogel samples under 808 nm NIR laser irradiation. The NIR irradiation power was 2 W/cm2, and the irradiation time was 5 min. As shown in Fig. 3C and D, BMPDA@CPMH demonstrated significant photothermal effects, with the maximum temperature reaching approximately 41.8 °C after 5 min of irradiation.

Musculoskeletal infections are catastrophic complications that lead to adverse outcomes, including the need for revision surgeries, increased medical burden, and decreased satisfaction [45]. Therefore, while actively promoting bone regeneration, it is essential to closely monitor the occurrence of infection events. In recent years, new antimicrobial strategies have emerged, with photothermal therapy (PTT) receiving widespread attention due to its non-invasive nature, controllable treatment intensity and duration, low drug resistance and systemic toxicity. This physical approach inhibits bacteria by disrupting cell membranes and accelerating protein denaturation, without relying on antibiotic drugs [46]. We used the spread plate method to validate the in vitro photothermal antibacterial properties of the hydrogels. In the absence of NIR irradiation, BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel showed the inhibitory effect on bacterial growth relative to the PBS group. In addition, the BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel + NIR group exhibited the better antibacterial activity (Fig. 3G). The quantitative results also confirmed statistical difference, both for Gram-negative Escherichia coli and Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (Fig. 3E and F). The observed antibacterial activity may be attributed to a synergistic mechanism. First, mild photothermal heating can disturb bacterial membrane integrity and increase membrane permeability, making bacteria more susceptible to antibacterial components within the hydrogel. Second, the elevated local temperature may weaken bacterial adhesion and biofilm stability, thereby reducing bacterial survival and colonization. Third, the intrinsic antibacterial properties of the hydrogel may be enhanced under NIR irradiation, resulting in improved antibacterial performance even at a relatively moderate temperature.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are chemical species generated from the incomplete reduction of molecular oxygen, exhibiting dose-dependent properties [47, 48]. Therefore, developing a biomaterial with ROS scavenging capabilities is a crucial strategy for restoring the homeostasis of the inflammatory environment both inside and outside the cell, and for protecting normal physiological metabolic processes from damage. We assessed the antioxidant capabilities of the hydrogels using the DPPH free radical scavenging assay. As shown in Fig. 3H, BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel exhibited excellent antioxidant capacity.

According to the literature, biomaterials that promote tissue regeneration need to match the initial mechanical properties of the host tissue [49]. Biomaterials with compressive modulus between 0.25 and 0.5 MPa are considered suitable for non-load-bearing bone repair scenarios [50]. If the mechanical strength is too high, it can impair the regeneration of bone tissue and the stability of the bone-implant interface. Conversely, materials with insufficient strength cannot provide the necessary mechanical environment for bone regeneration. In addition, excellent wet tissue adhesion is one of the essential properties for injectable hydrogels [51]. Wet tissue adhesion ensures that the hydrogel can effectively conform to the defect site, especially in orthopedic surgeries, where many procedures are accompanied by the flow and exudation of water or body fluids. Therefore, we evaluated the mechanical properties and adhesive strength of the hydrogels. Compared to the CPMH and BMP-2@CPMH hydrogels, the BMPDA@CPMH exhibited the highest compressive modulus (Fig. 3I). The compressive modulus of the BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel (280.0 ± 5.3 kPa) was 1.8 times higher than that of the CPMH (150.3 ± 10.6 kPa) and 1.6 times higher than that of the BMP-2@CPMH hydrogel (175.3 ± 5.0 kPa) (Fig. 3J). In terms of adhesive strength, the BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel (10.0 ± 1.3 kPa) was 2.8 times higher than that of the CPMH (3.6 ± 0.4 kPa) and 2.7 times higher than that of the BMP-2@CPMH (3.7 ± 0.2 kPa) (Fig. 3K). The higher compressive modulus of BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel may be related to its denser microstructure. In addition, PDA can introduce both covalent and noncovalent interactions into the hydrogel network, which enhances the compressive modulus and adhesion property [39, 52].

Furthermore, the images of rat organs and hydrogels showed that all tissues were firmly attached to the hydrogels, and when inverted, the organs did not detach or fall off (Fig. 3L). This demonstrates that the hydrogels can adhere to the surfaces of various organs, exhibiting excellent bio-tissue adhesion.

In vitro cytocompatibility and osteogenic potential

Hydrogels have complex three-dimensional structure, which closely resembles the extracellular matrix, making it an ideal carrier for cell adhesion and proliferation, as well as the storage reservoir for therapeutic ions and growth factors [53]. Therefore, verifying the cytocompatibility of hydrogel is an important procedure in evaluating potential for biological applications. The CCK-8 assay showed that cells in all groups exhibited cell proliferation over time (Fig. 4A). OD values of the BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel were significantly higher than those of the other three groups at days 1, 3, 5 and 7, indicating that BMPDA@CPMH hydrogels had strong effects in promoting cell growth. In the Live/Dead staining, as hardly any cell death was observed in three groups, and the cells showed high viability (Fig. 4B). Cytoskeleton staining showed that after co-incubation with BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel, the cells exhibited better spreading (Fig. 4C). The cell scratch assay revealed that the BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel had the least remaining scratch area (Fig. 4D). Quantitative analysis indicated that the migration rates at 12 h for CPMH, BMP-2@CPMH, and BMPDA@CPMH were 17.0 ± 1.3%, 31.4 ± 0.8%, and 59.3 ± 1.3%, respectively. The migration rates at 24 h for CPMH, BMP-2@CPMH, and BMPDA@CPMH were 33.1 ± 1.0%, 56.6 ± 1.5%, and 75.1 ± 1.0%, respectively. The migration rates in the BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel were significantly higher than that of the other two groups (Fig. 4E). The HUVEC tube formation assay showed that BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel could maximally promote endothelial cell proliferation and angiogenesis, which further strengthens the evidence for bone-angiogenic coupling (Fig. S3).

Due to the interplay between the skeletal and immune system, immunoregulation is a key factor in bone metabolism. According to previous studies, macrophages play a crucial role in regulating inflammatory responses and maintaining bone immune homeostasis [56, 57]. We evaluated the effects of different hydrogel samples on macrophage polarization in response to LPS stimulation using immunofluorescence staining. These results suggested the BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel could effectively promote M2 macrophage phenotype and reduce M1 macrophage phenotype in vivo, thereby suppressing the inflammatory response and creating favorable immune environment for bone regeneration.

Transcriptome sequencing

Despite evidence from qRT-PCR, western blot assay, and immunohistochemistry, the potential molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways involved in promoting bone regeneration remain unknown. Therefore, we employed transcriptome RNA sequencing to explore the underlying molecular mechanisms of osteogenesis induced by BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel. Transcriptome sequencing is an effective method for studying how cells respond to hydrogels, revealing gene expression levels, signaling pathways, and regulatory factors involved in the interaction between hydrogels and host cells [61, 62]. The comparison of Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million fragments mapped (FPKM) between the BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel and CPMH hydrogel suggested that the global gene distributions were consistent with each other (Fig. 9A). As shown in Fig. 9B, the heatmap illustrates the differential gene expression of BMSCs cultured with BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel or CPMH hydrogel. The expression levels of genes were represented by different colors, indicating variations in gene regulation under different culture conditions. 246 differentially expressed genes (P value < 0.05, |log2 Fold Change| > 1) were identified, including 129 upregulated genes and 117 downregulated genes (Fig. 9C). We conducted Gene Ontology (GO) analysis to classify the differentially expressed genes from the BMPDA@CPMH and CPMH hydrogels in terms of biological processes, cellular components and molecular functions (Fig. 9D). GO analysis showed that the main biological mechanisms included cellular process, biological regulation, inflammatory response, immune system process and cytoskeletal motor activity. The evolutionary genealogy of genes: Non-supervised Orthologous Groups (eggNOG) database was used to classify functions of differentially expressed genes (Fig. 9E). The eggNOG database classified differentially expressed genes into transcription, signal transduction mechanisms, and cytoskeleton. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis was performed to further investigate the differentially expressed genes and reveal potential osteogenesis-related signaling pathways. As shown in Fig. 9F-H, the TNF and MAPK signaling pathways were involved in the biological processes by which BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel promoted osteogenesis. TNF pathway is a crucial signaling pathway in the antioxidant process [63]. TNF-α, a key member of the TNF family, plays a role in inflammation, apoptosis, and immune cell activation. Upon external stimulation, immune cells secrete TNF-α to initiate an inflammatory response against external threats. TNFR1 stimulation activates a complex signaling cascade, integrating the NF-kB signaling pathway circuit with the MAPK signaling pathway [64]. These findings suggested that the hydrogel could regulate inflammatory and immune responses through the TNF signaling pathway.

Additionally, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on the signaling pathways, which also highlighted significant enrichment in pathways related to osteogenesis and immune regulation (Fig. 9I), including the NF-kB pathway and TNF pathway. The blockade of TNFR1 could suppress LPS-induced cell apoptosis and excessive inflammation through the NF-kB and MAPK signaling pathways [65, 66]. This was consistent with the results of KEGG enrichment analysis. In conclusion, transcriptome sequencing results suggested that the enhanced osteogenic effects and immunoregulation of BMPDA@CPMH hydrogel may be mediated through the TNF signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, and NF-kB signaling pathway.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The present study mainly focused on local bone regeneration, antibacterial activity, osteoimmune regulation, and preliminary biocompatibility, while comprehensive systemic safety was not fully evaluated. Potential systemic toxicity, major-organ responses, hematological and biochemical changes, long-term hydrogel degradation, remnant material status, and biodistribution of nanoparticles or degradation products require further investigation. In addition, because BMP-2 is a potent osteoinductive factor, the risk of ectopic ossification should be considered, especially if BMP-2 diffuses outside the defect area. Although the hydrogel was designed to achieve localized and sustained BMP-2 release, further long-term studies are needed to confirm whether this strategy can effectively minimize ectopic bone formation and ensure systemic safety.

Conclusions

We developed an injectable in situ forming hydrogel to integrate multiple functions into a single biomaterial. The hydrogel exhibits excellent physical properties, including injectability, self-healing, appropriate swelling and degradation rates, wet tissue adhesion, and sufficient mechanical strength—necessary conditions for application in minimally invasive surgery and bone regeneration scenarios. Moreover, the hydrogel demonstrates various biological effects, including controllable release of BMP-2 and Mg2+, antioxidant and antibacterial properties, promotion of cell adhesion and migration, regulation of osteoimmune microenvironment, and stimulation of osteogenesis and angiogenesis—key processes for enhancing bone regeneration and bone maturation. In summary, the injectable nanocomposite hydrogel may represent a promising minimally invasive solution for bone regeneration.
