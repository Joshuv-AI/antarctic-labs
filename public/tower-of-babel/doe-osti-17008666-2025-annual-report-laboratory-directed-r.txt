Introduction
Each year, Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL) is required to provide a report of its completed
Laboratory Directed Research and Development Program (LDRD) projects to the Department of
Energy (DOE) Office of Scientific and Technical Information in accordance with DOE Order
413.2C Chg1 (MinChg) dated August 2, 2018. This summary report provides an overview of all
the LDRD projects funded at Brookhaven that concluded in FY 2025 in satisfaction of that
requirement. The fiscal year in which each project was funded initially is indicated by the first two
digits of the LDRD project number.

The investments that BNL makes in its LDRD program support the Laboratory’s strategic goals
and priority programs as well as new areas of research and competencies at the Laboratory that are
consistent with the Lab’s vision and mission. Specifically, BNL’s LDRD Program funds projects
to advance science and technology and develop scientific tools that provide remarkable insights
into our physical and biological world in support of DOE’s mission. This discretionary R&D tool
stimulates the scientific-technological community by advancing the frontiers of science,
technology, and engineering; enabling agile responses to energy, environmental, and national
security challenges; and developing tomorrow’s scientific and technical workforce.

This Program Activities Report represents the future of BNL science and technology; it is an
impressive body of exploratory work that investigates many scientific and technical directions in
support of the DOE and BNL missions.

                                       LDRD 23-045
 Novel Climate-Energy Sector Modeling Framework to Project Energy Demands and
 Infrastructure Resiliency in Urban Environments
 Principal Investigator: Michael Jensen

PURPOSE:
There is an urgent need for research that provides better understanding, and a predictive
capability, of the relationship between extreme weather events (including storms, heatwaves, and
cold snaps) and the existing (often outdated) and future energy infrastructure across weather
event types and regions. To meet these challenges, this project builds a unique integrated
modeling framework to address a critical gap in community capabilities of anticipating risks to
the energy infrastructure, which is essential for reliability of the grid system. The framework is
being developed and tested in the New York City region resulting in significant immediate value
to regional stakeholders, but the framework will be transferable to other high-risk locations
across the U.S.

The significant impacts of extreme weather events on energy infrastructure point to the need for
a strong, adaptable, and flexible energy infrastructure where both generation and distribution can
continue providing energy services during and/or can recover efficiently after being exposed to
hazardous winds and flooding, and extreme temperatures. To meet this need, we are building an
integrated modeling framework for future risk mitigation analysis of the energy infrastructure in
areas of high energy demand resulting from extreme weather. To accomplish this, we are linking
existing urbanized weather community models, with existing electric grid and power outage
prediction models. This unified regional weather-energy modeling approach enables the study of
both past and future events defining energy demands and system resiliency under mean and
anomalous conditions such as heat waves, cold snaps, and hazardous storms.

The implementation of this holistic approach is based on a unique integrated modeling
framework that couples the weather/climate models and a power grid model to evaluate and
quantify the impacts on grid operation and to develop plans for their mitigation to ensure the
reliability and resilience of the power grid. Significant progress has been made:

• Downscaling large-scale weather model output – An open-source downscaling framework
 was customized to model spatial relationships between low- (12x12 km) and high-(3x3 km)
 resolution weather forecast data, focusing on variables critical for energy generation and
 energy system planning. When applied to temperature forecasts, the tailored model generated
 high-resolution data with a mean absolute error of 0.0425 during training (~46 minutes) and
 showed comparable results to outputs from current computationally intensive physical
 models. This work produced a tool capable of complementing or replacing physical models
 for generating high-resolution forecasts. The approach is generalizable and can be applied to
 downscale other key variables, such as global horizontal irradiance and wind speed.

• Feasibility to meet energy demands during extreme events – An integrated, multi-scale
 modeling framework was developed to improve electric load management during extreme
 heat events in New York City. A high-resolution urban weather modeling was coupled with
 building energy simulations and power system load analysis to better capture how extreme

 3

 temperatures, urban heat island effects, and building characteristics interact to drive peak
 electricity demand. By applying the framework to heatwave scenarios in New York City, it
 was demonstrated that coordinated modeling across atmospheric, building, and grid domains
 can enhance demand forecasting, identify vulnerable neighborhoods, and evaluate demand-
 response or load-shedding strategies. This unified architecture enables more accurate and
 actionable planning for grid resilience under intensifying heat extremes associated with
 climate change.

• Integrated energy demand and power outage forecasting - Motivated by demonstrated gains
 from integrating artificial intelligence/machine learning (AI/ML) with high-resolution
 modeling and observational datasets, a unified, end-to-end framework for the joint prediction
 of energy demand, renewable generation, and weather-driven outages, Weather2X, was
 developed. The framework advances beyond conventional sequential forecasting by tightly
 coupling predictive analytics with grid optimization. Weather2X can interface directly with
 power system planning models and accommodates heterogeneous weather inputs across
 spatial and temporal scales. When paired with a scalable stochastic optimization engine, it
 enables quantitatively optimized operational and mitigation strategies that improve grid
 reliability and reduce expected costs under both nominal and extreme weather regimes.

 At the core of Weather2X is an ML–based feature mixer that systematically extracts,
 weights, and fuses heterogeneous data streams to maximize predictive skill. The architecture
 integrates historical observations, real-time numerical weather forecasts, generation and load
 time series, and static geographic and infrastructure descriptors within a unified
 representation space. By explicitly learning cross-variable interactions and nonlinear
 dependencies, the feature mixer enhances forecast stability, reduces overfitting to individual
 data sources, and improves robustness under distributional shifts associated with extreme
 events.

 Weather2X includes two primary predictive modules. The first delivers high-resolution,
 multi-day forecasts of weather-induced power outages at the spatial granularity required for
 grid asset management. The second module generates joint, multi-horizon forecasts of
 electricity demand and renewable generation using a Temporal Fusion Transformer-based
 temporal mixer architecture.

 Forecast outputs are generated at spatial and temporal resolutions aligned with explicit grid
 component loads, generators, and transmission assets—represented in operational models. A
 dedicated integration layer ensures seamless transfer of predictive outputs into planning and
 dispatch workflows, enabling forecasts to directly inform optimization and reliability
 assessments.

 Operationally, Weather2X formulates grid management as a mixed-integer nonlinear
 programming problem that co-optimizes generation dispatch, reserve allocation, and
 mitigation actions. The objective function minimizes total system cost—including production
 costs, load shedding penalties, and weather-related transmission risks—subject to voltage,
 thermal, and reliability constraints. By embedding high-fidelity forecasts within this
 optimization framework, Weather2X delivers technically rigorous, resilience-focused
 planning solutions that measurably enhance system reliability and cost efficiency under
 extreme weather conditions.

                                      LDRD 23-046
 Examining Novel Isotope Production Pathways for a Medium to High Energy Cyclotron
 Principal Investigator: Jasmine Hatcher Lamarre

PURPOSE:
The purpose of this project was to develop targets and methods for their preparation for the
production of isotopes on a cyclotron with medium- to high-proton-energy capability. These
targets are typically designed to withstand higher currents and satisfy the geometrical constraints
of the cyclotron target station. The focus of this work was on developing and testing targets for
the production of the medical isotope lead (Pb)-203 and the commercial calibration source cobalt
(Co)-57.

Enriched nickel (Ni) and thallium (Tl) were chosen as target materials for the production of Co-
57 and Pb-203 through the 58Ni(p,2p) and 203Tl(p,n) nuclear reactions. Since both target materials
are enriched and can be obtained in elemental form, the electroplating method on various
substrates was chosen as the initial approach for target preparation. Experimental conditions such
as current, substrate, plating time, and plating solution were varied to obtain the target thickness
required for optimum irradiation yield. Both targets were planned to be tested at the Brookhaven
Linac Isotope Producer (BLIP) and Medical Isotope Research and Production Program (MIRP)
cyclotrons. Chemical processing was developed with the aim of separating the isotope of interest
from the target material and co-produced impurities.

Both directions have enjoyed notable progress. An electroplating method for the preparation of
Ni targets and its post-irradiation chemical workup has been developed, proceduralized, and
transferred to the production team. Production of Co-57 from the targets and chemical processes
developed under this project funding will commence immediately after MIRP cyclotron
commissioning.

Similarly, Pb-203 targets and processing methods were developed and tested. The results were
reported in Lin et al., Establishing Pb-203 Production from Electrodeposited Tl Targets at
Brookhaven National Laboratory, published in EJNMMI Radiopharmacy and Chemistry on
December 10, 2025, 10(1):78.

                                        LDRD 23-048
 Real-time Information Distillation on Novel AI Hardware
 Principal Investigator: Jin Huang

PURPOSE:
Nuclear science requires diverse experimental data with stringent systematic uncertainty control.
These scientific requirements drive the need for streaming data acquisition (DAQ) in the next
generation of nuclear experiments such as the detectors at the Electron-Ion Collider (EIC).
Distinct from most existing experiments, these new experiments demand a high throughput data
reduction in real-time which preserves all collision signal information while filtering out
background and noise reliably. We will address this unique challenge by building a set of
artificial intelligence (AI)-based information distillation algorithms that perform noise filtering,
feature building, and compression.

Compared to traditional methods, our approach is constructed to utilize novel AI hardware,
resulting in high throughput and energy efficiency. The approach is designed to be robust with
built-in safeguard metrics communicating with human operators. The algorithms will be co-
designed with and demonstrated on a selection of emerging AI hardware, the first in the field.

In FY23, we delivered the initial algorithm suite and feasibility studies on novel AI hardware,
meeting both proposal milestones. We developed multiple Bicephalous Convolutional
Autoencoder (BCAE)-based compressors for sPHENIX time projection chamber (TPC) data,
showed that reduced‑precision inference can increase throughput, and released code and example
datasets to support reproducibility. This work was presented at the Supercomputing 2023 (SC23)
Data Reduction Workshop, receiving an Honorable Mention paper award, and was published in
the SC23 workshops proceedings.

In FY24, we advanced integration with the experiment. We benchmarked BCAE inference on
non‑von Neumann accelerators (Graphcore IPU and Groq) via the Argonne Leadership
Computing Facility AI testbeds and established a local test stand in the sPHENIX counting
house, marking the first deployment of these “AI chips” in a nuclear physics experiment
environment. The project supported postdoctoral researchers and graduate students and
strengthened collaborations with Stony Brook University, Columbia University, UCLA, and
Texas A&M University.

In FY25, we refined the algorithms for sparse streaming data and delivered a key technical
milestone: the BCAE‑VS variable‑rate, sparsity‑aware compressor published in the journal
Patterns. On simulated sPHENIX TPC data, BCAE‑VS achieved an average compression of
about 34 times, while improving reconstruction accuracy (about a 73% reduction in mean
absolute error) compared with previous dense models. These results and their implication for
recording far more collision events without trigger bias were published in the journal Patterns,
DOI: 10.1016/j.patter.2025.101452, and featured in a Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL)
news story (“AI Streamlines Deluge of Data from Particle Collisions,”
https://www.bnl.gov/newsroom/news.php?a=122693 ).

 6

On the hardware side, we continued co‑design work with Groq and initiated custom kernel
development for Tenstorrent accelerators to prepare for future online demonstrations with
sPHENIX streaming data and simulated EIC data. Follow‑on impact includes multiple
Department of Energy proposal submissions and new BNL–Stony Brook seed‑funded
collaborations, positioning BNL to lead real‑time AI computing for EIC detector designs.

                                       LDRD 23-049
 Capturing Leadership at the Future Higgs Factory for BNL
 Principal Investigator: Marc-André Pleier

PURPOSE:
The objective of this project is to establish a program of Higgs boson property studies at a
prospective Higgs Factory to uniquely detail the interdependence of physics sensitivities,
interaction region (IR) magnet design, and detector parameters at a future Higgs Factory. This
research is performed in the context of the prospective electron-positron Future Circular Collider
(FCC-ee) Higgs Factory to inform detector and accelerator development and optimization,
supporting the FCC feasibility study at CERN.

Expected results are public documents, unique in detailing the interdependence of physics
sensitivities, interaction region magnet design, and detector parameters (such as tracking, timing,
and energy resolutions), and a public software package enabling further studies, accompanied by
Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL) leadership in FCC studies, organization, and U.S.
community building.

The successful execution of this project will establish BNL's leading role in the Higgs Factory
physics and detector communities, leveraging our involvement in ongoing detector R&D
programs at CERN. With the 2023 Particle Physics Project Prioritization Panel identification of
an offshore Higgs Factory as the highest-priority next collider facility, we expect to provide
leadership in this emerging program.

We will study three complementary physics cases and how they guide detector layout and
optimization (in particular, tracking and timing detectors and noble-liquid based calorimetry)
informed by the ongoing IR magnet design and the machine detector interface (MDI)
developments.

The three pursued physics analyses address Higgs boson properties that are poorly accessible at
the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) and ensure no stone will be left unturned at the future Higgs
Factory:
• Higgs boson decay into two charm quarks, which is hardly accessible at the High
 Luminosity-LHC (HL-LHC) due to its challenging hadronic production environment
• Higgs boson self-coupling, which can only be poorly constrained at the HL-LHC, but is
 critical for our understanding of the evolution of the universe
• Higgs boson decay rate into invisible final states beyond Standard Model predictions, which
 cannot be precisely determined at the HL-LHC, but could solve the puzzle of dark matter.

We aim to leverage our relationships with domestic and international institutions to build a
strong FCC-ee team and thus strengthen BNL’s external relations with universities and global
collaborators.

In the first year of the project, we started the development of a software framework to study the
impact of detector variations on physics sensitivities. We also started the generation of simulated

 8

events to reproduce current state-of-the-art studies for physics analysis and detector design in all
three analysis thrusts. In parallel, we initiated new theoretical computations for electron-positron
colliders supporting the physics objectives and formed connections with other FCC-ee IR
stakeholders through monthly online MDI meetings.

In the second year of the project, we continued the above efforts in close connection with the
international FCC-ee organization, including regular reports in the appropriate forums. We
published two theoretical papers in journals and submitted two experimental study notes as well
as contributed to the “Future Circular Collider Mid-term Report,” which was publicly released in
spring 2024. The addendum to the Department of Energy-CERN Memorandum of
Understanding was signed in May 2024 at CERN after a year of preparation, making BNL an
official FCC collaboration member (and the first U.S. national lab with this status).

In this final and third year, our effort culminated with the publication of the “Future Circular
Collider Feasibility Study Report,” which included our team’s contributions and identified no
showstoppers for the FCC moving forward. In addition, we published another batch of the results
of this project on detector performance and IR design as well as theoretical results in peer-
reviewed journals, contributed to the European Committee for Future Accelerators “Higgs,
electroweak, and top factory study,” and were prominently represented in the “Expressions of
Interest for the Development of Detector Concepts and Sub-detector Systems for the Future
Circular Collider.” Three more of our experimental studies were submitted as FCC notes and are
in various stages of submission to peer-reviewed journals.

In FY25, BNL scientists gave 28 formal presentations related to the work of this project in
addition to 15 topical talks at the U.S. FCC Hackathon hosted by BNL in the fall of 2025. Over
the three years of its funding, this project has led to the publication of 11 papers in peer-reviewed
journals as well as 13 reports, enabled 89 presentations, and supported five students in their
training and research. BNL scientists continue to hold significant leadership positions in the:
• International FCC Physics Experiments and Detectors (PED) organization with Detector
 Concepts group co-leadership and membership in PED coordination and international FCC
 workshop and conference organization
• Detector R&D Collaborations
• U.S. Higgs Factory Circular Collider Organization
• Engagement of the experimental and theory communities with topical workshops
• Engagement of early career researchers through panel discussions
• Detailed planning of the MDI at topical workshops.

Our efforts continue to build the community and strengthen BNL’s external relations with U.S.
institutes and global collaborators.

                                      LDRD 23-050
 A Second EIC Detector: Physics Case and Conceptual Design
 Principal Investigator: Thomas Ullrich

PURPOSE:
This project established the physics case, performance requirements, and a realistic conceptual
design path for a second general-purpose detector and interaction region (IR) at the Electron-Ion
Collider (EIC). The technical objective was to determine—through full-simulation studies and
technology assessments—what detector/IR features are required to provide genuine
complementarity to the project detector (ePIC) and to translate those requirements into viable
subsystem concepts (magnet, tracking, particle identification (PID), calorimetry, forward
tagging) and an associated R&D roadmap. The work is inherently exploratory: it probes
alternative technology choices and IR options that are not in the project baseline and evaluates
their impact on key measurements and systematic control. The principal deliverable is the
Closeout Paper/Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL) formal report (to be posted on arXiv and
released as an EIC User Group Note), intended as a community blueprint for follow-on design
studies, detector consortia formation, and future funding proposals for a second detector.

We pursued a physics-driven detector/interaction-region co-design study for a second EIC
detector at interaction point (IP)8. Benchmark measurements (exclusive and diffractive
processes, forward tagging, heavy flavor, and quarkonium) were used to translate physics reach
into performance requirements and to identify where complementarity to the project detector
(ePIC) is most impactful.

The work combined Geant4-based simulation of candidate detector concepts, pre-conceptual
interaction-region optics/integration studies for IP8 (including a secondary focus option), and
targeted technology scouting to map requirements onto realistic subsystem concepts and an R&D
roadmap. Results were consolidated into a comprehensive Closeout Paper/BNL report intended
as a community reference for follow-on design and proposal activities. Many people from the
wider community within and outside of BNL contributed to the effort and the Closeout Paper
and are listed as authors.

In FY25, the project was completed; it translated benchmark physics needs into quantified
detector and IP8 IR requirements, evaluating realistic option sets in Geant4-based full
simulation, and consolidating the results into the Closeout Paper (BNL Formal Report BNL-
229253-2025-FORE, arXiv 2602.04636, and also released as EIC User Group Note). The report
is written as a community handoff document that enables follow-on design studies and future
proposal activities. Key technical results captured in the Closeout Paper include:
• IP8 IR and far-forward acceptance: a pre-conceptual IP8 beamline/IR layout (including a
 secondary-focus option) and quantified acceptance implications for exclusive/diffractive
 benchmarks, resulting requirements and placement constraints for Roman pots far-forward
 tracking, and zero-degree calorimetry.
• Tracking emphasizing redundancy with low material: compared mixed-technology layouts
 (precision silicon vertexing plus a large-volume gaseous tracker/time projection chamber
 concept) and quantified momentum resolution, efficiency, and edge-of-acceptance behavior,

 10

 including the trade-off between hit redundancy (robust pattern recognition) and material
 (critical for exclusive channels and low-pT tracking).
• Ultra-low-material option assessment: evaluated a drift-chamber concept based on coated
 carbon-fiber wires and identified the primary feasibility/R&D gating items (mechanical
 stability, gain/aging, readout and timing, and alignment strategy).
• Complementarity options for PID, calorimetry, and muon capability: benchmarked option
 sets not in the ePIC project baseline, including a barrel muon-identification concept inspired
 by the Belle II Large K and Muon Detector, defined segmentation/absorber requirements and
 highlighted dimuon quarkonium channels as a systematic-control advantage for selected
 measurements.
• Software, reconstruction, and co-design workflow: implemented an end-to-end evaluation
 loop (benchmarks ⇒ requirements ⇒ geometry/IR variants ⇒ full simulation ⇒
 reconstruction and metrics) and documented where machine learning/artificial intelligence
 provides highest leverage (pattern recognition, PID, and systematic cross-checks) for
 community continuation.
• Integration and roadmap: documented key IP8 integration constraints (forward packaging,
 services, shielding, solenoid/flux-return considerations) and consolidated a focused R&D
 roadmap and critical detector-IR interfaces needed to mature a second-detector concept.

Overall, the project transformed an exploratory second-detector concept into a technically
grounded set of detector and IR options with quantified performance drivers and an actionable
path for follow-on R&D and proto-design. A second detector remains widely regarded as an
essential component of the long-term EIC science program. The Closeout Report produced under
this project will likely stand in the near term as the most comprehensive consolidated reference
on the physics case, detector requirements, and viable design concepts for a second EIC detector.

                                        LDRD 23-051
 RBRC Research from RHIC to the EIC
 Principal Investigator: David Morrison

PURPOSE:
This project advanced the RIKEN BNL Research Center (RBRC) theory and computing
programs from the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC) scientific campaign to the emerging
Electron–Ion Collider (EIC) era. The technical objective was to provide rigorous theoretical
guidance and first-principles Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) calculations for key observables
at sPHENIX and the EIC, including heavy-quark transport and jet quenching, single-spin
asymmetries, and the multidimensional (three-dimensional (3D) and five-dimensional (5D))
structure of hadrons and nuclei. A central goal was to establish precision lattice QCD capabilities
for form factors, parton distribution functions (PDF), transverse-momentum–dependent
distributions (TMD), and generalized parton distributions (GPD), while developing advanced
algorithms such as Field Transform Hybrid Monte Carlo (FT-HMC) and optimized graphics
processing unit (GPU)-based software required for next-generation high-performance
computing. By demonstrating the feasibility of high-precision QCD calculations directly
matched to EIC observables—particularly in gluonic structure and nucleon tomography—the
project aimed to strengthen Brookhaven National Laboratory’s (BNL) leadership in theory and
computing for the EIC and reinforce the BNL–RIKEN partnership.

The project was executed through the RBRC Theory and Computing groups under the leadership
of the Principal Investigators (PI) David Morrison (RBRC Deputy Director and sPHENIX Co-
Spokesperson), Yoshitaka Hatta (RBRC Theory Group Leader), and Taku Izubuchi (RBRC
Computing Group Leader). The effort was closely coordinated with the Nuclear Theory and
High Energy Theory groups in the Physics Department and with university partners through joint
appointments and collaborative research programs.

The theory program, led by Dr. Hatta, addressed perturbative and nonperturbative QCD relevant
to RHIC and EIC observables, including single-spin asymmetries, quantum anomalies, spin–
orbit correlations, gravitational form factors, and the factorization and higher-order structure of

On the theory and simulation side, an important advance in the dynamics of ultra-cold beams

achieve unprecedented densities and pressures when focused to a point at high energies. Tests of

also shown encouraging results.

grid struggles with transmission capacity expansion, DLR offers a cost-effective alternative by
dynamically adjusting transmission limits based on real-time environmental conditions, enabling
greater utilization of existing infrastructure. Unlike static line ratings (SLR), DLR adapts to real-
world weather conditions, reducing transmission congestion and supporting increased renewable
energy penetration—such as wind power—without costly new infrastructure. The New York
Power Authority’s pilot DLR study found a positive correlation between increased real-time
capacity and increased wind generation, and capacity increases of 30 to 44% over SLR,
underscoring its potential impact. A key innovation in this project is the incorporation of a
fundamental physics-based DLR model that accounts for cable icing in heat balance equations,
improving reliability and resilience, especially under high-impact, low-frequency severe weather

ensuring cybersecurity against potential threats from increased digital connectivity. Success will
position Brookhaven National Laboratory at the forefront of grid modernization, supporting
institutional priorities in renewable expansion, resilience, and infrastructure security.

The line thermal rating is determined by assessing the maximum temperature the line can
withstand without exceeding permitted clearance limits. The standard method for calculating the
current-temperature relationship relies on the conductor’s heat balance, as outlined in the
Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers Standard. The standard DLR calculation
traditionally overlooks the radial thermal gradient, presuming surface and core temperatures to
be identical. However, more recent models that incorporate icing scenarios have extended this
assumption to consider the surface and ice layer temperatures as equal. This simplification is
practical for some analyses, but as the ice layer thickness escalates, these assumptions grow
increasingly inaccurate, potentially yielding line rating estimates substantially larger than the
actual rating, thus jeopardizing the integrity of the lines. Therefore, we proposed a way to
differentiate the temperatures during the ice accumulation. The temperature of the ice layer is
derived from the ambient temperature based on the amount of ice accretion on the conductor.
Then, the temperature of the conductor’s surface can be determined by assessing the rates of heat
loss at the interface between the conductor surface and the ice layer, as well as at the interface
between the ice layer and the surrounding ambient air. Finally, the differentiation between the
conductor core temperature and the surface temperature is achieved by considering the radial
thermal gradient. Moreover, in our model, the rate of mass accumulation on the line due to
precipitation is considered using the Makkonen model, which is a function of including
parameters such as precipitation rate and relative humidity.

 39

In regions with substantial wind generation, strong wind speeds naturally cool transmission lines,
enabling them to support higher capacity when DLR is applied. This increased capacity helps
alleviate congestion and enhances the efficiency of electricity dispatch as demand continues to
grow. By implementing DLR on key transmission corridors, wind farm curtailment can be
minimized, maximizing distributed energy resources (DER) utilization. To address this, the
project introduces a power flow tracing algorithm to systematically identify and prioritize critical
transmission lines that predominantly carry wind-generated power. These selected lines undergo
DLR implementation, thereby accommodating increased capacity and deferring costly
infrastructure upgrades. Additionally, to maximize cost savings from deferred upgrades, we
propose reinvesting in solar DERs. Integrating solar energy diversifies the regional energy mix
and serves as a complementary solution to DLR, particularly during hot, sunny conditions when
DLR effectiveness is reduced. Deploying solar DERs on the distribution side not only alleviates
transmission congestion but also fosters community solar participation, enhancing grid flexibility
and resilience. The tool has been rigorously tested on a modified test system based on the

University.

existing methods. Results indicate that previous models tend to overestimate line capacity, while

wind power flows. The approach was tested on a modified Western Kansas system, with
estimated cost savings of $43.5M from implementing DLR on four selected lines, based on
recent regional transmission project costs.

Finally, we also developed an AI/ML-based real-time DLR estimation and forecast tool based on
time-series meteorological data and line conditions and properties, a dynamic neural network to

affect DLR to constrain DLR predictions. In addition, we have developed cyber-secure methods

Skyrmions are topologically protected spin textures characterized by definite spin vorticity and

 An Advanced X-Ray Scattering Technique for Microelectronics: Finding a Needle in a
 Haystack
 Principal Investigator: Yuan Gao

PURPOSE:
The goal of this project is to develop a high-throughput, high-spatial-sensitivity examination
method for in-line characterization of as-fabricated photoresist patterns and integrated chips.
This approach aims to bridge the gap in current X-ray metrology methods, significantly
enhancing Brookhaven National Laboratory’s (BNL) metrology capabilities for microelectronics
while fostering collaboration with research institutions and industry leaders such as the National
Institute of Standards and Technology and IBM. Successful execution of this research will
further establish BNL's leadership in metrology and increase the potential for securing external
funding through the National Semiconductor Technology Center.

Current X-ray metrology methods for microelectronics—namely critical-dimension small-angle
X-ray scattering (CD-SAXS) and ptychographic tomography—have notable limitations. While
CD-SAXS is widely used for in-line metrology in fabrication facilities to extract global structural
profiles, it lacks sensitivity to stochastic defects that are nanometer-sized and randomly
distributed. Conversely, ptychographic methods leverage coherent hard X-ray scattering to
achieve three-dimensional imaging of integrated circuits (IC) with nanometer spatial resolution.
However, due to stringent measurement conditions and a time-consuming image reconstruction
process, the practical imaging volume of ptychographic tomography is limited to just a few tens
of micrometers. These methods not only target different types of information but also differ in
spatial resolution and imaging volume by over three orders of magnitude, making their
integration into a practical characterization workflow challenging.

To bridge these approaches and develop a comprehensive, implementable metrology workflow,
we propose an advanced coherent SAXS technique. This method aims to efficiently examine an
as-fabricated 300 mm wafer and detect scattered, nanometer-sized stochastic defects. By raster
scanning a micron-sized coherent X-ray beam across the wafer, we can encode the structural
details of individual defects in far-field coherent scattering patterns. To achieve the required
throughput, we will employ machine learning methods to directly process reciprocal space
scattering patterns, identifying defect locations without the need for time-consuming real-space
imaging reconstruction.

• Neural Network for Defect Detection: We have developed a neural network capable of
 identifying abnormal coherent scattering patterns—those indicating defects in ICs—from a
 vast dataset of normal patterns from as-designed ICs. The network is implemented in a
 semi-supervised framework, enabling it to function as an effective binary discriminator even
 for highly biased datasets.

 The approach incorporates a novel process, named “dynamical relabeling,” which trades
 reduced reliance on trusted data labels for increased computational cost. As a result, the

 45

 method can be applied to datasets with low-confidence initial labels, eliminating the need
 for expensive generation of high-quality labeled or simulated training data.

 The network has demonstrated promising performance on simulated coherent scattering
 datasets generated from IC phantoms, achieving a defect detection rate greater than 95%
 after accounting for realistic experimental conditions such as measurement noise and sample
 positioning errors. A manuscript describing the network development is currently under
 review.

• Simulation of Grazing-Incidence (GI) Scattering: The network described above can, in
 principle, be applied to any scattering geometry; therefore, a GI forward model is not strictly
 required for this project. However, we believe that developing a rigorous GI propagation
 model—one that is more accurate than the widely used multislice method—is of
 fundamental scientific interest.

 To this end, we are developing a model named “ConvexPoly,” which incorporates Fresnel
 reflections at interfaces. This approach improves the accuracy of both reflectivity and
 transmissivity calculations and explicitly accounts for the polarization of the incident beam.
 Prototype test cases show a substantial reduction in reflectivity error—from ~10% with the
 multislice method to <0.1%. The model also accurately predicts the Brewster angle for p-
 polarized incident beams, which is completely neglected in the multislice approach.

• Test Pattern Design and Experimental Validation: The test pattern samples are currently
 under fabrication. We have received the first batch (i.e., partially fabricated samples) and

holding fields that preserve atomic spin orientation. The objective of this project is to evaluate

The proposed holding-field system consists of eight spherical permanent magnets positioned at

orientation of each magnet using a three-degree-of-freedom (3-DoF) gear mechanism, magnetic
fields of arbitrary direction can be generated at the center of the system. Crucially, the field

modeling. A dedicated laboratory setup was designed and assembled to characterize 50 mm

analytical and numerical calculations were developed to model the magnetic field generated by
spherical dipole magnets and to establish a framework for simulations of the future eight-magnet
system.

The magnet support structure and a prototype 3-DoF spherical gear assembly were designed in
Fusion 360 and Autodesk Inventor and fabricated using three-dimensional printing. The test
apparatus was used to characterize single-magnet dipole fields. MATLAB and Python scripts
were used for data analysis, field mapping, and comparison with theoretical predictions.

A magnetic-field characterization system was designed, fabricated, and commissioned. The
system securely housed a spherical NdFeB magnet and provided controlled positioning of the
three-axis Hall probe. Measurements of the three magnetic-field components were performed
over distances ranging from direct contact with the magnet surface to approximately 14 cm.
Repeated measurements demonstrated good reproducibility and confirmed that the magnetic
field exhibits the expected dipole-like behavior. Comparison of the measured data with
theoretical calculations showed agreement with the expected inverse-cubic field decay
characteristic of magnetic dipoles. These measurements also established a quantitative basis for
evaluating systematic effects associated with magnet positioning and orientation.

electrons that drift toward a readout electrode. However, the amplification process at the
electrode also generates a flux of positively charged ions that drift back into the detector volume.
This reverse flow of ions distorts the electric field inside the detector, degrading the precision of
particle tracking and position measurement. The goal of this project was to develop and test a
proof-of-concept solution by implementing a passive bi-polar grid (BPG) - a specially designed
electrode structure that suppresses the backward flow of ions without significantly reducing the
signal from the ionization electrons - integrated with a GridPix detector, a highly pixelated
gaseous detector technology developed at Bonn University. Success in this project would
directly influence the future design of next-generation drift chambers for nuclear physics
experiments.

Drift chambers are widely used in particle and nuclear physics to reconstruct the trajectories of
charged particles produced in collisions. The GridPix detector is a state-of-the-art variant that
uses a fine (55 μm) pixel grid for readout, offering exceptional spatial resolution. Ion back-flow
is a long-standing challenge in such detectors, particularly for experiments requiring continuous
or high-rate operation. The bi-polar grid concept - a passive electrode with alternating polarity
segments placed above the readout - offers a potential passive solution that does not require
active electronics.

The project was carried out in three stages. First, a detector setup was assembled at Brookhaven
National Laboratory (BNL) combining the GridPix detector - provided by Bonn University -
with a bi-polar grid and field-shaping electrodes inside a gas-filled enclosure. Electrons for
testing were generated above a stainless-steel slit (2 cm long, 100 μm wide) placed 12 mm above
the readout to provide a controlled, uniform source of ionization. The gas mixture used was
argon and isobutane (Ar/iC₄H₁₀ 95:5) at slightly above atmospheric pressure.

Second, simulations of electron drift, diffusion, gas attachment, and BPG transparency were
developed using Garfield++, an established particle detector simulation framework. This work
was carried out in collaboration with a summer undergraduate student (Theodore Alexis) through
BNL’s Science Undergraduate Laboratory Internship program. Simulations were produced for
both 0 T and 1.2 T magnetic fields oriented along the electric field direction, and for two gas
mixtures. Third, a Python-based graphical user interface (GUI) integrating high-voltage control
and data acquisition (DAQ) was developed to automate measurements planned for operation
inside a magnet.

Initial measurements of electron diffusion were performed successfully. With 5,000 randomly
triggered frames recorded, the spatial distribution of electron arrival positions at the GridPix
readout was mapped and analyzed. Noisy pixels were identified and removed from the data, and

the electron position distributions were compared to simulation results from Garfield++. The
simulations matched the experimental data well and additionally predicted millimeter-scale shifts
in the reconstructed electron position when the BPG is placed above the GridPix. Simulations
were extended to a second gas mixture, argon, carbon tetrafluoride, and isobutane (Ar/CF₄/iC₄H₁₀
75:20:5), and for several values of the electric field applied to the BPG, providing guidance for

 LDRD 25-043 Subtask 5
 Thermal Characterization of High-Power Cyclotron Targetry
 Principal Investigator: Jonathan Morrell

PURPOSE:
The 19 MeV cyclotron at the Medical Isotope Research and Production (MIRP) facility is
expected to produce high-purity actinium-225 (Ac-225) for the treatment of cancer by irradiating
radium-226 (Ra-226) salt targets with a proton beam. Ac-225 is an alpha-emitting isotope with
potential for targeted alpha therapy, in which radiation is delivered directly to cancer cells while
sparing healthy tissue. Producing useful quantities requires using high beam currents, sustained
over multiple days. Successful production requires safely dissipating the heat of the incident
beam and understanding the thermal limitations of the target. The beam deposits kilowatts of
power into a salt layer only fractions of a millimeter thick; if the target overheats it can melt or
decompose, risking the release of radioactive material. We proposed to establish a safe operating
beam current by developing a computational thermal model, coupled with a benchtop
experiment. The major uncertainties were the thermal properties of radium salt compounds,
which have never been directly measured and that must be estimated from related materials, and
the boiling heat transfer behavior in the target cooling channel.

The target assembly consists of a thin radium salt layer sandwiched between a helium-cooled
cover plate and a water-cooled backing plate, with the beam passing through upstream vacuum
windows before reaching the salt. Several aspects of this assembly are fixed (the beam diameter,
the cooling geometry, and the vacuum isolation windows), while the salt compound, target
inclination angle, structural materials, and mass (limited to 300 mg Ra-226 at this facility) can all
be varied. To identify the parameter combination that maximizes Ac-225 yield without
overheating, we developed a one-dimensional (1D) thermal model of the target stack. Because
radium is rare, expensive, and highly radioactive, its thermal properties have never been directly
measured and must be estimated from analogous barium compounds and computational
databases; these estimates are the largest source of modeling uncertainty.

Co-PI Wilson Lin designed and built a benchtop Temperature Coefficient of Resistance (TCR)
measurement apparatus: two copper rods with a controlled heater, a cooling fan, six
thermocouples and a calibrated pressure sensor. The thermal resistance at the contact interface
was extracted from steady-state temperature measurements at different pressures and compared
against the model.

To predict peak temperatures, the model must capture the beam energy deposition profile,
conduction through layers with poorly known thermal properties, and boiling heat transfer at the
cooling surface. The 1D model solves for the temperature profile through the target stack on a
fine spatial grid. The volumetric heating rate at each point is determined from the proton
stopping power, calculated using the Anderson and Ziegler formalism as implemented in the
open-source Python code Curie. The temperature distribution is then obtained by solving the heat
equation iteratively to account for temperature-dependent thermal conductivities. TCR at the
salt-backing interface is modeled with the Antonetti correlation, and boiling heat transfer at the
cooling surface with the Jens-Lottes correlation. The critical heat flux (CHF) was also estimated
at approximately 5 MW/m2 from the Zuber correlation, adjusted for the subcooled flow

 59

conditions (~120 °C subcooling). At the expected operating current, the slanted target produces a
peak wall heat flux of roughly 2 MW/m2, a factor-of-two margin. For a perpendicular target, the
smaller beam footprint raises this substantially: the CHF is reached at approximately 35 μA at 90
degrees, compared to about 100 μA at 20 degrees.

To validate the contact resistance component of the model, TCR measurements were performed
on a copper-copper interface, since target materials were not available at the time. The two rods
were brought into contact at a controlled pressure and heated to steady state. Six thermocouples
measured the temperature gradient along each rod, and the thermal resistance was extracted from
the temperature discontinuity at the interface. With this apparatus, the measured TCR ranged
from approximately 13 cm2K/W at 30-80 kPa down to about 6 cm2K/W at 160 kPa, consistent
with model predictions. Measurements with target-relevant materials are currently being
planned. In a real target, thermal compounds or indium foil could be utilized to reduce the
contact resistance, which could be quantified using this new apparatus.

The target design was then optimized across four radium compounds (oxide, chloride, carbonate,
and nitrate), inclination angles from 10 to 90 degrees, and masses up to 300 mg. Ac-225 yield
was calculated from the energy-dependent reaction cross section (TENDL nuclear data library)
folded with the proton flux through the salt. The figure of merit was maximum yield after a five-
day irradiation, subject to peak temperature remaining below two-thirds of the salt melting point.
It was found that a 20-degree inclination was optimal for all four target materials. Shallower
angles spread the beam over a larger area, reducing peak heat flux. However, this benefit is
counteracted by the longer path through the salt, increasing volumetric energy deposition.
Radium chloride (RaCl2) was identified as the preferred compound; radium oxide has higher
thermal conductivity and melting point, but peak temperatures approached 1130 °C, which
would damage surrounding components. Among structural materials, aluminum produced the
lowest peak salt temperatures due to its high thermal conductivity, despite its lower melting point
relative to Inconel or niobium. With the recommended configuration of a 20-degree slant, RaCl2,
aluminum components, and approximately 290 mg Ra-226, the predicted beam current limit was
found to be roughly 40 μA.

The modeling tools developed in this project informed operating parameters and safety
considerations for the first beam-on-target test at the MIRP cyclotron, conducted in early FY26
with a copper test target. The 1D model provides a foundation for three-dimensional
computational fluid dynamics simulations, planned in collaboration with Los Alamos National
Laboratory, and can be adapted for other isotope production targets as new campaigns are
planned in the future.

 Principal Investigator: Gabriele D’Amen

PURPOSE:
The purpose of this project is to characterize the impact of high-energy deposition on a class of
catastrophic failures affecting silicon detectors called Single Event Burnout (SEB). SEB is one of
the main limitations to the usage of silicon detectors in high-radiation environments, as it often
results in the destruction of the sensor. This effect has important consequences in the High
Luminosity-Large Hadron Collider experiments.

The investigation focuses on Low Gain Avalanche Diodes (LGAD), which are essential for high-
resolution four-dimensional (4D) timing applications in next-generation high-energy physics and
nuclear science experiments.

To simulate long-term operational wear, the silicon sensors undergo heavy pre-irradiation at the
Rhode Island Nuclear Science Center reactor using 1 MeV neutrons, reaching a severe fluence of
1.5e15 neq/cm2. Following irradiation, the sensors are integrated into custom-designed readout
boards engineered to handle stable power delivery and high-speed data transmission under active
beam exposure.

The core of the experimental setup leverages Brookhaven National Laboratory’s (BNL) Tandem
Van de Graaff accelerator facility. This facility enables the sensors to be exposed to a diverse
matrix of beam species and energies specifically chosen to deliver high amounts of localized
energy deposition into the silicon substrate. The experimental matrix was expanded to map
sensor responses across five distinct ion species: 28 MeV protons, carbon, oxygen, iron, and gold
ions. By systematically varying the internal electric field of the sensors up to and beyond the
nominal operation safety margins, the exact critical threshold voltage required to trigger
catastrophic SEB failure is determined dynamically as a function of particle species and
localized ionization density.

Significant technical milestones have been achieved, culminating in the complete construction
and commissioning of the described test-beam setup. The custom readout infrastructure,
mounting hardware, and specialized data acquisition systems were successfully deployed at the
BNL Tandem Van de Graaff facility. Utilizing this framework, the experimental team
successfully executed the full irradiation campaign, mapping out the precise mortality profiles of
more than 70 LGAD sensors under bombardment from protons, carbon, oxygen, iron, and gold
ion beams.

The resulting dataset has yielded a comprehensive characterization of energy-dependent sensor
mortality. We observed and measured a wide spectrum of mortality effects caused by particle
interactions, including SEB, permanent radiation damage, and fatalities caused by the collapse of
the sensor electric field. Under highly ionizing heavy ion fields, the observed phenomenology
proved to be distinct from the one observed for interactions with protons.

 71

The completed analysis highlights distinct, species-dependent failure signatures. The localized
charge plasma density generated by heavier ion tracks (such as iron and gold) initiates localized
thermal runaway and immediate breakdown deep within the high-field multiplication layer of the
sensor. In contrast, lighter ions and non-relativistic protons exhibit a more gradual, voltage-
dependent onset of breakdown, delineating clear physical differences in how different heavy ion
species and protons deposit energy and the resulting structural damage.

Based on these findings and the complete mapping of the SEB critical threshold boundaries, a
comprehensive paper was successfully submitted for review in April 2026 ([2604.11571]
Mortality of ultra-thin LGADs and PiN diodes from high energy deposition). This publication
provides the international physics community with the fundamental data and predictive
parameters necessary to safely operate 4D-tracking systems in future hadron colliders and high-
radiation experimental environments.

The objective of this project is to develop a proof-of-concept foundation model (FM) for

artificial intelligence (AI) to the next level—from large language models (LLM) to foundational

fundamentally from natural language: detector hits are sparsely distributed in three-dimensional

funding opportunities in AI for science.

We adapted the auto-regressive training paradigm, such as the foundation of GPT-like LLMs, to
particle physics detector data. The primary technical challenges center on two aspects. The first
is token generation: converting sparse detector hits into meaningful tokens. We explored
centroid-based clustering, grid-based 3D partitioning, and graph neural networks (GNN) to
process variable-sized hit groups into fixed-size token representations. The second challenge is
sequence ordering: we ordered tokens by radial distance from the collision center, which is
particularly intuitive in polar coordinates where charged-particle trajectories form locally
continuous structures. Relative positional prompting is applied to handle discontinuities between
particle tracks. To validate the FM’s generalization capability, we selected time projection
chamber (TPC) track pattern recognition in sPHENIX as the primary benchmark downstream
task—a computationally expensive and well-established challenge in nuclear and particle physics

 LDRD 25-046 Subtask 8
 Single-particle Density Matrix Functional Via Machine Learning and the Discrete Action
 Theory
 Principal Investigators: Niraj Aryal and Chris Marianetti

PURPOSE:
The goal of this project was to explore whether machine learning methods can enable an efficient
single-particle density matrix functional theory (SPDMFT) for strongly correlated materials. In
particular, the project focused on using neural network approximations within the Variational
Discrete Action Theory (VDAT) framework to capture Mott and Hund physics in multi-orbital
Hubbard models. This is a highly exploratory problem as no existing SPDMFT approximation
has successfully captured the Mott metal-insulator transition, even in simple model systems.
Therefore, demonstrating that machine learning methods can accurately learn the finite-qubit
problems arising in VDAT would represent an important feasibility milestone.

Strongly correlated materials remain a major challenge for first-principles materials theory.
Standard density functional theory (DFT) is often insufficient for such systems whereas more
accurate calculations such as dynamical mean-field theory are computationally costly for high-
throughput materials-discovery workflows. VDAT developed by Professor Marianetti’s group
offers a promising alternative. VDAT is a variational many-body framework that can be
systematically improved by adding higher-order terms. Of particular importance to this project,
the N = 3 VDAT ansatz can be mapped onto an effective finite-qubit problem. For multi-orbital
Hubbard models relevant to d- and f-electron systems, this mapping leads naturally to 10- and
14-qubit problems, respectively. Efficiently solving these interacting qubit problems is therefore
central to making VDAT computationally practical.

The Columbia team, led by Professor Chris Marianetti, developed the VDAT formalism and
generated finite-qubit datasets from effective spin-model calculations. The Brookhaven National
Laboratory (BNL) team, led by Principal Investigator Aryal, focused on the machine learning
component.

Our initial machine learning studies began with controlled finite-qubit test problems. In the
simplest benchmark, we considered a four-qubit model corresponding to a two-orbital Hubbard
model at half-filling. The inputs consisted of six exchange parameters and four onsite magnetic-
field or magnetization values, while the outputs were six spin correlators.

We first evaluated standard regression models, including decision trees and random forests, as
computationally inexpensive baselines. We then trained feedforward neural networks and
optimized their architecture. These tests were followed by more physics-informed strategies such
as mixture-of-experts and active-learning strategies, as well as graph-based representations to
map the spin problem.

During the project, the BNL group developed and benchmarked several machine learning
surrogate models for finite-qubit problems arising in the VDAT framework. The central
technical question was whether machine learning methods can accurately and efficiently learn

 86

the solution of interacting quantum spin models, thereby avoiding repeated expensive qubit
calculations inside the VDAT workflow.

As an initial benchmark, we trained models on a four-qubit ZZ-interaction problem. The dataset
used the six exchange parameters and four onsite field or magnetization values as inputs, and the
six spin correlators as outputs. Baseline decision-tree and random-forest models achieved mean-
squared errors below 10-3, demonstrating that even relatively simple supervised learning methods
can capture much of the structure of the finite-qubit problem.

Feedforward neural networks significantly improved the accuracy. After hyperparameter
optimization, the best neural-network model achieved a mean-squared error of ~10-5 on a dataset
of 105 samples, roughly two orders of magnitude improvement over the baseline tree-based
models.

The main limitation of the feedforward neural network appeared in the Ising-like strong-coupling
regime, where some inputs vanish and the correlator outputs approach nearly discrete values. In
this region, the regression problem effectively becomes closer to a classification problem. To
address this issue, we developed a mixture-of-experts strategy that treats the general finite-qubit
regression problem and the Ising-regime classification problem separately. In the Ising regime,
the classification model achieved greater than 99% accuracy in labeling the output values,
indicating that targeted physics-informed model decomposition can correct the worst neural
network predictions.

We also explored active-learning strategies to improve data efficiency and reduce model error in
difficult regions of parameter space. The active-learning approach was designed to identify
where the current model performs poorly and selectively enrich the training set in those regions.
This strategy is especially important for scaling to larger qubit problems, where exhaustive data
generation becomes increasingly expensive.

Although the initial work focused on four-qubit and up-to-six-qubit datasets, the project
established a clear route toward the larger 10- and 14-qubit problems needed for realistic d- and
f-electron multi-orbital Hubbard models. The graph neural network approach was also identified
as a promising next step because it can naturally encode the connectivity and interaction
structure of the finite-qubit problem. Preliminary comparisons indicate that graph-based models
are likely to be more data-efficient and scalable than fully connected neural networks as the
number of qubits and interaction parameters increases.

Overall, the project demonstrated that machine learning surrogate models can learn finite-qubit
problems relevant to VDAT with high accuracy, while also revealing the need for physics-
informed strategies such as mixture-of-experts models, active learning, and graph neural
networks. The immediate next step would be to integrate the trained machine learning solver into
the VDAT workflow and assess the resulting computational speedup for simple d- and f-electron
systems. The results provide a strong foundation for future work on machine-learned solvers for
VDAT and, more broadly, for efficient beyond-DFT simulations of strongly correlated materials.

transition metal catalysts to mediate the multi-electron reduction of N≡N (N2), forming metallo

independence.

electron transfer (PCET) offers a promising, atom-economical approach for designing efficient
N₂ reduction processes. This is a compelling strategy for synthesizing “non-classical” chemical
bonds, as it enables the simultaneous transfer of a proton and an electron to a substrate in a single
elementary step. This approach harnesses energy from visible light instead of relying on external
chemical reagents, significantly reducing waste and minimizing chemical overpotential. By
leveraging light as an energy source, PCET offers a more efficient pathway for ammonia
production, aligning with the goals of sunlight-driven chemical processes. The specific aims of
this project are:

• Identifying molecular systems that activate and render N2 splitting. Initial efforts to realize
 this objective will focus on identifying transition metal complexes that can bind dinitrogen

 carbene-containing ligands. Pilot efforts will focus on utilizing molecular systems involving
 these metals. These results will inform the design of platforms featuring earth-abundant
 transition metal analogues (vanadium, chromium, manganese, and iron). These molecules

 visible light to facilitate the cleavage of the N≡N bond resulting in the formation of metallo
 nitride, M≡N.

 low-valent metal center, the next step typically involves a net hydrogen-atom transfer (HAT)

 91

 or PCET step. Pioneering studies have shown that the first HAT step is often the most
 challenging and requires HAT donors with low bond dissociation free energies (BDFE). This

 that spontaneously loses H2 when the BDFE<49 kcal/mol. Thus, an ideal PCET donor should
 feature a low BDFE E−H (E = heteroatom) bond that quickly performs a net HAT reaction to
 the metal nitride bond.

 Delineating key steps in the catalytic cycle will involve isolating or trapping key catalytic

 LDRD 25-052 Subtask 2
  Enabling Studies of Materials at Ultra-high, Fusion-relevant Temperatures at NSLS-II
 Principal Investigator: Mehmet Topsakal

PURPOSE:
Fusion energy holds promise as a potential solution to humanity’s rapidly growing energy
demands and will provide an almost limitless, large-scale solution to replace fossil fuels and
meet base load energy demand under all environmental conditions. Fusion reactions occur in a
form of plasma made of charged particles. The plasma needs to stay extremely hot - over
millions of degrees Celsius - and condensed for the duration of the reaction. Several plasma
generating devices such as tokamaks have already been designed and are being actively
developed to achieve and sustain fusion reactions at a scale that will enable the study of a
controlled “burning” (self-heating) plasma. However, achieving sustained fusion reactions is
only one piece of the puzzle. To harness fusion as a practical energy source, several key
challenges must be addressed. The intense flux of high-energy neutrons and other particles
generated during fusion reactions subject reactors’ structural materials to extreme conditions.
Finding materials capable of withstanding these conditions while maintaining structural integrity
is a top priority. On the other hand, being able to study and understand the behavior of existing
materials - such as tungsten and its alloys or novel materials such as high-entropy alloys that
haven’t been discovered yet - at ultra-high temperatures relevant to fusion applications is another
challenge. Overcoming these challenges requires highly specialized and unique characterization
tools - such as synchrotrons - which offer focused and high-intensity beams resulting in superb
spatial resolution and sensitivity, along with fast data collection and non-destructive analysis.

In fusion science, tungsten is considered as a critical material due to its exceptionally high
melting point (3422°C), making it the leading candidate for components like the “divertor” in a
fusion reactor, which directly interacts with the extremely hot plasma, effectively acting as an
exhaust port to manage heat and impurities. To study the performance of tungsten or other
candidate materials in fusion applications in real time by utilizing synchrotron characterization
resources, we need to create temperature conditions as high as possible. Existing sample-heating
equipment at most National Synchrotron Light Source II (NSLS-II) beamlines is limited to the
1000-2000°C temperature range, which is well below the melting point of tungsten. Higher
temperatures can only be achieved through laser heating. This project aimed to develop a
compact laser-assisted heating system - primarily for the X-ray Powder Diffraction beamline but
also suitable for, among others, the Pair Distribution Function and High Energy Engineering X-
ray Scattering beamlines - that will enable studies of materials at fusion-relevant temperatures
(>3000oC) by using advanced synchrotron-based characterization techniques as presently
available at NSLS-II beamlines.

Commercial-scale energy generation through fusion might be a few decades away. However,
research and development in this promising field is expected to grow exponentially in the
coming years. This project enabled us to work on a proof-of-concept laser heater system that
consisted of four major components: a variable power laser source; an infrared temperature
sensor; sample translation/rotation stages; and an imaging camera. After extensive vendor search
and safety considerations, the vendors of these components were identified, and all necessary
equipment was procured. This new capability will strengthen NSLS-II and hence Brookhaven

 97

National Laboratory (BNL) in this rapidly evolving fusion research by attracting new scientists
to BNL for pioneering experiments and fostering new collaborations between BNL scientists and
other labs such as Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory. This new instrument will not only be
beneficial for fusion researchers, but it will also be useful for other researchers who are looking
for rapid heating of their samples in applications, such as sustainable steelmaking or accident-
tolerant nuclear fuels for advanced nuclear reactors.

 LDRD 25-052 Subtask 4
  Integration of Drone System (UAS) with Quadrupedal Robot (UGS) for Collaborative
 Operations
 Principal Investigator: Odera Dim

PURPOSE:
The objective of this effort was to evaluate integration and collaboration between a selected
Unmanned Ground System (UGS) and Unmanned Aerial System (UAS) system for nuclear
security applications. The prototype system consisted of a Spot robot (i.e., UGS) and a ModalAI
drone (i.e., UAS). Based on literature reviewed online at the time of this work, there was no
commercial vendor that provided a commercial off-the-Shelf solution for integrating them. Some
of the options which seemed like possible solutions had an associated hefty price tag due to
additional features we did not need. Additionally, our goal was to provide a simple frame of
reference that would enable coordinated operation of the two robotic systems. Successful
integration of a UAS into Spot’s GraphNav coordinate frame would directly enable scalable,
multi-agent ground-air operations for radiation monitoring, inspection, and emergency response,
significantly expanding coverage, speed, and resilience beyond single-platform deployments.
From an institutional perspective, this capability would position Brookhaven National
Laboratory to lead future Department of Energy/National Nuclear Security Administration
programs in autonomous nuclear security systems, strengthen its UGS/UAS research portfolio,
and support long-term strategy around artificial intelligence-enabled robotics, resilient sensing,
and reduced human exposure in high-consequence environments.

The central objective is to enable the drone to reliably express its pose relative to Spot’s
GraphNav coordinate frame, laying the groundwork for coordinated multi-agent missions in
complex or hazardous environments. The drone will perform pre-route scans that improve the
efficiency and safety of Spot’s ground-based inspections, while also serving as a secondary
inspector with an elevated perspective and additional insights based on multimodal sensor data.

Accurate relative localization is essential to ensure coordination between the UGS and UAS
platforms. The project explored multiple integration pathways. This included leveraging the
drone’s Visual-Inertial Odometry (VIO), tracking cameras and inertial measurement unit,
supplemented with Spot’s existing GraphNav system for global reference via fiducial marker(s).
The target operational threshold is 5-10 cm for position and ±10º for orientation. These
tolerances are consistent with reasonable values that would affect the performance of the system
when implemented for nuclear security tasks. For example, a drone with a small, high-sensitivity
detector predicting the source location and orientation with an uncertainty of ±5 cm and ±5º
respectively will be considered acceptable.

The Starling 2 Max drone is a developmental drone and requires extensive re-configuration of all
basic services, creation of new services, or modification of existing services to achieve our goal
of integrating drones within Spot’s GraphNav frame for executing collaborative missions. To
this end, some services were identified as essential and were configured and tested accordingly.
The VOXL services, such as voxl-vision-hub and voxl-mavlink-manager, were modified to
enable the detection and pose estimation of wall-mounted fiducial tags and to integrate the drone
onto Spot’s Wi-Fi network, allowing data to be streamed to a client script running on Spot’s
Core computer. The mavlink can stream health status and telemetry information, including

 100

battery state, flight status, and whether the drone is armed, but it does not allow streaming the
drone’s local or fixed pose information to a client program. To facilitate streaming of pose
information, a custom C# service, voxl-spotcore-broadcaster, was developed and deployed on the
drone. This service successfully streamed the local and global pose info to the client program
running on Spot’s Core computer. The client program on Spot’s Core computer is Python-based
and consists of a back-end server that performs all essential operations, such as streaming
images, telemetry, drone control, as well as a front-end server that hosts a web-based graphical
user interface (GUI). The GUI enables clients to view information such as images and telemetry
and to execute drone commands through a button-based interface. This user-facing component
also provides a framework for integrating more advanced supervisory control features in future
multi-agent deployments.

To evaluate the quality of pose synchronization between the drone and Spot, we performed
stability and reliability tests on the drone’s local (VIO) pose and its fixed (fiducial-based
GraphNav) pose. For the local pose tests, the drone repeatedly sampled its pose (x,y,z, yaw) at a
reference location in the test space. During each run, the drone followed a random path through
the test space before returning to the reference position, where its pose was recorded. For the
fixed pose tests, the drone first detected a fiducial marker and localized itself at various
distances. For each case, we moved the drone from the reference location, around the test space
(without the fiducial marker in the drone’s field of view) before returning it back to the reference
location. The drone’s estimated position was compared with Spot’s estimate of the drone’s
location. The reference location was chosen as the top of Spot’s body to enable direct
comparison of the robot’s GraphNav pose and the drone’s estimated pose. This process was
repeated 10 times to allow us to estimate the standard deviation from the mean behavior. Lohith
Annadevula is a co-investigator on this work.

The drone’s pose estimates in the local VIO frame were stable and highly reliable, with
positional variability of 3–5 cm in the XY plane, sub-centimeter accuracy in the Z direction, and
approximately 3.4º variation in yaw. These results meet typical precision requirements. In
contrast, pose estimates in the fixed GraphNav frame were substantially less reliable, exhibiting
significant systematic drift of 130–190 cm in position and 6–25º in yaw, along with poor
repeatability, as reflected by standard deviations of 18–50 cm in position and 4.8–18º in yaw.
This magnitude of the drift increased with the drone’s distance from the fiducial marker,
although increasing the running average parameter helped reduce it, particularly for yaw.
Collectively, these results indicate that the VIO system itself is sound, while the observed errors
stem from the drone’s fiducial detection or re-localization logic. Further refinement of the
fiducial detection and fixed-pose update algorithms will be required to bring the GraphNav pose
reliability into line with the VIO estimates.

In conclusion, our experiments demonstrate a clear and repeatable trend: when the drone
initializes its pose to the wall-mounted fiducial at close range, its estimated pose closely matches
Spot’s. However, as the distance between the drone and fiducial increases, the agreement of the
pose rapidly degrades, resulting in progressively larger pose errors. This distance-dependent loss
in localization accuracy is consistent with findings reported in the literature on fiducial-based
pose estimation, where marker-based pose and distance estimates become noisier and eventually
unreliable as the marker subtends fewer pixels and measurement/quantization errors accumulate.

 LDRD 25-053 Subtask 1
 Metamaterial Applications in 21-cm Astronomy
 Principal Investigator: Benjamin Saliwanchik

PURPOSE:
The purpose of this project was to begin to develop prototype metamaterial designs for
megahertz (MHz) frequency applications, particularly in 21 cm cosmology, planetary and stellar
physics, and earth science, and to verify these designs in electromagnetic simulations. These
simulations are useful for determining the feasibility of metamaterials in Radio Astronomy and
Earth Science fields. Successful completion of this work will enable us to begin developing
prototype materials for laboratory tests; further refine material designs through simulations and
measurements of prototypes; and develop improved antenna and radio frequency (RF) absorbers
for future ground- and space-based RF instruments, including successors to the Lunar Surface
Electromagnetic Experiment (LuSEE)-Night radio telescope. Future applications include
instruments that are currently being proposed to NASA funding opportunities.

Metamaterials are a new class of engineered materials, usually composite structures with
subwavelength features arranged in regular lattices, that exhibit novel behaviors that would not
be possible with traditional materials. This can include, for electromagnetic metamaterials,
negative relative permittivity, and/or negative relative permeability. This allows for the
construction, for example, of materials with enhanced absorption or resonances, antennas with
improved gain or efficiency, antenna arrays with reduced crosstalk, or lenses with sub-diffraction
limit imaging. The applications of metamaterials have been explored at gigahertz (GHz) and
terahertz (THz) frequencies for communications applications, but MHz frequency applications
remain largely unexplored. These materials may, however, have significant advantages for
scientific measurements performed at MHz frequencies, including 21 cm astronomy, planetary
and stellar physics, and earth science.

This project performed simulations of metamaterial absorbers tuned to 50 MHz, which is the RF
range required for detection of the 21 cm line from the Cosmic Dark Ages, the target of the
LuSEE-Night project, and successor instruments. These simulations demonstrate the potential
effectiveness of metamaterials to produce sub-wavelength, light-weight absorbers, which may be
required to reduce crosstalk in future arrays of RF receivers. Arrays of receivers are necessary to
reach the expected signal threshold of the Dark Ages signal, and crosstalk mitigation will be
necessary in such arrays to reduce systematics to required levels for signal detection. Classical
absorbers must be a significant fraction of a wavelength in size, rendering them unfeasible for
suppressing crosstalk at meter-scale wavelengths. Metamaterial absorbers offer the possibility of
absorbers significantly below wavelength scales. Initial estimates of material parameters were
computed analytically, and simulations to rigorously verify predictions were performed using
full-Maxwell electromagnetic simulation methods. Artificial Intelligence Large Language Model
methods were also employed for literature searches, brainstorming, and coding and calculations,
improving research efficiency.

Metamaterial absorbers were selected as a promising first application for radio astronomy, and a
literature search was performed to identify potential absorber geometries. Split-Ring Resonator
(SRR) style metamaterials were selected for broadband effect, and simplicity of simulations and

 102

fabrication. Initial calculations of material parameters led to a design with an SRR absorber with
unit cell consisting of two nested square split-rings, the larger with edge length 40 cm, the inner
with edge length 34 cm, both with trace widths of 2.5 cm, gap widths of 5 mm, and 300 Ohm
lumped resistors across the split ring gap. An array of these SRRs backed by a metal back plane
lambda/30 away (20 cm at 50 MHz) was calculated to produce an absorption feature from ~40-
60 MHz.

To verify this design, a Computer-aided design (CAD) was developed, and full-Maxwell
electromagnetic (EM) simulations of the system were performed. In the simulations, a sheet of
the absorptive metamaterial was placed halfway between two dipole test antennas suspended
above a dielectric ground and separated by 6 m, so that the absorber was in the far field of both
the transmit antenna and the antenna under test. The dielectric ground was modeled as a seven-
layer dielectric, with the lowest layer being infinite in depth. The material properties of the seven
layers were chosen to mimic the lunar regolith, using parameter values established by the
LuSEE-Night project. Results are expected to be similar for dielectric models corresponding to
earth soil for terrestrial radio astronomy or radar applications and will be explored in future
simulations. The S21 parameter (signal transmitted by one antenna measured by the second) was
measured with and without the absorber, to verify the absorptive properties. Performing these
simulations included developing the CAD model, writing Lua script code to create ports and
loads for the periodic elements in the metamaterial array, and running the full-Maxwell EM
simulations. The simulation showed 11 dB of signal suppression, verifying the performance of
this low-frequency absorber. This is a significant amount of signal suppression for a sub-
wavelength structure, and a significant improvement in size and weight over classical low-
frequency absorbers. Classical absorbers must be lambda/4 to lambda/2 in size, or 1.5–3m thick
for 50 MHz, whereas this metamaterial absorber is lambda/30, or 20 cm thick. This signal
suppression may be further improved by optimizing the metamaterial parameters, including SRR
unit cell and resistive loading design. Further steps would include performing parameter sweeps
in simulations to optimize the absorber design, and fabrication and laboratory verification of
material RF properties. Similar explorations of materials for other applications, such as lenses or
antennas, are also possible. A paper is in preparation describing these initial simulations and
implications for radio astronomy. These initial results imply that it is feasible to construct RF
metamaterial absorbers, which may be beneficial for future RF astronomy and Earth Science
radar systems.

 LDRD 25-053 Subtask 2
  Unlocking the Potential of RFSoC Technology for Next-Generation Radio Instruments
 Principal Investigator: Liju Philip

PURPOSE:
The goal of this project is to test a prototype backend for the Baryon Mapping Experiment
(BMX) telescope that replaces the current 8-bit digitizers with a modern Radio Frequency
System-on-Chip (RFSoC) platform (ZCU208). We are building and evaluating prototype
hardware together with interferometer firmware developed using the Collaboration for
Astronomy Signal Processing and Electronics Research (CASPER) toolflow and using the BMX
radio telescope as a real-world testbed. This work evaluates whether an integrated RFSoC design
can deliver cleaner data, handle a wider range of signal strengths, especially with radio-
frequency interference (RFI) present, and provide tighter timing and real-time processing than
the existing backend. The key outcome is working on-sky prototype hardware, CASPER-based
firmware, and a high-speed data path that enables a direct comparison with the legacy system. It
will provide a scalable backend design for BMX and future instruments, strengthen Brookhaven
National Laboratory’s expertise in RFSoC/Field Programmable Gate Array real-time Digital
Signal Processing (DSP) and high-rate data systems, and support future radio and transient
programs that need compact, powerful, and maintainable backends.

BMX operates between 1.1 and 1.5 GHz and is built to map 21 cm neutral hydrogen emission in
the local universe, which requires stable, well-calibrated spectra and reliable performance in the
presence of RFI. In the broader field, backends are shifting from older modular digitizers toward
integrated RFSoC systems because they offer better dynamic range, tighter synchronization, and
built-in real-time processing. The limitations of BMX’s current 8-bit digitizers, together with this
shift, motivated us to evaluate a ZCU208-based upgrade.

This work focuses on the full backend chain needed for BMX: higher-quality digitization, real-
time channelization and timing/synchronization, and practical integration with the existing
analog front end and data acquisition pipeline. A major part of the study is data transport, since
the upgraded backend must move high-rate data off the board reliably; for that reason we
targeted 100 Gigabit Ethernet (GbE) as the main path for sustained operation. The long-term
goal is to feed the ZCU208 data output into the existing Graphics Processing Unit (GPU)
systems and generate correlation products using GPU-based processing.

We carried out the study through system design, prototyping, and testing. We defined
requirements from the current backend, selected the ZCU208 platform, developed the
processing/streaming approach, and tested performance using known signals. We also validated
100 GbE transmission in the lab with stable end-to-end throughput, and we will use 100 GbE as
the primary data link for the upgraded BMX backend.

We developed the interferometer firmware for the upgraded backend using the CASPER
toolflow, producing a working DSP chain ready for on-sky testing. A 2-channel RFSoC
prototype was deployed and tested on site to validate the full path from digitization to
channelization and data output. The system performed well: we saw strong, expected signals
from global communication satellites (useful for verification) and clear interferometric fringes

 104

from known radio sources, showing that the backend can capture coherent sky signals and
maintain the phase and timing needed for interferometry.

In parallel, we designed and fabricated an 8-channel balun board to include all eight channels of
BMX into the upgraded digitization chain, which is a key step toward scaling to the full backend.
We also designed the high-rate data transport based on 100 GbE, in which Fast Fourier
Transform outputs are packaged as User Datagram Protocol (UDP) streams for correlation on
GPUs. This approach performs digitization and channelization on the RFSoC, while offloading
the computationally intensive correlation to GPUs, providing a practical and scalable
architecture. Taken together, the successful 2-channel on-site results, the completed 8-channel
balun hardware, and the defined 100 GbE UDP streaming design are major milestones that
reduce risk for deploying an RFSoC-based backend on BMX.

These results support the feasibility of the RFSoC approach for BMX and also open the door to
fast-transient science. The same timing capabilities that enable stable interferometry can be used
for observations of pulsars and fast radio bursts (FRB). With precise timing and coordinated
operation, BMX could be linked into the existing network of radio telescopes (such as the
Canadian Hydrogen Observatory and Radio-transient Detector [known as CHORD]) and used as
an outrigger element to help localize FRB emission.
