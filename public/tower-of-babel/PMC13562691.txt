Deciphering glutamine metabolic reprogramming: a novel therapeutic target ALDH18A1 in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Shuixian Li, Shenghan Gao, Jinsong Hu, Shuhan Lyu, Ju Yang, Chenyang Zhang, Chenshuo You, Xiuping Liu, Jigang Wang, Guoyuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) represents the most aggressive breast cancer subtype with limited therapeutic options and poor prognosis, highlighting an urgent need to identify novel metabolic vulnerabilities and prognostic biomarkers to improve patient outcomes. While glutamine metabolism has been implicated in cancer, the specific roles of glutamine metabolism-related genes (GMRGs) in TNBC remain poorly understood. Herein, through an integrating analysis encompassing multi-omics bioinformatics, consensus clustering and tumor microenvironment (TME) analysis, we established a glutamine metabolism-based prognostic classification for TNBC patients, which correlates with distinct survival outcomes and TME features. Furthermore, we identified ALDH18A1, one of the GMRGs that encodes P5CS for proline synthesis, as a novel prognostic biomarker and oncogenic driver. ALDH18A1 is overexpressed in TNBC and associated with larger tumor size, lymph node metastasis and poor survival, as well as an immunosuppressive TME. In vitro experiments confirmed ALDH18A1 activated the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, promoted the proliferation, migration and invasion of TNBC cells, and increased proline synthesis. Computational drug screening predicted six compounds with potential efficacy against ALDH18A1-high tumors. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that reprogramming of glutamine metabolism plays a crucial role in the malignant progression of TNBC and provide translational insights for precision metabolomic-immunotherapeutic strategies in ALDH18A1-high TNBC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56978-5.

Background

Breast cancer (BRCA) remains the most prevalent malignancy among women worldwide, posing a significant threat to their health1. The rising incidence of BRCA has been attributed to factors such as increased life expectancy, obesity, and improved screening practices2,3. Although overall BRCA mortality rates have declined, distant metastasis leads to a dramatic drop of 5-year survival rates (from 99% to 27%)4. Due to its high heterogeneity, BRCA is clinically classified into four major subtypes based on immunohistochemical profiles: Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2-enriched, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). TNBC accounts for 15%-20% of BRCA cases and represents an aggressive subtype characterized by early onset, high recurrence, metastasis, and mortality5. Given the lack of specific targets, chemotherapy and immunotherapy remain the primary treatments for TNBC. Although these therapies exhibit varying efficacy, drug resistance and relapse remain major challenges in clinical practice6,7. Consequently, researchers are actively investigating TNBC metabolic reprogramming to identify novel therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Metabolic remodeling, encompassing modifications in glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolism, assumes a pivotal role in the progression and metastasis of BRCA4,5,8. Glutamine, a non-essential amino acid abundant in human plasma (~ 20% of total amino acids)9, serves as a key carbon and nitrogen source for nucleotide/protein biosynthesis, TCA cycle anaplerosis, and glutathione production10–12. Many cancers, including BRCA, melanoma, and prostate cancer, exhibit glutamine addiction to sustain rapid proliferation13–16. Several inhibitors targeting glutamine metabolism have demonstrated anti-TNBC activity, either when used alone or in combination with existing therapies5. However, their clinical application encounters limitations. For instance, glutaminase inhibitor (CB-839) only benefits a specific subset of patients when used in combination with chemo-/immunotherapy17,18, whereas human Alanine/Serine/Cysteine Transporter 2 (ASCT2) inhibitor V9302, exhibits poor solubility, low delivery efficiency, and toxicity19,20. Thus, identifying novel effective therapeutic targets in glutamine metabolism for TNBC is urgently required.

Emerging evidence suggests that glutamine metabolic reprogramming in BRCA involves dynamic gene expression changes, yet the prognostic impact of glutamine metabolism-related genes (GMRGs) remains unclear. Here, we integrated bioinformatics analyses to systematically evaluate GMRG expression patterns and their prognostic significance in BRCA, and to identify specific candidate genes for further research. With immunohistochemistry and a series of functional assays, we identified ALDH18A1 as a pro-tumorigenic driver in TNBC and identified six potential drugs targeting ALDH18A1-overexpressing tumors. Our findings establish a glutamine metabolism-based prognostic framework for BRCA and provide a rationale for targeted therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Data collection and preprocessing

The genomic and clinical datasets, including transcriptomic profiles, copy number variations (CNVs), single nucleotide variants, and clinical annotations, were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas Breast Invasive Carcinoma (TCGA-BRCA) project. This cohort comprehensively represents patients with invasive breast carcinoma. Transcriptomic data were normalized and expressed as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). CNV analysis was performed using the GDC reference file corresponding to the remapped probe sets of SNP6 GRCh38. Clinical parameters included sex, age, cancer stage, molecular subtypes, survival time, and vital status. GMRGs were defined according to Gene Ontology term GO:0009064 (glutamine family amino acid metabolic process)21, and the comprehensive gene list is available in Supplementary Table S1.

Characterization of GMRGs in BRCA

GISTIC analysis was conducted on the GenePattern website22. This module defines the copy number segmentation profiles as “Deep deletion”, “Arm-level deletion”, “Diploid/normal”, “Arm-level gain” and “High amplification”. The results were then interpreted and plotted using the maftools package in R23. Circular layout was drawn with the circlizer R package24. The rest of the graphs was plotted with R packages such as ggplot2 and pheatmap25,26.

Consensus clustering

Consensus clustering was performed with ConsensusClusterPlus package, which supplies an algorithm for determining cluster count and membership based on stability evidence in unsupervised analysis27. The patients with BRCA were divided into two groups according to the expression characteristics of GMRGs by hierarchical clustering algorithm. The clustering results were then verified by principal component analysis using FactoMineR package in R28.

Tissue samples and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Nighty-five pairs of formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded TNBC specimens with matched adjacent normal tissues were obtained from Shanghai Fifth People’s Hospital. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Shanghai Fifth People’s Hospital (Approval No.: mwyjyx15), with all samples collected with informed consent from each patient or their guardian. IHC staining was performed using anti-P5CS (encoded by ALDH18A1) antibody (17719-1-AP; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA). The stained tissue slices were scored by two different pathologists blinded to patients’ clinical information. The intensity of IHC staining was scored as 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (medium), and 3 (strong). The percentage of positive cells in the whole tissue slice was recorded. Intensity score and positive rate score were then multiplied to calculate the overall score.

Cell lines

The human breast cancer cell line MCF-7, BT474, SKBR3, BT549 and MDA-MB-231, and human breast epithelial cells MCF-10A were purchased from Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 using their recommended growth media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (C04001-500; VivaCell, Denzlingen, Germany) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (C100C5; NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China). Specifically, MCF-10A cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA), MCF-7, BT474, SKBR3 and MDA-MB-231 cells were grown in DMEM medium (ThermoFisher), and BT549 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (ThermoFisher).

RNA interference

ALDH18A1-targeting siRNA (5’-CCUCAGUCGUACACAUGGCAA-3’, 5’-CGGAACCUCAAUGGAACACUU-3’, 5’-CCAUUAUUUGACCAGAUCAUU-3’) and negative control (NC) siRNA (5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′) were synthesized from Shanghai BioTNT Co, Shanghai, China. Cell transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein samples were harvested 48 h post-transfection, and knockdown efficiency was validated by western blotting analysis.

Plasmid construction and transient transfection

To construct mammalian expression vector pCMV-Myc- ALDH18A1, the full length open reading frame of ALDH18A1 was subcloned into pCMV-Myc (Clontech) with the primers for ALDH18A1 cDNA (F: 5’-ATGGAGGCCCGAATTATGTTGAGTCAAGTTTACCGCTGT-3’; R: 5’-TGGATCCCCGCGGCCTCAGTTGGTGTTTCTCTGAGGAATAGGG-3’). The construct was confirmed by DNA sequencing. For transient transfection, cells were transfected with pCMV-Myc or pCMV-Myc-ALDH18A1 with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen), and harvested at 48 h post transfection.

Western blotting

Total cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and quantified by BCA assay (Beyotime). Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk, membranes were sequentially incubated with primary antibodies and corresponding secondary antibodies. The blots were cut prior to hybridisation with antibodies during blotting. Immunoreactivity was visualized by using ECL chemiluminescent substrate (NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China). The related antibodies we used included anti-ALDH18A1 (17719-1-AP; Proteinteck), anti-phospho-AKT (9271S; Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-AKT (9272S; Cell Signaling), anti-phospho-mTOR (5536S; Cell Signaling), anti-mTOR (2983S; Cell Signaling), and anti-GAPDH (60004-1-Ig; Proteinteck).

Measurement of proline content

Cells in 10 cm dishes were digested with trypsin and 5 × 106 cells were collected for the measurement of proline content with the Proline (PRO) Content Assay Kit (AKAM003C; Boxbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Transfected cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well. Cell proliferation was assessed at 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h by adding CCK-8 reagent (NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The plates were incubated in dark at 37℃ for 2 h, and absorbance at 450 nm wavelength was measured. Independent experiments were repeated for five times.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well. After 10 days of culture, colonies were fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet, and counted. Only cell clusters containing > 50 cells were considered viable colonies.

Wound healing assays

Cells were grown to confluence in a 6-well plate, and a “wounding” line was scratched into the cell monolayer with a sterile 200-mL pipette tip. Rinse gently 3 times with PBS buffer to remove shed cell debris, followed by the addition of low-serum medium (containing 1% FBS). The width of the wound was measured under a microscope at 0 and 24 h after the scratch.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Serum-starved cells (2.0 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in the upper chamber (pre-coated with Matrigel for invasion assay). After 24-hour incubation, non-migrated/invaded cells were removed from the upper chamber with cotton swabs. Cells that migrated/invaded to the lower chamber were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and quantified by counting cells in five randomly selected microscopic fields per well.

Drug sensitivity prediction

Genomic and drug sensitivity data were downloaded from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (Release: GDSC2)29. The oncoPredict R package was used for building the drug sensitivity prediction model, with the GDSC gene expression data served as predictors and log2(IC50) values as the response variable30. The gene transcriptome data of TCGA-BRCA patients were used as the test set, and the Pearson test was used to predict the correlation between the IC50 values of 297 drugs contained in GDSC2 and gene expression.

Cell viability assay

Wild type or ALDH18A1 knockdown cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells per well, and grew overnight. Then, cells were treated with GW441756 or IOX2 (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) in gradient concentrations (0, 25, 50, 100, 150, 200µM). After cultured for 48 h, cell viability assay was performed using CCK8 assays.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in the R 4.5.1 version environment31, or GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). The significance of the differences was determined by Student t test. The statistical significance was set at a P value of < 0.05.

Additional experimental methods are described in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

As shown in Fig. 1A, most GMRGs were significantly upregulated in tumors compared to normal samples, suggesting these genes may be involved in BRCA malignant progression. Notably, GMRG expression profiles varied significantly across different molecular subtypes of BRCA (Fig. 1B).

Generation and characteristics of glutamine metabolism related subgroups in BRCA

To elucidate glutamine metabolic patterns in BRCA, we performed unsupervised consensus clustering of 71 GMRGs. The optimal cluster number (k = 2) was determined by consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF) analysis (Fig. 3A and Fig. S1A-F), dividing patients into two distinct subgroups: C1 (n = 550) and C2 (n = 529). Principal component analysis (PCA, Fig. 3B) and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (Fig. 3C) revealed significant molecular separation and survival differences between subgroups, with C1 showing poorer prognosis than C2 (P = 0.008).

Heatmap analysis demonstrated distinct GMRG expression patterns between subgroups (Fig. 3D). Notably, all TNBC cases were clustered within C1, exhibiting unique metabolic characteristics. These included upregulation of AADAT (involved in γ-aminobutyrate synthesis), ODC1 (related to polyamine metabolism), GLS (associated with glutaminolysis), and ASS1 (linked to arginine biosynthesis), as well as the downregulation of GLUL (involved in glutamine synthesis), GLUD1 (connected to glutamate catabolism), FAH (involved in tyrosine degradation), and ADSS1 (related to purine synthesis) when compared to other subtypes.

Tumor microenvironment (TME) analysis (Fig. 3E) revealed subtype-specific immune infiltration patterns. TNBC/C1 subgroup showed a decrease in eosinophils, mast cells, and neutrophils, alongside an increase in macrophages, MDSCs (Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells) and CD4 Treg, suggesting glutamine metabolic reprogramming influences immune landscape remodeling.

Thus, based on GMRGs expression profiles, we successfully stratified breast cancer patients into two distinct subtypes (C1 and C2) with significantly different prognostic outcomes, TME features and immune cell infiltration. TNBC patients were predominantly enriched in the C1 subtype, which exhibited significantly poorer overall survival.

The impact of GMRGs on patient prognosis

Through survival analysis of 71 GMRGs in the TCGA-BRCA cohort, totally six genes (TAT, GLUL, AZIN1, DDAH1, ALDH18A1, and ADHFE1) were identified whose mRNA expression associated with BRCA prognosis (Fig. 4A). High expression of TAT, GLUL, DDAH1, and ADHFE1 correlated with favorable outcomes, suggesting their tumor-suppressive roles, whereas elevated ALDH18A1 and AZIN1 levels predicted poor prognosis, indicating potential oncogenic functions of the two genes. Comparative expression analysis revealed significant downregulation of TAT and ADHFE1 in tumors compared with that of normal tissues, whereas the expression of AZIN1, DDAH1, and ALDH18A1 were upregulated (Fig. 4B). GLUL exhibited no differential expression. Taken together, overexpression of the two GMRGs, AZIN1 and ALDH18A1, was correlated significantly with poor prognosis of BRCA.

AZIN1 and ALDH18A1 play distinct roles in glutamine metabolism. AZIN1, an antizyme inhibitor, regulates polyamine homeostasis. Glutamine serves as a carbon donor for polyamine biosynthesis, potentially influencing AZIN1-mediated regulation, however the precise mechanisms remain unclear32,33. ALDH18A1 encodes pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthase (P5CS), which catalyzes glutamate-to-P5C conversion, a critical step in proline biosynthesis34,35. The enzyme participates in multiple metabolic pathways (Fig. 5A), including proline/ornithine metabolism and glutamine-family amino acid synthesis. Emerging evidence links proline metabolism to tumor progression, wherein it fuels proliferation and invasion36,37. Notably, patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models of BRCA revealed effect of the drug CB-839 (a glutaminase inhibitor) on tumor growth is correlated with glutamine-to-proline flux of the tumors, in which ALDH18A1 protein expression was upregulated35. Thus, based on the reported important functions of ALDH18A1 and our findings (Figs. 4A-B and 5A), we chose ALDH18A1 for further investigation as a promising therapeutic target in this study.

Increased ALDH18A1 expression correlated with an immunosuppressive TME and poorer prognosis

Analysis of the TCGA data revealed significantly elevated ALDH18A1 expression in multiple malignancies compared to normal tissues (Fig. 5B). In various tumors, elevated levels of ALDH18A1 showed negative correlations with regulatory T cells (Tregs), activated NK cells, and CD8 + T cells, while showing positive associations with resting memory CD4 + T cells, M2 macrophages, and naive B cells (Fig. 5C). In BRCA specifically, ALDH18A1 overexpression demonstrated strong negative correlations with Tregs, memory B cells, and activated NK cells, maintaining only a positive link with M2 macrophages, indicating a distinct immunosuppressive microenvironment.

The analysis of the UALCAN database verified the upregulation of ALDH18A1 in BRCA (Fig. 5D). Prognostic data from Oncolnc demonstrated that ALDH18A1 overexpression correlated with shorter disease-specific survival (DSS) of BRCA patients (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, we examined ALDH18A1 protein expression in different breast cancer (BC) cell lines with western blotting analysis. As shown in Fig. 5F, ALDH18A1 protein was also readily detected in the majority of BRCA cell lines examined. Compared with normal mammary epithelium cell line MCF-10A, ALDH18A1 is highly expressed in luminal A subtype-BC cell line MCF7 cells, luminal B subtype-BC cell line BT474 cells, and TNBC cell line BT549 cells, whereas no significant change was observed in HER2 + breast-cancer cell line SKBR3 cells.

IHC analysis of ALDH18A1 protein expression (Supplementary Fig. S2) in 95 surgically resected TNBC specimens were performed. As shown in Fig. 5G, ALDH18A1expressed diffusely in the cytoplasm of tumor cells, while it was only expressed at low levels in normal breast tissue. The positive staining of TNBC was significantly higher in tumors (55/95, 57.9%) than in nontumor tissues (5/95; 5.26%; P < 0.0001). Then with the correlation analysis between ALDH18A1 expression in TNBC tissues and clinicopathological features (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2), we discovered that high ALDH18A1 protein expression closely correlated with larger tumor size (P = 0.0334), lymph node metastasis (P = 0.0213), and vascular invasion (P = 0.0419). The results proved high ALDH18A1 expression correlated with TNBC progression.

The effect of ALDH18A1 on the malignant phenotype of TNBC cells

Recent studies have demonstrated that proline plays a critical role in cancer cell proliferation. Proline deprivation has been shown to suppress the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, which regulates cancer cell proliferation and survival by mediating cell cycle progression and metabolic reprogramming38–41. Our integrated analysis further revealed that TNBC exhibits distinct regulatory mechanisms in key pathways such as cell cycle control, DNA replication, and cellular senescence compared to other subtypes (Supplementary Fig. S3). To determine whether ALDH18A1 sustains the malignant phenotype of TNBC via the aforementioned mechanism, we conducted the following in vitro experiments.

First, with transient knockdown or overexpression of ALDH18A1, we assessed its impact on AKT/mTOR signaling and proline synthesis in TNBC cell lines BT549 and MDA-MB-231. As expected, transient depletion of ALDH18A1 with siRNA (Supplementary Fig. S4) significantly decreased the phosphorylation levels of AKT/mTOR (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. S5A). While ectopic ALDH18A1 overexpression with pCMV- ALDH18A1 transfection activated the signaling (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S5B). Alongside, ALDH18A1 positively regulated proline synthesis in the two TNBC cell lines (Fig. 6C). More importantly, exogenous proline can restore AKT/mTOR activation after ALDH18A1 knockdown (Fig. 6D-E and Supplementary Fig. S5C-D). Thus, ALDH18A1may activate the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway via proline synthesis.

Drug sensitivity prediction for ALDH18A1-high tumors

Using the GDSC database and oncoPredict R package, we established a drug sensitivity model by correlating gene expression (GDSC training set) with log₂(IC₅₀) values, validated against TCGA-BRCA transcriptomic data. This identified six candidate drugs targeting ALDH18A1-high tumors, including Bexarotene, BIBF-1120, GW441756, Motesanib, IOX2, and Niraparib (Fig. 8A). Notably, IOX2 and GW441756 demonstrated a marked decrease in IC₅₀ values as ALDH18A1 expression increased (P < 0.001), suggesting their potentially high sensitivity. BIBF-1120 and motesanib exhibited moderate efficacy, whereas niraparib and bexarotene displayed comparatively weaker effects. Furthermore, we tested drug response of IOX2 and GW441756 in ALDH18A1-overexpressing TNBC cells. As shown in Fig. 8B-C, the IC50 values of the two compounds in ALDH18A1- overexpressing TNBC cells demonstrated varying degrees of reduction in comparison to those of the control group.

Discussion

Glutamine, a non-essential amino acid, plays multifaceted roles in cellular metabolism. It is initially hydrolyzed by glutaminase (GLS) into glutamate, subsequently converted to α-ketoglutarate via glutamate dehydrogenase (GLUD) within mitochondria, thereby fueling the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to supply energy and biosynthetic precursors. Furthermore, the ammonia produced via glutaminolysis contributes to nucleotide and amino acid biosynthesis, and also aids in maintaining the cellular redox balance42,43. Cancer cells that proliferate rapidly often demonstrate an increased uptake of and reliance on glutamine, a condition known as “glutamine addiction,” which has been extensively documented in BRCA44. The expression patterns of GLS isoforms, GLS1 and GLS2, vary across molecular subtypes of breast cancer45. The conversion of glutamate to α-ketoglutarate occurs mainly through two pathways, one catalyzed by GLUD1/2, and the other involving various transaminases46. Notably, breast epithelial cells utilize distinct glutamate metabolic routes depending on their proliferative status47. Furthermore, glutamine and glutamate levels differ among molecular subtypes of BRCA. For instance, HER2-positive BRCA and TNBC exhibit higher glutamate and lower glutamine levels compared to ER-positive subtypes48, suggesting enhanced glutamine influx and active glutaminolysis in these aggressive subtypes. Thus, glutamine metabolic reprogramming represents a crucial metabolic adaptation in BRCA, intricately associated with molecular subtype, disease progression, and therapeutic vulnerability, highlighting its potential as a target for precision metabolism-based interventions. Through comprehensive bioinformatics and experimental validation, this study delineates the role of glutamine metabolism in TNBC, and identifies ALDH18A1 as an potential important factor connecting metabolic reprogramming, the immune microenvironment, and therapeutic susceptibility.

Initially, by multi-omics bioinformatics analysis using the TCGA-BRCA dataset, we discovered most GMRGs exhibited significantly higher expression in BRCA tissues (Fig. 1), and patients with BRCA are stratified into two subgroups (C1 and C2) with significantly different prognostic outcomes based on distinct GMRG expression patterns. All patients with TNBC were clustered in the C1 subgroup, which exhibited significantly poorer overall survival (Fig. 3). Moreover, distinct glutamine metabolism patterns are associated with distinct tumor immune microenvironments. Specifically, the C1 and C2 subgroups differed in TME features and immune cell infiltration, and the TNBC subtype showed marked differences from other molecular subtypes of BRCA with a distinct GMRG expression pattern (Fig. 4). The findings demonstrated glutamine metabolism reprogramming is intimately linked to the malignant progression of BRCA, especially the poor prognosis of TNBC, in accord with previous reports that glutamate metabolism differ among molecular subtypes of BRCA48.

Following this, we found overexpression of ALDH18A1 mRNA in BRCA was significantly associated with poor patient prognosis in TCGA-BRCA cohorts (Fig. 5D-E). Moreover, in our cohort containing 95 TNBC cases, elevated ALDH18A1 protein expression was closely associated with larger tumor size, lymph node metastasis, and vascular invasion (Fig. 5G; Table 1). ALDH18A1 increased cell proliferation and migration/invasion capabilities in vitro, as well as proline synthesis (Figs. 6 and 7). Our findings indicate that ALDH18A1 may be implicated in TNBC progression, potentially acting as a candidate oncogenic factor, which necessitates further investigation through in-vivo model (e.g., xenografts), metabolic flux tracing experiments (e.g., 13C-glutamine) and other methods to elucidate the role of ALDH18A1 in TNBC.

The oncogenic mechanisms of ALDH18A1may involve multiple aspects. It catalyzes the conversion of glutamate to pyrroline-5-carboxylate (P5C)49, thereby providing essential precursors for protein synthesis and energy sources to support the rapid proliferation of tumor cells. Additionally, we proved that ALDH18A1may regulate the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway possibly through proline synthesis (Fig. 6A-E), though direct biochemical interaction remains to be confirmed. The findings are aligned with a previous report that proline deprivation suppresses the AKT/mTOR pathway38, suggesting that targeting ALDH18A1 may suppress this key oncogenic signaling axis which warrants further study.

M2-type macrophages are immunosuppressive cells that can further suppress T cell function by secreting interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)50, while tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) are associated with improved survival in TNBC patients51. Our study showed that high ALDH18A1 expression was positively correlated with M2-type macrophage infiltration but significantly negatively correlated with the infiltration of effector immune cells such as CD8 + T cells and activated natural killer (NK) cells (Fig. 5C), which characterizes a typical immunosuppressive microenvironment. However, the correlation of high ALDH18A1 with an immunosuppressive TME, and its potential role in remodeling TME remains to be further verified with immune co-culture/localization experiments in vitro and some in-vivo models. We speculate that the specific regulatory mechanism may involve ALDH18A1-mediated promotion of proline synthesis, potentially leading to increase collagen deposition and the formation of a physical barrier, which in turn could hinder the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells, as reported by Özdemir BC et al. regarding the mechanism in pancreatic cancers52. Certainly, further investigations are needed to confirm the mechanism. Conceptually, these findings closely correspond to the mesenchymal (MES) subtype of TNBC in the “Fudan Classification”53, as this subtype is enriched in mesenchymal-like cells, exhibits low immune cell infiltration and an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and shows poor response to immunotherapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors. However, due to the lack of cohort stratification based on this classification and relevant enrichment analysis, this potential association remains interpretative which warrants further study.

Taken together, targeting ALDH18A1 may not only inhibit tumor growth and migration/invasion, but also exert a dual effect of “immune activation”, potentially providing a novel strategy for combination immunotherapy.

Finally, based on drug sensitivity prediction, we identified six potential drugs effective against BRCA tumors with high ALDH18A1 expression, which exhibited a distinct gradient of sensitivity (Fig. 8A). Two drugs, IOX2 and GW441756, which showed the highest sensitivity, were further proved to demonstrate lower IC50 values in ALDH18A1-overexpression TNBC cell lines compared with those in control cells (Fig. 8B-C). IOX2 is a selective prolyl hydroxylase 2 (PHD2) inhibitor that stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) by inhibiting PHD2 activity54, whereas GW441756 is a specific tropomyosin receptor kinase A (TrkA) inhibitor that blocks neurotrophin (e.g., nerve growth factor, NGF)-induced signaling by inhibiting TrkA activity55. Multi-target anti-angiogenic inhibitors BIBF-1120 (also known as nintedanib) and Motesanib56 displayed moderate sensitivity, while Niraparib [a poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitor]57 and Bexarotene [a retinoic acid X receptor (RXR) agonist)]58 showed relatively weak sensitivity. The biological rationale linking ALDH18A1 to these drugs, as well as their clinical utility, remains to be further elaborated and verified experimentally. We consider these results may provide some prioritized directions and a theoretical basis for the development of precision therapeutic strategies and clinical translation for TNBC patients with high ALDH18A1 expression.

This study has some limitations. The transcriptomic data utilized are obtained from the public database TCGA-BRCA. Conducting transcriptomic sequencing on our own BRCA cohort is crucial for further analysis and validation. Moreover, the precise molecular mechanisms through which ALDH18A1 modulates the immune microenvironment, for instance, via the direct impact of metabolites on immune cells, require further elucidation. Furthermore, the predicted drug sensitivities, the efficacy of IOX2/GW441756, and their synergies with chemotherapy or immunotherapy all require in vitro and in vivo experimental verification.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrated glutamine metabolism reprogramming is important in the malignant progression TNBC and identified ALDH18A1 as a pivotal oncogenic driver in TNBC. ALDH18A1 may facilitates tumor progression by orchestrating metabolic reprogramming such as proline synthesis, activating oncogenic AKT/mTOR signaling pathways, and remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment with induced M2 polarization and suppressed TILs infiltration. ALDH18A1 serves not only as a robust prognostic indicator but also as a highly promising therapeutic target. We identified several potential therapeutic agents, with the HIF-1α PHD inhibitor IOX2 and the TrkA inhibitor GW441756 as the most promising candidates, potentially providing a prioritized direction for subsequent translational research. This study provides the rationale for developing precision metabolomic-immunotherapeutic strategies tailored for ALDH18A1-high TNBC.
