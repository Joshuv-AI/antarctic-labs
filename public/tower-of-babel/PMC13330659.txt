What are the determinants of type of breast surgery following neoadjuvant chemotherapy? An analysis of breast cancer patients among Italian SENONET centers: NEOIMPACT study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConcerns remain regarding determinants of breast-conserving surgery (BCS) following NACT, particularly in presence of a pathologic complete response (pCR).MethodsRetrospective analysis of 65,926 women treated at 26 Italian Breast Centers between 2017 and 2022 using the data-warehouse, SENONET. Clinical and pathological data of patients treated with NACT were analyzed to identify factors influencing surgical decision-making.ResultsA total of 4366 BC patients received NACT, of whom 2008 women (46%) underwent BCS. A non-significant trend over time in the adoption of BCS was observed (p = 0.187). Lower rates of BCS were significantly associated with age ≤49 years, multifocal/multicentric disease, microcalcifications, presence of DCIS, tumor ≥2 cm or cN+ (p < 0.05). A pCR was achieved in 31% of patients but was not associated with higher BCS rates (p = 0.353). Among patients achieving a pCR with either unifocal or multicentric tumor, 283/783 (36%) and 104/117 (89%) women underwent mastectomy, respectively. After mastectomy, 1749/2358 patients (74%) received breast reconstruction, with an increase over time for both an immediate breast reconstruction (IBR) and a nipple-sparing mastectomy approach (p = 0.012). Age <50 years and pCR were significantly associated with IBR (p < 0.001) while cT2+ stage (p = 0.028), cN+ (p < 0.001), and age >70 years (p < 0.001) resulted negatively associated with IBR.ConclusionsBCS remains underutilized after NACT, and surgical choices continue to be predominantly guided by pre-treatment clinical features rather than tumor response. Even among patients with biologically responsive unicentric tumors and those achieving pCR a significant propensity for mastectomy persists. These findings highlight the urgent need for updated guidelines to reduce unnecessary mastectomies.

Introduction

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) has significantly evolved over recent decades and is now considered the standard of care for most patients with loco-regionally advanced breast cancer (BC) or for selected patients with early-stage and a disease with biologically aggressive features [1,2].

Today, a pathological complete response (pCR) is frequently achieved (up to 60-70% of cases), particularly for certain biological subtypes, such as triple-negative and HER2-positive disease, and it is associated with high overall survival [[3], [4], [5]] and optimal locoregional control [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]].

A natural consequence of NACT is a potential change in the surgical approach, allowing an increased adoption of breast-conserving surgery (BCS) [[6], [7], [8]] with reduced morbidity [[9], [10], [11], [12]] and improved quality of life [7,8]. Furthermore, a secondary analysis of a prospective multicenter NACT study involving 1662 women (I-SPY2) has demonstrated that BCS, if stratified by residual cancer burden, is not associated with an increased risk of locoregional recurrence or with reduced survival compared to mastectomy, at a median follow-up of 3.5 years [13].

Conversion rates to BCS-eligibility are highest for HER2+ and triple negative BC patients (from 42% to 53%) as demonstrated by several prospective trials [6,[14], [15], [16]], but this potential advantage appears to be still underutilized. In fact, an analysis of 59,568 patients from the National Cancer Database (NCDB) from the U.S. showed that rates of BCS did not change between 2010 and 2014 despite a significant increase in pCR rates from 33% to 46%, a finding that was not modified by age or molecular subtype [17]. Additionally, a recent single-center experience highlighted that nearly half of women who received mastectomy after NACT underwent a contralateral prophylactic mastectomy, and this was associated with younger age and earlier stage of disease [18].

The reasons for this apparent discrepancy are still unclear and a better understanding of the determinants of this decision process is needed [16,19]. It has been hypothesized that clinical factors at presentation (e.g. multifocal disease or nodal involvement), patient-related factors (e.g. younger age and personal preference for more radical, often bilateral, surgery) and institutional factors (e.g. treatment at low-volume hospitals) may all play a role [19]. Other studies have shown that possible determinants for choosing mastectomy include large initial tumor size, the presence of suspicious microcalcifications after NACT, and a patient-perceived cosmetic advantage [6,15,20]. At the same time, surgeons may be further influenced by pre-NACT clinical factors regardless of clinical or pathological response [21].

In this context, standardized recommendations or surgical guidelines are still lacking [22,23].

We recently studied the incidence of NACT in Italy among 24 Breast Centers participating in the national SENONET project and reported a low implementation (11%) across 58,661 cases analyzed in a 5-year period (2017-2022), although a small but significant increase was observed over time [24].

The aim of the present study is to assess the impact of NACT on the determinants of breast surgery among 26 Breast Centers participating in the SENONET project in the period 2017-2022, and to report how this evolved over time.

Materials and methods

Senonetwork represents the recognized network of Breast Centers in Italy. Participation in the network is voluntary and specific requirements are needed, including a minimum annual volume of new BC cases and a self-declared adherence to a defined set of quality standards. At present, 159 Breast Centers participate in Senonetwork, and they collectively manage the care of over 95% of women diagnosed with BC annually in Italy. In 2017, the SENONET data-warehouse was established as a national registry involving 26 high-volume Breast Centers, covering approximately 20% of the nationwide incidence of BC.

Each participating Breast Center is responsible for annually uploading patient data into SENONET data warehouse, a secure, web-based database specifically developed for this purpose. The database captures information on 279 variables for each patient record [24,25].

A retrospective analysis was performed using the SENONET data warehouse including patients with primary BC who underwent NACT between January 1st, 2017 and December 31st, 2022. The collected data included age at diagnosis, menopausal status, BRCA status, breast and axillary clinical-radiological assessments (such as tumor diameter, presence of multifocal or multicentric disease, presence or absence of microcalcifications at diagnosis, and lymph node status), tumor histological subtype, presence of a DCIS component, clinical tumor stage, pathological complete response (pCR), and the type of breast surgery performed. According to the immunohistochemical pattern, breast tumors were defined as Luminal A-like (ER +and/or PR+, HER2-, and Ki67 < 20%), Luminal B-like (ER+, PR −/+, and Ki67 ≥ 20%), HER2+ (ER negative, PR-, HER2+), triple-positive (ER+ and/or PR+, HER2+) and triple-negative (ER-, PR-, and HER2-) [26].

Data of radiologic response after NACT was not available for our cohort, therefore pCR was adopted as a surrogate marker of treatment efficacy since several studies have demonstrated that correlation between pCR and radiological assessment after NACT ranges between 75% and 85% [1,27].

Four main comparisons were conducted: (A) BCS versus mastectomy, (B) nipple-sparing mastectomy (NSM) versus other mastectomies (restricted to patients undergoing mastectomy), (C) immediate breast reconstruction (IBR) versus no reconstruction (restricted to patients undergoing mastectomy), and (D) one-stage versus two-stage reconstruction (restricted to patients undergoing IBR). For each comparison, univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were fitted. All available clinicopathological covariates (patient age, tumor and nodal stage, breast and axillary radiological assessments, tumor histological subtype, presence of a DCIS component, biological profile, year of treatment, and pathological complete response) were included in the multivariable models without variable selection. The Breast Center was included in all multivariable analyses as a categorical covariate to account for institutional clustering. Missing data in covariates were handled by case-wise deletion, and analyses were performed at the level of breast lesion.

The temporal trends for each of the four outcomes (BCS, NSM, IBR, and one-stage reconstruction) were tested by treating the year of diagnosis as a continuous variable and extracting the coefficient and p-value for year from the multivariable logistic models. Odds ratios (OR) with two-sided p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3.

Data extracted from SENONET for statistical analysis, being anonymous data, are non-subjects to GDPR rules and no other action or authorization is required from the Breast Centers that have already joined the Quality Control Project by signing the project documents and agreements.

Determinants of Breast Surgery (BCS vs. Mastectomy).

An additional analysis of the determinants of breast surgery was conducted excluding pCR as a covariate and the results were consistent with the previous findings, with the exception of patients with HER2-positive tumors, who presented a significantly higher likelihood of undergoing BCS compared to those with other biological subtypes (OR = 1.33, p = 0.03).

Overall, radiotherapy was delivered in 1761/1812 (97.2%) treated with BCS, including all patients with pCR in the breast, and in 1167/2015 (57.9%) treated with mastectomy. Among patients without pCR, RT was delivered in 1016/1510 mastectomy cases (67.3%), while in patients achieving pCR RT was delivered in 151/505 mastectomy patients (29.9%). Among ypN + patients, RT was delivered in 484/503 BCS patients (96.2%) and 728/874 mastectomy patients (83.3%).

Among patients undergoing mastectomy (n = 1732), 43% (757/1732) presented with nodal involvement. In this group, multivariate analysis demonstrated significantly higher pCR rates in HER2-positive, triple-negative, and triple-positive subtypes (OR = 19.18, 6.17, and 7.55, respectively; all p < 0.001).

Among 1732 patients undergoing mastectomy, 214 women were staged either cT1-3N + or cT3N0 and achieved a pCR, and among this group 65 cases (30%) received postmastectomy radiotherapy (PMRT).

UNIFOCAL TUMORS: Among 4256 lesions, there were 2053 patients with unifocal tumors (48%), of whom 783 (18%) achieved pCR. Although a significant increase in the BCS rate was documented in the latter group compared to all the unifocal tumors (64% vs. 42%; p < 0.001), 283/783 patients (36%) nonetheless underwent mastectomy. In the subgroup of patients with unifocal HER2+ or Triple Negative tumors who achieved a pCR, 500/783 (64%) of patients underwent BCS. Conversely, among women undergoing mastectomy, 177/1732 (10%) were diagnosed with a unifocal, HER2-positive or triple-negative tumor and achieved a pCR. In this setting, multivariate analysis revealed a significantly higher rate of NSMs (55%) compared to other mastectomy types (p < 0.001).

MULTICENTRIC TUMORS: Among patients with multicentric lesions, a pCR was achieved in 117/493 cases (24%), and a mastectomy was performed in 104 women (89%). In this setting, multivariate analysis showed a significant correlation between pCR and triple-positive and HER2-positive biological profiles (OR = 5.61, p < 0.001 and OR = 15.04, p < 0.001, respectively), along with a significant trend toward correlation with triple-negative tumors (OR = 2.54, p = 0.05). Conversely, cN + disease and the presence of a DCIS component at diagnosis were negatively associated with pCR (OR = 0.44, p = 0.017 and OR = 0.01, p < 0.001, respectively).

LOBULAR INVASIVE CARCINOMA: There were 162 women with lobular invasive carcinoma, and a mastectomy was performed in 114 cases (70%). In the subgroup of unifocal lesions only 30/70 (43%) underwent BCS.

In a multivariate analysis, there was a significant decrease in the adoption of BCS for patients with multicentric disease (OR = 0.01, p = 0.002) and initial tumor size ≥2 cm in this setting (OR = 0.11, p = 0.034).

CLINICAL T CATEGORY: Stratifying the type of breast surgery by clinical T (cT) category to test an interaction term between pCR and cT in multivariable models, across all cT categories, we documented that patients achieving pCR showed numerically higher rates of BCS. In addition, in cT2+ tumors, BCS rate increased from 38.3% in patients without pCR to 48.9% in those achieving pCR. However, mastectomy remained the most frequent surgical approach even among cT2+ responders (51.1%, 478/935). We did not find any significant interaction between pCR and pre-NACT cT category (global interaction p = 0.735).

Breast reconstruction vs. no-reconstruction

After mastectomy, 1749/2358 women (74%) received IBR. This increased significantly over time (OR = 1.12, p = 0.003) rising from 306/411 (74%) in 2017 to 273/353 (77%) in 2021 (Fig. 2, panel b).

At multivariate analysis, age <50 years and pCR were significantly associated with IBR (OR = 3.22, p < 0.001 and OR = 2.11, p < 0.001 respectively) while cT2+ stage (OR = 0.57, p = 0.028), pre-NACT clinical-radiological nodal involvement (OR = 0.53, p < 0.001), and age >70 years (OR = 0.08, p < 0.001) were negatively associated with IBR.

Nipple sparing mastectomy (NSM) vs. other types of mastectomy

Implementation of NSM increased significantly over time from 72/249 (29%) in 2017 to 94/251 (38%) in 2022 (OR = 1.17, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2, panel c).

Age <50 years (OR = 2.95, p < 0.001) and pCR (OR = 2.31, p < 0.001) were strongly associated with NSM in case of mastectomy, while axillary node involvement resulted negatively associated with NSM (OR = 0.61, p < 0.001) (Table 3). One-stage breast reconstruction vs. two-stages

There was a significant increase in the adoption of one-stage breast reconstruction after NACT over time, rising from 81/298 (27%) in 2017 to 92/206 (45%) in 2022 (OR = 1.45; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2, panel d), although we did not recognize any impact of NACT on the type of breast reconstruction adopted.

At multivariate analysis, age <50 years (OR = 0.66, p = 0.002) triple-negative biological profile (OR = 0.63, p = 0.005), pCR (OR = 0.57, p < 0.001) were negatively associated with two-stages breast reconstruction. Conversely, axillary lymph node involvement (cN+) increased significantly the likelihood of two-stages breast reconstruction (OR = 1.44, p = 0.003).

Discussion

Our study confirms that, despite the recognized potential benefits of NACT in facilitating BCS, this remains largely underutilized in this real-world analysis which represents one of the largest national reports in the literature on this issue, to date [1,5,24,28].

We found that among 4366 patients treated with NACT, only 46% of women underwent BCS, and this proportion did not significantly increase over the study period despite improvements in neoadjuvant therapies and higher pCR rates. We found that in addition to multifocality/multicentricity, both younger age and more advanced clinical stages of disease at presentation were associated with mastectomy, a finding already suggested by a systematic review of 25 studies published in 2018 [29]. This suggests that surgical practices still remain heavily influenced by pre-treatment clinical features, despite a clinical and radiological response after NACT.

In our cohort, a pCR was achieved in 31% of cases, which is consistent with the reported finding in contemporary meta-analyses for a heterogenous biological population [1,3]. However, pCR did not translate into higher BCS rates, despite recent evidence that patients achieving a complete response can safely undergo BCS without compromising oncological outcomes [13,28,30]. A recent retrospective study from the EUSOMA database on 1416 women surgically treated after NACT at 55 certified Breast Centers across 14 European countries reported that a mastectomy was performed in 32.5% of cases, and its incidence increased over the study period, confirming that many women received a mastectomy despite a pCR [31].

Our results are consistent with several analyses from the NCDB showing that the adoption of BCS after NACT in the United States has plateaued at around 40% [7,17,19] and mastectomies are still very frequently chosen in this setting [32,33]. A study of 59,568 patients in this setting treated in the years 2010-2014 confirmed that rates of BCS changed little over time and reported a rising trend for bilateral mastectomy with immediate reconstruction [17].

Different findings from other large international series have been reported, however, and a recent study from the Netherlands Cancer Registry on 5290 patients [32] showed a significant decrease in mastectomy rates for HER2-positive BC - from 62.6% in 2010 to 35.1% in 2019.

A meta-analysis including 1452 patients from seven randomized clinical trials documented that the adoption of BCS in this setting did not correlate with tumor response after NACT, as only 31% of patients who became eligible for BCS avoided a mastectomy, and among women initially candidates to mastectomy but reaching a pCR only 41% actually received BCS [21].

The oncological safety of BCS after NACT has been a critical issue, and a meta-analysis of 4756 women in ten randomized trials by the Early Breast Cancer Trialists’ Collaborative Group (EBCTCG) in 2018 reported a higher incidence of local recurrences after NACT compared to primary surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy (21.4% vs. 15.9%, p = 0.0001) [34]. However, in this analysis not all women received surgery as part of local therapy, weakening the significance of this finding. Furthermore, there is a need to interpret data in the contest of significant and recent improvements in several steps of the management of these patients, including diagnostic imaging, localization of non-palpable lesions after NACT, pathological assessment of residual disease and margin status.

In fact, other studies have provided robust evidence regarding the oncological safety of BCS after NACT, since then. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 3531 women from 16 studies [35], and a secondary analysis of the I-SPY2 randomized trial [13], reported LRR rates after BCS and mastectomy of 5.4% versus 7.0%, respectively, at a median follow-up of 3.5 years. Even in the triple-negative setting, a retrospective analysis of 2632 patients, cT1-2 cN0-ypN0 from the German Breast Group meta-database, concluded that BCS is not associated with an increased risk of loco-regional relapse [36].

We found that 36% of women with unifocal tumors reaching a pCR underwent a mastectomy, suggesting that there is currently a need to better identify those patients who may potentially avoid unnecessary radical surgery. Indeed, we found that even in clinical scenarios most favorable to BCS, mastectomy remains a frequently adopted component, raising the question whether this attitude is justified today, particularly in highly chemo-sensitive disease. Furthermore, our results suggest that the association between pathological response and surgical choice remained relatively consistent across different pre-NACT tumor sizes.

In a multicentric report from the Humanitas Cancer System, Gentile et al. [33] showed that some 40% of their patients with ypT0 status post-NACT received a mastectomy, a choice driven by initial multifocality and tumor size rather than residual disease after treatment. Additionally, a retrospective study from the EUSOMA database reported a similar local recurrence-free survival after BCS or mastectomy among women reaching pT0 status, at a median follow-up of 21 months [31]. These results suggest that a substantial proportion of mastectomies can potentially be avoided today, sparing patients a higher morbidity associated with more extensive surgery [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

These findings have raised questions as to what drives the decision-making process in this setting. Likely, a multifactorial component is involved including personal preferences of both surgeons and patients and treatment factors. Among them, an increased use of magnetic resonance, surgeon's preference, fear of recurrence, fear of additional therapies, improved access to breast reconstruction, desire for symmetry, or personal belief in a benefit from bilateral mastectomy, have all been reported, although incompletely studied [[37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]].

Selective complete elimination of surgery after NACT has raised growing interest, and a single-arm, prospective, phase 2 non-randomized trial of 50 patients with triple negative or HER2+ tumors was conducted at 7 U S. Centers between years 2017- 2021 [45]. At a median follow-up of 55 months no recurrences were noted, although the majority of patients still received some sort of surgical intervention in the breast or in the axilla, and some received both vacuum-assisted breast biopsy and surgery.

Another important consideration involves trends in breast reconstruction, as in our cohort IBR was performed in 74% on cases after mastectomy. This is consistent with data from the NCDB showing rising rates of NSM and immediate reconstruction in the U.S. and suggesting that reconstructive practices are evolving rapidly [[46], [47], [48]].

It is now well known that IBR is safe in case of mastectomy after NACT as reported by a multicentric retrospective study of 4726 patients [49] and by two systematic reviews with metanalysis [50,51], describing comparable complications and implant loss rate. Furthermore, NSM has been demonstrated to be safe after NACT compared to primary surgery [[52], [53], [54]].

We report that, although there was a significant increase in the adoption of NSM and one-stage breast reconstruction after NACT in the study period, only 35% and 55% of our patients undergoing mastectomy received these treatments, respectively. Again, axillary node involvement resulted negatively associated with both procedures.

Desire for “de-escalation” should include not only our surgical approach but also the necessity of postmastectomy radiotherapy (PMRT) following NACT. In our cohort, 30% of BC patients undergoing mastectomy and reaching a pCR still received PMRT. A recent meta-analysis of 15 studies including some 65,000 women showed no significant association between PMRT and OS or DMFS in patients reaching a pCR [55]. Recently, ASTRO-ASCO-SSO guidelines highlighted that PMRT is recommended after NACT for patients presenting with locally advanced disease and for those with residual nodal disease at the time of surgery [56]. In addition, PMRT is conditionally recommended for patients with cT1-3N1 or cT3N0 breast cancer with pathologically negative nodes after NACT (ypN0) [56].

Finally, although the results of our study are not driven by Breast Center-specific practices, our study shows a consistent variability among a group of high-volume, accredited, high-quality Breast Centers, and underscores an urgent need to standardize surgical practices at a national level.

There are several weaknesses of this study. First, this was a retrospective review of a prospective maintained multicenter data warehouse, and this may be associated with bias. Second, some items in the datasets were missing for incomplete data entry. Third, the analysis was limited to Breast Centers participating in SENONET, and it does not represent a complete picture at a national level. Fourth, we have no data on follow-up, and therefore our study is blind to final outcomes when comparing BCS and mastectomy. Nevertheless, this report analyzes a large number of cases from a prospectively maintained data warehouse, and it represents, therefore, a clear indication of how the clinical approach is evolving at a national level in this setting.

In conclusion, we analyzed the determinants of breast surgery after NACT finding a high mastectomy rate, with a small but non-significant trend reduction over the study period, despite a high incidence of pCR. We believe that this weakens the realization of all potential benefits of NACT and that surgeons should guide the surgical decision-making process by accounting for tumor biology and clinical-radiologic response. This approach may decrease morbidity as well as unnecessary procedures, and finally improve quality of life. The results of our study underscore an urgent need for updated, evidence-based, guidelines specifically addressing surgery after NACT, and support multidisciplinary audits and future prospective studies on surgical de-escalation. We are in the process of analyzing the determinants of axillary surgery after NACT within our cohort of patients to further contribute to this process.
