Complement C5a/C5aR1 pathway facilitates glioblastoma progression via fostering glioma stem cell-macrophage symbiosis
Authors: Hongtao Zhu, Lidong Cheng, Dan Liu, Yixuan Ma, Heng Fan, Shuting He, Wenjia Liang, Daluo Mei, Xiaoyu Ma, Ran Li, Hailong Mi, Junwen Wang, Jun Li, Xingjiang Yu, Suojun Zhang, Kai Shu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) symbiotically interact with glioma stem cells (GSCs) to facilitate GSCs stemness maintenance and glioblastoma (GBM) progression. Here we identified the complement 5a (C5a) as a key mediator of GSCs-TAMs symbiosis through integrative screening. C5a is preferentially expressed and secreted by GSCs. C5a activates the p-STAT3-cMyc-PD-L1 axis to promote GSCs proliferation, self-renewal and resistance against cytotoxic T cells through its receptor C5aR1. Moreover, GSCs-derived C5a trigger the infiltration and immunosuppressive polarization of TAMs through C5aR1-p-AKT T308 axis in tumor microenvironment. Importantly, silencing or pharmacological inhibition of C5a/C5aR1 disrupts both GSCs and TAMs and suppresses GBM tumor growth. In human GBM, the C5a/C5aR1 axis is activated and positively correlates with stemness, immunosuppressive TAMs and predicts poor prognosis. Collectively, these results demonstrate the key role of C5a/C5aR1 pathway in GSCs-TAMs symbiosis and indicate the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway for GBM treatment.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12974-026-03930-z.Keywords: Complement 5a, Glioblastoma, Glioma stem cell, Tumor-associated macrophage, Tumor-immune symbiosis

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most prevalent and malignant primary brain tumor in adults [1]. GBM frequently exhibits infiltrative growth and is typically refractory to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, even with maximal treatment, the median survival of GBM patients is less than 16 months [1, 2]. Glioma stem cells (GSCs), a subpopulation of cells within GBM tumors possess the capacity for sustained proliferation and self-renewal, which closely correlated with GBM malignancy [3]. Accumulated evidence indicated that GSCs are the root cause of GBM treatment resistance and recurrence, targeting GSCs is a promising strategy for GBM treatment [3].

GBM tumors are hallmarked by their complex microenvironment, where GSCs and microenvironment components symbiotically interact with each other to promote GSCs stemness maintenance, tumor growth and treatment resistance [4].Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) represent the largest proportion of non-tumor cells, comprising approximately 30–50% of all cells in GBM tumors [5]. TAMs are predominantly characterized as alternatively activated immunosuppressive macrophages in GBM, which play a pivotal role in orchestrating tumor progression and immunosuppression [5]. Increasing studies revealed the interactions between GSCs and TAMs formed a positive feedback loop to facilitate GBM progression [6–8]. For example, GSCs promote the recruitment and immunosuppressive polarization of TAMs through paracrine cytokines including POSTN, LGMN and TFPI2 [9–11], whereas immunosuppressive TAMs promote GSCs stemness maintenance and immunosuppression through the secretion of PTN and TGFβ [12, 13]. Therefore, elucidating the novel mechanism of GSCs-TAMs symbiosis and exploiting corresponding targeted therapies will lead to an improvement in the prognosis of GBM.

The complement system is an important component of innate immunity and participates in a multitude of immune response processes [14]. During activation of the complement system, C5a was cleaved from C5 and secreted extracellularly to act as a potent proinflammatory mediator, which plays a pivotal role in immune responses through its receptor, C5aR1 [15]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the complement C5a/C5aR1 signaling is widely involved in the malignant progression of tumors [16]. For instance, the complement C5a/C5aR1 signaling facilitates the growth of gastric and colorectal cancer cells through p21 and β-catenin [17, 18], enhances the invasion of breast cancer through the formation of Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs) [19], and induces the immunosuppression of ovarian cancers through the regulation of TAMs and T-cell [20]. Targeting the C5a/C5aR1 signaling pathway sensitizes colorectal cancer cells to radiotherapy [21]. In GBM, complement C5a/C5aR1 signaling is also involved in tumor malignant behaviors including proliferation, invasion, and resistance to chemotherapy [22, 23]. Nevertheless, the potential role of complement C5a/C5aR1 signaling in GSCs stemness maintenance, and in particular whether C5a/C5aR1 mediates GSCs-TAMs symbiosis remains unclear.

In this study, we identified the complement cascades pathway as the top hit of pathways that both enriched in patients with high macrophage signature and stemness signature. Further integrative screening suggests that C5 may be a key candidate in the complement pathway that mediating GSCs-TAMs symbiosis. C5/C5a is preferentially expressed/secreted by GSCs and activates the p-STAT3-cMyc-PD-L1 pathway to facilitate GSCs self-renewal and immune escape via C5aR1. Moreover, GSCs-derived C5a promotes recruitment and immunosuppressive polarization of TAMs through C5aR1-p-AKT T308 signaling in a paracrine manner. Pharmacological inhibition of C5a/C5aR1 disrupts the self-renewal of GSCs both in vitro and in vivo, and reduces the density of immunosuppressive TAMs in GBM tumor-bearing mice. Taken together, these results demonstrate the dual role of C5a/C5aR1 in maintaining GSCs and immunosuppressive TAMs in GBM, which prompts that C5a/C5aR1 is a therapeutic vulnerability for targeting GSCs-TAMs symbiosis.

Results

C5/C5a is preferentially expressed/secreted by GSCs and predicts poor prognosis for GBM patients

To directly investigate the correlation between the abundance of GSCs and the infiltration ratio of TAMs in GBM tumors, we calculated the stemness score of tumor tissues from patients in the TCGA, CGGA, and Rembrandt databases as we previously reported, and assessed the TAM infiltration ratio using the CIBERSORT algorithm. Correlation analysis revealed that the infiltration ratio of immunosuppressive macrophage is positively correlated with the stemness score in glioma patients across all three databases (Figure S1A-C). Furthermore, we validated this correlation at the protein level by performing immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining on a glioma tissue microarray (TMA) from the Tongji Cohort. The results confirmed a significantly positive correlation between the expression levels of stemness markers and macrophage markers (Figure S2A). Consistently, analysis of single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data from public database also revealed significant colocalization of GSCs and TAMs within GBM tumors (Figure S3A-C). Collectively, these findings further demonstrate the direct correlation between macrophage infiltration and stemness signature in GBM, highlighting the pivotal role of the GSCs-TAMs symbiosis in GBM progression.

To identify potential pathways and key mediators of GSCs-TAMs symbiosis in GBM, differentially enriched pathways were analyzed in tumors with different proportions of macrophage and immunosuppressive macrophage infiltration (Fig. 1A). The KEGG Enrichment analysis revealed that the Complement and coagulation cascades pathway was the only immune-related pathway that ranked among the top 10 activated in both high macrophage-infiltrating and high immunosuppressive macrophage-infiltrating GBM (Fig. 1B, Figure S4A-B). Moreover, based on a stemness signature we constructed previously [24], the GSEA analysis indicated that the complement pathway was also significantly activated in GBM with a high stemness index (Fig. 1B, D and Figure S4C). These results suggest that the complement pathway may be a key mediator of GSCs-TAMs symbiosis. To further identify the key molecules in the complement pathway that mediate GSCs-TAMs symbiosis, we employed the following integrative screening methods: 1) genes expression in the TCGA-GBM database were negatively correlated with patient survival; 2) encoded secreted proteins; 3) highly expressed in CD45− cells (based on the Brain-TIME database [25]) (Fig. 1C). C5 and C1S were identified ultimately (Figure S5A-B). Further Pearson correlation analyses indicated a strong correlation between the expression of C5 and GSCs markers (Figure S5C-D). We performed serum-induced differentiation assays on three GSC lines, and qRT-PCR demonstrated that differentiation led to a significant decrease in stemness markers and a marked increase in multilineage differentiation markers (Figure S5E), confirming the multilineage differentiation potential (stemness) of GSCs. Simultaneously, C5 was found to be preferentially expressed in GSCs, whereas C1S expression did not differ significantly between GSCs and NSTCs (Figure S5F). Collectively, these results suggest that C5 may be a key molecule mediating GSCs-TAMs symbiosis.

To elucidate the potential role of C5 in GBM progression, we first investigate the expression characteristic of C5 expression in GBM. Bioinformatics analysis indicates a significant elevation of C5 in GBM (Fig. 1E), high expression of C5 suggests poor prognosis in GBM patients (Fig. 1F). Immunohistochemical staining of TMA we constructed previously [26] and in HPA database showed that C5 expression was significantly elevated in glioma tissues compared to normal brain tissues and increased with tumor WHO grade (Fig. 1G and Figure S6A-B). Moreover, C5/C5a levels were significantly increased in tumor tissues, serum and cerebrospinal fluid of glioma patients compared to normal controls as demonstrated by ELISA and western blot (Fig. 1H-I). To verify the correlations between C5 expression and stemness, we performed immunofluorescence staining of GBM patient tissues and found significant co-localization/co-expression of C5 with GSCs markers (Fig. 1J and Figure S6C-G). We utilized serum to differentiate GSCs into NSTCs as previously reported [27], the western blot, ELISA and immunofluorescence staining revealed that C5/C5a was predominantly expressed/secreted by GSCs (Fig. 1K-L and Figure S6H-K). To further validate the expression specificity of C5 in GSCs at the single-cell level in patient tumor, we analyzed single-cell RNA-seq data from two independent cohorts of GBM patients. The results showed that C5 was predominantly expressed in tumor cells, and among C5-positive cells, 72.42% and 67.82% were also positive for the stemness marker SOX2, respectively (Figure S7A, B). Together, these findings demonstrated that C5/C5a is preferentially expressed/secreted by GSCs and predicts poor prognosis for GBM patients.

C5/C5aR1 is required for GSCs proliferation and self-renewal

To investigate the potential role of C5 in GSCs proliferation and self-renewal, we utilized lentivirus-based shRNA to knockdown the expression of C5 in GSCs. Various functional assays showed that knockdown of C5 significantly inhibited the proliferation and self-renewal of GSCs in vitro, as shown by inhibited cell viability in cell viability assay (Fig. 2A), reduced positive ratio in the limiting dilution assay (Fig. 2B), decreased number of tumor spheres in sphereformation assay (Fig. 2C). Consistent with the functional assays, western blot assay showed that knockdown of C5 in GSCs resulted in a significant increase in the expression of apoptotic markers (Cleaved PARP and Cleaved Caspase3) and a significant decrease in stemness markers (SOX2 and OLIG2) (Fig. 2D). Since C5 was usually cleaved into C5a and exerts biological functions through its receptor C5aR1 [15], we next evaluate the impact of C5aR1 knockdown on GSCs phenotype. We found that silencing C5aR1 also significantly inhibited the proliferation and self-renewal of GSCs, which was evidenced by the similar changes in the functional assays and western blot of GSCs with C5aR1 knockdown (Fig. 2E-H).

To gain further insight into the role of C5 in GSCs stemness maintenance and in vivo tumor growth, GSCs were transduced with lentivirus expressing shC5 and intracranially injected into nude mice. Silencing of C5 markedly impeded the tumor growth of GSCs in vivo and prolonged the survival of GSCs-derived GBM-bearing mice (Fig. 2I). The TUNEL staining indicated upregulated apoptosis in xenografts with C5 knockdown (Fig. 2J, K). Moreover, the apoptotic marker (Cleaved Caspase3) and differentiation marker (GFAP) were significantly increased in xenografts after C5 knockdown, while the stemness markers (CD15, CD133, SOX2) and proliferation marker (Ki67) were significantly decreased (Fig. 2J, K). The above data suggest that C5/C5aR1 is required for GSCs proliferation and self-renewal both in vitro and in vivo.

C5a/C5aR1 activates the p-STAT3-cMyc-PD-L1 pathway to promote GSCs proliferation, stemness and resistance against cytotoxic T cells

C5aR1 is a G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) that typically involved in signal transduction by phosphorylating downstream kinases [28]. To elucidate the mechanism by which C5a/C5aR1 promotes stemness maintenance in GSCs, we examined the phosphorylation status of seven candidate downstream signaling pathways previously reported to be associated with C5a/C5aR1 signaling [22]. Among seven pairs of phosphorylated kinases we examined in western blot, p-STAT3 was the sole candidate that markedly reduced in three GSCs with C5 or C5aR1 knockdown (Fig. 3A, B). Moreover, GSEA analysis revealed a significant activation of both STAT3 and MYC signaling pathways in contexts of high C5/C5aR1 expression. This signature was consistently observed not only in patient-derived glioblastoma stem cells but also in bulk tumor samples from independent cohorts within the TCGA, CGGA, and Rembrandt databases (Fig. 3C and Figure S8A-B). Given prior evidence linking the C5a/C5aR1 axis to downstream JAK-STAT, NF-κB, and PI3K signaling [21, 29, 30], we assessed the activation of these pathways in shNT- versus shC5-transduced GSCs and in C5-high vs. C5-low GBM patients from TCGA database. No significant alterations in pathway activity were detected in either dataset (Figure S8C). This suggests that the pro-tumorigenic function of C5a/C5aR1 signaling in GSCs may be mediated through the specific activation of the STAT3-Myc axis. It has been demonstrated that p-STAT3 facilitates stemness maintenance of cancer stem cells by promoting downstream cMyc expression [31], and cMyc is required for GSCs stemness maintenance [32]. However, it remains unclear whether p-STAT3 could enhances cMyc expression in GBM and, particularly in GSCs, by directly binding to the promoter region of MYC gene and promoting its transcription. We found that STAT3 could directly bind to the MYC promoter in GSCs through ChIP-qPCR experiments (Fig. 3D and Figure S8D), and C5/C5aR1 knockdown significantly reduced the protein expression of p-STAT3 and cMyc in GSCs (Fig. 3E, F). Silencing STAT3 also significantly reduced cMyc expression in GSCs (Fig. 3J). The above results suggest that C5a/C5aR1 promotes proliferation and stemness maintenance of GSCs through p-STAT3-cMyc pathway.

In addition to facilitating proliferation and self-renewal of cancer stem cells, cMyc also plays a role in tumor immune escape through transcriptional activation of immune checkpoint-associated molecules, such as PD-L1 [33, 34]. It has been reported that PD-L1 is highly expressed in GSCs and contributes to immune escape of GBM [35]. However, whether cMyc can transcriptionally activate PD-L1 expression in GSCs remains unclear. Interestingly, we found that there was a notable reduction in the infiltration of CD8+ T-cells of GBM patients with high C5/C5aR1 expression in the TCGA, CGGA and Rembrandt databases (Fig. 3G and Figure S8E). C5/C5aR1 knockdown significantly reduced p-STAT3, cMyc and PD-L1 expression within GSCs (Fig. 3E, F). Moreover, ChIP-qPCR assay confirmed that cMyc could directly bind to the PD-L1 promoter in GSCs (Fig. 3H and Figure S8D), and knockdown of STAT3 also led to a significant decline in cMyc and PD-L1 expression within GSCs (Fig. 3J). To further validate that C5/C5aR1 regulates the cMyc-PD-L1 axis through p-STAT3, GSCs were treated with Stattic to inhibit the phosphorylation of STAT3 during C5/C5aR1 silencing or rC5a stimulation. Stattic treatment abrogated the modulatory effects of C5/C5aR1 knockdown and rC5a stimulation on cMyc and PD-L1 expression in GSCs (Figure S9A-C). Importantly, the in vitro CD8+ T cell-mediated tumor cell killing assay demonstrated that C5/C5aR1 knockdown markedly increased CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity against GSCs, whereas this effect was abrogated upon PD-L1 blockade with Atezolizumab (Fig. 3I and Figure S8F). These results indicate that C5a/C5aR1 facilitates PD-L1 expression via p-STAT3-cMyc axis in GSCs, thereby mediating immune evasion of GBM. Lastly, the immunofluorescence staining suggested that the expression of cMyc and PD-L1 were significantly decreased in xenografts after C5 knockdown (Fig. 3K, L). The above data suggest that C5a/C5aR1 activates the p-STAT3-cMyc-PD-L1 pathway to promote GSCs proliferation, stemness and resistance against cytotoxic T cells.

Ectopic expression of cMyc partially rescues the effect caused by silence of C5/C5aR1 in GSCs.

To further confirm that C5a/C5aR1 promotes GSCs proliferation and self-renewal via the p-STAT3-cMyc axis, we next explored whether ectopic expression of cMyc could rescue the effect of C5/C5aR1 knockdown in GSCs. Sphereformation assay and cell-titer-based cell viability assay showed that knockdown of C5/C5aR1 significantly inhibited the proliferation and self-renewal ability of GSCs, whereas overexpression of CMYC partially rescued the impaired cell viability and sphereformation in GSCs caused by C5/C5aR1 silencing (Fig. 4A-C, E–G). Western blot assay also demonstrated that CMYC overexpression significantly reinforced the protein expression of stemness markers (SOX2, OLIG2) and PD-L1 in GSCs with C5/C5aR1 knockdown, while the expression of apoptosis marker (Cleaved PARP) was significantly decreased (Fig. 4D, H). These results suggest that the ectopic expression of cMyc partially rescues the effect caused by silence of C5/C5aR1 in GSCs, which highlights the critical role of C5/C5aR1-p-STAT3-cMyc axis in GSCs proliferation and self-renewal.

GSCs-derived C5a promotes recruitment and immunosuppressive polarization of TAMs

Next, we would like to investigate the effect of GSCs-secreted C5a on the infiltration and function of TAMs in GBM microenvironment. The bioinformatics analysis based on CIBERSORT algorithm revealed elevated immunosuppressive macrophage infiltration in the tumors of patients with high C5/C5aR1 expression in CGGA, TCGA, and Rembrandt databases (Fig. 5A). Moreover, since TAMs within GBM tumors include both infiltrating macrophages and resident microglia, which differ in origin and function, we performed single-cell analysis and found that macrophages exhibited higher C5AR1 positivity rates and average expression levels than microglia, as well as a higher proportion within GBM tumors (Figure S10A), suggesting that GSCs-derived C5a may primarily regulate the macrophage subset within TAMs. Immunofluorescence staining also showed significant downregulation of pan macrophage marker (Iba1) and immunosuppressive macrophage markers (CD163, Arg1) in the xenografts derived from GSCs with C5 knockdown (Fig. 5B, C and Figure S10B). To directly investigate the effect of GSCs-derived C5a on TAMs recruitment and polarization, we stimulated U937 and THP1-derived human macrophages, mouse primary bone marrow derived macrophage (BMDM) and peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)-derived human macrophages with recombinant C5a protein in vitro, the transwell migration assays showed that rC5a significantly enhanced macrophage migration (Fig. 5D and Figure S10C). Interestingly, the C5aR1 inhibitor PMX205 significantly inhibited the migration of TAMs treated with rC5a, suggesting that C5a exerts its recruitment effect on TAMs through its receptor C5aR1 (Fig. 5D and Figure S10C). Moreover, the cell viability of TAMs was not significantly altered by treatment with rC5a alone or in combination with PMX205 (Figure S10D). This finding underscores the important role of the C5a/C5aR1 axis in regulating TAM migration. The conditioned medium of GSCs containing C5a also significantly recruited TAMs, and C5 knockdown in GSCs or treatment with PMX205 significantly inhibited this effect (Fig. 5E, G and Figure S10E, F). In addition to C5aR1 inhibition, C5aR1 knockdown in TAMs by lentivirus-mediated shRNA also significantly inhibited the migration of TAMs treated with rC5a (Fig. 5F). Moreover, C5aR1 was co-localized/co-expressed with both pan macrophage marker (Iba1) and immunosuppressive macrophage markers (CD163, CD206, ARG1) in GBM patient tumors (Fig. 5H and Figure S11A). The above results suggest that GSCs-derived C5a promotes TAMs recruitment through its receptor C5aR1.

The effect of GSC-derived C5a on the polarization of TAMs was also been investigated. The spatial co‑localization of C5a-positive GSCs and immunosuppressive TAMs was confirmed by multiplexed immunofluorescence (mIF) of patient GBM tissue (Figure S11B). Treatment with rC5a significantly upregulated the protein levels of immunosuppressive markers (CD163, Arg1) of TAMs, and PMX205 inhibited this effect (Fig. 5I and Figure S12A). Given that we had previously detected C5AR1 expression in microglia by single-cell analysis, we performed the same assays on BV2 microglial cells and observed similar effects (Figure S12B). Consistent with expectations, conditioned medium of GSCs also significantly upregulated the protein levels of immunosuppressive markers (CD163, Arg1) within TAMs, and PMX205 inhibited this effect (Fig. 5J). In addition, qRT-PCR indicated that the mRNA levels of immunosuppressive markers were significantly increased in TAMs after rC5a treatment, whereas the mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory markers was significantly decreased (Fig. 5K, L and Figure S12C, D). Unsurprisingly, PMX205 treatment also suppressed this effect caused by rC5a in qRT-PCR assay (Fig. 5K, L and Figure S12C, D). Taken together, these results suggest that GSCs-derived C5a promotes recruitment and immunosuppressive polarization of TAMs via its receptor C5aR1.

C5a promotes TAMs recruitment and immunosuppressive polarization through C5aR1-AKT signaling

To further elucidate the mechanism by which C5a promotes TAMs recruitment and polarization, we conducted a similar detection of downstream phosphorylated kinases of C5aR1 in TAMs treated with C5a, with or without PMX205. The results showed that p-AKT T308 can be specifically activated by C5a in TAMs among seven pairs of phosphorylated kinases, and treatment with PMX205 counteracts this effect (Fig. 6A). Moreover, LY294002, an AKT inhibitor, was used to inhibit the phosphorylation of AKT in TAMs treated with C5a. Transwell assay showed that LY294002 significantly inhibited the migration of TAMs treated with rC5a or conditioned medium of GSCs (Fig. 6B, C). LY294002 also significantly inhibited the phosphorylation of AKT and the protein levels of immunosuppressive markers (CD163, Arg1) in TAMs treated with rC5a (Fig. 6D). Moreover, LY294002 significantly inhibited the mRNA expression levels of immunosuppressive markers in TAMs treated with C5a, whereas the mRNA expression levels of pro-inflammatory markers was significantly increased (Fig. 6E-G and Figure S13A, B). Taken together, these findings indicate that C5a promotes TAMs recruitment and immunosuppressive polarization through C5aR1-AKT signaling.

PMX205, a BBB-permeable C5aR1 antagonist, reduces GSCs and immunosuppressive TAMs and inhibits GBM tumor growth

PMX205 is a blood–brain barrier (BBB)-permeable and orally active cyclic hexapeptide that acts as a high-affinity and potent antagonist against C5aR1 [36]. To investigate the therapeutic significance of targeting C5a/C5aR1 signaling in GBM, we examined whether pharmacologic inhibition of this signaling by PMX205 could inhibit GBM tumor growth via regulation of GSCs and TAMs. PMX205 treatment significantly inhibited the proliferation and self-renewal of GSCs in vitro, as shown by inhibited cell viability in cell viability assay and decreased number of tumor spheres in sphereformation assay (Fig. 7A-C). Consistent with the functional assays, western blot assay showed that PMX205 treatment resulted in a significant increase in the expression of apoptotic markers (Cleaved PARP and Cleaved Caspase3) and a significant decrease in stemness markers (SOX2 and OLIG2), p-STAT3, cMyc and PD-L1 in GSCs (Fig. 7D-E). Moreover, PMX205 treatment resulted in a notable augmentation in T cell-mediated cytotoxicity against GSCs (Fig. 7F). The in vivo therapeutic effects of PMX205 in GBM-bearing mice derived from GSCs were also been deeply investigated. The penetration of PMX205 across the blood–brain barrier was verified by LC–MS/MS (Figure S14A-F), and no obvious toxicity was observed in mice treated with PMX205 (Figure S15A). Oral administration of PMX205 significantly inhibited the intracranial tumor growth of GSCs and prolonged the survival of tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 7G-I). The TUNEL staining indicates upregulated apoptosis in xenografts of mice treated with PMX205 (Fig. 7J, K). Immunofluorescence staining also suggested that the apoptotic marker (Cleaved Caspase3) and differentiation marker (GFAP) were significantly increased in xenografts treated with PMX205, while the stemness markers (CD15, CD133, SOX2), proliferation marker (Ki67), pan macrophage marker (Iba1), immunosuppressive macrophage markers (CD163, ARG1), cMyc and PD-L1were significantly decreased (Fig. 7J, K). These results suggest that PMX205 can inhibit GSCs-derived GBM growth both in vitro and in vivo by targeting both GSCs and immunosuppressive TAMs, suggesting that targeting C5a/C5aR1 is a highly promising therapeutic strategy for GBM.

To elucidate the clinical relevance of the C5a/C5aR1 pathway in GSCs stemness maintenance and macrophage infiltration of GBM patients, we initially employed bioinformatics to examine the correlation between C5/C5aR1 expression level and patient survival, GSCs characteristics, and macrophage characteristics in CGGA and Rembrandt databases. Both C5 and C5aR1 were significantly highly expressed in GBM and high expression of C5/C5aR1 was associated with a poor prognosis for patients with GBM (Fig. 1E, F and Figure S16A, B). Furthermore, C5aR1 expression was highest in the mesenchymal subtype of GBM, which is associated with the poorest prognosis (Figure S16C). Multivariable Cox regression analysis of C5/C5aR1 expression along with key clinical variables including age, grade, IDH status, MGMT methylation, and molecular subtype indicates that the C5/C5aR1 expression could independently predict prognosis of glioma patients (Figure S17A-B). GSEA analysis also suggests a significant increase in GSCs and immunosuppressive TAMs infiltration in GBM patients with high expression of C5/C5aR1 (Fig. 8A, B).

To further validate the results of the in-silico analysis, we conducted immunohistochemical staining for C5, C5aR1, cMyc, PD-L1, stemness markers (SOX2, OLIG2, CD133, CD15), pan macrophage marker (Iba1), and immunosuppressive macrophage markers (CD163, CD206, ARG1) in glioma patients tissue microarrays we constructed previously [26] (Fig. 8C-E). Moreover, IHC Profiler software [37], was utilized to score each tissue staining in the TMA. The correlation analysis revealed a significant and positive correlation between C5 and C5aR1 protein expression and cMyc, PD-L1, stemness markers, pan macrophage markers, and immunosuppressive macrophage markers (Fig. 8F). The correlations between genes encoding the above proteins were also analyzed in CGGA database. As excepted, the mRNA expression of C5/C5aR1 was significantly positively correlated with cMyc, PD-L1, stemness markers (SOX2, CD44, PROM1, FUT4), pan macrophage marker (ITGAM) and immunosuppressive macrophage marker (CD163, MRC2, ARG1) in CGGA database (Fig. 8G). Discussion

Emerging evidences have demonstrated that GSCs and TAMs form a mutually reinforcing circuit through multiple autocrine and paracrine factors, which promotes the malignant progression, recurrence and treatment resistance of GBM [6, 38]. Unraveling the mechanism of GSCs-TAMs symbiosis and developing corresponding targeted therapeutic strategies are expected to ‘kill two birds with one stone’, thereby improving the prognosis of GBM patients [39]. In this study, we utilized a series of integrative screening methods and identified the complement pathway was activated in GBM patients with higher GSCs/TAMs signature, and C5/C5a may serve as the key mediator of GSCs-TAMs symbiosis. We found that C5/C5a is preferentially expressed/secreted by GSCs and predicts poor prognosis for GBM patients, C5a/C5aR1 activates the p-STAT3-cMyc-PD-L1 pathway to promote GSCs proliferation, stemness and resistance against cytotoxic T cells. In addition to its GSCs intrinsic effects, GSCs-derived C5a promotes recruitment and immunosuppressive polarization of TAMs through C5aR1-p-AKT T308 signaling. Moreover, PMX205, a BBB-permeable C5aR1 antagonist, reduces GSCs and immunosuppressive TAMs and inhibits GBM tumor growth. Importantly, multiple GBM patients-based assays from databases and our own cohorts indicated that C5a/C5aR1 axis is activated in GBM patients and tracks with both Macrophage and GSCs signatures. This study uncovers the novel GSCs-derived C5a-C5aR1 signaling that links the symbiotic interaction between GSCs and TAMs, which sheds light on the therapeutic potential of targeting this axis to overcome GSCs-TAMs symbiosis in GBM.

Increasing evidence demonstrated that the complement system was aberrantly expressed during tumorigenesis and extensively involved in tumor malignant progression [16]. In previous work, we have reviewed the multidimensional functions of the highly activated complement system in glioma cell-microenvironment crosstalk and GBM progression [40]. The complement C5/C5a has also been reported to be involved in GBM progression and treatment resistance. The mesenchymal stemlike cells (MSLCs) derived C5a promotes GBM cell invasion through the p38-ZEB1 axis [22]. Microglia-derived C5a induced by lnc-TALC promotes GBM resistance to TMZ, and inhibition of C5a signaling enhances TMZ sensitivity [23]. However, the potential roles of C5/C5a in GSCs stemness maintenance and TAMs infiltration remain elusive. Herein, we confirmed that C5/C5a is preferentially expressed/secreted by GSCs and plays dual roles in maintaining GSCs and immunosuppressive TAMs. To our knowledge, this current study is the first article to explore the role and mechanism of the complement C5a/C5aR1 pathway in the stemness maintenance of glioma stem cells. Moreover, since C5a is a secreted protein, the protein levels of C5/C5a in the tumor tissues, serum and CSF were determined by western blot and ELISA. We found that the protein levels of C5/C5a were significantly elevated in GBM patients than non-tumor controls. Collectively, this study broadens the understanding of the function and mechanism of complement C5a in GBM progression, which highlights the potential of C5a as a target for non-invasive early diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring in GBM liquid biopsies.

GSCs were first identified by Singh et al. in GBM patient specimens as tumor-initiating cells [41]. GSCs exhibit greater tumorigenicity compared to differentiated glioma cells (DGCs) when intracranially injected into mouse brain, which function as key drivers of GBM progression [42]. We have previously reported that ROR1 promotes GSCs stemness maintenance through the GRB2-c-FOS signaling axis and found that hypoxia-induced Galectin-8 promotes stemness maintenance of GSCs through autophagy reinforcement [24, 27]. In this study, we identified the C5a/C5aR1 pathway that acts as a novel stemness maintainer of GSCs through integrative screening and extensive experimental validation, which broadened our understanding of the mechanism of GSCs stemness maintenance. In addition, this study revealed that C5a/C5aR1 maintains GSCs self-renewal through the p-STAT3-cMyc axis, which is confirmed by gene knockdown assays and rescue assays. Previous studies have shown that cMyc is highly expressed in GSCs and promotes stemness maintenance [32]. Inhibition of cMyc by biological camouflaged cascade brain-targeting nanosystem effectively depletes GSCs [43]. However, the upstream regulation mechanism of cMyc in GSCs remains unclear. Herein, we demonstrated that STAT3 can directly bind to the MYC promoter region and promote cMyc expression in GSCs by ChIP-qPCR experiments. Moreover, since transcription factors usually function in the cell nucleus and are usually considered as ‘undruggable’ [44, 45] and this study reveals that C5a/C5aR1 promotes cMyc expression in GSCs through p-STAT3, which providing an alternative indirect strategy for targeting cMyc in GSCs.

Abundant infiltration of immunosuppressive TAMs (up to 50% of all cells in the tumor) is a hallmark of GBM tumors and is closely associated with GBM proliferation, invasion, stemness maintenance, treatment resistance and recurrence [5]. Targeting TAMs is an obvious anti-GBM strategy. Three questions need to be addressed before developing TAMs-targeting strategies: 1) how TAMs infiltrate into the GBM microenvironment; 2) the mechanism by which TAMs polarize to the immunosuppressive type within the GBM microenvironment; and 3) the mechanism by which immunosuppressive TAMs promote GBM progression. We and other research groups have reported that GSCs or GBM cells can promote infiltration and immunosuppressive polarization of TAMs through the secretion of POSTN, TFPI2, CCLs, OPN, and PDCD10 [9–11, 46–48]. Reciprocally, immunosuppressive TAMs facilitate the malignant behaviors of GBM such as stemness maintenance, invasion and mesenchymal transition via PTN, TGFβ and CCL8 [12, 13, 49]. In this study, we found that GSCs-derived C5a promotes the infiltration and immunosuppressive polarization of TAMs via the C5aR1. This present study focuses on the role of the complement C5a/C5aR1 pathway in the GSCs-TAMs symbiosis and firstly reveals the novel mechanism that the GSCs-secreted C5a promotes the infiltration and polarization of TAMs via C5aR1 in GBM tumors. Notably, in contrast to previous studies confirming that p-AKT S473 plays an important role in TAMs infiltration and polarization [9], we found that GSCs-derived C5a activates p-AKT T308 in TAMs via its receptor C5aR1, and inhibition the phosphorylation of AKT by LY294002 significantly counteracts the effect of C5a on TAMs recruitment and polarization. The detailed mechanisms by which different factors secreted by GSCs phosphorylate different sites of AKT need to be further elucidated. Furthermore, in addition to TAMs, the GBM microenvironment comprises various non-neoplastic cellular constituents, such as microglia and neutrophils. Studies have shown that C5a can modulate the function of microglia in the brain [23]. Given that TAMs in GBM tumors are derived from both peripheral blood monocytes and resident microglia [5], it is plausible that GSCs-derived C5a may also influence the functional state of microglia within GBM. This could represent another key mechanism through which GSC-derived C5a promotes GBM progression. Although this study primarily focuses on the monocyte-derived TAM subpopulation and does not distinguish between the two TAM origins in in vivo experiments, we believe that investigating the regulatory role and underlying mechanisms of GSC-derived C5a on microglia represents a highly promising direction for our future research. The function of neutrophils within the tumor microenvironment is also regulated by C5a and exhibits context-dependent roles across different cancers. For instance, in melanoma, C5a can activate neutrophils to kill tumor cells [50], whereas in lung cancer, it induces polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs) to form neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), thereby promoting tumor cell proliferation and invasion [19]. Currently, how C5a regulates neutrophil function in the GBM microenvironment remains unclear. Elucidating this mechanism in the future will significantly advance our understanding of how the C5a/C5aR1 signaling axis promotes GBM progression and will aid in developing corresponding targeted therapeutic strategies. In conclusion, as both C5 and C5aR1 are good 'druggable' targets [21], these findings provide a new strategy for TAMs-targeted treatment of GBM.

PMX205 is a potent cyclic hexapeptide inhibitor of C5aR1 which is widely used to study C5aR1 pathobiology in mouse models of disease. When administered by tail vein injection or drinking water [51], PMX205 could across the BBB and permeates into the brain tissue and spinal cord of mice to ameliorate disease pathology in the mouse model of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [36].It has also been reported that PMX205 treatment triggers ferroptosis and inhibits GBM growth [52], but the effect of PMX205 on GSCs stemness maintenance and TAMs polarization within GBM remains elusive. In this study, we demonstrated that PMX205 significantly inhibited GSCs stemness maintenance, TAMs migration and immunosuppressive polarization in vitro. In GSCs-derived GBM tumor-bearing mice, oral administration of PMX205 through drinking water significantly reduced GSCs and immunosuppressive TAMs infiltration and prolonged the survival of tumor-bearing mice, which highlighting C5aR1 antagonism as an attractive therapeutic strategy for GBM by simultaneously targeting GSCs-TAMs symbiosis. In addition, we found that PMX205 treatment could inhibits PD-L1 expression in GSCs and enhances T-cell cytotoxicity against GSCs, suggesting that the combination strategy of C5a/C5aR1 inhibition and anti-PD1 immune checkpoint inhibition may yield improved outcomes for GBM patients [53]. However, it is noteworthy that due to the pivotal role of the complement system in normal human immune function, systemic complement inhibitors, including PMX205, carry potential side effects. These may include impacting the immune responses of microglia and increasing the risk of meningitis caused by Neisseria infections, particularly in high-dose administration or prolonged, continuous treatment [54]. Therefore, in the practical clinical application of complement inhibition therapy, a comprehensive consideration of the patient's underlying conditions and immune system status is essential to determine the optimal drug selection, treatment timing, and duration, aiming to achieve the best therapeutic outcome [54]. Concurrently, the development of more precise targeting strategies for the complement system, such as those incorporating drug delivery systems, represents a highly promising avenue for future research.

In conclusion, this study identified GSCs-derived C5a as a key regulator of GSCs-TAMs symbiosis. C5a plays dual roles in GSCs stemness maintenance and immunosuppressive TAMs infiltration through its receptor C5aR1, targeting C5a/C5aR1 pathway through lentiviral-mediated gene knockdown or pharmacological inhibition could potently suppress GBM growth via dual impeding GSCs and immunosuppressive TAMs. While our findings establish a pivotal role for the C5a/C5aR1 axis in maintaining the GSC-TAMs symbiosis, several limitations of our study must be acknowledged. Firstly, our experimental mouse models may not fully recapitulate the complexity and heterogeneity of the human GBM tumor microenvironment (TME), particularly the complete spectrum of immune cell interactions. Secondly, although pharmacological C5aR1 inhibition showed promising efficacy, significant translational barriers remain. These include the need to optimize blood–brain barrier penetration, assess potential systemic immunosuppressive side effects, and evaluate long-term safety and efficacy in the context of a genetically unstable and adaptive tumor. Furthermore, our study focused on the symbiosis between GSCs and monocyte-derived TAMs; the impact of C5aR1 inhibition on other critical TME components, such as resident microglia or neutrophils, warrants further investigation. These model constraints and clinical considerations highlight the gap between our preclinical proof-of-concept and therapeutic application, framing essential questions for future research.

Materials and methods

GBM patient samples

Fresh GBM patient samples were obtained from the Department of Neurosurgery of Tongji Hospital, which approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital. Specimens from both male and female patients with definite pathological diagnosis were included in this study, there was no attrition. For ELISA test, the serum and CSF of patients were collected and stored at −80℃. For immunofluorescent staining, the GBM patient tumor tissues were collected and embedded in OCT and sliced up into 10 μm frozen sections. For western blot, the patient tissues were weighed and lysed using RIPA lysis buffer to extract total proteins. The TMA containing tissues from 91 patients we constructed previously was used for immunohistochemical staining. Detailed clinical information (age, sex, WHO grade, IDH status, MGMT promoter methylation, and treatment history) of the patient samples used in this study is summarized in Table S1.

Cell culture

GSCs (T3359 GSCs, T3691 GSCs, T387 GSCs, T4121 GSCs and T456 GSCs) used in this study were isolated from primary GBM patients and were kind gifts from Dr. Shideng Bao (Cleveland Clinic) and Dr. Jeremy Rich (University of Pittsburgh). A series of functional assays were applied to validate the stemness phenotypes of these GSCs, including the sphereformation, in vitro limiting dilution assay, multipotent differentiation, and tumor initiation in immunocompromised mice in our previous studies [26, 27]. Specifically, the GSC lines used in this study were derived from GBM patients as follows: T3359 (primary, 31-year-old female), T3691 (primary, 59-year-old female), T387 (primary, 76-year-old female), T4121 (recurrent, 53-year-old male), and T456 (primary, 8-year-old female). GSCs were cultured in Neurobasal-A Medium (without phenol red, Gibco) supplemented with B-27 Supplement Minus Vitamin A (Gibco), Sodium Pyruvate (100 μM, Gibco), L-Glutamine (200 μM, Gibco), Penicillin Streptomycin (1%, Gibco), recombinant epidermal growth factor (EGF, 20 ng/mL, PrimeGene) and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, 20 ng/mL, PrimeGene). When differentiation assay was performed, GSCs were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Vazyme) for 2 weeks to differentiate into non-stem tumor cells (NSTCs). The human U973 (RRID:CVCL_0007) and THP1 (RRID:CVCL_0006, RPMI-1640) cell lines were purchased from Haixing Biosciences and cultured in U937 Cell Line Complete Medium (Haixing Biosciences, RPMI-1640, 10% FBS, 1% Penicillin Streptomycin) and THP1 Cell Line Complete Medium (Haixing Biosciences, 10% FBS, 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1% Penicillin Streptomycin) respectively. The HEK293T cell (RRID:CVCL_0063) was obtained from ATCC and cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Vazyme). The mouse primary bone marrow derived macrophage (BMDM) was cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Vazyme) and recombinant mouse CSF-1 (M-CSF, 20 ng/mL, Abclonal). All cells were tested negative for mycoplasma contamination and incubated in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Intracranial tumor formation and drug treatment

All animal procedures were performed following the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the University Animal Welfare Committee, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. 5-week male BALB/c nude mice were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and maintained in the pathogen-free barrier animal facility at the Experimental Animal Center of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. Animals were assigned to experimental groups using simple randomization. The protocols of intracranial tumor injection were described previously [27]. Briefly, GSCs were transduced with lentivirus expressing shNT or shC5 and 20,000 cells of each group were intracranially injected into the right cerebral cortex at a depth of 3.5 mm. A total of 10 mice per group were used for survival analysis. For the PMX205 treatment assay, mice bearing wild-type GSC-derived tumors were randomly assigned to the control group (normal drinking water) or the PMX205 treatment group (n = 6 per group). PMX205 (Selleck) was dissolved in drinking water at a concentration of 20 μg/mL, and the solution was freshly prepared every three days. Based on an estimated daily water intake of 4 mL per mouse and an average body weight of 20 g, the approximate daily dose of PMX205 was 4 mg/kg/day. Treatment was initiated from day 3 post-injection and continued until the end of the experiment. Humane endpoints were defined as follows: (1) Significant weight loss with a cachectic appearance; (2) hunched posture or severe lethargy; (3) neurological signs such as seizures, paralysis, or ataxia; (4) inability to reach food or water. Mice meeting any of these criteria were immediately euthanized and recorded as death for survival analysis. Exclusion criteria included failure of tumor engraftment confirmed by postmortem brain examination (if no visible tumor was found, the mouse was excluded from all analyses). Tumor burden was assessed by monitoring survival time (the primary endpoint) and confirmed by histological examination of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained brain sections from mice. For survival analysis, the endpoint was defined as reaching a humane endpoint, and survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method with the log-rank test. Because this was a pilot study, a formal power calculation was not required. All analyses were performed blinded such that experimenters performing intracranical injection, and data analysis were unaware of the groups and treatments.

The shRNAs targeting C5 and C5AR1 were cloned into pLKO.1-puro and the knockdown efficiency of shRNAs were rigorously verified by qRT-PCR and western blot prior to functional assays and animal experiments. For cMyc overexpression, the human cMYC coding sequence (CDS) was cloned into pLV3-FLAG-puro vector. The shRNA sequences of C5 and C5aR1 used in this study are:

PBMC, BMDM isolation and transwell migration assay

Under the approval of the Medical Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from donor blood using density gradient centrifugation as previously reported [9]. In brief, whole blood was diluted 1:1 in Hank's buffer and carefully layered over an equal volume of Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare) and centrifuged at 500 g for 30 min. The PBMC layer at the interface was harvested and cultured in RPMI/1640 medium (Gibco) containing 10% FBS (Gibco). Following a 4-h culture period, floating cells were removed via PBS washing. The adherent monocytes were further cultured for 6 days in complete medium supplemented with 40 ng/mL M-CSF (Abclonal) to differentiate them into PBMC-derived macrophages, which were then used for subsequent experiments. Mouse primary bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDM) were isolated from mouse femurs and cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco) containing 10% FBS (Gibco). After seven days stimulation of M-CSF (20 ng/mL, Abclonal), cells were used for downstream migration assay. For U937 and THP1-derived macrophages, U937 and THP1 cells were cultured with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for M0 macrophage differentiation for 24 h. The transwell migration assay was performed by 24-well transwell plates (Corning) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Briefly, 5 × 105 primed U937 or THP1 cells or 4 × 105 mature BMDM cells were inoculated into the upper chamber, rC5a (SinoBiological)/PMX205 (MedChemExpress) or the corresponding conditioned medium was added to the upper and lower chambers. After 48 h of migration, crystal violet staining was used to visualize the migrated cells.

LC–MS/MS analysis of PMX205

Whole brain samples were obtained and homogenized in an equal volume of Milli-Q water. Subsequently, 100 μL of tissue homogenate was deproteinized with 1:3 ice-cold LC–MS grade acetonitrile. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected and dried by vacuum concentration at room temperature. The dried residues were resuspended in 100 μL of 75% methanol/water (v/v), and 10 μL of the resulting solution was analyzed using a quadrupole-Orbitrap ultra-high-resolution mass spectrometer (Orbitrap Exploris 240, Thermo Scientific) coupled to an Agilent 1200 series HPLC system. Chromatographic separation was performed on a BEH C18 analytical column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) under binary gradient elution conditions. Quantification of PMX-205 was achieved by extracting the ion signal at m/z 420.25 from high-resolution MS1 data. PMX205 concentrations in brain tissue were expressed as ng/g tissue.

For method validation, an external calibration strategy using matrix‑matched, pre‑spiked calibration standards was employed. Blank brain homogenates were spiked with known concentrations of PMX205 before sample extraction and then processed using the same sample preparation procedure as the experimental brain samples. The calibration range was 1–100 ng/mL. This pre‑spiked matrix calibration approach was used instead of a solvent‑only calibration curve because it better accounts for the influence of sample processing and brain matrix effects on the analytical response. Quantification was performed using the peak area of the analyte based on the matrix‑matched external calibration curve.

Extraction recovery was evaluated at three quality control concentrations (1, 5, and 20 ng/mL) covering the low, medium, and high range relevant to the measured brain concentrations. Pre‑spiked matrix samples were generated by spiking known concentrations of PMX205 into blank brain homogenates before extraction, followed by the complete sample preparation procedure. Post‑spiked matrix samples were generated by extracting blank brain homogenates first and then adding the analyte to the final extracts at the same nominal concentrations. Recovery was calculated as: Extraction recovery (%) = Peak areapre-spiked matrix/Peak areapost-spiked matrix × 100%. The extraction recoveries were 76.56%, 78.65%, and 81.78% at 1, 5, and 20 ng/mL, respectively, with a mean recovery of approximately 79.0%, indicating consistent extraction performance across the concentration range of interest.

The limit of detection (LOD) was estimated using the signal‑to‑noise ratio (S/N) based on the root mean square (RMS) noise method. The signal was defined as the analyte peak height, and the noise was calculated as the RMS fluctuation of the baseline intensity in a noise region adjacent to the analyte peak. The S/N values measured at different analyte concentrations showed a linear relationship with concentration. Using the equation LOD = 3/k, where k represents the slope of the linear regression between S/N and analyte concentration, the LOD was estimated to be approximately 0.2648 ng/mL. The S/N at the lowest calibration standard (1 ng/mL) was 11, supporting 1 ng/mL as the lower limit of quantification for this assay.

Bioinformatics analysis

The microarray/RNA-seq mRNA expression and survival data of glioma patients of TCGA, CGGA and Rembrandt databases were downloaded from GlioVis (http://gliovis.bioinfo.cnio.es). The survival analysis of complement genes was performed by online tools of Gliovis, and survival curves were compared by the two‑sided log‑rank test. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed by R package ClusterProfiler (version 4.20.0, R version 4.5.1). The Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed by GSEA4.1.0 version software with the following parameters: number of permutations = 1000, permutation type = gene set, enrichment statistic = weighted, metric for ranking genes = Signal2Noise, and minimum size of gene set = 10. Gene sets used in this study were downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). A false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25 and nominal P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The Uniport database was used to identify complement genes that encode secreted proteins. The Brain-TIME database was used to acquire gene expression levels in CD45− cells.

For multivariable Cox regression analysis, the proportional hazards assumption was tested using the cox-ph function with a threshold of P > 0.05. A risk score based on the regression coefficients was calculated for each patient as the linear combination of expression values weighted by the corresponding Cox coefficients. Patients were divided into high‑risk and low‑risk groups using the median risk score as the cut‑off.

Single‑cell RNA‑seq data of GBM tumors were obtained from previously published datasets (GSE182109 and CGGA database). Raw count matrices were processed using the Seurat package (version 4.3.0) in R. Quality control thresholds were set as follows: genes expressed in ≥ 3 cells, cells with at least 200 detected genes, mitochondrial gene percentage < 25%. Data were normalized using the LogNormalize method with a scale factor of 10,000. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the top 2000 highly variable genes selected by the vst method. The first 20 principal components were used for uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) dimensionality reduction and graph‑based clustering (resolution = 0.5).

Spatial transcriptomics data of GBM samples were obtained from the GEO database (accession number GSM5833537). Data were processed using the Seurat package (version 4.3.0). Briefly, spots with fewer than 200 genes, more than 10,000 genes, fewer than 200 UMI counts, or more than 20% mitochondrial genes were filtered out. Normalization was performed using the SCTransform method with regression of mitochondrial percentage. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were performed using PCA (top 40 PCs) and graph‑based clustering (resolution = 0.8). We employed a permutation test‑based spatial neighborhood enrichment analysis, a widely used method in spatial transcriptomics, to quantitatively evaluate the spatial proximity between GSC‑high and TAM‑high spots. Both spot populations were defined using the AddModuleScore() function based on signature marker gene sets. For each GSC‑high spot, the total number of adjacent TAM‑high spots within 100 μm was quantified. We generated permuted datasets by keeping GSC‑high labels fixed and randomly permuting TAM‑high labels across all spots. Enrichment scores were calculated as the ratio of observed co‑occurrence frequency to the expected value. Interaction strengths were assessed using z‑scores, indicating the magnitude of the observed adjacency compared with permuted datasets (random background).

Statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were repeated at least three times unless otherwise indicated. Data were presented as the mean ± SEM and analyzed with GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 software unless otherwise specified. Differences were determined by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test between two groups, and one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For correlation analyses, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used. For survival analyses, overall survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were compared using the two‑sided log‑rank test. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additional materials and methods can be found in supplementary data.
