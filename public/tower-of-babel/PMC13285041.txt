Impact of laser-assisted and different pre-treatment modalities on dentin surface interface and bond strength of fiber posts (In-vitro study)
Authors: Rewaa G AboElhassan, Alaa M Eltaweel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDentin–cement interface can be considered a critical point in fiber post cementation. Post-space preparation may leave a smear layer and sodium hypochlorite remnants on root dentin, which can compromise bonding between fiber posts and self-adhesive resin cement. This study evaluated the effect of different post-space dentin pretreatment modalities on dentin surface characteristics and push-out bond strength of fiber posts.MethodsForty-five extracted single-rooted mandibular second premolars were endodontically treated and assigned to five groups according to post-space dentin pretreatment (n = 9): 2.5% chitosan (CH), 5% apple vinegar (AV), Er,Cr:YSGG laser (ErCr), 970 nm diode laser (DL), and 0.9% saline as control group (CG). Fiber posts were cemented using self-adhesive resin cement. Specimens were sectioned into coronal, middle, and apical slices for push-out bond strength testing and failure mode analysis. One additional specimen from each group was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM).ResultsPush-out bond strength was significantly influenced by surface treatment. The ErCr laser group showed the highest mean bond strength, followed by the chitosan, diode laser, and apple vinegar groups, all of which demonstrated significantly higher values than the control group. Differences between root regions were not statistically significant. Mixed failure was the predominant mode in all experimental groups. SEM revealed superior smear layer removal in the ErCr laser and chitosan groups, whereas the diode laser and apple vinegar groups showed only partial dentinal tubules exposure, and the control group retained a dense smear layer.ConclusionsPost-space dentin pretreatment improved fiber post bond strength and interfacial adaptation compared with saline treatment. ErCr laser and 2.5% chitosan showed the most favorable outcomes and may represent promising conditioning strategies before fiber post cementation.Keywords: Chitosan; Apple Vinegar; Diode laser; Er,Cr:YSGG laser; Push out bond strength

Background

Fiber post dislodgement, primarily occurring at the dentin-bonding interface, was showed as the most prevalent failure in restored endodontically treated teeth [1]. The optimized management of the dentin surface is essential for the long-term stability and success of fiber posts bonding to dentin [2]. As this interface considered the weakest point in intracanal bonding to fiber posts, demineralization of extrafibrillar dentin is an effective strategy to achieve a strong resin-dentin bond and creation of effective hybrid layer [3]. A primary challenge in this process is the inability to achieve complete infiltration of resin monomers throughout the full depth of the demineralized dentin surface, even after meticulous biomechanical cleansing of root canals. This often resulted in a zone of exposed collagen fibrils at the base of the hybrid layer, compromising the bond integrity between the dentin and the fiber post [4]. A further significant challenge is the effective removal of the smear layer formed during and after post-space preparation. This heterogeneous layer is composed of residual endodontic materials, such as sealer and gutta-percha, in addition to dentinal debris and potential microbial contaminants from oral cavity [5]. By occluding the dentinal tubules, this dense layer, which is resistant to water rinsing, physically obstructs resin monomer infiltration and chemically interferes with the adhesion mechanism [6]. NaOCl irrigation together with EDTA is the commonly used irrigation protocol during root canal treatment and post-space preparation to eliminate the smear layer [7]. NaOCl was reported to dissolve soft tissues and organic substrates of smear layer, inhibit bacterial activity, deproteinize dentin, and improve adhesive wettability [8]. However, residual NaOCl was showed to impair resin polymerization by releasing oxygen and chloramine-derived radicals, which interfere with the adhesive systems polymerization [9, 10]. EDTA has been described primarily as a calcium-chelating agent promoting superficial dentin demineralization and smear layer removal, which might facilitate the penetration and adaptation of self-adhesive resin cements to the underlying dentin surface [11, 12]. Nevertheless, there was a controversy in effectiveness of EDTA to enhance bond strength, as it was also declared that its use before fiber post insertion did not increase the mean push-out bond strength in any part of the root [13]. In addition to its erosive effect on dentin microstructure [14], and cytotoxicity if extruded beyond the apex [15], EDTA persists in water as stable, non-biodegradable metal complexes, making it an environmental pollutant [16]. Yet, the ideal characteristics of the dentin pretreatment solutions were not fully met by current synthetic chemicals, therefore, recent research showed a great shift towards evaluating the potential of natural alternatives.

Chitosan is one of the favorable natural alternatives that consists of a long biopolymer chain of N-acetyl glucosamine produced by deacetylation of chitin, and possesses a favorable biocompatible and biodegradable properties [17]. The protonated amino and free hydroxyl groups on this polymer enable it to act as an effective chelating agent on the negatively charged dentin surface. This could facilitate the cross-linking of demineralized collagen fibrils providing structural reinforcement to the collagen network, and thereby, improve its stability against enzymatic and hydrolytic degradation [18]. The subsequent deposition of a calcium phosphate layer on the dentinal surface could also preserve the integrity of the resin-dentin interface over time, making it a strategic biomodification treatment to enhance bond durability [19]. In addition, chitosan showed a broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against persistent root canals pathogens such as Enterococcus faecalis [20]. Furthermore, its performance in smear layer removal and enhancing dentin bond strength was comparable to 17% EDTA [21, 22], and even superior at higher concentrations [23]. Nevertheless, its comparative efficacy against laser irradiation and other natural chelators, such as apple vinegar, remains an area requiring further investigations.

Apple vinegar, another natural chelator, is extracted from apple fruit and highly biocompatible, cost-effective, and easily accessible solution [24]. This natural irrigant exhibited a bactericidal activity against microorganisms associated with root canals infections such as E. faecalis and Staphylococcus aureus [25]. Moreover, it has been shown to effectively remove the smear layer from the canal walls, enhancing the exposure of dentinal tubules. Its cleaning ability was also comparable to that of EDTA [24, 26]. Few studies have investigated apple vinegar’s potential as a root canal irrigant [27], however, to our knowledge, no investigations were applied yet to explore its efficacy on bonding of fiber post when it is used as a surface treatment of dentin.

The introduction of laser technology marks a significant step forward in dentin disinfection and surface treatment. ErCr laser was found to promote dentin permeability through opening of dentinal tubules and induce selective hard tissue ablation due to their strong affinity for water [28]. Additionally, it was also showed to superiorly remove smear layer, improve the irrigant dynamics, enhance penetration within dentinal tubules, and increase their antimicrobial and cleaning efficacy [28, 29]. Furthermore, ErCr laser irradiation revealed a potential to enhance the bonding quality of both sound and carious dentin substrates [30]. This synergistic effect could promote deeper resin infiltration, and in turn enhance the fiber post bonding strength. Another type of effective laser irradiation is diode laser, which can also positively affect the inaccessible areas to mechanical and chemical treatments in root canals [31–33]. Previous studies have reported that diode laser irradiation might enhance bond strength and cement penetration through dentinal tubules by modifying dentin microstructure and composition [34]. Diode laser irradiation has also been reported to promote smear layer removal and reduce apical leakage [32]. However, the thermo-mechanical effects induced by laser irradiation including mineral changes, carbonization, and microcrack formation could negatively influence dentin–resin adhesion, necessitating further comparative investigations with safer natural irrigants [35].

Despite the availability of several dentin conditioning strategies, evidence remains limited regarding their comparative effectiveness on post-space root dentin before fiber post cementation. Previous studies have mainly evaluated individual agents for smear layer removal or dentin surface modification, whereas direct comparisons between natural chelators and laser-assisted surface treatments under the same experimental conditions remain scarce. In particular, although chitosan has shown promising chelating and smear layer removal properties, its performance relative to laser-based protocols is still insufficiently clarified. Likewise, apple vinegar has demonstrated cleaning ability and antimicrobial potential as a natural irrigant, yet its influence on fiber post bonding to root dentin has not been adequately investigated. In addition, while laser irradiation may improve dentin permeability and surface cleanliness, concerns remain regarding possible thermo-mechanical alterations that may adversely affect dentin–resin adhesion.

Thus, the aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of different post-space dentin pretreatment modalities including 2.5% chitosan, 5% apple vinegar, 2780 nm ErCr laser, and 970 nm diode laser on the push-out bond strength of fiber posts cemented with self-adhesive resin cement. Failure modes after debonding and dentin surface morphology including smear layer removal and dentinal tubules exposure were also assessed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The null hypothesis was that the tested post-space pretreatment protocols would not produce significant differences in the push-out bond strength of fiber posts.

Materials and methods

Sample size estimation

Sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) based on an F-test for ANOVA [36]. The calculation assumed a significance level (α) of 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and an expected large effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.62) estimated from previous studies previous studies evaluating push-out bond strength after different dentin surface pretreatments [12, 28, 37, 38]. The minimum required sample size was 8 specimens per group. To compensate for possible laboratory processing loss, this number was increased to 9 specimens per group, resulting in a total of 45 specimens for quantitative testing. In addition, one extra specimen was included in each group and reserved exclusively for qualitative SEM evaluation before post insertion.

Samples selection and preparation

Samples preparation and examinations were conducted at the Conservative Dentistry Department Laboratory, Faculty of Dentistry, Alexandria University. Forty-five extracted human mandibular second premolars were selected for this study. Inclusion was limited to teeth with fully formed apices, no carious lesions, cracks, fractures, root resorption, or previous endodontic treatment. The presence of a single straight root canal was confirmed by pre-operative periapical radiographic examination, and teeth showing canal calcification, internal resorption, root dilaceration, or marked canal curvature were excluded. The external root surfaces were also inspected visually after removal of residual soft tissues and calculus deposits using a periodontal curette and an ultrasonic scaler (Woodpecker; Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Guilin, Guangxi, China). After rinsing with distilled water, the teeth were preserved in 0.1% thymol solution until use. Each specimen was then measured using a digital caliper (Tmt321501, TOTAL Tools, Suzhou Industrial Park, China) to confirm dimensions within the cervical line region of 7 ± 1 mm buccolingually and 6 ± 1 mm mesiodistally, as well as a root length of 15 ± 1 mm from the cemento-enamel junction to the root apex. Teeth with similar root length and diameter were subsequently stored in saline solution at room temperature until use [39].

All teeth were decoronated at the level of cement-enamel junction, with a cutting disc under copious water cooling, producing flat coronal surfaces with exposed root canals and ensuring a uniform root length of 15 mm. For better handling and manipulation during canal instrumentation and post space preparation, specimens were stabilized in a lower typodont acrylic model (Smile Dental Equipment Co., Ltd., Zhengzhou, Henan, China).

Root canal and post space preparation

For each root, canal patency was confirmed with a size #10 K-file, and the working length was established 1 mm short of the apical foramen using a size #15 K-file (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland). Root canal preparation was then performed in a crown-down manner using sequential HyFlex CM NiTi rotary files (COLTENE/Whaledent AG, Altstätten, Switzerland) in continuous rotation at 500 rpm and a torque setting of up to 2.5 Ncm, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, until a final apical size of 40/0.04 was reached. Throughout instrumentation, including patency procedures, the canals were irrigated with 5 ml of 5.25% sodium hypochlorite after each file change. Following completion of instrumentation, 5 ml of 17% EDTA (META BIOMED Co., Ltd., Cheongju-si, Chungcheongbuk-do, Republic of Korea), was used as the final chelating rinse and maintained within the canals for 1 min for smear layer removal, while reducing the risk of excessive dentinal erosion associated with prolonged exposure. The canals were then flushed with 5 ml of distilled water [40, 41].

After completion of irrigation, the canals were dried with sterile #40 absorbent paper points (META BIOMED Co., Ltd., Cheongju-si, Chungcheongbuk-do, Republic of Korea) until no visible moisture remained. Obturation was performed by cold lateral condensation using a master gutta-percha cone size 40/0.04 (META BIOMED Co., Ltd., Cheongju-si, Chungcheongbuk-do, Republic of Korea), and an epoxy resin-based sealer (AH Plus; Dentsply DeTrey GmbH, Konstanz, Germany). The sealer was mixed according to the manufacturer’s instructions by dispensing equal amounts of base and catalyst and blending them until a homogeneous creamy consistency was obtained. The master cone was lightly coated with sealer and inserted to the working length, and a pre-obturation periapical radiograph was taken to verify proper cone adaptation. Lateral compaction was then carried out using a preselected spreader advanced to 2 mm short of the working length, and accessory gutta-percha cones sizes #15/0.02 and #20/0.02 were inserted as needed to achieve complete canal obturation (META BIOMED Co., Ltd., Cheongju-si, Chungcheongbuk-do, Republic of Korea). Excess gutta-percha was removed at the canal orifice level using a heated plugger, vertically condensed with a cold plugger, and the canal orifices were sealed with a temporary restorative material (avit; 3M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany). A postoperative periapical radiograph was subsequently obtained to confirm the optimal quality of obturation and the absence of visible voids [40]. Specimens were stored for one week at 37 °C and 100% humidity in an incubator (BST 50–20; VEB MLW Dentalfabrik Leipzig, Leipzig, Germany).

Post spaces were then prepared to a standardized length of 11 mm, leaving a 3 mm for apical seal. Removal of gutta-percha was performed sequentially with Gates Glidden drills sizes #1–#3 (Dental Gates; Harald Nordin SA, Chailly/Montreux, Switzerland), followed by Peeso reamers up to size # 3 (Harald Nordin SA, Chailly/Montreux, Switzerland). The final post space was completed using size #3 post drill (Glassix Plus; Harald Nordin SA, Chailly/Montreux, Switzerland) [42]. Periapical radiographs were obtained to verify adequate canals emptying, confirm the standardized post-spaces length, and ensure preservation of the 3-mm apical seal. The post spaces were also inspected under magnification using 3.5 × loupes (Rose Micro Solutions, Amherst, NY, USA) for the absence of visible obturation remnants before application of the assigned surface treatments. To enhance standardization and minimize operator-related variability, all root canal treatment and post-space preparation procedures were performed by the same operator.

After post-space preparation, no additional common irrigation protocol was performed before group allocation. This was done to avoid introducing a confounding variable that could independently influence dentinal tubules permeability and bond strength. Instead, post-space debris and smear layer management were standardized by applying the assigned surface treatment directly to the prepared dentin in each group.

Fiber posts insertion and cementation

After surface treatments, the specimens were dried using absorbent paper points. Fiber posts (GLassix Plus #3; Nordin, Switzerland) were cleaned with 70% alcohol applied using a micro brush and subsequently dried with compressed air. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, self-adhesive resin cement (RelyX U200; 3M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany) was inserted into the prepared post spaces using an auto-mix cartridge. Each post was coated with cement and seated to full depth under gentle finger pressure. After removal of excess cement using a micro brush, polymerization was carried out using an LED light-curing unit (RTA Mini-Cure; Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Guilin, Guangxi, China). with a reported output intensity of (minimum 1000 mW/cm2) for at least 40 s, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To standardize light activation, the curing tip was positioned as close as possible to the coronal aspect of the post space ideally within 1 mm, primarily from the occlusal surface, positioning the tip at a right angle to the tooth surface and maintained in the same position and orientation for all specimens throughout the curing procedure. Additional curing from buccal and lingual surfaces was performed to ensure peripheral polymerization. The light guide tip was inspected from one sample to another for contaminants, scratches, or resin buildup and cleaned with alcohol. No silane or other coupling agent was applied to the posts prior to cementation, so that the post–cement interface remained uniform in all specimens and the effect of the tested post-space dentin pretreatments could be evaluated precisely. A single operator performed all specimens’ cementation to assess reproducibility. The coronal access of all specimens was sealed with a temporary filling material, and the samples were stored in an incubator at 37 °C and 100% humidity for 24 h before testing [23, 42].

Push-out bond strength testing

Specimens were embedded in cylindrical copper molds (15 mm in diameter × 18 mm in height) filled with chemically cured acrylic resin (Acrostone Dental & Medical Supplies, Cairo, Egypt) [45]. After 24 h, the specimens were sectioned perpendicular to the long axis using a low-speed diamond saw (Micracut 150; METKON Instruments Inc., Bursa, Turkey) under continuous water cooling. In each specimen, one 1.5 mm thick section was gained from each cervical, middle, and apical thirds of the root (Fig. 3). Section thickness and post diameters (coronal and apical) were verified using a digital caliper. This procedure yielded one slice from each root third, giving a total of 27 slices per group (n = 9 × 3 thirds).

Representative illustration of 1.5-mm-thick sections obtained from the coronal, middle, and apical thirds of the root and embedded in chemically cured acrylic resin blocks

Push-out testing was performed using a universal testing machine (Model 5ST; Tinius Olsen Ltd., Salfords, Surrey, England) (Fig. 4). To avoid taper-related discrepancies, each specimen was positioned on the machine’s metal support in an apical–coronal orientation, ensuring the post was displaced toward the broader area of the root slice. A 1.0 mm cylindrical plunger tip was aligned to contact only the fiber post without touching the surrounding dentin or cement (Fig. 5). Load was applied at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min in an apical-coronal direction until post dislodgment occurred. Bond failure was identified by visible protrusion of the post part from the root section.

Scanning electron microscopy evaluation

For qualitative analysis, the additional specimen was randomly allocated from each group for SEM evaluation to assess smear layer removal and dentinal tubules patency. Buccal and lingual longitudinal grooves were created on the external root surface under profuse irrigation without entering the canal lumen, using a low-speed double-faced diamond disk. The specimens were split vertically using a stainless-steel chisel to expose the dentin surfaces. The sections were dehydrated in ascending ethanol concentrations solutions (40%, 50%, 60%, 75%, 95%) for 20 min each, mounted on aluminum stubs, and sputter-coated with a thin layer of a gold–palladium alloy to enhance conductivity [44, 47]. Specimens were mounted on metal stubs using a conductive adhesive and examined under SEM (JSM-5510; JEOL Ltd., Akishima, Tokyo, Japan) at × 1500 magnification. Digital photomicrographs were obtained from the center of each third for comparative analysis among the groups [44, 47].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for push-out bond strength values. Normality of data distribution was assessed using descriptive statistics, plots, and the Shapiro–Wilk test, while homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Because the study design included two independent factors, namely surface treatment group and root canal third (coronal, middle, and apical), push-out bond strength values were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) to evaluate the main effects of both factors and their interaction. When significant effects were detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction. Estimated marginal means with standard error (SE) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Failure mode distribution was analyzed using the chi-square test with Monte Carlo correction. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. In addition, for each group, the qualitative assessment of smear layer removal and resin cement penetration from the SEM micrographs was performed descriptively to support the bond strength findings.

Results

The interaction between surface treatment and root canal thirds was also not significant (F = 1.43, p = 0.19) (Table 1).

SEM analysis showed clear group-dependent differences in dentin surface morphology after application of the tested surface treatments. The ErCr laser group (Fig. 7A) and the chitosan group (Fig. 7B) exhibited the most favorable surface pattern, characterized by effective smear layer removal and clearly exposed dentinal tubules. The diode laser group (Fig. 7C) and the apple vinegar group (Fig. 7D) showed partial to moderate smear layer removal, whereas the control group (Fig. 7E) retained a dense smear layer covering the dentin surface and occluding most dentinal tubules. SEM evaluation was performed as a descriptive assessment only, without the use of a scoring system or quantitative image analysis.

Discussion

The present study showed that post-space dentin pretreatment significantly influenced the push-out bond strength of fiber posts luted with self-adhesive resin cement, whereas root canal third and the interaction between surface treatment and root canal third were not statistically significant. Therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected. Among the tested protocols, ErCr laser and 2.5% chitosan produced the most favorable bond strength values and SEM surface characteristics, while diode laser and apple vinegar showed intermediate improvement. Additionally, all experimental pretreatment protocols in this study were associated with significantly higher bond strength values than the control group, which retained a dense smear layer and largely occluded dentinal tubules on SEM evaluation, supporting the relevance of surface conditioning before fiber-post cementation.

These findings might be explained by the influence of dentin surface cleanliness on the bonding behavior of self-adhesive resin cement. Although self-adhesive resin cements showed the capability to chemically interact with hydroxyapatite [48], their ability to penetrate or demineralize a thick smear layer might be limited [49, 50]. Therefore, removal or modification of the post-space smear layer could improve cement adaptation and micromechanical interaction with radicular dentin. This interpretation was supported by the SEM observations, where groups showed more effective smear layer removal and dentinal tubule exposure generally demonstrated higher bond strength values.

In the present study, ErCr laser showed the highest mean bond strength values (13.45 ± 4.24 MPa) which was significantly higher than both apple vinegar and control groups. This might be related to the dentin internal changes generated by irradiation. The thermomechanical process involved energizing water molecules that absorbed the laser energy and exploded within the tissue, producing micro-ablation that altered the dentin surface, leading to both structural and chemical modifications. Surface roughening and the exposure of intertubular dentin’s collagen matrix might further enhance the ability of the dentin walls to bond with resin cement [28, 51]. These potent cleansing mechanisms could also explain the distinct smear removal after laser application as observed in SEM micrographs of this group. The current findings agreed with many previous studies reporting that ErCr lasers effectively ablated the smear layer, enhanced micromechanical retention, and improved resin penetration into exposed dentinal tubules [28, 35, 46]. Moreover, a recent study by Ahmed and Hussein (2024) also showed significant increases in push-out strength and effective smear layer elimination when ErCr with different irradiation powers were used especially in apical regions, highlighting its clinical advantage for deep canal cleaning [52]. Another a recent study by Abaza et al. (2025) demonstrated superior smear layer removal with ErCr laser compared with diode laser and ultrasonic irrigation [29, 37]. Although ErCr lasers appeared to combine micro-roughening, favorable surface chemistry and effective smear removal, which have been translated into superior clinical bonding performance in this study, some investigations have reported less significant improvement in bond strength after ErCr treatment. This might be attributed to variations in laser parameters, lower output power, more application time, or interaction with different irrigants rather than those applied in this study [47]. Similarly, Kirmali et al. (2015) showed that when different intensities of ErCr were applied, the improvement in fiber post bonding strength to dentin was not always consistent [53]. Disagreement with study results was also likely related to methodological differences suggesting that different laser parameters could critically influence the outcomes.

The chitosan group in the current study showed comparable mean values (13.10 ± 8.15 MPa) to ErCr with no significant difference between them. SEM micrographs of dentin surface after 2.5% chitosan treatment also showed a marked smear layer removal with widened tubules orifices after irrigation. This favorable performance might be related to the chelating and dentin-biomodifying effects of the high-molecular weight chitosan at concentration of 2.5%, including effective smear layer removal, partial demineralization of the extrafibrillar dentin, preservation of the collagen fibrils integrity by retention of apatite crystallites within the collagen network, and limiting collagen denaturation. Meanwhile, the persistence of some smear plugs in this context might reduce dentin permeability, contributing positively to the long-term stability of bonding [54]. In addition to crosslinking with collagen, chitosan has been also shown to consume H⁺ ions and elevate the pH, reducing matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activation through the acidic conditions generated by adhesive systems, thereby reinforcing the dentin matrix [55, 56]. This could explain the emerging literature on chitosan’s role in improving long-term adhesion durability, particularly at higher concentrations as used in this study [21, 23, 43]. These findings were further supported by recent Alves et al. (2024) systematic review and meta-analysis of in vitro studies, as they concluded that dentin pretreatment with chitosan generally improves bond strength, supporting its biomodifying role at the adhesive interface [2]. In addition, Rifaat et al. (2025) and Ahmadi et al. (2025) reported favorable push-out bond strength outcomes when 2–2.5% chitosan was used as a post-space irrigant or root dentin pretreatment before fiber-post cementation with self-adhesive resin cement, which is in agreement with the high bond strength values observed in the present study [21, 23]. In contrast to these findings, Vasei et al. (2021) observed that lower concentrations of chitosan produced greater improvements in bond strength than 2.5% [57]. This divergence might be related to differences in the molecular weight of chitosan, contact time, and the type of bond used. Other investigations had also reported inconsistent or minimal improvements when using chitosan unless combined with ultrasonic activation [12]. As lower concentration 0.2% was used in previous study, the variability in current results might reflect these concentration-dependent effects. Thus, while these findings confirmed the potential of chitosan, they also highlighted the need for standardized protocols regarding molecular weight, concentration, and application time.

The diode laser group also showed a significant improvement in push-out bond strength compared with the control group, with a mean value of (11.28 ± 3.30 MPa). Although it showed lower mean bond strength than the ErCr laser and chitosan groups, the difference was not statistically significant. SEM evaluation revealed a partially modified dentin surface with only partial smear layer removal, suggesting that diode irradiation produced localized thermal surface alterations rather than complete dentin cleaning. Although such alterations were assumed to facilitate partial resin infiltration and improve bonding compared with untreated dentin, they did not achieve the uniformly clean surfaces observed with ErCr laser or chitosan. This might explain the moderate performance of diode laser conditioning in the present study. This interpretation was consistent with Alfredo et al. (2009), who reported that 980-nm diode laser irradiation at parameters close to those used in the current study produced a laser-modified radicular dentin surface characterized by residual smear layer, fissures, and limited dentinal tubules exposure [58]. Likewise, Borges et al. (2020) reported more favorable bond strength outcomes with ErCr laser than with diode laser with no significant difference in push-out test, while diode treatment showed an intermediate effect [28]. The mechanism of diode laser action has been attributed mainly to its deep penetration and preferential absorption by pigmented chromophores, with relatively limited interaction with hydroxyapatite and water [58]. Consequently, its effect on dentin surface morphology appears to be more thermal than ablative, which may restrict effective tubules opening and resin infiltration [31]. This might explain why the diode laser group in the present study showed lower mean bond strength than the ErCr laser group. Yet, the diode laser group in the present study still showed significantly higher bond strength than the control group and values comparable to those of the chitosan and apple vinegar groups, supporting its role as a useful dentin-conditioning modality. This finding was also consistent with Naik et al. (2024), who suggested that diode laser irradiation combined with an etch-and-rinse adhesive system might improve dentin bond strength compared with untreated surfaces, although their bonding protocol differed from that used in the present study [59].

Although the apple vinegar group showed lower bond strength values (9.99 ± 2.27 MPa) compared with chitosan and laser groups, it demonstrated a moderate but significant improvement in bond strength compared with control group. Its difference from the diode laser and chitosan groups was not statistically significant. In this context, Ali et al. (2020) reported that the push out bond strength results of apple vinegar were shown to be comparable to that of known potent chelators such as 17% EDTA when used as a final rinse [60]. Moreover, Elsheikh et al. (2022) showed that 5% of apple vinegar as final rinse demonstrated the highest mean value effect on the intra-radicular dentin roughness between all explored irrigating solutions, including 17% EDTA and 0.2% chitosan [61]. The higher surface roughness produced on dentin after rinsing might increase micromechanical interlocking and adhesion [62]. Another recent study by Farouk et al. (2025), showed that apple vinegar treatment had a positive impact on dentin micro-tensile bond strength with self-etch universal adhesive mode [63]. Although that previous study was not specifically applied on fiber posts, it suggested that the acidic components of apple vinegar might modify the dentin surface in a manner that could enhance bonding. These findings were compatible with SEM analysis of the apple vinegar group in the current study, which revealed a cleaner dentin surface with partially exposed tubules openings than control group after irrigation. Similar findings were reported by Safwat et al. (2017), Abdelghany et al. (2020), and Abdelfattah et al. (2025), who reported favorable effects of the apple vinegar final rinse on eliminating smear layer without affecting the calcium content of intra-radicular dentin [44, 64, 65]. On the other hand, studies by Spanó et al. (2009) and Kirchhoff et al. (2014) reported less efficient calcium removal by apple vinegar, with lower amounts of chelated Ca2 + in solution compared with other known chelators such as EDTA. [66, 67] This might explain the significant lower performance to that of ErCr laser group in this study. Nevertheless, this interpretation suggested that apple vinegar could improve dentin surface characteristics and smear layer removal in a way that might favor fiber post adhesion, although its effect appeared less consistent than that of stronger chelators or laser-based pretreatments.

Several factors, including variations in dentinal tubules densities, technical sensitivity of adhesive application within narrow post spaces, and reduced transmission of light to the apical region, were commonly cited as contributors to the expected regression in bond strength from coronal to apical dentin [68]. In this context, a previous study found a significant difference in push-out bond strength among different root regions when 2.5% chitosan was used, with lower bond strength values recorded in the apical third across all groups [23]. Nevertheless, in the present study, the location within the root canal did not significantly influence bond strength when self-adhesive resin cement was used, a finding consistent with previous reports [69]. Similarly, Elsharkawy (2021) also reported no significant difference among radicular regions after diode laser irradiation [70]. Despite their initially low pH, self-adhesive cements typically did not show to produce well-defined resin tags at the interface; instead, their bonding pattern more closely resembled that of conventional luting agents. This might be explained by their limited decalcification capacity and minimal penetration into the underlying dentin, which might also account for the presence of adhesive failures among the experimental groups. Other investigations have demonstrated that coronal dentin, in both bovine and human teeth, contains a higher density and larger diameter of tubules than apical dentin [71]. Such structural differences might favor greater cement infiltration and micromechanical interlocking in the cervical region after smear layer removal, which was also observed in the present study, although without a statistically significant difference compared with the middle and apical root thirds.

The distribution of failure modes might provide a valuable insight into the quality of bonding achieved by different surface treatment protocols. In the current study, failure mode analysis revealed that mixed failures were the predominant type across all experimental groups, followed by lower percentages of adhesive failures, indicating the improved interfacial adaptation between fiber posts and dentin after surface conditioning. Among the groups, chitosan and ErCr laser demonstrated the most favorable results, with 88.9% and 81.5% mixed failures, respectively. These findings supported the SEM observations, where both treatments produced cleaner dentin surfaces with exposed dentinal tubules that facilitated pronounced resin infiltration. In contrast, the control group predominantly exhibited adhesive failures at the cement–dentin interface, which was consistent with its significantly lower bond strength and the persistence of smear layer residues in SEM analysis. These patterns reflected a stronger and more integrated bond than that observed in the control group. Although Ahmadi et al. (2025) indicated that cohesive failure was the most frequent mode with 2.5% chitosan group [23], the present findings agreed with Paschoini et al. (2021) study, which reported that mixed failure mode was predominant when chitosan was used with self-adhesive systems with a decrease in adhesive failure frequency within the chitosan group [43]. Similarly, Vasei et al. (2021) demonstrated the predominance of mixed failure in 2.5% chitosan groups with different adhesive systems [57] The predominance of mixed failure in the ErCr group in the present study was also in agreement with previous laser-based report by Ahmed & Hussein (2024) showing that laser pretreatment could shift failure patterns away from predominantly adhesive failure in control groups toward mixed failure, suggesting improved interfacial integration [52].

This study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, as an in vitro investigation, it was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions that cannot fully reproduce the biological, thermal, and mechanical complexities of the oral environment. Second, the use of extracted teeth, a single fiber-post system, a single self-adhesive resin cement, and static push-out testing might limit generalizability to other clinical situations and restorative protocols. Third, no long-term thermomechanical aging or fatigue simulation was performed, which might affect the durability of the observed bond-strength improvements. Accordingly, the present findings should be regarded as preclinical evidence of bonding performance rather than direct proof of clinical superiority.

Despite these limitations, the present findings might offer a relevant preclinical perspective for clinical practice. Post-space dentin pretreatment appeared to influence the bonding quality of fiber posts cemented with self-adhesive resin cement. Within the limitations of this in-vitro study, ErCr laser and 2.5% chitosan showed the most favorable combination of bond strength and dentin surface conditioning, suggesting their potential as promising post-space pretreatment strategies. Clinically, these approaches might help to improve the bonding substrate by reducing smear layer interference and enhancing dentin surface receptivity. However, further long-term aging studies and clinical investigations are still required before definitive recommendations for routine clinical use can be made.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in-vitro study, post-space dentin pretreatment significantly influenced the push-out bond strength of fiber posts luted with self-adhesive resin cement. ErCr laser and 2.5% chitosan showed the most favorable outcomes, with higher bond strength values and more effective smear layer removal than the untreated control. Diode laser and apple vinegar were also associated with improved bonding compared with the control group, although to a lesser extent. These findings suggested that appropriate post-space dentin conditioning might enhance the smear layer removal and bonding substrate before fiber-post cementation.
