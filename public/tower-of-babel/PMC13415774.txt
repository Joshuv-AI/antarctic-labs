Challenges and achievements on the road to hepatitis elimination
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEvery year, World Hepatitis Day draws attention to a disease that causes over one million deaths a year despite being largely preventable due to the availability of an effective vaccine against hepatitis B, curative therapies for hepatitis C, treatment options for chronic hepatitis B as well as diagnostics for both viruses. Achieving the World Health Organization’s goal of eliminating viral hepatitis as a public health problem by 2030 will require major improvements in vaccination coverage, diagnosis, access to care and long-term follow-up. To discuss the progress made so far, we spoke with Professor Ekta Gupta, Head of Clinical Virology and Nodal Officer for the WHO Collaborating Centre on Viral Hepatitis at the Institute of Liver and Biliary Sciences (ILBS) in New Delhi, India. Gupta has more than 29 years of experience in clinical virology and has played an important role in the development and implementation of viral hepatitis control programmes at the Delhi State and National level with the National Viral Hepatitis Control Program in India.

Ekta Gupta

1. Despite the availability of effective vaccines, diagnostics and curative therapies, hepatitis B and C continue to cause more than a million deaths each year. What are the most persistent barriers to hepatitis elimination today, and why have these proved so difficult to overcome? Can we learn from countries like Egypt that have achieved control status of hepatitis?

Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and hepatitis C (HCV) infections often remain asymptomatic for many years, resulting in most infected individuals being unaware of their infection. This silent course delays diagnosis until significant liver damage or fibrosis develops. Therefore, expanding screening to asymptomatic populations is essential for achieving hepatitis elimination goals. Creating awareness in the general public and decentralizing the testing /screening activities will help in identifying early cases. This would require a strong political will and financial commitment. Another point of attrition is that once a serological diagnosis is confirmed, there is often substantial delay in confirmation of active infection via nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT). These delays frequently result in patients being lost to follow-up before appropriate care is initiated. Implementing decentralized molecular testing platforms and reflex testing approaches can minimize diagnostic delays and facilitate same-day test-and-treat pathways. Furthermore, the absence of a comprehensive “one-stop-shop” model—where screening, viral load confirmation, and baseline laboratory staging occur during a single visit—remains a critical bottleneck. Prospective data confirm that consolidating these steps under one roof significantly enhances treatment uptake and care cascade retention1,2. Egypt’s successful campaign against HCV, underscores the impact of structural transformation and political will3. Key transferable strategies for high-burden low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) include enhanced screening of asymptomatic individuals and decentralization through task-shifting. Crucially, integrating hepatitis screening into pre-existing healthcare networks (such as HIV, tuberculosis, and maternal health programs) optimizes resource utilization and disrupts ongoing transmission.

2. Universal access to the hepatitis B birth-dose vaccine is a cornerstone of elimination. How has HBV vaccination policy and coverage changed in the last ~ 10 years in your country and what are remaining challenges in scaling up timely birth-dose vaccination? How can these challenges be addressed?

Before the launch of the National Viral Hepatitis Control Programme (NVHCP) in 2018, India’s framework for Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) immunization began as a pilot project in select districts in 2002. It was subsequently expanded across the nation during 2007–2008 under the Universal Immunization Programme (UIP). India’s current routine immunization schedule mandates a monovalent HBV birth dose administered within 24 hours of birth for all institutional deliveries, followed by three doses co-administered within the pentavalent vaccine framework at 6, 10, and 14 weeks. Despite these clear programmatic policies, the timely uptake of the birth dose within the critical 24-hour window remains suboptimal at approximately 52% to 55%, falling short of the threshold required to achieve robust population immunity at birth4. National data indicate that this low coverage is primarily driven by a lack of awareness among parents, training gaps among healthcare providers, and systemic bottlenecks in vaccine distribution. Furthermore, a significant proportion of births continue to occur in non-institutional or home settings, where cold-chain-dependent vaccines cannot be easily or reliably accessed. Addressing these operational gaps requires targeted community education alongside innovative delivery strategies for home births. Deploying heat-stable vaccine formulations and leveraging local healthcare workers for home-based outreach could ensure birth-dose delivery within the strict 24-hour period. Recently, the government has taken steps to improve birth-dose coverage, by linking birth-dose administration with financial and diagnostic maternal care incentives5,6. Implementing real-time digital registries to log administered birth doses and cross-referencing them directly with active labor room delivery logs creates a highly effective, closed-loop data system. This integration will help in preventing zero-dose dropouts by ensuring every institutional birth transitions immediately into a mapped, traceable vaccination schedule7.

3. In addition to the vaccine, universal testing and access to treatment are essential for hepatitis elimination. Which gaps in the testing and treatment cascade are most critical to close and how can we ensure that vulnerable populations aren’t left behind?

The viral hepatitis elimination cascade faces significant attrition between initial screening and treatment enrollment. To bridge this critical diagnostic gap, reflex testing approaches, either laboratory based or clinician based should be implemented. Furthermore, non-specialist health care workers, even medically qualified laboratory physicians (microbiologists/virologists), can be cross-trained to safely initiate treatment in uncomplicated clinical cases. Decentralizing diagnostic infrastructure is key to scaling retention. Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) are commonly used for Point-of-Care (POC) hepatitis screening. These paper-based tests detect Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and Hepatitis C (HCV) antibodies in a quick, simple manner. They are pivotal for early case detection and linkage to care. However, lack of standardized, affordable, and highly sensitive RDT’s with consistently high sensitivity remains limited. Therefore, efforts should be made to make quality assured RDTs available8.

The use of near-POC molecular platforms, particularly cartridge-based multiplex detection systems, can facilitate rapid confirmation of serological screening results, enabling same-day diagnosis and improving linkage to care. Integrating these molecular assays with essential baseline screening, such as liver function tests and complete blood counts, will further help to evaluate treatment eligibility in one session. All this will allow early intervention. Unfortunately, such assays are not available. Self-testing kits for HBsAg and HCV antibody detection have the potential to improve early diagnosis by increasing access to testing among individuals who may not otherwise seek healthcare services. In 2024, WHO prequalified the first HCV self-test (OraQuick® HCV Self-Test), facilitating its adoption globally9. However, self-testing kits for HBsAg are not yet widely available. In India, neither HCV nor HBsAg self-testing kits are presently approved or available for public use. Even use of dried blood spot (DBS) cards for sample collection and transport offer an attractive alternative for sample collection among people living in remote areas and vulnerable populations10. Finally, programmatic integration is essential to maximize outreach. Treatment gaps can be addressed by further simplifying treatment eligibility criteria especially in CHB. Treat all approach in HBV, will decentralize the treatment implementation from the hands of specialists to the hands of general practitioners and even healthcare workers (HCWs). At present, there is an urgent need to update and strengthen the existing national viral hepatitis control programs across different countries if we are to accelerate progress towards viral hepatitis elimination11.

To ensure successful long-term monitoring, linking viral hepatitis testing with a unique national identity registry, provides a highly trackable longitudinal record, allowing healthcare networks to actively locate and recall individuals to successfully complete the care cascade.

4. Stigma and misinformation are repeatedly cited as major obstacles to hepatitis testing and care. In your experience, what strategies have been most effective at breaking down stigma at community and/or health-system levels?

Strategies that have been most effective in breaking down stigma associated with testing and care are aggressively increasing awareness and community-level interventions, like peer educator programs, patient advocacy networks, and deliberate integration of hepatitis messaging using common platforms such as maternal health and general wellness campaigns. Increased awareness is crucial for improving prevention, testing, and treatment uptake. Stigma, lack of knowledge, and limited healthcare-seeking behavior hinder early diagnosis. Expanding educational campaigns and community outreach can encourage timely testing and treatment. Programs like EMPATHY (Empowering People Against Viral Hepatitis), an initiative of our Institute, have provided a model of community-led advocacy, giving a voice to people living with hepatitis and supporting their cause for equal rights in society. At the health-system level, sensitization training for HCWs — addressing misconceptions about transmission and reinforcing confidentiality obligations — combined with clear institutional policies on the management of HBsAg-positive/or anti HCV positive employees, is an essential and currently neglected priority.

5. Are there any emerging tools or approaches, from point-of-care diagnostics to digital health or new therapeutics, that have great potential to accelerate elimination?

To accelerate progress toward viral hepatitis elimination, incorporating innovative diagnostic and therapeutic paradigms is essential. Rapid self-testing kits and POC molecular diagnostics have emerged as high-potential tools to overcome structural barriers. Drawing from the decentralized testing models optimized during the COVID-19 pandemic, implementing home-based self-testing for both HBV and HCV can drastically lower threshold barriers to entry, streamline the patient journey, and maximize early case detection. Quality-assured, artificial intelligence (AI) -enabled digital clinical pathways can facilitate seamless linkage to care by ensuring that individuals with positive test results are rapidly directed to appropriate diagnostic, treatment, and follow-up services, thereby reducing delays in clinical management. Concurrently, adopting a simplified “treat-all” framework for CHB infection represents a paradigm shift toward elimination. To further optimize this framework, expanding access to newer viral markers (such as quantitative HBsAg, HBV core related antigen, and HBV RNA) provides clinicians with objective metrics to monitor therapeutic responses and determine safe endpoints for treatment discontinuation or functional cure. Achieving complete elimination also demands broadening treatment access to vulnerable cohorts, specifically children and adolescents. Finally, integration at the macro-level can be achieved by linking one-time population-level screening results to a centralized national social identity infrastructure. This structural convergence establishes a secure, longitudinal tracking network that brings previously unidentified asymptomatic cases into the care cascade, enabling active recall and systematically preventing loss-to-follow-up.

6. Looking ahead to 2030, what would success in hepatitis control and elimination look like and what gives you the most hope in achieving these goals?

As we move toward the 2030 global health targets, achieving the elimination of viral hepatitis—particularly HCV infection—will require a substantial expansion of current diagnostic strategies. While WHO has set a goal of diagnosing 90% of individuals living with chronic viral hepatitis by 2030, identifying and linking even 70% of infected individuals to care worldwide would represent a transformative milestone. Achieving this objective will necessitate a shift from passive, facility-based testing models to proactive, decentralized, and patient-centered approaches that improve access to diagnosis and care. The successful validation of Egypt’s national hepatitis programme as the world’s first achievement in HCV elimination demonstrates that country-level elimination is both feasible and attainable within this decade. Sustained political commitment, adequate financing, strengthened healthcare infrastructure, widespread public awareness, and the adoption of innovative diagnostic technologies will be critical to accelerating progress toward viral hepatitis elimination and achieving global targets ahead of schedule.
