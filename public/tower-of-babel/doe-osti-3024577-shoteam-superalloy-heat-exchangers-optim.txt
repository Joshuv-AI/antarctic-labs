Public Executive Summary

UCLA developed an extreme-condition heat exchanger technology targeted to ultra-high
efficiency hybrid aviation power cycles. The heat exchanger targeted operation at 50 kW
(thermal) at supercritical CO2 pressures of 80 and 250 bar (1160 and 3626 psi) in hot and cold
streams, respectively and at a hot-stream inlet temperature of 800°C (1472°F). A metallic
superalloy capable of withstanding high temperature and pressure was used to fabricate a
microtube shell-and-tube-based design supplemented with 3D-printed tube augmentations.
The optimized design enhanced overall heat transfer while maintaining a small overall form
factor and low weight. This class of heat exchanger could dramatically improve efficiency,
power density, and cost effectiveness for new hybrid aviation power cycles, thus enabling
economically feasible routes to air vehicle propulsion with substantially less CO2 emissions to
the environment.

Accomplishments and Objectives

Today, advances in high-temperature materials and manufacturing promise a new generation
of improvements in efficiency and 'mass customization', yet the learnings and methodologies of
heat exchanger design practice and optimization must remain at the forefront of this new wave
to realize the benefits fully. Our team, led by UCLA and Honeywell Aerospace leveraged its
substantial experience in developing extreme-condition heat exchanger technology targeted to
ultra-high-efficiency hybrid aviation power cycles to achieve quantifiable technical goals
including operation at 50kW(thermal) at CO2 pressures of 80 and 300 bar in the respective hot
and cold streams. The efficiency gains from this heat exchanger derive from high-temperature
operation enabled by extracting a large fraction of energy from the exhaust stream to pre-heat
the inlet stream. This energy would otherwise be discharged as waste heat to the local
environment. The technology that enables this performance starts with a particular metal
superalloy that withstands both high temperature and pressure. We optimized standard flow
piping and shells made of this material with microtube bundles and 3D-printed components
that enhance the transfer of heat without using more total volume, thus maintaining a small
overall form factor while also enabling cost-effective commercial scale-up in the future. Our
50kW prototype was tested in an advanced facility for supercritical CO2 at Echogen, and the
test successfully demonstrated the functional performance of the technology. Success in this
heat exchanger technology enables dramatic improvements in efficiency and power density for
new hybrid aviation power cycles.

Project Activities
This project focused on designing and building a heat exchanger targeting recuperation in a
supercritical CO2 Brayton cycle for future use in hybrid aviation propulsion platforms. The
design progressed with modeling to identify a shell-and-tube architecture with microtube
bundles and used local and system-level models to optimize the design in terms of thermal
rating and cost. A 50 kW(thermal) scale prototype was manufactured and tested. At
approximately Q10, funding delays from the sponsor caused a prolonged ‘pause’ in program
activities, leading to an approximately 1.5 year extension of the project. The pause allowed the
team to: (a) identify a 3rd party testing vendor to conduct final testing, and (b) add new scope to
assess unique creep characteristics of microtube (cf, standard-sized tube). Eventually, the
project was the first to conduct and report 50kW-scale testing with the 3rd party, and the
results generally confirmed the thermal function of the prototype. The highest measured heat
exchange of 40 kW fell slightly short of the goal, and the difference was attributed to minor
leakage that resulted from gaps between the tubes and baffles that were apparent in the
constructed prototype. Ultimately, the technology is expected to be deployed at much larger
scales (>1 MW), and our validated tool can optimize designs for a wide range of real-world sizes
and conditions, keeping production costs at a minimal level (<$5000 K/kW). This model was
published in several papers and made available through open-source distribution.
