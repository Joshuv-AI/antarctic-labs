Optimization of the multimorbidity management model for older adults: a study on the mediating effect of health literacy on self-efficacy and health status
Authors: Linjin Li, Lutong Pan, Dan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prevalence of chronic diseases and chronic comorbidities among the older adults is increasing annually with the advent of an aging population, making health management for older patients with chronic conditions essential. However, optimizing management models requires mobilizing the patients’ own agency. Individual capacity, specifically self-efficacy and health literacy, plays a pivotal role in this process. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the relationship between self-efficacy, health literacy, and health status among older patients with comorbidities, and further explore the potential pathways by which self-efficacy and health literacy are associated with health status. The goal is to provide a scientific basis for informing the refinement of comorbidity management models for older adults and supporting better health status of patients.MethodsAdopting a multistage stratified cluster random sampling method, older patients with multimorbidity in communities of Shenzhen, Guangdong Province were selected as the survey subjects. Using the “Health Survey Questionnaire for older patients with multimorbidity” for face-to-face interviews, 1200 older adults were surveyed, and 987 valid patient data were selected. Descriptive analysis was conducted on the self-efficacy, health literacy, and health status of older patients with multimorbidity in Shenzhen, Guangdong Province. A structural equation model was constructed to analyze the mediating effect of health literacy between self-efficacy and health status.ResultsIn the survey of 987 older patients with multimorbidity in this study, the average score for self-efficacy was 8.07 ± 1.36, indicating a high level on average; the average score for health literacy was 32.16 ± 9.39, suggesting a possible inadequacy in health literacy level; and the average score for health status was 73.60 ± 19.70. The well-fitted structural equation model results demonstrate that self-efficacy (β = 0.148, P < 0.001) and health literacy (β = 0.317, P < 0.001) of older patients with multimorbidity are positively associated with health status. Specifically, the mediating effect of health literacy on the relationship between self-efficacy and health status accounts for 38.1% of the total effect (Effect size = 0.042, 95% CI: 0.022–0.069), serving as a partial mediator with a significant mediating effect.ConclusionsIn the multimorbidity management model for older adults, interventions such as patient peer support groups and case manager-led health education are proposed as theoretical strategies. These potential measures aim to support patient self-efficacy and facilitate health literacy, thereby potentially contributing to better overall health of patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07574-9.Keywords: Older patients with multimorbidity, Chronic disease, Multimorbidity management, Health promotion, Self-efficacy, Health literacy

Introduction

Multimorbidity is becoming the norm among older adults, driven by increased life expectancy and the rising prevalence of chronic diseases [1]. According to the definition by the World Health Organization (WHO), multimorbidity of chronic diseases refers to the concurrent presence of two or more chronic diseases in an individual [2]. The physical condition of older adults is already complex, and when combined with the impact of chronic multimorbidity, it not only consumes more medical resources but also reduces the quality of life of older adults, increasing the economic burden on families and society [3]. This creates a significant challenge for the management of multimorbidity in older adults. Although the concept of patient-centred care has been widely advocated since the 1990s, the management of multimorbidity in older adults remains strongly influenced by a disease-centred approach. This situation stems largely from the historical emphasis of healthcare systems on single diseases, with clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) primarily designed around disease-specific pathways rather than holistic needs [4, 5]. Consequently, interventions targeting single diseases often provide limited guidance for multimorbidity, leading to conflicting recommendations, increased treatment burden, and fragmentation of medical services [6]. These challenges highlight the persistent gap between the prevailing disease-oriented structures and the ideal of integrated, patient-centred management for older patients with multimorbidity [7]. In order to ensure the integrity and continuity of medical services for the older population with multimorbidity, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends the implementation of integrated care models, aimed at achieving comprehensive health management throughout the life cycle of the older population through a patient-centered integrated intervention model.

Our research team has summarized domestic and international practices and based on the PDSA theory, constructed an integrated management model for geriatric multimorbidity that is suitable for Chinese medical alliances and medical communities, aiming to meet the complex healthcare needs of older patients with multimorbidity [8]. The model uses primary healthcare institutions as healthcare service providers, establishing a collaborative framework with Level II or above hospitals and social non-medical resources. These entities are interconnected through specific pathways: a bidirectional ‘Transfer/Transition’ channel between primary care and hospitals, and ‘Support/Train’ interactions between primary care and social resources. Together, they form the multidisciplinary Geriatric Integrated Team (GIT). The management process follows a closed-loop cycle consisting of five sequential steps: comprehensive evaluation, population risk stratification, individualized intervention program, patient self-management, and continuous follow-up monitoring. Crucially, a PDCA circulation is embedded within these steps to ensure dynamic adjustment and continuous optimization of care. The entire model is underpinned by a Decision Support System (DSS) that relies on evidence guidance and data support. Ultimately, the model aims to achieve three core objectives: Accessibility, Quality, and Efficiency (as shown in Fig. 1).

An integrated management model for geriatric comorbidities

However, the effectiveness of any integrated management model ultimately depends on the patient’s active participation. Patients, as the primary responsible party for their own health, the control and improvement of chronic diseases mainly depend on patients’ self-management and behavioral changes [4]. Research has shown that self-management is the most effective management improvement model [1, 9]. Meanwhile, self-efficacy plays a key role in the process of patients engaging in self-management [10]. Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s subjective assessment of their ability to perform a specific behavior [11]. Moreover, research has shown that the level of self-efficacy is influenced by the level of health literacy and can predict individual health status [12, 13]. Individuals with higher self-efficacy are more likely to view health problems as challenges to be mastered rather than threats to be avoided. Consequently, they exhibit greater persistence in adhering to treatment regimens and adopting health-promoting behaviors, which directly translates into better physical and mental health outcomes [14]. Health literacy refers to the ability to integrate individual cognitive abilities and social skills to acquire, understand, evaluate, and apply health-related information and services to make decisions beneficial to one’s own health [15]. Many countries’ health policies and national strategies currently involve the improvement of health literacy, and recognize that inadequate health literacy will pose significant challenges to individuals and society [16, 17]. Health literacy is positively correlated with self-efficacy [18]. Individuals with high self-efficacy also have high levels of health literacy [19]. Previous studies have shown that both health literacy and self-efficacy are positively correlated with individual health status separately [20, 21]. Most of these studies focused on patients with a single chronic disease, and there are few studies on older patients with multimorbidity [22, 23].

Given that older patients with multimorbidity face unique challenges such as higher cognitive load and complex medication regimens, the specific mechanistic pathways within this population remain underexplored. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the associations among self-efficacy, health literacy, and health status in older patients with multimorbidity. The findings are intended to inform future refinement of the patient self-management process in the multimorbidity management model previously proposed by our team. Based on the concept model of health literacy influencing health status proposed by David W. Baker [24], we constructed a Structural Equation Model (SEM), to test the following hypotheses: (H1) Self-efficacy is positively associated with health status; (H2) Health literacy is positively associated with health status; (H3) Self-efficacy is positively associated with health literacy; and (H4) Health literacy mediates the relationship between self-efficacy and health status.

Methods

Data source and data sampling

The data for this study were obtained from a questionnaire survey conducted among older patients with chronic comorbidities in communities of Shenzhen, Guangdong Province. The research team developed the “Health Survey Questionnaire for Older Patients with Comorbidities” [see Additional file 1] for this study, based on the HLS-SF12 Health Literacy Survey, the 6-Item Chronic Disease Management Self-Efficacy Scale (SES6C), and the European Five-Dimensional Health Scale (EQ-5D). The questionnaire includes demographic and disease-related information such as gender, age, living conditions, educational level, and household registration type, etc. The validity of the questionnaire content was confirmed through the Delphi expert consultation method and pilot studies. Trained graduate students majoring in hospital management, standardized intern doctors in general practice, community general practitioners, and nurses served as investigators. Before the formal survey, all investigators received standardized training on the study purpose, questionnaire content, and interview skills. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews in community general clinics and through resident focus group settings. During each interview, participants self-reported their responses to all questionnaire items, while investigators provided clarification of item meanings when necessary but avoided any guidance that could influence answers. This procedure was designed to reduce comprehension bias and ensure data completeness.

This survey utilized the Shenzhen Community Resident Health Record Information System and employed a multi-stage stratified cluster random sampling method. In the first stage, three districts were randomly selected within Shenzhen based on economic level and geographic location. In the second stage, three communities were randomly selected within each district. In the third stage, investigations were conducted at the residents’ health service centers in each community. Upon obtaining informed consent from the participants, individuals aged 60 and above residing in the community for at least one year were surveyed. They had been diagnosed with two or more chronic diseases (predominantly including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, coronary heart disease, stroke, COPD, osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, and other common chronic diseases) by secondary or higher-level hospitals, had clear consciousness, were cooperative, free from mental illness and cognitive impairment, and interested in the project. According to the proportion of older patients with multimorbidity in the older population of the city or district, the required sample size was calculated using the formula for cross-sectional survey sample size, , with set at 0.05, and taking into account a 20% inefficiency rate. A total of 1200 older patients with multimorbidity were surveyed. An effective questionnaire was defined as one with complete responses (i.e., no missing data), excluding duplicate submissions. Consequently, a total of 987 valid datasets were obtained for the subsequent Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) analysis, with an effective questionnaire retrieval rate of 82.2%.

Measurements

Chronic disease management self-efficacy

The Chronic Disease Management Self-Efficacy Scale (SES6C), developed by the Chronic Disease Education Research Center at Stanford University, was used to assess the self-efficacy of older patients with multimorbidity [25]. This scale focuses on measuring confidence in disease management and was translated into Chinese by the School of Public Health at Fudan University in Shanghai. It is widely used in the field of chronic disease self-management research [26]. The scale consists of 6 items, with each item rated from no confidence to complete confidence on a scale of 1 to 10. The total score is the average of all items, and a higher score indicates a higher level of self-efficacy for the individual. When the self-efficacy score is above 7, the likelihood of completing a task is high. In this study, scores below 5 were categorized as low, scores of 5–7 as moderate, and scores above 7 as high [27]. In a pilot study involving 30 older patients with chronic diseases, the scale’s internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.94), showing good scale reliability [28]. In the current study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.897, confirming its good reliability.

Health literacy

The health literacy of older patients with multimorbidity was investigated using the HLS-SF12 developed by the Asian Health Literacy Association in a cross-sectional survey. This instrument was previously validated based on surveys from six countries (Cronbach’s α = 0.87) [29]. The questionnaire consisted of three dimensions: healthcare, disease prevention, and health promotion, with each dimension including 4 items on accessing, understanding, evaluating, and applying health information. Each item used a 4-level scoring system, with “very difficult,” “difficult,” “easy,” and “very easy” scored as 1 to 4 points. The total score ranged from 12 to 48, and was then standardized using the formula index score = (mean − 1) × (50/3) [30]. The standardized total score ranges from 0 to 50 points, with the following categories: health literacy inadequate if total score is < 26, potentially inadequate for scores between [26,34), adequate for scores between [34,43), and very adequate for scores between [43, 50] [31]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.947, demonstrating excellent reliability of the HLS-SF12 Scale.

Health status

The health status of older patients with multimorbidity was measured using the EuroQol Visual Analog Scale (EQ-VAS) [32]. Patients rate their health status on a vertical visual scale ranging from 0 (“worst imaginable health state”) to 100 (“best imaginable health state”). The higher the score, the better the health status. Extensive literature has demonstrated this indicator to be a valid and reliable reflection of an individual’s health status, encompassing both subjective and objective aspects [33]. The EQ-VAS was utilized as a standalone global measure because it effectively captures the cumulative, patient-perceived health burden of multimorbidity while minimizing respondent fatigue [34].

Control variables

According to the personal health concept framework proposed by Paasche-Orlow [35], when studying individual health-related information, it is necessary to consider the impact of demographic variables. Therefore, the control variables in this study are set as gender (male/female), educational level (primary school or below/junior high school/high school/junior college/undergraduate and above), household registration type (local urban residence/local rural residence/non-local urban residence/non-local rural residence), age (60–65 years/66–70 years/71–75 years), and living situation (living with spouse/living alone/living with children/living with spouse and children).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was conducted using SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 28.0 software, with all P values being two-tailed and the significance level set at P < 0.05. Descriptive analysis was performed on the basic information of older patients with multimorbidity surveyed, and ANOVA analysis was used to analyze whether there were differences in self-efficacy, health literacy, and health status among older patients with multimorbidity with different demographic characteristics. Prior to evaluating the structural paths, a Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted to confirm the measurement models. To investigate the pathways among self-efficacy, health literacy, and health status of older patients with multimorbidity, a structural equation model (SEM) was constructed and mediation analysis was conducted. In epidemiological research, mediation analysis can reveal different pathways through which exposure relates to outcomes [36]. In the process where the independent variable X is associated with the dependent variable Y, if the independent variable X relates to the variable M, thereby associating with the dependent variable Y, the variable M is considered as the mediating variable between the independent variable X and the dependent variable Y. Self-efficacy is taken as the independent variable, health status as the dependent variable, and health literacy as the mediating variable. The model parameters were estimated using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method. To control for potential confounding effects, demographic characteristics (gender, age, educational level, household registration type, and living situation) were incorporated into the model as covariates. Specifically, paths from these control variables to the endogenous variable (health status) were modeled to adjust for their associations while testing the main hypothesized relationships. To address potential deviations from strict multivariate normality assumptions for ML estimation, the bias-corrected nonparametric percentile bootstrap method was used to test the mediating effect, and the standard error of the mediation effect is calculated through 2000 repetitions of sample resampling to estimate the 95% confidence interval of the mediation effect as accurately as possible. Standardized regression coefficients were used to evaluate the associations of self-efficacy and health literacy with health status in a path model. The overall fit of the model is assessed using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). Descriptive analysis of self-efficacy, health literacy and health status of senile comorbid patients

The range of self-efficacy scores among older patients with multimorbidity in this survey was from 2.83 to 10 points, with an average score of 8.07 ± 1.36. According to the classification criteria from previous studies, a score greater than 7 indicates a high level. The average self-efficacy level of older patients with multimorbidity in Shenzhen, Guangdong Province in this survey was classified as high. The distribution of self-efficacy scores among older patients with multimorbidity showed statistical significance in terms of household registration type and living situation (P < 0.05). The self-efficacy scores of local older patients with multimorbidity were higher than those with non-local registrations, and among different living situations, older patients with multimorbidity living with both spouses and children had higher self-efficacy compared to those in other living arrangements.

Health literacy scores of older patients with multimorbidity ranged from 1.39 to 50, with an average score of 32.16 ± 9.39. According to the classification criteria from previous studies, the average health literacy level of older patients with multimorbidity surveyed in Shenzhen, Guangdong Province in this study was categorized as possibly inadequate. The distribution of health literacy scores among older patients with multimorbidity showed statistical significance in terms of gender, educational level, household registration type, and living situation (P < 0.05). The health literacy scores of female older patients with multimorbidity were higher than those of males. Higher educational levels were associated with higher health literacy scores among older patients with multimorbidity. Furthermore, older patients with multimorbidity living with a spouse or with children and a spouse had higher health literacy scores compared to those living alone or with children.

The health status scores of older patients with multimorbidity in Shenzhen, Guangdong Province ranged from 30 to 100, with an average score of 73.60 ± 19.70. The distribution of health status scores among older patients with multimorbidity showed statistical significance in terms of household registration type and living situation (P < 0.05). Regardless of local or non-local registration, older patients with multimorbidity with rural household registration had higher health status scores than those with urban household registration; and older patients with multimorbidity living with both spouses and children had higher health status scores compared to those in other living arrangements.

Confirmation of the measurement models

Prior to evaluating the hypothesized structural paths, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted to verify the measurement models for the multi-item scales. The results indicated that the measurement models for both scales demonstrated excellent fit to the data. Specifically, the SES6C model showed strong fit indices (IFI = 0.994, TLI = 0.983, CFI = 0.994, RMSEA = 0.062). Similarly, the HLS-SF12 model exhibited an optimal fit (IFI = 0.964, TLI = 0.953, CFI = 0.964, RMSEA = 0.067). Furthermore, construct reliability and convergent validity were firmly established. Specifically, the Composite Reliability (CR) values for all constructs exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70 (ranging from 0.821 to 0.892), and the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values were all above the 0.50 benchmark (ranging from 0.535 to 0.580). Additionally, all standardized factor loadings were statistically significant (P < 0.001). These robust results confirmed the structural adequacy of the measurement models for the subsequent SEM analysis.

Multiple control variables were adjusted, a structural equation model was fitted, and the significance of the mediating effect of health literacy was tested using the bias-corrected percentile Bootstrap method with 2000 resamples. Discussion

This study indicates that the self-efficacy and health literacy of older patients with comorbidities are positively associated with their health status, consistent with previous research results. For example, Jin et al.‘s study showed that sufficient self-efficacy and health literacy can significantly improve the survival status of older patients with chronic diseases, with the effect becoming more significant as the disease progresses [37]. In Li Hui’s study on the impact of health literacy on the incidence of chronic diseases in residents of Liaoning Province, it was concluded that health literacy is a protective factor against the incidence of chronic diseases [38]. In Fabbri et al.‘s meta-analysis study on the relationship between health literacy and health outcomes in heart failure patients, it was also shown that compared to patients with adequate health literacy, patients lacking health literacy had a mortality rate and hospitalization rate that were 1.41 and 1.12 times higher, respectively [39]. In Rose et al.‘s study on the relationship between self-efficacy and chronic disease self-management in type 2 diabetes patients, it was concluded that patients with high self-efficacy can better engage in chronic disease self-management [40]. At the same time, through the analysis of mediating effects, we have also confirmed that health literacy has a mediating effect of 38.1% in the impact of self-efficacy on the health status of older patients with comorbidities. In other words, self-efficacy may be indirectly associated with health status through its association with the health literacy of chronic disease patients. This result is consistent with similar research findings, for example, Osborn et al.‘s study concluded that self-efficacy plays a mediating role in the relationship between health literacy and self-blood glucose control in diabetes patients [41]. Kim et al.‘s study found that health literacy partially mediates the relationship between self-efficacy and physical and mental health in older adults [42].

Self-efficacy is a key factor in the process of translating knowledge into behavioral performance. Knowledge can enhance self-efficacy, motivating individuals to maintain the willingness and ability to engage in healthy behaviors [43]. According to the Protection Motivation Theory, individuals with higher self-efficacy are more motivated to adopt preventive behaviors and improve their health status [44]. In the context of chronic disease self-management, self-efficacy enhances patient agency, increases confidence in actively preventing and managing diseases, and promotes sustained engagement in health-promoting behaviours [45]. For older patients with multimorbidity, these pathways are particularly critical. Unlike those with single chronic conditions, this population often faces physiological functional decline, high cognitive load, and complex medication regimens, making disease management significantly more challenging [46, 47]. Their ability to integrate health information and maintain behavioural consistency depends heavily on strong self-efficacy and health literacy. Higher self-efficacy enables these patients to cope with uncertainty and coordinate care among multiple providers, while higher health literacy facilitates their understanding of complex medical instructions and their capacity to make informed health decisions [48]. Evidence confirms that populations with higher health literacy exhibit a lower incidence of chronic diseases. Patients with high self-efficacy could improve their ability to manage diseases, enhancing not only the confidence of chronic disease patients but also their level of self-management [16]. Thus, supporting health literacy and self-efficacy among older patients with multimorbidity may enhance their awareness of disease prevention, encourage active management of chronic conditions, and ultimately facilitate better health outcomes. Integrating health literacy education and self-efficacy support into multimorbidity management programmes is therefore a cost-effective strategy for this vulnerable population. Furthermore, better health status can positively reinforce patients’ motivation to acquire knowledge and change lifestyles, fostering a sustainable capacity for self-management [49, 50]. Crucially, these relationships are likely reciprocal rather than strictly linear. According to Bandura’s theory of reciprocal determinism [10], physiological states are a fundamental source of self-efficacy. Empirical studies further confirm that positive self-perceived health significantly predicts higher self-efficacy among older adults [51]. These findings are consistent with a hypothesized ‘virtuous cycle’: better health may provide the physical and cognitive reserve to foster confidence and learning, which in turn could be positively associated with health outcomes. Therefore, our findings suggest that supporting the self-efficacy and health literacy of older patients with comorbidities in chronic disease management is crucial for facilitating better health outcomes.

Based on the results of this study, a key theoretical and policy implication is the potential shift from a purely ‘disease-centric’ to a ‘patient-capacity-centric’ model. While subject to future empirical validation, relevant optimizations could conceptually be made to the comorbidity management model for older adults previously proposed by our team. In the specific implementation process of the comorbidity management model, with patient self-management as the core, it would be highly beneficial to incorporate content that enhances patient self-efficacy and improves health literacy. We could introduce case managers into the comorbidity management team for older adults as health managers to accompany patients throughout the process, serving as the primary point of contact for patients seeking health information. To make this actionable, case managers are encouraged to integrate the measurement tools validated in this study (HLS-SF12 and SES6C) into routine assessments for risk stratification, prioritizing patients with low scores for intensive follow-up. They could help establish a health-centered learning community, divide patients into different self-management teams based on the type of chronic disease, organize regular community health education activities, and enhance patients’ self-management capabilities through the establishment of close-knit teams with mutual trust and support relationships. Within these teams, specific techniques such as the ‘teach-back’ method and ‘small-step’ goal setting could be employed to bridge the gap between knowledge and behavior, thereby generating ‘mastery experiences’ that directly boost self-efficacy. Effective management of chronic diseases requires strong family and social support. Studies have shown a high positive correlation between social support and self-efficacy [52]. Therefore, management teams could collaborate with patient associations, health public welfare teams, and other parties to carry out activities such as peer support groups and health literacy improvement programs. Furthermore, patients’ family members might be encouraged to actively participate in the management team. Structuring these interactions around ‘expert patient’ modeling could further enhance self-efficacy through ‘vicarious experience,’ providing relatable examples for others. At the same time, case managers could conduct regular follow-up assessments on patients’ self-efficacy, health literacy, and other aspects. By incorporating regular assessments and considering the feedback from patients, management plans might be adjusted to help build patient self-efficacy and health literacy, ultimately aiming to facilitate better health outcomes.

This study has some limitations. Firstly, as this study is cross-sectional, although it demonstrates the correlation between self-efficacy, health literacy, and health status, it cannot track changes in outcomes over time, thus failing to provide clear evidence of causality. Secondly, all measures in this study were based on self-reported data, which may be subject to response bias or social desirability effects. Although face-to-face administration by trained investigators helped clarify questions and minimize missing data, the possibility of reporting bias cannot be completely excluded. Furthermore, assessing overall health status solely via the subjective EQ-VAS may inflate its observed association with self-efficacy due to shared method variance [53], despite their conceptual distinctness as a proactive capability belief versus a current perceived state [32, 54]. Thirdly, there are many unmeasured factors influencing the health of older patients with comorbidities. Due to the limited control variables included in this study, the explained variance for health status was modest (R2 = 0.151). Nevertheless, this finding remains clinically meaningful because these psychosocial factors are modifiable, offering valuable targets for intervention. Lastly, excluding cognitively impaired individuals to ensure valid self-reporting limits generalizability. Thus, optimizing management models and policies for the broader, more vulnerable multimorbidity population remains a subject for further research.

Conclusions

In summary, based on representative data from Shenzhen, Guangdong Province, it is shown that self-efficacy and health literacy are both positively associated with the health status of older patients with comorbidities. Additionally, health literacy has a mediating effect of 38.1% in the association between self-efficacy and the health status of older patients with comorbidities. Therefore, a key theoretical and policy implication for multimorbidity management models is the potential benefit of incorporating content designed to support patient self-efficacy and facilitate their health literacy, which may potentially contribute to better health.
