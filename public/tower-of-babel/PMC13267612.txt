Cellular immunology data enable clinical severity prediction via supervised machine learning
Authors: Yonghyun Nam, Michelle L. McKeague, Matei Ionita, Matthew E. Lee, Mark Painter, Sokratis Apostolidis, Jaesik Kim, Jakob Woerner, Sang-Hyuk Jung, Patryk Orzechowski, Joost Wagenaar, Allison R. Greenplate, E. John Wherry, Dokyoon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHigh-dimensional flow cytometry provides rich immunological data for examining immune responses and their relationships with disease pathology, but its complexity, heterogeneity, high-dimensionality, and modest sample sizes limit translation into clinical applications. We describe a translational immune health framework that applies supervised learning algorithms to integrate high-dimensional cellular immunology data with clinical outcomes. Using COVID-19 as a clinical scenario, we applied this framework to deep immune profiles from patients to classify disease severity and predict future severity changes from baseline profiles. We built predictive models with five supervised algorithms and interpreted associations between immune features and severity outcomes with SHapley Additive exPlanations. This approach identified immune features contributing to severity predictions and provided interpretable links between cellular immune profiles and clinical outcomes. Our findings support machine learning as a practical strategy for analyzing complex immunological data and advancing predictive modeling in translational immune health.

Introduction

Advances in human immunology have substantially improved our comprehension of complex immune phenotypes and are beginning to enable the development and/or application of treatments for immune-related disorders.1,2 High-throughput techniques such as flow cytometry, cytometry by time of flight (CyTOF), microarray, RNA sequencing, chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing, and single-cell RNA sequencing have provided significant insights into immune system dynamics/functioning. Immunology studies have successfully identified immune signatures associated with disease states, classified distinct immunophenotypes, and characterized immune responses to various conditions using samples from well-characterized cohorts with case-control studies.3,4,5,6,7,8

Despite these advances, the analysis of cellular immunology data remains challenging in translating these population-level insights into individual-level clinical applications. The high dimensionality of immune features derived from single-cell analyses, coupled with the relatively small number of subjects, makes individual-level predictive modeling difficult. Compared to other “-omics” research and blood- and/or urine-based biomarker studies linked to electronic health records (EHRs), which also deal with high-dimensional features but benefit from larger sample sizes, single-cell studies often involve fewer subjects. Additionally, the immunology data generated from each cohort has a specific experimental design, and the feature characteristics are highly heterogeneous across study-specific designs compared to other biological data obtained from community-based cohort studies (e.g., biobanks), making it difficult to integrate and use samples from different studies.

Most current immunology studies utilize exploratory data analysis methods (unsupervised learning) on unlabeled data—cellular immunology data not tagged with clinical outcomes during analysis—employing techniques such as clustering,9 dimensionality reduction, and outlier detection. While these methods have facilitated the identification of communities or clusters of cell populations related to specific immunophenotypes or immune responses,10,11,12 they generally do not focus on providing individual-level inferences, such as predicting which subjects with immune features are at higher risk for disease progression.13,14,15

To address these challenges and make population-level findings applicable at the patient level, it is necessary to apply supervised machine learning to develop predictive models that utilize large numbers of labeled data incorporating high-dimensional immune features with curated clinical endpoints.16,17 Supervised learning offers an efficient solution for providing individual-level prediction results, thereby advancing translational medicine and enabling personalized medicine. Due to the aforementioned inherent characteristics of current immunology data, including heterogeneity, high-dimensionality, and limited sample size, it has been challenging to directly apply supervised approaches. However, with the increasing availability of large-scale immunology datasets and advances in computational methods, it is now feasible to overcome these challenges and develop robust predictive models for clinical applications.

In this paper, we introduce a translational immune health framework that employs individual-level predictive models, leveraging supervised machine learning to analyze high-dimensional cellular immunology data alongside clinical outcomes. The primary contribution of this work is an end-to-end, interpretable modeling workflow that links immune features at the time of sampling to clinically relevant prediction tasks. As a proof-of-concept study, we use the deep immune profiling data of patients with COVID-19 obtained from high-dimensional flow cytometry analysis.18 This cohort provides deep immune profiling of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) with high-dimensional, hierarchically gated cell population features and paired clinical severity annotations, enabling supervised modeling of both baseline severity status and short-term severity changes.18 During the recent COVID-19 pandemic caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2, COVID-19 was clinically diagnosed using a test to detect virus such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to detect viral nucleic acids or antigen tests to detect viral proteins. Disease severity was defined as mild to severe based on specific criteria such as respiratory rate, oxygen saturation levels, and the presence of pneumonia or acute respiratory distress syndrome. From the unsupervised exploratory analysis conducted in previous studies,19 high-dimensional immune analysis identified different complex immune signatures or “immunotypes” that were associated with disease severity. The temporal stability of these immunotypes was highly complex and variable, with significant fluctuations observed over time within and across patients. This variability complicates the use of static immune profiles for predicting disease progression and highlights the need for dynamic modeling approaches. Thus, given the high-dimensional immune features and severity outcomes, we propose a predictive model for evaluating the severity status of patients with COVID-19 that can predict future severity changes from mild to severe based on identified immune profiles (Figure 1). Such predictive algorithms could provide evidence-based therapeutic guidance in clinical settings, allowing for personalized treatment strategies tailored to individual patient responses. Results

We developed a predictive model to determine the clinical outcomes of patients with COVID-19, demonstrating the potential of high-throughput cellular immunology data for clinical applications. The dataset consisted of 157 data points with 193-dimensional immune features, including individual T cell and B cell populations and phenotypes.18 The dataset includes 157 timepoint-specific PBMC blood samples from hospitalized patients with COVID-19. The 193 immune features are manually gated immune cell population frequencies derived from a hierarchical parent-child gating scheme rather than functional readouts. All 193 immune features, which are hierarchically interrelated, were used as input data. Clinical target outcomes for each patient were defined by stratifying the NIH ordinal scale for grading disease severity.

A key motivation of this study is to move beyond traditional immunology research, which often stops at identifying biological insights, and instead focus on integrating cellular immunology data with EHRs for clinical translation. While many studies characterize immune signatures linked to disease states, their clinical relevance remains underexplored. By incorporating machine learning approaches, this work demonstrates how immune profiling data can be leveraged for real-world severity prediction models.

We employed five supervised learning algorithms: decision trees (DTs), support vector machines (SVMs), artificial neural networks (ANNs), random forests (RF), and gradient boosting machines (GBMs). The data were split into a 80% development set for training and a 20% hold-out evaluation set for testing.20 We applied 5-fold cross-validation to avoid overfitting and tune hyperparameters. Each fold served as a validation set once, with the remaining 4-folds serving as the training set. Hyperparameters were grid-searched during cross-validation, and the best parameters were selected based on performance metrics on the validation set. To maintain class distribution in the split datasets, stratified random sampling was applied. Prediction performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) (Figure 2A). Prediction of severity status with baseline immune profiles

We constructed a predictive model to classify new patients with COVID-19 as having either high or less severe conditions based on their immune features. High severity was defined as patients who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 and needed ventilator support (COVID 2 and 3 on the NIH ordinal scale). Less severity was defined as patients who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 but did not have acute respiratory distress syndrome and did not require a ventilator (COVID 4 and 5). Our dataset included 97 records of high severity and 60 records of less severe conditions. The paired labeled dataset was divided into an 80% development set (n = 125) and a 20% evaluation set (n = 32) (Figures 2A and S1). Five predictive models were fitted with the best hyperparameters (Table S1). For evaluation, the models were tested using only the input immune features in the evaluation set, with the known target outcomes hidden from the trained model. Prediction performance—whether the unseen samples could be predicted to have high or less severe conditions—was measured by AUROC and AUPRC. Four models, excluding SVM, showed acceptable performance with AUROC over 0.85 (Figure 2B) and AUPRC over 0.85 (Figure S2). The RF model outperformed the others, achieving an AUROC of 0.8708 and an AUPRC of 0.86.

Key immune features and their clinical implications in predicting COVID-19 severity

The trained RF model distinguished whether a patient needed ventilator support (high severity) or not (less severity) based on 193 immune profiles measured at baseline. RF, an ensemble model, consists of multiple DTs, each trained on different subsets of data and features. Our model included ∼500 DTs with entropy as the criterion for splitting branches. While visualizing the entire RF can be challenging, we examined a randomly selected DT to describe the decision process (Figure 2C). This sampled DT uses 11 immune features, though all immune features are covered by other trees in the forest. For example, if a patient’s immune profiles showed a frequency of CD4 Effector Memory 1 T cells within CD3 T cells (percOf_CD4EM1_CD3pos) as 10, EOMES+ CD4 Naive T cells (CD4Naive_EOMES) as 3, and CD8 Naive T cells (CD8 naive) as 30, this tree would classify the patient into severe status following the decision path: percOf_CD4EM1_CD3pos (>9.353) → percOf_CD4Naive_EOMESpos (<3.231) → percOf_CD8Naive <43.45 → class as “high severity condition.” The final class assignment is determined by the majority vote from all trees.

We investigated the descriptions of the trained model indirectly by examining feature importance in RF, which represents the contribution of each feature in the decision-making process. In the RF, feature importance is calculated based on the information gain in impurity at each split in the DTs. This information gain is measured during branch splitting by calculating how a particular feature reduces impurity in the target variable. The importance value represents the relative importance of each feature for classification and is usually normalized so that they sum up to 1 (100%). We focused on the top 14 features by importance in RF, as they account for 30% of the cumulative importance, indicating their significant role in distinguishing between high and low severity condition groups (Figure 2D). The 193 immune features had a hierarchical structure according to the characteristics of cellular immunology data, with major immune response components identified via flow cytometry gating and distinguished according to expert knowledge (Figure S3). The frequency of CD4 T cells out of live cells (PercOf_Live_CD4) was a highly influential feature, along with CD4 and CD8 T cell populations. Feature importance for all 193 immune features is listed in Table S2.

While immune profiles in the upper hierarchy among the top 14 may show high importance, including the frequency of CD4 T cells out of live cells (percOf_Live_CD4; 5.73%, ranked 1), the frequency of non-T, non-B cells out of live cells (percOf_Live_nonTnonB; 2.18%, ranked 5), and the frequency of CD8 T cells out of live cells (percOf_live_CD8; 1.51%, ranked 8), these immune profiles are not entirely independent, and their interrelated nature likely explains their high rankings. The hierarchical structure within the cellular immunology data suggests that higher-order immune components may capture overlapping yet complementary aspects of immune response, amplifying their combined impact in the model. The immune profiles in the lower hierarchy (subpopulations of CD4 and CD8 T cells) were also highly associated with severity status. One interesting feature was the frequency of activated CD4 central memory T cells (percOf_CD4CM_HLADRposCD38pos; 2.56%, ranked 4), which is located at the lowest hierarchy of the CD4 T cell population.

We validated the 14 highly influential immune features for severity status prediction by comparing the distribution of the landscape of patients with COVID-19 using all immune features versus the 14 selected features. In the UMAP embedding with 193 features, patients with COVID-19 were not distinguished by annotated severity level. However, in the embedding space using the 14 selected features (30% of cumulative importances in RF), patients with high and low severity conditions clustered distinctly (Figure 2E), suggesting that feature selection considering target outcomes helped highlight the most predictive elements within the immune profiles, enabling better separation of severity groups.

Benchmarking RF-derived features using an alternative feature selection method

To evaluate whether the RF-derived immune signatures were robust to the choice of feature selection strategy, we benchmarked the features highlighted in Figure 2D against an adaptive LASSO-based selection procedure applied to the same baseline immune profiles. Adaptive LASSO selected 45 features from the full 193-feature space. Among the 14 features from RF, 6 features were also selected by adaptive LASSO, spanning multiple immune compartments, including CD4 T cell populations (percOf_Live_CD4, percOf_CD4CM_HLADRpos, and percOf_CD4EM2_CXCR5pos), CD8 T cell activation-related populations (percOf_CD8CM_HLADRpos and percOf_CD8EM1_HLADRpos), and a B cell-associated population (percOf_BcellPB_CXCR5pos). The observed overlap (6 of 14) exceeded the overlap expected by chance given the overall selection rate (expected overlap ≈3.26 of 14; Figure 4), indicating that several key contributors to severity are consistently highlighted across an ensemble-based nonlinear model and a sparsity-driven linear model.

Interpreting immune contributions to COVID-19 severity: SHAP analysis of predictive models

We utilized SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) analysis to interpret the severity status prediction model,22 linking cellular immunologic data with clinical outcomes (Figures 2F and 2G). The SHAP summary plot (Figure 2F) shows the top 20 immune features influencing the prediction model, illustrating each feature’s impact on clinical severity status. SHAP values indicate each feature’s contribution to predicting high severity (above 0) or less severity (below 0), with each dot representing a COVID-19 sample and color indicating feature value magnitude. The frequency of CD4 T cells out of live cells (percOf_Live_CD4), activated CD4 central memory T cells (percOf_CD4CM_HLADRposCD38pos and percOF_CD4CM_HLADRpos), and activated CD8 central memory T cells (percOf_CD8CM_HLADRpos) had a wide range of SHAP values for high severity, highlighting their significant predictive abilities. Patients with lower values of CD4 and CD8 T cells among live cells (percOf_Live_CD4 and percOf_Live_CD8) are predicted to have high severity, indicating that reduced levels of these essential immune cells may impair the ability to combat the virus, leading to severe conditions. Conversely, high values of non-T, non-B cells out of live cells (percOf_Live_nonTnonB) are associated with high severity. Comparing feature values for CD4 T cells among live cells (percOf_Live_CD4; ranked 1) and two subtypes of CD4 central memory T cells (percOf_CD4CM_HLADRpos; ranked 3 and percOf_CD4CM_CD38pos; ranked 9), we observe opposite effects: High values of central memory CD4 T cells (CD4CM) positive for HLA-DR (HLADRpos) and CD38 (CD38pos) predict high severity, while high values of CD4 T cells among live cells predict low severity.

We further analyzed the interaction effects between immune features in the model, showing how combinations of two immune features predict severity status (Figure 2G). The interaction between percOf_Live_CD4 and percOf_CD4EM1_HLADRpos indicates that low percOf_Live_CD4 (<10%) combined with high percOf_CD4EM1_HLADRpos (>30%) contributes to high severity. The interaction between percOf_CD4CM_HLADRposCD38pos and percOf_CD8EM2_EOMESpos suggests a complex interplay between different T cell subsets and activation states in predicting COVID-19 severity. High levels of CD8 effector memory T cells expressing EOMES (percOf_CD8EM2_EOMESpos) amplify the impact of high percOf_CD4CM_HLADRposCD38pos values on severity, indicating compounded effects of high activation states in both CD4 and CD8 T cells. Higher frequencies of CD4 central memory T cells that are HLA-DR positive, combined with low to moderate frequencies of CD4 T cells out of live cells, are associated with high severity. Similarly, the combined effect of high percOf_CD8CM_HLADRpos and high percOf_CD8EM1_HLADRpos is linked to higher SHAP values, pushing predictions toward high severity. Highly activated CD8 central and effector memory T cells may reflect intense immune responses, potentially leading to severe outcomes due to excessive activation. The SHAP analysis underscores the significant role of specific T cell populations in predicting COVID-19 severity. The balance between effective immune response and immunopathology could explain the wide range of SHAP values, as high levels of activated T cells may lead to either effective viral control or heightened immune-mediated damage, causing severe outcomes. While SHAP analysis enhances the interpretability of complex models and helps identify key immune contributors to disease severity, it can increase computational demands, particularly for ensemble models such as RFs. However, SHAP values can be approximated or precomputed offline to support practical deployment in clinical settings with limited resources. These findings validate the utility of deep immune profiling and the effectiveness of interpretable predictive modeling in translational medicine, as highlighted in previous research.18

Predictive modeling of classifying severity status demonstrates the feasibility and effectiveness of using high-dimensional immune profiling data in conjunction with machine learning models to predict clinical outcomes in patients with COVID-19. By focusing on key immune features and utilizing SHAP analysis for model interpretation, we provided insights into the immunological underpinnings of disease severity. This approach not only advances our understanding of COVID-19 but also has potential implications for managing other infectious diseases through predictive modeling.

Cross-platform evaluation in an independent whole-blood CyTOF cohort

To assess the translational robustness of the immune signature beyond the discovery dataset, we evaluated our framework using an independent Mount Sinai COVID-19 immune profiling cohort.23 This external cohort represents a marked domain shift relative to the discovery cohort, transitioning from PBMC-based flow cytometry to whole-blood CyTOF profiling, where granulocytes and other myeloid populations substantially alter the cellular landscape and the distributions of frequency-based immune features. We evaluated generalizability at the framework level by harmonizing feature definitions and reapplying the identical modeling pipeline within the external dataset. After harmonization, 54 features were aligned between cohorts based on consistent parent-population gate definitions, and a 9-feature core set was defined using predefined reliability criteria in the external data. In the discovery cohort, restricting the model to these 9 features preserved most of the predictive signal (AUROC 0.825 vs. 0.871 using all 193 features). In the external validation cohort, an RF model trained and evaluated using stratified 5-fold cross-validation achieved a mean AUROC of 0.6485 ± 0.0698 and a mean AUPRC of 0.6535 ± 0.0504, with the best-performing fold reaching an AUROC of 0.7407 (Figure S5). Feature importance analysis in the external cohort indicated that broad lymphoid and non-lymphoid composition measures (e.g., percOf_Live_CD8 and percOf_Live_nonTnonB) were among the leading contributors (Table S3). Although feature rankings differed from the PBMC-based model, consistent with whole-blood compositional effects, the harmonized core signature retained measurable discrimination in an independent cohort under a distinct platform and specimen context.

Although the original dataset was designed to compare healthy individuals and patients with COVID-19 to uncover immune perturbations caused by infection, our study focuses only on patients with COVID-19. By redefining clinical outcomes around intra-patient severity dynamics, we demonstrate how high-dimensional immune data can be repurposed to model meaningful changes in disease trajectory. Importantly, our work highlights the potential for expanding such targeted, well-curated cohorts through integration with EHRs to create clinically relevant prediction tasks. This approach provides a pathway toward more comprehensive, longitudinal modeling that incorporates both immune profiling and real-world clinical context.

The RF model was employed for this task, with data split into a 70% development set and a 30% evaluation set (Figure 3B). We designed four RF models with varying sets of immune features based on hierarchical structures derived from manual gating of flow cytometry data: (1) RFA with 4 top-hierarchy features, (2) RFB with 23 top- and second-hierarchy features, (3) RFC with all 193 features, and (4) RFD with features selected from RFC. All models were optimized using grid search for hyperparameter tuning (Table S3). Micro-average AUROC and AUPRC were used for performance evaluation.

Evaluating the role of deep immune profiles in predicting future severity changes in patients with COVID-19

Three models (RFA, RFB, and RFC) utilizing hierarchical immune features showed similar overall AUROC and AUPRC performance (Figure 3C). While AUROC slightly increased with lower-level features (0.7297–0.7314), AUPRC decreased (0.5808–0.5503). Due to severe class imbalance (1 vs. 4.1 vs. 4.5), overall AUPRC is a more reasonable evaluation metric in this task. This suggests that RFA, with 4 top-hierarchy features (frequencies of CD4, CD8, B cells, and non-T/non-B cells out of live cells), is the best model for predicting severity changes after 7 days. However, since this is not a binary classification, the reported AUROC and AUPRC (Figure 3C) are averaged by repeating the evaluation of one-class versus rest-class (OvR),24 such as severity progression versus the rest of the outcomes. Thus, we considered the evaluation criteria for selecting the best model as OvR AUROC and OvR AUPRC, especially for severity progression versus others. In clinical applications, it is more important to identify earlier which patients’ clinical conditions will worsen rather than remain stable or improve. RFB, with up to second-hierarchy features, showed the best prediction ability for severity progression (AUROC 0.5585 and AUPRC 0.1231) while maintaining similar performance for stability and improvement predictions compared to RFA and RFC (Figure 3D). Note that baseline OvR AUPRC for severity progression is 0.1087 (5 severity progressed patients in the evaluation set over 46 patients in the evaluation set). Looking at the OvR performance results for other classes (severity, stability, and improvement), there were cases where performance was further improved when considering the lower level of the feature hierarchy. This suggests that some of the deep immune profiles affect the performance improvement of future severity change prediction, as well as some are also detrimental to the model's predictive power.

We examined the feature importance of RFC to identify and remove redundant features using backward elimination. After iteratively removing less important features and hyperparameter tuning with 5-fold cross-validation, 96 immune features (29.87% of cumulative importance in RFC) were removed, leaving 97 features (70.14% cumulative importance). The RFD model, built with these selected features, showed the best overall performance (AUROC 0.7704 and AUPRC 0.5748; Figure 3C). For severity progression prediction, RFD showed an 11.37% improvement in OvR AUROC and a 43.22% improvement in OvR AUPRC compared to RFB (Figures 3D and S6). Cellular immune profiling benefits from hierarchical structures, allowing systematic categorization and analysis of immune cell subsets and activation states. This hierarchical approach enhances the understanding and prediction of disease outcomes by assessing both the presence and functional states of immune cells. Our findings underscore the importance of deep immune profiles in improving predictive performance for future severity changes in patients with COVID-19, demonstrating the utility of incorporating hierarchical immune data in model development.

Importance of immune features in predicting future severity changes in patients with COVID-19

97 deep immune features, which had 70.14% of cumulative importance, were selected for building the final RF model (Figures 3E and 3F). Interestingly, these features were not biased toward any specific cell population but were utilized evenly, contributing to the effectiveness of the RFD model. Similar to the severity status prediction model, the frequency of CD4 T cells out of live cells (percOf_Live_CD4) was the most important feature. While many top- and second-hierarchy features, such as CD8 Naive T cells, CD8EMRA T cells, and CD4EMRA T cells, were highly ranked, deeper immune features such as percOf_CD4cTfh_CX3CR1pos (3rd) and percOf_CD4EM1_HLADRpos (4th) were also significantly utilized.

We performed SHAP analysis for the future severity change prediction model (RFD). For multi-class classification, it is challenging to determine which features influence specific class predictions based on RF’s feature importance alone (Figure 3G). The SHAP summary plot visualizes the impact of top immune features on the model’s prediction for future severity changes in patients with COVID-19. The average absolute SHAP values indicate the average contribution of each feature to the model output across all samples, categorized by severity progression, stability, and improvement. The distribution of SHAP values highlights the importance of specific T cell and B cell populations. The frequency of CD4 T cells out of live cells was the most influential feature across all outcomes. Additionally, the frequencies of CD4 and CD8 T cell populations, such as CD4 effector memory T cells and CD8 effector memory T cells, and the frequency of switch memory B cells, were key features.

We investigated the influence of individual features in predicting each future severity change, with a focus on severity progression. percOf_CD8EM2_CD95pos emerged as the most critical variable for predicting severity progression. Specifically, a lower value of percOf_CD8EM2_CD95pos was associated with an increased risk of worsening severity after 7 days, indicating that reduced frequencies of CD8 effector memory 2 T cells expressing CD95 are a significant predictor of deteriorating clinical conditions in patients with COVID-19. Regarding both severity progression and improvement, the direction of effects for the “frequency of CD4 T cells out of live cells” shows a clear inverse relationship. Patients with a low value of percOf_Live_CD4 at the baseline time point are predicted to experience symptom worsening after 7 days, whereas patients with a high value of percOf_Live_CD4 at baseline are more likely to show symptom improvement.

These results demonstrate that deep immune profiling, when integrated with machine learning models, can effectively predict future changes in disease severity in patients with COVID-19. The hierarchical structure of immune features, combined with advanced predictive analytics, provides valuable insights into the immunologic factors influencing disease progression and recovery. This approach not only enhances our understanding of COVID-19 pathophysiology but also offers potential for improved patient management and personalized treatment strategies.

Comparative evaluation of immune and clinical features in predicting future severity changes

To determine whether immune profiling provides predictive value beyond routinely available clinical variables, we conducted an additional clinical-comparison analysis within the subset of samples that had complete clinical covariate data. Whereas the primary severity-change prediction analysis in Figure 3 used all available COVID-19 patient samples (n = 153), inclusion of clinical covariates required restricting to a complete-case cohort with no missing clinical variables (n = 107). Within this complete-case cohort, we compared three RF models trained on: (1) clinical covariates alone (13 variables), (2) immune features alone using the RFD feature set (selected from the full immune feature space based on cumulative feature importance; 97 variables), and (3) a combined feature set including both clinical covariates and RFD immune features. Clinical covariates included demographic factors (sex, age, body mass index, and self-reported race), complete blood count measures (total white blood cell count, absolute neutrophil count, absolute lymphocyte count, absolute monocyte count, absolute eosinophil count, and absolute basophil count), and major comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and hyperlipidemia). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the complete-case cohort are summarized in Table S4. Using the same 70% development and 30% evaluation split within the complete-case cohort, the clinical-only model achieved a micro-averaged AUROC of 0.682 (one-vs-rest AUROCs: progression 0.530, stability 0.550, improvement 0.605). In contrast, the immune-only model achieved a higher micro-AUROC of 0.762 (progression 0.478, stability 0.661, improvement 0.767), and the combined clinical-plus-immune model achieved a micro-AUROC of 0.758 (progression 0.547, stability 0.631, improvement 0.763; Table S5). These results indicate that immune-derived features provide substantial discriminative value beyond standard clinical covariates for modeling short-term severity dynamics among hospitalized patients with COVID-19.

Practical application of the predictive models: A case study

We demonstrate the practical implications for predictive models utilizing baseline immune features in relation to clinical phenotypes. We present a case study involving two patients with COVID-19 who had blood samples collected twice at 7-day intervals (days 0 and 7). Longitudinal clinical outcomes were assessed at three intervals (days 0, 7, and 14) by clinicians. Our objective was to predict the severity status of each patient at each time point using baseline immune features, without prior clinical knowledge, and to forecast the prognosis for severity changes at 7 days post-measured immune features. These case studies are intended to be illustrative demonstrations of interpretability at the individual-patient level and are not designed to provide independent evidence of model generalizability or clinical utility. Population-level performance is evaluated separately using held-out testing and cross-validation as reported above.

Imagine this scenario: A COVID-19 patient is admitted to the hospital, and blood samples are collected to assess their health status. Immunologists profile the patient’s cellular immunologic data, which is then analyzed to determine the current severity status and predict future severity changes using a model trained on cellular immunologic data and clinical phenotypes from previously hospitalized patients. While clinicians should assess a patient’s clinical symptoms based on their expertise, the predicted results by immune features can provide evidence of the current severity status and alert them to potential fluctuations in severity in the near future. In this retrospective illustration, model outputs are presented as complementary evidence rather than a replacement for physician assessment or clinical decision-making. Discussion

In this study, we present a translational immune health framework that can build predictive models by investigating the latent relationships between immunological data and clinical outcomes by utilizing several representative supervised approaches. Additionally, we describe learning systems that use explainable algorithms to embed these predictive outcomes into clinical use. Utilizing both predictive and descriptive frameworks is essential to convince clinical experts to use the supervised models with immunological data for clinical use. The set of steps for building supervised models makes generalized rules based on observations, meaning the model defines a specific theory based on the observed relationships among pairs of input immune profiles and output clinical outcomes. Applying the model in practical use is a process of making a specific decision from general principles, where new input data are applied to the trained model to get the target outcome. This framework provides a structured approach for developing models that capture observed relationships between immune features and clinical outcomes, allowing clinicians to apply these insights in real-world patient care. While this study utilizes established machine learning algorithms rather than proposing a novel mathematical model, its primary innovation lies in the integration of these tools into a specialized framework tailored for high-dimensional, low-sample-size immunology data. By incorporating hierarchical feature selection and interpretability layers, the framework provides a structured pathway to translate complex single-cell signatures into clinically relevant prognostic insights.

To guide the immunologist in transferring their knowledge at the molecular level to the epidemiological level by developing supervised learning approaches, we described the details of a learning process by defining two distinct clinical scenarios. Although the example flow cytometry data were analyzed for identifying the significant cell populations across healthy individuals and patients with COVID-19, we showed the possibility of predictive models within patients with COVID-19 to distinguish a binary class of severe and non-severe groups and a multi-class of severity changes by considering longitudinal aspects. Our findings demonstrated that incorporating deep immune profiles, obtained through flow cytometry data, significantly improved the predictive performance of the models. Expanding the comprehensive analysis to include lower-level immune profiles also provided important immune cell populations associated with COVID-19 severity status, enhancing the model’s prediction ability to forecast future severity changes in patient condition.

Furthermore, we addressed the challenge of model interpretability by employing explainable algorithms with SHAP that allowed us to understand the contribution of each immune feature to the model’s decision-making process, making it more transparent and understandable. This is required for engaging clinical experts and immunological researchers, as they can see the rationale behind the model’s prediction and use these insights to inform clinical trials. Overall, the translational immune health framework we suggested can bridge the gap between immunological researchers’ and clinicians’ needs.

In addition to our contributions, it is valuable to position this work within the broader context of machine learning approaches developed for immunological data analysis. Tomic et al.25 introduced an automated machine learning framework designed to analyze complex immunological datasets by automating algorithm selection across diverse data.25 Our study builds upon this foundation with a specific focus on COVID-19 severity prediction using flow cytometry data. While previous studies emphasized ML model selection across diverse datasets, our approach integrates predictive models and explainable algorithms to enhance model interpretability, allowing clinical experts to understand the rationale behind predictions. Additionally, we address the challenge of longitudinal data analysis, enabling predictions of disease progression over time. This aspect is particularly relevant for managing COVID-19, where patient conditions can fluctuate rapidly.

Our work emphasizes that cellular immunology data, when analyzed using appropriate machine learning strategies and paired with clinical phenotypes, have strong potential to inform patient care. It is worth noting that while we focused on patients with COVID-19 in this study, the framework we presented is not disease-specific and can be extended to other infectious conditions and even chronic diseases, including autoimmune and inflammatory disorders. As more immunology datasets are generated for chronic disease populations, our approach can be adapted to support early risk stratification and patient management strategies in those domains. This scalability supports the long-term translational potential of immune-health modeling using machine learning.

Limitations of the study

Although the dataset used in this study provides deep immune profiling across 193 features, capturing a wide range of T cell, B cell, and activation states, this high dimensionality introduces challenges related to overfitting and complexity. To address this, we applied supervised learning algorithms designed for high-dimensional settings, leveraged hierarchical feature structures, and incorporated feature selection and interpretability methods such as SHAP analysis. Although not originally intended for clinical prediction, our study builds on this deeply profiled dataset as a follow-up, demonstrating how such high-resolution immune data can inform predictive modeling of clinical outcomes. We note that the features analyzed in this study are primarily cell-type and subset frequencies derived from hierarchical gating, and the dataset does not include functional signaling readouts such as phospho-epitopes. In future work, integrating soluble plasma biomarkers (e.g., cytokines) with cellular immune profiling will be an important direction to assess whether combined models improve prediction performance and clinical utility. Such multimodal integration was not evaluated in the present study because soluble biomarker measurements were not available in this cohort. Accordingly, our results should be interpreted as identifying severity-associated immune composition patterns rather than direct intracellular signaling mechanisms or causal pathway activation. Nevertheless, compositional shifts in key immune compartments (e.g., CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets and non-T/non-B populations) are biologically meaningful and have been repeatedly associated with COVID-19 severity, supporting the relevance of these signatures for prognostic modeling.

We acknowledge that our proof-of-concept study has some limitations. Because this study retrospectively analyzed previously collected immune profiling datasets, prospective validation in clinically representative cohorts will be needed to establish clinical applicability. This study utilizes 157 patient samples, which may appear limited in size; however, it is derived from one of the largest high-dimensional flow cytometry datasets available, published by Mathew et al. (2020).18 The dataset provides rich immune profiling data, making it well-suited for machine learning-based predictive modeling. While our primary goal was to evaluate the feasibility of translating high-dimensional immune profiling into predictive tools for clinical outcomes, we recognize that a larger and more diverse dataset would further strengthen our findings. We also acknowledge that strong performance within a single cohort is insufficient to establish generalizability. To address this limitation, we performed an external cohort evaluation using an independent whole-blood CyTOF dataset.23 Because this validation involves a substantial technical and biological domain shift (PBMC-based flow cytometry versus whole-blood CyTOF), direct parameter transfer is not statistically stable. The reduced performance in the external cohort may reflect several sources of heterogeneity, including differences in immune profiling platforms, specimen source, cohort composition, preprocessing procedures, and unmeasured batch effects. Therefore, we evaluated generalizability at the framework/signature level by harmonizing feature definitions and reapplying the identical modeling strategy within the independent cohort; the retained discrimination supports that the identified immune signature captures reproducible severity-associated signals across heterogeneous experimental platforms. However, we believe that the value of this study lies in its translational framework, which demonstrates how immune profiles can inform predictive modeling in clinical settings. We have emphasized the need for future validation across independent, multi-institutional cohorts as a key direction for further research. Further strengthening translational interpretation will require prospective validation, orthogonal modality validation using single-cell RNA sequencing data, and extensive subgroup analyses.

Additionally, the multi-class classification task for predicting future severity changes was subject to class imbalance, with relatively fewer samples in the progression class. To mitigate this, we employed class weighting to ensure the minority class was given stronger influence during model training. We also evaluated model performance using micro-averaged metrics and one-vs-rest comparisons to assess the model’s ability to detect severity progression. Our results showed meaningful gains in predictive performance for the minority class. Nonetheless, we recognize that class imbalance remains a key challenge.

The longitudinal data used in this study are limited to a 7-day interval. While this constraint reflects the available structure of the dataset, it restricts our ability to model longer-term disease trajectories. As such, the current framework focuses on short-term clinical changes. Future studies with extended follow-up periods and more frequent time points will be critical for capturing the dynamic nature of immune responses and improving the accuracy of disease progression prediction over time. Future studies should also focus on external validation across independent cohorts, incorporating longitudinal patient data and EHR integration to enhance model generalizability and clinical applicability.

Experimental model and study participant details

Discovery cohort

This study used de-identified, publicly available clinical and immune profiling data originally collected by Mathew et al. (2020).18 A total of 116 hospitalized COVID-19 patients (SARS-CoV-2 PCR-confirmed) were included in the discovery cohort analysis, contributing 157 timepoint-specific peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples with 193-dimensional flow cytometry–derived immune features. Among these, a complete-case cohort of n = 107 patients with no missing clinical covariate data was used for the clinical-comparison analysis. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of this complete-case cohort are summarized in Table S4.

All participants in the discovery cohort were human subjects. Patient age ranged broadly (mean ± SD: 59.54 ± 14.18 years). The cohort was 56.1% male and 43.9% female. Sex differences in severity outcomes were observed: female patients were more frequently represented in the improvement group (57.8%) compared to the stable group (30.6%), and this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.029). The potential influence of sex on immune profiles and severity outcomes is acknowledged; however, the sample size was insufficient to perform sex-stratified subgroup analyses, which represents a limitation of the current study’s generalizability. Gender-specific analyses were not performed because gender information was not available in the analyzed public datasets.

Race and ethnicity data were collected as part of the original study. The cohort included African American (58.9%), Caucasian (32.7%), and Asian (8.4%) patients. Comorbidities were common: hypertension (75.7%), hyperlipidemia (57.0%), and diabetes mellitus (41.1%). No statistically significant differences in race/ethnicity or comorbidity distribution were observed across severity-change groups (all p > 0.05).

Patients and timepoint-specific samples were allocated to clinical severity or severity-change groups according to the clinical annotations and NIH Ordinal Severity Scale information provided in the original dataset from Mathew et al. (2020).18

External validation cohort

External validation was performed using the independent Mount Sinai COVID-19 immune profiling cohort (Chen et al.23; ImmPort Study ID: SDY2011). This cohort comprised whole-blood CyTOF profiles from hospitalized COVID-19 patients. Group allocation was based on the severe COVID-19 and moderate COVID-19 labels provided in the original publication.23 After quality control and harmonization procedures, 107 samples were included in the external evaluation. Detailed demographic information for this cohort is available in the original publication.23

Method details

Dataset overview and clinical scenario definition

This study used high-dimensional flow cytometry-derived immune profiling data from hospitalized patients with COVID-19 originally reported by Mathew et al. Two supervised prediction tasks were defined. The first task was severity status classification, which aimed to predict whether a patient was in a high-severity or low-severity condition using immune profiles measured at the time of blood collection. The second task was future severity change prediction, which aimed to predict whether disease severity would increase, remain stable, or improve over a 7-day interval using baseline immune profiles. After preprocessing, 116 patients with COVID-19 were included in the analysis. Samples were excluded if immune feature values were missing or if severity annotations were unavailable in the clinical metadata. Among included patients, 41 had immune profiles available at both day 0 (D0) and day 7 (D7), 68 had profiles available only at D0, and 7 had profiles available only at D7. Disease severity was graded according to the NIH Ordinal Severity Scale at 7-day intervals from enrollment. Across the two prediction tasks, 157 timepoint-specific data points with 193 immune features were analyzed. These features represented T cell and B cell population frequencies derived from hierarchical manual gating of flow cytometry data.

Development of supervised models for severity status prediction

Data splitting and cross-validation

For severity status classification, 157 timepoint-specific data points were used as the labeled dataset. Data were split into an 80% development set and a 20% held-out evaluation set by stratified random sampling to preserve class distribution. The held-out evaluation set was reserved exclusively for final model assessment and was not used for model training, feature selection, or hyperparameter tuning. Within the development set, stratified 5-fold cross-validation was performed for model optimization. In each cross-validation iteration, 1-fold was used as the validation set and the remaining 4-folds were used for training.

Algorithm selection and hyperparameter tuning

Five supervised learning algorithms were evaluated for severity status classification: decision tree, support vector machine with a radial basis function kernel, artificial neural network, random forest, and gradient boosting machine. Hyperparameters were tuned within the development set using stratified 5-fold cross-validation. For each model, the optimal hyperparameter configuration was selected by maximizing the mean AUROC across validation folds.

For decision trees, maximum tree depth was searched over {3, 5, 7, 10}, and the splitting criterion was searched over {gini, entropy}. For support vector machines, gamma was searched over {0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1}, and C was searched over {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1}. For artificial neural networks, the number of hidden layers was searched over {1, 2}, the number of nodes per hidden layer was searched over {50, 100, 200}, the random seed for weight initialization was searched over {1, 3, 5, 7, 9}, and the activation function was searched over {ReLU, tanh}. For random forests, the number of estimators was searched over {50, 100, 200, 300}, and maximum tree depth was searched over {3, 5, 7, 10}. For gradient boosting machines, the number of boosting stages was searched over {50, 100, 200, 300}, the learning rate was searched over {0.001, 0.01, 0.1}, and maximum tree depth was searched over {3, 5, 7, 10}.

Model evaluation

Final model performance was evaluated on the held-out evaluation set using sensitivity, specificity, AUROC, and AUPRC. For binary classification, model outputs were represented as predicted probabilities ranging from 0 to 1. Patients were classified as high severity when the predicted probability exceeded 0.5.

Feature importance and interpretability analysis

For the random forest model used in severity status classification, feature importance was calculated based on the mean decrease in impurity across all decision trees and normalized to sum to 1. The top 14 features, which accounted for approximately 30% of cumulative feature importance, were highlighted as RF-derived immune features associated with severity status prediction. To visualize an example decision process, one representative decision tree was selected from the trained random forest model. UMAP was applied to visualize patient distributions using all 193 immune features and, separately, using the 14 RF-derived features. These embeddings were used to assess whether the RF-derived feature subset improved separation between high-severity and low-severity groups. To evaluate the robustness of RF-derived feature selection, adaptive LASSO was applied to the same baseline immune profiles as a complementary linear and sparsity-driven feature selection approach. Features selected by adaptive LASSO were compared with the 14 RF-derived features. The observed overlap was compared with the expected overlap calculated from the overall adaptive LASSO selection rate across the full 193-feature space. SHAP analysis was applied to the trained random forest model to quantify feature-level contributions to individual predictions. SHAP summary plots were used to identify immune features contributing to high- or low-severity predictions, and SHAP interaction analyses were performed to examine pairwise feature effects on predicted severity status.

Development of the random forest model for future severity changes

Outcome definition and data construction

Clinical outcomes were defined as changes in the NIH Ordinal Severity Scale from baseline to 7 days after blood collection. When immune profiles were measured at day 0 (D0), the paired input-output consisted of the D0 immune profile and the change in severity from D0 to day 7 (D7). If a patient had immune profiles at both D0 and D7, two distinct paired data points were generated: (i) the D0 immune profile with the D0-to-D7 severity change and (ii) the D7 immune profile with the D7-to-day 14 (D14) severity change. Four samples were excluded because of missing 7-day severity annotations, yielding a final dataset of 153 data points: 16 with severity progression, 65 with stable severity, and 72 with severity improvement.

Data splitting and class imbalance handling

The dataset was divided into a 70% development set and a 30% held-out evaluation set using stratified random sampling to preserve class distribution, corresponding to approximately 10% progression, 43% stable severity, and 47% improvement. To address class imbalance, class weights were applied during model training and computed as the inverse of class frequencies, giving higher weight to the minority progression class.

Hierarchical feature set design

Four random forest models were designed using immune feature sets of increasing hierarchical depth. RFA included 4 top-hierarchy features, consisting of CD4, CD8, B cell, and non-T/non-B cell frequencies among live cells. RFB included 23 top- and second-hierarchy features. RFC included all 193 immune features. RFD included features selected from RFC through backward elimination based on cumulative feature importance.

Hyperparameter tuning

Hyperparameter tuning was performed within the development set using stratified 5-fold cross-validation. The number of estimators was searched over {50, 100, 200, 300}, the splitting criterion was fixed to entropy, and maximum tree depth was searched over {3, 5, 7, 10}. Iterative grid search was performed by updating the hyperparameter space around the previously best-performing configuration until convergence.

Feature selection through backward elimination

For RFC, features were ranked by feature importance, and the least informative features were iteratively removed. Hyperparameter tuning was repeated after each removal step until further removal degraded model performance. The resulting RFD model retained 97 immune features, representing 70.14% of cumulative feature importance in RFC.

Model evaluation and interpretation

Multi-class classification performance was assessed using micro-averaged AUROC and AUPRC. One-vs-rest AUROC and AUPRC were also reported for each severity-change class, with particular emphasis on severity progression because of its clinical relevance. SHAP analysis was applied to the trained RFD model to quantify feature-level contributions to individual predictions. SHAP values were computed for the 97 selected immune features across 153 data points and were used to evaluate each feature’s relative contribution to severity progression, stable severity, and severity improvement predictions.

External validation cohort and feature harmonization

External validation cohort

To evaluate generalizability beyond the discovery cohort, external validation was performed using an independent Mount Sinai COVID-19 immune profiling cohort, which is publicly available through ImmPort under Study ID SDY2011. This cohort included 137 FCS files representing high-dimensional CyTOF profiles from whole-blood samples of patients with COVID-19. Because the discovery cohort was profiled using PBMC-based flow cytometry, whereas the external cohort was profiled using whole-blood CyTOF, this analysis represented a cross-platform and specimen-level domain shift with differences in cellular composition and feature distributions.

Feature harmonization

Immune features were harmonized between the discovery and external cohorts based on shared hierarchical gating definitions. A total of 54 immune features in the external cohort were identified as corresponding to the parent-population gate structure used in the discovery cohort. Manual gating was performed on the validation FCS files to obtain raw cell counts for each gated population. Frequencies were calculated as the gated population count divided by the corresponding parent gate count and were scaled to a 0 to 100 range to match the feature scale used in the discovery analyses.

Quality control and core feature definition

To reduce instability from low-abundance immune populations, sample-feature level quality control was applied using a predefined cell-count threshold. For each sample, immune populations with fewer than 50 cells in the corresponding gate were considered unreliable and masked as missing. Masking rates were calculated across the 54 aligned features, and the core feature set was defined as features with masking rates below 30% in the external dataset. This procedure yielded 9 core immune features: percOf_Live_nonTnonB, percOf_Live_CD4, percOf_Live_Bcell, percOf_CD4_CD4CM, percOf_Bcell_BcellNaive, percOf_Live_CD8, percOf_CD4EM2_PD1pos, percOf_Bcell_BcellCD27negIgDneg, and percOf_CD4_CD4EM3.

External cohort inclusion and imputation

From the initial 137 samples, 14 COVID-negative samples and 4 samples lacking severity annotations were excluded, leaving 119 samples. Samples missing five or more of the nine core features were further excluded, resulting in a final external cohort of 107 samples. Among these, 64 samples were complete for all nine core features. For samples with one to four missing features, feature-wise median imputation was performed using values calculated within the external cohort.

External model evaluation

Given the cross-platform and specimen-level domain shift, direct parameter transfer from the discovery-trained model to the external cohort was not expected to be stable. Therefore, external evaluation was conducted by applying the same preprocessing, feature definitions, and modeling strategy within the external cohort using the harmonized nine core features. Model performance was primarily assessed using stratified 5-fold cross-validation and summarized as mean AUROC and mean AUPRC with standard deviations across folds. A complementary stratified 70%/30% train-test split within the external cohort was also performed. As a sensitivity analysis, a frozen-parameter transfer setting, in which the model was trained in the discovery cohort and tested in the external cohort after harmonization, was evaluated to illustrate the expected performance degradation under incompatible measurement spaces. This analysis was not used as the primary estimate of external generalizability.
