AI‐Driven Synthesis in Medicinal Chemistry: Integrating Large Language Models, Robotic Automation, and Sustainability Metrics to Accelerate Drug Discovery
Authors: Amit Gangwal, Antonio Lavecchia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTArtificial intelligence (AI) is transforming synthetic chemistry from task‐specific predictors into integrated platforms that unify retrosynthesis, reaction optimization, and closed‐loop robotic automation. This review highlights how AI‐assisted planning and robotic execution shorten cycle times, reduce step counts, and improve route sustainability in medicinal chemistry. Recent advances, including large language models (LLMs), template‐free retrosynthesis, and Bayesian optimization, are evaluated alongside key limitations in dataset quality, reproducibility, and deployment costs. To ensure translational relevance, reproducible benchmarks such as step count, time‐to‐in vitro, and green metrics (E‐factor, process mass intensity) are emphasized. This review proposes a hierarchical framework structured across three interconnected levels: cognitive planning, physical execution, and translational evaluation. Within this structure, key elements include LLM‐based synthesis planning, robotic and closed‐loop execution, interpretable decision‐making, sustainability‐by‐design, advanced reaction optimization, and multi‐objective retrosynthesis. Together, these components provide a conceptual basis for integrating digital intelligence with physical experimentation. By embedding green chemistry principles and regulatory awareness, AI is increasingly positioned not only as a predictive tool but also as an assistive collaborator supporting decision‐making in medicinal chemistry workflows. The convergence of AI, robotics, and sustainability metrics highlights an emerging transition; however, realizing a future where every experiment reliably feeds back into autonomous learning loops requires overcoming significant current barriers in data standardization and hardware interoperability.Keywords: artificial intelligence, autonomous synthesis, green chemistry, large language models, medicinal chemistry, retrosynthesis

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly influencing synthetic chemistry and reshaping several of its core paradigms. This review critically evaluates how AI‐driven synthesis technologies, including autonomous planning systems and robotic execution platforms, enhance capabilities in medicinal chemistry, supporting the design and planning of complex drug molecules with improved efficiency, safety, and environmental sustainability. Importantly, beyond providing a descriptive overview, this review critically examines the real‐world applicability, limitations, and translational readiness of current AI‐driven synthesis technologies, explicitly distinguishing between experimentally validated achievements, proof‐of‐concept systems, and forward‐looking expectations. Unlike traditional reviews that focus on individual computational tools, we emphasize the emerging integration of AI with laboratory automation, interpretable decision‐making, and sustainability‐oriented design. This integration reflects a shift from predictive assistance toward more coordinated systems that support medicinal chemistry decision‐making and drug discovery workflows.

Early AI tools showed strong performance in discrete tasks such as reaction prediction, retrosynthetic analysis, molecular design, and property optimization. The field is now progressing toward end‐to‐end frameworks that connect digital planning with physical execution, enabling streamlined drug discovery pipelines. This evolution is driven by advances in deep learning (DL), the expansion of reaction databases, and the emergence of large language models (LLMs), which show potential for capturing patterns related to structure–activity relationships (SAR) and synthetic accessibility, although their predictions remain dependent on training data and require experimental validation [1, 2].

Natural language processing (NLP) provides the conceptual foundation for these chemical LLMs. Transformer architectures, such as BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) and modern generative models like GPT‐4, encode and manipulate molecular representations (SMILES, SELFIES), enabling interpretation, translation, and generation of chemical structures [3]. When integrated with computer‐aided synthesis planning (CASP) tools, LLMs can propose candidate disconnections, assess synthetic feasibility, and suggest multi‐step routes; however, these capabilities remain dependent on integration with specialized tools and require experimental validation to ensure chemical correctness.

Self‐driving laboratories (SDLs) integrate planning algorithms, robotics, and real‐time analytics, exemplified by platforms such as ChemCrow [4], Sonidegib optimization, and closed‐loop Bayesian approaches [5, 6, 7, 8]. These systems accelerate medicinal chemistry workflows, enabling rapid identification of viable synthetic routes, multi‐objective optimization (MOO) of molecular properties, and integration of sustainability considerations.

Despite these successes, several open challenges remain, including the limited generalization of models to out‐of‐distribution substrates, the need for integrated evaluation of reaction conditions, and the alignment of AI‐generated routes with medicinal chemistry priorities such as scaffold diversity and late‐stage functionalization.

To provide a structured perspective, we organize the current landscape of AI‐driven synthesis into a three‐tiered hierarchical framework based on functional roles within synthesis workflows. This framework distinguishes between decision‐making and retrosynthetic reasoning, their experimental realization through automated systems, and the criteria required for real‐world applicability: (1) cognitive planning, encompassing LLM‐driven reasoning and multi‐objective retrosynthetic design; (2) physical execution, translating digital plans into reality through robotic closed‐loop systems and reaction optimization algorithms; and (3) translational evaluation, ensuring applicability through interpretability, sustainability, and scalability considerations. Rather than treating objectives such as green chemistry or condition optimization as isolated pillars, this hierarchy embeds them as cross‐cutting criteria guiding planning and execution. Accordingly, sustainability, reaction optimization, and multi‐objective design are treated as evaluation dimensions that influence decisions across all levels.

This framework provides a structured perspective for merging digital intelligence with physical experimentation, addressing both the efficiency and strategic requirements of modern drug discovery. Key challenges persist: template‐free retrosynthesis models, while flexible, often generate chemically implausible disconnections when applied to out‐of‐distribution substrates, with error rates exceeding 50% in some cases [9]. Similarly, models trained on existing reaction yield data have limited generalization for predicting high‐yield outcomes due to dataset biases toward low‐yield reactions [10]. This review addresses these limitations by emphasizing hybrid human–AI strategies, interpretable architectures, and prospective experimental validation as key requirements for pharmaceutical adoption.

AI‐Driven Synthesis Planning and Execution

Reaction Prediction and Retrosynthesis

Accurately predicting reaction outcomes, yields, selectivity, and kinetics remains a central challenge in synthetic chemistry, especially for designing and optimizing bioactive small molecules. Traditionally, this relied on time‐intensive trial‐and‐error and chemists' intuition [11]. Recent advances in AI have established a data‐driven foundation for chemical reasoning, where models learn from large reaction datasets rather than heuristic rules. Supervised learning, neural networks, decision trees, ensemble models, and graph neural networks (GNNs) now analyze millions of curated reactions to predict optimal conditions, improve selectivity, and propose feasible synthetic strategies [12]. These approaches enhance reagent and catalyst selection, reduce side‐product formation, and adapt to emerging paradigms such as flow chemistry and continuous manufacturing. Mechanistically‐annotated large‐scale datasets (e.g., mech‐USPTO‐31K) further improve model robustness for yield and condition prediction [13]. By integrating mechanistic and physicochemical constraints, AI‐based reaction prediction can support more reproducible and scalable synthesis design, although reproducibility depends strongly on data quality and experimental validation.

Building upon this predictive layer, retrosynthesis models extend AI's role from reaction‐level forecasting to multistep route design in medicinal chemistry [14]. Template‐free methods can propose plausible transformations, though they frequently generate chemically invalid or impractical suggestions, particularly outside training distributions. Approaches such as Retro‐Score perform full retrosynthetic analyses to evaluate synthesizability, accelerating lead optimization and reducing human bias [15, 16, 17].

While benchmark accuracies are high, multi‐step planning remains limited by error propagation and lack of interpretability [18]. To address these issues, recent models incorporate mechanistic filters, quantum descriptors, and brief human‐in‐the‐loop (HITL) validation. For example, the Self‐Corrected Transformer achieved 59.0% top‐1 accuracy on the USPTO‐50k benchmark while reducing invalid SMILES from 12.1% to 0.7% [19]. Grammar‐aware SMILES representations also improve syntactic validity and MaxFrag accuracy [20].

Hybrid frameworks that combine data‐driven prediction with symbolic reasoning, embedding chemical validity checks (valence, atom mapping, solvent/catalyst compatibility) [21], quantum‐augmented scoring (e.g., ml‐QM‐GNN) [22], and experience‐guided Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) [23] are emerging as the most reliable strategy for scalable retrosynthetic planning. Despite this progress, benchmarking still reveals persistent limitations in navigating broad chemical spaces for multi‐step synthesis, as exemplified by AiZynthFinder [24]. These predictive frameworks now underpin the next phase of automated synthesis, where LLMs and agentic systems extend AI reasoning beyond reaction‐level prediction.

LLMs and Agentic Frameworks for Synthesis Planning

Building on this conceptual foundation, LLMs extend chemical AI beyond reaction prediction toward reasoning, planning, and autonomous decision support. LLMs such as GPT‐4 and chemistry‐adapted variants are now explored as integrative components of synthesis planning and decision‐making, interpreting SMILES, extracting structured chemical knowledge, and proposing multi‐step strategies when connected to specialized toolchains. This paradigm shifts computational chemistry from static prediction to adaptive decision support, accelerating hypothesis refinement and route prioritization, while chemists retain control over mechanistic judgment and safety‐critical execution [25].

When strictly constrained by external validation tools and expert‐designed prompts, LLMs can serve as assistive interfaces to help draft candidate synthetic plans; however, their outputs are not inherently reliable and must be constrained by validation tools, mechanistic filters, and expert supervision. Rather than acting as independent reasoning engines, they rely entirely on integration with established predictive models and heuristic filters to prevent the generation of chemically implausible routes. This approach aligns closely with medicinal chemistry practice, requiring a balance between synthetic feasibility, absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) optimization, and structure‐based design under continuous data evolution. Structured validation pipelines, such as VALID‐Mol [26], markedly improve the fraction of chemically valid outputs (from ~3% to ~83%), highlighting the importance of combining generative LLM reasoning with expert‐guided filtering [4, 27].

Chemistry‐specific agents, such as ChemCrow [4] and community adaptations of Auto‐GPT [28], illustrate early implementations of this paradigm, serving as orchestration layers that combine LLM reasoning with domain‐specific tools. ChemCrow, for instance, can take a SMILES input, query chemical databases, propose retrosynthetic pathways, and refine suggestions based on synthetic feasibility, reagent availability, and cost‐efficiency [4]. These systems currently perform best in well‐defined problem spaces; robust performance in complex, high‐throughput campaigns remains under evaluation. Despite encouraging benchmark results, it is essential to distinguish between controlled computational evaluations and real‐world deployment scenarios, where reproducibility, experimental validation, and integration with laboratory workflows remain significant unresolved challenges.

Reflexive operation is a defining feature of agentic LLMs, allowing adaptation of synthetic plans in response to unexpected outcomes, resource constraints, or new computational analyses. This mirrors real‐world medicinal chemistry, where strategies evolve with ADMET readouts, scaffold challenges, and manufacturability barriers. Multi‐agent and domain‐tuned architectures extend this concept. For example, Coscientist, a GPT‐4‐driven multi‐agent system, autonomously proposes, plans, and executes complex experiments by integrating literature mining, code execution, and cloud‐lab interfacing [29]. Specialized chemical LLMs (ChemLLM, ChemDFM) achieve near GPT‐4‐level performance on synthesis planning, property prediction, and literature mining [30, 31]. ChemFM [32], a recent open‐source model trained on over 400 million molecules and 1.2 billion tokens, demonstrates robust generalization in low‐resource settings, outperforming established models such as ChemBERTa [37] and PubChemDeBERTa [38].

Despite encouraging benchmarks, practical deployment reveals recurrent limitations: hallucinations, edge‐case failures, stereochemical errors, and mechanistically implausible predictions [39, 40]. Robust uncertainty quantification, chemical validation, and prospective experimentation remain essential before LLM outputs can inform physical workflows. Training strategies and dataset engineering remain key performance levers. SMILES augmentation and improved decoding heuristics can match or exceed architectural upgrades [41]. Hybrid approaches that incorporate quantum‐mechanical or reaction‐mechanism priors further improve route quality, as exemplified by BatGPT‐Chem [33].

LLM‐assisted literature mining enables real‐time extraction of reaction conditions and experimental context. Prompt‐engineered GPT‐4 workflows such as ChemPrompt [34] extracted > 26,000 synthesis parameters from ~800 metal–organic frameworks with accuracies approaching 99%. ChemMiner [42] processed over 300 Suzuki reactions with F1 scores above 82%, greatly reducing manual curation. Tools such as ChemDataExtractor [35] extend this automation, populating machine learning (ML)‐ready datasets for synthesis planning and ADMET analysis.

In summary, LLM‐based reasoning establishes the cognitive interface between digital synthesis planning and robotic execution. Section 2.3 describes how these digital plans are operationalized through robotic automation, algorithmic optimization, and human–AI collaborative workflows. These capabilities establish the conceptual basis for translating AI‐driven planning into physical execution, as discussed in the following section.

Automated and Sustainable Synthesis Frameworks

This section describes how AI‐driven synthesis planning translates into physical execution through robotic infrastructures (Design–Make–Test–Analyze [DMTA] and SDL), algorithmic optimization strategies (Bayesian optimization [BO], active learning, MOO), human–AI collaborative workflows, and industrial case studies demonstrating quantifiable sustainability improvements.

Hardware Infrastructures and Closed‐Loop Execution (DMTA and SDL)

The robotic translation of LLM‐generated procedures into executable protocols represents a major emerging capability. Text procedures can be translated into machine‐readable languages such as extensible data language (XDL), validated for syntax and safety, and executed on automated platforms [43, 44]. This synergy of algorithmic reasoning and robotic precision enables data‐rich, feedback‐driven experimentation. However, its impact on production‐scale chemistry remains dependent on system integration and validation across diverse reaction classes.

In practical applications, AI‐driven DMTA pipelines have already demonstrated reported acceleration of hit‐to‐validation timelines in selected case studies. For example, Liu et al. screened ~7500 compounds using neural networks and identified Abaucin, a narrow‐spectrum antibacterial, achieving in vivo efficacy in under 60 days and reducing the hit‐to‐validation timeline by over 75% compared with conventional workflows [45]. This case exemplifies how predictive modeling prioritizes synthetically tractable chemistries before physical synthesis, minimizing redundant experimentation and reagent waste. In parallel, ML frameworks guide high‐throughput reaction screening across drug‐like fragments, with active learning and BO algorithms identifying optimal parameter regions efficiently (detailed algorithmic implementations are discussed in Section 2.3.2) [49]. Integration with AlphaFold predictions and Chemistry42 generative design enabled identification of cyclin‐dependent kinase 20 inhibitor scaffolds within 30 days using only seven compounds, supporting cost‐efficient and accelerated decision cycles [50]. These frameworks illustrate the emerging potential of algorithmic reasoning to guide physical experimentation. While currently limited to narrow, well‐characterized reaction classes, these proof‐of‐concept systems aim to eventually support miniaturized, resource‐efficient, and reproducible synthesis more broadly.

Representative robotic infrastructures such as AutoSyn, Chemputer, and the MIT ASKCOS platform exemplify the integration of route planning, flow chemistry, and autonomous execution within a single closed loop [51, 52, 53]. Microfluidic and flow‐based AI systems now perform real‐time reaction optimization through inline analytical monitoring integrated with model‐driven control architectures [54]. Techno‐economic analyses indicate that closed‐loop execution reduces time, labor, and material consumption by over 30%, supporting green‐by‐design manufacturing.

BO and Active Learning for Reaction Optimization

Building upon DMTA and SDL execution infrastructures, algorithmic frameworks refine reaction outcomes through iterative, data‐efficient experimentation. This section focuses on BO and active learning strategies that enable closed‐loop reaction optimization with minimal experimental overhead. The Cooper group's mobile robotic chemist exemplifies this approach: over 600 photocatalyst optimization experiments were conducted in 8 days, achieving a six‐fold activity improvement through BO‐guided parameter search [57, 59]. BO enables efficient exploration of high‐dimensional reaction spaces by focusing experimental resources on parameter regions offering maximum predicted improvement or model uncertainty reduction.

Traditional Design of Experiments methods often struggle with multi‐parameter optimization when reaction outcomes depend on non‐linear interactions between temperature, catalyst loading, solvent composition, and reaction time. Data‐driven frameworks address these challenges by constructing probabilistic surrogate models (typically Gaussian Processes [GPs]) that predict reaction outcomes and guide experimental design toward regions of high uncertainty or high predicted performance. These approaches have accelerated reactions such as C–N cross‐coupling [60] and low‐temperature Suzuki–Miyaura couplings [61].

Flow photochemistry platforms integrated with AI‐guided BO improve photon efficiency and convergence, reflecting sustainability potential. Structured reaction datasets such as the Open Reaction Database (ORD) [62] provide the data‐efficiency backbone supporting reproducible model development. Industrial platforms such as IBM RoboRXN [58] and AI4Green [63] exemplify the transition toward distributed, solvent‐efficient, and green‐by‐design synthesis optimization.

Within this framework, BO serves as a cornerstone for optimizing black‐box objectives, balancing yield, selectivity, and environmental impact [64]. GP surrogates quantify uncertainty, while acquisition functions enable MOO of reaction performance and sustainability [65]. Bayesian neural networks and multi‐fidelity extensions expand these methods to diverse reaction families, improving generalization across scales.

AI algorithms iteratively propose diverse candidate conditions, which are experimentally tested and refined [66, 67]. Platforms like Experimental Design via BO [8, 68] enable multi‐objective BO, balancing yield, selectivity, cost, and time. Closed‐loop high‐throughput experimentation (HTE) systems employing algorithms such as Phoenics [69] and Gryffin [70] have demonstrated robust performance across diverse reaction classes, extending seamlessly to medicinal chemistry screening arrays [71].

MOO for Green and Sustainable Retrosynthesis

While Section 2.3.2 focused on single‐reaction optimization, medicinal chemistry synthesis planning requires simultaneous optimization of multiple, often competing objectives: yield, cost, step count, environmental impact, and scalability. This section describes MOO frameworks that enable explicit trade‐off analysis and Pareto‐optimal route selection.

MOO frameworks identify synthetic routes that optimize trade‐offs across competing goals through algorithms such as genetic algorithms (GA), BO‐based approaches, and MORL [72]. Advanced strategies, including Thompson Sampling, Efficient Multi‐Objective Optimization and Phoenics, have improved sample efficiency and convergence in benchmark reactions such as Sonogashira couplings and Claisen–Schmidt condensations [73, 74, 75].

The Pareto front is central to MOO: it represents the set of solutions where improvement in one objective necessitates degradation in another. In retrosynthetic planning, each point on the Pareto front represents a chemically feasible route with distinct trade‐offs between cost, environmental impact, step count, and predicted yield. Metrics such as hypervolume and route diversity quantify overall performance, while interactive dashboards support dynamic weighting of trade‐offs, particularly for green chemistry objectives. In retrosynthesis, MOO enables explicit optimization of drug‐relevant criteria, including cost, yield, step count, environmental impact, and scalability [76, 77]. Interactive visual analytics support decision‐making, enabling chemists to explore Pareto‐optimal pathways and tailor trade‐offs to pharmaceutical constraints such as timeline, impurity control, and supply chain resilience. Techno‐economic analyses of continuous manufacturing processes confirm material and time savings relative to batch operations [78].

Among MOO algorithms, GAs, particularly Non‐dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA‐II) [79], along with BO‐based approaches and MORL [80], are widely applied. These methods simulate evolutionary processes (selection, crossover, mutation) to balance synthetic feasibility, cost, and sustainability in route selection. Open‐source frameworks such as DEAP and Platypus provide accessible implementations [81]. Recent uncertainty‐aware planners penalize pathways predicted to be synthetically infeasible, improving reliability under low‐data conditions [82]. MORL introduces adaptive, reward‐based learning that is especially effective for complex retrosynthetic graphs [80]. Agents iteratively interact with a simulated chemical environment, learning policies that favor high‐yield, short‐step, and low‐waste pathways while penalizing toxic reagents or harsh conditions [83, 84].

The synergy of NSGA‐II and MORL enables dynamic route optimization under evolving project priorities, minimizing late‐stage inefficiencies. In pharmaceutical contexts, these methods have optimized catalytic transformations critical to active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) synthesis, such as Suzuki–Miyaura and amide couplings, where maximizing conversion, stereoselectivity, and impurity control is essential.

Ensemble frameworks, including Chimera [85], enhance robustness by integrating models with complementary inductive biases, capturing underrepresented transformations common in medicinal chemistry scaffolds. Transformer‐based architectures combined with many‐objective optimization extend benefits into late‐stage design, generating molecules satisfying binding affinity, predicted ADMET, and synthetic feasibility [86]. Innovations such as Pareto Set Learning improve computational efficiency by decomposing complex MOO problems into solvable sub‐tasks [87].

Together, these advances demonstrate that yield‐only optimization is no longer sufficient. Route quality, scalability, and sustainability are now integral determinants of regulatory and commercial success. Sustainability objectives, including energy efficiency, solvent greenness, and catalyst recyclability, are embedded directly in MOO frameworks, aligning with SDL architectures for real‐time feedback and waste reduction [88]. Recent digital platforms such as AI4Green [63] and GreenRoute [89] employ MOO algorithms that balance yield, selectivity, and environmental impact, often incorporating one‐pot, telescoped, or continuous‐flow strategies to enhance overall resource efficiency. Benchmarking studies indicate that these systems can reduce the effective design space by approximately 25%–40% and accelerate identification of low‐waste pathways, although validation remains limited to well‐characterized reactions [90].

Critical Comparative Analysis of AI Methodologies

AI methodologies for chemical synthesis can be broadly categorized into retrosynthesis models, optimization algorithms, and emerging LLM‐based frameworks. While the rapid proliferation of these approaches has enabled significant advances, a comparative analysis reveals clear operational trade‐offs that determine their suitability across different stages of medicinal chemistry workflows.

Template‐based retrosynthesis methods remain the most reliable in industrial and late‐stage settings due to their high chemical validity, interpretability, and alignment with known reaction rules. However, their dependence on predefined transformations limits their ability to explore novel chemical space. As a result, these approaches are particularly suited for late‐stage optimization and process chemistry, but are less effective for early‐stage scaffold exploration and innovation. In contrast, template‐free models, typically based on transformer or GNN architectures, enable greater structural diversity and innovation by learning directly from data, but often suffer from reduced reliability, including chemically implausible predictions and sensitivity to training data bias [39, 91]. These limitations become especially pronounced in out‐of‐distribution scenarios, such as stereochemically complex molecules, natural products, or macrocycles.

Hybrid or semi‐template approaches attempt to balance these trade‐offs by combining data‐driven prediction with mechanistic constraints. These methods improve chemical validity relative to template‐free models while retaining greater flexibility than purely rule‐based systems, although they introduce additional computational complexity and may struggle with multi‐center transformations.

Recent LLM‐based systems extend retrosynthesis beyond prediction toward reasoning and workflow orchestration. While they enable integration of literature knowledge, reaction conditions, and multi‐step planning, their generalized architecture limits precision in reaction‐level predictions and introduces risks of hallucination and insufficient chemical grounding. As a result, they are currently best positioned as decision‐support tools rather than standalone synthesis planners [39, 40]. Their performance also remains dependent on external validation tools and structured pipelines to ensure chemical plausibility and experimental feasibility.

For reaction optimization, BO represents the most data‐efficient approach, particularly in low‐data experimental settings, enabling targeted exploration of high‐dimensional parameter spaces [8]. Active learning strategies further enhance this process by prioritizing the most informative experiments, improving convergence in data‐sparse regimes. In contrast, evolutionary algorithms and reinforcement learning offer advantages in multi‐objective and adaptive optimization but typically require higher computational or experimental cost and may exhibit reduced robustness in practical laboratory settings.

A key distinction also exists between LLM‐based systems and classical ML models such as GNNs and transformer‐based predictors. While classical models typically achieve higher predictive accuracy in well‐defined tasks such as reaction prediction and yield estimation, LLMs offer broader reasoning capabilities and cross‐task integration, albeit with reduced precision and higher uncertainty. This distinction reflects a fundamental trade‐off between task‐specific accuracy and general‐purpose reasoning: while classical models excel in well‐defined predictive tasks, LLM‐based systems provide flexible, context‐aware support across multiple stages of synthesis planning but require additional validation to ensure chemical correctness, particularly in experimentally constrained settings. Overall, no single methodology consistently outperforms others across all synthesis tasks, and each approach occupies a distinct operational niche defined by trade‐offs between interpretability, generalizability, data efficiency, and experimental reliability. In practice, robust workflows increasingly rely on integrated strategies that combine complementary methodologies with human expertise. From an expert perspective, a key limitation remains the fragmentation of current AI methodologies across prediction, optimization, and execution tasks, which are often treated independently rather than as components of unified synthesis workflows. Addressing this fragmentation represents a critical step toward translating AI‐driven synthesis into routine medicinal chemistry practice.

Human–AI Collaborative Optimization

The convergence of algorithmic optimization with human expertise through HITL frameworks represents a critical enabler for translating AI predictions into reliable experimental protocols, particularly in the context of the methodological trade‐offs outlined in Section 2.3.4. This section describes how substrate‐aware feedback, transfer learning, and interactive decision‐support tools enhance the robustness and interpretability of AI‐driven synthesis optimization.

Substrate‐aware feedback mechanisms within HITL loops enable early detection and correction of mispredicted conditions, thereby enhancing robustness in SAR interpretation and revealing critical developability constraints such as solubility limits, redox instability, or residual metal contamination [91, 92].

HITL approaches combine the sample efficiency of BO and active learning (Section 2.3.2) with chemists' mechanistic intuition, domain knowledge, and risk assessment capabilities. Interactive platforms allow chemists to modify algorithmic suggestions, flag safety concerns, or incorporate proprietary knowledge that is not captured in public datasets. This bidirectional feedback loop improves model calibration, reduces experimental failures, and maintains interpretability throughout the optimization cycle.

Industrial case studies highlight both opportunities and limitations of HITL integration. At AstraZeneca, the hybrid AI model SoBo, combining cheminformatics, density functional theory (DFT), and ML, has guided site‐selective arene borylation, a crucial late‐stage functionalization [93]. By predicting regioselectivity more accurately than human experts, SoBo has minimized protecting‐group manipulations and reagent excess, thereby reducing waste and improving energy efficiency. However, AI‐based route selection may sometimes prioritize theoretically low‐waste yet experimentally fragile conditions, emphasizing the need for HITL validation and iterative refinement to ensure reproducibility and robustness. For example, data‐driven optimization frameworks have been shown to favor reaction conditions that maximize predicted yield or minimize waste, but may rely on catalysts, solvents, or parameter combinations that are highly sensitive to experimental variability, catalyst instability, or scale‐up constraints [91]. These limitations illustrate the gap between computational optimality and practical robustness, reinforcing the importance of expert‐driven validation.

In summary, HITL frameworks ensure that AI‐driven reaction optimization remains grounded in experimental reality, chemical safety, and regulatory requirements, bridging the gap between algorithmic predictions and scalable manufacturing processes.

Industrial Case Studies in AI‐Guided Green Chemistry

The industrial deployment of AI‐driven sustainable synthesis strategies is exemplified by case studies demonstrating measurable improvements in environmental metrics, cost‐efficiency, and process robustness. Building on the algorithmic frameworks discussed in Sections 2.3.2–2.3.4, this section illustrates how AI‐driven sustainability translates into tangible process improvements at industrial scale.

AstraZeneca's AI‐guided late‐stage functionalization and photocatalytic platforms eliminated 3–5 synthetic steps per molecule while replacing palladium with cobalt or nickel catalysts, achieving 40%–60% waste reduction and improving E‐factors from 50–100 to 15–30. Miniaturized, automated reaction systems reduced solvent consumption by approximately 30%–50% and enabled more than 1000‐fold decreases in waste per exploratory reaction (https://www.astrazeneca.com/what-science-can-do/topics/sustainability/sustainable-drug-discovery-using-green-chemistry.html).

These achievements demonstrate that embedding sustainability metrics (e.g., step count, atom economy, solvent toxicity, life‐cycle impact) into AI reward functions can yield quantifiable environmental and cost benefits. However, large‐scale deployment under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions still demands stringent validation to ensure reproducibility, robustness, and regulatory compliance. These implementations illustrate how explainability and regulatory readiness, discussed in Section 4, become critical to scaling AI‐driven green chemistry under industrial constraints. Together, they signal a shift toward sustainability‐by‐design drug discovery, closing the loop between algorithmic intelligence and responsible manufacturing. This convergence of robotic infrastructures (Section 2.3.1), algorithmic optimization (Sections 2.3.2), human–AI collaboration (Section 2.3.4), and industrial validation collectively demonstrates that modern medicinal chemistry synthesis is transitioning toward fully integrated, feedback‐driven, and sustainability‐aware workflows. AI‐Accelerated Medicinal Chemistry Synthesis Workflows in the Design‐Make‐Test‐Learn (DMTL) Cycle

In recent successful discovery campaigns, the most significant efficiency gains have emerged from optimizing the “Make” phase within the DMTL cycle. In this context, AI‐driven synthesis planning and execution play an increasingly important role by prioritizing synthetically accessible compounds and enabling their rapid experimental realization through automated workflows. For example, in the development of INS018_055, the integration of generative design with strict synthetic feasibility filters enabled the team to synthesize fewer than 80 compounds to achieve a viable Phase II candidate. This represents a 92%–98% reduction compared to traditional campaigns, although such improvements are case‐specific and not yet generalizable across all medicinal chemistry programs, as supported by recent clinical and methodological reports [94, 95]. This drastic reduction in laboratory experimentation conserved reagents and solvents. It also translated into substantial synthesis‐related cost savings and a highly compressed 30‐month timeline. Importantly, these results were supported by experimental validation of AI‐prioritized compounds, demonstrating that computationally selected candidates could be successfully synthesized and tested within accelerated timelines. These results highlight that the primary impact of AI in these workflows arises from improved synthesis prioritization, reduced synthetic burden, and tighter integration between computational design and experimental execution.

Similarly, Exscientia's development of DSP‐1181 relied on a closed‐loop DMTL platform that paired multi‐parameter algorithmic scoring with robotic synthesis capabilities. By explicitly constraining the AI to prioritize synthetically accessible and automatable routes, the workflow required the physical synthesis of only 150–250 compounds, advancing the candidate to Phase I in approximately 12 months. This workflow therefore represents a fully integrated example of AI‐assisted synthesis in which retrosynthetic planning, compound prioritization, and experimental validation were tightly coupled within a closed‐loop system. While DSP‐1181 was ultimately discontinued, the campaign successfully validated the premise that predictive retrosynthesis and automated execution can effectively bypass traditional synthesis bottlenecks. These experiences underscore that AI's primary value in synthetic chemistry lies in preventing the physical execution of redundant or low‐probability routes, thereby maximizing the efficiency of automated laboratory infrastructure.

Collectively, these examples demonstrate that AI contributes to drug discovery not only through molecular design but, critically, through experimentally validated improvements in medicinal chemistry synthesis workflows, including reduced compound synthesis, accelerated iteration cycles, and more efficient resource utilization.

Autonomous Robotic Platforms and Experimental Acceleration

Beyond algorithmic design, robotic systems are increasingly closing the experimental loop. To reflect the diversity of current approaches, this section highlights complementary robotic architectures, including mobile platforms, modular flow systems, and closed‐loop self‐optimizing reactors. The University of Liverpool's mobile robotic chemist demonstrated autonomous decision‐making through AI control, Chemspeed synthesis, and on‐board liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry/NMR analytics [96]. These robots executed reaction optimization and scale‐up decisions 10–20 times faster than human chemists while maintaining reproducibility across urea and copper‐catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition reactions. Miniaturized reaction volumes (1–5 mL) and intelligent pre‐screening strategies minimized waste and solvent consumption. Although scalability and GMP compliance remain open challenges, this proof‐of‐concept highlights that decision logic (the AI‐driven interpretation of analytical data into synthetic choices) provides more value than hardware automation alone. Such modular and adaptable systems offer practical entry points for smaller organizations exploring robotic synthesis without a complete infrastructure overhaul.

To address the hardware limitations of traditional linear or iterative automated syntheses, Chatterjee et al. [97] developed a versatile approach utilizing continuous flow modules arranged radially around a central switching station. This fully automated radial instrument allows for sequential, non‐simultaneous reactions to be combined for multistep processes without requiring any manual reconfiguration between different synthetic steps. The capabilities of this central hub approach were extensively validated through the multistep synthesis of the anticonvulsant drug rufinamide, the preparation of two derivative libraries using varying reaction pathways, and the execution of metallaphotoredox carbon‐nitrogen cross‐couplings in a photochemical module [97].

Furthermore, recent advancements have targeted the specific challenges of optimizing, intensifying, and scaling photochemical processes in flow. Slattery et al. [98] introduced “RoboChem,” a benchtop robotic platform that integrates a liquid handler, syringe pumps, a tunable continuous‐flow photoreactor, and an in‐line benchtop NMR spectrometer. By employing a closed‐loop BO strategy, RoboChem systematically explores parametric spaces to autonomously identify conditions that maximize reaction yield, throughput, or a combination of both. The platform's high precision and reliability were demonstrated across 19 different molecules covering diverse facets of photocatalysis, consistently identifying optimal reaction conditions that matched or exceeded human‐optimized manual approaches while generating highly structured datasets of both positive and negative results [98].

Recent end‐to‐end autonomous platforms integrating LLMs with robotic execution further illustrate this trend toward fully automated synthesis development pipelines [99].

Collectively, these platforms illustrate that the integration of intelligent decision algorithms with adaptive hardware provides significant practical value by bridging the gap between digital retrosynthesis planning and physical, scalable execution, reflecting the transition of autonomous synthesis from proof‐of‐concept demonstrations to increasingly robust and generalizable experimental frameworks.

Failures, Data Gaps, and Lessons Learned for Medicinal Chemists

Not all AI‐designed molecules have succeeded. Programs from BenevolentAI, Recursion, and Exscientia have faced attrition at Phase II due to efficacy or pharmacokinetic liabilities unpredicted by AI models. Common causes include insufficient negative training data, limited representation of failed experiments, and over‐optimization for synthetic accessibility at the expense of novelty. Publication bias toward successful outcomes further skews datasets, underrepresenting toxicity, instability, and scale‐up challenges [100].

Despite notable advances, retrosynthesis algorithms still exhibit approximately 70%–75% single‐step accuracy, meaning roughly one in four AI‐proposed routes fails experimentally. Typical errors include reaction‐condition generalization, stereochemical mispredictions, and unrealistic precursor assumptions. Hybrid human–AI approaches, where chemists validate algorithmic suggestions, significantly improve success rates and maintain scalability [11, 101]. Ultimately, AI accelerates exploration but cannot substitute for mechanistic understanding or process‐chemistry expertise, particularly in complex or data‐sparse chemical spaces. Early involvement of manufacturing scientists remains essential to ensure scalability and regulatory compliance. From a critical perspective, current AI‐driven synthesis platforms remain highly effective in well‐defined chemical spaces but show limited robustness when extrapolated to novel scaffolds, complex stereochemical systems, or underrepresented reaction classes. This discrepancy highlights a persistent gap between algorithmic performance metrics and real‐world medicinal chemistry requirements, where failure modes, edge‐case behavior, and experimental uncertainty play a central role. Addressing this gap requires improved integration between computational prediction, experimental validation, and process‐level considerations. In our view, this also necessitates a cultural shift toward systematic reporting of failed experiments and negative data, which remain severely underrepresented in current training datasets and continue to limit model robustness.

Interpretable and Regulatory‐Ready Retrosynthesis

Continued progress in AI‐driven synthesis requires transparent benchmarking, sustainability‐aware objectives, and deeper integration with SDL infrastructures, supporting the emergence of sustainability‐by‐design medicinal chemistry ecosystems. Explainable AI (XAI) has become indispensable in AI‐guided retrosynthesis, ensuring that synthetic predictions are trustworthy, chemically interpretable, and experimentally auditable [102]. Clarity and interpretability are critical in medicinal chemistry, where synthetic choices affect project timelines, resource allocation, intellectual property strategy, safety, and manufacturing feasibility during preclinical and clinical development. Transparent architectures capable of justifying each disconnection strategy, through rule‐based systems, decision trees, or hybrid AI frameworks, are preferred over purely confidence‐based black‐box models, particularly due to the high cost of experimental failures during late‐stage scale‐up [103].

The Role of XAI in Medicinal Chemistry

Beyond optimization, interpretability and validation metrics ensure that AI‐guided retrosynthesis is transparent, auditable, and regulatory‐ready. However, rapid algorithmic progress has not always been matched by methodological rigor. Claims of generality require systematic benchmarking, ablation studies, and transparent dataset reporting to ensure true generalization rather than overfitting. Chemically meaningful validation metrics (yield, robustness, scalability) should complement conventional accuracy scores to reflect laboratory performance. Interpretability enhances synthetic risk assessment, identifies dataset biases, and supports regulatory traceability [104]. Because attention weights or raw confidence scores can be misleading when reported without mechanistic context [105, 106], best practice combines multiple explainability modalities that highlight plausible bond disconnections, leaving‐group behavior, and reagent compatibilities, while explicitly reporting uncertainty sources and data limitations.

In medicinal chemistry, this transparency bridges algorithmic predictions with chemical intuition, enabling experts to evaluate whether proposed routes are safe, scalable, and compliant with GMP. Transparent reasoning is also essential for knowledge transfer and reproducibility, particularly when models are used to support regulatory submissions.

Interpretable Retrosynthesis Models

Recent advances in AI‐assisted retrosynthesis can be broadly categorized into template‐based, semi‐template‐based, and template‐free approaches, each offering distinct advantages and limitations. Template‐based methods rely on predefined reaction rules derived from known transformations, providing high interpretability but limited generalization to novel chemistries. Template‐free approaches, often based on neural sequence‐to‐sequence or graph‐based models, offer greater flexibility and broader chemical coverage but may lack interpretability and mechanistic transparency. Hybrid and semi‐template‐based strategies attempt to balance these trade‐offs by integrating data‐driven learning with chemically meaningful constraints. Comprehensive recent reviews have highlighted how these methodological classes define the current landscape of retrosynthesis research and the key challenges associated with their practical deployment in medicinal chemistry [107].

Within this landscape, interpretable retrosynthesis frameworks aim to bridge predictive performance with mechanistic transparency, addressing key limitations of purely data‐driven approaches.

The methodological framework developed by Wang et al. [108, 109] illustrates a progressive evolution of mechanism‐informed retrosynthesis models. The group initially introduced MechRetro [109], a graph‐based architecture that integrates mechanistic reasoning with uncertainty estimation, achieving approximately 65% recovery of chemically plausible routes on curated datasets, although its validation remains limited for industrial‐scale applications. Building upon these foundational principles, the same group subsequently developed RetroExplainer [108], which represents a direct methodological extension of the MechRetro framework rather than an independent approach, by formulating retrosynthesis as a molecular assembly task using a multi‐sense, multi‐scale Graph Transformer combined with contrastive learning. This approach achieves an 86.9% single‐step literature match across 12 benchmarks, representing a significant methodological advancement. However, despite this improvement, its performance remains influenced by literature bias, which limits generalization to unexplored chemical spaces.

BioNavi, a hybrid neural and template‐based system, reached 75% success in biosynthetic pathway reproduction and supports interactive pathway exploration, although expert intervention remains necessary for resolving ambiguous steps [110]. Alternative approaches such as Graph2Edits [19] adopt graph‐editing strategies with different trade‐offs between interpretability and generalization. Graph2Edits, a semi‐template graph‐editing model, enables sequential fragment modifications with interpretable edits, though its mechanistic insight is limited by graph‐edit definition. Multi‐Modal Explainability and Evaluation Metrics

Current strategies for interpretable retrosynthesis emphasize that no single explanation layer sufficiently captures mechanistic causality. Methods such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [115] have been adapted in recent studies to provide atom‐ and descriptor‐level attributions in reaction and retrosynthesis prediction models. For example, Lu and Zhang applied SHAP [115] to a multitask T5Chem model to interpret sequence‐based reaction predictions at the functional group level, enabling identification of the chemical features driving model outputs across tasks including single‐step retrosynthesis [116]. Such approaches improve interpretability by linking model predictions to chemically meaningful substructures rather than relying solely on confidence scores, while graph‐specific explainers like GNNExplainer localize reactive substructures influencing predictions [117].

Symbolic rule extraction links AI disconnections to documented literature precedents, improving auditability and reproducibility [118]. Because explanations without uncertainty estimates can mislead experimental prioritization, best practice involves presenting mechanistic evidence, confidence intervals, and template support side‐by‐side, prioritizing routes with low execution risk.

Most learning‐based retrosynthesis tools still optimize for top‐k accuracy, which favors dataset performance over laboratory feasibility, creating an incentive misalignment. Key limitations include: (1) Lack of explicit condition prediction (solvents, catalysts, temperature) [108]; (2) Training biases toward common or historically favored reaction classes; and (3) Metrics such as top‐k accuracy failing to capture chemical feasibility [119]. For instance, a high‐accuracy disconnection may still require unscalable catalysts or unsafe solvents, or produce unstable intermediates. To mitigate this, recent initiatives introduce round‐trip verification [18, 120], safety and cost‐aware scoring, and route executability metrics (e.g., Syntheseus benchmarking) [121]. The RetroGFN framework, leveraging feasibility‐weighted GFlowNets, improves route diversity and execution confidence [114], thereby strengthening interpretability while maintaining balanced accuracy.

Toward Trustworthy, Auditable, and Regulatory‐Ready Retrosynthesis

Hybrid strategies combining data‐driven outputs with symbolic or physics‐informed validation are increasingly recognized as essential. For instance, DFT‐augmented filtering enhances assessment of intermediate stability and prevents the ranking of chemically implausible disconnections [122]. However, such workflows increase computational cost, and standardized validation protocols are still lacking. To support experimental adoption, models should rank pathways not only by computational score but also by predicted executability, providing explicit mechanistic rationales and transparent uncertainty estimates.

In summary, interpretable retrosynthesis must incorporate chemically grounded representations, hybrid predictive‐feasibility architectures, multi‐modal explainability, and decision metrics tied to experimental execution and safety. Authors should report chemist‐relevant performance metrics, validate key predictions experimentally, and document dataset biases and failure modes. Only models that demonstrate reliable performance in prospective, real‐world settings are likely to achieve broad adoption in the pharmaceutical industry. Ultimately, transparent retrosynthesis systems will underpin regulatory acceptance of AI‐enabled manufacturing routes by enabling auditability, quality‐by‐design principles, and compliance with emerging digital chemistry guidelines in drug development.

Beyond individual reaction optimization, AI is increasingly influencing how medicinal chemistry pipelines are designed by embedding sustainability metrics such as environmental, economic, and operational impact directly into synthetic route planning. For instance, random forest classifiers capable of estimating E‐factors have been validated industrially for forecasting waste generation and atom economy, providing actionable insights before experimental execution [123]. Together, these developments are transforming green chemistry from a post hoc evaluation into an active design parameter within AI‐assisted synthesis planning.

Toward Autonomous Green Chemistry and Circular Workflows

AI‐driven sustainability is progressing toward autonomous, circular synthesis through the fusion of predictive environmental screening, dynamic process control, and SDLs. Real‐time analytics and flow chemistry allow continuous adaptation of conditions to minimize energy and material waste.

Autonomous Green Chemistry

Aligned with circular economy principles, early‐stage computational tools are being explored to theoretically model waste‐stream compositions and suggest potential recyclability opportunities. While still in their infancy, integrating these models with life‐cycle assessment data may eventually enable the proactive identification of lower‐impact reagents, catalysts, and solvents. Broader applications within pharmaceutical research and development also demonstrate AI's potential in predictive maintenance, intelligent inventory management, and recycling strategies, ultimately enhancing resource efficiency and regulatory compliance [125].

Circular and Chemist‐in‐the‐Loop Frameworks

Critically, sustainable automation depends on chemist‐in‐the‐loop systems, which combine computational prediction with experimental intuition and evolving regulatory standards. Platforms such as AI4Green exemplify this integration, offering transparent solvent selection, energy optimization, and recovery strategies with full data auditability [63].

Embedding AI in sustainable synthesis therefore represents a strategic inflection point, moving beyond traditional metrics of yield and cost toward adaptive, transparent, and environmentally resilient chemistry [125].

Challenges and Limitations of AI in Synthesis

Data Quality and Availability

While AI tools have demonstrated significant promise in synthetic chemistry, their performance remains constrained by the quality, completeness, and representativeness of available data. Reaction datasets are often noisy, incomplete, and biased, reducing model robustness and generalizability [126]. Moreover, model collapse (the degradation that occurs when models train on their own generated data) can compromise novelty and diversity. Shumailov et al. [127] showed that generative LLMs and variational autoencoders gradually lose rare reaction types over successive training rounds, emphasizing the need for curated, diverse, and experimentally validated datasets. Improving data transparency and adopting open reaction standards (e.g., ORD) will be key to preventing generative collapse and ensuring model reproducibility.

Chemical Complexity and Model Limitations

Despite remarkable advances, AI models still face intrinsic difficulties in predicting stereochemical and mechanistic outcomes, particularly under multi‐step or flow conditions. Most ML models trained on coarse experimental descriptors lack quantum‐chemical precision at an affordable computational cost. Quantum machine learning (QML) offers conceptual promise, but current quantum hardware remains immature and unsuitable for routine synthetic planning.

Transformer‐based LLMs, while transformative in NLP, lack intrinsic chemical structure awareness. Decoder‐only models (e.g., GPT‐4, Gemini) are limited in their capacity to encode reaction context, whereas encoder‐based models (e.g., BERT, ChemBERTa [37]) help in reaction prediction but cannot fully capture multistep logic or three‐dimensional (3D) conformational nuances. Domain‐specific systems like ChemCrow [4], BatGPT‐Chem may yield syntactically valid yet chemically implausible SMILES, reinforcing their current role as assistive tools rather than autonomous chemists [128]. Multimodal models integrating text and visual data struggle with spectral interpretation and 3D reconstruction from NMR, limiting mechanistic analyses [129, 130].

Translational Challenges: Integration, Scalability, and Reproducibility

While robotic and autonomous synthesis platforms enable HTE and closed‐loop optimization, their large‐scale adoption remains constrained by multiple interconnected barriers that limit real‐world deployment and validation of AI‐driven synthesis workflows. In practice, these systems still operate within narrow chemical domains and require manual oversight for complex reaction classes [131]. Benchmarking indicates a persistent disconnect between digital retrosynthetic feasibility and multi‐step laboratory execution, with template‐free models often proposing implausible or infeasible pathways. Furthermore, the lack of standardized machine‐readable execution languages (e.g., XDL) and limited cross‐platform interoperability severely constrain automation scalability. Future progress will depend on the real‐time fusion of sensor feedback, adaptive learning, and robotic control, enabling the context‐aware decision‐making necessary for reliable autonomous synthesis.

Compounding these technical integration hurdles, economic and scalability constraints further limit the translation of these systems into routine pharmaceutical practice. Automated and continuous pharmaceutical manufacturing requires substantial upfront capital and integration costs, with the return on investment strongly dependent on production scale [132]. As Casas‐Orozco et al. [78] demonstrated, continuous manufacturing reduces per‐unit costs for high‐volume programs but offers only marginal advantages for small exploratory projects, limiting its adoption in early‐stage medicinal chemistry. Transitioning from small‐scale automated systems to industrial‐scale operations introduces further challenges regarding reaction yield, safety, and cost efficiency, which current AI–robotic platforms are not fully equipped to manage alone [99]. Hybrid digital–experimental models and modular flow systems could mitigate these limitations by enabling scalable, on‐demand synthesis.

Ultimately, overcoming these practical barriers requires establishing rigorous reproducibility and trust. DL models are highly sensitive to changes in data curation, software dependencies, and hardware, often producing non‐replicable results across laboratories. Their black‐box nature undermines interpretability, making XAI approaches essential. Addressing these challenges requires progress across multiple fronts: chemically informed model architectures, seamless integration with automated experimentation, and standardized interpretable frameworks. Embedding XAI layers and establishing community benchmarks are critical steps toward building reproducibility and trust, transforming AI from a purely predictive tool into a reliable, collaborative partner in molecular discovery. Ultimately, overcoming these challenges is essential to enable robust, scalable, and experimentally validated deployment of AI‐driven synthesis in real‐world pharmaceutical settings.

Critical Appraisal: Achievements, Proofs‐of‐Concept, and Future Expectations

Building on the comparative analysis and limitations discussed in previous sections, to critically assess the current landscape of AI‐driven synthesis, it is essential to distinguish between demonstrated industrial capabilities, academic proofs‐of‐concept, and future expectations, thereby enabling a structured interpretation of current progress and remaining limitations. At present, AI has achieved robust performance in well‐defined and data‐rich chemical domains. Predictive models for reaction outcomes, retrosynthesis planning for known scaffolds, and BO for reaction conditions are increasingly integrated into pharmaceutical workflows, where they contribute to measurable gains in efficiency and resource utilization. AI‐assisted retrosynthesis platforms and industrial route‐design systems have demonstrated practical utility in streamlining route design for known chemical scaffolds, particularly in industrial contexts. However, these successes remain context‐dependent and are often limited to constrained chemical spaces. Performance frequently degrades for highly novel, stereochemically complex, or out‐of‐distribution targets [39], where data scarcity and mechanistic uncertainty remain limiting factors.

In contrast, the integration of AI with automated experimental platforms, including SDLs [98] and robotic chemists [53], largely remains at the proof‐of‐concept stage. While recent studies demonstrate autonomous multi‐step synthesis and closed‐loop optimization, these systems are typically restricted to standardized reaction classes and require highly controlled environments, limiting their immediate applicability to broader medicinal chemistry practice. These platforms also rely on substantial hardware customization and are not yet capable of handling heterogeneous reactions or complex workup procedures common in exploratory synthesis.

Looking forward, the field increasingly aims toward fully autonomous, self‐optimizing discovery pipelines that integrate synthesis planning, experimental execution, and sustainability metrics. However, realizing this vision requires overcoming major challenges, including data standardization, robustness across out‐of‐distribution chemistry, and the incorporation of manufacturability, environmental constraints, and regulatory considerations into AI‐driven decision‐making. In particular, addressing the lack of standardized, machine‐readable experimental data and improving the capture of negative results remain critical bottlenecks.

Overall, a balanced perspective indicates that AI‐driven synthesis is best understood not as a replacement for chemists, but as an augmentative framework, where computational scalability and automation are guided by human expertise to ensure chemical validity, reproducibility, and practical relevance. From our perspective, the near‐term impact of AI in medicinal chemistry will not be defined by full automation, but by the effective integration of complementary AI methodologies within human‐guided workflows that prioritize chemical plausibility, reproducibility, and scalability.

Conclusion and Future Outlook

AI has contributed to measurable progress in reaction prediction, retrosynthesis, and laboratory automation, although its broader impact remains dependent on data quality, experimental validation, and integration with real‐world workflows. From an expert perspective, three major trends are currently shaping the field: the transition from isolated predictive models to integrated synthesis workflows, the emergence of LLM‐driven multi‐modal reasoning systems, and the increasing convergence of computational design with automated experimentation. Future progress will require combining deeper physical modeling with human–AI collaboration and improved understanding of chemical reactivity. QML holds conceptual promise [133], but routine application is limited by hardware constraints and signal noise. Classical hybrid strategies, combining ML with quantum‐chemical descriptors such as DFT‐augmented features, currently offer the best balance between accuracy and computational cost. In the short term, focusing on experimentally validated hybrid models is pragmatic, while QML matures alongside more robust hardware.

LLMs are being explored as interactive synthesis‐support tools, incorporating reaction conditions, spectral patterns, and mechanistic cues to assist experimental chemists. Systems such as ChemCrow [4] and Auto‐GPT derivatives integrate cheminformatics with literature mining to support retrosynthetic design. These models enhance creativity under uncertainty but remain collaborative aids rather than autonomous chemists. A realistic assessment of the field suggests that, while AI has demonstrated clear value in accelerating specific stages of synthesis and design, its broader impact is still constrained by data limitations, lack of standardization, and incomplete experimental validation across diverse chemical spaces. Future iterations may enable tighter integration with automated laboratories for closed‐loop experimentation, although such capabilities are still under active development and require further validation across diverse chemical domains.

Building community trust will require open benchmarks, standardized evaluation protocols, and transparent datasets. Initiatives such as Syntheseus [121] and SciHorizon [134], alongside ORD [62] and CASP‐style retrosynthesis competitions, help stress‐test AI across unexplored chemical spaces.

Operating in low‐data regimes via transfer learning, few‐shot modeling, and generative representations extends AI utility to rare targets, macrocycles, and natural products, which is critical for early‐stage drug discovery.

The convergence of AI, computational chemistry, and flow‐based experimentation is shifting synthesis from reactive troubleshooting to proactive, data‐driven exploration. Sustainability‐oriented tools such as AI4Green [63] embed environmental and operational metrics in route planning. Importantly, these innovations reinforce a model of AI‐augmented medicinal chemistry in which computational capabilities complement, rather than replace, human expertise.

As discussed in Section 6.4, distinguishing between current capabilities, proof‐of‐concept systems, and future expectations is essential for a realistic assessment of the field. To realize AI's full potential, three key enabling elements must be established: (1) Reproducibility through open science; (2) modular and transparent AI frameworks; and (3) strong feedback loops between computation and experiment. These elements collectively define a realistic pathway toward scalable and trustworthy AI‐driven synthesis. With these elements, AI has the potential to improve synthetic efficiency and sustainability, provided that its limitations are addressed through careful validation and integration with experimental workflows. Ultimately, the next frontier lies in the integration of AI‐driven reasoning with autonomous experimentation under sustainable constraints. Achieving this vision will require rigorous validation and effective integration with experimental workflows.
