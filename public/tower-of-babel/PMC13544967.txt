Hospitalization experience of ethno-racial older adults with dementia and caregivers: scoping review
Authors: Sadaf Murad-Kassam, Hannah O’Rourke, Kaitlyn Tate, Navjot Gill-Chawla, Jordana Salma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesDementia and hospitalization are complex, interwoven experiences, particularly for older adults from ethno-racial communities. Systemic racism, stigma, health care provider bias, and the lack of culturally and linguistically appropriate care impede access to equitable care while admitted to the hospital. This scoping review aims to describe the existing literature on the hospitalization experiences of ethno-racial older adults (age ≥ 60 years) with dementia and their caregivers and identify challenges in receiving care in hospitals.Research Design and MethodsA scoping review was conducted using JBI and Arksey and O’Malley’s 5-stage scoping review framework. Systematic searches were conducted across six databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Medline, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and Google Scholar. No restrictions based on publication year or geographical location. Data extracted using a structured data charting table.ResultsEleven studies were included in the review from an initial search of 3,395. Experiences and challenges in the journey of hospitalization pertained to (1) pre-hospitalization factors, (2) hospitalization and dementia care, (3) discharge and readmission, (4) challenges related to culturally tailored care practices, and (5) challenges encountered by caregivers.Discussion and ImplicationsThe review findings illustrate a persistent gap in providing culturally focused dementia care and addressing the needs of ethno-racial older adults and their caregivers during hospitalization, from admission to discharge and the transition back to home. Future studies are needed to explore how ethno-racial older adults living with dementia and their caregivers navigate the hospitalization journey and discharge process and to find ways to improve hospital care practices and policies.

Background and Objectives

Dementia and the needs of ethno-racial communities in health care settings are under-researched areas of inquiry (Botsford et al., 2011). Ethno-racial refers to a person or group categorized according to ethnic or racial characteristics and subjected to discrimination (Immigration Refugees and Citizenship Canada, 2022). Globally, the number of dementia diagnoses is projected to rise, particularly within ethno-racial communities living in high-income countries, including the United States, United Kingdom, and Canada (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2024; Arblaster, 2021; Chejor et al., 2022; Kiadarbandsari et al., 2024; Mukadam et al., 2011). Despite the growing prevalence of dementia among older adults from ethno-racial backgrounds, there remains a paucity of research examining their experiences of accessing health care systems, particularly within acute hospital settings (Co et al., 2021; Iroanyah et al., 2021).

Dementia is defined as a cluster of symptoms caused by disruption of brain function that can lead to irreversible loss of brain neurons and their function (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2022). Due to neuronal damage, an individual’s ability to perform activities of daily living may be affected, which may manifest as memory loss; difficulties with attention, language, and problem-solving; changes in mood and behavior; and disturbances in vision, balance, and movement (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2022). The five most common types of dementia are Alzheimer’s disease, vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia, frontotemporal dementia, and mixed dementia. Among these, Alzheimer’s disease is the most prevalent and is characterized by the abnormal accumulation of amyloid plaques and tau tangles in the brain (National Institute on Aging, 2022).

While age is the primary factor that increases the risk of dementia, it is not a normal part of aging (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2022). Due to aging, some memory challenges are expected, but dementia is a brain disease which often worsens with time, from mild to severe progression over a few months to years, and it requires diagnosis and treatment (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2022). Dementia diagnosis typically involves a comprehensive assessment, including a physical examination, medical and family history, blood tests, cognitive and neurological assessments, brain imaging, and, in some cases, cerebrospinal fluid analysis (CSF) (National Institute on Aging, 2022). However, due to delays in dementia diagnosis in community settings, most often older adults get diagnosed with dementia during their visit to the hospital due to any medical or surgical health issues. These delays are particularly pronounced among individuals from ethno-racial communities, who often get a dementia diagnosis in the hospital due to a lack of timely assessment and access to dementia care at the community level (Iroanyah et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2021).

For ethno-racial older adults with dementia and their caregivers, hospitalization can be a more intimidating event, as they face additional challenges such as unfamiliar environments, language barriers, cultural mismatches in care practices, nontraditional foods, and limited cultural competence among health care providers (Alzheimer Society of Europe, 2018; Greyson et al., 2025; Hermosura et al., 2020). Caregivers from ethno-racial communities often act as translators and cultural navigators to facilitate communication between health care providers (HCPs) and older adults via translating medical information into the individual’s first language, articulating culturally safe care preferences, and clarifying expectations around care delivery (Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025). Caregivers play a critical role in bridging cultural and linguistic gaps that may otherwise compromise the quality of care in hospitals, but this role can increase their physical and emotional stress (Abbott et al., 2022; Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2024; Chejor et al., 2022; Greyson et al., 2025). Moreover, hospitalization can lead to increased risks of physical decline and behavioral disturbances in individuals living with dementia, which may prolong hospital stays and further intensify the emotional, physical, and financial strain on caregivers (Abbott et al., 2022; Truswell et al., 2020).

Culturally focused dementia care in hospitals is not well understood or addressed in North American health care settings, and often, ethno-racial communities struggle to voice their needs for culturally focused care practices (Groleau, 2022). Task-oriented health care culture, lack of inclusion of cultural practices in hospital care, lack of translators, and shortage of well-trained dementia specialist frontline workers lead to burnout in ethno-racial older adults with dementia, their caregivers and their families as their individualized needs are not addressed (Alzheimer Society of Europe, 2018; Greyson et al., 2025; Hermosura et al., 2020; Naess & Moen, 2015). Pre-hospitalization factors such as lack of access to culturally focused dementia care, delay in dementia diagnosis, and financial challenges increase the risk of hospitalization in ethno-racial communities (Boltz et al., 2021; Co et al., 2021). Furthermore, a lack of involvement of individuals with dementia and their caregivers in the decision-making process increases their risk of prolonged hospitalization (Cox, 1996; Greyson et al., 2025).

The purpose of this scoping review was to describe (1) the existing literature on the hospitalization journey and care practice experiences of ethno-racial older adults with dementia and their caregivers and (2) identify research gaps in the current literature and challenges related to hospitalization and dementia in ethno-racial communities. A scoping review methodology was chosen to fit these descriptive aims and to provide the breadth and depth of the topic of inquiry and allow for the inclusion of a wide array of knowledge sources (Peters et al., 2024).

Research design and methods

Scoping review design

We used a scoping review methodology to develop a comprehensive description of the features of the available evidence and depict gaps in the literature on the hospitalization experience of ethno-racial older adults living with dementia and their caregivers. The five-stage Arksey and O’Malley scoping review framework (2005), with updated guidance by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) (Peters et al., 2024), was adopted. Arksey and O’Malley’s framework involves organizing a review in a five-stage process: (a) identifying the research question, (b) identifying relevant studies, (c) selecting eligible studies, (d) data charting, and (e) collecting the scoping review results. JBI’s refinements include the use of the Population Concept and Context (PCC) framework for developing research questions, developing a prior protocol before conducting a scoping review to reduce redundancy, and providing explicit guidance on data extraction, analysis, and presentation using figures, charts, and tables. Findings are presented in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist (Figure 1 and Supplementary file 1) (Tricco et al., 2018).

PRISMA diagram.

A scoping review was selected to identify the literature available on the hospitalization experience of ethno-racial older adults living with dementia and their caregivers and challenges reported in the literature related to the journey of hospitalization experience and to clarify what evidence exists on this issue of care. On the other hand, systematic reviews typically focus on narrow research questions (e.g., effectiveness, prevalence, risks, or experiences), assess the risk of bias in the included studies, and focus on the formal synthesis of the findings from the primary studies (Pollock et al., 2024). They are particularly valuable when aiming to change practice, inform recommendations, and support clinical guideline or policy development (Peters et al., 2024; Pollock et al., 2024). Scoping reviews are beneficial for exploring broad or under-researched topics and are well-suited for descriptive synthesis (Pollock et al., 2024). Given the high levels of stigma associated with dementia and hospitalization in ethno-racial communities (Chejor et al., 2022), a scoping review provides a valuable initial step in describing the features of the existing literature, identifying key areas of concern, and highlighting research gaps (Arksey & O’Malley, 2005; Pollock et al., 2024). We did not use any guiding framework in this review, as the aim of the study is to map the existing studies on the experience of hospitalization in ethno-racial communities with dementia. Using a framework may have narrowed the selection of studies and potentially excluded relevant evidence. Therefore, we adopted broad inclusion criteria and did not use a guiding framework.

Scoping reviews do not aim to synthesize findings from primary studies; therefore, quality appraisal is typically not required (Pollock et al., 2023, 2024). As such, we did not conduct a critical appraisal of the reviewed articles. Future systematic reviews may benefit from using appraisal tools such as the MMAT and GRADE-CERQual to assess study quality. Ethical approval was not necessary, as the review was based solely on publicly available published literature. The review has been registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF), which can be found on the OSF website at this link: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/2NUVT and a published protocol DOI: 10.1136/bmjopen-2024-096069.

Identifying research questions

The research questions used to guide this review were (1) What are the features of existing literature about the experiences of ethno-racial older adults living with dementia and their caregivers related to hospitalization care practices? (2) What are some key challenges in the hospitalization journey from pre-hospitalization, hospital care, discharge planning, and transition care identified in this body of literature that warrant further exploration?

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was developed in collaboration with an experienced health sciences librarian and the research team to identify relevant published studies. The Population Concept and Context (PCC) format was used to establish the research questions and a screening checklist, enhancing the transparency of the process (Appendix A, Supplementary). Systematic searches were conducted across six databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Medline, PsycINFO, Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), and Google Scholar. Additionally, the reference lists of all included studies were hand-searched for further potentially relevant studies. Databases were searched using a combination of natural language vocabulary (keywords) and controlled vocabulary with the Boolean operators AND and OR and medical subject headings (MeSH) whenever possible. The librarian (M.K.) and a researcher pilot tested and refined the search strategy using MeSH terms on the Medline and CINAHL databases. We then applied the search strategy to PubMed, Scopus, PsycINFO, ProQuest Dissertations and thesis, and Google Scholar. The initial search was conducted in July 2024, followed by a second search in May 2025, and to identify any new studies published in this area of inquiry. An updated search conducted on June 10, 2026, did not identify any additional studies directly related to the review topic.

Our search included published literature and grey literature (e.g., policies, thesis, reports, news, blogs). Search strategies are provided in Appendix B (see Supplementary file 2 database search). The keywords used for the electronic database and for the search of grey literature were included “ethnic minority,” “immigrant,” “refugee,” “racialized,” “ethno-racial,” “non-White,” “older adults,” “senior,” “aged,” “family caregivers,” “carers,” “dementia,” “dementia care and need of culturally sensitive care,” “acute care,” and “dementia” “Alzheimer’s,” “hospital,” and “acute care.” We also conducted a hand search of the reference lists of included studies to identify additional eligible studies not captured in the database searches. Authors of the included studies were not contacted to identify additional sources.

Selecting studies and charting data

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the review included peer-reviewed studies, policies, and grey literature published in English focusing on older adults (age 60 and above), as defined by the World Health Organization, from ethno-racial communities (population) and their caregivers, who are living with any stage of dementia or Alzheimer’s (concept). The research also had to examine hospital practices throughout the hospitalization process (context) (Appendix A, Supplementary). Studies with ethno-racial older adults living with dementia, caregivers and their hospitalization experience as part of a subanalysis were included. Studies on the experiences of caregivers involved in the hospitalization journey with persons living with dementia were also included. There were no restrictions based on the year of publication or geographical location.

Exclusion criteria

Studies or sources which addressed dementia care in general without explicitly focusing on ethno-racial older adults with dementia and their experiences in hospital settings were excluded. Studies on health care practices in outpatient settings, nursing homes, institutions, or community environments, as well as studies involving older adults with other medical conditions but without dementia, were also excluded. Additionally, studies examining the experiences of health care professionals providing care to ethno-racial persons with dementia were excluded, as this was beyond the review’s scope. Duplicate studies and those in non-English languages were excluded. Reasons for excluding full-text studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria were recorded (Prisma Figure 1).

Evidence screening and selection

The research team utilized Covidence, a web-based software for systematic reviews, to screen, select (using an inclusion-exclusion checklist), and extract data from studies that met the inclusion criteria using an inclusion-exclusion checklist (Covidence, 2025). We conducted, as primary investigator and research assistant (S.M.-K. and N.G.-C.), the initial screening of titles and abstracts independently to assess their relevance to the research question. We did this by pilot testing five titles and abstracts using our screening checklist (Appendix A, Supplementary). We held meetings before title/abstract screening, after screening the subset of 20 abstracts, and during the full-text screening to ensure a shared understanding of the eligibility criteria. We resolved disagreements through discussion with the senior researcher (J.S.), and a study was included if all team members agreed to it. We carefully maintained all references within the Zotero reference manager to ensure accuracy and accessibility.

Data charting

To ensure consistency among reviewers, detailed instructions for data charting were developed. These instructions outlined the specific data elements to be collected, indicated where these elements could be found within articles, and provided procedures for documenting them (Supplementary File 2). The guidelines included step-by-step directions for team members on how to identify and record relevant data in the data charting tables (O’Rourke et al., 2018; Peters et al., 2024). After conducting pilot testing by the lead researcher and the senior researcher (S.M.-K. and H.O.), amendments were made to the data charting instruction document.

Structured data charting tables were also created and tested on two primary research studies and on sources of grey literature by the lead and senior researchers (S.M.-K. and J.S.). The tables were subsequently shared with the research team to ensure accuracy, reduce redundancy, and identify any missing data (Munn et al., 2018). Any discrepancies that arose were resolved through discussions and consensus among team members.

Data extracted from all articles included the last name of the first author, year of publication, country of origin, source/study type (e.g., peer-reviewed articles, dissertations, reports, or news webpages), aim/purpose, and ethno-racial communities involved [Table 1 (Descriptive)]. For empirical quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods studies, the following additional information was documented: study design, theoretical framework, contextual details (i.e., setting based on descriptions of the recruitment sites (community or hospital), recruitment strategies, and information about the number of participants, sample size) [Table 1 (Descriptive)]. Demographic information for participants, including the age of older adults and their caregivers, gender, type of dementia, and severity of impairments, was extracted in a separate table to provide an overview of the study population (Table 2).

Information on the hospitalization experiences and challenges faced by older adults with dementia and their caregivers, found in the introduction, findings, discussion, or conclusion sections of the included studies, was read by the data extractors, who created short (1 to 2 sentence) summaries for inclusion in data extraction tables (Table 3). Data extractors were guided to identify gaps mentioned in the literature on hospitalization care practices, financial challenges, language difficulties, discharge planning, and the overall hospitalization journey. However, when we looked at data, we also found challenges related to insufficient support during the transition from hospital to home, and a lack of culturally focused care practices, limited understanding among health care providers regarding the needs of ethno-racial communities living with dementia and a lack of caregiver involvement in both the hospitalization and discharge processes. Therefore, we also added this information to our data extraction content.

Data analysis

Frequencies were calculated using basic descriptive statistics, including percentages, means, and standard deviations (SDs) (Pollock et al., 2023), to describe the demographic data of participants across studies. Qualitative data (i.e., the summary statements) related to challenges in hospital care practices experienced by older adults living with dementia and their caregivers from ethno-racial communities, as described in the extracted studies, were inductively analyzed using a basic qualitative data analysis approach via grouping the extracted recurring data into codes, subthemes, and themes (Peters et al., 2024).

First, the author conducted the initial analysis, which was then reviewed by the senior researcher (J.S.). After the senior researcher analyzed the findings, they were shared among all team members. Having two independent reviewers carry out the analysis enhanced the credibility of the findings. Any discrepancies related to the data analysis process, as well as the identified codes, subthemes, and themes, were resolved through discussion and review of the coded data among all team members until a consensus was reached. Amendments were made based on the discussions with all team members to ensure that the extracted data aligned with the research question and the scope of the review. A frequency table and flowchart diagram were created to highlight studies at different stages of the hospitalization journey, from pre-admission to the transition from hospital to home (Figures 2 and 3).

Results

Search results

We screened 3,395 titles and abstracts, out of which 339 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and nine studies met the inclusion criteria (see PRISMA Figure 1). Most studies were conducted in the United States (n = 6), with one study each from the United Kingdom, Australia, and Norway. Studies were published between 1996 and 2025. Four were quantitative (Boltz et al., 2021; Cox, 1996; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Hermosura et al., 2020), two were qualitative (Greyson et al., 2025; Naess & Moen, 2015), two were systematic reviews (Chejor et al., 2022; Co et al., 2021), and one used a mixed-methods approach (Pereira et al., 2022). From the systematic review studies, data related to hospitalization or acute service use among ethno-racial communities with dementia were extracted. All primary studies identified in these systematic reviews were independently screened using the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Through this process, four primary studies (n = 4) were identified. However, most focused on health outcomes, end-of-life care, and dementia diagnosis between ethnic minority and White older adults, or on health care providers’ knowledge gaps, rather than on hospitalization experiences of ethnic communities living with dementia in the hospital; therefore, they did not meet the inclusion criteria. One study (Cox, 1996) met the inclusion criteria and was already included as a primary study in this review. In addition to extracting relevant findings from the results sections, the discussion sections of the systematic reviews were also examined to capture a broader understanding of gaps related to care practice in hospitals toward older adults living with dementia from ethnic communities. We also reviewed two grey literature sources (n = 2), including one report from the Alzheimer’s Society of Canada and one news article from the Alzheimer’s Society of the United Kingdom (Rooke, 2023) [Table 1 (Descriptive)]. In total, 11 studies met the inclusion criteria, including nine (n = 9) peer-reviewed studies and two grey literature sources (n = 2).

Most studies employed a quantitative research approach, with only two studies adopting a qualitative approach; both focused on caregivers (Greyson et al., 2025; Naess & Moen, 2015). Out of all nine studies, only one mixed-methods study conducted direct interviews with both persons living with dementia from ethno-racial communities and their caregivers (Pereira et al., 2022). Two systematic reviews included data on older adults with dementia and their caregivers; however, these were exclusively based on quantitative data (Chejor et al., 2022; Co et al., 2021). Most quantitative studies relied on secondary data from hospital databases, with no active data collection to explore experiential dimensions of hospitalization.

Study population and ethno-racial composition

The ethno-racial communities most frequently studied included African Americans (n = 6), followed by Norwegian Pakistanis (n = 1), Native Hawaiians (n = 1), and Hispanic/Latinos (n = 1). The two systematic reviews, which aimed to examine the studies on experiences of older immigrants living with dementia and their care partners in accessing dementia and memory services in health care and community settings, encompassed a broad range of ethnic communities, including African Americans, Latinos, Asian Americans, British and American Caucasians, Black Caribbeans, East Asians, South Asians, Europeans, Iranians, Moroccans, Lebanese, and Turks (Chejor et al., 2022; Co et al., 2021). Across the published studies, older adult participants living with dementia were women, with sample sizes ranging from 32 to 131,649. The role of older adults living with dementia in research studies was predominantly as research participants (Boltz et al., 2021; Cox, 1996; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023), and these studies were quantitative in nature. None of these studies engaged older adults as active research partners, highlighting a continued reliance on participant-only roles rather than participatory or co-produced research approaches. The most commonly reported primary diagnosis was dementia, followed by Alzheimer’s disease, vascular dementia, and mixed dementia. Only a few studies specified that participants resided in urban areas with high ethno-racial diversity (Boltz et al., 2021; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Hermosura et al., 2020).

Similarly, caregivers from ethno-racial communities were predominantly women, with sample sizes ranging from 39 to 92 (Table 2). Older adults living with dementia and their caregivers were mainly involved as research participants, except in one study, in which family caregivers were involved as research partners (Boltz et al., 2021). However, their role as a research partner was not clearly defined in the research study.

Objective 1: Experiences of ethno-racial older adults living with dementia and their caregivers related to the hospitalization journey

The review highlighted the experiences, key barriers, and gaps in the hospitalization journey experienced by ethno-racial older adults living with dementia and their caregivers. Themes that emerged were (a) prehospitalization experiences, (b) hospitalization and dementia care, (c) lack of discharge planning, (d) transitional issues from hospital to home, and (e) caregivers’ challenges in navigating the hospitalization journey. These findings provided insights into research questions by featuring what is described in the existing literature about hospitalization experiences and identifying key barriers and gaps in the hospitalization journey that warrant further research studies.

Prehospitalization experience

Prehospitalization factors were explored in seven studies, such as limited access to community-based services and language barriers (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2024; Boltz et al., 2021; Chejor et al., 2022; Co et al., 2021; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Hermosura et al., 2020; Pereira et al., 2022). Reasons for hospitalization were identified in three studies (Boltz et al., 2021; Hermosura et al., 2020; Naess & Moen, 2015) (see Figures 2 and 3). Additionally, two studies compared health status at admission to hospital amongst ethno-racial and non-ethno-racial groups, focusing on associated risk factors for chronic diseases, functional scores, and mental health comorbidities (Boltz et al., 2021; Co et al., 2021). Pre-hospitalization barriers include systemic disparities toward ethno-racial communities, such as living in a disadvantaged neighborhood, lack of access to culturally focused dementia care, racism, and financial challenges were discussed in three studies (Boltz et al., 2021; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Pereira et al., 2022). Barriers such as limited access to outpatient services, discriminatory treatment toward individuals living with dementia and their caregivers in the community settings, financial challenges, immigration status, and the lack of culturally tailored services were discussed in five studies (Boltz et al., 2021; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Naess & Moen, 2015; Pereira et al., 2022; Rooke, 2023). Two out of eleven studies reported that caregivers felt gaps in accessing culturally focused care in community settings (Chejor et al., 2022; Greyson et al., 2025). Some studies also highlighted gaps in the integration of culturally and linguistically adapted dementia diagnostic tools for ethnic communities, contributing to delayed diagnoses that often remain undetected until individuals are admitted to acute care or emergency departments (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2024; Chejor et al., 2022).

Hospitalization and dementia care

All nine studies and two grey literature sources discussed the hospitalization experiences and challenges in receiving care (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2024; Boltz et al., 2021; Chejor et al., 2022; Co et al., 2021; Cox, 1996; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025; Hermosura et al., 2020; Naess & Moen, 2015; Pereira et al., 2022; Rooke, 2023). A few studies discussed the issue of admission to the emergency department due to health crises and hospitalization (Co et al., 2021; Pereira et al., 2022) and the barriers related to the high rate of hospitalization and longer hospital stays in ethno-racial older adults living with dementia as compared to Caucasian older adults (Boltz et al., 2021; Co et al., 2021; Cox, 1996; Pereira et al., 2022). One study compared hospital stays and the use of ambulances and emergency services among African Americans living with dementia and Caucasian older adults living with dementia (Pereira et al., 2022). Challenges related to physical and functional decline during hospitalization and the length of stay were highlighted in two studies (Boltz et al., 2021; Co et al., 2021). Additionally, issues related to the overuse of medication and negligence were depicted in three studies (Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025; Rooke, 2023). Furthermore, all studies reported challenges such as a lack of expertise in addressing the needs of individuals with dementia, experiences of discrimination in health care provider attitudes, and language discrimination (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2024; Boltz et al., 2021; Chejor et al., 2022; Co et al., 2021; Cox, 1996; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025; Hermosura et al., 2020; Naess & Moen, 2015; Pereira et al., 2022; Rooke, 2023).

Discharge and readmission

Five studies highlighted experience and challenges related to early and often inappropriate hospital discharges among ethno-racial older adults living with dementia (Boltz et al., 2021; Cox, 1996; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025; Hermosura et al., 2020) (Table 3 and Figure 3). Key barriers mentioned were racial discrimination in discharge planning and decision-making, early discharge, inadequate post-discharge support, and limited access to culturally oriented community-based resources (Cox, 1996; Greyson et al., 2025). A case study shared by the Alzheimer’s Society of the UK (Rooke, 2023) described caregivers’ experiences of their loved ones being discharged from the hospital with hospitalization-related complications. Additionally, Cox (1996) identified a lack of formal and informal care services available to support patients in their homes and communities after discharge. One study (Hermosura et al., 2020) discussed the challenges related to early hospital discharge faced by Native Hawaiians and Pacific Islanders with Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD). It noted that Native Hawaiians had a length of stay in the hospital that was one day shorter compared to non-Hispanic White older adults with ADRD. Additionally, readmission to the hospital within 30 days of discharge was more prevalent among Native Hawaiians than non-Hispanic White older adults with ADRD (Hermosura et al., 2020).

On the other hand, another study indicated that Black older adults with dementia who lived in disadvantaged neighborhoods had a higher rate of hospitalization (Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023). Their average length of stay during hospitalization was one day longer compared to non-Hispanic White older adults (Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023). Both studies noted that the rate of readmission within 30 days of discharge is higher in ethno-racial communities compared to Caucasian older adults with dementia (Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Hermosura et al., 2020). Four studies mentioned the cultural and religious expectations within ethno-racial communities to care for older adults at home, as well as the negative social perceptions associated with nursing homes and the cost associated to it which often led families to discharge older adults from hospitals to home and assume caregiving responsibilities at home (Cox, 1996; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Hermosura et al., 2020; Naess & Moen, 2015).

Objective 2: Challenges related to hospitalization and dementia in ethno-racial communities

Challenges related to culturally focused care practices

A major challenge reported in the studies was the lack of culturally focused dementia care in hospital practices. Eight studies reported that the caregivers felt that health care providers were not providing culturally focused dementia care to older adults living with dementia from ethno-racial communities when admitted to hospitals (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2024; Boltz et al., 2021; Chejor et al., 2022; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025; Hermosura et al., 2020; Naess & Moen, 2015; Rooke, 2023) (Table 3). Contributing factors that were explored included poor routine care, absence of culturally adapted resources, language barriers, and limited person-centered care approaches in hospitals (Boltz et al., 2021; Chejor et al., 2022; Greyson et al., 2025; Hermosura et al., 2020). Other studies reported experiences related to a lack of cultural foods in hospitals (Greyson et al., 2025), the minimal cultural competence training of health care providers (Greyson et al., 2025; Hermosura et al., 2020; Naess & Moen, 2015), and racial discrimination (Greyson et al., 2025; Naess & Moen, 2015).

Challenges related to discharge process encountered by caregivers

A total of nine studies identified the challenges encountered by caregivers in the hospitalization and discharge process (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2024; Chejor et al., 2022; Cox, 1996; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025; Hermosura et al., 2020; Naess & Moen, 2015; Pereira et al., 2022; Rooke, 2023) (see Table 3). Most caregivers across the studies were women (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2024; Boltz et al., 2021; Chejor et al., 2022; Co et al., 2021; Cox, 1996; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025; Hermosura et al., 2020; Naess & Moen, 2015; Pereira et al., 2022; Rooke, 2023). Caregivers reported financial, informational, and emotional challenges after hospital discharge (Cox, 1996; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025) (Tables 2 and 3). Caregiving stressors were described in the context of negative caregiver-health care provider interactions (Chejor et al., 2022; Cox, 1996; Greyson et al., 2025; Hermosura et al., 2020; Naess & Moen, 2015), exclusion of caregivers from discharge planning (Chejor et al., 2022; Cox, 1996; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025), and lack of access to caregiving supports in the community setting (Chejor et al., 2022; Greyson et al., 2025; Hermosura et al., 2020). One study compared caregiving stress between ethno-racial and White communities (Pereira et al., 2022).

Three studies specifically highlighted the challenges encountered by caregivers when transitioning their loved ones with dementia from the hospital to home and the impacts on caregivers’ well-being (Cox, 1996; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025). Challenges in care mentioned were the complexities of medication management, accessing culturally appropriate community care services, and the availability of linguistically accessible health care resources (Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025). Two studies (Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025) discussed caregivers’ concerns regarding their roles and responsibilities in post-hospital care for individuals with dementia (Greyson et al., 2025; Rooke, 2023). Barriers related to limited guidance from health care professionals in understanding their caregiving role, as well as challenges in accessing long-term supports and navigating post-hospital care needs for their family members living with dementia, were also mentioned in a few studies (Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025; Rooke, 2023).

Discussion

This scoping review aims to highlight from existing literature the types of experiences of ethno-racial older adults and their caregivers when receiving care in hospitals and to identify the possible range of challenges and barriers in hospital care practices. The reviewed literature revealed that gaps extend well beyond the hospital setting and start from accessing care in community settings, which often lead to health care crises and become a reason for hospitalization and navigating care post-discharge from the hospital (Boltz et al., 2021; Cox, 1996; Greyson et al., 2025; Pereira et al., 2022), which is also an issue mentioned in broader policy and advocacy literature (Alzheimer’s Disease International, 2022; Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, 2025). Several studies noted that experiences of racial discrimination acted as a precursor to premature discharge and increased risk of rehospitalization within 30 days among ethno-racial communities (Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025; Hermosura et al., 2020). Despite this, none of the studies explored a whole-journey perspective that examined how ethno-racial older adults living with dementia navigate care from the community through hospitalization and into post-discharge contexts.

Studies have reported that older adults and caregivers are dissatisfied with hospital services, describing care practices as fragmented, culturally insensitive, and unresponsive to their needs (Boltz et al., 2021; Chejor et al., 2022; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Naess & Moen, 2015). Cultural insensitivity in health care practice has been frequently highlighted in recent studies, particularly regarding dementia care and access to culturally responsive care (Lin et al., 2021; Quiñones-Cordero et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026). Caregivers often feel unsupported and excluded from decision-making processes by health care providers (Chejor et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2026). In addition, the lack of health care providers who speak different languages and discriminatory attitudes toward ethno-racial communities contribute to heightened stress among caregivers, who must not only support the needs of their family members but also advocate for their family member with dementia within the health care system (Lin et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2008; Quiñones-Cordero et al., 2025). Moreover, systemic racism and discrimination were consistently reported in studies, leading to inequities in access to dementia services and resources based on caregivers’ ethno-racial identity (Boltz et al., 2021; Chejor et al., 2022; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Hermosura et al., 2020; Pereira et al., 2022).

None of the studies included in this review reported the integration of culturally focused dementia care practices into hospital care for ethno-racial older adults living with dementia. The concept of cultural competence has been widely discussed in dementia care literature as an approach to improving inclusivity and responsiveness to the needs of diverse populations (Clark et al., 2018; Mayeda et al., 2016). Cultural competence has frequently been promoted as a strategy that health care providers can adopt to address the needs of ethno-racial and Indigenous communities. However, recent scholarship has criticized cultural competence for promoting a checklist-based understanding of culture, whereby culture is treated as a fixed rather than dynamic and evolving construct. Such approaches may position health care providers as cultural experts, reinforcing power imbalances between providers, patients, and caregivers (Ezell, 2024; Lekas et al., 2020).

The culture competency approach is unable to address the broader social, structural, and institutional factors that shape health experiences, including racism, structural inequalities, and power imbalances. Recent studies advocated for the use of cultural humility or a culturally focused dementia care approach (Du Toit et al., 2023; Gaviola et al., 2024; Lekas et al., 2020), where individuals from ethno-racial communities feel empowered to share what their specific needs are based on their ethno-racial identity.

Several studies in this review reported that health care providers often held assumptions about the caregiving practices and support needs of ethno-racial communities. For example, health care providers frequently assumed that older adults would return home rather than transition to institutional care following discharge and that family caregivers would assume primary responsibility for ongoing care (Chejor et al., 2022; Cox, 1996; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025). As a result, caregivers often felt overlooked and inadequately supported, as they were not routinely offered services, resources, or care planning tailored to their individual needs and circumstances (Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025). Therefore, using cultural competence approaches may unintentionally perpetuate stereotypes toward ethno-racial communities, resulting in standardized approaches to care that overlook within-group diversity and contribute to ongoing health inequities (Du Toit et al., 2023; Gaviola et al., 2024; Lekas et al., 2020).

Future efforts to improve dementia care in hospital settings should move beyond cultural competence toward cultural humility and culturally responsive care. Cultural humility and culturally focused care encourage health care providers to actively involve patients and their caregivers in the care process to better understand their unique cultural values, preferences, and needs. This collaborative approach fosters more equitable and balanced relationships between health care providers, patients, and families, thereby promoting culturally responsive dementia care (Du Toit et al., 2023; Gaviola et al., 2024; Lekas et al., 2020; Quiñones-Cordero et al., 2025).

Most studies in this review were conducted in urban settings; however, none of the studies examined the experiences of ethno-racial communities living in rural areas and the experience of hospitalization in rural settings, where access to dementia care services, hospital care practices, and resources may be even more constrained (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, 2025; Canadian Institute for Health Information, 2024).

Another critical challenge identified across the literature was the limited engagement of older adults and their caregivers as research partners. Engaging individuals with dementia and their caregivers as active research partners can enhance the relevance and cultural responsiveness of studies, ensure that findings reflect real-world experiences, and inform more equitable policies and care practices (Dabiri et al., 2024). Ethno-racial communities are often underrepresented in dementia research due to systemic mistrust, cultural stigma, and a lack of inclusive research practices (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2024; Chejor et al., 2022; Groleau, 2022). Active involvement of these communities in research, not merely as participants but as co-creators, can help address these barriers by fostering trust, improving representation, and generating culturally oriented health care resources and interventions (Brijnath et al., 2022; Marchant et al., 2025).

Across the included studies, the use of theoretical frameworks that explicitly address racism, systemic discrimination, and broader social and financial inequities was notably limited. Only one study employed the theory of “fundamental causes” to situate its findings within analyses of structural power, social justice, and institutional inequality (Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023). The remaining studies did not adopt a guiding theoretical framework to inform their research outcomes. The intersection of cultural identity, immigration status, and financial circumstances consistently emerged as a critical factor shaping hospitalization experiences and care outcomes for ethno-racial older adults living with dementia (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2024; Babulal et al., 2019). However, these intersecting influences were predominantly reported descriptively rather than integrated into a theoretical lens that could illuminate structural and systemic determinants of inequity in dementia care.

Future research would benefit from applying an intersectional framework alongside aging theories, particularly Life Course or Quality of Life theory, to better understand how experiences accumulated across the lifespan influence dementia risk, quality of life, and access to care. Using aging framework with an intersectional lens can provide insights into how racism, migration experiences, socioeconomic disadvantages, and intersecting identities of ethno-racial communities negatively affect their quality of life, shape their experience of hospitalization and with health care providers, and heighten their risk of inequalities in accessing dementia care and resources (Jonker et al., 2004; Victor et al., 2024). Such an approach would also help elucidate how structural and social determinants of health create barriers to accessing timely dementia diagnosis, navigating hospitalization journey, and culturally responsive dementia support for ethno-racial older adults living with dementia and their caregivers (Victor et al., 2024).

Most of the studies which involve older adults as research participants were quantitative in nature, which again restrains older adults from sharing their lived experience related to hospitalization and navigating care, which is a gap mostly documented in the literature (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2024; Arblaster, 2021; Brijnath et al., 2022). This absence of lived experience limits understanding of the specific changes required to make hospital care practices more inclusive and responsive to the needs of diverse ethnic communities living with dementia.

Recommendations for future research

There is a pressing need to do further empirical research and policy analysis to explore the experience of hospitalization encountered by ethno-racial communities and understand their dementia care needs. Most of the studies primarily included caregivers as participants, with limited representation of older adults with dementia themselves in narrative research. This creates critical gaps in understanding the specific needs of older adults, whose experiences are often excluded in creating acute hospital care policies. Future research studies need to find ways to engage older adults with dementia from diverse ethnic backgrounds to share their stories and experiences related to hospitalization and the need for culturally focused care practices.

Additionally, a recurring challenge discussed across studies was the limited cultural sensitivity among health care providers, with several studies highlighting insufficient training in delivering culturally focused dementia care (Alzheimer Society of Canada, 2024; Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Greyson et al., 2025; Naess & Moen, 2015). However, none of the studies mentioned what type of training might be effective in preparing health care providers to provide culturally focused care in hospital care settings to individuals with dementia from ethno-racial communities. Therefore, future studies are needed to explore which training programs should be implemented to equip health care workers with the skills to understand and deliver culturally focused dementia care in hospital settings. Additionally, the systematic factors influencing the delivery of these training programs, such as health care infrastructure, staffing issues, health care budgets, funding models, and policy contexts, require further investigation.

Studies reported that caregivers felt isolated in providing support and care to their family member admitted into the hospital, and navigating discharge care was a significant challenge (Cox, 1996; Greyson et al., 2025). None of the studies discussed the presence of transitional care support programs that are specifically tailored for ethno-racial communities during and after hospitalization. This represents a critical gap that requires further exploration in both research and practice. Future studies should investigate developing and implementing transition care programs and dementia resources that are culturally oriented to address the needs of ethno-racial communities living with dementia.

Strengths and limitations

Our scoping review identified hospitalization experiences of ethno-racial older adults living with dementia and their caregivers, which is a mostly unaddressed area of research in the ethno-racial context. This review employed a rigorous and comprehensive search strategy across multiple databases, encompassing both peer-reviewed and grey literature, to ensure a broad scope of relevant evidence. We conducted a comprehensive search, performed transparent, structured data extraction, and provided in-depth descriptions and syntheses of the data to explore gaps in the existing literature. The findings depicted the literature on the experiences of hospitalization among ethno-racial older adults and their caregivers living with dementia.

A few limitations of this review are that only English-language studies were included, which excluded relevant research in other languages, particularly from non-English-speaking ethno-racial communities. We included studies where participants were 60 years or older, which may have excluded research on individuals with early-onset dementia or studies that defined “older adults” differently, for example, some communities consider age 50 and above as older adulthood. Additionally, we did not assess study quality, as the aim was to map and identify research gaps rather than synthesize outcomes; therefore, the reliability of data extracted from selected studies cannot be fully appraised. The narrative data extracted reflect a brief summary of what was present or absent in the article regarding challenges/barriers, and helped identify a broad sense of concepts/areas explored in the literature, appropriate for a scoping review. The importance, weight, or quality of these challenges/barriers within each study cannot be assessed in this scoping review; this is an area to address in future systematic reviews.

Conclusion

The review highlights substantial gaps and challenges encountered by caregivers and older adults during their hospitalization journey, from admission to discharge and the transition back to home. Systemic issues, including racial disparities, language barriers, limited culturally oriented resources, and the absence of inclusive care practices, contribute to poorer health outcomes, delayed care-seeking, and increased caregivers’ burden. Findings also showed that barriers to dementia diagnosis and a lack of culturally oriented resources in community settings place ethno-racial older adults at greater risk of hospitalization.

Within hospitals, health care providers often fail to deliver culturally focused dementia care, which places additional stress and responsibility on caregivers (Greyson et al., 2025; Hermosura et al., 2020). The World Health Organization’s global dementia policy advocates tailoring health care practices to patients’ cultural contexts to enhance inclusivity in dementia care on a broader scale (World Health Organization, 2017). There remains a persistent gap in recognizing and addressing the needs of ethno-racial communities living with dementia within health care settings, including hospitals, which often serve as an entry point for this population (Balls-Berry & Babulal, 2022; Bowes & Wilkinson, 2003; Chejor et al., 2022; Pereira et al., 2022). This review also identified a lack of representation of ethno-racial older adults with dementia and their caregivers as research partners. Engaging these groups meaningfully in research design and implementation is essential to co-create care models, practices, and policies that reflect their lived realities (Marchant et al., 2025). Active involvement of these communities in research can also reduce stigma, improve trust in health care systems, and increase the cultural relevance of care.

As the global population of older adults with dementia becomes increasingly diverse, there is an urgent need for health care systems and researchers to prioritize hospital care practices and address the needs of older adults and their caregivers living with dementia. Hospitalization is often unavoidable during a health crisis; however, improving the hospital experience and providing early support, including clear discharge planning, culturally focused care, and meaningful dementia resources from the start of hospitalization, can benefit not only older adults and their caregivers but also health care providers. Addressing these gaps will not only improve hospitalization outcomes but also support the development of more equitable dementia care policies and practices for ethno-racial communities.
