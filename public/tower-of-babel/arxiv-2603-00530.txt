Bridge Matching Sampler: Scalable Sampling via Generalized Fixed-Point Diffusion Matching
Denis Blessing, Lorenz Richter, Julius Berner, Egor Malitskiy, Gerhard Neumann

Abstract
Sampling from unnormalized densities using diffusion models has emerged as a powerful paradigm. However, while recent approaches that use least-squares ‘matching’ objectives have improved scalability, they often necessitate significant trade-offs, such as restricting prior distributions or relying on unstable optimization schemes. By generalizing these methods as special forms of fixed-point iterations rooted in Nelson’s relation, we develop a new method that addresses these limitations, called Bridge Matching Sampler (BMS). Our approach enables learning a stochastic transport map between arbitrary prior and target distributions with a single, scalable, and stable objective. Furthermore, we introduce a damped variant of this iteration that incorporates a regularization term to mitigate mode collapse and further stabilize training. Empirically, we demonstrate that our method enables sampling at unprecedented scales while preserving mode diversity, achieving state-of-the-art results on complex synthetic densities and high-dimensional molecular benchmarks.

Definition 2.1

(Reciprocal class and reciprocal projection)
.

A path measure
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}
is in the
reciprocal class

\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{P})
of the reference process
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{M}
if
\Pi=\Pi_{0,T}\mathbb{P}_{|0,T}
. We define the
reciprocal projection
of
\Pi

\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{P})
as

\mathrm{proj}_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{P})}(\Pi)\coloneqq\Pi_{0,T}\mathbb{P}_{|0,T}.

(6)

It is important to note that, for suitable reference measures
\mathbbm{P}
, the conditioned process
\mathbbm{P}_{|0,T}
can be simulated efficiently. For instance, when
\mathbbm{P}
corresponds to (scaled) Brownian motion, the conditioned measure

is the classical

\xi

5

X_{t}\sim\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}
can be sampled directly, without integrating full trajectories. This eliminates the need to store entire paths in memory and leads to substantial computational savings, especially in high-dimensional settings.

In our general framework, we consider a (possibly non-Markovian) target path measure
\Pi^{*}
subject to the boundary constraints

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{prior}}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Pi^{*}_{0,T}(x_{0},x_{T})\,\mathrm{d}x_{T},\quad\mathrm{and}

(7)

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{target}}(x_{T})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Pi^{*}_{0,T}(x_{0},x_{T})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}.

(8)

Together with the bridge process introduced above, these constraints define a target path measure

\Pi^{*}=\Pi^{*}_{0,T}\mathbbm{P}_{|0,T}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbbm{P}),

(9)

which can be represented as the law of the stochastic process

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sigma(t)\xi(X,t)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t)\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad X_{0}\sim p_{\mathrm{prior}}.

(10)

Our objective is to approximate

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sigma(t)u^{*}(X_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t)\mathrm{d}B_{t},

2.2
Schrödinger half bridges

Schrödinger half bridges
(SHBs) solve problems of the form

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}^{u}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\|u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\ \text{s.t.}\ X_{T}\sim\Pi^{*}_{T}=p_{\mathrm{target}},

i.e., they are characterized by a kinetic optimal drift given a marginal constraint
3
3

3

One can also consider constraining the marginal at time
t=0
.
. Using that

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbbm{P}^{u}|{\mathbbm{P}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}^{u}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\|u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right],

(18)

and by the chain rule of the KL divergence
it is straightforward to see that the minimum is attained when the conditional measures match,
\mathbbm{P}^{u}_{|T}=\mathbbm{P}_{|T}
. Consequently, the optimal joint coupling is given by
\Pi^{*}_{0,T}=\Pi^{*}_{T}\mathbbm{P}_{0|T}
. However, this implies that the initial marginal is
\Pi^{*}_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}_{T}}[\mathbbm{P}_{0|T}]
, which generally depends on the target
\Pi^{*}_{T}
and differs from the reference prior
\mathbbm{P}_{0}
. To ensure that the optimal model actually starts at the prior (i.e.,
\Pi^{*}_{0}=p_{\mathrm{prior}}
), the reference must satisfy the independence condition
\mathbbm{P}_{0|T}=\mathbbm{P}_{0}
, a property commonly known as
memorylessness

(
16
)
.
For instance, this can be enforced by using a deterministic initial condition
X_{0}=x_{0}
, i.e.
p_{\mathrm{prior}}=\delta_{x_{0}}
. Using the Brownian motion reference process and
x_{0}=0
– a setting that is considered frequently in the literature – the generalized TSI in
Section

2.1
then becomes

\nabla\log\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}_{T|t}}\bigg[\frac{1}{\gamma(t)}\nabla_{X_{T}}\log\Pi^{*}_{T}(X_{T})|X_{t}=x\bigg].

Combining this with the general results for
v^{*}
in
Section

2.1
yields the following result, see
Section

B.3
for the proof.

Proposition 2.7

(Path-dependent drift for Schrödinger half bridges)
.

Under the assumptions of
Section

2.1
,
the path-dependent drift
\xi
corresponding to the non-Markovian SDE (
10
) that induces the target measure
\Pi^{*}=\Pi_{T}^{*}\mathbbm{P}_{|T}
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma(t)^{-1}\xi(X,t)=\nabla_{X_{T}}\log\frac{\Pi^{*}_{T}(X_{T})}{\mathbbm{P}_{T}(X_{T})}.

(19)

We note that the result in
Section

2.2
, together with the associated fixed-point iteration, coincides with the
adjoint sampling
(AS) method introduced by
30

However, as mentioned above, the memorylessness assumptions severely restricts the choice of the reference process and the prior distribution and, in practice, these choices lead to instabilities due to large diffusion coefficients
\sigma
. In particular, for the Dirac delta prior, we need a sufficiently large coefficient to guarantee initial exploration of the sampler. For a Gaussian prior, one requires sufficiently large diffusion coefficients such that the distribution of the time-reversed process approximates the prior at finite time
T
. In the following, we present a method that allows for the use of arbitrary prior distributions and reference processes, thereby generalizing the previously introduced Schrödinger half-bridges.

2.3

Similar to half-bridges,

X_{T}\sim\Pi^{*}_{T}=p_{\mathrm{target}}.\end{cases}

In order to update the control gradually and thereby improve the robustness of the fixed-point iteration, we extend (

Section

Proposition 2.11

(Variational characterization of damped fixed-point iteration)
.

Consider the fixed-point operator
\Phi
defined in (
15
).
Then, the damped fixed-point iteration (
25
) satisfies that

\displaystyle u_{i+1}=

\displaystyle\argmin_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigg\{\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\|\xi(X,t)-u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\,\,\,+\eta\,\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\|u_{i}(X_{t},t)-u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]\Bigg\},

with

and damping parameter
\eta=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}

Related work

Diffusion-based sampling.

Sampling from unnormalized densities has traditionally relied on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. Early learnable diffusion-based approaches utilized Schrödinger-Föllmer diffusions
(
89
;
65
;
81
)
or adaptations of denoising diffusion models to the energy-based setting
(
5
;
80
;
18
;
36
)
. From the perspective of stochastic optimal control, many of these approaches can be viewed as finding controls that minimize divergences between forward and backward processes
(
64
;
82
;
7
)
. A series of recent works improved the objectives, training schemes, and exploration of diffusion-based samplers
(
86
;
31
;
61
;
20
;
83
;
84
;
68
;
25
;
75
;
4
;
42
;
41
;
10
;
87
;
88
;
73
)
.
However, while effective, these methods typically require to keep SDE simulations in memory for the gradient computation, which is computationally expensive.

To address this,
30
introduced
adjoint sampling
(AS), a framework that leverages the adjoint method to enable memory-efficient gradient estimation. This allowed them to scale diffusion-based sampling to amortized conformer generation across many molecular system. However, AS relies on a
“memoryless”
condition, restricting the reference process and prior distributions to specific forms (e.g., Dirac priors). This condition necessitates large diffusion coefficients to achieve good performance, which can leads to instability.

Diffusion bridges.

To enable sampling between arbitrary boundary distributions, the problem can be framed as learning a Schrödinger Bridge (SB).
74
introduced
Diffusion Schrödinger Bridge Matching
(DSBM) for the data-driven setting, utilizing Iterative Markovian Fitting that alternates between projecting onto Markovian and reciprocal classes. Recently,
50
extended this to energy-based sampling with the
Adjoint Schrödinger Bridge Sampler
(ASBS), which generalizes AS by relaxing the memoryless condition, allowing for arbitrary priors. However, ASBS employs an alternating optimization scheme between the control and a corrector potential, which can be empirically unstable due to the interdependence of the objectives. While our method retains the flexibility of ASBS, it only relies on a single, scalable objective. To address mode collapse in complex landscapes,
56
proposed
Well-Tempered ASBS
(WT-ASBS), which augments diffusion samplers with metadynamics-inspired bias potentials along collective variables. While our work focuses on the stability and scalability of the core sampling algorithm, our method is compatible with such enhanced sampling techniques.

Target score estimation.

A core component of matching-based samplers is the estimation of the regression target.

15
formally introduced the
Target Score Identity
(TSI) and
Target Score Matching
(TSM), demonstrating that score estimation can be significantly improved by leveraging the known structure of the target score
\nabla\log p_{\text{target}}
. This identity underpins the regression targets used in AS, ASBS, and in our framework.

Numerical Experiments

We evaluate the performance of our proposed BMS by comparing it to key instances of our framework: the
adjoint sampler
(AS)
(
30
)
and the
adjoint Schrödinger bridge sampler
(ASBS)
(
50
)
.
Our evaluation spans three distinct testbeds: high-dimensional synthetic Gaussian mixture models,
n

Detailed descriptions of the experimental setup, benchmark problems, and evaluation metrics are provided in Appendix

, with supplementary results available in Appendix

.

4.1
Gaussian mixture models

In this experiment, we consider Gaussian mixture targets consisting of 20 components with unit variance and means sampled uniformly from
[-20,20]^{d}
. We assess the models based on their ability to capture all modes and the accuracy of their density fitting. Specifically, we utilize
mode TVD
– the total variation distance between empirical and ground-truth mixture weights – to quantify mode collapse. Additionally, we use the Wasserstein (
W_{2}
) distance to compare the model’s empirical density with ground truth samples from the target.

As shown in
Figure

1
, our analysis focuses on a comparison with ASBS, as the standard adjoint sampler did not produce stable or competitive results in this setting. While many contemporary studies focus on dimensions ranging from
d=2
to
50
, we push the complexity to
d=2500
. We observe that while ASBS is prone to instability at low damping values
\eta
, our method remains consistently stable, although it may exhibit some mode collapse. Notably, as the dimensionality increases, ASBS suffers from a clear degradation in performance, whereas our method maintains consistent and robust results across the entire range of tested dimensions. As sampling multimodal densities in such high dimensions was previously unattainable, we conjecture that damping will be a key ingredient in the future of generative sampling methods. We provide further results in
Appendix

E
, comparing the performance across different number of modes; see
Figure

2
for a qualitative result.

4.2
n
-body identical particle systems

We extend our evaluation to
n
-body systems of increasing complexity, spanning from DW-4 (
d=8
) to the high-dimensional LJ-55 (
d=165
). The results in Table
2
demonstrate that our approach consistently outperforms established baselines, including AS and ASBS, across both geometric Wasserstein-2 (
W_{2}
) and energy value distributions (
E(\cdot)\ W_{2}
). Critically, the most substantial benefits of our method emerge in the high-dimensional
d=165
regime, where it maintains superior accuracy in energy modeling while other methods begin to degrade. Furthermore, our approach exhibits significantly reduced variance across random seeds, underscoring its stability compared to existing approaches.

4.3
Molecular benchmarks: Peptide systems

We evaluate our method on two increasingly complex molecular benchmarks: Alanine Dipeptide (ALA2;
d=66
) and Alanine Tetrapeptide (ALA4;
d=126
). Notably, we train our model directly on Cartesian coordinates, a departure from existing literature which typically relies on prior domain knowledge – such as internal coordinates
(
55
;
50
)
, collective variables – to enhance exploration
(
56
)
, or pretraining on molecular dynamics (MD) data with high temperatures
(
1
)
. To assess the quality of the generated conformations, we analyze Ramachandran plots, which represent the joint distribution of the backbone dihedral angles
\phi
and
\psi
. These angles characterize the local conformation of the molecular backbone and are essential for identifying stable states. We compute the Jensen-Shannon divergence of pairs of backbone dihedral angles between MD data and samples generated by the model (
D_{\mathrm{JS}}
), averaged over all residues. Similarly, we compute the root-mean-squared error between the corresponding free-energy surfaces (
\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{\Delta F}}
). Additionally, we compute the Wasserstein-2 metric between the first two TIC components (TICA-
W_{2}
) to ensure the capture of global conformational dependencies. Qualitative results (Ramachandran and TICA visualizations) are presented in
Figures

3
and
9
for ALA2 and
Figure

10
for ALA4. Quantitative results are shown
Table

3
and
Figure

4
.

While our method achieves state-of-the-art performance across both systems, the results underscore the critical role of the proposed damped-fixed point iteration scheme. Specifically, omitting the damping component leads to poor performance and significant training instability in both ASBS and BMS, as evidenced in Figure
4
. Note that we were not able to obtain results for ALA4 without damping due to numerical instabilities. A notable characteristic across both systems is their mode-covering behavior. Unlike traditional sampling techniques that often suffer from mode-collapse – typically driven by the mode-seeking nature of the reverse KL divergence – our approach successfully maintains probability mass across all target regions. We conjecture that this mass-covering property is a direct result of our fixed-point iteration; as shown in (
14
), the fixed point corresponds to the Markovian projection, which is inherently linked to a forward KL objective.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we formulate diffusion-based sampling from unnormalized target densities as a generalized fixed-point iteration grounded in Nelson’s relation and a generalized target score identity (see
Section

2.1
). This perspective provides a unified theoretical framework encompassing several recently proposed matching-based algorithms, including Adjoint Sampling
(
30
)
and Adjoint Schrödinger Bridge Samplers
(
50
;
56
)
.
Building on this foundation, we introduce a new scalable and stable algorithm, termed the
Bridge Matching Sampler
(BMS), which addresses key limitations of existing approaches – most notably alternating objectives and restrictive choices of reference processes or priors. To further improve robustness and avoid mode collapse in high dimensions, we propose a damped fixed-point iteration. Empirically, BMS achieves state-of-the-art performance on challenging synthetic targets and high-dimensional molecular benchmarks, and scales successfully to highly multimodal distributions in dimensions up to
d=2500
.

Future work.

.
Several promising directions remain for future work. First, the performance of our sampler could be further improved by incorporating an adaptive schedule for the damping parameter
\eta
, in the spirit of trust-region methods
(
6
)
, or by integrating importance weighting strategies (see
Section

C.1
for a preliminary discussion). From a theoretical perspective, establishing convergence guarantees for the proposed fixed-point iteration, as well as conditions ensuring existence and uniqueness of its solution, remains an open problem. Finally, while our focus has been on the principled development of scalable and stable diffusion-based samplers, it would be natural to extend this framework to related settings, such as the estimation of normalizing constants
(
27
;
32
)
and sampling over discrete state spaces
(
35
;
67
;
90
;
40
;
76
;
28
)
.

A.2

Throughout our work, we make the same assumptions as in
59

17

74

(i)

The set
\mathcal{U}
of

is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\{u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})\,|\,\exists C>0,\,\forall(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T],\,u(x,s)\leq C(1+\|x\|)\}.

(26)

(ii)

The diffusion coefficient
\sigma(t)

\sigma(t)>0
for all

.

(iii)

For the target path measure
\Pi^{*}=\Pi_{0,T}^{*}\mathbbm{P}_{|0,T}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbbm{P})

(a)

For all

\Pi^{*}_{T|0}\ll\mathbbm{P}_{T|0}

(b)

The Radon-Nikodym derivative
r_{T}(x_{T}|x_{0})=\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi^{*}_{T|0}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}_{T|0}}(x_{T})

x_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(c)

The mapping
(t,x)\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[\xi(X,t)|X_{t}=x]
is locally Lipschitz and there exists
C>0
and
\psi\in C([0,T],\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that for any
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}_{T|t}}[\nabla\log\mathbbm{P}_{T|t}(X_{T}|X_{t})|X_{t}=x]\|\leq C\psi(t)(1+\|x\|).

(27)

(iv)

The target boundary coupling
\Pi^{*}_{0,T}(x_{0},x_{T})
is such that for any
t\in(0,T)
, the product
\Pi_{0,T}^{*}(x_{0},x_{T})\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}(x|x_{0},x_{T})

A.3
Useful identities

Throughout this section, we consider the following stochastic processes:

(i)

Reference Process
(X\sim\mathbbm{P})
: We fix it to a scaled Brownian motion:
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sigma(t)\mathrm{d}B_{t}
with
X_{0}\sim p_{\mathrm{prior}}
. We define the cumulative variance
\kappa(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)\mathrm{d}s
and the relative variance
\gamma(t)=\kappa(t)/\kappa(T)
.

(ii)

Controlled Process
(X\sim\mathbbm{P}^{u})
: The Markovian diffusion defined by
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sigma(t)u(X_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t)\mathrm{d}B_{t}
.

(iii)

Path-Dependent Process
(X\sim\Pi^{*}
or
\Pi^{i}
): The non-Markovian process defined by
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sigma(t)\xi(X,t)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t)\mathrm{d}B_{t}
.

Theorem A.1

(Girsanov’s theorem for path measures)
.

Let
u,v\in\mathcal{U}
. Then the Radon-Nikodym derivative between
\mathbb{P}^{u}
and
\mathbb{P}^{v}
, evaluated along
X
, is given by

\displaystyle\log\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}^{u}}{\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}^{v}}(X)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{-1}(u-v)(X_{t},t)\cdot\mathrm{d}X_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\|u\|^{2}-\|v\|^{2}\right)(X_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t.

(28)

Proof.

Lemma A.5

(Orthogonal Projection in
L^{2}
)
.

Let
\xi
be a path-dependent random variable such that
\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\xi^{2}]<\infty
, and let
\Phi(X_{t})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\xi|X_{t}]
be its Markovian projection. For any measurable Markovian function
u(X_{t},t)
, the following decomposition holds:

\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\|\xi(X,t)-u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\|\xi(X,t)-\Phi(X_{t})\|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\|\Phi(X_{t})-u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}].

(33)

Proof.

We begin by adding and subtracting the projection
\Phi(X_{t})
inside the norm, i.e.

\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\|\xi(X,t)-u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\|(\xi(X,t)-\Phi(X_{t}))+(\Phi(X_{t})-u(X_{t},t))\|^{2}].

(34)

Expanding the square, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\|\xi(X,t)-u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}]=&\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\|\xi(X,t)-\Phi(X_{t})\|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\|\Phi(X_{t})-u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}]\\
&+2\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\langle\xi(X,t)-\Phi(X_{t}),\Phi(X_{t})-u(X_{t},t)\rangle].\end{split}

(35)

We now show that the cross-term vanishes using the tower property. Let
g(X_{t})\coloneqq\Phi(X_{t})-u(X_{t},t)
, which is a function solely of the state at time
t
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\langle\xi(X,t)-\Phi(X_{t}),g(X_{t})\rangle]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}\big[\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\langle\xi(X,t)-\Phi(X_{t}),g(X_{t})\rangle|X_{t}]\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}\big[\langle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\xi(X,t)|X_{t}]-\Phi(X_{t}),g(X_{t})\rangle\big].

(36)

By the definition of the projection,
\Phi(X_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\xi(X,t)|X_{t}]
. Therefore, the inner term is

\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}\big[\langle\Phi(X_{t})-\Phi(X_{t}),g(X_{t})\rangle\big]=0.

(37)

The cross-term vanishes, leaving only the sum of the squared distances, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.6
.

Let
\Pi^{*},\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
such that
\Pi^{*}\ll\mathbbm{P}
. Let
X
denote the path and
X_{t}
the process value at time
t
. Let
\Pi^{*}_{t}
and
\mathbbm{P}_{t}
denote the marginal distributions at time
t
. Then,
\mathbbm{P}_{t}
-almost surely

\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi^{*}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}_{t}}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi^{*}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}}(X)\bigg|X_{t}=x\right].

(38)

Proof.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be an arbitrary bounded measurable test function. We compute the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[h(X_{t})]
in two distinct ways.

1. Via the marginal measure
\Pi^{*}_{t}
:
Using the definition of the Radon-Nikodym derivative on the marginal space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[h(X_{t})]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)\,\mathrm{d}\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi^{*}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}_{t}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}_{t}(x).

(39)

2. Via the path measure
\Pi^{*}
:
Using the Radon-Nikodym derivative
L=\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi^{*}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}}
on the full path space and the tower property of conditional expectation, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[h(X_{t})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}\left[h(X_{t})\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi^{*}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}}(X)\right]

(40a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}\left[h(X_{t})\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi^{*}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}}(X)\bigg|X_{t}\right]\right]

(40b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}\left[h(X_{t})\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi^{*}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}}(X)\bigg|X_{t}\right]\right].

(40c)

Note that we pulled
h(X_{t})
out of the inner expectation because it is
\sigma(X_{t})
-measurable.
We can rewrite (
40
) as an integral over the marginal measure
\mathbbm{P}_{t}
, i.e.

\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[h(X_{t})]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi^{*}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}}(X)\bigg|X_{t}=x\right]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}_{t}(x).

(41)

Comparing (
39
) and (
41
), we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi^{*}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}_{t}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}_{t}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi^{*}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}}(X)\bigg|X_{t}=x\right]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}_{t}(x).

(42)

Since this equality holds for any bounded measurable
h
, the integrands must be equal
\mathbbm{P}_{t}
-almost everywhere.
∎

Proposition B.1

(Denoising score identity)
.

For any path measure
\Pi^{*}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbbm{P})
with coupling
\Pi^{*}_{0,T}
, the score of the marginal density
\Pi^{*}_{t}
satisfies the Denoising Score Identity (DSI)

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\log\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}_{0,T|t}}\left[\nabla_{X_{t}}\log\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}(X_{t}|X_{0},X_{T})|X_{t}=x\right].

(43)

Proof.

We consider the marginal density
\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)
,
which for a general coupling
\Pi^{*}_{0,T}(x_{0},x_{T})
, is given by

\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}(x|x_{0},x_{T})\Pi^{*}_{0,T}(x_{0},x_{T})\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}x_{T}.

(44)

We differentiate
\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)
with respect to
x
. With Assumption
(iii)b
, we can interchange differentiation and integration, such that the gradient operates directly on the transition kernel
\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}
:

\nabla_{x}\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\nabla_{x}\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}(x|x_{0},x_{T})\Pi^{*}_{0,T}(x_{0},x_{T})\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}x_{T}.

(45)

Using the log-derivative identity
\nabla_{x}\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}=\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}\nabla_{x}\log\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}
, we rewrite the gradient as

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left(\nabla_{x}\log\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}(x|x_{0},x_{T})\right)\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}(x|x_{0},x_{T})\Pi^{*}_{0,T}(x_{0},x_{T})\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}x_{T}.

(46)

To recover the score
\nabla_{x}\log\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)
, we divide both sides by the marginal density
\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)
. By Bayes’ theorem, the posterior density of the boundaries given the current state is given as

\displaystyle\Pi^{*}_{0,T|t}(x_{0},x_{T}|x)=\frac{\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}(x|x_{0},x_{T})\Pi^{*}_{0,T}(x_{0},x_{T})}{\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)}.

(47)

Substituting this identity into the integral expression (
46
), we get:

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\log\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\nabla_{x}\log\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}(x|x_{0},x_{T})\Pi^{*}_{0,T|t}(x_{0},x_{T}|x)\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}x_{T}

(48a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}_{0,T|t}}\left[\nabla_{X_{t}}\log\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}(X_{t}|X_{0},X_{T})|X_{t}=x\right].

(48b)

This concludes the proof.
∎

B.2
Proofs for Schrödinger bridges

We consider the Schrödinger Bridge (SB) problem with reference measure
\mathbbm{P}
induced by the SDE
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sigma(t)\mathrm{d}B_{t}
. This problem is defined as the entropy-regularized optimal transport problem

\min_{\mathbbm{P}^{u}\in\mathcal{P}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbbm{P}^{u}|\mathbbm{P})\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathbbm{P}^{u}_{0}=\Pi^{*}_{0},\ \mathbbm{P}^{u}_{T}=\Pi^{*}_{T}.

(64)

This is equivalent to the stochastic optimal control problem

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\|u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ \begin{cases}X_{0}\sim\Pi^{*}_{0}=p_{\mathrm{prior}},\\
X_{T}\sim\Pi^{*}_{T}=p_{\mathrm{target}},\end{cases}

(65)

under the controlled dynamics

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sigma(t)u(X_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t)\mathrm{d}B_{t}.

(66)

The following derivations of the Schrödinger system and its associated optimal controls are standrad in the literature, and we provide them here primarily for the reader’s convenience.

Using Girsanov’s theorem and the Fokker planck equation, the evolution of the marginal density
\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)
can be enforced via the Lagrange multiplier
\psi(x,t)
:

\mathcal{L}(\Pi^{*},u,\psi)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\|u(x,t)\|^{2}\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)+\psi(x,t)\left(\partial_{t}\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)+\sigma(t)\nabla\cdot(\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)u(x,t))-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t)\operatorname{Tr}(\nabla^{2}\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)\right)\right)\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

(67)

Lemma B.2
.

The optimal marginal density
\Pi_{t}^{*}
and the optimal control
u^{*}
satisfy:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\psi+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\nabla^{2}\psi)+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\|\nabla\psi\|^{2}=0,\\
\partial_{t}\Pi^{*}_{t}+\sigma\nabla\cdot(\Pi^{*}_{t}u^{*})=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\nabla^{2}\Pi_{t}^{*}),\end{cases}

(68)

where the optimal control is given by
u^{*}=\sigma\nabla\psi_{t}
.

Proof.

Using integration by parts (where boundary terms vanish by Assumption
(iv)
), we can rewrite the term containing the divergence inside the Lagrangian:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(x,t)\sigma(t)\nabla\cdot(\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)u(x,t))\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sigma(t)\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)u(x,t)\cdot\nabla\psi(x,t)\mathrm{d}x.

(69)

The terms in the Lagrangian involving the control
u
are

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\|u(x,t)\|^{2}-\sigma(t)u(x,t)\cdot\nabla\psi(x,t)\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

(70)

Taking the functional derivative with respect to
u
and setting it to zero, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta u}=\Pi_{t}^{*}(u-\sigma\nabla\psi)=0.

(71)

Hence, we obtain the optimal control

\displaystyle u^{*}=\sigma\nabla\psi.

(72)

Substituting
u^{*}
into the Lagrangian yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}&(\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t)\|\nabla\psi(x,t)\|^{2}\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)+\psi(x,t)\partial_{t}\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)\\
&+\sigma^{2}(t)\psi(x,t)\nabla\cdot(\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)\nabla\psi(x,t))-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t)\psi(x,t)\operatorname{Tr}(\nabla^{2}\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)))\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(73)

We isolate
\Pi_{t}^{*}
in every term. For that, we use integration by parts on the last three terms:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\psi(x,t)\partial_{t}\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)\partial_{t}\psi(x,t)\mathrm{d}t,

(74)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sigma^{2}(t)\psi(x,t)\nabla\cdot(\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)\nabla\psi(x,t))\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sigma^{2}(t)\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)(\nabla\psi(x,t))\cdot\nabla\psi(x,t)\mathrm{d}x

(75)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sigma^{2}(t)\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)\|\nabla\psi(x,t)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}x,

(76)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sigma^{2}(t)\psi(x,t)\operatorname{Tr}(\nabla^{2}\Pi_{t}^{*}(x))\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sigma^{2}(t)\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)\operatorname{Tr}(\nabla^{2}\psi(x,t))\mathrm{d}x,

(77)

where the boundary terms vanish by Assumption
(iv)
. Substituting these back into the Lagrangian gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t)\|\nabla\psi(x,t)\|^{2}-\partial_{t}\psi(x,t)-\sigma^{2}(t)\|\nabla\psi(x,t)\|^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t)\operatorname{Tr}(\nabla^{2}\psi(x,t))\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

(78a)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)\left(-\partial_{t}\psi(x,t)-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t)\|\nabla\psi(x,t)\|^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t)\operatorname{Tr}(\nabla^{2}\psi(x,t))\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

(78b)

Taking the functional derivative with respect to
\Pi_{t}^{*}
and setting it to zero gives the first PDE in (
68
) (namely the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation). If we substitute the optimal control
u^{*}=\sigma\nabla\psi
into the Fokker-Planck equation of the general controlled process, we obtain the second PDE:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Pi^{*}_{t}+\sigma\nabla\cdot(\Pi^{*}_{t}u^{*})=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\nabla^{2}\Pi_{t}^{*}).

(79)

∎

Lemma B.3
.

The nonlinear system of PDEs in (
68
) can be linearized via the Hopf-Cole transformation to satisfy the linear system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\varphi=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\Delta\varphi,\\
\partial_{t}{\widehat{\varphi}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\Delta{\widehat{\varphi}},\end{cases}

(80)

subject to the coupled boundary conditions

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\varphi_{0}{\widehat{\varphi}}_{0}=\Pi_{0}^{*},\\
\varphi_{T}{\widehat{\varphi}}_{T}=\Pi_{T}^{*}.\end{cases}

(81)

Proof.

Define the mapping
(\Pi^{*}_{t},\psi)\mapsto(\varphi,{\widehat{\varphi}})
with

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\psi_{t}\right),

(82)

\displaystyle{\widehat{\varphi}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{t}^{*}\exp\left(-\psi_{t}\right)=\frac{\Pi_{t}^{*}}{\varphi_{t}}.

(83)

From
\psi=\log\varphi
, we calculate the derivatives

\displaystyle\nabla\psi=\frac{\nabla\varphi}{\varphi},\quad\Delta\psi=\frac{\Delta\varphi}{\varphi}-\frac{\|\nabla\varphi\|^{2}}{\varphi^{2}},\quad\partial_{t}\psi=\frac{\partial_{t}\varphi}{\varphi}.

(84)

Substituting these into the second PDE from (
68
) yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\nabla^{2}\psi)+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\|\nabla\psi\|^{2}=0

(85a)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{t}\varphi}{\varphi}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta\varphi}{\varphi}-\frac{\|\nabla\varphi\|^{2}}{\varphi^{2}}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\frac{\|\nabla\varphi\|^{2}}{\varphi^{2}}=0.

(85b)

Multiplying by
\varphi
, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\Delta\varphi=0,

(86)

which is the first PDE in (
81
). For
{\widehat{\varphi}}
, we use the product relation
\Pi_{t}^{*}=\varphi{\widehat{\varphi}}
and substitute it into the Fokker-Planck equation of the optimal density:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varphi{\widehat{\varphi}})+\sigma^{2}\nabla\cdot(\varphi{\widehat{\varphi}}\nabla\log\varphi)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\Delta(\varphi{\widehat{\varphi}}).

(87)

Applying the product rule and using
u^{*}=\sigma^{2}\frac{\nabla\varphi}{\varphi}
, we get

\displaystyle{\widehat{\varphi}}\partial_{t}\varphi+\varphi\partial_{t}{\widehat{\varphi}}+\sigma^{2}\nabla\cdot({\widehat{\varphi}}\nabla\varphi)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}({\widehat{\varphi}}\Delta\varphi+2\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla{\widehat{\varphi}}+\varphi\Delta{\widehat{\varphi}}).

(88)

Expanding the divergence term
\nabla\cdot({\widehat{\varphi}}\nabla\varphi)=\nabla{\widehat{\varphi}}\cdot\nabla\varphi+{\widehat{\varphi}}\Delta\varphi
and substituting
\partial_{t}\varphi=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\Delta\varphi
, the terms involving
\Delta\varphi
and
\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla{\widehat{\varphi}}
cancel out, leaving

\displaystyle\varphi\partial_{t}{\widehat{\varphi}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\varphi\Delta{\widehat{\varphi}}.

(89)

Dividing by
\varphi
yields the second PDE. The boundary constraints follow since
\Pi_{t}^{*}=\varphi\hat{\varphi}
.
∎

Proof of

.

By

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}(x_{0}){\widehat{\varphi}}_{0}(x_{0})\mathbbm{P}_{T|0}(x_{T}|x_{0})\frac{\varphi_{T}(x_{T})}{\varphi_{0}(x_{0})}

Proposition B.4
.

Consider the Schrödinger Bridge problem associated with the reference process
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sigma(t)\mathrm{d}B_{t}
. Let
\Pi^{*}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbbm{P})
be the unique bridge measure with marginals
\Pi_{0}^{*}
and
\Pi_{T}^{*}
The optimal forward drift
u^{*}
and backward drift
v^{*}
are characterized by the Schrödinger potentials
(\varphi,{\widehat{\varphi}})
and satisfy the identities

\displaystyle u^{*}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sigma(t)\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}_{T|t}}\Big[\nabla_{X_{T}}\log\frac{\Pi_{T}^{*}(X_{T})}{{\widehat{\varphi}}_{T}(X_{T})}\Big|X_{t}=x\Big],

(97)

\displaystyle v^{*}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sigma(t)\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}_{0|t}}\Big[\nabla_{X_{0}}\log\frac{\Pi_{0}^{*}(X_{0})}{\varphi_{0}(X_{0})}\Big|X_{t}=x\Big].

(98)

Proof.

For the optimal forward control
u^{*}
holds that

\displaystyle u^{*}(x,t)=\sigma(t)\nabla_{x}\log\varphi_{t}(x)=\sigma(t)\frac{\nabla_{x}\varphi_{t}(x)}{\varphi_{t}(x)}.

(99)

The potential
\varphi_{t}(x)
evolves backward in time according to (
20
), yielding

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbbm{P}_{T|t}(x_{T}|x)\varphi_{T}(x_{T})\mathrm{d}x_{T}.

(100)

For the reference process
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sigma(t)\mathrm{d}B_{t}
, the transition density
\mathbbm{P}_{T|t}(x_{T}|x)
depends only on the difference
(x_{T}-x)
. Explicitly,
\mathbbm{P}_{T|t}(x_{T}|x)=\mathcal{N}(x_{T};x,\Sigma_{T|t})
. This implies the identity

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\mathbbm{P}_{T|t}(x_{T}|x)=-\nabla_{x_{T}}\mathbbm{P}_{T|t}(x_{T}|x).

(101)

We differentiate the integral form of
\varphi
with respect to
x
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\varphi_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{x}\mathbbm{P}_{T|t}(x_{T}|x)\varphi_{T}(x_{T})\mathrm{d}x_{T}

(102a)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(-\nabla_{x_{T}}\mathbbm{P}_{T|t}(x_{T}|x)\right)\varphi_{T}(x_{T})\mathrm{d}x_{T}

(102b)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbbm{P}_{T|t}(x_{T}|x)\nabla_{x_{T}}\varphi_{T}(x_{T})\mathrm{d}x_{T}.

(102c)

Use the log-derivative trick
\nabla\varphi=\varphi\nabla\log\varphi
, we get

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\varphi_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbbm{P}_{T|t}(x_{T}|x)\varphi_{T}(x_{T})\nabla_{x_{T}}\log\varphi_{T}(x_{T})\mathrm{d}x_{T}.

(103)

We substitute this back into the expression for
u^{*}
and divide by
\varphi_{t}(x)
. We identify the bridge density
\Pi_{T|t}^{*}
using the reciprocal relation for Schrödinger Bridges:

\displaystyle\Pi_{T|t}^{*}(x_{T}|x)=\frac{\mathbbm{P}_{T|t}(x_{T}|x)\varphi_{T}(x_{T})}{\varphi_{t}(x)}.

(104)

Thus we get

\displaystyle u^{*}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sigma(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\underbrace{\frac{\mathbbm{P}_{T|t}(x_{T}|x)\varphi_{T}(x_{T})}{\varphi_{t}(x)}}_{=\Pi^{*}_{T|t}(x_{T}|x)}\nabla_{x_{T}}\log\varphi_{T}(x_{T})\mathrm{d}x_{T}

(105a)

\displaystyle=\sigma(t)\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}_{T|t}}\big[\nabla_{X_{T}}\log\varphi_{T}(X_{T})|X_{t}=x\big].

(105b)

At time
T
, the Schrödinger system boundary condition is
\Pi_{T}^{*}(x)=\varphi_{T}(x){\widehat{\varphi}}_{T}(x)
. Solving for
\varphi_{T}(x)
, we get

\displaystyle\varphi_{T}(x)=\frac{\Pi_{T}^{*}(x)}{{\widehat{\varphi}}_{T}(x)}.

(106)

Substituting this into the expectation yields the result

\displaystyle u^{*}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sigma(t)\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}_{T|t}}\Big[\nabla\log\frac{\Pi_{T}(X_{T})}{{\widehat{\varphi}}_{T}(X_{T})}\Big|X_{t}=x\Big].

(107)

The proof for
v^{*}
is analogous but proceeds forward in time.
∎

B.3
Proofs for Schrödinger half bridges

Under the memoryless condition, a Schrödinger Half Bridge can be formulated as a special case of a Schrödinger Bridge. Recall that we have the following minimization problem for SHBs:

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}^{u}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\|u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad X_{T}\sim\Pi_{T}^{*}=p_{\mathrm{target}}.

(119)

This optimization yields the optimal joint coupling
\Pi_{0,T}^{*}=\Pi_{T}^{*}\mathbbm{P}_{0|T}
. By the memoryless condition, the backward transition of the reference process becomes independent of the terminal state and thus
\mathbbm{P}_{0|T}=\mathbbm{P}_{0}
. Consequently, we also have
\Pi_{0}^{*}=\mathbbm{P}_{0}
.

Since both the initial and terminal marginals are determined, we can identify the corresponding potentials for the Schrödinger system
\Pi_{t}^{*}=\varphi_{t}\hat{\varphi}_{t}
.

•

Initial Potentials:
To satisfiy
\Pi_{0}^{*}=\varphi_{0}\hat{\varphi}_{0}=\mathbbm{P}_{0}
, we have the initial potentials as
\hat{\varphi}_{0}=\mathbbm{P}_{0}
and
\varphi_{0}=1
.

•

Terminal Potentials:
Under the reference dynamics, the forward potential evolves exactly as the reference marginal, yielding
\hat{\varphi}_{T}=\mathbbm{P}_{T}
. To satisfy the terminal constraint
\Pi_{T}^{*}=\varphi_{T}\hat{\varphi}_{T}
, the terminal backward potential must therefore be
\varphi_{T}=\Pi_{T}^{*}/\mathbbm{P}_{T}
.

B.5
Proofs for damped fixed-point iterates

Proof of
Section

2.5
.

The operator
\Phi(u_{i})
is defined as the Markovian projection of the drift
\xi
under the measure
\Pi^{i}
:

\displaystyle\Phi(u_{i})(x,t)=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\xi(X,t)|X_{t}=x].

(128)

Let
\mathcal{J}
be the objective functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(u)=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\xi(X,t)-u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}\|u_{i}(X_{t},t)-u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t\right].

(129)

Using the bias-variance decomposition (see
Section

A.3
), the first term of the objective can be decomposed as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\|\xi(X,t)-u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}|X_{t}=x]=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}[\|\xi(X,t)-\Phi(u_{i})(X_{t},t)\|^{2}|X_{t}=x]+\|\Phi(u_{i})(x,t)-u(x,t)\|^{2}.

(130)

Since the first term does not depend on
u
, minimizing the original objective functional
Equation

129
is equivalent to minimizing

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\|\Phi(u_{i})(X_{t},t)-u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}+\tfrac{\eta}{2}\|u_{i}(X_{t},t)-u(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(131)

We minimize the integrand pointwise for almost every
(x,t)
by taking the variational derivative with respect to
u
and setting it to zero, i.e.

\nabla_{u}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\Phi(u_{i})-u\|^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}\|u_{i}-u\|^{2}\right)=-(\Phi(u_{i})-u)-\eta(u_{i}-u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0.

(132)

Rearranging terms to isolate
u
gives

u=\frac{1}{1+\eta}\Phi(u_{i})+\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}u_{i}.

(133)

By substituting the definition of the damping parameter
\eta=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}
, we find that
\frac{1}{1+\eta}=\alpha
and
\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}=1-\alpha
. Thus, the minimizer exactly recovers the damped fixed-point update rule:

u_{i+1}(x,t)=\alpha\,\Phi(u_{i})(x,t)+(1-\alpha)u_{i}(x,t).

(134)

∎

Proof of

\displaystyle J(c)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}_{t}}\left[\operatorname{Var}_{\Pi^{*}_{|t}}\left(\sigma^{-1}(t)\xi^{c}(X,t|X_{t})\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\|\sigma^{-1}(t)\xi^{c}(X,t)-\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[\sigma^{-1}(t)\xi^{c}(X,t)|X_{t}]\|^{2}\right].

(135)

Substituting our definition
\sigma^{-1}(t)\xi^{c}=G_{0}(X)-G_{v}(X)-c(t)(G_{0}(X)-G_{T}(X))
into the expected conditional variance yields

\displaystyle J(c)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\Bigg[\Big\|\big(G_{0}(X)-G_{v}(X)-c(t)(G_{0}(X)-G_{T}(X))\big)-\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[G_{0}(X)-G_{v}(X)-c(t)(G_{0}(X)-G_{T}(X))|X_{t}]\Big\|^{2}\Bigg].

(136)

Crucially, because
G_{0}
and
G_{T}
are both unbiased estimators of the marginal score, their conditional expectations are equal. Therefore, the conditional expectation of their difference evaluates to zero:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[G_{0}(X)-G_{T}(X)|X_{t}]=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[G_{0}(X)|X_{t}]-\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[G_{T}(X)|X_{t}]=0.

(137)

This simplifies our objective to

\displaystyle J(c)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\left\|\big(G_{0}(X)-G_{v}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[G_{0}(X)-G_{v}(X)|X_{t}]\big)-c(t)(G_{0}(X)-G_{T}(X))\right\|^{2}\right].

(138)

Expanding the squared norm using the inner product, and applying our definitions for scalar variance and covariance, we obtain a quadratic function in
c(t)
, namely

\displaystyle\begin{split}J(c)&=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\|G_{0}(X)-G_{v}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[G_{0}(X)-G_{v}(X)|X_{t}]\|^{2}\right]\\
&\quad-2c(t)\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\langle G_{0}(X)-G_{v}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[G_{0}(X)-G_{v}(X)|X_{t}],\,G_{0}(X)-G_{T}(X)\rangle\right]+c(t)^{2}\operatorname{Var}(G_{0}-G_{T}).\end{split}

(139)

By the law of total expectation, and recalling that
\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[G_{0}(X)-G_{T}(X)|X_{t}]=0
, the cross-term simplifies to exactly the covariance between
(G_{0}-G_{v})
and
(G_{0}-G_{T})
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\langle G_{0}(X)-G_{v}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[G_{0}(X)-G_{v}(X)|X_{t}],\,G_{0}(X)-G_{T}(X)\rangle\right]

(140a)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}(G_{0}-G_{v},G_{0}-G_{T})-\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\langle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[G_{0}(X)-G_{v}(X)|X_{t}],\,\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[G_{0}(X)-G_{T}(X)|X_{t}]\rangle\right]

(140b)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}(G_{0}-G_{v},G_{0}-G_{T}).

(140c)

To find the minimum, we differentiate
J(c)
with respect to the scalar
c(t)
and set the derivative to zero:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J(c)}{\partial c(t)}=-2\,\mathrm{Cov}(G_{0}-G_{v},G_{0}-G_{T})+2c(t)\operatorname{Var}(G_{0}-G_{T})=0.

(141)

Solving for
c(t)
yields
c^{*}(t)=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(G_{0}-G_{v},G_{0}-G_{T})}{\operatorname{Var}(G_{0}-G_{T})}
. Finally, using the bilinearity of covariance to expand the numerator and denominator produces the full expression in the proposition.
∎

Proof of
Section

.

The left term on the right-hand side of the bias-variance decomposition in (

Appendix C
Further algorithmic details

C.1
Importance sampling and probability flow ordinary differential equation

Importance sampling in path space.

Our primary goal is to estimate the expectation of an observable
\Omega\in C(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
under a target distribution, denoted as
\mathbb{E}_{X_{T}\sim p_{\mathrm{target}}}\left[\Omega(X_{T})\right]
. Since the optimized process
\mathbbm{P}^{u}
serves as an approximation to the true target dynamics (i.e.,
\mathbbm{P}^{u}_{T}\approx p_{\mathrm{target}}
), we can correct for the resulting bias by employing importance sampling.
While importance sampling is classically applied to probability densities, it extends naturally to path space
(
5
)
, allowing for the reweighting of entire continuous trajectories of a stochastic process. We can express the target expectation as an integral over the proposal measure
\mathbbm{P}^{u}
:

\mathbb{E}_{X_{T}\sim p_{\mathrm{target}}}\left[\Omega(X_{T})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}^{u}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\mathbbm{P}\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\mathbbm{P}\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\mathbbm{P}\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}^{v}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}^{u}}(X)\,\Omega(X_{T})\right].

(151)

Here, the Radon-Nikodym derivative (RND),
w(X)\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\mathbbm{P}\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\mathbbm{P}\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\mathbbm{P}\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}^{v}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{P}^{u}}(X)
, is given by an extension of Girsanov’s theorem
(
59
;
82
;
64
)
:

w(X)=\frac{p_{\mathrm{target}}(X_{T})}{p_{\mathrm{prior}}(X_{0})}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{-1}(v-u)(X_{t},t)\cdot\mathrm{d}X_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\|v\|^{2}-\|u\|^{2}\right)(X_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t\right).

(152)

Practical implementation faces two distinct challenges. First, we typically only have access to
p_{\mathrm{target}}
up to an intractable normalization constant
\mathcal{Z}
. We therefore resort to self-normalized importance sampling
(
79
)
:

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}^{u}}\left[\widetilde{w}(X)\,\Omega(X_{T})\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}^{u}}\left[\widetilde{w}(X)\right]},

(153)

where
\widetilde{w}=w\mathcal{Z}
.
The second challenge is that evaluating the RND requires access to both the forward and the backward drift
u,v
, respectively. However, our proposed fixed-point iteration yields only
u
, making the direct computation of (
152
) impossible. To resolve this, we leverage Nelson’s identity (
16
) to formulate a regression problem for learning
v\approx v^{*}
and the score
\mathrm{s}\approx\sigma\nabla\log\Pi^{*}
separately, instead of
u
:

\mathcal{L}(v,\mathrm{s})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\|\xi^{v}(X,t)-v(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\|\xi^{\mathrm{s}}(X,t)-\mathrm{s}(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right],

(154)

with

v^{*}(x,t)=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\xi^{v}(X,t)|X_{t}=x\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma(t)\nabla\log\Pi^{*}_{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\xi^{\mathrm{s}}(X,t)|X_{t}=x\right],

(155)

where
\xi^{v}=\sigma\nabla\log\mathbbm{P}_{t|0}
and
\xi^{\mathrm{s}}
is inferred via the generalized target score identity.
Implementation-wise, it is possible to employ a shared neural backbone to approximate
v
and
\mathrm{s}
simultaneously, potentially improving parameter efficiency. However, we leave the exploration of such architectural optimizations for future work. Finally, the forward process (
1
) can be simulated as

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sigma(t)\left(\mathrm{s}-v\right)(X_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t)\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad X_{0}\sim p_{\mathrm{prior}}.

(156)

Probability flow ODE.

An alternative approach to importance sampling is to leverage the Probability Flow ODE (PF-ODE)
(
54
;
77
;
12
)
. This deterministic process shares the same marginal densities
(\Pi^{*}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
as the optimally controlled stochastic process but follows a smooth trajectory defined by:

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\underbrace{\left(\sigma u^{*}-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\nabla\log\Pi^{*}_{t}\right)(X_{t},t)}_{\coloneqq f(X_{t},t)}\mathrm{d}t.

(157)

By leveraging Nelson’s identity (
16
), we can express the drift term
f
in equivalent forms involving the backward control
v
, i.e.,

\sigma u^{*}-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\nabla\log\Pi^{*}_{t}=-\sigma v^{*}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\nabla\log\Pi^{*}_{t}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma(u^{*}-v^{*}),

(158)

suggesting that one needs two quantities among
u,v,\nabla\log\Pi^{*}
to simulate (
157
). Consequently, one can use the objective (
154
) to learn
v
and the score
\nabla\log\Pi^{*}
separately.
A key advantage of the ODE formulation is the ability to compute exact log-likelihoods via the
instantaneous change of variables
formula
(
11
)
. The evolution of the log-density is governed by the divergence of the drift field:

\log\mathbbm{P}^{u}_{T}(X_{T})=\log p_{\mathrm{prior}}(X_{0})-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\cdot f(X_{t},t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(159)

where
\nabla\cdot f=\Tr(\nabla f)
denotes the divergence. While this allows for exact density estimation, computing the trace of the Jacobian
\nabla f
is computationally expensive for high-dimensional problems. To mitigate this cost, one can trade exactness for efficiency by employing unbiased estimators, such as Hutchinson’s trace estimator
(
37
)
. Having access to
\mathbbm{P}^{u}_{T}
allows for importance sampling via (
153
) with
w={p_{\mathrm{target}}}/{\mathbbm{P}^{u}_{T}}
.

C.2
Variance reduction and control variates

The efficiency of learning the optimal drift
u^{*}
is limited by the stochasticity of the target drift
\xi
. To formalize this, we observe that the matching objective decomposes into two distinct components via
Section

A.3
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\|u(X_{t},t)-\xi(X,t)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\|u(X_{t},t)-\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\xi(X,t)|X_{t}\right]\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]}_{\text{bias}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\|\xi(X,t)-\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\xi(X,t)|X_{t}\right]\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]}_{\text{variance}}.

(160)

While the variance term

\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\|\xi(X,t)-\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}}\left[\xi(X,t)|X_{t}\right]\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]=\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}_{t}}\left[\operatorname{Var}_{\Pi^{*}_{|t}}\left(\xi(X,t|X_{t})\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t,

(161)

does not change the optimal drift
u^{*}
, it adds noise to the empirical gradient. To minimize its contribution, we first recall the definition of
\xi
:

\sigma^{-1}(t)\xi^{c}(X,t)=\frac{1-c(t)}{1-\gamma(t)}\nabla_{X_{0}}\log\Pi^{\ast}_{0,T}(X_{0},X_{T})+\frac{c(t)}{\gamma(t)}\,\nabla_{X_{T}}\log\Pi^{\ast}_{0,T}(X_{0},X_{T})-\nabla_{X_{t}}\log\mathbbm{P}_{t|0}(X_{t}|X_{0}),

(162)

where we make the dependence of
\xi
on
c
explicit. In what follows, we show that
c
can be used to reduce the empirical variance.

Control variate interpretation of
c
.

We start by making the following definitions:

\displaystyle G_{0}(X)\coloneqq\frac{1}{1-\gamma(t)}\nabla_{X_{0}}\log\Pi^{*}_{0,T},\quad G_{T}(X)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\gamma(t)}\nabla_{X_{T}}\log\Pi_{0,T}^{*},\quad G_{v}(X)\coloneqq\nabla_{X_{t}}\log\mathbbm{P}_{t|0}.

(163)

Using these definitions, we can express the scaled target field as
\sigma^{-1}\xi^{c}=(1-c)G_{0}+cG_{T}-G_{v}
.
Crucially, both
G_{0}
and
G_{T}
are unbiased estimators of the true score
\nabla_{x}\log\Pi_{t}^{*}(x)
when conditioned on
X_{t}
. This implies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\sigma^{-1}(t)\xi^{c}(X,t)\,|\,X_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[(1-c(t))G_{0}+c(t)G_{T}-G_{v}\,|\,X_{t}=x\right]

(164)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[G_{0}-G_{v}\,|\,X_{t}=x\right]}_{=\sigma^{-1}(t)u^{*}(x,t)}-c(t)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[G_{0}-G_{T}\,|\,X_{t}=x\right]}_{=0}.

(165)

The term
\Delta G\coloneqq G_{0}-G_{T}
acts as a zero-mean control variate. We can therefore optimize
c
to minimize the variance contribution in (
160
) by solving:

c^{*}=\argmin_{c}\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{i}_{t}}\left[\operatorname{Var}_{\Pi^{*}_{|t}}\left(\xi^{c}(X,t|X_{t})\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(166)

Proposition C.1

(Optimal scalar-valued control variate)
.

For any time
t\in[0,T]
, noting that the estimators are zero-mean, we define the scalar variance (equivalent to the trace of the covariance matrix) as
\operatorname{Var}(A)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[\|A\|^{2}]
and the scalar cross-covariance as
\mathrm{Cov}(A,B)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}[\langle A,B\rangle]
. The optimal control variate
c^{*}(t)
that minimizes the conditional variance of
\xi^{c}
is given by:

\displaystyle c^{*}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(G_{0})-\mathrm{Cov}(G_{0},G_{T})-\mathrm{Cov}(G_{0},G_{v})+\mathrm{Cov}(G_{T},G_{v})}{\operatorname{Var}(G_{0})+\operatorname{Var}(G_{T})-2\mathrm{Cov}(G_{0},G_{T})}.

(167)

Joint optimization of the drift and control variate.

In practice, evaluating the exact variances and covariances in the optimal schedule
c^{*}(t)
is intractable. However, we can circumvent this limitation and still achieve variance reduction by jointly learning the drift
u
and the schedule
c
. This approach is formally justified by the following proposition, which demonstrates that minimizing the fixed-point diffusion matching objective with respect to
c
exactly minimizes the conditional variance of the target.

Proposition C.2
.

Let
\xi^{c}
be defined as in (
162
). It holds that

\displaystyle c^{*}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{c}\ \mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\|u(X_{t},t)-\xi^{c}(X,t)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]=\argmin_{c}\ \int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}_{t}}\left[\operatorname{Var}_{\Pi^{*}_{|t}}\left(\xi^{c}(X,t|X_{t})\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(168)

Because
c(t)
strictly weights a zero-mean control variate, the conditional expectation of our target – and therefore the optimal drift
u^{*}
– remains perfectly invariant to its value. Looking at the decomposition of our main matching loss, this implies that any gradient taken with respect to
c
exclusively targets the irreducible conditional variance of
\xi^{c}
. Consequently, following
(
44
;
38
;
85
)
we can parameterize the control variate as a learnable function
c(t)
(e.g., a lightweight neural network) and jointly train it alongside the drift network
u
, i.e.,

\displaystyle u^{*},c^{*}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{u,c}\ \mathbb{E}_{\Pi^{*}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{2}\|u(X_{t},t)-\xi^{c}(X,t)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right],

(169)

using the exact same objective. By treating the variance reduction as an end-to-end learning problem, the model can dynamically discover the optimal interpolation schedule during optimization.

Note, that
Equation

164
only holds if the expectation is taken with respect to
\Pi^{*}
. However, when performing our proposed fixed-point iteration (
15
), we only have access to samples from
\Pi^{i}
. It is therefore unclear if a joint optimization (
169
) with samples from
\Pi^{i}
leads to variance reduction. We leave the numerical investigation for future work. For completeness, we provide a parameterization of the control variate
c
that avoids numerical instabilities at the boundaries
t=0
and
t=T
below.

While
Equation

169
assumes access to the true coupling
\Pi^{*}
, our fixed-point iteration (
15
) relies on samples from the intermediate distribution
\Pi^{i}
. The extent to which joint optimization preserves its variance-reduction properties under this distributional shift remains an open question, which we leave for future numerical study. For completeness, we detail below a parameterization of
c
designed to prevent numerical instabilities at the boundaries
t=0
and
t=T
.

Parameterization of the learned control variate.

A critical consideration when learning
c
is the numerical stability of the target drift
\xi^{c}
at the temporal boundaries. As defined in (
162
), the terms weighting the boundary score estimators are scaled by
(1-\gamma(t))^{-1}
and
\gamma(t)^{-1}
. Under standard interpolation schemes where
\gamma(0)=0
and
\gamma(T)=1
, these fractions introduce division-by-zero singularities at
t=0
and
t=T
, respectively. To prevent the empirical gradient from exploding, the control variate must strictly satisfy the boundary conditions
c(0)=0
and
c(T)=1
. This ensures the numerators vanish at the boundaries, allowing the limits to remain finite. To enforce this constraint by design while still retaining full expressivity, we parameterize the learnable schedule as

\displaystyle c^{\phi}(t)

\displaystyle=\gamma(t)+\gamma(t)(1-\gamma(t))\mathrm{NN}^{\phi}(t),

(170)

where
\mathrm{NN}^{\phi}(t)
is a neural network with learnable parameters
\phi
. In this formulation, the base term
\gamma(t)
provides a stable linear-equivalent baseline, while the multiplicative envelope
\gamma(t)(1-\gamma(t))
acts as a gating mechanism. It zeroes out the network’s contribution exactly at the boundaries, satisfying
c^{\phi}(0)=0
and
c^{\phi}(T)=1
. Substituting
c^{\phi}(t)
back into the coefficients of (
162
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{c^{\phi}(t)}{\gamma(t)}=1+(1-\gamma(t))\mathrm{NN}^{\phi}(t)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\frac{1-c^{\phi}(t)}{1-\gamma(t)}=1-\gamma(t)\mathrm{NN}^{\phi}(t),

(171)

and therefore (
162
) becomes

\displaystyle\sigma^{-1}(t)\xi^{\phi}(X,t)=

\displaystyle\left(1-\gamma(t)\mathrm{NN}^{\phi}(t)\right)\nabla_{X_{0}}\log\Pi^{\ast}_{0,T}(X_{0},X_{T})

(172)

\displaystyle+\left(1+(1-\gamma(t))\mathrm{NN}^{\phi}(t)\right)\,\nabla_{X_{T}}\log\Pi^{\ast}_{0,T}(X_{0},X_{T})-\nabla_{X_{t}}\log\mathbbm{P}_{t|0}(X_{t}|X_{0}).

(173)

Because both simplified coefficients are well-defined and bounded for all
t\in[0,T]
, this parameterization completely circumvents the boundary explosion, ensuring stable joint optimization of
u_{\theta}
and
c_{\phi}
.

C.3
Loss weighting and numerical stability

In this section, we analyze the variance properties of the matching objective and derive a weighting schedule to mitigate numerical instabilities near
t=0
. We introduce a time-dependent weighting function
\omega(t)>0
to the primary matching objective. This weighting balances the contribution of the loss over time without altering the optimal solution
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
at the critical points of the objective. The weighted loss is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u)=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\omega(t)\left\|u(X_{t},t)-\xi(X,t)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right].

(174)

The target vector field
\xi(X,t)
typically includes the score of the forward transition kernel
\nabla\log\mathbbm{P}_{t|0}(X_{t}|X_{0})
, which dominates the variance as
t\to 0
. For a general Gaussian reference process with variance schedule
\kappa(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)\mathrm{d}s
, the transition kernel is given by
\mathbbm{P}_{t|0}(X_{t}|X_{0})=\mathcal{N}(X_{t};X_{0},\kappa(t)I)
. Consequently, the scaled score term is

\displaystyle\sigma(t)\nabla_{X_{t}}\log\mathbbm{P}_{t|0}(X_{t}|X_{0})=\sigma(t)\frac{X_{0}-X_{t}}{\kappa(t)}.

(175)

Substituting the interpolant
X_{t}=\frac{\kappa(T)-\kappa(t)}{\kappa(T)}X_{0}+\frac{\kappa(t)}{\kappa(T)}X_{T}+\left(B_{\kappa(t)}-\frac{\kappa(t)}{\kappa(T)}B_{\kappa(T)}\right)
into the score expression allows us to isolate the stochastic component. Note that the stochastic part corresponds to a Brownian bridge with variance
\frac{\kappa(t)(\kappa(T)-\kappa(t))}{\kappa(T)}
which can be represented as
\sqrt{\frac{\kappa(t)(\kappa(T)-\kappa(t))}{\kappa(T)}}\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. The expansion of the score term yields

\displaystyle\sigma(t)\nabla_{X_{t}}\log\mathbbm{P}_{t|0}(X_{t}|X_{0})=\underbrace{\frac{\sigma(t)}{\kappa(T)}(X_{0}-X_{T})}_{\text{drift}}-\underbrace{\sigma(t)\sqrt{\frac{\kappa(T)-\kappa(t)}{\kappa(T)\kappa(t)}}\varepsilon}_{\text{noise}}.

(176)

We now analyze the variance of the noise term, denoted by
\Sigma_{\text{noise}}^{2}(t)
, as
t\to 0
. Since
\kappa(0)=0
, the term
\kappa(t)
in the denominator causes a singularity. Approximating
\kappa(T)-\kappa(t)\approx\kappa(T)
for small
t
, the variance behaves as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\text{noise}}^{2}(t)=\sigma^{2}(t)\frac{\kappa(T)-\kappa(t)}{\kappa(T)\kappa(t)}\approx\frac{\sigma^{2}(t)}{\kappa(t)}.

(177)

Assuming
\sigma(t)
is roughly constant near
t=0
,
\kappa(t)\approx\sigma^{2}t
, implying that the variance explodes as
\mathcal{O}(1/t)
. To enforce numerical stability, we select
\omega(t)
to counteract this variance, ensuring the effective loss contribution remains bounded. The optimal choice, often referred to as the
noise-prediction
weighting, is the inverse of the asymptotic variance factor:

\displaystyle\omega(t)=\frac{\kappa(t)}{\sigma^{2}(t)}.

(178)

Applying this weighting to the loss function neutralizes the singularity. By absorbing the weight into the norm, we obtain a numerically stable parameterization, namely

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\frac{\sqrt{\kappa(t)}}{\sigma(t)}u(X_{t},t)-\frac{\sqrt{\kappa(t)}}{\sigma(t)}\xi(X,t)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]

(179a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\widehat{u}(X_{t},t)-\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa(t)}}{\kappa(T)}(X_{0}-X_{T})-\sqrt{1-\frac{\kappa(t)}{\kappa(T)}}\varepsilon+\sqrt{\kappa(t)}\nabla\log\Pi^{*}_{t}(X_{t})\right)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right],

(179b)

with
\widehat{u}(x,t)=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa(t)}}{\sigma(t)}u(x,t)
. Here, the coefficient of the noise
\varepsilon
becomes
\sqrt{1-\kappa(t)/\kappa(T)}
, which approaches
1
as
t\to 0
, resulting in a stable, unit-variance objective throughout training.
However, while the weighting
\omega(t)
stabilizes training, it necessitates rescaling the drift of the forward SDE in (
1
) to recover the drift
u
.
Substituting
u(x,t)=\frac{\sigma(t)}{\sqrt{\kappa(t)}}\widehat{u}(x,t)
back into the forward process gives

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\frac{\sigma^{2}(t)}{\sqrt{\kappa(t)}}\widehat{u}(X_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t)\mathrm{d}B_{t}.

(180)

This formulation reveals a numerical challenge near the boundary
t=0
. Assuming
\sigma(t)\approx\sigma
is constant near the origin, we have
\kappa(t)\approx\sigma^{2}t
. Consequently, the effective drift coefficient scales as

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}(t)}{\sqrt{\kappa(t)}}\approx\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma\sqrt{t}}\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}.

(181)

This
t^{-1/2}
singularity implies that the drift velocity diverges as
t\to 0
. Standard fixed-step solvers (e.g., Euler-Maruyama) may exhibit instability or large discretization errors if evaluated strictly at
t=0
. In practice, this is mitigated by starting the integration at a small cutoff time, e.g.,
t=10^{-3}
.

C.4
Reference process

In this work, we use a reference measure
\mathbbm{P}
induced by scaled Brownian motion. The dynamics are governed by the driftless SDE
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sigma(t)\mathrm{d}B_{t}
, where
\sigma(t)
is a time-dependent noise schedule. To characterize the path properties, we define the cumulative variance schedule as
\kappa(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)\mathrm{d}s
. When conditioned on fixed boundary values
X_{0}=x_{0}
and
X_{T}=x_{T}
, the process admits a closed-form representation known as a stochastic interpolant
(
3
)
:

\displaystyle X_{t}=\frac{\kappa(T)-\kappa(t)}{\kappa(T)}x_{0}+\frac{\kappa(t)}{\kappa(T)}x_{T}+\left(B_{\kappa(t)}-\frac{\kappa(t)}{\kappa(T)}B_{\kappa(T)}\right).

(182)

Using
\gamma(t):=\frac{\kappa(t)}{\kappa(T)}
, we recover the simplified notation

\displaystyle X_{t}=(1-\gamma(t))x_{0}+\gamma(t)x_{T}+\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\gamma},

(183)

where
\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\gamma}
is a Gaussian process with mean zero and covariance
\kappa(T)\gamma(t)(1-\gamma(t))I
. This analytic form allows for the direct simulation of bridge states
X_{t}\sim\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}(\cdot|x_{0},x_{T})
without requiring numerical integration of the SDE. The relevant transition densities are given by

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}_{t|0,T}(x_{t}|x_{0},x_{T})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\big(x_{t}|(1-\gamma(t))x_{0}+\gamma(t)x_{T},\ \kappa(T)\gamma(t)(1-\gamma(t))I\big),

(184)

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}_{t|0}(x_{t}|x_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(x_{t}|x_{0},\ \kappa(t)I),

(185)

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}_{T|t}(x_{T}|x_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\left(x_{T}|x_{t},\ \kappa(T)\left(1-\gamma(t)\right)I\right).

(186)

Evaluation criteria.

The

metric is proposed by
6

(
8

24

. It uses the ground truth mixing weights
\pi_{i}
,

(190)

(191)

where we approximate the expectation in (
190
) using
2k
samples. Note that

where a value of 0 indicates that the model learned the same mixture weights as the target and therefore did not suffer from any mode-collapse.

to assess the accuracy of the density fitting by comparing empirical densities against the analytically available marginals. For a set of model samples

and target samples

P

. For each projection, we compute the 1D total variation distance between the resulting histograms:

(192)

In our experiments, we use
P=100
projections and
50
bins to estimate these 1D distributions.

model and target samples using the POT library

To measure the local backbone structural fidelity, we compared the probability distributions of the backbone dihedral angles,
\phi

where

\varepsilon=10^{-10}
is a small constant added for numerical stability. To account for relative free-energy differences rather than absolute values, each surface was zero-centered by subtracting its global minimum, i.e.,
\mathrm{F}^{\prime}_{i}(b)=\mathrm{F}_{i}(b)-\min_{b}\mathrm{F}_{i}(b).

To avoid noise from highly under-sampled, high-energy regions, we restricted our comparison to a valid subspace
\mathcal{M}_{i}
. A bin was included in
\mathcal{M}_{i}
if its probability in
either
the reference or the generated ensemble exceeded
10^{-4}
. The Root-Mean-Squared Error for residue
i
is then computed as

\mathrm{RMSE}_{\Delta\mathrm{F},i}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_{i}|}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{M}_{i}}\left(\mathrm{F}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{ref},i}(b)-\mathrm{F}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{gen},i}(b)\right)^{2}}.

(201)

The overall FES discrepancy is reported as the mean
\mathrm{RMSE}_{\Delta\mathrm{F},i}
across all residues.
To capture global conformational dynamics and long-range structural correlations, we evaluated the generated ensembles in a reduced, kinetically relevant latent space. We employed Time-lagged Independent Component Analysis (TICA), which identifies the slowest collective motions in a molecular system by finding projections that maximize the autocorrelation of the time-series MD data at a given lag time. We refer to
78
for a more detailed description.

Algorithm specifications.

Our implementation builds on the codebase by
56
(see
github.com/facebookresearch/wt-asbs
), where we removed the collective variables as we are interested in the performance without using prior knowledge. We use the same hyperparameter setting for ASBS and our method, as detailed below.

For the model architecture, the PaiNN architecture
(
72
)
was used, an E(3)-equivariant graph neural network, modified to include time-conditioning inputs and vector product layers
(
70
)
to break parity symmetry, effectively restricting the model to SE(3)-equivariance
(
49
)
. To improve training stability, equivariant layer normalization is applied. For ALA2 with
\eta=0
, we follow the setting of
(
56
)
and employ 4 layers, a hidden dimension of 128 and
250
diffusion steps, as we were facing numerical instabilities when using larger architectures. For
\eta=10
, we used 6 layers, a hidden dimension of 256, and
50
diffusion steps, training stably. For ALA4, we also use 6 layers, a hidden dimension of 256, and
50
diffusion steps. For the damped version we set
\eta=50
. A radial cutoff of
8.0\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolAngstrom}
is used for graph construction for all methods and systems. For all algorithms, we used
c=\gamma
when computing
\xi
.
The noise schedule is defined by the EDM variance exploding schedule
(
39
)
, i.e.,

\sigma(t)=\left[(1-t)\sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho}+t\sigma_{\min}^{1/\rho}\right]^{\rho},\\

(202)

with a minimum noise level
\sigma_{\min}=$0.001\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolAngstrom}$
, a maximum noise level
\sigma_{\max}=$6.0\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolAngstrom}$
, and an exponent
\rho=3.0
. The source distribution is modeled as a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,$1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolAngstrom}$^{2})
.
For optimization, the AdamW optimizer
(
53
)
with a weight decay of
10^{-3}
. We employ a cosine annealing learning rate schedule, decaying from an initial learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}
to a final learning rate of
3\times 10^{-5}
. To stabilize training, energy gradients are clipped at a maximum value of
100.0
.
The training utilizes a replay buffer with a capacity of 16,384 samples. At each outer step, we draw 2,048 samples from the buffer and perform
L=100
gradient updates. The diffusion process is integrated using the Euler-Maruyama method with 250 discretization steps.
For ALA2 and ALA4, we trained for
5
k and
8k
outer steps, respectively. For ASBS, we follow
(
56
)
and perform
500
corrector matching steps after every
1000
outer steps. Note that we do not count these corrector matching steps towards the outer iterations, meaning that we provide more computational budget to ASBS. For model selection, we always picked the last checkpoint.
