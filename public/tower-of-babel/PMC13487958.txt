Burden of polycystic ovary syndrome in low-and middle-income countries, 1990–2023: results from the global burden of disease study 2023
Authors: Binsong Liu, Md Harunor Rashid, Xiaoxu Bai, Ran An, Yujie Feng, Can Cui
Published: 2026

Background

As a chronic endocrine condition, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is complex and heterogeneous. This condition ranks among the principal etiologies of female infertility, with affected individuals facing markedly elevated susceptibility to obesity, insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and endometrial cancer [1, 2]. Women with PCOS also experience a markedly higher prevalence of anxiety and depression, which significantly impairs quality of life [3]. As it has been recognized as a general disorder, PCOS is recommended to renamed as polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS) recently, because PMOS is considered to be better for standing for the multisystem pathophysiologic characters of PCOS [4]. Consequently, lifelong management strategies for PCOS should extend beyond menstrual regulation and fertility restoration to include the prevention of metabolic disorders and cardiovascular diseases, which together impose a substantial global public health burden.

Global PCOS prevalence among reproductive-age women varies considerably, with estimates spanning 4% to 21% [5, 6]. Differences in methodological approaches, regional settings, and diagnostic standards largely account for this substantial variation [7]. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database, coordinated by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, provides a framework for systematic evaluation of disease burden at both global and regional levels. By integrating data across 204 countries and territories within 21 global regions, the GBD database facilitates standardized, comprehensive comparisons of PCOS prevalence and incidence. The Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) serves as an analytical instrument in GBD studies to explore how socioeconomic development correlates with health outcomes.

Important insights into PCOS burden at both global and regional levels have emerged from previous GBD-based investigations [8–10]. PCOS-related infertility showed significant growth between 1990 and 2021, paralleling the upward trend in PCOS prevalence, with Southeast Asia, South Asia, and East Asia recording the most pronounced increases [8]. The burden of PCOS in East Asia is anticipated to maintain its upward trajectory through 2031, according to current projections [9]. In parallel, age-standardized incidence rates (ASIRs) of PCOS-related conditions, including type 2 diabetes and uterine cancer, have also increased [11]. At the regional level, the High-income Asia Pacific region demonstrated the highest ASIR [10]. Japan exhibited the highest ASIR, whereas China showed a substantially higher age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) than both Japan and the global average [12]. Middle-SDI regions accounted for the largest share of PCOS cases. The documented positive association between SDI and PCOS prevalence reflects the significant role of socioeconomic factors in shaping PCOS epidemiology [13].

According to the Global Health Expenditure Database, low- and middle-income countries(LMICs) have per capita health expenditure substantially below levels observed in high-income countries. For example, health expenditure in low-income countries averages approximately US$43 per capita (GHED: https://apps.who.int/nha/database). Insufficient health investment may contribute to widespread underdiagnosis of PCOS, particularly in resource-limited settings.

In this study, we analyzed the possible factors related to the changes of prevalence, incidence, and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) of PCOS during the past three decades from a new sight and applying different statistics methods from previous research on PCOS burden according to GBD 2023. During the process, we quantified the prevalence, incidence, and DALYs of PCOS though Gross National Income (GNI) levels, assessed temporal trends (1990–2023) using estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs), evaluated socioeconomic disparities in disease burden through cross-country inequality analyses, quantified the contributions of population growth, aging, and epidemiological changes to variations in PCOS burden using decomposition analysis, and projected future burden trends through 2050. This is the first time to provided a comprehensive analysis of PCOS burden (1990–2023) in LMICs stratified by GNI. We hope that our results could provide new evidence for setting up appropriate PCOS-specific interventions and health policy development in future.

Methods

Overview

PCOS is an endocrine and metabolic disorder affecting women, characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovaries [14, 15]. Common clinical features include irregular menstruation, hirsutism, acne, and obesity [14]. For the GBD 2023 study, the case definition of PCOS was standardized according to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) criteria, which permit diagnosis through any of three recognized approaches: the National Institutes of Health (NIH) criteria, the Rotterdam criteria, or the Androgen Excess and PCOS Society (AE-PCOS Society) criteria [16–18].

This retrospective ecological study analyzed GBD 2023 data to evaluate PCOS burden in LMICs. Countries were classified according to the World Bank 2023 income categories (https://databank.worldbank.org), including low-income (GNI per capita ≤ USD 1145), lower-middle-income (USD 1146–4515), and upper-middle-income (USD 4516–14,005) groups. This classification enabled a systematic assessment of associations between national economic status and PCOS burden. Detailed descriptions of the GBD 2023 estimation framework have been published previously [19–22]. The publicly accessible Global Health Data Exchange served as the source for all data.

PCOS-attributable prevalence, incidence, and DALYs were assessed across 5-year age groups (10–54 years) stratified by World Bank income classification in 131 LMICs, including 26 low-income countries (LCs), 51 lower-middle-income countries (LMCs), and 54 upper-middle-income countries (UMCs). Additional stratification by GNI was performed. Analytical methods included temporal trend analysis (1990–2023), global choropleth mapping for spatial visualization, and inequality assessment using the slope index of inequality (SII) and concentration index (CI). The contributions of population growth, age structure, and epidemiological changes to burden variation were quantified through decomposition analyses. Future trends were projected using autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models.

All procedures complied with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) [23]. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1). All data were aggregated and anonymized, and no personally identifiable information was involved; therefore, ethics approval and informed consent were not required.

Statistical analysis

Both DALY rates and absolute DALY counts were used to quantify disease burden. DALY rates were standardized per 100,000 population, with absolute counts indicating total case numbers. All estimates are presented with 95% uncertainty intervals (UI), A threshold of P < 0.05 was adopted for statistical significance. Temporal trends from 1990 to 2023 were evaluated using EAPCs calculated from linear regression models. As a measure of average annual rate change over a defined period, EAPCs are extensively applied in GBD trend analyses [24]. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the associations between GNI per capita and the prevalence, incidence, and DALYs of PCOS in LMICs. Detailed descriptions of SII, CI, decomposition analysis, and ARIMA modeling are provided in the corresponding sections. R software (version 4.3.1) was used for all statistical analyses and visualizations.

Cross-country inequality analysis

Cross-country socioeconomic inequality in PCOS burden was quantified using SII and CI derived from country-level SDI rankings [25]. Countries and territories were ranked according to SDI and assigned a relative rank ranging from 0 to 1.

The SII was estimated using weighted linear regression to quantify the absolute difference in DALY rates across the SDI spectrum. Positive values indicate disproportionate burden among higher-SDI countries, whereas negative values indicate concentration among lower-SDI countries.

The CI was derived from concentration curves to evaluate relative socioeconomic inequality in DALY distribution [26]. CI values range from − 1 to + 1, with positive values denoting concentration of disease burden among higher-SDI countries and negative values among lower-SDI countries.

All inequality analyses included 131 countries and territories from the GBD 2023 study.

Decomposition analysis

The Das Gupta decomposition method was applied to quantify contributions of population growth, population aging, and epidemiological change to PCOS burden variations over 1990–2023 [27, 28]. This approach separates the overall change in DALYs into distinct components, thereby clarifying the relative influence of demographic and epidemiological factors. Decomposition analysis differs from conventional regression analyses by providing direct assessment of each determinant's contribution magnitude rather than focusing on associations.

ARIMA model prediction

The ARIMA model was used for time-series forecasting of PCOS burden. By combining autoregressive, differencing, and moving average components, this model captures temporal patterns and produces short-term predictions from historical data [29]. Within the ARIMA(p, d, q) framework, p indicates autoregressive term count, d indicates the differencing degree needed for stationarity, and q indicates moving average term count. Time series stationarity was assessed using the Kwiatkowski–Phillips–Schmidt–Shin test after differencing. Residual normality was examined using Q–Q plots. Optimal model selection was based on minimizing both the Akaike Information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information criterion (BIC). The Ljung–Box test was applied to assess residual independence. Residuals approximating white noise indicated adequate model fit and suitability for short-term prediction.

In 2023, UMCs exhibited the highest PCOS burden among LMICs, with prevalence rates of 1913.54 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 1341.34–2665.40) and DALY rates of 18.45 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 8.13–40.40) (Table 1). Age-specific analysis from 1990 to 2023 demonstrated a clear socioeconomic gradient in PCOS-related DALYs across all age groups, with UMCs showing the highest burden and LCs the lowest (Fig. 5A). In LMCs and UMCs, prevalence and DALYs remained relatively stable until approximately 50 years of age, followed by a marked decline. Cross-country health inequality analysis

Progressive widening of socioeconomic inequalities in PCOS burden occurred from 1990 to 2023. Countries with higher socioeconomic status consistently carried a disproportionate share of the burden. This trend was reflected by the slope index of inequality (SII), which increased from 6.40 (95% UI: 3.75–9.04) in 1990 to 10.44 (95% UI: 5.41–15.47) in 2023 (Fig. 5B (a)). A parallel increase in the concentration index (CI) for PCOS-related DALYs, from 0.16 to 0.28, indicated growing concentration of disease burden in more affluent settings (Fig. 5B (b)).

The relative contributions of population growth, population aging, and epidemiological changes to increasing PCOS burden in LMICs (1990–2023) were evaluated through decomposition analysis. Increases in prevalence, incidence, and DALYs were observed across all LMICs, with LMCs showing the most pronounced growth (Fig. 5C). In this group, the rise in DALYs was primarily attributable to population growth (56.75%), followed by epidemiological changes (40.53%), whereas population aging contributed minimally (2.73%) (Fig. 5C (c), Table 2).

ARIMA-based trend projection

ARIMA models were applied to project PCOS burden in LMICs from 2024 to 2050. All models demonstrated adequate fit, and residuals approximated white noise, as indicated by non-significant Ljung–Box test results (P > 0.05) (Table S4).

Projections indicate a substantial overall decline in PCOS burden across LMICs. A decline in prevalence rates from 1602.57 per 100,000 population in 2023 to 977.25 per 100,000 by 2050 is projected (Fig. 6A, Table S5). A decline in incidence rates from 55.97 per 100,000 in 2023 to 15.20 per 100,000 by 2050 is anticipated (Fig. 6B, Table S5). From 1990 to 2020, the DALYs rate rose from 10.09 to 15.93 per 100,000 population before beginning its decline. This downward trend is projected to continue, reaching an estimated 9.62 per 100,000 population by 2050 (Fig. 6C, Table S5).

Discussion

The first comprehensive assessment of PCOS burden across LMICs from 1990 to 2023 is presented in this study. In 2023, an estimated 67.91 million cases of PCOS occurred worldwide, of which 48.44 million were in LMICs. Among the 204 countries included in the GBD database, 131 were classified as LMICs, representing 64.22% of all countries yet accounting for 71.33% of global PCOS cases. This imbalance highlights a pronounced geographic concentration of the disease. The 131 LMICs included 26 LCs, 51 LMCs, and 54 UMCs. Most LCs were located in Africa, with the exception of North Korea, Afghanistan, Syria, and Yemen, and Africa also accounted for nearly half of all LMCs. In contrast, UMCs were more evenly distributed worldwide, with Asia and Africa together comprising approximately 43% of this group (Fig. 1).

The proportion of global PCOS cases occurring in LMICs increased substantially from 53.29% in 1990 (19.52 million of 36.63 million cases) to 71.33% in 2023 (48.44 million of 67.91 million cases), representing an increase of 18.04 percentage points over 33 years. Changes in population structure offer a partial explanation for this shift. Over the past three decades, the global population expanded from 5.299 billion to 8.062 billion, with approximately 78% of this growth occurring in LMICs, particularly in East and Southeast Asia (https://population.un.org/wpp/). Dietary transition likely contributed further. The critical role of carbohydrate quality in PCOS development and progression is supported by accumulating evidence [30, 31]. Diets characterized by high consumption of refined grains and low intake of fiber and whole grains have been shown to exacerbate PCOS risk [32]. Such dietary patterns, combined with widespread reliance on sugar as a major energy source, remain common in many LMICs.

Although PCOS incidence increased rapidly in LCs and LMCs, the rise in disease burden was not proportional to population growth alone. Economic development appears to be a stronger determinant, as regions with higher economic status, particularly UMCs, bore a disproportionately greater burden. One contributing factor is improved diagnostic capacity in LCs and LMCs, which has enabled the detection of previously unrecognized cases [33]. At the same time, healthcare systems in many of these countries continue to prioritize acute and life-threatening conditions over chronic metabolic disorders such as PCOS. This pattern reflects a broader public health challenge during socioeconomic transition, previously observed in Western countries and still evident in developing regions today.

The contrasting experiences of Equatorial Guinea and Albania illustrate the complex interaction between economic development, demography, and PCOS burden. Despite both countries advancing from lower-middle-income to upper-middle-income status, their PCOS incidence trends diverged in opposite directions. Equatorial Guinea, which experienced rapid economic growth driven by natural resources, showed a dramatic surge in PCOS burden, whereas Albania demonstrated a marked decline. Divergent population dynamics help explain this difference. Between 1990 and 2023, the population of Equatorial Guinea more than doubled, whereas Albania's population declined from 3.35 million to 2.76 million (https://population.un.org/wpp/). Population expansion in Equatorial Guinea substantially increased the size of the adolescent and reproductive-age female population, thereby amplifying the public health impact of PCOS, a leading cause of infertility. Additionally, dietary patterns shifted toward higher-calorie intake with socioeconomic development. According to the thrifty genotype hypothesis, such transitions promote insulin resistance, a central pathogenic mechanism of PCOS, among populations moving from scarcity to abundance [34]. Albania followed a distinctly different trajectory, maintaining a low birth rate for more than three decades and adhering to a Mediterranean dietary pattern prevalent in southeastern Europe.

Mexico provides an additional example of effective mitigation. Despite population growth of 3.85 million between 1990 and 2023 (https://population.un.org/wpp/), the burden of PCOS declined steadily. This trend may reflect proactive public health measures, including restrictions on the sale of sugary beverages to adolescents. Such findings suggest that targeted policy interventions can offset the adverse effects of economic growth and population expansion on PCOS burden [35].

Approximately 90% of incident PCOS cases in the GBD 2023 database were recorded within the 10–19-year age group. Caution is warranted in interpreting this finding, as it may partly reflect the age-group classification and modeling approach of the GBD framework rather than the true age at clinical onset. Nonetheless, the substantial burden observed during adolescence indicates that endocrine and metabolic abnormalities emerge early in the course of PCOS, reinforcing its recognition as a multisystem disorder beyond a purely reproductive condition. This view is consistent with the recent proposal to rename PCOS as PMOS, which emphasizes the need for lifelong management. Long-term metabolic disturbances associated with PCOS substantially increase the risk of cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome [36]. In most income groups, prevalence and DALYs remained relatively stable until approximately 50 years of age. Both indicators showed earlier decline in LCs, commencing around age 25. This pattern may be related to earlier marriage and childbearing in these settings and suggests that PCOS continues to be viewed primarily as a fertility-related condition in LCs, with insufficient attention to its long-term metabolic consequences.

Overall, socioeconomic development and geographic disparity exerted a strong influence on the global distribution of PCOS burden. Although LMICs showed the most rapid prevalence and incidence increases, the highest absolute burden (measured by incidence, prevalence, and DALYs) remained concentrated in high-income countries of Europe and North America [37]. In these settings, PCOS is widely recognized as a lifelong condition, and public health systems place greater emphasis on its metabolic sequelae. By contrast, most LMICs are located in Asia and Africa, where material deprivation and fertility-related concerns often remain dominant priorities. Enhanced diagnostic capacity and increased case detection in LMICs resulted from improved socioeconomic conditions, yet public health expenditure remained disproportionately focused on acute diseases, as the COVID-19 pandemic illustrated. The urgent need for public health strategy reform in LMICs is highlighted by these findings. In the coming decades, CKM syndrome is projected to become a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in these regions, with PCOS as a major contributor [36].

Projections indicate that prevalence, incidence, and DALYs of PCOS in LMICs will decline overall by 2050. A decline in prevalence rate from 1602.57 to 977.25 per 100,000 population is expected between 2023 and 2050, with incidence rate projected to decrease from 55.97 to 15.20 per 100,000 over the same period. The DALYs rate, which increased from 10.09 per 100,000 population in 1990 to a peak of 15.93 in 2020, is projected to decline to 9.62 per 100,000 by 2050 (Fig. 6C, Table S4). However, this downward trend is not uniform across income groups. DALYs are expected to decrease in LCs and UMCs but rise in LMCs, from 13.86 per 100,000 population in 2023 to 16.18 per 100,000 by 2050. This projected increase is driven largely by Asian countries, particularly in South Asia, where India contributes the greatest share of DALYs [9, 38]. Geographic, cultural, and lifestyle differences are likely key determinants of this heterogeneity.

The projected decline in PCOS burden warrants cautious interpretation. Historical data form the basis of GBD estimates, and the observed downturn occurred concurrently with the COVID-19 pandemic. Between 2020 and 2023, global public health systems prioritized the prevention and management of COVID-19, resulting in widespread disruption of screening, diagnosis, and long-term care for chronic conditions such as PCOS [39]. Continued surveillance is therefore essential to determine whether the projected trends reflect true epidemiological change or temporary healthcare disruption.

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first to comprehensively assess PCOS burden across LMICs from 1990 to 2023, stratified by GNI per capita. The analysis incorporated cross-country inequality assessment and decomposition of demographic and epidemiological drivers, and projected disease burden to 2050.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, despite efforts by the GBD 2023 study to incorporate all available data sources, limited surveillance infrastructure in some LMICs may have led to underestimation of the true PCOS burden. Second, heterogeneity in diagnostic criteria and clinical presentation across regions may have introduced bias into the recorded estimates. Third, the EAPC calculation assumes log-linear trends with constant proportional change over time, an assumption that may not hold during periods of healthcare disruption such as the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2023).

Conclusions

Substantial increases in PCOS prevalence, incidence, and DALYs occurred in LMICs from 1990 to 2023. Although approximately 71.33% of individuals with PCOS reside in LMICs, the highest measured burden remains concentrated in developed countries. The observed positive association between PCOS prevalence and gross national income indicates that socioeconomic factors contribute to PCOS epidemiology, although substantial heterogeneity remains unexplained. Effective prevention and control strategies must be tailored to age structure and economic context. A fundamental shift is required to recognize PCOS as a lifelong condition and to ensure that its metabolic consequences receive far greater priority in public health planning.
