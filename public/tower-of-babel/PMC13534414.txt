Acoustic deep learning for defect detection in aluminium wheel rims
Authors: Arkadiusz Rychlik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reliable detection of structural defects in aluminium wheel rims remains challenging in routine workshop diagnostics, where inspection is primarily based on visual and organoleptic assessment supported by operator experience. This study proposes RimNet, a deep-learning-based diagnostic framework for automated condition assessment of aluminium wheel rims using their acoustic response to a single impulse excitation. The method combines short-time Fourier transform representations with a convolutional neural network and incorporates domain filtering and hard-negative mining to improve the separability of defect-related conditions. The dataset comprised 930 labelled examples representing a broad spectrum of real-world rim states, including geometric deviations, material defects, and serviceable conditions. RimNet achieved strong classification performance, reaching 96.9% accuracy at the file level, while no wheel-level misclassifications were observed under the adopted evaluation protocol. Analysis of uncertainty measures—including prediction margins, entropy, Brier score, and energy-based indicators—revealed clear relationships between prediction stability and defect type. Misclassified cases were associated with reduced classification margins and increased uncertainty, particularly in samples influenced by geometric variability. The RimNet Predictor application enables real-time diagnostics and provides uncertainty estimates for individual rims. Because the proposed approach relies solely on microphone-based acquisition and does not require modifications to wheel balancing equipment, it can be readily integrated into workshop and service environments. These results demonstrate the potential of acoustic deep-learning-based diagnostics for structural health monitoring of lightweight mechanical components.

Introduction

Industrial motivation and problem background

Wheel rims are critical structural components affecting vehicle safety and performance. Their condition must be regularly assessed to ensure reliable operation.

To guarantee safe operation, the rim must satisfy strict geometric and strength requirements. Two sides of the rim are distinguished: the inner (IN) and outer (OUT). Correspondingly, axial runout (A), describing lateral deviations of the rim bead, and radial runout (R), describing deviations in the radial direction, are commonly used to quantify geometric accuracy.

Assessment of wheel rim condition is a mandatory part of vehicle inspections in Europe and worldwide. However, legal regulations specify only general safety requirements and do not define explicit numerical limits for radial or axial runout. In Poland, the legal basis is provided by national regulations1, while in Germany the applicable framework is defined by §29 StVZO and Annex VIII2. These regulations require the absence of cracks, severe deformation, significant corrosion, and improper mounting, but they do not specify quantitative tolerance values.

ECE regulation 1243 defines homologation requirements for passenger-car wheels and rims, focusing primarily on durability and fatigue resistance rather than geometric runout evaluation. More detailed geometric recommendations are provided by the European Tyre and Rim Technical Organisation (ETRTO)4, which defines compatibility and mounting dimensions for rims and tires. Based on these guidelines, typical runout tolerances of approximately 1.0–1.5 mm can be inferred for passenger-car rims. Manufacturer documentation provides similar limits, typically within the range of 1.0–1.5 mm for radial and lateral runout5,6, with some light-alloy rims requiring stricter tolerances of about 0.8 mm5,7. In contrast, steel rims may tolerate deviations of up to approximately 2.0 mm8.

In practical diagnostics, rim evaluation remains largely qualitative because the rim is usually assessed together with the mounted tire. Inspectors rely on visual inspection, observed wheel runout during rotation, and the overall condition of the tire and suspension components. As a result, only severe deformation cases can typically be detected during standard vehicle inspection procedures.

Consequently, current diagnostic practice suffers from limited sensitivity, subjectivity, and strong dependence on examiner experience. These limitations indicate a clear technological gap in rim condition assessment. Closing this gap requires diagnostic approaches that provide objective, quantitative, and repeatable evaluation of rim structural integrity.

Acoustic and vibration-based methods offer a promising solution. Impulse excitation of a rim generates characteristic acoustic signals that reflect both its geometric and material condition. Such signals can be acquired in a non-destructive and repeatable manner and subsequently used as input data for machine learning models. This enables the development of automated diagnostic systems capable of detecting subtle structural defects.

In the present work, these signals form the basis of a multi-stage diagnostic system employing deep neural networks for reliable classification of rim condition. Section "Methods" introduces the experimental dataset and presents the RimNet diagnostic pipeline used for automated analysis of acoustic impulse responses.

State of the art in wheel rim inspection

Inspection of wheel rim condition is an important element of vehicle diagnostics, as rim defects may significantly affect operational safety and durability of components subjected to dynamic loads. Existing research in this field includes modal and vibration-based diagnostics, acoustic approaches, imaging-based inspection techniques, and artificial intelligence–supported methods.

Modal and vibration-based methods

Vibration-based diagnostics of wheel rims has been investigated in several studies. Rychlik9 proposed a non-invasive method based on analysing natural-frequency spectra obtained during forced vibration excitation. Variations in modal parameters such as frequencies, amplitudes, and damping were linked to structural deformation, stiffness loss, cracking, and geometric deviations including radial and lateral runout.

Further developments were presented by Borecki et al.10, who combined vibration-spectrum analysis with artificial neural networks and integrated the diagnostic module with a commercial wheel balancer. This system enabled automatic classification of rims into operational categories such as “new”, “serviceable”, and “unserviceable”.

Subsequent work by Borecki, Rychlik, and co-authors8 incorporated damping-factor evaluation and dispersion metrics derived from experimental modal analysis, demonstrating high sensitivity to fatigue-related degradation of steel rims. In addition, Rychlik et al.11 applied self-organising maps (SOM) to analyse vibration-derived FFT features, enabling detection of subtle structural changes that may remain undetected in classical modal analysis.

Acoustic methods

Acoustic methods constitute an alternative diagnostic approach, particularly for lightweight aluminium rims. Rychlik12 demonstrated that acoustic parameters such as reverberation time, sound absorption coefficient, and acoustic energy can be used as diagnostic indicators of rim condition. Experimental data collected during fatigue testing were used to train a deep neural network capable of classifying rims originating from real service conditions.

Imaging-based methods: X-ray inspection and image analysis

Imaging-based inspection methods are mainly applied during the manufacturing stage of cast aluminium rims. Wu and Huang13 developed a system combining X-ray imaging with you only look once (YOLO)-based object detection to identify casting defects such as porosity, inclusions, and material discontinuities. Such approaches enable highly accurate detection of internal defects but require specialised equipment.

Design-supporting methods: optimisation and modal analysis

Research on rim dynamics has also been supported by computational modelling and optimisation. Topaloğlu et al.14 proposed surrogate models and neural networks for predicting structural parameters of rims, including mass, modal frequencies, and displacement fields, enabling optimisation of rim design with respect to weight and dynamic performance.

In contrast to traditional inspection methods such as visual assessment and geometric measurements, which rely on observable features and operator experience, the proposed RimNet framework provides an objective and data-driven approach to rim condition evaluation.

Compared to conventional vibration-based and acoustic methods, RimNet enables automated feature extraction and classification, reducing the dependence on expert interpretation and handcrafted signal features. Furthermore, unlike imaging-based techniques such as X-ray inspection, which require specialised equipment and controlled environments, the proposed method operates using a simple measurement setup based on impulse excitation and a microphone, making it suitable for practical workshop conditions.

The proposed RimNet framework extends existing acoustic and AI-based approaches by integrating domain-aware learning, uncertainty analysis, and multi-level decision fusion. This combination of simplicity, sensitivity, and automation positions RimNet as a practical solution for real-world diagnostic applications.

Overall, existing studies demonstrate substantial progress in wheel rim diagnostics; however, no universal solution currently enables fast, non-invasive, and fully automated assessment of wheel rim condition under typical workshop conditions. This limitation motivates the development of advanced diagnostic systems such as RimNet.

Deep learning in vibration and acoustic signal analysis

Deep learning methods have recently become widely applied in technical diagnostics and condition monitoring of mechanical systems. The increasing availability of sensing technologies, including accelerometers and microphones, combined with growing computational power, has enabled the use of neural networks for automatic extraction of diagnostically relevant features from vibration and acoustic signals. These signals constitute natural responses of mechanical structures to damage, material degradation, and fatigue processes.

Deep learning in vibration-based diagnostics

Vibration signals have long been used in the diagnostics of rotating machinery and thin-walled structures. Classical approaches relied on manually engineered features such as Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) spectra, envelopes, cepstrum coefficients, or statistical descriptors. In contrast, deep learning models enable end-to-end analysis directly from raw signals or time–frequency representations. Contemporary studies indicate that convolutional neural networks (CNN), long short-term memory (LSTM) and gated recurrent unit (GRU) networks, and hybrid architectures dominate modern vibration diagnostics and demonstrate high effectiveness in detecting weak faults in noisy environments22.

Another important research direction is transfer learning and domain adaptation, which allow effective model training when only limited or imbalanced datasets are available. For example, Zhao et al. 23 demonstrated that transfer learning significantly improves diagnostic performance in small-data scenarios typical of industrial applications. Autoencoder-based approaches have also been proposed for anomaly detection without requiring labelled fault data. Pawlik et al. 24 introduced the AE-FIT autoencoder-based method, in which an autoencoder trained on healthy data generated interpretable spectra enabling the identification of typical gearbox faults.

Deep learning in acoustic signal analysis

Acoustic sensing represents an attractive alternative when the use of contact sensors is difficult or impractical. Microphones enable simple and non-invasive measurements while still capturing diagnostically relevant information about structural condition. Safaei et al. 25 applied the continuous wavelet transform to convert acoustic signals into time–frequency representations classified using CNNs, achieving high diagnostic accuracy. Similar results were reported by Yurdakul and Taşdemir 26, who used DenseNet applied to mel-spectrograms and demonstrated high robustness to noise.

In addition to time–frequency representations, cepstral features derived from speech processing have also been successfully applied in structural health monitoring. In particular, mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) have been shown to effectively capture damage-induced changes in structural responses 16. Furthermore, enhanced cepstral analysis techniques based on nonlinearly spaced filterbanks have been investigated to improve sensitivity to structural dynamic behaviour and damage detection 27.

Data augmentation and generative models are increasingly used to improve model generalisation under varying acoustic conditions. For example, Cycle-GAN-based approaches have been proposed to enhance training datasets in acoustic diagnostics28. In addition, acoustic sensing has been applied in mobile and vehicle-based diagnostics, where Jagatheesaperumal et al. 29 demonstrated classification of road surface types based on wheel-area acoustic signals. Acoustic emission techniques are also widely used for detecting micro-cracks and structural damage30–33. In contrast to classical AE-based approaches, the present work uses broadband acoustic signatures generated by impulsive excitation, which enables diagnostics using only a microphone.

Decision fusion and reliability of deep learning diagnostics

In many industrial diagnostic applications, multiple measurements are collected from a single object at different locations or time instants. Such scenarios naturally lead to modelling within the multi-instance learning (MIL) framework. Tseng et al. 34 demonstrated that MIL improves classification performance in acoustic event analysis when combined with late fusion of CNN-extracted features.

Decision fusion techniques further improve robustness of diagnostic systems. Lin et al. 35 applied evidence-based fusion methods to ultrasonic diagnostics, demonstrating improved detection of subtle structural damage. Probability-based fusion, in which object-level decisions are derived from aggregated instance-level predictions, is particularly effective in handling signal variability and measurement noise.

Another important aspect of practical diagnostic systems is classifier calibration. Deep learning models often produce overconfident predictions, which may reduce reliability in safety-critical applications. Calibration techniques such as temperature scaling and expected calibration error analysis improve agreement between predicted probabilities and the true class distribution36. Improving model robustness and reliability under varying operating conditions therefore remains a key research direction in diagnostic signal analysis.

Overall, the literature indicates that deep learning has become a leading tool in vibration and acoustic diagnostics. Major research trends include the transition from manual feature engineering to end-to-end learning, the development of transfer learning and domain adaptation techniques, the use of multimodal data fusion, and increasing attention to reliability and robustness of diagnostic models under real operating conditions.

Methods

Materials

The objects of this study were aluminium automotive wheel rims collected during seasonal tyre replacement at a specialised tyre service station. Data acquisition was conducted over seven consecutive tyre-replacement seasons (summer and winter) between 2022 and 2025. This approach enabled the collection of a diverse population of rims with respect to age, operational mileage, and degree of material wear.

In total, 930 acoustic recordings of individual wheel rims were obtained. The rims originated from multiple manufacturers and covered a size range of 13–19 inches. Acoustic diagnostic features (ADF) method

Acoustic signal acquisition was performed using a proprietary experimental setup referred to as ADF (acoustic diagnostic features). The ADF method enables simultaneous identification of acoustic features and geometric parameters of wheel rims, allowing a comprehensive assessment of their technical condition. The same setup was used for all measurements in this study.

A general view and a schematic diagram of the experimental stand are shown in Fig. 1.Fig. 1General view and schematic diagram of the ADF diagnostic setup for identifying acoustic and geometric features of a wheel rim: (1) tested wheel rim, (2) shaft with a self-centering chuck, (3) RG-50 microphone, (4) vibration exciter, (5) sensor head for axial and radial runout measurement.

General view and schematic diagram of the ADF diagnostic setup for identifying acoustic and geometric features of a wheel rim: (1) tested wheel rim, (2) shaft with a self-centering chuck, (3) RG-50 microphone, (4) vibration exciter, (5) sensor head for axial and radial runout measurement.

The tested rim (1) was mounted on a vertical shaft equipped with a self-centering chuck (2), ensuring axial alignment with the measurement system and stable gravitational clamping against the hub surface. This configuration minimized positioning errors and ensured repeatable measurement conditions.

Excitation was introduced using an impulsive gravitational exciter (4), which impacted the lower rim flange in a tangential direction relative to the wheel-disc plane. The direction of excitation remained perpendicular to the microphone axis, enabling acquisition of the axial acoustic response of the structure. During each measurement the rim remained stationary.

The acoustic response was recorded using a free-field ROGA RG-50 microphone (3) positioned perpendicularly to the mounting shaft axis. The microphone captured sound-pressure waveforms corresponding to the resonant response of the rim.

After acoustic acquisition, bead runout was measured using a non-contact laser displacement sensor (Keyence LV-7080) (5). Measurements were performed in two planes—axial (A) and radial (R)—for both the inner (IN) and outer (OUT) rim sides. Maximum runout values were determined relative to the centring bore with an accuracy of ± 0.01 mm.

The ROGA RG-50 microphone provides a flat amplitude response within 20 Hz–4 kHz (± 1.0 dB) and 4 kHz–20 kHz (± 1.5 dB). Signals were sampled at 8 kHz, providing full coverage of the frequency range up to 4 kHz. Since rim resonance frequencies did not exceed 500 Hz, this sampling rate significantly exceeded the requirements of the Shannon criterion.

The measurement chain was interfaced with an NI USB-6210 data acquisition card (National Instruments). Prior to each measurement series, the system was calibrated using an acoustic calibrator generating 94 dB SPL at 1 kHz.

The exciter delivered an impact energy of 1 ± 0.1 J, determined by the hammer mass \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_m = 0.425$$\end{document} kg and free-fall height \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_m = 0.240$$\end{document} m.

Signal acquisition, processing, visualisation, and archiving were implemented in MATLAB. Dedicated scripts automatically detected the acoustic response and stored the waveform in .wav format together with metadata encoded in the filename (see Section "Acoustic diagnostic features (ADF) method").

ADF measurement procedure

Acoustic and geometric measurements were performed according to a predefined experimental protocol. Typically, four rims originating from a single vehicle were examined during seasonal tyre replacement.

First, basic rim information was recorded, including vehicle make and model, rim production year, operational mileage, and wheel position (FL/FR/RL/RR). This information was stored as metadata associated with each measurement file.

Each rim then underwent visual inspection to detect cracks, repair traces, welding marks, material loss, or visible deformation. Rims without visible defects were labelled VC (visual compliance), whereas rims exhibiting surface defects or cracks were labelled VD (visual defect).

Next, the rim was mounted in the ADF self-centering chuck and geometric runout measurements were performed. Axial (A) and radial (R) runout were measured for both the inner (IN) and outer (OUT) sides of the rim bead relative to the centring bore. Rims were classified as GC (geometric compliance) for runout values <1.25 mm and GD (geometric defect) for values ≥ 1.25 mm. The runout values were stored in the metadata in the order A_IN, R_IN, A_OUT, R_OUT.

Acoustic measurements were then performed by impulsive excitation of the rim and recording of the resulting acoustic response using the RG-50 microphone. Each waveform was saved in .wav format together with metadata encoded in the filename using the following structure:

vehicle_brand_model_year_mileage_VC/VD_GC/GD_AIN_RIN_AOUT_ROUT_FL/FR/RL/RR.

For statistical analysis, each rim was measured five times. After each measurement the rim was removed from the chuck, remounted, and re-excited. This procedure enabled evaluation of measurement repeatability and mounting-related variability.

For routine diagnostic measurements the protocol was simplified: the acoustic response was recorded twice without removing the rim from the chuck, reflecting real workshop conditions during seasonal tyre replacement.

During acquisition, a small number of rims exhibited clearly atypical acoustic responses. Such cases occurred in 10 out of 930 measurements (approximately 1.0%). These recordings were labelled as X (Ambiguity String) and stored in the dataset but excluded from neural-network training due to the lack of unambiguous material or geometric interpretation. A detailed analysis of these cases is presented in Section "Representative acoustic signatures".

Signal preprocessing

The acoustic signals acquired using the ADF setup were processed using a standardized preprocessing pipeline designed to ensure comparability between measurements and to isolate the most representative resonant responses. The algorithm was implemented in MATLAB and applied uniformly to all recordings. The general structure of the preprocessing procedure is illustrated in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Block diagram of the acoustic preprocessing procedure applied to wheel-rim signals prior to RimNet classification.

Block diagram of the acoustic preprocessing procedure applied to wheel-rim signals prior to RimNet classification.

After loading each signal, detrending was performed by subtracting the mean value, followed by normalization with respect to the maximum amplitude. This step removed the DC component and ensured a consistent amplitude scale for further analysis.

To identify individual resonant responses, the signal envelope was computed using the Hilbert transform and smoothed with a 60 ms moving window. Local maxima were detected using adaptive thresholds and a minimum inter-peak distance criterion. For each maximum, the corresponding high-energy segment containing a complete resonant response was extracted, producing a set of non-overlapping signal fragments.

Typically, four consecutive impact responses were recorded during a single measurement session. A segmentation procedure selected up to four of the longest and most stable fragments, which were treated as technically independent signal segments.

During RimNet training, only one representative segment per rim—the longest and least distorted fragment—was used as the input instance. The remaining segments were used exclusively for auxiliary analyses, including repeatability assessment, spectral comparison, and data-quality verification.

All selected segments were cropped or zero-padded to a fixed duration of 2.5 s and resampled to 8192 Hz, ensuring a uniform input representation for the neural network while preserving the dominant acoustic modes of the rim.

The processed segments were stored as .wav files with metadata encoded in the filename. In the final RimNet v3s-X dataset, a single acoustic segment per rim was used for training and evaluation, whereas additional repetitions served only for statistical analysis of measurement repeatability.

Proposed method: RimNet diagnostic pipeline

General concept of the diagnostic system

The RimNet diagnostic system performs a two-stage assessment of wheel-rim condition using impact-excited acoustic signals. At the level of a single recording (diagnostic instance), a convolutional neural network (CNN) estimates the probability of a material defect. These predictions are subsequently aggregated at the rim level using weighted probabilistic fusion (soft fusion), producing a stable final decision regarding the material condition of the rim.

These predictions are aggregated at the rim level using probabilistic fusion, and combined with geometric assessment to produce the final diagnostic decision.

Conceptual overview of the RimNet diagnostic pipeline. Acoustic signals generated by impulse excitation are transformed into time–frequency representations and analysed using a convolutional neural network (CNN) to estimate file-level defect probability. These predictions are aggregated at the rim level using probabilistic fusion, and combined with geometric assessment to produce the final diagnostic decision.

The acoustic diagnosis is combined with an independently determined geometric assessment (GC/GD), resulting in a three-state output: serviceable rim (OK), rim with a material defect (BAD), or rim with a geometric defect (GD).

To improve robustness, two additional mechanisms were introduced during training: (i) domain filtering, which excludes geometrically non-compliant recordings (class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {GD}$$\end{document}) and acoustically ambiguous samples (class X), and (ii) hard-negative mining, which selectively incorporates spectrally atypical but geometrically compliant rims into the negative class.

From a diagnostic perspective, it is important to note that the CNN model is trained primarily to detect material-related defects (VD), which produce strong and localized changes in the acoustic response. In contrast, geometric defects (GD), such as runout or deformation, affect the global shape of the rim and typically result in more subtle and distributed spectral variations.

As a consequence, GD cases are excluded from supervised training and can be interpreted as out-of-distribution or borderline samples with respect to the learned decision boundary. In the operational setting, however, all non-compliant cases, including GD, are treated as defective, and their presence is resolved through the integration of geometric assessment and uncertainty analysis.

This distinction resolves the apparent discrepancy between the training objective and the broader diagnostic interpretation used in practice.

Input representation of the acoustic signal

Each diagnostic instance corresponds to a single acoustic response obtained after impact excitation and preprocessing (signal duration 2.5 s, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{\textrm{s}} = 8192$$\end{document} Hz). Time-domain signals are converted into time–frequency representations in the form of log-mel spectrograms used as CNN inputs.

Spectrograms were generated using a Hanning window of 1024 samples with 75% overlap (hop size 256), a 1024-point FFT, and 64 mel frequency bands covering the informative frequency range of the rim response. The resulting power spectra were transformed to the logarithmic scale and globally normalised by mean subtraction and standard deviation scaling.

Each diagnostic instance is represented as a single-channel image (frequency × time × 1) and passed directly to the network.

RimNet network architecture

The core of the proposed method is a two-dimensional convolutional neural network designed for log-mel spectrogram analysis (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Schematic architecture of the RimNet network.

Schematic architecture of the RimNet network.

The network consists of three convolution–normalisation blocks followed by max-pooling layers, enabling extraction of progressively more complex time–frequency features while maintaining a compact number of parameters. This design reduces overfitting and improves generalisation across rims of different sizes and manufacturers. The final fully connected layer, regularised with dropout, outputs the confidence estimate of a material defect.

Training procedure and hard-negative mining

Domain filtering Before training the classifier, recordings dominated by geometric deviations or acoustically ambiguous responses were removed. Rims belonging to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {GD}$$\end{document} class and X-class recordings exhibit spectral variability primarily related to geometric distortion rather than material integrity. Removing these samples reduces intra-class variance and improves separation between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {OK}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {BAD}$$\end{document} rims.

Hard-negative mining Within the compliant subset, some rims exhibit atypical acoustic responses that partially resemble defective cases. These hard negatives are identified during training as samples either misclassified as positive or assigned high defect probability. They are then reintroduced into the negative class to sharpen the decision boundary and reduce false-positive detections.

Probability fusion and decision thresholds

The RimNet framework operates at two decision levels: the file level and the wheel level. For each acoustic recording, the convolutional neural network produces the probability of a material defect, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\textrm{file}} = p(\text {BAD})$$\end{document}.

In this study, Softmax outputs are interpreted as confidence estimates rather than calibrated statistical probabilities, since no explicit probability calibration procedure was applied.

When multiple recordings of the same rim are available, the individual predictions are aggregated using probabilistic fusion. The final rim-level probability is computed as the arithmetic mean of file-level probabilities:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\textrm{wheel}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum _{i=1}^{N} p_{\textrm{file},i},$$\end{document}where N denotes the number of acoustic recordings associated with the same rim.

The final classification is obtained by comparing the aggregated probability with a calibrated decision threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{\textrm{wheel}}$$\end{document}. If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\textrm{wheel}} \ge \tau _{\textrm{wheel}}$$\end{document}, the rim is classified as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {BAD}$$\end{document}; otherwise it is classified as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {OK}$$\end{document}.

The threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{\textrm{wheel}}$$\end{document} was selected on the validation set to maximise the F1 score of the defect class. This calibration step improves robustness to variability of individual impact recordings and stabilises the final rim-level decision.

In the final RimNet v3s-X configuration used in this study, a single representative acoustic recording per rim was used for classification. Nevertheless, the proposed probabilistic fusion framework allows straightforward extension to multi-instance diagnostic scenarios involving multiple impacts per rim.

Results and discussion

Evaluation protocol

The performance of the proposed RimNet framework was evaluated at multiple levels to reflect both the training setup and the intended operational use.

At the file level, the classifier was evaluated on a held-out test set consisting of individual acoustic recordings. Each recording corresponds to a single impact response acquired from a given rim. The dataset was split strictly at the rim level using unique WheelID identifiers to prevent information leakage between training, validation, and test sets.

In addition to file-level evaluation, aggregated predictions were analysed at the wheel level. In this setting, multiple recordings associated with the same rim are combined using probabilistic fusion to obtain a more stable and reliable diagnostic decision. This reflects the practical scenario in which multiple measurements may be available for a single wheel during inspection.

Furthermore, a full-dataset inference mode was considered to assess the behaviour of the trained model across the entire dataset, including samples excluded during supervised training (e.g., GD and X cases). This analysis provides insight into the generalisation capability of the model beyond the strictly defined training domain.

These evaluation modes are complementary and are reported separately throughout the manuscript to ensure clarity and consistency of interpretation.

These distinctions are essential, as the reported performance metrics correspond to different evaluation settings and are not directly comparable.

Such diversity enables reliable evaluation of the diagnostic system under realistic workshop conditions. The dataset reflects realistic workshop conditions rather than controlled laboratory experiments.

Repeatability of the acoustic signal for a single rim

To verify that acoustic signals obtained using the ADF (Acoustic Diagnostic Features) method are repeatable and not dominated by random variations caused by rim mounting or manual impulse excitation, a repeatability study was conducted for a reference rim. Four independent acoustic recordings (r1–r4) were acquired. After each measurement, the rim was removed from the fixture, remounted, and excited again using a new mechanical impulse.

The resulting signals were analysed both qualitatively and quantitatively, including comparisons of signal energy, Hilbert envelopes, and frequency-domain characteristics.

Basic signal quality metrics

After time alignment (delay compensation and resampling to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{\textrm{s}} = 8192$$\end{document} Hz), basic signal quality metrics were calculated. The analysed recordings exhibited inter-recording delays of 0–3 ms, RMS values in the range 0.1026–0.1498, and an energy coefficient of variation of approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CV \approx 16\%$$\end{document}. The peak amplitude remained constant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {PEAK} = 0.999$$\end{document}), while spectral centroid locations were nearly identical (4095.8–4095.9 Hz).

The observed RMS variability results primarily from differences in manual excitation energy, whereas the modal structure of the acoustic response remains stable.

Similarity of time-domain signals and envelopes

Similarity between recordings was evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients. For the raw time-domain signals, correlations ranged from r = 0.80 to 0.91 (mean r = 0.855). For the Hilbert envelopes, correlations were significantly higher, ranging from r = 0.956 to 0.987 (mean r = 0.966).

These results indicate a highly repeatable modal acoustic response of the rim and confirm that variations in rim positioning or excitation force do not significantly affect the underlying resonant structure.

Frequency-domain spectral agreement

Spectral similarity was further evaluated using cosine similarity, a common metric in time–frequency signal analysis. Cosine similarity values range from 0.946 to 0.967, with a mean value of 0.955. Such high similarity indicates that the dominant resonant frequencies and their relative amplitudes remain practically unchanged between measurements. In modal diagnostics, values above 0.90 are generally interpreted as strong evidence of spectral stability43.

Conclusions from the repeatability analysis

The results confirm that the acoustic response of a wheel rim remains highly repeatable even under quasi-workshop measurement conditions involving manual excitation and repeated mounting. Both envelope correlation and spectral similarity demonstrate the stability of the rim’s modal response, which constitutes a key prerequisite for the ADF measurement approach and the RimNet diagnostic pipeline.

These findings justify the use of normalised time–frequency representations as network inputs and provide a methodological basis for further analysis of the full dataset consisting of 930 recordings.

Analysis of X-type cases (ambiguity)

Cases marked as X (ambiguity class) represent a specific group of wheel rims for which the acoustic response differs audibly from other rims originating from the same vehicle, despite the absence of clear visual or geometric indicators of damage. In the analysed dataset, 10 such cases were identified among 930 rims (approximately 1.0%). These rims were characterised by a reduced resonant tone, poorly defined modal response, and atypical decay of acoustic energy.

To verify whether X-type rims constitute an acoustically distinct category, a comparative analysis was performed for one such rim and the remaining three rims from the same vehicle (X–FL, FR, RL, RR). The results were compared with a reference set of nominal rims obtained from another vehicle. Spectral and envelope similarity metrics

Similarity analysis was performed using three complementary metrics: correlation of the time-domain signals, correlation of Hilbert envelopes, and cosine similarity of amplitude spectra.

For nominal rims, spectral similarity typically exceeds 0.94–0.97 (Section "Frequency-domain spectral agreement"). In the analysed X-type case, considerably lower values were obtained: spectral similarity between non-X rims ranged from 0.726 to 0.735, while similarity between the X-type rim and the remaining rims was only 0.635–0.660. Similar discrepancies were observed for envelope correlation (0.594–0.852) and time-domain signal correlation (0.181–0.359), indicating a substantial difference in modal structure.

Modal and diagnostic interpretation

According to established modal-analysis criteria43, spectral similarity values below approximately 0.80 typically indicate significant structural changes such as stiffness loss, boundary-condition variations, material damage, or increased damping. The obtained results therefore confirm that X-type rims exhibit pronounced acoustic anomalies that distinguish them clearly from the remaining rims of the same vehicle.

Justification for excluding the X class from RimNet training

The presented analysis justifies excluding X-type samples from the RimNet training dataset. Their strongly deviating spectral characteristics could introduce noise into the feature space and prevent reliable assignment to either the “material defect” or “no defect” class. Furthermore, the lack of additional diagnostic evidence (e.g., X-ray inspection or Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM) service history) makes it impossible to assign a reliable ground-truth label.

Consequently, X-type rims are analysed only as case-study examples and are excluded from the supervised training process. This prevents blurring of the decision boundary between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {OK}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {BAD}$$\end{document} classes and improves stability of the trained classifier.

Impact of domain filtering and hard-negative mining

The performance of the RimNet classifier is strongly influenced by the structure of the training dataset. Two components of the proposed pipeline—domain filtering (DF) and hard-negative mining (HNM)—play a key role in stabilising the feature space and improving separability between the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {OK}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {BAD}$$\end{document} classes. Their influence is further analysed in the ablation study presented in Section "Ablation study of RimNet components".

Domain filtering

Domain filtering removes recordings associated with pronounced geometric deviations (GD) and acoustically ambiguous cases (X-type). As discussed in Sections "Representative acoustic signatures" and "Repeatability of the acoustic signal for a single rim", such recordings introduce spectral variability unrelated to material defects and may destabilise the training process.

Applying DF results in a more compact representation of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {OK}$$\end{document} class and reduces false alarms at the file level. In particular, the average values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\textrm{file}}$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {OK}$$\end{document} rims shift towards zero while their variance decreases. This leads to more consistent predictions at the wheel level, where fewer unstable file-level outputs influence the fused probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\textrm{wheel}}$$\end{document}.

Importantly, domain filtering does not remove any recordings corresponding to material-defect cases; it only eliminates variability caused by geometric deviations or measurement artefacts.

Hard-negative mining

Hard-negative mining complements domain filtering by explicitly introducing the most challenging \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {OK}$$\end{document} recordings during training. These include rims with atypical acoustic responses or irregular excitation conditions that could otherwise be misclassified as defective.

By repeatedly exposing the network to such difficult negative examples, HNM encourages the model to learn sharper decision boundaries. As shown in the ablation study, this mechanism reduces high-probability outliers within the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {OK}$$\end{document} class and improves precision and F1-score for the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {BAD}$$\end{document} class.

Effect of DF and HNM on network performance

The combined use of domain filtering and hard-negative mining significantly improves prediction stability. Without these mechanisms, the model exhibits larger fluctuations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\textrm{file}}$$\end{document} for the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {OK}$$\end{document} class and occasional false alarms. After applying DF and HNM, the class distributions become clearly separated and the calibrated threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{\textrm{wheel}}$$\end{document} lies within a distinct gap between the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {OK}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {BAD}$$\end{document} classes (Fig. 8).

This improvement is also reflected in ROC analysis: domain filtering removes acoustically noisy samples, while hard-negative mining increases the separation margin between classes. The resulting wheel-level AUC of 1.00 confirms the effectiveness of both mechanisms.

Significance for practical diagnostics

Domain filtering and hard-negative mining encode practical knowledge derived from real-world rim inspection. Domain filtering prevents geometric deviations from being interpreted as material defects, whereas HNM improves recognition of rims with unusual yet acceptable acoustic responses.

Together, these mechanisms increase the robustness and interpretability of RimNet under industrial conditions, where variability between rims and measurement conditions is unavoidable.

RimNet Predictor and uncertainty analysis of classification

After training the RimNet classifier in the binary configuration (OK/BAD), a lightweight diagnostic application termed RimNet Predictor was developed to enable inference under conditions similar to real workshop operation. The application loads the trained model (rimnet_3st_X_binary_*.mat), performs preprocessing identical to the training stage (ADF segmentation, normalisation, feature extraction), and treats each .wav recording as an unknown case.

For each recording the system computes class probabilities\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Score}_{\text {OK}}, \quad \text {Score}_{\text {BAD}}, \quad \text {Score}_{\text {OK}} + \text {Score}_{\text {BAD}} = 1,$$\end{document}and assigns the final label \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {PredLabel} \in \{\text {OK},\text {BAD}\}$$\end{document} using the argmax rule. Additionally, several uncertainty indicators are calculated, including prediction entropy, normalised margin, Brier score, and the corresponding logit value z.

To evaluate the behaviour of the system in inference mode, the predictor was executed on the complete dataset of 930 ADF recordings. Predicted labels were compared with reference labels recovered from file names, and the associated uncertainty measures were subsequently analysed.

The uncertainty analysis is based on several complementary measures describing prediction confidence and decision stability.

Prediction entropy is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H(p) = - \sum _{i} p_i \log p_i$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_i$$\end{document} denotes the predicted class probability. Higher entropy values indicate greater uncertainty of the classifier.

The decision margin is computed as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m = p_{\max } - p_{\text {second}}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\max }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\text {second}}$$\end{document} denote the two highest class probabilities. Lower margin values correspond to predictions closer to the decision boundary.

The Brier score is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$BS = \frac{1}{N}\sum _{i=1}^{N}(p_i - y_i)^2$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_i$$\end{document} is the predicted probability and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_i$$\end{document} is the reference label. Lower Brier scores indicate better agreement between predictions and observed labels.

The adopted uncertainty metrics follow standard definitions commonly used in uncertainty-aware machine-learning analysis 44–46.

Summary of the case studies

The presented case studies confirm the practical applicability of RimNet under real workshop conditions. The system correctly identifies both serviceable rims and rims with material defects, while borderline cases are accompanied by elevated uncertainty measures that clearly indicate reduced decision reliability.

The results show that RimNet combines accurate classification with interpretable diagnostics. In addition to the final class label, the system provides auxiliary information such as entropy, decision margin, and Brier score, which facilitates the identification of ambiguous cases and supports informed decision-making in service practice.

The analysed examples also demonstrate that the proposed pipeline is robust to natural acoustic variability and that its predictions are driven primarily by defect-related acoustic signatures rather than by incidental fluctuations of measurement conditions. As a result, RimNet can operate as a practical service-support tool for rapid and objective assessment of wheel rims during routine inspection and seasonal tyre replacement.

Future work will focus on several directions. First, the current framework will be extended towards continuous assessment of rim condition, including regression-based estimation of geometric deformation and tracking of degradation trends over time. Second, transfer learning and continual learning strategies will be investigated to enable adaptive improvement of the model without full retraining. Third, additional calibration techniques, such as temperature scaling and expected calibration error analysis, will be incorporated to further improve prediction reliability and interpretability. Finally, the system will be extended towards operation with heterogeneous acquisition devices and towards automatic reporting for complete wheel sets in practical service environments.

In its current form, RimNet v3s-X operates as a binary OK/BAD classifier, while complete diagnostic assessment combines material-state prediction with an independent geometric evaluation, reflecting actual workshop procedures. Overall, the case studies confirm that RimNet provides a repeatable, objective, and operationally relevant diagnostic solution for aluminium wheel rims.

Ablation study of RimNet components

The contribution of the main components of the RimNet pipeline—domain filtering (DF), hard-negative mining (HNM), and calibration (CAL)—was analysed through a qualitative ablation study. Four model configurations were considered: (i) a baseline CNN operating on individual acoustic recordings, (ii) CNN with domain filtering (DF), (iii) CNN with DF and hard-negative mining (HNM), and (iv) the full RimNet pipeline including wheel-level calibration (CAL).

Baseline CNN

The baseline model provides reasonable discrimination between OK and BAD rims but exhibits high variability of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\textrm{file}}$$\end{document} within the OK class. In several cases, geometrically compliant rims receive elevated defect probabilities, indicating sensitivity to geometric variability rather than material anomalies. This behaviour leads to unstable wheel-level aggregation and increased false alarms.

Limitations and future work

Although RimNet demonstrates high diagnostic performance under the evaluated conditions, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the model operates in a single-instance learning regime and does not explicitly exploit the multi-instance structure of repeated impact measurements. Incorporating multi-instance learning (MIL) or attention-based aggregation could further improve robustness to impact-level variability.

Second, the present study focuses exclusively on acoustic signals and does not include multimodal baselines combining acoustics with imaging or geometric inspection. Integrating complementary sensing modalities could improve defect localisation and diagnostic interpretability.

Third, the ablation analysis presented in this work is primarily qualitative. A fully quantitative component-wise evaluation was not performed due to limitations in industrial data availability and acquisition constraints.

Finally, although the dataset reflects realistic service conditions, it originates from a limited number of operational environments. Future work will therefore focus on expanding the dataset, incorporating multimodal information, and investigating advanced fusion and calibration strategies for robust large-scale deployment.

The current study compares RimNet primarily with classical machine-learning approaches and does not include additional deep neural architectures such as ResNet, EfficientNet, or transformer-based models.

Quantitative benchmarking of all intermediate RimNet variants was not systematically preserved during iterative model development.

The reported results should be interpreted in the context of the analysed dataset and may not fully generalise to all possible rim types and operating conditions.

Conclusions

This work introduces RimNet, a complete acoustic diagnostic framework for aluminium wheel rims based on convolutional neural networks and uncertainty-aware prediction analysis. The proposed pipeline integrates impulse-based acoustic measurements, time–frequency representations, domain filtering, and hard-negative mining, enabling robust detection of material-related rim defects.

Experimental results demonstrate high classification performance both at the file level and at the aggregated wheel level, with clear separation between serviceable rims and rims exhibiting structural defects. The analysis of uncertainty indicators—including entropy, decision margins, Brier score, and logit values—shows that high model confidence strongly correlates with correct decisions, whereas borderline cases are characterised by increased uncertainty.

An important practical advantage of the proposed approach is its minimal hardware requirement. The system relies only on a microphone and a single mechanical impulse, which enables straightforward deployment in workshop environments without specialised laboratory equipment.

The presented results demonstrate the potential of acoustic-based deep learning approaches for practical rim diagnostics under real-world conditions. At the same time, it should be noted that the performance is influenced by dataset characteristics, including class distribution and the availability of representative defect cases.

Future work will focus on extending the dataset, improving robustness under varying measurement conditions, and further refining the integration of acoustic and geometric assessment to enhance diagnostic reliability.

The proposed approach offers a promising direction for the development of low-cost, non-invasive diagnostic tools suitable for deployment in workshop environments.
