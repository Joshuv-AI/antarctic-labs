The JR blood group system: from discovery to the clinic
Authors: Xiaohui Ma, Jing Zhong, Yongquan Chen, Zhen Zhao, Hongmei Zhang, Xiaoying Lv, Yan Zhang, Lingbo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In 2012, the International Society of Blood Transfusion (ISBT) formalized the JR blood group system (ISBT 032), with Jra (ISBT 032001) as its only antigen. Jra is located on a multipass membrane glycoprotein named ABCG2. ABCG2, or ATP-binding cassette subfamily G member 2, is also known as breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) or CD338. This glycoprotein participates in various physiological, biochemical and metabolic processes in the human body. It is encoded by the ABCG2 gene, located on chromosome 4. In recent years, the ABCG2 gene variants have been gradually unraveled through the deeper study of the JR blood group system. Meanwhile, the JR blood group system is clinically important, being associated with fetal and neonatal anemia, hydrops fetalis, neonatal jaundice, hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN), and hemolytic transfusion reaction (HTR). Furthermore, its carrier molecule, ABCG2, is associated with hyperuricemia/gout and mirror syndrome. In addition, ABCG2 plays an important regulatory role in a variety of physiological and pathological processes, such as the regulation of uric acid metabolism, the maintenance of folate and porphyrin homeostasis, the regulation of blood-brain barrier substance exchange, and the regulation of anti-tumor drug transport and drug resistance. This study reviews the distributional characteristics, molecular biological features, immunological features and clinical significance of the JR blood group system and ABCG2.

Introduction

Currently, the international society of blood transfusion (ISBT) has recognized a total of 48 blood group systems containing 371 red blood cell antigens determined by 53 genes, with the latest blood group system being PIGZ (Duval et al., 2025). Of these, the Jra antigen was first reported by Stroup & Macllroy (1970), and after the big breakthrough happened relatively recently with the advancements of next-generation sequencing (NGS) and modern technologies, it was formalized by ISBT in 2012 (Zelinski et al., 2012; Saison et al., 2012). The JR blood group system consists of one antigen, Jra, which is a high prevalence antigen. Jra antigen, controlled by the ABCG2 gene, is one of the immune components present on red blood cells and ABCG2 is involved in various physiological, biochemical and metabolic processes in the human body. The JR blood group system is of importance in clinical blood transfusion, whilst ABCG2 plays an important regulatory role in a variety of physiological and pathological processes.

This article may be helpful to immunologists with a strong interest in human erythrocyte blood group systems, as well as professionals who are engaged in the study of transfusion medicine and hematology. Additionally, educators and students in the relevant fields may also use this article as a learning resource regarding the latest research into human JR blood group system.

Survey methodology

This review conducted a literature search across one scientific database: the PubMed database. The keywords employed were “Junior blood group”, “Junior blood group system”, “JR blood group”, “JR blood group system”, “Jra”, “ABCG2”, “Jr(a−) phenotype”, and “Jr(a−) blood group phenotype”. Additionally, the search covered publications from 1970 to 2025. Only peer-reviewed articles were considered.

History of the JR blood group system

In 1970, Stroup and Macllroy first reported the high prevalence antigen Jra on the surface of red blood cells and named it after the initials (JR) of one of the five probands, who all carried the same anti-Jra antibodies (Stroup & Macllroy, 1970). In 1974, Gellerman & Stroup (1975) also identified 18 cases of individuals carrying anti-Jra antibody, seven of which were Japanese. Subsequently, in 1978, Nakajima & Ito (1978) reported that the incidence of Jr(a−) individuals in Japan was 0.03%. In 1994, Miyazaki et al. (1994) produced a human monoclonal antibody against Jra, and the application of this antibody revealed that the incidence of Jr(a−) individuals in Japan was approximately 0.07%.

In 1990, Jra was included in the 901 series of high prevalence antigens by ISBT and was numbered as 901005 (Lewis et al., 1990). After 22 years of advances at the molecular level, in 2012, both Zelinski et al. (2012) and Saison et al. (2012) identified null alleles of ABCG2 as the molecular basis of the Jr(a−) phenotype. Subsequently, in the same year, the ISBT formalized the JR blood group system (ISBT 032).

Jra is a high prevalence antigen in the global populations, and the Jr(a−) phenotype is much less rare in East Asian populations, mainly in Japan and Korea (Castilho & Reid, 2013; Tanaka et al., 2014; Ogasawara et al., 2015; Bae, Kwon & Kim, 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Kwon et al., 2024; Cho et al., 2024; Song, Shin & Oh, 2024). In 2023, Bae, Kwon & Kim (2023) used high-throughput genome sequencing technology to analyse the antigen phenotypes of rare blood groups in a large Korean population, and the study showed that 0.02% of individuals have the Jr(a−) phenotype. The latest research in 2024 showed that the prevalence of the Jr(a−) phenotype in the Korean population was estimated to be 0.045% (Kim et al., 2024). The Jr(a−) phenotype is very rare in European and African populations (Castilho & Reid, 2013; Tanaka et al., 2014), such as West African (Saison, Cartron & Arnaud, 2015) and Polish population (Słomka et al., 2020). Słomka et al. (2020) comprehensively analyzed genetic variation of ABCG2 coding sequence in the Polish population and showed that the frequencies of the most common variants in the Polish population were not significantly different from those in other Caucasian populations.

Molecular biological features

Coding gene

The human JR blood group system is encoded by the ABCG2 gene, located at 4q22.1 (Castilho & Reid, 2013). The gene NCBI ID is 9429 and the full length is 148,059 bp (NG_032067.2). The ABCG2 gene consists of 16 exons and 15 introns, and the full length of the exons is 4,206 bp (NM_004827.3), with the length of each exon ranging from 60 bp to 2,113 bp. The CDS region is 1,968 bp in length and consists of exons 2 to 16, resulting in a 655 amino acids long product (NP_004818.2) (https://blooddatabase.isbtweb.org/system/JR). All of exon 1, a very small portion of exon 2, and most of exon 16 belong to the non-coding regions of the ABCG2 gene, also known as the flanking sequences that have regulatory roles. Exon 1 of the ABCG2 gene encodes a segment of the guide peptide chain, which is responsible for the localization and penetration of the guide peptide chain through the membrane (Table 1 and Fig. 1).

Gene product

The product of ABCG2 gene transcription and translation is the ABCG2 glycoprotein, also known as CD338, with a molecular weight of approximately 72 kDa. ABCG2 is a glycosylated glycoprotein with three putative N-glycosylation sites, of which only one verified N-glycosylation site at Asn596, and 12 cysteine residues (Robey et al., 2009). Structurally, the ABCG2 peptide chain has six transmembrane regions, and both the COOH and NH2 ends of the peptide chain are within the cell membrane, making it a type III transmembrane protein. The peptide chain has three loops outside the membrane, and Jra antigenic expression is mainly on the third loop (Reid, Lomas-Francis & Olsson, 2012). The amino terminal domain contains a nucleotide-binding domain featuring Walker A, Walker B and ABC signature motifs, and carboxyl terminus with six putative transmembrane domains, which is characteristic of ABC half-transporters and to be functionally active (Hue-Roye et al., 2013a). It has been shown that ABCG2 can form homodimers or homotetramers on cell membrane to exert its bio-functional activity (Castilho & Reid, 2013; Robey et al., 2009; Reid, Lomas-Francis & Olsson, 2012; Hue-Roye et al., 2013a; Xu et al., 2004; Leimanis & Georges, 2007; Castilho, 2019).

JR genetic variants

The ABCG2 genetic variants are mostly caused by single base substitutions, known as single nucleotide variants (SNV). Deletions, insertions and frame shift mutations can also occur, mostly leading to changes in protein function. According to the ISBT Blood Group Database (https://blooddatabase.isbtweb.org/system/JR), genetic mutations occur primarily in exon 5, followed by exon 2 and exon 7, and then exon 4, exon 9, exon 13 and exon 16.

In 2012, Zelinski et al. (2012) and Saison et al. (2012) successively unraveled the ABCG2 gene, showing that the ABCG2 null alleles define the Jr(a−) phenotype and that the nonsense mutations c.376C>T and c.706C>T are associated with the prevalence of the Jr(a−) phenotype in the Japanese and European Gypsy populations, respectively. The ABCG2 null alleles reported by Zelinski et al. (2012) are ABCG2*01N.01 (c.376C>T), ABCG2*01N.02.02 (c.34G>A; c.706C>T) and ABCG2*01N.03 (c.736C>T). Also, Saison et al. (2012) reported that the ABCG2 null alleles leading to the Jr(a−) phenotype are ABCG2*01N.02.01 (c.706C>T), ABCG2*01N.07 (187_197del), ABCG2*01N.08 (c.542dup), ABCG2*01N.09 (c.730C>T), ABCG2*01N.10 (c.791_792del), ABCG2*01N.11 (c.875_878dup) and ABCG2*01N.12 (c.1111_1112del).

Subsequently, in 2013, Hue-Roye et al. (2013a) reported three novel nonsense nucleotide changes and one novel nucleotide repeat change with the addition of four ABCG2 null alleles: ABCG2*01N.04 (c.337C>T), ABCG2*01N.05 (c.784G>T), ABCG2*01N.06 (c.34G>A; c.1591C>T) and ABCG2*01N.13 (c.34G>A; c.245dup). In the same year, Hue-Roye et al. (2013b) identified a novel ABCG2 null allele, ABCG2*01N.14 (c.1019_1021del), and also reported three novel ABCG2 alleles, ABCG2*01W.01 (c.421C>A), ABCG2*01W.02 (c.1858G>A) and ABCG2*01W.03 (c.1714A>C), associated with weakened Jr(a+) expression. Meanwhile, Tobita et al. (2013) identified seven novel ABCG2 null alleles, ABCG2*01N.17 (c.263+1G>A), ABCG2*01N.18 (c.289A>T), ABCG2*01N.19 (c.566del), ABCG2*01N.20 (c.1515del), ABCG2*01N.21 (c.1723C>T), ABCG2*01N.22 (c.1789_1790insT) and ABCG2*01N.28 (c.1820+1G>A), as well as four unclear Jr(a) phenotypes. In 2014, Tanaka et al. (2014) reported two novel ABCG2 null alleles, ABCG2*01N.24 (c.2T>C) and ABCG2*01N.25 (c.421C>A; c.1515del), leading to the Jr(a−) phenotype. In 2015, Ogasawara et al. (2015) reported a new ABCG2 null allele (ABCG2*01N.23) with a large deletion of 27,097 bp, including the promoter region. In the same year, Saison, Cartron & Arnaud (2015) reported a novel ABCG2 null allele, ABCG2*01N.27, due to a frame shift mutation c.204-193_531+13delinsCATTTCAGTGGTCTCCC. And in 2017, Berardi et al. (2017) and Wieckhusen et al. (2017) successively reported novel ABCG2 null alleles, which are ABCG2*01N.15 (c.420dup), ABCG2*01N.16 (c.986_987del) and ABCG2*01N.26 (c.439C>T) (Table 2).

Overview of JR genetic variant alleles.

The variants and prevalence of the ABCG2 gene have been extensively studied, with the c.376C>T mutation, which occurs in exon 4, being the most common in Asia (Japan and Korea), and ABCG2*01N.01 accounting for approximately 85% of null alleles in the Korean population (Tanaka et al., 2014; Kim et al., 2024). The c.706C>T mutation, which occurs in exon 7, is also common and has been associated not only with the Jr(a−) phenotype, but also with hyperuricemia/gout (Słomka et al., 2020; Toyoda et al., 2021), hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN) observed in reported cases (Xiao et al., 2023) and fetal anemia (Tran et al., 2023). In 2020, a study looking at European populations, Słomka et al. (2020) performed a complete analysis of the genetic variation of the ABCG2 gene in the Polish population using scanning with HRM, and the study revealed 17 variants, which showed that only c.34G>A (p.Val12Met) and c.421C>A (p.Gln141Lys) were the most common variants in the Polish population. It is noteworthy that new technology NGS has been introduced into JR blood group genotyping and has successfully detected the ABCG2*01N.01 allele (c.376C>T, p.Gln126Ter) (Kim et al., 2021). In 2024, Koo et al. (2024) developed an alternative approach to rapidly predict the presence of anti-Jra using the TaqMan single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) genotyping. There is still a portion of the Jr(a−) phenotype population that appears to have mutations whose association with the Jr(a−) phenotype is still unclear, and in the future, genetic variants in Jr(a−) individuals will gradually become apparent, and more diversity may emerge in the JR blood group system.

Immunological features

Jra, which was one of the original high-prevalence antigens included in the ISBT 901 series, is the only antigen in the JR blood group system. The previous ISBT designation (901005) was made obsolete in 2012, when the relationship between the ABCG2 gene and the Jr(a−) phenotype was discovered (Zelinski et al., 2012; Saison et al., 2012; Hue-Roye et al., 2013a). When mutations in the ABCG2 gene occur, they lead to changes in the ABCG2 glycoprotein on the red blood cell membrane, producing either a Jr(a−) phenotype or a weak Jr(a+) phenotype. Jra antigen is relatively chemically stable and cannot be destroyed by protease, trypsin, acid or 200 mmol/L DTT. Also, Jra antigen is highly immunogenic and stimulates the production of anti-Jra antibodies in people with the Jr(a−) phenotype. Most anti-Jra antibodies are immunoglobulin G (IgG) (IgG1 and IgG3) and a few are immunoglobulin M (IgM).

ABCG2 is widely distributed in different cell types and is preferentially expressed in immature human hematopoietic progenitors, and in umbilical cord red blood cells (Scharenberg, Harkey & Torok-Storb, 2002; Fujita et al., 2016; Kukal et al., 2021). Moreover, ABCG2 is differentially expressed in endothelial cells of human heart, where it may function as a protective barrier against cardiotoxic drugs such as anthracyclines or mitoxantrone (Meissner et al., 2006). However, ABCG2 expression is altered under various pathophysiological conditions, such as dilative and ischemic cardiomyopathies that induce its expression, and in certain cases, these changes also worsen the pathophysiological state and aggravate its severity (Kukal et al., 2021; Meissner et al., 2006). ABCG2 is expressed in all tumor types, but appears to be more common in adenocarcinomas of the digestive tract, endometrial carcinoma, lung cancer and melanoma (Diestra et al., 2002). In 2024, Li et al. (2024) showed that the rapid increase in ABCG2 expression plays a protective role in epileptogenesis and that ABCG2 may be a potential target for the treatment of epilepsy in the future.

Clinical significance

Years of research on the ABCG2 gene, ABCG2 glycoprotein, Jr(a) phenotype and anti-Jra have demonstrated the clinical importance of the JR blood group system and ABCG2 glycoprotein. As early as 1976, Kendall (1976) reported that anti-Jra was potent in vivo, establishing the clinical significance of anti-Jra in patients with the rare blood group Jr(a−). Subsequently, in 1981, Toy et al. (1981) detected anti-Jra in a Japanese woman during her first pregnancy, and anti-Jra was generated by immune stimulation during gestation and identified as IgG1. Since then, studies on anti-Jra alloimmunization have been gradually carried out, unveiling the correlation between anti-Jra and clinical diseases. In the 1980s, Toy et al. (1981) and Takabayashi et al. (1985) reported that anti-Jra caused mild neonatal jaundice and that the neonates were cured after treatment. In 2020, Tanaka et al. (2020) also reported that the neonate did not develop severe hyperbilirubinemia and was cured after receiving phototherapy for mild jaundice. Hemolytic transfusion reaction (HTR) caused by anti-Jra after blood transfusion has been reported (Kwon et al., 2004; Yuan et al., 2005). In 2004, Kwon et al. (2004) reported that the patient who received an additional unit of Jr(a+) red blood cells 1 week after the initial transfusion developed signs and symptoms of an acute HTR within 6 h of the transfusion. Then in 2005, Yuan et al. (2005) reported that a woman with a previously identified anti-Jra who received 15 units of Jr(a) untested red blood cells developed a mild delayed HTR by Day 10 post-transfusion.

When Jr(a−) individuals become pregnant, anti-Jra in their bodies can cause anemia and hemolytic disease in Jr(a+) fetuses or neonates. As early as 2006, Ishihara et al. (2006) reported a Japanese woman who developed fetal hydrops resulting in extremely severe anemia at the 29th week of gestation, and both the mother and the fetus had anti-Jra in their bodies, which required aggressive intrauterine treatment. In 2011, Sasamoto et al. (2011) reported a case of severe fetal and neonatal anemia associated with anti-Jra alloimmunization in a pregnant woman who delivered vaginally at 33 weeks and 5 days of gestation. Then in 2019, both Toshimitsu et al. (2019) and Kanagawa et al. (2019) also reported cases of severe fetal anemia caused by anti-Jra. Toshimitsu et al. (2019) showed that fetal anemia becomes severe in mid-gestation and may not develop in late gestation. Kanagawa et al. (2019) reported that of 16 fetuses with severe anemia, six received intrauterine blood transfusions (IUTs) between 27 and 32 weeks of gestation, and all but one delivered at full term with no neonates with severe anemia or jaundice, suggesting that spontaneous full-term delivery is expected even in fetuses receiving IUT before 32 weeks of gestation. In 2023, Tran et al. (2023) described a case of severe anti-Jra mediated fetal anemia and performed genetic testing of the mother and fetus, which detected maternal compound heterozygosity for two nonsense mutations, c.706C>T and c.784G>T, and fetal sequencing revealed the c.706C>T variant. Similarly Tanaka et al. (2020) reported a case of fetal hydrops and mirror syndrome in a pregnancy with anti-Jra alloimmunization and also performed the genetic testing, which showed that the mother had a homozygous point mutation c.376C>T (376TT) in her gene, and all three of her offspring exhibited 376CT heterozygosity. As reported in these articles (Tran et al., 2023; Toy et al., 1981; Takabayashi et al., 1985; Tanaka et al., 2020; Ishihara et al., 2006; Sasamoto et al., 2011; Toshimitsu et al., 2019; Kanagawa et al., 2019), anti-Jra alloimmunization can lead to hemolytic disease of the newborn, and the clinical importance of perinatal anti-Jra must be emphasized.

Anti-Jra is clinically important because it can cause HDFN and transfusion reactions, whereas hyperuricemia/gout is related to ABCG2 variants and transporter function rather than anti-Jra itself. Peyrard et al. (2008), Arriaga et al. (2009) and Kim et al. (2020) reported the first, second and third cases of fatal HDFN due to anti-Jra, respectively, suggesting that the clinical significance of anti-Jra as a cause of HDFN should be reassessed. Kim et al. (2010) also reported the first case of HDFN caused by anti-Jra alloimmunization in twins in Korea, in which the pregnant woman underwent emergency cesarean section at the 34th week of gestation and both neonates contained anti-Jra. In 2023, Xiao et al. (2023) reported the results of molecular analysis of a pregnant woman containing anti-Jra, showing the discovery of a novel frameshift mutation, c.717delC, as well as a previously identified mutation c.376C>T, and flow cytometry results showing that the Jra antigens on adult red blood cells were significantly less than those on the infant. It also suggests that differences in red blood cell antigen density between adults and infants may be a cause of severe HDFN rather than transfusion reactions, and that breastfeeding may result in slower recovery from HDFN (Xiao et al., 2023). Słomka et al. (2020) and Ristic et al. (2020) suggested in the same year that variants in the ABCG2 gene were associated with the presence of gout, Słomka et al. (2020) revealed 17 variants using scanning with high resolution melting, including variants associated with the presence of gout (c.421C>A, c.335C>A and c.1714A>C) and the Jr(a−) phenotype, and Ristic et al. (2020) reported that loss-of-function mutations in transporters mediating uric acid excretion are associated with gout, and agents capable of inducing ABCG2 expression have the potential to be a novel class of drugs for the treatment of gouty arthritis. The c.421C>A and c.1714A>C variants have been already recorded as weak variants of the JR blood group system. In the subsequent year, Homolya (2021) showed that ABCG2 plays a key role in uric acid clearance and its malfunction may lead to hyperuricemia and gout, and Toyoda et al. (2021) also showed that the ABCG2 gene is a well-established hyperuricemia/gout risk locus, and sequencing analyses identified two rare mutations, c.393G>T and c.706C>T, which is a plausible link between these mutations and the hyperuricemia and gout phenotypes. A recent study in 2024, Koo et al. (2024) developed a rapid, simple, accurate and cost-effective method for predicting the presence of anti-Jra.

Jra typing and identification of anti-Jra play an important role in the clinical management of neonatal jaundice, HTR, fetal and neonatal anemia, HDFN, tumor and gout. In summary, we update knowledge around the significance of Jra antigen and anti-Jra in the clinic.

Future Perspective

Since the discovery of the Jra antigen, the molecular mechanisms have become clearer after decades of research. ABCG2 gene variants are of critical clinical importance, and more are expected to emerge as time progresses. As research continues, it is likely that more clinically significant ABCG2 variants will be discovered and characterized.
