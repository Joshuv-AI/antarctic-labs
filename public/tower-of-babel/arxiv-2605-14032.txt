Abstract
. 5G networks provide low-latency, high throughput, and massive connectivity, yet the control plane remains exposed to several security threats. Among the most common and impactful threats are Denial-of-Service (DoS) attacks, with Radio Resource Control (RRC) signaling storms being particularly effective and difficult to mitigate. In this attack, a malicious User Equipment (UE) aims to exhaust Next Generation Node Base (gNB) resources, preventing legitimate UEs from establishing a connection. Existing defenses are typically limited to detection, only evaluated through numerical simulations, and cannot discern between high-load network conditions and attacks. Most of them also assume static setups and do not take mobility into account. In this paper, we first evaluate the feasibility of the signaling storm attack by using the OAI 5G protocol stack. Then, we propose StormShield, a signaling storm attack detection and mitigation technique implemented as an xApp on an O-RAN Near-Real-Time (near-RT) RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC) . It fingerprints and blocks Malicious UEs (MUEs) before gNB resources are exhausted. We prototyped our solution on an Over-The-Air (OTA) testbed with OAI , NVIDIA Aerial, and two different gNB setups. The first one leverages an USRP X410 Software-defined Radio (SDR) with 8.1 functional split; the second a commercial Foxconn Radio Unit (RU) with 7.2 functional split. Our experimental evaluation demonstrates that StormShield effectively prevents gNB resource exhaustion, identifying and blocking MUEs with an average detection accuracy of 97.6% within 106.5 ms from the beginning of the attack.

Introduction

As the
5th generation (5G)
of cellular networks scales up to support enhanced mobile broadband, ultra-reliable low-latency connections, and massive machine-type communications, ensuring high availability in the protocol stack control-plane emerges as a major security concern.
Both user and control planes are exposed to
Denial-of-Service (DoS)
attacks that can degrade or interrupt network service
(
11
;
13
;
5
)
, but control-plane attacks are more concerning, as they can be carried out by unauthenticated users.
Among these attacks, the
Radio Resource Control (RRC)
signaling storm constitutes a relevant risk for the control-plane.
In this attack, an adversarial
User Equipment (UE)
, which we will refer to as
Malicious UE (MUE)
throughout the rest of the paper, repeatedly generates
RRC
Setup Requests with different
Radio Network Temporary Identifiers (RNTIs)
without sending the
RRC
Setup Complete message
(
16
)
. In this way, the
MUE
avoids authenticating to the network but forces the
Next Generation Node Base (gNB)
to allocate resources for its session (as shown in Fig.
1(b)
).
This malicious behavior depletes control-plane capacity and prevents legitimate connections
(
15
)
.

The detection of these attacks is challenging because the initial observable behavior mimics genuine high-load events in which the
gNB
observes bursts of
RRC
Setup Requests within short time windows.
This makes rate-based thresholds
(
21
)
prone to false alarms.
Mitigation is even more challenging and far less explored in the literature. Since
MUEs
never complete the authentication procedure, their temporary identifiers (e.g.,
RNTI
) change at every new connection attempt. This makes it difficult for the
gNB
to attribute subsequent connection attempts to specific
UEs
, and, thus, to implement per-
UE
blocking policies.
Consequently, effective mitigation must rely on device- or transmission-level fingerprints (e.g.,
Timing Advance (TA)
)
(
6
)
.

We leverage O-RAN as an architectural enabler for addressing the above challenges and design our solution. By exposing standardized, open interfaces toward a
Near-Real-Time (near-RT)

RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC)
, O-RAN provides the observability and control loops needed to collect fine-grained
Key Performance Metrics (KPMs)
and react rapidly in case of an attack
(
3
)
.
Specifically, we introduce StormShield, a
near-RT
detection and mitigation algorithm for
RRC
signaling storm attacks that clusters
UEs
by using multiple radio-level indicators—
Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI)
and
TA
—and access pattern features (e.g., per-source burst of connection attempts and request-to-completion ratios) to build a robust
MUEs
fingerprint.
The combination of multiple indicators is crucial, as existing defenses—mainly detection-only or volume-based—struggle to separate genuine high load from attacks, and
TA
alone is insufficient in dense networks where
UEs
share similar values for this parameter.
Differently from prior work, StormShield (i) implements an effective attack detection and mitigation, rejecting connection attempts from
UEs
flagged as malicious before
gNB
resources saturate; (ii) discerns high-load vs. attack scenarios; and (iii) remains robust in challenging environments, including multiple simultaneous attackers and mobility.

We prototype StormShield on an
Over-The-Air (OTA)
testbed with programmable 5G protocol stacks (OAI, NVIDIA Aerial)
(
9
;
10
)
and two different
gNB
setups. In the first,
OpenAirInterface (OAI)
drives an USRP X410
Software-defined Radio (SDR)
with 8.1 functional split to perform signal transmission and reception. In the second setup, instead, the
OAI
protocol stack connects to the GPU-accelerated NVIDIA ARC solution, which implements the lower-layer functionalities of the
gNB
protocol stack, and connects to a commercial
Radio Unit (RU)
from Foxconn with 7.2 split. We prototype the malicious
UE
by customizing the
RRC
state machine in the OAI software
UE
, and use commercial smartphones as legitimate users.
Experimental results in the different setups demonstrate that StormShield is effective in detecting and mitigating signaling storm attacks before the
gNB
resources are depleted, achieving a
97.6%
average accuracy.
Our key contributions are the following:

•

Attack feasibility in realistic 5G setups -
We reproduce the
RRC
signaling storm attack over different
OTA
testbed setups, including effects such as mobility and multiple concurrent attackers.

•

Fingerprint-based detection of signaling storms -
We design a detection algorithm that combines
RRC
-layer statistics with spatial fingerprinting. This allows the system to distinguish signaling storm attacks from benign high-load network conditions even in challenging indoor scenarios where distance-based features alone are not sufficient.

•

Pre-authentication mitigation via fingerprint blocklisting -
We introduce a mitigation algorithm that operates before authentication and without relying on stable user identities. It maintains an adaptive block list of malicious fingerprints with aging timeouts that reinforce blocking for persistent attackers, while automatically expiring stale entries to avoid long-term exclusion of benign
UEs
. We demonstrate the effectiveness of this technique in the presence of multiple attackers, and both static and mobile devices.

•

O-RAN-based closed control loop implementation -
We deploy StormShield in an xApp over the
near-RT

RIC
, enabling closed-loop detection and mitigation without modifying the
Radio Access Network (RAN)
data plane. We extend the O-RAN E2 data-exposure capabilities with a new security-oriented
Service Model (SM)
, implemented according to O-RAN use case
(
16
)
, that defines telemetry and control semantics to obtain the
KPMs
required by StormShield.

2.2.
Signaling Storm Detection and Mitigation

These attacks are difficult to detect due to changing attacker identifiers and the similarity of the traffic pattern to that of a high-load network. Despite their impact, the literature lacks effective mitigation strategies, because attackers never complete the authentication phase and continuously change their temporary identifiers.
Table
1
summarizes the main literature approaches on this topic,
and highlights how our work differs in terms of setup, attack detection and mitigation capabilities.

In
(
19
)
, an Anomaly Traffic Detector xApp applies
RT
traffic classification using machine learning. It identifies anomalous users analyzing packets and using a random forest. If the
UE
anomaly ratio reaches 100%, the xApp triggers an
RRC

UE
connection release, causing the
UE
to disconnect from the network. However, this method assumes the attacker has completed the authentication process, which is not the case in signaling storm scenarios where the attacker avoids authentication altogether.

Another work
(
21
)
introduces a framework that detects several types of cellular protocol exploits in
Long Term Evolution (LTE)
networks, including attacks that exploit the
evolved Node Base (eNB)
(e.g.,
BTS
resource depletion), the
Mobility Management Entity (MME)
of the core network, and even the
UEs
themselves. Considering the resource depletion attack, its detection method relies on the fact that fake
RRC
connections—dubbed “transient
UEs
”—are typically short-lived due to a missing
Non-Access Stratum (NAS)
authentication response.
If the number of transient
UEs
exceeds a threshold, the attack is detected.
However, this approach cannot reliably distinguish an attack from a legitimate high-load condition of the network.

In
(
22
)
, the authors propose 6G-XSec, a framework that combines unsupervised deep learning with
Large Language Models (LLMs)
to monitor and interpret network anomalies, including
BTS
resource depletion attacks. The system first extends O-RAN with “MOBIFLOW” to collect telemetry data including encoded RRC/NAS messages and parameters such as
UE
identifiers and state. The collected time-series traces are then fed to an unsupervised anomaly-detection xApp (namely, “MOBIWATCH”), trained on benign traffic only. In the case of
BTS

DoS
attacks, the detector learns to flag sequences with abnormal patterns of
RRC
/
NAS
messages and device identifiers, such as rapid bursts of uncompleted connection attempts with changing
RNTI
values. An
LLM
-based xApp is subsequently used to classify and explain the detected anomalies. A limitation of this approach is that benign but unusual conditions (e.g., multiple retransmissions or connection failures) can produce similar anomalous patterns, making it difficult to reliably separate them from malicious activity.

In
(
15
)
, a threshold-based detection technique is proposed. This uses
RRC
-layer metrics like the number of MSG3 messages (
RRC
Connection Request), and the MSG5-to-MSG3 and MSG5-to-MSG4 ratios, where MSG4 and MSG5 correspond to the
RRC
Connection Setup and
RRC
Connection Setup Complete messages, respectively. A surge in MSG3 indicates abnormal behavior, while a drop in the ratios can help distinguish an attack from high-load conditions, since in high-load conditions most
UEs
that receive MSG4 still respond with MSG5, whereas this does not happen during an attack. However, this approach is limited by its reliance only on fixed thresholds, which are not adaptive to different network conditions and may lead to false positives.

Overall, the above methods mostly focus on detection rather than mitigation, as we instead do in this work.
A partially implemented mitigation solution is proposed in
(
6
)
, where a dedicated xApp uses
TA
to fingerprint devices early in the connection process. Since
TA
correlates with the physical distance between the
UE
and base station, this parameter helps to identify and block suspicious devices before they authenticate. However, this method becomes unreliable in indoor or dense scenarios, where
UEs
are close, as
TA
lacks the granularity to differentiate between them.
Additionally, most of the above solutions have been tested in simulations or in limited testbeds, often involving only a single stationary attacker. By contrast, our logic handles single or multiple, stationary or moving attackers, and was validated on an
OTA
testbed with different setups and configurations.

The Open
RAN

5G

RAN

O-RAN builds on the
3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP)

gNB

,

Both

host intelligent

/

The

RIC
hosts xApps that subscribe to telemetry (e.g.,
KPMs
) exposed over the O-RAN E2 interface and enforce control actions on E2 nodes (i.e.,
CU
,
DU
,
and legacy
eNB
supporting this interface
). xApps realize data-driven closed-control loops that can promptly react to network anomalies, such as the
DoS
attacks
we focus on in this work
.

The
non-RT

RIC
, instead, hosts rApps that receive telemetry at a larger granularity over the O-RAN O1 interface from the remaining Open
RAN
elements (i.e.,
near-RT

RIC
,
CU
,
DU
,
RU
, and legacy
eNB
supporting this interface). rApps push high-level network policies to the
near-RT

RIC
via the O-RAN A1 interface that connects the two
RICs
.
Finally, the
non-RT

RIC
connects to the O-Cloud infrastructure via the O-RAN O2 interface to carry monitoring operations, as well as programmatic provisioning, management, and update of network functions.

In this work, we leverage the openness and control capabilities of the Open
RAN
architecture to implement StormShield ’s attack detection and mitigation logic, as discussed in Sec.

.

4.
Attack Modeling

The
5G
connection establishment procedure, as defined in the
3GPP
specifications
(
1
)
, enables a
UE
to access the network for services such as data and voice communication.
As illustrated in Fig.
1(a)
, the procedure begins with cell search and synchronization. The
UE
then acquires the necessary uplink access parameters and initiates the
Random Access Channel (RACH)
procedure by transmitting a random preamble (MSG1) to the
gNB
. At this point, the
UE
also starts a timer (namely, timer T300), which sets the time limit for completing the connection setup. If the timer expires, the
UE
considers the attempt failed and restarts the process.

Upon receiving the preamble, the
gNB
detects the transmission and responds with a Random Access Response (MSG2), which includes a
TA
, an uplink grant, and a temporary C-RNTI. This allows the
UE
to proceed with MSG3, an
RRC
Setup Request message that carries the
UE
identity and the establishment cause.
The
gNB
, then, replies with an
RRC
Setup (MSG4), which defines radio bearer configurations and resolves contention. Upon receiving this message, the
UE
stops the T300 timer and responds with an
RRC
Setup Complete message (MSG5), which includes a
NAS
Registration Request. Finally, the
gNB
selects an appropriate
Access and Mobility Management Function (AMF)
and assigns a
Next Generation Application Protocol (NGAP)
identifier to the
UE
. This completes the
UE
connection process.

In contrast, in the case of a
RRC
signaling storm attack, the
MUE
exploits this mechanism as illustrated in Fig.
1(b)
.
The attacker initiates the connection process by sending MSG1 through MSG3 but intentionally avoids completing the setup by not replying with MSG5 once it receives MSG4 from the
gNB
.
Despite not completing the connection, the
gNB
still allocates radio resources and context to the
UE
based on MSG3, and starts a timer while waiting for MSG5
(
15
)
.
Once MSG3 is decoded, the
gNB
creates an
RRC

UE
context, assigning an internal
RRC

UE
identifier, binding the temporary
RNTI
, and allocating the associated control-plane state (e.g., security capabilities, bearer configuration and timers).
Even though the
MUE
never completes the
RRC
procedure, this per-
UE
context remains stored in the
UE
context maintained by the
gNB
, needlessly occupying
RRC
context resources.
Under a signaling storm attack, many of such unauthenticated contexts accumulate, rapidly exhausting the pool of
RRC
contexts available at the
gNB
and preventing new attachments from legitimate
UEs
.

5.1.
Detection Algorithm

The detection logic of StormShield is shown in Algorithm
1
. At a high level, it operates on periodic measurements collected over a sliding time window of duration
W
. For each window, we consider the number of
RRC
MSG3, MSG4, and MSG5 messages observed, denoted by
N_{3}
,
N_{4}
, and
N_{5}
, respectively, as well as
TA
and
RSSI
metrics that we use to
fingerprint

UEs
.
For each window, the detector monitors the amount of MSG3 and two ratios, similarly to what done in
(
15
)
:

(1)

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{5}}{N_{3}}

\displaystyle\qquad R_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{5}}{N_{4}}

A large number of MSG3 (
N_{3}>T_{1}
) indicates that many connection attempts are being made. If this is accompanied by low completion ratios (
R_{1}<T_{2}
and
R_{2}<T_{2}
), it suggests that a significant fraction of connection procedures do not reach MSG5, which is an indicator of a signaling storm attack rather than standard high-load conditions
(
15
)
, with
T_{1}
,

, and

tunable thresholds in StormShield.
Indeed, under a signaling storm, many MSG3 attempts from the same
MUE
are expected to cluster under similar fingerprints, whereas, under benign high-load conditions, MSG3 messages are spread across many distinct
UEs
and, thus, fingerprints.

This design choice is important to reduce false positives in realistic scenarios of legitimate mass access (e.g., many
UEs
attempting attachment concurrently when entering a new cell). In such cases, despite a temporary burst in MSG3, most procedures still complete successfully and progress beyond MSG5, implying high completion ratios. Conversely, during signaling storm attacks the system observes persistently low completion ratios for a dominant fingerprint cluster, since
MUEs
intentionally avoid completing the connection procedure.

When this coarse-grained event is triggered, the StormShield algorithm refines the attack/no-attack decision by analyzing the spatial structure of the
UE

(2)

\mathbf{x}_{i}=(\mathrm{TA}_{i},\mathrm{RSSI}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

that corresponds to a single MSG3
i

Mitigation Algorithm

As described in Algorithm
2

(6)

e=(\mu,c,\tau,t_{\mathrm{last}}),

where
\mu

c
is a match counter indicating how many times this fingerprint has matched incoming connection attempts;
\tau
is a timeout that defines how long the fingerprint entry remains active without new matches; and
t_{\mathrm{last}}
is the timestamp of the most recent match.

Whenever the detector produces a new set of malicious fingerprints
\mathcal{F}
, the mitigation logic updates the block list
\mathcal{B}
.
If a centroid
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{F}
is already present in
\mathcal{B}
, its counter
c

t_{\mathrm{last}}
is updated, and the timeout
\tau

If
\boldsymbol{\mu}

For each new

t_{\mathrm{now}}-t_{\mathrm{last}}\geq\tau
.
This aging mechanism is crucial, as, if an entry never expired, a benign
UE
establishing a subsequent connection with similar transmission conditions could match an old malicious fingerprint and incorrectly be blocked.

Conclusions

In this work, we considered the problem of
RRC
Signaling Storm attacks in
5G
networks, which can rapidly exhaust
gNB
resources by flooding connection requests in the early stages of the
RRC
connection procedure.
Through an experimental campaign on two
OTA
testbed setups, we first demonstrated that
RRC
signaling storm attacks can be reproduced and studied on realistic
5G
systems with single/multiple, static and mobile attackers.
Then, we proposed StormShield, a fingerprint-based detection and mitigation approach that combines
RRC
statistics with
(\mathrm{TA},\mathrm{RSSI})
fingerprints to distinguish attacks from high-load network conditions, and block malicious access attempts before
gNB
resources are exhausted.
We implemented StormShield as an xApp running on an O-RAN
near-RT

RIC
for closed-loop
gNB
control through a
SM
implemented as described in an O-RAN use case.

By running
\approx

389 experiments
on a real-world testbed, we demonstrated that our system achieved an average accuracy of
97.6%
in identifying and blocking
MUEs
, including in scenarios with mobility and multiple attackers. Detection and mitigation consistently occurred before
gNB
resource depletion, with an average response time of
106.5
ms, which shows the effectiveness of StormShield.

As future work, we plan to improve the accuracy of
UE
fingerprinting by integrating additional features, such as I/Q sample analysis to capture hardware-specific
RF
impairments and provide a more robust device signature. We also aim to expand the detection surface from a local-cell approach to a distributed system leveraging the centralized abstraction of the
RIC
. In addition, we will validate the proposed fingerprint in outdoor deployments, where
TA
granularity better separates spatial regions. Finally, we will extend the threat model to include a larger population of benign victim
UEs
to capture high-load scenarios, as well as more advanced, adaptive, and stealthy adversaries capable of completing
RRC
and
NAS
authentication and dynamically modifying transmit power to evade detection.
