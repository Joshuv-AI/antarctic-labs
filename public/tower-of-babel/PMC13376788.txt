Mutational analysis of multidrug efflux pump (AcrAB-TolC) in Enterobacter cloacae isolates from clinical samples
Authors: Sajeela Akbar, Sumera Afzal Khan, Muhammad Ajmal Khan, Noreen Asim, Muhammad Hamayun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In Gram-negative bacterial pathogens, multidrug efflux pump’s role is being studied as one of the critical mechanisms to develop antibiotics resistance. The current study was designed to characterize the AcrAB-TolC efflux pump genes to determine its role in multidrug resistance of hospital-acquired Enterobacter cloacae. In this cross-sectional descriptive study, a total of 200 clinical samples were collected for the isolation of E. cloacae in district Peshawar, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan. The isolates were screened for antibiotic sensitivity testing. The molecular tools were utilized for the characterization of efflux pump AcrAB-TolC and its regulator genes with mutational analysis. Total 120 samples were found positive for the existence of multidrug resistant E. cloacae isolates. Nine (09) isolates were found positive for harboring AcrA, AcrB, and TolC genes while the regulator genes (RobA, RamA, and MarA) were detected in all 120 isolates. In AcrA gene (2) silent and (4) missense mutations were found while in AcrB (2) missense and in TolC (6) and in RobA (4) missense mutations were found. No mutation has been reported in the RamA and MarA gene. The mutations detected were novel and affected protein stability. The regulatory factors and efflux pump components of hospital-acquired E. cloacae are crucial in causing resistance to a variety of antibiotics, highlighting their significance as a possible target for innovative antimicrobial tactics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53813-9.

Introduction

The genus Enterobacter includes Gram-negative, non-spore-forming bacteria, belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family, and was proposed by Hormaeche and Edwards in 1960. Enterobacter species are part of the human gastrointestinal microbiota but identified as opportunistic pathogens and member of ESKAPE bacterial species; including Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Enterobacter spp1–3. Enterobacter cloacae, a member of the Enterobacter cloacae complex, is a prominent nosocomial pathogen. It is associated with a wide range of infections, including bacteremia, endocarditis, surgical site infections, urinary tract infections (UTIs), septic arthritis, soft tissue infections, and hospital-acquired pneumonia4.

E. cloacae exhibited resistance to many antibiotics attributed to the expression of several proteins including membrane transporters like porins and efflux pumps and enzymes (lactamases)5. Efflux pumps are group of transporters proteins involved in the toxic substrates removal from the cells to environment to preserve bacterial viability. Even though certain efflux pumps require particular substrates, several transporters releases a variety of antimicrobials, leading to multidrug resistance (MDR)6. The overexpression of transporters can occasionally be brought about by additional substrates or organic solvents being extruded, which results in the co-selection of antimicrobial resistance traits7. In recent years researchers investigated the roles of transporters in bacterial virulence and defence against environmental toxins but how efflux pump transporters work and what are their active domains remain unexplored8.

Efflux transporters are classified into five families: the ATP-Binding Cassette (ABC) superfamily, the major facilitator superfamily (MFS), the resistance-nodulation-cell division (RND) superfamily, the multidrug and toxic compound extrusion (MATE) superfamily, and the small multidrug resistance (SMR) superfamily9. The energy sources utilized by these pumps are group specific. For example, RND, MFS, MATE, and SMR superfamilies use proton motive force (H+) or electrochemical gradient of Na+ whereas ABC family efflux pump gain energy via ATP hydrolysis10,11. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is caused via many mechanisms like drug target al.teration, drug degradation, reduced membrane’s permeability and enhanced efflux transporters. Efflux pumps and outer membrane proteins were considered once to work independently but a recent study on Burkholderia thailandensis exhibited direct link between outer membrane’s permeability and active efflux pumps12,13.

AcrAB-TolC, RND efflux pumps in E. cloacae, is the major system attributed to pathogenicity and increasing AMR14–16. This pump is made up periplasmic adaptor (AcrA), inner-membrane transporter (AcrB), and outer-membrane channel (TolC), similar to E. coli AcrAB-TolC system. It is arranged in an operon controlled by genes like mar, rob, and sox, just like other tripartite RND pumps and mutations in these regulators causes AcrAB-TolC overexpression and elevated minimum inhibitory concentration (MICs)17. Cephalosporins and penicillin are substrates of AcrAB-TolC pump18,19 and its overexpression confers resistance in bile, ethidium bromide, fatty acids, Triton X-100, triclosan, nalidixic acid, rifampin, novobiocin, fusidic acid, fluoroquinolones, tetracycline and β-lactams20–24. Studies have reported overexpression of AcrAB-TolC’s role in AMR especially its impact on fluoroquinolones and β-lactams and elevated inhibitory concentrations. The activation of additional regulatory resistance genes that cooperate with the AcrAB-TolC efflux pump also raises the inhibitory concentrations of the substrate25. Up till now the impact of AcrAB-TolC pump is not explored in population selected in this study.

Therefore, the current study was designed to systematically characterize the clinical E. cloacae isolates, assess their profiles of antibiotic resistance and detection of AcrAB-TolC efflux pump and its regulatory gene mutations, aiming to clarify their role in antibiotic resistance among hospital-acquired E. cloacae isolates from major hospitals in Peshawar Pakistan.

Materials and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional descriptive investigation was conducted for the purpose of isolating and molecularly characterizing hospital-acquired multidrug-resistant E. cloacae at district Peshawar (34°00′52″N 71°34′03″E), Pakistan from March 2022 to December 2023. The molecular tools were used to analyze the existence and mutations of the AcrAB-TolC efflux pump genes.

Sample collection

A total of 200 clinical samples (urine, blood, body fluid, sputum, and pus) were collected from the patients of Khyber Teaching Hospital (KTH), Rehman Medical Institute (RMI), and Hayatabad Medical Complex (HMC) located at district Peshawar (KPK), Pakistan. All samples were collected from both indoor and outdoor patients with detailed information about the patient. An informed consent was obtained from all patients and their legal guardian(s). The current study did not include any patients who were receiving therapy for fungal or other non-bacterial infections. The samples were transferred to Microbiology Laboratory at University of Peshawar, Pakistan to undergo further examination.

Sample processing and identification of bacterial isolates

The samples were inoculated on sterile MacConkey, eosin methylene blue (EMB) cystine-lactose-electrolyte deficient (CLED) and agar media plates (Oxoid, England). The plates were subjected to incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. The growth was examined after incubation26. The bacterial colonies were first examined macroscopically to determine their shape, uniformity, smell, color, and ability to ferment lactose. The EMB agar was further used to purify it from mixed cultures27. Through the Gram staining technique bacteria are divided into two categories because of the chemical structure of their cell walls; Gram-positive and Gram-negative28. The Gram-positive isolates were excluded. The API 20-E strips were utilized for additional identification. The final results were documented as identification codes29. The non-Enterobacter isolates were excluded for further processing.

Genomic DNA extraction and molecular identification of the isolates

Isolated colonies were then sub-cultured in tryptone soy broth (TSB) following incubation overnight. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the isolates’ genomic DNA was isolated using a GeneJet Genomic DNA purification kit (Thermo Scientific, USA). By evaluating the DNA’s integrity via gel electrophoresis with 1% agarose (w/v), purified DNA was then stored at −20℃30. 16SrRNA universal primers were used for the molecular identification of the isolates. For sequencing, the samples were sent to Macrogen Korea and the chromatograms were checked via Chromas software 2.6.6 for sequence refinements (http://technelysium.com.au/wp/chromas/). The retrieved sequences were submitted to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (NCBI), and phylogenetic evaluation was done using the NGPhylogeny.fr FastME tool (https://ngphylogeny.fr/) with default setting to showed strains similarity31. The phylogenetic tree was annotated with Interactive Tree Of Life (iTOL) version v532.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

All target isolates were tested for antimicrobial susceptibility against specific antibiotic discs (Table 1) by the standards set forth by the Clinical and Laboratory Standard Institute 2020 (CLSI-2020) using the described Kirby Bauer disc diffusion technique33. Each tested bacterial suspension’s turbidity was calibrated using 0.5 McFarland standards on a white backdrop with black lines. The inhibitory zones were measured by CLSI-2020 guidelines to interpret the results33. Colistin and Tigecycline susceptibility was tested by determining minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) via Broth microdilution (BMD) method using European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) breakpoint criteria34–36.

Molecular characterization of AcrAB-TolC efflux pump genes

The target isolates were screened for AcrAB-TolC efflux pump and their regulator genes by PCR using specific primers. The primer was designed by using Primer3 software. The primer was purchased from Macrogen, Korea as indicated in Table 2. The target genes were amplified using the optimal PCR conditions, as described37. E. coli (ATCC 35218) strain was used as positive control while Bacillus subtilis strain were used as negative control as RND efflux pump are mostly present in gram negative and absent in Gram-positive bacteria. The PCR products of every gene were sequenced at Macrogen, Korea.

Mutational analysis of AcrAB-TolC efflux pump genes

Using BioEdit software, a consensus sequence for the region of interest in each gene was prepared from the obtained forward and reverse raw sequences. The targeted genes partial sequences were analyzed and deposited under accession numbers in the GenBank through BankIt database. To identify mutations, the consensus sequences of these genes were compared to the sequences of genes that were already in the database using the nucleotide BLAST tool. Using the NCBI blast program’s blastx the amino acid substitutions were determined, and the consensus nucleotide sequences were converted into the appropriate amino acids. The data was analyzed using I-Mutant Suite (2.0) to ascertain the impact of the proteins on the alterations38.

Determination of the mutation novelty

To determine the novelty of a mutation, a comprehensive literature searches were conducted using PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and Google Scholar (https://scholar.google.com) to identify any prior reports of the mutation by searching for gene names, positions, and nucleotide or amino acid changes. Several significant databases were queried, including dbSNP (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp/), ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/), COSMIC (https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic) and UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org) to assess if the mutation has been previously documented, its clinical relevance, and any known functional effects. To further evaluate its population frequency, the mutations were checked in population genetics databases such as ExAC (https://exac.broadinstitute.org), gnomAD (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org), and the 1000 Genomes Project (https://www.internationalgenome.org) to determine whether it has been observed in broader or specific populations. If the mutation is absent from the literature, databases, and population studies, they are considered novel.

Results

Morphological and Biochemical identification of E. cloacae

Different culture media were used for the growth of clinical bacterial isolates, which were then identified by microscopic observation and biochemical examination. On MacConkey agar, the Gram-negative bacterium Lactose Fermenter (LF) formed pink colonies, while Non-Lactose Fermenter (NLF) produced colourless colonies. On CLED agar, the LF formed yellowish colonies, whereas on EMB agar, dark pink colonies were developed (Supplementary Fig. 1a-c). Out of 200 samples, 167 samples showed growth of which 163 were LF and 4 were NLF. Using the Gram staining technique, the bacterial isolates were categorized as Gram-positive and negative (Supplementary Fig. 1d). All Gram-negative rod isolates were then passed through API 20E for biochemical analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1e, Supplementary Table S1). Out of these samples, 120 isolates were identified as E. cloacae while the rest were Escherichia coli (8), Klebsiella pneumoniae (35), Vibrio cholerae (1), and Shigella dysenteriae (3) (Supplementary Table S2).

Frequency distribution of E. cloacae in the study cohort

The common source of E. cloacae was found in urine samples 67 (55.83%), followed by pus samples 23 (19.16%), other samples (HSV, tissues, catheter tip) 12 (10%), sputum 11 (9.16%), blood 04 (3.33%), and body fluids 03 (2.5%). The prevalence of E. cloacae was found higher in females with 78 (65%) cases compared to males with 42 (35%) cases. The highest E. cloacae infections were found in age group, 41–60 years 78 (65%), followed by age group 21–40 years having 22 (18.33%) cases whereas 9 (7.5%) cases in < 10 years age group. The lowest rates of 7 (5.83%) in 11–20 years and 4 (3.33%) in > 60 years were observed. In our study 80.83% of the E. cloacae were present in hospitalized patients while 19.16% were detected in outdoor patients, confirming the role of E. cloacae in HAIs (Supplementary table S3).

Molecular Identification of E. cloacae Isolates

All 120 E. cloacae isolates were subjected to molecular identification and isolation of DNA was accomplished by Genejet DNA Extraction kit. The DNA was verified for its purity and quality by passing it across a gel and Nanodrop (Supplementary Fig. 1f) and confirmed as E. cloacae by 16SrRNA sequencing. Phylogenetic analyses were carried out for strains identification with already published sequences (Fig. 1). Our current study sequence (PP712911.1 SA9, PX520815 SA39, PX520816 SA3 and PX520817 SA79) showed sequence similarity with published sequence of E. cloacae and clustered in one clade (highlighted in red) whereas our sequence (PP725561.1 SA29, PX520818 SA11, PX520819 SA46 and PP725591.1 SA89) clustered in another clade (highlighted in yellow) with other reported sequence of E. cloacae, confirming different strains of E. cloacae in our study. overall, our results confirm molecular identification of E. cloacae strains from 16SrRNA sequencing.

Antibiotics resistant profile of E. cloacae Isolates

The antimicrobial susceptibility testing of all E. cloacae isolates (n = 120) showed resistance to tested antibiotics. Colistin and tigecycline were found highly sensitive (92.5%) while cefixime, amikacin, cefepime, imipenem, and trimethoprim were found highly resistant (100%) among the tested antibiotics (Fig. 2 & Supplementary Fig. 2).

Mutational and Phylogenetics analysis of AcrAB-TolC efflux pump genes

All isolates of E. cloacae (n = 120) were then processed to detect the AcrA, AcrB, TolC, RobA, RamA, and MarA genes using specific primers (Supplementary Fig. 3a-f). The AcrA, AcrB, and TolC genes were detected in nine (9) samples while the regulator’s genes RobA, RamA, and MarA were present in all 120 isolates. The amplified gene products were sequenced through Macrogen, Korea and sequence analysis was done on BioEdit software. The mutational impacts were investigated with the I-Mutant software, which indicated both negative (lower stability) and positive (higher stability) consequences. Mutations were detected in AcrA gene (02 silent and 4 missense mutations), AcrB (2 missense mutations), TolC (6 missense mutations) and RobA (2 missense mutations) genes (Table 3). Prediction results were presented in Supplementary Table S5-8. The sequences for these genes were submitted to NCBI under accession numbers (PP566658.1, PP510225.1, PP580686.1, PP681641.1, PP809392.1 and PP835933.1) Supplementary Table S9. Phylogenetic analysis of AcrA gene clustered with reported sequence from Thailand, Senegal, Spain, and the USA (CP040827.1, CP130342.1, OW968328.1, and CP077221.1), respectively (Supplementary Figure S4a). Next our AcrB isolates (PP510225.1) showed clustering with sequences from the USA (CP028950.1), UK (CP039318.1), Prague (CP073310.1), Canada (CP135475.1), Tokyo (CP092635.1), Thailand (CP040827.1) and Switzerland (CP0567761.1) (Supplementary Figure S4b). Our TolC isolates (PP580686.1) showed similarities with sequence from Korea (CP009756.1), India (CP016906.1), Thailand (CP078536.1), USA (CP017475.1), China (CP071861.1), Singapore (CP138440.1), Bangladesh (CP123598.1), Prague (CP073310.1), UK (CP039303.1), Switzerland (CP092042.1), and Vietnam (CP020089.1) (Supplementary Figure S4c). Our RobA gene (PP681641.1) phylogenetics showed resemblance in-clade with genes from Thailand (CP040827.1), Senegal (CP1303421.1), UK (CP039311.1), Switzerland (CP056776.1) and USA (CP077211.1) (Supplementary Figure S4d). All these analyses showed sequence diversity in the current study cohort.

Discussion

The current study dissects the MDR driving efflux pump genes (AcrAB-TolC) in clinical samples collected from various hospitals in the Pakistan, district of Peshawar, KPK. The Gram-negative nature of isolates were confirmed by culturing bacteria on several mediums, including MacConkey, CLED, and EMB (used for isolation of nosocomial infection causing bacteria39,40. API 20E strips biochemically confirmed E. cloacae41.

The E. cloacae complex (ECC) diversity (E. cloacae, E hormaechei, E. asburiae, E. kobei, E. ludwigii, E. nimipressuralis, E. mori) makes identification difficult thorough biochemical tests. Numerous techniques like comparative genomic hybridization (CGH), multi-locus sequence analysis (MLSA), 16 S rRNA, hsp60 and rpoB genotyping were employed the identification. We performed molecular identification of E. cloacae through 16 S rRNA sequencing, a widely employed method for bacterial identification, but limitation associated with includes the lack of subspecies identification42. Study utilizing gold standard methods such as whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) warrants species and subspecies-level resolution but was not performed in the current study due to resources constraints.

Antibiotic susceptibility of the tested antibiotics revealed that 88.3% showed resistance to piperacillin-tazobactam, 91.6% were resistant to cefoxitin, 90% were resistant to cefotaxime, are in accordance to already reported studies43,44. E. cloacae was 7% resistant to tigecycline and colistin, contradict with a previous study findings45. Tigecycline resistance was found to be approximately 3% worldwide while in our study isolates it is higher attributed to upregulating of efflux pumps46,47. Studies have shown that upregulation of efflux pump AcrAB, ramA and rarA, as well as the related efflux pump genes acrAB and oqxAB, in E. cloacae attributed tigecycline resistance in clinical settings48,49. The bloodstream pathogens that exhibited colistin resistance among ECC isolates ranged from 4.4% to 20% annually, whereas in E. coli and Klebsiella species including K. aerogenes the rate remained below 2%50. Our study showed that all E. cloacae isolates were 100% resistant to imipenem, cefepime, and cefixime51–54. Globally, imipenem resistance varies across different countries, 78.57% in India, 70.96% in Bangladesh and 12.1% in Palestine55–57.

Ccolistin remained the last-resort treatment for MDR Gram-negative bacterial infections, including ECC but now in clinical settings globally, colistin-resistant ECC has emerged too58. ECC colistin resistance is multifactorial such as lipid A modification arbitrated by arnBCADTEF operon, phoP/phoQ and pmrA/pmrB mutations and plasmid mediated mcr genes59–61. Recent studies reported AcrAB-TolC efflux pump and its regulators role in MDR in ECC isolates. For example, the clinical ECC isolates investigation revealed acrA expression in addition to modification of lipid A and mcr genes, demonstrates the complex relation between efflux and membrane remodeling. This observation lends credence to the idea that efflux-mediated processes could be involved in the multidrug-resistant phenotype in ECC. Our results showed that 95.83% isolates were resistant to azithromycin and 90% to ciprofloxacin, consistent with a previous study46. Amikacin resistances were observed in all isolates, a trend documented by earlier studies62. Furthermore, 83.3% isolates showed resistance to chloramphenicol, 91.6% to nitrofurantoin, 89% to aztreonam and 100% to trimethoprim63,64.

Most of the clinical research focused on how these pumps contribute to antibiotic resistance, while few elucidate how pumps affect the pathogenicity and fitness of bacteria. Our study explored the function of the AcrAB-TolC efflux pump in antibiotic resistance in E. cloacae. Another study also reported the Efflux pump and its role in E. cloacae in 2007 [20 − 16]. In our analysis regulator’s genes were detected in all 120 isolates while AcrA, AcrB and TolC genes were found in 9 samples, and these showed AMR to tigecycline and colistin as reported previously19,65. Furthermore, deletion of efflux pump genes tend bacteria to become susceptible to antibiotics15,16,66. The presence of regulatory genes in all isolates was accordance to previous studies demonstrating the role of these global regulators in multidrug resistance67. However, a limitation associated in our current study is the lack of genotypically characterization of other resistance determinants such as β-lactamases, porins alterations etc. to fully comprehended efflux pump role in AMR and warrants further investigation. Furthermore, lacking functional test to measure efflux activity and MIC correlation limit to establish direct casual relationship with AMR and warrants further analysis on these mutations. Mutational analysis of AcrAB-TolC efflux pump genes in E. cloacae is very important to find out the variant circulating in a specific locality68–71. All the mutations detected in AcrA, AcrB and TolC were novel. Further evaluation of these mutations was important for details analysis.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the critical role of the AcrAB-TolC efflux pump and its regulatory elements in conferring MDR in E. cloacae isolates. Mutations in important efflux genes and their regulators point to adaptive genetic changes that improve bacterial resistance to a variety of antibiotics. To overcome antibiotic resistance in clinical E. cloacae infections, targeting efflux mechanism and regulatory networks offer new avenues to treat MDR in clinical E. cloacae infections. Further research is warranted to explore the possibility that deletion of these genes may play a role in the attenuation of E. cloacae isolates as a potential vaccination method to fight bacterial infections. In the battle against antibiotic resistance, in-silico methods are also essential because they provide strong instruments to hasten the development of new antibacterial drugs and disrupt resistance pathways.
