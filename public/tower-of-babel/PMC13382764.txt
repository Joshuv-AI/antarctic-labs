Applications and future development of solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance for investigating metal-organic frameworks
Authors: Shoushun Chen, Zhi-Peng Wang, Bryan E.G. Lucier, Wanli Zhang, Wei Wang
Published: 2027

ABSTRACT
Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are a class of porous materials that feature permanent porosity, high crystallinity, and diverse structures. The 2025 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to three pioneering scientists working in the MOF field. Over the past thirty years, a wide variety of MOFs have been documented. The plethora of robust structures and porosities available has opened the door to MOF applications in many fields. The vast array of possible MOF structures also renders characterization challenging and poses significant obstacles in establishing structure-property relationships. Solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (SSNMR) is one of the most accessible and informative techniques available for the characterization of MOFs. In this review, we discuss selected uses of SSNMR for probing the local environments surrounding MOF metal centers and organic linkers, along with impactful applications designed to enhance understanding of host-guest interactions within MOFs. Cutting-edge SSNMR research of MOFs is highlighted, emphasizing examples of breakthroughs achieved with recent advances in hardware and pulse sequences. MOF research is entering a new stage focused on practical applications and tailored rational design for industrial purposes. SSNMR will play a pivotal role in the future of MOF characterization, with both significant opportunities and challenges looming on the horizon.

Introduction

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), a novel class of porous crystalline materials composed of inorganic nodes and organic linkers, have garnered significant interest over the past three decades [1,2]. The 2025 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to Susumu Kitagawa (Kyoto University), Richard Robson (University of Melbourne) and Omar Yaghi (University of California, Berkeley) for their pioneering work in the development of MOFs. The Nobel committee chair Heiner Linke said that “this year, laureates have found ways to create materials, entirely novel materials, with large cavities on their insides.” [3] This Nobel Prize is a historical event that will inspire further research in the MOF field. There are over 100,000 publications focusing on MOFs (Google Scholar); the Cambridge Structural Database (CSD) database contains 137,167 MOF structures as of Feb 26, 2026, which constitutes ca. 10% of all structures in the CSD and situates MOFs as the largest family of documented crystal structures within the field of porous materials. The body of evidence positions MOFs as excellent candidates for engineering new porous materials featuring various functional groups to tackle practical issues. These materials have ultrahigh surface areas generally ranging from 100 to 10,000 m2/g, tunable pore sizes measuring between 3 and 100 Å, and can assume a diverse array of structural motifs. With prudent choice of the inorganic nodes and organic linkers, MOFs can also exhibit exceptionally high thermal and chemical stabilities, which opens the door to applications in diverse fields including gas storage/separation [4], water harvesting [5], carbon capture [6], catalysis [7], drug delivery [8], chemical sensing [9], and energy conversion [10], among others [11].

Historical origins of MOFs

The first report of MOFs traces back to 1959, when Yoshihiko et al. reported the synthesis and crystal structure of [Cu(NC(CH2)4CN)2]NO3 (Fig. 1) [12]. In 1989, Richard Robson and his collaborator Bernard Hoskins designed a material with diamond-shaped pores of the formula (CuI[C(C6H4·CN)4]BF4·xC6H5NO2), which was constructed from pyramidal modules with copper atoms linked by organic molecules [13]. The research field was formally classified by Yaghi et al., in 1995, when they proposed the concept of “metal–organic frameworks (MOFs)” to describe porous crystalline materials consisting of metal ions and long organic linkers; this was a critical distinction that separated these compounds from other porous materials such as zeolites. A major milestone was documented in 1997 by Kitagawa et al. with the report of successful methane, nitrogen and oxygen gas adsorption within an activated cobalt MOF (Co2(4,4′-bpy)3(NO3)4) at room temperature (Fig. 1) [14]. This work proved that MOFs could be stable enough to accommodate guest gas molecules without disrupting the host framework structure. Another major development was noted in 1999 by Yaghi et al., documenting the synthesis and structure of MOF-5; it was shown that activated MOF-5 is both highly porous and thermally stable up to 300 °C [15]. Following up on that work, Yaghi et al. then reported a series of IRMOFs (IR: isoreticular) with the same topological structure as MOF-5 but different pore sizes and properties, which was accomplished by changing the linker length and embedded functional groups [16].Fig. 1Selected milestones in the history of MOF development.

Rapid development of MOFs occurred during the first decade of the 21st century. In 2002, Férey et al. of the Matérial of Institute Lavoisier (MIL) detailed the MIL series of MOFs (Fig. 1), which have become extremely well-known [17]. The MIL MOFs incorporate linker carboxylate groups and trivalent metal ions in the nodes, which confers excellent thermal stability and also permits unique structural flexibility evidenced in phenomena such as the “breathing effect,” in which the pore structure can expand or contract to accommodate specific guests [18]. Other significant advances during this time include the development of MOF-74 [19] (2005) and UiO-66 [20] (2008), which exhibit exceptional gas adsorption affinities and high thermal/chemical stabilities.

The family of zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs) are a prominent class of MOFs that assume structural motifs similar to those of zeolites; the zeolite Si and O atoms of zeolites are replaced by metal centers and imidazolate-based linkers in ZIFs [21]. The first porous MOF featuring a zeolite-like topology was documented by the You research group in 2002 [22]. In 2003, the Chen group reported the MAF-3 (MAF: metal azolate framework, also known as ZIF-7, Fig. 1) MOF, which is composed of zinc ions and benzimidazole linkers [23]. MAF-3 assumes a sodalite (SOD) structural topology, exhibits permanent porosity and excellent stability, and remains one of the most popular MOF materials for addressing practical applications in fields such as electrochemistry [24,25]. In 2006, Yaghi et al. coined the term ZIF to encompass this specific MOF field, and reported a series of ZIFs that inspired further research and development [26,27].

Applications of MOFs

The high porosities, tunable pore sizes, and diverse structures of MOFs render these materials well-suited for applications in fields such as catalysis, gas storage, gas separation, water capture, chemical sensing, and proton conduction, among others [28]. Gas storage and gas separation are two areas of particularly intense study [4,29]. Climate change and the finite quantities of fossil fuels have presented unique and urgent challenges; researchers have looked towards MOFs to address storage of energy-related gases such as CH4 and H2, capture of greenhouse gases, separation and purification of hydrocarbons, and selective adsorption of pollution products including SO2 and H2S. MOFs are now competitive candidates for applications alongside traditional porous materials such as zeolites, activated carbon and porous metal oxides.

One of the first MOF applications explored pertained to H2 gas storage in MOF-5 under high pressure, which was found to have a total gravimetric capacity of 10 wt% at 100 bar in 2003 by Yaghi et al. [30] The highest H2 storage capacity reported to date was in 2010, when a total gravimetric H2 uptake of 17.6 wt% at 77 K and 80 bar in MOF-210 was recorded, owing to the very high Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area of 6200 m2/g [31]. H2 gas is typically adsorbed in MOFs at low temperatures due to weak host-guest interactions, but methane can be stored in MOFs at room temperature and reasonably high pressures [4], making practical methane storage applications realistically achievable in the coming years. Methane storage in MOFs is important due to the relatively lower CO2 emissions associated with methane combustion over other fossil fuels [32,33]. The gravimetric CH4 storage capacities of MOFs at high pressure have been directly connected to the amount of MOF pore volume and/or surface area. It follows that MOFs with very high BET surface areas are typically associated with appreciable CH4 loading capacities; for example, NU-111 has a measured BET surface area of 4930 m2/g and one of the highest measured CH4 gravimetric uptakes at 0.36 g/g [34,35]. In 2015, a new aluminum MOF termed Al-soc-MOF-1 was reported by Alezi et al., [36] which displayed an exceptionally high pore volume of 2.3 cm3/g and a BET surface area of 5585 m2/g. Owing to the high surface area, Al-soc-MOF-1 exhibited the highest reported total gravimetric CH4 capacity at that time of ca. 580 cm3 (STP)/g (ca. 0.42 g/g) at 298 K and 65 bar, which was 83% of the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) gravimetric target. The recently reported iron-based Fe-ncb-TPDC-II MOF has a BET surface area of 5157 m2/g and a gravimetric CH4 capacity of 0.441 g/g at 298 K and 80 bar [37]. At a temperature of 273 K and pressure of 80 bar, the gravimetric CH4 storage capacity of Fe-ncb-TPDC-II is a remarkable 0.598 g/g.

MOFs can selectively adsorb specific guests from a mixed stream and are thus excellent candidates for gas separation applications. Many industrially relevant gas separation scenarios could utilize MOFs as adsorbents, including from acetylene/carbon dioxide (C2H2/CO2) [38], methane/carbon dioxide (CH4/CO2) [39], xenon/krypton (Xe/Kr) [40], ethylene/ethane (C2H4/C2H6) [41], propylene/propane (C3H6/C3H8) [42], acetylene/ethylene (C2H2/C2H4) [43], and propyne/propylene (C3H4/C3H6) mixtures [44]. MOFs can also be used in the pursuit of the separation of C4, C6, and C8 isomers, the separation and selective adsorption of CO2, along with removal of toxic gases [[45], [46], [47]]. To be succinct, only C2H2/CO2 separation is briefly discussed here as an example. C2H2 is a critical feedstock chemical and fuel gas that usually contains small amounts of CO2 as a result of the production process [48]. In order to generate high-purity C2H2 for end-user applications, CO2 must be reduced or eliminated. Selective removal of CO2 from a C2H2 stream is challenging due to the similar physical properties of the two molecules; C2H2 measures 3.32 × 3.34 × 5.70 Å while CO2 is 3.18 × 3.33 × 5.36 Å, and the C2H2 boiling point of −84 °C is close to the CO2 boiling point of −78.5 °C [48]. Several MOFs have shown great promise for C2H2/CO2 separation; most are C2H2-selective adsorbents since the relatively more acidic and polarizable C2H2 is a better candidate for uptake [49]. In 2025, the synthesis of the Zn-bpy-DLmal MOF was reported. This MOF exhibited an exceptional C2H2 adsorption capacity of 1.4 mmol g−1 at 0.01 bar owing to the strong electrostatic interactions between C2H2 and the oxygen atoms of DLmal linkers, yet displayed a much lower CO2 adsorption capacity of 0.06 mmol g−1 [50]. Another approach to C2H2 purification uses CO2-selective MOFs to remove the CO2 contamination, yielding high purity C2H2. A good example of this strategy is the ultramicroporous Al(HCOO)3 MOF, which can adsorb 86.2 cm3/g of CO2 but only 3.3 cm3/g of C2H2 at 1 bar and 298 K [51]. The Al(HCOO)3 MOF has hydrogen-confined pore cavities of appropriate dimensions featuring an electropositive surface that prefers to bind CO2 via weak H⋯O and C⋯O host-guest interactions.

Solid-state NMR studies of MOFs

The structural diversity and guest-accessible functional groups within MOFs confer many advantages, but also present challenges with respect to structural characterization and establishing structure-property relationships. MOFs are generally highly crystalline materials that exhibit long-range order, which are ideal targets for diffraction-based characterization techniques to elucidate the crystal structure and phase purity. Such techniques include single crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD), powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), neutron diffraction, and electron diffraction [52]. Once the MOF structure is ascertained, other techniques can be employed to investigate other material properties such as stability, porosity, connectivity, guest loading capacity, morphology, etc., include gas sorption tests, elemental analysis, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) experiments, solution NMR spectroscopy, infrared spectroscopy (IR) and thermogravimetric analysis [53]. With this base knowledge in hand, more specific methods that target unique MOF features or yield application-specific information can then be performed. These advanced characterization methods include, but are not limited to, solid-state NMR (SSNMR), synchrotron radiation techniques such as extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) and X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES), cryo-electron microscopy, 3D electron diffraction (ED), and high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) [53]. Unravelling the structure-property relationships in MOFs typically demands the use of complementary information extracted from advanced characterization techniques performed under in situ and/or in operando conditions.

SSNMR is a powerful structural characterization tool that can provide molecular-level information on the short-range structure of MOFs [54]. SCXRD techniques can provide an unambiguous crystal structure and lay a foundation for investigating structure-property relationships; unfortunately, growing suitable single crystals for MOFs is a complex and unpredictable process, and not all MOF structures can be successfully grown into crystals suitable for SCXRD characterization [55]. In such circumstances, the long-range MOF structure can be solved using less informative characterization approaches obtained from several information streams, particularly when powder XRD and electron diffraction are used. In these situations, SSNMR can be a powerful complementary tool for structural refinement [55]. SSNMR experiments can provide information about short-range structure, local chemical environments, bonding, dynamics, local disorder, and host-guest interactions, which is challenging or impossible to obtain from other techniques [56,57]. SSNMR experiments can target multiple nuclei in the same sample, typically require quantities in the tens to hundreds of mg, and are non-destructive. Furthermore, SSNMR can also be used to study samples of low or inconsistent crystallinity.

The field of SSNMR spectroscopy has experienced rapid advances in recent years regarding accessible high magnetic fields, hardware, and pulse sequences. Cutting-edge hardware such as the 35.2 T series-connected hybrid ultra-highfield magnet at the National High Magnetic Field Laboratory (NHMFL) in Florida, USA [58], the proliferation of commercially available dynamic nuclear polarization (DNP) devices for sensitivity enhancement [59], and the continual increase in accessible magic angle spinning (MAS) spinning speeds (160 kHz at the time of print) [60] have unlocked a wide range of SSNMR experiments that were previously too time onerous or impossible to perform [61]. There are many examples involving the use of NMR for MOF structural elucidation and refinement [62,63]. SSNMR can provide a wealth of structural information in MOFs in three broad areas: framework metal and dopant metal sites, organic linkers and defect sites, and host-guest interactions. The host-guest category is particularly broad and includes guest dynamics, guest-responsive framework changes, and framework responses to temperature and pressure changes along with other stimuli [57]. In addition, the pore size and pore connectivity can also be probed by SSNMR experiments [64,65]. There is a wide portfolio of solid-state NMR techniques used to investigate MOFs, including MAS, multiple-quantum MAS, cross polarization (CP) and cross polarization magic angle spinning (CP/MAS), heteronuclear correlation (HETCOR) and pulsed field gradient (PFG) NMR experiments, among many others. A detailed discussion on these techniques is beyond the scope of this mini review and the reader is directed to other focused resources for further information [61,[66], [67], [68], [69]].

SSNMR of metal centers and doped metals in MOFs

The metal nodes of MOFs play a pivotal role in MOF structure and are often related to many applications. A good example is MOF-74(Mg), in which Mg centers are coordinated to both the framework atoms and solvent molecules upon synthesis, but the coordinated solvent can then be removed to generate coordinatively unsaturated open metal sites (OMSs) that are exposed to the pore interior and chemically active. These OMSs can establish strong bonding interactions with guest molecules, which can be exploited to realize enhanced gas adsorption capacities or improved catalytic activities [70]. Many chemically and structurally robust MOFs can also be used as host matrices for guest (i.e., extraneous) metal ion dopants that can introduce or enhance properties to address specific applications. Direct characterization of the inorganic nodes, metals, and dopant metals in MOFs is crucial for unravelling local structure and establishing structure-property links [71].

A diverse array of metals have been employed as MOF nodes. Many metals have a nuclear spin number I ≥ 1/2, which give rise to SSNMR spectra of characteristic shape that are broadened across tens to thousands of kHz. The SSNMR spectra of spin-1/2 nuclei are dominated by the chemical shift anisotropy (CSA) and magnetic dipolar coupling interactions, which can be partially or fully suppressed using MAS approaches. A MAS experiment involves packing the solid sample into a small rotor that is rapidly spun at the “magic” angle of 54.74° with respect to the z-axis of the NMR magnet. SSNMR spectra of spin-1/2 metals are typically dominated by CSA, which disperses the NMR signal across a significant frequency range. The spectral effects of CSA are proportional to the applied magnetic field, thus spin-1/2 metal spectra can be recorded at moderate magnetic fields such as 9.4 and 14.1 T. There are some spin-1/2 nuclear isotopes, including 111/113Cd and 117/119Sn, that usually are associated with less CSA and give rise to relatively narrower MAS SSNMR spectra that can be readily obtained with sufficient signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). In contrast, there are spin-1/2 metal nuclei such as 195Pt, 199Hg and 207Pb that are linked to high CSA values and extremely broad ultra-wideline (UW) SSNMR spectra spread across hundreds of kHz. In such cases, MAS spinning speeds are generally insufficient to fully remove CSA effects. UW SSNMR spectra are normally acquired under static (i.e., non-spinning) conditions using specialized pulse sequences such as WURST–CPMG (Wideband Uniform Rate Smooth Truncation-Carr-Purcell Meiboom-Gill) that afford enhanced SNR and reduced experimental times [69]

A good example of a spin-1/2 metal yielding a broad CSA-dominated SSNMR spectrum is detailed in a195Pt SSNMR study targeting Pt dopant metal in the MIL-121 MOF [72]. The NMR-active 195Pt isotope (I = 1/2) has a natural abundance (n.a.) of 33.8 %, but is associated with very large CSA and correspondingly broad SSNMR spectra that often fall in the UW category. In 2019, Chen et al. loaded the platinum (II) salt Pt (NH3)4(OH)2 into the MIL-121 MOF and collected the corresponding UW 195Pt spectra at 9.4 T using static 1H–195Pt cross-polarization BRAIN-CPMG (broadband adiabatic inversion-CPMG) experiments (Fig. 2a). The 195Pt SSNMR spectrum suggested that two distinct Pt species were present in the MOF, which were assigned to a four-coordinate square-planar Pt (II) species that was attached to the MOF along with a six-coordinate octahedral Pt (IV) environment produced from the reaction between the octahedral Pt (IV) complex of Pt (NH3)4(OH)2 with the anhydride group of the MOF (Fig. 2b). The 195Pt SSNMR signals were obtained from diluted Pt dopants at a magnetic field of 9.4 T, indicating that SSNMR at low to moderate fields is a viable route for the study of MOFs containing challenging nuclei of low natural or material abundance. A spin ≥1 nucleus has an intrinsic electric quadrupole moment. The spectra of these quadrupolar metals are dominated by the quadrupolar interaction (QI) between the quadrople moment and the electric field gradient. The QI can disperse a SSNMR resonance across an extremely large frequency range, tremendously decrease SNR, and influence the lineshape in a diagnostic manner. The spectra of quadrupolar metal nuclei are difficult to collect and interpret. There are 120 NMR-active nuclear isotopes in the periodic table, of which 89 are quadrupolar nuclei. Many MOFs feature NMR-active quadrupolar nuclei, including 25Mg, 27Al, 47/49Ti, 67Zn, and 91Zr. When dealing with quadrupolar nuclei, SSNMR experiments typically target the −1/2↔ +1/2 central transition, which is influenced by the second-order quadrupolar interaction. The magnitude of the second-order QI is generally between 103 and 107 Hz; when compared to the CS interaction that ranges from 102 to 105 Hz, the QI usually makes a significantly more impactful effect on spectral appearance and breadth. QI effects on quadrupolar nuclei NMR spectra are inversely proportional to the applied magnetic field, so high and ultra-high magnetic fields ≥14.1 T are typically employed to record SSNMR spectra.

The degree of difficulty associated with acquiring high resolution NMR spectra of quadrupolar nuclei is influenced by the nuclear quadrupole moment Q, the n.a., and the gyromagnetic ratio (i.e., Larmor frequency, γ) of the nucleus. Generally speaking, quadrupolar metal nuclei in MOFs can be divided into three types: (i) sensitive nuclei with small to moderate Q, high n.a., and high γ, such as 7Li, 27Al and 45Sc; (ii) challenging nuclei with high Q, γ, and n.a., including 115In, 69/71Ga, 139La, etc., and (iii) unreceptive nuclei with moderate to large Q, very low n.a., and low γ, with examples like 25Mg, 67Zn, 91Zr and 43Ca. In many cases, SSNMR spectra of (i) and (ii) may be acquired with moderate difficulty, but obtaining spectra of (iii) involves significant obstacles. Furthermore, when performing SSNMR experiments on any quadrupolar metal, the high MOF porosity and abundance of other atoms dilutes concentration of the target nucleus within the framework, leading to longer acquisition times [76]. Given that MOFs typically contain quadrupolar metal nuclei and the associated difficulties in acquiring quadrupolar NMR spectra, performing SSNMR experiments on quadrupolar metal nuclei in MOFs is normally not a straightforward task, and can sometimes require isotopic enrichment of an NMR-active isotope. Advances in NMR hardware and pulse sequences in recent decades have rendered quadrupolar spectral acquisition much more feasible; many quadrupolar metal nuclei in MOFs have been successfully investigated via SSNMR, providing valuable information on the local structure about metal centers [56,[77], [78], [79]].

Zinc is one of the most common metal centers incorporated within MOFs, and is found in materials ranging from MOF-5 to the ZIF family. The 67Zn isotope is a challenging nucleus for SSNMR acquisition, owing to the low γ (1.6767 × 107 rad T−1 s−1), small n.a. (4.04%) and moderate Q (122 mb) [61]. SSNMR studies on Zn MOFs are illustrative of how NMR hardware and pulse sequence development have rapidly advanced the field. The first high-resolution 67Zn SSNMR MOF spectra were collected in 2012 using a high field of 21.1 T without isotopic enrichment [80]. 67Zn NMR spectra of several Zn MOFs were successfully obtained, including those of MOF-5, ZIF-4, ZIF-7, ZIF-8, and ZIF-14. This marked a significant step forward, yet these Zn MOFs only contained one or two inequivalent Zn sites, which could be readily assigned from MAS or multiple-quantum magic-angle spinning (MQMAS) spectra. In many Zn MOFs, there are multiple chemically and crystallographically inequivalent metal sites, which may be challenging to resolve and assigned even when advanced two-dimensional MQMAS experiments at high fields are used. The recent development of the cryo-probe, which involves the entire NMR probe hardware and electronics operating at cryogenic temperature to minimize rf coil and probe/preamplifier circuit noise, can greatly enhance SNR in NMR experiments. In 2023, Zhang et al. used a cryoprobe at a field of 18.8 T to perform 67Zn SSNMR experiments and successfully resolve multiple overlapping natural abundance 67Zn resonances from the ZIF-4 and α-Zn3(HCOO)6 MOFs (Fig. 3) [81]. The two 67Zn signals in ZIF-4 and four 67Zn signals in Zn3(HCOO)6 (Fig. 3a) could be assigned to specific Zn sites in the corresponding crystal structures (Fig. 3a). Based on SSNMR data and density functional theory (DFT) calculations, the authors determined that some Zn–O bonds were shorter than indicated from the XRD-derived crystal structure. Acquiring SSNMR spectra of high Q quadrupolar metal nuclei in MOFs poses significant challenges [71,79]. The corresponding SSNMR spectra are usually >1 MHz in breadth and are typically acquired using a variable-offset cumulative spectrum (VOCS) strategy [82], which involves collecting UW SSNMR spectra by acquiring individual subspectra at evenly spaced frequency offsets. The subspectra are then summed in the frequency domain to yield the overall UW spectrum. The emergence of extremely high magnetic fields, such as the 35.2 T series-connected hybrid magnet at the NHMFL, has made practical SSNMR acquisition of high Q quadrupolar metal nuclei much more feasible. A recently reported 209Bi and 127I SSNMR study on several Bi- and I-containing MOFs at 35.2 T illustrates this point [83]. 209Bi and 127I both exhibit large γ values and are 100% n.a., which would typically be suggestive of high NMR receptivity. Unfortunately, both isotopes suffer from large Q, where Q (209Bi) = −516 mb and Q (127I) = −688 mb; both these values are over fourfold that of the challenging 67Zn nucleus and require the use of extremely high magnetic fields and specialized techniques for SSNMR acquisition. The 209Bi UW SSNMR spectrum of MOF IEF-3 at 35.2 T was >45 MHz in breadth and consisted of a detailed lineshape assembled from 100 subspectra (Fig. 4) corresponding to a single unique Bi site. At such large breadths, simulations required the use of a rigorous exact QI mathematical treatment, rather than the typical treatment of second-order QI as a perturbation upon the fundamental Zeeman interaction. The CQ(209Bi) value of 860 (2) MHz in IEF-3 is one of the highest reported CQ values in the NMR literature, and is significantly larger than those obtained from any Bi materials outside the MOF family [84,85]. 115In is another quadrupolar metal nucleus with a very large Q (772 mb) that has been studied to a limited extent in MOFs such as In-fumarate [86]. SSNMR of organic linkers in MOFs

The long- and short-range structure of a MOF is mutually dependent on the metal center and the organic linkers. While the linkers are often viewed only as struts that can bear functional groups, MOF linkers are a determinant of the overall framework topology, pore shape, macroscopic properties, and eventual applications. The functionality of MOF organic linkers can significantly influence the physical and chemical properties of MOFs. The two practical functionalization strategies regarding MOF linkers involve directly modifying the organic ligand precursor prior to MOF assembly or post-synthetic modification (PSM) of the framework after assembly. SSNMR is well-positioned to directly characterize MOF organic ligands in either scenario, as many NMR-active nuclei such as 1H, 13C, 15N, 17O, 19F, and 31P are commonly incorporated within linkers [62,87]. SSNMR experiments targeting linker nuclei have yielded rich local and long-range information in MOFs regarding the confirmation of incorporated organic linkers, the distribution of mixed linkers, local structure and order and the presence of crystalline defects [88]. When SSNMR experiments are used to probe MOF organic linkers, the effects of paramagnetic metal centers must be considered. Paramagnetic metal centers such as Cu2+, Ni2+, Co2+, Cr3+ and Fe2+/3+ are commonly featured in MOFs, owing to their high coordination numbers, high compatibility with a variety of linkers, and opportunities for post-synthetic modification. While paramagnetic metal centers may confer additional features or enhance the performance of MOFs, the unpaired electrons also introduce hyperfine and pseudocontact interactions that complicate SSNMR characterization of local structure, making spectral interpretation and resonance assignment very challenging [88]. Paramagnetic metal centers are typically associated with significantly broadened resonances and very rapid signal relaxation of nearby target nuclei [89]. Mitigation strategies for SSNMR studies of MOFs containing paramagnetic nuclei include hydrogen deuteration (which reduces the magnetic dipolar coupling to all nuclei), very fast MAS spinning to reduce dipolar couplings, and highly optimized cross-polarization conditions and pulses [90,91].

1H and 13C are the two most common targets for SSNMR of organic linkers in MOFs. 1H is a spin-1/2 nucleus with a very high n.a. of 99.99 % and an extremely favourable γ of 26.752 × 107 rad T−1 s−1, and is the most attractive target for SSNMR experiments. The organic linkers in MOFs are often hydrogen-rich and can be readily investigated via 1D and 2D 1H MAS SSNMR experiments. Since 1H–1H homonuclear magnetic dipolar coupling is very strong, 1H SSNMR experiments typically demand the use of moderate-to high-frequency MAS experiments to obtain sufficient resolution. 13C is also a spin 1/2 nucleus, although it is only 1.07% n.a and has a moderate γ value of 6.728 × 107 rad T−1 s−1.13C SSNMR signals can generally be obtained in hours, but the experimental time can be significantly reduced by employing signal enhancement strategies such as 1H–13C cross-polarization experiments. Given that 1H and 13C are ubiquitous in MOF structures, 1D and 2D 1H and 13C SSNMR experiments can typically resolve all linker structural assignments in MOFs. 1H and 13C SSNMR spectra were recently used to monitor time-based structural changes of the Zn-based MOF, ZnMOF-1, in water (Fig. 5) [92]. The researchers found that ZnMOF-1 could react with liquid water in two separate routes: one which involved multiple structural changes to eventually produce a water-stable Zn MOF, while the other pathway generated an unstable intermediate MOF that eventually decomposed [92]. 1H–13C CP/MAS and 1H MAS SSNMR reflected continuous changes in the local linker structure during structural evolution indicative of phase/structural changes in the overall MOF (Fig. 5). 1H and 13C SSNMR spectra suggested that guest dimethylformamide (DMF) remained in the ZnMOF-1 pores for an extended period of time, which prevented water from rapidly entering the framework and allowed for the material to evolve through several intermediates to eventually produce a water-stable Zn MOF. Investigating host-guest interaction in MOFs via SSNMR

Host-guest interactions in MOFs play a pivotal role that underpins many specific applications [93]. The diverse structures and various functional groups present in MOFs can give rise to a multitude of host-guest interactions such as coordinative, electrostatic, hydrogen bonding, and π─π interactions, among others, which makes molecular-level investigations of these interactions an utmost priority to further develop tailored materials [94]. The wide range of MOF applications means that host-guest studies inevitably involve a plethora of guest molecules, ranging from gaseous and liquid guests such as hydrogen, carbon dioxide, water, and methanol to larger guest molecules like drugs and biomolecules. Investigating host-guest interactions in MOFs can involve several NMR interactions; generally, the frequency, width, and shape of a SSNMR resonance is strongly influenced by CSA, magnetic dipolar coupling, and J-coupling interactions. Additionally, the QI exerts significant spectral influence when I > 1/2. While the combination of these NMR interactions can produce complicated spectra, the frequencies and features of the corresponding resonances often yield detailed local information on host-guest interactions and guest behavior. In recent years, many SSNMR approaches have offered unique insight regarding the local structure and dynamics of adsorbed guest molecules in MOFs, including in situ variable temperature (VT), 2D correlation, and pulsed field gradient (PFG) experiments [95]. The information available from such experiments includes the number and location of guest adsorption sites, guest binding strengths, guest dynamics and diffusion rates, and guest-induced changes in the host structure, which is invaluable for furthering our understanding of structure-property relationships in MOFs.

13C and 2H SSNMR experiments in conjunction with isotopic labeling are commonly used to investigate host-guest interactions in MOFs, since many simple guest molecules can be easily enriched with either isotope. The use of isotopic enrichment is often necessary to perform 13C or 2H SSNMR experiments targeting guest molecules, owing to the low n.a. of both isotopes and potential background interference from the framework signals. When isotopic labeling of guests is employed, the 13C and 2H signals originating from the framework are of negligible intensity or are wholly undetectable. Other labelling strategies are possible, including the use of NMR-active 17O.

The most abundant greenhouse gas on Earth is CO2, and a promising strategy for mitigating CO2 emissions is sequestration and capture using MOFs. In 2012, the Reimer group documented a novel use of VT 13C SSNMR to study 13CO2 adsorbed in MOF-74(Mg) [96]. They found that despite strong CO2 interaction with exposed Mg2+ open metal sites, there were significant dynamics of adsorbed CO2 molecules at the adsorption site. VT 13C NMR spectra obtained from 150 to 400 K showed that CO2 was engaged in uniaxial rotation at a fixed angle along the Mg–O bond vector. A following 17O SSNMR study by the Huang group targeting isotopically labeled C17O2 and leveraging the 17O EFG interaction proved there was both localized rotation, or “wobbling” of guest CO2 along with non-localized hopping of CO2 between OMSs [97]. In situ VT 13C SSNMR can also be applied to study CO adsorption in MOFs; like the prior case involving CO2, it was found that adsorbed CO guests in MOF-74(Mg, Zn) participate in a localized wobbling motion at the OMS along with a non-localized sixfold hopping motion of CO between OMSs [98]. The 2H nucleus is spin-1 and has a SSNMR lineshape that is typically dominated by the QI and is very sensitive to dynamics and the local environment. 2H SSNMR has been used to study adsorbed H2, light hydrocarbons, and water within MOFs. One example involves the use of VT 2H SSNMR to investigate adsorbed D2 dynamics in the MOF-74(Mg, Zn, Ni), UiO-66 and α-M3(HCOO)6(Mg, Zn) MOFs [99]. The D2 dynamics in each MOF were found to be distinct. The 2H resonances arising from D2 in UiO-66 were narrow and lacked QI features, suggesting that D2 was rapidly tumbling through space and that the host-guest adsorptive interaction was very weak. In contrast, the 2H SSNMR spectra of D2 in MOF-74(Mg, Zn, Ni) and α-M3(HCOO)6(Mg, Zn) were of considerable breadth and exhibited clear features arising from the QI, which indicated that these MOFs engaged in strong host-guest binding with D2. The lineshapes also yielded valuable information on adsorbed D2 dynamics.

SSNMR is a sensitive probe of gas separation processes in MOFs. In particular, separation of the noble gases Xe and Kr is of interest because both elements have radioactive isotopes and could pose environmental risks [100]. The 129Xe and 83Kr nuclei are viable targets for SSNMR experiments. 129Xe (I = 1/2) has a n.a. of 26.4% and a rather large chemical shift range of ca. 7500 ppm, which renders it a very sensitive probe for the investigation of pore structures in materials. Zeolites and porous metal oxides have been studied since the 1980s using 129Xe SSNMR experiments [101], and many MOFs have now also been examined. High pressure in situ 129Xe SSNMR was used alongside MD simulations in 2011 to study the flexible DUT-8(Ni) MOF, which exhibits a “gate-pressure effect” during gas adsorption [102]. The researchers interpreted 129Xe SSNMR spectra to indicate that DUT-8(Ni) had changed into the open-gate phase above a pressure of 12 bar, which allowed for the adsorption of Xe guests. 83Kr is more challenging to study via SSNMR, owing to its quadrupolar nature, small γ (=of −1.03310 × 107 rad T−1 s−1, and low n.a. of 11.5%. Recently, the first application of high-field 83Kr SSNMR studies on gas adsorption in eight MOFs was reported, with each material containing specific features such as ultra-microporosity, different functional groups, and OMSs [103]. 129Xe SSNMR experiments at 9.4 T were also performed. The authors separately loaded pure 129Xe and 83Kr gases, along with a gas mixture amounting to 2 Xe and 2 Kr per unit cell, into the α-Mg3(HCOO)6 MOF. This α-Mg3(HCOO)6 MOF is ultra-microporous and has one-dimensional (1D) zigzag channels (Fig. 6a). The 129Xe NMR spectrum of α-Mg3(HCOO)6 loaded with the Xe/Kr gas mixture produced a nearly identical resonance as that obtained of α-Mg3(HCOO)6 exposed to pure Xe (Fig. 6b), suggesting that the presence of Kr does not significantly affect Xe adsorption in this material. In contrast, the 83Kr NMR spectrum of α-Mg3(HCOO)6 dosed with a Xe/Kr mixture is dominated by the free Kr resonance, while the signal corresponding to strongly adsorbed Kr is very weak (Fig. 6c). These findings indicate that Kr adsorption sites are preferentially occupied by Xe, and reveal that the host-guest interactions between the MOF and Xe are much stronger than the MOF-Kr interactions. The relationship between water and MOFs is quite intricate. The strong coordination ability of water can influence or destroy MOF structural formation, which demands strict considerations on rational design; water is a ubiquitous molecule that impacts many realized and potential MOF applications. Capturing fresh water from air using structurally stable MOFs is one of the most impactful applications of MOFs [104]. MOFs with open metal sites (OMSs) can strongly adsorb water, even at low humidity levels. Xu et al. studied D2O adsorption in CPO-27-Mg/Zn using 2H VT SSNMR [105]. A single powder pattern indicated there was localized C2 π-flipping of adsorbed D2O in CPO-27-Mg (also named as MOF-74); the strong OMS-water interaction prevented hopping of guest D2O between Mg sites, yet CO2 and H2 could freely hop between adsorption sites. In CPO-27-Zn, a broad signal arose from D2O tightly bound to Zn OMSs, while a weaker, narrower resonance came from disordered D2O weakly interacting with Zn2+ sites. The Huang group recently examined the water adsorption mechanism in the yttrium-based Y-BTC MOF, which also features OMSs [106]. They acquired static 2H SSNMR spectra alongside Monte Carlo simulations that suggested there are three distinct water adsorption sites in Y-BTC MOF which exist at different relative humidity (RH) levels. At low RH (8%), two signals from different water species were observed: type 1 water tightly bound to Y3+ open metal sites with a CQ(2H)of 67 kHz and type 2 water adsorbed near BTC linkers with a CQ(2H) of 34 kHz. At higher RH levels of ≥23 %, a very more mobile type 3 water of CQ(2H) = 18 kHz within the pore center was also present. UiO-66 is another interesting case study. This MOF was long considered to be water-stable; however, a recent report revealed that adsorbed water molecules may displace the carboxylate groups coordinated to the Zr metal centers, generating “dangling” carboxylate species [107]. The authors confirmed this water-induced structural change via using a combination of 1H MAS, 13C CP/MAS SSNMR, 1H–1H 2D DQ-SQ correlation spectrum and DNP enhanced 13C CP/MAS spectra.

Significant MOF research has been focused on structural defects, as they significantly influence key properties such as gas separation, catalysis, and guest uptake capacity [108]. Defect characterization remains a significant challenge, since defect MOFs typically exhibit the same long-range structure as the parent MOF [109]. In 2021, the Kong group reported that UiO-66 defects enhance the uptake of pharmaceuticals containing phosphate or phosphonate groups [110]. The authors employed 31P MAS SSNMR, along with MD simulations and DFT calculations, to investigate host–guest interactions in this system (Fig. 7). They found that 31P MAS SSNMR resonances of adsorbed guests in defective UiO-66 did not match the corresponding solution NMR chemical shifts; the differences were attributed to guest coordination with defect Zr centers. The docking conformations of these guest molecules in defective UiO-66 were successfully determined by combining 31P SSNMR data with MD simulations. In their subsequent work, Kong's group used multinuclear SSNMR techniques including 31P MAS NMR, 13C MAS NMR, 19F MAS NMR, and 13C–19F REDOR experiments to probe defects in UiO-66 [111] and Mg2(dobpdc) [112]. Conclusions and outlook

Over the past thirty years, the field of MOFs has rapidly developed into the largest branch of research in porous materials. A spectacular array of MOFs featuring innumerable structural variations have demonstrated many potential applications and are plausible targets for SSNMR studies. Many metallic and organic components of MOFs can be directly analyzed by SSNMR. Lineshape analysis of SSNMR spectra, especially when paired with ab initio quantum chemical calculations, provides a wealth of valuable information regarding guest dynamics and host-guest interactions. There is a constantly increasing availability of high magnetic fields and continual progress in hardware advances, such as DNP and cryogenic probes. When these innovations are considered along with the advent of new pulse sequences and data acquisition technologies, it is clear that challenging unreceptive nuclei, especially in porous materials, are now viable experimental targets. SSNMR studies on MOFs can provide valuable information that is very difficult to obtain using other characterization techniques. SSNMR experiments can readily yield MOF information such as the number of metal sites, degree of local order, distribution of mixed linkers, selected modification of the linkers, guest location, the relationship between guest molecules and host functional groups, and guest-induced structural changes.

Several decades of development have progressed MOFs from academic laboratory research towards practical industrial applications. The increasing complexity of MOFs designed to address everyday real-life applications poses significant challenges from a SSNMR perspective. While the effectiveness of in situ SSNMR techniques has clearly demonstrated that the MOF structures and host-guest interaction can be closely monitored across variable temperatures and pressures, most in situ conditions remain distant from actual industrial operating conditions. For example, in practical gas separation scenarios, the gas mixture generated from hydrocarbon steam cracking typically encompasses a wide variety of molecules with similar properties; current in situ SSNMR studies normally examine gas streams containing only one to three components. Furthermore, while a large number of MOFs are used in catalytic processes such as photocatalysis, thermocatalysis, and electrocatalysis, SSNMR studies focusing on MOF performance and reaction mechanisms in these catalytic processes are challenging to perform.

Some efforts have begun to employ in situ and in operando SSNMR to explore the applications and mechanisms of MOFs for catalytic ends. For example, Long et al. used in operando MAS SSNMR to study the controllable selective synthesis of phenyltetrahydroquinoline or phenylquinoline by UiO-66-supported Pd catalysts in 2020 [113]. The in operando direct polarization 13C MAS-NMR spectra identified six transient intermediates and ten transformation steps. Notably, the key intermediate 2-phenyl-3,4-dihydroquinoline, which is undetectable by conventional chromatographic techniques, was found to reach a maximum concentration of 0.11 mol L−1 under in operando conditions. This work demonstrates that in operando SSNMR techniques have tremendous potential for the detection and quantification of reactive intermediates that are otherwise inaccessible by conventional ex situ methods.

The Nobel Prize in chemistry will undoubtedly accelerate MOF development, but at the same time, associated research and applications will become more complex and demanding. In the forthcoming years, SSNMR experimental design, hardware, and pulse sequences must continue to realize significant advances to keep pace with this rapidly evolving field. Among the rapid advances in solid-state NMR, DNP holds much promise. DNP techniques can overcome the inherent insensitivity of NMR and enhance signal intensities by two to three orders of magnitude [114]. These sensitivity gains, combined with the increasing availability of high magnetic fields and fast MAS frequencies, will enable researchers to further probe crucial MOF features such as surfaces, defect sites, and dilute species in increasing detail [75,115]. DNP has made experiments targeting high-resolution quadrupolar nuclei and those involving ultra-wideline spectra over 10,000 ppm much more feasible. MOFs can also serve as matrices for DNP polarizing agents, allowing for homogeneous radical distribution and further DNP enhancement in a wide variety of frameworks. This mutually reinforcing relationship highlights the utility of DNP advances in the MOF research field, unlocking the door to further advances in the years to come.
