Mixing and Small-Scale Formation in a Passive Divergence-Free Vector Field
Anuj Kumar, Franziska Weber

Abstract
We study mixing for a divergence-free passive vector field u u transported by another divergence-free vector field U U , where u u evolves according to ∂ t u + ( U ⋅ ∇ ) u + ∇ p = 0 . \partial_{t}u+(U\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p=0. In recent years, a lot of attention has been given to the question of optimal mixing in the scalar case, where there is a Sobolev constraint on the advecting velocity. In the vector setting considered here, however, the pressure term introduces substantial difficulties, since the simple Lagrangian perspective available in the scalar case is no longer applicable. In this paper, we investigate mixing on a torus 𝕋 d \mathbb{T}^{d} under the assumption that the field U U satisfies ‖ U ⁡ ( t ) ‖ W 1 , q ≤ C \|U(t)\|_{W^{1,q}}\leq C and we quantify mixing through the decay of the homogeneous H − α H^{-\alpha} norm of u u . We start with establishing conditions on U U that guarantee existence and uniqueness of solutions. We then derive lower bounds on the mixing rate for various ranges of q q and α \alpha . In addition, we carry out numerical simulations of mixing by choosing, at each time instant, a field U U that maximizes the instantaneous decay of the H − α H^{-\alpha} norm. These simulations provide evidence that the optimal mixing rate is at least exponential in time. More broadly, we view the present model and its diffusive analogue, as a useful framework for probing mechanisms of small-scale formation in divergence-free vector fields and for formulating simplified versions of open questions related to the incompressible Euler and Navier–Stokes equations.



1
Introduction

Mixing plays a crucial role both in engineering and in natural fluid flows. In chemical engineering, for example, mixing facilitates the desired reaction rates by ensuring thorough interaction of substances
[
24
]
. It also prevents stratification and leads to homogeneous fluid properties, which is crucial in applications like heat exchangers and chemical reactors
[
25
]
. In the oceans, mixing drives the meridional overturning circulation, which in turn, is responsible for the state of climate
[
23
,
33
]
. Furthermore, mixing in oceans is vital for the upward transport of nutrients from the seafloor to the surface, an essential component for plankton growth
[
6
]
.

Motivated by the considerations above, a natural question from a mathematical perspective is how fast a scalar can be mixed for a given budget of the incompressible advecting vector field
U
. In the past two decades, this question has gained a lot of attention, particularly in the case where the advecting vector field
U
belongs to the Sobolev space
W^{1,q}
for
q\in[1,\infty]
. Lin, Thiffeault and Doering investigated this question using numerical methods
[
22
]
. They demonstrated an exponential mixing rate when the velocity field is constrained in
W^{1,2}
. They quantified mixing through the decay of the
H^{-1}
-norm of the scalar. Crippa and De Lellis
[
7
]
, using a Lagrangian approach, rigorously established an exponentially decaying lower bound for the mixing rate (see also Seis
[
27
]
and Iyer, Kiselev & Xu
[
17
]
) when
U\in W^{1,q}
for
q>1
, making an important progress on Bressan’s conjecture
[
2
]
. Subsequent work by Alberti, Crippa & Mazzucato
[
1
]
, Yao & Zlatos
[
34
]
and Elgindi & Zlatos
[
14
]
provided constructions of the advecting vector field
W^{1,q}
for which the scalar exhibits an exponential mixing rate, demonstrating the sharpness of the lower bound established by Crippa and De Lellis.

While small scales in the velocity field are introduced in the scalar setting to produce mixing, a natural next interesting question is how such small scales arise in the velocity field in the first place. From a physical perspective, mixing, or more generally, the generation of small scales in the velocity field, is essential for turbulent flows to exhibit enhanced or anomalous dissipation
[
29
,
16
]
. Although our ultimate goal is to understand the formation of small scales at the level of the incompressible Euler equations, in this paper we restrict our attention to the following model, for several reasons explained below.

The primary purpose of this paper is to investigate mixing of an incompressible vector field
u:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
governed by the system of equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+(U\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p

\displaystyle=0,\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{T}^{d},

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}u

\displaystyle=0,\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{T}^{d},

(1.1b)

\displaystyle u(0,x)

\displaystyle=u_{0}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}

(1.1c)

where
U:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a smooth divergence-free advecting vector field and, for some
q\in[1,\infty]
, satisfies

\left\lVert U(t)\right\rVert_{W^{1,q}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C,

(1.2)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and some constant
C<\infty
, and a suitable mean-zero initial condition
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Here,
\mathbb{T}^{d}
is the
d
-dimensional torus of side length
L
.

Next, we explain the general reasons for working with this particular model. We also highlight several useful features, viewpoints and challenges associated with it that ultimately motivated our choice.

(i)

The main reason we study vector mixing in this model, rather than for example the linearized Euler equations around
U
or the vorticity equation, is that model (
1.1
) has two important structural properties: (a) it admits a natural energy (
L^{2}
) balance, and (b) when
u=U
, it reduces to the incompressible Euler equations. Since our ultimate goal is to understand the formation of small scales in Euler (and eventually Navier–Stokes), this provides a natural route: one may first analyze the more flexible model (
1.1
) and then, in future work, try to close the gap between
U
and
u
.

(ii)

We view model (
1.1
) as a more flexible version of the incompressible Euler equations (or of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, if one adds the term
\nu\Delta u
to the right-hand side of (
1.1b
)). In the fluid equations, one requires
u
and
U
to coincide pointwise. In contrast, in (
1.1
) one may impose only a weaker constraint that
U
and
u
are “structurally similar”. For instance, one could impose a constraint of the form
U=\mathbb{P}(u\circ\chi),

for some
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism
\chi
, where
\mathbb{P}
denotes the Leray projector. In this preliminary study, however, we take a more permissive viewpoint that
U
and
u
are completely independent.

(iii)

This added flexibility allows one to build and test various plausible mechanisms for generating small scales. For example, one may construct a candidate mechanism in
U
, and then investigate whether these mechanisms induce small-scale formation in
u
. If a proposed mechanism already fails at the level of model (
1.1
), then it is unlikely to hold for the Euler equations. In this way, the model serves as a useful testing ground for identifying which mechanisms may truly be relevant.

(iv)

Mathematically, the creation of small scales in equations (
1.1
) is considerably more challenging than in the scalar transport equation. In the transport equation, the scalar
\theta
follows the Lagrangian trajectories of the velocity field
U
, and mixing can be induced by generating small scales in
U
. This idea has been used extensively in the study of anomalous dissipation in passive scalars
[
11
,
13
]
, optimal mixing of scalars
[
34
,
1
]
, optimal scalar transport
[
10
,
21
,
32
]
, and even problems related to the nonuniqueness of solutions to the transport equation
[
8
,
20
,
5
]
. For the vector equation considered here, however, such an approach is no longer available. The pressure term introduces a substantial new difficulty and renders the Lagrangian viewpoint essentially inapplicable. At present, it is not even clear whether the pressure enhances or suppresses the formation of small scales. The framework studied in this paper provides a way to isolate and investigate the role of pressure, without the additional complications introduced by nonlinear terms.

(v)

Although in this paper we restrict our attention to mixing, we believe that model (
1.1
) may serve a broader purpose, namely, as a framework for probing conjectures related to the incompressible Euler and Navier–Stokes equations, for instance those concerning anomalous dissipation or singularity formation. Broadly speaking, one may ask the following question for model (
1.1
) (and, respectively, for the model with the added diffusion term
\nu\Delta u
): if one endows
U
with certain conjectured properties, do corresponding properties emerge in
u
? If this question cannot be answered even at the level of (
1.1
) (or its diffusive analogue), then resolving the original conjecture for Euler (or Navier–Stokes) is likely to be substantially more difficult. We outline two such questions of this type in the concluding section.

Once the model has been fixed, we now describe the main objectives of the paper and the questions we address.

Our goal is to investigate the maximal rate of mixing of the vector field
u
under a prescribed Sobolev
W^{1,q}
budget on the advecting velocity field
U
. As in the scalar setting
[
22
,
31
,
17
,
34
,
1
]
, we quantify mixing by means of the homogeneous norm
{H}^{-\alpha}
, for
1/2\leq\alpha\leq 1
, namely
\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{-\alpha}}
. More precisely, for a given
W^{1,q}
budget on
U
, our goal is to study

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|U\|_{W^{1,q}}\leq 1\\
\operatorname{div}U=0\end{subarray}}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{{H}^{-\alpha}}

as a function of time. In this framework, decay of the
{H}^{-\alpha}
norm of
u
is interpreted as a measure of the rate at which
u
generates small scales.

In
Section

2
, we establish conditions on
U
that ensure existence, uniqueness, and stability of solutions
u
to (
1.1
). In
Section

3
, we study the mixing rate and, in particular, derive lower bounds for mixing for various ranges of
q
and
\alpha
. In
Section

4
, we focus on the case of a
W^{1,2}
budget and perform numerical simulations of optimal mixing by identifying vector fields
U
that instantaneously minimize the
\|\cdot\|_{{H}^{-\alpha}}
norm of
u
. Finally, in
Section

5
, we conclude with a discussion of some directions for future work.

2
Existence of solutions

We start by showing that solutions exist to system (
1.1
). We do so via an approximation by a standard spectral method that will also be used later on to carry out numerical simulations. To define the method, we consider the Fourier transform for
f(t)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,

f(t,x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}_{k}(t)e^{i2\pi\frac{k\cdot x}{L}},\quad\hat{f}_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{L^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(t,x)e^{-i2\pi\frac{k\cdot x}{L}}\,{\rm d}x.

(2.1)

Given a velocity field
U
, we define its
truncation up to
N\in\mathbb{N}
modes as:

P_{N}U(t,x):=U_{N}(t,x)=\sum_{|k|_{\infty}\leq N}\hat{U}_{k}(t)e^{i2\pi\frac{k\cdot x}{L}},

(2.2)

where
|k|_{\infty}=\max_{j=1,\dots,d}|k_{j}|
. Then, we can define the following spectral scheme:
For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
u_{N}(t,x)=\sum_{|k|_{\infty}\leq N}\hat{u}_{k}^{N}(t)e^{i2\pi\frac{k\cdot x}{L}}
, where the
\hat{u}_{k}^{N}
are the solution of the following Cauchy problem:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{N}+\mathbf{P}_{N}((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})

\displaystyle=0,

(2.3a)

\displaystyle u_{N}(0)=\mathbf{P}_{N}u_{0},

(2.3b)

here
\mathbf{P}_{N}
is the projection onto divergence free functions and Fourier modes with frequency less than
N
, i.e.,

\mathbf{P}_{N}f(t,x)=\sum_{|k|_{\infty}\leq N}\left(\hat{f}_{k}(t)-\frac{\hat{f}_{k}(t)\cdot k}{|k|^{2}}k\right)e^{i2\pi\frac{k\cdot x}{L}},\quad\hat{f}_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{L^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(t,x)e^{-i2\pi\frac{k\cdot x}{L}}dx.

(2.4)

We note that
\mathbf{P}_{N}
is self-adjoint,

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathbf{P}_{N}f\cdot gdx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f\cdot\mathbf{P}_{N}gdx,

and satisfies
\mathbf{P}_{N}^{2}=\mathbf{P}_{N}
.
We also note that since

\operatorname{div}v=\frac{i2\pi}{L}\sum_{k}(\hat{v}_{k}\cdot k)e^{i2\pi\frac{k\cdot x}{L}},

we have that
v
is divergence free if and only if its Fourier coefficients satisfy
k\perp\hat{v}_{k}
for all
k
.
We also note that the projection onto the first
N
Fourier modes satisfies for sufficiently smooth functions,
s\geq 0
,

\left\lVert(I-P_{N})f\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq C_{s}\psi(N)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(2.5)

Here we denoted by
H^{s}
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
the homogeneous fractional Sobolev space norm (with the convention that
H^{0}:=L^{2}
):

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}:=\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{\alpha}\left\lVert\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|k|^{\alpha}\hat{f}_{k}e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L}k\cdot x}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

Now we can show the following:

Lemma 2.1

(Existence of solutions)
.

A weak solution
u
of the equations (
1.1
) exists for any initial data
u_{0}\in L^{2}_{\operatorname{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, in the sense that
u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+};L^{2}_{\operatorname{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(u\cdot\partial_{t}\varphi+[(U\cdot\nabla)\varphi]\cdot u\right)dxdt+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u_{0}\cdot\varphi(0,x)dx=0,

(2.6)

for any smooth and compactly in time supported test function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
.
In addition, the time derivative
\partial_{t}u
satisfies

\partial_{t}u\in\begin{cases}L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+};H^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\quad q>d,\\
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+};W^{-1,\frac{2dq}{dq+2d-2q}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\quad q<d.\end{cases}

Remark 2.2
.

Using the integrability of
u
and
U
, one can use the density of smooth functions to show that (
2.6
) is satisfied for any test function in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
when
q>d
and
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};W^{1,\frac{2dq}{dq-2d+2q}}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
when
q<d
.

Proof.

Scheme (
2.3
) is a system of coupled ODEs for the
\hat{u}^{N}_{k}(t)
,
|k|_{\infty}\leq N
and since the right hand side expressions are polynomials of
\hat{u}_{k}^{N}
, they are locally Lipschitz, and short time existence of a unique solution follows. In order to get existence for arbitrary times, we show a uniform in time and
N

L^{2}
-bound on
u_{N}(t)
: We multiply the scheme by
u_{N}
and integrate over the domain:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u_{N}\cdot\partial_{t}u_{N}dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u_{N}\cdot\mathbf{P}_{N}((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathbf{P}_{N}u_{N}\cdot((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u_{N}\cdot((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})dx

\displaystyle=0.

Thus

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert u_{N}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u_{N}\cdot\partial_{t}u_{N}dx=0,

and hence the
L^{2}
-norm of
u_{N}
satisfies for any
t\in(0,\infty)

\left\lVert u_{N}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\left\lVert u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Since
\left\lVert u_{N}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{|k|_{\infty}\leq N}|\hat{u}_{k}^{N}(t)|^{2}
, this implies a uniform in time bound on the
\hat{u}_{k}^{N}
and hence we can extend the solution of the ODE to all times. Since this estimate is uniform in
N
, we can also extract a weak* convergent subsequence in
L^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{+};L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
that converges to
u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+};L^{2}_{\operatorname{div}})
which is a weak solution of (
1.1
) thanks to the linearity of the equation. That the limit satisfies the divergence constraint almost everywhere follows since it is satisfied for each of the approximations and due to the linearity of the constraint. Due to the weak lower semicontinuity of the limit, we obtain

\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\left\lVert u_{N}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}},\quad\textrm{a.e. }\,t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

To show the estimate on the time derivative, we consider once more the numerical scheme and multiply it by a suitable test function. When
q>d
, we multiply by a test function

\varphi\in L^{1}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
and integrate:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{t}u_{N}\cdot\varphi dxdt\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathbf{P}_{N}\varphi\cdot(U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N}dxdt\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u_{N}\cdot(U_{N}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{P}_{N}\varphi dxdt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}\left\lVert U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}\left\lVert\nabla\mathbf{P}_{N}\varphi\right\rVert_{L^{1}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}\left\lVert U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}W^{1,q}_{x}}\left\lVert\nabla\varphi\right\rVert_{L^{1}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert\nabla\varphi\right\rVert_{L^{1}_{t}L^{2}_{x}},

thus
\partial_{t}u_{N}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
uniformly in
N
. If
q<d
, we instead estimate

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{t}u_{N}\cdot\varphi dxdt\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathbf{P}_{N}\varphi\cdot(U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N}dxdt\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u_{N}\cdot(U_{N}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{P}_{N}\varphi dxdt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}\left\lVert U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{q^{*}}_{t,x}}\left\lVert\nabla\mathbf{P}_{N}\varphi\right\rVert_{L^{1}_{t}L^{\frac{2q^{*}}{q^{*}-2}}_{x}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}\left\lVert U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}W^{1,q}_{x}}\left\lVert\nabla\varphi\right\rVert_{L^{1}_{t}L^{\frac{2q^{*}}{q^{*}-2}}_{x}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert\nabla\varphi\right\rVert_{L^{1}_{t}L^{\frac{2q^{*}}{q^{*}-2}}_{x}},

where
q^{*}=\frac{dq}{d-q}
is the Sobolev conjugate of
q
. Hence in this case
\partial_{t}u_{N}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{-1,\frac{2dq}{dq+2d-2q}}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
uniformly in
N
and thus the same holds for the limit.
∎

However, we do not know whether these weak solutions are unique or satisfy an energy equality instead of the inequality above. If
U
has more regularity, we can deduce higher order regularity for
u
also and show that the solution of (
1.1
) is unique.
We start by deriving a uniform
H^{1}
-norm on the approximations produced by (
2.3
):

Lemma 2.3
.

If
U\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+};W^{1,q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
and is divergence free, and
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, then the limit
u_{N}
obtained through the spectral scheme (
2.3
) satisfies for almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\left\lVert\nabla u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla U(s)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}ds\right).

(2.7)

Moreover,
\partial_{t}u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
.

Proof.

We derive the bound at the level of the approximations
u_{N}
, and can then, since it is uniform in
N
, pass to the limit
N\to\infty
to obtain a bound for the limit.
To derive an estimate on the gradient of
u_{N}
, we multiply the scheme (
2.3
) by
-\Delta u_{N}
, integrate, and integrate by parts to obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\nabla u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta u_{N}\partial_{t}u_{N}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta u_{N}\cdot\mathbf{P}_{N}((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta\mathbf{P}_{N}u_{N}\cdot((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta u_{N}\cdot((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla u_{N}:\nabla((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla u_{N}:((\nabla U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})dx-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla u_{N}:((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)\nabla u_{N})dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla u_{N}:((\nabla U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})dx

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\nabla u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\left\lVert\nabla U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}.

Using Grönwall’s inequality and
\left\lVert\nabla u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\left\lVert\nabla u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}
, we obtain

\left\lVert\nabla u_{N}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\lVert\nabla u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\exp\left(2\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla U_{N}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}ds\right),

thus if

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\nabla U(s)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}ds\leq C,\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{1}}\leq C,

we obtain that
u_{N}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
uniformly in
N
. Using the
H^{1}
-regularity of
u_{N}
and the Lipschitz continuity of
U
, we can also deduce a better estimate on the time derivative by squaring the equation (
2.3
) and integrating over space and then estimating:

\left\lVert\partial_{t}u_{N}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\left\lVert U_{N}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert\nabla u_{N}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C,

thus in this case
\partial_{t}u_{N}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
, uniformly in
N
.
∎

In a similar way, we can derive an estimate on the
H^{2}
-norm of the limit
u
:

Lemma 2.4
.

If
U\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+};W^{1,q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};W^{2,3}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
and is divergence free, and
u_{0}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\cap H^{1}_{\operatorname{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, then the limit
u_{N}
obtained through the spectral scheme (
2.3
) satisfies for almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

\left\lVert\nabla^{2}u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\left\lVert\nabla^{2}u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(\left\lVert\Delta U(s)\right\rVert_{L^{3}}+\left\lVert\nabla U(s)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}})ds\right).

(2.8)

Proof.

Again, we do all the estimates at the level of the approximation
u_{N}
and then since they are uniform with respect to
N
, we can pass
N\to\infty
to obtain them for the limit
u
.
First we note that, since we are using periodic boundary conditions, we have

\left\lVert\Delta f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\left\lVert\nabla^{2}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

using integration by parts. Then we multiply the numerical scheme (
2.3
) by
\Delta^{2}u_{N}
, integrate over the domain and integrate by parts:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\Delta u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta^{2}u_{N}\partial_{t}u_{N}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta^{2}u_{N}\cdot\mathbf{P}_{N}((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta^{2}u_{N}\cdot((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta u_{N}\cdot\Delta((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta u_{N}\cdot\operatorname{div}((\nabla U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N}+(U_{N}\cdot\nabla)\nabla u_{N})dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta u_{N}\cdot((\Delta U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N}+2(\nabla U_{N}\cdot\nabla)\nabla u_{N}+(U_{N}\cdot\nabla)\Delta u_{N})dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta u_{N}\cdot((\Delta U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N}+2(\nabla U_{N}\cdot\nabla)\nabla u_{N})dx

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\Delta u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert\nabla u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{6}}\left\lVert\Delta U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{3}}+\left\lVert\Delta u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert\nabla^{2}u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert\nabla U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\left\lVert\Delta U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{3}}+\left\lVert\nabla U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}})\left\lVert\Delta u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Again, with Grönwall’s inequality, we obtain

\left\lVert\Delta u_{N}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\left\lVert\Delta u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(\left\lVert\Delta U_{N}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{3}}+\left\lVert\nabla U_{N}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}})ds\right),

thus if
U
is bounded in
L^{1}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\cap W^{2,3}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
, and the initial data is in
H^{2}
, then we obtain that
u_{N}
is uniformly contained in
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
.
∎

When
U
is Lipschitz continuous in space and therefore
u
is
H^{1}
-regular in space, we can show that the solution is unique and that the spectral approximations
u_{N}
converge strongly to
u
with a convergence rate depending on the approximation quality of the Fourier truncations.
Let us start by showing uniqueness, which also implies stability.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
U\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+};W^{1,q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
for some
q\in(1,\infty]
, then weak solutions of (
1.1
) are unique and satisfy the stability estimate: For weak solutions
u
and
v
with initial data
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
respectively, we have

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|u(t,x)-v(t,x)|^{2}dx\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|u_{0}-v_{0}|^{2}dx.

Proof.

Assume first that
u_{0},v_{0}\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, and hence the corresponding weak solutions also satisfy
u,v\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+};H^{1}_{\operatorname{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
. Subtracting the weak formulations for the two solutions from each other, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(u-v)\cdot\partial_{\tau}\varphi dxd\tau+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(u_{0}-v_{0})\cdot\varphi(0,x)dx=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(U\cdot\nabla)\varphi\cdot(u-v)dxd\tau.

For a given
t>0
, we let
\theta_{\epsilon}(\tau)=\mathbf{1}_{(r,t)}\star\omega_{\epsilon}(\tau)
, where
\omega_{\epsilon}(s)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\omega(s/\epsilon)
and
\omega
is a mollifier (nonnegative, symmetric and compactly supported with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\omega(s)ds=1
) and
r>\epsilon>0
is a small number.
Then we take
\varphi=(u-v)\theta_{\epsilon}
as a test function, which is admissible since it is divergence free and in
L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap W^{1,\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
and compactly supported in time. Then we get

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(|u-v|^{2}\cdot\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}|u-v|^{2}\theta_{\epsilon}\right)dxd\tau+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|u_{0}-v_{0}|^{2}\theta_{\epsilon}(0)dx\\
=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(U\cdot\nabla)(u-v)\cdot(u-v)\theta_{\epsilon}(t)dxd\tau.

Note that since
r>\epsilon
, we have
\theta_{\epsilon}(0)=0
.
Integrating by parts and using that
U
is divergence free and
\theta_{\epsilon}
is compactly supported, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|u-v|^{2}\cdot\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\epsilon}dxd\tau=0.

(2.9)

We have

\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\epsilon}(\tau)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}_{(r,t)}(s)\partial_{\tau}\omega_{\epsilon}(\tau-s)ds=-\int_{r}^{t}\partial_{s}\omega_{\epsilon}(\tau-s)ds=\omega_{\epsilon}(\tau-r)-\omega_{\epsilon}(\tau-t).

Using this, (
2.9
) becomes

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|u-v|^{2}(\omega_{\epsilon}(\tau-t)-\omega_{\epsilon}(r-\tau))dxd\tau=0.

Letting
\epsilon\to 0
and using the time continuity of
u
and
v
, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|u(t)-v(t)|^{2}dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|u(r)-v(r)|^{2}dx.

Since we could choose
r
arbitrarily small, we can use time continuity of
u
and
v
to conclude that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|u(t)-v(t)|^{2}dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|u_{0}-v_{0}|^{2}dx.

(2.10)

For general
u_{0},v_{0}\in L^{2}_{\operatorname{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, we approximate them by smooth functions (e.g. by mollifying them), thus producing sufficiently regular
u
and
v
by the previous Lemma
2.3
. Then we note that (
2.10
) only depends on the
L^{2}
-norms of
u(t)
,
v(t)
,
u_{0}
,
v_{0}
, thus we can pass to the limit in the approximation and obtain the result for the limiting
u_{0},v_{0}\in L^{2}_{\operatorname{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. The uniqueness follows by setting
u_{0}=v_{0}
.
∎

Next, we prove a convergence rate of the spectral scheme (
2.3
).

Lemma 2.6

(Convergence rate)
.

If
U\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+};W^{1,q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
for some
q\in(1,\infty]
and
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, then the approximations
u_{N}
computed by the spectral scheme (
2.3
) satisfy

\left\lVert u-u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}(t)\leq C\left\lVert U-P_{N}U\right\rVert_{L^{1}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}}+CN^{-1}\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{1}_{x}}\exp\left(\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{L^{1}_{t}W^{1,\infty}_{x}}\right),

(2.11)

where
\left\lVert U-P_{N}U\right\rVert_{L^{1}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}}
goes to zero due to the approximation properties of the Fourier truncation
P_{N}
.

Proof.

We consider
u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u
, where
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
is a weak solution of (
1.1
) and
u_{N}
the approximation with the spectral scheme (
2.3
) for the corresponding
U
and initial data
u_{0}
. Due to the truncation,
\mathbf{P}_{N}u
is smooth in space, and therefore satisfies the PDE pointwise:

\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}_{N}u=\mathbf{P}_{N}((U\cdot\nabla)u).

Thus, the difference
u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u
satisfies

\partial_{t}(u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u)=\mathbf{P}_{N}\left((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N}-(U\cdot\nabla)u\right).

We multiply this equation by
u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u
, integrate, use chain rule and that
\mathbf{P}_{N}(u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u)=u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\mathbf{P}_{N}u-u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u)\cdot\partial_{t}(u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u)dx

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathbf{P}_{N}\left((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N}-(U\cdot\nabla)u\right)\cdot(u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u)dx

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N}-(U\cdot\nabla)u\right)\cdot(u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u)dx

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(((U_{N}-U)\cdot\nabla)u)\cdot(u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u)dx-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)(u-u_{N}))\cdot(u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u)dx

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\lVert U-U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)(u-\mathbf{P}_{N}u))\cdot(u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u)dx

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)(\mathbf{P}_{N}u-u_{N}))\cdot(u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u)dx

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\lVert U-U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert u-\mathbf{P}_{N}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert u_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{N}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Hence

\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\mathbf{P}_{N}u-u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert U-U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\left\lVert U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert u-\mathbf{P}_{N}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Since
\mathbf{P}_{N}u(0)-u_{N}(0)=0
, and both
U
and
u
are divergence free, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{P}_{N}u-u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\lVert U-\mathbf{P}_{N}U\right\rVert_{L^{1}(0,t;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}+\left\lVert u-\mathbf{P}_{N}u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}\right)

\displaystyle=C\left(\left\lVert U-P_{N}U\right\rVert_{L^{1}(0,t;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}+\left\lVert u-P_{N}u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}\right)

and in particular,

\displaystyle\left\lVert u-u_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\lVert U-P_{N}U\right\rVert_{L^{1}(0,t;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}+\left\lVert u-P_{N}u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\lVert U-P_{N}U\right\rVert_{L^{1}(0,t;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}+N^{-1}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{1}_{x}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\lVert U-P_{N}U\right\rVert_{L^{1}(0,t;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}+N^{-1}\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{1}_{x}}\exp\left(\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{L^{1}_{t}W^{1,\infty}_{x}}\right)\right),

using the approximation properties of the Fourier projection
P_{N}
and Lemma
2.3
.
The right hand side goes to zero at a rate depending on the smoothness of
U
and
u
as
n\to\infty
.

∎

3
Mixing estimates

The goal of this section is to derive estimates on the minimal mixing time of weak solutions of (
1.1
) in the
H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
norm for
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
. All the estimates can be derived at the level of the approximations
u_{N}
by the spectral scheme (
2.3
). As we shall see, these estimates are uniform in
N
and thus we can take the limit
N\to\infty
.
For any
\alpha>0
, following
[
22
]
, let us define

(-\Delta)^{\alpha}\varphi_{N}:=\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2\alpha}\sum_{|k|_{\infty}\leq N}|k|^{2\alpha}\hat{\varphi}_{k}(t)e^{i2\pi\frac{k\cdot x}{L}}:=u_{N},

and

|\nabla|^{\alpha}\varphi_{N}:=\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{\alpha}\sum_{|k|_{\infty}\leq N}|k|^{\alpha}\hat{\varphi}_{k}(t)e^{i2\pi\frac{k\cdot x}{L}}

Therefore,
\hat{\varphi}_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{|k|^{2\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{\alpha}\hat{u}_{k}(t)
and

\left\lVert u_{N}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=\left\lVert|\nabla|^{\alpha}\varphi_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

Next, we make the following computation for the time evolution of
\left\lVert u_{N}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}
using the PDE (
2.3
):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert u_{N}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert|\nabla|^{\alpha}\varphi_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varphi_{N}\cdot\partial_{t}u_{N}dx,

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathbf{P}_{N}((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})\cdot\varphi_{N}dx,

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)u_{N})\cdot\varphi_{N}dx,

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}((U_{N}\cdot\nabla)(-\Delta)^{\alpha}\varphi_{N})\cdot\varphi_{N}dx,

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}((|\nabla|^{\alpha}U_{N}\cdot\nabla)|\nabla|^{\alpha}\varphi_{N})\cdot\varphi_{N}dx,

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}((|\nabla|^{\alpha}U_{N}\cdot\nabla)\varphi_{N}):|\nabla|^{\alpha}\varphi_{N}dx.

We therefore obtain the following lower bound:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert u_{N}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{2}\geq-\left\lVert|\nabla|^{\alpha}U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{r}}\left\lVert|\nabla|^{\alpha}\varphi_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\frac{2r}{r-2}}}\left\lVert\nabla\varphi_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}},\end{split}

(3.1)

where
r\geq 1
is to be determined such that
\left\lVert|\nabla|^{\alpha}U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{r}}
is uniformly bounded with respect to
N
. We will distinguish the three cases
q(1-\alpha)<d
,
q(1-\alpha)=d
and
q(1-\alpha)>d
, since they will require either a fractional version of the Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev inequality or Morrey’s inequality. We will use
C
to denote a constant that can vary from line to line and may depend on
\alpha,d
and
q
but not on
N
or time
t
.

3.1
Case
q(1-\alpha)<d

This includes the case
\alpha=1
if
q\neq\infty
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that either
q(1-\alpha)<d
, or else
\alpha=1
and
q\neq\infty
. Then, we have for any
t\geq 0
,

\left\lVert u_{N}(t)\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}\geq\left[\left\lVert u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}-t\frac{C}{\alpha}\left\lVert\nabla U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}\left\lVert u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}\right]^{\frac{\alpha q}{d}}.

In particular, we have for any limit
u
of the sequence
u_{N}
,

\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}\geq\left[\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}-t\frac{C}{\alpha}\left\lVert\nabla U\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}\right]^{\frac{\alpha q}{d}}.

Thus the minimal mixing time is given by

t_{\min}=\frac{\alpha\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}}{C\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}\left\lVert\nabla U\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}}.

Proof.

We start with (
3.1
).
To find
r
, we use the fractional Sobolev inequality
[
9
, Thm. 6.5]
, or Theorem
A.1
in the Appendix, with
f=(|\nabla|^{\alpha}U_{N})_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,d
,

\left\lVert|\nabla|^{\alpha}U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{r}}\leq C\||\nabla|^{\alpha}U_{N}\|_{W^{1-\alpha,q}}=C\left\lVert\nabla U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{q}},

(3.2)

for
r=\frac{dq}{d-(1-\alpha)q}
, where we need
q(1-\alpha)<d
, and this estimate is independent of
N\in\mathbb{N}
due to the assumption (
1.2
).
We note that if
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
, the gradient of
\varphi
is a positive order Sobolev norm of
u
for which we may not have uniform bounds unless
U
also has higher regularity. As we assume that
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}
, we can use the fractional Sobolev interpolation inequality
[
3
]
, stated in the Appendix, Theorem
A.2
for
\left\lVert\nabla\varphi_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}
, using
s_{1}=\alpha
,
s_{2}=2\alpha
,
p=p_{1}=p_{2}=2
and
\theta=\frac{2\alpha-1}{\alpha}
, we get

\left\lVert\nabla\varphi_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\left\lVert\varphi_{N}\right\rVert_{W^{\alpha,2}}^{\frac{2\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\left\lVert\varphi_{N}\right\rVert_{W^{2\alpha,2}}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}=C\left\lVert u_{N}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{\frac{2\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\left\lVert u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}.

(3.3)

For the term
\left\lVert|\nabla|^{\alpha}\varphi_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\frac{2r}{r-2}}}
in (
3.1
), we use the fractional Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev inequality
[
4
, Thm 1]
, restated in the appendix in Theorem
A.3
. Taking in that theorem
\tilde{r}=0
,
s_{1}=0
,
s_{2}=\alpha
,
p_{1}=p_{2}=2
,
\tilde{q}=\frac{2r}{r-2}
, none of the exception cases state there apply, and thus we have

\left\lVert|\nabla|^{\alpha}\varphi_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\tilde{q}}}\leq C\left\lVert|\nabla|^{\alpha}\varphi_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\theta}\left\lVert(-\Delta)^{\alpha}\varphi_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1-\theta},

(3.4)

where
\theta
is given by

\theta=1+\frac{d}{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{q}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{2\tilde{q}\alpha+2d-\tilde{q}d}{2\tilde{q}\alpha}.

The value of
\theta
was obtained using the following:

\frac{1}{\tilde{q}}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{s}{d},\quad s=(1-\theta)\alpha.

Substituting
\tilde{q}=\frac{2r}{r-2}
, we get the beautiful expression:

\theta=1+\frac{d}{\alpha}\left(\frac{r-2}{2r}-\frac{1}{2}\right)=1-\frac{d}{\alpha r}=\frac{\alpha r-d}{\alpha r}.

(3.5)

Next, we use the expression we derived for
r
above:

\theta=\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{d}{\alpha q}=\frac{q-d}{\alpha q}.

We plug (
3.2
), (
3.3
), and (
3.4
) into (
3.1
) to obtain

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert u_{N}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{2}&\geq-C\left\lVert\nabla U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{q}}\left\lVert u_{N}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{\frac{2\alpha-1}{\alpha}+\frac{q-d}{\alpha q}}\left\lVert u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}+\frac{\alpha q-q+d}{\alpha q}}\\
&\geq-C\left\lVert\nabla U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{q}}\left\lVert u_{N}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{2-\frac{d}{\alpha q}}\left\lVert u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}.\end{split}

As we have
\frac{d}{\alpha q}>0
, so we can do the following manipulation:

\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert u_{N}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}\geq-\frac{d}{\alpha q}C\left\lVert\nabla U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{q}}\left\lVert u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}},

which integrates to

\left\lVert u_{N}(t)\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}\geq\left[\left\lVert u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}-\frac{d}{\alpha q}C\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla U_{N}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{q}}ds\left\lVert u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}\right]^{\frac{\alpha q}{d}}.

Since we have
\nabla U\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
, above expression simplifies to

\left\lVert u_{N}(t)\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}\geq\left[\left\lVert u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}-t\frac{C}{\alpha}\left\lVert\nabla U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}\left\lVert u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}\right]^{\frac{\alpha q}{d}}.

(3.6)

Now
u_{N}(0)\to u_{0}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
\nabla U_{N}\to\nabla U
in
L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
for any
T>0
, as
N\to\infty
and using that
L^{2}
is compactly embedded in
H^{-\alpha}
and the time continuity estimate from Lemma
2.1
, we can use the Aubin-Lions lemma
[
28
]
to obtain that
u_{N}\to u
in
C([0,T];H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
up to a subsequence as
N\to\infty
and thus we have in the limit:

\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}\geq\left[\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}-t\frac{C}{\alpha}\left\lVert\nabla U\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}\right]^{\frac{\alpha q}{d}}.

(3.7)

Thus the minimal mixing time is given by

t_{\min}=\frac{\alpha\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}}{C\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d}{\alpha q}}\left\lVert\nabla U\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}}.

∎

3.2
Case
q(1-\alpha)>d

This includes the case that
q=\infty
when
\alpha<1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
q
,
\alpha
, and
d
satisfy either
q(1-\alpha)>d
, or
q=\infty
and
\alpha<1
. Then, we have for any
t\geq 0
,

\left\lVert u_{N}(t)\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}\geq\left[\left\lVert u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}-t\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}C\left\lVert u_{N}(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\left\lVert U_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}W^{1,q}_{x}}\right]^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}.

In particular, we have for any limit
u
of the sequence
u_{N}
,

\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}\geq\left[\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}-t\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}C\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}W^{1,q}_{x}}\right]^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}.

Thus the minimal mixing time is given by

t_{\min}=\frac{\alpha\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}}{(1-\alpha)C\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}W^{1,q}_{x}}}.

Proof.

In this case, we obtain that
|\nabla|^{\alpha}U
is actually Hölder continuous by
[
9
, Thm. 8.2]
, restated in the Appendix as Theorem
A.4
. We first note that

Numerical Algorithm and Results

In this section, we perform numerical computations for mixing in two dimensions and provide evidence that an exponential mixing rate is achievable in the specific case
q=2
, that is, under the constraint
\|U\|_{W^{1,2}}\leq 1
. We work on a torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}

Our approach is to choose

[

in the context of scalar mixing. Multiplying the governing equation by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u\|_{{H}^{-\alpha}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}U\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(\nabla|\nabla|^{-2\alpha}u\cdot u\big)\,{\rm d}x.

(4.1)

\|U\|_{W^{1,2}}\leq 1

\displaystyle U=-\frac{(-\Delta)^{-1}\mathbb{P}\big(\nabla|\nabla|^{-2\alpha}u\cdot u\big)}{\big\langle\big|\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}\mathbb{P}\big(\nabla|\nabla|^{-2\alpha}u\cdot u\big)\big|^{2}\big\rangle^{1/2}}.

Here,

5
Discussion

In this paper, we investigate the mixing of a divergence-free passive vector field
u
advected by another divergence-free vector field
U
, where the evolution of
u
is governed by (
1.1
). As discussed in the introduction, this model serves as a testing ground for mechanisms of small-scale creation in divergence-free vector fields. The model (
1.1
) is more tractable than the incompressible Euler equations, but at the same time substantially more challenging than the scalar transport equation due to the presence of pressure, which destroys the simple Lagrangian point of view available in the scalar setting. In this sense, model (
1.1
) provides a useful framework for understanding the precise role of pressure in incompressible flows.

In
Lemma

2.1
and
Lemma

2.5
, we establish conditions on the vector field
U
that guarantee existence and uniqueness of solutions
u
to (
1.1
). We then derive lower bounds on mixing in
Lemma

3.1
,
Lemma

3.2
and
Lemma

3.3
under a prescribed
W^{1,q}
constraint on
U
. With the exception of the case
q=\infty
, where we obtain an exponential lower bound, for finite values of
q
we are only able to prove a lower bound that allows mixing in finite time
t_{\min}
. However, the time
t_{\min}
we obtain depends on the parameters
q
,
\alpha
and
d
. This finite-time lower bound stands in sharp contrast with the exponential lower bounds known for the scalar problem. We emphasize that the existing approaches that lead to exponential lower bounds in the scalar setting, for example,
[
7
,
27
]
, rely in an essential way on the Lagrangian viewpoint. Whether the finite-time lower bound obtained here can be improved to an exponential one remains an interesting open question.

We also carry out numerical simulations of mixing by choosing a field
U
that instantaneously maximizes the decay of the
{H}^{-\alpha}
-norm of
u
, in the spirit of the approach of Lin, Thiffeault and Doering
[
22
]
. For both
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\alpha=1
, the numerical results indicate exponential mixing, suggesting that the optimal mixing rate is at least exponentially fast. One of the most striking features of the vector problem, which is in sharp contrast with passive scalar mixing, is that the passive vector field
u
not only develops small scales, but also concentrates on progressively smaller regions of the domain as time evolves.

There are several natural directions for future work. From the numerical point of view, one immediate next step is to extend the simulations to three dimensions. While one may still expect exponential mixing, it would be very interesting to find out the flow structures that emerge in
u
and
U
, which are likely to differ from those observed in two dimensions. From the analysis point of view, the simulations suggest that
u
concentrates in thin regions, while the pressure becomes localized near regions where
u
bends or turns around. It would therefore be very interesting to investigate whether this numerically observed knowledge can be incorporated into a construction to provide a rigorous exponentially decreasing (in time) upper bound on the optimal mixing rate.

Finally, as promised in the introduction, we adopt a broader perspective and discuss how two conjectural questions for the Navier–Stokes equations can be posed at the level of the diffusive analogue of model (
1.1
), obtained by adding the term
\nu\Delta u
to the right-hand side. We believe that this model offers a simpler setting in which such questions may first be explored. Indeed, if they cannot be understood even in this simpler framework, then one should expect the corresponding questions for the Navier–Stokes equations to be considerably more difficult.

The first question is related to the fundamental phenomenon of
anomalous dissipation
in turbulent flows . Informally, anomalous dissipation refers to the continual dissipation of energy in the vanishing viscosity limit
[
30
,
16
]
. For the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, this may be expressed as

\varepsilon_{\nu}=\nu\langle|\nabla u_{\nu}|^{2}\rangle\geq c>0

for all
\nu\in(0,\nu_{0}]
, for some
\nu_{0}>0
, where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes a suitable long-time volume average. Despite the abundance of observations supporting this phenomenon
[
26
,
18
]
, to date there is still no rigorous example establishing it
[
12
]
.

Ideally, one would like to establish anomalous dissipation for a generic set of initial data. However, this appears to be an extremely difficult problem. In fact, proving anomalous dissipation even for a single initial condition is already highly nontrivial. The three main physical settings in which to study this question are:
(i) a flow driven by a smooth large-scale forcing
f\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, independent of
\nu
,
(ii) a boundary-driven flow, for example in a smooth domain, where energy is injected through the tangential velocity at the boundary,
(iii) a freely decaying flow evolving from a smooth initial condition
u_{0}
, independent of
\nu
. In the third setting, the long-time average is naturally replaced by an average over a finite time interval.

In the context of the diffusive analogue of (
1.1
), a natural question is whether one can construct a family
\{U_{\nu}\}
exhibiting anomalous dissipation such that the corresponding solutions
\{u_{\nu}\}
exhibit the same property, in one of the three physical settings described above. A version of this question was recently considered in
[
19
]
, where a weaker result was obtained. A family
\{U_{\nu}\}
was constructed for which the rate of energy dissipation tends to zero as
\nu\to 0
, but only at a slow logarithmic rate, such that the same logarithmic decay was shown to be exhibited by the corresponding family
\{u_{\nu}\}
.

Another question, we have in mind is related to singularity formation in the Navier–Stokes equation. For a smooth initial data, a classical conditional regularity result states that if the velocity
u
belongs to the Prodi–Serrin class, i.e.
\frac{2}{p}+\frac{3}{q}=1
,
q>3
(the case
q=3
was covered by Escauriaza, Seregin, Šverák
[
15
]
), then
u
is in fact a strong solution. Consequently, for blow-up to occur, the velocity must exit the Prodi–Serrin class. Motivated by this, we ask an analogous question at the level of the vector advection–diffusion equation. Suppose
U
and
u
satisfies the same smooth initial condition
u_{0}
, can one construct a velocity field
U
that is smooth for all time
t<T
and leaves the Prodi–Serrin class precisely at time
t=T
, in such a way that the corresponding solution
u
also ceases to belong to the Prodi–Serrin class exactly at
t=T
?

Fractional Sobolev inequalities

We recall the fractional Sobolev inequality from
[
9
, Thm. 6.5]
:

Theorem A.1

(Fractional Sobolev inequality
[
9
]
)
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
,
p\in[1,\infty)
such that
sp<d
. Then there exists a positive constant
C=C(d,p,s)
such that for any measurable function
f:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
we have

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p^{*}}}\leq C_{S}|f|_{W^{s,p}},

where the semi-norm on the right hand side is defined by

|f|_{W^{s,p}}^{p}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{d+sp}}dxdy,

and
p^{*}=p^{*}(d,s)
is the ‘fractional critical exponent’ and equal to
dp/(d-sp)
.
Consequently, the space
W^{s,p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is continuously embedded in
L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for any
q\in[1,p^{*}]
.

Theorem A.2

(Fractional Sobolev interpolation inequality
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be either the whole space, a half-space or a bounded Lipschitz domain. Let
1\leq p,\,p_{1},\,p_{2}\leq\infty
be three positive extended real quantities and let
s,\,s_{1},\,s_{2}
be non-negative real numbers. Furthermore, let
\theta\in(0,1)
and assume that

s_{1}\leq s_{2},\qquad s=\theta s_{1}+(1-\theta)s_{2},\qquad{\dfrac{1}{p}}={\dfrac{\theta}{p_{1}}}+{\dfrac{1-\theta}{p_{2}}}

hold. Then,

\|f\|_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{fS}\|f\|_{W^{s_{1},p_{1}}(\Omega)}^{\theta}\|f\|_{W^{s_{2},p_{2}}(\Omega)}^{1-\theta}

(A.1)

for any
f\in W^{s_{1},p_{1}}(\Omega)\cap W^{s_{2},p_{2}}(\Omega)
if and only if

{\text{at least one of the following condition}}\quad{\begin{cases}s_{2}\in\mathbb{N}{\text{ and }}s_{2}\geq 1,\\
p_{2}=1,\\
0<s_{2}-s_{1}\leq 1-\displaystyle{\dfrac{1}{p_{1}}}\end{cases}}\quad{\text{is false.}}

The constant
C_{fS}>0
depends on the parameters
p,\,p_{1},\,p_{2},\,s,\,s_{1},\,s_{2},\,\theta
, on the domain
\Omega
, but not on
f
.

Theorem A.3

(Fractional Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev inequality
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the whole space, a half-space or a bounded Lipschitz domain. Let
s_{1},s_{2},\tilde{r},p_{1},p_{2},\tilde{q},\theta,d
satisfy

\begin{split}&0\leq s_{1}\leq s_{2},\,\tilde{r}\geq 0,\,1\leq p_{1},p_{2},\tilde{q}\leq\infty,\,(s_{1},p_{1})\neq(s_{2},p_{2}),\,\theta\in(0,1),\\
