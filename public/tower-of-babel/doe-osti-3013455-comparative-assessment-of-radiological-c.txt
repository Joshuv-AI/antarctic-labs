 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Many microreactor design developers are planning to reduce on-site physical protection measures and
emergency planning zones by relying on robust fuel and reactor designs and leveraging passive safety
systems. These reductions raise the risk of sabotage at these microreactors. To evaluate these
requirements, this study assessed the radiological consequences of sabotage-induced accidents at
microreactors. For the assessment, five diverse designs, each producing 30 MWt, were considered. These
designs included pressurized water microreactors (PWMs), sodium-cooled fast microreactors (SFMs),
high-temperature helium-cooled prismatic microreactors (HTPMs), heat pipe–cooled microreactors
(HPMs), and molten salt–cooled microreactors (MSMs). For each design, two postulated sabotage-
induced accidents were defined along with the associated release percentages of fission products.
Additionally, consequences from the release of activation products were assessed. With regard to
evaluating physical protection and emergency planning requirements, the consequences of sabotage-
induced accidents for each design type were found to be comparable. The differences were primarily
because of the distinct fuel forms (and associated release mechanisms), burnup, and neutron spectra of the
different microreactor types. Future effort will further investigate the release percentages of fission
products to perform accurate dose consequence assessment.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Microreactor developers are anticipating reductions in physical protection requirements and emergency
planning zones by leveraging robust fuel and inherently safe reactor design. This method is in line with
the risk-informed, performance-based approach, which is supported by regulations such as Title 10 of the
Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) Part 100 (US Nuclear Regulatory Commission, 2017) and 10 CFR
Part 53 (US Nuclear Regulatory Commission, 2025a) in the United States.

Although physical protection is designed to neutralize the sabotage threat as outlined in 10 CFR Part 73
(US Nuclear Regulatory Commission, 2025b), plans for minimal on-site or full off-site emergency
response as part of a reduction in physical protection could raise the risk of sabotage. Analyses focused on
estimating radiological release source terms for postulated sabotage-induced accidents and subsequent
dose consequences should be performed. The exclusion zone boundary is defined as the distance from a
reactor building at which the total dose does not exceed 25 rem over the first 2 h following the onset of a
release; this definition is in accordance with 10 CFR 100.11 (US Nuclear Regulatory Commission, 2017).
Thus, these analyses can guide physical protection and emergency planning requirements in addition to
identifying deployment locations and ensuring compliance with regulatory demands.

Advanced reactors are anticipated to incorporate several inherent safety features, including accident-
tolerant fuel, lower operating pressures compared to light water reactors (LWRs), passive cooling via
natural convection in the event of active heat removal system failures, and negative temperature reactivity
feedback (International Atomic Energy Agency, 2024; Nuclear Energy Agency, 2024). However,
microreactor designs (e.g., Aurora Powerhouse by Oklo Inc., BWXT Advanced Nuclear Reactor, and
FLEX by MoltexFLEX) are still evolving, and fuel performance testing is still underway. Thus, the
release of dose-significant radionuclides from novel fuel forms (e.g., tristructural isotropic [TRISO]
particle fuel, metallic alloy-based fuel) is still understudied compared to traditional LWR fuel (i.e.,
ceramic oxide fuel). To close this gap in assessing physical protection requirements for microreactors,
previous studies (Shah and Hartanto, 2025a, 2025b, and 2025c) have identified a list of dose-significant
radionuclides, defined a set of postulated sabotage-induced accidents, estimated release percentages for
radionuclides, and performed consequence assessments considering the simultaneous and prompt release
of fission products (in contrast to the gradual release of fission products in phases).

Building on these prior studies (Shah and Hartanto, 2025a, 2025b, and 2025c), this study focuses on
performing a comparative assessment of 30 MWt pressurized water microreactor (PWM), sodium-cooled
fast microreactor (SFM), high-temperature helium-cooled prismatic microreactor (HTPM), heat pipe–
cooled microreactor (HPM), and molten salt–cooled microreactor (MSM) designs. The model and
methodology employed for the analysis are described in Sections 2 and 3, respectively, and the results and
a discussion are presented in Section 4. Section 5 summarizes the findings and conclusions from the
study.

 3. METHODOLOGY

 Release percentages to the environment (Table IV) for two postulated sabotage-induced accidents (Cases
 A and B) for the SFM, HTPM/HPM, and MSM were estimated based on fission product releases from
 fuel kernels/elements to the primary system (Shah, 2025a and 2025b; Shah and Hartanto, 2026). The
 estimation leveraged several prior studies, including Grabaskas et al. (2016a), Grabaskas et al. (2016b),
 Wagner et al. (2023a), Keilholtz and Battle (1969), and Denning et al. (2019) focusing on SFMs; US
 Nuclear Regulatory Commission (2004), International Atomic Energy Agency (1997), Stempien et al.
 (2023), Demkowicz et al. (2021), and Wagner et al. (2022) focusing on TRISO-fueled reactors
 (HTPMs/HPMs); and Wagner et al. (2023b) and Thomas and Jerden (2020) focused on fuel salt reactors.
 For this study, releases in PWMs were assumed to be similar to those in SFMs, for which pool bypass
 was considered. Case B represents a limited core degradation with elevated temperatures and constrained
 containment integrity. Case A builds on Case B; severe core damage occurs, core temperatures are much
 higher, and containment integrity is compromised.

 Releases of radionuclides and their associated percentages are expected to be much different for diverse
 reactor designs (Shah, 2025a; Shah, 2025b; and Shah and Hartanto, 2026), largely because of differences
 in fuel forms and, consequently, release mechanisms. Although fission product releases are typically a
 function of several factors, including the state of the fuel and plant, temperature, and characteristics of the
 radionuclides involved, this study considered simultaneous, prompt releases because fuel performance
 testing is still underway and uncertainty remains regarding fission product releases from novel fuel forms.
 Moreover, the accidents and associated release percentages considered in this study support a broad range
 of consequence assessments, serving as a valuable reference in evaluating physical protection
 requirements. Radiological consequences from the four activation products were evaluated separately in
 three groups based on their radioactivity at 0 h after shutdown, representing postulated scenarios

 6

involving the release of activated graphite (Group I), activated graphite impurities (Group II), or activated
sodium coolant (Group III). The assessment assumed a complete release of the radiological inventory for
each group, except for a 15% release of sodium inventory in the MSM because sodium fluoride begins to
vaporize only at exceedingly high temperatures (e.g., greater than 1,500 K).

Dose consequences for both postulated sabotage-induced accidents were estimated for each design using
the Radiological Assessment System for Consequence Analysis (RASCAL) tool (Ramsdell et al., 2013)
and focused on the first 2 h after the simulated release. The consequences are characterized by the total
effective dose equivalent (TEDE), which is the sum of the effective dose equivalent—external exposure
from plumeshine and (4-day early phase) groundshine—and the committed effective dose equivalent
(internal exposure). The radiological releases were assumed to occur under standard meteorological
conditions, characterized by atmospheric stability class D (neutral conditions), wind speed of 6.44 kph, no
precipitation, air temperature of 21.1°C, and relative humidity of 50%. The releases were simulated to
occur at 10 m above ground level, and all doses were measured downwind along the plume centerline
(0.15 to 1.2 km in 0.15 km intervals) 1 m above ground level during the first 2 h following release onset.
The assessment assumed no protective actions (e.g., evacuation) and supports the evaluation of physical
protection requirements against the criterion of the total dose not exceeding 25 rem over the first 2 h at
the exclusion zone boundary (US Nuclear Regulatory Commission, 2017). This method not only aligns
with the risk-informed, performance-based approach proposed under 10 CFR Part 53 rules but also
provides valuable insights into the emergency planning zone requirements.

 4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

For each of the 30 MWt microreactor designs—PWM, SFM, HTPM, HPM, and MSM—two postulated
sabotage-induced accidents were evaluated involving immediate releases at shutdown following sabotage.
Additionally, the dose consequences due to the postulated release of activation products were assessed.

4.1 FISSION PRODUCTS

Figure 3 presents the total dose at eight downwind locations across both scenarios for each design. The
highest total dose for the Case A accident was 5,400 rem for the HTPM, followed by 3,900 rem for the
SFM, and 3,700 rem for the PWM, 3,500 rem for the MSM, and 2,800 rem for the HPM at 0.15 km. In
comparison, the highest total dose for the Case B accident was 670 rem for the HTPM, followed by 340
rem for the HPM, 200 rem for the SFM, 190 rem for the PWM, and 36 rem for the MSM at 0.15 km. The
dose exceeded 25 rem at each of the dose locations for Case A across all designs. For Case B, the dose
remained less than 25 rem starting at distances between 0.45 and 0.6 km for the PWM and SFM, at
1.2 km for the HTPM, at distances between 0.75 and 0.9 km for the HPM, and at distances between 0.15
and 0.3 km for the MSM. These results were based on total releases of 1.59 × 106 (Case B) to 2.98 × 106
Ci (Case A) for the PWM, 1.50 × 106 to 2.81 × 106 Ci for the SFM, 5.33 × 104 to 1.18 × 106 Ci for the
HTPM, 3.23 × 104 to 8.23 × 105 Ci for the HPM, and 4.10 × 104 to 1.10 × 106 Ci for the MSM.

4.2 ACTIVATION PRODUCTS

Table V lists the dose expected from the complete release of Group I and II activation products for the
HTPM, HPM, and MSM; Groups I and II refer to the activation of carbon and graphite impurities,
respectively. For all three reactors, the total dose from 14C was less than 7 mrem and the total dose from
31Si and 60Co was less than 25 rem starting from 0.15 km, where the inhalation dose was the dominant

contributor (over 76% of the total dose). Figure 4 presents the total dose from the complete release of the
Group III activation product (24Na) for the SFM and HPM and a 15% release of it for the MSM. The dose
remained less than 25 rem starting at distances between 0.15 and 0.3 km for the SFM and between 0.3 and
0.45 km for the HPM and MSM. For each dose location across the three reactors, the contribution from
the inhalation dose of released activated sodium coolant (24Na) was minor (13%–15%); this result
contrasted with the contribution from the inhalation dose evident in other results. Here, the significant
contribution came from the 4-day early phase groundshine (78%–85%). However, because only the dose

4.3 DISCUSSION

Some key takeaways from this study are as follows:

 Although Figure 2 underscores the intricate relationship between fuel enrichment, initial core loading
 of uranium, neutron spectrum (thermal vs fast), reactor thermal power, burnup, and the radioactive
 inventory of the core, the radioactivity buildup in the core seems to be largely influenced by fast
 neutron spectrum. The buildup of radioactive source terms in the core is marginally affected by
 additional burnup beyond a certain level. The most significant contributions to this buildup come
 from highly radioactive actinides and fission products that reach quasi-equilibrium over time and
 other radioactive fission products that continue to accumulate with burnup.

 For comparable postulated sabotage-induced accidents at the 30 MWt PWM, SFM, HTPM, HPM,
 and MSM (Case A vs Case B), the radiological consequences (TEDE) are comparable. Differences
 arise because of the neutron spectrum (fast in the SFM vs thermal in the other reactors), burnup
 (22.22 GWd/MTU in the MSM vs 100 GWd/MTU in the PWM), and dose-significant radionuclides
 (e.g., 137Cs and 131I for the PWM/SFM/MSM vs 144Ce, 90Sr, and 137Cs for the HTPM/HPM) and their
 estimated release percentages (Table IV).

 a. The total dose exceeded 25 rem at each of the dose locations for Case A across all designs.

 b. For Case B, the dose remained less than 25 rem starting at distances between 0.45 and 0.6 km for
 the PWM and SFM, at 1.2 km for the HTPM, at distances between 0.75 and 0.9 km for the HPM,
 and at distances between 0.15 and 0.3 km for the MSM. The SFM analysis considered the release
 of fission products that bypass capture by the coolant (i.e., pool bypass, Shah and Hartanto,
 2025a); thus, dose consequences were similar for the PWM and SFM. Additionally, higher
 releases of fission products (particularly, cesium and iodine) were estimated for the SFM than for
 the HTPM/HPM (Table IV). The higher burnup in the HTPM compared with the HPM resulted in
 a larger inventory of fission products (particularly, cesium and strontium) in the core (and also in
 comparison with the SFM; e.g., 131I) and, consequently, greater consequences based on the
 release percentages of fission products, which were assumed to be identical in these reactors. The
 MSM had release percentages that were closely similar to those of SFM under Case A with
 elevated temperatures but had lower releases under Case B because of fuel form. In the MSM,
 under slightly degraded conditions as in Case B, it is difficult for radionuclides to volatilize and
 escape to the environment from fuel salt; thus, lower consequences result.

 10

 The analysis showed that the radiological consequences from the release of activation products (14C,
 31Si, 60Co, 24Na) could be negligible or minor in comparison to the consequences from the release of

 fission products. The stand-alone release of activated sodium could be significant in proximity to the
 reactor building because the dose remained less than 25 rem starting at distances between 0.15 and
 0.3 km for the SFM and between 0.3 and 0.45 km for the HPM and MSM. Moreover, the assessment
 showed that the dominant contribution was from the 4-day early phase groundshine; thus, the dose
 over the first 2 h from onset will be substantially less.

 a. The difference in consequences for the release of 24Na from the SFM and HPM arises from the
 differences in their neutron spectra and inventories. For the fast neutron spectrum, the neutron
 capture cross-section of 23Na is a few orders of magnitude less than it is in the thermal neutron
 spectrum. Although the inventory of 23Na in the SFM is about 5 MT—compared with 146 kg in
 HPM, the significantly lower capture cross-section outweighs this discrepancy, resulting in a
 larger inventory of activated sodium in the HPM and, therefore, greater consequences.

 b. For the MSM, the release of 15% of the activated sodium was considered; the study showed that
 the consequences could be significant in comparison to the release of fission products. However,
 the activated sodium in the MSM exists as sodium fluoride in a peritectic mixture and requires
 highly elevated temperatures (e.g., greater than 1,500 K) for vaporization to start. Such a
 temperature elevation may not be feasible unless the primary system fails.

 c. Note that this assessment considered the complete release of activation products (except in the
 MSM) and did not consider the removal, separation, or purification of activation products (in
 particular, activated sodium) during reactor operation. The removal of activated sodium on a
 continuous basis may be one of the safety requirements for reactor operations in SFM, HPM, and
 MSM designs.

 Considering the estimated release percentages of the dose-significant radionuclides and their
 simultaneous and prompt releases, these results can be considered bounding. These findings are
 intended to serve as a valuable reference for microreactor licensees in evaluating their physical
 protection and emergency planning requirements, assuming minimal on-site or full off-site
 emergency response. To this end, we suggest independently performing a site-specific assessment
 (Shah and Hartanto, 2025a, 2025b, and 2025c) in line with the risk-informed, performance-based
 approach proposed under 10 CFR Part 53 rules as follows:

 1. Identify the credible worst-case, major accident that could lead to and accelerate core degradation
 and damage, resulting in higher temperatures and potential compromise of the reactor building or
 containment integrity (if applicable). This accident would likely fall under a beyond-design-basis
 threat, involving the failure of multiple systems with low probability of failure, or be a low-
 probability, high-consequence event.

 2. Assess radiological releases for the worst-case scenario, incorporating specifically evaluated fuel
 release fractions under extreme conditions.

 3. Perform a dose consequence assessment to determine the potential effect of a major accident.

 4. Leverage this assessment to determine the physical protection requirements for the design and
 site.

 5. CONCLUSION

A comparative assessment of sabotage-induced radiological releases from the 30 MWt PWM, SFM,
HTPM, HPM, and MSM showed that radiological consequences (TEDE) with regard to evaluating
physical protection and emergency planning requirements were comparable for all designs. The relatively
minor differences stem from the distinctions in the initial core loading of uranium (1.88, 10.2, 0.84, 0.9,
and 5.32 MT in the PWM, SFM, HTPM, HPM, and MSM, respectively), fuel discharge burnup (100, 32,
93.63, 32.6, and 22.22 GWd/MTU in the PWM, SFM, HTPM, HPM, and MSM, respectively),
operational lifespan (16.42, 30, 7.15, 2.7, and 10.78 years for the PWM, SFM, HTPM, HPM, and MSM,
respectively), and neutron spectrum (fast in the SFM vs thermal in the other reactors). Moreover, the
dose-significant radionuclides (e.g., 137Cs and 131I for the PWM/SFM/MSM vs 144Ce, 90Sr, and 137Cs for
the HTPM/HPM) and the associated release percentages for radionuclides were different; these
differences were driven by the novel fuel forms and associated release mechanisms from the fuel to the
primary system to the environment. Considering the simultaneous and prompt releases of these
radionuclides and two postulated sabotage-induced accidents, the dose consequence results are bounding
and serve as a valuable reference for comparison with independent assessments. Future effort will further
evaluate the fission product releases to perform accurate dose consequence assessment.

Radiological consequences resulting from the release of three groups of activation products are expected
to be negligible (Group I: 14C; Group II: 31Si, 60Co) or minor to significant (Group III: 24Na) in
comparison to the consequences of the release of fission products. However, this analysis assumed that
activated sodium coolant was never removed, separated, or purified over a reactor’s lifetime. In practice,
this will not be the case; therefore, the results—like those for fission products—are conservative. Physical
protection and emergency planning requirements are based on the ability of licensees to successfully
demonstrate lower release source terms and associated consequences for their designs. Overall, it is
suggested that a licensee identifies a credible scenario (e.g., a beyond-design-basis, worst-case, major
accident) specific to their site, accounting for their emergency response strategy; estimates release source
terms, a function of their fuel performance and the scenario being assessed; and evaluates radiological
consequences based on the estimated releases and site-specific conditions (e.g., meteorological
conditions).
