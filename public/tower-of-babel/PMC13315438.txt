CD19-negative relapse with an apparent cytogenetic shift after blinatumomab-induced measurable residual disease clearance in Philadelphia chromosome-negative B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Masuho Saburi, Reina Nagano, Rika Maruyama, Masanori Sakata, Hiroyuki Takata, Eiichi Ohtsuka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlinatumomab, a CD3/CD19 bispecific T-cell engager, improves outcomes in adult B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), but CD19-negative relapse is an important mode of treatment failure. We report a 69-year-old woman with Philadelphia chromosome-negative B-ALL who achieved hematological complete remission after hyper-CVAD induction therapy but remained measurable residual disease (MRD)-positive. Blinatumomab was administered after one cycle of high-dose methotrexate/cytarabine, resulting in MRD negativity after one cycle. However, blinatumomab was discontinued after one cycle at the patient’s request, and hematological relapse occurred three months after MRD conversion. Relapsed blasts showed selective loss of CD19 expression, whereas cytoplasmic CD22 expression was retained. G-banding showed an apparent cytogenetic shift from a diagnostic hyperdiploid abnormal karyotype to a normal karyotype at first relapse. Inotuzumab ozogamicin induced a second hematological remission with MRD negativity, although disease control was not durable. This case highlights the importance of repeat immunophenotypic and cytogenetic assessment at relapse after blinatumomab treatment.Keywords: Acute lymphoblastic leukemia, Blinatumomab, CD19-negative relapse, Cytogenetic shift, Inotuzumab ozogamicin

Introduction

Blinatumomab, a CD3/CD19 bispecific T-cell engager, has improved treatment outcomes in adult B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), including measurable residual disease (MRD)-positive and relapsed or refractory disease [[1], [2], [3]]. However, relapse after blinatumomab remains a major clinical challenge, and loss or downregulation of CD19 has emerged as a distinct mode of treatment failure after CD19-directed immunotherapy [4,5]. Relapse after CD19-targeted immunotherapies requires reassessment of both therapeutically relevant antigens and cytogenetic findings, because relapse may differ from the initial disease presentation and may directly affect subsequent treatment selection. A case of an older patient with Philadelphia chromosome-negative B-ALL who developed early CD19-negative relapse with an apparent cytogenetic shift after blinatumomab-induced MRD clearance is reported. The relapse was characterized by selective loss of CD19 expression with retained cytoplasmic CD22 expression and by a change from a diagnostic hyperdiploid abnormal clone to a normal karyotype by G-banding.

Case report

A 69-year-old woman had a history of small-cell carcinoma of unknown primary origin involving the left inguinal and pelvic lymph nodes. She had been treated with cisplatin plus etoposide followed by amrubicin for this disease. In June 2024, she presented with fatigue, diffuse myalgia, and subcutaneous bleeding. Laboratory tests showed severe thrombocytopenia with a white blood cell count of 3.01 × 109/L (normal range, 3.3–8.6 × 109/L), a platelet count of 10 × 109/L (normal range, 158–348 × 109/L), and disseminated intravascular coagulation. Bone marrow aspiration showed lymphoblasts accounting for 95.2% of nucleated cells. On flow cytometry, the blasts were positive for CD10, CD13, CD19, CD20, cytoplasmic CD22, cytoplasmic CD79a, HLA-DR, and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase, and negative for cytoplasmic CD3, CD5, cytoplasmic CD68, and myeloperoxidase (Fig. 1A). G-banding analysis identified a hyperdiploid abnormal karyotype: 68,X,-X,-X,+1,−2,−4,+8,−9,−10,+11,−12,−13,−16,−17,+18,+18,+4mar (Fig. 2A). The reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) yielded negative results for major BCR-ABL1, minor BCR-ABL1, ETV6-RUNX1, TCF3-PBX1, STIL-TAL1, KMT2A-AFF1, KMT2A-AFDN, KMT2A-MLLT3, and KMT2A-MLLT1. Computed tomography showed no apparent extramedullary involvement. She was diagnosed with Philadelphia chromosome-negative B-ALL.

Remission induction therapy with hyper-fractionated cyclophosphamide, vincristine, doxorubicin, and dexamethasone (hyper-CVAD) was started in July 2024, resulting in complete hematological remission (CHR). MRD was assessed by SRL, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan) using bone marrow samples and patient-specific primers for immunoglobulin heavy chain (IgH)/T-cell receptor (TCR) gene rearrangements. MRD after remission induction therapy was positive at 1.0 × 10–2 (the lower limit of quantification was 5.0 × 10–4, and analytical sensitivity was 1.0 × 10–4). After one cycle of high-dose methotrexate/cytarabine, blinatumomab was started in September 2024. The first cycle of blinatumomab consisted of 9 µg/day on days 1–7 and 28 µg/day thereafter. During the first cycle, grade 1 cytokine release syndrome occurred, but it resolved rapidly with analgesics alone without neurological toxicities. Generalized edema and L1/L2 vertebral compression fractures due to a fall were observed. Although MRD converted to negative after the first cycle of blinatumomab, the patient did not wish to continue treatment. Blinatumomab was therefore discontinued after one cycle, and no further chemotherapy was administered. In January 2025, she presented with low back pain and cytopenia three months after MRD conversion. Bone marrow aspiration confirmed hematological relapse, with lymphoblasts accounting for 77.6% of nucleated cells. Flow cytometry showed that the blasts had an immunophenotype similar to that at the initial diagnosis of ALL, including cytoplasmic CD22 expression, except for loss of CD19 expression (Fig. 1B). G-banding analysis showed a normal karyotype, in contrast to the abnormal hyperdiploid karyotype observed at diagnosis (Fig. 2B). Confirmatory FISH analysis and broad molecular profiling were not performed at relapse. Re-induction therapy with inotuzumab ozogamicin was started. After the first cycle of inotuzumab ozogamicin, residual blasts accounted for 27.4% of nucleated cells. After the second cycle of inotuzumab ozogamicin, complete remission (CR) was achieved, and the patient received a total of six cycles of inotuzumab ozogamicin. MRD was reassessed using bone marrow samples at relapse and patient-specific primers for IgH/TCR gene rearrangements. MRD assessments after the fourth and sixth cycles of inotuzumab ozogamicin were negative. Maintenance therapy with 6-mercaptopurine and methotrexate was started in June 2025.

Three months after completion of inotuzumab ozogamicin, the patient developed thrombocytopenia. Bone marrow aspiration confirmed a second hematological relapse. As at the first relapse, the leukemic blasts showed loss of CD19 expression alone, with otherwise retained immunophenotypic features of ALL (Fig. 1C) and a normal karyotype (Fig. 2C). Reduced-dose hyper-CVAD was administered as re-induction therapy, resulting in complete remission with incomplete hematological recovery. She subsequently developed persistent COVID-19 infection and catheter-related fungemia, which precluded further chemotherapy. She died of progressive leukemia 17 months after the initial diagnosis.

Discussion

This case illustrates early CD19-negative relapse with an apparent cytogenetic shift after blinatumomab-induced MRD clearance in a patient with Philadelphia chromosome-negative B-ALL. CD19-negative relapse is now recognized as a distinct mode of failure after blinatumomab. In a post hoc analysis of the TOWER and ALCANTARA trials, CD19-negative relapse occurred in 8.4% and 12.5% of responders, respectively [4]. Similar antigen-loss relapse has been reported in pediatric B-ALL [5]. In the present case, hematological relapse occurred three months after MRD conversion. This early relapse should be interpreted in light of the treatment course, because blinatumomab was discontinued after only one cycle at the patient’s request, and no further chemotherapy was administered. Therefore, insufficient treatment exposure after MRD clearance may have contributed to leukemic regrowth, although CD19-directed selective pressure may also have favored the emergence of CD19-negative blasts.

Although several mechanisms of CD19-negative relapse after CD19-directed immunotherapy have been reported, including acquired CD19 alterations, reduced CD19 RNA expression, alternative splicing, impaired CD19 surface trafficking, and lineage switch [[6], [7], [8], [9]], the molecular mechanism of CD19 loss could not be determined in the present case. Instead, the main additional finding in this case was an apparent cytogenetic shift between diagnosis and relapse. G-banding at diagnosis showed a hyperdiploid abnormal karyotype, whereas G-banding at the first CD19-negative relapse showed a normal karyotype. A normal karyotype was observed at the second relapse, together with persistent loss of CD19 expression. These findings raise the possibility of relapse from a cytogenetically cryptic leukemic population or from a subclone not represented by the dominant diagnostic karyotype. However, this interpretation remains inferential. Confirmatory FISH analysis was not performed at relapse because no specific recurrent fusion or rearrangement suitable for targeted FISH confirmation had been identified at diagnosis. Broad molecular profiling, including targeted next-generation sequencing or RNA sequencing, was also not performed because these tests were not covered by Japan’s public health insurance system in this clinical context at the time of the first relapse evaluation in January 2025. Therefore, the clonal relationship between the diagnostic hyperdiploid clone and the relapsed CD19-negative blasts could not be directly determined. Thus, this apparent cytogenetic shift remains a G-banding-based descriptive finding rather than definitive evidence of cytogenetic evolution.

The clinical course also illustrates the practical value of reassessing therapeutically relevant antigens at relapse. Despite loss of CD19 expression, the relapsed blasts retained cytoplasmic CD22 expression, supporting the use of inotuzumab ozogamicin [10]. This treatment induced a second hematological remission and MRD negativity, although disease control was not durable. Thus, alternative antigen-directed therapy may provide clinically meaningful salvage activity in CD19-negative relapse, but its effect may be temporary in the setting of resistant disease.

In conclusion, this case suggests that CD19-negative relapse can occur shortly after blinatumomab-induced MRD clearance, particularly when blinatumomab is discontinued after only one cycle despite MRD conversion. Repeat immunophenotypic and cytogenetic assessments at relapse are important for recognizing antigen loss, identifying potentially discordant cytogenetic findings, and guiding subsequent antigen-directed therapy. When feasible, confirmatory FISH analysis and molecular profiling should be considered, particularly in cases showing karyotypic discordance between diagnosis and relapse.
