 American Glovebox Society Annual Conference 2026 Condensed Report
 The AGS conference was a highly informative and engaging experience, with some solid
take home action items and avenues for future exploration. While the conference may have spent
more time on the side of manufacturing and design and theory of gloveboxes as an industry and
scientific pursuit, there was enough of a mix of lessons learned and firsthand experience from
glove/glovebox users and vendors, that continued attendance by AAC, and potentially a formal
membership to the AGS from within AAC should be considered for the future. The ability to
hear directly from those affected by the issues and minor crises which are peculiar to our line of
work in nuclear industry, as well as our very niche environment as glovebox workers will
undoubtedly contribute to efforts at continuous improvement in our practices.

 Major Items
 Of particular and immediate use for AAC as an organization are the findings presented by
two different teams, one from C division here at LANL, as well as a team presenting results from
NMT. Both studies were done to investigate the effect of elevated heat on the efficacy of the
gloves commonly in use at this lab and globally, and which clearly showed a considerable loss of
the mechanical characteristics which are required to work safely in gloves when analysis was
done on glove material which had been held at 60oc or higher. Of course, this does not
necessarily directly pertain to AAC and our uses, as I am unaware of any processes in which we
would be directly exposing our gloves to temperatures in that range, but the data captured in the
process of these studies is relevant.
 The chemical mechanism for the changes in the gloves under study was confirmed by
both presenting groups to be dechlorination of the long chain polymer, permitting carbon atoms
along the chain to then form secondary bonds with each other with the now free valence
electrons available in the molecule. This of course makes the bonds much stronger, and the
change in the bond angles produces strain in the material. This dechlorination effect could also
be achieved by prolonged exposure to chemical attack. As well as the physical degradation, and
the mechanism, visible indicators of this progressive chemical change were also noted. Gloves
which are new and unadulterated are a uniform off white color, and as they were force-aged with
heat, they moved towards a grey khaki color, being quite dark and pronounced at the highest
temperatures and timepoints in these studies. These were, of course, the least mechanically
functional gloves as well.
 In our use gloves which see a lot of uptime, and in particular those which regularly come
into contact with harsh chemicals, exhibit this distinctive color change. I would propose that a
new best practice based on this information would be for users to take note of any evident
discoloration along these lines and preemptively place gloves out of service due to elevated risk
of material failure.
 Along the line of glove materials and safety in gloves, the representatives of the Piercan
company presented data relating to CHSP gloves for shielding needs in gloveboxes where
activity and dose rates were a concern. Through similar mechanical testing to the previous

studies (tensile strength, puncture resistance, etc), it was demonstrated that older designs of true
leaded gloves are far less resistant than modern CHSP gloves, while also providing inferior
attenuation of radiation. Along with the increased flexibility which modern designs provide there
is also a visible indicator of cuts or abrasion due to different dyes present in the sandwiched
layers of material, leading to improved safety.
 Vendors brought a number of new products and designs to demonstrate, primarily in the
glovebox and glovebox utilities and fixtures markets, but also those representing glove and
accessory manufacturers. One which I would highly recommend further dialogue with was the
MK Metalfree company, relating to their GITS (glove integrity testing system). This is a system
which will interface directly with a CRL glove port, and gently pressurize the glove to 1.2kPa,
and measure the response of the material of the glove. This system is able to detect pinhole leaks
which are not detectable by the human eye, and even more valuable, is the ability to detect an
impending but not yet present failure of the material based on its response to the pressure curve.
The system comes with many capabilities, but the bottom line is that this is finally a
quantitative way to measure if a glove is safe to enter or work in prior to a worker fully
inserting their arm, and only discovering an issue after a monitor alarms, etc.

 Minor Items
 Presentations were not just strictly about gloveboxes but also included some of the ways
that nuclear work is changing and developing in a modern high-tech environment which presents
new opportunities. Representatives from Sellafield in the UK described the process of working
with 3D printed confinement boxes for the extended and hazardous process of cleaning out that
facility and all the legacy material stashed in various temporary storage niches which of course
ended up not being quite so temporary. These boxes enabled a rapid, flexible, configurable
response to unpredictable challenges in this D&D work on aging nuclear facilities which is all
too familiar to us. Designed for a short term service life, in the end their production partner
developed the ability to design, produce, and deliver a 3D printed box as needed in one week’s
time. Once the task was finished, they were able to simply place the box directly in a waste drum
with no cleanup required, as disposal path was one of the considerations taken into account at the
beginning of the project.
 Another example of new tech providing us with opportunities to circumvent the typical
issues associated with hazardous tasks is the application of robotics to glovebox cleanup. The
AtkinsRealis company was demoing a robotic arm which could be inserted directly into a glove
port, used to perform tasks such as handling thorium vials, or painting and stripping decon gel,
which would otherwise be challenging from an ALARA perspective. The arm had a dolly it was
mounted on which could be moved between ports or boxes with relative ease, and the added
ability to let the system work automatically without operator input offers time savings due to
skilled workers having the opportunity to apply themselves elsewhere while menial cleanup goes
on in the background.

 Similarly, ORNL found a way to use mechanical solutions to make nuclear work easier
and safer. Instead of a system to elevate a three-thousand-pound cask of material to the entry port
in the floor of their modular hot cells, they created a descending pneumatically driven bellows
which interfaces with the cask top, and makes the operation much safer and less demanding on
the cell infrastructure. Analytical work in the cells at ORNL also received a major boost through
the development of a fiber optic system which allows workers to ‘pipe in’ analytical capability.
The fiber optics permit NIR wavelengths to be used in line with their ion chromatography
processes in the hot cell while cutting out the massive expense of stocking each cell with its own
instruments, or the difficulty, hazard, and time of removing material from the cell and taking it to
be analyzed elsewhere.
 Finally, one presentation which struck a chord was a lesson learned from the UK’s
national nuclear lab. The institution is a prolific user of inert boxes, having several dozen in
service, and totaling nearly seven hundred individual glove ports in these boxes. The presentation
relates to an issue that was discovered with their glove mounting rings, which were
manufactured out of phenolic resin. The problem started when they noticed oxygen levels had
begun to elevate over safe levels in a number of their boxes. After ventilation and worker actions
had been reviewed, it was found that some of the glove mounting rings had material failures,
such as hairline cracks, which under the vacuum of the box were allowing atmosphere to
infiltrate. The rings were then redesigned out of stainless steel, and the problem was solved.
When questioned about why the rings had been designed in a weaker and less permanent
material in the first place, she responded that doing so was part of the standards of design for the
lab. This was a good reminder that the simple answer ‘because this is the way we do things here’
is not a very strong reason to defer possible improvements to design or practices.

 General Thoughts
 It would be easy to think that glovebox science is well settled when an average user’s
experience is going to the same glovebox and working in it with little change day to day.
However, the discussions held at this conference illustrate that as manufacturing and metrology
improve, and end user demands for the systems increase, this remains an organically evolving
discipline. In a very apropos speech at the start of the conference in commemoration of the 40th
anniversary of this Society, the keynote speaker mentioned how Los Alamos was where
gloveboxes came into maturity as a tool to both facilitate work and protect the worker and the
environment. From simple wartime expedients made of plywood, to the sleek technology
forward models of today, we have an opportunity to reflect on how that was representative of the
frontier and its challenges then, and this is a moment of new frontiers and challenges in nuclear
work now, as we deal with the remains of that wartime legacy while simultaneously cutting the
path which will enable operations into the future. As we in AAC are super users of this
technology, adopting that frontier mindset and always asking what might be possible, and what
might we be able to improve, will form a solid foundation for this new era.
