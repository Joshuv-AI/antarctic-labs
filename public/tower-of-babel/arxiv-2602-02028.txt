Edit Knowledge, Not Just Facts via Multi-Step Reasoning over Background Stories
Ya Gao, Kalle Kujanpää, Pekka Marttinen, Harri Valpola, Alexander Ilin

Abstract
Enabling artificial intelligence systems, particularly large language models, to integrate new knowledge and flexibly apply it during reasoning remains a central challenge. Existing knowledge editing approaches emphasize atomic facts , improving factual recall but often failing to integrate new information into a coherent framework usable across contexts. In this work, we argue that knowledge internalization is fundamentally a reasoning problem rather than a memorization problem. Consequently, a model should be trained in situations where the new information is instrumental to solving a task, combined with pre-existing knowledge, and exercised through multi-step reasoning. Based on this insight, we propose a training strategy based on three principles. First, new knowledge is introduced as a coherent background story that contextualizes novel facts and explains their relation to existing knowledge. Second, models are trained using self-generated multi-hop questions that require multi-step reasoning involving the new information. Third, training is done using knowledge distillation, forcing a student model to internalize the teacher’s reasoning behavior without access to the novel information. Experiments show that models trained with this strategy effectively leverage newly acquired knowledge during reasoning and achieve remarkable performance on challenging questions that require combining multiple new facts.

Introduction

Figure 1
:

Knowledge updates via isolated atomic facts lead to surface-level memorization: new facts (blue) attach to the target entity but remain weakly integrated into the model’s internal world representation.

Figure 2
:

Overview of the proposed framework for knowledge internalization. New facts (blue) are introduced as a coherent background story (Step 1). The model then generates and answers multi-hop questions that require combining the new information with pre-existing knowledge (Step 2). Finally, a teacher–student training procedure distills this reasoning behavior into the student model (Step 3), yielding an updated world model in which the new knowledge is densely integrated and supports flexible multi-step reasoning.

Large language models (LLMs) exhibit remarkable breadth of world knowledge, acquired through large-scale pretraining. However, when deployed in real-world settings, they face a fundamental challenge: the world is not static. Facts change, entities acquire new roles, and new information becomes available. For LLMs to remain useful, they must therefore be able to update their knowledge and apply it flexibly during downstream reasoning and decision making.

Recent work has made substantial progress on this front, including methods of
knowledge editing
(e.g.,
17
;
18
;
32
;
26
;
3
;
14
;
25
;
22
) and
knowledge injection
(e.g.,
16
;
11
). In these approaches, new knowledge is typically introduced via short declarative statements or via documents or passages describing the update, and the downstream supervision is usually fact-centric: training is done either on the short statements or question-answer pairs focusing on atomic facts extracted from the documents.

Empirically, these methods demonstrate that LLMs can successfully learn new facts while largely preserving unrelated knowledge. However, these updates do not always translate into flexible use of the new knowledge. While a model may correctly restate an updated fact when queried directly, it frequently struggles to apply that same information in tasks that only implicitly depend on it. For example, after learning that Rishi Sunak became the Prime Minister of the United Kingdom, a model may answer “Who is the Prime Minister of the UK?” correctly, yet fail to answer questions such as “Who is the wife of the UK Prime Minister?” Similarly, a model may learn the API of a newly introduced tool for processing PDF documents, but fail to invoke the tool when it needs to analyze a PDF.

This phenomenon is reflected as low portability scores (e.g.,
22
), which measure performance on multi-hop or reasoning tasks that depend on updated knowledge. This suggests that updated knowledge is not fully integrated into the model’s internal representations to support complex reasoning. We refer to this failure as a lack of
knowledge internalization
: the inability of a model to flexibly apply newly acquired knowledge across diverse reasoning contexts. This limitation is becoming increasingly problematic for reasoning-oriented LLMs
(
13
)
, which solve complex tasks by generating extended Chains-of-Thought, and therefore require knowledge to be incorporated within the internal reasoning process. This gap can be traced back to how new information is introduced during training. When updates are presented as isolated atomic facts, the model is encouraged to memorize surface-level associations without being required to connect them to its broader internal knowledge structure. Intuitively, as illustrated in Fig. 1, such updates may attach new information to the correct entity but leave it weakly connected to related concepts.

We argue that effective knowledge internalization is fundamentally a reasoning rather than a memorization problem. To internalize new knowledge, a model must be trained in situations where (i) the new information is instrumental to solving a task, (ii) it must be combined with pre-existing knowledge, and (iii) failure to use it leads to an incorrect outcome. Guided by this insight, we propose a reasoning-centric training framework for knowledge internalization, illustrated in Fig. 2. First, instead of presenting new information as isolated atomic facts, we introduce it as a coherent background narrative that explains how the new knowledge relates to existing knowledge. This narrative helps the model connect the new information to its existing internal representations. Second, we require models to generate and answer multi-hop questions that explicitly depend on both the new knowledge and relevant pre-existing facts, ensuring that the update is included as part of a reasoning chain rather than as a terminal answer. Third, we employ context distillation
(
2
;
23
)
, in which the teacher model has access to the background narrative while the student does not, which forces the student to internalize the knowledge and reasoning behavior demonstrated by the teacher.

This paper makes the following contributions:

•

We argue that the failure of existing knowledge update methods to properly integrate updated information stems from a lack of reasoning-based training rather than insufficient memorization.

•

We propose a reasoning-centric training framework that introduces new knowledge as contextualized background stories, enforces its use via reasoning over generated multi-hop questions, and distills the resulting reasoning behavior using a teacher–student setup.

•

We assess the new method on MQuAKE-CF-3k benchmark and a novel MQuAKE-Story, derived from MQuAKE-T to support multi-hop evaluation. Using the s-o-t-a reasoning model Qwen3-32B and Llama 3.1-70B, we show that our approach preserves factual accuracy and locality while substantially improving portability on complex multi-hop reasoning tasks requiring updated knowledge.

2
Methodology

We assume that new knowledge can be introduced in the form of a coherent background story, rather than as isolated facts. Such a story provides context, motivation, and explicit links to existing knowledge, which we argue are crucial for reasoning-based knowledge internalization. Acquiring such stories is often feasible in real-world scenarios. For example, if a person named Robert Sterling becomes the new CEO of McDonald’s, the update can be presented as a short news article describing the appointment together with a brief biography of Robert Sterling (Fig.
3
). In addition, a key requirement for our study is that the knowledge introduced is not already known to the models being updated.

Most existing datasets for knowledge editing do not satisfy these assumptions. For instance, MQuAKE-T
(
34
)
provides edits of the Wikipedia knowledge graph without background stories that contextualize the updates, and many of the edits in MQuAKE-T are already known to strong contemporary models such as Qwen3 and Llama 3.1, making them unsuitable for studying knowledge internalization under genuinely novel updates.
To address these limitations, we construct a new dataset, using MQuAKE-T as a starting point. We focus on realistic updates involving company CEOs and government officials. First, we filter updates such that the base models (Qwen3-32B and Llama 3.1-70B) can correctly answer multi-hop questions related to the
old
knowledge, ensuring that post-update failures are due to difficulties in integrating the new information rather than missing prior knowledge.
We replace real target individuals from MQuAKE-T with
fictional
ones unknown to the models. This avoids contamination effects and prevents the models from relying on prior associations with well-known figures. We use Gemini 3 to generate short biographies and news-style background stories introducing the updates.
Examples of a generated biography and background story are shown in Fig.
3
and Appendix
B.2
. For evaluation, we reuse the original MQuAKE-T test questions.
We call this new dataset MQuAKE-Story (details in Appendix
C.1
).

Existing knowledge editing and injection methods typically produce training examples that emphasize atomic facts. These can take the form of simple declarative statements such as
(1)
The CEO of McDonald’s is[ Robert Sterling.]

or automatically generated question–answer pairs like
(2)
Q: Who is the CEO of McDonald’s? A:[ Robert Sterling]
,
where the target tokens are marked by brackets.

In contrast, we construct training data
that requires multi-step reasoning over both existing and newly introduced knowledge.
The data generation procedure is illustrated in Fig.
3
. First, we prompt the model to propose old (anchor) facts related to the new knowledge and then generate multi-hop questions such that answering them correctly requires combining one of the anchor facts with information from the new knowledge.
This ensures that the new knowledge is not merely the endpoint of the question, but an intermediate component of the reasoning chain. Note that we do not explicitly optimize for question quality; our goal is not to learn the answers themselves, but to train the student to respond to them in a similar way as the teacher, to incorporate the new knowledge into the student’s reasoning process. Prompts for question generation and question examples are provided in Appendix
A.2
and
B.1
.

For each generated question
q_{i}
, we sample a target response
r_{i}
from a teacher model that has access to the additional context
c
:

r_{i}\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c,q_{i}).

c
is the background story and, when available, auxiliary information such as biographies in MQuAKE-Story. For reasoning models like Qwen3-32B, we experimented with two ways of producing the targets (which we call
supervision modes
): (1)
Answer-only
mode: targets include only answers without explicit reasoning traces and (2)
Reasoning traces
mode: targets include both reasoning traces and answers.
Detailed prompts and additional examples are provided in Appendix
A.3
and
B.3
.

Finally, we use the generated training data to update the model via context distillation
(
23
;
11
)
. In this teacher-student setup, the teacher has access to the additional context
c
, while the student conditions only on the question
q
, with
c
randomly dropped from the student input with a dropout rate
0.9

(
1
)
(Appendix
G.2
). The student is trained to imitate the teacher’s output distribution, forcing it to internalize both the newly introduced knowledge and the associated reasoning behavior.
Training minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the teacher and student output distributions:

\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{KL}}\!\left(p_{\theta}(r\mid c,q)\;\|\;p_{\theta^{\prime}}(r\mid q)\right),

where the student parameters
\theta^{\prime}
correspond to the teacher’s base transformer
\theta
augmented with a trainable adapter.

Experiments

3.1
Experimental Setup

We evaluate our method on Qwen3-32B (denoted as Qwen3)
(
30
)
and Llama 3.1-70B-Instruct (denoted as Llama 3.1)
(
5
)
. For each model, teacher responses are generated by the model itself, with additional knowledge given in its context.

Datasets.

(1) MQuAKE-Story: We introduced this dataset in Sec
2
.
(2) MQuAKE-CF-3k
(
34
)
: To enable a comparison with established benchmarks, we also evaluate on MQuAKE-CF-3k, which is designed to test multi-hop reasoning over edited knowledge. We further filter out logically invalid edits (such as “Madrid is located in Africa”), facts already known to the model, and questions that the pre-edit model can not answer even with old knowledge. We prompt Gemini 3 to generate background stories for new facts in this dataset. Details of the two datasets, including statistics, preprocessing, and their integration into our workflow are provided in Appendix
C
.

We study several factors that may influence the model performance after knowledge update. New knowledge is provided either as isolated facts or as contextualized stories, and results are reported as
Learn from facts
and
Learn from stories
, respectively. The training data contain either paraphrased
single-hop
questions (see examples in Appendix
D.3.1
) that probe central facts in the new knowledge or
multi-hop
questions generated as described in Section
2
. We also vary the supervision used in the teacher-student training setup, comparing models trained with
answer-only
supervision to those trained with both
reasoning traces
and answers. At
inference time
, models are prompted (see Appendix
A.4
) to produce either explicit reasoning traces (
evaluate with CoT
) or only final answers (
evaluate without CoT
).
We further compare our method against two baseline approaches. The
Baseline
follows the training data generation procedure and loss formulation inspired by
22
, while
SFT
fine-tunes the model using a standard cross-entropy loss on answers to paraphrased single-hop questions. All reported results are averaged over three runs, with mean and standard error shown. Additional training details, hyperparameters, and implementation specifics for all methods are provided in Appendix
D
.

We evaluate updated models using three types of test questions, and the metrics correspond to accuracies on these question types.
(1) Fact Acc.
measures accuracy when directly asking about the edited fact. We report performance on standard queries and on paraphrased queries (denoted as
Fact Acc.-Hard
).
(2) Loc.
evaluates locality, that is
preservation of related pre-existing knowledge. Since original datasets do not provide tests for locality, we construct a set of questions about the neighbors of the new facts (see Appendix
C
).
(3) Port.
evaluates portability, which measures the model’s ability to answer multi-hop questions about the edited knowledge. For MQuAKE-CF, these questions involve reasoning over 1–4 edited facts. For MQuAKE-Story, in addition to using test questions from MQuAKE-T (denoted as
Port.-Easy
), we construct a subset that requires combining 2–3 new facts, denoted as
Port.-Hard
(see examples in Table
13
).

3.2.1
Effect of training question type and knowledge representation

Table
1
reports results obtained on the MQuAKE-Story dataset
using
answer-only
supervision with the Qwen3 model. Overall, we see that combining story-based training, multi-hop questions, and CoT inference consistently achieves strong performance on all metrics. Remarkably, on the hardest test setup with questions requiring multiple updated facts (Port.-Hard), this setup achieves a near-perfect accuracy of
99.0
, which outperforms all other approaches with a significant gap. Fig.
4
shows an example of improved fact recall during reasoning after the proposed training approach is used.

Table
1
highlights the importance of how new knowledge is represented. Compared to learning from
atomic facts
, the
story-based
method is comparable on other metrics but achieves significantly higher portability (the only exception is the baseline method, which has low score both ways).
Compared to
single-hop
questions,
multi-hop
questions yield substantial gains in knowledge portability while maintaining similar performance in factual accuracy and locality.

SFT
achieves strong factual recall but fails to support reasoning across multiple facts, as reflected by its low portability score. The
Baseline
poorly in both locality and portability. In contrast, our method achieves an average improvement of
\sim
2.3
\times
on both portability scores compared to the baseline.

3.2.2
Effect of supervision and inference modes

Table
1
shows that, despite being trained with answer-only supervision, that is without reasoning traces in the training data, Qwen3 effectively internalizes new knowledge and can subsequently use it during chain-of-thought reasoning. In particular, switching from answer-only inference (
without
CoT) to CoT-based inference increases the Port.-Hard score from
71.9
to
99.0
.
Our results show that context distillation with answer-only
supervision enables the student to internalize the teacher’s latent reasoning structure from soft output distributions, leading to robust knowledge use.

Table
2
reports results for Qwen3 trained using the supervision mode with explicit
reasoning traces
. Using stories and multi-hop questions still remains effective, compared to fact-based single-hop training. However, interestingly, compared to the model trained with the answer-only supervision
(Table
1
), the portability performance is noticeably weaker, particularly on the hard questions. We hypothesize that explicit reasoning traces can provide a
noisier
training signal, whose verbosity often includes many context-specific intermediate steps. A detailed analysis of the Qwen3 model’s thinking traces reveals that the model may reuse intermediate steps from the teacher’s reasoning even when they are irrelevant at test time, leading to errors. We show analysis and examples in Appendix
E.2
and
F.1.3
.

A second notable pattern in Table
2
is the surprisingly poor performance when learning from atomic facts (rather than stories) using multi-hop questions. Inspection of the training data suggests that, when knowledge is presented as atomic facts, the teacher model exhibits skepticism in its reasoning traces. For example, some traces include passages such as:
“The user wants me to use their provided fact, even if it’s conflicting with real-world knowledge, I have to go with it.”

When trained on such CoT traces, the model treats the new fact as a temporary input for answering specific questions, rather than a true update to its knowledge (see details in Appendix
F.3
). This again highlights the limitation of atomic facts: because they differ from how knowledge is represented in the real world, the model tends to reject the internalization of such knowledge. While using single-hop questions improves surface memorization (relatively high Fact Acc. of 75.4) through rephrased questions, it fails to enable reasoning (poor Port. of 1.2), further indicating a refusal to
utilize
the information.

3.2.3
Experiments with Llama 3.1

Table
3
reports results for Llama 3.1. Across all inference settings, the method using stories and multi-hop questions yields consistently strong
performance, indicating that our method generalizes beyond a single model family.

The finding regarding answer-only supervision also extends to Llama 3.1. Port.-Hard increases to 95.8 when evaluating
with
CoT. Llama 3.1 is not a reasoning-oriented model, thus it is less capable of producing long reasoning traces compared to reasoning models (Figure
6
). Nevertheless, after being trained with our strategy, the updated model can reliably apply new knowledge when instructed to reason step by step. This suggests that the observed improvements are mainly from the
training procedure
rather than from the model’s inherent reasoning capacity, highlighting the strong generalization and broad applicability of the proposed approach.

5
Discussion

We have discussed how to train large language models to acquire and internalize new knowledge such that it could be flexibly used during reasoning. We argued that effective knowledge internalization is a reasoning problem, and therefore we proposed a reasoning-centric training procedure that introduced new knowledge as contextualized background stories, enforced its use through generated multi-hop questions, and distilled the resulting reasoning behavior using a teacher–student setup.

Although the proposed approach improved knowledge internalization, several challenges remain. Supporting continual updates under multiple, potentially conflicting edits requires models to maintain a coherent and temporally grounded world model, while mitigating forgetting of previously learned information, which was visible as somewhat decreased locality scores after the knowledge updates. We also observed occasional over-application of updated knowledge, suggesting the need for better mechanisms to learn when the new knowledge should be applied. Beyond multi-hop question answering, many real-world applications involve more complex agentic and multi-step reasoning, motivating extensions of knowledge update methods to these settings. Finally, addressing uncertainty and hallucinations may require moving beyond passive updates toward more interactive knowledge acquisition strategies, where models continually seek, assess, and verify new information before integrating it.

We evaluated our approach on the newly introduced MQuAKE-Story, a benchmark tailored to include realistic knowledge updates, as well as on a common benchmark MQuAKE-CF-3k. Experiments with Qwen3-32B and Llama 3.1-70B showed that the proposed method substantially improved the use of updated knowledge in complex multi-hop reasoning tasks while preserving factual accuracy, which highlights the method’s ability to densely integrate the new knowledge into the LLM’s internal world model.

Impact Statement

This work studies how Large Language Models can effectively internalize updated factual knowledge, with a focus on enabling robust multi-step reasoning over edited information. Improving knowledge editing has positive potential impacts, including reducing the propagation of outdated or incorrect information, enabling safer model updates without full retraining, and supporting applications that require timely adaptation to new facts. At the same time, techniques for knowledge editing could be misused to intentionally insert false or misleading information into deployed models. Our work partially mitigates this risk by highlighting the importance of plausibility and contextual grounding for successful knowledge internalization. Overall, we view this work as advancing the scientific understanding of how models represent and reason over updated knowledge, while highlighting the need for responsible deployment and oversight of knowledge editing methods.

Appendix C
Dataset

C.1
MQuAKE-Story

Data construction and processing

We derive MQuAKE-Story from cases in MQuAKE-T.
Each case in MQuAKE-T consists of a newly edited atomic fact and a set of multi-hop test
questions, where one hop depends on the edited fact and the remaining hops depend on
unchanged real-world knowledge. We first filter cases to ensure that post-edit performance reflects difficulties in
integrating new knowledge rather than missing prior knowledge.
Specifically, we retain only cases that satisfy the following conditions: (1) both Qwen3-32B and Llama 3.1-70B correctly answer all unedited single-hop questions; and (2) both models correctly solve the full multi-hop test questions using the
old
knowledge. After filtering, 382 cases remain, corresponding to 38 unique new facts. For each retained case, we replace the original target entity with a
fictional
individual unknown to the models. For example, the original fact
“The current head of state of South Korea is Yoon Suk-yeol”
is replaced with
“The current head of state of South Korea is Min-jun Choi”
, where
Min-jun Choi
is a fictional individual whose biography is generated using Gemini 3 (Appendix
A.1
).

Test questions for each metric

We construct evaluation questions for each metric as follows.
(1) Factual Accuracy – Easy.
We use a direct question that explicitly queries the updated fact. For example, given the update
“The current head of state of South Korea is Min-jun Choi”
, we ask
“Who is the current head of state of South Korea?”

(2) Factual Accuracy – Hard.
For each new fact, we prompt Gemini 3 to generate three paraphrased versions of the corresponding Easy factual question.

(3) Locality.
To evaluate preservation of related knowledge, we prompt Gemini 3 to generate three factual question–answer pairs about the subject entity that are independent of the edited fact (shown in Table
12
). For example, for updates involving South Korea, locality questions may concern its geography, currency, or history. We verify that both base models can achieve a high locality score on these questions. In addition, we include all unedited single-hop questions from the original multi-hop tests. In total, this yields 559 locality test questions.

(4) Portability – Easy.
We use the original multi-hop test questions from MQuAKE-T corresponding to each updated fact, resulting in 1,080 questions.

(5) Portability – Hard.
We manually construct 32 challenging questions that require reasoning over multiple updated facts. Representative examples are shown in Table
13
.

Integration into our workflow

As introduced in Section
2
, for each new fact in MQuAKE-Story that
involves a person as the target entity, we first introduce a fictional individual and
generate a short biography for this person. The edited atomic fact is then represented as a news-style background story. Given the biography and the atomic fact, the model is prompted to generate multi-hop questions that require reasoning over both newly introduced and pre-existing knowledge. When sampling teacher responses, the model is provided with the biography (if available) and the target knowledge, which is presented either as a background story (our method) or as an atomic fact, together with the generated questions.

C.2
MQuAKE-CF

We use a curated subset of MQuAKE-CF-3k, which follows the same structure as MQuAKE-T but
poses a more challenging knowledge editing setting, as test questions in each case may
involve reasoning over multiple edited facts. We apply a series of filtering steps to ensure that evaluation focuses on knowledge integration rather than missing prior knowledge.
Specifically, we remove any case that satisfies one of the following conditions:
(1) the edited fact is already known to either Qwen3-32B or Llama 3.1-70B;
(2) either model fails to correctly answer any unedited single-hop question appearing in
the corresponding multi-hop test questions; or
(3) either model fails to solve the full multi-hop test question using its original
(pre-edit) knowledge.

During inspection of the dataset, we further observe that many edited facts are semantically implausible due to violations of basic spatial or temporal logic. We believe that knowledge editing on such facts is not meaningful and may even raise safety concerns, as it requires injecting logically invalid information into the model. Accordingly, we remove cases involving highly implausible edits, including: (1) facts with the WikiData relation
“…is located in the continent of”
(PID: 30), which often violate basic geometric constraints (e.g.,
“The United Kingdom is located in the continent of Oceania”
or
“Germany is located in the continent of Africa”
); and (2) facts with the WikiData relation
“The capital of …is”
(PID: 36), where the edited capital city is frequently replaced with a city from an unrelated country (e.g.,
“The capital of the United States of America is Harrisville”
or
“The capital of Denmark is Goroka”
). In addition, we manually examine the remaining cases to remove edits that are temporally illogical, such as spousal relationships where one individual passed away centuries before the other was born.
After all filtering steps, the resulting dataset contains 341 unique edited facts.

For evaluation, we follow a protocol analogous to MQuAKE-Story. For Factual Accuracy, we use one direct question from the original dataset (Easy) and three Gemini-generated paraphrases (Hard). For Locality, we prompt Gemini 3 to generate one factual question–answer pair about the subject entity in each fact. Portability is evaluated using the original multi-hop test questions, yielding a total of 756 questions.

Integration into Our Workflow

For MQuAKE-CF, after data filtering, we prompt Gemini 3 to convert the atomic facts into background stories. Unlike MQuAKE-Story, no fictional biographies are constructed, as we retain the original target entities from the dataset. However, multiple new facts in MQuAKE-CF may involve the same subject entity. To avoid generating questions that conflict with other updates for the same subject, we provide the model with all new atomic facts associated with that subject during question generation, and instruct it to generate multi-hop questions targeting one specific fact while remaining consistent with the others.

We implement model updates of the form

using LoRA

, where only the adapter parameters

(

)
with a maximum learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}

and a warm-up of 30 steps. We train the adapters for 10 epochs and select hyperparameters based on validation performance. After training, the adapters are merged into the base model. All experiments are conducted on four AMD MI250X GPUs.

D.3.2
Baseline

We reproduce the baseline following the data generation strategy and loss formulation of
22
, using their official
code repository
:

•

Data diversity in the baseline is achieved through paraphrased context. For MQuAKE-Story, we paraphrase each atomic fact, background story, and biography into five variants.
Combining paraphrased atomic facts or stories with biographies yields
5\times 5=25
context variants. When no biography is available, we paraphrase the corresponding atomic fact or story into 25 variants. For MQuAKE-CF, we paraphrase each atomic fact and background story into 12 variants.

•

To sample continuations, we construct model inputs of the form
[context, question, answer]
, for example:
Italy’s current government head is called Sofia Romano. Who is the current leader of the government? Sofia Romano,
where the model generates text following the comma. We use the sampling length and temperature recommended in
22
and their codebase. To ensure a fair comparison, we match the scale of training data by sampling six continuations per input.

•

The context is removed from the final training data. We construct training examples as
[question, answer, continuation]
, where the continuation corresponds to the sampled outputs from the previous step. All data are split into training and validation sets using a 9:1 ratio.

•

We train using cross-entropy loss, with the answer and the continuation as the prediction target. As argued by
22
, this setup is equivalent to combining context
distillation with KL divergence and supervised fine-tuning with cross-entropy loss. We train a LoRA adapter with rank 128 using a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}
, without warm-up, for 10 epochs and a batch size of 8. Hyperparameters are selected based on validation performance.

D.3.3
SFT

We construct SFT training data using the single-hop questions described in Section
D.3.1
. Each example is formatted as a question–answer pair, with the edited target as the prediction target. We train a LoRA adapter with rank 128 using cross-entropy loss. All hyperparameters match those used in Section
D.3.1
and are selected based on validation performance.

E.2
Answer-only supervision is sufficient for learning and using new knowledge

In the experiments on MQuAKE-Story, we find that explicit reasoning supervision (e.g., training Qwen3 using reasoning traces) exposes the student to many surface-level reasoning trajectories that are specific to the teacher’s solution paths. During inference, the model may partially reuse or attend to such intermediate steps, even when they are irrelevant to the test question, introducing unnecessary variability in multi-hop reasoning. In contrast, answer-only supervision encourages the model to abstract away from individual reasoning paths and internalize the underlying knowledge in a more query-agnostic form. It explains the potential reason why Qwen3 with answer-only supervision (Table
1
) performs better on Port. than Qwen3 trained on reasoning traces (Table
2
), when both are evaluated
with
CoT.

E.3
Semantically implausible updates reduce transfer to explicit reasoning

Analysis of failure cases, where the model answers correctly without CoT but fails with CoT (Table
4
), reveals that the model recalls the new knowledge during its reasoning trace but identifies it as “fictional”, subsequently self-correcting to pre-training knowledge (see example in Appendix
F.2.2
). This behavior mirrors
Obs 3
: the model remains skeptical of the update and refuses to use it during reasoning. We attribute this to the
implausible nature
of many MQuAKE-CF edits (e.g., “The official language of Japan is Swedish”), which strongly conflict with the model’s massive pre-training data. Answer-only supervision effectively encodes the
latent
reasoning path, but it lacks explicit contextual reinforcement necessary to override strong priors. Consequently, when the model is prompted to generate CoT, the explicit reasoning process exposes the conflicts between
implausible new knowledge
and the prior knowledge, leading to potential self-correction and incorrect final answers.

In contrast, SFT models show no such degradation because they fail to generate extended CoT (Figure
7
), bypassing the reasoning process. Similarly, Llama 3.1 shows a milder drop (Table
6
) due to its shorter reasoning traces, which reduce the opportunity to recall conflicting pre-existing knowledge and trigger answer reversal.

F.1.3
Failure cases of Qwen3 trained on reasoning traces

We present three representative failure cases in which Qwen3 trained on reasoning traces using our method.
In Listing
, the model correctly identifies the Prime Minister of the United Kingdom as Eleanor Sterling and the CEO of McDonald’s as Robert Sterling. However, it additionally recalls extraneous information about McDonald’s and Robert Sterling observed during training, such as his prior role as COO at an unrelated company. The model further reproduces an intermediate reasoning step from a training question,
“Who is the CEO of the company that sells Chicken McNuggets?”
, which is unrelated to the current query. This incidental reuse of training-specific reasoning trajectories leads the model to continue along an incorrect reasoning path.
In Listing
, the model hallucinates a news article about Austria that it encountered during training and explicitly reasons about whether the user is “testing” its ability to distinguish between countries. This spurious line of reasoning distracts the model from the relevant knowledge and prevents it from progressing toward the correct answer.
In Listing
, the model correctly recalls that
The Fireballs
originate from the United States, but is nevertheless triggered to generate unnecessary information about the Dominican Republic. This behavior is prompted by “the USA is a North American country, like the Dominican Republic,” that mirrors phrasing seen in the teacher’s intermediate reasoning steps during training. Although the model eventually recognizes that the question concerns the United States, the introduction of such irrelevant reasoning causes it to arrive at an incorrect final answer.
As discussed in Obs.4 (Section
3.2
), these cases suggest a plausible explanation for the weaker performance of Qwen3 trained on reasoning traces compared to Qwen3 with answer-only supervision. Explicit reasoning supervision exposes the student to many reasoning trajectories that are specific to the teacher’s solution paths. During inference, the model may partially reuse or attend to such intermediate steps, even when they are irrelevant to the test question, introducing unnecessary variability and instability in multi-hop reasoning.

F.3
Reasoning Model Learning from Atomic Facts

We trace the failure of reasoning models trained on atomic facts back to the properties of the training data itself. Specifically, the teacher’s reasoning traces frequently include explicit skepticism toward the provided updates, using phrasing such as
“That’s probably a fictional scenario”
or
“Even though in real life this might not be accurate”
, as shown in Listing
. When student models are trained on such reasoning traces, they learn to treat the edited knowledge as conditional or unreliable rather than as a genuine update to their world model.
As a result, models trained with multi-hop questions over atomic facts often refuse to memorize or apply the new knowledge. For example, in Listing
, the model explicitly states
“I don’t recall any mayor changes in NYC recently,”
indicating a failure to internalize the edited fact.

Although training with single-hop questions enables the model to memorize the updated fact through repeated paraphrased supervision, this memorization does not translate into effective reasoning. As shown in Listing
, the model correctly answers a direct query about the mayor of New York City, while still qualifying its response as
“hypothetical or fictional”
, but fails to recall or use the same fact when answering a multi-hop question about “the mayor in the location where Grove Press has its headquarters”.

A similar phenomenon is observed in the MQuAKE-CF setting. Training data from atomic facts frequently contain disclaimers such as
“That’s conflicting with what I know”
or
“Even though my prior knowledge says …, I have to go with …”
(Listing
). Training on such unreliable reasoning trajectories causes the resulting models to rely on the explicit presence of context at inference time, rather than genuinely internalizing the edited knowledge into their reasoning process.

G.3
Improving Locality with Conservative Samples

While our method largely preserves locality, we explore a simple strategy to further protect neighboring knowledge without degrading performance on the edited facts. The key idea is to augment training with conservative samples, which are questions that involve knowledge related to the edited entity but are unrelated to the updated fact itself.

For example, after editing the fact (United Kingdom, governed by, Sebastian I), attributes such as the UK’s monarch may change. To preserve other pre-existing knowledge about the UK, we add questions that are close in topic but independent of the edited attribute, such as “What river flows through London?”. We prompt the pre-edit model to answer these questions and collect its responses as training data, which we refer to as
conservative samples
.

During training, we augment the original knowledge editing data with these conservative samples. We optimize a weighted objective: KL divergence for the edited knowledge (as in our main method) and standard cross-entropy (CE) loss for conservative samples. We evaluate this strategy on MQuAKE-Story, focusing on the Qwen3 trained with (single-hop questions + reasoning traces + background stories). In this setting, the locality drops from 97.0 to 85.5 after editing (Table
2
). For each edited fact, we generate 60 neighboring questions and sample model responses, resulting in a conservative dataset with the same size as the knowledge editing data. We set the CE loss weight to 0.3.

Results are shown in Table
19
. Adding conservative samples improves locality from 85.5 to 90.1 while maintaining performance on all other metrics. Although preliminary, these results suggest that conservative sampling is an effective and promising direction for improving locality, which we leave for future work.
