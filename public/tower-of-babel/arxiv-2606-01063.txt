MindClaw: Closed-Loop Embodied Mental-State Reasoning for Precision Intervention
Ruoxuan Zhang, Qiaoqiao Wan, Zhengguang Wang, Chenghao Yu, Hongxia Xie, Jianlong Fu, Wen-Huang Cheng

Abstract
Theory of Mind (ToM) enables an agent to reason about another actor’s beliefs, goals, and intentions, which is essential for human-centered embodied assistance. Existing ToM benchmarks have advanced text and multimodal mental-state recognition, but they mostly evaluate offline question answering or final action prediction. They do not fully test whether an embodied agent can stay connected to a changing environment, update actor-specific beliefs, decide when reasoning is needed, and intervene only when help is useful. Building on MindPower, we extend robot-centric ToM reasoning to a real-time closed-loop setting and introduce MindClaw, a framework for embodied mental-state reasoning with precision intervention. MindClaw connects multi-source inputs, belief memory, an embodied cognitive trigger skill, mental reasoning, and action generation, allowing the agent to output helpful actions at the right time while remaining silent when intervention is unnecessary. Experiments show that direct VLM baselines struggle with task awareness and intervention calibration, while MindClaw achieves the best overall performance, demonstrating the importance of trigger-skill optimization for closed-loop embodied ToM assistance.



I
Introduction

Theory of Mind (ToM) is the ability to infer other agents’ mental states, including what they believe, desire, and intend. It is a core mechanism behind social reasoning and collaborative behavior
[
13
,
18
,
6
]
. In embodied intelligence, ToM is especially important because an assistant should not only perceive the physical world, but also understand how a human may subjectively interpret that world. The Belief–Desire–Intention (BDI) framework provides a classical computational view of this process: an agent forms beliefs from observations, derives desires from goals, and commits to intentions that guide action
[
23
]
. This perspective naturally raises a key question for embodied agents: can they reason about a human’s mental state and use that reasoning to decide when and how to help?

Recent multimodal ToM benchmarks have made important progress toward this goal. Text-based benchmarks evaluate social reasoning in narratives, while multimodal benchmarks introduce images or videos to test belief, goal, and intention understanding
[
24
,
11
,
17
]
. However, most existing benchmarks still treat ToM as an offline recognition or question-answering problem. The model is usually asked to infer a character’s belief or goal after observing a fixed clip. Such a formulation is useful for measuring mental-state recognition, but it does not require an embodied assistant to maintain state over time, decide whether reasoning should be activated, or act back into an environment. As a result, models can appear competent at answering ToM questions while still failing to behave as useful interactive helpers.

MindPower
[
29
]
takes an important step beyond role-centric ToM recognition by introducing a robot-centric reasoning hierarchy that connects perception, belief, desire, intention, decision, and action. It explicitly evaluates whether VLM-based embodied agents can generate decisions and action plans under false-belief correction and implicit-goal completion tasks. This is a major advance over benchmarks that only ask for human mental-state labels. Nevertheless, MindPower still represents assistance mainly as a final decision/action output after a complete reasoning hierarchy. The agent observes the scenario, reasons through the hierarchy, and then produces an action plan. It does not fully model real-time interaction where the assistant remains connected to a changing simulator, updates memory at each step, decides whether to trigger mental reasoning, and acts only when the current state warrants intervention.

This distinction matters because real embodied assistance is not equivalent to always producing an action. In human-centered environments, unnecessary help can be disruptive, intrusive, or even harmful
[
2
]
. A capable assistant must therefore follow a precision-intervention principle: intervene only when intervention is needed, and otherwise remain silent. For example, if a human is already aligned with the environment and progressing normally, the correct robot action is no action. If the human acts under a stale belief about an object location, the robot may need to reason about that mismatch and generate a minimal helpful action. Thus, the central problem is not only
what action should the robot output
, but
when should the system update memory, reason about another mind, generate help, or do nothing
.

We propose MindClaw as
an extension of MindPower
[
29
]
from final-stage robot-centric action generation to closed-loop embodied mental-state assistance. MindClaw keeps the robot-centric motivation of MindPower, but changes the computational problem from static hierarchy completion to online cognitive control. The system connects to multiple input sources, including static videos, VirtualHome
[
22
]
, ThreeDWorld
[
7
]
, and human-control events. At each step, it first obtains the observation, then uses a trigger module to decide the next internal cognitive operation. The trigger may update the belief table, invoke mental reasoning, call action generation, or emit
noop
. Only when reasoning indicates that help is warranted does MindClaw output a robot action.

The key idea is to formulate triggering as an embodied cognitive skill. Rather than mapping video directly to an action, MindClaw performs intermediate cognitive modeling over observation, actor-specific belief memory, recent operations, and valid scene candidates. This trigger skill decides whether the current state requires belief writing, mental reasoning, action generation, or no operation. We further collect skills from correct and incorrect trajectories, validate them across trajectories, group them by atomic operation, and use them as strong rules, preferred conditions, or negative conditions during inference. Strong rules can be matched deterministically, while ambiguous cases are handled by a skill-augmented trigger model.

Our contributions are threefold:

•

We extend robot-centric ToM reasoning from final-stage decision/action generation to a real-time closed-loop setting where the assistant remains connected to an environment and can act back into it.

•

We introduce precision intervention as the central objective for embodied mental-state assistance: the robot should generate helpful actions only when needed and output
none
otherwise.

•

We formulate the trigger as an embodied cognitive skill that performs intermediate operation selection over observation, belief memory, recent operations, and scene candidates, enabling explicit control over when to update memory, reason, act, or remain silent.

II
Related Work

II-A

Theory-of-Mind Benchmarks

Recent language and multimodal Theory-of-Mind (ToM) benchmarks have made social and mental-state reasoning measurable in modern foundation models. Text-based benchmarks such as FANToM
[
12
]
and Hi-ToM
[
26
]
evaluate whether language models can reason about social situations, nested beliefs, and character-specific mental states in narrative contexts. Multimodal benchmarks such as MMToM-QA
[
11
]
, MuMA-ToM
[
24
]
, and BDIQA
[
17
]
further introduce images or videos and ask models to infer beliefs, desires, intentions, or hidden goals from visual evidence. These datasets are important because they move ToM evaluation beyond purely textual reasoning. However, they still mainly formulate ToM as offline recognition or question answering: the model observes a fixed story, image, or clip and predicts a label or answer. This does not test whether an embodied agent can remain connected to a changing environment, maintain actor-specific memory over time, decide when ToM reasoning should be activated, or choose whether intervention is needed. MindPower
[
29
]
moves closer to embodied assistance by introducing a robot-centric reasoning hierarchy from perception to action, but it still mainly evaluates final decision/action generation after observing a scenario. MindClaw follows this robot-centric direction while shifting the problem to real-time closed-loop precision intervention, where the agent must decide when to update memory, reason, act, or remain silent.

II-B

Claw-Based Embodied Interaction Frameworks

Claw-style agent frameworks study how large models can be connected to tools, applications, and executable environments. OpenClaw
[
21
]
provides an agent runtime for tool invocation, interaction state, and external execution, and recent embodied extensions show that this architecture can support robotic interaction. RoboClaw
[
14
]
uses a VLM-driven agent controller for scalable long-horizon robotic tasks, while ABot-Claw
[
10
]
extends OpenClaw toward persistent, cooperative, and self-evolving robotic agents with a unified embodiment interface, multimodal memory, and critic-based feedback. These frameworks demonstrate that Claw-like systems can be used in embodied intelligence rather than only software automation. Nevertheless, their focus is mainly task execution, skill orchestration, robot capability scheduling, memory, and replanning. They do not explicitly model human mental states as part of the control problem, maintain actor-specific belief tables, or decide whether a human is acting under a false belief or hidden goal. MindClaw addresses this missing direction by combining Claw-style interaction and execution with ToM-driven precision intervention: its trigger is an embodied cognitive skill that decides when mental-state reasoning should affect robot action.

III
Preliminaries

III-A

MindPower Benchmark

MindPower
[
29
]
is a robot-centric theory-of-mind (ToM) benchmark designed to evaluate whether VLM-based embodied agents can link visual perception, mental-state reasoning, decision making, and action generation. As shown in Table
I
, it contains 590 video-text examples, where the model takes a video as input and produces structured textual outputs. Unlike benchmarks that only evaluate mental-state inference, MindPower provides labels that include not only belief, desire, and intention reasoning, but also downstream decisions and executable actions. It defines two task types:
False-Belief Correction
, where the agent assists a person who holds an incorrect belief about the environment, and
Implicit Goal Inference and Completion
, where the agent infers an unstated goal from human behavior and completes the missing assistance. MindPower further introduces a reasoning hierarchy that organizes perception, mental reasoning, decision making, and action generation, as shown in Fig.
2
and Tab.
I
. Compared with prior ToM benchmarks, MindPower focuses on scenarios in which mental reasoning is necessary for selecting appropriate decisions and actions.

III-B

Limitations of the Starting Setting

Each MindPower example mainly requires the model to observe a complete scenario and then generate a reasoning hierarchy together with a final decision or action. This setting is useful for evaluating robot-centric ToM reasoning, but it remains a static video-to-text formulation. The agent is not required to stay connected to a live simulator, maintain belief states over time, or determine whether and when mental reasoning should be triggered during interaction. MindClaw preserves the robot-centric ToM objective of MindPower, but extends it to a real-time embodied interaction setting. Specifically, it introduces actor-specific belief memory, trigger-controlled cognitive operations, and precision intervention, enabling the agent to reason and act only when the evolving scene requires it.

IV
Method

IV-A

Task Formulation

We study closed-loop embodied mental-state reasoning for precision intervention in settings where an assistant can be connected to an executable environment in real time. The input is not limited to a pre-recorded video. MindClaw supports multiple input sources: static video, live simulator streams from VirtualHome
[
22
]

or ThreeDWorld
[
7
]
, human instructions, and keyboard-control events. In the static-video setting, the system replays an offline clip as a sequence of visual windows. In the simulator setting, it connects to an executable environment, receives online state or rendered observations, and can send back executable commands. In the human-input setting, user instructions or control events are converted into the same runtime interface.

At each window or interaction step, the assistant must decide whether the current state requires internal cognitive updates and, ultimately, whether the robot should output a helpful action. This setting differs from static mental-state recognition because the goal is not only to infer what an actor believes. The goal is to control an online loop that turns observation and actor-specific mental-state memory into calibrated assistance. The simulator connection is therefore not only a data source. It is part of the task: the system must reason under changing state, decide whether intervention is necessary, output an executable robot action when help is needed, and then observe the effect of that action.

Let
w_{t}
denote the input window at step
t
, and let
s_{t}
denote the maintained runtime state before processing the window. In MindClaw,
s_{t}
contains actor-specific belief memory, scene context, recent trigger outputs, and execution history. Processing
w_{t}
produces a trace

\tau_{t}=(o_{t},z_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,z_{t}^{(K)},r_{t},a_{t},e_{t}),

(1)

where
o_{t}
is the structured observation extracted from
w_{t}
,
z_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,z_{t}^{(K)}
are internal cognitive operations selected by the trigger loop,
r_{t}
is the mental-reasoning result when reasoning is invoked,
a_{t}
is the final robot action or
none
, and
e_{t}
is the execution result returned by the environment. This notation separates internal trigger decisions from the action output. The trigger decides what computation should happen next; the framework is designed so that the final output
a_{t}
is a helpful executable action when intervention is warranted.

The trigger output is an internal cognitive operation:

z_{t}=\mathrm{verb}(\mathrm{actor},\mathrm{object},\mathrm{location}).

(2)

The operation space is defined in Sec.
IV-F
. It decomposes embodied assistance into visual-state tracking, actor-belief modeling, belief revision, reasoning activation, and intervention calibration. This decomposition is important because end-to-end failure can arise from different cognitive stages. A model may correctly observe that an object moved but fail to update an actor belief. It may maintain memory correctly but fail to invoke mental reasoning. It may invoke reasoning but fail to produce a useful robot action.

MindClaw follows a precision-intervention principle. If the actor is aligned with the observed world state and is progressing normally, the final robot action should be
none
. If the maintained belief state and current observation indicate a useful opportunity to resolve a false belief, hidden goal, or task-blocking mismatch, MindClaw should output a minimal helpful action
a_{t}
. Therefore,
noop
is a first-class trigger outcome, and
none
is a valid final action.

IV-B

Architecture Overview

MindClaw is organized into three parts, as shown in Fig.
3
: a multi-source input interface, a Claw Layer, and a Reasoning Layer. The input interface receives observations from VirtualHome, ThreeDWorld, static videos, or human input such as keyboard control. The Claw Layer maintains the online state of the interaction and decides which internal cognitive operation should be executed next. The Reasoning Layer provides three model-based services: observation, mental reasoning, and action generation. The two layers communicate in both directions. The Claw Layer sends the current window and state to the Reasoning Layer, and the Reasoning Layer returns structured outputs that update memory or produce robot actions.

For each step, MindClaw executes the following loop:

1.

receive input from a simulator, static video, or human-control interface;

2.

normalize the input through the Adapter and pass it to the Engine;

3.

call the Observation module to obtain a structured description
o_{t}
;

4.

use the Trigger to select the next internal operation
z_{t}
from
o_{t}
, Memory, and candidate slots;

5.

execute
z_{t}
: update the belief table if
z_{t}
is a belief operation, call Mental Reasoning if
z_{t}=\texttt{reasoning\_run}
, call Action Generation if
z_{t}=\texttt{action\_run}
, or stop the trigger loop if
z_{t}=\texttt{noop}
;

6.

output and execute a helpful robot action
a_{t}
when intervention is justified.

This architecture separates internal cognitive control from external robot behavior. The trigger output
z_{t}
controls the loop, while the robot action
a_{t}
is the final assistance output. This separation is important because the system should often update memory or reason without acting, and should output an action only when intervention is useful.

IV-C

Input Interface

The input interface makes MindClaw usable across different embodied settings. It supports four sources shown in the architecture: VirtualHome, ThreeDWorld, static video, and human input. VirtualHome and ThreeDWorld provide executable simulator environments. They can stream visual observations, expose symbolic scene information, and receive executable commands from the robot. Static video provides offline visual windows for replay and evaluation. Human input, including keyboard control or user instructions, provides interactive events that can change the task state or actor behavior.

All input sources are converted into a common window representation. A window may contain rendered frames, simulator state, object and actor identifiers, user-control metadata, task context, and candidate actor/object/location sets. This common representation allows the rest of the framework to remain unchanged across offline video analysis, real-time simulator execution, and human-controlled interaction. In the simulator setting, the interface is closed loop: after MindClaw outputs an action
a_{t}
, the action is sent to the environment, and the next input window reflects the updated state.

IV-D

Claw Layer

The Claw Layer is the control layer of MindClaw. It is responsible for maintaining the online state of the interaction and deciding when to call the Reasoning Layer. It contains four components: Engine, Adapter, Memory, and Trigger.

Engine.
The Engine manages the whole runtime loop. It receives normalized input windows, calls the Reasoning Layer when needed, applies trigger-selected state updates, and stores artifacts. It also enforces the step order of the system: observation first, trigger decision second, and then the operation selected by the trigger. Thus, belief-table modification is not an independent pre-trigger step. It happens only when the trigger emits a belief create or update operation. In simulator mode, the Engine keeps the loop synchronized with the environment so that every executed action can affect the next state.

Adapter.
The Adapter hides differences across input sources. It converts VirtualHome states, ThreeDWorld streams, static video frames, and human-control events into the same internal window format. It also maps simulator-specific object names, actor identifiers, and action commands into canonical names used by the Reasoning Layer. This allows the trigger and memory modules to operate on stable actor, object, and location slots rather than source-specific formats.

Memory.
Memory stores the information needed for closed-loop mental-state reasoning. It has two parts. The first is the recent operation history, which records previous trigger outputs, reasoning calls, action-generation calls, and execution results. The second is the belief table, which separates visually grounded facts from actor-specific beliefs:

\displaystyle\texttt{visual\_facts}[object]

\displaystyle\rightarrow location,

(3)

\displaystyle\texttt{actor\_beliefs}[actor][object]

\displaystyle\rightarrow location.

This representation is central to false-belief assistance. If an object is currently on a shelf but an actor still believes it is on a desk, the mismatch is visible in memory. The mismatch does not directly command the robot to act, but it provides the evidence used by the trigger and mental-reasoning modules. The belief table is updated through trigger operations, not by the Observation module directly.

Trigger.
The Trigger is implemented as an embodied cognitive skill that decides the next internal operation
z_{t}
. It reads the current observation, recent operations, belief table, and candidate slots, then chooses whether the system should update the belief table, run mental reasoning, run action generation, or do nothing. We describe the Trigger in detail in Sec.
IV-F
, because it is the main decision point that determines whether the robot can produce timely and non-intrusive help.

IV-E

Reasoning Layer

The Reasoning Layer contains the model-based components that interpret observations, infer mental states, and generate actions. It has three modules: Observation, Mental Reasoning, and Action Generation.

Observation.
The Observation module converts an input window into a structured description
o_{t}
. It summarizes visible actors, object movements, candidate object locations, visibility events, and task-relevant scene context. Its role is descriptive rather than prescriptive: it does not decide whether the robot should help. This keeps perception separate from intervention and makes later failures easier to diagnose.

Mental Reasoning.
The Mental Reasoning module is called when the Trigger emits
reasoning_run
. Given the current observation and belief table, it judges whether the focus actor is likely acting under a false belief, hidden goal, or other mental-state mismatch. It also decides whether the mismatch blocks task progress and whether intervention is warranted. Its output is a reasoning record
r_{t}
, not a robot command.

Action Generation.
The Action Generation module is called when the Trigger emits
action_run
. It converts the reasoning record into the final robot action
a_{t}
. If intervention is not justified,
a_{t}=\texttt{none}
. If help is needed,
a_{t}
is a minimal helpful action or action sequence, such as moving toward an object, opening a container, relocating an object, or guiding the actor toward the correct place. In simulator mode, the generated action is compiled into executable environment commands and sent back through the Adapter.

IV-F

Trigger as a Cognitive Dispatcher

The Trigger is the dispatcher between the Claw Layer and the Reasoning Layer. We formulate it as an
embodied cognitive skill
: a reusable intermediate policy that decides which cognitive operation should be executed before the robot produces an external action. This skill is embodied because its decision is grounded in the current observation, actor-specific belief memory, recent operations, and executable environment context. It is cognitive because its output is not a physical robot command, but an internal operation that controls memory writing, mental reasoning, action generation, or silence.

Let

x_{t}=(o_{t},B_{t-1},H_{t-1},C_{t})

(4)

denote the trigger context, where
o_{t}
is the structured observation,
B_{t-1}
is the current belief table,
H_{t-1}
is the recent operation history, and
C_{t}
contains the valid actor, object, and location candidates. The Trigger outputs

z_{t}=(v_{t},i_{t},j_{t},\ell_{t}),

(5)

where
v_{t}
is the operation verb, and
i_{t}
,
j_{t}
, and
\ell_{t}
are the actor, object, and location slots. The operation
z_{t}
is not the robot’s external action. It is a control signal that decides which internal computation should happen next.

We model the Trigger as a skill-conditioned policy:

\pi_{\theta}(z_{t}\mid x_{t},\mathcal{S})=P_{\theta}(v_{t},i_{t},j_{t},\ell_{t}\mid o_{t},B_{t-1},H_{t-1},C_{t},\mathcal{S}),

(6)

where
\mathcal{S}
is a collection of embodied cognitive skills or rules. In practice, the policy is implemented through skill-augmented prompting, supervised fine-tuning, or their combination. The skill representation provides intermediate modeling: instead of directly mapping video to robot action, MindClaw first predicts a structured cognitive operation and then uses that operation to update memory, invoke reasoning, or generate the final action.

We factor the trigger decision into operation selection and slot grounding:

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta}(z_{t}\mid x_{t},\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle=P_{\theta}(v_{t}\mid x_{t},\mathcal{S})

(7)

\displaystyle\cdot P_{\theta}(i_{t},j_{t},\ell_{t}\mid v_{t},x_{t},\mathcal{S}).

The first term decides what kind of cognitive step is needed, such as belief update, reasoning, action generation, or no operation. The second term grounds this decision to a specific actor, object, and location under the candidate constraints. This factorization is useful because many errors arise not only from choosing the wrong verb, but also from grounding the operation to the wrong actor or object.

The current operation space is

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}=\{

\displaystyle\texttt{belief\_create\_visual\_fact}(\texttt{none},j,\ell),

(8)

\displaystyle\texttt{belief\_update\_visual\_fact}(\texttt{none},j,\ell),

\displaystyle\texttt{belief\_create\_actor\_belief}(i,j,\ell),

\displaystyle\texttt{belief\_update\_actor\_belief}(i,j,\ell),

\displaystyle\texttt{reasoning\_run}(i,j,\texttt{none}),

\displaystyle\texttt{action\_run}(i,j,\texttt{none}),

\displaystyle\texttt{noop}(\texttt{none},\texttt{none},\texttt{none})\}.

Here
i
,
j
, and
\ell
must be selected from the valid actor, object, and location candidates in
C_{t}
. This constrained output format turns trigger prediction into a controllable operation-selection and slot-grounding problem.

The embodied cognitive skill follows three principles. First, observation precedes trigger decision: the system obtains
o_{t}
from the Observation module before deciding whether the belief table needs to be changed. Second, belief operations precede reasoning: if
o_{t}
indicates a new visual fact or actor belief, the Trigger should select the corresponding belief-table operation before using that memory for mental-state inference. Third, mental reasoning precedes robot action: a belief mismatch alone is not enough; the system must decide whether the mismatch blocks the actor’s goal before invoking action generation. These principles prevent over-helping and make no-action a valid outcome.

This formulation explains why the Trigger is the key component of MindClaw. If it emits
noop
when a belief update or reasoning call is needed, the system misses the chance to help. If it emits
action_run
too early, the robot may interrupt unnecessarily. By representing the trigger as an embodied cognitive skill, we can explicitly encode and learn the intermediate decisions that connect perception, memory, mental-state reasoning, and helpful action output.

IV-G

Skill Collection and Rule-Based Inference

As shown in Figure
4
, the skill set
\mathcal{S}
used by the Trigger is collected from paired correct and incorrect rollouts. Each rollout contains the observation, memory state, model prediction, and target operation. We provide these paired cases to a strong LLM, such as GPT and Qwen3-4B
[
27
]
, and ask it to summarize reusable decision patterns. Each summarized pattern is a candidate skill, for example a condition under which an actor belief should be created, a visual fact should be updated, or
noop
should be preferred.

The raw skills are then validated over the full trajectory set and grouped by atomic operation. For each operation
v\in\mathcal{Z}
, we collect the skills that support that operation and organize them into three categories:

\mathcal{S}_{v}=\left(\mathcal{S}^{+}_{v},\mathcal{S}^{\pm}_{v},\mathcal{S}^{-}_{v}\right),

(9)

where
\mathcal{S}^{+}_{v}
contains strongly preferred conditions,
\mathcal{S}^{\pm}_{v}
contains weaker preferred conditions, and
\mathcal{S}^{-}_{v}
contains negative conditions. A strongly preferred condition means that when the condition is satisfied, the corresponding operation should be selected. A preferred condition provides evidence for the operation but may require model judgment. A negative condition rules out the operation even if some surface cues appear relevant.

During inference, we use a two-stage procedure. First, the system applies deterministic matching for strongly preferred and negative conditions. The strict skills in
\mathcal{S}^{+}_{v}
are converted into regular-expression or structured-field matchers whenever possible. If a strong matcher fires and no negative condition blocks it, the system directly outputs the corresponding trigger operation. If no strong rule applies, the observation, memory, candidate slots, and the relevant preferred and negative skills are passed to the trigger model for skill-augmented prediction:

z_{t}=\begin{cases}R_{v}(x_{t}),&\begin{aligned} &\text{if }R_{v}\in\mathcal{S}^{+}_{v}\text{ matches}\\
&\text{and no }R^{-}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{v}\text{ matches},\end{aligned}\\
\arg\max\limits_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\pi_{\theta}(z\mid x_{t},\mathcal{S}),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(10)

This design combines precision and flexibility. Regex-style matching handles high-confidence cases that should not depend on model sampling. The skill-augmented trigger model handles ambiguous cases where multiple operations may be plausible or where the condition is not easily captured by a strict rule.

This process turns error analysis into an explicit intermediate representation. Instead of only fine-tuning on labels, we extract reusable embodied cognitive skills from correct and incorrect trajectories, validate them across all trajectories, sort them by atomic operation, and use them to guide inference. The resulting trigger is therefore both data-driven and skill-guided: strict skills provide deterministic shortcuts, while the learned trigger policy handles the remaining cases.

V
Experiments

V-A

Setup and Metrics

We evaluate MindClaw on MindPower Benchmark
[
29
]
. We mainly report three intervention metrics following the MindPower style:
Task Accuracy (TA)
, which measures whether the system understands the task target and relevant mental-state context;
Precision Intervention Accuracy (PIA)
, which measures whether the system intervenes only when assistance is needed; and
Closed-loop / Action Satisfaction (CS)
, which measures whether the generated action satisfies the closed-loop assistance goal.

When inference, we utilize Qwen3-4B
[
27
]
as Trigger, and GPT-5.5
[
20
]
as Observation, Mental Reasoning and action model.

V-B

Baseline and MindClaw Results

We first compare direct VLM baselines with MindClaw on the full embodied intervention task. As shown in Table
II
, direct video-language models struggle with both task understanding and intervention calibration. Several models obtain near-zero TA, indicating frequent failures in identifying the relevant task target and mental-state context. PIA remains low across baselines, suggesting that visual recognition alone is insufficient for deciding whether assistance is needed. Although some models achieve high CS among triggered actions, this does not imply effective assistance, because they often fail to trigger intervention at the appropriate time.

MindClaw provides a stronger and more balanced result. It improves TA and PIA over direct baselines while maintaining 100.00% CS, showing that better intervention timing and precision can be achieved without sacrificing action validity. This result supports the need for an explicit intervention pipeline rather than direct video-to-action generation: the agent must detect when assistance is required, maintain actor-specific belief states, and execute interventions in a closed loop.

VI
Conclusion

We presented MindClaw, a closed-loop framework for real-time interactive mental-state reasoning in embodied environments. Within this framework, the agent can decide whether a person is acting under a false belief, hidden goal, or task-blocking mismatch, and can produce a reasonable helpful action when assistance is needed.
We also introduced precision intervention as the central objective for embodied mental-state assistance. An embodied agent should not help by default. It should intervene at the right time, with the right target, and only when the current mental-state and environment state indicate that help is useful. To support this objective, we formulated the trigger as an embodied cognitive skill that mediates between observation, belief memory, mental reasoning, and action generation. By collecting and optimizing trigger skills, MindClaw improves the accuracy of intermediate cognitive-operation prediction and achieves the best reported performance in our experiments.
