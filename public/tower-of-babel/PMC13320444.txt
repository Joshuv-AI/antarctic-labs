Recurrent transplant renal vein thrombosis caused by May-Thurner syndrome: a case report and review of literature
Authors: Koosha kamali, Hossein Saffari, Ehsan Zolfi, Shervin Najafizadeh, Nasrollah Abian, Farhad Ahmadi, Vahid Vahedisoraki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transplant renal vein thrombosis (TRVT) affects 0.1% to 4.2% of transplants, leading to graft loss in almost all cases. Mechanical factors are the most common underlying cause of TRVT, a category that includes May-Thurner syndrome (MTS). We report a 62-year-old man underwent left-sided kidney transplantation but developed recurrent deep vein thrombosis and TRVT. Two emergency thrombectomies restored graft function, though no iatrogenic cause was identified. Further investigations revealed MTS as the underlying etiology. Left common iliac vein stenting with long-term anticoagulation was implemented, highlighting the importance of considering anatomic venous anomalies in transplant recipients with unexplained or recurrent thrombosis.

Introduction

May–Thurner syndrome (MTS) is characterized by compression of the iliocaval venous segment against the underlying vertebral body by the overlying arterial system, most commonly involving the left common iliac vein (LCIV). Although asymptomatic in most individuals, MTS can impair venous outflow and predispose patients to deep vein thrombosis (DVT).1

Transplant renal vein thrombosis (TRVT), which affects 0.1%–4.2% of kidney transplant recipients, occurs predominantly within the first 2 weeks after transplantation surgery2,3 and results in graft loss in most cases.2 Mechanical factors, including external compression of the venous system, are considered the most common contributors to TRVT.2,4 Therefore, May-Thurner syndrome (MTS) may predispose patients undergoing left-sided kidney transplantation to TRVT. However, reports of TRVT secondary to MTS in the English-language literature remain scarce. Here, we report a rare case of recurrent TRVT due to MTS and discuss its clinical presentation, diagnostic challenges, and management. We also provide a literature review in this regard.

Case presentation

A 62-year-old man with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease who was on hemodialysis for 2 years and had left nephrectomy one year prior due to severe flank pain, underwent living donor kidney transplantation on the left side of the pelvis. The transplant renal vein (TRV) and artery were anastomosed end-to-side to the left external iliac vein (LEIV) and artery, respectively. Immediate postoperative Doppler ultrasonography (DUS) showed good blood flow. Urine output was 700 mL/hour on the surgery day. Immunosuppressive medications included tacrolimus and prednisolone. By postoperative day (POD) 4, his serum creatinine decreased to 1.3 mg/dL.

On the morning of POD 5, the patient developed left leg swelling. DUS revealed left lower extremity DVT extending to the femoral vein sparing LEIV and TRV. Heparin infusion (1100 units/hour) was started. As the patient complained of surgical site bulging and pain, abdominopelvic CT scan was taken which showed no significant hematoma nor lymphocele. Urine output remained ∼250 mL/h but later that night he became anuric. Repeat DUS showed thrombus progression to the LEIV, TRVT, and severe reduction in transplant renal artery blood flow. Creatinine rose to 3.2 mg/dL.

Urgent surgical exploration revealed a congested graft with palpable thrombosis in the TRV and LEIV. Incising the LEIV proximal to the anastomosis, thrombi burst out. The graft color normalized and no anastomotic stenosis was detected. The incision was sutured and intraoperative DUS confirmed restored blood flow. Heparin was continued postoperatively. The patient's urine output improved, leg swelling resolved, and creatinine returned to 1.4 mg/dL after 24 hours.

2 days later, apixaban 5 mg twice daily replaced heparin. The following day was uneventful. However, on the next day, the patient again developed leg swelling, anuria, and increased creatinine (2.3 mg/dL). DUS confirmed recurrent leg thrombosis extending to LEIV and TRV. A second open thrombectomy was performed. Both heparin and apixaban were continued afterward. Creatinine normalized after 3 days, and follow-up DUS showed no thrombosis. The patient remained hospitalized for further investigations.

Reviewing our case in a multidisciplinary meeting with the presence of transplant surgery team, nephrologist, vascular surgeon, and radiologist, the compression of the LCIV by the left common iliac artery could be inferred from the CT scan (Fig. 1a and b) which suggested the possibility of MTS. Hence, CT venography by injecting contrast into the left femoral vein was proposed for the patient showing contrast in the collateral thigh and pelvic veins evading LEIV, corroborating high pressure in left iliac veins (Fig. 1c); further confirming presence of MTS. The patient was discharged 6 days after venous stenting on apixaban having creatinine of 1.4 mg/dL. 4 months later, he remained symptom-free with normal graft function (creatinine 1.3 mg/dL) and no recurrence of DVT or TRVT.

Discussion

MTS was first described in 1957 as a cause of pelvic vein thrombosis.5 A decade later, Cockett and Thomas established its connection to ipsilateral iliofemoral DVT.6 The exact prevalence of MTS in general population remains unclear as MTS is often asymptomatic.7 It is clinically linked to 2 to 5% of all DVT cases; however, MTS may have a more significant role given the disproportionately higher incidence of left-sided DVT.8

TRVT presents acutely with symptoms such as oliguria, deteriorating graft function, and painful graft, which in severe cases may lead to its rupture.3 Among numerous risk factors for TRVT, mechanical causes, including venous compression, are considered significant contributors2,3,7; and as MTS is a form of venous compression, it should be recognized as an important risk factor for TRVT. Ultrasonography is the first-line imaging modality for diagnosing TRVT. Gray-scale ultrasonography may show cortical edema, perirenal hematoma, and TRV thrombosis. DUS can detect venous obstruction signs namely reversed arterial diastolic flow, a spike-like systolic wave, and absence of renal vein visualization.2 Advanced imaging such as CT angiography (CTA) offers superior anatomical detail and can identify rare anomalies like MTS. Although conventional angiography remains the gold standard for vascular diagnosis, its invasiveness and risk of nephrotoxicity restrict its use to patients planned for endovascular interventions.2,3

As Table 2 depicts, endovascular treatment including balloon dilatation and stenting (with thrombectomy or thrombolysis whenever needed) seems to be a promising approach for managing MTS-related TRVT. Our unique experience in this patient confirms that urgent thrombectomies can save transplanted kidney before stent placement, but it is the balloon dilation and stenting which ultimately prevents recurrent thrombosis.

There is currently no literature supporting prophylactic stent placement in transplant candidates with MTS.10 However, given the potential “one too many” complication, i.e. graft loss, prophylactic stenting may be a reasonable strategy. Future studies may provide insight regarding the prophylactic role of such intervention in MTS patients undergoing left side kidney transplantation.

Conclusion

TRVT, especially recurrent one, may be secondary to unrecognized venous anomalies such as MTS. In such cases, early diagnosis and correction with thrombectomy plus iliac vein stenting can preserve graft function and prevent TRVT recurrence.
