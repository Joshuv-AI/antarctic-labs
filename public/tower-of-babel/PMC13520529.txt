The mitochondrial GTPase ERAL1 inhibits HBV replication by triggering the MAVS signaling cascade
Authors: Caorui Lin, Xiaosang Zheng, Kaixin Chen, Ning Ran, Linjie Luo, Xin Zhang, Ying Huang, Jiawei Zhang, Hanyong Zhu, Jieying He, Bin Yang, Qishui Ou, Can Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsMitochondrial GTPase ERA G-protein-like 1 (ERAL1) is considered an antiviral host factor against RNA viruses. However, its role in driving DNA virus infection, particularly hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, and its relevance to human liver disease are unknown. This study aimed to define the function and molecular mechanism of ERAL1 in HBV pathogenesis.MethodsComplementary models, including HBV-replicating cell lines, an rAAV8-HBV hydrodynamic mouse model, and clinical samples from patients across the natural history of chronic HBV infection were employed and analyzed via coimmunoprecipitation, confocal immunofluorescence, RNA sequencing, qPCR, immunoblotting, and immunohistochemistry.ResultsERAL1 expression was significantly suppressed in HBV patients, including an approximately 75% reduction in the livers of patients during the immune-reactive phase compared with those in healthy controls. Introduction

Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a considerable global health challenge, with an estimated 296 million individuals affected worldwide [1]. Current first-line therapies, including nucleos(t)ide analogs and interferon-α, can suppress viral replication but rarely achieve a functional cure, which is defined as the sustained loss of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) [2, 3]. The persistence of the viral covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) reservoir and complex virus–host interplay continue to drive disease progression toward cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [4, 5]. This therapeutic impasse underscores the critical need to identify novel host-targeting agents and new antiviral mechanisms.

In addition to their canonical role in driving energy metabolism, mitochondria are pivotal signaling hubs that govern innate immune responses [6–9]. Within the context of HBV infection, mitochondria are actively subverted, namely, the viral HBx protein localizes to mitochondria, impairing their function, promoting reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and dampening cellular antiviral defenses [10, 11]. Conversely, the host mounts a defense through mitochondrial adaptor proteins such as mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS), which is essential for triggering interferon-mediated responses upon viral sensing [12–14]. This dual role as both a target of viral sabotage and a platform for host defense positions mitochondrial integrity and signaling as compelling frontiers for therapeutic intervention against HBV infection [15–17].

Among the proteins that govern mitochondrial homeostasis, ERA G-protein-like 1 (ERAL1), a conserved mitochondrial GTPase and chaperone for 12 S ribosomal RNA, is critical for mitochondrial small ribosomal subunit assembly and translation [18, 19]. Genetic loss of ERAL1 disrupts mitochondrial proteostasis, increases superoxide levels, and promotes apoptosis, with mutations linked to Perrault syndrome [20, 21]. Intriguingly, recent work has revealed a noncanonical, extramitochondrial role for ERAL1 during RNA virus infection, where it facilitates MAVS aggregation and amplifies antiviral signaling [22]. Despite its established role in combating RNA viruses and maintaining mitochondrial fitness, whether ERAL1 participates in determining the host response against HBV, a DNA virus with a distinct replication cycle, is unknown.

Here, we demonstrate that HBV infection actively decreases ERAL1 expression in vitro and in vivo, with patient liver tissue analysis revealing a reduction in expression of approximately 75% during the immune-reactive phase. Functionally, ERAL1 overexpression results in potent antiviral activity, significantly inhibiting HBV replication and achieving nearly 60% HBsAg clearance in a mouse model of HBV infection. Mechanistically, we found that ERAL1 performs its anti-HBV function by interacting with MAVS, promoting MAVS aggregation, and subsequently activating the downstream Akt and MAPK signaling cascades. These findings identify ERAL1 as a crucial HBV-restricting host factor and reveal a novel mitochondria-centered defense pathway, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for achieving a functional cure in chronic hepatitis B patients.

Results

HBV infection downregulates ERAL1 expression in vitro and in vivo

To assess the impact of HBV infection on ERAL1 expression, we first analyzed ERAL1 expression in cell culture models. Both the mRNA and protein levels of ERAL1 were significantly lower in HBV-infected HepG2-NTCP cells than in uninfected control cells (Fig. 1A, B). This downregulation was also observed in HepG2.2.15 cells (Fig. S1A, B) and in Huh7 cells transiently transfected with an HBV plasmid (Fig. S1C, D). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed the reduction in the ERAL1 signal and revealed that its subcellular distribution shifted from exhibiting a diffuse pattern in control cells to aggregated puncta in HBV-infected cells (Fig. 1C).

We next extended our investigation to clinical samples. Analysis of human peritumoral liver tissues revealed that ERAL1 expression was significantly lower (by approximately 60%, Fig. 1D) in HBV-positive samples than in HBV-negative control samples (the patient characteristics are given in Supplementary Table 1). Notably, compared with that in livers from healthy controls (HC), ERAL1 expression in liver biopsies from patients in the immune-reactive phase (IA) (the patient characteristics are given in Supplementary Table 2) was nearly 75% lower (Fig. 1E). And observed downregulation of ERAL1 in patient tissues may reflect a combination of direct viral effects and the local inflammatory milieu. Consistent with the cellular and human tissue data, ERAL1 expression was markedly suppressed in the livers of rAAV8-1.3HBV-infected mice, as demonstrated by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT–PCR) analysis (Fig. S1E), Western blotting (Fig. S1F), and immunohistochemistry (Fig. S1G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that HBV infection robustly downregulates ERAL1 expression across experimental models and in humans with HBV infection and alters its subcellular localization in hepatocytes.

ERAL1 suppresses HBV replication in vitro

Given the downregulation of ERAL1 expression during HBV infection, we investigated the functional role of ERAL1 in multiple hepatocyte models. As shown in Fig. 2, overexpression of ERAL1 in HepG2.2.15 cells (Fig. 2A, B) significantly reduced the secretion of HBsAg and HBeAg (Fig. 2C). Similarly, ERAL1 overexpression following HBV plasmid transfection in Huh7 cells (Fig. S2A) markedly suppressed both HBsAg and HBeAg production (Fig. S2B, S2C). Conversely, shRNA-mediated knockdown of ERAL1 expression in HepG2-NTCP cells prior to HBV infection (Fig. 2D, E) increased the expression of viral markers (Fig. 2F). This pro-viral effect of ERAL1 depletion was validated in a more physiologically relevant system. The knockdown of ERAL1 in primary human hepatocytes (PHHs) (Fig. 2G, H) significantly promoted HBV infection, increasing HBsAg levels by approximately 60% (Fig. 2I). Taken together, these results suggest that ERAL1 exerts cell-intrinsic antiviral activity against HBV replication.

ERAL1 mediates potent and rapid HBV suppression in vivo

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of ERAL1, we employed an immunocompetent murine model of persistent HBV infection. Following the establishment of HBV replication via rAAV8-HBV, the mice received a single intravenous injection of an ERAL1-overexpressing adenovirus (AAV8-ERAL1) for hepatocyte-targeted delivery (Fig. 3A). Hepatic ERAL1 protein expression was significantly elevated at 12 days post-treatment, as evidenced by the Western blot results (Fig. 3B, C). This enforced expression of ERAL1 notably suppressed viral replication. Serum levels of HBsAg, HBeAg, and HBV DNA were significantly reduced during the 12-day observation period (Fig. 3D–F). Notably, ERAL1 overexpression resulted in nearly 60% clearance of serum HBsAg (Fig. 3D) and 70% clearance of serum HBeAg (Fig. 3E) on day 12. Consistent with the serological data, immunohistochemical analysis of liver sections revealed a marked reduction in intrahepatic HBsAg and HBcAg expression, with HBcAg suppression approaching 50% (Fig. 3G, H). In addition, as shown in the supplemental Fig. 3A and 3B, representative H&E‑stained liver sections showed no evidence of hepatocyte necrosis or morphological abnormalities in the ERAL1‑overexpressing group compared with controls. And serum ALT (alanine aminotransferase‌) and AST (aspartate aminotransferase) remained within normal ranges and were not significantly different between ERAL1-overexpressing and control mice. These findings demonstrate that ERAL1 is not merely a disease-associated host factor but also a therapeutically exploitable, cell-intrinsic restriction factor capable of mediating rapid and substantial HBV antigen clearance in vivo without inducing detectable hepatotoxicity.

ERAL1 promotes MAVS aggregation and activates downstream antiviral signaling

Given the established role of MAVS in shaping mitochondrial innate immunity and its reported functional link to ERAL1 during RNA virus infection, we next sought to determine whether ERAL1 similarly engages the MAVS pathway to restrict HBV infection. Immunofluorescence analysis of HepG2.2.15 cells revealed that ERAL1 overexpression strongly promoted the aggregation of MAVS into distinct puncta, which significantly colocalized with ERAL1 (Fig. 4A, B). This physical interaction was corroborated by coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP), in which ERAL1 overexpression markedly increased the amount of MAVS protein recovered from the immunoprecipitate (Fig. 4C).

We next assessed whether ERAL1-mediated MAVS aggregation leads to functional activation of the downstream signaling cascade. Indeed, ERAL1 overexpression significantly increased, including TBK1, IRF3, and IRF7 (Fig. 4D). Consistent with these findings, RT–PCR analysis demonstrated that ERAL1 upregulated the expression of canonical interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), such as radical s-adenosyl methionine domain-containing protein 2 (RSAD2, 2-fold) and interferon-stimulated gene 15 (ISG15, 3-fold) (Fig. 4E). Critically, knockdown of MAVS (Fig. S4A, B) completely eliminated the antiviral effect of ERAL1, as indicated by the reversal of HBsAg, HBeAg, and HBV DNA suppression (Fig. 4F). These data collectively demonstrate a specific and functional interaction between ERAL1 and MAVS (Fig. 4G). In summary, our results establish that ERAL1 exerts its anti-HBV activity by directly engaging and activating the MAVS signaling pathway, leading to the induction of a potent interferon-stimulated antiviral state.

ERAL1 activates the MAVS-dependent MAPK and PI3K-AKT signaling cascades

To identify the signaling networks downstream of the ERAL1-MAVS axis, we performed a transcriptomic analysis of ERAL1-overexpressing HepG2.2.15 cells. RNA sequencing revealed 1,157 differentially expressed genes (738 upregulated and 419 downregulated genes; Fig. 5A, B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant activation of both the MAPK signaling pathway and the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 5C). We next validated these findings at the protein level. ERAL1 overexpression markedly increased the phosphorylation of key kinases in both pathways, including p38 and ERK (MAPK cascade), as well as AKT (PI3K-AKT axis) (Fig. 5D, E). As shown in the supplemental Fig. 5, inhibition of the PI3K-AKT pathway significantly reversed ERAL1‑mediated suppression of HBsAg and HBeAg. These data demonstrate that PI3K-AKT is functionally required for the full antiviral effect of ERAL1. Importantly, genetic ablation of MAVS completely abrogated the ERAL1-mediated activation of these pathways (Fig. 5F), confirming that ERAL1 signals through MAVS to engage the MAPK and PI3K-AKT cascades. Together, these results reveal a MAVS-dependent signaling axis in which ERAL1 activates both the MAPK pathway and the PI3K-AKT pathway to restrict HBV infection.

Discussion

Despite the availability of nucleos(t)ide analogs (NAs) and interferon-based therapies, achieving a functional cure for chronic hepatitis B (CHB) remains a formidable challenge, primarily because of the persistence of the viral cccDNA reservoir and the limited effectiveness in clearing hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) [23, 24]. Therefore, there is a pressing need to identify novel host-directed targets that can potentiate intrinsic antiviral defenses and lead to sustained viral suppression. In this study, we identify the mitochondrial GTPase ERAL1 as a key intrinsic limiting factor against HBV and identify the ERAL1–MAVS–MAPK/PI3K signaling axis as a critical antiviral pathway that is subverted during chronic HBV infection.

Our findings reveal a consistent pattern of ERAL1 suppression across the spectrum of HBV infection. In clinical samples, ERAL1 expression was profoundly reduced in the liver tissues of CHB patients, with a striking decrease of approximately 75% observed during the critical immune-reactive phase. This downregulation was faithfully recapitulated in cellular and animal models, indicating that ERAL1 depletion is a hallmark of the HBV-infected liver microenvironment. Detailed analysis of ERAL1 expression in paired biopsies before and after antiviral therapy, or in well‑defined cohorts controlling for necroinflammatory activity, will be required to disentangle direct viral from indirect inflammatory effects. Functionally, rescuing ERAL1 expression resulted in potent antiviral activity. In murine models of persistent HBV infection, targeted hepatic overexpression of ERAL1 induced rapid and substantial viral clearance, resulting in approximately 60% and 70% reductions in the levels of serum HBsAg and HBeAg, respectively, within 12 days. This efficacy, coupled with an absence of detectable hepatotoxicity, underscores the therapeutic potential of modulating this host pathway.

Mechanistically, we demonstrate that the antiviral function of ERAL1 is executed through direct and functional interactions with the mitochondrial adaptor protein MAVS, a central hub of innate immune signaling [25, 26]. We found that ERAL1 not only colocalizes with MAVS but also actively promotes its aggregation, which is a prerequisite for signalosome assembly and downstream pathway activation. This physical interaction facilitates the robust phosphorylation of key effector kinases, including TBK1, IRF3, and IRF7, leading to the induction of a broad interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) program [27, 28]. Crucially, the entire antiviral effect of ERAL1 was contingent upon MAVS, as genetic ablation of MAVS completely reversed both signaling activation and the suppression of viral replication. ERAL1 thus provides a previously unappreciated layer of control that enhances the intrinsic antiviral tone. Given that HBV is a DNA virus and generally considered a weak inducer of canonical innate immunity, we propose that ERAL1 serves to amplify a low‑level, pre‑existing MAVS signal to a threshold sufficient for effective viral restriction. This mechanism does not exclude parallel pathways such as cGAS‑STING but rather highlights a mitochondria‑centered amplification node that is subverted by HBV. Furthermore, transcriptomic and biochemical analyses revealed that the ERAL1-MAVS axis specifically activated the MAPK (p38, ERK) and PI3K-AKT cascades, two signaling networks intimately linked to cell survival, proliferation, and antiviral transcriptional responses [29–31]. This multipathway engagement suggests that rather than activating a single linear pathway, ERAL1 orchestrates a coordinated cellular defense program.

The discovery of ERAL1 as an HBV restriction factor redefines its role beyond mitochondrial ribosome biogenesis and Perrault syndrome [19, 32, 33]. While prior research has highlighted its cytosolic translocation and immune-enhancing role during RNA virus infection [22], our work establishes a mitochondrion-centric mechanism of ERAL1 against a DNA virus. HBV appears to actively suppress ERAL1, potentially disarming this frontline defense and facilitating persistent infection. This host–pathogen dynamic positions ERAL1 not only as a disease-associated marker but also as a therapeutically exploitable host dependency factor. The notable antigen clearance observed in vivo, particularly the reduction in HBsAg, which is the key biomarker of a functional cure [34, 35], supports strategies to augment ERAL1 function or mimic its activity as a promising complementary approach to current regimens.

Our study has several limitations. The precise structural basis of the ERAL1-MAVS interaction remains to be defined. While we demonstrated effectiveness against a common core promoter mutation, the impact on other clinically relevant HBV variants should be investigated further. Finally, the therapeutic potential of ERAL1-based strategies must be validated in more advanced humanized liver models before clinical translation. While our proof‑of‑concept study employed adenovirus‑mediated ERAL1 overexpression in mice, clinical translation will require clinically feasible strategies. One approach is the development of optimized gene therapy vectors, such as adeno‑associated virus (AAV) serotypes with high hepatocyte tropism (e.g., AAV8 or AAV‑LK03), to safely and durably deliver ERAL1 to the liver. Alternatively, small‑molecule agonists that directly enhance ERAL1 expression, stabilize its protein structure, or potentiate the ERAL1‑MAVS interaction could represent a more conventional drug development path. High‑throughput screening of chemical libraries for compounds that promote MAVS aggregation or ERAL1‑MAVS binding may identify lead candidates. Such strategies would aim to restore or amplify this natural mitochondrial defense pathway without the need for exogenous gene delivery. Future studies should explore these avenues to translate the current findings into viable treatments for chronic hepatitis B. Future research should focus on developing specific pharmacological agents targeting the ERAL1 pathway and exploring rational combinations with NAs or immunomodulators to achieve synergistic antiviral effects and potentially durable HBsAg loss.

In conclusion, we revealed that ERAL1 serves as a guardian of mitochondrial antiviral immunity that is critically disabled during HBV infection. By elucidating the ERAL1-MAVS-MAPK/PI3K signaling axis, this work provides a novel mechanistic framework for understanding host defenses against HBV and unveils a druggable mitochondrial checkpoint. Restoring or enhancing this innate defense pathway represents a compelling new strategy in the pursuit of a functional cure for chronic hepatitis B infection.

Materials and methods

Patient and public involvement statement

This study included a total of 17 participants recruited from the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. These patients all were diagnosed with or not with chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. A summary of the patient characteristics is presented in Supplemental Tables 1, and Table 2.

The study adhered to the principles outlined in the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (Approval No. MRCTA, ECFAH of FMU [2021] 044). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study.

Cell culture and treatment

HepG2, Huh7, HepAD38, HepG2.2.15, HepG2‑NTCP, and primary human hepatocytes (PHHs, Liver Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin‑streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C under 5% CO₂.

HBV infection in HepG2‑NTCP cells was performed as previously described [36]. Briefly, cells were infected with concentrated HBV particles (from HepAD38 supernatant) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100 genome equivalents per cell in medium containing 4% PEG 8000, 2% DMSO and 3% FBS. After 24 h, cells were washed thoroughly with PBS and maintained in fresh medium containing 2% DMSO and 3% FBS.

Transient transfection model: Huh7 or HepG2 cells were transfected with 2 µg of HBV plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Supernatants and cells were harvested 48 h post‑transfection for analysis of HBV markers.

Knockdown experiments: siRNA oligos (final concentration 20 nM) were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000. Cells were harvested 48 h later for further analysis. The siRNA sequence targeting MAVS was 5′‑CCACCUUGAUGCCUGUGAA‑3′ (sense strand).

Lentiviral transduction: Cells were seeded in complete medium at 30–40% confluency and allowed to adhere for 24 h. After a 4 h pre‑incubation in fresh DMEM, cells were transduced with ERAL1‑overexpressing or shERAL1 lentivirus at the indicated multiplicity of infection (MOI) in the presence of polybrene (8 µg/mL). Following 12 h of incubation, the medium was replenished, and cells were cultured for an additional 48 h. Stable populations were selected with puromycin (1–2 µg/mL, depending on cell line) for 7–10 days before subsequent experiments. The lentiviruses were obtained from Genechem Company (Shanghai, China). The shRNA sequence targeting ERAL1 was 5′‑GCCTTACAATGTACAGCAGAA‑3′ (sense strand).

For PI3K-AKT mechanistic studies, HepG2.2.15 cells were additionally treated with LY294002 (AKT inhibitor, 30 µM, MCE, USA).

Protein extraction and western blotting

Cells were lysed on ice for 30 min using RIPA buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain total protein extracts. Proteins were separated by SDS‑PAGE on gels with a 5% stacking layer and either a 10% or 20% resolving layer, then transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5% non‑fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: ERAL1 (1:1000, #29465‑1‑AP, Proteintech, China), MAVS (1:500, sc‑166583, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Germany), IRF3 (1:1000, #11904, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), p‑IRF3 (1:1000, #37829, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), IRF7 (1:500, A0159, Abclonal, China), p‑IRF7 (1:1000, #12390, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), AKT (1:1000, #9272, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), p‑AKT (1:1000, #4060, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), TBK1 (1:1000, #3504, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), p‑TBK1 (1:1000, #5483, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), ERK (1:1000, #4695, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), p‑ERK (1:500, #4370, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), p38 MAPK (1:1000, #8690, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), p‑p38 MAPK (1:500, #4511, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), and GAPDH (1:1000, #92310, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) as a loading control. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP‑conjugated secondary antibodies (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Amersham Pharmacia Biosciences) and detected with a chemiluminescence imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, and relative protein levels were normalized to GAPDH.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Briefly, HepG2.2.15 cells overexpressing ERAL1 or empty vector were lysed in ice-cold buffer containing 0.025 M Tris (pH 7.4), 0.15 M NaCl, 0.001 M EDTA, 1% NP-40, and 5% glycerol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and incubated on ice for 30 min. Cell lysates were then immunoprecipitated with anti-ERAL1 antibody-conjugated beads overnight at 4 °C. After incubation, the beads were collected and washed three times with wash buffer. The immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted and separated by SDS–PAGE using 5% stacking and 4%–20% gradient resolving gels (Beyotime, China). Protein bands were subsequently detected by immunoblotting with corresponding antibodies.

RNA extraction and RT‒PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells using a commercial RNA extraction kit (Accurate Biology, China), and RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). One microgram of RNA was reverse‑transcribed into cDNA with a cDNA synthesis kit (Accurate Biology, China). qRT‑PCR was performed with SYBR Green master mix (Accurate Biology, China) on a 7500 Fast Real‑Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Melting‑curve analysis was used to confirm amplification specificity. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method. Immunocytochemistry

HepG2.2.15 or HepG2 cells were seeded at 1 × 10^⁴ cells per well on Matrigel‑coated coverslips in 24‑well plates and cultured for 24 h. For mitochondrial visualization, cells were stained with MitoTracker Deep Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X‑100 for 10 min, and blocked for 1.5 h at room temperature with a solution containing 20% goat serum and 5% bovine serum albumin. All steps were followed by three washes with PBS. Primary antibodies against ERAL1 (1:200, #29465‑1‑AP, Proteintech, China) and MAVS (1:200, sc‑166583, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Germany) were applied overnight at 4 °C. The next day, cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with goat anti‑mouse IgG‑Alexa Fluor 488 or 594 (1:500) and goat anti‑rabbit IgG‑Alexa Fluor 488 or 594 (1:500). Coverslips were mounted using DAPI‑containing fluorescent mounting medium and imaged with a confocal fluorescence microscope (LSM510 or FV1000, Zeiss/Olympus). Quantification was performed using ImageJ software.

RNA sequencing

For RNA sequencing, HepG2.2.15 cells were transfected with either an ERAL1‑overexpressing lentivirus or a control virus. Following successful overexpression, total RNA was extracted from both groups using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, USA) and subjected to transcriptomic profiling on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform.

HBV-infected mouse model and treatment

Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were housed under specific pathogen‑free conditions in a temperature‑ and airflow‑controlled environment. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Experiment Center of Fujian Medical University (IACUC FJMU 2023‑0312). To establish HBV persistence, mice received an intravenous injection of recombinant AAV8 carrying a 1.3‑mer wild‑type HBV genome with 1 × 10^11 viral genomes in 200 µl. Six weeks post‑injection, serum was collected from the mandibular vein and assayed for HBV markers to confirm successful infection. Confirmed HBV‑carrying mice were then randomly assigned to two groups and injected intravenously with either ERAL1‑overexpressing AAV8 or control AAV8. Serum and liver were harvested and subsequently monitored to evaluate treatment effects.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

For immunohistochemical staining of ERAL1, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded liver sections were dried at 60 °C for 30 min, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 85%, and 60%). After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, sections were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in DPBS for 10 min and blocked for 1 h with DPBS containing 20% goat serum and 20% bovine serum albumin (Sigma, USA). Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-ERAL1 antibody (1:100, #29465-1-AP, Proteintech, China). Detection was performed using a Mouse/Rabbit Enhanced Polymer Detection System (ZSGB-Bio, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Images were acquired using a bright-field microscope. Routine H&E staining was used to assess overall liver morphology and the extent of mononuclear cell infiltration.

For immunofluorescent detection of HBcAg and HBsAg, liver sections were similarly processed. After blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a mixture of mouse anti-HBcAg (1:500, ab8637, Abcam, UK) and rabbit anti-HBsAg (1:500, NB100-62652, Novus Biologicals, USA). Primary antibodies were detected using goat anti-mouse IgG-Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, USA) and goat anti-rabbit IgG-Alexa Fluor 594 (Invitrogen, USA). Coverslips were mounted with DAPI-containing fluorescent mounting medium and visualized by confocal fluorescence microscopy (LSM510 or FV1000). Quantification and colocalization analyses were performed using ImageJ and Image-Pro Plus software, respectively.

Clinical biochemistry

Serum levels of HBsAg and HBeAg were measured using an automated chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay system (Abbott I2000, USA). HBV genomic DNA was quantified by quantitative real‑time PCR on a Roche Light Cycler 480 system (Roche, Switzerland) using commercial kits (Sansure Biotech Inc., China). The levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were quantified using an ADVIA 2400 automatic biochemical analyzer (Siemens, Germany).

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as means ± SEMs. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using SigmaStat software (Systat Software Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Parametric or non‑parametric tests were applied as appropriate and are detailed in the respective figure legends. Sample sizes were determined a priori using G*Power 3.1.7 software to ensure adequate statistical power. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05. For animal experiments, age‑matched mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups.
