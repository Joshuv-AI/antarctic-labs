Abstract
The Burrowing Owl Survey Report for Sandia National Laboratories: 2025 provides data and
analysis on occupancy surveys that were conducted during the 2025 survey season. The surveys were
conducted in partnership with Kirtland Air Force Base’s Natural Resource Program and the United
States Fish and Wildlife Service to maintain and expand on the long-term dataset on burrowing owls
on base. Ecology Program biologists conducted roadside census surveys at survey areas previously
identified by Kirtland Air Force Base along with several additional survey areas deemed suitable for
occupancy. Remote sensor camera monitoring was also conducted on three nest burrows. In total,
12 breeding pairs of burrowing owls were confirmed in 2025. Several owls were confirmed to have
leg bands, indicating previous captures and recurrence of these specific individuals on base.
Occupancy surveys on burrowing owls continue to provide valuable information on ecosystem
health and can serve to inform future management direction and decisions.

1.0 Background
The Sikes Act of 1960 directs the Secretary of Defense to develop and implement programs that
emphasize the conservation of natural resources on military installations in cooperation with the
U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) and state fish and wildlife agencies (USFWS n.d.).
Title XXIX, Sikes Improvement Act of 1997, calls for the development and implementation of an
Integrated Natural Resources Management Plan to guide conservation actions and the management
of natural resources on military installations throughout the United States. With this, annual reviews
and reports relevant to the implemented programs and associated conservation efforts are a
requirement (Sikes Act Improvement Act 1997; USFWS n.d.). As such, Kirtland Air Force Base
(KAFB) has prioritized several species for ongoing or long-term monitoring and conservation,
which consistently includes the western burrowing owl (Athene cunicularia hypugaea, hereafter referred
to as burrowing owl). Burrowing owl occupancy surveys have been ongoing on KAFB since at least
1998, allowing for a more comprehensive understanding of long-term trends in population
distribution and abundance, overall habitat use, and productivity (Lundblad et al. 2021; McMurry
and Smith 2023). Continued efforts to monitor burrowing owls on base contribute to the long-term
dataset and can be used to inform future conservation efforts and land management decisions.

In 2025, Sandia National Laboratories (Sandia) Ecology Program biologists at Sandia New Mexico
(SNL/NM) partnered with KAFB Natural Resource Program personnel, USFWS biologists, and
New Mexico National Guard personnel to survey and monitor burrowing owls on KAFB and
adjacent lands that are permitted to SNL/NM. The Ecology Program biologists monitor a variety of
wildlife species on base and on SNL/NM permitted lands annually. However, biologists had not
formerly established surveys focusing on the distribution and abundance of burrowing owls, nor had
they conducted regular surveys on burrowing owls until 2024. Prior to 2024, observations of this
species had been recorded opportunistically while conducting other surveillance and monitoring
efforts or while performing other compliance-related work activities. The Ecology Program
biologists understand the importance of the KAFB long-term dataset on burrowing owls and the
overall importance of monitoring this species on KAFB and SNL/NM permitted lands. Ecology
Program biologists realize the ongoing opportunity to contribute to the dataset through survey
support and potential identification of additional suitable habitat. Coordinated monitoring efforts
conducted by KAFB personnel and the Ecology Program biologists streamline efforts, improve
survey efficiency, and help minimize or prevent data gaps, highlighting the value and importance of
partnership and collaboration between the two organizations.

2.0 Survey Methods

2.1. Site Occupancy
In 2025, similarly to 2024, Sandia Ecology Program biologists used survey methods that were
adapted from Evaluation and Management of the Natural Resources and Environmental Services at Kirtland
AFB: 2023 Annual Report and standard avian sampling methodology (McMurry and Smith 2023;
Rosenstock 2002). Personnel performed occupancy surveys on a subset of the sampling locations
that had been identified and prioritized for survey previously. These identified areas were surveyed
annually from 2016 to 2023. In addition, Ecology Program biologists included other suitable KAFB
lands and lands permitted to SNL/NM for survey in 2025.

During the 2023 surveys, Oklahoma State University technicians used two survey methods to detect
burrowing owls on KAFB (i.e., walking census surveys and broadcast call surveys). Remote sensor
camera monitoring was also conducted at each of the burrows to assist with nesting success
determinations. Ecology Program biologists used a single survey method (i.e., roadside census
surveys), as well as remote sensor camera monitoring at three separate nest burrows, for
detecting/monitoring burrowing owls in 2025. Surveys were conducted within the previously
identified areas and in three additional areas that were suspected to provide suitable habitat (i.e., the
Landfill, 9940, and the Solar Tower [Figure 2-1]). Census areas were surveyed by driving along
roadside transects and then stopping to scan for burrowing owls visually and auditorily.
Where feasible, roads were driven around the entire perimeter of the census areas. Where perimeter
roads did not exist, surveys were conducted along all available roadsides. Surveys were conducted
from March 26, 2025, through September 22, 2025. During that period, surveys were planned to be
conducted at least once every 2 weeks. Routes driven remained consistent throughout the survey
period, with the exception that some areas were removed early on due to a lack of prairie dog
activity, lack of suitable habitat, or both. Surveys intended to locate and confirm the numbers of
dispersed fledglings and adults were able to be completed in 2025, which was contrary to 2024 when
there were scheduling conflicts and work priority shifts.

3.0 Results
Once a single burrowing owl or a pair was detected, the owls were observed to determine the
location of potentially occupied nest burrows. Personnel made behavioral observations and noted
physical signs of occupancy (e.g., pellets, whitewash, feathers, feces, trash and litter, or other nest
lining materials) at the nearest burrow entrance and other nearby burrow entrances. These indicators
were used to determine nesting status and nest burrow location. Nest burrows were monitored
throughout the nesting season through fledgling dispersal and fall migration, which may start in
August and continue through October. The status of each of the burrows was determined at the end
of the 2025 survey period.

The burrows of nesting pairs were monitored throughout the nesting season using direct
observations, and the following data were recorded: survey date, surveyors, start time,
temperature (°F), cloud cover (percent), wind speed (miles per hour), coordinates (latitude and
longitude), burrow name, observation type (burrow, owl, or both), group type (single, pair, multiple,
none), age (adult, juvenile, mix, or not applicable), whether or not individuals had bands (yes, no, or
unknown), band descriptions (right leg, left leg, or a combination), final nesting status (abandoned,
predated, successful, or unconfirmed), the number of young fledged, and any additional notes.

4.0 Discussion
Long-term surveys and monitoring of the burrowing owl population on KAFB have been ongoing
since at least 1998. While KAFB personnel and KAFB contractors have conducted previous efforts
to survey and monitor burrowing owls, this was the second year that Sandia Ecology Program
biologists assisted with the survey and monitoring efforts. Consistent with the 2024 surveys, some
methods were excluded from the 2025 surveys that had formerly been included in survey and
monitoring methods conducted by KAFB and their contractors (e.g., call-back surveys, fledgling
capture and banding, and prairie dog counts), while other methods were adjusted in an effort to
improve efficiency and detection success (e.g., the walking census was changed to a driving roadside
transect survey) and others were reduced in scope due to various constraints and limitations
(i.e., remote sensor camera monitoring). Total fledgling counts and dispersal were performed and
determined for all nest burrows during the 2025 survey period, which allowed for improvement in
overall data collection and analysis.

With that, 12 breeding pairs of burrowing owls were found in 2025. This is lower than the number
of breeding pairs found in the previous 3 years, where 15 breeding pairs were found in 2024,
23 breeding pairs were found in 2023, and 13 breeding pairs were found in 2022 (Hendricks 2024,
McMurry and Smith 2023; Conway and Locatelli 2021). However, nest success was purported to be
higher in 2025 than in 2024, with 16 documented fledglings in 2024 versus 34 documented
fledglings in 2025. With this, however, there were seven nests in which the final status was not
confirmed in 2024. According to the surveys conducted in 2025, nearly all owls had dispersed from
KAFB by the month of October.

Sandia Ecology Program biologists surveyed all previously identified census areas, in addition to
three other locations where Ecology Program biologists and various partners observed prairie dogs.
Several areas were monitored less frequently during the 2025 surveys due to the absence of prairie
dogs and, consequently, suitable burrows and habitat for burrowing owls. While there were no
burrowing owl observations at some previously occupied areas during the 2025 survey, other areas
included in this year’s survey provided suitable habitat, and numerous burrowing owls were detected
at those locations, including the Gibson area, the landfill, and areas south of the Solar Tower.
Annual burrowing owl population fluctuations, occupancy, and the expansion and contraction of
prairie dog colonies on KAFB are likely influenced by ongoing land management decisions, which
ultimately influences the distribution and abundance of burrowing owls from year to year.

Given the number of owls that have been observed with bands (both in 2024 and 2025), it may be
worthwhile to review previous reports to identify when these owls may have been captured and
released and to maintain a solid record of newly banded owls. Such reports and records help to
provide data and information on the current age of these owls, conditions of their capture and
release, former health and body conditions, previous nesting location information, and other
potentially useful information related to their initial capture and release.

A multitude of interactions with other wildlife were documented at nest burrows that were
monitored with remote sensor cameras in 2025. A confirmed nest predation event was documented
at one of the Solar Tower burrows, in which an American badger dug out the burrow. Based on
photo and video documentation, both adults were able to survive the encounter. The status of the
nest prior to the encounter was not officially confirmed, although it is presumed that the adult
female was incubating eggs at the time based on previously observed behaviors and overall timing.

 19

The nest was eventually abandoned due to the predation event. Other types of documented
encounters included burrow visits by black-tailed jackrabbits, coyotes, and prairie dogs. Behaviors
ranged from digging, passing by, sitting nearby, and showing interest in the burrow or nearby owls.
Use of remote sensor cameras can be a powerful tool in documenting behaviors and interactions
between and among burrowing owls, as well as the behaviors and interactions between and among
burrowing owls and other wildlife and ecological resources. Such monitoring efforts allow for
flexibility in the type of data collected and allows for continuous monitoring. When feasible, remote
sensor camera monitoring should be part of the ongoing monitoring methods for burrowing owls
on KAFB.
