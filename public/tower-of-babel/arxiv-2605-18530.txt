Continuous Diffusion Scales Competitively with Discrete Diffusion for Language
Zhihan Yang, Wei Guo, Shuibai Zhang, Subham Sekhar Sahoo, Yongxin Chen, Arash Vahdat, Morteza Mardani, John Thickstun

Abstract
While diffusion has drawn considerable recent attention from the language modeling community, continuous diffusion has appeared less scalable than discrete approaches. To challenge this belief we revisit Plaid, a likelihood-based continuous diffusion language model (DLM), and construct RePlaid by aligning the architecture of Plaid with modern discrete DLMs. In this unified setting, we establish the first scaling law for continuous DLMs that rivals discrete DLMs: RePlaid exhibits a compute gap of only 20 × 20\times compared to autoregressive models, outperforms Duo while using fewer parameters, and outperforms MDLM in the over-trained regime. We benchmark RePlaid against recent continuous DLMs: on OpenWebText, RePlaid achieves a new state-of-the-art PPL bound of 22.1 22.1 among continuous DLMs and superior generation quality. These results suggest that continuous diffusion, when trained via likelihood, is a highly competitive and scalable alternative to discrete DLMs. Moreover, we offer theoretical insights to understand the advantage of likelihood-based training. We show that optimizing the noise schedule to minimize the ELBO’s variance naturally yields linear cross-entropy (information loss) over time. This evenly distributes denoising difficulty without any case-specific time reparameterization. In addition, we find that optimizing embeddings via likelihood creates structured geometries and drives the most significant likelihood gain.



1
Introduction

Discrete diffusion language models (DLMs) show significant promise, increasingly rivaling the performance and scalability of autoregressive (AR) models across various benchmarks
[
36
,
60
]
. Despite these successes, continuous diffusion remains a compelling alternative due to its inherent potential for controllability and acceleration. The smooth geometry of a continuous latent space enables structured editing
[
35
,
11
]

and allows for the application of efficient ODE-based solvers
[
71
,
32
]
, facilitating distillation of complex sampling trajectories into high-fidelity, single-step generators
[
52
,
58
]
. Unlike discrete diffusion, which often suffers from severe quality degradation under limited sampling budgets, continuous diffusion offers a principled path toward efficient text generation by enabling superior trade-offs between computational cost and sample quality
[
27
,
26
]
.

While continuous DLMs present unique advantages, previous work finds a significant training scalability gap compared to discrete DLMs. Previous studies show empirical evidence that continuous diffusion underperforms discrete diffusion and AR models when evaluated at equivalent training FLOPs and parameter counts
[
2
,
17
]
; the original Plaid study reported a significant
64\times
compute overhead compared to autoregressive models to achieve matching likelihood
[
17
]
. This figure is frequently cited as evidence of the poor scalability of continuous DLMs. By revisiting this principled likelihood-based paradigm, we aim to determine whether this performance gap is an inherent limitation of continuous diffusion or a consequence of incompatible scaling analyses.

To address this question, we introduce
RePlaid
, a revised version of Plaid featuring a transformer architecture carefully aligned with modern discrete DLM practices. While the original
64\times
gap suggested continuous diffusion was impractical at scale, those findings were obtained under a different setup from the protocols used to establish current discrete diffusion scaling laws. By adopting the exact protocol from
50
—including the same dataset, optimization hyperparameters, and compute budget—we perform the first unified scaling comparison between continuous and discrete DLMs. Crucially, in this controlled environment, the performance gap narrows to
20\times
for RePlaid versus AR, placing continuous diffusion on par with discrete diffusion and challenging the narrative of its inherent unscalability.

Furthermore, we find that RePlaid achieves consistent improvements in generation quality over other recent continuous models like FLM
[
28
]
and LangFlow
[
9
]
. In contrast to other recent work, RePlaid’s training objective (like Plaid) is explicitly derived from a
likelihood bound (ELBO)
, providing a theoretically grounded framework for training that heuristic cross-entropy or flow-matching methods lack. We attribute the success of RePlaid to two primary factors:
(i)
optimizing the noise schedule via ELBO variance naturally recovers a near-linear cross-entropy schedule that evenly distributes denoising difficulty without heuristic reparameterization, and
(ii)
ELBO-based embedding optimization inherently regularizes the latent geometry to improve likelihood, creating a structured, low-entropy space that prevents potential token dispersion in cross-entropy-based training.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

RePlaid & Benchmarking:
We introduce RePlaid, a modernized likelihood-based DLM achieving a state-of-the-art
22.1
perplexity among existing continuous DLMs on OpenWebText.

•

Unified Scaling Analysis:
We present the first unified scaling law comparison between continuous and discrete DLMs, showing that continuous models scale on par with discrete counterparts when properly aligned.

•

Theoretical Insights:
We show that the ELBO objective:
(i)
naturally recovers a linear cross-entropy noise schedule, eliminating heuristic time reparameterizations, and
(ii)
inherently regularizes embedding geometry for likelihood improvement and prevents token dispersion.

Background: Plaid—A Likelihood-Based Continuous DLM

Plaid

[
17
]
is a Variational Diffusion Model (VDM)
[
27
]
for text.
Let
[V]:=\{1,\ldots,V\}
be the set of vocabulary with size
V
. A length-
L
sequence
{\mathbf{x}}\in[V]^{L}
is identified with a matrix in
\{0,1\}^{L\times V}
whose
l
-th row
{\mathbf{x}}^{l}
is the one-hot vector of the
l
-th token. Plaid defines the embedded sequence
{\mathbf{e}}:={\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{e}}
, where
{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d_{e}}
is a learnable token-embedding matrix with each row constrained to unit Euclidean length; the
l
-th row of
{\mathbf{e}}
,
{\mathbf{e}}^{l}
, is the embedding of the non-zero index of
{\mathbf{x}}^{l}
. Unless otherwise stated, we use low-dimensional embeddings (
d_{e}=16
), as in Plaid.

Forward and reverse processes.

The forward process
q
is Gaussian noising on embedding
{\mathbf{e}}
:

\displaystyle q({\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid{\mathbf{x}})=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}{\mathbf{e}},\sigma_{t}^{2}{\mathbf{I}}),\quad t\in[0,1],

(1)

where
\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t}>0
are smooth scalar functions of
t
. VDMs assume a variance-preserving (VP) process,
\alpha_{t}^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}=1
, yielding a strictly decreasing signal-to-noise ratio
{\mathrm{SNR}}(t):=\alpha_{t}^{2}/\sigma_{t}^{2}
. The true reverse posterior
q({\mathbf{z}}_{s}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t},{\mathbf{x}})
for
s<t
is then

\displaystyle q({\mathbf{z}}_{s}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t},{\mathbf{x}})=\mathcal{N}\big((\alpha_{t|s}\sigma^{2}_{s}/\sigma_{t}^{2}){\mathbf{z}}_{t}+(\alpha_{s}\sigma^{2}_{t|s}/\sigma^{2}_{t}){\mathbf{e}},(\sigma^{2}_{t|s}\sigma^{2}_{s}/\sigma^{2}_{t}){\mathbf{I}}\big),

(2)

where
\alpha_{t|s}:=\alpha_{t}/\alpha_{s}
and
\sigma^{2}_{t|s}:=\sigma_{t}^{2}-\alpha_{t|s}^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}
. See
[
27
, App. A.2]
for the proof.

Training.

Let
{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t):\mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{e}}\times[0,1]\rightarrow(\Delta^{V})^{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{L\times V}
denote a time-conditioned denoising model outputting a categorical distribution over the vocabulary for every position (i.e., each row is a probability vector on the simplex
\Delta^{V}
). Before softmax, Plaid augments the final logit head output of
{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}
with output prior logits, a closed-form Gaussian log-density that helps focus the denoiser’s prediction to plausible tokens given
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
. Plaid parameterizes the reverse process by plugging the denoiser’s prediction into
Eq.

2
:

\displaystyle p_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{s}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=q({\mathbf{z}}_{s}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t},{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t)).

(3)

With prior
p({\mathbf{z}}_{1})=\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{I}})
, this yields the Negative Evidence Lower Bound (NELBO) containing respectively
prior loss
,
reconstruction loss
, and
diffusion loss
(see
Appendix

C
for the derivation):

\displaystyle-\log p_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NELBO}}({\mathbf{x}}):=

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(q({\mathbf{z}}_{1}\mid{\mathbf{x}})\,\|\,p({\mathbf{z}}_{1}))+\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{z}}_{0}\sim q({\mathbf{z}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\big[{\textstyle\sum}_{l=1}^{L}-\log\langle{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}^{l}({\mathbf{z}}_{0},0),{\mathbf{x}}^{l}\rangle\big]

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}\ \mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1],{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\sim q({\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}[{\mathrm{SNR}}^{\prime}(t)\|{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t)-{\mathbf{e}}\|^{2}],

(4)

where
{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t):={\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t){\mathbf{E}}
is the predicted clean embedding, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product. During training, Plaid adaptively allocates a fraction of each batch to the reconstruction loss (Monte Carlo over
{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
) and the remainder to the diffusion loss (Monte Carlo over
t
and
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
); the prior loss uses the whole batch. See
Appendix

B
for the training algorithm.

Learnable noise schedule.

Plaid inherits the VDM parameterization
[
27
]
of the noise schedule
\gamma(t)=\gamma_{0}+(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0})\tilde{\gamma}(t)
, with learnable scalar
endpoints

\gamma_{0}<\gamma_{1}
and
interior shape

\tilde{\gamma}:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
modeled by a monotonically increasing neural net. This gives
\alpha^{2}_{t}=\operatorname{sigmoid}(-\gamma(t))
,
\sigma^{2}_{t}=\operatorname{sigmoid}(\gamma(t))
, and
{\mathrm{SNR}}(t)={\mathrm{e}}^{-\gamma(t)}
. The endpoints and interior shape are trained to minimize the diffusion loss and its Monte-Carlo variance respectively (
Fig.

8
for pseudo-code). As with VDMs,
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
are always learned. Hence we use the term
noise schedule
to refer to the interior shape
\tilde{\gamma}(t)
.

Beyond VDMs.

On top of VDMs, Plaid adds two features besides the categorical reparameterization
{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta}={\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}{\mathbf{E}}
. First, it uses a learnable encoding via the embedding matrix
{\mathbf{E}}
. Second, it adds self-conditioning
[
8
]
: during training, for
25\%
of each batch the denoiser performs an initial gradient-free forward pass to estimate the clean data, which is fed back as an additional conditioning input for the actual prediction; at validation and sampling time, self-conditioning is enabled for all examples.

Cheaper embedding operations.

Although largely overlooked in the current literature, Plaid offers substantially cheaper embedding operations compared to DLMs that inject Gaussian noise into high-dimensional (e.g., one-hot
[
28
,
47
,
44
]
) embeddings. For example, projecting
V
-dimensional one-hot embeddings to the transformer hidden size
h
requires an
FP32
matrix multiplication of
[L,V]\times[V,h]
. Plaid reduces this to
[L,d_{e}]\times[d_{e},h]
plus categorical reparameterization
[L,V]\times[V,d_{e}]
at the output, which requires
\approx 50\times
fewer FLOPs when
V\approx 50
K (e.g., GPT-2
[
46
]
),
h=768
, and
d_{e}=16
.

3
Revisiting Scaling Laws of Continuous DLMs

In this section, we introduce RePlaid: a revised version of Plaid using a transformer architecture that is carefully aligned with modern discrete DLMs. This alignment allows us to benchmark RePlaid against discrete DLMs using the scaling law protocol from
50
. We find that RePlaid’s scaling is on par with discrete diffusion: to achieve the same validation loss as AR, RePlaid requires
20\times
compute with self-conditioning, and
27\times
compute without it. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first unified scaling comparison between continuous and discrete DLMs.

3.1
RePlaid: Aligning Plaid with Modern Discrete DLM Architecture

In
50
, MDLM and Duo employ a Diffusion Transformer (DiT)
[
42
]
equipped with bidirectional attention, RoPE
[
62
]
, and AdaLN-Zero
[
42
]
. The autoregressive (AR) baseline utilizes an identical architecture, differing only in its use of causal attention. For RePlaid, we retain all foundational algorithmic components of Plaid (
Section

2
) while fully aligning its transformer architecture to match the one used by MDLM and Duo. Specifically, this alignment incorporates LayerNorm, MLP biases, GELU(tanh) activations, and AdaLN-Zero modulation with learnable gating for residual connections. In addition, the original Plaid computes its final logit head in
FP32
due to its interaction with output prior logits (
Section

2
). We remove this substantial numerical confounder for fair comparison. See
Appendix

J
for a detailed pre- and post-alignment comparison on architecture and numerical precision.

3.2
A Unified Scaling Benchmark for Continuous and Discrete DLMs

Data.

We utilize the large-scale, deduplicated SlimPajama dataset
[
56
]
, which provides a sufficiently rich distribution to avoid data repetition during training. We use the Llama-2 tokenizer
[
63
]
(
V=32{,}001
with a special mask token), a batch size of
256
, and a sequence length of
2048
tokens.

Optimization.

AR, MDLM, and Duo use AdamW with a cosine learning rate schedule decaying from
2{\mathrm{e}}{-}4
to
2{\mathrm{e}}{-}5
, alongside
\beta_{1}{=}0.9
and
\beta_{2}{=}0.95
and
1\%
learning rate warmup (minimum
100
steps). Parameters are regularized with a weight decay of
0.1
without dropout. RePlaid applies this identical configuration, with minor adjustments to accommodate its learnable noise schedule and its length-normalized, low-dimensional (
d_{e}=16
) embeddings (
Table

7
), following Plaid. For reference, optimizing the
231
M MDLM for
1{\mathrm{e}}20
FLOPs requires about
32
hours on
8
NVIDIA GB200s.

Counting FLOPs.

We measure non-embedding FLOPs using
calflops

[
68
]
. Self-conditioning of
25\%
increases the non-embedding FLOPs by
\approx 8.3\%
, which we offset by reducing training steps.

5
Theoretical Insights

Having shown that RePlaid attains the best PPL bound and sample quality among continuous DLMs on OpenWebText, we now ask why, given that its training objective is a plain VDM NELBO with neither cross-entropy (CE) nor hand-engineered time reparameterization
[
14
,
28
,
44
,
47
,
9
]
. We argue that the answer is in the
NELBO
itself: it is a true upper bound on the negative log-likelihood while the alternatives are not, and thus the rest of this section traces two consequences of optimizing this bound.
(i)
Adding an extra CE term to the NELBO provides a
separative
force on the learned embeddings
{\mathbf{E}}
, which disrupts the low-rank embedding structure and hurts the PPL;
(ii)
Making the noise schedule a learnable part of the NELBO and training it to minimize Monte-Carlo variance automatically yields a
constant per-timestep diffusion loss
and a
near-linear per-timestep CE
—the time-uniformity property recent CE-based methods engineer with manually-designed time reparameterization.

5.1
ELBO versus Cross-Entropy Training

Recent embedding-based continuous DLMs such as CDCD
[
14
]
, FLM
[
28
]
, and LangFlow
[
9
]
adopt a CE loss instead of the MSE-style diffusion loss
Eq.

4
. This discrepancy brings a natural question: can RePlaid be trained using only CE loss? We found that this requires non-trivial modifications (e.g., LangFlow
[
9
]
is an attempt to achieve exactly this), as all components of RePlaid are jointly optimized for likelihood. To conduct a meaningful, isolated comparison where no component becomes degenerate under a standalone CE loss, we compare RePlaid (s.c.) trained with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NELBO}}({\mathbf{x}})
with RePlaid (s.c.) trained with a combined loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NELBO}}({\mathbf{x}})+w\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{CE}}({\mathbf{x}})
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{CE}}({\mathbf{x}})=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1],{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\sim q({\mathbf{z}}_{t}|{\mathbf{x}})}\left[{\textstyle\sum}_{l=1}^{L}-\log\langle{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}^{l}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t),{\mathbf{x}}^{l}\rangle\right]

(6)

and
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{CE}}
is constrained to only reach
{\mathbf{E}}
and
\theta
so noise schedule components stay non-degenerate.

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NELBO}}({\mathbf{x}})

5.2
Benefits of Learning the Noise Schedule

Prior work on continuous DLMs stresses
distributing denoising difficulty uniformly
across time, via manually-designed schedules that linearize token identifiability or the decoding error rate in time
[
45
,
28
,
44
]
, or match the noise density to the information-gain (measured by cross entropy) rate
[
9
]
. We show below that minimizing the variance of the ELBO loss recovers the same property automatically for
any

{\mathbf{E}}
: the optimal noise schedule makes the per-timestep diffusion loss constant in
t
(
Proposition

1
) and, under a Bayes-optimal denoiser, the per-timestep cross-entropy decomposes into a linear-in-
t
term and a residual term (
Proposition

2
).

For some given embeddings
{\mathbf{E}}
, a denoising model
{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}
, and a noise schedule
\gamma
with corresponding
{\mathrm{SNR}}(t)={\mathrm{e}}^{-\gamma(t)}
, the model’s expected diffusion loss at timestep
t
is

\displaystyle\ell_{\theta,\gamma}(t):=-\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathrm{SNR}}^{\prime}(t)\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathrm{data}}({\mathbf{x}})q({\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\|{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t)-{\mathbf{e}}\|^{2}=:-\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathrm{SNR}}^{\prime}(t)\operatorname{MSE}_{\theta}({\mathrm{SNR}}(t)).

(7)

27
showed that the diffusion loss
\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]}[\ell_{\theta,\gamma}(t)]
depends on
\theta
and
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
, but is invariant to the interior shape of
\gamma(t)
; for fixed
\theta
,
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
,
\operatorname{Var}_{t}[\ell_{\theta,\gamma}(t)]
depends only on the shape of
\gamma(t)
. We advance this by proving
Proposition

1
&
Lemma

3
, which holds for VDMs and (Re)Plaid.

Proposition 1

(
Constant Per-Timestep Diffusion Loss
)
.

Let
\kappa:=\mathbb{E}_{t}[\ell_{\theta,\gamma}(t)]
. Under the endpoint constraint
\gamma(0)=\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma(1)=\gamma_{1}
, there exists a unique noise schedule
\gamma^{*}
such that
\ell_{\theta,\gamma^{*}}(t)\equiv\kappa\geq 0
for all
t
, and consequently
\operatorname{Var}_{t}[\ell_{\theta,\gamma^{*}}(t)]=0
.

See
Appendix

F
for the proof, and see
Eqs.

31
and
32
for the closed-form formulas for
\gamma^{*}
and
\kappa
, respectively. We show that the learned noise schedule closely matches the theoretical closed-form solution computed for a RePlaid checkpoint obtained mid-training (
Fig.

6
). High
\alpha(t)
across a large range of
t
indicates that more denoising effort is spent near clean data. Replacing the learned schedule with the closed-form schedule leads to a perfectly flat per-timestep diffusion loss by construction (
Fig.

6
).

But the diffusion loss is a weighted MSE loss and does not reflect denoising difficulty. Thus we follow prior work
[
9
]
and measure the (expected) per-timestep CE loss for practical
one-shot decoding
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{CE}_{\theta,\gamma}(t)\!:=\!\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathrm{data}}({\mathbf{x}})\,q({\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}[-\log\hat{p}_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})]\!=\!\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathrm{data}}({\mathbf{x}})\,q({\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\big[{\textstyle\sum}_{l=1}^{L}\!-\!\log\langle{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}^{l}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t),{\mathbf{x}}^{l}\rangle\big].

(8)

While any model can achieve constant per-timestep diffusion loss, per-timestep CE loss cannot be analyzed for an arbitrary model, as one could imagine an adversary that is only bad at denoising at some
t
. For analyses below, we assume a Bayes-optimal denoiser, which we approach in training.

Combining

.

Assuming no embedding collapse, we have

6
Conclusion and Discussion

We present the first unified scaling law comparison between continuous and discrete DLMs, showing that continuous models scale on par with discrete counterparts when properly aligned in architecture and experimental setting. We introduce RePlaid, a modernized likelihood-based continuous DLM achieving a state-of-the-art
22.1
perplexity among continuous DLMs on OpenWebText and even rivaling discrete DLMs in terms of both likelihood and sample quality. We show that the ELBO objective:
(i)
naturally recovers near-linear cross-entropy over time, eliminating heuristic time reparameterizations, and
(ii)
inherently regularizes embedding geometry for likelihood improvement and prevents potential token dispersion in cross-entropy-based training.

While our scaling law study provides a rigorous comparison following prior work on scaling discrete DLMs
[
38
,
50
]
, we acknowledge that a
20\times
compute gap remains between RePlaid and AR models to achieve matching likelihood. Investigating whether these scaling trends persist in the multi-billion-parameter regime where modern LLMs operate is a critical next step. Furthermore, our analysis identifies learnable token embeddings as the primary driver of RePlaid’s perplexity gains; extending this framework with richer contextual encoders
[
37
]
that condition on the full sequence represents a promising path forward. Finally, the continuous nature of RePlaid opens unique avenues for efficiency research unavailable to discrete diffusion, most notably the distillation of sampling trajectories via the PFODE and the exploration of latent-space inference-time scaling.

Appendix A
Related Works

This appendix surveys diffusion-based approaches to language generation. We do not attempt to review the dominant autoregressive paradigm
[
65
,
46
]
, which serves as the reference baseline in our scaling-law comparisons (
Section

3
). Diffusion-based language models split into two families:
discrete
diffusion, which defines a Markov forward process directly on tokens, and
continuous
diffusion, which lifts tokens into a continuous space and runs a Gaussian (or Riemannian) diffusion there.

A.1
Discrete diffusion language models

Discrete diffusion was introduced by D3PM
[
2
]
, which defined absorbing-state and uniform-noise transition kernels over token sequences and was lifted to continuous-time Markov chains shortly after
[
5
]
. Subsequent work refined the parameterization: SEDD
[
30
]
and concrete score matching
[
34
]
replaced the categorical denoising objective with a score-matching surrogate, while MDLM
[
48
]
, MD4
[
55
]
and RADD
[
41
]
concurrently and independently identified that the absorbing-state model collapses to a clean per-position cross-entropy objective once the mask token is treated correctly. Block Diffusion
[
1
]
and Eso-LMs
[
51
]
interpolate between masked diffusion and autoregressive decoding to obtain unified families. In parallel, discrete flow matching
[
15
]
and generative flows on discrete state spaces
[
6
]
cast the discrete forward process in a flow-matching framework, and LLaDA
[
39
]
and Dream
[
70
]
scale masked diffusion to 7–8B parameters. Most relevant to our methodology,
38
and
50
provide the IsoFLOP scaling-law protocol that we adopt to benchmark continuous diffusion against its discrete counterparts on equal footing.

A.2
Continuous diffusion language models

Continuous diffusion language modeling lifts a Gaussian forward process onto a continuous representation of tokens, and falls roughly into three camps. The first and most studied embeds tokens into
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and diffuses the embeddings: Diffusion-LM
[
29
]
and SSD-LM
[
19
]
introduced the recipe with controllable generation in mind; CDCD
[
14
]
added a learned categorical-conditional schedule; DiffusionBERT
[
20
]
and self-conditioned embedding diffusion
[
61
]
explored hybrid masked/continuous training; latent diffusion language models
[
31
]
push the diffusion into a frozen pretrained latent space; and analog bits
[
8
]
encode tokens as binary vectors and diffuse them in
\mathbb{R}
. Plaid
[
17
]
, on which RePlaid is directly based, combined low-dimensional sphere-normalized embeddings, a softmax-categorical denoiser and a learned monotone noise schedule.

Concurrent work continues this thread: FLM
[
28
]
recasts the embedding-space diffusion as a flow-map model targeting one-step continuous denoising, and Discrete Flow Maps (DFM)
[
44
]
and Categorical Flow Maps (CFM)
[
47
]
are concurrent works that extend the flow-map machinery further. Both FLM and DFM uses fixed one-hot embeddings and cross-entropy loss. LangFlow
[
9
]
swaps the three-term NELBO in Plaid for a pure cross-entropy objective justified via a Bregman flow-matching identity, and uses learnable embeddings as in Plaid. A second camp parameterizes the diffusion on the simplex or its statistical manifold rather than in embedding space: categorical flow matching on statistical manifolds
[
10
]
, Fisher flow matching
[
13
]
and RDLM
[
25
]
construct flows respecting the Fisher–Rao geometry of categorical distributions. A third dequantization-based camp lifts categorical samples to continuous variables via argmax flows
[
23
]
or Dirichlet noise
[
3
]
; Duo
[
49
]
also belongs here, observing that uniform-state discrete diffusion arises as the argmax projection of an underlying Gaussian diffusion and exploiting this duality to import continuous-diffusion training tricks back into the discrete model. RePlaid revisits the embedding-space camp—specifically Plaid—under the IsoFLOP scaling protocol of
38
,
50
, isolating what fraction of Plaid’s reported compute gap stems from the method itself versus from the evaluation setup.

Appendix C
Derivation of the sequence-level NELBO for Plaid

[Back to
Section

2
in the main paper.]

Let
{\mathbf{x}}\in[V]^{L}
be a length-
L
sequence over a vocabulary of size
V
, identified with a matrix in
\{0,1\}^{L\times V}
whose
l
-th row
{\mathbf{x}}^{l}
is the one-hot vector of the
l
-th token, and
{\mathbf{e}}:={\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{e}}
its embedding (
Section

2
). Following VDMs
[
27
]
, the noisy latents
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{e}}
,
t\in[0,1]
, have conditional marginal

\displaystyle q({\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid{\mathbf{x}})=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}{\mathbf{e}},\ \sigma_{t}^{2}{\mathbf{I}}),

with
\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t}>0
smooth and
{\mathrm{SNR}}(t):=\alpha_{t}^{2}/\sigma_{t}^{2}
strictly decreasing in
t
.

For a discretization with
T
steps, let
s(i):=(i-1)/T
and
t(i):=i/T
, so the joint forward distribution can be written as
q({\mathbf{z}}_{0:1}\mid{\mathbf{x}}):=q({\mathbf{z}}_{1}\mid{\mathbf{x}})\prod_{i=1}^{T}q({\mathbf{z}}_{s(i)}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)},{\mathbf{x}})
, with each transition
q({\mathbf{z}}_{s(i)}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)},{\mathbf{x}})
the closed-form Gaussian posterior of VDMs
Eq.

2
. Plaid parameterizes the reverse process by plugging the denoiser’s clean-data prediction
{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)},t(i))
into that posterior in place of
{\mathbf{x}}
:

\displaystyle p_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{s(i)}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)}):=q\bigl({\mathbf{z}}_{s(i)}\,\big|\,{\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)},\ {\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)},t(i))\bigr),

(10)

where
{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{e}}\times[0,1]\to(\Delta^{V})^{L}
takes the entire noisy sequence as input.

Multiplying and dividing the integrand by
q({\mathbf{z}}_{0:1}\mid{\mathbf{x}})=q({\mathbf{z}}_{1}\mid{\mathbf{x}})\,\prod_{i=1}^{T}q({\mathbf{z}}_{s(i)}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)},{\mathbf{x}})
and applying Jensen’s inequality to the (concave) logarithm gives an upper bound on the negative log-likelihood:

\displaystyle-\log p_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=-\log\int p({\mathbf{z}}_{1})\,p_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{0})\,\prod_{i=1}^{T}p_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{s(i)}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)})\,{\mathrm{d}}{\mathbf{z}}_{0:1}

\displaystyle\leq-\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{z}}_{0:1}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\!\left[\log\frac{p({\mathbf{z}}_{1})\,p_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{0})\,\prod_{i=1}^{T}p_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{s(i)}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)})}{q({\mathbf{z}}_{1}\mid{\mathbf{x}})\,\prod_{i=1}^{T}q({\mathbf{z}}_{s(i)}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)},{\mathbf{x}})}\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{z}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\bigl[-\log p_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{0})\bigr]}_{\text{Reconstruction loss}}+\underbrace{\operatorname{KL}\bigl(q({\mathbf{z}}_{1}\mid{\mathbf{x}})\,\|\,p({\mathbf{z}}_{1})\bigr)}_{\text{Prior loss}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\operatorname{KL}\bigl(q({\mathbf{z}}_{s(i)}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)},{\mathbf{x}})\,\|\,p_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{s(i)}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)})\bigr)}_{\text{Diffusion loss }\mathcal{L}_{T}({\mathbf{x}})}.

Reconstruction loss.

Plaid’s denoiser emits an independent categorical distribution at every position,
p_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{0})=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\langle{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}^{l}({\mathbf{z}}_{0},0),\,{\mathbf{x}}^{l}\rangle
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{z}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\bigl[-\log p_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{0})\bigr]=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{z}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\bigl[-\log\langle{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}^{l}({\mathbf{z}}_{0},0),\,{\mathbf{x}}^{l}\rangle\bigr].

(11)

Prior loss.

Both
q({\mathbf{z}}_{1}\mid{\mathbf{x}})=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{1}{\mathbf{e}},\sigma_{1}^{2}{\mathbf{I}})
and
p({\mathbf{z}}_{1})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},{\mathbf{I}})
are isotropic Gaussians on
\mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{e}}
, giving the closed form

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\bigl(q({\mathbf{z}}_{1}\mid{\mathbf{x}})\,\|\,p({\mathbf{z}}_{1})\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\,L\,d_{e}\bigl(\sigma_{1}^{2}-1-\log\sigma_{1}^{2}\bigr)+\alpha_{1}^{2}\,\|{\mathbf{e}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,\Bigr],

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{F}}
is the Frobenius norm.

Diffusion loss.

Substituting Plaid’s parameterization
Eq.

10
into each per-step KL collapses it to a scaled squared distance between the predicted and ground-truth embeddings;
[
27
, Eq. (13)]
shows that summing over
i
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{T}({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{T}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\mathcal{U}\{1,\dots,T\},\,{\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)}\sim q({\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\!\Bigl[\bigl({\mathrm{SNR}}(s(i))-{\mathrm{SNR}}(t(i))\bigr)\,\bigl\|\,{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t(i)},t(i))-{\mathbf{e}}\,\bigr\|^{2}\Bigr],

(12)

where
{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t):={\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t)\,{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{e}}
is the predicted clean-embedding sequence. Taking
T\to\infty
converts the SNR difference into a derivative,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\infty}({\mathbf{x}})=-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1],\,{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\sim q({\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\!\Bigl[{\mathrm{SNR}}^{\prime}(t)\,\bigl\|\,{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t)-{\mathbf{e}}\,\bigr\|^{2}\Bigr],

(13)

which is non-negative because
{\mathrm{SNR}}^{\prime}(t)<0
.

Combining the three terms recovers the sequence-level NELBO of
Eq.

4
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NELBO}}({\mathbf{x}})=\operatorname{KL}\bigl(q({\mathbf{z}}_{1}\mid{\mathbf{x}})\,\|\,p({\mathbf{z}}_{1})\bigr)+\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{z}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\!\left[\sum_{l=1}^{L}-\log\langle{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}^{l}({\mathbf{z}}_{0},0),\,{\mathbf{x}}^{l}\rangle\right]+\mathcal{L}_{\infty}({\mathbf{x}}).

Appendix D
Sampler Update Formulas

[Back to
Section

4.2
in the main paper.]

This appendix gives the per-step updates for the four samplers used in the main paper: one stochastic ancestral sampler (DDPM-style) and three deterministic PFODE solvers (DDIM, DPM-Solver++(2M), Heun). All operate on the variance-preserving schedule
\sigma_{t}^{2}=\operatorname{sigmoid}(\gamma_{t})
,
\alpha_{t}^{2}=\operatorname{sigmoid}(-\gamma_{t})
, and step from
t
to
s<t
along a discretization of
[1,0]
into
T
steps. At each step the network is evaluated to obtain a clean-data prediction; we abbreviate

\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta}:={\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t){\mathbf{E}},\qquad\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}:=({\mathbf{z}}_{t}-\alpha_{t}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta})/\sigma_{t},

for the predicted clean embedding and the implied noise prediction. The PFODE velocity
Eq.

5
reads
{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t)=\dot{\alpha}_{t}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta}+\dot{\sigma}_{t}\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}
in this notation. After
T
updates the final tokens are read out as
\argmax
of the decoder logits at
t\approx 0
.

D.1
Stochastic Ancestral Sampler (DDPM)

This is the default sampler used to report all generation results unless otherwise noted. It draws
{\mathbf{z}}_{s}\sim p_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{s}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})
for
s<t
from the closed-form Gaussian posterior of VDM
[
27
, App. A.4]
:

{\mathbf{z}}_{s}=(1-c)\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\alpha_{t}}{\mathbf{z}}_{t}+c\alpha_{s}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta}+\sqrt{c(1-\alpha_{s}^{2})}\,{\bm{\epsilon}},\quad{\bm{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{I}}),\quad c=1-{\mathrm{e}}^{\gamma_{s}-\gamma_{t}},

(14)

where
c\in(0,1)
is the per-step SNR gap, since
{\mathrm{SNR}}(t)=\exp(-\gamma_{t})
implies
c=1-{\mathrm{SNR}}(t)/{\mathrm{SNR}}(s)
. Each step uses one network evaluation, giving
T+1
total NFEs including the final
\argmax
.

D.2
Deterministic PFODE Solvers

The three deterministic samplers below all integrate the PFODE
Eq.

5
, differing only in the discretization scheme.

DDIM
[
57
]
.

Reusing
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}
at both endpoints when stepping to
s<t
gives the update

{\mathbf{z}}_{s}=\alpha_{s}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta}+\sigma_{s}\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}.

(15)

This is the exponential-integrator step for
Eq.

5

linearized at
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta}
(i.e., with
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta}
held constant along the trajectory), and is therefore exact for any step size whenever the linearization is exact. A
T
-step schedule uses
T+1
NFEs including the final
\argmax
.

DPM-Solver++(2M)
[
33
]
.

A second-order multistep solver. Let
\lambda_{t}:=-\gamma_{t}/2=\log(\alpha_{t}/\sigma_{t})
denote the negative half log-SNR; stepping from
t_{i-1}
to
t_{i}<t_{i-1}
gives step size
h_{i}=\lambda_{t_{i}}-\lambda_{t_{i-1}}>0
in
\lambda

\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}^{(i)}:={\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t_{i}},t_{i})
, the update reads

\displaystyle{\mathbf{D}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{2r_{i}}\right)\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}^{(i-1)}{\mathbf{E}}-\frac{1}{2r_{i}}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}^{(i-2)}{\mathbf{E}},\quad r_{i}=\frac{h_{i-1}}{h_{i}}\quad(i>1);\quad{\mathbf{D}}_{1}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}^{(0)}{\mathbf{E}},

(16)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}_{t_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{t_{i}}}{\sigma_{t_{i-1}}}{\mathbf{z}}_{t_{i-1}}-\alpha_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{e}}^{-h_{i}}-1){\mathbf{D}}_{i}.

(17)

The multistep term
Eq.

16
linearly extrapolates
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}{\mathbf{E}}
along
\lambda
using the current prediction and the one cached from the previous step; at the first step, where the cached prediction is unavailable, we fall back to the DDIM update. A
T
-step schedule uses
T+1
NFEs including the final
\argmax
.

Heun
[
26
]
.

A second-order predictor–corrector solver. We reparametrize
Eq.

5
into the variance-exploding (VE) form via
\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{t}:={\mathbf{z}}_{t}/\alpha_{t}
and
\tilde{\sigma}_{t}:=\sigma_{t}/\alpha_{t}=\exp(\gamma_{t}/2)
, under which
\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid{\mathbf{e}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{e}},\tilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}{\mathbf{I}})
and the ODE collapses to the canonical

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\bar{\mathbf{z}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\sigma}}=\frac{\bar{\mathbf{z}}-\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\theta}}{\tilde{\sigma}}=:\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}(\bar{\mathbf{z}},\tilde{\sigma}).

(18)

The update from
\tilde{\sigma}_{t}
to
\tilde{\sigma}_{s}
is

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}_{s}

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{t}+(\tilde{\sigma}_{s}-\tilde{\sigma}_{t})\,\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}(\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{t},\tilde{\sigma}_{t}),

(19)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{s}

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\sigma}_{s}-\tilde{\sigma}_{t})\!\left[\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}(\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{t},\tilde{\sigma}_{t})+\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}(\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}_{s},\tilde{\sigma}_{s})\right],

(20)

with the VP latent recovered as

2T
total NFEs (including the final
\argmax

T

E.1
ODE-based likelihood identity

Lemma 1

(
ODE-based likelihood identity
)
.

Let
p_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{0}):=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\langle{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}^{l}({\mathbf{z}}_{0},0),{\mathbf{x}}^{l}\rangle
be the categorical decoder at
t=0

be the forward Gaussian proposal, and let

denote the PFODE trajectory

5
simulated from

p_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}})=\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{z}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\!\left[\frac{p_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{0})\,p_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{0})}{q({\mathbf{z}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\right],

(21)

\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{0})

\displaystyle=\log p({\mathbf{z}}_{1})+\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t){\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle=-\frac{Ld_{e}}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{z}}_{1}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t){\mathrm{d}}t,

and the following

[
4
]

\widehat{\log p_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}})}_{K}:=\log\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w^{(k)},\quad w^{(k)}:=\frac{p_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{0}^{(k)})\,p_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{0}^{(k)})}{q({\mathbf{z}}_{0}^{(k)}\mid{\mathbf{x}})},\quad\text{where}\penalty\ {\mathbf{z}}_{0}^{(k)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}q({\mathbf{z}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{x}}),

[

]

.

\sigma_{t}^{2}=\operatorname{sigmoid}(\gamma_{t})
,
\alpha_{t}^{2}=\operatorname{sigmoid}(-\gamma_{t})
. Reparametrizing the PFODE
Eq.

5
by
\gamma
via
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}(\gamma):={\mathbf{z}}_{t(\gamma)}
yields

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\gamma}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\alpha_{\gamma}^{2}\,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}-\alpha_{\gamma}\,{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\gamma){\mathbf{E}}\bigr)=:\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\gamma),

(25)

and the
\gamma
-space log-weight

\displaystyle\log w^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\log\langle{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}^{l}({\mathbf{z}}_{0}^{(k)},\gamma_{0}),{\mathbf{x}}^{l}\rangle+\frac{Ld_{e}}{2}\log\sigma_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}\|{\mathbf{z}}_{0}^{(k)}-\alpha_{0}{\mathbf{e}}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{z}}(\gamma_{1})^{(k)}\|^{2}+\int_{\gamma_{0}}^{\gamma_{1}}\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\gamma){\mathrm{d}}\gamma

(26)

equals the
t
-space log-weight
Section

E.1
as a continuous-time identity along the same trajectory.

Proof.

The VP schedule gives
2\sigma_{t}\dot{\sigma}_{t}=\operatorname{sigmoid}^{\prime}(\gamma_{t})\,\gamma^{\prime}(t)=\sigma_{t}^{2}\alpha_{t}^{2}\,\gamma^{\prime}(t)
via
\operatorname{sigmoid}^{\prime}(z)=\operatorname{sigmoid}(z)(1-\operatorname{sigmoid}(z))
, and
\alpha_{t}\dot{\alpha}_{t}+\sigma_{t}\dot{\sigma}_{t}=0
via
\alpha^{2}+\sigma^{2}=1
. Hence

\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}=\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{t}^{2}\,\gamma^{\prime}(t),\qquad\dot{\alpha}_{t}-\alpha_{t}\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}=-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{t}(\sigma_{t}^{2}+\alpha_{t}^{2})\,\gamma^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{t}\,\gamma^{\prime}(t).

Substituting into
Eq.

5
factors out a common
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
:

{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t)=\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}{\mathbf{z}}_{t}+\!\left(\dot{\alpha}_{t}-\alpha_{t}\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}\right)\!{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t){\mathbf{E}}=\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(t)}{2}\bigl(\alpha_{t}^{2}{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-\alpha_{t}{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t){\mathbf{E}}\bigr).

The chain rule with
{\mathrm{d}}\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}(t){\mathrm{d}}t
gives
{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}/{\mathrm{d}}\gamma={\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t)/\gamma^{\prime}(t)
, which cancels the
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
in the velocity above and yields
Eq.

25
; the backbone
{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}(\cdot,\gamma)
already conditions on
\gamma
directly, so no schedule inversion is needed.

The two velocity fields satisfy
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}},\gamma)={\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}},t(\gamma))/\gamma^{\prime}(t(\gamma))
. Since
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
depends on
\gamma
alone (not on
{\mathbf{z}}
), the spatial Jacobian inherits the same scalar factor:
\partial_{\mathbf{z}}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}},\gamma)=\partial_{\mathbf{z}}{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}},t(\gamma))/\gamma^{\prime}(t(\gamma))
. Taking traces and substituting
{\mathrm{d}}t={\mathrm{d}}\gamma/\gamma^{\prime}(t)
in the
t
-space trace integral gives

\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t){\mathrm{d}}t=\int_{\gamma_{0}}^{\gamma_{1}}\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\gamma){\mathrm{d}}\gamma,

(27)

by change of variable plus the pointwise trace identity. Substituting into
Lemma

1
and
Section

E.1
yields
Lemma

2
.
∎

Numerical advantage.

Although
Section

E.1
and
Lemma

2
are exactly equal in continuous time, they differ as numerical objects: integrating along
\gamma
removes the schedule-induced stiffness factor
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
near
t\to 0,1
, where
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
blows up under typical learned schedules. We use
Lemma

2
for all reported PPL numbers.

E.2
Self-conditioning and the Chain-rule Trace

With self-conditioning enabled, the denoiser carries an extra input
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{e}}
, written
{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\gamma,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}})
. At inference time, both
9
and our pipeline resolve
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}
at every solver step from a single bootstrap forward pass with
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}\leftarrow{\mathbf{0}}
:

{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\gamma):={\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\gamma,{\mathbf{0}}){\mathbf{E}},

making
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}
a deterministic function of the current state. The PFODE
Eq.

25
actually integrated then has effective drift
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\mapsto\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\gamma,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\gamma))
, and Liouville’s identity in
Lemma

1
requires the divergence of this composition. By the chain rule,

\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\cdot\Bigl[\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}},\gamma,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}({\mathbf{z}},\gamma))\Bigr]=\underbrace{\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\partial_{\mathbf{z}}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}\bigr)}_{\text{partial}}+\underbrace{\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\partial_{{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}\cdot\partial_{\mathbf{z}}{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}\bigr)}_{\text{chain rule}},

(28)

with all partials of
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}
evaluated at the unperturbed
({\mathbf{z}},\gamma,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}({\mathbf{z}},\gamma))
. The standard Hutchinson trace estimator—
{\bm{\xi}}^{\top}[\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}+h{\bm{\xi}},\gamma,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}^{\star})-\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}-h{\bm{\xi}},\gamma,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}^{\star})]/(2h)
in the finite-difference path, or its JVP analogue—caches
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}^{\star}:={\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}({\mathbf{z}},\gamma)
at the unperturbed state and reuses it across the two perturbations, recovering only the partial term; the chain-rule term is silently dropped. The integrated divergence is then that of
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}(\cdot,\gamma,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}^{\star})
, a different velocity field from the one in
Eq.

25
, and the resulting “
-\widehat{\log p_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}})}
” is not a Liouville identity for
p_{\theta}
, hence not an upper bound on
-\log p_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}})
.

The correction is to recompute
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}
at each perturbed state (FD path) or to leave the bootstrap forward inside the JVP graph (JVP path), at a cost of one extra backbone forward per perturbation. With self-conditioning disabled (
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}\equiv{\mathbf{0}}
) the chain-rule term vanishes (
\partial_{\mathbf{z}}{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}={\mathbf{0}}
) and the standard estimator is unbiased.

Why dropping the chain-rule term inflates the estimate?

The PFODE drift
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}
contains a denoising contribution that contracts the trajectory toward the network’s predicted clean embedding
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\gamma,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}){\mathbf{E}}
; with self-conditioning enabled, the bootstrap
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\gamma)
co-moves with
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}
in the high-SNR regime where the prediction is most informative, so the closed-loop velocity actually integrated by the solver is
less
sensitive to perturbations of
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}
than the open-loop velocity
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}(\cdot,\gamma,{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}^{\star})
that the cached Hutchinson trace estimator probes. Equivalently, since
\partial_{{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}\propto-\alpha_{\gamma}\,\partial_{{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}\,{\mathbf{E}}
, the missing chain-rule trace
\operatorname{tr}(\partial_{{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}\cdot\partial_{\mathbf{z}}{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}})
is typically negative for a self-conditioning denoiser whose two input pathways agree on the same predicted token. Dropping a negative summand overestimates the integrated divergence
\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta}\,{\mathrm{d}}t
, which via the Liouville closure
Lemma

1
inflates
\log p_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{0})
and the log-weight
\log w^{(k)}
, deflating the reported PPL. The bias grows with
\partial_{\mathbf{z}}{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{sc}}
and is therefore largest in the high-SNR regime where the bootstrap is most accurate.

Appendix F
Constant Per-Timestep Diffusion Loss

[Back to
Proposition

1
in the main paper]

See
1

Proof.

In
\gamma
-coordinates the per-timestep loss is

\displaystyle\ell_{\theta,\gamma}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\,\gamma^{\prime}(t)\,w(\gamma(t)),\qquad w(\gamma):={\mathrm{e}}^{-\gamma}\,\operatorname{MSE}_{\theta}({\mathrm{e}}^{-\gamma}),

(29)

since
{\mathrm{SNR}}(t)={\mathrm{e}}^{-\gamma(t)}
implies
{\mathrm{SNR}}^{\prime}(t)=-\gamma^{\prime}(t)\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\gamma(t)}
. By assumption
w
is positive and continuous on
[\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}]
. Changing variables to
\gamma
,

\displaystyle\kappa=\mathbb{E}_{t}[\ell_{\theta,\gamma}(t)]=\frac{1}{2}\!\int_{\gamma_{0}}^{\gamma_{1}}w(\gamma){\mathrm{d}}\gamma,

(30)

which depends only on the endpoints, recovering the schedule invariance of
27
. Define the cumulative weight
G(\gamma):=\int_{\gamma_{0}}^{\gamma}w(\gamma){\mathrm{d}}\gamma
; then
G(\gamma_{0})=0
,
G(\gamma_{1})=2\kappa
, and
G
is strictly increasing on
[\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}]
. Set

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}(t):=G^{-1}(2\kappa\,t),

(31)

which gives
\gamma^{*}(0)=\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma^{*}(1)=\gamma_{1}
by construction. Differentiating
G(\gamma^{*}(t))=2\kappa t
yields
(\gamma^{*})^{\prime}(t)=2\kappa/w(\gamma^{*}(t))
, so

\displaystyle\ell_{\theta,\gamma^{*}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\,(\gamma^{*})^{\prime}(t)\,w(\gamma^{*}(t))=\kappa\qquad\text{for all }t\in[0,1],

(32)

hence
\operatorname{Var}_{t}[\ell_{\theta,\gamma^{*}}(t)]=0
. Uniqueness of
\gamma^{*}
follows from the strict monotonicity of
G
.
∎

Appendix G
Linear Information Decay Under Optimality

[Back to
Proposition

2
in the main paper]

Lemma 3

(
Linear Information Decay Under Optimality
)
.

Assuming a Bayes-optimal model
\theta^{*}
with its optimal noise schedule
\gamma^{*}
(
Proposition

1
) that achieves constant
\ell_{\theta^{*},\gamma^{*}}(t)=\kappa\geq 0
, we have

I({\mathbf{e}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=I({\mathbf{e}};{\mathbf{z}}_{0})-\kappa t.

(33)

Intuitively,
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
contains less information about what
{\mathbf{e}}
is as
t
increases.

Proof.

Write
\nu_{t}:={\mathrm{SNR}}(t)
and recall the minimum mean-square error of estimating
{\mathbf{e}}
from
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{MMSE}(\nu_{t}):=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathrm{data}}({\mathbf{x}})\,q({\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\bigl[\,\|{\mathbf{e}}-\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{e}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t}]\|^{2}\,\bigr].

(34)

Rescaling by
\sigma_{t}
casts
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
into canonical Gaussian-channel form,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}_{t}/\sigma_{t}=\sqrt{\nu_{t}}\,{\mathbf{e}}+{\bm{\epsilon}},\qquad{\bm{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{I}}),

(35)

and dividing by the deterministic
\sigma_{t}
preserves information about
{\mathbf{e}}
. The I-MMSE identity
[
18
, Thm. 2]
therefore yields

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\nu_{t}}\,I({\mathbf{e}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\,\operatorname{MMSE}(\nu_{t}).

(36)

By
Definition

1
,
\operatorname{MSE}_{\theta^{*}}(\nu_{t})=\operatorname{MMSE}(\nu_{t})
, so the constant-loss condition from
Proposition

1
becomes

\displaystyle\ell_{\theta^{*},\gamma^{*}}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\nu_{t}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\,\operatorname{MMSE}(\nu_{t})=\kappa.

(37)

Combining the two displays via the chain rule,

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\,I({\mathbf{e}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}I}{{\mathrm{d}}\nu_{t}}\,\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\nu_{t}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=\frac{1}{2}\,\operatorname{MMSE}(\nu_{t})\,\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\nu_{t}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=-\kappa,

(38)

and integrating from
0
to
t
gives
I({\mathbf{e}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=I({\mathbf{e}};{\mathbf{z}}_{0})-\kappa\,t
.
∎

Appendix H
Per-Timestep CE Under Optimality

[Back to
Proposition

2
in the main paper]

See
2

Proof.

By inserting
q({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})
inside the log, the per-timestep CE loss decomposes into a conditional entropy plus a model gap:

\displaystyle\operatorname{CE}_{\theta^{*},\gamma^{*}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathrm{data}}({\mathbf{x}})\,q({\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}[-\log\hat{p}_{\theta^{*}}({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})]

(39)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathrm{data}}({\mathbf{x}})\,q({\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid{\mathbf{x}})}\!\left[-\log q({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})-\log\frac{\hat{p}_{\theta^{*}}({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})}{q({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})}\right]

(40)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})}_{\text{conditional entropy}}\;+\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{z}}_{t})}\bigl[\operatorname{KL}\bigl(q({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})\,\|\,\hat{p}_{\theta^{*}}({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})\bigr)\bigr]}_{\text{model gap}}.

(41)

Computing the model gap.

By
Definition

1
, the Bayes-optimal denoiser realizes a per-position factorized model
\hat{p}_{\theta^{*}}({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=\prod_{l}q({\mathbf{x}}^{l}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})
. The KL between the true joint posterior and this factorized product is

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\bigl(q({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})\,\big\|\,\textstyle\prod_{l}q({\mathbf{x}}^{l}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})\bigr)=\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})}\Big[\log q({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})-\sum_{l}\log q({\mathbf{x}}^{l}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})\Big],

(42)

and taking expectation over
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\sim q({\mathbf{z}}_{t})
gives, after rearrangement,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{z}}_{t})}\bigl[\operatorname{KL}(q({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})\,\|\,\textstyle\prod_{l}q({\mathbf{x}}^{l}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t}))\bigr]=\sum_{l}H({\mathbf{x}}^{l}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})-H({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})\;=:\;C({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t}),

(43)

i.e., the conditional total correlation of
{\mathbf{x}}
given
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
. It is non-negative, and zero iff tokens of
{\mathbf{x}}
are conditionally independent given
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
. For language data
q({\mathbf{x}})
has dimensional correlations, and as
q({\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid{\mathbf{x}})=\prod_{l}q({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{l}\mid{\mathbf{x}}^{l})
, the posterior
q({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})
also exhibits dimensional correlation. Therefore,
C({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})>0
generically.

Computing the conditional entropy.

By the definition of mutual information,

\displaystyle H({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=H({\mathbf{x}})-I({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t}).

(44)

Under no embedding collapse,
{\mathbf{x}}^{l}\leftrightarrow{\mathbf{e}}^{l}
is bijective per position, so
I({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=I({\mathbf{e}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})
, and
Lemma

3
gives

\displaystyle I({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=I({\mathbf{e}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=I({\mathbf{e}};{\mathbf{z}}_{0})-\kappa t\implies H({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=H({\mathbf{x}})-I({\mathbf{e}};{\mathbf{z}}_{0})+\kappa t.

(45)

Putting it together.

Combining the conditional entropy and the model gap,

\displaystyle\operatorname{CE}_{\theta^{*},\gamma^{*}}(t)=H({\mathbf{x}})-I({\mathbf{e}};{\mathbf{z}}_{0})+\kappa t+C({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t}).

(46)

Monotonicity of
C({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})
in
t
.

Substituting
H({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=H({\mathbf{x}})-I({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})
and
H({\mathbf{x}}^{l}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=H({\mathbf{x}}^{l})-I({\mathbf{x}}^{l};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})
into the definition of
C({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})
,

\displaystyle C({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=C({\mathbf{x}})-\Bigl[\sum_{l}I({\mathbf{x}}^{l};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})-I({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})\Bigr],

(47)

so it suffices to show that
\sum_{l}I({\mathbf{x}}^{l};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})-I({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})
is non-increasing in
t
, or equivalently non-decreasing in the SNR
\nu_{t}:=\alpha_{t}^{2}/\sigma_{t}^{2}
. Writing the channel in the standard form
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}/\sigma_{t}=\sqrt{\nu_{t}}\,{\mathbf{e}}+\xi
with
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\mathbf{I}})
and applying the I-MMSE identity
[
18
]
,

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\nu_{t}}I({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l}M^{l}(\nu_{t}),\qquad M^{l}(\nu_{t}):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|{\mathbf{e}}^{l}-\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{e}}^{l}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t}]\|^{2}\right].

(48)

Combining the chain rule
I({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=I({\mathbf{x}}^{l};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})+I({\mathbf{x}}^{-l};{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid{\mathbf{x}}^{l})
with the conditional I-MMSE identity applied to the second term gives

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\nu_{t}}I({\mathbf{x}}^{l};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\nu_{t}}I({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l^{\prime}\neq l}M^{l^{\prime}}_{\,\mid{\mathbf{x}}^{l}}(\nu_{t}),\quad M^{l^{\prime}}_{\,\mid{\mathbf{x}}^{l}}(\nu_{t}):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|{\mathbf{e}}^{l^{\prime}}-\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{e}}^{l^{\prime}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t},{\mathbf{x}}^{l}]\|^{2}\right].

(49)

Summing over
l
and rearranging,

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\nu_{t}}\!\left[\sum_{l}I({\mathbf{x}}^{l};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})-I({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{z}}_{t})\right]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l\neq l^{\prime}}\bigl[M^{l^{\prime}}(\nu_{t})-M^{l^{\prime}}_{\,\mid{\mathbf{x}}^{l}}(\nu_{t})\bigr]\geq 0,

(50)

where the inequality is due to tower property: conditioning on
{\mathbf{x}}^{l}
in addition to
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
can only reduce the MMSE of
{\mathbf{e}}^{l^{\prime}}
. Therefore
C({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})
is non-decreasing in
t
, with strict increase whenever any pair
({\mathbf{x}}^{l},{\mathbf{x}}^{l^{\prime}})
with
l\neq l^{\prime}
remains correlated under the Bayes posterior
q({\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t})
.

[Back to

in the main paper]

To analyze embedding geometry conditioned on syntactic role, we precompute a dominant
Universal POS tag for each GPT-2 subword token over a reference corpus. The
challenge is that GPT-2 BPE splits words into subwords (e.g.,
running

\rightarrow

␣run
,
ning
), whereas POS tags
are defined on whole words. We resolve this by aligning subwords to spaCy
[
22
]
word
spans via character offsets.

P.1
Pipeline

Given a corpus of token-id rows of length
L=1024
, we proceed as follows:

1.

Decode.
Convert each row of token ids back to text using the
GPT-2 tokenizer.

2.

Tag.
Run spaCy’s POS tagger on the decoded text to obtain
word-level tags and their character offsets.

3.

Re-encode.
Re-tokenize the decoded text with the fast GPT-2
tokenizer to recover
(token_id, char_span)
pairs for each
subword. We use the re-encoded ids rather than the original ids because
chunk boundaries in the dataset may truncate multi-byte UTF-8 sequences;
re-encoding guarantees that subword spans align with the actual text spaCy
saw.

Align.
For each subword, inherit the POS tag of the spaCy

Tally.
Accumulate a count matrix

V
is the vocabulary size and

P{=}18
tags (17 UPOS tags plus a
Space
bucket). The dominant

\argmax_{p}C_{v,p}
.
