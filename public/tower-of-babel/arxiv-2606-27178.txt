De la Vallée Poussin type approximation for solving some Fredholm integral equations
Domenico Mezzanotte, Donatella Occorsio, Mario Pezzella, Woula Themistoclakis

Abstract
In the present paper, we introduce a numerical method for second-kind Fredholm integral equations (FIEs) based on de la Vallée Poussin-type (VP) polynomial approximations at Jacobi zeros. This class of approximations offers several advantages over classical Lagrange interpolation at the same nodes. In particular, it guarantees uniformly bounded Lebesgue constants in suitable weighted function spaces and provides near-best uniform approximation for functions in these spaces, while also significantly mitigating the Gibbs phenomenon. We show how these properties can be exploited in the numerical solution of FIEs. In particular, the proposed approach effectively handles functions with possible algebraic endpoint singularities and kernel functions featuring weak singularities or highly oscillatory behavior. Under suitable assumptions, we prove stability and convergence of the method in weighted uniform spaces. Furthermore, we develop an efficient implementation based on the solution of a well-conditioned linear system. Numerical results confirm the theoretical error estimates and show that the proposed method achieves higher local accuracy than the corresponding Lagrange-based projection method.



Keywords:

Fredholm integral equations;
Weakly singular kernels;
Highly oscillatory kernels;
De la Vallée Poussin approximation;
Polynomial approximation;
Orthogonal polynomials.

1
Introduction

The paper deals with the numerical solution of the following type of second kind Fredholm Integral Equation (FIE)

f(y)-\nu\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)k(x,y)w(x)\ dx=g(y),\quad y\in(-1,1),\ \ \ \nu\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},

(1)

where
w(x)=v^{\rho,\sigma}(x):=(1-x)^{\rho}(1+x)^{\sigma},
with
\rho,\sigma>-1,
is a given Jacobi weight,
g(y)
and
k(x,y)
are known functions defined on
(-1,1)
and
(-1,1)^{2},
respectively, and
f
is the unknown function to be determined. The equation (
1
) can be written in operator form as follows

(I-\nu K)f=g,

(2)

where
I
denotes the identity operator and

Kf(y)=\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)k(x,y)w(x)\ dx,\qquad y\in(-1,1).

(3)

Among the extensive literature devoted to the numerical solution of FIEs (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
12
,
10
]
), we focus on the case in which the known functions may exhibit endpoint singularities that can be controlled by a Jacobi weight
u,
generally different from
w
. This setting naturally favors to study the equation in the space
C_{u}
of locally continuous functions endowed with weighted uniform norms. A global approach, hereafter referred to as the
\mathcal{L}
-method, based on the Lagrange projector
\mathcal{L}_{n}(w)
associated with the weight
w
in (
1
), has been proposed in
[
5
]
. The resulting projection method requires the solution of a linear system of
n
equations. However, due to the unboundedness of the Lebesgue constants of
\mathcal{L}_{n}(w)
, some restrictions on the choice of weight
u
and a higher regularity of
g\in C_{u}
are needed to ensure its convergence and stability.

The aim of the present study is is to overcome the above limitations by employing the
de la Vallée Poussin
(VP) polynomial quasi-projector
V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta})
, associated with a suitable Jacobi weight
v^{\alpha,\beta}
, which does not need to coincide with
w
. This operator, originally introduced in the trigonometric approximation
[
6
]
, has been first generalized to the algebraic setting in
[
24
]
and then studied in several papers
[
18
,
20
,
25
,
26
]
and applied to solve several singular integral equations
[
4
,
15
,
17
]
. Similarly to classical Lagrange interpolation, it is computed from evaluations of the target function at the Jacobi zeros of order
n
corresponding to
v^{\alpha,\beta}.
However, it interpolates the function at these nodes only in some special cases (e.g.,
|\alpha|=|\beta|=1/2
). Its main peculiarity is the dependence on an additional parameter
1<m<n
. The uniform boundedness of the Lebesgue constants and the uniform convergence in
C_{u}
with the same approximation order of the best polynomial approximation in
C_{u}
, for suitable choices of
\alpha
and
\beta,
are assured when
m\sim n\sim(n-m),
as
n\to\infty.
Moreover, for a given number
n
of nodes, we can suitably modulate
m
to improve the local approximation and reduce the Gibbs phenomenon arising, for instance, in presence of steep function variations.

We assume that, for all
g\in C_{u}
, the equation (
2
) admits a unique solution
f^{*}\in C_{u}.
To approximate this solution, we consider the finite-dimensional equation

(I-\nu K_{n}^{m})f_{n}^{m}=g_{n}^{m},

(4)

where
f_{n}^{m}
is the unknown polynomial, obtained by replacing
Kf
and
g
in (
2
) with their respective VP approximations, namely

K_{n}^{m}f:=V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta},Kf),\qquad\text{and}\qquad g_{n}^{m}:=V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta},g).

Although
V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta})
is generally not a polynomial projector, we prove that equation (
4
) admits a unique polynomial solution
f_{n}^{m}
that can be determined by solving a linear system of
n
equations. The proposed method, hereafter referred to as the
VP
-method, inherits the advantages of VP approximation over Lagrange interpolation. These advantages emerge from both the theoretical and computational perspectives.

In particular, we prove that
f_{n}^{m}
converges to
f^{*}
in
C_{u}
with a near-best approximation order. The same result can be achieved by the
\mathcal{L}
-method only in Hölder–Zygmund type subspaces of
C_{u}
, with an additional
\log n
factor in the error estimates. Moreover, contrarily to the
\mathcal{L}
-method, our analysis covers the more general setting in which the weight
v^{\alpha,\beta}
does not necessarily coincide with the weight
w=v^{\rho,\sigma}
defining the integral operator in (
3
). This possibility, enlarge significantly the range of applicability requiring no restriction on the space
C_{u}
where the equation is studied. Furthermore, when both
\mathcal{L}
-method and
VP
-method can be applied and converge, we point out that the additional
\log n
factor does not produce a significant difference in the maximum error produced by the two methods. Nevertheless, significant gains can be observed for the pointwise error, especially in presence of Gibbs-type oscillations. Finally, we remark that, similarly to the
\mathcal{L}
-method, the construction of the linear system associated with (
4
) requires the computation of the modified moments
Kp_{n}
with
p_{n}
an arbitrary Jacobi polynomial. We provide a detailed analysis of this problem in the special case of highly oscillatory kernels and weakly singular kernels exhibiting algebraic or logarithmic singularities. In the latter case, as a by-product, we establish sufficient conditions ensuring the compactness of the operator
K
in arbitrary weighted spaces
C_{u}
and in Hölder–Zygmund subspaces of
C_{u}
. To our knowledge, these findings are known in literature only for particular weights
u
and
w
.

The paper is structured as follows. Section
2
contains preliminary notation and a collection of well-established results supporting the main findings of Section
3
. There, the numerical method is constructed and analyzed from a theoretical viewpoint. Specifically, stability and convergence are established, and the associated finite-dimensional linear system is derived. A thorough comparison with the Lagrange projection method of
[
5
]
is carried out in Section
4
. Section
5
is devoted to the computation of recurrence relations for modified moments associated with weakly singular and highly oscillating kernels. Several numerical experiments are presented in Section
6
. Finally, Section
7
details the proofs of the main results, whereas Section
8
is devoted to the conclusions and a discussion of the obtained results.

{\mathcal{C}}^{-1}B\leq A\leq{\mathcal{C}}B

As usual, by

m
and the notation

) is used for a bivariate function

2.1
Function spaces

Let
u(x)=v^{\gamma,\delta}(x):=(1-x)^{\gamma}(1+x)^{\delta}
with
\gamma,\delta\geq 0
and let
C_{u}
be the space of the locally continuous functions
f
on
(-1,1)
satisfying the limit conditions

\lim\limits_{x\to 1^{-}}f(x)u(x)=0,\quad\text{if}\ \gamma>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim\limits_{x\to-1^{+}}f(x)u(x)=0,\quad\text{if}\ \delta>0.

(5)

In the case
\gamma=\delta=0
, the space
C_{u}
coincides with the space
C([-1,1])
of continuous functions in
[-1,1]
.

Equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{C_{u}}:=\|fu\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in[-1,1]}|(fu)(x)|,

C_{u}
is a Banach space.
For any operator
A:C_{u}\to C_{u}
, let

\|A\|=\sup_{f\neq 0}\frac{\|Af\|_{C_{u}}}{\|f\|_{C_{u}}}
denote its operator norm.

Denoting by

E_{m}(f)_{u}:=\inf_{P\in{\mathbb{P}}_{m}}\|f-P\|_{C_{u}},

the error of best polynomial approximation of
f\in C_{u}
, the limit conditions (
5
) are necessary to ensure that (see for instance
[
13
]
)

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}E_{m}(f)_{u}=0,\quad\forall f\in C_{u}.

For any
f\in C_{u}
, the main part of the
\varphi
-modulus of smoothness

[
7
, p. 90]
of order
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
is defined as

\Omega_{\varphi}^{k}(f,t)_{u}=\sup_{0<h\leq t}\|\Delta^{k}_{h\varphi}fu\|_{I_{kh}},

where
I_{kh}=[-1+(2kh)^{2},1-(2kh)^{2}]
, and

\displaystyle\Delta^{k}_{h\varphi}f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
j\end{array}\right)f\left(x+(k-2j)\frac{h}{2}\varphi(x)\right).

Through
\Omega_{\varphi}^{k}(f,t)_{u}
it is possible to define the Hölder-Zygmund space
Z_{r}(u)
of order
r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
as

Z_{r}(u)=\left\{f\in C_{u}\ :\ \sup_{t>0}\frac{\Omega_{\varphi}^{k}(f,t)_{u}}{t^{r}}<\infty,\quad k>r\right\},

equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{Z_{r}(u)}=\|f\|_{C_{u}}+\sup_{t>0}\frac{\Omega_{\varphi}^{k}(f,t)_{u}}{t^{r}}.

In the case
\gamma=\delta=0
, i.e.
u=1
, we set
Z_{r}:=Z_{r}(u).

Finally, we recall that for any
f\in Z_{r}(u)
and
m
sufficiently large, (say
m>m_{0}
)

E_{m}(f)_{u}\leq\mathcal{C}\,\frac{\lVert f\rVert_{Z_{r}(u)}}{m^{r}},\quad{\mathcal{C}}\neq{\mathcal{C}}(f,m).

(6)

Finally,
L^{1}
will denote the space of summable functions defined as

L^{1}:=L^{1}([-1,1])=\left\{f:\ \|f\|_{1}=\int_{-1}^{1}|f(x)|dx<\infty.\right\}

2.2
The solvability of the integral equation

Equation (
1
) has been widely studied in the literature and several theorems exist regarding the existence of a unique solution and its smoothness properties (see e.g.
[
1
,
12
,
10
]
and the references there in).

For our aims, a crucial role is played by the compactness of the maps
K:C_{u}\to C_{u}
and
K:Z_{r}(u)\to Z_{r}(u)
. These assumptions and
\ker(I-\nu K)=\{0\}
allow us to apply the Fredholm alternative theorem, ensuring that for any function
g\in C_{u}
(resp.
g\in Z_{r}(u)
), equation (
1
) admits a unique solution
f^{*}\in C_{u}
(resp.
f\in Z_{r}(u)
).

There are several sufficient conditions in the literature on the kernel
k(x,y)
that guarantee the compactness of the operator
K
(see for instance,
[
5
,
10
,
13
]
). In the present study, we focus on two particular cases:

1.

Highly oscillating kernels

K^{\omega}f(y)=\int_{-1}^{1}k^{\omega}(x,y)f(x)w(x)dx,\quad\text{with}\qquad k^{\omega}(x,y)=h(y)\begin{cases}\sin(\omega x),\\
\cos(\omega x),\end{cases}\ \ \omega\in{\mathbb{R}}.

2.

Weakly singular kernels

K^{\mu}f(y)=\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)k^{\mu}(x,y)w(x)dx,\quad\text{with}\quad k^{\mu}(x,y)=\begin{cases}|x-y|^{\mu},&\mu>-1,\ \mu\neq 0,\\
\log|x-y|,&\mu=0,\end{cases}

where
y\in(-1,1)
and
w=v^{\rho,\sigma}
.

Regarding the solvability of equation (
1
) in the case of highly oscillatory kernels, the following theorem follows from a result in
[
5
]
.

Theorem 2.1

If
\frac{w}{u}\in L^{1}

then
K^{\omega}:C_{u}\to C_{u}
is a compact operator. Moreover, for any
r>0
, the map
K^{\omega}:C_{u}\to Z_{r}(u)
is bounded, and
K^{\omega}:Z_{r}(u)\to Z_{r}(u)
is compact.

Regarding the case of weakly singular kernels,
we point out that in the case
w=u\equiv 1,
the compactness of
K^{\mu}:C([-1,1])\to C([-1,1])
is well-known (see, e.g.,

[
1
, p.11]
. Moreover, the compactness of
K^{\mu}
between Zygmund spaces with different weights, has been proved in the context of Cauchy Singular Integral Equations (see, e.g.,
[
14
, Lemma 4.1]
).
In the general case here considered, we state the following results, that, to our knowledge, are not present in literature.

Lemma 2.2

For all
\mu>-1
, the operator
K^{\mu}:C_{u}\to C_{u}
is compact provided that one of the following assumptions is satisfied

\displaystyle\mu\geq 0

and

\displaystyle\quad\frac{w(x)}{u(x)}\in L^{1},

\displaystyle-1<\mu<0

and

\displaystyle\quad\frac{w(x)(1-x^{2})^{\mu}}{u(x)}\in L^{1}.

Lemma 2.3

Let
\mu>-1.
If
\frac{w}{u}\in C([-1,1])
then
K^{\mu}:C_{u}\to Z_{r}(u)
is a bounded operator for any
r\leq 1+\mu
if
\mu\neq 0
and for all
0<r<1
if
\mu=0
. Moreover, for the same values of
r
,
K^{\mu}:Z_{r}(u)\to Z_{r}(u)
is compact.

2.3
VP approximation

Given a Jacobi weight
v^{\alpha,\beta}(x),\,\alpha,\beta>-1
, let us denote by
p_{n}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x)
the Jacobi orthonormal polynomial of degree
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
with positive leading coefficient. Moreover, let us denote by
\{x_{k}:=x_{n,k}(v^{\alpha,\beta})\}_{k=1}^{n}
the zeros of
p_{n}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x)
and by
\{\lambda_{k}:=\lambda_{n,k}(v^{\alpha,\beta})\}_{k=1}^{n}
the Christoffel numbers related to
v^{\alpha,\beta},
namely

\lambda_{k}:=\lambda_{n,k}(v^{\alpha,\beta})=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}p_{i}^{2}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x_{k})\right)^{-1},\qquad k=1,\ldots,n.

(7)

For any pair of integers
1<m<n
, setting

{\mu_{n,j}^{m}}:=\begin{cases}1&0\leq j\leq n-m,\\
\dfrac{n+m-j}{2m}&n-m<j<n+m,\\
0&j\geq n+m,\end{cases}

the fundamental VP polynomials associated with
v^{\alpha,\beta}
are defined by
[
25
]

\Phi_{n,k}^{m}(x):=\lambda_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{n+m-1}{\mu_{n,j}^{m}}p_{j}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x_{k})p_{j}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x),\qquad\qquad|x|\leq 1,

(8)

and the VP polynomial approximating an arbitrary function
f
defined on
[-1,1]
, is given by

{V_{n}^{m}}(v^{\alpha,\beta},f,x):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(x_{k})\Phi_{n,k}^{m}(x),\qquad\qquad|x|\leq 1.

(9)

The properties of these polynomials, the uniform boundedness of the operator
V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta}):f\to V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta},f)
and the error estimates of the VP approximation have been studied in the literature for several functional spaces (see, e.g.,
[
25
,
23
,
26
,
18
,
3
,
19
]
). In what follows, we recall some results relevant to our purposes.

First of all, we highlight the similarity of the definitions (
8
)–(
9
) with the following classical Lagrange polynomial
\mathcal{L}_{n}(v^{\alpha,\beta},f)\in{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1}
interpolating
f
at the same zeros
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}

\mathcal{L}_{n}(v^{\alpha,\beta},f,x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(x_{k})\ell_{n,k}(x),\quad\text{where}\quad\ell_{n,k}(x)=\lambda_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}p_{j}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x_{k})p_{j}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x).

(10)

It is well–known that the Lebesgue constant of Lagrange interpolation (namely the norm of
\mathcal{L}_{n}(v^{\alpha,\beta}):C_{u}\to C_{u}
) increases with
n
at least as
\log n
(see, e.g.
[
13
]
). Furthermore, the bound

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|f(x_{i})|u(x_{i})\leq\|\mathcal{L}_{n}(v^{\alpha,\beta},f)\|_{C_{u}}\leq{\mathcal{C}}\log n\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|f(x_{i})|u(x_{i})\right],\quad{\mathcal{C}}\neq{\mathcal{C}}(n,f),\quad\quad\ \forall f\in C_{u},

holds if and only if

\max\left(0,\ \frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)\leq\gamma\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{5}{4},\qquad\max\left(0,\ \frac{\beta}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)\leq\delta\leq\frac{\beta}{2}+\frac{5}{4}.

(11)

On the contrary, the norm of the VP operator
V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta}):C_{u}\to C_{u}
can be uniformly bounded as stated in the following.

Theorem 2.4

[
23
, Theorem 4.1]

Let
\theta\in(0,1)
be arbitrarily fixed,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
be arbitrarily large, and
m=\lfloor\theta n\rfloor
. If the Jacobi weights
v^{\alpha,\beta}
and
u=v^{\gamma,\delta}
, with
\gamma,\delta\geq 0
, satisfy the following conditions

\displaystyle-1<\gamma-\delta-\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2}<1,

(12)

\displaystyle\max\left(0,\ \frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)<\gamma\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{5}{4},

(13)

\displaystyle\max\left(0,\ \frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)<\delta\leq\frac{\beta}{2}+\frac{5}{4},

(14)

then the map
V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta}):C_{u}\to C_{u}
is uniformly bounded with respect to
n
, and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta},f)-f\|_{C_{u}}=0,\qquad\forall f\in C_{u},

(15)

holds at the same convergence rate of
E_{n}(f)_{u}

It is also well–known that
\mathcal{L}_{n}(v^{\alpha,\beta})
is a projection on
{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1}
, whereas the VP operator satisfies

V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta},P)=P,\qquad\forall P\in{\mathbb{P}}_{n-m}.

(16)

Moreover, the polynomial range space of
{V_{n}^{m}}(v^{\alpha,\beta}),
given by

S_{n}^{m}:=\text{span}\left\{\Phi_{n,i}^{m}(x)\ :\ i=1,\ldots,n\right\},

(17)

is nested between two canonical polynomial spaces

{\mathbb{P}}_{n-m}\subseteq S_{n}^{m}\subset{\mathbb{P}}_{n+m-1},

and, as proved with the following result, has dimension
n
.

Theorem 2.5

For any Jacobi weight
v^{\alpha,\beta}
and any pair of integers
1<m<n
, the fundamental VP polynomials
\{\Phi_{n,k}^{m}(x)\}_{k=1}^{n}
defined in (
8
) are linearly independent.

A very special case is when

is a Chebyshev weight. In such a case, the following theorem establishes that the VP operator is a projector on
S_{n}^{m}
satisfying the same interpolation property of the Lagrange polynomial (
10
).

Theorem 2.6

[
25
]

If
|\alpha|=|\beta|=1/2
, then for all
n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
m<n
we have that

\Phi_{n,i}^{m}(x_{j})=\delta_{i,j},\qquad i,j=1,\dots,n,

(18)

and hence, for any function
f
defined on
[-1,1],
we get

V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta},f,x_{j})=f(x_{j}),\qquad j=1,\dots,n.\qquad

(19)

Moreover,
{V_{n}^{m}}
is a projector on

satisfying

f\in S_{n}^{m}\Longleftrightarrow f=V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta},f).

(20)

Furthermore, regarding the uniform boundedness of the Lebesgue constants of VP operator, we have the following result which improves Theorem

.

Theorem 2.7

[
18
]

Let
\theta\in(0,1)
be arbitrarily fixed,

arbitrarily large and

. For

with

, we have

if and only if

3
The
VP
-method for FIE

In this section, we use the de la Vallé Poussin operator
V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta})
to approximate the solution of equation (
1
) in the weighted space
C_{u}
. From now on, we will refer to the resulting method as the
VP
-method
. We underline that studying equation (
1
) in
C_{u}
allows us to deal with the right-hand side
g
with possible singularities in
\pm 1
and the kernel
k
presenting singularities at the endpoints with respect to the external variable
y
.

The
VP
-method is obtained by applying the quasi-projector
V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta})
to (
2
), leading to the finite-dimensional equation

(I-\nu K_{n}^{m})f_{n}^{m}=g_{n}^{m},

(22)

where

K_{n}^{m}f(y)={V_{n}^{m}}(v^{\alpha,\beta},Kf,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}Kf(x_{k})\Phi_{n,k}^{m}(y),

g_{n}^{m}(y)={V_{n}^{m}}(v^{\alpha,\beta},g,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}g(x_{k})\Phi_{n,k}^{m}(y).

The next theorem ensures that if (
1
) has a unique solution
f^{*}\in C_{u}
then also equation (
22
) admits a unique solution that uniformly converges to
f^{*}
under suitable assumptions.

Theorem 3.1

Assuming
\mathrm{ker}(I-\nu K)=\{0\}
in
C_{u}
, and
K:C_{u}\to C_{u}
compact, let
f^{*}
be the unique solution of (
1
) for a given right-hand side
g\in C_{u}
.
Under the hypotheses of Theorem
2.4
(Theorem
2.7
in the case
|\alpha|=|\beta|=\frac{1}{2}
), for
n
sufficiently large (say
n>n_{0}
), the finite dimensional equation (
22
) admits a unique solution
f_{n}^{m}\in S_{n}^{m}
satisfying the following estimate

\|(f^{*}-f_{n}^{m})\|_{C_{u}}\leq{\mathcal{C}}\left[E_{n-m}(g)_{u}+E_{n-m}(Kf^{*})_{u}\right],\quad{\mathcal{C}}\neq{\mathcal{C}}(n,f^{*}).

(23)

Moreover, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|I-\nu K_{n}^{m}\|\|(I-\nu K_{n}^{m})^{-1}\|=\|I-\nu K\|\|(I-\nu K)^{-1}\|.

(24)

In the case that the solution
f^{*}
belongs to some Zygmund subspace of
C_{u}
, the following corollary states that the approximate solution
f_{n}^{m}
converges to
f^{*}
at the same rate of
E_{n}(f^{*})_{u}.

Corollary 3.2

Assume that
\mathrm{ker}(I-\nu K)=\{0\}
and that
K:C_{u}\to Z_{r}(u)
is bounded for some
r>0
. For any
g\in Z_{s}(u)

s\leq r
, equation (
1
) has a unique solution
f^{*}\in Z_{s}(u)
. Moreover, under the hypotheses of Theorem
2.4
(Theorem
2.7
in the case
|\alpha|=|\beta|=\frac{1}{2}
), the approximate equation (
22
) has a unique solution
f_{n}^{m}
which satisfies the following estimate

\|(f^{*}-f_{n}^{m})\|_{C_{u}}\leq{\mathcal{C}}\ \frac{\|g\|_{Z_{s}(u)}}{n^{s}},\quad{\mathcal{C}}\neq{\mathcal{C}}(n,f^{*}).

(25)

Note that the weight
w
is fixed by the integral operator
K
in (
3
) and the weight
u
characterizes the space to which the solution belongs. On the other hand, the weight

v^{\alpha,\beta}
that defines (

)-(

), to improve the approximation. We refer the reader to Section
4

The computation of the resulting approximate solution
f_{n}^{m}
is investigated in the next subsection.

3.1
The final linear system

Since the approximate solution of (
1
) belongs to
S_{n}^{m}
, we can represent it in the basis
\mathcal{B}=\left\{\frac{\Phi_{n,j}^{m}(y)}{u(x_{j})}\right\}_{j=1}^{n}
of
S_{n}^{m}
, as follows

f_{n}^{m}(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}\frac{\Phi_{n,j}^{m}(y)}{u(x_{j})},\qquad|y|\leq 1,

where the coefficients
d_{j}
are unknown. The expansion of the discrete equation (
22
) in the same basis reads

\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\frac{\Phi_{n,i}^{m}(y)}{u(x_{i})}-\nu\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{u(x_{i})}{u(x_{j})}d_{j}\int_{-1}^{1}k(x,x_{i})\Phi_{n,j}^{m}(x)w(x)\,dx\right]\frac{\Phi_{n,i}^{m}(y)}{u(x_{i})}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\frac{\Phi_{n,i}^{m}(y)}{u(x_{i})},\quad|y|\leq 1,

where we set
e_{i}=g(x_{i})u(x_{i})
. Equating the coefficients of the basis
\mathcal{B}
, we get the following linear system of order
n
in the unknowns
\{d_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},

d_{i}-\nu\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}\frac{u(x_{i})}{u(x_{j})}\int_{-1}^{1}k(x,x_{i})\Phi_{n,j}^{m}(x)w(x)\,dx\right]=e_{i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

which, due to (
8
), can be explicitly written as

d_{i}-\nu\ u(x_{i})\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{u(x_{j})}\sum_{k=0}^{n+m-1}\mu_{n,k}^{m}\,p_{k}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x_{j})\int_{-1}^{1}p_{k}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x)k(x,x_{i})w(x)\,dx=e_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

Equivalently,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left[\delta_{i\,j}-\nu u(x_{i})\frac{\lambda_{j}}{u(x_{j})}\sum_{k=0}^{n+m-1}\mu_{n,k}^{m}\,p_{k}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x_{j})\widetilde{M}_{k}(x_{i})\right]d_{j}=e_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

(26)

where the integrals

\widetilde{M}_{k}(y):=\int_{-1}^{1}p_{k}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x)k(x,y)w(x)\,dx,\quad k=0,\ldots,n+m-1,

(27)

are the so-called
modified moments
.

Denoting by
\bf{I}_{n}
the identity matrix of order
n
, the system (
26
) takes the following matrix form

(\bm{I}_{n}-\nu\bm{K}_{n})\bm{d}_{n}=\bm{e}_{n}.

(28)

where we have set

\bm{d}_{n}=[d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}]^{T},\qquad\bm{e}_{n}=[e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}]^{T},\qquad\bm{K}_{n}=\bm{U}\bm{\widetilde{M}}\bm{\Psi}\bm{P}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{U}^{-1},

with

\begin{gathered}\bm{U}=\operatorname{diag}\left(u(x_{1}),\ldots,u(x_{n})\right),\quad\bm{\Lambda}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\\
\bm{\Psi}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\mu_{n,0}^{m},\ldots,\mu_{n,n+m-1}^{m}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)},\\
\bm{\widetilde{M}}=\left(\widetilde{m}_{ij}\right)_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n+m)},\qquad\widetilde{m}_{ij}=\widetilde{M}_{j}(x_{i}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,\qquad j=0,\ldots,n+m-1,\\
\bm{P}=\left(p_{ij}\right)_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+m)\times n},\qquad p_{ij}=p_{i}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x_{j}),\qquad i=0,\ldots,n+m-1,\qquad j=1,\ldots,n.\end{gathered}

Additional details on the computation of the matrix
\bm{\widetilde{M}}
are provided in Section
5
for specific choices of the kernel
k(x,y)
.

We conclude by establishing the well-conditioning of the final linear system (
28
). Setting
\bm{N}_{n}=\bm{I}_{n}-\nu\bm{K}_{n},
and denoting by
\kappa_{\infty}(\bm{N}_{n}):=\left\|\bm{N}_{n}\right\|_{\infty}\,\left\|\bm{N}^{-1}_{n}\right\|_{\infty}
its condition number in the infinity norm (or row sum norm), the following result holds true.

Theorem 3.3

Assume that
|\alpha|=|\beta|=\frac{1}{2}
. Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
, setting

\kappa(I-\nu K):=\|I-\nu K\|\|(I-\nu K)^{-1}\|,

we have

{\mathcal{C}}^{-1}\kappa(I-\nu K)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\kappa_{\infty}(\bm{N}_{n})\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\kappa_{\infty}(\bm{N}_{n})\leq{\mathcal{C}}\kappa(I-\nu K).

(29)

4
A comparison with a Lagrange based method

In this section, we briefly report the projection method introduced in
[
5
]
, which is based on the Lagrange operator
\mathcal{L}_{n}(v^{\alpha,\beta})
in (
10
). From now on, we refer to it as the
\mathcal{L}
-method
. As mentioned above, it represents in a certain sense the global method closest to the
VP
-method, also because
V_{n}^{m}(v^{\alpha,\beta})
coincides with
\mathcal{L}_{n}(v^{\alpha,\beta})
when
m=1
. However, the applicability of the
\mathcal{L}
-method in
[
5
]
requires the weight
v^{\alpha,\beta}
employed in
\mathcal{L}_{n}(v^{\alpha,\beta})
to coincide with the weight
w
defining the operator
K
in (
3
).

The application of the Lagrange operator to (
2
) yields the discrete equation

(I-\nu\tilde{K}_{n})f_{n}^{L}=\tilde{g}_{n},\qquad(\tilde{K}_{n}f)(y)=\mathcal{L}_{n}(v^{\alpha,\beta},Kf,y),\qquad\tilde{g}_{n}(y)=\mathcal{L}_{n}(v^{\alpha,\beta},g,y),

(30)

with

f_{n}^{L}(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{b}_{j}\frac{\ell_{n,j}(y)}{u(x_{j})}\in{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1}.

The unknown coefficients
\tilde{b}_{i}
,
\ i=1,\dots,n,
are then obtained as the solution of the linear system

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left[\delta_{i\,j}-\nu u(x_{i})\frac{\lambda_{j}}{u(x_{j})}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\,p_{k}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x_{j})M_{k}(x_{i})\right]\tilde{b}_{j}=e_{i},\qquad i=1,2,\dots,n,

(31)

where
e_{i}=g(x_{i})u(x_{i}),\ i=1,2,\dots,n,
and

M_{k}(y):=\int_{-1}^{1}p_{k}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x)k(x,y)v^{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx,\quad k=0,1,\dots,n-1,\qquad

are the modified moments. The system (
31
) admits the following compact representation

(\bm{I}_{n}-\nu\bm{H}_{n})\bm{\widetilde{b}}_{n}=\bm{e}_{n},

(32)

where

\tilde{\bm{b}}_{n}=[\tilde{b}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{b}_{n}]^{T},\qquad\bm{e}_{n}=[e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}]^{T},\qquad\bm{H}_{n}=\bm{U}\bm{M}\bm{Q}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{U}^{-1},

\begin{gathered}\bm{M}=\left(m_{ij}\right)_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\quad m_{ij}=M_{j}(x_{i}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n,\;j=0,\ldots,n-1,\\
\bm{Q}=\left(q_{ij}\right)_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\quad q_{ij}=p_{i}(w,x_{j}),\quad i=0,\ldots,n-1,\;j=1,\ldots,n.\end{gathered}

The following result, proved in
[
5
]
, addresses the convergence of the
\mathcal{L}
-method.

Theorem 4.1

Let
\mathrm{ker}(I-\nu K)=\{0\}
and
K:C_{u}\to Z_{r}(u)
be bounded for some
r>0
. For any
g\in Z_{s}(u)

s\leq r
equation (
1
) has a unique solution
f^{*}\in Z_{s}(u)
.
If in addition

\max\left\{0,\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right\}\leq\gamma<\min\left\{\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{3}{4},\alpha+1\right\},\quad\max\left\{0,\frac{\beta}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right\}\leq\delta<\min\left\{\frac{\beta}{2}+\frac{3}{4},\beta+1\right\},

(33)

then, for
n
sufficiently large (say
n>n_{0}
), also the finite dimensional equation (
30
) admits a unique solution
f_{n}^{L}\in\mathbb{P}_{n-1}
and the following estimate holds

\|(f^{*}-f_{n}^{L})\|_{C_{u}}\leq{\mathcal{C}}\log n\frac{\|g\|_{Z_{s}(u)}}{n^{s}},\quad{\mathcal{C}}\neq{\mathcal{C}}(n,f^{*}).

(34)

We note that Theorem
4.1
is the analogous of Corollary
3.2
, but its assumptions on the weights
w
and
u
are more restrictive (compare (
33
) with (
12
)–(
14
)). Moreover, in (
34
) we have an extra
\log n
-factor. Finally, we remark that the analogous of Theorem
3.1
cannot be stated for the
\mathcal{L}
-method since, due to the unboundedness of the Lebesgue constants of Lagrange projector, we need
Kf
to be smooth enough to ensure that

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\log n\ E_{n}(Kf)_{C_{u}}=0,\qquad\forall f\in C_{u}.

In conclusion, we point out that the
VP
-method is based on an additional weight
v^{\alpha,\beta}
that can be different from the weight
w
in the equation. Consequently, it can happen that the
VP
-method is applicable whereas the
\mathcal{L}
-method is not. For instance, in the case
u=v^{0,0},\ w=v^{1,1}
, by taking
v^{\alpha,\beta}=w
none of the assumptions (
33
) and (
12
)–(
14
) are satisfied. Hence, the
\mathcal{L}
-method cannot be applied whereas considerable flexibility is available in the implementation of the
VP
-method by choosing the weight
v^{\alpha,\beta}
according to (
12
)–(
14
).

Anyway, in the case that both the
VP
-method and the
\mathcal{L}
-method are applicable with the same weight
v^{\alpha,\beta}
, the numerical tests reveal that the additional logarithmic factor
\log n
does not affect the maximum errors significantly, but the presence of the free parameter
m
may improve the pointwise approximation provided by the
VP
-method.

5
On the computation of the modified moments for some kernels

In this section, we provide some recurrence relations for some families of modified moments
\widetilde{M}_{k}(y)
. First, we recall the three-term recurrence relation satisfied by the sequence
\{p_{k}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma},y)\},

b_{k}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})p_{k}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma},y)=(y-a_{k-1}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma}))p_{k-1}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma},y)-b_{k-1}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})p_{k-2}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma},y),\qquad\ \ k\geq 1.

(35)

with
p_{-1}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})=0,

p_{0}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})=\left(\int_{-1}^{1}v^{\rho,\,\sigma}(x)\,dx\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\left(2^{\rho+\sigma+2}\frac{\Gamma(\rho+1)\Gamma(\sigma+1)}{\Gamma(\rho+\sigma+2)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\begin{array}[]{ll}a_{0}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})=\dfrac{\sigma-\rho}{\rho+\sigma+2},&b_{0}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})=0,\\
a_{1}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})=\dfrac{\sigma^{2}-\rho^{2}}{(\rho+\sigma+2)(\rho+\sigma+4)},&b_{1}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})=\sqrt{\dfrac{4(\rho+1)(\sigma+1)}{(\rho+\sigma+2)^{2}(\rho+\sigma+3)}},\\
a_{k}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})=\dfrac{\sigma^{2}-\rho^{2}}{(\rho+\sigma+2k)(\rho+\sigma+2+2k)},&b_{k}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})=\sqrt{\dfrac{4k(\rho+k)(\sigma+k)(\rho+\sigma+k)}{(\rho+\sigma+2k)^{2}(\rho+\sigma+1+2k)(\rho+\sigma-1+2k)}},\end{array}

(36)

for which we refer to
[
13
, pp. 131–133]
and references therein.

5.1
Weakly singular kernel

A recurrence relation for the modified moments

M_{k}(y)=\int_{-1}^{1}p_{k}(v^{\rho,\sigma},x)\left|x-y\right|^{\mu}v^{\rho,\sigma}(x)\,dx,\qquad\mu>-1,\quad\mu\neq 0,

has been derived in
[
14
]
. Specifically,

b_{k+1}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})\left(1+\dfrac{\mu+1}{k+\rho+\sigma+1}\right)M_{k+1}(y)=(y+\gamma_{k})M_{k}(y)+b_{k}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})\left(\dfrac{\mu+1}{k}-1\right)M_{k-1}(y),\quad k\geq 1,

with

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{(\rho-\sigma)(\rho+\sigma+2\mu+2)}{(2k+\rho+\sigma)(2k+\rho+\sigma+2)}.

The initial moments are

\displaystyle M_{0}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{0}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})(m_{-}(\rho,\sigma,\mu,y)+m_{+}(\rho,\sigma,\mu,y)),

\displaystyle M_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{p_{0}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})}{b_{1}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})}(m_{+}(\rho,\sigma,\mu+1,y)-m_{-}(\rho,\sigma,\mu+1,y))+\dfrac{1}{b_{1}(v^{\rho,\,\sigma})}(y-a_{0})M_{0}(y)

and

\displaystyle m_{-}(\rho,\sigma,\mu,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{\rho}(1+y)^{\sigma+\mu+1}B(1+\sigma,1+\mu)\,_{2}F_{1}\left(-\rho,1+\sigma,\sigma+\mu+2,\dfrac{1+y}{2}\right),

\displaystyle m_{+}(\rho,\sigma,\mu,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{\sigma}(1-y)^{\rho+\mu+1}B(1+\rho,1+\mu)\,_{2}F_{1}\left(-\sigma,1+\rho,\rho+\mu+2,\dfrac{1-y}{2}\right).

In the case of the first kind Chebyshev polynomials, i.e. for modified moments of the type

M_{k}^{T}(y)=\int_{-1}^{1}p\left(v^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}},x\right)|x-y|^{\mu}v^{\rho,\sigma}(x)\,dx,

the following recurrence relation has been derived in
[
11
]

\displaystyle(\rho+\sigma+\mu+k+3)M_{k+2}^{T}(y)=-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}

\displaystyle\bigg[

\displaystyle 2\left((1-y)(\rho+1)-(1+y)(\sigma+1)-ky\right)M_{k+1}^{T}(y)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2\left((1-2y)(\rho+1)+(1+2y)(\sigma+1)-(\mu+1)\right)M_{k}^{T}(y)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2\left((1-y)(\rho+1)-(1+y)(\sigma+1)+ky\right)M_{k-1}^{T}(y)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle(\rho+\sigma+\mu-k+3)M_{k-2}(y)\bigg],\quad k\geq 1.

The required initial moments are

\displaystyle M_{0}^{T}(y)

\displaystyle=m_{-}(\rho,\sigma,\mu,y)+m_{-}(\sigma,\rho,\mu,-y),

\displaystyle M_{1}^{T}(y)

\displaystyle=m_{-}(\rho,\sigma+1,\mu,y)+m_{-}(\sigma+1,\rho,\mu,-y)-M_{0}^{T}(y),

\displaystyle M_{2}^{T}(y)

\displaystyle=2[m_{-}(\rho+2,\sigma,\mu,y)+m_{-}(\sigma,\rho+2,\mu,-y)]

\displaystyle+4[m_{-}(\rho,\sigma+1,\mu,y)+m_{-}(\sigma+1,\rho,\mu,-y)]-7M_{0}^{T}(y),

due to the fact that
M_{-1}^{T}(y)=M_{1}^{T}(y)
and
M_{-2}^{T}(y)=M_{2}^{T}(y)
.

Finally, after computing
\left\{M_{k}(y)\right\}_{k}
, the modified moments
\left\{\widetilde{M}_{k}(y)\right\}_{k}
are obtained by means of a simple basis transformation matrix, as detailed in what follows.

Proposition 5.1

Setting

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{n+m}}=[M_{0}(y),M_{1}(y),\dots,M_{n+m-1}(y)]^{T},

and

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{n+m}}=[\widetilde{M}_{0}(y),\widetilde{M}_{1}(y),\dots,\widetilde{M}_{n+m-1}(y)]^{T},

the following transformation rule holds

\mathcal{\widetilde{M}}_{\mathbf{n+m}}=\bm{C}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{n+m}},

(37)

where the matrix
\bm{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)},
is defined as

\bm{C}=\bm{P}_{v}\bm{\Lambda}_{n+m}\bm{P}_{w},

with

\begin{gathered}\bm{P}_{v}=\left(p_{k,h}\right)_{k,h}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)},\quad p_{k,h}=p_{k}(v^{\alpha,\beta},x_{n+m,h}(w)),\quad k=0,\ldots,n+m-1,\;h=1,\ldots,n+m,\\
\\
\bm{\Lambda}_{n+m}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\lambda_{n+m,1}(w),\ldots,\lambda_{n+m,n+m}(w)\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)},\\
\\
\bm{P}_{w}=\left(\overline{p}_{h,j}\right)_{h,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)},\quad\overline{p}_{h,j}=p_{j}(w,x_{n+m,h}(w)),\quad h=1,\ldots,n+m,\;j=0,\ldots,n+m-1.\end{gathered}

Similarly,
\left\{\widetilde{M}_{k}(y)\right\}_{k}
can be obtained by
\left\{{M}_{k}^{T}(y)\right\}_{k}.

5.2
Logarithmic kernel

Let us now consider modified moments of the following type

M_{k}(y)=\int_{-1}^{1}\log\lvert x-y\rvert\,p_{k}(v^{\rho,\sigma},x)\,v^{\rho,\sigma}(x)\,dx,

(38)

for which the following result holds.

Proposition 5.2

The modified moments defined in (
38

\dfrac{b_{k}(v^{\rho+1,\sigma+1})}{c_{k+1}}M_{k+1}(y)=\dfrac{(y-a_{k-1}(v^{\rho+1,\sigma+1}))}{c_{k}}M_{k}(y)-\dfrac{b_{k-1}(v^{\rho+1,\sigma+1})}{c_{k-1}}M_{k-1}(y),\qquad\text{for}\ k\geq 2,

(39)

with

\begin{split}\qquad M_{1}(y)=&c_{1}\left(2^{\rho+\sigma+4}\frac{\Gamma(\rho+2)\Gamma(\sigma+2)}{\Gamma(\rho+\sigma+4)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(v^{\rho+1,\sigma+1}(y)\,\pi\cot(\pi\rho)-\frac{2^{\rho+\sigma+2}\Gamma(\rho+1)\Gamma(\sigma+2)}{\Gamma(\rho+\sigma+3)}\right.\times\\
&\left.{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-\rho-\sigma-2,\ 1;\ -\rho;\ \frac{1-y}{2}\right)\right),\end{split}

\displaystyle M_{2}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{2}\left(2^{\rho+\sigma+4}\frac{\Gamma(\rho+2)\Gamma(\sigma+2)}{\Gamma(\rho+\sigma+4)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\dfrac{(\rho+\sigma+4)^{2}(\rho+\sigma+5)}{4(\rho+2)(\sigma+2)}}\left[\left(2^{\rho+\sigma+4}\frac{\Gamma(\rho+2)\Gamma(\sigma+2)}{\Gamma(\rho+\sigma+4)}\right)\right.

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\left(y-\dfrac{(\sigma-\rho)(\sigma+\rho-2)}{(\rho+\sigma+4)(\rho+\sigma+6)}\right)\right]\left(v^{\rho+1,\sigma+1}(y)\,\pi\cot(\pi\rho)-\frac{2^{\rho+\sigma+2}\Gamma(\rho+1)\Gamma(\sigma+2)}{\Gamma(\rho+\sigma+3)}\right.\times

\displaystyle\left.{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-\rho-\sigma-2,\ 1;\ -\rho;\ \frac{1-y}{2}\right)\right)

where
{}_{2}F_{1}
denotes the Hypergeometric function, and

c_{k}=\left(\sqrt{k(k+\rho+\sigma+1)}\right)^{-1},\ k\geq 1.

(40)

Moreover, if the weight exponents satisfy the conditions

\rho\in(-1,0),\quad\sigma\in(-1,0)\quad\text{and}\quad\rho+\sigma=-1,

(41)

then the moments admit the explicit representation

M_{k}(y)=c_{k}\pi\left(\,\cot(\pi\rho)\,p_{k-1}(v^{\rho+1,\,\sigma+1},y)+\csc(\pi\rho)\,)\,p_{k+\rho+\sigma+1}(v^{-\rho-1,\,-\sigma-1},y)\right),\qquad\text{for}\ k\geq 1.

(42)

Remark 5.3

We point out that the initial moment
M_{0}(y)
can be found in
[
8
]
for some choices of
\rho,\sigma
.

In this case also, after computing
\left\{M_{k}(y)\right\}_{k}
, the modified moments
\left\{\widetilde{M}_{k}(y)\right\}_{k}
are obtained by using the transformation given in Proposition

.

5.3
Highly oscillating kernel

Finally, we address modified moments of the following type

M_{k}=\int_{-1}^{1}p_{k}\left(v^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}},x\right)e^{i\omega x}v^{\rho,\sigma}(x)\,dx.

By separating the kernel
e^{i\omega x}
into its real and imaginary parts, we obtain moments of the type

\overline{M}_{k}=\int_{-1}^{1}p_{k}\left(v^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}},x\right)\eta(\omega x)v^{\rho,\sigma}(x)\,dx,\quad\eta(\omega x)=\begin{cases}\cos{(\omega x)},\\
\sin{(\omega x)}.\\
\end{cases}

The following five-term recurrence relation holds
[
21
]

i\omega M_{k+2}=-2(k+\rho+\sigma+2)M_{k+1}+2(2\rho-2\sigma+i\omega)M_{k}+2(k-\rho-\sigma-2)M_{k-1}-i\omega M_{k-2},\quad k\geq 2.

Denoting by

G(\rho,\sigma,\omega):=2^{\rho+\sigma+1}e^{-i\omega}\frac{\Gamma(\rho+1)\Gamma(\sigma+1)}{\Gamma(\rho+\sigma+2)}\,_{1}F_{1}(\rho+1,\rho+\sigma+2,2i\omega),

the initial moments are

\displaystyle M_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(\rho,\sigma,\omega),

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(\rho+1,\sigma,\omega)-M_{0},

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\omega}[2(\rho+\sigma+2)M_{1}-(2\rho-2\sigma+i\omega)M_{0}],

\displaystyle M_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\omega}[2(\rho+\sigma+3)M_{2}-(4\rho-4\sigma+i\omega)M_{1}+2(\rho+\sigma+1)M_{0}].

This recurrence relation is forward stable for high values of
\omega
. On the other hand, for
n>2|\omega|
there is a significant loss of precision, so we suggest using
n\sim|\omega|
for better results.

Once the sequence
\left\{M_{k}\right\}_{k}
has been computed, the modified moments
\left\{\widetilde{M}_{k}\right\}_{k}
are retrieved through the transformation detailed in Proposition
5.1
.

6
Numerical tests

In this section, we provide some numerical examples to assess the accuracy of
VP
-method and compare its performance with the
\mathcal{L}
-method of
[
5
]
. Among the extensive set of numerical experiments performed, we selected some representative test cases involving different kernels
k
, various levels of smoothness of the function
g
and different combinations of the weights
u,

w
and
v^{\alpha,\beta}
. Here, the superscripts
VP
and
\mathcal{L}
denote quantities associated with the
VP
-method and the
\mathcal{L}
-method, respectively.

Given a sufficiently fine uniform grid
Y
of points in
[-1,1]
, we compute

\varepsilon_{n}^{VP}(f;y)=\left|\left(f^{*}(y)-f_{n}^{m}(y)\right)u(y)\right|,\qquad\varepsilon_{n}^{L}(f;y)=\left|\left(f^{*}(y)-f_{n}^{L}(y)\right)u(y)\right|,

with
f^{*}
denoting a reference solution, given by the exact solution when available, or by the
VP
-method with
n=1024
and

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Let us first prove that
K^{\mu}:C_{u}\to C_{u}
is a bounded operator.

For
\mu\neq 0
, start from

u(y)|K^{\mu}f(y)|\leq u(y)\|f\|_{C_{u}}\int_{-1}^{1}|x-y|^{\mu}\frac{w(x)}{u(x)}dx=:\|f\|_{C_{u}}T_{\mu}(y).

If
\mu>0
and
w/u\in L^{1}
, then the statement follows from

T_{\mu}(y)\leq{\mathcal{C}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{w(x)}{u(x)}dx\leq{\mathcal{C}}\neq{\mathcal{C}}(y).

Consider now the case
-1<\mu<0
.
If
|y|<1
, the boundedness of
T_{\mu}
follows taking into account that
\frac{w(x)(1-x^{2})^{\mu}}{u(x)}\in L^{1}
implies
\frac{w(x)}{u(x)}\in L^{1}
, and recalling the following lemma

Lemma A
[
2
]
:
For
-1<\mu<0
,
0\leq\tau,\nu<1
, and
-1<y<1
, it is

\int_{-1}^{1}|x-y|^{\mu}v^{-\tau,-\nu}(x)dx\leq[\mathrm{B}(1-\nu,1+\mu)(1+y)^{1+\mu}+\mathrm{B}(1-\tau,1+\mu)(1-y)^{1+\mu}]v^{-\tau,-\nu}(y),

where
\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)=\int_{0}^{1}t^{\alpha-1}(1-t)^{\beta-1}dt,\alpha,\beta>0,
denotes the Beta function.

For
y=\pm 1,
the assumption
w(x)(1-x^{2})^{\mu}\in L^{1}
is necessary to assure the boundedness of
T_{\mu}(\pm 1)
.

Finally, in the case
\mu=0
, the boundedness of
K^{\mu}:C_{u}\to C_{u}
follows as in the previous case, recalling that

\int_{0}^{a}t^{\eta}\log tdt<\infty
, for any
\eta>-1
, and
taking into account that

|\log|x-y||\leq|x-y|^{-\varepsilon},\ \forall\varepsilon>0.

Now we prove that
K^{\mu}:C_{u}\to C_{u}
is a compact operator.
In view of the boundedness of the linear operator
K^{\mu}:C_{u}\to C_{u}
, we have to prove that the set

\mathcal{F}=\{K^{\mu}f:\|f\|_{C_{u}}\leq 1\}

is relatively compact in
C_{u}
.
To this end, since
C_{u}
is a compact metric space, in view of the Ascoli-Arzelà theorem, we have to prove that
F
is a uniformly bounded and a equicontinuous family, i.e.

\sup_{|y|\leq 1}u(y)\int_{-1}^{1}|k^{\mu}(x,y)|\frac{w(x)}{u(x)}<\infty,

(51)

\lim_{y\to y_{1}}|K_{\mu}f(y)u(y)-K_{\mu}f(y_{1})u(y_{1})|=0,\quad\forall y_{1}\in[-1,1].

(52)

The bound (
51
) holds in view of the boundedness of
K^{\mu}
. To prove (
52
), for

Conclusions

In this paper, we introduced and analyzed the
VP
-numerical method, based on the de la Vallée Poussin polynomial approximation, for the solution of second-kind Fredholm integral equations. The proposed approach was motivated by the need to overcome some limitations of the classical
\mathcal{L}
-method, a projection method relying on Lagrange interpolation at Jacobi nodes.

The theoretical analysis established the stability and convergence of the method in suitable weighted uniform spaces. In particular, the approximation error was shown to be governed by the error of the best weighted polynomial approximation, yielding convergence rates comparable with the optimal ones attainable in the considered functional setting. Unlike the
\mathcal{L}
-method, the proposed scheme benefits from uniformly bounded Lebesgue constants, which eliminate the logarithmic growth typically affecting Lagrange operators and provides a more robust approximation framework. Furthermore, the
VP
-method was shown to have a broader applicability, since its convergence can be guaranteed under assumptions that are less restrictive than those required by the corresponding Lagrange-based method, allowing the treatment of wider classes of weight functions and solution behaviors.

From a computational point of view, the
VP
-method leads to well-conditioned linear systems, whose condition numbers do not increase as the discretization dimension increases. This property is particularly relevant for large-scale computations, where numerical stability and reliability play a crucial role in the overall performance of the algorithm.

The numerical experiments fully confirmed the theoretical findings. Although the
VP
-method and the
\mathcal{L}
-method had comparable global maximum errors, the first consistently achieved superior local accuracy. In particular, the damping of Gibbs-type oscillations resulted in more accurate pointwise reconstructions of the solution, especially in critical regions characterized by steep variations or reduced smoothness.

Overall, the proposed method combines favorable approximation properties, stability, wider applicability, and computational efficiency, making it a valuable alternative to classical interpolation-based techniques for Fredholm integral equations.
