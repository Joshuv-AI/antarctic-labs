Extreme weather effects on marine predator breeding outcomes in a global climate change hotspot
Authors: Milan Sojitra, Stuart Corney, Mark Hemer, Sophie Bestley, Sheryl Hamilton, Sam Thalamann, Mary-Anne Lea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extreme weather events (EWEs) are increasing in both intensity and frequency globally. For long-lived, slow-reproducing marine predators, repeated or sequential EWE-driven breeding failures can have population-level consequences. We quantified effects of EWEs on reproductive output and identified temporal windows of vulnerability during breeding in three sentinel species across 14 colonies with varying population trajectories in Tasmania, Australia. Using long-term breeding datasets and daily weather records, we found that Australian fur seals (Arctocephalus pusillus doriferus), short-tailed shearwaters (Ardenna tenuirostris), and shy albatross (Thalassarche cauta) exhibited species- and colony-specific vulnerabilities. Storm surges reduced pup production in a low-lying fur seal colony, extreme rainfall lowered shearwater breeding success, and albatross productivity declined with exposure to extreme heat, rainfall, and wave events. These results highlight the importance of identifying critical extreme weather thresholds and periods of vulnerability to inform ecological forecasting. Proactive, climate-informed management strategies tailored to specific colonies are needed to enhance the resilience of vulnerable populations under accelerating climate change.

INTRODUCTION

Extreme weather events (EWEs)—including heatwaves, severe storms, heavy rainfall, and storm surges—are growing threats to ecological systems worldwide (1). Driven primarily by anthropogenic climate change, their frequency and intensity are projected to increase substantially in coming decades (2). Although typically short-lived (lasting from hours to weeks), EWEs can have profound ecological impacts due to their unpredictability and the limited capacity of many species to respond rapidly (1, 3). Understanding species-specific responses to EWEs is essential for identifying vulnerable taxa and informing effective conservation.

Marine predators that breed terrestrially, such as seabirds and pinnipeds, are vulnerable to climate change because they rely on both marine and terrestrial habitats. This dual dependency increases vulnerability, particularly during breeding when their movements are restricted to breeding sites (4). EWEs can cause direct mortality via heat stress, wind chill, flooding, or drowning, disproportionately affecting sensitive life stages such as chicks and pups (5–9). For example, storm surges frequently cause pinniped pup mortality (9, 10), while burrow-nesting seabirds often experience breeding failure from nest flooding (11–13).

Beyond direct mortality, EWEs can trigger cascading ecological effects with broader ecosystem implications (14). These include disruptions to primary productivity, prey availability, and increased disease risk—factors that may reduce foraging success, body condition, reproductive performance, and survival (15, 16). For instance, a marine heatwave in the northeast Pacific Ocean caused mass common murres (Uria aalge) mortality due to prey depletion and widespread reproductive failure (17) and increased emaciation-related mortality in Guadalupe fur seal (Arctocephalus townsendi) pups (18). However, not all impacts are negative; some species with greater ecological flexibility or broader trophic niches may benefit under certain conditions (19).

Globally, about 27% of seabird species are considered at risk from climate change and EWEs (20), with many pinniped species facing similar threats (21, 22). Vulnerability is shaped by ecological and life history traits, including reproductive strategy, habitat use, and diet (4, 23, 24). Species with specialized niches—such as pelagic seabirds with low reproductive rates or ice-dependent pinnipeds—are particularly susceptible (21, 23, 24). Nest type may also influence risk: cliff nesters are vulnerable to wind and wave impacts (25), burrow nesters to flooding (11–13), and ground nesters to a broad suite of weather extremes (5, 26, 27).

EWE impacts are also shaped by timing, frequency, duration, intensity, and spatial extent. Events coinciding with sensitive breeding stages—incubation, pupping, or rearing—can cause elevated offspring mortality or total breeding failure (7, 8, 13). Repeated or sequential events may compound these effects beyond a single breeding year, influencing juvenile and adult survival and population trajectories (1, 15, 28–30). These effects are especially pronounced for K-selected species such as seabirds and pinnipeds, which are long-lived, slow to reproduce, and invest heavily in a few offspring (31). Under adverse conditions, these species may prioritize adult survival by deferring reproduction. While adaptive for individual fitness, this strategy increases population-level sensitivity to repeated reproductive failure. Although reproductive success is shaped by intrinsic (e.g., age and experience) (32) and extrinsic (e.g., prey availability) factors (33), EWEs are acute, unpredictable stressors that may amplify existing pressures.

Tasmania and its surrounding marine region represent a global climate change hotspot. This region is warming three to four times faster than the global average (34) and is experiencing increased frequency and intensity of EWEs, including extreme rainfall, compound hot-dry extremes, and atmospheric heatwaves (35). It supports breeding populations of numerous seabirds and two pinniped species (36). While climate change threats to these populations are acknowledged (12, 23, 37), important gaps remain in understanding species-specific responses and identifying periods of vulnerability during the breeding season. Filling these gaps is essential for enhancing the resilience of at-risk marine predators in this rapidly changing region and informing targeted conservation and adaptation strategies.

In this study, we investigate the effects of EWEs on reproductive output of three sentinel marine predators breeding on Tasmanian offshore islands: the Australian fur seal (Arctocephalus pusillus doriferus), the yula/short-tailed shearwater (Ardenna tenuirostris), and the shy albatross (Thalassarche cauta). These colonial terrestrially breeding marine predators serve as sentinel species because they rely on both terrestrial and marine habitats, and their reproductive output provides sensitive, timely indicators of ecosystem health and climate change impacts across both environments (38). We focus on these species due to the availability of long-term datasets and the exposure of the study colonies to varying climatic conditions across Tasmania and the Bass Strait.

We use a sliding window approach to identify periods during which reproductive output is most sensitive to EWEs. This method evaluates one climate variable at a time across many possible temporal windows, allowing the best-supported climate window to be selected on the basis of model comparison (39). Weather and oceanographic data were sourced from the nearest weather stations and reanalysis products appropriate for each colony. To isolate the contribution of EWEs from broader temporal influences, and to ask whether extreme weather explains variation in reproductive output beyond shared directional trends over time, we complemented sliding window models with structural equation models (SEMs). These SEMs decompose the association between year and reproductive output into two pathways: an EWE pathway, representing the indirect effect mediated by EWEs, and the other pathway, representing the direct net effect of year due to all other drivers.

Specifically, we aim to (i) quantify colony-specific effects of EWEs on reproductive output, (ii) identify temporal windows of vulnerability, (iii) assess long-term trends in reproductive output and EWE characteristics during these windows, and (iv) evaluate the influence of interannual climate variability and longer-term climate change on observed reproductive trends. This integrative approach reveals which species, colonies, and breeding stages are most vulnerable to EWEs, laying a foundation for targeted conservation and adaptive management under accelerating climate change.

RESULTS

Our study encompassed 14 breeding colonies across Tasmania and the Bass Strait, comprising six Australian fur seal, five short-tailed shearwater, and three shy albatross populations and associated Australian Bureau of Meteorology (BOM) weather stations (Fig. 1 and table S1). The analysis revealed species- and colony-specific responses to EWEs, with distinct temporal windows during which reproductive output was significantly affected.

Among the six Australian fur seal colonies, we detected significant EWE effects on pup productivity only at Tenth Island (Table 1). Among the five short-tailed shearwater colonies, we detected significant EWE effects on indicative breeding success only at East Kangaroo Island (Table 1). At this colony, cumulative very wet precipitation days were associated with reduced indicative breeding success (Fig. 2B), with breeding success declining by an estimated 0.103 ± 0.02 during the best climate window (Pc = 0.007; cross-validated R2 = 0.44; tables S1 and S3). The best and median climate windows aligned with the incubation to early chick-rearing period (Fig. 3B).

All three colonies exhibited colony-specific EWE effects on reproductive output (Table 1). At Albatross Island, breeding success declined with increases in both cumulative extremely wet precipitation days (−0.069 ± 0.024; Pc = 0.006; cross-validated R2 = 0.46; Fig. 2C, Table 1, and table S3) and warm days (−0.009 ± 0.003; Pc = 0.015; cross-validated R2 = 0.44; Fig. 2D, Table 1, and table S3), coinciding with incubation to early broodguard and broodguard to chick-rearing stages, respectively (Fig. 3C). At Pedra Branca, increases in cumulative extreme wave energy were associated with reduced chick count (Fig. 2E), with chick numbers declining by an estimated 0.063 ± 0.013% per kW/m during the best climate window (Pc = 0.017; cross-validated R2 = 0.46; Table 1 and table S3), spanning from late incubation to early chick-rearing (Fig. 3C). At Mewstone, increases in cumulative wet day precipitation during hatching and broodguard stages were associated with reduced chick count (Figs. 2F and 3C), with chick numbers declining by 0.79 ± 0.10%/mm during the best climate window (Pc = 0.042; cross-validated R2 = 0.51; Table 1 and table S3).

Long-term trends in Australian fur seal pup production varied among the colonies. Pup numbers increased at Bull Rock (7.99 ± 1.7%/year, P < 0.001; Fig. 4A) and Reid Rocks (2.91 ± 1.12%/year, P = 0.01; Fig. 4B), while a decline was recorded at Tenth Island (−1.24 ± 0.63%/year, P = 0.045; Fig. 4C). No temporal trends in pup productivity were evident at other colonies.

Temporal trends in EWE characteristics

We found no significant temporal trends in any EWE characteristics (cumulative frequency, intensity, or total duration) within the median climate windows for Tenth Island, East Kangaroo Island, or Pedra Branca. In contrast, two shy albatross colonies exhibited significant trends in EWE characteristics (Fig. 5). At Albatross Island, the frequency of warm days increased by 1.9 ± 0.92%/year, and their total duration increased by 2.62 ± 1.26%/year (both P = 0.039; Fig. 5A) within the median climate window. At the Mewstone, cumulative intensity (−1.43 ± 0.32%/year, P < 0.001; Fig. 5C) and total duration (−0.64 ± 0.32%/year, P = 0.001; Fig. 5D) of wet days declined within the median climate window.

Effects of temporal change in EWE on reproductive output

We used path analysis to quantify how temporal changes in significant EWEs contributed to observed changes in reproductive output across colonies (Fig. 6 and table S4). For Australian fur seals at Tenth Island, extreme wave energy increased over time (0.40 ± 0.16, P = 0.010) and negatively affected pup productivity (−0.61 ± 0.13, P < 0.001; Fig. 6A and table S4). Changes in pup productivity over time were mostly (76.79%) due to the EWE Pathway through extreme wave energy. After accounting for the indirect effect, the direct effect of year on pup productivity was minimal and nonsignificant (−0.07 ± 0.11, P = 0.516; Fig. 6A and table S4).

For short-tailed shearwaters at East Kangaroo Island, very wet precipitation days had a strong negative effect on indicative breeding success (−0.71 ± 0.13, P < 0.001), although their temporal trend was nonsignificant (−0.29 ± 0.22, P = 0.18; Fig. 6B and table S4). Changes in indicative breeding success over time were mostly (73.8%) mediated by the EWE Pathway through very wet precipitation days; the direct effect from Other Pathway was nonsignificant (0.07 ± 0.13, P = 0.58; Fig. 6B and table S4).

Shy albatross colonies exhibited distinct climate influence patterns (Fig. 6C and table S4). At Albatross Island, extremely wet precipitation days and warm days had strong direct negative effects on breeding success (−0.40 ± 0.15, P = 0.010; −0.39 ± 0.15, P = 0.008, respectively), as did year (−0.30 ± 0.15, P = 0.045; Fig. 6C and table S4). The EWE Pathways through extremely wet precipitation days and warm days accounted for 20.37% and 19.9%, respectively, of the total change in breeding success, while the remaining effect (59.73%) was due to the Other Pathway. Temporal trends in both EWEs were nonsignificant (0.26 ± 0.17, P = 0.13; 0.26 ± 0.21, P = 0.22, respectively; Fig. 6C and table S4).

At Pedra Branca, extreme wave energy increased over time (0.60 ± 0.17, P = 0.001), but its direct effect on chick productivity was nonsignificant (−0.06 ± 0.12, P = 0.63; Fig. 6C and table S4). Temporal changes in chick count were largely (96.04%) driven by the direct negative effect of year (−0.82 ± 0.13, P < 0.001) (Fig. 6C and table S4).

At the Mewstone, wet day precipitation had a strong negative effect on chick count (−0.92 ± 0.19, P < 0.001), although its temporal trend was nonsignificant (−0.47 ± 0.31, P = 0.13; Fig. 6C and table S4). Most of the temporal change in chick productivity (91.65%) was driven by EWE pathway, while the direct effect of year was nonsignificant (−0.04 ± 0.17, P = 0.82; Fig. 6C and table S4).

DISCUSSION

Our study demonstrates that terrestrially breeding marine predators in Tasmania are vulnerable to EWEs, within defined climate windows, during which these events significantly affect reproductive output. Marked species- and colony-specific responses underscore differential sensitivities across taxa, populations, and life-history stages, emphasizing that variability in reproductive output—not a uniform climatic response—is the central signal emerging from this system. This underscores the urgency of developing nuanced, population-specific conservation strategies.

Australian fur seal

Endemic Australian fur seals, which breed on low-lying offshore rocky platforms in southeastern Australia, are among the most geographically constrained seal species. At Tenth Island, pup productivity declined significantly in response to extreme wave events during peak pupping and early rearing (late November to early January). This aligns with the median pupping date at Tenth Island (26 November), with 90% of pups born within a 48-day window between 2 November and 20 December (10). These events likely reflect storm surges or wave wash, causing pup mortality through inundation, separation from mothers, or being washed into the sea. This site is at carrying capacity and low-lying (maximum elevation 10 m; Table 1), exacerbating exposure to inundation under projected sea level rise (40). Previous studies have documented pup mortality from high waves and storm surges (10, 41, 42); our findings quantify this relationship with a specific vulnerability window. Whether vulnerability is driven by physical hazard (e.g., wave height) or biological sensitivity (e.g., pup age) remains unclear. Future research linking fine-scale oceanography with age-specific pup survival is needed to disentangle these mechanisms.

In contrast, no significant EWE effects were detected at other fur seal colonies. This likely reflects differences in topographic exposure, productivity growth, or data constraints. Judgement Rocks and West Moncoeur Island are steeply elevated (maximum of 30- and 90-m elevations, respectively; Table 1), potentially offering natural protection against storm surge and wave wash. Other low-lying sites such as Reid Rocks and Bull Rock (12- and 5-m maximum elevations, respectively; Table 1) are still experiencing population growth (Fig. 4, A and B), which may obscure EWE-driven productivity declines. Moriarty Rocks, although low-lying (maximum elevation of 7.6 m; Table 1), did not show a statistically significant climate window for extreme wave events (0.58 ± 0.12%, best window: 28 November to 4 February, Pc = 0.12). This is likely influenced by data constraints, as monitoring at this colony has been infrequent (18 censuses over 36 years with uneven gaps), in contrast to the annual surveys at Tenth Island, potentially hindering the detection of EWE impacts. Notably, previous studies have suggested storm-related pup mortalities at Moriarty Rocks (10, 42), and pup numbers fluctuate markedly (range: 80 to 1035), suggesting that storm surge could be one of the contributing factors despite the absence of a clear statistical signal. Increased monitoring frequency, such as annual censuses, may be required to enhance detection of EWE effects at this colony.

Changes in pup productivity may also reflect variation in birth and survival rates and site-specific threats, with multiple stressors (such as foraging overlap and bycatch in fisheries, aquaculture interactions, disease, and climate-driven strengthening of the East Australian Current) likely acting synergistically (41). Although Australian fur seals are strongly philopatric, long-term monitoring shows a southward shift in breeding distribution, with new colonies expanding through female emigration—a pattern attributed to broad-scale environmental change (40). Consequently, some movement away from colonies with poor breeding performance is plausible, even if this dispersal remains constrained by strong natal site fidelity.

Yula/short-tailed shearwater

For burrow-nesting short-tailed shearwaters, we found significant negative effects of extreme precipitation on breeding success at East Kangaroo Island during incubation and early chick-rearing. While burrows buffer against thermal extremes (43), they remain susceptible to collapse and flooding during intense rainfall, increasing energetic costs, hypothermia risk, and breeding failure (11, 44). At East Kangaroo Island, all survey transects occur on generally flat terrain with areas of sparse vegetation potentially exposing some burrows more to the elements, and patches of the introduced African boxthorn (44), possibly reducing drainage.

We found no significant EWE effects at Big Green, Little Dog, Little Green, and Maatsuyker islands, although earlier work using monthly rainfall data reported that increased precipitation led to reduced breeding success at several Furneaux Group colonies (44, 45). In contrast, our analyses based on daily weather records did not detect comparable rainfall-driven effects at Big Green, Little Dog, or Little Green Islands, potentially reflecting differences in data resolution, analytical sensitivity, or colony-specific environmental buffering and microclimate (see below). Little Dog and Little Green islands have deep soils and more natural vegetation (44), likely aiding drainage and protection for burrows. At Big Green, despite being largely cleared for grazing (44), soil is compacted by stock, and burrows tend to be deep, which may buffer against surface flooding.

At Maatsuyker Island, the absence of EWE effects may partly reflect strong habitat heterogeneity combined with limited temporal data. Only 11 years of monitoring are available for this colony, and the nine breeding transects are distributed across forest/scrub, pigface, and Poa tussock habitats. Field observations indicate that the south-facing tussock slopes are likely more exposed to intense rainfall and runoff compared to forested areas, suggesting that susceptibility to precipitation-driven breeding failure may vary between habitats within this colony. This within-site variability, combined with the short time series, may reduce the power to detect colony-level climate signals even when localized impacts occur.

Shy albatross

Endemic shy albatross exhibited colony-specific responses. At Albatross Island, extremely wet precipitation days and warm days reduced breeding success. These findings align with previous research that identified critical thresholds—maximum air temperatures above 23°C (46) and wet-bulb temperatures above 20°C (5)—as key drivers of heat stress affecting reproductive output. Our analysis further refines this understanding by pinpointing specific windows of vulnerability: exposure to extreme heat during broodguard to early chick-rearing stages and extreme rainfall during incubation to early broodguard stages. During the broodguard to early chick-rearing stages, chicks’ natal down, adapted for cold conditions, provide limited protection against extreme heat (47), rendering them particularly vulnerable on exposed rocky terrain during hot, sunny, and calm-wind conditions. Extreme rainfall can flood or compromise nest structures, especially in localized flood-prone areas within the colony (48), with the greatest risk occurring during incubation and early broodguard stages.

The Mewstone, a remote granite island rising to 200 m with sharp ridgelines and steep cliffs, supports the largest shy albatross colony (49) and is located in one of the wettest regions in Australia ((50), see fig. S1). Cumulative wet day precipitation during hatching and broodguard was negatively associated with chick productivity, similar to patterns at Albatross Island and in other albatross species such as black-browed (Thalassarche melanophris) (26) and wandering (Diomedea exulans) albatrosses (27). Downy chicks lack waterproofing, making them vulnerable to hypothermia if exposed, and intense rainfall can saturate exposed nesting ledges and flood nests. Heavy rainfall has also been shown to reduce nest attendance in seabirds (51), potentially further compounding risk.

Although both Albatross Island and the Mewstone shy albatross colonies showed sensitivities to EWEs, the precise windows of vulnerability differ. At Albatross Island, breeding success was most sensitive to extremely wet precipitation days (precipitation >99th percentile), spanning incubation through early broodguard. At the Mewstone, wet day precipitation (precipitation ≥1 mm) during hatching to broodguard had the greatest impact. This difference is likely driven by variation in rainfall intensity and patterns (fig. S1), in combination with local microclimate and colony topography. These site-specific patterns highlight the need for further targeted data collection to better understand the mechanisms driving vulnerability.

Pedra Branca, the smallest and declining shy albatross colony, is a narrow granite islet ∼270-m long, 100-m wide, and rising to 55-m, with most nesting habitat confined to exposed ridgelines and ledges (49). Located off Tasmania’s south coast—Australia’s most wave-exposed region due to its southernmost position and direct exposure to the largest and most energetic swells from Southern Ocean storms (52, 53)—this site experienced reduced chick productivity associated with extreme wave energy. Camera evidence from the Marine Conservation Program (MCP) documented nest loss during large wave events (see fig. S2). Although extreme wave energy did not have a significant direct effect on chick productivity in the path analysis, it increased substantially over time, and there was a strong negative direct effect of year on productivity. This suggests that while extreme wave energy has intensified, it does not fully explain the observed temporal decline in productivity, pointing to additional environmental or ecological drivers warranting further investigation.

One possible driver could be potential interspecific competition with Australasian gannets (Morus serrator). As generalist foragers capable of relaying after nest failure and often producing multiple clutches (54), gannets may be more resilient to environmental variability than shy albatross. Their overlap with shy albatross may result in spatial competition, nest usurpation, or disturbance, particularly problematic at such a spatially constrained site. This highlights how interacting climatic and ecological stressors may be driving sustained reproductive decline at Pedra Branca. Further research should focus on high-resolution digital elevation models to identify exposed nesting sites and assess interspecific competition, thereby informing conservation strategies.

Ecological consequences of regional EWE patterns

Globally, seabird and pinniped populations are increasingly affected by EWEs (6, 9, 23, 28), which are increasing in frequency, spatial extent, and intensity under climate change (2). Coastal breeding habitats—home to many marine predators—are especially vulnerable (55), particularly where colonies are spatially constrained and already at their carrying capacity (e.g., Tenth Island, Moriarty Rocks, and Pedra Branca). Tasmania sits at the junction of diverging rainfall trends: drying in the west and wetting in the east (56, 57). In the northeast (e.g., Furneaux Group), rainfall is driven by westerlies and episodic easterly systems, with variability linked to El Niño–Southern Oscillation and atmospheric blocking (58). Projections suggest more frequent and intense rainfall due to increased tropical moisture and a southward-shifted East Australian Current (58). Conversely, the southwest (e.g., Maatsuyker and the Mewstone) is projected to experience declining warm-season rainfall (50, 59) and reduced intensity of compound rainfall events due to southward shift of the storm track (57). These trends align with our observed declines in precipitation extremes within median climate windows at the Mewstone across years (Fig. 5, C and D).

These opposing trends have site- and species-specific implications. In the northeast, frequent extreme rainfall may increase burrow collapse and flooding risk, potentially reducing breeding success if current patterns persist. In contrast, projected drying in the west may reduce flood risk at southwestern colonies and Albatross Island, potentially mitigating precipitation-related breeding failure. However, drier conditions could also lead to nest bowl degradation and reduced vegetation, which may negatively affect breeding success and decrease thermal buffering. At Albatross Island, there has already been an increase in the frequency and duration of warm days (Fig. 5, A and B), and future warming is expected to further exacerbate these conditions (2, 35). This trend raises the likelihood of heat-related breeding failures for the Albatross Island population. Overall, the interaction of changing EWE patterns with species-specific life histories reinforces the need for colony-specific impact assessments alongside adaptive management.

Improving monitoring and understanding mechanisms

Although we identify clear relationships between EWEs and reproductive output, some data limitations exist. For example, for some colonies, pup and chick counts are not standardized by breeding attempts, meaning that variation in broader demographic processes—including adult survival, recruitment, and emigration—may also influence observed productivity trends. For short-tailed shearwater and shy albatross colonies, breeding success indices are based on surveys conducted during egg-laying and prefledging periods, providing conservative estimates since some chicks may fail to fledge after surveys are completed.

Breeding success data from Furneaux Group short-tailed shearwater colonies (e.g., Big Green, Little Dog, Little Green, and East Kangaroo Islands) were based on transect sampling intended to index rather than comprehensively quantify breeding success. Sampling is unlikely to consistently revisit the same burrows annually or within a census (December versus March survey), the March sample was halved, and observer effort may also vary. Remote shy albatross colonies such as the Mewstone and Pedra Branca face logistical constraints that limit temporal sampling resolution.

To improve EWE impact detection, we advocate for standardized measures of breeding success where possible, particularly for Furneaux shearwater colonies, and increasing monitoring frequency, such as annual fur seal censuses at Moriarty Rocks. At Maatsuyker Island, assessing how breeding success varies among different habitat types—for example, burrow susceptibility to flooding across forest, scrub, and tussock habitats—could provide insight into habitat-specific risks.

Framing risk in terms of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability offers a valuable approach. Biologically meaningful thresholds—such as wave height, rainfall, or temperature thresholds beyond which breeding failure occurs—help define hazard, but risk also depends on periods of biological sensitivity (e.g., downy chick or pup age) and colony-specific exposure (e.g., site elevation, drainage, or vegetation). Disentangling these factors will require integrating demographic, behavioral, and fine-scale environmental data. Incorporating habitat characteristics—such as elevation, slope aspect, soil type, vegetation cover, and nest or burrow structure—into monitoring will enhance understanding of how microhabitats influence exposure and resilience to EWEs (43, 60). Central to this approach is the development of predictive triggers combining climatic thresholds with biological sensitivity to support timely and responsive management to ameliorate impacts.

While our focus was on EWE effects on reproductive output, these marine predators face additional year-round threats: fisheries bycatch, entanglement in marine debris, habitat degradation, pollution, changes in prey availability, and emerging diseases (12, 20, 21, 23, 41, 49), as well as potential impacts from offshore wind farm development in key habitats (61). These multiple pressures can interact with EWE-driven stress, potentially amplifying impacts on reproduction and long-term population resilience. Recognizing EWE impacts within this broader threat context is therefore essential, as EWEs represent an intensifying pressure on these populations and should be explicitly integrated into conservation risk assessments and management plans.

Targeted conservation and adaptation strategies

Our identification of colony-specific climate windows and the types of EWEs most strongly affecting reproduction provides a direct pathway to targeted conservation and climate adaptation planning. Because these climate windows indicate when breeding stages are most sensitive, monitoring efforts and adaptation measures can be strategically timed before or during periods of heightened risk.

The results suggest that vulnerability may be mediated by colony characteristics, which can support the development of site-level sensitivity indices to guide conservation prioritization. For example, for fur seal colonies, low-elevation colonies with limited vertical escape from storm surge could be classified as higher risk and prioritized for disturbance avoidance and habitat protection (41, 62). Similarly, for shearwater and albatross colonies, burrow or nest substrate properties, drainage capacity, and vegetation cover could be integrated into risk assessments to identify areas most likely to experience higher breeding failure during intense rainfall or heat events (44, 48).

Site-specific adaptation measures are feasible in several colonies to enhance breeding colony resilience. For instance, at the East Kangaroo Island shearwater colony, the removal of invasive African boxthorn and restoration of native Poa grassland may improve burrow stability during extreme rainfall years. At Albatross Island, artificial nests for shy albatross have been trialled (in 2017) to reduce the risk of breeding failure from poor quality nest bowls and to offset climate-related declines in breeding success. For vulnerable shy albatross chicks, additional heat stress mitigation measures, such as temporary misting during extreme heat events, could follow approaches used for northern royal albatross (Diomedea sanfordi) at Taiaroa Head, New Zealand (63). For highly wave-exposed and topographically constrained shy albatross colonies such as Pedra Branca, where in situ mitigation options are extremely limited, assisted colony establishment and translocation to more suitable sites may provide a viable long-term climate adaptation strategy (64, 65).

In summary, synthesizing across taxa, our results reveal a clear pattern: While all species inhabit the same climatic region, the mechanisms and magnitudes of EWE effects on reproductive output differ substantially. Wave-driven mortality dominates in Australian fur seals; rainfall extremes drive failure in short-tailed shearwaters; and extreme heat, precipitation, and wave events limit shy albatross productivity. This diversity of responses, within a relatively small geographical area, underscores the complexity of climate-reproductive linkages in sympatric terrestrially breeding marine predators. This study demonstrates that biologically meaningful climate impacts can be detected even where demographic datasets are coarse when integrated with high-resolution daily weather records. The approach presented here is broadly applicable to other animal species globally, particularly where long-term census datasets exist but fine-scale demographic monitoring is logistically challenging. As EWEs intensify under climate change, approaches that identify climate vulnerability windows, site-specific sensitivities, and thresholds for reproductive failure will be essential for informing conservation prioritization and climate adaptation strategies. Future work should incorporate microhabitat-scale modeling, refine EWE thresholds, and develop adaptive monitoring frameworks capable of responding dynamically to weather extremes at effective spatiotemporal scales.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We examined how EWEs influence reproductive output in three marine predator species, Australian fur seal, short-tailed shearwater, and shy albatross, using breeding and census data from the MCP, Department of Natural Resources and Environment Tasmania (NRE Tas), and the Friends of Maatsuyker Island (FOMI, Wildcare Inc.). In this study, year refers to the breeding season, which spans two calendar years, and is designated by the year when egg-laying or pupping commenced (e.g., 2016 indicates the 2016–2017 breeding season).

Study populations and breeding data

Australian fur seal

Australian fur seals, currently listed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List, breed annually at 14 colonies around Tasmania, predominantly on offshore islands (41). They exhibit a single annual pupping period during the austral summer, with ∼90% of pups born between 2 November and 20 December, and no births recorded after December (10). The MCP has conducted annual pup censuses since 1989 using ground, aerial, or capture-mark-recapture techniques, depending on colony accessibility. For this study, we analyzed data from the six colonies with ≥10 years of pup productivity data, excluding breeding seasons with missing weather data or those corresponding to newly established colonies (see section 1.1 in the Supplementary Materials). Missing census date stamps were replaced with colony-specific median monitoring dates.

Yula/short-tailed shearwater

The migratory short-tailed shearwater, currently listed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List, breeds in southeast Australia with a highly synchronized breeding cycle. Following annual migration from Northern Hemisphere austral winter foraging areas, breeding colonies are occupied by 25 September, with peak egg-laying in late November, hatching in mid-January, and fledging from late April (66). The MCP conducts biseasonal surveys (early December and early March) using standard 100 m–by–2 m transects on four islands in the Furneaux Group, with a breeding success index calculated from burrow occupancy in March (%) as a proportion of burrow occupancy in December (%). Transects are not fixed, and locations may vary slightly between surveys. The FOMI conducts biseasonal surveys in three marked study plots on Maatsuyker Island, with breeding success calculated as the number of burrows with chicks in March as a proportion of the number of burrows with incubating adults in December.

Shy albatross

The shy albatross, currently listed as Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List, breeds annually on only three islands globally—all located in Tasmania: Albatross Island, Pedra Branca, and the Mewstone. The breeding season includes egg laying from late August to early October, hatching from late November to mid-December, and fledging in late April to early May (49, 67). Albatross Island has been monitored annually with consistent methodology since 1998 using ground surveys and aerial photography, with nest counts conducted around 1 October and chick counts around 1 April. Because of their remote locations and limited accessibility, the Mewstone and Pedra Branca are monitored via aerial surveys conducted in late March to early April to count prefledging chicks. Missing monitoring date stamps were replaced with colony-specific median dates.

Notes on breeding data

The breeding metrics analyzed in this study varied in the degree of standardization across taxa. For short-tailed shearwaters and the Albatross Island colony of shy albatross, breeding success parameters were estimated from two standardized surveys—one conducted during the egg-laying period and another near the end of the breeding season when most chicks were close to fledging. This approach provides a robust and consistent index of reproductive performance, reflecting the proportion of active burrows or nests that successfully produced prefledging chicks. A small number of chicks may have failed to fledge after surveys were completed. For Australian fur seals and the two aerially surveyed albatross colonies (the Mewstone and Pedra Branca), pup or chick counts provide annual indices of productivity that are not standardized for breeding attempts, as comprehensive individual-based data collection is not feasible at these largely inaccessible colonies due to logistical constraints. These approaches reflect established monitoring protocols and provide sound, long-term indicators suitable for reproductive outputs at the population level.

Weather data

We obtained weather data from the BOM Australia, the BARRA2 reanalysis dataset (68), and the Collaboration for Australian Weather and Climate Research (CAWCR) wave hindcast dataset (69). BOM stations were selected based on data availability and proximity to each breeding colony (Fig. 1 and table S1). Daily and synoptic (1 to 24 observations per day) weather records were aggregated into daily summaries to incorporate meteorological variables not available in standard daily datasets.

Solar radiation data from the nearest BARRA2 grid cells were combined with BOM records to calculate wet-bulb globe temperature and apparent temperature. Wave energy and direction data were derived from the nearest CAWCR hindcast grid cells.

Extreme weather indices

We calculated extreme weather indices based on BOM definitions. Percentile-based thresholds were derived from 5-day moving windows across the annual cycle using a 30-year climatological baseline where possible. The heatwaveR package (70) was used to compute these percentile-based indices. For each extreme weather index, two datasets were generated, a binary version in which days exceeding the threshold were coded as 1 (extreme) and 0 otherwise and a continuous version in which values below the threshold were set to zero, while values above the threshold were retained. This produced two representations per EWE: one capturing the occurrence of extreme conditions, and the other preserving the magnitude of extreme values for those days. EWEs considered in this study are listed in table S2.

Statistical analysis

Sliding window analysis

To identify the timing and duration of climate sensitivity, we applied a sliding window approach using the R package climwin (71) and following the workflow outlined in (39). We treated annual reproductive output as the biological response and used extreme weather indices derived from daily weather data as predictors. We fitted baseline models using linear models (LMs) for breeding success (72) and generalized LMs (GLMs) with a negative binomial error structure for count data (e.g., chicks or pups).

Absolute time windows were applied across all colonies. For each extreme weather index, we applied the sum function to aggregate values across each candidate window. This approach was chosen to capture the cumulative effects of extreme conditions, which are not adequately captured by mean values. We tested only linear relationships for all EWE indices, as the aim was to assess whether cumulative exposure to extreme events affected reproductive output. For circular predictors such as wave direction, we tested both linear and quadratic terms to capture potential nonlinear effects from directional wraparound. We also applied a custom aggregate statistic to calculate circular means for these predictors.

All candidate windows were restricted to biologically relevant periods of the breeding season for each site. The maximum range of candidate climate windows—i.e., how far back in time to search for climate effects—was defined relative to a reference date (day 0), which was colony specific and set as the latest monitoring date across the entire study period for each colony. The furthest point back from day 0, representing the earliest point considered in the climate window search, was determined by species- and colony-specific breeding phenology or monitoring effort. These furthest points were set as 26 October for Australian fur seals based on the first recorded pup birth at Tenth Island (10); 1 December for short-tailed shearwaters, corresponding to when burrow occupancy by incubating adults is assessed; and 1 October for shy albatross, aligning with the census of incubating adults. We allowed the duration of candidate windows between this start point and day 0 to vary.

We ran models separately using both binary and continuous representations of each EWE index, fitting all candidate temporal windows for each index. This produced a best-supported window model for each EWE index based on Akaike Information Criterion difference (ΔAICc) comparisons among the candidate windows. We then compared the best-supported binary and continuous models for each EWE index and selected the overall best model as the one with the lowest ΔAICc compared to the null model, provided that the window duration exceeded 14 days. This window duration threshold was applied to avoid biologically implausible short-term effects that can lead to statistical artifacts (39). We defined the best climate window as the temporal window in the best-supported model with the lowest ΔAICc. We also identified a median climate window, defined as the central window among models within 95% of the lowest ΔAICc value. The best window identifies the most parsimonious effect period, while the median window provides a robust summary of the informative period across top-performing models.

To evaluate the robustness of detected relationships and control for the elevated risk of false positives from repeated testing of overlapping windows, we assessed model significance using a randomization approach. We applied the randwin function with 10 randomizations of the response variable dates. This approach removes any underlying relationship between climate and the response variable, generating a null distribution of model fits. We used the “C” metric to calculate Pc values representing the probability that the observed ΔAICc could arise by chance in the absence of a true relationship. We retained models with Pc ≤ 0.05 for interpretation. To account for potential biases and overfitting, we applied 10-fold cross-validation to derive R2 values. Where multiple EWE predictors produced robust models, we assessed multicollinearity using Pearson correlation coefficients. For correlated pairs with |r| ≥ 0.7, we retained only the variable with the lowest ΔAICc to minimize redundancy in model selection and subsequent interpretation.

Temporal trends

We summarized significant EWEs within the median climate windows identified for each population, rather than across the full breeding season, to ensure a conservative approach. We evaluated long-term trends in EWE frequency, total duration, and cumulative intensity using the full available weather data for each variable within its defined median window. We tested for autocorrelation using the Durbin-Watson statistic. Where autocorrelation was present, we fitted generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) with an autocorrelation structure using the glmmTMB package (73). Otherwise, we applied simpler GLMMs without autocorrelation. We selected model families and error structures based on the distribution properties and characteristics of each response variable (details in the Supplementary Materials).

Path analysis

Although sliding window analyses quantify relationships between EWE and reproductive output, they may overlook potential influences from unmeasured, nonclimatic factors. To account for these shared temporal patterns, we applied SEMs using the sem function in the lavaan R package (74). For each population with significant EWE effects, we incorporated the best-supported model output into a site-specific SEM. We standardized all SEM variables (mean = 0, SD = 1) and assumed Gaussian distributions to allow direct comparisons of path coefficients (75).

Our SEMs were fully specified, so conventional overall fit indices could not be calculated. Instead, we verified that each path was plausible, residuals were approximately normal, and SEs were reasonable following (76), criteria that were met by all models. We estimated SEs using nonparametric bootstrapping with 1000 iterations. Our SEMs explicitly partitioned the effect of time (year) on reproductive output into two pathways: a direct effect (other pathway: year → reproductive output), reflecting nonclimatic influences, and an indirect effect (EWE pathway: year → EWE → reproductive output), representing climate-driven effects.

We quantified the “total effect” of year on reproductive output as the sum of all indirect effects plus any remaining direct effect. To determine the contribution of a specific climate pathway to temporal changes in reproductive output, we calculated the proportion of that pathway’s effect relative to the sum of absolute values of all pathways (76). For instance, at Tenth Island, the EWE Pathway accounted for 0.246 of a total 0.32 effect, representing 76.79% of the observed change in pup productivity over time. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.2. (77), and the detailed analytical workflow is provided in the Supplementary Materials.
