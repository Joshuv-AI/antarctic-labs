Uncovering Neuropathic Pain in CANVAS: Lessons from a Case-Based Literature Review
Authors: Panayiota Neophytou, Stefania Kalampokini, Constantinos Avraam, Andreas Anagiotos, Constantinos Deltas, Christiana Ioannou, Christiana Polydorou, Georgios M. Hadjigeorgiou, Panagiotis Zis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCerebellar ataxia with neuronopathy and vestibular areflexia syndrome (CANVAS) is a rare, late-onset neurodegenerative disorder caused by biallelic intronic AAGGG repeat expansions in the RFC1 gene. Its phenotype is heterogeneous, ranging from isolated sensory neuronopathy to the full clinical triad. Although neuronopathy is present in the majority—if not all—of the cases, the prevalence and clinical significance of neuropathic pain remain insufficiently characterized.MethodsWe report a genetically confirmed case of CANVAS in a 58-year-old woman presenting predominantly with asymmetrical neuropathic pain. Clinical, neurophysiological, vestibular, and genetic assessments were performed, with longitudinal follow-up over 3 years. Additionally, a case-driven systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. A search was conducted in May 2026 in two databases (MEDLINE and Scopus) that identified cohorts of CANVAS cases with data on pain. Fourteen studies met our inclusion criteria. Pooled prevalence of neuropathic pain was calculated using a random-effects model.ResultsThe systematic review included 445 patients with CANVAS. The pooled prevalence of neuropathic pain was 43.2% (95% confidence intervals 34.0–52.9%) with moderate heterogeneity across these studies (I2 = 65%). Evidence from included studies suggests that pain may occur early and represents a significant contributor to disease burden.ConclusionsNeuropathic pain is a frequent and potentially early manifestation of CANVAS, likely reflecting underlying sensory neuronopathy. CANVAS should be considered in patients with idiopathic sensory neuronopathy, even when pain is present. Early recognition and targeted genetic testing are essential to improve diagnosis and optimize patient management in this underdiagnosed condition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40122-026-00867-2.

Introduction

Cerebellar ataxia with neuronopathy and vestibular areflexia syndrome (CANVAS) is a rare disorder of recessive autosomal inheritance, caused by biallelic intronic AAGGG repeat expansion, ranging from 400 to more than 2000 repeats, in the replication factor C subunit 1 (RFC1) gene, located on chromosome 4 [1, 2].

The disease is neurodegenerative and seems to follow a spatial progression, from early involvement of sensory neurons to the later appearance of vestibular and cerebellar dysfunction [3]. Not all patients exhibit the complete triad of symptoms. However, when genetic criteria are applied, the phenotypic spectrum of CANVAS appears to be broader, including patients with isolated neuronopathy [4]. In a recent series of genetically confirmed cases, Cortese et al. reported that 63% exhibited the complete CANVAS triad, while 15% showed isolated sensory neuronopathy, 16% combined ataxia and neuronopathy, and 6% combined vestibular areflexia and neuronopathy [3]. Chronic cough is a very common symptom in CANVAS.

The disease is slowly progressive but often disabling, with half of the patients needing walking aid after 10 years of disease duration and a quarter being wheelchair dependent after 15 years [3]. Excluding cough, the age of onset of other symptoms is around 52 years, whereas the symptom duration at the time of diagnosis is approximately 11 years [4]. Therefore, CANVAS is considered a late-onset ataxic disorder; the ataxic syndrome is explained by a combination of cerebellar dysfunction, proprioceptive deficit due to sensory neuronopathy, and/or vestibular deficit.

However, the timing of onset of its components varies, and the diagnosis may be challenging at the beginning [4]. The most common presenting neurological symptoms are postural imbalance, dizziness, and/or falls, but patients may mainly complain of sensory symptoms or oscillopsia [4]. Progressive imbalance is the most common complaint and the presenting symptom in half of the cases [1]. Evidence of bilateral vestibular impairment is the severely reduced or absent vestibulo-ocular reflex [4]. A long-standing, non-productive cough with normal ENT and pulmonary investigation may precede the ataxic manifestations for decades [3, 4]. Cough may result from vagal neuronopathy and hypersensitivity of the upper airway and esophageal pathways secondary to denervation [3, 6, 7].

Sensory symptoms occur because of degeneration of neurons from the dorsal root ganglia [8]. Clinically, evidence of sensory neuronopathy is given by the non-length-dependent pure sensory deficits, including impairment of vibration sensation, joint position sensation, pinprick perception, sensory ataxia and, less frequently, temperature sensation [3, 4]. More than two-thirds of individuals complain of sensory symptoms, such as loss of feeling, pins and needles, and neuropathic pain, in many cases since the onset of the disease [1, 3]. Nerve conduction studies (NCS) confirm the presence of sensory neuronopathy [4]. Autonomic dysfunction in CANVAS is supposed to be part of the primary sensory neuronopathy and is usually encountered in the later stages of the disease [9, 10].

Cerebellar symptoms in CANVAS likely reflect progressive multisystem neurodegeneration with late cerebellar involvement; magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) commonly shows vermian-predominant cerebellar atrophy, and pathological studies demonstrate Purkinje cell loss; however, the exact molecular effect of the RFC1 intronic repeat expansion remains unresolved [2, 5].

In this paper, we report a genetically confirmed case of CANVAS from Cyprus of a middle-aged woman with predominantly neuropathic pain at presentation. Furthermore, we conducted a systematic review of the literature of all published CANVAS cases focusing on the association with pain in an attempt to assess the prevalence of pain in CANVAS syndrome.

Methods

This study is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.

Selection Processes and Eligibility Criteria

A systematic literature search was performed on May 18, 2026, in the MEDLINE and Scopus databases to identify reports of CANVAS associated with pain. In MEDLINE, two Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms were used; Term A was ‘CANVAS or RFC1’ and Term B was ‘pain or painful or neuropathy or ganglionopathy or neuronopathy’. Scopus search terms were: Term A: ‘RFC1 or CANVAS’ and term B was ‘pain or painful’. No filters were applied. The reference lists of articles that met the eligibility criteria were further screened to identify additional studies that may fall within the scope of this review.

All articles eligible to be included in this review met the following inclusion criteria: (1) human subjects were involved, (2) the full text was retrieved in English, and (3) articles reported CANVAS case series and provided information on the presence or absence of pain. Non-original articles (i.e., review articles, guidelines) and single case reports were excluded from this review.

Articles were screened independently by three authors. Any uncertainty about inclusion eligibility was resolved through discussion between the reviewers to reach consensus.

Data Collection Process

Data were extracted from each study in a structured coding scheme using Microsoft Excel. Data collected included the number of CANVAS patients and the number of patients with symptoms regarding ataxia, vestibulopathy, neuronopathy, and pain.

Study Risk of Bias Assessment

We evaluated the methodological quality of prevalence studies using a checklist from Hoy et al. [11]. It consists of ten questions that assess the representativeness of the sample, the sampling technique, the response rate, the data collection method, the measurement tools, the case definitions, and the statistical reporting. Each checked question was scored either as “Y”, “N” or “U” for YES, NO or UNCLEAR, respectively. Every “Y” scored 1 point. The total score ranged from 0 to 10, with the overall score categorized as follows: 7–10 = low risk of bias, 4–6 = moderate risk, and 0–3 = high risk of bias. The quality assessment is available as Supplementary material.

Synthesis of Results

This study used aggregate data where possible. Statistical calculation of pooled proportions was conducted in R language using the default settings of the ‘‘meta’’ package and the ‘‘metaprop’’ function with a random effects model [12]. Forest plots are presented for each meta-analysis along with the I2 statistic, which is used to evaluate heterogeneity [13]. This statistic suggests whether chance or study heterogeneity is responsible for the variation. Negative I2 values are set to zero, and values range between 0 and 100% [13]. Heterogeneity can be quantified as low, moderate, and high, with upper limits of 25%, 50%, and 75% for I2, respectively [13]. Often, data did not lend itself to meta-analysis and therefore a narrative approach was taken.

Ethical Considerations

Written informed consent was obtained for the case presentation. Ethical approval was not required for the systematic review, as it was based exclusively on previously published data.

Case Presentation

A 58-year-old woman presented in the neurological department of Nicosia General Hospital in Cyprus, complaining of tingling, pins and needles, cramps, and burning sensation in the lower extremities as well as asymmetric neuropathic pain in the left thigh for approximately 5 years. The pain had neuropathic characteristics, such as getting worse at night and having exacerbations, including electric shock-like sensations. She had a medical history of gastroesophageal reflux and had previously been investigated by pulmonologists for chronic spasmodic dry cough, which started at least 10 years before, with inconclusive etiology.

On neurological examination, she was alert and oriented. No abnormal findings were noted on cranial nerve examination. She had muscle strength scoring 5/5 MRC (Medical Research Council) scale in all limbs. She had present tendon reflexes, including ankle reflexes. There was mild hypoesthesia (light touch and pain) in the left leg, mainly in the thigh and the foot. She had abolished vibration perception and reduced monofilament perception of the lower extremities. There were no signs of sensory or cerebellar ataxia. There were no pyramidal signs.

NCS showed asymmetrically attenuated sensory nerve action potentials (non-length-dependent sensory neuronopathy). Sympathetic skin response (SSR) was normal in the palm and foot. Quantitative sensory testing (QST) showed abnormal cold thermal thresholds in the hands and the left foot and abnormal warm sensation in the left hand. SNAP sensory nerve action potential

On otorhinolaryngological evaluation, the vHIT revealed deficits of the posterior semicircular canals on both sides, indicating bilateral posterior vestibular nerve dysfunction. The reduced vestibulo-ocular-reflex (VOR) of the posterior canals was recorded by the reduced gain with abnormal catch-up saccades. HIT responses were normal for the horizontal and anterior semicircular canals. Interestingly, the patient had an additional left-sided mixed hearing loss, as evident in the pure-tone audiogram, attributed to unilateral otosclerosis.

The patient fulfilled both the Camdessanché (total score 6.6) [14] and the Zis criteria [15] (two SNAP asymmetries) for sensory neuronopathy.

Due to the presence of sensory neuronopathy, the patient underwent our department’s protocol for identifying the cause [16], including hematological studies (full blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, vitamin B12, and folate levels); biochemical investigations (fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, renal function, liver function, thyroid function, serum protein electrophoresis, serum angiotensin-converting enzyme, oral glucose tolerance test, and urinary Bence–Jones protein analysis); coeliac disease screening (gliadin IgA, gliadin IgG, tissue transglutaminase IgA, and endomysial antibodies); Sjögren's syndrome screening (anti-ENA); and immunological testing (antinuclear antibodies, anti-neuronal antibodies, anti-dsDNA antibodies, rheumatoid factor, p-ANCA, c-ANCA, anti-HIV, and anti-HCV antibodies). All were negative.

Tumor screening including CT of the thorax and abdomen was also negative. Based on these, a genetic test was conducted by using two different assays: (a) Sanger sequencing analysis of all coding regions of the RFC1 gene (25 exons) to identify pathogenic variants associated with the genetic disease followed by (b) gDNA fragment analysis method, regarding the typical repetition of the intronic sequence of pentanucleotide repeats (AAGGG)n in intron 2 of the RFC1 gene. Systematic Review

Study Selection Process

The above-mentioned literature search strategy revealed 394 results. During the abstract screening for eligibility assessment, 237 articles were excluded. During the full-text screening 143 more articles were excluded. Ultimately, a total of 14 studies met our inclusion criteria and were included [3, 7, 17–28]. These articles were published between 2018 and 2026. None of these studies had high risk of bias during quality assessment. Discussion

The AAGGG allele frequency in the general population is 0.7–6.8%, resulting in an estimated prevalence of biallelic carriers at birth, at risk of developing the disease ranging from 1 in 20.000 to 1 in 200 individuals, suggesting that the disease is, if not largely underdiagnosed, possibly misdiagnosed [3]. In this paper, we report a case of CANVAS in which the initial symptom prompting the patient to seek medical attention was neuropathic pain. A review of the existing literature did not identify any previously reported cases in which pain was documented as the first clinical manifestation of CANVAS. However, this does not necessarily indicate that pain cannot be present early in the disease course; rather, it may reflect under-documentation of pain in clinical practice. Motivated by this observation, we conducted a systematic review of the literature to determine the pooled prevalence of pain in CANVAS. This was found to be 43.2%, suggesting that sensory neuronopathy in the context of CANVAS can be painful.

RFC1-related disease appears to unmask a distinct pattern of small fiber neuronopathy (SFN) that may contribute substantially to neuropathic pain. Recent pathology and skin biopsy data show that RFC1 mutations are associated with early and widespread loss of somatic small afferents, including both thinly myelinated A delta (Aδ) and unmyelinated C fibers, while autonomic efferent fibers are relatively spared at least in early stages. This is in keeping with our case, where although nociceptive and thermal small fiber involvement was clinically evident, sudomotor involvement (as assessed through the SSR) was not present. This predominantly sensory afferent SFN likely underlies the frequent reports of neuropathic pain, allodynia, burning dysesthesias, and reduced thermal sensitivity among RFC1 carriers, with a non-length-dependent pattern of nociceptive dysfunction that resembles sensory neuronopathy rather than classic distal symmetric SFN [29–31].

Mechanistically, repeat expansion mediated RFC1 dysfunction is thought to impair DNA replication and repair, leading to chronic stress and selective vulnerability of dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons and their peripheral terminals, which are enriched for small fiber subtypes. The striking epidermal and proximal dermal denervation documented in RFC1 patients, even in the absence of severe large fiber involvement, suggests that pure small fiber loss can drive disabling pain and sensory symptoms, and that skin biopsy documented SFN may be an early and sensitive biomarker that can heighten clinical suspicion for RFC1-related disease. [3, 8, 30]

Magy et al. confirmed near complete loss of intraepidermal nerve fiber density in skin biopsy and important loss of small myelinated and unmyelinated fibers in the sural or radial nerve in a small number of patients [22].

Curro et al., reported that the vast majority of RFC1-positive patients (98%) had symptoms of sensory neuronopathy at onset including distal numbness, tingling or pins and needles and pain [20]. In this cohort, the pain prevalence increased from one-quarter of patients at disease onset to approximately 40% at evaluation later on, over a follow-up period of approximately 9 years.

It is noteworthy that all patients in the case series of Magy et al. described neuropathic pain as one of the most disabling symptoms. Indeed, neuropathic pain is an important determinant for the quality of life of patients [22, 32].

In our case, the pain was asymmetrical, which is not surprising given the fact that sensory neuronopathies – at least in the beginning – present with asymmetrical sensory symptoms. This can be demonstrated electrophysiologically, where the presence of asymmetrically attenuated sensory nerve action potentials, with normal motor conduction studies, should raise the suspicion of a sensory neuronopathy [5, 20].

Limitations

The pooled prevalence of pain should be interpreted with caution due to the moderate heterogeneity observed across studies. This heterogeneity likely reflects the fact that pain is not systematically assessed or reported in CANVAS cohorts. Moreover, the included studies did not consistently describe how pain was assessed or whether validated pain assessment tools, such as DN4 or painDETECT, were used. Consequently, we relied on the authors' reports that the pain experienced by patients was related to CANVAS, without being able to independently verify its neuropathic nature or other pain characteristics. Furthermore, the absence of pain was often not explicitly reported, introducing the possibility of reporting bias and potentially affecting the accuracy of the pooled prevalence estimate. Future studies should systematically assess and report both the presence and absence of pain in CANVAS using validated instruments. In addition, studies should also provide detailed information regarding pain intensity, distribution, temporal characteristics, and associated sensory symptoms to facilitate more accurate characterization of pain in this population.

Conclusions

CANVAS should be considered in the differential diagnosis of any idiopathic sensory neuronopathy, even when neuropathic pain is present. Genetic testing, once the disease is suspected, is crucial; noteworthy is that pathogenic RFC1 AAGGG repeat expansions cannot be detected by sequence-based multigene panels or exome sequencing [1]. Timely diagnosis is crucial for optimal patient management, especially in the early disease stages, when the results of the neurodegenerative processes are likely more amenable to therapeutic interventions.
