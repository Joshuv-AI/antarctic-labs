Semiparametric Inference under Dual Positivity Boundaries: Nested Identification with Administrative Censoring and Confounded Treatment Lin Li ∗


Abstract
When a long-term outcome is administratively censored for a substantial fraction of a study cohort while a short-term intermediate variable remains broadly available, the target causal parameter can be identified through a nested functional that integrates the outcome regression over the conditional intermediate distribution, avoiding inverse censoring weights entirely. In observational studies where treatment is also confounded, this nested identification creates a semiparametric structure with two distinct positivity boundaries — one from the censoring mechanism and one from the treatment assignment — that enter the efficient influence function in fundamentally different roles. The censoring boundary is removed from the identification by the nested functional but remains in the efficient score; the treatment boundary appears in both. We develop the inference theory for this dual-boundary structure. Three results are established. First, a fluctuation coupling proposition shows that when a targeted estimator solves the nested score equation, the rate of the intermediate regression depends on the outcome model quality but not on the treatment propensity quality at leading order, because the score residuals have conditional mean zero under any propensity weighting (Proposition 3). Second, the doubly robust remainder decomposes into two bilinear products with an asymmetric three-component robustness geometry: the treatment propensity must be consistent at any rate for n \sqrt{n} -consistency, but correct outcome modelling alone — sufficient in the single-boundary case — places the estimator at the product-rate boundary when the propensity is misspecified, producing a near-boundary regime with persistent sandwich undercoverage (Theorem 4). Third, the two remainder components can interact through a covariance term c 12 c_{12} that is positive when both boundaries concentrate in the same covariate region, amplifying the variance gap beyond the sum of individual contributions (Proposition 6). The leave-one-out jackknife captures the full dual-boundary variance and restores nominal coverage uniformly across all regimes. Simulations calibrated to observational comparative effectiveness studies confirm that the dual-boundary regime produces variance inflation exceeding the single-boundary case, and that the nested identification strategy achieves lower near-boundary severity than standard inverse-weighted estimators when administrative censoring is the dominant positivity concern. Keywords: semiparametric inference; positivity boundary; nested identification; administrative censoring; doubly robust estimation; variance decomposition; jackknife; targeted learning.

Introduction

The missing-data structure

A recurring structure in longitudinal studies and comparative
effectiveness research involves two distinct sources of incomplete
observation operating simultaneously. The first is administrative
censoring: a primary outcome requires extended follow-up, and a
non-trivial fraction of individuals exit the study before the
outcome is ascertained, creating a censoring probability
g_{\Delta}

that can approach zero for late enrollees or short-enrollment
subgroups. The second is treatment confounding: in observational
settings, treatment assignment depends on measured covariates, and
the propensity score
g_{A}
can approach zero or one in subgroups
with strongly confounded treatment selection.

Each source alone is well understood. Administrative censoring is
addressed by inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW;

Robins et al. 1994
;
Robins and Rotnitzky 1995
),
which becomes unstable as
g_{\Delta}\to 0
. Treatment confounding
is addressed by inverse propensity weighting or doubly robust
augmentation (
Bang and Robins 2005
;

Chernozhukov et al. 2018
), which becomes unstable as
g_{A}\to 0

or
1
. Standard doubly robust estimators combine both corrections
by inverse-weighting with the product
g_{\Delta}^{-1}g_{A}^{-1}
,
compounding the two positivity concerns.

The interaction between these two sources — when they are
simultaneously active and potentially concentrated in the same
covariate region — has received less theoretical attention. This
paper develops the inference theory for that interaction in the
context of a nested identification strategy that separates the two
boundaries into distinct roles.

Prevalence

The dual-boundary structure is common. In pharmacoepidemiologic
studies of statin initiation, nearly half of elderly patients
discontinue therapy within the first year

(
Benner et al. 2002
)
; those who disenroll contribute no long-term
cardiovascular outcome data while their 3-month LDL cholesterol is
already recorded. In comparative effectiveness studies of diabetes
therapies,
Dagan et al. 2018
report that over 58% of a
40,000-patient cohort were censored within 12 months. In both
settings, a short-term biomarker is available for a substantially
larger fraction of the cohort because it is measured before the
censoring event occurs.

Nested identification and the two boundaries

When a short-term intermediate variable
S
satisfies a
surrogate-mediated missing-at-random condition (
\Delta\perp Y\mid S,A,W
), the causal parameter can be identified through
a nested functional:

\Psi(P_{0})=E_{W}\bigl[E_{S|A=1,W}[\bar{Q}_{Y}(S,1,W)]-E_{S|A=0,W}[\bar{Q}_{Y}(S,0,W)]\bigr],

(1)

where
\bar{Q}_{Y}(S,a,W)=E[Y\mid S,A\!=\!a,W,\Delta\!=\!1]

(
Gilbert et al. 2008
;
Athey et al. 2020
;
Li 2026a
).
This avoids
g_{\Delta}^{-1}
entirely: the censoring propensity does
not appear in the identification.

The treatment propensity
g_{A}
, however, remains in the efficient
influence function through the clever covariates that define the
targeting step. This creates a structural asymmetry: one boundary
(censoring) is removed from the identification but retained in the
score; the other (treatment) appears in both. The resulting
structure differs from both the classical single-propensity doubly
robust problem and from the multiply robust framework of

Tchetgen Tchetgen and Shpitser 2012
, where nuisance components enter
symmetrically.

A further complication is specific to nested functionals. The
identification (
1
) involves an outer integration over
the conditional distribution of
S
. A one-step debiased estimator
plugging in separate estimates
\hat{\bar{Q}}_{Y}
and
\hat{f}_{S}
produces a
cross-product remainder
R_{SY}
with no doubly robust complement
that cross-fitting does not remove (
Li 2026a
,
Proposition 1). A two-stage targeting construction can absorb

R_{SY}
algebraically without estimating
f_{S}
, but in the
observational setting the targeting step uses
\hat{g}_{A}
-weighted
empirical means, coupling the intermediate regression to the
propensity quality.

Contributions

We make four contributions to semiparametric missing-data
methodology.

First, the fluctuation coupling
(Proposition
3
).

When a targeted estimator solves the nested score equation, the
post-targeting intermediate regression converges at rate

O_{P}(r_{Q}+n^{-1/2})
regardless of propensity quality. The
mechanism is that the score residuals have conditional mean zero
given the variables that the propensity depends on, so any
propensity reweighting preserves the CLT rate. The actual cost of
wrong propensity weights is not bias but an irreducible

O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
term that places the second remainder at the
product-rate boundary.

Second, the three-component robustness geometry
(Theorem
4
,
Corollary
5
).

The remainder decomposes into
R_{2}^{(1)}
(outcome
\times

censoring) and
R_{2}^{(2)}
(intermediate
\times
treatment).
The treatment propensity must be consistent at any rate — a
necessary condition with no analog in the single-boundary case.
The geometry is three-component but not multiply robust in the
sense of
Tchetgen Tchetgen and Shpitser 2012
: no single correct component
guarantees
\sqrt{n}
-consistency.

Third, the dual near-boundary variance decomposition
(Proposition
6
).

The sampling variance includes two remainder-variance terms and
their covariance
c_{12}
. We show
c_{12}>0
when both
boundaries concentrate in the same covariate region, so the total
gap exceeds the sum of individual contributions.

Fourth, uniform jackknife validity
(Theorem
10
).

The jackknife is consistent for the total variance across all four
regimes (strong decay, single-censoring, single-treatment, dual)
and the Wald interval achieves exact coverage uniformly.

Related work

Doubly robust estimation.

The classical theory (
Robins et al. 1994
;

Bang and Robins 2005
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
) addresses a
single missing-data mechanism with two-component robustness. The
nested identification generates a remainder
R_{SY}
with no doubly
robust complement, and the observational treatment creates a
three-component structure.

Multiply robust estimation.

Tchetgen Tchetgen and Shpitser 2012
develop estimators consistent if any
one of
K
models is correct. The geometry here is different: no
single component suffices. This is a form of asymmetric partial
robustness.

Positivity violations.

Weight truncation
(
Crump et al. 2009
)
, modified treatment rules

(
Díaz and van der Laan 2012
)
, and overlap weighting

(
Li et al. 2018
)
address single-propensity boundaries.
The present work extends the variance-decomposition approach of

Li 2026b
from one boundary to two interacting boundaries.

Nested identification.

Athey et al. 2020
develop the surrogate index without censoring
or positivity concerns;
Li 2026a
develop the estimator for
randomized trials with known treatment assignment. Neither
addresses the dual-boundary structure.

2
Nested Identification under Two Positivity Boundaries

We formalize the semiparametric structure described in Section 1.
The observational cohort is the motivating instance; the
identification theorem and the dual-boundary framework apply to any
setting where a nested functional is combined with administrative
censoring and confounded treatment.

2.1
Observed-data structure

Consider a cohort of
J
clusters (e.g., clinics), each
contributing
m
individuals, for
N=Jm
total observations. The
observed data for individual
i
in cluster
j
are

O_{ij}=(W_{ij},A_{ij},S_{ij},\Delta_{ij},\Delta_{ij}Y_{ij})\sim P_{0},

(2)

where
W_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are baseline covariates,
A_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
is the treatment received,
S_{ij}
is the short-term
intermediate variable,
\Delta_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
indicates whether
the long-term outcome was observed, and
Y_{ij}
is that outcome.
A cluster random effect
b_{j}\sim N(0,\sigma_{b}^{2})
induces
within-cluster correlation. Cluster-level vectors
O_{j}=\{O_{ij}\}_{i=1}^{m}
are independent across
j
.

The target parameter is the average treatment effect

\Psi(P_{0})=E[Y_{ij}(1)-Y_{ij}(0)]
.

2.2
Conditions defining the dual-boundary structure

Five conditions formalize the setting.

Assumption 1

(Consistency)
.

For
A_{ij}=a
:
S_{ij}=S_{ij}(a)
and
Y_{ij}=Y_{ij}(a)
.

Assumption 2

(No unmeasured confounders)
.

(Y(0),Y(1),S(0),S(1))\perp A_{ij}\mid W_{ij}
.

Assumption 3

(Surrogate-mediated MAR)
.

\Delta_{ij}\perp Y_{ij}\mid S_{ij},A_{ij},W_{ij}
.
This is the structural condition enabling the nested identification:
once the intermediate variable is observed, censoring carries no
additional information about the outcome.

Assumption 4

(Censoring support positivity)
.

P(\Delta=1\mid S,A,W)\geq c_{\Delta}>0
on the relevant
support. This is a support condition for estimating
\bar{Q}_{Y}
, not
an inverse-weighting condition: the nested identification avoids

g_{\Delta}^{-1}
.

Assumption 5

(Treatment positivity)
.

c_{A}\leq g_{A}^{0}(a\mid w)\leq 1-c_{A}
for some
c_{A}>0
.
Unlike Assumption
4
, this is an inverse-weighting
condition:
g_{A}^{-1}
appears in the targeting step. This is the
second positivity boundary.

The asymmetry between Assumptions
4

and
5
is the structural source of the dual-boundary
phenomenon: the nested identification removes
g_{\Delta}^{-1}
from
the target parameter (Assumption
4
is a support
condition only) but retains
g_{A}^{-1}
in the efficient score
(Assumption
5
is an inverse-weighting condition).

2.3
Identification

Theorem 1

(Identification via the nested functional)
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
5
:

\Psi(P_{0})=E_{W}\bigl[E_{S|A=1,W}[\bar{Q}_{Y}(S,1,W)]-E_{S|A=0,W}[\bar{Q}_{Y}(S,0,W)]\bigr],

(3)

where
\bar{Q}_{Y}(S,a,W)=E[Y\mid S,A\!=\!a,W,\Delta\!=\!1]
.

Proof.

By Assumptions
1
–
2
,

E[Y(a)\mid S(a),W]=E[Y\mid S,A\!=\!a,W]
. By
Assumptions
3
–
4
,

E[Y\mid S,A\!=\!a,W]=E[Y\mid S,A\!=\!a,W,\Delta\!=\!1]
.
Integrating over
P(S\mid a,W)
and marginalizing gives
(
3
).
∎

Remark 1

(What the nested functional separates)
.

The identification (
3
) does not contain
g_{\Delta}^{-1}
.
The censoring propensity enters the EIF (Section 3) but not the
identification. The treatment propensity
g_{A}^{-1}
appears in the
targeting clever covariates. The two boundaries therefore serve
different structural roles:
g_{\Delta}
is a nuisance for efficient
estimation;
g_{A}
is a nuisance for both identification (through
Assumption
2
) and estimation. This asymmetry
propagates through the remainder structure and the robustness
geometry.

3
Semiparametric Theory

We develop the EIF, the remainder decomposition, and the robustness
structure for nested functionals under the dual-boundary
conditions of Section 2.

3.1
Efficient influence function

The individual-level EIF components are, for
a\in\{0,1\}
:

\displaystyle D_{Y,a}(O)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{I}(A\!=\!a)\,\Delta}{g_{A}(a\mid W)\,g_{\Delta}(S,a,W)}\bigl\{Y-\bar{Q}_{Y}(S,a,W)\bigr\},

(4)

\displaystyle D_{S,a}(O)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{I}(A\!=\!a)}{g_{A}(a\mid W)}\bigl\{\bar{Q}_{Y}(S,a,W)-\bar{Q}_{\mathrm{int}}(a,W)\bigr\},

(5)

\displaystyle D_{W,a}(O)

\displaystyle=\bar{Q}_{\mathrm{int}}(a,W)-\Psi_{a},

(6)

with
D^{*}(O)=\sum_{a}(-1)^{1-a}(D_{Y,a}+D_{S,a}+D_{W,a})
.
The derivation follows the tangent-space factorization

\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{W}\oplus\mathcal{T}_{A}\oplus\mathcal{T}_{S}\oplus\mathcal{T}_{\Delta}\oplus\mathcal{T}_{Y}
.
The pathwise derivative in the
\mathcal{T}_{\Delta}
direction
vanishes under Assumption
3
: the EIF has no
censoring-mechanism component. In clustered data, the
cluster-level EIC is
\mathrm{EIF}_{j}=\sum_{i}D^{*}(O_{ij})
.

Both boundaries are visible in the EIF:
g_{\Delta}
enters the
denominator of
D_{Y,a}
(first boundary);
g_{A}
enters the
denominator of both
D_{Y,a}
and
D_{S,a}
(second boundary).

3.2
Remainder decomposition

After two-stage targeting (Section 4), the nested cross-product

R_{SY}
involving the conditional intermediate distribution is
eliminated algebraically (Lemma
2
). The surviving
bilinear remainder is

R_{2}=\underbrace{\int\bigl(\hat{\bar{Q}}_{Y}^{*}-\bar{Q}_{Y}^{0}\bigr)\Bigl(\frac{\hat{g}_{\Delta}}{g_{\Delta}^{0}}-1\Bigr)\,dP_{0}}_{\displaystyle R_{2}^{(1)}:\;\text{censoring boundary}}\;+\;\underbrace{\int\bigl(\hat{\bar{Q}}_{\mathrm{int}}^{*}-\bar{Q}_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\bigr)\Bigl(\frac{\hat{g}_{A}}{g_{A}^{0}}-1\Bigr)\,dP_{0}}_{\displaystyle R_{2}^{(2)}:\;\text{treatment boundary}}.

(7)

The two products correspond to the two boundaries. In the
single-boundary case (known
g_{A}
),
R_{2}^{(2)}\equiv 0
; the
second product is the new object of study in the dual-boundary
setting.

Lemma 2

(Algebraic elimination of
R_{SY}
)
.

The second targeting step solves

P_{n}\bigl[D_{S,a}(O;\,\hat{\bar{Q}}_{Y}^{*},\,\hat{\bar{Q}}_{\mathrm{int}}^{*},\,\hat{g}_{A})\bigr]=0

for each
a
, eliminating the cross-product

R_{SY}=\int(\hat{\bar{Q}}_{Y}-\bar{Q}_{Y}^{0})(\hat{f}_{S}-f_{S}^{0})\,d\mu\,dP_{0}

without estimating
f_{S}
. The elimination holds for any
\hat{g}_{A}
:
it is a consequence of the score equation being solved, not of

\hat{g}_{A}
being correct.

3.3
Fluctuation coupling in nested functionals

The central new phenomenon in the dual-boundary setting is the
interaction between the targeting step and propensity estimation.
The following result characterizes this interaction for general
estimators of nested functionals.

Proposition 3

(Fluctuation coupling)
.

Let
\hat{\Psi}
be a targeted estimator of the nested
functional (
3
) that solves the score equation for

D_{S,a}
using
\hat{g}_{A}
-weighted empirical means. Under
Assumptions
4
–
5
:

\bigl\|\hat{\bar{Q}}_{\mathrm{int}}^{*}-\bar{Q}_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\bigr\|_{P_{0}}=O_{P}(r_{Q}+n^{-1/2}),

(8)

where
r_{Q}=\|\hat{\bar{Q}}_{Y}^{*}-\bar{Q}_{Y}^{0}\|_{P_{0}}
. The rate
does not depend on
\|\hat{g}_{A}-g_{A}^{0}\|_{P_{0}}
.

The proof proceeds in four steps.
Step 1:
the initial
intermediate regression
\hat{\bar{Q}}_{\mathrm{int}}^{(\mathrm{init})}

targets
E[\hat{\bar{Q}}_{Y}^{*}(S,a,W)\mid A,W]
by regression, which does
not involve
\hat{g}_{A}
; the
L^{2}
contraction of conditional
expectation gives
\|\hat{\bar{Q}}_{\mathrm{int}}^{(\mathrm{init})}-\bar{Q}_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\|=O_{P}(r_{Q}+n^{-1/2})
.
Step 2:

the score residuals
\hat{\bar{Q}}_{Y}^{*}-\hat{\bar{Q}}_{\mathrm{int}}^{(\mathrm{init})}
have conditional mean

O_{P}(n^{-\beta})
given
(A,W)
. For any weight function

\omega(A,W)
— including
\hat{g}_{A}^{-1}
regardless of its
correctness — the weighted empirical mean is
O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
by
the CLT.
Step 3:
the fluctuation parameter satisfies

|\varepsilon_{S}|=O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
by the implicit function theorem.

Step 4:
the logistic Lipschitz bound (constant
1/4
)
gives the perturbation as
O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
uniformly. The full
proof is in Web Appendix A.

Remark 2

(The structural role of conditional mean zero)
.

The coupling result depends on a single structural property: the
score residuals have small conditional mean given the covariates
that the propensity depends on. This property holds for any
estimator of a nested functional where the intermediate regression
is constructed as a conditional mean of the outcome regression,
regardless of the specific targeting mechanism. It is a feature
of the nested identification (
3
), not of a particular
estimator.

Remark 3

(What propensity misspecification costs)
.

The cost is not bias but rate limitation. When
\hat{g}_{A}
is
inconsistent (
r_{g_{A}}\to c>0
), the
n^{-1/2}
fluctuation term
multiplied by
c
gives
R_{2}^{(2)}=O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
— at the
product-rate boundary of
Li 2026b
, not inconsistency. The
point estimator remains consistent; the variance estimator is
affected.

3.4
Three-component robustness geometry

Theorem 4

(Product-rate conditions for the dual-boundary functional)
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
5
and bounded
outcomes/nuisances, the targeted estimator is asymptotically linear
provided two product-rate conditions hold:

\displaystyle\text{(PR-1)}\quad r_{\bar{Q}_{Y}}\cdot r_{g_{\Delta}}

\displaystyle=o_{P}(n^{-1/2}),

(9)

\displaystyle\text{(PR-2)}\quad(r_{\bar{Q}_{Y}}+n^{-1/2})\cdot r_{g_{A}}

\displaystyle=o_{P}(n^{-1/2}).

(10)

(PR-1) is the standard censoring–outcome product from the
single-boundary case. (PR-2) is new: it couples the outcome
model rate to the treatment propensity rate through the fluctuation
term
n^{-1/2}
. When
g_{A}
is known, (PR-2) is vacuous.

A key consequence: (PR-2) requires
r_{g_{A}}=o_{P}(1)
— the
treatment propensity must be consistent, at any rate. No
convergence rate is required beyond consistency, but consistency
itself is necessary. This has no counterpart in the single-boundary
case.

Corollary 5

(Sufficient configurations and their regimes)
.

(a)
Standard rates: all three at

o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
. Both conditions hold; strong decay.

(b)
Good outcome, consistent propensity:

r_{\bar{Q}_{Y}}=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
,
r_{g_{A}}=o_{P}(1)
. (PR-2)
holds; (PR-1) requires
r_{g_{\Delta}}=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
.

(c)
Both propensities correct, outcome
inconsistent:

r_{g_{\Delta}}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
and
r_{g_{A}}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})

— both at the
superparametric
rate — with

r_{\bar{Q}_{Y}}=\Theta(1)
. Then (PR-1):
\Theta(1)\cdot o_{P}(n^{-1/2})=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
; (PR-2):
({\Theta(1)+n^{-1/2}})\cdot o_{P}(n^{-1/2})=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
. Both hold,
but the superparametric rate requirement
o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
on
both propensities is substantially stronger than the standard

o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
needed in (a): it is achievable by correctly
specified parametric models but not by typical nonparametric
learners. True outcome-free robustness is thus available in
principle but fragile in practice.

(d)
Good outcome, inconsistent propensity:

r_{\bar{Q}_{Y}}=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
,
r_{g_{A}}\to c>0
. The
estimator is consistent, but
R_{2}^{(2)}
is at the product-rate
boundary:
n\cdot\mathrm{Var}(R_{2}^{(2)})\to c_{2}>0
. The
sandwich undercovers; the jackknife is required.

Remark 4

(Contrast with classical and multiply robust structures)
.

Classical two-component double robustness: correct outcome OR
correct propensity suffices. Multiply robust estimation
(
Tchetgen Tchetgen and Shpitser 2012
): any one of
K
correct models
suffices. The three-component geometry here: no single correct
component suffices, and the conditions are asymmetric across
components.
\bar{Q}_{Y}
enters both (PR-1) and (PR-2);

g_{\Delta}
enters only (PR-1);
g_{A}
enters only (PR-2) but must
be consistent. This is asymmetric partial robustness — a
structure not previously characterized in the semiparametric
missing-data literature.

Remark 5

(Practical nuisance hierarchy)
.

Priority ordering for estimation resources: (1)
\bar{Q}_{Y}
(enters
both conditions); (2)
g_{A}
(must be consistent); (3)
g_{\Delta}

(interchangeable with
\bar{Q}_{Y}
via double robustness).

3.5
Cross-fitting

Cross-fitting removes the Donsker condition for the first-order
empirical process term but does not eliminate
R_{2}^{(1)}
or

R_{2}^{(2)}
, which are
P_{0}
-expectations. Under cross-fitting,

\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{D}_{n},R_{2})=o_{P}(n^{-1})
.

3.6
Dual near-boundary variance decomposition

Proposition 6

(Dual near-boundary decomposition)
.

Define
c_{k}=\lim n\cdot\mathrm{Var}(R_{2}^{(k)})
for
k=1,2

and
c_{12}=\lim n\cdot\mathrm{Cov}(R_{2}^{(1)},R_{2}^{(2)})
.
Under finite fourth moments:

(a)

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\Psi}-\Psi)=\sigma_{\mathrm{EIF}}^{2}/n+\mathrm{Var}(R_{2}^{(1)})+\mathrm{Var}(R_{2}^{(2)})+2\,\mathrm{Cov}(R_{2}^{(1)},R_{2}^{(2)})+o(n^{-1})
.

(b)

\hat{\rho}\xrightarrow{P}1+(c_{1}+c_{2}+2c_{12})/\sigma_{\mathrm{EIF}}^{2}.

(11)

(c)

c_{12}>0
when both boundaries concentrate in the same covariate
region (reinforcing interaction);
c_{12}\approx 0
when they
operate in disjoint regions;
c_{12}<0
is possible but
pathological.

The proof applies the variance operator to
\hat{\Psi}-\Psi=\bar{D}_{n}+R_{2}^{(1)}+R_{2}^{(2)}+o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
and uses
cross-fitting to control the covariance between the empirical
process term and the remainders. Full proof in Web
Appendix A.2. The analogous single-remainder decomposition
appears in
Li 2026b
.

Remark 6

(Oracle decomposition)
.

A single
\hat{\rho}
cannot distinguish
c_{1}
from
c_{2}
. An
oracle substitution replacing
\hat{g}_{A}
with the true
g_{A}^{0}

and recomputing
\hat{\rho}^{(\mathrm{oracle})}
isolates the
treatment-boundary contribution:
\hat{\rho}-\hat{\rho}^{(\mathrm{oracle})}
estimates

(c_{2}+2c_{12})/\sigma_{\mathrm{EIF}}^{2}
. If
\hat{\rho}\approx\hat{\rho}^{(\mathrm{oracle})}
, the censoring boundary dominates;
if
\hat{\rho}\gg\hat{\rho}^{(\mathrm{oracle})}
, the treatment
boundary is the primary source.

4
Targeted Estimator Construction

We instantiate the general theory of Section 3 through a specific
targeted minimum loss estimator for the nested
functional (
3
). The construction follows
Li 2026a
,
adapted to accommodate estimated
g_{A}
. Details of the Super
Learner specification and cross-validation are in Web Appendix B.

Phase 1 (Initial estimation).

Four nuisance functions —
\bar{Q}_{Y}
,
\bar{Q}_{\mathrm{int}}
,

g_{A}
,
g_{\Delta}
— are estimated by
V
-fold cluster-level
cross-validated Super Learner. The initial

\bar{Q}_{\mathrm{int}}
is fit by regressing
\hat{\bar{Q}}_{Y}(S,A,W)
on

(A,W)
without involving
\hat{g}_{A}
— a design choice essential
for the coupling result (Proposition
3
).

Phase 2 (Two-stage targeting).

Stage 1 solves
P_{n}[D_{Y,a}]=0
via logistic fluctuation with
clever covariate
H_{Y}=(\hat{g}_{A}\hat{g}_{\Delta})^{-1}
.
Stage 2 solves
P_{n}[D_{S,a}]=0
via logistic fluctuation with
clever covariate
H_{S}=\hat{g}_{A}^{-1}
.
Stage 2 eliminates
R_{SY}
(Lemma
2
).

Phase 3 (Point estimate and variance).

\hat{\Psi}=n^{-1}\sum_{v}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}^{(v)}}[\bar{Q}_{\mathrm{int}}^{*(v)}(1,W_{i})-\bar{Q}_{\mathrm{int}}^{*(v)}(0,W_{i})]
.
Variance: cluster-robust sandwich

\hat{V}_{\mathrm{Sand}}=[J(J-1)]^{-1}\sum_{j}(\mathrm{EIF}_{j}-\overline{\mathrm{EIF}})^{2}

(
Liang and Zeger 1986
)

and leave-one-cluster-out jackknife

\hat{V}_{\mathrm{JK}}=(J-1)J^{-1}\sum_{j}(\hat{\Psi}^{(-j)}-\bar{\Psi}^{(-\cdot)})^{2}

(
Miller 1974
)
,
with both fluctuation steps recomputed for each held-out cluster.

5
Asymptotic Theory

Theorem 7

(Asymptotic normality)
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
5
, bounded
outcomes, and (PR-1)–(PR-2):

\hat{\Psi}-\Psi=J^{-1}\sum_{j}\mathrm{EIF}_{j}+o_{P}(J^{-1/2})

and
\sqrt{J}(\hat{\Psi}-\Psi)\to_{d}N(0,\sigma^{2})
with

\sigma^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{EIF}_{j})
.

Theorem 8

(Sandwich iff condition)
.

\hat{V}_{\mathrm{Sand}}
is consistent for

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\Psi}-\Psi)
if and only if

c_{1}+c_{2}+2c_{12}=0
.

Definition 9

(Four regimes of the dual-boundary structure)
.

Strong decay:
c_{1}=c_{2}=0
.
Single-censoring:
c_{1}>0
,
c_{2}=0
.
Single-treatment:
c_{1}=0
,
c_{2}>0
.
Dual near-boundary:
c_{1}>0
,
c_{2}>0
.

Theorem 10

(Jackknife consistency across all regimes)
.

Under the LOO stability condition
\max_{j}\|\hat{\eta}^{(-j)}_{k}-\hat{\eta}_{k}\|=O_{P}(J^{-1/2})
for each nuisance
k
:

J\cdot\hat{V}_{\mathrm{JK}}\xrightarrow{P}\sigma^{2}+c_{1}+c_{2}+2c_{12}

in all four regimes. The jackknife Wald interval

\hat{\Psi}\pm t_{J-1,\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat{V}_{\mathrm{JK}}}

achieves asymptotically exact coverage uniformly.

The proof proceeds via a LOO expansion that decomposes the
perturbation
\delta_{j}
into contributions from the EIF and from
both remainder components
R_{2}^{(1)}
and
R_{2}^{(2)}
; the
stability condition controls each term uniformly in
j
.
A complete self-contained proof is given in Web Appendix A.4.
The single-remainder analogue of this result is treated in

Li 2026b
.

Remark 7

(Nested identification versus standard AIPW)
.

Standard AIPW inverse-weights by
g_{\Delta}^{-1}g_{A}^{-1}
, with a
single near-boundary constant
c_{\mathrm{AIPW}}
driven by the
product boundary. The nested identification separates the two
boundaries into
R_{2}^{(1)}
and
R_{2}^{(2)}
with distinct
constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
. When the censoring boundary dominates
(
c_{1}\gg c_{2}
), the nested estimator has lower total
\hat{\rho}

because it avoids
g_{\Delta}^{-1}
in the identification. When the
treatment boundary dominates (
c_{2}\gg c_{1}
), the two are
comparable.

Remark 8

(Inference recommendations)
.

Use the jackknife as the default variance estimator.
Compute
\hat{\rho}=\hat{V}_{\mathrm{JK}}/\hat{V}_{\mathrm{Sand}}

as a diagnostic, not as a selection criterion: the simulation
demonstrates that
\hat{\rho}<1
throughout the strong-decay
regime (Block I), yet the sandwich systematically over-covers
(
\mathrm{CP}_{\mathrm{S}}
up to 1.000) due to finite-sample IPW
inflation in the plug-in EIC independent of the near-boundary
remainder. The sandwich is reliable only when
J
is large
enough for the EIC heavy tails to average out — a condition that
cannot be verified from
\hat{\rho}
alone. If
\hat{\rho}\geq 1
:
near-boundary second-order remainder is present; sandwich
undercovers; jackknife is required.
The oracle decomposition (substituting a high-quality
\hat{g}_{A}

estimate and recomputing
\hat{\rho}
) identifies which boundary
dominates when
\hat{\rho}\geq 1
.

Methodological implications

This application demonstrates three practical points.
First, the sandwich estimator is not merely conservative at small

J
— it can be entirely vacuous. The plug-in EIC with nested
inverse-probability weights requires the jackknife even for basic
interpretability when

Second, the surrogate bridge is operational in real clinical data:
the 24-hour lactate clearance is available for all patients, the
censoring rate (26.5%) is substantial, and the SA-TMLE produces a
clinically directional estimate despite the missing 28-day
outcomes.

not method failure: a multi-center study with

8
Discussion

Summary of contributions

This paper develops the semiparametric inference theory for
nested identification functionals under two simultaneous positivity
boundaries. The three structural results — the fluctuation
coupling, the three-component robustness geometry, and the dual
near-boundary variance decomposition — characterize a class of
estimation problems that arises whenever administrative censoring
and confounded treatment are both present and a nested
identification strategy separates the two boundaries into distinct
roles.

The fluctuation coupling (Proposition
3
)
identifies a general mechanism in targeted estimation of nested
functionals: the score residuals’ conditional-mean-zero property
insulates the intermediate regression rate from propensity quality.
This holds for any estimator that constructs the intermediate
regression as a conditional mean of the outcome regression, not only
for the specific construction of Section 4.

The three-component geometry
(Theorem
4
, Corollary
5
)
reveals that the nested identification creates a robustness
structure that is neither classically doubly robust nor multiply
robust. The practical implication — invest in both the outcome
model and the treatment propensity; the censoring propensity is
lowest priority — reverses the intuition from the single-boundary
case, where outcome modelling alone suffices.

The dual near-boundary decomposition
(Proposition
6
) extends the variance-decomposition
framework of
Li 2026b
from one to two interacting sources.
The covariance interaction
c_{12}
is a new phenomenon with
concrete practical consequences: when the most confounded patients
are also the most censored (a common pattern), the variance gap
exceeds the sum of the individual boundary contributions.

Limitations

The surrogate-mediated MAR assumption (Assumption
3
) is
not testable. In observational settings, this assumption is more
delicate than in designed experiments. A partial diagnostic —
regressing
Y
on the censoring propensity residual within

\{\Delta=1\}
— extends to this setting; a formal sensitivity
analysis for violations is an important extension.

The no-unmeasured-confounders assumption
(Assumption
2
) is standard but unverifiable.
The nested identification does not weaken this requirement; it
addresses the censoring problem but not the confounding problem.

The simulation uses a parametric DGP. A real-data application
using EHR or claims data would strengthen the practical relevance.

The jackknife requires
J
refits. The HC-corrected sandwich
(
\hat{V}_{\mathrm{HC}}=\hat{\rho}\cdot\hat{V}_{\mathrm{Sand}}
)
provides the same coverage with a single calibration run.

Closed-form approximations for
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
as functions of DGP
parameters would enable design-stage power calculations but are not
derived here.

Extensions

Three directions extend this work. First, continuous censoring
times under a counting-process framework. Second, multiply nested
functionals with sequential intermediate variables. Third,
real-data application to a CER study with delayed outcomes and a
plausible intermediate variable.

Conclusion

The dual-boundary structure is not exotic: it is the default
operating condition in observational CER with administrative
censoring. Whenever a nested identification strategy is employed to
avoid inverse censoring weights, the treatment propensity boundary
creates a second near-boundary source that interacts with the
first. The theory developed here provides the variance
decomposition, the robustness characterization, and the inference
correction for this structure.
