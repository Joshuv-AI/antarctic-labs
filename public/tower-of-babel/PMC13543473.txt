Penetrating trauma leading to a transfixing common bile duct injury: case report and surgical management
Authors: Yassine Kallel, Aymen Laaribi, Mohamed Ali Kallel, Hazem Beji, Mohamed Mongi Mighri, Hassen Touinsi

Background

Trauma-related injuries to the biliary tract are rare, occurring in approximately 0.1% of trauma admissions, and are frequently associated with concomitant injuries to adjacent organs [1]. Among traumatic biliary injuries, the gallbladder is the most commonly affected structure [2], whereas injuries to the common bile duct (CBD) following penetrating trauma have been reported only in a limited number of cases [3–7].

Diagnosis of extrahepatic biliary duct injuries is often challenging and may be missed during the initial trauma assessment, as CT scan findings are frequently non-specific, leading to a diagnostic dilemma [8]. Consequently, CBD injuries are commonly detected intraoperatively, typically when unexpected bile leakage is observed in the porta hepatis region [9].

Such injuries may be partial or complete (total transection); however, reports of transfixing injuries of the CBD following penetrating trauma are exceedingly rare.

We therefore present a case of a transfixing injury of the CBD resulting from penetrating abdominal trauma.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old previously healthy male, victim of a stab wound following an assault, presented to the emergency department 2 h after the trauma with a penetrating wound to the right hypochondrium.

On examination, the patient was calm and hemodynamically stable, with a blood pressure of 110/72 mmHg, heart rate of 64 bpm, oxygen saturation of 97%, and temperature of 37.4 °C.

Physical examination revealed tenderness in the right hypochondrium at the site of a 1.5-cm linear wound with minimal external bleeding. The stabbing object was not retained at presentation, and the wound had been sutured under local anesthesia.

Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis (white blood cell count 14,000/µL) with neutrophilia and a hemoglobin level of 10 g/dL, while serum lipase was within normal limits.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a 4-cm laceration of hepatic segment IVb, associated with moderate intra-abdominal fluid and no evidence of pneumoperitoneum (Fig. 1).

Contrast-enhanced CT of abdomen showing a laceration of hepatic segment IVb, associated with intra-abdominal fluid

The patient was managed as a case of hepatic trauma with hemoperitoneum, and an initial conservative treatment approach was attempted.

Six hours later, the patient developed a fever, tachycardia, and signs of peritoneal irritation. These clinical findings, alongside the presence of free intra-abdominal fluid and a liver laceration near the gallbladder and hepatic pedicle, prompted the decision to proceed with diagnostic laparoscopy.

During laparoscopy, the peritoneal fluid appeared bilious and was present throughout the abdominal cavity. A transfixing laceration of liver segment IVb was identified without active bleeding, and the gallbladder remained intact. No injury to the duodenum or other sources of bile leak was observed. Given the presence of diffuse biliary contamination without a clearly identified source and the need for complete exploration of the hepatoduodenal ligament, conversion to open laparotomy was performed.

After exposure of the hepatic pedicle, a transfixing injury of the supraduodenal segment of the CBD was identified, with no involvement of the portal vein (Fig. 2). No other injury to any organ was found.

Intraoperative photograph showing a transfixing injury of the supraduodenal segment of the common bile duct

Although the gallbladder was macroscopically intact, a prophylactic cholecystectomy was performed to optimize surgical exposure of the hepatoduodenal ligament and to prevent postoperative acute cholecystitis secondary to transient biliary stasis. This was followed by repair of the posterior defect of the non-dilated CBD using interrupted sutures through the anterior defect after longitudinal enlargement of the laceration. As a precaution, the anterior wall was then closed over a 12 Fr Kehr’s T-tube, and a subhepatic drain was placed.

Postoperative course was uneventful. A T-tube cholangiogram performed on postoperative day 8 demonstrated normal opacification of the extrahepatic biliary tree with no evidence of contrast leakage and free passage of contrast into the duodenum (Fig. 3).

Postoperative T-tube cholangiogram (day 8) demonstrating a patent biliary tree with normal contrast flow into the duodenum and no evidence of leakage

The patient was discharged on postoperative day 9 without complications, with the Kehr’s T-tube left in place and clamped, while the subhepatic drain was removed at the time of discharge.

Given the traumatic etiology and rarity of this injury, the T-tube was maintained for 60 days to ensure adequate ductal healing, allow tract maturation, and minimize the risk of stricture formation. A repeat cholangiogram at that time confirmed ductal patency without leakage or stenosis, and the tube was subsequently removed without complications.

The exit site was managed with simple sterile dressings, and no electrolyte imbalance occurred during the postoperative follow-up. At 1-year follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic.

Discussion

Penetrating injuries to the extrahepatic biliary tree are exceptionally rare and typically result from direct mechanical disruption, such as stab or gunshot wounds. The resulting structural damage can range from minor partial lacerations to complete ductal transections, which are categorized using the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) grading system [10].

The clinical presentation of extrahepatic bile duct injuries is often insidious, making the initial evaluation highly challenging and increasing the risk of a missed diagnosis during the primary trauma assessment [11, 12]. Patients may initially appear hemodynamically stable with minimal physical signs, particularly if the trauma is isolated or the biliary leakage is initially contained.

In the present case of penetrating trauma, an initial conservative approach was adopted, as hemodynamically stable patients with abdominal stab wounds without signs of peritonitis or diffuse abdominal tenderness (away from the wounding site) may be managed nonoperatively according to current practice management guidelines [13]. However, delayed diagnosis of biliary injuries may lead to significant morbidity [10, 14]. Therefore, a high index of suspicion should be maintained in cases of penetrating abdominal trauma, and the possibility of biliary injuries should always be considered, particularly when the trauma is associated with hepatic lacerations in close proximity to the hepatic pedicle or the gallbladder.

Preoperative radiological evaluation is frequently non-specific in the early stages of biliary trauma. Modalities such as ultrasound and contrast-enhanced CT scans may reveal free intra-abdominal fluid or adjacent hepatic lacerations, but they often yield false-negative results regarding the specific biliary tear [10, 15]. Due to these diagnostic limitations, early diagnostic laparoscopy serves as an invaluable screening and therapeutic tool for hemodynamically stable patients when clinical deterioration occurs [16]. When laparotomy is indicated and a ductal breach is highly suspected but not macroscopically visible, an intraoperative cholangiogram should be systematically performed to rule out occult or posterior transfixing punctures [10].

Traumatic injuries of the CBD are commonly classified according to the grading system proposed by the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) [10], which categorizes injuries based on their severity and circumferential involvement of the duct. This classification allows differentiation between partial lacerations involving less than 50% of the duct circumference, major transections involving more than 50% of the circumference, and complete transections with full disruption of the duct (Table 1) in order to guide surgical management strategy.

In contrast to most previously reported injuries, this case represents a unique transfixing lesion involving both anterior and posterior walls of the CBD without complete transection. While such a pattern represents an intermediate state between a major laceration and complete transection, it is classified as Grade IV according to the AAST system. This classification is justified by the fact that circumferential involvement remained below 50%, ductal continuity was preserved, and there was no tissue loss sufficient to qualify as a Grade V injury.

The surgical management of extrahepatic biliary trauma must be strictly guided by intraoperative findings. Extensive injuries involving more than 50% of the ductal circumference (AAST grade V, World Society of Emergency Surgery [WSES] class III) typically necessitate biliary-enteric reconstruction, most commonly a Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy, particularly when there is no associated vascular injury that could compromise the blood supply to the anastomosis. In contrast, primary repair over a T-tube represents an effective and less invasive option for selected AAST grade IV injuries (WSES class II) when the ductal tissue is viable, the defect is limited, and tension-free repair can be achieved [10, 15].

In this case, several intraoperative factors justified our decision to perform a primary repair with T-tube drainage rather than a hepaticojejunostomy. First, despite being a transfixing injury, there was no significant tissue loss, and the circumferential involvement remained under 50% with preserved ductal continuity [15, 17]. Second, the edges of the laceration were viable, ensuring adequate vascularization for primary healing. Third, the patient presented with a normal, small-caliber CBD; performing a Roux-en-Y reconstruction on an undilated duct in an acute trauma setting carries a notably high risk of subsequent stricture [18]. Finally, the early timing of the operation prevented the development of severe local inflammation or purulent peritonitis. The placement of a T-tube was a vital adjunct, serving to decompress the biliary tree and allow for postoperative cholangiographic monitoring [19]. Accordingly, primary repair over a T-tube may be favored when all of these conditions are met, whereas the absence of any one factor should prompt consideration of Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy.

The mechanism of injury, hemodynamic status at presentation, timing of diagnosis, and surgical management may vary considerably among reported cases. To promptly detect postoperative complications following primary repair, we recommend a standardized surveillance protocol:

Early monitoring focuses on detecting leaks via clinical assessment and drain output observation. An early postoperative T-tube cholangiogram is critical to confirm repair integrity.

In the intermediate phase (2–8 weeks), a repeat cholangiogram before T-tube removal rules out early stricture and ensures tract maturation to prevent fistula formation [22]. The optimal duration of T-tube drainage after traumatic bile duct repair is not standardized [10, 19]. While elective cases typically require 2–4 weeks, trauma cases may justify 6–8 weeks to reduce stricture risk and confirm ductal healing before removal.

Finally, because biliary and fibrotic strictures are recognized late complications that may occur months or even years after the initial trauma —often resulting from inadequate primary repair or compromised vascular supply [23] — clinical evaluation and liver function tests are recommended every 3 to 6 months during the first year, with Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) reserved for suspected abnormalities [24].

Reported stricture rates are particularly high after end-to-end anastomosis, reaching up to 55–60% in some series [25], whereas limited ductal injuries with preserved tissue may be successfully managed with primary repair over a T-tube with a lower risk of late stricture formation [12].

While selective nonoperative management (SNOM) is a well-established approach for hemodynamically stable patients with penetrating abdominal trauma, as supported by both the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma (EAST) guidelines [13], wounds involving the hepatic pedicle demand strict vigilance. As illustrated by our clinical case, extrahepatic biliary tract injuries (EHBTI) can present with a free bilious effusion but no macroscopic ductal tear, making them highly susceptible to being missed [10]. To prevent the morbidity of delayed diagnosis, we propose a streamlined, two-step approach:

Vigilant Exploration : When laparotomy is indicated, systematic inspection of the hepatoduodenal ligament is mandatory. The intraoperative discovery of free intraperitoneal bile or a bile-stained hematoma is a critical indicator of EHBTI that requires complete ductal visualization, even if the primary defect is occult [10].

Although the patient remained asymptomatic at one-year follow-up, it is important to acknowledge that biliary strictures can occasionally develop beyond one year. Therefore, a longer follow-up would provide additional reassurance regarding long-term outcomes.

Finally, the insights from this case report, while valuable for guiding surgical decision-making in similar scenarios, are nevertheless limited by its single-case design, which restricts the generalizability of its findings.

Conclusions

True transfixing injuries of the CBD are exceptionally rare and represent a highly deceptive diagnostic challenge following penetrating abdominal trauma. This case illustrates that a high index of suspicion and a low threshold for early surgical exploration can be crucial when the clinical evolution is atypical. Furthermore, it suggests that when the ductal tissue is viable and the defect is limited, a tissue-sparing primary repair over a T-tube may be considered as a feasible management option, potentially avoiding the long-term morbidity associated with complex biliary reconstructions. However, given the single-case nature of this report, further data are required before definitive treatment recommendations can be established.
