1 Introduction
Inelastic scattering reactions including (n,2n) are particularly difficult to measure.
Hardly any measurements on radioactive nuclei exist. The reason is that tradi-
tional methods require the detection of gammas or neutrons in a background of
elastically scattered neutrons, which can interact with the detector or other mate-
rials.
During a nuclear explosion, high-energy neutrons interact with the surrounding
material mostly via (n,2n) reactions populating neutron-deficient radioactive iso-
topes, Figure 1.

Figure 1: Schematic nuclear reaction network during a nuclear explosion starting
from stable 75 As (black square). High-energy neutrons remove neutrons via (n,2n)
reactions populating neutron-deficient arsenic isotopes, which are β + -unstable
(red squares). Low energy neutrons get captured by the isotopes left after the
high-energy exposure. This phase populates neutron-rich arsenic isotopes, which
are β − -unstable (blue squares).

half-lives of the radioactive isotopes are of order of days, which makes arsenic
well-suited for radchem analysis.

The 73 As(n,2n) and the 73 As(n,2n) reaction cross sections are the only neutron
removal cross sections that can be measured with current methods. The corre-
sponding cross sections for the short-lived isotopes 71,72,74 As can only be directly
measured in inverse kinematics [1, 2]. Since 75 As is the only stable isotope of
this element, the sample production is no problem at all. This report is therefore
focused on the sample production for a possible 73 As(n,2n) campaign.

2 Sample production
2.1 General considerations
So far, no direct measurement on 73 As(n,2n) has ever been reported. Even a single
point would therefore already be a large improvement. It is possible to perform
such a measurement, but it needs to be a dedicated effort across several institu-
tions. The possible path forward is an activation measurement (details below):

      • sample with 1015 atoms (100 MBq, 120 ng)
 – production at the IPF [3] facility at LANSCE or using a cyclotron, e.g
 at Texas A&M [4]
 – it is important to avoid the production of 72,74 As
 – separate As from Se to retrieve a clean sample
 – cover the sample with high-Z material to shield the low-energy gam-
 mas and X-rays from the decay of 73 As

 3

 • measure 73 As(n,2n) via activation

 – irradiate 73 As sample with 14 MeV neutrons for about 2 days at a
 high-power DT-facility
 – count 72 As decay for 2 days
 – repeat steps above if necessary

 • it might be advantageous to produce 2 samples, since activation and count-
 ing can occur simultaneously if multiple samples are available

This activation experiment would be possible on intermediate time scales - see the
following paragraphs for details. As a long-term perspective, the future Neutron
Target Facility [5, 1, 2] should be used to improve the experimental information
about this cross section from one data point to an actual energy-dependent data
set.
While this report is mainly focused on the production of a 73 As to investigate the
(n,2n) reaction via activation, other reaction cross sections are of interest too. The
corresponding requirements concerning sample nice and purity can be largely dif-
ferent. Examples are neutron capture using DANCE [6, 7] or total cross section
measurements using DICER [8, 9]. More information are provided in the corre-
sponding sections.

2.1.1 Sample production and handling
It is feasible to produce a sample of 1015 ..1016 atoms at the IPF facility at LAN-
SCE. This corresponds to 120 ng .. 1.2 µg of material with an activity of 108 ..109 Bq.
It is important to keep the amount of the stable 75 As in the sample as low as pos-
sible. This means, the production target at the IPF should not be As, but rather
(enriched) Ge or Se in order to produce 73 As e.g. via 74 Se(p,2n), 76 Se(p,4n),
72
 Ge(p,γ), see Figure 2.

 4

The ideal shielding from a photon cross section point of view (Figure 3) would be
about 0.6 mm of gadolinium. The corresponding K-shell energy is just below the
energy of the 53 keV γ-ray following the decay of 73 As. In addition, gadolinium
is a very efficient shield against thermal neutrons. This aspect might turn out
to be advantageous because many of the neutron production facilities experience
neutron room return background. A layer of 0.6 mm gadolinium is ideally suited
to suppress the 53 keV γ-ray following the decay of 73 As by more than a factor
of 1000 while being almost completely transparent (99%) for the 834 keV γ-ray
following the decay of 72 As.
A 1 mm lead layer would also reduce the 53 keV gammas following the 73 As
decay, it is however not quite as transparent for the 834 keV gammas (0.94%).
This shielding is not only useful from a radiation safety perspective, but abso-
lutely necessary for the detection of the 834 keV γ-rays, since the detector would
otherwise be overwhelmed with events due to 73 As decays.

2.1.2 γ-detection
The detection of the 834 keV γ-ray following the decay of 72 As is best done using
a HPGe detector. At close distance, a decay detection efficiency (this includes the
line intensity) of about 3% can be achieved with a single 35% detector readily
available at LANSCE [10], Figure 4. Bigger detectors and face-to-face setups can
easily push this number to 10%.

 6

Figure 4: Detection efficiency for the decays of 72 As as a function of distance
for a 35% HPGe detector available at LANSCE [10] using the most prominent
lines. These efficiency curves are based on GEANT simulations confirmed with
calibration sources.

2.1.3 DT facilities
There are several DT-neutron generators installed or commercially available in
the US, see [11] for an overview. Most of these sources are able to produce
108 ..109 n/s, which typically results in up to 107 n/s/cm2 at the position of the
activation sample.
There are much stronger sources available outside of the US. The source in Lanzhou,
China used for one of the activation measurements mentioned above [12] achieves
neutron fluxes at the source of more than 1013 n/s/cm2 . The DT neutron generator
at the Technical University Dresden, Germany [13] reaches a sources strength of
1010 ..1011 n/s resulting in a neutron flux at the irradiation position in the order of
109 n/s/cm2 .

3 Conclusion
 • best path using IPF: enriched 74 Se+p, in particular suited for a (n,tot) mea-
 surement
