Single-cell reconstruction of whole-brain efferent projections from mouse ventral posteromedial thalamus
Authors: Heng-Zheng Wei, Yi-Yao Li, Hong-Yi Jia, Tian-Yu Zhao, Xiao Ren, Xin-Tong Qiu, Fu-Xing Zhang, Yun-Qing Li, Ming-Ming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ventral posteromedial thalamic nucleus (VPM) relays orofacial somatosensory signals to the primary somatosensory cortex (SSp), yet its functional heterogeneity remains poorly defined at single-cell resolution. We combined fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography (fMOST) with single-neuron whole-brain reconstruction to map projections of vesicular glutamate transporter 2-positive (VGluT2⁺) excitatory neurons in the ventrolateral subdivision of VPM (vlVPM). Using 88 high-quality neurons with validated completeness, we identified pronounced structural heterogeneity and classified two clusters with distinct axonal profiles. Notably, we report two key findings that challenge the classical view: vlVPM neurons preferentially target superficial SSp layers (L1–3) rather than layer 4 (L4), and a multi-target subpopulation simultaneously innervates the motor cortex and basal ganglia via collaterals, establishing direct anatomical links to motor regulatory networks. These anatomical findings demonstrate that vlVPM establishes diverse projection pathways beyond canonical sensory relays, enabling direct structural crosstalk between sensory and motor circuits. This work provides a single-cell resolution framework for interpreting the anatomical basis of thalamic involvement in sensorimotor coordination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40708-026-00315-z.

Introduction

The ventral posteromedial nucleus of the thalamus (VPM) serves as a pivotal relay in the somatosensory system, primarily responsible for transmitting tactile, pain, and thermal sensations from the orofacial region to the cerebral cortex [1, 2]. In humans, the VPM mediates fine tactile discrimination, facial sensation, and the integration of certain visceral sensations, and its dysfunction underlies diverse sensory pathologies. In rodents, the VPM has emerged as a canonical model for studying sensory information processing due to its close association with the whisker somatosensory system [3]. Rather than merely relaying ascending signals, VPM neurons actively transform and integrate multi-whisker inputs, functioning as a critical node in distributed tactile computation [4].

Anatomically, the mouse VPM is parcellated into the dorsomedial (dmVPM) and ventrolateral (vlVPM) subdivisions based on cytoarchitecture and connectivity patterns. The dmVPM harbors barreloid structures that maintain strict one-to-one topography with individual whiskers, whereas the vlVPM exhibits a more diffuse projection pattern [5–7]. The vlVPM has garnered significant attention due to its unique position within the two main parallel pathways of the whisker somatosensory system. Traditionally, the vlVPM is considered a critical relay station of the extralemniscal pathway, primarily receiving input from the spinal trigeminal nucleus interpolaris (Sp5i) and projecting to the septal zones of the primary somatosensory cortex (SSp) and the secondary somatosensory cortex (SSs) [8]. However, whether vlVPM neurons directly innervate the barrel structures of the primary somatosensory cortex has long been debated. Early retrograde tracing studies (e.g., using horseradish peroxidase) suffered from extensive tracer diffusion, making it difficult to precisely target individual barrel structures and thus precluding definitive conclusions regarding direct vlVPM projections to barrels [5, 9].

Despite accumulated knowledge of VPM circuitry, methodological constraints have historically prevented comprehensive characterization of long-range projection patterns at single-neuron resolution, particularly for vlVPM neurons. Traditional tracing methods (e.g., Fluoro-Gold, horseradish peroxidase) result in dense labeling of proximal fibers but weak signals in distal axons, and only allow visualization of neuronal morphology in discontinuous sections, making single-cell-level whole-brain reconstruction unattainable [10, 11]. Consequently, fundamental architectural questions persist: Do individual vlVPM neurons project to barrel columns? Do they give off transcortical collateral branches? What is the full complement of their subcortical and cortical targets?

Within the functionally heterogeneous VPM, neurons expressing vesicular glutamate transporter 2 (VGluT2) constitute the predominant excitatory output population. As a canonical marker for glutamatergic neurons, VGluT2 mediates their excitatory neurotransmission via glutamate release [12, 13]. However, VGluT2 expression alone cannot resolve functionally distinct subpopulations within this heterogeneous group, a gap that necessitates single-neuron-resolution connectomic approaches. To date, the whole-brain projection patterns of individual VPMVGluT2 neurons—including the full diversity of their axonal collaterals and target specificity—remain uncharacterized at single-neuron resolution.

By combining sparse and bright labeling strategies with fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography (fMOST), it is now possible to reconstruct the complete axonal morphology of individual neurons and precisely delineate their projection targets and collateral patterns [14, 15]. Recent large-scale single-cell transcriptomic studies have revealed remarkable molecular diversity within thalamic nuclei, highlighting the need for integrated connectomic and transcriptomic characterization [16]. Leveraging these technical capabilities, the present study focuses on excitatory neurons in the vlVPM that express VGluT2 (vlVPMVGluT2). Using single-neuron whole-brain reconstruction, we aim to systematically characterize the output circuit heterogeneity of this neuronal population. Prior work suggests that VPM neurons may exhibit diverse projection patterns, potentially extending to non-classical sensory targets such as the motor cortex or basal ganglia [17]. Our investigation will therefore examine whether vlVPMVGluT2 neurons participate in motor regulation beyond somatosensory processing, providing a fine-scale structural foundation for understanding the complex role of the VPM in sensorimotor integration.

Methods

Animals

Nine male VGluT2-Cre transgenic mice (JAX Stock #016963), aged 8–12 weeks and weighing 25–30 g, were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Table S1). In this line, Cre recombinase is selectively expressed in VGluT2+ neurons. Animals were housed under controlled temperature (22–26 °C) and humidity (55–60%) with a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Fourth Military Medical University Guide for the Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Fourth Military Medical University Ethics Committee (protocol No. 20211018, Xi’an, China).

Surgery and stereotaxic injection

Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium (0.1 mL/10 g body weight) and secured in a stereotaxic frame (Model 68803, RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). For anterograde tracing, tracers were delivered via Hamilton microsyringes (80100, Hamilton Company, Reno, NV, USA) at a rate of 35 nL/min. For axonal AAV tracing, 100 nL of AAV-CSSP-YFP-8E3 (2 × 10¹² vg/mL, BC-SL004, BrainCase, Shenzhen, China) was injected into the vlVPM at three sites along the rostrocaudal axis: AP − 1.43 mm, ML ± 1.50 mm, DV − 4.00 mm; AP − 1.79 mm, ML ± 1.60 mm, DV − 3.75 mm; and AP − 2.03 mm, ML ± 1.75 mm, DV − 3.90 mm. Mice were perfused 4 weeks post-injection.

Fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography (fMOST)

The overall experimental workflow—including viral injection, tissue preparation, fMOST imaging, neuron tracing, and registration—is summarized in Fig. 1. Mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with 0.01 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were extracted, post-fixed in 4% PFA, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in Lowicryl HM20 resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 14340). Whole-brain imaging was performed using a high-definition fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography (HD-fMOST) system (in-house constructed, HUST-Suzhou Institute for Brainsmatics, Suzhou, China) integrating line-illumination modulation (LiMo) microscopy with thin histological sectioning [18]. Propidium iodide (PI) was added to the imaging solution for nuclear counterstaining to facilitate registration to the Allen Mouse Brain Common Coordinate Framework version 3 (CCFv3). The system automatically iterated imaging and sectioning cycles until whole-brain data acquisition was complete. Raw data were saved as 16-bit LZW-compressed TIFF files, comprising two channels: yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) for neurite tracing and PI for anatomical registration. Images were acquired at 0.32 × 0.32 μm (x-y) and 1 μm (z) resolution, yielding ~ 10,000 coronal sections. Neuronal reconstruction was performed using Fast Neurite Tracer (FNT) software as previously described [14]. Two trained annotators independently traced each neuron; discrepancies were adjudicated by a third tracer to generate consensus reconstructions. All reconstructed neurons were exported as SWC (Standardized Wireframe Cell morphology) files.

Neuron inclusion criteria and reconstruction completeness

Of the 102 labeled neurons, 88 met inclusion criteria: (1) soma unambiguously located within vlVPM boundaries; (2) complete axonal arborization from soma to terminal tips; (3) signal-to-noise ratio > 3:1 for distal axons; (4) no obvious tracing artifacts (breaks, false continuations). Fourteen neurons were excluded: incomplete distal labeling (n = 8), off-target soma position (n = 4), tracing artifacts (n = 2). Reconstruction completeness was estimated by: (i) axonal length ratio (reconstructed length / predicted length based on stereotyped branching patterns); (ii) terminal density analysis, quantified as the number of detected terminals per unit reconstructed axonal length within each target region, compared against established density benchmarks from previous fMOST studies [14, 15]. Mean completeness was 87.4 ± 6.8% (range: 76–96%). Neurons with completeness < 75% were excluded.

Image processing and quantification

Reconstructed neurons were registered to CCFv3 using a previously described pipeline. Briefly, anatomical regions were segmented as landmarks based on cytoarchitectural features from PI staining. Affine transformation followed by symmetric image normalization (Advanced Normalization Tools, v2.x) was applied to obtain transformation parameters, which were subsequently used for centroid-based neuron registration and high-resolution image alignment.

Axonal filaments were segmented from images as follows. A Gaussian filter was applied for noise reduction, followed by square-root transformation to suppress second-order effects while preserving signal discriminability in low-intensity ranges. An empirical threshold was preset based on segmentation of selected coronal images using the Otsu method in Fiji. The final segmentation threshold for each slice was computed as a weighted average of the empirical and Otsu-derived thresholds. Axon terminals were distinguished from passing fibers by: (i) presence of varicosities (> 2× shaft diameter); (ii) tortuosity index > 1.5; (iii) local axon density increase; (iv) terminal branching pattern. Custom scripts were written in Python 3.10.7 and executed on dedicated workstations. Artifacts were manually corrected in post-processing. Projection strength for each subregion was quantified as the normalized axonal volume (subregion volume divided by total brain volume).

Visualization and statistical analysis

Registered somata and detected volumes were loaded into NeuronView software for three-dimensional visualization. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v9.5.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R (v4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Data were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two independent groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test, while comparisons among three or more groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. For data that did not meet the assumption of normality, non-parametric analyses were applied: comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and comparisons among three or more groups were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test with Bonferroni correction. Data are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) and visualized using box-and-whisker plots. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Output connectivity of vlVPMVGluT2 neurons

A total of 102 vlVPMVGluT2 neurons were labeled via viral tracing and reconstructed. Of these, 88 neurons (86.3%) met quality criteria for high-resolution labeling and were included in subsequent analyses (Fig. 2A). Registration of the reconstructed neurons to the Allen Mouse Brain CCFv3 enabled comprehensive three-dimensional morphological analysis. The labeled neurons were predominantly distributed in the medial portion of the vlVPM, spanning the rostrocaudal and dorsoventral extents of the nucleus (Fig. 2B). Collectively, the vlVPMVGluT2 neuronal population innervated 31 anatomically defined brain structures (based on Allen Mouse Brain CCFv3 ontology terms), with each structure receiving projections from at least one vlVPMVGluT2 neuron. Of these, 27 structures contained identifiable axon terminals, indicating direct synaptic innervation potential. Individual neurons extended ascending axons that elaborated terminal arborizations of varying density—ranging from sparse to dense—thereby innervating distinct combinations of ipsilateral cortical and/or subcortical regions.

Morphometric analysis revealed that the sensory cortices and thalamus represented the principal targets of vlVPMVGluT2 neurons. The primary somatosensory cortex (SSp) received particularly dense innervation across multiple somatotopic subregions: the barrel field (SSp-bfd), upper limb (SSp-ul), lower limb (SSp-ll), mouth (SSp-m), trunk (SSp-tr), supplemental somatosensory cortex (SSs), and unassigned areas (SSp-un). These seven cortical areas collectively accounted for 79% of the total ascending axon length (2.7 × 106 μm of 3.4 × 106 μm). Remarkably, all 88 reconstructed neurons projected to sensory cortices (Fig. 2A), indicating that vlVPMVGluT2 neurons predominantly innervate somatosensory systems.

Beyond the primary somatosensory targets, vlVPMVGluT2 neurons exhibited substantial projections to higher-order sensory cortices, including auditory cortices (dorsal auditory cortex, AUDd; ventral auditory cortex, AUDv; primary auditory cortex, AUDp; posterior auditory cortex, AUDpo), gustatory cortex (GU), visual cortices (anterior visual cortex, VISa; rostrolateral visual cortex, VISrl), and visceral cortex (VISC), as well as agranular insular cortices (posterior agranular insular cortex, AIp; dorsal agranular insular cortex, AId), temporal association cortex (TEa), and ectorhinal cortex (ECT). Notably, these neurons also innervated motor cortices—specifically the primary motor cortex (MOp) and secondary motor cortex (MOs)—and basal ganglia nuclei including the globus pallidus external segment (GPe), globus pallidus internal segment (GPi), and striatum (STR), suggesting a role in sensorimotor integration (Fig. 2A).

The divergent projection patterns of individual vlVPMVGluT2 neurons were further characterized using correlation-based analysis of single-neuron projectomes. Pairwise comparison matrices revealed distinct structural clusters comprising regions with highly correlated projection strengths (Fig. 2C). For instance, the STR showed strong coupling with the posterior nucleus (PO), ventral anterior-lateral complex (VAL), and MOs, indicating that these regions are frequently co-targeted by individual neurons.

To distinguish terminal innervation from axonal passage, we identified “projection areas” as regions containing ≥ 1 axon terminal. While axon-bearing structures (including those with passing fibers) exceeding 100 μm in total intramural length outnumbered terminal-containing regions (Fig. 2D), 27 axon-connected structures contained terminals at the population level. Thus, each of these 27 structures receives axonal projections from at least a subset of vlVPMVGluT2 neurons, indicating that axons that form substantial arborizations within target regions can form terminals in multiple areas, though individual neurons exhibit distinct target combinations.

Topographic grouping of vlVPMVGluT2 neurons

To dissect functional subnetworks within the vlVPMVGluT2 population, we performed unsupervised clustering of single-neuron projectomes based on projection patterns and strengths across the 31 identified targets. This analysis partitioned the 88 neurons into two projectomically and spatially distinct clusters (Fig. 3A).

Cluster 1 neurons (n = 38) predominantly innervated SSp subregions, including SSp-bfd, SSp-ul, SSp-ll, SSp-m, SSp-tr, SSp-un, and SSs, as well as AUDd (Fig. 3B). This pattern aligns with established roles of VPM neurons in head and facial sensation. Conversely, Cluster 2 (n = 50) neurons exhibited markedly reduced SSp innervation while projecting substantially to motor cortices—specifically MOp and MOs—along with basal ganglia nuclei (STR, GPe, GPi) (Fig. 3B), suggesting sensorimotor integrative functions. Of note, the projection patterns of these two clusters did not differ significantly within the thalamus, particularly in the ventral posterolateral nucleus (VPL) and the thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN).

Spatial mapping revealed that Cluster 1 neurons were enriched in the rostral vlVPM, while Cluster 2 neurons occupied a broader rostrocaudal extent (Fig. 3C and D). Morphometric analysis further distinguished the clusters: Cluster 2 neurons possessed significantly more elaborate dendritic trees, with increased primary and secondary branch counts and a greater number of dendritic terminals compared to Cluster 1 (Fig. 3E). Moreover, the axonal complexity was also greater in Cluster 2 (total axon length: 4.2 × 10⁴ ± 3.1 × 10³ µm vs. 2.8 × 10⁴ ± 2.4 × 10³ µm, p < 0.001; terminal count: 181.6 ± 12.4 vs. 165.1 ± 12.9, p < 0.001).

Consistent with the above, all 88 reconstructed neurons sent cortical projections, which accounted for the vast majority of terminals across the brain. To dissect functional heterogeneity within the vlVPMVGluT2 population, we performed hierarchical clustering of cortical projection patterns, identifying eight projectomically distinct subtypes (S1–S8) nested within the two major clusters (Fig. 4A and B). Specifically, subtypes S1–S4 constituted subdivisions of Cluster 1, whereas subtypes S5–S8 constituted subdivisions of Cluster 2. The cortical-projection clustering (S1–S8) was highly concordant with the whole-target clustering (C1/C2), confirming that cortical projection patterns represent the principal determinant distinguishing the two major clusters.

Cluster 1 subtypes (S1–S4) displayed somatotopically organized projections to SSp. S1 neurons innervated limb and trunk representations (SSp-ul, SSp-ll, SSp-tr) as well as the SSp-bfd and SSp-un. S2 neurons extended this pattern to include SSs and AUDd. S3 showed selective targeting of SSp-ul, SSp-bfd, and SSp-un, whereas S4 projected to SSp-bfd, SSp-un, and SSs.

Cluster 2 subtypes (S5–S8) were distinguished by convergent projections to MOp alongside differential SSp targeting: S5 innervated SSp-ul, SSp-ll, and SSp-un; S6 targeted SSp-ul and SSp-m; S7 projected to SSp-m, SSs, GU, and VISC; and S8 exhibited the most extensive SSp coverage, spanning SSp-ul, nose (SSp-n), SSp-m, and SSp-un, as well as SSs.

These subtype-specific connectivity patterns suggest modular organization of sensory processing streams. Notably, Cluster 2 neurons (S5–S8) displayed significantly broader projection repertoires than Cluster 1 neurons (S1–S4), innervating both sensory and motor targets (Fig. 4C and D), suggesting a specialized role in sensorimotor integration.

Cortical innervation patterns of vlVPMVGluT2 neurons

To provide a comprehensive overview of vlVPM projections within the thalamocortical system, 22 anatomically defined cortical regions—encompassing both isocortex and allocortex—were analyzed based on terminal magnitude (Table 1). These 22 cortical regions were selected because they each contained at least one axon terminal from the 88 reconstructed vlVPMVGluT2 neurons; cortical areas devoid of terminals were excluded. We further analyzed and compared the spatial characteristics of neuronal projections across different subtypes of vlVPMVGluT2 neurons.

Cluster 1 subtypes (S1–S4) displayed prominent innervation of SSp subregions. Subtype 1 neurons targeted SSp-bfd, SSp-ul, SSp-ll, SSp-tr, and SSp-un. Subtype 2 extended projections to the same regions as S1 while additionally innervating SSs and AUDd. Subtype 3 showed selective targeting of SSp-ul, SSp-bfd, and SSp-un, whereas Subtype 4 projected to SSp-bfd, SSp-un, and SSs (Fig. 5A–C). The Sankey diagrams illustrated that S1–S4 primarily targeted somatosensory cortices (Fig. 5D). Notably, quantitative analysis revealed that S2 neurons exhibited the highest number of axon terminals among Cluster 1 subtypes, significantly exceeding S1, S3, and S4 (Fig. 5E; p < 0.001). Similarly, total axon length was greatest in S2 neurons, followed by S4, S3, and S1 (Fig. 5F; p < 0.05).

Laminar targeting profile of vlVPMVGluT2 neuron projections

Laminar distribution analysis revealed distinct terminal patterns across cortical laminae (Fig. 7A; Table 1). All analyzed somatosensory and motor cortical areas received axon terminals across superficial layers (L1–3) and deep layers (L4–6) from the vlVPMVGluT2 neuron ensemble. Contrary to the canonical view of thalamocortical projections targeting primarily L4, vlVPMVGluT2 axons predominantly terminated in superficial layers L1–3 (L1–3: 45.1% of terminals vs. L4: 25.4%, L5–6: 29.5%; n = 88 neurons). In MOp, terminals were distributed as follows: L2/3 (1,031), L5 (483), L6a (205), and L1 (48), with L1–3 accounting for 1,079 terminals and L5–6 for 688 terminals. Primary somatosensory subregions exhibited layer-specific patterns: SSp-bfd showed the highest terminal density in L4 (772), followed by L2/3 (472) and L5 (337); SSp-ul displayed prominent L4 (988) and L2/3 (862) innervation; SSp-ll was dominated by L2/3 input (798 vs. 433 in L4); and SSp-tr showed preferential superficial layer targeting (L2/3: 521; L4: 211). Additionally, SSs received robust input, primarily to L2/3 (813) with a smaller component to L4 (389).

Laminar distribution of axon terminals exhibited substantial variation across individual neurons and brain areas (Fig. 7A–D). Notably, Cluster 2 neurons (S5–S8) exhibited significantly broader projection repertoires than Cluster 1 neurons (S1–S4), innervating both sensory and motor targets (Fig. 7E), consistent with a specialized role in sensorimotor integration. For example, different neuronal subtypes exhibited distinct laminar patterns within the same target region, and individual neurons displayed different laminar patterns across different target regions.

Discussion

The present study provides the first comprehensive, single-cell resolution atlas of vlVPMVGluT2 neuron projections throughout the mouse brain. By reconstructing 88 individual neurons, we uncovered pronounced structural heterogeneity within this population, leading to a novel classification framework based on axonal branching patterns. To quantify output connectivity, two complementary metrics were employed: total axon length, reflecting overall axonal distribution across 31 target structures (excluding VPM), and axon terminal counts, reflecting the distribution of terminal arborizations across 27 structures (excluding VPM). The discrepancy arises because regions containing traversing fibers without terminal arborizations were included in the former but excluded from the latter. Our findings challenge the traditional view of the VPM as a purely sensory relay in two fundamental ways: first, we demonstrate that vlVPMVGluT2 axons predominantly target superficial layers (L1–3) of the primary somatosensory cortex rather than the canonical L4, suggesting a modulatory rather than a simple relay function; second, most notably, we identified a distinct subset of multi-target neurons that simultaneously innervate the motor cortex and basal ganglia, establishing a direct anatomical link between the vlVPM and motor control circuits (Fig. 8). This newly identified VPM-motor-basal ganglia pathway provides a structural foundation for understanding how the thalamus may integrate sensory and motor information, potentially facilitating rapid sensorimotor coordination. Together, these findings establish vlVPMVGluT2 neurons as a heterogeneous population with both sensory and sensorimotor functions [19], offering new perspectives on thalamic contributions to behavior and opening avenues for investigating thalamic involvement in movement disorders.

Efferent projection patterns of vlVPMVGluT2 neurons

A comprehensive understanding of the brain’s connectome—particularly the thalamus, which orchestrates information flow between subcortical and cortical networks—is essential for deciphering how neural processing unfolds across both local circuits and global systems [20, 21]. Our analysis demonstrates that vlVPMVGluT2 neurons innervate a total of 31 anatomically defined brain structures, with SSp and its subregions receiving the most substantial input. Notably, individual vlVPMVGluT2 neurons exhibited pronounced heterogeneity in their projection targets, ranging from those that innervated only a few cortical regions to those that distributed axons broadly across both cortical and subcortical structures.

The most striking finding regarding projection profiles is the laminar distribution of vlVPMVGluT2 axon terminals within the SSp. Contrary to the classical view that thalamocortical projections primarily target L4, we found that vlVPMVGluT2 axons predominantly terminated in superficial layers L1–3 at the population level. This pattern is exemplified by projections to MOp, SSp-tr, and SSs, where L1–3 terminals markedly outnumbered those in L4 (Table 1; see also Fig. 7E for representative S5–S8 subtype projections to MOp). This non-classical laminar pattern suggests that these neurons may serve modulatory functions rather than simply relaying ascending sensory information [22, 23]. Importantly, the overall superficial layer projection bias does not conflict with the L4 dominance seen in specific subregions. As quantified in the Results, vlVPMVGluT2 terminals were most dense in L4 of SSp-bfd (772), but predominantly located in L1–3 of MOp, SSp-tr, and SSs. This apparent contradiction reflects a highly target-specific laminar pattern, with vlVPMVGluT2 neurons adapting their layer targeting to the functional needs of different cortical areas. However, we acknowledge alternative interpretations: (i) superficial targeting may reflect developmental plasticity or species-specific organization; (ii) some L1–3 terminals may represent feedback projections from higher-order areas; (iii) the relative balance of driver vs. modulator inputs may vary across cortical states. Superficial layers are known to integrate feedback signals from higher-order cortical areas and attentional modulation [24], raising the possibility that vlVPMVGluT2 inputs to L1–3 may influence cortical state and context-dependent sensory processing. Our finding aligns with previous observations that certain thalamic nuclei project to superficial layers to regulate cortical excitability and gain [25].

Beyond cortical projections, we identified robust vlVPMVGluT2 innervation of STR, globus pallidus (GPe and GPi), and motor cortices (MOp and MOs). We carefully distinguished terminals from passing fibers using morphological criteria (varicosities, tortuosity, local density, branching patterns). While we cannot entirely exclude that some axons in motor areas represent collaterals en passage, the high density of varicosities and tortuosity suggests substantial synaptic innervation. These subcortical and motor-related projections were particularly pronounced in a subset of neurons (Cluster 2), which exhibited branching axons that simultaneously targeted multiple regions. This multi-target innervation pattern suggests that vlVPMVGluT2 neurons may coordinate activity across distributed sensorimotor networks, a concept that has gained increasing support from recent single-neuron tracing studies [15, 26].

Structural parallels to established thalamic organizational principles

In the present study, the classification of vlVPMVGluT2 neurons into two major clusters—those primarily innervating sensory cortices (Cluster 1) and those projecting to both sensory and motor/basal ganglia structures (Cluster 2)—parallels the classic “core-matrix” organization proposed for thalamic nuclei [27–29]. Core neurons typically project focally to middle cortical layers (primarily L4) and are associated with precise, driver-type information transfer, whereas matrix neurons project more diffusely to superficial layers (L1) and are thought to mediate modulatory, context-dependent functions [27, 30].

Cluster 1 neurons share key features with core-type cells: they project predominantly to SSp subregions. The laminar distributions, while not exclusively confined to L4, show concentrated innervation of L4 alongside L2/3 aligns with core-like properties. However, the substantial L2/3 input we observed suggests that even within core-like neurons, the strict L4-only projection pattern may be an oversimplification. This is consistent with recent studies demonstrating that thalamocortical projections often exhibit broader laminar distributions than previously appreciated [31].

Cluster 2 neurons more closely resemble matrix-type cells, characterized by their broader projection targets across multiple cortical areas (including motor cortices) and subcortical structures, as well as their prominent innervation of superficial layers. The branching projections to both cortex and basal ganglia are particularly reminiscent of “matrix” or “non-specific” thalamic neurons that are thought to synchronize activity across large-scale brain networks [28, 32]. This core-matrix framework provides a useful conceptual scaffold for understanding the functional segregation we observed, though we acknowledge that definitive assignment requires additional transcriptomic profiling (e.g., single-cell RNA-seq) and functional validation.

Structural substrates for thalamic sensorimotor coordination

The identification of two functionally distinct vlVPMVGluT2 neuron groups has significant implications for understanding sensorimotor integration. Cluster 1 neurons, with their focused projections to SSp subregions, particularly in SSp-bfd, likely contribute to fine tactile discrimination and sensory processing [33]. The prominent L2/3 innervation we observed suggests that these neurons may modulate cortical processing beyond simple sensory relay, potentially contributing to context-dependent sensory gating or attentional modulation [34, 35].

Cluster 2 neurons, which innervate both sensory and motor circuits, may serve as a structural substrate for sensorimotor coordination. The simultaneous projection to the motor cortex and basal ganglia via axon collaterals positions these neurons to rapidly integrate sensory feedback with motor commands. These anatomical projections suggest potential functional roles, though physiological validation during behavior remains to be determined. We hypothesize that these multi-target neurons may play several functional roles: (1) Sensory-guided movement initiation: By simultaneously activating the motor cortex and modulating basal ganglia output, they could facilitate rapid behavioral responses to tactile stimuli [36]; (2) Motor program updating: Orofacial tactile feedback during whisking or chewing may be transmitted via these neurons to update ongoing motor programs [37]; and (3) Sensorimotor integration during active exploration: During active whisking, these neurons could coordinate sensory sampling with motor output, enabling closed-loop control of exploratory behavior [3, 6].

However, we emphasize that these functional hypotheses remain speculative pending in vivo physiological recording during behavior. The precise conditions under which Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 2 neurons are recruited, and their differential impact on cortical processing, remain to be determined.

The involvement of vlVPM in motor functions beyond classical somatosensory relay is supported by previous studies showing that VPM neurons can respond to motor-related signals and that VPM activity is modulated by motor cortex output [17, 35]. Our findings extend these observations by providing direct anatomical evidence for a VPM-motor cortex-basal ganglia circuit. This circuit may represent a phylogenetically conserved pathway for sensorimotor coordination, potentially analogous to the role of the posterior thalamus in primates [38].

Study limitations and unresolved questions

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, our analysis is based solely on structural connectivity; functional validation via electrophysiology and optogenetics is needed to confirm synaptic connectivity and physiological roles. Second, while our sample size (88 neurons) is substantial for single-cell reconstruction, it may not capture the full diversity of vlVPMVGluT2 projection patterns. Third, our study focused exclusively on VGluT2-expressing excitatory neurons; other VPM cell types remain to be characterized. Recent single-cell transcriptomic studies have identified multiple glutamatergic and GABAergic subtypes within thalamic nuclei, suggesting that the full cellular diversity of the VPM remains to be explored [16]. Fourth, experiments were conducted only in male mice aged 8–12 weeks; sex differences and developmental plasticity represent important avenues for future investigation. Finally, the precise synaptic targets of identified projections—whether they contact specific cell types such as pyramidal tract neurons or interneurons—remain to be resolved.

Conclusion

This study provides the first comprehensive, single-cell resolution atlas of vlVPMVGluT2 neuron projections throughout the mouse brain. By reconstructing 88 individual neurons with validated completeness metrics, we uncovered pronounced structural heterogeneity within this population and established a novel classification framework based on axonal projection patterns. Our findings challenge the traditional view of the VPM as a purely sensory relay: vlVPMVGluT2 axons predominantly target superficial layers (L1–3) of the primary somatosensory cortex rather than exclusively innervating the canonical L4, and a distinct subset of multi-target neurons simultaneously innervates the motor cortex and basal ganglia, establishing a direct anatomical link between vlVPM and motor control circuits. This newly identified VPM-motor-basal ganglia pathway provides a structural foundation for understanding thalamic contributions to sensorimotor integration and opens new avenues for investigating thalamic involvement in movement disorders.
