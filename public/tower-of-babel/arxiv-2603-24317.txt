Abstract
We study the complexity of computing Bayes-Nash equilibria in single-item first-price auctions. We present the first efficient algorithms for the problem, when the bidders’ values for the item are independently drawn from the same continuous distribution, under both established variants of continuous and finite bidding sets. More precisely, we design polynomial-time algorithms for the white-box model, where the distribution is provided directly as part of the input, and query-efficient algorithms for the black-box model, where the distribution is accessed via oracle calls. Our results settle the computational complexity of the problem for bidders with i.i.d. values.

Introduction

First-price auctions have been used since antiquity for the sale of various commodities such as land, property, farming rights, livestock, or tax and public procurement contracts
[
7
]
. Today, these auctions are widely used in markets for advertising space, in the form of “ad auctions” (see, e.g.,

The development of the theory of first-price auctions dates back to the early 1960s and the pioneering work of
43
. Vickrey studied the
equilibria
of the auction—or, more precisely, of the strategic game induced by the incentives of the bidders to underbid their values for the item—when values are independently drawn from the uniform distribution. Over the next decades, several variants of the auction were studied systematically in a plethora of works in economics, e.g., see
[

;

;
34
;
10
;
38
;
29
;
26
;
27
;
30
;
28
;
3
;
39
]
. Most of these works were mainly concerned with establishing the existence, and often the uniqueness, of equilibria of the auction. For the simpler case of
symmetric
first-price auctions (most notably for i.i.d. values), the literature has managed to produce elegant analytic solutions, e.g., see
[
43
;
40
;
34
]
. Symmetric auctions capture cases where the bidders’ information is generated by the same technologies or market parameters, and in fact constitute the “textbook” model studied in the auction literature
[
36
;
31
;
25
]
.

While the economic theory established the existence of equilibria and managed, via the aforementioned analytic formulas, to
describe
them for symmetric variants, it did not address the question of how these equilibria can be formally
computed
. The computation of equilibria in games has been a focal point of research in computer science over the past two decades, e.g., see
[
12
;
8
;
32
;
41
]
; for the first-price auction in particular, this question was notably studied in a series of recent works
[
20
;
9
;
18
;
19
]
. While these works provided computational hardness results for computing equilibria, these results only concern variants of the auction with rather general assumptions on the value distributions, namely
subjectivity

[
20
;
18
]
or
arbitrary correlation

[
19
]
, or variants with
nonstandard tie-breaking rules

[
9
]
. On the other hand, the positive results obtained in these works, even for simple symmetric settings like auctions with i.i.d. values, are somewhat limited: currently, the best known result for these settings is a polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS), i.e., an algorithm which computes an
\varepsilon
-approximate equilibrium in time polynomial in the input parameters, but possibly exponential in
1/\varepsilon
, see
20
;
19
. The inconclusive nature of these results leaves us with an “asymmetry” for symmetric auctions: mathematically, the equilibria of those auctions can be described by clean analytic formulas, but corresponding polynomial-time algorithms for computing them are unknown.

Aiming to address this asymmetry, we are faced with two main challenges. First, the aforementioned analytic solutions are not readily appropriate for computational purposes. Hence, making use of them to obtain polynomial-time algorithms requires careful consideration of the representation of the inputs and outputs to the problem and a meticulous “translation” of the mathematical insights to computational methods. In fact, considering
approximate
equilibria might be necessary, as exact equilibria might involve irrational numbers in their description
[
20
]
, and hence cannot be found in standard models of computation. In those cases, a computational algorithm is one that achieves an approximation within the best possible precision, and the goal is to design such algorithms that run in polynomial time.

The second challenge stems from the fact that these analytic formulas only apply when the bidding space is
continuous
, e.g., an interval of real numbers. For
discrete
bidding spaces, which are typically studied in most of the related work in computer science
[
20
;
9
;
18
;
19
]
, such analytic solutions are not generally available. To the best of our knowledge, the only known result of this flavour is due to
10
, who studies the equilibria of the first-price auction under two crucial assumptions, namely that the (discrete) bids are
equidistant
, and that the values are
uniformly distributed
. Chwe’s equilibrium existence result can be interpreted as a
non-computational
method to describe an equilibrium of the auction in this special case. Thus, the first challenge highlighted above applies here as well, together with the additional challenge of extending the results beyond the case of equidistant bids, and beyond the uniform distribution.

1.1
Our Results and Techniques

We study first-price auctions in which a set of
n
bidders submit their bids for one item for sale. Each bidder knows her own value for the item, and has incomplete information about the values of her competitors, via a (common) continuous distribution, from which the values are independently drawn (i.i.d.). Our goal is to design
efficient algorithms
that compute equilibria, and in fact, even
symmetric equilibria
of the auction. These are (Bayes-Nash) equilibria in which every bidder employs the same strategy, i.e., the same mapping from values to bids. We consider two variants of the auction, depending on whether the bidding space is
continuous
or
discrete
; we refer to the two variants as the
Continuous First-price Auction with Continuous Bids (CCFPA)
and the
Continuous First-price Auction with Discrete Bids (CDFPA)
, respectively. Our results can be summarized in the following informal theorem.

Informal Theorem 1
.

A (symmetric) equilibrium of the First-Price Auction can be computed efficiently, for auctions with either continuous or discrete bids.

To be more precise, when the distribution is given to the algorithm directly as part of the input, an algorithm is deemed “efficient” if it runs in polynomial time in the size of the input representation. This representation can be
explicit
, i.e., via some succinctly described function (e.g., piecewise polynomial), or
implicit
, where the input is some meaningful computational device (e.g., an appropriate arithmetic circuit) that the algorithm can use to compute the distribution’s cdf. We also (for the first time in the literature of the problem) consider the
black-box
model, where the algorithm has
query
or
oracle access
to the distribution. In this case, the efficiency of the algorithm is measured by the number of queries that it performs to the cdf oracle, and the number of arithmetic operations that it performs.

Furthermore, we will consider an algorithm to be efficient if it computes either an
exact
equilibrium whenever possible (i.e., whenever the equilibrium can be described using rational numbers), or otherwise an
approximate
equilibrium with the best possible accuracy (ideally, exponentially small error). An algorithm that computes an
\varepsilon
-approximate equilibrium in time polynomial in the input size and
1/\varepsilon
will be referred to as a
Fully Polynomial-Time Approximation Scheme (FPTAS)
, whereas a PTAS, mentioned earlier, runs in polynomial time for every
fixed

\varepsilon
(but may be exponential in
1/\varepsilon
). For a more in-depth discussion of these (standard) approximation notions, the interested reader is referred to the classic textbook of
42
. In the black-box model, we will deem an algorithm efficient if it computes an
\varepsilon
-approximate equilibrium using the (provably, qualitatively) smallest possible number of queries as a function of
\varepsilon
, as well as a polynomial number of arithmetic operations.

Algorithms for the CCFPA.

For the CCFPA, leveraging the analytic formulas from the work in economics, we are able to obtain the following results:

For the black-box model, in

Via a careful inspection of our analysis in the black-box model above, it further follows that the same algorithm can be transformed into an FPTAS, for the case when the distribution is provided in the input implicitly via an arithmetic circuit, see
Section

.

-

Finally, in

1.2
Related Work and Discussion

The systematic study of equilibrium computation in first-price auctions was initiated by
20
, where the CDFPA was formally introduced and studied. The main result of this work is the computational hardness (specifically PPAD-hardness and FIXP-hardness, see
[
15
]
) of computing equilibria of the auction for distributions that are not only different for every agent, but also
subjective
, i.e., different from the
perspective
of each agent.
9
subsequently showed a similar PPAD-hardness result without the subjectivity assumption, but for an involved (trilateral) tie-breaking rule, rather than the standard uniform tie-breaking that is typically used in the literature of the auction, e.g., see
[
43
;
40
;
34
;
3
;
25
]
.
18
;
19
showed corresponding NP-hardness results for a variant of the auction with discrete distributions, under the same subjectivity assumption, or for distributions that are arbitrarily correlated between bidders.

In terms of positive results,
20
provided a polynomial-time algorithm for the case where both the number of bidders and the number of bids is fixed, which employs off-the-shelf results of
22
for approximately solving systems of polynomial inequalities. Using a “bidding shrinkage lemma” due to
9
,
18
;
19
observed that the same algorithm can be transformed into a PTAS for a fixed number of bidders, and any bidding space. Furthermore, the dependency on the number of bidders can be removed for symmetric auctions, yielding a PTAS that holds under no further assumptions. As we explained earlier, a PTAS is only efficient when the approximation parameter
\varepsilon
is constant; in contrast, our algorithm in
Section

4
achieves the best possible (inversely exponential) approximation for the same setting.

To the best of our knowledge, the complexity of equilibrium computation in the CCFPA had not been studied prior to our work. This variant of the auction was studied only as a technical tool by
19
, as part of their “bid densification” approach: in a quest to achieve positive results for the CDFPA, the authors consider how its equilibria would be approximated by equilibria of the CCFPA, for which analytic solutions are known (by the seminal work of
34
). Despite this connection, the authors did not show how to compute equilibria of the CCFPA in polynomial time. Furthermore, their resulting algorithm for the CDFPA, although displaying a graceful dependency on
\varepsilon
, crucially also depends on other, non-controllable parameters such as explicit bounds on the pdf of the distribution and the granularity of the bidding space. For our results in
Section

4
we do not impose any such assumptions (other than a mild Lipschitz-continuity assumption).

In a complementary direction,
1
study structural properties of equilibria in standard first-price auctions, showing uniqueness of Bayesian coarse correlated equilibria (and extending the analysis to all-pay auctions).
There is also a growing line of work that employs tools from online learning and optimization to characterize (and in some cases compute) stable outcome notions in first-price auctions, particularly in repeated and non-stationary environments
[
17
;
5
;
45
;
24
]
.

Preliminaries

2.1
Symmetric Auctions

In a
symmetric (continuous, Bayesian) first-price auction
, there is a set
N=[n]
of bidders and one item for sale (where
[n]\coloneq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
). There is a common set of values
V=[0,1]
(the
value space
) and a set of bids
B\subseteq[0,1]
(the
bidding space
); each bidder
i
has a value
v_{i}\in V
for the item and submits a bid
b_{i}\in B
.
Similarly to previous work
[
18
;
9
;
19
]
, we assume that
0\in B
, which can be interpreted as the option to abstain from the auction.

Given a
bid profile

\bm{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
, the item is sold to one of the highest bidders
i^{*}\in W(\bm{b})\coloneq\argmax_{i\in N}b_{i}
for a payment equal to her bid
b_{i^{*}}
, breaking ties
uniformly at random
.
We define the
ex-post
utility of bidder
i
with (true) value
v_{i}
, with respect to submitted bids
\bm{b}
, by:

\tilde{u}_{i}(\bm{b};v_{i})\coloneq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|W(\bm{b})|}(v_{i}-b_{i}),&\text{if}\;\;i\in W(\bm{b}),\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(1)

Value Distributions.

The value of each bidder is private information to her only; bidders have
incomplete information
about the values of others.
More precisely, we assume that, from the perspective of some bidder
i
, the values of the other bidders for the item are i.i.d. random variables, drawn from an (absolutely) continuous distribution (with cdf)
F
over
V=[0,1]
.
We let
f
denote the distribution’s pdf and
\supportdistro\left(F\right)
its support. Note that, without loss of generality,
\supportdistro\left(F\right)
can be assumed to be a union of (countably many, disjoint)
subintervals of
[0,1]
.
We use
\underline{v}\coloneqq\inf\supportdistro\left(F\right)
to denote the leftmost point of
F
’s support; the distribution
F
that
\underline{v}
refers to will be clear from the context.

Bidding Strategies.

A
(pure, bidding) strategy
of a bidder
i
is a function
\beta:V\to B
mapping values to bids.
A
strategy profile
is a collection of strategies, one for each bidder, i.e., a vector
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})\in B^{V}\times\ldots\times B^{V}
.
In this work, we will only be interested in
symmetric
strategy profiles, in which
\beta_{i}=\beta_{j}
for all
i,j\in N
; in this case, we will slightly abuse notation and use
\beta\in B^{V}
to denote the symmetric strategy profile
\bm{\beta}=(\beta,\ldots,\beta)
.

Utilities and Equilibria.

Given a symmetric strategy profile
\beta
, the (interim)
utility
of any bidder
i
with (true) value
v\in V
, when she bids
b\in B
, is given by

\displaystyle u(b,\beta;v)

\displaystyle\coloneq\expectation_{\bm{v}_{-i}\sim\times_{j=1}^{n-1}F}\nolimits\left[\tilde{u}_{i}(b,\beta(\bm{v}_{-i});v)\right],

(2)

where
\beta(\bm{v}_{-i})
denotes the vector
\times_{j\in N\setminus{\{i\}}}\beta(v_{j})
.
Notice that the subscript
i
is deliberately omitted (that is, we simply write
u
instead of
u_{i}
), due to the symmetry of definition (
2
). Whenever the strategy
\beta
is clear from the context, we will drop it from the notation of the utility and simply write
u(b;v)
instead.

In this paper, we consider the following standard notion of (approximate) equilibrium for symmetric auctions, e.g., see
[
19
;
25
;
35
]
.

Definition 1

(
\varepsilon
-approximate symmetric Bayes-Nash equilibrium of
the FPA)
.

Let
\varepsilon\geq 0
. A symmetric strategy profile
\beta

is an
(interim)
\varepsilon
-approximate symmetric (pure) Bayes-Nash equilibrium
(
\varepsilon
-BNE)
if, for any value
v\in\supportdistro\left(F\right)
, it holds that:

u(\beta(v),\beta;v)\geq u(b,\beta;v)-\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all}\;\;b\in B.

(3)

We will refer to a
0
-BNE as an
exact
symmetric BNE.

As it is standard in the literature, we will be interested in equilibria that satisfy two desirable properties, namely,
no overbidding
and
monotonicity
.
The former property stipulates that bidders do not submit bids larger than their values, as doing that would always result in non-positive utility. The latter property considers strategies that are non-decreasing in the bidder’s value. From now on, we will only consider symmetric equilibria which are both non-overbidding and monotone.

Remark 1
.

For positive results like the ones presented in our work, restricting attention to symmetric, non-overbidding, monotone equilibria is without loss of generality, as it only makes the results stronger.

2.2
Distribution Representation

Since
F
is a continuous distribution, we need to discuss how to represent it in a way that is suitable for computational purposes. We will consider three different representations, all of which are standard in the literature of related problems with continuous functions in their inputs, e.g., see
[
13
;
23
;
20
]
.

Black-box (Oracle) Model:
In this model, we assume that the algorithm has
oracle
or
query access
to the distribution, i.e., the distribution will be accessed via
queries
that input a value
x\in V=[0,1]
and output the cdf
F(x)
of the distribution. The efficiency of an algorithm will then be measured by the (worst-case) number of such queries that it performs. Furthermore, for our positive results, we will make sure that the number of any additional arithmetic operations needed are polynomial (in the relevant parameters). Such a model is of particular interest for applications where the designer cannot have perfect knowledge of the distributional value prior, but can perform some kind of “market analysis” and “estimate” the bidder population quantiles.

White-box Implicit Model:
In this model, we assume that the algorithm has access to some “meaningful” computational device, e.g., a Turing machine or an appropriate arithmetic circuit (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Appendix B]
). The algorithm will then access the cdf of the distribution for a given value
x\in V=[0,1]
via “feeding”
x
to this device and obtaining
F(x)
. Since the input to the algorithm includes a description of this device rather than an abstract cdf oracle, this model is typically referred to as the
white-box
model.

Concretely, we will assume that the input to the algorithm is a
well-behaved arithmetic circuit

x\mapsto F(x)
, as defined in
[
16
, Section 3.1.3]
. These are circuits with gates performing the arithmetic operations
\{+,-,\times,\max,\min,>\}
and rational constants, with the additional restriction that, for any computational path that leads to an output, there is at most a logarithmic number of multiplication gates. This restriction is required to ensure that the circuit does not “internally” generate numbers of magnitude (inversely) doubly-exponential (in the size of the input and the description of the circuit), preventing its efficient evaluation. Furthermore, we will assume that the input to the problem also includes a Lipschitz parameter
L
, such that the distribution is
L
-Lipschitz.

Explicit Model:
In this model, we assume that the distribution can be represented in a succinct form, and thus can be given directly as input to the algorithm. More precisely, following
[
20
, Sec. 6]
, we will assume that the cdf of the distribution is piecewise polynomial, and hence it will be provided in the input in terms of the coefficients of the polynomial in each piece. Formally, the distribution is given in the input by:

•

a partition of
V
into
k
subintervals
\left\{[v_{j-1},v_{j}]\right\}_{j=1}^{k}
, given by their (rational) end-points

0=v_{0}<v_{1}<v_{2}<\ldots<v_{k}=1;

•

for each
j\in[k]
, a vector of
(d+1)
rationals
(a_{j,0},a_{j,1},\dots,a_{j,d})
; this induces the semantics that the value of the cdf
F
within the
j
-th interval is given by the
d
-degree polynomial

F_{j}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}a_{j,\ell}x^{\ell},\qquad\forall x\in[v_{j-1},v_{j}].

(4)

Note that, in order for this representation to be valid, it needs to respect the boundary conditions

F_{1}(0)=0,\qquad F_{k}(1)=1,\quad\text{and}\quad F_{j}(v_{j})=F_{j+1}(v_{j})\quad\text{for all}\;\;j\in[k-1],

which are all checkable in polynomial time.

Our algorithms for our positive results in
Section

3
and
Section

4
will be applicable to both the black-box and the white-box models, by ensuring that, besides a polynomial number of oracle calls, they perform at most polynomially-many arithmetic operations, and the magnitude of associated numerical values is polynomially bounded. For the natural, special case of explicit representations, we are able to obtain crisper results, which justifies their independent study.

Black-Box Input

Before we provide the proof of the theorem, we offer some useful discussion. As we mentioned in the introduction, in the context of equilibrium computation, the CDFPA is inherently more challenging than the CCFPA. This is because, unlike for the equilibria of the CCFPA, the literature in economics has not in general managed to provide analytic solutions for the equilibria of the CDFPA. An exception to this is the work of
10
, which provides a method to obtain an equilibrium under two assumptions: (i) the distribution being uniform, and (ii) the bids being equidistant, i.e.,
B=\{0,\frac{1}{q},\frac{2}{q},\dots,\frac{q-1}{q},1\}
for some integer
q
.

Our algorithm solves the problem for the general setting, without any of these two assumptions. In order to achieve this, we make the following crucial observation: if we know the utility achieved by the bidders at equilibrium at the highest point in the value space, i.e., at
v=1
, then we can efficiently compute the equilibrium, i.e., the set of jump points that describe the equilibrium bidding strategy. This observation already yields the result of
10
as a special case. Indeed, we can reinterpret Chwe’s method as follows. It turns out that for the uniform distribution, the utility at equilibrium at point
v=1
is always
1/n
, where
n
is the number of bidders. As a result, the crucial observation yields a method for efficiently computing the equilibrium when the distribution is uniform. Note that this method works even for non-equidistant bids, so it already allows us to remove assumption (ii).

In order to remove assumption (i), namely the uniformity assumption, we make the following additional observation. When we run our method for obtaining an equilibrium from a utility value
U
at
v=1
that does not correspond to the utility value at an equilibrium, it still outputs a set of jump points. Of course, these jump points do not correspond to an equilibrium in that case, but we can still extract useful information from them. We show that our method for computing a set of jump points
s=(s_{0},\dots,s_{m})
from a utility value has the following two crucial properties: (a) on input
U=0
, the method outputs
s
with
s_{0}=0
, and (b) on input
U=1
, it outputs
s
with
s_{0}>0
. This suggests the following approach: perform binary search on the value
U
in order to find a value
U^{*}
where the output
s
is such that
s_{0}
is barely above
0
. Indeed, we are able to show that if we can find such a value
U^{*}
then we obtain an approximate equilibrium. The most challenging part of the analysis is showing that our method is continuous, in the sense that the jump points that it outputs depend continuously on the input value
U
. This is crucial in order to argue that binary search can locate a solution. The analysis of our algorithm also yields a new proof of existence of equilibria, which comes with an efficient algorithm, unlike previous existence proofs.

. We will show that

\varepsilon

\delta

Thus, since
\delta\leq\varepsilon/3
, in order to show that

\varepsilon

Let
(s_{0},\dots,s_{m})
be the jump point representation of

we can write
u^{\prime}(b_{i};v)=(v-b_{i})\phi(F^{\prime}(s_{i-1}),F(s_{i}))

∎

ComputeStrategy
subroutine

A few words about the step that performs a binary search are needed. First of all, note that we necessarily have
s_{i}>b_{i}
, since the algorithm never sets the value
s_{i}
below
b_{i+1}
. Observe that if the algorithm reaches the “else” branch, then the equation
(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(s_{i-1},s_{i})=U_{i}
is guaranteed to have a solution. Indeed, since we did not enter the “if” branch, we have
(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(x,s_{i})>U_{i}
for
x=s_{i}
. Since we did not enter the “else if” branch, we also have
(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(x,s_{i})<U_{i}
for
x=b_{i}
. As a result, there exists some
x\in(b_{i},s_{i})
such that
(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(x,s_{i})=U_{i}
. Furthermore, we can use binary search to find an approximate solution. Since
|s_{i}-b_{i}|\leq 1
, the function
x\mapsto(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(x,s_{i})
is
nL
-Lipschitz; this can be seen by an application of a simple lemma (
Lemma

A.3
) which we state and prove in
Appendix

A
. Thus, after at most
\log(nL/\delta)
steps of binary search we are guaranteed to have found
x\in(b_{i},s_{i})
satisfying
|(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(x,s_{i})-U_{i}|\leq\delta
. The algorithm then sets
s_{i-1}:=x
and proceeds. Note that
\log(nL/\delta)
is polynomial in the relevant parameters, namely
n,m,\log L,\log(1/\varepsilon),
and
\log(1/\alpha)
.

The while loop of the main algorithm is executed
\operatorname{poly}(n,m,\log L,\log(1/\varepsilon),\log(1/\alpha))
times. As a result, the total number of queries to
F
as well as the number of simple operations is bounded by a polynomial in
n,m,\log L,\log(1/\varepsilon),
and
\log(1/\alpha)
. In the white-box model, all these quantities are polynomial in the size of the input, so the algorithms runs in polynomial time.

4.3
Correctness of the Algorithm

We begin by showing a straightforward property of the algorithm.

Lemma 4.4
.

The while loop in the algorithm maintains the following invariant: the values
s^{\ell}=\textsf{{ComputeStrategy}}(U^{\ell})
and
s^{r}=\textsf{{ComputeStrategy}}(U^{r})
satisfy
s^{\ell}_{0}=0
and
s^{r}_{0}>0
.

Proof.

Consider what happens when we run the
ComputeStrategy
subroutine with input
U=0
.
Since
s_{m}>b_{m}
we have
(s_{m}-b_{m})\Delta(s_{m},s_{m})>U_{m}=0
. Thus, we enter the second branch and set
s_{m-1}:=b_{m}
and
U_{m-1}=0
. Since the utility remains
0
, we will end up in the second branch and set
s_{i-1}:=b_{i}
and
U_{i-1}=0
for all subsequent
i
. In particular, we will set
s_{0}=0
.

Now consider what happens when we run the subroutine with input
U=1
. We always have
(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(s_{i},s_{i})\leq 1
and thus will always end up in the first branch, since this branch just copies over the utility value
U_{i-1}=U_{i}=1
. As a result, we will have
s_{0}=1
.

At the beginning we have
U^{\ell}=0
and
U^{r}=1
.
Thus we have that
s^{\ell}=\textsf{{ComputeStrategy}}(U^{\ell})
and
s^{r}=\textsf{{ComputeStrategy}}(U^{r})
satisfy
s^{\ell}_{0}=0
and
s^{r}_{0}>0
. The while loop implements a binary search where
U^{\ell}
is replaced by
U
only if
s=\textsf{{ComputeStrategy}}(U)
satisfies
s_{0}=0
. Otherwise, namely when
s_{0}>0
, the algorithm instead replaces
U^{r}
by
U
. Thus, in both cases the invariant is maintained.
∎

Next, we show that the
ComputeStrategy
subroutine satisfies a certain form of continuity. This will be crucial to establish that the output of our algorithm is an approximate BNE.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\delta>0
. Let
U,U^{\prime}\in[0,1]
with
|U-U^{\prime}|\leq\delta
and let
s
and
s^{\prime}
denote the output of
\textsf{{ComputeStrategy}}(U,\delta)
and
\textsf{{ComputeStrategy}}(U^{\prime},\delta)
respectively. Then we have

|s_{0}-s_{0}^{\prime}|\leq(6n^{2}L^{3}\varepsilon^{-4n}\alpha^{-2n})^{m}\delta.

Proof.

We proceed by induction. We will use
s_{i}
and
U_{i}
to denote the variables in the execution of
\textsf{{ComputeStrategy}}(U,\delta)
, and
s_{i}^{\prime}
and
U_{i}^{\prime}
to denote the variables in the execution of
\textsf{{ComputeStrategy}}(U^{\prime},\delta)
. We show that for all
i\in\{m,m-1,1,0\}
we have
|s_{i}-s_{i}^{\prime}|\leq M_{i}
and
|U_{i}-U_{i}^{\prime}|\leq M_{i}
, where
M_{i}:=(6n^{2}L^{3}\varepsilon^{-4n}\alpha^{-2n})^{m-i}\delta
. Clearly, this holds for
i=m
.

Now consider the
i
th step and assume the bound holds for all previous steps. In particular,
|s_{i}-s_{i}^{\prime}|\leq M_{i}
and
|U_{i}-U_{i}^{\prime}|\leq M_{i}
. Our goal is to show that
|s_{i-1}-s_{i-1}^{\prime}|\leq M_{i-1}
and
|U_{i-1}-U_{i-1}^{\prime}|\leq M_{i-1}
.

Observe that no matter which branch of the “if” statement we enter, we necessarily pick
s_{i-1}\in[b_{i},s_{i}]
satisfying

|(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(s_{i-1},s_{i})-\widehat{U}_{i}|\leq\delta

(21)

where

\widehat{U}_{i}:=\max\{(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(b_{i},s_{i}),\min\{U_{i},(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(s_{i},s_{i})\}\}

and similarly for
s_{i-1}^{\prime}
. Indeed, if we enter the first branch, then we must have
(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(s_{i},s_{i})\leq U_{i}
and thus
\widehat{U}_{i}=(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(s_{i},s_{i})
, since we always have
(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(b_{i},s_{i})<(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(s_{i},s_{i})
(because
s_{i}\geq b_{i+1}>b_{i}
). In that branch,
s_{i-1}
is set to
s_{i}
and thus we have
(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(s_{i-1},s_{i})=\widehat{U}_{i}
, which implies (
21
). Similarly, if we enter the second branch, it follows that
\widehat{U}_{i}=(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(b_{i},s_{i})
. Since in this branch we set
s_{i-1}=b_{i}
, we again have
(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(s_{i-1},s_{i})=\widehat{U}_{i}
, which implies (
21
). Finally, if we enter the third branch, then it must be that
\widehat{U}_{i}=U_{i}
and it is immediate that (
21
) holds.

By
Lemma

A.3
we know that
\Delta
is
nL
-Lipschitz. Using
Lemma

A.2
it follows that the function
(s_{i},U_{i})\mapsto\widehat{U}_{i}
is
(1+nL)
-Lipschitz, where we used that
|\cdot-b_{i}|\leq 1
and
|\Delta(\cdot,\cdot)|\leq 1
. It follows that
|\widehat{U}_{i}-\widehat{U}_{i}^{\prime}|\leq(1+nL)M_{i}
.

Let
g
be the function that maps
(x,y)
to the solution
z\in[b_{i},x]
of the equation
(x-b_{i})\Delta(z,x)=y
. In
Lemma

A.5
, we show that

is
(L\varepsilon^{-4n}(b_{i+1}-b_{i})^{-2n})
-Lipschitz. Furthermore, we know that
s_{i-1}=g(s_{i},\tilde{U}_{i})
for some
\tilde{U}_{i}
satisfying
|\tilde{U}_{i}-\widehat{U}_{i}|\leq\delta
. The same also holds for
s_{i-1}^{\prime}
. Thus, we have that
s_{i-1}=g(s_{i},\tilde{U}_{i})
and
s_{i-1}^{\prime}=g(s_{i}^{\prime},\tilde{U}_{i}^{\prime})

and

|\tilde{U}_{i}-\tilde{U}_{i}^{\prime}|\leq|\widehat{U}_{i}-\widehat{U}_{i}^{\prime}|+2\delta\leq(1+nL)M_{i}+2\delta\leq 2nLM_{i}.

As a result, we obtain that

|s_{i-1}-s_{i-1}^{\prime}|\leq(L\varepsilon^{-4n}(b_{i+1}-b_{i})^{-2n})2nLM_{i}\leq 2nL^{2}\varepsilon^{-4n}(b_{i+1}-b_{i})^{-2n}M_{i}.

Finally, observe that no matter which branch of the “if” statement we enter, we necessarily set

U_{i-1}:=(s_{i-1}-b_{i})\Delta(s_{i-1},s_{i})+\max\{0,U_{i}-(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(s_{i},s_{i})\}

and similarly for
U_{i-1}^{\prime}
. By
Lemma

A.3
we know that
\Delta
is
nL
-Lipschitz. By
Lemma

A.2
it follows that the function
(s_{i-1},s_{i})\mapsto(s_{i-1}-b_{i})\Delta(s_{i-1},s_{i})
is
(1+nL)
-Lipschitz, where we used that
|\cdot-b_{i}|\leq 1
and
|\Delta(\cdot,\cdot)|\leq 1
. Similarly, by
Lemma

A.2
it also follows that the function
(s_{i},U_{i})\mapsto\max\{0,U_{i}-(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(s_{i},s_{i})\}

(1+1+nL)
-Lipschitz. As a result, the function
(s_{i-1},s_{i},U_{i})\mapsto U_{i-1}
is

-Lipschitz, since

. Thus, we obtain that

|U_{i-1}-U_{i-1}^{\prime}|\leq 3nL\cdot 2nL^{2}\varepsilon^{-4n}(b_{i+1}-b_{i})^{-2n}M_{i}=6n^{2}L^{3}\varepsilon^{-4n}(b_{i+1}-b_{i})^{-2n}M_{i}.

Using the fact that

Proposition 4.7

(Approximate Equilibrium Conditions)
.

Let
\gamma\geq 0
.
Consider a symmetric strategy profile where all bidders use a strategy
\beta
, given by its canonical representation
0=s_{0}\leq s_{1}\leq\dots\leq s_{m}=1
. Furthermore, assume that there exist
U_{0},U_{1},\dots,U_{m}\in[0,1]
such that for any
i\in[m]
we have:

(1)

if
s_{i-1}<s_{i}
then
\lvert u(b_{i};s_{i})-U_{i}\rvert\leq\gamma
and
\lvert u(b_{i};s_{i-1})-U_{i-1}\rvert\leq\gamma

(2)

if
s_{i-1}=s_{i}
then
U_{i-1}=U_{i}
and
u(b_{i};s_{i})\leq U_{i}+\gamma

(3)

s_{i-1}\geq b_{i}

Then, this strategy profile constitutes a
2\gamma m
-BNE.

Proof.

To simplify notation, it is useful to distinguish between bids that are “actually” used by the strategy
\beta
, and those that are not.
In this proof, the former, i.e., bids
b_{i}
with
s_{i-1}<s_{i}
, will be called
effective bids
.
To begin with, consider two consecutive effective bids,
b_{j}
and
b_{j^{\prime}}
. This means that we have
s_{j-1}<s_{j}=s_{j^{\prime}-1}<s_{j^{\prime}}
and
s_{i}=s_{j}
for any
i
satisfying
j<i<j^{\prime}
.
By
(2)
, it must be that
U_{j}=U_{i}=U_{j^{\prime}-1}
for any
i
with
j<i<j^{\prime}
.
Then, by
(1)
, we get that
\left|u(b_{j};s_{j})-U_{j}\right|\leq\gamma\text{ and }\left|u(b_{j^{\prime}};s_{j})-U_{j}\right|\leq\gamma
.
From this, we can derive that, for any consecutive effective bids
b_{j}
and
b_{j^{\prime}}
it must be that:

\left|u(b_{j};s_{j})-u(b_{j^{\prime}};s_{j})\right|\leq 2\gamma

(22)

Furthermore, for any non-effective bid
b_{i}
lying between consecutive effective bids
b_{j}
and
b_{j^{\prime}}
,
(2)
yields

u(b_{i};s_{i})-u(b_{j^{\prime}};s_{i})\leq 2\gamma\quad\text{ and }\quad u(b_{i};s_{i})-u(b_{j};s_{i})\leq 2\gamma

(23)

To show that
\beta
is an approximate equilibrium, consider some value
v\in(s_{\ell-1},s_{\ell}]
, for some
\ell\in[m]
with
s_{\ell-1}<s_{\ell}
.
By definition of the strategies, we know that
\beta(v)=b_{\ell}
.
Consider any deviation to some
b_{i}
; we will show that this cannot increase the bidder’s utility by more than
2\gamma m
.
First, consider the case where
i<\ell
.
Define
j_{+}
to be the smallest index that is greater than or equal to
i
for which
b_{j_{+}}
is an effective bid, i.e.

j_{+}\coloneq\min\{j:i\leq j\wedge s_{j-1}<s_{j}\}

Notice that, if
b_{i}
is an effective bid, then
b_{i}=b_{j_{+}}
.
Let
b_{r_{1}},b_{r_{2}},\ldots,b_{r_{p}}
be the effective bids from
b_{j_{+}}
to
b_{\ell}
(inclusive).
Here, we note that it could be that
p=1
, in which case
b_{j_{+}}=b_{\ell}
, meaning that
b_{\ell}
is precisely the first effective bid that is greater than or equal to
b_{i}
.
Then, we have:

u(b_{i};v)-u(b_{\ell};v)=u(b_{i};v)-u(b_{j_{+}};v)+\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\left[u(b_{r_{k}};v)-u(b_{r_{k+1}};v)\right]

(24)

Consider the difference
u(b_{i};v)-u(b_{j_{+}};v)
.
As we mentioned earlier, we can see that if
b_{i}
is an effective bid, then
b_{i}=b_{j_{+}}
and this difference is equal to
0
. If
b_{i}
is not an effective bid, then
b_{j_{+}}
is the closest larger bid that is effective and by (
23
) we obtain that
u(b_{i};s_{i})-u(b_{j_{+}};s_{i})\leq 2\gamma
.
Using the definition of the utilities, we can rewrite the inequality as follows:

\displaystyle\qquad(s_{i}-b_{i})\Delta(s_{i-1},s_{i})-(s_{i}-b_{j_{+}})\Delta(s_{j_{+}-1},s_{j_{+}})\leq 2\gamma

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow s_{i}(\Delta(s_{i-1},s_{i})-\Delta(s_{j_{+}-1},s_{j_{+}}))-b_{i}\Delta(s_{i-1},s_{i})+b_{j_{+}}\Delta(s_{j_{+}-1},s_{j_{+}})\leq 2\gamma

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow v(\Delta(s_{i-1},s_{i})-\Delta(s_{j_{+}-1},s_{j_{+}}))-b_{i}\Delta(s_{i-1},s_{i})+b_{j_{+}}\Delta(s_{j_{+}-1},s_{j_{+}})\leq 2\gamma

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow u(b_{i};v)-u(b_{j_{+}};v)\leq 2\gamma

where in the second step we used the fact that
s_{i}\leq v
and
\Delta(s_{i-1},s_{i})-\Delta(s_{j_{+}-1},s_{j_{+}})\leq 0
, since the winning probability when bidding

is at least as large as when bidding

(because
b_{j_{+}}\geq b_{i}
).

Similarly, for any two consecutive effective bids
b_{r_{k}}
and
b_{r_{k+1}}
, by (

) we have
u(b_{r_{k}};s_{r_{k}})-u(b_{r_{k+1}};s_{r_{k}})\leq 2\gamma
. Using the same argument as above, we obtain that

. For this, we use the fact that

and the probability of winning when bidding

is at least as large as when bidding

.

Thus, looking at the sum of (

.
Hence, we obtain

The case for

follows by a symmetric argument.
Therefore, we have shown that, for any value

cannot increase a bidder’s utility by more than

is a

-BNE.
∎

5
Conclusion and Future Work

Our work provides the first efficient algorithms for computing equilibria in symmetric first-price auctions, with efficiency measured either by the number of queries to the distribution oracle (in the black-box model) or by running time (in the white-box model). The existence of these algorithms essentially settles the computational complexity of the problem for the case of i.i.d. private values, closing an important gap in the literature. This allows future work to focus on
asymmetric
auctions, in particular the case of bidders with independent private values (IPV), for which the computational complexity of equilibrium computation is still open.

Within the realm of symmetric auctions, the only (minor) gap left by our results in this paper is the case of inverse-exponential approximations in the implicit white-box model for the CCFPA, for which our algorithm is an FPTAS; in contrast, in the explicit-input model our corresponding algorithm computes an exact equilibrium in polynomial time. Additionally, our lower bound in
Section

3.1
for the black-box model suggests that any white-box algorithm for
\varepsilon
-approximate equilibria that runs in time polynomial in
\log(1/\varepsilon)
would need to “open the box” provided in the input. We suspect that the existence of such an algorithm would be effectively precluded by an associated PPAD-hardness result. Given the absence of PPAD-hardness results for the more general IPV case, however, obtaining a corresponding result for the symmetric auction setting seems quite challenging.

Finally, one could aim to generalize our algorithms to symmetric settings beyond auctions with i.i.d. private values. In particular, it would be meaningful to study the case of
symmetric affiliated private values
of
34
; for this case, the best known positive result is a PTAS due to
19
.

We do this iteratively, by computing the coefficients of the polynomial
representation of
F^{\mu}_{j}
, for all
\mu=1,2,\dots,n-1
. Let us denote
this representation by

F^{\mu}_{j}(x)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\mu d}b_{j,\mu,\ell}x^{\ell},\qquad\text{for}\;\;x\in[v_{j-1},v_{j}].

(31)

•

For
\mu=1
, we readily have our initial polynomial
representation, i.e.
b_{j,1,\ell}=a_{j,\ell}
for

\ell=0,1,\dots,[d]
.

•

For
\mu\geq 2
, it is

F_{j}^{\mu}(x)=F_{j}^{\mu-1}(x)\cdot F_{j}(x)=\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{(\mu-1)d}b_{j,\mu-1,\ell}x^{\ell}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}a_{j,\ell}x^{\ell}\right)

for
x\in[v_{j-1},v_{j}]
. Therefore, the
coefficients of the polynomial representation of
F_{j}^{\mu}

(see (
31
)) can be
computed, by performing
O(\mu d^{2})
multiplications and additions, as

b_{j,\mu,\ell}=\sum_{\xi=\max\{0,\ell-(\mu-1)d\}}^{\min\{\ell,d\}}b_{j,\mu-1,\ell-\xi}a_{j,\xi}\qquad\qquad\text{for}\;\;\ell=0,1,\dots,\mu d.

At the end of this iterative process, i.e. for
\mu=n-1
, we have computed
the polynomial representation of
F_{j}^{n-1}
; that is, parameters

\{b_{j,n-1,\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d}

in (
31
) for
\mu=n-1
. This
requires
O\left(\sum_{\mu=1}^{n-1}\mu d^{2}\right)=(n^{2}d^{2})
basic arithmetic
operations. It is important to also note that the magnitude of these
parameters is upper-bounded by

\max_{\ell=0,1,\dots,(n-1)d}b_{j,n-1,\ell}\leq(d+1)\cdot\max_{\ell}b_{j,n-2,\ell}\cdot\max_{\ell}a_{j,\ell}\leq\dots\leq(d+1)^{n}\left(\max_{\ell=0,1,\dots,d}a_{j,\ell}\right)^{n}

and, therefore,
their bit-size representation is polynomial (with respect to the binary
representation of the auction input). Performing the above computation for
all intervals in
F
’s representation, we can efficiently compute, with

O(kn^{2}d)
operations, the polynomial representations for all
F_{j}^{n-1}
,

j\in[k]
.

Based on this, we will next show that the integral
\int_{0}^{x}F^{n-1}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
, as a function of
x\in V=[0,1]
, can be succinctly expressed
as a piecewise polynomial function of degree (at most)
(n-1)d+1
. More
specifically, we will efficiently compute parameters

\{c_{j,\ell}\}_{j\in[k],\;\ell=0,1,\dots(n-1)d+1}
such that

\int_{0}^{x}F^{n-1}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d+1}c_{j,\ell}x^{\ell},\qquad\text{for all}\;\;x\in[v_{j-1},v_{j}],\;\;j\in[k].

(32)

We do that iteratively, over the intervals of
F
’s piecewise
representation:

•

For
j=1
, we have that for any
x\in[0,v_{1}]
it is:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}F^{n-1}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}F_{1}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{x}\sum_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d}b_{1,n-1,\ell}t^{\ell}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d}\frac{b_{1,n-1,\ell}}{\ell+1}t^{\ell+1}\right]_{t=0}^{t=x}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{(n-1)d+1}\frac{b_{1,n-1,\ell-1}}{\ell}t^{\ell},

where for the second equality we
are using the previously computed representation of
F_{1}^{n-1}

from (
31
). Therefore, we can
compute the desired
c_{i,\ell}
parameters by setting:

c_{1,0}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad c_{1,\ell}=\frac{b_{1,n-1,\ell-1}}{\ell}\quad\text{for}\;\;\ell=1,2,\dots,(n-1)d+1.

•

For
j=2,3,\dots,k
and
x\in[v_{j-1},v_{j}]
we can express the integral as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}F^{n-1}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{v_{j-1}}^{x}F^{n-1}_{j}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{v_{j-1}}F^{n-1}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{v_{j-1}}^{x}\sum_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d}b_{j,n-1,\ell}t^{\ell}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d+1}c_{j-1,\ell}v_{j-1}^{\ell},\ \ \ \ \text{using~\eqref{eq:CCFPA-succinct-poly-represent-powers-cdf} and~\eqref{eq:CCFPA-succinct-poly-represent-integral-numerator-equilibrium}}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d}\frac{b_{j,n-1,\ell}}{\ell+1}t^{\ell+1}\right]_{t=v_{j-1}}^{t=x}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d+1}c_{j-1,\ell}v_{j-1}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{(n-1)d+1}\frac{b_{j,n-1,\ell-1}}{\ell}x^{\ell}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{(n-1)d+1}\frac{b_{j,n-1,\ell-1}}{\ell}v_{j-1}^{\ell}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d+1}c_{j-1,\ell}v_{j-1}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{(n-1)d+1}\frac{b_{j,n-1,\ell-1}}{\ell}x^{\ell}+\underbrace{c_{j-1,0}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{(n-1)d+1}\left(c_{j-1,\ell}-\frac{b_{j,n-1,\ell-1}}{\ell}\right)v_{j-1}^{\ell}}_{\text{constant}},

so we need to set

\displaystyle c_{j,0}

\displaystyle=c_{j-1,0}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{(n-1)d+1}\left(c_{j-1,\ell}-\frac{b_{j,n-1,\ell-1}}{\ell}\right)v_{j-1}^{\ell}\ \text{ and }

\displaystyle c_{j,\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{j,n-1,\ell-1}}{\ell}\quad\text{for}\;\;\ell=1,2,\dots,(n-1)d+1.

Note that the iterative process above requires
O(kn^{2}d^{2})
basic arithmetic
operations, in order to compute all parameters
\{c_{j,\ell}\}
.

Finally, we can now argue about the succinct representation of the canonical
equilibrium bidding function in (
5
), as a
rational function. For any interval of
F
’s representation
[v_{j-1},v_{j}]
,

j\in[k]
, and any value
x\in[v_{j-1},v_{j}]
in this interval, we can use
representations (
31
)
and (
32
)
to write (
5
) as

\displaystyle\beta^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=x-\frac{\int_{0}^{x}F^{n-1}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}{F_{j}^{n-1}(x)}=x-\frac{\sum_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d+1}c_{j,\ell}x^{\ell}}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d}b_{j,n-1,\ell}x^{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d}b_{j,n-1,\ell}x^{\ell+1}-\sum_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d+1}c_{j,\ell}x^{\ell}}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d}b_{j,n-1,\ell}x^{\ell}}=\frac{c_{j,0}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{(n-1)d+1}(b_{j,n-1,\ell-1}-c_{j,\ell})x^{\ell}}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{(n-1)d}b_{j,n-1,\ell}x^{\ell}},

being the ratio of two polynomials, the one on the numerator having degree
(at most)
(n-1)d+1
and the one on the denominator having degree
(n-1)d
.
