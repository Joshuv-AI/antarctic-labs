 Run of River
 Run of River
 • Streaming hydro systems borrow a
 portion of the stream’s water to
 produce power, returning it to the
 stream after the energy is extracted
 while not changing the natural course
 of the stream or storing water for future
 use.
 o Little or no reservoir capacity to store
 water.
 o Typically thought to have less
 environmental impact than
 conventional.

 8

 Conventional Hydropower

• Conventional hydropower
  refers to the use of dams or
  impoundments to store water in
  a reservoir.
• Water released from the
  reservoir flows through a
  turbine to generate electricity.

 9

 In-Conduit
• Piped water with excess pressure can
  produce electricity using an in-conduit
  generator, rather than having the
  excess pressure relieved through a
  pressure-reducing valve.
• The in-conduit hydropower system
  places a turbine inside an existing
  pipeline or conduit and does not require
  a dam nor a flowing river, with minimum
  capital cost and environmental impacts.

 10

 Pumped Storage Hydropower (PSH)
• When energy is needed on the grid,
  the system sends water from a higher-
  elevation reservoir to a lower-elevation
  one through a series of pipes—turning
  electricity-generating turbines along
  the way.
• When there is low energy demand on
  the grid, water is pumped back up to
  the upper reservoir for later use.
• Scalable to also serve as a battery in
  smaller systems, e.g., a CO
  hydropower developer has
  designed one that can be scaled down
  to the size of an irrigation pump. Image: DOE

 U.S. Hydropower Potential
 Hydropower provides 6–8% of the nation’s electricity

• These assets provide cost-competitive, low-carbon, reliable, and flexible
  energy to the grid across the United States.
• Hydropower has the potential to increase its contribution to the nation’s clean
  generation portfolio.
  o At the end of 2022, the U.S. hydropower development pipeline included projects
 to construct 117 new facilities with a combined capacity of 1.2 GW, as well as 96
 pumped storage hydropower (PSH) projects, with a combined power storage
 capacity of 91 GW.
  o In addition, 23 active upgrade projects would increase the capacity of the
 existing fleet by 254 MW.
 Sources: U.S. Hydropower Market Report 2023 Edition (osti.gov) EIA, Electric Power Monthly (February 2024).

 13

 Hydropower Resource Potential
• Non-Powered Dams (Oak Ridge National Laboratory)
 • Dams that do not currently generate electricity.
 • Technical potential to add up to 12.1 GW (12,100 MW) at non-powered dams in the United States.
 o This estimate does not consider economic limitations on facility capacity—it does include assumptions
 that all of the water passing a facility can be converted to electrical energy and that hydraulic head is
 constant at facilities.
• New Stream Reach Potential (Oak Ridge National Laboratory)
 • Rivers and stream that do not have hydroelectric facilities.
 • The resource potential is 85 GW of capacity. When federally protected lands—national parks, national
 wild and scenic rivers, and wilderness areas—are excluded, the potential is over 65 GW of capacity or
 347 TWh/year of generation.
• PSH (National Renewable Energy Laboratory)
 • In the United States, there are currently 40 pumped storage plants in operation with a combined capacity of
 22 GW, accounting for 95% of all energy storage capacity in the power grid.

about 80,000 dams, of which
