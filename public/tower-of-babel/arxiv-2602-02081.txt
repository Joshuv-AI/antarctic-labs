Active learning from positive and unlabeled examples
Farnam Mansouri, Sandra Zilles, Shai Ben-David

Abstract
Learning from positive and unlabeled data (PU learning) is a weakly supervised variant of binary classification in which the learner receives labels only for (some) positively labeled instances, while all other examples remain unlabeled. Motivated by applications such as advertising and anomaly detection, we study an active PU learning setting where the learner can adaptively query instances from an unlabeled pool, but a queried label is revealed only when the instance is positive and an independent coin flip succeeds; otherwise the learner receives no information. In this paper, we provide the first theoretical analysis of the label complexity of active PU learning.



1
Introduction

Learning from positive and unlabeled data (PU learning) is a weakly supervised variant of binary classification in which the training data only consists of positively labeled and unlabeled examples. PU learning appears naturally in many real-world problems, including personal advertisement,
land cover classification
(
22
)
,
prediction of protein similarity
(
11
)
, as well as applications such as knowledge base completion
(
3
)
and disease-gene identification
(
32
)
.

In active learning, a learning algorithm is given access to a large pool of unlabeled examples, and is allowed to request the label of any particular example from that pool. This is in contrast with standard passive learning, where the data are assumed to be collected randomly and labeled independently of the learner’s choices. The objective of active learning is to learn an accurate classifier while requesting as few labels as possible, thus in particular reducing the workload of human annotators by carefully selecting the examples from the unlabeled pool that should be labeled.

Just as in standard machine learning, the labeling process can also be difficult for many PU learning applications. For example, in recommender systems, labels are often collected based on whether a recommendation is relevant (a positive outcome), while non-interactions are typically ambiguous and treated as unlabeled. Similarly, in anomaly detection, since anomalies are rare, the learner may only have access to data labeled as normal (which we view as the positive class). In both settings, obtaining labels can be costly, and may potentially benefit from more carefully selecting which examples from the unlabeled pool to query
(
27
;
30
)
.

We therefore study
active PU learning
, in which the learner may adaptively query instances, but the label request is given to an expert that is only able to detect positive labels for a portion of instances. We make the simplifying assumption that, for each queried positive instance, the expert detects its label independently with probability
\omega
. This assumption is equivalent to the
selected-completely-at-random
(SCAR) assumption, which is often employed in passive PU learning
(
23
;
5
;
10
;
2
;
25
)
.

Since the objective of active learning is to identify an accurate classifier with as small a number of queries as possible, a standard measure of evaluation in active learning is the so-called
label complexity
, i.e., the number of label requests that are necessary and sufficient to learn an accurate classifier. In this paper, we provide the first label complexity analysis for active PU learning, using a disagreement-based analysis. Our bounds depend critically on the
disagreement coefficient

\theta
, a standard quantity in the theory of active learning
(
9
;
15
;
16
;
17
;
18
)
. They also rely on a lower bound on the positive class prior
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
, without requiring the learner to know
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
in advance. Ignoring the dependence on
\omega
and
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
, our bounds incur only a multiplicative
\theta
gap compared to established label complexity bounds for the classical CAL algorithm in standard active learning
(
8
;
16
)
.

Our analysis is based on the assumption that the underlying data distribution is continuous. This is a natural assumption, as a substantial line of work in active learning studies settings where the instance distribution admits a density and the decision boundary (or regression function) satisfies smoothness or regularity conditions; under such assumptions, one can obtain refined label complexity guarantees via geometric and disagreement-based arguments
(
7
;
31
;
24
;
21
)
.

1.1
Related Work

While active learning has been extensively studied in the fully supervised setting, it has received only limited attention in the context of PU learning.

Notably, active PU learning (also referred to as active one-class classification) has been studied using a variety of uncertainty sampling approaches, including Query-by-Committee, least confidence, margin-based, and entropy-based sampling methods
(
1
;
14
;
13
;
28
)
. Prior work has also considered strategies that prioritize queries that are most likely to be positive
(
20
;
12
)
. More recently,
27
study active methods for empirical estimation of the class prior (i.e., the prevalence of positive examples) for PU learning.

However, with the exception of
(
27
)
, these works are purely empirical, and even
27
do not provide finite label complexity guarantees. In this context, our work is the first study establishing formal guarantees on the label complexity of active PU learning.

Preliminaries

2.1
Active Learning

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a
domain set
. The
hypothesis class

\mathcal{H}
is a set of functions
h:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
. By
\mathcal{D}
, we denote a distribution over
\mathcal{X}
called the data generating distribution. We use
\ell:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
to denote an underlying labeling rule.
In the study of active learning, there is a pool of data
\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}
sampled i.i.d. from
\mathcal{D}
. An active learner, at each time
t
, requests the label of an instance
x\in\mathcal{X}
from the pool and receives
\ell(x)
. The goal of the learner is to output a function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
that minimizes

\operatorname{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(f,\ell)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(x)\neq\ell(x)].

For any multiset
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
define

\hat{\operatorname{err}}_{S}(f,\ell):=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{x\in S}\mathbbm{1}\{f(x)\neq\ell(x)\}\,.

Also, define
\hat{\Pr}_{S}
as the empirical distribution induced by
S
, i.e., for any event
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
,
\Pr_{S}[A]
is defined as the fraction of elements in the multiset
S
that also belong to
A
. Formally,

\hat{\Pr}_{S}[A]:=\frac{|\{x\in S:x\in A\}|}{|S|}\,,

where both numerator and denominator account for elements in
S
with their multiplicity in
S
.

Definition 2.1
.

Define the pseudo metrics
\rho_{\mathcal{D}},\rho_{S}:2^{\mathcal{X}}\times 2^{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\Re^{\geq 0}
over
2^{\mathcal{X}}
by

\displaystyle\forall f,g\in 2^{\mathcal{X}},\;\rho_{\mathcal{D}}(f,g)

\displaystyle:=\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[f(x)\neq g(x)\right]\,,

\displaystyle\forall f,g\in 2^{\mathcal{X}},\;\rho_{S}(f,g)

\displaystyle:=\frac{|\{x:f(x)\neq g(x)\}|}{{|S|}}\,.

Definition 2.2

(Region of Disagreement
(
15
)
)
.

The region of disagreement of any set
V\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is defined as

\operatorname{DIS}(V)=\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\exists h_{1},h_{2}\in V\text{ s.t. }h_{1}(x)\neq h_{2}(x)\right\}\,.

Definition 2.3

(Disagreement Rate
(
15
)
)
.

The
disagreement rate
, and the
empirical disagreement rate
, respectively, of any set
V\subseteq\mathcal{H}
are defined as

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(V)

\displaystyle:=\Pr_{x\in\mathcal{D}}[x\in\operatorname{DIS}(V)]\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{S}(V)

\displaystyle:=\hat{\Pr}_{S}[\operatorname{DIS}(V)]\,.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
and
r>0
.
The
r
-ball around
f

with respect to
(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{H})
is defined
as

\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{D}}(f,r)=\left\{h\in\mathcal{H}:\rho_{\mathcal{D}}(f,h)\leq r\right\}\,.

For a multiset
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, define the
empirical
r
-ball
centered around
f
with respect to
(S,\mathcal{H})
as

\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{S}(f,r)=\left\{h\in\mathcal{H}:\rho_{S}(f,h)\leq r\right\}\,.

Definition 2.5

(Disagreement Coefficient
(
15
)
)
.

The disagreement coefficient
is defined as

\theta=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H},r>0}\frac{\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{D}}(h,r))}{r}\,.

Passive PU Learning

The study of learning from positive and unlabeled examples (PU learning) considers the setting where the learner has access only to positive examples and unlabeled data. In the following, we state an existing result from passive PU learning due to

Theorem 2.6

(Theorem 1 of
(

)
)

Let

\varepsilon,\delta>0

over

Setup

Fix
\omega\in(0,1]
and define a function
s:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
as follows. Suppose there is a coin that flips heads with probability
\omega
and tails with probability
1-\omega
. For every

, if

then
s(x)=0
. Otherwise, i.e., if
\ell(x)=1
, the coin is tossed; if it lands on heads, then

, else

. We assume an active PU learner which, at each time
t\in[n]
, can query a datum
x

; otherwise, the learner receives feedback

.

Moreover, denote

\pi_{\mathcal{D}}:=\mathcal{D}\!\big(\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\ell(x)=1\}\big),

and for any multiset
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
define

\hat{\pi}_{S}:=\frac{\lvert\{x\in S:\ell(x)=1\}\rvert}{\lvert S\rvert}.

Furthermore, define the positive disagreement region of
\mathcal{H}
as

\operatorname{DIS}^{P}(\mathcal{H}):=\{x\in\operatorname{DIS}(\mathcal{H}):\ell(x)=1\}.

Throughout this paper, we focus on the realizable setting; that is, we
assume
\ell\in\mathcal{H}
. We also assume that the VC dimension of
\mathcal{H}
is

\operatorname{VCD}(\mathcal{H})=d
.

Assumption 3.1
.

\mathcal{D}
is a continuous distribution, i.e., for every

we have

.

Remark 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution that satisfies Assumption

. Let

be an i.i.d. sample of

4
Analysis with Known
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}

In this section, we study active PU learning for the case when
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
is known. For this setting, we propose Algorithm
1
, which is simply the classical CAL algorithm of
8
applied to the restricted hypothesis class consisting of all
h\in\mathcal{H}
whose predicted positive mass over an unlabeled sample is approximately at most
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
.

In Theorem
4.3
we analyze the label complexity of this setup. The proof is inspired by Hanneke’s
(
16
)
analysis of the CAL algorithm for active learning. The key ideas behind the analysis are as follows: (i) The initial pruning of the hypothesis class ensures that, for every
h
,

\displaystyle\Pr[\ell(x)=0,h(x)=1]-\Pr[\ell(x)=1,h(x)=0]

\displaystyle\quad=\Pr[h(x)=1]-\Pr[\ell(x)=1]\lessapprox 0.

This means that the false positive rate can always be bounded from above by the false negative rate. Thus, using positively labeled data, we can bound the false negative rate with standard PAC bounds. Consequently, also the total error can be bounded. (ii) Using this with an argument similar to Hanneke’s
(
16
)
, we can show that after every
\tilde{O}(d\theta)
instances with labels revealed, the disagreement rate
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(V_{t})
can be cut in half with high probability. (iii) Using Lemma
4.2
, we guarantee that the proportion of positively labeled points in
\operatorname{DIS}(V_{t})
has a lower bound of
\Omega(1/\theta)

for all
t
. This ensures that the query response rate remains at least

\Omega(\omega/\theta)
.

5
Analysis with Unknown
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}

In this section, we study active PU learning in the more general setting where
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
is unknown. For this case, we propose Algorithm
3
, with the formal analysis presented in Theorem
5.5
.

When
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
is unknown, the learner is unable to restrict the version space to hypotheses whose predicted positive rate over a pool of unlabeled data is close to
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
. This increases the difficulty of the problem, as in the previous section we were only able to control the response rate of queries in Lemma
4.2
by exploiting this property. The key idea in our analysis is to employ a binary-search–style procedure for estimating
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
and restricting the version space accordingly.

We first obtain an estimate
\hat{\omega}
of
\omega
, using Algorithm
2
. By Theorem
5.3
, this estimate is within a constant factor of the true value
\omega
. We then sample a large unlabeled set
S_{1}
. For each iteration
i
, let
u_{i}
and
b_{i}
denote upper and lower bounds, respectively, on
\hat{\pi}_{S_{1}}
, the empirical positive rate in
S_{1}
. We initialize
u_{0}=1
. At iteration
i
, we restrict the version space to hypotheses whose predicted positive rate over
S_{1}
is at most
u_{i}
, and we set
b_{i}
to be the fraction of samples in
S_{1}
that are predicted positive by all
h\in V_{i}
.

In Lemma
5.4
, we show that whenever the response rate of queries from
\operatorname{DIS}(V_{i})
is less than
O(\hat{\omega}/\theta)
, it must hold that

\hat{\pi}_{S_{1}}<\tfrac{u_{i}+b_{i}}{2}.

In this case, we update the upper bound by
u_{i+1}\leftarrow(u_{i}+b_{i})/2
.

The empirical error (which is close to the true error) of each classifier is always bounded from above by
2(u_{i}-b_{i})
. Consequently, such a halving can occur at most
O(\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon))
times. After this point, for all remaining queries we are guaranteed a response rate of at least
\Omega(\omega/\theta)
. From then on, the analysis proceeds analogously to the case where
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
is known. In particular, using
\tilde{O}(d\theta)
queries drawn from
\mathcal{D}
and restricted to instances
x\in\operatorname{DIS}(V_{i})
, we can reduce
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(V_{i})
by a factor of two.

6
Conclusion and Open Problems

This paper provides the first formal label complexity analysis for active learning from positive and unlabeled data. Our main result establishes that the label complexity of active PU learning can be bounded by

\displaystyle O\Bigg(\frac{\ln(1/\varepsilon)\,\theta^{2}\,\big(d\ln(\theta)+\ln\ln(1/\varepsilon)+\ln(1/\delta)\big)}{\omega}\;+

\displaystyle\frac{\theta\big(d\ln(\theta/\pi_{\mathcal{D}})+\ln(1/\delta)\big)}{\pi_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}\,\omega}\Bigg).

Ignoring the second term, which is independent of
\varepsilon
, this bound differs from the classical active learning label complexity bound of
(
16
)
for the CAL algorithm,

\ln(1/\varepsilon)\,\theta\,\big(d\ln(\theta)+\ln\ln(1/\varepsilon)+\ln(1/\delta)\big),

by only a factor of
\theta/\omega
. The dependence on
\omega
in our bound is inherent to the PU setting, since the effective rate of obtaining labels is always proportional to
\omega
. Whether the
\theta^{2}
dependence in our active PU learning bound is fundamental, or can be reduced to
\theta
, remains an open question.

The additional
\theta
factor in our bound stems from the fact that, in our analysis, the rate at which positive instances are queried is at most
1/\theta
. A similar phenomenon is already observed in passive PU learning. In particular,
25
established that the number of positive examples required for learning is bounded from below by

\Omega\!\left(\frac{d+\ln(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right).

However, collecting this many random positive examples requires querying as many as

\Omega\!\left(\frac{d+\ln(1/\delta)}{\pi_{\mathcal{D}}\varepsilon}\right)

random pool examples, incurring an unavoidable
1/\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
overhead in terms of sample complexity compared to fully supervised passive learning.

A second direction for future work is the study of active PU learning under discrete distributions. In such settings, a fundamental difficulty arises: if querying an instance with large probability mass yields no feedback, then the learner may no longer be able to extract additional information from that instance, rendering learning impossible.
One possible approach to address this issue is to allow the learner, at test time, to refrain from labeling instances that received the feedback
\star
during training. However, this introduces a new challenge, as the i.i.d. property on the selected instances (see Remark
3.2
) would no longer hold.

A third problem left for future research is to analyze the label complexity of active PU learning in the general agnostic setting.
