MUC1 promotes the proliferation and invasion of lung cancer cells by regulating PI3K/AKT pathway
Authors: Yang Liu, Zhiping Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mucin 1 (MUC1) has been found to be significantly up-regulated in various types of cancer. However, the precise role and underlying mechanisms of MUC1 in lung cancer remain elusive. In this study, MUC1 expression was markedly elevated in lung cancer tissues and cell lines. Patients with low MUC1 expression showed improved overall survival rates compared to those with high expression levels. Additionally, knockdown of MUC1 significantly inhibited the viability and proliferation of A549 and H1299 cells, as demonstrated by CCK-8, EdU and colony formation assays. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that down-regulation of MUC1 induced apoptosis, decreased the protein expression levels of Bcl-2, and increased the expressions of Bax, caspase-3 and caspase-9 in both cell lines. Additionally, wound healing, Transwell migration and invasion assays indicated that down-regulation of MUC1 suppressed the migration and invasion capacities of A549 and H1299 cells, while also reducing the expression levels of MMP-2, MMP-9 and COX-2. Moreover, sh-MUC1 inhibited the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) progress in both cell lines. Interestingly, low MUC1 expression was associated with reduced activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. As expected, over-expression of MUC1 exerted the opposite effects. Furthermore, the PI3K/AKT inhibitor LY294002 enhanced the effects of sh-MUC1 on the proliferation, apoptosis, migration, invasion and EMT progress, while the activator SC79 partially restored these effects. Finally, down-regulation of MUC1 inhibited the tumorigenesis in nude mice by modulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. To conclude, these findings suggested that MUC1 played an important role in lung cancer progression, potentially through the PI3K/AKT pathway. This positioned MUC1 as a molecule worthy of further investigation for its therapeutic potential.

Introduction

The incidence and mortality rates of lung cancer rank highest among all cancer types globally [1]. Annually, there are approximately 2.1 million new cases and 1.77 million deaths caused by lung cancer worldwide [2]. In China, around 600, 000 new lung cancer cases are diagnosed each year, leading to 490, 000 deaths, which accounts for 24.9% of all malignant tumors [3]. Clinical studies indicate that the treatment rate for lung cancer in situ approaches 100%, while the 5-year survival rate for patients with stage I lung cancer ranges between 60% and 90%. In contrast, the 5-year survival rate drops to only 5–20% for those diagnosed with stage III and IV diseases, underscoring the critical importance of early diagnosis for patient survival [4]. Although numerous large-scale clinical trials have evaluated lung cancer screening methods, including chest X-ray, sputum cytology, and chest CT, these methods present several limitations [5]. Furthermore, the accuracy of tumor markers, such as carcinoembryonic antigen, neuron-specific enolase and CYFRA21-1, in early diagnosis and prognosis remains relatively low [6]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify a convenient, safe, and effective method for the early detection and prognostic evaluation of lung cancer.

Mucin 1 (MUC1) is a high-molecular-weight glycoprotein secreted by epithelial cells, and is located on chromosome 1q21, playing essential roles in lubricating and protecting epithelial surfaces of the choroid [7]. It has been reported that altered expression of MUC1 significantly influences tumorigenesis and cell differentiation [8]. In many malignancies, MUC1 expression is aberrant, partially in cancers originating from epithelial cells, such as the salivary glands, digestive tract, respiratory tract, breast, and reproductive systems [9–11]. Current studies reveal MUC1 expression is elevated in lung cancer tissues compared with that in adjacent tissues. However, where its expression influences the biological behavior of lung cancer cells remains insufficiently explored [12]. It is well established that treatment failure in lung cancer often results from local tumors and distant metastasis [13]. Investigating the biological functions of lung cancer cells, including gene regulation of proliferation, apoptosis, metastasis, autophagy, and metabolism, as well as elucidating the signaling pathways involved in malignant behavior, may lay a new foundation for clinical drug development and potentially lead to breakthroughs in lung cancer treatment [14]. Therefore, examining the effects of MUC1 modulation on the biological behavior of lung cancer holds significant relevance for clinical therapeutic strategies.

Signaling pathways serve as critical mechanisms through which cells receive and integrate external cues to regulate gene expression, and subsequently influence cellular functions such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, and migration [15]. The phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) is one of the most commonly dysregulated signaling cascades in tumor development [16]. The mechanism involves activated PI3K collaborating with 3-phosphate inositol dependent protein kinase 1 (PDK1) to phosphorylate AKT at Ser473 and Thr308 sites, leading to AKT activation. Activated AKT then translocates to the nucleus to modulate downstream effectors, thereby regulating cell survival, proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and invasion [17–19]. Researches indicate that the PI3K/AKT pathway is abnormally activated in many malignancies, including lung cancer, liver cancer, and gastrointestinal tumors, and is closely associated with tumor initiation and progression [20–22]. Previous studies have demonstrated that MUC1 knockdown inhibited the migration and invasion of oral squamous cell carcinoma by suppressing the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [23]. Nevertheless, the role of the PI3K/AKT pathway in MUC1 mediated regulation of lung cancer cells has not been studied. Hence, this study was designed to investigate whether MUC1 exerted its functional role in the proliferation and invasion of lung cancer by regulating the PI3K/AKT pathway.

Materials and methods

Analysis of MUC1 expression

The expression profile of MUC1 among patients with lung cancer was analyzed using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Additionally, the prognostic significance of MUC1 expression was evaluated through Kaplan-Meier survival analysis.

Clinical samples

A total of 10 paired samples of cancerous and adjacent normal tissues were obtained from patients diagnosed with lung cancer, who underwent surgical resections at General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University. None of the patients had received prior radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy or interventional therapy. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University (NMU-20240828).

Cell culture and transfection

The human bronchial epithelioid cell line 16HBE, and human lung cancer cell lines H1299, A549, H520 and H358 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and cultured in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator (5% CO2). To modulate MUC1 expression, MUC1 over-expression plasmid (plvx-MUC1), empty vector (plvx-NC), as well as sh-MUC1, and sh-NC were constructed. During the logarithmic growth phase, lung cancer cells were separately transfected with lentiviruses carrying sh-NC, sh-MUC1, plvx-NC, and plvx-MUC1. Stably knocked down and over-expressed cell lines were then selected using puromycin (5 µg/mL, Sigma).

QRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from lung cancer tissues and cell lines using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.), and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the TaqMan Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). Routine qRT-PCR was performed on an ABI 7300-fast RT-PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) with the SYBR Green PCR Kit (Qiagen, Inc.) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference gene. The primer sequences used were as follows: MUC1 forward, 5’-CGTCGTGGACATTG ATGGTACC-3’, and reverse, 5’-GGTACCTCCTCTCACCTCCTCCAA-3’. GAPDH forward, 5’-TGG AAGGTGGACAGTGAG-3’, and reverse, 5’-GAGGGAAATCGTGCGTGAC-3’.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from tissues and cells using RIPA lysis buffer, and quantified with a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology). Proteins were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (EMD Millipore). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following washes, membranes were probed with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1: 2, 000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (EMD Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA) and quantified with ImageJ software (NIH, version 4.3). Primary antibodies used were as follows: anti-MUC1 (1: 1, 000, ab109185), anti-Ki67 (1: 1, 000, ab16667), anti-PCNA (1: 1, 000, ab92552), anti-Bax (1: 1, 000, ab182733), anti-Bcl-2 (1: 1, 000, ab182858), anti-caspase-3 (1: 1, 000, ab184787), anti-caspase-9 (1: 1, 000, ab202068), anti-MMP-2 (1: 1, 000, ab92536), anti-MMP-9 (1: 1, 000, ab283575), anti-COX-2 (1: 1, 000, ab179800), anti-PI3K (1: 1, 000, ab302958), anti-p-PI3K (1: 1, 000, ab278545), anti-AKT (1: 1, 000, ab8805), anti-p-AKT (1: 1, 000, ab38449), and anti-GAPDH (1: 1, 000, ab8245). All antibodies were purchased from Abcam.

CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit (CCK-8) (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology). Briefly, lung cancer cells were seeded into 96-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well) and transfected for 48 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 regent was added to each well, and after incubation, the optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

EdU assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). ‌Cells from different treatment groups were incubated with 10 µM EdU for 2 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and stained using the Click-iT EdU Alexa Fluor 488 Kit (Invitrogen). Fluorescence images were captured with an Olympus IX73 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The proliferation rate was calculated as the ratio of EdU-positive cells to total DAPI-stained nuclei.

Colony formation assay

The clonogenic ability of lung cancer cells were examined via colony formation assay. Cells were seeded into 6-well plates (1 × 103/well) and cultured for 2 weeks, with medium refreshed every 2–3 d. Subsequently, colonies were fixed and stained with 1% crystal violet for 30 min at room temperature, and images were captured using a light microscope (Nikon Corporation).

Flow cytometric apoptosis analysis

The apoptotic rate of the transfected cells was detected by flow cytometry. Transfected cells were collected 48 h later. The samples were washed with PBS, and FITC-labeled Annexin V and PI (5 µL each) were added to 500 µL cell suspension, prior to mixing and incubation at room temperature for 5–15 min in the dark. The rate of apoptosis was then detected by flow cytometry.

Wound healing assay

Cell migration ability was assessed by wound healing assay. Lung cancer cells were transfected in the logarithmic phase and then seeded into 6-well plates at the appropriate density. The medium was replaced with DMEM containing 1% FBS, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C. When the cells have grown to 100% confluence, a uniform scratch was made using a 200-µL pipette tip. After 0 and 48 h of incubation, wound closure was observed using an inverted microscope (200x) and the wound-closure distance was recorded.

Transwell assay

Cell migration and invasion were evaluated using Transwell chambers. Lung cancer cells were trypsinized into a single cell suspension, washed 3 times in serum-free medium, and then adjusted to a concentration of 2 × 105/mL. For Transwell invasion assay, Matrigel was pre-diluted with serum-free DMEM at a ratio of 1: 3, uniformly covered with polycarbonate film on the Transwell chamber, and left to solidify for 1 h at room temperature. The lower chambers were filled with medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. Following a 48-h incubation period at 37 °C at room temperature, the migrated and invaded cells through the membrane were stained with 0.1% crystal violet and counted under a microscope (Nikon Corporation).

Xenograft model

BALB/c nude mice (4–5 weeks old) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Stable cells transfected with sh-NC, sh-MUC1, plvx-NC, and plvx-MUC1 suspended in PBS, were subcutaneously injected into the mice. Tumor volume was measured every 4 d, and after 4 weeks, mice were sacrificed and the tumors were excised and weighted. All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University (NMU-202401008) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines.

HE staining

Tumor tissues were embedded in paraffin. After dewaxing, tissue sections were rehydrated with gradient ethanol (100%, 95%, and 75%, each for 30 s). The sections were stained with hematoxylin for 10 min, rinsed with tap water for 2 s, and then stained with eosin for 5 min. After rinsing with tap water for 1 s, the sections were dehydrated with gradient ethanol (75%, 95%, and 100%, each for 5 s) and air-dried. The tumor structure changes were observed under a light microscope.

Immunohistochemical assay (IHC) analysis

The tumor tissues were firstly fixed by 4% PFA. Consecutive 4 μm sections were cut to used for IHC analysis. The sections were blocked with 4% BSA for 30 min, and then incubated with anti-Ki-67 antibodies (Abcam) for 2 h at room temperature. Following that, the goat-rabbit secondary antibody with high-sensitivity streptavidin-HRP conjugate (Abcam) was incubated. DAB was used as the chromogen, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were taken with a microscope (Nikon, Tokyo).

TUNEL staining

Briefly, paraffin-embedded tissue Sect. (5 μm) were fixed in 4% PFA for 1 h at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 in 0.1% sodium citrate for 30 min on ice. After washed with PBS, samples were incubated with TUNEL reaction mixture for 1 h at 37 °C in a humidified chamber. Nuclei were counterstained with 1 µg/mL DAPI for 5 min. Fluorescence images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed suing SPSS Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Emon. New York, United States), GraphPad Prism 9 software from GraphPad Inc. in La Jolla, CA, United States, as well as Origin Pro 2018 C (OriginLab Corp., mom USA). Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated with R software (version 3.6.3). Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Independent sample t-tests were used for comparison between two groups, and one-way ANOVA was used for multiple group comparisons. A P-value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

MUC1 was highly expressed in lung cancer tissues and cells

Firstly, a pan-cancer analysis of MUC1 was conducted using the TCGA database. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, MUC1 expression showed an up-regulated trend in various cancers, including lung cancer, compared to normal tissues. To further validate these findings, the expression level of MUC1 in 10 pairs of lung cancer and adjacent non-cancerous tissues was evaluated by qRT-PCR and western blot assays. Data presented in Fig. 1B and C showed that MUC1 expression was significantly elevated in lung cancer tissues. Moreover, Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis revealed that lung cancer patients with high MUC1 expression was associated with a poorer survival rate compared to those with low expression (Fig. 1D). Subsequently, the expression of MUC1 in several lung cancer cell lines (H1299, A549, H520 and H358) and HBE cells was further investigated. Consistent with the tissue data, MUC1 was consistently expressed at higher levels in all cancer cell lines (Fig. 1E and F). These findings suggested that MUC1 was over-expressed in lung cancer, and its higher expression was associated with an unfavorable prognosis in the analyzed cohort.

MUC1 promoted proliferation and suppressed apoptosis of lung cancer cells

To investigate the functional role of MUC1 in lung cancer progression, sh-NC, sh-MUC1, plvx-NC, and plvx-MUC1 were transfected into lung cancer cells, and the transfection efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR and western blot assays (Fig. 2A and B). Cell viability, as assessed by CCK-8 assay, was significantly inhibited upon MUC1 knockdown, whereas MUC1 over-expression enhanced cell viability (Fig. 2C). EdU assay was carried out to assess the effects of MUC1 on the proliferation of lung cancer cells. As demonstrated in Fig. 2D, down-regulation MUC1 resulted in reduced proliferation of lung cancer cells, while up-regulation of MUC1 resulted in increased proliferation of lung cancer cells. Colony formation assay was carried out to assess the effects of MUC1 on the clonogenic ability of lung cancer cells, and the data of Fig. 2E indicated that down-regulation of MUC1 suppressed the formation of of lung cancer cell colonies, and up-regulation of MUC1 enhanced the formation of of lung cancer cell colonies. In addition, western blot was performed to analyze the expressions of proteins related to proliferation. As demonstrated in Fig. 2F, down-regulation of MUC1 correlated with suppressed expression levels of Ki67 and PCNA, and up-regulation of MUC1 showed the opposite effects. Moreover, flow cytometry analysis was adopted to evaluate the effects of MUC1 on the apoptosis of lung cancer cells, and the data of Fig. 3A displayed that down-regulation of MUC1 led to an increase in the apoptosis of lung cancer cells, while up-regulation resulted in a decrease. Furthermore, western blot was performed to analyze the expressions of proteins related to apoptosis. As demonstrated in Fig. 3B, down-regulation of MUC1 reduced the protein expression level of Bcl-2, while promoted those of Bax, Caspase-3 and Caspase-9 in lung cancer cells. Up-regulation of MUC1 showed the opposite effects. These findings were consistent with the interpretation that altering MUC1 expression levels could influence the proliferation and apoptotic behaviors of lung cancer cells in the experimental setting.

MUC1 enhanced migration and invasion of lung cancer cells by inducing EMT

Moreover, wound healing and Transwell assays were performed to analyze the effects of MUC1 on the migration and invasion of lung cancer cells. As shown in Fig. 4A, MUC1 knockdown impeded wound closure, while MUC1 over-expression accelerated it. Transwell migration and invasion assays yielded consistent results that MUC1 silencing suppressed cell migration and invasion, whereas MUC1 over-expression promoted these processes (Fig. 4B and C). Western blot was performed to analyze the expressions of proteins related to metastasis. As demonstrated in Fig. 4D, down-regulation of MUC1 reduced the expression levels of MMP-2, MMP-9 and COX-2, and up-regulation of MUC1 showed the opposite effects. To explore the underlying mechanisms, western blot was performed to analyze the expressions of EMT markers. As demonstrated in Fig. 4E, down-regulation of MUC1 increased the protein expression level of E-cadherin, while decreased the expression levels of N-cadherin and Vimentin. Up-regulation of MUC1 showed the opposite effects. These findings suggested that MUC1 enhanced migration and invasion of lung cancer cells by potentially influencing EMT progress.

MUC1 promoted lung cancer progression through PI3K/AKT pathway activation

Given the critical role of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in lung cancer progression [24], we investigated whether MUC1 exerted its effects via this pathway. Western blot analysis showed that down-regulation of MUC1 was associated with reduced levels of PI3K/AKT pathway, while up-regulation of MUC1 was associated with increased levels (Fig. 5A). To further confirm the involvement of the PI3K/AKT pathway, sh-MUC1-transfected cells were treated with PI3K/AKT inhibitor (LY294002) and activator (SC79), and the expression of PI3K/AKT pathway was further detected (Fig. 5B). Moreover, functional assays demonstrated that LY294002 potentiated the inhibitory effects of sh-MUC1 on cell viability (CCK-8, Fig. 6A), proliferation (EdU, Fig. 6B, colony formation, Fig. 6C), and migration/invasion (wound healing and Transwell, ‌Fig. 6EG‌), while also enhancing apoptosis (flow cytometry, ‌Figure 6D‌). Conversely, SC79 partially reversed these effects. These findings suggested that the oncogenic functions of MUC1 observed were closely linked to the activity status of the PI3K/AKT pathway.

MUC1 promoted tumor development in vivo

To validate our in vitro findings, we established a xenograft tumor model by subcutaneously injecting nude mice with A549 cells stably expressing sh-NC, sh-MUC1, plvx-NC, or plvx-MUC1. Down-regulation of MUC1 inhibited the increase of tumor volume and weight, while up-regulation of MUC1 promoted tumor growth (Fig. 7A and B). HE staining of tumor sections revealed that tumor cells in the sh-MUC1 group of nude mice began to regress and nuclear fragments were produced in the tumor tissues, while the tumor tissue lesions in the plvx-MUC1 group of nude mice were more severe (Fig. 7C). IHC analysis demonstrated that Ki-67 expression, a marker of cell proliferation, was decreased in the sh-MUC1 group, and increased the plvx-MUC1 group (Fig. 7D). Moreover, TUNEL staining indicated a higher number of apoptotic cells in tumors from the sh-MUC1 group compared to controls, and fewer apoptotic cells in the plvx-MUC1 group (Fig. 7E). Finally, western blot analysis of tumor lysates confirmed that down-regulation of MUC1 suppressed the expression levels of MUC1 and PI3K/AKT pathway, while up-regulation of MUC1 showed the opposite effects (Fig. 7F and H). These findings suggested that MUC1 contributed to tumor development in vivo through a mechanism involving PI3K/AKT signaling pathway activation.

Discussion

Lung cancer is a malignant tumor that has attracted worldwide attention [25]. Numerous foundational studies both domestically and internationally have investigated gene-based interventions in lung cancer cells. For example, mesenchymal homologous box protein 1 (MEOX1) has been shown to inhibit the growth of lung cancer cells by suppressing the cell cycle checkpoint gene (CCNB1) [26]. Translocated methylcytosine dioxygenase (TET) 2 could restrain the migration and invasion of lung cancer cells [27]. Ubiquitin binding enzyme E2T (UBE2T) promoted autophagy in lung cancer via the p53/adenosine monophosphate protein kinase (AMPK)/rapamycin target protein (mTOR) signaling pathway [28]. Gene-targeted interventions for lung cancer have progressively emerged as a prominent focus of research.

MUC1, a type I transmembrane glycoprotein, plays a crucial role in mediating tumor growth and cell differentiation across various cancer types [8]. Existing research indicates that the expression level of MUC1 in lung cancer was higher than that in non-cancerous cells [29]. Our study also found high expression of MUC1 in both lung cancer tissues and cell lines. However, the precise impact of MUC1 on the malignant phenotype of lung cancer was not yet clear. In this study, we focused on stable lung cancer cell lines with either silenced or over-expressed MUC1 to explore its effects on cellular proliferation and invasion abilities. Firstly, CCK-8, colony formation, and EdU assays were performed to evaluate the effects of MUC1 on the viability and proliferation of lung cancer cells. The results showed that MUC1 down-regulation suppressed the viability and proliferation of lung cancer cells, while up-regulation of MUC1 showed the opposite effects. These findings were consistent with previous reports. For example, Tsutsumida et al. found that inhibition of MUC1 reduced the proliferative ability of S2-013 cells [30]. Similarly, Kufe D et al. reported that over-expression of MUC1 in 3Y1 cells induced malignant transformation and increased colony forming ability [31].

It has been reported that the abnormal over-expression of MUC1 can promote the migration and invasion of cancer cells, including breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, lung cancer, kidney cancer and other cells, thereby facilitating tumor metastasis [32, 33]. Lan et al. found that down-regulating MUC1 expression increased the intercellular aggregation ability of oral squamous cell carcinoma cells [34]. To investigate the effects of MUC1 on the migration and invasion abilities of lung cancer cells, wound healing and Transwell assays were performed. As anticipated, MUC1 silencing decreased the migration and invasion abilities of lung cancer cells, while up-regulation of MUC1 showed the opposite effects. Intercellular adhesion connections, primarily composed of cadherin-catenin complexes, are essential for maintaining epidermal morphology and mediating cell-cell interactions [35]. Sahraei et al. reported that down-regulation of MUC1 in pancreatic cancer PANC1 cells and breast cancer MCF-7 cells resulted in increased expression of E-cadherin [36]. Consistently, our data showed that down-regulation of MUC1 increased the protein expression level of E-cadherin, while decreased the expression levels of N-cadherin and Vimentin in lung cancer cells. Conversely, up-regulation of MUC1 yielded the opposite effects on these EMT markers.

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is closely related to tumor cell growth and has been extensively studied in the context of lung cancer [37]. For example, Wu et al. displayed that SJB2-043, a USP1 inhibitor, suppressed the proliferation, migration and EMT progress in lung cancer cells through modulating PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [38]. Yang et al. indicated that MIR4435-2HG enhanced the migration and promotion of lung cancer cells by targeting the miR-371a-5p/PI3K/AKT axis [39]. Moreover, FOXA1 regulated HER2 transcription to enhance the tumorigenic capacity of lung cancer through PI3K/AKT pathway [40]. In our study, down-regulation of MUC1 inhibited the expression levels of the PI3K/AKT pathway, whereas up-regulation of MUC1 enhanced the expression levels of PI3K/AKT pathway. The PI3K/AKT inhibitor LY294002 enhanced the effects of MUC1 knockdown on the viability, proliferation, apoptosis, migration and invasion of lung cancer cells. In contrast, the PI3K/AKT activator SC79 partially restored these cellular functions in MUC1-knockdown cells. Furthermore, in vivo validation using xenograft models further corroborated these findings. MUC1 knockdown reduced tumor growth, decreased Ki-67 (a proliferation marker) proliferation, increased apoptotic cell counts, and inhibited PI3K/AKT pathway activation, consistent with our in vitro observations.

Although these results advanced our understanding of MUC1’s role in lung cancer, several limitations warranted careful consideration. First, while the reliance on in vitro cell lines and xenograft models was informative, it might not fully simulate the complexity of the human tumor microenvironment and patient heterogeneity. Future studies utilizing patient-derived organoids or orthotopic models can strengthen the translational relevance. Second, the clinical correlation relied on retrospective data from public database (TCGA) and a relatively small single-center cohort, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of MUC1’s prognostic value. Future verification requires multi-centered, large-sample prospective studies. Third, our investigation primarily focused on on the PI3K/AKT pathway. The potential crosstalk with other critical signaling pathways (e.g., MAPK, wnt/β-catenin) in mediating MUC1’s effects remains unexplored and warrants further research. Finally‌, although MUC1 silencing showed promising anti-tumor effects in vivo, significant translational hurdles, such as the development of efficient and safe in vivo delivery systems for MUC1-targeting agents, and potential off-target effects, need to be addressed before clinical application.

Conclusion

To conclude, the findings of this study revealed that MUC1 was observed to be up-regulated in lung cancer tissues and cell lines, and that high MUC1 expression was associated with poor patient prognosis. Moreover, functional experiments confirmed that MUC1 promoted proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT, while inhibited apoptosis in lung cancer cells. Mechanistically, MUC1 exerted its oncogenic effects by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. These findings indicated that targeting MUC1-mediated PI3K/AKT pathway activation may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for the clinical treatment of lung cancer.
