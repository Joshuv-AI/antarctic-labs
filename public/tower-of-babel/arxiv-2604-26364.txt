Automaton-based characterisations of first order logic over infinite trees
Massimo Benerecetti \rsuper a, Dario Della Monica \rsuper b, Angelo Matteo \rsuper b, Fabio Mogavero \rsuper a, Gabriele Puppis \rsuper b

Abstract
. We study the expressive power of First-Order Logic ( FO ) over (unordered) infinite trees, with the aim of identifying robust characterisations in terms of branching-time specification formalisms. While such correspondences are well understood in the linear-time setting, the branching-time case presents well-known structural challenges. To this end, we introduce two classes of hesitant tree automata and show that they capture precisely the expressive power of two branching-time temporal logics, namely PastCTL ± and CTL ∗ f {}^{*}_{f} , both of which have been previously shown to be equivalent to FO over infinite trees. These results provide uniform automata-theoretic characterisations and yield a natural normal form for the latter in terms of a new fragment of CTL ∗ called CTL ∗ ± {}^{*}_{\pm} . As a consequence, we identify a fundamental limitation of FO in this setting: along each branch, it can express only properties that are either safety or co-safety, thereby revealing a sharp expressive boundary for first-order definability over infinite trees.

Words and trees.

A

over an alphabet
\Sigma

w=a_{1}\dots a_{n}
of letters from
\Sigma

to denote the letter from
\Sigma

We denote by

\Sigma

Similarly, we define an
\omega
-word
over
\Sigma

\Sigma
;
we denote by

the set of all

-words over
\Sigma
, and call
\omega
-language

any subset of
\Sigma^{\omega}

We shall also work with trees, and precisely with unranked, unordered, leafless trees.
By

we mean that every node can have any finite number of immediate successors;
by

we mean that trees cannot be distinguished by just reordering the successors of a node;
by

we mean that every node has at least one successor.
We adopt the usual terminology for trees: ancestor, descendant, parent, sibling, root, etc.
We denote by
\mathop{Dom}(t)
the set of nodes of a tree
t
.
Given an alphabet
\Sigma
, a
\Sigma
-labelled tree
(
\Sigma
-tree for
short) is a tree
t
equipped with a labelling
\lambda_{t}:\mathop{Dom}(t)\to\Sigma
,
which associates a letter from
\Sigma
with each node of
t
.
By a slight abuse of notation, we also denote the resulting
\Sigma
-tree by
t
,
and given
v\in\mathop{Dom}(t)
, we write
t(v)
for the label assigned to
v
by
\lambda_{t}
.
A
tree language
over
\Sigma
is a set of
\Sigma
-trees.
We denote by
T_{\Sigma}
the language of all
\Sigma
-trees.

A

of a tree (or a
\Sigma
-tree)

is a finite or infinite sequence
\pi=v_{0}v_{1}\dots

of nodes totally ordered by the successor relation (i.e., each

is a successor of

for all
i>0
),
but not necessarily starting from the root. If the path starts from the root it is called

.
We denote by
\pi(i)
the
i
-th node

along the path
\pi=v_{0}v_{1}\dots
.
A

is a path that cannot be prolonged to the right;
in particular, because we restrict ourselves to leafless trees, a maximal path is necessarily infinite.
We denote by

the

of
t
rooted at node
u
,
namely, the tree
t
restricted to the set of all descendants of
u
, including

Of course, if

is a

is inherited from

for all

.

First-order logic.

We introduce
monadic first-order logic on trees with the ancestor relation
(
FO
for short).
Let us fix a finite set
\mathit{AP}
of atomic propositions and a countable set
\mathit{Var}
of variables.
Formulae of
FO
are generated by the following grammar,
where
p
ranges over
\mathit{AP}
and
x,x^{\prime}
range over
\mathit{Var}
:

\varphi~::=~(x=x^{\prime})~\bigm|~(x\leq x^{\prime})~\bigm|~p(x)~\bigm|~\neg\varphi~\bigm|~\varphi\lor\varphi~\bigm|~\exists x.~\varphi

A variable is
free
in a formula if it does not occur under the scope of any quantifier.
A
sentence
is a formula without free variables.

FO
formulae are interpreted over tree structures of the form

\mathcal{T}=(t,\zeta)
, where
t
is a
\Sigma
-tree, with
\Sigma=2^{\mathit{AP}}
,
and
\zeta:\mathit{Var}\to\mathop{Dom}(t)
associates a node of
t
with each variable.
This results in a satisfaction relation
\models
between

that is defined inductively as follows:

•

\mathcal{T}\models(x=x^{\prime})
iff
\zeta
maps
x
and
x^{\prime}
to the same node,

•

\mathcal{T}\models(x\leq x^{\prime})
iff
\zeta(x)
is an ancestor of
\zeta(x^{\prime})
,

•

\mathcal{T}\models p(x)
iff
p\in t(\zeta(x))
,

•

\mathcal{T}\models\neg\varphi
iff
\mathcal{T}\not\models\varphi
,

•

\mathcal{T}\models\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}
iff
\mathcal{T}\models\varphi_{1}
or
\mathcal{T}\models\varphi_{2}
,

•

\mathcal{T}\models\exists x.~\varphi
iff there is a node
v\in\mathop{Dom}(t)
such that

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\models\varphi
, where
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=(t,\zeta^{\prime})
and
\zeta^{\prime}
is defined
by
\zeta^{\prime}(x)=v
and
\zeta^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=\zeta(x^{\prime})
for all
x^{\prime}\in\mathit{Var}\setminus\{x\}
.

Note that sentences can be interpreted directly over trees, as the mapping
\zeta
for the
variables is immaterial.

Word automata.

A
nondeterministic Büchi word automaton
(

) is a tuple

\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\Delta,q_{I},F)
,
where
Q
is a finite set of states,

\Sigma
is a finite alphabet,

\Delta\subseteq Q\times\Sigma\times Q

is a transition relation,

q_{I}\in Q
is an initial state, and

F\subseteq Q
is a set of
final states
.
An NBA is
deterministic
if
\Delta
can be seen
as a partial function from
Q\times\Sigma
to
Q
.

A
path
of
\mathcal{A}
on a finite or infinite word
w=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\dots

is a sequence of states
\rho=q_{0}q_{1}\dots
of length
|w|+1
(if
w
is infinite, then the path is also infinite)
such that
(q_{i-1},\sigma_{i},q_{i})\in\Delta
for all positions
i
in
w
.
Such a path
\rho
is called a

of
\mathcal{A}
on
w
if it starts with the initial state
q_{I}
of the automaton.
We denote by
\inf(\rho)
the set of states visited infinitely often in an infinite run
\rho
,
and say that
\rho
is
successful
if there is a
q\in F
such that
q\in\inf(\rho)
.
An infinite word
w
is
accepted
by
\mathcal{A}
if there is a successful run
of
\mathcal{A}
on
w
.
The language
\Lang(\mathcal{A})

recognised
by
\mathcal{A}
is
the set of words accepted by
\mathcal{A}
.
Two automata are
equivalent
if they recognise the same language.

Variants of NBAs are obtained by modifying the acceptance condition.
One way is to simply turn existential nondeterminism into universal nondeterminism,
that is, to declare that the automaton accepts an infinite word

whenever
every
run
on

is successful; we call these models

(e.g., we have

Another way is to replace a Büchi acceptance condition with a coBüchi one:
formally, a run
\rho
of a

(

for short) is successful if there is no

such that

; accordingly, the states of

are called

states
.

An automaton
\mathcal{A}
is

if for all states
q

all finite words

if
\mathcal{A}
admits a path on

that starts and ends at

that starts and ends at

.

Proposition 3
.

[

3.
Temporal logics equivalent to
FO

In this section, we introduce and discuss two tree temporal logics that are
known to be equivalent to
FO
over infinite trees.
Although the equivalence results are not new, we here include a rather detailed
section dedicated to these two logics, with the aim of recasting them in a
syntax closer to the community standard and of presenting brand new results
about them (including a new normal form) to shed light on their behaviour.
Doing this has already helped us better understand the behaviour of
FO
over
infinite trees and it might also be useful to others working on the same
topic.

3.1.
Polarised
PastCTL

The first formalism we present is called
Polarised
PastCTL

, denoted

PastCTL
±
. The logic was already introduced in
[
54
]
,
under a different notation, namely
\{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{X}_{k}\}

over infinite trees under initial equivalence
[
54

.
Here we present it in a revised syntax, more similar to the other logics we will work with.

{defi}

[
PastCTL
±
syntax]
Formulae of
PastCTL
±
are generated by the following grammar, where
p
ranges over a set
\mathit{AP}
of propositional letters:

\displaystyle\varphi~::=

\displaystyle~p~\bigm|~\neg\varphi~\bigm|~\varphi\lor\varphi~\bigm|~{\mathsf{D}^{n}\varphi}~\bigm|~\mathsf{E}\mathsf{X}\varphi~\bigm|~\mathsf{E}\varphi\mathsf{U}\varphi~\bigm|~\mathsf{E}\mathsf{Y}\varphi~\bigm|~\mathsf{E}\varphi\mathsf{S}\varphi.

We make a few comments about the adopted syntax, compared to the original one
from
[
54
]
.
In his work, Schlingloff uses a strict version of the until operator, which he denoted
\mathcal{U}
, with an implicit
path quantification (see, e.g.,
[
54
, Example 2.7]
). Every formula

.
Conversely,
PastCTL

\mathsf{E}\mathsf{X}\psi
and
\mathsf{E}\psi_{1}\mathsf{U}\psi_{2}

using Schlingloff’s until operator

\bot\mathcal{U}\psi
and
\psi_{2}\lor(\psi_{1}\land\psi_{1}\mathcal{U}\psi_{2})
.
A similar (back and forth) translation exists between operators
\mathsf{E}\mathsf{Y}
(resp.,

\mathsf{E}\mathsf{U}
) and
\mathcal{S}
.
As far as the counting modality is concerned, Schlingloff’s operator

is simply renamed to

[

Finally, to maintain uniformity with both the syntax and semantics of

we require the past temporal operators

and
\mathsf{S}
to be preceded by a path
quantifier as well.
At an intuitive level, this may appear redundant, since nodes of a tree admit
only one past.
Formally, however, it is necessary under our convention of always considering
initial paths.
Overall, this is how we can move between Schlingloff’s original formalism and
the syntax of
PastCTL
.

However, there is an important difference between
PastCTL
and its polarised
fragment

±
, which concerns the
possibility of obtaining formulae that are initially equivalent to
\mathsf{E}(\psi_{1}\mathsf{R}\psi_{2})
and
\mathsf{A}(\psi_{1}\mathsf{U}\psi_{2})

For example, these formulae cannot be directly generated by the grammar of
PastCTL

, since the grammar enforces
a “polarisation” condition that requires the operator
\mathsf{E}
(resp.,
\mathsf{A}
) to
always appear in front of
\mathsf{X}
or
\mathsf{U}
(resp.,
\mathsf{X}
or
\mathsf{R}
).
It is also easy to see that the properties defined by

and

\mathsf{A}(\psi_{1}\mathsf{U}\psi_{2})

CTL
±

in which past temporal operators are prohibited.
Yet, in principle, this does not exclude the possibility that those properties
are definable in
PastCTL
±
, for example using past operators.
However, Item (2) of the theorem below excludes this possibility.
In its turn, the presence of past operators raises additional questions that are
concerned with the expressive power of these formalisms.
We have already seen that adding past operators to a logical formalism does not
always increase its expressive power: this is the case for
CTL
∗
, for instance,
whose past operators can be removed while preserving initial equivalence

).
Here, we will see an opposite situation:

±

Theorem 4
.

Clearly, we have
\textnormal{{CTL${}_{\pm}$}}\subseteq\textnormal{{PastCTL${}_{\pm}$}}\subseteq\textnormal{{PastCTL}}

(1)

(3)

Proof 3.1

All results can be derived from the literature.

The second logic that we discuss here,

In

Below, we report only those clauses in the semantic definition of

[

We notice an important consequence of the finite-path semantics:

For example,

While the finite-path semantics has no particular consequence for the operator

\psi_{2}\land(\psi_{1}\lor\tilde{\mathsf{X}}(\psi_{1}\mathsf{R}\psi_{2}))

Lemma 5
.

The following equivalence between

Proof 3.2
.

The proof of the equivalence for path formulae relies on simple inductive
arguments and the use of the unfolded semantics of the operators
\mathsf{U}
and
\mathsf{R}
.
We start by proving the left-to-right direction of this equivalence.
Suppose
(t,\pi,i)\models\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi
, for some path-pointed tree

(t,\pi,i)

\pi

By the unfolding of
\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi

(t,\pi,i)\models\psi\land(\varphi\lor\tilde{\mathsf{X}}(\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi))

We exploit an induction on
|\pi|-i

(t,\pi,i)\models\alpha_{\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi}
.
The base case is where
i
is the last position of
\pi
, which implies
that
(t,\pi,i)\models\tilde{\mathsf{X}}\bot
holds vacuously.
Together with
(t,\pi,i)\models\psi
, this immediately implies
(t,\pi,i)\models\alpha_{\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi}
.
For the inductive step, we assume that
i
is not the last position of
\pi
,
and recall that both formulae
\psi

\varphi\lor\tilde{\mathsf{X}}(\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi)
hold at position

We distinguish two subcases, depending on whether
(t,\pi,i)\models\varphi
or

(t,\pi,i+1)\models\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi

(t,\pi,i)\models\psi
, we easily get
(t,\pi,i)\models\alpha_{\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi}
.
In the latter subcase, we exploit the inductive hypothesis to get
(t,\pi,i+1)\models\alpha_{\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi}
, which paired with
(t,\pi,i)\models\psi
gives

(t,\pi,i)\models\alpha_{\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi}
.

For the other direction, suppose
(t,\pi,i)\models\alpha_{\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi}
.
By the unfolding of
\alpha_{\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi}

(t,\pi,i)\models((\tilde{\mathsf{X}}\bot\lor\varphi)\land\psi)\lor(\psi\land\mathsf{X}\alpha_{\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi})
.
Accordingly, we distinguish two cases.
If
(t,\pi,i)\models(\tilde{\mathsf{X}}\bot\lor\varphi)\land\psi

(t,\pi,i)\models\varphi\land\psi

(t,\pi,i)\models\tilde{\mathsf{X}}\bot\land\psi

\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi

For the other case, where
(t,\pi,i)\models\psi\land\mathsf{X}\alpha_{\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi}

\pi

We can then exploit the inductive hypothesis on

Because
(t,\pi,i)\models\psi

(t,\pi,i)\models\varphi\mathsf{R}\psi

3.3.
Connections with Weak Chain Logic

In the previous sections we have seen that

More precisely, the normal form

binds existential path quantification with co-safety properties,
and, dually, universal path quantification with safety properties.
We will now see that, from a broad perspective, this polarisation phenomenon is
not unique, and is shared by other formalisms more expressive than

\mu

Interestingly, this polarisation between

We shall explain this in more detail, after the preliminary definitions for the

Monadic Second Order Logic

(

(

Finally, a couple of even more restricted fragments are

To put these logics in context, it is easy to see that

since every finite path can be represented by its first and last nodes,
and the ancestor relation is available in

Similarly, it was shown in
[

is equivalent to

CTL
∗

The relative expressive power of these common fragments of

summarised as follows:

Proposition 9

(
[

•

•

•

•

It turns out that

To explain this polarisation property, we quickly recall some expressiveness
results from the literature and present them
in the simpler setting of infinite, binary trees.
Note that on binary trees counting modalities are useless, and so they are not

The first important reference is
[

On binary trees, this is the same as saying that

Moreover, an earlier work

characterized

\mu_{ca}

Its syntax is reported below (for succinctness, we omit the standard semantics).

\displaystyle\varphi

4.
Graded Tree Automata

In this section we recall the definitions for a general model of automaton
running on infinite, unranked trees, called Graded Tree Automaton, and we
specialise this notion into different classes of automata that we are going to
work with later.
The model is based on classical alternating Büchi tree automata
[
60
, Section
2.2]
, enhanced with graded modalities as done in, e.g.,

[
35
, Section 3]
.
For simplicity, we will start from a one-way automaton model, where transitions
propagate states from node to children, and then we will progressively restrict
or enhance the model so as to match the expressive power of the different
fragments of
PastCTL
∗
.
Since every tree automaton introduced here is alternating, Büchi, and graded,
we will not repeat these properties every time or include them in the names of
the modified models.
In particular, we will drop the term ‘graded’ early on, instead introducing
qualifications for more important semantic conditions.

4.1.
Graded Tree Automata

Let
X
be a set.
We denote by
\mathcal{B}^{+}(X)
the set of
positive Boolean formulae
over

X

formulae
\top
(true),
\bot
(false), and

.
A
Graded Tree Automaton
(

) is a tuple
\mathcal{A}=\langle Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{I},F\rangle
, where
Q
is a finite set of states,
\Sigma
is
a finite alphabet,
\delta:Q\times\Sigma\rightarrow\mathcal{B}^{+}(\{\Diamond_{k},\Box_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\times Q)
is the
transition function,
q_{I}
is the initial state, and

is the set
of accepting states.
The function

describes a one-way (or downward) transition that, based
on the current state

of the automaton and the symbol

read at the current
node of the input tree, sends a set of target states to the possible children of
the current node, so as to satisfy the positive Boolean formula
\delta(q,a)
,
where each atom
(\Diamond_{k},q^{\prime})
(resp.,
(\Box_{k},q^{\prime})
) of the formula is
considered as satisfied if state

is sent to at least

q^{\prime}
is sent to all but

In particular, the atom
(\Diamond_{1},q^{\prime})
(often abbreviated

)
requires

to be sent to at least one child, and can be used, for instance,
to check a formula like

(\Box_{1},q^{\prime})
(often
abbreviated
(\Box,q^{\prime})

to be sent to all children, and can be
used to check a formula like
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{X}\phi
.
Of course, the atoms
(\Diamond_{k},q^{\prime})
and
(\Box_{k},q^{\prime})
for arbitrary

Given a

To ease some translations later, we also introduce a variant of the previous
notation: given a positive Boolean formula

Let

To define a run of

Note that such a tree labels every node

Intuitively, the labeling

Let

\mathcal{A}

We formally define the satisfaction relation between a node

We are now ready to define the notion of run of

As a matter of fact, because later we will enhance the model with two-way transitions,

Let

(2)

Concerning the above condition (2), we observe that the definition of the satisfaction

This turns out to be useful in order to avoid dealing with runs with leaves,
and thus respecting our adopted definition of tree: indeed, even in the presence

In addition, we highlight an equivalent characterisation of runs, which
will come handy later.

Remark 4.1
.

A tree

(1)

A run on

4.2.
Hesitant Tree Automata

The class of
Hesitant Tree Automata
was introduced in
[
36
, Section
5.1]
and here we adapt it for our purposes.
A
Hesitant Tree Automaton
(
HTA
) is defined as a suitable restriction of
a
GTA

\mathcal{A}=\langle Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{I},F\rangle
.
First, it must admit a partition of the state set
Q
into disjoint non-empty
sets
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n}
, that we call
components
, and a total order

\leq
on these components such that, for every
q\in Q_{i}
and
q^{\prime}\in Q_{j}
, if

q^{\prime}
appears in some atom of
\delta(q,\sigma)
for some
\sigma
, then
j\leq i
.
Intuitively, transitions can only stay in a component or move to a lower
component. For brevity, we call
Q_{i}
-atom
any pair of the form

(\Diamond_{k},q)
or
(\Box_{k},q)
whose state
q
belongs to
Q_{i}
.
In addition, each component
Q_{i}
must be of precisely one of the following
three types:

•

transient
, when there is no
Q_{i}
-atom in
\delta(q,\sigma)
, for any

q\in Q_{i}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma
,

•

existential
, when, for all
q\in Q_{i}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma
, all

Q_{i}
-atoms in
\delta(q,\sigma)
are of the form
(\Diamond,q^{\prime})
, and there
is at most one
Q_{i}
-atom in each clause of the disjunctive normal form of

\delta(q,\sigma)
;

•

universal
, when, for all
q\in Q_{i}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma
, all

Q_{i}
-atoms in
\delta(q,\sigma)
are of the form
(\Box,q^{\prime})
, and there
is at most one
Q_{i}
-atom in each clause of the conjunctive normal form of

\delta(q,\sigma)
.

Note that in the above definition, all counting modalities
\Diamond_{k}

(resp.
\Box_{k}
) associated with atoms that stay in the same existential
(resp. universal) component have cardinality
k=1
.
This intuitively means that a
HTA
can traverse at most one node-to-child
relationship while remaining in the same existential component. Moreover, by the
above restrictions, every infinite path of a run of a
HTA
eventually gets stuck
in an existential or universal component.
All together, these properties let us capture the semantics of existential and
universal path quantification of tree temporal logics by allowing the
HTA
to
guess and check a single path in the input tree while remaining in the same
component.

4.3.
Polarised Hesitant Tree Automata

A
Polarised Hesitant Tree Automaton
(
HTA
±
) is an

where every state

q
of an existential component is such that
q\notin F
and, dually, every
state
q
of a universal component is such that
q\in F
(for states of
transient components, it does not matter whether they belong or not to
F
, as
they can occur at most once along an infinite path of a run).
The

condition, originally introduced in the conference
version of this work
[
5
]
, is a stronger restriction
than the classical
weak
condition
[
46
]
.
The latter only requires each component of an automaton to contain exclusively
accepting states or exclusively rejecting states, without imposing any
constraint on the type of component or on the structural properties of its
transitions.
As the name suggests, the polarised restriction simulates the polarisation
phenomenon observed in
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
.

5.
Automaton-based characterisation of

The restriction is simple: we require components of the hesitant partition of a

The head movement enhancement is instead less trivial. We allow for

•

Proof 5.1
.

Working with a state-set partitioned into singletons, we follow the approach of

[
39
, Section 7]
and
[
11
, Section
3]
, who dealt with a similar problem.

Since
\mathcal{A}
is linear, some of the restrictions imposed on the
hesitant types become redundant.
In particular, if
\{q\}
is an existential component, then
\delta(q,\sigma,\rho)
contains
q
only in atoms of the form
(\Diamond,q)
, for
all
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
\rho\in\{\mathit{root},\mathit{non\text{-}root}\}
;
therefore, the requirement that
q
occurs in at most one atom in each clause
of the disjunctive normal form of
\delta(q,\sigma,\rho)
becomes
redundant, since
(\Diamond,q_{i})\land(\Diamond,q_{i})
is equivalent to

(\Diamond,q_{i})
.
Similarly, if
\{q\}
is a universal component, then
q
only occurs in
atoms of the form
(\Box,q)
; therefore, it is of no use to impose the
requirement that
q
occurs in at most one atom in each clause of the
conjunctive normal form of
\delta(q,\sigma,\rho)
.
Consequently, we can assume, without loss of generality, that

\delta(q,\sigma,\rho)
contains
at most one occurrence
of an atom
in which
q
appears, because of the following:

•

if
q
is transient, then
q
never appears in
\delta(q,\sigma,\rho)
by construction;

•

if
q
is existential, then the disjunctive normal form of

\delta(q,\sigma,\rho)
is (equivalent to) a formula of the form

((\Diamond,q)\land\alpha_{1})\lor...\lor((\Diamond,q)\land\alpha_{k})\lor\alpha_{k+1}...\lor\alpha_{m}
, where, for each
i\in[1,m]
,

\alpha_{i}
is a conjunction of atoms in which
q
does not appear.
This formula can be rewritten as
((\Diamond,q)\land(\alpha_{1}\lor...\lor\alpha_{k}))\lor(\alpha_{k+1}\lor...\lor\alpha_{m})
;

•

if
q
is universal, then the disjunctive normal form of
\delta(q,\sigma,\rho)
is (equivalent to) a formula of the form
((\Box,q)\land\alpha_{1})\lor...\lor((\Box,q)\land\alpha_{k})\lor\alpha_{k+1}...\lor\alpha_{m}
, where, for each
i\in[1,m]
,
\alpha_{i}
is a
conjunction of atoms in which
q
does not appear.
This formula can be rewritten as (
(\Box,q)\land(\alpha_{1}\lor...\lor\alpha_{k}))\lor(\alpha_{k+1}\lor...\lor\alpha_{m})
;

•

if
q
is upward, then one can apply to the disjunctive normal form of

\delta(q,\sigma,\rho)
the same rewriting described for the
existential case, and obtain a formula in the form
((\Uparrow,q)\land(\alpha_{1}\lor...\lor\alpha_{k}))\lor(\alpha_{k+1}\lor...\lor\alpha_{m})
, where, for each
i\in[1,m]
,
\alpha_{i}
is a conjunction of
atoms in which
q
does not appear.

Thus,
\delta(q,\sigma,\rho)
is in a very precise form:

\delta(q,\sigma,\rho)=\begin{cases}\alpha^{\prime}_{q,\sigma,\rho}&\text{if }\{q\}\text{ is transient}\\
((\Diamond,q)\land\alpha_{q,\sigma,\rho})\lor\alpha^{\prime}_{q,\sigma,\rho}&\text{if }\{q\}\text{ is existential}\\
((\Box,q)\land\alpha_{q,\sigma,\rho})\lor\alpha^{\prime}_{q,\sigma,\rho}&\text{if }\{q\}\text{ is universal}\\
((\Uparrow,q)\land\alpha_{q,\sigma,\rho})\lor\alpha^{\prime}_{q,\sigma,\rho}&\text{if }\{q\}\text{ is upward}\end{cases}

where
\alpha_{q,\sigma,\rho}
and
\alpha^{\prime}_{q,\sigma,\rho}
are
formulae in disjunctive normal form not containing atoms in which
q
occurs:
they only contain states belonging to lower partitions.
It is important to observe that if a
(q,s)
-labeled node of a run satisfies

\alpha^{\prime}_{q,\sigma,\rho}
, where
\sigma
is the label of node
s
in the
input tree
t
and
\rho=\mathit{root}
(resp.,
\rho=\mathit{non\text{-}root}
) if
s

is (resp., is not) the root of
t
, then the automaton can leave (and never go
back to) the component
\{q\}
when going down along the run-tree; conversely,
the sole satisfaction of
\alpha_{q,\sigma,\rho}
(i.e., the node satisfies

\alpha_{q,\sigma,\rho}
but not
\alpha^{\prime}_{q,\sigma,\rho}
) implies
that the automaton must remain in the component.

Definition of the translation function
f
.

Towards the definition of the translation function
f
from automata to
formulae of
PastCTL
±
, we define the following abbreviation for every
\sigma\in\Sigma

\psi_{\sigma}=\bigwedge_{p\in\sigma}p\land\bigwedge_{p\notin\sigma}\neg p.

Clearly,
\psi_{\sigma}
is true on a node
s
of an input tree
t
if and only
if
\sigma
is the label of
s
.

Our translation function
f
associates a formula of
PastCTL
±
to every state
q\in Q
of an automaton
\mathcal{A}
and every formula
\theta\in\mathcal{B}^{+}(\{\Diamond_{k},\Box_{k},\Uparrow\}\times Q)
.
Formally,
f
is defined as:

•

for all
\theta\in\mathcal{B}^{+}(\{\Diamond_{k},\Box_{k},\Uparrow\}\times Q)
,

f(\theta)=\begin{cases}\top&\text{if }\theta=\top\\
\bot&\text{if }\theta=\bot\\
f(\theta_{1})\land f(\theta_{2})&\text{if }\theta=\theta_{1}\land\theta_{2}\\
f(\theta_{1})\lor f(\theta_{2})&\text{if }\theta=\theta_{1}\lor\theta_{2}\\
\mathsf{D}^{k}f(q)&\text{if }\theta=(\Diamond_{k},q)\\
\neg\mathsf{D}^{k}\neg f(q)&\text{if }\theta=(\Box_{k},q)\\
\mathsf{E}\mathsf{Y}f(q)&\text{if }\theta=(\Uparrow,q)\end{cases}

•

for all
q\in Q
,

f(q)=\begin{cases}\beta^{\prime}_{q}&\text{if }\{q\}\text{ is transient}\\
\mathsf{E}(\beta_{q}\mathsf{U}\beta^{\prime}_{q})&\text{if }\{q\}\text{ is existential}\\
\mathsf{A}(\beta_{q}\mathsf{W}\beta^{\prime}_{q})&\text{if }\{q\}\text{ is universal}\\
\mathsf{E}(\beta_{q}\mathsf{S}\beta^{\prime}_{q})&\text{if }\{q\}\text{ is upward}\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle\beta_{q}=\quad

\displaystyle\mathsf{Y}\top\rightarrow\bigwedge\nolimits_{\sigma\in\Sigma}(\psi_{\sigma}\rightarrow f(\alpha_{q,\sigma,\mathit{non\text{-}root}}))

\displaystyle\wedge\neg

\displaystyle\mathsf{Y}\top\rightarrow\bigwedge\nolimits_{\sigma\in\Sigma}(\psi_{\sigma}\rightarrow f(\alpha_{q,\sigma,\mathit{root}}))

and

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}_{q}=\quad

\displaystyle\mathsf{Y}\top\rightarrow\bigwedge\nolimits_{\sigma\in\Sigma}(\psi_{\sigma}\rightarrow f(\alpha^{\prime}_{q,\sigma,\mathit{non\text{-}root}}))

\displaystyle\wedge\neg

\displaystyle\mathsf{Y}\top\rightarrow\bigwedge\nolimits_{\sigma\in\Sigma}(\psi_{\sigma}\rightarrow f(\alpha^{\prime}_{q,\sigma,\mathit{root}}))

Note that, for all
\Sigma
-tree
t
and nodes
s\in\mathop{Dom}(t)
, we have that

(t,s)\models\beta_{q}
(resp.,
(t,s)\models\beta^{\prime}_{q}
) if and only if

(t,s)\models f(\alpha_{q,\sigma,\rho})
(resp.,
(t,s)\models f(\alpha^{\prime}_{q,\sigma,\rho})
), where
\sigma
is the label of
s
, and

\rho=\mathit{root}
if
s
is the root of
t
, while
\rho=\mathit{non\text{-}root}

if
s
is not the root of
t
.
Additionally, observe that formula
\mathsf{A}(\beta_{q}\mathsf{W}\beta^{\prime}_{q})
can be
rewritten as
\neg\mathsf{E}(\neg\beta^{\prime}_{q}\mathsf{U}(\neg\beta_{q}\land\neg\beta^{\prime}_{q}))
, to match the syntactic definition of
PastCTL
±
given in
Definition
3.1
.
Finally, the translation of automaton
\mathcal{A}
is the formula

\varphi_{\mathcal{A}}=f(q_{I})
.

Before showing soundness of our translation, we show that the above definition
is based on a well-founded induction.
Let
\mathit{rank}(q)=i
for all
q\in Q_{i}
and
\mathit{rank}(\theta)
be the highest rank

\mathit{rank}(q)
associated with states

occurring in some atom of formula
\theta
,
i.e.,
\mathit{rank}(\theta)=\max{\{\mathit{rank}(q)\mid q\text{ occurs in some atom of }\theta\}}
.
Consider the strict partial order
\prec
(based on function
\mathit{rank}
) over
the domain of
f
, namely
Q\cup\mathcal{B}^{+}(\{\Diamond_{k},\Box_{k},\Uparrow\}\times Q)
, defined as follows:

•

for all

,
q\prec q^{\prime}
if and only if
\mathit{rank}(q)<\mathit{rank}(q^{\prime})
;

•

for all

•

In particular, observe that

,

6.
Automaton-based characterisations of
SafeLTL
and
coSafeLTL

This section is preliminary to the results presented in Section
7
, which give another automaton-based characterisation for
FO
.
Here we present two specific classes of
\omega
-word automata that characterise
respectively
SafeLTL
and
coSafeLTL
.
These latter results in the setting of word languages are, in our opinion,
interesting on their own.

Here we will mostly consider nondeterministic Büchi / coBüchi automata
running on
\omega
-words, which we present as tuples of the form
\mathcal{A}=\langle Q,\Sigma,\Delta,q_{I},F\rangle
, where
\Delta\subseteq Q\times\Sigma\times Q
is the transition relation (we use the capital
\Delta

to distinguish it from a transition function),
q_{I}
is an initial state, and

F
is a set of final states.
We recall from the preliminaries that such an automaton is
counter-free

if, for every state
q\in Q
, every finite word
u\in\Sigma^{*}
, and every
n>0
, if
(q,u^{n},q)\in\Delta^{*}
, then
(q,u,q)\in\Delta^{*}
, where
\Delta^{*}

is the transitive closure of
\Delta
, i.e., the standard extension of the
transition relation to finite words.
We introduce an additional property below.

{defi}

A nondeterministic automaton
\mathcal{A}=\langle Q,\Sigma,\Delta,q_{I},F\rangle
is
looping
if
F=Q\setminus\{q_{\mathrm{sink}}\}
and

q_{\mathrm{sink}}\in Q
is such that
(q_{\mathrm{sink}},\sigma,q_{\mathrm{sink}})\in\Delta
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma
.

Note that the above definitions make no explicit reference to the acceptance
condition of the automaton (be it Büchi or coBüchi) or to the semantics of
nondeterminism (whether it should be interpreted as an existential or a
universal choice): these definitions make sense in every combination of such
cases.
Below, we shall use the shorthands
UBA
and
NCA
respectively for a universal
Büchi automaton (accepting an
\omega
-word if all runs on it visit infinitely
often a final state) and an existential coBüchi automaton (accepting an

\omega
-word if at least one run on it visits only finitely often the final
states).

We now recall the following automaton-based characterisation of the
LTL
fragments
SafeLTL
and
coSafeLTL
.

Proposition 16

(
[
12
]
)
.

Counter-free looping
deterministic
Büchi (resp., coBüchi) automata
define the same class of languages of
\omega
-words as
SafeLTL
(resp.,

coSafeLTL
).

Our alternative characterisation for
SafeLTL
and
coSafeLTL
essentially shows
that one can drop the assumption of determinism, provided that the
interpretation of nondeterministic choices is universal for automata with
Büchi acceptance conditions and existential for automata with coBüchi
acceptance conditions.
Note that the latter requirement is as expected, since the two automaton models
for
SafeLTL
and
coSafeLTL
need to be duals of each other, meaning that
complementing an automaton in one class gives an automaton in the other class.
This alternative characterisation will turn out useful in the next section.

Lemma 17
.

Counter-free looping
UBA
and
NCA
define the same class of languages of

\omega
-words as
SafeLTL
and
coSafeLTL
, respectively.

Proof 6.1
.

In view of the duality between
UBA
and
NCA
, it suffices to prove only the
characterisation of
SafeLTL
in terms of
UBA
.
As a matter of fact, since deterministic Büchi automata can be seen as a
special case of
UBA
, it suffices to prove that counter-free looping

can
only define
SafeLTL
properties.

Consider a counter-free looping

\mathcal{A}=\langle Q,\Sigma,\Delta,q_{I},Q\rangle
.
We show that, by applying a variant of the subset construction, it is possible
to turn
\mathcal{A}
into an equivalent counter-free looping deterministic Büchi
automaton, which by Proposition
16
defines a
SafeLTL
property.
More precisely, we use the Miyano-Hayashi breakpoint construction, which was
introduced in
[
43
, Theorem 4.1]
to obtain a

starting from an alternating Büchi automaton.
We briefly recall this construction. While originally introduced to convert alternating B”uchi automata into
NBA
, applying this construction specifically to a
UBA
yields a
deterministic
B”uchi automaton. We briefly review this procedure. Given
\mathcal{A}
, the states of the resulting deterministic automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
are pairs
(S,O)
satisfying

, with the following intended semantics:

•

S
represents the classical subset construction, and contains the states
reached along any run induced by the consumed prefix of the input,

•

O
represents obligations for visiting
F
infinitely often, namely, it
contains the states reached along those runs that have not yet visited
F

since the last breakpoint (a breakpoint happens whenever
O
becomes empty).

Formally, the transitions of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
are described by the deterministic
function
\Delta^{\prime}
that maps a pair
(S,O)
and a symbol
\sigma
to the pair

(S^{\prime},O^{\prime})
defined by:

\displaystyle S^{\prime}

\displaystyle~=~\big\{q^{\prime}\in Q\>:\>\exists{q\in S}~~(q,\sigma,q^{\prime})\in\Delta\big\}

\displaystyle O^{\prime}

\displaystyle~=~\begin{cases}\big\{q^{\prime}\in Q\setminus F\>:\>\exists{q\in O}~~(q,\sigma,q^{\prime})\in\Delta\big\}&\text{if $O\neq\emptyset$,}\\
S^{\prime}\setminus F&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The initial state of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is the pair
(\{q_{I}\},\{q_{I}\}\setminus F)

the final states are those pairs
(S,O)
with

enforcing infinitely many breakpoints.
The constructed deterministic automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

(for a formal

[
43

It remains to show that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is counter-free and looping. For this, we
exploit the assumption that the initial automaton
\mathcal{A}
is counter-free
and looping, and we focus our attention on the second component
O
of the

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

Since all states in
\mathcal{A}
but

are final, the second component
can only be empty or the singleton
\{q_{\mathrm{sink}}\}
.
Moreover, whenever it becomes the singleton
\{q_{\mathrm{sink}}\}

since
(q_{\mathrm{sink}},\sigma,q_{\mathrm{sink}})\in\Delta

\sigma\in\Sigma
.
This produces potentially many sink states of the form

in

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

into a single macro-state, say
(\bot,\{q_{\mathrm{sink}}\})
, where the first component
is forgotten (we accordingly modify the transition rules for the pairs where the
second component is
\{q_{\mathrm{sink}}\}

The resulting Büchi automaton

is still deterministic and

\mathcal{A}
, but now it is also looping: its sink state is

(\bot,\{q_{\mathrm{sink}}\})
.
As a matter of fact, this shows that, when the initial automaton is looping, the
breakpoint construction can be simplified.
For similar reasons, precisely because the projection of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}
onto
the second component

is already counter-free, in order to prove that

\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}
is counter-free, it suffices to show that the projection onto
the first component
S
is also counter-free.
For this we observe that the latter projection is a classical subset
construction, and we exploit
[
19

, where it is
shown that the subset construction of a counter-free automaton yields again a
counter-free automaton.
We thus conclude that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}
is counter-free and looping, and hence by
Proposition
16
it defines a
SafeLTL
property, and so
does
\mathcal{A}
.

Automaton-based characterisation of

∗

In this section we present an automaton-based characterisation of the logic
CTL
{}^{*}_{f}
;
more precisely, we will provide effective translations between a restricted variant of
(one-way)
HTA
±
and the normal form
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
of

{}^{*}_{f}
. By the equivalence of the latter logic with
FO
,
it follows that our automaton model captures precisely the expressive power of
FO
.

Towards introducing the restrictions on
HTA
±
that match the expressive power of
CTL
{}^{*}_{f}
,
we start by describing a

operation on the components of a given
HTA
±
.
Intuitively, this will let us view each transformed component as an
\omega
-word automaton,
thus enabling the use of classical characterisations of
FO
-definable word languages.
Unfortunately, this first definition is quite technical.

{defi}

Let
\mathcal{A}=\langle Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{I},F\rangle
be a
HTA
±
,

Q_{i}
be a non-transient component of it, and
q\in Q_{i}
.
We define the
\omega
-word automaton
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}=\langle Q_{i}\uplus\{q_{\mathrm{exit}}\},\Sigma\times 2^{B},\Delta,q,Q_{i}\rangle
,
where
q_{\mathrm{exit}}
is a fresh state,
B
is the set of atoms with states outside the component
Q_{i}
,

q
is the new initial state,
Q_{i}
is the set of final states, and
\Delta
is the transition relation defined by a
case distinction as follows:

•

If
Q_{i}
is an existential component,
then
\Delta
contains triples of the form
(q^{\prime},(\sigma,C),q^{\prime\prime})
,
with
\sigma\in\Sigma
and

,
satisfying one of the following conditions:

(1)

q^{\prime}=q^{\prime\prime}=q_{\mathrm{exit}}
,

(2)

q^{\prime}\in Q_{i}
,
q^{\prime\prime}=q_{\mathrm{exit}}
, and the disjunctive normal form of
\delta(q^{\prime},\sigma)
contains a clause of the form
\bigwedge C
,

(3)

q^{\prime},q^{\prime\prime}\in Q_{i}
and the disjunctive normal form of
\delta(q^{\prime},\sigma)
contains a clause of the form
(\Diamond,q^{\prime\prime})\land\bigwedge C
.

In this case we interpret
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
as a nondeterministic coBüchi automaton (
NCA
),
which accepts an
\omega
-word whenever some run on it eventually leaves the component
Q_{i}
.

•

Dually, if
Q_{i}
is a universal component,
then
\Delta
contains triples of the form
(q^{\prime},(\sigma,C),q^{\prime\prime})
,
with
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
C\subseteq B
,
satisfying one of the following conditions:

(1)

q^{\prime}=q^{\prime\prime}=q_{\mathrm{exit}}
,

(2)

q^{\prime}\in Q_{i}
,
q^{\prime\prime}=q_{\mathrm{exit}}
, and the conjunctive normal form of
\delta(q^{\prime},\sigma)
contains a clause of the form
\bigvee C
,

(3)

q^{\prime},q^{\prime\prime}\in Q_{i}
and the conjunctive normal form of
\delta(q^{\prime},\sigma)
contains a clause of the form
(\Box,q^{\prime\prime})\lor\bigvee C

In this case we interpret
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
as a universal Büchi automaton (

which accepts an
\omega
-word whenever all runs on it stay forever inside the component

.

We observe that the automata

The above definition allows to consider every non-transient component as an
\omega
-word automaton,
while carrying the branching information through a suitable annotation of the input (i.e., the sets
C
of atoms with states outside the component).
This interpretation is particularly adequate for formalising restrictions directly on the linearisations of the components.
A first natural restriction is to require the linearised components to be
counter-free
, in order to match the expressive
power of
FO
, at least at the level of the annotated
\omega
-word languages.
However, this does not suffice to capture the expressive power of
FO
on
trees
, essentially because spurious annotations
may be introduced that conceal counting behaviour behind an apparently counter-free semantics.
Towards solving this problem, we enforce an additional restriction on the use of annotations, called
visibility

(the restriction was originally introdued in
[
4
]
and used in
[
5
]
under
the name of
mutual exclusion
).
Hereafter, whenever we work with an automaton
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
and an annotation
C
, we tacitly assume that
C

appears in the transition relation
\Delta
of
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
, namely, there exist
q^{\prime}
in the same component
of
q
,
\sigma\in\Sigma
, and
q^{\prime\prime}
in a different component of
q
such that
(q^{\prime},(\sigma,C),q^{\prime\prime})\in\Delta
.

{defi}

Let
Q_{i}
be a non-transient component of a
HTA
±

\mathcal{A}
.
We say that
Q_{i}
is
visible
if for every pair of distinct annotations
C\neq C^{\prime}
that appear
in some transitions of
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
, for any
q\in Q_{i}
,
there are atoms
\theta\in C
and
\theta^{\prime}\in C^{\prime}
for which the tree languages

\Lang(\mathcal{A}^{\theta})
and of
\Lang(\mathcal{A}^{\theta^{\prime}})
are the complement of one another.
Accordingly, we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
visible
if every one of its non-transient components
is visible.

Note that if
C,C^{\prime}
are distinct annotations of a component of a visible
HTA
±

\mathcal{A}
,
then, in particular, we have that
\Lang(\mathcal{A}^{\bigwedge C})\cap\Lang(\mathcal{A}^{\bigwedge C^{\prime}})
is empty
and, symmetrically,
\Lang(\mathcal{A}^{\bigvee C})\cup\Lang(\mathcal{A}^{\bigvee C^{\prime}})
contains all possible trees over
\Sigma
.
These two properties will turn out useful when the component is existential, resp., universal.

We further discuss the visibility restriction with an example, adapted from
[
4
, Example 5.1]
.

{exa}

Let
a,b
be atomic propositions and
\Sigma=2^{\{a,b\}}
. Consider the language
L
of trees that have a finite access path of even length such that

(1)

the path is uniformly labeled by
\emptyset
,

(2)

along the path every node at an even position has a child outside the path that is labeled by
\{a\}
, and

(3)

the last node of the path has a child labeled by
\{b\}
.

It is easy to see that
FO
cannot define such a language, since capturing the above properties requires counting modulo two,
which
FO
cannot do in the standard signature for unordered trees — the situation would be different if the signature
contained the
i

[

[

Now, let us define the

\mathcal{A}=\langle\{q_{I},q,q_{a},q_{b}\},\Sigma,\delta,q_{I},\emptyset\rangle

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}\delta(q_{I},\sigma)&=&\begin{cases}\big((\Diamond,q)\land(\Diamond,q_{a})\big)\lor(\Diamond,q_{b})&\text{if $\sigma=\emptyset$},\\
\bot&\text{otherwise;}\end{cases}&\delta(q,\sigma)&=&\begin{cases}(\Diamond,q_{I})&\text{if $\sigma=\emptyset$},\\
\bot&\text{otherwise;}\end{cases}\\[17.22217pt]
\delta(q_{a},\sigma)&=&\begin{cases}\top&\text{if $a\in\sigma$},\\
\bot&\text{otherwise;}\end{cases}&\delta(q_{b},\sigma)&=&\begin{cases}\top&\text{if $b\in\sigma$},\\
\bot&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\end{array}

It is easy to see that
\mathcal{A}

.
Moreover, the state set of

is partitioned into three components:
an existential component

and two transient components

and

We can therefore linearise

It is also worth noting that

On the other hand, if we insist in having a

Despite the tedious definitions above, the intuition for this new automaton

For example, the previous transition function

As a matter of fact, the process of making those atomic commitments visible in a

Coming back to our example, we claim that

There are now five components: one existential component

The linearisation of

In the following, we will prove that if a

. We anticipate here that the property of being visible is also connected to the

Theorem 18
.

Given a counter-free, visible
HTA
±

\mathcal{A}=\langle Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{I},F\rangle
over
\Sigma=2^{\mathrm{AP}}
, there is a
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
formula
\varphi_{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\Lang(\mathcal{A})=\Lang(\varphi_{\mathcal{A}})
.

Proof 7.1
.

Fix a counter-free, visible
HTA
±

\mathcal{A}=\langle Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{I},F\rangle
.
Similarly to the proof of Theorem
10
, for each
\theta\in\mathcal{B}^{+}(\{\Diamond_{k},\Box_{k}\}\times Q)

(resp.,
q\in Q
), we aim at constructing a corresponding
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
formula
\varphi^{\theta}
(resp.,
\varphi^{q}
)
such that
\Lang(\mathcal{A}^{\theta})=\Lang(\varphi^{\theta})
(resp.,
\Lang(\mathcal{A}^{q})=\Lang(\varphi^{q})
).
The desired formula that defines the original language
\Lang(\mathcal{A})
would then be
\varphi_{\mathcal{A}}=\varphi^{q_{I}}
.
We will define the formulae
\varphi^{\theta}
and
\varphi^{q}
by a mutual induction. The case for
\theta
is straightforward:

•

\varphi^{\top}=\top
,

•

\varphi^{\bot}=\bot
,

•

\varphi^{\theta_{1}\land\theta_{2}}=\varphi^{\theta_{1}}\land\varphi^{\theta_{2}}
,

•

\varphi^{\theta_{1}\lor\theta_{2}}=\varphi^{\theta_{1}}\lor\varphi^{\theta_{2}}
,

•

\varphi^{(\Diamond_{k},q)}=\mathsf{D}^{k}\varphi^{q}
,

•

\varphi^{(\Box_{k},q)}=\neg\mathsf{D}^{k}\neg\varphi^{q}
.

In particular, as degenerate cases of the last two items, we get
\varphi^{(\Diamond,q)}=\mathsf{E}\mathsf{X}\varphi^{q}
and
\varphi^{(\Box,q)}=\mathsf{A}\mathsf{X}\varphi^{q}
.

The rest of the proof is devoted to constructing the formulae
\varphi^{q}
.
For this, we shall exploit a natural induction based on the order of the components of
\mathcal{A}
, starting from
the smallest components (recall that the transitions of
\mathcal{A}
can only move from one component to the same
component or to a smaller one).
We distinguish some cases based on whether the considered state
q
is in a transient, existential, or universal component:

•

If
q
belongs to a transient component
Q_{i}
, then by construction, for every
\sigma\in\Sigma
,

\delta(q,\sigma)=\theta_{q,\sigma}
is a positive Boolean combination of atoms that contain
only states from components of order lower than
Q_{i}
. By the inductive hypothesis, we
know how to construct corresponding formulae
\varphi^{\theta_{q,\sigma}}
using the previous definitions,
and we can then define

\varphi^{q}~=~\bigvee\nolimits_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\Big(\bigwedge\nolimits_{a\in\sigma}a~\land~\bigwedge\nolimits_{a\in\mathrm{AP}\setminus\sigma}\neg a~\land~\varphi^{\theta_{q,\sigma}}\Big).

•

If
q
belongs to an existential component
Q_{i}
, then we consider the linearisation
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
of
Q_{i}

as defined in Definition
7
.
By construction, this is an
NCA

\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}=\langle Q_{i}\uplus\{q_{exit}\},\Sigma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime},q,Q_{i}\rangle

over an alphabet
\Sigma^{\prime}=\Sigma\times 2^{B}
that is obtained by annotating elements of
\Sigma
with sets that contain
only atoms with states from components strictly smaller than
Q_{i}
.

We remark a few important properties that will be used later:

(1)

\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
is both counter-free and looping.

(2)

For every annotation
C
that appears in a transition of
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
,

C
contains only atoms with states from components of order lower than
Q_{i}
;
we can thus activate the inductive hypothesis and obtain a formula

\varphi^{\bigwedge C}
that defines the language
\Lang(\mathcal{A}^{\bigwedge C})
.

(3)

Because
Q_{i}
is visible, for every pair of distinct annotations
C,C^{\prime}

used by
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
, we have
\Lang(\varphi^{\bigwedge C})\cap\Lang(\varphi^{\bigwedge C^{\prime}})=\emptyset
.

We begin by discussing Property P1. In view of this property, it is tempting to
claim a use of Lemma
16
for translating
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}

into an equivalent
coSafeLTL
formula. However, this is not immediately possible because the
alphabet of
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
contains
pairs
of the
form
(\sigma,C)
, with
\sigma\subseteq\mathrm{AP}
and
C\subseteq B
. It is however straightforwad
to encode
every such pair
(\sigma,C)
by the disjoint union
\sigma\uplus C
, and accordingly see the
alphabet
\Sigma^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
as a powerset of a new set
\mathrm{AP}_{\mathrm{exit}}=\mathrm{AP}\uplus B

of atomic propositions. To highlight this, we call this alphabet
\Sigma^{\prime}_{\mathrm{exit}}
.
Now, we can apply Lemma
16
correctly and
obtain a
coSafeLTL
formula equivalent to
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
, as summarised in the following claim:

Claim 19
.

There is a
coSafeLTL
formula
\varphi^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
that, up to the encoding of
\Sigma^{\prime}
into
2^{\mathrm{AP}_{\mathrm{exit}}}
,
is equivalent to the
NCA

\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
.

We have basically done the first main step towards translating
\mathcal{A}^{q}
into an equivalent
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
formula
\varphi^{q}
.
To avoid any misunderstanding, we remark that the encoding that we have used above is a minor technical
detail, which is not directly related to the visibility assumption
(this assumption will be used later, to show the correctness of a certain construction based on guessing annotations “on the fly”).

Note that the formula
\varphi^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
obtained above is in negation normal form and only uses the modal operators

\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{U}
, as required by the grammar of
coSafeLTL
(see Section
2
).
We can take advantage of this normal form, to perform a further rewriting on
\varphi^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
,
so as to obtain a formula that, whenever uses any atomic predicate from
\Sigma^{\prime}_{\mathrm{exit}}\cap B
,
it does so within a subformula of the form

\varphi^{C}_{\mathrm{exit}}~=~\bigwedge\nolimits_{\theta\in C}\theta~\land~\bigwedge\nolimits_{\theta\in B\setminus C}\neg\theta.

Having enforced this stronger normal form, will allow us to manipulate entire subformulae
\varphi^{C}_{\mathrm{exit}}

as if they were atoms, e.g., enabling substitutions of
\varphi^{C}_{\mathrm{exit}}
by other formulae.

Below, we give another important claim (without proof, since it is easy to verify), which characterises
acceptance of a tree by
\mathcal{A}^{q}
by the existence of an annotated path accepted by
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
:

Claim 20
.

A tree

is accepted by
\mathcal{A}^{q}
if and only if there are an infinite path
\pi=v_{0}v_{1}\dots

from the root, spelling out the sequence of labels
\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}\dots
, and a sequence of annotations
C_{0},C_{1},\dots

such that (1) the
\omega
-word
(\sigma_{0},C_{0})\>(\sigma_{1},C_{2})\>\dots
is accepted by
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
,
and (2) for every
i\geq 0
, the subtree
t_{v_{i}}
of
t
rooted at
v_{i}
is accepted by
\mathcal{A}^{\bigwedge C_{i}}

(note the latter condition for
i=0
is weaker than asking that
t
is accepted by
\mathcal{A}^{q}
).

We recall that, in order for
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
to accept an annotated path like above, it must eventually
exit the component
Q_{i}
and reach state
q_{\mathrm{exit}}
, after which
\mathcal{A}^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
accepts no matter what.
So the claim above highlights the fact that acceptance of an infinite tree starting from an existential
component of a
HTA
±
is witnessed by the existence of a suitable finite annotated path.

The next step is rather straightforward and consists of replacing the conditions in the previous claim,
which use automata, by equivalent conditions that use formulae. Specifically, we replace condition (1)
by the requirement that the
\omega
-word
(\sigma_{0},C_{0})\>(\sigma_{1},C_{2})\>\dots
satisfies the

coSafeLTL
formula
\varphi^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
. Equivalence of this new condition is implied by Claim 1.
Similarly, thanks to Property P2, we can replace condition (2) by the requirement that, for every

i\geq 0
, the subtree
t_{v_{i}}
rooted at
v_{i}
satisfies
\varphi^{\bigwedge C}
.
Overall, this allows us to rewrite Claim 2 as follows:

Claim 21
.

A tree
t
is accepted by
\mathcal{A}^{q}
if and only if there are an infinite path
\pi=v_{0}v_{1}\dots

from the root, spelling out the sequence of labels
\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}\dots
, and a sequence of annotations
C_{0},C_{1},\dots

such that (1) the
\omega
-word
(\sigma_{0},C_{0})\>(\sigma_{1},C_{2})\>\dots
satisfies
\varphi^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}
,
and (2) for every
i\geq 0
, the subtree
t_{v_{i}}
of
t
rooted at
v_{i}
satisfies
\varphi^{\bigwedge C_{i}}
.

The final step is to derive a characterisation that does not require guessing beforehand the correct
sequence of annotations
C_{0},C_{1},\dots
along a path, since modal logics like
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
do not have this ability.
The idea is instead to guess “on the fly”, while traversing the path
\pi=v_{0}v_{1}\dots
, each annotation
C_{i}

and check that the current subtree
t_{v_{i}}
satisfies the corresponding formula
\varphi^{\bigwedge C_{i}}
.
However, because subtrees are nested one inside another, this raises the following crux:
is it possible that guessing annotations independently at the different nodes along the path
\pi=v_{0}v_{1}\dots

results in an inconsistent sequence
C_{0},C_{1},\dots
that cannot be realised on the given tree
t
?
Note that the latter issue can only arise if there are multiple sequences of annotations
that satisfy the required formulae.
Luckily, this is precisely the part where we can exploit the visibility assumption, and in particular Property P3:
it is not possible that two distinct sequences
C_{0},C_{1},\dots
and
C^{\prime}_{0},C^{\prime}_{1},\dots

can be used to annotate the same sequence of labels
\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}\dots
along the same path

\pi=v_{0}v_{1}\dots
of

, and yet satisfy (1)
(\sigma_{0},C_{0})\>(\sigma_{1},C_{2})\>\dots\models\varphi^{q}_{\mathrm{exit}}

and (2)

for all
i\geq 0
. Indeed, if this happened,
then there would exist a subtree
t_{v_{i}}
that witnesses a non-empty intersection between the languages

\Lang(\varphi^{\bigwedge C_{i}})
and
\Lang(\varphi^{\bigwedge C^{\prime}_{i}})
.
As a result of this, we can simplify our characterisation of acceptance and replace
the quantification on sequences of annotations by simpler disjunctions evaluated at nodes:

Claim 22
.

A tree
t
is accepted by
\mathcal{A}^{q}
if and only if there is an infinite path
\pi=v_{0}v_{1}\dots

from the root such that

t,\pi,0~\models~\varphi_{\mathrm{exit}}^{q}\big[\varphi^{C}_{\mathrm{exit}}/\varphi^{\bigwedge C}\big]

where the formula on the right hand-side is obtained from
\varphi_{\mathrm{exit}}^{q}
by replacing every occurrence
of a subformula
\varphi^{C}_{\mathrm{exit}}
(recall that these are plain propositional formulae fully describing
a local annotation

) by
\varphi^{\bigwedge C}
.

Note that the formula written above is a path formula of
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
over the original set
\mathrm{AP}

of atomic propositions. What remains to be done is to simply add the existential path quantifier
\mathsf{E}

on top of this, whose semantics reproduces precisely the characterisation given above,
and obtain in this way the desired state formula of
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
equivalent to
\mathcal{A}^{q}
:

Claim 23
.

A tree
t
is accepted by
\mathcal{A}^{q}
if and only if it satisfies the
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
state formula

\varphi^{q}~=~\mathsf{E}\Big(\varphi_{\mathrm{exit}}^{q}\big[\varphi^{C}_{\mathrm{exit}}/\varphi^{\bigwedge C}\big]\Big).

•

It remains to consider the case where
q
is a state from a universal component
Q_{i}
.
Here we apply a standard dualisation argument to reduce this case to the existential one.
Let
\overline{\mathcal{A}}=\langle Q,\Sigma,\overline{\delta},q_{I},\overline{F}\rangle

be the
dual
automaton of
\mathcal{A}
, defined by letting

\overline{F}=Q\setminus F
, and for every
q\in Q
and
\sigma\in\Sigma
,

\overline{\delta}(q,\sigma)=\overline{\delta(q,\sigma)}
, where
\overline{\theta}

is the dual of the positive Boolean formula
\theta
,
defined inductively as follows:

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\overline{\top}=\bot&\qquad\qquad\overline{\theta_{1}\land\theta_{2}}=\overline{\theta_{1}}\lor\overline{\theta_{2}}&\qquad\qquad\overline{(\Diamond_{k},q)}=(\Box_{k},q)\\[8.61108pt]
\overline{\bot}=\top&\qquad\qquad\overline{\theta_{1}\lor\theta_{2}}=\overline{\theta_{1}}\land\overline{\theta_{2}}&\qquad\qquad\overline{(\Box_{k},q)}=(\Diamond_{k},q).\end{array}

\mathcal{A}
and
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
clearly share the same structural constraints, and every existential (resp., universal) component in
\mathcal{A}
is a universal (resp., existential) component in
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
. Moreover, if
\mathcal{A}
is counter-free and visible,
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
is too, since no modification is performed at the structural level of the components.
Since the component of
q
in
\mathcal{A}
is universal, the corresponding component in
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
is existential.
We can thus apply our construction for the existential case.
Moreover, by an easy adaptation of
[

, we know that

\overline{\mathcal{A}^{q}}
recognises the complement of the language
\Lang(\mathcal{A}^{q})
, and
hence the negation of
\varphi_{\overline{\mathcal{A}^{q}}}
is the desired formula.

We conclude the section by providing the translation in the other direction,
from
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
to a counter-free visible
HTA
±
.

Theorem 24
.

Given a
PastCTL
±
formula
\varphi
over a set of atomic propositions
\mathrm{AP}
, there is a counter-free visible
HTA
±

\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
such that
\Lang(\varphi)=\Lang(\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
).

Proof 7.2
.

We follow
[
4
, Sec. 5.1]
and employ a
simple form
of
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
, in which state formulae of the form
\mathsf{E}\psi
in the grammar of
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
are replaced by equivalent state formulae of the form
\mathsf{D}^{1}\mathsf{E}\psi
. This simple form is expressively complete for
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
, as it follows by a straightforward adaptation of
[
4
, Proposition B.4]
, and it is needed to enforce the visibility property.
The proof goes by induction on the structure of
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
state formulae in simple form.
In particular, we consider a
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
state formula
\psi
in simple form, and construct
a corresponding
HTA
±

\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
, by distinguishing the following cases:

•

\psi=p
. An automaton equivalent to an atomic proposition
p
just needs one state. We can set
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}=\langle\{q_{I}\},\Sigma,\delta,q_{I},\emptyset\rangle
, with
\delta(q_{I},\sigma)=\top
or
\delta(q_{I},\sigma)=\bot
depending on whether
p\in\sigma
or not.

•

\psi=\neg\psi^{\prime}
. By inductive hypothesis, there is an automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\psi^{\prime}}
equivalent to
\psi^{\prime}
. Then, it suffices to construct the dual automaton
\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\psi^{\prime}}}
, which recognises the complement language of
\Lang(\mathcal{A}_{\psi^{\prime}})
.

•

\psi=\psi_{1}\lor\psi_{2}
. By inductive hypothesis, we can construct two automata
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{1}}=\langle Q_{1},\Sigma,\delta_{1},q_{1},F_{1}\rangle
and
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{2}}=\langle Q_{2},\Sigma,\delta_{2},q_{2},F_{2}\rangle
, equivalent to
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
, respectively.
We then define
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}=\langle Q_{1}\uplus Q_{2}\uplus\{q_{I}\},\Sigma,\delta,q_{I},F_{1}\uplus F_{2}\rangle
,
where
q_{I}
is a fresh state.
The new state set
Q_{1}\uplus Q_{2}\uplus\{q_{I}\}
inherits the partitions into components from
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{1}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{2}}
, plus a singleton, transient component
\{q_{I}\}
. The types of components for
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
are the same as in the original automata. The ordering of the components is defined hierarchically: the singleton component
\{q_{I}\}
is the highest-order component, then come the components of
Q_{1}
, followed by those of
Q_{2}
.
Note that the set of final states of
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
is the union of the final states of
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{1}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{2}}
.
Finally, the transition function
\delta_{\psi}
coincides with
\delta_{1}
(resp.,
\delta_{2}
) when applied to states in
Q_{1}
(resp.,
Q_{2}
), and maps
(q_{I},\sigma)
, for any
\sigma\in\Sigma
, to the positive Boolean formula

\delta_{\psi}(q_{1},\sigma)\lor\delta_{\psi}(q_{2},\sigma)
.

It is easy to see that the automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
recognises precisely the union of the languages of
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{1}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{2}}
, while still remaining counter-free and visible.

•

\psi=\mathsf{D}^{n}\psi^{\prime}
. By inductive hypothesis, we can construct an automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\psi^{\prime}}=\langle Q^{\prime},\Sigma,\delta^{\prime},q^{\prime}_{I},F^{\prime}\rangle
equivalent to
\psi^{\prime}
.
We define
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}=\langle Q\uplus\{q_{I}\},\Sigma,\delta,q_{I},F^{\prime}\rangle
as follows.
The state set is obtained by extending the original set
Q
with a fresh state
q_{I}
, which is the new initial state of
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
.
This state set inherits the partition into components from the original automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\psi^{\prime}}
, and a new singleton, transient component
\{q_{I}\}
is added.
The same set
F^{\prime}
of final states is adopted as set of final states in
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
.
Moreover, the transition function
\delta
is the extension of
\delta^{\prime}
that maps any pair
(q_{I},\sigma)
, with
\sigma\in\Sigma
, to
(\Diamond_{n},q^{\prime}_{I})
.

Intuitively, the automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
starts at the root and immediately selects
n
children along which it sends copies of
\mathcal{A}_{\psi^{\prime}}
that check satisfaction of the subformula
\psi^{\prime}
.
It is then straightforward to check that
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
recognises precisely the language defined by
\psi=\mathsf{D}^{n}\psi^{\prime}
.

•

\psi=\mathsf{E}\gamma
. While in the previous cases we could use the inductive hypothesis, in the case of
\psi=\mathsf{E}\gamma
we cannot,
since
\gamma
is not a
state
subformula, but a
path
subformula.
We proceed by following the classical approach of substituting problematic state subformulae by new atomic propositions, see e.g.
[
36
, Theorem 5.3]
.
First, we identify
maximal
state subformulae of
\gamma
of the form
\mathsf{E}\gamma_{1}
or
\mathsf{D}^{n}\psi_{1}
, and collect them in the set
\max(\gamma)
.
Formally,
\max(\gamma)
contains all and only those formulae
\mathsf{E}\gamma_{1}
or
\mathsf{D}^{n}\psi_{1}
that occur as subformulae of
\gamma
but are not themselves
contained in larger subformulae of
\varphi
of the form
\mathsf{E}\gamma_{1}
or
\mathsf{D}^{n}\psi_{1}
.

By the assumption on
\varphi
being in simple form, we deduce that no formula in
\max(\gamma)
is of the form
\mathsf{E}\gamma_{1}
. Hence, we have
\max(\gamma)=\{\mathsf{D}^{n_{1}}\psi_{1},...,\mathsf{D}^{n_{k}}\psi_{k}\}
.
By the inductive hypothesis, for every
i\leq k
, we can construct an automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{i}}=\langle Q_{i},\Sigma,\delta_{i},q_{i},F_{i}\rangle
equivalent to
\psi_{i}
.
We then consider the dual versions
\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{i}}}=\langle\overline{Q_{i}},\Sigma,\overline{\delta_{i}},\overline{q_{i}},\overline{F_{i}}\rangle
of these automata.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that all state sets
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{k},\overline{Q_{1}},\dots,\overline{Q_{k}}
are pairwise disjoint.

We continue by introducing a fresh atomic proposition
p_{i}
for each formula
\mathsf{D}^{n_{i}}\psi_{i}
in the set
\max(\gamma)
. We let
\mathrm{AP}^{\prime}=\mathrm{AP}\uplus\{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\}
,
and we replace in
\gamma
every occurrence of
\mathsf{D}^{n_{i}}\psi_{i}
(for
i=1,\dots,k
) with the corresponding atomic proposition
p_{i}
, obtaining a formula
\gamma^{\prime}
over the extended
signature
\mathrm{AP}^{\prime}
. By construction, thanks to the form of the grammar of
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
, the formula
\gamma^{\prime}
turns out to be a
coSafeLTL
formula.
By Lemma
16
, there is a counter-free, looping
NCA

\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
equivalent to
\gamma^{\prime}
. We now claim the following (the proof is simple and thus omitted):

Claim 25
.

A tree
t
satisfies
\mathsf{E}\gamma
if and only if there are an infinite path
\pi
in
t
and an
\omega
-word

w\in\Lang(\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{\prime}})
(note that this word is over the extended alphabet
\Sigma^{\prime}=2^{\mathrm{AP}^{\prime}}
)
such that, for every
j\geq 0
:

(1)

w(j)\cap\mathrm{AP}=t(\pi(j))
, namely, the restriction of the symbol
w(j)
to the original set
\mathrm{AP}

of atomic propositions coincides with the label at position
j
in
\pi
according to
t
,

(2)

for every
i=1,\dots,k
,
p_{i}\in w(j)
iff
t,\pi(j)\models\mathsf{D}^{n_{i}}\psi_{i}
.

Intuitively, in the above claim the
\omega
-word
w
encodes entirely the information available along the path
\pi
that is relevant for checking satisfaction of
the formula
\mathsf{E}\gamma
.
Accordingly, the desired automaton for
\mathsf{E}\gamma
will guess a path
\pi
and simulate the
NCA

\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
along this path, while sending copies of
the automata
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{i}}
and
\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{i}}}
(for every
i\in[1,k]
) to check satisfaction of the subformulae
\mathsf{D}^{n_{i}}\psi_{i}
.
Formally, we define
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}=\langle Q\cup\bigcup\nolimits_{i=1,\dots,k}(Q_{i}\cup\overline{Q_{i}}),\Sigma,\delta_{\psi},q_{I},F\rangle
as follows.
The set
Q
is the state set of the
NCA

\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
, and forms a single existential component inside
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
.
The other sets
Q_{i}
and
\overline{Q_{i}}
inherit as usual the partition into components from their respective automata
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{i}}
and
\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{i}}}
.
As for the ordering on components,
Q
is the component of highest order, and the other components are ordered as in their respective automata, with some arbitrary choices
for components belonging to different automata.
The set
F
of final states of
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
is the union of the sets of final states of the automata
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{i}}
and
\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{i}}}
, for every
i\in[1,k]
.
Finally, the transition function
\delta_{\psi}
is the union of the transition functions
\delta_{i}
and
\overline{\delta_{i}}
on their respective domains
Q_{i}
and
\overline{Q_{i}}

(for
i=1,\dots,k
), extended so as to satisfy, for every
q\in Q
and
\sigma\in\Sigma
,

\delta_{\psi}(q,\sigma)~=~\bigvee\nolimits_{I\subseteq[1,k]}\bigvee\nolimits_{q^{\prime}\text{ s.t.~}(q,\sigma^{+},q^{\prime})\in\Delta}\Big((\Diamond,q^{\prime})~\land~\bigwedge\nolimits_{i\in I}(\Diamond_{n_{i}},q_{i})~\land~\bigwedge\nolimits_{i\in[1,k]\setminus I}(\Box_{n_{i}},\overline{q_{i}})\Big)

where
\sigma^{+}=\sigma\cup\bigcup_{i\in I}\{p_{i}\}
and
\Delta
is the transition relation of the
NCA

\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
.
In other words, the above transition function allows the automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
to read an input symbol
\sigma
from the current state
q
,
guess an annotation
\sigma^{+}
of
\sigma
that represents, for each
i=1,\dots,k
, whether
\mathsf{D}^{n_{i}}\psi_{i}
or its negation holds at the current subtree,
and accordingly simulate a
\sigma^{+}
-labeled transition of the
NCA

\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
from
q
to
q^{\prime}
while moving towards a child, eventually
witnessing a path in the input tree that satisfies
\gamma
.

By the above claim and thanks to the fact that each
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{i}}
(resp.,
\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{i}}}
) is a
HTA
±
equivalent to
\mathsf{D}^{n_{i}}\psi_{i}
(resp.,
\neg\mathsf{D}^{n_{i}}\psi_{i}
),
we obtain that the thus constructed automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
is visible and equivalent to
\psi=\mathsf{E}\gamma
.
To prove that the automaton is also counter-free, we need to show that the linearisation of each component of
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
is counter-free. We recall that the components of
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
are basically given by the components of the automata
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{i}}
and
\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{i}}}
,
whose linearisations are already counter-free, plus an additional existential component
Q
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be the linearisation of the latter component
Q
.
The crucial observation is that
\mathcal{A}
is isomorphic to the
NCA

\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
, which we know is already counter-free.
Indeed, the only difference between the two automata
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
is in their alphabets:
by definition of linearisation, the former automaton is over the alphabet
\Sigma\times 2^{B}
, where
B
is the set of atoms with states in
\bigcup\nolimits_{i}(Q_{i}\cup\overline{Q_{i}})
,
and the latter automaton is over the alphabet
\Sigma^{\prime}=2^{\mathrm{AP}^{\prime}}=2^{\mathrm{AP}\uplus\{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\}}=\Sigma\times 2^{\{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\}}
.
There is a natural bijection between the subsets
P\subseteq\{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\}
and the subsets
C\subseteq B
, which maps any
P\subseteq\{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\}

to the set
C=\bigcup\nolimits_{i\text{ s.t.~}p_{i}\in P}(\Diamond_{n_{i}},q_{i})\>\cup\>\bigcup\nolimits_{i\text{ s.t.~}p_{i}\notin P}(\Box_{n_{i}},\overline{q_{i}})
.
Moreover, it is routine to verify that this bijection is an isomorphism between the automata
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
,
in the sense that it maps transitions of
\mathcal{A}
to corresponding transitions of
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
, while preserving the source and target states.
This shows that the linearisation of
Q
is counter-free, and hence
\mathcal{A}_{\psi_{i}}
is a counter-free
HTA
±
.

The above two theorems let us conclude the following.

Corollary 26
.

Counter-free visible
HTA
±
and
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
are equivalent formalisms.

By the equivalence between
FO
and
CTL
{}^{*}_{\pm}
, one can also conclude the following.

Corollary 27
.

Counter-free visible
HTA
±
and
FO
are equivalent formalisms.

Finally, going through the respective equivalence with
FO
, there is a final result concerning equivalence of automata.

Corollary 28
.

Counter-free visible
HTA
±
and two-way linear
HTA
±
are equivalent formalisms.

Notice that the translation between counter-free visible
HTA
±
and two-way linear
HTA
±
is not direct,
and the structure of two equivalent instances of these automata models can be very different.
In particular, it is not clear how to move efficiently from one model to another.
We leave for the next and final section further considerations about the results here obtained.

8.
Discussion

In this paper, we investigated the expressive power of
FO
over infinite trees.
We first considered two branching-time temporal logics, already known to be
equivalent to
FO
, and studied their behaviour and structural properties.
We highlighted a common
polarisation property
, relating it to recent work
on Weak Chain Logic.
We also provided two automaton-based characterisations of
FO
over infinite
trees: the first is based on a two-way automaton whose state space can be
partitioned into singletons, while the second relies on a one-way automaton
whose state space can be partitioned into components of arbitrary size, subject,
however, to strong expressiveness restrictions when viewed as word automata.

We conclude this work by discussing some consequences of our
results and a number of related open questions.

Finite trees

It is natural to investigate the implications of our results in the setting of
finite trees.
To date, no automaton-based characterisation of
FO
over finite trees is known.
To be precise, in his Ph.D. thesis
[
9
, Theorem 2.5.7]
,
Bojańczyk showed that
FO
is equivalent to the
cascade product of
aperiodic wordsum automata
over finite binary trees. This characterisation
implies that every deterministic automaton recognising an
FO
-definable language
must admit such a decomposition.
However, no general
decomposition theorem
for deterministic tree automata
is known, in contrast with the Krohn-Rhodes theorem for word
automata
[
34
]
.
As a consequence, given a deterministic tree automaton, there is currently no
effective way to determine whether it admits such a decomposition and, hence,
whether it defines an
FO
language.
Let us recall that, over finite trees, deterministic bottom-up tree automata
(
\ie
, automata scanning the tree from the leaves to the root) recognise all
regular tree languages, unlike the infinite-tree setting, where no class of
deterministic automata captures all regular languages.
Our characterisations rely, indeed, on
alternating
automata over

unranked
trees, suggesting that variants of our constructions could be
considered over finite trees with arbitrary branching.
However, several obstacles immediately arise.
Most notably, the acceptance condition becomes problematic: in the alternating
setting over finite trees, one may define acceptance in terms of runs whose
branches end in a trivial condition
\top
, making the polarised acceptance
condition vacuous.
For this and related reasons, it is unclear how to obtain an alternating
automata characterisation of
FO
over finite trees, even though it seems
reasonable to believe that such a characterisation would be just an adaptation
of the ones presented here.
Nevertheless, the central challenge in the finite-tree setting is to obtain a
characterisation in terms of
deterministic
automata.
Such a result would have major consequences, as it would resolve the
long-standing problem of deciding whether a regular tree language definable in

MSO
is already definable in
FO
.

Simulation of the two classes of automata

In Corollary
28
, we established the equivalence between
counter-free distinct
HTA
±
and two-way linear
HTA
±
via a logical detour.
A natural question is whether a direct translation between these two classes
exists.
This appears to be highly non-trivial: the structural restrictions imposed on
the two models are fundamentally different, and there is no obvious way to
transform, for instance, a two-way automaton with singleton components into a
one-way automaton whose components correspond to counter-free word automata.
A direct construction, although likely to be technically involved, could provide
further insight into the structural constraints underlying
FO
over infinite
trees.

The expressive power of
PastCTL

We have already mentioned that the authors of
[
44
]
proved

CTL
∗
equivalent to
MPL
.
In contrast, this work focuses on Weak
MPL
, namely
FO
, which was shown by
Schlingloff to be equivalent to
PastCTL
±
.
We proved that
PastCTL
±
is strictly less expressive than
PastCTL
(Theorem
4
).
Since
PastCTL
is itself strictly less expressive than
CTL
∗
(and hence
MPL
), it
follows that
PastCTL
lies strictly in between
FO
and
MPL
.
Identifying the fragment of
MSO
(or, rather, of
WMSO
) corresponding to
PastCTL
thus emerges as a
natural and interesting problem, as it would yield a fragment strictly
intermediate between
FO
and
MPL
in expressive power.
