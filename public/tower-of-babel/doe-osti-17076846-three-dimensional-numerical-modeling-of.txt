 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report presents the system design and numerical analysis of a mine water heat exchanger system
developed to extract thermal energy from an abandoned underground mine shaft in Butte, Montana. The
extracted thermal energy is used to provide space heating for the nearby Mansfield Courthouse through a
heat pump. The system design is driven by building thermal demand and evaluates the effects of U-tube
loop count on hydraulic performance and the resulting parasitic pumping power. A detailed 3D numerical
model is developed using the TOUGH3 (EOS1) simulator to represent heat extraction from the mine shaft
under time-varying operating conditions using a closed-loop heat exchanger. Multiple U-shaped loops are
installed inside the shaft, and system performance is evaluated primarily in terms of outlet temperature
response. Shaft temperature contours are also analyzed to characterize spatial temperature evolution and
thermal mixing behavior over seasonal timescales. A sensitivity analysis is conducted to quantify the
effect of lateral groundwater flow toward the Berkeley Pit on heat exchanger performance. The results
indicate that, for the base-case scenario without lateral flow, the outlet temperature from the heat
exchangers remains well within favorable operating conditions for the heat pump even during peak winter
conditions. Although the heat exchangers are installed at a relatively shallow depth, buoyancy-driven
convection enables use of thermal energy from the full shaft depth. When lateral flow is introduced, the
resulting advective thermal replenishment further enhances heat transfer of the heat exchangers and
substantially improves thermal performance throughout the year.

 1. INTRODUCTION

The mine-water heat pump system contains three major components: the building, the heat pump, and the
underground mine shaft. Figure 1 presents system schematics. The shaft, identified as the Steward Shaft,
extends to a depth of about 1,250 m (approximately 4,100 ft). The shaft is filled with water from the
bottom up to about 150 m (500 ft) below the ground surface. The u-shaped loops made with high density
polyethylene (often referred to as U-tube heat exchangers) are installed in the upper 240 m
(approximately 790 ft) section of the shaft that is filled with water. These heat exchangers hang vertically
inside the shaft and are connected in parallel with the source side of a heat pump. Each U-tube loop
circulates fluid down one leg and back up the other, extracting heat from the mine-water environment
within and surrounding the shaft. Circulating fluid from all U-tubes combines and delivers thermal energy
to a heat pump located above the ground. The heat pump raises the combined U-tube outlet temperature to
a level suitable for space heating and supplies it to the building. The building draws heat from the heat
pump, and the cooled fluid returns to the U-tube heat exchangers for continued heat extraction from the
mine shaft.

Steward Shaft is a three-compartment shaft with an additional service compartment, as documented in the
stope books (Duaime et al. 2004) and shown in Figure 2. Each compartment is lined; however, the lining
material could not be located in the stope books. Each compartment is approximately 1.5 × 1.5 m (about
5 × 5 ft). The combined cross-sectional footprint of the shaft therefore spans roughly 4.5 × 1.5 m (about
15 × 5 ft), and this dimension was used in the numerical modeling. Mine water from the west side of
Steward Shaft flows toward the shaft and then continues eastward toward the Berkeley Pit at multiple
depths (Duaime et al. 2023). The FedGeo project team conducted several field tests to characterize mine-
water flow within the Steward Shaft, and the details of these field tests are presented in a companion
report (Field Tests at Steward Shaft). This report is organized to first present the building thermal demand
estimation, followed by the heat exchanger design and the 3D numerical model development. Simulation
results from the 3D model and conclusions drawn from simulation results are discussed at the end.

 2. THERMAL LOADS

The thermal load imposed on the mine-water heat exchangers was calculated using the ground-source
heat pump (GSHP) design and analysis tool developed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) (Liu
et al. 2022). An energy simulation model of the Mansfield Courthouse building was created using the
tool. The hourly heat transfer rate and flow rate of the mine-water heat exchanger required by the heat
pump for space heating and cooling the building were calculated using the EnergyPlus program (DOE
2025), which is integrated into ORNL’s GSHP tool.

The project location is in a heating-dominant region. Although the building has some cooling demand in
the summer, the temperature of the water in the mine shaft is approximately 31.5°C (88.7°F). This
temperature exceeds typical outdoor ambient temperatures encountered in the summer at the location, and
hence the mine-water heat pump system will be less efficient than conventional cooling systems that
reject heat from the building to the outdoor ambient air. This analysis therefore considers only heating
demand, and a separate 2D modeling study conducted by another FedGeo team member evaluates both
heating and cooling operations that use shaft water (Peery and Spitler 2025).

Figure 3 presents the calculated hourly heat extraction rate and flow rate of the mine-water heat
exchanger required to maintain the building’s indoor temperature. Although the ORNL GSHP tool
outputs both heat extraction and heat rejection rates, Figure 3 shows only the heat extraction results,
which are later used as boundary conditions in the analysis. The flow rate reaches approximately
18.9 kg/s (300 gal/min) in January, and the corresponding heat extraction rate exceeds 205 kW
(700 kBtu/h). There is negligible heating demand in the summer and intermediate but variable heating
demand in shoulder seasons.

 3. HEAT EXCHANGER DESIGN

The length of the U-tube heat exchanger is a key design parameter for the mine-water heat exchanger.
According to the digitized geospatial mine drawings and stope books (M25, M27, N25, and N27)
obtained from the Montana Bureau of Mines, most lateral connections occur between approximately
152 and 213 m (500 and 700 ft) below the water table. Therefore, heat exchangers that each have a length
of 240 m (787 ft) measured downward from the water table are selected. An additional 150 m (492 ft) of
each heat exchanger extends above the water table and is exposed to air because the water table occurs at
approximately 152 m (500 ft) below the surface. Another design parameter is the number of U-tube heat
exchangers. The required winter flow rate establishes the upper bound for hydraulic design, which is
defined by allowable pressure drop, pumping power, and the resulting constraints on the number and
diameter of U-tube heat exchangers. Although a single U-tube loop could theoretically carry 19 kg/s (300
gal/min), the resulting pressure drop would be excessive, leading to impractically high pumping power
and insufficient heat-transfer area to meet the thermal load. Installing multiple U-tube loops in parallel
reduces the flow rate per loop, lowers pumping power requirements, and increases the total heat-transfer
area. However, increasing the number of loops also raises installation cost, construction complexity, and
maintenance requirements and may be limited by physical access to the shaft during installation.

Figure 4 and Figure 5 quantify these relationships for various combinations of internal pipe diameters and
U-tube counts using the maximum and average system flow rate, respectively. Other parameters used in
the calculations are listed in Table 1. The Reynolds number contours in Figure 4 show that pipe diameter
and loop count jointly determine the flow regime. A low loop count forces high velocity and higher
Reynolds numbers, whereas a high loop count lowers velocity and reduces Reynolds numbers. The pump-
power contours in Figure 5 show the hydraulic consequence. A low loop count requires significant
pumping power at high flow rates, particularly for pipe diameters less than 38 mm (1.5 in.). Increasing the

loop count rapidly reduces the required pumping power because of reduced per-loop velocity and friction
losses.

From a numerical modeling perspective, higher numbers of U-tubes significantly increase the meshing
complexity and overall computational cost in 3D simulations. The simulations in this study were therefore
conducted using five U-tube heat exchangers, each with an internal diameter of 2.5 in. (63.5 mm).

For this configuration, at the average total system flow rate of 7.6 kg/s (120.9 gal/min), the per-loop flow
rate is 1.5 kg/s (24.2 gal/min), resulting in a Reynolds number of 3.05 × 104, indicating fully turbulent

 5

flow. At the maximum total system flow rate of 18.7 kg/s (295.5 gal/min), the per-loop flow rate
increases to 3.7 kg/s (59.1 gal/min), corresponding to a Reynolds number of 7.44 × 104.

The corresponding pumping requirements for the five-loop, 2.5 in. configuration were quantified using
the full U-tube hydraulic length, including the surface piping, the pipe exposed to air within the shaft, and
the pipe submerged in the shaft water. At maximum flow, the calculated pressure drop is 182.24 kPa
(equivalent to a head of 18.63 m or 26.4 psi), resulting in a total pumping power requirement of 4.85 kW
(6.51 hp) assuming a pump efficiency of 70%. At average flow conditions, the pressure drop is 37.30 kPa
(head of 3.81 m or 5.42 psi), yielding a total pumping power of 0.41 kW (0.55 hp).

To place these values in context, for a peak building thermal demand of approximately 700 kBtu/h
(205 kW) and an assumed heating coefficient of performance (COP) of 5, the corresponding heat-pump
compressor power is approximately 44 kW (59 hp). Under peak flow conditions, the required pumping
power of approximately 5 kW (6.7 hp) therefore represents about 10%–12% of the heat-pump electrical
input and roughly 2% of the building thermal demand. Under average operating conditions, pumping
power is substantially lower and constitutes only a minor fraction of total system electricity consumption.

Because system flow rates are below the peak value for most of the operating period, a variable-speed
pump is required in practice to modulate flow in response to thermal demand. Variable-speed operation
substantially reduces pumping power during part-load conditions, making the peak pumping requirement
a conservative upper bound and lowering annual parasitic pumping energy relative to fixed-speed
operation.

 4. NUMERICAL MODEL DEVELOPMENT

The 3D numerical model was developed using the TOUGH3 (Transport of Unsaturated Groundwater and
Heat) numerical simulator (Jung et al. 2018; Pruess 2004). It is a multiphase, multicomponent simulator
for fluid flow and heat transport through porous or fractured geologic media. TOUGH3 has been widely
used for a variety of applications, including geologic sequestration of CO2 and nuclear waste,
environmental remediation, oil and gas production, conventional geothermal, enhanced geothermal
systems (EGS), and shallow geothermal (Blanco-Martin et al. 2015). TOUGH3 is also used for detailed
geothermal heat pump studies involving one or a few boreholes (Hu et al. 2020, 2022; Doughty et al.
2021) and simplified ground heat exchanger studies involving large borehole fields consisting of many
borehole heat exchangers (Doughty et al. 2025).

4.1 SIMULATION DOMAIN AND DISCRETIZATION

The discretized 3D domain of the numerical domain, shown in Figure 6, extends from the water table
downward to a depth of 1,250 m (4,100 ft) in vertical direction. The portion of the pipe exposed to air in
the shaft above the water table is assumed to be well insulated, resulting in negligible heat loss;
consequently, the water temperature at the heat pump inlet equals the pipe outlet temperature at the water
table elevation. The horizontal domain spans 201.5 × 204.5 m (661 × 671 ft). The mine shaft is at the
center of this simulation domain. The shaft consists of a three-compartment cross section with overall
dimensions of 1.5 × 4.5 m (5 ×15 ft). Five U-tube heat exchangers are placed inside the shaft. Each U-
tube has a length of 240 m (787 ft).

The domain uses a structured hexahedral discretization mesh generated in Geometry, Mesh, Solver, and
Human–computer interface (GMSH) (Geuzaine and Remacle 2009). The mesh includes fine resolution in
and around the shaft compartments and coarser resolution in the far-field rock surrounding the shaft. The
GMSH mesh was converted to a TOUGH-format grid and imported into TOUGH3 for simulation.

4.2 MATERIAL PROPERTIES AND INITIAL AND BOUNDARY CONDITIONS

In TOUGH3, discretized domains are represented as porous continua, and the numerical model is solved
using a porous-media formulation. The porous-media properties are adjusted to represent true domain
properties. In the numerical model created for this study, the shaft water domain is modeled as a highly
porous medium with porosity of 0.95 and permeability of 1.0 × 10−7 m2 (1.0 × 10−6 ft2) in all directions,
which represents the water in the shaft to be a freely moving fluid. Temperature-dependent water
properties are used so that natural convection driven by density changes introduced by heat exchanging
with the U-tubes is captured. The five U-tube heat exchangers are represented as a separate porous
continuum embedded within the shaft water. This continuum uses high porosity (0.95) and high
anisotropic permeability of 1.0 × 10−7 m2 (1.0 × 10−6 ft2) oriented along the flow direction, which forces
the water circulating through the U-tube geometry.

For the base-case scenario, the surrounding rock domain is represented as having low porosity and low
permeability, ensuring minimal fluid movement and predominantly conductive heat transfer in the
geological formation. However, for the sensitivity analysis, the effect of regional lateral groundwater flow
within the mine shaft is modeled explicitly. This is implemented by injecting a prescribed mass of water
at a specified depth on one vertical wall of the shaft and extracting the same mass at another depth on the
opposite wall of the shaft. This configuration generates a vertical velocity component within the shaft that
approximately represents the water movement in the shaft observed during the field tests. The flow
direction is assumed based on the regional lateral groundwater flow in Butte, where water moves from the
west side of the shaft toward the Berkeley Pit, which is a few miles away on the east side of the shaft.
Material properties of the surrounding rock used in the model are listed in Table 2. These values are based
on the properties of quartz monzonite, which is the dominant bedrock in this region (Davis, Michael G.,
et al. 2007).

Mass-flow boundary conditions are applied at the inlet and outlet of each U-tube. The inlet receives a
prescribed mass flow rate that varies hourly, as shown in Figure 3. The precalculated hourly heat
extraction rate (discussed previously in the “Thermal Loads” section) is imposed by prescribing a
negative heat flux at the inlet. This approach approximates recirculating the water cooled by the
evaporator of the heat pump back to the mine-water heat exchangers. TOUGH3 then computes the outlet
water temperature of each U-tube at each time step.

The lateral, top, and bottom boundaries of the rock domain use no-flow conditions for mass and no-flux
conditions for heat, with the distance to the boundaries chosen to be large enough to ensure that the far-
field remains thermally undisturbed. The initial condition of the entire simulation domain (including heat
exchangers, shaft water, and the surrounding rock) is a uniform temperature of 31.5°C (88.7°F), which is
consistent with the field test results. Hydrostatic pressure is assigned according to the full depth of the
shaft.

The numerical model can account for convective and conductive heat transfer between the mine water
and the heat exchangers, the natural convection of water flow in the mine shaft induced by temperature
differentials resulting from heat exchange, and the forced water flow within the U-tube heat exchangers
under time-varying thermal loading conditions.

4.3 RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS

Outlet temperature at the exit of the U-tubes represents the primary output of the numerical simulations.
In addition to this point-wise result, the full temperature distribution within the domain is extracted from
the output files at different simulation times. The outlet temperature of the circulating fluid directly enters
the heat pump evaporator and therefore governs the COP of the system.

Figure 7 compares the inlet and outlet temperatures for one representative U-tube out of the five
simulated loops. System operation is assumed to begin at the start of 2027, and the results therefore
represent one full year of temperature variation from January through December. In this simulation, the
lateral water flow in the mine shaft was not accounted for. As shown in Figure 7, the minimum outlet
temperature reaches approximately 59°F (15°C), and the outlet temperature remains above this value for
the rest of the year. An outlet source temperature of approximately 59°F (15°C) under peak winter
operation represents a highly favorable condition for heat pump performance and typically corresponds to
a COP greater than 5. During all active heating periods, a sustained temperature difference exists between
the inlet and outlet, which confirms sustained heat extraction from the mine water throughout the heating
season.

 8

 Figure 7. Inlet and outlet water temperatures from one of the five U-tubes in the mine water.

Strong short-term temperature fluctuations occur during January through March and October through
December. These oscillations reflect the transient response of the shaft water and surrounding rock to
rapidly varying winter heating demand. From March through June, a gradual recovery of both inlet and
outlet temperatures is observed as building load declines and thermal recharge from the surrounding
formation becomes dominant. From mid-June through late August, the inlet and outlet temperatures
converge and remain nearly identical at the initial undisturbed temperature of 88.25°F (31.25°C). This
plateau confirms complete system shutdown and thermal equilibrium in the shaft during the non-heating
season. During fall reactivation in September and thereafter, temperatures decline again because of
renewed heating demand.

Another critical aspect of thermal drawdown in the mine shaft is the degree of thermal mixing driven by
natural convection. Mixing behavior directly controls thermal stratification within the shaft and influences
the depth design of the U-tubes. To evaluate this behavior, temperature contours of the shaft were
extracted at selected times during the simulation. Figure 8 shows the vertical temperature distribution at
four selected times: (a) t = 0.00 days, (b) t = 2.89 days, (c) t = 12.15 days, and (d) t = 203.7 days.

At t = 0.00 days, corresponding to the initial condition on January 1, the shaft exhibits a uniform
temperature distribution equal to the undisturbed mine-water temperature. No stratification or thermal
plume is present at this stage. By t = 2.89 days, a distinct cooled region develops near the upper portion of
the shaft surrounding the U-tube heat exchangers. The temperature contours show the initiation of a
thermal plume that propagates downward, indicating the buoyancy-driven flow caused by local cooling.
At t = 12.15 days, the cooled plume extends to the shaft bottom, and the temperature field shows strong
redistribution—cooler water in the center of the shaft whereas water adjacent to the shaft wall is kept near
initial conditions. The horizontal temperature difference between the wall and the center of the shaft
results in conductive heat transfer from the surrounding rock to the shaft water. This behavior confirms
active natural convection within the shaft, which enhances vertical mixing and suppresses long-term
thermal stratification.

By t = 203.7 days, corresponding to mid-summer operation, thermal stratification disappears entirely. The
shaft becomes well mixed, and the temperature distribution returns to nearly uniform conditions that are
close to the initial undisturbed shaft temperature. This behavior occurs because the system remains
inactive during summer months, and the absence of heat extraction allows natural convection and
conduction from the surrounding rock to fully homogenize the shaft temperature. These results
demonstrate that natural convection within the water-filled shaft produces strong vertical mixing during
heating operation and prevents persistent stratification. As a result, heat exchangers installed at different
depths remain thermally coupled to the full shaft water column rather than operating within an isolated
thermal layer. This behavior supports effective long-term thermal performance of the mine-water heat
pump system.

This simulation establishes a base-case scenario in which advective transport within the shaft is neglected.
In this configuration, the shaft behaves as a hydraulically isolated water column with no connections to
the surrounding mine network and is bounded laterally by impermeable rock. Field tests led by Professor
James Tinjum at the University of Wisconsin–Madison indicate that water enters the Steward Shaft at one
depth and exits at another, producing a vertical velocity component within the shaft (Kim et al., 2026). To
account for this behavior, additional simulations are conducted and described in the next section. Notably,
the base case represents a conservative lower bound on outlet water temperature and constitutes a worst-
case thermal scenario that is unlikely under realistic hydrogeologic conditions.

4.4 LATERAL FLOW AND SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS

Lateral flow is introduced by prescribing a mass influx at approximately 66 ft (20 m) below the water
table and an equal mass outflux at approximately 853 ft (260 m) below the water table on the opposite
side of the shaft. This source–sink pair establishes an effective lateral hydraulic gradient toward the
Berkeley Pit. Conservation of mass converts the imposed discharge into a sustained, shaft-averaged
vertical flow within the water column, which in turn drives advective heat transport consistent with
observed field behavior. In this lateral-flow scenario, heat transfer results from the combined effects of
forced vertical advection, buoyancy-driven natural convection caused by temperature-dependent density
variations, and thermal diffusion through the shaft water and surrounding rock.

Two representative cases are simulated with shaft-averaged vertical velocities of 0.5 ft/min (0.0025 m/s)
and 1.0 ft/min (0.0050 m/s) to evaluate sensitivity to advective strength. These velocities are not
prescribed independently; they arise from the imposed volumetric flow rates and the shaft cross-sectional
area. The corresponding total shaft-to-pit discharges are approximately 0.35 and 0.75 million gallons per
day (0.0153 and 0.0329 m3/s), respectively, as shown in Figure 9. For reference, the total pumping rate
from the Berkeley Pit is approximately 3 million gallons per day (0.131 m3/s). The simulated cases

The results for the lateral-flow sensitivity cases are evaluated in terms of the outlet water temperature
from the U-tubes. The effect of sustained vertical flow on outlet temperature is shown in Figure 10, which
compares the base-case condition with no imposed advection (0 ft/min) against the two lateral-flow cases
with vertical velocities of 0.5 ft/min and 1.0 ft/min. The time shown corresponds to the first 3 months of
the year to clearly reveal winter thermal behavior and avoid visual compression of the temperature trends,
but the trend continues throughout the year.

Figure 10 shows that the introduction of sustained vertical flow produces a consistent increase in outlet
temperature during winter operation. The 0.5 ft/min case exhibits a measurable upward shift relative to
the base case, whereas the 1.0 ft/min case produces the highest outlet temperatures throughout the winter
period. During the coldest period in the first week of January, the base-case outlet temperature drops to
approximately 58°F. The 0.5 ft/min and 1.0 ft/min lateral-flow cases increase the minimum outlet
temperature to about 66°F and 68°F, respectively, corresponding to an improvement of roughly 8°F–10°F
due to induced vertical flow. This behavior confirms that advective replenishment of warm water into the
shaft directly offsets thermal drawdown caused by heat extraction and improves the thermal resilience of
the system during periods of maximum heating demand. In addition to time-series comparison,
temperature contours for the 1.0 ft/min case are also compared against the base-case scenario to visualize
the corresponding changes in plume structure and shaft-scale thermal mixing.

Figure 11 presents a comparison of shaft water temperature contours on the second day of January for the
base-case scenario and the lateral-flow case with a vertical velocity of 1.0 ft/min. The contours are shown
on two vertical planes passing through the shaft center. In the base case, the temperature field is governed
primarily by buoyancy-driven natural convection. A cooled thermal plume develops around the U-tube
region and extends downward into the shaft because of density-driven circulation.

In the lateral-flow case, the thermal plume reaches approximately the same depth as in the base case, but
the temperature within the plume is noticeably higher. The imposed vertical advection continuously
transports warmer water toward the U-tube region, limiting localized thermal depletion near the heat
exchangers. The mass outflux is visible on the right boundary of the XY plane, whereas the corresponding
mass influx occurs on the opposite side at a temperature close to the undisturbed shaft temperature and
therefore does not appear distinctly in the contour plot. The sustained advection enhances advective heat
replenishment at the U-tube boundary, which keeps the water temperature near the heat exchangers close
to the initial shaft water temperature and reduces the effective thermal resistance of the shaft water
surrounding the heat exchangers. This in turn leads to higher outlet water temperatures from the U-tube
heat exchangers.

Figure 12 presents a comparison of shaft temperature contours on the nineteenth day of operation for the
base case and the 1.0 ft/min lateral-flow case. In the base case, the cooled thermal plume has propagated
through the full depth of the shaft and reaches the bottom predominantly through buoyancy-driven natural
convection. The temperature field shows strong vertical penetration and well water mixing.

In contrast, the lateral-flow case shows a warmer and more vertically uniform temperature field across the
entire shaft depth. The imposed vertical advection continuously transports warmer water toward the U-
tube region while simultaneously discharging cooler water from the shaft. A portion of the cooled water is
redistributed into deeper depths and mixed with the mine water there. However, the strength of the
resulting thermal plume remains weak. Although the plume extends through nearly the full depth of the
shaft during periods of highest thermal demand, the shaft temperature gradient is very small and
effectively negligible. This indicates that advective mixing dominates over localized thermal
accumulation, preventing the formation of strong stratification. These contour plots correspond to peak
heating conditions. During other months, when thermal demand is lower, the temperature contours remain
sharply uniform across the entire shaft, indicating an even weaker thermal perturbation and effectively
negligible effect on the bulk shaft water temperature throughout the year.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

This study developed a 3D numerical model using the TOUGH3 program to evaluate a heat exchanger
design for extracting heat from an abandoned mine shaft in Butte, Montana, to provide space heating
through a heat pump to the nearby Mansfield Courthouse building. The model accounts for the geometry
of the mine shaft and a heat exchanger immersed in the mine water, which includes five parallelly
connected U-tube loops. This model calculates the heat transfer between mine water and the heat
exchanger, as well as the heat transfer between the mine water and the surrounding ground formation.
With the precalculated thermal demand and flow rate of the heat exchanger as input, this model predicts
the inlet and outlet water temperatures of the heat exchanger over a year. Simulation results indicate that,
even without accounting for lateral groundwater flow inside the mine shaft, the outlet water temperatures
of the heat exchanger are kept above 15°C (59°F) during heating operation over the entire year. With this
heat source temperature, the heat pump can provide space heating to the building at a COP higher than 5.
Detailed simulation results of the temperature distribution inside the mine shaft indicate that the
buoyancy-driven convection enables effective use of thermal energy from the full shaft depth despite the
relatively short heat exchangers. When lateral groundwater flow inside the mine shaft is accounted for in
the model, sustained advective replenishment consistently increases outlet temperature relative to the base
case, with the maximum increase occurring during peak heating demand and reaching approximately
10°F. From a thermal performance standpoint, the mine shaft represents a robust and resilient heat source
to support effective heating operation of a heat pump.
