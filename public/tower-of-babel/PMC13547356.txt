Enzymatic deinking of used-paper using laccase and cellulase from Metarhizium granulomatis and Aspergillus niger: a sustainable upcycling approach
Authors: Sweta Shrivastava, Sarika Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pulp and paper industry is one of the main pillars of the global economy. The average global paper upcycling rate is 60%, with < 30% paper upcycling rate in India. The present investigation focuses the exploration of enzymatic deinking technology (EDT) for the upcycling of used-paper (photocopied/print) using laccase and cellulase enzymes, produced from fungal isolates. Among all the isolated strains, Metarhizium granulomatis and Aspergillus niger showed maximum enzyme activity for laccase (196.786 U/mL) and cellulase (8.471 U/mL), respectively. Gene-specific analysis and protein profiling approach revealed the presence of genes (β-tubulin, eglB, and cbh) responsible for the respective enzyme production. A comparative analysis for paper upcycling between the experimental set (enzyme extract, commercial cellulase, and chemical treatment) revealed an enhancement in the optical/physical properties of upcycled paper (enzyme extract treated). Microbial cellulase achieved the highest deinking efficiency (95.29%) as compared to others. Current novel technology can be deployed for upcycling of used-paper having potential for revitalizing paper upcycling industry globally.Keywords: Paper industry, Upcycling, Biobleaching, Metarhizium granulomatis, Aspergillus nigerSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

Pulp and Paper industry is the fifth largest industrial sector that is directly tied to socio-economic development, manufacturing a variety of products either using raw virgin fibres/ lignocellulosic biomass/ used-paper. India accounts for 5% of the worldwide paper market, now ranking as the 15th largest manufacturer globally (https://www.infomerics.com)1. In view of clean and sustainable development, the paper upcycling rate is rising with respect to manufacturing from virgin fibres. A report by Statista Research Department (2024), states that worldwide uptake of paper and paperboard is projected to increase from 400 million tonnes/year to 476 million tonnes annually by 2032 (https://www.statista.com/statistics/1089078/demand-paper-globally-until-2030/). In India, the paper consumption is 16 million tonnes/year and is predicted to reach 30 million tonnes annually by 2027 (Indian Pulp and Paper Technical Association, 2023)2. The rising demand of paper and paper-based products is driven by increased industrial activity, rising literacy rates, and sustainable packaging. Consequently, to fulfil the increasing requirements, present developing technologies are grounded on the sustainable use of renewable resources having prospects for the revitalisation along with the meteoric expansion of the pulp and paper industry. Enzyme biotechnology is considered as one of the emerging field for paper upcycling which is more environment-friendly as compared to conventional methods that rely on chemicals, which have an adverse effects on paper quality and the environment3. Therefore, enzyme-based technology deployment is the need of the hour; aligning with the sustainable development goals (SDGs).

Paper is a multilayer carpet mainly consisting of a network of cellulose fibres reinforced by a matrix of hemicelluloses impregnated with lignin, bonded together by intermolecular forces4. Paper manufacturing essentially requires crushing, beating, pulping, and bleaching (deinking) whereas during the process of paper upcycling deinking is the foremost step. It involves the dislodgement of ink fragments entrapped in pulp fibres followed by their elimination from the fibre surface by flotation, and subsequent washing5 to attain upcycled fibres, further reintegrating them into paper, ultimately led to a reduction in wood (primary fibre) processing load for the fibre production. Advancements in the biopulping and biobleaching processes have encouraged the utilization of wide range of secondary (used-paper) fibres such as photocopied/print paper, pen-ink paper, news print, mixed-office waste for making copier papers6. It involves the usage of enzymes in conjunction with mechanical forces to break, hydrate, and depolymerize the paper to disintegrate the ink fragments7. Utilization of different microbial enzymes viz., laccase, cellulase, xylanase, α-Amylase, and lipase has been reported for the deinking of used-paper either alone or in combination8,9. During the processing of the paper, biopulping involves the catalysis of lignin by laccase, while xylanase aids in severing the lignin–hemicellulose bonds, which increases delignification, respectively10. Whereas, deinking results in increasing the number of hydroxyl groups (-OH) on the fibre surface and strengthening the bonding link between fibres, which enhances the deinking frequency, pulp yield, brightness, and while minimizing the chemical load in the effluent/spent medium. Consequently, this declines the detrimental impact on the environment and reduces the expense of effluent treatment from the paper industrial ecosystem11. Researchers reported that utilising a chemical-enzymatic method to deink mixed-office waste pulp lowering the demand of traditional chemicals by 60% while concurrently enhancing the paper strength and reducing the effluent load12. Enzymes have been proposed as an eco-friendly substitute to deinking chemicals along with the reduction in the detrimental effect on the environment in line with the SDG goals of the UNDP13. By reducing the demand for raw wood pulp, it mitigates deforestation and preserves terrestrial biodiversity (SDG 15), while its lower energy and water requirement significantly decrease the carbon footprint of production compared to primary manufacturing (SDG 13). The paper upcycling industry directly advances the UNDP SDGs by transforming waste into high-value resources, thereby decoupling economic growth from environmental degradation (SDG 12 and 9).

The current study attempts to offer a patented technology for the upcycling of used-paper (photocopied/print). It emphasizes on the exploration of the untapped potential exhibited by enzymes to attain the sustainable process for paper upcycling. It also focuses on identifying the gene and protein moieties responsible for exhibiting the action mechanism facilitating the enzymatic deinking of the used-paper. The impetus of the study arose to meet the challenge of raising the national paper upcycling rate from ~ 30% to the global average of ~ 60%. Consequently, to make the technology industrially feasible in terms of socio-economic impact, microbial-assisted upcycling of used-paper shows promising potential for future industrial application.

Results

Isolation, screening, and identification of potent fungi for EDT

Soil sample harbor an extensive range of microbial flora capable of producing variety of enzymes adapted to their survival conditions. The collected samples were subjected to screening for the identification of fungal isolates possessing enzymes-producing capabilities. Ten fungal isolates were isolated and assigned with an accession number (BV101, BV102, BV103, BV104, BV105, BV106, BV107, BV108, BV109, and BV110) followed by their morphological and microscopic analysis (Fig. 1).

Screening for laccase and cellulase producers

Isolated fungi were subjected to screening for laccase and cellulase enzyme production. During primary screening, out of the ten fungal isolates, four showed laccase enzyme production by degrading Methyl Orange dye with halo/clear zone ranging from 20 to 24 mm. Correspondingly, five fungal isolates produced a halo around their growth on carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) agar plate with diameters ranging from 10 to 25 mm when screened by Congo-red method. During the secondary screening, these isolates were further processed for quantitative analysis. The guaiacol assay of selected fungal isolates demonstrated that BV108 showed the maximum enzyme activity (196.786 ± 4.781 U/mL), followed by BV102 (160.285 ± 3.489 U/mL), and BV103 (138.061 ± 3.631 U/mL) while BV105 (52.393 ± 2.621 U/mL) exhibited the least activity (Fig. 2a). A one-way ANOVA revealed that there was a statistically significant difference in laccase enzyme activity among the four different isolates (p = 0.02). Analogously, DNS assay of selected fungal isolates demonstrated that BV109 shows maximum enzyme activity (8.472 ± 0.795 U/mL), followed by BV102 (5.390 ± 0.548 U/mL), BV104 (4.091 ± 0.485 U/mL), and BV105 (3.945 ± 0.265 U/mL) while BV106 (1.166 ± 0.215 U/mL) showed the least activity (Fig. 2b). Likewise, cellulase activity also differed significantly among the five isolates (p = 0.01). The p-value clearly indicate that the data are statistically significant (Fig. 3).

Identification of potent fungi for EDT

Identification of the screened isolates BV108 and BV109 was carried out using 18S rDNA sequencing. Chromosomal DNA was isolated from the respective fungal isolate and the amplified polymerase chain reaction (PCR) product of the 18S gene (380 bp) was analysed on 1% agarose gel (well 1) as shown in Fig. 4b. Amplicons of the fungal isolates were sequenced at the National Centre of Microbial Resource (NCMR), Pune and the FASTA sequences of the 18S rDNA was used to perform BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) search. The 18S rDNA sequence of fungal isolate BV108 was submitted to the NCBI GenBank database as Metarhizium granulomatis (Accession number PX133017.1), and isolate BV109 as Aspergillus niger (Accession number PX133018.1), respectively. Phylogenetic analysis of the 18S rDNA sequence of the respective identified isolates was performed based on the maximum identity scores. The phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3) showed the relationship between the consensus and the obtained aligned sequences using BLASTn. The tree was constructed using 18S rDNA sequences of fungal isolates, including the screened/identified isolates obtained from soil samples (accession numbers marked with green dot). The tree was generated using the maximum likelihood method, incorporating a suitable evolutionary model to determine relationship among the sequences. The phylogenetic tree of Metarhizium granulomatis (Fig. 3a) demonstrates that PX133017.1 exhibited a high degree of similarity with HM152770.1. This indicates a close evolutionary relationship by forming a small clade together and sharing a common ancestor. Additionally, the tree indicated that PX133017.1 and HM152770.1 groups with a nearby subgroup containing JQ278018.1 followed by KU350745.1 forming a well-defined subclade. Likewise, the phylogenetic tree of Aspergillus niger (Fig. 3b) demonstrates that PX133018.1 exhibited maximum similarity with MF093522.1, subsequently forming a distinct subclade with OQ231482.1 as a sister taxon. This analysis comprehensively describes the evolutionary lineage of the screened/identified isolates and its potential adaptation mechanisms, which could be beneficial in deinking of the used-paper. After confirming their viability, purity, and authenticity, the strains were deposited at NCCS, Pune and assigned accession numbers MCC 9740 and MCC 9680, respectively.

Gene-specific analysis and protein profiling

Isolation of genomic DNA and PCR

Genomic DNA of Metarhizium granulomatis and Aspergillus niger was isolated and visualized on 1% agarose gel. The concentration and purity (A260/A280) of Metarhizium granulomatis DNA was 1310 ng/µL and 1.72, respectively while for Aspergillus niger was 900 ng/µL and 1.76. Subsequent PCR amplification confirmed the presence of the targeted gene fragments. According to the product length of primer BLAST hit result, β-tubulin gene was taken as internal positive control (IPC) while Lac4 and Lac7 was selected for the gene-specific analysis of laccase enzyme. Likewise, Endoglucanase B and Cellobiohydrolase gene was preferred for cellulase enzyme (Table 1). The IPC gene, β-tubulin was successfully amplified from the genomic template of M. granulomatis, yielding a distinct band at 3000 bp in well 2 (Fig. 4a). For A. niger, successful amplification was observed for both cellulase-associated genes: the endoglucanase B gene produced an 800 bp amplicon in well 3, and the cellobiohydrolase gene yielded a 2500 bp amplicon in well 4. Additionally, a clear 380 bp band representing the 18S rDNA reference fragment was resolved in well 1. (Fig. 4a).

Isolation of protein and SDS-PAGE

Total proteins were isolated from liquid nitrogen-crushed mycelia and spore suspension by using Mg/CHAP modified method and compared against salt precipitation method (20%, 40%, 60%, and 80% saturation). Quantitative estimation demonstrated that the Mg/CHAPS extraction of fungal mycelia yielded significantly higher protein concentrations than both the spore suspension extractions and the salt precipitation fractions. The concentration of the proteins isolated from M. granulomatis and A. niger were 1.585 ± 0.707 µg/µL and 0.71 ± 0.208 µg/µL, respectively. The SDS-PAGE analysis of the crude mycelial extracts revealed distinct protein profiles for each isolate (Fig. 4b). The M. granulomatis profile (Lane 1) exhibited prominent bands at molecular weights of 13, 15, 17, 18, 22, 23, and 27 kDa. Conversely, the A. niger extract (Lane 2) resolved into bands corresponding to 16, 20, 22, 23, 25, 27, and 28 kDa.

Gene-specific analysis provides an in-depth insight of the availability of particular gene in the respective isolates while protein bands inferring laccase and cellulase enzyme production, respectively.

Upcycling of used-paper and papermaking

Bulk production of laccase and cellulase enzyme

Crude laccase and cellulase enzyme extracts were further scaled up for used-paper upcycling at a specific activity of 5 U/g of dried pulp.

Upcycling of used-paper

Initial hydropulping and pre-treatment of used photocopied/print paper facilitated efficient fiber dispersion and stable pH regulation during subsequent treatments. Three experimental set were conducted for the upcycling of used-paper involved enzyme extract (laccase and cellulase), commercial cellulase (Sigma-Alrich), and conventional chemical treatment. Enzymatic treatment leads to the dislodgement of ink fragments entrapped within the pulp fibres. The addition of the surfactant Tween-20 (0.5%) effectively enhanced enzyme stability, accelerated ink dislodgement efficiency, throughout the process thereby improving various pulp properties such as brightness, freeness, softness of the pulp. Subsequent washing stages successfully removed the detached ink fragments, preventing their redeposition onto the pulp matrix. Analogously, in the chemical treatment group, sodium silicate provided pH buffering, stabilized the hydrogen peroxide, and helped in ink removal along with whitening of the pulp. Sodium hydroxide facilitated pulping and deinking while hydrogen peroxide mainly acted as bleaching agent to counteract alkaline yellowing.

Casting of the untreated and treated pulp (laccase, cellulase, commercial cellulase, and chemical treated) provided the comparative (visual and physical) analysis demonstrating that the cellulase enzyme worked more efficiently followed by other experimental set (Fig. 5c). All the samples of handsheet (untreated and treated) were tested at MSME central testing laboratory facilty at MSME Development Institute, Jaipur to validate these structural and optical improvements.

Characterization of handsheet

The handsheet of laccase and cellulase treated pulp showed similarity in brightness index (90%) followed by commercial cellulase (86%), and chemical treated pulp (80%), respectively. Alternatively, the tensile index of laccase treated pulp was comparatively higher (7.3 ± 0.365 Nm/g) followed by commercial cellulase (5.5 ± 0.330 Nm/g), and microbial cellulase (5.0 ± 0.250 Nm/g). The tensile index of chemical treated handsheet is lowest with the value of 1.9 ± 0.100 Nm/g. A one-way ANOVA revealed that the different treatment applied to the photocopied/print paper had a statistically significant effect in the tensile index of paper. Laccase treated paper exhibited the highest tensile index, and statistically highly significant (p < 0.0001), compared with untreated, cellulase treated, commercial cellulase treated, and chemical treated paper. Categorically, the deinking efficiency of pulp treated with microbial cellulase (95.29%) was comparatively higher than commercial cellulase (94.4%), and microbial laccase (93.84%), respectively as shown in Fig. 5a.

Characterization of spent medium

UV–Visible spectral analysis of the spent medium revealed a characteristic absorbance peak in the 230–260 nm region, indicating the successful removal and solubilization of lignin components during enzymatic treatment. Altogether, similar observations were recorded in the spent medium of other experimental sets i.e. commercial cellulase and chemical deinking, suggesting the removal of lignin. Consequently, the removal of lignin cleaved the bond links between the fibre and ink thereby facilitating the release and separation of entrapped ink fragments from pulp fibres. Hence, a gradual decline in the spectral scan was observed across 400–800 nm (Fig. 5b).

Discussion

The paper-making industry faces an urgent pressure to reduce its carbon footprint and heavy reliance on water and energy. The ongoing research primarily focuses on optimizing conventional methods rather than pioneering sustainable upcycling technologies to create high-value upcycled products14. Furthermore, a significant economic disparity exists where the large-scale mills can afford purified commercial enzymes, while the small-scale operations must rely on toxic chemical deinking to remain viable. These harmful chemical treatments severely degrade the optical and mechanical properties of the upcycled paper. Additionally, generate effluents with high chemical oxygen demand (COD) and subsequent treatment cost15. Consequently, the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB, 2025) classifies the pulp and paper industry (agro and wood, upcycled fibre/waste paper based) in the critical “RED category” with an overall Pollution Index ranging from 86.3–98.1 (https://cpcb.nic.in/categorization-of-industrial-sectors/). The recent plastics ban and rising worries about plastic packaging, a bio-based upcycling technology is essential to addressing these environmental and economic challenges16.

The current study demonstrates the critical relevance of fungal enzymes as sustainable alternatives to hazardous chemical deinking and the untreated paper mill effluent17,18. Additionally, a comparative study between biodeinking and chemical deinking infers that biodeinking is an efficient way of upcycling method to overcome the existing problem19,20. The role of various enzymes in pulp and paper industry has reported but emphases mainly on cellulase, hemicellulase, laccase, and xylanase3,21–24. Additionally, previous literature highlighting fungi25,26 particularly Fusarium sp. and Aspergillus sp.27 as robust producers of industrial laccases followed by cellulases. The phylogenetic analysis of the 18S rDNA sequences successfully identified the screened fungal isolates as Metarhizium granulomatis and Aspergillus niger, respectively. The experimental amplicon size (380 bp) aligns with the previously reported range of 350–800 bp28, which closely approximates the BLAST predicted size of 414 bp. This genetic confirmation establishes these specific strains as viable candidates for further exploration in paper upcycling applications. Furthermore, in order to evaluate the deinking efficiency, the activity of particular enzyme plays a key role for upcycling the used-paper. In this study, M. granulomatis exhibited a significant maximum laccase activity of 196.786 ± 4.781 U/mL (p = 0.02). This yield is significantly higher than the reported laccase activity of 51.84 U/mL25, yet notably lower than 440 U/mL29 demonstrating an intermediate yield compared to previous reports. Meanwhile, A. niger produced a maximum cellulase activity of 8.472 ± 0.795 U/mL (p = 0.01). The attained cellulase activity is comparatively lower than the 25.12 U/mL30 reported for other geographically diverse Aspergillus sp. These quantitative disparities can be primarily attributed to inherent strain variations, differences in culture conditions, and distinct analytical methodologies. Even within closely related taxa, individual environmental isolates possess distinct genetic architectures, and metabolic diversity leading to varying basal levels of extracellular enzyme secretion. To better understand the underlying enzymatic action, it is essential to assess the associated gene motifs and protein moieties. In silico analysis was utilized to predict amplicon sizes provides a prior insight of the primer efficacy. Hence, significantly streamlined primer selection for the study and reducing the need for extensive trial-and-error optimization. Moreover, the experimentally detected amplicon sizes closely aligned with the BLAST prediction. Notably, the β-tubulin gene in M. granulomatis yielded an amplicon of 3000 bp. This result strongly corroborates our in silico BLAST prediction of 2944 bp, despite being substantially larger than the 220 bp31 amplicon previously reported for Cerrena sp. On the other hand, a significant variation in the detected amplicon size were observed for cellulase related genes. The detected Endoglucanase B amplicon (800 bp) deviated from both the literature reported size of 149 bp32 and conversely the BLAST prediction of 2130 bp. Conversely, the Cellobiohydrolase amplicon (2500 bp) aligned more closely with the BLAST prediction (3087 bp) as compared to the previously reported RT-PCR amplicons 86 bp32 and 587 bp33, respectively. A slight shift in the experimental amplicon sizes was analysed as compared to the previously reported literature followed by the BLAST prediction. This discrepancy highlights the inherent genetic diversity among environmental fungal strains. Over evolutionary time, diverse ecological pressure drives the natural accumulation of genetic variations, which typically evident at the molecular level as small genomic insertions or deletions (indels) within the target sequences. Whereas, in case of gene-specific analysis for laccase, the selected genes were not amplified and visualized on agarose gel. This is possibly due to the difference in fungal strains or sequence divergence/natural polymorphisms in the specific isolate that could prevent primer annealing. Consequently, it interprets that there might other gene present in Metarhizium granulomatis responsible for laccase production or the selected genes might be present, but the DNA sequence at the exact spot where the primers bind has altered (polymorphisms). Hence, the primers don’t match perfectly and they fail to bind, resulting in no amplification. Consequently, the current data cannot definitively conclude whether the observed enzymatic activity is driven by novel laccase genes or by divergent homologues of Lac4 and Lac7. Also, it is important to note that the amplified amplicons of Endoglucanase B and Cellobiohydrolase gene were not subjected to downstream DNA sequencing. Consequently, their exact nucleotide identity remains unconfirmed. This represents a limitation of the current genetic analysis, and future sequencing efforts are required to definitively validate the identity of these target genes. SDS-PAGE protein profiling corroborated the enzymatic activities of both isolates. For M. granulomatis, the profile revealed multiple bands of 13, 15, 17, 18, 22, 23, and 27 kDa. Notably, the 18 kDa and 27 kDa bands align precisely with the previously reported laccase subunits from Pleurotus sp.34,35 and Thermus thermophilus36, respectively. Similarly, the cellulase producing A. niger extract displayed bands ranging from 16, 20, 22, 23, 25, 27, and 28 kDa. The observed 22, 23, and 25 kDa bands are highly consistent with cellulase molecular weights reported for A. niger (24 kDa)37 and A. flavus (23 kDa)38, respectively.

The screened fungal isolates were further subjected to upcycling the used-paper to confirm their functional efficacy. The paper upcycling using EDT has been reported by several researcher5,19,23,39,40 using different types of used-paper viz., news print, photocopied paper, pen-ink paper, and mixed-office waste. Adapting slightly modified established protocol40, an enzyme dose of 5 U/g5,20 of dried pulp was utilized, the treated handsheets exhibited an appreciable increases in brightness and deinking efficiency, supported by various studies20,30,40. Alternatively, spectral analysis of the spent medium revealed a characteristic peak between 230 and 260 nm, indicating successful lignin removal and subsequent ink dislodgement. This aligns with the previous reports of lignin associated peaks at 205 nm41, 260 nm12,39, and 280 nm12,39,41, respectively. Furthermore, comparative evaluation demonstrated that enzyme cellulase works more efficiently in terms of brightness, removal of lignin, and dislodgement of ink fragments. While, laccase treatment yielded significantly higher tensile strength (maximum stress required to break a piece of paper) of the handsheet. This highlights the distinct yet complementary role of each enzyme during the upcycling process, leads to a better outcome of the final casted handsheet.

The present study suggests that employing microbial-assisted technology for enzymatic deinking of used-paper to restructure paper upcycling industry can be a breakthrough to tackle with the quality of upcycled paper followed by environmental implications confronted due to conventional (chemical) deinking processes. This strategy would also minimizes energy consumption, uptake of water during processing of paper, carbondioxide emission, and effluent treatment cost. Hence, the microbial-assisted technology applied at lab scale during real-time upcycling of used-paper and the crafted handsheet specifications suggests the potential of technology to be suitablly implemented at industrial scale as a preferred alternative.

Conclusion

The current study emphasizes the significant efficacy of fungal enzymes, specifically laccase and cellulase derived from M. granulomatis and A. niger, respectively in the deinking of used-paper (photocopied/print) under laboratory condition. Consequently, this demonstrates a highly promising and sustainable biological approach to paper upcycling. Despite these favourable enzymatic and macroscopic results, certain limitations regarding the molecular characterization of these isolates were encountered. Notably, the amplification of the Lac4 and Lac7 genes using the selected specific primers was unsuccessful. This lack of amplification represents a methodological limitation, as it may result from high sequence divergence or polymorphisms at the primer-binding sites rather than the true absence of these genes. Additionally, a clear discrepancy was observed between the BLAST predicted amplicon sizes and the experimental PCR products for the cellulase gene. Consequently, the precise nucleotide sequence of this putative cellulase amplicon remains unconfirmed. To definitively identify the genetic determinants responsible for the observed laccase and cellulase production in these isolates, future investigations utilizing degenerate primers or comprehensive whole-genome sequencing approaches are required. These subsequent sequencing efforts will be essential to definitively validate the identity of the target genes. In parallel, the authors are currently planning for pilot-scale industrial trials and socio-economic assessments to formally evaluate the feasibility of microbial-assisted enzymatic deinking technology. These future investigations aim to further ensure the environmental compatibility and sustainable commercial application of these fungal enzymes.

Methods

Isolation, screening, and identification of potent fungi for EDT

All chemicals used in the current investigation were obtained from HiMedia unless stated otherwise. The samples collected from Bhimtal and Naukuchiatal lake around Nainital, Uttarakhand, India (2023) were used to isolate fungi. Traditional agar plating method supplemented with potato dextrose agar (PDA) was employed for isolation purpose42. The plates were kept at 28 °C for 5–7 days and subsequently variety of fungal colonies appeared on the incubated plates, which was further sub-cultured to get a pure plate of each fungal isolate.

Screening for laccase and cellulase producers

Primary screening of potent fungal isolates for enzymatic deinking technology (EDT) was performed using selective agar media. The medium utilized for the screening (primary) of potent fungi for EDT contains PDA supplemented with 0.5% Methyl Orange for laccase and 1% CMC for cellulase, respectively, followed by incubation at 28 °C for 3–5 days43. Laccase-positive isolates were identified via the direct visual appearance of a clear halo zone against the orange background. To visualize cellulase activity, CMC agar plates were flooded with a 1% (w/v) Congo red solution, allowed to stand at room temperature (RT) for 15 min, and subsequently destained with 1 M NaCl44. Cellulolytic activity was indicated by a clear zone surrounding the colonies, which was quantified using an antibiotic zone scale (HiMedia). Fungal isolates exhibiting prominent zones of clearance were selected for secondary screening via submerged fermentation.

During secondary screening, guaiacol method was employed to estimate the activity of laccase enzyme45. Crude enzyme was added to 0.8 mL of acetate buffer (10 mM, pH 5) and 0.1 mL of guaiacol (10 mM) followed by incubation at RT for 15 min, measuring absorbance at 450 nm. To quantify laccase production, guaiacol calibration curve was employed. For laccase, one unit is defined as the amount of enzyme that oxidizes 1 µmol of substrate per minute under specific assay conditions. Likewise, the enzymatic activity of cellulase was assayed using 3,5-Dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) reagent46 by measuring the amount of reducing sugars liberated from CMC. To 0.5 mL of 1% CMC in 0.05 M citrate buffer (pH 8), crude enzyme was added and kept at 50 °C for 30 min. Following incubation, 3 mL of DNS reagent was added to halt the reaction and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. The liberated sugars were estimated by measuring absorbance at 540 nm. For cellulase, one unit is defined as the amount of enzyme that releases 1 µmol of reducing sugar (expressed as glucose equivalents) per minute under specific assay conditions. The calibration curve of glucose was employed to estimate the production of cellulase enzyme.

Identification of potent fungi for EDT

The isolate which shows significant laccase and cellulase enzyme activity was identified based on its morphological characteristics, staining, followed by 18S rDNA sequencing. Chromosomal DNA was extracted and amplified using ITS 1F and ITS 4R primer sequence for 18S gene (Table 1). PCR cycle for 18S gene was carried out initial denaturation at 94 °C for 4 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation, annealing, and extension (94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 1 min); final extension at 72 °C for 4 min followed by hold for infinity at 4°C28. The amplicon was visualized on 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, further subjected to sequencing followed by phylogenetic analysis. The sequence of respective fungal isolate compared against sequences in the NCBI GenBank database at the NCBI BLAST tool (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) to taxonomically characterize the fungal strain33,38 followed by construction of phylogenetic tree using MEGA12 software. The construction of phylogenetic tree was done by maximum likelihood method and bootstrap confidence values were obtained with 1000 resampling. The evolutionary model used in the phylogenetic analysis are Kimura 2-parameter. The culture was further deposited at NCCS, Pune.

Gene-specific analysis and protein profiling

Isolation of genomic DNA and PCR

For gene-specific analysis, genomic DNA was isolated from 7-day old mycelial biomass using a modified phenol–chloroform-isoamyl alcohol extraction method (buffer composition, centrifugation condition, and incubation time)47. Mycelia were homogenized along with small volume of growing culture, subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 1.5 mL cell lysis buffer (10% SDS, 5 M NaCl, 1 M tris–HCl, 0.5 M EDTA), and incubated at 60 °C for 60 min followed by centrifugation. The appeared supernatant was collected safely and mixed with equal volume of phenol, chloroform, and isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1). Following centrifugation, the uppermost aqueous phase was carefully aspirated and mixed with double volume of chilled ethanol and ammonium acetate (100 µL, 4.5 M). Samples were chilled at -20 °C for 1 h, centrifuged, and the resulting DNA pellets were washed, air-dried, and resuspended in 100 µL of TE buffer for storage at -20°C47. Qualitative analysis of DNA was confirmed by 1% agarose-gel electrophoresis. Concentration of DNA was determined by spectrophotometric method.

Further, gene-specific analysis was proceeded to determine the gene responsible for laccase and cellulase enzyme production. Primers were purchased as DNA oligonucleotides from Eurofins Genomics, Bangalore, India. Primarily, the genes selected for gene-specific analysis as IPC; β-tubulin, GAPDH, RPB2, and TEF1 while for laccase; Lac1, Lac2, Lac3, Lac4, Lac5, Lac6, Lac7, and Lac831. Likewise, for cellulase actin gene considered as IPC followed by Endoglucanase-A, Endoglucanase-B, Endoglucanase-C, Cellobiohydrolase, and Exonuclease for gene-specific analysis32. However, the genes finalised for laccase and cellulase enzyme production by performing primer BLAST hit at the screened species, respectively. The complete list of oligonucleotide primers selected for the amplification of the targeted genes is provided in Table 1. The PCR was performed in 25 µL reaction mixture containing 2.5 µL of 10X assay buffer, 1 µL dNTP mix of 2.5 mM, 2.5 µL of MgCl2, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primer, 0.5 µL taq polymerase (3 U/µL), 50 ng of template DNA and rest volume make up with nuclease free water. PCR cycle for IPC gene (β-tubulin) was carried out according to the reported protocol48 with several modifications i.e., initial denaturation at 95 °C for 4 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation, annealing, and extension (95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 2 min and 72 °C for 2 min); final extension at 72 °C for 5 min followed by hold for infinity at 4 °C. Similarly, PCR cycle for laccase gene (Lac4 and Lac7) was carried out initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation, annealing, and extension (94 °C for 1 min, 54 °C for 2 min and 72 °C for 5 min); final extension at 72 °C for 10 min followed by hold for infinity at 4°C48. Likewise, PCR cycle for cellulase gene (Endoglucanase B and Cellobiohydrolase) was carried out initial denaturation at 95 °C for 4 min followed by 40 cycles of denaturation, annealing, and extension (95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 30 s); final extension at 72 °C for 5 min followed by hold for infinity at 4°C33. The presence of amplicons was confirmed on 1% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide and to analyse amplicons size, a reference DNA marker of 1 kb was used (250–10,000 bp, HiMedia).

Isolation of protein and SDS-PAGE

Seven days grown fungal mycelia were used for protein isolation by Mg/CHAP (Cholamidopropyldimethyl ammonio propanesulfonate) modified method (buffer-I composition and centrifugation condition)49. Fungal mycelia were filtered using Whatman No.1 filter paper and transferred into sterilized mortar-pestle with the help of spatula inside laminar air flow chamber. Mycelia were completely thawed using liquid nitrogen. 5 g of mycelia were taken and homogenized in 10 mL of ice-chilled buffer-I (0.5 M tris–HCl, 2% SDS, 20 mM MgCl2, 2% β-mercaptoethanol, pH 8.3) followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The appeared supernatant recovered carefully and mixed with 10 mL of ice-chilled Solution-I (10% trichloroacetic acid in acetone, 0.007% β-mercaptoethanol) followed by centrifugation at similar condition. Finally, pellet was washed twice with 200 µL mixture of ice-chilled acetone containing 0.007% β-mercaptoethanol. After final washing resuspended the pellet in 200 µL phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and kept at -20 °C for further use. Similarly, spore suspension of seven days grown pure fungal plate was used for isolation purpose using similar approach of Mg/CHAP method by scraping the entire plate followed by homogenization and so on. Additionally, salt precipitation method was also used for protein isolation where 20%, 40%, 60%, and 80% saturation were done using ammonium sulphate salt and extracellular enzyme of respective isolates. Fractions were collected after each incubation period (2 h) to proceed further, simultaneously pellet washing done twice using PBS and resuspended in PBS itself for further analysis50. Furthermore, lowry’s method was employed to estimate the concentration of protein using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a protein standard51. The isolated proteins were further analysed on SDS-PAGE52 with several modifications so as to determine the molecular weight of each protein band. Briefly, an electrophoresis apparatus was used to create the SDS-PAGE gel slabs comprising 7% stacking gel and 12% resolving gel. The protein samples were added to sample buffer (0.2 M tris–HCL pH 6.8, 0.05% bromophenol blue, 10% β-mercaptoethanol, 10% SDS, 20% glycerol) and kept at 72 °C for 15 min prior to loading. The entire gel run was maintained with the help of running buffer (0.192 M glycine, 0.25 M tris, 1% SDS) applied with a uniform current of 50 V throughout the gel. Post-run, the resolved bands were observed using the coomassie brilliant blue (CBB) R-250 staining technique followed by destaining. The molecular weight of unknown proteins was determined by making a comparison with standard broad range protein ladder ranging from 10–100 kDa.

Bulk production of opportunistic enzymes and upcycling of used-paper

Bulk production of laccase and cellulase enzyme

This was carried out through the process of submerged fermentation. Inoculation of spore suspension of the respective isolates for laccase and cellulase production was done according to the McFarland standard53. It ensures to maintain the turbidity of bacterial suspension in each production medium once incubated at 28 °C for 6–7 days. Following incubation, crude enzyme was harvested and subjected to enzyme activity determination using guaiacol and DNS assay, for upcycling the used-paper.

Upcycling of used-paper

Photocopied/print paper was used for the upcycling purpose by modified method of enzymatic deinking technology40. The papers were shredded into tiny fragments (~ 1 × 1 inch) and immersed overnight in water (10% w/v) followed by washing and disintegration with grinder to make a smooth pulp slurry. The slurry was further oven drying at 45 °C for 10–15 min after squeezing the excess water and left undisturbed for 24–48 h in order to allow them to dry at room temperature. Pre-treatment were done by taking around 15–20 g of dried pulp and soaked in sodium citrate buffer (50 mM, pH 7) (25% w/v) for 1 h followed by enzymatic deinking treatment. For the enzymatic treatments, either laccase or cellulase was applied independently at a standardized concentration of (5 U/g of dried pulp)20. Thenafter, the reaction was maintained at 160 rpm, 45 °C for 1 h. Consequently, to stop the enzymatic activity pulp slurry was incubated at 80 °C for 15 min20,54. The operating parameters of 5 U/g and 45 °C were selected based on preliminary optimization trials. The tested enzymatic dosage was ranging from 1 U/g to 20 U/g (at intervals of 5 U/g) and temperatures were 45 °C, 50 °C, and 55 °C. The combination of 5 U/g at 45 °C yielded the optimal deinking efficiency. Furthermore, this optimized parameters are consistent with the previously established protocols20,54. The spent medium was collected in a small aliquot for further analysis and the dislodged ink fragments were discarded followed by washing the treated pulp with Tween-20 (0.5% w/v) by raising the temperature at 45 °C for 15 min. The froth was efficiently washed off by subsequent washing with tap water followed by collection of spent medium at final washing. The treated pulp was recovered to make handsheets using casting tray and allowed it to set at RT for overnight.

The experimental set I and II of EDT (laccase and cellulase) was created, and to draw comparison another set were allocated as commercial cellulase and conventional chemical treatment, respectively. Experimental set III comprises of treatment with commercial cellulase (Sigma-Alrich), similar approach was employed for upcycling of used-paper. Alternatively, the experimental set IV was allocated for the chemical processing, usually it involves deinking of pulp with sodium silicate (2%), and sodium hydroxide (1.5%) at 40 °C for 20 min, subsequently washed with Tween-20 (1%) and so on20. Experimental set V referred as the control set (untreated inked pulp) that was processed concurrently under similar conditions to draw comparison in the processed pulp of all the sets. All the experimental processed pulp was subjected to optical/physical analysis to evaluate enzyme efficacy.

The optical/physical properties of processed handsheets was further analysed according to standard TAPPI method for pulp brightness (TAPPI T452 “Brightness of pulp, paper, and paperboard, directional reflectance at 457 nm), tensile strength (TAPPI T494 “Tensile breaking strength and elongation of paper and paperboard”), and deinking efficiency30 which was computed using a formula:

The term BF represents the brightness of a standard laboratory crafted handsheet prepared from the pulp after the complete deinking treatment (e.g., laccase/cellulase treatment) and subsequent flotation/washing steps. This represents the achieved experimental result. Hence, the term BP represents the brightness of a handsheet prepared from the used-paper pulp before any deinking or flotation treatment. Because the ink is fully dispersed throughout the pulp matrix at this stage, represents the lowest brightness value. The term BB represents the brightness of the original paper fibers or unprinted margins of the used-paper, devoid of ink fragments. Consequently, pulped under the exact conditions as the experimental samples. This represents the theoretical maximum brightness (100% deinking efficiency) that the process could possibly achieve if every single ink particle were removed without damaging the fibers.

Characterization of spent medium

The spent medium generated post enzymatic and chemical deinking method was collected and stored until further analysis. The removal of lignin and ejection of the ink fragments were analysed under UV–Vis range (200–800 nm)39.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out in triplicates independently and experimental results were represented as the mean ± standard deviation of three identical values. The significance of the difference between each test variable were determined by one way ANOVA analysis computed using GraphPad Prism 9 software.
