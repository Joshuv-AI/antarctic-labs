 CalderaCast User Manual
 Version 1.0
 1. INTRODUCTION
 1.1. Purpose
 CalderaCast is a user-friendly web-based tool for electrical-load forecast, providing stakeholders with
a fully customizable decision-support framework that estimates the likely power draw from a possible
future electric-vehicle (EV) charging station at a given location on a given day along an alternative fuel
corridor (AFC). These EV charging profiles are accurately modeled in CalderaCast using the Caldera
software framework developed by Idaho National Laboratories (INL), reflecting the realistic charging
levels observed in actual charge events.
 This tool was developed as part of the National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure (NEVI) program,
which is quickly generating substantial interest from would-be charging station operators (CSO), large
and small electric utilities, and state transportation planners, some of whom had not seriously considered
EV charging previously. All these entities—with or without background in EV infrastructure—must
estimate the electricity load that a proposed charging station will generate. This load forecast is critically
important for a utility to properly assess the capacity of their distribution network to support the proposed
station or properly size grid upgrades for potential load growth due to future EV adoption, vehicle
technology improvements, or station growth.
 1.2. Systems Requirements
 This software can be run on any web browser. The tool will run on Windows, Mac, or Linux
computers as well as phones or tablets that use Apple or Android operating systems.

 Figure 2 presents the flow of navigation to results. Users first input the location of the candidate
station and date when load is to be forecast. They then acknowledge or modify assumptions made for the
simulation, and then design the envisioned EV station before finally observing results. It is impossible for
users to skip steps. Once results for the initial run are obtained, users can go back to any of the previous
steps to update inputs for subsequent runs, running as many simulations as desired. The various steps are
explained in greater detail in the following sections.

 2.1. Date and Location Tab
 The purpose of this tab is to enter a date and a location where the candidate station is envisioned for
load estimation. Figure 4 displays the screen that users see on the Date and Location tab. Users enter a
date by clicking on a calendar pop-up. Dates range from 2020 to 2040.
 There are three possible ways to enter a location: (1) enter an address, (2) enter a latitude and
longitude, or (3) drop a pin on the Google map. For the first two options, the map will automatically
update and take users to the selected location. For the third option, users can navigate through the map by
clicking and dragging the map to a location of choice. Once the pin is dropped, the latitude and longitude
will automatically populate in the latitude and longitude bars. For a dropped pin, only the latitude and
longitude are populated, not the full address.
 Examples of locations for a candidate station include supermarkets, gas stations, hotels, recreation
centers, churches, and more. Figure 5 displays the example of the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) in
Washington D.C. In this case, both address and latitude/longitude are populated.
 The maps in Figure 4 and Figure 5 also display buffers, highlighted in blue, along AFCs. These
buffers extend 5-miles on either side of the corridor, defining where the tool's predictions are most
accurate.
 Once the location and dates have been selected, users can click on the Next: Assumptions button at
the bottom of the page to move to the next tab.

 2.2. Assumptions Tab
 This tab familiarizes users with assumptions made in the simulation. This is critical because
simulation results derive meaning from the specific assumptions made. Figure 6 through Figure 9 show
the different assumptions made to provide load-forecast results. These assumptions are made for traffic
estimation, EV-adoption rate, EV fleet, and random number generation (RNG).
 At the top of Figure 6, users are presented with traffic-estimation assumptions. CalderaCast leverages
traffic data captured by Travel Monitoring Analysis System (TMAS) stations, which are used to estimate
the traffic volume at the location selected in the previous step. More specifically, traffic volume at the
selected location is assumed to be the same as the closest TMAS station. Users can see the location of the
nearest TMAS station, as well as how far the TMAS station is from the selected location.
 For this version, the adoption rate varies by state and is obtained from the National Renewable
Energy Laboratory's (NREL’s) Tempo tool. Figure 6 shows the Assumptions page, where users are
presented with the expected trend of EV adoption for the selected location in the U.S., from 2020 to 2040.
The value of the rate displayed corresponds to the year of the date entered at the location provided on the
previous page. In this example, the location shows Virginia as the state, so the EV adoption rate
corresponding to that state is selected. Users are also given the opportunity to modify the rate by using the
slider, or clicking on the (+) or (-) signs.

Figure 6. EV adoption rate assumption section.

 Figure 7 displays the warning shown to users when the candidate station is located outside of the
buffer (See Section 2.1). Because the TMAS stations are used to estimate nearby traffic volume, the
farther the selection is from the corridor, the less accurate the estimation will be.

 Figure 8 displays a table with generalized EV types and fleet proportions considered in this tool.
Battery size, range, efficiency, C-rate, and peak voltage are shown for each generalized vehicle model.
The fleet vehicle-type proportions are determined based on data obtained from the National Renewable
Energy Laboratory (NREL) Tempo tool, and are not user-editable. The load forecast is modeled using
data specific to these vehicle types. In the section below the EV information table, users are provided with

given hour of a day (i.e., the day entered on the previous page). A summary of input data, including
location selected and EV adoption rate, is displayed as is the data (i.e., traffic volume and EV station)
used to build this profile.

Figure 8. EV fleet assumption section.

 The bottom of the Assumptions tab (Figure 9) provides a brief explanation on the use of RNG in our
results. Users can click on the, “What is a random number generator?” link to prompt a dropdown that
provides a quick overview of RNG. By leaving the Use random seed checkmark checked, the RNG seed
is randomly changed on each subsequent run, yielding different plausible outcomes each time the
simulation is run. This is to capture randomness, allowing users to see different plausible real-life
outcomes in load forecasts. However, if users wish to repeat the same simulation each run, users can
uncheck the Use random seed checkmark and enter any value between 0 and 999999999 to specify the
seed. This ensures reproducibility. If, on subsequent runs, the same seed is used, the same sequence of
pseudorandom numbers will be generated, and the same outputs, obtained. The random seed used for each
simulation is listed on the Results tab. The user may input the seed reported on the Results tab to
reproduce the results of the simulation on a subsequent run. More information about pseudorandom
numbers can be found here.

 2.3. Station Design Tab
 This tab defines the type and power level of each charging port that will be modeled at the proposed
EV station at the selected location. The charger types that the user may consider are displayed in
Figure 10, which include direct-current fast charge (DCFC) and Level 2 (L2) chargers, with various levels
of power output. Users can build the station of their choice by selecting the number of ports of each
charger type that will be available. A minimum of one port is required for the model to run.
 For this version of the software, power sharing is not taken into account. Instead, each single car at
any point during its charge session is assumed to use the full power level defined by its power profile
curve, as determined by the Caldera software framework. Users can click on the What is power sharing?
link, which prompts a dropdown that shares a quick overview of this notion. The table below provides the
power details of each charger, with maximum power, current, and voltage.
 Once satisfied, users can click on the Run Simulation button to navigate to the next tab.

 2.4. Results Tab
 The purpose of this tab is to display the results of the simulation. Figure 11 displays the upper part of
the Results section. This portion displays (1) the summary of all inputs from the user entered in previous
pages, including date (day, month, and year), location for the candidate station (latitude and longitude),
station design (maximum power and amperage per port, number of ports), EV adoption rate selected,
RNG seed; and (2) the summary of simulation outputs, including total energy drawn on the selected day,
number of charging events and number of cars turned away. Users can click on the “What is a vehicle
turned away?” link to reveal a dropdown to get a description of this notion.

 Once a run is completed, users can perform other runs by clicking on the Rerun Simulation button. In
this case, users will obtain different results based on the same input due to the default setting of using a
different RNG seed each run. Otherwise, users will need to copy the RNG seed number seen in Figure 10,
and paste it in the corresponding box in the Assumptions tab (Figure 8), before running the simulations
again. This allows users to run a simulation with the same inputs and reproduce the same results.
 Users can choose to make changes in the Station Design and/or Assumptions sections simply by
clicking on the corresponding tabs. All subsequent tabs will be greyed out, requiring the user to proceed
to the next steps via the buttons at the bottom of the page. However, users can still click on the Rerun
Simulation button to run another simulation after changes are made (Figure 14). Exception applies to
changing the date and/or location. If either the date or location is changed, users will be prompted to
review assumptions and station design before running the scenario again, even if they click on the Rerun
button. Users will be taken to the Assumptions page and will see a note: The date and/or location has
changed. Please review the assumptions settings before proceeding (Figure 15).

 3. USER STORIES
 Not being able to accurately predict EV charging load is a significant concern for grid planning
because it can lead to unreliable grid operations, inadequate infrastructure, increased costs, and reduced
grid resilience. While other types of loads, such as apartment buildings or factories, are known quantities
with which grid operators are familiar, the grid-load characteristics of EV charging stations are not
commonly understood and may represent increased risk to the grid operator. By providing charging-load
forecasts, CalderaCast responds to this critical need, ensuring a more-efficient and resilient grid. In this
section, several use cases are presented, highlighting the practical importance and relevance of
CalderaCast.
Use Case 1: Utility Operators
 What is the utility operator doing?
• Deciding whether to approve an interconnection request for service to power an EV charging station.
• Determining whether existing infrastructure meets the needs of the new load or new infrastructure
 will be needed.
 Why does the operator need CalderaCast?
• CalderaCast provides estimates for the expected power profile of the planned EV charging station,
 which will provide insight into the hardware that will be necessary to support the station.
• CalderaCast can help the utility ensure that the planned infrastructure will be able to support the
 expected power load of the EV charging station, while also minimizing cost.

 13

Use Case 2: State Agencies
 What is a state agency doing?
• Reviewing applications from entities applying for state or federal funding for the installation of fast
 DC charging stations.
• Providing a set of suggested locations to applicants where EV charging stations may be most needed.
 Why do they need CalderaCast?
• CalderaCast can help state agencies in infrastructure planning, deciding where to deploy charging
 infrastructure, and helping prioritize state/federal applications for proposed EV charging locations.
• CalderaCast can help state agencies understand grid impact assessment, estimate load growth due to
 EV adoption, and enable coordination with utility commissions for grid upgrades.
Use Case 3: Businesses (such as restaurants, movie theaters, existing gas
stations, property development, etc.)
 What is the business doing?
• Taking advantage of a potential business opportunity by installing EV charging stations adjacent to
 their business to attract customers or directly profit from EV charging.
• Assessing cost and viability of installing EV charging equipment adjacent to businesses.
 Why do they need CalderaCast?
• CalderaCast can help anticipate EV charging or energy demand and coordinate with utilities for grid
 capacity and equipment requirements
• CalderaCast can help justify investment by answering such questions such as:
 - What is the demand for a charging station, given the traffic on a nearby highway?
 - Which electric vehicle supply equipment (EVSE) types and how many ports would be most
 ideal?
Other possible use cases:
• Charging Networks: While larger and more-established charging networks may have access to their
 own private data and simulation tools, other smaller charging networks may not yet have this
 capability. These smaller charging networks may be seeking to expand their networks by finding the
 ideal locations to install new charging stations and determining the correct port-count and EVSE
 speed that maximizes utility and minimizes cost. For these charging networks, CalderaCast would be
 a useful tool to accomplish these tasks.
• EVSE Manufacturers: Many companies (such as ChargePoint among others) manufacture EVSE
 equipment that other private entities purchase and privately operate. These companies may have an
 interest in using CalderaCast to scope possible use-cases for the hardware they sell.
• EV Manufacturers: EV manufacturers (e.g., GM, Ford, Rivian) may want to investigate holes in the
 current charging network and explore the possibility of new stations in new places for their
 customers’ driving needs. CalderaCast may help them understand what kind of new stations would
 benefit their customers’ driving habits to overcome a lack of EV charging infrastructure in some
 areas.
