Machine learning-guided screening and validation of antioxidant small molecules from Clausena lansium
Authors: Yufan Tong, Min Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo discover natural antioxidants from Clausena lansium (Lour.) Skeels for food applications, we established a 474-compound database and applied a multiscale workflow integrating ensemble machine learning, molecular docking, structural clustering, molecular dynamics simulations, and quantum chemical calculations. The ensemble models achieved ROC-AUC values of 0.85–0.99 across eight antioxidant assays. Multi-criteria screening yielded 47 candidates, and 30 structurally diverse candidates underwent molecular dynamics simulations. Quercetin 3-arabinoside was prioritized, exhibiting stable non-covalent binding within the Keap1 Kelch domain during a 500 ns simulation. Quantum chemical calculations showed a HOMO–LUMO gap of 4.0589 eV, 25.5% lower than that of vitamin C. Given standard availability, its structural isomer Avicularin was assessed and exhibited dose-dependent antioxidant activity, with DPPH and ABTS IC50 values of 26.84 and 6.94 μM, respectively, and a FRAP value of 1.327 ± 0.218 mmol FeSO4 equivalents L−1 at 25 μM. This study supports antioxidant discovery from edible fruits.Keywords: Clausena lansium, Antioxidant, Machine learning, Molecular dynamics simulations, Quantum chemical calculations

Introduction

Antioxidants scavenge free radicals by donating hydrogen or electrons, chelating metal ions, or terminating free radical chain reactions, thereby slowing oxidative deterioration in food systems and maintaining food quality, nutritional value, and storage stability (Huang et al., 2005). In addition, oxidative stress is associated with various chronic diseases (Birben et al., 2012), and the search for safe and effective antioxidants remains a focus of ongoing research. Currently, widely used synthetic antioxidants include butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA) (Pandey & Kumar, 2021), butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), and tert-butylhydroquinone (TBHQ) (Carocho & Ferreira, 2013). However, recent studies have shown that these antioxidants may cause DNA damage and genotoxic effects, raising concerns about their safety (Eskandani et al., 2014, Pandey and Kumar, 2021). In contrast, natural antioxidants derived from fruits offer advantages such as low toxicity and easy availability (Carocho & Ferreira, 2013). In addition, the antioxidant activity of plant-derived natural products can be readily assessed using complementary in vitro chemical assays, including radical-scavenging and reducing-power assays (Acquaviva et al., 2023, Kurt-Celep et al., 2023, Zengin et al., 2021). Consequently, antioxidant research is gradually shifting from synthetic antioxidants toward safer natural sources.

Clausena lansium (Lour.) Skeels is a plant of significant medicinal and nutritional value (Huang et al., 2023). Its flesh, seeds, peel, stems, and leaves are rich in various bioactive compounds (Peng et al., 2019, Zhang et al., 2024), including flavonoids, coumarins, and alkaloids, which exhibit considerable potential for development as natural antioxidants (Chang et al., 2022). However, existing research has primarily focused on isolated compounds or small-molecule constituents derived from specific parts of the plant (Xu et al., 2014), while its comprehensive small-molecule chemical profile has not yet been systematically characterized. Furthermore, systematic screening and investigations of molecular mechanisms regarding the antioxidant activity of the bioactive components of Clausena lansium remain limited. In particular, systematic high-throughput screening of these constituents is still lacking. Consequently, these limitations have hindered the comprehensive development and utilization of Clausena lansium as a source of natural antioxidants.

The Keap1 (Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1)-Nrf2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) pathway is a key regulatory axis in the oxidative stress response (Bellezza et al., 2018), in which the Kelch domain of Keap1 mediates the recognition and binding of Nrf2 (Lo et al., 2006). Previous studies have shown that certain natural polyphenolic or flavonoid compounds can bind to the Keap1 Kelch domain via non-covalent interactions (Culletta et al., 2024), suggesting a potential antioxidant regulatory role at the structural level. Therefore, the Keap1 Kelch domain can serve as a structural target to assist in evaluating the binding potential of natural antioxidant candidate molecules. However, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations can only provide inferences at the structural and mechanistic levels. Antioxidant activity still requires validation through chemical antioxidant assays.

Traditional screening of bioactive small molecules typically relies on extensive in vitro experimentation and therefore requires considerable time. In recent years, machine learning, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations have been used to improve the efficiency of screening bioactive constituents from natural products. In addition, quantum chemical calculations can characterize the electronic reactivity of candidate molecules and provide theoretical insight into their potential electron transfer behavior (Farzandi et al., 2026). Together, these methods provide complementary information at the levels of activity prediction, binding characteristics, dynamic stability and electronic reactivity (Feizi-Dehnayebi et al., 2026). Therefore, the integration of these complementary methods into a unified workflow may enable more systematic screening of natural antioxidant candidates.

Accordingly, this study first established a database containing 474 small molecules from Clausena lansium and used multiple machine learning models to predict the antioxidant activity of the compounds in the database through ensemble modeling based on experimental data from various antioxidant assays. Subsequently, two molecular docking programs with different scoring functions were used to evaluate the binding potential of the candidate molecules. The machine learning predictions and the results from both docking programs were then integrated, followed by structural clustering and comprehensive ranking. Molecular dynamics simulations were then conducted in successive stages on the candidate complexes to identify priority candidates, followed by quantum chemical calculations on the selected candidates. Finally, the in vitro chemical antioxidant activity of representative candidate structures was evaluated using DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP assays. This study integrates activity prediction, structural screening, preservation of structural diversity, evaluation of dynamic stability, and analysis of electronic reactivity into a unified hierarchical screening workflow, and links this workflow with in vitro chemical antioxidant assays to provide complementary computational and experimental evidence. This study provides a systematic strategy for the rapid screening and further development of natural antioxidants from food sources.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Avicularin was purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE; Cat. No. HY-N0223; CAS No. 572-30-5). Commercial assay kits for DPPH radical-scavenging activity and ABTS total antioxidant capacity were obtained from Solarbio (China) and Beyotime Biotechnology (China), respectively. The FRAP total antioxidant capacity assay kit (BTK053) was obtained from Bio-Swamp (China).

Construction of the Clausena lansium small-molecule database

To construct a database of bioactive small molecules derived from Clausena lansium (Lour.) Skeels, an extensive literature search was conducted across major databases, including PubMed, ScienceDirect, and CNKI, to identify studies detailing its chemical constituents. Chemical constituents reported from diverse parts of the plant — including the fruits, seeds, peels, stems, and leaves — were manually extracted. Subsequently, the SMILES representations of the relevant compounds were retrieved from the PubChem database (Kim et al., 2019) and organized to construct a preliminary small-molecule database. Furthermore, RDKit 2022.09.5 (Landrum et al., 2023) was used to standardize molecular structures and remove duplicate entries, ultimately yielding a refined database of small-molecule constituents to serve as a foundation for subsequent bioactivity prediction and virtual screening.

Construction of an antioxidant small-molecule database

The training data for antioxidant activity were acquired from the publicly accessible repository https://github.com/idrugLab/AOP (Zhao et al., 2024). The datasets provide predefined binary labels (active or inactive) based on internal curation schemes applied to raw experimental analyses. These predefined labels were used directly, without introducing additional manual thresholds. The training set included data from eight standard antioxidant assays. These specifically include 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging, 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) radical scavenging, superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activity, metal chelating ability (MCA), ferric reducing antioxidant capacity (FRAP), hydroxyl radical scavenging activity (HRSA), nitric oxide scavenging activity (NOSA), and oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC). The detailed dataset composition is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Data from these diverse in vitro antioxidant assays were used to construct an antioxidant activity prediction model, which was subsequently used to characterize the overall antioxidant potential of small molecules.

Machine learning for antioxidant activity prediction

To construct a predictive model for antioxidant activity, a multi-model ensemble framework based on ligand prediction was employed to evaluate candidate small molecules. This framework comprises three traditional machine learning models, including multilayer perceptrons (MLP) (Rumelhart et al., 1986), random forests (RF) (Breiman, 2001), and support vector machines (SVM) (Cortes & Vapnik, 1995), alongside two deep learning models: the graph neural network (WeaveGNN) (Kearnes et al., 2016) and the Knowledge-Guided Pre-training of Graph Transformer (KPGT) (Li et al., 2022). The five models were independently evaluated using five-fold cross-validation on eight antioxidant activity datasets, outputting the predicted probabilities for each molecule. Regarding model implementation, MLP, WeaveGNN, and KPGT were implemented using PyTorch 2.1 (Paszke et al., 2019), whereas RF and SVM were implemented utilizing scikit-learn 1.3 (Pedregosa et al., 2011). Due to architectural differences in models, the required input features varied. The traditional machine learning models extracted molecular structural features via RDKit, employing Morgan fingerprints (Rogers & Hahn, 2010) (radius = 2, nBits = 1024) as inputs. Conversely, WeaveGNN and KPGT used molecular graph structures, representing atoms as nodes and chemical bonds as edges.

For each category of antioxidant assays, the five binary classification models were trained independently and evaluated via five-fold cross-validation, with performance primarily characterized by the ROC-AUC metric. After model training was completed, the prediction scores for each ligand within the Clausena lansium small-molecule chemical database were recorded across all five models, serving as a crucial foundation for subsequent screening and comparison.

Molecular docking and multi-criteria screening

To investigate the interactions between candidate small molecules and Keap1, molecular docking analyses were conducted utilizing the crystal structure of Keap1. The Keap1 protein crystal structure (PDB ID: 4IQK) (Marcotte et al., 2013) and the corresponding sequence data were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) database (Berman et al., 2000). The docking pocket was identified and defined utilizing the Getbox plugin within PyMOL 3.1 (Schrödinger, LLC, 2025).

Considering the inherent variations in scoring functions and search strategies among docking programs (Huang et al., 2010), two docking methods, AutoDock Vina 1.2.7 (Eberhardt et al., 2021) and the LibDock module in BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2021 (Dassault Systèmes BIOVIA, 2021, Rao et al., 2007), were used to evaluate ligands from the Clausena lansium small-molecule database. For AutoDock Vina, 10 independent molecular docking runs were performed for each ligand under identical parameter settings. The median binding affinity from the 10 runs was defined as Vinamed. For LibDock, a single molecular docking run was performed for each ligand, and the maximum score was extracted. To validate both docking protocols, the cocrystallized ligand was extracted from the Keap1 crystal structure and redocked into the original binding pocket using the same protein preparation and docking settings as those used for virtual screening. The pose superposition and RMSD (root-mean-square deviation) calculations were performed using PyMOL 3.1.

Subsequently, based on the distribution characteristics of LBPavg, Vinamed, and LibDock scores across the entire Clausena lansium small-molecule library, a strategy integrating intra-library percentiles with empirical cutoffs was applied to establish the screening thresholds. Only ligands that simultaneously satisfied the preset thresholds for all three metrics were incorporated into the final candidate set. The aforementioned docking binding affinities and scoring results described served as critical reference metrics for subsequent comprehensive screening and comparative analyses.

Structural clustering and diversity selection

To mitigate structural redundancy and preserve chemical diversity, the screened candidate molecules were initially ranked according to a priority order: predominantly by descending LBPavg, followed by ascending Vinamed, and supplemented by descending LibDock scores. Subsequently, the SMILES of the candidate molecules were canonicalized, and structural clustering was executed using a leader-based greedy clustering algorithm. Specifically, the Morgan fingerprint (Rogers & Hahn, 2010) (radius = 2, 2048 bits, useChirality = False) was employed to characterize the molecular structures, while Tanimoto similarity was used as the metric for intermolecular similarity (Bajusz et al., 2015), applying a threshold of Tc=0.7. The molecules were traversed in the aforementioned order, with similarity comparisons conducted exclusively against the representative molecule of each structural cluster. If the similarity was ≥Tc, the molecule was assigned to the corresponding structural cluster; otherwise, a novel structural cluster was established. Upon the completion of clustering, the priority order of each structural cluster was determined. Candidate molecules within each cluster were then selected according to their priority, with retention restricted to a maximum of two molecules per cluster. Ultimately, the top 30 candidate molecules were compiled using a round-robin selection approach for subsequent molecular dynamics simulations and quantum chemical calculations.

Molecular dynamics simulations

All molecular dynamics simulations in this study were performed using the AMBER24 software package (Case et al., 2023). The simulations were divided into four stages, employing a stepwise screening strategy for long-term analysis. The first stage involved simulations of the unbound Keap1 system for a duration of 500 ns to evaluate the conformational stability and intrinsic kinetic behavior of the protein under ligand-free conditions. Stages 2 through 4 comprised molecular dynamics screening simulations of complexes formed between Keap1 and small-molecule ligands derived from Clausena lansium. In this study, the 20 ns stage was used for rapid pre-screening, the 100 ns stage involved combined screening based on conformational stability and hydrogen-bond persistence, and the 500 ns stage further confirmed the final stable binding complexes through long-term trajectory stability and the position of the center of mass (COM) at the end of the simulation. The detailed kinetic and hydrogen-bond screening criteria used at each MD stage are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. For complexes satisfying the aforementioned stability criteria, the final 50 ns of the 500 ns simulation trajectory was selected as the analysis window. The distance between the COM of the ligand and the binding pocket was calculated to assess the sustained occupancy within the binding site. The binding was considered stable, and the complex was subsequently used for binding free energy calculations only when the COM distance showed no sustained increase or detachment trend. Subsequently, the representative complex was subjected to principal component analysis (PCA) based on protein Cα atoms to characterize the major collective motions of the protein during the simulation. Trajectories were projected onto the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), and a free energy landscape (FEL) was constructed based on PC1 and PC2 to characterize the conformational distribution and energy features of the system in the stable binding state.

A unified force field configuration was employed for all systems, using the ff19SB force field (Tian et al., 2020) for the protein and the GAFF2 force field (Wang et al., 2004) for the ligand. The partial atomic charges for each ligand were calculated using the AM1-BCC method (Jakalian et al., 2002), and the solvent was modeled using the TIP3P water model (Jorgensen et al., 1983). Prior to the production simulations, each system underwent a 6000-step energy minimization (the first 3000 steps using the steepest descent method and the last 3000 steps using the conjugate gradient method) to ensure stability of the system. Subsequently, the system was heated under NVT conditions, with the temperature gradually increased from 0 K to 310 K and maintained at a constant level using a Langevin thermostat (Loncharich et al., 1992). Equilibrium and production simulations were subsequently performed under NPT conditions, with the pressure maintained at 1 atm using a Monte Carlo barostat (Åqvist et al., 2004), and the volume of the system allowed to vary freely to achieve complete equilibrium. For the complex systems, the simulation durations for the production phases were 20 ns, 100 ns, and 500 ns, using a time step of 2 fs. Trajectories were recorded every 0.1 ns during the simulations. Throughout the simulations, long-range electrostatic interactions were computed using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method (Darden et al., 1993), with the cutoff distance set to 8 Å. Trajectory analysis was performed using the CPPTRAJ module (Roe & Cheatham, 2013) within AMBER to calculate the following metrics: RMSD, Rg (radius of gyration), SASA (solvent-accessible surface area), RMSF (root-mean-square fluctuation), PCA, and FEL. The binding free energies of the complexes were calculated using the MM-PBSA method, while the MM-GBSA method was employed for further analysis regarding the energy contributions of individual residues (Kollman et al., 2000, Miller et al., 2012).

Quantum chemical calculations

Quantum chemical calculations were performed for Quercetin 3-arabinoside, Avicularin, and L-ascorbic acid (vitamin C) using the Gaussian 16 software package (Frisch et al., 2016). Geometry optimizations and frequency calculations for all three molecules were conducted at the B3LYP/6-311G(d,p) level (Becke, 1993, Krishnan et al., 1980), and solvent effects within the aqueous environment were simulated using the Polarizable Continuum Model (PCM) (Tomasi et al., 2005). Vitamin C was selected as a reference compound for comparison of the HOMO and LUMO energies and the corresponding energy gap. Subsequently, the energy levels of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) were extracted from the Gaussian output files using the Multiwfn program (Lu & Chen, 2012). The spatial distribution of these molecular orbitals was visualized using GaussView 6 (Dennington et al., 2016), with the orbital isosurface set to 0.02 a.u. Molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) calculations were performed for Quercetin 3-arabinoside and Avicularin using Gaussian 16 under the same computational conditions (Hemmati et al., 2025, Suresh and Anila, 2023). The electrostatic potential was mapped onto the total electron density surface and visualized using GaussView 6. To facilitate direct visual comparison, the same electrostatic potential range from −0.123 to +0.123 a.u. and an electron density isovalue of 0.001 a.u. were applied to both molecules. Global reactivity descriptors were calculated for Quercetin 3-arabinoside and Avicularin to further compare their electronic reactivity, including absolute electronegativity (χ), global hardness (η), chemical potential (μ), electrophilicity index (ω), global softness (σ), and the maximum electronic charge transfer (ΔNmax). Statistical analysis

All chemical antioxidant assays were conducted in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Concentration–scavenging rate curves for the DPPH and ABTS assays were fitted using a 4PL regression model, and IC50 values were calculated from the fitted curves. For the FRAP assay, FeSO4 standard curves were generated by linear regression, and FRAP values were calculated from the corresponding regression equation. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 11.0.0.

Results and discussion

Construction of a chemical composition database for Clausena lansium

Through literature searches and database compilation, 600 small-molecule compounds derived from various anatomical parts of Clausena lansium (flesh, seeds, peel, stems, and leaves) were identified, and their corresponding SMILES representations were retrieved. Following structural standardization utilizing RDKit, molecules that could not be parsed and duplicate structures were removed, yielding a final set of 474 unique small molecules.

Machine learning prediction of antioxidant activity

Across the eight antioxidant activity datasets, all five machine learning models demonstrated strong predictive performance, with ROC-AUC values ranging from 0.85 to 0.99 under five-fold cross-validation (Fig. 1). These results indicated that these models exhibited strong capabilities for ranking candidate molecules and effectively distinguishing between active and inactive molecules.

Performance evaluation of five machine learning models across eight antioxidant activity datasets. The heatmap summarizes Recall, TN-specificity, Precision, Accuracy, F1-score, MCC, and ROC-AUC under five-fold cross-validation.

However, the ROC-AUC metric primarily reflects the overall discrimination ability of a model across various classification thresholds and is insufficient to fully represent the stability of binary classification at a fixed threshold. Therefore, this study incorporated additional metrics, including the MCC, F1-score, Recall, and Specificity to conduct a more comprehensive evaluation of the classification performance of the various models across the diverse antioxidant datasets (Chicco & Jurman, 2020). For class-imbalanced datasets such as NOSA and ORAC, the majority of the models maintained high F1-score and MCC, demonstrating relatively stable classification performance. However, instances where the MCC falls below 0.60 were observed in specific datasets (DPPH, FRAP, and SOD), all of which occurred exclusively in the WeaveGNN model. The evaluation results suggested that the classification stability of an individual model is not entirely consistent across different antioxidant tasks. Therefore, relying exclusively on the prediction results of a single model may introduce biases inherent to the architectural framework of the model.

Following model validation, the antioxidant activity of 474 small molecules from Clausena lansium was predicted. The results indicated that the predictive trends of the five models were generally consistent for the majority of small molecules, although slight discrepancies in the predictions were observed for specific compounds. Based on the prediction results of the five models, the average predicted score (LBPavg) was calculated for each molecule and used as an input metric for subsequent virtual screening and clustering analysis. This strategy mitigates the impact of biases originating from individual models or datasets on the final screening results and provides a more robust foundation for subsequent structural clustering, molecular dynamics simulations, quantum chemical calculations, and chemical antioxidant validation.

Molecular docking and multi-criteria screening

Using the crystal structure of the Keap1 Kelch domain, molecular docking was performed on 474 small molecules. Since scores from a single machine learning model or a single molecular docking algorithm may be susceptible to inherent biases, this study employed molecular docking methods based on different principles to evaluate the capacity of small molecules to form favorable binding interactions with the Keap1 Kelch domain. AutoDock Vina relies on binding free energy approximations, whereas LibDock relies on scoring based on hotspots and conformational matching (Diller & Merz, 2001). Redocking reproduced the crystallographic ligand pose with RMSD values of 1.974 Å for AutoDock Vina and 1.551 Å for LibDock. Both values were below the commonly used threshold of 2 Å, indicating acceptable agreement between the redocked and crystallographic poses for both docking protocols (Ramírez & Caballero, 2018). The superpositions of the crystallographic and redocked ligand poses are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. The results from 10 independent AutoDock Vina docking runs indicated an average Vinamed value of approximately −6.5 kcal/mol, while the average LibDock score was approximately 78.8.

Based on the distributions of LBPavg, Vinamed, and LibDock scores (Fig. 2), the screening thresholds were set as LBPavg ≥ 0.470 (P70; Fig. 2a), LibDock ≥ 83.119 (P60; Fig. 2b), and Vinamed ≤ −7.0 kcal/mol (Fig. 2c). The LBPavg and LibDock thresholds were determined using intra-library percentiles, whereas the Vinamed threshold was selected based on its score distribution and previously reported empirical docking cutoffs (Zhang et al., 2021).

Distribution of the three screening metrics in the Clausena lansium small-molecule library. (a) Distribution of LBPavg scores and the P70 threshold; (b) distribution of LibDock scores and the P60 threshold; (c) distribution of Vinamed values and the empirical cutoff of −7.0 kcal/mol.

Under the combined screening criteria of the three metrics, 47 candidate small molecules were ultimately identified, accounting for 9.9% of the entire molecular library. Subsequently, the correlations among the three metrics were analyzed (Fig. 3). The correlation coefficients between LBPavg and LibDock were Pearson r=0.04 and Spearman ρ=0.07 (p=0.804) (Fig. 3a). Furthermore, the correlation coefficients between LBPavg and Vinamed were Pearson r=−0.32 and Spearman ρ=−0.33 (p=0.026) (Fig. 3b), whereas those between LibDock and Vinamed were Pearson r=0.09 and Spearman ρ=0.04 (p=0.568) (Fig. 3c). This indicated that the three metrics did not provide redundant information; rather, they described the properties of the candidate molecules from different perspectives, including antioxidant activity prediction, binding free energy approximation, and conformational matching. Consequently, a combined screening approach using these three metrics mitigates the bias introduced by a single model or a single docking algorithm. Notably, the screening thresholds for LBPavg, LibDock, and Vinamed were primarily used to progressively refine the candidate pool within the small-molecule library in this study, rather than to define absolute boundaries for Keap1 binding affinity.

Correlation analysis among LBPavg, LibDock, and Vinamed for the screened candidate molecules. (a) LBPavg versus LibDock; (b) LBPavg versus Vinamed; (c) LibDock versus Vinamed.

Structural clustering and diversity assessment

To mitigate structural redundancy and maximize chemical diversity, the 47 molecules identified through multi-criteria screening were subjected to cluster analysis using Morgan fingerprints (radius = 2, 2048 bits) and Tanimoto similarity (Tc=0.7). The analysis showed that these 47 molecules were classified into 42 structural clusters, of which 37 had a size of 1 and only 5 had a size of 2, with an average cluster size of 1.12 (Fig. 4). These results indicated minimal structural redundancy and excellent chemical diversity among the candidate molecules, suggesting that the multi-criteria screening prevented the candidate set from becoming disproportionately concentrated within a single structural class. By restricting the retention to a maximum of two molecules per structural cluster and applying a comprehensive ranking using the three metrics LBPavg, Vinamed, and LibDock, 30 candidate ligands were ultimately selected for subsequent molecular dynamics simulations. Notably, this clustering step was used to balance screening depth, structural diversity, and computational cost, rather than to define strict chemical or bioactivity classes.

Molecular dynamics simulation analysis

A multi-stage molecular dynamics simulation protocol was employed to screen 30 candidate complexes. Following 20 ns short-term simulations, 12 candidate complexes advanced to the 100 ns simulation stage. Subsequently, these candidate complexes were narrowed down to 5, which proceeded to the 500 ns long-term simulation. The detailed results of the multistage MD screening are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. Based on the stability evaluation of the 500 ns trajectories, three candidate complexes were selected for COM analysis between the ligand and the binding pocket over the final 50 ns (Fig. 5a). The results indicated that the COM distances for the Keap1–Quercetin 3-arabinoside complex and the Keap1–clauselansine D complex remained consistently below 4.5 Å within this interval. The third system showed larger COM-distance fluctuations and was therefore excluded from subsequent free-energy analysis.

Molecular dynamics-based screening, binding mode, and conformational stability analysis of the Keap1–Quercetin 3-arabinoside complex. (a) Comparison of ligand–pocket COM distances for the three candidate complexes during the final 50 ns (450–500 ns) of the 500 ns simulations; (b) Representative binding conformation of the Keap1–Quercetin 3-arabinoside complex; (c) Per-residue energy decomposition of the Keap1–Quercetin 3-arabinoside complex; (d) Two-dimensional FEL constructed from PC1 and PC2.

Subsequently, MM-PBSA calculations revealed that the Keap1–Quercetin 3-arabinoside complex exhibited a lower total binding free energy than the Keap1–clauselansine D complex; therefore, it was selected as the representative complex for subsequent analysis. The detailed MM-PBSA energy components are summarized in Supplementary Table S4. Regarding energy composition, both van der Waals and electrostatic interactions contributed favorably to the binding process, whereas polar solvation energy exerted a negative influence (Wang et al., 2019). This phenomenon is consistent with previously reported energetic compensation effects for highly polar polyhydroxy compounds in aqueous binding environments (Wang et al., 2019).

The representative binding conformation demonstrated that Quercetin 3-arabinoside stably occupied the binding pocket of the Keap1 Kelch domain and formed multipoint interactions with key residues, including ARG-415, ASN-414, ALA-510, LEU-557, and SER-602 (Fig. 5b). Notably, ARG-415 formed multiple hydrogen bonds with the ligand, serving as a crucial anchoring site to maintain the stability of the complex.

Further residue decomposition analysis indicated that ARG-415 contributed most significantly to the binding affinity, with an energy contribution of approximately −3.4 kcal/mol (Fig. 5c). Over the entire 500 ns trajectory, the cumulative hydrogen-bond occupancy of ARG-415 reached 120%, suggesting that the guanidinium group potentially acted as a multivalent donor. The location of ARG-415 within the central binding region of the Keap1 Kelch domain and near the Nrf2 ETGE/DLG motif recognition interface (Lo et al., 2006, Marcotte et al., 2013) provides a structural basis for its parallel or alternating polyvalent hydrogen-bond interactions with multiple oxygen acceptors on the ligand. This multi-point anchoring mode helps enhance the stability of the ligand within the binding pocket, thereby explaining the stable occupancy characteristics of the complex.

The two-dimensional FEL, constructed based on PC1 and PC2, demonstrated that the Keap1–Quercetin 3-arabinoside complex primarily concentrated within a single prominent low-energy basin during the late stages of the simulation (Fig. 5d), suggesting that the system tended toward a relatively convergent conformational state after the ligand stably occupied the binding pocket. Based on the COM distance, key residue interactions, MM-PBSA binding free energy, and FEL results, Quercetin 3-arabinoside exhibited robust binding stability within the Keap1 Kelch pocket at the simulation level. These results collectively support the capability of the compound to interact stably with the Keap1 Kelch domain via non-covalent mechanisms.

A comparison of the overall dynamics between Apo-Keap1 and the Keap1–Quercetin 3-arabinoside complex revealed that both systems converged rapidly at the beginning of the simulation and subsequently exhibited relatively stable fluctuations throughout the entire 500 ns simulation (Fig. 6a). Specifically, the Cα-RMSD of the complex system was slightly lower overall than that of the Apo system, primarily distributed within the range of 1.00 to 1.25 Å. Rg analysis revealed that both systems maintained structural stability throughout the 500 ns simulation, fluctuating within a narrow range (Fig. 6b). SASA results demonstrated that the distribution for the Apo system remained within a similar range to that of the complex system (Fig. 6c), although the overall SASA values for the complex system were marginally lower. Further comparison of the RMSF distributions between the two systems revealed largely consistent overall trends in flexibility (Fig. 6d), with significant fluctuations primarily concentrated at the protein termini and within flexible loop regions. Compared to the Apo system, the complex system exhibited slightly lower RMSF values for specific residues near the binding pocket, whereas no significant differences were observed across the main secondary structure regions. These results indicate that the binding of Quercetin 3-arabinoside did not significantly disrupt the overall conformational stability of Keap1. Based on subsequent pocket occupancy and interaction analyses, this ligand is more likely to exert its effects by stably occupying an existing binding pocket rather than by inducing significant conformational rearrangements in the Keap1 Kelch domain.

Quantum chemical calculations

Quantum chemical calculations were employed to characterize the electronic structures of Quercetin 3-arabinoside, Avicularin, and vitamin C. For Quercetin 3-arabinoside, the HOMO energy was −0.2195 a.u. (−5.9726eV) and the LUMO energy was −0.0703 a.u. (−1.9138eV), with a corresponding HOMO–LUMO energy gap of 0.1492 a.u. (4.0589 eV). Under the same computational conditions, the HOMO and LUMO energies of its isomer Avicularin were calculated to be −0.2236 a.u. (−6.0849eV) and −0.0767 a.u. (−2.0868eV), respectively, with a corresponding HOMO–LUMO energy gap of 0.1469 a.u. (3.9980 eV), while the HOMO and LUMO energies of the reference natural antioxidant vitamin C were calculated to be −0.2345 a.u. (−6.3815eV) and −0.0344 a.u. (−0.9353eV), respectively, with a corresponding HOMO–LUMO energy gap of 0.2001 a.u. (5.4462 eV). Compared with vitamin C, the HOMO–LUMO gaps of Quercetin 3-arabinoside and Avicularin were reduced by 1.3873 eV and 1.4482 eV, respectively. Notably, the energy gap of Quercetin 3-arabinoside was only 0.0609 eV larger than that of Avicularin, indicating highly similar frontier orbital energy separations between the two isomers. The HOMO–LUMO gap is commonly used as a qualitative descriptor of molecular reactivity. A smaller energy gap indicates that the electron density can be more readily perturbed and is generally associated with greater electronic reactivity and a higher propensity to participate in electron transfer processes (Alov et al., 2015). Accordingly, the narrower energy gaps of Quercetin 3-arabinoside and Avicularin relative to vitamin C suggest greater electronic responsiveness under the same computational conditions. The two isomers showed similar frontier orbital localization, with both the HOMO and LUMO predominantly distributed over the conjugated flavonoid core rather than concentrated within the sugar moiety (Fig. 7a, b, d, and e). This phenomenon suggested that the electronic reactivity of the molecule primarily originates from the polyphenolic hydroxyl group, carbonyl group, and conjugated π system within the quercetin skeleton (Rice-Evans et al., 1996).

The molecular orbital isovalue was 0.02 a.u., and the MEP range was −0.123 to +0.123 a.u. on an electron density isosurface of 0.001 a.u. For the MEP maps, red and blue represent more negative and more positive electrostatic potentials, respectively.

Under the same electrostatic potential range and electron density isovalue, both Quercetin 3-arabinoside and Avicularin exhibited heterogeneous surface electrostatic potential distributions (Fig. 7c and f). Regions of more negative potential correspond primarily to surfaces near the carbonyl oxygen, phenolic hydroxyl oxygen, glycosyl oxygen, and glycosidic oxygen, while regions of more positive potential are mainly distributed around the exposed hydroxyl hydrogen atoms. Most of the surface of the central aromatic carbon skeleton exhibited intermediate electrostatic potential. Both isomers showed broadly similar electrostatic potential distributions in the shared quercetin core region, while local differences were primarily observed in the sugar and glycosidic bond regions, which may be related to their different sugar configurations and spatial orientations. Combined with similar frontier molecular orbital localizations and HOMO–LUMO energy gaps, these results supported the conclusion that the two isomers possess similar but not entirely identical electronic characteristics. This provided theoretical support for using Avicularin as a structurally related analog for experimental assessment to preliminarily evaluate the antioxidant potential of the common flavonoid glycoside skeleton.

The results of the global reactivity descriptors indicated that the global hardness and softness values of the two isomers were very close. The detailed values are provided in Supplementary Table S5. The global hardness and softness of Quercetin 3-arabinoside were 2.0294 eV and 0.4927 eV−1, respectively, while those of Avicularin were 1.9990 eV and 0.5002 eV−1, respectively. The slightly lower hardness and slightly higher softness of Avicularin suggested that its electron density may be slightly more responsive to external perturbations. The absolute electronegativity, electrophilicity index, and maximum electronic charge transfer of Avicularin were 4.0859 eV, 4.1756 eV, and 2.0439, respectively, all of which were slightly higher than the corresponding values for Quercetin 3-arabinoside, which were 3.9432 eV, 3.8308 eV, and 1.9430, respectively, indicating that Avicularin exhibited slightly stronger electronegative and electrophilic characteristics. Nevertheless, the various descriptors of the two molecules generally remained within similar ranges, indicating a high degree of similarity in their overall electronic reactivity.

Collectively, the similar HOMO–LUMO energy gaps, frontier molecular orbital localization patterns, surface electrostatic potential distributions, and global reactivity descriptors of Quercetin 3-arabinoside and Avicularin indicated that, despite local differences in their glycosidic configurations, Avicularin retained core electronic structure features similar to those of Quercetin 3-arabinoside. These findings provided a theoretical basis for its use as a structurally related experimental analog.

Chemical antioxidant activity evaluation of Avicularin

In the DPPH assay, both Avicularin and the positive control vitamin C caused a concentration-dependent decrease in absorbance at 515 nm, indicating dose-dependent DPPH radical-scavenging activity. The fitting results showed that the IC50 of Avicularin was 26.84 μM, and the IC50 of vitamin C was 13.21 μM (Fig. 8a).

Chemical antioxidant assays of Avicularin. (a) DPPH radical-scavenging curves of Avicularin and vitamin C; (b) ABTS radical-scavenging curves of Avicularin and Trolox; (c) FRAP reducing antioxidant power of Avicularin.

In the ABTS assay, both Avicularin and the positive control Trolox exhibited a concentration-dependent increase in ABTS radical scavenging rate, indicating dose-dependent ABTS radical-scavenging activity. The fitting results showed that the IC50 of Avicularin was 6.94 μM, and the IC50 of Trolox was 24.70 μM (Fig. 8b).

In the FRAP assay, Avicularin caused a concentration-dependent increase in FRAP values, indicating dose-dependent ferric reducing antioxidant power. The FRAP value of Avicularin increased from 0.013 ± 0.008 mmol FeSO4 equivalents L−1 at 0.3906 μM to 1.327 ± 0.218 mmol FeSO4 equivalents L−1 at 25 μM (Fig. 8c).

These experimental observations are consistent with the electronic features suggested by the quantum chemical calculations, which align with the electron-transfer or hydrogen-atom-transfer processes involved in DPPH and ABTS radical-scavenging reactions and the electron-transfer process reflected by the FRAP assay (Huang et al., 2005), providing a theoretical basis for the antioxidant potential of this flavonoid glycoside scaffold.

Notably, the candidate molecule analyzed in this study was Quercetin 3-arabinoside, whereas the in vitro experiments used its structurally similar isomer Avicularin. Both compounds share an identical molecular formula and key antioxidant structural features, including the quercetin flavonoid core, polyphenolic hydroxyl groups, and conjugated systems. Given that the frontier molecular orbitals were primarily distributed across the flavonoid core, Avicularin served as a highly relevant structural analog for the preliminary evaluation of the radical scavenging potential of this class of compounds. However, differences in glycosidic configuration and spatial orientation between the two compounds persist. Therefore, the experimental results for Avicularin should be interpreted as experimental support for the antioxidant potential of this class of flavonoid glycoside scaffolds, rather than a definitive substitute for direct chemical antioxidant validation of Quercetin 3-arabinoside. Meanwhile, these experimental results provided preliminary support for the feasibility of the preceding computational screening workflow in prioritizing potential natural antioxidant candidates.

Conclusions

This study established a framework combining multiscale computations and experiments, covering activity prediction, structural screening, dynamic stability, electronic structure analysis, and chemical antioxidant assays. This framework was used to rapidly screen antioxidant candidate compounds from the edible fruit Clausena lansium and conduct experimental evaluations. First, a database containing 474 Clausena lansium small molecules was established. Five machine learning models demonstrated strong predictive performance across eight antioxidant datasets, with ROC-AUC values ranging from 0.85 to 0.99. Through multi-criteria screening, 47 candidate small molecules were identified. After structural clustering and diversity selection, 30 representative candidates were selected for molecular dynamics simulations. Through multi-stage molecular dynamics simulations, Quercetin 3-arabinoside was prioritized as a candidate molecule with potential antioxidant activity. Quantum chemical calculations revealed that the HOMO–LUMO energy gap of Quercetin 3-arabinoside was 4.0589 eV, which was 25.5% lower than that of vitamin C, suggesting high electronic reactivity and potential for participation in electron transfer processes. Given the limited commercial availability and purity assurance of the Quercetin 3-arabinoside reference standard, this study selected its structural isomer Avicularin for experimental assessment. The theoretical basis for this selection was provided by their similar HOMO–LUMO energy gaps, similar frontier molecular orbital localization, broadly similar MEP distributions, and similar global reactivity descriptors. Avicularin exhibited IC50 values of 26.84 μM and 6.94 μM in the DPPH and ABTS assays, respectively, and its FRAP value at 25 μM was 1.327 ± 0.218 mmol FeSO4 equivalents L−1. These experimental results provided support for the antioxidant potential of the shared flavonoid glycoside scaffold and were generally consistent with the electronic characteristics revealed by the quantum chemical calculations, thereby supporting the feasibility of the computational screening workflow. Overall, these results indicated that Clausena lansium is a promising source of food-derived natural antioxidant small molecules and provided methodological guidance for the screening and development of its antioxidant constituents. Furthermore, this computational workflow can be extended to the screening of antioxidant small molecules in other natural product systems, reducing reliance on extensive preliminary experimental screening and providing a transferable strategy for the further development of natural antioxidants and functional food ingredients.
