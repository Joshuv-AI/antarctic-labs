Development and pilot of a drug use and HIV stigma reduction training for Tajik migrants who work in Moscow: the SRI-AVLOD intervention
Authors: Mary Ellen Mackesy-Amiti, Judith A. Levy, Leslie D. Williams, Jonbek Jonbekov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTajik labor migrants who inject drugs while working in Moscow are subject to the negative effects of stigma within the diaspora community in Russia. They are also at risk of acquiring HIV, which further compounds their stigmatization. An intervention to reduce both drug-related and HIV-related stigma within the Tajik diaspora community is needed to prevent adverse social and health effects among its members who inject drugs.MethodsWe conducted a series of focus groups and interviews, from January to June 2024, with Tajik migrant workers (n = 65) and health workers (n = 18) who treat migrants in Moscow to inform the development of a four-session anti-discrimination training for Tajik community leaders. The training sessions are designed to educate leaders about substance use disorder and HIV and prepare them to act as change agents in reducing stigma toward both disorders within the Moscow Tajik diaspora community. In September 2025, we pilot tested the Stigma Reduction Intervention Approach Via Leaders of Diaspora (SRI-AVLOD) for cultural acceptability and feasibility with two groups of 8 traditional migrant community (avlod) leaders while residing in Tajikistan before returning to Moscow for work. Measures of drug use and HIV-related stigma were administered prior to the first and after the final training session. We collected participant feedback on the acceptability and feasibility of the intervention and conducted a brief follow-up interview post-training to assess participants’ experience in delivering the anti-stigma messages to their community.ResultsParticipant ratings and comments indicated high acceptability and satisfaction with the training. Ratings of acceptability, feasibility, appropriateness and usefulness were uniformly high. Measures of stigmatizing attitudes among the avlod leaders were reduced following the intervention. In follow-up interviews, participants reported favorable experiences in delivering the anti-stigma message to people in their community.ConclusionThe SRI-AVLOD intervention proved feasible and acceptable to Tajik community leaders and was judged to have considerable potential for effective diffusion within the migrant diaspora community.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28274-4.

Background

Stigma toward international migrants is a global phenomenon with significant consequences for individuals and public health [1, 2]. Tajikistan, a small country in Central Asia with a high unemployment rate and an ongoing drug epidemic [3–5] exports more than a million temporary labor migrants to Russia for work each year [6, 7]. In 2024, international remittances from Tajik migrants to their home country, mostly from Russia, accounted for 49% of the country’s GDP and supported 21% of households [8]. Most Tajik migrants find employment in the Russian Federation working in trade, agriculture, maintenance, construction (men), and the domestic sector (women) [9, 10]. As true for labor migrants in many countries, life in Russia for Tajik workers often is hard. Many are employed in Russia’s most dangerous and undesirable jobs [11]. In addition they often are the focus of Russian hostility and violence [12–14], endure poor living conditions, and may have limited access to Russian health care if sick or injured [15]. At the same time, some unknown number of Tajik migrants having entered Russia illegally must constantly hide from Russian immigration authorities.

These factors, along with the psychosocial stress and separation from family and friends can coalesce to produce emotional distress including depression and feelings of loneliness that can lead to initial or accelerated drug use, encourage risky sexual and/or drug behavior, discourage HIV testing, and result in poor HIV medication adherence and treatment outcomes [16–20]. Opiate use has been a significant problem in both Russia and Tajikistan since the 1990’s [4, 21, 22], nonetheless migrants who use drugs in Russia face certain disadvantages, including the absence of medication treatment for opioid use and few harm reduction services compared to what is available in Tajikistan. In general, the prevalence of HIV in Russia among its citizenry is high (estimated > 800 per 100,000 population) [23], especially among people who inject drugs [24–26]. Treatment for HIV is not available to most migrants, and a recent study provides evidence that Tajik migrants with HIV working in Russia are at increased risk of late presentation to treatment [27].

Most Tajik migrants in Moscow reside in close-knit diaspora communities where those who inject drugs, especially if they acquire HIV, are subject to extensive censure and marginalization from their non-using Tajik peers [28, 29]. Such social exclusion and adverse treatment while in diaspora can compound or exacerbate the emotional distress commonly associated with being a migrant. No interventions currently exist to mitigate the social beliefs and behavior within a diaspora migrant community that underpin and reinforce stigma related to drug use and HIV. To address this unmet need, we investigated the character of stigma in the Tajik labor migrant community and the effects of intersecting forms of stigma on the health and well-being of Tajik migrant people who inject drugs (PWID) and people living with HIV (PLWH) in diaspora. Rather than focusing on the largely intractable attitudes of a host society, we direct our intervention efforts to changing stigmatizing attitudes and behavior within the Tajik diaspora community itself.

Using a community participatory approach to inform the intervention, the model that we developed draws upon Tajikistan’s traditional avlod leadership arrangement that forms the basic unit of Tajik social structure. The avlod system of authority in Tajikistan is based on Tajik tradition that accords a cadre of highly respected community leaders command and influence over the behavior and beliefs of their extended kin and village members [30, 31]. The avlod system is many centuries old and has its roots in the collectivism of early land policy in which authority and farm labor was divided among several generations of family members [31]. This tradition continues to dominate today’s Tajik society with the avlod kinship structure holding primacy in leadership, custom, and influence over that of the family or individual.

We consulted avlod leaders of the Moscow Tajik diaspora community as key participants in developing a network-based host-country leadership HIV and drug-related stigma intervention. Drawing on the insight and findings gained through formative research, we developed and pilot tested the acceptability and feasibility of the Stigma Reduction Intervention Approach Via Leaders of Diaspora (SRI-AVLOD) model. This model is specifically designed to counter the convergent negative effects of HIV-related and drug-related stigma that can propel risk behavior and impede prevention and treatment among people who use drugs. A third dimension of stigma that we address explicitly is stigma against labor migrants by members of the host country, due their outsider status.

Our research efforts build on the integration of 4 conceptual models: (1) community participatory research in which stakeholders and those affected by an intervention are included as partners in contributing expertise and sharing in the decision-making concerning the model’s development and delivery; (2) Social cognitive theory that considers the influence of observational learning, modeling, and perceived self-efficacy when developing effective interventions to promote sustained and translatable behavior change [32]; (3) Social Identity Theory, which posits that people are stigmatized when considered to be members of an “out-group” with few similarities to one’s “in-groups” [32], and conversely that increasing the salience of commonalities with one’s “out-group” members can help to reduce stigma [33]; and (4) Diffusion of Innovation Theory that posits that new information, norms, and/or attitudes can be disseminated and integrated into a setting or group via a small number of influential individuals [34].

Close-knit Tajik communities in diaspora are ideal socio-cultural settings for implementing a successful HIV and drug use stigma-reduction intervention. As our prior research shows: (1) Tajik diaspora communities have clear and approachable traditional leaders (avlod) who are highly respected and can shift perceived norms and thereby influence others to adopt positive behavioral change; and (2) members of diaspora communities tend to interact closely with each other and with this leadership. Consequently, new norms and behaviors can be effectively diffused and promoted within and across migrant social networks if endorsed by leaders whom the members of these networks trust. SRI-AVLOD builds on both advantages by opening up conversation by avlod leaders within the Tajik diaspora community about the direct effects of stigma on its members who inject drugs and/or are living with HIV and its indirect effects on their family, friends, and the community at large. SRI-AVLOD recruits and trains Tajik diaspora community leaders as potential agents of change in reducing drug-related and HIV-related stigmatizing beliefs and actions within the Tajik community that negatively affect risk behavior, prevention, and treatment among its members who inject drugs.

The SRI-AVLOD intervention leadership model

Upon migrating to Russia and other destinations, labor migrants join other Tajiks who have migrated before them to create close social ties, provide mutual support, and establish a migrant network of avlod leaders abroad. These powerful leaders and the networks of migrant workers whom they influence offer a strong, well-established social structure through which to effect normative and behavioral change.

The SRI-AVLOD intervention begins by targeting possible stigmatizing beliefs, attitudes, and behavior held by avlod leaders toward the diaspora community’s PWID and PLWH. The goal is to positively change any negative beliefs and behavior that these influential leaders might hold toward people living with these two disorders with the expectation that, in turn, they will use their social network leadership to similarly educate other Tajiks. This premise is based on Diffusion of Innovation Theory, which posits that new information, norms, and/or attitudes can be disseminated and integrated into a setting or group via a small number of influential individuals. Comprised of four educational sessions, the SRI-AVLOD intervention is delivered to groups of 6–8 avlod leaders. It includes an overview of the challenges that PWID confront in ending or practicing safer drug use and how stigmatizing attitudes, norms, and behavior can hinder this process and lead to heightened HIV risk behavior, undermine HIV prevention, and discourage HIV testing and/or seeking treatment if needed. It uses narrative vignettes to generate empathy for PWID and to illustrate similarities between SRI-AVLOD participants and PWID. These vignettes reflect an application of Social Identity Theory, which posits that people are stigmatized when considered to be members of an “out-group” with few similarities to one’s “in-groups”, and that increasing the salience of commonalities with one’s “out-group” members can help to reduce stigma. Participants are also given scenarios and asked to role play during sessions as PWID, PLWH, and/or their family members seeking help or advice, and also as themselves, responding to requests for such advice. This role play applies Social Cognitive Theory, which considers the influence of observational learning, modeling, and perceived self-efficacy when developing effective interventions to promote sustained and translatable behavior change. At the end of the four sessions, each leader is asked as homework to select a venue or other opportunity to speak with other Tajik migrants in discussing and promoting positive normative and behavioral change within the Tajik diaspora community toward its members who are PWID and/or PLWH.

We utilized a community participatory research framework in our development of the SRI-AVLOD intervention, including stakeholders and those affected by the intervention as partners in contributing expertise and sharing in the decision-making concerning the model’s development and delivery. Evaluation measures in this preliminary pilot assess behavioral and attitudinal change post intervention among the participants, success in discussing new norms and beliefs with other Tajik migrants, and participant satisfaction with and perceived usefulness of the training including its feasibility in bringing about positive community change.

Methods

The study’s research design has 3 phases: (1) formative research in developing a working version of the SRI-AVLOD curricula and manual, (2) a pretest of the intervention and curricula followed by revisions as needed, and (3) delivery and evaluation of the final version of the intervention. While we originally planned to conduct the research in Moscow, international events transpired that necessitated modifying the protocol to conduct the research with returning migrants in Tajikistan. Since Tajik migrants tend to be cyclic workers who move periodically between Tajikistan and Moscow, returning migrants while in their home country can provide relevant perspectives on life in Russian diaspora, and avlod leaders can be trained in Tajikistan prior to circling back to Moscow.

Study procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Belmont Report and approved by the institutional review boards of the University of Illinois Chicago (USA) and PRISMA Research Center (Tajikistan). All participants provided written informed consent.

Phase 1: formative research: development of the SRI-AVLOD curricula and manual

We explored the nature and effects of stigma specifically related to drug use and HIV through a series of focus groups (FGs) and in-depth interviews (Table 1). Our intent was to identify and understand the cultural drivers of stigma that influence collective norms, personal beliefs, and actions toward Tajiks who inject drugs and/or are HIV positive. Migrant workers who self-identified as non-drug users were recruited using convenience sampling for 5 FGs (5 participants each) with the assistance of community health promoters. Diaspora community avlod leaders were recruited via purposive sampling through referrals from local village organizations for 5 focus groups of 5 participants. Participants in both informant categories had returned to Tajikistan from Moscow within the past 6 months and intended to return to Moscow in the future. Both men and women were included.

Focus groups were held in five locations representing the capital city of Dushanbe, the Region of Republican Subordination (RRP), Gorno-Badakshan Autonomous Oblast (GBAO), and the regions of Sughd and Khatlon. We held 3 FGs of health care workers recruited from the 3 Moscow clinics that serve Tajik patients in Moscow. These healthcare workers participated remotely by videoconference. In addition, we interviewed remotely 3 medical doctors in Moscow who head these clinics. The interviews and focus groups with healthcare workers focused on their perceptions of drug-related stigma among both diaspora and Moscow health care providers and its impact on the health of migrants in Moscow who inject drugs, their service use, and HIV risk, prevention, and treatment. We also conducted in-depth interviews with 15 Tajik returning migrants who were current or former PWID to gain a thorough understanding of enacted and internalized stigma as experienced by them in Moscow. Inquiry included their perception of the effects of stigma on HIV risk behavior, prevention, and treatment.

Separate focus group discussion guides were developed and tailored to fit each of the three categories of FG participants. In addition to discussing HIV and drug-related topics relevant to each group’s organizational, workplace and/or community experience, attention was directed to key topics related to stigma and stigmatizing attitudes and behavior that participants may have observed or personally enacted toward people with HIV and/or drug use disorder.

Both the focus group discussions and in-depth interviews explored possible Tajik community and organizational-level facilitators available to support the SRI-AVLOD HIV and drug use stigma reduction intervention, possible impediments to its implementation, and cultural mores and practices to consider in developing and mounting it. Importantly, since HIV-related and drug use-related stigma enacted towards women is tied to traditional gender expectations in Tajikistan and typically is more severe toward them than toward men [35, 36], we explored potential gender differences that could affect the messaging and delivery of the intervention.

Each focus group session and interview was audio-recorded, transcribed, and translated into English to permit analyses by non-Tajik speaking team members. An initial list of codes and their definitions was created deductively based upon constructs from the study’s conceptual framework. Additional codes and definitions were created inductively based on new themes that emerged during the group discussions and interviews. Each focus group and interview transcript was coded independently by two research team members and subsequently reviewed by two other members. All investigators participated in discussions to resolve coding discrepancies. The findings were organized according to topics and relevance in developing the proposed SRI-AVLOD intervention including attention to social drivers of stigma and common misconceptions about HIV and drug use.

The stigma experiences that PWID reported informed the creation of relatable vignettes to explain the serious consequences of drug-related and HIV-related stigma. Focus group data revealed a number of common misconceptions and beliefs in misinformation or myths about HIV transmission risk (e.g., that HIV can be transmitted by being in the same home or room with someone living with HIV), the effects of HIV on health (e.g., that everyone diagnosed with HIV will die soon), and the nature of substance use disorder and addiction (e.g., the belief that quitting drug use is merely a matter of will power). To address these, we developed a “Fallacies and Facts” format within the curricula to debunk myths and misunderstandings and replace them with corresponding scientific fact.

Focus group discussions alerted us to the importance in our training of addressing women’s special biological and gender risk for HIV related to reproduction and traditional gender expectations that stigma further exacerbates. Curricula included examples of misogynistic attitudes that contribute to heightened stigma for women and its effects on their vulnerability to HIV and adoption of preventive behavior. Opinions as to the acceptability of women as avlod leaders were explored to help identify and prepare women who assume this role to positively confront and effectively mitigate possible stigma that they might experience. Gender-related topics also included the added stigma that women experience compared to their male counterparts if they inject drugs or acquire HIV.

Avlod focus group participants were strongly affirmative when asked if they personally believed that avlod training and its delivery would prove of significant value to the Tajik Moscow diaspora community. One leader saw real merit in the intervention’s potential to improve health care services for migrants.

From the focus group findings, the investigators collaboratively developed the SRI-AVLOD training and intervention manual. The 4-session training was designed to reduce both HIV and substance-use related stigma among avlod leaders and later among Tajik community members via social network diffusion. Curricula included the biological and trauma-related etiology of substance use disorder, dispelling false beliefs about HIV transmission, increasing understanding and empathy for PWID and PLWH, and training avlod leaders in effective communication strategies. The draft manual was presented to the study’s Tajik Community Advisory Board from which feedback was requested and incorporated as needed.

Phase 2: pretest of the SRI-AVLOD intervention

We delivered the 4 training sessions to 2 groups of 5–7 diaspora community leaders in Tajikistan, one held in the capital city of Dushanbe and one in GBAO. We presented each session in its entirety, followed by participant discussion and critique. A focus group guide helped to direct the discussion to key topics such as attendees’ comfort with the content of the sessions, their cultural appropriateness for use with Tajik migrants, the perceived usefulness of the sessions’ content in reducing HIV-related and drug use-related stigma, how the information could best be disseminated through their networks, and suggested modifications. The sessions were audio-taped, transcribed, and translated for analysis.

The results were used to further develop the SRI-AVLOD intervention and its leadership training sessions. Focus group participants made a number of concrete suggestions for additions and revisions, such as including statistics on HIV (e.g., prevalence in Tajikistan), clarifying the meaning of certain terms (e.g. differentiation vs. discrimination), providing more examples of positive (anti-stigma) behavior, connecting empathy to religious teachings, and discussing the cultural significance of shame and shame as a barrier to seeking help. It was recommended to change the gender of the principal character in one vignette from a woman to a man. Participants also asked for more visual aids. The revised manual was presented to the Community Advisory Board for requested feedback. An outline of the finalized manual is available in Appendix A (see Additional File 1).

Phase 3: pilot testing the intervention

The intervention was delivered to two groups of eight diaspora community leaders. At the start of the first session, we collected sociodemographic information from the participants, including gender, age, occupation, and other social roles held in the community, as well as measures of leaders’ knowledge about HIV, and attitudes regarding Tajik PWID and PLWH. At the end of the program, we readministered the measures of HIV knowledge and attitudes regarding PWID and PLWH and collected program evaluations. We also asked participants to describe their plans for speaking with Tajik migrants with whom they interact in a leadership role to discuss and promote positive normative and behavioral change within the Tajik diaspora community toward its members who use drugs or are living with HIV. We conducted a brief telephone interview with participants 4–6 weeks after the last session to ask them about their interactions with community members (e.g. how many people they talked to about HIV and/or drug use, whether individually or in groups), how receptive community members were to their message, and their opinions about how well the intervention prepared them to have these discussions.

Measures

Both process and training outcome measures were adapted from existing English-language measures. Items were translated into Tajik by a native speaker and independently back-translated to English for verification. We pretested the draft instruments with five native Tajik speakers using cognitive interviewing. Items that were identified as confusing or culturally inappropriate were removed or revised. After making these first-step revisions, a survey was administered to 60 returned migrants to test performance of the instruments including internal reliability and cross-correlations. We made additional revisions based on these survey results. Items with poor reliability were removed or replaced if a wording change was deemed appropriate. The final measures are available in Appendix B (see Additional File 2).

Process measures

Questions to assess acceptability, feasibility, and appropriateness were adapted from the Acceptability, Feasibility, and Appropriateness Scale (AFAS) [37]. Acceptability was measured with 6 items assessing satisfaction with organization, delivery, and content. Feasibility was measured with 2 items assessing perceived potential utility. Appropriateness was measured with 2 items assessing the cultural relevance of the content and compatibility with the avlod leader role. Statements were rated on a 5-point scale (1 = not at all, 5 = extremely).

Seven items were created to assess usefulness of the intervention, including understanding of content, self-efficacy to disseminate the message, intention to follow through, and general attitude toward the intervention. Each statement was rated on a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree).

Familiarity with drug use disorder and HIV

These two measures, based on the Level of Contact Report [38, 39], each consist of a series of twelve yes/no items regarding familiarity with HIV or drug use disorder (DUD). The items are summed to form an index ranging from 1 (“I have never [observed | known] a person with a [drug use disorder | HIV]”) to 12 (“I have a [drug use disorder | HIV]”).

HIV knowledge

The HIV knowledge measure was previously developed for another study [40]. It included 8 items that described behaviors that respondents were asked to identify as safe or unsafe with regard to transmission of HIV and 8 true/false items pertaining to HIV infection, testing, and treatment. Items were scored as correct or incorrect with responses of “unsure” scored as incorrect. The total score is calculated as the number of items correct (alpha = 0.80).

Brief opioid stigma scale (BOSS) [41]

This scale includes four statements of things that “most people believe” about a person who is addicted to opioids (measuring perceived societal stigma), and four corresponding statements of things that “I believe” (measuring individual stigmatizing attitudes), e.g., “[I / Most people] believe that a person who is addicted to opioids cannot be trusted.” We added two additional culturally relevant statements, “[I / Most people] believe that a woman who is addicted to opioids is promiscuous.”] Items are rated on a scale from 1 “strongly disagree” to 5 “strongly agree” (3 = unsure). Item ratings on each subscale are summed to produce a score ranging from 5 to 25 (alpha = 0.64 for individual stigmatizing attitudes; 0.62 for perceived societal stigma).

Stigmatizing attitudes towards PLWH scale - SAT-PLWH-S [42]

The adapted scale consists of 20 positive and negative statements (reduced from 27) to assess stigmatizing attitudes toward people living with HIV rated on a Likert scale from 1 “strongly disagree” to 5 “strongly agree.” We changed “AIDS” to “HIV” and we made some other minor changes to the wording of some items. Positive statements are reverse scored so that higher scores indicate a more negative attitude. Item ratings are averaged to produce a continuous score ranging from 1 to 5. After eliminating 7 items with poor reliability, alpha = 0.90.

Attribution questionnaire

This measure of negative attitudes towards people with DUD was adapted from Corrigan et al. Attribution Questionnaire Short Form (AQ-27) [43]. After reading a vignette describing a person with opioid use disorder (OUD), participants respond to a series of 20 statements representing constructs of personal responsibility beliefs, pity, anger, fear, helping and avoidance, and support for coercion and segregation. Each item was rated on a nine-point semantic differential type scale (1 = “not at all”, 9 = “very much”). The scale score was computed as the average of item ratings with positive items reversed so that higher scores represent more negative attitudes (alpha = 0.89).

Social distance

This measure was adapted from Link et al. [44] to measure desire for social distance. After reading a vignette about a person in recovery for opioid use disorder, participants rated their willingness to engage in 7 behaviors (e.g., sharing an apartment, recommending for a job). Items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = “definitely willing”, 4 = “definitely unwilling”). Next, participants were asked to respond to the same items after learning that the person also is receiving HIV medication. Subscale scores were averaged to form a social distance score. Internal reliability was excellent for both the initial ratings (alpha = 0.86) and the subsequent ratings (alpha = 0.93).

Statistical analysis

We computed means and standard deviations of post-intervention process ratings and conducted t-tests on subscale scores by sex and location. We computed means and standard deviations of pre- and post-intervention HIV knowledge and stigma measures, and conducted mixed effects regressions models with random subject intercepts testing the time effect and including sex, age, and location as covariates. We report the change coefficients and 95% confidence intervals. Analyses were conducted using Stata v.18. A formal power analysis was not conducted as the primary outcomes were qualitative or exploratory.

Pilot participants

Most participants (82%) were well educated with some level of higher education or having completed a college degree. The number of lifetime trips to Russia ranged from 2 to 8 (mean = 4.6), and the cumulative number years living in Russia ranged from 4 to 11 (mean = 7.4). Follow-up qualitative interviews

Avlod leaders reported delivering anti-stigma messaging to an average of 16 people including both individuals and groups. All reported feeling very confident talking to other Tajiks about stigma related to substance use and HIV in part because of their position as a trusted leader in the community, but they also credited the training.“The training helped me consolidate the material on discrimination, the importance of personal communication in breaking down prejudices, and the understanding that every conversation is a step toward a more just and humanized society.” – Dushanbe [15]

Comments by avlod leaders indicated that generally their messages were received positively by those with whom they shared information. Some conversations shifted from initial surprise or caution about discussing such stigmatized disorders to openness. Tajiks with whom they spoke commonly reported learning about stigma and its harmful effects to be eye-opening, and they acknowledged the presence of discrimination within Tajik society including in diaspora. People initially agreed or came to agree through discussion that drug use disorder (DUD) and HIV should be treated as health conditions and that they understood the need for empathy and community support. Discussion

Our formative research with Tajik labor migrants, diaspora community leaders, healthcare workers, and PWID was instrumental in shaping a culturally relevant intervention to train avlod leaders on reducing stigma related to drug use and HIV in their social networks and the diaspora community. Through interviews and focus groups, we identified and then presented during the avlod leader training concrete examples of stigmatization, discrimination, and the consequences of stigma as well as countering damaging misconceptions about HIV and people who use drugs. The intervention was designed to explicitly address the convergence of stigmas related to drug use, HIV, and migrant status.

Pilot testing of the intervention demonstrated the SRI-AVLOD model’s feasibility and acceptability among Tajik avlod leaders who work in Moscow. During their training and in the weeks that followed, participants actively engaged in conversations with people in their communities individually and in groups about HIV, drug use, and related stigma. They reported that network members with whom they discussed HIV-related and drug-related stigma indicated that they found the information acceptable, appropriate, and useful, as they themselves did at the end of their training. They reported positive engagement experiences with their networks that they perceived helped Tajik diaspora community members to recognize the harm caused by stigma and to shift their attitudes to be more empathetic and inclusive.

Introducing the topic of HIV or drug dependency into an informal conversation can prove challenging. Tajiks widely consider both to be negative conditions with untoward connotations. Identifying a conversation starter can help smooth a possibly awkward introduction. For example, in pioneering opinion leader research, Kelly and colleagues [45] mounted posters with 3-color traffic lights (representing high, medium, and low HIV sexual risk practices) in bars where men who have sex with men tend to congregate but without explanation as to what the lights meant. Opinion leaders wore pins with the same image, a visual cue prompting men to ask them to explain what the lights meant. Suggestions for avlod leaders in this study during training were simpler but effective. One included mentioning to diaspora peers that they recently attended a workshop on HIV and learned some very interesting things. This statement tended to evoke a question asking them to explain what they had learned. Also Tajik community members likely have grown used to being informed about critical issues by avlod leaders and coming to them for information and advice.

Although traditionally charged with responsibility to help ensure the well-being of other Tajiks, the kinship structure of Tajik avlod leadership doesn’t automatically translate into understanding and empathy toward all Tajik members. Findings from pre-intervention attitudinal data reveal that stigma among avlod leaders toward Tajiks who inject drugs or are HIV positive is not uncommon. As evidenced by post-intervention results, SRI-AVLOD training exerts a positive effect in favorably changing these attitudes. Yet mean scores post-intervention also indicate that some vestige of stigmatization among avlod leaders remains, a finding that demonstrates how deeply embedded and resistant to change such attitudes and beliefs are. The highly accepted Thomas Theorem posits that things defined as real are real in their consequences [46]. Possibly in actively promoting destigmatization within the Tajik diaspora community towards people who use drugs or are living with HIV, over time through repetitiveness these negative residual judgments might abate. This possibility is consistent with the Social Identity and Social Cognitive theories that inform the intervention. Occasional avlod booster sessions held periodically post formal training also might have a mitigating effect.

Limitations

We originally conceived conducting the stigma reduction trainings in Moscow, however international events transpired that necessitated modifying the protocol to conduct the trainings with returning migrants in Tajikistan. This resulted in variability in the lag between the training and applying what was learned in the diaspora environment. Some of the participants had not yet returned to Moscow when the follow-up interviews were conducted and instead practiced what they had learned with community members in their home country. As conceived, the intervention aims to reduce stigma within the Tajik diaspora community and does not aim to modify stigma from the host society.

Internal consistency reliability of the BOSS scales measuring individual stigmatizing attitudes and perceived societal stigma against opioid use did not reach an acceptable level and would not be recommended for use in future research with this population. However, the Attribution Questionnaire for OUD and the Social Distance Scale performed well.

Evaluation and outcome measures may be susceptible to social desirability bias. Pre- and post-intervention questionnaires were completed confidentially to reduce the potential for socially desirable responses; however, this may have had a limited effect given the small group sizes. Also, this research is a pilot study conducted with a small sample and without a comparison group. Future research should build upon the preliminary findings presented here and address these limitations by conducting a randomized trial of the SRI-AVLOD intervention to test its efficacy experimentally.

Conclusions

Overall, the SRI-AVLOD Intervention Leadership Model holds considerable promise for reducing HIV and drug-related stigma among members of the Tajik Moscow diaspora community and also likely their counterparts in Tajikistan. Moreover, every year thousands of migrant workers from Central Asian countries other than Tajikistan travel to Moscow for temporary work. Likely among them are people who use drugs or acquire HIV. Building on the cultural similarities that they share with Tajikistan along with possible cultural adaptation needed to fit each country’s uniqueness, the SRI-AVLOD model may hold promise for their migrant populations living in diaspora as well.
