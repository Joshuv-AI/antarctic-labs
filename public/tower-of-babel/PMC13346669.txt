Comparative evaluation of neural networks and ensemble models for vegetation trend prediction in a semiarid mountain ecosystem, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Asma A. Al-Huqail, Zubairul Islam, Chigozie Edson Utazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates vegetation dynamics in a semiarid mountain ecosystem in southwestern Saudi Arabia from 1990 to 2024 by integrating multisource remote sensing data, bioclimatic variables, and machine learning models. Trends in vegetation greenness (NDVI), water content (NDWI), and land surface temperature (LST) were quantified via Kendall’s τ and Sen’s slope estimators. To capture fine-scale climatic heterogeneity in complex terrains, CHELSA bioclimatic variables were downscaled from ~ 1 km to 30 m resolution via random forest regression. These predictors were used to model NDVI trend patterns through a comparative framework including a baseline artificial neural network (ANN), metaheuristic-optimized ANN variants (ANN–PSO and ANN–GWO), and ensemble models (random forest and XGBoost). Model performance was assessed via independent validation, error metrics (RMSE, MAE, R²), bootstrap uncertainty analysis, and spatial residual diagnostics. All the models exhibited strong predictive ability, with XGBoost achieving the highest accuracy (RMSE ≈ 0.051; R² ≈ 0.92) and the lowest residual spatial autocorrelation. The spatial results indicate dominant greening across ~ 73–74% of the area, whereas ~ 25% of the area exhibits degradation concentrated in thermal and moisture-stressed zones. These findings highlight the value of integrated spectral-climatic modeling for monitoring vegetation changes in semiarid mountainous environments.

Introduction

Monitoring vegetation dynamics is essential for understanding ecosystem responses to climatic variability and anthropogenic pressures, particularly in regions where strong topographic and climatic gradients shape plant distributions1–4. Even subtle shifts in vegetation cover may signal changes in ecological stability, land productivity, or degradation processes5,6. In the southwestern highlands of Saudi Arabia, especially the Al Baha region, orographic effects generate cooler temperatures and enhanced precipitation that sustain localized zones of dense and diverse vegetation, including Juniperus procera woodlands and endemic flora, within an otherwise arid landscape7,8. These montane ecosystems are increasingly exposed to warming trends and recurrent droughts9. Although satellite observations have documented that vegetation greening in parts of Saudi Arabia’s mountainous regions is often attributed to rainfall variability and conservation initiatives10–12, localized vegetation decline and ecological stress persist in certain areas13. Such contrasting dynamics underscore the need for spatially explicit, site-specific assessments in ecologically sensitive environments such as Bani Hasan.

Recent studies emphasize that vegetation dynamics in semiarid environments are coupled with hydrological processes14. Complementary evidence shows that interactions between socioeconomic development and environmental drivers, significantly influence ecosystem stability, often resulting in imbalanced ecological responses and habitat degradation in water-dependent landscapes15. Seasonal investigations further reveal that nutrient dynamics and their interaction with climatic variability strongly regulate primary productivity and ecosystem responses16. In addition, land-use change and rainfall variability exert strong spatial and temporal controls on ecosystem processes, with agricultural runoff and altered hydrological regimes significantly influencing environmental quality and ecosystem resilience17.

Satellite remote sensing provides an indispensable framework for monitoring vegetation changes through spectral indices derived from the near-infrared and red bands. The normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) remains widely applied for quantifying vegetation greenness and density across ecosystems18,19. Long-term NDVI time series have been used to detect trends and evaluate ecosystem responses to climatic drivers, particularly precipitation and temperature variability20,21. In complex mountain terrains, these datasets reveal spatially heterogeneous vegetation responses. Nonparametric approaches such as Kendall’s tau and Sen’s slope are commonly employed to detect monotonic trends in NDVI time series because of their robustness to nonnormality and outliers22–24. However, while effective in characterizing temporal change, these methods do not explicitly model nonlinear interactions among multiple environmental drivers25.

Machine learning approaches have therefore gained prominence for modeling complex vegetation–environment relationships. Artificial neural networks (ANNs) have shown strong performance in capturing nonlinear vegetation–climate interactions and predicting spatial patterns of change26–29. Metaheuristic optimization techniques, including particle swarm optimization and gray wolf optimization, further enhance ANN performance by improving hyperparameter selection. Ensemble tree-based models such as random forest (RF) and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) also offer robustness and high predictive accuracy in environmental applications. Nevertheless, comprehensive comparisons between baseline and optimized ANN variants and ensemble models for vegetation trend prediction in mountainous Saudi Arabia’s semiarid mountainous environments remain limited.

Moreover, mismatches between coarse-resolution climate datasets and fine-resolution vegetation indices constrain local-scale analysis. Random forest–based downscaling of CHELSA bioclimatic variables provides a pathway for resolving climatic heterogeneity in complex terrain. Therefore, this study aims to (1) analyse long-term vegetation trends in the Bani Hasan mountainous region using remote sensing indices, (2) downscale CHELSA bioclimatic variables to high spatial resolution, and (3) compare the performance of neural network and ensemble machine-learning models for predicting NDVI trend patterns. This study addresses a key gap by systematically comparing baseline and optimized neural networks with ensemble models in a semiarid mountainous environment, where strong terrain-driven heterogeneity may limit the performance of simpler modeling approaches.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Bani Hasan mountainous subregion (20° N, 41° E) lies within Al Baha Province along the Sarawat Mountains in southwestern Saudi Arabia (Fig. 1). Covering ~ 128.85 km², the elevation ranges from 1183 to 2562 m, generating strong local gradients in temperature and precipitation. The climate is semiarid, with cooler, wetter highlands receiving 200–500 mm of annual rainfall compared with 100–250 mm at lower elevations30. Vegetation reflects this topographic control: Juniperus procera woodlands and mixed shrublands (> 80% cover) occur in moist uplands, whereas sparse xerophytic shrubs (< 20% cover) dominate valley bottoms31. Rising temperatures and shifting precipitation patterns have contributed to habitat degradation and altered vegetation dynamics in the Al Baha highlands32. Bani Hasan, which spans subtropical and warm temperate dry forest zones33, provides a heterogeneous landscape for analyzing vegetation–climate interactions and long-term ecological trends.

Landsat surface reflectance (SR)

Atmospherically corrected Landsat-5 TM, Landsat-7 ETM+, and Landsat-8 OLI imagery (1990–2024) were used38. The NDVI39, EVI40, NDWI41, and LST42 were derived annually via median composites to minimize noise and residual cloud contamination43.

Cross-sensor harmonization was applied using a regression-based bias correction approach to ensure temporal consistency across Landsat sensors. This approach accounts for differences in spectral response functions and ensures consistency of derived vegetation indices across the multi-sensor Landsat archive44.

Landsat-7 ETM+ scenes affected by the Scan Line Corrector (SLC-off) failure (post-2003) were corrected using a multi-temporal gap-filling approach, whereby missing pixels were reconstructed from temporally adjacent observations within the same seasonal window to preserve phenological consistency. Remaining data gaps were further mitigated through annual median compositing, resulting in spatially continuous datasets and reducing potential bias in long-term trend estimation.

Downscaling and change analysis of bioclimatic variables

CHELSA v2.1 BIO1–BIO19 variables (1981–2010; 2011–2040) were downscaled from ~ 1 km to 30 m via random forest regression45,46. The model was trained using elevation, slope, coordinates, and long-term NDVI as predictors, with CHELSA bioclimatic variables as the target. Training samples were generated from the coarse-resolution data. The models used 500 trees with out-of-bag validation47. Independent in-situ validation data were not available; therefore, model performance was evaluated using out-of-bag validation and statistical metrics (R² and RMSE).

Predictive modeling

The predictors included τ-based NDWI and LST trends and bioclimatic change variables (ΔBIO); the τ-based NDVI trend was the response. The features were standardized:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{i}^{{\prime\:}}=\frac{{x}_{i}-{\mu\:}_{x}}{{\sigma\:}_{x}}$$\end{document}

The dataset was split into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets.

A feed-forward ANN minimized the MSE with L2 regularization50–53.6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MSE}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\:_{i=1}^{n}({y}_{i}-{\widehat{y}}_{i}{)}^{2}$$\end{document}

Hyperparameters (hidden-layer size and decay) for the baseline ANN were optimized using 5-fold cross-validation over a predefined grid (3, 5, and 7 neurons; decay = 0.00, 0.01, and 0.03). Metaheuristic ANN variants (PSO and GWO) were applied to further optimize these parameters by minimizing cross-validated RMSE on a representative subsample of the training data (n = 4000) using 2-fold cross-validation, thereby enhancing model stability and reducing sensitivity to initialization54.

For the ensemble models, random forest (RF) models nonlinear interactions via bootstrap aggregation55 and was implemented using 500 trees, with the square root of the number of predictors considered at each split and a minimum node size of 5. XGBoost applies regularized gradient boosting with early stopping56,57 and was configured with a learning rate of 0.05, maximum tree depth of 6, subsampling ratio of 0.8, and column sampling ratio of 0.8.

Model performance was primarily evaluated on an independent test dataset using RMSE, MAE, and R² to ensure consistent comparison across all models. Bootstrap resampling was additionally applied to the ensemble models (RF and XGB) to quantify predictive uncertainty and assess model stability.

Integrated model comparison and selection

To ensure transparent comparison, multiple evaluation criteria were integrated into the final ranking: RMSE, MAE, R², residual standard deviation, residual IQR, residual Moran’s I, and the percentage of significant LISA clusters. All metrics were normalized using min–max scaling. Cost-type metrics (RMSE, MAE, residual standard deviation, residual IQR, Moran’s I, and significant LISA proportion) were inverted, whereas R² was treated as a benefit-type metric. The final composite score was calculated as a weighted sum of the normalized criteria, with weights assigned as follows: RMSE = 0.25, MAE = 0.20, R² = 0.20, residual standard deviation = 0.075, residual IQR = 0.075, residual Moran’s I = 0.10, and significant LISA proportion = 0.10. Higher composite scores indicate better overall model performance. The selected weights emphasize predictive accuracy while retaining spatial consistency, and preliminary testing indicated that moderate variations in weights did not alter the overall model ranking.

Results

Spatiotemporal trends in vegetation and land surface temperature (1990–2024)

Figure 3 summarizes the spatial magnitudes and monotonic trends of vegetation moisture and thermal conditions in Bani Hasan (1990–2024) via Sen’s slope and Kendall’s τ. Trend surfaces show strong spatial heterogeneity, with zones of increasing (EVI, NDWI) alongside areas where the NDWI decreases while the LST increases. Regionally, the annual mean NDVI shows a modest but significant greening of + 0.019 NDVI units per decade (τ = 0.25, p = 0.034). This signal is consistent across seasonal aggregates, with significant increases in the wet-season (+ 0.0179 per decade, τ = 0.25, p = 0.034) and dry-season (+ 0.0182 per decade, τ = 0.251, p = 0.0366) signals.

The spatial patterns indicate that positive trend in NDVI and NDWI is predominantly distributed across the central and southern parts of the study area and warming trends (positive LST slopes) are more pronounced in the southern and western regions, especially along exposed mountain and hill slopes. Areas exhibiting declining NDWI combined with increasing LST highlight localized zones of moisture stress, primarily in topographically constrained and lower-elevation regions.

Downscaled bioclimatic variables

Figures 4 and 5 present 30 m downscaled CHELSA bioclimatic variables produced via random forest regression via elevation, slope, long-term NDVI, and coordinates. The panel metrics indicate strong downscaling performance (R² = 0.85–0.98), with RMSE values expressed in native units (°C for temperature and mm for precipitation) ranging from 0.0004 to 9.39. (Table 2). The downscaled variables show clear spatial patterns, with temperature decreasing with elevation and precipitation exhibiting contrasts between windward and leeward slopes, reflecting strong orographic controls across the mountainous terrain. These results indicate that the approach captures the fine-scale climatic variability needed for vegetation modeling in complex terrains. The projected climate-change signals (Fig. 6) show coherent warming: BIO1 increases by 0.70 °C on average (0.34–1.00 °C), with increases in BIO10 (+ 0.50 °C), BIO8 (+ 0.62 °C), and BIO5 (+ 0.61 °C) and the strongest shift in BIO6 (+ 1.20 °C), suggesting pronounced cold-season warming. Thermal variability generally decreases (BIO2 − 0.26 °C; BIO4 ~ − 10; BIO7 − 0.59 °C), whereas BIO3 remains near zero. Precipitation increases overall, with BIO12 increasing by 35.44 mm (21.56–46.78 mm) and increasing across BIO13, BIO17, BIO18, and BIO19; BIO15 decreases by 1.15, indicating modestly reduced precipitation variability.

Discussion

Multisource geospatial inputs for vegetation change drivers

In this study, combining the NDVI, EVI, NDWI, LST, CHELSA-derived bioclimatic surfaces, and topographic predictors enabled the representation of interacting climatic, hydrological, and physiographic controls on vegetation change. Trend analysis via Sen’s slope and Kendall’s τ revealed strong spatial heterogeneity: zones with positive EVIs and NDWI values indicate greening and improved moisture availability, whereas a declining NDWI coupled with increasing LST suggests localized warming and moisture stress. The observed greening likely reflects a combination of natural climatic variability and potential anthropogenic influences; however, these relationships are based on statistical associations rather than explicit causal inference, and attribution remains limited due to the lack of detailed land-use and management data. Similar evidence from dryland ecosystems indicates that vegetation trends emerge from climate variability interacting with external pressures62. The downscaled CHELSA surfaces further captured orographic effects, including cooler conditions and enhanced precipitation at higher elevations36, which likely reduce evaporative demand and support higher productivity. The high downscaling performance is attributable to strong elevation control on climate variables in mountainous terrain, where orographic effects drive temperature and precipitation patterns, indicating a physically meaningful relationship rather than model overfitting.

predictive modeling performance and comparison of machine learning approaches

The baseline ANN showed strong predictive ability, which is consistent with prior work demonstrating ANN effectiveness for vegetation and ecohydrological modeling63,64. However, comparisons across metaheuristic-optimized ANN variants and ensemble models revealed differences in accuracy, robustness, and spatial behavior. ANN–PSO and ANN–GWO provided modest improvements over the baseline ANN, indicating that optimization can refine hyperparameter selection and reduce sensitivity to initialization. Tree-based ensembles offered additional advantages: RF produced stable predictions with reduced residual dispersion but tended to smooth subtle spatial gradients, contributing to slightly lower accuracy. XGBoost achieved the highest accuracy and lowest uncertainty, suggesting that its boosting framework better captures higher-order interactions among climatic, thermal, and vegetation predictors. Similar performance advantages of gradient boosting in vegetation–climate modeling have been reported elsewhere65,66.

Model robustness, uncertainty, and residual behavior

Model reliability is critical for ecological interpretation. Bootstrap uncertainty for RF and XGBoost showed spatially structured uncertainty, with lower uncertainty in more homogeneous areas and higher uncertainty in transitional zones. Higher uncertainty is primarily observed in transitional ecotones between montane woodlands and lower-elevation dry shrublands, where sharp gradients in elevation, moisture, and temperature lead to more variable vegetation responses. The superior performance of XGBoost likely reflects its ability to iteratively minimize prediction errors and capture complex nonlinear interactions and threshold effects in vegetation–environment relationships, particularly under strong elevation and moisture gradients. Residual diagnostics indicated that optimized ANNs and ensemble models reduced heteroscedasticity and residual spread relative to the baseline ANN, implying improved stability across the prediction range. Spatial autocorrelation analyses further revealed that although the observed NDVI trends were strongly clustered, the residual Moran’s I decreased for all the models, indicating that key spatial structures were captured. XGBoost produced the largest reduction in residual spatial autocorrelation and the fewest significant local clusters, supporting its superior spatial consistency for modeling structured vegetation processes.

Comparison with similar studies and ecological implications

The observed warming trends have important ecological implications, particularly in semiarid mountainous systems where temperature increases can elevate evapotranspiration and intensify moisture stress. Such conditions may shift species composition, constrain the distribution of moisture-sensitive species, and alter ecosystem resilience and productivity. However, the observed decrease in precipitation variability (BIO15) should be interpreted cautiously, as reduced variability does not necessarily imply improved water availability; changes in rainfall timing and intensity may still impose constraints on vegetation in water-limited environments.

The predictive modelling results align with broader evidence that vegetation change in semiarid systems reflects combined climate and human influences [67] and that ensemble learning can effectively predict the NDVI via bioclimatic and topographic predictors66. This study extends prior work by comparing baseline and optimized neural networks against ensemble models at high spatial resolution in a semiarid mountainous context. The strong performance of XGBoost suggests that boosting-based ensembles provide a practical balance of accuracy, robustness, and interpretability for operational vegetation monitoring and land management in drylands.

Limitations and future research directions

Key limitations include the absence of explicit socioeconomic and land-management drivers, which may explain localized dynamics. Future studies should incorporate such proxies to improve causal attribution. Vegetation responses may also be influenced by time-lagged climatic effects, which were not explicitly considered here and should be incorporated in future studies. Testing deep learning approaches (e.g., CNNs for spatial features and recurrent/LSTM models for temporal dependencies) may further enhance prediction and uncertainty characterization. Probabilistic and Bayesian frameworks could also strengthen decision support for ecosystem management in semiarid mountainous regions. The Google Earth comparison provides qualitative support only; the reported coordinates represent centroids of 700 m × 700 m areas, and future studies should incorporate systematic sampling and quantitative validation metrics.

Conclusions

This study provides a comparative evaluation of neural network variants and ensemble machine-learning models for predicting long-term vegetation trends in the semiarid mountainous environment of Bani Hasan, Saudi Arabia. By integrating the NDVI, NDWI, and LST trends with downscaled CHELSA bioclimatic variables, the framework enables consistent assessment of a baseline ANN, metaheuristic-optimized ANN variants (ANN–PSO, ANN–GWO), random forest (RF), and XGBoost (XGB). Metaheuristic optimization improved ANN generalization and reduced sensitivity to initialization, confirming the benefit of adaptive hyperparameter tuning in heterogeneous landscapes. The ensemble models demonstrated greater robustness overall. RF produced stable but comparatively smoother predictions, whereas XGBoost achieved the highest predictive accuracy, lowest uncertainty, and strongest reduction in residual spatial autocorrelation. Bootstrap analysis further revealed that XGBoost was more resilient to sampling variability than were the individual neural network models. Spatial diagnostics indicated that optimized and ensemble approaches captured dominant vegetation processes and substantially reduced residual spatial clustering. The proposed framework is transferable to other Sarawat Mountain subregions; however, local recalibration of downscaling and model parameters may be required to account for site-specific environmental variability. The findings highlight the importance of multisource geospatial integration and boosting-based ensemble learning for reliable vegetation monitoring and ecosystem assessment in semiarid mountainous regions. Based on the XGBoost map, conservation efforts should prioritize degradation hotspots.
