 16. Abstract
 This report examines the critical data exchanges between automated vehicle (AV) service providers and the cities and
 municipalities they serve. It assists municipal authorities in navigating the often complex and real-time digital data
 exchanges needed to support AV mobility services, with emphasis in three areas: (1) critical safety data for broad-
 area situational awareness of hazards typically associated emergency dispatch or roadway work zones; (2)
 performance metrics of AV services that inform the quantity, quality, spatial extents, and impact on the roadway
 network; and (3) regulatory and policy information, particularly dynamic information that governs how AV services
 interact with the roadway network, with emphasis on curb space. The report reviews existing practices and emerging
 protocols and standards and identifies key gaps to address moving forward.

Executive Summary
Data for Autonomous Transportation Awareness examines the critical data exchanges between
automated vehicle (AV) service providers and the cities and municipalities they serve,
documents current practice, and identifies critical gaps. Information in this report was obtained
through stakeholder engagement, standards and literature reviews, and assessment of parallel
and historical major technology innovations. This document is intended to assist municipal
authorities in understanding, planning, and navigating the complex and critical data exchanges
needed as AV services begin to scale across the United States.

The types of critical data exchanges are characterized into three areas:

 1. Critical safety data include hazard information that falls beyond the sensor capability of
 any AV sensor yet is critical for safe operations. Examples include fires, floods, riots, and
 active shooters. Such threats and hazards typically involve broad-area situational
 awareness typically associated with emergency dispatch functions that results in
 directives to the AV fleets of locations and times on the roadway network to avoid.
 2. Service performance characteristics of the AV services that reflect the quantity, quality,
 spatial extents, and impact on the roadway network. Such measures reflect both the
 contribution and impact of AV service providers to the overall transportation system.
 3. Regulatory and policy information, both static and dynamic, that governs how AV
 services interact with the roadway network and the built environment, and how they
 utilize curb space. Curb policy exemplifies the curb space component of this category,
 specifying where and when AVs may use roadside shoulders and curbs as safe pickup
 and drop-off areas. Another emerging critical area is detailed and timely construction
 and work zone information, particularly on high-speed facilities.

All these data areas are rapidly developing with various data standards and data services,
evolving quickly from information systems that served taxi, ride-hailing, and public transit fleets
to formats and means that are inherently digital, supporting computer-to-computer secured and
verified information exchanges to enable fully autonomous services. Previous systems, though
perhaps computer assisted, still relied on human interaction for day-to-day operations. However,
in AV operations, human oversight is reserved only for events and circumstances outside any
anticipated operational design domain. Standards such the Mobility Data Specification, Curb
Data Specification, and Work Zone Data Exchange are examples of emerging data schemas that
have been authored in recent years. These standards are currently in initial stages of testing,
demonstration, deployment, and adoption to meet the critical real-time information exchange
needs identified above.

The primary gap identified in current practice inhibiting the rapid scaling of AV mobility services
is an appropriate business model for the exchange of critical data between and among AV
operators and multiple municipal agents. This report proposes a new entity, referred to herein
as a real-time information broker (RTIB), that will be primarily responsible for the rapid, secure,
validated, and archived exchange of data between AV operators and multiple contributing
municipal authorities. The RTIB business model approach consolidates the burden of data
transaction to a single entity, enforces data integrity and security, and provides a means to

avoid conflict of interest in the information transaction process. The RTIB function creates a
data clearinghouse parallel in concept and function to the framework in the banking industry
known as the Automated Clearing House that facilitates check clearance between banks.
Similar to standard data schemas, examples of this business function are beginning to emerge
but require additional focus to enable rapid scaling of AV services.

AV operations represent one of the first technological services that is rapidly scaling in our
society that requires integrated, real-time, and verified data exchange across a variety of actors
to address safety, security, performance, and policy. This document identifies critical
information exchanges and points toward emerging data standards and data business
practices that are enabling operator-free information exchange. It outlines a framework for
cities and municipalities to facilitate collaboration, exchange, understanding, planning, and
deployment of data services to support current and future data services needed for robust AV
service operations.

Introduction
Context
After more than two decades of anticipation of automated vehicles (AVs) incubated by the
Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) Grand Challenge in 2004, AV
deployments have accelerated over the past couple of years, and operational AVs and AV
systems are poised for rapid growth over the coming decades. In particular, robotaxi
deployments are increasing rapidly in terms of areas served and number of rides, and they will
shape perception and acceptance as initial experience points for much of the public. As of late
2025, Waymo and/or Zoox deployments are underway or planned in 19 U.S. states (see Figure
1), with Waymo approaching 500,000 rides per week (see Figure 2). By the end of 2026, Waymo
expects to provide more than 1 million trips per week [1]. Sundar Pichai, CEO of Alphabet
(Waymo’s parent company), recently commented that Waymo is focused on “scaling up pretty
aggressively” [2].

Figure 1: Waymo and Zoox deployments as of late 2025

Although rapidly scaling with observed doubling rates between 3 and 9 months throughout
2024 and 2025 (Figure 2), the total volume of AV service trips is only in its infancy compared to
other modes such as New York City’s subway (3.2 million trips per day), U.S. airline trips (2.9
million trips per day), or even ride-hail trips (approaching 20 million daily trips in the United
States). At sustained 6-month doubling periods (quadrupling every year), AV robotaxi fleets will
only begin to approach current ride-hailing volumes in 2030, and if the doubling period for
robotaxi operations is 12 months, then it will take until 2035 to intersect. Ride-hailing itself has
been growing at a year-over-year rate at approximately 20% [3], pushing the point at which AV
operational parity reaches that of ride-hailing overall even further into the future.

 Regardless of the timeline for the expansion of AV operations, the rapid and increasingly
 coinciding growth of these and other on-demand mobility systems underscores the need for
 coordination between AV providers and the agencies responsible for managing infrastructure
 and responding to emergencies where AVs operate. Advances in the quality, security, efficiency,
 and timeliness of these communications will also provide added operational benefits to
 traditional transportation modes through improved routing and traffic and emergency response
 condition awareness. Municipal agencies need to communicate basic information to AVs, such
 as areas to avoid or fee-related information, through a digital information exchange. The
 exchange of information can tap into existing data and information streams. This is particularly
 true for periodic reporting and static policy and regulatory information. For example, a condition
 of operation required by many states is that AV companies report performance data on safety
 and the extent, frequency, and utilization of the public roadway network. Static regulatory and
 policy information (e.g., speed limits, curb policy) is also sufficiently conveyed with existing
 systems so as to allow the current rapid expansion of service. However, the quantity and
 velocity of data required to support scaling of real-time AV services either stresses or exceeds
 the capability of existing systems, particularly for real-time safety- and hazard-related data, as
 well as dynamic policy and regulatory information. To date, such coordination has occurred on
 a fragmented and limited basis.

Objectives
This research effort seeks to identify and characterize the need for data exchange among AV
operators and public agencies (including emergency responders), summarize the current state
of the practice, and identify barriers and gaps to data sharing/exchange as well as emerging
standards and practices.

The operational deployment and rapid scaling of AV fleets to serve public mobility requires
expertise and knowledge well beyond the technical boundaries of automated driving systems
(ADS) of the vehicle. Robotaxi operations must integrate, leverage, and conform to the
operational roadway and curbside environment, respond appropriately to wide-area
environmental and societal hazards, and, in real time, exchange and coordinate with multiple
agencies responsible for emergency response, health and human safety, and regulation of the
built environment. These are not new functions or completely new information exchanges but
have their roots in nondigital (typically analog and voice) communication systems that support
traditional taxi, ride-hailing, and public transit services, and even driver information systems.
Critical communication functions have precedence in systems primarily supporting manually
operated systems of conveyance in which the human driver is a critical link in such information
exchanges, typically through some type of audio alert or communication. Current data exchange
systems may use computer-assisted technologies but rely heavily on human-prompted or
human-in-the-loop methodologies. These legacy methods and systems are challenged by real-
time data needs, machine readability, and scaling requirements of emerging AV mobility
services. Although these critical communications needs are presented primarily in the context
of initial emergence of automated ride-hailing fleets (or robotaxis), the same principles apply to
other emerging forms of mobility automation such as for local delivery, freight, public transit,
and even automated personal vehicles. Without the human in the loop, any form of automated
conveyance on our public roadway system will require similar critical information exchanges as
discussed herein.

The objective of this document is to:

 • Identify and characterize the types of critical information exchange between AV
 operators and various municipal actors for a city in which the AV operations occur.
 • Identify emerging data schemas and data practices designed to convey critical
 information in a standardized and scalable context.
 • Identify any critical gaps to produce information systems to supply these critical data
 exchanges.

Methodology
To carry out the research, the team performed four interrelated tasks: (1) policy review, (2)
informal literature review, (3) stakeholder interviews, and (4) data standards and practice
review.

Policy Review
Policies governing AV operations have been under development across the United States for
many years, with no signs of slowing. Even as this document is being written, federal authorities
are hosting hearings to gather information and considering unifying a patchwork of state
policies to create a unifying national framework for the safe and rapid deployment of AVs to
hasten both their safety and economic benefits. According to the National Conference of State
Legislatures’ Automated Vehicle Legislation Database, 70 bills from 25 states were either
adopted or proposed in 2025 [4]. In total, 745 bills pertaining to AVs are included in the
database, but the majority of these were never adopted. Legislation often pertains to AV testing
and permitting processes; very few address the operational communication or data exchange
issues between AV operators and municipal authorities. To identify policies with relevance to
the data exchange topic, searches using the following keywords were performed: data,
emergency, automated vehicles, AV systems.

The relevant policies are summarized in the following sections, with an emphasis on aspects
related to information exchange and emergency response. Most state regulations concentrate
on incident reporting and manual interaction protocols (e.g., what to do when a police officer
encounters an AV and must interact with it). Such topics are common to most state AV
regulations and are out of scope with respect to critical information exchange as discussed
herein. The most relevant regulations and practices come from states with current AV
operations: California, Arizona, and Texas.

California
California has perhaps the most comprehensive set of AV regulations among U.S. states. Its
department of motor vehicles is responsible for developing AV regulations in response to state
legislation. Currently adopted regulations focus on AV testing and deployment. Revisions have
been proposed, and a rulemaking process is underway, with several new provisions related to
emergency response situations. Key aspects of the current and proposed regulations are noted
in this section.

Current Department of Motor Vehicles AV Testing and Deployment Regulations [5]

The operational design domain (ODD) is central to the concept of AV operations and is highly
relevant to the discussion about data exchange. California defines it as follows: “the specific
operating domain(s) in which an automated function or system is designed to properly operate,
including but not limited to geographic area, roadway type, speed range, environmental
conditions (weather, daytime/nighttime, etc.), and other domain constraints” [5].

To perform driverless testing, the manufacturer must submit details such as the ODD, public
roads where testing will occur, and days and times when testing will occur. The manufacturer
must also certify that vehicles are incapable of operating in autonomous mode outside the ODD
and are able to identify ODD-restricted conditions such as snow, fog, black ice, wet road
surfaces, construction zones, and geofencing by location or road type.

The AV manufacturer must certify that the autonomous technology can detect and respond to
roadway situations in compliance with California Vehicle Code and location regulations
applicable to the ODD. Additionally, manufacturers must submit a law enforcement interaction
plan, which details how law enforcement and emergency responders should interact with AVs in
emergency situations. This includes how to communicate with a remote operator of a vehicle,
with an emphasis on interactions during an incident. Collisions involving an AV must be
reported within 10 days. Provisions such as those described above are common to most states
that have passed AV regulations.

Draft Regulations

California Department of Motor Vehicles’ regulations surrounding testing and deployment of
AVs were made effective in 2021 and 2022, respectively. Since then, updates to the regulations
have been proposed and are now under review through the rulemaking process [6]. Notably, the
revisions to the AV regulations include important provisions related to AV operations during
emergency incidents. For example, the draft adds definitions for the following terms:

 • Alternate route: A temporary routing initiated due to an emergent situation and at the
 discretion of the California Highway Patrol, California Department of Transportation
 (Caltrans), or applicable local authorities, which may result in routing not otherwise legal
 for certain size combination vehicles, and which exists only for the duration of the
 incident or closure of the regular legal route.
 • Avoidance area: A defined location identified by emergency response officials through
 an emergency geofencing message, from which a manufacturer must issue directions to
 its fleet of autonomous vehicles to leave or avoid for the initial duration provided by the
 emergency response official or for an extended duration, when specified by the
 emergency response official.
 • Detour: A designated route established by a local public agency or the Caltrans for the
 express purpose of routing traffic around a temporary closure of a street, as defined by
 California Vehicle Code Section 590, for any purpose.
 • Emergency geofencing message: A message using commonly available communication
 methods to identify a location using a street address, intersection, coordinates, or any
 other reasonable and customary way of identifying a location that directs an
 autonomous vehicle to leave or avoid an area because of an emergency.

Additionally, the definition for ODD is modified as follows:

 • ODD: The operating conditions under which a given ADS or feature thereof is specifically
 designed to function, including but not limited to environmental, geographic, and time-of-
 day restrictions; and/or the requisite presence or absence of certain traffic or roadway
 characteristics.

The draft regulations additionally specify that for vehicles 10,000 pounds or less, emergency
response telephone lines must be made available and staffed to ensure calls are answered
within 30 seconds. These phone lines must connect to remote operations support who can
operate the vehicle as directed by emergency response officials.

California Autonomous Vehicle Geofencing Law, Section 38751

In September 2024, California amended its vehicle code to require AV manufacturers to be
responsive to geofencing messages [7]. This requirement goes into effect in July 2026 and
includes the following key definitions and provisions:

 • Emergency geofencing message: A message delivered using commonly available
 communication methods to identify a location using a street address, intersection,
 coordinates, or any other reasonable and customary way of identifying a location that
 directs an autonomous vehicle to leave or avoid an area because of an emergency.
 • Emergency: A sudden, unexpected occurrence demanding immediate action to prevent
 or mitigate loss or damage to life, health, property, or essential public services.
 • Within 2 minutes of receiving an emergency geofencing message, a manufacturer shall
 issue direction to its fleet to leave or avoid the area identified.
 • Within 30 business days of receiving a notice that an emergency response official
 wishes to begin issuing emergency geofencing messages, a manufacturer shall provide
 the emergency response official with all information necessary for the emergency
 response official to begin issuing and for the manufacturer to receive and respond to
 emergency geofencing messages.
 • An emergency geofencing message shall include the initial duration for the defined
 avoidance area based on a reasonable assessment of the emergency. Where necessary,
 an emergency response official may extend the duration for the avoidance area.

Arizona
Arizona’s regulatory structure was initiated through gubernatorial order, allowing for testing and
operation on public roadways, provided the vehicle can achieve a “minimum risk condition” in
the event of a system failure, similar to many other state statutes. In 2026, state legislative
statute expanded the regulatory framework, establishing that the Arizona Department of
Transportation evaluate and approve the ODD and service areas of AV operators [8]. With
respect to communications, regulations with Arizona relations are limited to the interaction
protocols with emergency services, with policy similar to that of Texas (as per below).

The 2026 legislation begins to address additional reporting and communications, including
detailed information of the AV state and behavior preceding any collision, reports of any AV
disengagements, “near-miss event” data, and any sensor failures or processing errors. Of note
is the Arizona Statewide Communication Interoperability Plan, established in 2019, which
addresses issues of emergency communications interoperability but under the premise of a
human in the loop, well before the introduction of AV services in the Phoenix region. Although
the documents address thematically similar hazard and emergency-critical communications, as
is the focus of this report, it did not address or anticipate the large-scale operations of AV fleets
and the need to communicate in real time in a wholly digital format.

Texas
Texas established a legal framework for AVs beginning in 2017 with periodic updates (the latest
being in 2026, establishing the AV permitting process) [9, 10]. AV regulations are centralized at
the state level (similar to California) to provide a harmonized policy framework for the state. As
with most state legislation, the framework deals primarily with safety of the ADS, requiring
compliance with Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards. Required communications within the
Texas policy mandate that the AV operator submit a plan to the Texas Department of Public
Safety defining how the vehicle interacts with police, fire, and medical responders, including
how emergency personnel can safely disable the vehicle, establish communications with a
remote operator, and access vehicle data.

Data requirements deal mainly with crash scenarios, requiring AVs to be equipped with a
recording device (similar to a flight data recorder) in order to archive and convey any data to the
authorities in the event of a collision. AV operators are required to notify the state of any
collision within 48 hours. Although there are active initiatives related to connected vehicle
architectures that could impact AV operation in the future, real-time critical data exchanges are
not currently addressed by Texas regulation.

Other States
A sample review of other states’ AV regulations (most of which do not have active AV
deployments) shows similar progression of regulatory approach. Initial regulation addresses
safety with respect to known standards (typically Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards),
establishes protocols for communication between the AV operator and emergency response
personnel in the event of a collision, establishes reporting requirements for collisions, and
defines design domains. The need for systematic, continuous, real-time data exchange is
becoming increasingly apparent from early operations in California, Arizona, Texas, and most
recently Georgia. Experience and subsequent policy and regulations from states wrestling with
such issues will set precedent for states that follow.

Media Analysis
The deployment of AVs on public roadways is covered in the popular media on a regular basis.
Numerous articles and media reports describing situations where AVs failed to effectively
navigate unpredictable circumstances were found as part of the literature review. The articles
cover instances where AVs encountered floods, riots, DUI checkpoints, construction zones, and
other unpredictable scenarios that may be considered to be outside the AV’s ODD. The
following examples highlight a few of these scenarios.

 • In December 2024, a Waymo vehicle entered a construction zone at Laguna Honda
 Hospital, driving through wet cement in the process [11].
 • In June 2025, several Waymo vehicles were set on fire during protests in Los Angeles.
 According to Electrek, the vehicles were purposely called to the area before being
 vandalized [12]. In a separate incident, passengers set fire to the backseat of a Waymo
 vehicle [13]. While no injuries were reported in these events, Waymo undoubtedly lost
 some capital and revenue as a result.

 • In September 2025, the Phoenix area received significant rainfall, causing roadways to
 flood. Waymo vehicles were observed to be stalled in flooded roadways, as reported by
 ABC15 [14]. Waymo subsequently suspended its operations during the weather event.
 • The San Francisco Chronicle reported in September 2025 that a Waymo vehicle
 performed an illegal U-turn at a DUI checkpoint in Bruno, California [15]. The vehicle was
 pulled over by law enforcement.
 • In September 2025, reports from Atlanta began identifying Waymo vehicles that “failed
 to remain stopped when approaching a school bus that was stopped with its red lights
 flashing, stop arm deployed, and crossing control arm deployed” [16].
 • In December 2025, while officers were conducting a high-risk felony arrest after a stolen
 vehicle pursuit at around 3:40 a.m., a Waymo vehicle drove through an intersection,
 according to the Los Angeles Police Department [17].
 • In December 2025, San Francisco experienced a prolonged power outage that left
 roughly 130,00 residents without electricity, causing streetlights and traffic lights to go
 dark. Waymo’s self-driving vehicles had a “very rough time coping,” as evidenced by
 several videos and photos posted on social media showing Waymo vehicles “sitting still
 in intersections, with their hazard lights on, apparently unsure how to cross areas that
 had previously had functioning streetlights” [18].

These are but a sampling of the numerous recent instances of AVs that have encountered
circumstances outside of their ODD for which either loss of time and resources, or loss of
capital investment has resulted, but thankfully no loss to human life has occurred to date. The
final instance was a reported failure of the ADS to appropriately respond to a common
occurrence on the roadway. Hazardous situations that arise within the defined operating areas
and roadways (referred herein as the geo-network or geonet) may be areawide or location
specific, and the hazard is typically not able to be sensed using the AV sensor capabilities but
represents a hazard outside the anticipated ODD of the automated vehicle. In addition to the
examples above, other examples may include human induced hazards such as active shooters,
other weather events such as tornados and hurricanes, and other natural disasters such as
forest fires, just to name a few. In manually operated taxi, ride-hailing, or other types of human
driven modes, the driver is typically a critical link in receiving, comprehending, and acting upon
hazards of this nature. Within an AV fleet with a human driver or attendant, it is paramount that
municipal authorities communicate these hazardous situations rapidly, securely, and
automatically to AV operations staff and/or devices to appropriately direct the AV fleet for
overall safety and security of the traveling public.

Additional Literature
Communication of policy and legislation, particularly for existing, static aspects of the traffic
code, primarily speed limits, was the subject of a recent FHWA technical report, titled Automated
Driving Systems Operational Behavior and Traffic Regulations Information: Model Plan [19]. This
model plan addresses the challenges of establishing an inherently digital database of traffic
laws and regulations, as well as methods for sharing information within the ADS ecosystem.
The plan is designed for collaboration involving infrastructure owner-operators (IOOs), traffic
regulation and code practitioners, ADS practitioners, software developers, and Government
agencies. However, digital communication of static traffic regulations has proven not to be a
large barrier to the deployment of AV services such as robotaxis, as existing commercial maps

as well as AI image perception to read regulatory signs has proven sufficient to inform of speed
limits and other static traffic laws to enable the existing deployment and growth of services.

Another ongoing effort by the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), for
which a notice of proposed rulemaking is currently active, is called the ADS-equipped Vehicle
Safety, Transparency, and Evaluation Program (AV STEP) [20]: “This initiative would establish a
national voluntary program for ADS-equipped vehicles that operate or may operate on public
roads in the United States under NHTSA's oversight with the goal of improving public
transparency related to the safety of certain ADS-equipped vehicles, while allowing for
responsible development of this technology. This proposal includes procedures for application,
participation, public reporting, and program administration.” Similar to most state level policy
and regulation, AV STEP addresses primarily the safety of the ADS, requiring participants to be
more transparent with respect to the functionality of the ADS system and to submit safety
cases that demonstrate its functionality in various scenarios. It does not, however, address
critical information exchange on an ongoing basis—particularly with respect to transient
hazards and other concurrent emergency and police operations.

Data Standards Review
Mobility Data Specification
MDS is an open-source standard by OMF that defines APIs enabling two-way communication
between cities and shared mobility providers. Various versions of the MDS have been released
with each subsequent version expanding the data standardization and exchange between cities
and mobility providers (Figure 4).

Core components:

 1. Provider API: Enables mobility companies to share data like device locations, status
 updates, trip history to cities. A city queries a provider’s API endpoints when it wants to

 2. Policy API: Enables cities to digitally define local rules (e.g., geofencing, work zones,
 operational restrictions)
 3. Agency API: Enables the mobility provider to post events/trips directly into the city’s

 must notify the city’s Agency API. The provider is in control and must send events to the
 city whenever they occur.
 4. Geography API: GeoJSON areas that policies refer to (parking areas, slow zones,

Work Zone Data Exchange
A connected work zone (CWZ) is defined as a set of technologies that generates or collects
work zone information (either automatically or manually), as well as the infrastructure that
broadcasts/distributes this information with the aim of ensuring work zone data are consistent
and interoperable to be shared with the public, navigation companies, and AVs. The CWZ data
exchange is based on two complementary standards: WZDx and SAE J2735. WZDx has already
been developed, and the latest version published in February 2023, WZDx v4.2, is currently in
use by IOOs to provide machine-readable work zone data in the form of GeoJSON-based feeds.
CWZ establishes the interoperability framework that leverages the WZDx specification and turns
it into a formally defined end-to-end standard, as well as the governance and implementation
guidance necessary to support scaled deployments of CWZs.

Key standards in the connected work zone:

 1. WZDx: National data exchange specifications for work zones and smart devices.

 20

 2. SAE J2735 Traveler Information Message: Vehicle-to-everything message set used to
 broadcast work zone information.

CWZ data sharing stack:

 1. Field devices and systems create data (e.g., cones, sensors, lane closures).
 2. WZDx GeoJSON feeds publish events/devices.
 3. Consumers (e.g., original equipment manufacturers, mapping, TMC tools) ingest data
 feeds.
 4. Vehicle-to-everything broadcast messages (e.g., Traveler Information Message) or apps
 deliver warnings in the vehicle.

Whereas WZDx is a data exchange specification, CWZ addresses the message sharing stack.
CWZ is an emerging framework, whereas WZDx has precedent in various demonstrations and
deployments. Note that common concerns raised with work zone data are accuracy and
timeliness. Verification/validation or confidence or quality scoring has yet to be addressed in
the WZDx specification or CWZ framework and remains a gap in critical information exchange.

Also noted is that many forms of minor roadway maintenance, particularly on lower-volume and
lower-class roadways, will have limited information with respect to work zones. An IOO may
issue a work permit for such items as striping, pavement repair, or signage maintenance
providing only an allowed date range (e.g., 90 days), with the exact date and time left to the
contractor.

Theme 1: Real-Time Operational Data is a High Priority with Near-Term
Opportunities
Interviewees agreed that information that contributes to the ability of vehicles and mobility
services to safely navigate changing conditions in a city is critical. This critical data exchange
from the IOO and emergency response to AV operators includes construction/work zone
information, emergency conflict zones, major events or dynamic disruptions and curb

 22

availability. Seattle's Digital Conflict Area Awareness Management Program (dCAAMP) is one
example that is taking information related to emergency/911 calls for emergency conflict zones
and converting them into a format mobility providers can access in near real time in a native
digital format with the goal of improving operational decisions in real time (noting that Seattle
does not yet have active AV robotaxi deployments) [21]. Even before broader data exchange
between AVs and cities matures, cities can make immediate gains in safety and efficiency by
standardizing and automating disruption messaging (emergency conflict zones derived from
emergency dispatch). Policies such as California’s AV geofencing message law establish a
legal foundation for this type of data exchange between IOOs and AV operators. The dCAAMP
initiative is an early example of a data clearinghouse for one dimension of critical information
exchange—that of emergency conflict zones. A scalable, robust method of critical data
exchange spanning all the needed dimensions is discussed as a key gap in data exchange
between IOOs and AV operators.

Autonomous freight interests as discussed at the Texas Autonomous Trucking Research
Symposium hosted in Austin, Texas, in December 2025 emphasized the need for the active and
accurate sharing of construction and work zone data. Although this has been voiced before in
various forums with respect to the need for such data in a timely and consistent fashion, it is
typically with respect to travel information and the need to provide navigation apps such as
Waze, Google Maps and Apple Maps with information to convey to users. The symposium,
which was attended by the majority of AV freight developers, jointly put additional emphasis on
the need for such data on high-speed interstate roadways where the majority of long-haul
freight travels. An unexpected change in traffic patterns at high speed is more than a navigation
or a routing efficiency issue, but rather a core safety concern due to the high speeds and limited
time for AVs to respond if work zone conditions unexpectedly arise.

Theme 2: Standards are Key to Going From “Pilots” to “Systems”
AV companies need interoperability across jurisdictions for scalable deployment, and cities
need a scalable way to communicate policy and operations. Open Mobility Foundation (OMF)
Mobility Data Specification (MDS) is one of the data sharing standards that is being developed
as a “digital language” for policy and operations. Los Angeles has used MDS for data exchange
between mobility operators (non-AV) and IOOs, allowing for geofencing policies and event-
based messages to be communicated. Another standard maintained by OMF is the Curb Data
Specification (CDS), which provides a machine-readable format for both static curb regulations
as well as those that may vary over time of day or for varying conditions. CDS, particularly for
static curb regulations, has gained market traction with several vendors supporting the CDS for
smart city products that digitally encode existing curb infrastructure and policy—particularly for
relatively static information. Examples of dynamic curb data are limited with the
aforementioned dCAAMP initiative being the most prominent. One of the major barriers is that
data specifications such as MDS and CDS have been deployed on a city-by-city basis, whereas
scalable implementation would require broader alignment potentially at a regional or state level.

For work zones, the U.S. Department of Transportation-defined Work Zone Data Exchange
(WZDx) was originally developed for standardized information exchange to improve traffic
management around often complex work zone activities during major construction. Targeted
applications include automating traveler information such as 511 message alerts and
communication between IOOs and commercial navigation companies (e.g., Waze, Google). The
WZDx standard could also be utilized to share work zone information with AV companies.

The above three examples of MDS, CDS, and WZDx provide a data schema standardization path
for the exchange of information. However, all three are just beginning to proliferate, evolving
from demonstrations to deployed systems for communication between IOOs and AV operations
in specific geographic regions. At this stage, the level of consistency and interoperability of
these systems developed using these standards is not known (i.e., how consistently these
standards have been implemented from city to city). Standard schemas provide the scalable
framework to enable repeatable, enforceable, and scalable transfer of data and are an important
aspect for large-scale deployment of AVs. While these standards are already established to
facilitate data sharing, questions of data reliability, accuracy, and validation (and any associated
liability if incorrect) have yet to be addressed. Such concerns may arise as AV operators begin
to utilize this data into their daily operations.

Theme 3: Data Sharing from AVs To IOOs is Recognized as Valuable Yet
Underutilized
Interviewees consistently expressed that data from AV operators can be useful to
municipalities. However, it was acknowledged that there is uncertainty as to specifically what
data municipalities desire from AVs and how this data would be used. AV companies have been
reluctant to share data, and current data sharing requirements are based on regulations (e.g.,
permitting applications or NHTSA reporting for crashes) rather than collaboration for enhanced
operations. AV operators may consider certain data (for instance, high fidelity sensing) as an
asset that could be sold to third-party companies rather than shared as a public good. Without a
clear distinction on what data the city would require for operation purposes, how it would be
utilized in their municipal workflows, with whom it would be shared, and how data sharing and
dissemination policies would be enforced, data sharing requests from municipalities to AV
companies are typically not effective.

The California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) reporting requirements for AVs for
passenger-carrying service is the primary example of data flow from AVs to IOOs. These data
are required to be submitted by AV operators once the number of trips exceeds 300 per quarter.
CPUC and Caltrans work together to provide permission for AV operators to use California
public roadways, and the operators in exchange are required to submit data, which CPUC and
Caltrans use to monitor and enforce safety and accessibility of public services. A heavily
redacted version of the data is also provided to the public through a portal on the CPUC
website. The richest vein of data lies in the monthly aggregate data reflecting the quantity of
trips, as well as average time and vehicle miles traveled (VMT). These data reveal cumulative
miles traveled in three different periods defined as P1 (often referred to as deadheading miles
in which a vehicle is waiting or being repositioned in anticipation of a ride request), P2 (the time
and distance traveled once dispatched to pick up a ride), and P3 (the time and distance with a
passenger on board). Figure 3 provides a graphical representation of this vehicle miles travelled
(VMT) for each period P1, P2 and P3 as defined above. Monthly aggregates of the proportion of
time and miles in these three categories reveals both impact on the roadway system as well as
the relative efficiency of the AV fleet to service requests.

Theme 4: Curb Management is Critical for Cities to Manage Multiple
Mobility Services
The curb has emerged as one of the most contested and operationally complex elements of the
urban transportation system and is the primary interface where AVs, delivery services, ride-
hailing companies, microtransit, traditional public transit, freight, and micromobility services
converge. As mobility services diversify and on-demand activity increases, curb space is
becoming a critical bottleneck affecting safety, congestion, accessibility, and system efficiency.

Unlike many other components of the transportation network, curb regulation and policy are
typically under direct municipal control. This gives cities both significant responsibility and an
opportunity to shape outcomes. Seattle, for example, identified curb space management as
both a key constraint and a strategic opportunity. As part of this work, the city is transforming
existing curb inventory data into a standardized digital format to improve clarity, enforcement,
and interoperability with emerging mobility technologies.

One priority area is the exploration of deploying sensors and digital tools to compensate for
limited parking enforcement resources. With growing demand and dynamic curb uses (e.g.,
time-of-day loading zones, pickup/drop-off areas, freight access), traditional enforcement
methods are increasingly insufficient.

 25

OMF similarly emphasized the difficulty associated with managing dynamic curb space. In
response, cities across the United States are accelerating the digitization of curb space. OMF’s
Curb Data Specification provides a structured framework for converting analog curb signage
into rules and machine-readable data. By standardizing how curb regulations are presented
digitally, CDS reduces ambiguity for human users while enabling consistent interpretation by
automated systems, including AVs. This also creates opportunities for automated monitoring,
dynamic pricing, and more efficient enforcement.

The current state of digital curb management in the United States reflects a shift from static
signage and manual enforcement toward data-driven, sensor-enabled, and policy-integrated
systems. Several cities (including Pittsburgh, Los Angeles, and Santa Monica) have deployed
Automated License Plate Recognition and computer vision technologies to monitor curb
activity, support smart or zero-emission loading zones, and/or enable data-informed pricing and
enforcement strategies. In Pittsburgh, for example, the transition from pilot to permanent Smart
Loading Zones, supported by automated citation-by-mail enforcement, resulted in a reported
95% reduction in double parking and measurable improvements in traffic flow. These
deployments demonstrate that digital curb management is moving beyond experimentation
toward operational integration, though challenges remain related to enforcement authority,
privacy concerns, interoperability, and public acceptance.

Emerging Need for Real-Time Information Brokers
The data standards cited above address standardization of data schemas but do not address
the complexity of the business arrangements and rules for supply and dissemination of such
data. A critical gap was exposed during stakeholder engagement with respect to a sustainable
business model of information collection and dissemination to support critical data exchange
between IOOs and AV operations. What is the business model for this information exchange? Is
it the responsibility of the IOO to establish, fund, and maintain the critical information links? Is it
the burden of the AV operator? In this new “data economy,” how is this function provided,
maintained, and funded? Data can be generated in standard formats (as previously described),
but how is it discovered, updated, and utilized? Without the recognition of the criticality of the
data exchange process and the need to address it in a systematic, prioritized method (as
opposed to being subjugated to secondary or tertiary objectives of existing processes), it makes
it difficult to put into operational use.

The data economy with sources and consumers of information is analogous to the fiscal
economy in which businesses, individuals, and financial institutions exchange funds, hold funds,
and provide credit and debit instruments. In the fiscal economy, the Federal Reserve began
operating an electronic Automated Clearing House (ACH) system in the 1970s. ACH is a
nationwide electronic network used for the batch processing of financial transactions [22]. It
acts as a middleman between banks, allowing them to “clear” and “settle” electronic credits
(like direct deposits) and debits (like bill payments) without using paper checks. The ACH does
not hold funds, issue debits, or credit funds. It serves only as an electronic intermediary
between actors in the financial economy for efficiency and scale (at the time, the practice of
clearing paper checks was creating a bottleneck to financial growth) [23]. Comparably, in the
data economy there are creators and consumers of crucial data (AV operators, IOOs,
emergency response [911], fire, police, traffic management, policy, and regulators). The

 26

Data for Autonomous Transportation Awareness | Data Exchange Use Cases, Standards, and Barriers

individual, one-to-one, manual exchange of information between any two actors, though
possible, creates an inefficiency, or bottleneck, to scaling. A business entity, analogous to the
fiscal ACH, is needed to act as a “data clearinghouse”: an entity who neither sources nor
consumes data, but whose sole responsibility is the efficient, timely and secure transaction of
data. This emerged as a key principle for critical data exchanges of various types.

The Seattle dCAAMP initiative is an example of a data clearinghouse, or real-time information
broker, as described for one type of data—that of emergency conflict zones. The full scope of
function for a real-time information broker (RTIB) includes:

 • Securely ingest data: Ingested data may come from police, fire, 911, traffic
 management centers, departments of transportation, and construction contractors
 (related to work zone information). It may even be data from AV operators with respect
 to performance or in-vehicle emergencies.
 • Normalize, filter, and validate: For all data input, perform quality control and quality
 assurance (QC/QA) checks, eliminate or flag any suspect data, and (where feasible)
 validate to known reference or ground truth. This may also include assigning a
 confidence or accuracy score that is proportional to relative agreement among multiple
 data sources, or validation against reference data. Lastly, normalize data to standard
 data schema specifications.
 • Dissemination and conformation of data transmittal: Securely distribute data to
 authorized actors—such as distributing critical safety information and policy and
 regulation to AVs, or performance data to IOOs. This last area encompasses verification
 of data receipt, an independent log or archive of the transmission to support after
 action or incident investigation.

This business function, RTIB, is identified as a key gap that is essential to enabling AV
proliferation by way of facilitating fully digital exchange of critical data. Although real-time
information brokering may be provided by the IOO or by the AV operator, it can also be provided
by an independent business agent, similar in concept to the ACH for fiscal transactions,
specializing in the secure relay of critical digital data as described above. An independent
agent can also provide protection/immunity from conflicts of interest, meaning that any
advantages of controlling the data exchange process (either by the IOO or AV operator) may be
neutralized though an independent agent.

Progress toward this concept is seen in initiatives in Europe and the United States.

In Europe, the Traveler Information Services Association (TISA) plays a critical role in
harmonizing the deployment of Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) [24]. The TISA
standards are the technical backbone for the European Union's regulatory framework regarding
National Access Points (NAPs) that support real-time traffic information. NAPs are mandated
by the European Union's (EU) ITS Directive [25] and subsequent regulations [26, 27]. NAPs are
digital interfaces where transport data (static, dynamic, and real-time) is made available for
exchange and reuse. The directive and subsequent regulations establish the specifications for
EU-wide multimodal travel information services, requiring member states to set up NAPs for
access to static and dynamic travel data. The latest regulations established EU-wide real-time
traffic information (RTTI) services, mandating that data holders make specific data types (like
work zone and speed limits) accessible through the NAPs.

The most recent initiative from TISA establishes a rating system for the data quality submitted
to NAPs, referred to as the RTTI 5-Star Rating specification [28]. This initiative addressed the
long-standing concern of data quality provided by road authorities (via NAPs) and the quality
requirements of private service providers (e.g., Google Maps, Waze, TomTom, HERE). In
essence, it puts a quality assessment and rating system on data provided by IOOs for
dissemination to industry users. Although limited (currently) in content with respect to
construction activities, work zones, and static speed limits, the framework of NAPs and 5-Star
Rating specification parallels the RTIB functional specifications. The combination of NAPs
along with RTTI 5-Star Rating system acts as a “service-level agreement alternative,” allowing
public and private sectors to exchange data to known levels of fidelity.

Within the United States, the initiative to make transportation roadway level data available in a
native digital format (somewhat analogous to NAPs in Europe) with respect to dynamic
roadway conditions can be seen at a state-by-state level by IOOs. Some examples include:

 • Pennsylvania Department of Transportation (PennDOT): PennDOT makes available
 through API links various real-time datasets including the real-time information for
 traffic incidents, roadwork, winter road conditions, and other events, as well as real-time
 streaming video images from over 950 traffic cameras [29].
 • New Jersey and New York departments of transportation: New Jersey Department of
 Transportation and New York State Department of Transportation participate in the
 Transportation Operations Coordinating Committee (branded TRANSCOM) and make
 real-time event and link (travel time) data available for various applications [30].
 • Virginia Department of Transportation: The Virginia RM3P Data-Exchange Platform is a
 continuously updated, cloud-based data storage and exchange system. It is used by
 regional partners and third-party providers to capture, process, and exchange
 information on real-time and historic multimodal travel conditions [31].

These are only three examples that reflect the state of emerging practice where available real-
time traffic data are made available in a digital format. However, each of these interfaces is
different, requiring custom integration (no overarching standards from state to state) as well as
application and approval to each unique portal, with no known data standardization, quality
assessment, or association ratings.

Discussion
Current AV Data Exchange Landscape and Barriers to AV
Data Sharing
The current data sharing state of practice is highly fragmented with multiple data sharing
standards, lack of national-scale data sharing policy, differences in data sharing and reporting
requirements between different states, and skepticism around municipal data quality leading to
concerns about the usability, timeliness, reliability, and trustworthiness of municipal data. The
following sections describe the current AV data exchange ecosystem with respect to policy,
data standards, and data sharing concerns. Although this report was written in the context of
streamlining AV robotaxi ride-hailing services, AV operations of any type will experience similar
critical data communication flows.

Fragmented Policies
The current data exchange environment for AVs is primarily driven by compliance reporting
rather than normal operational data sharing. At the federal level, NHTSA has mandated that
manufacturers and operators of AVs report all types of crashes that involve vehicles equipped
with ADS and SAE Level 2+ systems. At the same time, various states have their own sets of
requirements, resulting in a patchwork of heterogeneous requirements that differ significantly
from one region to another. This has been evident in various aspects of AV-related data
reporting and management. For example, the California Department of Motor Vehicles has
mandated that various types of collision reports for AV testing need to be filed within a specific
time frame if they have crossed a certain threshold. Likewise, CPUC requires that AV passenger
services operating in the state submit quarterly reports, including operational data such as
vehicle miles traveled and the number of trips completed. While these reporting requirements
are mandatory in the state of California, similar reporting structures are lacking or fragmented in
other states where there are active AV deployments. This creates voids in data completeness
when understanding the overall national deployment of AVs. Additionally, the California model
is still mostly periodic and historical in nature, as it is based on a quarterly reporting structure.
There is still no clear policy framework in place that allows for the access of relevant AV data by
cities in a manner that is timely and relevant for transportation management, operations, and
planning.

Lack of Consistent, Implemented Standards
Even with a consensus on the importance of data sharing between the city and AVs, a lack of
consistency in data standards and dissemination methodology makes scaling challenging.
Having a schema is useful if there is a viable model for data coordination, management, and
publishing. Otherwise, every city, county, and department of transportation represents a unique
integration problem that AV operators would need to resolve individually. Multiple data sharing
standards are emerging that may address the AV ecosystems for different purposes. For

 29

Data for Autonomous Transportation Awareness | Data Exchange Use Cases, Standards, and Barriers

example, OMF sees MDS as a means for cities to share digital policy and operational
constraints in a standardized manner that would include autonomous vehicles and other
mobility services. Some cities are exploring its use for real-time operational coordination. For
instance, the Seattle Department of Transportation with its dCAAMP program is developing a
way of ensuring that emergency conflict zones are visible to AVs and connected vehicles in
near real time using MDS. But there are other competing methods; for example, Austin, Phoenix,
and San Francisco use a common email protocol that emanated from common emergency
dispatch software to share emergency zones for AV operators to avoid. This heterogeneity in
implementation presents a barrier to adoption of standards because AV companies want
solutions that are effective across all jurisdictions in which they operate. They may hesitate to
implement “city-by-city” solutions or standards, particularly if the standard involves data
exchanges that AV operators are not willing to ingest, source, and/or share.

Data Sharing Concerns
For AV companies considering the inclusion of public sector feeds, a primary concern is
whether the feed is reliable and sufficiently trustworthy for decision-making in a safety-critical
system. This raises accountability questions: Who guarantees the data quality (accuracy,
timeliness, and precision), and what happens when it is inaccurate or does not suit operational
needs? Cities and infrastructure providers may be reluctant to guarantee the availability of high
data quality with respect to accuracy, completeness, and latency, which requires staffing,
monitoring, and validation. The possible exposure to liability usually results in some type of
indemnity or disclaimer. From the AV operators’ perspective, companies may be reluctant to
incorporate external agency feeds into their core automation or fleet decisions because these
feeds may be incomplete, old, and/or inconsistently maintained across different jurisdictions.
Precedence to solve this “chicken and egg” problem is emerging from Europe, where standards
from TISA are being instituted in EU NAPs, and 5-star quality ratings act as a de facto service-
level standard for data quality. For AV operators, the integration of public sector data feed into
operations may be committing them to a dependency to meet operational guarantees, which
they are hesitant to comply with.

For data flowing the other direction from AV operators to municipalities, the AV companies may
be reluctant to share their own operational data with the city in order to maintain a competitive
advantage or because they are concerned that the datasets could be used out of context to
support a restrictive operating environment or present increased legal/liability risk.

Conclusions
Identified Use Cases
Data for Autonomous Transportation Awareness first examines the critical data exchanges
between AV service providers and the cities and municipalities that they serve. Through
stakeholder engagement, standards and literature reviews, and assessment of parallel and
historical major technology innovations, this report identifies and classifies critical data
exchanges that need to occur between AV service providers and municipal agents, including
IOOs (primarily the road network authority), as well as various other municipal authorities,
particularly for emergency response (police, fire, and 911) into three primary categories:

 1. Critical safety data: Critical safety data includes hazard information that falls beyond the
 sensor capability of any AV sensor yet is critical for safe operations. Examples include
 fires, floods, riots, and active shooters. Such threats and hazards typically involve broad-
 area situational awareness typically associated with emergency dispatch functions that
 results in directives to the AV fleets of locations and times on the roadway network to
 avoid. It also includes better, consistent and real-time road construction and work zone
 data, particularly for high-speed facilities such as freeways. For construction and work
 zones in lower-speed urban facilities, permits are often issued for a bracketed period
 (perhaps 90 days), and the contractor has the freedom to schedule within that period to
 accommodate scope (e.g., maintenance, striping, utilities), work schedule, weather, and
 project constraints, among others. Navigating work zone areas appropriately and picking
 up and dropping off in safe and efficient areas remain challenging and an area that
 continues to evolve. However, on high-speed facilities with unexpected lane drops,
 detours, and irregular traffic flow, pre-knowledge of these spatial locations and as much
 information on the traffic control in place is a regularly recurring theme, particularly for
 autonomous operations of freight.
 2. Service performance characteristics: Municipalities setting regulatory and policy
 initiatives need information on performance characteristics of AV services that reflect
 on the quantity, quality, spatial extents, and impact on the roadway network. Safety
 criteria, such as the reporting of crashes, disengagements, and other irregularities of the
 ADS as it navigates the ODD, are typically already part of the AV regulations for any state
 and likely to appear at the national level. In addition to safety, within California, AV
 services are required to report a variety of service-level performance parameters that
 reflect both the contribution and impact of AV service providers to the overall
 transportation system. The only documented consistent transfer of data (apart from
 safety data) was associated with permitting, and that was primarily in California with
 respect to CPUC and its associated performance metric requirements. Other potential
 performance characteristics based on discussions with stakeholders include the
 infrastructure condition of signs, pavement, and other aspects of roadways.
 3. Regulatory and policy information: Regulatory and policy data can be critical
 information for AV operations, particularly data that are dynamic and govern how AV
 services interact with the roadway network, built environment, and utilize curb space.

 31

Data for Autonomous Transportation Awareness | Data Exchange Use Cases, Standards, and Barriers

 Curb policy exemplifies this last area, referring to where and when AVs can use roadside
 shoulders and curbs as safe pickup and drop-off areas to interact with customers. The
 CDS is emerging as a leading data schema to convey curb data in a natively digital
 format. The CDS is rapidly gaining market traction, though initial use is primarily for
 static information. However, it is beginning to be demonstrated for dynamic use in
 places such as Seattle and Los Angeles. It is not confirmed whether any existing AV
 service providers utilize CDS data from municipalities that have it available.

All these data areas are rapidly evolving with various new data standards/specifications and
frameworks. Data systems that served taxi, ride-hailing, and public transit fleets need to evolve
into native digital formats that support computer-to-computer secured and verified information
exchanges to enable fully autonomous services. Previous systems, though perhaps computer
assisted, still relied on human interaction for day-to-day operations—and the first generation of
information exchange with AV operators appears to rely on “human-in-the-loop” processes.
However, in AV operations, human oversight needs to be reserved only for events and
circumstances outside any anticipated ODD. Standards such MDS, CDS, and WZDx are
examples of emerging data schemas that have been authored in recent years. These standards
are currently in the early stages of testing, demonstration, deployment, and adoption to meet
the critical real-time information needs identified above.

Future Directions, Gaps and
Research Needs
Real-Time Information Broker Business Model
AV operations may highly benefit from integrated, real-time, verified data exchange across a
variety of actors to address safety, security, and performance—without a human in the loop. In
addition to standards-based data schemas, this report identifies appropriate business models
for data exchange platforms as a gap in current practice. A new entity, RTIBs, are proposed and
would have the primary responsibility for the rapid, secure, validated, and archived exchange of
data between multiple transportation industry and municipal authorities. The RTIB function
would create a data clearinghouse similar in concept and function to the ACH framework that
the banking industry uses to facilitate trusted fiscal transaction clearance between banks.
Concurrent with the fledgling adoption of standard data schemas (MDS, CDS, and WZDx),
examples of this RTIB business function are beginning to emerge in siloed contexts. However,
continued emphasis and focus on the critical functions of an RTIB are needed to establish
holistic services to support rapid scaling.

Convene Industry and Public Sector
In this rapidly scaling environment with approximately 20 cities expected to have active robotaxi
services by the end of 2026, convening AV developers and operators with the multiple city
authorities periodically and consistently is critical to identifying and addressing issues as they
emerge. Currently, six cities (San Francisco, Las Vegas, Austin, Atlanta, Las Vegas, and Miami)
have passenger-carrying service without a safety driver or attendant, and three airport
curbfronts are currently accessed (Phoenix Sky Harbor, San Jose, and San Francisco). To
streamline scaling of AV technology, it is critical for municipalities to identify and exchange
lessons learned and areas of friction as opportunities for collaboration between the public
sector and industry.

Further Development and Implementation of Data
Standards
Data standards to address AV operations interacting with hazards, curbs, and system safety
and operational performance are in their infancy. Likewise, standards for ADS safety are
beginning to be addressed at the national scale. Continued application and integration of such
standards to normalize critical communications between AV operators and municipal
authorities will continue to be a key issue to enable rapid scaling of AV service technology. The
consistency and interoperability of various implementation of data standards as they move
from demonstration to deployment is an identified area for additional attention.
