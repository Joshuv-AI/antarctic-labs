Association between Onychogryphosis and Lower Extremity Complications: A Multicenter Cohort Study Using TriNetX
Authors: Natalia Chalupczak, Shari R. Lipner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOnychogryphosis has been associated with chronic foot injury and vascular disease. We evaluated whether onychogryphosis is associated with increased 5-year risk of lower extremity complications.MethodsUsing TriNetX Research Network patients with onychogryphosis were propensity score matched 1:1 to controls for age, sex, race, obesity, type II diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and smoking. Cox proportional hazards models estimated 5-year hazard ratios (HRs) for lower extremity complications, with subgroup analyses for diabetes and peripheral artery disease (PAD).ResultsAfter matching (174,537 per cohort), onychogryphosis was associated with increased 5-year risk of foot/toe amputation procedures (HR 2.798, 95% CI: 2.516–3.111), ulcer (HR 2.108, 95% CI: 2.023–2.196), cellulitis (HR 1.743, 95% CI: 1.685–1.803), osteomyelitis (HR 1.673, 95% CI: 1.583–1.769), and gangrene (HR 1.485, 95% CI: 1.382–1.597) (all p < 0.0001). Associations remained significant in diabetes and PAD restricted analyses.ConclusionOnychogryphosis was associated with substantially increased 5-year risk of major lower extremity complications in a large matched cohort, with consistent findings among patients with type II diabetes and PAD. Identification of onychogryphosis should prompt focused lower extremity assessment and brief risk screening with a low threshold for vascular or primary care referral in high-risk patients.

Introduction

Onychogryphosis is characterized by a markedly thickened hypercurved nail plate often resembling a ram’s horn. Older adults and patients with impaired mobility and self-care are most often affected [1, 2]. Onychogryphosis has been associated with chronic foot injury and vascular disease, suggesting that it may reflect broader lower extremity pathology [3]. However, population level evidence linking onychogryphosis to lower extremity complications is limited. Given the morbidity of diabetic and vasculopathic foot events, we assessed whether onychogryphosis is associated with subsequent lower extremity complications using a large multicenter real-world database.

Methods

TriNetX, a research network of ≥100 healthcare organizations that applies data quality checks for internal validation, was queried on March 2, 2026, for patients with onychogryphosis and a control group without onychogryphosis presenting for a general medical encounter. The index date was the first recorded onychogryphosis diagnosis (and the corresponding matched date for controls). Cohorts were propensity score matched 1:1 for age, sex, race, ethnicity, type II diabetes, chronic kidney disease, obesity, and smoking. Cox proportional hazards models estimated 5 year hazard ratios (HRs) for clinically significant lower extremity outcomes, including nonpressure chronic ulcer of lower limb, cellulitis of limb, osteomyelitis, gangrene, and foot/toe amputation procedures. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (online suppl. File 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551347).

Results

After matching, each cohort included 174,537 patients (mean age 61.05 years, 55.46% female for the entire cohort). Baseline characteristics were well balanced after matching (all standardized mean differences <0.10) (Table 1). Onychogryphosis was associated with risk of foot/toe amputation procedures (HR 2.798, 95% CI: 2.516–3.111), nonpressure chronic ulcer of the lower limb (HR 2.108, 95% CI: 2.023–2.196), cellulitis of the limb (HR 1.743, 95% CI: 1.685–1.803), osteomyelitis (HR 1.673, 95% CI: 1.583–1.769), and gangrene (HR 1.485, 95% CI: 1.382–1.597) (all p < 0.0001) (Table 2). These associations remained significant in subgroup analyses restricted to patients with type II diabetes and peripheral artery disease (PAD), indicating that onychogryphosis independently predicts complications beyond these established risk factors (Table 3).

Discussion

We found that onychogryphosis was associated with increased 5 year risk of lower extremity complications, including amputation procedures, ulceration, infection, and gangrene. These findings corroborate and extend limited existing epidemiologic data using a propensity score matched, time to event design with consistent findings in diabetes and PAD restricted subgroups. Similarly, in a retrospective cohort study using the All of Us Research Program database including 5,679 patients, onychogryphosis was associated with type II diabetes, PAD, and lower extremity ulcer (all p < 1E−6). Prior evidence is otherwise largely limited to case reports.

Since normal keratinization depends on adequate blood flow and oxygen delivery, vascular disease may impair nail matrix repair from repetitive microtrauma and increase susceptibility to onychodystrophy [4]. Onychogryphosis therefore may be an indicator of underlying vascular disease, which is particularly important in patients with type II diabetes and PAD, who are at high risk for severe progressive complications. For example, in a retrospective cohort study including 8,905 patients with type I or II diabetes, 514 (5.8%) developed a foot ulcer, of which 77 (15%) developed osteomyelitis and 80 (15.6%) requiring amputation [5]. Several causal models may explain this association. Onychogryphosis may serve as a marker of underlying vascular disease (shared etiology), result from trauma that also causes subsequent complications (common cause), or directly contribute through soft tissue injury creating infection portals (direct causation). These models are not mutually exclusive and prospective studies are needed to elucidate mechanisms. Because onychogryphosis is a clinical diagnosis, and can be readily appreciated during physical examination, its identification should prompt focused lower extremity assessment, brief lower extremity risk screen (history of diabetes/PAD, prior ulceration or infection), with a low threshold for referral to vascular or primary care for preventive care and vascular risk optimization in high-risk patients.

Study limitations include retrospective cohort design, potential diagnostic and procedural miscoding, residual confounding (neuropathy severity, socioeconomic factors, footwear, and mobility limitations), and potential surveillance bias due to differential healthcare utilization. Nonetheless, consistent associations across type II diabetes and PAD restricted analyses support the robustness of the observed relationship. Correlation does not equal causation.

In sum, we found that onychogryphosis was associated with an increased 5 year risk of lower extremity complications in a large multicenter matched cohort. Therefore, we recommend that diagnosis of onychogryphosis prompts inspection of the feet and interdigital spaces, a brief lower extremity risk screen, and a low threshold for referral to vascular or primary care for preventive care and vascular risk optimization. Prospective studies are needed to confirm temporality and test whether targeted screening and early interventions reduce subsequent complications.

Statement of Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was based entirely on de-identified patient data obtained under license from the TriNetX research network. No individually identifiable patient information was accessed.
