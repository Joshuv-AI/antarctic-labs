Theory-based dyadic co-learning intervention to enhance family resilience in men with prostate cancer and their spouses in Taiwan: protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Ching-Hui Chien, Cheng-Keng Chuang, Chun-Te Wu, See-Tong Pang, Kai-Jei Yu, Po-Hung Lin, Kuan-Lin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe diagnosis and treatment of prostate cancer often disrupt patients’ and their spouses’ established family lives, requiring them to reestablish a sense of balance. This study aims to develop and evaluate a dyadic co-learning intervention derived from the activation-reinforcement-adjustment theory of family resilience for patients with prostate cancer and their spouses.Methods and analysisAn experimental study design was employed. Prostate cancer patients and their spouses (n=60 pairs) will be recruited from the outpatient department. Following baseline assessment, participants will be randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the control group (1:1). The intervention group receives an 8 week dyadic co-learning intervention and usual care, whereas the control group receives usual care. All participants complete follow-up assessments at 10 weeks and 16 weeks after the pretest. Structured questionnaires included the Resilience Scale, the Walsh Family Resilience Questionnaire, the Loneliness Scale, the Chinese Happiness Inventory, the Brief Fatigue Inventory and the Expanded Prostate Cancer Index Composite. Statistical analyses include independent-samples t tests, χ2 tests and mixed linear models.Ethics and disseminationThis study protocol was approved by the Chang Gung Medical Foundation Institutional Review Board (No. 202 202 198B0). The study findings will be disseminated through international conferences and publication in peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration numberClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT07196488 (First Submitted 2025-09-06).

Introduction

Globally, prostate cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer among men in 118 countries.1 In Taiwan, prostate cancer ranks third in cancer incidence among men and sixth among causes of cancer-related mortality2. In addition, the median age at diagnosis decreased from 74 years in 2011 (age range: 35 years to over 85 years) to 72 years in 2019 (age range: 30 years to over 85 years)3. Treatment for prostate cancer is varied, with commonly used approaches including radiotherapy, radical prostatectomy and hormone therapy4. Regardless of the treatment modality, prostate cancer patients may experience varying degrees of disease-related and treatment-related complications, such as anxiety, depression, fatigue, lack of vitality, erectile dysfunction, loss of libido, urinary incontinence, increased bowel movement frequency and tenesmus.59 Previous studies have found that 25.7% of prostate cancer patients meet the criteria for loneliness and 59% meet the criteria for social isolation.10 A study conducted in the USA reported that loneliness among prostate cancer patients was associated with sexual dysfunction.11 These factors may disrupt patients’ and spouses’ existing family relationships, roles, divisions of labour and family functioning12 13 and may even affect family resilience.14 15 Furthermore, research data indicate that among Taiwanese prostate cancer patients, family environmental adjustment (domestic environment) and extended family environment were significantly worse at 1 month and 6 months after treatment compared with before diagnosis.13 Between 7% and 10% of patients or spouses reported relationship satisfaction levels that did not reach a satisfactory standard.16

Resilience is a dynamic process defined as an individual’s ability to recover from adversity or stressful events and return to their original state. Resilience can be conceptualised as a capacity, process or outcome of positive development.1719 Similarly, family resilience is a dynamic process defined as a family’s ability, process or outcome of recovering from stressors and reestablishing adaptation, and it may even promote individual growth.142022 Walsh proposed the family resilience framework, which categorises the key transformational processes that enable families to recover from adversity into three major domains and applies them in practice to enhance family resilience. These domains include shared belief systems, organisational processes and communication/problem-solving processes.22 Qualitative research conducted among Taiwanese prostate cancer patients and their spouses has shown that family resilience is a dynamic process, activated at the time of a new diagnosis, strengthened during the treatment phase and adjusted according to circumstances during the follow-up period. During this process, prostate cancer patients and their families adopted various approaches, including accompanying the patient throughout treatment, using different health coping strategies, demonstrating care and tolerance and providing psychological support.15 Previous research conducted in South Africa identified resources that facilitate family resilience among prostate cancer patients and their spouses, including intrafamilial support, open communication, social support and professional support and knowledge.23 Findings from observational studies further support that higher family resilience has been shown to be related to improved social support,24 individual resilience,24 positive affect25 and quality of life,21 as well as reduced negative affect25 and posttraumatic stress responses.26 However, to date, no studies have examined the effectiveness of family resilience-based interventions for prostate cancer patients and their spouses in improving family resilience related outcomes.

A review of the literature indicates that interventions targeting prostate cancer patients and their spouses or primary caregivers that use video, multimedia, web-based platforms, mobile applications or other high-technology tools include sexual counselling,27 28 educational programmes,2932 psychoeducational interventions33 and psychosocial interventions.3436 Most of these studies were conducted in the USA2729 3133 37 or Canada30 35 38 and primarily delivered interventions via web-based platforms. Only one study was conducted in Taiwan and employed a multimedia-based approach.36 The most commonly applied theoretical framework was stress and coping theory.273133 35 36 38 Intervention durations varied, including single-session interventions,30 6-week interventions,33 36 8-week interventions,29 10-week interventions,35 12–16 week interventions27 32 34 or longer. These interventions were shown to improve sexual function or sexual activity,27 28 psychological or social domains of quality of life,29 35 overall quality of life,33 anxiety35 and emotional distress.33 In addition, interventions were found to improve urinary symptoms and general symptoms among prostate cancer patients.29

In a previous grounded theory study, we developed a substantive theory of family resilience conceptualised as a three-phase process—activation, reinforcement and adjustment—hereafter referred to as the activation-reinforcement-adjustment (ARA) theory of family resilience.15

Family resilience at different stages of the disease may be influenced by different internal family factors, including family beliefs and faith, family health and functioning, family emotional bonds and connections and family communication and decision-making.15 In the Taiwanese cultural context, married spouses and unmarried intimate partners may have different obligations and responsibilities toward an ill partner and the partner’s family due to differing social expectations and legal norms.39 40 Therefore, only married couples living together were included in this preliminary study to reduce potential research bias. Building on the ARA theory of family resilience,15 the present study aims to: (a) develop an app-assisted, theory-informed family resilience intervention, termed the dyadic co-learning intervention (DCII), for men with prostate cancer and their spouses and (b) evaluate the effectiveness of the DCII in this population (figure 1).

Conceptual framework of the dyadic co-learning intervention. ARA Theory of Family Resilience, activation–reinforcement–adjustment theory of family resilience; PSA, prostate-specific antigen.

Research hypotheses: (a) the intervention group will demonstrate significantly higher levels of family resilience, individual resilience and subjective well-being compared with the control group; (b) the intervention group will exhibit significantly lower levels of symptom distress and loneliness compared with the control group; (c) within the intervention group, posttest levels of family resilience, individual resilience, loneliness, subjective well-being and symptom distress will be significantly improved compared with pretest levels.

Methods

Study design

This study adopts an experimental, two-group randomised design. The study is expected to be conducted from 13 October 2025 to 31 July 2026. Participants will be recruited using convenience sampling from urology outpatient department and urologic oncology outpatient department at two hospitals in northern Taiwan.

Study sample

Inclusion criteria for patients are as follows: (1) diagnosis of prostate cancer confirmed by a urologist; (2) having decided on a treatment modality or having completed treatment within the past 2 years; (3) an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score of 0 or 1. Inclusion criteria for spouses are as follows: (1) being a cohabiting spouse in a marital relationship with a prostate cancer patient and aged 20 years or older and (2) a Barthel Index score of 91 or above. Exclusion criteria include either the patient or the spouse: (1) having another type of cancer or (2) having cognitive or psychiatric disorders, such as dementia, depression or anxiety disorders. At least one member of the dyad (patient or spouse) must own a smartphone to enable installation of intervention app.

Usual care

Under usual care, prostate cancer patients receive the health education booklet Understanding Prostate Cancer.41 The booklet includes information on prostate cancer risk factors, symptoms, common examinations, cancer staging, treatment options, postoperative care considerations, treatment-related complications, home care instructions and preventive healthcare. In addition, healthcare professionals provide individualised health education and care based on patients’ specific concerns.

Dyadic co-learning intervention

The intervention was developed based on the ARA theory of family resilience.15 The purpose of the family resilience intervention is to facilitate patients, spouses and other family members in jointly overcoming the impacts of prostate cancer; to improve or prevent patients’ symptom distress and to enhance patients’ and spouses’ individual resilience, reduce loneliness and improve subjective well-being. The DCII consists of three components: knowledge empowerment resources, a co-learning app and professional support (figure 1). The intervention lasts for 8 weeks and includes one learning module and one professional support session per week. Participants learn using the co-learning app in conjunction with a handbook and are required to complete symptom distress recordings at least once per week. The components are described in detail below.

Knowledge empowerment resources

Initially, the principal investigator (PI) developed 10 module topics, objectives and content based on the most common treatment-related complications and health issues among prostate cancer patients, as well as ARA theory of family resilience.15 Experts from relevant fields were then invited to validate and review the content. The family resilience modules include five mandatory learning modules, two optional learning modules and one comprehensive review module (reviewing and integrating learnt content). The mandatory modules include: introduction to co-learning; communication and sharing; utilisation of internal and external family resources; fatigue prevention and care; caring for the mind and spirit; review and integration of learning. Optional learning modules include: surgery and urinary incontinence care; treatment and sexual care; radiotherapy and care; hormone therapy and care (table 1). After revisions based on expert feedback, multimedia videos incorporating images, illustrations, music, audio and text were produced, along with an accompanying handbook.

Co-learning app

The app includes functions for recording patients’ symptoms and symptom distress, accessing the electronic version of the handbook and viewing multimedia videos. The selection of symptom items was based on a literature review and clinical experience regarding common treatment-related side effects or comorbid symptoms among prostate cancer patients. These symptoms include urinary incontinence, urinary frequency, diarrhoea, constipation, fatigue, low mood, tension, lack of vitality, hot flushes and other symptoms. Patients are required to record their symptoms at least once per week. In addition, serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels can be recorded.

Sexual dysfunction is a common treatment-related complication of prostate cancer,42 which may affect the pre-existing marital relationship between couples15; however, influenced by Confucian culture, older adults in Taiwan tend to remain conservative in discussing sexual issues,43 and sexual function is considered a sensitive and private issue. Therefore, sexual dysfunction was not included in the app-based symptom recording. Researchers can instead assess patients’ sexual function through questionnaire data. If necessary, patients may record related concerns in the ‘other symptoms’ section of the app (figure 2).

Screenshot of the co-learning app.

Professional support

Professional support is provided by two trained nurses who conduct weekly discussions at times agreed on with the patient and their spouse. Prior to each discussion, patients are required to complete a symptom and distress report so that the professional support providers can understand their current condition. The interventionists assess participants’ understanding of the learning content and provide clarification as needed through voice calls. In addition, based on the symptoms and distress reported by the patient, the interventionists attend to the patient’s condition, provide timely health education and encourage the application of previously learnt knowledge to manage and care for the illness and its symptoms. When necessary, and with the patient’s consent, the research staff will refer the patient to a case manager for further management. Through the following semistructured questions, the interventionists assess the learning and self-care status of the patient and their spouse: (a) were there any parts of this week’s learning module that were unclear? (b) Regarding the modules learnt previously and last week were there any difficulties or problems in applying them? (c) How are the XX symptoms you recorded? (d) Are they manageable? (e) How did you take care of yourself? (f) How do you feel now? (g) Are there any other issues that need clarification?

Outcomes

Primary outcome

Walsh Family Resilience Questionnaire

In this study, the Chinese version of the Walsh Family Resilience Questionnaire (WFRQ) will be used to assess family resilience among prostate cancer patients and their spouses.44 The original scale was developed by Walsh based on family resilience theory and consists of 32 items.22 Wang and Lu translated the original scale into Chinese and evaluated its reliability and validity in a sample of 800 community adults. Through item analysis, six items were removed, resulting in a 26-item Chinese version. The Chinese version of the WFRQ comprises three domains: family belief systems (10 items), organisation, communication and problem-solving (13 items) and utilisation of external resources (three items). Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale (1–5), with total scores ranging from 26 to 130. Higher scores indicate greater family resilience. Confirmatory factor analysis supported the three-factor structure, demonstrating construct validity. The internal consistency of the total scale was excellent, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.97. The intraclass correlation coefficient over a 2-week interval was 0.96, indicating good test-retest reliability.44

Secondary outcomes

Expanded Prostate Cancer Index Composite (EPIC-26)

The Chinese version of the Expanded Prostate Cancer Index Composite (EPIC-26) will be used to assess symptom distress among prostate cancer patients. This 26-item scale is a shortened version of the EPIC-5045 and includes the following domains: urinary incontinence, irritative/obstructive urinary symptoms, bowel symptoms and distress, sexual function and distress and vitality/hormonal function and distress. Scoring is calculated as the sum of linearly transformed item scores (0–100) divided by the number of items in each domain.46 The correlations between corresponding domains of the EPIC-26 and EPIC-50 were all >0.96, while correlations between non-corresponding domains ranged from 0.19 to 0.4846. Confirmatory factor analysis in previous studies has shown that the EPIC-26 retains the same factor structure as the original scale, indicating construct validity. Cronbach’s α coefficients for the domains ranged from 0.55 to 0.9147.

Brief Fatigue Inventory

The Chinese version of the Brief Fatigue Inventory will be used to assess the severity of cancer-related fatigue over the past 24 hours and its impact on daily functioning among prostate cancer patients.48 49 The scale consists of nine items rated on an 11-point scale (0–10), with higher scores indicating greater fatigue severity or greater interference with daily activities.48 Both the original English version49 and the Chinese version48 have demonstrated good reliability and validity. For the Taiwanese version, internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) was 0.96 for fatigue severity, 0.95 for fatigue interference with daily activities and 0.97 for the total scale. Test-retest reliability over a 3-day interval was 0.89 for fatigue severity and 0.91 for fatigue interference. The scale demonstrated convergent validity and known-groups validity.48

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale

The 10-item version of the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale will be used to measure individual resilience among prostate cancer patients and their spouses.50 The original scale consisted of 25 items, from which a 10-item version was later derived.51 Each item is rated on a scale from 0 to 4, yielding total scores ranging from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater resilience.52 Confirmatory factor analysis supported the unidimensional structure of the Chinese 10-item version and demonstrated good model fit, indicating construct validity. The internal consistency of the scale was excellent, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.94.52

Loneliness Scale

The Chinese version of the Loneliness Scale will be used to measure loneliness among prostate cancer patients and their spouses. This scale was translated and adapted by Lin et al53 from the Loneliness Scale developed by Gierveld et al.54 The Chinese version consists of nine items and includes two subscales: social loneliness and emotional loneliness. Items are scored using a dichotomous format, with total scores ranging from 0 to 9. Higher scores indicate greater loneliness. The scale demonstrated construct validity, discriminant validity and convergent validity. Internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) was 0.86 for the total scale, 0.62 for the social loneliness subscale and 0.87 for the emotional loneliness subscale.53

Chinese Happiness Inventory

The ultra-short version of the Chinese Happiness Inventory, developed by Liu specifically for Chinese populations, will be used to assess subjective well-being among prostate cancer patients and their spouses.55 The original Chinese Happiness Inventory consists of 48 items, including 28 items from the Oxford Happiness Questionnaire and 20 items reflecting culturally specific sources of happiness among Chinese individuals. The original scale demonstrated good and acceptable reliability and validity.56 Subsequent revisions yielded 20-item, 10-item and five-item versions. This study uses the 10-item version,55 which assesses positive affect, negative affect and life satisfaction. Items are rated on a four-point scale (0–3), with total scores ranging from 0 to 30. Higher scores indicate greater subjective well-being. The scale demonstrated acceptable validity, and the internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) of the total scale was 0.87.55

Program Satisfaction Questionnaire

Programme satisfaction in the intervention group will be assessed using a modified version of the Multimedia Satisfaction Questionnaire.36 The questionnaire consists of five items, each scored on a scale from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction with the intervention.

Other variables

Demographic and disease-related/health-related variables

A self-developed questionnaire will be used to collect demographic and disease-related/health-related information from patients and spouses. Patient demographic variables include age, educational level, religious belief, employment status, number of children, living arrangement and relationship status. Disease-related variables include cancer stage, time since diagnosis (months), PSA level, Gleason score, primary treatment modality, history of cancer recurrence, history of chronic illness and subjective health status. Spouse demographic variables include age, educational level, religious belief, employment status and number of children. Health-related variables include menopausal status, menopause, history of chronic illness and subjective health status.

Randomiation

Participants will first be stratified by site into hospital A and hospital B, and then further stratified by time since initiation of treatment: (a) before initiating treatment; (b) within 3 months after initiating treatment; (c) 3–12 months after initiating treatment and (d) 13–24 months after initiating treatment. Stratification according to treatment timing was based on the ARA theory of family resilience, which proposes that family resilience processes may vary across different stages of the illness trajectory.15 This approach was used to enhance comparability between the intervention and control groups. Subsequently, the PI will use Random Allocation Software V.1.057 to perform block randomisation using a 2×2 block design. Participants will be randomly assigned to the intervention group or the control group at a 1:1 ratio. Allocation results will be placed in opaque sealed envelopes and provided to research personnel responsible for participant enrolment.

Blinded design

Urologists and nurses in the outpatient departments will not be informed whether patients and their spouses are participating in the study or of their group assignments. In addition, statistical analyses will be conducted by a researcher who is not involved in participant recruitment, group allocation, intervention delivery or questionnaire collection. A blinded data analyst design will be employed during statistical analysis.

Study procedures

Participant recruitment will be conducted at urology outpatient department and urologic oncology outpatient department. During the recruitment period, physicians will refer prostate cancer patients and their spouses who may meet eligibility criteria to the research team. Researchers will assess eligibility through interactions with patients and spouses and review of relevant medical records. For potential participants who meet the inclusion criteria, research personnel will explain the study objectives, procedures and participation requirements. After written informed consent is obtained (see online supplemental file 1), baseline (time 0) questionnaire data will be collected. On completion of baseline assessment, patients and spouses will be randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the control group (1:1). The intervention group will receive usual care in addition to the DCII for a total of 8 weeks, including one learning module and professional support per week, and will record symptoms and symptom distress using the app at least once per week. The control group will receive usual care only. Both groups will complete follow-up assessments at 10 weeks after baseline (post-test 1, time 1) and 16 weeks after baseline (post-test 2, time 2), as shown in figure 3.

Sample size estimation

Because no previous studies have examined the effects of interventions on family resilience among patients with prostate cancer and their spouses, sample size estimation was based on psychological domain quality-of-life scores reported in previous studies involving prostate cancer patients and their primary caregivers.35 The mean (SD) psychological domain quality-of-life scores at post-test for the intervention group and control group were 54.7 (5.3) and 47.6 (9.6), respectively, with a pooled SD of 8.04. The statistical method used was analysis of variance (ANOVA): repeated measures, between factors. The type I error rate was set at 0.05, and statistical power was set at 0.80. The estimated total sample size was 30 dyads, with 15 dyads per group. Based on prior studies in this population, the attrition rate was estimated to range from 0% to 12%.36 In addition, considering that each group should include at least 30 dyads to meet the minimum requirement for normal distribution, the target sample size was set at 30 dyads per group.

Data management

Structured paper-based questionnaires will be used for data collection. Paper questionnaires will be stored in a locked filing cabinet in the PI’s office. Electronic data will be stored on a password-protected computer located in the same office. Data will be used solely for academic research and publication purposes. Raw data will not be publicly disclosed or shared to protect participant privacy and data security. Given the minimal risk nature of this psychosocial intervention, no interim analyses are planned. A formal data monitoring committee is not deemed necessary. Trial conduct and participant safety will be overseen by the PI. Any adverse events will be documented and reported to the institutional review board (IRB) in accordance with institutional policy. The PI retains the authority to discontinue the study if any unexpected safety concerns arise.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will be conducted using IBM SPSS V.20.0. Descriptive statistics, including means, SD, minimums, maximums, ranges, frequencies and percentages, will be used to summarise participants’ demographic characteristics, health-related/disease-related variables and outcome variables in the intervention and control groups. χ2 tests and independent-samples t-tests will be used to assess baseline homogeneity between the two groups. All randomised participants will be included in the analysis according to their originally assigned groups, following the intention-to-treat principle. Linear mixed models will be used to evaluate the effectiveness of the DCII over time while accounting for correlations among repeated measurements. Linear mixed models can accommodate incomplete repeated outcome data under the assumption that data are missing at random. Although treatment timing was included as a stratification factor, subgroup analyses are not primarily planned because the study is not powered for subgroup comparisons. However, exploratory subgroup analyses may be conducted if sufficient participant numbers are available. Statistical significance will be set at α=0.05, with p<0.05.

Monitoring and management of intervention fidelity

Multiple strategies will be used to ensure intervention fidelity.58 First, recruitment manuals, intervention implementation manuals and intervention implementation record forms will be developed to clearly outline procedures and requirements for research personnel. Prior to study initiation, the PI will conduct training sessions and consensus meetings with research staff and intervention providers. Teach-back methods will be used to confirm that personnel can correctly implement study procedures. Regular meetings will be held to discuss study progress and implementation issues to maintain intervention fidelity and consistency. During the intervention, providers will use semistructured questions to confirm participants’ understanding of module content and encourage application of learnt skills in daily life. After completing each intervention module, providers will complete an intervention implementation record form. The PI will review these records to monitor participants’ engagement and intervention delivery.

Ethics and dissemination

This study has been reviewed and approved by the Chang Gung Medical Foundation Institutional Review Board (IRB number: 202 202 198B0; approval date: 8 February 2023) and was registered as a clinical trial prior to participant enrolment (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT07196488, see online supplemental file 2). Researchers will adhere to IRB regulations and ensure participant privacy protection by using study codes to represent participant identities; these codes will not include participants’ names, identification numbers or addresses. Participants have the right to withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason, and withdrawal will not compromise their access to medical care or their legal rights. Participants will receive a gift card valued at New Taiwan $100 after completing each questionnaire as a token of appreciation for their time and contribution to the study. The results of this study will be presented at international academic conferences and submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not directly involved in the design of the current trial. The intervention was developed based on prior qualitative findings.15

Discussion

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this study is the first to focus on prostate cancer patients and their spouses as the primary unit of intervention and to examine the effectiveness of an intervention developed based on the ARA theory of family resilience.15 Accordingly, family resilience was selected as the primary outcome because it directly corresponds to the theoretical framework. Secondary outcomes were selected to capture domains that may be indirectly improved through enhanced family resilience, including subjective well-being, loneliness and physical symptoms and distress. In addition, participant satisfaction with the intervention was also assessed as a secondary outcome.

The family is a system in which members mutually influence one another.15 Therefore, inviting both patients and their spouses to participate in the intervention was expected to facilitate the implementation of strategies proposed in ARA theory of family resilience through joint engagement and communication between partners. The intervention duration was set at 8 weeks based on previous intervention studies involving prostate cancer patients and their spouses29 and included a comprehensive review module. This review module used successful cases to guide patients and spouses in integrative discussions, assisting couples in reflecting on previously learnt content and encouraging continued application of acquired skills in daily life. The preliminary findings of this study may serve as a reference for future large-scale intervention studies as well as for clinical practice. Previous literature has noted that intervention studies targeting patients and their primary caregivers face greater recruitment challenges, as participation requires alignment of both partners’ needs and schedules.59 During the past 4 months, 16 couples were recruited into the study. Recruitment is ongoing and the enrolment rate is expected to be slower than anticipated, requiring a longer recruitment period.

One limitation of this study is that the sample size calculation was based on psychological domain quality-of-life scores rather than the primary outcome of family resilience, which may limit the precision of power estimation for the primary outcome. A second limitation of this study is a fully blinded design beyond statistical analysis. However, healthcare providers were not informed about whether patients and their spouses were participating in the study or about group assignments. In addition, prostate cancer patients and their spouses live in the community and do not have routine contact with healthcare providers, research personnel or peer patients. Even during the intervention, communication between intervention providers and participants will occur via weekly voice calls. Posttest assessments will also primarily be conducted through mailed paper questionnaires. These procedures are intended to minimise potential biases associated with the lack of a fully blinded design. A third limitation is that this study included patients with prostate cancer and their spouses across different stages of the disease, regardless of their level of family resilience. However, patients and families at different disease stages may have distinct needs. When subgroup analyses cannot be conducted because of a small sample size, the intervention effects may be diluted. Future studies are recommended to provide tailored interventions for patients and families with lower levels of family resilience according to different disease stages. Furthermore, the use of multiple stratification variables may have resulted in small numbers of participants within certain strata, which could potentially limit optimal balance between groups. Future studies may consider alternative allocation strategies, such as covariate-adaptive randomisation or minimisation, to improve balance across important prognostic factors.60 In addition, the app-based symptom recording did not include sexual function or related distress; therefore, patients’ conditions in this domain could not be monitored weekly in a timely manner. Future studies may consider further adjustments and improvements based on cultural contexts and participant characteristics across different countries.
