DFT studies of doped and encapsulated of B12N12 nanocage using nickel and platinum metals as carriers for Sunvozertinib drug
Authors: Nosrat Madadi Mahani, Narges Ghadiri, Fatemeh Banifatemeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDensity functional theory and time-dependent calculations were performed to investigation the electronic interaction mechanisms between the anticancer drug Sunvozertinib and boron nitride (B12N12) nanocages encapsulated, and doped with Ni and Pt metals. Adsorption energies resulted displayed that Ni doping remarkably increases the thermodynamic stability and strengthens the chemical affinity of the complex, while Pt doping shows little weaker yet still favorable adsorption compared to the minimal physisorption seen with the encapsulated and pristine BN systems. Analysis of quantum reactivity descriptors and frontier orbitals sugested a reduced band gap and notable charge transfer in the doped B12N12 nanocages, indicating modified electronic conductivity and potential applications in sensing. Also, TD-DFT absorption spectra demonstrated red-shifted transitions upon drug adsorption, affirming orbital coupling between the drug molecule and dopant sites. Supplementary investigations, including density of states (DOS) and reduced density gradient (RDG) approaches, displayed evidence of partially covalent interactions specifically in the Ni-doped model. Analysis of Electron localization function (ELF) maps indicated sharing of localized electron. Collectively, these findings propose that Ni-doped B12N12 nanocages are an excellent platform for efficient drug loading and controlled delivery of Sunvozertinib drug. Against, encapsulated BN nanocages act as inert systems suitable for sustained drug release purposes.Keywords: Doped B12N12, Encapsulated B12N12, Time dependent-density functional theory (TD-DFT), Sunvozertinib, Drug deliverySubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains the leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally. Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have displayed excellent effect in patients with EGFR mutations1,2. Sunvozertinib is irreversible, irreversible, and potent oral drug that has used in treatment wide range of EGFR mutations. This drug drastically aims sensitizing mutations like L858R and exon 19 deletion (exon19del), along with exon 20 insertion mutations (exon20ins) and T790M, rare mutations, while demonstrating minimum effect on wild-type EGFR. Additionally, Sunvozertinib has displayed significant activity against BTK. Global clinical studies have validated its intense and long-lasting antitumor effects in treating NSCLC patients with EGFR exon20ins3,4.

Sunvozertinib is a medication used to treat non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) that has either metastasized to other parts of the body or spread to nearby tissues (locally advanced). It is specifically intended for patients with an abnormal epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene mutation, particularly exon 20 insertion mutations. This treatment is prescribed for individuals who have already undergone other cancer therapies, such as platinum-based drugs, without achieving successful results5. Sunvozertinib, categorized as an antineoplastic agent used in cancer treatment, functions by inhibiting the growth of cancer cells, eventually causing their destruction. Wang and his team have explored the effectiveness and safety of sunvozertinib as a standalone therapy for patients with NSCLC and EGFR mutations who have developed resistance to EGFR TKIs6. Side effects, interactions with CYP3A, the risk of lung inflammation, limited efficacy against specific mutations, and the development of resistance remain significant challenges. Furthermore, poor water solubility and high Lipophilicity continue to pose major formulation hurdles.

Over the past few decades, various nanostructures have been developed as drug delivery systems, taking advantage of their distinctive features, including their small size, enhanced drug loading capacity, and ability to enable controlled drug release7. These nanostructures not only have the potential to reduce adverse effects but also enhance solubility and ensure better-targeted drug accumulation in tumor sites. Carbon fullerene nanostructures, boron nitride clusters, and nanotubes have garnered significant attention owing to their extraordinary physical and chemical characteristics. Chemically, boron nitride nanomaterials exhibit higher stability than their carbon-based counterparts. Their exclusion chemical activity makes them new candidates for biomedicine applications8. Research has recognized B12N12 as the most stable nanocage in boron nitride structures9. Given their biocompatibility, and promising potential, and non-toxic properties for therapeutic and diagnostic applications, considerable research has been assigned to investigating their pattern in drug delivery systems10,11.

Computational analysis has further demonstrated that the B12N12 structure consists of eight hexagonal and six square rings, offering greater durability than arrangements featuring hexagons and pentagons12. The B12N12 nanocage holds promising potential in the pharmaceutical sector, especially for enhancing the targeted therapeutic performance of medications while reducing associated side effects. However, to efficiently develop BN-based drug delivery systems, it is crucial to gain a comprehensive understanding of their surface chemistry and interactions with drug molecules13. Many studies have explored the efficiency of the B12N12 nanocage in adsorbing various drugs using computational methods14–22. Various methods have been explored to enhance the properties of B12N12, including the doping of group III or transition metal atoms through the substitution of a single boron atom, as highlighted in studies23–26. Furthermore, research has also focused on encapsulating different atoms within BN nanocages to investigate their potential27,28.

Pristine B12N12, as well as its doped or encapsulated variations, has been widely studied for diverse applications, ranging from drug delivery systems and gas sensors to metal-ion battery anodes and nonlinear optical materials. Recent research has shown a growing focus on exploring the interactions between the B12N12 cage and larger pharmaceutical compounds, including ciprofloxacin, cefalexin, monacolin K, curcumin, norfloxacin, and molnupiravir29–34. Shabavi et al. examined the adsorption characteristics of gemifloxacin (GFX) on pristine B12N12 nanocages and B12N12 nanocages encapsulated with alkali and alkaline earth metals35. Their study aimed to assess the potential of these nanostructures for effectively removing gemifloxacin from aqueous solutions.

A detailed investigation has been performed using DFT calculations at the B3LYP/6–31++ (d, p) level of theory to explore the interaction between metformin and pristine B12N12 nanoclusters, as well as nanoclusters encapsulating group I(Li, Na, and K) metals36. Huang et al. performed a computational analysis investigating the adsorption behavior of D-Penicillamine on a boron nitride surface modified with aluminum and gallium doping, emphasizing its potential use in drug delivery systems37. The findings revealed that the engineered material holds considerable potential in this area.

This study explored the doping and encapsulation of Pt and Ni metal atoms within B12N12 nanocaes to examine their interactions with the Sunvozertinib molecule on the surfaces of these nanocages. The structural and electronic properties of the doped clusters were analysed and compared to those of pure B12N12 nanocages. The results demonstrated that the incorporation of these metal atoms substantially influences the characteristics of the nanocages.

Computational details

The complete geometry optimization of the Sunvozertinib (Sun) drug, along with B12N12Pt and B12N12Ni encapsulated nanoccages, as well as B11N12Pt and B11N12Ni doped nanocages, was conducted using the B3LYP functional. This utilized the LANL2DZ basis set for Ni and Pt atoms38,39 and the 6–311 + + G (d, p) basis set for other atoms, under density functional theory (DFT). Frequency analysis confirmed the absence of imaginary frequencies in all compounds. The optimization process is constrained by strict convergence criteria, with maximum force and displacement limits set at 0.00045 Hartree and 0.0018 Hartree/Bohr respectively.

A restricted method was utilized for the geometry optimization and electronic property analysis of pure B12N12 nanocages, whereas the spin-unrestricted approach was employed for the encapsulated clusters. Various analyses were conducted to investigate key parameters, encompassing molecular electrostatic potential (MEP), frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis, density of states (DOS), reduced density gradient (RDG) surface evaluation, UV-Visible spectroscopy, and the Electron Localization Function (ELF). These were carried out using the Gaussian 09 software package40.

The basis set superposition error (BSSE) calculations were conducted for all structures, while DOS plots were determined using GaussSum. The GaussView program was employed for visualizing optimized geometries and charge density distributions. These evaluations proved effective for assessing adsorption characteristics and enhancing drug-carrier interactions. Additionally, the B3LYP-D3 level41, which incorporates dispersion correction, was utilized for geometry optimization across all complexes without symmetry constraints. This methodology has been recognized as a high-performing approach in numerous recent investigations.

The Multiwfn 3.8 (dev) code42 and the VMD visualizer were employed to analyze the reduced density gradient (RDG) surface, as well as the Electron Localization Function (ELF) and non-covalent interactions (NCI). Time Dependent-Density Functional Theory (TD-DFT) with an optimally tuned CAM-B3LYP functional43 is used for the investigation of UV-Vis spectra.

Electronic properties and reactivity descriptors

The frontier molecular orbital (FMO) energies and global reactivity descriptors were calculated to evaluate the electronic stability and interaction potential of the drug molecule with both pristine and metal-doped boron nitride (BN) nanostructures. Based on the work of Zhan et al., the energy gap is defined as Eg = ELUMO - EHOMO, chemical hardness is expressed as η = Eg/2, Mulliken electronegativity is determined by χ = ELUMO + EHOMO, and chemical softness, representing the inverse of hardness, is calculated as S = 1/η44.

As listed in Table 1, the pure drug molecule displays a relatively small HOMO–LUMO energy gap of 3.80 eV, suggestive of high chemical reactivity but confined proper stability. Additionally, its large dipole moment highlights significant polarity, which could promote strong, though possibly nonspecific, interactions in biological environments. Although, pure B12N12, illustrates a notably larger energy gap of 5.93 eV along with high chemical hardness and low softness, characteristics that emphasis its electronic activity and structural stability. Such properties make it an excellent candidate for use as a nanocarrier, as these properties decrease the verisimilitude of undesired chemical reactions while preserving structural correctness. In addition, the nearly zero dipole moment of pure B12N12 indicates weak spontaneous interactions with its surrounding environment. Metal doping significantly changes the electronic properties of B12N12. Both Ni and Pt-doped B12N12 nanocages display reduced HOMO–LUMO gaps, 4.42 eV for Ni doping and 4.70 eV for Pt doping, also, increased softness and electrophilicity. These conversions implicate modified surface reactivity and greater electronic affinity, which could enable stronger interactions with the drug and modify loading efficiency. On the other hand, the generation of finite dipole moments upon doping suggests an increase in electrostatic interactions between the drug molecule and the carrier. Encapsulating the drug within doped B12N12 nanocages further modifies their electronic characteristics. The BN–Ni–encapsulated and BN–Pt–encapsulated systems exhibit intermediate energy gaps of 4.37 eV and 4.51 eV, respectively, denoting a balance between stability and reactivity.

In summary, these findings highlight a clear relationship between adsorption energy and recovery time that is influenced by surface modifications. Metal doping greatly enhances the stability of drug absorption, while encapsulation serves as a suitable strategy to balance absorption with controlled release properties. These properties are particularly relevant for applications in controlled drug delivery systems.

In an isolated system, energy levels are discrete, adding complexity to the interpretation of DOS. Although, by imperceptibly expanding these discrete energy levels into a continuous curve, the DOS diagram becomes a useful tool for showing the distribution of orbitals. Additionally, the curve associated with the partial density of states (PDOS) provides a more detailed representation of the orbital compositions. The Density of States (DOS) characterizes the number of electronic states accessible within a specified energy range for each energy level. A higher DOS at a particular energy level indicates the existence of countless states that electrons can potentially occupy. Conversely, a DOS value of zero indicates that there are no states available for electrons to occupy at that particular energy level.

The total and projected density of states (DOS) for the isolated drug molecule, as well as its complexes with both pure and metal-doped boron nitride (BN) nanocages, are depicted in Fig. 4. For the pristine BN (Fig. E), the DOS diagram shows a distinct energy gap between the occupied and unoccupied states, which reinforces the nature of its wide band gap and electronic inertness. The absence of electronic states near the Fermi level highlights its limited intrinsic conductivity and weak electronic interaction with the drug molecule. When the drug molecule is adsorbed or encapsulated, significant changes in the DOS distribution are observed. In the BN-Ni and BN-Pt doped systems (Figs. A–D), new electronic states appear in the band gap region of the pristine BN. These mid-gap states are primarily attributed to the d orbitals of the Ni and Pt atoms, which are strongly hybridized with the molecular orbitals of the drug. This hybridization is evident from the overlap between the DOS of the drug and the BN-derived states near the Fermi level. The spin-separated DOS for the doped systems shows an asymmetric contribution of α and β orbitals, indicating that metal doping induces spin polarization. This effect is particularly pronounced in the Ni-doped structures, indicating stronger electronic coupling and a higher density of accessible states near the Fermi level. The increased intensity of the DOS around the highest occupied states indicates an increased potential for charge transfer between the drug molecule and the doped B12N12 framework. Encapsulation leads to a broader DOS distribution and greater overlap between occupied and unoccupied states, indicating a partial delocalization of the electronic states and a significant reduction in the sufficient band gaps of the hybrid systems.

Among the doped B12N12 systems, Ni-doped complexes exhibit a higher density of states close to the Fermi level than Pt-doped cases, indicating more robust interactions between the drug and carrier coupled with heightened electronic activity. In summary, the DOS analysis confirms that metal doping and encapsulation are effective strategies to modify and tune the electronic structure of BN nanocarriers. The introduction of metal-induced states promotes orbital hybridization and charge redistribution upon drug adsorption, aligning with observations of reduced HOMO–LUMO gaps and increased adsorption energies. These electronic properties are anticipated to enhance drug stabilization and facilitate controlled release, making them highly advantageous for drug-delivery applications.

Here, ρ(r) represents the electron density, while ∇ρ(r) denotes its first derivative48. The characteristics and intensity of interactions between the drug molecule and BN nanocages were assessed through the Reduced Density Gradient (RDG) versus sign (λ₂)ρ methodology. This approach offers a robust means of identifying and characterizing noncovalent interactions. The RDG plots for the interaction the Sunvozertinib drug with studied nancages are displayed in Fig. 5.

The RDG plots of the best structures of the Sunvozertinib drug interacting with nancages: (A) B11N12Ni, (B) B11N12Pt, (C) B12N12Ni, (D) B12N12Pt, and (E) pristine B12N12.

In all the systems studied (A-E), characteristic butterfly-shaped RDG patterns emerged, indicating weak to moderate types of interactions. In these plots, negative values of the sign (λ2)ρ indicate attractive interactions such as hydrogen bonding, dispersion forces, or π-π stacking, while positive values indicate steric repulsion. The electron density (ρ) color scale further quantifies the strength of the interaction: blue regions indicate weak van der Waals forces, green regions indicate electrostatic interactions or intermediate hydrogen bonding, and red regions indicate strong, localized covalent bonds. Metal doping was observed to significantly alter the negative sign(λ2)ρ region. For the BN nanocage doped with Ni, a dense cluster of data points appeared between − 0.01 and − 0.04 a.u. in the RDG plot, accompanied by relatively high electron density values (≈ 0.015–0.02 a.u.).

This indicates an increase in electronic coupling between the drug and the nickel site, indicating a partial covalent bond or quasi-coordination bond. This finding is consistent with the electron localization function (ELF) maps showing the local electron density near the Ni-N/O contacts. The strong polarization effect of the nickel atom activates the BN surface and facilitates charge transfer and local electron sharing between the drug and the doped site. In contrast, platinum-doped BN showed a broader but less intense distribution towards lower RDG values. The color gradient in this case shifted more towards green, indicating a weaker and less overlapping of non-local orbitals compared to nickel doping. The platinum center, which is more polarizable but less prone to strong hybridization, is mainly involved in electrostatic interactions and dispersion rather than covalent bonding. In general, metal doping transforms the otherwise inert BN cage into an electronically active interface, the degree of charge sharing of which depends on the specific metal incorporated. Nickel exhibits stronger electron localization and binding strength, while platinum enables softer physical adsorption. Encapsulating metals within the BN cage creates a completely different electronic environment. RDG plots versus (λ₂)ρ for Ni- and Pt-encapsulated BN show narrower distributions dominated by blue-green regions with minimal red regions. These observations indicate diffuse and non-directional van der Waals interactions. Furthermore, the ELF maps for the encapsulated systems indicate a largely unstable electron density, indicating that the BN shell acts as an insulating barrier and suppresses direct charge transfer between the trapped metal and external molecules such as drugs. While encapsulation thermodynamically stabilizes the nanocavity, it also reduces its chemical reactivity and reduces the strength of potential interactions with external species. As a baseline comparison, the original BN-drug complex highlights the intrinsically inert nature of the undoped BN surface. Its noncovalent interaction (NCI) diagram exhibits a blue-green symmetric distribution around the (λ₂)ρ ≈ 0 mark, highlighting interactions dominated by long-range dispersion forces with negligible electronic coupling. The associated ELF map further confirms this by showing minimal perturbation of the electron density within either the drug or the BN surface. Such behavior reinforces the physisorptive property of pristine BN cages. The trend of the relative interaction strengths is similar to adsorption energies.

This trend shows how the type and position of the metal critically affect electron localization and charge transfer. Surface doping especially with Ni introduces accessible frontier orbitals that enable hybridization with the drug molecule, leading to stronger binding and more localized ELF basins. On the other hand, metal encapsulation limits this effect to the BN cavity, favoring weaker and more diffuse interactions. The pristine BN surface is completely devoid of d-state contributions, making it electronically inert. These findings demonstrate how the metal composition can tune the electronic properties of BN nanocages, paving the way for various applications depending on the interaction profiles. Ni doping is particularly promising for scenarios that require strong anchoring or charge transfer mechanisms, such as targeted adsorption or catalytic processes. Conversely, Pt doping may suit applications requiring reversible or tunable interactions due to its delocalized profiles. Encapsulated systems present unique opportunities for controlled drug release or scenarios demanding minimal electronic interference, thus offering versatile potential for diverse biomedical and catalytic applications.

The electron localization function (ELF) analysis

The electron localization function (ELF) serves as a very effective tool for investigating the bonding properties of electronic structures derived from density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Directly correlated with the electron density and statistical potential, the ELF provides insights into the nature of chemical bonds, whether covalent, ionic, or van der Waals. In contrast to the Baader theory, which divides molecular space into atomic basins or regions of charge concentration, the ELF divides space into electron localization basins. where D(r) is local kinetic energy due to Pauli repulsion, and Dh(r) is the same quantity for a homogenous electron gas49.

The spatial distribution of attractors identified by ELF allows the differentiation of core basinstypically located around nuclei (excluding hydrogen atoms) and valence basins, which are classified based on their connectivity to the core basins. A topological analysis of the ELF forms a robust mathematical framework for characterizing chemical bonds, with the Multiwfn program used to generate 2D representations of ELF isosurfaces for the systems under study.

Figure 6 shows the shaded ELF surface maps with imaging effects, showing different colors corresponding to different degrees of electron localization. The red and orange colors indicate regions with strong electron localization, while the blue color indicates regions with depletion between the inner and valence layers. Notably, hydrogen and carbon exhibit minimal values for the localized orbital locator, whereas good electron space delocalization between the drug and nanocages is observed. For a homogeneous electron gas modeled based on the jelly approach, ELF values range from 0 to 1. Specifically, an ELF value of 1.0 corresponds to covalent bonds and lone pair electrons (red areas), 0.50 corresponds to free electron gas-like behavior (green areas), and 0.0 indicates no electron localization50,51. ELF analysis not only provides a direct visualization of the electron distribution at the drug-BN interface, but also facilitates a quantitative assessment of the interaction properties in the five studied systems (A–E). The color-coded ELF mapping ranges from red/yellow (high electronic localization, ELF ≈ 1.0) to blue/violet (low localization, ELF ≈ 0.0), effectively indicating the transition from covalent to unstable and weakly interacting regions. In the nickel-doped system (A), the ELF plots show significant red and yellow regions near the adsorption interface, with ELF values ranging from 0.8 to 1.0. This intense localization indicates significant charge redistribution and partial electron sharing between the drug molecule and the nickel-doped sites, indicating strong chemical bonding with a clear covalent component. The ELF topology is consistent with the findings of non-covalent interaction (NCI) and electron density difference analyses, highlighting significant hybridization between nickel d orbitals and absorbing molecular orbitals. In contrast, the platinum-doped system (B) shows fewer regions of strong localization dominated by yellow-green regions with ELF values of about 0.6–0.8. This indicates an interaction with less covalent character and increased polarity, as moderate orbital hybridization plays a role alongside dipole and dispersion forces. Although electronic coupling is still significant, the bonding here is weaker and more non-directional compared to system A. For the encapsulated systems (C and D), the ELF plots show extensive dark blue and purple regions at the interface, with only scattered red or yellow dots around the atomic nuclei. These low ELF values (≈ 0.2–0.4) indicate a minimum electron density shared between species on either side of the BN encapsulation layer. This suggests that the encapsulation acts as an electronic barrier that significantly restricts charge transfer, resulting in interactions dominated by van der Waals forces and long-range electrostatic effects defining weak physical adsorption properties. Finally, in the pristine BN system (E), the ELF intensities across the interaction region are exceptionally low (≈ 0.0–0.3), indicating a purely physical interaction with no significant electron sharing. Here, electron localization is largely confined to single atomic centers with no overlap between the drug molecule and the pristine BN plane, consistent with the chemically inert nature of the pristine BN. Overall, the ELF analysis displays a clear hierarchy in the strength of the interaction between the studied systems like adsorption energies and RDG analysis.

Conversely, BN encapsulation or pristine surfaces limit electronic coupling, leading to weak and non-specific adsorption. The ELF findings thus reinforce the conclusions drawn from NCI and charge density studies and provide a comprehensive understanding of how doping, encapsulation, and electronic localization affect the adsorption mechanism.

TD-DFT absorption spectra analysis

To gain deeper insights into drug delivery mechanisms, it is essential to monitor drug concentrations within the delivery system. In recent years, considerable interest has been directed towards the advancement of online detection technologies, such as sensors, biosensors, and optical devices. Among these, UV-Vis detectors are very effective for continuous monitoring by measuring light absorption levels and analyzing wavelength positions. As part of this approach, UV-Vis spectra for all complexes are calculated using the TD-DFT method to increase accuracy and understanding.

The UV–Vis absorption spectra of the drug molecule (Sun) calculated using TD-DFT, along with its complexes involving pristine and metal-doped BN nanostructures, are provided in Fig. 7. The drug molecule itself exhibits a prominent absorption band in the ultraviolet range, approximately between 350 and 380 nm, which is primarily due to π→π* electronic transitions in its conjugated molecular structure. The sharpness and high intensity of this peak highlight a localized electronic excitation with limited involvement in charge transfer. Upon interaction with pure B12N12 (B12N12@Sun), a red shift is observed with a significant broadening of the main absorption band, accompanied by a decrease in intensity. This shift indicates electronic coupling between the drug molecule and the BN cage, which leads to partial delocalization of excited states and changes in transition energies. Metal doping produces further changes in the optical properties. In Ni- and Pt-doped systems (B12N12@Sun and B12N12Pt@Sun), the absorption peaks shift towards longer wavelengths and extend into the visible spectrum. This red shift indicates a decrease in excitation energy due to the formation of metal-induced electronic states near the Fermi level. These states enhance the intramolecular charge transfer between the drug molecule and the doped BN framework. Furthermore, the broadening of the absorption bands indicates the availability of more accessible excited states. Of particular interest are encapsulated systems such as B12N12Ni@Sun, which exhibit distinct absorption features in the near-infrared (NIR) range (approximately 700–1100 nm). These low-energy transitions are attributed to strong charge-transfer excitations induced by metal doping and confinement effects. The appearance of NIR absorption strongly indicates a significant electronic interaction between the drug molecule and its metal-doped BN carrier. TD-DFT results show that doped and encapsulated with metal of B12N12 effectively modulate the optical behavior of the solar molecule, causing a shift to longer wavelengths and broadening of the absorption bands. It is noteworthy that Ni-doped B12N12 displays the largest broadening into the visible and NIR regions, indicating improved charge transport properties and stronger electronic coupling. It is widely recognized that the near-infrared wavelength range can serve as an effective tool for diagnostics and various biological applications52,53. These findings suggest intriguing potential for stimuli-responsive or photo-activated drug delivery applications leveraging these tailored optical properties.

Conclusion

A detailed quantum chemical analysis employing techniques such as adsorption energy evaluation, frontier molecular orbital theory, time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT), density of states (DOS), reduced density gradient (RDG), and electron localization function (ELF) mapping has illuminated the interaction dynamics between Sunvozertinib and various B12N12-based nanocage systems concluding of Ni-doped, Pt-doped, Ni encapsulated, Pt encapsulated, and pure B12N12. Among them, Ni-doped B12N12 nanocages exhibited the most exothermic adsorption energy (Eads), emphasizing their superior binding affinity and thermodynamic stability. Pt-doped nanocages also demonstrated favorable adsorption, although their interactions were slightly weaker compared to Ni-doped systems. In contrast, the encapsulated and pristine BN systems predominantly displayed weak physisorption due to their limited surface reactivity.

Quantum descriptors, such as HOMO-LUMO energy gap, electronegativity, chemical hardness, and electrophilicity index, explained a significant reduction in the energy gap for Ni- and Pt-doped B12N12 systems after Sunvozertinib adsorption. This reduction indicated improved charge transport properties and improved conductivity, features that are useful for applications such as sensors or controlled drug release systems. On the other hand, the relatively unchanged wider energy gaps of encapsulated and pristine BN systems reflect their inherent chemical inertness and restricted reactivity.

Analysis of TD-DFT absorption spectra for the doped nanocages revealed moderate red shifts in the absorption maxima (λmax), indicating electronic interactions between the Sunvozertinib orbitals and the doped-induced states. This behavior confirms the improved electronic coupling and enhanced photosensitivity in these doped systems. In support of this, DOS and PDOS analyses highlighted orbital hybridization at the Fermi level in the Ni- and Pt-doped systems, particularly through significant Ni 3d-C/O2p interactions, indicating partial covalent features during absorption. On the contrary, minimal overlap was observed for the encapsulated and pristine BN systems, indicating physical adsorption due to van der Waals. RDG (sign(λ2)ρ) scatter plots revealed localized negative regions in Ni- and Pt-doped systems, which are characteristic of covalent or hybrid bonding interactions.

This observation was corroborated by ELF mapping, which displayed intense localized regions (red/yellow zones) for Ni-doped nanocages, signifying shared electron density. In contrast, uniformly delocalized blue regions dominated the encapsulated and pristine BN systems, affirming weak physisorptive interactions. The comprehensive integration of these electronic and topological descriptors ranks the interaction strength, charge transfer efficiency, and electronic coupling with Sunvozertinib across systems as follows: Ni-doped BN > Pt-doped BN > Encapsulated BN ≈ Pristine BN.

Our calculated BSSE-corrected adsorption energies are in range − 18.99 to − 21.87 kcal/mol which, indicating moderate and favorable host–guest interactions. These values are smaller in magnitude than those reported for some previously studied drug/nanocage systems, such as curcumin32 adsorbed on pure and Pt-functionalized B12N12 nanocages (− 62.03 and 64.34 kcal/mol), metformin36 on alkaline-metal-doped B12N12 clusters (− 21.46 to − 55.03 kcal/mol), and gemifloxacin35 on metal-encapsulated B12N12 nanocages (− 37.33 to − 45.58 kcal/mol). This difference can be attributed to the larger steric demand and distinct functional-group distribution of Sunvozertinib, which may limit close surface contact and the number of simultaneous noncovalent interactions with the nanocage. Nevertheless, the obtained adsorption energies suggest sufficiently strong binding for efficient drug loading, while remaining moderate enough to allow possible release at the target site, which is an essential requirement for an effective drug delivery platform.

Crucially, these moderate adsorption energies translate to significantly shorter recovery times (τ). The lower absolute adsorption energies result in a faster recovery of the nanocage carrier. While systems with high adsorption face significant challenges regarding drug release and carrier recyclability due to excessively long recovery times, the Sunvozertinib-nanocage complexes reported here demonstrate a well-balanced profile. This ensures sufficient stability for drug transport while facilitating a feasible desorption process, highlighting the potential of Ni- and Pt-functionalized B12N12 as efficient and reusable drug-delivery vehicles.

Nickel-doped B12N12 nanocage emerges to be very promising for targeted drug delivery, efficient adsorption-mediated transport, and controlled drug release platforms due to their strong adsorption capabilities, high electronic responsiveness, and strong charge transport properties However, encapsulated B12N12 systems have the potential to be used as protective carriers or slow-release platforms to maintain drug stability during drug delivery due to their structural integrity and minimal electronic disorder.
