Endometrial thickness in patients with postmenopausal bleeding and endometrial cancer: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Joyce Liu, Angela C Nolin, Shakthi Unnithan, Alaattin Erkanli, Katherine Penvose, Sage Atkins, Sharonne Holtzman, Isabel Chess, Aliza Vattakalam, Stephanie V Blank, Jason D Wright, Benjamin B Albright, William D Hazelton, Evan R Myers, Laura J Havrilesky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveUpdated ACOG guidelines recommend a combined approach of transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) and endometrial sampling for evaluation of most postmenopausal bleeding (PMB). Although prior studies investigating the reliability of TVUS suggest that non-endometrioid endometrial cancer subtypes present with thinner endometrium, some included cases with incomplete endometrial visualization. We therefore evaluated endometrial thickness (ET) by cancer subtype while controlling for incomplete endometrial visualization.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study of patients with PMB who underwent TVUS followed by tissue-confirmed endometrial cancer at three academic centers (2013−2022). Cancers were classified as endometrioid or non-endometrioid (serous, clear cell, carcinosarcoma, undifferentiated). Fibroid presence, endometrial visualization, and race/ethnicity were recorded. Patients with incomplete endometrial visualization were excluded from endometrial thickness analysis. Appropriate nonparametric statistical comparisons were performed.ResultsAmong 171 analysis-eligible patients (121 endometrioid, 50 non-endometrioid), non-endometrioid cancers were more common among non-Hispanic Black (53%) than non-Hispanic White (20%) or Hispanic patients (25%). Patients with non-endometrioid cancers were more likely to have incompletely visualized endometria (34% vs 9.1%, p < 0.01). Among patients with complete visualization, mean ET did not differ between endometrioid and non-endometrioid cancers (16.9 vs 19.5 mm) or by race/ethnicity. ET ≤4 mm occurred in 5.6% of cancers with fully visualized endometria, without differences between subtypes.ConclusionWhen endometrium was fully visualized, non-endometrioid cancers did not present with thinner ET than endometrioid cancers, and a 4 mm cutoff may miss 5.6% of cancers. Incomplete visualization, more common in non-endometrioid disease, may lead to additional missed diagnoses, supporting universal endometrial sampling for PMB.Keywords: Endometrial cancer, Postmenopausal bleeding, Transvaginal ultrasound, Endometrial thickness, Endometrial visualization

Introduction

Postmenopausal bleeding requires prompt diagnostic workup to exclude gynecologic malignancy. From 2009 to 2026, the standard of care for the assessment of postmenopausal bleeding as recommended by the American College of Obstetrics and Gynecologists consisted of either endometrial sampling or a transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) to evaluate the thickness of the endometrium (ACOG, 2018; ACOG, 2009). If TVUS was performed, endometrial sampling was recommended when endometrial thickness exceeded 4 mm; an adequately visualized endometrium with a thickness of ≤4 mm was reported to have a 99% negative predictive value to rule out endometrial cancer (ACOG, 2018; ACOG, 2009). In April 2026, ACOG updated its guidelines to recommend a combined approach of transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) and endometrial sampling for most patients with postmenopausal bleeding, limiting TVUS alone to a small subset of patients: those with a single episode of postmenopausal bleeding, fully visualized endometrium ≤4 mm, no strong risk factors for endometrial cancer, and no barriers to prompt gynecologic evaluation (Obstet. Gynecol., 2026). This update reflects a major practice shift in response to rising endometrial cancer incidence and growing evidence that highlights the limitations of TVUS for ruling out endometrial cancer (C., 2025). However, the applicability of this new approach across all cancer subtypes and patient demographics remains unclear.

Previous evidence has suggested that the 4 mm threshold may be less reliable for non-endometrioid endometrial cancer subtypes, such as serous and clear cell carcinomas (Wang et al., 2006). Unlike endometrioid endometrial cancers, which typically arise from a background of atypical endometrial hyperplasia driven by unopposed estrogen, certain high-risk subtypes, such as serous and carcinosarcoma, are thought to develop from different molecular pathways driven by TP53 (Doll et al., 2008) and may present in a background of atrophic endometrium (Crosbie et al., 2022). Some studies have found that non-endometrioid endometrial cancers are more likely to present with a thinner endometrium on transvaginal ultrasound than endometrioid cancers (Kiff et al., 2023; Billingsley et al., 2015). However, others have reported no difference in endometrial thickness between endometrioid and non-endometrioid subtypes (Chandavarkar et al., 2013) or have even found that non-endometrioid cancers more commonly present with a thicker endometrium (Hosoi et al., 2013; Epstein et al., 2018). The ability of transvaginal ultrasound to reliably rule out endometrial cancer, and particularly its non-endometrioid subtypes, has therefore been called into question (Schramm et al., 2017).

Racial disparities in endometrial cancer subtypes are well-documented, with Black women disproportionately affected by high-risk subtypes and endometrial cancer deaths (Hicks et al., 2024). Compared to White women, Black women are diagnosed with higher grade and more advanced stage disease (Cote et al., 2015). Delays in diagnosis, particularly for high-risk subtypes of cancer, can have significant clinical impact and may exacerbate racial disparities in prognosis. Prior studies have reported lower diagnostic performance of ultrasound in Black women (Doll et al., 2024; Nolin et al., 2025). Possible underlying causes of this disparity include suboptimal ultrasound visualization of the endometrium due to a higher prevalence of obscuring fibroids among Black women (Nolin et al., 2025; Orellana et al., 2025), in whom high-risk cancer subtypes are also more common (Hicks et al., 2024; Long et al., 2013; Abel et al., 2021). Of note, the updated ACOG guidelines highlight Black race as a factor that is strongly associated with increased risk of endometrial cancer, effectively excluding Black patients from receiving a workup that is based on TVUS alone. The interplay between high fibroid incidence, disproportionate rates of non-endometrioid cancer, poor endometrial visualization on ultrasound, and Black race make the limitations of TVUS particularly worthy of further investigation in this population.

A better understanding of endometrial thickness across different endometrial cancer subtypes, with attention to race and ethnicity, is warranted to improve diagnostic accuracy and reduce disparities in care. To evaluate this, we utilized data from our PostMenopausal Bleeding Assessment (PUMBA) study, a multisite collaborative retrospective cohort study designed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of transvaginal ultrasound across different racial and ethnic backgrounds and to identify factors associated with suboptimal ultrasound-based diagnostic follow up (Nolin et al., 2025). In the current study, we compared the endometrial visibility and thickness on TVUS of endometrial cancers of endometrioid versus non-endometrioid histology from the PUMBA study, with attention to patient race, ethnicity, and the presence of fibroids.

Materials and methods

As previously described (Nolin et al., 2025; Atkins et al., 2025), the PUMBA study is an IRB-approved (Pro00111807) multisite collaborative retrospective cohort study performed at three academic centers. For the original PUMBA cohort, data were collected through an electronic medical record (EMR), Epic (Verona, Wisconsin) at each of the three academic centers. We included encounters between 1/1/2013 and 12/31/2022 for women aged 45 or older with receipt of an initial transvaginal ultrasound for a provider-coded diagnosis of postmenopausal bleeding. Patients were excluded from the current analysis if they did not have an intact uterus, had a prior transvaginal ultrasound for the same indication, or had an endometrial biopsy within 3 months prior to their ultrasound. Only the initial ultrasound performed for a coded diagnosis of postmenopausal bleeding was included; repeat ultrasounds were excluded from the analysis.

The current study is a subcohort of PUMBA participants, a retrospective cohort study including only patients with a tissue diagnosis of endometrial cancer made within 3 months after the initial transvaginal ultrasound. Our primary objective was to characterize the endometrial thickness and the false negative rate (percentage of cancers with an adequately visualized endometrium ≤4 mm) comparing endometrioid to non-endometrioid endometrial cancer subtypes. Based on our clinical observations, we hypothesized that there would be no significant difference in endometrial thickness between endometrial cancers of endometrioid and non-endometrioid histologic types. Our secondary objective was to describe the endometrial thickness of endometrioid and non-endometrioid endometrial cancers by race and ethnicity. We additionally explored fibroid status as a factor possibly affecting endometrial visualization. We hypothesized that if there was any difference in endometrial thickness between endometrial cancers of endometrioid and non-endometrioid histology, these differences would be consistent across race/ethnicity cohorts.

As previously reported (Nolin et al., 2025; Atkins et al., 2025), demographic data imported from the EMR included age, body mass index, self-reported race and ethnicity, insurance status, relevant surgical history (endometrial ablation or myomectomy), and ordering provider credentials and specialty. Race and ethnicity cohorts were defined as non-Hispanic White; non-Hispanic Black; Hispanic; and other, which included Asian, American Indian, Alaskan Native, Native Hawaiian, other Pacific Islander, and not reported. Ultrasound and pathology reports were imported from the EMR and manually verified. Variables from these reports included endometrial thickness, ability to visualize the entire endometrium (yes/no), presence/absence of fibroids, and histologic type and grade of endometrial cancer. Fibroid size and location were not specifically captured. An endometrium was deemed “not fully visualized” if there was no endometrial thickness recorded, if the endometrium was described as not visible, not fully identified, partially visible, partially obscured, or not clearly delineated (Nolin et al., 2025). All data were collected and stored within our institution's Protected Analytics Computing Environment, a secure research server designed for protected health information, with deidentified data stored in REDCap (Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN).

Histologic subtypes of endometrial cancer were classified as endometrioid vs non-endometrioid (serous, clear cell, carcinosarcoma, and undifferentiated), or other (mixed and otherwise unclassified). An alternative classification based on risk level was also evaluated, in which the low-risk category included only grade 1 and grade 2 endometrioid, while high-risk included grade 3 endometrioid, serous, clear cell, carcinosarcoma, and undifferentiated tumors given varied opinions on the risk classification of grade 3 endometrioid endometrial cancers based on clinical vs. molecular frameworks (Crosbie et al., 2022; Bosse et al., 2018). Given the ten-year clinical data collection timeframe, molecular classification was not feasible. Cases classified as “other” histological subtypes and those missing subtype information were excluded from high-risk vs. low-risk comparisons but were included in analysis involving endometrial visualization status.

Endometrial thickness (ET) was measured using TVUS and categorized as thin (≤4 mm) or thick (>4 mm) based on ACOG guidelines (ACOG, 2018; ACOG, 2009). Cases with incompletely visualized endometrium were excluded from endometrial thickness analyses but were included in comparisons of endometrial visualization status.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics. Pearson's chi-square or Fisher's exact tests were performed as appropriate to assess whether the proportions of patients with incompletely visualized, thin, or thick endometria differed significantly between those with endometrioid and non-endometrioid cancers. This was a convenience sample, and an a priori power calculation was not performed.

Results

Overall cohort description

From the PUMBA cohort of 3614 patients undergoing initial transvaginal ultrasounds for postmenopausal bleeding, 185 patients (5.1%) were diagnosed with endometrial cancer within 3 months after the index ultrasound. Among the final included cohort, no patients were younger than 50 years. 173/185 (93.5%) patients were 55 years or older, and 12/185 (6.5%) patients were ages 50–54. Of all 185 patients with endometrial cancer, 13 (7.0%) were Hispanic, 50 (27.0%) were non-Hispanic Black (NHB), 105 (56.8%) were non-Hispanic White (NHW), and 17 (9.2%) were of other/not specified race/ethnicity (Table 1). Of the 171 patients with a known histologic subtype, 121 (70.7%) were endometrioid, 23 (13.5%) were serous, 17 (9.9%) were carcinosarcoma, 7 (4.1%) clear cell, and 3 (1.8%) were undifferentiated/dedifferentiated carcinoma. NHB patients had a significantly higher rate of non-endometrioid cancers compared to NHW and Hispanic patients (26/49 [53%] vs. 20/99 [20%] vs. 3/12 [25%], χ2 = 17.43, p < 0.01; Table 2). The most common non-endometrioid cancer subtype across all race/ethnicity cohorts was serous carcinoma. Fibroids were present in 118/185 (63.8%) of patients, with the highest prevalence observed among NHB (39/50, 78%) and Hispanic patients (10/13, 76.9%) (Table 1). The endometrium was fully visualized in 81.1% of cancers (150/185).

Endometrial visualization in endometrial cancers

Incomplete endometrial visualization occurred in 18.9% of the overall cohort and varied based on race/ethnicity, histologic subtype, and fibroid status. The endometrium was fully visualized in 93/105 (88.6%) of NHW patients, 38/50 (76%) NHB patients, 9/13 (69.2%) Hispanic patients, and 10/17 (58.8%) patients of other race/ethnicities not categorized as NHB or Hispanic (Table 1). Non-endometrioid cancers were significantly more likely to have incompletely visualized endometrium than endometrioid cancers (17/50, 34% vs 11/121: 9.1%, p < 0.0001) (Table 2). 26/118 (22%) of those with fibroids had an incompletely visualized endometrium compared to 9/67 (13%) without fibroids (p = 0.18). 38/50 (76%) of patients with non-endometrioid cancers had fibroids compared to 74/121 (61%) with endometrioid cancers (p = 0.06) (Table 2). Among patients with fibroids, incomplete visualization of the endometrium was more common in non-endometrioid cancers than endometrioid cancers (39% [15/38] vs 9.5% [7/74], p < 0.01) (Supplemental Table 3). In patients with fibroids, NHB patients also had a higher percentage of incompletely visualized endometrium (11/38, 28.9%) than NHW patients (6/60, 10.0%) (p = 0.02) (Supplemental Table 3). Small subgroup sample sizes and zero-event cells limited further formal statistical comparisons (Supplemental Table 3).

Endometrial thickness

Among 143 patients with a fully visualized endometrium and known cancer subtype, 8/143 (5.6%) cancers presented with a false negative thin (≤4 mm) endometrium (Table 2). 5.6% of patients with endometrioid (6/110, 5.5%) and (2/33, 6.1%) non-endometrioid cancers had a false negative thin (≤4 mm) endometrium, with no significant difference between the endometrioid and non-endometrioid cancers (p > 0.99). The percentage of cancers presenting with a thin endometrium also did not differ significantly across race/ethnicity cohorts, although the number of cases within each cohort was small (Supplemental Table 2). There was no significant difference in the mean endometrial thickness when comparing endometrioid cancers (16.9 ± 9.1 mm) to non-endometrioid cancers (19.5 ± 11.2 mm) (p = 0.3096) (Table 2). There was also no significant difference in the mean endometrial thickness of endometrioid vs. non-endometrioid cancers across race/ethnicity cohorts (Supplemental Table 2).

When combining cancers that present with a thin endometrium or present with an incompletely visualized endometrium, 16/121 (13.2%) of endometrioid cancers and 19/50 (38%) of non-endometrioid cancers fail to demonstrate a thickened endometrium by transvaginal ultrasound (p = 0.003) (Fig. 1).

When the alternative classification of low vs. high-risk cancers (in which grade 3 endometrioid and non-endometrioid cancers were considered high-risk) was applied, there was no significant difference in the percentage of high-risk compared to low-risk cancers with a thin endometrium (2/41 = 4.9% vs. 6/103 = 5.8%, p > 0.99) (Supplemental Table 1). There was similarly no significant difference in the mean endometrial thickness when comparing low-risk cancers (16.6 ± 8.3 mm) to high-risk cancers (19.9 ± 12.2 mm) (p = 0.2494) (Supplemental Table 1).

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective cohort study, we found that even in the setting of complete endometrial visualization, 5.6% of all endometrial cancers diagnosed within 3 months after a triage TVUS performed for postmenopausal bleeding, regardless of histologic subtype, would have been missed by TVUS alone. For 18.9% of cancers, the endometrium was inadequately visualized on TVUS, necessitating endometrial biopsy, and a significantly higher proportion of non-endometrioid cancers were incompletely visualized than endometrioid cancers. Further, there were no significant differences in mean endometrial thickness or in the proportion of tumors presenting with a false negative thin endometrium between cancer subtypes after excluding inadequately visualized endometria. These findings underscore the limitations of TVUS as a reliable diagnostic tool for endometrial cancer, regardless of tumor subtype, after postmenopausal bleeding.

Our study contributes to broader debate in the literature on the reliability of TVUS as a diagnostic tool for endometrial cancer in patients presenting with postmenopausal bleeding and directly supports the updated ACOG guidance in April 2026 to limit the role of TVUS in the workup of postmenopausal bleeding. Prior to guideline changes, ACOG had cited a negative predictive value of 99% for TVUS as an initial workup for diagnosis of endometrial cancer after postmenopausal bleeding (ACOG, 2018; ACOG, 2009). However, this recommendation was challenged by studies reporting substantial false-negative rates for TVUS ranging from 7%–36%., with some suggesting higher rates among non-endometrioid endometrial cancer subtypes. (Wang et al., 2006; Kiff et al., 2023; Billingsley et al., 2015; Chandavarkar et al., 2013; Hosoi et al., 2013; Wagar et al., 2025) There has been particular emphasis on non-endometrioid cancers, likely due to hypothesized differences in etiology between endometrioid and non-endometrioid cancers. For example, uterine serous carcinomas may arise in a background of endometrial atrophy rather than hyperplasia, and some non-endometrioid cancers are less sensitive to estrogen than endometrioid cancers, potentially limiting endometrial thickening (Doll et al., 2008; Crosbie et al., 2022; del Carmen et al., 2012). These concerns likely informed ACOG's decision to shift from recommending TVUS as first line triage to recommending a combination of TVUS and endometrial biopsy for most patients with postmenopausal bleeding. However, our study demonstrates that false-negative TVUS findings are not limited to high-risk disease. We found similar proportions of both endometrioid and non-endometrioid cancers presenting with a falsely reassuring thin endometrium, even when the endometrium was fully visualized. Our results suggest that reliance on TVUS may result in missed diagnoses across all endometrial cancer histologies, rather than selectively among non-endometrioid subtypes. Our study therefore also provides support for the most recent ACOG update and underscores the importance of endometrial biopsy for most patients with postmenopausal bleeding.

Notably, at least one prior study that analyzed both endometrioid and non-endometrioid histologies similarly found no significant differences in TVUS performance between cancer subtypes, reinforcing that false-negative findings on TVUS are not limited to non-endometrioid cancers. In a study by Chandavarkar et al., 37% of low-risk endometrial cancers and 34% of high-risk endometrial cancers (36% overall) would have been missed by a 4 mm cutoff on TVUS, even with complete endometrial visualization (Chandavarkar et al., 2013). Hosoi et al. reported no significant difference in median endometrial thickness between low-risk and high-risk cancers, but using a 4 mm cutoff, more low-risk cancers were missed (Hosoi et al., 2013). In contrast, Epstein et al. found that high-risk tumors demonstrated greater endometrial thickness on TVUS than low-risk tumors (Epstein et al., 2018). In the current study, 5.6% of all cancers would have been missed, even under the most recent guideline update. When considering both false negatives and incompletely visualized endometrium cases, the overall percentage of cases in which ultrasound could miss cancer in our study exceeded 20% (Fig. 1).

Differences in reported false-negative rates may reflect methodological variation between our study and others that have evaluated the performance of TVUS. Some papers have included cases in which endometrial visualization was incomplete, some have defined a thin endometrium as ≤5 mm rather than ≤4 mm, and some only included patients who received a diagnosis of endometrial cancer via surgical pathology after hysterectomy. Our study restricted analysis of endometrial thickness measurements to cases in which the endometrium was fully visualized, defined a thin endometrium as both fully visualized and ≤ 4 mm, and included patients who received their diagnosis via endometrial biopsy, dilation and curettage or hysterectomy. These methodological differences likely contribute to the lower false-negative rate we observed compared with some prior reports. Nevertheless, even under our more restrictive inclusion criteria, our findings of 5.6% endometrial cancers missed underscore that limitations of TVUS persist even when imaging is technically adequate.

Although a 5% miss rate may seem modest, it is important to contextualize this finding with other diagnostic tests in medicine. In symptomatic patients who undergo a rule-out test for possible malignancy, diagnostic tests should ideally have a sensitivity higher than that of screening tests. One reasonable comparison is chest CT in high-risk individuals such as chronic smokers being evaluated for possible lung cancer, for whom the lifetime incidence of lung cancer is approximately 10% (Faselis et al., 2022). In these patients, a screening chest CT has a sensitivity around 97% (Bhamani et al., 2025). In contrast, our findings suggest that even when visualization is technically adequate, TVUS falls short of this level in the evaluation of postmenopausal bleeding, which is concerning given the increased pretest probability of up to 10% (Clarke et al., 2018). We also believe 5% may be an underestimate of the true false negative rate, as patients with heavy or persistent postmenopausal bleeding due to cancer may be more likely to proceed directly to endometrial sampling without a triage TVUS. Regardless, TVUS has been associated with delays in care; a recent study by Mehta et al. found that patients who underwent triage TVUS for postmenopausal bleeding had an additional 62-day delay in reaching oncology care compared to those proceeding directly to endometrial sampling (Mehta et al., n.d.).

Inadequate visualization on TVUS was a prominent finding, occurring in 18.9% of all endometrial cancers in our cohort, with a significant difference in adequate visualization between non-endometrioid and endometrioid cancers (34 vs 9%). Under new ACOG guidelines, which require full endometrial visualization for TVUS-based triage, 18.9% of patients with endometrial cancer and 34% of non-endometrioid cancers would have been ineligible for TVUS alone. Our findings also raise an important question regarding the mechanism underlying poorer endometrial visualization in non-endometrioid cancers, which has important implications for racial disparities in endometrial cancer outcomes. In fact, Black race is identified as a factor strongly associated with increased risk of endometrial cancer under the new ACOG guidelines (Obstet. Gynecol., 2026). Non-endometrioid endometrial cancer subtypes are more prevalent in Black patients (Long et al., 2013; Abel et al., 2021), and inferior performance of TVUS in Black patients has been demonstrated (Doll et al., 2024). Although it has previously been hypothesized that non-endometrioid cancers present with a thin endometrium, we found no significant difference in endometrial thickness between endometrioid and non-endometrioid cancer presentation on TVUS. Instead, our results suggest that inadequate endometrial visualization may play a more central role in diagnostic disparities. While not statistically significant, obscuring fibroids were more common in non-endometrioid cancers in our cohort (Table 2), and our group has previously demonstrated an association between fibroids and inadequate endometrial visualization in a large (N = 3614) sub-population of the PUMBA study that also included patients without a cancer diagnosis (Nolin et al., 2025). Black women are more likely to have more and larger fibroids associated with worse endometrial visualization (Nolin et al., 2025; Day Baird et al., 2003); in our current study, NHB patients with fibroids had a higher percentage of incompletely visualized endometrium than NHW patients with fibroids (29% vs 10%). Prior work by Lee et al. also found inferior endometrial visualization on TVUS in non-endometrioid endometrial cancers (Lee et al., 2024). Together, these findings suggest a plausible mechanism by which non-endometrioid cancers may be disproportionately missed or diagnosed later than endometrioid cancers. These findings also provide support for the ACOG's recommendation for endometrial biopsy in Black patients, as reliance on TVUS alone as a triage tool may exacerbate racial disparities in endometrial cancer outcomes.

Our study has several important limitations. First, its retrospective design limits our ability to infer causality and introduces the possibility of inconsistent or inaccurate documentation that may vary by ultrasound reader and clinical practice. For example, we found a small number of cases in which an endometrial thickness was reported despite incomplete visualization. Because we relied on reported values in the electronic medical record rather than original images, we could not standardize or verify endometrial thickness measurement techniques across institutions. However, this variability likely reflects real-world practice across diverse clinical settings where ultrasound techniques are not uniformly standardized. Additionally, because data were collected from three academic health systems, each of which included numerous clinical locations, we were unable to retrospectively classify whether individual sites were academic or community centers. Our findings may therefore not be fully generalizable to community practices, where greater variability in ultrasound reading experience may exist. Second, a potential limitation of this study is our use of an age cutoff ≤45 and electronic health record coding-based identification of postmenopausal bleeding, which may have risked inclusion of patients who were not postmenopausal due to coding inaccuracies. However, in our final cohort of patients with cancer, all were aged 50 years or older, supporting the validity of our selection methodology. Additionally, our cohort was limited to women who presented with postmenopausal bleeding and received a TVUS followed by a tissue biopsy within 3 months. It is possible that our 5% false negative rate is an incorrect estimation of the percentage of cancers that are missed, as some cancer cases with a thin endometrium on TVUS, may have had a biopsy after 3 months or failed to get a biopsy and ultimately been lost to follow up in our system. It is possible that non-endometrioid cancers may present with worsening symptoms sooner, so endometrioid cancers might be preferentially missed by our limited 3-month follow-up window. Finally, our cohort was small, especially when broken down by race/ethnicity, and retrospectively performed power calculations would not be informative. However, there is no known biologic basis to suggest that cancers of the same molecular or histologic type would have different characteristics between race/ethnicity cohorts, after controlling for fibroids and endometrial visibility. Strengths of this study include its multicenter design and racial/ethnic diversity. We controlled for incomplete visualization of the endometrium, which was not consistently seen in other studies that investigate the reliability of TVUS.

In conclusion, our underscore the limitations of TVUS for triaging endometrial cancer, regardless of endometrioid or non-endometrioid histology, and provide support for the updated ACOG guidelines that recommend a combined approach of TVUS and endometrial biopsy in most patients with postmenopausal bleeding. Even when endometrial visualization is adequate, reliance on a thin endometrium on ultrasound may delay diagnosis in at least 5% of cancers. In the setting of postmenopausal bleeding, tissue-based diagnosis should not be delayed, regardless of TVUS results.
