Synthesis and Antifungal Evaluation of a Ynone-Tethered 2-Pyrone Derivative against Phytopathogenic Fungi
Authors: Heeeun Kim, Juwon Yang, Jiyeun Park, Hyerim Kim, Min-Gi Kim, Kye Sung Park, Keedon Han, Hokyoung Son, Yonghoon Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Numerous fungicides are essential for controlling phytopathogens, enabling modern industrial agriculture to maximize productivity and profitability. However, the extensive use of widely employed fungicides, such as tebuconazole and carbendazim, has led to reduced efficacy due to the emergence of resistant plant-pathogenic fungi. Accordingly, in this study, we synthesized a 2-pyrone chemotype bearing a ynone substituent, designated as ynone 6 and evaluated its potential as an antifungal scaffold. This representative lead compound exhibited broad-spectrum antifungal activity against five major phytopathogens, including Fusarium graminearum and Botrytis cinerea. Ynone 6 demonstrated potent in planta control efficacy across various host tissues, including wheat spikes, fruits, and leaves, with inhibitory effects comparable to those of tebuconazole. Sensitivity screening using six knockout mutants targeting well-established antifungal pathways revealed that the sensitivity profiles of the mutants differed significantly between conidial germination and hyphal growth, suggesting that the mode of action of ynone 6 is stage-dependent and may involve multiple targets. Collectively, the potent in vitro activity of ynone 6 and its consistent in planta efficacy across multiple host-pathogen systems highlight the ynone-tethered 2-pyrone scaffold as a promising lead scaffold for the development of effective antifungal agents.

Crop diseases caused by fungal pathogens remain a major constraint on global food security, leading to substantial yield losses and economic damage. It is estimated that approximately 14% of global crop production is lost to plant diseases, with fungi accounting for a significant proportion (42%) of these losses (Savary et al., 2019; Van Alfen, 2014). Among phytopathogenic fungi, Botrytis cinerea and Fusarium graminearum are particularly important due to their broad host range and increasing prevalence worldwide. B. cinerea, a necrotrophic pathogen, infects over 200 plant species and causes gray mold disease, whereas F. graminearum is the primary causal agent of Fusarium head blight (FHB) in major cereal crops, leading not only to yield loss but also to contamination with harmful mycotoxins (Chen et al., 2021; Fillinger and Walker, 2015; Hahn, 2014; Jeon et al., 2024; Naqvi et al., 2025).

Management of these pathogens relies heavily on chemical fungicides. However, the long-term efficacy of existing fungicides has been severely compromised by the widespread emergence of resistance in field populations. Many fungicide classes, including quinone outside inhibitors, succinate dehydrogenase inhibitors, demethylation inhibitors, have all encountered resistance issues due to their extensive and repeated use (Lim et al., 2025; Ma and Michailides, 2005; Subramanian et al., 2025; Vleeshouwers et al., 2011). In addition, increasing regulatory restrictions, as well as environmental and human health concerns associated with pesticide exposure and residues, further limit the applicability of conventional fungicides (Fisher et al., 2018; Lucas et al., 2015; Steinberg and Gurr, 2020). These challenges highlight the urgent need for antifungal agents with distinct modes of action and effective activity against resistant pathogens that can overcome or circumvent existing resistance mechanisms.

To address these challenges, expanding the fungicide portfolio with new molecular scaffolds represents a key strategy for achieving sustainable disease control. In particular, antifungal agents that exhibit robust biological activity while maintaining stability and selectivity are highly desirable for practical agricultural applications (Brauer et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2018). From a biological perspective, compounds capable of interacting with multiple cellular processes or stress response pathways may provide an advantage in overcoming existing resistance mechanisms and reducing the likelihood of resistance development.

Within this context, 2-pyrone (α-pyrone) motifs have attracted attention as biologically relevant scaffolds. These structures are widely found in natural products and have been reported to exhibit diverse antibacterial and antifungal activities (Degani and Gordani, 2022; Evidente et al., 2003; McGlacken and Fairlamb, 2005; Shin et al., 2014; Yin et al., 2019). Previous studies have suggested that the 2-pyrone core can affect key biological properties, including membrane permeability and the ability to interact with molecular targets. Furthermore, structural variations of this scaffold have been shown to modulate antifungal activity and its spectrum, with demonstrated efficacy against a broad range of phytopathogenic fungi in both in vitro and in planta systems (Ishii et al., 2013; Li et al., 2024; McGlacken and Fairlamb, 2005; Wu et al., 2023; Xing et al., 2023).

In this study, we focused on the ynone (alkynyl-ketone) tether to the 2-pyrone scaffold as a promising antifungal lead. This element provides a compact and conformationally restricted structure while preserving a planar, π-extended surface for potential target engagement. Building on our previous synthetic investigations (Kim et al., 2026), we evaluated the broad-spectrum antifungal potential of this compound against major phytopathogenic fungi. In addition, in planta assays were conducted to assess its effectiveness in controlling FHB and gray mold. To gain further insight into its biological mode of action, we analyzed the sensitivity of F. graminearum deletion mutants lacking key genes that serve as core components of established antifungal pathways. Our findings demonstrate the potential of the ynone-tethered 2-pyrone as a promising platform for antifungal development and provide new biological insight into its activity against major plant pathogens.

Materials and Methods

General information for chemical synthesis

Unless otherwise noted, all reactions were performed in flame-dried glassware under an argon atmosphere using anhydrous solvents. Anhydrous solvents were prepared by distillation over the indicated drying agents and transferred under argon: tetrahydrofuran (THF) and diethyl ether (Et2O) over Mg/anthracene; toluene over Na/K; dichloromethane (CH2Cl2) and methanol (MeOH) over Mg. N,N-Dimethylformamide and triethylamine were dried using an adsorption solvent purification system based on molecular sieves.

Thin-layer chromatography was performed on Macherey-Nagel POLYGRAM SIL/UV254 plates (Düren, Germany). Flash column chromatography was conducted using Merck silica gel 60 (40–63 μm; Darmstadt, Germany) with technical grade solvents. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded on Bruker AVANCE III (400 and 600 MHz; Karlsruhe, Germany) in the solvents indicated. The solvent signals were used as references, and the chemical shifts were converted to the TMS scale (CDCl3: δC = 77.0 ppm; residual CHCl3 in CDCl3: δH = 7.26 ppm; CD3CN: δC = 118.26 and 1.32 ppm). FT-IR spectra were obtained on a Thermo Scientific Nicolet 6700 (Madison, WI, USA) and are reported as absorption frequencies (cm−1). High-resolution mass spectra were recorded on an AB SCIEX Q-TOF 5600 (Concord, ON, Canada).

Synthesis and characterization of the ynone-tethered 2-pyrone

The ynone-tethered 2-pyrone (6) was synthesized in six steps starting from 4-hydroxy-6-methyl-2H-pyran-2-one (1), which was purchased from Sigma Aldrich, following the methodology previously reported by our group (Kim et al., 2026) with minor modification for scale optimization for this study. Specifically, to a solution of 1 (7.5 g, 60 mmol, 1.0 equiv) in acetone (100 mL) were added potassium carbonate (K2CO3) (25.2 g, 183 mmol, 3.0 equiv) and dimethyl sulfate (DMS) (11.4 mL, 120 mmol, 2.0 equiv). The mixture was stirred overnight at room temperature. After removal of the solvent under reduced pressure and purification by flash column chromatography, methylated product 2 was isolated as a white solid (85% yield).

Following the established aldol condensation protocol, a mixture of 2 (6.5 g, 46 mmol, 1.0 equiv) and benzaldehyde (5.7 mL, 56 mmol, 1.2 equiv) was added to a solution of magnesium methoxide, prepared in situ from magnesium turnings (3.3 g, 136 mmol, 3.0 equiv) in dry MeOH (100 mL). The reaction was heated under reflux at 80°C for 5 h. After cooling, the residue was treated with aqueous acetic acid (AcOH/H2O, 1:4) and extracted with dichloromethane (CH2Cl2). Recrystallization from MeOH afforded alkene 3 as a yellow solid (44% yield). The installation of the aldehyde functionality was achieved through dihydroxylation and subsequent oxidative cleavage. Compound 3 (4.3 g, 19 mmol, 1.0 equiv) was treated with potassium osmate(VI) dihydrate (K2OsO4·2H2O) (0.14 g, 0.38 mmol, 0.02 equiv) and 4-methylmorpholine N-oxide (NMO) (2.7 g, 23 mmol, 1.2 equiv), and citric acid monohydrate (7.9 g, 38 mmol, 2.0 equiv) in t-butanol (t-BuOH)/water mixture (1:1). The resulting crude diol was oxidatively cleaved by using phenyliodine(III) diacetate (PIDA) (6.7 g, 21 mmol, 1.1 equiv) in CH2Cl2 (90 mL). Purification via flash column chromatography provided the key intermediate, 4-methoxy-2-oxo-2H-pyran-6-carbaldehyde (4), as a white solid (77% yield). To a solution of aldehyde 4 (400 mg, 2.6 mmol) in THF (20 mL), 1-propynylmagnesium bromide (0.5 M in THF, 6.8 mL, 3.4 mmol, 1.3 equiv) was added dropwise at 0°C. The reaction was maintained at 0°C for 1 h and then quenched with saturated aqueous NH4Cl at room temperature. The organic mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate (EtOAc) and concentrated under reduced pressure to give the crude alcohol.

Purification by flash column chromatography (5:1 dichloromethane/ethyl acetate) afforded propargyl alcohol 5 as a white solid (88% yield): Rf 0.26 (5:1 dichloromethane/ethyl acetate); 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) δ 6.24 (dd, J = 2.2, 0.7 Hz, 1H), 5.48 (d, J = 2.3 Hz, 1H), 5.12–5.07 (m, 1H), 3.82 (s, 3H), 1.89 (d, J = 2.3 Hz, 3H); 13C NMR (151 MHz, CD3CN) δ 172.2, 164.5, 163.5, 99.6, 89.3, 83.4, 76.5, 61.6, 57.2, 3.4; HRMS (ESI) m/z: [M+H]+ Calcd for C10H11O4 195.07; Found 195.0649. Subsequently, oxidation was carried out by treating 5 with manganese(IV) oxide (MnO2) (4.5 g, 52 mmol, 20 equiv) in CH2Cl2 (25 mL) overnight. After filtration through a Celite pad and chromatographic purification (3:2 hexane/EtOAc), the target 6-(but-2-ynoyl)-4-methoxy-2H-pyran-2-one (ynone 6) was isolated as a white solid (91% yield). The spectral data for compounds 2–4 and 6 were consistent with those previously reported (Kim et al., 2026), and the raw spectral data for all compounds analyzed in this study are provided in the Supplementary information (Supplementary Fig. 1–11).

Fungal strains and culture conditions

Five phytopathogenic fungi, F. graminearum Z-3639, Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici 4287, B. cinerea B05.10, Magnaporthe oryzae KJ201, and Stemphylium vesicarium KACC 44528, were used in this study. All strains were maintained on potato dextrose agar (PDA) at 25°C and stored as mycelia and conidia in 20% glycerol at −80°C.

To prepare conidial inocula for antifungal activity assays, species-specific induction conditions were applied as previously described. For B. cinerea, F. graminearum, and S. vesicarium, conidia were harvested from mycelia exposed to blue light as a stress condition (Leslie and Summerell, 2006; Liu et al., 2019; Suzuki et al., 1977). F. oxysporum conidia were obtained after incubating mycelial plugs in potato dextrose broth (PDB) with shaking (Di Pietro et al., 2001). For M. oryzae, conidiation was induced by exposing agar plugs to fluorescent light (Kim et al., 2009).

For genetic transformation, the F. graminearum wild-type strain Z-3639 was used as the parental strain, and culture media were prepared according to the Fusarium laboratory manual (Leslie and Summerell, 2006).

Antifungal activity assays

Antifungal activity was evaluated through conidial germination and mycelial growth inhibition assays performed with reference to the CLSI M38 inoculum recommendations for filamentous fungi. Conidial germination assays were performed in 96-well microtiter plates using 0.5× PDB, with a final concentration of 1 × 104 conidia/mL in all assays. Fungal growth in liquid culture was quantified by measuring optical density after 2 days of incubation with the indicated compounds. The viability of F. graminearum knockout mutants was further assessed using PrestoBlue reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions to evaluate fungal metabolic activity. For mycelial growth inhibition assays, fresh mycelial plugs (5 mm diameter) were placed at the center of PDA plates (containing 1.5% micro agar from Duchefa Biochemie) supplemented with various concentrations of ynone 6. Plates were incubated at 25°C until the control colonies reached the edge of the Petri dish (55 × 12 mm). The final solvent concentration in all assays was maintained below 2%, at which no antifungal activity was observed. The half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) was determined by measuring colony diameters followed by statistical analysis.

Fluorescence staining assay for fungal viability

To evaluate the viability of germinated conidia, samples were treated with ynone 6, with an equal volume of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) serving as a control. Following treatment, the fungal samples were collected by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was removed. The samples were then incubated in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing a mixture of fluorescein diacetate (FDA; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and propidium iodide (PI; Sigma-Aldrich) at final concentrations of 5 μg/mL and 10 μg/mL, respectively. After incubation for 7 min at room temperature, the samples were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in sterile distilled water. The stained conidia were observed using a DM6B microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with a Leica DMC6200 camera.

Plant infection assays

The control potential of ynone 6 against FHB was assessed in a greenhouse with the wheat cultivar Jokyung. Seeds were planted in 17 cm pots (15 seeds/pot) containing a soil mixture (potting soil/compost, 3:1, v/v) and grown in a greenhouse at 24°C with a 16 h day/8 h night light cycle. For spray inoculation, flowering wheat head spikes were sprayed with different concentrations of ynone 6 using an atomizer. Once the chemical had completely dried, an equal volume of conidial suspensions (1 × 106 conidia/mL). For point inoculation, wheat heads were inoculated with 10 μL of a 1:1 mixture of ynone 6 and conidial suspensions (1 × 106 conidia/mL). The final concentrations of ynone 6 were adjusted to be identical to those used in the spray inoculation. Mock-inoculated plants were treated with 10 μL of sterile distilled water, and the control groups were sprayed with an equal volume of DMSO. Plastic bags were used to cover the wheat heads for 24 h to maintain high humidity. The experiments were performed with five replicates per concentration, and disease progression was assessed two weeks post-inoculation. Tebuconazole was used as a reference to evaluate the in vivo antifungal activity of ynone 6.

The control efficacy of ynone 6 against gray mold was evaluated on four different ripe fruits, including strawberry (Fragaria x ananassa cv. Kuemsil), blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum cv. Duke), apple (Malus domestica cv. Fuji), and tomato (Solanum lycopersicum cv. Amoroso), as well as detached tomato leaves (S. lycopersicum cv. Seogwang). Before inoculation, ynone 6 and tebuconazole were applied at various concentrations using an atomizer. For fruit assays, 5 μL droplets of conidial suspension (5 × 105 conidia/mL in 0.5× PDB) were placed onto the surface of the host tissue after the chemicals had completely dried. For leaf assays, fresh mycelial plugs (5 mm in diameter) were placed at the center of detached tomato leaves. The inoculated plant materials were incubated in the dark at 23°C in a moist box. The assays were performed with five biological replicates for strawberry, blueberry, and tomato fruit, whereas apple fruit and detached tomato leaf assays were conducted with two and three biological replicates, respectively. Disease severity was quantified by measuring the total lesion area, including the initial area of the inoculum (droplet or plug), using ImageJ software (version 1.54p; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Genetic manipulations and fungal mutant construction

Fusion PCR products for gene knockout were constructed through the double-joint PCR method (Yu et al., 2004). To generate knockout mutants, the 5′ and 3′ flanking regions of the target genes were amplified from wild-type F. graminearum genomic DNA extracted via the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide-based method (Leslie and Summerell, 2006). These regions were amplified using the primers 5F/5R and 3F/3R, respectively, and subsequently fused with the geneticin resistance cassette obtained from the pII99 plasmid (Namiki et al., 2001). Final constructs were amplified using nested primers according to the split-marker approach and transformed into wild-type protoplasts, as previously described (Han et al., 2007). All transformants were confirmed through PCR and Southern blot hybridization (Supplementary Fig. 12). Southern blot hybridization was performed using the North2South Biotin Random Prime Labeling Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The PCR primers used in this study were synthesized by the oligonucleotide synthesis facility (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea) and are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software. Differences between groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test. Significant differences were indicated with asterisks (*P < 0.05 **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001).

Results

Synthesis of a ynone-tethered 2-pyrone

To implement the integration of 2-pyrone and ynone moieties, we used the ynone-tethered 2-pyrone 6 as a representative molecule (Fig. 1). For the terminal propyne substituent (R), a minimal methyl group was introduced to maintain structural simplicity and facilitate the initial validation of the scaffold’s biological activity.

Compound 6 was synthesized from commercially available 2-pyrone 1 following our previously established protocol (Kim et al., 2026), beginning with the methylation of 1 to afford 2. To install the aldehyde functional group, direct benzylic oxidation of 2 using selenium dioxide was initially considered based on previous reports (Suzuki et al., 1975). However, we found that the aldol condensation followed by oxidative cleavage, as developed by the Trauner group (McCracken et al., 2012; Rizzo and Trauner, 2018), offered a more practical and reliable alternative. By employing this strategy, the key intermediate aldehyde 4 was successfully synthesized on a gram-scale (~5 g). Subsequent addition of 1-propynylmagnesium bromide to 4 afforded propargylic alcohol 5, which was then oxidized with MnO2 to yield ynone 6. The overall synthetic route was robust and scalable.

In vitro antifungal activity of 2-pyrone derivatives series

To evaluate the antifungal efficacy of the ynone-tethered 2-pyrone scaffold, we screened the 2-pyrone derivatives against five aggressive phytopathogens, including B. cinerea, F. graminearum, F. oxysporum, M. oryzae, and S. vesicarium, using a high-throughput screening platform. This platform, based on fungal conidia, was employed to assess the early germination responses of these pathogens to the compounds. While the majority of compounds showed no detectable antifungal activity at concentrations up to 100 μg/mL, ynone 6 exhibited broad-spectrum activity across all five tested pathogens (Table 1). Specifically, B. cinerea was the most sensitive to ynone 6, with growth inhibited at 6.25 μg/mL. Significant activity was also observed against F. graminearum, F. oxysporum, and S. vesicarium at 12.5 μg/mL. Notably, when compared to the commercial fungicides, ynone 6 demonstrated superior or comparable control efficiency. For instance, it showed stronger activity against F. oxysporum than tebuconazole, with minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values of 12.5 μg/mL and 25 μg/mL, respectively.

The effect of ynone 6 on conidial viability was evaluated using an FDA and PI double-staining assay (Fig. 2). Within living cells, non-fluorescent FDA is enzymatically converted into green fluorescent fluorescein, whereas the nuclear dye PI cannot penetrate cells with intact membranes. In the untreated control, all five pathogens exhibited normal growth with distinct green fluorescence. However, treatment with ynone 6 at the MIC significantly inhibited the growth of germinated hyphae across the tested pathogens. Notably, while the apical regions often retained green fluorescence, the conidia and preceding hyphal segments germinated from the conidia primarily exhibited intense red fluorescence. This distinct staining pattern indicates that while the growth tips remained partially metabolically active, the initially formed hyphal tissues underwent significant membrane disruption following treatment with ynone 6.

Antifungal activity of ynone 6 on various host tissues

To evaluate the in planta antifungal efficacy of ynone 6, we investigated its control efficiency using diverse pathogen-host systems targeting F. graminearum and B. cinerea. First, in the wheat head assay, F. graminearum was inoculated using two independent methods, comprising point inoculation and spray inoculation. In the point inoculation assay, the untreated control exhibited typical FHB symptoms, where severe and rapid blight spread from the initial infection site across the entire wheat heads and rachis (Fig. 3A). Tebuconazole treatment showed a dose-dependent inhibitory effect, with a significant decrease in disease severity observed at 40 μg/mL (Fig. 3B). Similarly, ynone 6 treatment effectively suppressed disease progression, restricting symptoms to only a few spikelets while most tissues remained healthy and green. Specifically, at concentrations of 40 μg/mL and 50 μg/mL, ynone 6 treatment led to a significant reduction in the number of infected spikelets.

In the spray inoculation assay, although disease progression in the wild-type strain was less aggressive than in the point inoculation assay, ynone 6 still demonstrated strong antifungal efficacy (Fig. 3C, 3D). At 7 days post-inoculation, the wild-type inoculated wheat spikes exhibited necrosis primarily in the basal region. In contrast, no necrotic symptoms were detected in the groups treated with 12.5 μg/mL of either tebuconazole or ynone 6.

Given the broad host spectrum of B. cinerea, the antifungal activity of ynone 6 was evaluated across four different fruits and tomato leaves. Except for strawberries, which were incubated for 72 h due to their rapid softening, all inoculated fruits were incubated for 6 days to allow sufficient disease development. Consistent with the results observed for F. graminearum inoculation, ynone 6 exhibited effective, dose-dependent disease suppression across most tested hosts (Fig. 4A, 4B). Specifically, in strawberry, apple, and tomato, a significant suppression compared to the untreated control was confirmed starting from a concentration of 25 μg/mL, and significant suppression in blueberry was observed from 50 μg/mL onwards. Notably, the compound reduced lesion development not only on the surface but also within internal tissues, as evidenced by the limited expansion of necrotic areas in cross-sectioned fruits (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, a significant reduction in lesion size was also observed in tomato leaves following ynone 6 treatment, starting from a concentration of 50 μg/mL (Fig. 4D, 4E).

Collectively, these results demonstrate the potent in planta efficacy of ynone 6 and suggest its strong capability to inhibit both surface colonization and internal tissue invasion by pathogen across diverse host-pathogen systems.

Sensitivity screening of knockout mutants targeting known antifungal pathways

To investigate the potential mode of action of ynone 6, we performed a mutant sensitivity screening, a phenotype-based approach used to infer functional pathways associated with antifungal responses. We have previously established this methodology in F. graminearum, demonstrating that distinct sensitivity profiles of knockout mutants can support the prediction of the mode of action (Shin et al., 2026). In the previous screening system, mutants associated with fungicide target pathways of known modes of action were employed, including FgSDHC1, which encodes a succinate dehydrogenase involved in mitochondrial respiration, FgHOG1, which is a key component of the HOG pathway involved in osmotic stress signaling, and FgCYP51A, which encodes a sterol-modifying enzyme involved in ergosterol biosynthesis (Table 2). To further expand the coverage of fungicide target pathways, three additional knockout mutants, Δfgcgs1, Δfgorp1, and Δfgerg24b, were generated and incorporated into the existing screening system in this study (Fig. 5A). FgCGS1 is involved in methionine biosynthesis and is associated with the target pathway of anilinopyrimidine fungicides. FgORP1 functions in lipid homeostasis, transfer, and storage and is related to oxysterol-binding protein inhibitor-type fungicides. FgERG24B functions in sterol biosynthesis in cellular membranes and is associated with amine fungicides. The generated mutants exhibited no detectable growth defects compared with the wild-type strain (Supplementary Fig. 13).

Conidial germination assays revealed that all six knockout mutants exhibited increased sensitivity to ynone 6 compared to the F. graminearum wild-type strain (Fig. 5B, 5C). Notably, the Δfghog1 mutant showed the highest sensitivity, displaying a pronounced growth defect at 1.5 μg/mL ynone 6. To determine whether this sensitivity was specific to conidial germination or also affected vegetative growth, a traditional agar plate-based assay was performed. Interestingly, the results of the hyphal growth assay differed markedly from those observed in the germination assay (Fig. 6A, 6B). While hyphal growth patterns were somewhat irregular, at the highest concentration tested (100 μg/mL), the mutants generally exhibited greater sensitivity compared to the wild type, consistent with the conidia germination assay. However, at lower concentrations, several mutants paradoxically displayed enhanced resistance relative to the wild type. In particular, the Δfghog1 and Δfgcgs1 mutants showed greater resistance to ynone 6 than the wild type at all concentrations except 100 μg/mL.

Although the specific antifungal targets among the six candidates in this screening system could not be identified, the observed inconsistency between developmental stages suggests that the efficacy and mode of action of ynone 6 may be stage-dependent. Furthermore, the varying sensitivity profiles among the mutants suggest the possibility that ynone 6 may act through multiple targets.

Discussion

The versatile nature of the 2-pyrone motif as a building block played a crucial role in our structural strategy. As the 2-pyrone core is easily accessible from common β-ketoesters, it allowed for the efficient and systemic introduction of various side chains (Lee, 2015). This synthetic accessibility is well-documented in several studies, highlighting 2-pyrone as a scaffold that can be readily functionalized to modulate biological potency. For instance, substitutions around the pyrone ring, such as at the C-3 or C-5 positions, or the incorporation of heteroatoms, have been reported to modulate both potency and spectrum, with antimicrobial activity against both ascomycete and oomycete pathogens (Ishii et al., 2013; Li et al., 2024; McGlacken and Fairlamb, 2005; Wu et al., 2023; Xing et al., 2023). Building on these precedents, the findings in the present study further extend the scope of this scaffold by focusing on an ynone (alkynyl-ketone) tether at other strategic positions to fine-tune both electronic and steric properties. Notably, alkyne-bearing substituents are recognized features in crop-protection chemistry and are found in several commercial fungicide classes (Del Rio Flores et al., 2022; Lamberth, 2009; Li et al., 2021). Indeed, the ynone-tethered analog 6 was successfully evaluated and exhibited potent antifungal activity against a diverse range of fungal pathogens. This demonstrates that the 2-pyrone architecture provides a highly adaptable structural platform, with the integrated ynone moiety serving as the key functional group for driving effective antifungal activity.

Our study demonstrated that ynone 6 possesses broad-spectrum antifungal activity against the conidia of various phytopathogens. Treatment with ynone 6 resulted in severe growth defects and significant membrane disruption during the early stages of fungal development. This finding is critical, as conidia represent a vital stage in the life cycle of phytopathogenic fungi, playing a pivotal role in both plant infection and disease dissemination under field conditions (Drakopoulos et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2023). For instance, in F. graminearum, conidial germination on flowering wheat head constitutes a critical early developmental step in the establishment of FHB. Similarly, in B. cinerea, conidial germination on the host surface is essential for initiating infection, as it enables germ tube emergence, surface recognition, and penetration, ultimately leading to necrotrophic colonization of plant tissues. Expanding upon these observations, the in planta efficacy of ynone 6 was further validated through both infection models and spray application assays. For F. graminearum, treatment with ynone 6 led to substantial disease suppression on flowering wheat heads. Furthermore, the protective efficacy of ynone 6 was clearly demonstrated against B. cinerea across various fruits and leaf models. Notably, our results confirmed that ynone 6 not only inhibited superficial fungal growth on the host surface but also achieved significant disease suppression within the fruit interior. This sustained inhibition suggests that ynone 6 may possess localized penetrative activity, establishing a chemical barrier within the superficial host tissues.

To investigate the potential mode of action of bioactive compounds, chemical-genetic profiling systems have been established in various fungal species to identify pathway-specific sensitivity patterns associated with defined molecular targets (Jiang et al., 2008; Parsons et al., 2006; Rodriguez-Suarez et al., 2007). In these systems, compounds with single-target modes of action typically induce characteristic sensitivity or resistance phenotypes in mutants associated with the corresponding target pathway. Similarly, in F. graminearum, this screening platform has previously established a clear precedent for phenotypic patterns when a specific target pathway is disrupted (Shin et al., 2026). A well-characterized example of a defined fungicide mode of action is provided by fludioxonil, which targets the Os-1 histidine kinase and disrupts osmotic stress signaling pathways (Ochiai et al., 2001; Wang et al., 2025). Upon application to the Δfgos1 mutant, fludioxonil elicits pronounced resistance during both conidial germination and hyphal growth. In this study, to gain mechanistic insight into the mode of action of ynone 6, we employed a mutant sensitivity screening assay utilizing six gene knockout mutants corresponding to known antifungal targets and their associated pathways. However, ynone 6 did not induce a clearly defined sensitivity pattern restricted to a specific fungicide-target pathway mutant. Although Δfghog1 exhibited particularly high sensitivity during germination, all tested mutants displayed altered sensitivity relative to the wild-type strain. Previous chemical-genetic profiling studies have demonstrated that compounds associated with multiple cellular functions can exhibit complex sensitivity profiles involving diverse functional pathways (Parsons et al., 2006), raising the possibility that the broad sensitivity changes observed across the tested mutants following ynone 6 treatment may result from a mode of action involving multiple cellular pathways rather than a single defined target.

Ynone 6 activity also appeared to be stage dependent. In the germination assays, all tested mutants exhibited increased sensitivity to ynone 6 relative to the wild-type strain, whereas vegetative growth assays revealed diverse hyphal growth patterns and sensitivity profiles among the mutants. While most mutants displayed resistance to ynone 6 at lower concentrations, distinct phenotypic divergence became apparent at the highest concentration of 100 μg/mL. This discrepancy suggests that the inhibitory response to ynone 6 differs depending on the developmental stage and physiological state of the fungus. Our FDA and PI double-staining results further support this stage-dependent inhibitory model. We observed that while the newly extended hyphal tips occasionally maintained viability, the proximal segments, which emerged from the conidia, exhibited extensive membrane damage. These findings collectively indicate that the inhibitory impact of ynone 6 is differentially modulated by the physiological state of the fungus, pointing toward a mode of action distinct from that of conventional single-target fungicides.

Taken together, our findings demonstrate that ynone 6 exhibits notable antifungal potency, positioning it as a promising candidate for future development. The structural features of ynone 6 provide a versatile framework amenable to systematic modification, offering substantial scope for lead optimization.
