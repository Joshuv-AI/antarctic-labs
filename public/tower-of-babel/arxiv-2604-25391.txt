Effects on \varv ​ sin ⁡ i \varv\sin i determinations of O-stars from 3D model atmospheres with large turbulent velocities
L. Delbroek, J.O. Sundqvist, F. Backs, T. Ceulemans, D. Debnath, P. Schillemans

Abstract
Context. When studying massive stars and their life cycles, rotation plays a key role. Hence, understanding the rotation of these stars is crucial when determining their properties, or for constraining evolutionary models. Aims. We examine the reliability of the standard methods to derive projected rotation speeds \varv ​ sin ⁡ i \varv\sin i from photospheric spectra of hot, massive stars in the presence of large turbulent velocities. Methods. We include rotation in the spectral synthesis of O-stars by means of three-dimensional (3D) model atmospheres showing significant photospheric turbulent velocities. We then use these as mock-observations to back-test the Fourier Transform (FT) and goodness-of-fit (GOF) methods commonly used for empirical determination of \varv ​ sin ⁡ i \varv\sin i when the turbulent velocity field is not known. Results. When the expected \varv ​ sin ⁡ i > \varv macro \varv\sin i>\varv_{\rm macro} , with \varv macro \varv_{\rm macro} the macroturbulent velocity, FT \varv ​ sin ⁡ i \varv\sin i determinations (most of the time) give reasonable results. However, if \varv ​ sin ⁡ i < \varv macro \varv\sin i<\varv_{\rm macro} the method is no longer reliable. Results from the GOF method show that if one parameter is not significantly larger than the other, empirical best-fit values may be located in a large region of the \varv ​ sin ⁡ i \varv\sin i – \varv macro \varv_{\rm macro} parameter space, independent of the true values. The degenerate region follows well the shape of the empirical formula found by 11 , C = \varv macro 2 / 2 + ( \varv ​ sin ⁡ i ) 2 / 4 C=\sqrt{\varv_{\rm macro}^{2}/2+(\varv\sin i)^{2}/4} , with C i ≥ C e C_{i}\geq C_{e} for C i C_{i} the inferred value and C e C_{e} the expected. Conclusions. Our analysis shows clearly that, generally, only the sum \varv macro 2 / 2 + ( \varv ​ sin ⁡ i ) 2 / 4 \sqrt{\varv_{\rm macro}^{2}/2+(\varv\sin i)^{2}/4} can be approximated through the standard spectroscopic methods used to infer these parameters individually. Only in the case where one of the two clearly dominates, can good constraints on the dominating parameter be derived. This demonstrates that previously found empirical correlations between \varv ​ sin ⁡ i \varv\sin i and \varv macro \varv_{\rm macro} as well as derived statistical distributions of observed rotation rates for O-star populations will need to be re-analysed and re-interpreted.

Introduction

The influence of rotation on massive stars already begins during the initial collapse of a rotating molecular cloud
(
2
;
23
;
5
)
, and lasts up to their brutal deaths as a core-collapse supernova or hypernova (
19

17
;
15
).

Even though rotation influences the entire life cycle of a massive star, we can not directly measure the rotation rate in most cases (an exception to this is a small number of strongly magnetic massive O-stars, e.g.
30
). Hence, the projected stellar rotation,
\varv\sin i
, is commonly deduced (
i
being the inclination angle, in this work sometimes also referred to as
\alpha
) from the observed broadened line-spectrum. This approach, however, has one primary issue, which is the potential degeneracy between the rotational line broadening and the other broadening processes in the line-formation regions. From observations of absorption lines in O-stars, it has long been known that a wide range of velocities must be present in their atmospheres
(
29
;
3
)
. The lines are much broader (typical velocity dispersions
\sigma_{\rm V}\sim 50
km/s) than implied by thermal motions and observed line shapes cannot be explained by stellar rotation alone
(
3
;
10
;
22
;
28
;
14
;
27
;
25
)
. To overcome the issue of separating rotational broadening from this ‘turbulent’ contributor, empirical techniques such as the Fourier transform (FT) method, which looks for the first zero in the Fourier power spectrum to determine
\varv\sin i
(see section
3.1
), and the goodness-of-fit (GOF) method (see section
3.2
), which uses the different shapes of the rotational and turbulent broadening to find
\varv\sin i
through a fitting algorithm, have been utilised
(
7
;
28
)
. However,
31
showed that these methods may become unreliable at
\varv\sin i\la 50
km/s due to the degeneracy of the broadening components (see also
11
;
16
).

Recent spectral synthesis directly from 3D model atmospheres
(
24
;
4
)
shows that this ‘extra broadening’ indeed is naturally reproduced by the large turbulent velocities present in such simulations of O-star photospheres.

We note that this mimics results previously found for the Sun
(

, however the characteristic turbulent velocities are more than an order of magnitude larger for O-stars.
These models, however, did not include rotation and thus the question regarding separation between turbulent and rotational broadening could not be addressed. In this letter we introduce rotation in the spectral synthesis of our 3D models and use this to analyse the total broadening as well as back-track the reliability of the empirical standard 1D methods.

3.2
Goodness-of-fit determination

For our macroturbulent velocity determinations we assume an isotropic Gaussian, not the radial-tangential method
(
6
)
. This gives a straightforward connection to the velocity dispersion
\sigma_{\rm V}=\varv_{\rm macro}/\sqrt{2}
measured in the RHD models
(
4
)
. To compare to observational results, we further need to estimate the variance of our synthetic data. For this we assume errors are independent and identically distributed, following a normal distribution with the region defined by the FWHM of the line. The total standard deviation is then estimated by the standard deviation of the residual within the FWHM region, and used to determine our chi-squared distribution function and derive confidence intervals. Obviously, this method is far from perfect, meaning confidence intervals should be viewed as approximations only. Even so, best-fit values should remain unaffected and we focus on general trends of our results. In Fig.
5
every subfigure has a curved white shape in which the best-fit value lies. Depending on rotation rate and observation angle, this shape (within our confidence intervals) is more focused or stretches a larger range. When placing the observer at the equator for
\varv_{\rm rot,in}=200
km/s (Fig.
5
a), the best-fit region is small (with the
\sigma
-contours still outlining the full region), while
i=75^{\circ}
and
\varv_{\rm rot,in}=100
km/s (Fig.
5
d) has a much larger ‘banana-shaped’ best-fit region. Moreover, even for a polar observer we derive
\varv\sin i>0
; indeed, from Fig.
5

1\sigma

\varv\sin i=0
.

4
Conclusions

Both the FT and GOF methods have severe limitations for determining
\varv\sin i
in the presence of large atmospheric turbulent velocities. If
\varv\sin i
is not significantly higher than
\varv_{\rm macro}
, an accurate determination of the projected rotation rate is only possible if
\varv_{\rm macro}
can be calibrated independently with sufficient confidence (for example from 3D atmosphere simulations). If not known independently, the macroturbulent velocity can only be empirically determined if it dominates the broadening. This may significantly alter previous determinations of observed rotation rates for O-stars, and a range of previous results must likely be re-analysed and re-interpreted. This includes fiducial correlations between
\varv\sin i
and
\varv_{\rm macro}

(
26
)
as well as the statistical distribution of observed rotation speeds in O-star populations
(
21
)
.
