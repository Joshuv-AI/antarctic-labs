Balancing Weights, Directed Sparsification, and Augmenting Paths
Jason Li

Abstract
We present a randomized augmenting paths-based algorithm to compute the maximum flow in a directed, uncapacitated graph in almost m + n ​ F m+nF time, matching the algorithm of Karger and Levine for undirected graphs (SICOMP 2015). Combined with an initial n \sqrt{n} rounds of blocking flow to reduce the value of F F , we obtain a maximum flow algorithm with running time m ​ n 1 / 2 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) mn^{1/2+o(1)} . For combinatorial, augmenting paths-based algorithms, this is the first improvement over Dinic’s algorithm for moderately sparse graphs. To obtain our algorithm, we introduce a new technique to re-weight the edges of a strongly connected directed graph so that each cut is approximately balanced: the total weight of edges in one direction is within a constant factor of the total weight in the other direction. We then adapt Karger and Levine’s technique of sampling edges from the newly weighted residual graph, ensuring that an augmenting path exists in the sampled graph with high probability. One technical difficulty is that our balancing weights have to be dynamically maintained upon changes to the residual graph. Surprisingly, we can black box the dynamic data structure from the recent interior point method-based flow algorithm of van den Brand et al. (FOCS 2024).

Introduction

In the (uncapacitated) maximum flow problem, we are given a directed graph and two vertices
s
and

]
, push-relabel

There is a randomized, augmenting paths-based algorithm that computes the maximum flow on a directed, uncapacitated graph in time

There is a randomized, augmenting paths-based algorithm that computes the maximum flow on a directed, uncapacitated graph in time

1.1
Our Techniques

Our starting point is Karger and Levine’s algorithm on
undirected
graphs
[
KL15
]
, which finds each augmenting path in a random sample of the residual graph. At a high level, Karger and Levine show that if the algorithm has sent a
(1-\epsilon)
-fraction of the maximum flow, then the residual graph is
O(1/\epsilon)
-balanced in the following sense: for any cut, viewed as a bipartition
(S,V\setminus S)
of the vertex set, the number of edges going from
S
to
V\setminus S
is within a multiplicative
O(1/\epsilon)
-factor of the number of edges going from
V\setminus S
to
S
. Then, they employ the random sampling method of Benczúr and Karger for undirected graphs
[
BK15
]
, accounting for the balance of
O(1/\epsilon)
by over-sampling at a rate of
O(1/\epsilon)
. Overall, they show that sampling
\tilde{O}(n/\epsilon)
1
1

1

\tilde{O}(\cdot)
ignores factors poly-logarithmic in
n
.
edges is sufficient with high probability
2
2

2

With high probability
means with probability
1-1/n^{c}
for arbitrarily large constant
c
.
, so the next augmenting path can be found in time
\tilde{O}(n/\epsilon)
. Since most of the augmenting paths are found when
\epsilon
is still constant, the amortized running time per augmenting path is
\tilde{O}(n)
, establishing their running time of
\tilde{O}(m+nF)
.

Unfortunately, the balance guarantee no longer holds if the input graph is directed. For example, suppose the input graph has an unbalanced cut
(S,V\setminus S)
with
s\in S
and
t\in V\setminus S
, with only a single edge going from
S
to
V\setminus S
but many edges in the opposite direction. Since Karger and Levine’s algorithm treats the residual graph as undirected for the purpose of sampling, the single edge from
S
to
V\setminus S
cannot be distinguished from the edges in the opposite direction, and is therefore unlikely to be sampled. If the edge is not sampled, then there cannot be a path from
s
to
t
, invalidating the approach.

Our main idea is to re-weight the residual graph so that any cut is balanced in terms of weight: the total weight of edges in one direction is within a constant factor of the total weight in the other direction. In the example above, the single edge from
S
to
V\setminus S
must have much higher weight than the edges in the opposite direction. We then treat the graph as undirected but
weighted
when sampling edges, so that the single edge from
S
to
V\setminus S
is likely to be sampled.

One technical difficulty is that our balancing weights have to be dynamically maintained upon changes to the residual graph. More precisely, when an augmenting path is found, the residual graph is updated by flipping each edge along the path. Since Karger and Levine treat the graph as undirected for the purpose of sampling, flipping an edge does not change their sampling probabilities. However, our balancing weights are sensitive to edge flips, and a dynamic algorithm is required to maintain them. Surprisingly, we show that the
minimum ratio cut
framework from the recent interior point method-based flow algorithm of van den Brand et al.
[
VDBCK+24
]
can be adapted to compute balancing weights. In particular, we completely black box their data structure for maintaining approximate minimum ratio cuts. We remark that this data structure is purely combinatorial and is comparable in complexity to the data structures in
[
CK24a
,
CK24b
]
.

1.2
Related Work

The concept of
balance
for directed graphs is introduced by Ene et al.
[
EMPS16
]
, who show that existing techniques for undirected flow-based problems can be translated to directed graphs that are approximately balanced. For graphs that are
perfectly
balanced, namely the Eulerian graphs, Cohen et al.
[
CKK+18
]
use directed graph sparsification to obtain fast algorithms for solving directed Laplacian systems. Sparsification for approximately balanced graphs is systematically studied by Cen et al.
[
CCPS21
]
, who obtain tight upper and lower bounds for various types of sparsification. All of these results are restricted to balanced graphs, and to the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to employ techniques from balanced graphs in the context of general directed graphs.

1.3
Preliminaries

Throughout the paper, all graphs are uncapacitated, either weighted or unweighted, and either directed or undirected. We refer to directed edges as
arcs
and undirected edges as
edges
. The input directed graph to maximum flow is assumed to be
G=(V,E)
, with vertices
s,t\in V
. We define a
flow
from
s
to
t
as a set of arc-disjoint paths from
s
to
t
in
G
, and the
value
of the flow is the number of paths in the set. The
residual graph

G_{f}
is obtained from
G
by flipping each arc along each path in
f
. The standard augmenting paths framework attempts to find a path from
s
to
t
in
G_{f}
, called an
augmenting path
. If a path is found, then each arc along the path is flipped, and the flow
f
is updated accordingly. Otherwise, if no path exists, then
f
is a maximum flow, and the algorithm terminates.

A
cut
is a bipartition
(S,V\setminus S)
of the vertex set into two non-empty subsets, represented as one of the sides
S\subseteq V
. The
boundary

\partial S
of the cut is the set of arcs/edges with exactly one endpoint in
S
. For a directed graph, the out-boundary
\partial^{+}S
is the set of arcs with only the tail in
S
, and the in-boundary
\partial^{-}S
is the set of arcs with only the head in
S
. For a weighted graph with weight function
w
, let
w(\partial S)
denote the total weight of arcs/edges in
\partial S
, and define
w(\partial^{+}S)
and
w(\partial^{-}S)
similarly. For an unweighted graph, let
|\partial S|
denote the number of arcs/edges in
\partial S
, and define
|\partial^{+}S|
and
|\partial^{-}S|
similarly.

1.4
Preprocessing the Graph

We first preprocess the graph as follows. First, add
m
many parallel arcs
(t,s)
, which does not affect the maximum flow from
s
to
t
. Then, compute the strongly connected components of the new graph. If
s
and
t
do not belong to the same strongly connected component, then the existence of arc
(t,s)
implies that there is no path from
s
to
t
, and the algorithm concludes that the maximum flow from
s
to
t
has value
0
. Otherwise, discard all strongly connected components except the one containing
s
and
t
, and solve the maximum flow problem on the remaining component. This operation is safe because there is no path from
s
to
t
that contains vertices outside the strongly connected component containing
s
and
t
.

Let
G
be the resulting graph after preprocessing. The lemma below ensures that any residual graph
G_{f}
remains strongly connected as long as the current flow is not maximum.

Lemma 3
.

Let
f
be a flow from
s
to
t
in
G
that does not use any arc
(t,s)
. If there is a path from
s
to
t
in
G_{f}
, then
G_{f}
is strongly connected.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\partial^{+}_{G_{f}}S\neq\emptyset
for any cut
S\subseteq V
. By assumption, all arcs
(t,s)
in
G
remain in
G_{f}
, and there is a path from
s
to
t
in
G_{f}
. If
t\in S
and
s\notin S
, then the arcs
(t,s)
are in
\partial^{+}_{G_{f}}S
, so
\partial^{+}_{G_{f}}S\neq\emptyset
. If
s\in S
and
t\notin S
, then a path from
s
to
t
in
G_{f}
must contain an edge in
\partial^{+}_{G_{f}}S
, so
\partial^{+}_{G_{f}}S\neq\emptyset
. Finally, if
s,t\in S
or
s,t\notin S
, then by the conservation of flow, we have
|\partial^{+}_{G_{f}}S|=|\partial^{+}_{G}S|
, and since
G
is strongly connected by preprocessing, we conclude that
|\partial^{+}_{G_{f}}S|=|\partial^{+}_{G}S|\neq 0
.
∎

Our algorithm always finds augmenting paths that avoid the arcs
(t,s)
, so while the current flow is not maximum, we may assume that
G_{f}
is strongly connected.

2
Balancing Weights

In this section, we construct our balancing weights on the residual graph and describe a data structure to maintain them over the course of the algorithm. The weights assigned to the arcs are determined by a potential function
\mathbf{y}:V\to\mathbb{R}
on the vertices. For a given potential function
\mathbf{y}
, the weight of arc
(u,v)
is

w(u,v)=\frac{1}{\max\{\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u),\,0\}+1}.

In particular, we have
w(u,v)=\frac{1}{\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)+1}
whenever
\mathbf{y}(v)\geq\mathbf{y}(u)
, and
w(u,v)=1
whenever
\mathbf{y}(v)\leq\mathbf{y}(u)
.

The algorithm tries to find a potential function
\mathbf{y}:V\to\mathbb{R}
such that all cuts are approximately balanced in the two directions: for any set
S\subseteq V
, we require that
\frac{1}{2}w(\partial^{-}S)\leq w(\partial^{+}S)\leq 2w(\partial^{-}S)
. Actually, we only need to enforce
\frac{1}{2}w(\partial^{-}S)\leq w(\partial^{+}S)
because the other inequality follows by replacing
S
with
V\setminus S
. If there is a violating set
S
with
w(\partial^{+}S)<\frac{1}{2}w(\partial^{-}S)
, then the algorithm increases the potential slightly for all vertices in
S
, i.e., sets
\mathbf{y}(v)\leftarrow\mathbf{y}(v)+\Delta
for all
v\in S
, where
\Delta>0
is small enough. By a potential function argument, this process must terminate eventually. In particular, a balancing function
\mathbf{y}
exists, which is already a non-trivial statement.

To ensure a fast algorithm, we need to find a balancing function
\mathbf{y}
quickly, and moreover, dynamically maintain the function upon finding augmenting paths. We interpret the balance of a directed graph as the
minimum ratio cut
of the underlying
undirected
graph, and then use the dynamic minimum ratio cut data structure from a recent interior-point method-based flow algorithm
[
VDBCK+24
]
.

We first define the minimum ratio cut of an undirected graph, whose input includes a
gradient function

\mathbf{g}:V\to\mathbb{R}
with
\mathbf{g}\perp\mathbbm{1}
, i.e.,
\sum_{v\in V}\mathbf{g}(v)=0
.

Definition 4
.

The
minimum ratio cut
of a positively weighted, undirected graph with gradient function
\mathbf{g}\perp\mathbbm{1}
is the minimum ratio
\mathbf{g}(S)/w(\partial S)
over all
S\subseteq V
with
w(\partial S)\neq 0
.

Observe that the requirement
\mathbf{g}\perp\mathbbm{1}
means that
\mathbf{g}(S)+\mathbf{g}(V\setminus S)=0
for any
S\subseteq V
, so either
S
or
V\setminus S
has non-positive ratio. Intuitively, the goal is to maximize
|\mathbf{g}(S)|
while minimizing
w(\partial S)
, and then take either
S
or
V\setminus S
, whichever has non-positive ratio.

We construct our minimum ratio cut instance as follows. Consider the
undirected
graph with edge weights
w(u,v)
together with the gradient function
\mathbf{g}:V\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

\mathbf{g}=\sum_{(u,v)}w(u,v)\cdot(\mathbbm{1}_{u}-\mathbbm{1}_{v}),

i.e., for each arc
(u,v)
in the residual graph, add
w(u,v)
to the tail
u
and subtract
w(u,v)
from the head
v
. In other words, we “undirect” the residual graph and encode the arc directions through a gradient function.

Lemma 5
.

For any set
S\subseteq V
, we have
\mathbf{g}(S)=w(\partial^{+}S)-w(\partial^{-}S)
.

Proof.

Any arc
(u,v)\in\partial^{+}S
contributes
w(u,v)
to
\mathbf{g}(S)
since we add
w(u,v)
to the tail
u\in S
, while the subtraction at the head
v\notin S
is not counted in
\mathbf{g}(S)
. Similarly, any arc
(u,v)\in\partial^{-}S
contributes
-w(u,v)
to
\mathbf{g}(S)
. All other arcs contribute
0
since either
u,v\notin S
in which case there is no contribution, or
u,v\in S
in which case the contributions of
+w(u,v)
and
-w(u,v)
cancel out.
∎

Lemma 6
.

If the minimum ratio cut has ratio
\mathbf{g}(S)/w(\partial S)\geq-1/3
, then
w(\partial^{+}S)\geq\frac{1}{2}w(\partial^{-}S)
for all
S\subseteq V
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that a cut
S\subseteq V
with
w(\partial^{+}S)<\frac{1}{2}w(\partial^{-}S)
implies that
\mathbf{g}(S)/w(\partial S)<-1/3
. By the previous lemma, we have
\mathbf{g}(S)=w(\partial^{+}S)-w(\partial^{-}S)
. Together with
w(\partial S)=w(\partial^{+}S)+w(\partial^{-}S)
, we obtain

\displaystyle 3\cdot\mathbf{g}(S)+w(\partial S)

\displaystyle=3\cdot(w(\partial^{+}S)-w(\partial^{-}S))+(w(\partial^{+}S)+w(\partial^{-}S))

\displaystyle=4\cdot w(\partial^{+}S)-2\cdot w(\partial^{-}S)

\displaystyle<2\cdot w(\partial^{-}S)-2\cdot w(\partial^{-}S)

\displaystyle=0,

or equivalently
\mathbf{g}(S)/w(\partial S)<-1/3
, as desired.
∎

3.1
Stability

Since edge weights only get updated when they are returned in
E^{\prime}
, the data structures only maintain approximations of the true weights. In more detail, for each arc
(u,v)
, define the real weight
w(u,v)=\frac{1}{\max\{\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u),0\}+1}
based on the current potential function
\mathbf{y}
, and define the approximate weight
\tilde{w}(u,v)=\mathbf{u}(u,v)
as the current weight maintained by the data structure, which is the real weight at the last time the corresponding edge
(u,v)
was inserted or returned in
E^{\prime}
.

Our algorithm requires that the approximate weights
\tilde{w}(u,v)
indeed approximate the real weights. We begin with a stability guarantee on the weight function that is used throughout the analysis.

Lemma 11

(Stability)
.

Consider two values
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, and define the weights
w_{i}=\frac{1}{\max\{x_{i},0\}+1}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. For any parameter
0\leq\delta<1
, if
|x_{1}-x_{2}|\leq\delta/w_{1}
, then
\frac{1}{1+\delta}w_{1}\leq w_{2}\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta}w_{1}
.

Proof.

We bound

\displaystyle w_{2}=\frac{1}{\max\{x_{2},0\}+1}\geq\frac{1}{\max\{x_{1}+\delta/w_{1},0\}+1}\geq\frac{1}{\max\{x_{1},0\}+1+\delta/w_{1}}=\frac{1}{1/w_{1}+\delta/w_{1}}=\frac{w_{1}}{1+\delta}

and

\displaystyle w_{2}=\frac{1}{\max\{x_{2},0\}+1}\leq\frac{1}{\max\{x_{1}-\delta/w_{1},0\}+1}\leq\frac{1}{\max\{x_{1},0\}+1-\delta/w_{1}}=\frac{1}{1/w_{1}-\delta/w_{1}}=\frac{w_{1}}{1-\delta}

as desired.
∎

Lemma 12

(Approximation of weights)
.

For any arc
(u,v)
, we have
\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}\tilde{w}(u,v)\leq w(u,v)\leq\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\tilde{w}(u,v)
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
be the potential function when the edge
(u,v)
was last inserted or returned in
E^{\prime}
. In particular,
\tilde{w}(u,v)=\frac{1}{\max\{\tilde{\mathbf{y}}(v)-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}(u),0\}+1}
.
By the guarantee of the data structure, we have

\tilde{w}(u,v)\cdot|(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}(u)-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}(v))-(\mathbf{y}(u)-\mathbf{y}(v))|<\epsilon\iff|(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}(u)-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}(v))-(\mathbf{y}(u)-\mathbf{y}(v))|<\epsilon/\tilde{w}(u,v).

Applying
Lemma

11
with
x_{1}=\tilde{\mathbf{y}}(u)-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}(v)
and
x_{2}=\mathbf{y}(u)-\mathbf{y}(v)
and
\delta=\epsilon
finishes the proof.
∎

, at which point

, then the (approximate) edge weights maintained by the data structure are balanced. In particular,

with
\beta=9
returns a subgraph containing an augmenting path from

to

with high probability.

.

If
g/u>-1/(3\alpha)
, then
\tilde{w}(\partial^{+}S)\geq\frac{1}{9}\tilde{w}(\partial S)
for all

.

Proof.

By

, we have
w(\partial^{+}S)\geq\frac{1}{2}w(\partial^{-}S)
for all

. Together with

, we have

, so

3.2
Energy Bounds

To bound the running time, we need to track the number of calls to
ToggleCut
. Let
M=n^{O(1)}
be large enough, and define the
energy
of arc
(u,v)
as

\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\int_{\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)}^{M}\frac{1}{\max\{x,0\}+1}\,dx.

Observe that

\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\begin{cases}\ln(M+1)-\ln(\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)+1)&\text{if }\mathbf{y}(v)\geq\mathbf{y}(u),\\
\ln(M+1)+\mathbf{y}(u)-\mathbf{y}(v)&\text{if }\mathbf{y}(v)\leq\mathbf{y}(u).\end{cases}

The following lemma shows that each call to
ToggleCut
decreases the total energy by an additive
1/n^{o(1)}
.

Lemma 14

(Energy decrease)
.

On each call to
ToggleCut
, the total energy decreases by at least
\Omega(1/\alpha^{3})
.

Proof.

On each call to
\textsc{ToggleCut}(\eta)
, the implicit cut
C
satisfies
g/u\leq-1/(3\alpha)
, where
\mathbf{g}(C)=g<0
and
\mathbf{u}(\partial C)\leq u
. We also have
\mathbf{u}(\partial C)\geq u/\alpha
since otherwise, if
\mathbf{u}(\partial C)<u/\alpha
, then the ratio
\mathbf{g}(C)/\mathbf{u}(\partial C)
would be less than
\mathbf{g}(C)/(u/\alpha)=\alpha\cdot g/u
, contradicting the fact that
g/u
is at most
1/\alpha
times the min-ratio cut.

We now analyze the contribution of
\textsc{ToggleCut}(\eta)
to the total energy. Let
w
and
\mathbf{y}
be the weight and potential function immediately before
\textsc{ToggleCut}(\eta)
. For each arc
(u,v)\in\partial^{+}C
, the value of
\mathbf{y}(u)
is increased by
\eta
, so the energy
\mathcal{E}(u,v)
increases by

\int_{\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)-\eta}^{\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)}\frac{1}{\max\{x,0\}+1}\,dx.

Similarly, for each arc
(u,v)\in\partial^{-}C
, the value of
\mathbf{y}(v)
is increased by
\eta
, so the energy
\mathcal{E}(u,v)
decreases by

\int_{\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)}^{\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)+\eta}\frac{1}{\max\{x,0\}+1}\,dx.

By the approximation of weights (
Lemma

12
), we have
w(u,v)\leq 2\tilde{w}(u,v)
, so for any arc
(u,v)\in\partial C
, we have
u\geq\mathbf{u}(\partial C)\geq\tilde{w}(u,v)\geq\frac{1}{2}w(u,v)
.
Since
\eta=\frac{1}{16\alpha u}\leq\frac{1}{8\alpha\cdot w(u,v)}
, by the stability guarantee (
Lemma

11
) with
\delta=\frac{1}{8\alpha}
, we have

\frac{1}{1+1/(8\alpha)}w(u,v)\leq\frac{1}{\max\{x,0\}+1}\leq\frac{1}{1-1/(8\alpha)}w(u,v)\text{ for all }x\in[\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)-\eta,\,\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)+\eta].

In particular, the energy increase of each arc
(u,v)\in\partial^{+}C
is at most
\eta\cdot\frac{1}{1-1/(8\alpha)}\cdot w(u,v)
, and the energy decrease from each arc
(u,v)\in\partial^{-}C
is at least
\eta\cdot\frac{1}{1+1/(8\alpha)}\cdot w(u,v)
. Summed over all such arcs, the net change in energy is at most

\eta\cdot\frac{1}{1-1/(8\alpha)}\cdot w(\partial^{+}C)-\eta\cdot\frac{1}{1+1/(8\alpha)}\cdot w(\partial^{-}C).

Recall that
\mathbf{g}(C)/\mathbf{u}(\partial C)\leq g/u\leq-1/(3\alpha)
and
\mathbf{g}(C)=\tilde{w}(\partial^{+}C)-\tilde{w}(\partial^{-}C)
and
\mathbf{u}(\partial C)=\tilde{w}(\partial^{+}C)+\tilde{w}(\partial^{-}C)
. We have

\displaystyle 3\alpha\cdot\mathbf{g}(C)+\mathbf{u}(\partial C)\leq 0

\displaystyle\iff(3\alpha+1)\cdot\tilde{w}(\partial^{+}C)-(3\alpha-1)\cdot\tilde{w}(\partial^{-}C)\leq 0

\displaystyle\iff\tilde{w}(\partial^{+}C)\leq\frac{3\alpha-1}{3\alpha+1}\cdot\tilde{w}(\partial^{-}C).

Together with the approximation of weights (
Lemma

12
), we have

w(\partial^{+}C)\leq\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\cdot\tilde{w}(\partial^{+}C)\leq\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\cdot\frac{3\alpha-1}{3\alpha+1}\cdot\tilde{w}(\partial^{-}C)\leq\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\cdot\frac{3\alpha-1}{3\alpha+1}\cdot(1+\epsilon)\cdot w(\partial^{-}C).

Since
\epsilon=1/(8\alpha)
, it follows that the net change in energy is at most

\displaystyle\eta\cdot\frac{1}{1-1/(8\alpha)}\cdot w(\partial^{+}C)-\eta\cdot\frac{1}{1+1/(8\alpha)}\cdot w(\partial^{-}C)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\eta\cdot\frac{1}{1-1/(8\alpha)}\cdot\frac{1+1/(8\alpha)}{1-1/(8\alpha)}\cdot\frac{3\alpha-1}{3\alpha+1}\cdot w(\partial^{-}C)-\eta\cdot\frac{1}{1+1/(8\alpha)}\cdot w(\partial^{-}C)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\eta\cdot-\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\cdot w(\partial^{-}C)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\eta\cdot-\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\cdot\tilde{w}(\partial^{-}C).

Recall that
\mathbf{u}(\partial C)\geq u/\alpha
, which means that
\eta=\frac{1}{16\alpha u}\geq\frac{1}{16\alpha^{2}\mathbf{u}(\partial C)}
. Also, since
\mathbf{g}(C)<0
, we have
\tilde{w}(\partial^{-}C)\geq\frac{1}{2}\tilde{w}(\partial C)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}(\partial C)
. We conclude that the net change in energy is at most

\frac{1}{16\alpha^{2}\mathbf{u}(\partial C)}\cdot-\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}(\partial C)=-\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{3}}\right),

as desired.
∎

Next, suppose that the algorithm finds an augmenting path from
s
to
t
. The following lemma bounds the total increase in energy from flipping the arcs along the augmenting path.

Lemma 15

(Energy increase)
.

Assume that all vertex potentials are within an additive
M
of each other, i.e.,
|\mathbf{y}(u)-\mathbf{y}(v)|\leq M
for all
u,v\in V
. Suppose the algorithm flips all arcs along a (simple) path from
s
to
t
. The total energy increases by at most
\mathbf{y}(t)-\mathbf{y}(s)+(n-1)\ln(M+1)
.

Proof.

Recall that

\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\begin{cases}\ln(M+1)-\ln(\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)+1)&\text{if }\mathbf{y}(v)\geq\mathbf{y}(u),\\
\ln(M+1)+\mathbf{y}(u)-\mathbf{y}(v)&\text{if }\mathbf{y}(v)\leq\mathbf{y}(u).\end{cases}

Consider the energy difference from flipping an individual arc
(u,v)
. If
\mathbf{y}(v)\geq\mathbf{y}(u)
, then the original energy is
\ln(M+1)-\ln(\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)+1)
and the new energy is
\ln(M+1)+\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)
, so the net change in energy is

\displaystyle\big(\ln(M+1)+\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)\big)-\big(\ln(M+1)-\ln(\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)+1)\big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)+\ln(\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)+1)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)+\ln(M+1),

where we use the assumption that
\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)\leq M
.
Similarly, if
\mathbf{y}(v)\leq\mathbf{y}(u)
, then the original energy is
\ln(M+1)+\mathbf{y}(u)-\mathbf{y}(v)
and the new energy is
\ln(M+1)-\ln(\mathbf{y}(u)-\mathbf{y}(v)+1)
, so the net change in energy is

\displaystyle\big(\ln(M+1)-\ln(\mathbf{y}(u)-\mathbf{y}(v)+1)\big)-\big(\ln(M+1)+\mathbf{y}(u)-\mathbf{y}(v)\big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)-\ln(\mathbf{y}(u)-\mathbf{y}(v)+1)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u).

In both cases, the net change in energy is at most
\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)+\ln(M+1)
. Summed over all arcs
(u,v)
on the path, the
\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)
terms telescope to
\mathbf{y}(t)-\mathbf{y}(s)
, and the
\ln(M+1)
terms sum to at most
(n-1)\ln(M+1)
.
∎

3.3
Running Time

Outside of the calls to
ToggleCut
, the algorithm spends
n^{1+o(1)}
time on each augmenting path: the algorithm computes a sparsifier, finds an augmenting path, flips the path, and updates the data structures. To bound the number of calls to
ToggleCut
, it suffices to bound the total energy increase over the course of the algorithm, since the total energy starts at
m\ln(M+1)
and decreases by
\Omega(\alpha^{3})=1/n^{o(1)}
on each call to
ToggleCut
(
Lemma

14
).

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the current total energy. By
Lemma

15
, the energy increase is at most
\mathbf{y}(t)-\mathbf{y}(s)+(n-1)\ln(M+1)
after flipping the next augmenting path. If
\mathbf{y}(t)-\mathbf{y}(s)\geq 0
, then we bound
\mathbf{y}(t)-\mathbf{y}(s)
as follows. Each of the arcs
(t,s)
added during preprocessing has energy
\ln(M+1)+\mathbf{y}(t)-\mathbf{y}(s)\geq\mathbf{y}(t)-\mathbf{y}(s)
. There are
\Omega(m)
such arcs, so their total energy
\Omega(m)\cdot(\mathbf{y}(t)-\mathbf{y}(s))
is at most
\mathcal{E}
. We conclude that
\mathbf{y}(t)-\mathbf{y}(s)\leq O(\mathcal{E}/m)
, and the total energy after flipping the augmenting path is at most
(1+O(1/m))\cdot\mathcal{E}+(n-1)\ln(M+1)
. The total number of augmenting paths is
F\leq m
, so the total energy increase is at most
(1+O(1/m))^{F}\cdot(m\ln(M+1)+F(n-1)\ln(M+1))\leq O((m+nF)\ln(M+1))
.

At this point, we can also justify the assumption
\max_{u,v}|\mathbf{y}(u)-\mathbf{y}(v)|\leq M
from
Lemma

15
for
M=n^{O(1)}
as long as the residual graph is strongly connected. For the sake of argument, re-define
M
as the largest value of
\max_{u,v}|\mathbf{y}(u)-\mathbf{y}(v)|
over the course of the algorithm, and suppose for contradiction that
M
is too large. Consider the moment of the algorithm at which
\mathbf{y}(u)-\mathbf{y}(v)=M
for some
u,v\in V
. Consider a path from
u
to
v
of at most
n-1
arcs in the current residual graph. There is an arc
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
on the path with
\mathbf{y}(u^{\prime})-\mathbf{y}(v^{\prime})\geq M/(n-1)
, which has energy
\ln(M+1)+\mathbf{y}(u^{\prime})-\mathbf{y}(v^{\prime})\geq M/(n-1)
. In particular, the total energy is at least
M/(n-1)
. However, by the previous energy bound, the total energy at this point is at most
O((m+nF)\ln(M+1))
. For large enough
M=n^{O(1)}
, we obtain a contradiction.

We conclude that the algorithm has total running time
(m+nF)\cdot n^{o(1)}
while the residual graph is strongly connected. When the flow is maximum, the residual graph
G_{f}
is no longer strongly connected, and the algorithm may have undefined behavior. However, we can impose a running time limit of
(m+n|f|)\cdot n^{o(1)}
where
|f|
is the current flow value, and terminate the algorithm if the limit is exceeded. This completes the proof of our main result (
Theorem

1
).

To obtain
Corollary

2
, we use the fact that
\sqrt{n}
rounds of Dinic’s blocking flow algorithm
[
Din70
]
reduces the maximum flow of the residual graph to at most
m/\sqrt{n}

[
Kar73
,
ET75
]
. Computing the blocking flows takes time
O(m\sqrt{n})
, and applying
Theorem

1
with
F\leq m/\sqrt{n}
on the residual graph takes time
mn^{1/2+o(1)}
.

4.1
Reduction to Unweighted Graphs

The dynamic algorithm first reduces to the case of unweighted graphs by bucketing the arcs and running an unweighted dynamic sparsifier with quality
n^{o(1)}
on each bucket. More precisely, for each integer
i
, let
H_{i}\subseteq H
be the subgraph of all arcs with weight in the range
[2^{i},2^{i+1})
. Since
\mathbf{u}(e)\in[1/U,U]
, there are only
O(\log U)
non-empty subgraphs
H_{i}
. For each
H_{i}
, treated as a dynamic
unweighted
graph, initialize a dynamic sparsifier data structure where all arcs have weight
1
. Finally, on each query to
\textsc{Sparsifier}(\beta)
, the algorithm calls
\textsc{Sparsifier}(2\beta)
on the data structure for each
H_{i}
, obtaining a subgraph
\widetilde{H}_{i}\subseteq H_{i}
. The algorithm outputs the union of the subgraphs
\widetilde{H}_{i}
as the final sparsifier
\widetilde{H}
, which has
q\cdot\beta n\cdot O(\log U)
arcs.

To prove correctness of this reduction, consider a cut
S\subseteq V
with
\mathbf{u}(\partial_{H}^{+}S)\geq\frac{1}{\beta}\mathbf{u}(\partial_{H}S)
, rewritten as
\beta\cdot\mathbf{u}(\partial^{+}_{H}S)-\mathbf{u}(\partial_{H}S)\geq 0
. By our bucketing scheme, we can bound
\mathbf{u}(\partial^{+}_{H}S)
and
\mathbf{u}(\partial_{H}S)
by

\mathbf{u}(\partial^{+}_{H}S)\leq\sum_{i}2^{i+1}\cdot|\partial^{+}_{H_{i}}S|\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{u}(\partial_{H}S)\geq\sum_{i}2^{i}\cdot|\partial_{H_{i}}S|,

so

\sum_{i}2^{i}\cdot(2\beta\cdot|\partial^{+}_{H_{i}}S|-|\partial_{H_{i}}S|)=\beta\cdot\sum_{i}2^{i+1}\cdot|\partial^{+}_{H_{i}}S|-\sum_{i}2^{i}\cdot|\partial_{H_{i}}S|\geq\beta\cdot\mathbf{u}(\partial^{+}_{H}S)-\mathbf{u}(\partial_{H}S)\geq 0.

In particular, there exists an
i
with
2\beta\cdot|\partial^{+}_{H_{i}}S|-|\partial_{H_{i}}S|\geq 0
, or equivalently
|\partial^{+}_{H_{i}}S|\geq\frac{1}{2\beta}|\partial_{H_{i}}S|
. Assuming that the guarantee of
\textsc{Sparsifier}(2\beta)
on
H_{i}
holds, we conclude that
\partial^{+}_{\widetilde{H}_{i}}S\neq\emptyset
as well as
\partial^{+}_{\widetilde{H}}S\neq\emptyset
, completing the reduction.

4.2
Dynamic Unweighted Sparsifier

For the dynamic unweighted sparsifier, the algorithm maintains a sequence of explicit
expander decompositions
of the underlying undirected graph. We begin with some expander decomposition preliminaries on unweighted, undirected graphs. For a subset of vertices
S\subseteq V
, let
\textbf{{vol}}(S)=\sum_{v\in S}\deg(v)
denote the sum of vertex degrees in
S
. For a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\}
of
V
into clusters, let
\partial\mathcal{P}=\cup_{i=1}^{k}\partial V_{i}
denote the edges whose endpoints belong to different clusters.

Definition 16

(
\phi
-expander)
.

An undirected graph is a
\phi
-expander
if
|\partial S|\geq\phi\cdot\min\{\textbf{{vol}}(S)
,
\textbf{{vol}}(V\setminus S)\}
for all
S\subseteq V
.

Definition 17

(
\phi
-expander decomposition with slack
s
)
.

A
\phi
-expander decomposition of an undirected graph
G
with slack
s\geq 1
is a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\}
of
V
into clusters such that

1.

|\partial\mathcal{P}|\leq\tilde{O}(\phi m)
, and

2.

Each induced subgraph
G[V_{i}]
is a
(\phi/s)
-expander.

We are interested in a dynamic expander decomposition algorithm with low
recourse
with respect to the set
\partial\mathcal{P}
. We say that the algorithm has amortized recourse
\rho
if for all
k\geq 1
, the set
\partial\mathcal{P}
changes by at most
\rho\cdot k
edges after
k
edge updates to the graph.

Theorem 18

(Theorem 7.2 of
[
GRST21
]
)
.

Let
0<\phi<1
be a parameter. There is a randomized algorithm that, given an unweighted graph
G
undergoing edge insertions and deletions, maintains an explicit
\phi
-expander decomposition with slack
s=2^{-O(\log^{1/2}n)}
together with the explicit set of edges
\partial\mathcal{P}
. The algorithm has update time
2^{O(\sqrt{\log n})}/\phi^{2}
and amortized recourse
2^{O(\sqrt{\log n})}
with respect to
\partial\mathcal{P}
. The algorithm succeeds with high probability against an adaptive adversary.

We now describe the dynamic sparsification algorithm. Given the graph
H
, the algorithm first adds between
m/n
and
2m/n
self-loops to each vertex in
H
. This initial pre-processing has amortized recourse
O(1)
since it takes
\Omega(m)
edge updates before the value of
m/n
changes by a constant factor, at which point
O(m)
time is spent updating the self-loops. After the addition of self-loops, the graph still has
O(m)
edges. The algorithm maintains a dynamic expander decomposition (
Theorem

18
) with parameter
\phi=2^{-\log^{3/4}n}
on the graph with self-loops. On the subgraph of
H
consisting of the edges
\partial\mathcal{P}
, the algorithm
recursively
performs the same procedure, starting with adding
|\partial\mathcal{P}|/n
self-loops to each vertex. Since
|\partial\mathcal{P}|
is a factor
2^{\Omega(\log^{3/4}n)}
smaller, the recursion depth is
t=O(\log^{1/4}n)
. Each level of recursion has amortized recourse
2^{O(\sqrt{\log n})}
, so the overall recourse (and update time) is
2^{O(\log^{3/4}n)}
.

Given the explicitly maintained expander decompositions, we now implement the procedure
\textsc{Sparsifier}(\beta)
. On recursion level
i\geq 0
, let
G_{i}\subseteq H
be the initial subgraph at level
i\geq 0
of recursion (before the addition of self-loops), and let
m_{i}
be the number of edges in
G_{i}
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{i}
be the expander decomposition at this level, and let
H_{i}=G_{i}\setminus\partial\mathcal{P}_{i}
be the subgraph of edges with both endpoints in the same cluster. By construction, the input graph
H
is the disjoint union of the subgraphs
H_{i}
. The algorithm independently samples each edge in
H_{i}
with probability
\frac{q\beta}{2t}\cdot\frac{n}{m_{i}}
, and outputs the sparsifier
\widetilde{H}
as the union of all sampled edges (over all
i
).

For the running time, observe that for each
i
, the random sampling in
H_{i}
can be done in time proportional to the number of samples: first determine the number of samples
s
, which can be sampled from a binomial distribution, and then randomly sample
s
edges from the edges in
H_{i}
. The expected number of samples in
H_{i}
is at most
\frac{q\beta}{2t}\cdot n
, so the sparsifier
\widetilde{H}
has expected size at most
q\beta n/2
. A simple concentration bound shows that the size is at most
q\beta n
with high probability, so the running time is also
O(q\beta n)
with high probability.

It remains to establish the sparsifier guarantee. Consider a cut
S\subseteq V
with
|\partial_{H}^{+}S|\geq\frac{1}{\beta}|\partial_{H}S|
, rewritten as
\beta\cdot|\partial^{+}_{H}S|-|\partial_{H}S|\geq 0
. Since
H
is the disjoint union of subgraphs
H_{i}
, we can write
|\partial^{+}_{H}S|=\sum_{i}|\partial^{+}_{H_{i}}S|
and similarly for
\partial_{H}S
. In particular,

\sum_{i}(\beta\cdot|\partial^{+}_{H_{i}}S|-|\partial_{H_{i}}S|)=\beta\cdot\sum_{i}|\partial^{+}_{H_{i}}S|-\sum_{i}|\partial_{H_{i}}S|=\beta\cdot|\partial^{+}_{H}S|-|\partial_{H}S|\geq 0,

so for some
i
, we have
\beta\cdot|\partial^{+}_{H_{i}}S|-|\partial_{H_{i}}S|\geq 0
. We can further write
H_{i}
as the disjoint union of
\phi
-expanders from the decomposition
\mathcal{P}_{i}
, and by a similar argument, there is a
\phi
-expander
X\subseteq H_{i}
supported on
U\subseteq V
with
\beta\cdot|\partial^{+}_{X}(S\cap U)|-|\partial_{X}(S\cap U)|\geq 0
.

Our goal is to show that for a fixed
\phi
-expander
X\subseteq H_{i}
supported on
U\subseteq V
, with high probability, any cut
R\subseteq U
with
\beta\cdot|\partial^{+}_{X}R|-|\partial_{X}R|\geq 0
has at least one sampled out-arc in
X
. Taking a union bound over all expanders
X
(over all levels of recursion) then handles all cuts
S\subseteq V
in the sparsifier guarantee, completing the proof.

Consider a
\phi
-expander
X\subseteq H_{i}
supported on
U\subseteq V
. We establish a “cut counting” style union bound by grouping the cuts
R\subseteq U
with
\beta\cdot|\partial^{+}_{X}R|-|\partial_{X}R|\geq 0
by the value
\min\{|R|,|U\setminus R|\}
. For each integer
k\geq 1
, there are at most
2\cdot|U|^{k}
many cuts
R\subseteq U
with
\min\{|R|,|U\setminus R|\}=k
. By the
\phi
-expander guarantee, each such cut satisfies
|\partial_{X}R|\geq\phi\cdot\min\{\textbf{{vol}}_{X}(R),\textbf{{vol}}_{X}(U\setminus R)\}
. Since at least
m_{i}/n
self-loops were added to each vertex before the expander decomposition, we have
\min\{\textbf{{vol}}_{X}(R),\textbf{{vol}}_{X}(U\setminus R)\}\geq m_{i}/n\cdot\min\{|R|,|U\setminus R|\}=km_{i}/n
. In particular, if
\beta\cdot|\partial^{+}_{X}R|-|\partial_{X}R|\geq 0
, then
|\partial^{+}_{X}R|\geq\frac{1}{\beta}|\partial_{X}R|\geq\frac{k\phi}{\beta}\cdot\frac{m_{i}}{n}
. Each edge in
X
is sampled with probability
\frac{q\beta}{2t}\cdot\frac{n}{m_{i}}
, so the probability of sampling no edge in
\partial^{+}_{X}R
is

\left(1-\frac{q\beta}{2t}\cdot\frac{n}{m_{i}}\right)^{|\partial^{+}_{X}R|}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{q\beta}{2t}\cdot\frac{n}{m_{i}}\cdot|\partial^{+}_{X}R|\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{q\beta}{2t}\cdot\frac{n}{m_{i}}\cdot\frac{k\phi}{\beta}\cdot\frac{m_{i}}{n}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{kq\phi}{2t}\right).

Setting
q=2ct/\phi\cdot\ln n
for an arbitrary constant
c>0
, this probability is at most
\exp(-ck\ln n)=n^{-ck}
. Taking a union bound over all cuts
R\subseteq U
with
\min\{|R|,|U\setminus R|\}=k
, the probability of failure is at most
2\cdot|U|^{k}\cdot n^{-ck}\leq 2n^{-(c-1)k}
for this value of
k
. Finally, taking a union bound over all
k\geq 1
, the overall probability of failure is at most
\sum_{k\geq 1}2n^{-(c-1)k}\leq O(n^{-(c-1)})
, as desired.

5
Conclusion

We conclude with some future directions. The first question is whether our weight function
w(u,v)=\frac{1}{\max\{\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u),\,0\}+1}
is the “right” function. A more natural function is
\frac{1}{\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)+1}
, which induces a “barrier” that enforces
\mathbf{y}(v)\geq\mathbf{y}(u)-1
in the same way the push-relabel algorithm requires
d(v)\geq d(u)-1
on arcs
(u,v)
in the residual graph
[
GT88
]
. However, our algorithm may flip an “upward” arc
(v,u)
with
\mathbf{y}(v)\ll\mathbf{y}(u)-1
, causing the new weight
\frac{1}{\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)+1}
to be undefined. On the other hand, the push-relabel algorithm never sends flow along upward arcs, so perhaps the algorithm should only flip downward arcs. We leave as an open question whether our algorithm can be adapted to behave like a “continuous” push-relabel algorithm.

Another question is whether the balancing weights technique can be applied to other directed graph problems. Balanced directed graphs behave similarly to undirected graphs, so the method can be viewed as a way to apply the powerful techniques for undirected graphs to the more difficult setting of directed graphs. Indeed, the recent interior point method-based flow algorithms
[
CKL+25
,
VDBCK+24
]
all adopt this strategy to great success: each iteration reduces to a problem on an undirected graph, which is solved quickly by dynamic data structures.

Finally, one limit to the current balancing weights approach is the requirement that the graph is strongly connected. We leave as an open question whether the technique can be extended to general directed graphs. One difficulty in handling cuts with no arcs in one direction is that the arcs in the other direction may have their weights approaching
0
, which means that
\mathbf{y}(v)-\mathbf{y}(u)
becomes arbitrarily large. One possible approach is to employ a “softer” weight function to control the potential differences as the weights tend to
0
.
