A novel Bacillus subtilis strain alleviates necrotic enteritis in chickens by reducing NetB toxin from Clostridium perfringens
Authors: Shabnoor Binte Dayem, Hai Yu, Mohammadali Alizadeh, Jeremiah Cheung, Ali Tolooe, Dion Lepp, Nitish Boodhoo, Shayan Sharif, Joshua Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Probiotic strategies that attenuate virulence-associated toxins are increasingly recognized as effective approaches to mitigate disease progression. We screened for wildtype Bacillus strains from healthy chickens with the aim of identifying specific isolates capable of reducing Clostridium perfringens toxin production and necrotic enteritis (NE) lesions in chickens and consequently selected a strain (Bacillus subtilis CG4). The cell-free culture supernatant (CFS) of CG4 did not inhibit the growth of a C. perfringens Type G strain. However, the bacterium significantly reduced the level of NetB toxin (a key toxin for NE development) produced by C. perfringens. B. subtilis CG4 was therefore further characterized in vitro and evaluated in vivo for its ability to reduce NE. In the in vitro studies, CG4 produced nearly 3.0 × 10⁸ spores/mL and exhibited resistance to tetracycline and the penicillin only among tested antibiotics. In addition, the CG4 CFS was sensitive to heat and proteinase K treatments, suggesting the proteinaceous nature of a bioactive component. The proteolytic activity of CG4 CFS possessed a relatively broad substrate spectrum, which effectively digested bovine serum albumin (BSA) and casein. In a C. perfringens infection chicken trial that contained three groups of birds (no treatment, pathogen infection only, and pathogen infection with the treatment of CG4) and used a completely random design, the supplementation of B. subtilis CG4 in feed resulted in a significant reduction in NE lesions (p ≤ 0.05) compared to the birds infected with the pathogen only. The average NE sore of birds from the CG4-treated and C. perfringens-infected only groups were 2.47 and 3.75, respectively, while the untreated group had 0 NE lesions. Additionally, CG4-treated chickens exhibited a noticeable increase in body weight (p ≤ 0.05) at the end of trial, presumably through proteolytic activity in CG4.

Introduction

Necrotic enteritis (NE) is an important enteric disease resulting in severe intestinal damage, impairment of nutritional absorption, and high mortality in chickens, which is estimated to cost the poultry industry $6 billion yearly world-wide (Wade et al., 2015). NE is mainly caused by Clostridium perfringens Type G strains, a gram-positive, spore-forming, anaerobic bacterium (Rood et al., 2018). The pathogen destroys the intestinal lining to acquire nutrients from the host animal by secreting a key virulence toxin called necrotic enteritis B-like toxin (NetB; Keyburn et al., 2008). NetB is a pore-forming toxin and belongs to the β-barrel pore-forming toxin family, which primarily targets poultry cells. The toxin induces intestinal necrosis by triggering calcium influx and potassium efflux from the cell by destroying the phospholipid membrane bilayer.

The poultry industry used to rely on the use of dietary antibiotics to control NE disease. In the post-antibiotic era, there is an urgent need to replace antibiotics to control the disease in poultry production. This has led to many studies to investigate the potential of various non-antibiotic control strategies (Mahmood et al., 2014; Mora et al., 2020). Among them, probiotic bacteria, specifically Bacillus subtilis, have shown promising prospects in restraining enteric infections in flocks. Several meta-studies have shown that Bacillus strains, including B. subtilis, demonstrated antimicrobial capability against C. perfringens (Menconi et al., 2020; Ghimire et al., 2024). One of the meta-analyses revealed an approximate 34% decrease in NE lesions and an approximate 7.5% decrease in mortality by in-feed B. subtilis supplementation in broiler chickens (Menconi et al., 2020). The ability of B. subtilis to inhibit C. perfringens growth or its toxin production was proposed to be a potential mechanism (Ghimire et al., 2024). In addition, Bacillus bacteria are highly attractive as feed additives due to their resilience to harsh environmental conditions, including heat, acidic pH, and bile salts, enabling effective survival and activity in the avian gastrointestinal tract (Ghimire et al., 2024). They also possess strong protein secretion capacity, which supports their potential use in functional applications (Harwood and Cranenburgh, 2008).

Despite these promising properties, most current probiotic-based interventions primarily rely on bactericidal or growth-inhibitory activities against C. perfringens. This approach may raise concerns regarding long-term sustainability and the potential development of antimicrobial resistance. In contrast, anti-virulence strategies, which target key pathogenic mechanisms rather than bacterial viability, represent a more targeted and evolutionarily stable alternative for disease control. NetB toxin is a critical virulence factor in NE pathogenesis (Keyburn et al., 2008). While its importance in disease development is well established (Prescott et al., 2016a), the strategy to directly neutralize NetB activity as a primary disease control approach remains largely unexplored. Targeting toxin activity rather than bacterial growth may therefore offer a means of reducing intestinal damage while minimizing selective pressure for resistance development. Accordingly, this study was focused on the identification and characterization of B. subtilis strains capable of neutralizing NetB toxin without affecting bacterial growth, with the aim of evaluating their potential to mitigate intestinal damage and reduce NE severity in broiler chickens challenged with C. perfringens.

Materials and methods

Bacteria and growth

C. perfringens CP1 and CP4 are both Type G strains originally isolated from a NE case in Ontario (Thompson et al., 2006). CP4 was used for the experiment to determine the cellular location of NetB toxin, and CP1 was used for all the in vitro and in vivo studies. A mutant generated from CP1, which has spontaneously lost the NELoc-1-encoding plasmid, thus lacking NetB toxin gene (netB-; Zhou et al., 2017), was used as a negative control in immunoblot analyses. C. perfringens was grown in anaerobically-prepared Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) medium by incubation at 37°C in an anaerobic chamber (COY Lab Products, Grass Lake, MI, USA) maintained with a gas mixture of 5% hydrogen, 10% CO₂, and balance nitrogen (Lepp et al., 2010).

Bacillus isolation and culture preparation

The isolation and culture preparation of Bacillus bacteria were conducted using the method described previously (Novodvorski et al., 2025). Briefly, chicken intestinal and faecal samples were collected from the intestine of laying hens (HG) and broilers (CG), and chicken faeces (CF) from chicken farms in Ontario, which were raised without the use of antibiotics. After homogenising multiple samples, 0.5 g of each sample was mixed with 5 mL sterile saline and heat-treated at 80°C for 10 min to select spore-forming bacteria. Following a 10-fold serial dilution, the treated sample was plated on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar, followed by incubation at 37°C for 24 h. Single colonies were selected and propagated in LB broth at 37°C for 48 h with shaking (100 rpm). Cell-free culture supernatant (CFS) was prepared by centrifuging the culture at 13,000 × g for 2 min, and the CFS was then filtered through 0.22 µm syringe filters (Millipore, USA). The filtered CFS was stored at −20°C for subsequent analysis. 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed to identify Bacillus isolates, including B. subtilis.

Antimicrobial activity assay

A microdilution assay was performed to test the antimicrobial activity of the CFS from Bacillus isolates using the microdilution method described previously (Novodvorski et al., 2025). Briefly, the sterile CFS from selected Bacillus isolates was added to a 16 h culture of C. perfringens CP1 at a final concentration of 1% (v/v) in BHI broth. Two-fold serial dilutions of each test sample were prepared in BHI broth. For each dilution, 100 μL of the CFS was mixed with 100 μL of the CP1 culture in BHI in a sterile, clear-bottom 96-well microtiter plate (Costar, Corning Inc., New York, USA). Plates were incubated at 37°C for 18–20 h under an anaerobic atmosphere (5% hydrogen, 10% CO₂, and 85% N₂). After incubation, optical density (OD₆₀₀) values were measured using a Biotek microplate reader (Agilent Technologies, Mississauga, ON) to assess bacterial growth. No inhibitory effect on CP1 growth was considered when an OD₆₀₀ value above 0.5 was observed after subtracting a background of the assay mixtures. To confirm bacterial viability, aliquots from each well were plated onto blood agar or inoculated into BHI broth and incubated for 16 h anaerobically.

Antimicrobial resistance assay

The antimicrobial resistance pattern of B. subtilis CG4 was evaluated using the agar disc diffusion method as described previously (Yang et al., 2014). A bacterial lawn of CG4 was prepared on LB agar plates. Antibiotic discs (Oxoid, Canada) containing penicillin G (10 µg/disc), ampicillin (10 µg/disc), erythromycin (15 µg/disc), ciprofloxacin (5 µg/disc), bacitracin (10 µg/disc), nalidixic acid (30 µg/disc), ampicillin/sulbactam (20 µg/disc), chloramphenicol (30 µg/disc), and tetracycline (30 µg/disc) were placed on the plates, with four different antibiotics applied per plate and each antimicrobial with three disc replicates. Following incubation at 37°C for 18–24 h, the plates were examined, and the diameter of each inhibition zone was measured in millimetres using a ruler. In addition to the disk diffusion assay, minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) test strips for penicillin, ampicillin, vancomycin, and gentamicin (concentration range: 0.016–256 µg/mL) were applied to bacterial lawns of each CG4 LB agar plate. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 16 h, and MIC values were determined according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Interpretation of susceptibility was based on the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) and the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines for Bacillus spp.

Spore formation

Difco Sporulation Medium (DSM) was prepared following Nicholson and Setlow’s formulation (Nicholson and Setlow, 1990). The DSM formulation was compared to LB medium to assess its ability to support sporulation versus vegetative growth. For spore production, 30 µL of a 16 h-grown B. subtilis CG4 culture was inoculated into 3 mL of DSM broth and incubated at 37°C with shaking at 180 rpm. At day 5, colony-forming units (CFU) per millilitre were determined for CG4. The examination was conducted after 1 and 5 days of incubation, respectively, to determine the optimal sporulation time and to compare spore formation in DSM versus LB broth. To confirm spore formation, a 10 µL culture sample of CG4 was mounted on a glass slide for microscopic examination under 400 × magnification using a phase-contrast microscope (courtesy of the Guelph Lab Services Division, University of Guelph).

Protein quantification, SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, and immunoblot analysis

The protein concentration of the B. subtilis CG4 CFS was quantified using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, a standard curve was prepared using bovine serum albumin (BSA) diluted in distilled water to final concentrations of 25, 125, 250, 500, 750, 1000, and 2000 µg/ml. The working reagent was freshly prepared by combining reagents A and B in a 50:1 ratio. Standards and samples (25 µL each) were added to a 96-well plate, followed by 200 µL of the working reagent. The plate was mixed for 30 s, incubated at 37°C for 30 min, and then cooled to 22°C. Absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a microplate reader, and protein concentrations of the samples were calculated from the BSA standard curve.

SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and immunoblot analyses were performed according to the manual from Bio-Rad Laboratories. Briefly, sample proteins were separated on a 4–20% SDS-polyacrylamide gradient gel using Tris–Glycine–SDS running buffer at 150 V and 400 mA for approximately 120 min or until the dye front reached the gel bottom. Protein transfer to a PVDF (Polyvinylidene Fluoride) membrane was performed using the Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad) with the low molecular weight protocol (4 min). Membranes were washed twice with 1 × Tris-buffered saline (TBS) and blocked at 4°C for 16 h with BSA blocking solution (30 mg/mL). After washing with distilled water, membranes were incubated with mouse anti-NetB primary antibody (monoclonal, 1:2000 dilution) at 22°C for 1 h. Following four washes with TBS-Tween 20 (TBST), membranes were incubated with polyclonal anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:2000 dilution) at 22°C for 1 h. Protein bands on the membrane were visualised using 1-Step™ Ultra TMB-Blotting Solution (Thermo Scientific) after final washes with TBST and distilled water. To visualize protein bands in an SDS-PAGE gel, the gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 at 22°C for 1 h and subsequently destained in a solution containing 40% ethanol, 10% acetic acid, and 50% distilled water for 1.5 h, with destaining solution replaced every 30 min. The gel was imaged using a Gel Doc imaging system (Bio-Rad) to visualise protein bands.

Anti-NetB production assay

The effect of B. subtilis isolate CFS on the level of NetB toxin produced by CP1 was examined by culturing CP1 in BHI containing 50% CFS from different B. subtilis cultures, followed by immunoblot analysis using a monoclonal antibody against NetB toxin. The B. subtilis strains that did not inhibit the growth of CP1 were subjected to the immunoblot analysis, unless indicated otherwise. The culturing CP1 with Bacillus CFS was conducted in a 96-well plate using a microdilution assay method similar to the antimicrobial activity assay described above, except that BHI and LB broth were mixed in equal volumes (1:1, 200 μL total per well) in the assay mixtures. The dilutions were up to 16x. The plate was incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 16–24 h. The assay mixtures were then analysed by the immunoblot analysis, as described above, to determine the effect of the CFS from B. subtilis cultures on the level of NetB toxin produced by C. perfringens CP1. The NetB toxin has a molecular weight of approximately 33 kDa.

Assays for measuring heat and protease sensitivity of CG4 CFS

To evaluate the effect of heat treatment of B. subtilis CG4 CFS on its activity to abolish NetB toxin, the CFS was heat-treated at 100°C for 1 to 10 minutes, followed by the anti-NetB production assay described above. Heat treatment at 80°C for 1 min was also conducted.

To evaluate the sensitivity of B. subtilis CG4 CFS to protease digestion and its ability to abolish NetB toxin, the CFS was treated with proteinase K and chymotrypsin, which in a 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) at final enzyme concentrations of 1 mg/mL and 100 µg/mL, respectively. Proteinase K digestion was conducted at 56°C for 30 minutes, while chymotrypsin digestion was performed at 37°C for 1 hour. After digestion, the samples were used for the anti-NetB production assay to determine the effect of the protease treatment. In parallel, heat-inactivated (100°C, 5 min) proteinase K or chymotrypsin was mixed with the CG4 CFS, which were subjected to the assay to serve as a control.

Determination of proteolytic activity in CG4 CFS

To determine whether the CFS from CG4 possessed proteolytic activity, the CFS from both the CP1 and CG4 cultures was co-incubated under conditions similar to those in the microdilution assay described above. The CP1 CFS was diluted 2 × with distilled water, while the CG4 CFS at different dilutions of 2x, 4x, 8x, and 16x was mixed with 2x diluted CP1 CFS in each of the assay mixtures. These assay mixtures, containing either the CP1 CFS alone or both the CP1 CFS and CG4 CFS, were subjected to two different treatments: one incubated at 37°C for 16 h, and the other without incubation. All samples were subsequently analysed by the immunoblot analysis as described above. The presence or reduction in the intensity of the NetB toxin band (∼33 kDa) was used to assess proteolytic degradation.

To further confirm the proteolytic activity in the CG4 CFS and the spectrum of the activity, digestion assays of BSA and casein by the CG4 CFS were conducted. After the digestion, a total of 81.0 µg, 40.5 µg, 20.3 µg, and 10.0 µg BSA and 80.0 µg casein protein were loaded onto an SDS–PAGE gel, respectively. The protein degradation was visualised by Coomassie blue staining. In the BSA digestion assay, the tested samples included: 1) the CG4 CFS alone (control); 2) BSA standards at final concentrations of 7200, 3600, 1800, and 900 µg/mL; 3) CG4 CFS incubated with BSA at the same concentration as in 2). All mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 1 h before the SDS-PAGE electrophoresis.

In the casein digestion assay, assay mixtures were prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using 2 × casein stock solution in a final volume of 200 µL per tube. The assay included two treatments: casein-only and casein incubated with the CG4 CFS. While the concentration of casein protein was kept consistent in the assay mixture (80 µg/µL), the diluted CG4 CFS at 1 ×, 2 ×, 4 ×, 8 ×, and 16 × was tested. The assay mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 1 h before the SDS-PAGE electrophoresis. Each lane in the SDS-PAGE gel contained an equivalent amount of casein (80 µg).

Experimental C. perfringens infection in chickens

The experiment with chickens and conditions for their use were approved by the University of Guelph Animal Care Committee in accordance with the Canadian Council on Animal Care’s Guidelines. NE was experimentally induced in chickens as previously described (Shojadoost et al., 2022). To prepare the C. perfringens inoculum for infecting chickens, CP1 was cultured anaerobically at 37°C for 16 h in Cooked Meat Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Mississauga, ON, Canada). Subsequently, 3% (v/v) of this culture was inoculated into Fluid Thioglycollate Medium (FTG; Sigma-Aldrich, Oakville, ON, Canada) and incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 15 h. The growth of the pathogen was monitored by measuring the OD₆₀₀ of the culture to ensure that each chicken received at least 3 × 10⁸ CFU of bacterial cells when the inoculum was used to challenge the bird. The trial design was the completely random design (CRD). Fifty-two one-day-old male broiler chickens (Ross 708) were randomly assigned to three groups with each bird treated individually as an experiment unit: 1) no CP1 infection and no B. subtilis CG4 supplementation (negative control – NC; 12 birds); 2) CP1 infection only (positive control – PC; 20 birds); 3) CP1 infection and B. subtilis CG4 supplementation (20 birds). Chicks were vaccinated at the hatchery according to the standard vaccination program for broiler chickens. Birds were housed by treatment group in separate floor pens with wood shavings as bedding and provided ad libitum access to feed and water in the isolation facility at the Ontario Veterinary College. The ambient temperature was maintained at 32°C during the first week and gradually reduced by 2°C per week till the end of the trial (26°C). Relative humidity was maintained at approximately 40% throughout the experimental period. The basal diet was a broiler Starter diet (in mash form) for days 0-14. From Day 15 to Day 24, chicks were fed a wheat-based diet containing high levels of non-starch polysaccharides and fish meal. The diet components are displayed in Table 1. B. subtilis CG4 was supplemented in feed in the form of endospores (106 CFU/g feed) from Day 0 to Day 24. C. perfringens infection was achieved by oral gavage of birds with CP1 (at least 3 × 10⁸ CFU/bird) on Day 22 and Day 23, twice daily. On Day 24, birds were euthanised using CO2 for NE lesion scoring and sample collection. The small intestine was examined for NE lesions and scored on a 0–6 scale following the Australian lesion scoring system (Keyburn et al., 2008), where 0 indicates no visible lesions, and 6 represents severe necrosis. Birds from all three groups were weighed on days 14 (before the diet switch), 21 (before CP1 challenge), and 24. Faecal samples were collected on days 14, 21, and 23, and ileal digesta was sampled on Day 24 when the birds were euthanised. Faecal samples were collected from the floor of each room at five different locations (four corners and the centre). Data analysis

Power analyses (with a target power of 0.85) were conducted to determine the appropriate sample size needed to detect statistically significant effects and the data for lesion scoring were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test as described previously (Alizadeh et al., 2026). PROC GLM was used to analyse unbalanced data followed by the Tukey’s multiple comparison test (SAS Studio). Fisher’s exact test was analysed using PROC FREQ to identify differences in the distribution of NE incidence. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation error of mean (SEM) and statistical significances were determined at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Cellular location of NetB toxin in C. perfringens cultures

The NetB cellular location in C. perfringens CP1 and CP4 cultures was assessed by an immunoblot analysis (Fig. 1). A visible band (∼33 kDa) corresponding to NetB was observed predominantly in the CFS, demonstrating that the toxin is secreted into the extracellular environment (Fig. 1; Lanes 3, 4, 7 and 8). No NetB band was observed for the spontaneous mutant of CP1 (Lane 2), which has lost the NE plasmid and therefore lacks the netB gene, confirming the specificity of the assay. Antimicrobial and anti-NetB activities of B. subtilis strains against C. perfringens

Approximately 100 Bacillus isolates were initially screened for antimicrobial activity against C. perfringens CP1 using a microdilution growth inhibition assay. Isolates that did not inhibit CP1 growth (OD₆₀₀ > 0.5) were selected for further analysis of their effect on NetB toxin production. This approach reduced the samples to 30 isolates, all of which were identified as Bacillus subtilis, collected from the intestine of laying hens (HG) and broilers (CG) as well as chicken faecal samples (CF). The CFS of these 30 isolates were screened by immunoblot analysis to assess their impact on NetB production in CP1 cultures. Among them, only the CFS from B. subtilis CG4 consistently abolished the NetB band (∼33 kDa) (Fig. 2A). CFS from other isolates produced a detectable NetB signal and were therefore not pursued further. To determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of CG4 CFS, the CFS was serially diluted up to 64-fold and the NetB level in CP1 culture was tested. Antibiotic resistance and spore formation of B. subtilis CG4

B. subtilis CG4 was evaluated for antibiotic susceptibility using nine antibiotic discs and four MIC test strips. The CG4 exhibited resistance exclusively to tetracycline and the penicillin MIC strip, while remaining sensitive to all other tested antibiotics, reflecting a narrow spectrum of resistance. CG4 was also evaluated for its spore-forming ability. The culture produced approximately 99% spores at the concentration near 3.0 × 10⁸ spores/mL after 5 days of incubation.

Sensitivity of NetB-reducing component in CG4 CFS to heat and protease treatments

To determine the heat sensitivity of the B. subtilis CG4 CFS, the CFS was heated at 100°C for up to 10 min before the anti-NetB production assay. As shown in Fig. 3A, the untreated CG4 CFS at both 2 × and 4 × dilutions completely eliminated NetB toxin produced by C. perfringens CP1, as evidenced by the absence of the 33 kDa band in Lanes 3 and 4. In contrast, all of the heat-treated samples of CG4 CFS demonstrated a full restoration of the NetB band (Lanes 5–10). Low heat treatment at 80°C for 1 min also abolished the CFS activity in eliminating NetB. To investigate the sensitivity of CG4 CFS to proteases, the samples were subjected to protease digestion by proteinase K or chymotrypsin prior to the anti-NetB production assay. As shown in Fig. 3B, proteinase K treatment eliminated the NetB band (Lanes 6-7). However, chymotrypsin did not show significant digestion of either NetB in the CP1 culture (Lanes 11 and 12), or the NetB-reducing component in the CG4 CFS, as indicated by the disappearance of the NetB band in Lanes 13-15. This result indicated that the bioactive component is resistant to chymotrypsin digestion. Inactivated proteinase K and chymotrypsin showed no effect on the NetB toxin level produced by C. perfringens CP1. These results suggested that the bioactive component in the CFS is proteinaceous, and is not equally susceptible to different proteases. Together, the heat treatment and protease digestion results strongly suggest that the CG4 CFS component responsible for abolishing NetB toxin produced by CP1 is proteinaceous in nature.

Proteolytic activity in CG4 CFS

To investigate the nature of the bioactive component in the CFS of B. subtilis CG4, we examined if the disappearance of the NetB band in the immunoblot was due to degradation by proteolytic activity from the CFS. To achieve the goal, the CFS from the CP1 culture instead of the CP1 culture itself was used in the NetB production assay. The CP1 CFS and CG4 CFS were first mixed at different ratios and then incubated at 37°C for 16 h. The control was the samples collected immediately without incubation. As shown in Fig. 4, in the samples without incubation, the detectable NetB degradation decreased with increasing dilution (Lanes 6-9). However, in 16-h incubated samples, NetB degradation was observed at up to 16 × dilutions (Lanes 10-13), indicating that the incubation was responsible for the degradation. In addition, since the CFS rather than the entire CP1 culture was used in the assay, no NetB was continuously produced from CP1 cells during the incubation. Reduction of C. perfringens–induced NE lesions in broilers by B. subtilis CG4

To examine the effect of B. subtilis CG4 on reducing NE lesions in chickens, experimental C. perfringens infection in broiler chickens was conducted. As shown in Fig. 7A, the chickens with CP1-infected only (positive control) showed a marked increase (p < 0.0001) in NE severity compared with the untreated group of chickens (negative control; average NE score = 0). However, supplementation with CG4 endospores significantly reduced NE lesions (p = 0.04) in the chickens infected with C. perfringens CP1. The average NE score in the positive control group was 3.75 ± 0.43, while the average NE score in the CG4-treated group was 2.47 ± 0.41. These results demonstrated that supplementation of B. subtilis CG4 was able to reduce NE severity in C. perfringens-infected chickens. Supplementation of B. subtilis did not significantly reduce NE incidence (p = 0.47), although the incidence in the CG4-treated group was 75% in contrast to 84% in the positive control group (Fig. 7B). Discussion

The present study aimed to select Bacillus strains producing extracellular components capable of abolishing or reducing the level of NetB toxin produced by C. perfringens to control NE disease in broiler chickens. The advantage of this strategy over the selection of Bacillus isolates with the ability to kill C. perfringens is the reduction of the possibility of developing antimicrobial resistance in the pathogen (Waglechner and Wright, 2017). To achieve this goal, the research started with screening Bacillus isolates that did not inhibit C. perfringens CP1 growth in the antimicrobial assays, followed by selecting Bacillus isolates that could abolish or reduce the NetB toxin level through a NetB production assay involving the use of immunoblot analysis with a monoclonal antibody against NetB. In the antimicrobial assay, among different Bacillus species, most isolates that did not inhibit C. perfringens growth belonged to B. subtilis. Thus, the most used isolates for the screening with the immunoblot analysis are B. subtilis.

B. subtilis CG4 was isolated from the intestine of broiler chickens. This strain did not inhibit the growth of C. perfringens, but exhibited a novel proteolytic activity against NetB produced by C. perfringens, and was able to reduce C. perfringens-induced NE lesions in chickens, presumably through reduction of NetB toxin by its extracellular proteolytic activity. This is the first report, to our knowledge, that proteolytic activity in a B. subtilis culture was able to lessen NE disease in chickens. Moreover, the finding provides further evidence to support the strategy to control an enteric infection through targeting a key virulence factor without killing the pathogen. Here, we also acknowledge that the present NE model in the current study did not include a coccidial predisposing factor (Eimeria spp.). Our objective was to employ a subclinical NE model to address the control of subclinical NE disease. The model has been developed over years through dietary manipulation, specifically using diets high in crude protein and non-starch polysaccharides, which consistently induce intestinal lesions, a characteristic of subclinical NE (Prescott et al., 2016b).

Previously, Piewngam et al. (2018) reported the control of Staphylococcus aureus infections in the rural Thai population by B. subtilis, not through killing the pathogen, but through specifically blocking its quorum sensing system. In their study, the bioactive component was identified to be fengycin, which is a cyclic lipopeptide. Unlike proteinaceous factors, fengycin is heat-resistant and retains its protective activity even at elevated temperatures. In contrast to the results of Piewngam et al. (2018), the present study found the presence of proteolytic activity in the B. subtilis CG4 CFS, which was capable of degrading NetB toxin and other proteins such as BSA and casein. In addition, the reference strain (ZK3814) of B. subtilis used for their study (Piewngam et al., 2018) was tested for its ability to block Agr-like quorum sensing in C. perfringens (Yu et al., 2017) to abolish NetB toxin in the present study. However, ZK 3814 showed no effect on NetB production (Fig. 2A), suggesting that the quorum-sensing system in C. perfringens is not the same as that in S. aureus, although both pathogens are Gram-positive bacteria and possess an Agr quorum sensing system.

The findings in the present study are consistent with previous reports showing that probiotic-derived proteases can degrade enterotoxins produced by C. difficile, a major causative pathogen for diarrhoea and septicaemia in both animals and humans (Castagliuolo et al., 1996, 1999; Ripert et al., 2016). Almost three decades ago, Castagliuolo et al. (1996, 1999) reported that serine proteases from Saccharomyces boulardii could degrade two toxin molecules produced by C. difficile. Later, Ripert et al. (2016) demonstrated that a serine protease produced by the probiotic strain B. clausii O/C could hydrolyse the peptide bonds within the enterotoxins of C. difficile and B. cereus. Collectively, these previous studies provide evidence supporting the protective effects of protease enzymes from probiotics to control enteric diseases. Thus, the findings from the present study offer an opportunity to use protease-producing probiotics to control NE disease in poultry.

Structural analyses of both Bacillus-derived proteases and NetB toxin provide a rational explanation for the underlying proteolytic process. Such proteases are known to preferentially cleave peptide bonds within conformationally flexible regions of substrate proteins (López-Otín and Bond, 2008). The NetB toxin contains structurally vulnerable regions, including a disordered loop and an aromatic residue–rich rim loop essential for membrane binding and pore assembly (Yan et al., 2013). These structural susceptibilities align well with the proteolytic preferences of proteases, providing a potential structural basis for NetB inactivation.

Previously, Jayaraman et al. (2013) demonstrated that dietary supplementation with B. subtilis PB6 significantly reduced C. perfringens-induced NE lesion scores in broiler chickens, although no statistically significant effect on body weight gain was observed. Similarly, Aljumaah et al. (2020) reported that continuous supplementation with B. subtilis PB6 improved post-NE recovery by decreasing lesion scores, improving intestinal morphology, and enhancing feed conversion ratio and growth performance. The biological relevance of selecting B. subtilis CG4 with the ability to reduce NetB toxin level in the present study became evident by the results of a C. perfringens-infection chicken experiment, in which supplemented spores of B. subtilis CG4 significantly not only reduced NE lesions but also appeared to increase bird body weight.

Bacillus-derived proteases have been widely applied in various industries owing to their stability across a broad range of pH and temperature, as well as their tolerance to harsh conditions such as alkaline environments and organic solvents (Gupta et al., 2002; Rehman et al., 2017). This stability has enabled their extensive use in industries including food, beverage, detergent, leather, pharmaceutical, and medical sectors (Gupta et al., 2002; Sumantha et al., 2006). The supplementation of protease in poultry feed has long been used in practice (Sumantha et al., 2006). Previous studies have reported that protease supplementation is a gut-friendly feed additive for broiler chickens, enhancing growth performance, weight gain, and a balanced intestinal microbiota (Huyan et al., 2022; McCafferty et al., 2022). Two particular studies on the use of B. subtilis-derived proteases to improve the recovery of C. perfringens-infected chickens were recently reported by Sokale et al. (2019) and Tajudeen et al. (2022). Both studies demonstrated that supplementation with B. subtilis-derived exogenous proteases markedly improved bird performance, intestinal morphology, and survival rates of broilers that had been infected with C. perfringens CP1. Hence, the findings from the present study further highlight the potential of Bacillus strains with proteolytic activity as beneficial feed additives for promoting intestine health and growth performance.

In summary, the current study provides evidence that B. subtilis CG4 has potent proteolytic activity, which degraded NetB toxin and reduced NE lesions in broiler chickens infected with C. perfringens. In addition, the supplementation of CG4 in feed increased bird body weight. These results support the anti-virulence strategy to control NE disease in poultry. Additionally, the effects of CG4 on reducing of NE toxin and increasing bird bodyweight promise the potential to use specific Bacillus strains with potent proteolytic activity to control an enteric disease and promote animal growth. Nonetheless, further studies are required to provide direct evidence on the function of protease from CG4 in alleviating NE disease.
