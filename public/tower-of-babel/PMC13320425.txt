Valorization of roasted sesame hulls for flavored sesame oil with improved quality: Optimization, flavor profile, and storage performance
Authors: Yuan-Qing Luo, Rui Wang, Jia-Xuan Bai, Hua-Min Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study optimized the procedures for preparing flavored sesame oil using roasted sesame hulls and elucidated the contribution of hulls-derived compounds to flavor enhancement and oxidative stability. The optimal extraction conditions were a hull-to-oil ratio of 1:5 at 45 °C for 8 h. The resulting oil displayed the most balanced flavor profile (enhanced caramel, roasty, and nutty notes), attributed to efficient transfer of key aromatics from the roasted sesame hulls. Accelerated storage revealed that the flavored oil exhibited a slightly shorter initial oxidation induction time and higher acid value (AV) and p-anisidine value (AnV). However, after 40 days, increases in AV, peroxide value, and AnV were significantly lower than in the control. Volatiles analysis also showed reduced accumulation of lipid oxidation-derived off-flavor compounds (e.g., 2-pentylfuran, aldehydes). These findings demonstrate the dual functionality of roasted sesame hulls in flavor enhancement and oxidative stabilization, offering practical strategies for high-quality flavored oil production.Keywords: Flavored sesame oil, Sesame hulls, Sensory evaluation, Oxidative stability, Volatile compounds

Introduction

Sesame oil (SO), a vegetable oil extracted from sesame (Sesamum indicum L.) seeds, is highly appreciated by consumers for its distinctive roasted aroma and nutritional properties (Kuang et al., 2025). It can be broadly classified into two categories according to whether the manufacturing process includes high-temperature roasting, a critical step for flavor formation (Xu et al., 2025): (i) unroasted sesame oil, including cold-pressed and refined sesame oils, is produced without high-temperature processing of sesame seeds (Yu, Ma, et al., 2021). Such oils are characterized by a relatively mild flavor, with the proteins in their byproducts largely retaining the natural structure; (ii) roasted sesame oil, including aqueous-extracted roasted sesame oil and pressed roasted sesame oil, exhibits a distinctive and intense roasted aroma (Kuang et al., 2025; Sharma et al., 2025). This distinctive flavor primarily stems from a complex mixture of volatile compounds produced during the high-temperature (180–250 °C) roasting of sesame seeds through the Maillard reaction, lipid oxidation, caramelization, and their synergistic effects (Huang et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2021). However, the traditional roasted sesame oil processing strategies aimed at producing desirable roasted aromas often result in severe thermal denaturation of sesame seed proteins (Hu et al., 2025). Consequently, the utilization value of sesame meal/cake, the by-product of oil processing, is markedly reduced, posing a challenge to the sustainable development of the sesame processing industry (Zhang et al., 2023). Therefore, striking an optimal balance between enhancing characteristic sesame flavor and maximally preserving the nutritional and functional properties of raw materials remains a critical challenge in current sesame oil production.

In recent years, with the growing emphasis on the valorization of sesame processing by-products, sesame hulls, an important by-product of sesame seed processing, have garnered increasing attention for their potential functional and flavor properties (Miran et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2023; Wang, Guo, et al., 2024). Previous studies have demonstrated that lignins and polysaccharides in sesame hulls undergo thermal degradation during roasting, leading to the formation of abundant phenolic compounds and Maillard reaction precursors (Liu et al., 2023; Zhao, Zhu, et al., 2025). Wang, Wu, et al. (2024) reported that, compared with whole sesame seeds and dehulled kernels, roasted sesame hulls produced significantly higher levels of aroma-active compounds, predominantly furans, aldehydes, ketones, and phenolic volatiles. The synergistic interactions among these compounds resulted in caramel-like and burnt flavor at a roasting temperature of 180 °C, whereas sesame hulls roasted at 200 °C exhibited intense caramel aroma, pronounced burnt smell, and moderate fatty aroma intensity. Furthermore, phenolic compounds, such as vanillin and 1-(4-hydroxy-3-methoxyphenyl)butan-1-one, generated from the thermal degradation of lignin during sesame hulls roasting have been shown to enhance the oxidative stability of sesame oil (Qin et al., 2023). These findings suggest that roasted sesame hulls may serve not only as a natural source of flavor-active compounds for sesame oil but also as a contributor of antioxidant components. From a sustainability perspective, the valorization of sesame hulls aligns well with the current trends in the use of by-products and the development of clean-label ingredients. However, the migration behavior of flavor compounds from roasted sesame hulls into sesame oil and its subsequent impact on oil quality remain poorly understood. Specifically, it remains unclear whether key aroma compounds can efficiently partition into the oil phase, whether distinct classes of volatiles exhibit differential migration behaviors, and whether these migrated components synergistically enhance the flavor and oxidative stability of oil.

Accordingly, the present study aims to optimize the production of flavored sesame oil by oil-phase extraction of roasted sesame hulls and to elucidate the dual role of volatile compounds derived from roasted sesame hulls in flavor enhancement and quality stabilization of refined sesame oil. Specifically, the study will undertake the following tasks: (i) optimize the preparation process of roasted sesame hulls flavored sesame oil, including extraction temperature, time, and hull-to-oil ratio, based on key aroma compound concentrations and sensory evaluation; (ii) elucidate the migration behavior of key aroma compounds from roasted sesame hulls into the oil phase; and (iii) evaluate the effects of volatile compounds derived from sesame hulls on the oxidative stability and quality evolution of sesame oil under accelerated storage conditions. Headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS) will be employed to characterize key aroma compounds in sesame oil, in combination with sensory evaluation to screen optimal processing parameters. This work elucidates the effects of roasted sesame hulls flavor compounds on the flavor and quality of refined sesame oil, offering both theoretical insights and practical advice for the development of high-quality flavored edible oils.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The raw sesame seeds (Zhuzhi 22) were obtained from Henan Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Zhengzhou, China). Sesame hulls were produced in the laboratory by dehulling and sieving sesame seeds, following a well-established procedure (Wang, Wu, et al., 2024). The flavor extraction from the roasted sesame hulls was performed using commercial Taibai Sesame Oil (Takemoto Oil and Fat Co. Ltd., Japan), characterized by its light color and negligible odor. 4-Nonanol and dichloromethane were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The n-alkane mixture (C7-C30) was from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All reagents and solvents used in the experiments were of analytical grade or higher purity.

Preparation of flavored sesame oil

Sesame hulls (10.00 ± 0.01 g) were packed into 50 mL pressure-resistant bottles and heated in an oil bath at 200 °C for 30 min. After cooling to ambient temperature, the roasted hulls were mixed with sesame oil for flavor extraction. A series of single-factor experiments were conducted to investigate the effects of three key parameters, including sesame hull-to-oil ratio, extraction temperature, and extraction time.

Effect of hull-to-oil ratio: Roasted hulls were mixed with sesame oil at different ratios (1:5, 1:6, 1:7, 1:8, 1:9 w/w) and shaken in a water bath maintained at 25 °C for 4 h. The mixture was subsequently separated by vacuum filtration to obtain the flavored oil for SPME-GC–MS analysis and sensory evaluation.

Effect of extraction temperature: Sesame hulls were mixed with sesame oil in the ratio of 1:5 (w/w) and shaken in a water bath at different temperatures (25, 35, 45, 55, 65 °C) for 4 h. Subsequently, the sesame oil was separated from the hulls by vacuum filtration. The resulting flavored sesame oil was used for SPME-GC–MS analysis and sensory evaluation.

Effect of extraction time: Sesame hulls and sesame oil were mixed at a 1:5 (w/w) ratio and shaken in a 45 °C water bath for varying durations (4, 6, 8, 10, 12 h). Subsequently, sesame oil was separated from the hulls via vacuum filtration. The resulting flavored sesame oil was used for SPME-GC–MS analysis and sensory evaluation.

HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis

Approximately 3.00 g of flavored sesame oil was placed into a glass vial with a threaded cap for HS-SPME using a fully automated autosampler (SARR11 – DVB/CWR120/20 - P1). The samples were shaken at 60 °C for 40 min to promote the volatilization of flavor compounds prior to analysis by GC–MS. Flavor extraction was performed using an extraction head (DVB/C - WR/PDMS, 20 mm × 120 μm) that had undergone a one-hour aging treatment. The extraction duration was 30 min, with an insertion depth of 1 cm.

The extracted volatiles were analyzed using a 7000E Triple Quadrupole gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) system operated in full scan mode. The GC temperature program was set as follows: initial temperature held at 40 °C for 5 min, increased to 130 °C at a rate of 3 °C/min and held for 5 min, then raised to 250 °C at 10 °C/min and finally maintained at 250 °C for 5 min. The mass spectrometer was operated in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV, with a scanning range of m/z 33–400 at a scan speed of 2.0 scans/s.

Qualitative analysis

Volatile compounds were identified by matching their mass spectra against the NIST20 library. Further confirmation was achieved by comparing the calculated linear retention indices (RIs) of the detected compounds with the reported RI values for a homologous series of C7–C30 alkanes analyzed under the same chromatographic conditions.

Quantification analysis

As described by Yin et al. (2021), the relative concentration of each volatile compound was determined by HS-SPME-GC–MS employing 4-nonanol as an internal standard, where the peak area ratio of the analyte to the internal standard was used for quantification.

Odor activity value (OAV)

The OAVs for the target key aroma compounds that migrated from the roasted sesame hulls into the flavored sesame oil were calculated as the ratio of their concentrations to their corresponding odor thresholds. The odor thresholds of these target analytes were obtained from the reported work (Jia et al., 2019; Jia et al., 2020; Van Gemert, 2011; Yin et al., 2021).

Sensory evaluation

The sensory evaluation of flavored sesame oil samples was conducted following the method described by Yin et al. (2021). A sensory evaluation panel consisting of 11 trained panelists (6 females and 5 males, aged 22–26 years) from Henan University of Technology, each with at least two years of sensory evaluation experience, participated in this study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the evaluation of the aroma attributes of the samples. They scored the intensity of nine aroma attributes (green, woody, roasty, nutty, caramel-like, fatty, smoky, burnt, rancidity) in flavored sesame oil samples on a 10 cm line scale (“0” indicating imperceptible, “10” indicating strongly perceptible).

Accelerated storage experiment

The sesame oil sample exhibiting the most desirable flavor profile from each single-factor experiment, along with untreated Taibai sesame oil as a blank control (SOBC), was subjected to accelerated storage. Each sample was sealed in a glass vial and stored at 63 ± 1 °C for 40 days. Every five days, a sample aliquot was taken for the determination of acid value (AV), peroxide value (POV), and p-anisidine value (AnV). In addition, volatile compounds were analyzed every ten days using HS-SPME-GC–MS to monitor the changes in the volatile profile during storage.

Determination of oxidation induction time (OIT)

The OITs of the flavored oil samples were determined using a 743 Rancimat (Metrohm AG, Herisau, Switzerland). Approximately 5.0 g of oil was heated to 110 °C under an airflow of 20 L/h. The volatile oxidation products were transferred into a vessel containing deionized water, and the resulting change in conductivity was continuously recorded. The induction time, corresponding to the point of a sharp increase in conductivity, was automatically determined by the instrument as an indicator of oxidative rancidity.

Acid value (AV), peroxide value (POV), and p-anisidine value (AnV) analysis

The AV, POV, and AnV were determined in accordance with the standard methods of ISO 660:2020, ISO 3960:2017, and ISO 6885:2016, respectively.

Color analysis

Color changes of the oil samples during storage were monitored using a spectrophotometric colorimeter (CS-821 N, CHN Spec, Hangzhou, China). The data for color were represented by L* (Lightness, ranging from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating greater brightness), a* (Red-green values, positive values for red and negative for green), b* (Yellow-blue values, positive values for yellow and negative for blue).

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were presented as “mean ± standard deviation (SD)”. Statistical significance was analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test in IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 20). The significance level was set at p = 0.05, with p < 0.05 indicating a significant difference. All figures were generated using Origin software (version 2024b).

Results and discussion

Optimization of flavored sesame oil preparation conditions

Effect of hull-to-oil ratio on flavor and sensory attributes of sesame oil

Flavored sesame oils prepared with varying ratios of sesame hull-to-oil were analyzed by HS-SPME-GC–MS, identifying a total of 123 volatile compounds. As shown in Figs. 1A–B, the primary volatile components belong to categories including furans, pyrazines, pyrroles, pyridines, aldehydes, ketones, acids, phenols, and others. Among these, furan compounds exhibit the highest content across all samples.The high retention rate of furans in lipid-containing systems is attributed to their strong lipophilicity and volatility (Van Lancker et al., 2009). The primary furan derivatives detected in flavored sesame oil include carbonyl furans, alkyl furans, aryl furans, acetyl furans, and furfural. These compounds typically originate from pathways such as the Maillard reaction, carbohydrate degradation, thermal decomposition of amino acids, and lipid oxidation (Emektar et al., 2022; Schöpf et al., 2025). Sesame hulls are inherently rich in precursor substances such as polysaccharides, proteins, and trace lipids, thereby providing the fundamental reactants for the formation of furan compounds. Among the identified furan derivatives, furfural showed the highest concentration (20,524 ± 326 μg/kg) (Table 1). Previous studies have demonstrated that furfural is one of the key aroma compounds in roasted sesame hulls, imparting characteristic caramel-like, smoky, and burnt flavors. The presence of furfural in the flavored sesame oil indicates its effective transfer from roasted sesame hulls to the oil phase, thereby markedly enhancing the overall flavor profile of the oil.

As the proportion of sesame oil increases (i.e., the hull-to-oil ratio changes from 1:5 to 1:9), the total concentration of volatile compounds in flavored sesame oil decreases. This trend may be explained by two factors: (i) the amount of volatiles that can migrate from a fixed mass of sesame hulls into the oil phase is limited; and (ii) the increase in oil phase volume dilutes the migrated flavor compounds, leading to a lower volatile concentration per unit volume of oil. Notably, the extent of the concentration decrease varies among different classes of volatile compounds. This is likely related to differences in their polarity, hydrophobicity, and volatility, all of which influence their efficiency of migration into the oil phase. Specifically, aldehydes and pyridines typically exhibit moderate polarity, resulting in relatively limited partitioning into the nonpolar oil phase. Consequently, the dilution effect is most pronounced for these compounds, resulting in the greatest reduction in concentration. In contrast, furans, pyrroles, pyrazines, and certain phenolic compounds (such as sesamol and maltol derivatives) possess relatively stronger hydrophobic structural characteristics, which may facilitate their transfer to and retention in the oil phase and thus cause their concentration to decrease more slowly. Acids were detected only at the hull-to-oil ratio of 1:5 (82 μg/kg), suggesting that their relatively high polarity hindered their migration into the nonpolar oil matrix. Furthermore, differences in volatility may also influence the retention and detectability of individual compounds in the oil phase.

In our previous study (Wang, Wu, et al., 2024), 25 key aroma compounds in roasted sesame hulls were identified based on odor activity values (OAV) analysis. In the present study, these 25 hulls-derived volatiles were selected as targeted analytes to investigate their migration from roasted sesame hulls into the prepared flavored sesame oils. As shown in Table 1, Table 2, Table 3, 15 of the 25 key aroma compounds identified previously in roasted sesame hulls were detected in the flavored sesame oils prepared under different conditions (Fig. 2). To further evaluate the sensory contribution of these migrated aroma compounds in flavored sesame oil, the OAV for each compound were calculated based on odor thresholds (according to literature data). The results indicate that these 15 targeted compounds are not only the key aroma compounds in roasted sesame hulls but also in flavored sesame oil (OAV > 1). However, the detection levels and sensory contributions of the 15 targeted analytes derived from roasted sesame hulls varied in flavored sesame oil prepared under different conditions.

Table 1 shows the concentrations and OAVs of the aroma compounds transferred from roasted sesame hulls into flavored sesame oils prepared at different hull-to-oil ratios. In the oil sample produced at a hull-to-oil ratio of 1:5, furfural was present at the highest concentration (20,524 μg/kg), followed by hexanal (3281 μg/kg), 2-methylpyrazine (2979 μg/kg), and nonanal (1131 μg/kg). In samples prepared at hull-to-oil ratios of 1:6–1:8, furfural remained the predominant transferred aroma compound, with concentrations ranging from 10,510 to 18,559 μg/kg, followed by 2-methylpyrazine (1278–2433 μg/kg) and phenylacetaldehyde (464–918 μg/kg). In the 1:9 sample, in addition to furfural (8799 μg/kg) and 2-methylpyrazine (915 μg/kg), 3-hydroxy-2-butanone was also present at a relatively high level (265 μg/kg). Overall, the total concentration of the migrated aroma compounds reached its maximum at the 1:5 ratio, indicating that this condition was most favorable for their transfer and accumulation in the oil phase. Further increasing the proportion of oil led to a marked decrease in the total concentration of the migrated aroma compounds, which was most likely attributable to dilution effects.

The OAV results further highlighted the potential sensory contributions of individual compounds in the flavored sesame oils. In the 1:5 sample, hexanal exhibited the highest OAV among all target analytes (OAV = 33), followed by furfural (OAV = 29), 2-methoxyphenol (OAV = 22), and 2-methylpyrazine (OAV = 15). Hexanal is typically associated with grassy and fatty notes and is an important contributor to the fatty note of blank sesame oil (Feng et al., 2025). Sensory evaluation results (Fig. 3A) indicated that the unprocessed sesame oil primarily exhibits fatty odor characteristics. With the migration of flavor compounds from roasted sesame hulls, the intensity of roasty, nutty, caramel-like, smoky, and burnt flavors significantly increased. Oil sample with a hull-to-oil mass ratio of 1:5 achieved the highest overall sensory scores. This sensory enhancement was closely associated with the relatively high OAVs of compounds such as furfural (delivering caramel-like and burnt flavors), 2-methylpyrazine (contributing roasty and nutty flavors), and 2-methoxyphenol (providing smoky flavor) (Hu et al., 2024; (Wang, Wu, et al., 2024)). Although hexanal showed the highest OAV, its characteristic grassy/fatty odor did not dominate the sensory profile of the flavored oils, suggesting that roasted-note compounds exerted a more pronounced impact on the overall aroma perception in this system. A comprehensive assessment integrating quantitative analysis of key aroma compounds and sensory evaluation results confirms that the 1:5 hull-to-seed ratio is the optimal proportion for preparing flavored sesame oil. The flavored sesame oil obtained at the hull-to-oil ratio of 1:5 was designated as SOOR.

Effects of different extraction temperature on flavor and sensory of sesame oils

Flavored sesame oils prepared at a fixed hull-to-oil ratio of 1:5 across a temperature range of 25–65 °C were analyzed by HS-SPME-GC–MS, identifying a total of 103 volatile compounds. The results showed that extraction temperature significantly influenced the migration and accumulation behavior of volatile classes. As shown in Figs.1C–D, furans, aldehydes, phenols, pyridines, acids, and other minor compounds generally reached their highest levels at 45 °C, whereas ketones peaked at 55 °C, pyrroles at 65 °C, and pyrazines were most abundant at 25 °C. This compound-dependent migration behavior can be attributed to the combined effects of differences in diffusion rates, oil–solid partitioning, and thermal stability. Moderate heating can enhance mass transfer by increasing diffusivity and facilitating the release of aroma compounds from the roasted sesame hulls, whereas excessive high temperature may simultaneously increase volatilization losses and promote secondary oxidation or thermal degradation in oils.

The quantitative results for the target aroma compounds further confirm that the extraction temperature significantly influences the key aroma compound profile of flavored sesame oils. As shown in Table 2, the maximum total concentration of key aroma compounds were observed at 45 °C, indicating the optimal migration efficiency at this temperature. Subsequently, the concentration decreased significantly at 55 °C, but increased again when the temperature was raised to 65 °C. This increase is ascribed to the higher temperature, which was sufficient to induce thermal oxidation of the oil itself (Kiralan & Ramadan, 2016; Li et al., 2025), generating additional volatiles (e.g., aldehydes and furans), thereby leading to a renewed increase in the total concentration of aroma compounds at 65 °C. Across all temperature treatments, furfural (caramel-like, burnt), hexanal (grassy, fatty), 2-methoxyphenol (smoky), and 2-methylpyrazine (roasty, nutty), consistently showed high OAV (OAV >10), establishing them as the predominant key aroma compounds transferred from the sesame hulls. As shown in Fig. 2B, the sensory evaluation results further corroborated the chemical analyses. The sample extracted at 45 °C received the highest scores for caramel-like, roasty, nutty, and fatty notes, while exhibiting the lowest intensities of green and rancidity notes, presenting the most desirable overall flavor profile. As temperatures rose to 55 °C and 65 °C, significant enhancements in rancidity, burnt, and smoky flavors were observed in the samples, indicating that higher temperatures may accelerate the negatively impact on oil quality. Therefore, a temperature of 45 °C was identified as the optimal extraction condition, achieving effective migration of key aroma compounds while maximizing the flavor quality of the sesame oil. The flavored sesame oil obtained at 45 °C was designated as SOTemp.

Effects of different extraction time on flavor and sensory of sesame oils

Following the determination of the optimal hull-to-oil ratio (1:5) and extraction temperature (45 °C), the influence of extraction time (4–12 h) on the flavor composition and sensory quality of the flavored sesame oil was investigated. A total of 103 volatile compounds were identified across the samples at different extraction times, with their categories and total concentration distributions shown in Figs. 1E–F. The results indicate that furans remained the predominant group of flavor-active components in all samples, confirming their role as major contributors to the characteristic flavor of the oil. The total concentration of furan compounds exhibited a decreasing trend with prolonged extraction time, primarily due to the reduction in key derivatives such as 2-pentylfuran, 2-acetylfuran, and methyl 2-furoate. Concurrently, the concentration of aldehyde compounds also decreased as the extraction time increased. This may be attributed to further reactions occurring during prolonged heating, including oxidation, reduction, or condensation of aldehydes. Additionally, extended heating may accelerate the oxidation of sesame oil itself, adversely affecting the quality of oil.

A total of 14 migrated key aroma compounds (OAV > 1) derived from sesame hulls were detected in the flavored sesame oils obtained at different extraction times (Table 3). Among them, furfural, 2-methoxyphenol, hexanal, and 2-methylpyrazine consistently maintained high OAV (OAVs >10) in oil samples across all extraction times. In the samples extracted for 4–10 h, furfural, 2-methylpyrazine, and hexanal were consistently present at relatively high concentrations. When the extraction time was extended to 10–12 h, the concentrations of hexanal, nonanal, and benzeneacetaldehyde decreased significantly, indicating a marked intensification of lipid oxidation and secondary reactions. Overall, the total concentration of the migrated key aroma compounds showed no significant difference between 4 and 8 h, but decreased significantly at 10 and 12 h. This suggests that prolonging the extraction time did not lead to further accumulation of flavor compounds. Instead, it likely caused losses due to volatilization, degradation, or other chemical reaction, resulting in an overall reduction of aroma potential.

Sensory evaluation results (Fig. 2C) further supported the chemical analysis. The sample extracted for 8 h received the highest scores for roasty and nutty notes, and also performed well in caramel-like flavor, exhibiting the most balanced overall flavor profile. In contrast, the sample extracted for 12 h showed significantly enhanced intensities of undesirable attributes such as smoky, burnt, rancidity, fatty, and woody notes. This indicates that prolonged heating may accelerate oil deterioration and introduce off-flavors. Based on the comprehensive analysis of volatile compounds and sensory evaluation, an extraction time of 8 h was determined to be optimal for preparing the flavored sesame oil. The flavored sesame oil obtained at 8 h was designated as SOET.

Changes in quality of flavored sesame oil during accelerated storage

To investigate the effect of flavor compounds derived from roasted sesame hulls on the oxidative stability of sesame oil, three representative flavored sesame oils (SOOR, SOTemp and SOET) were subjected to accelerated storage at 63 ± 1 °C for 40 days. The quality evolution of these flavored oils during storage was evaluated by monitoring key quality indicators, including OIT, AV, POV, AnV, and color.

Oxidative induction time (OIT) of flavored sesame oil

The OIT is a key indicator for evaluating the oxidative stability and predicting the shelf-life of edible oils (Shim & Lee, 2011). At the beginning of the accelerated storage experiment (day 0), the OITs of the three representative flavored sesame oils (SOOR, SOTemp and SOET) and the unflavored control (SOBC) were determined. As shown in Fig. 4A, there is no significant difference in OIT between SOOR (11.11 ± 0.18 h) and SOBC (11.29 ± 0.25 h). Conversely, the OITs of both SOTemp (10.37 ± 0.08 h) and SOET (10.66 ± 0.01 h) were significantly shorter than those of SOBC and SOOR. These findings indicate that the flavor compounds migrating from roasted sesame hulls into the oil phase not only contribute to the characteristic aroma but also affect the oxidative stability of the oil.

Changes in acid value (AV), peroxide value (POV), and p-anisidine value (AnV) of flavored sesame oil during storage

Edible oils inevitably undergo deterioration in flavor and nutritional quality during production, transportation, and distribution as a result of chemical reactions such as oxidation, hydrolysis, and polymerization. Therefore, it is essential to continuously monitor changes in oil quality during storage. AV, POV, and AnV are commonly used indicators for evaluating lipid hydrolysis and oxidation, reflecting the accumulation of free fatty acids, primary oxidation products, and secondary oxidation products, respectively (Yousefi et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2025). The changes in the AV, POV, and AnV of flavored sesame oil during accelerated storage are presented in Figs. 4B–C.

It can be seen from Fig. 4B that the AV of all samples increased with prolonged storage time, indicating a continuous accumulation of free fatty acids. This increase can be attributed to the hydrolysis of triglycerides, and to some extent, to the migration of acidic substances generated from roasted sesame hulls during oil-phase extraction at elevated temperatures. For all samples, AV remained relatively stable during the first 10 days, increased rapidly between days 10 and 35, and then tended to stabilize from days 35 to 40. Although the AVs of all flavored sesame oil samples were consistently higher than that of the blank sesame oil (SOBC), their rate of increase over time was significantly lower. After 40 days of storage, the AV increments for SOOR, SOTemp, and SOET were 0.46, 0.49, and 0.47 mg/g, respectively, all of which were lower than that of SOBC (0.54 mg/g). Throughout the storage period, the AV of all samples remained below the 3.0 mg/g limit specified in the Chinese national standard for sesame oil (GB/T 8233–2018). These results suggest that while the processing of flavored sesame oil may introduce additional acidic components, the bioactive compounds (e.g., phenolic substances) transferred from roasted sesame hulls may partially inhibit lipid hydrolysis and subsequent oxidation reactions during storage.

Fig. 4C shows that POVs for all samples exhibited an overall upward trend during storage, reflecting a continuous increase in peroxide content within the oils. At day 0, there was no significant difference in POV between the flavored sesame oil and the blank control. During the early storage stage (0–10 days), the POVs of flavored sesame oils were slightly higher than or comparable to those of SOBC. However, after day 10, the POV of SOET consistently remained lower than that of SOBC, while the POVs of SOOR and SOTemp fell below that of the control after day 25. The ΔPOV over 40 days for SOOR, SOTemp, SOET, and SOBC were 2.26, 2.14, 2.43, and 2.82 mmol/kg, respectively. Considering that hydroperoxides are unstable and readily decompose into secondary oxidation products such as aldehydes and ketones, AnV was further employed to evaluate oxidative deterioration.

As shown in Fig. 4D, flavored sesame oils generally exhibited higher AnV values than the blank control, which can be attributed to the migration of aldehydes and ketones derived from roasted sesame hulls. However, the increases in AnV during storage (ΔAnV) were smaller for flavored sesame oils than for SOBC. Specifically, the ΔAnV over 40 days for SOOR, SOTemp, SOET, and SOBC were 7.05, 5.56, 5.18, and 6.63, respectively. In terms of AnV dynamics, the oxidative stability of flavored sesame oils followed the order SOET > SOTemp > SOOR. Overall, the sample (SOET) prepared under extraction conditions of a hull-to-oil ratio of 1:5, extraction temperature of 45 °C, and extraction time of 8 h exhibited the most favorable storage stability. These findings indicate that although prolonged thermal extraction may promote initial oxidation, antioxidant compounds originating from roasted sesame hulls exert sustained protective effects during storage, effectively suppressing the accumulation of both primary and secondary oxidation products.

Changes in color of flavored sesame oil during storage

Color is a key sensory attribute influencing consumer acceptance and also serves as an important indicator of oil quality changes during storage (Li et al., 2023). Monitoring color parameters during storage therefore provides insight into the transformation of oil pigments and quality stability of oils. During the oil-phase extraction of roasted sesame hulls, both intrinsic pigments and brown-colored compounds formed through Maillard and caramelization reactions migrated into the oil phase, resulting in a noticeable darkening of flavored sesame oil. As shown in Fig. 5, compared to the blank sesame oil, all flavored sesame oil samples exhibited reduced lightness (L* value), a negative shift in redness/greenness (a* value), and a significant increase in yellowness/blueness (b* value). This indicates that the migration component from sesame hulls shifted the overall oil color toward a darker yellowish-green hue. During accelerated storage, the L* value of flavored sesame oil gradually increased, while b* values showed a decreasing trend and a* values remained relatively unchanged. These results indicate a gradual lightening of oil color and a reduction in yellow intensity over time. This phenomenon is likely associated with the oxidative degradation of certain pigment components during storage (Li et al., 2023). Notably, despite the initial darkening induced by sesame hulls-derived pigments, no abnormal browning or abrupt color changes were observed in flavored sesame oils throughout the storage period. These findings suggest the pigments introduced from roasted sesame hulls exhibit relatively good stability under the applied storage conditions.

Changes in volatiles of flavored sesame oil during accelerated storage

To elucidate the dynamic changes in flavor composition of flavored sesame oil during accelerated storage, the evolution of heterocyclic compounds (nitrogen- and oxygen-containing heterocycles), phenolic compounds, and carbonyl compounds was investigated (Fig. 6). As shown in Fig. 6A and Table S1, except for nitrogen-containing heterocyclic and phenols compounds, the concentrations of most categories of volatile compounds in SOBC increased with storage time, with aldehydes exhibiting the most significant accumulation. In contrast, in the three flavored sesame oils (SOOR, SOTemp, and SOET), the concentrations of nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds gradually decreased during storage, whereas the concentrations of other volatile classes increased. However, the overall increase rates of these compounds were markedly lower than those observed in the blank control. These results suggest that the formation of volatile oxidation products in flavored sesame oil was partially suppressed during storage, likely due to the presence of antioxidant components transferred from roasted sesame hulls into the oil phase.

Changes in volatile compound concentrations of flavored sesame oils during accelerated storage: (A) SOBC (control); (B) SOOR (1:5, 25 °C, 4 h); (C) SOTemp (1:5, 45 °C, 4 h); (D) SOET (1:5, 45 °C, 8 h). Different letters above bars of the same color in the figure indicate significant differences (p < 0.05) in the same volatile type during storage.

Nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds in flavored sesame oil mainly consisted of pyrazines, pyrroles, and pyridines, which are typically generated by Strecker degradation in the Maillard reaction (Yu, Zhang, et al., 2021). These compounds are key contributors to the characteristic roasty and nutty flavors of flavored sesame oil. Throughout the entire storage period, nitrogen-containing heterocycles were not detected in the SOBC, further confirming that these compounds predominantly originated from roasted sesame hulls. In SOOR, SOTemp, and SOET (Figs. 6B–D and Tables S2–S4), a gradual decrease was observed in the total concentration of nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds (primarily 2-methylpyrazine) over extended storage, indicating a corresponding loss of roasty and nutty flavors in the flavored sesame oil.

Oxygen-containing heterocyclic compounds were mainly represented by furans, which contribute sweet and caramel-like aromas. Furans can be formed via various pathways, including the Maillard reaction, lipid oxidation, caramelization, and the degradation of precursors such as ascorbic acid and carotenoids (Märk et al., 2006; Meng et al., 2024). In the blank sesame oil (Tables S1), the concentration of oxygen-containing heterocycles increased significantly with storage time (0–1737.80 μg/kg), primarily due to the accumulation of 2-pentylfuran and 2-propylfuran. Among these, 2-pentylfuran is a typical product of lipid oxidation and is widely regarded as an indicator of oxidative rancidity in oils (Ghorbani Gorji et al., 2019; Krishnamurthy et al., 2006). In flavored sesame oils, oxygen-containing heterocycles also exhibited an increasing trend during storage, mainly driven by the accumulation of 2-pentylfuran. Comparing with samples stored for 0 day, the total concentrations of oxygen-containing heterocycles in SOOR, SOTemp, and SOET increased by 407.41, 3381.40, and 5562.95 μg/kg, respectively (Figs. 6B–D and Tables S2–S4), reflecting the influence of different preparation conditions on the oxidative progression of the oils.

Carbonyl compounds, including aldehydes, ketones, and acids, are the major products of lipid oxidation. During accelerated storage, the concentrations of carbonyl compounds in all samples continuously increased. Notably, hexanal, (E)-2-heptenal, octanal, (E)-2-octenal, nonanal, (2E)-2-nonenal, decanal, (E)-2-decenal, (E,E)-2,4-decadienal, acetone, and pentanoic acid exhibited pronounced changes. These compounds are mainly derived from the oxidative degradation of unsaturated fatty acids such as oleic acid and linoleic acid (Biswas & Mano, 2021). Their accumulation not only directly contributes to the development of undesirable rancid odors but is also closely associated with quality deterioration, as reflected by increased acid value and reduced oxidative stability. Notably, though the initial concentrations of aldehydes and ketones were higher in the flavored sesame oils than in the control, their cumulative levels over the 40-day storage period remained significantly lower in all flavored samples. The result further confirms that components migrating from roasted sesame hulls effectively delayed oxidative deterioration and suppressed the formation of off-flavor compounds during storage.

The observed phenomenon can be further explained by the changes in the levels of phenolic compounds. In SOBC, only 53 μg/kg of phenolic volatiles was detected at the initial storage stage. In contrast, SOOR, SOTemp, and SOET contained significantly higher levels of phenolic compounds at day 0 (598, 664, and 888 μg/kg, respectively), which were introduced during processing together with other volatiles. The major phenolic components in flavored sesame oils included 2-methoxyphenol, 2-methoxy-5-vinylphenol, 2-methoxy-4-vinylphenol, and maltol. These methoxyphenols are typically originate from the thermal degradation of lignin, exhibit smoky, spicy, and roasted notes and are recognized as the key aroma contributors to the smoky flavor of sesame oil (Zhao, Wang, et al., 2025). Furthermore, these phenolic compounds possess hydrogen-donating and free-radical-scavenging activities and have been shown to markedly enhance the oxidative stability of purified sesame oil (Zhao et al., 2026). By the end of storage (day 40), the levels of phenolic volatiles in SOOR, SOTemp, and SOET decreased to 277, 277, and 346 μg/kg, respectively. The consumption of these phenolic compounds during the inhibition of oxidation will lead to a corresponding dissipation of the associated flavor notes. Notably, SOET retained relatively higher levels of phenolics, which can account for its more superior oxidative stability.

Conclusion

This study elucidated the migration behavior of flavor components derived from roasted sesame hulls in refined sesame oil and their impact on the flavor quality and oxidative stability of resulting products. Based on the migrated key aroma compounds concentrations and sensory evaluation, the optimal process for preparing flavored sesame oil via oil-phase extraction was determined as follows: hull-to-oil ratio of 1:5, extraction temperature of 45 °C, and extraction time of 8 h. 15 key aroma compounds derived from roasted sesame hulls were detected in the flavored sesame oil. Among these compounds, furfural exhibited the highest OAV, primarily contributing caramel, sweet, and burnt flavors to the flavored sesame oil. It was followed by hexanal, 2-methoxyphenol, and 2-methylpyrazine, which were associated with fatty/grassy, smoky, roasty/nutty aromas, respectively. The introduction of key aroma compounds from roasted sesame hulls significantly enhanced the overall flavor profile of sesame oil. Compared to the control oil (11.29 ± 0.25 h), the optimized flavored sesame oil initially showed a marginally shorter OIT (10.66 ± 0.01 h), elevated AV and AnV, and a noticeably darker color. However, by the end of accelerated storage, the increments in AV, POV, and AnV were significantly lower in the flavored oil (0.47 mg/g, 2.43 mmol/kg, and 5.18, respectively) than in the control (0.54 mg/g, 2.82 mmol/kg, and 6.63, respectively), demonstrating that components migrating from roasted sesame hulls effectively retarded lipid oxidation. Furthermore, the pigment components introduced from roasted sesame hulls did not cause abnormal color deterioration during storage. The dynamic evolution of volatile compounds further confirmed that, compared to the control oil, flavored sesame oil accumulated lower levels of off-flavor compounds (such as 2-pentylfuran and aldehyde/ketone compounds) derived from lipid oxidation during storage. In summary, roasted sesame hulls effectively enhance the sensory properties of refined sesame oil as natural flavor precursors while regulating oil oxidative stability through their antioxidant active components. This study provides theoretical support and technical references for the high-value utilization of sesame processing byproducts and the development of flavored oils with distinctive flavors and excellent storage stability.
