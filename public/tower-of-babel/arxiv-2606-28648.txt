Failure of Strong Convergence of Matrices with Fermionic Entries
Dimitri Shlyakhtenko

Abstract
Let Q N ( k ) Q^{(k)}_{N} be an N × N N\times N matrices with entries satisfying CAR, normalized to have variance 1 / N 1/\sqrt{N} with respect to the trace of the CAR algebra. We show that, although the operator norm of the real part of an individual matrix Q N ( k ) Q^{(k)}_{N} converges as N → ∞ N\to\infty to the semicircular limit, the family of matrices does not converge to the free probability limit strongly. In fact, even the operator space structure of the linear spans of the real and imaginary parts of Q N ( k ) Q^{(k)}_{N} ’s, k = 1 , … , M k=1,\dots,M , does not converge to the semicircular limit.



1
Introduction.

In this paper, we consider the asymptotics of
N\times N
block matrices
A^{(k)}_{N}
,
1\leq k\leq M
with entries given by generators satisfing Canonical Anti-Commutation Relations (CAR) (see §
1.2
for the precise definitions and notations). These matrices are fermionic analogs of GUE matrices, in the sense that the Gaussian random entries of GUE matrices are replaced by “iid” fermionic variables. It has been known, since the work of Voiculescu, that, under a natural trace, such “CAR-UE” matrices converge in moments to a free semicircular family
S_{1},\dots,S_{M}
(see
[
15
]
). However, two questions were left open after Voiculescu’s work. The first is the behavior of the operator norm of a single matrix
A_{N}=A_{N}^{(1)}
. The second is the question of strong convergence: whether it is true that operator norms of arbitrary polynomials in the matrices
A_{N}^{(1)},A_{N}^{(2)},\dots
converge to the operator norm of the same polynomial but evaluated in the free semicircular family
S_{1},S_{2},\dots
. This question was communicated to the author by Gilles Pisier, and it came up, for example, in the work
[
9
]
where the matrices
A_{N}^{(k)}
were used as one of the ways to prove the QWEP property for
C^{*}
-algebras generated by generalized circular elements.

Our main result is that for all
N
,
\|A_{N}\|=\sqrt{4-3/N}<2
(in fact, we give a precise description of the spectrum of
A_{N}
, see Theorem
2
); moreover,
\|A_{N}\|\to 2
, which is the operator norm of the semicircular limit. However, we show that strong convergence fails if we consider simultaneously
12
or more such matrices. More precisely, if
A_{N}^{(1)},B_{N}^{(2)},\dots,A_{N}^{(M)},B_{N}^{(M)}
are “CAR-iid” copies of
A_{N}^{(1)}
, and we set
Q_{N}^{(j)}=A_{N}^{(j)}+iB_{N}^{(j)}
, then
Q_{N}^{(j)}
converge in
*
-moments to a family of freely independent circular elements
Q^{(1)},\dots,Q^{(M)}
, but

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{M}Q_{N}^{(j)}(Q_{N}^{(j)})^{*}\Big\|\gneq\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{M}Q^{(j)}(Q^{(j)})^{*}\Big\|.

(see Theorem
3
below). The same example shows that the operator space structure of the span of
A_{N}^{(1)},B_{N}^{(2)},\dots,A_{N}^{(M)},B_{N}^{(M)}
does not converge, as
N\to\infty
to the free probability limit. Thus the asymptotics of the “CAR-UE” matrices gives another example of a real Hilbertian operator space
[
10
]
, which is not isomorphic to the complex span of semicircular variables, and whose precise operator structure we cannot elucidate. Convergence to the semicircular limit does hold for real-linear combinations, but we are unable to prove the complex case (although we suspect that the statement is true). There is another obstruction to strong convergence, where a different degree-two polynomial involving commutators of the “CAR-UE” matrices is used. This computation is given in Theorem

AI use: How the results were obtained

This paper also represents an experiment in the use of AI for research in operator algebras. The counterexample to strong convergence was found by utilizing software written by ChatGPT 5.4 Pro, and ChatGPT was able to find an expression for the special vector

used to give a lower bound on the norm of sums of commutators in Lemma

. However, it did not realize that this gives a counterexample to strong convergence. ChatGPT, however, showed remarkable performance on the question of convergence of

(though when asked directly it hallucinated incorrect operator-space inequalities). It hallucinated the combinatorial identity (

) discussed in §

for the moments of the matrices

. When explained that this identity does exist in the paper

]
and asked to give a direct proof, it retraced the steps back to the matrix

, found the representation theory proof presented in this paper, and derived the identity as a consequence. ChatGPT 5.5 Pro was also helpful in the proof of the

A partial formalization of the results of this paper in Lean is available at

. The formalization covers §2, including the underlying representation-theory tools, as well as Lemma

, down to theorems and definitions in Mathlib v4.32.0-rc1 with no additional axioms or hypotheses. We intend to pursue formalization of the free probability norm estimates necessary to see the violation of norm convergence later on.

1.2
Setup and Notation

For fixed integers
M
and
N
, consider the real Hilbert space

H=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{M}\bigl(H_{k}^{(1)}\oplus H_{k}^{(2)}\bigr),

where for each

k=1,2,\dots,M
and
r=1,2
,
H_{k}^{(r)}
is a real Hilbert space of dimension
N^{2}
with basis
h_{pq}^{(r,k)}
,
1\leq p,q\leq N
. Let
\mathscr{F}
denote the associated antisymmetric Fock space (see e.g.
[
2
]
), and let
\Omega
be the vacuum vector. Denote by
\mathbb{B}
the space of (bounded) operators on the finite-dimensional space
\mathscr{F}
.

For
h\in H\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
, let
a(h)^{*}\in\mathbb{B}
be the creation operator given on elementary antisymmetric tensors by
a(h)^{*}(h_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge h_{k})=h\wedge h_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge h_{k}
,
and let
a(h)
be its adjoint. These operators satisfy the Canonical Anti-Commutation Relations: for
h,g\in H\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
,

a(h)a(g)+a(g)a(h)=0,\ a(h)a(g)^{*}+a(g)^{*}a(h)=\langle h,g\rangle.

For
h\in H
, let

b(h)=a(h)+a(h)^{*}

be the associated Majorana operator.

Let
e_{pq}\in M_{N\times N}
,
1\leq p,q\leq N
, be elementary
N\times N
matrices, and consider the operators

Q^{(k)}_{N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{p,q=1}^{N}e_{pq}\otimes\bigl(b(h^{(1,k)}_{pq})+i\,b(h^{(2,k)}_{pq})\bigr)\in M_{N\times N}\otimes\mathbb{B}.

Let

A^{(k)}_{N}=\frac{Q^{(k)}_{N}+(Q^{(k)}_{N})^{*}}{2},\qquad B^{(k)}_{N}=\frac{Q^{(k)}_{N}-(Q^{(k)}_{N})^{*}}{2i}

be the real and imaginary parts of
Q_{N}^{(k)}
. Denote by
\phi:\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{C}
the vector state
x\mapsto\langle\Omega,x\Omega\rangle
and by
\phi_{N}:M_{N\times N}\otimes\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{C}
the state
\phi_{N}=N^{-1}Tr\otimes\phi
.

By
[
15
]
, the family of matrices
(A_{N}^{(1)},B_{N}^{(1)},\dots,A_{N}^{(M)},B_{N}^{(M)})
converges in distribution to a free semicircular family.

2
Norm of real linear combinations.

In this section we show that operator norms of real linear combinations of words of length
1
in the matrices
A_{N}^{(r)}
and
B_{N}^{(r)}
do converge to operator norms of their free probability limit laws. We start with the case of a single matrix.

2.1
A Clifford algebra picture of the entries of
A_{N}
.

To simplify notation, let us set

A_{N}=A_{N}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum e_{pq}\otimes d_{pq},

where

\displaystyle d_{pq}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(b(h^{(1)}_{pq})+ib(h^{(2)}_{pq})+b(h^{(1)}_{qp})-ib(h^{(2)}_{qp}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigl(a(h^{(1)}_{pq})+a(h^{(1)}_{pq})^{*}+a(ih^{(2)}_{pq})+a(-ih^{(2)}_{pq})^{*}

\displaystyle+a(h^{(1)}_{qp})+a(h^{(1)}_{qp})^{*}+a(-ih^{(2)}_{qp})+a(ih^{(2)}_{qp})^{*}\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\bigl(\frac{1}{2}(h^{(1)}_{pq}+ih^{(2)}_{pq}+h^{(1)}_{qp}-ih^{(2)}_{qp})\bigr)

\displaystyle+a\bigl(\frac{1}{2}(h^{(1)}_{pq}-ih^{(2)}_{pq}+h^{(1)}_{qp}+ih^{(2)}_{qp})\bigr)^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a(g_{pq})+a(g_{qp})^{*}

where
g_{pq}=\frac{1}{2}(h^{(1)}_{pq}+ih^{(2)}_{pq}+h^{(1)}_{qp}-ih^{(2)}_{qp})
are orthonormal vectors.

Note that
d_{pq}=d_{qp}^{*}
; using CAR one easily sees that
d_{pq}
satisfy the Clifford relations:

d_{pq}d_{q^{\prime}p^{\prime}}+d_{q^{\prime}p^{\prime}}d_{pq}=2\delta_{pp^{\prime}}\delta_{qq^{\prime}}.

Thus the algebra generated by the entries
d_{pq}
is isomorphic to the Clifford algebra
\operatorname{Cl}(W,B)
, where
W=M_{N\times N}(\mathbb{C})
and
B
is the bilinear form
B(x,y)=Tr(xy)
.

If we denote by
S
the underlying complex vector space of
\operatorname{Cl}(W,B)
, then
\dim S=2^{N^{2}}
. The state
\phi
then corresponds to the normalized trace on
\textrm{End}(S)
restricted to
\operatorname{Cl}(W,B)
acting by left multiplication.

2.2
The
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
module structure of the Clifford algebra

The left multiplication action of
\operatorname{Cl}(W,B)
gives rise to a natural
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
action on
S
which will be useful to understand the action of the matrix
A_{N}
. We introduce this action below. The main result of this section is Proposition
1
, which identifies
S
as a multiple of a single irreducible highest weight representation. We could not find this exact result in the literature; for similar results, see for example
[
8
, Proposition 2.4(ii), Example 2.5(1)]
and
[
7
, pp. 356-358]
. We thus give a direct argument.

For
1\leq a,b\leq N
, let

F_{ab}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}d_{ak}d_{kb}.

Lemma 1
.

For all
a,b,p,q
,

[F_{ab},d_{pq}]=\delta_{bp}d_{aq}-\delta_{aq}d_{pb}.

Proof.

Using the Clifford relations, we have:

\displaystyle[F_{ab},d_{pq}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}[d_{ak}d_{kb},d_{pq}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}d_{ak}d_{kb}d_{pq}-d_{pq}d_{ak}d_{kb}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}2\delta_{pb}\delta_{qk}d_{ak}-d_{ak}d_{pq}d_{kb}-2\delta_{pk}\delta_{qa}d_{kb}+d_{ak}d_{pq}d_{kb}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{bp}d_{aq}-\delta_{aq}d_{pb},

as claimed.
∎

Lemma 2
.

The operators
F_{ab}
satisfy:

[F_{ab},F_{cd}]=\delta_{bc}F_{ad}-\delta_{ad}F_{cb}.

Thus if
E_{ij}
are the generators of
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
corresponding to elementary matrices with a single nonzero entry in the
i,j
-th coordinate, then the map
\pi_{S}:E_{ab}\mapsto F_{ab}
(with
F_{ab}
acting on
S
by left multiplication) is a Lie algebra representation of
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
on
S
.

Proof.

We compute, using the previous Lemma and the fact that
[F_{ab},\cdot]
is a derivation:

\displaystyle[F_{ab},F_{cd}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}[F_{ab},d_{ck}d_{kd}]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}[F_{ab},d_{ck}]d_{kd}+d_{ck}[F_{ab},d_{kd}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\delta_{bc}d_{ak}d_{kd}-\delta_{ak}d_{cb}d_{kd}+d_{ck}\delta_{bk}d_{ad}-d_{ck}\delta_{ad}d_{kb}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{bc}F_{ad}-\frac{1}{2}d_{cb}d_{ad}+\frac{1}{2}d_{cb}d_{ad}-\delta_{ad}F_{cb}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{bc}F_{ad}-\delta_{ad}F_{cb},

as claimed. These are exactly the relations between the generators
E_{ab}
of
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
and thus the map
E_{ab}\mapsto F_{ab}
is a Lie algebra homomorphism.
∎

2.3
The highest weight vectors
h_{I,\eta}

Given a subset
I\subset\{1,\dots,N\}
, define

d_{I}=\prod_{p\in I}d_{pp}

(ordered product taken in increasing order).

Fix once and for all an order on the set
\{(p,q):p<q\}
. Given a function
\eta:\{(p,q):p<q\}\to\{0,1\}
, let

y^{\eta}_{pq}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}d_{pq}d_{qp},&if $\eta(p,q)=0$\\
d_{pq},&otherwise.\end{cases}

Finally, let

h_{I,\eta}=d_{I}\prod_{p<q}y_{pq}^{\eta}

(product taken in the fixed order on the index set), and consider the submodule
S_{I,\eta}
of
S
generated by
h_{I,\eta}
.

For each
I
and
\eta
, define

r_{I,\eta}=d_{I}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j\\
\eta(i,j)=1\end{subarray}}s_{ij},\qquad\text{where }s_{ab}=d_{ab}+d_{ba}.

Lemma 3
.

Let
h_{0}=h_{\emptyset,0}=\prod_{p<q}\frac{1}{2}d_{pq}d_{qp}
.
Then:

(i)

r_{I,\eta}
is invertible

(ii)

h_{0}\,r_{I,\eta}=h_{I,\eta}.

Proof.

If
a<b
, then
s_{ab}^{2}=d_{ab}d_{ab}+d_{ab}d_{ba}+d_{ba}d_{ab}+d_{ba}d_{ba}=2
so that
s_{ab}
is invertible and
s_{ab}^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}s_{ab}
. Furthermore,
d_{pp}d_{pp}=1
, so
d_{pp}
is also invertible. Thus
r_{I,\eta}
is a product of invertible elements. This gives (i).

For (ii), we note that if
p<q
,
a<b
, and
(p,q)\neq(a,b)
, then the even element

d_{pq}d_{qp}
commutes with
s_{ab}=d_{ab}+d_{ba}
, and thus

\bigl(\frac{1}{2}d_{pq}d_{qp}\bigr)s_{ab}=s_{ab}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}d_{pq}d_{qp}\bigr).

If on the other hand
(p,q)=(a,b)
, then

\displaystyle\bigl(\frac{1}{2}d_{pq}d_{qp}\bigr)s_{pq}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(d_{pq}d_{qp}d_{pq}+d_{pq}d_{qp}d_{qp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}d_{pq}(2-d_{pq}d_{qp})+\frac{1}{2}d_{pq}d_{qp}d_{qp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{pq}-\frac{1}{2}d_{pq}d_{pq}d_{qp}+\frac{1}{2}d_{pq}d_{qp}d_{qp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{pq}.

Similarly, if
p<q
and
a
is arbitrary, then

\bigl(\frac{1}{2}d_{pq}d_{qp})d_{aa}=d_{aa}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}d_{pq}d_{qp})

because
(p,q)\neq(a,a)
,
(q,p)\neq(a,a)
, and thus the even element
d_{pq}d_{qp}
commutes with
d_{aa}
. Thus

\displaystyle h_{0}r_{I,\eta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{p<q}\frac{1}{2}d_{pq}d_{qp}\cdot\prod_{a\in I}d_{aa}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j\\
\eta(i,j)=1\end{subarray}}s_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{a\in I}d_{aa}\cdot\prod_{p<q}\frac{1}{2}d_{pq}d_{qp}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j\\
\eta(i,j)=1\end{subarray}}s_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{a\in I}d_{aa}\prod_{p<q}y_{pq}^{\eta}=h_{I,\eta}.

∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
S_{I,\eta}
be the
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
-submodule of
S
generated by
h_{I,\eta}
. Then
S_{I,\eta}
is isomorphic as a
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
module to the irreducible module
L_{\nu}
with highest weight
\nu=(N-1/2,N-3/2,\dots,3/2,1/2)
and dimension
2^{N(N-1)/2}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3
, it’s sufficient to prove that
S_{\emptyset,0}
, the submodule generated by
h_{0}
, is
L_{\nu}
. Indeed, the invertible map

x\mapsto xr_{I,\eta}

clearly commutes with the left multiplication action of
\operatorname{Cl}(W,B)
on itself, and thus with the action of
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
; moreover, it carries
h_{0}
into
h_{I,\eta}
.

The action of
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
on
S
is semisimple, and thus
S
decomposes into a direct sum of irreducible modules.

We now check that
h_{0}=\prod_{p<q}\frac{1}{2}d_{pq}d_{qp}
is a highest weight vector (see e.g.
[
6
, §3.2.1]
). Note that if
(p,q)\neq(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})
,
p<q
,
p^{\prime}<q^{\prime}
, then
d_{pq}d_{qp}
and
d_{p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}d_{q^{\prime}p^{\prime}}
commute. Thus we can reorder the terms in the product defining
h_{0}
at will.

We first check that the action of the raising operators
F_{ab}
for
a<b
is trivial.

Assume that
a<k
and write
h_{0}=d_{ak}d_{ka}h^{\prime}
, where
h^{\prime}
is (one half times) the product of
d_{pq}d_{qp}
for
p<q
and
(p,q)\neq(a,k)
.

Then

\displaystyle d_{ak}d_{kb}h_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{ak}d_{kb}d_{ak}d_{ka}h^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-d_{ak}d_{ak}d_{kb}d_{ka}h^{\prime}=0,

since
d_{ak}^{2}=0
.

Assume now that
a\geq k
, so
k<b
. Write
h_{0}=d_{kb}d_{bk}h^{\prime\prime}
. Then

d_{ak}d_{kb}h_{0}=d_{ak}d_{kb}d_{kb}d_{bk}h^{\prime\prime}=0,

since
d_{kb}d_{kb}=0
.

It follows that for any
a<b
,

F_{ab}h_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}d_{ak}d_{kb}h_{0}=0.

Next we check that
h_{0}
is an eigenvector for the Cartan subalgebra. Suppose that
a<k
.
Write
h_{0}=d_{ak}d_{ka}h^{\prime}
.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}d_{ak}d_{ka}h_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}d_{ak}d_{ka}d_{ak}d_{ka}h^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}d_{ak}(2-d_{ak}d_{ka})d_{ka}h^{\prime}=h_{0}.

Suppose that
a=k
. Then

\frac{1}{2}d_{aa}d_{aa}h_{0}=\frac{1}{2}h_{0}.

Finally, suppose that
a>k
. Write

h_{0}=d_{ka}d_{ak}h^{\prime\prime}
. Then

\frac{1}{2}d_{ak}d_{ka}h_{0}=\frac{1}{2}d_{ak}d_{ka}d_{ka}d_{ak}h^{\prime\prime}=0.

Thus

\displaystyle\pi_{S}(E_{aa})h_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k:1\leq k\leq N}d_{ak}d_{ka}h_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k:a<k\leq N}\frac{1}{2}d_{ak}d_{ka}h_{0}+\sum_{k:k=a}\frac{1}{2}d_{kk}d_{kk}h_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k:a>k\geq 1}d_{ak}d_{ka}h_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k:a<k}h_{0}\right)+\frac{1}{2}h_{0}=(N-a+\frac{1}{2})h_{0}.

Thus if we fix
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}
and consider the Cartan subalgebra element

H^{\lambda}=\sum_{a=1}^{N}\lambda_{a}E_{aa}

then

\pi_{S}(H^{\lambda})h_{0}=\sum_{a=1}^{N}\left[\bigl(N-a+\frac{1}{2}\bigr)\lambda_{a}\right]h_{0}.

It follows that
h_{0}
is indeed the highest weight vector with weight
\nu
.
Since the
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
action is semisimple, the cyclic submodule generated by a highest-weight vector of weight
\nu
is a direct sum of copies of
L_{\nu}
; but because it is generated by a single highest-weight vector, it must be a single copy and thus is isomorphic to
L_{\nu}
.

By the Weyl dimension formula (see e.g.,
[
6
, §7.1, Theorem 7.1.9]
),

\dim L_{\nu}=\prod_{1\leq p<q\leq N}\frac{N-p+\frac{1}{2}-N+q-\frac{1}{2}+q-p}{q-p}=2^{N(N-1)/2}.

∎

Proposition 1
.

Let
S_{I,\eta}
be the
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
-submodule of
S
generated by
h_{I,\eta}
. Then
S=\bigoplus_{I,\eta}S_{I,\eta}
as
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
-modules.

Thus
S
is isomorphic to a multiple of a highest weight representation:

S\cong L^{\oplus 2^{N(N+1)/2}}_{\nu},\qquad\nu=(N-1/2,N-3/2,\dots,3/2,1/2).

Proof.

Fix an ordering on the set of pairs
(p,q)
so that
(p,q)<(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})
if
p<q
and
p^{\prime}<q^{\prime}
, and so that
(p^{\prime},p^{\prime})<(p,q)
for any
p^{\prime}
and any
p<q
. Then the set of ordered products
d_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots d_{\alpha_{k}}
where
\alpha_{j}<\alpha_{j+1}
for all
j
as
k
ranges from
0
to
N^{2}
form a linear basis for
\operatorname{Cl}(W,B)=S
. It is not hard to see that in this case each
h_{I,\eta}
is, up to a constant multiple and sign, one of such monomials. Thus the highest weight vectors
(h_{I,\eta}:I\subset\{1,\dots,N\},\eta:\{(p,q):p<q\}\to\{0,1\})
are linearly independent. The dimension of their span is the number of such vectors, which is
2^{N}\cdot 2^{|\{(p,q):1\leq p<q\leq N|\}|}=2^{N}2^{N(N-1)/2}=2^{N(N+1)/2}
.

The natural map
\bigoplus_{I,\eta}S_{I,\eta}\to S
is injective. Indeed, if its kernel were nonzero, semisimplicity would imply that the kernel contains a highest weight vector. Such a vector would give a nontrivial linear relation among the highest weight vectors
h_{I,\eta}
, which is a contradiction.

Finally,

\displaystyle\dim\bigoplus_{I,\eta}S_{I,\eta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{N}\cdot 2^{|\{(p,q):1\leq p<q\leq N\}|}\cdot\dim S_{I,\eta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{N}2^{N(N-1)/2}2^{N(N-1)/2}=2^{N+N(N-1)}=2^{N^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dim S.

It follows that
S
must equal the direct sum of
S_{I,\eta}
.
∎

2.4
The spectrum of
A_{N}

Let
V=\mathbb{C}^{N}
, and let
V^{*}

Lemma 5

As an element of

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\sum_{a,b=1}^{N}\pi_{V^{*}}(E_{ab})\otimes\pi_{S}(E_{ba})

2.5
The spectral measure of
A_{N}

While we do not need this for the rest of the paper, we give an exact expression for the spectral measure of
A_{N}^{2}
with respect to the state
\phi_{N}
. Indeed the spectral measure is the weighted sum of delta functions

\mu_{A_{N}^{2}}=\sum_{r=0}^{N-1}w_{r}\delta_{(4r+1)/N}.

The weight
w_{r}
is the multiplicity of the
r
-th eigenvalue normalized by the dimension of the entire space.
Since the
r
-th eigenvalue arises from
L_{\nu-\epsilon_{N-r}}
viewed as an irreducible component of

, we see that

w_{r}=\frac{2^{N(N+1)/2}\dim L_{\nu-\epsilon_{N-r}}}{2^{N(N+1)/2}\dim V^{*}\otimes L_{\nu}}=\frac{1}{N}\frac{\dim L_{\nu-\epsilon_{N-r}}}{\dim L_{\nu}}.

Using Weyl’s dimension formula we end up with the expression

w_{r}=\frac{1}{4^{N-1}}G(r,N-1-r)

with

G(r,s)=\frac{2s+1}{r+s+1}\binom{2r}{r}\binom{2s}{s}.

In particular, this leads to the moment formula

\phi_{N}(A^{2p}_{N})=\frac{1}{4^{N-1}}\frac{1}{N^{p}}\sum_{r=0}^{N-1}G(r,N-1-r)(4r+1)^{p}.

as signed sums over pair-partitions (see
[

). This leads to the following combinatorial identity, valid for

(1)

Here each
\pi
is a pair-partition,
\operatorname{cr}(\pi)
is the number of crossings of
\pi

∎

In this way we again obtain a version of
3
:

Theorem 4
.

Let
M\geq 9
. Then the family
(A_{N}^{(1)},B_{N}^{(1)},\dots,A_{N}^{(M)},B_{N}^{(M)})
converges to the free semicircular family
(A^{(1)},B^{(1)},\dots,A^{(M)},B^{(M)})
in law but not strongly. In fact,

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{M}i[A^{(k)}_{N},B^{(k)}_{N}]\right\|\geq 2M>8+2\sqrt{2M}\geq\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{M}i[A^{(k)},B^{(k)}]\right\|.

Proof.

The stated inequalities are direct consequences of Lemma
11
, the fact that
2M>8+2\sqrt{2M}
for
M\geq 9
, and Lemma
12
. Convergence in law holds because of
[
11
]
. Strong convergence would imply that for any non-commutative polynomial
P
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left\|P(A_{N}^{(1)},B_{N}^{(1)},\dots,A_{N}^{(M)},B_{N}^{(M)})\right\|=\left\|P(A^{(1)},B^{(1)},\dots,A^{(M)},B^{(M)})\right\|,

which fails with

P(a_{1},b_{1},\dots,a_{M},b_{M})=\sum_{k=1}^{M}i[a_{k},b_{k}]
.
∎

3.4
Further remarks

It may be tempting to ask whether it is possible to “remove” the extraneous eigenvectors of the commutators
C^{(k)}_{N}
and deduce convergence on a suitable subspace (by analogy with strong convergence of permutation matrices, where one needs to restrict to the orthocomplement of the all-ones vector). To this end, we note that there are further counterexamples that can be constructed, in the following way.

Fix
\epsilon:\{1,\dots,M\}\to\{\pm 1\}
, and let

\Omega_{J,M,\epsilon}=2^{-MN^{2}/2}\prod_{k=1}^{M}\prod_{p,q=1}^{N}\left(1-i\epsilon(k)\,a(h^{(1,k)}_{pq})^{*}a(h^{(2,k)}_{pq})^{*}\right)\Omega,

(in this way our previous construction corresponds to
\epsilon
being identically equal to
1
). Furthermore, fix any unitary
u
in the commutant of the
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(d^{(k)}_{pq}):1\leq k\leq M,1\leq p,q\leq N)
viewed as an algebra of operators on
\mathscr{F}
, and let
w\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
be an arbitrary unit vector. Set

\eta=w\otimes(u\Omega_{J,M,\epsilon}).

Then a similar computation shows that

C_{N}^{(k)}\eta=-2\epsilon(k)\eta
so that

\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{M}\epsilon(k)C_{N}^{(k)}\right\|\geq 2M,

while the norm of the operator
\sum\epsilon(k)C^{(k)}
does not depend on
\epsilon
. In other words, strong convergence cannot happen after “removal” of just a few “bad vectors”.

4
The

-Gaussian case,
-1<q<1
: Strong Convergence.

Bożejko and Speicher constructed a one-parameter family of

]
.
In this section, we consider the
q

We briefly recall the construction to set the notation. Let
H_{\mathbb{C}}
be a complex Hilbert space, and consider the algebraic tensor product

\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\operatorname{alg}}=\mathbb{C}\Omega\oplus\bigoplus_{k\geq 1}(H_{\mathbb{C}})^{\dot{\otimes}k}

where we use
\dot{\otimes}
to denote the algebraic tensor product. For a fixed number
-1\leq q\leq 1
, define a scalar product on
\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\operatorname{alg}}
by

\langle\xi_{1}\dot{\otimes}\cdots\dot{\otimes}\xi_{k},\zeta_{1}\dot{\otimes}\cdots\dot{\otimes}\zeta_{m}\rangle=\delta_{km}\sum_{\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{k}}(q)^{\operatorname{inv}(\pi)}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\langle\xi_{j},\zeta_{\pi(j)}\rangle,

where
\mathbb{S}_{k}
denotes the group of permutations of the set
\{1,\dots,k\}
, and

\operatorname{inv}(\pi)=\bigl|\{(i,j):1\leq i<j\leq k\textrm{ but }\pi(i)>\pi(j)\}\bigr|

denotes the number of inversions of a permutation
\pi
.

By
[
1
, Proposition 1]
, this sesquilinear scalar product is positive semi-definite for all values of
q\in[-1,1]
, and is positive definite the moment
|q|<1
. (In the extreme cases
q=\pm 1
one can quotient
\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\operatorname{alg}}
by the kernel of the scalar product; in that case one obtains precisely the symmetric (
q=1
) or anti-symmetric (
q=-1
) Fock spaces).

We will denote by
\mathscr{F}_{q}
the completion of
\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\operatorname{alg}}
(modulo the kernel of the scalar product in cases

) with respect to the norm arising from this scalar product.

For
h\in H_{\mathbb{C}}
denote by
\ell(h)
the
q
-creation operator given on
\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\operatorname{alg}}
by linearity and

\ell(h)\xi_{1}\dot{\otimes}\cdots\dot{\otimes}\xi_{k}=h\dot{\otimes}\xi_{1}\dot{\otimes}\cdots\dot{\otimes}\xi_{k}.

The operator
\ell(h)
turns out to be bounded for all
-1\leq q<1
and thus extends to all of
\mathscr{F}_{q}
; we abuse notation and denote it by the same symbol. Its adjoint is then given by the formula

\ell(h)^{*}\xi_{1}\dot{\otimes}\cdots\dot{\otimes}\xi_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}q^{j-1}\langle h,\xi_{j}\rangle\xi_{1}\dot{\otimes}\cdots\dot{\otimes}\xi_{j-1}\dot{\otimes}\widehat{\xi_{j}}\dot{\otimes}\xi_{j+1}\dot{\otimes}\cdots\dot{\otimes}\xi_{k},

where
\widehat{\cdot}
denotes omission of a term. These operators satisfy the following
q
-commutation relations:

\ell(h)^{*}\ell(g)=\langle h,g\rangle 1+q\ell(g)\ell(h)^{*},\qquad h,g\in H_{\mathbb{C}}.

(7)

4.1
The matrices
A_{N}^{(k,q)}
,
Q_{N}^{(k,q)}
and

.

Let

where

are matrix units for the algebra of
N\times N

Note that

\displaystyle b_{q}(h_{rs}^{(1,k)})+ib_{q}(h_{rs}^{(2,k)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell(h_{rs}^{(1,k)}+ih_{rs}^{(2,k)})+\ell(h_{rs}^{(1,k)}-ih_{rs}^{(2,k)})^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}(\ell(g_{rs}^{(1,k)})+\ell(g_{rs}^{(2,k)})^{*})

where

(We omit the superscript

Q_{N}^{(k)}=Q_{N}^{(k,-1)},\qquad A_{N}^{(k)}=A_{N}^{(k,-1)},\qquad B_{N}^{(k)}=B_{N}^{(k,-1)}.

4.2
Technical estimates on the operator
\mathscr{L}
.

We begin with a technical lemma. We fix
-1<q<1
.
Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{s}
be an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{C}^{s}
, and let
g_{1},\dots,g_{s}
be an orthonormal family of vectors in
H_{\mathbb{C}}
. For
R=0,1,2,\dots
, denote by

\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\leq R}
the subspace of
\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\operatorname{alg}}
consisting of tensors of rank
R
or less (by convention
\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\leq 0}=\mathbb{C}\Omega
), and consider the map

\mathscr{L}_{R}:\mathbb{C}^{s}\otimes\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\leq R}\to\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\leq R+1}

given by

\mathscr{L}_{R}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{s}e_{j}\otimes\xi_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell(g_{j})\xi_{j}.

Lemma 13
.

Let
c_{R}=(1+|q|+\cdots+|q|^{R})^{1/2}
. Then, with the above notation,

\mathscr{L}_{R}^{*}(\xi)=\sum_{u}e_{u}\otimes\ell(g_{u})^{*}\xi,\qquad\forall\xi\in\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\leq R+1}

(8)

and

\|\mathscr{L}_{R}\|\leq c_{R}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|q|}}.

In particular, there exists a bounded operator
\mathscr{L}:\mathbb{C}^{s}\otimes\mathscr{F}_{q}\to\mathscr{F}_{q}
whose restriction to
\mathbb{C}^{s}\otimes\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\leq R}
is
\mathscr{L}_{R}
for each
R
. Moreover,

\mathscr{L}^{*}(\xi)=\sum_{u}e_{u}\otimes\ell(g_{u})^{*}\xi,\qquad\forall\xi\in\mathscr{F}_{q}

(9)

and
\|\mathscr{L}\|\leq(1-|q|)^{-1/2}
.

Proof.

If
\sum_{t}e_{t}\otimes\zeta_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{s}\otimes\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\leq R}
, then for any
\xi\in\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\leq R+1}

\displaystyle\langle\sum_{u}e_{u}\otimes\ell^{*}(g_{u})\xi,\sum_{t}e_{t}\otimes\zeta_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u}\langle\xi,\ell(g_{u})\zeta_{u}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\xi,\mathscr{L}_{R}(\sum_{u}e_{u}\otimes\zeta_{u})\rangle.

Since
\ell^{*}(g_{u})\xi\in\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\leq R}
and
\sum e_{t}\otimes\zeta_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{s}\otimes\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\leq R}
was arbitrary, this implies that

\mathscr{L}_{R}^{*}(\xi)=\sum_{u}e_{u}\otimes\ell^{*}(g_{u})\xi

which is precisely (
8
).

We will prove that
\|\mathscr{L}_{R}\|\leq c_{R}
by induction on
R
.

If
R=0
and
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{s}\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

\mathscr{L}_{0}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{s}e_{r}\otimes\lambda_{r}\Omega\right)=\sum_{r=1}^{s}\lambda_{r}g_{r}

which, by orthonormality of
g_{1},\dots,g_{s}
, has the same norm as
\sum_{r=1}^{s}e_{r}\otimes\lambda_{r}\Omega
. Thus
\|\mathscr{L}_{0}\|=1=c_{0}
.

Assume now that the norm inequality is true for
R
, i.e.,
\|\mathscr{L}_{R}\|\leq c_{R}
, regardless of the value of
s
and the choice of orthonormal basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{s}
and orthonormal family
g_{1},\dots,g_{s}
.

Let
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{s}\in\mathscr{F}_{q}^{\leq R+1}
.
Using the
q
-commutation relations (
7
), we compute:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathscr{L}_{R+1}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{s}e_{r}\otimes\xi_{r}\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v=1}^{s}\langle\ell(g_{u})\xi_{u},\ell(g_{v})\xi_{v}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v=1}^{s}\langle\xi_{u},\ell^{*}(g_{u})\ell(g_{v})\xi_{v}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u=1}^{s}\langle\xi_{u},\xi_{u}\rangle+q\sum_{u,v=1}^{s}\langle\ell(g_{v})^{*}\xi_{u},\ell(g_{u})^{*}\xi_{v}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\sum e_{r}\otimes\xi_{r}\|^{2}+qB

where we put
B=\sum_{u,v=1}^{s}\langle\ell(g_{v})^{*}\xi_{u},\ell(g_{u})^{*}\xi_{v}\rangle
.

Next,

\displaystyle|B|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\sum_{u,v}e_{u}\otimes e_{v}\otimes\ell(g_{u})^{*}\xi_{v},\sum_{u,v}e_{v}\otimes e_{u}\otimes\ell(g_{u})^{*}\xi_{v}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle(U\otimes I)(\sum_{u,v}e_{v}\otimes e_{u}\otimes\ell(g_{u})^{*}\xi_{v}),\sum_{u,v}e_{v}\otimes e_{u}\otimes\ell(g_{u})^{*}\xi_{v}\right\rangle\right|

where we put
U:\mathbb{C}^{s}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{s}\to\mathbb{C}^{s}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{s}
the isometric map

U(e_{u}\otimes e_{v})=e_{v}\otimes e_{u}

. Since
\|U\|=1
,

\displaystyle|B|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\sum_{u,v}e_{u}\otimes e_{v}\otimes\ell(g_{u})^{*}\xi_{v},\sum_{u,v}e_{u}\otimes e_{v}\otimes\ell(g_{u})^{*}\xi_{v}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v}\Bigl\|\sum_{u}e_{u}\otimes\ell(g_{u})^{*}\xi_{v}\Bigr\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v}\Bigl\|\mathscr{L}_{R}^{*}(\xi_{v})\Bigr\|^{2},

where in the last step we applied (
8
).

Thus

\displaystyle|B|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{v}\|\mathscr{L}_{R}\|^{2}\|\xi_{v}\|^{2}\leq c_{R}^{2}\Bigl\|\sum_{v}e_{v}\otimes\xi_{v}\Bigr\|^{2}.

Putting everything together gives us:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathscr{L}_{R+1}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{s}e_{r}\otimes\xi_{r}\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\sum e_{r}\otimes\xi_{r}\Bigr\|^{2}+|q||B|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\sum e_{r}\otimes\xi_{r}\Bigr\|^{2}+|q|c_{R}^{2}\Bigl\|\sum_{v}e_{v}\otimes\xi_{v}\Bigr\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+|q|c_{R}^{2})\Bigl\|\sum_{v}e_{v}\otimes\xi_{v}\Bigr\|^{2}.

Since
c_{R}^{2}=1+\cdots+|q|^{R}
,
1+|q|c_{R}^{2}=1+\cdots+|q|^{R+1}=c_{R+1}^{2}
and we conclude that

\|\mathscr{L}_{R+1}\|\leq c_{R+1},

completing the inductive step.

∎

q=-1
.

Consider the anti-symmetric Fock space associated to a finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{s}
with orthonormal basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{s}
. Then for any
1\leq r\leq s
there is an automorphism
\alpha
of the CAR algebra that takes
a(e_{k})
to
a(e_{k})
if
1\leq k\leq r
, and
a(e_{k})
to
a(e_{k})^{*}
if
r<k\leq s
, called the “particle-hole duality”. To see this, let
\sigma_{j}=a(e_{j})+a(e_{j})^{*}
; then CAR implies that
\sigma_{j}
is a self-adjoint unitary. Let
U=\sigma_{r+1}\cdots\sigma_{s}
. It turns out that
Ua(e_{k})U^{*}=a((-1)^{s-r}e_{k})
if
1\leq k\leq r
and
Ua(e_{k})U^{*}=a((-1)^{s-r+1}e_{k})^{*}
if
r<k\leq s
. Thus composing this automorphism with an appropriate Bogoljubov automorphism that changes the signs of
e_{k}
’s gives the desired map.

In particular, this means that there exists an automorphism that changes
\ell(g_{ij}^{(1,k)})
to
\ell(g_{ij}^{(1,k)})
and
\ell(g_{ij}^{(2,k)})^{*}
to
\ell(g_{ij}^{(2,k)})
(note that in our notation
\ell(\cdot)=a(\cdot)^{*}
when
q=-1
). Such an automorphism transforms
Q_{N}^{(k,-1)}=\sqrt{2}(L_{N}^{(1,k)}+(L_{N}^{(2,k)})^{*})
to the sum
\sqrt{2}(L_{N}^{(1,k)}+L_{N}^{(2,k)})=N^{-1/2}\sqrt{2}\sum_{uv}e_{uv}\otimes\ell(g_{uv}^{(1,k)}+g_{uv}^{(2,k)})={2}N^{-1/2}\sum_{uv}e_{uv}\otimes\ell(\hat{g}_{uv}^{(k)})
where
\hat{g}_{uv}^{(k)}
is some other orthonormal family. It follows that

\|Q_{N}^{(k,-1)}\|={2}\|L_{N}^{(1,k)}\|.

On the other hand,
Q_{N}^{(k)}
converges in
*
-distribution to a circular variable defined as
A^{(k)}+iB^{(k)}
where
A^{(k)}
and
B^{(k)}
are semicircular variables of norm
2
, and so
\liminf_{N}\|Q_{N}^{(k,-1)}\|\geq 2\sqrt{2}
, and thus
\liminf_{N}\|L_{N}^{(1,k)}\|\geq\sqrt{2}.
By
[
11
]
,
L_{N}^{(1,k)}
converges to an extended Cuntz isometry in
*
-moments, but that isometry has operator norm
1\neq\sqrt{2}
. Thus this
*
-convergence cannot be upgraded to strong convergence.
