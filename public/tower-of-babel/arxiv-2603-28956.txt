Minimum Norm Interpolation via The Local Theory of Banach Spaces: The Role of 2 2 -Uniform Convexity
Gil Kur, Pierre Bizeul

Abstract
The minimum-norm interpolator (MNI) framework has recently attracted considerable attention as a tool for understanding generalization in overparameterized models, such as neural networks. In this work, we study the MNI under a 2 2 -uniform convexity assumption, which is weaker than requiring the norm to be induced by an inner product, and it typically does not admit a closed-form solution. At a high level, we show that this condition yields an upper bound on the MNI bias in both linear and nonlinear models. We further show that this bound is sharp for overparameterized linear regression when the unit ball of the norm is in isotropic or, John’s position, and the covariates are isotropic, symmetric, i.i.d. sub-Gaussian, such as vectors with i.i.d. Bernoulli entries. Finally, under the same assumption on the covariates, we prove sharp generalization bounds for the ℓ p \ell_{p} -MNI when p ∈ ( 1 + C / log d , 2 ] p\in\bigl(1+C/\log d,2\bigr] . To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to establish sharp bounds for non-Gaussian covariates in linear models when the norm is not induced by an inner product. This work is deeply inspired by classical works on K K -convexity, and more modern work on the geometry of 2-uniform and isotropic convex bodies. 1 1 1 A Preliminary work of the authors “Minimum Norm Interpolation Meets The Local Theory of Banach Spaces” appeared at the International Conference of Machine Learning 2024. This work has substantially more results, a detailed discussion, simplified proofs, and improved accessibility to non-experts in functional analysis.

Introduction

Experiments with neural networks have revealed a phenomenon that defies traditional statistical intuition: regularization is critical for large models when fitting noisy data. Instead, it seems that in the overparameterized regime, interpolators that achieve zero training error can still generalize well and do not profit from sacrificing datafit, or in other words, interpolation is harmless (see e.g. experiments in
60
). A call to explain this counter-intuitive observation (see, e.g.
79
;
11
) gave rise to a line of work known as
benign overfitting
that
set out to prove
generalization bounds for
interpolating
overparameterized models; in the case of regression, the focus of this paper, interpolation corresponds to achieving zero square loss on training data.

As there are infinitely many interpolating solutions in overparameterized regimes, the specific choice of interpolator can drastically vary generalization performance.
Most commonly studied in the literature is the minimum-norm interpolator (MNI)— a natural choice if the ground truth has a simple structure such as a small norm in a Banach space.
For additional motivation, first-order methods on the square loss initialized at zero typically exhibit an implicit bias towards (i.e. converge to) such
minimum-norm solutions
33
;
61
;
69
;
22
.

The statistical analysis of the Mean Squared Error (MSE) of the MNI is primarily focused on the overparameterized linear regression literature when the norm is the
\ell_{2}
; and on Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS)—where the MNI has a closed-form solution—as in both cases the norm emerges from an inner product.
In overparametrized linear models, the literature consists of asymptotic results on the
\ell_{2}
-MNI in the proportional regime where
d/n\to\gamma\in(1,\infty)

In more details,

These proofs take advantage of the inner-product structure that allows for an explicit analysis of the closed-form solution. Further, consistency heavily relies on the eigenvalue decay of the covariates distribution, as the
\ell_{2}
-MNI cannot capture “structural assumptions” and, under isotropic covariates in the high-dimensional setting, suffers from high bias, with respect to the bias variance decomposition of the MSE.

Moving away from the

-MNI,

Their approach only applies to Gaussian covariates and is based on a local uniform convergence approach and crucially relies on the Convex Gaussian Minimax Theorem (CGMT)

Follow-up works

However, their analysis shares the deficiency that it crucially relies on the Gaussianity of the covariates. To our knowledge, in the case of linear models, those are the only non-inner-product MNIs for which sharp rates have been proven.

In non-linear models, the phenomenon of harmless interpolation, or benign overfitting, is much less well understood. Most work so far has considered instances

In this work, we relate the generalization properties of MNIs for regression under additive

1.1
Minimum Norm Interpolator

Let
\mathbb{P}
be a probability measure over some domain
\mathcal{X}
. We consider the Banach space
(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}),\|\cdot\|)
, where
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\subset L_{2}(\mathbb{P})
; and its unit ball is denoted by

\mathcal{F}:=\{f\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}):\|f\|\leq 1\}.

(1)

For any data points

\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the MNI is defined by

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{eq:minnorm}\hfil}eq:minnorm}\widehat{f}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}):=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{f\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}):\ \boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}\|f\|,

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}:=(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{n}))
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
In words, from all possible functions in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
that interpolate the observations
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}
over the data points
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}
, we choose the one with the lowest norm. Note that this interpolating estimator differs from the standard (constrained) ERM solution that would search only in the function class
{\cal F}
, for example, the (constrained) least squares

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{Eq:LSE}\hfil}Eq:LSE}\bar{f}_{n}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-f(x_{i}))^{2}.

(3)

In general, the solution in (
2
) may not be unique, but under some “mild” uniform convexity for some
q\in[2,\infty)
, uniqueness is also guaranteed. From a computational perspective, depending on the case, it may be computed efficiently, or it may be an implicit bias of a first-order method for a convex optimization problem.

In this work
, we study the statistical performance of the MNI in the well-specified regression model

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{Eq:Model}\hfil}Eq:Model}Y=f^{*}(X)+\xi,

(4)

where
X\sim\mathbb{P}
,
\xi\sim N(0,1)
, and
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
. Unless stated otherwise, we write
\widehat{f}_{n}:=\widehat{f}_{n}(\mathcal{D})
where the dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
that consists of
n
i.i.d. samples from (
4
) above, and we use the notation
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Y}}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
.

Throughout the work, we make two assumptions on our model:

Assumption 1
.

The norm of
f^{*}
in both
L_{2}(\mathbb{P})
and
\|\cdot\|
is of order one, i.e.
\|f^{*}\|_{\mathbb{P}}\asymp\|f^{*}\|\asymp 1
.

This assumption rules out the (pathological) case when the norm of
f^{*}
(which we refer to as “true signal”) vanishes with the number of samples
n
. In the high-dimensional setting, such an assumption is crucial. Next, we need an “inductive bias” assumption:

Assumption 2
.

There exists a large enough absolute constant
C\geq 1
, such that with probability at least
1-n^{-2}
, over
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}
, it holds that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{Eq:ANS}\hfil}Eq:ANS}\|\widehat{f}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}})\|\geq C.

(5)

It is easy to see that
\|\widehat{f}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f^{*}}})\|\lesssim 1
with probability one, by noting that
f^{*}
is a possible interpolator. The assumption’s inequality indicates that the norm has an “inductive bias” towards the ground truth
f^{*}
, in that it requires a larger norm to interpolate pure noise rather than the underlying “true signal”
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
. Motivating example is
\ell_{p}
-MNI in the case of overparametrized linear regression, when
p\geq 1
, see Example
1
below, for further details.

1.2
2-Uniform Convexity

We say that
(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}),\|\cdot\|)
is 2-uniform convexity (
UC(2)
) with constant
t\geq 0
, if for any
f,g\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
, it holds

\left\|\frac{f+g}{2}\right\|^{2}+t\left\|\frac{f-g}{2}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{\left\|f\right\|^{2}+\left\|g\right\|^{2}}{2}.

To give some intuition on the
UC(2)
-norm, one should think about it in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, as a lower bound on the minimal singular value of the Hessian on its unit ball. By Pythagoras law, any inner product norm is
UC(2)
with constant
1
. Yet, the
\ell_{p}
norm when
p\in(1,2]
in any dimension is
UC(2)
with constant
t=p-1
.

This definition first appeared in
20
and plays a key role in high-dimensional geometry, as it implies the concentration of Lipschitz functionals; see the influential works of
32
;
31
. Next, we introduce a weaker notion of curvature, known as cotype
2
(
CO(2)
) with constant
t>0
. It means that
for all
m\geq 1
and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
, it holds that

t^{2}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|f_{i}\|^{2}\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\epsilon}}}\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\epsilon_{i}f_{i}\|^{2},

where
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{m},
are i.i.d. Rademacher random variables. The following is a well-known fact:

Fact 1
.

If
(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}),\|\cdot\|)
is
UC(2)
with constant
t\geq 0
, then it is
CO(2)
with constant
\sqrt{t}\geq 0
.

Note that the weaker defintion of cotype
2
is much less restrictive, as for example, it is preserved under norm-equivalence (which is an
isomorphic
definition), compared to
UC(2)
that is very sensitive to small perturbations of norm (i.e.
isometric
definition).

For regularized estimators (in the under-parameterized case), it is more natural to analyze the bias and the variance separately to bound the MSE. Roughly speaking, in that case, the bias can be interpreted as an approximation error of

However, in overparameterized models, i.e., when

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{eq:decomplevel2}\hfil}eq:decomplevel2}T_{1}={\mathbb{E}}\|\mathcal{P}_{f^{*}}({\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\widehat{f}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})-f^{*}\|_{\mathbb{P}}^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}\|\underbrace{\mathcal{P}_{(f^{*})^{\perp}}({\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\widehat{f}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})}_{:=f_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}\|_{\mathbb{P}}^{2},

(7)

and the first term in (

f^{*}

As we know that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\widehat{f}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}
interpolates

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}

\mathcal{P}_{f^{*}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\widehat{f}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}

\lambda\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f^{*}}}
. Hence, the second term in (

(in expectation) to the minimum norm solution. The

1.5
Positions

For any
symmetric
convex set
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, i.e.
K=-K
with a non-empty interior, its Minkowski norm is defined via

\|z\|_{K}:=\inf\{r>0:z\in rK\}.

and its polar body of
K
,
K^{\circ}
, is defined as

K^{\circ}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\sup_{y\in K}\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\leq 1\}.

The notion of a
position
is a fundamental topic in high-dimensional convex geometry. Given a convex body
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(that we will assume symmetric), one seeks an invertible linear map
T
so that the image
TK
is more “regular” with respect to a chosen functional. Here, we introduce few known examples:

•

K
is in
John’s
position if among all ellipsoids contained in
K
, the ball
B_{2}^{d}
has maximal volume.

•

K
is in
Isotropic
position if the uniform measure over
K
has zero mean and identity covariance.

•

K
is in
M
-position if for some constant
C\geq 0
, that is independent of
K
and
n
, it holds that

\max\{\mathcal{N}(K,B_{d}),\mathcal{N}(B_{d},K),\mathcal{N}(K^{\circ},B_{d}),\mathcal{N}(B_{d},K^{\circ})\}\leq C^{d},

where
\mathcal{N}(A,B)
denotes the minimal number of translates of
A
required to cover
B
.

In the case of
\ell_{p}
balls, all these are in the same position; however, for general norms, these positions can be extremely different, see
28
. We remark that
M
-position is not unique - even up to rotation; for example, recent progress (cf.
14
and references within) shows that isotropic convex bodies are, up to universal constants, in
M
-position.

In the case of
linear
models, the
position
plays a key role in the statistical performance of the MNI. We say that a norm is in a certain position when its unit ball is in this position up to some normalization, i.e.,
\lambda\mathcal{F}
, for some
\lambda\geq 0
is in this position.

1.6
Convex Concentration Property

In the setting of an overparametrized linear regression, our proof deeply relies on the convex concentration property (with a constant
L\geq 0
), in shorthand CCP, cf.
1
. We say that a random vector
X\sim G
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
satisfies CCP, if for every convex

-Lipschitz function
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{Eq:CCP}\hfil}Eq:CCP}\Pr_{X\sim G}(|F(X)-{\mathbb{E}}[F])|\geq t)\leq 2\exp(t^{2}/L^{2}),

(8)

CCP is stronger than sub-Gaussianity, but it is satisfied for many

distributions. For example, a random vector with uniformly bounded i.i.d. entries, say by

, satisfies CCP with constant

due to Talagrand’s convex distance inequality (cf.

) with constant. Furthermore, any isotropic vector with i.i.d. s.g. entries satisfies the CCP with constant of
L\lesssim\sqrt{\log(d)}

(

. Note that CCP is significantly weaker than concentration over Lipschitz functionals, which, for example, is not satisfied by Rademacher random variables, cf.

Let

The gaussian and spherical means of a norm

are defined (respectively) via

We then write
M_{g}^{*}(K)=M_{g}(K^{\circ})
or (
M_{s}^{*}(K)=M_{s}(K^{\circ})
) for the mean of the dual norm
\|\cdot\|_{K^{\circ}}

The dual norm
\|\cdot\|_{K^{\circ}}
is
also referred to as the support function

of the original body
K

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}

K
from the origin in that direction.
It is not hard to verify that for any convex symmetric set

1\leq M_{s}(K)M_{s}^{*}(K)

At the same time, intuitively, one expects that
M_{s}^{*}(K)\cdot M_{s}(K)\lesssim 1
, when

does not lie in a low-dimensional subspace and is “balanced”. Remarkably, the works of

25

, there exists a linear transformation

M_{s}(TK)M_{s}^{*}(TK)\lesssim\log(n).

Throughout this paper, we use the shorthands for the mean (and dual) norm averaged over

M_{n}(\mathcal{F}):={\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}M_{g}(\mathcal{F}_{n})\text{ and }M_{n}^{*}(\mathcal{F}):={\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}M_{g}^{*}(\mathcal{F}_{n})

R_{MM^{*}}(\mathcal{F}):=\frac{M_{n}(\mathcal{F})M_{n}^{*}(\mathcal{F})}{n}\approx{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}\left[M_{s}(\mathcal{F}_{n})M_{s}^{*}(\mathcal{F}_{n})\right].

(motivated by the definition of “

where

is the inner

radius of

2.2
Regularity Assumptions

We introduce two regularity assumptions on the function class and our distribution: The first ensures that with high probability, the quantities
M(\mathcal{F}_{n}),M^{*}(\mathcal{F}_{n})
equal their mean (up to an absolute constant) with high probability. This additional assumption is essential, as we do not assume that
\mathcal{F}
is uniformly bounded or has a bounded envelope or any strong structure on the covariates, and it is weaker than assuming concentration of those two quantities.

Assumption 3
.

With probability at least
1-n^{-2}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
, the random set
\mathcal{F}_{n}
satisfies:

M_{g}(\mathcal{F}_{n})\asymp M_{n}(\mathcal{F})\text{ and }M_{g}^{*}(\mathcal{F}_{n})\asymp M_{n}^{*}(\mathcal{F}).

The last assumption will be used to ensure that the

and

L_{1}(\mathbb{P})
error.

Assumption 4
.

There exists a universal constant

such that

\mathcal{I}_{L}(n,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})\leq C_{4}\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{F})^{2}.

In further details, it is well known that by symetrization and contraction lemmas (cf.
(
41
, Thms 2.1 and 2.4)

\|f\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{P})}\geq\|f\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}-C\cdot\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})

The key idea of the seminal work of

2.3
Curvature notions in Banach spaces

We now recall some basic concepts of non-linear functional analysis, cf.
(
4

Definition 4
.

(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}),\|\cdot\|)
is

with constant

\left\|{\frac{f+g}{2}}\right\|^{q}+t\left\|{\frac{f-g}{2}}\right\|^{q}\leq\frac{\|f\|^{q}+\|g\|^{q}}{2}

and
p\in(1,2]

\left\|{\frac{f+g}{2}}\right\|^{p}+s\left\|{\frac{f-g}{2}}\right\|^{p}\geq\frac{\|f\|^{p}+\|g\|^{p}}{2}.

The following is a classical fact
(
47
)
:

Fact 2
.

(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}),\|\cdot\|)
is
UC(q)
with constant
t

\iff
its dual norm is
US(p)
with constant
s
, where

t^{1/q}=s^{-1/p}
.

The fact that the optimal exponents are
q=2
for uniform convexity and
p=2
for uniform smoothness is already visible in dimension one.

Moreover, the optimal constants are
t\leq 1
for uniform convexity and
s\geq 1
for uniform smoothness, and these bounds are sharp. They are attained in Hilbert spaces, where both inequalities hold with equality by the parallelogram identity.

As uniform convexity and uniform smoothness are considered to be quite strong notions of curvature (for example, the
\ell_{1}
-norm itself does not satisfy them); we also consider weaker notions of curvature in Banach spaces, which are known as
type

p\in[1,2]
and

q\in[2,\infty)

(
67
;
44

Definition 5
.

(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}),\|\cdot\|)

type

p\in[1,2]

with constant

m\geq 1

Note that Hilbert spaces are both

and

with constant

. In some sense, the opposite is true as well, as

2.3.2
Sobolev Spaces

Let

N_{k}:=\#\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:\ |\alpha|\leq k\}=\binom{d+k}{k},

T:W^{k,p}(\Omega)\to L_{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N_{k}}),\qquad Tf=(D^{\alpha}f)_{|\alpha|\leq k},

4.1
Tradeoffs between type
p
and Gaussian complexity

As briefly discussed above,
Theorem

1
may suggest that the upper bound is improving under additional smoothness, i.e when

US(p)
with
p>1
with constant
s^{p}\geq 0
, which implies that it also is type
p
with a constant
s\geq 0
. We now provide more details on why this is not necessarily true. Here, we consider the case of overparametrized linear models under isotropic s.g. covariates, in which
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{P}}=\|\cdot\|_{2}
.

First, we argue
that when the class contains
m
“well-separated” signals in
L_{2}(\mathbb{P})
, there is a price to be paid, as the Gaussian complexity of the class is at least of order
s^{-1}\tfrac{m^{1-1/p}}{\sqrt{n}}
.
Therefore, larger
p
does not automatically lead to an improved upper bound in
Theorem

1
.

First, let
m
be the maximal number of elements
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\in\mathcal{F}
that are orthogonal and well-separated, namely

\forall 1\leq i<j\leq m\quad\left\langle f_{i},f_{j}\right\rangle=0\quad\|f_{i}-f_{i}\|_{2}\gtrsim 1,

where
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
denotes the inner product with respect to
\ell_{2}
-norm.
Then, if
({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\|\cdot\|)
is type
p
with constant
s\geq 0
, its Gaussian complexity is lower-bounded by

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{eq:gclower}\hfil}eq:gclower}\mathcal{G}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})\gtrsim s^{-1}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\max\{m^{2-2/p},\log(m)\}}{n}},

(15)

when

\tilde{\Omega}(n)=m=\tilde{O}(n^{q/2})
. In particular, for type
2
spaces, we have
\mathcal{G}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})\gtrsim\sqrt{m/n}
. In contrast, for a space that is both cotype
2
and type
1
, such as the
\ell_{1}
-ball, we only pay a logarithmic price in the number of different signals, which is tight in Example
1
.

To simplify the presentation, we now provide the argument for type
2
spaces, while the lower bound (
15
) for general type
p
follows analogously. In fact, in a
T(2)
space with constant
s
, we have

\displaystyle\left({\mathbb{E}}\left\|\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\epsilon_{i}f_{i}}{\sqrt{m}}\right\|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\epsilon_{i}f_{i}\|^{2}}{m}\lesssim s^{2},

where we used Kahane’s inequality (cf.
58
) and that
\|f_{i}\|\leq 1
for all
1\leq i\leq m
.
Moreover, by the orthogonality of
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\}
, we have that

{\mathbb{E}}\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\epsilon_{i}f_{i}\|_{2}\asymp 1

By combining the last two equations, it implies that the unit ball
\mathcal{F}
contains a set of
2^{\Omega(m)}
functions, constructed by choosing random signs in the last equality, that are
\Theta(s^{-1})
far from each other (in terms of
\|\cdot\|_{2}
). Hence, under isotropic s.g. covariates (which also satisfies the small ball assumption), we know that for each two functions

that

\|f-g\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}\geq c_{4}\|f-g\|_{2}\gtrsim s^{-1},

with probability of at least
1-\exp(-cn)
. By the union bound, we obtain that there are

mixture signals that are well-separated and belong to
\mathcal{F}_{n}
with high probability.
By Sudakov’s minoration inequality (cf.
76
), it immediately follows that

\mathcal{G}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})\gtrsim s^{-1}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\min\{m,n\}}{n}}.

Therefore, we conclude that additional smoothness may increase Gaussian complexity and reduce the possibility of benign overfitting.

4.3
Quantitative Anderson’s Theorem

A basic ingredient in our analysis is Anderson’s inequality, which implies that for any norm
\|\cdot\|
and any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}+x\|^{2}\geq{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}
is an isotropic Gaussian vector. In this paper we assume that the norm is
UC(2)
with constant
t>0
, which yields the quantitative improvement

{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}+x\|^{2}\geq{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|^{2}+t\|x\|^{2},

valid for any symmetric noise. However, the assumption of
2
-uniform convexity appears to be stronger than what is intrinsically needed. Indeed,
UC(2)
is a
pointwise
curvature requirement on the norm, whereas the inequality above only probes curvature
on average
under the noise distribution. Evidence for this comes from
\ell_{p}
spaces. In
13
, we prove that for every
p\in[1,2]
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}+x\|_{p}^{2}\geq{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{p}^{2}+c\|x\|_{p}^{2},

for an absolute constant
c>0
independent of
d
and
p
. Thus a dimension-free quantitative Anderson inequality holds even though
\ell_{p}
is not
UC(2)
when
p<2
.

In contrast, the situation depends strongly on the distribution of the noise. For example, if
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\epsilon}}\sim U(\{-1,1\}^{d})
is Rademacher noise and we work with the
\ell_{1}
norm, then for the diagonal direction
u=(1/d,\dots,1/d)
with
\|u\|_{1}=1
, one has for
t\leq d
,

{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\epsilon}}+tu\|_{1}^{2}=d^{2}+\frac{t^{2}}{d}={\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\epsilon}}\|_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{d}\|tu\|_{1}^{2}.

Thus, sensitivity to an additional signal deteriorates with dimensionality. It is plausible that for Gaussian noise, a norm with cotype
2
satisfies a dimension-free quantitative Anderson inequality once the body is placed in a suitable position. Note that the choice of position plays a crucial role. Consider a norm that its unit ball

K=[-1,1]\times B_{2}^{n-1}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

which is in John’s position and cotype
2
with an absolute constant, one easily checks that

{\mathbb{E}}\|e_{1}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|^{2}\leq{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|^{2}+Ce^{-cn}.

On the other hand, after rescaling the Euclidean factor and considering

\widetilde{K}=[-1,1]\times\sqrt{n}\,B_{2}^{n-1},

One obtains the desired dimension-free quantitative Anderson inequality; i.e., we believe that a tailored position is required. We refer the reader to the forthcoming manuscript
13
, where the investigation of such quantitative Anderson inequalities is presented.

Conclusions

This work presents a novel geometric perspective that allows to analyze MNIs under less restrictive assumptions on the covariates when the norm is
UC(2)
and for models that may not be linear. We further demonstrate how these results can be used to recover tight bounds from prior work that employed highly specialized proof techniques applicable only to Gaussian covariates. We believe that our approach contributes to a more fundamental understanding of benign overfitting phenomena in high-dimensional settings. Our proofs also show that when the model and the covariates structure are connected, we do not rely on the noise distribution. Furthermore, we should consider the role of the norm’s position in the context of MNIs.

R(\mathcal{V}_{k})\asymp\Big(\frac{d}{k}\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}L^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}=(\log d)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}(\log\log d)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}.

We now want to compute the width of
\mathcal{V}_{k}
. For any
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

h_{\mathcal{V}_{k}}(u)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{V}_{k}}\langle u,x\rangle=\sup_{|\sigma|\asymp s}\ \sup_{x\in A_{\sigma}}\langle u,x\rangle=\sup_{|\sigma|\asymp s}\ a\sum_{i\in\sigma}|u_{i}|.

Let
|u|_{(1)}\geq\cdots\geq|u|_{(d)}
denote the non-increasing rearrangement of
(|u_{i}|)_{i\leq d}
.

h_{\mathcal{V}_{k}}(u)\asymp a\sum_{i=1}^{s}|u|_{(i)}.

Consequently,

M^{*}(\mathcal{V}_{k})\asymp\frac{a}{\sqrt{d}}\,{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{i=1}^{s}|G|_{(i)},\qquad G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}).

Let
q:=s/d\asymp(\log d\,\log\log d)^{-1}
and let
t>0
be defined by
{\mathbb{P}}(|g|\geq t)=q
for
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Then
t\asymp\sqrt{\log(1/q)}\asymp\sqrt{\log\log d}
.
Standard gaussian tail estimates yield

{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{i=1}^{s}|G|_{(i)}\asymp s\,t\asymp s\sqrt{\log\log d}.

Plugging this into the expression for
M^{*}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
gives

M^{*}(\mathcal{V}_{k})\asymp\Big(\frac{d}{k}\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}L^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{s}{d}}\;\sqrt{\log\log d}.

Since
\sqrt{s/d}=\sqrt{k/(dL)}=(\log d\,\log\log d)^{-1/2}
and
d/k=\log d
, we conclude that

M^{*}(\mathcal{V}_{k})\asymp(\log d)^{\frac{1}{p}-1}(\log\log d)^{\frac{1}{p}-1}\,\sqrt{\log\log d}\asymp\sqrt{\log\log d},

where we used that
p-1=\Theta(1/\log\log d)
, hence
(\log d)^{-(p-1)}\asymp 1
and
(\log\log d)^{-(p-1)}\asymp 1
.

By what precedes we have
\frac{M^{*}(\mathcal{V}_{k})}{R(\mathcal{V}_{k})}\asymp\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log d}}
, hence the critical dimension of
\mathcal{V}_{k}
is of order
d/\log d\gg n
.
Now, we apply Dvoreztky’s
Lemma

2
, and conclude that

c_{1}M^{*}(\mathcal{V}_{\frac{d}{\log(d)}})B_{n}\subset P\left(\mathrm{conv}\left\{\mathcal{V}_{\frac{d}{\log(d)}}\right\}\right).

As a consequence,

M(P\tilde{B}_{p}^{d})\lesssim\alpha_{d}.

∎

Now, we prove the corresponding lower bound for
M(P\tilde{B}_{p}^{d})
. Since for any convex body
K
we have

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{eq451}\hfil}eq451}MM^{*}(K)\geq 1,

(31)

it suffices to upper bound
M^{*}(P\tilde{B}_{p}^{d})
. Note that when
P
is Gaussian, it is an exercise to check that

\mathbb{E}M^{*}(PK)\asymp\mathbb{E}M^{*}(K).

Applying this to
\tilde{B}_{p}^{d}
would yield the desired upper bound in expectation, since

M^{*}(\tilde{B}_{p}^{d})\asymp(p-1)^{-1/2}.

However, we are interested in high-probability bounds, and using Lipschitz concentration of
P\mapsto M^{*}(PK)
is insufficient for our purposes here. Instead, we use a more direct and computational approach, which works when
P
is merely sub-Gaussian.

Lemma 7
.

With probability at least
1-\exp(-cn)
we have

M^{*}(P\tilde{B}_{p}^{d})\lesssim\alpha_{d}

when
P
is subgaussian.

Proof.
Let
x\in\tilde{B}_{p}^{d}
. We write
x
as

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{eq:dyadic-decomp}\hfil}eq:dyadic-decomp}x=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}\big(\Pi_{k}(x)-\Pi_{k/2}(x)\big)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}\delta_{k}\,u_{k}(x),

(32)

where
\mathcal{I}
is the set of dyadic indices

\mathcal{I}=\{2,4,8,\dots,{2^{\lfloor\log_{2}d\rfloor}}\}.

Here
\Pi_{k}(x)
is the vector obtained from
x
by keeping the
k
largest
coordinates in absolute value and setting the others to zero. Moreover,

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}\delta_{k}^{p}=\|x\|_{p}^{p}\leq 1,\qquad\|u_{k}(x)\|_{p}=1

unless
\Pi_{k}(x)-\Pi_{k/2}(x)=0
, in which case
u_{k}(x)=0
.

Let
G_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tfrac{1}{n}I_{n})
. Then

\displaystyle M^{*}(P\tilde{B}_{p}^{d})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\sup_{y\in P\tilde{B}_{p}^{d}}\langle y,G_{n}\rangle=\mathbb{E}\sup_{x\in\tilde{B}_{p}^{d}}\langle Px,G_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\sup_{x\in\tilde{B}_{p}^{d}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}\delta_{k}\langle Pu_{k}(x),G_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}\delta_{k}^{p}\Big)^{1/p}\Big(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbb{E}\sup_{x\in\tilde{B}_{p}^{d}}\langle Pu_{k}(x),G_{n}\rangle^{q}\Big)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq S^{1/q},

where

S=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbb{E}\sup_{x\in\tilde{B}_{p}^{d}}\langle Pu_{k}(x),G_{n}\rangle^{q},

and
q
is the conjugate exponent to
p
.

We claim that for all
k\in\mathcal{I}
,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{eq:key-claim}\hfil}eq:key-claim}\mathbb{E}\sup_{x\in\tilde{B}_{p}^{d}}\langle Pu_{k}(x),G_{n}\rangle\;\lesssim\;\Big(\frac{d}{k}\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}-1}\sqrt{\log\!\Big(\frac{ed}{k}\Big)}

(33)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-cn)
.

To see this, we first bound
\|Pu_{k}(x)\|_{2}
.
We split the indices into two parts. Define

\mathcal{I}_{1}=\Big\{k\in\mathcal{I}:k\log\!\Big(\frac{ed}{k}\Big)\leq n\Big\}.

Then for all
k\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
and all supports
\sigma
with
|\sigma|=k
,
we have

\mathbb{P}\Big(\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{\sigma},\;\|Px\|_{2}\leq C\sqrt{\tfrac{k}{n}}\,\|x\|_{2}\Big)\geq 1-\exp(-2n),

for a large enough constant
C>0
.

By taking a union bound over all such
\sigma
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\Big(\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;\|Px\|_{2}\leq C\sqrt{\tfrac{k}{n}}\,\|x\|_{2}\Big)\geq 1-\exp\!\big(-2n+k\log(\tfrac{ed}{k})\big)\geq 1-\exp(-n).

Taking again a union bound over all
k\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
, this holds
uniformly with probability at least
1-\exp(-n/2)
.

On this event, for all
x\in\tilde{B}_{p}^{d}
and
k\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
,

\|Pu_{k}(x)\|_{2}\leq\|\Pi_{k}(x)\|_{2}+\|\Pi_{k/2}(x)\|_{2}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\Big(\frac{d}{k}\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{x\in\tilde{B}_{p}^{d}}\langle Pu_{k}(x),G_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\sup_{u\in\bigcup_{|\sigma|=k}B_{2}^{\sigma}}\langle u,G_{n}\rangle\sqrt{\tfrac{k}{n}}\Big(\frac{d}{k}\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{k\log\!\Big(\frac{ed}{k}\Big)}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big(\frac{d}{k}\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{d}{k}\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}-1}\sqrt{\log\!\Big(\frac{ed}{k}\Big)}.

This proves the claim when
k\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
.

We now treat the case
k\in\mathcal{I}_{2}:=\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{1}
.
By a similar argument, using a union bound with an appropriate choice of

\varepsilon_{k}\asymp\sqrt{\tfrac{k\log(ed/k)}{d}}
, we obtain

\|Pu_{k}(x)\|_{2}\lesssim\sqrt{\tfrac{k\log(ed/k)}{d}}\,\|u_{k}(x)\|_{2}

with probability at least
1-\exp(-cn)
, uniformly over
k\in\mathcal{I}_{2}
.
By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{x\in\tilde{B}_{p}^{d}}\langle Pu_{k}(x),G_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{x\in\tilde{B}_{p}^{d}}\|Pu_{k}(x)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\tfrac{k\log(ed/k)}{d}}\Big(\frac{d}{k}\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{d}{k}\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}-1}\sqrt{\log\!\Big(\frac{ed}{k}\Big)}.

This proves the claim (
33
).

With the claim proved, under this high-probability event we compute

S\;\lesssim\;\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}\Big(\frac{d}{k}\Big)^{q(\frac{1}{p}-1)}\log^{q/2}\!\Big(\frac{ed}{k}\Big).

Writing
k=cd2^{-i}
for
i=0,1,\dots,\lfloor\log(d/2)\rfloor

and some constant
c\in[1/2,1]
, we get

S\;\lesssim\;\sum_{i\geq 0}2^{-i}\,i^{q/2}\;\lesssim\;(Cq)^{q/2},

where the last inequality follows from a standard computation of moments
of the (discrete) exponential distribution.

We conclude that

M^{*}(P\tilde{B}_{p}^{d})\;\lesssim\;S^{1/q}\;\lesssim\;\sqrt{q}\;\lesssim\;\sqrt{\log\log d},

with probability at least
1-\exp(-cn)
.
∎

Combining Lemma
6
for the upper bound and Lemma
7
together with (
31
) for the lower bound, we have now established (
29
). To prove Lemma
5
, we work on the event where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
satisfies (
30
) and where (
29
) holds. We then use the convex Lipschitz concentration of the map
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\mapsto\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{n}}{{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{n}}
(which is
O(1)
-Lipschitz), and conclude that with probability at least
1-\exp(-cn)
, it holds that

\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{n}\asymp\alpha_{d},

which we condition on
throughout our proof
, as mentioned in Step 0. On this event, the following holds:

•

For all
k\geq 1
, it holds that
w_{k}\in\alpha_{d}\cdot\mathcal{V}_{\lfloor k\log(d/k)\rfloor}
.

•

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{2}}\delta_{k}^{p}\asymp 1
.

•

We have the estimates

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{Eq:stars}\hfil}Eq:stars}M(P\tilde{B}_{p}^{d})\asymp M^{*}(P\tilde{B}_{p}^{d})^{-1}\asymp b(P\tilde{B}_{p}^{d})\asymp\alpha_{d}.

(34)

If we knew that
\widehat{w}_{p}\in C\sqrt{\log(d)}\cdot\mathcal{C}_{d}
, we could compare the
\ell_{2}
norm with the
\ell_{p}
norm at a small cost, and we would be done. In the following, we show that all
\delta_{k}
are sufficiently small for

\|\widehat{w}_{n}\|_{2}=\widetilde{O}(1)

to hold with high probability over
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
, for
any
fixed vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}
such that
\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{n}\asymp\alpha_{d}
.

7.1.2
Step II: Entries in
\mathcal{R}_{1}

Here, we show that for
k\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
it holds that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{Eq:StationaryR}\hfil}Eq:StationaryR}\delta_{k}\lesssim\left(k/d\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\log(d/k)^{\frac{1}{2(p-1)}}

(35)

for
k\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
in terms of the decomposition above. Recall that in the range
\mathcal{R}_{1}
, the deviation term is dominating the expectation, in terms of (
25
) above.
As
w_{k}
and
\widehat{w}_{n}-w_{k}
lie in different coordinates, it must hold that

\|\widehat{w}_{n}-\delta_{k}w_{k}\|_{p}\leq(1-\Theta(\delta_{k}^{p}))\|\widehat{w}_{n}\|_{p},

(36)

where we used
\|\widehat{w}_{n}\|_{p}\asymp\|w_{k}\|_{p}
.
Now, using the last step, we know that

c\tilde{\alpha}_{d}\cdot B_{n}\subset\mathrm{conv}\{\mathcal{V}_{\frac{d}{\log(d)}}\}

and hence we can find an interpolator to
\delta_{k}Pw_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, which we denote by
\widetilde{w}_{k}
, that lies in

C\alpha_{d}\delta_{k}\|Pw_{k}\|_{2}\cdot\mathrm{conv}\{\mathcal{V}_{\frac{d}{\log(d)}}\}.

Consider the interpolator to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}
that is defined via

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{Eq:VariationalOne}\hfil}Eq:VariationalOne}\bar{w}_{n}:=\widehat{w}_{n}-\delta_{k}\cdot w_{k}+\widetilde{w}_{k},

(37)

As
\widehat{w}_{n}
is the MNI, using the triangle inequality, and the last two equations, it must hold

\delta_{k}^{p}\lesssim\alpha_{d}\delta_{k}\|Pw_{k}\|_{2}.

Therefore, using (
28
). we obtain that

\displaystyle\delta_{k}^{p}\lesssim\alpha_{d}\cdot\delta_{k}\cdot\|Pw_{k}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\iff\delta_{k}^{p-1}\lesssim\left(k/d\right)^{1-1/p}\left(\frac{\log(d/k)}{\alpha_{d}^{-2}}\right)^{1/2}

(38)

\displaystyle\iff\delta_{k}^{p-1}\lesssim\left(k/d\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\left(\frac{\log(d/k)}{\alpha_{d}^{-2}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\iff\delta_{k}\lesssim\left(k/d\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\frac{C_{1}\log(d/k)}{\alpha_{d}^{-2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2(p-1)}}

for some
C_{1}>0
. Therefore, by the definition of
\mathcal{V}_{\lfloor k\log(d/k)\rfloor}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\delta_{k}\|w_{k}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\tilde{\alpha}_{d}\cdot\delta_{k}\bar{\lambda}_{k}\lesssim\left(\frac{k}{d}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{C_{1}\log(d/k)}{\log\log(d)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2(p-1)}}

(39)

With our choice of
p
, the right hand side is maximized at the largest index
k\asymp d/\log(d)
. Hence, for all
k\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
for some large enough
C\geq 0
, it holds:

\delta_{k}\bar{\lambda}_{k}\lesssim\log(d)^{C_{2}},

for some
C_{2}\geq 0
.
Summing over
k\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
, we obtain

\|\sum_{k\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}\delta_{k}w_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\log(d)^{C_{2}+1}.

7.1.3
Step III: Entries in
\mathcal{R}_{2}

In this regime, we will have to use a different argument as the expectation is the dominant term when
k\lesssim n/\log(d/n)
(see (
25
) above). For simplicity of notation, we take the first
k
-entries of the
\tilde{B}_{p}^{d}
, and then take a union bound on all the elements in
\mathcal{V}_{k}
. First, we only analyze the case of
k=1
, i.e., the maximal entry. Then, similarly, we analyze the remaining entries.

Control of the maximal entry

We aim to control
\|\widehat{w}_{n}\|_{\infty}
. It is therefore sufficient to control a single coordinate. Without loss of generality, we focus on the first coordinate.

We begin by decomposing the minimizer along this coordinate. We write

\widehat{w}_{n}=\delta\alpha_{d}\lambda_{1}e_{1}+w^{\perp},

where
\delta\geq 0
,
\lambda_{1}=d^{1/p-1/2}
, and
w^{\perp}\perp e_{1}
. Our goal is therefore to upper bound
\delta
, which in turn yields a bound on the first coordinate of
\widehat{w}_{n}
, and hence on
\|\widehat{w}_{n}\|_{\infty}
(by applying the same argument to all coordinates and a union bound).

Fix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
. By the splitting property of the
\ell_{p}
norm, we have

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{Eq:PierreOne_clean4}\hfil}Eq:PierreOne_{c}lean4}\delta=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\delta\in[0,2]}\left\{\delta^{p}+\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}-\beta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{1}\|_{\tilde{n}}^{p}\right\},

(40)

and

\|\widehat{w}_{n}\|_{p}^{p}=\min_{\delta\in[0,2]}\left\{\delta^{p}+\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}-\beta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{1}\|_{\tilde{n}}^{p}\right\},

where

\beta:=\frac{\alpha_{d}\lambda_{1}\delta}{\sqrt{d}}.

We now discretize the interval
[0,2]
into
d^{100}
subintervals, and let
\mathcal{Z}
denote the corresponding grid. For every fixed
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
, this yields

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{Eq:PierreTwo_clean4}\hfil}Eq:PierreTwo_{c}lean4}\tilde{\delta}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\delta\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\delta^{p}+\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}-\beta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{1}\|_{\tilde{n}}^{p}\right\},

(41)

and

\|\widehat{w}_{n}\|_{p}^{p}=\min_{\delta\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\delta^{p}+\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}-\beta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{1}\|_{\tilde{n}}^{p}\right\}\pm d^{-98}.

Moreover,

|\tilde{\delta}-\delta|\leq d^{-99}.

This follows from the uniform convexity of the loss, together with the fact that each
P_{i}\ell_{p}^{d}
contains a Euclidean ball by Kashin’s theorem, which allows us to apply the triangle inequality and only pay a negligible error.

To upper bound
\delta
, we prove that with high probability (over
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{1}
), for every fixed
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{-1}
,
\delta
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}
satisfying
\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{n}\asymp\alpha_{d}
, it holds that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{Eq:PierreMain_clean4}\hfil}Eq:PierreMain_{c}lean4}\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{1}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}-\beta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{1}\|_{\tilde{n}}\geq\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{\tilde{n}}-C\sqrt{\log d}\,\beta\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{\tilde{n}}\right)\geq 1-d^{-200}.

(42)

Intersecting over all discretized values of
\delta
, we obtain that with probability at least
1-d^{-100}
,

\forall\delta\in\mathcal{Z},\qquad\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}-\beta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{1}\|_{\tilde{n}}\geq\left(1-C\sqrt{\log d}\,\beta\right)\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{\tilde{n}}.

Fix such a realization of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}
. Replacing the continuous minimization by the discretized one, at the cost of the error
d^{-98}
above, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{n}^{p}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{\delta\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\left(1-C\sqrt{\log d}\,\beta\right)\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{\tilde{n}}^{p}+\delta^{p}-d^{-98}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{\delta\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\left(1-\frac{C\sqrt{\log d}\,\alpha_{d}\lambda_{1}\delta}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{\tilde{n}}^{p}+\delta^{p}-d^{-98}\right\}.

Using that
\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{\tilde{n}}^{p}\geq\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{n}^{p}
, it follows that
\delta
is bounded, up to absolute constants, by the stationary point of

\delta\mapsto\delta^{p}-\frac{\alpha_{d}\lambda_{1}\sqrt{\log d}}{\sqrt{d}}\,\delta.

Thus,

\delta^{p}\asymp\frac{\alpha_{d}\lambda_{1}\sqrt{\log d}}{\sqrt{d}}\,\delta,

and therefore

\delta\lesssim\left(\frac{\alpha_{d}\lambda_{1}\sqrt{\log d}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}.

Finally,

\|\widehat{w}_{n}\|_{\infty}\lesssim\delta\lambda_{1}\lesssim d^{1/p-1/2}\,\delta\lesssim\left(\frac{\log d}{\log\log d}\right)^{\frac{1}{2(p-1)}}d^{-1/2},

as desired.

Remaining
1\lesssim k\lesssim n/\log(d/n)
entries:

We now adapt the previous argument to the case of a block of size
k
, for

1\lesssim k\lesssim\frac{n}{\log(d/n)}.

The only essential difference is that one now has to take a union bound over all supports of cardinality
k
, i.e. over
\binom{d}{k}
possible subsets. Accordingly, we set

\bar{\lambda}_{k}:=\lambda_{\lfloor k\log(d/k)\rfloor},

which corresponds to taking a union bound on the set
\mathcal{V}_{\lfloor k\log(d/k)\rfloor}
.

We fix the first
k
coordinates, a vector
w_{k}
, and define

\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}_{k}:=[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{k}],\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}:=\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}_{k}w_{k}.

Then, for every
\delta_{k}\geq 0
, one has

\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{n}=(1+\Theta(\delta_{k}^{p}))\cdot\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}-\delta_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{\tilde{n}},

where
\|\cdot\|_{\tilde{n}}
is the norm induced by projecting

(1-\Theta(\delta_{k}^{p}))\ell_{p}^{d-k}
onto the last
d-k
coordinates, namely using the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
restricted to the columns
k+1,\dots,d
.

Thus, exactly as in the case
k=1
, the problem reduces to controlling the fluctuation of

\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}_{k}\mapsto\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}-\beta_{k}\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}_{k}w_{k}\|_{\tilde{n}},\qquad\beta_{k}:=\frac{\delta_{k}\bar{\lambda}_{k}\alpha_{d}}{\sqrt{d}}.

The Lipschitz constant of this convex function is bounded by

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\beta_{k}\,\sigma_{\max}(\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}_{k}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}_{k}^{\prime})\,\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{\tilde{n}}}{\|\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}_{k}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}_{k}^{\prime}\|_{F}}\lesssim\beta_{k}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{\tilde{n}},

by the same considerations as in the case
k=1
.

Applying Gaussian concentration therefore gives

\Pr_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}_{k}}\left(\left|\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}-\delta_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{\tilde{n}}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}-\delta_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{\tilde{n}}\right|\lesssim\sqrt{k\log(d/k)}\,\beta_{k}\,\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{\tilde{n}}\right)\geq 1-\exp(-C_{4}k\log(d/k)).

Taking a union bound over all supports of size
k
, equivalently over
\mathcal{V}_{\lfloor k\log(d/k)\rfloor}
, we obtain

\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{n}=(1+\Theta(\delta_{k}^{p}))\cdot\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}-\delta_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{\tilde{n}}\geq\left(1-\Theta\big(\sqrt{k\log(d/k)}\,\beta_{k}-\delta_{k}^{p}\big)\right)\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{\tilde{n}}.

At this point the argument is identical to the case
k=1
: one has to balance the loss term and the fluctuation term, namely

\delta_{k}^{p}\lesssim\sqrt{k\log(d/k)}\,\beta_{k}.

Recalling that

\beta_{k}=\frac{\delta_{k}\bar{\lambda}_{k}\alpha_{d}}{\sqrt{d}},

this yields

\delta_{k}^{p-1}\lesssim\sqrt{k\log(d/k)}\,\frac{\bar{\lambda}_{k}\alpha_{d}}{\sqrt{d}}.

Solving for
\delta_{k}
, one obtains

\delta_{k}\lesssim\left(\frac{k}{d}\right)^{1/p}\left(\frac{\log(d/k)}{\log\log d}\right)^{\frac{1}{2(p-1)}}.

Therefore, in terms of the decomposition from the first step,

\|\delta_{k}w_{k}\|_{2}\lesssim\delta_{k}\bar{\lambda}_{k}\lesssim\left(\frac{k}{d}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\log(d/k)}{\log\log d}\right)^{\frac{1}{2(p-1)}}.

Summing over the dyadic values

k=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\log(d/n)}\right\rfloor,\ \left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\frac{n}{\log(d/n)}\right\rfloor,\ \dots,\ 1,

we obtain

\left\|\sum_{k=1,2,\dots,\lfloor n/\log(d/n)\rfloor}\delta_{k}w_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\log(d)^{-c_{1}(C)},

where
c_{1}(C)>0
is decreasing in
C
.

This concludes the argument. Finally, to obtain a high-probability bound on the MSE, note that the largest entry carries the heaviest tail. Hence

\|\widehat{w}_{p}\|_{2}=\tilde{O}(1)

with probability at least

1-\exp\big(-\tilde{O}(d^{\,p-1})\big).

7.1.4
Step IV: Extending to
O(1)
-sparse and upper bounding
E_{2}
:

Note that the
\ell_{p}

\widehat{f}_{n}(\mathcal{D})|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})-{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\left[\widehat{f}_{n}(\mathcal{D})|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\right],

(55)

and exploit the linear structure behind it.

For a realization of the vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
, consider the linearization of
M_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}
with respect to the span of the Hermite polynomials of degree one, precisely

L_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\xi_{i},

where
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
are defined as

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\xi_{i}\cdot M_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}})d\gamma_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{|\xi_{i}|}{2}\cdot(M_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}_{+}^{i}))-M_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}_{-}^{i}))d\gamma_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}})

and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}_{\pm}^{i}=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\pm|\xi_{i}|,\ldots,\xi_{n})
.
Note that each
\alpha_{i}
is an interpolator to

(0,\ldots,{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi\sim N(0,1)}|\xi|^{2},\ldots,0)=(0,\ldots,1,\ldots,0).

Now, since our space is of cotype
2
, we apply the
K
-convexity bound which guarantees that

\ell(M_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}})^{2}\gtrsim K_{\|\cdot\|}^{-2}\cdot\ell(L_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}})^{2},

or equivalently,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\left[\left\|\widehat{f}_{n}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\left[\widehat{f}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\right]\right\|^{2}\right]\gtrsim K_{\|\cdot\|}^{-2}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\alpha_{i}\xi_{i}\|^{2}.

Taking expectation over
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
the left hand side gives the conditional variance. Next, using the cotype
2
-property and Jensen’s inequality, we obtain that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\left[\left\|\widehat{f}_{n}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\left[\widehat{f}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\right]\right\|^{2}\right]\gtrsim t^{2}\cdot K_{\|\cdot\|}^{-2}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\alpha_{i}\|^{2}.

As,
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
depend on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
, we take expectation over
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
, we know that

{\mathbb{E}}\|\alpha_{1}\|^{2}=\ldots={\mathbb{E}}\|\alpha_{i}\|^{2}=\ldots={\mathbb{E}}\|\alpha_{n}\|^{2}

and therefore by Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var_{\|\cdot\|}}\left[{\widehat{f}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Var_{\|\cdot\|}}\left[{\widehat{f}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}\right]\gtrsim n\cdot K_{\|\cdot\|}^{-2}\cdot t^{2}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}\|\alpha_{1}\|^{2}\geq n\cdot K_{\|\cdot\|}^{-2}\cdot t^{2}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}\|\widehat{f}_{X}\|^{2},

where in the last inequality we used the definition of the minimum norm solution.

Finally, we prove the right-hand side of the bound. First, we consider by
f^{*}\equiv 0
, and using Borell’s lemma (cf.
17
)

\mathrm{Var_{\|\cdot\|}}\left[{\widehat{f}_{n}}\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}\ell^{2}(\widehat{f}_{n})={\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}\int\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{n}^{2}d\gamma_{n}\lesssim{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}\left(\int\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{n}d\gamma_{n}\right)^{2}\lesssim M_{n}(\mathcal{F})^{2},

and the claim follows when
f^{*}\equiv 0
.
Finally, under Assumption
2
, we conclude that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\|\widehat{f}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f^{*}}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}})\|^{2}\lesssim{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\|_{n}^{2}

and as
\|{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\widehat{f}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f^{*}}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}})\|\lesssim M(\mathcal{F}_{n})
by simply using Jensen’s, and taking expectation over
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
, the proof is complete.
∎

7.4.1
Proof of Theorem
3

In what follows, recall that
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{P}}
denotes the
L_{2}(\mathbb{P})
norm. Here, we lower bound the expected conditional variance of
\widehat{f}_{n}
, that is

{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{f}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})}.

Following Theorem
6
, we consider an operator that is defined for every realization of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
, denoted by
M_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}
, defined via

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}\mapsto\widehat{f}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\widehat{f}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}.

And we will inspect it with respect to different two norms
\|\cdot\|
,
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{P}}
and lower bound the expected conditional variance. More accurately, first we linearize the operator
M_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}
, to obtain
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
(that depend on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
) defined by

\alpha_{i}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{|\xi_{i}|}{2}\cdot(\widehat{f}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f^{*}}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}_{+}^{i}))-\widehat{f}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f^{*}}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}_{-}^{i}))d\gamma_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}),

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}_{\pm}^{i}=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\pm|\xi_{i}|,\ldots,\xi_{n})
. Using Theorem
6
, and that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i.i.d.

{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}\|\alpha_{i}\|^{2}={\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}\|\alpha_{i}\|^{2}\lesssim(t^{-1}/\sqrt{n})^{2}\cdot K_{\|\cdot\|}\cdot M_{n}(\mathcal{F})^{2}

Then, by Jensen’s inequality, we obtain that

{\mathbb{E}}\|\alpha_{i}\|\lesssim\frac{t^{-1}\cdot K_{\|\cdot\|}}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot M_{n}(\mathcal{F}):=\frac{t_{\|\cdot\|}^{-1}}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot M_{n}(\mathcal{F}).

The linearization of
M_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}
writes

\widetilde{L}_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\xi_{i}

and note that for each realization of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}
, it holds

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\widetilde{L_{n}}}}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}.

As
K_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{P}}}=1
(since it is Hilbert space), we obtain that

{\mathbb{E}}\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{f}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})={\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\|\widehat{f}_{n}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}}\widehat{f}_{n}(\mathcal{D})\|_{\mathbb{P}}^{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\right]\geq{\mathbb{E}}\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\xi_{i}\|_{\mathbb{P}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}\|\alpha_{i}\|_{\mathbb{P}}^{2}=n\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\|\alpha_{1}\|_{\mathbb{P}}^{2}.

where in the last inequality we used that
n
samples are
(X_{i},\xi_{i})
are independent and identically distributed.

Now, recall that for every realization
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
, we have that

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{i}=(0,\ldots,1,\ldots,0),

and that
{\mathbb{E}}\|\alpha_{i}\|\lesssim\frac{t_{\|\cdot\|}^{-1}\cdot M(\mathcal{F}_{n})}{\sqrt{n}}
. Therefore, by Markov’s inequality,
\alpha_{1}\in r\cdot\mathcal{F}
with probability at least
3/4
for
r=c\cdot\frac{t_{\|\cdot\|}^{-1}\cdot M(\mathcal{F}_{n})}{\sqrt{n}}
for an appropriate
c>0
. Thus, with probability at least
3/4
,
\alpha_{1}\in r\cdot\mathcal{F}
, which implies that:

{\mathbb{E}}\|\alpha_{1}\|_{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\gtrsim\Psi_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{e_{1}}},\frac{C\cdot t_{\|\cdot\|}^{-1}M(\mathcal{F}_{n})}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2}.

By combining all that we obtained

{\mathbb{E}}\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{f}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})\gtrsim n\cdot\Psi_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{e_{1}}},C\cdot\frac{K_{\|\cdot\|}\cdot t^{-1}\cdot M_{n}(\mathcal{F})}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2}.

And the claim follows. We conclude the proof with two remarks.
∎

7.4.2
Concluding Remarks

Remark 12

(On Efron-Stein’s type bounds in Banach Spaces)
.

We begin by recalling Efron-Stein’s inequality (cf.
16
) for a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with random inputs
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.

\operatorname{Var}(F(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}))\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[(F(X_{-i},X_{i})-F(X_{-i},X_{i}^{\prime}))^{2}\right],

where
X_{i}^{\prime}
is independent copy of
X_{i}
.

Using the classical observations of
23
;
24
, known in these days as the Enflo’s type property, where it is shown that for any non-linear operator
F:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to(\mathcal{Z},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}})
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
is a Hilbert space that

\mathrm{Var}_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}}(F)\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|(F(\epsilon_{-i},\epsilon_{i})-F(\epsilon_{-i},-\epsilon_{i}))^{2}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right]

here randomness is with respect to the uniform measure over the hypercube. Later,
66
extended this to any type
2
norm – with a price of a
\log(d)
-factor. Finally, in the recent paper of
35
, they removed the
\log(d)
-factor by showing that for any type
2
norm

{\mathbb{E}}\|F(\epsilon)-F(-\epsilon)\|^{2}\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|(F(\epsilon_{-i},\epsilon_{i})-F(\epsilon_{-i},-\epsilon_{i}))\|^{2}\right].

Unfortunately, the most similar version for such bound for
CO(2)
-spaces
52
, is not applicable in our setting. Yet, Pisier’s
K
-convexity constant (for Rademacher or Gaussian noise) is the closest equivalent version to a “reverse Efron Stein” inequality, as it implies that

\log(1+d_{BM}(\|\cdot\|,\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}))^{-1}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\mathbb{E}}_{-i}\left[F(\epsilon_{-i},\epsilon_{i})-F(\epsilon_{-i},-\epsilon_{i})\right]\|^{2}\lesssim\mathrm{Var}_{\|\cdot\|}(F).

∎
