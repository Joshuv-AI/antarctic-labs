 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The Subsurface Tunnel Imaging LeveraGed by Analysis of Rayleigh wave ellipticity (STILGAR) project
demonstrated an integrated geophysical approach for detecting, locating, and characterizing underground
structural changes using dense seismic arrays and advanced inversion techniques. Field campaigns were
conducted at two operational mines—the Redmond salt mine (Utah) and Graymont Pleasant Gap
limestone mine (Pennsylvania)—providing real-world testbeds for monitoring anthropogenic subsurface
activity. At the Redmond salt mine, seismic interferometry combined with back-projection inversion
successfully identified continuous, low-amplitude signals from mining operations. The approach
differentiated stationary from migrating anthropogenic sources, captured daily operational cycles, and
validated the potential of passive seismic monitoring for remote detection of underground activity. At the
Graymont Pleasant Gap mine, two dense seismic deployments in the spring and fall of 2023 generated
over 4 TB of high-resolution data. Key outcomes included the relocation of 199 underground and 8
surface explosions with accuracies within tens of meters and the development of a 3D P-wave velocity
model using the triple-difference tomography algorithm (tomoTD) that resolved major structural features
such as the mine entrance, low-velocity tunnels, and roof-collapse areas. Ambient noise cross-correlation
and back-projection analyses revealed persistent sources linked to ongoing mining activity, whereas
horizontal-to-vertical spectral ratio (HVSR) and ellipticity studies confirmed stable site responses across
seasons and identified soil thickness trends consistent with regional erosional and depositional processes.
Checkerboard and sensitivity tests further validated the robustness of the tomographic results.

Overall, the findings emphasize that although significant progress has been made in subsurface imaging,
further work is needed to enhance the detection and localization of underground structures. Accurate
imaging requires higher frequencies, yet anthropogenic sources tend to dominate the seismic record at
those frequencies, and high-frequency surface waves are affected by higher modes that complicate
interpretation. The improved detection and localization of human-induced signals enabled detailed
temporal and spatial mapping of daily mine operations, demonstrating the feasibility of continuous
anthropogenic source monitoring. Sensitivity to signals from nontraditional sources, such as fan
operations, highlights the broader applicability of this approach to other industrial environments in which
continuous and impulsive signals are present.

The field campaigns produced a substantial volume of high-quality seismic data, supporting the
development and testing of new methods for seismic source characterization and subsurface imaging.
Future deployments should include sensors capable of recording lower frequencies to probe deeper
structures, increase bandwidth to enhance resolution and sensitivity to both shallow and deep targets, and
collect additional large-scale datasets to refine imaging and source characterization techniques. Moreover,
conducting 3D modeling studies of seismic wavefields at higher frequencies will provide a better
understanding of wave scattering and cavity–wavefield interactions in complex underground
environments.

In conclusion, the STILGAR project demonstrated that integrated seismic monitoring can effectively
characterize underground operations, capturing both natural and anthropogenic signals. The approaches
developed provide a foundation for improved detection, localization, and imaging of subsurface structures
and are directly transferable to broader industrial monitoring applications.

 1. PROJECT DESCRIPTION

Detecting and locating underground structures of varying sizes and depths remains a significant
challenge, as no existing sensor network can directly and precisely confirm their presence. Nevertheless,
temporal changes in the subsurface—caused by excavation, collapse, or other anthropogenic processes—
can be inferred through geophysical signals observable across multiple spatial and temporal scales
(Maceira et al., 2021). Building on prior work that demonstrated these effects in seismic and gravity data,
we developed a robust multivariate inversion framework capable of identifying such underground
changes.

The Subsurface Tunnel Imaging LeveraGed by Analysis of Rayleigh wave ellipticity (STILGAR) project
served as a real-world capability demonstration, carried out at two active mine sites in Utah and
Pennsylvania. By instrumenting and monitoring these environments, we validated our methodology for
detecting, locating, and characterizing subsurface structural changes, independently of local geological
complexity.

A key focus of this effort was the integration of dense observational arrays with site-specific geological
and structural information. This combined approach enhanced our ability to isolate and interpret
anthropogenic seismic signals. The tools and methods developed through STILGAR have direct
applications for national security, offering novel and effective means for monitoring subsurface activity in
support of nonproliferation and underground facility detection missions.

 3. REDMOND SALT MINE

We investigated how seismic interferometry can be used to monitor human activity in industrial and
subsurface environments. We used the Redmond salt mine in Utah (Downey et al., 2022) as a controlled
analog to test methods for detecting and locating continuous, low-amplitude seismic sources produced by
human operations such as heavy machinery use and blast preparations.

At the Redmond site, 15 three-component nodal seismometers were deployed across the surface above the
underground mine workings for 32 days in 2018 (Error! Reference source not found.). Each node
recorded 12 h of continuous data nightly—between 7:00 p.m. and 7:00 a.m. local time—coinciding with
the mine’s work schedule and blasting operations. Instead of focusing on impulsive events such as
individual blasts, we analyzed persistent vibrations by cross-correlating seismic noise between all station
pairs—a technique known as seismic interferometry. This approach enables the extraction of coherent
energy generated by continuous human activity, allowing its temporal variation and approximate location
to be determined.

The cross-correlation results revealed clear, cyclic patterns that repeated with the mine’s work schedule
(Error! Reference source not found.). During weekends, when the mine was inactive, the data
contained only low-amplitude background noise. On weekdays, however, two distinct types of persistent
signals emerged. The first, which appeared in the early evenings before scheduled blasting, exhibited
dominant frequencies between 1 and 4 Hz and was termed the pre-blast signal. The second, which
appeared after blasting and persisted for several hours into the morning, had higher frequency content
greater than 8 Hz and was termed the post-blast signal. The distinct timing and frequency ranges of these
signals suggested that each was associated with a different phase of mining activity.

To determine the source of these signals, we applied a modified back-projection and inversion method.
By mapping the amplitude envelopes of the cross-correlation functions (CCFs) into 3D space and solving
them with a nonnegative least-squares algorithm, we identified the likely locations of the continuous
sources. This improved inversion approach reduced spatial smearing and was able to separate multiple
simultaneous sources. Two propagation velocities best matched the data: 1,500 m/s for the pre-blast
signal, consistent with surface wave propagation, and 340 m/s for the post-blast signal, consistent with an
air-coupled source.

The resulting source locations (Error! Reference source not found. and Error! Reference source not
found.) showed that pre-blast seismic energy clustered near the active mining drifts where blast
preparations were occurring, closely matching documented blast zones. These pre-blast signals were
therefore interpreted as originating from machinery or excavation activities associated with blast
preparation. In contrast, the post-blast signal was consistently localized at the surface near the mine’s
northern exit portal. Its stationary position, high-frequency content, and air-wave propagation velocity
pointed to a large industrial ventilation fan as the source.

When the analysis was extended over the full 32 day period, the pre-blast sources migrated gradually,
tracking the advance of mining drifts, while the post-blast source remained fixed, reinforcing its
association with the fan. The close spatial and temporal correspondence between seismic energy and
documented operations confirmed that these persistent signals were anthropogenic and could serve as
reliable indicators of underground activity.

The results demonstrate that seismic interferometry, combined with back-projection inversion, provides a
powerful, passive, and noninvasive means of monitoring industrial operations. Unlike event-based
seismic methods that require impulsive signals, this approach can detect continuous, low-level vibrations
generated by machinery and infrastructure. It provides both temporal and spatial resolution of activity,
even without prior knowledge of a site’s layout or operator cooperation. In settings where direct access is
limited or cooperation is not possible, such as sensitive subsurface facilities, this technique offers an
effective monitoring tool.

At the Redmond mine, the approach successfully distinguished between stationary and migrating
anthropogenic sources, captured the timing and nature of daily operations, and demonstrated that subtle,
repetitive noise signatures can reveal detailed information about industrial activity. By showing that
surface seismic networks, when analyzed interferometrically, can resolve dynamic human processes
underground, this work provides a framework for future monitoring and verification applications in
complex or restricted environments.

 4. PLEASANT GAP MINE: FIELD DEPLOYMENTS

We performed two dense seismic network experiments at the Graymont limestone mine in Pleasant Gap,
Pennsylvania (Error! Reference source not found.) during the spring and fall of 2023. The active mine,
located in central Pennsylvania within the Valley and Ridge province, primarily consists of shale,
limestone, and dolomite formations (Berg et al., 1980). The underground workings form a complex
system of tunnels and pillars that gradually deepen from northwest to southeast, reaching depths of
approximately 600 m below ground. The mine extends beneath a rising ridgeline to the southeast, so that
increasing surface elevation corresponds to greater subsurface depth. Active surface pit mining operations
are present at two sites situated in the northeast and southwest portions of the array.

Information on blasting and mining activities was provided by mine personnel and integrated with
seismic observations to develop a ground-truth event database. Deployment and retrieval operations were
carried out by teams from Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), Sandia National Laboratories, and
Pennsylvania State University. Details on the instrumentation, deployment conditions, network
configuration, and data collection are summarized in Table 1.

4.1 NETWORK DESIGN

Site accessibility and safety constraints strongly influenced the seismic array deployment design. Land
access restrictions and dense vegetation along the southern and southwestern ridgelines limited station
placement above the deepest portions of the mine. Deployments within or immediately adjacent to the
two active pit mines were prohibited, and active farming in the northern and northeastern areas posed a
risk of equipment damage, so no stations were installed in cultivated fields. Previous roof collapses in the
northeastern section also created safety hazards, making those areas off-limits. Considering these

 7

constraints, most stations were installed along existing roads; several smaller subarrays were positioned in
open, non-farming fields. A semipermanent station was also deployed in the mine’s core shed.

Two surface seismic arrays were deployed—one in the spring and one in the fall of 2023—covering much
of the mine’s footprint, with additional stations located to the northeast and west. Station spacing ranged
from 100 to 400 m to support high-resolution imaging and event location. For effective ambient noise
imaging, interstation spacing should exceed twice the wavelength of the target surface waves, which
depends on subsurface depth, frequency, and seismic velocity (Lin et al., 2008). To image structures to a
depth of approximately 0.5 km, we estimated an optimal spacing of 100–500 m, which is also suitable for
passive imaging, anthropogenic noise back-projection, and travel-time tomography. Each campaign
included three to four smaller subarrays with 10–15 m spacing (Error! Reference source not found. aa–
ab and ba–bc) to assess measurement consistency, improve source location accuracy, and evaluate data
quality.

Three-component seismic data were recorded continuously at all stations. In the first campaign, Fairfield
nodal seismometers recorded at 500 Hz, while Geospace sensors with digitizers recorded at 1,000 Hz,
producing about 1.8 TB of data. In the second campaign, all stations recorded at 1,000 Hz, generating
about 2.5 TB (Table 1). High sampling rates enabled continuous monitoring of subsurface and mining
activities.

Campaign 1 included one semipermanent and 95 temporary stations deployed May 2–4, 2023, and
retrieved June 5–8, 2023. Stations recorded continuously for up to 24 days. The array comprised 84
Fairfield nodal seismometers and 11 Geospace GS-ONE sensors with GSX4 digitizers. Three smaller
subarrays were installed south, north, and northeast of the core shed (Error! Reference source not
found. aa–ab). Despite saturated ground conditions, all equipment was successfully recovered, and no tilt
or repositioning issues were observed. One station (713) stopped recording prematurely on May 28, 2023;
all others recorded as long as expected, although 22 ceased recording before retrieval because of battery
depletion.

Campaign 2 reused the semipermanent station and deployed 88 temporary stations between October
9 and 11, 2023; they were retrieved November 6–8, 2023. The array largely overlapped Campaign 1 to
expand the dataset and evaluate seasonal effects. The temporary stations included 53 Fairfield nodal
seismometers and 35 Geospace sensors—27 with GSX3 and 8 with GSX4 digitizers. Four smaller
subarrays were installed: two near the core shed, one in the northeast crossing a shallow portion of the
mine, and one in the southwest across another shallow section (Error! Reference source not found. ba–
bc). All equipment was successfully recovered, with no reported instrument issues.

 5. DATA GATHERING, PREPROCESSING, AND STORAGE

All project data are stored in the Compute and Data Environment for Science (CADES) at ORNL.

Seismic data from the Redmond salt mine, including both nodal and broadband recordings, were saved as
standard daylong miniSEED files per station. These files and their associated instrument responses were
uploaded to CADES via the free and secure Globus high-throughput file transfer system. The raw seismic
data were corrected to remove instrument responses, and 5 min segments were cross-correlated between
all station component pairs to generate three-component CCFs. Daily collections of these functions were
stored in Python data structures and archived on the ORNL-hosted server.

The 4.4 TB of continuous seismic data from the Pleasant Gap limestone mine, also in MiniSEED format,
were published through ORNL Constellation and the US Department of Energy Office of Scientific and

 8

Technical Information (OSTI) (Cunningham et al., 2025). Geospace stations recorded hour-long files per
component, whereas Fairfield stations recorded daylong files. Data access is provided through Globus.

Instrument response correction was required to recover the true physical signal (displacement, velocity, or
acceleration) and ensure amplitude consistency across instrument types. Corrections were applied using
station XML files (Fairfield) and RESP files (Geospace and Nanometrics Centaur) via the ObsPy module
(Beyreuther et al., 2010) or Seismic Analysis Code (SAC) (Goldstein et al., 2003). Geospace digitizers
record in millivolts, whereas Fairfield nodal stations require a factor-of-eight amplitude correction to
compensate for a gain initialization issue. After correction, colocated Fairfield and Geospace stations
(<0.5 m apart) show consistent amplitude and phase responses (Error! Reference source not found.),
confirming the reliability of the correction process.

6.1 TEMPORAL AND SPECTRAL CHARACTERISTICS OF THE SEISMIC WAVEFIELD

We investigated the general spatiotemporal characteristics of the elastic wavefield at Pleasant Gap mine
using data from the May 2023 deployment. Our analysis focuses on the temporal and spectral features of
persistent signals, and we propose a framework for source localizations based on signal attenuation. We
selected a subset of 45 stations; these were chosen by enforcing a minimum separation of 100 m between
stations. This criterion helps mitigate spatial heterogeneity bias in the deployment. The stations were
arranged sequentially from east to west. Two key trends are evident in Error! Reference source not
found.: (1) increasing noise levels from east to west, and (2) a distinct weekday–weekend pattern in the
noise levels.

6.3 CROSS-CORRELATION BACK-PROJECTION

We obtained source locations for the primary cross-correlation signals observed over the Pleasant Gap
array. We refined our stacking velocity estimate to match local geology and located the primary cross-
correlation signal at a 5 min interval for an example day of mine activity. The pattern of locations
indicates a shift from activity at the plant to haul trucks along the access road to the underground rock
crusher and back to the plant over 24 h cycles. Thus, we demonstrated the ability to observe multiple
types of nonimpulsive, anthropogenic seismic signals and track their evolution on a minute-to-minute
timescale.

Stacking Velocity. Using the data from May 4, 2023, as an example working day at the mine, we refined
our estimate of the stacking velocity needed for robust locations of the primary seismic arrival. Testing a
set of velocities from 340 m/s (speed of sound in air) up to 3,500 m/s (a typical hard rock velocity), we
found that 3,000 m/s produced reasonable locations laterally and in depth. A stacking velocity of
3,000 m/s aligns with the S-wave velocity obtained both from core sample testing results in the literature
and from locating mining blasts during the array deployment.

Back-Projection. We bandpass-filtered the cross-correlations in three frequency bands (1–10, 10–20, and
20–40 Hz) to capture the high-amplitude noise that was found to cycle over 24 h. We back-projected each
5 min cross-correlation for the 24 h spanning May 4, 2023, starting at UTC 00:00. We combined all
location estimates colored by time to show the evolution of the primary noise source over 24 h at 1–
10 Hz, 10–20 Hz and 20–40 Hz (Error! Reference source not found.).

Location Analysis. We hypothesize that machinery operations surrounding the plant, including crushing
and transporting rock, are the primary driver of high-amplitude cross-correlation energy. Before the start
of the workday, activity is concentrated around the continuously operating surface processing plant and
kiln in the northwest. At UTC 02:20, activity briefly moves to the far right edge of the mine, consistent
with the underground blasting time for this day. At UTC 09:10, the source location moves to the bottom
of the surface pit and alternates between the pit, either the southwest processing area or the underground
rock crusher, and the northwest processing plant. The southwest processing area signal is evident only in
the 1–10 Hz band, and the underground rock crusher is evident only in the higher-frequency bands. Mine
haul trucks move intermittently between the surface pit and the surface southwest facilities along a
connecting road, and we expect that locations associated with these areas are a result of haul truck
movement. In between hypothesized truck movement or the underground rock crusher operation, the
noise source shifts back to the processing plant. At UTC 18:20, the noise source moves back to the
processing plant for the remainder of the day. In the absence of other sources during normal shift hours
(~UTC 09:00/EDT 05:00 to ~UTC 19:00/EDT 15:00), the processing plant appears to generally dominate
the noise record.

6.4 AMBIENT NOISE SURFACE WAVE DISPERSION

We used ambient noise interferometry to measure surface wave dispersion curves, a key input for the
modeling task. Following the method of Bensen et al. (2007), we adjusted parameters to target high
frequencies sensitive to shallow (<1 km) subsurface structures. Using data from the initial May 2023
deployment, we calculated vertical cross-correlations for 45 well-spaced station pairs (Error! Reference
source not found. [left]; 1,035 total) during quiet hours (12:00–4:00 a.m. local time), minimizing
contamination from anthropogenic noise at the mine. We applied 1 bit time normalization and spectral
whitening to mitigate impulsive signals and computed cross-correlations using 300 s windows with a 100
s overlap. The vertical cross-correlations were then linearly stacked for each pair.

 16

Given our interest in shallow subsurface structures, we focused on frequencies between 1 and 10 Hz,
much higher than those used in traditional ambient noise analyses. The array-stacked results for all station
pairs, binned by distance in 0.01 km increments, are shown in Error! Reference source not found..
Coherent signals are symmetric and observed at offsets greater than 3 km. We also analyzed cross-
correlations from individual stations and noted persistent localized sources from mine operations,
particularly at high frequencies, which can bias surface wave dispersion measurements.

 Figure 15. Stacked cross-correlations for 1,035 station pairs (45 stations) filtered between 1 and 10 Hz. The

Surface waves are inherently dispersive—different frequencies probe different depths and therefore travel
at different velocities. To extract group velocities and arrival times, we applied our Surface Wave
Multiple Filter Analysis software to the ambient noise cross-correlations. Synthetic forward modeling
confirmed that frequencies between 1–10 Hz are sensitive to the subsurface structure at our target depths.
Dispersion curves were measured for all interstation paths; however, several results were affected by
localized noise sources and/or interference from higher-mode Rayleigh waves.

To evaluate measurement reliability, we assigned a qualitative quality factor to each dispersion curve
based on attributes such as signal amplitude and variance. Only 30%–40% of the 1,035 paths yielded
dispersion curves of acceptable quality. The absence of spatial clustering among poor measurements
suggests that the issues were not tied to specific stations. A closer examination of four comparable station
pairs (Error! Reference source not found.) indicates that, although the measurements appear broadly
consistent, distinguishing between fundamental and higher-mode Rayleigh waves remains challenging.
Higher modes can overlap with or obscure the fundamental mode, particularly in areas with
heterogeneous noise sources or shallow high-velocity contrasts—such as near the Graymont Pleasant Gap
mine. Accurate identification and characterization of both modes are essential for constraining the shear
wave velocity structure.

Given the limitations of ambient noise interferometry in this setting, we also investigated the use of
surface wave dispersion from nearby mining blasts. However, the complex nature of blast events—their
dominant body wave energy, variable frequency content, and close proximity to some stations—made
surface waves difficult to isolate and often contaminated by body wave arrivals. Consequently, no reliable
surface wave dispersion data were incorporated into the subsurface inversion.

6.5 ELLIPTICITY

6.5.1 Horizontal-to-Vertical Spectral Ratio Analysis

To enhance data constraints on the subsurface structure, we calculated HVSR measurements using
continuous seismic data collected during the first field deployment in May 2023 at the Pleasant Gap mine.
HVSR analysis is sensitive to near-surface features, particularly shear wave velocity contrasts, and
provides an empirical estimate of site resonance frequencies.

For each seismic station, the continuous three-component data (north–south, east–west, and vertical) were
divided into equal-length time segments. The spectrum of each component was computed using a fast
Fourier transform. The north–south and east–west spectra were then averaged to obtain the horizontal
spectrum, and both horizontal and vertical spectra were smoothed to reduce random noise. The HVSR for
each segment was calculated as the ratio of the horizontal to the vertical spectrum, and the resulting
HVSR measurements were averaged over all available segments for each station.

Although HVSR is an empirical method, its spectral peaks are largely controlled by Rayleigh wave
ellipticity—the frequency-dependent ratio of horizontal to vertical motion in Rayleigh wave particle
orbits. When the ambient noise field is dominated by Rayleigh waves, the HVSR curve effectively
approximates the theoretical Rayleigh wave ellipticity function. Accordingly, we interpret HVSR as a
proxy for Rayleigh wave ellipticity; this approach enables us to extract information on shear wave
velocity contrasts and resonance behavior without requiring full-waveform modeling. Comparison of

 18

observed HVSR curves with theoretical ellipticity estimates helps validate the velocity structure and
identify the frequencies most sensitive to shallow subsurface variations.

Error! Reference source not found. shows that the HVSR measurements at different stations exhibit
distinct spectral patterns. Some stations that are close to each other display similar HVSR signatures,
suggesting comparable subsurface structures beneath them. We also examined HVSR variability over
diurnal time scales by comparing measurements computed for each hour of the day. The HVSR curves for
the same station were largely consistent throughout the day, indicating stable site response characteristics.
However, differences were evident in certain frequency ranges between daytime and nighttime
measurements, likely reflecting contamination from persistent anthropogenic noise sources such as

6.5.2 Inversion for Shallow Geological Structure

Surface wave ellipticity measurements were made at 84 stations across frequencies ranging from 1 to
30 Hz. Single-layer Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) inversions of these measurements constrain
variations in the sediment layer directly beneath each seismic station. The resulting single-station, single-

 19

layer inversions contribute to a shallow seismic model of the region above the Graymont Pleasant Gap
mine.

We estimated Rayleigh-wave ellipticity using the Rayleigh wave random decrement (RayDec) method
(Hobiger et al, 2009, Error! Reference source not found.) applied to observations from the early
morning hours of both the spring and fall 2023 deployments. Before processing all the observations, we
compared the measurements for several stations at different times of day and found that the quiet times
produced the smoothest histograms of the measurements.

 Figure 18. The algorithm used to implement ellipticity measurements using the RayDec method.

The subsurface structure at the Graymont Pleasant Gap mine consists of a shallow soil layer overlying
limestone bedrock. This structure can be approximated using a layer over half-space model, in which the
peak frequency of ellipticity measurements constrains the thickness, density, and seismic velocities of the
soil layer. The misfit was evaluated using an MCMC approach with a chain length of 20,000 models
(Error! Reference source not found.). The ellipticity spectrum shows a good fit; the overall pattern,
smoothness, and maximum values are well-matched. The model is relatively simple, featuring a uniform
soil layer over a basement with two transition layers underlain by a half-space with a P-wave speed of 6.0
km/s. The search algorithm estimates only the soil layer properties, and the basement remains unchanged.
From each search, we obtain a chain of models and evaluate the best-fitting ones. Although density
constraints are weak, the ellipticity spectrum’s shape generally provides strong constraints on soil layer
thickness and shear wave speed and fair to good constraints on P-wave speed, possibly aided by its
relationship to S-wave speed.

Ellipticity measurements were inverted for both the 1–20 and 1–30 Hz frequency bands. We hypothesize
that the 1–20 Hz band may better capture the fundamental mode, informing our inversion approach. The
data from the two deployments provided an opportunity to evaluate the consistency of the procedure. The
results indicate strong consistency between the spring and fall observations (Error! Reference source
not found.).

 7. INVERSION AND MODELING

The Graymont Pleasant Gap mine is an active limestone operation that is undergoing continuous drift
expansion. Controlled blasting is carried out at the tunnel faces every few days to advance excavation and
expose fresh rock from the host formation. These blasts, which typically occur between 10:00 p.m. and
12:00 a.m., also provide valuable opportunities to investigate local geological conditions, particularly
within the near-surface zone.

Each blast generates P-wave energy that propagates through the subsurface, enabling measurements of
P-wave travel times between the blast locations and the dense surface seismic array. These data were used
to construct a 3D seismic P-wave velocity model using the triple-difference tomography algorithm
(tomoTD). This approach successfully resolved key geological features, including the seismic expression
of the mine’s tunnel entrance, the low-velocity zone associated with subsurface mine workings, and the
locations and depths of previous rockfalls in the northeastern section of the underground mine.

7.1 LOCATING UNDERGROUND BLASTS

The first step in utilizing the underground blast data was to accurately determine the source locations.
Although the mine provided nominal coordinates for each blast, these positions were refined using our
seismic observations, with particular attention to explosion timing. The updated locations represented a
substantial improvement over the mine’s reported values; however, several events remained difficult to
constrain precisely. Many blasts occurred outside the geographic footprint of the seismic array,
introducing trade-offs among latitude, longitude, depth, and origin time that complicated accurate event

localization. In addition, some tunnel faces are separated by less than 25 m, adding the challenge of
distinguishing between closely spaced events.

7.2 TOMOGRAPHY METHODOLOGY

P-wave arrival time picks and event catalogs were jointly inverted to determine event locations and a 3D
P‑wave velocity model using tomoTD (Guo and Thurber 2021, Guo et al. 2021). tomoTD extends the
widely used double-difference tomography algorithm (tomoDD) (Zhang and Thurber 2003) by
incorporating both absolute arrival times and multiple types of differential time (dt) data, which together
improve the resolution of absolute and relative event locations as well as subsurface velocity structure.

The dt data are derived from event catalogs (initial times and locations) and P-wave picks at each station.
Absolute arrival times constrain absolute event locations and large-scale velocity features, whereas station
pair dt data are sensitive to the velocity beneath stations, and event pair dt data constrain relative event

 25

locations and local velocity variations. By combining these data, tomoTD yields high-precision absolute
and relative locations and high-resolution velocity models beneath the seismic array.

Accurate initial event locations and velocity models are critical because tomoTD linearizes the nonlinear
location and tomography problem. Errors in either parameter can propagate, given the strong coupling
between event locations and the velocity field. In addition, event pair dt data are formed by linking nearby
events, so large initial mislocations can introduce significant errors.

To improve accuracy, we conducted multiple inversion iterations. Because the blast locations were
relatively well constrained, we first inverted for the P-wave velocity model alone, then performed a
second joint inversion for both event location and velocity structure using the updated model.

A hierarchical weighting strategy was applied to integrate the datasets effectively. In the first stage,
absolute arrival times were given higher weight (1 for absolute data, 0.1 for catalog dt) to constrain large-
scale structure and absolute locations. In the second stage, catalog dt data were given higher weight (1 for
dt, 0.1 for absolute) to refine relative event locations and small-scale velocity variations.

The blast events and seismic stations at the Graymont Pleasant Gap mine provide excellent coverage for
imaging the subsurface mine structure, particularly in the northeastern sector (Error! Reference source
not found.). P-wave arrival times aligned with a fast 6.4–6.5 km/s move-out, consistent with expected
velocities for limestone; these arrival times were used as the basis for the initial P-wave velocity model.

Tomographic inversion parameters were carefully selected to balance resolution and stability. The
inversion was performed on a 2 × 2 km grid with 0.05 km spacing in both the horizontal and vertical
directions across 15 depth slices, providing sufficient ray path coverage within the mine footprint. An
L-curve analysis, based on 35 trial inversions with damping values of 2–100 and smoothing values of
0.2–10, was used to identify optimal parameters—a damping value of 100 and smoothing value of 0.5.

Model resolution was evaluated using checkerboard tests introducing ±5% seismic P-wave velocity VP
anomalies at three scales: 0.1, 0.5, and 1 km. Synthetic absolute travel time and catalog dt data were
generated from these true models and inverted using the same parameters as were used for the real data.

mine, coinciding in both depth and location with zones of roof collapse and subsurface subsidence
identified on the bedrock map.

 8. SUMMARY AND RECOMMENDATIONS

The STILGAR project aimed to demonstrate an integrated geophysical approach for detecting, locating,
and characterizing underground structural changes using dense seismic arrays and advanced inversion
techniques. Field campaigns were successfully conducted at two operational mines—Redmond salt mine
(Utah) and Graymont Pleasant Gap limestone mine (Pennsylvania)—providing real-world test beds for
monitoring anthropogenic subsurface activity.

At the Redmond salt mine, seismic interferometry combined with back-projection inversion was used to
effectively identify continuous, low-amplitude signals from mining operations. This analysis
distinguished between stationary and migrating anthropogenic sources, captured daily operational cycles,
and validated the potential of passive seismic monitoring for remotely detecting underground activity.

At the Graymont Pleasant Gap mine, two dense seismic deployments in spring and fall 2023 generated
over 4 TB of high-resolution data, supporting a comprehensive suite of analyses:

 Explosion relocation: 199 underground and 8 surface explosions were relocated, achieving source
 accuracy within tens of meters.

 Tomography: A 3D P-wave velocity model was developed using tomoTD, resolving key structural
 features such as the mine entrance, low-velocity tunnel zones, and roof-collapse areas.

 28

 Ambient noise back-projection: Cross-correlation analyses identified persistent seismic sources
 linked to mining operations.

 HVSR and ellipticity analyses: These analyses revealed stable site responses, consistent shallow-
 structure models across seasons, and soil thickness trends consistent with erosional and depositional
 processes along Mount Nittany Ridge.

 Model validation: Checkerboard tests and sensitivity analyses confirmed tomographic robustness
 against variations in initial model parameters.

Key takeaways include the following:

1. Subsurface imaging improvements: Further work is needed to enhance detection and localization of
 underground structures. Achieving accurate results requires higher frequencies than initially
 anticipated, although anthropogenic sources dominate the seismic record at these frequencies. High-
 frequency surface waves are further complicated by the influence of higher modes.

2. Anthropogenic source characterization: Detection and localization of human-induced seismic
 sources improved, enabling detailed temporal and spatial mapping of daily operations.

The project’s success in monitoring mining activity suggests broader applicability for industrial
monitoring. Seismic records revealed sensitivity to nontraditional sources, such as fan operation,
highlighting the value of instrumenting other installations in the Office of Defense Nuclear
Nonproliferation Research and Development (NA-22) portfolio where signals are generated both
continuously and impulsively.

STILGAR field deployments produced a substantial volume of high-quality seismic data suitable for
exploring natural and anthropogenic signals in active underground environments. This dataset supports
the testing of new seismological approaches for source characterization and subsurface imaging. In future
deployments, adding sensors capable of recording lower frequencies and increasing overall bandwidth
should be considered, because resolution in seismological analyses is proportional to bandwidth. Lower
frequencies would have enabled deeper probing, including to mine depths. For instance, near-regional
explosion signals from other central Pennsylvania mines enabled preliminary investigation of phase
velocities across the mine. Although the number of usable events was limited, results at the lower
frequency edge (1 Hz) suggested reduced surface wave speeds near tunnels and revealed interactions
between large mine pits and fundamental surface waves.

Temporal and spatial variations in subsurface structures and associated operational activities can be
detected across multiple data modalities and scales. Seismoacoustic methods have proven particularly
effective in detecting and characterizing underground operations. Recent studies indicate that higher-
frequency signals, beyond those originally considered, may be required to fully image and characterize
tunnels. A scoping study incorporating 3D modeling of seismic wavefields at these frequencies and
spatial scales is recommended to guide future research, offering critical insight into wave scattering and
cavity–wavefield interactions.
