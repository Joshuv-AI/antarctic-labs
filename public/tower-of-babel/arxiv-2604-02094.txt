Importance sampling for Bayesian inference: polynomial-dimension dependent error bounds
Fabián González, Víctor Elvira, Joaquín Míguez

Abstract
Many Bayesian inference problems involve high-dimensional models where the performance of standard importance sampling (IS) methods often degrades rapidly as the dimensionality increases. Classical analyses of IS typically rely on the assumption that observations are arbitrary but fixed (i.e., deterministic), thereby neglecting the probabilistic structure that the Bayesian model induces on the data. In this paper, we adopt the perspective that observations are themselves random variables whose distribution is governed by the underlying model. Within this probabilistic framework, we identify a model-dependent function, referred to as the link function , which connects the fixed- and random-observation formulations. We provide a characterization of the L 2 L^{2} Monte Carlo estimation error: specifically we show that the L 2 L^{2} error bounds are finite and converge at the standard Monte Carlo rate 𝒪 ( N − 1 / 2 ) {\mathcal{O}}(N^{-1/2}) , for arbitrarily large dimension, if and only if the link function is Bochner integrable. This result reveals the fundamental quantity controlling the error of the approximation and establishes a mechanism to manage the dependence on the model state dimension. Consequently, our approach provides a principled way to alleviate the challenges of high dimensionality, offering insights that transcend the worst-case analyses dominant in the existing literature. Finally, we derive explicit analytical examples of the dimensional scaling of the associated errors for several model classes, including linear–Gaussian systems and models with bounded observation functions.

Introduction

Bayesian inference is a cornerstone of modern statistical methodology, widely used in fields such as signal theory, image processing, and machine learning. Many problems in these domains are naturally framed in terms of high-dimensional probabilistic models, where solutions are given as integrals. Unfortunately, this integration can only be performed analytically for a limited set of models for which the prior distribution and the likelihood function are specifically selected to yield a closed-form posterior distribution, as it is the case for a handful of conjugate families
[
5
]
. The obvious alternative is numerical integration
[
32
]
.

1.1
Motivation and background

One widely used approach for handling intractable integrals in Bayesian inference is importance sampling (IS)
[
32
,
37
,
8
]
. IS is a Monte Carlo integration technique that employs a collection of random samples
X=x^{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
, drawn from a proposal probability measure
\nu
to approximate integrals with respect to (w.r.t.) a target probability measure
\pi
. Each sample
x^{i}
is assigned a weight
w^{i}
, which is

•

proportional to the relative density
\frac{{\sf d}\pi}{{\sf d}\nu}(x^{i})
,

•

and normalised,
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{i}=1
.

Under mild assumptions, weighted averages can be proved to converge to the corresponding integrals
[
32
,
11
]
, i.e.,

\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{i}\varphi(x^{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int\varphi(x)\pi({\sf d}x),

where
\varphi(\cdot)
is a test function.

IS is a classic methodology with a plethora of applications
[
16
,
32
,
22
,
29
,
8
]
. It provides a flexible and easy-to-use method for approximating expectations w.r.t. complex probability distributions.
Over the past decade, several advanced IS-based methods,
[
3
,
10
,
13
,
38
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
2
]
have been developed to extend IS and handle inference in increasingly sophisticated models, either static or dynamic. See also
[
1
,
9
]
for a recent account of the fundamentals of IS.

One problem where IS schemes are often applied is Bayesian inference. In this setting, the target probability measure
\pi
is the posterior distribution of a random variable (r.v.)
X
(sometimes termed the state variable, or simply the state) conditional on some r.v.
Y
that represents the observations available in the system of interest. To be specific, the objective is to determine the conditional probability law of
X
given a realization
Y=y
. This is formulated via Bayes’ theorem:

\pi({\sf d}x)=\frac{l_{y}(x)\pi_{0}({\sf d}x)}{\int l_{y}(x^{\prime})\pi_{0}({\sf d}x^{\prime})}=\frac{l_{y}(x)\pi_{0}({\sf d}x)}{\pi_{0}(l_{y})},

where,
l_{y}(x)
denotes the likelihood of the observation
Y=y
given
X=x
,
\pi_{0}({\sf d}x)
is the prior probability measure of the state
X
, and
\pi_{0}(l_{y})=\int l_{y}(x^{\prime})\pi_{0}({\sf d}x^{\prime})
is the marginal likelihood, or model evidence, which acts as the normalization constant for the posterior distribution
\pi({\sf d}x)
.

1.2
Impact of dimension

Despite its versatility, IS is known to suffer from limitations, most notably the so-called
curse of dimensionality

[
1
,
9
,
31
,
35
]
. Theoretical and empirical studies suggest that performance can deteriorate significantly when inference involves high-dimensional state spaces (where the number of scalar r.v.’s or parameters to be inferred is referred to as the dimension of the state space).
For example, in
[
9
]
the authors show that, in general, the number of samples required to approximate the posterior probability law
\pi({\sf d}x)
(within a specified accuracy) can scale exponentially with the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the target and proposal distributions. Also, importance samplers can suffer from weight degeneracy when the dimension of the state space is large
[
4
,
35
,
36
]
. While sophisticated methods have been proposed to overcome this difficulty in various setups
[
25
,
7
,
23
,
31
,
6
,
34
]
, they are often computationally costly and rely on assumptions which may be hard to assess.

Recent works have begun to characterise the multiple roles of dimension in the analysis of IS algorithms. In particular, the authors of
[
1
]
have identified a key quantity governing the efficiency of IS estimators: the second moment of the Radon–Nikodym derivative of the posterior (target) w.r.t. the prior (proposal) measure:

\frac{\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{2})}{\pi_{0}(l_{y})^{2}}

This quantity is further related to important metrics such as the effective sample size (ESS)
[
27
,
20
]
, and it also appears in bounds on the mean squared error of IS approximations
[
2
]
. It plays a key role in our analysis throughout this paper.

Other works have demonstrated that the degradation of performance in high-dimensional settings can be alleviated under certain conditions. Adaptive and structure-aware variants of IS (such as those found in the adaptive particle filtering literature
[
25
,
31
,
6
,
34
,
24
]
and in sequential Monte Carlo samplers
[
7
]
) exploit problem-specific geometric features or sequential data structure to maintain accuracy in moderately high dimensional problems.

1.3
Contributions

In this paper, we investigate the role of the dimension of the state (denoted
d_{x}
) in the
error bounds achieved by IS methods for Bayesian inference. Unlike much of the previous literature, which assumes fixed observations, we treat the data as r.v.s generated by the model.
This approach has been employed in
[
28
]
in the context of study of stability for the optimal filter
[
12
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, it has not been applied to IS schemes or Monte Carlo approximations.
This natural probabilistic setting allows us to define random posterior measures and, correspondingly, random error bounds, enabling the use of functional-analytic and measure-theoretic tools to exploit the model structure.
We first derive
L^{1}
and
L^{2}
error bounds for general Bayesian models under random observations and explicitly identify their dependence on the dimension of the model.
A central theoretical contribution of the paper is a characterization of
the
L^{2}
Monte Carlo error of the IS estimator in this setting. We show that the canonical

\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})
convergence rate holds if and only if a certain
model-dependent function (refereed as the
link function
) introduced in the analysis is square-integrable
with respect to the joint law of the observations and the prior
(formalized later using Bochner integrability). This integrability
condition emerges as the fundamental quantity governing the error of
the approximation. In particular, it provides a direct mechanism to
control the dependence of the error bounds on the state dimension
d_{x}
,
and in some models even on the observation dimension
d_{y}
, as we
explicitly demonstrate in Section
4.1.1
.
Our analysis relies on the notion of Bochner integrability from functional analysis
[
39
]
, which, to our best knowledge, has not been previously used for the study of IS estimators.

We then focus on the dimensional scaling of these errors. For several classes of models, including linear/Gaussian systems, models with pointwise-bounded likelihoods and models with bounded observation functions.
These examples are not artificial or contrived cases, but practically relevant models that arise naturally in a wide range of problems. For instance, sensors that measure physical magnitudes (pressure, velocity, voltage, etc.) typically have a limited range of operation, which translates into bounded observation functions when modelled mathematically. Consequently, these examples are representative of a broad class of settings to which our theoretical findings apply.

To derive these results, we first bridge the gap between abstract functional-analytic conditions and practical Bayesian modeling by establishing a concrete, easily verifiable criterion for the integrability of the link function. Specifically, for likelihoods belonging to elliptically symmetric families
[
15
]
, which encompass diverse noise models such as Gaussian, Laplace, Student-
t
and Cauchy distributions. We prove that the high-dimensional Bochner integrability condition reduces to the finiteness of a simple, one-dimensional radial integral for models with bounded observations functions.

This reduction allows us to provide explicit analytical constructions showing that, approximation errors grow at most polynomially with the state dimension
d_{x}
. Notably, we identify cases where the polynomial degree is zero, implying error bounds that hold uniformly over
d_{x}
. These findings demonstrate that the exponential complexity typically associated with the
curse of dimensionality
can be overcome in some practically relevant settings, such as those involving sensors with limited operational ranges.

This framework offers a straightforward diagnostic for practitioners: if the observation map is bounded and the radial profile of the noise satisfies a manageable tail-growth condition, polynomial or even uniform error scaling is guaranteed. By reducing the complex stability analysis of IS to the evaluation of a univariate integral, we provide a systematic method to verify the robustness of inference schemes across the various noise structures specified in Remark
8
.

Importance samplers

2.1
Model

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and let

X\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\mapsto{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and

Y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\mapsto{\mathcal{Y}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}

denote two multidimensional r.v.s. To be specific, the r.v.
X
represents the

d_{x}
-dimensional signal of interest (or state), which we aim to estimate, and

Y
represents some
d_{y}
-dimensional observed data.

The complete model can be specified as follows:

•

The prior probability law of the state of interest
X
is denoted by

\pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)
, and we assume the ability to generate random samples from
this distribution.

•

Given
X=x
, the observation
Y
admits a conditional probability
density function (pdf) w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure, denoted by
g(y\mid x)
.

We introduce the notation
g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=g(y\mid x)
and define an
associated likelihood function

l_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathtt{c}\,g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x),

(1)

where
\mathtt{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a positive (possibly unknown) constant,
independent of
x
.
The superscript
d_{x}
explicitly denotes the dependence of the
conditional pdf
g(y\mid x)
on the dimension of
X
. Similar superscript notation is used for other dimension-dependent objects
throughout this paper.

We refer to a pair
\mathcal{M}^{d_{x}}=(\pi_{0},g^{d_{x}})
as a model for Bayesian inference. Throughout this work, we consider a family of models
\{\mathcal{M}^{d_{x}}\}_{d_{x}\in\mathbb{N}}
, indexed by the dimension
d_{x}
of the state variable
X
. Intuitively, the models in the family
\{\mathcal{M}^{d_{x}}\}_{d_{x}\in\mathbb{N}}
may be expected to be “of the same type”, i.e., similar in structure and with differences due mainly to the increasing dimension
d_{x}
. An explicit example (of a family of linear and Gaussian models) is given in Section
4.1
. Neverheless, the conditions required by the main theorems in Sections
3
and
4
actually allow for families where the individual models can be rather different. The dimension of the observation space,
d_{y}
, is assumed to be fixed unless stated otherwise.

Usually, the aim is to approximate the posterior law of the state
X
conditional on the observation
Y=y
, denoted
\pi_{y}({\sf d}x)
, using IS. This posterior probability measure can be written as

\pi_{y}({\sf d}x)=\frac{g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\pi_{0}({\sf d}x)}{\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})}=\frac{l_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\pi_{0}({\sf d}x)}{\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})},

(2)

where

\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\pi_{0}({\sf d}x)

is the normalization constant of the posterior distribution for a fixed
observation
y\in{\mathcal{Y}}
, and coincides with the pdf of
the r.v.
Y
when
y\in{\mathcal{Y}}
is regarded as the independent variable of the function
y\leadsto\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})
.

We remark that
\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})
is analytically intractable in general. One important exception is the case of linear Gaussian models, which we explicitly discuss in Section
4.1
.

We assume that
g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\in L^{2}(\pi_{0})
,
\forall\;y\in{\mathcal{Y}}
, i.e.,

\|g_{y}^{d_{x}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)^{2}\,\pi_{0}({\sf d}x)<\infty.

(3)

This condition holds if and only if the normalized importance sampling weights have finite variance under the proposal distribution
\pi_{0}
(see Appendix
B
). In particular, it is trivially satisfied whenever
g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)
is uniformly bounded for all

x\in{\mathcal{X}},\,y\in{\mathcal{Y}}
.

The approximation of the posterior law
\pi_{y}({\sf d}x)
for a given (fixed)
observation
Y=y
can be carried out using a simple IS scheme as shown in
Section
2.2
.

2.2
Importance sampler

Assume a model
{\mathcal{M}}^{d_{x}}
as described in Section
2.1
. To describe a general IS scheme for the approximation of
\pi_{y}({\sf d}x)
, let us introduce an importance probability measure
\nu
such that
\pi_{0}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\nu
(denoted
\pi_{0}\ll\nu
). In this setting, we let

\rho(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{{\sf d}\pi_{0}}{{\sf d}\nu}(x),\qquad x\in{\mathcal{X}},

denote the Radon–Nikodym derivative of
\pi_{0}
w.r.t.
\nu
. A general IS procedure, with proposal
\nu
, is outlined in Algorithm
1
below.

Algorithm 1
.

A general importance sampler

1.

Draw
N
iid samples
x^{1},\ldots,x^{N}
with common probability law

\nu
.

2.

Compute the likelihood values
l_{y}^{d_{x}}(x^{i})
for

i=1,\ldots,N
.

3.

Compute normalised importance weights

w_{\nu}^{i}=\frac{l_{y}^{d_{x}}(x^{i})\rho(x^{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}l_{y}^{d_{x}}(x^{j})\rho(x^{j})}
,
i=1,...,N
.

The samples
x^{1},\ldots,x^{N}
and their importance weights yield a Monte Carlo
estimator of the posterior measure
\pi_{y}({\sf d}x)
, namely

\pi_{y,\nu}^{N}({\sf d}x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{\nu}^{i}\delta_{x^{i}}({\sf d}x),

where
\delta_{x^{i}}({\sf d}x)
is the Dirac delta measure located at
x^{i}
.

For clarity, we carry out our analysis for a simpler version of this general
algorithm where the prior is used as the proposal, i.e.
\nu=\pi_{0}
. The resulting scheme is displayed as
Algorithm
2
. Hereafter we refer to this specific procedure as standard
IS.

Algorithm 2
.

Importance sampler

1.

Draw
N
iid samples
x^{1},\ldots,x^{N}
from the prior law
\pi_{0}
.

2.

Compute the likelihood values
l_{y}^{d_{x}}(x^{i})
for

i=1,\ldots,N
.

3.

Compute normalised importance weights

w^{i}=\frac{l_{y}^{d_{x}}(x^{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}l_{y}^{d_{x}}(x^{j})}
.

The standard importance sampler outputs the random probability measure

\pi_{y}^{N}({\sf d}x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{i}\delta_{x^{i}}({\sf d}x).

(4)

This measure can be easily used to approximate posterior expectations of
X
, i.e., integrals w.r.t.
\pi_{y}({\sf d}x)
. For a given real test function

f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, let us denote

\pi_{y}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)\pi_{y}({\sf d}x)=\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Y=y],

i.e.,
\pi_{y}(f)
is the expected value of the r.v.
f(X)
conditional on
Y=y
.
We can naturally approximate

\pi_{y}(f)\approx\pi_{y}^{N}(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(x^{i})w^{i},

and then analyze the random errors
\pi_{y}(f)-\pi_{y}^{N}(f)
.

Remark 1
.

There is no loss of generality in analysing the Algorithm
2
instead of the general importance sampler outlined in Algorithm
1
. As discussed in detail in Appendix
A
, any general importance sampler with proposal
\nu
for a model
{\mathcal{M}}^{d_{x}}=(\pi_{0},g^{d_{x}})
can be interpreted as a standard algorithm for a modified model
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}^{d_{x}}=(\nu,\widetilde{g}^{d_{x}})
, with prior
\nu
and likelihood

\widetilde{g}_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\,\frac{{\sf d}\pi_{0}}{{\sf d}\nu}(x).

Consequently, it suffices to analyze the performance of Algorithm
2
applied to
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}^{d_{x}}
.

Remark 2
.

It is pertinent to observe that the likelihood function
l_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\propto g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)
serves as the foundation for the algorithmic approximation
\pi_{y}^{N}
of the posterior measure
\pi_{y}
. While this likelihood appears pervasively throughout the subsequent analysis, the density
g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)
remains the primary object of interest. Notably, since the IS approximation
\pi_{y}^{N}
is constructed via self-normalized weights, the resulting measure is invariant to the choice of the proportionality constant in
l_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)
.

2.3
Error bounds

We aim to obtain an upper bound for the approximation error

\|\pi_{y}(f)-\pi_{y}^{N}(f)\|_{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\big|\pi_{y}(f)-\pi_{y}^{N}(f)\big|^{p}\right]^{1/p},

(5)

where the expectation is taken w.r.t. the probability law of the random samples
x^{1},\ldots,x^{N}
.

Under mild assumptions on the model defined in Section
2.1
, as specified in Theorem
2.1
, we derive an explicit error bound for the estimator
\pi_{y}^{N}(f)
when
f\in B({\mathcal{X}})
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Fix
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, and assume that
g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)
is strictly
positive and bounded, i.e.,

g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)>0\quad\text{for all }x\in{\mathcal{X}},\quad\text{and}\quad\|g_{y}^{d_{x}}\|_{\infty}<\infty.

Then, for any
f\in B({\mathcal{X}})
and
p\geq 1
, there exists a constant
C_{y}^{d_{x}}<\infty
, independent of
N
and
f
, such that

\|\pi_{y}(f)-\pi_{y}^{N}(f)\|_{p}\leq\frac{C_{y}^{d_{x}}\,\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{N}}.

(6)

Proof:
See Appendix
C
. ∎

The inequality in (
6
) is well known
[
1
]
; it states that the approximation error rate is
\mathcal{O}(N^{-\frac{1}{2}})
. Moreover, our analysis provides an explicit upper bound for the constant
C_{y}^{d_{x}}
in (
6
), namely

C_{y}^{d_{x}}\leq\frac{B_{p}\|g_{y}^{d_{x}}\|_{\infty}}{\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})},

(7)

where
B_{p}
is a finite constant depending solely on
p
(refer to (
63
) in Appendix
C
). This constant arises from the Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund inequality and is independent of the dimensions
d_{x}
and
d_{y}
.

Remark 3
.

The pdf of the r.v.
Y
, is precisely the normalizing constant
\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})
that appears explicitly in the upper bound of (
7
). The ratio
\|g_{y}^{d_{x}}\|_{\infty}/\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})
captures the possible dependence of this bound on the state dimension
d_{x}
. A closely related quantity, obtained by replacing
\|g_{y}^{d_{x}}\|_{\infty}
with
\|g_{y}^{d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}
, plays a central role in the subsequent analysis, where
Y
is treated as a r.v. This is the main subject of Section
3
.

3
Random observations

In this section, we adopt the viewpoint that
Y
itself is a r.v. generated by the model
{\mathcal{M}}^{d_{x}}
instead of use it as given (fixed) data. With this perspective, the likelihood function, the normalization constant and the posterior measure also become random objects. In particular, the quantity

\big|\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\big|

becomes a
Y
-dependent random variable. The subscript
Y
emphasizes the randomness of the observation, the same notation is used hereafter for other observation-dependent objects.
To derive an upper bound on this random error, we take the expectation
with respect to the law of
Y
in order to obtain an averaged error.
This leads us naturally to examine how integration over the random
observation is performed.
The central tool for this development is the Bochner integrability

[
14
]
of a specially constructed function, which acts
as a link between the fixed and random observation formulations.

3.1
Random posterior measure

We adopt the perspective that the observation
Y
is a r.v. generated by the model in Section
2.1
. In that setting,

l_{Y}^{d_{x}}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathtt{c}\,g(Y\mid x)\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{0}(l_{Y}^{d_{x}})

are real-valued r.v.s (recall that
\mathtt{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is an arbitrary positive constant). Consequently, the posterior distribution becomes a random probability
measure, which we denote as

\pi_{Y}({\sf d}x)=\frac{l_{Y}^{d_{x}}(x)\,\pi_{0}({\sf d}x)}{\pi_{0}(l_{Y}^{d_{x}})}=\frac{g_{Y}^{d_{x}}(x)\,\pi_{0}({\sf d}x)}{\pi_{0}(g_{Y}^{d_{x}})}.

Recall that
g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)=g(y\mid x)
coincides with the
conditional pdf of
Y
given
X=x
.
As a consequence,
g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)
is strictly positive, that is,

g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)>0,\quad\forall(x,y)\in{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}},

and it is normalized, i.e.,

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\,{\sf d}y=1,\quad\forall x\in{\mathcal{X}},

where
{\sf d}y
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{Y}
.
Finally, we recall the assumption that
g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\in L^{2}(\pi_{0})
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. It is clear that Algorithm
2
yields an estimator
\pi_{Y}^{N}
of
\pi_{Y}
, using a likelihood function of the form
l_{Y}^{d_{x}}(x)=\mathtt{c}\,g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)
for an arbitrary constant
\mathtt{c}>0
independent of
x
. Since the algorithm relies on normalized weights, the particular value of this constant has no effect on the resulting estimator. The key difference is that the estimator
\pi_{Y}^{N}
now depends on the realizations of the random variable
Y
. Consequently,
\pi_{Y}^{N}
is itself a random probability measure, whose randomness arises both from the Monte Carlo samples
X=x^{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
, and from the observation
Y
.

We aim to derive an upper bound for the approximation error in (
5
), averaged over the observations, where the expectation is taken with respect to the joint law of the random samples
x^{1},\ldots,x^{N}
and the observation
Y
. Much of the analysis to be elaborated in this section depends on two
objects that we introduce next:

•

The probability measure
\eta\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Y}})
, defined as

\eta({\sf d}y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})\,{\sf d}y,

(8)

which corresponds to the marginal probability law of the random variable
Y
induced by the model
\mathcal{M}^{d_{x}}
and also serves as the normalization constant in Eq. (
2
).

•

The measurable map

\begin{array}[]{llll}\ell^{\,d_{x}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}&\mathcal{Y}&\mapsto&L^{2}(\pi_{0})\\
&y&\leadsto&\ell_{y}^{\,d_{x}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\dfrac{g_{y}^{d_{x}}(\cdot)}{\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})}=\dfrac{l_{y}^{d_{x}}(\cdot)}{\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})},\end{array}

(9)

hereafter termed the
link function
, which assigns to each observation

y
a normalized density over
{\mathcal{X}}
. Since
\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})>0,\;\forall y\in{\mathcal{Y}}
, the map
\ell^{\,d_{x}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{Y}}\mapsto L^{2}(\pi_{0})
is well defined.

For any
p\in\mathbb{N}
, the
L^{2}(\pi_{0})
-norm of
\ell_{y}^{\,d_{x}}
can be written as

\|\ell_{y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{p}=\frac{\|g_{y}^{d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{p}}{\bigl(\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})\bigr)^{p}}.

(10)

For any test function
\varphi\in L^{2}(\pi_{0})
, the posterior expectation under the random observation
Y
is given by

\pi_{Y}(\varphi)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)\,\pi_{Y}({\sf d}x)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\varphi(X)\mid\mathcal{G}\right],

(11)

where
\mathcal{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sigma(Y)
denotes the sigma-algebra generated by
Y
. Moreover, this quantity admits the representation

\pi_{Y}(\varphi)=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)\,g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\,\pi_{0}({\sf d}x)}{\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})}=\langle\varphi,\ell_{Y}^{\,d_{x}}\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})},

(12)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}
denotes the inner product
in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\pi_{0})
.

3.2
Bochner integrability

Take test functions
\varphi,\psi\in L^{2}(\pi_{0})
.
The quantity
\mathbb{E}[\pi_{Y}(\varphi)]
defines a linear functional on
L^{2}(\pi_{0})
,
while
\mathbb{E}[\pi_{Y}^{2}(\varphi)]
defines a quadratic form induced by the symmetric bilinear form

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\mathrm{B}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}&L^{2}(\pi_{0})\times L^{2}(\pi_{0})&\mapsto&\mathbb{R},\\[2.84526pt]
&(\varphi,\psi)&\leadsto&\mathbb{E}[\pi_{Y}(\varphi)\,\pi_{Y}(\psi)],\end{array}

where the expectation is taken w.r.t. the law of the random observation
Y
.

By the inner-product representation in
Eq. (
12
), the boundedness of

\mathbb{E}[\pi_{Y}^{p}(\varphi)]
,
p\in\{1,2\}
, is closely related to the
Bochner integrability of the map
\ell^{\,d_{x}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}y\leadsto\ell_{y}^{\,d_{x}}
. This connection is made precise below:

The function
\ell^{\,d_{x}}
is called Bochner
p
-integrable if and only if it is strongly
\eta
-measurable
[
39
]
, and

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\|\ell_{y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{p}\,\eta({\sf d}y)=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}K_{p}^{d_{x}}<\infty.

(13)

The strong
\eta
-measurability condition is satisfied under mild regularity assumptions on
g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)
, which already hold for the models of interest (see Lemma
D.1
in Appendix
D
). Hence, in the remainder of the paper we only require the finiteness of

K_{p}^{\,d_{x}}
to guarantee the Bochner
p
-integrability of
\ell^{\,d_{x}}
.

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we denote by

L^{p}(\mathcal{Y};L^{2}(\pi_{0}))

the Banach space of Bochner
p
-integrable functions

\upsilon\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{Y}\mapsto L^{2}(\pi_{0})

w.r.t. the measure
\eta
(cf.
[
39
]
).
In particular, for
p=\infty
, the norm is defined as

\|\upsilon\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y};L^{2}(\pi_{0}))}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\|\upsilon_{y}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}<\infty.

Since
\eta
is a probability measure, for
1\leq p<\infty
,
we have the continuous embedding

L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y};L^{2}(\pi_{0}))\subseteq L^{p+1}(\mathcal{Y};L^{2}(\pi_{0}))\subseteq L^{p}(\mathcal{Y};L^{2}(\pi_{0})).

With this notation, we formalize the above discussion in the following

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
\ell^{\,d_{x}}
is Bochner
p
-integrable
for some
p\in\{1,2\}
. Then, for all
\varphi\in L^{2}(\pi_{0})
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\pi_{Y}^{p}(\varphi)\right]\leq K_{p}^{d_{x}}\,\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{p},

where
K_{p}^{d_{x}}<\infty
is defined in (
13
).

See Lemma
D.2
and Remark
10
in Appendix
D
.
∎

3.3
Approximation errors

We now provide a convergence result for the IS Monte Carlo approximation of the posterior
expectation with random observations.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume that
g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\in L^{2}(\pi_{0})
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
Then, for any
f\in B({\mathcal{X}})
, the following statements are equivalent:

i)

There is a constant
C_{2}^{d_{x}}<\infty
, independent of
N
and

f
, such that

\|\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\|_{2}\leq\frac{C_{2}^{d_{x}}\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{N}}.

(14)

ii)

\ell^{\,d_{x}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Y};L^{2}(\pi_{0}))
, that is,
\ell^{\,d_{x}}
is Bochner
2
-integrable.

Moreover,
C_{2}^{d_{x}}
can be decomposed as

C_{2}^{d_{x}}=B_{2}\,K_{2}^{d_{x}},

where
B_{2}
is a constant independent of the state dimension
d_{x}
and
d_{y}
arising from the
Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund inequality and

K_{2}^{d_{x}}
accounts for the possible dependence on
d_{x}
through the
Bochner integrability of the link function
\ell^{\,d_{x}}
.

See Appendix
E
.
∎

Theorem
3.2
provides a necessary and sufficient condition for the

L^{2}
approximation error of the IS estimator
\pi_{Y}^{N}
,
produced by Algorithm
2
, to converge at the canonical rate

\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})
. The result holds in the setting where the observation

Y
is treated as a r.v. and yields a complete characterization of
the
L^{2}
Monte Carlo error of the posterior approximation: the rate
holds if and only if the link function is
square integrable in the Bochner space
L^{2}(\mathcal{Y};L^{2}(\pi_{0}))
.
In particular, the convergence of the random-observation Monte Carlo approximation is
characterized by this intrinsic integrability condition on the link
function, which separates the statistical properties of the observation model
from those of the sampling scheme. Moreover, this characterization provides
a direct mechanism to control the dependence of the error bounds on the state
dimension
d_{x}
, a feature that is made explicit in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

Assume that
\ell^{\,d_{x}}
is Bochner
p
-integrable
for some
p\in\{1,2\}
. Suppose further that there exists a polynomial

P_{n,p}(d_{x})
of degree
n
, such that

K_{p}^{d_{x}}\leq P_{n,p}(d_{x}),\qquad\forall d_{x}\in\mathbb{N}.

(15)

Then, for any
f\in B({\mathcal{X}})
,

\|\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\|_{p}\leq\frac{B_{p}P_{n,p}(d_{x})\,\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{N}},

where
B_{p}
is a constant arising from the Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund inequality and depends only on
p
.

See Appendix
F
.
∎

Remark 4
.

The error bounds established in Theorem
3.3
demonstrate how specific structural properties of the model can mitigate the curse of dimensionality in importance samplers. Specifically, under these assumptions, the number of samples required to approximate the posterior expectation
\pi_{Y}(f)
with a prescribed average error increases at most polynomially with the dimension
d_{x}
, rather than exponentially.
From a practical perspective, this provides a direct prescription for the sample complexity required to maintain a prescribed accuracy. Given a target tolerance
\varepsilon>0
such that
\|\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\|_{p}\leq\varepsilon
, the sample size
N
should be tuned relative to the state dimension according to:

N\;\geq\;\left(\frac{P_{n,p}(d_{x})\|f\|_{\infty}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}.

(16)

In models where the observation map scales linearly with dimension (i.e.,
n=1
), a quadratic increase in
N
is sufficient to preserve the fidelity of the approximation. We provide an explicit construction of such a system, demonstrating this manageable polynomial scaling, in Section
4.3
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.3
, suppose that
n=0
, i.e., there
exists a constant
\mathcal{K}_{p}<\infty
, independent of
d_{x}
, such
that

\sup_{d_{x}\in\mathbb{N}}K_{p}^{d_{x}}\leq\mathcal{K}_{p},

(17)

then, for any
f\in B({\mathcal{X}})
, there exists a constant

C_{p}<\infty
, independent of
N
,
f
, and
d_{x}
,
such that

\|\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\|_{p}\leq\frac{C_{p}\,\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{N}},\qquad p\in\{1,2\}.

In particular,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\|_{p}=0,

uniformly with respect to the dimension state
d_{x}
.

Remark 5
.

Corollary
3.4
is a straightforward consequence of Theorem
3.3
.
The constant
C_{p}<\infty
admits the decomposition

C_{p}=B_{p}\,\mathcal{K}_{p},

where
B_{p}
is a constant depending only on
p
, arising from the
Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund inequality.

4
Special cases

In this section, we examine specific scenarios under which the assumptions of Theorem
3.3
and Corollary
3.4
are satisfied, that is, when the inequalities (
15
) and (
17
) holds. It is worth emphasizing that the bounds derived here are not intended to be tight; rather, our goal is to demonstrate that polynomial and uniform error bounds for the IS estimator
\pi_{Y}^{N}
can be established as functions of the state dimension
d_{x}
. We focus on the case
p=2
, which subsumes the case
p=1
.

Remark 6
.

Observe that

\|\ell_{y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}=\frac{\pi_{0}([g_{y}^{d_{x}}]^{2})}{\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})^{2}},

which is directly related to the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between the posterior
and the prior distributions. This quantity is also related to the
relative variance of the Monte Carlo estimator of the normalization
constant
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})
when sampling from the prior
(see Appendix
B
) and has already been found to be relevant in the analysis of IS methods; see, e.g.,
[
1
,
2
]
.

In the setting of the present work, where the observation
Y
is assumed random, we can use Eq. (
10
) and the identity
\eta(\mathrm{d}y)=\pi_{0}(g_{y})\,{\sf d}y
to obtain

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\ell_{Y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{p}\right]=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\|\ell_{y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{p}\,\eta(\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\|g_{y}^{d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{p}}{\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})^{p-1}}\,{\sf d}y.

(18)

In particular, for the case
p=2
we have

\hskip 49.79231pt\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\|\ell_{y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}\,\eta(\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)^{2}}{\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)\pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}x).

(19)

If
\ell^{\,d_{x}}_{Y}
is regarded as a random element in
L^{2}(\pi_{0})
, the Bochner integrability condition in (
13
) is equivalent to the moment condition

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\ell_{Y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}\right]<\infty.

(20)

The previous discussion shows that the key condition required for the
dimension-dependent error bounds of Theorem
3.3
reduces to the
finiteness of the moment in (
20
). In other words, the error
of the IS estimator is governed by the integrability of the link function

\ell_{Y}^{d_{x}}
as a random element of
L^{2}(\pi_{0})
.
In the remainder of this section, we verify this condition for several
relevant observation models. The analysis illustrates how the structure
of the likelihood determines the dependence of the error bounds on the
state dimension
d_{x}
, and shows that polynomial, or even uniform,
bounds can arise in common statistical models. We begin with the linear
Gaussian model, since in this case all the relevant distributions can be
computed analytically, regardless of the dimensionality of the problem.

4.1
Linear Gaussian model

We consider a family of linear Gaussian models
\{{\mathcal{M}}^{d_{x}}\}_{d_{x}\in\mathbb{N}}
, indexed by the state dimension
d_{x}
. Let
X\sim\pi_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{x}^{\,d_{x}},\Sigma_{x}^{\,d_{x}})
be a
d_{x}
-dimensional Gaussian random vector with mean
\mu_{x}^{\,d_{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and positive definite covariance matrix
\Sigma_{x}^{\,d_{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}\times d_{x}}
.
The observation model is defined as

Y=A^{d_{x}}X+V,

(21)

where
A^{d_{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times d_{x}}
is a fixed matrix and
V\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R)
is a zero-mean Gaussian noise vector with positive definite covariance matrix
R\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times d_{y}}
, independent of
X
and also independent of it dimension
d_{x}
.
As a result, the r.v.
Y
is Gaussian with law

Y\sim\mathcal{N}({\sf d}y;\mu_{y},\Sigma_{y}),

where

\mu_{y}=A^{d_{x}}\mu_{x}^{d_{x}},\qquad\Sigma_{y}=A^{d_{x}}\Sigma_{x}^{\,d_{x}}[A^{d_{x}}]^{\top}+R.

(22)

In particular,

\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})={\mathcal{N}}(y;\mu_{y},\Sigma_{y}),

(23)

i.e.,
\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})
is the pdf of
Y
.

We now show that, for the linear Gaussian model described above, there is a finite constant
{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
, independent of the state-space dimension
d_{x}
, that satisfies the inequality (
17
) (which implies that the assumptions of Corollary
3.4
are satisfied). First, let us note that, since both
A^{d_{x}}\Sigma_{x}^{\,d_{x}}[A^{d_{x}}]^{\top}
and
R
are positive definite, it follows that
\Sigma_{y}
in (
22
) is positive definite.

In addition, the likelihood function
g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)
corresponds to the Gaussian pdf of
Y
conditional on
X=x
, namely,

g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)=\mathcal{N}(y;A^{d_{x}}x,R).

Squaring this pdf yields

g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)^{2}=\frac{1}{(2\uppi)^{d_{y}}|R|}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}(y-A^{d_{x}}x)^{\top}(2R^{-1})(y-A^{d_{x}}x)\right),

which is proportional to a Gaussian density with covariance
\tfrac{1}{2}R
. Consequently,

\int_{\mathcal{X}}g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)^{2}\,\pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{1}{(2\uppi)^{d_{y}}|R|}\mathcal{N}(y;\mu_{y},\Sigma_{y}-\tfrac{1}{2}R).

(24)

Using (
23
) and (
24
), we realise that in order to satisfy the integrability condition (
20
) it suffices to find a finite bound for the expectation

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\ell_{Y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{(2\uppi)^{d_{y}}|R|}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\mathcal{N}(y;\mu_{y},\Sigma_{y}-\tfrac{1}{2}R)}{\mathcal{N}(y;\mu_{y},\Sigma_{y})}\,{\sf d}y.

(25)

Let
S_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Sigma_{y}
and
S_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Sigma_{y}-\tfrac{1}{2}R
. The integrand in (
25
) can be rewritten as

\frac{\mathcal{N}(y;\mu_{y},S_{2})}{\mathcal{N}(y;\mu_{y},S_{1})}=\left(\frac{|S_{1}|}{|S_{2}|}\right)^{1/2}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(y-\mu_{y})^{\top}(S_{2}^{-1}-S_{1}^{-1})(y-\mu_{y})\right).

Moreover, both
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are positive definite, and

S_{1}=\Sigma_{y}\succ\Sigma_{y}-\tfrac{1}{2}R=S_{2},

where
\succ
denotes the Loewner order (see Section 7.7 in
[
21
]
). Let us also define

S^{-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=S_{2}^{-1}-S_{1}^{-1}.

(26)

By the operator monotonicity of matrix inversion on the cone of positive definite matrices (see Theorem 24 in
[
26
]
), it follows that

S_{1}\succ S_{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad S_{1}^{-1}\prec S_{2}^{-1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad S^{-1}\succ 0

(hence,
S^{-1}
is positive definite). The integrand in (
25
) is thus proportional to the density of a Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu_{y}
and covariance
S
. As a consequence, the integral in (
25
) can be computed exactly to yield

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\mathcal{N}(y;\mu_{y},S_{2})}{\mathcal{N}(y;\mu_{y},S_{1})}\,{\sf d}y=\left(\frac{1}{(2\uppi)^{d_{y}}|S|}\frac{|S_{1}|}{|S_{2}|}\right)^{1/2}.

(27)

Using the identity
S_{2}^{-1}-S_{1}^{-1}=S_{1}^{-1}(S_{1}-S_{2})S_{2}^{-1}
, and since
S_{1}-S_{2}=\cfrac{1}{2}R
,
Eq. (
26
) readily yields

S^{-1}=S_{1}^{-1}\left(\cfrac{1}{2}R\right)S_{2}^{-1}

and, as a consequence,

S=S_{2}(2R^{-1})S_{1}

(28)

and

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S\right\rvert}=2^{d_{y}}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S_{1}\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S_{2}\right\rvert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert R\right\rvert}}.

(29)

Substituting (
29
) into (
27
) we arrive at

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\mathcal{N}({\sf d}y;\mu_{y},S_{2})}{\mathcal{N}({\sf d}y;\mu_{y},S_{1})}\,{\sf d}y=\frac{|R|^{\frac{1}{2}}}{2^{\frac{d_{y}}{2}}(2\uppi)^{\frac{d_{y}}{2}}|S_{2}|}

(30)

and then, combining (
30
) and (
25
), we readily see that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\ell_{Y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{\left(2^{d_{y}}(2\uppi)^{3d_{y}}\,|R|\right)^{1/2}\,|S_{2}|}=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}K_{2}^{d_{x}},

(31)

which is always finite (provided that
R\succ 0
, which implies
S_{2}\succ 0
as well), ensuring that the moment condition (
20
) holds and so does the Bochner integrability condition (
13
).

Moreover, the quantity in (
31
) can be bounded in terms of the model parameters. Indeed, recalling (
22
), we readily see that

S_{2}=\Sigma_{y}-\tfrac{1}{2}R=A\Sigma A^{\top}+\tfrac{1}{2}R\succ 0,

If we additionally observe that
S_{2}-\frac{1}{2}R\succ 0
then, by Theorem 25 in
[
26
]
,

|S_{2}|\geq\cfrac{1}{2^{d_{y}}}|R|,\quad\text{hence}\quad\frac{1}{|S_{2}|}\leq\frac{2^{d_{y}}}{|R|},

and the expectation in (
31
) can be bounded as

K_{2}^{d_{x}}\leq\frac{1}{\,\left((2^{\frac{2}{3}}\uppi)^{d_{y}}\,|R|\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{K}_{2}<\infty,

(32)

that does not depend on the state dimension
d_{x}
. In other words,

K_{2}^{d_{x}}
is bounded by a polynomial of degree zero in the sense of
Corollary
3.4
, i.e.,
n=0
, and hence the assumptions
of the corollary are satisfied.

Remark 7
.

We have found that, in the linear Gaussian setting, the Bochner integrability condition (
31
) holds with a finite constant
\mathcal{K}_{2}
that is independent of the state dimension. Consequently, the assumptions of Corollary
3.4
are satisfied, and the corresponding error bounds for the random IS estimator hold uniformly over
d_{x}
and decay at the rate
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})
.

4.1.1
Linear Gaussian model with arbitrary
d_{x}
and increasing
d_{y}

Based on the inequality (
32
), which holds for any
d_{x}\in\mathbb{N}
, we study the behaviour of the error bound as the observation dimension
d_{y}
increases. Assume that there is a sequence of positive definite
d_{y}\times d_{y}
matrices
\{R^{d_{y}}\}_{d_{y}\in\mathbb{N}}
and fix a constant
a>0
. If we let
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d_{y}}>0
denote the eigenvalues of
R^{d_{y}}
, arranged in decreasing order, then

a^{d_{y}}|R^{d_{y}}|=a^{d_{y}}\prod_{i=1}^{d_{y}}\lambda_{i}.

If there is some constant
b>0
, independent of
d_{y}
, such that
|R^{d_{y}}|\geq a^{-d_{y}}b
, then we easily see that

\lim_{d_{y}\to\infty}a^{d_{y}}|R^{d_{y}}|=\lim_{d_{y}\to\infty}a^{d_{y}}\prod_{i=1}^{d_{y}}\lambda_{i}\geq b>0.

(33)

From (
33
), it is straightforward to provide sufficient conditions in terms of the eigenvalues of matrix
R
that guarantee
\lim_{d_{y}\to\infty}a^{d_{y}}|R^{d_{y}}|>0
. Specifically:

(i)

If
\lambda_{d_{y}}\geq\frac{1}{a}
for all
d_{y}
, then
|R^{d_{y}}|a^{d_{y}}\geq 1
and

\lim_{d_{y}\to\infty}a^{d_{y}}|R^{d_{y}}|\geq 1.

(ii)

Moreover, if there exists
\delta>0
such that

\lambda_{d_{y}}\geq\frac{1+\delta}{a}\quad\text{for all }d_{y},

then

\lim_{d_{y}\to\infty}a^{d_{y}}|R^{d_{y}}|\geq\lim_{d_{y}\to\infty}(1+\delta)^{d_{y}}=\infty.

By inspection of (
32
), we notice that the constant of interest is
a=2^{\frac{2}{3}}\pi
. In case (i) above, if
\lambda_{d_{y}}\geq 2^{-\frac{2}{3}}\pi^{-1}
, then

\lim_{d_{y}\to\infty}\left(\left(2^{\frac{2}{3}}\pi\right)^{d_{y}}|R^{d_{y}}|\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\geq 1,\;\;\implies\lim_{d_{y}\to\infty}{\mathcal{K}}_{2}<\infty,

and, using Corollary
3.4
, the approximation error can be upper bounded as

\lim_{d_{y}\to\infty}\|\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\|_{2}\leq\frac{B_{p}\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{N}},

where the constant
B_{p}
is independent of
d_{x}
,
d_{y}
and
N
.

Furthermore, in case (ii), we obtain

\lim_{d_{y}\to\infty}\|\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\|_{2}\leq\lim_{d_{y}\to\infty}\frac{B_{p}\|f\|_{\infty}}{(1+\delta)^{d_{y}}\sqrt{N}}=0,

i.e., the approximation error converges towards 0 as
d_{y}\to\infty
. This is the case for a “sufficiently regular” linear and Gaussian model, where increasing the dimension of the observations always improves the estimation.

As discussed previously, the key quantity governing the dependence of
the error bounds on the state dimension
d_{x}
is the moment condition
(
20
). However, verifying this condition directly is generally difficult in
nonlinear or non-Gaussian models, since it involves high-dimensional
integrals. In the following subsection, we show that, under suitable pointwise
domination assumptions on the likelihood, the condition can instead be
verified through simple bounds. These assumptions yield explicit
estimates for the second moment of the link function required in
Theorem
3.3
.

4.2
Pointwise Bounded Likelihood

Assume that the likelihood function
g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)
admits a product-form pointwise bound

g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\leq m^{d_{x}}(x)q(y),

(34)

where
m^{d_{x}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{X}}\to[0,\infty)
and
q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{Y}\to[0,\infty)
are measurable functions satisfying

\|m^{d_{x}}\|_{\infty}\leq M(d_{x})<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\|\,q\,\|_{L^{1}(\mathcal{Y})}\leq Q<\infty,

(35)

for constants
M(d_{x})
and
Q
. Specifically note that
\|m^{d_{x}}\|_{\infty}
may depend on the dimension
d_{x}
but the upper bound
Q
does not. Under conditions (
34
), we readily see from Remark
6
that the squared norm of the link function
\ell_{y}^{\,d_{x}}
satisfies

\|\ell_{y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}\leq q(y)\frac{\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}m^{d_{x}}(x)g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)}{\left[\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)\right]^{2}}

(36)

for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. By treating the observation
Y
as a r.v. and taking the expectation on both sides of (
36
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\ell_{Y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{Y}}q(y)\frac{\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}m^{d_{x}}(x)g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)\pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}q(y)\pi_{y}(m^{d_{x}})\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq M(d_{x})\int_{\mathcal{Y}}q(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=M(d_{x})Q=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}K_{2}^{d_{x}}<\infty,

where the first inequality follows from the construction of the marginal law of
Y
(see Eq. (
8
)) and then we simply apply the bounds
\|m^{d_{x}}\|_{\infty}\leq M(d_{x})
in (
35
).

Consequently, if (
34
) and (
35
) hold, the second moment is finite, implying that
\ell^{\,d_{x}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Y};L^{2}(\pi_{0}))
. Furthermore, if there exists a uniform constant
M
such that
M(d_{x})\leq M
for all
d_{x}\in\mathbb{N}
, then the requirements of Corollary
3.4
are satisfied for the class of models described in Section
4.2
.

The second-moment condition (
20
) generally involves
high-dimensional integrals, which may be difficult to verify directly in
practical models. In the following subsection, we bridge the gap between
this abstract functional-analytic condition and concrete Bayesian
modelling by deriving a tractable criterion for the integrability of the
link function.
Specifically, we consider likelihoods belonging to the class of
elliptically symmetric families
[
15
]
, which
include a wide range of noise models such as Gaussian, Laplace,
Student-
t
, and Cauchy distributions. For this class of models, we show
that, when the observation function is bounded, the Bochner
integrability condition reduces to the finiteness of a one-dimensional
radial integral.
This reduction enables explicit analytical constructions showing that
the approximation error grows at most polynomially with the state
dimension
d_{x}
. In particular, we identify scenarios in which the
polynomial degree is zero, yielding error bounds that hold uniformly in

d_{x}
.

4.3
Uniformly bounded observation function

Let us consider the observation model

Y=h(X)+V,

(37)

where
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
is a (generally nonlinear) deterministic function
and
V
is an observation noise term modelling sensor and measurement errors.
We assume that
V
is independent of
X
and admits a density on
{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Conditionally on
X=x
, the observation
Y
then admits a density

g(y\mid x).

A broad class of observation likelihoods can be represented in the

radial–shift form

g(y\mid x)=\phi\!\big(\psi(\|y-h(x)\|_{R})\big),

(38)

where
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
is non-increasing,

\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is non-decreasing, and

\|\cdot\|_{R}
denotes a Mahalanobis norm on
{\mathcal{Y}}
defined below.

The discrepancy between an observation
y
and its state-dependent
prediction
h(x)
is measured through the quadratic form

(y-h(x))^{\top}R(y-h(x)),

(39)

where
R\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times d_{y}}
is a fixed symmetric positive definite
matrix independent of the state. This quadratic form induces the
Mahalanobis inner product

\langle y_{1},y_{2}\rangle_{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=y_{1}^{\top}Ry_{2},

and the associated norm

\|y\|_{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sqrt{\langle y,y\rangle_{R}},\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}.

Representation (
38
) separates two
modelling components:

•

the
geometric structure
, determined by the Mahalanobis metric
induced by
R
together with the translation
h(x)
, and

•

the
radial profile

\phi\circ\psi
, which governs the tail
behaviour of the likelihood.

Conditional densities of the form
(
38
) yield the class
of
elliptically symmetric likelihoods
.

\mathcal{M}^{d_{x}}=\{\pi_{0},g_{y}^{d_{x}}\}

h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}

by a function
M\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{N}\mapsto[0,\infty)

M(d_{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\|h(x)\|<\infty.

(40)

Under this assumption, it is straightforward to show (see Appendix
G.1
) that

M_{R}^{d_{x}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\|h(x)\|_{R}\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(R)}\,M(d_{x}).

(41)

Let us denote

\mathcal{S}_{R}^{d_{y}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=|R|^{1/2}\,\mathbb{S}^{d_{y}-1}=|R|^{1/2}\,\frac{2\pi^{d_{y}/2}}{\Gamma(d_{y}/2)},

(42)

is the surface area of the

\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
, and
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function.

The following result reduces the high-dimensional Bochner integrability condition on the link function
\ell^{\,d_{x}}
to the finiteness of a one-dimensional radial integral.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let the observation likelihood
g(y\mid x)
belong to the class of elliptically symmetric families defined in (
38
), and assume the observation map
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto\mathcal{Y}
satisfies (
40
). Then the second moment of the link function
\ell_{Y}^{\,d_{x}}
is bounded by a one-dimensional radial integral, namely,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\ell_{Y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}\right]\leq\mathcal{S}_{R}^{d_{y}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\phi^{2}\big(\psi(r)\big)}{\phi\big(\psi(r+2M_{R}^{d_{x}})\big)}\,r^{d_{y}-1}\,\mathrm{d}r.

(43)

A proof is provided in Appendix
G.2
(see also Appendix
G.1
).

Corollary 4.2
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
hold. For the observation noise models listed in Tables A and B, the integral on the right hand side of (
43
) is finite and can be bounded by a
d_{x}
–dependent constant
K_{2}^{d_{x}}<\infty
. Therefore

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\ell_{Y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}\right]\leq K_{2}^{d_{x}}.

Hence
\ell^{\,d_{x}}
is Bochner
2
-integrable for this class of observation models.
Furthermore, we have the following:

1.

If there exists a uniform bound
M<\infty
, such that
\sup_{d_{x}\in\mathbb{N}}M(d_{x})\leq M
, then the conditions of Corollary
3.4
are satisfied for all observation models in Tables A and B.

2.

For the polynomial radial profiles in Table B, if

for some polynomial

of

K_{2}^{d_{x}}
satisfies (

3.3
.

A proof is provided in Appendix
G.3
.

Example: Polynomial growth of
M(d_{x})
.

To illustrate the asymptotic behavior characterized in Corollary
4.2
, we consider a high-dimensional Bayesian inverse problem on a state space
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
. Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d_{x}})^{\top}\in{\mathcal{X}}
. We define the observation map
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto\mathcal{Y}
through a system of
d_{y}
observation channels. For each channel
i\in\{1,\dots,d_{y}\}
, the measurement is given by

h_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{x}}a_{ij}\sigma(x_{j}),

(44)

where
a_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
are coupling coefficients and
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a saturating nonlinearity (e.g.,
\sigma(x)=\tanh(x)
or
\sigma(x)=\operatorname{erf}(x)
). We introduce the following assumptions to characterize the operator’s growth:

1.

Uniform local saturation: The nonlinearity
\sigma
is globally bounded, i.e., there exists
M_{\sigma}>0
, independent of the dimension
d_{x}
, such that
\|\sigma\|_{\infty}\leq M_{\sigma}
.

2.

Sensitivity uniformity: The coupling coefficients are bounded such that
|a_{ij}|\leq A
for some
A>0
for all
i,j
and all
d_{x}
.

The supremum of the observation magnitude,
M(d_{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\|h(\mathbf{x})\|
, characterizes the maximum signal energy the system captures as a function of the state dimension
d_{x}
. Under the assumptions of local saturation and uniform sensitivity, the forward map satisfies

M(d_{x})\leq\sqrt{d_{y}}AM_{\sigma}d_{x}.

(45)

Indeed, by the triangle inequality and the uniform bound on
\sigma
, the
i
-th observation channel satisfies

|h_{i}(\mathbf{x})|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d_{x}}|a_{ij}||\sigma(x_{j})|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d_{x}}AM_{\sigma}=AM_{\sigma}d_{x}.

(46)

Taking the Euclidean norm of the vector
h(\mathbf{x})=(h_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,h_{d_{y}}(\mathbf{x}))^{\top}
, we obtain

\|h(\mathbf{x})\|=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{y}}|h_{i}(\mathbf{x})|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{y}}(AM_{\sigma}d_{x})^{2}\right)^{1/2}=\sqrt{d_{y}}AM_{\sigma}d_{x}.

(47)

Since this bound holds for all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
, the result follows. This formulation ensures that for any finite dimension
d_{x}
, the observation operator remains bounded on the domain
{\mathcal{X}}
. Notably, the growth of the observation map satisfies
M(d_{x})\leq P(d_{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=a_{1}d_{x}
for
a_{1}=\sqrt{d_{y}}AM_{\sigma}>0
, where
P
is a polynomial of degree 1 in the state dimension. Consequently, for any observation model
Y=h(X)+V
where the noise
V
admits a polynomial radial profile as described in Remark
8

M(d_{x})\propto d_{x}
grows linearly and satisfies the conditions of Corollary
4.2
(item 2). This ensures that the requirements of Theorem
3.3
are fulfilled, guaranteeing that the error of the IS estimator
\pi_{Y}^{N}
scales at most polynomially (in fact, linearly) with the state dimension
d_{x}
.

5
Comparison with some existing results

The performance of IS estimators has been studied by several authors. Here we present a brief comparison of the results introduced in Sections
3
and
4
with the analysis of
[
9
]
and
[
1
]
, respectively. This is certainly not exhaustive, but we believe these references are significant and representative of the state of the art.

5.1
Arbitrary target distribution
\pi

In
[
9
]
, Chatterjee and Diaconis present a performance analysis of a generic importance sampler with target distribution
\pi
and given proposal
\nu
, with
\pi\ll\nu
and

{\rm KL}(\pi,\nu)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int\log\left(\frac{{\sf d}\pi}{{\sf d}\nu}\right){\sf d}\pi<\infty,

where KL stands for the Kullback-Leibler divergence. The estimator of the target measure
\pi
is constructed as

\widehat{\pi}^{N}({\sf d}x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{i}\delta_{x^{i}}({\sf d}x),

where
x^{i}\sim\nu
iid, for
i=1,\ldots,N
,

w^{i}=\frac{\rho(x^{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\rho(x^{j})}\quad\text{and}\quad\rho(x)\propto\frac{{\sf d}\pi}{{\sf d}\nu}(x).

Under these minimal assumptions, Chatterjee and Diaconis prove that, for any test function
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
and denoting
D\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\rm KL}(\pi,\nu)
,

\text{if $\log N=D+o(D)$ then}~~\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\widehat{\pi}^{N}(f)-\pi(f)\right|\geq\frac{2\|f\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\varepsilon_{D}}{1-\varepsilon_{D}}\right)\leq 2\varepsilon_{D},

where (under a mild assumption on the tails of
\rho(x)
)
\lim_{D\to\infty}\varepsilon_{D}=0
, i.e.,

if
\log N=D+o(D)
then
\lim_{D\to\infty}\widehat{\pi}^{N}=\pi
in probability.

They also show that taking
\log N=D-o(D)
can lead to significant errors with non-negligible probability for a certain choice of
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
. Therefore,

\widehat{\pi}^{N}\to\pi~~\text{in probability}~~\Leftrightarrow~~N\asymp\exp\left\{{\rm KL}(\pi,\nu)\right\}.

(48)

If the KL divergence between the target
\pi
and the proposal
\nu
scales linearly with the state dimension
d_{x}
(as it is the case if, e.g.,
\pi
and
\nu
have independent marginals) then expression (
48
) implies that

\widehat{\pi}^{N}\to\pi~~\text{in probability}~~\Leftrightarrow~~N\propto\exp\left\{d_{x}\right\}.

(49)

Expression (
49
) may seem at odds with Theorem
3.3
, which states that (under regularity assumptions),

\|\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)-\pi_{Y}(f)\|_{2}\leq\frac{P_{n,2}(d_{x})\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{N}},

(50)

where
P_{n,2}(\cdot)
is a polynomial of degree
n
. Expressions (
49
) and (
50
) can be reconciled if we recall that:

(i)

the target distribution
\pi_{Y}
in our analysis is not arbitrary, but the posterior distribution in a Bayesian inference problem, hence
\pi_{Y}({\sf d}x)\propto g_{Y}^{d_{x}}(x)\pi_{0}({\sf d}x)
, and

(ii)

g_{Y}^{d_{x}}
is a family of likelihood functions indexed by the random observation
Y
(i.e., a function-valued r.v.).

The analysis of
[
9
]
is carried out for a given (almost arbitrary) relative density
\rho(x)=\frac{{\sf d}\pi}{{\sf d}\nu}(x)
and the goal is to determine the computational cost (value of
N
) needed to guarantee “small errors” without further assumptions. In this paper, instead, we work with a weight function
\propto g_{Y}^{d_{x}}(x)
which is random and endowed with considerable structure, and the goal is to determine the regularity conditions that guarantee “small
average
errors” with a computational cost that depends at most polynomially on
d_{x}
. Theorem
3.3
provides such regularity conditions in an abstract setting, while in Section
4
we describe a few concrete examples, i.e., specific constructions of the likelihood
g_{Y}^{d_{x}}
that yield small average errors with non-exponential computational cost.

5.2
Importance sampling for Bayesian inference

Agapiou
et al.

[
1
]
analyse the performance of
importance sampling for Bayesian inference in the linear–Gaussian
in close analogy with the framework developed in Section
4.1
. Their framework
considers a fixed observation
Y=y
and targets the posterior

\pi_{y}({\sf d}x)
defined in (
2
). The IS algorithm coincides with
Algorithm
2
: particles are drawn independently from the prior

x^{i}\sim\pi_{0}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
, and the posterior is approximated by

\pi_{y}^{N}
in (
4
).

A central quantity in
[
1
]
is

\rho_{y}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{2})}{\pi_{0}(g_{y})^{2}},

which is the second moment of the normalised importance weights.
Equivalently,

\rho_{y}=1+\chi^{2}(\pi_{y},\pi_{0}),

so that
\rho_{y}
measures the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between posterior and
prior. Using the link-function notation, by
Remark
6
, we have

\rho_{y}=\|\ell_{y}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}.

(51)

One main result of
[
1
]
yields the conditional MSE
bound

\sup_{\|\phi\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\pi_{y}(\phi)-\pi_{y}^{N}(\phi))^{2}\right]\leq\frac{4}{N}\rho_{y}.

(52)

For the linear–Gaussian model,
\rho_{y}
can be related to the

intrinsic dimension
, (see Section
4.1
)

\tau=\mathrm{trace}(C),\qquad C=\sqrt{\Sigma_{x}^{d_{x}}}\,[A^{d_{x}}]^{\top}R^{-1}A^{d_{x}}\,\sqrt{\Sigma_{x}^{d_{x}}},

whose eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{C,i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{x}}
satisfy

\tau=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{x}}\lambda_{C,i},\qquad\log\rho_{y}=\mathrm{cst}(y)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{x}}\log(1+\lambda_{C,i}).

Consequently,

\rho_{y}\gtrsim\exp\!\Big\{\tfrac{1}{2}\tau\Big\},

and therefore (
52
) suggest an exponential growth of the
required number of particles

\mathrm{MSE}\lesssim\frac{1}{N}\exp\!\Big\{\tfrac{1}{2}\tau\Big\}.

To achieve
\mathrm{MSE}\leq\varepsilon
, one needs

N\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-1}\exp\{\tau\}
.
Thus the computational cost can grows exponentially with the intrinsic
dimension.

Relation with the present work.

The key difference in our analysis is that the observation
Y
is kept
random. Instead of the conditional error in (
52
), we
control the averaged quantity

\big\|\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\big\|_{2}.

Conditionally on
\mathcal{G}=\sigma(Y)
, an argument analogous to
(
52
) yields

\mathbb{E}\!\left[|\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)|^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{G}\right]\leq\frac{C\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}}{N}\rho_{Y}.

However, instead of estimating
\rho_{Y}
pointwise in
Y
, we integrate
with respect to the law of
Y
and exploit structural properties of the
model. For the linear–Gaussian setting, Corollary
3.4

combined with (
32
) yields the uniform bound

\|\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\|_{2}\leq\frac{B_{p}\|f\|_{\infty}}{\left((2^{2/3}\pi)^{d_{y}}|R|\right)^{3/2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}.

All constants are independent of the state dimension
d_{x}
, and the model
dependence enters only through the observation noise covariance
R
and
the observation dimension
d_{y}
. In particular, if the eigenvalues of
R

are sufficiently large (see Section
4.1.1
), the
bound remains finite, and may even decrease, as
d_{y}\to\infty
.

Hence, while the fixed-observation analysis suggests an exponential
dependence on the intrinsic dimension, averaging over the law of
Y

allows us to recover the
N^{-1/2}
convergence rate uniformly in the
state dimension.

6
Conclusions

We have investigated the approximation errors of a random importance sampling algorithm for numerical Bayesian inference in high-dimensional settings. The analysis is carried out in a probabilistic framework in which the observations are treated as random variables, and the posterior distribution is therefore itself a random measure. This setting allows us to exploit the probabilistic structure of the model and to formulate error bounds in expectation, rather than conditioning on a fixed realization of the data, and analyse the specifically, worst-case scenario.

Let
\pi_{Y}
and
\pi_{Y}^{N}
denote the true posterior distribution of a
d_{x}
-dimensional r.v.
X
given an observation
Y
and its IS approximation, respectively. In full generality, classical upper bounds for the
L^{2}
approximation error
|\pi_{y}(f)-\pi_{y}^{N}(f)|
, for a given realization
Y=y
and for any bounded test functions
f
, may grow exponentially with dimension, in agreement with existing results for IS and related Monte Carlo schemes
[
4
,
35
,
36
,
1
,
9
]
.

In this paper, we have introduced a refined analysis, treating observations as r.v.s, we relate the approximation error
|\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)|
to the Bochner integrability of a suitable link function
\ell^{\,d_{x}}
, which generates normalized likelihoods for the model. We have shown that the
L^{2}
error bounds are finite and converge at the standard Monte Carlo rate
{\mathcal{O}}(N^{-1/2})
, for arbitrarily large dimension, if and only if the link function is Bochner integrable. This result provides a precise and explicit characterization of when high dimensionality does not lead to a breakdown of the algorithm.

Moreover, the dependence of the error bounds on dimension can be quantified in terms of the moments of the link function with respect to the marginal distribution of the observations. This perspective enables the identification of sufficient conditions under which the
L^{1}
and
L^{2}
error bounds grow only polynomially with dimension, as well as conditions under which they remain uniformly bounded. These results provide a rigorous explanation of how the curse of dimensionality can be alleviated for some families of models.

We have illustrated the general theory through several concrete examples. In particular, for linear and Gaussian models, we have proved analytically that Bochner
2
-integrability of the link function alone is sufficient to ensure uniform
L^{2}
error bounds, independently of dimension. We have also considered models with bounded or dominated observation functions, conditions that are commonly satisfied in practical applications, for instance when sensors operate within a finite dynamic range. In all cases, we provide explicit analytical arguments showing how the structure of the model leads to favorable dimensional scaling of the average errors.

Statistical analysis of the normalization constant

In the context of the posterior measure
\pi_{y}^{N}
, the normalization
constant

(also known as the

)
is defined as the expectation of the likelihood function

under the prior measure

(56)

Given a set of

(57)

This corresponds to a particular instance of the IS
framework in which the proposal distribution
\nu
coincides with the prior

\pi_{0}
. In this case, the estimator reduces to standard Monte Carlo
integration under
\pi_{0}
.

Variance analysis and
L^{2}(\pi_{0})
convergence

To quantify the concentration of the estimator
\hat{Z}_{N}
around the true

Z
, we analyze its variance. By independence of the samples and
linearity of expectation, the variance of

satisfies

Expressing this relation in terms of the

(59)

Hence, the stability of the Monte Carlo approximation is governed by the
second moment of the likelihood function under the prior distribution.
A useful quantity to assess the magnitude of fluctuations is the

relative variance

\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{Z}_{N})}{Z^{2}}=\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{\|g_{y}^{d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}}{Z^{2}}-1\right).

(60)

Using the link function notation introduced earlier and
Remark
6
, we have

\|\ell_{y}^{d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}=\frac{\|g_{y}^{d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}}{Z^{2}}.

Therefore, the relative variance can be written as

\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{Z}_{N})}{Z^{2}}=\frac{1}{N}\left(\|\ell_{y}^{d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}-1\right).

This expression highlights that the efficiency of the estimator is
directly controlled by the
L^{2}(\pi_{0})
norm of the link function.

D.2
Boundedness of expectation functionals

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a linear normed space over the field of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
. A mapping
\mathrm{F}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{H}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is called a functional if it is linear; that is, for all
\upsilon,\varsigma\in\mathcal{H}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathrm{F}(\alpha\upsilon+\beta\varsigma)=\alpha\mathrm{F}(\upsilon)+\beta\mathrm{F}(\varsigma).

A functional
\mathrm{F}
is called bounded if there exists a positive finite constant
C
such that

|\mathrm{F}(\upsilon)|\leq C\|\upsilon\|,\quad\text{for all }\,\upsilon\in\mathcal{H}.

The norm of the functional
\mathrm{F}
is defined as the smallest such constant
C
, given by

\|\mathrm{F}\|=\sup_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{H},\upsilon\neq 0}\frac{|\mathrm{F}(\upsilon)|}{\|\upsilon\|}=\sup_{\|\upsilon\|=1}|\mathrm{F}(\upsilon)|.

A mapping
\mathrm{B}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is called a bilinear form (or bifunctional) if it is linear in each argument separately.
A bilinear form
\mathrm{B}
is called symmetric if
\mathrm{B}(\upsilon,\varsigma)=\mathrm{B}(\varsigma,\upsilon)
for all
\upsilon,\varsigma\in\mathcal{H}
.
A bilinear form
\mathrm{B}
is called bounded if there exists a positive finite constant
C
such that

|\mathrm{B}(\upsilon,\varsigma)|\leq C\|\upsilon\|\|\varsigma\|,\quad\text{for all }\,\upsilon,\varsigma\in\mathcal{H}.

The norm of the bilinear form
\mathrm{B}
is defined as

\|\mathrm{B}\|=\sup_{\upsilon,\varsigma\in\mathcal{H},\upsilon,\varsigma\neq 0}\frac{|\mathrm{B}(\upsilon,\varsigma)|}{\|\upsilon\|\|\varsigma\|}=\sup_{\|\upsilon\|=1,\|\varsigma\|=1}|\mathrm{B}(\upsilon,\varsigma)|.

A quadratic form
\mathrm{Q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{H}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is induced by a symmetric bilinear form
\mathrm{B}
by

\mathrm{Q}(\upsilon)=\mathrm{B}(\upsilon,\upsilon),\quad\text{for all }\,\upsilon\in\mathcal{H}.

The quadratic form
\mathrm{Q}
is called bounded if there exists a positive finite constant
C
such that

|\mathrm{Q}(\upsilon)|\leq C\|\upsilon\|^{2},\quad\text{for all }\,\upsilon\in\mathcal{H}.

The norm of the quadratic form
\mathrm{Q}
is defined as

\|\mathrm{Q}\|=\sup_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{H},\upsilon\neq 0}\frac{|\mathrm{Q}(\upsilon)|}{\|\upsilon\|^{2}}=\sup_{\|\upsilon\|=1}|\mathrm{Q}(\upsilon)|.

C.1
, we obtain

\left|\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\right|\leq\frac{1}{\pi_{0}(l_{Y}^{d_{x}})}\left[\left|\pi_{0}(f\,l_{Y}^{d_{x}})-\pi_{0}^{N}(f\,l_{Y}^{d_{x}})\right|+\|f\|_{\infty}\left|\pi_{0}^{N}(l_{Y}^{d_{x}})-\pi_{0}(l_{Y}^{d_{x}})\right|\right].

(66)

Since the second term corresponds to the special case
f\equiv 1
,
it suffices to bound the first term. Raising it to the power
p
, and using
the independence of
Y
from the Monte Carlo samples
\{x^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{\pi_{0}(l_{Y}^{d_{x}})^{p}}\left|\pi_{0}(f\,l_{Y}^{d_{x}})-\pi_{0}^{N}(f\,l_{Y}^{d_{x}})\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{1}{\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})^{p}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\pi_{0}(f\,l_{y}^{d_{x}})-\pi_{0}^{N}(f\,l_{y}^{d_{x}})\right|^{p}\right]\,\eta(\mathrm{d}y).

Applying Lemma
C.3
together with Eq. (
10
) gives

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{1}{\mathtt{c}^{p}\,\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})^{p}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\pi_{0}(f\,l_{y}^{d_{x}})-\pi_{0}^{N}(f\,l_{y}^{d_{x}})\right|^{p}\right]\,\eta(\mathrm{d}y)\leq\frac{\widetilde{B}_{p}\|f\|_{\infty}^{p}}{N^{p/2}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\|\ell^{\,d_{x}}_{y}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{p}\,\eta(\mathrm{d}y).

For
f\equiv 1
, the same arguments gives

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{1}{\mathtt{c}^{p}\,\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}})^{p}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})-\pi_{0}^{N}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})\right|^{p}\right]\,\eta(\mathrm{d}y)\leq\frac{\widehat{B}_{p}}{N^{p/2}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\|\ell^{\,d_{x}}_{y}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{p}\,\eta(\mathrm{d}y).

Combining the two bounds after using the elementary inequality

\lvert x+y\rvert^{p}\leq 2^{p-1}(\lvert x\rvert^{p}+\lvert y\rvert^{p})
,
we conclude that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\right|^{p}\right]\leq\frac{B_{p}\|f\|_{\infty}^{p}}{N^{p/2}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\|\ell^{\,d_{x}}_{y}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{p}\,\eta(\mathrm{d}y).

We now prove
(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
.

Assume that inequality (
14
) holds for all
f\in B({\mathcal{X}})
.
Observe first that

\displaystyle(\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f))^{2}=\frac{1}{\pi_{0}(l_{Y}^{d_{x}})^{2}}\Big[

\displaystyle\big(\pi_{0}(f\,l_{Y}^{d_{x}})-\pi_{0}^{N}(f\,l_{Y}^{d_{x}})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle+2\,\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\big(\pi_{0}(f\,l_{Y}^{d_{x}})-\pi_{0}^{N}(f\,l_{Y}^{d_{x}})\big)\big(\pi_{0}^{N}(l_{Y}^{d_{x}})-\pi_{0}(l_{Y}^{d_{x}})\big)

\displaystyle+\big(\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\big)^{2}\big(\pi_{0}^{N}(l_{Y}^{d_{x}})-\pi_{0}(l_{Y}^{d_{x}})\big)^{2}\Big].

(67)

Taking expectation and using the independence between
Y
and the Monte
Carlo samples, a necessary condition for

\mathbb{E}\big[(\pi_{Y}(f)-\pi_{Y}^{N}(f))^{2}\big]<\infty

is that

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{1}{\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})^{2}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big(\pi_{Y}^{N}(f)\big)^{2}\big(\pi_{0}^{N}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})-\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})\big)^{2}\right]\eta(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty.

(68)

Choose
f
positive and bounded away from zero, i.e. there exists a constant
\epsilon>0

such that
f(x)\geq\epsilon
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
. Then Eq. (
68
) yields

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{1}{\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})^{2}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big(\pi_{0}^{N}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})-\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})\big)^{2}\right]\eta(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty.

(69)

For fixed
y
, define the iid zero-mean r.v.s

U_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{N}\big(\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})-l_{y}^{d_{x}}(x^{i})\big),\qquad i=1,\dots,N,

so that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}U_{i}=\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})-\pi_{0}^{N}(l_{y}^{d_{x}}).

Applying the lower bound in the Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund inequality yields

A_{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}U_{i}^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}U_{i}\right|^{2}\right],

where
A_{2}>0
only depends on
p=2
. Since

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}U_{i}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{N}\big(\pi_{0}([l_{y}^{d_{x}}]^{2})-\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})^{2}\big),

we obtain

\frac{A_{2}}{N}\big(\pi_{0}([l_{y}^{d_{x}}]^{2})-\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})^{2}\big)\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big|\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})-\pi_{0}^{N}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})\big|^{2}\right].

Dividing by
\pi_{0}(\ell_{y}^{\,d_{x}})^{2}
, integrating over
\mathcal{Y}
, and applying (
69
) along with Remark
6
, we obtain, in particular,

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\|\ell_{y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}\,\eta(\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\pi_{0}([l_{y}^{d_{x}}]^{2})}{\pi_{0}(l_{y}^{d_{x}})^{2}}\,\eta(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty,

which proves that
\ell^{\,d_{x}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Y};L^{2}(\pi_{0}))
.
∎

The result follows directly from the sufficiency argument in the proof of Theorem
3.2
(i.e., (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i)), which remains valid for both
p=1
and
p=2
. Under the assumption that there is a polynomial
P_{n,p}(d_{x})
bounding

G
Elliptically symmetric likelihood families

G.1
Mahalanobis-distance geometry

Throughout this section, geometric arguments are conducted w.r.t. the Mahalanobis norm
\|\cdot\|_{R}
. We assume the observation map
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\to\mathcal{Y}
is uniformly bounded in the Euclidean norm; specifically, there exists a
d_{x}
-dependent constant

M(d_{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\|h(x)\|<\infty.

Let
R^{1/2}
denote the unique symmetric positive definite square root of
R
, such that
\|y\|_{R}=\|R^{1/2}y\|
. If
\lambda_{1}(R)
denotes the largest eigenvalue of
R
, the induced operator 2-norm of
R^{1/2}
is
\|R^{1/2}\|=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(R)}
. It follows from the submultiplicativity of the norm that

\|h(x)\|_{R}=\|R^{1/2}h(x)\|\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(R)}\,\|h(x)\|\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(R)}M(d_{x}).

Accordingly, we define the uniform bound in the
R
-norm as

M_{R}^{d_{x}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\|h(x)\|_{R}\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(R)}M(d_{x}).

By the triangle inequality, for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, we have

\|y-h(x)\|_{R}\leq\|y\|_{R}+M_{R}^{d_{x}}.

(70)

Propagation through
\psi
and
\phi
.

Let
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be non-decreasing and
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
be non-increasing. Applying
\psi
to the inequality in (
70
) yields a bound that is uniform in
x\in\mathcal{X}

\psi\big(\|y-h(x)\|_{R}\big)\leq\psi\big(\|y\|_{R}+M_{R}^{d_{x}}\big).

(71)

Since
\phi
is non-increasing, the inequality direction reverses, providing the lower bound

\phi\big(\psi(\|y\|_{R}+M_{R}^{d_{x}})\big)\leq\pi_{0}\left(\phi\big(\psi(\|y-h(x)\|_{R})\big)\right)=\pi_{0}(g_{y}^{d_{x}}).

(72)

The bound in (
72
) establishes that the normalization constant of any elliptically symmetric likelihood of the form (
38
) is bounded below by a radial profile depending solely on the Mahalanobis norm of
y
and the uniform bound
M_{R}^{d_{x}}
.

G.2
Proof of Theorem
4.1

The squared likelihood for the elliptically symmetric family is expressed as

g(y\mid x)^{2}=\phi^{2}\big(\psi(\|y-h(x)\|_{R})\big).

(73)

From the general bounds established in (
19
) and (
72
), the second moment of the link function satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\ell_{Y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}\right]\leq\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{g_{y}^{d_{x}}(x)^{2}}{\phi\big(\psi(\|y\|_{R}+M_{R}^{d_{x}})\big)}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)\,\pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}x).

(74)

Applying the change of variables
\tilde{y}=y-h(x)
to the inner integral, we obtain

I(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\phi^{2}\big(\psi(\|\tilde{y}\|_{R})\big)}{\phi\big(\psi(\|\tilde{y}+h(x)\|_{R}+M_{R}^{d_{x}})\big)}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{y}.

(75)

By the triangle inequality for the Mahalanobis norm and the definition of
M_{R}^{d_{x}}
in (
40
), we have

\|\tilde{y}+h(x)\|_{R}+M_{R}^{d_{x}}\leq\|\tilde{y}\|_{R}+2M_{R}^{d_{x}}.

Recall that
\psi
is non-decreasing and
\phi
is non-increasing. Consequently, the denominator in the integrand is lower bounded by

\phi\big(\psi(\|\tilde{y}+h(x)\|_{R}+M_{R}^{d_{x}})\big)\geq\phi\big(\psi(\|\tilde{y}\|_{R}+2M_{R}^{d_{x}})\big).

This yields a uniform bound for the integrand that is independent of
x\in{\mathcal{X}}

\frac{\phi^{2}\big(\psi(\|\tilde{y}\|_{R})\big)}{\phi\big(\psi(\|\tilde{y}+h(x)\|_{R}+M_{R}^{d_{x}})\big)}\leq\frac{\phi^{2}\big(\psi(\|\tilde{y}\|_{R})\big)}{\phi\big(\psi(\|\tilde{y}\|_{R}+2M_{R}^{d_{x}})\big)}.

(76)

Defining the map

k(y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\phi^{2}\big(\psi(\|y\|_{R})\big)}{\phi\big(\psi(\|y\|_{R}+2M_{R}^{d_{x}})\big)},

(77)

and substituting the uniform bound (
77
) into (
74
), the second moment of the link function simplifies to

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\ell_{Y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}\right]\leq\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{Y}}k(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right)\pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}x).

(78)

Since the inner integral is independent of
x
and
\pi_{0}
is a probability measure on
{\mathcal{X}}
, the expression reduces to

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\ell_{Y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}\right]\leq\int_{\mathcal{Y}}k(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

(79)

To evaluate this, we transform to radial coordinates
r=\|y\|_{R}
. The Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{Y}
decomposes as
\mathrm{d}y=\mathcal{S}_{R}^{d_{y}}r^{d_{y}-1}\mathrm{d}r
, where
\mathcal{S}_{R}^{d_{y}}=|R|^{1/2}\frac{2\pi^{d_{y}/2}}{\Gamma(d_{y}/2)}
denotes the weighted surface area of the
d_{y}
–unit sphere. It follows that

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}k(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\mathcal{S}_{R}^{d_{y}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\phi^{2}\big(\psi(r)\big)}{\phi\big(\psi(r+2M_{R}^{d_{x}})\big)}\,r^{d_{y}-1}\,\mathrm{d}r.

(80)

Thus, the
L^{2}
-integrability of the link function is guaranteed if the one-dimensional radial integral in (
80
) is finite. This condition is determined solely by the tail behavior of the profile
\phi\circ\psi
, the observation dimension
d_{y}
, and the observation map bound
M_{R}^{d_{x}}
. ∎

G.3
Proof of Corollary
4.2

The integrability of the link function is governed by the radial integral defined in (
80
).
We analyze separately the two classes of radial profiles described in Tables A and B of Remark
8
.

G.3.1
Case 1: Exponential radial profiles

Let
\phi(s)=\mathrm{C}\,e^{-s}
and
\psi(r)=ar^{\beta}
for constants
\mathrm{C},a,\beta>0
. The radial integral in (
80
) then takes the form

\mathcal{J}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{C}\,\mathcal{S}_{R}^{d_{y}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(\psi(r+2M_{R}^{d_{x}})-2\psi(r)\right)r^{d_{y}-1}\,\mathrm{d}r.

(81)

The integrability is determined by the asymptotic behavior of the exponent
Q(r)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=a[(r+2M_{R}^{d_{x}})^{\beta}-2r^{\beta}]
. Factoring out the dominant term, we obtain

Q(r)=ar^{\beta}\left[\left(1+\frac{2M_{R}^{d_{x}}}{r}\right)^{\beta}-2\right].

Since
\lim_{r\to\infty}(1+2M_{R}^{d_{x}}/r)^{\beta}=1
, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there exists a radius
R_{\varepsilon}
such that for all
r\geq R_{\varepsilon}
,
Q(r)\leq-a(1-\varepsilon)r^{\beta}
. Setting
\varepsilon=1/2
yields
Q(r)\leq-\frac{a}{2}r^{\beta}
for
r\geq 4M_{R}^{d_{x}}
. We decompose the integral
\mathcal{J}
into a finite part
\mathcal{J}_{1}
and a tail part
\mathcal{J}_{2}
:

1.

Finite part:
For
r\in[0,4M_{R}^{d_{x}}]
, the exponent is bounded by
Q(r)\leq a(6M_{R}^{d_{x}})^{\beta}
. Thus,

\mathcal{J}_{1}\leq\mathrm{C}\,\mathcal{S}_{R}^{d_{y}}\exp\left(a(6M_{R}^{d_{x}})^{\beta}\right)\frac{(4M_{R}^{d_{x}})^{d_{y}}}{d_{y}}.

(82)

2.

Tail part:
For
r>4M_{R}^{d_{x}}
, the integrand is bounded by
\exp(-br^{\beta})r^{d_{y}-1}
with
b=a/2
. The change of variables
t=br^{\beta}
yields

\mathcal{J}_{2}\leq\frac{\mathrm{C}\,\mathcal{S}_{R}^{d_{y}}}{\beta b^{d_{y}/\beta}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d_{y}}{\beta},b(4M_{R}^{d_{x}})^{\beta}\right),

(83)

where
\Gamma(s,x)
is the upper incomplete Gamma function.

The sum
\mathcal{J}_{1}+\mathcal{J}_{2}
provides a finite bound
K_{2}^{d_{x}}
. If
\sup_{d_{x}\in\mathbb{N}}M_{R}^{d_{x}}\leq M
, the bound is independent of
d_{x}
.

G.3.2
Case 2: Polynomial-tail profiles

Let
\phi(s)=\mathrm{C}\,s^{-\alpha}
and
\psi(r)=1+ar^{2}
for
\alpha,a>0
. The integral (
80
) becomes

\mathcal{J}=\mathrm{C}\,\mathcal{S}_{R}^{d_{y}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1+a(r+2M_{R}^{d_{x}})^{2}}{(1+ar^{2})^{2}}\right)^{\alpha}r^{d_{y}-1}\,\mathrm{d}r.

(84)

Using the inequality
(r+k)^{2}\leq 2(r^{2}+k^{2})
, the numerator satisfies

1+a(r+2M_{R}^{d_{x}})^{2}\leq 2(1+a(2M_{R}^{d_{x}})^{2})(1+ar^{2}).

Substituting this into (
84
), we obtain

\mathcal{J}\leq 2^{\alpha}(1+a(2M_{R}^{d_{x}})^{2})^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{C}\,\mathcal{S}_{R}^{d_{y}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+ar^{2})^{-\alpha}r^{d_{y}-1}\,\mathrm{d}r.

This integral converges for
\alpha>d_{y}/2
and evaluates to a constant. Indeed,
by the normalization of the likelihood,
\mathrm{C}\,\mathcal{S}_{R}^{d_{y}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+ar^{2})^{-\alpha}r^{d_{y}-1}\,\mathrm{d}r=1
. Thus, the second moment is bounded by

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\ell_{Y}^{\,d_{x}}\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{0})}^{2}\right]\leq 2^{\alpha}(1+a(2M_{R}^{d_{x}})^{2})^{\alpha}=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}K_{2}^{d_{x}},

(85)

completing the proof.
Finally, note that if
M_{R}^{d_{x}}\leq P(d_{x})
for some polynomial
P
in
d_{x}
, then
K_{2}^{d_{x}}
satisfies (
15
). ∎
