Long-term follow-up and response to elosulfase alfa in mucopolysaccharidosis type IVA: a single-center cohort from the Czech Republic
Authors: Robert Šáhó, Lenka Murgašová, Markéta Tesařová, Helena Poupětová, Igor Nestrašil, Jiří Zeman, Martin Magner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMucopolysaccharidosis type IVA (MPS IVA, Morquio A syndrome) is a rare lysosomal storage disease primarily characterized by severe skeletal dysplasia. Clinical and laboratory data, including treatment responses, are reported for a cohort of Czech patients with MPS IVA.ResultsNineteen patients with MPS IVA from 17 families (13 M/6F) were included; only one patient exhibited a mild form. The median age at first symptom onset was 3.0 years (IQR 1.6–3.4), whereas the median age at confirmed diagnosis was 5.0 years (IQR 3.9–5.7). At the time of diagnosis, all patients had skeletal symptoms (pectus carinatum in 58%, short stature in 47%, hip dysplasia in 47%), and 63% had nonskeletal symptoms. Each patient underwent at least one surgical procedure (77% knee, 59% spine, 41% adenotomy). On spinal magnetic resonance imaging, 76% of patients presented with cervical stenosis, and 41% had cervical myelopathy. Eight patients received enzyme replacement therapy for a median duration of 5.5 years (IQR 4.2–6.4). Stabilization or improvement in monitored parameters, including mean forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), and mean 6-Minute Walk Test distance, were observed in treated patients. Additionally, the hidradenitis suppurativa observed in three patients improved with biologic therapy. We also describe two novel variants (c.421_422dupTG, c.482G > C).ConclusionsDocumenting the natural history of MPS IVA in this population may aid early diagnosis. Despite the disease’s progressive nature, ERT led to stabilization of monitored parameters in treated children.

Background

Mucopolysaccharidosis type IVA (MPS IVA), also known as Morquio syndrome type A (OMIM #253000), is a rare but significant lysosomal storage disorder inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. It is caused by mutations in the GALNS gene, resulting in a deficiency of the enzyme N-acetylgalactosamine-6-sulfatase (GALNS; EC 3.1.6.4) [1]. This enzyme deficiency results in the progressive accumulation of keratan sulfate (KS) and chondroitin-6-sulfate (CS) within lysosomes, particularly within connective tissues and the cornea [2], ultimately driving the hallmark pathophysiology of the disease.

Pronounced skeletal abnormalities, including short stature, long-bone deformities, spinal curvature, pectus carinatum, and joint laxity, dominate the clinical presentation of MPS IVA [1, 3]. However, its manifestations extend beyond the skeletal system: patients often experience respiratory impairment, valvular heart disease leading to subsequent cardiac hypertrophy, corneal clouding, vision and hearing loss, and characteristic coarse facial features [1, 4]. The severity of these clinical features contributes to a highly variable life expectancy. Individuals with milder forms of MPS IVA may live 20–40 years, with some cases reported to exceed 60 years. In contrast, severe phenotypes are often limited to late childhood or adolescence, with mortality typically resulting from spinal cord compression or respiratory failure [5]. The birth prevalence of MPS IVA varies by population, ranging from 1 in 140,000 in the Czech Republic to 1 in 872,000 in Australia [6].

Since its approval in 2014, enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with elosulfase alfa has been recommended as the first-line treatment according to international guidelines [7]. Clinical trials and registry data have shown that ERT improves quality of life, increases endurance, as evidenced by the 6-minute walk test (6MWT), preservers respiratory function, enhances daily activity performance, and reduces urinary KS levels [8, 9]. Long-term stabilization of endurance and respiratory function has been reported in the Morquio A Registry Study (MARS) [10]. However, ERT has not demonstrated efficacy in addressing the skeletal dysplasia and bone defects associated with MPS IVA [11].

While natural history studies have documented the progression of MPS IVA, including a high rate of surgical intervention and a gradual decline in physical endurance [2, 12–16], real-world data on the therapeutic impact of ERT remain limited [17–21]. This retrospective observational study reviewed the clinical history, genotypic characteristics, and phenotypic profiles of 19 Czech patients with MPS IVA from 1976 onward. Additionally, we evaluated the effects of ERT in a subset of treated patients, providing valuable insights into the real-world ERT outcomes in this cohort.

Patients and methods

Nineteen patients who were diagnosed with MPS IVA at the Department of Pediatrics and Inherited Metabolic Disorders, First Faculty of Medicine, Charles University and General University Hospital, Prague, Czech Republic, were included in this study. Diagnosis was confirmed through enzymatic and/or genetic testing. Patients in the cohort were followed longitudinally from 1976 to 2023, and data were analyzed at the conclusion of the study. Clinical, laboratory, and genetic data, including data from both outpatient and inpatient visits, were retrospectively collected from patient records.

Clinical symptoms

Clinical data included the disease onset, initial presenting symptoms, age at diagnosis, age at the start of ERT, anthropometric measures, and the presence of hepatomegaly.

On the basis of MPS IVA-specific growth charts [22], patients were assigned a “classic” designation to classify phenotypes if their height fell below the 75th percentile. Endurance was evaluated via the 6MWT following established guidelines [23]. Additional clinical evaluations included two-dimensional Doppler echocardiography, spinal MRI, audiometry, ocular examinations of the fundus and anterior segment, spirometry assessments for forced vital capacity (FVC) and forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), and polysomnography. Determination of urinary keratan sulfate levels was unavailable due to laboratory limitations at our center. Not all clinical assessments were available for every patient, as evaluations were performed at different time points over the long follow-up period, and some data were missing from historical records. Therefore, denominators vary across specific analyses and are indicated accordingly throughout the Results section.

Enzymology

White blood cell enzyme activity was measured in leukocytes isolated by dextran sedimentation, with protein concentration determined by the Hartree method [24], [25]. GALNS and β-galactosidase activities were assessed fluorometrically using MU-based substrates. The fluorescence of released 4-methylumbelliferone was subsequently quantified to determine enzymatic activity [26, 27].

Molecular genetic analyses

Genomic DNA was extracted from blood samples, and all exons of the *GALNS* gene (NM_000512.5) were amplified and analyzed by Sanger sequencing via the ABI3500xL Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA).

Ethics

All data were accessed in compliance with relevant laws and ethical standards for the study period and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, as revised in 2013 [28]. Informed consent for genetic testing was obtained from all participants. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the General University Hospital in Prague (Approval Number: 100/23 S-IV).

Results

This study included 19 patients (13 males, 6 females) from 17 unrelated families, all with a confirmed diagnosis of MPS IVA. At the time of data analysis, the cohort’s median age was 24.7 years (IQR 13.7–32.2; range 3.3–52.0 years), and the median follow-up period was 19 years (IQR 9.9–24.8; range: 1.3–47.1 years). Notably, patient 17 died at 30.5 years of age from respiratory complications associated with COVID-19. The median age at symptom onset was 3.0 years (IQR 1.6–3.4; range: 0.3–5.8 years), whereas the median age at confirmed diagnosis was 5.0 years (IQR 3.9–5.7; range: 2–16.2 years), with a median diagnostic delay of 2.1 years (IQR 0.7–3.4; range: 0–11.2 years). For patient 19, an initial diagnosis of mucopolysaccharidosis was made at age 5 without subtype specification; the patient was later lost to follow-up and re-evaluated at age 48 when MPS IVA was confirmed.

Among the 19 patients, 8 (6 males, 2 females) received ERT with elosulfase alfa. The median age at the start of ERT was 6.0 years (IQR 3.9–10.4; range 2.4–20.5 years). At the time of the study, the median duration of ERT was 5.5 years (IQR 4.2–6.4; range: 0.9–8.9 years), representing a median interval of 1.1 years (IQR 0.5–5.2; range: 0.3–14.4 years) between diagnosis and initiation of ERT.

Clinical outcomes

Disease onset

The most common initial symptoms observed in the cohort are depicted in Fig. 1 and summarized in Table 1. Skeletal symptoms were universal at onset among all 19 patients, with specific findings including pectus carinatum (n = 11), short stature (n = 9), acetabular dysplasia of the hip (n = 9), abnormal gait (n = 8), scoliosis (n = 7), genu valgum (n = 6), and kyphosis (n = 6). Additionally, 63% of patients presented initial nonskeletal symptoms, such as acute otitis media (n = 6), frequent respiratory infections (n = 5), and coarse facial features (n = 4). In two patients, the diagnosis was prompted by a family history of MPS IVA. At the time of diagnosis, all patients with available radiographic data (n = 17/19) presented classic features of multiplex dysostosis, whereas hepatomegaly was noted in only 19% of patients (3/16).

Anthropometry and growth

Among 18 MPS IVA patients with recorded birth data, the mean birth weight was 3,632 ± 534 g (range 2,650–4370 g), and the mean gestational age was 38.8 ± 1.2 weeks. The mean birth length in this group was 52.6 ± 3.0 cm (range 48–57 cm). Based on height assessments, 18 patients exhibited the classic MPS IVA phenotype, whereas one presented with an attenuated phenotype. Short stature was present in 85% (11/13) of patients at diagnosis, with a mean height Z-score of -3.2 ± 1.72 (range − 5.34 to -0.56). However, some patients initially grew within the normal percentile range (97th–25th percentile, data not shown) on Czech growth charts during the first 18 months of life. Patient 6, identified with an attenuated phenotype, had a Z-score of 0.56 at age 5.5 years. At the last recorded visit, the mean Z-score for height across the cohort (n = 18) was − 7.64 ± 2.87 (range − 11.33 to -1.54), with patient 6 continuing to have a relatively high Z-score of -1.54 (Table 1). When ERT-treated patients were compared with ERT-naïve patients (8 vs. 11), the current mean height Z-scores were − 6.75 ± 3.47 and − 8.35 ± 2.23, respectively, although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.48). The baseline Z-score for head circumference was 0.81 ± 1.18 (n = 11).

Endurance, spirometry, and assistive devices

Spirometric data for FVC and FEV1 from at least one visit were available for 12 patients (6 ERT-treated, 6 ERT-naive) and are presented in Table 2; Fig. 2. The differences in the FVC between the treated and untreated groups ranged from 1.05 L to 0.25 L, and the FEV1 ranged from 0.89 L to 0.18 L. Unlike the expected decline observed in natural history data [14, 20, 29], lung function remained stable or improved across age groups in our cohort. Notably, direct statistical analysis was not possible due to the lack of robust source data from natural history studies.

Mobility and endurance assessment

In the most recent follow-up, 10 patients (53%) were nonambulatory and relied on wheelchairs, with a median age of 15.1 years (IQR 13–17.8 years) at the time they ceased walking (data available for 5 patients). Additionally, three patients (16%) used a wheelchair for longer distances, whereas two (11%) required mobility aids such as walkers or crutches. Data on the 6MWT were unavailable for ERT-naïve patients in this cohort. Cross-sectional and longitudinal data from ERT-treated patients are shown in Table 3; Fig. 3, indicating a trend toward stabilization or improvement. Variability in performance was noted in several cases and may have been influenced by orthopedic and spinal surgical interventions affecting lower-limb function and mobility. Direct statistical comparison with natural history studies [14, 20, 29] was not possible.

Surgical history

All patients for whom data were available (n = 17) underwent surgery at some point. The mean age at first surgical intervention was 6 years. Of these patients, 13 (77%) required at least one knee surgery, and 10 (59%) underwent spinal cord surgery. Additional surgeries included adenotomy in 41% (7/17), hip surgery in 29% (5/17), and tympanostomy in 24% (4/17). Hernia repair and strabismus surgeries were performed in 6% of the patients each. The median ages at significant surgical intervention were as follows: knee surgery, 8.8 years (IQR 7.4–11.3; range 5.5–35.5 years); spinal cord surgery, 14.0 years (IQR 12–16; range 8.1–31 years); adenotomy 3.7 years (IQR 3.3–3.9; range 2.9–8.8 years); and hip surgery, 8.1 years (IQR 7–9.2; range 6.4–9.7 years). Spinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was available for 17 patients, revealing cervical stenosis in 13 (76%), cervical myelopathy in 7 (41%), and thoracic stenosis and odontoid hypoplasia, equally in 5 patients (29%). Patient 4 experienced a significant decline in clinical status at age 15 following spinal cord surgery, which resulted in postoperative paraplegia and sensory impairment distal from the Th12 level. Subsequent complications included the development of neurogenic bladder and bowel.

Cardiologic evaluation

Echocardiographic data from the most recent follow-up were available for 18 patients. The most commonly observed cardiac anomalies were mitral and tricuspid regurgitation (both present in 56% of patients), followed by aortic regurgitation in 44%, aortic and tricuspid valve thickening in 50%, mitral valve thickening in 28%, and pulmonary regurgitation in 22%. The median age of patients with valvular heart disease was 27.9 years (IQR 23.8–32.3 years), whereas the median age of those without valvular heart disease was 11.2 years (IQR 10–13 years). Mild valvular disease was predominantly observed in patients aged 3.3–18 years, whereas more advanced cases were observed in those older than 18 years. One patient was identified with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Ejection fraction remained within the normal range (≥ 55%) for all patients, with mean values of 71% at baseline and 68% at follow-up, indicating preserved systolic function.

ENT presentation, vision, and others

Among the 15 patients evaluated for ENT manifestations, 47% (7/15) experienced recurrent upper respiratory tract infections (RURIs), 20% (3/15) had recurrent otitis media (ROMA), and 47% (7/15) had at least one episode of acute otitis media (OMA). Audiometric assessments revealed hearing loss in 67% of patients (10/15), with conductive hearing loss accounting for 53%, while the remaining cases were attributed to sensorineural and mixed hearing loss. Five patients were already using hearing aids, and the median age at which hearing loss was documented was 10.5 years (IQR 6.8–15, n = 8). Four patients had severe obstructive sleep apnea requiring nocturnal noninvasive ventilation (bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP)). Surgical interventions for ENT issues are summarized in the surgery history section.

Sixteen patients underwent a complete ophthalmologic assessment. Corneal clouding was observed in 75% of cases, typically without visual impairment. No patients required corneal transplantation. One patient (6%) developed glaucoma. Cycloplegic refraction results revealed that hyperopia was the most common refractive error, affecting 69% of patients, followed by astigmatism at 63%. Myopia, strabismus, and amblyopia each affected 19% of patients.

In addition, three patients with severe hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) received biologic therapy. This condition is notably uncommon in lysosomal storage disorders.

Laboratory data

Enzymatic analyses

All patients exhibited significantly reduced GALNS enzyme activity, with levels below 1% of the long-term control average (34.5 nmol/17 h/mg). In both the classic and attenuated phenotypes, GALNS activity remained below 1% of the control average (Table 1).

Molecular‒genetic analyses

Nineteen variants of the GALNS gene were identified in 18 patients (12 males, 6 females) from 16 families. These variants included 13 missense mutations, two large deletions, two null variants (one small in-frame deletion/insertion and one intronic), and one duplication. The most common variants were c.1219 A > C p.(Asn407His), found in 7 unrelated patients; c.1156 C > T p.(Arg386Cys), present in 5 patients from 4 families; c.740G > A p.(Gly247Asp), found in 3 patients from 2 families; and c.860 C > T p.(Ser287Leu), found in 3 patients from 2 families (Table 1).

Notably, Patient 17 presented with a rare codiagnosis of adenine phosphoribosyltransferase (APRT) deficiency, leading to 2,8-dihydroxyadenine urolithiasis, and GALNS deficiency. Both the GALNS and APRT genes are located on chromosome 16q24.3, and genetic analysis revealed a homozygous deletion involving both genes. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) confirmed a novel junction formed by the fusion of sequences distal to GALNS exon 2 and proximal to APRT exon 3, with an estimated deletion size of approximately 100 kb, spanning GALNS intron 2 and APRT intron 2. This case was reported elsewhere [30].

This study identified two previously unreported variants in the literature or public databases (the Human Genome Mutation Database and ClinVar): a missense variant, c.482G > C (p.Gly161Ala), and a duplication, c.421_422dupTG. Notably, a different substitution at the same nucleotide position, c.482G > A, has been associated with an intermediate phenotype in a Chinese cohort [31]. With respect to duplication, a similar variant located one nucleotide upstream (closer to the intron) has been described previously [32]. Prediction algorithms provide conflicting interpretations of the impact of this duplication. Franklin and Varsome predict normal splicing, with effects limited to an amino acid substitution and a frameshift leading to p.Trp141Cysfs*13, suggesting that the impact may require reassessment. In contrast, SpliceAI predicts additional effects on splicing, indicating the potential for a more complex pathogenic mechanism. Functional validation was not performed.

Zygosity analysis revealed that 3 patients were homozygous, 1 was hemizygous, and 15 were compound heterozygous. Sixteen variants were associated with the classic phenotype, whereas the variants c.740G > A p.(Gly247Asp) and c.1219 A > C p.(Asn407His) were associated with both classic and attenuated phenotypes in this cohort.

Discussion

This retrospective observational study provides a comprehensive overview of clinical and laboratory characteristics and evaluates the effectiveness of ERT in a cohort of 19 Czech patients with MPS IVA. Most patients (18/19) exhibited the classic phenotype, with only one presenting an attenuated form. This prevalence is slightly higher than previously reported, indicating that the classic phenotype occurs in approximately 75% of cases [33]. The age of onset in toddlerhood and the diagnostic delay observed in this cohort are consistent with other published MPS IVA data. Symptoms often emerge between 1 and 3 years of age, with a mean age of onset reported at approximately 2.0 to 2.6 years. Diagnostic delays are also similar to our findings of 4.7–6.9 years [2, 13, 14].

As expected, skeletal symptoms, including pectus carinatum, short stature, acetabular dysplasia, and abnormal gait, were universally present. Although infants with MPS IVA are frequently born with average or above-average birth weight and length, growth failure often emerges in the second or third year of life.

Recurrent acute otitis media was a prevalent initial symptom in our cohort, underscoring that ENT symptoms, especially hearing loss, are relatively common in MPS IVA. In our cohort, 73% of patients experienced hearing loss, predominantly conductive (two-thirds). Hearing loss in MPS IVA is reported in 67% to 94% of cases [34–36]. Consistent with other forms of MPS, younger patients predominantly exhibit conductive hearing loss, whereas sensorineural or mixed hearing loss becomes more prevalent with age [37]. A study by Riedner and Levin found that conductive hearing loss was universal in patients under 8 years of age, whereas older patients were more likely to develop sensorineural or mixed hearing loss [36]. Awareness of these ENT symptoms can facilitate earlier detection and long-term management by specialists, who play a crucial role in identifying hearing impairments potentially caused by ossicle deformity, recurrent upper respiratory infections, recurrent otitis media, and glycosaminoglycan accumulation. A major life-threatening complication in patients with MPS IVA is tracheal disease and deformity. In our cohort, we have not yet documented any tracheal pathology on imaging studies, nor has any surgical procedure been performed on the trachea. However, a ray of hope was provided by the publication by Kenth et al., reporting improved airway patency, lung function, and quality of life in seven patients with severe MPS IVA after a transcervical tracheal resection with partial manubriectomy. Patients showed marked gains in FEV1 and other spirometric measures, with no major long-term complications [38].

Although less often discussed, skin conditions such as hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) were observed in three patients, as previously reported in Fialova et al. [39]. Its exact pathophysiology in MPS IVA remains unknown.

Orthopedic and spinal surgeries pose significant challenges in managing MPS IVA. All patients underwent surgery, with a median age of 5.5 years (IQR 3.4–7.9 years) at their first procedure. Knee surgeries were the most common (77%), followed by spinal cord surgeries (59%). Cervical spine involvement, especially stenosis and instability, appears nearly universal in MPS IVA, and often leads to myelopathy, paralysis, or sudden death. Previous studies reported overall surgery rates of 33% to 71%, underscoring inconsistent approaches to surgical indications. Broomfield et al. reported that 31% (26/82) of MPS IVA patients underwent cervical spine surgery at a median age of 6.1 years, emphasizing the importance of early intervention to preserve long-term neurological function [40]. The sparse literature lacks standardized recommendations for managing these orthopedic and neurosurgical challenges. The timing and indications for surgery are especially critical in spinal procedures, which have been associated with suboptimal outcomes, as observed in our cohort and other studies [2]. This clinical gap can be addressed through surveillance using spine MRI protocols sensitive to presymptomatic microstructural changes in the spinal cord, similar to those developed by the University of Minnesota group based on their experience with degenerative cervical myelopathy [41, 42].

Genetic analysis revealed that missense mutations are the predominant type in the GALNS gene, which is consistent with findings from global databases. The c.1219 A > C variant was the most prevalent variant in our cohort, accounting for 22% of all variants. This study also revealed two novel mutations (c.482G > C and c.421_422dupTG). The c.1156 C > T variant, identified as the most common variant globally in the literature, was the second most frequent variant in our cohort [43]. Jezela Stanek et al. reported that heterozygotes for the c.121-9T > G variant exhibit less severe growth defects [44]. However, in our cohort, this was observed in only one patient with a mild disease form, whereas five others carrying the same mutation experienced severe growth impairment. This variant appears to be less commonly reported in the literature. In contrast, the c.860 C > T variant, which was the fourth most common worldwide and the third most common in our cohort, is notably the most prevalent variant among the Indian population [43]. Additionally, our findings suggest that combined APRT/GALNS deficiencies, although rare, may be more common than previously recognized, with two cases documented to date, including one in our cohort [30, 45].

The impact of ERT on the natural history of mobility outcomes in MPS, including the pivotal phase 3 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled MOR-004 clinical trial, revealed a statistically significant improvement in 6MWT distance compared with placebo, sustained over 120 weeks [8, 46, 47]. In their review, Parini et al. reported improvement or stabilization of the 6MWT in MPS IVA patients after 1–3 years of treatment [48]. Pintos-Morel and colleagues reported improved walking distance in all but one patient cohort after 8 months of ERT [49]. In the study of Lin et al., the mean 6MWT distance increased by 30% in four of six patients after ERT for 4.0–6.5 years [50]. Other studies have also reported improvements in walking distance and/or performance on the 3-minute stair climb test after the initiation of ERT [51, 52]. Data from our cohort show stabilization or improvement in the majority of treated patients; however, statistical comparisons with natural history data were not possible.

Numerous studies have conclusively demonstrated that ERT improves pulmonary function [8, 9, 46, 49, 53]. MARS, the longest and largest observational study of MPS IVA patients to date, also provided real-world evidence of long-term stabilization of endurance and respiratory function among ERT-treated patients [10]. Data from our cohort similarly show stabilization or improvement in the majority of treated patients; statistical comparisons with natural history data were not possible.

Mitral and tricuspid regurgitation were the most common symptoms of heart involvement in our cohort, with more advanced cases observed in patients older than 18 years. Only one patient manifested hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. None of our patients had yet been indicated for valve replacement. However, ERT likely has a negligible effect on cardiovascular outcomes, as no significant changes in cardiac structure or function have been noted [18, 54].

ERT had no effect on growth in our patients, whereas the youngest treated patient began therapy at 2.4 years of age. Unfortunately, the effect of ERT on growth in MPS IVA has not been sufficiently documented [52, 55, 56]. We emphasize that the anthropometric data for our patients during the first 1.5-2 years were normal. Therefore, normal growth at this age does not rule out the diagnosis of MPS IVA.

Our findings should be interpreted in light of study limitations, including the limited number of patients, variable frequencies and intervals between clinical examinations, and incomplete data, particularly regarding childhood functional tests in our adult patients. The long study period (1976–2023) introduces temporal bias, as clinical practices have evolved over time. Because ERT became available only after 2014, treated patients may constitute a distinct subgroup, introducing additional selection bias. However, our study provides real-world clinical and longitudinal data on a national cohort of MPS IVA patients, including ERT treatment outcomes, which may be essential for understanding treatment variability and optimizing clinical decisions.

Conclusions

There is a pressing need for international guidelines on indications for skeletal and, in particular, spinal surgeries and tracheal management. Enzyme replacement therapy generally led to stabilization or improvement in respiratory and endurance functions, although direct comparison with untreated patients was not possible. There was no observable impact on growth or skeletal structure. Concurrent conditions, such as HS or APRT deficiency, were observed in our cohort as isolated findings, highlighting the need for careful clinical assessment and awareness of rare comorbidities.
