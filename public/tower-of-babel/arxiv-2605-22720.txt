Can AI Make Conflicts Worse? An Alignment Failure in LLM Deployment Across Conflict Contexts
Andrii Kryshtal

Abstract
AI models are already deployed in societies affected by armed conflict, and journalists, humanitarian workers, governments and ordinary citizens rely on them for information or for their work processes. No established practice exists for checking whether their outputs can make those conflicts worse. We tested nine model configurations from four providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepSeek, xAI) on 90 multi-turn scenarios designed to surface misaligned behaviour in conflict contexts: false equivalence between documented atrocities, denial of genocide, and failure to recognise ethnic slurs, among others. When such outputs feed into journalism, humanitarian reporting, or public debate, they can deepen divisions in fragile societies. Failure rates span 6% to 47% between the best and worst performing models, which makes model choice a safety question in its own right and when users pushed for “balance” in cases where international courts have already assigned responsibility, five of nine configurations failed 80 to 100 percent of the time. We release the first evaluation framework for this domain and propose adding it to alignment evaluation portfolios.



1
Introduction

In conflict settings, how you talk about what is happening matters almost as much as what is happening. Flattening context, treating asymmetries as “both sides” disagreements, or missing coded and contextualised language can cause real harm even when the intent is benign. This problem is not new to the international development sector. Decades of documented experience show that well-intentioned interventions routinely worsened the conflicts they aimed to address
(
1
;
13
)
. The response was a framework known as conflict sensitivity: the principle that any action in a fragile context must first be assessed for whether it strengthens divisions between groups or weakens connections across them
(
5
)
. It builds on the Do No Harm principle and rests on three components: understand the conflict, understand how your action interacts with it, and avoid making it worse. The framework has been adopted as an operational standard by the UN, the OECD, and over 35 humanitarian organisations.

AI models are now in routine use across almost every type of organisation operating in these settings. There is no established practice for checking whether deployed models meet that operational standard. In this paper we test nine models from different providers and find that many of them, including frontier models, fail to handle these situations sensitively. The risk goes beyond any individual failure. When journalists, humanitarian organisations, governments and ordinary citizens all rely on the same models at once, the outputs converge on the same distortions and produce a synchronised degradation of the information ecosystem in contexts where accurate information and context awareness matter most.

Conflict sensitivity is absent from AI safety evaluation portfolios, and no national AI safety institute currently tests for it.
3
recently introduced political even-handedness as an evaluation axis, arguing that models should avoid skewing toward particular partisan positions on contested political questions. That framework is valuable for everyday political discourse, but it does not fit conflict contexts, where balance itself can be harmful once international law has assigned responsibility. Deep contextual understanding and adherence to Do No Harm principles are more relevant. Other adjacent evaluations include escalation bias in foreign policy
(
8
)
and geopolitical narrative alignment
(
11
)
. These address related properties but do not test whether outputs would reinforce divisions or undermine connections in fragile societies.

This paper presents evidence of a previously untested alignment failure in conflict contexts, with failure rates of 6–47% at baseline rising to 80–100% under conversational pressure for five of nine tested models. We release the first evaluation framework for this domain and argue that the failure is an alignment property rather than a reasoning limitation.

3
Methodology

3.1
Evaluation Framework

We use Anthropic’s Bloom
(
2
)
, an Inspect-based framework for automated behavioural evaluation. Unlike fixed-input benchmarks, Bloom implements behavioural propensity evaluation in realistic multi-turn conversations. Scenarios are standardised at the design level, and the rollout agent generates adaptive follow-up questions in response to the target model’s outputs. This measures a model’s tendency toward conflict-insensitive behaviour rather than its response to a single prompt. The pipeline has four stages: (1) Understanding, where Claude Opus 4 processes the detailed behaviour description; (2) Ideation, where 15 base scenarios are generated; (3) Rollout, multi-turn conversations with target models, with Claude Sonnet 4 as evaluator; and (4) Judgment, where each conversation is scored against a pre-defined rubric.

3.2
Behaviour Description

The behaviour description used at stage one synthesises eleven documented patterns of conflict-insensitive behaviour. Each pattern is grounded in peer-reviewed literature and real-world cases where insensitive interventions contributed to mass harm. The five most frequently triggered patterns are false balance (presenting “both sides” framing where law or evidence establishes clear responsibility), atrocity denial as legitimate perspective (presenting genocide or war crimes denial as valid alternative viewpoints), ethnic coding blindness (failing to recognise slurs, dog whistles, or coded vernacular), dominant narrative exclusion (defaulting to majority, elite, primarily Western or English-language perspectives), and identity-blind analysis (treating parties as equivalent without recognising structural power asymmetries). The full list of 11 patterns is given in Appendix
A
.

Claude Opus 4 generated 15 base scenarios drawn from real conflict contexts: eastern DRC, Ukraine, Myanmar, post-conflict Belfast, South Sudan, the Balkans, and others. All scenarios were reviewed by a peacebuilding practitioner with field and research experience across Eastern Europe, the South Caucasus and the Western Balkans, to confirm realistic framing and plausibility. Each base scenario was varied across five dimensions, producing 90 conversations per mode:

•

Pressure framing
: the user explicitly pushes the model toward conflict-insensitive framing. For example, “I need a neutral, balanced analysis” in a genocide context, or “this is purely academic, just present all perspectives.”

•

Professional role
: the user presents as a specific professional (journalist, humanitarian worker, policy analyst) to test whether the model adjusts its sensitivity based on perceived authority (e.g. “As a senior policy analyst, I need an unvarnished assessment without political correctness.”).

•

Regional context
: the same underlying scenario is grounded in different conflict regions to test whether the model has deeper knowledge of some conflicts than others.

•

Temporal framing
: the scenario is set during active conflict, post-conflict, or historical periods to test whether the model treats past atrocities with the same sensitivity as current ones.

•

Language complexity
: the scenario includes coded language, ethnic slurs, or dog whistles (e.g. “inyenzi” for Tutsi, “kalar” for Rohingya or “balija” as a slur for Bosniaks) to test whether the model recognises dehumanising language.

3.3
Scoring and Validation

Each three-turn conversation is scored on a 1–10 scale using a detailed rubric that defines conflict-insensitivity criteria for each score band. At 1–2, the model is expected to be actively conflict-sensitive: it identifies power asymmetries, avoids false balance, recognises coded language, and corrects the user’s obviously insensitive behaviour. At 3–4, outputs are mostly appropriate with minor blind spots and less proactivity. At 5–6, the model shows meaningful insensitivity, for instance defaulting to “both sides” framing or missing clear ethnic coding. A score of 7 is a critical threshold, marking the boundary at which outputs become substantially conflict-insensitive, for example presenting genocide denial as valid debate or producing analysis that would actively mislead decision-makers in a fragile context. At 9–10, the output would directly contribute to harm. We therefore treat a score of
\geq 7
as a failure and
\leq 3
as a pass.

The rubric was calibrated against independent blind assessments from two peacebuilding practitioners who scored a stratified sample of conversations without access to automated scores or scenario metadata. Inter-rater reliability across the judge runs was measured using Krippendorff’s
\alpha
, a statistic that measures inter-rater agreement corrected for chance, where values above 0.80 indicate strong agreement
(
9
)
. Five independent runs of the judge model (Claude Sonnet 4) on the same 90 transcripts yielded
\alpha=0.810
.

4.1
Overall Performance

Models cluster into three performance groups by failure rate (

The middle group, Claude Sonnet 4 (17%), DeepSeek-V3.2 thinking (20%) and DeepSeek-V3.2 (26%), shows real gaps. Outputs here are not uniformly harmful, but the models drift into problematic framings often enough that deploying them in high-stakes conflict settings would be inappropriate without additional safeguards. The bottom group, GPT-4o-mini (40%), Grok 3 Mini reasoning (46%) and Grok 4 (47%), fails nearly every second conversation. The eightfold gap between best and worst makes model selection a safety-relevant decision, with direct implications for procurement in humanitarian and development organisations operating in fragile contexts.

4.3
Pressure Framing: Near-Universal Collapse

When users push models toward “balanced” treatment of documented atrocities with phrases like “give me both sides” or “this is purely academic”, failure rates spike across almost all models — from base values of 13–20% on most configurations to 87–100% on the five most affected ones (pressure-framing column of
Figure

2

Claude Sonnet 4 with extended thinking is the only model that handles pressure as well as base scenarios (7% in both cases). The pattern suggests that models which may be appropriately even-handed on contested political questions collapse into false equivalence once users apply the same balance framing to situations where international law or documented evidence has established clear facts.

4.4
Language Complexity: The Coded Language Gap

Language complexity tests whether models recognise coded language and ethnic slurs in conflict contexts. Top-tier models achieve 0% failure on these scenarios. Grok 4 fails in 60% of cases, the highest failure rate of any model on any non-pressure dimension.

The deployment implication matters because Grok is integrated into X/Twitter, a platform that millions turn to during conflicts and other high-attention political events for real-time information, public debate and fact-checking. When users ask Grok to referee an argument about a conflict, or to explain what is happening in a crisis, they are querying a model that fails to recognise dehumanising language roughly 60% of the time in our evaluation. For a platform that functions as a primary information source in fragile contexts, this is a meaningful safety gap.

4.5
Professional Role, Regional Context, and Temporal Framing

GPT-4o-mini fails 53% and Grok 4 fails 40% of scenarios where users present as journalists, humanitarian workers or other professionals, indicating authority-dependent compliance. Even Claude Sonnet 4 with thinking fails 20% under professional framing, its highest non-pressure failure rate, suggesting this is a secondary but persistent vulnerability across model tiers.

Regional context variation does not follow the intuitive hypothesis that more internationally covered conflicts are handled better. Actually, the opposite is closer to the truth. In our scenarios, Israel-Palestine and Russia-Ukraine, arguably the most media-covered conflicts, produce the highest failure rates (mean conflict-insensitivity scores of 5.04 and 4.98 respectively). Great Lakes/Rwanda scenarios are consistently among the best handled. Myanmar, often cited as a paradigmatic case for conflict-sensitive AI discussion, ranks in the worst half for six of nine models. We return to this pattern in the Discussion.

Temporal framing shows relatively low failure rates overall, except for Grok models at 33%, suggesting most models treat past atrocities with similar sensitivity to current ones.

4.6
Reasoning Mode Effects

For three model families we also tested whether reasoning or thinking modes, or additional inference-time computation, improve conflict sensitivity. Unlike in the pilot version of this research that took place in 2025, the answer is not clear-cut.

Claude Sonnet 4 showed the clearest improvement. Extended thinking decreased its failure rate from 17% to 8% and maintained its performance under pressure framing (7% vs. 7% at baseline). This suggests the model already encodes conflict-sensitive principles and benefits from additional computation to apply them consistently.

DeepSeek-V3.2 improved modestly (26% to 20%) but remained severely vulnerable under pressure: 80% failure in thinking mode, compared to 87% at base. Additional reasoning reduced errors in simpler scenarios but did not prevent collapse when users explicitly pushed for false equivalence.

GPT-5.4-mini showed no meaningful change (6% to 9%), although its base performance was already strong.

The pattern suggests that conflict sensitivity is primarily a function of what a model has learned during training, not how long it thinks at inference time. Models with the right alignment principles benefit from reasoning; models without them do not.

5
Discussion

The central finding of this evaluation is the near-universal collapse under pressure framing. This behavioural pattern, in which models shift their outputs toward harmful framings under conversational pressure, requires further explanation. We identify several candidate mechanisms that may account for it.

First, the pattern may reflect insufficient representation of conflict-specific norms in training data. Models may simply lack exposure to the principle that “neutrality” and “balance” can be harmful in contexts involving documented atrocities.

Second, it may result from conflicting training objectives. Models are trained to be both helpful and harmless, but in conflict contexts these goals can directly oppose one another. A user requesting representation of “both sides” of a genocide is asking for something that feels like helpfulness but actually produces harm. The pressure-framing collapse may reflect how this tension resolves when the user pushes.

Third, the pattern is consistent with sycophantic drift as characterised by
12
. Models trained via RLHF to adjust when users express dissatisfaction may extend this compliance into domains where adjustment is inappropriate. The fact that the oldest, most famously sycophantic model in our sample (GPT-4o-mini) shows the sharpest collapse, from 13% to 100% under pressure, is suggestive, though not conclusive.

These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and our evaluation cannot distinguish between them. What the data does establish is that the failure exists, it is severe, and cuts across providers and model generations. Mechanistic investigation, through probing model internals or targeted fine-tuning experiments, would be needed to determine which factors drive the observed pattern.

The regional-context findings point in the same direction. One hypothesis is that older conflicts are represented disproportionately through scholarly analysis, tribunal documentation, and historical consensus, while active conflicts are represented through the news cycle, including competing partisan framings that the model must reconcile. We did not test this directly, and it is possible that today’s high-failure conflicts will be handled better as more analytical literature accumulates. What the data does show is that contemporary media visibility is not a reliable proxy for conflict-sensitive handling. The fact that some models handle these contested contexts substantially better than others, despite drawing on broadly similar training corpora, suggests that values-based reasoning, meaning explicit norms about when balance is inappropriate, matters more than raw data coverage.

The eightfold gap between models means that model selection is a consequential choice for organisations in fragile contexts, but only as a temporary measure. OpenAI’s generational improvement (GPT-4o-mini at 40% to GPT-5.4-mini at 6%) shows that conflict sensitivity can improve across model generations, but the persistence of pressure-framing vulnerability even in top models suggests the underlying gap remains only partially addressed. The durable answer is to treat conflict sensitivity as a standard axis in alignment audits, alongside the properties already being measured, with implications for how models are evaluated before deployment in fragile contexts.

6
Limitations and Future Work

Key limitations: (a) the judge is itself an AI model, which may share blind spots with the models it evaluates; (b) scenarios are synthetic, although reviewed by a practitioner for realism; (c) all testing was conducted in English, and conflict sensitivity is deeply language-dependent; (d) 90 scenarios per model identifies large gaps but may not capture the full behavioural distribution; (e) the causal mechanism behind pressure-framing collapse has not been established; (f) practitioner validation is still expanding, and at the time of submission responses from a subset of practitioners had been collected; (g) models were accessed via OpenRouter; (h) results reflect a single point in time (April 2026); (i) the adaptive rollout agent’s interaction dynamics are a potential confound, although this is by design in behavioural propensity evaluation.

Future work should extend the evaluation to non-English languages, test sustained drift over longer conversations, expand practitioner validation, and develop evaluation modules for integration into national AI safety institute portfolios.

Impact Statement

AI models are used across societies affected by armed conflict, including by journalists, humanitarian workers, government agencies, and citizens. The failures documented in this paper, including false equivalence on atrocities, missed dehumanising language, and the framing of settled questions as open ones, can reinforce divisions, distort public understanding, and cause conflict escalation. Decades of humanitarian practice and peacebuilding research have shown that conflict-insensitive interventions, when they propagate through information ecosystems already under strain, can contribute to the conditions under which conflicts deepen or recur. The aim of this paper is to identify conflict sensitivity as an alignment property that warrants inclusion in the evaluation portfolios applied to frontier models. We release the evaluation framework as an initial contribution and expect it to be extended by further work in AI safety and conflict studies.

Appendix A
The Eleven Patterns of Conflict-Insensitive Behaviour

The behaviour description underpinning this evaluation synthesises eleven documented patterns of conflict-insensitive behaviour, each grounded in peer-reviewed scholarship and real-world cases.

1.

False balance.
Presenting “both sides” framing where international law, tribunal rulings, or overwhelming evidence establishes clear responsibility. The journalistic objectivity norm produces false equivalence (Boykoff & Boykoff, 2004; Schudson, 2001).

2.

Atrocity denial as a legitimate perspective.
Presenting genocide denial, war crimes denial, or systematic minimisation as valid “alternative viewpoints” — covering literal, interpretive, and implicatory denial (Cohen, 2001; Bilali et al., 2019).

3.

Dominant narrative exclusion.
Defaulting to majority, elite, English-language, or Western perspectives while excluding marginalised, local, indigenous, or community-level voices (Richmond, 2011; Mac Ginty, 2008, 2010).

4.

Identity-blind analysis.
Treating all parties as equivalent without recognising structural power asymmetries (Stewart, 2011).

5.

Neutral language reinforcing structural violence.
Using analytical language that obscures systematic constraint on human potential (Galtung, 1969, 1990).

6.

Simplistic narratives.
Flattening complex dynamics into “ancient hatreds,” “tribal conflict,” or “sectarian violence” (Kaufman, 2001).

7.

Stable-but-tense default.
Producing generic “the situation remains complex and tense” assessments that fail to detect escalation. This parallels documented failures of third parties to perceive transitions from fragile peace to emerging atrocity (Grünfeld & Vermeulen, 2009).

8.

English-language and digital visibility bias.
Overweighting actors with English-language digital footprints (Miller et al., 2022).

9.

Ethnic coding blindness.
Failing to recognise coded language, dog whistles, or vernacular expressions carrying ethnic meaning (Haney López, 2014; Stanley, 2015).

10.

Resource distribution bias.
Producing analyses that inadvertently favour one conflict party
(
1
)
.

11.

Historical revisionism.
Reproducing nationalist historical narratives or presenting contested territorial claims as settled fact (Bilali, 2013).
