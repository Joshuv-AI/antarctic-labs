Recurrent Pulmonary Vein Stenosis After Right Upper Pulmonary Vein Venting
Authors: Mohit Bandla, Christopher Zhou, Gene Osuoha, Jeffrey Taylor, Zaid Zayyad, Joaquim Barboza, Khalil Ibrahim, Khaled Abdelhady
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIatrogenic pulmonary vein (PV) stenosis results from radiofrequency ablation or surgical trauma, causing venous narrowing that may lead to irreversible pulmonary hypertension if untreated.Case SummaryA 77-year-old woman with recent mitral valve replacement aided by right upper PV (RUPV) venting, left ventricular pseudoaneurysm repair, and atrial flutter radiofrequency ablation presents with progressive dyspnea. History of Presentation

A 77-year-old woman presented to the cardiology clinic for follow-up with symptomatic dyspnea. Her symptoms had been progressive after multiple recent cardiac interventions, including a redo surgical mitral valve replacement (MVR) 8 months prior and percutaneous left ventricle pseudoaneurysm repair 1 month prior. An electrocardiogram showed a normal sinus rhythm without acute ischemic changes. Transthoracic echocardiography was completed, which demonstrated normal left ventricular function with an ejection fraction of 55% to 60%, mildly enlarged right ventricle, moderate tricuspid regurgitation, and a smaller residual pseudoaneurysm compared with the prior study. The new bioprosthetic valve was functioning well with no concerns for valve failure.

Physical Examination

On examination, blood pressure was 127/78 mm Hg, with a heart rate of 88 beats/min. The patient had an oxygen saturation of 98% on room air and was in no acute distress; cardiopulmonary examination was otherwise unremarkable. There was no evidence of lower extremity edema or volume overload.

Past Medical History

The patient has a complex cardiac history, including bioprosthetic MVR open surgery (2024) and a left ventricle apical pseudoaneurysm treated percutaneously with an Amplatzer (Abbott) Ventricular Septal Defect occluder device (2024). Additional comorbidities include heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, atrial flutter status post radiofrequency ablation (RFA), hypertension, type 2 diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and chronic kidney disease.

Differential Diagnosis

Given elevated right-sided pressures on echocardiography in the setting of recent cardiac procedures and chronic dyspnea, the highest on the differential was pulmonary hypertension (PHTN), prompting pursuit of a right heart catheterization (RHC) to determine the PHTN group. There was direct cannulation and closure of the right upper pulmonary vein (RUPV) to vent the left ventricle after the patient's surgical MVR. RFA can be considered; however, the atrial flutter ablation occurred at the cavotricuspid isthmus, making pulmonary vein (PV) stenosis less likely.

Other diagnoses considered were prosthetic mitral valve dysfunction, pseudoaneurysm repair complication, and/or heart failure exacerbation. Pulmonary embolism remained on the differential given her elevated risk.

Investigations

Initial RHC revealed mild elevation in pulmonary artery pressures to 39/18/27 mm Hg and an unequal pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) of 25 mm Hg in the RUPV territory compared with 14 mm Hg in the right lower, left upper, and left lower pulmonary arteries, suggesting regional venous obstruction. The left ventriculogram showed that the left ventricular pseudoaneurysm remained fully occluded. A chest computed tomography angiography (CTA) then showed narrowed right-sided PVs when compared with the left (no pulmonary embolism) (Figure 1). Several attempts were made to wire the RUPV but without success. Intraprocedural transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) showed trivial RUPV flow into the left atrium with normal flow from the other PVs (Video 1). An initial angiogram of RUPV raised concern for chronic total occlusion (defined as functionally occluded severe stenosis with residual microchannel flow), with surrounding collaterals suggesting isolated stenosis (Video 2). Once the occlusion was successfully crossed, intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) also supported a focal area of stenosis (Figure 2).

Management

A heart team approach determined a percutaneous approach given the patient's 2 prior sternotomies. After an initial unsuccessful attempt, the patient elected to retry the procedure via shared decision-making.

Femoral vein access was obtained and positioned with transseptal puncture using a Baylis RF needle (Boston Scientific) and an Agilis steerable sheath (Abbott). A Turnpike LP microcatheter supported wire escalation, eventually crossing with an Astato 20 wire (ASAHI INTECC) (with some difficulty). The RUPV was sequentially dilated until IVUS could cross, revealing focal stenosis and a small maximal luminal area in the healthy PV segment. Angioplasty was performed using 3.5- and 4.0-mm noncompliant balloons. Persistent focal stenosis required a 4.0 × 10-mm Wolverine Cutting balloon (Boston Scientific), which dilated the area. Finally, 3.5 × 30-mm Agent and 4.0 × 40-mm IN.PACT drug-coated balloons (Boston Scientific) (DCBs) were deployed (Videos 3 and 4). The procedure was complicated by hemoptysis, pulmonary edema, and delayed extubation from pulmonary reperfusion injury. Postprocedure TEE showed restored RUPV flow. The patient was discharged on rivaroxaban and clopidogrel, with approximately 2 months of improved dyspnea before unfortunate symptom recurrence.

Three months post-DCB angioplasty, repeat TEE revealed high-velocity RUPV flow into the left atrium, consistent with restenosis (Video 5). Repeat RHC showed recurrent PHTN with pulmonary artery pressures of 54/21/37 mm Hg and PCWP of 21 mm Hg. Transseptal puncture was performed, and the Agilis catheter was advanced toward the RUPV using a Sion blue guidewire (ASAHI INTECC). TEE and venogram confirmed persistent stenosis with patency. The PV was predilated with 4.0- and 6.0-mm noncompliant balloons, followed by repeat DCB angioplasty using a 6 × 40-mm IN.PACT balloon (Medtronic). Follow-up TEE showed restored flow with reduced pulmonary artery pressure to 45/18/27 mm Hg. However, dyspnea recurred within 1 month.

One month after the second DCB, TEE again demonstrated RUPV flow acceleration consistent with restenosis. Given recurrent DCB failures, a 6 × 37-mm Medtronic Visipro (Medtronic) balloon-expandable stent was deployed via a transseptal approach under TEE and IVUS guidance and postdilated to 7 mm (Video 6). IVUS and angiogram confirmed excellent flow (Video 7).

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient continued to be dyspneic at all follow-up visits; however, she was now able to complete housework and instrumental activities of daily living, a substantial improvement for her. A repeat RHC 1 month after stent placement showed only precapillary PHTN with a normal PCWP of 11 mm Hg, including the RUPV territory. A TEE 2 months later confirmed a normal flow pattern through the RUPV stent, and there has been no further evidence of restenosis. She continued to be followed in the clinic and was most recently seen 3 months postprocedure and continued to do well from a functional and respiratory standpoint. Her refractory dyspnea may be explained by precapillary PHTN and chronic left heart failure.

Discussion

PV stenosis remains a challenging iatrogenic complication of cardiac interventions, with RFA for atrial fibrillation being the most common iatrogenic cause in contemporary practice.1 However, incidence has decreased with newer technologies such as pulsed-field ablation.2 PV stenosis can also occur due to congenital causes (such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia) and acquired noniatrogenic causes (such as sarcoidosis or malignancy).3 The pathophysiology involves vascular injury (thermal from RFA or mechanical from trauma) leading to replacement of necrotic myocardial tissue with collagen and intimal hyperplasia. This can progress to venous narrowing that can extend upstream to small pulmonary vessels and result in irreversible PHTN if left untreated.

Iatrogenic PV stenosis can occur after RUPV venting, either through direct damage from cannulation or a suture clamping down on the vein during closure. In the literature, there is one documented case of this, in which a left atrial vent was placed during cardiopulmonary bypass through the RUPV, which was sutured closed on removal, with the most plausible explanation being PV stenosis at the cannulation site.4 Clinical manifestations typically appear 3 to 6 months postprocedure as the fibroproliferative response evolves. This patient's symptoms began approximately 8 months after MVR, consistent with the expected timeline. PV stenosis presents nonspecifically with dyspnea, cough, hemoptysis, and chest discomfort, often leading to diagnostic delays. In one large study, 82% of patients with severe PV stenosis were symptomatic at diagnosis, with 67% experiencing dyspnea, 45% with cough, and 27% with hemoptysis.5 This patient's persistent dyspnea, elevated right-sided pressures on RHC, and normal pulmonary function tests appropriately prompted CTA evaluation for PVS.

Management of PV stenosis involves balancing appropriate care against restenosis risk. Literature suggests that stenting may show superior outcomes to DCB alone.6 A meta-analysis of 579 PVs demonstrated an overall 22.3% restenosis rate with stenting compared with 54% for DCB alone.7 PV stenting for occlusions is typically aborted when angiography confirms the absence of residual microchannels, as successful intervention requires at least a remnant channel, which is found in approximately one-third of veins deemed occluded by computed tomography.8 Even when a channel is present and intervention is technically feasible, totally occluded veins have the highest rates of restenosis and reintervention due to upstream negative remodeling, fibrosis, and anatomic limitations that often necessitate smaller stent sizes.

In our patient, despite microchannels being present, the small vessel caliber and complex RUPV anatomy prevented stent placement for initial treatment. Because of the rare etiology of the PV stenosis, using a cutting balloon was essential in this procedure to address the focal stenosis caused by the suture, which could not be resolved with angioplasty alone. Although we had hoped that the DCBs would prevent restenosis, we suspect that external factors related to the suture led to recurrence. Overall, we found that despite the restenosis, the prior DCBs created a more favorable environment for stenting, resulting in a vessel that was comparatively more patent.

Stent diameters ≥7 to 8 mm are preferred for durability, though achieving optimal sizing in totally occluded veins is often not possible, making this patient's eventual successful 6-mm stent to 7-mm postdilation particularly important for long-term patency.9 This may have only been possible because of the prior DCB treatments. A staged DCB angioplasty followed by stenting may be a promising new approach for the management of PV chronic total occlusions.9 DCBs can be the bridge to stenting, especially when stenting is not initially feasible or safe.

Conclusions

PV stenosis can be a rare but serious cause of symptomatic dyspnea, hemoptysis, and PHTN, and should be considered when there are persistent symptoms despite routine dyspnea work-up. The suspicion for PV stenosis should be even greater when there is an intervention or procedure in the left atrium that may cause anatomic injury to the PVs.

This case illustrates the pathophysiology, clinical progression, and management challenges associated with iatrogenic PV chronic total occlusions following direct mechanical injury after RUPV cannulation and closure. A sequential approach that starts with DCB and progresses to definitive stenting may be a reasonable strategy. Current literature supports stenting as the gold standard intervention for most cases of severe PV stenosis. Although this patient was not an ideal candidate for stenting at first, it eventually was able to be performed after 2 DCB angioplasties. Early recognition through PV imaging, such as CTA, in patients with unexplained dyspnea following cardiac procedures, is crucial to prevent progression to irreversible PHTN.
