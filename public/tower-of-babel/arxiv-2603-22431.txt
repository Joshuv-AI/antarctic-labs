Korn’s inequality from the viewpoint of calculus of variations
Gabriele Cassese

Abstract
. We study the best possible constants in Korn-type inequalities and their connection with Morrey’s problem in the calculus of variations. We adapt techniques from the analysis of the Beurling-Ahlfors transform to Korn’s inequality. In particular we show that the constant in Korn’s inequality admits a dimension-free bound, and we obtain an estimate that is sharp up to a factor of 3 \sqrt{3} . In the radial case, the estimate is sharp. We also establish several improvements to estimates in various function spaces. Using a weighted version of Burkholder’s differential subordination theorem, recently introduced in [J. Reine Angew. Math. 824 (2025), pp. 137-166], we also prove a dimension-free weighted version of the inequality for Muckenhoupt weights.



1.
Introduction

This paper addresses the problem dual to the one
studied in
[
23
]
:
obtaining upper bounds for the optimal constant
in Korn-type inequalities.
Korn’s inequality, proved by
Korn
[
53
,
54
]
for
p=2
and by
Krylov
[
56
]

in the general case, states that
for an open subset
\Omega
of

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, an exponent
p\in(1,\infty)

and
u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one has

(1)

\left(\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{A}(u)|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C(p,\Omega)\left(\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{E}(u)|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where

\mathcal{A}(u)=P_{\mathrm{Skew}}(\nabla u)\mathrel{:}=1/2(\nabla u-\nabla u^{t})
,

\mathcal{E}(u)\mathrel{:}=1/2(\nabla u+\nabla u^{t})

and
|\cdot|
is the Frobenius norm
on
M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
.
One often writes this equivalently as

(2)

\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C_{\mathrm{KN}}(p,\Omega)\left(\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{E}(u)|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

A stronger trace-free version of Korn’s inequality
also holds:

(3)

\left(\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{A}_{0}(u)|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C_{0}(p,\Omega,d)\left(\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{E}_{0}(u)|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where

\mathcal{E}_{0}(u)=\mathcal{E}(u)-\mathrm{Tr}(\nabla u)(\mathrm{Id})/d

and

\mathcal{A}_{0}(u)=\mathcal{A}(u)+\mathrm{Tr}(\nabla u)(\mathrm{Id})/d
.
These inequalities are particular cases
of the more general Calderón-Zygmund type inequality

(4)

\|D^{k}(f)\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim_{p,P}\|P(f)\|_{L^{p}}

that is valid for any elliptic homogeneous
differential operator of order
k
with constant
coefficients.
It is well known that the
inequality (
4
) fails
in the cases
p=1
and
p=\infty
unless
P

is trivial
(see
[
63
,
26
,
52
]
).
In the case of (
1
),
the failure is well understood.
By
[
20
]
, Korn’s inequality holds in the
Sobolev-Orlicz space
\dot{W}^{1}L^{\Phi}

if and only if

\Phi
satisfies both
the
\nabla_{2}
and
\Delta_{2}
conditions
(see section
6.1
for details).
This result clearly identifies the obstruction
at the endpoints:
at
p=1
, the function
\Phi_{1}(t)=t

satisfies
\Delta_{2}
but not
\nabla_{2}

while for
p=\infty

\Phi_{\infty}(t):=\begin{cases}t,\ &t\leq 1\\
\infty,\ &t>1\end{cases}

satisfies
\nabla_{2}

but not
\Delta_{2}
.
Most of the literature on
the inequality has focused on
the case
p=2
. In that setting,
the inequality is a direct consequence
of the identity

\|\mathcal{E}(u)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|\mathcal{A}(u)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\text{div}(u)\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

which implies

C(2,\Omega)=1
. The constant is in fact
domain-independent for all
p\in(1,\infty)
:

C(p,\Omega)=C(p,\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Indeed,
approximation with compactly supported functions
shows that

C(p,\mathbb{R}^{d})=\lim_{r\to\infty}C(p,B_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,r))=C(p,B_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,1))\leq C(p,\mathbb{R}^{d})

where the second equality follows from
the dilation invariance of Korn’s inequality.
Since for any domain
\Omega
containing
a ball
B(x_{0},r)
we have

C(p,B(x_{0},r))\leq C(p,\Omega)\leq C(p,\mathbb{R}^{d}),

the result follows.
Accordingly, we henceforth write
C(p,d)
to denote
Korn’s constant for domains in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Since a similar result holds for
C_{0}

and
C_{\mathrm{KN}}
, we will drop the dependence
on
\Omega
for them as well.

Korn’s inequality has many applications,
notably in linearised elasticity, where it
is employed to prove existence and regularity results
through coercivity estimates, and in
fluid mechanics; see
[
38
]
and references
therein. Moreover, in recent years a
new effort has been made to obtain simpler proofs
of the inequality (see for instance
[
28
]

and references therein) and to
extend it to different function spaces
(see for instance
[
72
]
).

The main objective of this paper is
to prove an almost sharp
dimension-free bound for Korn’s inequality.
More precisely, we prove the following

Theorem 1.1
.

Given
u\in\dot{W}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\|\mathcal{A}(u)\|_{L^{p}}\leq\sqrt{3}(p^{*}-1)\|\mathcal{E}(u)\|_{L^{p}},

where
p^{*}=\max(p^{\prime},p)
,
p^{\prime}
is the
Hölder conjugate of
p
.
In other words

(5)

C(p,d)\leq\sqrt{3}(p^{*}-1)

Moreover,

(6)

C(p,d)\geq p^{*}-1.

The same result holds true if we consider the
trace-free version of Korn’s inequality for
d\geq 3
:

(7)

p^{*}-1\leq C_{0}(p,d)\leq\sqrt{3}(p^{*}-1).

Finally, if
u
is generalised-radial,
meaning that
u(x)=r(|x|)x
for some

r\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}\to M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, then
we have the following stronger result:

(8)

\|\mathcal{A}(u)\|_{L^{p}}\leq(p^{*}-1)\|\mathcal{E}(u)\|_{L^{p}}

and, if
p\geq 2,d=2
, the constant cannot be improved.

These results naturally suggest the conjecture
that
p^{*}-1
is in fact the sharp constant
in all the above inequalities
. This
is in line with the Iwaniec conjecture
about the norm of
the Beurling-Ahlfors operator
and its generalisation to higher dimensions.
As we will show in section
5
,
the above conjecture would follow if one could show
that a certain rank-one convex integrand
is quasiconvex at
0
, just as in the
case of Beurling-Ahlfors.
These
L^{p}
estimates combine naturally
with a version of the Riesz-Thorin
theorem for Orlicz spaces obtained
in
[
49
]
.
As a consequence, we obtain a dimension-free
bound for the Korn
inequality in all Orlicz spaces
in which the inequality holds:

Theorem 1.2
.

Given an
N
-function
\Phi
satisfying
both the
\Delta_{2}

and
\nabla_{2}
conditions
and
u\in\dot{W}^{1}L^{\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\|\mathcal{A}(u)\|_{L^{\Phi}}\leq K\max(p^{*}-1,q^{*}-1)\|\mathcal{E}(u)\|_{L^{\Phi}},

where
p,q
are
\Phi
’s indices
(see section
6.1
)
and K is a constant that can be explicitly estimated
and in particular is independent of
d
.
A modular version
of the inequality also holds.

Finally, combining our bounds with
recent results from
[
29
]

on weighted estimates for
differentially subordinated martingales,
we obtain an
A_{p}
-weighted version of our estimates
(see
[
50
]
for more
information about weighted Sobolev spaces):

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
,
w\in A_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

u\in\dot{W}^{1,p}(w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Then

\|\mathcal{A}(u)\|_{L^{p}(w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)}\leq K(p^{*}-1)[w]_{A_{p}^{\text{Heat}}}^{\max\left(1,\frac{1}{p-1}\right)}\|\mathcal{E}(u)\|_{L^{p}(w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)},

where
K
is a constant that can be explicitly estimated
and in particular is independent of
d
.

Here
[\cdot]_{A_{p}^{\text{Heat}}}
denotes
a quantity equivalent to the usual
Muckenhoupt characteristic
that will be defined below.

These upper bounds rest on new estimates for
R\otimes R
, a matrix-valued analogue of the second-order
Riesz transform.
Although the improved Korn inequality
is of independent interest,
it also yields stronger
coercivity estimates when
p^{*}-1
is sufficiently large,
and hence new existence results
for related variational problems.

The inequality admits several other forms
on bounded domains with
boundary conditions; see, for instance
[
66
]
.
We do not discuss those variants here,
although our methods should also lead to improved
estimates in those settings.

(summing over the index of the Brownian motion, instead
of summing over
\partial_{i}
) is that we can now
leverage the orthogonality of the coordinates
of
\mathcal{B}
,
which implies

\langle\mathcal{Z}^{T},\mathcal{Z}^{T}\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}|\partial_{i}U(f_{k,j})(Y_{s}^{T})|^{2}\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{t}|\nabla U(f)(Y_{s}^{T})|^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

It follows that
\langle\mathcal{Z}^{T},\mathcal{Z}^{T}\rangle=\langle M^{T},M^{T}\rangle
,
so
\mathcal{Z}^{T}
is differentially subordinate
to
M^{T}
.
As one easily checks,
even in the matricial case we have convergence
in
L^{p}
as
T\to\infty

of
(R\otimes R)_{T}(f)(x)\mathrel{:}=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{Z}^{T}_{T}|Y_{T}^{T}=(x,0)\right)
to
R\otimes R(f)
. This allows us to obtain

\displaystyle\|R\otimes R(f)\|_{L^{p}(M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}\leq\lim_{T\to\infty}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathcal{Z}^{T}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L^{p}}\leq\lim_{T\to\infty}(p^{*}-1){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|M^{T}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq(p^{*}-1)\|f\|_{L^{p}}.

∎

Remark 5
.

The martingale formula we used is not the best
possible when one is concerned with
the scalar second-order Riesz transform

in particular, taking

(where

allows one to see that

(and the
inequality is actually attained,

[

using this choice of

makes it harder to use the
orthogonality properties of

and results
in an overall worse bound.
As we shall see presently, we still have
a linear algebra trick up our sleeve
to improve the bound.

This, however, is not enough to imply the upper bound
in Theorem

advantage of the problem’s structure via
the following lemma:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathrm{Sym}(d)
be a tensor.
Then

\sum_{i,j,k}|a_{i,j,k}-a_{j,i,k}|^{2}\leq 3|a|^{2}

and the constant
3
is sharp.

Proof.

Let
V=(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes 3}
and let
W
be the subspace

W=\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathrm{Sym}(d)
.
We define the operators
\sigma,\tau

on
V
as follows:

\sigma(a)_{i,j,k}=a_{j,i,k}
and

\tau(a)_{i,j,k}=a_{i,k,j}
or
in other words
\sigma(a)(x,y,z)=a(y,x,z)
and

\tau(a)(x,y,z)=a(x,z,y)
. Then proving the
inequality is equivalent to proving that,
on
W
, we have

|(I-\sigma)a|^{2}\leq 3|a|^{2}.

Since
|(I-\sigma)a|^{2}=2|a|^{2}-2\langle a,\sigma a\rangle=2|a|^{2}-2\langle a,\pi\circ\sigma a\rangle
,
where
\pi
is the projection from
V
onto
W
,
it is enough to prove that the minimal eigenvalue

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}
of the operator

T\mathrel{:}=\pi\circ\sigma\circ\pi

acting on
W
is
-1/2
.
As one can check via some arithmetic,
T
satisfies

T^{2}=\frac{1}{4}(I+\tau)+\frac{1}{2}T.

Restricting this to
W
we get

T^{2}=\frac{1}{2}I+\frac{1}{2}T,

i.e.
(T-\mathrm{I})(2T+I)=0
. The result follows,
since
T
is self-adjoint and hence diagonalizable.
∎

Proof of the upper bound
in Theorem

.

Instead of dealing with the martingale
representation of

and then applying the projection, we directly deal with
the martingale representation of

with the added assumption that

Namely, we set

The quadratic variation of this process is

\langle Z^{T}_{t}\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k,i,j}|\partial_{i}Uf_{j,k}(Y_{s}^{T})-\partial_{j}Uf_{i,k}(Y_{s}^{T})|^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Notice that the tensor

We can thus apply Lemma

where

is defined as above.
The rest of the proof follows by the same argument as in
Proposition

.

[
19
]
:

Proposition 6.2
.

Given a bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
the inequality

(31)

C_{1}+\int_{\Omega}\Phi\Bigl(C_{2}|P_{\mathrm{Sym}(d)}(\nabla u)|\Bigr)\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{\Omega}\Psi\Bigl(|P_{\mathrm{Skew}(d)}(\nabla u)|\Bigr)\mathrm{d}x

holds for some
C_{1},C_{2}
for all
u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})

if and only if the Calderón operator
S=C+C^{*}
, where

Cf(t)\mathrel{:}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f(s)\mathrm{d}s

and
C^{*}
is its formal adjoint, is bounded between

L^{\Phi}([0,1])

and
L^{\Psi}([0,1])
. The same holds for Korn’s
trace-free inequality.

Proof.

We can without loss of generality assume
\Psi

to be real-valued, for otherwise a smooth
approximation of
u_{1}(x)
defined as
in Proposition
4.4

(and similar functions
in higher dimensions)
proves the inequality cannot hold.

Let
f_{C}
denote the integrand associated
to (
31
),
that is

f_{C}(A)\mathrel{:}=\Phi(C|P_{\mathrm{Sym}(d)}(A)|)-\Psi(|P_{\mathrm{Skew}(d)}(A)|).

Then a necessary condition for (
31
) to hold is

f_{C}^{rc}(0)>-\infty
.
By considering the martingale

f_{n}(e_{1}\otimes e_{d}+e_{d}\otimes e_{1})+g_{n}(e_{1}\otimes e_{d}-e_{d}\otimes e_{1})
,
this can only happen if

\mathbb{E}(\Phi(C|f_{n}|))\geq\mathbb{E}(\Psi(|g_{n}|)).

This only happens if the martingale
\pm 1
transform is bounded,
which by
[
6
]
is equivalent to the
boundedness of
S
. The sufficiency of this condition follows
from standard interpolation arguments:
since
R\otimes R
is self adjoint
and bounded on
L^{2}
, it follows that
(see
[
22
, Appendix]
)

(R\otimes Rf)^{*}(x)\lesssim Sf^{*}(x),

where
f^{*}
is the decreasing right-continuous
rearrangement of
f
.
The result follows, since rearrangement
preserves Orlicz modulars and if
S

is bounded between
L^{\Phi}
and
L^{\Psi}

it automatically satisfies the required modular inequality.
∎

Remark 12
.

The above argument can be extended
to prove equivalence with the norm
inequality, and to more general function spaces and
differential operators, though
this requires some new arguments.
This will be explored in future work,
in part jointly with Bernd Kirchheim, Jan
Kristensen and Wen Qi Zhang.

6.2.
Korn in weighted Sobolev spaces

Recently, Domelevo, Petermichl and
Skreb (
[
29
]
) have
extended Burkholder’s differential subordination results
to weighted
L^{p}
spaces.
This result, combined
with the space-time martingale
representation of
R\otimes R
,
yields a weighted Korn inequality.

It is well known that, given a Calderón-Zygmund operator
and a Muckenhoupt
A_{p}
weight
w
, it acts continuously
on weighted
L^{p}(w)
spaces,
with the following bound:

\|T\|_{L^{p}(w)\to L^{p}(w)}\leq C_{d,p}(T)[w]_{A_{p}}^{\max(1,\frac{1}{p-1})}.

This result, known as the
A_{p}
conjecture,
was proved by Hytönen in
[
40
]
. In our context, it is
natural to conjecture
\|R\otimes R\|_{L^{p}(w)\to L^{p}(w)}\lesssim_{d}(p^{*}-1)[w]_{A_{p}}^{\max(1,1/(p-1))}
and perhaps even that the inequality
should hold dimension-free.
We will prove this is indeed the case as a straightforward
application of the weighted
L^{p}
estimates
proved in
[
29
]
,
provided one replaces
[\cdot]_{A_{p}}
by
an equivalent characteristic.

We define the following notion of weights,
which, given our
reliance on heating methods,
is much better suited to our approach
than Muckenhoupt’s original definition:

Definition
6

(Heat weight)
.

Given a function
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{Loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

we say
f\in A_{p}^{\mathrm{Heat}}
(the
p
-class of heat
weights) if
f>0
almost everywhere and

[f]_{A^{\text{Heat}}_{p}}\mathrel{:}=\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+}}|Uf(x,t)Ug(x,t)^{p-1}|<\infty,

where
Uf
denotes the heat extension of
f

and
Ug
the one of
g(x)\mathrel{:}=f(x)^{-1/(p-1)}
.

Considering this class of weights does not entail any loss of
generality, thanks to the following folklore result

Proposition 6.3

(
[
33
, Theorem 3.4]
,

[
68
, Theorem 3.2]
)
.

For
d\geq 1
,
p\in(1,\infty)

A^{\text{Heat}}_{p}=A_{p}

Remark 13
.

It is important to note that,
while the two spaces
A_{p}

and
A^{\text{{Heat}}}_{p}
coincide, this
does not imply that the Muckenhoupt and the heat
characteristic are linearly equivalent,
i.e. that

[w]_{A_{p}}\lesssim[w]_{A^{\text{Heat}}_{p}}\lesssim[w]_{A_{p}}
.
Instead, what we obtain is

[w]_{A_{p}}\lesssim[w]_{A^{\text{Heat}}_{p}}\lesssim D(w)[w]_{A_{p}},

where
D(w)
is the doubling constant
associated to the measure
w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
(which
can be bounded from above by

D(w)\lesssim 2^{dp}[w]_{A_{p}}
).
We also remark that the previous
result fails for the Poisson kernel because
of its slow decay, see section 3
of
[
33
]
for more details.
This is, in particular, the reason
why we choose to use the space-time
Brownian motion representation of
R_{i}R_{j}

instead of the background-diffusion one.

We will now use a weighted version of
Burkholder’s method
(
[
29
, corollary 1]
) to obtain

Theorem 6.3
.

Given a weight
w\in A_{p}^{\text{Heat}}

we have

C(p,w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\mathbb{R}^{d})\lesssim(p^{*}-1)[w]_{A_{p}^{\text{Heat}}}^{\max(1,\frac{1}{p-1})},

where
C(p,w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the best constant
for the associated Korn inequality.

Let us now recall the weighted
martingale estimate that
underlies the proof of the
A_{p}
-version
of our estimates:

Theorem 6.4

(
[
29
, Corollary 1]
)
.

Let
(X_{t}),(Y_{t})
be Hilbert-space-valued martingales and
assume that
X\preccurlyeq Y
.
Then, given a martingale
w\in A_{p}

with
p\in(1,\infty)
we have

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|X\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim(p^{*}-1)[w]_{A_{p}^{\text{mart}}}^{\max\left(\frac{1}{p-1},1\right)}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|Y\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L^{p}(w)}.

The last ingredient we shall need is the connection between
heat weights and martingale weights,
which is a straightforward consequence
of Itô’s formula.

Lemma 6.5

(

[
13
, Section 4]
)
.

Given a heat weight

w
, define
W
as
W^{T,n}_{t}=Uw(Y_{t}^{T,n})
,
where
Y_{t}^{T,n}
is the space-time Brownian motion with
uniform initial distribution on
[-n,n]^{d}
and
Uw

the heat extension of
w
. Then

[w]_{A_{p}^{\mathrm{Heat}}}\geq[W^{T,n}]_{A_{p}^{\mathrm{mart}}}

Remark 14
.

The reason we deal with a restricted version of
W

rather than with
W
itself is so as to sidestep the issue of
uniform integrability, which is not entirely trivial since
w

itself is not a priori in
L^{1}
and so
W
is not
necessarily uniformly
integrable so
W\notin A_{p}
.
This problem, however, disappears when one is working
on manifolds with finite measure;
in that case one can actually prove that we have
equality between the two notions of weight, see

[
59
, Theorem 4.2]
.

The weighted Korn estimates now follow from the
above weighted Burkholder estimate:

Proof.

Define the two martingales

\mathcal{Y}^{T,n}_{t}\mathrel{:}=Uf(Y^{T,n}_{t})
and

(\mathcal{Z}^{T,n}_{t})_{i,j}\mathrel{:}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{i}Uf_{k,j}(Y^{T,n}_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{B}^{k}_{s}

and similarly the non-truncated versions

\mathcal{Z}^{T}_{t}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{T}_{t}
.
The same proof as in Theorem
1.1

allows us to obtain

\mathcal{Z}^{T,n}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{Y}^{T,n}.

To connect the martingale-weighted Burkholder
with our weighted estimates for
R\otimes R
,
first notice that

\displaystyle\|R\otimes R(f)\|^{p}_{L^{p}(w)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|R\otimes R(f)|^{p}w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{Z}^{T}_{T}|Y^{T}_{T}=(x,0)\right)|^{p}w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left(|\mathcal{Z}_{T}^{T}|^{p}|Y_{T}^{T}=(x,0)\right)w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left(|\mathcal{Z}_{T}^{T}|^{p}Uw(Y_{T}^{T})|Y_{T}^{T}=(x,0)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{T}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathcal{Z}^{T}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{p}_{L^{p}(W^{T})}

and applying Theorem
6.4

to
\mathcal{Z}^{T,n}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{T,n}
, we get

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathcal{Z}^{T,n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L^{p}(W^{T,n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim(p^{*}-1)[W^{T,n}]_{A_{p}^{\mathrm{mart}}}^{\max\left(\frac{1}{p-1},1\right)}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathcal{Y}^{T,n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L^{p}(W^{T,n})}

\displaystyle\leq(p^{*}-1)[w]_{A_{p}^{\text{Heat}}}^{\max\left(\frac{1}{p-1},1\right)}\|f\|_{L^{p}(w)}.

Combining this with the previous estimate and standard limiting
arguments (recall that
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}
so
Uf(\cdot,t)

decays exponentially for each
t
) yields

\displaystyle\|R\otimes R(f)\|_{L^{p}(w)}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{T}\limsup_{n}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathcal{Z}^{T,n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L^{p}(W^{T,n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim(p^{*}-1)\limsup_{T}\limsup_{n}[W^{T,n}]_{A_{p}^{\text{mart}}}^{\max\left(\frac{1}{p-1},1\right)}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathcal{Y}^{T,n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L^{p}(W^{T,n})}

\displaystyle\leq(p^{*}-1)[w]_{A_{p}^{\text{Heat}}}^{\max\left(\frac{1}{p-1},1\right)}\|f\|_{L^{p}(w)},

which is enough to prove the theorem
thanks to (
9
).
∎

7.
Concluding remarks

7.1.
Burkholder functions and
quasiconvexity

Burkholder’s functional, in its form

\mathcal{G}_{p}(|P_{\mathrm{CO}^{-}(2)}(A)|,|P_{\mathrm{CO}^{+}(2)}(A)|),

has been the subject of very extensive study, in particular
on its conjectured quasiconvexity at
0
(see
[
4
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
35
]
). In view of the lower bound in Proposition

5.3
, investigating which properties
generalise to the decomposition

X\oplus X^{\perp}
in
M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
is a natural
direction of research.
In particular, the quasiconvexity
at
0
of the generalised Burkholder functional
seems interesting: in the general setting, many
rather useful symmetries (such as isotropy) break, and
as
X
gets smaller the bad part of
\mathcal{G}_{p}

(i.e.
|P_{X^{\perp}}(A)|
) gets harder to control.
This means that answering the quasiconvexity question
negatively (if at all possible) should be easier
in the generalised case, especially in high dimensions
(where the ratio
\mathrm{dim}(X)/\mathrm{dim}(M_{d}(\mathbb{R}))

gets smaller and smaller). Other than being
useful in the study of Morrey’s problem, such an
example could also be helpful in the study
of Iwaniec-Martin’s conjecture, as it would
give an indication of
which properties one has to leverage to prove the
conjecture.

7.2.
Extensions to other spaces

General Riemannian manifolds admit
an inequality of Korn-type (see
[
24
]
):
it would be interesting to see whether
the argument applied above admits an extension to such a general
setting.

In a different direction, one can ask whether estimates such as
this also hold for domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
when we impose
different boundary conditions rather than

u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
. One popular such condition is

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{A}(u)=0
, i.e. the
0
-momentum condition;
with these conditions, however, the
geometry of the domain becomes much more important
(see
[
47
]
and
[
58
]
).
For that formulation of Korn’s inequality,
and similarly for Korn’s inequality in its second form,
the calculus of variations approach used here
is not applicable any more
(at least, not as straightforwardly),
so such a study seems to be much more complicated.
