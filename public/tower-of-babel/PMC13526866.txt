Fatigue-associated gut bacteria in Japanese healthy adults characterized by metagenomic analysis
Authors: Hiroaki Masuoka, Takumi Miyatake, Jonguk Park, Hiroki Negishi, Rina Kurokawa, Hanae Tsuchihashi, Seiya Makino, Wataru Suda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emerging evidence suggests that fatigue caused by accumulated stress may serve as a prodromal symptom of psychiatric disorders, and gut microbiome dysbiosis has been reported in many such conditions. However, little is known about microbial and metabolic signatures associated with fatigue in otherwise healthy individuals. This study aimed to investigate associations between fatigue, the gut microbiome, and fecal metabolites in healthy Japanese adults. We identified characteristic microbial and metabolic differences specific to fatigued healthy individuals. Taxonomic analysis revealed a reduction in potentially beneficial bacteria and an enrichment of Escherichia coli in their gut microbiome. Functional profiling demonstrated enrichment of KEGG orthologs related to oxidative stress and depletion of energy-producing pathways. Correspondingly, key energy metabolites such as citrate were decreased. Notably, some fatigue-associated bacterial alterations overlapped with findings from external datasets on psychiatric disorders and myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome, suggesting associative overlap in gut microbial alterations. These findings suggest associations between host fatigue and gut microbiome alterations involving oxidative stress and impaired energy metabolism. The consistent overlap of fatigue-associated microbial changes with those observed in psychiatric disorders highlights the potential relevance of gut microbial signatures in fatigue-related biological states. This study provides a foundation for future studies on gut microbial and metabolic pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56821-x.

Introduction

The gut microbiome exists in the intestines of animals, including humans, and plays an important role in regulating host physiological functions. Dysregulation of the gut microbiome—commonly referred to as dysbiosis1—has been observed in patients with various diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease2 and metabolic diseases such as diabetes3. Dysbiosis has also been observed in psychiatric disorders, including depression4, bipolar disorder5, and schizophrenia6, as well as in myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS)7–11, highlighting a potential link between the gut microbiome, mental health, and fatigue.

Psychiatric disorders are illnesses of the brain and mind that significantly impair an individual’s ability to function in daily life. Their onset has long been discussed within theoretical frameworks such as the vulnerability-stress model12 and the differential susceptibility hypothesis13. These models suggest that individuals with heightened sensitivity to stress may experience progressive deterioration of mental functioning in response to psychosocial stress, eventually leading to overt psychiatric disorders. Notably, several psychiatric conditions are preceded by a transitional or prodromal phase—representing an early stage of progression toward clinical illness—characterized by non-specific symptoms such as fatigue, sleep disturbances, and reduced motivation14,15. Given the frequent occurrence of gut microbiome dysbiosis in individuals with psychiatric disorders, it is plausible that gut microbiome shifts may occur even during this pre-disease stage, potentially associated with later mental health deterioration. Thus, investigating gut microbiome characteristics in otherwise healthy individuals experiencing fatigue has considerable scientific importance for population-based prevention and early risk stratification.

What occurs within the host’s body during fatigue? From a physiological perspective, fatigue has been associated with increased oxidative stress resulting from an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidant defense systems16,17. Excessive accumulation of ROS is known to damage cells and DNA, thereby contributing to fatigue and the development of various diseases. In response, the host produces antioxidants to neutralize ROS and maintain redox homeostasis. Recent studies have suggested that the gut microbiome may also contribute to antioxidant production, and that alterations in microbial antioxidant capacity may be associated with increased oxidative stress in the gut under certain disease conditions18. For example, bacterial antioxidant systems, including superoxide dismutases and thioredoxin/glutaredoxin-related redox systems, are involved in detoxifying reactive oxygen species and maintaining microbial redox homeostasis19,20. These findings raise the possibility that microbiome-mediated redox imbalance may be linked to fatigue even in the absence of overt disease.

Despite its clinical relevance, there is no universally accepted standard method for assessing fatigue in healthy individuals. Non-invasive stress markers such as salivary cortisol have been explored, however, their variability due to sex differences and hormonal status limits their practical utility in mixed populations21. Consequently, subjective questionnaires, such as the Chalder Fatigue Questionnaire (CFQ), remain widely used and provide a validated, practical approach for assessing fatigue22.

In this study, we recruited 50 otherwise healthy adults and investigated the association between subjective fatigue assessed using the CFQ and the gut microbiome. Fecal samples were collected concurrently with fatigue assessment. Extracted DNA from the fecal microbiome was analyzed using shotgun metagenomic sequencing to characterize microbial composition and functional potential, and fecal metabolites were quantified by capillary electrophoresis-mass spectrometry and high-performance liquid chromatography. By focusing on subclinical fatigue in otherwise healthy individuals, this study aims to identify early microbiome-based signatures that may contribute to future disease risk assessment and preventive strategies.

Material & methods

Sample collection

Fifty healthy adult participants were recruited between April 2019 and March 2021 from employees of Meiji Co., Ltd. through a call for voluntary participation. All participants were asked to complete a questionnaire assessing fatigue levels and to submit a fecal sample. The physical information of participants was represented as Supplementary Table S1.

Evaluating the extent of fatigue

To assess fatigue, the CFQ was administered as a 14-item questionnaire using a Likert scoring method22(Supplementary Table S2), and a CFQ score ≥ 17 was used to define the fatigue group23. However, since no standardized cutoff for the CFQ has been established for the Japanese population, we evaluated the validity of this threshold using the Japanese version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), both of which have established criteria24–26(Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). Specifically, participants with a PSQI score ≥ 6 and an ESS score ≥ 5 were defined as having clearly evident sleep-related fatigue. Based on this definition, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to determine the cutoff of CFQ score that maximized sensitivity while maintaining a specificity of at least 80%.

Organic-acid analysis

The supernatant obtained after a ten-fold dilution of freshly collected feces in 300 µL Milli-Q water was sequentially mixed with 7.5 µL of 535 mg/mL ZnSO₄·7 H₂O and 7.5 µL of 172 mg/mL K₄[Fe(CN)₆]·3 H₂O. After vortexing, the suspension was centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.2 μm PVDF filter vial (1030–19022; Thomson Scientific, Oceanside, CA, USA) and kept on ice until analysis.

Organic acids (citrate, lactate, formate, acetate, propionate, butyrate, isobutyrate, isovalerate, malate, fumarate, and pyruvate) were quantified using a high-performance liquid chromatograph equipped with a conductivity detector (CDD-10Avp; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). A 10 µL aliquot of the clarified extract was injected onto an ICSep ICE ORH-801 analytical column (6.5 mm I.D. × 300 mm) fitted with an ICSep ICE ORH-801 guard cartridge (4.0 mm I.D. × 20 mm) (both from Transgenomic, Omaha, NE, USA) maintained at 55 °C. Isocratic elution was performed at a flow rate of 0.50 mL/ min using 7.5 mM p-toluenesulfonic acid as the mobile phase. The post-column conductivity reagent contained 7.5 mM p-toluenesulfonic acid, 150 µM EDTA-2Na, and 30 mM Bis-Tris.

External four-point calibration curves (0.5–10 mM) yielded coefficients of determination (r² ≥ 0.999) for all analytes. Peak areas were integrated using LabSolutions v5.97 (Shimadzu) and converted to concentrations (µmol/g wet weight) using the calibration equations.

Metabolomic analysis

Metabolomic analysis was conducted by Human Metabolome Technologies, Inc. (HMT) (Tsuruoka, Yamagata, Japan) using the Basic Scan service package. Culture supernatants were obtained by centrifugation to remove cellular debris. Each 80 µL sample was mixed with 20 µL of Milli-Q water containing internal standards (H3304–1002; HMT). The mixture was then subjected to filtration with a 5 kDa cutoff filter (ULTRAFREE MC PLHCC; Millipore, HMT) at 9,100 × g for 120 min at 4 °C to remove macromolecules. The resulting filtrate was subsequently analyzed. Measurements were performed using capillary electrophoresis time-of-flight mass spectrometry (CE-TOFMS), as previously described27–29. Separation was achieved with a fused silica capillary (50 μm I.D., 80 cm total length) and commercial electrophoresis buffers (H3301–1001 for cations and I3302–1023 for anions; HMT) as electrolytes. The mass spectrometer was operated in full-scan mode from m/z 50 to 1000. Peak extraction was carried out using MasterHands software (Keio University, Tsuruoka, Yamagata, Japan), which provided m/z, peak area, and migration time (MT)30. Signals corresponding to isotopomers, adducts, or fragments of known metabolites were removed. Remaining peaks were annotated against the HMT metabolite database based on m/z and MT. Peak areas were normalized to internal standards and sample volumes to obtain relative metabolite levels.

DNA extraction and Whole genome shotgun sequencing

Fecal samples were self-collected by participants on any day during the week prior to the follow-up test. Approximately 20 mg of each fecal sample was homogenized using the FastPrep-24 5G (MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA, USA) with 0.1 mm zirconia beads (EZ-Extract for DNA/RNA, AMR, Tokyo, Japan). DNA was then extracted using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), following the ‘Protocol Q’ described by Costea et al. 31, with a slight modification: the bead-beating time was reduced from 8 min to 4 min. The concentration of extracted DNA was quantified with a Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After sonication for DNA shearing using the ME220 (Covaris), a shotgun sequencing library was constructed using the TruSeq DNA Nano kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Library quality was evaluated with Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). The concentration of the library was also quantified using the Qubit Fluorometer. 150-bp paired-end reads were generated on the NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA). All sequencing was performed at the Meiji Holdings Co., Ltd.

Metagenome data processing

Metagenome data processing was performed according to previously described procedures with modification32. Duplicate reads and reads with a quality value (QV) of 20 or lower were removed using ParDRe (version 2.1.5-PC170509)33 and fastp (v0.20.0)34. Reads in which more than half of the bases had QV ≤ 20, as well as those shorter than 50 bp, were also excluded. Reads originating from the human genome (hg38) or the phiX bacteriophage were filtered out by mapping against the respective reference sequences using minimap2 (version 2.17-r941)35. The number of retained reads and their total lengths were summarized in Supplementary Table S5. To standardize sequencing depth across samples for comparison between groups, reads were randomly subsampled to 10 million reads per sample using seqtk (v1.2-r102-dirty)36. A depth of 10 million reads was selected because all samples exceeded this threshold after quality filtering, allowing us to retain all 50 participants while standardizing sequencing depth across samples. Using these subsampled reads, we calculated the composition (relative abundance) of taxa at the genus and species levels, (metagenome-assembled genomes (MAG), and functional genes.

Taxonomic profiling at both genus and species levels was performed using MetaPhlAn437. Reads passing quality control were then assembled into contigs with metaSPAdes (default settings), and contigs shorter than 500 bp were removed using seqkit (v2.3.0)38. Prediction of open reading frames was carried out using Prodigal (v2.6.3)39. For construction of non-redundant gene and protein sets, CD-HIT (v4.6) was applied after concatenating the nucleotide and amino acid sequences40,41. These de novo gene and protein collections were subsequently clustered with reference non-redundant sets derived from human gut MAGs available in the HumGut database, resulting in a comprehensive catalog of genes and proteins42. The amino acid catalog was functionally annotated against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database using KofamScan43–46. The gene catalog was also mapped to 10 million subsampled reads using Bowtie2 (v2.4.5)47 and KEGG orthology (KO) profiles were calculated as TPM using msamtools with thresholds of 95% identity and 75% coverage48.

Analysis of gut bacterial diversity

Taxonomic composition at the species level, as determined by MetaPhlAn437, was used to analyze gut bacterial diversity. Alpha diversity of the gut microbiome was assessed using species richness, the Shannon index, Pielou’s evenness index and the Simpson index49–51. Taxonomic composition was converted into a distance matrix based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity52 using the vegan package in R and Principal Coordinate of Analysis (PCoA) was performed to compare subjects with and without fatigue53. Bray–Curtis distances52 were then compared among three group combinations: fatigue vs. fatigue, fatigue vs. non-fatigue, and non-fatigue vs. non-fatigue.

Contig binning

All short reads were mapped to contigs from the respective samples using Bowtie2 47 and converted to BAM files with SAMtools54. Contig binning for individual samples was performed using MetaBAT2 55 and SemiBin2 56, and subsequently refined with MAGScoT57. MAGs obtained by MAGScoT were evaluated using CheckM2 58 and GUNC59. MAGs exhibiting > 90% completeness, < 5% contamination, and meeting the chimera detection criteria assessed by GUNC were classified as high-quality MAGs. High-quality MAGs were clustered into species level representatives using dRep (fastANI mode)60 with 95% average nucleotide identity and 50% coverage. Taxonomic annotations were performed using GTDB-Tk (v2.3.2)61 with GTDB release 214 62. Representative high-quality MAGs were also mapped to subsampled short reads using Bowtie2 47, and their compositions were calculated as relative abundances by msamtools (identity 95% and coverage 75%)48. GTDB-Tk marker gene alignments were used to generate a phylogenetic tree of all high-quality MAGs, excluding one archaeal MAG. Functional profiles at the MAG level were obtained and summarized from KofamScan results43.

Validation with external public disease data

Publicly available metagenomic datasets were obtained for validation purposes: major depression disorder (Chinese cohort; CNP0001162), bipolar disorder (Chinese cohort; CNP0002003), ME/CFS (US cohort; PRJNA379741, US cohort; PRJNA751448), and obesity with impaired glucose tolerance (Japanese cohort; PRJDB11444)4,5,7,8,63. The profiling of representative MAGs was performed for each public dataset and compared with our dataset.

Statistical tests

Statistical comparisons between two or more groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test64, and p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method65. Multivariable associations between metadata and features (bacterial genus, species, MAGs, KO and metabolites) were analyzed using the R package Maaslin2 66. All MaAsLin2 parameters were kept at the default values, and BMI was included as a fixed effect to adjust for potential confounding because BMI differed significantly between the fatigue and non-fatigue groups. Statistically significant KOs by MaAsLin2 were subjected to KEGG enrichment analysis at both the module and pathway levels using the clusterProfiler package. KEGG modules and pathways with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. For module enrichment analysis, minGSSize was set to 5, while all other parameters were left at their default values. Bray–Curtis dissimilarities between subjects with and without fatigue were tested using the permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) test67. Random Forest classification was performed as an exploratory analysis to evaluate whether fatigue-associated microbial features contained discriminatory information for fatigue status. The analysis was performed separately using genus-level, species-level, and KO-level features that were significantly associated with fatigue status in the MaAsLin2 analysis. The dataset was divided into training and test sets using stratified 70:30 random splitting, and this procedure was repeated five times. Within each training set, feature ranking was performed using Mean Decrease Accuracy, and the optimal number of selected features was determined based on out-of-bag (OOB) AUC. ROC curves shown in Fig. 2 were generated from out-of-bag votes of the optimized Random Forest models. Held-out test-set AUCs were additionally calculated across the five repeated splits. Sensitivity and specificity were calculated at a probability threshold of 0.5 for both out-of-bag votes and held-out test-set predictions. All statistical analyses were performed using R software. Unless otherwise specified, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For comparisons involving three groups, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure, and an adjusted p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. KEGG enrichment results were considered significant at a p-value adjusted by BH < 0.05.

Results

Association between fatigue and gut microbiome composition in healthy individuals

Fifty healthy adults were recruited, of whom 34 were classified into the non-fatigue group (CFQ score < 17) and 16 into the fatigue group (CFQ score ≥ 17) (Supplementary Table S1). Score for the CFQ, PSQI and ESS were significantly higher in the fatigue group (P = 1.5E-08, 0.014 and 0.027, respectively; Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Supplementary Table S1 and S6). Comparison between the two groups revealed a significant difference in BMI (P = 0.021, Wilcoxon rank-sum test), which was therefore treated as a confounding factor in subsequent analyses (Supplementary Table S1).

Analysis of gut microbiome diversity showed no significant differences in alpha diversity between the two groups, nor in beta diversity based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity via PCoA (Supplementary Figures S2A and S2B). However, when Bray–Curtis dissimilarity values were stratified into three categories–intragroup distances within the fatigue group, intergroup distances between fatigue and non-fatigue groups, and intragroup distances within the non-fatigue group–significant differences were observed among all comparisons. Notably, the intragroup distances in the fatigue group exhibited the highest dissimilarity values (Fig. 1A). To identify the microbial features underlying this inter-individual heterogeneity, we next examined group differences in taxonomic composition.

Taxonomic profiling based on marker gene analysis using MetaPhlAn4 detected a total of 15 phyla, 405 genera, and 945 species across all 50 samples. At the genus level, 6 genera were significantly more abundant in the fatigue group, whereas 11 genera were significantly more abundant in the non-fatigue group (Supplementary Figure S2C). At the species level, 10 species were significantly more abundant in the fatigue group, whereas 17 species were significantly more abundant in the non-fatigue group (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table S7).

Metabolomic analysis of fecal samples identified 110 water-soluble metabolites by CE-MS and 11 organic acids by HPLC. Among these, adenosine and citrate levels were significantly lower in the fatigue group, while GABA and tyramine were significantly higher in the fatigue group (Fig. 1C). We therefore asked whether these compositional and metabolite differences were accompanied by shifts in microbial functional potential using KO-based profiling.

Functional alterations in the gut microbiome associated with fatigue

Functional profiling based on KOs revealed a total of 8,739 KOs across all 50 samples. Comparative analysis by MaAsLin2 between the fatigue and non-fatigue groups showed that 452 KOs were significantly enriched in the fatigue group, while 197 KOs were significantly enriched in the non-fatigue group (Supplementary Figure S3, Supplementary Table S8).

To determine whether microbial features contained discriminatory information for fatigue status, we performed exploratory Random Forest classification using significantly altered KOs (649 KOs: 452 enriched and 197 depleted in the fatigue group), genera (17 genera: 6 enriched and 11 depleted in the fatigue group), and species (27 species: 10 enriched and 17 depleted in the fatigue group). Among these feature types, KO-based models showed high out-of-bag discriminatory ability across the five repeated analyses, with a median OOB AUC of 0.972 (SD, 0.017; range, 0.941–0.986). At a probability threshold of 0.5, the median OOB-vote-based sensitivity and specificity were 0.667 (range, 0.667–0.750) and 0.958 (range, 0.917–1.000), respectively. However, held-out test-set AUCs were lower and more variable across the five 70:30 splits, with a median test AUC of 0.675 (SD, 0.142; range, 0.575–0.950). At the same probability threshold, held-out test-set sensitivity and specificity were also variable, with median values of 0.750 (range, 0.000–0.750) and 0.900 (range, 0.600–0.900), respectively. Therefore, these results were interpreted as exploratory evidence that fatigue-associated KO profiles contain discriminatory information, rather than as validation of a predictive classifier (Fig. 2A, B, Supplementary Figures S4A, S4B).

Enrichment analysis of the significantly altered KOs at the module level revealed increased potential in the fatigue group for modules involved in 2-oxoglutarate production and metabolism, including parts of the glycine cleavage system and the TCA cycle, as well as pathways for urate production (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table S9). Modules decreased in the fatigue group included those related to F-type ATPase (involved in bacterial ATP synthesis), the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway (carbon fixation via CO₂ to acetyl-CoA), glycogen and trehalose biosynthesis, and biosynthesis of cofactors such as molybdenum cofactors and cobalamin (vitamin B12) (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table S10). Methanogenesis pathways were also downregulated, potentially due to shared metabolic routes with the CO₂-to-acetyl-CoA conversion pathway (Fig. 2E). Additionally, the urea metabolism pathway was significantly upregulated in the fatigue group. As this pathway partially overlaps with urate biosynthesis, and because the conversion of urea to ammonia (NH₃) was downregulated, it is suggested that only the urate-producing branch, generating urate known for its radical-scavenging function, was activated. In addition to these module-level shifts, multiple oxidative stress–response KOs were significantly enriched in the fatigue group (Supplementary Table S8). KOs assigned to superoxide dismutase, Fe-Mn family (K04564); superoxide dismutase, Cu-Zn family (K04565); superoxide oxidase (K12262); methionine sulfoxide reductase catalytic subunit (K07147); monothiol glutaredoxin (K07390); glutathione-regulated potassium-efflux system protein KefC (K11745); glutathione-regulated potassium-efflux system protein KefB (K11747); and thioredoxin 2 (K03672) also showed significant increases in the fatigue group (Supplementary Table S8).

At the pathway level, enrichment was observed not only in pathways associated with the aforementioned modules but also in phosphonate and phosphinate metabolism, biofilm formation (related to envelope stress), bacterial secretion systems (Type II and Type IV), flagellar assembly, cationic antimicrobial peptide (CAMP) resistance, the phosphotransferase system (PTS), and nucleotide metabolism. Conversely, pathways such as the sulfur relay system, selenocompound metabolism, and purine metabolism were significantly downregulated. Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism as well as carbon metabolism also showed significant enrichment in both upregulated and downregulated module groups, suggesting complex regulatory dynamics (Supplementary Figures S5A and S5B).

Correlation between fatigue-associated functional genes, bacterial genomes, and metabolites

To link the fatigue-associated functional shifts to specific microbial genomes, we next reconstructed MAGs and evaluated their contributions to the key KO signatures, resulting in 1,351 high-quality MAGs. After dereplication using dRep, we identified 332 representative MAGs (Fig. 3A). Mapping short reads to these representative MAGs yielded a median (SD) genome coverage of 68.7% (0.036) (Supplementary Figure S6A).

Comparative analysis between the fatigue and non-fatigue groups revealed that 5 MAGs were significantly enriched in the fatigue group, while 23 MAGs were significantly enriched in the non-fatigue group (Supplementary Figure S6B). Functional annotation for each MAG was subsequently performed using KOs. As a result, 57 fatigue-enriched KOs were identified from the MAGs enriched in the fatigue group, and 62 fatigue-depleted KOs were identified from those depleted in the fatigue group (Supplementary Figures S7A and S7B). We then focused on MAGs that contained 18 enriched and 20 depleted KOs associated with the fatigue group, which belonged to KEGG modules that were significantly altered in the earlier module enrichment analysis (Fig. 3B and C). Among the fatigue-enriched MAGs, the representative Escherichia coli MAG encoded dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase (K00382), purine/pyrimidine nucleoside phosphorylase (K09913), and 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase E2 component (K00658). The MAGs of Lachnoclostridium_B faecipullorum and Aaeroglobus micronuciformis also carried K00382 (Fig. 3B). Similarly, among the 23 MAGs reduced in the fatigue group, all except for the Clostridium_A leptum representative MAG possessed at least one of the identified KOs. In particular, the MAGs of Fusicatenibacter saccharivorans, Hominisplanchenecus faecis, and Oliverpabstia faecicola contained 10 key KOs of interest, while Blautia_A obeum contained 8. Other MAGs also frequently encoded F-type H+-transporting ATPase subunit c (K02110) and F-type H+/Na+-transporting ATPase subunit beta (K02112), associated with F-type ATPase (M00157), and KOs related to molybdenum cofactor biosynthesis (M00880) and cobalamin biosynthesis (M00925) were widely distributed as well (Fig. 3C).

We next evaluated whether the total relative abundance of MAGs encoding these KOs differed significantly between the groups. Among the fatigue-associated KOs, only MAGs encoding 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase E2 component (K00658) showed significantly higher total abundance in the fatigue group; no significant differences were observed for the other KOs (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Figure S8A). For KOs reduced in the fatigue group, significant reductions were observed in MAGs encoding formate dehydrogenase (coenzyme F420) beta subunit (K00125), acetyl-CoA decarbonylase/synthase, CODH/ACS complex subunit delta (K00194), acetyl-CoA decarbonylase/synthase, CODH/ACS complex subunit gamma (K00197), cobalt-precorrin-6B (C15)-methyltransferase (K02191), heterodisulfide reductase subunit A2 (K03388), precorrin-6 A/cobalt-precorrin-6 A reductase (K05895), and acetyl-CoA synthase (K14138), whereas MAGs encoding K02110, K02112, and molybdopterin adenylyltransferase (K03831) did not show significant differences (Fig. 3E, Supplementary Figure S8B). These results suggest that the group-level differences in these eight KOs were largely attributable to changes in the gut bacterial composition.

Based on these findings, we investigated the correlations between the relative abundance of key MAGs—F. saccharivorans, H. faecis, O. faecicola, B. obeum, and E. coli—selected as MAGs to maximize coverage of significantly altered KEGG modules through their key KOs and the fecal concentrations of metabolites that showed significant differences between groups—adenosine, citrate, GABA, and tyramine. Significant positive correlations were observed between H. faecis and F. saccharivorans and citrate levels. Additionally, the abundance of E. coli was positively correlated with both GABA and tyramine levels. Conversely, O. faecicola and B. obeum showed significant negative correlations with GABA concentrations (Fig. 3F).

Fatigue-associated MAG profiles in fatigue-related conditions

We investigated the profiles of fatigue-associated MAGs in several conditions in which fatigue is a primary or contributing symptom. To assess the generalizability of these fatigue-associated MAG patterns, we compared their effect directions and sizes with external disease cohorts. These conditions included major depressive disorder (MDD), bipolar disorder (BD), myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), obesity, and impaired glucose tolerance (IGT). Using MAGs that showed significant differences between the fatigue and non-fatigue groups in our cohort, we compared feature importance (effect sizes) across disease cohorts to identify shared and distinct microbial signatures.

Of the 28 MAGs that exhibited significant differences in our fatigue group, all were detected in the MDD4, BD5, obesity, and IGT datasets63. In the ME/CFS cohorts, 17 and 22 MAGs were detected in the Nagy-Szakal et al. and Guo et al. datasets7,8, respectively. When considering consistency in both directionality and statistical significance, the highest number of concordant MAGs was found in the ME/CFS cohort from Guo et al. (22 MAGs), followed by ME/CFS from Nagy-Szakal et al. (16 MAGs), MDD (15 MAGs), and BD (11 MAGs). In contrast, no concordant MAGs were identified in the obesity or IGT cohorts (Supplementary Figure S9).

Focusing on five representative MAGs–F. saccharivorans, H. faecis, O. faecicola, B. obeum, and E. coli–all were detected in the external cohorts, except for E. coli and B. obeum in the ME/CFS dataset from Nagy-Szakal et al. Notably, in the ME/CFS cohort from Guo et al., all five MAGs showed consistent directionality and statistical significance. This was followed by MDD with four consistent MAGs and the ME/CFS cohort from Nagy-Szakal et al. with three. No consistent MAGs were found in BD, obesity, or IGT (Fig. 4A).

Next, we conducted correlation analyses between the effect sizes of all representative MAGs in our fatigue versus non-fatigue comparison and those from case–control comparisons in the external cohorts. Significant positive correlations were observed for the ME/CFS cohorts from Guo et al., and Nagy-Szakal et al., as well as MDD and BD cohorts. No correlation was found with obesity, whereas IGT exhibited a significant negative correlation (Supplementary Figure S9).

To statistically compare these correlation coefficients, we performed pairwise tests using the cocor package. All four positively correlated cohorts–ME/CFS from Guo et al., ME/CFS from Nagy-Szakal et al., MDD, and BD–showed significant differences in correlation coefficients when compared with IGT. Among these, only BD did not show significant difference when compared with obesity. Within the positively correlated cohorts, a significant difference was observed only between the ME/CFS from Guo et al. and the BD cohort (Fig. 4B). We performed a correlation analysis between the MaAsLin2 coefficients based on MAG-level compositional data comparing fatigue and non-fatigue groups and the MaAsLin2 coefficients associated with each disease. Significant positive correlations were observed with MDD, BD, and both cohorts of ME/CFS. No correlation was found with obesity, while a significant negative correlation was identified with IGT (Supplementary Figure S10).

Discussion

Multiple lines of evidence suggest that fatigue induces complex, multilayered changes in the gut microbial ecosystem. In this study, we comprehensively investigated the association between subjective fatigue assessments and both compositional and functional alterations in the gut microbiome at the metagenomic level in healthy adults.

Although no significant differences were observed in alpha or beta diversity between groups (Supplementary Figures S2A and S2B), a stratified analysis using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity revealed greater inter-individual variation among participants who reported experiencing fatigue (Fig. 1A). In contrast, certain psychiatric disorders and ME/CFS have been reported to exhibit gut microbiome dysbiosis4,5,68. This finding suggests that there may still be multiple microbial signatures associated with the fatigued state, which may vary across individuals. Given that the human gut microbiome can be classified into distinct types69–71, it is plausible that individual responses to fatigue may also vary accordingly. However, to translate such findings into therapeutic or predictive applications, it is necessary to identify shared or consistent alterations. Therefore, our analysis focused on bacterial species and functional genes to uncover common alterations.

In the microbial analysis, E. coli was enriched in the fatigue group, whereas Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, B. obeum, and F. saccharivorans were significantly depleted (Supplementary Figure S6). Because E. coli is a facultative anaerobe, its increased abundance may be consistent with changes in oxygen-related microbial ecology in the gut. However, intestinal oxygen levels were not directly measured in this study, and this interpretation should therefore be considered indirect. If such oxygen-related ecological changes occur, they could adversely affect obligate anaerobic microbes and thereby contribute to the depletion of short-chain-fatty-acid-producing taxa72. Faecalibacterium73, Blautia74, and Fusicatenibacter75 are known producers of short-chain fatty acids and may exert beneficial effects on the host by promoting anti-inflammatory responses within the gut environment. Although only a few studies have investigated the relationship between the gut microbiome and fatigue, Boolani et al. examined associations between gut bacterial composition and mental energy, mental fatigue, physical energy, and physical fatigue76. A positive correlation between the genus Turicibacter and mental energy reported in that study is consistent with our finding of Turicibacter depletion in the fatigue group. A recent study has shown that certain Turicibacter strains can modify taurine-conjugated bile acids and influence host lipid and energy metabolism77. Taurine is known to support mitochondrial function and reduce fatigue78, suggesting that Turicibacter enrichment in the non-fatigue group may contribute to a gut environment associated with higher energy levels. In contrast, some gut species associated with fatigue and energy in the previous study did not show consistent patterns in our data (Supplementary Figure S1C). Differences in ethnicity, methodologies, and reference databases used across study may contribute to the discrepancies in observed associations between the gut microbiome and fatigue79. This pattern implies that fatigue may be associated with a shift in microbial balance, characterized by a reduction in beneficial bacteria and a potential increase in opportunistic or stress-tolerant species. Notably, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, a well-known butyrate-producing bacterium, has been reported to be decreased in individuals with ME/CFS4.

A notable finding of our study is that functional profiling revealed KO-level functional features provided greater discriminatory power between the fatigue and non-fatigue groups than taxonomic features at the genus or species level (Fig. 2A and B). This finding suggests that functional changes in the gut microbiome are more consistently associated with fatigue than are taxonomic shifts. It also implies that microbial functions are not confined to specific strains but are likely distributed across multiple species in a host-dependent manner, potentially playing redundant or cooperative roles among microbial taxa.

Citrate and adenosine levels were significantly lower in the fatigue group based on metabolite profiles (Fig. 1C). Citrate is a key intermediate in the TCA cycle, which generates reducing equivalents (electrons) for energy production. A reduction in citrate levels in the gut environment may lead to decreased electron availability and membrane potential, ultimately resulting in reduced ATP production. Consequently, this metabolic shift could contribute to the observed decrease in adenosine levels. This hypothesis is supported by our KEGG module enrichment analysis, which identified depletion of key energy-generating pathways in the fatigue group, including the acetyl-CoA pathway (M00422), the Wood-Ljungdahl pathway (M00377), and F-type ATPase (M00157) (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table S10). Alternatively, enrichment of the glycine cleavage system could contribute, as both this system and the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway utilize tetrahydrofolate as a cofactor, suggesting that upregulation of one could suppress the other due to competition for shared intermediates. This may reflect reduced CO₂ availability—an essential substrate for the acetyl-CoA pathway—in the gut environment.

In contrast, GABA and tyramine were more abundant in the fatigue group (Fig. 1C). Both GABA and tyramine are neuroactive amines produced by gut bacteria80,81, but they serve opposing functions: GABA acts as an inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system, whereas tyramine stimulates the sympathetic nervous system. Mullowney et al. reported that certain gut bacteria can conjugate GABA and tyramine to bile acids82, suggesting that these amines may be modulated by shared microbial pathways and that host fatigue could disrupt these functions. These results suggest that not only the balance of neurotransmitters, but also their absolute concentrations, may be important in the context of host fatigue.

Another important finding from the functional analyses was the enrichment of KOs related to urate biosynthesis and 2-oxoglutarate metabolism in the fatigue group (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table S9). Urate is known antioxidant that also functions in the gut83,84, and enrichment of its biosynthetic pathway may be consistent with increased oxidative stress in the gut environment. Consistent with this interpretation, orthologs related to superoxide dismutase, superoxide oxidase, methionine sulfoxide reductase, monothiol glutaredoxin, the glutathione-regulated potassium-efflux system, and thioredoxin were enriched in the fatigue group (Supplementary Table S8). However, these findings are based on metagenomic functional potential and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of increased enzymatic activity or oxidative stress. The involvement of 2-oxoglutarate metabolism should be interpreted as a hypothesis. Given the reduced citrate levels and depletion of citrate-associated bacteria, enrichment of pathways connected to 2-oxoglutarate may reflect a possible shift in microbial metabolic potential involving downstream citrate cycle-related intermediates. However, this interpretation remains speculative and requires validation by targeted metabolomic and experimental analyses.

It is also noteworthy that several fatigue-associated MAGs showed overlapping directions of change in external datasets of MDD and ME/CFS (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Figure S9). However, this overlap should be interpreted cautiously. A recent review emphasized that associations between gut microbiota and mental health may be causative, correlative, or bidirectional, and that distinguishing these possibilities remains a major challenge85. Therefore, the overlap observed here should be interpreted as associative similarity in microbial alterations rather than evidence of a shared causal mechanism. ME/CFS is a condition characterized by persistent and debilitating fatigue of unknown origin, and early identification of its underlying mechanisms remains a major clinical challenge. Historically, links to Epstein–Barr virus infection have been proposed86, and more recently, cases with similar symptomatology have been reported following SARS-CoV-2 infection or vaccination, suggesting the involvement of infectious or immune-mediated triggers87,88. In this context, the observation that several fatigue-associated MAGs were also altered in these disorders is consistent with the possibility that gut microbiome alterations associated with fatigue overlap with those reported in psychiatric or neuroimmune conditions. Interestingly, the ME/CFS cohort from Guo et al. showed the highest concordance in both effect direction and statistical significance with the fatigue group in our study8 (Fig. 4B). This suggests that microbial taxa and functions associated with fatigue in healthy individuals resemble patterns reported in MDD, BD and ME/CFS and may represent early, fatigue-linked microbial changes relevant to stress-related conditions, although the present cross-sectional design does not allow inference of temporal ordering or causality. The absence of such associations in the obesity and impaired glucose tolerance cohorts further suggests that these microbial features may be more closely aligned with psychiatric and neuroimmune disorders, rather than metabolic diseases. Notably, distinct patterns of dysbiosis have been reported in individuals with both MDD and ME/CFS. In the present study, feature analysis based on MAG abundance revealed significant alterations across a broad range of microbial taxa, with fatigue-associated MAGs accounting for only a minor fraction of these changes. These results are consistent with the hypothesis that fatigue alone is not sufficient to drive psychiatric disorders; rather, the progression to such conditions may require additional host-related factors, such as heightened stress sensitivity12,13.

One limitation of the present study is that fatigue assessment was based on a subjective measure—the Chalder Fatigue Scale22. Because all participants voluntarily enrolled in the study and the setting was controlled, their responses are likely to be more reliable than those obtained from general health screening surveys. However, the 14-items Likert scoring system of the CFQ lacks standardized criteria; therefore, a cut-off value that could partially explain PSQI and ESS scores was determined in the present study24,26. The PSQI is an instrument used to assess the severity of sleep disturbances, whereas the ESS measures the degree of daytime sleepiness. A strong correlation between CFQ and PSQI scores has been reported in previous studies89. Moreover, multiple lines of evidence from this study demonstrated a strong concordance between subjective fatigue measures and objective metagenomic signatures, suggesting the potential utility of microbiome-derived features as biomarkers. Given that fatigue is a common prodromal or aggravating factor in various diseases, establishing an objective, microbiome-based evaluation system for fatigue could contribute significantly to preventive healthcare. Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. Dietary intake, physical activity, and detailed lifestyle variables were not available, and residual confounding by these factors may have influenced the observed microbiome associations. In addition, metagenomic functional profiling infers functional potential rather than directly measuring gene expression, enzyme activity, or metabolic flux. This study included only 50 participants, and although the Random Forest classifier demonstrated discriminative performance, its predictive robustness is limited by the relatively small sample size. Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate and refine these findings. Furthermore, the present analysis was cross-sectional, and causality cannot be inferred. In addition, comparisons with external cohorts may be influenced by differences in participant characteristics and study protocols, and therefore should be interpreted with caution. Longitudinal studies, animal models, and integrated time-series analyses combining microbiome and host physiological data will be essential to uncover mechanistic insights.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that mental fatigue in healthy adults is associated with marked alterations in the functional composition of the gut microbiome. In particular, the identification of fatigue-specific KOs, together with the observed relationships among bacterial taxa, functional genes, and metabolites, suggests a potential link between subjective fatigue and psychiatric vulnerability that may involve gut microbiome–related pathways. These findings provide a foundation for future strategies aimed at early detection and preventive interventions for psychiatric disorders by targeting gut microbial signatures associated with fatigue.
