User-driven potential for scalable, cost-effective, and flexible vehicle-to-grid resources in China
Authors: Xiaoli Han, Bo Wang, Suyang Zhou, Bin Lu, Jiucun Yuan, Jingjing Zhang, Jing Li, Zhaohua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryVehicle-to-grid (V2G) technology is widely recognized for enhancing power system flexibility and supporting renewable energy integration. However, assessments of its cost effectiveness based on user willingness and its response capability under real-world conditions remain limited. This study evaluates the flexibility potential and economic performance of V2G in China by incorporating user preferences and regional heterogeneity. Using a discrete choice experiment (DCE) with 1,412 electric vehicle (EV) owners, we estimate user willingness to accept (WTA) and quantify V2G potential. Results show that V2G could provide up to 348 GW of flexibility, exceeding 11 times the current national energy storage capacity. Under emergency demand response scenarios, V2G is cost-competitive, with WTA around half that of conventional storage. However, WTA varies substantially ($0.14–$0.85 per kWh) and is sensitive to temperature, with winter conditions reducing participation by 21% and increasing costs by 17%. These findings highlight the need for behavior-aware policy and market design.

Introduction

Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) technology has gained increasing attention from scholars and policymakers as a core element of resilient energy systems for its dynamic power regulation capabilities.1,2 The recent Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) 6th Assessment Report emphasizes that the rapid development of EVs provides a valuable opportunity for the coordinated decarbonization of the power and transportation sectors, with V2G as an effective means to achieve the cross-sector decarbonization goals.3 It has been projected that by 2030, the 16 TWh of energy stored in global EVs could be actively supplied to the grid through V2G technology at optimal times.2 If 5% of the total battery capacity of EVs is used for peak load management, it could provide approximately 600 GW of flexible load globally, helping to offset the lower renewable energy output during peak hours and meet the increased demand for power storage.2 Recognizing the multiple benefits provided by V2G, a variety of incentives, e.g., financial subsidies,4 tax reductions,5 and the establishment of orderly charging and discharging standards,6 have been implemented globally to support V2G adoption.

With the rapid expansion of the electric vehicle (EV) market and the steady deployment of renewable energy, the power system requires improved flexibility and resilience.7 According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), global EV sales approached 14 million units in 2023, with China accounting for approximately 60% of the total sales.7,8 By 2030, China’s theoretical energy storage capacity from EVs is expected to surpass that of traditional energy storage, such as pumped hydro and electrochemical energy storage combined, highlighting the significant potential for V2G applications.9 Currently, countries like the US and China have implemented over 100 pilot projects since 2002, and the technical feasibility of V2G has been confirmed, offering services such as shaving peak load, facilitating renewable energy absorption, and enhancing grid quality.4,10,11

However, the success of V2G market penetration relies not only on continuous technological advancements but also on public acceptance. At present, users largely perceive EVs as transportation tools that consume energy from the grid, rather than as potential grid resources that could enhance the power system resilience and accelerate decarbonization goals. The potential of EVs as a flexible grid resource remains largely untapped. Beyond social and legal considerations, the economic compensation for discharging influences EV owner participation in V2G programs. Frequent charging and discharging cycles, integral to V2G operations, can accelerate battery degradation, raising concerns among users about the potential impact on battery lifespan and limiting the widespread adoption of V2G technology.12 Currently, the predominant V2G business model relies on exploiting peak-to-valley electricity price differentials. However, the economic performance of V2G participation depends on market conditions, pricing mechanisms, and operational strategies, as well as considerations related to battery degradation.13,14,15 From the user perspective, uncertainty regarding long-term battery health and actual net returns may influence participation decisions. Understanding the flexibility provided by EVs and users’ willingness to accept (WTA) compensation across various scenarios is crucial for energy system planning. Further research is imperative to investigate the factors influencing user V2G behavior and to develop comprehensive economic compensation mechanisms that align with user expectations and mitigate perceived risks.16,17 Such efforts are critical for bolstering the vehicles to grid integration, enhancing the flexibility and resilience of the power network, and accelerating clean energy transitions.

Despite growing recognition among researchers of the importance of willingness to participate in V2G interactions, the estimation of the flexibility capacity and corresponding costs provided by V2G, based on the user’s willingness, remains largely unexplored. Existing studies primarily focus on unidirectional V2G charging scenarios18,19 or consumers’ purchase intentions for EVs equipped with V2G functionality.20 Preferences for V2G discharging behavior, a more complex and critical aspect of V2G adoption, have received less attention, with limited findings highlighting barriers to this functionality.21,22 Additionally, existing literature in this field has largely neglected to account for various contextual factors, such as the impacts of seasonal variations, time-of-use dynamics within daily cycles, regional disparities in energy landscapes and EV adoption, and the effects of extreme temperature fluctuations on V2G availability. These scenarios significantly impact energy supply and demand dynamics. Understanding the flexibility and related costs that V2G can provide in these contexts is essential for the effective deployment of infrastructure and the formulation of supportive policies.

This study is the first to assess the flexibility resources provided by V2G discharging in China from the perspective of users’ willingness to participate. This study quantifies the potential of V2G, including the significant variation in user WTA across different scenarios. We consider 144 scenarios, by accounting for the aforementioned contextual factors. Based on the analysis of more than 1,400 survey responses from EV owners, our study highlights the importance of understanding user preferences and developing appropriate policy and market mechanisms to incentivize V2G participation. We find that the maximum response potential of EVs can reach up to 348 GW per day, equivalent to 11 times the national installed storage capacity. Additionally, participants in the EV demand response (DR) program can receive an economic compensation of $6.3 per day on average. The amount of compensation is closely tied to the features that impact the energy supply and demand dynamics, such as weather conditions and regional characteristics. Compared to traditional energy storage, V2G can adjust grid demand without additional investment. Under the emergency demand response (EDR) scenario, the cost of traditional energy storage is 2–17 times the WTA of V2G, making V2G a more cost-effective option. This study assesses the flexibility potential of EVs in V2G interactions and analyzes user preferences and compensation expectations, providing a theoretical basis for the deployment of EVs, energy storage infrastructure, and the formulation of market incentives to improve power system stability.

Results

Diverse user preferences for V2G DR program participation

We used DCE to design various V2G DR programs, with attributes including seasonal temperature condition, discharge period, discharge frequency, single discharge degree, and compensation (Table 2; see details in STAR Methods). This approach enabled the collection of preference data across various hypothetical scenarios, shedding light on individuals’ preferences when certain behaviors or events were hypothetical or had not occurred. Data on users’ preferences for different attribute combinations of V2G discharge contracts were collected through a questionnaire survey, which included responses from 1,118 users across two samples, with a total of 1,412 completed questionnaires. A random parameter logit (RPL) model was then employed to analyze user preferences for participating in V2G discharge contracts and estimate the relative importance of each attribute. This model’s mean coefficient reflected the respondents’ preference levels, while the standard errors of the parameters indicated the degree of heterogeneity among individuals. To determine the relative importance of each attribute, we calculated the ratio of the largest difference in its estimated coefficients to the sum of the largest differences across all attributes.23 In our model, all attributes except for the “Compensation” attribute, which is treated as a continuous variable, are designated as dummy variables. The reference categories include normal temperature, a discharge period between 00:00 and 06:00, a discharge frequency of four times per month, and a 20% discharge degree. Details of the data and models can be found in the STAR Methods section.

As shown in Table 1, columns 3 and 4, we used the estimated parameters to further obtain the average marginal willingness to accept (MWTA) and relative importance for each attribute. The analysis revealed that compensation plays a critical role in the selection of V2G contracts, contributing to 46.4% of the total importance. Seasonal temperature condition follows with 18.9%, where respondents seek an additional $0.114 per kilowatt-hour (kWh) subsidy in peak summer and $0.252 per kWh in extreme winter conditions. Furthermore, the single discharge degree (13.4%) and the discharge time period (12.6%) significantly influence decision-making.

Heterogeneity of WTA in different contexts

We compared the heterogeneity across non-working days and different regions to identify variations in user preferences and behaviors. Based on the analysis of the MWTA of each attribute, we estimated the WTA (MWTA refers to the compensation increment required for a specific change in an attribute, such as the additional compensation expected when the temperature increases from normal to high summer levels; WTA, on the other hand, emphasizes the overall change, indicating the total compensation expectation in response to a broader transformation.) for specific V2G contracts by constructing a series of scenario combinations. The WTA is calculated by examining the changes in MWTA relative to the benchmark scenario of the attributes. Benchmark scenario setting can be found in the STAR Methods section.

Figure 1 shows the results of WTA for both the full sample (covering working and non-working days) and the non-working day sample. As illustrated in Figure 1, the WTA of users varies considerably across different scenarios, ranging from $0.139–$0.852 per kWh. The average industrial and commercial electricity prices in China range from approximately $0.051 per kWh during valley periods to $0.163 per kWh during peak periods. Compared with these benchmarks, the estimated WTA values are generally higher than the prevailing electricity tariffs, indicating that users require a substantial premium to participate in V2G discharge activities. Specifically, the lowest compensation, at $0.139 per kWh, is observed during discharges at normal temperatures from 18:00 to 24:00, occurring 4 times per month with a discharge level of 20% (Figure 1A). In this scenario, the MWTA for each attribute is the lowest, thereby requiring lower total compensation levels. In stark contrast, during the cold, early hours of winter (06:00–12:00), compensation expectations surge to $0.852 per kWh (Figure 1B), which is more than five times the peak electricity price. This pattern indicates that compensation requirements increase markedly under low-temperature winter conditions, especially during the 06:00–12:00 period and with higher discharge frequencies and discharge degrees. As shown in Figures 1C and 1D, the WTA on non-working days fluctuates between $0.173 and $0.604 per kWh, equivalent to approximately 1.1–3.7 times the peak electricity price. Considering the differences in the promotion of EVs, infrastructure deployment, and economic development levels across different regions,25,26,27 we further examined the spatial heterogeneity by analyzing the WTA among respondents from different grid regions (for details on the regional division, see the STAR Methods section). As shown in Figure 2, compensation willingness varies significantly across regions, ranging from $0.103–$1.169 per kWh. Using the national peak electricity tariff as a benchmark, regional WTA values correspond to roughly 0.6–7.2 times the prevailing peak electricity price. Particularly during the low temperature conditions in winter, the WTA is relatively higher, and it increases with the frequency and extent of discharge. Notably, users in the northwestern, southwestern, and eastern regions of China exhibit higher WTA values, with the northwestern and southwestern regions exceeding $0.411 per kWh in most scenarios. In contrast, the northeastern, southern, and northern regions of China show relatively lower and less variable WTA values. The central region of China displays a more balanced WTA across different scenarios. Several structural factors may help explain this regional variation. In the northwest and southwest regions, relatively lower EV penetration and less mature charging infrastructure may be associated with higher perceived inconvenience or uncertainty related to V2G participation, which could contribute to higher compensation expectations. Flexibility resources supplied by V2G could potentially exceed 11 times the current national new energy storage capacity

By the end of 2023, the number of EVs in China reached 20.41 million.29 Assuming an average battery capacity of 45 kWh per EV, the total onboard storage capacity exceeds 900 million kWh. Based on the estimates above, this study simulates the maximum energy storage capacity that users could theoretically provide daily through V2G discharging activities in different scenarios (detailed calculation can be found in STAR Methods).

As shown in Figure 3B, we calculate that the maximum daily support capacity of EVs for the grid can reach up to 347.55 GW, which is approximately 11.07 times the installed capacity of new energy storage in China by the end of 2023. The flexibility resources provided by users during nighttime (18:00–24:00) and early morning hours (00:00–06:00) are relatively higher, with a peak storage capacity of 94.95 GW per day. However, the flexible resources provided during the day are lower, yet users expect higher compensation during these times. As shown in Figures 3A and 3B, seasonal temperature condition fluctuations significantly affect the capacity of flexible storage, with low temperatures in winter having the most noticeable impact. Compared to normal temperatures, the maximum response capacity of EVs decreases by approximately 21.2% in winter, while daily compensation costs increase by about 16.6%. For instance, with a discharge frequency of 12 times per month, during the 18:00–24:00 period under normal temperature, a maximum of 94.95 GW of storage resources can be provided, with users expecting compensation of $6.233 per day. Under low-temperature winter conditions, however, the maximum capacity drops to 79.78 GW, and the compensation increases to $9.863 per day. Furthermore, as the discharge frequency increases, compensation rises significantly, but the increase in V2G supply does not proportionally match the rise in costs. Despite a substantial increase in compensation, the V2G supply grows at a much slower rate. Users’ WTA adjusts non-linearly with discharge frequency, resulting in a mismatch between the increase in compensation and the corresponding increase in supply. Therefore, relying solely on higher compensation to increase supply is inadequate when designing V2G incentive mechanisms. A more comprehensive approach, which addresses users’ diverse motivations and potential constraints, is required.

We further analyzed the flexibility storage potential across different grid regions. Figure 4 shows the distribution of flexibility resources activated by V2G discharging in these regions. Specifically, the eastern, northern, and southern regions of China can provide significantly higher flexibility resources compared to other regions, with east China capable of providing up to 104.92 GW per day. In contrast, the northwest, southwest, and northeast regions exhibit relatively limited flexibility resource capacities. For example, some provinces in the southwest can provide a maximum daily response of only 0.083 GW. This distribution of flexible resources effectively complements the inverse distribution of electricity supply and demand in China. Users’ participation in V2G discharge programs is restricted under extreme weather conditions. We investigated four extreme weather scenarios—high temperatures, severe cold, drought, and heavy rain—which reveal that while most respondents demonstrate some acceptance of somewhat extreme weather, participation willingness drops significantly as weather intensity increases. Under conditions of scorching heat, severe cold, or torrential rain, approximately 70% of users are unwilling to participate (see Figure S1). Further analysis, as detailed in Figure S2, shows that the average additional compensation required increases under these conditions. Notably, under extreme heat and severe cold, the required additional compensation rises to over 0.548 USD per kWh, yet around 10% of users remain reluctant to participate (see Figure S2). These findings highlight the challenges in motivating user participation under extreme conditions and underscore the need for further systematic research on discharge willingness in such scenarios.

WTA is at most about half of traditional energy storage in the EDR scenario

We used the levelized cost of storage (LCOS) over the entire life cycle as the core evaluation metric to measure and compare the economic efficiency of V2G technology with that of current mainstream electrochemical energy storage technologies, including lithium-ion batteries and vanadium redox batteries (VRBs). Based on this, we calculated the LCOS of the two electrochemical storage technologies at different annual cycle frequencies, using a 40 MWh/40 MW system as an example (detailed calculation can be found in STAR Methods).

Considering that the frequency of 5% peak load occurrences throughout the year is approximately 10–40 times, and the frequency for 3% peak load occurrences is between 6 to 25 times,30,31 we assumed that EDR will be required around 30 times per year. In this EDR scenario, the cost of VRB would reach $2.325 per kWh, approximately 16.67 times the minimum WTA and 2.73 times the maximum WTA. Meanwhile, the cost per kWh for lithium-ion batteries would reach $1.708, about 12.25 times the minimum WTA and 2 times the maximum WTA (see Figure S3). This scenario highlights the higher cost effectiveness of V2G technology in meeting short-term load demands and improving grid stability.

Discussion

In this study, we employ the DCE method, utilizing 1,412 survey responses collected from seven regions across China, to explore the heterogeneity of preferences and WTA, while also assessing the potential and cost effectiveness of EVs as a flexible resource for the grid across various scenarios. Our results indicate that EVs can provide a maximum daily support capacity of 347.55 GW for the grid, which is about 11.07 times the new energy storage capacity installed in China by the end of 2023, with the WTA varying considerably across different scenarios, ranging from $0.139–$0.852 per kWh. In the EDR scenario, the LCOS for VRB reaches $2.325, which is approximately 2.73 times the maximum WTA, while the LCOS for lithium-ion batteries is 2 times this maximum amount. Compared to traditional energy storage, V2G offers significantly higher economic benefits in the EDR scenario. Seasonal temperature condition fluctuations significantly affect capacity and compensation costs, with EV flexibility resources decreasing by 21.2% and daily costs rising by 16.6% in low winter temperatures compared with normal conditions. Additionally, extreme weather conditions, particularly extreme heat and cold, have a significant impact on the availability of flexibility resources.

Our results have important implications for the design and implementation of flexible grid resources not only for China but also for any country or region seeking to optimize the integration of EVs into the energy grid. Major countries around the world are seeking to integrate EVs as flexible resources to support grid stability and promote the integration of renewable energy sources.32,33 Given the diverse user preferences across different attributes and contexts and the associated uncertainties in providing flexibility under demand response scenarios, our study highlights the significant potential of EVs and V2G interactions. These findings offer valuable insights for developing effective, regionally tailored policies to drive large-scale V2G integration, enhance grid stability, and support the transition to a more sustainable energy future.

Several measures and policy approaches can be adopted to facilitate the integration of EVs into the grid. Firstly, in terms of V2G discharging compensation strategies, compensation levels should vary by region and context, with adjustments made based on WTA, temperature, timing, and demand response potential—especially during peak electricity usage periods to encourage broader participation in V2G programs. Secondly, the deployment of EVs and the expansion of supporting infrastructure are crucial for enhancing grid support capacity. In regions where V2G can provide high levels of demand response flexibility, policies should focus on strengthening infrastructure, optimizing charger connectivity and grid flexibility, and improving the efficiency of discharging systems. Conversely, in regions with low V2G demand response flexibility, efforts should be made to promote transportation electrification while also strengthening traditional energy storage and backup capacity to ensure sufficient reserve power supply during peak hours. Lastly, in addition to considering user preferences, the effective integration of EVs into the energy grid should also involve establishing clear regulations for V2G participation and developing robust battery insurance systems. Long-term battery degradation should also be explicitly addressed, as frequent charge-discharge cycles can accelerate capacity loss, thereby affecting both user willingness to participate and the overall economic viability of V2G systems. Incorporating degradation effects into policy frameworks and technical planning is essential to ensuring the sustained reliability and cost-effectiveness of large-scale V2G deployment. Furthermore, planning should account for the impact of extreme weather on flexibility resources and build resilient systems to cope with grid fluctuations caused by extreme weather conditions.

Limitations of the study

The flexibility potential estimated in this study is assessed at the regional level and does not explicitly distinguish heterogeneity in charging context, such as charging location (e.g., residential or workplace settings) and charging mode (e.g., slow versus fast charging). In practice, both the spatial setting of charging and the power level of charging facilities may influence connection duration, vehicle availability during peak periods, and the economic feasibility of V2G participation. More refined analyses incorporating heterogeneity in charging infrastructure deployment, charging behavior patterns, and connection characteristics could provide more operationally relevant insights for local policymakers and grid operators. In addition, differences in bidirectional charging readiness and infrastructure maturity across regions may further affect the realizable flexibility potential. While this study quantifies flexibility resources and compensation expectations under various scenarios, integrating charging location characteristics and infrastructure constraints at a finer spatial and technical resolution would improve the precision of economic feasibility assessments. These aspects warrant further investigation in future research.

Experimental model and study participant details

The study is based on an online questionnaire survey conducted among EV owners in China between late February and early March 2024. The questionnaire collected information on vehicle use, preferences for participation in V2G discharging activities, responses to DCE scenarios, willingness to participate under different weather conditions, and basic demographic characteristics. Two versions of the questionnaire were designed: one assessed preferences for V2G participation without distinguishing day types, while the other focused specifically on participation during non-working days.

A total of 1,540 questionnaires were distributed. After removing incomplete and invalid responses, 1,173 valid responses were retained from the first questionnaire, including 1,118 respondents willing to participate in V2G discharging activities and 55 unwilling to participate. For the second questionnaire, 294 respondents remained willing to participate in V2G discharge activities on non-working days. Overall, 1,412 valid questionnaires completed by 1,118 respondents were included in the analysis. All respondents were EV owners, with most relying primarily on fast charging. The sample was largely composed of individuals aged between 18 and 40. Among those willing to participate in V2G discharging activities, the majority were married. In addition, over 70% of respondents held a bachelor’s degree or higher.

Method details

Choice experiment design

Discrete choice experiment (DCE) is employed to explore the preferences influencing users’ engagement in V2G discharge activities. DCE is a widely recognized method in decision-making research, particularly useful for understanding preferences and trade-offs among different service attributes.34 It is particularly suitable for the present study, as V2G participation involves complex trade-offs among economic incentives, operational constraints, and environmental considerations. In a DCE, respondents are presented with hypothetical choice scenarios composed of different attribute combinations and are asked to select their preferred option. The observed choices allow for the estimation of the relative importance of each attribute and users’ willingness to accept trade-offs. DCE offers flexibility by including multiple attributes and simulating real-world decision-making.35 However, it also has limitations, as overly complex choice scenarios may increase respondents’ cognitive burden and potentially affect response consistency. The study’s structure comprises three primary phases: crafting the attribute level table, formulating the V2G discharge contract, and generating choice set.36 The attribute level table was constructed with careful consideration, pinpointing five key attributes believed to exert influence on V2G contracts, based on a literature review. Attributes and levels in the DCE

Compensation

Offering sufficient financial incentives is crucial for encouraging user participation, particularly when they must consider the potential impact of discharging on battery longevity.37 In previous studies, there has been considerable attention on the influence of fixed payment structures on user engagement within V2G contracts.24,38 Nonetheless, fixed payment schemes fail to accommodate variations in battery capacity, rendering them inequitable, particularly for vehicles equipped with high-capacity batteries. In view of this, our investigation introduces a novel approach, whereby compensation is calculated per kWh of electricity discharged into the grid. Moreover, users involved in the V2G discharge process incur costs primarily consisting of the extra electricity charges from V2G charging and the depreciation of the battery.39 Therefore, based on the cost burdens and incentive needs of users, we propose three tiers of compensation: 1.5 RMB/kWh(0.205$/kWh), 3 RMB/kWh(0.411 $/kWh), and 4.5 RMB/kWh(0.616$kWh), with an exchange rate of approximately 1 USD = 7.3 RMB.

Seasonal temperature condition

Battery performance is significantly influenced by ambient temperature. Hao et al. indicate that the actual range of EVs for personal use in winter amounts to only 64% of the estimated range under the New European Driving Cycle.40 Additionally, Higher ambient temperatures prolong the time required for battery packs to reach ambient levels, potentially leading to battery damage.41 To capture the influence of seasonal thermal environments on V2G participation, we define three levels of seasonal temperature condition: high temperature in summer (June–August), low temperature in winter (December–February), and normal temperature (other months). These categories are defined according to calendar months and are intended to represent typical seasonal temperature conditions. The variable therefore reflects seasonal temperature differences that may affect battery performance and user participation behavior.

Discharge time period

Users’ vehicle usage habits are influenced by their daily activity patterns. These patterns, in turn, influence users’ charging behavior and their readiness to participate in V2G discharging activities.42 To probe users’ preferences across different temporal segments, the day was divided into four-time slots: 0:00-6:00, 06:00-12:00, 12:00-18:00, and 18:00-24:00.

Discharge frequency

V2G activities contribute to an escalation in the number of EV cycles, potentially intensifying battery stress and accelerating battery degradation. Accelerated battery aging significantly dissuades EV owners from engaging in V2G discharging activities.22 Accordingly, this study examines the influence of discharge frequency by delineating three levels: 4 times per month, 8 times per month, and 12 times per month.

Single discharge degree

Increased discharges elevate the risk of rendering users unable to drive, consequently amplifying “mileage anxiety” and diminishing user willingness to engage in V2G. Huang et al. underscored EV drivers’ acute preoccupation with range, and they observed a positive influence of residual battery charge on V2G discharge activity.22 In this study, we employ a single discharge level to provide users with intuitive control of both the amount discharged and the remaining battery capacity, ensuring a minimum battery level. The single discharge level is categorized into four levels: 20%, 40%, 60%, and 80%.

The synthesis of the above-mentioned five attributes and levels gives rise to a total of 432 (42∗33) V2G discharge contracts. However, presenting all these combinations to respondents is impracticable. Hence, our DCE design employs randomized combinations. Each respondent receives a set of 8 choices. Within each choice set, there are three randomized combinations of attributes and levels for the discharge contracts, accompanied by a “neither” option. Respondents are required to select their preferred contract or the “neither” option in each choice set, ensuring that they were not forced to choose among contracts that were all unappealing to them, thus better mimicking real-world scenarios.24 Random assignment ensures an equal probability of selecting any option, thus minimizing the impact of selection biases and maximizing the internal validity of our study43 (Choice sets are illustrated in Figure S4).

Data collection

The survey questionnaire is structured into five main sections. The initial section focuses on respondents’ car usage patterns. Given our target audience of EV owners, the primary objective here is to determine whether respondents own an EV (either a battery electric vehicle or a plug-in hybrid electric vehicle). Following this, respondents are queried about their car purchase timeline, average monthly electricity expenditure, and their inclination toward participating in V2G discharging activities. The second segment encompasses the aforementioned choice experiment, serving as the centerpiece of our study. The third section examines respondents' willingness to participate in V2G under various extreme weather conditions and the required level of additional compensation. The fourth part is a 7-level Likert scale part based on the Theory of Planned Behavior, which is used to analyze the respondents’ level of participation. Lastly, respondents are asked to provide basic demographic information such as age and income.

The survey is conducted from late February to early March 2024, targeting Chinese EV owners through an online platform for distributing questionnaires. We hypothesize that there is heterogeneity in user participation in V2G discharging activities between working days and non-working. To examine this variance, we designed two different questionnaires. The first assesses preferences for V2G engagement irrespective of the day type, while the second specifically addresses participation on non-working only. Two sets of questionnaires were distributed, totaling 1540 copies. Among these, 1240 surveys were conducted without distinguishing whether they were on working days or not, and 300 questionnaires were distributed to further investigate the preferences of participants in V2G discharge activities on non-working days, based on those who participated in V2G discharge activities. It is worth noting that considering the number of non-working days per month, the levels of discharge frequency on non-working days were set at: 2 times/month, 4 times/month, and 8 times/month. Apart from discharge frequency, all other questions are identical to the first questionnaire, except for the explicit specification of non-working days in the scenario setting. After discarding incomplete and invalid responses, the first questionnaire yielded 1173 valid responses, of which 1,118 participants expressed their willingness to engage in V2G discharge activities, whereas 55 declined. In the second questionnaire, 6 respondents who initially agreed to participate in V2G declined to do so only on non-working days, yet 294 continued to express their willingness. Therefore, we collected a total of 1,412 valid questionnaires completed by 1,118 respondents.

Segmentation of the power grid areas

In China, the electricity grid is primarily managed by two major entities: the State Grid Corporation and the Southern Grid Corporation. Based on their management, China is divided into seven main regions: the Northeast (Heilongjiang, Jilin, Liaoning), North China (Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Shanxi, Inner Mongolia, Shandong), East China (Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, Anhui), Central China (Hunan, Hubei, Henan, Jiangxi), Northwest (Xinjiang, Gansu, Qinghai, Shaanxi, Ningxia), Southwest (Sichuan, Chongqing, Tibet), and the South (Guangdong, Guangxi, Yunnan, Guizhou, Hainan). Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan are not included due to their relatively independent power grids and lower regional electricity demands. The regional division is shown in Figure S5.

The estimation models

The theoretical framework of DCEs is grounded in Lancaster consumer theory and Random utility theory.44 The utility function of the chosen option comprises a systematic utility and a stochastic utility component.(Equation 1)Ui=Vi+εi.

Where Vi represents system utility, and represents system components.

We employ the RPL model to analyze respondents’ preferences for V2G discharging activities using data obtained from DCE. Distinguishing itself from the traditional logit model, this approach adeptly captures preference variability among individuals as well as within individuals.45 According to Lancaster’s consumer theory, individuals derive utility from the attributes of a product or service rather than from the product or service itself.44 Therefore, in the choice set, an individual’s utility is described as a combination of observable attributes and unobserved random components:(Equation 2)Uijt=βi′Xijt+εijtIn the framework of our research, Uijt denotes the utility derived by individual i when selecting option j from the choice set t. Xijt constitutes a vector encompassing observable variables pertinent to option j and the respondent. βi represents a vector of preference coefficients associated with these observable variables. Meanwhile, εijt captures the unobserved stochastic error term.46

The random effects model posits that decision-makers select alternatives within a choice set based on the maximum utility they can derive. Therefore, based on the principle of maximizing utility, the likelihood Pijt of respondent i opting for alternative j within scenario t is expressed through Equation 3.(Equation 3)Pijt=Pr(Uijt>Uikt,∀k≠j)=Pr(Vijt+εijt>Vikt+εikt,∀k≠j)=Pr(Vijt−Vikt>εikt−εijt,∀k≠j)

Assuming that the random error term within the utility function conforms to an independent and identically distributed Type I extreme value distribution (Gumbel distribution), and hypothesizing the preference coefficient β as a random variable with a probability density function of f(β|θ), wherein the coefficient vector varies across individuals as βi, and θ represents the vector of true parameters. Consequently, the probability of individual i selecting alternative j from the choice set can be formulated as:(Equation 4)Pi(θ)=∫∏t=1Texp(βi′Xijt)∑k=1Jexp(βi′Xikt)f(β|θ)dβ

The aforementioned equation lacks a closed-form solution and the selection probability is estimated via maximum likelihood simulation. We utilize the mixlogit package, which has been custom-built by users within Stata, for parameter estimation of the mode.47

After employing an RPL model for parameter estimation, MWTA for various attributes can be derived. Subsequently, we will utilize the estimated coefficients to calculate the MWTA, which represents the amount respondents are willing to accept to maintain constant utility when attribute k changes by one unit.48 Its computational formula is illustrated in Equation 5:(Equation 5)MWTAk=∂U∂xk∂U∂xcompensation=−βkβcompensation

The MWTA for attribute k is determined by dividing the preference weight for attribute k by the marginal utility of the monetary attribute. Notably, the latter corresponds to the negative of the preference weight assigned to the payment attribute.49

Furthermore, based on the estimated model parameters, the mixlpred command is used to predict the probability of selecting a particular alternative in an RPL model.50 By incorporating the attributes of the alternatives and the individual characteristics from the prediction scenario as input, the utility calculation formula is applied. For each individual, the utility prediction for each alternative is computed, and the average probability of selection for each alternative is obtained by averaging the choice probabilities across different individuals for the same alternative.

Calculation of WTA for different V2G discharge contracts

To calculate the WTA for different V2G discharge contracts, we start by setting a baseline contract. We assume normal temperature, a discharge period from 00:00 to 06:00, four discharges per month, a 20% discharge degree, and a compensation of 0.205$ per kWh. Based on the MWTA estimation results, the WTA is then calculated by varying the attributes of the baseline scenario. For example, in the full sample, the MWTA for high temperature in summer is 0.114$. In a scenario where the discharge period is between 00:00 and 06:00, with a discharge frequency of 4 times per month and a discharge level of 20%, the compensation willingness for EVs would be the base compensation of 0.205$ plus the 0.114$ for the high summer temperature condition.

Calculation of the flexibility resources supplied by V2G

This study estimates the theoretical flexibility potential that EVs could provide under infrastructure-ready conditions. The calculation is conditional on the availability of bidirectional charging facilities and a proportion of EVs being connected to the grid during discharge periods, with participation rates derived from the DCE results. Accordingly, the estimated flexibility represents a technical upper-bound potential.

According to statistics, the number of EVs in 2023 is approximately 20.41 million.29 Assuming each EV has a battery capacity of 45 kWh, the formula for the flexibility resources that can be provided under different scenarios is as follows:(Equation 6)Fresourcei=Pratei∗Ninventory∗Fsinglei

Where, Fresourcei represents the flexibility resources available in scenario i, Pratei denotes the participation rate in scenario i, Ninventory is the number of EVs, and Fsinglei indicates the flexibility resources that a single individual can provide in scenario i.

Assuming that each month has 30 days, the expression for Fsinglei is given by:(Equation 7)Fsinglei=EVcapacity∗EVdegreei∗EVfrequencei30

EVcapacity represents the battery capacity of the EV, EVdegreei indicates the discharge level of the EV in scenario i, EVfrequencei denotes the discharge frequency in scenario i.

The average daily equivalent economic benefit under different scenarios is given by:(Equation 8)Buseri=Fsinglei∗WTAi

Where Buseri represents the economic benefit that the user receives daily in scenario i, WTAi denotes the user’s willingness to accept compensation per kWh of electricity in scenario i.

The reported daily benefit is obtained by normalizing the monthly discharge participation over 30 days. It does not imply that users engage in V2G discharge on a daily basis.

Cost-benefit analysis of V2G and electrochemical energy storage

EVs, as mobile distributed energy storage systems, could achieve similar system application value to traditional energy storage. This paper introduces the Levelized Cost of Storage (LCOS) as the core evaluation metric, aiming to measure and compare the economic efficiency of V2G technology against current mainstream electrochemical storage technologies (lithium-ion batteries and VRB).

Given the significant impact of factors such as the diversity of battery technologies and cycle life on the economic costs of energy storage, this study calculates and analyzes the LCOS for the two representative battery technologies under various annual charge-discharge cycles (see Table S1).

We have developed an assessment framework that centers on comparing the expected compensation per kWh contributed by users to the grid with the average cost per kWh of the energy storage system over its entire life cycle (i.e., LCOS).51 This approach aims to reflect the economic attractiveness and feasibility of V2G technology in practical applications, providing scientific and objective references for decision-makers to promote the sustainable development and widespread adoption of V2G technology.

LCOS refers to the total cost invested in the entire life cycle of the energy storage system divided by the cumulative electricity delivered over its full life cycle.52 It can be calculated using Equation 9:(Equation 9)LCOS=CInv+CO&M+CRepl+CpelED

Where CInv represents the initial investment cost, CO&M refers to the operation and maintenance costs, CRepl denotes the replacement costs, Cpel is the charging cost, ED stands for the total amount of electricity fed into the grid.

The initial investment cost is calculated using Equation 10:(Equation 10)CInv=CP∗WP+CE∗QE

where CP is the cost per unit power capacity, WP is the nominal power capacity, CE is the cost per unit energy capacity, and QE is the energy capacity.

The maintenance costs are the funds invested to ensure the proper functioning of the energy storage system throughout its lifespan. These costs are generally estimated as a percentage of the initial investment, as follows:(Equation 11)CO&M=∑n=1NuCInv(1+r)n

where u represents the operating and maintenance expense coefficient for the energy storage, r is the discount rate, and N is the operational lifespan of the energy storage system.

Typically, due to the limited charge and discharge time of the battery, the lifespan of the storage container is shorter than that of the energy storage power station, thus requiring periodic replacement of the batteries. The replacement cost per unit of power is used to calculate each replacement cost.(Equation 12)CRepl=∑n=1NrepCr∗Wp(1+r)n

Where Cr is the replacement cost per unit of power and Nrep is the number of replacements.

During the operation of the energy storage system, charging is required, which incurs corresponding charging costs. The calculation formula is shown in Equation 13:(Equation 13)Cpel=∑n=1Np∗QE∗θDODη(1+r)n

Where p is the charging cost, θDOD is the depth of discharge of the energy storage system, η is the charge-discharge efficiency of the energy storage system.

The total amount of electricity delivered to the grid refers to the annual electricity delivered to the grid by the energy storage system, which is related to the storage capacity, self-discharge rate, cycle decay rate, annual cycle times, and depth of discharge.(Equation 14)ED=∑n=1NNpay∗QE∗θDOD∗(1−θDeg)(1+r)n

Where Npay is the annual cycle times of the energy storage system, and θDeg is the average annual cycle decay rate of the energy storage system.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using an RPL model based on the DCE. Model estimations were implemented in STATA 17.0, and statistical significance was evaluated based on p-values, with significance levels indicated at the 1%, 5%, and 10% levels. Subsequent calculations, including MWTA estimation and related metrics, were derived from the model results.
