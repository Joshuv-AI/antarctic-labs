The role of SUMOylation in regulating proteins that drive neuronal disease progression
Authors: Aditya Sidharth, Daesung Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMOylation is a post-translational modification in which a Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier (SUMO) protein is reversibly attached to a lysine residue on a target protein in an ATP-dependent process. This modification can affect the function of target proteins by enhancing their stability or changing cellular translocation, thereby making SUMOylation a critical regulator in the pathogenesis of multiple diseases. The functional consequences of SUMOylation, however, are highly context dependent. In Alzheimer's disease, SUMOylation stabilizes proteins that drive disease progression and enhances neurotoxicity, thereby exacerbating these conditions. Similarly, in Progressive Supranuclear Palsy, SUMO-1 conjugation stabilizes truncated tau and blocks its ubiquitination, whereas SUMO-2/3 conjugation promotes Tau clearance and recovery from neuroinflammation, illustrating how distinct SUMO paralogues can exert opposing effects within the same disease. Conversely, increased SUMOylation can be neuroprotective in cerebral ischemia and Parkinson's disease by promoting autophagic clearance of pathogenic proteins. Beyond alterations in protein stability, aberrant SUMOylation can also lead to mis-localization of target proteins, which has been identified as a pathogenic mechanism in disorders such as Huntington's disease and Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis that results in impaired clearance and pathogenic buildup, which results in neuronal death. From a therapeutic standpoint, the SUMO inhibitor TAK-981 has shown promise in both Multiple Sclerosis and in pre-clinical glioblastoma models, underscoring the translational potential of targeting of this pathway. This review examines the multifaceted role of SUMOylation across diverse neurological conditions, evaluates the therapeutic potential of SUMO inhibitors and activators, and highlights the opportunities and challenges of modulating this pathway in currently incurable neurological disorders.

Introduction

Post-translational protein modifications (PTMs) serve a vital role in regulating proteins critical for the pathogenesis of various diseases, including neurodegenerative diseases, which is a significantly growing cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide, especially among the elderly [1]. Any alteration in PTMs and related protein quality control systems, for example, molecular chaperone, ubiquitin-proteasome system, autophagy-lysosomal degradation pathway, can affect neuronal functions and homeostasis [2].

PTMs like phosphorylation, ubiquitination, glycosylation, acetylation, and SUMOylation enable a single protein to perform multiple functions, significantly increasing proteome complexity. The most common PTM, phosphorylation, regulates fundamental cellular processes like metabolism, growth, differentiation, and membrane transport [3]. Ubiquitination targets proteins for proteasomal degradation through the transfer of ubiquitin (Ub), a 76 amino-acid protein, to lysine residues on the target protein (reviewed in Ref. [4]). Glycosylation involves the irreversible transfer of polysaccharides to asparagine, serine, threonine, or tryptophan residues by glycosyltransferases, therefore affecting protein stability and trafficking (reviewed in Ref. [5]). Finally, acetylation is another PTM, in which acetyl groups are reversibly added on histone or non-histone targets, plays multiple roles such as regulating gene function or facilitating phase separation for the formation of subcellular organelles (reviewed in Ref. [6]).

SUMOylation attaches the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) to the lysine residues in target proteins [7,8]. It is known that SUMOylation has a variety of functions such as altering the function of proteins, subcellular localization of target proteins, promoting ubiquitin mediated clearance, inhibition of degradation by blocking lysine residues involved in other PTMs [[9], [10], [11]]. SUMO proteins have a molecular weight of approximately 11-kDa in size and have a similar protein structure to ubiquitin [12]. The SUMO family consists of 5 paralogs (SUMO-1 through SUMO-5), each transcribed by a separate gene. Unlike SUMO-1/2/3, SUMO-4 cannot form covalent isopeptide bonds and act as modifier for other proteins and instead acts through using non-covalent interactions [13]. SUMO-5 is known to be expressed only in primates not in rodents with restricted expressions to specific tissues including testes and blood cells [14,15]. Due to low expression levels, both SUMO-4 and SUMO-5 are also considered pseudogenes with uncertain function. Interestingly, SUMO-2 and SUMO-3 have similar peptide sequence homology around 97%, implying they have shared functions and are collectively referred to as SUMO-2/3 [12,16,17]. Similar to ubiquitination, SUMO-proteins form isopeptide bonds with the ε-amino group of lysine residues in target proteins [16,18,19]. SUMOylation is an ATP-dependent, enzymatic cascade that involves E1 activators, E2 conjugators, and E3 SUMO-ligases [20]. The E1 enzyme contains both SUMO-activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) and subunit 2 (SAE2, also known as Uba2) [21]. SUMO proteins are activated via a two-step reaction driven by ATP. SAE1 first catalyzes the adenylation of the C-terminal diglycine motif, and this adenylation promotes a structural change that enables the formation of a thioester bond between SUMO and cysteine residues in the SAE2 enzyme. SUMO attaches to the SAE2 enzyme and is then transferred to the cysteine residue of the E2-conjugating enzyme. SUMOylation is catalyzed by a single E2 enzyme. UBC9, which facilitates the transfer of the SUMO protein on the target lysine residue [21,22]. This final step in the process requires a SUMO E3 ligase to stabilize the isopeptide bond between the target lysine and the SUMO E2 thioester conjugate [23]. The E3 ligase facilitates this process: It forms a complex with charged E2-SUMO and the substrate lysine residue. This allows for the discharge of thioester-bound SUMO from the E2 enzyme onto the target [23,24]. In the second mechanism, where substrates bind directly to E2, the E3 ligase bypasses substrate recruitment and instead facilitates the discharge of SUMO onto the target [23,25]. The attached SUMO moiety to a lysine residue can be reversibly removed by Sentrin-specific protease (SENP) via a process known as de-SUMOylation [26]. Interestingly, SENP is also involved in the activation process of SUMO by the E1 enzyme (reviewed in Refs. [[27], [28], [29]]).

Another way SUMO interacts with targets is through SUMO-interacting motifs (SIMs). These motifs are essential for the SUMO conjugation reaction and for driving the downstream effects of SUMOylation [30,31]. SIMs promote SUMO modification and prevent its de-SUMOylation by modulating SENP deconjugating enzymes [32,33]. The role of SIMs in receptor proteins involves recognizing SUMOylated substrates and accordingly intracellular trafficking, protein-protein interactions, and subcellular localization of the modified substrate [34]. SUMO proteins contain ubiquitin-like α-helix and β-sheet structures [35] and the SIM binds to the surface between the α-helix and β-sheet of SUMO and results in the extension of the β-sheet in either antiparallel or parallel direction [36]. Studies have shown that SIMs preferentially interact with SUMO-1 to −3 for binding [32,37]. SUMO-1 can also regulate protein functions through non-covalent interactions with SIM motifs, a mechanism that is particularly critical in its relationship with α-synuclein and neurotoxicity [38]. This study reveals that SUMO-1 transiently binds to the N-terminus of α-synuclein, causing structural compaction that effectively delays the protein's transition into harmful fibril structures. Beyond its neuroprotective potential, this interaction exemplifies how intrinsically disordered proteins adapt their structures to perform diverse biological roles. Numerous recent studies have reported that aberrant SUMOylation may contribute to the pathogenesis of various diseases due to its versatile roles in regulating protein stability, activity, and localization [8,[39], [40], [41], [42]]. This review highlights recent progress in the research of multiple SUMOylated proteins with significant roles in relevant disease conditions.

Key features of SUMOylation

SUMOylation is distinct from small group modifications like acetylation and phosphorylation as it entails the covalent attachment of an entire protein, SUMO, to a substrate. This process primarily serves a regulatory role, acting as a reversible molecular switch for protein function, often exerting a significant effect even when only a minor section of the target protein is modified. SUMOylation plays a role in conjunction with ubiquitination, where the target protein is first tagged with a SUMO protein and then the SUMO protein itself gets tagged with ubiquitin, which results in proteasomal degradation of the target protein [43,44]. SUMOylation's uniqueness stems from its mechanism of action: the attachment of a SUMO domain facilitates further protein interactions, including those involving other PTMs or SUMO-interacting motifs [45,46]. Unlike ubiquitination, which simply requires a lysine residue for binding, SUMOylation exhibits sequence specificity, binding to a variety of consensus motifs, the original consensus motif of ΨKXE/D (where Ψ represents a hydrophobic amino acid, K is lysine, X is any amino acid, and E/D is glutamic acid or aspartic acid) is known to cover 50% of all SUMOylation sites [47]. In addition to this, there have been other motifs found such as: the inverse motif E/DXKΨ [48], phosphorylation dependent SUMOylation motif (PDSM) ΨKXEXX (pS)P, where P is a proline residue and pS is a phosphorylated serine residue [49], hydrophobic SUMOylation consensus motif ΨΨΨKXE [48], and negatively charged amino-acid dependent SUMOylation motif (NDSM) ΨKXEXXEEEE [50]. These additional consensus motifs highlight the variety of roles that are involved in the regulation of SUMOylation and its interplay with other PTM's. By targeting the same lysine residues, SUMOylation can compete with ubiquitination to block these sites, thereby protecting the protein from degradation. This competition allows SUMOylation to serve a vital role in promoting protein stability. Furthermore, the relatively large size of the SUMO subunit (approximately 11 kDa), coupled with the ability of SUMO-2/3 to form chains on target proteins, can contribute to protein stability. This SUMOylation has already been shown to play a critical role in several common diseases, as evidenced by numerous examples [40,42,51,52]. If disease progression is exacerbated by SUMOylation-mediated stabilization of target proteins, inhibiting this PTM could offer therapeutic potential. Conversely, if enhanced protein stability provides a protective effect, augmenting the activity of SUMOylation enzymes may represent a viable therapeutic approach.

SUMOylation and protein stability

SUMOylation can modulate protein stability and interactions (reviewed in Ref. [53]). For example, Ran GTPase-activating protein (RanGAP1) requires SUMO-1 mediated SUMOylation to associate with the 358-kDa Ran-GTP-binding protein (RanBP2) at the cytoplasmic periphery of the nuclear pore complex [54]. Conversely, SUMOylation can disrupt protein-protein interactions. For example, SUMOylation of ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2-25K inhibits its binding to the ubiquitin E1 enzyme [55]. X-ray crystallography studies showed that SUMO modification often does not induce significant conformational changes in the target protein [23,55], suggesting that altered protein interactions may result from SUMO masking or creating new binding sites. However, in the case of thymine DNA glycosylase (TDG), SUMO-1 binding induces a conformational change that impairs its DNA-binding ability [56]. Thus, the effect of SUMOylation on protein stability and interactions is highly target dependent.

SUMOylation increases tau protein stability in progressive supranuclear palsy

When SUMOylation increases the stability of a target protein directly involved in driving disease progression, this PTM can exacerbate the disease itself. A well-known example can be found in Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), which is characterized by executive dysfunction, unsteady walking, cognitive slowing, and speech alterations (reviewed in Ref. [57]). The pathological prominent feature of PSP is the accumulation of the 4-repeat Tau isoform, increasing both hyperphosphorylated Tau in astrocytes and neurofibrillary tangles in neurons [58]. Truncated Tau favors polymerization in both neurons and glia of PSP patients. Consequently, this process leads to neuronal loss and astrogliosis, contributing to the broader disease pathology. Takamura et al. showed that SUMO-1 conjugates to N-terminally truncated Tau fibrils found in the brain of PSP patients [42], indicating the modulatory role of SUMOylation for the formation of Tau aggregates in PSP. Using Tau proteins fused to different SUMO isoforms, they discovered that SUMO-1 modification not SUMO-2 resulted in selective promotion of both oligomerization and accumulation of truncated Tau associated with PSP. In PSP, SUMO-1 modification of the truncated Tau in PSP may impair the clearance of protein deposits, driving buildup of neurofibrillary tangles (Fig. 1). This toxicity likely stems from the fact that SUMOylation at Lys 340 residue on the Tau protein competes with ubiquitination, shielding Tau from proteasomal degradation [10]. By blocking these proteasomal degradation sites, SUMO-1 selectively drives the oligomerization and accumulation of truncated Tau, in contrast to its full-length protein. However, research from Zhang et al. have shown that SUMOylation of Tau proteins via SUMO-2/3 promotes ubiquitination-mediated clearance [59]. In Tau isoforms that are prone to aggregation, Tripartite motif (TRIM-11) acts as an E3 ligase and forms poly SUMO-2/3 chains which results in downstream degradation via ubiquitination. In addition, they also found that TRIM-11 levels are downregulated in Alzheimer's patients and intracranial injections of TRIM-11 resulted in reduced neuroinflammation and neuronal loss in a Tauopathy mouse model. In contrast to SUMO-1, SUMO-2/3 can form poly-SUMO chains [21], which could serve as a binding platform for ubiquitin, promoting proteasomal clearance. This suggests that SUMOylation is a mechanism by which TRIM-11 acts on Tau aggregates and enhances its clearance. However, it is possible that TRIM-11 also functions as a molecular chaperone that can relieve Tau misfolding and dissolving Tau deposits [59]. SUMOylation regulates Aβ clearance in Alzheimer's disease models

Alzheimer's Disease (AD) primarily affects an aging population. Its onset and progression are influenced by a combination of hereditary and non-hereditary genetic mutations, lifestyle factors, and environmental exposures. Currently, over 50 million people have been diagnosed with AD or other types of dementia, a number projected to reach 152 million by 2050 [60]. AD is considered a multifactorial disease, with two distinct hypotheses considered as the main causes: the cholinergic and amyloid hypotheses [61]. The cholinergic hypothesis is based on the association between the deficiency of choline acetyltransferase and the neocortical and presynaptic cholinergic neuronal dysfunctions [62]. Degeneration of these cholinergic neurons, characteristic of AD, leads to cognitive deficits and memory loss. β-Amyloid (Aβ) is believed to impair cholinergic neurotransmission by reducing choline uptake and the release of acetylcholine—a process that can potentially be mitigated by compounds promoting acetylcholine synthesis (reviewed in Ref. [63]). The amyloid cascade hypothesis, which is the most widely accepted model for inherited AD, states that Aβ deposition in the brain parenchyma is a key event in disease pathogenesis (reviewed in Refs. [1,60,64]). Aβ is generated by the cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP), initially by β-secretase (BACE1) and then by γ-secretase (reviewed in Ref. [65]). BACE1 level is highly increased in the brains and blood plasma of AD patients and known to initiate the formation of monomeric forms of Aβ in the amyloidogenic pathway [65,66]. A recent study showed that SUMOylation by SUMO-1 at Lys501 residue in BACE1 enhances its stability and escalates its protease activity [40] (Fig. 2). The opposite effect occurs when BACE1 is ubiquitinated at the same Lys501 site, leading to its degradation in the lysosome [67]. Phosphorylation promotes BACE1 retrograde trafficking from the endosomes to the trans Golgi network and concurrently reduces its enzymatic activity [68]. Another recent study showed that SUMOylation of BACE1 downregulates its phosphorylation; phosphorylated BACE1 has reduced SUMOylation and is prone to be degraded. SUMOylation also competes with ubiquitination of BACE1. This antagonism mirrors earlier observations that SUMO-1 opposes ubiquitination in regulating IκBα (nuclear factor of polypeptide gene enhancer in B cells inhibitor alpha) [69]. As AD progresses, BACE1 SUMOylation increases while phosphorylation and ubiquitination decrease, implying a reciprocal relationship between BACE1 SUMOylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination [39]. In an in vivo study using amyloid precursor protein (APP)/presenilin (PS1) overexpressing mice, which replicates the buildup of amyloid beta plaques developing cognitive dysfunction (reviewed in Ref. [70]), demonstrated that APP accumulation leads to an increase in SUMOylated BACE-1 and reductions of both phosphorylated and ubiquitinated BACE-1 [39], emphasizing the role of SUMOylation as primary modulator of BACE-1 for the progression of AD.Fig. 2SUMOylation of BACE-1 increases Aβ plaque-mediated neurodegeneration. In healthy neurons, BACE-1 is ubiquitinated and is degraded. In AD, SUMO-1 modifies BACE-1, which blocks the ubiquitination sites and eventually increases both APP generation and Aβ plaque formation.

Matsuzaki et al. identified multiple SUMO-1 targets by using a neuron-specific SUMO-1 overexpression mouse model [71]. Deregulated SUMOylation of synaptotagmin-1 was highly associated with an age-dependent reduction in synaptic transmission and impaired presynaptic function along with learning and memory deficits. These findings show that SUMO-1 plays a role in decreased neural functioning that occurs in neurodegenerative conditions and the inhibition of this process could be a therapeutic avenue. SUMO-1 modification also affects the processing of APP, leading to the more Aβ deposition. Knock et al. found that the SUMO-1/APP double transgenic mice had normal APP processing but there was increased formation of Aβ plaques [72]. The increased plaque formation was accompanied by reduced dendritic spine density, and further worsening of synaptic functioning and memory. This builds on the previous discovery and suggests the role of SUMO-1 as an indirect regulator of Aβ clearance rather than causing direct changes in amyloid production.

SUMOylation modulates biomolecular condensates

Biomolecular condensates are micron-sized compartments within eukaryotic cells that lack membranes but serve the function of concentrating proteins and nucleic acids to facilitate biological processes such as RNA metabolism, ribosome formation, DNA damage response, and signal transduction (reviewed in Ref. [73]). Their dynamic assembly and disassembly are critical for neuronal health (reviewed in Ref. [74]). SUMOylation plays a direct role in breaking down biomolecular condensates. For example, poly-SUMO-2/3 chains can recruit SUMO-targeted ubiquitin ligases (STubLs) such as Ring Finger Protein 4 (RNF4) [75,76], which subsequently ubiquitinate the modified target proteins to trigger their eventual degradation (Fig. 3). Stress granules (SG) are a type of biomolecular condensate that are formed through the phase separation between non-translating mRNA and RNA-binding proteins [[77], [78], [79]]. Marmor-Kollet et al. demonstrated that Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) pathology is characterized by impaired SUMOylation, which lead to a failure in SG disassembly [80]. ALS is a fatal neurodegenerative disease that causes progressive degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons, ultimately resulting in denervation and paralysis of voluntary muscles (reviewed in Ref. [81]). They showed that hyper-SUMOylation resulted in attenuation of neurodegeneration in an in vivo C9orf72 drosophila model for ALS, implying that SUMOylation acts as a critical regulator of stress granules dynamics.Fig. 3SUMOylation enhances ubiquitin-mediated degradation of stress granules in neurons. Poly-SUMOylation of RNA binding proteins in stress granules via SUMO-2/3 results in their ubiquitination via RNF-4 which breaks down stress granules in healthy neurons. However, in ALS, stress granules are not SUMOylated as poly proline-arginine repeats misfolded proteins inhibit RanBAP2 the SUMO E3 ligase stopping the attachment of SUMO-2. This results in increased SG buildup, leading to cell stress and neurodegeneration.

SUMOylation regulates the clearance of mis-folded proteins in spinocerebellar ataxias neurons

Spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs) are a genetically heterogenous group of autosomal dominant progressive neurodegenerative condition that is known primarily manifested by a loss of balance and coordination and slurred speech that occurs during adult life (reviewed in Ref. [82]). While neuropathology is predominantly localized to cerebellar Purkinje neurons, similar dysfunction has been reported in other regions, including the basal ganglia, spinal cord, and pontine nuclei [82]. Guo et al. demonstrated that SUMOylation and the subsequent ubiquitination of misfolded proteins (polyQ-ataxin 1) via promyelocytic leukemia protein (PML) is vital for neuronal function [83]. They found that PML is unable to degrade misfolded proteins via ubiquitin-mediated clearance in SUMO-2/3-deficient cells. In a mouse model for SCA1, PML-deficient mice exhibited increased motor deficits and buildup of protein aggregates in Purkinje neurons (Fig. 4), highlighting that SUMOylation mediated protein clearance plays a vital role in maintaining neuronal homeostasis. Marinello et al. also reported similar findings in Spinocerebellar ataxia 7 (SCA7) patients and mouse models [11], showing that SUMOylation of misfolded ataxin 7 (polyQ-ATX7) under physiological stress resulted in its subsequent ubiquitination and degradation. In a SCA7 knock-in mice (100 CAG repeats) SUMO-2/3 colocalized with polyQ-ATX7 in the cerebellum and retina. SUMOylated proteins with SUMO-2/3 were abnormally accumulated in the cerebellum of both SCA7 mice and human patients. These findings suggest that SUMOylation mediated ubiquitination is important for the removal of misfolded proteins associated with the pathogenesis of Spinocerebellar ataxias.Fig. 4SUMOylation facilitates the clearance of mis-folded proteins in Spinocerebellar ataxias neurons. In healthy cerebellar neurons, the promyelocytic leukemia (PML) protein catalyzes the attachment of SUMO-2 to misfolded ATXN1, facilitating its RNF4-mediated ubiquitination. Conversely, in Spinocerebellar Ataxia Type 1 (SCA1), misfolded ATXN1 evades SUMOylation, leading to the formation of insoluble aggregates. This accumulation induces severe stress in Purkinje neurons, ultimately driving cerebellar damage and motor dysfunction.

SUMOylation regulates neuronal survival in Parkinson's disease

SUMOylation of UBC9, the SUMO E2 conjugator, can protect Parkinson's neurons from degeneration. Using two different PD models induced either by 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) or pre-formed fibrils (PFF) of α-synuclein, Verma et al. showed that when UBC9 was overexpressed in the N-27 rat dopaminergic neurons, there was increased cell viability against both MPTP and PFF-mediated toxicity [84]. MPTP specifically targets dopaminergic neurons by damaging the mitochondria and causing oxidative stress [84,85]. α-Synuclein PFF can induce protein buildup-mediated PD pathology. In addition, UBC9-overexpression protected the dopaminergic neurons in the striatum from MPTP-induced neuronal damage. SUMOylation of a α-synuclein impairs lysosomal degradation in vitro due to increased solubility of the protein, though α-synuclein can still be degraded via ubiquitination-mediated proteasomes. This also highlights that SUMO-1-mediated ubiquitination clearance of α-synuclein is more critical for maintaining neuronal survival than lysosomal clearance, given that impaired lysosomal degradation did not compromise cell viability. However, Shahpasandzadeh et al. found that SUMOylation facilitates autophagic clearance in yeast, whereas mammalian SUMO-1-mediated clearance bypasses this pathway [86]. This discrepancy might be due to the species-specific difference of SUMOylation target or effect on target protein: the former study was conducted in yeast, which express only a single SUMO ortholog, whereas the latter utilized mammalian cells, which possess at least five distinct SUMO paralogs. These results support a previously finding that SUMOylation delays α-synuclein clearance but prevents pathological aggregation with increased solubility [87,88], implying that the global induction of SUMOylation may protect dopaminergic neurons from degeneration in PD.

SUMOylation and protein activity

SUMOylation can directly enhance the activity of target proteins as well. Aberrant SUMOylation is implicated in a range of neurological conditions, which are associated with target protein activity.

De-SUMOylation attenuates senescence in Alzheimer's disease model

Wan et al. investigated the role of SUMOylation in p53 concerning AD [41]. P53 is a transcription factor regulating apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, DNA repair, and senescence (reviewed in Ref. [89]). SUMOylation of p53 by SUMO-1 is known to boost its transcriptional activity and promote phosphorylation at the Ser 15 residue, which mediates neuronal senescence. P53 SUMOylation also affects synaptic damage and cognitive decline in an AD mouse model [41]. In primary murine neurons, the SUMOylation inhibitor Ginkgolic acid attenuated senescent phenotypes resulting from p53 SUMOylation. Ginkgolic acid inhibits the E1 enzyme SAE by blocking the formation of the E1-SUMO complex [90], though Ginkgolic acid can also affect other PTM pathways [91]. Therefore, the phenotypic attenuation may not stem solely from the inhibition of p53 SUMOylation, as other pathways could also be affected by Ginkgolic acid. These findings underscore that the therapeutic efficacy of selective SUMOylation inhibition depends heavily on the specific target proteins driving disease progression.

Ischemic stress protection via SUMOylation

Cerebral ischemia is the fifth-highest cause of death in the United States (reviewed in Ref. [92]). The main cause of ischemic stroke is intravascular thrombosis, which leads to tissue necrosis in the brain and focal neuronal deficits. Severe neuronal and brain damage can occur shortly after the initial episode. Importantly, SUMOylation is found to be globally upregulated in cerebral ischemia [93]. In a rat model of cerebral ischemia employing middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO), there was a dramatic increase in global SUMOylation by both SUMO-1 and SUMO-2/3 at the infarct site in the striatum and in the hippocampus, areas away from the injury site.

Another study demonstrated that overexpression of UBC9, the SUMO E2 conjugating enzyme, in transgenic mice conferred greater tolerance to ischemic injury [94], suggesting that SUMOylation can provide a neuroprotective effect against ischemia (Fig. 5). Notably, SUMOylation by SUMO-2/3 directly modifies Annexin-A1 (ANXA1), which is a glucocorticoid-regulated protein that promotes the resolution of inflammation and is crucial to microglial polarization, which inflammatory status, neutrophil migration, and macrophage phagocytosis in cerebral ischemia [51,[95], [96], [97]]. Neuronal cell death and subsequent brain damage are imminent in cerebral ischemia [92]. SUMOylated ANXA1 was found to suppress nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) activation and the generation of proinflammatory cytokines in an in vitro model for cerebral ischemia using oxygen-glucose deprivation [51,98,99]. Enhancing SUMOylation of ANXA-1 in vivo improves the motor coordination defect of the mice post-MCAO [51], highlighting that boosting the SUMOylation of ANXA-1 can be a potential therapeutic target for effective clinical treatment of cerebral ischemia.Fig. 5SUMOylation suppresses inflammation in cerebral ischemia. SUMO-2-mediated SUMOylation of ANXA1 enables its binding to IKKα for autophagic degradation. This suppresses NF-κB signaling, thereby halting the pro-inflammatory response and activating anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as TGF-β. Ultimately, this pathway enhances recovery from ischemic stress in both in vitro and in vivo models.

SUMOylation promotes autophagic clearance of α-synuclein in Parkinson's disease

Autophagy delivers unnecessary intracellular proteins and organelles to the lysosome for degradation and recycling (reviewed in Ref. [100]). SUMOylation is a key regulatory mechanism that controls autophagic clearance in Parkinson's disease (PD), which is the second-most common neurodegenerative disorder, affecting 2–3% of the population aged 65 and older (reviewed in Ref. [101]). The neuropathological hallmarks of PD are neuronal loss in the substantia nigra, resulting in striatal dopamine deficiency, and intracellular inclusions with aggregates of α-synuclein. While clinical features such as the presence of bradykinesia and related motor dysfunction are the major symptoms, PD is also coupled with other numerous non-motor symptoms such as depression, loss of smell and anxiety that can contribute to overall disability.

Lewy bodies, a PD hallmark, contain α-synuclein accumulation, which can be modulated by both SUMOylation and phosphorylation [102,103]. Suppression of SUMOylation slowed down the growth rate of the PD yeast model, along with an increased cell inclusion, suggesting that SUMOylation can regulate the buildup of α-synuclein. In addition, the SUMOylation of α-synuclein promoted autophagy-mediated clearance of the protein preferentially. Interestingly, SUMOylation is prone to being suppressed by the phosphorylation at Ser 129 residue in the human G protein-coupled receptor kinase 5 (GRK5) in a yeast PD model, indicating that phosphorylation-mediated clearance of α-synuclein may compensate for the loss of SUMOylation to prevent the buildup of Lewy bodies. These findings suggest that impaired α-synuclein SUMOylation promotes the accumulation of Lewy bodies, an effect that is reversed by phosphorylation. This reveals direct functional interplay between both modifications.

Global de-SUMOylation ameliorates demyelination in Multiple Sclerosis

Given the diverse role of SUMOylation in modulating protein activity, targeting this PTM represents a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating neurotoxicity. Multiple Sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disorder of the central nervous system which results in demyelination, axonal loss and neurodegeneration [104]. Kim et al. found that inhibition of SUMOylation via the global SUMO inhibitor TAK-981 in oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPC) resulted in increased production of myelin alongside increased expression of transcription factors Sox 10 and Myrf [105]. TAK-981 is a compound that directly inhibits the SUMO-E1 enzyme, preventing SUMO activation and its subsequent cascade [106]. In an in vivo mice model for demyelination via experimental autoimmune encephalitis (EAE), treatment of TAK-981 resulted in increased remyelination and increased number of mature oligodendrocytes along with reduced pathological severity, showing that de-SUMOylation could modulate immune mediated neurotoxicity in MS. Furthermore, Fernandez et al. have shown that SUMOylation influences the function of other glial cells, such as astrocytes; where this PTM plays a modulatory role in protein synthesis and the regulation of neuronal dendrite morphology [107]. The study demonstrated that treatment with corticosterone, a known activator of SUMOylation, stimulated the release of astrocytic small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) that enhanced neuronal protein synthesis and increased both dendritic branching and length. Conversely, sEVs derived from SUMOylation-deficient astrocytes failed to induce these structural changes. These findings indicate that astrocytic SUMOylation is a critical modulator of neuronal homeostasis, the disruption of which may drive neurotoxicity.

SUMOylation is dynamically regulated by aging

Age-related changes in SUMOylation are known to occur in mouse brain [108]. In the cortex, levels of both SUMO-1 and SUMO-2/3 modified proteins peak at 6 months of age before gradually declining through 18 months. A similar temporal profile has been observed in mouse plasma, suggesting a systemic correlation between SUMOylation dynamics and aging processes. Furthermore, Yang et al. demonstrated that 25 months old, aged mice exhibit deficits in learning and memory, which correlate with elevated levels of unconjugated SUMO-3 in the hippocampus [109]. This finding suggests that critical hippocampal proteins involved in learning and memory may lose essential SUMO-3 modifications with age. This loss of conjugation could render these target proteins vulnerable to accelerated degradation, ultimately culminating in age-related cognitive decline. However, a key limitation of this study is that the authors did not specify how they biochemically distinguished between SUMO-2 and SUMO-3. Furthermore, the study establishes only a correlation, failing to identify the specific target proteins whose disrupted modification might be driven by this expanded pool of free SUMO-3. Separately, Stankova et al. reported that while SUMO-1-conjugated protein levels remain unaltered in 5XFAD Alzheimer's disease mouse models [110], normal aging process drives a significant decline in these conjugates – especially proteins predominantly involved in RNA processing. Collectively, these findings suggest that SUMOylation has a role in preserving nuclear homeostasis and RNA metabolism in aging neurons.

SUMOylation and protein translocation

SUMOylation can also alter the subcellular localization of target proteins, thereby influencing their resultant function (reviewed in Ref. [53]). For example, Signal Transducer and Activation of Transcription 5 (STAT5) requires phosphorylation and acetylation for nuclear translocation. However, SUMOylation of STAT5 inhibits its phosphorylation, preventing nuclear translocation and inactivating the protein [111]. This translocation effect has implications for neurodegenerative diseases such as Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS).

SUMOylation reduces pathological aggregation in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis neurons

Transactive response DNA-binding protein-43 (TDP-43), an RNA-binding protein genetically linked to ALS pathology (reviewed in Refs. [81,112]), exhibits mis-localization and aggregation in ALS neurons [112]. Interestingly, SUMOylation at Lys 408 of TDP-43 by SUMO-2 occurs as a cellular response to oxidative stress in cortical neurons [113], indicating a correlation between SUMOylation, oxidative stress, and ALS pathogenesis. Further studies have shown that TDP-43 localization can affect the aggregation extent and toxicity of TDP-43 in neurons [9,114]. SUMO-2 conjugation to TDP-43 protects neurons from sodium arsenite-induced oxidative stress by stopping its cytoplasmic accumulation and retaining it in the nucleus. Recruitment of TDP-43 to promyelocytic leukemia (PML) nuclear bodies enhances its SUMOylation, triggering ubiquitin-mediated clearance of the protein via the STubL RNF4, ultimately reducing pathogenic aggregation (Fig. 6). These dynamics align with findings from Verde et al. that showed that oxidative stress-induced SUMOylation increases TDP-43 solubility, thereby preventing the formation of toxic protein aggregates [9]. This study demonstrated that TDP-43 SUMOylation has a neuroprotective effect against oxidative stress by preventing TDP-43 aggregation and subsequent neurodegeneration. Mis-localized SUMO-mGluR7 promotes Huntington's disease pathology

Mis-localization of SUMO-targeted proteins is also implicated in Huntington's disease (HD), which is caused by a CAG trinucleotide-repeat expansion mutation in the Huntingtin (HTT) gene [115], leading to a range of neurological impairments, such as cortical atrophy, dysfunction of striatal medium spiny neurons, and broader synaptic alterations [116,117]. These are underpinned by a disruption in the cortico-striatal circuit (reviewed in Ref. [118]). Recent studies using a HD mouse model have shown that SUMOylation of diverse proteins—including those involved in synaptic function, mitochondrial activity, RNA-binding, and calcium signaling—is dysregulated in HD [119]. Metabotropic Glutamate receptor 7 (mGluR7) is shown to have decreased internalization in HD neurons. However, siRNA-mediated silencing of the SUMO E3 ligase PIAS1 (protein inhibitor of activated STAT1) rescues this phenotype, underscoring the direct involvement of aberrant SUMOylation in mGluR7 mis-localization (Fig. 7). Functionally, this impaired internalization traps mGluR7 at the cell surface, disrupting downstream glutamatergic signaling via a mechanism driven by SUMO-1 conjugation. Reducing SUMOylation in this context could be potential avenue of treatment for HD.Fig. 7SUMOylated-mediated mis-localization of mGluR7 in Huntington's disease. SUMOylation inhibition reduces angiogenesis in glioblastoma

Glioblastoma is an aggressive, malignant brain cancer, originating from glial cells, and it is frequently associated with secondary neurodegeneration and neurological decline (reviewed in Ref. [120]). Although glioblastoma is not a neurological disease, its preclinical studies involving SUMO inhibitors offer helpful insights [121]. The glioma immunosuppressive microenvironment is known to promote malignant behavior, though the mechanism driving its formation and its interaction with cancer cells remain poorly understood (reviewed in Ref. [122]). One key mechanism appears to involve SUMOylation of heterogeneous nuclear ribonuclear-protein A2/B1 (hnRNP A2/B1), which leads to its cytoplasmic translocation and the ablation of the tumor suppressor miR-204-3P [123] (Fig. 8). SUMOylation and mitochondrial dynamics

SUMOylation is known to modulate mitochondrial dynamics. Mitochondrial fission, a multi-step process by which mitochondria divide (reviewed in Ref. [124]), is regulated by dynamin-related proteins (Drp1) and Optic Atrophy 1 (Opa1), which control inner membrane fusion [125,126].

SUMOylation regulates mitochondrial fission in PD

Both Drp1 and Opa1 have been implicated in PD progression [127]. Mitochondrial fission recruits the GTPase enzyme Drp1, along with various outer mitochondrial membrane proteins, including mitochondrial fission 1 (Fis1), mitochondrial fission factor (Mff), mitochondrial dynamics protein (MiD)-49, and MiD 51 [128,129]. The activity of Drp1 is modulated by SUMOylation by different SUMO proteins: SUMO-1 modification inhibits its lysosomal degradation [130], whereas de-SUMOylation of SUMO-2/3 by the de-SUMOylating enzyme SENP3 allows Drp1 to interact with Mff and promote mitochondrial fission, which results in neuronal apoptosis [131]. A common in vitro model of PD involves the treatment with rotenone, an inducer of dopaminergic neuron loss [52,132]. Rotenone, a formerly used pesticide, induces mitochondrial and oxidative stress in neurons and in vivo, resulting in damage to brain regions affected in PD [133]. Suppressing SENP3 increases both SUMO-2/3 conjugates and Opa1, while reducing Drp1 [52,131,134], thereby preventing superoxide production, reduced mitochondrial fission and eventual cell death [52], and memory and cognitive impairments [135].

SUMOylation propagates mitochondrial dysfunction in AD

In an in vitro AD model, Aβ1-42 was found to have a direct reciprocal effect on global SUMOylation in primary cultured neurons from E18 Wistar rat embryos [136,137]. The treatment of Aβ1–42 decreases global SUMOylation by reducing the levels of de-SUMOylation enzyme SENP3, the E3 ligase PIAS1 and E1 activating enzyme SAE2 levels [136]. Aβ accumulation also decreases total levels of mitochondrial fission proteins Drp1 and Mff, yet increases cleaved caspase-3, an effect attenuated by SENP3 knockdown. These findings suggest that Aβ-induced de-SUMOylation alters mitochondrial dynamics, contributing to caspase 3 activation and subsequent neurodegeneration. Collectively, SUMOylation can regulate mitochondrial dynamics in both PD and AD. The differential effects on mitochondrial dynamics observed in both PD vs AD maybe due to Aβ directly inducing cleaved caspase 3 activity while simultaneously reducing the mitochondrial fission protein level. Normally, a reduction in mitochondrial fission protein level would be expected to decrease cleaved caspase 3 activation; however, the direct induction of cleaved caspase 3 by Aβ bypasses this regulatory mechanism and results in neuronal loss.

Conclusion

Based on many recent studies, it appears that SUMOylation is a versatile regulator of protein function, impacting a broad range of cellular processes (Table 1). Specifically, SUMOylation influences target protein stability by modulating degradation rates; for instance, it stabilizes BACE-1, thereby promoting the accumulation of Aβ plaques. Furthermore, SUMOylation impairs the clearance of these plaques, ultimately driving neuronal damage. SUMOylation can also regulate protein-protein interactions by creating or blocking binding sites and modulate protein activity—exemplified by ANXA-1, which inhibits NF-κB activation. Additionally, SUMOylation can alter the subcellular localization of proteins critical to disease processes such as Huntington's disease. In spinocerebellar ataxia, deregulated SUMOylation of ataxins could result in the buildup of misfolded protein aggregates which result in neurotoxicity and further neurodegeneration. SUMOylation of TDP-43 and α-synuclein also critical factors for neurotoxicity. While these effects often contribute to disease progression, certain SUMOylation pathways also present protective roles by shielding the cells from degenerative stresses. Targeting SUMOylation with small molecule inhibitors presents a promising further investigation for novel treatments for various disease conditions. Currently, most of the proposed treatments that target the SUMOylation pathway are in cancer treatment [106]. There are no active clinical trials for SUMO inhibitors or activators for neurodegeneration. SUMO inhibitor TAK-981 has been shown to be effective as a potential therapeutic for glioblastoma in pre-clinical study [123]. SUMO inhibitors could be considered as potential therapeutic avenue for AD and SUMO activators could be used in a variety of neurodegenerative conditions such as cerebral ischemia, HD, SCA and ALS. However, SUMOylation is critical to maintaining regular neuronal physiology as well so global inhibitors such as TAK-981 could potentially have adverse side effects when used in human trials. TAK-981 is known to inhibit the E1 enzyme of the SUMO cycle stopping the cycle at its initiation [106]. Although no E3 ligase inhibitors are currently available, targeting these enzymes represents an attractive pharmacological strategy due to their substrate specificity. Exploiting this selectivity would allow for precise therapeutic tuning of SUMOylation while mitigating the risk of global pathway disruption.
