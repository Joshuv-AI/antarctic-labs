Vessel wall MRI detection of an angiographically occult A3 blister aneurysm presenting with subarachnoid hemorrhage: illustrative case

Blister aneurysms (BAs), also referred to as blood blister–like aneurysms, represent a rare but progressively acknowledged subtype of intracranial vascular lesions. They originate from nonbranching segments of the parent artery and manifest as small, sessile outpouchings without a discrete neck, which renders them challenging to capture on routine angiographic studies.

Despite their low incidence, the delicate wall structure—lined solely by adventitia—predisposes these lesions to a disproportionately high risk of subarachnoid hemorrhage. Moreover, BAs display rapid progression and a pronounced tendency toward rerupture, both of which are linked to increased morbidity and mortality.1–4

Herein, we report a case of a ruptured BA of the pericallosal artery in which conventional lumen-based imaging was inconclusive, whereas expeditious diagnosis and precise characterization were achieved using vessel wall MRI (VW-MRI). This report highlights the emerging role of VW-MRI as a valuable complementary technique in the evaluation of suspected BAs.

Illustrative Case

A 49-year-old woman was referred to our neurosurgical department with a spontaneous subarachnoid hemorrhage of unclear etiology. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) performed at the referring institution raised suspicion of an anterior cerebral artery aneurysm; however, the findings were indeterminate. On admission, the patient exhibited mild impairment of consciousness. Neurological examination revealed nuchal rigidity without focal deficits. Her vital parameters were stable, and routine laboratory investigations were unremarkable.

Initial conventional MRI confirmed a subarachnoid hemorrhage within the interhemispheric fissure and along the falx cerebri, most prominent anteriorly. An early subacute intraparenchymal hemorrhage involved the cingulate and medial frontal gyri, accompanied by vasogenic cerebral edema (Fig. 1). The patient was classified as Hunt-Hess grade I and Fisher grade 2. Three-dimensional time-of-flight (TOF) imaging identified a minute focal bulge (approximately 1 mm) along a nonbranching portion of the pericallosal artery (Fig. 2). High-resolution VW-MRI was performed the following day. Precontrast T1-SPACE (sampling perfection with application optimized contrasts using different flip-angle evolutions) imaging disclosed a small half-dome shape without a discernible neck, arising from the posterolateral wall of the left A3 segment (Fig. 3). The lesion measured approximately 2 × 1.5 mm, reflecting measurable growth compared with prior imaging. Contrary to the equivocal appearance on prior lumen-based angiographic methods, VW-MRI afforded improved delineation of the aneurysm, thereby permitting more precise assessment of its wall-based origin and morphological features. In addition, eccentric mural thickening was observed along the parent artery proximal to the aneurysm, extending nearly 8 mm (Fig. 4). This mural abnormality disclosed intrinsic hyperintensity on both T1- and T2-weighted sequences, compatible with an intramural hematoma. Postcontrast T1-SPACE imaging showed aneurysmal wall enhancement, a feature associated with recent rupture in the setting of a subarachnoid hemorrhage, reinforcing the likelihood that this lesion was the culprit aneurysm (Fig. 3). DSA confirmed the previously identified lesion, demonstrating a dissecting pseudoaneurysm arising from the A3 segment of the anterior cerebral artery with an irregular contour (Fig. 5). No angiographic evidence of cerebral vasospasm was observed. After multidisciplinary discussion and detailed counseling with the patient’s family, several therapeutic options were considered, including primary coiling with or without stent assistance, flow diversion, and surgical trapping with or without bypass. Endovascular flow diversion was ultimately selected. A p48 flow diverter stent (2.0 × 20 mm) was successfully deployed across the aneurysm segment, achieving satisfactory device apposition and coverage of the lesion. The patient tolerated the procedure without periprocedural complications and exhibited no new neurological deficits. Discussion

Observations

BAs differ from saccular aneurysms with respect to both their anatomical location and morphology. They are typically situated along nonbranching segments of the parent artery, in contrast to their saccular counterparts, which predominantly arise at arterial bifurcations. BAs are characterized by small, shallow, broad-based protrusions with an ill-defined neck and may assume a hemispherical or conical contour. Their diminutive size and subtle configuration combined with an atypical site often hamper early recognition; consequently, they are frequently diagnosed only after rupture, as observed in the present case.1–4

The pathogenesis of BAs remains incompletely clarified. Available evidence suggests that these lesions represent a subtype of pseudoaneurysm associated with arterial dissection. Histopathological analyses have demonstrated that BAs involve a focal disruption of the arterial intima, accompanied by intramural hematoma formation and subsequent weakening of the vessel wall. In addition, imaging features indicative of dissection have been described in a substantial proportion of BA cases across published series.5,6

Despite substantial advances in vascular neuroimaging, conventional lumen-based modalities, including CT angiography (CTA), MR angiography (MRA), and DSA, may have limited sensitivity for the detection of BAs. As these techniques primarily depict the vascular lumen, they provide limited information regarding the arterial wall, thereby failing to capture the dissecting nature of BAs.1–4

This case underscores the diagnostic utility of VW-MRI as an important adjunctive imaging modality. Submillimeter isotropic acquisition improves spatial resolution and enhances depiction of subtle wall-based abnormalities. The incorporation of black-blood techniques increases wall-lumen contrast, resulting in clearer visualization of the aneurysm and associated mural enhancement.7–10 Accumulating evidence supports aneurysm wall enhancement as a robust radiographic correlate of rupture, aiding in precise localization of the culpable lesion in patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage.11,12 Furthermore, 3D T1-weighted sequences enable multiplanar reconstructions, providing comprehensive assessment of the aneurysmal wall and its relationship to the adjacent parent vessel.7–10 Beyond improved lesion conspicuity, VW-MRI also yields direct evaluation of the parent artery wall. In this patient, eccentric mural T1 hyperintensity consistent with intramural hematoma was found along the parent artery proximal to the aneurysm. This hallmark sign of arterial dissection substantiates the involvement of an underlying dissecting process in the pathogenesis of BAs.13,14

BAs are distinguished by a markedly fragile wall composed primarily of a thin adventitial layer, lacking the normal trilaminar arterial architecture.5,6 This structural deficiency confers inherent instability, predisposing these lesions to rapid growth and an aggressive clinical course. Such dynamic progression is associated with a high risk of rebleeding, often resulting in catastrophic clinical deterioration.1–4 In this patient, rapid interval enlargement of the aneurysm was documented on two consecutive MRI examinations. Early recognition based on VW-MRI allowed timely therapeutic intervention and no hemorrhage recurred during the subsequent clinical course.

Lessons

In patients presenting with a subarachnoid hemorrhage suspicious for aneurysmal rupture, a negative initial CTA or MRA finding does not reliably exclude an underlying vascular lesion. In this clinical setting, DSA remains the reference standard for subsequent luminal evaluation because of its superior spatial and temporal resolution. However, when angiographic findings are equivocal, VW-MRI may provide incremental diagnostic value, particularly in cases of BAs.

BAs are small, broad-based outpouchings that are frequently occult on conventional angiographic studies. Vessel wall MRI shifts the diagnostic focus from luminal contour to the arterial wall itself, facilitating recognition of BAs and associated parent artery pathology. This approach has important clinical implications for risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making in patients with spontaneous subarachnoid hemorrhage of uncertain origin.
