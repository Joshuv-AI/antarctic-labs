Adverse childhood experiences and depressive symptoms among Thai medical students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Wanmai Woonkaew, Kreuwan Jongbowonwiwat, Jarurin Pitanupong, Thunphicha Pitanupong, Natthayada Saelim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are associated with increased risk of depressive symptoms across the life course. Medical students are exposed to sustained academic and psychosocial stress, which may interact with earlier adversity to influence mental health outcomes. However, evidence from non-Western contexts remains limited. This study examined the prevalence of ACEs and their association with depressive symptoms among Thai medical students.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between April and July 2025 among medical students at one faculty of medicine and two affiliated medical education centers in Southern Thailand. All participants completed an anonymous online survey that included the Thai version of the Adverse Childhood Experiences questionnaire and the validated Thai version of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). ACE exposure was categorized as low (0–1), moderate (2–3), and high (≥ 4). Depressive symptoms were defined as PHQ-9 scores ≥ 9. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to examine associations between ACE exposure and depressive symptoms, adjusting for demographic variables, physical illness, and recent life stress.ResultsAmong 540 participants (median age 21 years; 60.4% female), 54.6% reported at least one ACE. Moderate and high ACE exposure were observed in 20.2% and 6.7% of students, respectively. The most commonly reported ACE domains were physical neglect (31.9%), parental divorce or separation (28.8%), and household mental illness (26.8%). Depressive symptoms were present in 17.6% of participants. In multivariable analyses, moderate ACE exposure was independently associated with depressive symptoms (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 2.26; 95% CI: 1.29–3.93; p = 0.004). High ACE exposure showed a similar magnitude of association but did not reach statistical significance. Current life stress (aOR = 5.83; 95% CI: 2.77–14.31; p < 0.001) and physical illness (aOR = 2.30; 95% CI: 1.19–4.36; p = 0.012) were also independently associated with depressive symptoms.ConclusionACEs were common among Thai medical students and were associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms after adjustment for current stressors. These findings highlight the role of early-life adversity as a potential vulnerability factor for psychological distress in medical training. Longitudinal research is warranted to clarify temporal pathways and inform culturally appropriate mental health support strategies in medical education settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05044-w.

Background

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), including abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction during childhood or adolescence, are well-established forms of early-life adversity that may disrupt psychological development and increase long-term vulnerability to mental health problems [1–3]. Although ACEs are temporally distal and non-modifiable exposures, their effects may persist across the life course through influences on emotional regulation, cognitive schemas, interpersonal functioning, and stress reactivity [4, 5]. Substantial epidemiological evidence indicates that ACE exposure is associated with an increased risk of depressive symptoms in adulthood, with a graded relationship observed across levels of cumulative adversity [6–8].

Within stress-diathesis and stress-sensitization frameworks, ACEs are conceptualized as distal vulnerability factors that increase susceptibility to later psychological distress rather than acting as direct causes of depressive episodes [9–11]. From this perspective, early-life adversity may heighten sensitivity to subsequent stressors, thereby increasing the likelihood of depressive symptoms in response to later life challenges. Empirical studies among university populations support this framework, demonstrating that individuals with a history of childhood trauma report higher levels of depressive symptoms compared with those without such exposure [12, 13]. These findings underscore the continued relevance of early adversity during emerging adulthood, including the college years [14, 15].

Medical students represent a population exposed to sustained academic, emotional, and interpersonal stress. Meta-analytic evidence suggests that a substantial proportion of medical students experience depressive symptoms during training, with prevalence estimates commonly ranging from approximately 10% to 30% depending on measurement methods and settings [15–17]. Medical students report substantial levels of psychological distress, which may be comparable to or higher than those observed in other student populations [14–18]. This increased vulnerability has been attributed to the intensity, duration, and evaluative nature of medical training [14, 15].

In addition to mental health outcomes, ACEs may also affect academic functioning. Early-life adversity has been associated with difficulties in concentration, impaired cognitive performance, and poorer educational outcomes [19, 20]. In the context of medical education, where sustained attention and high-level cognitive processing are essential, these effects may have important implications for student performance and progression. Among individuals with prior ACE exposure, the combined burden of early adversity and ongoing stress may further increase vulnerability to depressive symptoms [6, 7, 13].

Beyond depression, ACE exposure has been linked to a broad range of adverse mental health outcomes among medical students, including anxiety, burnout, and psychological distress [21, 22]. Epidemiological studies indicate that ACE exposure is relatively common in this population, with approximately 40–70% of medical students reporting at least one ACE across studies conducted in North America, Europe, and Asia, although estimates vary depending on measurement methods and thresholds [21–23].

In Thailand and the broader Southeast Asian context, studies have similarly documented substantial exposure to ACEs. Systematic reviews suggest that more than half of adults in Southeast Asia report at least one form of childhood adversity [24, 25]. However, it remains unclear whether medical students, who may differ in socioeconomic background and educational trajectories, exhibit similar patterns of ACE exposure.

Despite growing global evidence, empirical studies examining ACEs and depressive symptoms among Thai medical students remain limited. Cultural and contextual factors in low- and middle-income settings may influence both exposure patterns and mental health outcomes [26, 27]. Moreover, ACE exposure may influence interpersonal functioning, including the ability to form secure relationships and maintain social support networks, which are critical for both well-being and professional development in medical training.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to examine the prevalence and characteristics of ACEs among Thai medical students. The co-primary objective was to investigate the association between cumulative ACE exposure and current depressive symptoms while accounting for contemporaneous factors, including physical illness and recent life stress. By examining early-life adversity alongside current factors within the same analytical framework, this study aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of determinants of depressive symptoms in a non-Western medical education context.

Several studies in Thailand have reported a substantial burden of depressive symptoms or poor mental health among university and medical student populations [28, 29], with prevalence estimates varying across measurement tools and study settings. However, most existing studies have focused primarily on current psychosocial stressors and academic-related factors, with limited attention to the potential role of early-life adversity such as ACEs. In addition, empirical evidence examining the relationship between ACE exposure and depressive symptoms among Thai medical students remains scarce. This study, therefore, seeks to address this gap by examining both the prevalence of ACEs and their association with depressive symptoms within the Thai medical education context.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted between April and July 2025 following approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine, Prince of Songkla University (REC: 67-606-3-1). The study population comprised first- to sixth-year medical students enrolled at one Faculty of Medicine and two affiliated Medical Education Centers in Southern Thailand.

Participants

Eligible participants were Thai-speaking medical students aged 18 years or older who provided informed consent and completed the full questionnaire. Students were excluded if they reported a previous or current diagnosis of a psychiatric disorder (e.g., depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, dysthymia), were unable to complete the questionnaire, or withdrew from the study. The exclusion of students with diagnosed psychiatric conditions was intended to reduce potential confounding related to ongoing treatment and established clinical severity when evaluating current depressive symptoms. Psychiatric diagnosis was assessed using a self-report item: “Have you ever been diagnosed with a psychiatric disorder (e.g., depression, anxiety disorders) by a healthcare professional?” (yes/no).

Recruitment and data collection

Participants were recruited using convenience-based sampling. Study invitations and posters were disseminated through class-year Line groups, student dormitories, and common areas. A sub-investigator without conflicts of interest distributed QR codes during classroom sessions, and online announcements were shared via institutional communication platforms.

Participants accessed the anonymous online questionnaire through a QR code or web link and completed it voluntarily at their convenience. Because no personal identifiers were collected, informed consent was obtained electronically through implied consent by selecting “Agree and Proceed” after reviewing the participant information sheet. The ethics committee waived the requirement for written informed consent. Participants were informed that they could withdraw at any time without academic consequences. All data were stored securely and accessible only to the research team.

The questionnaire was administered via REDCap, and all data were stored on a password-protected computer accessible only to the research team. To minimize the risk of duplicate responses, participants were required to provide an identifiable name solely for compensation purposes. These identifiers were used to screen for potential duplicate submissions by cross-checking names alongside submission timestamps and response patterns. All identifiers were removed prior to analysis to ensure anonymity. No duplicate entries were identified.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was based on the primary objective of estimating the prevalence of ACEs. Using a single population proportion formula with a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), an expected prevalence of 50% based on previous studies among medical students [21, 23, 25], and a margin of error of 5%, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 385 participants. To account for an anticipated non-response rate of 20% (e.g., incomplete or missing data), the initial sample size was adjusted, resulting in a final target sample size of 482 participants.

Although the minimum required sample size was 482, all eligible students were invited to participate during the study period to maximize statistical power and to account for potential exclusions and missing data. The achieved sample size was therefore considered adequate for both prevalence estimation and analysis of associations.

Questionnaires

Demographic characteristics

Participants reported age, gender, religion, academic year, and cumulative grade point average (GPA).

Current health-related factors

Participants were also asked about the presence of self-reported physical illness and recent life stress. Current life stress was assessed using a single self-report item: “During the past two weeks, have you experienced significant stress?” (yes/no), reflecting perceived stress during the preceding two-week period. This variable was analyzed as a dichotomous variable (present vs. absent).

Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs)

Childhood adversity was assessed using the Thai version of the Adverse Childhood Experiences questionnaire [30]. The instrument consists of 28 items covering 10 domains: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect, domestic violence, household substance abuse, household mental illness, parental divorce or separation, and criminal behavior by a household member.

Items assessing abuse, neglect, and domestic violence are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from “never” to “very often.” In accordance with standard ACE scoring procedures, responses of “sometimes,” “often,” or “very often” were coded as exposure, whereas “never” or “rarely” were coded as non-exposure. Items in the remaining domains are assessed using binary (yes/no) responses. A domain was considered present if at least one item met criteria for exposure (any-item rule).

Physical neglect in the ACE questionnaire includes conditions such as inadequate access to food, wearing dirty clothes, lacking consistent care and protection, or the absence of appropriate medical care. It may also include situations in which caregivers were impaired by substance use to the extent that they were unable to provide adequate care. Parental divorce or separation refers to the loss of a parent due to separation or abandonment during childhood. Household mental illness includes having a family member with a mental health condition, such as depression or a history of suicide attempts.

The total ACE score was calculated by summing the number of endorsed domains (range 0 to 10), with higher scores indicating greater cumulative exposure. The Thai ACE questionnaire has demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.79 for abuse domains, 0.82 for neglect domains, and 0.66 for domestic violence domains [30]. For analytic purposes, ACE scores were categorized into three levels: low (0–1), moderate (2–3), and high (≥ 4). This categorization was adopted based on prior epidemiological studies demonstrating a graded (dose–response) relationship between cumulative ACE exposure and adverse health outcomes, with a threshold of ≥ 4 ACEs consistently associated with substantially increased risk [4, 8]. The three-level categorization allows differentiation between no/low exposure, intermediate exposure, and high-risk groups, and has been commonly used in previous ACE studies to facilitate interpretation and comparability across studies. In addition, this approach facilitates comparison with prior ACE studies that commonly use threshold-based groupings [2].

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Thai version of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [31, 32]. The PHQ-9 consists of nine items corresponding to DSM diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder. Each item is rated on a four-point Likert scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), yielding total scores ranging from 0 to 27. Symptom severity is categorized as none/minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–27) [31]. Although a score of ≥ 10 is commonly used to indicate moderate depression, a threshold of ≥ 9 has also been applied in previous studies to improve sensitivity in identifying depressive symptoms in non-clinical populations [33]. In this study, a cut-off of ≥ 9 was selected to capture a broader range of clinically relevant depressive symptoms among medical students, consistent with prior studies in similar populations [33]. The Thai PHQ-9 has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.79, sensitivity of 0.53, and specificity of 0.98 [34]. In this study, the PHQ-9 demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.84.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as proportions, means with standard deviations (SD) for normally distribution, or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non-normal distribution. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality of continuous variables. Group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate.

Depressive symptoms were treated as the dependent variable, and ACE exposure was the primary independent variable. Binary logistic regression analyses were performed to examine associations between ACE exposure and depressive symptoms. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Two sequential multivariable models were constructed. Model 1 adjusted for demographic variables (gender, age, academic year, and religion) and ACE level. Model 2 additionally adjusted for current physical illness and recent life stress to assess whether associations persisted after accounting for contemporaneous factors.

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values below 5 indicating no substantial collinearity. Multivariable analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach; participants with missing data on any covariates included in regression models were excluded. Missing data were minimal (< 5%) across all variables and were handled using complete-case analysis. This resulted in a final analytic sample of 516 participants for regression analyses.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 1144 medical students were invited to participate in the study, of whom 603 completed the questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 52.7%. Of 603 respondents, 63 were excluded due to self-reported psychiatric diagnosis. No participants were excluded due to incomplete questionnaire responses or withdrawal prior to submission. The final analytic sample comprised 540 participants. The median age was 21.0 years (IQR: 19.0–23.0), and the median GPA was 3.5 (IQR: 3.3–3.7). Most participants were female (60.4%) and Buddhist (83.9%) (Table 1).

Demographic characteristics (N = 540)

Current life stress within the past two weeks was reported by 69.3% of participants Among those reporting stress, the most frequently reported source was academic pressure and upcoming examinations (63.1%), followed by issues related to friendships (14.6%), financial concerns (14.6%), and intimate relationships (10.2%).

To assess potential response bias, demographic characteristics of respondents were compared with aggregated administrative data from the total enrolled medical student population at the participating institutions (n = 1144). No statistically significant differences were observed in gender distribution or year of study (all p > 0.05).

Prevalence and characteristics of ACEs

Among participants, 245 (45.4%) reported no ACE exposure, while 54.6% reported at least one ACE. The median ACE score was 1.0 (IQR: 0–2.0). Moderate (ACEs score of 2–3) and high ACE (ACEs score ≥ 4) exposure were reported by 20.2% and 6.7% of participants, respectively (Table 2).

Prevalence of depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 ≥ 9) were observed in 95 participants (17.6%). The median PHQ-9 score was 4.0 (IQR: 2.0–7.0). Based on PHQ-9 severity categories, most participants were classified as having minimal (51.7%) or mild symptoms (36.5%), while a smaller proportion fell within the moderate to severe range (11.8%), consistent with the overall prevalence of depressive symptoms observed in this sample.

Among covariates in Model 2, current life stress demonstrated a strong independent association with depressive symptoms (aOR = 5.83; 95% CI: 2.77–14.31; p < 0.001). Physical illness was also independently associated (aOR = 2.30; 95% CI: 1.19–4.36; p = 0.012). Gender was not significantly associated with depressive symptoms. Students in clinical years had lower odds of depressive symptoms compared with those in preclinical years (aOR = 0.60; 95% CI: 0.37–0.98; p = 0.044).

An interaction term between ACE level and life stress was tested to explore potential effect modification; however, no statistically significant interaction was observed (p = 0.324).

Domain-specific analyses indicated that emotional abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect, and exposure to domestic violence were significantly associated with depressive symptoms (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study examined the prevalence of ACEs and their association with depressive symptoms among Thai medical students. More than half of participants reported at least one ACE, and moderate cumulative ACE exposure was independently associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms even after adjustment for current physical illness and recent life stress.

These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that early-life adversity may act as a vulnerability factor for psychological distress in high-demand educational settings.

The prevalence of ACE exposure in this study (54.6% reporting at least one ACE) is broadly consistent with estimates reported in medical student populations internationally, which typically range from approximately 40% to 70%, including studies conducted in the United States and Europe [21–23], as well as in Asian settings [24, 25]. Although direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in measurement and reporting, these findings suggest that ACE exposure is common even among high-achieving student populations. This pattern is generally consistent with prior studies in medical student populations, where household dysfunction and neglect-related adversities are commonly reported, although some studies have identified emotional abuse as the most prevalent domain [21–23].

Within stress-diathesis and stress-sensitization frameworks, ACEs may be conceptualized as distal vulnerability factors that increase susceptibility to depressive symptoms [3–5, 9–11]. In this study, the observed association between moderate ACE exposure and depressive symptoms is consistent with this theoretical perspective. However, given the cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established, and these frameworks are used to provide conceptual context rather than causal inference.

Current life stress demonstrated a strong association with depressive symptoms in the fully adjusted model. This finding is broadly consistent with prior study highlighting the substantial psychological burden experienced by medical students [14–17]. These results underscore the importance of proximal stressors as immediate correlates of depressive symptoms. In the Thai medical education context, hierarchical teacher-student relationships and experiences of mistreatment, alongside cultural expectations regarding emotional restraint, may further influence stress experiences and limit help-seeking behaviors among students [35].

Domain-specific analyses indicated that emotional abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect, and exposure to domestic violence were significantly associated with depressive symptoms.

Emotional and neglect-related adversities have been linked to disruptions in attachment processes, maladaptive self-schema development, and increased vulnerability to negative cognitive styles, which are central mechanisms underlying depression [4–7]. These pathways may provide one possible explanation of psychological vulnerability across the life course.

Interestingly, students in clinical years demonstrated lower odds of depressive symptoms compared with those in preclinical years. This pattern may reflect adaptation to academic demands, differences in stressor types, or selective attrition of students experiencing more severe distress. Previous studies have reported variability in depressive symptom prevalence across stages of medical training, with early transitions often representing periods of heightened vulnerability [14, 16, 17].

Notably, high ACE exposure (≥ 4 ACEs) was not statistically significantly associated with depressive symptoms, despite a similar magnitude of effect. This finding may reflect limited statistical power due to the relatively small number of participants in the high ACE group, as well as heterogeneity in the severity and context of adversity. In addition, the PHQ-9 captures symptoms over a relatively short time frame, which may be more sensitive to proximal stressors than to distal exposures.

The findings should also be interpreted within the Thai sociocultural context. Cultural norms related to family structure and emotional expression, as well as potential stigma surrounding mental health, may influence both exposures to adversity and reporting behaviors. Some studies in Thai populations suggest that stigma and cultural expectations may influence help-seeking behaviors and the expression of psychological distress [27].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it examines ACE exposure and mental health outcomes within a non-Western medical student population, addressing an important gap in the literature. Second, the study integrates both distal (ACEs) and proximal (current stress and physical illness) factors within a unified analytical framework. Third, the use of validated instruments (Thai ACE questionnaire and PHQ-9) enhances measurement reliability. Finally, the relatively large sample size and inclusion of students across multiple training years strengthen the robustness of the findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the response rate of 52.7% may introduce non-response bias. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although ACEs are temporally distal exposures, depressive symptoms were assessed concurrently, limiting conclusions regarding temporal directionality and potential causal pathways. Longitudinal studies are required to clarify whether ACE exposure prospectively predicts depressive symptom trajectories in medical training.

Third, all variables were assessed using self-report instruments, which may introduce recall bias and social desirability bias. Retrospective reporting of childhood adversity may be influenced by underreporting due to stigma or avoidance, while current mood states could affect recall through mood-congruent memory processes.

Fourth, students with self-reported psychiatric diagnoses were excluded to reduce confounding related to ongoing treatment and established mental health conditions. However, this decision may have resulted in selection bias and underestimation of depressive symptom prevalence, thereby limiting generalizability to the broader medical student population. In particular, some excluded students may have developed psychiatric conditions partly related to prior ACE exposure. Their exclusion may therefore have attenuated the observed association between ACEs and depressive symptoms and reduced the representativeness of the sample with respect to individuals at highest risk.

Fifth, recruitment was conducted using convenience sampling from a single faculty and affiliated centers in Southern Thailand. Although comparisons suggested no major demographic differences between respondents and the underlying student population, unmeasured differences may remain. Furthermore, the exclusion of students with psychiatric diagnoses may have further limited the representativeness of the sample. External validity may therefore be limited.

Sixth, although the overall sample size was adequate for prevalence estimation, the relatively small proportion of participants in the high ACE category may have limited statistical power to detect significant associations in this subgroup, as reflected in the wide confidence intervals observed.

Seventh, life stress was measured using a single dichotomous item, which may not adequately capture the severity, duration, or cumulative burden of stress exposure. Similarly, the cumulative ACE scoring approach assigns equal weight to each domain and does not account for intensity, chronicity, or developmental timing of adversity, potentially resulting in residual misclassification.

Eighth, the study did not assess help-seeking behavior or utilization of formal mental health services among students with depressive symptoms. As a result, it remains unclear to what extent affected individuals accessed professional support, which is an important consideration for interpreting the practical implications of the findings.

Ninth, sensitivity analyses using alternative PHQ-9 cutoff scores were not conducted. Given that different thresholds may capture varying levels of symptom severity, future studies should examine whether the observed associations remain consistent across alternative definitions of depressive symptoms.

Finally, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Unmeasured factors such as childhood socioeconomic status, social support, coping strategies, and personality traits may have influenced the associations observed.

Implications

The findings have several practical implications. At the individual level, early identification of students with a history of ACE exposure may be useful to consider in future development of targeted mental health support and resilience-building interventions. At the educational program level, medical schools may consider integrating trauma-informed approaches, including mental health screening, counseling services, and stress management programs. At the institutional level, routine surveillance of student well-being may help identify emerging mental health needs. At the policy level, these findings may support the importance of incorporating mental health promotion and early intervention strategies within medical education systems, particularly in culturally diverse contexts.

Given the strong association between current life stress and depressive symptoms, alongside the independent contribution of ACE exposure, a dual-focus approach may be warranted. Medical schools could consider implementing both universal stress-reduction strategies (e.g., workload management, peer support systems) and targeted interventions for students with known vulnerability factors, such as trauma-informed counseling services.

Screening approaches that integrate both current psychological distress and history of adversity may improve early identification of at-risk students. However, the feasibility and acceptability of such approaches should be evaluated within the Thai cultural and institutional context.

Future research employing longitudinal and multi-center designs is needed to strengthen causal inference and enhance generalizability. Prospective studies following students across different stages of medical training would help clarify temporal pathways linking early-life adversity, evolving stress exposure, and depressive symptom trajectories.

More comprehensive assessments of childhood adversity, including severity, timing, and chronicity, as well as validated multidimensional measures of current stress and psychosocial resources, may help clarify underlying mechanisms. Examination of potential mediators (e.g., emotion regulation, resilience, perceived social support) and moderators (e.g., coping styles, academic environment) would further advance understanding of vulnerability processes.

In addition, qualitative or mixed-methods research may provide deeper insight into how sociocultural factors influence disclosure, coping, and help-seeking behaviors among Thai medical students. Before considering integration into medical education settings, future studies should evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and potential impact of culturally appropriate trauma-informed screening or support strategies.

Conclusion

Exposure to ACEs was common among Thai medical students. Moderate cumulative ACE exposure and specific domains of adversity were associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms, independent of current stressors. Current life stress demonstrated a strong concurrent association with depressive symptoms, underscoring the relevance of proximal psychosocial demands during medical training. These findings are consistent with a conceptual model in which early-life adversity may be associated with vulnerability to depressive symptoms, while ongoing stressors may play a more immediate role in psychological distress. Longitudinal and multi-center research is warranted to clarify temporal relationships and enhance generalizability.
