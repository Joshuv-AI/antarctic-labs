 ABSTRACT

The implementation of advanced digital instrumentation and control (DI&C) systems in U.S.
nuclear power plants (NPPs) can bring significant advancements in reliability, monitoring,
and control capabilities. However, these systems also introduce new challenges, particularly
in assessing risks such as common-cause failures (CCFs) and establishing robust reliability
estimates for DI&C components. Addressing these challenges is critical for ensuring the
safe and efficient operation of NPPs. Recently, Idaho National Laboratory was tasked by the
U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) to conduct a DI&C reliability study using
operating experience data from the nuclear industry. The two operating experience data
sources for the study are the Institute of Nuclear Power Operations’ Industry Reporting and
Information System (IRIS) and the NRC’s Licensee Event Report database which is hosted
at Idaho National Laboratory at https://lersearch.inl.gov/LERSearchCriteria.aspx.
This report provides a comprehensive examination of DI&C systems, including their
architecture, operational advantages, and associated challenges. It reviews existing industry
DI&C studies and failure mode taxonomies, along with reliability data from various
industries. Through a detailed analysis of these databases, the study provides insights into
DI&C system performance. Considerations should be given to incorporate DI&C failure data
into the NRC's Integrated Data Collection and Coding System and updating the Reliability
and Availability Data System to support ongoing DI&C reliability studies. Recommendations
are also provided for modeling DI&C reliability and CCF in probabilistic risk assessment,
thereby supporting risk-informed decision-making and enhancing the reliability and safety of
NPPs.

 1 INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background

Digital instrumentation and control (DI&C) systems are central to the safe and efficient operation
of modern nuclear power plants (NPPs). Unlike analog systems, which rely on mechanical and
electrical components for signal transmission and processing, DI&C systems leverage
advanced digital technologies, including microprocessors, programmable logic controllers
(PLCs), and software algorithms. These technologies have enabled greater precision, flexibility,
and operational monitoring in nuclear facilities.

However, adopting DI&C systems also introduces new challenges. Unlike their analog
counterparts, digital systems are characterized by unique failure modes, software reliability
issues, hardware-software interdependence, and common-cause failures (CCFs). These
challenges are particularly significant in assessing risks such as CCFs and establishing robust
reliability estimates for DI&C components. Addressing these challenges is critical to ensuring
the safe and reliable operation of NPPs in an increasingly digital landscape.

To address these challenges, regulatory bodies and industry organizations have prioritized the
study of DI&C failures. The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) including its
contractors Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL),
and Idaho National Laboratory (INL); the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI); and other
organizations have conducted extensive research and reviews of DI&C operating experiences,
identifying patterns of failure and proposing mitigation strategies. These efforts have been
instrumental in advancing the understanding of DI&C reliability, yet the rapid pace of
technological innovation necessitates continual evaluation and adaptation of safety practices.

In fiscal year 2024, INL was tasked by the NRC to estimate DI&C component and system
reliability and CCF parameters by reviewing relevant nuclear industry operating experience
data. Using real-world event data from the Institute of Nuclear Power Operations’ (INPO)
Industry Reporting and Information System (IRIS) and the NRC’s Licensee Event Report (LER)
database which is hosted at INL at https://lersearch.inl.gov/LERSearchCriteria.aspx, this study
investigates DI&C system-related failure events, failure modes, and failure causes; evaluates
the feasibility to integrate DI&C failures into the existing NRC hardware failure database and
quantify DI&C component and system reliability and CCF parameters; and proposes appropriate
approaches to evaluate the risk impact of DI&C in NPP probabilistic risk assessment (PRA)
models. This report presents the 2024 study results, which includes a comprehensive analysis
of LERs and IRIS databases for DI&C failure events. The findings from the operating experience
data analysis are then used to inform and explore the process and approaches for DI&C
reliability and CCF evaluation and risk assessment to enable risk-informed decision-making for
the design, implementation, maintenance, and risk mitigation of DI&C systems.

 1-1

1.2 Objective

The objective of this DI&C study is to evaluate the feasibility of estimating DI&C component and
system reliability parameters—including CCF parameters—using existing operating experience
data from the INPO IRIS database and LERs. This study aims to enhance the understanding of
DI&C systems in NPPs by addressing their unique challenges and leveraging operational
experience to conduct DI&C reliability and risk assessment. To achieve this, the report focuses
on:

• Analyzing Operating Experience Data: Leveraging data from LERs and IRIS databases to
 uncover trends, failure modes, common causes, and risk impacts of DI&C failures.
• Categorizing Failure Modes: Reviewing and synthesizing existing taxonomies of DI&C
 failures, including hardware, software, and CCFs, and providing recommendations for
 standardized classifications.
• Understanding Failure Modes: Identifying and analyzing the failure modes unique to DI&C
 systems, including both hardware- and software-related issues, and evaluating their
 implications for plant safety and reliability.
• DI&C Event Coding and Integration into the Existing NRC Failure Database: Proposing a
 structured approach for documenting DI&C-related events and leveraging operational
 experience data to estimate DI&C system reliability.
• DI&C Reliability, CCF, and Risk Assessment: Exploring and proposing methodologies for
 incorporating DI&C reliability into PRA models and providing risk insights to decision-makers
 for DI&C design, implementation, and operation.
1.3 Limitations

While this report offers a detailed exploration of DI&C systems and their reliability, it is essential
to acknowledge its limitations:

• Data Limitations: The analysis relies heavily on the LER and IRIS databases. While these
 reports provide valuable insights, they are not exhaustive. Some events may go unreported,
 and the level of detail provided may vary significantly across reports.
• Regional Focus: The study focuses exclusively on U.S. NPPs. While this provides a clear
 scope, it limits the applicability of findings to other regulatory environments and operational
 practices.
• Technological Scope: The report emphasizes hardware- and software-related failures but
 does not comprehensively address other aspects of DI&C systems, such as cybersecurity,
 which warrants dedicated study.
• Dynamic Nature of DI&C Systems: The rapid pace of technological advancement means
 that the findings of this report may become outdated as new technologies and
 methodologies emerge.
Despite these limitations, the report aims to contribute to a foundational understanding of DI&C
systems, supporting the ongoing efforts to enable the reliability and risk assessment of DI&C
toward its successful implementation in NPPs.

 1-2

1.4 Structure

This report contains the following sections:

• Section 2 provides an overview of DI&C systems, discussing their architecture, operational
 principles, and advantages over analog controls. It sets the stage by highlighting the
 benefits and challenges associated with digital technologies in NPPs.
• Section 3 examines industry practices to understand lessons learned from operational
 experiences and the state-of-the-art DI&C reliability research.
• Section 4 focuses on failure mode taxonomies for DI&C systems. It establishes structured
 classifications for hardware and software failures, incorporating industry standards such as
 Nuclear Energy Agency (NEA) and ORNL frameworks.
• Section 5 presents the findings from the INL operating experience study, which includes
 analyses of the LER and IRIS databases. The analysis identifies failure trends,
 characterizes failure modes and causes, assesses the operational and safety impacts of
 DI&C failures, and discusses their implications for PRA models.
• Section 6 reviews existing DI&C reliability data from various industries and proposes a
 structured plan for integrating DI&C failure events into existing databases and estimating
 DI&C reliability. It includes the development of a standardized DI&C failure database and
 methodologies for modeling DI&C-related risks, such as internal and external CCFs, in PRA.
• Section 7 concludes the report by summarizing the key findings.

 2 DIGITAL INSTRUMENTATION AND CONTROL OVERVIEW

Instrumentation and control (I&C) systems are essential for the safe, reliable, and efficient
operation of NPPs. Over the years, these systems have evolved from analog-based
architectures to digital solutions, bringing enhanced functionality, precision, and adaptability.
This section provides an overview of the primary functions of I&C systems, their architecture,
and the transition from analog to digital technologies.

2.1 Functions of Instrumentation and Control Systems

I&C systems act as the central nervous system of an NPP, coordinating multiple functions to
ensure safety and efficiency. These systems comprise sensors, signal processors, actuators,
and human-system interfaces that work together to monitor and regulate critical plant
parameters, including temperature, pressure, and flow rates.

As shown in Figure 2-1, the operational flow of I&C functions begins with sensors measuring
process parameters. The raw signals from sensors, which vary in format and scale, undergo
signal conditioning and data acquisition to standardize and normalize the data. This processing
ensures consistency, enabling the signals to be compared against predefined setpoints or
operational thresholds. The next step, signal processing, involves calculating deviations from
desired values and generating corrective commands. Finally, these commands drive the
actuators, such as valves or motors, to implement necessary adjustments. The overall objective
is to maintain plant conditions within the design limits, minimizing deviations and ensuring
operational stability [1].

Figure 2-1 Typical I&C Functions

The modular nature of I&C systems allows them to fill various functional roles:

• Data acquisition: Gathering measurements and signals from sensors.
• Control and limitation: Maintaining process parameters within acceptable ranges.
• Fault Detection and Alarm Management: Identifying anomalies and alerting operators.
• Human-System Interface: Providing critical plant information and enabling manual
 interventions.

 2-1

By integrating these functions, I&C systems enable automated responses to off-normal events
while also supporting operators in decision-making. This balance between automation and
human oversight is particularly important in the high-stakes environment of NPPs.

2.2 Functional Subdivision of Instrumentation and Control Systems

I&C systems monitor and control hundreds or thousands of plant parameters and can be
functionally subdivided into several groups, each designed to address specific aspects of plant
monitoring, control, and response. According to the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA)
report [1], these functional groups include sensors, operational systems, safety systems,
communication systems, human-system interfaces, and actuators. Table 2-1 provides an
overview of these groups.

Table 2-1 Functional Subdivision of I&C Systems

 Functional Group Description
 Sensors Measure critical parameters, such as temperature,
 pressure, flow rates, and neutron flux, to provide real-
 time plant data.
 Operational Control, Regulation, Maintain stable operating conditions by managing
 and Monitoring Systems parameters within setpoints and initiating corrective
 actions when needed.
 Safety Systems Prevent and mitigate abnormal conditions or accidents
 through mechanisms like reactor trips and emergency
 shutdowns.
 Communication Systems Facilitate a seamless data exchange between sensors,
 controllers, actuators, and human-system interfaces.

 Human-System Interfaces Provide operators with accessible displays and controls
 for real-time monitoring and decision-making.

 Actuators Execute control commands by physically adjusting plant
 systems, such as valves, pumps, or control rods.

2.3 Analog Versus Digital Instrumentation and Control

I&C systems have transitioned from analog to digital architecture driven by advancements in
electronics, computing, and communication technologies. While both analog and digital systems
share the same fundamental operational principles, their physical implementations and
technological capabilities differ significantly.

According to the Nuclear Energy Institute [2], “A digital asset may be identified as a
programmable device (e.g., Erasable Programmable Read Only Memory and microprocessor)
that uses any combination of hardware, firmware, and/or software to execute internally stored
programs and algorithms, including numerous arithmetic or logic operations, without operator
action.”

 2-2

As depicted in Figure 2-2, the primary distinction lies in the method of signal processing. Analog
systems use continuous voltages or currents to represent measured parameters. These signals
are processed using analog electronics, such as amplifiers and filters, and directly control
actuators. In contrast, digital systems convert analog signals into binary representations using
analog-to-digital converters (A/D). The digital data is then processed by computer processors,
which execute control algorithms to determine the appropriate outputs. The processed digital
signals are converted back to analog form via digital-to-analog converters (D/A) to drive
actuators.

Figure 2-2 Analog Versus DI&C Systems
Source: Adapted from [1].

Enclosure 2 of SECY-91-292 [3] discusses the differences between analog and digital I&C
systems. Analog I&C systems typically use direct, hardwired connections between components
configured for single parameters, with common elements often limited to cable routing fixtures
and power supplies. Control algorithms in analog systems are traditionally implemented using
dedicated analog circuitry, with each circuit designed to perform a specific mathematical or logic
function.

 2-3

In contrast, DI&C systems generally consist of distributed input/output circuit boards interfacing
with plant sensors and actuators. These boards are connected through multiplexed data links or
data highways to a set of equipment with shared computer platforms performing numerical and
logical computations for multiple control functions. As a result, significant interdependencies can
arise within the shared processing environment, particularly when common software modules
are used across different functions. It is this functional and architectural sharing that raises key
concerns regarding reliability and the potential for CCF in DI&C systems.

2.4 Components of Digital Instrumentation and Control Systems

DI&C systems have become integral to the safe and efficient operation of modern NPPs. These
systems leverage advanced architectures that integrate sensors, data processors,
communication links, logic units, and actuators.

As illustrated in Figure 2-3, DI&C systems consist of modular components that work in
sequence to ensure effective monitoring, processing, and control [4].

Figure 2-3 Components of DI&C Systems

The components of DI&C systems typically include:

• Sensors: Devices that capture real-time physical parameters, such as temperature,
 pressure, flow rates, and neutron flux. These sensors serve as the primary input sources for
 I&C systems, providing data critical to plant safety and efficiency.
• Data Acquisition and Signal Processing Units: These electronics process raw sensor data,
 applying error-checking algorithms and converting signals into formats suitable for
 computational analysis.
• Input/Output (I/O) Processors: These modules act as intermediaries between data
 acquisition units and computational logic, ensuring efficient communication and error-free
 data transmission.
• Logic Units: These units execute programmed algorithms to evaluate input parameters
 against predefined criteria, determining whether control actions are required. Logic units
 often incorporate advanced computational methods, such as neural networks and fuzzy
 logic, to optimize decision-making.
• Actuators: Final control elements, such as valves or motors, that implement the system’s
 decisions by adjusting physical processes.
In such systems, signals from sensors are processed through data acquisition and error-
checking units before being communicated to logic units for decision-making. These logic units
execute internal software that compares processed parameters against predefined criteria,
triggering actuation as needed. Communication buses link all subsystems, ensuring information
flow to actuators and control room displays [4].

 3 EXISTING DIGITAL INSTRUMENTATION AND CONTROL
 STUDIES REVIEW

This section presents a review of several key studies conducted by leading organizations, such
as the U.S. NRC, national labs, EPRI, and European entities, focusing on their contributions to
advancing reliability, safety, and risk assessment methodologies for DI&C systems. The section
synthesizes insights into CCFs, lifecycle vulnerabilities, and lessons learned from operating
experience, while also identifying gaps in existing research and opportunities for future
development. By highlighting these studies, the aim is to provide a comprehensive
understanding of the state-of-the-art DI&C reliability research and to inform strategies for
improving digital system performance in NPPs.

3.1 Nuclear Regulatory Commission and National Lab Studies on Digital
 Instrumentation and Control Reliability

The U.S. NRC has conducted extensive research to address the challenges of modeling DI&C
systems in PRA for NPPs. The transition from analog to digital systems in NPPs has brought
numerous advantages, including improved precision, automation, and flexibility. However, it has
also introduced significant challenges, particularly in understanding and modeling the unique
failure modes and interactions inherent to digital systems. These challenges have necessitated
significant research efforts, as traditional PRA methods were not originally designed to address
the complexities of software failures, interdependencies, and time-dependent behaviors found in
digital systems. This section outlines key NRC studies and their contributions to advancing the
field.

3.1.1 Evaluation of Traditional Probabilistic Risk Assessment Methods (NUREG/CR-6962)

Traditional PRA methods, while foundational for analog systems, exhibit significant limitations
when applied to DI&C systems, as highlighted in NUREG/CR-6962 [5]. The study identified
three primary challenges: modeling software reliability; addressing CCFs; and accounting for
interdependencies within digital components. Traditional methods, such as fault tree analysis,
are hardware-centric and rely on failure rate data that is not applicable to software. Similarly,
CCF modeling in traditional PRA does not account for the shared software or configuration
errors that can propagate across digital systems.

The report called for enhancements to traditional PRA methodologies, such as integrating
software reliability models and expert elicitation techniques. While these recommendations
provide a pathway for improving PRAs, the study acknowledged that the scarcity of data on
digital system failures remains a critical barrier to implementation.

3.1.2 Digital Feedwater Control Systems Case Study (NUREG/CR-6997)

NUREG/CR-6997 [6] applied traditional PRA methods to a digital feedwater control system,
providing insights into their practical application. The analysis employed Markov models, failure
modes, and effects analysis to evaluate hardware and software reliability. The study found that
traditional methods adequately modeled hardware failures but were insufficient for addressing
software timing errors and intercomponent interactions. Markov models, which incorporate state
transitions and probabilistic dependencies, proved more effective in capturing these
complexities.

 3-1

However, the study identified limitations in scaling Markov models to larger systems due to their
computational intensity and the challenges of defining state transition probabilities. Furthermore,
the lack of empirical data on software failures constrained the accuracy of the reliability
estimates.

3.1.3 Dynamic Reliability Modeling (NUREG/CR-6942)

Dynamic reliability modeling was introduced by the NRC as an enhancement to traditional static
PRA methods. NUREG/CR-6942 [7] explored the use of dynamic models to account for the
sequential, time-dependent behaviors of digital systems. The report emphasized that digital
systems often exhibit operational complexities that static methods, such as fault tree and event
tree analyses, fail to capture. For instance, software faults and timing anomalies are highly
dependent on the operational sequence of events. Dynamic reliability modeling integrates the
temporal aspect, providing a more realistic representation of how failures propagate and interact
within digital systems.

The report illustrated the effectiveness of dynamic modeling in addressing failure scenarios
unique to digital systems, such as software timing mismatches and cascading component
failures. By simulating operational scenarios, dynamic models identified vulnerabilities that could
lead to the loss of safety-critical functions. However, these models also introduced challenges,
including increased computational demands and the complexity of defining accurate failure
parameters. The report emphasized the need for empirical data to support dynamic modeling,
particularly for software-related faults.

3.1.4 Brookhaven National Laboratory Study on Common-Cause Failures

A BNL study analyzed DI&C system failure events reported in U.S. NPPs between 2005 and
2015 [8]. The primary purpose of the study was to identify trends in digital system vulnerabilities,
particularly focusing on potential CCFs. By examining LERs, the study aimed to better
understand the risks associated with DI&C systems and their implications for safety and
reliability in NPPs.

3.1.4.1 Objectives and Scope

The study pursued two main objectives. The first was to identify and categorize DI&C-related
failure events documented in the LER database maintained by the NRC. The second objective
was to evaluate these events to determine whether they involved potential or confirmed CCFs,
which could simultaneously affect multiple components or systems. The scope included both
safety-related and non-safety-related systems, encompassing control systems, instrumentation,
and monitoring systems. A particular focus was placed on digital failures in safety systems due
to their potential impact on plant operations and safety margins.

3.1.4.2 Data Source and Time Period

The analysis was based on a search of the NRC’s LER database conducted on May 5, 2016, for
the period between January 1, 2005, and May 4, 2016. The search employed a series of
keywords such as “digital,” “software,” “processor,” and “PLC.” A total of 618 potentially relevant
LERs were identified during the initial search. These reports were further reviewed, and 97
events were confirmed as involving DI&C systems. Detailed reviews of these events included
categorization based on their characteristics, functional impacts, and safety significance.

 3-2

3.1.4.3 Key Findings and Results

The analysis reviewed 618 LERs and identified 97 events that were related to DI&C failures.
These events were categorized based on their characteristics, whether they involved safety-
related systems, and whether they were potential CCFs. Of these, 18 events were identified as
potential or confirmed CCFs. The study further analyzed the failure events, considering their
implications for plant operations and safety.

Failures in non-safety-related systems, such as control systems, were of less immediate
concern. These systems often lack redundancy, which could allow faults to propagate and
potentially result in plant-level impacts.

In safety-related systems, several failures were identified as having the potential to lead to
CCFs. At Oconee 2 in 2011, an incorrect torque value caused containment isolation valves to
fail to close, representing a critical failure in maintaining containment integrity. Similarly, in 2005,
the Core Protection Calculator system at Palo Verde 2 experienced a hardware failure that
could have affected multiple redundant channels, raising concerns about common-cause
vulnerabilities in safety-critical systems.

3.1.5 Analysis and Evaluation of Operational Data Report on Digital System Failures

The Analysis and Evaluation of Operational Data report [9] provides an analysis of digital
system failures documented in U.S. NPPs between 1990 and 1993. The study focuses on
understanding the vulnerabilities of DI&C systems, which were increasingly replacing analog
systems due to obsolescence. By reviewing LERs and safety evaluation reports, the study
aimed to identify common failure modes, assess their underlying causes, and evaluate how the
NRC approaches the review of digital system upgrades. This effort provided a foundation for
improving the reliability and safety of digital systems in NPPs.

3.1.5.1 Objectives and Scope

The primary objectives of the study were to identify and categorize the types of digital system
failures and to assess the NRC’s approach to reviewing digital system upgrades for
electromagnetic compatibility, software reliability, and human-machine interface (HMI) design.
The scope of the analysis encompassed both operational failures reported in LERs and specific
safety evaluations of analog-to-digital upgrades.

3.1.5.2 Data Source and Time Period

The analysis was based on the LERs submitted to the NRC between 1990 and 1993. The LERs
were retrieved using a structured data search method. The Nuclear Documents System was
used to identify applicable events involving digital equipment failures. A combination of subject-
and Boolean-based search terms, including “microprocessor,” “data communication,” “data
processing,” “memory,” “software,” and “CPU,” was applied. The search yielded more than 200
LERs, from which 79 relevant cases were selected for detailed analysis. In addition, the study
reviewed two safety evaluation reports for analog-to-digital system upgrades, one for a General
Electric plant and another for a Westinghouse plant.

electromagnetic interference (EMI), and random component failures.

Software errors were the most frequent, accounting for 30 events. Most software errors were

performance, or interface requirements. Configuration control failures, involving the inadequate
management of software changes, were another significant contributor to this category.
HMI errors, reported in 25 events, stemmed from insufficient operator training, poorly designed
interfaces, unauthorized data entries, and deviations from established procedures. These errors
highlighted the critical role of ergonomics and operator proficiency in the reliable operation of
digital systems.

3.2 Electric Power Research Institute Study on Common-Cause Failures

EPRI has conducted several key studies that provide valuable insights into the behavior and
management of DI&C systems in NPPs. One study titled “Operating Experience Insights on
Common-Cause Failures in Digital Instrumentation and Control Systems [11],” was published in
2008. This work evaluated the operational history of U.S. NPPs over two decades to assess
trends, insights, and lessons learned from CCFs in digital systems. Additionally, the 2014 follow-
up study, “Digital Common-Cause Failure Susceptibility: 2014 Project Status [12]”, provided
updated insights on evolving challenges and strategies for mitigating vulnerabilities. This section
primarily focuses on the 2008 report, which offers an in-depth analysis of operating experiences
with digital I&C systems in NPPs.

3.2.1 Objectives and Scope

The primary aim of the study was to leverage operational experience data to enhance diversity
and defense-in-depth evaluations, focusing on mitigating risks associated with CCFs.
Secondary goals included compiling failure statistics to inform reliability models and PRAs for
digital systems. The study examined both safety related (1E) and non-safety related (non-1E)
system events, with a strong focus on digital CCFs that could potentially degrade or disable
critical safety functions.

 3-5

3.2.2 Data Source and Time Period

The research utilized event reports from the INPO and NRC public Agencywide Documents
Access and Management System database. Data covered a 20-year span, from 1987 to 2006,
encompassing events reported in LERs, event notifications, and other industry reports. Of 504
identified events, 322 were fully evaluated using detailed source documents and were
categorized as:

• 49 Safety Related (1E) Events: These involved 1E systems such as reactor protection,
 engineered safety features actuation, diesel load sequencer, post-accident monitoring.
• 273 Non-Safety Related (non-1E) Events: These pertained to systems like turbine
 electrohydraulic control and feedwater controls.
Events were further analyzed to identify the presence of single defects (localized to one
division) or common defects (spanning redundant systems).

3.2.3 Key Findings and Results

The analysis revealed critical insights into the causes and dynamics of failures in digital
systems. Among the 49 safety-related events, 27 were identified as involving common defects—
vulnerabilities that existed across redundant channels. Importantly, no system-level actual CCFs
were observed in safety-related systems, underscoring their inherent resilience due to design
simplicity and regulatory requirements for redundancy and independence. The only potential
system-level CCF linked to software was attributed to a design flaw in an automated self-testing
feature, which could have prevented safety injection signals under specific conditions. However,
manual intervention by operators would have mitigated this issue.

In contrast, the 273 non-safety-related events revealed greater vulnerability to CCFs, with 77
cases involving common defects. Non-safety systems often lacked the stringent independence
required for safety-related systems, making them more susceptible to failures stemming from
shared resources such as power supplies and communication links. The study highlighted
specific cases where hardware failures or inadequate system designs led to significant
operational challenges, including unplanned power transients.

The causes of these events varied between the two system categories. For safety-related
systems, hardware failures, human errors, and procedural inadequacies were more prevalent,
while software design issues were a minor contributor. For non-safety-related systems,
inadequate hardware and software designs featured prominently, reflecting differences in the
rigor of quality assurance processes.

Corrective actions were diverse and tailored to the specific causes of the events. In safety-
related systems, the most common actions included hardware replacements and software
updates to enhance monitoring and alarm capabilities. In non-safety systems, software changes
were frequently implemented to address vulnerabilities, even when software was not the
primary cause of the event. This trend reflected an adaptive use of software to mitigate risks
associated with hardware and process deficiencies.

 3-6

3.2.4 Limitations and Perspectives

The study acknowledged several limitations and important perspectives on the data and
findings. One key limitation is the inherent bias in the data, which primarily focuses on failures
and misbehaviors rather than successes. This imbalance can lead to a skewed perception of
the reliability of digital systems. For example, while anecdotal evidence suggests that digital
feedwater control upgrades have greatly enhanced plant reliability and safety, such successes
are rarely documented in the datasets used for this study. Instead, the dataset includes several
examples of CCFs in digital feedwater control systems, potentially offering a misleading view of
their overall impact.

Another limitation is the shortage of statistically significant data on safety system demands and
failures-to-actuate-on-demand, as safety systems are intentionally designed to be simple,
rigorously tested, and rarely called upon to perform their safety functions. The lack of extensive
safety system failure data might also reflect the effectiveness of current design and
development practices. The study highlights that focusing solely on failures could obscure the
broader benefits of DI&C systems.

Despite these limitations, the report provides valuable insights into the types of problems
experienced in digital systems and how to mitigate CCFs. It also emphasizes the need to
examine aspects of digital system implementation and management that have proven more
problematic than software, such as hardware and process-related issues. These observations
underline the importance of balanced data collection and analysis to better understand the full
impact of DI&C systems in safety-critical applications.

3.3 European Clearinghouse Study on Digital Instrumentation and Control
 Systems

Another significant study in the area of DI&C systems is the “Operating Experience with Digital
I&C Systems at Nuclear Power Plants [13],” published by the European Clearinghouse on
Operating Experience Feedback in 2019. This study provides a detailed analysis of international
operating experience, focusing on DI&C systems across NPPs and identifying key challenges
and lessons learned.

3.3.1 Objectives and Scope

The study aimed to analyze operating experience to identify challenges and lessons learned in
the implementation of DI&C systems in NPPs. Its primary focus was on deriving insights to
improve the design, implementation, and operational practices for these systems. The study
aimed to review recent operating experience to highlight challenges encountered in the
implementation of DI&C systems and to derive lessons learned for improving design and
operational practices. A total of 82 events were identified initially, with 25 selected for detailed
analysis after a rigorous screening process.

The research focused on systems classified as digital based on their use of computer-based or
hardware-programmed components, emphasizing events where software played a significant
role. The selected events were analyzed across the lifecycle of DI&C systems, including design,
verification and validation (V&V), operation, maintenance, and modifications.

 3-7

3.3.2 Data Source and Time Period

The study reviewed event reports from two primary databases: the International Reporting
System for Operating Experience (IRS), operated by the IAEA and the NEA of the Organization
for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), and the U.S. NRC LERs. These
databases were selected for their comprehensive coverage of operational events in NPPs. The
IAEA/OECD IRS database provides worldwide coverage of events of interest to the international
nuclear community, while the U.S. NRC LERs database offers a consistent and detailed
account of events in U.S. nuclear facilities.

The review covered events reported between 2013 and 2018. The search was conducted in
September 2018, ensuring the inclusion of most events within this period. However, events from
late 2017 and 2018 may not have been fully captured in the IAEA/OECD IRS database due to
reporting delays.

3.3.3 Key Findings and Results

The study identified and analyzed 25 significant events from a pool of 82 reports sourced from
international databases, focusing on DI&C systems in NPPs. These events were categorized
based on the affected DI&C systems and their respective lifecycle phases to extract detailed
insights.

The events were distributed across various DI&C systems, prominently featuring turbine
generator instrumentation and control systems. Specifically, eight events were related to the
digital electrohydraulic control systems of turbine generators. Although these systems are non-
safety-related, their proper functioning is essential for plant availability and operation. Five
events involved plant process computers, with three cases affecting the entire plant digital
control system in highly digitized environments, while two were related to auxiliary systems.
Feedwater control systems were implicated in five events, often leading to reactor trips due to
software-related issues affecting water level control. Other events involved leak monitoring and
engineered safety features actuation systems.

The lifecycle phase analysis revealed that the design phase accounted for the largest proportion
of incidents, comprising approximately 35% of the events. The operational and maintenance
phase contributed to 23% of the events, reflecting challenges in managing and maintaining
digital systems. Requirement specification issues were identified in 16% of the cases,
highlighting the importance of accurate initial system requirements to prevent downstream
failures. V&V issues accounted for 13% of events, particularly due to limitations in identifying
and addressing latent software defects. Finally, the modification phase represented another
13%, with poor documentation and testing during system upgrades contributing to operational
issues.

These findings demonstrate that failures were not confined to safety-critical systems but
extended to balance-of-plant and auxiliary systems, indirectly impacting plant operation and
safety. The events also underscored the importance of robust design, rigorous validation, and
effective maintenance practices across all lifecycle phases of DI&C systems to ensure their
reliability and performance.

 3-8

3.3.4 Insights from Event Analyses

The study highlighted several recurring themes and technical challenges associated with the
reported events involving DI&C systems. These insights provide a deeper understanding of the
vulnerabilities in system design, configuration, and operation. Three main areas emerged as
significant contributors to the events reviewed: the use of smart devices, configuration
management, and digital communications.

3.3.4.1 Use of Smart Devices

Smart devices, which refer to sensors and actuators containing computer-based technology
such as microprocessors with firmware or embedded software, have become increasingly
prevalent in NPPs. While they offer enhanced functionalities compared to traditional analog
devices, their integration presents notable challenges. One significant issue is the qualification
of smart devices for nuclear applications. Independent access to proprietary software, such as
source code, is often restricted, making it difficult for licensees to perform adequate V&V. This
lack of transparency can lead to unanticipated failure modes.

The study detailed an event where the presence of embedded software in relays used for
emergency diesel generator control was discovered years after installation, revealing a potential
CCF mode. Other examples included:

• A generator trip due to inadequate validation of a smart device’s automatic voltage
 regulator.
• Reactor trips caused by errors in embedded software for feedwater control, which contained
 unverified parameters and contributed to fluctuating reactor water levels.
• Operator errors due to poor ergonomic design in digital controls, such as inadvertent
 activation of a compressor control display.
These cases underscore the importance of comprehensive verification processes for smart
devices, including evaluating their embedded software and ensuring compatibility with plant-
specific requirements.

3.3.4.2 Configuration Management

Weaknesses in configuration management emerged as a common cause of several events.
DI&C systems are inherently complex, with extensive documentation and frequent software
updates required. Poorly managed modifications and insufficient documentation often result in
operational failures.

For example, modifications in turbine control systems and feedwater systems were cited as
failure causes of several reactor trips. These failures often occurred because the impact of
changes on plant behavior was not fully understood or documented, highlighting the need for
rigorous quality assurance processes during software upgrades and system modifications.

 3-9

3.3.4.3 Digital Communications

The integration of digital communication networks introduces new failure modes that do not
present in analog systems. Unlike point-to-point connections, digital systems often share data
across networks, creating the potential for fault propagation. Maintaining independence between
redundant safety systems and between safety and non-safety systems is critical to prevent
cascading failures.

Two similar events involving reactors with digital platforms demonstrated how excessive central
processing unit (CPU) loads caused operator station screens to go blank, requiring manual
control transfer. Another event detailed a communication hub failure that rendered a redundant
CPU unable to take over, leading to a turbine trip. These incidents highlight the importance of
designing communication networks with robust fault isolation capabilities to prevent single-point
failures from affecting multiple systems.

3.4 Light Water Reactor Sustainability Program Study

The Light Water Reactor Sustainability (LWRS) Program, funded by the U.S. Department of
Energy, has developed methodologies for modeling DI&C systems in PRA. One development is
an integrated risk assessment process that combines qualitative hazard identification and
quantitative reliability assessments. This approach considers challenges posed by DI&C
systems, particularly software CCFs, which traditional PRA methods often fail to adequately
model. This section reviews two studies that contribute to methodologies for modeling DI&C
systems in PRA:

3.4.1 Integrated Risk Assessment for Digital Upgrades

Bao et al. [14] propose an integrated risk assessment process tailored to evaluate DI&C system
upgrades in NPPs. Their methodology combines both qualitative and quantitative approaches to
assess the reliability and safety implications of transitioning from analog to digital systems. This
work focuses on systematically identifying and modeling critical failure modes specific to digital
systems, including software and hardware interactions, data communication failures, and HMI
challenges.

This approach employs fault tree analysis alongside dynamic event tree modeling to capture
static and time-dependent failure mechanisms. By incorporating diverse failure scenarios, the
methodology highlights vulnerabilities that could propagate through interconnected digital
systems. Despite its strengths, the process relies heavily on expert judgment to estimate
software failure probabilities, which introduces subjectivity into the analysis. Moreover, the
scarcity of empirical data on digital system failures limits the robustness of quantitative
assessments. The scalability of the proposed framework to more complex systems or larger
digital architecture also remains a challenge.

3.4.2 Quantitative Risk Analysis

Another LWRS DI&C report is titled “Quantitative Risk Analysis of High Safety-Significant
Safety-Related Digital Instrumentation and Control Systems in Nuclear Power Plants Using
IRADIC Technology” [15]. This report focuses on developing risk-informed methodologies to
evaluate safety margins for high-safety-significant safety-related DI&C systems, particularly in
contexts with limited operational data.

 3-10

The study introduces approaches, such as the Bayesian and HRA-Aided Method for the
Reliability Analysis of Software methodology, to estimate software and hardware failure
probabilities. A key focus is the modeling of CCFs, which are crucial for understanding the
reliability of interconnected digital systems. By incorporating probabilistic techniques, the
methodology provides insights into safety margins and system vulnerabilities under different
operational scenarios.

While the report offers methodological advancements, its reliance on assumed data, such as, a
hypothetical beta factor range, limits its applicability for NRC licensing actions, where empirical
data is essential. The use of hypothetical values undermines the reliability of its outcomes in
regulatory contexts. Nevertheless, the structured analysis of CCF methodologies and the
innovative treatment of software reliability modeling provide a foundation for bridging the gap
between academic research and regulatory standards.

In general, both studies highlight several overarching challenges in modeling DI&C systems for
PRA. A primary issue is the lack of comprehensive failure data specific to digital technologies,
which constrains the ability to develop accurate and validated reliability models. Additionally, the
non-physical nature of software failures poses significant difficulties for traditional PRA
frameworks, necessitating innovative modeling techniques. Digital systems also introduce
complex interdependencies between hardware, software, and communication networks, making
it challenging to capture cascading failures accurately. Finally, ensuring the validity of digital risk
models is impeded by the limited operational experience with these systems and the novelty of
their integration into NPPs.

 4 DIGITAL INSTRUMENTATION AND CONTROL FAILURE
 MODE REVIEW

DI&C systems, unlike their analog counterparts, introduce complex failure modes stemming
from software, hardware, and human interactions. These failure modes require updated and
structured taxonomies for effective risk management and system improvement. Currently, there
is no international consensus on a standardized taxonomy for DI&C failure modes, posing
challenges for PRA methodologies. Existing PRA models often employ varied approaches—
ranging from simple functional analyses to detailed component-based models. To ensure
transparent and sound modeling of DI&C failures, PRA techniques need to evolve to account for
these unique characteristics effectively.

This section provides a comprehensive overview of different failure modes in DI&C systems
proposed by various researchers and organizations. These taxonomies are critical for
understanding, categorizing, and addressing the unique failure modes in DI&C systems. The
goal is to synthesize existing taxonomies and insights, presenting a unified understanding of
failure modes specific to DI&C systems in NPPs.

4.1 Nuclear Regulatory Commission Studies on Digital Instrumentation and
 Control Failure Modes

This section reviews major studies conducted or supported by the U.S. NRC to characterize and
address failure modes in DI&C systems.

4.1.1 Interim Staff Guidance on Failure Mode Taxonomy

The Interim Staff Guidance (ISG) taxonomy [16], developed under the NRC’s broader
framework, provides a structured framework for categorizing processor-level failure modes in
DI&C systems. While the draft ISG does not aim to develop a comprehensive methodology for
failure mode identification, it highlights the importance of standardizing failure mode
classifications to support consistent reviews and analyses.

The taxonomy serves as a starting point for a broader understanding of DI&C vulnerabilities,
categorizing failures that can occur at the task and processor/system levels [16]:

• Task-Level Failures
 • Task Crash: The control software task exits unexpectedly.
 • Task Hang: The process goes into an infinite loop.
 • Task Late Response: The output of the task exceeds the specified response time.
 • Task Early Response: The output of the task is too early.
 • Task Incorrect Response: The output of the task is timely but violates specifications.
 • Task No Response: There is no output from the task (but the task is not hung).
• Processor/System-Level Failures

 4-1

 • Processor Crash: The processor software kernel (or operating system) crashes, bringing
 down all tasks running on the operating system.
 • Corrupted Input: The input signal from the plant sensors receives corrupted data due to
 either an analog electrical problem, an analog-to-digital conversion problem, or noise in
 a digital network.
 • Corrupted Output: The output signal to plant actuators is corrupted due to an analog
 electrical problem, a digital-to-analog conversion problem, or noise in a digital network.
 • Out-of-Sequence Data: Data packets arrive at the destination in a different sequence
 than expected (applicable to digital networks using Transmission Control
 Protocol/Internet Protocol and other protocols collectively referred to as “field buses” or
 “data highways”).
The effects of these failure modes can propagate across three levels: (1) at the processor
boundary, failures are confined to the processor, potentially affecting task execution and data
integrity; (2) at the control or safety system level, failures can disrupt the functionality of broader
systems, such as reactor protection or emergency cooling; and (3) at the plant level, failures
may escalate to impact overall plant operations, potentially compromising safety or causing
systemwide disruptions. These classifications are particularly useful for evaluating CCFs, where
shared vulnerabilities, such as algorithmic flaws in application software, can lead to
simultaneous failures across systems. However, timing-related failures like task crashes or
hangs may not propagate uniformly [16].

The ISG taxonomy’s categorization provides a robust foundation for analyzing DI&C PRAs. By
focusing on failure mode identification and the cascading effects of these failures, this taxonomy
enhances the understanding of risks, surpassing the traditional focus on sensitivity analyses
and probabilities. Its integration into PRA methodologies can significantly improve the accuracy
and reliability of risk assessments for DI&C systems.

4.1.2 NUREG/CR-6579

NUREG/CR-6579 [10] provides a comprehensive discussion of the failure modes in DI&C
systems, emphasizing their differences from analog systems and the unique challenges posed
by digital technology. These failure modes are categorized into two main types: those that

In addition to the component-level failures, the report also presents system-level failure modes
derived from operational experience in NPPs and related industries. These failures are
categorized into spurious operations and system malfunctions. These categories include
spurious system actuations, loss of monitoring or control functions, incorrect or incomplete
parameter evaluation, and others. Each category reflects distinct challenges and vulnerabilities
in DI&C systems.

4.1.2.1 Spurious Trips and System Actuations

This category includes events where protection or safety systems actuate without actual need.
These spurious trips were predominantly caused by EMI, which introduced false signals or
caused key components, such as microprocessors, to malfunction. Human interaction errors,
such as operational missteps, were also a significant contributor. Additionally, the lack of
adequate hardware redundancy in some systems led to single points of failure, increasing the
likelihood of spurious system responses.

4.1.2.2 Loss of Monitoring or Control Function

Failures in this category were often due to the unavailability of critical components like
microprocessors or computers. Such losses can pose significant safety risks, particularly under
abnormal or accident conditions. A common cause of these failures was incorrect software
entries by operators, compounded by software configuration and verification errors. The inability
to monitor or control critical parameters can remain unannounced, further exacerbating the risk
during crucial operational scenarios.

4.1.2.3 Parameter Evaluation Errors

The incorrect or incomplete evaluation of system parameters was identified as another
significant failure mode. These errors were caused by software issues, such as incompatibility
or corrupted data, and by human errors, including inadequate procedures or misoperations.
Such failures can result in critical system responses being missed, such as failing to provide a
trip signal when necessary to ensure plant safety.

 4-4

4.1.2.4 Other Failures

Miscellaneous failures included violations of technical specification requirements and
nonconservative setpoint settings. In several instances, human errors were directly responsible
for these events. For example, inappropriate setpoint configurations led to three specific system
failures, illustrating the importance of accurate configuration and strict adherence to operational
protocols.

4.3 Nuclear Energy Agency Failure Mode Taxonomy

The NEA Failure Mode Taxonomy [19] provides a hierarchical framework for classifying and
analyzing failure modes in DI&C systems. Conducted under the Committee on the Safety of
Nuclear Installations, this study aims to improve reliability assessments in PRAs for NPPs. It
highlights hardware and software vulnerabilities at various abstraction levels, providing a
detailed understanding of their origins, interactions, and impacts on system performance.

The taxonomy employs a hierarchical structure with five abstraction levels, capturing failures
from systemwide impacts to basic component behaviors:

• System Level: Examines overarching system failures, such as reactor protection system
 faults.
• Division Level: Considers the physical separation of DI&C systems.

• I&C Unit Level: Focuses on elements performing specific functional roles, such as data
 processing and communication.
• Module Level: Addresses detailed failures in hardware/software modules, like CPUs or I/O
 cards.
• Basic Component Level: Investigates failures at the most granular level, such as sensors or
 microprocessors.

The NEA Failure Mode Taxonomy represents an important step toward standardizing failure
mode classifications for DI&C systems. However, several observations highlight the variability
and challenges in developing a universally applicable taxonomy. Input from participants
revealed inconsistencies in the level of detail and focus. While some participants included failure
effects as part of their failure modes and effects analysis, others did not, reflecting differences in
how failure effects are influenced by system design and vendor-specific fault tolerance features.
These discrepancies emphasize the need for system-specific adaptations of the taxonomy,
particularly as failure modes for protection systems are generally simpler compared to the more
complex functionality and feedback-dependent nature of control systems.

Hardware failure modes received more attention than software failure modes in participant
input, leading to a preliminary software failure mode taxonomy developed during a workshop.
Additionally, CCFs—a critical aspect of DI&C reliability—were inconsistently addressed, with
some organizations treating CCFs as a combination of failure modes rather than a distinct
category. This variability underscores the lack of consensus on how to model CCFs effectively.
Despite these challenges, this taxonomy serves as a valuable framework, though its application
to complex systems such as control systems remains limited by its current scope.

 4-11

The study also raises important questions about the completeness and clarity of the taxonomy.
For instance, the lack of an intermediate level between the division and module levels in the
software taxonomy suggests a need to introduce an I&C unit level for software failure modes,
similar to hardware. Additional concerns include whether failure modes are defined
hierarchically, exhaustively, and analogously across different components, and whether
categories like “malfunction” or “corrective maintenance” should be explicitly defined as failure
modes. Finally, the availability of reliable data to support the taxonomy is a significant limitation,
impacting on its ability to provide exhaustive and unambiguous classifications. These
observations highlight the need for further refinement and development to ensure the
taxonomy’s applicability across diverse DI&C systems in NPPs.

The operational performance and reliability of DI&C systems in NPPs are critical to ensuring
safety and efficiency. To provide DI&C component and system reliability and CCF parameter

primary data sources are reviewed: LERs and INPO IRIS. The combined analysis of these data

Section 3 provided an extensive review of existing studies and industry insights, highlighting the
gaps in empirical data and the need for advanced methodologies to model and mitigate risks.
Section 4 subsequently conducted a comprehensive review of taxonomy of DI&C failure modes,
setting the foundation for categorizing and analyzing real-world operational data. This section
advances those discussions through two targeted studies conducted at INL: the analysis of the
LER and IRIS databases.

The LER and IRIS studies offer complementary perspectives on DI&C reliability. LERs, as
regulatory-driven reports, focus on safety-critical events, providing valuable insights into high-
impact failures and their operational consequences. The IRIS database, on the other hand,
captures routine operational challenges, offering a broader view of system performance under
normal conditions. Together, these datasets enable a holistic analysis of DI&C system reliability,
bridging the gap between theoretical taxonomy and real-world data. By characterizing failure
modes, failure causes, and operational impacts, the LER and IRIS analysis lays the groundwork
for integrating empirical failure data into PRA models, enabling more accurate risk predictions
and supporting risk-informed decision-making.

The objectives of this section are as follows:

• Identify and Analyze Failure Trends: Examine LERs and IRIS data to uncover trends in
 hardware and software-related failures across DI&C systems.
• Characterize Failure Modes and Failure Causes: Extend and validate the taxonomies of
 failure modes discussed in Section 4 with empirical data from operational events.
• Evaluate Operational and Safety Impacts: Assess the implications of DI&C failures on plant
 safety and operations, including reactor trips and system performance disruptions.
• Enhance PRA Methodologies: Provide data-driven insights that improve the accuracy and

 5-1

5.1 Analysis of the Licensee Event Report Database

5.1.1 Introduction

The NRC’s LER database (https://lersearch.inl.gov/LERSearchCriteria.aspx) serves as a
comprehensive repository of operating experience data from NPPs. These reports document
events and conditions that deviate from normal operations, including equipment failures, safety
system malfunctions, and human errors, as required by the NRC Regulations in Title 10 of the
Code of Federal Regulations Section 50.73, “Licensee event report system” [20]. As such, the
LER database is a critical resource for understanding failure modes, identifying failure causes,
and improving risk modeling and system design in the nuclear industry.

DI&C systems, as integral components of modern NPPs, are increasingly featured in LERs due
to their widespread adoption and unique failure characteristics. Unlike traditional analog
systems, DI&C systems exhibit new and complex failures, such as software bugs, configuration
errors, and interactions with cybersecurity measures. These failures not only challenge system
reliability but also impact plant safety and operational efficiency, making their analysis a priority
for industry stakeholders.

The analysis of LER data in this study builds on prior efforts by organizations such as EPRI [11]
and BNL [8], which examined DI&C-related failure events from 2005 to 2016. By focusing on the
period from 2016 (the conclusion of the BNL study) to 2023, this study provides an updated
perspective on DI&C system performance in real-world conditions, reflecting advances in
technology and evolving operational challenges. Preliminary results of this LER analysis were
presented in [21].

5.1.2 Methodology

The analysis of the LER database was conducted in a systematic manner to identify and
characterize failure events related to DI&C systems in NPPs. This study focused on events
reported to the U.S. NRC between 2016 and 2023, leveraging a combination of keyword-based
searches, detailed screening, and structured categorization to ensure the inclusion of relevant
data.

The first step in the methodology involved data collection from the NRC’s publicly accessible
LER Search Tool. Specific keywords, including “digital,” “software,” “computer,”
“microprocessor,” “CPU,” “memory,” “PLC,” “FPGA,” “program,” and “digital feedwater control,”
were used to capture a broad spectrum of reports potentially related to DI&C failures. This
keyword-based search yielded an initial set of 206 LERs.

To refine the dataset, a rigorous screening process was applied. Each LER identified in the
initial search was manually reviewed to confirm its relevance to DI&C systems. Reports that did
not pertain to DI&C systems, such as those focused solely on mechanical or analog
components, were excluded. Ultimately, 43 LERs were verified as DI&C-related and included in
the analysis. A list of these LERs, including the plant name, LER number, and event date, is
provided in Appendix A.

 5-2

The verified LERs were analyzed to classify failure events and extract meaningful insights. Each
failure was categorized as either hardware- or software-related based on the nature of the
system or component that failed. Hardware-related failures included component defects, aging,
and environmental factors, while software-related failures encompassed design and logic errors,
configuration issues, and procedural deficiencies. For each failure event, specific DI&C systems
or components involved—such as the digital electrohydraulic control system, feedwater control
system, or core monitoring system—were identified and documented.

The broader implications of the failures were also assessed. This included an evaluation of the
operational impact, such as reactor trips or other significant disruptions to plant operations. The
failure causes were systematically identified and grouped into categories, including design
errors, human factors, and environmental factors.

To provide additional context, operational data were analyzed to determine the plant’s mode at
the time of the failure (e.g., power operation, cold shutdown) and the method by which the
failure was detected (e.g., during normal operation, testing, or calibration activities). These
factors were examined to understand the circumstances under which DI&C failures most
commonly occur and how they are identified.

Both quantitative and qualitative analytical methods were employed. Statistical trends were
analyzed to assess the frequency of hardware versus software failures, track changes in failure
rates over time, and identify recurring failure causes. Qualitative analyses were conducted to
interpret patterns in failure modes and their operational impacts, providing insights into systemic
vulnerabilities and areas for improvement.

While the methodology was designed to be comprehensive, certain limitations were noted. The
reliance on keyword-based searches may have excluded relevant reports that did not explicitly
include the selected terms. Additionally, variability in the completeness and detail of LER
documentation posed challenges for consistent data extraction. Despite these limitations, the
methodology provides a robust framework for examining DI&C-related failures and offers critical
insights to improve the reliability and safety of these systems in NPPs.

The review of the LERs from 2016 to 2023 identified failure characteristics and operational
impacts of DI&C systems in U.S. NPPs. A total of 1,580 LERs were reported during this period,
of which 206 were initially screened as potentially related to DI&C failures through keyword-
based search. After manual verification, 43 events were confirmed as DI&C-related. While the
current report does not perform a statistical analysis to determine the significance of trends in
the number and percentage of digital I&C LERs each year, this could be considered for future
studies.

5.1.3.3 Digital Instrumentation and Control Failure Mode and Cause

The analysis of the confirmed DI&C-related LERs identified distinct failure modes and causes
for hardware- and software-related failures, reflecting the complexity and uniqueness of DI&C
systems compared to traditional analog systems.

The failure modes observed in DI&C systems predominantly included “fail to operate” or “loss of
function” and “output errors.”

• Fail to Operate/Loss of Function: This mode represented cases where components, such as
 processors, memory modules, or sensors, ceased functioning entirely. These failures
 resulted in a complete loss of system capability and often had immediate and significant
 impacts on plant operations.
• Output Errors: In this mode, components or software generated incorrect or unstable
 signals, disrupting downstream systems and processes. Such failures were particularly
 challenging as they introduced erroneous data into control systems, potentially leading to
 misdiagnosis or delayed corrective actions.

The analysis of LERs from 2016 to 2023 highlights the contribution of DI&C-related failures to

5.3 Implications for Risk Assessment and Probabilistic Risk Assessment
 Models

5.3.1 Introduction

The findings from the LER and IRIS analyses can be used to inform PRA methodologies and
advance the reliability of DI&C systems in NPPs. The preliminarily identified 636 potential DI&C-
related events were subjected to structured categorization, grouping failures by system,
component, failure mode, and failure cause. The same analytical framework employed in the
LER study was applied to analyze these events, enabling direct comparability between the two
datasets. Both quantitative and qualitative methods were used to extract insights into trends,
recurring failure patterns, and systemic vulnerabilities. While the LER analysis focused on
regulatory-significant events, the IRIS analysis provides a broader operational context, offering
insights into routine challenges and potential precursors to major failures.

5.3.2 Empirical Trends and Implications for Probabilistic Risk Assessment Models

The analysis of both datasets revealed distinct trends in hardware and software failures over
time. Hardware failures consistently outnumbered software failures, with dominant failure modes
including fail to operate, loss of communication, and lockup. Software failures, though fewer,
often introduce complexities due to configuration errors and programming faults.

For PRA models, these empirical trends provide updated failure probabilities for both hardware
and software components. Specifically:

• Time-Dependent Reliability: The observed hardware trends, particularly aging-related
 failures, emphasize the need for time-dependent models that account for degradation over
 the operational lifecycle of DI&C systems. Dynamic reliability models can incorporate the
 observed aging failure rates to better predict future vulnerabilities.
• Failure Probabilities for Software: While software failures lack clear aging mechanisms, the
 variability and recurring issues (e.g., configuration errors) indicate the importance of
 considering HMIs and software testing coverage in PRA scenarios.
By using this empirical data, PRA models can more accurately reflect real-world reliability
challenges, enabling plant operators to prioritize components with the highest failure risks.

5.3.3 Modeling Interdependencies Between Hardware and Software

The presence of combined hardware-software failures, although less frequent, highlights the
interdependence inherent in modern DI&C systems. Events where hardware failures triggered
software disruptions (and vice versa) demonstrate the need to model system-level
interdependencies in PRA methodologies. Traditional PRA approaches, which often treat
hardware and software failures independently, may underestimate the risk of cascading failures.

 5-21

To address this, the following improvements can be incorporated:

• Coupled Failure Models: Develop coupled failure modes for critical systems where hardware
 degradation or faults may propagate to software logic and lead to system-level disruptions.
• Cascading Effects: Integrate models that assess how single-point failures, such as CPU
 lockup or communication module faults, can trigger downstream failures in interconnected
 subsystems.
These refinements help ensure that PRA models capture the complexities of digital systems and
their reliance on integrated hardware and software components. Alternate approaches could
also be considered, such as using surrogate events to bound the impact of individual or
common-cause failures of DI&C systems, whether hardware or software, on plant equipment,
functions, and operator actions modeled in the PRA.

5.3.4 Enhancing Probabilistic Risk Assessment for System-Specific Risk Scenarios

The system-level analysis in both LER and IRIS studies identified key systems frequently
affected by DI&C failures, such as the radiation monitoring system, digital turbine control
system, and core monitoring system. Although most of those systems are associated with non-
PRA systems and could be excluded from future DI&C risk, they are retained in this section’s
general analysis to provide a more complete understanding of the broader risk landscape.
Furthermore, they play critical roles in plant safety and operational stability. Their vulnerability to
failures necessitates system-specific PRA enhancements that:

• Prioritize high-risk systems where failures can lead to reactor trips or safety parameter
 degradation.
• Develop system-specific fault trees and reliability models that reflect dominant failure modes
 observed in empirical data (e.g., fail to operate, loss of communication).
• Incorporate operational mode dependencies: While failures were predominantly observed
 during power operation, likely due to the high capacity factors, it is important not to neglect
 the possibility of failures occurring during other plant operating modes (e.g., startup,
 shutdown).
This system-specific approach ensures PRA models can identify and mitigate the most
significant risks, improving plant safety margins.

5.3.5 Addressing Data Gaps for Probabilistic Risk Assessment Integration

A critical limitation observed in the IRIS analysis was the large proportion of failures classified
as unknown causes (88.5% for hardware and 61.2% for software). While the LER database
provides more detailed failure cause information, the absence of precise diagnostic data in IRIS
limits the ability to fully quantify failure mechanisms in PRA models. To address these data
gaps:

• Improved failure documentation and reporting practices must be encouraged to reduce
 “unknown causes” and enhance the granularity of failure data.
• Integrate advanced diagnostics and condition monitoring systems to identify failure causes
 of hardware and software failures more effectively.

 5-22

• Consider adopting advanced techniques, including an integrated DI&C data lake and
 validated digital twin capabilities, to aggregate and harmonize fragmented operating
 experience data, enable model calibration, and generate synthetic failure scenarios that
 strengthen reliability and risk modeling.
• Leverage machine learning and artificial intelligence-based analytics to extract meaningful
 patterns from incomplete or ambiguous data, supporting more accurate failure modeling.
Closing these gaps will improve the robustness and applicability of PRA models, ensuring that
risk assessments are grounded in comprehensive and reliable operational experience.

5.3.6 Considerations for an Integrated DI&C Probabilistic Risk Assessment

Although the objective of this study is to evaluate the feasibility of estimating DI&C component
reliability parameters—including CCF parameters—using existing operating experience data,
rather than to develop a new methodology or framework for an integrated DI&C PRA, several
valuable insights were provided by the technical reviewers of this report. These considerations
are captured below to inform future studies on integrated DI&C PRA development.

(1) Graded Modeling Approach

 To manage the size and complexity of a PRA that incorporates DI&C systems, a graded
 approach could be adopted such that the level of modeling detail is commensurate with the
 scope and importance of the application or function being assessed. For example, when
 evaluating the impact of the Reactor Protection System (RPS) on a specific accident
 sequence or overall plant risk, the RPS model would need to be more detailed than other
 less risk-significant systems.

(2) Explicit Modeling Elements in DI&C PRA

 A DI&C PRA model should explicitly address the following:

 a. DI&C CCFs – NRC guidance on defense-in-depth and diversity requirements
 provides key protection against CCF in DI&C systems. The impact of compliance
 with these requirements on DI&C CCF modeling should be evaluated. For example,
 can the design attributes (e.g., diversity) justify screening out specific CCF scenarios
 from further consideration?

 b. DI&C Equipment Unavailability – The unavailability of DI&C system equipment
 should be explicitly modeled, as it may significantly contribute to overall system
 failure probabilities.

 c. Human Actions – DI&C related human actions, particularly those that are safety- or
 risk-significant, should be incorporated into DI&C PRAs.

(3) Modeling of Fault Tolerance Features

 DI&C systems often incorporate self-monitoring algorithms and fault-tolerant features that
 detect and recover from certain faults before they lead to system failure. Consideration
 should be given to explicitly modeling these features in DI&C PRA. Faults may need to be
 categorized as recoverable or non-recoverable to appropriately reflect system behavior.

 5-23

(4) Function integration

 Software and hardware provide the capability to integrate several different functions within a
 DI&C system (e.g., integration of control and actuation functions of different DI&C systems),
 each with a specific impact on plant risk. Therefore, PRA models of integrated DI&C
 systems should be decomposed to each function with the associated impact on accident
 sequences and plant risk.

(5) Uncertainty Characterization

 Sources of uncertainty specific to DI&C systems should be identified and quantified for
 incorporation into DI&C PRA models. The uncertainty distributions of the plant risk resulting
 from PRAs with and without DI&C systems should be determined. Application-specific
 sensitivity analyses should also be performed.

(6) Development of DI&C PRA Guidance

 Enhanced guidance for DI&C PRA should be developed to address the challenges such as
 those described above. This effort may benefit from pilot applications to demonstrate
 practical modeling approaches and identify best practices.

It is important to note that hardware random failure rates can be statistically estimated, as
demonstrated above, whereas software does not fail from wear-out and thus cannot be
assigned a meaningful failure rate in the same way. Software reliability issues stem from design
defects (e.g., bugs) that remain latent until triggered. Therefore, while hardware might have a
mean time between failures, software reliability is typically addressed through rigorous
validation rather than a numeric failure rate [29].

In analog systems, CCFs were often attributed to physical phenomena like corrosion or aging
affecting multiple parts simultaneously. Other reasons included environmental factors, design
flaws, manufacturing defects, and improper maintenance practices. In digital systems, the
predominant CCF concern is systematic faults, especially software bugs or logic design errors
that exist in all redundant channels and can be triggered under certain conditions [29]. In other
words, if two safety processors are running the same flawed code, a single triggering event
could make both fail in the same way at the same time, defeating redundancy. CCFs are
significant for software-based systems because any design fault is common to all copies of the
software. If the same software runs on redundant controllers, a single bug could simultaneously
crash both. The IAEA notes that identical software in redundant trains will produce identical
failures when a latent bug is triggered [29]. Therefore, nuclear safety guidelines often require
either diverse software implementations or extensive analysis to ensure a postulated software
bug cannot disable all redundant safety channels.

The EPRI white paper [30] delves into the complexities of failure in DI&C systems, making a
critical distinction between random failures and systematic failures. Random failures occur at
unpredictable times and can be statistically assessed, while systematic failures stem from
underlying deterministic errors and have a failure probability of 1.0 if the triggering cause
occurs. The engineering community generally agrees that the probability of systematic errors
cannot be quantified, and applying generic data in these cases is inappropriate, as such errors
are design-specific. In contrast, the PRA community categorizes failures by their effects on
model logic, and the likelihood of failure can be estimated through data, models, expert

 6-6

judgment, or uncertainty treatments, without making as sharp a distinction between random and
systematic failures. The paper underscores that both types of failures can result in multiple
component failures.

The concept of CCFs and dependencies could be a source of misunderstanding between
engineering and risk communities. Broadly, CCFs refer to failures of multiple items of equipment
caused by a shared, common cause. However, in PRA community, CCFs are more specifically
defined as failures of two or more components occurring within a short period due to a single
common cause. These underlying causes are often not explicitly modeled and are treated
parametrically, typically involving failures of identical components under similar operating
conditions. While digital systems may increase the potential for dependencies, the fundamental
types of dependencies and their modeling in PRA remain similar to those for analog systems,
with dependencies often modeled explicitly in PRA.

To tackle the challenges of modeling failures in digital systems, particularly CCFs, the white
paper introduces a new approach based on the EPRI Digital Systems Engineering Framework.
This approach begins by confirming the Control Effectiveness Profile, which provides plant-
specific justification that systematic errors have been properly addressed. Once this is
established, identified dependencies are explicitly modeled in the PRA, while remaining CCFs
are modeled using a data-driven best-estimate CCF factor. In the near term, assuming the EPRI
framework is applied, a global CCF factor of 0.05 is proposed, based on pooled data from
multiple sources, to represent the fraction of digital component failures that could lead to CCFs.
This method aims to offer a more realistic representation of digital systems in PRA and enhance
risk-informed decision-making. EPRI is also working on the expansion of this white paper to
include additional methodologies and case studies to further validate and refine the proposed
approach.

As discussed in Section 3.4.2, the LWRS DI&C report titled “Quantitative Risk Analysis of High
Safety-Significant Safety-Related Digital Instrumentation and Control Systems in Nuclear Power
Plants Using IRADIC Technology” [15] introduces approaches, such as the Bayesian and HRA-
Aided Method for the Reliability Analysis of Software (BAHAMAS) methodology, to estimate
failure probabilities and CCF parameters for DI&C components. For quantification of CCF, the
modified beta factor model was used, which accounts for multiple common-cause component
groups (CCCGs) and employs Partial Beta Factor method [31] to define beta factors for each
CCCG. In the case study for the research, Table 6 of [14] presents the DI&C hardware failure
probabilities based on [32], while the total software failure probability was quantified using the
BAHAMAS methodology with a value of 1.87E-4. The hardware and software beta factors for
components of reactor trip systems, including bistable processors (BPs), logic processors
(LPs), digital output modules, selective processors, undervoltage trip devices, shunt trip
devices, and reactor trip breakers, are provided in Tables 11 and 12 of [15]. Specifically, the
beta factors for BPs and LPs (hardware and software) are 0.1694 and 0.2132, respectively.
More detailed data and results of the case study can be found in the referenced report.

6.1.2 DI&C Reliability Data in the Aerospace Industry

According to the report titled Assessment of Technical Feasibility of Risk-Informed Approaches
and Gaps Associated with Further Integrating Risk Insights into Regulatory Reviews for DI&C
Systems and Components [33], the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA)
employs both industry generic data and NASA-specific data for risk assessment and reliability
evaluation. NASA's data collection systems encompass a wide array of sources to capture and
report incidents, anomalies, and system failures.

 6-7

These systems include the NASA Incident Reporting System, which incorporates the Problem
Reporting and Corrective Action system, and various center-specific problem reporting systems
designed to record both pre-operational and operational anomalies. Additionally, the Spacecraft
On-Orbit Anomaly Reporting System, the Problem Report/Problem Failure Report system, and
incident, surprise, and anomaly reports contribute to NASA's extensive data collection
framework. Further, PRA and reliability analysis archives, such as those from the Shuttle and
International Space Station missions, Apollo Mission Reports, the Mars Exploration Rover
Problem Tracking Database, and results from NASA expert elicitation, provide a comprehensive
repository of reliability data [33].

In addressing human error probabilities associated with DI&C systems, NASA utilizes a variety
of Human Reliability Analysis (HRA) models and quantification techniques, including the
Technique for Human Error Rate Prediction. These models are critical for evaluating human
interactions with DI&C systems, especially during manual actuation of diverse systems [34].

Regarding CCFs in DI&C systems, NASA's methodology aligns closely with approaches utilized
by the U.S. NRC. Although specific databases for CCF contributions in DI&C systems were not
identified, NASA acknowledges the predominance of design and specification errors in software
failures, indicating a high dependency of these failures. To mitigate risks associated with
software CCFs, NASA relies on diverse manual actions and, when feasible, abort systems to
enhance overall system reliability and safety [33].

Similarly, the aviation industry also prioritizes the collection and analysis of failure data to
ensure the reliability and safety of its DI&C systems. As reported in a study conducted by ORNL
[28], the Aviation Safety Information Analysis and Sharing (ASIAS) System integrates, analyzes,
and shares aviation safety data. ASIAS enables users to perform integrated queries across
multiple databases, search for an extensive warehouse of safety data, and display pertinent
elements in various useful formats. The Accident/Incident Data System (AIDS) database, part of
ASIAS, contains records for general aviation and commercial air carrier incidents since 1978,
using the National Transportation Safety Board accident database as the primary source for
accident information.

The ORNL study conducted a search of the AIDS database for digital-instrumentation-related
incidents, identifying 67 computer-related incidents out of a total of 86,682 reports. Based on
these 67 failures and the aggregated time in service (~3.36 × 108 hours) from 1980 to 2004, the
study estimated the computer failure rate for the commercial aviation industry to be
approximately 2.00 × 10-7 per hour. Given that the reporting system is voluntary, the actual
number of incidents is likely higher, making this failure rate a lower bound estimate [28].

6.1.4 DI&C Reliability Data in the Chemical Industry

The chemical industry, both in the United States and internationally, widely employs DI&C
systems for various applications. These facilities often utilize similar components and systems,
such as evaporators, chemical reactors, and heat exchangers, which are governed by DI&C
systems. Failure data and rate estimates are typically made at the system or function level,
allowing for simpler risk models. Many chemical facilities adhere to IEC 61508 standards [36] for
DI&C systems, utilizing the reliability data provided by these standards [33].

The chemical industry benefits from extensive service data on hardware failures, maintenance
errors, and pilot errors, supported by detailed accident databases such as those maintained by
the Chemical Safety Board and the industry-specific databases like the Process Equipment

 6-10

Reliability Database. This wealth of data includes industry-wide generic reliability and CCF data
for Safety Integrity Level (SIL) certified hardware and software [33].

To address CCFs, methods like Layers of Protection Analysis and other qualitative techniques
are employed, considering Independent Protection Layers. Programmable controllers are
particularly vulnerable to CCFs due to their multiple control tasks residing in the same
equipment. To mitigate these risks, diverse software, hardware, and interfaces are used,
ensuring functional separation to prevent process control failures from affecting safety functions.
Additionally, fault-tolerant architectures and diagnostics that switch operations to backup
equipment are employed to enhance system reliability. For estimating and evaluating CCFs, the
chemical sector applies methods described in Annex D of IEC 61508-6 [36] to estimate the beta
factor. Case-specific CCF probabilities are estimated using risk reduction factors (RRFs). IEC
61511-1 [37] includes evaluation of common causes for Safety Critical Automation and
Instrumentation layers, requiring quantitative estimation of systematic contributions to CCFs
when RRF requirements exceed 10,000 [33].

IEC 61508 Part 6 Annex D provides a structured methodology for quantifying the impact of
hardware-related CCFs in E/E/PE systems, particularly in those employing redundancy for
safety. The methodology is critical in ensuring the safety integrity of such systems by identifying
and estimating the effects of failures that could cause simultaneous malfunctions across
redundant elements.

The primary tool introduced in IEC 61508 Part 6 Annex D is the beta factor methodology, which
estimates the proportion of random hardware failures in one channel that could result in the
simultaneous failure of all redundant channels. Two distinct types of beta factors are described:

1. β: Represents the fraction of undetected dangerous failures attributable to common causes
 that affect all redundant channels simultaneously.
2. βD: Refers to the equivalent fraction for detectable dangerous failures. It is typically lower
 than β due to diagnostic measures that may reveal non-simultaneous CCFs before they
 impact all safety functions.
IEC 61508 Part 6 Annex D emphasizes the importance of design measures and operational
strategies to mitigate CCFs. Key recommendations include:

• Physical separation: Keeping redundant components physically separated to reduce the
 likelihood of simultaneous failures.
• Design diversity: Incorporating diverse designs for redundant systems to ensure that a
 single common cause is less likely to impact all components.
The methodology also proposes a scoring system to assess the effectiveness of measures that
reduce CCFs, which considers factors such as logic subsystems and final elements. This
scoring, combined with diagnostic test intervals and coverage, helps to calculate intermediate
failure factors, such as βint and βDint. The methodology offers multiplication factors to adjust βint
and βDint based on the system’s redundancy level. This allows practitioners to estimate the final
beta factor for use in reliability analyses, which is crucial for meeting functional safety
requirements.

While IEC 61508 Part 6 provides a valuable framework for addressing hardware-related CCFs,
it relies on engineering judgment due to the limited availability of field data. Additionally, it does
not explicitly cover software-related CCFs. Nonetheless, it remains an essential tool for
practitioners aiming to account for the impact of hardware-related CCFs in their functional safety
assessments under the IEC standard.

6.1.7 Nordic Studies

Estimating failure probabilities for DI&C systems, particularly software, presents significant
challenges due to the lack of sufficient relevant failure data. This data scarcity often leads to
reliance on expert judgment and operational experience, which are used to derive probability
estimates for PRA. In the absence of specific data, conservative generic values—such as 1E-4
per demand—are sometimes employed as screening criteria or as lower bounds for reliability
claims, especially in the context of software-related CCFs [40].

The report titled Modelling of Digital I&C - Interim report 2015 [41] outlines the Nordic approach
to software reliability analysis, which aims to go beyond generic values. This approach involves
dividing application software into modules and considering different failure modes: fatal and
non-fatal failures. Non-fatal failure probability is quantified using a Bayesian Belief Network
(BBN) approach, which considers factors such as software complexity and the level of
verification and validation (V&V). For fatal failures, operational experience is used to derive
probability estimates. For hardware failure probabilities, while established reliability analysis
principles are applicable, the lack of specific failure data for DI&C components often leads to the
use of generic parameters or expert judgment, potentially resulting in conservative estimates.

Regarding CCFs, which are a crucial aspect of DI&C reliability analysis, there is a general
understanding that CCFs between identical redundant software modules are often modeled
assuming complete dependence, represented by a beta factor of 1.0 [40]. However, when
diversity is introduced, such as through non-identical software or deployment across different
systems, modeling CCFs becomes more complex. While a beta factor of 1.0 may be overly
conservative in these cases, estimating appropriate lower values remains challenging due to the
data limitations. A simple beta factor method was proposed to account for CCF between
application software modules, based on the similarity of inputs and functions [41]. This method
suggests that if the inputs and functions of application software modules are identical, the beta
factor should be assumed to be 1.0. If the modules are similar but not identical (based on
reasonably similar logic), a rough estimate of the beta factor can be derived, with the
assumption that a reduced beta factor should be applied if functions or inputs are not identical.

For hardware CCFs in DI&C, standard CCF analysis principles and models can be applied.
However, the lack of specific operating data often necessitates the use of generic parameters or
engineering judgment-based methods, such as those in IEC 61508 [36], which can lead to
conservative outcomes. The modeling of large and asymmetric CCF groups in DI&C hardware
remains a particularly challenging area.

While structured approaches like the Nordic method provide improvements in software reliability
analysis, and the beta factor model is commonly used for CCFs, continued data collection,
international collaboration, and the development of more refined modeling methodologies are
necessary to enhance the accuracy and realism of PRA for NPPs employing digital systems.

In conclusion, this section highlights the complexities and challenges in assessing the reliability
and safety of DI&C systems, particularly regarding estimating failure probabilities and CCFs. It
is evident that while there are established approaches to quantifying these failures, the lack of
sufficient relevant data continues to be a significant barrier. The section also emphasizes the
importance of operational experience data and realistic risk assessment models to establish
achievable reliability goals, acknowledging the need for continued development in data
collection, international collaboration, and refined modeling practices to enhance the safety and
dependability of DI&C systems in critical applications.

6.2 Incorporation of Digital Instrumentation and Control Failures into IDCCS

The expected development of a DI&C failure database will necessitate a revision of the existing
IDCCS system. The revision would include adding a DI&C study that is similar to, but distinct
from, the current failure study and CCF study. A standardized approach to coding DI&C events
is essential for establishing a reliable and comprehensive database. Such a framework would
enable systematic event documentation and facilitate data-driven analyses. The following are
preliminary considerations on the coding structure:

• Event Identification and Categorization
 Each DI&C event must be assigned a unique identifier for traceability, including the
 following primary attributes:
 • Event Date: Timestamp for the occurrence.
 • Plant Identifier: A code representing the NPP where the event occurred.
 • System/Component: The specific DI&C system and component involved.
 • Impacted System/Component: The system and component that are impacted by the
 DI&C failure. These are usually key components such as valves and pumps that are
 modeled in PRA.
• Failure Mode Classification
 • Failure Mode for DI&C Component: The DI&C failure mode taxonomies described in
 Section 4 should be considered along with the INL study in Section 5 and the general
 practice in PRA to provide reasonable failure mode values proper for DI&C.
 • Failure Mode for Impacted Component: This would be similar to existing hardware failure
 modes, such as valves failing to open/close or opening/closing spuriously.
• Failure Cause Classification
 Existing failure cause categories (i.e., component related, design related, environment
 related, human related, and others/unknown) for hardware should be considered along
 with the DI&C specific failure causes, for example,
 • Hardware Related: Aging, design deficiencies, installation errors.
 • Software Related: Configuration/setpoint errors, programming flaws, logic errors.
 • Human Related: Procedural mistakes, insufficient training.
 • Environmental: EMI interference, temperature extremes.
• Data Entry Protocols
 • Standardize data entry fields for consistent documentation, including free-text
 descriptions for context and predefined drop-down options for key attributes.
 • Ensure compatibility with existing databases (e.g., IDCCS) to enable data integration
 and analysis.
 • Implement periodic audits to verify the quality and integrity of the database.
6.3 Estimating Digital Instrumentation and Control Reliability Using Operating
 Experience

As part of the enhancements in the area of DI&C, the NRC web-based application for PRA
calculations, RADS, would be revised to estimate DI&C component reliability using the

 6-15

operating experience data in IDCCS and IRIS. Specifically, RADS needs to identify the device
identifications for the components within the DI&C systems and collect the operating experience
data of these DI&C components, including operating times, demands, and failures. If such data
are available in IDCCS or IRIS and can be characterized with the coding considerations in
Section 6.1, we will be able to estimate the DI&C reliability similarly to how we estimate the
reliability of hardware such as valves, pumps, and diesel generators. However, the current IRIS
database architecture is primarily designed for hardware components such as pumps, valves,
and generators, providing demand and run-hour data for these key elements. Although IRIS
incorporates digital failure causes within its Cause Groups—facilitating searches for digital-
related failures—it does not explicitly associate device identifiers with software, nor does it
capture demand or run-hour data for software components. A more robust approach would
involve enhancing IRIS to include device identification for DI&C components and enabling
plants to report corresponding demand and run-hour data. Without such enhancements,
analyses would rely on the demand and run-hour data of hardware components linked to DI&C
and software failures. This workaround, however, would yield an incomplete representation of
DI&C and software reliability, as components without recorded failures would remain
unaccounted for.

For DI&C CCF, we should first distinguish between the two different types of CCF:

• DI&C Internal CCF: CCFs occurred in redundant trains of a DI&C system, e.g., redundant
 software that fulfills the same function.
• DI&C External CCF: CCFs caused by the DI&C failure that impacts multiple components,
 potentially across multiple systems, for the same or different functions.
Note that the DI&C external CCF defined above does not correspond to the traditional definition
of CCF (i.e., the simultaneous failure of redundant components due to a shared cause). Rather,
it refers to intrinsic dependencies that are typically modeled explicitly within the system logic
model. However, this type of dependency is sometimes perceived as the CCF of concern when
applying DI&C technology. To distinguish it from DI&C internal CCF—where redundant DI&C
trains or components fail simultaneously due to the same cause—we used the term “DI&C
external CCF.”

The regulatory requirements for redundant and diverse DI&C system design and
implementation are primarily aimed at mitigating the potential risks of multiple component or
function failures due to a single DI&C failure, or the DI&C external CCF risk. The treatment of
this kind of CCFs would differ from the classical CCF parametric modeling approach for
hardware. The functional dependencies among a DI&C system and its impacting systems
should be explicitly modeled in PRA for DI&C risk assessment.

 7 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

This report evaluates the feasibility of estimating DI&C component and system reliability
parameters—including CCF parameters—using existing operating experience data from the
INPO IRIS database and LERs. It also analyzes DI&C system performance in NPPs, focusing
on unique failure modes, CCFs, and their implications for plant safety and reliability. Initially, the
report provides an overview of the evolution from analog to digital I&C systems, highlighting the
primary functions and architecture of these systems. It emphasizes the enhanced functionality,
precision, and adaptability of DI&C systems, and discusses their components and operational
principles.

Following this, the report reviews key studies conducted by leading organizations such as the
NRC, national laboratories, EPRI, and European entities. These studies contribute to
understanding DI&C reliability, safety, and risk assessment methodologies by identifying gaps in
research and opportunities for future development. The report then examines the complex
failure modes introduced by DI&C systems, including hardware, software, and human
interactions. It reviews various taxonomies proposed by researchers and organizations,
emphasizing the need for a standardized approach to effectively manage and model these
failures in PRA methodologies.

The analysis of operating experience data from LER and INPO IRIS databases provides
insights into DI&C failures, characterizes failure modes, and assesses operational and safety
impacts. This analysis lays the groundwork for enhancing PRA methodologies. In addition to the
data analysis, this study also provides a review of key studies related to the reliability data of
DI&C systems. Finally, the report outlines a plan for incorporating DI&C failure data into the
existing NRC IDCCS and updating the NRC RADS web-based application. It provides
recommendations on how to model DI&C reliability and CCF in PRA for risk assessment.

The findings from this report underscore the significance of understanding and addressing the
unique challenges posed by DI&C systems in NPPs. The transition from analog to digital
controls introduces complexity and unique failure modes that necessitate tailored reliability and
risk assessment methodologies. While DI&C systems offer enhanced precision and operational
monitoring, they are also susceptible to software reliability issues, hardware-software
interdependence, and CCFs.

Real-world event data from LERs and IRIS databases provide valuable insights into DI&C
system failures, identifying trends, failure modes, and CCFs that inform risk mitigation
strategies. Establishing standardized taxonomies and classifications for DI&C failures is crucial
for consistent documentation and analysis. Standardization facilitates better integration of DI&C
failure data into existing NRC databases and improves the accuracy of reliability estimates.

Given the rapid pace of technological innovation, continuous evaluation and adaptation of
reliability and risk assessment methodologies are vital. Ongoing research and collaboration
among regulatory bodies, industry organizations, and research institutions are key to advancing
DI&C system reliability. Incorporating DI&C reliability into PRA models and developing
structured plans for documenting DI&C-related events are essential for risk-informed decision-
making. These efforts support the design, implementation, maintenance, and risk mitigation of
DI&C systems in NPPs.
