Posterolateral Portal Use During Hip Arthroscopy for Resection of Posterior Hip Cysts
Authors: Peng Chen, Yueyang Tian, Jiang Guo, Peng Zhang, Jingtao Huang, Hongxin Zhuo, Xintao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHip arthroscopy is a widely accepted, minimally invasive technique for treating various hip joint disorders. The traditional approach typically involves the supine position with traction and an anterolateral entry point. However, this approach presents challenges, including insufficient exposure of posterior hip lesions and an increased risk of nerve injury. In this technical note, we describe a posterolateral portal in the lateral decubitus position, eliminating the need for traction and fluoroscopy. This technique provides direct access to posterior hip lesions and facilitates effective resection with minimal muscle damage. Additionally, it avoids interference with anterior structures and circumvents potential complications associated with traction and fluoroscopy. This approach offers a promising alternative for arthroscopic management of posterior hip pathologies.

Posterolateral portal use during hip arthroscopy for resection of posterior hip cysts. Following subarachnoid anesthesia, the patient was placed in the lateral decubitus position with the affected side up. Standard sterile preparation and draping of the hip and ipsilateral lower extremity were performed without traction. Preoperatively, the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) and the apex of the greater trochanter were marked. A line connecting these two landmarks was extended distally. The intersection of this line with the posterior cortical extension of the femoral shaft was designated as the site of the first arthroscopic portal. A second portal was established approximately 4 cm proximal to the first portal. After incision of the skin and fascia, blunt dissection was performed along the surface of the iliotibial tract using a periosteal elevator to create the arthroscopic working space. Arthroscopically, soft tissue debris within the cavity was initially debrided. After identifying the bony prominence of the greater trochanter using radiofrequency ablation, the gluteus maximus muscle was split along the direction of its fibers slightly posterior and proximal to the greater trochanter. The deep layer of the gluteus maximus was then incised to expose the gluteus medius muscle. The gluteus medius was longitudinally split approximately 3 cm. Access to the deep layer was achieved through the interval anterior to the piriformis tendon and posterior to the gluteus minimus, allowing palpation of the posterior hip joint capsule. The capsule was incised and explored posterior to the acetabulum. Exploration along the acetabular direction led to the identification of the cyst. After puncturing the cyst wall, cystic fluid extravasation was observed, confirming the cyst's location at the posteromedial aspect of the hip joint. The cyst was completely resected using a shaver. Hemostasis was achieved intraoperatively using a radiofrequency probe. Due to the limited capsular incision, suture repair of the capsule was not performed. Finally, the skin incision was sutured. Hip joint cysts are relatively rare and typically originate from the synovium, labrum, or bone. Although these cysts are often asymptomatic in their early stages, they may cause local pain, stiffness, and reduced range of motion as they enlarge or exert pressure on surrounding structures. In severe cases, these cysts can lead to secondary joint instability or impingement, ultimately compromising hip function. 1 , 2 Ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are effective diagnostic tools for identifying these lesions. 3 , 4

Surgical intervention is often required for symptomatic hip cysts or those affecting joint mechanics. With the advancements in hip arthroscopy, this technique has become a minimally invasive and effective treatment option. Hip arthroscopy is increasingly favored by patients and physicians due to its advantages, including minimal invasiveness, reduced postoperative pain, and faster recovery. 5

When treating posterior hip lesions, most surgeons use the traditional supine position with traction, which combines an anterolateral portal with a posterolateral portal to access the posterior hip. However, this method cannot entirely avoid complications such as nerve injury from traction and blind spots inherent to posteromedial hip surgery. 6

In this technical note, we describe a modified approach using the posterolateral portal in the lateral decubitus position, without traction or fluoroscopy, for resecting posterior hip cysts (Video 1). This technique enables effective removal of posterior hip lesions and minimizes joint damage, preserving hip function and promoting long‐term recovery.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Positioning

The procedure is performed under general or spinal anesthesia with the patient in the lateral decubitus position, with the affected side facing upward. Traction is not applied after sterilizing the hips and lower limbs.

Portals

The anterior superior iliac spine and the apex of the greater trochanter are palpated and marked. A line is drawn connecting the 2 landmarks. The first arthroscopic portal is established at the intersection of this line with the posterior cortical extension of the femoral shaft. A second portal is created approximately 4 cm proximal to the first portal (Figure 1).

SURGICAL PROCEDURE

Each incision is approximately 1 cm in length. After making skin and fascia incisions, a periosteal elevator is used to perform blunt dissection along the iliotibial band, creating an artificial working space for the arthroscope. An arthroscope and radiofrequency probe are introduced into the working space. Initial debridement is cleared from the cystic cavity. The apex of the greater trochanter is identified using a probe under arthroscopic guidance. Using a radiofrequency device, the gluteus maximus and deep gluteus medius are incised longitudinally along the direction of the muscle fibers, posterior and proximal to the greater trochanter apex. The incision is approximately 3 cm in length. Care is taken to minimize muscle fiber disruption and ensure hemostasis.

The piriformis tendon is located deep within the gluteus medius. Access to the posterior hip joint capsule is achieved by advancing through the interval between the anterior aspect of the piriformis tendon and gluteus minimus (Figure 2). A limited incision is made in the joint capsule along the acetabular rim, allowing direct visualization of the posterior and posteromedial hip joints. Further exploration of the posteromedial region is performed by bypassing the piriformis tendon if necessary (Figure 3). The cyst is identified, and a sample was collected for pathological analysis. The cyst is completely excised using a shaver (Figure 4). Given to the limited extent of the capsular incision, capsule suturing was not required.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Postoperative rehabilitation begins with partial weight‐bearing immediately and transitioning to full weight‐bearing over the course of 2 weeks. During the first month after surgery, activities placing excessive stress on the posterior hip, such as deep squatting or sitting on low surfaces, should be avoided to reduce the risk of posterior dislocation. Follow‐up magnetic resonance imaging 1 month postoperatively confirmed complete excision of the cyst with no evidence of recurrence (Figure 5). The patient reported complete resolution of symptoms and showed complete recovery of hip function. Strengthening exercises targeting the gluteus maximus and medius are encouraged. Patients may gradually resume sports and other high‐impact activities beginning 3 months after surgery.

DISCUSSION

Contemporary advances in arthroscopic instrumentation and high‐definition imaging have driven a substantial global increase in hip arthroscopy volumes over the past decade. A nationwide retrospective cohort analysis of the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program by Montgomery et al. documented a 600% rise in hip arthroscopy procedures between 2006 and 2010, followed by an additional 85% increase from 2011 to 2018. 7 , 8 Despite this growth, lesions confined to the posterolateral or posteromedial compartments represent only 4.1% (95% confidence interval, 3.2%‐5.0%) of all arthroscopically addressable pathology. 9 , 10 The most common entities in this subset include posterior synovial cysts and chondromatosis, pigmented villonodular synovitis, and recurrent posterior instability. Although their overall incidence is low, these conditions are typically refractory to conservative management, associated with high misdiagnosis rates, and exert a disproportionately negative impact on hip function, underscoring their clinical significance. 11 Current arthroscopic strategies for hip pathology largely follow the “supine‐traction‐anterior/anterolateral (AL)‐distal anterolateral accessory” paradigm. Although this approach provides excellent visualization of the anterolateral compartment, posterior extension of the arthroscope is restricted by femoral head retroversion and the intact posterior capsule, leaving up to 34% of the posteromedial compartment in a blind zone. 12 In addition, prolonged traction is associated with neurapraxia of the sciatic, pudendal, and dorsal sural nerves in 1.2% to 3.4% of cases, 13 necessitating intraoperative neuromonitoring and prolonging postoperative rehabilitation. More importantly, posterior lesions often require adjunctive procedures such as a Kocher‐Langenbeck open approach or a Ganz surgical dislocation, which increase operative time by an average of 45 minutes and raise fluoroscopic exposure 1.8‐fold. 14 , 15 , 16 Lin et al. employed the Kocher‐Langenbeck incision with arthroscopic assistance, but this technique disrupted the gluteus maximus‐medius complex and compromised posterior column blood supply, leading to longer incisions, greater blood loss, higher rates of postoperative scar pain, and an increased risk of heterotopic ossification while still providing only limited visualization of the posteromedial recess. 17 Similarly, Rami et al. performed an extended T‐shaped capsulotomy through the conventional anterolateral portal to resect a posterior giant‐cell tumor of the synovium. Although the tumor was removed successfully, the extensive anterior capsulotomy compromised anterior hip stability, requiring prolonged activity restriction to prevent microinstability. Furthermore, the acute posterior extension of the T‐shaped incision increased the risk of sciatic nerve traction injury. 18

Drawing on extensive experience with minimally invasive gluteal contracture release in the lateral decubitus position, we recognized that a limited capsulotomy could adequately expose both the posterolateral and posteromedial structures. 13 Consequently, we developed a “nontraction, lateral‐decubitus, posterolateral hip approach.” With the patient in the lateral decubitus position and without traction or fluoroscopy, the first portal is established at the intersection of the line connecting the anterior superior iliac spine and the apex of the greater trochanter with the posterior cortical extension of the femoral shaft. A second portal is created 4 cm proximal to the first, with both skin incisions measuring <1 cm. Under arthroscopic guidance, blunt dissection is carried out along the fibers of the iliotibial tract, followed by a 2 to 3 cm longitudinal split of the gluteus maximus along its fibers. The posterior border of the gluteus medius is then separated in line with its orientation, allowing palpation of the piriformis tendon. The deep surface of this tendon overlies the posterior capsule. A 1 to 1.5 cm capsulotomy perpendicular to the femoral neck axis provides direct visualization of the posterolateral and posteromedial compartments, enabling cyst excision, synovectomy, and other indicated procedures.

This approach offers several distinct advantages (Table 1). The lateral decubitus position simplifies patient setup and reduces anesthesia induction time by 8 to 10 minute. The absence of traction eliminates traction‐related nerve injury and removes the need for fluoroscopy, significantly reducing radiation exposure. Arthroscopic instruments are directed straight to posterior capsular lesions without disturbing anterior structures, resulting in lower postoperative pain scores and faster recovery. Nonetheless, inherent risks and limitations remain (Table 1). The portals traverse the iliotibial tract, gluteus maximus, and gluteus medius longitudinally, crossing terminal branches of the inferior gluteal artery and ascending branches of the medial circumflex femoral artery. Inadequate hemostasis may cause intramuscular hematoma and impair visualization. Although the piriformis tendon is a reliable landmark, the sciatic nerve lies immediately lateral to it; even minimal lateral deviation may result in traction or thermal injury. Thus, meticulous identification of the piriformis tendon, detailed knowledge of neurovascular anatomy, and proficiency in arthroscopic hemostasis are essential for safe execution. Moreover, because the procedure is performed without traction, the femoral head remains congruent with the acetabulum, precluding access to central compartment lesions (e.g., posterior‐column chondral pathology). In such cases, conversion to the conventional supine‐traction technique is advisable. Obesity increases gluteal bulk, which deepens the working field and may reduce maneuverability; longer cannulae or specialized instruments may therefore be required. Despite these limitations, the proposed approach provides an efficient, minimally invasive, and neurovascularly safe option for localized posterolateral or posteromedial pathology. With increasing experience and further refinement of instrumentation, its application to more complex posterior lesions warrants continued exploration.
