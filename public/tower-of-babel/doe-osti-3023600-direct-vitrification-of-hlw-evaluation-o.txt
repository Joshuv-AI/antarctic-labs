 SECTION 1.0
 INTRODUCTION

 This report describes work performed to identify tank wastes that are candidates for
direct vitrification of High Level Waste (HLW) with little or no pretreatment, and includes the
development and testing of new glass and feed formulations for the identified candidates to
support planning for HLW direct vitrification. A direct feed HLW (DFHLW) option is under
consideration to start up and operate the Hanford Tank Waste Treatment and Immobilization
Plant (WTP) HLW vitrification facility. Under this option, the WTP pretreatment facility would
be bypassed in order to support an earlier start-up of the vitrification facility. The direct feed
option may also provide a mitigating process strategy for wastes that may challenge the baseline
WTP design (e.g., waste from tanks with large and fast-settling plutonium particles may
challenge the baseline design for mixing) [1]. If the HLW is directly fed to the vitrification
facility, ultrafiltration and caustic leaching that would otherwise have been performed at the
pretreatment facility would be either omitted or replaced by interim pretreatment functions such
as in-tank washing and settling. Modification of the pretreatment process may result in:
(i) Higher aluminum contents if caustic leaching is not performed; (ii) Higher chromium contents
if oxidative leaching is not performed1; (iii) Higher fraction of supernate in the HLW feed due to
lower efficiency of in-tank washing; and (iv) Higher water content due to the lower effectiveness
of in-tank settling compared to ultrafiltration. These changes would likely affect glass
formulations and the associated waste loadings; they would also impact the downstream
vitrification process, including the glass production rate. The viability of the HLW direct feed
approach has been demonstrated in glass formulation and melter testing work at the Vitreous
State Laboratory (VSL) for the Department of Energy (DOE) Office of River Protection (ORP),
and the relative merits of various waste pretreatment strategies have been investigated [2, 3]. A
more recent direct feed testing program investigated select blending and washing strategies [4]
that were developed by VSL/Atkins for Washington River Protection Solutions (WRPS) [5].
Optimum blended and washed waste compositions from that work were selected for further
study. Crucible melts were formulated and tested to investigate the effects on glass compositions
and waste loadings; melter tests assessed impacts on processability and melt rates as well as the
need for redox control resulting from the higher levels of nitrates from the increased supernate
fraction. Off-gas data were collected to assess the potential impacts of increased NOx generation
on the WTP HLW facility. The melter tests were conducted on the DM100 system with
representative HLW feeds at solids contents extending below the present WTP baseline, which
are likely for the direct feed option. The effects on glass production rate, melter operations, and
off-gas carryover were determined. In addition, the ability of increased bubbling to compensate
for the increased evaporative load was investigated. The results from these tests form the basis
for future larger-scale tests on the DM1200 HLW Pilot Melter, where the effects of enhanced
bubbler configurations can also be investigated. One of the objectives was to determine the
extent to which the shortfall in feed solids content can be compensated by improved HLW melter
bubbling. This work builds on previous work performed at the VSL for ORP to increase waste

1.1 Background

 Projections of the number of HLW canisters to be produced at the WTP (e.g., [15]) are
based upon the inventory of the tank wastes, the anticipated performance of the sludge treatment
processes, and current understanding of the capability of the borosilicate glass waste form. The
WTP HLW melter design, unlike earlier DOE melter designs, incorporates an active glass pool
bubbler system. The bubblers create active glass pool convection and thereby improve heat
transfer and glass melting rate. The WTP HLW melters each have a glass surface area of 3.75 m2
and depth of ~1.1 m. The two melters in the HLW facility together are designed to produce up to
7.5 MT of glass per day at 100% availability. Further increases in HLW waste processing rates
can potentially be achieved by optimization of the feed and glass formulations, increasing the
melter operating temperature above 1150C, and by increasing the waste loading in the glass
product. Increasing the waste loading also has the added benefit of decreasing the number of
canisters for storage.

 The baseline estimates and glass formulation efforts have been conservative in terms of
achievable waste loadings. These baseline formulations have been specified to ensure that the
glasses are homogenous, contain essentially no crystalline phases, are processable in joule-
heated, ceramic-lined melters and meet WTP Contract terms. The WTP’s overall mission will
require the immobilization of tank waste compositions that are dominated by mixtures of
aluminum, chromium, bismuth, iron, phosphorous, zirconium, and sulfur compounds as waste-
loading-limiting components. Glass compositions for these waste mixtures have been developed
based upon previous experience and current glass property models. In order to improve waste
loadings, DOE initiated a testing program at VSL to develop and characterize HLW glasses for
wastes that are limited by Al, Al plus Na, Bi, and Cr [15, 16]. Results of that work have
demonstrated the feasibility of increases in waste-loading from about 25 wt% to 33-50 wt%
(based on oxide loading) in the glass, depending on the waste stream. It is expected that these
higher waste loading glasses will reduce the HLW canister production requirement by about 25%
or more [10]. Furthermore, it has been shown that a key technological risk area relates to the
strong dependence of glass production rate on waste composition [10]. The extent of this
variation across the full spectrum of HLW waste types needs to be quantified in order to
accurately project waste treatment rates.

 Under a separate contract with BNI to support the WTP, VSL has developed and tested
glass formulations for WTP HLW compositions to provide data to meet the WTP contract
requirements and to support system design activities [17-22]. That work was based upon small-
scale batch melts (“crucible melts”) using waste simulants. Selected formulations were also
tested in small-scale, continuously fed, joule-heated melters (DM100 and DM1200) [23-29].
That testing was directed towards waste streams from the then-planned early feed tanks for the
WTP (i.e., AZ-101, AZ-102, C-106/AY-102, and C-104/AY-101). These wastes are high in iron
(AZ-101, AZ-102 and C-106/AY-102) or thorium (C-104/AY-101) and are significantly
different than those used in more recent enhancement tests performed for ORP (i.e., wastes

 9

limited by Al, Al/Na, Bi, Cr, S, etc.). Baseline glass formulations to treat these high-Fe wastes
were developed under the BNI contract. During that time, the throughput requirement for the
HLW melter was initially 400 kg/(m2·day), which was subsequently increased to
800 kg/(m2·day). As a result, the baseline high-Fe HLW glass formulations for WTP perform
only slightly better than the 800 kg/(m2·day) processing rate requirement. Furthermore, the
baseline waste loadings for the Fe-limited HLW compositions are only slightly higher than the
BNI contract minimum. Since that time, in work performed for ORP on other HLW
compositions, VSL has developed small-scale test methods to assess processing rates of melter
feeds and included them as an integral part of glass formulation development. This methodology
was used successfully to develop glass formulations for high-Al Hanford HLW that showed
processing rates in excess of 2000 kg/(m2·day) while achieving high waste loadings. The same
methodology can be applied to the development of improved glass formulations for other
Hanford HLW in order to provide ORP with a significantly more robust operating envelope with
reduced risk of throughput shortfalls.

1.2 Test Objectives

 The initial objective of the present work was to review Hanford HLW compositions to
identify HLW streams that are good candidates for the HLW direct feed vitrification option.
Another objective was to develop and identify HLW glass compositions and glass forming
additive blends for vitrification of two HLW streams identified from the review that could be
treated with little or no washing or other pretreatment and would be, therefore, good direct feed
candidates. The HLW glass compositions and additive blends were developed to maintain high
waste loadings, high processing rates, and acceptable product glass properties. This was
accomplished through a combination of crucible-scale and DM100 melter system tests. The tests
were performed according to a Test Plan developed for this work [30] that is responsive to the
ORP statement of work (SOW) [31].

1.3 Test Overview

 The general approach for developing and identifying glass compositions for an HLW
waste that has undergone little or no washing involved the following steps:

 (1) A review was performed on the Hanford tank waste compositions to identify those that
 require no or minimal pretreatment. Identification of these wastes could expedite the
 processing of HLW at the WTP. Optimization of the achievable waste loading for the
 identified wastes were conducted by varying the mix of glass forming additives using
 HLW property-composition models to ensure that all processing and property
 constraints are met. Based on the review and optimization results, two HLW streams
 were selected for ORP approval to support subsequent glass formulation development
 and melter testing. The HLW glass property-composition models for high waste
 loading HLW glasses, developed by VSL for ORP, were employed in this work.

 (2) Glass formulations for the selected HLW streams were prepared at crucible scale and
 characterized. Characterization data supported the selection of compositions with high
 waste loadings that meet all of the processing constraints and product quality
 requirements. The glass formulations were actively designed in that characterization
 data from the preceding set(s) of crucible melts were fed back to design subsequent sets
 of formulations. Initial glass formulations may be similar to the compositions derived
 from optimization based on the property-composition models. The models were also
 used to guide the development and refinement of subsequent formulations. Since new
 glass compositions often reside outside the validity range of the models, model
 predictions were used judiciously in the selection of new formulations for testing (i.e., a
 new composition that is predicted by the models to have properties outside the
 acceptable ranges may still be selected, provided past experience or information from
 literature indicate benefits). Characterization of the crucible melts included
 composition, crystal content, melt viscosity, melt electrical conductivity, Product
 Consistency Test (PCT), and Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP).
 Additional characterization including sulfate solubility and Inconel corrosion were
 performed, as warranted. Not all crucible melts were characterized for all properties.
 Approximately 20 crucible melts were prepared and characterized to develop the new
 glass formulations.

 (3) DM100 melter tests were performed using the most promising formulations as
 identified by the crucible-scale tests. The total duration of the DM100 melter tests was
 about 200 hours. Testing was divided into four test segments to determine the
 throughput of each composition with nominal and optimized bubbling. Samples of the
 glass product were collected and characterized to provide comparison to the results
 obtained from the crucible glasses. All melter tests were performed at 1150ºC. Data on
 processing rate and any processing difficulties, off-gas composition, and system
 operating parameters were collected and are reported here.

1.5 DM100 Melter System

        1.5.1    DM100 Feed System

 A schematic diagram of the DM100 vitrification system is shown in Figure 1.1. The
melter feed is introduced in batches into a feed container that is mounted on a load cell for
weight monitoring. The feed is stirred with a variable speed mixer and constantly recirculated
except for periodic, momentary interruptions during which the weight is recorded. A peristaltic
pump is used in order to provide a uniform delivery of feed to the melt surface. Feed is directed
from the recirculation loop that extends to the top of the melter and then diverted to the
peristaltic pump, which regulates the flow of feed through a Teflon-lined feed line and
water-cooled feed tube into the melter.

 1.5.2 Melter System

 Cross-sectional diagrams of the DM100-BL melter are shown in Figures 1.2.a-c. The
DM100-BL unit is a ceramic refractory-lined melter fitted with five electrodes: two pairs of
opposing Inconel 690 plate electrodes and a bottom electrode. Power can be supplied in either
three-phase or single-phase configurations. All of the tests in the present work were performed
with the upper and lower electrodes on each side connected together and powered by a
single-phase supply; the bottom electrode was not powered. Melt pool agitation is achieved by
either a removable lance entering from the top of the melter or a permanent bubbler installed
through the bottom electrode. In these tests, the lance bubbler was used. The glass product is
removed from the melter by means of an airlift discharge system. The melter has a melt surface
area of 0.108 m 2 and a variable glass inventory of between 110 kg, when only the bottom pair of
 P P

electrodes is used, and about 170 kg when both pairs of electrodes are used, which was the case
in the present tests.

 1.5.3 Off-Gas System

 For operational simplicity, the DM100-BL is equipped with a dry off-gas treatment
system involving gas filtration operations only. Exhaust gases leave the melter plenum through a
film cooler device that minimizes the formation of solid deposits. The film-cooler air has
constant flow rate and its temperature is thermostatically controlled. Consequently, under
steady-state operating conditions, the exhaust gases passing through the transition line (between
the melter and the first filtration device) can be sampled at constant temperature and airflow rate.
The geometry of the transition line conforms to the requirements of the 40-CFR-60 air sampling
techniques. Immediately downstream of the transition line are cyclonic filters followed by
conventional pre-filters and high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters. The temperature of the
cyclonic filters is maintained above 150°C while the temperatures in the HEPAs are kept
sufficiently high to prevent moisture condensation. The entire train of gas filtration operations is
duplicated, and each train is used alternately. An induced draft fan completes the system.

1.6 Experimental Procedures and Methods

 1.6.1 Feed Samples from Melter Tests

 Feed samples were taken directly from as-received drums and the melter feed
recirculation line during each test. Feed samples are poured into a platinum/gold crucible and
placed into a programmed furnace for drying and fusion to form a glass. The glass produced
from this fusion is ground to less than 200 mesh and sealed in 20-ml vials for subsequent
analysis by X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF), or by acid digestion followed by
direct-current plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (DCP-AES) on the resulting solution. The
feed samples are also characterized for their density, pH, water content, and glass yield.

 1.6.2 Glass Product

 The glass product is discharged from the melter into 5-gallon steel pails periodically
using an air-lift system. The discharged product glass is sampled at the end of each test by
removing sufficient glass from the top of the cans for compositional analysis and secondary
phase determination. In addition, the Product Consistency Test (PCT, 7 days at 90°C) and TCLP
were performed on samples of the glass product from the DM100 melter tests. Prior to those
tests, the PCT and TCLP were also performed on the crucible melt compositions that were
selected for the melter tests to ensure their compliance with the present WTP contract
requirements. All of these procedures are routinely conducted at VSL and, therefore, standard
operating procedures (SOPs) are in place.

 Sample preparation for chemical analysis typically involves size reduction and sieving. All
samples are subjected to XRF to determine the concentration of all elements except boron and
lithium. A series of National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) reference materials
are used for confirmation of the XRF data. Boron and lithium are determined by microwave-
assisted total acid dissolution of ground glass samples in HF/HNO3 and subjecting the resulting
solutions to DCP-AES analysis.

                 1.6.2.1      Viscosity

 The melt viscosity, η, is measured using a Brookfield viscometer. Measurements are
performed in the temperature range of 950-1250ºC and the data are interpolated to standard
temperatures using the Vogel-Fulcher equation: ln η = [A/(T-To)]+B, where A, B, and To are
fitting parameters. The equipment is calibrated at room temperature using standard oils of known
viscosity and then checked at 950-1250ºC using a NIST standard reference glass (SRM 711).
Both precision and accuracy of the viscosity measurements are estimated to be within ±15
relative%.

 13

 1.6.2.2 Electrical Conductivity

 The electrical conductivity, σ, of each glass is determined by measuring the resistance of
the glass melt as a function of frequency using a calibrated platinum/rhodium electrode probe
attached to a Hewlett-Packard model 4194A impedance analyzer. Measurements are performed
over similar temperature ranges to those employed for the melt viscosity measurements. The
results are analyzed to obtain the DC electrical conductivity. The electrical conductivity data are
then interpolated to standard temperatures using the Vogel-Fulcher equation: ln  = [A/(T−To)]
+ B, where A, B and To are fitting parameters. Estimated uncertainties in the electrical
conductivity measurements are  20 relative%.

 1.6.2.3 Product Consistency Test (PCT)

 The product consistency test (PCT, ASTM C 1285) is used to evaluate the relative
chemical durability of glasses by measuring the concentrations of the chemical species released
from 100-200 mesh crushed glass (75-149 μm) to the test solution (de-ionized water in this case).
PCT on the HLW glasses are performed at 90ºC, in accordance with the current WTP contract
requirement. The ratio of the glass surface area to the solution volume for this test is about 2000
m-1 (typically, 4 g of 100-200 mesh glass is immersed in 40 ml of deionized water). All tests are
conducted in triplicate, in 304L stainless steel vessels, and in parallel with a standard glass
included in each test set. The internal standard is the Argonne National Laboratory Low Activity
Waste Reference Material (ANL-LRM) glass [34] and/or the Defense Waste Processing Facility
(DWPF)-Environmental Assessment (EA) glass [35], both of which have undergone round-robin
testing. The leachates are sampled at seven days. One milliliter of sampled leachate is mixed
with 20 ml of 1M HNO3 and the resulting solution is analyzed by DCP-AES; another 3 ml of
sampled leachate is used for pH measurement.

 1.6.2.4 Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP)

 The TCLP was performed at VSL using SW-846 Method 1311, which employs leaching
of crushed glass (< 3/8”) in a sodium acetate buffer solution for 18 hours at 22C with constant
end-over-end agitation. A mass of about 100 grams of glass is leached in 2 liters of TCLP
extract, according to the extraction method for non-volatiles. The surface area to volume ratio for
this test is about 20 m-1, which is about two orders of magnitude lower than that in the PCT. The
leachates are analyzed by DCP-AES according to VSL standard operating procedures.

 1.6.2.5 Secondary Phases

 Secondary phases in the glass samples are determined by optical microscopy and
scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS).
Secondary phases due to crystallization and phase separation can be identified using these
methods. Quantitative determination of the amount of crystals in glass samples is made by SEM
in conjunction with image analysis.

 SECTION 2.0
 WASTE LOADING OPTIMIZATION, WASTE
 SIMULANTS, AND GLASS FORMULATIONS

 According to the WTP baseline, separation of the dissolved and un-dissolved fractions in
tank waste will be achieved through ultrafiltration in the WTP pretreatment facility. The
dissolved fraction, combined with liquids generated from subsequent washing and leaching of
the solids, will be treated in the low activity waste (LAW) vitrification facility whereas the solids
will be treated in the HLW vitrification facility. A primary objective of the present work was to
evaluate feed compositions that may arise as a result of bypassing the pretreatment facility. In
such direct-feed scenarios, some of the functions of the WTP ultrafiltration process would be
replaced by interim alternatives such as in-tank settling and washing. Since these processes are
likely to be less efficient than ultrafiltration, the resulting HLW stream would retain larger
amounts of the solution fraction and wash water, which may have significant impacts on glass
formulations and glass production rates. Data on glass volume and processing time for candidate
HLW direct feed source tanks are required to assess the relative benefits of progressively more
extensive HLW pretreatment, versus the associated increases in operational costs and supporting
equipment costs. As the projections of glass volume and processing time are dependent on the
compositions of the HLW solids and the LAW fraction in the respective candidate source tanks,
they vary from tank to tank and thus require individual evaluation. This section presents the
results of tank-by-tank evaluations over a representative set of candidate source tanks.

 Evaluations of candidate source tanks included: (i) calculations of the amounts and
compositions of the unwashed and partially washed solids using tank waste inventory data and
associated wash factors, (ii) estimations of waste loadings by optimizing HLW glass
formulations for the waste stream, and (iii) projections of total glass volume resulting from
direct-feed HLW processing. The evaluation results formed the basis for selecting two waste
streams as starting points for subsequent glass formulation development and testing. Section 2.2
presents the evaluation results while Section 2.3 discusses the HLW glass development. Based
on the results obtained from glass formulation testing, a glass with high waste loading and
acceptable durability and processing characteristics was selected for each waste stream as the
target composition to support DM100 melter testing (see Section 3.0).

2.1 Source Tanks and In-Tank Pretreatment

 A review of selected Hanford tank waste compositions was conducted to identify tanks
that require no or little pretreatment. The primary consideration focused on whether the waste
loading in the associated glass will benefit from pretreatment of the waste feed beyond limited
water wash(es) at the tank farms. Identification of wastes that can produce glass products with
high waste loadings and production rates, even with little or no washing, could expedite HLW
processing at the WTP. A previous study that investigated various blending and washing
strategies considered two groups of candidate source tanks that are located in the 200-East Area

of past HLW direct feed studies [2-4]. Wastes from these 15 tanks were therefore excluded from
consideration in this work. In addition, review of the waste compositions also did not include the
following tanks:

 • Tanks located in the 200-West Area at Hanford, since additional new facilities may
 be needed for tank waste staging between these tanks and WTP. According to the
 River Protection System Plan (Rev. 6), additional new facilities for the East Area B
 tanks are also required but will be available before those for the West Area tanks and
 single-shell tank retrieval for the East Area tanks will be completed before the West

 including (i) the single-shell tank farms A, AX, B, BX, BY, and C (66 tanks total);
 and (ii) the double-shell tank farms AN, AP, AW, AY, and AZ (25 tanks total);
 • Tanks with wastes that are designated as transuranic (TRU) in the Hanford Site Tank

 • Tanks with no or small sludge inventory.

 To evaluate scenarios where the HLW solids are partially washed, general assumptions
can be formulated to address the uncertainty that surrounds the degree to which the liquid and
solid fractions of a tank can be separated by settling. Thus, an in-tank wash/settle/decant cycle
was assumed in this work to result in a three-fold dilution of the soluble fraction, followed by
settling to produce slurry with 15 wt% un-dissolved solids. For calculation purposes, it was
further assumed that the tank wastes are settled to give slurry composed of the washed solids and
a solution of the soluble fraction, the compositions of which could be determined from the
corresponding wash factors. Since this work focused on identifying HLW direct-feed candidates
that are projected to have high waste loadings in the glass product with minimal pretreatment,
only the following scenarios were considered:

 • Unwashed solids with the supernate removed;
 • One in-tank wash/settle cycle with the resulting waste consisting of the fully washed
 solids and one-third of the soluble fraction (referred to as 1/3 washed solids herein).

In order to determine whether more extensive washes would provide any further benefits in
terms of increasing the waste loadings and reducing the glass volume, additional wash scenarios
were considered for the waste streams selected to support glass formulation development (see
below). The additional wash scenarios were:

 16

 • Two in-tank wash/settle cycles with the resulting waste consisting of the fully washed
 solids and one-ninth of the soluble fraction (referred to as 1/9 washed solids herein);

 • Three in-tank wash/ settle cycles with the resulting waste consisting of the fully
 washed solids and one-twenty-seventh of the soluble fraction (referred to as 1/27
 washed solids herein);

 • Fully washed solids with none of the soluble fraction.

 Waste compositions corresponding to the five pretreatments described above were
calculated as follows. The Tank Waste Information Network System (TWINS) Best-Basis

evaluation. Compositions of the unwashed solids as non-volatile oxides (in both kg and wt%)
were calculated using elemental data in the BBI while compositions of the fully washed solids
were derived by applying the tank-specific wash factors to the unwashed solids [38, 39]. The
difference between the unwashed and fully washed solids compositions allowed the calculation

proportions according to the number of wash cycles as described above, to arrive at the
compositions of the partially washed solids.

 Of the 91 tanks located in the 200-East Area in Hanford, 15 have been studied previously
while 4 are designated as TRU waste tanks, leaving 72 tanks for evaluation. The calculated oxide
contents of the unwashed solids in these 72 tanks ranged between 0 and 1666 MT. Tanks with
< 15 MT oxide were excluded from further evaluations since it is unlikely that the relatively

2.2 Optimization of Waste Loadings in Glass Formulations

 For each of the unwashed and 1/3 washed wastes from the candidate source tanks
described above, HLW glass formulations were developed with optimization to maximize the
waste loading. This was achieved through projections of various glass properties associated with
the glass formulations based on predictions made using HLW property-composition models. The
HLW glass formulations must meet a variety of processability and product quality requirements
that are given in terms of limits on various properties of the melt and the product glass. Since
these properties are determined by the glass composition, they will be controlled indirectly
during production by controlling the melter feed composition and, thereby, the composition of
the melt and glass. Models that relate key properties to composition have been developed and
regularly updated for WTP HLW glasses. The property-composition models developed include
PCT responses (i.e., PCT-B, -Li, and -Na releases), one-percent spinel crystal fraction
temperature (spinel T1%), electrical conductivity, viscosity, TCLP Cd-release, and nepheline
formation [40-42]. The present work employed the most recent global HLW models developed

 17

by VSL for ORP [41]; local HLW models were also developed in that work to cover smaller
compositional regions but the global models were used in the present work so that the widest
glass compositional space could be explored. Table 2.1 lists the property and composition
constraints used in waste loading optimization; these are the same as those found in the River
Protection Project System Plan, Rev. 6 and in the earlier work at VSL for estimating the total
glass volume [5, 36]. The earlier optimization at VSL, however, employed the same HLW
property-composition models as those in the Project System Plan, Rev. 6, which were developed
for a considerably smaller compositional space [36]. In addition, component limits were applied
in optimization to address the limited solubilities of sulfate (0.5 wt% SO3 in glass) and calcium
phosphate (CaO × P2O5 < 6.5 wt%) in glass.

 For a given waste composition, Microsoft Excel™ with the Frontline Premium Solver
Platform™ Add-In (including the Large Scale Generalized Reduced Gradient nonlinear solver)
was used to determine the amount of glass forming additives that would maximize the achievable
waste loading. The typical glass forming additives found in HLW glass formulations were used
in the optimization: Al2O3, B2O3, Fe2O3, Li2O, Na2O, and SiO2. To facilitate the search for
solutions, representative HLW glass formulations were used as starting points and the
optimization results were checked against various initial conditions. In order to maintain
computing times at a practical level, iterations were run with a convergence value of 0.0001 (i.e.,
the search was stopped when the relative change in the objective was less than 0.0001 for the last
iteration). It should be noted that waste loading optimization falls under nonlinear programming
because the physical properties that are part of the optimization routines are nonlinear in the
components. Ensuring the computed solution is the true global optimum can therefore be
challenging. Repeating the optimization with different starting conditions (i.e., glass former
mixes) provided one means of checking. In general, optimized results obtained from different
runs for the same waste were found to give very similar waste loadings, although the glass
formulations could show larger variations.

 Table 2.2 summarizes the optimization results for the unwashed and 1/3 washed wastes
from 39 candidate source tanks. Results from previous optimization work for other 200-East
Area tanks are also included for comparison [5]. Table 2.2 gives for each waste the amount of
waste oxide, both the optimized total and HLW waste loadings, the primary constraint that
limited waste loading, and the number of glass canisters produced. Estimates of the number of
canisters produced was based on the optimized total waste loading and the assumption that a
canister would be filled to 95% on average and thus contain 3.02 MT of HLW glass (average
glass density = 2.66 g/ml) [36]. The calculated total waste loadings for unwashed solids in Table
2.2 vary between 5.82% (AW-103) to 49.68% (BX-102), while the calculated HLW loadings are
lower and range between 2.02% (B-104) and 40.99% (BX-102). Partial (1/3 washed) washing of
the solids generally increased the achievable waste loadings, primarily through removal of
soluble components that constrained the waste loading (e.g., sulfate). The optimized total waste
loadings for the 1/3 washed solids therefore increased to between 7.25% (AW-103) and 70.77%
(BX-102), with the corresponding HLW loadings being 5.14% (AW-103) and 66.10% (BX-102).
When the efficiency of removing the limiting component by washing is high, very significant
gains in waste loadings can be realized (e.g., AN-106 and BX-107) and extra wash/settle/decant
cycles are likely to yield additional benefits in increased waste loadings. In a few cases, however,
partial washing resulted in lower total waste loadings; for example, the total waste loading of

 18

high-aluminum AW-102 waste decreased from 26.94% (unwashed) to 24.84% (1/3 washed)
because of excessive removal of Na2O such that more Na2O needed to be introduced as additive
in processing of the 1/3 washed solids. Consequently, the canister count in this case showed only
a very modest reduction from 190 to 181, while 19 MT of LAW oxides resulting from washing
remained untreated. These and earlier results clearly demonstrate that the optimum washing
strategy to support the direct feed option, in terms of maximizing waste loading and minimizing
the number of HLW canisters, is dependent on the specific waste composition and requires a
tank-by-tank evaluation. Overall, partial (1/3) washing of wastes from the 39 source tanks
reduced the HLW canister count from 20259 to 10134. Finally, the principal waste loading
limiting components and factors listed in Table 2.2 are nepheline formation, sulfate, and spinel
T1%, which can be compared with the projections made in the Baseline Case of the System Plan
for all Hanford tanks: Al2O3 indirectly via the nepheline discriminator (29.8%), spinel T1%
(24.7%), and SO3 (21.9%) [36].

 The optimization results in Table 2.2 were used to select two waste streams to support
glass formulation development and subsequent melter tests. The major focus was to identify
waste streams that were minimally pretreated (1/3 washed in the present case) and projected to
have high waste loadings in the glass products. The amount of waste oxide available for delivery
to WTP was also considered. The wastes selected were AN-106 1/3 washed solids and BX-102
1/3 washed solids, for which the projected total waste loadings were 46.41% and 70.77%,
respectively. As seen in Table 2.2, treatment of both AN-106 and BX-102 benefited greatly from
a single in tank wash/settle/decant cycle, which resulted in significant increases in the achievable
waste loadings. The pretreatment effectively removed sulfate from the unwashed solids in both
cases – the SO3 concentrations were lowered from 1.42 wt% to 0.60 wt% in the AN-106 solids
and from 1.01 wt% to 0.42 wt% in the BX-102 solids – such that waste loadings in the 1/3
washed solids became limited by Al2O3 (via nepheline formation) instead of SO3.

 To investigate whether further benefits could be derived for both AN-106 and BX-102
wastes by additional washing, optimization was performed for the 1/9 washed, 1/27 washed, and
fully washed solids; the results are summarized in Table 2.2. As expected from the composition
of the LAW fraction in the wastes, additional washes continued to remove significant amounts of
sulfate and sodium but not aluminum in both cases. The optimized HLW waste loadings for the
wastes that received additional washes therefore continued to be constrained by alumina (via
nepheline formation) and showed only marginal improvements, while the total waste loadings
actually showed a slight decline. When compared to the 1/3 washed solids, fully washing the
solids produced extra savings of only 15 and 3 HLW canisters, respectively, for AN-106 and
BX-102. Figures 2.1 and 2.2 display the effects of repeated wash/settle/decant cycles on total and
HLW waste loadings on the AN-106 and BX-102 solids, respectively. They also show the
concentrations of soluble constituents that are potentially waste-loading limiting (i.e., SO3, F,
and Cl). Similar to earlier findings [2], the present evaluations demonstrate that most of the
benefit of washing in terms of HLW waste loading arose from the first wash cycle while multiple
wash cycles produced only rapidly diminishing returns.

 2.4.1 Glass Formulations for AN-106 1/3 Washed Solids

 The optimized glass for AN-106 1/3 washed solids has a target total waste loading of
46.41 wt%, which is limited by nepheline formation and melt viscosity (see Table 2.2). A review
of the optimization results, however, shows that the predicted spinel T1% (946ºC) is only
marginally beneath the constraint value of 950ºC, suggesting that spinel crystallization will also

in Table 2.7. The first four members of the AN106-W glass series have total waste loadings

 Heat treatments of AN106-W01 through -W-04 at 950ºC and below invariably resulted in

of sodium as an additive. The principal secondary phase observed in the heat treated samples of
AN106-W05 through -W08 continued to be spinel, but the vol% crystallinity was reduced
considerably when compared to the earlier members of the series. For AN106-W05 and -W06,
the spinel crystallinity amounts after heat treatment at 950ºC were both < 1 vol% and considered
acceptable for melter testing. The primary secondary phases identified in the CCC samples of
AN106-W05 through -W08 were spinel and calcium/lithium phosphate while small amounts of
an unidentified silicate phase were also observed for AN106-W05 (nepheline discriminator
= 0.56) and -W06 (0.55). Acceptability of these two glasses in terms of chemical durability, as
defined by the PCT, was consequently assessed with CCC samples. Both glasses showed
relatively high normalized PCT releases but AN106-W05 met the requirement of surpassing the
performance limits set by the benchmark DWPF-EA glass. This glass was further characterized
with respect to melt viscosity, electrical conductivity (Table 2.8), and TCLP (Table 2.9). All the
measured property values of AN106-W05 met the respective requirements specified for WTP
HLW glasses.

 Based on the characterization data collected, AN106-W05 was selected as the target glass
formulation to support melter testing. This glass has a total waste loading of 47.00 wt%. On a
HLW basis, the waste loading is 41.78 wt%. It is relatively high in alumina, incorporating
16.81 wt% of Al2O3, followed by 9.15 wt% of Na2O (from waste, with an extra 3.00 wt% as
additive) and 8.51 wt% of Fe2O3.

4.1 Analysis of Feed Samples

 Feed samples from as-received drums were analyzed to confirm physical properties and

of deviations from the target in the as-received feed samples, and the inconsistencies between

 SECTION 5.0
 MONITORED OFF-GAS EMISSIONS

5.1 Particulate Sampling

 The melter exhaust was sampled for metals/particles according to 40-CFR-60 Methods 3,
5, and 29 at steady-state operating conditions during the two tests conducted with nominal
bubbling. The concentrations of off-gas species that are present as particulates and gaseous
species that are collected in impinger solutions were derived from laboratory data on solutions
extracted from air samples (filters and various solutions) together with measurements of the
volume of air sampled. Particulate collection required isokinetic sampling, which entails
removing gas from the exhaust at the same velocity that the air is flowing in the duct (40-CFR-
60, Methods 1-5). Typically, a sample size of 30 dry standard cubic feet (dscf) was taken at a
rate of between 0.5 and 0.75 dry standard cubic feet per minute (dscfm). Total particulate loading
was determined by combining gravimetric analysis of the standard particle filter and chemical
analysis of probe rinse solutions. An additional impinger containing 2 N NaOH was added to the
sampling train to ensure complete scrubbing of all acid gases. The collected materials were
analyzed using DCP-AES for the majority of the constituents and ion chromatography (IC) for
anions. Melter emission fluxes are compared to feed fluxes and emission samples taken while
processing the two feed compositions in Table 5.1. Notice the distinction that is made between
constituents sampled as particles and as "gas". The "gaseous" constituents are operationally
defined as those species that are scrubbed in the impinger solutions after the air stream has
passed through a 0.3 µm heated filter. Both samples were within the 90 – 110% limits for
isokinetic sampling.

 Particulate emissions constituted 0.28 and 0.41 percent of feed solids for feeds containing
AN106-W05 and BX102-W03 partially washed solids processed with bubbling fixed at 9 lpm,
respectively. These levels of solids carryover are generally lower than measured while
processing various feeds containing high iron HLW simulants processed on the same melter at a
temperature of 1150oC: C-106/AY-102 SIPP (0.61 percent) [25]; the former C-106/AY-102
baseline (0.3 - 0.74 percent) [45]; a C-106/AY-102 high waste loading formulation (0.66) [8],
HLW AZ-101 (0.46 percent) [55], feeds containing unwashed to partially washed AY-102 HLW
solids (0.46 to 0.53 percent) [2], feeds containing unwashed to partially washed A-104 HLW
solids (0.42 to 0.57 percent) [3], and feeds containing partially washed A-AP106-01 HLW solids
and unwashed AY102-AP102 HLW solids (1.23 and 0.73 percent) [4]. The lower solids
carryover during the present tests with the high water content of the feed was unexpected since
particulate carryover often increases with increased water content. The relationship between feed
water content and particulate defined emissions trends during the first HLW direct feed tests [2]
and the feed with the lowest particulate emissions also had the lowest water content during
earlier HLW direct feed tests [3].

 As expected, the feed elements emitted at the lowest melter decontamination factor (DF)
were fluorine and sulfur. Other elements with low calculated DF values such as chlorine,

 33

potassium, and chromium are biased by the low target feed concentration and likelihood of low
level contamination of the feed. The amount of chlorine measured in the exhaust was greater
than the amount corresponding to the target concentration corroborating this notion. Boron was
another element exhibiting some volatile behavior and was present in the exhaust stream in both
gaseous and particulate forms. The expected increasing volatility of alkali metals with increasing
molecular weight was observed: potassium carryover being the highest followed by sodium, then
lithium. Boron, chlorine, fluorine, and sulfur were the only elements detected in the impinger
solutions collected downstream of the heated particle filter in the sampling train, which
constitutes the “gas” fraction of the melter emissions. A larger portion of the fluorine and sulfur
was observed as gaseous, and more than in most previous tests, due to the emission of HF and
sulfuric acid mist from the high water content feeds.

5.2 Particulate Sampled from Off-Gas System

 Material accumulated in the cyclones downstream of the film cooler was removed,
quantified, and analyzed after processing each composition. The amounts and analysis results for
material removed from the cyclone after each test is provided in Table 5.2. The limited quantity
of particulate removed from the cyclone after these tests supports the low solids carryover
measured during the tests. The amount of material removed from the cyclone is not totally
indicative of the total amount of particulate emitted from the melter since much of the material
emitted is fine particulate, which is not captured in the cyclones. Analysis of material
accumulated in the cyclones shows all of the feed components in similar proportions to the feed
for most of the elements, enrichments over feed for volatile elements, most notably chlorine and
sulfur, as well as depletions of non-volatile silicon, and contamination with magnesium,
potassium, titanium, zinc, and zirconium from earlier tests. Chlorine and sulfur, which had the
lowest measured DF across the melter, have the greatest enrichment (31X and 4X, respectively)
in the cyclone powders over the amounts in the target glass. The observed enrichment for
chlorine is not only due to the volatility of the element but also trace level contamination of the

 SECTION 6.0
 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

 In the HLW direct feed option that is under consideration for early WTP operations, the
pretreatment facility would be bypassed in order to support an earlier start-up of the vitrification
facility. An evaluation was performed previously to assess various blending and washing
strategies for feed batches considered as candidates for processing under the HLW direct feed
flowsheet [5]. It was concluded that increased number of wash cycles generally resulted in
higher HLW loadings, decreased canister count, and decreased glass yield in the melter feed.
These effects varied from tank to tank, however, since the projections of glass volume and
processing time are dependent on the compositions of the HLW solids and the LAW fraction in
the waste batch. For example, while a single wash cycle produced a > 3-fold gain in waste
loading and a corresponding reduction in canister count for the A-R-AP106 waste (all A tanks
retrieved into AP-106), similar benefits from washing for AY-101 and AZ-101 were much
smaller such that direct treatment of the unwashed waste may be more practical. These earlier
results therefore demonstrated that the optimum washing strategy to support the HLW direct feed
option, in terms of maximizing waste loadings and minimizing canister count, requires individual
tank-by-tank evaluations.

 The present work evaluated 39 candidate source tanks located in the Hanford 200-East
Area. The other 52 East Area tanks were not considered because (i) they have been studied
before [5], (ii) their wastes are classified as TRU, or (iii) they contain little or no (< 15 MT on an
oxide basis) sludge inventory. The evaluation was performed on the unwashed and 1/3 washed
(one wash/settle/decant cycle) solids only since the focus of the present work was to identify
tank waste candidates that were projected to have high waste loadings in the glass with minimal
pretreatment. For each of the wastes under consideration, a HLW glass formulation was
developed and optimized to give the highest waste loading based on projections of various melt
and glass properties. The latest HLW glass property-composition models available at the time
[41] were used to ensure the optimized glass would meet all applicable processing and product
constraints. The optimization results confirmed the earlier findings in that partial (1/3) washing
generally produced higher waste loadings in glass. The canister count required for processing the
39 source tanks was substantially reduced, going from 20259 (unwashed) to 10134 (1/3 washed).
As was the case in the previous work, the details of the waste loading improvements and canister
savings vary from tank to tank. In addition, the primary waste loading limiting components and
factors for the tank wastes under evaluation were identified to be nepheline formation, sulfate,
and spinel T1%.

 The optimization results guided the selection of two waste streams to support glass
formulation development and subsequent melter testing. The selection considered the achievable
waste loadings and the amounts of waste oxides available for delivery to WTP. The wastes
selected were AN-106 1/3 washed solids and BX-102 1/3 washed solids. The unwashed solids
from both of these tanks were relatively high in sulfate (> 1 wt%) and the optimization results
showed that a single wash cycle effectively removed enough sulfate in both cases such that

 36

glasses for the 1/3 washed solids were limited by nepheline formation instead of sulfate, with
significant increases in waste loadings. Additional optimization was performed for both AN-106
and BX-102 wastes to determine whether more pretreatment (i.e., 1/9 washed, 1/27 washed, and
fully washed solids) would be beneficial. Since the extra washes did not remove aluminum from
the AN-106 and BX-102 solids to any considerable extent and their glass formulations continued
to be limited by nepheline formation, it was shown that only marginal gains could be realized
with additional washings. Similar findings have been reported before from evaluation of other
wastes (e.g., AX tanks retrieved into tank AZ-102) [5].

 Melter tests were conducted on the DM100-BL vitrification system processing the singly
washed AN-106 and BX-102 solids with glass forming additives optimized for each blend. The
four tests are distinguished by the two waste and glass compositions processed with nominal
fixed and optimized bubbling. The feed solids content targeted 0.282 and 0.194 kg glass per kg
feed for AN-106 and BX-102, respectively; these values are relatively low as a result of the low
concentration of dissolved solids in the waste and the water associated with the 15 wt% HLW
oxides in each waste. The feed rate was adjusted to maintain a complete cold cap during each
test. The principal results of these tests can be summarized as follows:

 • Both feed formulations were readily processed with HLW oxide loadings of 41.8 and
 69.1 wt% and total waste oxide loadings of 47 and 74 wt% for the AN106-W05 and
 BX102-W03 formulations, respectively, while meeting all WTP processing and
 product quality requirements and maintaining acceptable glass and feed processing
 properties.

 • Glass production rates of 700 and 600 kg/m 2 /day were achieved for the AN106-W05
 P P

 and BX102-W03 compositions, respectively, while processing with nominal, fixed
 (fixed at 9 lpm) bubbling. These production rates are below the 800 kg/m 2 /day P P

 baseline rate for the WTP HLW vitrification system due to the low solids content of
 the feeds, which is known to decrease production rates.

 • Glass production rates were increased to 1000 and 800 kg/m 2 /day for the P P

 AN106-W05 and BX102-W03 compositions with the use of optimized bubbling,
 partially mitigating the effect of the high water content in the feeds. These production
 rates are at or above the 800 kg/m 2 /day baseline rate for WTP HLW vitrification.
 P P

 • HLW oxide processing rates were 292 and 414 kg/m 2 /day for singly washed AN-106
 P P

 and BX-102 solids, respectively, at nominal bubbling. HLW oxide processing rates
 were increased to 418 and 553 kg/m 2 /day for singly washed AN-106 and BX-102
 P P

 solids, respectively, with optimized bubbling.

 • Total waste oxide processing rates were 329 and 444 kg/m 2 /day for singly washed
 P P

 AN-106 and BX-102 solids, respectively, at nominal bubbling. Total oxide
 processing rates were increased to 470 and 592 kg/m 2 /day for singly washed AN-106
 P P

 and BX-102 solids, respectively, with optimized bubbling.

 37

 • Melter exhaust was sampled during each test to determine the effect of feed
 composition on particulate and gaseous emissions. Melter DFs were determined for
 most elements in the feed. Particulate emissions constituted from 0.28 to 0.41 percent
 of feed solids, which are lower than for previous HLW direct feeds and other feeds
 containing high iron HLW simulants.

 • Measured concentrations of nitrogen oxides in the melter exhaust stream were
 relatively low throughout the tests due to the low concentrations of nitrates, nitrites,
 and organic carbon in the wastes. No ammonia or hydrogen cyanide was measured in
 the exhaust with very low concentrations of carbon monoxide. Emissions of nitrogen
 oxides and products of incomplete combustion increase with greater nitrate and nitrite
 content of the melter feed and therefore nitrogen oxide concentrations while
 processing the BX102-W03 composition were higher than when processing the
 AN106-W05 composition. The decrease in nitrogen oxide emissions relative to the
 nitrate/nitrite concentrations in the feed with the addition of sugar to the feed (0.75
 moles of carbon per mole of nitrogen oxide) using procedures developed for
 vitrifying LAW streams is consistent with extensive previous observation with LAW
 feeds.

 • Glass samples from the crucible and melter tests were subjected to leach testing using
 the PCT and TCLP methods in order to evaluate product quality. Despite the high
 waste loading of HLW oxides in each composition tested, the glass products
 significantly out-performed the DWPF-EA benchmark glass on the PCT leaching
 procedure by at least an order of magnitude and exhibited TCLP leachate
 concentrations that are well below the WTP delisting limits. Leach test results were
 also below acceptable limits for samples heat treated per canister centerline cooling
 profile.

 Collectively, past [2-4] and present studies demonstrate that the optimum washing
strategy, in terms of minimizing the number of HLW canisters, processing duration, and
secondary waste generation varies depending on the tank waste being treated. The two
compositions studied in the present work demonstrate that singly washed HLW solids provide
the optimum washing strategy for HLW from tanks AN-106 and BX-102.

6.1 Implications for HLW Direct Feed at WTP

 The results from the present and past [2-4] HLW glass formulation and melter testing
demonstrate the viability of the HLW direct feed approach and illustrate the relative merits for
each waste pretreatment strategy. The amount of time required to vitrify the Hanford tank
inventory of AN-106 and BX-102 HLW oxides using a single HLW melter with a melt surface
area of 3.75 m2 operated at 70% total operating efficiency (TOE) is compared to time to vitrify
other singly-washed direct feed streams [2-4] in Table 6.1. Also given is the number of HLW
canisters, each assumed to contain 3020 kg of glass [36], required for HLW oxides in each tank
waste. The singly washed AN-106 and BX-102 wastes would produce 576 and 72 HLW
canisters. The 576 canisters for AN-106 wastes is higher than for any other tank waste evaluated

 38

due to the amount of HLW oxides in that tank being more than twice that in any of the other
tanks. The 72 canisters for BX-102 wastes is lower than for any other tank waste evaluated due
to the high HLW oxides loading achieved for that tank. The number of days required to process
the HLW oxides in each feed is dependent on the glass production rate, the HLW oxide loading,
and the amount of waste oxides to be processed. The large mass of AN-106 HLW oxides results
in the longer time of 947 and 663 days with nominal fixed and optimized bubbling. Only 138 and
103 days would be required to process the BX-102 HLW oxides due to smaller inventory of
HLW oxides and a much higher waste loading. However, it should be noted that other factors
that could constrain throughput, such as feed preparation, off-gas system constraints, or canister
handling limitations, were not considered in this analysis.

 The importance of maintaining sufficiently high solids content in the HLW feed to the
vitrification facility was clearly demonstrated in these tests. Although the processing rates met or
exceeded the baseline rate for the WTP HLW vitrification system when bubbling was optimized,
glass production rates were significantly reduced as a result of the higher water content of the
feed. Identifying pretreatment strategies that maximize solids content would contribute to faster
processing rates.

 It should be noted that the AY-102, A-104, and A-R-AP106 supernates evaluated in
previous studies [2, 3] as well as the singly washed wastes in the present tests are relatively low
in sulfate and halides and therefore the primary benefit of washing on waste loading is via
removal of sodium. Consequently, excessive washing is counter-productive since sodium is a
required additive for HLW vitrification. This is particularly true for the A-104 supernate and the
two HLW compositions selected for the previous tests [4], which have relatively low dissolved
solids content. For supernates with high levels of sulfur or halides, more extensive washing may
be required, particularly in view of the fact that, unlike the WTP LAW melter systems, the WTP
HLW melter systems are not designed to tolerate high levels of these species. A single wash
cycle was sufficient for the AN-106 and BX-102 wastes evaluated in the present work to reduce
sulfur or halides to sufficiently low concentrations without any deleterious effects on the melter
system or the glass product. Together, these results illustrate that the washing strategy will
depend on the specific tank waste composition and will need to be evaluated on a tank-by-tank
basis.

6.2 Recommendations for Future Work

 The results of the testing presented herein and the earlier phases of testing [2-4]
demonstrate the viability of the WTP HLW direct feed strategy, which involves minimal or no
pretreatment. It is recommended that testing and assessment of these strategies be continued in
order to provide a solid basis for their evaluation and implementation in order to maximize the
cost and schedule benefits while minimizing technical risk. Further work that is recommended
for optimization of processing of WTP HLW direct feed is outlined below.

• Other WTP Direct Feed HLW Pretreatment Strategies: The present and previous testing [2-
 4] was based on a simple in-tank settle/decant washing strategy. Other pretreatment strategies
 could be evaluated to optimize the HLW direct feed approach at the WTP.

 39

• Other WTP Direct Feed HLW Tank Waste: Previous testing [2-4] was based on two HLW
 tank compositions from the Hanford tanks. The present testing was based on two Hanford
 HLW tank compositions selected for treatment as unwashed or singly washed. Subsequent
 work should extend these results to address the full range of HLW direct feed candidates
 expected to be processed at the WTP. In particular, HLW feeds for which the supernate is
 high in sulfate and/or halides need to be evaluated since the acceptable limits for these
 components in HLW glass are much lower than those for sodium. Also, identification of
 tanks requiring no or minimal pretreatment such as those evaluated in the present tests, could
 expedite the processing of HLW at the WTP.

• Glass Formulation: The results from the glass formulation work indicate that further
 improvements may be possible through continued glass formulation optimization using the
 results of the present work as a basis. In particular, the development of HLW formulations
 that have improved tolerance to species in the supernate can decrease the burden on the
 washing process.

• Salt Formation and Metal Corrosion: The potential for molten salt formation and increased
 metal corrosion (bubblers, thermowells, levels detectors, etc.) increases as the levels of
 halides and sulfates in the HLW feed increase. Consequently, for HLW feeds for which the
 supernate is high in sulfate and/or halides, these properties will determine the level of
 washing that is required to reduce these species to acceptable levels. Testing is needed to
 define these limits.

• Scale-Up Testing: As in the previous enhancement work for ORP, testing should be extended
 to larger-scale melter systems in order to address potential risks associated with scale-up,
 particularly with respect to processing rates. Testing could be conducted at the DM1200
 WTP HLW Pilot Melter scale (1.2 m2). Optimization of bubbling rate is a critical variable
 and therefore testing with bubblers in the prototypical orientation at larger scale is required to
 confirm these findings.

• Integrated System Testing: Testing on the DM1200 WTP HLW Pilot Melter system provides
 data from a one-third scale system with a prototypical feed delivery system and off-gas
 treatment train. Such testing is necessary to evaluate potential interactive effects on system
 operation arising from implementation of the direct feed HLW strategy and to provide data
 on the performance of each unit operation, input for flow-sheet models and regulatory
 requirements, and information about recycle streams.

• Throughput: A key risk area addressed in the present work relates to the strong dependence
 of glass production rates on waste composition, feed water content, and the extent to which
 shortfalls in processing rate can be mitigated through glass formulation design and
 optimization of bubbling. The strategy can be extended to evaluate other pretreatment
 options and corresponding HLW compositions.
