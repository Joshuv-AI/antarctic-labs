Safety and effectiveness of the Colovac anastomosis protection device compared with diverting ileostomy after low anterior resection: protocol for an international, multicentre, prospective, non-randomised comparative study (SAFE-3)
Authors: Clément Pastier, Patricia Sylla, Sang W Lee, Antonino Spinelli, Niels Komen, John Marks, Joseph Martz, Rebecca Rhee, Justin Maykel, Jorge Marcet, Quentin Denost, Karen Zaghiyan, Jennifer S Davids, Nivedh Paluvoi, David Hiller, Elisabeth C McLemore, Leon Maggiori, Andre D’hoore, Mehraneh D Jafari, Vitaliy Poylin, Christopher Jerome Buzas, Jean-Jaques Tuech, Jérémie H Lefèvre

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDiverting loop ileostomy (DLI) after low anterior resection for rectal cancer reduces the clinical consequences of anastomotic leakage but is associated with significant morbidity, impaired quality of life and the need for a second operation for stoma closure. Temporary intraluminal bypass devices have been developed to protect the anastomosis while avoiding DLI. However, high-quality prospective data comparing such devices with standard DLI remain limited. This study aims to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of the Colovac device compared with DLI.Methods and analysisSafeHeal Studies (SAFE-3), consisting of SafeHeal Standard of Care (Diverting Ileostomy) study (SH-SOC23) and SafeHeal Colovac Anastomosis Protection Device Evaluation Pivotal Study (SAFE-3CV), is an international, multicentre, prospective, non-randomised comparative study comprising two sequential cohorts: SH-SOC23 (standard-of-care diverting ileostomy control) and SAFE-3CV (Colovac anastomosis protection device). A total of 233 patients will be enrolled (SAFE-3CV n=108–120; SH-SOC23 n=132) across 25 centres in 4 countries. The primary endpoints are the rate of major complications at 9 months for safety and stoma avoidance at day 10 for effectiveness. Secondary outcomes include overall postoperative morbidity, stoma-related complications, reoperation rates, length of stay and stoma avoidance. Sample size calculation is based on non-inferiority assumptions. Data will be analysed using intention-to-treat principles, with propensity score adjustment to account for baseline differences between cohorts. Comparative analyses will include logistic regression and sensitivity analyses.Ethics and disseminationThe study was approved by ethics committees at the country level or at individual sites as per individual country requirements. An independent safety monitoring committee regularly reviews adverse events and safety data throughout the study. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and presentations at international meetings.Trial registration numbersSH-SOC23: NCT06152276 and SAFE-3CV: NCT07116668.Keywords: COLORECTAL CANCER, GASTROINTESTINAL FISTULAE, SURGICAL RESECTION

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Anastomotic leakage after low anterior resection occurs in approximately 5–15% of patients and remains a major cause of postoperative morbidity. Diverting loop ileostomy reduces the severity of septic complications but is associated with significant morbidity and permanent stoma rates reported in up to 15% of cases. Stoma closure requires additional surgery and carries its own complication risk. Intraluminal anastomotic protection devices have been proposed as alternatives, but evidence remains limited and mostly non-comparative. High-quality prospective data are lacking.

Introduction

Background and rationale

Colorectal surgery with anastomosis is performed for a broad range of indications, including mainly colorectal cancer along with diverticular disease, inflammatory bowel disease, trauma and other benign conditions. In fact, colorectal cancer remains the third most common cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, with approximately 1.8 million new cases and 860 000 deaths annually.1 2 Anastomotic leakage (AL) is the most feared complication after colorectal resection, defined as a defect at the anastomotic site causing communication between intraluminal and extraluminal compartments. Risk factors for AL include low anastomotic height, impaired perfusion and technical challenges related to total mesorectal excision.3,6 AL can lead to peritonitis, sepsis from pelvic or intra-abdominal collections and multiorgan failure; AL-associated mortality ranges around 3.9% at 90 days, largely driven by leaks requiring reintervention.3 7 Across colorectal resections, reported AL rates vary from 1.5% to 19%, with the highest rates after low anterior resection (LAR)4,6 up to 25% at 1-year follow-up.8 9

To mitigate the septic consequences of AL after low pelvic anastomoses, the current standard of care is routine creation of a protective diverting loop ileostomy (DLI). Both European and American guidelines recommend diversion when the anastomosis lies within 10 cm of the anal verge.10 11 Meta-analyses indicate that diverting stomas reduce symptomatic AL (OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.28 to 0.67) and reoperation (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.40 to 0.94) after LAR11 12 while evidence is mixed regarding whether diversion truly reduces the incidence of AL.12 13

However, DLI is associated with substantial morbidity. Early stoma-related complications occur in 10–60% of cases, including high-output stoma and dehydration (10–40%), acute kidney injury and maybe chronic kidney failure,14 15 stomal necrosis (7%) and peristomal dermatitis (10–73%).10 16 17 Late complications affect up to 25% of patients, most commonly prolapse and parastomal hernia.10 17 Living with a stoma negatively affects quality of life and reversal requires an additional operation under general anaesthesia, with a 10.3% major complication rate (including bowel obstruction 7% and incisional hernia 10.5%).18,20 Moreover, up to 15% of temporary ostomies are never reversed, resulting in a permanent stoma.21 22

Given these limitations, there is growing interest in alternative strategies that protect low colorectal anastomoses while avoiding routine faecal diversion or reserving stoma creation for selected high-risk patients.23,25 The SafeHeal Colovac device (CD) is a temporary endoluminal bypass system designed to protect the anastomosis during the critical early healing period by diverting faecal stream through the Colovac sheath, with stoma creation reserved for patients requiring additional time for anastomotic healing at the time of device removal. AL is frequently diagnosed around postoperative days (POD) 7–10, often preceded by clinical or biological changes, making early protection particularly relevant.925,27

The SafeHeal CD has evolved through successive generations. The initial SAFE-1 feasibility study (NCT03352570), conducted in 15 patients between 2017 and 2018, demonstrated that the first-generation CD could effectively protect colorectal anastomoses during the early postoperative period, with 80% successful protection (cleanliness of the colon), 67% stoma avoidance rate and 13% AL rate, comparable to reported AL rates following LAR with diversion.28 Building on these results, the SAFE-2019 study (NCT05180565), conducted in 23 patients between 2019 and 2022, evaluated the Colovac+, which incorporated a double-flared nitinol stent and a dual vacuum system (Redon vials), as well as a Vacuum Loss Alert System (VLAS) for continuous vacuum pressure monitoring. This iteration improved ease of use, with stoma avoidance rates of 73% and no major device-related complications.29

The SAFE-2 pivotal trial (NCT05010850) conducted in 33 patients in 2022, was the first randomised comparison between the Colovac+ with VLAS and standard DLI, confirming the feasibility and safety of the device, with a 70% rate of stoma avoidance in the randomised arm and 100% rate of clean anastomoses.30 A higher-than-expected migration rate (30%) prompted suspension of the trial to review the device’s technical performance. A root cause analysis demonstrated that the Redon vials of the VLAS system did not reliably maintain negative pressure, affecting the device’s anchoring. As a result, the study was closed and a Colovac-specific vacuum source (SmartVac) was developed alongside the new generation (Colovac 2) device with enhanced stent anchoring and improved introducer ergonomics. In the SAFE-2023 feasibility study (NCT06540807) conducted in 24 patients between 2024–2025 (unpublished data), the Colovac 2 design was associated with 100% successful implantation and retrieval, 8.3% clinically significant migration rate, 87.5% stoma avoidance rate at POD10 and 95.8% rate of clean anastomosis.

The SafeHeal Studies (SAFE-3), consisting of SafeHeal Standard of Care (Diverting Ileostomy) study (SH-SOC23) and SafeHeal Colovac Anastomosis Protection Device Evaluation Pivotal Study (SAFE-3CV), consists of a comparative non-randomised trial of two prospective, conducted sequentially, cohorts of patients undergoing LAR and enrolled into the control DLI arm (SH-SOC23 Study; protocol V.4.0 as of 9 October 2025) or the CD arm (SAFE-3CV; protocol V.2.0 as of 11 August 2025). Both trials will enrol patients across the same study sites using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria, and outcomes will be compared using a propensity score model (PSM) based on pre-specified criteria.

Methods and analysis

Study design

SAFE-3 includes two prospective, non-randomised multicentre cohorts: the SH-SOC23 control group treated with DLI and the SAFE-3CV Investigational Device Exemption pivotal study enrolling LAR patients treated with Colovac. Both datasets will be analysed under a unified statistical plan. To limit bias, both cohorts are prospectively enrolled at the same sites with aligned eligibility criteria. A prespecified analysis plan using propensity score methods will address non-randomised treatment allocation and estimate the average treatment effect for major complications. The sequential design requires completion of control enrolment before initiating the investigational cohort, reducing selection bias. All serious adverse events, device-related events and anastomotic leaks will undergo independent Clinical Events Committee (CEC) adjudication to ensure objective assessment. The content of all this protocol is described according to (1) the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials checklist (online supplemental table S1), (2) the relevant STROBE statements (including items applicable to observational studies and propensity score analyses) (online supplemental table S2) and (3) the TIDieR checklist (online supplemental table S3).

Participating centres

The study is designed to enrol a minimum of 245 subjects, with a maximum of 120 subjects in the SAFE-3CV group (Colovac cohort) and at least 125 subjects in the SH-SOC23 group (DLI cohort). No site may enrol more than 20% of the total study enrolment and at least 50% of participants will be enrolled at US centres. Overall, up to 25 centres are expected to participate, including up to 20 sites in the USA and up to 10 sites in Europe. The list of participating centres is available in online supplemental table S4.

Patient eligibility

Potential participants are patients scheduled to undergo sphincter-preserving LAR with a planned DLI for rectal adenocarcinoma who will be approached for participation in the study. The anastomosis should be within 10 cm of the anal verge, including low and very low coloanal anastomosis. LAR with total mesorectal excision can be performed by a laparoscopic, robotic, transanal, open or hybrid approach, as per the surgeon’s preference. Patients who receive neoadjuvant chemoradiation are also eligible.

Interventions and treatment

Device overview

The Colovac system consists of a sterile, single-use intraluminal device preloaded in an introducer and connected to the external SmartVac vacuum unit (figure 1A,B). The CE-marked device includes a self-expanding nitinol stent with a vacuum chamber and a flexible sheath that diverts faecal flow to protect the anastomosis. SmartVac maintains continuous negative pressure to anchor the stent, providing automatic regulation of the vacuum and alerts (figure 1F). The introducer enables endoscopic deployment above the sacral promontory, where the stent expands and the sheath forms a temporary bypass during early healing (figure 1C–E).

The Colovac device. (A) The Colovac device is composed of: an anchor consisting of a covered stent delimiting an air-tight and water-tight volume (ie, vacuum chamber) in which a vacuum is pulled upon delivery in the colon through two vacuum tubes (stent) and, a flexible cylindrical sheath attached to the anchor, covering the anastomosis, and with appropriate length so that it protrudes outside the patient’s anus (sheath). (B) Colovac device implant dimensions. (C) Introducer pre-loaded with the Colovac device. (D) Introducer handle during placement of stent (top—stent completely inside introducer; middle—stent partially deployed; bottom—stent fully deployed). (E) Location of the device above the sacral promontory junction. (F) The SmartVac is a single-patient use, non-sterile active device. The device attaches directly to the Colovac device and supplies vacuum. It also monitors the vacuum level at the anchoring site, turning on the onboard vacuum pump if the measured vacuum level is outside the predetermined acceptable range. It provides caregivers with visual and auditory alerts in the event of the following: vacuum leak detected; tubing occlusion detected; fluid collection canister full (replacement needed); low battery (replacement needed).

The Colovac and SmartVac Devices received CE mark per European Union Medical Devices Regulation 2017/745 in 2025. In the USA, the Colovac and SmartVac Devices are investigational devices limited by law to investigational use only.

Practical use

After creation of the anastomosis in the Colovac cohort, the device is inserted transanally with an introducer, positioned 10–15 cm above the sacral promontory, deployed and connected to SmartVac; a baseline X-ray is obtained on day 0–1 (figure 1D,F). The main innovation is the SmartVac system—a single-patient active vacuum unit that maintains stable negative pressure, provides safety alerts and supports updated Colovac design features including a 360° vacuum chamber, hourglass stent and widened sealing rings (figure 1B).30 SmartVac is portable, enabling patient mobility and showering (figure 1F).

Preoperative assessments

Before surgery, all participants undergo standardised preoperative evaluation and staging, including CT or MRI with pseudonymised imaging reviewed independently to assess colon diameter proximal to the splenic flexure. Medical history, physical examination, demographics, smoking status, tumour characteristics, prior treatments and pregnancy testing (when applicable) are documented. Preoperative functional and quality-of-life assessments include ECOG Performance Status and EuroQol 5 Dimensions, 5 Levels.

Postoperative course with no anastomotic complications

Patients remain hospitalised until Colovac retrieval around POD10, following standardised postoperative care including dietary restrictions (low residue diet), clinical and laboratory monitoring and device checks two times per day. Baseline imaging is performed early, and a POD9 CT scan determines whether planned POD10 (−1/+2 days) endoscopic retrieval proceeds or whether early retrieval with possible DLI creation is required in case of anastomotic leak. Before stent retrieval, the endoscopy assesses the bowel cleanliness and detects AL. Follow-up continues for all patients through 9 months, with an endoscopic evaluation of the anastomosis and anchoring site at the 9-month visit (table 1 and figure 2).

Sample size

For the primary safety endpoint, sample size calculations assuming a 10% major complication rate in controls, a non-inferiority margin of 0.125 based on clinical judgement as the maximum additional risk of major complications that would be considered acceptable given the expected benefit of avoiding diverting ileostomy and its downstream morbidity, one-sided α=0.025 and 80% power indicated a need for 91 patients per group. Accounting for 15% PSM trimming and 15% attrition, the final target enrolment was set at 233 subjects (SAFE-3CV/Colovac cohort: n=108–120; SH-SOC23 control cohort: n=132) ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT07116668 and NCT06152276, respectively). For the primary effectiveness endpoint, the study is powered to confirm ≥50% ostomy avoidance, with ≥85% power even if the true rate is as low as 65%.

Recruitment for the standard-of-care control cohort (SH-SOC23; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06152276) began on 1 January 2024 and reached primary completion on 29 January 2026. Recruitment for the Colovac cohort (SAFE-3CV; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT07116668) began on 8 January 2026. Enrolment for SAFE-3CV is expected to be completed by late 2026.

Study endpoints

Primary endpoints

The primary safety endpoint of SAFE-3 is to determine whether the CD demonstrates a positive benefit–risk profile by assessing non-inferiority with respect to rate of major complications through 9 months relative to the control arm, using a predefined margin and a sequential design to control type I error and limit selection bias. Major complications are defined as device-related (including placement and retrieval) or ostomy-related (including creation and reversal) adverse events, and classified as (1) Clavien-Dindo Severity Grade III, IV or V,32 (2) adjudicated by the CEC as severe, life-threatening, or fatal or requiring hospital readmission or extended hospitalisation or (3) leading to failure to reverse the stoma within 9 months of surgery, acknowledging that closure after LAR is frequently delayed beyond 3 months, particularly in patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy.31 33 Reasons for delayed or absent stoma reversal will be prospectively collected (eg, adjuvant chemotherapy, postoperative complications, anastomotic concerns, comorbidity, organisational/logistical constraints or patient preference). Each component of the composite outcome will be reported separately to facilitate clinical interpretation.

The primary effectiveness endpoint is the ostomy avoidance rate at day 10, comparing the lower bound of the 95% CI with a predefined performance goal. Early Colovac removal with conversion to DLI will be treated as a failure of the stoma-avoidance strategy for effectiveness endpoints; such cases will remain analysed within the SAFE-3CV cohort in intention-to-treat (primary) cohort.

Secondary endpoints

Secondary safety endpoints include anastomotic integrity (based on CT scan and endoscopic evaluation on POD9 and POD10, respectively), mucosal appearance at device retrieval, AL rate, clinically significant AL rate at 9 months,34 Comprehensive Complication Index,35 length of stay, stoma reversal failure rate and rate of early device retrieval due to AL with diversion failure. Effectiveness endpoints include rate of successful faecal diversion, significant device migrations and QoL (EQ-5D, Stoma QoL).36 37 Usability endpoints assess patient tolerability and investigators’ and staff’s ease of device insertion, retrieval and SmartVac use.

Data adjudication

The CEC is an independent panel of six external physicians who are not involved in the study or employed by SafeHeal. Following a predefined charter, the CEC objectively reviews all serious adverse events, device-related events and anastomotic leaks, assessing severity, seriousness and relatedness to surgery, stoma or device. It also classifies major complications and adjudicates anastomotic leak grades according to International Study Group of Rectal Cancer criteria.34 Independent radiology and endoscopy specialists evaluate anastomotic healing, mucosal integrity, Boston Bowel Preparation Scale and baseline colon diameter. All assessments follow standardised procedures to ensure consistent and unbiased event classification. Additionally, an independent data safety management board (DSMB) of at least three members monitors study progress, safety data and key performance outcomes, providing recommendations to the sponsor per its charter. The DSMB will review cumulative safety data at predefined intervals and may recommend protocol modification, temporary enrolment pause or termination in the event of an unacceptable rate or severity of serious device-related adverse events (including clinically significant migration requiring unplanned intervention).

Statistical analysis

The non-inferiority margin for the primary safety endpoint was prespecified as an absolute risk difference of 12.5%; non-inferiority will be concluded if the upper bound of the one-sided 97.5% CI for the risk difference (Colovac minus control) is below 12.5%. To address baseline imbalances inherent to this non-randomised design, propensity score methods (including quintile subclassification and matching/weighting as appropriate) will be used to improve covariate balance and estimate the average treatment effect on the treated—that is, how major complication rates in the Colovac cohort would have differed under standard care. The propensity score will prioritise clinically relevant confounders and determinants of major complications, including patient factors (sex, age, body mass index, diabetes, smoking, alcohol use, cardiovascular history), oncologic treatment and disease characteristics (neoadjuvant therapy, tumour stage/size), and operative factors (surgical approach, anastomotic level and configuration/technique). Prespecified subgroup analyses will assess heterogeneity by anastomotic height (distance from the anal verge; ≤5 cm vs 5–10 cm). Reversal status will be analysed at the primary 9-month time point. Standard LAR techniques (double-stapled or Transanal Transection and Single-Stapled/single-stapled; stapled or handsewn; including Transanal Total Mesorectal Excision where applicable) are permitted.

Multiple mitigations have been implemented to reduce any potential bias inherent in a non-randomised study design, including that both studies are conducted at the same centres with the same inclusion/exclusion criteria, with a prespecified analysis plan, and using propensity score methods to account for the non-randomised treatment group. Subject selection bias is limited due to the sequential nature of this study, and the SAFE-3CV study commenced enrolment before completion of the last visit in SH-SOC23. Moreover, safety events and imaging are reviewed by an independent CEC, ensuring an objective and uniform assessment across studies.

In case of missing data, multiple imputation (MI) will be used to produce the primary Average Treatment effect on the Treated (ATT) estimate of the impact of Colovac on the rate of major complications.

For the primary analysis, a participant is considered to be lost to follow-up (LTF) if they are not followed for the full 9-month safety period and are not known to have experienced a major complication prior to being LTF. To account for this missing data, each LTF subject will have their missing data (eg, whether they experienced a major adverse event between the last observed follow-up and month 9) imputed using an MI approach. The specific MI approach depends on whether the Performance Status subclass design is used, or the subject specific ATT weighted approach is used.

Discussion

The present study aims to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of a temporary intraluminal bypass device (Colovac) designed to protect low colorectal anastomoses following LAR relative to standard of care involving DLI. The trial has been structured as a non-inferiority analysis, with the primary objective of demonstrating that Colovac achieves comparable major complication rates while potentially avoiding the morbidity associated with stoma creation and reversal.

The concept of temporary intraluminal bypass to protect a fresh colorectal anastomosis is not new. As early as 1984, Ravo and Ger first described the use of a synthetic intraluminal tube introduced intraoperatively to divert faecal stream and reduce the risk of leakage—an early attempt that laid the foundation for modern anastomotic protection devices.38 Since then, several generations of devices have sought to reproduce this protective effect through less invasive and more standardised means, though few have achieved broad clinical adoption.

Among the most studied devices, the C-Seal was a biodegradable, flexible intraluminal sheath stapled directly to the proximal edge of the anastomosis using a standard circular stapler.39 40 Despite promising early reports, the randomised controlled trial by Bakker et al revealed a higher rate of AL in the C-Seal group compared with standard care. Several factors may explain this outcome: unlike other devices, the C-Seal provided immediate diversion at the level of the anastomosis, not upstream, potentially exposing the suture line to faecal content during the critical early healing phase. The device was designed to biodegrade and be expelled after approximately 2 weeks, but these mechanical and anatomical limitations may have contributed to its failure in the trial, despite encouraging preliminary studies.

Another notable approach was evaluated in a Korean randomised controlled trial, which investigated a two-part faecal diverting device composed of a silicone intraluminal tube anchored by an intra-abdominal resorbable prosthetic band placed proximal to the anastomosis.41 While the study reported shorter hospital stays and lower overall costs compared with conventional stoma diversion, the need for intra-abdominal placement of a resorbable fixation band made the technique less generalisable and was associated with cases of colonic erosion. In addition, the study’s small sample size (n=54) and single-centre design limit the robustness of its conclusions.

In this context, the CD developed by Khosrovani et al42 represents the most recent and refined iteration of the intraluminal bypass concept initially pioneered by Ravo.38 The Colovac system offers a fully endoluminal, endoscopically retrievable and minimally invasive solution that temporarily isolates the anastomosis from the faecal stream, without the need for abdominal dissection or stoma formation. Its design allows early retrieval once anastomotic healing is confirmed, combining the physiological protection of a diversion with the benefits of a stoma-free recovery.

Beyond the technological innovation, this study is expected to generate high-quality, prospective, multicentre data on patients undergoing LAR, both with and without protective ostomy, thereby reflecting real-world surgical practice. This is particularly relevant given the evolving landscape of rectal cancer management, where total neoadjuvant therapy—as demonstrated in the Rectal cancer And Preoperative Induction therapy followed by Dedicated Operation (RAPIDO) and Partenariat de Recherche en Oncologie DIGEstive 23, (PRODIGE 23)—has replaced adjuvant therapy and improved disease-free survival.43,45 However, the effect of total neoadjuvant therapy on early anastomotic healing and leak risk remains poorly understood. The present study will thus provide crucial insight into outcomes under contemporary oncologic protocols, including minimally invasive and robotic LAR approaches.

Moreover, most available evidence on anastomotic healing and leakage derives from assessments performed after stoma reversal (>3 months post-LAR). A recent presentation at the Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons highlighted that the rate of anastomotic complications beyond 30 days following LAR was considerably higher than previously reported (+5%).46 The current study will thus generate robust early postoperative data on anastomotic complications and leaks associated with LAR.

Crucially, the study’s rigorous methodological design will allow for an unprecedented evaluation of early anastomotic healing dynamics. A systematic endoscopic examination will be performed on POD10 (−1/+2 days) to directly visualise the anastomosis and assess mucosal integrity. This standardised approach will enable the establishment of a clear correlation between anatomical dehiscence and clinical or radiological manifestations, bridging the current gap between structural and functional leak detection. These data may ultimately inform earlier clinical decision-making, including timely stoma reversal or targeted management of anastomotic complications. Furthermore, time to reversal may be influenced by non-surgical factors—particularly adjuvant chemotherapy—and by healthcare system constraints; we therefore report reasons for non-reversal. Because anastomotic height is a key determinant of leak risk and may be variably assessed across centres (and influenced by neoadjuvant treatment), we used a ≤10 cm eligibility threshold to ensure inclusion of truly low anastomoses while reporting results stratified by height (<5 cm vs 5–10 cm).

Although randomised trials (including our prior SAFE-2 study) have been conducted, SAFE-3 was deliberately designed as a prospective, sequential two-cohort study to evaluate the current Colovac strategy in a pragmatic multinational roll-out after iterative refinements, to confirm safety/feasibility and reproducibility across centres and to derive robust real-world event rates and effect-size estimates, in a context where randomisation between diverting ileostomy and a stoma-avoidance strategy may face substantial crossover challenges with patients and clinicians preferring a potentially stoma-free option.

Given the non-randomised design, several safeguards have been implemented to ensure validity and reproducibility. Sequential enrolment, using the same study sites and inclusion/exclusion criteria, and objective primary endpoints will minimise selection and assessment bias. The primary safety endpoint (major complications) will be independently adjudicated by a CEC according to a predefined charter, while a predefined Statistical Analysis Plan will be finalised prior to data analysis. The same centres will participate in both the SH-SOC23 control cohort and the SAFE-3CV Colovac study, with propensity score adjustment used to account for baseline imbalances. Independent monitoring and source data verification will ensure compliance and data integrity throughout.

This study has inherent methodological limitations. First, as a non-randomised comparison, it remains susceptible to selection bias and residual confounding, although selection bias is limited by ensuring all eligible patients who meet inclusion/exclusion criteria are screened for the study and approached for participation. Propensity-score methods can balance measured covariates but cannot account for unmeasured factors, although covariates were thoughtfully selected. Second, the sequential cohort design may be affected by temporal trends in perioperative care, centre experience and practice patterns, potentially biasing between-cohort comparisons, although this is minimised with the limited time that occurs between the completion of the SH-SOC23 enrolment and the commencement of SAFE-3CV.

Ultimately, this study is designed not only to test non-inferiority of Colovac relative to standard care but also to redefine the paradigm of anastomotic protection in rectal surgery. Should the device demonstrate safety comparable to DLI with a substantial ostomy avoidance, it could mark a significant step forward toward stoma-free recovery in selected patients after LAR.
