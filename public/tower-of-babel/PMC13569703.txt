Eustachian Tube Opening in Children With and Without Cleft Palate and Association With Otitis Media
Authors: Kawin Atipas, Kitirat Ungkanont, Archwin Tanphaichitr, Vannipa Vathanophas, Thanakrit Wannarong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo characterize morphology and opening pattern of the Eustachian tube (ET) in children with and without cleft palate (CP), and to examine the associations between endoscopic findings and otologic outcomes.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study included children aged 4–12 years with CP (n = 25) and without CP (n = 25) who underwent transnasal endoscopy for clinical indications. Endoscopic evaluation assessed adenoid, torus tubarius, tubal tonsil, discharge at the opening, ET opening visibility and movement, and the Eustachian Tube Inflammation Scale (ETIS). Findings were compared between groups using the chi‐square or Fisher's exact test. Associations between endoscopic findings and middle ear outcomes were analyzed using Spearman's rank correlation.ResultsTorus tubarius anomalies were more frequent in children with CP than in non‐CP children (52% vs. 20%, p = 0.02), with all anomalies involving the posterior lip of the torus tubarius. Copious discharge at the ET opening was observed more frequently in children without CP (p = 0.04). Adenoid size, dynamic ET opening visibility, and ETIS grades showed significant correlations with both middle ear status and the rate of myringotomy in children with CP, whereas discharge‐related findings were more associated with middle ear status in children without CP.ConclusionTransnasal endoscopy provides important insights into ET opening in children with CP, in whom torus tubarius anomalies are common. Associations between adenoid size, ET lumen visibility, and middle ear disease highlighted the potential role of ET opening assessment for individualized management in this population.Level of Evidence3.Keywords: cleft palate, Eustachian tube, myringotomy, otitis media, transnasal endoscopy

Introduction

The Eustachian tube (ET) connects the nasopharynx to the middle ear and functions in pressure regulation, protection, and clearance of secretions. ET dysfunction (ETD) may result in middle ear diseases, including acute otitis media (AOM), otitis media with effusion (OME), and chronic otitis media. Its pathophysiology involves infection, inflammation, mechanical obstruction from hypertrophic adenoid tissue or tubal tonsil, as well as structural or functional abnormalities, particularly in cleft palate (CP) and other craniofacial anomalies.

Various diagnostic tests have been developed to evaluate ETD. Transnasal endoscopy enables direct visualization of the ET, assessment of opening dynamics, and endotype‐based classification of dilatory ETD [1, 2]. ETD endotypes include stricture ETD (ETD‐S) due to structural abnormalities; restrictive ETD (ETD‐R) associated with adenoid hypertrophy or tubal tonsil enlargement; inflammatory ETD (ETD‐I) characterized by secretion obstructing the ET orifice and inflamed mucosa of the torus tubarius and the ET lumen; and muscular ETD (ETD‐M) related to impaired active opening function [2].

Children with CP have inherent anatomical and functional ET abnormalities that predispose them to middle ear disease. Previously reported findings include a reduced insertion ratio of the tensor veli palatini (TVP) muscle to the tubal cartilage, an increased cartilage–TVP angle, shortened ET length, increased cartilage cell density, and reduced luminal curvature [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Children with CP experienced higher cumulative incidences of OME, reaching 81.1% by 2 years of age [10], whereas the corresponding incidence in children without CP was 60% [11]. Although this association is well established, endoscopic data on ET opening remain limited. Yamashita et al. [12] reported abnormal ET opening patterns in two children with CP, including levator veli palatini misdirection and a floppy ET orifice. These observations suggest that endoscopic evaluation may reveal different ET opening patterns in children with CP, warranting further investigation and classification.

The primary objective of this study was to classify ET morphology and opening patterns in children with CP and compare these findings with those in children without CP. The secondary objective was to investigate the association between the endoscopic ET opening patterns and middle ear status assessed by otoscopy, otitis event rate, and myringotomy rate. These findings may be useful for planning individualized management strategies for otitis media in children with CP based on ETD endotypes, with the aim of improving management outcomes.

Materials and Methods

This cross‐sectional study included children aged 4–12 years with and without CP who attended the otorhinolaryngology outpatient clinic. Eligible participants had at least one of the following indications for transnasal endoscopy, including obstructive sleep–disordered breathing (OSDB), sinonasal diseases, or otitis media. Children with anatomical abnormalities of the nasal cavity, including tumors, choanal stenosis or atresia, nasal pyriform aperture stenosis, or arhinia, were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria were bilateral aural atresia and conditions associated with an increased bleeding risk. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institution (COA Si 455/2024). Informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians, and assent was obtained from children older than 7 years. The research was conducted in accordance with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Helsinki Declaration).

The sample size was calculated based on the study of Kuo et al. [13], who reported that the cumulative incidence of OME at 1 year of age was approximately 50% in children without CP and 90% in children with CP. Based on a two‐sided significance level of 0.05 and a power of 80%, we estimated the necessary sample size as 50 participants, with 25 children in each group.

After the informed consent was obtained, the middle ear status was assessed by otoscopy and classified into four ordinal categories: (1) normal tympanic membrane (TM) without middle ear effusion; (2) presence of a tympanostomy tube without otorrhea or TM perforation; (3) TM retraction; and (4) AOM, OME, or tube with otorrhea.

Topical 1% lidocaine with 0.025% or 0.05% oxymetazoline, adjusted according to age, was applied to the nasal cavity to reduce discomfort and mucosal congestion. Younger children were examined seated on a caregiver's lap with gentle support, with additional head stabilization by a nurse when needed, whereas older children sat independently. No additional restraint or sedation was used. Transnasal endoscopy was performed using a 2.4‐mm fiberoptic nasopharyngoscope (Pentax, Japan) connected to a MGB Aristro‐V3 camera system. The endoscope was advanced along the nasal cavity at the level of the inferior turbinate. After passing its posterior end, the adenoid was assessed, and the scope was angled approximately 45° to visualize the ET opening and surrounding structures. Participants were instructed to swallow to assess dynamic ET opening. Bilateral examinations were performed, and recordings were reviewed for ET morphology and opening dynamics.

The adenoid was assessed for the attachment site, choanal obstruction, and inflammatory status. Attachment was categorized as not visible, confined to the nasopharyngeal roof, or attached to the torus tubarius, vomer, or soft palate [14]. Size was graded according to the degree of choanal obstruction as not visible, obstruction of up to one‐third of the choana, between one‐third to two‐thirds of the choana and greater than two thirds, or complete choanal obstruction [15]. Inflammation was classified as no inflammation, erythema without discharge, or erythema with discharge (Figure S1).

The torus tubarius was evaluated for edema, erythema, and anatomical abnormalities. Edema and erythema were graded as none, partial, or marked (Figures S2 and S3). Anatomy of the torus tubarius was categorized as normal shape and direction, misdirection, abnormal shape, abnormal shape with misdirection, or incomplete torus tubarius (Figure 1).

Anatomy of the torus tubarius: (A) normal shape and direction; (B) abnormal torus tubarius, including (I) misdirection, (II) abnormal shape with horizontal separation (arrow) of the posterior lip at different levels, (III) abnormal shape with vertical separation (arrow) of the posterior lip, (IV) combined abnormal shape and misdirection, and (V) incomplete torus.

Tubal tonsil and its relationship to the ET lumen were classified as absent, present without luminal involvement, present with partial luminal involvement, and present with complete luminal obstruction (Figure S4). Discharge at the pharyngeal opening of the ET was assessed for characteristics (absent, clear, mucoid, purulent) and quantity (none, scant, copious).

Dynamic ET function was assessed during swallowing. Visibility of ET opening was recorded as fully visible, partially visible, or not visible (Figure 2). ET opening movement was classified as normal, limited, or absent. Movement was defined as a sequential process from palatal elevation during swallowing, followed by rotation of the posterior lip of the ET cartilage, lateral excursion of the lateral wall, and subsequent dilation of the ET lumen [2, 16]. Overall ET inflammation was graded using the Eustachian tube inflammation scale (ETIS) [17, 18, 19]. Grade I indicates a fully open ET with normal mucosa; Grade II, a fully open ET with mild inflammatory changes; Grade III, a partially open ET with moderate inflammatory changes; and Grade IV, no visible ET opening with severe inflammatory changes (Figure 3).

Visibility of dynamic Eustachian tube opening: (A) fully visible, (B) partially visible, (C) not visible.

Eustachian inflammation scale: (A) I = full open, no inflammatory mucosa, (B) II = full open, mild inflammatory mucosa, (C) III = partial open, moderate inflammatory mucosa, (D) IV = no open, severe inflammatory mucosa.

Clinical data were obtained from medical records, including otologic history, adenoidectomy, and tympanostomy tube insertion. In children with CP, history of palatoplasty was reviewed, including age at palatoplasty, cleft side, Veau classification [20], palatal gap, technique of palatoplasty, and postoperative complications.

All endoscopic findings were independently evaluated by two experienced otolaryngologists who were blinded to the clinical data. Interrater reliability was assessed using Cohen's kappa coefficient, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. Endoscopic findings were compared between children with and without CP. Correlations between endoscopic findings and current middle ear status, rate of myringotomy, and otologic events were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), with a two‐sided p value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Fifty‐six children underwent transnasal endoscopic examination without the use of restraint or sedation. Six were excluded: three due to refusal or excessive crying, two due to inadequate image quality, and one due to an inability to swallow. Fifty children (25 with CP and 25 without CP) were included, and bilateral evaluation was successfully achieved in all cases. Demographic data and clinical history are presented in Table 1. The mean age was 7.5 ± 2.3 years in the CP group and 8.6 ± 2.2 years in the non‐CP group, with no significant differences in age or sex. Lower respiratory disease and developmental delay were more common in children with CP (p = 0.01 and p = 0.02, respectively). History of adenoidectomy was uncommon and did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.10). The duration of follow‐up was significantly longer in the CP group compared with the non‐CP group (median 80.7 vs. 10.3 months, p < 0.001).

A history of myringotomy with tympanostomy tube insertion was present in 96% of children with CP, compared with 8% in the non‐CP group (p < 0.001). Annual rates of myringotomy and otitis events were also higher in the CP group (0.3 and 1.1 events per person‐year, respectively; both p < 0.001). Children with CP had a higher prevalence of active middle ear disease, including AOM, OME, or otorrhea through tubes (24%).

Abnormal torus tubarius morphology was significantly more common in children with CP than in non‐CP children (52% vs. 20%, p = 0.02). Figure 1A,B illustrate normal and abnormal torus tubarius observed in children with CP. All abnormalities involved the posterior lip of the torus tubarius. Misdirection of the ET, characterized by a more vertically oriented posterior lip compared with normal anatomy, was identified in 12% of children with CP. Abnormal ET shape was observed in 28% of children with CP and classified into two patterns: horizontal separation of the posterior lip at different levels (6 cases, 24%; Figure 1B‐II) and vertical separation of the posterior lip at the distal portion (1 case, 4%; Figure 1B‐III). Combined abnormal shape and misdirection were observed in 8% of cases, all representing horizontal separation. Absence of the posterior lip was identified in one child (4%).

Other endoscopic parameters, including tubal tonsil enlargement, dynamic ET opening visibility, ETIS grading, and discharge characteristics did not differ significantly between groups. However, causes of limitation of the ET opening differed. Ineffective muscular function of the ET was more common in children with CP (6 cases, 24%) than in those without CP (1 case, 4%), whereas adenoid or tubal tonsil impingement was more frequent in children without CP (60%) than in children with CP (44%).

Correlations between endoscopic findings and otologic outcomes are shown in Table 4. The adenoid size was correlated with abnormal middle ear status in children with CP (Spearman's rank correlation coefficient [r] = 0.46, p = 0.02). In children without CP, discharge characteristics (r = 0.51, p = 0.01) and discharge amount (r = 0.45, p = 0.03) at the ET opening were significantly correlated with abnormal middle ear status.

In children with CP, the rate of myringotomy per year showed significant correlations with adenoid size (r = 0.49, p = 0.01), visibility of dynamic ET opening (r = 0.42, p = 0.04), and the ETIS (r = 0.42, p = 0.04). In contrast, no endoscopic findings were significantly associated with the rate of otitis events per follow‐up year in children with CP.

Discussion

ETD plays an important role in the pathophysiology of otitis media, especially in children with CP. We evaluated ET morphology and opening patterns at the nasopharynx using transnasal endoscopy and investigated the associations among ET opening, adenoid findings, and middle ear status. The ETD endotype classification of Alper et al. [2] was used to describe the endoscopic findings. Structural abnormalities of the torus tubarius (ETD‐S) were a distinguished feature in children with CP. The classification of posterior lip abnormalities in this study added the details and provided the insight to understand the ETD. Most cases demonstrated horizontal separation of the posterior lip at different levels (8 cases, 32%), whereas vertical separation at the distal portion was less common (1 case, 4%). The findings of abnormal shape, misdirection, or combined deformities of the posterior lip are consistent with previous histologic studies demonstrating abnormal ET cartilage configuration and altered muscular orientation in children with CP [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. A previous study reported the endoscopic findings of misdirection and abnormal morphology in children with CP [12]. Such abnormalities are known to compromise active ET opening during swallowing, resulting in ineffective middle ear ventilation.

Inflammatory (ETD‐I) findings, including adenoid inflammation, torus tubarius edema and erythema, ETIS grades, and restrictive (ETD‐R) findings, including adenoid and tubal tonsil enlargement, were not significantly different between children with and without CP. The amount of discharge at the ET opening was significantly greater in children without CP, reflecting more prevalence of nasopharyngeal inflammation. No significant differences were observed in dynamic ET opening visibility between groups, as these dynamic parameters may be influenced by inflammation of the ET [2]. Notably, muscular dysfunction (ETD‐M) appeared to be a more common contributor to limited ET movement in children with CP.

Our findings suggested the different underlying causes of otitis media in children with and without CP. Adenoid size correlated with both current middle ear status and myringotomy rate in children with CP. Adenoid hypertrophy may contribute to middle ear disease through mechanical ET obstruction, impaired active ET opening, or biofilm‐related bacterial reservoir formation [21, 22]. In addition, adenoid obstruction causes negative nasopharyngeal pressure transmitted to the middle ear. Dynamic ET opening visibility and ETIS grades were significantly associated with myringotomy rate in children with CP. ET lumen visibility may be influenced by inflammatory changes (ETD‐I), adenoid or tubal tonsil impingement (ETD‐R), muscular dysfunction (ETD‐M), or structural abnormalities of the ET (ETD‐S). Ineffective ET opening may impair middle ear pressure regulation, contributing to recurrent OME and repeated myringotomy.

Transnasal endoscopy is an informative tool for assessing ET function, with substantial item‐specific interrater agreement. Endoscopy is available in most otolaryngology clinics and provides direct visualization of the ET opening [2, 23, 24, 25]. Simultaneous evaluation of both structural and functional components of ET facilitates the classification of ETD endotypes. When integrated with clinical history and otoscopic findings, transnasal endoscopy can improve understanding of ET pathophysiology and support endotype‐directed management strategies. In children with CP, endoscopic assessment of the ET and related structures may help identify patients at higher risk for persistent middle ear disease, repeated tympanostomy tube placement, or the need for prolonged otologic surveillance.

This study has several limitations. The sample size was calculated based on the primary objective of characterizing ET morphology and endoscopic opening patterns and may be underpowered for secondary analyses. Healthy children without otologic or nasopharyngeal disease were not included, as all participants underwent transnasal endoscopy for clinical indications (e.g., otitis media, OSDB, or sinonasal disease), which may explain the similar inflammatory findings between groups. In addition, endoscopic evaluation was performed at a single time point without follow‐up, and findings may change after resolution of inflammation. Patient cooperation may have affected both the feasibility and quality of the examination. Some children were unable to complete the procedure due to refusal or excessive crying, and certain recordings were excluded because of poor visualization or inability to perform a swallowing maneuver. In addition, complete visualization of all nasopharyngeal and ET structures was not achievable in all cases, particularly in younger or less cooperative children, which may have influenced both anatomical and dynamic assessments. The detail measurement of the size and angle of the ET opening during the dynamic assessment could not be done in this setup. Future study is required.

Another limitation is the subjective nature of several semiquantitative, observer‐dependent endoscopic variables. Although interrater agreement was high, variability in individual parameters may still exist. Furthermore, dynamic findings may be influenced by patient cooperation, swallowing performance, and endoscope positioning. Finally, selecting the worse side for analysis may increase susceptibility to observer‐dependent bias.

This study provided the detailed endoscopic classification of the ET morphology and dynamic opening characteristics in children with and without CP. Our findings should suggest the potential contributors to ETD and middle ear disease in this population. The association between the ET findings and the middle ear status should be useful for long‐term management and prognostic counseling.

Conclusion

Endoscopic findings demonstrated that children with CP had distinct features of structural abnormalities of the torus tubarius, which were different from those of children without CP. Middle ear diseases in children with CP had an association with the adenoid size and ET opening function, whereas the amount and quality of discharge at the torus tubarius had more effect on the middle ear status of the children without CP. Endoscopic evaluation provided valuable insights into both structural and functional aspects of the ET in children with CP who are at increased risk for recurrent otologic diseases. Transnasal endoscopic assessment of the ET may support individualized management strategies and improve long‐term care of otitis media in children with CP and otitis‐prone children.
