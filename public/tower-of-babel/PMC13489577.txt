Diet during Pregnancy and Allergy Onset in the Childhood: A Systematic Literature Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Lini Anisfatus Sholihah, Irvan Adhin Cholilie, Desty Muzarofatus Sholikhah, Fista Utami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Maternal diet during pregnancy may influence childhood allergy risk. Our objective is to study the relationship between maternal diet during pregnancy and childhood’s allergy onset.Methods:We systematically search SCOPUS and PubMed (2014 to 2024) for randomized controlled trials (RCTs), case control, and cohorts investigating maternal diet during pregnancy and childhood allergy outcomes. The study protocol was registered in the PROSPERO (CRD42024570531). The evidence was critically appraised using Newcastle Ottawa Scale for case-control and cohort studies and Revised Cochrane risk of bias for cluster randomized trials (RoB2) tool for RCTs by two independent reviewers. Random-effects meta-analyses were performed to pool effect estimates, and heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic.Results:A total of 41 studies (37 cohorts, 1 case-control, and 3 RCTs) were analyzed. Meta-analyses suggest that maternal egg intake during pregnancy has a protective role on childhood eczema (OR: 0.51; 95% CI: 0.32, 0.80) and food allergy (OR: 0.60, 95% CI: 0.38, 0.96), and vitamin D has a negative association with childhood rhinitis (OR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.76, 0.97). No significant association was found between maternal vitamin D intake on childhood asthma and fish oil supplementation on childhood hay fever and asthma. Variability in methodology, dietary assessment methods, and follow-up duration contributed to heterogeneity and becomes the limitation in our study.Conclusions:specific maternal diet patterns, such as egg and vitamin D consumption during pregnancy, may have help preventing childhood eczema, food allergy, and rhinitis. Further RCTs are needed to explore the effects of other dietary components on allergy outcomes.

Introduction

Allergic diseases, including food allergy, atopic dermatitis (AD, also known as eczema), asthma, and hay-fever (also known as rhinitis), have become increasingly prevalent over the past few decades. It is estimated that approximately 10–30% of the global population is affected by allergy.[12] The prevalence of allergic conditions such as asthma and AD is higher in infancy and childhood, which peaks at the age of 5–9 years and decreases in older people.[34] According to the United State National Health Interview Survey, 27.2% of all children suffer from one or more of three allergic conditions (seasonal allergy, food allergy, and eczema).[5]

Giving the rising burden of allergic diseases, identifying modifiable risk factors is crucial for prevention. Recently, maternal diet during pregnancy has emerged as a potential determinant of childhood allergy risk as it plays a key role in shaping the fetal immune system. Various mechanisms may explain this relationship, including maternal diet altering offspring’s microbiome colonization. Diet can alter maternal microbiome in vagina, skin, and gastrointestinal tract, which can pass to fetus through birth canal or cesarean section. Additionally, dietary components may directly alter the maternal microbiome, affecting immune signalling pathways through placental transfer and amniotic fluid ingestion by the fetus.[67]

With modernization, there has been an increased consumption of high-fat diets, including among pregnant women. Related with this condition, the role of fat composition and quality in maternal diet on offspring’s allergy has received attention by many researchers. Moreover, the association of offspring’s allergy and sugar sweetened beverage, prebiotics, vegetable, and other dietary factors have been evaluated in human studies. Unfortunately, whether particular food or diet has a protective role in allergy is not universally consistent across studies and this highlights the need for a comprehensive synthesis of available evidence.[6]

Existing reviews on maternal diet and offspring allergy do not specifically highlight the timing of dietary exposure during pregnancy or differentiate between various allergic conditions. Our review aims to address this gap by examining the influence of specific maternal dietary patterns on distinct allergy outcomes in children, providing a more detailed and targeted analysis than previous reviews. By summarizing and analyzing the current scientific literature on this topic, we aim to provide a comprehensive understanding of the potential role of maternal diet in shaping the risk of allergic diseases in children. Our finding might be helpful to provide dietary recommendation for pregnant women as protective factors in childhood allergy.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This present systematic review and meta-analysis study was conducted according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Intervention, following the Preferred Reporting Item for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) framework. The study protocol was registered in the PROSPERO (CRD42024570531).

Search strategy

This study used two databases to search for relevant studies, namely, PubMed/Medline and Scopus. We searched on August 19, 2024 using keywords that matched the MeSH browser: (“Maternal diet” OR “maternal nutrition” OR “pregnancy diet” OR “pregnancy nutrition”) AND (“allergy” OR “allergic disease” OR “immune system”) AND (“offspring” OR “childhood” OR “infant”). Filters used: full text available, clinical trial, observational study, and RCTs were used to search the literature in the database.

Study eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria followed the PICO framework (patient/problem, intervention/exposure, comparison/control, outcome) and comprised (1) type of study: RCTs, cohort, or case-control; (2) study population: pair of mother and infant, toddler, or child; (3) intervention: maternal diet during pregnancy; and (4) outcomes: childhood allergy (atopic dermatitis/eczema, asthma, wheezing, rhinitis, hay-fever, food allergy). Meanwhile, the exclusion criteria were set to (1) studies that were not complete at the time of retrieval, (2) studies with irretrievable full-text articles due to restricted access, (3) studies in languages other than English as an international language, and (4) studies conducted in animals. Furthermore, duplicate removal was also performed using Mendeley Reference Manager ver. 1.19.8. The titles and abstracts of studies were screened according to the PICO criteria as mentioned above by two independent reviewers (LAS and IAC). Any disagreements were discussed to consensus.

Data extraction

We extracted the included studies using a predetermined outcome sheet in tabular form, which consisted of (1) author and year of publication; (2) study population, including sample size; (3) study design; (4) exposure assessment; (5) maternal diet exposure; (6) outcome assessment; and (5) study outcomes, including the effect estimates and P value. Study characteristics were assessed qualitatively by two reviewers (DMS and FU), and another author (LAS) rechecked the accuracy of the extracted data while performing statistical analyses.

Quality data assessment

All observational studies were assessed for the methodological quality using Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) for case-control and cohort studies[8] by two authors independently (DMS and FU). The assessment included a “star system” in which the study was assessed on three domains: 1) Selection (4 items), 2) Comparability (2 items), and 3) Outcome (2 items). The studies were considered as high quality if they scored ≥7, moderate if 5/6, and low if ≤5. The RCT studies were assessed for their quality using RoB2 Tool (Revised Cochrane risk of bias for cluster randomized trials) for Assessing Risk of Bias[9] by FU and LAS independently. The RoB2 tool assesses six domains, namely, 1) Randomization process, 2) Recruitment of Participants, 3) Intended intervention, 4) Missing Outcome Data, 5) Measurement of the outcome, and 6) Reported Result.

Quantitative data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Review Manager ver. 5.4 (The Nordic Cochrane Center, The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark). Adjusted odds ratio (OR) or adjusted relative risk (RR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and P value were extracted from studies for the exposure and the outcomes. We then interpreted pooled effects using random-effects models. We used forest plots to visually summarize all significant outcomes found by the studies. If more than one significant outcome was found in a manuscript, then only the largest observed difference in effect size was included in this review.

The main results used in the statistical analysis were the odds ratio (OR) and risk ratio (RR), which are presented in a forest plot. We used an inverse variance and DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model as proposed by Riley et al.[10] as we considered that heterogeneity outside the study could also be discovered. Heterogeneity was further evaluated using I2 statistics based on the Cochrane threshold, with cutoff limits of 0%, 25%, 50%, and 75% as insignificant, low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively.[10]

Results

Characteristics of included study and critical appraisal

In total, 1304 articles published in 2014 to 2024 were screened. After removing 3 duplicates, 1301 articles were selected for the title and abstract review. Finally, 41 articles were assessed for full-text review. Of 41 articles selected and presented in tables, one article has case-control design and three were followed-up studies of RCTs, while the remaining 37 articles were prospective cohort studies [Figure 1].

PRISMA flow diagram

Most studies assessed maternal outcomes using a semiquantitative food frequency questionnaire (SQ-FFQ), while the remaining studies used Automated Self-Administered 24-hour dietary recall (ASA-24). Maternal pregnancy diet that was studied varied from diet quality, dietary inflammatory index, vitamins (A, C, D, E, K, and folic acid), minerals (iron, selenium, and zinc), food groups, ultra processed food (UPF), oil and fatty acid, advanced glycation end (AGE) products, probiotics and prebiotics, fibers, protein, and diet pattern (western and plant-based diet). Meanwhile, allergic diseases that were mostly observed from the selected articles were atopic dermatitis (AD), wheeze, eczema, asthma, food allergy, hay fever, and cow’s milk allergy (CMA). Risk of bias assessment with NOS for the observational studies reports all high-quality research in all studies [Figure 2], while the RoB2 tool assessment suggests that mostly the RCTs included in this present review have a good quality.

Summary of Literatures Review

Systematic review

Diet quality and diet inflammatory index

Three cohorts[111232] reported the association between diet quality and allergic outcomes in offspring, while two other cohorts[1827] reported diet no association between those variables.

Vitamins

Three cohorts[233041] reported that there was no association between offspring’s allergy and pregnancy vitamin A or its constituent (carotene) intakes. One cohort[41] evaluated that vitamin C had no association with offspring’s asthma and atopy.

Three cohorts[243646] evaluated the association of vitamin D from diet and allergy, three other cohorts[525354] reported the association from supplemental vitamin D only, and another cohort[13] evaluated both dietary and supplemental vitamin D during pregnancy and its risk on offspring allergy. There was a significant association found between vitamin D supplementation and AD during pregnancy. No association was found between dietary vitamin D intake and child’s asthma and wheezing. Supplemental vitamin D was not associated with wheezing, asthma, and rhinitis but was a significant risk factor for CMA. The relationship between dietary vitamin D on child’s asthma was debatable, where two cohorts reported a protective association; however, another cohort did not report any significant relationship.

Four cohorts[23364147] reported the association between dietary vitamin E intake and children’s allergy. The articles suggested that vitamin E intake was associated with wheeze but not with atopy and CMA. However, its association with asthma was still unclear, where two cohorts reported different results.[3641] Only one cohort[30] reported the null association between vitamin K intake during pregnancy and offspring’s allergy. Folate intake during pregnancy and its association with childhood allergy was studied by five cohorts.[3033455053] Of five articles, only two (26, 27) suggested a significant association between folate and childhood allergies (wheeze, eczema, and atopic).

Minerals

With regard to the association of minerals consumption during pregnancy and offspring’s allergy, one cohort investigated the role of iron[17] and selenium,[41] while two cohorts studied zinc.[2341] No significant association was found between maternal minerals during pregnancy and any childhood allergies.

Food groups

Various food groups that reported by previous cohorts including fish and seafood, milk and milk product, legume, poultry, vegetable, fiber, cereal, peanut, egg, and starch. In two cohorts,[1112] legume was associated with multiallergic and wheeze without asthma condition. Fish and shellfish intakes have been associated with food allergy[11] and eczema,[21] although another article did not find any significant association with eczema.[3138] One cohort reported that higher intake of milk and milk product was associated with rhinitis,[43] food allergy and eczema,[21] in contrast with another cohort.[31] Two cohorts[1143] reported nut and peanut had association with multiallergy and food allergy conditions, while another cohort[55] did not find any significant relationship between nut and eczema. Egg was studied by four cohorts; one cohort reported no significant association between egg and food allergy, another cohort reported its link with rhinitis,[39] and two cohorts[4355] did not find any significant relationship between egg and allergic condition, including eczema.

Fish oil

We found two articles,[4449] which were RCT followed-up studies, investigating the long-term effect of fish- versus vegetable oil on offspring’s allergy. One article supported that fish oil supplementation during pregnancy has significant effects on offspring’s later asthma, while another one did not suggest any significant effect on it.

Prebiotics and probiotics

There are two articles that studied the relationship between probiotics and offspring’s allergy onset, including natto (Japanese local fermented food from soy), supplement, and yogurt, while another article reported resistant starch and its link with offspring’s allergy.

Meta-analysis

We conducted four meta-analyses to investigate the association between pregnancy vitamin D intake and childhood rhinitis [Figure 3], association between pregnancy egg intake and childhood eczema [Figure 4] and food allergy [Figure 5], and association between pregnancy fish oil supplementation and childhood hay-fever [Figure 6] and asthma [Figure 7]. Our analysis indicated that vitamin D intake during pregnancy is associated with lower risk of offspring’s rhinitis (pooled OR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.76; 0.97). In addition, maternal egg intake is linked with a lower incidence of offspring’s eczema (pooled OR: 0.51, 95% CI: 0.32; 0.80) and food allergy (pooled OR: 0.60; 95% CI: 0.38; 0.96). However, maternal fish oil intake has no association with offspring’s hay-fever (pooled OR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.63; 1.02) and asthma (pooled OR: 1.08; 95% CI: 0.87; 1.34).

Vitamin D

Combining two studies, maternal dietary vitamin D intake during pregnancy is associated with offspring’s rhinitis (overall OR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.76, 0.97; P = 0.02, I2 = 51%) [Figure 3]. When analyzed its association with asthma in the offspring from three studies, no association can be found (overall OR: 0.98; 95% CI: 0.76, 1.27; P = 0.90; I2 = 84%); however, since the heterogeneity analysis (I2 = 84%) is very high, the meta-analysis is not presented in this study (data are not shown).

Egg

From two separate articles, maternal egg consumption in pregnancy is associated with childhood eczema (overall OR: 0.51, 95% CI: 0.32, 0.80; P = 0.004, I2 = 0%) and food allergy (overall OR: 0.60, 95%CI: 0.38, 0.96; P = 0.03, I2 = 0%) as depicted in Figures 4 and 5, respectively.

Fish oil supplement

From two studies we analyzed, fish oil supplementation is associated with hay-fever outcomes in the offspring (overall RR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.63, 1.02; P = 0.08; I2 = 0%) [Figure 6]. However, when it is associated with childhood asthma, the relationship is not significant (overall RR: 1.08, 95% CI: 0.87, 1.34, P = 0.48; I2 = 0%) [Figure 7].

Discussion

Summary of findings

Most of the studies in the literature recruited mothers with singleton pregnancy. Dietary exposure during pregnancy was mostly assessed using self-reported SQFFQ. Similarly, the allergic outcomes among the offspring were mostly reported by parents, although some cohorts used children medical records and laboratory assessment such as skin prick test (SPT).

Our pooled analyses suggest that maternal dietary vitamin D, egg, and fish oil supplementation during pregnancy play important roles in the particular offspring allergic conditions. From the follow-up of RCT studies, fish oil supplementation has a protective role on the offspring’s hay-fever. Egg consumption during pregnancy also has a negative association with childhood eczema and food allergy. Meanwhile, vitamin D during pregnancy protects childhood rhinitis.

Our finding is different with a conclusion drawn from another systematic literature review in terms of vitamin D and asthma.[56] Venter et al. (2020)[56] suggest that vitamin D and asthma have a protective effect on childhood asthma/wheeze, while our study does not support this result. The gap between our findings might be attributed to the variability in the study design. Our result is based not only on RCTs but also on observational studies (case control and cohort), whereas Venter et al. 2020)[56] use the findings only from RCTs. Furthermore, Venter et al. (2020)[56] include trials conducted in pregnancy and postpartum duration, whereas our study focuses only on maternal intake. The dosage of vitamin D also may contribute to our discrepancy. All RCTs in the Venter et al.[56] review gave higher doses of vitamin D as supplementation compared with the studies we analyzed (800–2400 IU/day vs 400 IU/day as the highest quartile). This might indicate that the effect of vitamin D on offspring’s asthma could be dose-dependent. Further research is warranted to confirm this result. Although our result is different with Venter et al.[56] review, another meta-analysis conducted by Nurmatov, et al. (2011),[57] where its analysis was derived from observational studies, is in accordance with us.

Another interesting discussion from our study is that we found a protective relationship between maternal egg intake and offspring’s eczema and food allergy. This result may provide an important recommendation whether an allergenic diet should be introduced or not during the pregnancy. It is known that egg is one of the major common allergenic foods.[58] In the 1980s and 1990s, there was a recommendation that a decrease in egg consumption during pregnancy might reduce food allergy among the infants. However, later in 2000s, this recommendation is no longer supported by other Cochrane reviews. Delayed introduction of egg is associated with higher risk for eczema.[59] Evidence from animal studies indicates that an introduction of maternal egg consumption via breast milk could increase plasma egg-specific IgG4 levels in infants, which is associated with better egg tolerance.[60] While many studies focus on maternal intake of egg and offspring allergy during lactation, studies investigating how pregnancy egg intake may reduce allergic conditions among offspring are still lacking.[61]

With regard to the negative relationship between pregnancy vitamin D intake and rhinitis, previous studies show that during embryonic development, vitamin D has important roles in the development of lung and immune systems, especially the balance between Th1 and Th2 cells.[6263] Rhinitis is a chronic airway inflammation caused by an IgE-mediated allergic reaction to airborne particles. A meta-analysis suggests that there is a significant negative relationship between serum 25 (OH) D and rhinitis prevalence in adults.[64] Further mechanistic studies are warranted to substantiate the pathways.

Our study has various implications emphasizing on maternal nutrition as a potential preventive strategy for offspring’s allergic diseases, particularly regarding egg and vitamin D intake. Clinicians and dietitians may consider recommending adequate maternal egg and vitamin D intake as part of prenatal nutrition counseling as they may have a protective role against offspring’s eczema, food allergy, and rhinitis. Egg can be promoted as a nutritional balanced maternal diet. In addition, vitamin D fortification programs could be considered by policy makers, especially in populations at risk of deficiency.

Strengths and limitations

We acknowledge that the articles included in this present review are heterogeneous in terms of the diet types and the allergic parameters. Most existing studies are observational, and this limits the ability to infer causality. Large-scale RCTs should assess the optimal prenatal dietary intake levels required to reduce allergy risk in the offspring and should consider the timing of the dietary exposure (e.g., early vs late pregnancy). Due to the high studies’ variability in the method and nutrient dosage, only eight articles can be analyzed quantitatively in our meta-analyses and only two until three articles can be included in each pooled analysis. Moreover, since we only included human studies, this literature review only can assess the relationship between diet in pregnancy and offsprings’ outcomes of allergy without explaining any physiological mechanism underlying the relationship. Future research investigating how maternal diet modulates fetal immune system development and allergic condition is warranted. This study can be done by exploring the role of microbiome colonization, epigenetic modification, and inflammatory response induced by specific prenatal diets.

Another limitation in our study is attributed to the quality of the articles we evaluated. The quality assessment indicates that more than 50% of our observational articles have a high risk of bias in ascertainment of exposure and assessment of outcome. With regard to RCTs, our quality assessment finds that there is one article that has a medium risk for missing outcome data. We also acknowledge that our search was limited to peer-reviewed articles published in English. In addition, we did not contact study authors for additional unpublished data, nor did we search gray literature or other databases such as Cochrane, Web of Science, or Embase, which may represent a limitation of our study. While our approach ensures a focus on high-quality, indexed studies, future research could benefit from broader search strategies, including direct engagement with study authors and exploration of additional data sources. Another condition that needs to be reported is the absence of publication bias assessment in our study due to the limited number of previous studies in our meta-analysis. When the number of studies is very limited, the funnel plot is prone to appearing asymmetric by chance rather than indicating true publication bias.

Our strength is that we used up-to-date articles and included bias assessment risk according to the type of the study. In addition, we provide a comprehensive review on various diet types (e.g., diet quality, dietary index, vitamins, minerals, food groups, UPF, oil and fatty acid, AGEs products, probiotics and prebiotics, fibres, protein, and diet pattern) rather than a single diet and their relationship with childhood allergic conditions.

Conclusions

To summarize, the pregnancy diets, especially egg and vitamin D, may protect the offspring from allergic conditions. As stated in our limitation, many observational articles within this study are lacking in the exposure ascertainment and outcome assessment. Therefore, we recommend creating any prospective cohorts with more accurate method to assess the exposure (e.g., interview instead of self-reported SQFFQ) and the allergy outcome (e.g., medical record or direct physical examination instead of parental report).
