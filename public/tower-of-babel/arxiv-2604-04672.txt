Connected components and topological ends of stationary planar forests
Tom Garcia-Sanchez

Abstract
We study the topological structure of random geometric forests G G in the Euclidean plane under mild assumptions: non-crossing edges, stationarity, and finite edge intensity. The framework covers a broad range of constructions, including models based on stationary point processes as well as lattices, and encompasses many already well-studied examples among drainage networks, geodesic forests arising from first- and last-passage percolation, and minimal or uniform spanning trees. First, denoting by N k N_{k} the number of k k -ended connected components in G G for each k ≥ 0 k\geq 0 , we show that almost surely, all trees of G G have at most two topological ends, N 0 ∈ { 0 , ∞ } N_{0}\in\{0,\infty\} , N 1 ≤ 2 N_{1}\leq 2 , and N 1 = 2 ⟹ N 2 < ∞ N_{1}=2\implies N_{2}<\infty . We then construct explicit examples realizing all possibilities compatible with these constraints, yielding a complete classification of the admissible topological structures for G G . As a second result, we prove that under the additional assumptions that G G is non-empty, oriented, out-degree one, with all its directed paths going to infinity along a fixed deterministic direction, the situation reduces to a dichotomy: G G consists almost surely of either a unique one-ended tree, or infinitely many two-ended trees. The latter extends a theorem of Chaika and Krishnan (2019), who considered a lattice setting. Our proofs combine classical Burton–Keane type arguments with substantial new conceptual ideas using planar topology, resulting in a robust, unified approach.

Introduction

Consider a
random geometric forest

G
in the plane. Formally,

G=(V,E):(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})\to\mathcal{G}

is a random graph valued in the configuration space
\mathcal{G}
of pairs
(V,E)
, where
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is locally finite and
E\subset V\times V\setminus\{(v,v):v\in V\}
, endowed with the
\sigma
-field generated by the counting functions
(V,E)\mapsto\#(V\cap D)
and
(V,E)\mapsto\#(E\cap H)
for bounded Borel sets
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We assume that
G
is a forest in the sense that its underlying undirected graph is almost surely acyclic, and require that with probability one all vertices of
G
have finite degree. Note that we allow directed
2
-cycles in
G
to provide a unified treatment of the case where the graph is effectively undirected, with
(v,v^{\prime})\in E\iff(v^{\prime},v)\in E
. By contrast, we say that
G
is
oriented
if for all
(v,v^{\prime})\in E
,
(v^{\prime},v)\notin E
.

Throughout this paper, we focus on the undirected structure of
G
, while certain assumptions will require us to consider directed edges. We write
v\leftrightarrow v^{\prime}
for vertices
v,v^{\prime}\in V
if there exists an undirected path between them in
G
. The
connected components
of
G
are then defined as equivalence classes of vertices under this relation. For each connected component
C\subset V
, a
topological end
is an equivalence class of infinite undirected simple paths in
C
under the relation of
coalescence
, that is, two such elements are equivalent if their vertex sets differ by only finitely many vertices. We then say that
C
is
n
-ended, with
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
, if the number of topological ends in
C
is
n
.

A fundamental problem is to understand the topological structure of random graphs by determining, under general assumptions, the number of
n
-ended connected components they possess for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
. Classical tools to address such questions include Burton–Keane type arguments
[
9
]
, various forms of mass-transport principles, and, in some cases, planarity. As an example, one may cite the influential work concerning group-invariant percolation on graphs by Benjamini, Lyons, Peres, and Schramm
[
5
]
, which establishes several results under mild hypotheses. In this spirit, we study the problem in the setting of random forests. Since, without further restrictions, essentially any behavior is possible, we impose the following additional natural hypotheses throughout this work.

Assumption A
.

The random geometric forest
G
satisfies the following conditions:

•

Planarity.
For any pair of edges
(u,u^{\prime})
and
(v,v^{\prime})
in
E
, the corresponding line segments in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can only intersect at a common extremity when they are distinct.

•

Stationarity and ergodicity.
The distribution of
G
is stationary and ergodic with respect to translations of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, i.e., there exists a measurable, measure-preserving, ergodic action
(\theta_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}
on
\Omega
such that
G\circ\theta_{s}=s+G
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
s+G
denotes a geometric graph translation.

•

Finite edge intensity.
The edge set of the graph admits a finite intensity in the sense that

\Lambda\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\#\{(u,v)\in E:[u,v]\cap[0,1]^{2}\neq\emptyset\}]<\infty.

(1.1)

Remark 1.1
.

The
ergodicity
requirement may be relaxed by the ergodic decomposition theorem for stationary measures, up to treating the corresponding factors separately. Moreover,
stationarity and ergodicity
may be taken with respect to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-translations: by enlarging the probability space and applying an independent uniform random shift in
[0,1]^{2}
to the graph, one recovers the condition as originally formulated.

These conditions, up to Remark
1.1
, hold for all standard examples, as will be illustrated later.

Main results

Our first result provides a classification of the possible topological structures for
G
.

Theorem I
.

With probability one, every connected component of
G
is a tree with at most
2
topological ends. Let
N_{0},N_{1}
and
N_{2}
respectively denote the number of finite, one-ended and two-ended connected components of
G
, taking values in
\mathbb{Z}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then,
(N_{0},N_{1},N_{2})
is constant on a full-probability event, and the following holds almost surely:

•

N_{0}\in\{0,\infty\}
,

•

N_{1}\leq 2
, and

•

N_{1}=2\implies N_{2}<\infty
.

Moreover, this classification is optimal: every value for
(N_{0},N_{1},N_{2})
satisfying the above constraints is realized by some model on which the result applies.

Note that the constancy of
(N_{0},N_{1},N_{2})
on a full-probability event is an immediate consequence of
ergodicity
since this variable is invariant under translations. Beyond this, the main content of Theorem
I
is the almost sure statements
N_{1}\leq 2
and
N_{1}=2\implies N_{2}<\infty
. The other assertions are not specific to the planar setting and extend directly to higher dimensions, as will be explained throughout the paper. More precisely,
N_{0}\in\{0,\infty\}
a.s. is a straightforward consequence of
stationarity
while the fact that with probability one all connected components have at most
2
ends follows from a classical Burton–Keane type argument involving
trifurcations
, relying on the combinatorial structure of forests together with
stationarity
and
finite edge intensity
.

In contrast, the bound
N_{1}\leq 2
a.s. is achieved through a delicate Burton–Keane type argument, exploiting the planar structure and relying on the notion of
finite pendant trees
(Definition
2.7
, Section
2
).

The implication
N_{1}=2\implies N_{2}<\infty
a.s. constitutes the most challenging part of the theorem. The proof we provide for this final piece, which enables the optimal classification once examples realizing all the cases are constructed, is, to our knowledge, entirely novel and represents a key conceptual contribution of our work. Roughly, we formalize the intuition that, thanks to
planarity
, when
N_{1}=2
the two one-ended components are intertwined so that the space between them looks like a bi-infinite
corridor
, by introducing a notion of
doors
. Using the planar structure, we show that the set of doors can be endowed with a suitable
betweenness
relation induced by a total order isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Z},<)
. Notably, this requires the development of a general deterministic topological result of independent interest (Theorem
III
, Section
5
). Then we prove that
stationarity
together with
finite edge intensity
forces two
-
ended components to cross every door while each of them can be crossed only finitely many times, yielding the desired conclusion.

As a second result, we establish a dichotomy under additional assumptions, which will be derived from Theorem
I
via an elementary yet efficient argument.

Theorem II
.

Assume additionally that with probability one,
G
is oriented with out-degree
1
, non-empty and all directed infinite path
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in
G
satisfies
\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}\cdot e_{2}=+\infty
, where
e_{2}\coloneqq(0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\cdot
denotes the standard scalar product. Then, exactly one of the following holds almost surely:

•

G
is a single one-ended tree, or

•

G
consists of infinitely many disjoint two-ended trees.

Remark 1.2
.

The proofs of both Theorems
I
and
II
are robust and extend directly to the setting where the line segments associated with the edges of
G
, appearing explicitly in the
planarity
and
finite edge intensity
conditions, are replaced by arbitrary, and possibly random, curves homeomorphic to
[0,1]
.

It is important to note that the analogue of Theorem
II
for subgraphs of the
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
lattice was previously established by Chaika and Krishnan
[
10
]
. Their approach is, however, rather technical and exploits specific features of such setting, which limits its generalization. In fact, as they observe, their argument does not even directly extend to all rotations of the lattice, although they suggest this should be achievable with additional work. In contrast, the present work adopts a different, more conceptual perspective, allowing the result to be established seamlessly in considerably greater generality.

Similarly, some studies on specific models such as
[
13
,
11
]
, following the seminal work of Licea and Newman
[
19
]
, all of which fall within the scope of Theorem
II
up to Remark
1.1
, proved that the graphs they consider are almost surely connected via a Burton–Keane type argument using
planarity
and
local modifications
. All these approaches are nonetheless intrinsically tied to the specificities of each model, and it remains an open problem to determine the minimal general assumptions required to distinguish between the alternatives of Theorems
I
and
II
.

Higher dimensions

While it is natural to ask what the classification of possible topological structures for
G
becomes under the analogue of our assumptions beyond dimension
2
, the problem is, perhaps surprisingly, simpler. Indeed, in dimension
d\geq 3
, the
planarity
assumption loses both meaning and rigidity, becoming a
non-crossing property
that is essentially non-restrictive via a superposition principle. More precisely, if
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are two independent random geometric graphs satisfying the analogues of our assumptions in dimension
d\geq 3
, then their union also satisfies them. To see this, it suffices to verify that with probability
1
, no two line segments of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
intersect. Since two line segments can only intersect if all four endpoints lie in a common affine plane, which has zero Lebesgue measure in dimension
d\geq 3
, one can then check that this holds thanks to independence,
stationarity
, and
finite edge intensity
of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. Furthermore, examples of a single one-ended tree and of a single two-ended tree satisfying the assumptions up to Remark
1.1
are known to exist in any dimension
d\geq 3
, see
[
22
,
16
]
using that the
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
lattice is amenable, unimodular, one-ended, and a Cayley graph. In light of previous remarks, combining these constructions with the superposition principle shows that for
d\geq 3
, the classification becomes essentially trivial: each component has at most two ends a.s., the triple
(N_{0},N_{1},N_{2})
is constant on a full-probability event,
N_{0}\in\{0,\infty\}
a.s., and examples exist realizing all possible values for
(N_{0},N_{1},N_{2})
respecting these constraints. In this sense, the additional exclusions of Theorem
I
reflect the genuine rigidity of the planar setting.

Concerning the analogous question for Theorem
II
, what remains in dimension
d\geq 3
is that
N_{2}\in\{0,\infty\}
almost surely, as will be explained later. Furthermore, examples of a single one-ended tree satisfying the analogues of the assumptions in Theorem
II
up to Remark
1.1
are known to exist in any dimension
d\geq 3
, see the construction provided in
[
8
]
. Therefore, by the same superposition principle, in dimension
d\geq 3
, Theorem
II
takes the following form: almost surely, each component has at most
2
ends,
N_{0}=0
,
N_{2}\in\{0,\infty\}
, and examples exist realizing all possible values for
(N_{1},N_{2})
respecting these constraints.

Overall, our results complete the classification of possible topological structures for stationary random forests in all dimensions under mild assumptions.

An important application

Among the examples covered by our setting is a particular class of oriented out-degree one forests constructed via deterministic translation-covariant rules on a point process, sometimes called
drainage networks
. As expanded below, in this setting our results yield a new route for establishing coalescence, i.e., almost sure connectedness of the corresponding graphs. Formally, let
\mathcal{N}
be a stationary and ergodic point process in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and define

E_{\Psi}\coloneqq\{(v,v+\Psi[\mathcal{N}-v]):v\in\mathcal{N}\},

where
\Psi
is a fixed deterministic measurable mapping such that the graph
G_{\Psi}\coloneq(\mathcal{N},E_{\Psi})
is well defined and almost surely acyclic. Then, considering
G\coloneqq G_{\Psi}
, the assumptions of our setup are satisfied whenever the
planarity
condition holds,
\mathcal{N}
has a
finite intensity

\lambda\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\#(\mathcal{N}\cap[0,1]^{2})]
, and
G_{\Psi}
admits a
finite typical squared out-going edge length
, that is
\mathbb{E}^{0}[\|\Psi(\mathcal{N})\|^{2}]<\infty
, where
\|\cdot\|
is the standard Euclidean norm and
\mathbb{E}^{0}
denotes the expectation with respect to the Palm measure of
\mathcal{N}
. Indeed, one can check that any line segment
[v,v+\Psi(\mathcal{N}-v)]
with
v\in\mathcal{N}
that intersects
[0,1]^{2}
must satisfy
\|\Psi(\mathcal{N}-v)\|\geq\|v\|-\rho
where
\rho\coloneqq\sup_{z\in[0,1]^{2}}\|z\|
, so that recalling the definition of
\Lambda
in (
1.1
),

\begin{split}\Lambda\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v\in\mathcal{N}}\mathds{1}_{\|\Psi(\mathcal{N}-v)\|\geq\|v\|-\rho}\right]&=\lambda\mathbb{E}^{0}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathds{1}_{\|\Psi(\mathcal{N})\|\geq\|z\|-\rho}\mathrm{d}z\right]\\
&=\lambda\mathbb{E}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}2\pi r\mathds{1}_{\rho+\|\Psi(\mathcal{N})\|\geq r}\mathrm{d}r\right]\\
&=\lambda\pi\mathbb{E}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{\rho+\|\Psi(\mathcal{N})\|}2r\mathrm{d}r\right]=\lambda\pi\mathbb{E}^{0}[(\rho+\|\Psi(\mathcal{N})\|)^{2}],\end{split}

where the first equality follows from the Campbell-Mecke formula. Analogous considerations apply when the construction is based on a marked point process.

Many works, such as
[
14
,
21
,
12
,
3
,
15
]
, have investigated such drainage networks generalized in arbitrary dimension
d\geq 2
. In dimension
d=2
, these models satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
II
up to Remark
1.1
. The main objective of these studies is to establish coalescence almost surely if and only if
d\leq 3
, which involves a delicate recurrence analysis of suitable random walks. Several of these papers then show separately that, regardless of dimension, there are almost surely no bi-infinite paths, i.e., every connected component has a single end. While this two-step strategy is natural and effective in arbitrary dimensions, Theorem
II
shows that in the planar setting it is sufficient to focus solely on either coalescence or absence of bi-infinite paths. In practice, although a unified proof is difficult due to dependencies on model-specific features, ruling out bi-infinite paths is often straightforward regardless of the dimension: in many instances, one can argue by contradiction that if bi-infinite paths existed, a local modification would allow two of them to merge, producing a three-ended component with positive probability, which is impossible by our results. In fact, the alternative behavior described in Theorem
II
, in which bi-infinite paths exist, can appear pathological, and it is natural to ask whether one can construct an example in which it arises spontaneously, without being explicitly designed.

Beyond drainage networks, our framework encompasses a broad class of constructions, including already well-studied examples ranging from

2
Trifurcations

The goal of this section is to show that with probability one, all components of
G
have at most two ends, and
N_{1}\leq 2
. Throughout this work,
\Omega_{0}
denotes a full-probability event that may change from line to line on which all almost sure properties encountered hold, starting with the assumptions on
G
. The two results of this section are obtained through different applications of the classical Burton–Keane argument with
trifurcations
formalized in the following lemma and subsequent proposition. Recall that
(\theta_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}
denote the shift operators on
\Omega
mentioned in Assumption
A
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any integer
n\geq 1
, define the random variable that counts the number of edges crossing
\partial([0,n]^{2})
by

\chi_{n}\coloneqq\#\{(u,v)\in E:[u,v]\cap\partial([0,n]^{2})\neq\emptyset\}.

Then
\mathbb{E}[\chi_{n}]\leq 4\Lambda n
.

Proof.

Fix
n\geq 1
. Observe that one can cover
\partial([0,n]^{2})
with
4n
translations of
[0,1]^{2}
by writing
\partial([0,n]^{2})\subset\bigcup_{i=0}^{n-1}\{(i,0),(i,n),(0,i),(n,i)\}+[0,1]^{2}
, where
+
denotes the Minkowski sum of sets. Then, denoting
X\coloneq\#\{(u,v)\in E:[u,v]\cap[0,1]^{2}\neq\emptyset\}
, it comes

\chi_{n}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}[X\circ\theta_{-(i,0)}+X\circ\theta_{-(i,n)}+X\circ\theta_{-(0,i)}+X\circ\theta_{-(n,i)}].

Finally, taking the expectation using
stationarity
and the
finite edge intensity
assumptions, one obtains
\mathbb{E}[\chi_{n}]\leq 4n\mathbb{E}[X]=4\Lambda n
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 2.2

(Burton–Keane argument)
.

Fix an event
T\in\mathcal{F}
, a collection of centers
(c_{i,j})_{i,j\geq 1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
and consider for each
n\geq 1
the random variable
\eta_{n}
defined for all
\omega\in\Omega
by

\eta_{n}(\omega)\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathds{1}_{\theta_{-c_{i,j}}(\omega)\in T}.

Assume that there exists an integer
r\geq 1
such that
\chi_{rn}\geq\eta_{n}
a.s. for all
n\geq 1
. Then,
\mathbb{P}(T)=0
.

Proof.

Fix
n\geq 1
. By definition of
\eta_{n}
and
stationarity
,
\mathbb{E}[\eta_{n}]=n^{2}\mathbb{P}(T)
. Additionally, since
\chi_{rn}\geq\eta_{n}
almost surely, one has in particular
\mathbb{E}[\chi_{rn}]\geq\mathbb{E}[\eta_{n}]
. Together, this implies

\mathbb{P}(T)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\eta_{n}]}{n^{2}}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\chi_{rn}]}{n^{2}}\leq\frac{4\Lambda r}{n},

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
2.1
. Letting
n\to\infty
yields
\mathbb{P}(T)=0
, as desired.
∎

Informally, in Proposition
2.2
,
T
is typically called a
trifurcation
event, and the random variables
(\eta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
count suitably translated occurrences of

that induce a.s. at least as many edges crossing the boundaries of boxes of side lengths
(rn)_{n\geq 1}
. In what follows we will consider two different possibilities for such an event, namely those defined in (

) and (

).

Now, for each
v\in V
, denote by
\deg_{\infty}(v)
the number of vertices

that are neighbors of
v
, i.e.
(v,u)\in E
or
(u,v)\in E
, and such that there exists an infinite simple undirected path starting at

and passing through

. The following result is a standard application of the Burton–Keane argument.

Proposition 2.3
.

Almost surely, for all
v\in V
,
\deg_{\infty}(v)\leq 2
. In particular, with probability one, the number of ends of any connected component of
G
is at most
2
.

Proof.

Fix
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
. Consider the sub-graph
G_{\infty}
of
G
induced by the set of vertices

V_{\infty}\coloneqq\{v\in V:\deg_{\infty}(v)\geq 2\},

and denote its edge set by
E_{\infty}
. By construction, no vertex of this graph has degree less than
2
, so it forms a forest without any leaves. Now fix
n\geq 1
and denote by
G_{\infty}^{n}
the sub-graph of
G_{\infty}
induced by the set of vertices

V_{\infty}^{n}\coloneqq\bigcup_{(u,v)\in E_{\infty}^{n}}\{u,v\}\quad\text{where}\quad E_{\infty}^{n}\coloneqq\{(u,v)\in E_{\infty}:[u,v]\cap[0,n]^{2}\neq\emptyset\}.

By construction,
G_{\infty}^{n}
has no isolated vertices, and it forms a finite forest since
\#E_{\infty}^{n}<\infty
from
finite edge intensity
combined with
stationarity
. Next, observe that if
v\in V\cap[0,n]^{2}
, then all its adjacent edges must cross
[0,n]^{2}
, so if
\deg_{\infty}(v)\geq 2
, then
v
is an internal vertex of
G_{\infty}^{n}
with degree
\deg_{\infty}(v)
. Therefore, if
v\in V
is a leaf of
G_{\infty}^{n}
, then
v\notin[0,n]^{2}
and, since

is not isolated, there exists a unique

such that
(u,v)
or

is an edge of

, which must cross
\partial([0,n]^{2})
. This shows that the number of edges of

\partial([0,n]^{2})
is larger than its number of leaves. As it is a classical consequence of the degree-sum formula that, in a finite forest, the leaf count is larger than the number of vertices with degree at least 3, it follows

\chi_{n}\geq\#\{v\in[0,n]^{2}:\deg_{\infty}(v)\geq 3\}.

Now, consider the random variables
(\eta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
associated to the event

T\coloneqq\{\exists u\in[0,1)^{2}:\deg_{\infty}(u)\geq 3\}

(2.1)

and the centers
(c_{i,j})_{i,j\geq 1}\coloneqq[(i,j)]_{i,j\geq 1}
by Proposition

. By construction, for all

,

, which implies
\mathbb{P}(T)=0

∎

Definition 2.5

(Continuous infinite path)

Definition 2.6

(Jordan curve)
.

A subset

\mathbb{S}^{1}\to J

Definition 2.7

(Finite pendant trees)

For any

\mathrm{Pend}(K)\coloneqq\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,v)\in E\\

Lemma 2.8

Almost surely,
\mathrm{Pend}(v)
is finite for each
v\in V
. In particular, for every compact set

Fix

. Next, we define
contour
of a sub-graph of the
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
lattice.

Definition 4.1

(Contour)
.

If
\mathcal{Z}=(V_{\mathcal{Z}},E_{\mathcal{Z}})
is a sub-graph of the
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
lattice and

\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
, its contour at distance
\varepsilon

\mathrm{Cont}[\mathcal{Z},\varepsilon]
with vertex set

, where

, and whose edges consist of

•

pairs
(v+\varepsilon\delta,v+\varepsilon\delta^{\prime})
with

and distinct
\delta,\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta
such that

\bigl(v,\,v+\tfrac{\delta+\delta^{\prime}}{2}\bigr)\notin E_{\mathcal{Z}}
,
and

•

pairs
(v+\varepsilon\delta,v^{\prime}+\varepsilon\delta^{\prime})
with
(v,v^{\prime})\in E_{\mathcal{Z}}

and
\delta,\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta

\tfrac{\delta-\delta^{\prime}}{2}=v^{\prime}-v
.

We now establish the key property that the contours of
G_{\mathrm{UST}}
are almost surely bi-infinite paths.

Lemma 4.2
.

For all
\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
, almost surely, the graph

\mathrm{Cont}[G_{\mathrm{UST}},\varepsilon]
is isomorphic to the
\mathbb{Z}
lattice, hence in particular a two-ended tree.

5
Corridor structure

The goal of this section is to prove the following proposition, which is the last point of Theorem
I
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Almost surely,
N_{1}=2\implies N_{2}<\infty
.

Since
N_{1}
is constant on an event of full-probability, let us assume without loss of generality in this section that
N_{1}(\omega)=2
for all
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
. Before turning to the formal argument, we briefly describe the overall strategy. Owing to the planar structure, the region between the two one-ended components may be viewed as a
corridor
equipped with a naturally ordered family of
doors
, isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Z},<)
. Then, every two-ended component lies in this corridor, and by
finite edge intensity
, each door is crossed by only finitely many of them. This gives two consequences. First, only finitely many two-ended components can cross every door. Second, the existence of components that do not cross every door is almost surely impossible, since one could otherwise identify a smallest minimal or largest maximal crossed door among such components, which is incompatible with stationarity. Together, these observations yield that only finitely many distinct two-ended components can exist when there are two one-ended components. The remaining part of the section is dedicated to making this argument rigorous. Let us start by introducing some definitions. For all
1\leq k<\ell
, define the event

D_{k,\ell}\coloneqq\{N_{1}([-k,k]^{2})=2\}\cap\{\mathrm{Pend}([-k,k]^{2})\subset(-\ell,\ell)^{2}\}.

Then,

1=\mathbb{P}(N_{1}=2)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[N_{1}([-k,k]^{2})=2]=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(D_{k,\ell}),

and we can choose
1\leq k<\ell
such that

\mathbb{P}(D_{k,\ell})>0.

From now on, we fix such values of
k
and
\ell
. For each
\omega\in D_{k,\ell}
, let
u_{1}
denote the westmost-southmost (i.e., minimizing first the horizontal coordinate and then the vertical coordinate) vertex of
V_{1}\cap[-k,k]^{2}
. Similarly, let
u_{2}
denote the westmost-southmost vertex of
[-k,k]^{2}
that belongs to the other one-ended component of
G
. Set

\pi_{u_{1}}^{+}\coloneqq\pi_{u_{1}}[\cdot-t^{+}_{u_{1}}([-k,k]^{2})]\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{u_{2}}^{+}\coloneqq\pi_{u_{2}}[\cdot-t^{+}_{u_{2}}([-k,k]^{2})].

In words,
\pi_{u_{1}}^{+}
and
\pi_{u_{2}}^{+}
corresponds to the continuous simple path started at
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
after their last intersection with
[-k,k]^{2}
. This is well defined since
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\pi_{u_{1}}(t)\|=\infty
for each
i\in\{1,2\}
. We can now properly define
doors
as follows.

Definition 5.2

(Doors)
.

Let us define the set of
doors
as the random variable

\mathcal{D}:\omega\in\Omega\mapsto\mathcal{D}(\omega)\coloneqq\{d\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\theta_{-2\ell d}(\omega)\in D_{k,\ell}\}.

For each
d\in\mathcal{D}
and
i\in\{1,2\}
, set also

\pi_{d,i}^{+}\coloneqq 2\ell d+\pi_{u_{i}}^{+}(\cdot)\circ\theta_{-2\ell d}.

Intuitively, one can view doors as locations of the space where the two one-ended components of
G
are close in some suitable sense. Note that although
\mathcal{D}
may not be almost surely non-empty, one has

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{D}\neq\emptyset)\geq\mathbb{P}(0\in\mathcal{D})=\mathbb{P}(D_{k,\ell})>0.

We now want to argue that because of the planar structure, the two one-ended components of
G
induce a natural order on the set of doors. To do so, we first introduce the following definition.

Definition 5.3

(Topological lines and portions)
.

A subset
\gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called a
topological line
if there exists a homeomorphism
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to\gamma
. When
\gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a topological line, a subset
\pi\subset\gamma
is called a
portion
of
\gamma
if
\pi
, in the subspace topology of
\gamma
, is open, connected, and relatively compact. Equivalently,
\pi\subset\gamma
is a portion of the topological line
\gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
if for some and hence any homeomorphism
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to\gamma
, the set
\Phi^{-1}(\pi)
is a bounded open interval of
\mathbb{R}
.

Next, we define topological lines associated with the doors. Fix
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
. For every
d\in\mathcal{D}
and
i\in\{1,2\}
, the function
\pi_{d,i}^{+}
is continuous, injective, satisfies
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\pi_{d,i}^{+}(t)\|=\infty
and hence realizes an homeomorphism on its image. Therefore, by construction, for each
d\in\mathcal{D}
, the set

\gamma_{d}\coloneqq\pi_{d,1}^{+}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\cup[\pi_{d,1}^{+}(0),\pi_{d,2}^{+}(0)]\cup\pi_{d,2}^{+}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

is a topological line. Furthermore, for all distinct
d,d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
, one has
\gamma_{d}\neq\gamma_{d^{\prime}}
and the difference

\gamma_{d}\setminus\gamma_{d^{\prime}}

is a portion of
\gamma_{d}
. Indeed, if
d,d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
are distinct, then both
\pi_{d,1}^{+}
and
\pi_{d,2}^{+}
avoid
2\ell d^{\prime}+[-k,k]^{2}
using Lemma
2.8
, since by construction they correspond to continuous paths in one-ended components started outside
\mathrm{Pend}(2\ell d^{\prime}+[-k,k]^{2})\subset 2\ell d^{\prime}+(-\ell,\ell)^{2}
. Hence
\gamma_{d}\neq\gamma_{d^{\prime}}
, and since
N_{2}=1
, then the continuous paths
\pi_{d,1}^{+}
and
\pi_{d,2}^{+}
must coalesce with
\pi_{d^{\prime},1}^{+}
and
\pi_{d^{\prime},2}^{+}
, or
\pi_{d^{\prime},2}^{+}
and
\pi_{d^{\prime},1}^{+}
, respectively. One can then verify this yields that
\gamma_{d}\setminus\gamma_{d^{\prime}}
is a portion of
\gamma_{d}
. Now, define the random set of topological lines

\Gamma\coloneqq\{\gamma_{d}:d\in\mathcal{D}\}.

By construction,
\Gamma
is in natural bijection with
\mathcal{D}
via
d\mapsto\gamma_{d}
, and satisfies the property that for all
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
and
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\omega)
,
\gamma\setminus\gamma^{\prime}
is a portion of
\gamma
. The following general deterministic theorem ensures that these properties are sufficient to exploit planarity and endow
\Gamma(\omega)
, and hence
\mathcal{D}(\omega)
, with a natural total order. Its proof is deferred to Section
6
.

Theorem III

(Planar ordering)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a set of topological lines of the plane, such that for all distinct
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
, the difference
\gamma\setminus\gamma^{\prime}
is a portion of
\gamma
. Then, for all distinct
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
, denoting by
\triangle
the symmetric difference operator, the set

J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\coloneqq\overline{\gamma\triangle\gamma^{\prime}}

is a Jordan curve. Furthermore, setting

(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})_{\Gamma}\coloneq\{\sigma\in\Gamma\setminus\{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\}:J(\gamma,\sigma)\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\}

for all distinct
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
, the ternary relation
\cdot\in(\cdot,\cdot)_{\Gamma}
induces a
betweenness
structure on
\Gamma
, that is, there exists a strict total order
<_{\Gamma}
on
\Gamma
, unique up to reversal, such that for all distinct
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime},\sigma\in\Gamma
,

\sigma\in(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})_{\Gamma}\iff[\gamma<_{\Gamma}\sigma<_{\Gamma}\gamma^{\prime}\text{ or }\gamma^{\prime}<_{\Gamma}\sigma<_{\Gamma}\gamma].

Finally, for all distinct
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime},\sigma\in\Gamma
,
\sigma\notin(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})_{\Gamma}\iff J(\gamma,\sigma)\subset\overline{\mathrm{Ext}}J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
.

\text{$(\mathcal{D},<_{\mathcal{D}})$ is discrete},

(d,d^{\prime})_{\mathcal{D}}

\omega\in\Omega_{0}
, if
d,d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
are distinct, one can observe that by construction, for each
d^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\{d,d^{\prime}\}
,
2\ell d^{\prime\prime}+[-k,k]^{2}
intersects
\gamma_{d^{\prime\prime}}
but avoids both
\gamma_{d}
and
\gamma_{d^{\prime}}
. Therefore, if
d^{\prime\prime}\in(d,d^{\prime})_{\mathcal{D}}

2\ell d^{\prime\prime}\in\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma_{d},\gamma_{d^{\prime}})
which implies that
(d,d^{\prime})_{\mathcal{D}}
is finite since
\#\{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:2\ell k\in\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma_{d},\gamma_{d^{\prime}})\}<\infty
as
\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma_{d},\gamma_{d^{\prime}})
is bounded.

Before proceeding further, we record the following observation. Although the total order
<_{\mathcal{D}}
on
\mathcal{D}
is only defined up to reversal for every
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
, and hence arbitrarily chosen, the associated betweenness relation is uniquely determined and measurable, as stated precisely in the lemma below.

Lemma 5.4
.

For all distinct
d,d^{\prime},d^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, the map
\omega\in\Omega\mapsto\mathds{1}_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\mathds{1}_{d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}}\mathds{1}_{d^{\prime\prime}\in(d,d^{\prime})_{\mathcal{D}}}
is measurable.

Proof.

Fix distinct
d,d^{\prime},d^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Measurability of
\omega\mapsto\mathds{1}_{d\in\mathcal{D}}
and
\omega\mapsto\mathds{1}_{d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}}
is immediate. It therefore suffices to show that, fixing
\omega\in\Omega
and assuming
d,d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
, the condition
d^{\prime\prime}\in(d,d^{\prime})_{\mathcal{D}}
, that is

J(\gamma_{d},\gamma_{d^{\prime\prime}})\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma_{d},\gamma_{d^{\prime}}),

can be expressed in a measurable way. This follows from the fact that membership in the interior
\mathrm{Int}J
of a Jordan curve
J\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
admits a countable characterization. Indeed, open sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are polygonally path connected with intermediate points in
\mathbb{Q}^{2}
. Hence, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, one has
z\in\mathrm{Int}J
if and only if every polygonal path starting at
z
and ending outside the closed ball of radius
\sup_{y\in J}\|y\|
, with vertices in
\mathbb{Q}^{2}
except for
z
, intersects
J
. One can then deduce a countable description of the event of interest, and hence its measurability.
∎

In what follows, after justifying that all statements can equivalently be formulated in terms of the betweenness relation alone, we will use
<_{\mathcal{D}}
, which is more intuitive and convenient to handle. We now introduce the key notion of
extreme points
.

Definition 5.5

(Extreme points)
.

For any subset
X\subset\mathcal{D}
, denote by
\partial_{\mathcal{D}}(X)
its set of extreme points, that is, elements
x\in X
which are either a maximum or a minimum in
X
with respect to
<_{\mathcal{D}}
. Since this definition is invariant under reversal of the order
<_{\mathcal{D}}
, it can equivalently be expressed using only the associated betweenness relation.

Next, we establish in the following lemma that
\mathcal{D}
admits almost surely no extreme points.

Lemma 5.6
.

The set of doors
\mathcal{D}
has almost surely no extreme points, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}[\partial_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{D})\neq\emptyset]=0
.

Proof.

First, observe that
\partial_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{D})
is a measurable set using Lemma
5.4
. Then, consider the random variable

X\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\#\partial_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{D})}\sum_{j\in\partial_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{D})}j&\text{if $\partial_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{D})\neq\emptyset$,}\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Since
\#\partial_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathcal{D}(\omega)]\leq 2
by definition, one has

It is worth mentioning that thanks to the previous lemma, for each

Fix

6
Proof of the planar ordering theorem

This section is devoted to proving Theorem

\gamma

Lemma 6.1
.

Fix
\gamma\in\Gamma
and an homeomorphism
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to\gamma

\sigma\in\Gamma\setminus\{\gamma\}

\phi_{\sigma}
and a non-empty bounded open interval

\text{$\Phi=\phi_{\sigma}$ on $\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{\sigma}$},\quad\phi_{\sigma}(I_{\sigma})=\sigma\setminus\gamma,\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi(I_{\sigma})=\gamma\setminus\sigma.

Let
\sigma\in\Gamma\setminus\{\gamma\}

\psi_{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}\to

\gamma\setminus\sigma\neq\emptyset

Indeed, assuming
\gamma\setminus\sigma=\emptyset

\gamma\subset\sigma

\gamma

\sigma

\psi_{\sigma}^{-1}(\gamma)

\mathbb{R}

\psi_{\sigma}^{-1}(\sigma\setminus\gamma)=\mathbb{R}\setminus\psi^{-1}(\gamma)

\sigma\setminus\gamma

\sigma

\gamma\setminus\sigma

\gamma

a_{\sigma}<b_{\sigma}

Additionally, since
\sigma\setminus\gamma

\sigma

Now, consider the map

b_{\sigma^{\prime}}\notin\partial I_{\sigma}
as
b_{\sigma^{\prime}}>a_{\sigma^{\prime}}\geq b_{\sigma}
.

It now remains to prove the last assertion. Fix distinct
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Gamma\setminus\{\gamma\}
. Let us prove that
J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma)
is a connected subset of
J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
. First, observe that by construction, one can write

J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})=\Phi(\overline{I_{\sigma^{\prime}}})\cup\phi_{\sigma^{\prime}}(I_{\sigma^{\prime}})\quad\text{and}\quad J(\gamma,\sigma)=\Phi(\overline{I_{\sigma}})\cup\phi_{\sigma}(I_{\sigma}).

Then, taking the difference, it comes

\begin{split}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma)&=\Phi(\overline{I_{\sigma^{\prime}}})\setminus[\Phi(\overline{I_{\sigma}})\cup\phi_{\sigma}(I_{\sigma})]\cup\phi_{\sigma^{\prime}}(I_{\sigma^{\prime}})\setminus[\Phi(\overline{I_{\sigma}})\cup\phi_{\sigma}(I_{\sigma})]\\
&=\Phi(\overline{{I_{\sigma^{\prime}}}}\setminus\overline{I_{\sigma}})\cup\phi_{\sigma^{\prime}}(I_{\sigma^{\prime}})\setminus\phi_{\sigma}(I_{\sigma})\\
&=\Phi(\overline{{I_{\sigma^{\prime}}}}\setminus\overline{I_{\sigma}})\cup\phi_{\sigma^{\prime}}(I_{\sigma^{\prime}}\cap I_{\sigma^{\prime}|\sigma})\end{split}

(6.1)

where using the definition of
I_{\sigma}
and
I_{\sigma^{\prime}}
, the first equality follows from
\phi_{\sigma}(I_{\sigma})\cap\Phi(\overline{I_{\sigma^{\prime}}})\subset\phi_{\sigma}(I_{\sigma})\cap\gamma=\emptyset
as well as
\phi_{\sigma^{\prime}}(I_{\sigma^{\prime}})\cap\Phi(\overline{I_{\sigma}})\subset\phi_{\sigma^{\prime}}(I_{\sigma^{\prime}})\cap\gamma=\emptyset
, and
\phi_{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{\sigma})\cap\phi_{\sigma^{\prime}}(I_{\sigma^{\prime}})\subset\gamma\cap\phi_{\sigma^{\prime}}(I_{\sigma^{\prime}})=\emptyset
yields the last line. Now, by construction,
\phi_{\sigma^{\prime}}(I_{\sigma^{\prime}}\cap I_{\sigma^{\prime}|\sigma})\subset\phi_{\sigma^{\prime}}(I_{\sigma^{\prime}|\sigma})=\sigma^{\prime}\setminus\sigma
, and

\Phi(\overline{{I_{\sigma^{\prime}}}}\setminus\overline{I_{\sigma}})\subset\overline{\Phi(I_{\sigma^{\prime}})\setminus\Phi(I_{\sigma})}\subset\overline{(\gamma\setminus\sigma^{\prime})\setminus(\gamma\setminus\sigma)}\subset\overline{\sigma\setminus\sigma^{\prime}}.

Injected in (
6.1
), it shows

J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma)\subset\overline{\sigma\setminus\sigma^{\prime}}\cup\sigma\setminus\sigma^{\prime}\subset\overline{\sigma\triangle\sigma^{\prime}}=J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}).

It remains to check connectedness. To that end, observe that since
I_{\sigma^{\prime}}\cap I_{\sigma^{\prime}|\sigma}
is an open interval,

\phi_{\sigma^{\prime}}(I_{\sigma^{\prime}}\cap I_{\sigma^{\prime}|\sigma})

is connected. Then, observe that although
\overline{{I_{\sigma^{\prime}}}}\setminus\overline{I_{\sigma}}
may be disconnected, each of its non-empty components must contain a point of
(\partial I_{\sigma^{\prime}})\setminus\overline{I_{\sigma}}
. Finally, since

(\partial I_{\sigma^{\prime}})\setminus\overline{I_{\sigma}}\subset\overline{I_{\sigma^{\prime}|\sigma}}\cap\overline{I_{\sigma^{\prime}}}=\overline{I_{\sigma^{\prime}}\cap I_{\sigma^{\prime}|\sigma}}

from Lemma
6.1
, each component of
\Phi(\overline{{I_{\sigma^{\prime}}}}\setminus\overline{I_{\sigma}})
connects to
\phi_{\sigma^{\prime}}(I_{\sigma^{\prime}}\cap I_{\sigma^{\prime}|\sigma})
, so that injecting in (
6.1
), it comes
J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma)
is connected.
∎

It remains to check that
\cdot\in(\cdot,\cdot)_{\Gamma}
satisfies the axioms of a betweenness relation given by
[
18
]
, namely symmetry (Corollary
6.3
) as well as transitivity and the trichotomy property (Proposition
6.7
). The following is a consequence of the previous proposition and already establishes symmetry.

Corollary 6.3
.

For all distinct
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime},\sigma\in\Gamma
, one has either

J(\gamma,\sigma)\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\quad\text{or}\quad J(\gamma,\sigma)\subset\overline{\mathrm{Ext}}J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}).

Furthermore,
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\Gamma}
is
symmetric
, i.e., for all distinct
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
,
(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})_{\Gamma}=(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma)_{\Gamma}
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime},\sigma\in\Gamma
be distinct. Since
J(\gamma,\sigma)\setminus J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
avoids
J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
, and is connected by Proposition
6.2
, one has either

J(\gamma,\sigma)\setminus J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\subset\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\quad\text{or}\quad J(\gamma,\sigma)\setminus J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\subset\mathrm{Ext}J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}).

Then, taking the union with
J(\gamma,\sigma)\cap J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\subset J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})=\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\cap\overline{\mathrm{Ext}}J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
, the first assertion follows. Then, one can observe that since

\emptyset\neq\sigma\setminus(\gamma\cup\gamma^{\prime})\subset J(\gamma,\sigma)\setminus J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})

from Proposition
6.2
and
J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})=J(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma)
, one can write

\begin{split}\sigma\in(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})_{\Gamma}&\iff J(\gamma,\sigma)\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\\
&\iff\sigma\setminus(\gamma\cup\gamma^{\prime})\subset\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\\
&\iff\sigma\setminus(\gamma^{\prime}\cup\gamma)\subset\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma)\\
&\iff J(\gamma^{\prime},\sigma)\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma)\iff\sigma\in(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma)_{\Gamma},\end{split}

which proves symmetry.
∎

Now, in order to tackle the geometry of interacting Jordan curves and to obtain the remaining sought properties, we will need the two following technical lemmas.

Lemma 6.4
.

For any Jordan curves
J,J^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, one has
J\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J^{\prime}\implies\mathrm{Int}J\subset\mathrm{Int}J^{\prime}
, and

[J\subset\overline{\mathrm{Ext}}J^{\prime}\text{ and }J^{\prime}\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J]\implies\mathrm{Int}J\subset\mathrm{Ext}J^{\prime}.

Proof.

Fix
z\in\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J
and consider an unbounded continuous path
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
f(0)=z
. Since
\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J
is bounded, there exists
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
f(t_{0})\in\partial\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J=J
. Then, as
J\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J^{\prime}
and
\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J^{\prime}
is unbounded, there must also exist
t_{1}\geq t_{0}
such that
f(t_{1})\in\partial\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J^{\prime}=J^{\prime}
. This shows that all unbounded continuous path started at
z
eventually cross
J^{\prime}
, i.e.,
z\in\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J^{\prime}
. Then, one has
\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J^{\prime}
and finally, removing the boundaries, it comes
\mathrm{Int}J\subset\mathrm{Int}J^{\prime}
.

Now remains to check that
[J\subset\overline{\mathrm{Ext}}J^{\prime}\text{ and }J^{\prime}\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J]\implies\mathrm{Int}J\subset\mathrm{Ext}J^{\prime}
. To do so, observe that it is sufficient by contrapositive to show
[\mathrm{Int}J\not\subset\mathrm{Ext}J^{\prime}\text{ and }J^{\prime}\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J]\implies J\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Ext}}J^{\prime}
. Assume that
\mathrm{Int}J\not\subset\mathrm{Ext}J
and
J^{\prime}\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J
. From
\mathrm{Int}J\not\subset\mathrm{Ext}J^{\prime}
, one has
\mathrm{Int}J\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Ext}}J^{\prime}
and there must exist

z_{0}\in\mathrm{Int}J\cap\mathrm{Int}J^{\prime}.

Additionally, one has
\mathrm{Int}J^{\prime}\not\subset\mathrm{Int}J
from
\partial\mathrm{Int}J^{\prime}=J^{\prime}\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J
, and there must exist

z_{1}\in\mathrm{Int}J\setminus\mathrm{Int}J^{\prime}.

Then, as
z_{0}
and
z_{1}
both belong to
\mathrm{Int}J
which is path-connected, there exists a continuous path
g:[0,1]\to\mathrm{Int}J
with
g(0)=z_{0}
and
g(1)=z_{1}
. Since
g(0)\in\mathrm{Int}J^{\prime}
but
g(1)\notin\mathrm{Int}J^{\prime}
, and
g
is continuous, there exists
t\in[0,1]
such that
g(t)\in\partial\mathrm{Int}J^{\prime}=J^{\prime}
. Finally, as
g(t)\in\mathrm{Int}J^{\prime}
by construction of
g
, it shows
g(t)\in J^{\prime}\cap\mathrm{Int}J
and thus,
J^{\prime}\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Ext}}J
. The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

For any distinct
\gamma,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
and
z\in J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma)
, there exists an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
containing
z
such that

\{U\cap\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime}),U\cap\mathrm{Ext}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\}=\{U\cap\mathrm{Int}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}),U\cap\mathrm{Ext}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\}

Proof.

Let
\gamma,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
. Fix
z\in J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma)
. Applying the Jordan-Schönflies theorem (see
[
20
]
), there exists a homeomorphism

\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

such that
\Psi[J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})]=\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Since
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma)
and
\Psi[\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})]
is open, there exist
\varepsilon>0
small enough so that the open disk

D\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\|y-\Psi(x)\|<\varepsilon\}

is included in
\Psi[\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma)]
. Clearly,

D\setminus\mathbb{S}^{1}

has exactly two connected components. Now set
U\coloneqq\Psi^{-1}(D)
. Then,

U\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})=\Psi^{-1}(D\setminus\mathbb{S}^{1})

must consist of two components, which are necessarily
U\cap\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
and
U\cap\mathrm{Ext}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
. Now, observe that

\begin{split}[U\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})]\triangle[U\setminus J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})]&=U\cap[J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\triangle J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})]\\
&\subset U\cap J(\gamma,\sigma)\\
&\subset[\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma)]\cap J(\gamma,\sigma)\\
&=\emptyset,\end{split}

where the first inclusion uses that both
J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
and
J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
are subsets of
J(\gamma,\sigma)
from Proposition
6.2
. This shows
U\setminus J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=U\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
, so that the two components of those sets can be alternatively expressed as
U\cap\mathrm{Int}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
and
U\cap\mathrm{Ext}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
, and the result follows by identifying.
∎

The last key ingredient for establishing the transitive property of
\cdot\in(\cdot,\cdot)_{\Gamma}
is provided by the following proposition.

Proposition 6.6
.

For all distinct
\gamma,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
,

\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma)\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}).

Proof.

Fix distinct
\gamma,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
. If
J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma)
, then
\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma)=\emptyset
using Lemma
6.4
and the result is clear. Let us then assume that

J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma)

Let
z\in\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma)
. If
z\in J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
, since
J(\gamma,\sigma)\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma)
, one gets from Proposition
6.2
that

z\in J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma)\subset J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}).

Now, assume
z\notin J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
, i.e.,

z\in\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\cap\mathrm{Ext}J(\gamma,\sigma).

Let us distinguish the two cases
J(\gamma,\sigma)\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
and
J(\gamma,\sigma)\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
.

Case 1.
Assume
J(\gamma,\sigma)\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
. Then, Corollary
6.3
gives
J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
and Lemma
6.4
yields

\mathrm{Int}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\subset\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Ext}(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\subset\mathrm{Ext}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}).

(6.2)

Since
z\in\mathrm{Ext}J(\gamma,\sigma)
, there exists an unbounded continuous path
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathrm{Ext}J(\gamma,\sigma)
with
f(0)=z
. As
z\in\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
and
\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
is unbounded, there exists
t_{0}>0
such that

f(t_{0})\in\partial J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})=J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad\forall t\in[0,t_{0}),\penalty\ f(t)\in\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime}).

Observing that
f(t_{0})\in J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma)
from
f(t_{0})\in\mathrm{Ext}J(\gamma,\sigma)
, one can apply Lemma
6.5
together with (
6.2
) to get an open neighborhood
U
of
f(t_{0})
such that

U\cap\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})=U\cap\mathrm{Int}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}).

Then, since
f(t)\in\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
for
t\in[0,t_{0})
, one deduces by continuity of
f
that there exists
t_{0}^{-}\in[0,t_{0})
such that

f(t_{0}^{-})\in\mathrm{Int}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}).

Finally, observing that
f([0,t_{0}])\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
avoids
J(\gamma,\sigma)
and
J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\subset J(\gamma,\sigma)
from Proposition
6.2
, one gets that
f([0,t_{0}])
avoids
J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
, hence
z=f(0)
and
f(t_{0})\in\mathrm{Int}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
must lie in the same component of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
, implying
z\in\mathrm{Int}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
.

Case 2.
Now, assume
J(\gamma,\sigma)\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
. Since one has also
J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma)
, Lemma
6.4
together with Corollary
6.3
gives

\mathrm{Int}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\subset\mathrm{Ext}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Int}(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\subset\mathrm{Ext}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}).

(6.3)

Let
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an unbounded continuous path with
f(0)=z
. Since
\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma)\cup\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
is bounded, there exists
t_{0}>0
such that

f(t_{0})\in\partial[\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma)\cup\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})]\subset\partial\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma)\cup\partial\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})=J(\gamma,\sigma)\cup J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime}).

Assume by contradiction that
f(t_{0})\notin J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
. Then, applying Lemma
6.5
with (
6.3
), there exists an open neighborhood
U
of
f(t_{0})
such that either

U\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})=U\cap[\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\sigma)\cup\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})]\quad\text{or}\quad U\setminus J(\gamma,\sigma)=U\cap[\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\sigma)\cup\mathrm{Int}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})].

Both cases yield
U\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma)\cup\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})
and thus
f(t_{0})\notin\partial[\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma)\cup\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})]
, which is absurd. Therefore, one must have

f(t_{0})\in J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}).

This shows that any unbounded continuous path started at
z
must eventually cross
J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
, i.e.,
z\in\mathrm{Int}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
, and thus
\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\gamma,\sigma)\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
.
∎

The following proposition is the last step before proving Theorem
III
. It establishes the sought transitivity and trichotomy properties on
\cdot\in(\cdot,\cdot)_{\Gamma}
.

Proposition 6.7
.

The relation
\cdot\in(\cdot,\cdot)_{\Gamma}
is
transitive
, that is, for all distinct
\gamma,\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\Gamma
,

\gamma\in(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})_{\Gamma}\setminus(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}\implies\gamma\in(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}.

Furthermore, for all distinct
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\Gamma
, exactly one of the assertions
\sigma\in(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}
,
\sigma^{\prime}\in(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}
and
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})_{\Gamma}
holds.

Proof.

Let
\gamma,\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\Gamma
be distinct. Assume that

\gamma\in(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})_{\Gamma}\setminus(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}.

Then, using Corollary
6.3
, one must have
\gamma\in\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\cap\overline{\mathrm{Ext}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})
, so that using Propositions
6.2
and
6.6
,

\emptyset\neq\gamma\setminus(\sigma\cup\sigma^{\prime}\cup\sigma^{\prime\prime})\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}).

Now, since
\gamma\setminus(\sigma\cup\sigma^{\prime}\cup\sigma^{\prime\prime})
is contained in
J(\sigma^{\prime},\gamma)
and avoids
J(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime})
, it follows from Corollary
6.3
that
J(\sigma^{\prime},\gamma)\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime})
, i.e.,
\gamma\in(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}
.

It remains to check the second assertion. Let
\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\Gamma
be distinct. To show that exactly one of the assertions
\sigma\in(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}
,
\sigma^{\prime}\in(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}
, and
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})_{\Gamma}
holds, let us first check that at most one of them can hold. Assume
\sigma\in(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}
. Then
J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})
, so that
\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})
using Lemma
6.4
. Now, as

\partial\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\neq J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\partial\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})

since
\emptyset\neq\sigma^{\prime}\setminus(\sigma\cup\sigma^{\prime\prime})\subset J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\setminus J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})
from Proposition
6.2
, it implies that

\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}).

Therefore, using Lemma
6.4
, it comes
J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
, i.e.,
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\notin(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})_{\Gamma}
. This shows

\sigma^{\prime}\in(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}\implies\sigma^{\prime\prime}\notin(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})_{\Gamma}.

Then, permuting the roles of
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}
and using the symmetry of
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\Gamma}
from Corollary
6.3
, it follows that at most one of the assertions
\sigma\in(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}
,
\sigma^{\prime}\in(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}
and
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})_{\Gamma}
can hold. It remains finally to check that at least one of them holds. If
\sigma\in(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}
or
\sigma^{\prime}\in(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}
, there is nothing to prove. One can then assume that

\sigma\notin(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma^{\prime}\notin(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})_{\Gamma}.

Then since using the symmetry of Corollary
6.3
, one gets

J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})\not\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}).

Therefore, Lemma
6.4
gives that
\mathrm{Int}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})\subset\mathrm{Ext}J(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime})
, and one can write

\mathrm{Int}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})\setminus\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime})\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})

using Proposition
6.6
, so that taking the boundary,
J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})=\partial\mathrm{Int}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})\subset\overline{\mathrm{Int}}J(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
. This shows
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})_{\Gamma}
and concludes the proof.
∎

Putting everything together, we finally prove Theorem
III
.

Proof of Theorem
III
.

Proposition
6.2
gives that for each distinct
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
,
J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
is a Jordan curve. Then, Proposition
6.7
and Corollary
6.3
ensure that the ternary relation induced on
\Gamma
by
\cdot\in(\cdot,\cdot)_{\Gamma}
setting
(\gamma,\gamma)_{\Gamma}\coloneq\emptyset
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
satisfies the axioms of betweenness (see
[
18
]
). The existence and unicity, up to reversal, of an induced total order follows by construction of betweenness relations. Finally,
\sigma\notin(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})_{\Gamma}\iff J(\gamma,\sigma)\subset\overline{\mathrm{Ext}}J(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
for all distinct
\sigma,\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
is given by Corollary
6.3
.
∎
