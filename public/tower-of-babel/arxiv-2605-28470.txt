Line, Spiral, Dense in Space
Atakora Agoro, Alastair N. Fletcher

Abstract
. Dobbs proved that the second iterate of almost every line in the complex plane under the exponential function is dense in the plane. In this paper, we prove an analogous result for the second iterate of the Zorich map in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} .



1.
Introduction

It is a standard homework exercise in complex analysis to show that the image of an oblique line under
\exp
is a spiral. It is much less standard to show that the image of almost all oblique lines under
\exp\circ\exp
is a dense curve in
\mathbb{C}
. This remarkable result is due to Dobbs
[
2
, Theorem 1]
. Dobbs’ method was generalized by Vaskouski, Prochorov, and Sheshko
[
6
]
to cover a larger class of periodic functions in
\mathbb{C}
.

The aim of this paper is to prove an analogous result for a quasiregular version of the exponential function in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Such maps are called Zorich maps, as they were first constructed by Zorich
[
7
]
and the dynamics of these maps have been well studied in the literature, see for example the work of Bergweiler
[
1
]
and Tsantaris
[
5
]
.

We will fix a particular Zorich map
\mathcal{Z}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
to work with in Section
2
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For almost every line
L
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the curve
(\mathcal{Z}\circ\mathcal{Z})(L)
is dense in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

The overarching strategy for proving Theorem
1.1
is the same as Dobbs, but there are more technical difficulties to overcome in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Given a ball
U
, we will study its second pre-image
(\mathcal{Z}\circ\mathcal{Z})^{-1}(U)
. Then instead of looking at a line to see whether it intersects this set, we will fatten it slightly, in a suitable sense, to give a cone and show that a certain proportion of this cone must intersect the second pre-image of the ball. We will then use measure theoretical considerations to obtain the result.

For the exponential map, the real axis is forward invariant, which means there are certainly some lines which must be excluded. For our Zorich map
\mathcal{Z}
, there are analogous lines that will need to be excluded, so we certainly cannot obtain Theorem
1.1
for every line in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

We leave open several related questions. First, Dobbs
[
2
, Theorem 3]
showed that the image of every oblique line in
\mathbb{C}
was dense in
\mathbb{C}
under the third iterate of
\exp
. It seems plausible that the same might hold for
\mathcal{Z}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Moreover, there is a whole family of Zorich maps that one could study in the context of generalizing Theorem
1.1
and, more generally, the same result could plausibly hold for any strongly automorphic map in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
or higher dimensions. We, however, will satisfy ourselves with just proving Theorem
1.1
here.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
we will define the particular Zorich map we will use and recall various properties of it. In Section
3
, we will prove Theorem
1.1
.

This paper arises from the Ph.D. dissertation of the first named author. The external examiner for the dissertation was Neil Dobbs, and both authors would like to thank him for many invaluable comments and suggestions.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
A Zorich map

Let
B
denote the square

(2.1)

B=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\right\},

and let
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
denote the upper hemisphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
given by

\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}=1,x_{3}\geq 0\right\}.

To build the Zorich map we will use, we start by defining the bi-Lipschitz map
h:B\to\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
given by

(2.2)

h(x_{1},x_{2})=\left(\frac{x_{1}\sin M(x_{1},x_{2})}{\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}},\frac{x_{2}\sin M(x_{1},x_{2})}{\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}},\cos M(x_{1},x_{2})\right),

where
M(x_{1},x_{2})=\max\{|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|\}
. By reflecting in the sides of squares in the domain and in the equator of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
in the range, we obtain a doubly periodic map that, by an abuse of notation, we still call
h
from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
onto
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

The Zorich map
\mathcal{Z}
is then defined via

(2.3)

\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=e^{x_{3}}h(x_{1},x_{2}).

If we let
B_{0}
be the closed beam in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined by
B_{0}=B\times\mathbb{R}
, then it is clear that
\mathcal{Z}
maps
B_{0}
onto the closed upper half-space in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the origin removed. Moreover, if we set

B_{1}=\left[\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}\right]\times\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\times\mathbb{R}

to be one of the beams neighbouring
B_{0}
, then the image of
B_{1}
under
\mathcal{Z}
is the closed lower half-space in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the origin removed.

This produces a quasiregular map
\mathcal{Z}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
. At this stage, we point out that we do not really need that
\mathcal{Z}
is quasiregular. It is only important for us that
\mathcal{Z}
is an analogue of
\exp
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In fact, restricting to either the
\{x_{1}=0\}
plane of the
\{x_{2}=0\}
plane yields embedded copies of
\exp
. We refer to, for example,
[
4
, Appendix A2]
for a proof of the fact that
h
defined in (
2.2
) is bi-Lipschitz and
\mathcal{Z}
is quasiregular.

The branch set
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Z})
is the set of points where
\mathcal{Z}
fails to be a local homeomorphism. From the construction, we see that the branch set consists of the lines at the edges of faces of the beams, that is

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Z})=\bigcup_{j,k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\left\{\frac{\pi}{2}+j\pi\right\}\times\left\{\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi\right\}\times\mathbb{R}\right).

2.2.
The group of automorphisms

It turns out that Zorich maps are strongly automorphic with respect to a discrete group of isometries. This means that there is such a group
G
with

(a)

\mathcal{Z}(g(x))=\mathcal{Z}(x)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and any
g\in G
, and

(b)

if
\mathcal{Z}(x)=\mathcal{Z}(y)
, then there exists
g\in G
with
y=g(x)
.

It is clear from the construction that
\mathcal{Z}
is periodic with periods
(2\pi,0,0)
and
(0,2\pi,0)
. However,
G
also contains rotations by
\pi
about lines in the branch set. If we set
g_{1}(x)=x+(2\pi,0,0)
,
g_{2}(x)=x+(0,2\pi,0)
and

g_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(\pi-x_{1},\pi-x_{2},x_{3}),

then
G
is generated by
g_{1},g_{2}
, and
g_{3}
.

As
\mathcal{Z}
maps
\operatorname{int}(B_{0})
bijectively onto the open upper half-space, and maps
\operatorname{int}(B_{1})
bijectively onto the open lower half-space, it follows that
\operatorname{int}(B_{0}\cup B_{1})
together with some of its boundary forms a fundamental domain for the action of
G
that we call
\Omega
. We will see later that this
domino
behaviour where a fundamental domain is naturally split into two regions has large consequences for our analysis.

2.3.
Mapping properties of our Zorich map

Here we record some important mapping properties of
\mathcal{Z}
that we will need later.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}
, let
H_{t}
be the plane

H_{t}=\{(x_{1},x_{2},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Then
\mathcal{Z}
maps
H_{t}
onto the sphere
S(0,e^{t})=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x|=e^{t}\}
. Conversely,
\mathcal{Z}^{-1}(S(0,r))=H_{\ln r}
for any
r>0
.

This follows directly from the construction of
\mathcal{Z}
and so we omit the proof.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
t>0
and let
S_{t}\subset B_{0}
be defined via

S_{t}=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in B_{0}:e^{x_{3}}\cos M(x_{1},x_{2})=t\}.

Then
\mathcal{Z}
maps
S_{t}
bijectively onto the plane
H_{t}
.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{Z}
is injective on
B_{0}
, it is enough to find the pre-image of
H_{t}
under
\mathcal{Z}
contained in
B_{0}
. By (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), this follows by solving
e^{x_{3}}\cos M(x_{1},x_{2})=t
. Re-writing this expression, we also obtain that

S_{t}=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in B_{0}:x_{3}=\ln\left(\frac{t}{\cos M(x_{1},x_{2})}\right)\right\}.

∎

For
t<0
, we define
S_{t}\subset B_{1}
to be the reflection of
S_{-t}
across the common face of
B_{0}
and
B_{1}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then:

(i)

for
t>0
,
\mathcal{Z}^{-1}(H_{t})
is
\bigcup_{g\in G}g(S_{t})
,

(ii)

for
t<0
,
\mathcal{Z}^{-1}(H_{t})
is
\bigcup_{g\in G}g(S_{t})
,

(iii)

for
t=0
,
\mathcal{Z}^{-1}(H_{t})
is the union of all of the faces of
g(B_{0})
and
g(B_{1})
over all
g\in G
.

Proof.

Part (a) follows from Lemma
2.2
and using the strong automorphy of
\mathcal{Z}
with respect to
G
. Part (b) follows analogously, as if
t<0
, then by construction,
\mathcal{Z}
maps
S_{t}
bijectively onto
H_{t}
. Part (c) follows immediately by the construction of
\mathcal{Z}
.
∎

It is clear that
S_{t}
is a cone with vertex
(0,0,\ln t)
and that every cross-section of
S_{t}
with a plane
\{x_{3}=s\}
for
s>\ln t
is a square given by
M(x_{1},x_{2})=\cos^{-1}[te^{-s}]
. In particular, as
s\to\infty
, the cross-sections get closer and closer to the sides of the square beam that
S_{t}
lies in. We make this observation precise with the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
t>0
. Given
\xi>0
, there exists
s_{0}>0
such that if
x\in S_{t}
with
x_{3}>s_{0}
, then
\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial B_{0})<\xi
.

By applying a translation or rotation, exactly the same result holds for
g(S_{t})
for any
t\neq 0
and
g\in G
.

Proof.

By the proof of Lemma
2.2
,
S_{t}
is given by the equation

x_{3}=\ln\left(\frac{t}{\cos M(x_{1},x_{2})}\right).

Suppose that
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in S_{t}
and set
y=\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial B_{0})
. By construction, it follows that

\cos(\pi/2-y)=te^{-x_{3}},

and thus

\sin y=te^{-x_{3}}.

The lemma then follows from the continuity of the sine function.
∎

2.4.
A notion of distortion

A tool used by Dobbs
[
2
]
, as well as in
[
6
]
, is the notion of the distortion of a differentiable, non-constant function
f
on a set
E\subset\mathbb{C}
via

D(f,E)=\frac{\sup_{z\in E}|f^{\prime}(z)|}{\inf_{z\in E}|f^{\prime}(z)|}.

As our Zorich map is not differentiable everywhere, we will need to modify this quantity in order to utilize it. In fact, as quasiregular maps are in general just locally Hölder, we will need to verify
\mathcal{Z}
has better regularity than this. We start with a definition that comes up when dealing with bounded length distortion maps, or BLD maps for short.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a domain. A function
f:E\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called infinitesimally bi-Lipschitz if there exists a constant
\lambda\geq 1
such that for all
x_{0}\in E
, we have

\frac{1}{\lambda}\leq\liminf_{x\to x_{0}}\frac{|f(x)-f(x_{0})|}{|x-x_{0}|}\leq\limsup_{x\to x_{0}}\frac{|f(x)-f(x_{0})|}{|x-x_{0}|}\leq\lambda.

For example, the extended map
h:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
certainly is not bi-Lipschitz as it is doubly periodic and thus not injective. However, it is infinitesimally bi-Lipschitz with the same constant
\lambda
. Again we refer to
[
4
, Appendix A2]
for this fact.

Next, if
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a domain and
f:E\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a non-constant continuous map, for
x\in E
and
0<r<\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial E)
we recall the linear dilatations

L(x,f,r)=\sup_{|y-x|=r}|f(y)-f(x)|,\quad\ell(x,f,r)=\inf_{|y-x|=r}|f(y)-f(x)|.

Definition 2.6
.

With the notation above, the upper and lower pointwise Lipschitz constants of
f
at
x\in E
are, respectively,

\widetilde{L}_{f}(x)=\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{L(x,f,r)}{r},\quad\widetilde{\ell}_{f}(x)=\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\ell(x,f,r)}{r}.

The relative distortion of
f
in
E
is then defined by

D(f,E)=\frac{\sup_{x\in E}\widetilde{L}_{f}(x)}{\inf_{x\in E}\widetilde{\ell}_{f}(x)},

whenever both quantities are finite and the denominator is strictly positive.

Issues arise for
D(f,E)
when
f
is only a Hölder map. Consider for example the radial map
f(x)=x|x|^{a-1}
, for
0<a<1
. However, it is clear that if
f
is infinitesimally bi-Lipschitz with constant
\lambda>1
, then
D(f,E)\leq\lambda^{2}
.

Recall that
h:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
is infinitesimally bi-Lipschitz with constant, say,
\lambda(h)
. We will want to control the relative distortion of our Zorich map
\mathcal{Z}
in certain regions, as given in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
t_{1}<t_{2}
and let
E
be the slab
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(t_{1},t_{2})\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then

D(\mathcal{Z},E)\leq\lambda(h)^{2}e^{t_{2}-t_{1}}.

Proof.

It follows directly from (
2.3
) that if
x\in E
, then

\frac{e^{t_{1}}}{\lambda}\leq\widetilde{\ell}_{\mathcal{Z}}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\leq\widetilde{L}_{\mathcal{Z}}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\leq\lambda e^{t_{2}}.

The lemma follows.
∎

We will also need to record how relative areas change under a map with controlled relative distortion.

Proposition 2.8
.

Suppose that
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Lebesgue measurable set with
m(E)>0
and
U\subset E
is a measurable subset. If
f:E\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a homeomorphism that satisfies
D(f,E)\leq\lambda
, then

\frac{1}{\lambda^{n}}\cdot\frac{m(U)}{m(E)}\leq\frac{m(f(U))}{m(f(E))}\leq\lambda^{n}\cdot\frac{m(U)}{m(E)}.

Proof.

By the hypotheses, the
Jacobian
J_{f}
exists almost everywhere on
U
.
Moreover, we have, for any
x\in U
where
J_{f}(x)
exists,

J_{f}(x)\leq\widetilde{L}_{f}(x)^{n},\quad J_{f}(x)\geq\widetilde{l}_{f}(x)^{n}.

It follows that if
f
has relative distortion
D(f,U)=\alpha
, then

\frac{\sup_{x\in U}J_{f}(x)}{\inf_{x\in U}J_{f}(x)}\leq\frac{\sup_{x\in U}\widetilde{L}_{f}(x)^{n}}{\inf_{x\in U}\widetilde{l}_{f}(x)^{n}}\leq\alpha^{n}.

Using the change-of-variables formula, we obtain

m\bigl(f(E)\bigr)=\int_{E}J_{f}(x)\,dx\;\geq\;\inf_{x\in U}J_{f}(x)\,m(E),

and

m\bigl(f(U)\bigr)=\int_{U}J_{f}(x)\,dx\;\leq\;\sup_{x\in U}J_{f}(x)\,m(U).

Combining these estimates yields

\frac{m\bigl(f(E)\bigr)}{m(f(U))}\;\geq\;\frac{\inf_{x\in U}J_{f}(x)}{\sup_{x\in U}J_{f}(x)}\,\frac{m(E)}{m(U)}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{\alpha^{n}}\,\frac{m(E)}{m(U)}.

The other inequality follows analogously and so this completes the proof.
∎

We will apply a variation of this result when
U\subset E
are measurable subsets of a surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
- the conclusion holds with
n=2
.

2.5.
The Lebesgue Density Theorem

A key ingredient to Dobbs’ proof, and thus to ours, is the following measure theoretical result. Recall that if
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Lebesgue measurable set and if
\epsilon>0
, then a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called an
\epsilon
-density point of
E
if

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{m(E\cap B(x,r))}{m(B(x,r))}\geq\epsilon,

if the limit exists.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a Lebesgue measurable set. Then almost every point of
E
is a
1
-density point of
E
.

We refer to, for example,
[
3
, p.45]
for this result.

3.
Dense curves

3.1.
The set-up

In this section we will prove Theorem
1.1
. With the Zorich map
\mathcal{Z}
as defined in Section
2.1
, we set
f=\mathcal{Z}\circ\mathcal{Z}
. Next, instead of considering all lines in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we will consider all lines passing through an arbitrary point
P=(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Now, as any line passing through
P
in the
x_{3}=p_{3}
plane will be mapped by
\mathcal{Z}
into a sphere, and thus
f
will map this line into a bounded region, we will remove these lines from consideration without affecting the result. Moreover, by the symmetry of the Zorich map, it is possible that lines in either plane
x_{1}=p_{1}
or
x_{2}=p_{2}
will be mapped by
\mathcal{Z}
into the same plane (c.f. the comment that
\mathcal{Z}
restricted to the
x_{1}=0
or
x_{2}=0
planes is an embedded copy of
\exp
). Therefore the image of such a line under
f
may be contained in a plane and thus cannot be dense in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For technical reasons, we will also remove the planes given by
x_{2}=\pm x_{1}
. Removing lines in these planes from consideration will not affect the result.

Every other line that passes through
P
may be parametrized as passing through a unique point of

(3.1)

Y=\{P+(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}):M(x_{1},x_{2})=1,x_{3}>0,x_{1}x_{2}(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})\neq 0\}.

Then
Y
is part of the boundary of a semi-infinite square beam. For
\alpha\in Y
, we denote by
L_{\alpha}
the unique line through
P
and
\alpha
.

Now, let
(q_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a dense sequence in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
|q_{n}|
is not
0
or
1
for all
n
. Then let
(\delta_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a decreasing sequence with
\delta_{n}\to 0
and
0<\delta_{n}<|q_{n}|
so that

\mathcal{U}=\{B(q_{n},\delta_{n}):n\geq 1\}

forms a countable base for the usual topology of
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus(S^{2}\cup\{0\})
. The fact we have excluded
0
and the unit sphere here will not affect the result, as if a set is dense in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus(S^{2}\cup\{0\})
, then it is dense in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Observe that
f(L_{\alpha})
is dense in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus(S^{2}\cup\{0\})
if and only if
f(L_{\alpha})\cap U\neq\emptyset
for all
U\in\mathcal{U}
. Let us therefore define
X_{U}
to be

X_{U}=\left\{\alpha\in Y:f(L_{\alpha})\cap U\neq\emptyset\right\}.

As each
U\in\mathcal{U}
is open and
f
is continuous, it follows that
X_{U}
is also open, and hence Lebesgue measurable. If we set

X^{\prime}=\bigcap_{U\in\mathcal{U}}X_{U},

then if each
X_{U}
has full measure in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, so does
X^{\prime}
, as a countable intersection of full measure sets. Now, given a set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, if almost every point in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is an
\epsilon
-density point of
X
for some
\epsilon>0
, then the set of
1
-density points for the complement of
X
has zero measure, and hence by the Lebesgue Density Theorem, Theorem
2.9
,
X
has full measure. Proving Theorem
1.1
then reduces to proving the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
U\subset\mathcal{U}
. Then there exists
\epsilon>0
such that each
\alpha\in Y
is an
\epsilon
-density point for
X_{U}
.

3.2.
Pre-images under the Zorich map

Towards a proof of Theorem
3.1
, we suppose that
U\in\mathcal{U}
and we set
x_{0}=q_{n}
and
r_{0}=\delta_{n}
so that
U=B(x_{0},r_{0})
. Recall that
0<r_{0}<|x_{0}|
so that
\overline{U}
avoids the origin. Moreover,
|x_{0}|\neq 1
. We set
R=\partial B(0,|x_{0}|)
,
H=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}(R)
,
\widetilde{U}=U\cap R
,
V=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}(U)
, and
\widetilde{V}=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}(\widetilde{U})
. By Lemma
2.1
, we have
H=\{x_{3}=\ln|x_{0}|\}
. As
|x_{0}|\neq 1
,
H
is not the
x_{3}=0
plane.

Recall that
h
from (
2.2
) is
\lambda
-bi-Lipschitz for some
\lambda>1
, and then that if
h
is extended by reflections to all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, then
h
is infinitesimally
\lambda
-bi-Lipschitz. We use this to show that there are disks of definite radius in each component of
\widetilde{V}\subset H
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Any connected component of
\widetilde{V}\subset H
contains a disk of radius at least
r_{0}(8\lambda e^{|x_{0}|})^{-1}
.

Proof.

We split into three cases, depending on the location of
\widetilde{U}
in
U
relative to the
x_{3}=0
plane.

(a)

For the first case,
\widetilde{U}\subset R\cap\{x_{3}>0\}
. Then
\widetilde{U}
is a spherical disk sitting on
R
of diameter at most
2r_{0}
. There is a branch of
\mathcal{Z}^{-1}
that maps
\widetilde{U}
homeomorphically and, in fact, in a
\lambda e^{|x_{0}|}
-bi-Lipschitz way, onto a topological disk contained in
\operatorname{int}(B_{0})\cap H
. It follows that this component of
\widetilde{V}
contains a disk of radius at least
r_{0}(\lambda e^{|x_{0}|})^{-1}
. All other components of
\widetilde{V}
are obtained from this one under the action of
G
. As
G
consists of isometries, the same lower bound holds for all components of
\widetilde{V}
.

(b)

For the second case,
\widetilde{U}\subset R\cap\{x_{3}<0\}
. This is analogous to the first case, except this time the initial component of
\widetilde{V}
is contained in
\operatorname{int}(B_{1})\cap H
.

(c)

For the final case, we consider when
\widetilde{U}
intersects the equator
R\cap\{x_{3}=0\}
. Then either
\widetilde{U}\cap\{x_{3}>0\}
or
\widetilde{U}\cap\{x_{3}<0\}
contains a spherical disk of diameter
r_{0}/4
. By applying the argument in the previous two cases to whichever situation we are in, we obtain a disk of radius at least
r_{0}(8\lambda e^{|x_{0}|})^{-1}
in each component of
\widetilde{V}
.

∎

This lemma shows that each component of
\mathcal{Z}^{-1}(U)
contains disks of a definite size contained in the plane
H
. We need to take another pre-image under
\mathcal{Z}
and for our purposes, it will suffice to consider pre-images of
H
. However, this has already been addressed by Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.3
. As
|x_{0}|\neq 1
, we have
\ln|x_{0}|\neq 0
and so for convenience, we will set
S=S_{\ln|x_{0}|}
. We recall that this means that if
|x_{0}|>1
then
S\subset B_{0}
and if
0<|x_{0}|<1
then
S\subset B_{1}
. We will also set
S_{g}=g(S)
and so by Lemma
2.3
, we have
f^{-1}(R)=(\mathcal{Z}\circ\mathcal{Z})^{-1}(R)=\bigcup_{g\in G}S_{g}
.

Each
S_{g}
is a cone with square cross-sections and a vertex lying in the plane
\{x_{3}=\ln\left|\ln|x_{0}|\right|\}
and, moreover,
S_{g}
has four faces. Pick one of the faces and call it
F
. For
\ln\left|\ln|x_{0}|\right|<t_{1}<t_{2}
, denote by
F(t_{1},t_{2})
the region

(3.2)

F(t_{1},t_{2}):=F\cap\{t_{1}<x_{3}<t_{2}\}.

Informally,
F(t_{1},t_{2})
is almost a flat rectangle.

Lemma 3.3
.

Given
|x_{0}|\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus\{0,1\}
, there exists
a>0
such that if
t_{1}>\ln\left|\ln|x_{0}|\right|
and
t_{2}>t_{1}+a
, then

\frac{e^{t_{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}-e^{t_{1}}>2\pi.

Moreover, we may choose
a
so that
e^{2a}>3
.

Proof.

Via a direct computation, we see that we can take

a>\ln\left[\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{2\pi}{|\ln|x_{0}||}+1\right)\right].

For the final claim, we just increase
a
if necessary.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
t_{1}>\ln\left|\ln|x_{0}|\right|
and let
t_{2}>t_{1}+a
, where
a
is from Lemma
3.3
. Then

\frac{m(f^{-1}(U)\cap F(t_{1},t_{2}))}{m(F(t_{1},t_{2}))}\geq C,

where
C
depends only on the input data
r_{0},|x_{0}|
and
\lambda
.

Proof.

First, by construction
\mathcal{Z}
maps
F
onto a quadrant of the plane
H
. Without loss of generality, we may assume this quadrant is

Q=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}):x_{1}\geq|x_{2}|,x_{3}=\ln|x_{0}|\}.

Our analysis for the other quadrants runs analogously. Then
\mathcal{Z}
maps
F(t_{1},t_{2})
onto a logarithmic square in
Q
. That is, two sides of
\mathcal{Z}(F(t_{1},t_{2}))
run along the lines
x_{1}=\pm x_{2}
, and the other two sides are arcs of circles with radii
e^{t_{1}}
and
e^{t_{2}}
respectively.

In particular,
\mathcal{Z}(F(t_{1},t_{2}))
contains a trapezoid
T
in
Q
whose vertical line segments are contained in the lines
x_{1}=e^{t_{1}}
and
x_{1}=e^{t_{2}}/\sqrt{2}
, and the other boundary line segments are contained in the lines
x_{1}=\pm x_{2}
. By the hypotheses and Lemma
3.3
, it follows that
T
is non-trivial.

If we compare the areas of
\mathcal{Z}(F(t_{1},t_{2}))
and
T
, we have

m(T)=(e^{t_{2}}/\sqrt{2}-e^{t_{1}})(e^{t_{2}}/\sqrt{2}+e^{t_{1}})=\frac{e^{2t_{2}}}{2}-e^{2t_{1}}

and

m(\mathcal{Z}(F(t_{1},t_{2})))=\frac{\pi}{4}(e^{2t_{2}}-e^{2t_{1}}).

It follows that

\frac{m(\mathcal{Z}(F(t_{1},t_{2})))}{m(T)}=\frac{\pi}{4}\left(\frac{e^{2(t_{2}-t_{1})}-1}{e^{2(t_{t}-t_{1})}/2-1}\right).

By the hypotheses,
t_{2}-t_{1}\geq a
and we may assume by Lemma
3.3
that
e^{2a}>3
. The real function
g(t)=\frac{t-1}{t/2-1}
is decreasing on
(2,\infty)
and it follows that we obtain

(3.3)

\frac{m(\mathcal{Z}(F(t_{1},t_{2})))}{m(T)}\leq\frac{\pi g(3)}{4}<2\pi.

Next, Lemma
3.3
implies that the width of the trapezoid
T
is at least
2\pi
.
Suppose first that
T
has width between
2\pi
and
4\pi
so that

(3.4)

m(T)\leq 4\pi(2e^{t_{1}}+4\pi).

Moreover, as
t_{1}>\ln|\ln|x_{0}||
, the condition that
e^{t_{2}}/\sqrt{2}-e^{t_{1}}\leq 4\pi
can be rearranged to

(3.5)

e^{t_{2}-t_{1}}\leq\sqrt{2}\left(1+\frac{4\pi}{|\ln|x_{0}||}\right).

Then
T
contains a column made up of squares taken alternately from
G(B_{0}\cap H)
and
G(B_{1}\cap H)
. As the left-hand edge of
T
has length
2e^{t_{1}}
, it follows that this column has a total of
2\lceil e^{t_{1}}\rceil
squares. By Lemma
3.2
, each pair of squares will contain a disk contained in
\widetilde{V}
of radius at least
r_{0}(8\lambda e^{|x_{0}|})^{-1}
.

It follows from this and (
3.4
) that

(3.6)

\frac{m(\widetilde{V}\cap T)}{m(T)}\geq\frac{\lceil e^{t_{1}}\rceil\cdot\pi r_{0}^{2}(8\lambda e^{|x_{0}|})^{-2}}{4\pi(2e^{t_{1}}+4\pi)}\geq\frac{r_{0}^{2}}{2^{9}\lambda^{2}e^{2|x_{0}|}}.

Putting our various estimates together, by Lemma
2.7
, Proposition
2.8
, (
3.3
), (
3.5
) and (
3.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{m(f^{-1}(U)\cap F(t_{1},t_{2}))}{m(F(t_{1},t_{2}))}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}e^{t_{2}-t_{1}}}\cdot\frac{m(\widetilde{V}\cap\mathcal{Z}(F(t_{1},t_{2}))}{m(\mathcal{Z}(F(t_{1},t_{2})))}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}e^{t_{2}-t_{1}}}\cdot\frac{m(\widetilde{V}\cap T)}{m(T)\cdot 2\pi}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{r_{0}^{2}}{2^{11}\lambda^{4}\pi e^{2|x_{0}|}(1+4\pi/|\ln|x_{0}||)}.

Now, if
T
has width larger than
4\pi
, then we can split
T
up into trapezoids which have width between
2\pi
and
4\pi
and so the proportion estimate above will persist.
∎

3.3.
Projections

Recall the parametrization of lines through
P
given by
Y
in (
3.1
). As
Y
has four faces, without loss of generality we may work with the face given by
x_{1}=1
, denoted by
Y_{1}
. The argument for the other three faces follows analogously.

For
M>>0
, let
\Omega_{M}
be the plane

\Omega_{M}=\left\{x_{1}=\frac{\pi}{2}+M\pi\right\}.

We define
\Pi_{M}
to be the nearest point projection of
Y_{1}
from
P
onto
\Omega_{M}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

The projection
\Pi_{M}
maps
Y_{1}
conformally into
\Omega_{M}
. In particular, the relative distortion
D(\Pi_{M},Y_{1})=1
.

Proof.

Consider
u\in Y_{1}
so that
u=(p_{1}+1,p_{2}+u_{2},p_{3}+u_{3})
for
u_{2}\in(-1,1)\setminus\{0\}
and
u_{3}>0
. Then
\Pi_{M}(u)=(\pi/2+M\pi,v_{2},v_{3})
for some
v_{2},v_{3}
in
\mathbb{R}
.

Suppose we set
P_{1}=(p_{1}+1,p_{2},p_{3})
,
P_{2}=(p_{1}+1,p_{2},p_{3}+u_{3})
,
P_{1}^{\prime}=(\pi/2+M\pi,p_{2},v_{3})
, and
P_{2}^{\prime}=(\pi/2+M\pi,p_{2},v_{3})
. Then
PP_{1}P_{2}
and
PP_{1}^{\prime}P_{2}^{\prime}
are similar triangles and it follows that if we set
c=\pi/2+M\pi-p_{1}
, we obtain

(3.7)

\frac{v_{3}-p_{3}}{u_{3}}=\frac{c}{1}

and so

v_{3}=cu_{3}+p_{3}.

Next, if we set
P_{3}=(p_{1}+1,p_{2}+u_{2},p_{3})
,
P_{4}=(p_{1}+1,p_{2}+u_{2},p_{3}+u_{3})
,
P_{3}^{\prime}=(\pi/2+M\pi,y_{2},p_{3})
, and
P_{4}^{\prime}=(\pi/2+M\pi,y_{2},y_{3})
, then
PP_{3}P_{4}
and
PP_{3}^{\prime}P_{4}^{\prime}
are similar triangles. From (
3.7
), we obtain

\frac{\sqrt{c^{2}+(v_{2}-p_{2})^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+u_{2}^{2}}}=\frac{v_{3}-p_{3}}{u_{3}}-c.

By simplifying this, we obtain

v_{2}=p_{2}+cu_{2}.

Putting this together, we have shown that

\displaystyle\Pi_{M}(p_{1}+1,p_{2}+u_{2},p_{3}+u_{3})

\displaystyle=(\pi/2+M\pi,p_{2}+cu_{2},p_{3}+cu_{3})

\displaystyle=P+c(1,u_{2},u_{3}),

from which the lemma follows.
∎

The point of introducing
\Omega_{M}
is that
f^{-1}(\widetilde{R})
lies on
f^{-1}(R)
. While there are infinitely many components of
f^{-1}(R)
, once the
x_{3}
-coordinate is large enough, there are components of
f^{-1}(R)
very close to
\Omega_{M}
.

To make this idea more precise, suppose that
M\in\mathbb{N}
is large and for
l\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
g_{l}\in G
be such that the corresponding component
S_{g_{l}}
of
Z^{-1}(H)
lies in a beam adjacent to
\Omega_{M}
and intersects the plane
x_{2}=l\pi
.
Moreover, for each
l\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
F_{l}\subset S_{g_{l}}
denote the face of
S_{g_{l}}
that is adjacent to
\Omega_{M}
.

There are clearly gaps between the union of the faces
F_{l}
that we need to address. The idea is to exclude certain strips from
\Omega_{M}
and then show that any line from
P
that goes through the remaining region must also pass through the union of the
F_{l}
’s.

To that end, suppose that
M>>0
and
s>0
are given, fix
l\in\mathbb{Z}
and for
\eta>0
define the vertical strip

(3.8)

K_{l}:=\left\{x_{1}=\frac{\pi}{2}+M\pi\right\}\times\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+(l-1)\pi+\eta,\frac{\pi}{2}+l\pi-\eta\right)\times(s,\infty).

Then
K_{l}
is a subset of
\Omega_{M}
adjacent to the face
F_{l}
.

We will be interested in those
K_{l}
that lie in the quadrant we are working in. More precisely, given
M
and
s
, let
l_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that strips
K_{l}
, for
l=-l_{0},\ldots,l_{0}
all lie in the region
x_{1}>|x_{2}|
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\delta>0
. Given
\eta>0
, by Lemma
2.4
, let
s_{0}>0
be sufficiently large that for
x_{3}>s_{0}
and any
x\in F_{l}
, we have
\operatorname{dist}(x,\Omega_{M})<\eta/3
. Then there exists
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that if
-l_{0}\leq l\leq l_{0}
, any line segment
L
from
p
passing through
Y_{1}
and
K_{l}
, as defined in (
3.8
) with the same
\eta
as above, intersects
F_{l}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
s
is larger than
s_{0}
. Then the intersection of
F_{l}
with the plane
x_{3}=s
is the line segment

\{\pi/2+M\pi\pm\xi\}\times[\pi/2+(l-1)\pi+\xi,\pi/2+l\pi-\xi]\times\{s\},

where
\sin\xi=e^{-s}|\ln|x_{0}||
by the computations in Lemma
2.4
, and the
\pm
corresponds to whether the face
F_{l}
is in front or behind
\Omega_{M}
from the point of view of
P
.

Now, let
L
be a ray from
P
passing through
K_{l}
. Its projection onto the
(x_{1},x_{2})
-plane makes an angle
\theta
with the
x_{1}
-axis, where
|\theta|\leq\pi/4
. Let

A=(\pi/2+M\pi,a_{2},a_{3})

be the intersection of
L
with
K_{l}
, with
a_{2}\in(\pi/2+(l-1)\pi+\eta,\pi/2+l\pi-\eta)
and
a_{3}>s
.

Consider the point
B=(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})\in L
where the ray’s
x_{1}
-coordinate matches the face, i.e.,
|b_{1}-(\pi/2+M\pi)|=\xi
. As
|\tan\theta|\leq 1
,

|b_{2}-a_{2}|\leq|\tan\theta|\cdot|b_{1}-(\pi/2+M\pi)|\leq 1\cdot\xi=\xi.

Substituting the bounds for
a_{2}
, the
x_{2}
-coordinate
b_{2}
satisfies

b_{2}\in[\pi/2+(l-1)\pi+\eta-\xi,\,\pi/2+l\pi-\eta+\xi].

Since
\xi<\eta/3
, we have
\eta-\xi>2\eta/3>\xi
. Thus

b_{2}\in(\pi/2+(l-1)\pi+\xi,\,\pi/2+l\pi-\xi),

and we conclude the ray

must intersect the face
F_{l}
.
∎

We next show that projecting from
Y_{1}
onto one of the faces
F_{l}
is close to projecting onto
\Omega_{M}
. To make this more precise, for
-l_{0}\leq l\leq l_{0}
, set

J_{l}=\Pi_{M}^{-1}(K_{l})\subset Y_{1}.

Then
J_{l}
is a subset of
Y_{1}
for which any ray from
P
through
J_{l}
is guaranteed to intersect
F_{l}
. It therefore makes sense to define the nearest point projection from
P
of
J_{l}
to
F_{l}
via the map

\Pi_{F_{l}}:J_{l}\to F_{l}.

Lemma 3.7
.

With the set-up above, if
\delta>0
is sufficiently small and
s,M
are sufficiently large, then the relative distortion of
\Pi_{F_{l}}
satisfies

D(\Pi_{F_{l}},J_{l})\leq 2.

Proof.

Fix
x\in J_{l}
and set
y=\Pi_{F_{l}}(x)\in F_{l}
. Since
F_{l}
is a
C^{1}
surface, it admits a well-defined tangent plane at every
point. Let
T_{l}(y)
denote the tangent plane to
F_{l}
at
y
, and let

\Pi_{y}:J_{l}\to T_{l}(y)

denote the projection of
J_{l}
from
P
onto this tangent plane.

It follows that the linear distortions of the projections onto
F_{l}
and
T_{l}(x)
coincide at
x
:

(3.9)

\tilde{L}_{\Pi_{F_{l}}}(x)=\tilde{L}_{\Pi_{y}}(x),\qquad\tilde{l}_{\Pi_{F_{l}}}(x)=\tilde{l}_{\Pi_{y}}(x).

For
x_{3}=s
sufficiently large, the tangent plane
T_{l}(x)
is nearly vertical, and so in a neighbourhood of
x
,
T_{l}(x)
is very close to
\Omega_{M}
. Since the pointwise Lipschitz constants of the projection depend continuously on
x
, we may choose
s
and
M
large enough so that

(3.10)

\tilde{L}_{\Pi_{y}}(x)\leq\sqrt{2}\tilde{L}_{\Pi_{M}}(x)\,\qquad\tilde{l}_{\Pi_{y}}(x)\geq\frac{\tilde{l}_{\Pi_{M}}(x)}{\sqrt{2}},\quad\text{for all }x\in J_{l}.

Combining (
3.9
), (
3.10
), and Lemma
3.5
, we get

D(\Pi_{F_{l}},J_{l})=\frac{\sup_{x\in J_{l}}\tilde{L}_{\Pi_{F_{l}}}(x)}{\inf_{x\in J_{l}}\tilde{l}_{\Pi_{F_{l}}}(x)}=\frac{\sup_{x\in J_{l}}\tilde{L}_{\Pi_{y}}(x)}{\inf_{x\in J_{l}}\tilde{l}_{\Pi_{y}}(x)}\leq 2\frac{\sup_{x\in J_{l}}\tilde{L}_{\Pi_{M}}(x)}{\inf_{x\in J_{l}}\tilde{l}_{\Pi_{M}}(x)}=2,

as required.
∎

3.4.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Let
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in Y
and, without loss of generality, we may assume that
x\in Y_{1}
so that

. Let

in the analogous way.

Given

, choose

) is less than

\max\{4\pi,a\}\leq w\leq 10\max\{4\pi,a\},

Following Section

, we construct the vertical strips

of

for

\frac{m(\Pi_{M}(E_{\delta})\cap\bigcup_{l=l_{1}}^{l_{2}}K_{l})}{m(\Pi_{M}(E_{\delta}))}\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\pi-2\eta}{\pi}\geq\frac{1}{4},

\frac{m(X_{U}\cap J_{l,\delta})}{m(J_{l,\delta})}\geq\frac{1}{D(\Pi_{F_{l}},J_{l,\delta})^{2}}\cdot\frac{m(f^{-1}(U)\cap F_{l}(t_{1},t_{2}))}{m(F_{l}(t_{1},t_{2}))}\geq\frac{C}{4}.
