 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report discusses international experiences from site selection of deep geological repositories
(DGR) for radioactive waste disposal. Based on experiences from three countries with recent or
ongoing site selections, the report describes different site selection approaches and summarizes
lessons learned. The German and Swiss programs are two slightly different examples of one end of
the spectrum of potential siting approaches: Starting with the entire country, a technically based
downselection is performed to select the repository site. The Canadian program is an example of the
other end of the spectrum: Here, the program started with an extensive engagement program with
Canadians to collaboratively develop a socially acceptable and technically sound national approach
for the long-term management of used nuclear fuel. This was followed by the launch of a
collaboratively developed consent-based siting process designed to seek an informed and willing
host with suitable sites, considering both technical and community well-being site requirements.
The Swiss program recently finalized its site selection process, proposing a site in argillite host rock
in Northern Switzerland. Starting from a “white map” of the country, suitable geological siting regions
were first identified based on existing geologic knowledge (Stage 1), followed by a narrowing down of
the locations identified earlier based on quantitative provisional safety analyses and a safety-related
comparison (Stage 2). Three siting regions remained for further analysis, all in the northern part of
the country in the same argillite host rock with only moderate variations in characteristics and
properties. Based on a comprehensive characterization and drilling program conducted for each
siting region (Stage 3), the Swiss program was able to demonstrate that post-closure safety can be
ensured in all of them. In late 2024, the Swiss waste management organization Nagra submitted its
license application for one of the three sites, which compared to the other offers a superior
geological barrier, has the highest stability against erosion, and features considerable flexibility for
the layout of the repository. Switzerland has a long history of URLs, hosting two international
research sites (Grimsel in crystalline rock and Mont Terri in argillite), both of which provided valuable
information in the site selection process. Switzerland’s regulation requires finding a site that is
sufficiently safe, protecting the population from radioactive waste for a million years.
The German program is currently making progress on siting in the first of three siting phases. The
search began a few years ago with a white map of Germany, initially screening out areas that based
on existing geological information could be disqualified for one or more hazards to avoid. This leaves
a large portion of the country to further investigate in the future. Currently, the program is
determining how to best rank sites from different rock types (argillite, crystalline, bedded salt, and
domal salt), which have different disposal concepts, and may rely on engineered barriers to lesser or
greater degrees. Although following a procedure analogous to Switzerland, Germany by regulation
must demonstrate it has identified the “best” site for a DGR in all the country. The goal of finding the
“best” site rather than a “sufficiently safe” site is ambitious, as it makes the site selection and
comparison process complex and time-consuming. In Germany, the target of selecting a site may be
decades away, unless changes are made to accelerate the search process.
Canada has taken a different approach. In 2010, the Nuclear Waste Management Organization
(NWMO) initiated a consent-based siting process to seek an informed and willing host for a DGR to
contain and isolate Canada’s used nuclear fuel. The process was developed over two years of
dialogue to seek input from a broad cross-section of Canadians who shared ideas, experience, and
best advice on what an open, transparent, fair, and inclusive process should include. By 2012, 22
municipalities and Indigenous communities had voluntarily shown interest in learning more about
the site selection process. However, there was no obligation to host the DGR until the safety of the
site was confirmed, the potential impacts on community well-being were assessed, and the residents
of the interested communities had expressed their willingness to host the project based on mutually
derived hosting agreements. The number of communities was gradually narrowed down through a
series of multidisciplinary technical assessments and community well-being studies. In 2024, NWMO

selected Wabigoon Lake Ojibway Nation and the Township of Ignace area as the host for the DGR.
Work is currently ongoing to prepare for the regulatory decision-making process.
The United States (US) site selection program started in the 1950s with an initial exclusive focus on
salt as a host rock. The Nuclear Waste Policy Act in 1982 formalized a significant effort to locate two
repositories for spent nuclear fuel (SNF) and defense high-level waste (HLW). Using a search process
that started at the national level and became more refined and detailed with time, nine potential
sites were proposed, which were downselected to five, and eventually three. Two key tenets of the
US search were to maintain a diversity of host rock types among proposed sites, and to champion
the use of already-contaminated federal sites. During the search, an amendment to the Nuclear
Waste Policy Act in 1987 specified Yucca Mountain as the site for the first repository, and the search
for a second repository also stopped. Starting in the 1970s, the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP)
was sited in bedded salt for defense transuranic waste. WIPP has been a licensed and operating
DGR since 1999 and is a success story in the long history of repository siting in the US.
In 2002, the Yucca Mountain environmental impact statement was submitted to Congress. The State
of Nevada protested, and Congress overruled their protest to select the site. In 2008, the Yucca
Mountain License Application was submitted to the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, which started
its regulatory review. However, the next year a change in administration defunded the Yucca
Mountain Project because it was “not a workable option.” Efforts to re-start the siting process in
terms of consent-based siting approach (as recommended in 2012 by the “Blue Ribbon Commission
on America’s Nuclear Future”) have since come and gone. The current waste management strategy
first seeks to site a consolidated interim storage facility before siting a DGR. Any approach the US
may take (aside from proceeding with Yucca Mountain) will require a change in regulations.
The final section of this report summarizes important lessons learned from this international review
of siting practices, as realized across four countries. There is a spectrum of siting strategies, from a
technical downselection process to a solely volunteer process. Either end of this spectrum has been
demonstrated to work, but the process must accommodate both the social and the technical aspects
to be successful.
There are many factors to consider in a national DGR siting process. Since the 1950s, when geologic
disposal was first considered, the needed technical basis and understanding required for siting and
safety analysis have been developed thoroughly. It is understood today that a successful siting
process needs to find a balance between technical considerations and social aspects. On the
technical side, the Swiss, German and Canadian programs applied quite similar site evaluation
practices and used similar site selection criteria. The largest difference between these countries was
in their approaches to building stakeholder confidence. Canada used a volunteer process that
empowers individual sites to come forward, while Switzerland and Germany have used a technical
approach that builds confidence through a phased exclusion and weighting of sites, starting with a
“white map” of the entire country. Another difference appears in the order the technical and social
aspects of the siting process are applied. In Switzerland and Germany, engagement of stakeholders
begins after a high-level site screening and exclusion process. In Canada, the stakeholder
engagement came first, with screening and exclusion applied only to those sites that volunteered.
One of the main factors in siting a DGR is the nature of the local geology. The wide diversity of
geologies leads to a diversity of disposal concepts for deep geological disposal, at least in detail
(e.g., the extent and nature of engineered components in a repository design). This inherent
variability makes comparison of different geologies a complex endeavor, because some designs may
vary in their reliance on engineered components. Comparisons should not be made between the
geologies alone, but between the disposal concepts, which include both man-made and geological
components. The availability of existing data at the repository depth is strongly correlated to the rock
type and the potential existence of natural resources for exploitation. Stakeholder confidence can be
improved through the development or updating of host-rock specific siting criteria and a clear

roles of each party in the siting, design, construction, operation, and closure of the DGR.

 SPENT FUEL AND HIGH-LEVEL WASTE DISPOSITION/GENERIC DGR
 CONSIDERATIONS
This fiscal year 2026 report serves as an M3 milestone (M3SF-26SN010202042) for the Generic
Deep Geologic Repository (DGR) Considerations workpackage at Sandia National Laboratories
SF-26SN01020204. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory was funded through their workpackage
on the same topic (SF-26LB01020203). The report presents historical background on the siting
process for a deep geological repository (DGR) for radioactive waste, as it has occurred or is
occurring in four countries that span the spectrum of different siting strategies. In the future, this
work may provide context to inform the US radioactive waste disposal program in future efforts to
site a DGR for spent nuclear fuel (SNF) and high-level radioactive waste (HLW).
Beyond this report, the review of international siting experiences will continue in fiscal year 2026,
with a focus on (1) developing guidelines for preliminary site screening using qualitative and
quantitative criteria, and (2) reviewing site characterization frameworks for host-rock specific site
evaluations.
A spectrum of siting approaches exists. One end of the spectrum is a technical downselect nationally
driven approach that starts with a large area (often the entire country) and downselects based on
technical and other criteria. This approach is exemplified to some degree by Switzerland and
Germany. The other end of the spectrum is a purely volunteer approach, where the siting process
evaluates sites from interested communities that come forward, and (if there is more than one
volunteer site) downselects based on a comparative evaluation. This approach is exemplified to
some degree by Canada. Historically, the US approach to siting has varied. In the early years (1960s
to 1980s), there were aspects of the technical downselect approach and after the Yucca Mountain
Project was defunded in 2010, a consent-based and volunteer-based siting approach was proposed
but not actually implemented.
The chapters of this report can be considered stand-alone reports on the siting approach or history of
the different countries. Each country has its own references and lessons learned section. The first
section is a history of the Swiss siting approach, which has recently selected a site using a technical
downselect approach. The second section is a summary of the German siting approach, which is
currently in the first phase of its technical downselect site selection process. The third section is a
summary of the Canadian siting approach, which has recently identified its preferred site after
downselecting from multiple volunteer locations. The fourth section is a summary of the US siting
history, discussing some of the historical artifacts that influence the process. The US is currently
preparing for a potential restart of its siting process, after historically selecting a site for SNF and
HLW (Yucca Mountain). The final section is a discussion of lessons learned and themes from the
previous chapters, including a synthesis of the spectrum of approaches.

1 SITE SELECTION IN SWITZERLAND

1.1 INTRODUCTION
Nagra (National Co-operative for the Disposal of Radioactive Waste) was established by the Swiss
federal government in 1972 to design and execute a program for the safe disposal of the country’s
high-, intermediate-, and low-level radioactive waste (Zuidema & Schneider, 2008). This program
included waste inventory and characterization, collection and evaluation of geologic data needed for
site selection and safety assessment of potential disposal locations, repository design, performance
assessment analysis, database development, international collaboration, communication with the
public, and providing expert services to third parties (Frank, 1999; Vomvoris et al., 2013). The Swiss
federal government provides the legal and regulatory framework for management and disposal of
radioactive waste.
A series of desk and field studies were initiated in the 1970s to identify suitable host rocks and
regions within Switzerland for developing a geologic repository for the disposal of HLW. A report in
1978 (VSE, 1978) outlined a review of HLW disposal options in Switzerland, with crystalline rock and
Opalinus Clay identified as potential host rocks. The Atomic Act ruling in 1978 set out requirements
for the demonstration that safe disposal of radioactive waste in Switzerland is feasible as a
prerequisite for the construction of any new nuclear power plants (i.e., closing the nuclear fuel cycle);
this included demonstrating engineering feasibility, long-term safety to humans and the
environment, and site feasibility. This was the basis for Projekt Gewaehr (Project Guarantee), which
launched a series of field studies in Switzerland (Figure 1-1) and covered a wide range of potential
host rocks (Nagra, 2009; Vomvoris et al., 2013). Based on the results of extensive field
investigations, a decision was reached in 1994 that HLW repositories in Switzerland should focus on
the Opalinus Clay as a host rock (Zuidema & Schneider, 2008). A subsequent phase, beginning in
2002 (Project Entsorgungsnachweis – disposal certification), looked at the feasibility for siting a HLW
repository within the Opalinus Clay as a host rock (Nagra, 2002).

1.2 OVERVIEW OF REGULATORY PROCESS AND SITE SELECTION PHASE
As described in the Nagra Research Development & Demonstration plan for the disposal of
radioactive waste in Switzerland (Nagra, 2009), there are three acts that provide the legal framework
for the geologic disposal of radioactive waste in Switzerland: the Nuclear Energy Act (2003), the
Environmental Protection Act (1983), and the Spatial Planning Act (1979).
The following requirements are derived from these acts and associated ordinances:
 • The Nuclear Energy Act requires monitored geologic disposal, including the possibility of
 retrievability during emplacement and an observation period until final closure of the
 repository.
 • The federal government is responsible for licensing the site characterization work and the
 construction, operation, and closure of a repository, with the general license to be approved
 by parliament and subject to a national referendum.
 • Disposal must occur within Switzerland (no export/import of waste).
 • Site selection is to be based on a Sectoral Plan under the leadership of the federal
 government.
The concept portion of the Sectoral Plan for the Geologic Repository (SFOE, 2008) describes the
procedure to be followed, the roles of the different participants, and the technical selection criteria
and project timing.
The development of the Sectoral Plan to oversee the siting and development of a DGR for HLW was
begun in 2006 and completed in 2008. This effort was led by the Swiss Federal Office of Energy
(SFOE), supported by the Federal Office for Spatial Development, the Swiss Federal Nuclear Safety
Inspectorate, the Commission for Radioactive Waste Disposal, and the Federal Nuclear Safety
Commission (SFOE, 2008; Ernst et al., 2010; 2013; Vomvoris et al., 2013), and had strong
involvement of a wide range of interest groups and broad consultation. The Sectoral Plan lays out the
safety and technical feasibility criteria to govern the siting of DGR for the disposal of high-level as
well as low- and intermediate-level radioactive waste, discusses spatial and socio-economic factors
to be considered, defines a three-stage procedure for the site selection process, and ensures that
the process is fair, transparent, and participatory in nature. This process involves the Swiss federal
government agencies listed above, as well as cantonal authorities and consultation with neighboring
countries.
The overall configuration of the Sectoral Plan is illustrated in Figure 1-2. While the federal
government plays a major role in overseeing this process, the cantons perform their own spatial
planning, and any conflicts need to be resolved between the siting cantons, neighboring cantons,
and the federal authorities, with the Federal Council taking the final decision based on all of the
reviews and consultations. While the sites being considered are proposed by Nagra and reviewed by
the regulator and other formal committees, regional conferences involving cantonal representatives,
local officials, and the public were used to discuss the location of surface facilities at the potential
sites being considered with respect to environmental and land use issues (Ernst et al., 2013). These
interactions promoted clear communication with local stakeholders and were seen as a critical
component of the site selection process (Figure 1-3).

1.2.2 WEIGHTING CRITERIA
Each of the technical criteria identified in the Sectoral Plan were used in the evaluation of the
prospective candidate sites. The criteria were ranked as being very favorable, favorable, partially
favorable, or unfavorable; see details in Nagra (2014a). In addition, each criterion was evaluated for
each specific location under consideration with respect to whether they had the greatest safety
margin, a medium safety margin, or the smallest safety margin. Overall rankings based on this
methodology were used to determine the site with the highest technical suitability.

1.4 ROLE OF UNDERGROUND RESEARCH LABORATORIES IN
 SWITZERLAND
Switzerland hosts two international underground research laboratories (URLs), Grimsel and Mont
Terri, which have played a prominent role in evaluating key processes related to barrier performance
and repository safety, especially during the early phases of the program. Field tests conducted in
URLs provide critical information that can be used to validate numerical models for long-term
performance and safety assessment (e.g., Pusch, 2008; Vomvoris et al., 2013; Birkholzer et al.,
2019). The two Swiss URLs are sited in two prominent host rock types considered for HLW
repositories, argillite and crystalline rock, and have hosted a wide range of experiments conducted
by international researchers to assess key processes related to the performance of engineered and
natural barriers in safely storing HLW materials (e.g., Bossart and Milnes, 2018; Blechschmidt et al.,
2023). The field experiments and associated modeling work conducted at Grimsel
(https://grimsel.com/gts-projects/overview-of-ongoing-gts-projects) and Mont Terri
(https://www.mont-terri.ch/en/experiments) have provided important generic (i.e., non-site specific)
information providing confidence in the models used to assess the long-term performance and
evaluate the safety case for proposed repository sites within Switzerland.

1.5 PERFORMANCE AND SAFETY ASSESSMENT
Performance and safety assessment studies are a key component, in addition to detailed field
investigations, in the safety case process that evaluates the suitability of a site for hosting a HLW
repository. Here we define performance assessments as predictive studies that evaluate the long-
term performance of barriers, whereas safety assessments denote radionuclide transport modeling
to calculate maximum dose rates and compare them to the safety requirements. As part of Phase 2
of the Sectoral Plan assessment, Nagra was required to develop provisional safety assessments for
at least one site within each of the regions under consideration to facilitate a quantified safety-based
comparison of the sites (Poller et al., 2011; Zuidema & Schneider, 2013; Nagra, 2014a;b). The
safety cases needed to demonstrate how the different engineered and geologic barriers contribute to
long-term safety and evaluate how they perform in the context of a conceptual model for
radionuclide release. In Switzerland, the safety assessment timeframe for the HLW repository is one
million years, and specific model parameters for the reference case were specified by ENSI, as
described by Poller et al. (2011) and Nagra (2014b;2024c).
Papafotiuo et al. (2022) present the results of Nagra’s performance assessment modeling for the
sites identified following Stage 2. These models allowed for assessing the performance of the host
rock as a barrier to transport, the influence of the repository design on safety, the impact of
uncertainty on safety, and how the system would respond to different types of geologic scenarios
(such as ice ages, deep erosion and fault activation). The models also evaluated the long-term
repository performance through the incorporation of coupled processes that impact radionuclide
release and transport. This approach is documented in much greater detail in Nagra reports (e.g.,
Nagra, 2024b, c), which use thousands of probabilistic simulations to derive estimates of the
maximum dose rates for the studied sites under a variety of scenarios. These performance and
safety assessment modeling tools can then be used to compare the different sites, as schematically
illustrated in Figure 1-9.

1.6 SWISS LESSONS LEARNED
1.6.1 SOCIAL ACCEPTANCE AND COMMUNICATION
The Swiss siting program underwent a major change with the adoption of the Sectoral Plan in 2008.
This plan expanded previous efforts, which focused on technical feasibility, to include involvement of
regional and local stakeholders, with extensive outreach and communications efforts throughout the
process. Early efforts to site a low- and intermediate-level waste repository (the proposed Wellenberg
repository in central Switzerland) were met with local opposition, which halted these efforts (Nagra,
2009; Flüeler, 2024). Local acceptance has long been seen as a key challenge for siting geologic
repositories for HLW (Seidl et al., 2013; Flüeler, 2024). For this new approach, the geologic disposal
of HLW was viewed as an issue of national importance, and the roles and responsibilities of the
siting process were clearly identified, with a process owner, an implementer, and a regulator. The
revised approach also recognized the importance of land use planning involving local stakeholders
as part of the process.
Under the Sectoral Plan, a much more concerted effort was made to include extensive public
engagement. Over 40 plenary assemblies were held by the regional conference for the Zürich
Nordost study area alone (Flüeler, 2024), including technical working groups on surface
infrastructure, safety, regional development, and infrastructure communities. Nagra and all involved
government entities have strived to make the site selection process as transparent and accessible to
the public as possible, with reports and data available online in multiple languages (Figure 1-10).

1.6.2 STARTING FROM A WHITE MAP
A great deal of research conducted by Nagra prior to the initiation of the Sectoral Plan led them to
focus on locations hosted in Opalinus Clay for a HLW repository. However, at the onset of the
Sectoral Plan, this newly reborn program began by considering all of Switzerland as being a potential
site. The overall approach, as laid out in the sectoral plan (SFOE, 2008) was noted by Flüeler (2024)
as starting with a “white map” and making sure the site selection is primarily being focused on
safety. This approach was positively received by the public, who viewed a comparative evaluation of
all possible regions and sites in Switzerland as fair and balanced.

2 SITE SELECTION IN GERMANY
2.1 INTRODUCTION
Germany has a long history of research and exploration work focusing on the disposal of HLW and
SNF in domal salt. In the 1970s, the Gorleben site in Northern Germany was selected as a potential
location for a DGR for HLW disposal in a salt dome. Later, the site was also chosen to host an above-
ground interim storage facility, which opened in 1983. This plan led to decades of intense protests
and civil disobedience from the local population and environmentalists, making the site an iconic
symbol of Germany's anti-nuclear movement. Gorleben became such a contentious political issue
that in 1999 the German Government established an independent advisory group (referred to as
AKEnd) to develop a procedure and criteria to select a site for a DGR. In 2000 they announced a
temporary halt to any testing or characterization at the Gorleben salt dome as part of a consensus-
building process. The group published a final report in 2002 with recommendations for a new site
selection process. This report was very influential in subsequent discussions among policymakers
and stakeholders, and it essentially led to a “reset” and later “new start from scratch” of Germany’s
disposal program.
In 2011, Germany made the decision to phase out nuclear power following the Fukushima disaster
in Japan. While the transition away from nuclear power has been challenging, as it led to a greater
reliance on fossil fuels, it helped with the national consensus building on the need to find a
permanent solution for the disposal of the Germany’s HLW and SNF. Phasing out nuclear power
essentially decoupled the waste disposal issue from the continued operation of nuclear power
plants. In July 2013, Germany adopted a new national law on the Search and Selection of a Site for a
Repository for Highly Radioactive Waste (StandAG). To achieve a national consensus, the StandAG
was first evaluated by a joint federal/state committee of members representing different interests,
the “Commission on the Storage of High-Level Radioactive Waste.”
The Commission developed criteria and recommendations for selecting a repository site that ensures
safe disposal for one million years. Based on the commission’s final report, the further developed
Site Selection Act (StandAG) was amended in May 2017. According to amended act, all regions in
Germany with suitable geologies such as rock salt (bedded and domal), clay rock, and crystalline
rock (such as granite) needed to be considered in a multi-stage site selection procedure. Instead of
pursuing a volunteer-based approach like Canada or the United Kingdom, Germany believed that a
site selection based on clear technical criteria would be best suited for gaining broad consensus. In
some ways, the German approach is like the criteria-based site selection framework that led to a
successful site selection in Switzerland in 2024. However, there is a major difference: in Germany,
the goal is explicitly to find the best possible site in the country whereas in Switzerland (and most
other countries) the goal is to select a site that can reliably contain the waste for up to one million
years.
With the development of the legal framework, Germany also redefined responsibilities for radioactive
waste disposal, which in 2017 was moved from industry to the Federal Government. BGE, the
Federal Company for Radioactive Waste Disposal, was entrusted with implementing site selection
procedures for a final repository, particularly for heat-generating radioactive waste. BGE belongs to
the Federal Ministry for the Environment, Climate Action, Nature Conservation and Nuclear Safety
(BMUKN), which acts as its sole shareholder and provides technical and legal supervision. BASE, the
Federal Office for the Safety of Nuclear Waste Management, is the regulator. BASE performs
regulatory, licensing, and supervisory tasks concerning disposing of, storing, handling, and
transporting HLW. BASE also regulates the site selection procedure for a disposal site for radioactive
waste, monitors the completion of the process, and organizes public participation in the search for a
site. Public participation and transparency play an important role in the site selection. Note that
BASE also belongs to the BMUKN, meaning that both the operator and the regulator report to the

February 2026                                                                                             19

same federal ministry. Meanwhile, BGR, the Federal Institute for Geosciences and Natural Resources
(i.e., the German Geological Survey), is tasked to provide the German Government with independent
and neutral advice on all geoscientific and geotechnical issues involved in the Federal Government's
nuclear repository projects.
BGE initiated the search for a repository site in 2017 by identifying an initial set of regions in
Germany that, based on existing geological information, seemed suitable for hosting a repository. In
an interim report issued in 2020, the identified areas still included 54% of Germany’s onshore area,
meaning a significant level of downselection still needed to be done before a few siting regions will
be selected to be investigated in more detail. In August 2022, BGE announced that the reduction
from a so-called “white map” of Germany to sub-areas to a few siting regions would be delayed
considerably. In addition, the predecessor to the BMUKN (the BMUV) declared in November 2022
that the goal of selecting a final site in 2031 as originally stipulated by the law will be missed by a
wide margin. Some studies conducted by BGE and BASE suggest that a decision on a site can be
expected by 2068 at the earliest if all available locations in Germany are evaluated according to
StandAG requirements. This speaks to the complexity of selecting a repository site starting from an
entire country and comparing a broad range of host rock options.
Since the delayed schedule became public, several German stakeholders have called for an
acceleration of the program, which would require modifications to the multi-stage site selection
procedure. Both BGE and BASE have stated that a 2050 timeline for site selection should be
achievable provided that meaningful modifications to the procedure are made (BASE, 2025).
Meanwhile, the waste from Germany’s nuclear program (consisting of SNF and HLW from
reprocessing related to the fleet of approximately thirty shut-down nuclear power plants) will
continue to be stored at temporary storage facilities near decommissioned nuclear power stations
and in central interim repositories.

2.2 OVERVIEW OF REGULATORY PROCESS AND SITE SELECTION PHASES
The StandAG establishes a three-phase, transparent, science-based, and self-learning process for
finding a DGR for HLW. The process, designed to be transparent and inclusive of public participation,
aims to find a site that guarantees the highest possible level of safety for one million years. Criteria
for site selection are based on scientific knowledge and technological state-of-the-art.
Figure 2-1 shows the three phases for site selection defined in the StandAG. The phases proceed
with an increasing level of detail over a decreasing number of potential regions and total area until
the proposal of the best possible site at the end of the third phase. A parliamentary decision is
required at the end of each phase to move into the next phase.
The objective of Phase I is to identify potential sub-areas in Germany based on existing geoscientific
data, then narrowing them down to a few regions that are to be considered in subsequent phases for
surface and subsurface exploration. Phase I started in 2017 with a white map of Germany without a
priori specifying any areas to be included or excluded from the search. The first step of Phase I
applied legally defined exclusion criteria, minimum requirements, and geoscientific weighting criteria
for determining sub-areas to expect favorable geological conditions for the safe disposal of HLW.
Completed by BGE in 2020, Step 1 resulted in the selection of 90 sub-areas that cover
approximately 54% of the onshore area of Germany (BGE, 2020).
The German site selection program is currently in the second step of Phase I. The goal of this step is
to apply additional criteria and preliminary safety evaluations to further narrow the sub-areas to a
manageable number of potential siting regions. These regions will be large enough to hold a
repository and suitable for detailed exploration (i.e., they potentially include the site providing the
highest level of safety in Germany). The overall number and size of these siting regions will be small
enough to be characterized in a reasonable amount of time and effort level with geoscientific surface

February 2026                                                                                              20

investigations like 3D seismic surveys and borehole-based explorations. Given that the sub-areas
total 194,157 km2, a significant areal reduction is required from sub-areas to siting regions,
therefore much more stringent criteria for site comparison and downselection need to be applied.
BGE is currently in the middle of this downselection process and expects that Step 2 of Phase I will
be finalized in late 2027.

Figure 2-1. Schematic overview of the three phases of the site selection procedure in Germany
 according to StandAG, the Repository Site Selection Act (BGE, 2020).

Phase II will involve exploration of the selected siting regions using detailed surface-based and
borehole-based characterization to further reduce the number of sites for potential underground
investigation to be conducted in Phase III. According to current plans, the few potential sites
remaining after Phase II will be further explored in Phase III with URLs for detailed subsurface
characterization and testing. A comparative assessment of the explored sites will then be made,
leading to the proposal of the best possible site for the repository.
BASE investigated potential acceleration of the process by modifying to the complex three-phase
procedure described above (BASE, 2025). Most promising are the following proposed changes: (1)
Limits on the number of siting regions selected after Phase I (i.e., a maximum of 6 siting regions), (2)
Accelerated permitting for surface and subsurface investigations and borehole drilling, (3) Site
selection based on surface and borehole investigation rather than URLs, and (4) Merging of Phase II
and III. Discussions among German stakeholders are ongoing in 2025, at the time of writing this
report, but to date no changes to the current law have been made.

2.3 SITING CRITERIA
The regulatory framework in the StandAG defines three different categories of quantitative and
qualitative siting criteria, which were applied in the order: (1) exclusion criteria, (2) minimum
requirements, and (3) qualitative geoscientific weighting criteria. Below we briefly describe each of
these categories together with a few examples.

2.3.1 EXCLUSION CRITERIA
The exclusion criteria from the StandAG regulation are (1) large-scale vertical motion of the land
surface, (2) proximity to active fault zones, (3) influence of present or past mining or drilling activity,

February 2026                                                                                                 21

(4) seismic activity, (5) volcanic activity, and (6) the presence of young groundwater (Table 2-1). As a
first step in the siting process, these criteria were used to exclude areas where one or more of the six
criteria are not met.
Some exclusion areas are applied together with a defined buffer zone around features that are to be
avoided, such as active fault zones or volcanic activity. These are to be defined on a case-by-case
basis, though minimum distances are given in the StandAG. For example, a buffer zone of at least 1
km around active faults and 10 km around volcanic activity.

Table 2-1. Exclusion criteria from Section 22 of the StandAG (BGE, 2020)

2.3.2 MINIMUM REQUIREMENTS
Minimum requirements are quantitative measures for the performance of the natural barrier
(“containment-providing rock zone” in the StandAG), with regards to its (1) hydraulic conductivity, (2)
thickness, (3) minimum depth, (4) area, and (5) long-term barrier integrity (Table 2-2). If one of the
five requirements cannot be fulfilled, the area is not suitable to host a repository and is hence
excluded from the search. Note that the containment-providing rock zone is defined in the German
regulation as part of the rock strata that ensures safe containment of the radioactive waste in a
repository. It can be larger than the actual host rock zone in geologic settings where overlying and
underlying rock zones can provide additional barrier functions. The StandAG has defined a special
rule for crystalline rock if an effective containment zone cannot be demonstrated unless significantly
higher demands are placed on the long-term integrity of the engineered barriers. In such cases,
evidence must be provided that technical and geotechnical barriers can ensure the safe
containment of radionuclides for one million years.

Table 2-2. Minimum requirements from Section 23 of the StandAG (BGE, 2020)

2.3.3 GEOSCIENTIFIC WEIGHTING CRITERIA
The StandAG also defines eleven geoscientific weighting criteria for the containment-providing rock
zone: (1) transport of radioactive substances by groundwater movement, (2) configuration of the
rock bodies, (3) ease of spatial characterization, (4) long-term stability of favorable conditions, (5)
favorable rock mechanical properties, (6) tendency to form fluid flow paths, (7) gas generation, (8)
temperature tolerance, (9) retention capacity, (10) hydrochemical conditions, and (11) protection by
the overburden. These weighting criteria may be considered the starting point for definition of so-
called “safety functions” (i.e., safety principles) which can be used in site evaluations via assessment
of “safety function indicators”, a common practice in many international programs (IAEA, 2012). In
Phase I of the siting process, these weighting criteria are applied in a mostly qualitative manner, by
using a rating scheme that for each criterion classifies the previously identified sub-areas as
“favorable,” “conditionally favorable” or “less favorable.” The purpose of applying the geoscientific
weighting criteria is to enable comparative evaluation of areas regarding their suitability as repository
sites. The StandAG provides detailed definitions of the weighting functions and ratings (Section 24 of
the StandAG) but leaves it at the discretion of BGE to determine how the overall evaluation of the
respective criteria should ultimately be conducted.
Information on all geoscientific weighting criteria can be found in Appendix A. As an example, the first
table in Appendix A lists quantitative criteria for transport of radioactive substances by groundwater
movement in the containment zone. In this case, a favorable rating is given if the distance velocity of
the groundwater is less than 0.1 mm/year, the characteristic hydraulic conductivity of the host rock
is less than 10-12 m/s, the characteristic effective diffusion coefficient is less than 10-11 m2/s, and
porosity is less than 20%. These quantitative criteria denote very low-permeability host rock with
minimal groundwater movement and low diffusive transport potential.
The regulations in the StandAG provide a set of secondary indicators, referred to as “planning-
scientific weighting criteria.” These refer to the spatial relationship between repository sites and
other land uses. There are three groups of such land uses, with Group 1 including land use for cities
and villages, and groundwater uses, Group 2 including land uses for natural and regional parks, and
cultural assets, and Group 3 including land uses for subsurface resources such as hydrocarbons or
critical minerals, geothermal energy production, and subsurface energy storage. Higher importance
is given to Group 1 versus Group 2 and Group 3.

2.4 IDENTIFICATION OF SITING REGIONS IN PHASE I
The German disposal program is currently in Phase I of the site selection procedure, where the goals
are to first reduce the white map of Germany to sub-areas with tentatively favorable geological

February 2026                                                                                               23

conditions (Step 1) and then to further narrow the sub-areas to a manageable number of potential
siting regions suitable for further exploration in subsequent phases (Step 2). In 2025, at the writing
of this report, Germany is in the middle of the second step in Phase I. Below we describe the
methodologies and (preliminary) outcomes of each of these steps.
The identification of preliminary siting regions in Phase I is done based on existing geoscientific data
provided by federal and state geological surveys and other agencies, such as the BGR. New
geoscientific data will be added through a site characterization program once Phase II and Phase III
of the site selection start. To ensure a seamless and effective handover of existing data, in 2020
Germany adopted a new law, the so-called Geological Data Act, which regulates the transmission
and permanent safeguarding of geological data from the geological surveys for the fulfillment of
public tasks such as the site selection procedure. This law has made an important contribution to
the transparency and efficiency of the process.

2.4.1 STEP 1 OF PHASE I
Step 1 of the site selection procedure started with application of the exclusion criteria followed by
application of the minimum requirements. Areas were excluded if any one of the six exclusions
criteria was met or if any one of the five minimum requirements was not fulfilled. Finally, BGE
evaluated the remaining area by means of the eleven geoscientific weighting criteria. The latter
turned out to be a less straightforward process, as the categorization into “favorable,” “less
favorable” or “unfavorable” indicators was based on a qualitative evaluation of BGE experts. BGE
developed an evaluation module that provided experts with interactive assistance and guided them
through the evaluation process, together with a set of rules that comprised detailed instructions on
the precise handling of the evaluation module and the evaluation process for each indicator, each
criterion and the summarized evaluation (BGE, 2020). In Step 1, BGE also decided to apply the
weighting criteria rather conservatively. If a clear categorization was not possible for a certain area
(perhaps due to missing data or dissent among experts) the area would be classified as “favorable.”
The rationale was that as the site-specific knowledge of that area improves, the area will be
evaluated again in later siting steps with a better assessment regarding data quantity and quality.

2.4.2 STEP 2 OF PHASE I
Here we summarize the ongoing process of identifying siting regions for detailed site characterization
to be conducted in Phases II and III, starting with the previously identified sub-areas. BGE
implemented a thorough, systematic downselection strategy that is feasible, transparent, and
adaptable to account for possible data gaps because the number and size of siting regions must be
limited, even though the sub-areas remaining after Step 1 comprise roughly 54 % of Germany’s
onshore land mass. This downselection is performed via so-called “representative preliminary safety
evaluations” (rvSU) based on a similar criteria-based evaluation process using exclusion criteria,
minimum requirements, and geo-scientific weighting criteria (Hoyer et al., 2021). However, in Step 2,
the evaluation is more thorough and applies more stringent criteria. The evaluation brings in more
geoscientific data from available sources, may analyze the safety of the site by means of simplified
radionuclide transport models, and lastly involves a comparison of sites both qualitatively and
quantitatively. Furthermore, planning-scientific weighting criteria are applied in Step 2 for the first
time.
Figure 2-4 gives an overview of the downselection process. First, the sub-areas of Germany with
potentially suitable host rocks are categorized into Suitability Categories A through D, with Category A
being the most favorable and D being the least favorable in terms of expected repository safety. This
is done by conducting representative preliminary safety evaluations for each potential siting region in
each sub-area. All siting regions identified as Category A are then compared with each other,
applying first the geoscientific weighting criteria and, if more downselection is needed, the planning-
scientific weighting criteria. By the end of 2027, this process will lead to a proposal for a limited
number of siting regions to be reviewed by the regulator and to be approved by the German
parliament before moving into Phase II.

Figure 2-4. Process of identifying siting regions in Step 2 of Phase I (BGE, 2023).

Germany’s regulation views preliminary safety evaluations as an important instrument within the site
selection procedure (Hoyer et al., 2021), to be conducted three times with an increasing level of
detail (Phase I: representative preliminary safety evaluations, Phase II: further-developed preliminary
safety evaluations, Phase III: comprehensive preliminary safety evaluations).The safety evaluations
serve to assess “the extent to which the safe containment of radioactive waste can be expected by
exploiting the geological site conditions at the site.”
Figure 2-5 illustrates the elements of the representative preliminary safety evaluations as conducted
by BGE. The methodology is consistent with international best practices on safety assessments for
DGR sites comprising many important elements like compilation and interpretation of all
geoscientific information, developing a safety strategy and preliminary design, a screening of
“Features, Events & Processes,” a discussion of the evolution of the disposal system, an assessment

February 2026                                                                                              26

of geoscientific weighting criteria, and finally an evaluation of the long-term safety (in a later phase).
Because the process is very time-consuming and it must be conducted for each potential siting
region, the StandAG allows for some simplifications in Phase I (which are not allowed in Phases II
and III). For example, the analysis of the disposal system in the representative preliminary safety
evaluations can be performed based on rough estimates and analogous considerations. It is
furthermore assumed that all technical and geotechnical barriers fulfill their safety functions as
intended. Hence, the focus in this current Phase I is on the comparative safety of geological settings
and on the geological processes. The evaluations may be supported by numerical models that
provide quantitative estimates of the transport of radionuclides in the subsurface, to demonstrate
the effectiveness of the natural barrier by calculating the rate of radionuclides escaping the natural
barrier over time. In Phase I, such models may have a comparatively low degree of complexity (i.e.,
low dimensionality or simplified physical and chemical processes) compared to the later phases of
the site selection procedure.

criteria defined in the StandAG and amend them with additional host-rock specific metrics. The result
is a host-rock specific criteria catalog (referred to as rvSU criteria), which is consistent with the

One important principle of Germany’s national consensus law for waste disposal is the equal

The German siting process is complex and involves multiple assessment and comparison steps.
Early in the process, BGE decided the best way to develop an efficient assessment procedure was to
test different methodologies utilizing practical examples that consider area-specific data availability.
Therefore, four sub-areas, one in each host rock type (clay, crystalline, bedded salt, and domal salt),

approaches developed for these areas, the methodologies for the execution of the representative
preliminary safety evaluations were iteratively improved and transferred to the remaining areas
(Hoyer et al., 2021).
Application of geoscientific weighting criteria often involved additional research studies to provide
regional information or develop new assessment methodologies. The German disposal program
therefore launched a comprehensive research program to support the siting process, on both the
implementor and regulator sides, involving many institutions and universities. As an example for a
typical study, the project KaStör reviewed the current knowledge on active faults and fault zones in
Germany and recommended methodological approaches to date and identify the activity of faulting
(Liebscher et al., 2020). Other research address subjects such as radionuclide migration, glacial
erosion processes, modeling of fissure formation in crystalline rocks, development of numerical and
stochastic models for simulating Hydraulic–Thermal–Mechanical–Chemical processes, age-dating
methodologies, and characteristics of clay minerals or salt slack as a geotechnical barrier
(Schafmeister, 2023).
In Germany, transparency and public participation are key elements of the site selection procedure,
which attempts to combine geoscientific and societal principles for improved public acceptance. The
government has established various formats, such as regional conferences and public hearings, but
creating a truly inclusive and effective dialog to gain public acceptance and trust remains a key
challenge. To enable effective information sharing, BGE developed a suite of web-based navigable
geographical information system (GIS) interfaces to keep the public abreast of the site selection
process. For example, as soon as a potential siting region is placed into Categories D through A, the
related information is published online via a site selection navigation system that is open to the
public. This is work in progress; to date BGE has finalized Category D and is currently working on

2.5 GERMAN LESSONS LEARNED
Germany’s disposal program is currently in Phase I of a complex criteria-based site selection
program that started with a white map of the country and ultimately aims to find one site that
provides the highest possible level of safety for one million years. Criteria for site selection are based
on scientific knowledge and state-of-the-art technology. The German regulation defines three
different categories of quantitative and qualitative siting criteria: (1) exclusion criteria, (2) minimum
requirements, and (3) geoscientific weighting criteria. The representative preliminary safety
evaluations for site selection take these criteria into account and by regulation have the option to
add additional criteria.
Below is a summary of relevant lessons learned to date.
 • The goal of finding the “best” site rather than a “sufficiently safe” site is ambitious. It makes
 the site selection and comparison process more complex and time-consuming. Because the
 German siting process started with a white map of the entire country and needs to compare
 a broad range of host rock options, it is not a surprise that a site decision could be as far out
 as 2068. Since then, several German stakeholders have called for an acceleration of the
 program, which would require modifications to the multi-stage site selection procedure
        (BASE, 2025).
 • Assessment and comparison of sites in different host rocks and presumably different
 engineered barrier systems requires host-rock specific criteria. Qualitative criteria may need
 to be turned into numerical metrics for efficient comparison across multiple siting regions.
 • Early stages of site assessment and comparison often use existing geoscientific data.
 However, different sites may have vastly different data availability. Often, there is a host rock
 bias. Areas with historical hydrocarbon exploration often have an abundance of data (e.g.,
 sedimentary layers with claystone), whereas crystalline rock areas may lack geologic
 characterization data due to minimal economic interest. In such cases, the siting process
 may need to rely on knowledge transfer from geologically comparable units with better data
 availability or may use general geoscientific understanding of host rocks to identify their
 strengths and weaknesses. For example, in general crystalline rock can have hydrologic
 heterogeneity (i.e., fast channels), compared to rocks like with good self-sealing properties
 like clay and rock salt.
 • It is important to have a comprehensive research program to support the siting process, to
 provide regional information or develop new assessment methodologies.

February 2026                                                                                                32

 • GIS-based systems are not only essential for the technical work of site assessment and
 comparison, but they can also serve as an important communication tool to display from the
 site selection program to stakeholders and the interested public.

3 SITE SELECTION IN CANADA

3.1 THE NUCLEAR WASTE MANAGEMENT ORGANIZATION AND CANADA’S
 PLAN
Canada has a long history of managing used nuclear fuel. Since the start of the Canada Deuterium
Uranium (CANDU) program, various long-term solutions have been considered, with a royal
commission reviewing options in 1977. The governments of Canada and Ontario formally launched a
waste management program, assigning Atomic Energy of Canada (AECL) to develop a deep
underground repository concept in the Canadian Shield. Site selection was postponed until after
approval of a disposal concept. In 1994, AECL submitted a DGR concept for federal review.
The Seaborn Panel, which gathered input from stakeholders and public hearings, concluded in 1998
that deep geological disposal had proven technical safety but lacked broad public support; site
searches should wait until public acceptance is achieved (Seaborn, 1998). The panel advised
forming an independent agency to manage all aspects of nuclear fuel waste (Assessment Agency of
Canada, 2001). In 2002, Parliament passed the Nuclear Fuel Waste Act (NFWA), requiring nuclear
energy corporations to create a not-for-profit waste management organization to study, recommend,
and implement long-term approaches for used fuel disposal (NFWA, 2002).
The Nuclear Waste Management Organization (NWMO) was subsequently established in 2002 by the
nuclear energy corporations (Ontario Power Generation, Hydro-Quebec, and New Brunswick Power).
The NWMO’s first mandate was to work collaboratively with Canadians to develop a management
approach for the long-term care of Canada’s used fuel that is socially acceptable, technically sound,
environmentally responsible and economically feasible.
From 2002 to 2005, the NWMO studied various approaches to the long-term management of
Canada’s used fuel and consulted extensively across the country. NWMO met with more than
18,000 Canadians, 2,500 Aboriginal people, 500 specialists (geologists, engagement specialists,
ethicists, traditional knowledge holders, etc.) and held 120 information and discussion sessions in
every Canadian province and territory. From this dialogue, much common ground emerged (NWMO,
2005):
 • First, Canadians said that they expected the country to assume responsibility now, in this
 generation, for the waste produced to meet their energy needs. It was simply not acceptable
 to leave the burden of providing for and funding the management of used fuel to future
 generations.
 • Second, Canadians did not want an approach that was irreversible. They wanted a flexible
 approach that would allow succeeding generations to make improvements based on new
 knowledge or changing priorities.
 • Third, while the chosen approach must obviously meet several objectives, Canadians were
 absolutely clear that safety and security be pre-eminent.
In 2005, the NWMO recommended the Adaptive Phased Management approach to the Minister of
Natural Resources (NWMO, 2005). Adaptive Phased Management was approved by the by the
Government of Canada in June 2007, and the NWMO assumed responsibility for implementing the
Adaptive Phased Management approach, Canada’s plan for the long-term management of used
nuclear fuel (NWMO, 2005)
 • The technical method involves: centralized containment and isolation of the used fuel in a
 DGR built in a suitable rock formation. It provides for the continuous monitoring of used fuel
 and offers the potential to retrieve the fuel for an extended time.

February 2026                                                                                             34

 • The management system involves: phased decision-making support by public engagement
 and continuous learning; adaptability so that it is responsive to advances in technology, new
 research, Aboriginal Traditional Knowledge or changing societal values; the DGR located in
 an informed and willing host community selected through an open, inclusive and fair siting
 process; and engagement of interested and potentially affected citizens and communities in
 decision-making throughout implementation of the plan.
Since then, the NWMO has made a great deal of progress on many fronts, including: working
collaboratively with communities to build resilient partnerships; advancing reconciliation with
Indigenous communities; identifying a safe and socially acceptable site to host the DGR, developing
conceptual and site-specific engineering repository designs, conducting site evaluation and safety
assessment to build confidence in the suitability of candidate sites, developing readiness for the
regulatory process and becoming a competent implementing organization.

3.2 NWMO SITE SELECTION PROCESS
NWMO developed and initiated the site selection process in 2010 (NWMO, 2010). Highlights from
the development, the launch and implementation of the site selection process are provided in the
following sections.

3.2.1 DEVELOPMENT OF THE SITE SELECTION PROCESS
NWMO developed and initiated the site selection process in 2010 to seek an informed and willing
host for a DGR for Canada’s used nuclear fuel (NWMO, 2010). The site selection process was
developed collaboratively with Canadians and a wide range of stakeholders over a two-year dialogue
period (2008 and 2009). The dialogues revolved around the important principles and elements that
would ensure a fair site selection process. A draft selection process was presented in 2009 providing
an opportunity for people to provide their thoughts and comment on the proposed process.
Suggestions from people were incorporated and the site selection process was finalized in early
2010. During these dialogues, the following 13 principles emerged as the foundation upon which the
siting process should be built:
 • Focus on safety: Safety, security, and protection of people and the environment are central to
 the siting process.
 • Meet or exceed regulatory requirements: The outcome of the site selection process, as for all
 aspects of Adaptive Phased Management, must meet, and if possible, exceed, all applicable
 regulatory requirements, and respect Canada's international commitments on the peaceful
 use of nuclear energy.
 • Informed and willing host community: The host community, the local geographic community
 in which the facility is to be located, must be informed and willing to accept the project. The
 local community must understand the project and how it is likely to be impacted by the
 project. As well, the local community must demonstrate that it is willing to accept the project.
 • Focus on the nuclear provinces: As identified by Canadians involved in the NWMO study,
 fairness is best achieved with the site selection process focused within the provinces directly
 involved in the nuclear fuel cycle (Saskatchewan, Ontario, Quebec, and New Brunswick).
 • Right to withdraw: Communities that decide to engage in the process for selecting a site as
 potential hosts must have the right to end their involvement in the siting process at any point
 up to and until the final agreement is signed, subject to all regulatory requirements being
 met and regulatory approval received.
 • Siting process led by interested communities: The steps in the siting process must be driven
 or triggered by communities expressing interest in exploring their potential suitability as host.
 A community will proceed to the next step only if it chooses to do so. Potentially interested
 communities may explore their interest in the project in the way they see fit, with the support
 of the NWMO, and with funding available to seek independent advice and peer review, and to
 involve residents in the community, at each stage.
 • Indigenous rights, treaties and land claims: The siting process will respect Aboriginal rights
 and treaties and will consider that there may be unresolved claims between Aboriginal
 peoples and the Crown. The NWMO recognizes the Crown’s duty to consult and
 accommodate, if necessary, when potential Aboriginal and treaty rights may be adversely
 affected by proposed Crown conduct when the NWMO selects a site. Prior to that, the NWMO
 must continue to engage with Aboriginal peoples and encourage potentially interested
 communities, including Aboriginal communities, to engage with First Nations, Métis, and Inuit
 in the area.

February 2026                                                                                               36

 • Shared decision-making: The site selection decision will be made in stages and will entail a
 series of decisions about whether and how to proceed. Each potential host community, and
 later the selected host community, must be involved in decision-making throughout the
 process. For example, criteria and procedures to assess the effects of the project on the
 community must be collaboratively developed and assessed with the NWMO.
 • Inclusiveness: The NWMO must respond to, and address where appropriate, the views of
 others who are most likely to be affected by implementation, including the transportation
 that would be required of used nuclear fuel. Full opportunity must be provided to surrounding
 communities, provincial governments, Aboriginal communities and transportation
 communities as a large group with a shared interest to have their questions and concerns
 heard and considered in decision-making on a preferred site.
 • Support capacity building: The site selection process must assist the potential host
 community in thinking carefully and thoroughly about the potential benefits and impacts to
 its community associated with this project when assessing its interest, and ultimately,
 willingness. The NWMO must provide the forms of assistance communities potentially
 affected by the implementation of the project need to participate in the process.
 • Informing the process: The selection of a site must be informed by the best available
 knowledge – including science, social science, Indigenous Knowledge and ethics – relevant
 to deciding or formulating a recommendation throughout the process. Consistent with the
 NWMO’s commitment to transparency in its work, the information that is collected and used
 to assess the potential suitability of a site must be published on the NWMO website for
 public review and scrutiny and be the subject of third-party review at major milestones.
 • Community well-being: Any community that agrees to host the facility has a right to benefit
 from doing so. The project must be implemented in a manner that fosters the long-term well-
 being or quality of life of the community and region in which it is implemented.
 • Ongoing engagement of governments: While this national initiative is federally mandated, the
 NWMO must involve all potentially affected provincial governments in the siting decision.

3.3 SITE REQUIREMENTS
The site selection process (NWMO, 2010) outlines initial screening criteria as well as detailed
technical and community well-being site requirements. Overall, the combination of social and
technical criteria was used to assess the suitability of the various siting areas to ensure the
following:
 • Used nuclear fuel can be safely contained and isolated to protect humans and the
 environment over the very long term.
 • Resilient partnerships can be developed, and the project can be implemented in a way that
 fosters the well-being of the local community and region.
 • A safe and socially acceptable used nuclear fuel transportation plan can be developed.

3.3.1 INITIAL SCREENING CRITERIA
Initial Screenings were conducted at the request of communities that expressed an interest in
learning more after the NWMO initiated the site selection process. Potential suitability of these
communities was quickly assessed based on readily available information for the five following initial
screening criteria (NWMO, 2010):
 a) The site must have available land of sufficient size to accommodate the surface and
 underground facilities.
 b) This available land must be outside protected areas, heritage sites, provincial parks, and
 national parks.
 c) This available land must not contain known groundwater resources at the repository depth
 that could be used for drinking, agriculture, or industrial uses, so that the repository site is
 unlikely to be disturbed by future generations.
 d) This available land must not contain economically exploitable natural resources as known
 today, so that the repository site is unlikely to be disturbed by future generations.
 e) This available land must not be located in areas with known geological and hydrogeological
 characteristics that would prevent the site from being safe.

3.3.2 DETAILED TECHNICAL SITE REQUIREMENTS
The list of detailed criteria was included in the draft site selection process, and a wide range of
stakeholders had the opportunity to comment on their adequacy, including the regulator (Table 3-3).
The criteria were drafted with community engagement in mind and used simple language. Any
site that is selected to host the project must ultimately satisfy six safety functions and related
questions:
 a) Safe containment and isolation of used nuclear fuel. Are the characteristics of the rock at the
 site appropriate to ensure the long-term containment and isolation of used nuclear fuel from
 humans, the environment and surface disturbances caused by human activities and natural
 events?
 b) Long-term resilience to future geological processes and climate change. Is the rock formation
 at the siting area geologically stable and likely to remain stable over the very long term in a
 manner that will ensure the repository will not be substantially affected by geological and
 climate change processes such as earthquakes and glacial cycles?
 c) Safe construction, operation and closure of the repository. Are conditions at the site suitable
 for the safe construction, operation, and closure of the repository?
 d) Isolation of used fuel from future human activities. Is human intrusion at the site unlikely, for
 instance through future exploration or mining?
 e) Amenability to site characterization and data interpretation activities. Can the geologic
 conditions at the site be practically studied and described on dimensions that are important
 for demonstrating long-term safety?
 f) Safe transportation. Does the site have a route that exists or is amenable to being created
 that enables the safe and secure transportation of used fuel from interim storage sites to the
 repository site?

3.5 CANADIAN LESSONS LEARNED
3.5.1 PROGRAMME DEVELOPMENT AND IMPLEMENTATION
Like in many other countries, the Canadian programme for the long-term management of used
nuclear fuel has a long history. In its early days, the programme was technically driven with little
emphasis on societal issues, but its success ultimately required a dialogue-driven approach which
involved having extensive dialogues with Canadians and Indigenous people to shape the national
narrative and develop a socially acceptable and technically sound plan for the long-term
management of Canada’s used nuclear fuel.
International experience shows that there is no universal template for building public trust and
confidence in DGRs for radioactive waste, as it is influenced by a wide range of constantly evolving
economic, social, political, and cultural aspects that are specific to each country. In Canada, the
chances of success for siting a DGR for used nuclear fuel are increased as the programme is
managed under a robust implementation framework that includes the following key pillars:
 • A compelling national narrative for geological disposal, developed over many years of
 dialogue with Canadians and Indigenous people;
 • A dialogue driven flexible, phased, and adaptable implementation approach with a clear and
 transparent decision-making process, including a fair and inclusive site selection process
 designed to seek informed and willing hosts; and
 • A comprehensive national legislative and regulatory framework, sustained by political will and
 commitment.
A more detailed discussion of each of the above pillars is provided in Ben-Belfadhel et al. (2021;
2022) and NTI (2022).

3.5.2 SITE EVALUATIONS PROCESS AND SITE SELECTION CRITERIA
The following learnings were identified by the author through his involvement with the Canadian site
evaluation process.
 • Assessing the suitability of potential sites and identifying a preferred site can take decades.
 The chances of success are greatly increased when site evaluations are conducted within a
 holistic site evaluation framework describing how site evaluations will be conducted in
 terms of evaluation phases, objectives and approach for each phase, timelines, decision
 points and criteria. Having a clear site evaluation framework is key for the traceability,
 fairness and credibility of findings and building stakeholders confidence in the overall siting
 process.
 • It is helpful when technical siting criteria are developed with engagement in mind, and
 ideally with the involvement of key stakeholders. It is also helpful when the categorization
 of site selection criteria provides clear linkages to DGR safety functions and expected
 performance objectives, to illustrate the many safety dimensions that are involved when
 siting DGRs.
 • Applying exclusion and siting criteria during the early stages of the site evaluations can be
 challenging especially when dealing with many potential candidate sites and limited
 amount and quality of field data, which is often the case during the early phases. In this
 context, the following aspects need to be carefully considered to preserve the fairness and
 credibility of site evaluations during the early stages of site evaluations:
 - Set achievable and realistic objectives considering the extent of data availability.

February 2026                                                                                              47

 - Identify site characteristics and criteria that can be realistically assessed with available
 data at each evaluation phase.
 - Avoid inadvertent exclusion of sites because of lack of data or differences in data
 resolution.
 - Avoid inadvertent over-interpretation of field data, which can be challenged.
 - Document rationale for findings in a traceable manner that is understandable by non-
 technical audiences, and
 - Qualify the findings of the evaluations through acknowledging and documenting
 uncertainties and additional work needed to address them.

3.5.3 COMMUNITY ENGAGEMENT DURING IMPLEMENTATION OF THE SITE SELECTION
 PROCESS
The NWMO began its work with a deep understanding that public trust and confidence regarding the
long-term management of radioactive waste requires a dialogue-driven approach where citizens are
involved from the very beginning. The NWMO implemented Canada’s plan for the long-term
management of used nuclear fuel, including a consent-based siting process, with the involvement of
interested municipal and indigenous communities and the public at each implementation and
decision-making step. Some of the lessons learned include:
 • Meaningful engagement takes time and resources for both the implementing organization
 and communities participating in the site selection process. It is important to ensure that
 sufficient human and financial resources are allocated to achieve the objectives of the
 engagement programme.
 • It is important to identify all stakeholders, understand their concerns and how they would like
 to be engaged. The engagement programme should start as early as possible to avoid
 surprises.

4 SITE SELECTION IN THE UNITED STATES

4.1 INTRODUCTION
Compared to the rest of the world, the United States (US) started out with a lead on the production,
management, regulation, and disposal of radioactive waste. Despite the head start, many now-
obvious complexities arose during the simultaneous and iterative development of the disposal
concept, the development of regulations, the evolution of the siting process, and the development of
the technical understanding about disposal of radioactive waste. There have been multiple starts,
stops, restarts, successes, and cancellations in the US radwaste disposal program during its
approximately 70-year life.
The Waste Isolation Pilot Plan (WIPP) near Carlsbad, NM is an example of a success. It is a licensed
and operating deep geologic disposal facility for transuranic defense waste in salt. The Yucca
Mountain Project was planned to be the deep geologic disposal facility for SNF and HLW in tuff, but it
currently remains defunded. The US disposal program for SNF and HLW is currently at a standstill,
striving to start over, but needing to revise the current laws and regulations to achieve this.
Until 1978, when President Jimmy Carter banned the reprocessing of SNF, all wastes to be disposed
of were going to be vitrified reprocessing wastes, as it was initially believed the availability of
uranium ore was limited. After this decision, commercial SNF was considered for direct disposal.
Most liquid reprocessing waste comes from the defense activities during the Cold War (i.e., through
early 1990s) at the Hanford, Idaho, and Savannah River Department of Energy (DOE) sites, which will
be vitrified before disposal and are classified as HLW.
The US is a large country, with the benefit of a wide range of different available geologies to
consider. Early siting efforts focused on salt rocks, but crystalline and argillite rocks have been
considered at different points along the way, as well as DOE-owned sites with still different geologies
(i.e., basalt at Hanford and volcanic tuff at Yucca Mountain). The disposal concept for a mined deep
geologic repository is somewhat different for each host rock type, and the disposal concept has
evolved with time since the first concepts in salt were proposed in the 1950s, due to both US and
international advances. Deep borehole disposal is an alternative concept for physically small
inventories, but the siting guidelines for boreholes are not clear.
There have been several major pieces of legislation and federal government organization that have
controlled the disposal of radioactive waste in the United States:
 • Atomic Energy Act of 1946 (created Atomic Energy Commission, AEC)
 • Atomic Energy Act of 1954 (allowed commercial power generation)
 • National Environmental Policy Act of 1969 (requirement of environmental assessments and
 environmental impact statements for federal repositories)
 • Energy Reorganization Act of 1974 (Split AEC into Nuclear Regulatory Commission, NRC, and
 Energy Research and Development Agency, ERDA)
 • Department of Energy Organization Act of 1977 (merged ERDA into Cabinet-level DOE)
 • Department of Energy National Security and Military Applications of Nuclear Energy
 Authorization Act of 1980 (established WIPP for disposal of defense TRU)
 • Nuclear Waste Policy Act of 1982 (formalized the siting process for two repositories and
 established the Nuclear Waste Fund to finance repository development)
 • Nuclear Waste Policy Act Amendment of 1987 (designated Yucca Mountain as sole
 repository)

February 2026                                                                                             51

 • The Waste Isolation Pilot Plant Land Withdrawal Act (LWA) of 1996 (set aside land for WIPP
 and defined disposal limits).
The following sub-sections of this chapter draw from several historical summaries of the US siting
process for a DGR for radioactive waste (Johnson & Garland, 1980; Lomenick, 1996; Rechard,
1999; Mora, 1999; Kuhlman et al., 2012; Rechard et al., 2014; NWTRB, 2015; Swift, 2019; US
Congress, 2021).

4.2 THE BEGINNING (1946-1962)
The Atomic Energy Act of 1946 established the Atomic Energy Commission (AEC) for civilian control
over Manhattan Project activities after the end of World War II (US Congress, 1946). This Act made it
the government’s responsibility for control and ownership of radioactive materials. Through 1954,
the US HLW inventory was small and solely due to military weapons and research activities. The HLW
was mostly in above-ground containers at Idaho (the National Reactor Testing Station), Oak Ridge
National Lab, and Hanford Engineer Works.
The Atomic Energy Act of 1954 provided legal framework for commercial electricity generation and
the impetus for locating radioactive waste disposal facilities (US Congress, 1954). In 1955, the US
National Academies of Science’s National Research Council held a workshop at Princeton with 65
geologists and geophysicists, forming the Committee on Waste Disposal (National Research Council,
1957) to investigate alternatives to shallow land disposal. This group considered the disposal
problem for two years and was the first panel to formally investigate deep geological disposal of
liquid radioactive waste (using salt cavities and injection into deep anticlines). They proposed
investigating both liquid and solid waste disposal. While the committee suggested disposal should
be feasible at many sites within the US (due to the large number of potential sites available), it also
stated “the research to ascertain feasibility of disposal has for the most part not been done.”
The first US commercial nuclear power reactor was at Shippingport, Pennsylvania in 1957. At this
time, the need to reprocess SNF from US power reactors was foreseen to be important. Siting
consideration was given to co-locating power plants, reprocessing facilities, and abandoned salt
mines or creating solution-mined cavities for disposal (Serata & Gloyna, 1959). At this early point,
power plants were in the northeastern quadrant of the US, so it was considered fair to locate
reprocessing and disposal in that same quadrant (Figure 4-1).
During the period 1955-1957, the main criterion for siting a geologic repository was the presence of
bedded rock salt in the subsurface (proximity to waterways for transportation was a minor
consideration), as it was the only rock type under serious consideration. The US Geological Survey
(Pierce & Rich, 1958) discussed the earliest geological selection criteria for a repository in salt: 1)
presence of impermeable enclosing beds (e.g., bedded salt), 2) suitable thickness, and 3) suitable
purity of the salt. They found were several salt deposits in the US that fit these criteria (Figure 4-2).

Figure 4-1. Early disposal and siting concept of co-locating nuclear power plant, reprocessing, and
 disposal facility (Serata & Gloyna, 1959)

In 1961-1962, early underground experiments in the Carey Salt Mine (Hutchinson, Kansas) showed
it was impractical to dispose of liquid reprocessing waste directly in salt. Concerns included gas
generation, dissolution of the salt, and reaction of the liquids with non-salt components of the
evaporites. Some waste solidification processes were already being investigated as suggested by the
NAS panel (National Research Council, 1957), but these observations formalized the now-accepted
premise that liquid reprocessing waste should be solidified before disposal.

4.3 BEDDED SALT IN KANSAS (1962-1972)
During the decade 1962-1972, the US disposal siting program was focused on Kansas bedded salt.
The Carey Salt mine in Lyons hosted Project Salt Vault (1964-1967), which was run by Oak Ridge
National Laboratory. In this key science and engineering project, the disposal concept was matured
through full-scale field tests and demonstrations. The final report from this project was arguably the
first formal “safety case” for radioactive waste disposal in any medium (Bradshaw & McClain, 1971).
The report included both laboratory and field experiments for validation of the concept. Project Salt
Vault was then followed by an unsuccessful “demonstration repository” in the same mine. Moving
quickly from a field test to a demonstration repository led to the first disagreements between the AEC
and state, county, and city agencies.

4.3.1 PROJECT SALT VAULT
In the very early days, salt was seen to be the ideal solution for radioactive waste disposal. During
Project Salt Vault, salt was first noted to be susceptible to dissolution and therefore a disposal
location must be carefully located away from unplugged wells and solution mining activities in the
salt. This realization motivated the first consideration of other rock types for disposal.
Despite the focus on bedded salt, some initial investigations in the 1960s were made into the
potential for deep (460 m [1500 ft]) geologic disposal into metamorphic basement rocks at the
Savannah River site in South Carolina (Proctor & Marine, 1965; Parker, 1968). This effort was
eventually abandoned over concerns about the overlying regional aquifer, contained within the 300
m [1000 ft] of overlying sediments (Lomenick, 1996).
During this period, the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) of 1969 mandated the
Environmental Assessments and Environmental Impact Statement processes, which also applied to
federal disposal facilities (US Congress, 1969). The creation of the Environmental Protection Agency
(EPA) occurred soon after, in 1970. The EPA promulgated several of the regulatory standards that
govern both generic (Title 40 Code of Federal Regulations Part 191 (i.e., 40 CFR 191) in 1985) and
site-specific (40 CFR 197 in 2001 for Yucca Mountain) radioactive waste disposal in the US.
In June 1970, the AEC and the Kansas Governor announced tentative selection of a repository site
near Lyons, Kansas for a demonstration salt-mine repository. The Lyons site (where Project Salt Vault
was located) was stated to have the qualities of:
 • Tectonic stability
 • Low seismic risk
 • Geographically extensive flat-lying thick salt beds
 • Stable interior of the continent
 • Relatively shallow depth to the disposal horizon

February 2026                                                                                           54

 • Characterization data already available from Project Salt Vault
 • Only available, accessible, non-producing mine considered
 • Served by two major railroads and a US highway
 • A favorable reception from state politicians, local officials, and private citizens was expected.
These then are possibly the earliest example of informal siting criteria used in the US (Lomenick,
1996). This also indicates the naïve optimism that existed, since the idea of siting a deep geological
disposal facility was new to people.

4.3.2 REJECTION OF LYONS SITE AND FURTHER INVESTIGATIONS
Soon after proposing the Lyons, Kansas site, it was dismissed for having “inadequate buffer zone
because of adjacent solution mining and too many nearby unplugged gas and oil drill boreholes”
(Lomenick, 1996).
Until mid-1971, the state and the public were both generally positive of the AEC-led effort at Lyons
(including Project Salt Vault). The Governor was initially neutral on the project, but by mid-1971 they
became negative due to Kansas Geological Survey technical concerns over plugging of boreholes
near the site and solution mining operations at a salt mine, among other shortcomings (Lomenick,
1996). The local government of Lyons remained receptive and supportive, but state officials
terminated the project.
Once efforts at Lyons were discontinued, eight central and western Kansas sites were investigated
by Oak Ridge National Laboratory and the Kansas Geological Survey. A subjective initial ranking by
Kansas Geological Survey narrowed it from eight to three sites. At each of the three sites, a deep test
well was cored and hydrologically characterized to quantify stratigraphy and hydrology. This
investigation found none of the three proposed areas had adequate thickness or purity of salt to
continue.
At this time, the first more formal technical siting criteria for radioactive waste disposal in the US
were published by Oak Ridge (Culler, 1971):
 • Salt formation should be bedded, approximately horizontal, and structurally undisturbed, so
 previous research is applicable
 • Formation should extend a considerable horizontal distance (i.e., tens of miles)
 • Formation should not be less than 150 m [500 ft] deep or 60 m [200 ft] thick for heat
 dissipation and isolation.
 • At the disposal site, top of salt should not be greater than 610 m [2000 ft] deep (due to cost
 and operational considerations)
 • Formation should not be associated with, or being in vicinity of, valuable reserves of
 petroleum or other minerals
 • Area should be tectonically stable

4.3.3 EVOLUTION OF THE DISPOSAL CONCEPT
Although deep geological disposal was the primary focus of the siting effort, alternative disposal
options were formally considered in 1972. These alternatives were considered based on technical
feasibility, safety, cost, environmental considerations, policy conflicts, public response, and R&D
needs. The alternatives disposal concepts were:

February 2026                                                                                               55

 • Disposal in terrestrial locations (land, seabed, and ice sheets),
 • Disposal into space (orbit around sun), and
 • Elimination by nuclear transmutation (in fission reactors, in fusion reactors, or with nuclear
 explosives).
At this point, the preferred disposal concept had evolved to be what was demonstrated in Project
Salt Vault (Bradshaw & McClain, 1971). The concept assumed cylindrical containers placed in
vertical holes drilled into the floor of underground mined excavations. Vertical boreholes in the floor
are logistically the simplest way to emplace the waste, so the heat-generating waste is maximally
adjacent to intact salt, for improve heat conduction and reduce maximum temperatures. Later
testing at WIPP observed that vertical boreholes from mined rooms tend to accumulate brine (i.e., a
sump) via the damage region surrounding excavations in salt (Kuhlman & Mills, 2025).

4.4 EXPANDING TO OTHER ROCK TYPES (1973-1975)
Into the mid-1970s, geologic salt deposits remained the emphasis for siting, but the siting process
was expanded to include domal salt. The emphasis on bedded salt had moved from central Kansas
to southeastern New Mexico, the eventual site for WIPP. Oak Ridge National Laboratory did the initial
characterization of the southeastern New Mexico area with the US Geological Survey, but the project
was handed over to Sandia National Laboratories in 1975. Sandia recommended a site in center of
the Delaware Basin of southeastern New Mexico for transuranic waste. Other rock types (clay-rich
strata, chalk, granitoid igneous, and metamorphic rocks) were considered to a lesser degree during
this period.
In 1975, The Energy Reorganization Act split the AEC into NRC and ERDA, to separate the regulatory
and research branches of the US nuclear organization (US Congress, 1974). Until this point, the AEC
was responsible for self-regulated management and disposal of radioactive waste. This act
transferred the Atomic Safety and Licensing Board Panel to the NRC. The NRC was responsible for
promulgating several of the generic (10 CFR 60 in 1981) and site-specific (10 CFR 63 in 2001 for
Yucca Mountain) regulations on implementation of radioactive waste disposal.

4.4.1 WASTE MANAGEMENT STUDY PROGRAM
After the initial effort in Lyons Kansas, the AEC Waste Management Study Program was the first
centralized deep geologic disposal siting effort, which started as an investigation (March 1972) to
site a “pilot repository program” in both salt and non-salt rocks (Pittman, 1974). During siting in New
Mexico for a pilot repository, AEC made special efforts to ensure state and local entities (Sandia
National Laboratories, New Mexico Bureau of Mines, University of New Mexico, and New Mexico
Tech) were used for technical investigations and all local organizations were briefed on policy
developments. State universities (Louisiana State University and University of Texas) were utilized
during the site investigations of salt domes in the Gulf Coast.
Guidance for the Waste Management Study Program included the following assumptions:
 • Initial repository would be a “pilot plant” capable of monitoring and maintaining emplaced
 waste, but remaining retrievable
 • Transuranic and HLW could (but not must) be stored together
 • Preference was given to government-owned sites
 • Surface-handling facilities as well as the size, shape, weight, and heat content of waste
 containers and shipping casks would emulate Project Salt Vault (as they should be the same
 for any geology)

 • Sufficient information on the proposed site would be needed to complete safety analysis and
 an environmental impact statement by 1973.
The Waste Management Study Program considered bedded and domal salt sites across the US:
 • four in southeastern New Mexico (including the current WIPP location),
 • one in the Paradox Basin in southeastern Utah,
 • thirty-six salt domes in the Gulf Coast (Texas, Louisiana, Mississippi & Alabama),
 • two salt deposits in Arizona (Luke and Supai),
 • the Michigan Basin (Michigan), and
 • the Appalachian Basin (New York and Ohio).
At the direction of Oak Ridge National Lab, the US Geological Survey investigated argillaceous
formations (i.e., shale, mudstone, and claystone) of marine origin in areas of low structural
deformation and minor seismic risk. This study identified the Pierre Shale as a target formation,
present in several midwestern states (Merewether et al., 1973). They developed the following list of
characteristics of argillaceous rocks during in their search for target formations in the US:
 • Formation thickness in the range of depths 150-610 m [500-2000 ft], assuming a minimum
 total formation thickness of 150 m [500 ft]
 • Marine argillaceous rocks consisting of >80% shale, mudstone, or claystone
 • Consideration of potentially relevant minor constituents (i.e., petroliferous, carbonaceous, or

 • Argillite plasticity (but there were insufficient data to consider it quantitatively)
 • Abundance of fractures and proximity of adjacent aquifers
 • Regional seismicity
 • Availability of data from drill-holes or mines (outcrop data were used too).

4.4.2 GEOLOGIC DISPOSAL EVALUATION PROGRAM
In 1974, the Geologic Disposal Evaluation Program (GDEP) was created as a follow on the previous
Waste Management Study Program, to direct a more comprehensive nationwide site-investigation
program. The objectives of GDEP were to evaluate suitability of potential geologic host rocks for
permanent disposal of liquid, solid, or transuranic radioactive waste, and carry out R&D to establish
repositories using one or more disposal concepts (McClain et al., 1975). The GDEP effort included
geologic and hydrologic investigations, development, and engineering assessments.
GDEP site investigation process included bedded salt (southeastern New Mexico), salt domes (Gulf
Coast), salt anticlines (Paradox Basin in Colorado & Utah), shale (Pierre and Green River Formations),
existing storage caverns (across 49 US sites), dry mines in limestones (Ohio, Kansas, and Missouri),
dry mines in the Precambrian shield (Minnesota, Michigan and Wisconsin), basalt (Hanford
Washington), volcanic tuff (Nevada Test Site, Nevada), and metamorphic rocks (Savannah River,
South Carolina). The US Geological Survey investigated the geohydrologic environments available in
the US that might be used in alternative deep disposal concepts, including deep boreholes and deep
mined or explosively derived cavities (Ekren et al., 1974; DeBuchananne & Twenhofel, 1975).

4.5 MULTIPLE CONCURRENT PROGRAMS AND WIPP (1976-1982)
President Carter banned commercial reprocessing of SNF due to plutonium proliferation concerns,
leading to the once-through fuel cycle. The West Valley facility in New York was the only licensed
private reprocessing plant to operate in the US (1966-1972); two other facilities were built, but never
licensed (Morris, Illinois and Barnwell, South Carolina). In 1981, President Ronald Reagan lifted this
ban on reprocessing, but there have not been any non-defense reprocessing facilities in the US since
the Carter ban.

4.5.1 NATIONAL WASTE TERMINAL STORAGE PROGRAM
When the research portion of AEC became ERDA in 1975, the National Waste Terminal Storage
(NWTS) Program was formed to significantly expand and re-organize the existing efforts towards
siting a DGR in the US. In 1977, ERDA was then merged into the cabinet-level DOE with other energy
organizations from the federal government.
In 1978, a Task Force for Review of Nuclear Waste Management was formed, which produced the
“Deutch Report” (US DOE, 1978). The findings of this report included:
 • The consensus is that geological disposal is safe, but requires validation of technical choices
 • Reprocessing is not required for safe disposal of SNF
 • WIPP should conduct an SNF demonstration
 • Away-from-reactor storage should be studied
 • The NEPA process is an essential part of nuclear waste management
 • Policy and program responsibilities for radwaste management should be elevated to a higher
 level within DOE
The ERDA NWTS program was the successor to the AEC GDEP program and was “to provide facilities
in various deep geologic formation at multiple locations in the United States which could be used to
safely dispose of commercial radioactive waste, which must be delivered to a federal repository for
terminal storage” (Boch, 1976).
The NWTS program was significantly influenced by continuing foreign interest in disposal into non-
salt rocks (i.e., Sweden’s interest in crystalline rocks). At about the same time, The American Physical
Society (Hebel et al., 1978) issued an extensive external review of the entire nuclear fuel cycle
(including power, reprocessing, and disposal) and considered disposal in salt to be satisfactory but
indicated disposal in crystalline or argillaceous rocks may offer advantages.
The NWTS program was directly influenced by the recommendations of President Carter’s
Interagency Review Group on Nuclear Waste Management (1979), which indicated:
 • DOE should focus on mined repositories (sub-seabed disposal and very deep borehole
 disposal were seen as less mature alternatives)
 • R&D and site characterization will allow sites selection from multiple host rock types
 • By 1999, two or more repositories should be operational in different regions
 • Construction and operation of a repository should proceed in a technically conservative,
 stepwise manner, allowing initial retrievability

4.5.2 OFFICE OF WASTE ISOLATION
The Office of Waste Isolation (OWI) was created by Union Carbide to manage NWTS projects at Oak
Ridge (Lomenick, 1996). The WIPP project was an NWTS project, but it was instead run by ERDA
from their Albuquerque Office, starting in 1975. This ERDA program sought as many as six geologic
repositories to be sited and constructed. The first two sites would be in salt (operations starting in
1985), with the remaining four sites in other rock types (e.g., shale, crystalline, basalt, and quartzite)
to be opened by 1997 and 2006.
OWI managed the development and operation of federal repositories from the siting search through

 • Crystalline rocks (only a minor effort)
 • Other rocks (basalt at the Hanford Site, argillite and quartzite Eleana Formation at the

4.5.3 OFFICE OF NUCLEAR WASTE ISOLATION
Since the 1950s, the R&D center for radwaste disposal was at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. In
1978, the Oak Ridge prime contractor (Union Carbide) withdrew from the commercial-waste disposal
program. DOE then selected Battelle, who established ONWI. At this time there were three separate
repository siting efforts:
 1. Disposal studies at Hanford, which became the Basalt Waste Isolation Project.
 2. Disposal studies of several rock types at the Nevada Test Site (argillite, granite and tuff)
 became the Nevada Nuclear Waste Storage Investigations.
 3. ONWI, who initially considering bedded and domal salt, focusing on non-DOE controlled sites.
 They expanded to sedimentary rocks, argillaceous rocks, and crystalline rocks.
OWI was winding down as ONWI was beginning. The ONWI procedure for identifying suitable
repository sites was essentially a technical downselect (i.e., progressively more detailed studies at
national, regional, area, location, and site levels).

4.5.4 DEVELOPMENT OF SITING CRITERIA
In 1978, the National Academy of Sciences published a short set of geological criteria for HLW
repositories (National Research Council, 1978). Following this in 1979, DOE issued a much larger
four-volume document “NWTS Program Criteria for Mined Geologic Disposal of Nuclear Wastes” that
specified criteria for a repository system, agnostic of rock type. This Criteria document was intended
to leave options open to general rock types, but it was criticized for its use of vague value
judgements (i.e., “sufficient, acceptable, and adequate”). This criticism of vague language in siting
criteria was reiterated by the Nuclear Waste Technical Review Board in 2015.
During this period, the disposal concept was a deep mined geologic repository that would include
low-, intermediate-, and high-level waste together. The design assumed all investigation and
experimentation phases would have positive results and engineering could be used to overcome any
deficiencies in waste packages or waste-rock interactions. Both retrievable storage and permanent
disposal were considered. Carter’s ban on commercial reprocessing then led to an emphasized
interest in retrievability. The repository design included significant conservatisms, to overcome
uncertainties in the system, and it utilized a multibarrier concept for containment and isolation.
In 1980, President Carter called for a National Plan for earth science R&D for SNF repositories. This
plan’s preference was crystalline rocks over shale for a second repository, due to the perceived
complexity of shale site-specific characterization efforts. DOE also initiated a more formalized
approach to site selection in a 1980 Environmental Impact Statement “Management of
Commercially Generated Radioactive Waste” (US DOE, 1980). In this Statement, the deep geological
disposal concept was formally chosen over other alternatives (such as disposal in: very deep holes,
rock melting, island-based, sub-seabed, ice sheet, well injection, transmutation, space) and the “no
action” option.

4.5.5 ISSUES WITH PUBLIC OPINON AND CONSENSUS
In November 1976, ERDA contacted the governors of 36 states that included potential repository
sites. ERDA initially believed simultaneously siting several locations would fairly split the “burden” of
hosting a repository and improve the public perception of the national effort. Instead, this outreach
(including states that had not been previously involved) led to “unexpected political furor” (Lomenick,
1996). This response contributed to the quick demise of this ERDA initiative and led to increased
interest in Hanford and the Nevada Test Site as potential sites.

Public consensus was sought by providing technical information from the site investigations to the
states, allowing them to review (i.e., the “Consultation and Concurrence” directive by President
Carter). State agencies were informed of work conducted in their state and technical meetings kept
them informed. Efforts were made to use state research agencies when possible. Despite these
efforts, adverse political situations evolved related to deep test drilling (in Michigan, Louisiana &
Texas). Louisiana even passed state laws outlawing repositories or deep drilling for site
characterization of repositories. Some of these initial hurdles were eventually overcome, and test
drilling was eventually conducted in multiple states.
To improve relationships with local governments, in February 1980, President Carter created the
State Planning Council with representatives (i.e., governors, county chairpersons, and mayors) from
14 states and tribes to provide state perspectives and advice to Executive Branch regarding waste
management and disposal (Carter, 1980). DOE also set up ten regional offices to interface with
states, localities and the public.

4.5.6 AUTHORIZING THE WASTE ISOLATION PILOT PLANT
In 1980, Congress authorized WIPP as an R&D site to demonstrate the safe disposal of radioactive
waste generated from US defense activities, and exempted it from NRC regulation (US Congress,
1979). In the 1970s during siting efforts, WIPP was conceived to dispose of high-, intermediate-, and
low-level wastes from both commercial and defense origins, however the 1980 Congressional Act
limited the WIPP disposal site to only defense-generated waste (US Congress, 1979). The Act also
required the federal government to enter into an agreement with the State of New Mexico to allow
for better communication between the parties. The first “Consultation and Cooperation” Agreement
was established in 1981 (State of New Mexico, 1981). The WIPP Environmental Impact Statement
was completed in 1980, allowing the first shaft to be constructed in 1981 and full construction to
begin in 1983.

4.6 THE NUCLEAR WASTE POLICY ACT AND AMENDMENT (1983-1987)
The Nuclear Waste Policy Act (NWPA), passed in 1982, was the first comprehensive US legislation
regarding SNF and HLW disposal (US Congress, 1982). It created the Office of Civilian Radioactive
Waste Management under the DOE to develop, construct, and operate a system for SNF and HLW
disposal, including a permanent geologic repository, interim storage capability, and a transportation
system (i.e., implement the NWPA). The NWPA ensured participation of affected states, tribes, and
the public in the process.
The NWPA also created the Nuclear Waste Fund to finance for the repository development process,
through fees levied against electrical utilities operating nuclear power plants. The Fund would pay
the costs to construct and operate a permanent repository. The fee was set at one mill per kilowatt-
hour of nuclear power generated. Utilities were charged a one-time fee for storage of spent fuel
created before the NWPA.

4.6.1 DOE GENERIC SITING GUIDELINES (10 CFR 960)
In accordance with the NWPA, DOE published generic Siting Guidelines as 10 CFR 960, in 1984. The
guidelines were a system of qualifying and disqualifying conditions. They included:
 • Specify detailed geologic considerations for selection of sites
 • Specify factors that quality or disqualify a site from development into a repository
 • Take into consideration the proximity of the sites where waste is stored or generated and
 include the transportation and safety factors involved in moving the waste

 • To the extent possible, the Secretary should recommend sites in different rock types
As examples, in these Siting Guidelines one qualifying technical geohydrology condition is
“Geohydrologic setting is compatible with waste containment and isolation” and a disqualifying
technical geohydrology condition is “Ground-water travel time is less than 1,000 years along paths of
likely and significant radionuclide travel.” The technical system guidelines included both pre-closure
(siting, construction, operations, closure and decommissioning) and post-closure components.
The DOE Siting Guidelines outlined a siting process that involves four decisions:
 • The identification of potentially acceptable sites;
 • The nomination of sites as suitable for characterization;
 • The recommendation of sites as candidate sites for site characterization; and
 • After the completion of site characterization and non-geologic data gathering, the
 recommendation of a candidate site for the development of a repository.
Appendix IV of the Siting Guidelines lists the types of information for the nomination of sites as
suitable for characterization, including:
 • Geohydrology (e.g., hydraulic properties, recharge/discharge)
 • Geochemistry (e.g., mineralogy and presence of reducing/oxidizing conditions)
 • Rock characteristics (e.g., geology, stratigraphy, and structural makeup)
 • Climatic changes (e.g., effect of expected climatic cycles on surface- and ground-water)
 • Erosion (e.g., overlying rocks and rates of geomorphic processes)
 • Dissolution (e.g., presence of water-soluble minerals)
 • Tectonics (e.g., geothermal gradient, stress gradient and history of seismicity)
 • Human interference (e.g., natural resources and site ownership/control)
 • Meteorology (e.g., wind, precipitation, and extreme weather)
 • Offsite installations and operations (e.g., nearby industrial, transportation, and government)
 • Environmental quality (e.g., existing air quality, groundwater quality, and aesthetic)
 • Socioeconomic impacts (e.g., population, economic base, housing, social problems)
 • Transportation (e.g., cost and risk of transporting waste to site)
 • Surface characteristics (e.g., topography and existing or planned bodies of water)
The full text of the generic siting guidelines are available online (https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-
10/chapter-III/part-960) and have been recently referred to by the Nuclear Waste Technical Review
Board (NWTRB, 2015) as a sound basis for future rock-type specific guidelines.

4.6.2 SECOND REPOSITORY PROGRAMS
In 1982, DOE had a National Plan for Siting HLW Repositories. This continued to follow a technical
downselect approach, starting from a nationwide search, and eventually identifying candidate sites,
one or more of which would be identified as a permanent waste repository. This Plan was
superseded by the more specific NWPA in 1982. The NWPA took the general plan and provided DOE
with a schedule, and a sequence of steps that must be followed for siting, licensing, and operating
the first geologic repository by 1998 and for siting a second geologic repository.

4.6.4 MULTIATTRIBUTE UTILITY ANALYSIS FOR SITE DOWNSELECTION
In 1986, DOE published a “Multiattribute Utility Analysis of Sites Nominated for Characterization for
the First Radioactive Waste Repository – A Decision-Aiding Methodology” (US DOE 1986b; Merkhofer
& Keeney, 1987; Keeney, 1987), which was used to rank the five sites that had been nominated as
part of the draft environmental assessment process by that point. This more formal analysis was
based on the generic siting guidelines in 10 CFR 960 and was developed partly in response to the
large number of comments on the inadequacy of previous methods.
The multiattribute analysis stated the site-recommendation decision must be based on geophysical,
geological, geochemical, and hydrologic data; other information; information reported in
assessments; and a consideration of geological diversity. The methodology involved a sensitivity

analysis that explicitly weighed the pros and cons of the nominated sites, as an aide to policymakers
and to allow better communication of the basis for a siting decision to the public.
The multiattribute analysis included possible postclosure performance of the repositories for a range
of scenarios that may lead to releases. Postclosure performance was balanced against preclosure
performance, which included costs and socioeconomic factors (Figure 4-5).

The multiattribute analysis resulted in a ranking of the five sites in the following order:
 1. Yucca Mountain, Nevada (volcanic tuff)
 2. Richton Dome, Mississippi (salt dome)

The main difference among the sites that contributed most to the overall ranking was the estimated
difference in preclosure impacts, which in turn was dominated by the expected total costs
(Merkhover & Keeney, 1987). The analysis essentially considered that the estimated differences
between the sites in terms of health and safety, environment, and socioeconomic impacts were not
significant compared to the difference in total costs. The costs differences were due to waste
transportation, repository development, construction, operation, and decommissioning.
On 28 May 1986, DOE announced it was recommending three sites for characterization:
 • Hanford, Washington (basalt),
 • Yucca Mountain, Nevada (unsaturated zone volcanic tuff), and
 • Deaf Smith, Texas (bedded salt).
The disparity between the ranking due to the multiattribute analysis and the three chosen sites was
due to factors beyond those considered in the formal analysis (Merkhover & Keeney, 1987). A key
consideration was to maintain a diversity of rock types, which strongly influenced the downselection
of the three final sites. The initial list of nine sites also encompassed three geologies, and only one of
the 7 salt sites was recommended.

4.6.5 DEFENSE/COMMERCIAL COMMINGLING DECISION
The NWPA required disposal of defense HLW with SNF in a single repository, unless the President
determined a separate facility is required (transuranic defense waste was already destined for
WIPP). On 30 April 1985, President Reagan stated in a memo that he found no basis to conclude,
based on the circumstances at the time, a separate Defense HLW repository was required. DOE
analysis at the time indicated co-disposing civilian and defense waste could save up to $1.5 Billion.
This had the effect of “commingling” defense and civilian wastes (US DOE, 2015). This also made
accessible funding sources for repository development from government agencies associated with
defense waste, to supplement the existing commercially derived funding from Nuclear Waste Fund.
In 1993, Congress changed this and instead established a separate Defense Nuclear Waste
Disposal Appropriation for accumulating funds related to defense waste disposal (US DOE Inspector
General, 2024).

4.6.6 NUCLEAR WASTE POLICY AMENDMENTS ACT
The Nuclear Waste Policy Amendments Act (NWPAA) was passed in 1987, ending nearly 30 years of
site selection with a single mandated site (Yucca Mountain), with the intention to determine after
selection if it was a suitable site for a repository. The NWPAA directed that if, at any time Yucca
Mountain was found unsuitable, studies were to be stopped immediately, the site restored, and DOE
was to report to Congress within six months on a recommended course of action.
The NWPAA (US Congress, 1987) phased out all site-specific activities at sites other than Yucca
Mountain, and had the following effects on the storage and disposal program in the US:
 • Termination of all site-specific activities for a second repository
 • Opportunity for Nevada and Nye County to conduct oversight of the process at the expense of
 the Waste Fund.
 • A report to Congress on potential impacts to Nevada from site characterization
 • Revocation of previous DOE proposal for Monitored Retrievable Storage, but creating a
 Nuclear Waste Negotiator to find a state or tribe willing to host Monitored Retrievable
 Storage
 • Establishment of the Nuclear Waste Technical Review Board (NWTRB)
 • Establishment of requirements and restrictions on transportation of nuclear waste
 • Investigation into sub-seabed disposal as an alternative
 • Study and evaluation of the use of dry-cask storage technology until a permanent repository
 has been constructed, licensed, and can receive SNF

4.7 YUCCA MOUNTAIN AND WIPP DEVELOPMENT (1988-2008)
4.7.1 WIPP LICENSING
In 1996, the Land Withdrawal Act (LWA) was passed; it was the most significant piece of legislation
for WIPP. The main intent of the LWA was to withdraw land from public use for use by WIPP, task EPA
as the regulator, and list tasks the DOE would need to complete before the WIPP could start disposal
operations. The LWA limited the WIPP to dispose of only defense transuranic waste. The LWA also
more precisely defined transuranic waste bound for WIPP, including the definition of contact and
remote handled waste types based on the outer waste container’s dose rate. The total quantity of
transuranic waste that could be emplaced at WIPP was then defined as 175,560 m3 [6.2 million ft3]

February 2026                                                                                           67

(the Consultation and Cooperation Agreement with New Mexico already set the remote-handled
waste limit to 7,080 m3 [250,000 ft3]).
The WIPP was certified in 1999 for disposal of mixed waste (combined radioactive and hazardous
wastes) and received its first shipment of waste from Los Alamos in March 1999. WIPP is jointly
regulated by both the EPA (for nuclear waste) and the New Mexico Environment Department (for
hazardous waste). WIPP has been accepting transuranic defense waste since certification, with re-
certifications nominally occurring every 5 years.

4.7.2 YUCCA MOUNTAIN LICENSING
In 2001, the National Academy of Sciences concluded that after 40 years of study “geologic disposal
remains the only scientifically and technically credible long-term solution available to meet safety
needs.”
While there was significant activity on the Yucca Mountain Project from 1988 through 2008
(Rechard et al., 2014), the siting process for a repository for SNF and HLW was complete with the
passage of the NWPAA. In 2002, DOE completed the final Yucca Mountain Environmental Impact
Statement on site selection (4,904 pages long). After nine months of review, the Secretary of Energy
(Abraham) then recommended Yucca Mountain to President George W. Bush, who then
recommended Yucca Mountain to Congress. Nevada formally disapproved the recommendation, as
allowed by the NWPA. Congress then voted to override Nevada’s disapproval. President Bush signed
the Yucca Mountain Development Act, authorizing DOE to apply to NRC for construction
authorization. In 2004, multiple courts rejected Nevada’s appeals.
In 2008, DOE submitted the Safety Analysis Report and License Application to the NRC for review.
That same year, the Secretary of Energy (Bodman) also gave three options to the President and
Congress (US DOE, 2008) regarding the growing inventory of SNF from commercial reactors, which
would soon exceed the legally defined limit for the Yucca Mountain site (70,000 metric tons heavy
metal):
 • increase the statutory limit for the maximum amount of waste that could be emplaced in
 Yucca Mountain by at least three times,
 • begin the process to site a second repository, or
 • defer the decision indefinitely and store SNF and HLW.

4.8 THE POST-YUCCA MOUNTAIN PERIOD (AFTER 2008)
After the successful submission of the Yucca Mountain Safety Analysis Report and License
Application to the NRC, in 2010, the Obama administration zeroed the budget for the Yucca
Mountain Project. DOE filed a motion with the Atomic Safety Licensing Board to withdraw the License
Application with prejudice because the Yucca Mountain Project was “not a workable option” and “the
nation needs a different solution for nuclear waste disposal” (Rechard et al., 2014).
In 2013, the US Federal Court of Appeals ordered the NRC to complete its staff review of the Yucca
Mountain License Application with the remaining funds they had. In 2015, the NRC staff completed
the review and found that “DOE has demonstrated compliance with the NRC regulatory
requirements” for both pre-closure and post-closure safety for Yucca Mountain. The Yucca Mountain
licensing process is still suspended indefinitely (i.e., no funding appropriation from Congress).
Approximately 300 technical contentions remain to be heard and resolved before a licensing board
can reach a decision.
The NWPAA states no site-specific research related to nuclear waste disposal can occur, except at
Yucca Mountain. On top of this, since the administration has continued to zero the budget for the

Yucca Mountain Project, no work can occur on Yucca Mountain. To continue work despite this
impasse, generic research and development was carried out by the DOE Office of Nuclear Energy
(DOE-NE) under the Used Fuel Disposition Program (2010-2016), followed by the Spent Fuel and
Waste Science and Technology Program (2017-2025). This generic research considered disposal in
salt, crystalline, argillite rocks, and unsaturated alluvium for a mined disposal concept and the

conducted, international collaborations with other countries was kept up, including collaborating with
countries that were actively siting a repository.

4.8.1 THE BLUE RIBBON COMMISION
In 2010, once the Yucca Mountain Project budget was zeroed out, DOE assembled a “Blue Ribbon
Commission on America’s Nuclear Future” to explore options for nuclear waste disposal, following
shutdown of the Yucca Mountain Project. The resulting 2012 report produced by the Blue-Ribbon
Commission recommended DOE adopt a consent-based siting approach, and had several specific
recommendations relevant to siting:
 • The US should undertake an integrated nuclear waste management program to develop
 permanent deep geological disposal facilities for SNF and HLW.
 • A new, single-purpose organization (i.e., a “Fedcorp”) is needed to develop and implement a
 program for transportation, storage and disposal of radioactive waste.
 • The Nuclear Waste Fund must be made available to the new organization.
 • A new approach is needed to site and develop facilities, which should be:
 o Adaptive: a flexible process, producing decisions that are responsive to new
 information, and technical, social, or political developments
 o Staged: decisions are taken as necessary, rather than being pre-determined
 o Consent-based: affected communities can decide to accept the facility and retain
 local control
 o Transparent: stakeholders can understand and engage in the process
 o Standards and science-based: public confidence that facilities have met rigorous,
 objective, and consistently applied standards
 o Governed by partnership: arrangements or legally enforceable agreements should be
 made with affected communities
 • The current division of regulatory responsibilities between the NRC and EPA is appropriate
 and should continue, and they should develop new site-independent safety standards.
 • The roles, responsibilities, and authorities of local, state, and tribal governments must be an
 element of negotiation between the federal government and affected parties. All parties
 should have a role in important decisions. States and tribes should retain authority of
 aspects of regulation.
The Commission also found that the Nuclear Waste Fund was entangled in recent budget rules to
combat the deficit. Originally, the Nuclear Waste Fund was created to make the siting, construction,
and maintenance of a repository completely self-financed, but decades of layers of budget

4.8.2 DOE DISPOSAL OPTIONS
In October 2014, following up on the Blue-Ribbon Commission’s report, DOE-NE published a Disposal
Options study (US DOE, 2014). This study considered the wide range of different waste types DOE is
required to manage and dispose (e.g., commercial SNF, defense HLW, naval reactor SNF, research
reactor SNF, and other smaller waste streams). The Disposal Options report indicated the three
primary options available to DOE for disposal of several different waste streams, all previously
destined for Yucca Mountain, were:
 • Dispose of all HLW and SNF (regardless of origin) into a common repository (i.e., status quo)
 • Dispose of some DOE-managed HLW and SNF into a separate mined repository (i.e., a
 defense repository), which could separate the thermally cooler defense HLW from thermally
 hotter commercial SNF.
 • Dispose of physically smaller waste forms into deep boreholes. This alternative could allow
 separate disposal of the physically small 1,936 cesium and strontium capsules currently
 stored at the Waste Encapsulation and Storage Facility at Hanford.
The report went on to recommend pursuing parallel disposal options for some DOE-managed HLW
and SNF. Following the Blue-Ribbon Commission’s recommendations, the report recommended DOE
begin implementing a phased, adaptive, and consent-based strategy with development of a separate
mined repository for some DOE-managed HLW and cooler DOE-managed SNF, potentially including
some naval SNF.

4.8.3 DE-COMMINGLING DECISION
On March 24, 2015, President Barrak Obama released a memo stating, “In accordance with the
[Nuclear Waste Policy] Act, I find the development of a repository for the disposal of high-level
radioactive waste resulting from the atomic energy defense activities only is required.” This had the
effect of “de-commingling” defense HLW from commercial SNF (i.e., reversing the 1985 memo by
President Reagan) and allowed the potential siting and development of a Defense HLW repository
aside from Yucca Mountain, for example the Deep Borehole Field Test for physically small waste
forms (US DOE, 2015).

4.8.4 CONSENT-BASED SITING
Based on the Blue-Ribbon Commission’s recommendations, DOE initiated a consent-based siting
process in 2013 for a deep geological disposal facility for SNF and HLW. The 2013 DOE Strategy
called for “a phased, adaptive, and consent-based approach to siting and implementing a
comprehensive management and disposal system.” In December 2015, the Federal Register
solicited public comments on design of a consent-based siting process for both storage and disposal
facilities (80 FR 79872). Drawing from feedback obtained at public meetings held at 10 locations
throughout 2016, efforts were made to develop a deliberate strategy for siting that included the
principles of: Equity, Trust, Well-being, Power, and Longevity (Richter et al., 2022).
This system was envisioned to include (US DOE, 2017; US Congress, 2021):
 • A pilot interim storage facility, focused on accepting SNF from shutdown reactors
 • A larger consolidated interim storage facility (potentially co-located with the pilot facility
 and/or a geological repository)
 • One or more geologic repositories for SNF and HLW
 • Investigation of the concept of deep borehole disposal.

4.8.5 SITING OTHER NUCLEAR FACILITIES AND CONSOLIDATED INTERIM STORAGE
Local public support for the WIPP project in southeastern New Mexico is generally high. This has led
to other nuclear-related facilities being sited or seeking to be sited in southeastern New Mexico.
Urenco USA built a uranium enrichment facility that has been in operation since 2010. The
enrichment facility is near Eunice, New Mexico (~80 km east of WIPP).
Waste Control Specialists built and licensed a Class A, B, and C low-level radioactive near-surface
disposal facility in 2011. It has been receiving low-level radioactive waste from several states
involved in disposal compacts with Texas. Waste Control Specialists’ site is near Andrews, Texas and
is immediately across the border from Urenco in NM.
In 2016, Interim Storage Partners (comprised of Waste Control Specialists and Orano) applied to the
NRC to for a consolidated interim storage facility for SNF in Andrews, County Texas. Then in 2017,
Holtec International also applied to the NRC for a consolidated interim storage facility for SNF in Lea
County, New Mexico (adjacent to Andrews County and WIPP). Both applications have been
challenged in federal court multiple times, but they remain on the NRC docket. Consolidated interim
storage of SNF in New Mexico and Texas has seen strong political opposition from the state level in
New Mexico (US Congress, 2021; 2024).

4.8.6 RECENT EXTERNAL RECOMMENDATIONS REGARDING REGULATIONS
In November 2015, the NWTRB published an extensive historical summary report on US and
international experience with the repository siting process. The report recommended designing a
future siting process for the US (NWTRB, 2015). The board had four main recommendations:
 1. DOE’s 1984 Siting Guidelines (10 CFR 960) should be adopted as a basis for siting in
 diverse rock types, rather than using the DOE’s 2001 site-specific regulations for Yucca
 Mountain.
 2. The Siting Guidelines should be supplemented with host-rock-specific criteria that are
 applicable to geology-specific disposal concepts.
 3. Ambiguity in the interpretation of the 1984 Siting Guidelines should be minimized, to the
 extent possible, in future site-suitability criteria, ensuring process objectivity and public
 confidence.
 4. Any new siting process should preserve the 1982 NWPA requirement that a final choice of
 site awaits extensive underground site characterization.
As was found during the Crystalline Repository Project (Trask et al., 1986), the direct application of
the 1984 DOE Siting Guidelines may be difficult in some rock types, due to lack of existing
information at the required depths. In rock types associated with resource exploitation (e.g., mining
or oil and gas), there is generally more information available for the early stages of the siting
evaluation. Development of host-rock specific criteria would make future development more
straightforward.
In 2023, the American Nuclear Society convened an expert panel that considered requirements for
regulations on radwaste disposal. Consistent with the Blue-Ribbon Commission’s, the panel
published a recommendation that new NRC and EPA standards be developed for radioactive waste
disposal, specifically:

February 2026                                                                                             71

 • Current generic standards developed by the EPA for SNF, HLW, and transuranic wastes are
 more than 30 years old and are inconsistent with modern international approaches to health
 and safety standards.
 • An apparent disconnect has formed between release limits and public health and safety, so
 the standards do not instill public confidence in effective regulatory oversight.
 • The current generic standards were developed for mined geological repository systems and
 are difficult to apply directly to alternatives like deep borehole disposal.

4.9 LESSONS LEARNED FROM US SITING
The siting process for a geologic repository for HLW and SNF evolved significantly since it started in
the 1950s. The US siting program started off singularly focused on bedded salt. During the 1970s,
several initiatives then gradually expanded the search to wider varieties of rock types (domal salt,
then crystalline rocks, and finally argillite) and therefore more potential regions around the US. After
the NWPA, the siting process was formalized and intentionally included a diversity of rock types (i.e.,
include rock types found at contaminated DOE-owned sites) and siting at least two repositories for
HLW and SNF. With the WIPP sited in salt for defense transuranic waste, the desire for diversification
of rock types that host repositories, and the prioritization of already-contaminated DOE-owned sites,
led to the selection of Yucca Mountain in tuff for the nation’s SNF and HLW.
Formal analysis is an internationally supported approach to decision making (i.e., it makes the
process more transparent, and may help justifying decisions to the public), but it is only useful if one
includes all the processes that might impact decisions. During the down selection from five to three
sites, DOE chose to not follow the recommendations of the Multiattribute Utility Analysis. In the US
siting and licensing process, the regulations have often been developed in tandem with the site
investigation and licensing processes. Internationally, it is generally considered good practice to have
clear regulations agreed upon before the process begins.
Many of the technical siting criteria and processes used in the US program are like those used in
other international programs. Two aspects of the US DGR siting program stand out as somewhat
different: 1) the explicit desire to maintain geologic diversity during the downselection process, and
2) the desire to prioritize already-contaminated federally owned sites.
Some of the most recent publications on the topic of siting (NWTRB, 2015; ANS, 2023) have focused
on the need for new generic US regulations on siting and disposal standards, to bring the US into line
with health and safety standards used across the rest of the world. Generic US disposal standards
are from the 1980s (10 CFR 960). More recent (2001) site-specific disposal standards from Yucca
Mountain do not generalize well to other sites (i.e., the volcanic tuff of Yucca Mountain is unique) or
alternative disposal concepts, like deep borehole disposal.

5 SITING SUMMARY AND LESSONS LEARNED
This report presents three international case studies and US history related to siting of a deep
geological repository (DGR) for high-level radioactive waste (HLW) disposal. Each country has its own
approach, and these processes must be seen in the context of the specific societal, economic,
cultural, legal, and political conditions associated with each national program. The following sections
discuss some commonalities and lessons learned from international experience in siting DGRs.

5.1 SUMMARY OF NATIONAL CASE STUDIES
The Swiss program recently finalized its site selection process, proposing a site in argillite host rock
in Northern Switzerland. Starting from a “white map” of the country, suitable geological siting areas
were first identified based on existing geologic knowledge, then progressively narrowed down to a
single site. Switzerland’s regulation requires finding a site that is safe enough to protect the
population from radioactive waste for one million years.
The German program is currently making progress on siting in the first of three siting phases. The
search began with a white map of Germany, initially screening out areas using existing geological
information that could be disqualified for one or more hazards to avoid. A large portion of the country
remains to be investigated further in future siting and downselection efforts. Currently, the program
is ranking sites from different rock types (argillite, crystalline, bedded salt, and domal salt), which by
necessity have different disposal concepts, and these different rock types have different degrees of
data availability. The comparison is further complicated because each disposal concept may rely on
engineered barriers to lesser or greater degrees. Germany, by regulation, must demonstrate that it
has identified the “best” site for a DGR in all the country.
Canada has taken a different approach to siting and has recently selected a host site from an initially
large number of candidate sites that came forward on a volunteer basis. The site selection process
emerged through a two-year national dialogue. The process was designed to seek informed and
willing hosts, and meet the highest scientific, professional and ethical standards. It reflected the
ideas, experience, and best advice of a broad cross-section of Canadians who shared their thoughts
on what an open, transparent, fair, and inclusive process for making this decision should include.
The following 13 principles emerged as the foundation upon which the siting process should be built,
offering a valuable blueprint for a successful site selection framework:

 Focus on safety Shared decision-making
 Meet or exceed regulatory requirements Inclusiveness

 Informed and willing host community Support capacity building

 Focus on the nuclear provinces Informing the process

 Right to withdraw Community well-being

 Siting process led by interested communities Ongoing engagement of government

 Indigenous rights, treaties and land claims

In 2024, the Canadian Nuclear Waste Management Organization (NWMO) selected Wabigoon Lake
Ojibway Nation and the Township of Ignace as the host for their DGR.
Although the United States site selection program started informally in the 1950s and was expanded
and refined through the 1970s, the siting process began officially with the Nuclear Waste Policy Act
in 1982. This act formalized a significant effort to locate both a primary and secondary repository for

February 2026                                                                                                77

spent nuclear fuel (SNF) and defense HLW. It was a technical downselection process that started
with a variety of host rock types and regions at the national level and became more refined and
detailed as it progressed. For the first repository, nine potentially acceptable sites were found across
five regions. These were downselected to five sites (one from each region) that were later nominated
for site investigation, and eventually three sites were selected for characterization. Two unique
tenets of the US search were the desire to maintain a diversity of host rock types among proposed
sites, and to champion the use of federally controlled sites that were already contaminated from
defense-related activities. The Nuclear Waste Policy Amendments Act in 1987 cut short the ongoing
parallel site-characterization process at the three selected sites and specified Yucca Mountain as the
site for the first repository and stopped the search for a second repository.
In 2002, the Yucca Mountain environmental impact statement was submitted to Congress. The state
of Nevada protested, and Congress overruled their protest to select the site. In 2008, the Yucca
Mountain License Application was submitted to the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, and the next
year a change in administration defunded the Yucca Mountain Project because it was “not a
workable option.” In 2015, efforts were made to re-start the siting process as a consent-based siting
approach (as recommended in 2012 by the “Blue Ribbon Commission on America’s Nuclear
Future”), but in 2017 these efforts were terminated. The current administration has begun an effort
to first site a consolidated interim storage facility for SNF, before siting a DGR. Any approach the US
may take (aside from continuing with Yucca Mountain) will require the 1982 Nuclear Waste Policy
Act and its 1987 amendment to be changed.

5.2 SPECTRUM OF SITING STRATEGIES
The earliest investigation into radioactive waste disposal (National Research Council, 1957)
suggested that deep geological disposal in the US should be feasible at many sites (due to the large
number and diversity of potential sites available) but also stated “the research to ascertain feasibility
of disposal has for the most part not been done.” Since this time, through numerous international
efforts including safety assessments and experimental programs in generic and site-specific
underground research laboratories (URLs) around the world, this foundational research has now
largely been done.
Today there is consensus within the international scientific community that geological disposal in
DGRs is the safest method available to isolate waste from the environment and public. It is currently
being pursued by most countries with nuclear programs. It is also internationally acknowledged that
successful siting of a DGR needs to be supported by fair, open, transparent, and inclusive social
engagement programs to build public acceptance and confidence (NTI, 2022).
There exists a spectrum of siting approaches, which range from “downselection starting with a white
map of the entire country” to “evaluate volunteer sites that come forward within a consent-based
siting process.” Regardless of the approach, it is generally agreed that community and stakeholder
support is necessary at the local, state, and federal levels. As illustrated in this report, this support
can be achieved, in parallel with site evaluation, using a range of siting strategies.
In Switzerland, the siting process was based on a clearly defined procedure that, under the
leadership of the federal government, was based on a comparative technical evaluation of all
suitable regions in Switzerland. The strategy was predicated upon an expectation that the Swiss
public would accept a final geologic disposal site at the local, regional, and national level provided
the safety and technical feasibility of a site were convincingly demonstrated and the comparative
selection process was considered fair, transparent, and participatory in nature. The so-called
Sectoral Plan, developed with strong involvement of a wide range of interest groups and broad
consultation, laid out the selection framework, clearly defining safety and technical feasibility criteria,
discussing additional spatial and socio-economic factors to be considered, and setting up a three-
stage procedure for the site selection process. The plan also defined the role of multiple

February 2026                                                                                                 78

stakeholders. While the federal government played a major role in overseeing the process, regions in
Switzerland (i.e., cantons) performed their own spatial planning, and any conflicts were resolved
between the siting cantons, neighboring cantons, and the federal authorities. The Sectoral Plan also
made a concerted effort to ensure extensive public engagement via regional conferences. One siting
area alone hosted over 40 plenary assemblies, including technical working groups on surface
infrastructure, safety, regional development, and infrastructure communities. These interactions
promoted clear communication with local stakeholders and were seen as a critical component of the
site selection process.
After several setbacks due to public opposition, Germany restarted its siting program with the
development of new national law on the Search and Selection of a Site for a Repository for Highly
Radioactive Waste (StandAG). To achieve a national consensus, the StandAG was evaluated by a
joint federal/state committee of members representing different stakeholders and interests, the
“Commission on the Storage of High-Level Radioactive Waste.” Based on the commission’s final
report, Germany in 2017 implemented a site selection process that included all regions in Germany
with suitable geologies, including rock salt (bedded and domal), clay rock, and crystalline rock (i.e.,
granite or metamorphic). Germany believes that site selection based on a national assessment with
clear technical criteria would be best suited for gaining broad consensus. In some ways, the German
approach is like the criteria-based site selection framework that led to a successful site selection in
Switzerland. And like in Switzerland, transparency and public participation are key elements of the
German site selection procedure, which attempts to combine geoscientific and societal principles for
improved public acceptance. The government established various formats, such as regional
conferences and public hearings, but as the country has already learned during its ongoing Phase I
assessments, creating a truly inclusive and effective dialog to gain public acceptance and trust
remains a key challenge.
In Canada, the siting strategy was to seek informed and willing hosts. Communities were initially
invited to learn more about the siting process with no obligation to host the project until the safety of
the site was confirmed, the potential impacts on community well-being were assessed, and the
residents of the interested communities had expressed their willingness to host the project based on
mutually agreed hosting agreements. The site selection process was initiated in 2010, and by 2012
22 communities expressed interest in learning more. The number of interested communities was
gradually narrowed down through a series of technical and socio-economic-cultural assessments
(well-being), designed to assess sites’ ability to meet the project’s robust safety requirements, the
possibility of developing a safe and socially acceptable plan to transport the used nuclear fuel to the
site, and the potential to build supportive and resilient partnerships in the siting areas. The two last
communities remaining in the site selection process were required to develop their own processes
for defining willingness and determining how they would express it.
In the US, the strategy for siting a DGR was laid out in the Nuclear Waste Policy Act (1982) and in the
generic siting guidelines (10 CFR 960) from 1984. From 1982 to 1987, the US siting process was
analogous to technical downselection processes in Switzerland and Germany. Two unique aspects of
the technical US siting strategy were 1) the focus on already-polluted federally owned sites, and 2)
the desire to maintain diversity of host rock types throughout the siting downselection process. The
siting process progressed from nine potentially acceptable sites to three sites for characterization
but was then cut short by selection of the Yucca Mountain site through an amendment to the
Nuclear Waste Policy Act in 1987. During the siting and licensing process in the US (for both WIPP
and Yucca Mountain), the regulations were being developed and refined as the sites were being
selected and characterized. While this approach can be successful (e.g., WIPP), the process appears
more transparent and fairer when a clear set of procedures and regulations are in place at the
beginning.
International disposal programs have come to understand that the siting of a radioactive waste DGR
is no longer solely a technical problem to solve or optimize, it is a balance between technical and

February 2026                                                                                               79

social issues. Each country may choose different ways to address the social aspects of siting.
Transparency, collaboration, trust, and technical rigor all can be used to provide confidence in the
process to the participating communities, hoping to reduce potential fears or doubts regarding the
process. A 1979 US Comptroller General report observed that political opposition to radwaste
disposal has not centered on the need for a repository, nor the concept of geological disposal itself.
Political opposition always centered on proposed locations. No one wants a nuclear waste repository
in their backyard because of the fear that the wastes can escape to the environment. This fear
cannot be overcome until the risk of specific repository proposals can be assessed and found
acceptably safe. Such assurance cannot be given, of course, until a repository is built and tested.
Although no repository for SNF has yet been fully constructed and implemented (Finland is
constructing one currently), repositories for low-level waste, intermediate-level waste, and
transuranic waste have been implemented successfully in multiple countries and in multiple rock
types. These successes help to instill confidence that the same can be done for HLW.

5.3 COMMONALITIES AND DIFFERENCES BETWEEN APPROACHES
Efforts to site DGRs for HLW and SNF have been ongoing for many decades, and there are many
lessons to be learned from both the successes and failures of DGR siting programs around the
world. Some of the key learnings include the importance of:
 • a robust implementation framework at the outset to guide the implementation of the
 overall DGR siting process as well as later construction, operation and closure;
 • clear and traceable site evaluation process and criteria established before starting; and
 • early involvement of stakeholders to build public trust and confidence in deep geological
 disposal and the siting process.

5.3.1 BUILDING STAKEHOLDER CONFIDENCE
There is no universal template for the best implementation framework for every country. A successful
approach will be shaped by the specific socio-economic, cultural, political, and legal conditions in
each country. Despite this lack of a universal standard, it generally believed that seeking confidence
of stakeholders improves the chances of the success for the overall DGR program.
Stakeholder confidence in the site selection process can be improved through the following
enablers:
 • Confidence amongst most stakeholders that geological disposal is the best approach for the
 safe and secure long-term management of SNF, in a manner that will protect this and future
 generations. The safety, community well-being, and ethical arguments in favor of disposal
 should be clearly articulated and understood by both the implementing organization and
 communities involved in the site selection process.
 Ideally, the elements of this narrative are developed early in the programs with the
 involvement of stakeholders, and include dialogues around key questions such as:
 a. What is the nature of the problem and why it should it be addressed now?
 b. What are the solutions are available?
 c. What principles and approaches should be used to identify and implement the
 best solution?
 • The existence of a clear legislative and regulatory framework at the start of the process. The
 framework should include clear policies and waste management strategies, and adequate
 human and financial resources to cover all phases of the DGR program. The definition of the

February 2026                                                                                            80

 ground rules for the process up front increases transparency and can build public trust and
 confidence.
 • Clear enumeration of the various stakeholders involved in decision making and indication of
 their accountabilities. This listing should include all relevant portions of the government:
 regulators, waste owners, transportation safety groups, and implementing organizations.
 Well-defined roles and responsibilities tend to increase public trust and confidence.
 • A strong, independent, and technically savvy regulator with an open and transparent
 decision-making process that allows for public input and participation.
 • A trusted, competent, and potentially independent implementing organization with adequate
 resources to keep the project on track (e.g., Nagra, NWMO, or a Fedcorp).
 • Broad and enduring political support for the overall DGR program. Ensure continued
 engagement and sufficient resources during the long process to site selection and
 constructions (i.e., spanning multiple political administrations).
 • Support for continued research and development related to siting, design, construction,
 operation, and closure of a DGR. Research and development necessarily require a
 commitment to international cooperation.
 • A socially acceptable site selection process to identify a suitable site that meets safety
 requirements and is guided by clear site requirements and traceable site evaluation process.
 • Stakeholder engagement programs from the beginning, allowing for two-way dialogues to
 build awareness, mutual learning, addressing questions and concerns and ultimately building
 support.
 • Independent of the process, the question “Why here and not there?” must have a simple and
 convincing answer.
The above enablers of stakeholder confidence are consistent with the outcome of the Blue-Ribbon
Commission and other studies in the US.

5.3.2 SITE EVALUATION PROCESS AND SITE SELECTION CRITERIA
This review of the Swiss, German, Canadian and US siting approaches found many commonalities
between them in terms of the technical site evaluation process and the technical criteria for site
assessment and comparison. Practices are well established internationally and generally quite
consistent from country to country. Such consistency is in part due to widely accepted guidance
documents from international agencies such as the International Atomic Energy Agency (e.g., IAEA,
2012). In contrast, social criteria and socio-economic assessments may vary from country to country
as they depend on socio-economic conditions and specific regulatory requirements.
When investigating any site or comparing sites (be it sites chosen from a national downselect, or
sites that have come forward voluntarily), the investigation typically progresses in a stepwise and
graded manner. First, exclusion criteria and minimum requirements are applied. These criteria will
check, for example, whether the proposed location violates any regulations (e.g., within a national
park) or is too close to a geological feature that could cause problems (e.g., active volcanoes or
faults).
Sites that are not excluded in a preliminary vetting via exclusion criteria are then evaluated against
progressively more detailed siting criteria, some qualitative and some quantitative. These siting
criteria are designed to feed into the eventual safety case which must be made for any site that is
selected and licensed. The safety case includes both pre-closure and post-closure considerations
about the future repository, so the siting process should include these factors as well. The siting

February 2026                                                                                            81

process should consider the safety functions provided by both the host rock and any man-made
engineered features in the disposal concept. In many programs, detailed criteria are sorted into
higher-level categories, such as criteria related to suitability of “Host rock properties”, criteria that
support “Long-term stability” of a site over a million-year timeframe, criteria that investigate the
“Reliability of geological findings” meaning how well sites can be characterized, and finally criteria
ensuring “Engineering suitability.” When comparing sites with each other, programs use the criteria
to rank sites in which case they may use weighting criteria and rating schemes like “favorable,”
“conditionally favorable” or “less favorable.” The ultimate purpose is to enable comparative
evaluation of areas regarding their suitability as repository sites.
To feed into more detailed siting studies (including preliminary postclosure performance assessment
modeling), increasingly more detailed information will be needed about the relevant geological
systems at the site. At first the information will come from the existing data sources and the open
literature. After initial downselection, more involved site characterization is conducted to obtain
detailed information about the site, typically involving surface geophysics, and purpose-drilled
boreholes.
The characterization process may culminate in a site-specific URL, where the most detailed
information on the site can be obtained, and long-term experiments and demonstrations can be
conducted.
Early site comparisons based on existing data may be biased because of site data availability. Areas
with historical hydrocarbon or mineral exploration often have an abundance of data (e.g.,
sedimentary layers with claystone), whereas crystalline rock areas may lack geologic characterization
data due to minimal economic interest. A DGR is often intentionally sited away from active resource
exploitation, but this also means the site characterization process may need to start with little
existing information about the rocks at depth. Siting programs need to carefully address such
concerns about data availability bias to avoid unwarranted exclusion of sites or rock types.

5.4 CONCLUDING LESSONS LEARNED
There are many aspects that are agreed upon internationally (i.e., they have each built upon one
another, learning from mistakes and building on successes). Drawing from the commonalities
observed in the siting process, the following lessons learned from the site evaluation process were
identified by on one of the authors from their experience in the Canadian program.
 • Assessing the suitability of potential sites and identifying a preferred site can take decades.
 The chances of success are greatly increased when site evaluations are conducted within a
 holistic site evaluation framework describing how site evaluations will be conducted in terms
 of evaluation phases, objectives and approach for each phase, timelines, decision points and
 criteria. Having a clear site evaluation framework is key for the traceability, fairness and
 credibility of findings and building stakeholders confidence in the overall siting process.
 • It is helpful when technical siting criteria are developed with engagement in mind, and ideally
 with the involvement of key stakeholders. It is also helpful when the categorization of site
 selection criteria provides clear linkages to DGR safety functions and expected performance
 objectives, to illustrate the many safety dimensions that are involved when siting DGRs.
 • Applying exclusion and siting criteria during the early stages of the site evaluations can be
 challenging especially when dealing with many potential candidate sites and limited amount
 and quality of field data which is often the case during the early phases. In this context, the
 following aspects need to be carefully considered to preserve the fairness and credibility of
 site evaluations during the early stages of site evaluations:
 - Set achievable and realistic objectives considering the extent of data availability,

February 2026                                                                                               82

 - Identify site characteristics and criteria that can be realistically assessed with available
 data at each evaluation phase,
 - Avoid inadvertent exclusion of sites because of lack of data or differences in data
 resolution,
 - Avoid inadvertent over-interpretation of field data, which can be challenged,
 - Document rationale for findings in a traceable manner that is understandable by non-
 technical audiences, and
 - Qualify the findings of the evaluations through acknowledging and documenting
 uncertainties and additional work needed to address them.
In summary, after reviewing international practices on DGR siting, we find that the process is largely
consistent across multiple countries, despite their apparent differences in approaches taken. Well-
developed internationally recognized siting approaches exist. In the US, generic Siting Guidelines
exist (10 CFR 960), and despite their age (more than 40 years old), they make a good foundation for
future activities. Making comparisons between sites associated with different host rock types (and
therefore slightly different disposal concepts) is complex, and the development of host-rock specific
siting criteria would make the process more transparent moving forward.

Criterion for evaluation of hydrochemical circumstances
The chemical composition of the deep waters and the solid mineral phases in the effective
containment zone should positively affect the retention of radionuclides, even after the
emplacement of containers and support materials, and should not chemically attack the material of
technical and geotechnical barriers. The indicators in this regard are:
 1. chemical equilibrium between the host rock in the effective containment zone and the deep
 groundwater it contains,
 2. neutral to slightly alkaline conditions (pH value 7 to 8) in the deep groundwater,
 3. an anoxic or reducing environment in the deep groundwater,
 4. the lowest possible content of colloids and complexing agents in the deep groundwater, and
 5. a low carbonate concentration in the deep groundwater.
