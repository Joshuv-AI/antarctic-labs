A1 Specialized Centers of Research Excellence (SCORE) on sex differences: Introduction to the scientific abstracts submitted for the SCORE 2025 annual meeting

Rajeev K. Agarwal, Chyren Hunter

Office of Research on Women’s Health (ORWH), National Institutes of Health (NIH), Bethesda, MD, USA

Correspondence: Rajeev K. Agarwal (rajeev.agarwal@nih.gov)

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(23):A1

The Specialized Centers of Research Excellence (SCORE) program on sex differences is supported through a U54 cooperative agreement and serves as a flagship initiative of the Office of Research on Women’s Health (ORWH), NIH. This program is co-funded by eight NIH Institutes and Centers (ICs): NHLBI, NIA, NIAAA, NIAMS, NIDA, NIDDK, NIEHS, and NIMH.

The primary objective of the SCORE is to accelerate the development and application of new knowledge related to diseases that affect women, deepen understanding of their etiology, and foster improved strategies for treatment and prevention. SCORE represents an innovative, interdisciplinary research effort focused on sex differences and major medical conditions impacting women.

Each SCORE center serves as a national resource for translational research; these centers together represent vital hubs for disease agnostic research and dissemination of innovative sex-based and sex-informed translational research methods and best practices. A new Career Enhancement Core has been introduced to support pilot research initiatives and to train next generation of scientists.

SCORE programs emphasize rigor and reproducibility in biomedical research. They provide leadership in developing and promoting standards and policies for considering sex as a biological variable (SABV) in research. Understanding the contributions of biological sex is essential for explaining health outcomes and informing the next generation of interventions and treatments to improve women’s health. ORWH advances sex differences research across these centers and coordinates cross-SCORE collaborations by partnering strategically with NIH ICs.

The current SCORE U54 program builds on more than 20 years of prior investment. Research conducted by these specialized centers has led to seminal contributions in the study of sex differences and women’s health. Notably, the ORWH SCORE program remains the only NIH specialized center program supporting disease-agnostic research on sex differences.

Currently, twelve active SCORE centers across the United States are co-funded by ORWH and seven NIH ICs (NHLBI, NIA, NIAAA, NIAMS, NIDA, NIDDK, NIMH), representing an annual investment of more than $18 million.

The SCORE 2025 Annual Meeting was held on March 17, 2026, at the Natcher Conference Center, NIH Bethesda Campus, Maryland, USA. The theme of meeting was, “From the Bench to the Health of the Nation.” The abstracts presented in this issue are from the SCORE 2025 Annual Meeting, including an abstract from the Keynote presenter, Dr. Jill Becker, University of Michigan.

Abstracts are organized by SCORE, beginning with the plenary talk delivered by that center’s senior leadership, followed by poster presentation abstracts. The research highlighted by these SCOREs represents the highest quality of scientific work supported by ORWH and participating NIH Institutes. It is a wonderful opportunity to showcase the excellence and impact of the science being conducted within these centers.

The keynote presentation by Dr. Jill Becker is available via NIH Videocast: https://videocast.nih.gov/watch=57220.

A2 Integrating sex differences research into advancing women’s health

Jill B. Becker

University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, MI

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(23):A2

Research that includes both sexes and compares the results by sex, in other words, sex differences research, is necessary for scientific research to find rigorous, reliable, and reproducible answers for the causes and treatment of diseases. This is because a patient’s sex can affect their health, disease susceptibility, and optimal treatment. How can we integrate sex differences research into outcomes that advance women’s health? First, the scientific and medical communities need to consider that not all sex differences are the same. The statistical tests we use to compare groups must account not only for qualitative and quantitative differences but also for population differences. Furthermore, even when one aspect of a trait is fundamentally the same, there may be latent or mechanistic differences mediating the trait that differ between the sexes. Finally, males and females exhibit different developmental trajectories throughout their lives, so a finding of no sex differences at one age may not hold true at a different age. Thus, research and clinical trials of treatments must account for potential differences in the underlying causes of a syndrome or disease that may benefit from sex-specific treatments. Examples include the mechanisms mediating sex differences in addiction and possible treatments that are sex specific. The more we learn, the clearer it becomes that biomedical research into sex differences in neurological disorders, immunology, cardiovascular disease and function, obesity and metabolism, addiction and motivation, mental health, bone health and function, cancer, aging, and so on is important and is needed now. By studying females and males, important insights are gained that can improve treatments for both sexes and advance women’s health.

A3 Design and dissemination of educational illustrated materials for cardiovascular health outreach among women in the rural South-East of the United States

Amanda Y. Behr1, Laura Castro1, Samuel Strecker1, Brianna McIndoe2, Britanny Pinkerton3, Michael Ryan4, Marlo Vernon2, Daria Ilatovskaya5, Jennifer Sullivan5

1Department of Medical Illustration, Augusta University, Augusta, Georgia, USA; 2School of Public Health, Department of Institute for Public and Preventive Health, Augusta University, Augusta, Georgia, USA; 3Department of Kinesiology, Augusta University, Augusta, Georgia, USA; 4U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs, Columbia, South Carolina, USA; 5Department of Physiology, Augusta University, Augusta, Georgia, USA

Correspondence: Amanda Y. Behr (abehr@augusta.edu)

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(23):A3

CVD particularly affect women in rural US areas. Low health literacy increases morbidity and mortality by 1.71 times among rural patients. Rural communities face limited access to health information while educational illustrations aid comprehension and retention. The AU ROAR SCORE is uniquely positioned to design educational materials via its partnership with AU Medical Illustrations. English and Spanish illustrative materials (postcards, bookmarks, coloring pages, stickers, posters) were created to facilitate rural outreach. The materials were distributed in conjunction with blood pressure (BP) checks at farmers markets, schools, health and sporting events. A culturally aware educational exhibit with take-home handouts and a BP cuff was created with a goal to empower rural women with access to BP machines.

In 2024-25, the AU SCORE reached out to over 3,000 individuals in GA and SC. Pilot results showed strong interest in stickers promoting CVD health, bookmarks featuring BP categories, and postcards with women-specific symptoms of stroke. The educational exhibit debuted at the Harris Literacy Center in GA and has been on display at the “Back to school” event at the Augusta HUB (800+ participants), and the Costa Layman Health Fair for migrant farm workers (1,000+ attendees). Viewers were asked to rate exhibit on a five-point scale. How to be Healthy, What is BP, and Understanding BP, and Self-Care station (which offered access to a BP machine) panels received average scores of 4.8, 4.9, 4.9 and 5.0 for clarity, 4.5, 4.6, 4.6 and 4.5 for motivation, and 4.2, 4.8, 4.3 and 4.8 for creativity, respectively. The ROAR outreach materials received positive feedback for motivating viewers to learn more about CVD and BP and for the clarity of information presentation. Future research will evaluate the impact of the exhibitions paired with access to BP cuffs for checkout in rural libraries, which has been initiated in Hancock County (GA).

A4 Harnessing skeletal muscle estrogen to preserve lean mass and improve metabolic health in type 2 diabetes

Reilly T. Enos, Christian A. Unger, Marion C. Hope III, M. Chase Kettering, Cassidy E. Socia, Barton C. Rice, Darya Niamira, Cody G. King

1Department of Pathology, Microbiology, and Immunology, University of South Carolina-School of Medicine, Columbia, SC, USA

Correspondence: Reilly T. Enos (enosr@email.sc.edu)

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(23):A4

Background

Obesity, type II diabetes (T2D), and skeletal muscle (SkM) atrophy form a vicious cycle, with ~90% of the 35 million US T2D patients being overweight and facing doubled risk of sarcopenia. Current therapies, including glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), manage T2D and obesity but risk lean mass loss, potentially exacerbating SkM atrophy [1, 2]. We propose SkM-derived estrogen as a novel therapeutic target to simultaneously combat SkM atrophy and obesity, leveraging our finding that local estrogen enrichment enhances SkM mass and fatty acid oxidation while reducing adiposity.

Materials and methods

Using a novel transgenic mouse model overexpressing aromatase in SkM to mimic human physiology, we will study estrogen’s role in obesity-induced T2D (db/db model). Aim 1 will assess protein turnover via stable isotope labeling and mass spectrometry to identify pathways preserving SkM mass. Aim 2 will investigate estrogen’s regulation of pyruvate carboxylase (PCX) using in vitro studies and two novel models: inducible SkM-PCX deletion (SkM-AROM↑,SkM-PCX-/-) and SkM-PCX overexpression (SkM-PCX↑). Hormone levels will be measured using sensitive analytical methods.

Results

Preliminary data show SkM aromatase overexpression increases muscle mass, enhances fatty acid oxidation, and reduces adiposity, while SkM estrogen receptor-alpha deletion promotes muscle loss and fat accumulation. Estrogen upregulates SkM PCX, suggesting a novel mechanism for fat oxidation.

Conclusions

SkM-derived estrogen offers a transformative approach to mitigate T2D-associated SkM atrophy and obesity by regulating protein turnover and PCX-mediated fat oxidation. This work, may establish aromatase and PCX as therapeutic targets, advancing precision treatments for T2D and sarcopenia in both sexes.

A5 Unraveling netrin-1: a key mediator of sex-based differences in ADPKD progression

Riyaz Mohamed1, Lily Witherington2, Prabhav Gundeboina2, Jennifer C. Sullivan1

1Department of Physiology, Augusta University, Augusta, GA 30912; 2Department of Biological Science, Augusta University, Augusta, GA 30912

Correspondence: Riyaz Mohamed (rmohamed@augusta.edu)

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(23):A5

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is the most common inherited cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD), characterized by excessive cellular proliferation and fluid-filled cyst formation in the kidneys, ultimately leading to kidney failure. Notably, a sex difference has been observed in PKD, with males exhibiting larger cysts and faster disease progression in both clinical cases and rodent PKD models. However, the underlying mechanisms driving these differences remain poorly understood. Netrin-1, a laminin-related secreted protein that is upregulated following kidney injury, is known to play roles in cell proliferation, migration, and tumor growth. However, its involvement in PKD and potential sex-specific differences has not been explored. We hypothesize that greater upregulation of netrin-1 in male mice contributes to accelerated cyst growth compared to females. To test this, we disrupted netrin-1 signaling in PKD by genetically deleting the netrin-1 gene in renal tubules of the Pkd1RC/RC (RC/RC) mouse model of ADPKD. In parallel, 10-week-old wild-type (WT) and RC/RC male and female mice were randomized to receive either vehicle or a netrin-1–specific neutralizing monoclonal antibody (10mg/kg/ip/48h, n=4–5/group) for 4-weeks. At 14 weeks of age, all mice were euthanized, and blood and kidney samples were collected for histological, biochemical, and Western blot analyses. Renal netrin-1 protein levels were quantified using Western blot and ELISA, renal function was evaluated via measurement of blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and cell proliferation was via ki67 staining. Our results showed that renal netrin-1 was significantly greater (P<0.001) in Pkd1RC/RC mice, accompanied by elevated BUN (P<0.0001), cell proliferation (P<0.001) and cyst growth (P<0.0001) compared to respective WT. Moreover, netrin-1 expression, cyst growth, and kidney dysfunction were significantly higher in male versus female RC/RC mice (Psex*TxT<0.002). Tubular netrin-1 overexpression induces cyst growth only in males (Psex*TxT<0.0001). Importantly, genetic deletion or pharmacological neutralization of netrin-1 significantly reduced cell proliferation (PTxT<0.001) and cyst growth (PTxT<0.01) and improved kidney function (PTxT<0.01) in both sexes, however the effect was more pronounced in males (Psex* PTxT<0.002). These findings suggest that netrin-1 contributes to sex-specific differences in cyst progression in male and female Pkd1RC/RC mice. Future studies will aim to elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which netrin-1 mediates cyst growth in both sexes.

A6 Investigating sex-specific responses and the role of estrogen in anti-melanoma therapy

Samson E. Simon1, Tiannan Wang1, David W. Wolff1,2

1Georgia Cancer Center, Augusta University, Augusta, GA 30912; 2Department of Pathology, Medical College of Georgia, Augusta University, Augusta, GA 30912

Correspondence: David W. Wolff (dwolff1@augusta.edu)

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(23):A6

Metastatic melanoma is a highly lethal cancer which causes approximately 8,000 deaths annually in the United States. Current standard-of-care therapies including inhibition of mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) and immune checkpoint blockade have significantly improved outcomes for patients with advanced melanoma, with median survival improving from 6-11 months in 2013 to 2-3 years at present. However, roughly one third of melanoma patients do not respond to immunotherapy, and most responders eventually experience disease progression. Additionally, patients who receive MAPK inhibitors as second line therapy following failure of immune checkpoint blockade have a median progression free survival of approximately 7 months. Thus, there is an unmet clinical need to increase the efficacy of MAPK inhibition and delay the onset of drug resistance in patients who progress despite receiving immunotherapy. To this end, our group has identified and is currently evaluating a novel drug combination which aims to use an FDA-approved compound to prevent resistance to MAPK inhibitors. We utilize a genetic melanoma mouse model driven by an activating mutation in the MAPK BRAF, and our encouraging preliminary results show durable anti-tumor control in this model. Interestingly, a meta-analysis of clinical data revealed that women with melanoma respond more robustly to MAPK inhibition than men, with an approximately 30% increase in progression free survival. However, the underlying biology producing this discrepancy is unknown. Therefore, we will begin addressing this question by assessing the role of estrogen in response to MAPK inhibition in melanoma. First, we will utilize the BRAF-driven mouse model to establish whether male and female mice bearing melanoma tumors recapitulate clinical observations by responding differently to MAPK inhibition. Second, we will directly test the role of estrogen in progression of melanoma tumors and response to MAPK inhibition. The results of these studies may inform more optimized treatment plans for both men and women afflicted with melanoma in the future.

A7 Psychosocial stressors in relation to a number of menopause-related symptoms in midlife women

Emily Oken, Jorge Chavarro

Harvard Pilgrim Health Care Institute and Harvard TH Chan School of Public Health

Correspondence: Emily Oken

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(23):A7

The goals of our ROSA SCORE Project were to evaluate associations of lifetime stressors with health outcomes among midlife women. We hypothesized that psychosocial stressors in early life and across the reproductive years would predict more menopause-related symptoms in midlife.

We used data from Project Viva, an existing longitudinal cohort study enrolled 1999-2002 in early pregnancy and followed for over 2 decades. We collected exposures of lifetime psychosocial stressors and infertility history at study enrollment. With SCORE support we assessed outcomes in midlife, including menopausal symptoms using the Menopause Rating Scale (MRS), a validated 11-item questionnaire that assesses somatic, psychological, and urogenital symptoms, as well as depressive symptoms and sleep quality.

Over 800 women provided data at a Mid-Life Visit (2017-2021, median 51 years, 43% post-menopausal). We found that history of physical abuse (reported by 37%) was associated with more symptoms in the somato-vegetative and psychological MRS domains and with worse general health (OR, 1.73; 95% CI, 1.17, 2.55) and greater depressive symptoms (OR, 1.74; 95% CI, 1.05, 2.87). History of sexual abuse (8%) was associated with worse menopausal symptoms (2.81 points; 95% CI, 1.05, 4.56) and worse general health (OR, 2.04; 95% CI, 1.04, 4.03) but not with depressive symptoms. History of financial instability (11%) was associated with worse menopausal symptoms (1.92 points; 0.49, 3.34), worse general health (OR, 2.16; 95% CI, 1.24, 3.75), and greater depressive symptoms (OR, 2.68; 95% CI, 1.44, 4.98). Women with prior infertility (37%) were more likely to score above the median on the MRS (OR 1.45; 95%CI: 1.04, 2.01) and had higher odds for any depressive mood (OR 1.56; 95% CI: 1.12, 2.16) and irritability (OR, 1.57; 95% CI: 1.13, 2.19). There was a trend toward greater severity of sleep problems among women with prior infertility. We also found that any adverse lifetime experiences, especially adverse childhood experiences (ACE), had deleterious associations with sleep in midlife. Each additional ACE exposure was associated with higher self-reported sleep disturbance and sleep-related impairment, and with sleep duration < 6 h/night (OR 1.19, 95% CI: 1.00, 1.42) in midlife.

Among this cohort of parous women, social stressors in early life are strong predictors of hot flashes, sleep problems, and mood symptoms in midlife.

A8 Modulation of body temperature by Mkrn3 in a mouse model of menopause

Delphine Franssen1, Yun Li1, Encarnacion Torres1, Miguel Sena Esteves2, Stephanie A Roberts1, Rona S Carroll1, Ursula B Kaiser1

1Division of Endocrinology, Diabetes and Hypertension, Brigham and Women’s Hospital and Harvard Medical School, Boston, MA, USA; 2Department of Neurology, UMass Chan Medical School, Worcester, MA, USA

Correspondence: Delphine Franssen

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(23):A8

Background

Menopause, characterized by estradiol depletion, frequently causes vasomotor symptoms (VMS) that substantially impair quality of life. Recent research implicates hypothalamic neurokinin B (NKB) in VMS onset. MKRN3, an E3 ubiquitin ligase highly expressed during the juvenile period, has been shown to reduce NKB levels. This study investigates whether Mkrn3 overexpression can regulate NKB and decrease menopausal VMS using an estrogen-deficient mouse model.

Materials and Methods

Adult female wild-type mice received bilateral arcuate nucleus (ARC) injections of either AAV-CMV-Mkrn3-EGFP (overexpression group) or AAV-CMV-EGFP (control) via stereotactic delivery. Three weeks post-injection, mice underwent ovariectomy (OVX) to induce estrogen deficiency. Core and skin temperatures were monitored at 10-min intervals using DST-nano probes (ventral tail for skin; abdominal for core) starting one-week pre-OVX and continuing two weeks post-OVX. This protocol was also applied to Mkrn3 knockout female mice. Upon completion, mice were perfused, and brain tissue collected for immunohistochemical analysis of Mkrn3 and NKB expression.

Results

Pre-OVX, Mkrn3 overexpression mice showed significantly elevated core temperatures during light phase and lower temperatures during dark phase compared to controls, resulting in reduced circadian variation. Skin temperatures showed no significant differences between groups before or after OVX. Immunohistochemistry confirmed accurate viral targeting, with EGFP detected in both groups but Mkrn3 expression restricted to ARC in overexpression mice. Two weeks post-OVX, ARC NKB levels were not significantly different between groups. In Mkrn3 knockout mice, OVX induced significant core and skin temperature reductions, not observed in wild-type mice.

Conclusion

Modification of Mkrn3 levels in the ARC influences adult female mouse core temperature. Mkrn3 overexpression modulates core temperature phase-dependently, with opposite light/dark effects that disrupt circadian rhythmicity. Contrary to the initial hypothesis, these effects occurred without measurable NKB changes. Finally, Mkrn3 deletion affected post-OVX temperature responses, reducing both skin and core temperatures. These findings suggest a role of Mkrn3 in thermoregulatory control and circadian temperature dynamics, offering insights for potential non-hormonal menopausal VMS treatments.

A9 Molecular profiling of pregnancy through the lens of biological aging

Jesse R. Poganik, Stephen Albright, Vadim N. Gladyshev

Division of Genetics, Department of Medicine, Brigham and Women’s Hospital, Harvard Medical School, Boston, MA, USA

Correspondence: Jesse R. Poganik (jpoganik@bwh.harvard.edu)

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(23):A9

The United States has significantly higher maternal mortality rates compared to other developed nations, highlighting the need for improved approaches to maternal health monitoring. Recent advances in aging research have provided powerful new tools for health assessment. Our previous work demonstrated that pregnancy induces a transient but reproducible acceleration in biological age, as measured by DNA methylation biomarkers across independent cohorts [1]. This finding has subsequently been validated by multiple research groups, establishing pregnancy as a physiological state that temporarily accelerates aging processes [2–4]. Given the strong correlation between aging biomarkers and adverse health outcomes, this discovery has opened new avenues for understanding maternal health through aging biology.

We are conducting a study extending our analysis of pregnancy using plasma proteomic profiling. Our approach leverages organ-specific aging signatures to understand how different biological systems respond to pregnancy, with particular focus on the influence of maternal age. Additionally, we are integrating clinical outcomes to identify molecular signatures associated with pregnancy complications. Our work is generating new insights into pregnancy-related proteomic changes and identifying candidate biomarkers for adverse pregnancy outcomes. We anticipate providing immediately actionable insights into pregnancy biology while establishing the foundation for future large-scale studies to develop tools for pregnancy monitoring and risk assessment.

A10 The role of sleep as a potential mediator between environmental exposures and mood

Matthew D. Weaver1, Shadab A. Rahman1, Elizabeth B. Klerman1, Trang M. VoPham2

1Division of Sleep and Circadian Disorders, Mass General Brigham; Division of Sleep Medicine, Harvard Medical School, Boston, MA, USA; 2Epidemiology Program, Public Health Sciences Division, Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center, Seattle, WA, USA

Correspondence: Matthew D. Weaver

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(23):A10

Background Place-based exposures and characteristics of our environment have a pervasive impact on health and well-being, including our sleep and mood. The magnitude and direction of these relationships remain poorly understood due to a lack of multi-level study designs that incorporate both individual- and place-based exposures. We sought to characterize these relationships in a large, nationally representative study population.

Materials and Methods We utilized data from 5 distinct US nationwide surveys administered in 2022. Demographics included age, sex, race, ethnicity, and education. Geocoded addresses were linked with environmental exposures of particulate matter ≤ 2.5 microns air pollution (PM2.5), outdoor artificial light-at-night (aLAN), and the Area Deprivation Index (ADI) using a geographic information system. Potential mediators included sleep duration (< 7 h considered insufficient) and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) score. Mood disturbance was operationalized as depression or anxiety on the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-4). Multilevel, multivariable logistic regression models controlled for confounders and mutually adjusted for the other environmental variables. Causal mediation analysis was conducted using structural equation models.

Results Cross-sectional responses from 24,908 US adults were analyzed. Higher annual average PM2.5 exposure (highest [>8.8 μg/m3] vs. lowest [<6.6 μg/m3] quintile: aOR 1.13, 95% CI 1.03-1.25) and higher ADI (highest [>80] vs. lowest [≤20] quintile: aOR 1.28, 95% CI 1.17-1.41) were each associated with increased odds of mood disturbance. aLAN was not associated with mood. Insufficient and poor quality sleep were more common among females (p<0.001), with highest prevalence among females age 45-54. Insufficient (aOR 2.36, 95% CI 2.22-2.51) and poor quality sleep (aOR 3.66, 95% CI 3.43-3.91) were associated with mood disturbance. Insufficient sleep mediated 8.5% of the total effect of PM2.5 on mood, while poor sleep quality mediated 28.6% of the total effect of ADI on mood.

Conclusions Environmental exposures, insufficient and poor quality sleep are each independently associated with mood disturbance. The relationship between environmental exposures and mood is mediated by sleep health.

A11 Development of next-generation in vitro vascular models for the identification of disease-relevant, sex-based biomarkers of cardiovascular disease

Nethika R. Ariyasinghe1, Sean Escopete2, Divya Gupta2, Ellery Jones3, Kevin Shores3, Ben Ngu4, Roberta De Souza Santos5, Andrew Gross5, Megan L. McCain4, Dhruv Sareen5, Anjaparavanda P. Naren1, C. Noel Bairey Merz2, George Truskey3, Sarah J. Parker2

1Department of Medicine, Cedars-Sinai Medical Center, Los Angeles, CA, USA; 2Smidt Heart Institute, Cedars-Sinai SCORE, Cedars-Sinai Medical Center, Los Angeles, CA, USA; 3Department of Biomedical Engineering, Duke University, Durham, NC, USA; 4Alfred E. Mann Department of Biomedical Engineering, University of Southern California, Los Angeles, CA, USA; 5Cedars-Sinai Biomanufacturing Center, Cedars-Sinai Medical Center, West Hollywood, CA, USA

Correspondence: Nethika R. Ariyasinghe (nethika.ariyasinghe@cshs.org)

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(23):A11

Risk of aortic cardiovascular complications during pregnancy is highest during the third trimester and the early postpartum period [1], and the effects of pregnancy on vascular structure and function are not fully understood. Recently, a murine model demonstrated pregnancy-associated vascular remodeling, including aortic wall thickening, luminal diameter expansion, and altered aortic stiffness [2]. While changes in vascular structure after pregnancy have been observed, the influence of pregnancy-related fluctuations in female sex hormones on aortic wall strength is unclear. A female-specific human aortic model could further our understanding of the effects of pregnancy on cardiovascular health. Therefore, our goal is to develop a tissue-engineered, female-specific model to investigate drivers of pregnancy-associated cardiovascular diseases. To this end, we have conducted proteomic analysis to examine sex differences in induced Pluripotent Stem Cell (iPSC)-derived vascular smooth muscle cells, and we developed and optimized an iPSC-derived tissue-engineered aorta model. In the future, we aim to generate a female engineered aortic model to examine the molecular and functional effects of pregnancy-related hormone changes on the aorta.

Background: With menopause and the loss of estradiol, some women experience memory decline, decreased glucose metabolism, and major depressive disorder (MDD), resulting in a period of vulnerability in women. Here, we assessed sex differences in the impact of oxidative stress and MDD on metabolic health and memory decline in midlife and the role of menopause.

Materials and Methods: 140 participants (73M:67F) underwent memory assessments at ages 45-55 (T1) and 55-63 (T2). At T1, we measured oxidative stress and MDD status. At T2, we assessed metabolic health and mitochondrial function (Seahorse assay). From T1 to T2, 37 women transitioned from pre/peri- to postmenopause and 26 were postmenopause.

Results: Higher oxidative stress at T1 was significantly associated with lower mitochondria function at T2 (β=-14.02, p=0.01), with significant sex differences (p=0.05). Conclusions: Results suggest that oxidative stress impacts cellular respiration and has a greater longitudinal impact on memory circuitry in women v. men as women transition through menopause. Further, MDD during menopause has a higher comorbidity with metabolic disorders and greater impact on memory decline.

Background

Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death in both men and women in Westernized countries, but there are sex differences in pathology and clinical outcomes. There are knowledge gaps regarding atherogenesis in females due to underrepresentation in basic research and clinical trials, and an alarming trend of increasing coronary artery disease in young women. Atherosclerotic plaques in men tend to have lipid-rich necrotic cores, high inflammatory profile, and thin fibrous caps that increase vulnerability to rupture. Plaques in women are typically higher in fibrotic content, have a thicker fibrous cap, and are more likely to undergo erosion rather than rupture. Materials and methods

To identify the contributions of gonadal sex (ovaries vs. testes) and genetic sex (XX vs. XY chromosomes) to atherogenesis, we studied Four Core Genotypes (FCG) mice (XX mice with ovaries or testes, and XY mice with ovaries or testes). The sex-biased events detected here in a mouse model parallel those noted in humans and allow increased mechanistic insight about the underlying sex determinants.

Conclusion

Our analysis of sex determinants of atherosclerosis has revealed independent influence of gonadal and chromosomal sex on lesion pathology and gene expression at the single cell level. Our data identify sex components that are associated with increased cellular transdifferentiation, fibrous cap thickness, and inflammatory gene expression that may provide sex-specific targets in human atherosclerosis evaluation and treatment.
