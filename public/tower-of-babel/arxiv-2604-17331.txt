Evaluation of Gauss-Legendre curves
Filip Chudy, Paweł Woźny

Abstract
We present new representations of Gauss–Legendre polynomials and their derivatives in the shifted power basis and in bases related to symmetric orthogonal Jacobi polynomials. Using these representations and certain recurrence relations, we propose efficient O ⁡ ( n 2 + d ​ n ) O(n^{2}+dn) methods for evaluating a Gauss–Legendre curve of degree n n in 𝔼 d \mathbb{E}^{d} . We also propose algorithms for multipoint evaluation with computational complexity O ⁡ ( M ​ d ​ n + d ​ n 2 ) O(Mdn+dn^{2}) , where M M is the number of evaluation points.



1
Introduction

The
Gauss–Legendre curves
(
GL curves
for short), recently
introduced in
[
7
]
(see also
[
8
]
), are polynomial
parametric curves closely related to Gauss–Legendre quadratures and Legendre
orthogonal polynomials (see, e.g.,
[
2
]
,
[
6
]
). They have
recently started gaining traction in CAGD, partly due to their very good shape
control properties. Unlike Bézier curves (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
),
GL curves closely follow the control polygon, even at high degrees.
Therefore, they may have many practical applications, for example in numerical
analysis and computer graphics.

However, the so-called
Gauss–Legendre poly nomials

(
GL polynomials
for short), which form the basis of GL curves, are
defined in a rather complicated manner, and their efficient evaluation is
nontrivial. Note that the evaluation of points on GL curves is not discussed
in
[
7
]
.

The article is organized as follows. In Section
3
, we briefly
describe the GL polynomials and GL curves and recall their main properties.
The new representations of the GL polynomials and their derivatives are presented
in Section
4
, where we consider expansions with respect to the
shifted power basis and some orthogonal bases related to symmetric Jacobi
polynomials (cf. §
2
). Then, in Section
5
, using
these representations, we propose fast and numerically stable algorithms for
evaluating GL curves. Their computational complexity is
O(n^{2}+dn)
when
computing a point on a GL curve of degree
n\in\mathbb{N}
in
\mathbb{E}^{d}

(d\geq 1)
. Moreover, we investigate the problem of multipoint evaluation of GL
curves and propose algorithms that run in
O(Mdn+dn^{2})
time, where
M
denotes
the number of evaluation points. Usually,
M\gg n
and
d
is small, so the
computational complexity is
O(Mdn)
. Results of the conducted numerical
experiments are presented in §
6
.

2
Symmetric Jacobi polynomials

We now recall several well-known properties of orthogonal polynomials from the

Jacobi family
(of which the Legendre polynomials are a member) that
will be useful in the study presented in this paper. For details and proofs of
the results given below, we refer, for example, to
[
3
]
,

[
6
]
,
[
9
]
, and the references given therein.

The
Jacobi polynomials

P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{m}\in\Pi_{m}\setminus\Pi_{m-1}

(m\in\mathbb{N};\,\alpha,\beta>-1)
are orthogonal in the sense that

\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{i}(x)P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{j}(x)\,\mbox{d}x=\delta_{ij}h_{i},

where
\delta_{ij}
denotes the
Kronecker delta
,
h_{i}>0
, and

\Pi_{\ell}
is the set of polynomials of degree
\leq\ell

(
\Pi_{-1}:=\emptyset
).

We use the standard normalization

P_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(1)=\binom{k+\alpha}{k}\qquad(k=0,1,\ldots)

which uniquely defines the Jacobi polynomials.

In this paper, we focus on the case
\alpha=\beta
(which corresponds to the
so-called
Gegenbauer polynomials
). Thus, for simplicity, we set

P^{(\alpha)}_{k}(x):=P^{(\alpha,\alpha)}_{k}(x)\qquad(k=0,1,\ldots;\,\alpha>-1).

(2.1)

The polynomials
P^{(\alpha)}_{k}
have the following explicit representation:

\displaystyle P^{(\alpha)}_{k}(x)=\frac{(\alpha+1)_{k}}{k!}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\displaystyle{-k,\,k+2\alpha+1}}\\[1.07639pt]
{\displaystyle{\alpha+1}}\end{array}\bigg|\,{\displaystyle{\frac{1-x}{2}}}\right)\qquad(k=0,1,\ldots),

(2.2)

where
{}_{p}F_{q}
is the
generalized hypergeometric function
, and

(a)_{i}
denotes the
Pochhammer symbol
(see, e.g.,
[
3
]
,

[
6
]
).

They fulfill a recurrence relation, namely

\displaystyle P_{k}^{(\alpha)}(x)=x\,\xi^{(\alpha)}_{1}(k)P_{k-1}^{(\alpha)}(x)-\xi^{(\alpha)}_{2}(k)P_{k-2}^{(\alpha)}(x)\qquad(k=2,3,\ldots),

(2.3)

where

\xi^{(\alpha)}_{1}(k):=\frac{(k+\alpha)(2k+2\alpha-1)}{k(k+2\alpha)}\quad(k\geq 1),\qquad\xi^{(\alpha)}_{2}(k):=\frac{(k+\alpha-1)_{2}}{k(k+2\alpha)}\quad(k\geq 2),

and
P_{0}^{(\alpha)}(x)=1
,

P_{1}^{(\alpha)}(x)=\xi^{(\alpha)}_{1}(1)\,x=(\alpha+1)x
. Hence, for given

x\in\mathbb{R}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha>-1
,
P^{(\alpha)}_{m}(x)
can be
evaluated in
O(m)
time.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have the following symmetry:

P_{k}^{(\alpha)}(x)=(-1)^{k}P_{k}^{(\alpha)}(-x).

(2.4)

The
i
th derivative of
P_{k}^{(\alpha)}
is also a symmetric Jacobi polynomial,
i.e.,

\frac{\mbox{d}^{i}}{\mbox{d}x^{i}}P_{k}^{(\alpha)}(x)=\frac{(k+2\alpha+1)_{i}}{2^{i}}P_{k-i}^{(\alpha+i)}(x),

(2.5)

where
k,i\in\mathbb{N}
and we adopt the convention—used throughout the
sequel—that
P_{\ell}^{(\alpha)}\equiv 0
for
\ell=-1,-2,\ldots
.

Let us consider the polynomial
u_{m}\in\Pi_{m}

(m\in\mathbb{N})
given in the

symmetric Jacobi form
,

u_{m}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}v_{k}P_{k}^{(\alpha)}(x),

where the coefficients
v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
are known. Recall that,
for a given
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the value
u_{m}(x)
can be computed in
O(m)
time
using the famous
Clenshaw algorithm
(see, e.g,
[
2
]
):

1.

Set
B_{m+1}=B_{m+2}:=0
.

2.

For
k=m,m-1,\ldots,0
, set

B_{k}:=v_{k}+x\,\xi^{(\alpha)}_{1}(k+1)B_{k+1}-\xi^{(\alpha)}_{2}(k+2)B_{k+2}.

Then
u_{m}(x)=B_{0}
.

Note that the Clenshaw algorithm follows from the recurrence
relation (
2.3
).

2.1
Legendre polynomials

We write
P_{k}(x):=P_{k}^{(0)}(x)
for

k=0,1,\ldots
. Clearly, the polynomials
P_{k}
are the well-known

Legendre polynomials
.

We will use the following classical Christoffel–Darboux formula for Legendre
polynomials:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}(2i+1)P_{i}(x)P_{i}(y)=(k+1)\frac{P_{k}(y)P_{k+1}(x)-P_{k}(x)P_{k+1}(y)}{x-y},

(2.6)

where
x\neq y
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

It is also true that the indefinite integral of the polynomial
P_{k}
is given
by

\int P_{k}(x)\,\mbox{d}x=\frac{1}{2k+1}\left(P_{k+1}(x)-P_{k-1}(x)\right)+\mbox{const.},

(2.7)

where
k\geq 0
. Furthermore, it can be checked that

\int P_{k}(x)\,\mbox{d}x=\frac{1}{k(k+1)}(x^{2}-1)\frac{\mbox{d}}{\mbox{d}x}P_{k}(x)+\mbox{const.}\\

(2.8)

for
k\geq 1
.

Assumption 2.1
.

From now on, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed. Let
\tau_{i}\equiv\tau^{(n)}_{i}

(i=1,2,\ldots,n;\ n>0)
,

-1<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\cdots<\tau_{n}<1,

(2.9)

be the zeros of the
n
th Legendre polynomial.

In this paper, we assume that the zeros
\tau^{(n)}_{i}

(i=1,2,\ldots,n)
are
known with high numerical accuracy, since they can be precomputed using, for
example, the results presented in
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
.

3
Gauss-Legendre polynomials and curves

The GL polynomials and the GL curves were recently introduced
in
[
7
]
. We now recall their definitions, as well as some of their
main properties proved in that paper.

The GL polynomials
F^{n}_{i}\in\Pi_{n}\setminus\Pi_{n-1}

(i=0,1,\ldots,n)
are
given by

F^{n}_{i}(t):=G^{n}_{i}(t)-G^{n}_{i+1}(t),

(3.1)

where
G^{n}_{0}(t)=-G^{n}_{n+1}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{2}
, and

G^{n}_{i}(t):=\frac{nP_{n-1}(\tau_{i})}{2}\int_{-1}^{t}\frac{P_{n}(x)}{x-\tau_{i}}\,\mbox{d}x-\frac{1}{2},

(3.2)

for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
.

Polynomials
F^{n}_{0},F^{n}_{1},\ldots,F^{n}_{n}
form a basis of
\Pi_{n}
. They also
give the
partition of unity
, i.e.,

\sum_{i=0}^{n}F^{n}_{i}(t)\equiv 1\qquad(t\in\mathbb{R}).

We have

F^{n}_{i}(-1)=\delta_{0i},\qquad F^{n}_{i}(1)=\delta_{ni}\qquad(i=0,1,\ldots,n).

(3.3)

Moreover, the following symmetries hold:

F^{n}_{i}(-t)=-F^{n}_{n-i}(t)\quad(0\leq i\leq n),

(3.4)

because
G^{n}_{i}(-t)=-G^{n}_{n-i+1}(t)
for
0\leq i\leq n+1
.

The GL curve
\mbox{{P}}_{n}:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{E}^{d}

(d\geq 1)
of
degree

n

is a parametric polynomial curve of the form

\mbox{{P}}_{n}(t):=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\mbox{{W}}_{i}F^{n}_{i}(t),

(3.5)

where
\mbox{{W}}_{0},\mbox{{W}}_{1},\ldots,\mbox{{W}}_{n}\in\mathbb{E}^{d}
are the
so-called
control points
.

Note that
\mbox{{P}}_{n}(-1)=\mbox{{W}}_{0}
,
\mbox{{P}}_{n}(1)=\mbox{{W}}_{n}

(cf. (
3.3
)). Although the GL curve does not lie in the convex
hull of its control points, its graph closely follows its control polygon. This
property has, in recent years, contributed to the growing popularity of these
curves.

4
New representations for GL polynomials

As mentioned in §
1
, the problem of evaluating GL curves was
not considered in
[
7
]
. The purpose of this article is therefore to
propose fast and numerically stable methods for computing points on a GL
curve (
3.5
).

In view of Eq. (
3.1
), the main problem is to efficiently evaluate

G^{n}_{i}(t)
for a given
t\in[-1,1]
and for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n

(cf. (
3.2
)). To this end, we study the representations of
polynomials
G^{n}_{i}
in
(i)
shifted power basis, and
(ii)

in symmetric Jacobi basis.

4.1
Shifted power basis

Using (
2.4
) and (
2.2
), we obtain

P_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}(x+1)^{k},

(4.1)

where

a_{k}:=\frac{(-1)^{n-k}}{2^{k}}\binom{n}{k}\binom{n+k}{k}\qquad(0\leq k\leq n).

Observe that

2(k+1)^{2}a_{k+1}+(n-k)(n+k+1)a_{k}=0,

(4.2)

where
k=n-1,n-2,\ldots,0
, and

a_{n}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\binom{2n}{n}.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let the coefficients
b^{(i)}_{k}

(k=0,1,\ldots,n-1;\,1\leq i\leq n)
be such
that

\frac{P_{n}(x)}{x-\tau_{i}}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b^{(i)}_{k}(x+1)^{k}

(4.3)

(cf. (
3.2
)).

The following non-homogeneous first-order recurrence relation holds:

b^{(i)}_{k-1}-(\tau_{i}+1)b^{(i)}_{k}=a_{k}\qquad(k=n,n-1,\ldots,1),

(4.4)

where
b^{(i)}_{n}:=0
.

In addition, the coefficients
b^{(i)}_{k}
satisfy the second-order homogeneous
recurrence relation of the form

(n-k)(n+k+1)b^{(i)}_{k-1}+(2(k+1)^{2}-(\tau_{i}+1)(n-k)(n+k+1))b^{(i)}_{k}-2(\tau_{i}+1)(k+1)^{2}b^{(i)}_{k+1}=0,

(4.5)

where
k=n-1,n-2,\ldots,1
,
b^{(i)}_{n-1}=a_{n}
, and
b^{(i)}_{n}:=0
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x-\tau_{i})\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b^{(i)}_{k}(x+1)^{k}=(x+1-(\tau_{i}+1))\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b^{(i)}_{k}(x+1)^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b^{(i)}_{k}(x+1)^{k+1}-(\tau_{i}+1)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b^{(i)}_{k}(x+1)^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(b^{(i)}_{k-1}-(\tau_{i}+1)b^{(i)}_{k}\right)(x+1)^{k},

where
b^{(i)}_{-1}=b^{(i)}_{n}:=0
. It follows that
the recurrence (
4.4
) holds.

Combining (
4.4
) with (
4.2
) yields
the recurrence (
4.5
).
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Solving the recurrence (
4.4
) gives an explicit expression
for the coefficients
b^{(i)}_{k}
,

b^{(i)}_{k}=a_{k+1}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\!\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\displaystyle{k-n+1,\,n+k+2,\,1}}\\[1.07639pt]
{\displaystyle{k+2,\,k+2}}\end{array}\bigg|\,{\displaystyle{\frac{\tau_{i}+1}{2}}}\right)

(cf. (
4.1
)), where
k=0,1,\ldots,n-1
.

Now, it is easy to integrate the expansion (
4.3
) over

x\in[-1,t]

(-1\leq t\leq 1)
and obtain a representation of the polynomials

F^{n}_{i}

(i=0,1,\ldots,n)
in the shifted power basis.

Summarizing, the following statements are true.

Theorem 4.3
.

The GL polynomial
F^{n}_{i}
(cf. (
3.1
)) has the following
representation in the shifted power basis:

F^{n}_{i}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c^{(i)}_{k}(t+1)^{k},

where
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, and

c^{(i)}_{k}:=\frac{n}{2k}\left(P_{n-1}(\tau_{i})b^{(i)}_{k-1}-P_{n-1}(\tau_{i+1})b^{(i+1)}_{k-1}\right)\qquad(1\leq k\leq n).

(4.6)

We also have

\displaystyle F^{n}_{0}(t)=1-\frac{1}{2}nP_{n-1}(\tau_{1})\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}b^{(1)}_{k-1}(t+1)^{k},

\displaystyle F^{n}_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{2}nP_{n-1}(\tau_{n})\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}b^{(n)}_{k-1}(t+1)^{k}.

Hence, for fixed
0\leq i\leq n
,
F^{n}_{i}(t)

(t\in\mathbb{R})
can be evaluated
using Horner’s scheme (see, e.g.,
[
2
]
) in
O(n)
time
(cf. recurrences (
2.3
), (
4.2
) and
Lemma
4.1
).

Corollary 4.4
.

For a given
t\in(-1,1)
(cf. (
3.3
)), all the values

F^{n}_{0}(t),F^{n}_{1}(t),\ldots,F^{n}_{n}(t)

can be evaluated in
O(n^{2})
time using Horner’s schemes for representations
given in Theorem
4.3
. Note that the cost of the
computations can be reduced using the symmetries (
2.4
),
(
3.4
). This is discussed in Section
5.1
.

Thus, using the approach described in this paragraph, a point on the GL
curve (
3.5
) in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
can be computed component-wise
with a computational complexity of
O(n^{2}+dn)
.

4.2
Symmetric Jacobi basis

In this paragraph, we show that, using the Christoffel–Darboux
identity (
2.6
) and other results presented in
Section
2
, one can derive simple expansions of the polynomials

G^{n}_{i}

(i=1,\ldots,n)
closely related to the symmetric Jacobi bases. Note
that the coefficients in those representations are given in terms of the values
of Legendre polynomials at the zeros
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{n}
of
P_{n}
.

Let us define

R_{k}(t):=P_{k+1}(t)-P_{k-1}(t)\qquad(k=0,1,\ldots).

(4.7)

Lemma 4.5
.

For
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, the following identities are true:

G^{n}_{i}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}R_{k}(\tau_{i})P_{k}(t)+\frac{1}{2}P_{n-1}(\tau_{i})P_{n}(t).

(4.8)

G^{n}_{i}(t)=\frac{t}{2}+\frac{t^{2}-1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{2k+1}{2k}P_{k}(\tau_{i})P^{(1)}_{k-1}(t).

(4.9)

Proof.

From the recurrence relation (
2.3
) for
k:=n+1
,
\alpha:=0
,
and
x:=\tau_{i}
(cf. (
2.9
)), it follows that

(n+1)P_{n+1}(\tau_{i})=-nP_{n-1}(\tau_{i}),

(4.10)

because
P_{n}(\tau_{i})=0
. While from the Christoffel–Darboux
formula (
2.6
) with
k:=n
and
y:=\tau_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}(2k+1)P_{k}(\tau_{i})P_{k}(x)=-(n+1)P_{n+1}(\tau_{i})\frac{P_{n}(x)}{x-\tau_{i}}=nP_{n-1}(\tau_{i})\frac{P_{n}(x)}{x-\tau_{i}},

(4.11)

where we used (
4.10
). Here
i=1,2,\ldots,n
.

Integrating the right-hand side of (
4.11
) over
x\in[-1,t]

with
t\in[-1,1]
and using (
2.7
), we get (
4.8
),
because
P_{k}(-1)=(-1)^{k}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
(cf. (
4.1
)).

Applying (
2.5
) for
\alpha:=0
,
i:=1
to (
2.8
),
we obtain

\int P_{k}(x)\,\mbox{d}x=\frac{1}{2k}(x^{2}-1)P^{(1)}_{k-1}(x)+\mbox{const.}\qquad(k\geq 1)

(cf. (
2.1
)). Integrating as above and using the latter,
we derive the identity (
4.9
).
∎

We are now ready to formulate the main results of this paragraph.

Theorem 4.6
.

The GL polynomial
F^{n}_{i}
(cf. (
3.1
)) has the following
representation in the Legendre basis:

F^{n}_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(P_{n-1}(\tau_{i})-P_{n-1}(\tau_{i+1})\right)P_{n}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(R_{k}(\tau_{i+1})-R_{k}(\tau_{i})\right)P_{k}(t),

where
i=1,\ldots,n-1
. We also have

\displaystyle F^{n}_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}P_{n-1}(\tau_{1})P_{n}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}R_{k}(\tau_{1})P_{k}(t),

\displaystyle F^{n}_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}P_{n-1}(\tau_{n})P_{n}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}R_{k}(\tau_{n})P_{k}(t).

Theorem 4.7
.

The GL polynomial
F^{n}_{i}
can be written as:

F^{n}_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{2}(t^{2}-1)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{2k+1}{2k}(P_{k}(\tau_{i})-P_{k}(\tau_{i+1}))P^{(1)}_{k-1}(t)\right)

(cf. (
2.1
)), where
i=1,\ldots,n-1
. It is also true that

\displaystyle F^{n}_{0}(t)=1-\frac{1}{2}(t+1)\left(1+(t-1)\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{2k+1}{2k}P_{k}(\tau_{1})P^{(1)}_{k-1}(t)\right),

\displaystyle F^{n}_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{2}(t+1)\left(1+(t-1)\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{2k+1}{2k}P_{k}(\tau_{n})P^{(1)}_{k-1}(t)\right).

Observe that the expansions given in Theorem
4.7
immediately
imply (
3.3
). These representations are also better suited for
practical applications than those in the Legendre basis
(cf. Theorem
4.6
and Eq. (
4.7
)). However, by
using both results, the recurrence relation (
2.3
) and the
Clenshaw algorithm (see §
2
, p.
2
), the value

F^{n}_{i}(t)
for a given
t\in\mathbb{R}
can be computed with
O(n)

computational complexity.

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
t\in(-1,1)
(cf. (
3.3
)). All the values

F^{n}_{0}(t),F^{n}_{1}(t),\ldots,F^{n}_{n}(t)

can be evaluated in
O(n^{2})
time using the Clenshaw algorithm for the
representations given in Theorems
4.6

or
4.7
. As noted (cf. Corollary
4.4
), the
computational cost can be decreased using the symmetries (
2.4
),
(
3.4
). This is discussed in Section
5.2
, where
we make use of Theorem
4.7
.

In summary, using the methods proposed in this paragraph, one can evaluate
a point on the GL curve (
3.5
) in

\mathbb{E}^{d}
component-wise with
O(n^{2}+dn)
computational complexity.

4.3
Derivatives

In
[
7
, Theorem 16]
a complicated general formula for the
m
th
derivative of a GL polynomial
F^{n}_{i}
was derived, where the derivative is
expressed in terms of
elementary symmetric polynomials
.

It is now clear that, using the representations of GL polynomials
from §
4.1
and §
4.2
, we can
find simple expressions for their
m
th derivatives.

For example, if
1\leq i\leq n-1
and
m>0
, we have

\frac{\mbox{d}^{m}}{\mbox{d}t^{m}}F^{n}_{i}(t)=\sum_{k=m}^{n}(k-m+1)_{m}c^{(i)}_{k}(t+1)^{k-m}

(4.12)

(cf. Theorem
4.3
) and, in particular,

\frac{\mbox{d}^{m}}{\mbox{d}t^{m}}F^{n}_{i}(-1)=(-1)^{m+1}\frac{\mbox{d}^{m}}{\mbox{d}t^{m}}F^{n}_{n-i}(1)=m!c^{(i)}_{m}\qquad(i=1,2,\ldots,n-1).

On the other hand, using (
2.5
) for
\alpha:=0
yields the
following formula:

\frac{\mbox{d}^{m}}{\mbox{d}t^{m}}F^{n}_{i}(t)=\frac{(n+1)_{m}}{2^{m+1}}\left(P_{n-1}(\tau_{i})-P_{n-1}(\tau_{i+1})\right)P_{n-m}^{(m)}(t)+\frac{1}{2^{m+1}}\sum_{k=m}^{n-1}(k+1)_{m}d^{(i)}_{k}P_{k-m}^{(m)}(t),

(4.13)

where
d^{(i)}_{k}:=R_{k}(\tau_{i+1})-R_{k}(\tau_{i})

(see Theorem
4.6
and (
4.7
)).

Note that the expression (
4.12
) can be evaluated in
O(n-m)

time using the Horner’s scheme. Similarly, for a given
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
Eq. (
4.13
) can be computed with the same computational
complexity by applying the recurrence (
2.3
) and the Clenshaw
algorithm (cf. §
2
, p.
2
).

Certainly, the derivatives of
F^{n}_{0}
and
F^{n}_{n}
can be obtained in a similar
way.

5
Evaluation

We now turn to the problem of evaluating a GL curve
\mbox{{P}}_{n}
of degree
n
in

\mathbb{E}^{d}
(see (
3.5
)).

More precisely, based on the results presented in §
4
, we
propose two methods for computing

F^{n}_{0}(t),F^{n}_{1}(t),\ldots,F^{n}_{n}(t)

for a fixed
t\in\mathbb{(}-1,1)
(cf. (
3.3
)), which run
with
O(n^{2})
computational complexity. Once all these values are known, one
can determine a point
\mbox{{P}}_{n}(t)
component-wise in
O(nd)
time. This
results in a total computational complexity of
O(n^{2}+dn)
(see
Corollaries
4.4
,
4.8
).

Moreover, we present a sketch of the algorithms for the evaluation of
M

points on the curve
\mbox{{P}}_{n}
, which work in
O(Mdn+dn^{2})
time.

First of all, let us note that there is a possibility of reducing the
computational cost by using the symmetries (
3.4
). Indeed, it is
enough to consider the problem of evaluating

F^{n}_{0},F^{n}_{1},\ldots,F^{n}_{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor},

because we have

\mbox{{P}}_{n}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor}\mbox{{W}}_{i}F^{n}_{i}(t)-\sum_{i=\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor+1}^{n}\mbox{{W}}_{i}F^{n}_{n-i}(-t).

(5.1)

5.1
First approach: shifted power basis

Let us use the results from §
4.1
.

First, we need to compute

P_{n-1}(\tau_{i})\qquad(i=1,2,\ldots,\left\lceil{n/2}\right\rceil)

(cf. Assumption
2.1
). This can be accomplished by the
recurrence relation (
2.3
) with
\alpha:=0
in

O(\frac{1}{2}n^{2})
time. It is also worth making use of the following simple
observation. For odd
n=2m+1
, one of the zeros of
P_{n}
is equal to
0
. Then

n-1
is even and

P_{n-1}(0)=\frac{(-1)^{m}}{4^{m}}\binom{2m}{m}.

In the second step, we determine the coefficients
b^{(i)}_{k}

(see (
4.3
)) for all
i=1,2,\ldots,\left\lceil{n/2}\right\rceil

(cf. (
4.6
)) and all
k=0,1,\ldots,n-1
. It should be done with
the help of the recurrence relations (
4.4
)
and (
4.2
).

Finally, for a given
t\in(-1,1)
, we obtain the values

F^{n}_{0}(\pm t),F^{n}_{1}(\pm t),\ldots,F^{n}_{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor}(\pm t)

(5.2)

by using the Horner’s schemes for expansions given in
Theorem
4.3
and thus we also find component-wise the point

\mbox{{P}}_{n}(t)
(cf. (
5.1
)).

Note that this approach has
O(n^{2}+dn)
computational complexity when
computing a single point on an
n
th-degree GL curve in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
.

5.2
Second approach: symmetric Jacobi basis

Now, we present another technique for evaluating GL curves, which uses the
representations from Theorem
4.7
. Note that the same can be
done for the representations from Theorem
4.6
.

To this end, in the first step, we have to find the following values of the
Legendre polynomials:

P_{k}(\tau_{i})\qquad\mbox{for all $k=1,2,\ldots,n-1$ and $i=1,2,\ldots,\left\lceil{n/2}\right\rceil$}.

Again, this can be done using the recurrence relation (
2.3
)
with
\alpha:=0
in
O(\frac{1}{2}n^{2})
computational complexity. If
n
is
odd, one of the zeros of
P_{n}
is equal to
0
. In this case, we have

P_{k}(0)=0\quad\mbox{for odd $k$},\qquad P_{k}(0)=\frac{(-1)^{k/2}}{2^{k}}\binom{k}{k/2}\quad\mbox{for even $k$}.

Next, for a fixed
-1<t<1
, we obtain all the
values (
5.2
) using the Clenshaw algorithms with

\alpha:=1
(see §
2
, p.
2
) for the
expansions given in Theorem
4.7
.

The presented method also has a computational complexity of
O(n^{2}+dn)
when
computing a single point on a GL curve of degree
n
in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
.

5.3
Multipoint evaluation

Let us now focus on the multipoint evaluation of the GL curve, as this problem
is essential for its rendering. Suppose we want to compute

\mbox{{P}}_{n}(t_{0}),\mbox{{P}}_{n}(t_{0}),\ldots,\mbox{{P}}_{n}(t_{M}),

(5.3)

where
-1\leq t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{M}\leq 1

(M\in\mathbb{N})
, and

\mbox{{P}}_{n}:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{E}^{d}
is a GL curve of degree
n

(see (
3.5
)). Since
M\gg n
and
d
is small in practical
applications, we will consider this case in the sequel.

If we use results form §
5.1
and §
5.2
,
we can find all the points (
5.3
) in
O(M(n^{2}+dn))=O(Mn^{2})
time.

Let us observe that it is possible to find these points much faster, meaning
with a computational complexity of
O(Mdn+n^{2})=O(Mdn)
.

Remark 5.1
.

For clarity, we sketch a solution which does not use the
symmetries (
3.4
) (it means the form (
5.1
)), as this
better presents the main idea of fast multipoint evaluation.

As shown above, we can efficiently compute all the coefficients

c^{(i)}_{k}

(i=0,1,\ldots,n;\,k=0,1,\ldots,n)
such that

F^{n}_{i}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c^{(i)}_{k}(t+1)^{k}\qquad(0\leq i\leq n)

(cf. Theorem
4.3
) with
O(n^{2})
computational complexity.

For simplicity, let us suppose that
d=1
(for higher dimensions of the curve,
the computations can be done component-wise with the same approach). Set

p_{n}(t):=\sum_{i=0}^{n}w_{i}F^{n}_{i}(t)\qquad(w_{0},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\mathbb{R}).

(5.4)

We have

p_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q_{k}(t+1)^{k},\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad q_{k}:=\sum_{i=0}^{n}w_{i}c^{(i)}_{k}.

Then all the coefficients
q_{k}

(0\leq k\leq n)
are independent of
t
and
they can be computed in
O(n^{2})
time. Next, all the values

p_{n}(t_{0}),p_{n}(t_{1}),\ldots,p_{n}(t_{M})

(5.5)

can be evaluated using Horner’s scheme
M+1
times with
O(Mn)
total
computational complexity.

A similar improvement is possible if we prefer the representations given in
Theorem
4.6
or Theorem
4.7
.
For example, as observed above, we can determine all the coefficients

g^{(i)}_{k}

(i=0,1,\ldots,n;\,k=0,1,\ldots,n-2)
such that

\displaystyle F^{n}_{0}(t)=\frac{1-t}{2}+\frac{t^{2}-1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}g^{(0)}_{k}P_{k}^{(1)}(t),

\displaystyle F^{n}_{i}(t)=\frac{t^{2}-1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}g^{(i)}_{k}P_{k}^{(1)}(t)\qquad(1\leq i\leq n-1),

\displaystyle F^{n}_{n}(t)=\frac{1+t}{2}+\frac{t^{2}-1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}g^{(n)}_{k}P_{k}^{(1)}(t)

in
O(n^{2})
time. Thus, we have

\displaystyle p_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-t}{2}w_{0}+\frac{1+t}{2}w_{n}+\frac{t^{2}-1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{n}w_{i}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}g^{(i)}_{k}P^{(1)}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-t}{2}w_{0}+\frac{1+t}{2}w_{n}+\frac{t^{2}-1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}s_{k}P^{(1)}_{k}(t)

(cf. (
5.4
)), where

s_{k}:=\sum_{i=0}^{n}w_{i}g^{(i)}_{k}.

The coefficients
s_{k}

(0\leq k\leq n)
are independent of
t
and they can be
found with a computational complexity of
O(n^{2})
. After that, all the
values (
5.5
) can be evaluated using the Clenshaw algorithm

M+1
times in
O(Mn)
time in total.

Remark 5.2
.

Note that the methods for the fast multipoint evaluation of GL curves
described above can be further optimized using the symmetries (
3.4
).
For example, since (
5.1
) holds, it suffices to compute only the
first half of the coefficients
g^{(i)}_{k}
with respect to
i

(cf. Remark
5.1
). Certainly, similar optimizations are possible for
shifted power and Legendre bases.

Numerical experiments

We have tested the multipoint GL curve evaluation strategies
(cf. §
5.3
) based on the shifted power
(Theorem
4.3
), Legendre (Theorem
4.6
),
and symmetric Jacobi (Theorem
4.7
) representations,
employing the symmetries (
3.4
) (cf. Remark
5.2
). We have
compared them with the direct evaluation of the formulas given
in
[
7
]
(cf. the integral representations (
3.1
),
(

)).

All evaluations were done using
Python 3.12
with the

NumPy 2.4.4
library.
We have used the double (
flot64
) precision. To evaluate the

3.2
), we used the
SciPy 1.17.1
function

quad
. The roots of Legendre polynomials were obtained using the

roots_legendre
method from the
SciPy
library.

https://github.com/filipchudy/gauss-legendre-curve-evaluation
.

. We have observed that the results using the explicit integral

), (

) and

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we have given new representations of Gauss–Legendre
polynomials and their derivatives in the shifted power basis and in some
orthogonal bases closely related to Jacobi polynomials. We also have proposed
efficient methods for evaluating Gauss–Legendre curves, including multipoint
evaluation. The numerical tests demonstrate that the new algorithms are stable
and computationally efficient, even for high-degree curves when orthogonal
expansions are used.

Note that we are currently conducting research on the connection between the
Bernstein and Gauss–Legendre bases, as well as on the construction of the dual
Gauss–Legendre basis, which may be useful in many applications, for example in
approximation problems. We intend to publish these results in the near future.
