Optimized convolutional neural network - kernel ridge regression-error correction method: an advanced model for predicting soil saturated hydraulic conductivity
Authors: Parisa Kahkhamoghadam, Mohammad Mahdi Chari, Mohammad ehteram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Predicting soil saturated hydraulic conductivity (Ks) is essential for understanding water movement in soils and designing efficient irrigation and drainage systems. Thus, our paper proposes the mutated grasshopper optimization (MGRO) algorithm- convolutional neural network (CNN)- kernel ridge regression (KRR)-error correction (ERC) method to predict Ks. The MGRO-CNN-KRR-ERC (MCKE) model is executed in several steps. First, the MGRO algorithm optimizes the hyperparameters of the CNN and KRR techniques. Then, the CNN technique is trained on the input data to produce a set of outputs called the summary vector, which represents the compact and meaningful descriptor of the original dataset for subsequent KRR processing. Next, the summary vector is fed into KRR to produce predicted Ks data. Finally, the ERC method reduces the difference between these data and measured Ks values to improve the overall prediction accuracy. The model uses four soil properties as input data. Our study uses the mutual information index to select the most informative features, and the results indicate that all four properties significantly affect the precision of predictions. The current research also utilizes the kernel estimation method to determine the uncertainty of the predicted Ks values. The study results demonstrate that the MCKE model significantly outperforms KRR, CNN, and hybrid approaches in terms of predictive accuracy, error reduction, and robustness. Specifically, the MCKE model achieves a reduction of 51–93% in mean absolute percentage error (MAPE to all other predictive models. In general, the MCKE model provides a robust and reliable framework for predicting Ks under diverse environmental conditions.

Introduction

Soil saturated hydraulic conductivity (KS) is a fundamental soil physical property that quantifies the ability of soil to transmit water under fully saturated conditions (e.g., when soil pores are completely filled with water)1. This property plays a key role in regulating water retention and aeration in the root zone2. It also serves as a critical factor in determining irrigation efficiency, which directly influences the uniformity of water distribution in the field3.

In recent years, KS has received increasing attention, and numerous studies have focused on predicting it. Predicting KS is essential for evaluating soil permeability and designing efficient irrigation and drainage systems2. It is also needed for accurately simulating solute transport and the movement of nutrients and contaminants in soil profiles under saturated conditions3–5.

Thus, various tools such as machine learning (MAL) models have been developed to predict KS.

MAL models can effectively model nonlinear relationships between soil properties, climate factors, and flows of water under both natural and managed conditions6. They can also uncover hidden patterns and nonlinear and handle complexity of the data7,8.

MAL models can incorporate different types of data, such as soil physical properties (e.g., texture, porosity, and bulk density), which are needed for the accurate prediction of KS9. They also reduce the dependency on extensive field measurements by learning patterns directly from available datasets6.

In recent years, scholars have proposed various MAL models to predict Ks. For example, Jain et al.10 predicted Ks using the random forest (RF) and ANN models. It was found that the ANN model achieved an R² of 0.83 and produced more accurate outputs compared to the RF model. However, these two models are sensitive to hyperparameter settings and may suffer from overfitting when applied to heterogeneous datasets.

Albalasmeh et al.11 proposed the generalized regression neural network (GRNN) to predict Ks. It was found that the GRNN model achieved different prediction accuracies with various input combinations. However, the GRNN model was not able to effectively handle high-dimensional data and complex nonlinear interactions among soil properties.

Moosavi et al.12 proposed the particle swarm optimization (PSO) algorithm-artificial neural network (ANN) to predict Ks. The study results demonstrated that the PSO-ANN model reduced the root mean square error (RMSE) of the genetic algorithm-ANN by 15%. However, these models may experience premature convergence and local optima issues during the optimization process.

However, previous models such as numerical models, empirical techniques, ANN, and regression approaches have different drawbacks. For example, classical regression approaches are generally unable to capture strong nonlinear relationships between soil properties and Ks. The empirical and numerical models may not adequately capture temporal and spatial variability in hydraulic conductivity. The ANN technique may struggle to extract long-range dependencies from highly heterogeneous soil systems12,13.

The existing Ks prediction models have disadvantages such as weak nonlinear fitting ability, large systematic errors, and difficulty in practical deployment. These models also suffer from limited generalization capability when applied to diverse environmental conditions and heterogeneous soil datasets.

Therefore, the main motivation of the current paper is to propose a Ks prediction model that addresses these drawbacks and can accurately predict soil saturated hydraulic conductivity.

In recent years, data scientists have proposed other types of MAL models, such as the kernel ridge regression (KRR) technique. The KRR technique combines the advantages of ridge regression and kernel methods to model complex nonlinear relationships in time series and hydrological processes14,15.

In recent years, scholars have utilized the KRR technique to forecast different variables. For example, Ali et al.16 combined a decomposition algorithm with KRR to forecast rainfall. It was found that the hybrid technique reduced the mean absolute error (MAE) of the KRR by 59%.

Ahmed et al.17 developed CEEMDAN- grey wolf optimization (GWO)- KRR technique to predict wheat yield. The study results showed that the proposed CEEMDAN- GWO-KRR approach provided reliable estimates and reduced prediction errors of the KRR by 20%. However, the performance of this approach was influenced by different factors such as the size and diversity of the training dataset.

Deo et al.18 used the KRR technique to monitor solar energy. Their findings indicated that KRR produced higher accuracy compared to simple regression method. However, this technique was not able to effectively handle highly noisy input data under rapidly changing atmospheric conditions.

Kordani et al.19 used different types of the KRR technique to forecast water quality index. It was found that the KRR-based models achieved high prediction accuracy and strong generalization performance. Specifically, the regularized locally weighted (GRKR) method reduced the MAPE of the LSSVM model by 1.3%.

Ahmadianfar et al.20 combined the singular value decomposition (SVD) algorithm with the KRR technique to forecast water levels. The study results showed that the combined approach reduced the MAPE of the KRR method by 68%.

However, hydrologists need more accurate predictions of KS to develop sustainable land and water management strategies under changing climate conditions. Moreover, these predictions are vital for ensuring sustainable crop production and minimizing soil erosion13. Thus, it is essential to enhance the predictive performance of the KRR technique by combining it with the advanced tools such as deep learning techniques.

Deep learning techniques are advanced computational methods for simulating complex systems21. These techniques can capture complex nonlinear relationships in large and high-dimensional datasets22,23. They also offer significant improvements in modeling dynamic processes in soil profiles24.

Deep leaning techniques includes different variants such as the convolutional neural network (CNN). The CNN technique improves the ability of the machine learning models to extract nonlinear features (e.g. structural dependencies and multiscale patterns) from input data24. This enhancement is necessary as it helps these models to obtain insights into spatial and temporal variability in soil properties under different climate and land-use conditions, ultimately leading to more accurate predictions of Ks24.

In recent years, the CNN technique has been widely combined with different machine learning models to create advanced predictive frameworks for forecasting various environmental variables and ecosystem indicators. For example, Ehteram et al.21 coupled the CNN technique with a decision tree (DT) model to forecast water quality index. They reported that the CNN-DT model provided more reliable predictions compared DT. Ehteram et al.24 integrated the CNN technique with the support vector model to forecast rainfall in a large basin. Their findings indicated that the CNN-SVM model achieved higher prediction accuracy compared to traditional SVM and standalone CNN models.

Thus, our study combines the CNN technique with KRR to improve its predictive performance. The resulting hybrid system, called CNN-KRR, is then utilized to predict Ks. However, to further enhance the performance of KRR, the CNN-KRR framework can be combined with tools such as error correction (ERC) method. The ERC method minimizes the difference between predicted and observed outputs25. This minimization is essential as it helps the prediction model reduce systematic errors and improve overall prediction accuracy.

Recently, developers have widely coupled ERC with hybrid prediction approaches to improve their precision. For example, Ye et al.25 combined the error correction method with the gradient boosting tree (GBT) to forecast wind speed. It was found that the ERC-GBT model achieved more reliable outputs compared to standalone GBT. Xu et al.26 developed the ERC-artificial neural network (ANN) model to forecast runoff. It was found that the ERC-ANN model significantly improved runoff prediction accuracy.

Thus, our study combines the ERC method with the CNN-KRR framework to develop the hybrid CNN-KRR-ERC (CKE) model for predicting KS. The model effectively reduces systematic prediction errors and provides reliable predictions of KS. It can also serve as an analytical tool for understanding spatial and temporal variability of water flow in heterogeneous soils.

However, the CKE model includes CNN and KRR parameters, which should be effectively adjusted to achieve optimal prediction accuracy and generalization performance. Recently, modelers have developed various tools, such as optimization techniques, to effectively tune the parameters of hybrid models. These techniques systematically adjust parameters of prediction models, allowing them to achieve optimal predictive performance27. They also improve the adaptability of the hybrid model to different soil types and environmental conditions28.

Optimization algorithms includes different types such as grasshopper optimization (GRO) technique, which is inspired by the natural behavior of grasshoppers in nature. The GRO technique adjusts model parameters by systematically exploring the parameter space28. It also offers several advantages such as the flexibility to handle both continuous and discrete variables27.

However, to more effectively set the parameters of the prediction models, the effectiveness of the GRO technique should be enhanced by combining it with advanced tools such as the mutation operator. The mutation operator, which is an optimization strategy, alters the location of a selected grasshopper, which serves as a search agent within the search space. This alteration is essential as it enables GRO to diversify the search paths, leading to the discovery of high-quality solutions. The mutation operator imrpoves the search capability of the algorithm by introducing random variations into the population. It also improves the balance between exploration and exploitation, allowing the algorithm to search both locally and globally within the solution space.

Thus, our study combines the mutation operator with the GRO technique to develop a new optimization algorithm called the mutated GRO (MGRO) technique. The MGRO technique offers enhanced global search capabilities, enabling it to explore a wider solution space. It can also handle multi-objective optimization problems, allowing simultaneous optimization of competing objectives such as accuracy, computational cost, and generalization performance.

Thus, our research combines the MGRO technique with CNN-KRR-ERC to properly set its parameters. The resulting hybrid approach, called MGRO-CNN-KRR-ERC (MCKE), is then used to predict KS.

Thus, the main novelty of the paper is the development of the MCKE model. The model serves as an effective framework for evaluating the influence of soil texture, porosity, and environmental factors on water movement. It also offers insights into soil hydraulic behavior under varying climate conditions. These insights are essential for supporting sustainable agricultural management and optimizing soil and water conservation practices.

The MCKE model produce reliable outputs under variable environmental conditions by updating its predictions based on incoming weather and climate information, such as rainfall, temperature, and evapotranspiration. It can also incorporate irrigation and water management schedules, enabling the accurate simulation of dynamic soil–water interactions.

The MCKE model consists of multiple components, which enable it to capture nonlinear relationships, correct prediction errors, and optimize model parameters simultaneously. Without using CNN, the model fails to effectively represent the complex nonlinear relationships in soil hydraulic processes. The lack of error correction method leads to the persistence of systematic biases in the predicted Ks values.

While recent studies, including Zhang et al.29 and Zhang et al.25, have developed various hybrid models for predicting different target variables, the proposed MCKE model introduces a comprehensive framework that simultaneously improves feature extraction, nonlinear mapping, and error minimization in soil saturated hydraulic conductivity prediction. Compared to existing hybrid prediction models, the MCKE model significantly improves the representation of multiscale soil characteristics, leading to more reliable estimations of saturated hydraulic conductivity.

Unlike existing hybrid prediction models, which may suffer from high bias and limited generalization, the MCKE model reduces systematic errors through an integrated error correction mechanism. The MCKE model also provides improved generalization performance across different soil types and environmental conditions due to its hybrid learning structure.

CNN technique

The CNN technique is a deep learning model that has gained significant attention in recent years. This technique offers different advantages such as the ability to automatically extract features from complex datasets21. It also effectively handles heterogeneous data and complex structured data such as video frames, audio spectrograms, and biomedical signals24.

KRR technique

The KRR technique is the predictive engine of the MCKE model. This technique is an extension of linear ridge regression that can effectively address nonlinear problems19. It can also effectively process heterogeneous data with nonuniform distributions, enabling robust modeling Ks under diverse environmental conditions.

Determination of the most input variables

Our study predicts KS using soil bulk density (SBD) and the percentages of clay (CL), sand (SA), and silt (SI) in the soil profile. These four physical properties are selected because of their significant influence on soil texture, which directly affects the soil’s ability to transmit water under saturated conditions. By including these properties, the MCKE model can accurately capture the variability in soil hydraulic behavior, which governs soil–water interactions and determines how water moves in the soil profile.

However, these four properties produce 24−1 input combinations, which can increase the computational complexity of the prediction model. These input combinations may also include extreme or uncommon soil property values, which can negatively affect model stability and prediction accuracy.

Thus, to address these challenges, it is essential to choose the most important input variables. These variables are defined as soil physical properties that have a significant effect on KS. They also contain critical information about the structure, texture, and porosity of soil, which directly influence its interactions with water.

By quantifying the uncertainty, researchers can provide insights into the sensitivity of the model to input variability and assumptions. The KDE technique is an estimation method for approximating the probability density function of a random variable39. This technique can be applied to large and heterogeneous datasets, which are commonly used for predicting Ks. It also provides probabilistic insights into model predictions, which are essential for understanding the likelihood of different outcomes40.

Case study

Sistan and Balochistan Province

Sistan and Balochistan Province, located in the southeastern region of Iran, is characterized by vast geographical diversity, making it a key area for the cultivation of tropical and subtropical fruits. This province covers an area of 182,000 km² and is influenced by an arid to semi-arid climate. It also includes several seasonal rivers and ephemeral streams that flow primarily during short and intense rainfall events.

In recent years, the province has experienced increasing water scarcity and irregular rainfall patterns, which have posed significant challenges for food security and water resource management. These challenges include fluctuations in food prices and conflicts over water allocation between agricultural and urban areas.

Therefore, to mitigate these challenges, provincial authorities have developed various strategies such as the implementation of modern irrigation systems, water recycling programs, and rainwater harvesting techniques. They have also emphasized the importance of understanding key soil hydraulic properties, such as KS, which can significantly influence the efficiency of irrigation systems and the overall management of water resources in agricultural fields. Data set

125 samples are collected from various agricultural fields across Sistan and Balochistan Province to produce the input and output datasets. The input dataset comprises bulk density (SBD) and the percentages of clay (CL), sand (SA), and silt (SI) in the soil profile while the output dataset includes Ks values.

Table 2 shows the statistical details of these two data sets. From this table, it is observed that the difference between maximum and minimum value of KS is 61.2 (mm/hr), which is significant. This significant difference indicates a considerable variability in Ks across the sampled sites. It also can be attributed to differences in soil texture, structure, and porosity across the study area. Soils with higher sand content and lower bulk density have larger pore spaces and higher Ks values. In contrast, soils with higher clay content and more compacted structures have smaller pores, slower infiltration rates, and lower KS values. Additionally, variations in silt content and land management practices, such as tillage or compaction, further contribute to the observed heterogeneity. To determine the percentages of sand, silt, and clay, soil samples were first collected from multiple locations within the study area. The samples were then sent to a certified soil testing laboratory in the province. In the laboratory, particle size distribution was measured using the hydrometer method, and Ks was determined using standard permeameter tests, including the constant-head method for coarse-textured soils and the falling-head method for fine-textured soils. In the falling-head test, the decline in water head was recorded to calculate Ks. In the constant-head test, a constant hydraulic head difference was maintained across the soil sample, and the steady-state flow rate of water was measured to determine Ks.

The soil samples (n = 125) were collected from agricultural fields located in the Sistan region with the permission of the respective landowners and in accordance with local regulations for soil sampling and field research. No protected areas were involved in the sampling process.

Discussion and results

Determining data size for training models

The size of the training dataset is an important parameter that directly impacts the performance of perdition models. A smaller dataset limits the ability of the prediction models to learn spatial and temporal variations in soil behavior, leading to inaccurate predictions of Ks. Conversely, a larger dataset improves the model’s ability to learn more comprehensive and complex patterns. However, it also increases the computational burden and complicates the training process. Thus, it is necessary to determine the optimal size of the training dataset to achieve an optimal balance between accuracy and computational efficiency.

To address this necessity, our study first changes the size of the training dataset from 50% to 90% of the total dataset. Then, the impact of each size on the MAPE of the MCKE model is carefully evaluated to determine how the volume of training data affects predictive accuracy. It is important to note that an ideal size generates the lowest MAPE value.

Figure 3 shows how MAPE values of the model change with different sizes of the training and testing datasets. It is observed that the lowest MAPE values are obtained when the training and testing datasets consist of 70% and 30% of the soil samples, respectively. Therefore, this study uses these two ratios for constructing the training and testing datasets.

Evaluating the precision of prediction models using evaluation metrics

To provide deep insights into the performance of the MCEK model and other predictive models, the results of this section are presented based on six comparisons of models. First, KRR is benchmarked against CNN to determine which model better captures nonlinear relationships in the dataset and provides higher predictive accuracy. Then, the MGRO-KRR model is compared with KRR to demonstrate how MGRO enhances the predictive performance of the KRR model. In the next step, the MGRO-CNN model is compared with the GRO-CNN framework to demonstrate the superiority of MGRO over GRO. Subsequently, the CNN-KRR model is compared with KRR to demonstrate how CNN improves the modeling capability of KRR. Next, the KRR-ERC model is benchmarked against KRR to demonstrate how the ERC method reduces systematic prediction errors and improves overall model accuracy. Finally, the MCKE model is compared with all other forecasting models to demonstrate its superiority in terms of predictive accuracy, error reduction, and generalization capability. These comparisons are described as follows:

CNN vs. KRR: In Table 4, evaluation metrics of all prediction models are presented. The CNN technique reduces the MAP value of KRR by 1.8%. This technique also shows an improvement of 4.1% in WI and an enhancement of 7.4% in LMI compared to KRR. These results show that CNNs provide higher prediction accuracy compared to KRR. This higher accuracy can be attributed to the ability of CNNs to extract nonlinear and multiscale features from input data.

MGRO-KRR vs. KRR: The MGRO-KRR model shows an enhancement of 10% in KGE and an improvement of 23% in MAPE compared to KRR. These improvements highlight the effectiveness of the MGRO optimization strategy in systematically tuning the internal parameters of the KRR model.

Notably, the parameters of the KRR model are manually adjusted. However, this manual adjustment has led to its suboptimal performance and reduced predictive reliability.

MGRO-CNN vs. CNN-GRO: The MGRO-CNN improves LMI and KGE values of the GRO-CNN by 2.5 and 3.8%, respectively. The primary reason for this improvement is the inclusion of the mutation operator in the MGRO algorithm, which enhances its global search capability. MGRO explores a wide hyperparameter space, allowing it to identify the most effective combinations of CNN parameters.

CNN-KRR vs. KRR: The CNN-KRR achieves an improvement of 17%% in MPAE and an improvement of 6.9% in KGE compared to KRR. These improvements highlight the effectiveness of combining deep learning with kernel-based regression techniques.

Unlike the KRR model, which does not account for spatial patterns in the input data, the CNN-KRR model extracts spatial and structural features from the soil dataset. The CNN-KRR model also provides a robust framework for integrating heterogeneous soil and environmental data.

KRR-ERC vs. KRR: The KRR-ERC model decreases the MAPE value of the KRR by 10%. This reduction demonstrates the effectiveness of the ERC method in minimizing systematic prediction errors. The ERC method improves the predictive capability of KRR by identifying systematic deviations between predicted and observed values and applying corrective adjustments iteratively.

MCKE vs. All other prediction models: The MCKE model achieves an improvement of 4–29% in KGE and an improvement of 4.−30% in LMI compared to all other models. The model also reduces the MAPE values of all other prediction models by 51–93%.

A comparison between this research and previous studies

To demonstrate how the MCKE model addresses the limitations of previous studies, these studies should be investigated and compared with the current work. This investigation and comparison are conducted as follows:

Saha et al.6 predicted Ks using the support vector machine and MLR models. Their analysis showed that the SVM model outperformed MLR. However, the SVM model produced predictions that contained systematic deviations.

In contrast, the current study develops the MCEK model, which uses the ERC method to identify and correct systematic errors in the initial predictions. The ERC method significantly enhances model accuracy and optimizes predictive performance.

Albalasmeh et al.11 developed different types of the generalized regression neural networks (GRNNs) to predict Ks. They reported that the GRRNN models produced accurate predictions of Ks. However, their model was not able to effectively extract spatial patterns and structural relationships from soil datasets.

In contrast, the current paper develops the MCKE model, which effectively captures nonlinear spatial features and structural patterns in the input data set using the CNN component.

Mozaffari et al.13 proposed the ANN model to estimate Ks. The study results showed that their ANN model achieved good predictive accuracy. However, the model used a backpropagation algorithm, which could not properly set ANN parameters.

In contrast, our study develops the MCKE model, which uses the MGRO algorithm to set CNN and KRR parameters optimally. Using a mutation operator, the algorithm diversifies the search trajectories, leading to the discovery of the most effective combination of CNN and KRR parameters for the accurate prediction of Ks.

Mouaddine et al.2 used the random forest (RF) model to map Ks. The study results showed that the RF model showed high predictive capability. However, RF was not able to effectively capture spatial heterogeneity in soil properties across complex landscapes.

In contrast, our study uses the MCKE model, which uses CNN to extract multi-scale spatial features from soil data. The CNN method enables automatic feature extraction from complex soil datasets.

Capabilities and restrictions of the MCKE model

Our article suggests the MCKE model to estimate Ks. According to the results, the MCKE model demonstrates higher accuracy compared to other models, including KRR, CNN, and hybrid approaches. The MCEK model can produce spatial maps of KS, allowing farmers to match crop types with soil water availability. The model can also provide insights into areas susceptible to water stress or excess moisture, enabling the selection of crops whose water requirements align with local soil hydraulic conditions.

The MCEK model provides a tool for research on soil–water interactions, which are important for designing sustainable water management practices, including drainage, infiltration control, and soil conservation measures. It can also provide a deep scientific basis for formulating climate-resilient agricultural practices that maintain soil health and improve long-term sustainability of farming systems.

However, the MCEK model may limitations, which should be carefully addressed. For example, the fluctuations in the input time series may significantly compromise its predictive accuracy. However, future studies can address this limitation by employing decomposition techniques. These techniques can convert time series into subseries with lower variability, allowing modelers to train the model on simpler sequences.

Conclusion

Predicting Ks is crucial for effective water management in agriculture, as it influences irrigation strategies and crop yield optimization. Thus, our article proposes the MCEK model to predict Ks. The model consists of multiple components, which operate in several steps. First, the MGRO algorithm is employed to optimally tune the parameters of the CNN and KRR models. Then, the CNN technique is trained on the dataset to produce the summary vector. Next, this vector is incorporated into the KRR component to produce predicted Ks data. Finally, the ERC method reduces the difference between predicted and observed Ks data to achieve more accurate predictions.

Our results demonstrate that the MCEK model outperforms traditional models, such as KRR and CNN, as well as hybrid models, in terms of predictive accuracy and generalization. Specifically, the model achieves a reduction of 51–93% in MAPE and an improvement of 5–12% R2 compared to all other predictive models. The model also demonstrates high adaptability to datasets with varying characteristics, making it suitable for predicting Ks under diverse environmental conditions.

The study results show that the MGRO, CNN, and ERC techniques the overall predictive performance of the MCEK model. These techniques also ensure that the model can provide accurate predictions even under changing environmental conditions, such as fluctuating moisture levels or extreme weather events.

However, the next studies can improve the accuracy of Ks predictions by employing additional soil properties, such as soil organic matter or microbial activity, which may further influence water movement and retention. Moreover, these studies can use meteorological parameters, such as precipitation patterns, temperature fluctuations, and humidity levels, to enhance the model’s responsiveness to changing environmental conditions.
