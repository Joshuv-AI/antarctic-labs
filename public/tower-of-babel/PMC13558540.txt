Introduction

The periorbital and perioral regions are among the most challenging areas in facial rejuvenation owing to their thin dermal architecture, high mobility, and constant exposure to dynamic muscular activity. Fine wrinkles in these regions tend to develop early and recur rapidly, while treatment options remain limited. High-G′ hyaluronic acid (HA) dermal fillers, although effective for volumization in deeper planes, carry an increased risk of palpable irregularities and the Tyndall effect when injected superficially in thin and highly dynamic areas, leading clinicians and patients to exercise caution or avoid filler-based approaches altogether [1, 2].

To overcome these limitations, intradermal HA-based skin boosters have gained popularity. Skin boosters are broadly defined as microinjected biological agents—predominantly non-cross-linked or low-cross-linked hyaluronic acid—administered directly into the superficial or mid-dermis. Unlike conventional dermal fillers intended for mechanical volumization and structural projection, skin boosters are specifically designed to improve the dermal microenvironment by restoring hydration, enhancing elasticity, and refining overall skin quality [3–5]. Non-cross-linked HA formulations can enhance hydration and skin texture; however, their clinical effects are often short-lived due to rapid enzymatic degradation and limited intradermal persistence, particularly in regions subjected to continuous mechanical stress [6, 7].

The clinical behavior of hyaluronic acid (HA) injectables in thin, highly dynamic areas is influenced not only by the degree of cross-linking but also by rheological properties, including elastic modulus (G′), cohesivity, viscosity, and water affinity. Fillers with high elastic modulus (G′) are designed to provide structural projection in deeper planes, but their rigidity may increase the risk of palpability, contour irregularities, or visible product when placed superficially. Low-G′ HA formulations with favorable cohesivity, low complex viscosity, and high water affinity were developed to address this therapeutic gap by allowing uniform intradermal integration and localized hydration without exerting excessive volumetric effects. This strategy may provide subtle structural support for fine wrinkles while minimizing the risks associated with fillers with high elastic modulus (G′) [8].

Beyond their rheological properties, intradermal HA injections have been reported to influence dermal hydration, viscoelasticity, and fibroblast-mediated remodeling processes, contributing to improvements in overall skin quality [9]. However, objective, instrument-based evidence demonstrating durable structural and functional skin changes following low-G′ HA treatment—especially in thin and highly dynamic regions such as the periorbital and perioral areas—remains limited.

Most existing studies on skin boosters emphasize short-term outcomes, global facial treatment, or subjective assessments, with relatively few incorporating quantitative biophysical or imaging-based evaluations of dermal remodeling. Moreover, region-specific responses in anatomically delicate facial areas have not been adequately characterized, underscoring the need for focused, objective investigation.

Accordingly, this observational study evaluated the efficacy, durability, and safety of a low-G′ hyaluronic acid formulation administered using an intradermal microdepot injection protocol for facial rejuvenation. Changes in periorbital and perioral wrinkle severity, global aesthetic improvement, and objective skin quality parameters were assessed over a 6-month follow-up period. High-frequency ultrasound imaging was additionally used to evaluate changes in dermal thickness and echogenicity as surrogate markers of dermal remodeling.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Public Institutional Bioethics Committee designated by the Ministry of Health and Welfare, Republic of Korea (IRB no. P01-202607-01-014). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All data were handled in a deidentified manner to ensure patient confidentiality. Written informed consent for publication of clinical data and images was obtained from all participants. Participants were informed that anonymized data and images may be published in scientific journals. The study was designed as a single-center, single-arm observational study.

Participants were consecutively recruited from patients who visited the clinic between November 2025 and March 2026 and voluntarily sought treatment for facial wrinkles and overall skin quality improvement. Inclusion criteria required visible periorbital and perioral wrinkles corresponding to a Wrinkle Severity Rating Scale (WSRS) score ≥ 2, willingness to undergo three treatment sessions at 4-week intervals, and compliance with all follow-up visits through 6 months.

Key exclusion criteria included active dermatologic disease or infection in the treatment area, a history of injectable treatments or energy-based devices applied to the treatment zone within the preceding 6 months, known hypersensitivity to hyaluronic acid or lidocaine, autoimmune or immunosuppressive conditions, pregnancy or breastfeeding, and any condition deemed by the investigator to potentially confound study outcomes or compromise subject safety.

Materials

The formulation used in this study was BYRYZN® SKINBOOSTER HA (ACROSS Co., Ltd., Gangwon-do, Republic of Korea), a low-G′ hyaluronic acid injectable gel intended for intradermal microdepot use. This formulation was selected because its rheological properties, characterized by a low elastic modulus, low-crosslinking density, and high-water affinity, are suited for application in thin and dynamic facial regions. The formulation consisted of monophasic hyaluronic acid (20 mg/mL), cross-linked with 1,4-butanediol diglycidyl ether (BDDE), lidocaine hydrochloride (3 mg/mL, 0.3%), and phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.0). Rheologically, this formulation exhibits a low elastic modulus (G′) of 3 Pa, a viscous modulus (G″) of 2.96 Pa, and a phase angle of 44.6°. Furthermore, it is characterized as a cohesive, low-cross-linked monophasic gel with a microparticle profile suitable for intradermal microdepot injection. Notably, analytical data confirm the purity of the formulation, with unreacted BDDE being nondetectable and free 1,4-butanediol bis(2,3-dihydroxypropyl) ether (BDPE), a hydrolysis byproduct of BDDE, measured at 0.4 ppm [10]. These specific physicochemical properties support uniform intradermal distribution and optimal tissue integration while minimizing the risk of palpability in highly dynamic areas. The product was supplied in a 1.1-mL prefilled syringe with a 33-gauge, 4-mm needle.

Treatment Protocol

Prior to treatment, topical anesthesia was achieved with an application of EMLA cream (lidocaine 2.5% and prilocaine 2.5%) for 30–45 min. The skin was disinfected with povidone–iodine solution followed by normal saline.

A fixed total volume of 5.0 mL was administered per session using a multiple microinjection technique. Each injection point delivered 0.03 mL of product at approximately 0.5–0.7-cm intervals, targeting the mid-to-deep dermis across the full face. The total number of injection points per session ranged from approximately 150 to 200, depending on individual facial anatomy and treatment distribution.

Following injection, ice compresses were applied for 5–10 min. Subjects were instructed to avoid strong pressure or massage of the treated areas on the day of treatment. Concomitant injectable or energy-based procedures in the treatment zones were prohibited throughout the study period.

Treatments were administered in three sessions at week 0 (baseline), week 4, and week 8, with follow-up assessments conducted at months 3 and 6 post-baseline (weeks 12 and 24). Although conventional cross-linked HA fillers are commonly administered as a single-session volumizing treatment, the present low-G′ formulation was administered using a microdepot technique targeting the mid-to-deep dermis to improve skin quality and correct fine wrinkles. In this approach, repeated small-volume injections are intended to promote restoration of a hydrated extracellular matrix, facilitate uniform dermal integration, and provide sustained mechanical stimulation without excessive bolus volume in thin, dynamic facial regions. The 4-week interval was selected to allow early tissue response and resolution of transient injection reactions between sessions, while maintaining cumulative intradermal stimulation. This protocol is consistent with previous intradermal HA skin rejuvenation approaches using repeated treatment sessions at regular intervals [3, 4].

Evaluation

The primary endpoints were changes in Wrinkle Severity Rating Scale (WSRS) scores for periorbital and perioral wrinkles, assessed by a blinded investigator using a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (absent) to 5 (extreme) [11], and Global Aesthetic Improvement Scale (GAIS) scores, assessed by both the investigator and the subjects using a 5-point scale ranging from 0 (worse) to 4 (very much improved) [12].

Secondary outcomes comprised assessments of overall facial skin quality, with objective ultrasound measurements performed at the cheek area at baseline and at months 3 and 6.

Safety Assessment

Safety was evaluated throughout the study by monitoring local and systemic adverse events, including bruising, edema, nodules, hypersensitivity reactions, and injection-site discomfort. Adverse events were recorded with respect to severity, onset, duration, and relationship to treatment.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Changes in outcome measures over time were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as appropriate. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study Population

This study evaluated the efficacy, durability, and safety of a low-G′ hyaluronic acid (HA) formulation in subjects who sought improvement in periorbital and perioral wrinkles as well as overall facial skin quality. Adult subjects aged ≥ 20 years with visible periorbital and perioral wrinkles (Wrinkle Severity Rating Scale ≥ 2) were enrolled.

A total of 25 subjects were included at baseline. Three subjects were lost to follow-up, leaving 22 subjects in the final analysis. The study population comprised 18 women (81.8%) and 4 men (18.2%), with a mean age of 47.5 ± 7.2 years (range, 35–60 years). Participants underwent three full-face intradermal treatment sessions at weeks 0, 4, and 8, followed by outcome assessments at weeks 12 and 24.

Primary Endpoints

Wrinkle Severity Rating Scale (WSRS)

Periorbital wrinkles demonstrated a significant and progressive reduction in WSRS scores over time, with a marked early response. The mean WSRS score improved from 3.06 ± 0.51 at baseline to 2.08 ± 0.50 at week 12 and reached 1.79 ± 0.45 at week 24 (RM-ANOVA, p < 0.001). The mean absolute reduction from baseline at week 24 was −1.27 points.

Perioral wrinkles showed a more gradual but sustained pattern of improvement.

Mean WSRS scores decreased from 3.18 ± 0.49 at baseline to 2.42 ± 0.47 at week 12, with further improvement to 2.01 ± 0.46 at week 24 (RM-ANOVA, p < 0.001), corresponding to a mean reduction of −1.17 points at month 6. Secondary Endpoints

Dermal Thickness and Echogenicity

At baseline, mean dermal thickness in the periorbital region was 0.72 ± 0.09 mm, which increased to 0.79 ± 0.10 mm at week 12 and further to 0.84 ± 0.11 mm at week 24 (p < 0.001). Dermal echogenicity showed a parallel increase, rising from 65.4 ± 8.1 grayscale units at baseline to 72.8 ± 8.9 at week 12 and 78.6 ± 9.4 at week 24 (p < 0.001).

Baseline dermal thickness in the perioral region measured 1.05 ± 0.14 mm, increasing to 1.13 ± 0.15 mm at week 12 and 1.21 ± 0.16 mm at week 24 (p < 0.001). Mean dermal echogenicity increased from 84.1 ± 9.3 grayscale units at baseline to 92.6 ± 10.0 at week 12 and 99.4 ± 10.7 at week 24 (p < 0.001).

Across both anatomical regions, ultrasound measurements demonstrated progressive increases in dermal thickness and echogenicity from baseline through week 24, with greater absolute thickness values observed in the perioral region and comparable relative increases over time.

Safety

Treatment was well tolerated, and no serious adverse events were reported.

The most common adverse events were transient injection-site erythema, edema, and mild bruising. Injection-site erythema occurred in 45.5% (10/22) of subjects, edema in 36.4% (8/22), and mild bruising in 27.3% (6/22). All reported local adverse events were mild and resolved spontaneously within 5 days of treatment without intervention. No nodules, delayed hypersensitivity reactions, vascular complications, or other unexpected adverse events were observed during the study period.

Discussion

The periorbital and perioral regions possess anatomical characteristics that distinguish them from other facial areas and pose unique challenges for wrinkle treatment. Both regions are characterized by a relatively thin dermis, limited subcutaneous support, and continuous dynamic movement related to facial expression. These features predispose the skin to early formation of fine wrinkles and reduce tolerance to volumetric correction, particularly in the superficial planes.

The periorbital region is especially vulnerable owing to its thin skin and superficial vascular network. Bravo et al. described this area as a high-risk zone for filler-related complications, emphasizing that inappropriate product selection or excessive stiffness may result in contour irregularities, visible discoloration, or foreign-body sensation when hyaluronic-acid-based materials are used superficially [1]. These characteristics necessitate careful consideration of product rheology and precise intradermal injection depth when treating periorbital wrinkles.

The perioral region presents similar challenges, with wrinkle formation strongly influenced by repetitive muscle activity, intrinsic aging, and extrinsic factors such as smoking. Chien et al. demonstrated that perioral wrinkles exhibit distinct morphological patterns and progression compared with other facial wrinkles, reflecting the unique mechanical environment of the perioral skin [2]. Histologic analyses have further shown that the perioral dermis exhibits relatively reduced thickness and altered collagen organization, suggesting structural vulnerability that may limit the durability of treatments relying primarily on volumetric expansion [7]. Collectively, these anatomical and functional characteristics underscore the need for treatment strategies that improve fine wrinkles and skin quality without excessive volumization or rigidity.

Hyaluronic-acid-based injectable treatments have been widely used to address fine wrinkles and age-related changes in the facial regions. High-G′ cross-linked HA fillers have demonstrated efficacy in improving contour and wrinkle appearance, particularly in the perioral region and lips, when placed in deeper planes [9, 13]. However, because high-G′ fillers possess greater stiffness and projection capacity, they are generally intended for deeper tissue support rather than superficial intradermal placement. In thin and highly mobile areas such as the periorbital and perioral regions, these products are not ideal candidates for superficial wrinkle correction, as repetitive muscular movement and limited dermal thickness may increase the likelihood of palpability, contour irregularities, visible product, and an unnatural appearance [1, 2, 14].

To overcome these limitations, intradermal approaches using non-cross-linked HA and microfiller formulations have been introduced with the aim of improving fine wrinkles and skin quality rather than restoring volume. Clinical studies have shown that intradermal HA delivery can enhance hydration, elasticity, and surface texture, supporting its role as a skin-quality-oriented treatment [3, 5]. Similar intradermal HA-based strategies have been applied in other thin-skin regions such as the neck and décolletage, where minimally invasive HA injections have been reported to improve horizontal wrinkles with acceptable safety profiles [15–20]. For superficial fine lines in dynamic facial areas, softer HA formulations with lower elastic modulus are preferred because they can better accommodate tissue movement, integrate more uniformly within the dermis, and reduce the risk of visible or palpable irregularities [14, 21]. In highly dynamic facial regions, low-dose botulinum toxin is also commonly used in combination with HA fillers to improve outcomes by addressing the muscular component of wrinkle formation. By inhibiting acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction, botulinum toxin temporarily reduces repetitive muscle contraction and dynamic mechanical stress on the treated skin. This complements the hydrating and structural effects of HA, allowing combined treatment to address both dynamic wrinkle formation and superficial dermal changes in areas such as the periorbital and perioral regions [22, 23].

Despite these advantages, non-cross-linked HA formulations are prone to rapid enzymatic degradation, resulting in short-lived clinical effects and limited durability. In contrast, fillers with high elastic modulus (G′) provide greater projection and persistence but are often unsuitable for superficial dermal placement in periorbital and perioral skin because their rigidity may impair uniform intradermal integration and increase the risk of palpability, contour irregularities, or visible discoloration in thin, highly dynamic tissues. Low-G′ HA formulations were developed to bridge this therapeutic gap by maintaining high water affinity and sufficient intradermal residence while allowing favorable cohesivity, low complex viscosity, and tissue integration.

From a biological perspective, cross-linked HA has been shown to stimulate tissue repair and biostimulation through mechanical signaling, fibroblast activation, and induction of de novo collagen production [24, 25]. While alternative agents such as polynucleotides (PN) are specifically utilized for their regenerative properties—providing DNA precursors to promote physiological tissue repair at the cellular level [8]—intradermal HA exerts its clinical effects primarily through distinct physical and biostimulatory pathways. It rapidly promotes restoration of a hydrated extracellular matrix and provides immediate structural scaffolding. This scaffolding directly attenuates fine wrinkles while generating mechanical tension on local fibroblasts, thereby driving continuous skin quality improvement. Rheological properties, including elastic modulus (G′), cohesivity, viscosity, and water affinity, critically influence intradermal spread, tissue integration, hydration behavior, palpability, contour regularity, and the durability of wrinkle improvement of hyaluronic acid products [26]. Accordingly, low-G′ HA formulations represent a rational therapeutic option for fine wrinkles in thin, dynamic facial regions, where subtle structural support and improvement in skin quality are required without volumetric distortion associated with fillers with high elastic modulus (G′).

In the present study microdepot injection of a low-G′ HA formulation into the mid-to-deep dermis resulted in significant and sustained improvement in both periorbital and perioral wrinkles over a 6-month follow-up period. Periorbital wrinkles demonstrated a more pronounced early reduction in wrinkle severity, whereas perioral wrinkles exhibited a more gradual but continuously progressive improvement over time.

The high proportion of both investigator- and subject-rated improvement at week 24 further supports the clinical relevance of the observed wrinkle changes across regions. The differential temporal response between the two regions is biologically plausible. The periorbital dermis is thinner and more compact, with relatively limited subcutaneous support, allowing hydration-induced dermal expansion to translate more rapidly into visible wrinkle attenuation. In contrast, the perioral region is subject to repetitive multidirectional muscle contraction and higher mechanical strain, which may delay the clinical manifestation of wrinkle improvement despite ongoing dermal remodeling.

It should also be noted that the injection protocol was intentionally adjusted to account for regional anatomical vulnerability. While prior wrinkle-reduction studies conducted in less delicate facial regions, including the nasolabial folds and cheeks, have typically employed injection volumes of approximately 0.05 mL per injection point [3, 27], the present study adopted a fixed volume of 0.03 mL per injection point to facilitate more uniform distribution and to minimize the risk of palpability or contour irregularities in the thin, highly dynamic periorbital and perioral skin.

Beyond wrinkle severity, improvements in overall facial skin quality were observed across multiple objective skin quality parameters, including elasticity, hydration-related indices, surface texture, and barrier function. The progressive increase in elasticity (R2) suggests an improvement in the skin’s ability to deform and recover under mechanical stress, which may contribute to reduced wrinkle visibility during facial movement. Concurrent improvements in surface roughness and texture irregularity indicate a more uniform epidermal–dermal interface, reflecting attenuation of fine surface lines rather than deep volumetric changes.

Improvements in hydration-related optical indices, together with a sustained reduction in transepidermal water loss (TEWL), indicate enhanced epidermal water retention and barrier integrity over time. Given that elevated TEWL has been associated with impaired barrier function and accelerated wrinkle formation in aged and photodamaged skin, the observed TEWL reduction supports a functional improvement of the stratum corneum beyond transient post-injection hydration.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the clinical benefits observed in this study were not limited to superficial wrinkle smoothing, but were accompanied by coordinated improvements in dermal elasticity, epidermal hydration balance, surface uniformity, and barrier function—hallmarks of global skin quality enhancement rather than a purely volumetric or space-occupying effect.

High-frequency ultrasound imaging provided additional insight into structural skin changes following treatment. Progressive increases in dermal thickness and echogenicity were observed in both the periorbital and perioral regions from baseline through week 24. Increased echogenicity has been associated with enhanced dermal density and collagen organization in prior studies of HA-based interventions [24, 25]. While most previous skin booster studies have focused on clinical grading or optical surface parameters, the present study incorporated high-frequency ultrasound imaging to directly evaluate structural dermal changes. The observed increases in dermal thickness and echogenicity provide objective evidence supporting the interpretation that a low-G′ HA with favorable cohesivity, low complex viscosity, and water affinity may contribute to dermal remodeling, consistent with tissue repair and biostimulation beyond transient hydration.

Conclusions

The present findings support the clinical feasibility and utility of low-G′ hyaluronic acid as an effective intradermal treatment approach for the simultaneous improvement of fine wrinkles and global skin quality in thin, highly dynamic facial regions. While this study demonstrates a favorable safety profile and sustained multidimensional benefits over 6 months, further randomized controlled studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are required to clarify maximum durability, optimize treatment parameters, and define the comparative role of this approach within existing esthetic treatment strategies.
