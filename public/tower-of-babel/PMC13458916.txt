Balloon Mitral Valvotomy in Rheumatic Mitral Stenosis
Authors: Arghadip Bose
Published: 2026

I read with great interest the paper by Chichareon et al1 introducing the Wilkins-Integrated Clinical PBMV score to predict procedural success of percutaneous balloon mitral valvuloplasty (PBMV) in rheumatic mitral stenosis (MS). This is a methodologically robust effort; however, I would like to address its clinical interpretation and potential use in rheumatic-endemic, resource-limited settings.

Several variables designated as “unfavorable” in the new score represent successive stages along the same hemodynamic continuum of severe MS. A smaller mitral valve area is associated with higher transmitral gradients, causing stepwise increases in left atrial, pulmonary venous, pulmonary arterial, and right ventricular pressures, and ultimately, functional tricuspid regurgitation. In these patients, even modest enlargement of mitral valve area and reduction in filling pressures following PBMV may yield significant symptomatic benefit. A model optimized to predict a strict definition of “success” may underestimate clinically meaningful benefit in patients with the most advanced hemodynamic compromise.

Although the authors state that the updated score should complement, rather than replace, anatomical assessment, there is a risk that a score framed as predicting “success” will be used pragmatically as a gatekeeping tool. In many countries where rheumatic heart disease remains prevalent, it disproportionately affects lower socioeconomic strata, for whom PBMV is often the only accessible intervention because of its minimally invasive nature, lower cost, and reduced perioperative care.2 In such settings, using hard exclusions may inadvertently restrict access to PBMV in those with the greatest symptom burden and lowest likelihood of receiving timely surgery. For some of these patients, even a “suboptimal” PBMV may translate into meaningful improvement in functional capacity or provide physiological stabilization as a bridge to future mitral valve replacement.3

Atrial fibrillation in rheumatic MS reflects long-standing pressure overload, rather than intrinsically poor suitability for PBMV. Although atrial fibrillation is associated with an increased prevalence of left atrial or appendage thrombus and higher embolic risk, technical adaptations such as meticulous transesophageal echocardiography screening, careful anticoagulation, and over-the-wire PBMV techniques have been described to enable PBMV in these patients.4,5 In these scenarios, the feasibility, safety, and durability of PBMV remain primarily determined by the morphology of the mitral apparatus, rather than by rhythm status alone.

Before this score is used to substantially narrow PBMV eligibility in broader practice, prospective external validation is needed to confirm its influence on treatment allocation and patient-centered outcomes. Therefore, I view the updated score as an adjunct to detailed anatomical assessment and multidisciplinary judgment, rather than as a rigid filter that could reduce access to PBMV in rheumatic-endemic, resource-constrained environments.
