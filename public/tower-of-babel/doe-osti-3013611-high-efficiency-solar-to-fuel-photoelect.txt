 Executive Summary:

 This project investigated how photonic glass (PG) photoelectrodes—disordered
arrangements of dielectric scatterers—can serve as scalable, tunable platforms for light
trapping in photoelectrochemical (PEC) solar-to-fuel systems. By leveraging disorder-
driven optical phenomena such as multiple scattering resonances and light localization,
PG structures offer an alternative to conventional photonic crystals and inverse opals that
require high structural precision. The scientific goals were to twofold: (1) develop
approaches to predictive models for high performance PG electrodes based on light
absorption simulations, and (2) fabricate, characterize, and optimize PG-based
photoelectrodes for solar-to-hydrogen and solar-to-fuel photoelectrochemical
applications. To overcome the complexity of ensemble optical simulations for disordered
materials, the researchers developed a machine-learning-accelerated emulation of all
configurations in the design space. With this approach, PG photoelectrodes based on a
TiO2 semiconductor were designed to enhance PEC currents of up to one hundred times
higher than the equivalent ultra-thin film photoanodes and several times higher than the
equivalent photonic crystal. The research also explored integrated systems for
electrochemical hydrogen production based on replacing water oxidation with the specific
glycerol oxidation electrocatalysis. Overall, the project outlined an approach to a simple-
to-fabricate photoelectrode system to drive photoelectrochemical reactions relevant to
solar photochemical energy conversion.

 1. Major Goals of the Project

 The theme of the project was to understand and engineer the emergent light-trapping
properties of photonic glasses—disordered assemblies of light scatterers—and to
integrate these properties into photoelectrodes for solar-to-fuels applications. The primary
application under investigation is semiconductor-based photoelectrochemical (PEC)
solar-to-hydrogen conversion, although the objectives more broadly encompass the study
of light–matter interactions in solar energy conversion. Light-trapping strategies play a
critical role in maximizing light absorption within regions of a photoelectrode that exhibit
high incident photon-to-carrier conversion efficiency (IPCE), while minimizing absorption
in regions prone to parasitic losses or high recombination rates. The approach of interest
to this project contrasts with more commonly studied photonic crystal, waveguiding, or
plasmonic strategies, which generate predictable optical behaviors—such as resonances
and evanescent modes—through precisely engineered structures. A key advantage of
photonic glasses in these applications is the inherent simplicity of their fabrication: the
structure does not rely on precision. The emergent photonic properties arise from the
statistical characteristics of the disordered material—such as packing fraction, scatterer
or void size, and coordination number—rather than from the strict dielectric contrast
required by conventional semiconductor microfabrication. Harnessing these optical
effects in disordered materials may offer scalable routes to photonic light trapping in
photoelectrochemical systems.

 Prior work demonstrated that control over the statistical structure of colloidal
assemblies can enhance IPCE in ultrathin metal oxide electrodes. Using micron-scale
composite films of polystyrene and silica nanospheres, atomic layer deposition can
selectively bind silica spheres while the polystyrene spheres are subsequently removed.
This method enables precise control over the statistical structure within a disordered
assembly of light scatterers. The remaining challenge lies in predicting the optical
properties of a given photonic glass and assessing its light-concentrating performance for
a target semiconductor. Because photonic glasses are defined by ensembles of
configurations rather than a single deterministic structure, accurate predictions require
simulations that account for all possible configurations within the ensemble. Given the
potential of using photonic glasses as a generalizable photoelectrode structure for light
trapping, the goals of the project were to predict the optimal statistical structure of a
photonic glass for a specific semiconductor, and to experimentally develop and
characterize photoelectrode systems informed by these predictions.

 The primary obstacle for the theoretical objective is the computational intractability of
simulating the ensemble associated with a single electrode design. The cost of
brute-force ensemble simulation increases combinatorially with system complexity, far

exceeding that of comparable photonic crystals. The main theoretical objective in the
early phase of the project was to employ machine learning algorithms to improve the
computational efficiency of these ensemble simulations—particularly finite-difference
time-domain (FDTD) calculations of absorption in nanoscale semiconductors embedded
within a photonic glass. The experimental focus was to characterize colloidal structures
produced by the synthetic method developed in the researchers prior work and to
integrate thin-film semiconductor materials into rationally-designed photonic glass-based
photoelectrodes.

prepared by simple methods such as doctor blading, spray deposition, or even painting.
Additionally, a disordered PG can have the same light trapping properties exhibited by
the IO via multiple scattering or emergent localization in the structure.

 TiO2-based photonic glass photoanodes: As a model system, the researchers
targeted the PEC properties of photonic glass photoelectrodes based on an ultra-thin
layer of ALD-deposited TiO2. The band gap of TiO2 (3.1-3.2 eV) and its high abundance
does not necessitate this approach. However, they identified that its ubiquity in research
and the well characterized properties in water oxidation reactions made it a particularly
useful material for developing rationally-designed photonic glass photoanodes.
Conformal, ultra-thin layers are easy to deposit on the tortuous structure via ALD. The
material itself is electrochemically stable for experiments with long durations (e.g.,
spectral response measurements of IPCE). More broadly, TiO2 is a potential transparent
conducting oxide (such as in a dye sensitized solar cell or when doped) or as a precursor
layer for chemical modification to form more complex materials with improved visible light
absorption. This summary describes research provided in detail in a paper published
during the project. [1]
 For clarity, the PG electrodes are enumerated in this report by the ratio of colloidal
spheres in the template composite. A 100-0 PG electrode represents one fabricated with
100% silica as the composition of the PG structure, while a 70-30 PG electrode was
fabricated with 70% silica and 30% PS (by partial volume of each) as the composition of
the structure. A smaller fraction of silica means that the PG electrode is more porous after
fabrication as more of the composite structure was removed. One important observation
is that when the spheres were the same diameter, the long-range order of the close-
packed crystal lattice remained with omitted sites where PS had been present (hence an
“omission photonic glass”). These structures exhibit the angle-dependent opalescence
one may expect from a photonic crystal. When the silica and PS were different diameters,
the structure was truly disordered (no long-range order, Figure 1b) and exhibited angle-
independent structural color.
 A significant observation of the studies of the TiO2-functionalized PG photoelectrodes
is that the structure with the most semiconductor (i.e., a 100-0 PG) does not generate the
highest light absorption. One property of ALD-TiO2 (from tetrakisdimethylamidotitanium
[TDMAT] and H2O) is that, unlike conventional rutile or anatase TiO2, it absorbs visible
light. This is due to the presence of organic material from incomplete precursor
decomposition at the low ALD processing temperatures (80-250 °C). Figure 2 shows the
optical spectra of a series of PG photoelectrodes based on a 200 nm silica-200 nm PS
composite coated with a 20 nm layer of ALD-TiO2. The absorption both in the visible
portion of the spectrum (Figure 2a) and near the band edge at λ = 380-400 nm (Figure
2b) is maximized for the 50-50 electrode, here at all wavelengths. The openness of this
structure lends itself to electrolyte access compared to electrodes with smaller omission
fractions. This effect had been identified in a thin film ZnO PG photoelectrode structure in
FDTD simulations, though not for a broad portion of the spectrum. After annealing in air
at 500 °C, the ligand dopants were removed from the TiO2 coating. The trend near the
band gap improved due to a weakening of the UV absorption in the PG electrodes with

smaller degrees of omission (Figure 2c). Visible light absorption was reduced due to the
removal of the ligand detritus (Figure 2d), though it is not clear if the absorption is truly
absorption here, of if it is due to escaped light from the electrode sides or the integrating
sphere. The 50-50 electrode generates a nearly 70-fold increase in photocurrent
compared to the identical, planar 20 nm TiO2 photoanode under a 365 nm LED (500 mW
cm-2) with a ferricyanide/ferrocyanide couple in
aqueous electrolyte (Figure 3).

 Machine-learning based emulation of disordered, colloidal light absorbers:
Another success of this research project was the development of new approaches to the
simulation-based design of these disordered photoelectrodes. For ordered materials such
as the IO photoelectrode, the optical properties are determined by the unit cell and the
periodicity. The “design problem” is localized to modifications of the unit cell and space
symmetry. The difficulty may then come from the fabrication of precise structures. For

materials that use intrinsic disorder as a functional or fabrication strategy, simulations on
the same small scale impart artificial periodicity in the system, and the results are not
representative. As a result, simulations need to be extended to very large volumes where
the unavoidable periodic boundary conditions extend beyond the characteristic length
scales where electric fields in different portions of the electrode structure become
uncorrelated. Additionally, the ensemble of structures in the design space is what needs
to be characterized, rather than individual examples. The number of simulations
increases combinatorially and making the design problem in disordered photoelectrodes
a difficult problem.
 The team developed approaches to “designing” PG photoelectrodes by improving the
efficiency of searches through the PG ensemble structure space. In that period, we have
developed approaches based on FDTD simulations of photoelectrode structures
combined with neural networks to emulate the results of all potential simulations based
on a subset of actual simulations in the space. A brief summary: Maxwell’s equations that
determine the propagation of the FDTD simulations are limited by boundary and continuity
conditions that can be “learned” by a neural network (NN). The NN learns the mapping of
a representation of the photoelectrode structure (a binary representation of the occupancy
of the “omission glass” (Figure 1a) lattice, for example) to the results of an FDTD
simulation (real-valued electric field data) on that specific structure. Given a sufficient set
of simulations for various structures in the ensemble (various PS-SiO2 ratios), the NN can
emulate the simulation results for the entire ensemble across the free parameter space.
This accelerates the “design” process by the ratio of the size of the ensemble to the
number of simulations used to train the NN. Additionally, the error in the emulation can be
estimated from a small number of additional simulations as a test set. This summary
describes research provided in detail in a paper published during the project.[2]
 This procedure has been shown to emulate localized light absorption in a simple 2D
omission glass structure with an embedded light absorber. In the example system used
in this work, 250nm GaAs cylinder embedded in the center of 7 layers of 250nm SiO2
cylinders (N=41 total) defined with periodic boundary conditions for FDTD simulations.
The disorder in this system is generated by removing a fraction of the dielectric scatterers
at random. For the system as defined, there is one configuration for the case where no
SiO2 cylinders are omitted (k=0 case), 41 unique ways to omit one cylinder (k=1) and 820
ways to omit two cylinders (k=2). In general, the number of configurations for a given
degree of omission k in N cylinders is NCk = N! / ((N-k)! k!) (‘N choose k’), which quickly
becomes large. For N = 41 there are 101,270 omission configurations for k=4 and 1.12
x109 for k = 10. However, computing the ensemble-averaged absorption spectrum of a
single k=10 omission glass requires an impossible amount of computation. IPCE is a
function of the spatial distribution of absorption in the semiconductor. The complexity and
ensemble of this model system are well-defined, and yet representative of the relevant
experimental capabilities for fabricating PG electrodes. This demonstrates the need for

 Machine learning approaches were used to develop an approximate model (an
emulator) for all the possible configurations of a given (N,k) ensemble for the omission
glass. While the configuration of scatterers around the absorber has effects profile of
absorption in the GaAs cylinder, there are correlations between configurations. These
correlations are not predictable or subject to analytical analysis, but machine learning can
infer these correlations from a subset of samples to model the entire ensemble. The
machine learning models can also be subjected to statistical analysis and validation by
comparing the predicted results to a different test subset of the ensemble that was not
used to train the model. Specifically, a neural network (or multi-level perceptron (MLP))
algorithm was trained to infer the relationship between the vectorized, binary
representation (for k=3, a randomized list of 38 ones and three zeros) of an omission
configuration to the 16x16 real-number array representing the absorption profile and
boundary of the GaAs absorber generated by an FDTD simulation of that configuration.
Given a ‘train’ library of simulations, the MLP can infer the statistical dependence between
the input and output by backpropagation. The error in fitting can be evaluated for a ‘test’
set of simulations that were left out of the MLP training. Examples of the data
representations and individual predictions are show in Figure 4. The omission glass
problem is a useful problem for evaluating the MLP approach to modeling an ensemble
because certain values of k are reasonably solvable.
 The emulator acts as a simplified analytical tool for the entire structure of the omission
glass. After training the model it allows researchers to ask questions such as: what is k
value for that generates the most broadband absorption in the GaAs absorber? what is
the IPCE for a GaAs photocatalyst with a short minority carrier diffusion length (which
depends on the absorption profile in each configuration)? More generally, it helps to
efficiently calculate the ensemble properties for a single point in a search of the chemical
space (absorber diameter, absorber geometry, scatterer diameter, omission fraction, etc.)
that is relevant to our photonic glass electrode. The MLP approach also allows methods

of dimensionality reduction in order to provide training sets that are more representative
of the distribution of absorption profiles in a given ensemble or other improved
efficiencies.
 These results have shown that disordered electrodes based on hierarchically porous
colloidal composites can dramatically increase the PEC energy conversion efficiency of
ultra-thin film photoelectrodes. The PG electrode allows for the macroporosity of the SiO2
scaffold can be tailored to optimize light absorption, layer structure, and mass transport
throughout the 3-D electrode. There are several fundamental and technological
challenges to this that need to be overcome to be successful. The first is to develop a
composite/macroporous electrode structure that integrates the knowledge developed
during the previous task. The porosity serves multiple functions in a material for solar
fuels: light scattering and trapping, scaffolding for the thin film light absorber, back contact
for carrier collection, and generating sufficient mass transport for the whole structure to
function. We aim to design these electrodes rationally, with the results of research on the
planar photoanodes built into the structure. Another fundamental issue is the materials
chemistry of the scaffold itself, and the integration of a transparent and conductive
material to act as the light-scattering electron collector. These advances will come from
the carefully considered integration of finite element modeling (light absorption and mass
transport) and new experimental materials chemistry and characterization.
 Glycerol electrooxidation as an alternative to water oxidation for solar hydrogen
generation: The research described above focused mainly on the solar (light-matter
interactions) portion of solar photochemistry, particularly for photoelectrochemical (PEC)
energy conversion. The PG structural motif works to passively concentrate light using
light localization and multiple scattering. The research team used this to motivate a
specific solar photochemistry problem to tackle. They developed an electrocatalytic
system that enables unassisted solar-to-hydrogen (STH) evolution on a single
photoelectrode by replacing water oxidation with glycerol electrooxidation (GEOR) on a
Au-Pt-Bi electrocatalyst. While glycerol electrooxidation has been of interest for this
exchange for some time, they showed during this project that limiting the anode to low
geometric current densities (< 30mA cm-2) allows for durable STH with as little as 500 mV
of total cell potential. Hence, a system based on a single silicon photovoltaic is sufficient
to drive it. This summary describes research provided in detail in a paper published during
the project.[3]
 One of the advances was the development of a novel electrocatalyst for glycerol
oxidation that enables low overall cell potentials for PEC-based solar-to-hydrogen
photocatalysis. The electrocatalyst - a unique combination of Au, Pt, and Bi – sustained
low overall cell potentials for hydrogen evolution. The simple synthesis of the
electrocatalyst (galvanic replacement deposition of Au and Pt, then brief electrodeposition
of Bi) facilitates the translation to high surface area Ni foam electrodes. This resulted in
extremely low cell potentials (Ecell) to achieve practical current densities. For example,

10 mA cm-2 is a target current density for PEC solar-to-hydrogen applications. Using the
electrocatalyst in a model crude glycerol solution (20% (w/w) glycerol in aqueous 2 M
KOH) and a Ni foam-based Pt cathode required only Ecell = 0.73 V, where achieving the
same current density would require >1.7 V for unassisted water splitting. This is a
considerable energy conversion efficiency increase per mol of H2 produced. This
facilitated glycerol-assisted solar-to-H2 electrolysis with a single monocrystalline Si PV
(Voc = 0.675 V) and on a single GaAs PV (Voc = 0.966 V) at relevant current densities.
This is thermodynamically impossible for water splitting.
 The team then characterized the reaction products to better understand the
mechanism for such an improvement. Many research groups have considered replacing
water oxidation with glycerol electrooxidation, but frequently the improvements at these
current densities only improve the cell potentials by a few hundred millivolts. Thus the
need for analytical characterization to understand the reaction at these relatively low
absolute potentials. The observable products (via NMR) of glycerol electrooxidation
under these conditions were lactate and formate. However, it was found that while
glycerol can be oxidized at very low electrode potentials (E > 0.3 V vs RHE), the first two
products of glycerol electrooxidation (dihydroxyacetone and glyceraldehyde) are not
electrochemically oxidized without much more positive potentials (E > 1.2 V vs. RHE).
Dihydroxyacetone and glyceraldehyde are constitutional isomers and can react with the
highly alkaline solution spontaneously. Many of the reported observations of glycerol
electrooxidation to formate, acetaldehyde, CO2, or even the observation of lactate in this
work are likely due to either very high electrode potentials (approaching EOER) or
generated chemically in the alkaline conditions. This somewhat limits the prospects of
the full 14-electron oxidation of glycerol to CO2 and H2 on this Au-Pt-Bi electrocatalyst.
However, it does suggest that the PEC-driven oxidation of glycerol can be utilized for the
selective valorization of glycerol to the two 2-electron oxidation products (glyceraldehyde
and dihydroxyacetone) while improving the energy payback time potential for a simple
PEC solar-to-H2 integrated system. This process can potentially be driven by
photoelectrodes based on the PG structure.
 In later work, the generality of GEOR-assisted solar-to-fuels reactions was extended
via the integration of a bipolar membrane (BPM). This summary describes research
provided in detail in a paper published during the project.[4] The BPM enabled a wide
range of catholytes to be used on the HER half-reaction, particularly intended to decouple
that half-cell from the crude glycerol anolyte used for the oxidation reaction. This work
showed that HER in any environment (e.g. acidic, neutral seawater, or basic) can benefit
from the same cell voltage savings that were demonstrated for alkaline HER when a BPM
is used as the membrane separator. Fundamentally, the operating point of this cell is
predictable based on load-line analyses of conventional three-electrode characteristics of
each component electrode. Most relevant to this goal, this work showed that a GEOR-

STH cell with a Pt cathode in 1M H2SO4 and a GEOR anode in 2 M NaOH can operate
at ~550 mV for 10 h and < 700 mV for 80 h at a geometric current density of 10 mA cm-2.
