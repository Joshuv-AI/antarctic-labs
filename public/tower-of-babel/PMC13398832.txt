Methodological considerations on the associations of serum CTRP3 and CysC with bone metabolism in elderly patients with T2DM
Authors: Lijun Zhu, Bin Song
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the recent article by Wang et al. 1 , which reported that serum CTRP3 and CysC levels are significantly associated with glucose‐lipid metabolism, bone turnover markers, and BMD in elderly patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) complicated by osteoporosis (OP). This study addresses an important clinical question, providing potential biomarkers for early identification of OP in this population. However, we have several methodological concerns that potentially threaten the validity of the conclusions.

First, the cross‐sectional design limits causal interpretation of the associations between CTRP3/CysC and bone metabolism. While the study demonstrated correlations between serum CTRP3 and CysC with BMD and bone turnover markers (Figure 4a–n), temporal relationships cannot be established, and reverse causation remains possible. For example, elevated CysC may reflect early renal impairment, which itself could influence bone turnover independently of CTRP3 levels. Prospective cohort studies or repeated longitudinal measurements of CTRP3 and CysC are needed to confirm whether changes in these biomarkers precede alterations in bone metabolism rather than merely co‐occurring with disease progression.

Second, potential confounding by unmeasured factors may bias the reported associations. Although the authors adjusted for age, gender, T2DM duration, and glucose‐lipid parameters in multivariate logistic regression, critical covariates such as vitamin D status, physical activity, and direct measures of body composition (visceral fat or lean mass) were not included. Given that BMI alone cannot distinguish between fat and muscle mass 2 , residual confounding may exaggerate or obscure the relationships between serum biomarkers and OP risk. Future analyses incorporating these variables, alongside stratification by menopausal status, would provide more robust evidence for the independent predictive value of CTRP3 and CysC.

In summary, while the study by Wang et al. provides valuable insights into the potential utility of CTRP3 and CysC as markers of bone health in elderly T2DM patients, addressing these methodological limitations is essential to accurately interpret the findings and guide future clinical interventions and research.

Importantly, until longitudinal evidence becomes available, serum CTRP3 and CysC should be interpreted as associative rather than causal biomarkers of osteoporosis in T2DM, and clinicians should interpret them together with renal function (e.g., eGFR) and vitamin D status. Future prospective cohort studies or Mendelian randomization analyses may help clarify the causal relationships among CysC‐related renal dysfunction, CTRP3‐associated inflammation, and bone turnover abnormalities.
