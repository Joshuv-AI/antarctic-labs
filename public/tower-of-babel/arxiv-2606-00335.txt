Equations in Products of Free Groups and 3-Manifold Groups, I
Olga Kharlampovich, Alina Vdovina

Abstract




Abstract:

Perelman’s proof of the Poincare conjecture shows that every simply
connected closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to the 3-sphere. The fundamental groups of 3-manifolds attract lots of interest from mathematicians of different fields. As it was stated in a famous survey of Allen Hatcher "The classification of 3-manifolds", one would want to know exactly which groups occur as fundamental groups of these manifolds.

The Stallings–Jaco–Hempel reformulation of the Poincare conjecture
inspired several connections between low-dimensional topology, equations
over free groups, and combinatorial group theory. The reformulation reduces the problem to study epimorphisms from the fundamental group of a closed orientable surface onto the direct product of two free groups (they correspond to Heegaard splittings of 3-manifolds and were named splitting homomorphisms). Olshankii (1989) constructed (in non-explicit form) first non-trivial examples of such splitting epimorphisms and verified
the standardness of some of them.

We construct up to equivalence all the splitting coordinate-surjective homomorphisms (among them, the genuine splitting epimorphisms are exactly those for which our constructed associated group balanced presentation is trivial). We give generators and relations of the corresponding balanced presentation (so all closed orientable 3-manifold groups) that can be studied by algebraic
methods. We analyse a big class of such homomorphisms/presentations (including all Olshanskii’s epimorphisms)
and show that splitting epimorphisms are very rare, in this case the corresponding balanced presentation of the trivial group can be reduced to the standard one by Andrews-Curtis transformations and the epimorphisms (in small genera in this paper) are standard.

Introduction

Perelman’s theorem that resolved the Poincare conjecture, states: Every simply connected, closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to the 3-sphere. The fundamental groups of 3-manifolds attract lots of interest from mathematicians of different fields. As it was stated in Allen Hatcher’s survey
[
10
]
one would want to know exactly which groups occur as fundamental groups of these manifolds.

Stallings-Jaco-Hempel’s reformulation (1966-1976) of Poincare conjecture (Perelman theorem) inspired
many interesting mathematical connections. The reformulation reduces the problem to study epimorphisms from the fundamental group of a closed orientable surface onto the direct product of two free groups (they correspond to Heegaard splittings of 3-manifolds and were named splitting homomorphisms). Grigorchuk-Kurchanov wrote a survey on this (1990). Olshankii (1989) constructed (in non-explicit form) first non-trivial examples of such splitting epimorphisms and verified
the standardness of some of them.

We construct up to equivalence all the splitting coordinate-surjective homomorphisms (among them, the genuine splitting epimorphisms are exactly those for which
the associated balanced presentation is trivial). We give a concrete simple relators of the corresponding balanced presentation, see, as one of the simplest examples, relations (
6
) (so all closed orientable 3-manifold groups) that can be studied by algebraic
methods. Our construction makes the associated presentations explicit enough to compute with and analyse. The construction is inspired by Kharlampovich-Miasnikov’s proof of the implicit function theorem. We also analyse a big class of such homomorphisms/presentations (including all Olshanskii’s epimorphisms)
and show that splitting epimorphisms are very rare and in this case the corresponding balanced presentation of the trivial group can be reduced to the standard one by Andrews-Curtis transformations.

There are two main motivations of this paper. One is the possibility of obtaining a purely group theoretic proof of the Poincare conjecture. Another is to study fundamental groups of closed orientable 3-manifolds in Hatcher’s spirit that become clear from their presentations that we construct.

1.4
Andrews–Curtis transformations

Let

F_{n}=F(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

be the free group on the generators
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, and let

\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{m})\in F_{n}^{m}

be an ordered
m
-tuple of words. An
Andrews–Curtis transformation

replaces
\mathbf{r}
by another tuple obtained by one of the following elementary
moves:

1.

Inversion of a relator:

r_{i}\longmapsto r_{i}^{-1}.

2.

Multiplication of one relator by another:
for
i\neq j
,

r_{i}\longmapsto r_{i}r_{j}\quad\text{or, equivalently,}\quad r_{i}\longmapsto r_{j}r_{i}.

3.

Conjugation of a relator:
for any word
w\in F_{n}
,

r_{i}\longmapsto wr_{i}w^{-1}.

4.

Change of free basis:
for an automorphism
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(F_{n})
,

(r_{1},\dots,r_{m})\longmapsto(\alpha(r_{1}),\dots,\alpha(r_{m})).

Two tuples are called
Andrews–Curtis equivalent
if one can be obtained
from the other by a finite sequence of these transformations and their inverses.
Equivalently, the same terminology may be applied to presentations

\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\mid r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\rangle.

In the balanced case
m=n
, the Andrews–Curtis conjecture says that every
balanced presentation of the trivial group,

\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\mid r_{1},\dots,r_{n}\rangle\cong 1,

is Andrews–Curtis equivalent to the standard trivial presentation

\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\mid x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle.

Some authors separate the last operation from the first three and call it a
Nielsen change of generators. Others include it among the extended
Andrews–Curtis or extended Nielsen transformations. The essential operations
on relators are inversion, multiplication by another relator, and conjugation.

3
Andrews-Curtis trivialization of one-layer balanced presentations defining the trivial group

In this section we study balanced presentations arising from one-layer
automorphisms from Definition
2.2
. We recall that we consider the case when all
k_{i}
’s are non-zeros. We show that the corresponding groups are highly restricted.
In particular, trivial groups occur only in very special situations, while
generic parameter choices produce nontrivial quotients, often related to
generalized triangle groups.
We also show in Theorem
3.6
that the trivial cases are
Andrews-Curtis equivalent to the standard presentation and, therefore, standard.

Our main method for proving nontriviality is to construct suitable quotients
of the associated balanced presentations. In many cases these quotients are
generalized triangle groups, whose nontriviality is known from results of
Baumslag, Morgan, Shalen and others. Recall that a generalized triangle group is a group of the form

\langle x,y\mid x^{m},y^{n},w(x,y)^{k}\rangle.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a balanced presentation arising from a one-layer automorphism
of genus
3
. Then
G
is cyclic.

Proof.

These groups have relations

(a_{1}a_{2})^{k_{1}}a_{1}=1,

b_{2}=a_{2}(a_{1}a_{2})^{k_{1}},\ (a_{1}a_{2})^{k_{1}}(a_{2}^{b_{2}}a_{3})^{k_{2}}a_{2}=1,

(a_{2}^{b_{2}}a_{3})^{k_{2}}a_{3}=1.

The second two relations can be written as

(a_{2}^{b_{2}}a_{3})^{k_{2}}a_{2}a_{1}^{-1}=1,

a_{3}a_{1}a_{2}^{-1}=1.

The first relation implies that
[a_{1},a_{2}]=1
. Let
x=a_{1}a_{2}
, then
a_{1}=x^{-k_{1}},a_{2}=x^{k_{1}+1}
,
b_{2}=x^{2k_{1}+1}
and
a_{3}=a_{2}a_{1}^{-1}=x^{2k_{1}+1}.
The lemma is proved. ∎

Every epimorphism corresponding to the trivial group of genus 3 with one layer is, therefore, standard.

Lemma 3.3

(Base of induction)
.

Every trivial group of genus 3 with relations

(a_{1}a_{2})^{k_{1}}a_{1}=1,

(a_{1}a_{2})^{k_{1}}(a_{2}a_{3}^{s})^{k_{2}}a_{2}=1,

(a_{2}a_{3}^{s})^{k_{2}}a_{3}^{k_{3}}=1,

can be AC-trivialized.

Proof.

Let
x=a_{1}a_{2}.
, then
a_{1}=x^{-{k_{1}}},a_{2}=x^{k_{1}+1}.
Relations become

x^{2k_{1}+1}(x^{k_{1}+1}a_{3}^{s})^{k_{2}}=1,\ {a_{3}}^{k_{3}}(x^{k_{1}}a_{3}^{s})^{k_{2}}=1.

If
|k_{2}|,|k_{3}|>1,k_{1}\neq-1
, this group is non-trivial.
It is trivial only in exceptional cases
k_{1}=-1
or
k_{2}=\pm 1
or
k_{3}=1
(and, in addition, the abelianization must be trivial).

Denote
a=a_{3}
. Let
k_{1}=-1
. The first relator becomes
x^{-1}a^{sk_{2}}=1,

hence

x=a^{sk_{2}}.

Substituting into the second relator gives

a^{k_{3}}(a^{sk_{2}})^{-k_{2}}a^{sk_{2}}=1,

that is,

a^{k_{3}+sk_{2}(1-k_{2})}=1.
This is a cyclic group. Therefore the group, when trivial, can be AC-trivialized.

Just for the completeness

G=1\iff\left|k_{3}+sk_{2}(1-k_{2})\right|=1.

Let
k_{2}=1
. The relations are

x^{3k_{1}+2}a^{s}=1,\qquad a^{k_{3}}x^{k_{1}}a^{s}=1.

From the first relation,

a^{-s}=x^{3k_{1}+2}.

Substituting into the second relation gives

a^{k_{3}}=x^{-k_{1}}a^{-s}=x^{-k_{1}}x^{3k_{1}+2}=x^{2k_{1}+2}.

Hence
G
is cyclic, generated by

, with defining relation

x^{k_{3}(3k_{1}+2)+s(2k_{1}+2)}=1.

k_{2}=-1

Using the first relation in the second gives

hence

Thus

G\cong\left\langle x,a\ \middle|\ x^{k_{1}}=a^{s},\ a^{k_{3}-2s}=1\right\rangle.

The group is trivial exactly when both generators have trivial image, namely

|k_{1}|=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad|k_{3}-2s|=1.

Again, it can be AC-trivialized.

The case
|k_{3}|=1

Definition 3.4

(Generalized triangle group)
.

Let
|\ell|,|m|,|n|>1
. A group
G
is called a

generalized triangle group
if it admits a presentation of the form

G=\left\langle a,b\ \middle|\ a^{\ell}=b^{m}=w^{n}=1\right\rangle,

where
w
is a cyclically reduced word in the free group
F(a,b)
which,
after replacing
w
by a cyclic conjugate if necessary, may be written as

w=a^{r_{1}}b^{s_{1}}\cdots a^{r_{k}}b^{s_{k}},

with

k\geq 1,\qquad 0<r_{i}<\ell,\qquad 0<s_{i}<m

for all
i=1,\ldots,k
.

The ordinary triangle group is the special case
w=ab
.

By
[
1
]
there exists
a homomorphism

\rho:G\longrightarrow\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})

such that

\operatorname{ord}(\rho(a))=\ell,\qquad\operatorname{ord}(\rho(b))=m,\qquad\operatorname{ord}(\rho(w))=n.

In particular, the elements
a,b,w\in G
have orders
\ell,m,n
,
respectively.

Theorem 3.6
.

For every trivial group with one layer given by the presentation

(a_{1}a_{2})^{k_{1}}a_{1}=1,

(a_{1}a_{2})^{k_{1}}(a_{2}a_{3})^{k_{2}}a_{2}=1,

(a_{2}a_{3})^{k_{2}}(a_{3}^{b_{3}}a_{4})^{k_{3}}a_{3}=1,

(a_{3}^{b_{3}}a_{4})^{k_{3}}(a_{4}^{b_{4}}a_{5})^{k_{4}}a_{4}=1,

\ldots

(a_{n-1}a_{n})^{k_{n-1}}(a_{n}a_{n+1}^{s})^{k_{n}}a_{n}=1,

(a_{n}a_{n+1}^{s})^{k_{n}}a_{n+1}^{m}=1,

the presentation (denote it
G(n+1,m)
) can be AC-trivialised.

Proof.

Let
G(n+1,s,m)
be the group given by this presentation for
m=1
.
We will use induction on
n
. Assume the statement is true for

G(n-1)
. We denote

p=k_{n-1},\qquad q=k_{n}.

Lemma 3.7

(Terminal reduction)
.

Let
G(n+1;s,m)
be a one-layer presentation whose last two relations are

(a_{n-1}a_{n})^{p}(a_{n}a_{n+1}^{s})^{q}a_{n}=1,

(a_{n}a_{n+1}^{s})^{q}a_{n+1}^{m}=1.

Put

A=a_{n-1},\qquad B=a_{n},\qquad C=a_{n+1},\qquad X=BC^{s}.

If one of the terminal primitive cases described in the table occurs, then the
presentation is Andrews–Curtis equivalent to a one-layer presentation of
genus
2n-1
, with terminal parameters given by the corresponding formula.

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}\text{case}&\text{new parameter }s^{\prime}&\text{new parameter }m^{\prime}\\
\hline\cr p=-1,\ q=1&-m&m+s\\
p=-1,\ q=-1&-m&s-m\\
p=-1,\ m=1&q&-1-qs\\
p=-1,\ m=-1&q&qs-1\\
p=1,\ q=1&3m+2s&5m+3s\\
p=1,\ q=-1&2s-m&3s-m\\
p=1,\ m=1&-2-q(2s+1)&-3-q(3s+2)\\
p=1,\ m=-1&-2-q+2qs&-3-2q+3qs\end{array}

Table 1:
Terminal reductions for the generalized one-layer presentation.
In each case the presentation
G(n+1;s,m)
is Andrews–Curtis equivalent
to a presentation of type
G(n-1;s^{\prime},m^{\prime})
.

Proof.

All transformations used below are elementary Nielsen transformations on the
terminal generators, together with replacements of relators by products of
relators and their inverses. Hence they are Andrews–Curtis transformations.

We write

R_{0}=(DA)^{r}(AB)^{p}A,\qquad R_{1}=(AB)^{p}X^{q}B,\qquad R_{2}=X^{q}C^{m},

where

X=BC^{s}.

The relator
R_{0}
is the only one involving the previous generator
D
;
the relators
R_{1},R_{2}
will be used to express two of the three terminal
generators
A,B,C
in terms of a single new terminal generator
T
.

First suppose
p=-1
. Then

R_{1}=(AB)^{-1}X^{q}B=1

implies

X^{q}=A.

Together with

R_{2}=X^{q}C^{m}=1

this gives

A=C^{-m}.

Thus, when
q=1
, we have

X=C^{-m},\qquad B=XC^{-s}=C^{-m-s}.

Taking
T=C
, the relator
R_{0}
becomes

(DC^{-m})^{r}B^{-1}=(DC^{-m})^{r}C^{m+s}.

Therefore in this case

s^{\prime}=-m,\qquad m^{\prime}=m+s.

When
p=-1
and
q=-1
, we have

X^{-1}=C^{-m},

so

X=C^{m},\qquad B=XC^{-s}=C^{m-s}.

Again taking
T=C
, the relator
R_{0}
becomes

(DC^{-m})^{r}B^{-1}=(DC^{-m})^{r}C^{s-m}.

Hence

s^{\prime}=-m,\qquad m^{\prime}=s-m.

Still assuming
p=-1
, suppose now that
m=1
. We take
T=X
. Since

X^{q}C=1,

we get

C=X^{-q}.

Also

Thus

becomes

(DX^{q})^{r}X^{-1-qs}.

Therefore

If
p=-1
and
m=-1
, we again take
T=X
. From

A=X^{q},\qquad B=XC^{-s}=X^{1-qs}.

R_{0}=(DA)^{r}B^{-1}

(a_{3}^{b_{3}}a_{4})^{k_{3}}(a_{4}^{b_{4}}a_{5})^{k_{4}}a_{4}=1,

Safe library of automorphisms

Single-handle twists and the move

Lemma 4.1

(Basis criterion for standardness)
.

Let

\phi:\pi_{1}(S_{g})\longrightarrow F(b_{1},\ldots,b_{g})\times F(a_{1},\ldots,a_{g})

be one of the one-layer splitting homomorphisms considered here, with

\phi(x_{i})=b_{i}a_{i}.

Suppose
U
is a surface group automorphism such that the images

c_{i}:=\phi U(y_{i}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,g,

form a free basis of
F(a_{1},\ldots,a_{g})
.
Write the original generators
a_{i}
as words in this basis:

a_{i}=u_{i}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{g}).

Then
\phi U(x_{i})=B_{i}C_{i}
, where
B_{i}\in F(b)
and
C_{i}\in F(c)
.

Then
\phi
is standard: after further precomposition by a surface automorphism fixing the
y_{i}
’s, and after automorphisms of the target free factors
F(a)
,
F(b)
,
\phi
becomes

x_{i}\mapsto b_{i}c_{i},\qquad y_{i}\mapsto c_{i}.

Proof.

Since
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{g})
is a free basis, there is an automorphism

\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}F(a_{1},\ldots,a_{g})

such that

\alpha(c_{i})=a_{i}.

Moreover, the tuple
C_{1},\ldots,C_{g}
is obtained as
C_{i}=\delta W(x_{i})
where
W
is a surface automorphism fixing
y_{i}
’s and
\delta(x_{i})=\delta(y_{i})=c_{i}
.
Then
\phi UW(x_{i})=B_{i}c_{i}
,
\phi UW(y_{i})=c_{i}
. The tuple
B_{1},\ldots,B_{g}
must be a basis of
F(b)
because this is an epimorphism.
Hence
\phi
is standard.
∎

It follows from the lemma that to show that
\phi
is standard, it is sufficient to find a surface group automorphism

such that
\phi U(y_{i})
form a basis of
F(a)
. In this case we say that the automorphism

is
standardizing
.

4.3
Genus 4 standardization

Theorem 4.3
.

Every splitting homomorphism
\phi
in genus
4
defining a balanced
presentation with one layer that produces a trivial group, is standard.

Let the genus four surface relation be in normal commutator form:

[x_{1},y_{1}][x_{2},y_{2}][x_{3},y_{3}][x_{4},y_{4}]=1.

Let

\phi(x_{i})=b_{i}a_{i}.

For the one-layer exponents

k_{1},\ k_{2},\ k_{3},

define first

Y_{1}=a_{1}(a_{1}a_{2})^{k_{1}},\qquad Y_{2}=a_{2}(a_{2}a_{1})^{k_{1}}.

Put

P_{2}=a_{2}^{Y_{2}}=Y_{2}^{-1}a_{2}Y_{2},\qquad A_{2}=P_{2}a_{3}.

Then define

Z_{1}=Y_{1},\qquad Z_{2}=Y_{2}A_{2}^{k_{2}},\qquad Z_{3}=a_{3}(a_{3}P_{2})^{k_{2}}.

Next put

P_{3}=a_{3}^{Z_{3}}=Z_{3}^{-1}a_{3}Z_{3},\qquad A_{3}=P_{3}a_{4}.

Then the images of the
y_{i}
’s under
\phi
are

\boxed{\begin{aligned} \phi(y_{1})&=Z_{1},\\
\phi(y_{2})&=Z_{2},\\
\phi(y_{3})&=Z_{3}A_{3}^{k_{3}},\\
\phi(y_{4})&=a_{4}(a_{4}P_{3})^{k_{3}}.\end{aligned}}

Let

G(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})

be the genus four one-layer presentation

\displaystyle G(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})=\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\mid

\displaystyle(a_{1}a_{2})^{k_{1}}a_{1},

\displaystyle(a_{2}a_{3})^{k_{2}}a_{2}a_{1}^{-1},

\displaystyle(a_{3}a_{4})^{k_{3}}a_{3}a_{1}a_{2}^{-1},

\displaystyle a_{4}a_{2}a_{1}^{-1}a_{3}^{-1}\rangle.

The abelianization matrix is

M(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})=\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}+1&k_{1}&0&0\\
-1&k_{2}+1&k_{2}&0\\
1&-1&k_{3}+1&k_{3}\\
-1&1&-1&1\end{pmatrix}.

Hence

\det M(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})=4k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}+3k_{1}k_{2}+4k_{1}k_{3}+3k_{2}k_{3}+2k_{1}+2k_{2}+2k_{3}+1.

Therefore, if
G(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})
is trivial, then necessarily

\det M(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})=\pm 1.

We now record the converse.

Lemma 4.4
.

The group
G(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})
is trivial if and only if

4k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}+3k_{1}k_{2}+4k_{1}k_{3}+3k_{2}k_{3}+2k_{1}+2k_{2}+2k_{3}+1=\pm 1.

Proof.

The necessity follows from abelianization. We prove sufficiency.

First suppose
k_{2}=-1
. The second relator gives

(a_{2}a_{3})^{-1}a_{2}a_{1}^{-1}=1,

hence

a_{3}=a_{1}^{-1}.

The last relator gives

a_{4}=a_{2}^{-1}.

Put

s=a_{1}a_{2}.

From the first relator,

a_{1}=s^{-k_{1}},\qquad a_{2}=s^{k_{1}+1}.

The third relator then becomes

s^{-(k_{1}+k_{3}+1)}=1.

Thus

G(k_{1},-1,k_{3})\cong\langle s\mid s^{k_{1}+k_{3}+1}\rangle.

So in this case the group is trivial exactly when

k_{1}+k_{3}+1=\pm 1,

that is,

k_{3}=-k_{1}\qquad\text{or}\qquad k_{3}=-k_{1}-2.

Next suppose
k_{1}=-1
. The first relator gives

a_{2}=1.

The second relator gives

a_{1}=a_{3}^{k_{2}}.

The last relator gives

a_{4}=a_{3}a_{1}=a_{3}^{k_{2}+1}.

The third relator becomes

a_{3}^{k_{2}k_{3}+k_{2}+2k_{3}+1}=1.

Thus

G(-1,k_{2},k_{3})\cong\langle a_{3}\mid a_{3}^{k_{2}k_{3}+k_{2}+2k_{3}+1}\rangle.

So this group is trivial exactly when

k_{2}k_{3}+k_{2}+2k_{3}+1=\pm 1.

Similarly, if
k_{3}=-1
, then the third and fourth relators imply

a_{3}=1.

The second relator gives

a_{1}=a_{2}^{k_{2}+1}.

The first relator becomes

a_{2}^{k_{1}k_{2}+2k_{1}+k_{2}+1}=1.

Hence

G(k_{1},k_{2},-1)\cong\langle a_{2}\mid a_{2}^{k_{1}k_{2}+2k_{1}+k_{2}+1}\rangle,

and the group is trivial exactly when

k_{1}k_{2}+2k_{1}+k_{2}+1=\pm 1.

It remains to list the integer triples for which the determinant is
\pm 1
. Solving

4k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}+3k_{1}k_{2}+4k_{1}k_{3}+3k_{2}k_{3}+2k_{1}+2k_{2}+2k_{3}+1=\pm 1

gives the following families:

(k_{1},-2,-1),\qquad k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z},

(-1,k_{2},-1),\qquad k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z},

(-1,-2,k_{3}),\qquad k_{3}\in\mathbb{Z},

and

(k_{1},-1,-k_{1}),\qquad k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z},

(k_{1},-1,-k_{1}-2),\qquad k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}.

In addition, there are the isolated solutions

(0,0,0),

(-1,0,0),\quad(-1,-3,-3),\quad(-1,-4,-2),

and

(0,0,-1),\quad(-2,-4,-1),\quad(-3,-3,-1).

All the infinite families above have one of
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}
equal to
-1
, or are contained in the cyclic reductions just computed. In each case, the determinant condition
\det M=\pm 1
is exactly the condition that the resulting cyclic group has exponent
\pm 1
, hence is trivial.

For the remaining isolated solution
(0,0,0)
, the presentation is

\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\mid a_{1},\ a_{2}a_{1}^{-1},\ a_{3}a_{1}a_{2}^{-1},\ a_{4}a_{2}a_{1}^{-1}a_{3}^{-1}\rangle,

which plainly kills
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}
. Hence

G(0,0,0)=1.

The other isolated solutions are contained in the cases
k_{1}=-1
or
k_{3}=-1
treated above, and the cyclic exponent is
\pm 1
in each case. Therefore
G(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})=1
for every determinant-
\pm 1
triple.

This proves the lemma.
∎

5
Generalized one-layer presentations and conjecture

Definition 5.1

(Graph-layer presentation)
.

Let

\Sigma_{g}=\left\langle x_{1},y_{1},\ldots,x_{g},y_{g}\ \middle|\ [x_{1},y_{1}]\cdots[x_{g},y_{g}]=1\right\rangle

be the genus
g
surface group. Let

\gamma:\Sigma_{g}\longrightarrow F(a_{1},\ldots,a_{g})

be the diagonal homomorphism defined by

\gamma(x_{i})=a_{i},\qquad\gamma(y_{i})=a_{i}.

Since

\gamma([x_{i},y_{i}])=[a_{i},a_{i}]=1,

this is a well-defined homomorphism.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{g}
denote the subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(\Sigma_{g})
generated by the Dehn twists

T_{ij},\qquad 1\leq i<j\leq g,

with the relations between these twists given in Section
2
.
A
graph-layer word
is a word

\Omega=T_{i_{m}j_{m}}^{k_{m}}\cdots T_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{k_{1}}

in these twists. The associated
graph-layer presentation
is

G(\Omega)=\left\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{g}\ \middle|\ \gamma\Omega(y_{1}),\ldots,\gamma\Omega(y_{g})\right\rangle.

Here
\Omega
is considered up to the defining relations among the twists
T_{ij}
. In particular, different words representing the same element of
\mathcal{T}_{g}
define the same graph-layer presentation. The graph is the simple support graph.

Definition 5.2

(Recursively peelable twist element)
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathcal{T}_{g}
be represented by a word in the twists
T_{ij}
.
We say that
\Omega
is
recursively peelable
if, after using the defining relations among the twists
T_{ij}
, it has a representative which can be constructed recursively as follows.

1.

The empty word is recursively peelable.

2.

If
U
and
V
are recursively peelable words supported on disjoint sets of vertices, then their product
UV
is recursively peelable. Equivalently, disconnected components may be treated independently.

3.

In the connected case, a nonempty word is recursively peelable if it has a representative of the form

T_{uv}^{\alpha}\,U\,T_{uv}^{\beta},

where
v
is a leaf vertex,
u
is its unique neighbour,
U
is recursively peelable on the vertex set with
v
removed, and

(\alpha,\beta)\neq(0,0).

Thus, at each step, one peels off a leaf edge. The same leaf edge may appear on both sides of the smaller word, but it must appear only as the two outer boundary powers surrounding the smaller recursively peelable word.

Definition 5.3

(Generalized one-layer presentation)
.

A graph-layer presentation
G(\Omega)
is called a

generalized one-layer presentation
if the twist element

\Omega\in\mathcal{T}_{g}
is recursively peelable.

Equivalently,
G(\Omega)
is generalized one-layer if the word

\Omega
, after applying the relations among the twists
T_{ij}
,
admits a representative which can be reduced by repeatedly peeling off
leaf twists.

The ordinary one-layer presentations are recursively peelable. For example,
consider

\Omega=T_{34}^{k_{3}}T_{23}^{k_{2}}T_{12}^{k_{1}}.

Its support graph is the path

1-2-3-4.

One peels the leaves in the order

34,\qquad 23,\qquad 12.

Indeed,

\Omega=T_{34}^{k_{3}}\bigl(T_{23}^{k_{2}}T_{12}^{k_{1}}\bigr),

so the first step peels the leaf vertex
4
attached to
3
. The remaining
word

T_{23}^{k_{2}}T_{12}^{k_{1}}

then peels the leaf vertex
3
attached to
2
, and finally

T_{12}^{k_{1}}
peels the leaf vertex
2
attached to
1
. Thus every
strict one-layer path word is recursively peelable by peeling the path from an
endpoint.

The definition also allows nested repetitions of a leaf edge. For instance,

T_{12}^{k_{3}}T_{23}^{k_{2}}T_{12}^{k_{1}}

is recursively peelable, because it has the form

T_{12}^{k_{3}}\,\bigl(T_{23}^{k_{2}}\bigr)\,T_{12}^{k_{1}}.

Here one first peels the leaf edge
12
, with one power on each side of the
smaller word. This is why a repeated edge does not automatically make a word
higher-layer.

Disjoint commutations are also allowed. For example,

T_{34}^{k_{4}}T_{12}^{k_{3}}T_{34}^{k_{2}}T_{23}^{k_{1}}

is generalized one-layer, since
T_{34}
and
T_{12}
have disjoint
supports and commute. Hence it is equivalent to

T_{12}^{k_{3}}T_{34}^{k_{2}+k_{4}}T_{23}^{k_{1}}.

If
k_{2}+k_{4}=0
, the
34
-edge disappears; otherwise the resulting support
is the path
1-2-3-4
. In either case the word is recursively peelable.

On the other hand,

T_{23}^{k_{4}}T_{12}^{k_{3}}T_{23}^{k_{2}}T_{12}^{k_{1}}

has the interleaving edge pattern

23,\quad 12,\quad 23,\quad 12.

This is a genuine two-layer pattern unless the twist relations transform it
into a recursively peelable representative. Similarly, a triangle word such as

T_{13}^{k_{3}}T_{23}^{k_{2}}T_{12}^{k_{1}}

contains a genuine cycle in its support graph and should be regarded as a
cycle-layer word, not as a generalized one-layer word, unless the twist
relations make it peelable.

Thus the principle is that generalized one-layer words are precisely
those twist elements which, after using the available twist relations, admit a
leaf-peeling representative. Repeated vertices are allowed, and even repeated
edges are allowed when they occur in a nested peelable way; what is excluded is
non-peelable interleaving or a genuine cycle.

Definition 5.4

(Framed graph-layer presentation)
.

Let

\Omega=T_{i_{m}j_{m}}^{k_{m}}\cdots T_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{k_{1}}

be a graph-layer word in the twists
T_{ij}
, and let

\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{g})\in\mathbb{Z}^{g}.

Put

V_{\mathbf{m}}=T_{x_{1}}^{m_{1}}\cdots T_{x_{g}}^{m_{g}}.

The associated framed graph-layer presentation is

G(\mathbf{m},\Omega)=\left\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{g}\ \middle|\ \gamma V_{\mathbf{m}}\Omega(y_{1}),\ldots,\gamma V_{\mathbf{m}}\Omega(y_{g})\right\rangle.

Since the twists
T_{x_{i}}
commute with the paper twists
T_{ij}
, this presentation is equivalently

G(\mathbf{m},\Omega)=\left\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{g}\ \middle|\ a_{1}^{m_{1}}\gamma\Omega(y_{1}),\ldots,a_{g}^{m_{g}}\gamma\Omega(y_{g})\right\rangle.

Definition 5.5

(Framed generalized one-layer presentation)
.

A framed graph-layer presentation
G(\mathbf{m},\Omega)
is called
a framed generalized one-layer presentation if the underlying
graph-layer word
\Omega
is generalized one-layer, i.e. if

\Omega
is equivalent, using the defining relations among the
twists
T_{ij}
, to a recursively peelable word.

All the presentation obtained from a component-surjective homomorphism
\phi
such that
\phi(x_{i})=b_{i}a_{i},\phi(y_{i})=v(a)
are framed graph-layer presentations.

Conjecture 5.6
.

If a (framed) graph-layer presentation defines a trivial group, then it is a (framed) generalized one-layer presentation.

is allowed cyclically across the chosen initial point of the boundary of a
principal region.

Proof.

Take a parallel copy of the path labelled

Starting from a reduced Van Kampen diagram over the cyclically reduced
relators

incident to the corresponding part of a complete period. If such a surgery
cannot be performed because the opposite path changes neighbour before the

marked incidence; all degree estimates below only improve. If the missing
prefix lies on the exterior boundary, it is left untouched, and again it
contributes an additional exterior incidence. Thus the worst case for the

After completion we use the following marked contours:

\boxed{R_{1}^{\sharp}=a_{1}\;Q^{\ell}\;X^{\ell},}

\boxed{R_{2}^{\sharp}=P^{-(k-1)}\;a_{3}^{-1}\;a_{2}\;Q^{\ell}\;a_{3}\;A^{k-1}\;X^{\ell},}

Lemma 6.19

we impose

Then

admits the two-generator presentation

{\overline{K}\cong\left\langle A,D\ \middle|\ D^{2k+1},\;\left(A^{2}D^{k}A^{-1}\right)^{2l-1},\;\left(A^{2}D^{-1}\right)^{l},\;\left(AD^{-k}A^{-1}D^{-k}\right)^{k}\right\rangle.}

Moreover, the eliminated generators are

{B=A,\qquad Y=A^{2}D^{-1},\qquad C=AD^{-k}A^{-1}D^{-k},\qquad X=\left(A^{2}D^{k}A^{-1}\right)^{2}.}

Proof.

We use the convention

For the genus-four presentation with exponent pairs

(l,-l),\qquad(-1,1),\qquad(k,-1-k),

the corresponding six-generator presentation in the Dehn-twist generators

is

\displaystyle K\cong\langle X,Y,A,B,C,D\mid

\displaystyle Y=X^{l}Y^{-l}B^{-1}X^{l}AY^{-l},

\displaystyle A=Y^{-l}B^{-1}X^{l}AD^{k+1}B^{-1}C^{-k}A,

\displaystyle C=AD^{k+1}B^{-1}C^{-k}D^{k+1}C^{-k},

\displaystyle X=Y^{-l}X^{l}AY^{-l}B^{-1}X^{l},

\displaystyle B=X^{l}AY^{-l}B^{-1}C^{-k}AD^{k+1}B^{-1},

\displaystyle D=B^{-1}C^{-k}AD^{k+1}C^{-k}D^{k+1}\rangle.

Now pass to the quotient

\overline{K}=K/\left\langle\!\left\langle Y^{l},\ C^{k}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle.

Thus in
\overline{K}
we impose

Y^{l}=1,\qquad C^{k}=1.

Hence
Y^{-l}=1
and
C^{-k}=1
, and the six relations reduce to

Y=X^{l}B^{-1}X^{l}A,

A=B^{-1}X^{l}AD^{k+1}B^{-1}A,

C=AD^{k+1}B^{-1}D^{k+1},

X=X^{l}AB^{-1}X^{l},

B=X^{l}AB^{-1}AD^{k+1}B^{-1},

and

D=B^{-1}AD^{2k+2}.

We now eliminate
B,X,Y,C
in terms of
A
and
D
.

From

we get

X^{1-l}=AB^{-1}X^{l},

and hence

X^{1-2l}=AB^{-1}.

Therefore

B^{-1}=A^{-1}X^{1-2l},

so

B=X^{2l-1}A.

Using this in the last relation

D=B^{-1}AD^{2k+2},

we obtain

D=A^{-1}X^{1-2l}AD^{2k+2}.

Multiplying on the right by
D^{-2k-2}
, we get

D^{-2k-1}=A^{-1}X^{1-2l}A.

Equivalently,

X^{1-2l}=AD^{-2k-1}A^{-1}.

Next, use the second relation

A=B^{-1}X^{l}AD^{k+1}B^{-1}A.

Substituting
B^{-1}=A^{-1}X^{1-2l}
, we get

A=A^{-1}X^{1-l}AD^{k+1}A^{-1}X^{1-2l}A.

Using

A^{-1}X^{1-2l}A=D^{-2k-1},

this becomes

A=A^{-1}X^{1-l}AD^{-k}.

Hence

X^{1-l}=A^{2}D^{k}A^{-1}.

Now use the fifth relation

B=X^{l}AB^{-1}AD^{k+1}B^{-1}.

After substituting

B=X^{2l-1}A,\qquad B^{-1}=A^{-1}X^{1-2l},

we obtain

X^{2l-1}A=X^{1-l}AD^{k+1}A^{-1}X^{1-2l}.

Using

X^{1-l}=A^{2}D^{k}A^{-1},\qquad X^{1-2l}=AD^{-2k-1}A^{-1},

the right-hand side simplifies to
A
. Thus

X^{2l-1}A=A.

Therefore

X^{2l-1}=1.

But we also have

X^{1-2l}=AD^{-2k-1}A^{-1}.

Since
X^{2l-1}=1
, it follows that
X^{1-2l}=1
, and hence

AD^{-2k-1}A^{-1}=1.

Thus

Since
D^{2k+1}=1
, the relation

X^{1-2l}=AD^{-2k-1}A^{-1}

gives

X^{1-2l}=1.

Hence

X^{2l-1}=1.

Consequently,

Substituting this into

, we obtain

\left(A^{2}D^{k}A^{-1}\right)^{2l}=A^{2}D^{k}A^{-1}.

Therefore

\left(A^{2}D^{k}A^{-1}\right)^{2l-1}=1.

Now the first relation gives

Y=X^{l}B^{-1}X^{l}A.

Since
X^{2l-1}=1
, we have

B=X^{2l-1}A=A.

Therefore

Y=X^{l}A^{-1}X^{l}A.

Using

X^{l}=A^{2}D^{k}A^{-1},

we get

Since
D^{2k+1}=1
, this becomes

Because

in
\overline{K}
, we obtain the relator

\left(A^{2}D^{-1}\right)^{l}=1.

Finally, the third relation gives

C=AD^{k+1}B^{-1}D^{k+1}.

Since
B=A
, this becomes

C=AD^{k+1}A^{-1}D^{k+1}.

Using
D^{2k+1}=1
, we have

D^{k+1}=D^{-k}.

Thus

C=AD^{-k}A^{-1}D^{-k}.

Because
C^{k}=1
in
\overline{K}
, we obtain

\left(AD^{-k}A^{-1}D^{-k}\right)^{k}=1.

We have therefore eliminated
B,X,Y,C
, leaving only the generators

A
and
D
. The remaining relators are

\left(A^{2}D^{k}A^{-1}\right)^{2l-1}=1,

\left(A^{2}D^{-1}\right)^{l}=1,

and

\left(AD^{-k}A^{-1}D^{-k}\right)^{k}=1.

Therefore

\overline{K}\cong\left\langle A,D\ \middle|\ D^{2k+1},\;\left(A^{2}D^{k}A^{-1}\right)^{2l-1},\;\left(A^{2}D^{-1}\right)^{l},\;\left(AD^{-k}A^{-1}D^{-k}\right)^{k}\right\rangle.

∎

All the remaining cases can be considered similarly. For special parameters when the quotients are trivial, we substitute them in the original group and prove existence of L2-quotients or finite simple quotients using Magma. This proves Theorem
