Effectiveness of couple-based maternity education on husbands’ involvement in maternity continuum of care service utilization in rural communities of Central Ethiopia: Protocol of cluster randomized controlled trial
Authors: Lonsako Abute Woiloro, Takele Tadesse Adafrie, Amene Abebe Kerbo, Mengistu Meskele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the availability of high-impact maternal survival interventions along the continuum of care strategies, maternal mortality remains the major tragedy in resource poor settings. In low income countries interventions addressing role of husband and its effect on maternal health is not well studied, particularly in rural set up of Ethiopia. Therefore this study is designed to evaluate the effect of couple-based maternity education on husband involvement on maternity continuum of care.MethodsWe will use a cluster randomized controlled trial of two parallel groups to evaluate the effectiveness of couple-based maternity education on improving husband involvement in maternity continuum of care. Sample size is calculated by using G-power 3.1 and a 1:1 allocation ratio will be used. Sixteen kebeles will be randomly assigned to 8 interventions and 8 control groups with 248 couples in each group. Women who are in the early stages of their second trimester with their husbands will participate in the study. Group maternity education and home visits will be the intervention packages. Data analysis will be performed with an intention-to-treat analysis approach and will be analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 software. An independent effect of the intervention on the outcome of the interest will be assessed using multivariable logistic regression with generalized estimating equation model.ConclusionThis trial result will generate conclusive evidence to policy makers about the effectiveness of couple-based maternity education to empower husband involvement in maternity continuum of care in rural Ethiopia.Trial registrationThis trial was prospectively registered with the African Clinical Trials Registry on 04 December 2025 (PACTR202512868895726).Keywords: Couple, Maternity education, Husband involvement, Continuum, Ethiopia

Introduction

Globally, the rates of maternal and infant mortality are on the rise again in many countries and pregnancy-related avoidable causes claimed the lives of one woman every 2 min. Maternal mortality ratio (MMR) of 223 deaths per 100,000 livebirths indicates that an average of 712 women die every day during pregnancy or childbirth, one woman every 2 min [1]. This report finds that a staggering 260,000 women died indicates that the world remains far apart from the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal of reducing maternal mortality to 70 deaths per 100,000 live births by 2030 [2,3]. It is fact that the majority of maternal and newborn fatalities happen during delivery and the first few hours after giving birth and when care is either delivered in inadequately equipped or inappropriately located healthcare facilities [3,4].

Even though MCH is determined by many factors including socio-economic status, healthcare facilities, the lack of women's autonomy and environmental factors, one important factor that has been neglected over the years is the husband's role [5,6]. Different study findings reveal that in underdeveloped nations pregnancy and delivery are viewed as women's domain and husbands are typically not expected to accompany their wives to the prenatal clinic, be there during child birth, or assist with postnatal care. Husbands usually make the decisions about a woman's access to healthcare, including when and where it should be provided [[5], [6], [7]]. Study finding from different resource poor countries indicate that there is low husband involvement on mCoC. In Nigeria 24% involved during ANC and 27.1% participated on delivery service utilization [8], in Kenya only 18% of husbands involved in ANC [9,10] and in Ghana 44% and 23% in Rwanda of the husbands accompanied their partners to skilled delivery service [11,12].

Ethiopia is one of the country with poor husband involvement in MCH with only 19.7% husbands' participated on ANC in Eastern part of the Ethiopia, in Bale zone Oromia region 41.4% and Northern part of Ethiopia, Gondar city 40.1% of the husbands' participated on ANC [[13], [14], [15]]. Access and use of mCoC services are essential for the survival and wellbeing of the mother and newborn and can avert 71% of MMR but it can be reduced to 37% if one of the services is missing from the mCoC [16]. There is also high gap in completion of CoC as we go from ANC to PNC in Ethiopia and only 9.1% of mothers completed the CoC and studies across different regions reveal significant gaps on the completion of care [17]. In West Gondar zone, North West Ethiopia only 6.19% [18], in Arba Minch Zuriya Woreda, Gamo zone 9.7% [19], in Guragie zone, Southwest Ethiopia 15.5% [20], and 11.3% in Hadiya zone, Southern Ethiopia complete continuum of care [21].

International communities like ICPD and WHO have put forward the involvement of husbands in mCoC as an essential intervention strategy to improve maternal and neonatal health outcomes and formally recognized the dynamic role of husbands in promoting mother and child health [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. Ethiopia's MoH also stated that husbands should be encouraged to participate in the country's reproductive health strategy plan as well as to be change agents in their families and communities, supporting women's and children's health [26]. Study findings highlight that husband engagement can encourage and assist in changing their behavior, which can enhance health care-seeking, at-home care practices and lead to greater levels of love, happiness, and emotional closeness in partnerships. They can act as leaders in their communities, religions, politics and as they have the power to alter societal norms and behaviors [8,24,27].

Nevertheless, the value of actively involving husbands in mCoC practice is becoming more widely acknowledged, research shows that a substantial part of husbands do not participated in mCoC in Ethiopia [28,29]. Majority of studies in SSA including Ethiopia give much concern on the view point of women, barriers for the maternal health service utilization and factors related with women. The husband's perspective is mostly ignored part by researchers as well as by programmers and behavioral change interventions also narrowly addresses the women part. This has led to husbands being marginalized on the involvement in MCH and make challenge to put programs and strategies into practice [[30], [31], [32]].

It is vital to adapt interventions in a specific cultural context that involve both pregnant women and their husbands. This interventional study is designed to provide opportunity to promote and empower husband involvement on mCoC and helpful to achieve SDG 3.1 to reduce MMR of 70/100,000 by the year 2030 [33]. Therefore, this cluster-randomized trial is designed to evaluate the effectiveness of couple-based maternity education on husbands’ involvement in mCoC service utilization in rural communities of Central Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design

A two arm parallel-group cluster randomized controlled trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio is designed to examine whether the couple based maternity education through group maternity education and home visit counseling to increases husbands’ involvement in maternity continuum of care. The randomized controlled trial protocol was developed using the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials guidelines and the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials Statement: Extension to Cluster Randomized Trials. It was prospectively registered with the pan African Clinical Trials Registry as PACTR202512868895726 on 04 December 2025.

Study setting

The study will be carried out in the Hadiya Zone, central Ethiopia and it is located 230 km far from Addis Ababa. Hadiya Zone is one of the seven administrative zones of the central Ethiopia regional state. It is located on the latitude of 7.5 and longitude of 37.75 and divided into 13 rural districts and four administrative towns with total of 329 kebeles from which 303 of them were rural (the lowest administrative units) in Ethiopia. In the zone, there are a total of 1,727,920 people (49.56% males and 50.44% females) with an estimated pregnant mothers of 59,613 [34].

Eligibility criteria

A couple whose wife with in the beginning of second trimester of pregnancy and living together, who had a child of under 3 years, stayed in the study area for at least six months before starting the intervention and volunteered to take part in couple-based maternal education will be eligible to participate in this trial. In addition to that couples should have to be a permanent residents in the study area for at least one year after recruitment since we are going to make intervention for six months and there is also data collection to evaluate the effect of intervention on husbands and pregnant mothers behavioral change regards to maternity continuum of care service utilization. Couples who are seriously ill or who intend to leave the intervention and control groups within the next six months will be excluded from the trial.

Participant identification and recruitment

Recruitment

To identify the eligible pregnant women, kebele Health Extension Workers logbook will be identified after randomization of clusters. In addition to HEW logbook, pregnancy screening will be conducted to address those pregnant women at community by using pregnancy screening questions adapted from the Kenya family planning enrolment questionnaire [35] (Table 1). If the mother responses "yes," for at least one of the six screening questions, pregnancy will be ruled out. If a woman responses "no" for the six pregnancy screening questions, she will be suspected for pregnancy and will be linked to a nearby healthcare facility (a hospital, health center, or clinic) for a pregnancy test. Once pregnancy is detected, the woman and her husband will be recruited in a sampling frame for the trial study. All the randomization of the clusters into two groups will be done before identifying the couples in the trial. Both at the individual and cluster levels will informed consent be obtained for those who fulfilled the eligibility criteria and they will be contacted and enrolled in trial in November, 2025.

Randomization

In this study, the cluster is kebele, will serve as the unit of randomization, and the units of observation will be couples with women in the beginning of the second trimester of pregnancy within the cluster. Since participants in control group might have contact with intervention groups in social events (marketing and burial ceremonies), we will use buffer zone and well separation approaches, leaving at least one cluster between non-adjacent clusters to minimize information contamination. Then, the clusters (kebeles) will be listed alphabetically by their names and closed in an opaque envelope with their ID. A list of random numbers will be generated in MS Excel 2010, and the generated values will be fixed by copying them as ‘values’ next to the alphabetic list of the clusters. The produced random number will then be used to sort these in ascending order. Ultimately, depending on the generated random numbers, the clusters will be divided into two, first half of the clusters and the second half of the clusters.

The clusters will be randomly assigned as intervention and control group and the allocation sequence will be made by a statistician who is blind to the study groups and not participating in the research. Finally the study arms will be similarly distributed due to the classification and randomization approaches that will distribute actual and potential confounding factors similarly throughout the arms. Couples who gratified the requirements for inclusion will be assigned to the same arm within a cluster (either the control or intervention arms). Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) for cluster randomized trials criteria will be used to report the study's findings (Fig: 1) [36].

Participant study flow.

Allocation concealment and blinding

The study's hypothesis will be hidden from participants and data collectors. Additionally, by coding the groups, the data input clerk will be rendered blind. Zonal and district health officials in particular, who serve as cluster gatekeepers, would be told not to intervene in the control clusters until the study is completed.

The intervention

We will have two parts of intervention comprises of group maternity education and individual home visit. Couples in the intervention group will receive the intervention at five different points of contact. Group maternity education will be the first and third encounters, while home visits will be the second, fourth and fifth. The couples in the control groups continue receiving the usual care. Health professionals will administer the intervention after receiving thorough training on it.

Group education

All couples in a cluster participating in the intervention group will be congregated in one location and undergo two sessions of group maternity education. Health extension workers in coordination with kebele leaders and participants will chose the location for health education. At the first month of intervention period, group maternity education will be delivered for pregnant women and their husbands focusing on the importance of husband's participation in ANC and the services they provide during antenatal care visits, pregnancy risk indicators and difficulties, the benefits of institutional birth, and the advantages of postnatal care visit. It will center on the importance of open communication between partners throughout the pregnancy, the role of the husband in accompanying his partner to the health institution for prenatal care, delivery, and PNC, the need for emotional support during pregnancy, and providing financial support, including transportation to the health institution during pregnancy.

Additionally, as part of maternity education, husbands will be advised to assist with childbirth-related expenses such as baby clothes, medical fees, and transportation, as well as help with household chores like washing clothes, cleaning, and meal preparation. They will also be expected to contribute to baby care duties. Following talks with participants, a recommended timetable for home visits will be developed, with a focus on contacting couples at their home.

The second phase of group maternity education will be delivered at the third month of intervention period. In this period of the intervention, the revision of previous session, question and answer, using of a training manual, group discussion regards to husbands' involvement towards maternity continuum of care will be taken. Husbands’ perception and attitude towards their participation on maternity continuum of care will be addressed. In addition to that their intention and readiness to accompany their wives for ANC, skilled delivery and PNC will be raised and husbands practice to accompany his wife to antenatal care will be assessed. Participants will stay one and half hour (90 min) per group maternity education with a total of 3 h (180 min) since they have two contact. (PDF) S2 File. Study protocol.

Individual home visit

Individual home visits will be offered three times, at the second, fourth and fifth months of the intervention period. The first phase of individual home visit will be delivered one month following the first phase of maternity education intervention (i.e., at the second month of the intervention period). Couples will receive counseling on prenatal care and spouse support during this visit. Husbands will receive guidance on how to assist their wives and motivate them to seek prenatal care, give birth in a health institution, and postpartum visit to the health institution to have the mother and infant checked out will all be topics of discussion.

After the second phase of group maternity education, or at the fourth month of the intervention, will be the time of the second home visit. In this visit, couples will be evaluated about attending ANC together, their choice of birth place, their intention to attend a PNC, and their plan for getting to the closest health facility in the event of complications or emergencies during delivery or the postpartum period. Moreover, couples will be questioned about their birth preparation and complication readiness and their emotional feelings on participation in maternal and child health. Husbands will be urged to assist their wives in the home and to participate in institutional delivery. The last phase of intervention package will be home visit that will be happened at the fifth month of intervention period. In this phase revision of the previous session, question and answer and husbands will be asked about their emotional feelings and readiness to be involved during an institutional delivery and postnatal care. A written invitation letter will be given to the couples, along with an approximate expected delivery date, to help them remember the appointment. One individual home visit contact will take 1 h (60 min) and there are a total of three contact with 3 h (180 min). (PDF) S2 File, Study protocol. The study protocol was developed based on the guidance of the SPIRIT (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials) checklist S1 Table.

Control group (usual standard of care)

In this trial, the control group means the group that is on usual standard care. That means there are usual standard health promotion and disease prevention services that are delivered to public healthcare in rural communities of Ethiopia. These usual standard care services are going to be delivered through health extension workers in Ethiopia, and it includes infection prevention, family planning, personal and environmental hygiene, and health information and communication for all families (control and intervention group) [37]. Those in the control group did not receive couple-based maternity education intervention packages, but those in intervention group will receive couple-based maternity education intervention packages such as group maternity education and individual home visits.

Intervention fidelity

We will implement the following actions to enhance the intervention's fidelity. A trainer manual has already been developed; extensive training will be provided for health professionals, spot checks will be conducted by field supervisors during each intervention session; and feedback meetings will be held after each session with individuals who give intervention [38]. A fidelity checklist that addresses adherence and intensity of the intervention will be used to evaluate intervention fidelity at the end of each session.

Compliance assessment

Eligible couples in the intervention clusters will be repeatedly visited in a case of absence to solve the problem of compliance to a full intervention package. The number of participants who attended two group maternity education and contacted at the three home visits will be used to determine the participants' adherence with the intervention. Compliance checklist will be used to track the level of couple's participation to our proposed intervention package. Interventionists will use the tracking database to carefully document each contact with participants, including the type (in person, phone). This will enable us to determine the total number and type of contacts per participant. To account for variances, the intervention and control groups from each district will have an equal number of clusters, and an equal number of participants per cluster will be selected. The assessment will focus on how husband involvement on maternity education changes their involvement in skilled delivery service utilization and skilled delivery service utilization of pregnant women. In the control arm, there is no active intervention (only passive materials, usual care) so we cannot assess adherence or engagement.

Outcome Measures & Time points.

Outcome measure

The outcome considered in this study will be change in husband involvement in skilled delivery service utilization and skilled delivery service utilization. The outcome will be measured for each trial arm at the end line.

Husband involvement in the maternity continuum of care: The concept of husband involvement is composite/latent variable that can be affected by many socio-cultural, traditional and behavioral factors which cannot be measured by a single variable. To measure husband involvement on maternity continuum of care we will use Global framework that developed by an international Delphi study for assessing male involvement in maternity continuum of care. It is standardized and valid tool that contains 5 parameters and includes husbands involvement in communication with their wives on the issues of pregnancy and child birth, husbands involvement by taking part in decision-making about the utilization of ANC, place delivery and PNC, physical involvement of husbands by accompany his wife to ANC, skilled delivery and PNC and practical involvement of husbands on house hold chores, child caring and emotional support of their pregnant wife during prenatal, delivery and post-natal periods. It will be given 1 if the response is ‘Yes’ and 0 if ‘No’ response. Then they will be sum up and classified as “Good involvement” if the respondent scores ≥11 from 21 items and “Poor involvement” if the respondent scores <11 out of 21 items [39].

Husband involvement in the skilled delivery service utilization: Husband involvement in the skilled delivery service utilization will be measured by using standardized and valid tool that contains 5 parameters and includes husbands involvement in communication with their wives about child birth, husbands involvement by taking part in decision-making about the place delivery, physical involvement of husbands by accompany his wife to skilled delivery and practical involvement of husbands on house hold chores, newborn caring and emotional support for the wife during child birth. It will be given 1 if the response is ‘Yes’ and 0 if ‘No’ response. Then they will be sum up and classified as “Good involvement” if the respondent scores above the mean and “Poor involvement” if the respondent scores below the mean [39].

Skilled delivery service utilization: Skilled delivery service utilization will be measured based on women who gave birth in health center and hospital by assistance of health professionals that have midwifery skills including Midwife, Nurse, Health Officers and Doctors as reported by the participant [40].

Sample size determination and procedures

Sample size determination

The sample size estimates will be calculated to detect change in the proportion of husband involvement in skilled delivery service utilization by using G-power 3.1 program for Fisher's exact test based on the following assumptions. Tail (s): One; effect size (d): 0.15; α error probability = 0.05; power (1-β error probability) = 0.8, and 1 for ‘allocation ratio N2/N1’. This gives a sample size of 143 in each arm. To account for cluster design, DE = 1 + ICC (m-1) = 1.58 [41], where assumed cluster size (m) of 30, ICC = 0.02 [42], and allowing for a 10% loss to follow up, the total sample size is 496 (N1 = 248 and N2 = 248). N1 and N2 are sample sizes in the control and intervention group, respectively. Based on these assumptions, the estimated sample size is: 248 pregnant women with their husbands per group to detect a 15-percentage point anticipated difference in the proportion of husband involvement in skilled delivery service utilization (an increase from P1 = 52.99% to P2 = 67.99%) [43].

Sampling procedures

From the total of 13 rural districts in Hadiya Zone, 2 rural districts (Ameka and Lemo) will be selected purposively for a randomized control trial study. There are about 36 rural kebeles in the selected two districts. Sixteen geographically nonadjacent clusters will be identified randomly from the existing kebeles and will be divided into eight intervention arms and eight control arms. A pregnancy test will be done for the study women at the beginning of the surveillance. The intervention and control groups will be assigned to these clusters at random. In each cluster, there will be on average 30 pregnant women with their husbands who will participate, making 248 couples per arm.

Data quality control

To ensure data quality, data collectors and supervisors will be trained, and regular monitoring and follow-up will be done by supervisors and principal investigators. In addition, data integrity and consistency will be checked regularly on a daily basis. The questionnaire is translated into Hadiyyisa (local language) and then into English by a translator who is language expert and unfamiliar with the original questionnaire. Pilot testing of the translated tool will be conducted on those who will not be included in the actual trial to assess its understanding, comprehension, and appropriateness. Additionally, subject experts will evaluate the content, and the necessary changes will be made. The tools' internal consistency will be tested using Cronbach's alpha. A tool with a Crombach alpha of >0.7 will be considered reliable [44]. Finally, the questionnaires will be utilized at the study's baseline and completion.

Data management

Regular supervision, check-up and follow-up of the data will be done on a daily basis by supervisors and the principal investigator. Every day, data collected by KoBo Collect (the mobile application) will be checked for completeness and consistency before being sent to the server (KoBo Toolbox). The supervisor and principal investigator will consider the following points during the checkup of the collected data: the length of time to finish each survey, the collection of Global Positioning System (GPS) points, and their random distribution. Trial participants who are loss to follow up will be listed along with their reasons. Only the study team will have access to the data set. The anonymous record and coding of personal data will not be disclosed to any third party to ensure strict secrecy. Data will be stored on a password-protected data server (KoBo Toolbox) and only accessible by the research team.

Data analysis

The data will be entered in to Epi Data Version 3.1 and then exported in to SPSS version 25.0. The outcomes variable is dichotomous and will be analyzed with logistic regression. Data of baseline and end-line surveys will be combined and analyzed using SPSS version 25.0. Analyses will be based on the intention-to-treat principle and compared differences of the outcomes between the intervention and control at both individual and cluster levels. First, Univariate analyses will be performed to explore the characteristics of respondents. Then we will use logistic regression with random effects to estimate the effect of the intervention on husband involvement in maternal health behavior, a model of longitudinal data analysis, the GEE (Generalized Estimating Equation) will be used to test the independent effect of the intervention. During the analysis, the effect size, confidence interval, and p-value will be calculated. The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) will be used to assess model fitness and its values will be calculated for each model and compared. A model with lower diagnostic criteria (deviance or −2 x log-likelihood ratio) will be used as the best-fitted model. Sensitivity analysis will be conducted to assess the robustness of the intervention effect by changing the assumptions made in the model, such as the working correlation structure, outliers, missing data, and controlling potential confounders.

Data monitoring

The researchers will form a group of supervisors to monitor and audit the data-gathering process at baseline and end line evaluations.

Plan of dissemination

The trial results will be shared with concerned stakeholders in the health sector and other governmental and non-governmental organizations. The findings will also be disseminated to scientific scholars through publications and conference presentations.

Discussion

In Ethiopia, husband involvement on maternity continuum of care is low and its rates vary across regions in the country. Majority of studies in Ethiopia give concern on the view point and barriers of women regards to maternal health service utilization, and the husband's perspective is mostly ignored part. This has led to husbands being marginalized in the involvement in MCH and make challenge to put programs and strategies into practice. Hence, this CRT aims to examine the effect of CBME on husband involvement in maternity continuum of care service utilization in rural Ethiopia. The findings also may help policymakers and health practitioners to design viable strategies to enhance husband involvement in the maternity continuum of care that used as an input to improve maternal and child health seeking behavior in rural Ethiopia.
