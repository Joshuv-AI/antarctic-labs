The role of metacognition in fibromyalgia impact and psychological distress: a cross-sectional study in Turkish women with fibromyalgia
Authors: Yunus Bayram Koçyiğit, Belgin Karaoğlan, Aslı Kuruoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We examined whether metacognitive beliefs were associated with fibromyalgia impact and compared metacognitive beliefs between patients with Fibromyalgia Syndrome (FMS) and matched healthy controls. We hypothesized that Metacognitions Questionnaire-30 (MCQ-30) scores would be higher in FMS and would show a significant association with Revised Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQR) total scores. This cross-sectional study included 56 women with FMS and 69 healthy women controls who were comparable in age and education level. Participants completed MCQ-30, the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), and FIQR. Group differences were evaluated using independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, depending on distributional characteristics. Associations between variables were examined using Pearson’s correlations or Spearman’s rank correlations. The association between MCQ-30 total scores and FIQR total scores was tested using simple linear regression. Analytical assumptions were evaluated, and the robustness of the findings was examined using Huber M-regression. Compared with controls, the FMS group had higher scores on MCQ-30 Cognitive Confidence, Uncontrollability/Danger, Cognitive Self-Consciousness, Need to Control Thoughts, and MCQ-30 total scores (t = 3.610–4.805, all p < .001; d = 0.66–0.88). The FMS group also had higher scores across all FIQR indices (p values ranging from .020 to < .001; d = 0.42–2.60). In the patient group, FIQR total scores were positively associated with MCQ-30 total scores (r = .395, p < .01). MCQ-30 total scores were significantly associated with FIQR total scores (B = 0.417, SE = 0.173, β = .31, R2 = .10, p = .020; 95% CI = 0.078–0.756), and this association was retained in Huber M-regression (B ≈ 0.40, p < .05). In exploratory subgroup analyses, significant differences in MCQ-30 and FIQR scores were observed between patients with moderate-to-severe depressive/anxiety symptoms, as defined by BDI/BAI cutoffs, and those below these thresholds. MCQ-30 scores were higher in FMS than in healthy controls and were associated with disease impact. These findings suggest that metacognitive beliefs may represent a hypothesis-generating process marker related to symptom perception and functioning in FMS; however, causal inferences cannot be drawn because of the cross-sectional design. The unexpected subgroup pattern should be interpreted cautiously and examined in longitudinal studies using pre-specified moderator analyses.

Introduction

Fibromyalgia Syndrome (FMS) is a chronic and potentially disabling condition whose etiology and pathogenesis remain incompletely understood; it is characterized by widespread pain, disturbances in sleep and energy, cognitive complaints, and affective symptoms1. Integrative approaches emphasizing the interaction of biological, genetic, psychosocial and sociocultural factors in the development and maintenance of FMS have gained increasing importance2.

Patients with FMS tend to use dysfunctional strategies in coping with pain, such as pain catastrophizing, which is closely associated with psychological distress3. While catastrophizing reflects the negative thought content related to pain, metacognitive beliefs provide a higher-level framework that explains how these thoughts are evaluated, to what extent they are perceived as threatening, and whether the individual attempts to control these thoughts. Metacognition refers to an individual’s awareness of the events and processes occurring in their own mind, as well as their beliefs about these processes4.

According to the metacognitive model, dysfunctional metacognitive beliefs may maintain the Cognitive Attentional Syndrome (CAS). CAS is characterized by threat monitoring, worry/rumination, the directing of attention toward bodily sensations, and rigid coping efforts aimed at controlling thoughts or symptoms. In the context of FMS, these processes may contribute to a more intense experience of disease impact through increased attention to, and threat perception of, bodily sensations such as pain and fatigue5,6.

Although catastrophizing, depression, anxiety, and other psychological variables have been examined relatively more extensively in FMS, studies directly addressing the role of metacognitive beliefs remain limited7–9. This gap is particularly evident in Turkish clinical samples.

The aim of this study was to examine metacognitive beliefs in women with FMS in comparison with healthy controls and to evaluate the association between metacognitive beliefs and fibromyalgia disease impact. In addition, exploratory subgroup analyses based on the severity of depressive and anxiety symptoms were conducted to investigate patterns linking these symptoms with metacognitive beliefs and disease impact. We hypothesized that metacognitive belief scores would be higher in the FMS group than in controls and that MCQ-30 total scores would be positively associated with FIQR total scores.

Materials and methods

Study setting and design

This study is a cross-sectional research conducted at Gazi University Faculty of Medicine Hospital.

Participants

Patients aged 18–65 years who had been diagnosed with FMS in the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation at Gazi University Faculty of Medicine, together with a healthy control group for comparison, were included in the study. Psychiatric disorders were excluded using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5). Individuals with organic diseases that could affect cognitive functioning were not included, and participants using psychotropic medication were also excluded. The healthy control group consisted of participants who had no psychiatric complaints, did not use psychotropic medication, and had no pain-related organic diseases.

During the study period, a total of 75 patients were evaluated for the FMS group; 7 patients were excluded because of antidepressant use and 12 because they did not complete the scales fully, resulting in 56 patients being included in the analysis. In the control group, 75 healthy volunteers were evaluated; 6 individuals who did not complete the scales fully were excluded, and 69 controls were included in the analysis. All participants were women. To control for sex, only women were included in the control group as well. The mean age was 39.14 years in the patient group and 38.67 years in the control group, with no significant difference between the groups.

The education levels of both groups were matched, and the groups consisted of high school and university graduates. Participants with relatively higher education levels were selected to reduce potential confounding, as lower educational attainment has been associated with worse pain and functional outcomes in fibromyalgia10.

Patients were informed about the study and written consent was obtained. Following the initial interview, all participants completed Metacognitions Questionnaire-30 (MCQ-30), originally developed by Wells and Cartwright-Hatton4 and subsequently validated in Turkish populations by Tosun and Irak11. Disease impact was measured with the Turkish version of the Revised Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQR), which was originally developed by Bennett et al.12 and validated in Turkish populations by Ediz et al.13. Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI; Beck et al., 1961), and anxiety symptoms were assessed with the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI; Beck et al., 1988).

Efforts to address potential sources of bias included the exclusion of current psychiatric diagnoses through SCID-5 assessment, restriction to female participants to control for gender effects, and matching the control group on age and education. Measurement bias was minimized by using validated Turkish versions of all scales (MCQ-30, FIQR, BDI, BAI). Residual sources of selection bias cannot be fully excluded due to the single-center design.

Sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients diagnosed with fibromyalgia at Gazi University Hospital during the recruitment period (n = 56). No a priori power calculation was conducted; therefore, findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Nevertheless, several observed group differences were in the medium-to-large range.

The unequal group sizes reflect the naturalistic recruitment of consecutive patients with fibromyalgia during the study period, whereas the control group was recruited on a voluntary basis under strict eligibility criteria. Although the groups were not numerically equal, they were matched on key demographic variables (age and education). Statistical analyses robust to unequal group sizes were applied, and assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were tested. Where appropriate, non-parametric tests were used.

The MCQ-30 has demonstrated good internal consistency in previous studies (Cronbach’s α ≈ 0.72–0.934,11. The FIQR has shown excellent reliability (α ≈ 0.95)12,13. The BDI and BAI have also demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α values typically above 0.85.

Example items include the following: for the MCQ-30, “I have little confidence in my memory for words and names” and “My worrying thoughts persist, no matter how I try to stop them”; for the FIQR, “How much has fibromyalgia interfered with your ability to do daily activities?” and “Please rate the level of your pain”; for the BDI, “I feel sad” and “I feel I am a failure”; and for the BAI, “I feel nervous” and “I feel unable to relax”.

Depressive symptoms were assessed with the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI; Beck et al., 1961). We classified severity according to established cut-offs: 0–13 minimal, 14–19 mild, 20–28 moderate, and 29–63 severe (Beck et al., 1988). Anxiety symptoms were evaluated using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI; Beck et al., 1988), with cut-offs of 0–7 minimal, 8–15 mild, 16–25 moderate, and 26–63 severe. For subgroup analyses, patients at moderate-to-severe levels (≥ 20 on BDI; ≥ 16 on BAI) were contrasted with those below these thresholds, in line with prior validation studies.

All quantitative variables (MCQ-30, FIQR, BDI, BAI scores; age; disease duration) were analyzed as continuous variables when assumptions of normality were met. Group comparisons used independent samples t-tests; if distributions were non-normal or subgroup sizes < 30, Mann–Whitney U tests were applied. For descriptive and subgroup purposes, depression and anxiety scores were additionally categorized according to established BDI and BAI severity cut-offs (minimal, mild, moderate, severe) to contrast patients with moderate-to-severe symptoms against those with lower severity. This grouping was chosen to ensure clinical interpretability and comparability with previous validation studies.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 23.0. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots. Homogeneity of variance was examined using Levene’s test. Between-group comparisons were conducted using independent-samples t-tests when assumptions were met, and Mann–Whitney U tests when assumptions were not met or when subgroup sample sizes were small. Categorical variables were evaluated using χ2 tests. Associations between continuous variables were examined using Pearson’s correlation or Spearman’s rank correlation, depending on distributional characteristics. The association between FIQR total scores and MCQ-30 total scores was tested using simple linear regression analysis. Regression assumptions and influential observations were examined using Cook’s distance and leverage values. In addition, Huber M-estimator regression and Spearman’s rank correlation were used to assess the robustness of the findings.

Assumption checks and robustness

Normality and homoscedasticity assumptions were examined via Q–Q plots and Levene’s test, and heteroskedasticity was probed using Breusch–Pagan tests. Influence diagnostics (Cook’s distance and leverage) were inspected to detect potential outliers. To assess robustness, we repeated key associations using Spearman’s rho and Huber M-estimator regressions; inferences remained qualitatively unchanged.

Ethical approval

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Gazi University, Türkiye (approval no. 2019/130). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional policies. All participants were adults (≥ 18 years), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment.

This observational study followed the STROBE guidelines for reporting.

Discussion

Metacognition is a higher-order cognitive process related to the monitoring, evaluation, and regulation of one’s own cognitive processes. According to Wells’ metacognitive model, the maintenance of psychological distress may be determined not only by the content of negative thoughts, but also by the individual’s metacognitive beliefs about these thoughts and internal experiences4. It has been proposed that dysfunctional metacognitive beliefs may maintain the Cognitive Attentional Syndrome (CAS), which is characterized by perseverative thinking, worry and rumination, threat monitoring, attention directed toward internal stimuli, and maladaptive strategies aimed at controlling thoughts5,6. Within this framework, the elevated scores observed on MCQ-30 subscales in patients with FMS may be associated with threat monitoring of bodily sensations, maintenance of symptom-focused attention, perseverative thinking, and efforts to control internal experiences. These processes may provide a theoretical framework for understanding the salience and functional impact of fibromyalgia symptoms. However, because the MCQ-30 does not directly measure CAS processes, this interpretation should be considered not as a finding directly demonstrated by the present data, but as a theoretical explanation based on the metacognitive model.

When the subcomponents of metacognition were examined, the FMS group was found to have significantly higher scores than healthy controls on the Cognitive Confidence, Uncontrollability and Danger, Cognitive Self-Consciousness, and Need to Control Thoughts subscales. This pattern suggests that patients with FMS may report higher levels of metacognitive beliefs that are considered maladaptive within the metacognitive model. In particular, the elevation in the Uncontrollability and Danger subscale indicates that worry and distressing internal experiences may be appraised as uncontrollable and potentially threatening4. Higher scores on the Need to Control Thoughts subscale suggest that efforts to suppress, control, or regulate unwanted thoughts and sensations may be more prominent. Within Wells’ metacognitive model, such beliefs may be linked to perseverative thinking processes such as worry and rumination, threat monitoring, and an attentional focus on internal experiences.

When total metacognition scores were evaluated, positive correlations were observed with all FIQR subscales and FIQR total scores. This finding indicates that metacognitive beliefs may be associated with disease impact and functional impairment in FMS. In particular, the elevation in the Cognitive Self-Consciousness subscale may be interpreted not as general illness awareness, but rather as increased attentional orientation toward one’s own thoughts and internal processes. Within the CAS framework, this may be associated with continuous monitoring of bodily sensations, maintenance of symptom-focused attention, and threat-focused processing. Similarly, higher scores on the Cognitive Confidence subscale may reflect reduced confidence in cognitive functioning, which may contribute to greater monitoring of cognitive complaints frequently reported in fibromyalgia or to their appraisal as threatening14.

Therefore, the present findings suggest that symptom perception and disease impact in FMS may be associated not only with the content of symptoms, but also with metacognitive appraisals of these symptoms, perseverative thinking, threat monitoring, cognitive control efforts, and symptom-focused attentional processes. However, because the MCQ-30 does not directly measure CAS itself or processes such as threat monitoring, rumination, and symptom-focused attention, these interpretations should be regarded not as conclusions directly derived from the present data, but as theoretical inferences based on Wells’ metacognitive model. Moreover, these findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting that metacognitive functioning may differ in patients with FMS8. Nevertheless, although elevated MCQ-30 scores may indicate a vulnerability to CAS-like response patterns, behaviors such as worry, rumination, attentional fixation, reassurance seeking, symptom checking, and avoidance were not directly assessed in this study.

In our study, depression and anxiety scores were higher in the patient group than in the control group, and this finding is consistent with the literature15. However, an unexpected subgroup pattern was observed in MCQ-30 and FIQR scores among patients with FMS who had moderate-to-severe depressive or anxiety symptoms as defined by BDI/BAI cutoffs. This finding indicates that the associations between depressive and anxiety symptoms and metacognitive beliefs or disease impact did not emerge in the expected direction in this sample; however, the present dataset does not directly explain the reasons for this pattern. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted in the context of methodological and clinical factors such as small subgroup sample sizes, cutoff-based classification, restricted variance, measurement overlap, treatment status, disease duration, sample characteristics, or unmeasured clinical differences. In addition, the expression of depressive and anxiety symptoms through bodily complaints, or different labeling of emotional distress in a cultural context, may also be a possible explanation; however, this possibility was not directly tested in the present study.

An important methodological clarification concerns the distinction between psychiatric diagnosis and symptom severity. Although current DSM-5 psychiatric disorder diagnoses were excluded using the SCID-5, BDI and BAI scores reflect symptom severity rather than DSM-5 diagnoses. Therefore, the depression and anxiety subgroup analyses should be considered analyses of subclinical or symptom-level psychological distress rather than analyses of diagnostic psychiatric disorders.

Our study has several limitations. The relatively small sample size, the use of cutoff scores for the BDI and BAI, reliance on self-report measures, and the single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. In addition, CAS processes and behavioral processes such as threat monitoring, rumination, reassurance seeking, symptom checking, and avoidance were not directly measured. Treatment status, disease duration, and unmeasured clinical variables may have influenced the subgroup findings.

In conclusion, metacognitive beliefs appear to be more prominent in patients with FMS than in healthy controls. These findings suggest that, within a broader biopsychosocial framework, metacognitive beliefs may represent a hypothesis-generating process marker related to symptom perception and functioning in FMS, but should not be regarded as a standalone explanatory factor.
