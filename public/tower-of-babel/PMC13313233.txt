Reduced T cell epitope cross-conservation and immunogenic potential is associated with emergence of a dominant influenza subclade K in 2025
Authors: Andres H. Gutierrez, Jacob Tivin, Ben Gabriel, Anne S. De Groot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSubclade K is a newly emergent influenza A/H3N2 strain that became the dominant strain circulating globally; more than 90% of influenza A virus (IAV) cases in 2025–26 were subclade K. While seasonal vaccines elicited measurable cross-reactive hemagglutination inhibition (HI) and neutralizing antibody responses against the new strain were deemed to be protective, T cells contribute substantially to protection against infection and severe disease. T cell epitopes that are conserved between vaccine strains and circulating strains may have a protective effect. We hypothesized that mutations in the HA antigen introduced by viral evolution resulted in the loss of some cross-conserved T cell epitopes and that this loss may have contributed to the emergence of subclade K. Using a new immunoinformatics tool, EpiCC, we assessed T cell epitope cross-conservation between subclade K, other circulating H3N2 strains, and the most recent seasonal vaccine strains. Evaluation of 706 H3N2 HA sequences demonstrated a 10% reduction in cross-conserved T cell epitope content between seasonal vaccines, circulating strains and subclade K. Decreased cross-conservation may signal the emergence of new flu strains. Proactive analysis of cross-conserved T cell epitopes may improve vaccine selection. Loss of T cell epitopes could also reduce the efficacy of new subclade K influenza vaccines in fall 2026.

Introduction

Influenza evolution and vaccine strain selection

T cell epitope cross-reactivity between new and emergent strains of influenza and previously circulating strains has not been systematically evaluated for the current emergent subclade K nor any previous emergent strains such as pandemic H1N1, and is not currently part of the global influenza strain selection process. The decision not to evaluate T cell epitope cross-conservation runs contrary to decades of evidence that T cell memory response contributes to protection against influenza infection and disease. To address this gap, we developed an algorithm called EpiCC for “T cell Epitope Content Comparison” that evaluates and quantitates cross-conserved T cell epitopes between vaccines and emergent strains. This new tool provides information that has already been proven to be useful in other public health contexts. Examples include study of SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern (VOC) during the COVID pandemic1 and studies that defined a protective threshold for T cell epitope cross-conservation for swine influenza A, when protective antibody titers are not achieved by vaccination.2 A commercial version of this tool (CircoMatch) is currently in use to select vaccine strains to protect against PCV2 infection in pigs.3,4

Current ferret antibody (and bioinformatic) methods for identifying effective vaccines

Influenza virus vaccines are updated on average every 1.5 y due to the rate of mutational change in the viral hemagglutinin protein (HA) targeted by vaccination. Amino acid mutations in the HA protein contribute to escape from antibody and T cell-mediated protection. The process of vaccine strain selection currently relies on the measurement of protective antibodies against newly circulating strains. This is achieved by vaccinating (or exposing) ferrets and determining the highest serum dilution that reduces agglutination of red blood cells (hemagglutination) by a new flu strain. This titer is the hemagglutination inhibition titer or “HI titer.” A post-vaccination HI titer of 1:40 has traditionally been used as a regulatory benchmark associated with a 50% reduction in the risk of influenza infection in healthy adults.5 Direct virus neutralization assays, which measure the inhibition of viral entry into target cells and are reported as neutralizing antibody titers (nAbs), are also used to confirm the ability of ferret antibodies to neutralize the virus. A 4-fold reduction in the HI titer of ferret antisera against a novel strain compared to the titer obtained against the homologous reference virus suggests that the HA of the new strain is antigenically distinct.

Interestingly, these studies use influenza-naïve ferrets, meaning that cross-conserved memory T cell responses do not contribute to the antibody response against a new or emerging influenza strains. Furthermore, ferret MHC class II molecules likely differ from human HLA-DR alleles in their peptide-binding specificity. Since CD4+ T cell help, which depends on MHC class II-restricted antigen presentation, is a prerequisite for generating high-affinity antibody responses in germinal centers, the fundamentally different antigen presentation landscape in ferrets means that ferret-derived HI titers reflect a ferret-specific immune response that may not accurately predict human vaccine-induced antibody affinity.

Influenza strains that are considered to have higher potential to spread in a population are classified as “antigenically distinct” from prior circulating strains or vaccines using defined criteria. Current methods for antigenic characterization integrate HI (from ferret studies) and neutralization titers with genotypic information to generate antigenic maps, which help visualize the antigenic distance between viruses, the clustering of related strains, and emerging drift variants.

Strain selection, which is discussed twice per year by global health experts, is based on information gleaned from the integration of multiple datasets including antigenic characterization Based on multiple criteria that do not currently include T cell epitope analysis, a number of strains are selected for evaluation as potential seasonal flu vaccine candidates and additional ferret vaccination studies are performed.

T cells protect against flu following vaccination or by infection

While antibody binding and titers are known markers of protection for many viruses, the role of T cells in bolstering the protective response against influenza and preventing severe disease has been extensively documented. Evidence from numerous studies in mice, ferrets, and humans supports that there is a strong correlation between T cell responses and protection from infection and severe influenza disease.6–10 Memory T cell responses are particularly important to preventing severe disease.11–13 Given the continued threat of influenza and the emergence of new variants, vaccine developers and public health experts are always seeking ways to improve protection.

Thus, we asked: How did subclade K becomes the dominant strain worldwide, replacing other strains of H3N2 and causing more than 90% of all infections globally, when antibody responses to the new virus were relatively conserved? Based on previous observations that dominant strains of SARS-CoV-2 (Omicron) emerged following shifts in conserved T cell epitope content,1 we speculated that cross-conserved T cell response was likely to be playing a role. We therefore evaluated T cell epitope conservation between emerging influenza clade K, prior circulating strains, and current vaccine strains, using a recently developed algorithm called EpiCC (T cell Epitope Content Comparison).

We hypothesized, and show here, that a reduction in T cell epitope cross-conservation may have diminished population-level T cell recognition and contributed to the rapid expansion and dominance of subclade K in 2025. Notably, this analysis was performed for the HA antigen using epitope mapping tools focused on (human) class II restricted epitopes that contribute to the formation of neutralizing and high affinity antibodies in the germinal center. This also suggests that current (ferret-dependent) methods for influenza virus selection might not correctly quantify emergent strain differences and supports the examination of cross-conserved HLA-restricted T cell epitopes between vaccines and emergent strains to improve the accuracy of the global influenza strain selection process.

Methods

H3N2 HA sequences included in the analysis

For this case study, we obtained clade annotation and amino acid sequences for 54,479 hemagglutinin (HA) proteins from human influenza A/H3N2 viruses isolated between 2024 and 2025 from GISAID. The HA proteins were grouped by their amino acid sequences, and a frequency analysis was performed to generate a non-redundant dataset (retaining HA sequences observed ten or more times). A final set of 704 nonredundant HA representing 36,699 viruses was analyzed (Supplemental data S1). For each group, the sequence with the earliest collection date was analyzed for T cell epitopes restricted by nine supertype HLA-DR alleles using EpICC as previously described,14 and using EpiMatrix, JanusMatrix, and ClustiMer (tools described below), the HA protein sequences of two seasonal vaccines recommended for the 2024–2025 (A/Massachusetts/18/2022 (H3N2)-like) and 2025–2026 (A/District of Columbia/27/2023) influenza seasons were also obtained and analyzed for comparison with circulating strains (shown in Supplemental S1 and additional sequence genotyping and GISAID ID is provided in Supplemental Information).

Circulating H3N2 virus sequence data used in EpiCC case study

The first detection of subclade K in the non-redundant dataset (Supplemental data Table S1) occurred in January 2025; however, clade K did not become 90% dominant until June 26, 2025.15,16 Four representative strains were selected for further analysis, by T cell epitope cluster-by-cluster analysis (Supplemental Table S2), including the HA sequence from the first subclade K identified in Australia (Darwin), the first isolate in the US (Baltimore), a strain from Kenya which was isolated on date when subclade K became the dominant strain in the database, and a strain from the Netherlands marking the last time point in 2025 when a subclade K strain was isolated.

Immunoinformatics tool: EpiCC

EpiCC (T cell Epitope Content Comparison) is a computational tool that uses EpiMatrix and JanusMatrix (14 and Supplemental Tools Section) to quantify the relatedness of T cell epitope content between two protein sequences, such as a vaccine strain and a circulating viral strain. Rather than relying solely on the overall sequence identity, EpiCC focuses specifically on predicted CD4+ T cell epitopes and then evaluates whether TCR-facing residues within those epitopes are conserved across strains. The algorithm first uses EpiMatrix to identify putative MHC class II – restricted nine-mer epitopes within each protein sequence across a panel of supertype HLA-DR alleles (see iVAX methods publication and supplemental tools section for more information).14 For each predicted epitope in the reference sequence (usually a vaccine strain), EpiCC searches for corresponding peptides in the comparison sequence (in a dataset of circulating influenza strains, for example) and evaluates conservation at the TCR-facing residues (using JanusMatrix) while allowing variability at MHC anchor residues.

Each epitope pair is counted if the two epitopes share TCR-facing residues and predicted HLA binding. The aggregate of these pairwise comparisons generates an EpiCC score, which reflects the extent of shared T cell epitope content between the two sequences. Higher EpiCC scores indicate greater predicted cross-conservation of CD4+ T cell epitopes and therefore a greater likelihood of T cell cross-reactivity. Conversely, lower EpiCC scores suggest epitope divergence and potential loss of T cell cross-recognition.2 Importantly, EpiCC does not measure overall sequence homology nor does it examine the individual T cell epitopes for cross-conservation against the human peptidome; rather, it provides a numerical comparison of the predicted T cell epitope landscapes. This makes it particularly useful for assessing the impact of antigenic drift on cellular immune recognition and for evaluating vaccine strain updates where preservation or loss of T cell cross-conservation may influence cross-protective immunity.

For this case study evaluating subclade K, cross-conservation of T cell epitopes between two seasonal vaccine HA sequences and circulating field isolates (Supplemental Table S1) was evaluated using EpiCC. Cross-conservation between influenza HLA-DR-restricted class II epitopes was defined using JanusMatrix14 for the EpiCC analysis. Epitopes were considered cross-conserved if (1) both epitopes were predicted to bind the same HLA-DR and (2) their TCR-facing residues were identical. For each field isolate, EpiCC produces three scores: (1) Baseline T cell epitope content for each strain and vaccine that is examined, (2) T cell epitope content that is cross-conserved with a specific vaccine (EpiCC score), and (3) T cell epitope coverage, calculated as the EpiCC score for the vaccine-strain comparison, divided by the Baseline epitope content and expressed as a percentage. Higher EpiCC scores indicate greater cross-conservation and therefore higher T cell epitope coverage, less chance of immune escape from memory T cell responses. A cluster by cluster analysis was also performed using EpiMatrix and ClustiMer.

Description of EpiMatrix, JanusMatrix and ClustiMer: see supplemental tools section

Statistical analysis

Differences in T cell epitope coverage as defined by EpiCC (see above) between influenza A subclade K HA sequences and sequences of circulating strains were assessed using a Wilcoxon rank sum test implemented in the stats package in R v4.5. P-values < .05 were considered statistically significant.

Thus, all subclade K strains exhibit a net loss of one EpiBar (124–141) and loss of T cell epitope content (5%) relative to the vaccine strain and all exhibited a reduction of the number of T cell epitopes present in a second high-scoring cluster (144–165; a decrease in the number of T cell epitopes in the cluster). These changes are consistent with the overall loss of 10% of the T cell epitope conservation as measured by EpiCC. Note that individual T cell epitopes that are not in clusters may have changed in the subclade K sequence, these are counted in EpiCC but not included in the cluster-by-cluster analysis. Overall, the change reported in the EpiCC coverage analysis appears to be primarily driven by the loss of high-scoring, vaccine-associated clustered T cell epitope regions.

Published sequence comparisons between subclade K strains and seasonal vaccine strains have defined seven distinct changes in the HA1 globular head domain of the HA protein compared to the strains recommended by WHO for the southern hemisphere (A/Sydney/1359/2024 and A/Singapore/GP20238/2024), and nine changes compared to the northern hemisphere vaccine recommendation (A/Croatia/10136RV/2023)15,16: The defining subclade mutations include K2N, S144N (introduces a new N-linked glycosylation site, +CHO), N158D, I160K, Q173R, T328A, and S378N. The mutations T135K (−CHO) and K189R are only identified in the northern hemisphere vaccine comparison. It would be natural to assume that those mutations predominantly affect antibody binding to HA; however, the mutations could also affect T cell epitopes. An analysis of the changes shows that seven of the nine mutations, plus N145S (compared to 2025–2026 vaccine), do occur in the epitope clusters identified in the above cluster analysis, removing one cluster entirely and reducing the immunogenicity of another.

A search for similarity with published epitopes on the IEDB database showed that four of the subclade K clusters (328–342, 347–364, 374–392, and 417–439, highlighted in gray in the table) are substantially similar (>80%) to previously published epitopes that were cross conserved between seasonal H3N2 strains and the avian influenza virus H7N9 (2013).17,18

Preservation of a potentially tolerogenic epitope in subclade K HA

Following the analysis of immunogenic T cell epitope content in subclade K, a subsequent analysis investigated whether conserved epitopes contribute to immune evasion. Viral epitopes that mimic host self-peptides at the TCR-facing residues have been shown to engage regulatory T cells or fail to elicit robust effector responses.19–21 Therefore, we examined whether any of the conserved clusters, between vaccines and sequences representing subclade K HA, exhibited evidence of increased human HLA-DR-ligand cross-conservation at the TCR residues (human homology) which might confer a selective advantage to the emerging virus.

Interestingly, one cluster of T cell epitopes in circulating strains and in the seasonal vaccines, that did have high cross-conservation with the human immunopeptidome (cluster 515–538, with a JanusMatrix human homology score of 9.94), is identical in subclade K. If this region is shown to be tolerated or tolerogenic, it may support the emergence of subclade K strains and thus the preservation of the potentially tolerated or tolerogenic cluster could be beneficial for the virus. In Supplemental Table S2, we provide information on the cross-conservation of cluster 515–538 in the human immunopeptidome. In this analysis, a single highly promiscuous nine-mer (frame 525, “FLLCIALLG”) is conserved at its TCR face with a total of 62 similar-HLA-DR-binding self-peptides in the human proteome.

Discussion

T cell memory against influenza is protective against challenge with new strains

As previously discussed, numerous studies have demonstrated that effective HI responses can depend on previous T cell memory responses to cross-conserved T cell epitopes. For example, using sequential infection and immunization with drifted influenza strains in mice, Gage et al.22 demonstrated that MHC Class II haplotype and CD4+ T-cell memory influence the affinity of antibodies generated to influenza. Enhanced memory T cell responses correlated with increased germinal center B cells and T-follicular helper cells specific to the second strain. Furthermore, we have demonstrated that antibody responses to the H7N9 avian influenza HA antigen were compromised due to reduced T cell epitope density and/or the presence of regulatory T cell epitopes within the vaccine antigen. Modification of three amino acids, which introduced a previously validated and cross-conserved (memory) T cell epitope and removed the tolerogenic epitope, induced higher B cell responses and higher antibody titers (in humanized mice).23 This observation was later confirmed by exposing mice to infection or vaccination from a seasonal influenza strain that contained the memory T cell epitope and vaccinating with the modified H7N9 vaccine.24

Impact on cross-reactive antibody response: seasonal flu vaccine vs. Subclade K

After subclade K was first detected in Australia in July 2025, it spread rapidly throughout Australia and New Zealand before disseminating globally. Usually, immune protection against emerging strains of influenza has been linked to antibody cross-reactivity. It is interesting to observe that there was documented antibody cross-reactivity to subclade K, and even so, the subclade still became the dominant circulating strain.25 That suggests that there is ‘more to’ protection against emergence than antibody cross-conservation, at least as currently measured (using ferret antisera).

A recent study carried out in the U.S. determined that individuals vaccinated with the 2025–2026 seasonal influenza vaccine (H3N2, A/Croatia/10136RV/2023; subclade J.2) had antibodies that could protect against subclade K. Specifically, sera collected from 76 vaccination study participants about 1 month after seasonal flu vaccination showed increased HI titers against an H3N2 subclade K virus (A/New York/GKISBBBG87773/2025). Following vaccination, 71% of subjects were seropositive (i.e., had an antibody titer >1:40) to the vaccine strain, and 39% were seropositive to the new subclade K strain. The authors concluded that “the current seasonal [vaccine] will … be somewhat effective at preventing H3N2 subclade K virus infections.”21

A serosurvey conducted in August 2025 in Portugal26 during the early phase of subclade K expansion also found comparable titers to the seasonal vaccine strain (A/District of Columbia/27/2023) in vaccinated or exposed individuals. The majority of the population tested (61.8%) exhibited protective titers against seasonal influenza (>40) and cross-reactive antibodies against the newly emerged H3N2 subclade K were also detected in (22.3%) of those with positive seasonal titers, indicating but not proving that partial antibody cross-reactivity between the vaccine strain and the emerging subclade K strain could be present.

In Europe, the 2025/26 influenza season started at least 2 weeks early due to the emergence of the subclade K viruses. A Finnish group published data showing cross-reactive antibody responses between seasonal influenza vaccine strains and subclade K using ferret and vaccinated human antibody sera.27 Their study showed that, ferret antisera raised against both the 2024/25 and 2025/26 vaccine strains was poorly cross-reactive with subclade K viruses, confirming antigenic divergence, thus in this study protective titers could not be demonstrated. This also appeared to be the case in the UK, where subclade K viruses isolated between August and October 2025 showed a >32-fold reduction in reactivity with ferret antisera raised against A/Croatia/10136RV/2023 and at least eightfold reduction with antisera raised against A/District of Columbia/27/2023.28 Titers against the original Southern Hemisphere subclade K exhibited increased cross-reactivity, suggesting that additional divergence had occurred in the interval between Southern and Northern hemisphere circulation of subclade K.

Finally, a recent study from Canada29 demonstrated that vaccination with the seasonal influenza vaccine reduced symptomatic illness in confirmed cases of influenza and cough, despite the dominant presence of subclade K during the flu season of 2025–2026. Sequencing of isolated influenza strains revealed that all of the 79 subclade K and K-like viruses were vaccine mismatched, including 61 (77%) for which there was a greater than 16-fold HI titer reduction (using ferret anti-sera). This finding suggests the potential for antibody-related divergence; however, the reduction in clinical signs following vaccination may be attributed to the remaining T cell epitope cross-conservation.

Decreased T cell responses may have contributed to the emergence of the new strain

On average, subclade K HA sequences demonstrated a greater than 10% reduction in T cell epitope cross-conservation with the HA of circulating strains and vaccine strains. We conclude that reduced T cell epitope cross-conservation may contribute to strain emergence and dominance. Incorporating T cell epitope analysis into vaccine strain selection could improve influenza vaccine effectiveness and prolong the efficacy of vaccines against future viral escape.

Related to the observation of “reduced clinical signs,’ the question as to whether T cell responses generated by cross-reactive memory T cells generated following vaccination recognize epitopes in the new subclade K, and are protective, or not, remains unanswered, due to the fact that T cell assays are not routinely performed for emergent influenza strains. The current study suggests that 10 of 15 (66.7%) T cell epitope clusters are conserved between previously circulating strains and subclade K. If T cell epitope clusters are conserved in the new variant subclade K strain, T memory may be present for those promiscuous and conserved T cell epitopes.

However, a notable change in cluster 124–141 shows the reduction in immunogenic potential in the subclade K virus. In addition, changes to cluster 144–165 result in a 50% decrease in the T cell epitope concentration within the same region of the HA of the new subclade. This suggests that at least two key epitopes are not conserved in subclade K viruses.

In addition to reduced helper epitope density, the preservation of tolerogenic clusters could theoretically affect T follicular helper cell support and antibody maturation. Regulatory T cell responses could lead to reductions in immune memory to emergent subclade K, and if vaccines are produced using subclade K sequences, reductions in vaccine efficacy.31

Is immunoinformatics analysis reliable predicting human immune responses to influenza?

This type of analysis of conserved T cell epitopes has previously contributed to understanding the pandemic potential of novel influenza strains and COVID. For example, in 2009 when “pandemic” influenza H1N1 (California/2009) emerged the HA and NA proteins of the H1N1 “pandemic” flu strain A/California/04/2009 H1N1 were evaluated, and it was determined that 13 out of 16 promiscuous (EpiBar) epitopes within HA were 90–100% conserved with the corresponding sequence in the previously circulating dominant flu strain (and vaccine strain) A/Brisbane/59/2007.30 This finding suggested that vaccinated (or exposed) individuals were unlikely to develop severe “pandemic” H1N126,27 and that seasonal vaccines remained effective. In vitro studies with human PMBC confirmed this finding.14

Following the integration of JanusMatrix in 2013, the accuracy of EpiCC analyses was enhanced by the definition of epitopes that were less than 90–100% conserved by evaluating conserved TCR-facing amino acid residues using JanusMatrix.15 JanusMatrix has been used in three previous vaccine research studies for the US government, including the FDA, DTRA, and the CDC.31–33 JanusMatrix is now combined with EpiMatrix in the EpiCC application,3 which is in use for evaluation of emerging PCV2 infections in swine, identifying cross-conserved epitopes in emergent PCV2 strains.3

Additional examples of T cell epitope loss and strain emergence have been identified

This case study underscores the value of EpiCC approaches for the detection of emerging strains with altered immunological properties before or during their expansion. The slight 10% change in conserved T cell epitope content defined in this study of subclade K’s emergence is supported by prior work on H3N3 by Swan Tan et al., in 2022 (Swan Tan, University of Georgia PhD thesis).34 Tan et al. examined the evolution of the human CD4+ T cell epitopes in hundreds of human influenza A/H3N2 HA sequences using EpiCC. The results showed a consistent decline in conserved class II-restricted CD4+ T cell epitopes in the years following the introduction of new vaccine strains, suggesting that immune pressure at the population level (by infection or vaccination) contributed to viral evolution.

T cell epitope loss may also be relevant to the emergence of SARS-CoV-2 Omicron. A similar analysis was previously performed for SARS-CoV-2 using EpiCC.1 Prior to the emergence of Omicron, new ‘variants of concern’ (VOC) were highly (>95%) conserved, based on EpiCC analysis, with the original Wuhan strain spike, and with commercial vaccines. As shown in Figure 2, when Omicron emerged, there was a sudden drop off in conservation with circulating strains. At that point, T cell epitopes contained in the spike protein of the original Wuhan strain (and vaccine strain) had less than 85% cross-conservation with the circulating VOC (similar to the drop in conservation seen in the current subclade K emergence). This data suggests that a decline in the conservation between the T cell epitopes in SARS-CoV-2 VOCs, such as Omicron, may contribute to the emergence of a new strain in the human population and can also predict the need to change the dominant vaccine strain. Limitations to using ferret antibodies for vaccine efficacy

There are several limitations to the antibody approach to determining whether a vaccine will work against an emerging strain of influenza. First, the titer method is incremental, not gradual, and reflects mutations to the surface of the HA antigen that cause differences in antibody binding. While it is true that this will affect antibody targeting, it may or may not have an effect on T cell responses, as T cell epitopes can be internal or external, they are linear and not three dimensional, and a single mutation that affects antibody binding may not lead to changes in a T cell epitope. Second, the antibody response that is measured (the sera used for vaccine efficacy testing) is generated in ferrets, not humans. Thanks to the contributions of Arlene Sharpe, Peter Sage and many others, we know that the antibody response to influenza HA is directly related to the quality of T cell help in the germinal center, both in terms of titer and affinity to the target.36 A real-life example of this key concept occurred in 2013 with the emergence of H7N9, an avian-origin H3-like virus with pandemic potential. In Guo et al.,10 titers of antibody to H7N9 after exposure (in humans) were low, and antibody affinity was also decreased as compared to titers and affinity of H1N1 or H3N2 antibodies in the same subjects.

Third, MHC class II molecules in ferrets differ from human HLA-DR alleles in their peptide-binding specificity. Since CD4+ T cell help is MHC class II-restricted, epitopes that drive T helper cell responses in ferret germinal centers are unlikely to match those in humans. As a result, the quality of T cell help shaping ferret antibody responses, including titer and affinity maturation, may not reflect the human immune response. This may lead to two possible outcomes: 1) ferret-derived HI data suggest adequate cross-protection, but human vaccine effectiveness is lower than expected, or 2) ferret antisera show limited cross-reactivity, but humans retain functional antibodies due to memory T cell help against epitopes not presented in ferrets.

Fourth, and probably most important for this discussion, the ferret method does not examine T cell responses to other antigens that may be present in the vaccine. Vaccines made in eggs include a copy of HA (hemagglutinin) and NA from the selected circulating strain, as well as internal genes (PB2, PB1, PA, NP, M, NS) from the ‘carrier’ virus (A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (PR8)). Furthermore, the ferrets are naïve, meaning they lack circulating memory T cells to boost B cell responses after infection. While memory T cell responses to the internal proteins can contribute to immune responses and protection by reducing the severity and duration of influenza illness following infection, for the purpose of evaluating vaccine efficacy, it is reasonable to discount internal proteins. Vaccine strain selection uses HA alone, so the vaccines contain only the HA and NA of the selected strain.

In fact, ferrets confirm our observation that T cell (memory) epitope conservation is important when ferrets are exposed to different strains of influenza.37 Studies in ferrets have also shown that prior exposure to a circulating seasonal strain (e.g., Brisbane H1N1) can protect against an emergent, potentially pandemic influenza strain (e.g., pandemic H1N1 2009). In a ferret model, prior infection with seasonal influenza A viruses or homologous vaccination reduced infection rates, viral shedding, and transmission following A(H1N1)pdm challenge. This is also observed in humans vaccinated with seasonal influenza vaccines, when they are infected with subclade K.38

Limits to the claims made in this commentary

This analysis relies on computational immunoinformatics predictions rather than direct experimental measurements of T cell responses. Although the EpiMatrix and JanusMatrix tools used here are well-validated and have been extensively applied to epitope discovery, immune engineering, and vaccine design (De Groot et al., 2020),16 predicted CD4+ T cell epitopes do not necessarily translate into immunodominant or protective responses in vivo. In particular, the highest-scoring vaccine-associated cluster identified in this study has not yet been independently validated in publicly available human datasets, underscoring the need for prospective experimental confirmation of its immunogenicity.

We can support the computational identification of valid T cell epitopes in this case, however. Four of the clusters have been previously identified and published in two independent studies.14,15 Furthermore, other published studies have validated the “pan HLA-DR” promiscuous or “EpiBar” epitope predictions for influenza in human PBMC or HLA transgenic mouse studies.9,15 For example, a study involving vaccination of HLA-DR transgenic mice with Pan-DR cross-conserved HLA class II T cell epitopes demonstrated protection against both H1N1 and H3N2 influenza challenges.10 A separate study conducted by EpiVax found that 16 of 19 highly cross-conserved promiscuous (pan-HLA-DR) T cell epitopes derived from H1N1 influenza were found to stimulate memory T cell responses from human PBMC.9,39 In general, 80–90% of T cell epitopes identified in this manner, that are restricted by multiple HLA class II DR supertypes, have been confirmed in human PBMC assays.15

Regardless, functional assays assessing CD4+ T cell responses to conserved and subclade K-modified regions will be required to determine whether the predicted reduction in epitope density in subclade K has biological or clinical relevance. In particular, since some of the clusters analyzed here were determined to be promiscuous binders across multiple HLA-DR alleles but potentially tolerogenic, their impact on antigenicity should be determined. While this computational analysis provides a quantitative comparison of predicted epitope cross-conservation, it also identifies potentially tolerogenic, and cross-conserved T cell epitope clusters, for which clinical and immunologic validation would be illuminating and could potentially confirm the real-world implications of this analysis to influenza strain emergence and vaccine efficacy.

The following approach may help strengthen future applications of the EpiCC approach to emerging strain analysis:

Step 1. Detection and Characterization. Cross-conserved class II T cell epitopes must be identified using well characterized and validated computational methods (see Supplemental Tools for more information on EpiCC, EpiMatrix and JanusMatrix). These epitopes should be characterized in both the vaccine strain and the new circulating strain.

Step 2: Immunogenicity. Prior exposure to the source strain (either through natural infection or vaccination) must generate a measurable, class II T cell response against these conserved epitopes in an in vitro assay. This should be quantifiable (e.g., by ELISpot, flow cytometry, or other validated T cell assays) and preferably associated with protective immunity against the original strain.

Step 3: Epidemiology. A decline in cross-conserved class II epitope identity of less than 10% should be associated with maintained vaccine efficacy against the new strain in a vaccinated population. Conversely, a loss of greater than 10% epitope identity should correlate with reduced vaccine efficacy (relative to historical baseline or age-matched comparators).

Step 4: T-cell Dependency. The protective effect must be demonstrably mediated by class II T cell responses (not antibodies or innate immunity) through:

Demonstration that antibody titers alone do not predict vaccine efficacy when epitope conservation varies (this appears to have been achieved during the expansion of subclade K).Evidence that individuals with robust T cell responses but lower antibody titers still show protection if epitope conservation is high.

Conclusions

The emergence of subclade K in 2025 is correlated with a significant reduction in T-cell epitope cross-conservation, as measured by the EpiCC tool. In silico analyses indicate that the transition to subclade K resulted in the reduction of the immunogenic potential of at least one highly promiscuous T cell epitope. While overall antibody titers to subclade K were diminished, a residual proportion (>30%) of individuals vaccinated with the seasonal 2025 vaccine had cross-reactive antibodies to subclade K at protective levels (>1:40). Antibody cross-reactivity alone did not predict dominance.

These data suggest that loss of T-cell epitope conservation between vaccines and emerging strains may serve as an early-warning metric for viral emergence. The data also support the idea that vaccine strain selection and design should prioritize preserving T-cell epitope content and evaluate the presence of cross-conserved memory T cell epitope content. T-cell epitope content in the critical influenza antigen (hemagglutinin, or HA) should also be included in when evaluating vaccine potency.

Furthermore, immunoinformatics tools like EpiCC provide a scalable method for integrating T cell analysis into public health decision-making processes. Given the significant potential impact of T cell epitope cross-conservation, it is crucial to consider the impact of cross-conserved epitopes when selecting vaccine antigens, whether for influenza, SARS-CoV-2, or any other highly infectious, rapidly mutating pathogens.
