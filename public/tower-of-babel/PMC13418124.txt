How should retrospectively registered protocols be interpreted in systematic reviews?
Authors: João Vitor dos Santos Canellas, Fernando César Paraizo Borges
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prospective protocol registration is widely regarded as the preferred standard in systematic reviews. It promotes transparency from the outset of the research process, supports the prespecification of methods, contributes to reducing the risk of selective reporting and unnecessary duplication of effort, and is associated with improved methodological quality and reporting standards. However, despite these well-recognized advantages, not all systematic reviews are prospectively registered. In practice, retrospectively registered protocols continue to occur despite widespread recommendations for prospective registration. This raises an important question: how should retrospectively registered protocols be interpreted in systematic reviews? Within this context, the present commentary does not seek to position retrospective registration as equivalent to prospective registration. Rather, it aims to examine how retrospectively registered protocols may be interpreted in a transparent and methodologically informed manner, acknowledging that retrospective registration alone cannot demonstrate that methodological decisions were prespecified before the review process commenced. We propose that retrospectively registered protocols may be more appropriately interpreted through a transparency-oriented appraisal rather than a strictly timing-based judgment. Such an appraisal may consider whether the retrospective nature of registration is clearly reported, whether the reasons for delayed registration are explicitly described, whether deviations from the initial plan are transparently documented and justified, and whether overall methodological rigor and completeness of reporting are preserved in accordance with established guidance. Retrospective registration has inherent limitations, particularly its inability to prevent undisclosed methodological changes or reduce duplication of effort. Nevertheless, when clearly labeled, sufficiently detailed, and transparently reported, it may still contribute to the interpretability of the review process. Emphasizing transparency, consistency between planned and reported methods, and clarity of methodological decisions may support more robust critical appraisal and informed editorial decision-making in systematic reviews.

Introduction

Prospective protocol registration is widely regarded as the preferred standard in systematic reviews. It promotes transparency from the outset of the research process, supports the prespecification of methods, and contributes to reducing the risk of selective reporting and unnecessary duplication of effort. In addition, empirical meta-epidemiological evidence indicates that prospectively registered systematic reviews are associated with improved methodological quality and a lower risk of bias, including biases related to study selection and outcome reporting [1–3].

However, despite these well-recognized advantages, not all systematic reviews are prospectively registered. In practice, retrospectively registered protocols continue to occur for a variety of reasons. This raises an important question: how should retrospectively registered protocols be interpreted in systematic reviews?

This article is presented as a commentary. Its objective is not to challenge current editorial policies nor to argue for relaxing the standards of prospective registration, but rather to examine how retrospectively registered protocols may be interpreted in practice using a transparency-oriented perspective, particularly in situations where prospective registration was not undertaken.

Although definitions may vary across sources, in this commentary the terms “prospective” and “retrospective” registration are used to refer to the timing of protocol registration relative to key stages of the review process. Specifically, prospective registration refers to registration conducted before study selection and data extraction, consistent with methodological guidance emphasizing the development of a protocol prior to the conduct of a review [4, 5]. Conversely, retrospective registration refers to registration undertaken after one or more of these stages have already occurred.

Editorial policies, funding requirements, and methodological guidance documents have increasingly emphasized protocol registration as an important practice in systematic reviews. These entities, however, serve distinct roles within the research ecosystem. Regulatory bodies establish formal requirements for the approval of interventions, scientific journals define editorial expectations based on scope and relevance, and reporting guidelines provide recommendations to improve transparency and reproducibility.

Why prospective registration remains the preferred standard

Although prospective registration is widely recommended by journals, funders, and methodological guidance, its absence may at times lead to an oversimplified interpretation in which the absence of prospective registration is interpreted as indicating poor methodological quality. However, prospective registration and methodological quality are related but distinct concepts. Furthermore, some registries [6, 7] permit retrospective protocol registration, allowing authors to publicly document their methodological plans when prospective registration was not undertaken. Importantly, retrospective registration is generally framed as an exception rather than the preferred standards, with prospective registration remaining the recommended approach. One of the authors (JVC) is affiliated with INPLASY [8], a registry that permits retrospective registration but explicitly endorses prospective registration as the standard practice.

Many well-designed systematic reviews were published before protocol registration became standard practice, and a considerable proportion of systematic reviews continue to be published without formal registration [9]. This observation is not restricted to systematic reviews. Recent meta-research of scoping reviews demonstrated substantial variability in protocol registration practices and widespread non-registration [10]. Nevertheless, these evidence syntheses have informed clinical guidelines, shaped policy decisions, and contributed to the advancement of their respective fields. The absence of a protocol did not necessarily diminish their scientific value. Retrospectively judging such work to be of lower methodological quality, or applying this standard prospectively without nuance, risks oversimplifying the complex relationship between prospective registration and scientific quality.

A central argument in favor of prospective registration is its potential to reduce selective reporting and enhance methodological transparency [11]. This rationale remains highly relevant and is well supported in the context of systematic reviews. In addition to reducing the risk of selective reporting, prospective registration also contributes to minimizing unnecessary duplication of effort by making ongoing or planned reviews publicly visible, which is particularly relevant given that systematic reviews often require substantial time and resources to complete.

By contrast, retrospective registration cannot serve this purpose, as it occurs after key stages of the review process have already taken place and therefore cannot inform other researchers about ongoing work. This limitation reflects an inherent consequence of its timing, as retrospective registration occurs after the point at which it could meaningfully contribute to the prospective visibility of ongoing research activity.

Methodological quality and protocol registration

While it is not suggested that systematic reviews and randomized controlled trials are equivalent or subject to identical methodological considerations, differences in how evidence is generated and interpreted may influence how the role of prospective registration is understood in each context. In randomized trials, outcomes are typically unknown at the outset because data have not yet been produced or collected. In contrast, systematic reviews synthesize already published evidence, and researchers may have prior familiarity with the existing literature.

While prospective registration helps mitigate subjective influences in both contexts, it does not fully eliminate the potential impact of prior knowledge on methodological decisions. Therefore, although prospective registration remains an important safeguard, its presence alone should not be interpreted as a guarantee of methodological rigor, nor should its absence be automatically equated with poor methodological quality or reporting standards.

Considerations surrounding retrospective registration

This dynamic introduces a more complex concern: even in the absence of intentional misconduct, prior familiarity with a topic may influence decisions regarding framing, eligibility criteria, or outcome prioritization. Researchers often select review topics that align with their established research trajectories. Consequently, subconscious selective emphasis on favorable outcomes may occur, irrespective of whether a protocol is registered prospectively or retrospectively.

In this context, retrospective protocol registration warrants careful consideration. Legitimate circumstances may preclude prospective registration, including technical or administrative issues within registration platforms, lack of awareness of registration practices at the planning stage, or logistical barriers. In such situations, authors may still have followed a predefined methodological plan that was not prospectively registered and may subsequently choose to enhance transparency by retrospectively registering and publicly sharing that methodological plan.

However, retrospective registration alone cannot demonstrate that the methodological plan was established before the review process commenced and therefore should not be interpreted as evidence of prespecification. Instead, its value depends on the completeness of reporting, the transparency with which methodological decisions are described, and the consistency between the documented protocol and the final review. A retrospectively registered protocol that clearly documents the originally intended methods, along with any subsequent modifications, may still provide valuable transparency by allowing readers to critically appraise the consistency between the documented protocol and the final review. However, it does not replace the methodological advantages associated with prospective registration.

Transparency-oriented interpretation

Retrospective registration, when clearly labeled and adequately justified, still offers tangible benefits. It creates a permanent, time-stamped record of what was planned, what was modified, and why. This record allows editors, reviewers, and readers to assess deviations between the protocol and the final report, even when the protocol is registered at a later stage, thereby fostering critical appraisal rather than automatic judgments based solely on the timing of registration. Far from undermining transparency, a clearly documented and transparently reported retrospective protocol may enhance the interpretability of the review process.

Careful consideration should be given to how registration information is reported in the methods section of systematic reviews. Simply listing a registry identifier is insufficient; explicitly stating whether registration was prospective or retrospective, reporting its timing relative to key review stages, justifying retrospective registration, and clarifying whether and when protocol updates occurred provides substantially greater transparency for readers. Such disclosure at the first point of contact enables a clearer assessment of methodological planning and deviations, strengthens interpretability, and fosters trust in the integrity of the review. This level of transparency may further enhance reader confidence in the interpretation of the findings.

Limitations of retrospective registration

It is also important to recognize the inherent limitations of retrospective registration. Because the protocol is registered after key stages of the review process may have already occurred, it cannot fully prevent undisclosed methodological changes or selective reporting in the same way as prospective registration. In addition, when insufficiently detailed or incompletely reported, retrospectively registered protocols may offer limited value for assessing methodological consistency.

For these reasons, retrospective registration should not be considered equivalent to prospective registration. Instead, its value lies in the extent to which it provides transparent and interpretable documentation of the review process.

Conversely, a well-documented and appropriately justified retrospective registration can also improve methodological clarity and responsibility. The decisive factors for interpreting retrospectively registered protocols extend beyond the timing of registration and include the clarity of the methodological plan, the justification for any deviations, and the transparency of how the review is conducted and reported. These qualities should be considered when interpreting both prospectively and retrospectively registered protocols, although retrospective registration cannot provide the same assurance of prespecification as prospective registration. As editorial policies continue to evolve, a more balanced approach that considers not only the timing of protocol registration but also transparent reporting, clarity of methodological decisions, and consistency between planned and reported methods may better support rigorous research and informed editorial decision-making.

Conclusion

Prospective protocol registration remains the preferred standard for systematic reviews and should continue to be strongly encouraged. It plays a central role in promoting transparency, reducing the risk of bias, and supporting methodological planning. However, the absence of prospective registration should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of poor methodological quality or reporting standards. When retrospectively registered protocols are clearly labeled, sufficiently detailed, and transparently reported, they may still contribute to the interpretability of the review process.

To facilitate this interpretation, retrospectively registered protocols may be more appropriately assessed through a transparency-oriented appraisal rather than a binary judgment based solely on timing. This may include consideration of whether the registration is clearly labeled as retrospective and whether its timing relative to key review stages is reported; whether the reasons for delayed registration are explicitly described and contextually plausible; whether authors explicitly state whether deviations from the initial plan occurred, even when assessed retrospectively, and, where applicable, whether such deviations are transparently documented and justified; and whether overall methodological rigor and completeness of reporting are preserved in line with established guidance. Together, these elements may support a more balanced and transparent interpretation of retrospectively registered protocols, particularly when prospective registration was not undertaken.

Such an approach does not diminish the importance of prospective registration, but rather strengthens the transparent interpretation of retrospectively registered protocols in practice.
