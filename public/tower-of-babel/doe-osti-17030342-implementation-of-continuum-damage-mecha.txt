1 Introduction
In this report, we describe the necessary steps for implementing a damage model, based on Con-
tinuum Damage Mechanics (CDM) [1], in the radiation-hydrodynamics code xRAGE [2]. First, the
strength calculation workflow currently applied in xRAGE is presented, followed by the fundamental
concepts of CDM, and the damage model proposed for implementation [3]. Then, we outline the
modifications of the strength calculation workflow needed for incorporating the proposed damage
model. This reports concludes with a brief discussion on limitations and future work.

3 Continuum Damage Mechanics
Continuum Damage Mechanics (CDM) is a general framework for damage-induced degradation of
materials caused by micro-structural defects [1]. Unlike Linear-Elastic Fracture Mechanics (LEFM)
based frameworks [6], where discrete cracks are explicitly tracked, CDM treats damage as a contin-
uous field across the material volume. In this section, we briefly describe the fundamental concepts
of CDM and the specifications of the proposed damage model [3].
 First, we consider the Cauchy stress tensor, also true or total stress tensor, of an undamaged
material:
                                           σ ∗ = −pI + s,                                        (9)
which can be decomposed into its volumetric part, described by pressure p, and its deviatoric part,
described by deviatoric stress s. CDM assumes that the Cauchy stress tensor degrades with damage:

                             σ = (1 − D)σ ∗ = −p(1 − D)I + (1 − D)s,                               (10)

where D ∈ [0, 1] is the corresponding damage variable. Note that D = 0 describes an undamaged
material, while D = 1 indicates a fully damaged material.
 The damage variable is described by a damage evolution law, which can be a function of yield

related quantities:
                                           Ḋ = f (Y, ϵpeq , ϵ̇peq , ...)                             (11)
  The damage model and corresponding damage evolution law proposed for implementation in
xRAGE [3] is based on the following ordinary differential equation:

                                                    Ḋ = A(1 − D),                                                      (12)

where A is a coefficient that describes how fast damage variable D grows from 0 to 1 asymptotically.

 Lemaitre formulated the Hypothesis of Strain Equivalence [7], which states that an undam-
aged material subjected to nominal stress (σ) experiences the same strain as a damaged material
subjected to effective stress (σ̃).
 In the following section, we discuss the implementation of these CDM concepts in xRAGE, in
particular Eq. (10) and Eqs. (12-15), and how it changes the strength calculation workflow (Fig. 1).

4 Modified Workflow
Fig. 3 shows the modified strength calculation workflow.

Figure 3: Illustration of the modified strength calculation workflow, which incorporates the CDM concepts stress degradation,
 damage evolution, and effective stress.

 First, the procedure starts with the velocity gradient ∇u as done previously.
 The damage evolution law is computed independently of the radial return mapping algorithm,
since it requires information about the linear strain rate tensor ϵ̇ and its deviatoric part ϵ̇dev , see
Eqs. (13-14), but not the stress history. Furthermore, to be consistent with the current structure
of both the Split-Lagrange-Remap (SLR) and the Unsplit Hydro implementations in xRAGE, we
integrate Eq. (12) using Forward Euler:

                                            Dn+1 = Dn + A(1 − Dn )△t                                                    (16)

 The calculated damage variable is applied to the deviatoric trial stress strial , analogous to

After the deviatoric stress is updated based on the radial return fraction, s̃n+1 is degraded using
the damage variable, see Eq. (10). This can be regarded as a transformation back to the nominal
stress space sn+1 .
 Finally, the updated stress is corrected using the Jaumann-Noll rate as done previously.

5 Application
The implementation of the CDM model in both SLR and Unsplit Hydro in xRAGE is tested using
a simple 1D strength tension problem, which is illustrated in Fig. 4.

 Figure 4: Illustration of the 1D strength tension test.

 The model consist of 50 cells, each 1 cm in length, with an initial velocity in the x-direction ux .
25 cells on the left side experience ux = −1e+4 cm/s, while 25 cells on the right side experience
ux = +1e+4 cm/s, which induces tension in the model. This velocity is chosen to be sufficiently
small to exclude melting and vaporization effects, which can also influence damage and stress.
 The single material within the model is steel (SS304L). Furthermore, we employ the Elastic-
Perfectly-Plastic (EPP) flow-stress model and the implemented damage model with parameters
c1−3 = a1−3 = 1 for simplicity.
 Fig. 5 shows the resulting failure flag (fail) and deviatoric stress in the x-direction (sxx) for
both SLR and Unsplit Hydro at the end of the simulation.
 For the failure flag (top row), we observe a slight difference between SLR and Unsplit Hydro,
i.e., fail = 0.14 versus fail = 0.09. Further investigation revealed that this deviation originates
from how the velocity gradient ∇u is calculated in both versions. Consequently, sxx shows slight
differences as well (bottom row). Overall, the results of the CDM model between SLR and Unsplit
Hydro agree well.

6 Discussion
In this section, we discuss limitations of the proposed model and the current implementation as well
as potential solutions and directions for future work.
 First, in the proposed model, damage initiates as soon as the material is subjected to loading,
i.e., ϵ̇ > 0, which is not common for many materials. For example, ductile metals typically have an
elastic threshold after which damage starts to form and accumulate. Therefore, in the future, we
plan to modify the model to include such elastic limit and prevent immediate damage.
 Second, the current implementation only degrades the deviatoric part of the Cauchy stress
tensor σ, i.e., the deviatoric stress, but not the volumetric part and hence the pressure. The
full integration of Eq. (10) is not technically challenging. However, the pressure degradation with
regards to consistency with the equation of state still needs to be discussed.
 Finally, to verify the implementation of the CDM model in xRAGE SLR and Unsplit Hydro, a
comparison with the implementation in the hydrocode FLAG is desired.
