Medical debt and subsequent use of alternative financial services among US adults
Authors: Hridika Shah, Kyle J Moon, Katherine Miller, Sandro Galea, Catherine K Ettman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMedical debt is persistent among adults in the United States, challenging an individual's ability to meet other financial obligations. However, less is known about financial wellbeing impacts beyond the traditional banking system.MethodsWe conducted a prospective longitudinal study to investigate the association between medical debt and subsequent use of alternative financial services, including buy now, pay later loans, payday loans, and cash advance products, using a nationally representative sample of 2021 adults from the Cumulative Life Stressors Impact on Mental Health and Well Being Study 2023-2025.ResultsOf the analytic sample, 451 (22.3% weighted) had ever reported medical debt, and 477 individuals (23.9% weighted) reported subsequent alternative financial service use. Having any medical debt in the baseline year was associated with a 11.9% point [95% CI: 7.3, 16.6] increase in the probability of subsequent alternative financial service use. This finding was significant for those with $500 to $999, $1000 to $2,499, and more than $5000 in medical debt at baseline.ConclusionIndividuals with medical debt are more likely to report using alternative financial services, reflecting a broader cycle in which health challenges and costly medical care are associated with financial precarity and poorer health.

Introduction

One in 3 US households had medical debt in 2024.1 Heightened health and socioeconomic need are theorized as both the cause and consequence of medical debt.2-6 Being uninsured or under-insured, having low or middle income, and experiencing hospitalization or disability are associated with heightened risk of medical debt accrual.7 Medical debt can be categorized as problematic and non-problematic, the latter of which refers to bills being paid over time by people able to afford these payments.1 On the other hand, those who incur problematic medical debt have difficulty repaying these bills and are more likely to have a cascade of negative outcomes such as being unable to pay rent or mortgage, experiencing food insecurity, or moving due to eviction or foreclosure at the extreme.7,8 They are less likely to seek physical and mental health care, potentially worsening self-reported health and mental health.9 The rising prevalence of medical debt and its harms have prompted federal and state policy response.10,11 In 2022, credit agencies extended the delay in reporting medical bills from 6 months to 1 year and removed medical debt below $500 from reports, in part, contributing to a 9% point reduction in the number of US adults with medical debt on their credit reports between 2022 to 2023.12 Still, medical debt persists; in 2024, mean medical debt in collection was approximately $2500.1

Measuring medical debt alone fails to capture how medical expenses influence other decisions regarding families' financial assets, such as savings, investments, and employment, that in turn may affect health.13 Experimental evidence suggests that medical debt forgiveness raises consumer credit scores.14 However, few studies extend these investigations beyond financial outcomes to consider financial behaviors. A recent survey-based study found that 17% of US households had taken out a loan or used a credit card to pay off a medical bill.1 There is scant peer-reviewed literature investigating how incurring medical debt influences financial decisions beyond the traditional banking system. Considering these financial decisions may provide a better understanding of the long-term consequences of medical debt and financial precarity on poor health over the life course.

An emerging measure of financial precarity may be the use of alternative financial services (AFS), defined as financial products provided by companies outside the traditional banking system.15,16 AFS can include products provided by car title lenders, payday lenders, and pawnbrokers, as compared to mainstream credit products such as credit cards and bank loans.17,18 Buy now, pay later (BNPL), payday loans, and cash advance services are 3 forms of AFS that provide easy access to short-term credit. BNPL is an interest-free loan repaid in installments over 4 to 6 months, while payday loans are high-interest loans, typically less than $500, repaid in one installment, 2 to 4 weeks after made.19 Cash advance services, often delivered via mobile apps, provide borrowers with short-term, interest-free funds repayed at the borrower's next paycheck.20,21 With minimal or no credit checks, these products are disproportionately used by financially precarious populations.22 In particular, these AFS pose a high risk when borrowers accumulate unsustainable debt loads, simultaneously take on multiple loans across multiple lenders, experience financial disruption, and face late fees, high interest rates, and automatic withdrawals from their bank account, leading to debt spirals.20,23,24 The Consumer Finance Protection Bureau (CFPB) and Federal Trade Commission (FTC) have made efforts to regulate these lenders, citing concerns of BNPL loan stacking and questionable marketing tactics, excessive payday loan fees and abusive collection practices, and undisclosed fees and interest rates among cash advance services.19-21,25

Grounded in cumulative disadvantage theory, whereby people with less advantage accrue disadvantage at a faster rate,26 we consider whether problematic medical debt affects subsequent use of AFS. Unlike secured debts, which are the result of active choices consumers make (such as to purchase a home, automobile, or consumer goods), problematic medical debt is often unexpected, unplanned, and follows a health event requiring medical care.1-3,9,27 Problematic medical debt can constrain access to credit and deplete available household financial resources, which may in turn increase reliance on alternative financial products such as payday loans, BNPL, and cash advance products to meet immediate financial needs.13 Moreover, use of these services is associated with poor health, including higher body mass index, waist circumference, and anxiety.28,29 Higher area-level concentration of traditional AFS lenders is associated with declines in health status and increases in all-cause mortality.30 More recently, a cross-sectional analysis found that BNPL use was associated with probable depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress.22 Problematic medical debt is not simply an indication of financial hardship, but may be a mechanism through which disadvantage compounds over time. In this study, we aimed to assess the potential association between medical debt and subsequent use of AFS (BNPL, payday loans, and cash advance products) among US adults.

Methods

Data

We used data from the Cumulative Life Stressors Impact on Mental Health and Well Being (CLIMB) Study, a nationally representative longitudinal survey of US adults aged 18 years or older. The CLIMB study draws from the NORC AmeriSpeak panel that represents 97% of US households. This prospective longitudinal study used CLIMB waves 4 (2023), 5 (2024), and 6 (2025) with online and phone-based surveys fielded annually between March and April in 2023, 2024, and 2025, respectively. The CLIMB study conducted a replenishment in wave 4, where new AmeriSpeak panel members were invited to join the CLIMB study. Among 7802 invited AmeriSpeak panelists for wave 4, 2479 completed the survey (response rate = 31.8%). In wave 5, 2144 of 3005 eligible CLIMB participants completed the survey (completion rate = 71.3%), while in wave 6, 2020 of 2969 eligible CLIMB participants completed the survey (completion rate = 68.0%). For this study, we included any participants with at least 2 years of consecutive survey participation and complete medical debt and AFS use data, yielding a final analytic sample of 2021 individuals. Sample construction is detailed in eFigure S1 in the Supplement. We applied survey weights to account for nonresponse and align the sample with the US Current Population Survey, generating nationally representative estimates. The parent study was deemed exempt by the NORC Institutional Review Board at the University of Chicago. The Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health Institutional Review Board deemed the de-identified secondary data analysis not human subjects research.

Measuring AFS use

The primary outcome, AFS use, was defined as a composite binary variable of 2 questions from the survey. In both waves 5 and 6 of the CLIMB survey, participants were asked:

“In the past 12 months, have you used a “buy now, pay later” plan?”

“In the past 12 months, have you used a “buy now, pay later” plan?”

“In the past 12 months, have you used payday loan or cash advance services where you borrow money to be repaid out of your next paycheck?”

“In the past 12 months, have you used payday loan or cash advance services where you borrow money to be repaid out of your next paycheck?”

We created a binary variable for any AFS use in the past year. If a participant endorsed at least BNPL use, payday loan, or cash advance service use in the past year, AFS use was coded as 1, otherwise 0.

Measuring medical debt

The primary exposure variable was an indicator for medical debt collected in waves 4 and 5. The CLIMB survey assessed medical debt with the following question about medical financial hardship, “In the past 12 months, did you have problems paying or an inability to pay any medical bills, such as bills for doctors, dentists, medication, or home care? Please include any bills you have had problems paying over the past 12 months, even if the initial bill was incurred more than 12 months ago.”3,31,32 Among those who reported having any medical debt, an additional survey item ascertained the amount of medical debt: “What was the total amount owed for the medical bills you’ve had problems paying in the last 12 months? Please estimate the total amount that you personally owed for medical bills, not including any amount paid for by health insurance. Please include the total amount that was owed, even if you have paid off part of that amount.” Amount of medical debt was captured as a categorical variable: less than $500, $500-$999, $1000-$2,499, $2500-$4,999, and $5000 or more.

Sociodemographic covariates

Additional covariates included age, gender, race, ethnicity, education, marital status, employment, household income, household savings, self-reported health status, and health insurance. Additional details on covariates are specified in the eMethods. Where possible, we imputed missing covariates from other survey items and the previous wave of CLIMB. For remaining missingness, we used multiple imputation by chained equations, constructing 20 imputed datasets to complete covariate data for all individuals in the sample.

Analytic approach

First, we summarized baseline sociodemographic characteristics, using the first observed wave for each individual (either wave 4 or wave 5). We calculated unweighted frequencies and weighted percentages for the total analytic sample and stratified the sample by those who ever reported medical debt as compared to those who never reported medical debt. Next, we ran 2 logistic regression models using generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable correlation structure, clustered at the individual level to account for repeated observations, to examine the association between medical debt at baseline and any use of AFS in the following wave, adjusting for baseline age, gender, race, ethnicity, marital status, education, employment, household income, household savings, self-reported health status, and health insurance. We compared possible correlation structures (autoregressive, exchangeable, unstructured) by computing Quasi-likelihood Information Criterion for each working model. Exchangeable and unstructured had nearly identical, low QIC values (QIC = 2835.443) compared to autoregressive with one lag (QIC = 2850.761), thus, exchangeable was selected for both fit and parsimony. Population average models were used to account for non-independent observations.

Model 1 used the binary medical debt measure as the primary independent variable, and Model 2 used the categorical measure of medical debt as the independent variable. We present estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CI) as the mean differences in predicted probabilities (ie, differential effects) of subsequent AFS use by baseline medical debt from both models, comparing those with medical debt to those without.

Finally, we conducted sensitivity analyses to assess robustness of our findings. A priori theory suggests that prior use of AFS is the strongest predictor of subsequent use. However, baseline measures of AFS use were not included in the main models because the CLIMB survey first collected these data in wave 5, and the limited sample of participants observed in waves 5 and 6 precluded inclusion of this adjustment in the primary analyses. Restricting the sample to participants observed in waves 5 and 6, we estimated 2 survey-weighted multivariable logistic regressions, Models 3 and 4, to examine the association between medical debt (binary and categorical measures) at wave 5 and AFS use at wave 6, adjusting for baseline AFS use and other covariates included in the main models. We computed robust standard errors for these models. P-values were 2-sided, statistical significance was set at P = 0.05, and STATA 18.0 was used for statistical analyses.

Descriptive characteristics

Multivariable logistic regression

In Figures 1 and 2, we present the results from the population average models examining the relationship between medical debt and subsequent AFS use. In model 1, having any medical debt was associated with a 11.9% point [95% CI: 7.3, 16.6] increase in the predicted probability of using AFS in the subsequent wave (P < 0.001). In model 2, compared to individuals with no medical debt, carrying $500-999 in medical debt was associated with a 14.2% point [95% CI: 5.6, 22.8] increase in the probability of subsequent AFS use, and carrying $1000-2499 was associated with a 17.4% point [95% CI: 7.6, 27.3] increase. Individuals with more than $5000 in medical debt were 10.2% points [95% CI: 1.3, 19.1] more likely to report AFS use than individuals with no medical debt. The probability of AFS use was comparable among adults with no medical debt and those carrying less than $500 (P = 0.151) and between $2500 and $4999 in medical debt (P = 0.094). Overall, our findings were robust to alternative debt categorizations between models 1 and 2.

Across both model specifications, females were 5.7% and 5.5% points more likely to use AFS as compared to males. Married individuals were 3.6% and 3.7% points less likely to use AFS than those not married. Importantly, higher household savings significantly reduced the probability of AFS use. For individuals with savings of $65 000 or greater, there was an associated 10.5% point reduction in predicted probability of AFS use as compared to individuals with less than $5000 in household savings in both models. Sensitivity analyses

In the sensitivity analysis, even after adjusting for baseline AFS use, estimates resembled those from the main analyses. We found qualitatively similar associations between medical debt and subsequent AFS use. In model 3, having any medical debt at wave 4 was associated with a 10.9% point [95% CI: 4.0, 17.9] increase in the probability of using AFS at wave 5 (P < 0.01). Again, when considering differences by medical debt amount in model 4, AFS use was significantly higher among individuals with $500 to $999 in debt (AME = 16.8% points, P < 0.05) and $1000 to $2499 (AME = 20.1% points, P < 0.01), while smaller or larger debt categories showed no significant associations. This pattern mimics that found within the GEE models. Consistent with a priori theory, across all models, baseline AFS use remained the strongest predictor of future use. eTable S2 presents full models 3 and 4 results from population-weighted logistic regressions with binary and categorical measures of medical debt, estimating the association between medical debt and subsequent AFS use.

Discussion

In this longitudinal, nationally representative cohort study of US adults, we found that individuals who had any medical debt were 11.9% points more likely to report any use of AFS in the following year, adjusting for demographic characteristics, household savings, and health status. In particular, this association was most pronounced among individuals reporting between $500 and $2500 of medical debt; there were no significant differences in the probability of AFS use when comparing those with modest (less than $500) and sizeable (between $2500 and $5000) medical debt burdens to those with no medical debt. Those with the most medical debt (more than $5000) were 10.2% points more likely to report AFS use in the subsequent year as compared to those with no medical debt; however, this was not significant in sensitivity analyses.

Our findings are comparable to Bickham and colleagues (2015), who found a 1% increase in the log sum of medical debt was associated with a $257 increase in payday loan debt, after adjusting for homeownership, student loans, and other unsecured debt.33 However, their findings were limited to individuals who filed for personal bankruptcy between 2005 and 2011 in the US Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District. As such, it is likely that their sample faced greater financial instability and higher levels of payday loan debt than the general population of adults with medical debt.33 A fifth of the sample had payday loan debt, while approximately 18% of our sample had reported either internet payday loan use or BNPL use.33 Few other quantitative studies have explored the relationship between medical debt and AFS use, partially due to challenges in measuring AFS left off credit reports. However, qualitative evidence provides useful insights about how households financially cope with a combination of payment plans, credit card debt, loans from family and friends, and payday loans, in the aftermath of medical crises and medical debt.34 The present study's contributions are centered around medical debt among a nationally representative sample of US adults and the exploration of multiple AFS, which may have more widespread use than only storefront payday loans.

Notably, we found no evidence of an association between small (< $500) or sizeable ($2500-$4999) amounts of medical debt and subsequent AFS use. It is possible that a $500 debt is too small an amount to induce financial strain and prompt use of AFS. A nationally representative KFF survey of US adults suggests that the most common (61%) financial response to medical debt accrual is cutting back on spending for food, clothing, and basic household items.35 Individuals may pay for modest amounts of medical debt with these cost-shifting strategies rather than taking on more debt. Moreover, in 2023, medical debt under $500 was removed from credit reports.12 It is also possible that without a negative impact on their credit score, those with less than $500 of medical debt are less likely to engage with services outside of the traditional banking system. It remains unclear why medical debt in the amount of $2500-4999 was not associated with AFS use. On one hand, this amount of debt may be too large to manage through BNPL, payday loans, or cash advance products alone,34,35 as payday loans and cash advances are typically less than $500, and in 2023, average BNPL loan size was $135.20,21,25,36 However, this would not explain the association between medical debt and AFS use among adults with medical debt ≥ $5000.

Another plausible explanation is that individuals who incur medical debt may also face limited access to conventional credit because of subprime credit scores or a lack of credit history, prior to accruing the debt.37 As a result, they may be more likely to use AFS at baseline, which often do not rely on traditional underwriting criteria (ie, credit scores).22 Lack of financial knowledge may also play a role in the observed association, as prior work suggests it is associated with both problematic medical debt and AFS use.38,39 Our findings indicate a short-term association between medical debt and AFS use; more work is needed to understand larger debts undertaken to finance medical bills.

Implications for policy and practice

Despite the gains of the ACA and Medicaid expansion in the past decade, almost half of all US adults report difficulty with health care affordability in 2025.40 A constellation of factors contribute to health care affordability constraints, including but not limited to increasing health care prices, uninsurance and underinsurance, high levels of cost sharing, limited awareness of third-party payment plans, and barriers to patient financial assistance programs.41,42 Prior research suggests patients' awareness of financial assistance programs may be limited, and in some cases, this has been deliberate, with some health systems devising strategies to prevent patients from learning about charity care programs.43,44 Moreover, while nonprofit hospitals are required to have charity care policies (Section 501r4 of the Internal Revenue Code), hospitals vary widely in their eligibility criteria for financial assistance programs, and prior research suggests that hospitals' charity care provision is not aligned with their obligations.45,46 In recent years, several states have enacted policies that establish minimum standards for financial assistance programs, among other policies that aim to stem the tide of medical debt.47

The unaffordability of care is likely to be exacerbated by emerging policies. The 2025 Budget Reconciliation Act and the expiring ACA enhanced premium tax credits together are projected to leave 14 million Americans uninsured by 2034, dramatically increasing the risk of medical debt.48,49 Moreover, the loss of the premium tax credits is estimated to double average premium payments from $888 to $1904 for Marketplace enrollees.50 Conversely, in June 2025, a federal court overturned a new CFPB rule entirely prohibiting the inclusion of medical debt on credit reports and credit scores.51,52 Soon after, a new CFPB interpretive rule preempted laws in 15 states prohibiting medical debt on credit reports.53 These policies and mounting medical costs may lead to a greater share of consumers with medical debt on their credit report, reduced access to traditional forms of credit, and more likely to use AFS.51,54,55

In addition to taking advantage of financial precarity, AFS use may contribute to accumulating disadvantage in health among consumers with medical debt.2,19,25,27,56 In this study, we consider the short-term association of medical debt with the use of AFS in the following year. Drawing from cumulative disadvantage theory, future work should build on these findings, exploring how accumulating financial burden can manifest as poor health and inequality throughout the life course.26

Limitations

Strengths of our study include the nationally representative survey sample as well as the longitudinal design that allowed us to assess the longitudinal association between medical debt and use of AFS. However, this study is not without its limitations. Critically, we were unable to include a lagged measure of AFS use as a covariate in our primary models, leading to ambiguous temporal ordering between our exposure and outcome. Individuals who report medical debt may already be using AFS at baseline, and our sensitivity analyses suggest prior use of AFS is the strongest predictor of subsequent AFS use. However, our sensitivity analyses using logistic regression models with only 2 waves of CLIMB and accounting for AFS use in the prior year yield similar and significant associations of medical debt with the probability of using AFS. Future work with longer durations of panel data should assess the impact of medical debt on transitions to and between AFS use. Second, our analysis is subject to residual confounding from health shocks, which may lead individuals to both incur medical debt and rely on AFS. Although we adjust for self-reported health status, this measure is unlikely to account for all relevant confounding factors. Third, the AFS use and medical debt measures were based on self-report, and thus, subject to recall bias. It is possible that we underestimate the prevalence of both medical debt and AFS use. Surprise billing, lapses in insurance, and interest fees can all contribute to underreporting of medical debt.3,44 While increasingly popular, BNPL use may have been underreported due to consumers not realizing they were using them or associating use with specific brand names (ie, Klarna) rather than the phrase “buy now, pay later” that was used in the CLIMB survey question. Finally, AFS use was defined as any use of BNPL, payday loans, or cash advance services within the last 12 months, masking heterogeneity in frequency of AFS use or amount of AFS debt. We cannot differentiate between infrequent AFS use and sustained use, the latter of which may be of greater theoretical interest when considering financial and health consequences.

Conclusion

In summary, we find that having any medical debt is significantly associated with use of AFS in the following year, adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics and other household financial assets. The longitudinal association between medical debt and AFS use may reflect a broader cycle in which health challenges and costly medical care are associated with financial precarity and poorer health.
