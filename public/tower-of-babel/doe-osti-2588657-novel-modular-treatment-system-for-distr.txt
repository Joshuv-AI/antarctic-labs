Executive Summary

The overarching goal of this work was to accelerate the commercialization of a distributed,
modular, agile treatment technology - the Modular Encapsulated Two-stage Anaerobic
Biological (METAB) system - by advancing it to pilot-scale. Specifically, the METAB
system was developed to treat high strength wastewater (5,000-35,000 mg/L chemical
oxygen demand (COD)), produce hydrogen (H2) and methane (CH4), and achieve these
outcomes without a membrane for retention of microorganisms. Eliminating the membrane
lowers costs and substantially reduces operational energy consumption. This is directly in
line with the DOE – IEDO goal of accelerating the innovation and adoption of cost-effective
technologies that reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions by generating energy from high
strength wastewater, rather than using energy to treat the COD in the wastewater in a
centralized manner. The innovation behind the system is the use of encapsulated
microorganisms to ensure that microorganisms are present at a high population density and
are retained in the treatment system, even as the wastewater flows through at a relatively high
rate. The scope of this project involved addressing several previously identified barriers that
prevented successful utilization of the METAB system: the lack of a robust encapsulant
chemistry, the need to enrich microorganisms on a given feed prior to their encapsulation and
use, the lack of a techno-economic assessment (TEA) and life cycle analysis (LCA) on the
system, and confusion regarding whether a two-stage system provided benefits over a one-
stage system and whether active gas extraction in a 1st-stage could improve system
performance.

During this project all proposed milestones were met. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) was
identified as a robust and biocompatible encapsulant when formed into a bead configuration.
In addition, the encapsulation of methanogenic organisms grown on activated carbon
improved CH4 production by about 20 times. Nevertheless, this resulted in a less stable
encapsulant bead. Aggressively mixed reactors, such as those with a rapidly spinning stir bar
or mixing paddle, resulted in breakage of the PAC-containing beads, whereas a flow-through
reactor configuration resulted in stable beads. PEG beads containing suspended cultures were
always stable. A seed culture consisting of organisms enriched on brewery and protein
wastewater (for 1st-stage fermentative reactors) or brewery, protein, and dairy wastewater
(for 2nd-stage methanogenic reactors) was shown to be flexible and able to degrade >90% of
the soluble COD in the feed within a 4-week period when fed lipid, dairy, or brewery
wastewater. No “keystone” organisms consistently drove system COD removal and gas
production, which demonstrated the construction of a community a priori for encapsulation
may not be necessary for effective performance. It was hypothesized, and verified during
pilot testing, that a mixture of pre-enriched cultures could be used to create a viable and
effective seed culture.

We also developed predictive models that effectively described gas extraction. The extraction
of H2 increased biological H2 production. Gas extraction was also effective in capturing
nearly all of the gas produced, resulting in no residual gas in the dissolved phase in the
effluent. Gas extraction did not, however, improve fermentation or COD removal. Gas
extraction could still be important if the removal of dissolved CH4 were important for
reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from the effluent stream.

 6

We have modified the ADM1-based model of the METAB system to enable better prediction
of 1st-stage H2 production rates with gas extraction and ethanol in the feed. The development
of an open-source simulator to describe METAB performance and provide LCA and TEA of
the complete METAB system was completed. Additionally, the impact of METAB
deployment on operational cost and emissions of centralized water resource recovery
facilities (WRRFs) was estimated using benchmark WRRF models. This allowed the
opportunity space for industrial wastewater pretreatment across the US to be identified.

A pilot study was completed at our partner location, Fulton Brewery. Both the two-stage and
single stage systems degraded >90% of the influent COD at a total hydraulic residence time
(HRT) of 3 days. An active gas extraction system was trialed on the 1st-stage of the METAB
system during a portion of the pilot study. As in the lab-scale system, gas extraction was able
to remove all of the dissolved gas in the system but did not improve fermentation or COD
removal. We varied the overall system HRT between 13 and 3 days twice to determine if
biomass growth and decay inside the encapsulant would have a negative impact on
encapsulant stability. No impact on the encapsulant integrity was observed. As the
temperature dropped in Minneapolis over the winter months, the COD removal efficiency in
the two-stage system declined to around 80%. This was attributed to a decline in
fermentation in the 1st-stage of the system with lower temperatures. Little difference in
overall performance between the two-stage METAB system and the single-stage
encapsulated system was observed throughout the experiment, though performance in the
single-stage system was not impacted by the decline in temperature as much as it was in the
two-stage system. Both the single-stage and two-stage systems performed exceptionally well.

With respect to commercialization plans and status, the technology was accepted by the
Chevron Studio program, a partnership between Chevron Technology Ventures and the National
Renewable Energy Laboratory to advance scale-up and commercialization of “innovative, early-
stage technologies that have the potential to enable a lower carbon future” (Chevron Studio,
accessed 2025). An entrepreneur has been paired with the technology and is in the process of
customer discovery and assessing scale-up options. We are hopeful that additional funding can be
obtained through a variety of sources to continue to advance this promising technology through
commercialization.

1. Introduction

In the United States, approximately 32 billion gallons of wastewater/day are treated by over
15,000 WRRF (Rauch-Williams et al., 2018). This wastewater includes the wastewater
produced by industries, such as dairies and food and beverage producers, as well as lower
strength municipal wastewater. Industrial wastewater is often high in organic carbon,
measured as chemical oxygen demand (COD) (~5,000-35,000 mg/L COD), variable in flow
and strength over time, and discharged to centralized WRRFs where it is diluted with lower
strength municipal wastewater (~150-300 mg/L COD) and treated aerobically to convert
COD to carbon dioxide (CO2) and waste biosolids (Alayu and Yirgu, 2018; Amenorfenyo et
al., 2019; Arantes et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2021; Eckenfelder, 2000; Pikaar et al., 2022; Rao
et al., 2007). This blending results in simultaneous (1) dilution of the high-strength
wastewater, decreasing or eliminating the possibility of recovering resources from it, and (2)
an increase in COD and waste variability for blended waste streams, with a concomitant
increase in the difficulty, cost, and energy intensity of aerobic treatment. One of the most
 7

common WRRF treatment technologies is conventional activated sludge, where energy-
intensive aeration, responsible for about 50% of the total energy use at a WRRF, is used to
degrade the wastewater COD prior to discharge (Chen et al., 2022; Duncan and Noran, 2003;
Rosso et al., 2008). With wastewater treatment responsible for about 1-3% of the United
States’ electricity demand (USEPA, 2006), methods are needed to recover the energy present
in industrial wastewater rather than expend energy for its treatment at a centralized WRRF.

A variety of anaerobic biological technologies are applicable to decentralized industrial
wastewater treatment and energy (hydrogen (H2) and methane (CH4)) recovery, including
continuously stirred-tank reactors (CSTRs), plug-flow reactors (PFRs), upflow anaerobic
sludge blanket reactors (UASBs), and anaerobic membrane bioreactors (AnMBRs) (Nkuna et
al., 2021; Rocha-Meneses et al., 2022). Some of these are characterized by high maintenance
or energy requirements and/or costs (Cowley and Brorsen, 2018; Maaz et al., 2019; Mariraj
Mohan and Swathi, 2022; Shin and Bae, 2018), or have the drawback of biomass washout.
An alternative to these existing technologies is the Modular Encapsulated Two-stage
Anaerobic Biological (METAB) system investigated in this research (Figure 1) (Chen et al.,
2022; Zhu et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2020). The METAB system is designed
to treat high-strength food and beverage wastewater anaerobically, using microbial
encapsulation to separate the solids and hydraulic residence times (SRT and HRT,
respectively). Microbial encapsulation retains biomass in a polymer matrix within the
reactor, preventing microbial washout and allowing for a smaller reactor footprint (Lay et al.,
2019). An additional benefit is that a high density of biomass can be encapsulated and added
to the reactors, resulting in high activity, low HRTs, and rapid startup (Ali et al., 2015; Li et
al., 2009; Sekoai and Daramola, 2018; Zhang et al., 2020). Finally, the encapsulated
microbial community can be carefully selected, which should support the degradation of
target compounds and the production of desired products (Abigail and Das, 2015; Huang et
al., 2022).

The METAB system has great promise. For example, in the Twin Cities metropolitan area
alone, high strength industrial discharges contribute approximately 22% of the COD
discharged to the system of WRRFs (~33 million kg COD/yr (Sprouse, 2020), which is
treated aerobically. This results in the annual use of ~25 million kWh (0.8 kWh/kg COD) and
costs of ~$1.7M/yr (Sprouse, 2020). This is representative of the mixture of flows that
WRRFs receive nationally. Indeed, in 2023, federal and state governments' spending on
water utilities in the United States was approximately $173 billion, equivalent to 0.6% of the
 8

GDP (Congressional Budget Office, accessed 2025). The food and beverage industry in the
United States accounts for 6% of total industrial CO2 emissions (Cresko et al., 2022). The
U.S. Department of Energy has put forward a decarbonization roadmap for major CO2
emitting industries (Bhadbhade and Patel, 2020; Meyers et al., 2016), which includes
expanding the use of anaerobic treatment for high-organic strength food industry wastewaters
(Cresko et al., 2022) to reduce on-site energy consumption (Chen et al., 2016). If
successfully commercialized and deployed to treat industry wastewaters, the METAB system
would decrease the energy used by WRRFs. It would also facilitate the recovery of energy
onsite at industries where it could be used to offset heating or electricity use.

With respect to commercialization plans and status, the general basis of the METAB
technology was accepted by the Chevron Studio program, a partnership between Chevron
Technology Ventures and the National Renewable Energy Laboratory to advance scale-up and
commercialization of “innovative, early-stage technologies that have the potential to enable a lower
carbon future” (Chevron Studio, accessed 2025). An entrepreneur has been paired with the
technology and is in the process of customer discovery and assessing scale-up options. We are
hopeful that additional funding can be obtained through a variety of sources to continue to advance
this extremely promising technology through commercialization.

2. Background

Anaerobic wastewater treatment facilitates the degradation of organic compounds via four
distinct phases: hydrolysis, acidogenesis, acetogenesis, and methanogenesis, with CH4 and
CO2 as end products (Uddin and Wright, 2023). This process may be performed in single-
and two-stage configurations. Because the organisms responsible for methanogenesis are
particularly slow-growing and require different operational conditions for optimum
performance (Meegoda et al., 2018; Onwosi et al., 2019), the separation of the
hydrolysis/acidogenesis/acetogenesis stages (i.e., 1st-stage) from the
acetogenesis/methanogenesis stages (i.e., 2nd-stage) has advantages. Researchers have
observed better stability with high organic loading and improved degradation and energy
recovery in two-stage systems (Fuentes et al., 2021; Rajendran et al., 2020). Research by
Paranjpe and coworkers found a 41.9% and 9.8% increase in biogas yields and volatile solids
removal efficiency, respectively, in a two-stage system compared to a single-stage system co-
digesting food waste and sewage sludge (Paranjpe et al., 2023). Another study observed that
a two-stage system maintained its performance in terms of COD reduction and CH4
production across different hydraulic residence times (HRTs), whereas a single-stage
comparison system declined in performance with varying HRT (Maspolim et al., 2015).
Similarly, Schievano et al. (2014) reported the ability of two-stage systems to increase
energy recovery from biomass digestion compared to single-stage AD systems. Nevertheless,
two-stage systems may require more space, capital equipment, and operational cost. Thus, it
is important to test their efficacy prior to deployment.

In addition to separating the various stages of anaerobic digestion into different reactors, the
separation of HRT and SRT is critical for effective small-footprint anaerobic treatment,
because slow-growing methanogens and syntrophs may readily wash out of a system. As
mentioned above, AnMBRs can be cost-effective, because they have the potential to use less
net energy compared to aerobic processes used in WRRFs (Vinardell et al., 2021). AnMBRs
are known for operational stability, which provides the potential to treat challenging
 9

industrial wastes characterized by high strength, high salinity, and poor biomass granulation
(Dvořák et al., 2016). Nevertheless, while AnMBRs demonstrate a number of benefits over
traditional systems, a variety of drawbacks also exist. Membrane fouling is a major drawback
of AnMBRs (Meng et al., 2009; Liao et al., 2007; Maaz et al., 2019), requiring chemical
cleaning and periodic backwashing that ultimately result in increased chemical expenses,
higher energy requirements (from 0.038 to 5.68 kW h m-3), membrane replacement, lower
system productivity, and shorter membrane lifespans (Maaz et al., 2019; Shin & Bae, 2018).
Alternative reactor designs that enable the low-energy separation of HRT and SRT are
needed.

Encapsulation technology has garnered interest because of its ability to separate HRT from
SRT, while also facilitating rapid system startup and the application of specific desired
organisms (Chen et al., 2021; Lutz et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2020).
Encapsulation also protects microbes from stressful environmental and operational
conditions, allowing them to grow within the encapsulant (e.g., Wang et al., 2023) and
improving effluent quality (Qin et al., 2020). Community shifts are also seen within an
encapsulant, allowing for some degree of adaptation to occur over time (e.g., Xu et al., 2017;
Yang et al., 2020).

A variety of encapsulant chemistries have been explored in the literature. These include
polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) (Senko et al., 2019), alginate (Cruz et al., 2013; Sekoai and
Daramola, 2018; Springael, 2019), alginate/polyacrylamide blends (Darnell et al., 2013; Sun
et al., 2012), carrageenan (dos Santos et al., 1996; Wijffels et al, 1994), polyurethane, agar,
polyethylene glycol (PEG) (Hamid et al., 2014; Isaka et al., 2012; Singh et al., 2013), and
chitosan (Peniche et al., 2003). Core-shell beads have also been explored, enabling the
creation two separate environments inside one bead (Bae et al., 2017; Minh et al., 2021;
Tang et al., 2021), though they are a challenge to fabricate. Solid particles may also be added
to polymers to create certain desired encapsulant bead properties. For example, activated
carbon has been added to encapsulants to increase strength (Garrido-Herrera et al., 2006;
Hoshi et al., 2020; Park et al., 2007; Qiu et al., 2007). With respect to PEG, encapsulants
have been used for the remediation of trichloroethylene (Hamid et al., 2014), the treatment of
palm oil mill effluent (Singh et al., 2013), and, commonly, for anammox-based nitrogen
removal processes. PEG is biocompatible and permeable, yet strong (Killion et al., 2011).
Perhaps its greatest advantage is its longevity. Isaka et al. operated a full-scale nitritation-
anammox plant using PEG encapsulation for over a year with the encapsulants still intact
(Isaka et al., 2017). A generous estimated lifetime for PEG encapsulants is 10 years (Isaka et
al., 2012). Prior to this research, however, only alginate encapsulants had been tested in an
anaerobic system containing high strength wastewater (Chen et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2020),
and these encapsulants were not sufficiently robust for long-term treatment (Chen et al.,
2022).

In prior research performed by our research groups, a pilot-scale METAB system was
installed from September 2020 to April 2021 at Fulton Brewing Company in Minneapolis,
MN, to treat their high-strength wastewater (Chen et al., 2022). From this, it was found that
the METAB system had a fast start-up period of 16 days, after which CH4 was at least 60%
of the methanogenic reactor headspace. Nevertheless, the alginate encapsulant used in the
system, although biologically compatible, was not robust and broke apart after about 25 days.
This physical deterioration resulted in biomass escape and washout, lower volumes of gas
 10

production, and lower soluble COD (sCOD) removal than a side-by-side AnMBR, which
functioned as a positive control. In addition, the encapsulated community developed
differently than the community in the AnMBR, resulting in a community that was not
optimal for the treatment of the brewery wastewater. Finally, no robust modeling tools
existed to help determine where deployment of the METAB system would be most beneficial
to industries and to the centralized WRRF, with respect to cost and energy savings.

Encapsulation has great potential, but more work was needed to understand how to optimize
this technology for improved performance. A major concern when this research began was
the stability of the encapsulant over an extended period (Rodrigues et al., 2020; Wang et al.,
2022). It was also not known how the community within encapsulants adapted over time and
whether organisms could leak into the encapsulants from the bulk liquid and result in
unintended interactions or a loss of the desired, and originally deployed, community
(Nordmeier and Chidambaram, 2018). The collection of gas from an anaerobic system was
also a continual challenge, with most systems simply recovering biogas from the headspace
of the reactor (Crone et al., 2016; Souza et al., 2011), leaving a significant portion of the CH4
produced during anaerobic treatment dissolved in the liquid effluent (Crone et al, 2016).
Having a predictive understanding of how gas extraction affected fermentation and gas
collection was therefore critical to understanding how, and if, it should be used. Finally,
being able to successfully deploy new technologies to accomplish low-carbon and low-cost
treatment requires simulation tools that can be adapted to describe a variety of industries and
local cost drivers. The development of such a simulation tool to describe the METAB system
was considered critical to ensure that the METAB system could be optimized for a range of
practical applications.

As a result, the goals of this research were to advance the METAB system to pilot-scale, and
specifically, to (1) develop a robust and reliable microbial seed that could be encapsulated for
flexible deployment, (2) develop an encapsulant chemistry that was stable, biocompatible,
easy to produce, and safe, (3) develop and validate new and existing process models,
including ones related to active gas extraction (H2 and/or CH4), (4) develop an open-source
process simulator for the METAB system, and (5) demonstrate METAB system performance
at a pilot scale with real high strength industrial wastewater (Figure 2).

When we began this research our team had a strong base of knowledge in this area. The
University of Minnesota team had developed a distributed, two-stage laboratory-scale system

and CH4 (2nd-stage) as fuel gases (Prieto et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2019; Zhu

multiple wastewater streams, modeled the system, and determined the effect of technical and
operational parameters on performance. We had also set up a complete two-stage system at a
local brewery and treated real process wastewater while generating a mixed H2-CH4 gas
stream. In addition, the Guest group had experience developing simulation tools such as
those proposed in this research, including BioSTEAM – the Biorefinery Simulation and
Techno-Economic Analysis Modules (Cortes-Peña et al., 2020; Shi and Guest, 2020; Cortes-

(<0.1 s per simulation) mass/energy balances, techno-economic analysis (TEA), and life
cycle assessment (LCA). The team was well positioned to perform this research, which was
demonstrated by the success achieved throughout the project.

3. Results and Discussion

The results and discussion are organized by task, below.

Task 1: Develop robust microbial seed
For Task 1, our specific goals were to establish adaptable H2-producing and methanogenic
cultures for encapsulation. Encapsulation is extremely promising, but is itself material and
cost intensive, necessitating that a single encapsulated community be able to flexibly degrade
 12

a variety of influent waste streams. In this work, we focused on assembling and
encapsulating pre-enriched microbial communities to evaluate their performance on various
feeds in a two-stage anaerobic system with the goal of determining if one community could
be assembled that was able to adapt and degrade multiple waste streams effectively.

To do this, two-stage flow-through suspended growth reactors were set-up to enrich
microorganisms fed (to the 1st-stage) starch-rich, protein-rich, and lipid-rich synthetic
wastewater as well as real brewery and dairy wastewater. Each of the 1st-stage reactors
discharged to an intermediate tank and then to a 2nd-stage methanogenic reactor. All reactors
were monitored for the production of volatile fatty acids (VFAs), COD degradation, and gas
production. Samples were taken for deep 16S rRNA gene sequencing in an attempt to test the
hypothesis that specific microbial community members would be correlated to good (>80%)
COD degradation in a given feed. After sequence analysis, communities were constructed
from the suspended microbial communities in these reactors, encapsulated, and tested for
COD degradation performance with the same five wastewaters (synthetic starch-rich,
protein-rich, and lipid-rich wastewater and real brewery and dairy wastewater).

Studies focused on the flow-through suspended growth reactors have been published (Ajayi
et al., 2025). In the flow-through suspended growth reactors, COD degradation reached 77-
96% across both reactor stages and was stable. Results showed that the number of total
bacteria (as 16S rRNA gene copies) did not correlate to soluble chemical oxygen demand
(sCOD) removal, indicating that the community structure and/or specific community
members, not just biomass quantity, were important for good performance. Results also
showed that feed type strongly influenced carbon removal and microbial community
structure for 1st-stage fermenting communities, but not 2nd-stage methanogenic communities.
In this study, the “core” community members were defined as organisms common to all of
either the 1st- or 2nd-stage reactors irrespective of the feed they received and were present in
at least 50% of the samples throughout the entire experiment. “Unique” community members
were specific to a single feed, and hence, only present in either the 1st- or 2nd-stage reactors
receiving that feed. In both 1st- or 2nd-stage communities, only one core genera and no unique
genera were positively and significantly correlated to sCOD removal. Our results suggested
that one cannot construct a community containing specific populations in lieu of enrichment.
Nevertheless, a single diverse encapsulated anaerobic community should provide good
(>80%) carbon removal when fed a variety of influents, if time is provided for enrichment
after deployment. Given these results, we were interested in understanding whether there
were particular enriched communities that were better suited for degradation of a variety of
wastewaters after encapsulation.

Communities were taken from these flow-through suspended growth enrichments and mixed
in different proportions, encapsulated, and tested on different wastewater streams to
determine their performance, adaptability to certain feeds, and the evolution of microbial
community structure over time. The communities were also tested to ensure that 90% of the
influent COD removal could be degraded by these encapsulated cultures. To do this, a three-
stage reactor system was used. Briefly, reactors were operated in three phases: an initial
batch phase, a continuous plug-flow phase, and a final batch phase. The batch phases were
set up in 120-mL serum bottles containing 60 mL of specific wastewater feeds. Encapsulated
cultures were added to the reactors (15 beads/reactor) and VFA production and sCOD
degradation were monitored for 5 days. Following the initial batch phase, the beads were
 13

moved from the batch reactors into 7-mL flow-through reactors. These flow-through reactors
were supplied with the same feeds used in the initial batch phase. The flow-through reactors
were operated for 2 weeks with an HRT of 45 min. After the 2-week flow-through period, the
beads were again removed and transferred into a final batch phase reactor that was operated
identically to the 1st batch phase reactor. The communities in the initial and final beads were
compared.

Results (Ajayi, 2024) showed that 1st-stage fermenting communities (brewery and protein)
and 2nd-stage methanogenic communities (brewery, dairy, and protein) were capable of
degrading multiple high-strength wastewaters, achieving COD removal > 90% within four
weeks of startup. Initial experiments showed that encapsulated communities did not degrade
brewery wastewater as effectively as the other feeds. Nevertheless, when a brewery
wastewater-adapted community containing Selenomonas was added to a protein-adapted
community, it effectively degraded not only brewery wastewater but also the other feed
types. We also observed that similar organisms were able to adapt to and degrade (i) dairy
and protein-rich wastes and (ii) protein and lipid-rich wastes, regardless of the composition
of the initial microbial community. We assessed the importance of HRT on COD removal
and overall performance and found that for brewery wastewater performance improved when
the 1st-stage HRT was maintained at or below 2 days. Hence, we concluded that 1st-stage
HRT significantly affects microbial activity and performance across both treatment stages for
brewery, and likely other high strength wastewater feeds. In the 2nd-stage, excellent COD
removal was achieved within an additional 28 days. For full-scale application, however,
adjusting operational parameters is crucial to achieve the optimal HRT for maximum
performance. Finally, we observed that the 1st-stage communities evolved and shifted in
response to the feedstock, while the 2nd-stage communities remained consistent and flexible
regardless of the initial (1st-stage) influent that they received. These results validated our
hypothesis that encapsulated communities can adapt to their environment, as the brewery and
protein-adapted cultures diversified following enrichment on different feeds. We also
hypothesized that a single community could be assembled to degrade a variety of wastes as
long as the important organisms needed for degradation of each feedstock were present and
enriched. This was also confirmed. The findings from Task 1 were directly applied to Task 5.
This work is currently being revised for publication.

PEG beads performed well when tested for biocompatibility, with alginate as a positive control.
PEG encapsulation of fermentative (1st-stage) biomass and methanogenic (2nd-stage) biomass
resulted in beads that were able to produce gas at levels near or above that produced in alginate
beads. With PEG encapsulation of methanogenic biomass, a minimum threshold of biomass had
to be encapsulated to observe gas production. It was hypothesized that below that level, the
biomass was killed, resulting in no gas production. It was thought that when higher biomass
concentrations were encapsulated, they were able to protect at least some of the organisms
present from inactivation by ammonium persulfate, the strong oxidant used for polymerization of
PEG.

Because of the synergistic relationships present in methanogenic communities (Li et al., 2019;
McCarty and Smith, 1986), we hypothesized that CH4 production in PEG beads would be
improved by encapsulating methanogenic communities already established as granules or
biofilms (as compared to unaggregated suspended cultures); experimental results supported this
hypothesis (Figure 4). Results were normalized by bead number, because a large quantity of
extracellular polymeric substance-associated protein was expected in encapsulants containing
biofilm and the beads were all the same size. When normalized in this manner, PEG beads
encapsulating granular cultures had significantly higher cumulative gas production, 2.8 times
more, compared to beads encapsulating suspended cultures (p = 0.003) and PEG beads
encapsulating PAC-supported biofilms (Table 2, bead 13) achieved ~20 times more CH4
production than encapsulated suspended cultures (p = 3.8 × 10-4) and also significantly more CH4
production than granular cultures (p = 7.4 × 10-4). Replicate batches of PAC-supported biofilms
produced significantly different amounts of CH4 (p = 2.7 × 10-4), though all produced much
higher quantities than those observed with encapsulated suspended or granular biomass.

Task 3: Process model development and validation

For Task 3, the goal was to first develop and validate a model for dissolved gas (H2 and CH4)
extraction from the first-stage METAB reactor. The validated dissolved gas extraction model
was then integrated into QSDsan and used in conjunction with a modified ADM1 model to
predict the effects of dissolved gas extraction on the performance of the first-stage METAB
reactor. The results of this task are presented in Song et al. (2024) and Song et al. (2025).

The developed gas extraction model makes the following major assumptions: (i) liquid in the
reactor is well-mixed, and acts as an ideal continuously stirred tank reactor, (ii) the hollow
fiber membrane acts as an ideal plug flow reactor, (iii) the shell-side of the membrane has
uniform pressure, and the gas composition in each shell-side fragment is well-mixed, (iv) all
gases are ideal. The model did not use fitting parameters to fit the model to our own data set.
However, due to the lack of a closed-form formula for the diffusion coefficients of the gases
of interest in water, the association parameters in the Wilke-Chang correlation were fitted to
experimentally determined diffusion coefficients from various papers (O2 from Han and
Bartles, 1996; N2 from Sharma and Adhikari, 2014; H2 and CH4 from the CRC Handbook).
Additionally, liquid mass transfer coefficients were approximated using the Leveque
approximation. We first compared our model results to O2 removal efficiency data provided
by Permselect. The model successfully predicted the O2 removal efficiency to an average
error of 1%.

To further validate the model for all relevant gases, we performed a set of gas extraction
experiments using a PDMS hollow-fiber membrane module (Permselect, PDMSXA-1000).
H2, CH4, and a mixed H2/CH4 gas stream were bubbled into a 500-mL flask of pure water
using an aquarium air-stone. The water was then pumped through the membrane module and
a vacuum pressure was applied to the membrane module to extract the dissolved gases
(Figure 6).

After confirming model accuracy in pure water, we performed a similar experiment to extract
CH4 from synthetic wastewater. Because synthetic wastewater contains various surfactants,
bubbling gases directly into the flask caused significant foaming. Therefore, the synthetic

wastewater was first pumped through a hollow fiber membrane module with a counter-

membrane module to extract CH4 from the liquid. This data shows a fit similar to the mixed
gas data, with a slope of 0.91.

Overall, experimental validation showed that the model had good predictive capability for
gas recovery from pure water over the range of operating parameters (linear flow velocities,
temperatures, and vacuum pressures), with an average absolute error of 10%–16% in lab-
scale gas recovery experiments, and 11% when compared against published data from the
literature. Additionally, the model was predictive of 98% of the variation of removal
efficiencies from lab-scale testing, and 96% of the variation of removal efficiencies from
published data sets.

System-wide simulations using QSDsan suggest that the removal of H2 from the first-stage
reactor in a two-stage system is not economically feasible due to the addition of the side-
stream pumping module and limited improvement of fermentation kinetics. Nevertheless, we
performed bench-scale and pilot-scale testing of H2 gas extraction from the first-stage
reactor, while monitoring COD removal, VFA production, and gas production to verify these
predictions (Task 5).

The gas extraction model was implemented in QSDsan and used to simulate a single-stage
METAB system (fermentation only) and was validated using a laboratory-scale first-stage
METAB reactor with brewery wastewater feed. Data from a sister project (Figure 8) shows a
significant increase in H2 production when a side-stream hollow fiber membrane was used to
recover dissolved gases.

Baseline ADM1 was found to have limitations when it came to predicting the effects of
dissolved H2 recovery on H2 production in an anaerobic fermentation reactor: (i) ADM1 does
 22

not have a way to control methanogen growth; methanogenic activity quickly overshadows
fermentation activity (H2 production), and (ii) ADM1 kinetic parameters were not optimized
for fermentation reactors. Therefore, a modified ADM1 (mADM1) model was synthesized
by adding metabolic pathways for the production and accumulation of ethanol and lactate,
two important fermentation byproducts that are normally negligible in single-stage AD
reactors, but not in anaerobic fermentation.

Parameter fitting of both ADM1 and mADM1 was performed against the bench-scale data
that was collected for the sister project (Figure 8). The data are for two parallel first-stage
fermentation reactors containing encapsulated microbial communities and fed with real
brewery wastewater (BWW). Low HRTs were not an issue as the encapsulation matrix
prevented washout. Due to the widely varying influent concentrations, the BWW influent
characteristics were averaged for the first 28 days, followed by the remaining 22 days.
Unknown influent substrates (sugar, amino acid, long chain fatty acids, ethanol, and lactate)
and unknown influent particulate matter (composites, carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins)
were fitted for both sets of influents. It was assumed that no methanogens existed in the
encapsulation matrix and first BWW influent, but that a small amount of methanogens were
present in the second BWW influent. This is due to the sudden spike in methanogenesis after
refilling the BWW influent on Day 28. Global sensitivity analysis was performed on the
kinetic parameters, and parameter fitting was performed through the use of a genetic
algorithm to minimize RMSE on cumulative H2 and CH4 production.

Both the fitted baseline ADM1 and mADM1 models were able to capture the H2 and CH4
production in the first-stage METAB reactor (Figure 9). Total RMSE between the
experimental data and simulated H2 collection rates from ADM1 and mADM1 were 43.8 mL
and 43.5 mL, respectively.

Analysis of fitted parameters, including the fractionation of the wastewater, indicated that
the addition of lactate and ethanol state variables can be useful for the modeling of alcohol-
laden wastewaters, such as those originating from breweries. It was also interesting that the
half-saturation constant for H2 inhibition was significantly larger than default ADM1 values
in both the fitted ADM1 and mADM1 parameter sets. This suggests that fermentation
reactors have significantly different kinetic parameters when properly controlled, either
through pre-treatment of microbial communities, as in this work, or through temperature and
pH controls. The results of Task 3 indicate that we need a better understanding of how feed
composition and reactor operating conditions change the behavior of the AD system. Gaining
this understanding will allow us to better design systems that can efficiently treat a variety of
influent wastewaters.

Task 4: Process simulator development
For Task 4, the goal was to develop an open-source process simulator for the METAB
system, and to evaluate the implications of METAB deployment on centralized WRRFs. The
results of this task are presented in Zhang et al. (2024) and in a manuscript that is in
preparation. A summary of the project outcomes is provided below.

To guide early-stage research and development of the METAB system, we developed an
integrated process simulator in QSDsan, an open-source simulator in Python. Our simulator
couples process modeling with design algorithms to support TEA and LCA, enabling robust
 24

sustainability evaluation under uncertainty. Using this tool, we assessed 3,552 distinct
combinations of 11 design and operational decisions, conducted uncertainty and sensitivity
analyses, and mapped optimized designs and operational conditions (Figure 11). Key design
and technological parameters that were varied included HRT, encapsulant volume and
longevity, reactor type, and operational conditions, among others.

Figure 11. Illustration of the modeling and analysis framework used in this study. Solid
arrows indicate the order in which the stages of the analysis were performed as well as the
flows of information. Reproduced from Zhang et al. (2024).

Our analysis revealed that the viability of METAB systems depends heavily on a relatively
small set of influential parameters. In particular, HRT and encapsulant longevity (i.e., bead
lifetime) emerged as the primary drivers of both economic performance and environmental
impact. Across simulations, systems with longer-lasting encapsulants (≥5 years) consistently
demonstrated lower levelized costs and carbon intensities, highlighting encapsulant durability
as a clear R&D priority. Reactor configuration also played a critical role in system
performance. While packed bed reactors generally achieved higher COD removal
efficiencies (rCOD), fluidized bed systems offered superior cost-effectiveness and life cycle
environmental performance, especially when operated at optimized HRTs. This trade-off

 25

underscores the importance of aligning system design with specific treatment goals—whether
maximizing pollutant removal or minimizing environmental and financial burdens.

HRT itself was found to be the most consequential design decision. For fluidized bed
systems, reducing HRT lowered costs but could undermine COD removal performance. In
packed bed systems, where a large fraction of reactor volume is filled with beads, increasing
HRT significantly raised both reactor size and encapsulant needs—resulting in steep
increases in cost and emissions. This sensitivity to HRT was confirmed through Monte Carlo
filtering and sensitivity analyses, which showed that system performance metrics such as
rCOD, levelized cost (LC), and carbon intensity (CI) were most sensitive to changes in HRT.

Despite the challenges, our simulations identified 108 design configurations capable of
achieving net-negative life cycle carbon emissions while maintaining competitive costs.
These high-performing designs typically paired fluidized bed configurations with single-
stage treatment, moderate HRTs, and leveraged encapsulants with extended operational
lifetimes. Their costs ranged from $32 to $848 per tonne of COD removed, demonstrating
that specific design choices can enable both environmentally beneficial and financially viable
outcomes.

Finally, encapsulant longevity proved to be the most critical technological parameter
influencing system sustainability. Short bead lifespans led to frequent replacement,
substantially increasing operating costs. This effect was magnified in packed bed systems
due to their higher bead volume requirements. As such, materials research aimed at
extending encapsulant life will be pivotal to advancing the METAB system as a scalable,
low-carbon treatment solution for high-strength industrial wastewaters.

To simulate the impact of METAB deployment on centralized WRRFs, we evaluated the
potential impact across four representative WRRF configurations and at a collaborating
utility’s facility in Minneapolis, MN. Our analysis focused on four representative WRRF
configurations: those designed for removal of carbonaceous biochemical oxygen demand
(rBOD) and systems designed for nitrification (NIT), biological nutrient removal (BNR), and
enhanced nutrient removal (ENR). For WRRFs designed primarily for BOD removal and
nitrification (i.e., rBOD and NIT), upstream industrial pretreatment resulted in net cost
savings—13% and 14% median reductions in operational cost, respectively (Figure 12).
These savings were largely driven by reduced aeration requirements and lower sludge
production. In rBOD and NIT systems, aeration demand dropped by 19% and 24%,
respectively, due to the removal of biodegradable organic carbon from influent wastewater.
This reduced energy use and associated costs, with additional savings coming from decreases
in residual solids that would otherwise require thickening, dewatering, and disposal. For
instance, sludge production dropped by over 12% in these systems, translating to measurable
financial relief in landfill disposal costs.

In contrast, pretreatment increased operational costs at WRRFs designed for nutrient
removal. BNR and ENR systems - both based on A2O configurations - experienced median
cost increases of 42% and 45%, respectively. This reversal in cost trend is primarily due to
the need for external carbon addition to meet stringent effluent nutrient limits once influent
organic loading is reduced. In these systems, acetic acid is added to support enhanced
biological phosphorus removal, and methanol is added for denitrification. The cost of
additional chemical addition outweighed any savings from reduced aeration or sludge
disposal, leading to a net financial burden. For example, BNR systems incurred an added
median cost of 0.024 USD∙m⁻³ for external carbon, while ENR systems incurred 0.027
USD∙m⁻³ - both substantial enough to offset other benefits.

Beyond financial metrics, pretreatment also reduced the production of biogas through
anaerobic digestion, as less organic matter reached the digesters. This necessitated increased
use of supplemental natural gas to meet facility heating needs, particularly in rBOD and NIT
configurations. However, the impact was modest and largely negligible in BNR and ENR
systems, where baseline methane production is already lower.

Taken together, the results demonstrate that upstream valorization of industrial organics can
either enhance or undermine WRRF sustainability depending on context. In rBOD and NIT
systems, the approach offers clear environmental and economic advantages. However, in
nutrient removal systems, the reduced availability of influent carbon creates a reliance on
costly chemical inputs and compromises operational efficiency. These trade-offs highlight
the importance of coordinated planning between industrial pretreatment systems and
centralized WRRFs. Efforts to advance upstream valorization will benefit from identifying
and prioritizing deployment in regions or treatment networks where centralized facilities are
well-positioned to capture the resulting sustainability gains.

of the two systems was monitored by collecting liquid and gas samples. To improve the
METAB 1st-stage reactor performance, the HRT was reduced to a final value of 0.33 days
during the start-up period, and the pH was lowered once to limit methanogenic activity and
increase fermentation, as seen in Table 3. As the HRT of the METAB 1st-stage reactor was
lowered, the HRT of the METAB 2nd-stage reactor was adjusted to maintain a constant HRT
across both systems. Additionally, as more beads were added, the HRT was adjusted
according to the liquid volume in the reactors to maintain a consistent HRT. During the last
bead deployment event (bringing the total volume of beads in the 30-L reactors to 6.5 L), the
HRT of the METAB and single-stage systems was reduced to 13 days.

The performance of the METAB and single-stage systems with respect to sCOD removal is
shown in Figure 15. The sCOD removal during each phase of the pilot is summarized in
Table 4. Once in phase 5 of the pilot (HRT=13 days), the METAB system quickly reached
>90% sCOD removal, with the single-stage system reaching >90% sCOD removal about 10
days later. The systems maintained >90% sCOD removal for the duration of phase 5. During
phase 6 (HRT=7 days), both systems quickly adjusted to the HRT change. The METAB
system maintained >90% sCOD removal for the entirety of the phase, with an average
effluent sCOD of 202 mg/L, and a 96% removal efficiency. Similarly, the single-stage
system maintained >90% for the majority of phase 6, with an average effluent sCOD of 276
mg/L and a 94% removal efficiency. During phase 7 and 8, the system HRT was reduced to 3
days. The implementation of active gas extraction denotes the beginning of phase 8. In phase
7, the decrease in HRT to 3 days led to a transient decrease in sCOD removal for both
systems during Days 147-153, likely a result of biomass growth to match the increased
organic load. Nevertheless, both systems quickly adjusted, with the METAB system once
again reaching >90% sCOD removal for the duration of phases 7 and 8. During phase 8, the
effluent of the two systems was more variable, likely a result of the variable influent COD
values combined with the short HRT. Active gas extraction was removed from the METAB
system at the end of phase 8. In phases 9 and 10, the HRT of the system was increased to 13
days, then reduced to 3 days, respectively. The purpose of the adjustment of the HRT was to
observe biomass growth and the resiliency of the bead structure when exposed to an
increased organic load similar to that observed in phase 8. During phase 9, the METAB and
single-stage systems maintained >90% COD removal efficiency. In phase 10, the average
COD removal of the METAB and single-stage systems was 71% and 84%. For the first time
since the pilot was deployed, the single-stage system performance with respect to COD
removal was significantly greater than the METAB system (P<0.05). The decrease in COD
removal efficiency during phase 10 was thought to be a result of the decreased temperature
within the wastewater room at the brewery (Minnesota location, December and January time
frame). For the duration of phase 10, the temperature was about 10°C lower than in phases 5-
9.

4. Key Achievements

 Major accomplishments are given below in bulleted form.

  • Three Ph.D. students and three M.S. students were trained on this project. A
 postdoctoral researcher spent time on this project as well.
  • Six papers were published from research supported by this project. An additional five
 manuscripts are being written and are anticipated from research supported by this
 project.
  • Task 1: When two-stage experiments were set-up with encapsulated 1st stage
 communities enriched on brewery wastewater plus protein-rich wastewater and
 encapsulated 2nd stage communities enriched on brewery wastewater, plus lipid-rich
 wastewater, plus dairy wastewater, they were able to degrade lipid-rich, dairy, and
 brewery wastewater efficiently with COD removal >90%; these were deemed to be
 robust and flexible communities.
  • Task 2: PEG, when used to encapsulate biomass in beads (“PEG beads”) were able to
 be made reproducibly and in such a way as to not irreversibly inhibit methanogenic
 biomass.
  • Task 2: Excellent biomass retention in PEG beads over a two-week flow-through
 period was demonstrated.
  • Task 2: Methane production was improved if the encapsulated 2nd-stage biomass was
 first grown as biofilms on powdered activated carbon (PAC); the beads became more
 fragile after encapsulating PAC-supported biofilms compared to suspended biomass.
  • Task 5: PEG beads encapsulating suspended biomass were deployed at the pilot scale
 and resulted in excellent performance over 289 days without bead breakage.
  • Task 3: A gas extraction model was developed, incorporating multiple gases, gas-
 phase resistance and fouling, and water vapor permeation. Comparison of this model
 and experimental extraction data showed good agreement to an average error of 13%.
 In water, the model successfully predicted ~99% of the gas extraction rate changes
 due to the operating parameters.
  • Task 3: Comparison of gas extraction model and experimental extraction data for CH4
 extraction from a synthetic wastewater stream also showed good agreement with a
 13% average error rate.
  • Task 3: The gas extraction model was modified to allow for faster solving times and
 converted to python for ease of use with QSDSan.
  • Task 3: A modified ADM1 model was created with the inclusion of ethanol and
 lactate; the model was validated against bench-scale data from a 1st-stage
 (fermentation) reactor with gas extraction, showing better predictive capability
 compared to the baseline ADM1 model.
  • Task 3: Experimental results showed increased hydrogen production in a 1st-stage
 (fermentation) reactor when the hydrogen was actively extracted with a hollow fiber
 membrane-based gas extraction system. No improved COD degradation or
 fermentation was observed with gas extraction, however.
  • Task 4: Developed a comprehensive process simulator for encapsulated anaerobic
 treatment systems, enabling integrated TEA and LCA under uncertainty to guide
 early-stage design and R&D prioritization.

5. Benefits Assessment

Ultimately, we found encapsulated anaerobic systems with optimized fluidized bed design
have significant potential to provide affordable, carbon-negative, distributed COD removal
from high strength organic wastewaters if encapsulant longevity can be maintained at 5 years
 39

or above. Additionally, if these systems are deployed in regions where WRRF permits are
primarily driven by BOD removal and/or ammonia removal, the benefits of the METAB
system will be enhanced by operational changes at the centralized WRRF (specifically,
reduced aeration requirements and reduced sludge production). These results underscore the
importance of tailoring industrial pretreatment investments to specific contexts, considering
both industry needs and local WRRF configurations and permits.

6. Lessons Learned

This study demonstrated the efficacy and reliability of the METAB system, and its
significant potential for decentralized industrial wastewater valorization. At the same time,
this work showed that there is no single design of the METAB system that is optimal in all
contexts. Both fluidized bed and packed bed reactor designs have distinct advantages
depending on site-specific goals and constraints (e.g., available space, treatment targets,
energy sources). Our analysis showed that achieving low carbon emissions and cost-
effective treatment hinges on a few key factors, especially how long the beads (i.e.,
encapsulant) last and how effectively the microbes inside the beads can break down the
organics in the industrial wastewater. In general, fluidized beds offer more flexibility and
require less material to achieve cost targets, but packed beds may be more appropriate in
space-limited settings where high removal efficiency is critical. Importantly, durable
encapsulant material is essential across both systems, but this must be balanced with
concerns about environmental impacts at the end of the beads’ life. During pilot operation
the beads were robust and undamaged for the entire operational period of 289 days; longer
tests would be useful to further delineate expected encapsulant lifetime under operational
conditions. Looking ahead, further research is needed to understand how material
properties, microbial activity, and system design interact, and to ensure these systems are
not only effective and affordable but also deployable in settings with limited operational
oversight.

7. Conclusions

Overall, the project was a success. We accomplished our goal of advancing a distributed
modular, agile treatment technology - the METAB system - to pilot-scale. In fact, a small
pilot system was operated continuously for 289 days at a local brewery during which time
COD degradation generally exceeded 90% and methane production was excellent and around
70% of the produced biogas. The system did remove less COD and produce less gas when
the temperature was around 25 °C, with the two-stage system being more sensitive to lower
temperatures than the single-stage system utilizing encapsulated organisms. We developed a
robust and stable encapsulation recipe that enabled microbial survival and retention. We
developed a protocol for ensuring that the correct organisms were present to enable
adaptation over time as well as rapid start-up. Models that accurately described gas extraction
and the effect of gas extraction on microbial fermentation were developed and should be
particularly useful to facilitate predictive gas extraction and two-stage anaerobic treatment.
Although gas extraction did not improve fermentation or COD removal, thereby suggesting
that it is not beneficial or necessary for the operation of the METAB system as originally
conceived, it may be helpful for removing any dissolved methane present in a 2nd-stage
reactor that could contribute to GHG emissions from distributed treatment. Finally, an open-
 40

source simulator was produced that enables LCA and TEA on wastewater treatment systems
utilizing encapsulated biomass. The impact of distributed high-strength industrial wastewater
treatment resulted in lower costs and energy expenditures at centralized WRRFs that
performed COD removal and nitrification, but not at WRRFs that performed total nitrogen
removal (e.g., denitrification).

Commercialization of the technology should be pursued. The TEA and LCA work
demonstrated clear opportunity spaces where the technology would be beneficial from both a
cost and GHG emissions perspective. Further applied research on biomass selection for
deployment should be performed. This will further validate that the predictions from Task 1
and verified in Task 5 regarding which mixtures of enriched biomass should be encapsulated
for rapid start-up and flexible treatment are in fact valid. This would be important before
additional scale-up and commercialization.

8. Future Work/Recommendations

As stated above, commercialization should be pursued. The technology was accepted by the
Chevron Studio program, a partnership between Chevron Technology Ventures and the National
Renewable Energy Laboratory to advance scale-up and commercialization of “innovative, early-
stage technologies that have the potential to enable a lower carbon future” (Chevron Studio,
accessed 2025). An entrepreneur has been paired with the technology and is in the process of
customer discovery and assessing scale-up options. If resources are available to accelerate this
process, they should be deployed. As also stated above, additional applied research on biomass
selection for deployment should be performed. This will further validate that the predictions
from Task 1 and verified in Task 5 regarding which mixtures of enriched biomass should be
encapsulated for rapid start-up and flexible treatment are in fact valid. This will provide
critical validation that the technology can operate flexibly with a range of wastewaters.
Novak and the entrepreneur are working together and hope to submit proposals for additional
funding to help with the scale-up process.
