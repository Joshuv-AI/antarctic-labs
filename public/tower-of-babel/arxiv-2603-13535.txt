Edgewise Envelopes Between Balanced Forman and Ollivier-Ricci Curvature
Giorgio Micaletto gmicaletto@uchicago.edu, Tebe Nigrelli tebe.nigrelli@studbocconi.it

Abstract
Evaluating 15 (OR) curvature on large-scale graphs is computationally prohibitive due to the necessity of solving an optimal transport problem for every edge. We bypass this computational bottleneck by deriving explicit, two-sided, piecewise-affine transfer moduli between the transport-based OR curvature and 23 ’s combinatorial Balanced Forman (BF) curvature. By constructing a lazy transport envelope and augmenting the 10 bound with a cross-edge matching statistic, we establish deterministic bounds for 𝔠 O ​ R ​ ( i , j ) \mathfrak{c}_{OR}(i,j) parameterized by 2-hop local graph combinatorics. This formulation reduces the edgewise evaluation complexity from an optimal transport linear program to a worst-case 𝒪 ⁡ ( max v ∈ V ⁡ deg ⁡ ( v ) 1.5 ) \mathcal{O}(\max_{v\in V}\deg(v)^{1.5}) time, entirely eliminating the reliance on global solvers. We validate these bounds via distributional analyses on canonical random graphs and empirical networks; the derived analytical bands enclose the empirical distributions independent of degree heterogeneity, geometry, or clustering, providing a scalable, computationally efficient framework for statistical network analysis.

Introduction

Discrete analogues of Ricci curvature on graphs have become popular tools in both network science and geometry processing. Two main families account for the majority of approaches in this area: (i) transport-based coarse Ricci curvatures, introduced by
15
(OR) and extended by
12
(LLY), which notably converge to smooth Ricci curvature in the setting of random geometric graphs
(
14
;
24
)
; and (ii) combinatorial curvatures in the sense of
6
(FR), together with their network adaptations and extensions incorporating
n
-cycles
(
19
;
27
;
17
)
(AFR).

The literature includes several empirical studies on comparing these notions across graph models and real networks, often reporting substantial, but not universal, agreement while also highlighting regimes where they differ
(
20
;
17
;
18
)
. From a transport perspective, OR curvature increases when local motifs, such as triangles and quadrangles, reduce the Wasserstein cost between one-step measures
(
10
;
4
)
, while by contrast, FR and its variants are designed in a purely combinatorial fashion to reward the presence of recurring motifs
(
19
;
27
)
.

Recent work has attempted to reconcile these two perspectives:
23
introduced Balanced Forman curvature (BF), a combinatorial edge-based notion that provides a sharp lower bound for LLY curvature on graphs, while other studies have proposed extensions of Forman curvature aimed at bringing it into closer alignment with OR in applications
(
22
;
17
)
.

Positioning of This Work.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, despite many empirical comparisons, there is no
analytic, two-sided
relation between transport-based and combinatorial curvatures at the
edge level
on general graphs.
Our contribution is to develop a
lazy transport envelope
that upper-bounds the edgewise Wasserstein–1 cost by isolating (i) zero-cost matches, (ii) endpoint residuals, and (iii) cross-edge unit-cost flow.
This yields an explicit, coupling-free,
piecewise-affine
envelope for OR curvature as a function of degrees, laziness, triangle count, and a 4-cycle coverage (Proposition
3.6
). Building on the BF identity and a sharper version of
10
’s lower bound (Theorem
3.7
), we obtain
two-sided, edgewise transfer moduli
between BF and OR.
Upper
transfer from BF to OR is obtained directly from the envelope (Theorem
4.2
), while
lower
transfer from BF follows from the sharpened bound (Theorem
4.1
).
In the reverse direction, the envelope implies a
necessary triangle threshold
that turns OR lower bounds into BF lower bounds (Theorem
4.3
), and inverting the inequality of Theorem
3.7
yields an OR to BF upper transfer (Theorem
4.4
).

What We Mean by “Transfer Moduli”.

We define
edgewise transfer moduli
as pairs of nondecreasing functions,
\varphi^{(i,j)}
and
\psiit^{(i,j)}
, that convert a bound on one curvature into a bound on the other.
For
\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}\to\mathfrak{c}_{\rm OR}
, and symmetrically for
\mathfrak{c}_{\rm OR}\to\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}
, we require:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}(i,j)\geq\zetait\ \implies\mathfrak{c}_{\rm OR}(i,j)\geq\varphi^{(i,j)}_{\rm BF\to OR}(\zetait),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}(i,j)\leq\vartheta\ \implies\mathfrak{c}_{\rm OR}(i,j)\leq\psiit^{(i,j)}_{\rm BF\to OR}(\vartheta).

The strength of our theoretical result lies in the
tightness
of the derived moduli: they are
piecewise-affine functions
parameterized by the local combinatorics of
G
(degrees, triangles, and
4
-cycles).
By encoding the specific geometric bottlenecks that constrain mass transport, these moduli offer a significant improvement over naive bounds, “transferring” the input curvature value into a sharp estimate for the target curvature.
Furthermore, because the parameters of these functions depend solely on 2-hop neighborhoods, they are explicit and computable in worst-case
\mathcal{O}(\max_{v\in V}\deg(v)^{1.5})
amortized time without solving the optimal transport problem, allowing us to generate “bounding bands” that enclose the empirical curvature distributions observed in Section
5
.

Distributional Predictions and Experimental Scope.

Our goal is to understand
how
edgewise curvatures are distributed within a graph and
which
mechanisms shape those distributions. We therefore examine full empirical
histograms
of OR and BF curvature and compare them to the transfer bounds described above. The bounds are plotted as envelopes that act as “bounding bands” for the observed distributions, complementing prior comparative work that emphasized edgewise scatter plots and rank correlations
(
20
;
17
)
. We group our testbeds into three families that isolate distinct drivers of curvature:

(i)

Random graph models
that control degree heterogeneity, clustering, and geometry:

(a)

Erdős–Rényi
G(n,p)
, which provides a neutral baseline with low clustering and light-tailed degrees (for the studied case of
p\approx c/n
); OR mass concentrates near nonpositive values, and BF tracks degrees tightly
(
5
)
.

(b)

Watts–Strogatz small–world graphs, which interpolates between lattice-like clustering and random rewiring, letting us sweep from positive-OR, triangle-rich regimes to sparse-overlap regimes
(
26
)
.

(c)

Barabási–Albert preferential attachment, which introduces heavy-tailed degrees; hubs broaden BF and create many low-overlap, hub–periphery edges that create a heavier negative OR tail.
(
1
)
.

(d)

Random geometric graphs in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which add an explicit Euclidean geometry: spatial proximity induces clustering and short-range transport, linking OR to geometric overlap and providing a pathway to smooth-limit interpretations
(
16
;
24
)
.

(e)

Random
d
-regular simple graphs, which fix degrees and are locally tree-like, with BF collapsing to a narrow band, and the scarcity of triangles pushes OR toward negative values, isolating degree effects from clustering
(
21
)
.

(f)

Homogeneous Stochastic Block Models (SBM), which inject controlled community structure: within-block edges have many shared neighbors (OR higher), while cross-block edges do not (OR lower). The disassortative regime stresses the lower envelope and approaches bipartite structure
(
9
)
.

(g)

Hyperbolic random graphs (HRG), which combine heavy-tailed degrees with latent negative curvature in the embedding space, providing a geometric foil where OR skews negative away from dense cores and BF spreads with degree
(
11
)
.

(ii)

Canonical combinatorial families
that provide clean baselines and extremes:

(a)

Cycles
, that realize a one-dimensional “flat” baseline with uniform local structure.

(b)

Rectangular grids
that expose boundary effects (degree-
3
vertices) that perturb curvature, whereas
toroidal grids
remove boundaries and produce spatially homogeneous distributions.

(c)

d
-ary trees
that probe expanding, triangle-scarce structure that drives OR strongly negative away from leaves and yields BF values controlled by branching.

(d)

Complete graphs
that represent the opposite extreme: maximally overlapping neighborhoods push OR strongly positive, while large degrees drive BF to 1.

(iii)

Real networks
that supply heterogeneous, noisy ground truth: the Karate Club network
(
29
)
, the Jazz Collaboration network
(
8
)
, the Western US power grid
(
26
)
, the yeast transcription network
(
13
)
, and the arXiv HepPh citation network
(
7
)
.

This experimental scope follows standard practice in comparative-curvature studies
(
20
;
17
;
18
;
4
)
, with two heuristics guiding the selection. First, OR is primarily driven by neighborhood overlap and local clustering: edges whose endpoints share many neighbors push OR upward; edges that bridge poorly overlapping neighborhoods push it downward. Second, in unweighted graphs BF tracks the endpoint degrees tightly, so degree control tends to collapse BF into a narrow band, whereas degree variability broadens it. Together, this suite stresses our transfer bounds along five axes, degree control, clustering, geometry, community structure, and real-world heterogeneity, while keeping the comparisons readable at the level of full distributions rather than individual edges.

Preliminaries

Remark 2.1
.

Proofs of all stated results are provided in Appendix

In the following, we fix the notation, collect the basic objects used for transport, the curvature notions of interest, and the combinatorial summaries that parameterize our explicit couplings and bounds used in the next sections. We work with a simple (undirected, loopless, no multi-edges), finite, connected graph
G=(V,E)
, where
n:=|V|
and degrees are defined as

\varrho_{u}:=\deg_{G}(u)=|\mathcal{N}(u)|,

where
\mathcal{N}(u)
denotes the set of vertices adjacent to
u
,

\mathcal{N}(u):=\{v\in V:(u,v)\in E\}.

Definition 2.2

(Walks and Paths)
.

A
walk
in
G
of length
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is a finite sequence
(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
of vertices with
(x_{i-1},x_{i})\in E
for
i=1,\dots,k
. We write
u\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\leadsto}}v
if there exists a walk of length
k
from
u
to
v
, writing
u\leadsto v
whenever
u\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\leadsto}}v
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Definition 2.3

(Graph Distance)
.

Given the walk notation of Definition
2.2
, the
graph distance

\mathrm{dist}_{G}:V\times V\to\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
is defined by

\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)\;:=\;\inf\left\{\,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:u\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\leadsto}}v\right\}.

Remark 2.4
.

The graph distance, as defined in Definition
2.3
is a metric on
V
, with
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)<\infty
for all
u,v\in V
, and
\mathrm{dist}_{G}:V\times V\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
(
3
, Chapter 12)

Definition 2.5

(Lazy Random-Walk Measure)
.

For each vertex
u\in V
, define the
one-step (lazy) random-walk measure
on
V
by

m_{u}:=\;\alphait_{u}\,\deltait_{u}\;+\;(1-\alphait_{u})\,\nuit_{u},

where

\alphait_{u}\;=\;\frac{1}{\varrho_{u}+1},

(2.1)

and:

(i)

\deltait_{u}
denotes the
Dirac mass
at
u
, i.e.

\deltait_{u}(k)\;:=\;\mathds{1}_{\{k=u\}}(k)\quad\text{for all }k\in V;

(ii)

\nuit_{u}
is the
random-walk adjacency measure
(or
neighbor law
) at
u
, defined as the probability measure
\nuit_{u}:V\to[0,1]
,

\nuit_{u}(k)\;:=\;\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varrho_{u}},&\text{if }k\in\mathcal{N}(u),\\[10.00002pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Remark 2.6

(Uniform Mass on the Closed Neighborhood)
.

The measure
m_{u}
is uniform on the closed neighborhood of
u
:

m_{u}(w)=\begin{cases}\alphait_{u},&w=u,\\[2.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1-\alphait_{u}}{\varrho_{u}}=\alphait_{u},&w\in\mathcal{N}(u),\\[4.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Equivalently,

m_{u}=\alphait_{u}\sum_{x\in\{u\}\cup\mathcal{N}(u)}\deltait_{x},

such that that

\mathrm{supp}(m_{u})=\mathcal{B}_{1}(u):=\{u\}\cup\mathcal{N}(u).

Definition 2.7

(Couplings and Transport Plans)
.

Let
(V,\mathrm{dist}_{G})
be a finite metric space and let
\mathcal{P}(V)
denote the set of probability measures on
V
.
Given
\muit,\nuit\in\mathcal{P}(V)
, a
coupling
(or
transport plan
) between
\muit
and
\nuit
is a probability measure

\piit\in\mathcal{P}(V\times V)
whose marginals are
\muit
and
\nuit
, i.e.

\piit(\cdot,V)=\muit

and

\piit(V,\cdot)=\nuit.

We write

\Piup(\muit,\nuit):=\bigl\{\piit\in\mathcal{P}(V\times V):\piit(\cdot,V)=\muit,\ \piit(V,\cdot)=\nuit\bigr\}.

For
\piit\in\Piup(\muit,\nuit)
, its
transport cost
(with respect to
\mathrm{dist}_{G}
) is

C_{G}(\piit):=\sum_{(x,y)\in V\times V}\mathrm{dist}_{G}(x,y)\,\piit(x,y).

If
\mathcal{T}:V\to V
is a map and
\nuit=\mathcal{T}_{\#}\muit
is the push-forward defined by

(\mathcal{T}_{\#}\muit)(A)=\muit\bigl(\mathcal{T}^{-1}(A)\bigr),

then

\piit_{\mathcal{T}}:=(\mathrm{id},\mathcal{T})_{\#}\muit\quad\text{with}\quad\piit_{\mathcal{T}}(x,y)=\sum_{z\in V}\muit(z)\,\mathds{1}_{\{x=z,\ y=\mathcal{T}(z)\}}

is a
Monge coupling

(
25
, Chapter 1)
concentrated on the graph of
\mathcal{T}
.

Remark 2.8
.

A coupling
\piit\in\Piup(\muit,\nuit)
prescribes how the unit mass of
\muit
is split and reassigned to match
\nuit
.
Two useful (not necessarily optimal) choices are:

(a)

Product coupling:

\piit=\muit\otimes\nuit
, which ignores geometry but witnesses

\Piup(\muit,\nuit)\neq\varnothing.

(b)

Map-induced coupling:
if a map
\mathcal{T}
satisfies
\mathcal{T}_{\#}\muit=\nuit
, then
\piit_{\mathcal{T}}=(\mathrm{id},\mathcal{T})_{\#}\muit
transports mass along the (deterministic) arrows
x\mapsto\mathcal{T}(x)
with cost

\sum_{x}\mathrm{dist}_{G}\bigl(x,\mathcal{T}(x)\bigr)\muit(x).

For the lazy random-walk measures
m_{u},m_{v}
associated with an edge
(u,v)
, every feasible coupling lives on

\bigl(\{u\}\cup\mathcal{N}(u)\bigr)\times\bigl(\{v\}\cup\mathcal{N}(v)\bigr)
by Remark
2.6
, so it suffices to reason about transfers between those
1
-step neighborhoods.

Definition 2.9

(
W_{1}
Distance on a Graph)
.

For
\muit,\nuit\in\mathcal{P}(V)
, define

W_{1}(\muit,\nuit)\;:=\;\inf_{\piit\in\Piup(\muit,\nuit)}\ \sum_{x,y\in V}\mathrm{dist}_{G}(x,y)\,\piit(x,y),

with
\Piup(\muit,\nuit)
as in Definition
2.7

Lemma 2.10

(Existence of Optimal Couplings on Finite Graphs)
.

Let
\muit,\!\nuit\!\in\!\!\mathcal{P}(V)

\piit^{\star}\in\Piup(\muit,\nuit)

Remark 2.11

(Kantorovich–Rubinstein)
.

Equivalently,

W_{1}(\muit,\nuit)\;=\;\sup\left\{\sum_{v\in V}f(v)\,\bigl(\muit(v)-\nuit(v)\bigr):\ f:V\to\mathbb{R},\ \operatorname{Lip}_{G}(f)\leq 1\right\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Lip}_{G}(f):=\sup_{u\neq v}\frac{|f(u)-f(v)|}{\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)}.

Remark 2.12

(Saturating the Diagonal)
.

Given
\muit,\nuit\in\mathcal{P}(V)
, let
\gammait:V\to[0,1]
be the common mass

\gammait(x):=\min\{\muit(x),\nuit(x)\}
. By
saturating the diagonal
we mean the operation
of first assigning

\piit(x,x)\ \leftarrow\ \gammait(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in V,

and then coupling only the residual measures

\muit^{\perp}:=\muit-\gammait
and
\nuit^{\perp}:=\nuit-\gammait
, which have disjoint supports.
This preprocessing neither changes nor increases the optimal cost,

W_{1}(\muit,\nuit)\;=\;W_{1}(\muit^{\perp},\nuit^{\perp}),

because mass placed on the diagonal incurs zero transportation cost.

Remark 2.13

(Laziness of the Measure)
.

It is customary to call
m_{u}
the one-step law of a
lazy
random walk because the walker has a nonzero probability of remaining at
u
, writing the one-step transition kernel
P
as

P(u,w)=m_{u}(w)=\begin{cases}\alphait_{u}&w=u,\\[2.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1-\alphait_{u}}{\varrho_{u}}&w\in\mathcal{N}(u),\\[4.0pt]
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

From an optimal-transport perspective, laziness manifests as
available mass on the diagonal
: whenever
x
lies in both supports of the measures being compared, the common mass can be matched at zero cost (Remark
2.12
). Here
\alphait_{u}
is a convenient choice, by making
m_{u}
uniform on
\mathcal{B}_{1}(u)
, reducing the problem to the combinatorics of
1

Definition 2.16

(Edgewise Comparison Parameters)
.

For a given edge
(i,j)\in E
, we define:

(a)

the
symmetric degree shift

\mathfrak{S}(i,j):=\frac{2}{\varrho_{i}}+\frac{2}{\varrho_{j}}-2;

(b)

the
triangle scaling term

\mathfrak{T}(i,j):=\frac{2}{\varrho_{\max\{i,j\}}}+\frac{1}{\varrho_{\min\{i,j\}}};

(c)

the
residual curvature factor

\mathfrak{K}(i,j):=1-\frac{1}{\varrho_{\min\{i,j\}}}-\frac{1}{\varrho_{\max\{i,j\}}};

(d)

the
normalized overlap

\mathfrak{Z}_{\bullet}(i,j):=\frac{\triangle(i,j)}{\bullet\{\varrho_{i},\varrho_{j}\}},\qquad\text{for }\bullet\in\{\min,\max\}.

Remark 2.17

(Role of the Comparison Moduli)
.

(a)

\mathfrak{S}(i,j)=\tfrac{2}{\varrho_{i}}+\tfrac{2}{\varrho_{j}}-2
is a symmetric degree shift that feeds the
intercept of the affine upper envelope (it disappears at large degrees and captures the small-degree penalty).

(b)

\mathfrak{T}(i,j)=\tfrac{2}{\varrho_{\max\{i,j\}}}+\tfrac{1}{\varrho_{\min\{i,j\}}}
is the triangle-scaling coefficient that determines
the slope with which
\triangle(i,j)
influences the envelope.

(c)

\mathfrak{K}(i,j)=1-\tfrac{1}{\varrho_{\min\{i,j\}}}-\tfrac{1}{\varrho_{\max\{i,j\}}}
collects the “residual curvature” once degree effects are peeled off; it multiplies the portion not explained by triangles.

(d)

\mathfrak{Z}_{\bullet}(i,j)=\triangle(i,j)/\bullet\{\varrho_{i},\varrho_{j}\}
is a normalized overlap used to pick the tight normalization depending on which side is bottlenecking.

4
Transfer Principles Between
\mathfrak{c}_{\rm OR}
and
\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}

The central idea is to decouple
structure
from
transport
:
\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}
on an edge
(i,j)
admits the decomposition (
2.2
), making transparent the contributions of degree imbalance, triangle overlap, and
4
-cycle witnesses, whereas
\mathfrak{c}_{\rm OR}
is defined through an optimal transport problem and is therefore costly to evaluate directly.
Using the structural results of Section
3
, we obtain closed-form, monotone, piecewise-affine moduli that depend solely on the combinatoric summaries around
(i,j)
.

We establish four transfer principles:

(i)

Given a target BF level
\zetait
, we determine the minimum triangle mass
Z^{(i,j)}(\zetait)
, which, together with a square-matching floor, yields an explicit modulus
\varphi^{(i,j)}_{\rm BF\to OR}(\zetait)
with

\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}(i,j)\geq\zetait\implies\mathfrak{c}_{\rm OR}(i,j)\geq\varphi^{(i,j)}_{\rm BF\to OR}(\zetait).

(ii)

Interpreting the constraint
\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{BF}}\leq\zetait
as a budget on the unit–cost operations that increase
\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{OR}}
, we obtain simultaneous bounds on triangle mass and cross–edges,

0\leq\triangle\leq\triangle_{\max}(\zetait),\qquad 0\leq\Xiup_{ij}\leq\Xiup_{\max}(\triangle),

which induce a continuous, piecewise–affine function
\widehat{\Psiup}_{\alphait}(\triangle)
whose maximizer, lying in the finite knot set (
4.12
), yields the upper modulus

\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{OR}}(i,j)\leq\psiit^{(i,j)}_{\mathrm{BF}\to\mathrm{OR}}(\zetait).

(iii)

The coverage envelope
\Thetaup_{\alphait}(\triangle)
is invertible: observing
\mathfrak{c}_{\rm OR}\geq\vartheta
enforces at least
t_{\min}(\vartheta)
shared neighbors.
Using this guaranteed overlap and discarding the nonnegative
4
-cycle term gives the lower bound

\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}(i,j)\ \geq\ \mathfrak{S}(i,j)+\mathfrak{T}(i,j)\,t_{\min}(\vartheta).

(iv)

Combining the lazy to non-lazy reduction with a quadrangle-effective deficit
\mathfrak{K}_{\square}
(which penalizes degrees via the matching statistic), we construct a piecewise-affine triangle envelope
\mathfrak{u}_{\max}^{(i,j)}(\vartheta)
.
Substituting into
\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}
yields

\mathfrak{c}_{\rm OR}(i,j)\leq\vartheta\implies\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}(i,j)\ \leq\ \mathfrak{S}(i,j)+\mathfrak{T}(i,j)\,\mathfrak{u}_{\max}^{(i,j)}(\vartheta)+\mathfrak{C}_{4}(i,j).

Together, these moduli (a) provide computable curvature intervals using only local combinatorics, (b) isolate the precise roles of degree imbalance, triangle overlap, and coarse
4
-cycle evidence, (c) work directly in the lazy setting with explicit dependence on
(\alphait_{i},\alphait_{j})
, and (d) avoid any optimal transport computation.
They enable fast screening (e.g. certifying the sign or a prescribed threshold of one curvature from the other), offer interpretable sensitivity to local edits (e.g. effects of adding a triangle or cross-edge), and support structural inference: large OR curvature forces a minimum triangle count through
t_{\min}
, while a BF budget imposes strict limits on how unique-unique and unique-common mass can increase
\mathfrak{c}_{\rm OR}
.
The piecewise-affine form also makes these envelopes amenable to optimization and embedding procedures in which curvature appears as a constraint or regularizer.

t_{\min}(\vartheta):=\min\left\{\left[\frac{\ \vartheta-\mathrm{Const}_{\alphait}\ }{\ \mathrm{Slope}_{\alphait}\ }\right]_{+},\ \left(\varrho_{\min\{i,j\}}-1\right)\right\},

(4.13)

with
\mathrm{Const}_{\alphait},\mathrm{Slope}_{\alphait}
from (

). Then

\mathfrak{c}_{\rm OR}(i,j)\ \geq\ \vartheta\implies\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}(i,j)\ \geq\ \varphi_{\rm OR\to BF}^{(i,j)}(\vartheta):=\ \mathfrak{S}(i,j)+\mathfrak{T}(i,j)\,t_{\min}(\vartheta).

(4.14)

Moreover,

(a)

If
\vartheta\leq\mathrm{Const}_{\alphait}
, then (

) reduces to the degree-only bound
\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}\geq\mathfrak{S}(i,j)
.

(b)

If
\vartheta>\Thetaup_{\alphait}(\varrho_{\min\{i,j\}}-1)
(with
\Thetaup_{\alphait}
as in (

)), then no edge with the given
(\varrho_{i},\varrho_{j},\alphait_{i},\alphait_{j})
can satisfy
\mathfrak{c}_{\rm OR}\geq\vartheta
; thus the implication is vacuous.

(c)

The modulus
\varphi^{(i,j)}_{\rm OR\to BF}
depends only on
(\varrho_{i},\varrho_{j},\alphait_{i},\alphait_{j})
and is independent of
\Xiup_{ij}
and other higher-order edge substructures as (

).

Theorem 4.4

(

to
\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}
Upper Transfer Modulus)
.

Let
(i,j)\in E
and define the
non-lazy proxy

\mathfrak{s}_{0}^{(i,j)}(\vartheta)\ :=\ \frac{\vartheta+\Deltaup_{ij}(\alphait)}{\,1-\alphait_{\star}\,}.

Set the
quadrangle-effective deficit

\mathfrak{K}_{\square}(i,j)\ :=\ \bigl[\mathfrak{K}(i,j)-S(i,j)\bigr]_{+}\ \ (\geq 0),

and the corresponding breakpoint value

\mathfrak{s}_{\mathfrak{u}}^{\square}(i,j)\ :=\ \mathfrak{K}_{\square}(i,j)\,\Bigl(2\,\frac{\varrho_{\min\{i,j\}}}{\varrho_{\max\{i,j\}}}-1\Bigr).

Define the piecewise
quadrangle triangle-envelope

\mathfrak{u}_{\max}^{(i,j)}(\vartheta)\ :=\ \begin{cases}0,&\mathfrak{s}_{0}^{(i,j)}(\vartheta)\leq-2\,\mathfrak{K}_{\square}(i,j),\\[8.0pt]

\psiit^{(i,j)}_{\rm OR\to BF}(\vartheta)\ :=\ \mathfrak{S}(i,j)\ +\ \mathfrak{T}(i,j)\,\mathfrak{u}_{\max}^{(i,j)}(\vartheta)\ +\ \mathfrak{C}_{4}(i,j).

(4.16)

Then the following implication holds:

\mathfrak{c}_{\rm OR}(i,j)\ \leq\ \vartheta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}(i,j)\ \leq\ \psiit^{(i,j)}_{\rm OR\to BF}(\vartheta).

6
Conclusion and Limitations

We introduced two complementary mechanisms that link transport-based and combinatorial edge curvatures on general simple graphs, producing the first
two-sided, edgewise transfer moduli
between BF and OR curvature:

\mathfrak{c}_{\rm BF}\ \mapsto\ \bigl[\varphi^{(i,j)}_{\rm BF\to OR},\ \psiit^{(i,j)}_{\rm BF\to OR}\bigr],\qquad\mathfrak{c}_{\rm OR}\ \mapsto\ \bigl[\varphi^{(i,j)}_{\rm OR\to BF},\ \psiit^{(i,j)}_{\rm OR\to BF}\bigr],

with all four maps explicit, edge-local, and piecewise-affine in
2
-hop summaries. All quantities required by our bounds are 2-hop local; with per-vertex caches, the evaluation of each modulus is bounded by the cross-edge matching cost
\mathcal{O}(|V[B_{ij}]|\sqrt{|E[B_{ij}]|})
per edge. In particular, no Wasserstein solve nor max-flow on the full coupling is needed.

6.1
Limitations

Our work is heavily restricted to unweighted, undirected, and simple graphs. Consequently, extending the lazy envelope and the transfer moduli to weighted graphs (unequal edge lengths, weighted transitions), directed graphs (asymmetric neighborhoods) is non-trivial: the unit-cost partition, the diagonal saturation argument, and the matching-based lower would have to be re-derived; furthermore, all results presented are edgewise and local in nature, we do not establish direct control on vertex-based curvature quantities, coarse Ricci flows, or global geometric invariants such as hyperbolicity or isoperimetric profiles.

6.2
Open Problems and Future Directions

A natural next step is to formalize the asymptotic behavior of the envelopes under geometric random graphs. For
\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{OR}}
, the limiting behavior is already known, however for
\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{BF}}
, no analogous limit theorem exists yet. A promising route would be to first to derive an explicit analytic description of
\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{BF}}
on
\mathrm{RGG}(n,r)
by expressing its triangle and
4
-cycle terms in terms of local Poisson-point statistics, yielding the expected scaling of
\triangle(i,j)
and
\Xiup_{ij}
as functions of
r
and ambient density, allowing one to view
\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{BF}}
as a local functional of the induced geometric complex.
Once this geometric formulation is in place, one could then examine the
limiting structure of the transfer inequalities
, specifically, whether the envelopes converge to deterministic curves. Formally, this amounts to studying the limits

\varphi^{(i,j)}_{\mathrm{BF}\to\mathrm{OR}}(\zetait)\ \longrightarrow\ \Phiup_{\mathrm{BF}\to\mathrm{OR}}(\zetait),\qquad\psiit^{(i,j)}_{\mathrm{BF}\to\mathrm{OR}}(\zetait)\ \longrightarrow\ \Psiup_{\mathrm{BF}\to\mathrm{OR}}(\zetait),

under the random-geometric scaling, and determining whether these limiting moduli collapse or persist.
Two additional straightforward generalizations are (i) a family of idleness schedules
\alphait_{u}=f(\varrho_{u})
and (ii) degree-biased neighbor laws (e.g.,
\nuit_{u}\propto w_{uv}
), which would broaden the applications of Proposition
3.11
and Theorem
4.3
.

6.3
Take-away

Transport and combinatorial curvatures react to the same local motifs but with different objective functions. By isolating the ways in which one-step mass can move, we turned this intuition into quantitative, computable, and two-sided relations that are sharp in structured regimes and informative in heterogeneous ones.
