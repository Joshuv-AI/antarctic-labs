Bone changes following horizontal bone augmentation with resorbable and non-resorbable membranes: a retrospective study
Authors: Berceste Guler Ayyildiz, Halil Ayyildiz, Tolga Fikret Tözüm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlveolar bone augmentation (ABA) procedures are performed using a variety of substitute; however, studies comparing non-resorbable (NRM) and resorbable (RCM) membranes for horizontal ABA are limited. The objective of the present study is to conduct a radiologically comparison of the RCM and NRM on bone changes (BC), utilized in horizontal ABA treatment of edentulous sites.MethodsThe present retrospective-designed study included patients who had received horizontal ABA treatment and, had pre- (T0) and post-treatment (T1) cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans were automatically superimposed by Dolphin Software. Two groups were defined in the study: horizontal ABA treatment with RCM and/or NRM. Radiographic measurements such as ridge width(RW), ridge height (RH), ridge angulation (RA), concavity location (CL), concavity depth (CD), cortical bone thickness (CT) on horizontal and vertical BC at 1, 3, 6, 9 and, 12 mm from the alveolar crest. Logistic regression analysis was applied.ResultsHorizontal BC were not statistically significantly different between NRM and RCM at all levels. Membrane stabilization type, RA (T0) and CT-lingual/palatal variables had statistically significant effects on horizontal BC (1 mm); the CA-T0 measurement among the variables of interest had a statistically significant effect on horizontal BC (3 mm) and horizontal BC (6 mm). CD-T0 and CT-coronal values showed a statistically significant association with bone gain; however, the magnitude of this effect per unit increase was limited.ConclusionsNon-resorbable membranes used for horizontal augmentation may be particularly important for stabilization at the alveolar crest. It has been shown that membrane stabilization type, radiographic angles and measurements may be also alter bone changes, highlighting the importance of preoperative radiographic evaluation. However, prospective studies with a more homogeneous distribution are needed.

Introduction

Dental implants are a successful and widely used treatment option for replacing missing teeth. However, this procedure can be challenging in numerous cases because of alveolar bone loss in both the vertical and horizontal dimensions, which is often a result of periodontal disease or traumatic tooth extraction [1, 2]. Guided bone regeneration (GBR) is a primary technique for alveolar bone augmentation (ABA) [3–6] and is used in over 40% of dental implant placements [7–9]. Documented success rates for the implementation of ABA procedures have been shown to be approximately 95% [10, 11].

Non-resorbable membranes (NRM), such as titanium mesh, expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (e-PTFE), and high-density polytetrafluoroethylene (d-PTFE) [12], and second-generation membranes, including resorbable collagen membranes (RCM), non-cross-linked collagen membranes, cross-linked collagen membranes, and resorbable synthetic membranes, have been used [13]. An experimental study reported that collagen membranes and bone graft materials were displaced in the apical direction nine weeks after GBR [14]. Compared with RCM, titanium meshes can generate a greater degree of mechanical support and, therefore, provide a more stable osteogenic space [15].

Studies comparing horizontal bone augmentation with and without implant placement have reported inconsistent results. A cone beam computed tomography (CBCT)-based evaluation comparing injectable platelet-rich fibrin and bone grafts alone with RCM reported clinically significant vertical bone gain (BG) in cases treated with RCM but horizontal BG was lower in those treated with RCM alone [16]. In contrast, a study that evaluated horizontal ABA procedures for defect resolution in horizontal ridge deficiencies after implant placement found that horizontal ABA performed in the presence of a membrane had significantly more favorable outcomes than bone substitution alone [17]. Similarly, a study on horizontal ABA with simultaneous implant placement reported that NRM application resulted in reduced horizontal bone reduction after six years compared to that with RCM [18]. However, a three-year follow-up study found no significant difference between the NRM and RCM applications [19]. In an experimental study, five distinct cases of critical-size defects treated with bone grafts, either membrane-free or NRM, were histologically evaluated. It has been reported that NRM has no significant effect on the formation of new bone [20].

Considering the above information, although studies comparing various graft materials with barrier membranes have been conducted to date, a complete consensus has not been reached due to the multitude of different uses and material choices, and the studies provide controversial and limited information. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of the horizontal application of ABA using NRM and RCM on bone changes (BC) at various horizontal and vertical bone levels using radiographic and retrospective analyses. The null hypothesis of the present study was that there would be no difference between the effects of NRM and RCM application on horizontal and vertical BG during horizontal ABA treatment.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample size

This retrospective observational study included patients who underwent horizontal ABA treatment at the College of Dentistry, University of Illinois, Chicago, between January 2002 and January 2024. The study design and protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Illinois Chicago (IRB approval number: 2024–0336). This study was conducted in accordance with the revised principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013). Prior to the surgical procedure, all participants provided written informed consent as required by institutional guidelines. This retrospective observational study evaluated postoperative data from the AxiUm system of the university. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines [21].

The following criteria were used to select patients for inclusion in this study: (1) patients aged ≥ 18 years; (2) patients with controlled systemic health or medical conditions; (3) patients who received horizontal ABA with RCM or NRM; (4) patients who underwent pre- and post-treatment CBCT images; (5) residual bone width < 4 mm; (6) CBCT scans with the same field of view (FOV) pre- and post-treatment; (7) complete superimposition of CBCT images within the software program; and (8) diagnostic quality of CBCT images.

The exclusion criteria were defined as follows for patient selection: (1) the presence of systemic diseases (radiotherapy, chemotherapy, neurological or psychiatric disability that may interfere with good oral hygiene, immunosuppressant disease, or use of immunosuppressant medications); (2) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (3) incomplete records; (4) heavy smokers (> 10 cigarettes per day); (5) socket augmentation; (6) combined augmentation including vertical and horizontal augmentation or maxillary sinus augmentation; (7) membrane exposure or any complications following two augmentation process; and (8) using additional biologics like emdogain or platelet rich fibrin.

Measurements were evaluated using CBCT images obtained before treatment (T0) and after healing time (4–6 months post-treatment; T1).

A previous study was used as a reference [22], and G Power software (version 3.1, Franz Faul, Germany) was used to determine the minimum total sample size required. This was found to be 20 patients when the effect size was 0.25, alpha value was 0.05, power value was 0.95, and two groups.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcomes of the present study were the horizontal and vertical BC values. The secondary outcomes were to evaluate the effects of sex, age, surgical area localization, baseline ridge width, ridge height, ridge angulation, concavity depth, CA, CL, and CT at the buccal (CTB), lingual or palatal (CTP), apical (CTA), and coronal (CTC) sites on horizontal and vertical BC.

Data collection methods

CBCT images were evaluated both preoperatively and postoperatively. CBCT images were obtained using an i-CAT™ FLX V17 3D CBCT unit (Imaging Sciences International LLC, Hatfield, Philadelphia, USA) operating at 1.4 mA and 120 kV, providing an 11 cm field of view at 0.2 voxel resolution. The output format was digital imaging and communication in medicine (DICOM). Two sets of DICOM data were output and transferred to an imaging software program (Dolphin Imaging version 11.5 Premium, Patterson Dental Supply, Inc., dba Dolphin Imaging and Management Solutions, Chatsworth, California, USA) for superimposition and measurement. Postoperative CBCT images of all subjects included in this study were obtained 4–6 months after the completion of the treatment. The resulting CBCT images were processed in the DICOM format, and the pretreatment and post-treatment images were automatically combined based on adjacent anatomical structures, such as the teeth, mandibular border, cranial base in the maxilla, lower border, angle in the mandible, and sinus floor. The DICOM data of the two CBCT images were manually checked to ensure a perfect match by varying the degree of transparency of the section [23]. Images were viewed on a Dell HDR screen (Dell Technologies, Austin, Texas, USA) with a resolution of 2560 × 1440 pixels, 60 Hz, and 24-bit. Pre- and post-treatment CBCT scans were loaded into the Dolphin Software Program in the DICOM format in accordance with the program instructions. Two cranial images were displayed on the screen (Fig. 1-A). To prevent image mixing after superimposition, the post-treatment image was marked yellow (Fig. 1-B). Both images were transferred to the Gonion-Menton plane, and three anatomical landmarks were marked in approximately the same regions. The images were superimposed on delineated regions. Region of interst (ROI)-based image superimpositions were repeated in the augmented areas to evaluate small deviations, and complete superimpositions were performed (Fig. 1-B). Figure 1-C and D present the pretreatment and post-treatment images after image superimposition, respectively. After superimposition, the color of the post-treatment CBCT image was modified from yellow to the original radiograph color to circumvent measurement bias. Subsequently, linear measurements were obtained by determining the reference points. To facilitate the collection of pretreatment and post-treatment measurements from a single point and mitigate the potential for bias, a specific point on the residual alveolar bone, either palatal, lingual, or apical, was determined and maintained constant in both CBCT images.

Statistical analyses

Radiographic CBCT measurements were performed by two researchers (B.G.A. and H.A.). The researchers conducted a calibration session to standardize the radiographic measurements. To assess intra- and inter-observer agreements, two researchers repeated the radiographic measurements of 10 surgical areas on two occasions, with a two-week interval between each repetition.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables are presented as frequencies. The data distribution was checked using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Parametric data were compared using the independent sample t-test, whereas nonparametric data were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. To model the categorical dependent variable with independent variables, Forward Conditional Binary Logistic Regression Analysis was applied. In the regression analysis, the dependent variable was defined based on the direction of change in bone dimensions following the ABA procedure. The cases were categorized as either “increasing” or “decreasing” BC. This dichotomization was preferred to reflect decision-making, in which the primary outcome of interest is whether BG is achieved. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors associated with BG. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis before the binary logistic regression analysis. All VIF values were < 3, indicating no significant multicollinearity. Analyses were performed using the International Business Machines Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (version 27.0, Armonk, New York, USA), and the significance level was set at 0.05.

A total of 1220 CBCT images were screened, and 69 patients with at least two CBCT images who received horizontal ABA at 120 sites were included in the study (Fig. 3). No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of age, sex, smoking status, surgical area, or graft type. However, a statistically significant difference was observed between the groups regarding the fixation type (Table 1).

Regression results

Regression analysis revealed that fixation type, RA-T0, and CTP were significantly associated with horizontal BC at 1 mm (p < 0.05). To assess the goodness of fit of the resulting model, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test was performed, and it was determined that the model fitted the data well (p > 0.05). In view of the fact that logistic regression models do not provide comprehensive information, the Nagelkerke R² was examined to ascertain the model’s explanatory power. This analysis yielded a result of 28.7%. The classification table revealed that the model accurately predicted 81.7% of the data. The fixation type-tack was associated with a lower odds ratio of BG compared to the fixation type mini-screw (OR: 0.005). RA-T0 was negatively associated with BG (OR: 0.955), indicating that each unit increase slightly decreased the odds of BG. For each one-unit increase in CTP, the odds of BG were 3.861 times the odds of bone loss (OR: 3.861) (Table 5).

Discussion

The present study showed that resorbable and non-resorbable barrier membranes used in horizontal ABA treatment had a significant effect on vertical BG, whereas no significant difference was observed in the horizontal BG. Therefore, the null hypothesis of the study, ' There is no difference between the effect of NRM and RCM applications on horizontal and vertical BC in horizontal ABA treatments,” was partially accepted in terms of horizontal BG or loss but partially rejected in terms of vertical BC.

In one study, ABA was performed on 28 maxillary incisor sites, and hard tissue stabilization was evaluated using superimposed CBCT images. Following horizontal augmentation with a bovine bone graft and RCM with titanium pins, significant bone reduction in the alveolar crest, particularly in the coronal region, and reduced new bone formation were observed in the patient [25]. In a study by Meloni et al., the average horizontal BG was 5.03 ± 2.15 mm, with approximately 1 mm of gain in the coronal region [26]. In one study, alveolar RW changes were evaluated after alveolar ridge preservation with superimposition. At the 4-month follow-up, the bone width decreased from 1.6 mm to 1 mm at the 1 mm level of the alveolar crest [27]. Another study reported that horizontal augmentation resulted in an average gain of 4.33 mm in RW at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months; however, no information was provided regarding the 1 mm level [24]. In the present study, no statistically significant difference in the radiographic horizontal BC was observed between the NRM and RCM groups. In this study, there was no statistically significant difference in horizontal BC; however, the mean gain per mm was numerically higher in the NRM group (2.03 mm) than in the RCM group (0.98 mm), with a p-value of 0.060, which was close to significance. In this study, the NRM group had a smaller sample size and included cases without complications, which may have been the reason for these results, owing to good radiographic ABA stabilization. Although several associations reached statistical significance, some odds ratios were relatively close to 1, suggesting limited effect size. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and statistical significance should not be considered equivalent to the strong biological or clinical relevance of the findings. This should be evaluated in further studies with larger and more balanced sample sizes.

In cases treated with the sandwich bone augmentation technique, the mean RA was 119.05 ± 10.03°, and the mean CD, CA, and CL were 4.39 mm, 146.32°, and 12.31 mm, respectively, prior to horizontal augmentation. The authors concluded that the higher the CA, the lower the RW gain; however, the effect of CA on the average RW gain was significant only at 9 and 12 mm [24]. Another study demonstrated a correlation between increased maxillary labial concavity and decreased labial cortical wall thickness [28]. One study reported that in sinus lifting procedures, increased concavity resulted in better graft stabilization and bone formation [29]. The present study demonstrated a positive correlation between BG at both the 3 mm and 6 mm levels and the CA value at T0, with the augmentation of BG correlating with an increase in CA value. Although some variables showed statistically significant associations, their odds ratios were close to 1, indicating a relatively small effect size per unit increase in the variables. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution regarding their clinical relevance. However, cumulative changes in these variables may still have a significant impact on clinical outcomes.

Naenni et al. found that narrower RW has a greater potential for BG than wider ridges [30]. One study found that the buccal bone obtained after augmentation with NRM demonstrated a moderate correlation with the estimated baseline ridge width [31]. It has been emphasized that CT measurements in the buccal, palatal, and coronal directions are related to primary and secondary stabilization with bone augmentation [32]. One study measured the thickness of the palatal and buccal bone. The results indicated that the palatal bone was thicker than the buccal bone [33]. A further study employing CBCT indicated that CBT at the coronal level was lower and more susceptible to resorption than that at the apical portion, particularly within the female group [34]. The present study highlighted that while there was a decline in radiographic BG with increasing coronal CT values, there was an increase in BG with increasing palatal CT values. However, studies evaluating bone augmentation using CT are limited, and further research is needed in this area.

In one study, no significant difference in BG was observed between fixation and non-fixation with a porcine membrane [35]. Yang et al. compared onlay bone grafting and the tenting screw technique, and the volumetric bone resorption rate was lower and better stabilization was observed in the tenting screw group [36]. In contrast, a recent study reported that GBR with additional membrane fixation in the aesthetic region yielded more positive outcomes regarding the volume stability of the enlarged area than cases without fixation. The study revealed no statistically significant difference in bone volume stability between the two fixation methods: membrane fixation with titanium pins and fixation with absorbable sutures [37]. In cases of simultaneous GBR and implant placement, the use of tenting screws resulted in greater BG and lower volumetric bone resorption rates [38]. Barrier membrane stabilization does not create a clinically significant difference in alveolar socket preservation in teeth with periodontal compromise [39]. In a comparative retrospective CBCT study of horizontal ABA treatment with tenting screws, a significant difference in RW changes, particularly at levels 1 and 3, was reported in the tenting screw group compared to the control group [40]. In this study, membrane tack was used in the RCM group, which may be associated with a decrease in BG at the 1 mm level in the RCM group. However, a limitation of our study is that information regarding whether the membrane was tacked coronally or apically could not be obtained from patient records and radiographs. The relationship between the localization of the membrane tack and alveolar BC should be investigated in future studies.

In the present study, xenografts and collagen membranes were placed simultaneously with the implant, and horizontal and vertical BC were evaluated using intraoral scanning and CBCT. Consequently, a loss of vertical dimension was observed in nine of the 13 patients following horizontal augmentation [41]. In a study by Lin et al., changes were reported in both the vertical and horizontal dimensions using Deproteinized bovine bone mineral and RCM or NRM for alveolar socket preservation. The midcrestal ridge height was significantly lower in the control group than in the test groups; however, no significant difference was observed between the NRM and RM groups [42]. Despite the use of allografts, xenografts, or combined grafting and membranes from various sources such as bovine tendon or pericardium in the present study, vertical BC were evaluated after horizontal ABA. The presence of multiple treatment combinations, various graft and membrane types, and numerous fixation methods may restrict the interpretation of the independent effects observed in the regression model. Although the absence of significant multicollinearity among these variables in the regression analysis contributes to the interpretation of the data, the large number of variables remains a limitation of this study. Therefore, variability should be reduced in future studies. It was concluded that more stable radiographic values were formed at the alveolar crest in the NRM group and that the height remained stable in terms of vertical BC.

In the voxel-based CBCT superimposition method, modelling is performed using the histogram density of the entire 3D volume, which has been reported to maximize superimposition. [43–44] In one study, the reliability of superimposition of the anterior cranial base or middle anterior cranial base structures using Dolphin 3D software was evaluated. The results demonstrated excellent reliability and reproducibility for the anterior cranial base and moderate reliability and reproducibility for the middle cranial base [45]. In a study evaluating the repeatability and reliability of voxel-based software before and after maxillary sinus augmentation procedures, Dolphin Imaging software was reported to provide high reliability and repeatability; however, minimal effects may occur depending on different time points and operators [46]. In our study, two different CBCT images were superimposed using Dolphin imaging software, and inter- and intra-rater calibrations were performed. Using a system that automatically superimposes images taken at different times with different angles and sections would be advantageous in our study; however, a significant limitation is that the margin of error in 100% superimposition during the superimposition of non-standard CBCT images could not be evaluated.

Our study has some limitations owing to its retrospective observational design. The present study evaluated the risk factors for postoperative complications in ABA operations and indicated that the surgeons’ experience was not a contributing factor [47]. However, another study emphasized that surgeon experience is important for ABA [48]. Horizontal ABA treatments were not performed by a single surgeon, and since information on surgical techniques and soft tissue values were not available in the system, their effects could not be evaluated. The present study evaluated the long-term stabilization of ABA using the 3D alveolar bone technique [49]. However, our study had limitations due to the lack of detailed information on the techniques used in the system and the inability to evaluate the post-implant CBCT. Intraoperative confounding factors, such as alveolar crest thickness, keratinized gingival width, and keratinized gingival thickness, could not be evaluated. Keratinized soft tissue tension is related to membrane tension and soft tissue healing and cannot be measured clinically. The heterogeneity of the membranes, grafts, and fixation methods used in this study is another limitation. In a study presenting the superimposition of patients’ digital data and CBCT data, it was shown that hard tissue volume changes and linear measurements provided better results for understanding treatment outcomes [50]. However, in the present study, volume assessment could not be performed because of its retrospective design and the absence of digital data. The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the radiographic criteria affecting horizontal augmentation and their impact on augmentation, as well as to assess the effectiveness of two different barrier membranes. Therefore, as a limitation, evaluations regarding the placement of dental implants, their long-term effectiveness, and marginal bone loss were not performed. However, future studies are needed to evaluate the long-term effectiveness of implants after augmentation. Another limitation of the present study is the dichotomization of continuous radiographic dimensional changes into binary outcome categories (increase vs. decrease). Although this approach was preferred to improve clinical interpretability and facilitate regression modelling, it may have reduced statistical sensitivity and resulted in a partial loss of quantitative information regarding the biologically complex bone remodeling processes. Nevertheless, future studies incorporating continuous outcome models may provide additional insights, such as dental implants, suprastructure success, and patient-reported outcomes, into the magnitude of these changes.

In a previous study, two different techniques for onlay grafting for autogenous horizontal ABA were compared, and it was reported that stabilization screws applied per graft were effective in maintaining horizontal contour [51]. A limitation of this study was the inability to evaluate the number of screws used because of the absence of homogeneous data, which was a confounding factor in the comparison of screw differences. Similarly, horizontal ABA was performed with collagen membranes with different degradation times, and no significant difference was observed in BC [52]. Despite the presence of varying degrees of degradation in the membranes of the RCM group, as evidenced in this study, all membranes exhibited a degree of biodegradation that facilitated the underlying biological processes. However, further studies with more homogeneous sample sizes are required. A review of the extant literature reveals that studies in this field generally consist of clinical trials in which only one variable differs while the other variables remain constant or retrospective presentations of long-term follow-up results. The present study is distinct from the extant literature in its objective to evaluate the impact of divergent graft membranes and membrane stabilization systems on radiological outcomes within a university clinic using a unified dataset. A wide variety of graft materials, membrane types, and fixation models are used for horizontal ABA, and studies evaluating all these simultaneously are quite limited. Notwithstanding the study’s limitations, which include a small sample size, brief follow-up period, and absence of post-implant evaluation, it offers a comprehensive overview of various graft horizontal ABA treatments.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, the findings showed that although there was no significant difference between the use of non-resorbable and resorbable membranes for horizontal augmentation, NRM may be more effective in stabilizing the coronal portion of the alveolar ridge and maintaining ridge height. It should be emphasized that the present study is based solely on radiographic outcomes, and no direct clinical, implant success, soft tissue, or patient-reported outcome measures were included. Therefore, the clinical interpretation of these findings should be made with caution. However, further studies with standardized protocols, homogeneous samples, and long-term clinical outcomes are required.
