Preparing for an ageing Tanzania: cross-sectional study assessing the undergraduate health students’ knowledge, attitudes and barriers to geriatric nutrition assessment
Authors: Rashid M Rashid, Dedius E Peter, Walter C Millanzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs Tanzania experiences a rapid demographic transition towards an ageing population, the burden of geriatric malnutrition is increasing. The capacity of the health workforce to effectively screen and manage nutritional status in the elderly is critical. However, the preparedness of undergraduate health students regarding geriatric nutrition assessment remains underexplored.ObjectiveThis study assessed knowledge, attitudes and perceived challenges regarding geriatric nutrition assessment among undergraduate health students in Dodoma, Tanzania.MethodsA cross-sectional analytical study involving 384 undergraduate health students, selected through simple random sampling, was conducted. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire and analysed using SPSS V.27. All independent variables with p≤0.2 were included in the multivariable logistic model. Independent variables with p≤0.05 and 95% CI were considered statistically significant.ResultsA total of 384 participants with a mean age of 23.61 years and an SD of ±2.00 years were included in the study. The study highlighted that 47.7% (n=183) had inadequate knowledge, 53.1% (n=204) had positive attitudes and 58.9% (n=226) experienced significant challenges. Factors such as age (20–23) years (adjusted OR (AOR)=6.998, 95% CI 2.222 to 22.038, p=0.001), marital status (AOR=6.413, 95% CI 1.279 to 32.139, p=0.024), geriatric training (AOR=0.380, 95% CI 0.222 to 0.650, p=0.001) and living with older adults (AOR=0.619, 95% CI 0.392 to 0.977, p=0.039) were significantly associated with knowledge. However, having already conducted an assessment of older adult nutritional status (AOR=1.748, 95% CI 1.085 to 2.814, p=0.022) was significantly associated with attitudes.ConclusionsUndergraduate health students in Tanzania show moderate knowledge and attitudes but substantial challenges in geriatric nutrition assessment, largely due to limited training and exposure. Integrating dedicated geriatric nutrition modules and practical experiences into curricula is urgently needed to prepare the workforce for Tanzania’s ageing population.

Introduction

The global population is undergoing a profound demographic transition characterised by a rapid increase in the proportion of older adults. According to the United Nations, the number of individuals aged 60 years and above is projected to rise from approximately 1 billion in 2020 to 2.1 billion by 2050, with the fastest growth occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 In sub-Saharan Africa, the elderly population is projected to increase by 218% between 2019 and 2050.2 3 In Tanzania, this demographic shift is increasingly evident, exemplifying this regional pattern, where the total population is anticipated to be more than double from 61.7 million in 2023 to between 120 and 137 million by 2050, with the proportion of individuals aged 60 years and above rising from about 4% to 10%, resulting in an elderly population exceeding 13 million.4 This transition poses significant challenges to healthcare systems that have traditionally focused on acute and infectious diseases rather than chronic and age-related conditions.

This demographic change worsens vulnerabilities such as geriatric malnutrition, which is a critical yet under-recognised condition that affects up to 50% of older adults in community settings and is linked to higher morbidity, longer hospital stays and increased mortality rates.5 Data show that over 97.6 million elderly individuals experienced malnutrition in 2021, with a prevalence of 18.6% worldwide.6 7 These figures are even more worrying in LMICs, where malnutrition in older populations affects up to 50% of community-dwelling elderly individuals and an even higher proportion in hospitalised and institutionalised settings.8 It is associated with adverse outcomes, including increased morbidity, frailty, sarcopenia, prolonged hospital stays, reduced quality of life and higher mortality rates.8 9 Age-related physiological changes such as reduced appetite, impaired nutrient absorption, multimorbidity and polypharmacy further exacerbate nutritional vulnerability in this population. Despite its significant impact, malnutrition often remains underdiagnosed and undertreated in routine clinical practice, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Early identification of malnutrition risk is therefore essential for improving health outcomes among older adults. Standardised tools such as the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) and Subjective Global Assessment have been widely validated and are recommended for use in clinical and community settings.10 11 Early nutritional screening enables timely interventions that can prevent deterioration, reduce healthcare costs and improve functional outcomes. However, effective implementation of these tools depends heavily on the knowledge, skills and attitudes of healthcare providers.

Despite the availability of validated assessment tools, substantial gaps persist in the nutritional care of older adults. Studies across LMICs have consistently reported inadequate knowledge and skills among healthcare professionals and students regarding geriatric nutrition assessment.12 13 Factors contributing to these gaps include limited curricular emphasis on geriatrics, insufficient clinical exposure to older adults, lack of training opportunities and systemic constraints such as high patient loads and resource limitations.14 15 These challenges are particularly pronounced in sub-Saharan Africa, where geriatric care infrastructure remains underdeveloped, and prioritisation of elderly health is still emerging. Furthermore, health professions education plays a critical role in addressing these gaps by equipping future healthcare providers with the competencies required to deliver effective geriatric care. Undergraduate health students represent the future workforce and are central to the sustainability of healthcare systems in ageing societies. Evidence suggests that early exposure to geriatric care, including nutrition assessment, significantly improves both knowledge and attitudes towards older adults.16

Effective geriatric nutrition assessment involves tools like the MNA, which evaluates dietary intake, anthropometric measures and functional status to inform tailored interventions.11 However, the success of such assessment rests on the competence of healthcare providers, who must integrate biological, psychological and sociocultural dimensions of ageing into their practice.8 9 Undergraduate health students, as future front-line providers, play a pivotal role in geriatric care. Yet, evidence from Ethiopia and Nigeria indicates widespread deficiencies in knowledge and negative attitudes towards elderly care among nursing and medical students.11 17 This underscores the persistent deficiencies in their preparedness.12 Several studies have highlighted inadequate knowledge of geriatric nutrition among nursing and medical students, with only 17%–40% demonstrating sufficient understanding of malnutrition screening and management in older adults.12 13 18

Moreover, attitudes towards geriatric care are equally concerning, with negative perceptions such as viewing elderly patients as burdensome or low-priority correlating with reluctance to specialise in geriatrics.18 19 Perceived challenges, including limited clinical exposure, resource scarcity and insufficient training, further compound these issues, as reported in scoping reviews on nutrition literacy among older adults and their caregivers.20 This gap is critical in Dodoma City Council, a rapidly urbanising area with a population of over 3 million, where undergraduate health programmes at institutions like the University of Dodoma offer potential for intervention.21

Although several studies have explored general knowledge and attitudes towards elderly care, there is limited evidence focusing specifically on geriatric nutrition assessment, particularly in the Tanzanian context. Given the growing burden of malnutrition among older adults and the critical role of early nutritional assessment in preventing adverse outcomes, it is essential to understand the preparedness of undergraduate health students in this domain. Assessing their knowledge, attitudes and perceived challenges provides valuable insights into existing educational gaps and informs targeted curriculum improvements. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes and perceived challenges regarding geriatric nutrition assessment among undergraduate health students at the University of Dodoma, Tanzania. The findings of this study provide an insight that can help to inform curriculum enhancements, foster positive mindsets towards elderly care and ultimately contribute to improved nutritional outcomes for Tanzania’s ageing population, aligning with Sustainable Development Goal 3 on health and well-being.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional analytical study design was employed to gather data from undergraduate health students at the University of Dodoma on their knowledge, attitudes and perceived challenges related to geriatric nutrition assessment. A quantitative research approach was appropriate for this study as it allows for measurements of variables, statistical analysis and identification of patterns and relationships.22 This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study setting

This study was conducted at the University of Dodoma, specifically in the School of Nursing and Public Health and the School of Medicine and Dentistry, in Dodoma, Tanzania. The University of Dodoma is a public university located in Dodoma, the capital city of Tanzania. The University is the largest in Tanzania by size and has a vast infrastructure supporting diverse academic programmes. Regarding health-related education, it has notable faculties including the School of Nursing and Public Health and the School of Medicine and Dentistry. These schools enrol a large number of students in health programmes, including Bachelor of Science in Nursing, Bachelor of Science in Clinical Medicine, Diploma in Nursing and Midwifery and Bachelor of Science in Clinical Nutrition and Dietetics. More than 2500 undergraduate health students are enrolled in these schools in various programmes each year. The university has a well-equipped infrastructure for a conducive teaching and learning environment.

Study population

This study involved undergraduate students pursuing a Bachelor of Science in Nursing, Bachelor of Science in Clinical Medicine, Diploma in Nursing and Midwifery and Bachelor of Science in Clinical Nutrition and Dietetics. Moreover, best practices and evidence-based care that can improve older adults’ nutrition care outcomes are identified through research. In order to maintain a high standard of geriatric nutrition, these upcoming healthcare providers must be well-trained in geriatric nutrition assessment.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To minimise bias and enhance the reliability of the collected data, specific inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. Undergraduate health students enrolled at the University of Dodoma who gave consent were eligible to participate. Students from other national or international institutions undertaking short-term elective rotations were excluded, as their training curricula and experiences may differ from the local context. In addition, students who were on official leave, including medical, administrative or suspension, during the data collection period (May to June 2025) were excluded from the study. Furthermore, individuals who participated in the pretesting and validation of the questionnaire were excluded to prevent potential test bias or familiarity with the instrument. Finally, incomplete or improperly filled questionnaires were excluded from the analysis to ensure data quality and consistency.

Sample size estimation and sampling procedure

The sample size was calculated using the Cochran formula.23 Employing the following formula: N=(Z1−α⁄2)² p (1−p)/d². Z1−α⁄2 was 1.96 at an alpha level of 5%, p is the estimated proportion of an attribute present in the students, and d was set at 0.05, the margin of error set at 95%. The total sample size was 384 undergraduate health students. The sample was proportionately distributed across programmes, including BSc Nursing (n=105, 27.3%), Clinical nutrition and dietetics (n=104, 27.1%), Diploma in nursing (n=81, 21.1%) and medical doctor (n=94, 24.5%). To ensure representativeness across the various stages of training, a proportionate stratified random sampling technique was employed. Students were first stratified by their academic year of study and then by their programme. They were further stratified into three distinct periods, including the beginning of the course, characterised by the foundational theoretical sciences (year 2), the middle of the course, representing the transition into initial clinical clerkships (year 3), and the end of the course, representing the demonstration of higher autonomy in geriatric nutrition assessment (years 4–5).

Within each stratum, participants were selected using a simple random sampling (Lottery) method. This approach ensured that the final-stage students, who have higher exposure to geriatric modules, are adequately represented in the analysis and ensured fairness in participant selection.

Data collection tools and procedures

This tool was adapted from the previous study conducted by Bauer et al.10 To ensure the tool was contextually appropriate for the Tanzanian healthcare environment and undergraduate health curricula, it underwent a formal adaptation process. Adaptation involved contextual modification to suit undergraduate health students in Tanzania while maintaining the conceptual domains of knowledge, attitudes and perceived challenges. This included a forward translation into Swahili by a lingual health expert and a subsequent back-translation by an independent linguist to ensure semantic equivalence and linguistic validity as suggested by Tsang et al.24 The data collection tool had four sections: section A included demographic characteristics; section B focused on knowledge—with 10 questions; section C with 10 Likert-scale items with response options ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (4) and D consisted of questions addressing perceived challenges 10 Likert-scale items with response options ranging from ‘I don’t know’ (1) to ‘major challenge’ (4) (see online supplemental file 1). The data collection tool was developed in the English language and then translated from English to Swahili, which is the national language in Tanzania.

Data collection was done from May 2025 to June 2025. The principal investigator collected the data in all programmes. The participants were approached by the principal investigator and informed of their participation in the study. Those who agreed to participate were given a consent form to read and sign; thereafter, they were asked to fill out a questionnaire. The time to fill in the questionnaire was not more than 30 min, and a follow-up was conducted to ensure that the completed forms were returned. The principal investigator supervised the whole process of data collection and ensured that each step was followed during data collection.

Validity and reliability

The psychometric integrity of the tools was established through both validity and reliability. Face and content validity were confirmed by a panel of four senior nutritionists and geriatric specialists who evaluated the items’ clarity, relevance and cultural sensitivity. Their feedback helped establish coding and revise the tool based on observed difficulties and comments regarding the clarity of the questions to improve understanding. In the current study, the tool demonstrated high internal consistency across its primary domains. The questionnaire’s internal consistency of Cronbach’s Alpha for knowledge, attitude and perceived challenges was 0.80, 0.75 and 0.83, respectively, indicating good internal consistency. The corrected item-total correlations ranged from 0.43 to 0.68. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was 0.83, indicating very good adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (x2=885.788, p<0.001), supporting the suitability of the data for factor analysis. Principal component analysis with varimax rotation was used to extract factors. Factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were retained.

Variable measurement

This study measured three primary outcome variables: knowledge, attitudes and perceived challenges regarding geriatric nutrition assessment among undergraduate health students. Knowledge of geriatric nutrition assessment was measured using 10 structured questions, where each correct response was scored as ‘1’ and incorrect responses as ‘0’. The total score was converted into a percentage, and scores ≥75% were classified as adequate knowledge, while scores <75% were classified as inadequate knowledge.20

Attitudes towards geriatric nutrition assessment were assessed using ten Likert-scale items with response options ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (4). A composite score was calculated, and participants scoring equal to or above the mean (28.48) were categorised as having a positive attitude, while those scoring below the mean were categorised as having a negative attitude.

Perceived challenges were measured using ten Likert-scale items with response options ranging from ‘I don’t know’ (1) to ‘major challenge’ (4). A composite score was computed, and scores equal to or above the mean (30.42) were classified as major challenges, while scores below the mean were considered minor challenges.

The independent (exposure) variables included sociodemographic and academic characteristics of the participants. These variables comprised age (categorised as 20–23, 24–27 and ≥28 years), gender (male/female), marital status (single, married, divorced), programme of study (BSc Nursing, Degree of Medicine, Diploma in Nursing and Midwifery, Clinical Nutrition and Dietetics), year of study, training in general nutrition (yes/no), training in geriatric nutrition (yes/no), prior experience in assessing older adults’ nutritional status (yes/no) and living with older adults (yes/no).

Data analysis

Before analysis, we screened and cleaned the data to check for consistency and completeness. SPSS V.27 was used for both data management and statistical analysis. Descriptive analysis was done to establish frequencies and percentages of demographic characteristics, level of knowledge, level of attitude and perceived challenges. The logistic regression was done to determine the association between the knowledge and attitude regarding geriatric nutrition assessment. A p<0.05 at a 95% CI was used to ascertain the association between the dependent variables, such as knowledge and attitude, and independent variables. Moreover, the multivariable logistic regression model was used to control for potential confounding effects and included all independent variables from the bivariate logistic regression model with a p≤0.2. Variables with an adjusted OR and a p<0.05 were deemed to have a significant association with the knowledge, attitudes and perceived challenges regarding geriatric nutrition assessment.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of respondents

A total of 384 participants with a mean age of 23.61 years and an SD of ±2.00 years were included in the study. Out of these 384 undergraduate health students with a 96% response rate, 54.7% (n=210) were aged between 20 and 23 years. More than half, 57.8% (n=222), of the study respondents were male. Regarding marital status, the majority, 83.6% (n=321), were single. More than two-thirds of the respondents, 70.6% (n=271), attained training in nutrition. In addition, more than half, 56.5% (n=217), of the study respondents did not receive training in geriatric nutrition. Furthermore, about 63% (n=242) of the respondents do not live with older adults (table 1).

Perceived challenges regarding geriatric nutrition assessment among respondents

More than half, 58.9% (n=226) of respondents had experienced a major challenge regarding geriatric nutrition assessment. In addition, more than three-quarters scored 76.5% (3.06±0.918) on the item about time constraints during hospital rounds. Regarding limited clinical exposure and cultural beliefs about ageing and food, more than three-quarters perceived a major challenge with a score of 76.2% (3.05±0.946) (table 5).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess undergraduate health students’ knowledge, attitudes and perceived challenges regarding geriatric nutrition assessment among undergraduate health students at the University of Dodoma, Tanzania. The findings revealed that nearly half of the participants (47.7%) had inadequate knowledge of geriatric nutrition assessment, while slightly more than half (53.1%) demonstrated positive attitudes towards it. The majority (58.9%) perceived major challenges in performing geriatric nutrition assessments. Training in geriatric nutrition, living with older adults, year of study, prior experience in assessing older adults’ nutritional status, gender and general nutrition training were significantly associated with knowledge, attitudes and perceived challenges, respectively.

The finding that 47.7% of undergraduate health students had inadequate knowledge of geriatric nutrition assessment is concerning but not surprising, given the limited emphasis on geriatrics and geriatric nutrition in most African health professions curricula. These results align with previous studies in Ethiopia,15 Ghana,25 Palestine26 and Zanzibar,13 where nursing and medical students showed similar deficiencies in geriatric nutrition knowledge. In contrast, European medical and nursing programmes consistently report that 60%–75% of students demonstrate adequate knowledge.27 The probable reasons for this disparity of findings are attributable to compulsory gerontology or geriatric nutrition modules, high-fidelity simulation laboratories, and abundant clinical placements in dedicated geriatric units or long-term care facilities in developed countries. The low knowledge level can be attributed to several factors, such as geriatric nutrition is often not a stand-alone course but rather a small module within broader nutrition or medical-surgical nursing courses, limited clinical exposure to older adults during training, scarcity of dedicated geriatric wards or long-term care facilities in Tanzania, and the relatively recent recognition of population ageing as a public health priority in LMICs.

Only 54.9% of respondents could correctly define geriatric nutrition, and knowledge gaps were particularly evident in foundational concepts such as the definition of the field itself and the most appropriate assessment tools. The particularly low correct response rate for defining geriatric nutrition in the present study mirrors findings from Kenya and South Africa, where health professions students and clinicians demonstrate an inadequate grasp of malnutrition screening tools like the MNA in geriatrics.28 29 The findings underscore that the knowledge deficit identified in this study has direct clinical implications such that future healthcare providers who cannot accurately identify malnutrition risk in older adults may fail to initiate timely interventions, thereby contributing to higher rates of sarcopenia, frailty, falls, prolonged hospital stays and mortality among Tanzania’s rapidly ageing population.

The study found that 53.1% of respondents had positive attitudes towards geriatric nutrition assessment, which represents a relatively encouraging starting point. Students strongly agreed that nutrition assessment in older patients is important (79%), that elderly patients are at higher risk of malnutrition (78.8%) and that nutrition significantly affects health outcomes in this population (76.8%). However, notably lower scores were recorded for items such as geriatric nutrition is a priority in healthcare (56.8%) and assessment of elderly nutrition should not be the role of nutritionists only (56.5%). This suggests that while students intellectually acknowledge the importance of geriatric nutrition, they do not yet see it as a shared professional responsibility or a high-priority area within the broader healthcare system.

This pattern mirrors findings from Ethiopia, where nursing and medical students viewed care of older adults as low-status, burdensome or less rewarding than paediatric or acute care specialities.30 Students who had already performed nutritional assessment on older adults were almost twice as likely to have positive attitudes, confirming the well-established link between hands-on experience and attitudinal shift.16 These results reinforce the need for early, repeated and structured clinical exposure to older adults throughout the curriculum rather than concentrating on theoretical aspects often. Perceived challenges were rated as major by 58.9% of respondents, with the highest scores for lack of training opportunities (80.2%), lack of knowledge on geriatric nutrition (79.8%), time constraints during hospital rounds (76.5%), limited clinical exposure (76.2%) and cultural beliefs about ageing and food (76.2%). This constellation of barriers is not so new as it has been reported in previous studies conducted in African countries.14 15 25

The strong perception of lack of training opportunities and inadequate learning materials reflects the reality that most health professions curricula in Tanzania do not include geriatric nutrition as an independent course. Time constraints and limited clinical exposure are systemic issues tied to high patient loads, understaffed wards and the predominance of acute-care teaching hospitals with few long-stay geriatric patients.13 The strong protective effect of specific geriatric nutrition training and living with older adults on knowledge provides clear direction for intervention. Students who have daily interaction with older adults through co-residence develop practical insight into age-related physiological changes, food preferences, chewing difficulties, and polypharmacy effects, experiences that classroom teaching alone cannot replicate.26

Similarly, the fact that prior assessment experience almost doubled the odds of positive attitudes highlights the power of experiential learning. These findings collectively indicate that curriculum reform should prioritise a dedicated geriatric nutrition module with simulated and real-patient assessment practice, community-based placements in areas with high elderly populations, interprofessional education, and integration of validated tools such as the MNA into routine clinical skills training.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study included undergraduate health students from multiple programmes, which improved the representativeness of the sample within the institution. In addition, the use of stratified and simple random sampling techniques minimised selection bias. Also, the application of multivariable logistic regression analysis further strengthened the study by controlling for potential confounding variables, thereby enhancing the internal validity of the findings; however, these variables may not represent an exhaustive list. Other potential influencing factors, such as institutional resources, quality of teaching, clinical supervision, personal interest in geriatrics, and cultural beliefs, were not directly measured and may have contributed to the observed outcomes. Also, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes causal inference, and the use of self-reported measures may have introduced social desirability bias. Moreover, the single-institution setting may limit the generalisability of the results beyond similar academic contexts. Additionally, the study did not assess test–retest reliability, which limits conclusions regarding the stability of the instrument over time.

Conclusions

The study demonstrates moderate knowledge, modestly positive attitudes and substantial perceived challenges towards geriatric nutrition assessment. As Tanzania’s elderly population surges, these findings underscore the imperative for immediate curricular integration of comprehensive geriatric nutrition content to equip future providers and reduce malnutrition burden in older adults. Failure to prepare the future healthcare workforce for geriatric nutrition care will translate into preventable malnutrition, increased healthcare costs, and reduced quality of life. The findings highlight immediate curriculum enhancement, increased clinical exposure and community engagement initiatives to foster competent, confident and compassionate providers ready to meet the nutritional needs of an ageing population.
