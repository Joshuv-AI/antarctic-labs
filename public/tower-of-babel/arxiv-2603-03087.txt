1
Introduction

The line graph
L(G)
is a classical construction in graph theory, encoding the
adjacency relations among the edges of a graph
G
.
Two edges of
G
are adjacent in
L(G)
precisely when they share a common
endpoint.
While this construction preserves many combinatorial and spectral properties,
the passage from
G
to
L(G)
is inherently lossy.
In particular, the line graph records that two edges meet, but not
how they meet: it forgets whether the edges form a coherent local
configuration along a
2
–path or a conflicting one.
This loss of local structural information underlies classical reconstruction
ambiguities, most notably Whitney’s theorem, which shows that the triangle

K_{3}
and the claw
K_{1,3}
have isomorphic line graphs
[
17
]
.

In this paper we introduce the
antisymmetric line graph
(ALG), a canonical
signed graph associated to any finite simple graph
G
.
The antisymmetric line graph refines the ordinary line graph by assigning a sign

\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}
to each adjacency in
L(G)
, encoding the local orientation
structure of the corresponding
2
–paths.
Unlike general signed graphs, whose signings are typically chosen to model
external constraints or balance conditions, the signing underlying ALG is

canonical
.
It arises functorially from the incidence structure of
G
, and is independent
of any arbitrary labeling or reference orientation used during construction,
up to switching equivalence.

Conceptually, ALG sits strictly between
G
and its line graph.
On the one hand, forgetting the signs of ALG recovers the ordinary line graph

L(G)
.
On the other hand, the signed structure carries strictly more information than

L(G)
alone.
We show that the switching class of the antisymmetric line graph determines the
base graph
G
up to isomorphism, modulo isolated vertices, thereby resolving
Whitney’s exceptional ambiguity.
In this sense, ALG provides a minimal signed refinement of the line graph that
restores injectivity of the passage from graphs to edge–adjacency structures.

A key point (made explicit below) is that the adjacency matrix of
\mathcal{A}(G)
is a shifted Gram matrix
D^{\mathsf{T}}D-2I
, so its spectrum is equivalent to the spectrum of the Laplacian
DD^{\mathsf{T}}
of
G
up to a uniform shift and padding.
We view this not as a limitation but as a structural advantage:
\mathcal{A}(G)
provides a
canonical geometric realization of the Laplacian
as a signed graph on the edge set of
G
.
While purely spectral information in
\mathcal{A}(G)
collapses to Laplacian spectral data of
G
, the full switching class—and switching invariants such as the frustration index and signed cycle structure—can carry additional, genuinely non-spectral information.

In particular, several classical optimization measures on
G
(Max-Cut defect and odd cycle
transversal) admit exact or constant-factor expressions in terms of frustration parameters of

\mathcal{A}(G)
, giving a concrete complexity-theoretic aspect to this signed refinement.

The antisymmetric line graph (ALG) is a canonical signed refinement of the ordinary line graph.
It may be introduced intrinsically via oriented incidence: to each finite simple graph
G
we
associate a signed graph
\mathcal{A}(G)
on vertex set
E(G)
whose underlying unsigned graph is

L(G)
, and whose signing is well-defined up to switching.

This signed refinement also arises naturally from symmetric lift theory: in
[
4
]
we
constructed the level–
2
symmetric lift
\mathrm{HL}^{\prime}_{2}(G)
and showed that its natural involution
splits it into symmetric and antisymmetric sectors. The symmetric sector is governed by
L(G)
,
and the antisymmetric sector is governed by the signed graph
\mathcal{A}(G)
(denoted
L^{-}(G)

in
[
4
]
). In the present paper we isolate
\mathcal{A}(G)
as an object of independent
interest and develop its intrinsic theory.

Main results.

The central structural fact is that
\mathcal{A}(G)
is balanced if and only if
G
is bipartite, so
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
measures non-bipartiteness in a switching-invariant way.
We derive quantitative bounds comparing
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
to standard deletion parameters, including

\mathrm{def}(G)\ \leq\ \ell(\mathcal{A}(G))\ \leq\ (\Delta(G)-1)\,\mathrm{def}(G).

Our strongest theorem identifies the cubic case exactly:
for every cubic graph
G
,

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))\ =\ 2\,\mathrm{oct}(G).

Equivalently, minimizing the frustration index of
\mathcal{A}(G)
is (up to the scaling factor
2
) exactly the Odd Cycle Transversal problem on cubic graphs, and therefore transfers its complexity and algorithmic landscape to the antisymmetric line graph setting.

What is new in this paper.

We obtain the following structural and algorithmic results for
\mathcal{A}(G)
:

(i)

Reconstruction.
The switching class of
\mathcal{A}(G)
determines
G
up to
isomorphism modulo isolated vertices, resolving Whitney’s exceptional ambiguity.

(ii)

Signed subgraph rigidity.
We prove a cycle-parity law: every
k
–cycle in
G

induces a
k
–cycle in
L(G)
of sign
(-1)^{k}
in
\mathcal{A}(G)
. Consequently
\mathcal{A}(G)
is
balanced if and only if
G
is bipartite, and every induced cycle of length
k\geq 4
in
\mathcal{A}(G)

has rigid sign
(-1)^{k}
, yielding an infinite family of forbidden signed induced cycles.

(iii)

Low-order switching invariants.
The signed triangle imbalance

\Delta_{3}(G)=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(S^{3})}{6}=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\binom{d(v)}{3}-\#\triangle(G)

admits a completely elementary combinatorial interpretation (Theorem
4
).

(iv)

Frustration and optimization.
We relate frustration parameters of
\mathcal{A}(G)

to classical cut-type invariants of
G
, including
vf(\mathcal{A}(G))=\mathrm{def}(G)
and, for cubic graphs,

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))=2\,\mathrm{oct}(G)
, implying NP-hardness of computing
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))

even on cubic inputs.

(v)

Computational evidence.
On graphs with at most seven vertices, signed invariants of

\mathcal{A}(G)
distinguish examples not separated by unsigned line-graph data, including
line-graph cospectrality.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2

\mathcal{A}(G)
via incidence matrices in section
3
. The next section
4

The rest of the paper focusses on the new graph invariants that arise as a result. The signed triangle imbalance and its combinatorial interpretation are analyzed in Section
5
. Section

2
Preliminaries

In this section we fix notation and recall standard definitions concerning line
graphs and signed graphs.
All graphs in this paper are finite, simple, and undirected unless stated
otherwise.

2.1
Graphs and Line Graphs

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.
The
line graph

L(G)
is the graph whose vertex set is
E(G)
, with two
vertices adjacent if and only if the corresponding edges of
G
share a common
endpoint.
We write
A_{L(G)}
for the adjacency matrix of
L(G)
.

The line graph construction is functorial with respect to graph isomorphisms, but
it is not injective.
Whitney’s classical theorem characterizes the failure of injectivity.

Theorem 1

(Whitney
[
17
]
)
.

Let
G
and
H
be connected graphs.
If
L(G)\cong L(H)
, then either
G\cong H
, or
\{G,H\}=\{K_{3},K_{1,3}\}
.

Since line graphs are defined on edges, they do not detect isolated vertices.
Accordingly, reconstruction results involving
L(G)
are always understood up to
the addition or removal of isolated vertices.

2.2
Signed Graphs

A
signed graph
is a pair
(\Gamma,\sigma)
, where
\Gamma=(V,E)
is a graph
and
\sigma:E\to\{+1,-1\}
assigns a sign to each edge.
Equivalently, a signed graph may be specified by a symmetric matrix

A=(a_{ij})_{i,j\in V}
with
a_{ij}\in\{0,+1,-1\}
, where
|a_{ij}|
records
adjacency and
\operatorname{sign}(a_{ij})
records the edge sign.

A fundamental notion in signed graph theory is
switching
.
Given a signed graph
(\Gamma,\sigma)
and a vertex
v\in V
, switching at
v

means reversing the signs of all edges incident to
v
.

Definition 1

(Switching class)
.

Two signed graphs
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
on the same underlying unsigned graph are

switching equivalent
if
\Sigma_{2}
can be obtained from
\Sigma_{1}
by a sequence of switchings.
The corresponding equivalence class is called the
switching class
of
\Sigma
.

Switching equivalence preserves all intrinsic signed-graph properties, including
cycle balance, frustration index, and signed spectra.
Accordingly, signed graphs are typically studied up to switching equivalence.

2.3
Cycles and Balance

Let
(\Gamma,\sigma)
be a signed graph.
For a cycle
C\subseteq\Gamma
, the
sign of
C
is defined as the product
of the signs of its edges.
A cycle is called
positive
if this product is
+1
and
negative
if
it is
-1
.
A signed graph is said to be
balanced
if all of its cycles are positive.

In the present work, we will be particularly interested in signed triangles in
the antisymmetric line graph.
The imbalance between positive and negative triangles will give rise to a
canonical invariant of ordinary graphs, introduced in later sections.

2.4
Incidence Matrices

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.
Fix an arbitrary orientation of each edge.
The (oriented) incidence matrix
D
of
G
is the
|V|\times|E|
matrix defined by

D_{ve}=\begin{cases}+1&\text{if $v$ is the head of $e$,}\\
-1&\text{if $v$ is the tail of $e$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The matrix
D^{T}D
encodes adjacency relations among edges together with their
relative orientations.
Changing the orientation of a single edge corresponds to multiplying the
corresponding column of
D
by
-1
.
As we shall see, this operation induces a switching transformation at the
corresponding vertex of the line graph.

This observation underlies the canonical nature of the antisymmetric line graph
construction developed in Section
3
.

3
Construction of the Antisymmetric Line Graph

The antisymmetric line graph may be obtained abstractly as a component of the
symmetric lift
\mathrm{HL}^{\prime}_{2}(G)
introduced in earlier work

[
4
]
.
For the purposes of the present paper, however, it is convenient to give a
direct and intrinsic construction that makes no reference to the full lift
formalism.
We show that this construction is canonical and coincides with the antisymmetric
sector of
\mathrm{HL}^{\prime}_{2}(G)
.

Relation to signed graph theory.

Signed graphs and their switching classes have been studied extensively,
notably in the foundational work of Zaslavsky and subsequent developments.
However, the antisymmetric line graph
\mathcal{A}(G)
introduced here is not an
arbitrary signing of the line graph
L(G)
, nor does it depend on external
choices or optimization criteria. Instead, the signing is forced canonically
by the bipartite double cover underlying the symmetric lift, and is uniquely
determined up to switching by the incidence structure of
G
itself.
In this sense,
\mathcal{A}(G)
constitutes a minimal signed refinement of the line
graph: it preserves all adjacency information present in
L(G)
while
recording orientation-consistency data that is otherwise lost. This additional
signed structure is precisely what restores injectivity beyond Whitney’s
theorem, distinguishing graphs with isomorphic line graphs without introducing
extraneous degrees of freedom.

3.1
Canonical Orientation and Incidence

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite simple graph.
Although
G
is undirected, we fix an arbitrary reference orientation of its
edges for the purpose of computation.
This choice plays no structural role and will be shown to affect the
construction only up to switching equivalence.

Let
D
be the
|V|\times|E|
oriented incidence matrix of
G
, defined as in
Section
2
by

D_{ve}=\begin{cases}+1&\text{if $v$ is the head of $e$,}\\
-1&\text{if $v$ is the tail of $e$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The matrix
D^{T}D
encodes both adjacency relations among edges and their relative
orientations.

Lemma 1

(ALG refines the line graph)
.

Let
G
be a finite graph and let
\mathcal{A}(G)
be its antisymmetric line graph.
Then

|A_{\mathcal{A}(G)}|\;=\;A_{L(G)}.

Equivalently, the underlying unsigned graph of
\mathcal{A}(G)
is exactly the
line graph
L(G)
.

Proof.

By definition,

A_{\mathcal{A}(G)}\;=\;D^{\mathsf{T}}D-2I.

The matrix
D^{\mathsf{T}}D
has diagonal entries
2
, and its off–diagonal entry

(D^{\mathsf{T}}D)_{ef}
is nonzero if and only if the distinct edges
e
and
f

share a common endpoint in
G
; in that case
(D^{\mathsf{T}}D)_{ef}\in\{\pm 1\}
.
Subtracting
2I
therefore removes the diagonal and leaves the same
0/1
support
pattern as the line graph adjacency matrix. Taking entrywise absolute values
yields
|A_{\mathcal{A}(G)}|=A_{L(G)}
.
∎

3.2
Definition via Signed Adjacency

Recall that the adjacency matrix of the ordinary line graph can be written as

A_{L(G)}=|D^{T}D|-2I,

where
|\cdot|
denotes entrywise absolute value and
I
is the identity matrix.
In particular,
|D^{T}D|
records which pairs of edges share a common endpoint.

Definition 2

(Antisymmetric Line Graph)
.

The
antisymmetric line graph

\mathcal{A}(G)
is the signed graph with
vertex set
E(G)
and signed adjacency matrix

A_{\mathcal{A}(G)}\;:=\;D^{T}D-2I.

(1)

Equivalently, since
A_{L(G)}
has
0/1
off–diagonal entries and zero diagonal,
one may write

A_{\mathcal{A}(G)}\;=\;(D^{T}D)\circ A_{L(G)},

where
\circ
denotes the Hadamard (entrywise) product.

Remark 1

(Laplacian spectrum versus switching class)
.

Since the nonzero eigenvalues of
D^{\mathsf{T}}D
coincide with those of
DD^{\mathsf{T}}
the graph Laplacian, the spectrum of

S=D^{\mathsf{T}}D-2I

is the Laplacian spectrum of
G
shifted by
-2
, together with additional eigenvalues equal to
-2
coming from the kernel of
D^{\mathsf{T}}D
.
Consequently, any
purely spectral
invariant of
\mathcal{A}(G)
is algebraically equivalent to a Laplacian spectral invariant of
G
.
The primary value of
\mathcal{A}(G)
lies not in its spectrum alone, but in its canonical
switching class
as a signed graph on
E(G)
, which supports non-spectral switching invariants such as the frustration index and signed cycle structure; see, e.g.,
[
11
,
13
,
6
]
.

Equivalently, two distinct edges
e,f\in E(G)
are adjacent in

\mathcal{A}(G)
if and only if they share a common endpoint in
G
.
The sign of this adjacency is determined as follows:

•

if
e
and
f
have the
same incidence
at their common vertex
(both enter or both leave), the corresponding edge of

\mathcal{A}(G)
is assigned sign
+1
;

•

if
e
and
f
are oriented
transitively
through their common
vertex (one enters and the other leaves), the corresponding edge is
assigned sign
-1
.

Edges of
G
that do not share a vertex are nonadjacent in

\mathcal{A}(G)
.
By construction, the underlying unsigned graph of
\mathcal{A}(G)
is precisely
the line graph
L(G)
.

3.3
Canonical Nature and Switching Invariance

Although the definition above depends on a chosen reference orientation of
G
,
the resulting signed graph is canonical in the sense of signed graph theory.

Proposition 1

(Switching invariance)
.

Let
\vec{G}
and
\vec{G}^{\prime}
be two reference orientations of the same graph
G
.
Then the corresponding antisymmetric line graphs

\mathcal{A}(\vec{G})
and
\mathcal{A}(\vec{G}^{\prime})
are switching equivalent.

Proof.

Reversing the orientation of a single edge
e
multiplies the corresponding column of the oriented
incidence matrix
D
by
-1
. Hence in
D^{T}D
the
e
th row and
e
th column are multiplied by
-1
,
while all other entries are unchanged. This is exactly switching at the vertex
e
of the signed graph

\mathcal{A}(G)
(whose vertices are the edges of
G
). Therefore any two choices of orientation yield
switching-equivalent signed graphs.
∎

The construction above is not ad hoc. In the symmetric lift
\mathrm{HL}^{\prime}_{2}(G)
, the involution reversing edge orientations induces a decomposition into symmetric and antisymmetric subspaces

[
4
]
.
The operator governing the antisymmetric subspace is precisely
D^{T}D-2I
,
restricted to edge adjacencies. Under this identification, the ordinary line graph
L(G)
governs the symmetric sector of the lift, while the antisymmetric line graph
\mathcal{A}(G)
governs the antisymmetric sector. The present work isolates
\mathcal{A}(G)
as an object of independent interest.

4
Antisymmetric Line Graphs within Signed Graph Theory

The antisymmetric line graph embeds ordinary graphs into the theory of signed
graphs in a highly constrained and canonical way.
In this section we clarify how antisymmetric line graphs fit within the broader
landscape of signed graph theory, and how they differ from arbitrary signings of
line graphs.

4.1
Signed Line Graphs versus Antisymmetric Line Graphs

Signed graphs have been studied extensively, beginning with the foundational
work of Harary and Zaslavsky, motivated by balance theory, frustration, and
applications in social networks and combinatorial optimization

[
10
,
18
,
19
]
.
In this general setting, the choice of signs on edges is typically external to
the underlying graph structure.

By contrast, the antisymmetric line graph
\mathcal{A}(G)
assigns signs
canonically to the edges of the line graph
L(G)
.
The signing is determined entirely by the incidence structure of the base graph
and is invariant under relabeling and reorientation, up to switching.
Thus
\mathcal{A}(G)
defines a highly restricted subclass of signed graphs whose
structure is inherited from an unsigned object.

It is important to note that not every signing whose underlying
unsigned graph is a line graph arises as an antisymmetric line graph.
Indeed, arbitrary signings of
L(G)
generally violate the local compatibility
conditions imposed by shared
2
–paths in the base graph.

4.2
Rigidity of the ALG Signing

The signing of
\mathcal{A}(G)
is rigid in a strong sense.

Proposition 2

(Canonical signing)
.

For every finite simple graph
G
, the signed graph
\mathcal{A}(G)
is uniquely determined up to switching.

Proof.

The symmetric lift
{\mathrm{HL}}^{\prime}_{2}(G)
admits an involution

\sigma:(u,v)\mapsto(v,u)
, yielding an orthogonal decomposition of its
adjacency operator into symmetric and antisymmetric sectors,

\mathbb{R}^{E(G)}=V^{+}\oplus V^{-}.

The adjacency operator of
{\mathrm{HL}}^{\prime}_{2}(G)
is canonical, and its restriction to
V^{-}
defines the signed adjacency operator of
\mathcal{A}(G)
.
A change of reference orientation of the edges of
G
corresponds to conjugation by a diagonal
\{\pm 1\}
-matrix on
V^{-}
, which is precisely switching in the sense of signed graph theory. Hence
\mathcal{A}(G)
is uniquely determined up to switching.
∎

This rigidity sharply contrasts with the general theory of signed graphs, where a
single underlying graph may admit exponentially many non–switching–equivalent
signings.
For antisymmetric line graphs, the signs are not free parameters: they are forced
by the local incidence geometry of
G
.

From a signed–graph perspective, antisymmetric line graphs should therefore be
viewed not as arbitrary signings of line graphs, but as a canonical lift of the
line graph construction itself.

4.3
Forbidden Structure and Necessary Conditions

Classical results characterize which graphs arise as line graphs via forbidden
induced subgraphs
[
5
,
16
]
.
Since the underlying unsigned graph of
\mathcal{A}(G)
is always a line graph,
any signed graph arising as an antisymmetric line graph must, in particular,
satisfy all of these classical constraints.

Beyond the unsigned structure, the signing imposes additional restrictions.
In particular:

•

the sign of any triangle in
\mathcal{A}(G)
is determined by whether the
corresponding triple of edges in
G
forms a cyclic or branching configuration;

•

signed cycles must be compatible across overlapping
2
–paths;

•

switching equivalence cannot eliminate signed obstructions arising from
odd cycles in
G
.

These conditions rule out the vast majority of signed graphs, even among those
supported on line graphs.

4.4
Partial characterization via forbidden signed induced subgraphs

A classical theorem of Beineke characterizes line graphs by a list of nine forbidden

unsigned
induced subgraphs
[
5
]
. Because the underlying unsigned graph
of
\mathcal{A}(G)
is
L(G)
, any signed graph in the antisymmetric line graph family must,
at a minimum, have underlying graph avoiding these nine structures.

However, the canonical signing of
\mathcal{A}(G)
imposes additional geometric constraints.
In particular, the cycle-parity phenomenon established in Theorem
5

forces an infinite family of forbidden
signed
induced subgraphs.

Theorem 2

(Forbidden signed induced cycles)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a signed graph. If
\Sigma
is switching-equivalent to
\mathcal{A}(G)
for
some finite simple graph
G
, then for any
k\geq 4
, every induced
k
-cycle in
\Sigma

has sign
(-1)^{k}
.

Equivalently, no
\mathcal{A}(G)
(and hence no switching of it) contains, as an induced
signed subgraph,

1.

an induced
negative
cycle of even length
k\geq 4
, or

2.

an induced
positive
cycle of odd length
k\geq 5
.

Proof.

Let
C
be an induced
k
-cycle in
\Sigma
, with
k\geq 4
. Switching preserves the sign
of every cycle, so we may assume
\Sigma=\mathcal{A}(G)
. Then the underlying graph

|\Sigma|
is
L(G)
, and
C
corresponds to an induced
k
-cycle in
L(G)
with vertex set

\{e_{1},\dots,e_{k}\}
, where each
e_{i}
is an edge of
G
and
e_{i}
is adjacent in
L(G)
to

e_{i-1}
and
e_{i+1}
(indices modulo
k
).

Since
C
is induced,
e_{i-1}
is
not
adjacent to
e_{i+1}
in
L(G)
, hence the
edges
e_{i-1}
and
e_{i+1}
do not share a vertex in
G
. In particular,
e_{i}
cannot
meet both
e_{i-1}
and
e_{i+1}
at the same endpoint (otherwise
e_{i-1}
and
e_{i+1}

would share that endpoint as well). Therefore
e_{i}
meets
e_{i-1}
at one endpoint and
meets
e_{i+1}
at its other endpoint.

Define
v_{i}:=e_{i}\cap e_{i+1}
for
i=1,\dots,k
(indices modulo
k
). The preceding
paragraph implies that each
e_{i}
has endpoints
v_{i-1}
and
v_{i}
, so
e_{i}=v_{i-1}v_{i}
.
Thus the edges
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
form a simple
k
-cycle

v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{k}v_{1}

in
G
. The corresponding cycle on vertices
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
in
\mathcal{A}(G)
is exactly
the canonical lift of this
k
-cycle, and by Theorem
5
its sign is

(-1)^{k}
. Hence the induced cycle
C
has sign
(-1)^{k}
, as claimed.
∎

Remark 2
.

The restriction to
k\geq 4
is necessary. An induced
3
-cycle in
L(G)
can arise either
from a triangle in
G
or from a
3
-star in
G
. These yield (in general) opposite signs in

\mathcal{A}(G)
, cf. Theorem
5
and
Theorem
4
. Thus both positive and negative induced triangles
can occur in antisymmetric line graphs.

4.5
Resolution of Line-Graph Ambiguities

The rigidity of antisymmetric line graphs has an important consequence: it
eliminates the classical noninjectivity of the line graph construction.
Whitney’s exceptional pair
\{K_{3},K_{1,3}\}
arises because both graphs yield the
same underlying line graph.
However, their antisymmetric line graphs are distinguished by signed triangle
structure.

Excluding the Whitney pair.

It remains to rule out the exceptional possibility

\{G,H\}=\{K_{3},K_{1,3}\}
.
Although

5
The Signed Triangle Imbalance Invariant

One of the principal advantages of the antisymmetric line graph is that it
supports canonical signed-graph invariants that have no analogue at the level of
the ordinary line graph.

In this section we introduce the simplest such invariant, the
signed
triangle imbalance
, and explain its combinatorial meaning.

5.1
Definition of the Triangle Imbalance

Let
\mathcal{A}(G)
be the antisymmetric line graph of a graph
G
.
Since the underlying unsigned graph of
\mathcal{A}(G)
is the line graph

L(G)
, its triangles correspond to triples of edges in
G
that are pairwise
adjacent.
Each such triangle carries a sign given by the product of the signs of its three
edges in
\mathcal{A}(G)
.

Definition 3

(Signed triangle imbalance)
.

Let
t^{+}
and
t^{-}
denote the number of positive and negative triangles in

\mathcal{A}(G)
, respectively.
The
signed triangle imbalance
of
G
is defined by

\Delta_{3}(G)\;:=\;t^{+}-t^{-}.

Since switching operations preserve the sign of every cycle,
\Delta_{3}(G)
is
well-defined on the switching class of
\mathcal{A}(G)
and therefore defines a
canonical invariant of the base graph
G
.

5.2
Combinatorial interpretation

Triangles in the line graph correspond to triples of edges in
G
that are
pairwise adjacent, hence to one of two configurations: either the three edges
are incident to a common vertex (a
3
–edge star), or they form a triangle in

G
.

Theorem 4

(Tripods versus triangles)
.

Let
G
be a finite simple graph. Let
\Delta_{3}(G)=t^{+}-t^{-}
be the signed
triangle imbalance of
\mathcal{A}(G)
.
Then every triangle of
\mathcal{A}(G)
is positive if and only if it arises from
three edges incident to a common vertex of
G
, and every triangle of

\mathcal{A}(G)
is negative if and only if it arises from a
3
–cycle in
G
.
Consequently,

t^{+}(G)=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\binom{d(v)}{3},\qquad t^{-}(G)=\#\triangle(G),

and hence

\Delta_{3}(G)=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\binom{d(v)}{3}\;-\;\#\triangle(G).

(2)

In particular, the total number of triangles in the line graph satisfies

T(G)=t^{+}(G)+t^{-}(G)=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\binom{d(v)}{3}\;+\;\#\triangle(G).

Proof.

A triangle in
L(G)
corresponds to three edges of
G
that are pairwise adjacent.
Such a triple either (i) is incident to a common vertex
v
(contributing

\binom{d(v)}{3}
choices at each
v
), or (ii) forms a
3
–cycle in
G

(contributing
\#\triangle(G)
choices); these are the only possibilities.

For case (i), after switching if necessary, we may assume the common vertex
v

is a tail (or head) for all three edges, so each adjacent pair has the same
incidence at
v
, giving sign
+1
on each of the three edges of the triangle in

\mathcal{A}(G)
and hence a positive triangle.
For case (ii), the sign of a triangle in
\mathcal{A}(G)
is switching-invariant,
so it suffices to compute it for a convenient reference orientation.
Orient the
3
–cycle cyclically.
At each vertex of the cycle, the two incident edges have opposite incidence
(one enters and one leaves), hence each of the three adjacencies in the induced
triangle of
\mathcal{A}(G)
has sign
-1
, and the product of the three signs is

(-1)^{3}=-1
. Therefore every triangle arising from a
3
–cycle in
G
is negative.

The stated formulas for
t^{+}
,
t^{-}
,
\Delta_{3}
, and
T
follow immediately.
∎

Remark 3
.

Equation (
2
) shows that
\Delta_{3}(G)
depends only
on the degree sequence of
G
and the number of triangles in
G
. Thus
\Delta_{3}

is a genuine signed invariant, but it is intentionally low-order and
cannot be expected to separate graphs that already agree on these basic counts.

Theorem 5

(Cycle parity in
\mathcal{A}(G)
)
.

Let
C\subseteq G
be a simple cycle of length
k\geq 3
.
Then the corresponding
k
-cycle in the line graph
L(G)

has sign
(-1)^{k}
in
\mathcal{A}(G)
.
In particular, odd cycles in
G
lift to negative cycles
in
\mathcal{A}(G)
, and even cycles lift to positive cycles.

Proof.

Write
C=v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{k}v_{1}
and let
e_{i}=v_{i}v_{i+1}
(indices mod
k
) be its edges.
These
k
edges are pairwise distinct, and consecutive edges share exactly one endpoint,
so
e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{k}
form a simple
k
–cycle in the line graph
L(G)
.

The sign of a cycle is switching-invariant, so it suffices to compute it for a
convenient reference orientation of the edges of
C
.
Orient
C
cyclically. At each vertex of
C
, the two incident cycle edges have
opposite incidence (one enters and one leaves), hence each of the
k
adjacencies
along the induced
k
–cycle in
\mathcal{A}(G)
has sign
-1
.
Therefore the cycle sign is
(-1)^{k}
.
∎

Corollary 1

(Balance and bipartiteness)
.

\mathcal{A}(G)
is balanced if and only if
G
is bipartite.

Proof.

Suppose
G
is bipartite with parts
A\sqcup B
.
Orient every edge of
G
from
A
to
B
.
If two edges share a vertex
v\in A
then both
leave

v
, and if they share
a vertex
v\in B
then both
enter

v
.
Thus every adjacency in
\mathcal{A}(G)
has sign
+1
in this reference orientation,
so
\mathcal{A}(G)
is (trivially) balanced.

Conversely, if
G
contains an odd cycle of length
k
,
then the corresponding cycle in
\mathcal{A}(G)

has sign
(-1)^{k}=-1
by Theorem
5
.
Hence
\mathcal{A}(G)
contains a negative cycle and is therefore unbalanced.
∎

Proposition 3

(Bipartite collapse)
.

If
G
is bipartite, then
\mathcal{A}(G)
is switching-equivalent to the ordinary line graph
L(G)
with all edges positive. In particular, every signed triangle in
\mathcal{A}(G)
is positive, so

\Delta_{3}(G)=t^{+}(G)=\#\triangle(L(G))=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\binom{d(v)}{3}.

Proof.

Let
G
be bipartite with parts
A\sqcup B
and orient every edge from
A
to
B
.
If two edges of
G
share a vertex
v\in A
then both
leave

v
, and if they share a
vertex
v\in B
then both
enter

v
.
Hence every adjacency in
\mathcal{A}(G)
has sign

\mathcal{A}(G)
is switching-equivalent to the all-positive signing of

In particular, every triangle in

For bipartite

5.3
Normalization and Comparison with Classical Invariants

To compare graphs of different sizes, it is often useful to consider a normalized
form of the triangle imbalance.
Let
T(G)
denote the total number of triangles in
L(G)
.
We define the normalized imbalance

\tau_{3}(G)\;:=\;\frac{\Delta_{3}(G)}{T(G)},

whenever
T(G)>0
.

The invariant
\tau_{3}(G)
should be contrasted with classical graph invariants
such as triangle density, clustering coefficient, or spectral gap

[
9
,
16
]
.
While these invariants capture global density or expansion properties,

\tau_{3}(G)
captures a purely local signed coherence phenomenon that is invisible
to unsigned constructions.

From the perspective of signed graph theory,
\Delta_{3}(G)
may also be viewed as
a low-order instance of more general cycle-balance statistics studied in the
theory of signed graphs and gain graphs
[
18
,
19
]
.
The novelty here lies in the fact that the signing is canonical and derived from
an unsigned graph, rather than imposed externally.

Because of this, any invariant of signed graphs that is stable under switching gives rise to an invariant of ordinary graphs when applied to
\mathcal{A}(G)
.
The triangle imbalance provides the simplest nontrivial example of this
principle. The resulting invariant is closely related in spirit to classical notions of frustration and imbalance in signed graphs.

\Delta_{3}(G)
is not intended as a universal descriptor of graph
structure, nor does it subsume classical notions such as expansion or
Hamiltonicity.
Rather, it exposes a layer of local structural information—namely, the signed
coherence of edge adjacencies—that is erased by the ordinary line graph.
As such, it illustrates the broader potential of antisymmetric line graphs as a
bridge between graph theory and signed graph invariants.

6.3
Interpretation and scope

Several qualitative regimes are immediately visible.

•

Graphs with no triangles in
L(G)
(trees, paths, grids, cycles) satisfy

T=0
, hence
\Delta_{3}=0
identically.
This reflects the absence of competing local edge interactions.

•

Dense or highly redundant graphs exhibit large

and nontrivial

\Delta_{3}
, with
\tau_{3}<1
measuring the degree of signed imbalance among
2–path configurations.

•

Complete graphs and unions of cliques sit at an extreme end of this signed refinement:
their antisymmetric line graphs exhibit highly constrained signed structure and large deviation
from tree–like behavior, as reflected by large trace statistics and nontrivial
\tau_{3}
.

6.4
Switching-invariant signed statistics

We briefly record several standard examples.
Let
(\Gamma,\sigma)
be a signed graph and let
S
denote its signed adjacency
matrix.

•

Balance and cycle-balance profiles.

A signed graph is balanced if every cycle has positive sign.
More generally, one may record the fraction of positive cycles of length
\ell
,
or the imbalance statistics for fixed
\ell
(such as the triangle imbalance
studied here).
These quantities are switching-invariant because switching preserves the sign of
every cycle
[
18
,
19
]
.

•

Frustration index (edge deletion to balance).

The frustration index (also called the line index of balance) is the minimum
number of edges whose deletion makes the signed graph balanced.
Equivalently, it is the minimum number of “disagreeing” edges in a
two-clustering interpretation.
This invariant has been widely studied and is known to be NP-hard in general,
with a substantial optimization literature
[
18
]
.

•

Signed spectral statistics.

The spectrum of the signed adjacency matrix
S
is invariant under switching,
since switching corresponds to conjugation by a diagonal
\{\pm 1\}
-matrix.
Signed spectral bounds interact with balance and frustration and have been used
as structural and algorithmic proxies
[
7
]
.

Applying these to
\Gamma=\mathcal{A}(G)
yields invariants of
G
.
For example, the frustration index of
\mathcal{A}(G)
becomes an invariant that
measures the minimum signed “tension” present in the local edge-incidence
structure of
G
.

This import of signed invariants is not merely a formal rebranding.
For an arbitrary graph
\Gamma
, a signing
\sigma
is typically an external
choice and the resulting signed invariants are model-dependent.
In contrast, the ALG signing is forced by incidence geometry and is unique up to
switching. Thus the induced signed invariants are
intrinsic
invariants of the base graph.

This canonical interface provides a broad research direction: one can ask, for a
given signed-graph invariant
\mathfrak{I}
, what classical properties of
G

control
\mathfrak{I}(\mathcal{A}(G))
?
The triangle imbalance
\Delta_{3}(G)
developed above is the first instance of
this principle.
It is low-order, exactly computable by trace identities, and already detects a
layer of structure that is invisible to the ordinary line graph.

Among the remaining non-bipartite graphs, there are

For this non-bipartite sample:

There also exist non-isomorphic

7
Frustration index, Max-Cut defect, and cubic exactness

A central switching-invariant of a signed graph
\Sigma
is its
frustration index

\ell(\Sigma)
, defined as the minimum number of edges whose deletion makes
\Sigma

balanced. Equivalently, by Harary’s switching characterization, it is the minimum possible
number of negative edges among all switchings of
\Sigma

We refer to
[
18
,
2
,
3
]
for background, algorithmic
formulations, and computational aspects.

Applied to
\Sigma=\mathcal{A}(G)
, the quantity
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
becomes an integer invariant of
G
measuring how far the antisymmetric line graph is from being switching-equivalent to the all-positive line graph. Since
\mathcal{A}(G)
is balanced if and only if
G
is bipartite (Corollary
1
),
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
may be viewed as a “distance from bipartiteness” measured through the signed incidence refinement.

Lemma 2

(Orientation characterization)
.

For any finite simple graph
G
, one has

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))\;=\;\min_{\vec{G}}\;\sum_{v\in V(G)}d^{+}_{\vec{G}}(v)\,d^{-}_{\vec{G}}(v),

where the minimum is taken over all reference orientations
\vec{G}
of
G
.

Proof.

Fix an orientation
\vec{G}
of
G
. An edge of
\mathcal{A}(G)
corresponds to a pair of
adjacent edges
e,f
of
G
sharing a unique vertex
v
. By the defining sign rule for

\mathcal{A}(G)
, this signed edge is negative if and only if
e
and
f
have opposite
incidence at
v
(one enters
v
and the other leaves). The number of such pairs at
v

is exactly
d^{+}_{\vec{G}}(v)d^{-}_{\vec{G}}(v)
, and summing over
v
counts each signed adjacency
once.

Switching
\mathcal{A}(G)
corresponds to reorienting individual edges of
G

(Proposition
1
), so minimizing the number of negative edges over the
switching class is equivalent to minimizing over orientations of
G
.
∎

It is natural to compare
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
with the
Max-Cut defect

\mathrm{def}(G):=|E(G)|-\mathrm{MaxCut}(G),

which is the minimum number of edges whose removal makes
G
bipartite (equivalently,
the minimum number of edges violating a maximum bipartition).

Theorem 6

(Lower bound via Max-Cut defect)
.

For any finite simple graph
G
, one has

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))\;\geq\;\mathrm{def}(G).

Proof.

Let
\vec{G}
be an orientation achieving Lemma
2
, so that

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))=\sum_{v}d^{+}(v)d^{-}(v)
. For each vertex
v
, set

m(v):=\min(d^{+}(v),d^{-}(v))
. Since
d^{+}(v),d^{-}(v)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, one has

m(v)\leq d^{+}(v)d^{-}(v)
for all
v
.

Form a subgraph
H\subseteq G
by deleting, for each vertex
v
, all edges incident to
v

that are oriented in the minority direction at
v
. Let
F
be the union of deleted edges.
Then each edge of
F
is charged to at least one endpoint at which it lies in the minority
direction. Each deleted edge is in the minority direction at at least one of its endpoints, so it is counted at least once
in
\sum_{v}m(v)
(and possibly twice), hence
|F|\leq\sum_{v}m(v)
.

|F|\leq\sum_{v}m(v)\leq\sum_{v}d^{+}(v)d^{-}(v)=\ell(\mathcal{A}(G)).

In the remaining oriented graph
H=G\setminus F
, every vertex is either a source or a sink,
so
H
is bipartite. Therefore
\mathrm{def}(G)\leq|F|\leq\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
.

∎

The lower bound shows that
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
dominates the classical defect.
One may also bound
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
from above in terms of a maximum cut and the degrees
of its defect edges.

Definition 4

(Vertex frustration number)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a signed graph. Its
vertex frustration number
(also called the

frustration number
) is

vf(\Sigma)\;:=\;\min\{|S|:S\subseteq V(\Sigma)\ \text{and}\ \Sigma-S\ \text{is balanced}\}.

Theorem 7

(Vertex frustration equals Max-Cut defect)
.

For any finite simple graph
G
, one has

vf(\mathcal{A}(G))\;=\;\mathrm{def}(G).

Proof.

A vertex of
\mathcal{A}(G)
is an edge of
G
. Thus, for any set

S\subseteq V(\mathcal{A}(G))=E(G)
, deleting
S
from
\mathcal{A}(G)
simply removes those
edges of
G
from the construction, and one has an induced signed-graph identity

\mathcal{A}(G)-S\;\cong\;\mathcal{A}(G\setminus S).

(6)

By Corollary
1
,
\mathcal{A}(H)
is balanced if and only if

H
is bipartite. Therefore, by (
6
),

\mathcal{A}(G)-S\ \text{is balanced}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{A}(G\setminus S)\ \text{is balanced}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G\setminus S\ \text{is bipartite}.

Consequently,

vf(\mathcal{A}(G))=\min\{|S|:S\subseteq E(G)\ \text{and}\ G\setminus S\ \text{is bipartite}\}.

The right-hand side is exactly the Max-Cut defect
\mathrm{def}(G)
, since

\mathrm{def}(G)=|E(G)|-\mathrm{MaxCut}(G)
equals the minimum number of edges that must be
deleted to make
G
bipartite. Hence
vf(\mathcal{A}(G))=\mathrm{def}(G)
.
∎

Theorem 8

(Upper bound via defect amplification)
.

Let
V(G)=A\sqcup B
be a maximum cut, and let
E_{\mathrm{def}}
be the set of defect edges
(those with both endpoints in
A
or both in
B
), so that
|E_{\mathrm{def}}|=\mathrm{def}(G)
.
Then

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))\;\leq\;\sum_{uv\in E_{\mathrm{def}}}\min(d(u)-1,d(v)-1).

In particular,

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))\leq(\Delta(G)-1)\,\mathrm{def}(G).

Proof.

Orient all cut edges from
A
to
B
. For each defect edge
uv\subseteq A
, orient it toward
the endpoint of smaller degree; for each defect edge
uv\subseteq B
, orient it away from the
endpoint of smaller degree. Let
k^{-}(v)
denote the number of defect edges entering
v\in A
.
Then
d^{-}(v)=k^{-}(v)
and
d^{+}(v)=d(v)-k^{-}(v)
, so

d^{+}(v)d^{-}(v)=k^{-}(v)\bigl(d(v)-k^{-}(v)\bigr)\leq k^{-}(v)\,(d(v)-1).

Summing over
v\in A
, each defect edge
uv\subseteq A
oriented as
u\to v
contributes
exactly
1
to
k^{-}(v)
and
0
to
k^{-}(u)
, hence the total contribution from
A
is at most

\sum_{uv\subseteq A}\min(d(u)-1,d(v)-1)
. The argument for
B
is symmetric (using outgoing
defect edges). Summing both sides yields the claimed bound, and the final inequality follows
from
\min(d(u)-1,d(v)-1)\leq\Delta(G)-1
.
∎

The sandwich

\mathrm{def}(G)\ \leq\ \ell(\mathcal{A}(G))\ \leq\ (\Delta(G)-1)\,\mathrm{def}(G)

shows that, for graphs of bounded maximum degree,
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
and the Max-Cut
defect are within a constant factor of one another. In particular, this ties the frustration
index of
\mathcal{A}(G)
to a classical NP-hard optimization objective.

A stronger statement holds in the cubic case: the factor-
\Delta(G)-1
slack collapses,
and
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
becomes
exactly
twice the odd cycle transversal number.

Theorem 9

(Exact frustration on cubic graphs)
.

Let
G
be a cubic (3-regular) graph, and let
\mathrm{oct}(G)
denote the
odd cycle
transversal number
of
G
, i.e. the minimum size of a set
S\subseteq V(G)
such that the
induced subgraph
G[V(G)\setminus S]
is bipartite. Then

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))\;=\;2\,\mathrm{oct}(G).

Proof.

By Lemma
2
,

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))=\min_{\vec{G}}\ \sum_{v\in V(G)}d^{+}(v)\,d^{-}(v).

Assume
G
is cubic. Then
d^{+}(v)+d^{-}(v)=3
for all
v
, hence

d^{+}(v)d^{-}(v)\in\{0,2\},

with
d^{+}(v)d^{-}(v)=0
if and only if
v
is a pure source or pure sink in
\vec{G}
, and

d^{+}(v)d^{-}(v)=2
otherwise. Let

\mathrm{Mix}(\vec{G}):=\{v\in V(G):0<d^{+}(v)<3\}

be the set of mixed vertices. Then for any orientation
\vec{G}
,

\sum_{v\in V(G)}d^{+}(v)\,d^{-}(v)=2\,|\mathrm{Mix}(\vec{G})|,

so

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))=2\cdot\min_{\vec{G}}|\mathrm{Mix}(\vec{G})|.

(7)

Fix an orientation
\vec{G}
and set
S:=\mathrm{Mix}(\vec{G})
and
V_{0}:=V(G)\setminus S
.
Partition
V_{0}=A\sqcup B
where
A
consists of pure sources and
B
of pure sinks.
There is no edge inside
A
(an edge between two sources would force one endpoint to have
an incoming edge) and similarly no edge inside
B
. Hence
G[V_{0}]
is bipartite, so
S
is
an odd cycle transversal. Thus
|S|\geq\mathrm{oct}(G)
, and (
7
) yields

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))\geq 2\,\mathrm{oct}(G)
.

Conversely, let
S\subseteq V(G)
be an odd cycle transversal of size
\mathrm{oct}(G)
and
write
V_{0}:=V(G)\setminus S
. Choose a bipartition
V_{0}=A\sqcup B
of the induced subgraph

G[V_{0}]
. Define an orientation
\vec{G}
of all edges of
G
by orienting every edge
incident to a vertex of
A
away from that vertex, and every edge incident to a vertex of

B
towards that vertex; orient edges with both endpoints in
S
arbitrarily. This is
well-defined: if
uv
has
u\in A
then we set
u\to v
, and if
uv
has
u\in B
then we
set
v\to u
, and for edges
uv
with
u\in A
,
v\in B
the two prescriptions agree.

With this orientation, every vertex in
A
is a pure source and every vertex in
B
is a
pure sink, hence
\mathrm{Mix}(\vec{G})\subseteq S
and
|\mathrm{Mix}(\vec{G})|\leq|S|
.
Applying (
7
) gives
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))\leq 2|S|=2\,\mathrm{oct}(G)
.
Combining the two inequalities proves the theorem.
∎

Proposition 4

(Equivalence mechanism on cubic graphs)
.

Let
G
be a cubic graph. For an orientation
\vec{G}
of
G
, define

\mathrm{Mix}(\vec{G})\ :=\ \{v\in V(G):0<d^{+}(v)<3\},

the set of
mixed
vertices (equivalently, vertices with both an incoming and an outgoing edge).

1.

For every orientation
\vec{G}
of
G
, the set
S=\mathrm{Mix}(\vec{G})
is an odd cycle transversal of
G
; i.e.
G[V(G)\setminus S]
is bipartite.

2.

Conversely, for every odd cycle transversal
S\subseteq V(G)
and every bipartition

V(G)\setminus S=A\sqcup B
of the bipartite graph
G[V(G)\setminus S]
, there exists an orientation
\vec{G}

such that every vertex of
A
is a pure source, every vertex of
B
is a pure sink, and

\mathrm{Mix}(\vec{G})\ \subseteq\ S.

Consequently,

\min_{\vec{G}}|\mathrm{Mix}(\vec{G})|\ =\ \mathrm{oct}(G),

and, using the cubic specialization of Lemma
2
,

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))\ =\ 2\,\mathrm{oct}(G).

Proof.

This is exactly the two-way construction used in the proof of Theorem
9
:
odd cycles in a cubic graph force a mixed vertex under any orientation, while given a bipartition of
G\setminus S
one orients all edges away from
A
and into
B
, ensuring that only vertices in
S
can be mixed. Edges of
G[S]
may be oriented arbitrarily: this can only affect whether vertices of
S
are mixed or pure,
but cannot create mixed vertices outside
S
. Thus
\mathrm{Mix}(\vec{G})\subseteq S
still holds.
∎

Corollary 2

(FPT transfer on cubic graphs)
.

On cubic graphs, computing
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
is fixed-parameter tractable when parameterized by

k=\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
(equivalently by
k/2=\mathrm{oct}(G)
),
since Odd Cycle Transversal is fixed-parameter tractable
[
15
]
.

Corollary 3

(Kernelization transfer (randomized))
.

On cubic graphs, computing
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
admits a randomized polynomial kernelization
parameterized by
k=\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
, via the equivalence
k/2=\mathrm{oct}(G)

and the randomized polynomial kernel for Odd Cycle Transversal
of Kratsch–Wahlström
[
12
]
.

Corollary 4

(Approximation transfer)
.

Any polynomial-time
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for Odd Cycle Transversal on cubic graphs yields,
in polynomial time, an
\alpha
-approximation for
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
on cubic graphs via

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))=2\,\mathrm{oct}(G)
.

Corollary 5

(NP-hardness)
.

Computing
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
is NP-hard, even when the input graph
G
is restricted to be cubic.

Proof.

Odd Cycle Transversal (Graph Bipartization) is NP-hard and remains NP-complete on graphs of
maximum degree
3

[
8
]
. Theorem
9
yields a
polynomial-time many-one reduction from
\mathrm{oct}(G)
to
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
on cubic inputs.
∎

The results of this section show that the antisymmetric line graph does not introduce a parameter foreign to classical graph theory. Rather, its switching-invariant frustration measures recover, in natural graph classes, fundamental bipartiteness parameters such as the Max-Cut defect and the odd cycle transversal number. In particular, on cubic graphs the edge-frustration index
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
coincides exactly with
2\,\mathrm{oct}(G)
, so that a canonical signed invariant encodes a central vertex-deletion parameter of graph theory. In this sense, signed graph theory here does not extend ordinary graph theory away from its classical core, but instead provides an alternative structural encoding of it.

Empirical comparison.

For computation, on a large sample of connected non-bipartite graphs from the NetworkX atlas
with at most seven vertices and with
|E(G)|\leq 12
(to allow exact computation under a
per-instance cutoff), we computed
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
and
\mathrm{def}(G)
for
657
graphs
(with
24
timeouts). The Pearson correlation between
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
and
\mathrm{def}(G)

was
0.852
, indicating a strong relationship. Here
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
was computed by
exhaustive switching search on
\mathcal{A}(G)
(fixing one vertex to reduce to

2^{|V(\mathcal{A}(G))|-1}
switchings), and the
24
timeouts refer to instances where this
exact computation exceeded the per-graph cutoff.

However,
\mathrm{def}(G)
does not determine
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
.
For instance, within the sample we observed that graphs with
\mathrm{def}(G)=3
can have

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
ranging from
3
up to
10
, and similarly
\mathrm{def}(G)=2
allows

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))\in\{2,3,4,5,6,7\}
.
As an explicit witness, the following two non-isomorphic graphs on
7
vertices with
9
edges
satisfy
\mathrm{def}(G)=\mathrm{def}(H)=2
but have different frustration indices:

\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))=2,\qquad\ell(\mathcal{A}(H))=4,

where

E(G)=\{01,02,12,14,23,34,35,36,56\},\qquad E(H)=\{01,02,03,04,05,12,13,23,56\}.

Thus
\ell(\mathcal{A}(G))
provides a refinement of the classical Max-Cut defect, capturing
additional incidence-level obstruction beyond vertex bipartition alone.

8
Discussion and outlook

We have introduced the antisymmetric line graph
\mathcal{A}(G)
as a canonical
signed refinement of the classical line graph.
The construction is intrinsic: it may be given purely in terms of incidence
matrices and is independent of auxiliary choices up to switching equivalence.
Its underlying unsigned graph is always
L(G)
, yet the signed structure carries
strictly more information.
In particular, the switching class of
\mathcal{A}(G)
determines
G
up to
isomorphism (modulo isolated vertices), thereby resolving the classical
noninjectivity of the line graph construction governed by Whitney’s theorem

[
17
]
.

Beyond reconstruction,
\mathcal{A}(G)
exposes new invariants of
ordinary graphs.
The signed triangle imbalance
\Delta_{3}(G)
and its normalized form
\tau_{3}(G)

illustrate how local edge-incidence geometry can be quantified by switching
invariant signed statistics.
More broadly, any switching-invariant notion from signed graph theory (balance profiles, frustration measures, signed spectral statistics) becomes an invariant of
G
when applied to
\mathcal{A}(G)
.
This provides a principled bridge between classical graph theory and the rich
theory of signed graphs
[
18
,
19
]
.

Several directions appear especially natural.

(1) Characterization and recognition.

Which signed graphs arise, up to switching, as antisymmetric line graphs?
Since the underlying unsigned graph must be a line graph, classical forbidden
subgraph characterizations (e.g. Beineke’s theorem) provide necessary
constraints
[
5
]
.
However, the signing imposes additional local compatibility conditions inherited
from shared
2
–paths.
Obtaining an intrinsic characterization within signed graph theory would locate
ALG as a distinguished family of “geometric” signed graphs.

(2) Higher-order cycle statistics.

The triangle imbalance
\Delta_{3}(G)
is the first nontrivial cycle statistic,
admitting exact trace formulas.
It is natural to define analogous invariants
\Delta_{\ell}(G)
from signed

\ell
–cycles in
\mathcal{A}(G)
, and to study which of these contain new
information beyond
L(G)
.
Understanding which cycle lengths produce genuinely independent invariants is an
appealing structural problem within signed refinements.

(3) Optimization and algorithmic proxies.

Signed invariants such as frustration index have deep connections to
combinatorial optimization and 2-clustering
[
1
]
.
It would be particularly interesting to identify settings where optimizing over

\mathcal{A}(G)
yields new algorithmic insight or fast proxies for structural
properties not visible to
L(G)
.

In summary, the antisymmetric line graph is not merely a byproduct of lift
theory.
It is a minimal, rigidity-enforcing refinement of the line graph construction,
and it provides an entry point for importing signed graph invariants
into ordinary graph theory.
We expect
\mathcal{A}(G)
to merit further study as a foundational object in its
own right.
