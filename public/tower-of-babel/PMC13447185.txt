The Two-Minute Step Test in Heart Failure: A Practical Step Forward, Pending Validation
Authors: Dong Hoon Lee
Published: 2026

Exercise intolerance is among the most defining and prognostically important features of chronic heart failure (HF).1) Reduced functional capacity is not merely a marker of symptom burden; it predicts hospitalization and death and reflects the multisystem character of the syndrome, encompassing impaired cardiac reserve alongside pulmonary, vascular, and skeletal muscle abnormalities. Measuring it accurately and repeatably is therefore central to risk stratification and to monitoring the response to cardiac rehabilitation. Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) with peak oxygen uptake remains the reference standard, yet its equipment, cost, and space requirements place it beyond reach in most routine settings.2)

For this reason, the six-minute walk test (6MWT)3) has become the most widely used field measure of functional capacity in HF, with well-established prognostic value in this population.4) The 6MWT nonetheless has an important practical constraint: it requires a long, unobstructed corridor that many outpatient clinics, community programs, and home-based services do not have. This limitation has renewed interest in step-based tests requiring minimal space and equipment. The two-minute step test (2MST), originally developed for community-dwelling older adults,5) is among the most extensively studied of these. Its reliability and construct validity have been reported across diverse populations,6) yet its interpretation in HF remains constrained by the absence of a disease-specific threshold,7) a gap that the present study sets out to address.

In this issue of the International Journal of Heart Failure, the authors8) report a cross-sectional study of 60 clinically stable patients with chronic HF and reduced ejection fraction, each of whom completed the 2MST, the 6MWT, and quadriceps strength testing in randomized order. Using receiver operating characteristic analysis against a six-minute walk distance of 350 m, they identified a cut-off of 94 steps that discriminated reduced functional capacity with good accuracy (area under the curve, 0.82) and high sensitivity (83%). After adjustment for age, sex, and New York Heart Association (NYHA) class, patients meeting the threshold walked approximately 34 m farther, a difference exceeding commonly reported minimal clinically important differences, and reported lower post-exercise leg fatigue. Cardiorespiratory and perceptual responses were otherwise comparable to the 6MWT, differing chiefly in greater localized leg fatigue.

These findings are encouraging but warrant the caution the authors themselves advocate. This is a single-center study of modest size in a relatively young, clinically stable cohort largely in NYHA class I–II. How the 94-step threshold performs in older patients, in the underrepresented women of the sample, or in more advanced disease remains unknown. Critically, the cut-off was both derived and evaluated within the same cohort, so its apparent performance is likely optimistic, and external validation in independent samples is indispensable before the threshold can be applied clinically. The moderate specificity (64%) implies a non-trivial false-positive rate if the test is used beyond initial screening. The cross-sectional design precludes inference regarding responsiveness to training or prognostic value, and the absence of CPET means the 2MST has not been anchored to a gold-standard physiological measure. Near-universal beta-blocker use may also have attenuated the heart-rate responses on which submaximal comparisons partly depend.

None of these caveats diminishes the study’s practical value. A test requiring minimal floor space, no specialized equipment, and only two minutes to administer is well suited to community clinics, resource-limited settings, and home-based rehabilitation where corridor-based testing is not feasible. The independent association with walking distance beyond the minimal clinically important difference, together with the observation that step performance was not explained by maximal quadriceps strength alone, suggests that the 2MST reflects integrated cardiorespiratory and skeletal-muscle function.9) The task now is to advance the 94-step threshold from a promising single-cohort estimate to a validated tool: adequately powered, multicenter studies that externally validate the cut-off in older and more heterogeneous HF populations, including HF with preserved ejection fraction, anchor it against CPET-derived peak oxygen uptake, and follow patients longitudinally to determine whether it tracks rehabilitation response and predicts clinical outcomes.

For now, the 2MST is best regarded as a complementary screening measure rather than a replacement for established functional testing. The present study makes a valuable contribution: it is pragmatic, carefully conducted, and clinically motivated, though its proposed threshold remains provisional. Whether the 2MST earns a durable place in the functional assessment of HF will depend on the external validation and longitudinal studies that this work should now prompt.
