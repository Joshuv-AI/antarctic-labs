Gamma-ray attenuation and spectral–angular transport in transparent graded-Z multilayer shields
Authors: S. Alsalmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transparent radiation shields that combine photon attenuation with controlled secondary emission are relevant to medical imaging, nuclear medicine, and radiation-monitoring systems operating in the 0.06–1.33 MeV energy range. In this study, a graded-Z multilayer composed of bismuth-rich borosilicate (BBS), lutetium aluminium garnet (LuAG), and silica (SiO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}) is evaluated using Geant4 Monte Carlo simulations and benchmarked against commercial RS-253 glass at matched areal density. The graded-Z structure exhibits higher mass attenuation coefficients and reduced half-value layers across the investigated energies. Comparison with representative Bi\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}-based glass systems from the literature indicates that the graded-Z design provides enhanced attenuation beyond composition-based optimization. Spectral analysis reveals energy-dependent fluorescence associated with the staggered K-edges of Bi and Lu, while angular scoring indicates enhanced forward-directed transmission relative to RS-253 at higher energies. These results show that graded-Z shielding modifies photon transport both spectrally and directionally. However, the multilayer design also increases forward-directed energy transport at higher energies, highlighting a tradeoff between attenuation performance and directional energy leakage. This simulation-based study establishes a quantitative framework for spectral-angular evaluation of transparent graded-Z shielding architectures. Experimental validation remains an important direction for future work.

Introduction

Transparent radiation shielding is required in medical imaging, nuclear medicine, and radiation-monitoring environments where both visibility and protection from ionizing radiation are essential. Owing to toxicity concerns and increasing regulatory restrictions on lead-containing materials, lead-free heavy-metal-oxide glasses have emerged as promising alternatives for transparent shielding windows.

Incorporation of high-Z oxides such as BaO and Bi\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document} into borate, silicate, and germanate glass networks increases density and mass attenuation coefficient (MAC) while reducing half-value layer (HVL) and mean free path (MFP), particularly in the diagnostic energy range where photoelectric absorption dominates1–5. These systems can achieve attenuation performance comparable to conventional shielding glasses while retaining optical transparency and structural stability under irradiation. Such results demonstrate that compositional engineering is an effective strategy for enhancing bulk photon attenuation in transparent materials. Similar trends have been reported in other homogeneous systems, including doped ferrites and composite materials, where attenuation improvements are primarily achieved through increased density and incorporation of high-Z constituents6,7.

Beyond optimization of homogeneous glass compositions, the graded-Z concept provides an additional design strategy. In graded-Z architectures, layers of progressively decreasing atomic number are arranged to promote energy down-scattering and partial reabsorption of secondary radiation generated in the high-Z layers, thereby modifying the spectral and angular distribution of transmitted photons. This approach has been implemented in opaque metallic and composite systems, including space-radiation shielding8, high-energy astrophysical instrumentation9, and additively manufactured composite structures10. These studies show that multilayer configurations influence not only primary attenuation but also secondary particle production and transport.

Despite these advances, graded-Z shielding has been almost exclusively realized using opaque materials. Transparent multilayer implementations suitable for optical shielding windows have not been systematically investigated. Moreover, prior evaluations of transparent shielding materials largely focus on bulk attenuation metrics such as MAC, HVL, and MFP, with limited attention to secondary photon generation and the angular distribution of transmitted radiation. Because high-Z constituents can produce characteristic fluorescence and scattered photons that emerge directionally, spectral and angular transport effects may significantly influence effective shielding performance.

To address this gap, we investigate a graded-Z multilayer composed of bismuth-rich borosilicate glass (BBS), lutetium aluminium garnet (LuAG), and silica glass (SiO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}). Each material is optically transparent in the visible range: BBS forms clear amorphous networks within the Bi\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}–B\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}–SiO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} system11; LuAG (Lu\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}Al\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_5$$\end{document}O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{12}$$\end{document}) is a cubic garnet crystal widely used in optical and scintillation applications12; and silica exhibits intrinsically low visible absorption13. The multilayer configuration enables graded-Z functionality while preserving optical transparency.

Using Geant4 simulations validated against NIST XCOM data, the structure is benchmarked against commercial RS-253 shielding glass at matched areal density14. RS-253 was selected as a representative radiation-shielding glass commonly used in medical and nuclear laboratory viewing windows. Because it combines optical transparency with heavy-metal-oxide attenuation properties, it provides a practical and well-characterized benchmark for evaluating transparent shielding materials under matched areal-density conditions. RS-253 therefore serves as a realistic reference material for performance comparison. To further position the results within the current literature, comparisons with representative Bi\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}-based glass systems are also considered. In addition to conventional attenuation metrics, the analysis evaluates secondary photon energy spectra and angle-resolved transmitted fluence, establishing a quantitative spectral–angular framework for assessing transparent graded-Z shielding architectures.

Photon transport simulations were performed using the Geant4 toolkit (version 11.2.2)15, a Monte Carlo radiation transport code widely used for modeling particle-matter interactions. Electromagnetic processes were modeled using the G4EmStandardPhysics_option4 physics list, which provides accurate treatment of photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering, and Rayleigh scattering over the investigated energy range16. This physics list is optimized for high-accuracy electromagnetic simulations and has been extensively validated against experimental and reference data. The simulation tracks individual photon interactions and the production of secondary particles within the multilayer structure. A monoenergetic pencil beam was generated at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(-2.0~\textrm{cm},0,0)$$\end{document} and directed along +x, ensuring full interception of the stack. The use of a pencil beam provides a well-defined collimated photon geometry consistent with narrow-beam attenuation conditions used in the definition of MAC and HVL. In addition, it ensures that the observed angular distribution of transmitted photons arises solely from photon–matter interactions within the shield, rather than from geometric beam divergence. This allows for a physically meaningful evaluation of angular redistribution effects induced by the graded-Z structure. The investigated photon energies span the range 0.06–1.33 MeV, covering characteristic gamma-ray energies relevant to medical imaging, nuclear medicine, and radiation protection applications, including emissions from commonly used sources such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{241}$$\end{document}Am, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{57}$$\end{document}Co, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{131}$$\end{document}I, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{137}$$\end{document}Cs, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{60}$$\end{document}Co.

Transmitted radiation was recorded using a 0.1 mm-thick air-filled scoring volume (SV) of size 5× 5 cm\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document}, positioned 1 mm behind the exit surface of the silica layer. Photons were counted upon first entry into the SV to avoid double counting. For each transmitted photon, the kinetic energy and momentum direction were stored.

The scoring volume serves solely as a detection region for transmitted photons and does not influence the attenuation results. Because of the very low density of air (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 1.2\times 10^{-3}$$\end{document} g/cm\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^3$$\end{document}) and the small thickness of the scoring region, photon attenuation within the SV is negligible and does not affect the calculated transmission or attenuation coefficients. As photon energy increases, both materials show the expected monotonic decrease in MAC associated with the transition to Compton-dominated interactions. In the intermediate range (150–300 keV), the graded-Z design maintains a clear advantage due to the combined contribution of the BBS and LuAG layers. At higher energies (> 500 keV), where Compton scattering dominates, the MAC values converge, yet the graded-Z configuration retains a measurable improvement. These trends confirm that the layered architecture enhances attenuation efficiency without increasing areal density.

The ratio of the simulated MAC values to the corresponding NIST XCOM reference data is shown in Fig. 2b. Across the investigated energy range, the Geant4-derived attenuation coefficients are consistently slightly lower than the tabulated XCOM values. The ratio remains close to unity at lower energies (≈ 0.99) and gradually decreases to approximately 0.96 at the highest investigated energies. Deviations of this magnitude are consistent with previously reported comparisons between Monte Carlo transport simulations and tabulated attenuation coefficients, where Geant4-derived mass attenuation coefficients typically agree with XCOM and experimental data within a few percent21. These differences arise from cross-section interpolation, physics-model approximations, and statistical sampling inherent to Monte Carlo transport calculations. Overall, the agreement confirms the validity of the material definitions and electromagnetic interaction models used in the Geant4 implementation.

The absolute fluorescence intensity decreases with increasing photon energy for both materials. This trend reflects the reduced photoelectric cross section and the growing dominance of Compton scattering, which generates fewer K-shell vacancies. The graded-Z shield therefore exhibits three distinct emission regimes: (i) no fluorescence below the Lu K-edge, (ii) Lu-dominated emission between 63 and 90.5 keV, and (iii) Bi-dominated emission above the Bi K-edge. These results demonstrate that multilayer architectures redistribute radiation spectrally in a manner governed by engineered K-edge positions.

Figure 4 shows the angle-resolved transmitted energy fluence, dE/dΩ, including both primary and secondary photons. For all investigated energies, both materials exhibit forward-biased distributions. At low energies (59.5 and 80 keV), transmission is limited and dominated by minimally scattered primaries, resulting in broad but forward-weighted profiles.

Above 122 keV, Compton scattering governs transport, and the angular distributions develop sharper forward peaks consistent with Klein–Nishina anisotropy. The graded-Z multilayer produces systematically higher forward-directed energy fluence than RS-253, reflecting differences in material composition and layered structure. At larger polar angles (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta > 15^\circ$$\end{document}), the distributions converge, indicating that wide-angle scattering is primarily controlled by single Compton events and is relatively insensitive to layering.

Together, the spectral and angular analyses show that graded-Z shielding modifies photon transport beyond simple attenuation. While the architecture enhances overall attenuation efficiency, it also redistributes transmitted energy directionally. Such behavior may benefit applications requiring spectral shaping or directional filtering, but it does not universally minimize forward-directed energy leakage.

In practical shielding applications, the significance of this redistribution depends on the intended function of the barrier. For personnel protection, where minimizing transmitted dose in the forward direction is the primary objective, increased forward-directed energy fluence may reduce the advantage of multilayer designs. In contrast, in detector shielding, imaging systems, or radiation-monitoring windows, controlled spectral and directional transport can be beneficial by shaping the transmitted photon spectrum or reducing secondary radiation at large angles. These results therefore indicate that graded-Z architectures should be evaluated within the context of application-specific performance requirements.

The present study is simulation-based and establishes a quantitative framework for evaluating spectral–angular photon transport in transparent graded-Z architectures. Although the electromagnetic models in Geant4 are validated against evaluated cross-section data, experimental validation of angular distributions remains an important future step. Practical implementation also requires consideration of optical performance, manufacturability, and scalability constraints. Beyond conventional attenuation metrics, the multilayer configuration modifies photon transport through energy-dependent secondary fluorescence and interface-mediated scattering. Activation of Lu and Bi K-edges introduces characteristic re-emission features, while Compton scattering governs transmission behavior at higher energies. Although both materials produce strongly forward-peaked transmission, the graded-Z structure directs a larger fraction of transmitted energy into small polar angles, demonstrating that multilayer shielding alters not only attenuation magnitude but also the spectral and angular redistribution of radiation.

These results suggest that graded-Z shielding can provide tunable performance advantages where compact attenuation or spectral shaping is required. However, the enhanced forward-directed energy fluence indicates that such architectures may not universally minimize directional leakage, and their suitability depends on application-specific constraints. Overall, transparent graded-Z multilayers represent a promising design strategy for next-generation shielding windows, enabling attenuation performance, spectral redistribution, and angular transport to be jointly engineered through material selection and layer sequencing.
