Synergistic efficacy of radiotherapy and immunotherapy enhances clinical outcomes in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Jiafeng Liang, Lucheng Zhu, Kan Wu, Minna Zhang, Yi Tang, Kaicheng Pan, Bing Wang, Shenglin Ma, Xueqin Chen, Bing Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmunotherapy has become a primary treatment for advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), although drug resistance is inevitable. Radiotherapy enhances immunotherapy efficacy, particularly in early to mid-stage NSCLC. However, the synergistic effects of radiotherapy and immunotherapy in advanced NSCLC remain controversial.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed data from 141 stage IV NSCLC patients treated with first-line immunotherapy or chemoimmunotherapy at Hangzhou Cancer Hospital. Patients were divided into groups receiving radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy versus immunotherapy alone. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were evaluated using the Cox regression method. Subgroup Cox regression analyses were performed to optimize the combination regimens.ResultsThe combined therapy group showed longer PFS (16.9 vs. 8.49 months, HR 0.57, p = 0.006) and OS (69.93 vs. 24.62 months, HR 0.60, p = 0.04). Subgroup analysis indicated that non-squamous NSCLC patients without immunotherapy rechallenge benefited most. Radiotherapy added to immunotherapy in lung or brain metastases showed a trend toward improved PFS (lung: 19.34 vs. 6.03 months, HR 0.62, p = 0.30; brain: 22.72 vs. 7.80 months, HR 0.59, p = 0.29). Concurrent (HR 0.41, p = 0.02) or consolidation radiotherapy (HR 0.32, p = 0.003) during immunotherapy offered greater PFS benefits than symptom-relief radiotherapy. Both stereotactic body radiotherapy and conventional radiotherapy yielded similar survival outcomes. Incidence of grade 3 or higher pneumonia post-lung-radiotherapy was 3.45%.ConclusionsOur study highlights the synergistic efficacy of combining radiotherapy with immunotherapy in advanced NSCLC. Optimizing patient selection, targeting specific sites, effective timing, and tailored radiotherapy regimens significantly improved PFS and OS, providing crucial insights for enhancing clinical outcomes in advanced NSCLC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12672-026-05393-3.

Introduction

Lung cancer stands as the foremost cause of cancer-related deaths both in China and globally, with more than half of patients diagnosed at an advanced stage [1]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) constitutes 85% of cases and is marked by significant molecular heterogeneity [2]. Recent advancements in immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), have improved NSCLC patients’ treatment outcomes. These therapies have extended the 5-year survival rate to 20%, and even up to 40% in patients exhibiting high PD-L1 expression [3]. Consequently, immunotherapy, either alone or combined with chemotherapy, has become the standard first-line treatment for metastatic NSCLC without targetable mutations.

However, the efficacy of immunotherapy remains modest, with an overall response rate of approximately 30%. Additionally, nearly half of initially responsive patients develop acquired resistance, typically within two years [4, 5]. Addressing these challenges to enhance sensitivity to immunotherapy and overcome resistance is a critical imperative.

Radiotherapy has evolved as a potent local treatment for cancer, owing to advancements in technology that have enhanced its precision and broadened its therapeutic applications in curative, prophylactic, and palliative care settings [6]. In the era of immunotherapy, radiotherapy has emerged as a novel strategy to augment the efficacy of ICIs in advanced lung cancer [7]. Preclinical studies indicate that radiotherapy can induce immunogenic cell death and localized inflammatory responses, activating tumor-specific immunity and potentially triggering abscopal effects [8, 9]. Clinical evidence, exemplified by the PACIFIC study, demonstrates that combining ICIs with concurrent chemoradiotherapy for locally advanced NSCLC significantly increases the 5-year survival rate to 43% [3], aligning with preclinical findings and highlighting synergistic benefits. However, research into the role of radiation therapy in integrative treatment of advanced NSCLC remains limited and has yielded conflicting results [8, 10–12].

Two notable clinical studies, the phase II PEMBRO-RT trial and a phase I/II trial by MD Anderson Cancer Center, have explored combined radiation-immunotherapy but have not definitively established superiority over monotherapy [8, 11]. Meta-analyses reviewed by Welsh suggest reduced risks of disease progression and mortality with the addition of radiation therapy, further supporting its potential to enhance immunotherapy outcomes [12]. Meanwhile, some studies have demonstrated that the addition of radiotherapy to immunotherapy in metastatic NSCLC patients prolongs survival times [13, 14]. However, the benefit was not always consistent, as evidenced by the THUNDER-2 study, where the combined therapy failed to improve prognosis in stage IV NSCLC patients [15]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms underlying this synergy in advanced NSCLC require further investigation.

Our retrospective analysis aims to evaluate the sensitizing effects of incorporating radiotherapy into first-line immunotherapy or chemoimmunotherapy for NSCLC patients, focusing on patient selection, site-specific targeting, intervention timing, and radiotherapeutic strategies. These results seek to illuminate the role of radiation therapy in the immunotherapy era.

Methods

Study design

Patients diagnosed with stage IV NSCLC in Hangzhou Cancer Hospital who received first-line immunotherapy or chemoimmunotherapy from October 2021 to October 2023 were recruited. Follow-up was extended until May 9, 2024. Clinical data collected from medical records included demographic information (gender, age, smoking status), Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (PS) [16], TNM stage, histology, PD-L1 expression, immunotherapy regimen, concurrent chemotherapy, immunotherapy rechallenge status (whether patients received a second course of immunotherapy after this first-line treatment exposure), timing and regimen of radiotherapy, clinical outcomes, and adverse events. Clinical responses were categorized according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 [17], which complete and partial responses were classified as responders, and stable and progressive disease denoted non-responders. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the duration from the initiation of first-line treatment to disease progression or death from any cause; overall survival (OS) was determined from first-line treatment initiation to death or last follow-up. Safety assessment utilized the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0. Extra-regional lymph nodes were classified as metastasis if present. Lung lesions and lung-draining lymph nodes receiving radiation were categorized as lung targets, while lymph nodes receiving radiation alone were categorized as lymph node targets.

Treatment

Immunotherapy included PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, with or without chemotherapy. The decision to administer radiotherapy was made by a multidisciplinary tumor board, considering tumor burden, metastatic sites, symptoms, and performance status. Radiotherapy was delivered to selected patients either before, during, or after immunotherapy, with a time window of within 30 days prior to or during immunotherapy. Radiotherapy techniques included stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) or conventional fractionated radiotherapy, depending on the clinical scenario and metastatic sites. The choice of radiotherapy regimen was at the discretion of the radiation oncologist, based on the treatment intent and lesion location.

Study outcomes and analyses

First, to evaluate the overall efficacy of combining radiotherapy with immunotherapy, patients were stratified into two primary groups: those receiving radiotherapy within 30 days prior to or during immunotherapy (Rad plus IO) and those receiving immunotherapy alone (IO).

Second, within the Rad plus IO group, we performed exploratory analyses to identify optimal radiotherapy strategies. Patients were further classified according to the clinical intent and timing of radiotherapy: (1) symptom relief (e.g., for bone pain or intracranial hypertension), (2) concurrent (initiated within the first 3 cycles of immunotherapy), (3) consolidation (initiated after disease stabilization or response, typically following ≥ 3 cycles but still during immunotherapy), and (4) management of oligoprogression.

Statistical analyses

Median follow-up duration was calculated for patients who did not experience death events. Between-group comparisons employed the chi-squared test for categorical data. Fisher’s exact test was applied when the smallest expected value was < 5. Survival analysis utilized the Kaplan–Meier method, with comparison via the log-rank test. For unicox and multicox analysis, multiple imputation using the R package mice with logistic regression (method “logreg”) was performed to replace missing values for risk factor variables, and Cox proportional-hazards models were repeated on imputed data. One patient was excluded from the PFS analysis due to missing follow‑up assessment but was included in the OS analysis. The proportional hazards assumption was tested for each covariate using Schoenfeld residuals. The variable concurrent chemotherapy in PFS analysis violated this assumption; therefore, it was used as a stratification factor in multivariate Cox regression models rather than included as a covariate. All other variables satisfied the proportional hazards assumption. All clinically relevant variables listed in unicox model were entered into the multivariate Cox model, irrespective of their univariate p-values, as variable selection was based on clinical importance rather than statistical filtering. And a both direction multivariate Cox regression analysis was employed. Subgroup survival analysis is conducted using Cox regression models. All subgroup analyses were exploratory in nature, and no adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed. Statistical significance was defined at two-sided p values < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.2.0).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 141 patients diagnosed with stage IV NSCLC who undergoing first-line immunotherapy or chemoimmunotherapy in Hangzhou Cancer Hospital were enrolled. Follow-up continued until May 9, 2024. At the time of data cutoff, 65.96% of patients had experienced disease progression or death, and 47.52% had died. Among the patients, 121 were male, and 128 received PD-1 antibody treatment. A total of 107 patients had a performance status ≤ 1, and half of the patients demonstrated a response to the treatment. Detailed treatment protocols, including immunotherapy type, radiotherapy doses, and fractionation regimens, are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

After a median follow-up of 29.64 months, median PFS was 10.72 months [interquartile range (IQR) 8.92–16.56], and median OS was 27.61 months [IQR 22.82–NA]. One patient was excluded from PFS evaluation due to missed assessments post-therapy initiation. Figure 1A illustrates the PFS as a waterfall plot. In this plot, red bars represent patients in the Rad plus IO group, blue bars represent patients in the IO group, blank squares at the top indicate patients who have reached the PFS endpoint, arrows denote those who have not, colored squares at the bottom indicate metastasis sites at initial diagnosis, and asterisks denote radiotherapy sites (see figure caption for detailed symbol definitions).

Patients were stratified into two cohorts based on whether they received radiotherapy within 30 days prior to or during immunotherapy: the radiotherapy plus immunotherapy group (Rad plus IO, 68 patients) and the group receiving immunotherapy or chemoimmunotherapy alone (IO, 73 patients). The primary radiotherapy sites included lung (29 cases), lymph nodes (7 cases), bones (17 cases), and brain (19 cases) (Supplementary Fig. 1 A; Supplementary Table 2). Radiotherapy was administered at various intervals relative to immunotherapy initiation, ranging from 3 months before to 32.52 months after (Supplementary Fig. 1B), with 44 patients within 90 days and 18 patients exceeding 180 days.

Baseline patient characteristics

Supplementary Table 3 outlines the distribution of metastatic sites between the two groups, showing no statistical differences in the incidence of liver, bone, lung, pleural, adrenal gland, or lymph node metastases, except for a higher prevalence of brain metastases in the Rad plus IO group. Further survival analysis demonstrated similar PFS (p = 0.80; Supplementary Fig. 1 C) and OS (p = 0.46; Supplementary Fig. 1E) outcomes regardless of brain metastases imbalance.

PFS and OS analysis

Median PFS was significantly prolonged in the Rad plus IO group (16.79 months [IQR 11.25–29.21]) compared to the IO group (8.49 months [6.89–12.75]), with a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.57 (95% CI 0.38–0.86; p = 0.006; Fig. 1B). Similarly, median OS favored the Rad plus IO group (69.93 months [IQR 22.82–NA]) over the IO group (24.62 months [14.46–32.33]), with an HR of 0.60 (95% CI 0.37–0.97; p = 0.04; Fig. 1C). Also, we calculated 1-year and 2-year survival rates (Supplementary Table 4). The 1-year PFS rate was significantly higher in the Rad plus IO group compared to the IO alone group (59.29% vs. 37.00%, p = 0.01), consistent with the median PFS benefit. The 2-year PFS rate showed a numerical improvement that did not reach statistical significance (35.34% vs. 20.86%, p = 0.09). The 1-year and 2-year OS rates were numerically higher in the Rad plus IO group but did not achieve statistical significance (1-year: 80.30% vs. 68.45%, p = 0.12; 2-year: 61.44% vs. 53.57%, p = 0.40).

Hazard Ratio with Cox Regression Analysis for Overall Survival

Patients without immunotherapy rechallenge and those with non-squamous cell carcinoma are ideal candidates for radiotherapy combination

To determine which subgroup is more suitable for adding radiotherapy, subgroup analysis was conducted. Subgroup analyses of PFS indicated that the combination of immunotherapy and radiotherapy provided specific benefits to male patients (HR 0.54, 95% CI 0.34–0.84; p = 0.007), individuals under 65 years of age (HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.24–0.83; p = 0.01), patients concurrently treated with chemotherapy (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.39–0.96; p = 0.03), those without immunotherapy rechallenge (HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.35–0.90; p = 0.02), smokers (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.32–0.88; p = 0.01), patients with non-squamous cell carcinoma (HR 0.46, 95% CI 0.25–0.85; p = 0.01), and individuals with only one metastatic site (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.30–0.95; p = 0.03) (Fig. 2A). Patients without immunotherapy rechallenge (HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.32–0.98; p = 0.04) and those with non-squamous cell carcinoma (HR 0.38, 95% CI 0.18–0.84; p = 0.02) also experienced sustained clinical benefits in OS subgroup analysis within the Rad plus IO group (Fig. 2B).

Radiotherapy is more suitable for targeting the lung and brain

We conducted an investigation to determine the most effective organ for radiation targeting to enhance immunotherapy efficacy. Specifically, our analysis focused on patients with lung metastases, revealing a trend towards improved PFS (Rad plus IO vs. IO, 19.34 months [IQR 12.33–NA] vs. 6.03 months [IQR 4.16–NA]; HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.25–1; p = 0.30; Fig. 3A) and OS (69.93 months [IQR NA–NA] vs. 14.07 months [IQR 9.18–NA], HR 0.37, 95% CI 0.12–1.16; p = 0.10; Fig. 3B) following addition of radiotherapy to lung lesions, although statistical significance was not reached in either case. Similar findings were observed in patients with brain metastases, with extended PFS (22.72 months [7.15–NA] vs. 7.80 months [4.59–NA]; HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.19–1.8; p = 0.29; Fig. 3C) and OS (NA months [28.95–NA] vs. 16.00 months [12.00–NA]; HR 0.42, 95% CI 0.11–1.67; p = 0.18; Fig. 3D) following radiation to brain lesions, albeit without statistical significance. Conversely, no PFS or OS benefit was noted among patients with bone metastases receiving bone-targeted radiotherapy (p = 0.38 and p = 0.83, respectively; Fig. 3E, F).

SBRT and conventional radiotherapy show comparable effects in sensitizing immunotherapy

Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) is recognized for its ability to induce tumor immunogenicity and activate immune responses. Accordingly, we compared the outcomes of SBRT and conventional radiotherapy as adjuncts to first-line immunotherapy or chemoimmunotherapy in stage IV NSCLC patients. The analysis revealed no significant differences in either PFS (p = 0.25; Fig. 4A) or OS (p = 0.27; Fig. 4B) between patients treated with SBRT and those treated with conventional radiotherapy.

Concurrent radiotherapy during immunotherapy and consolidation radiotherapy post disease stabilization or remission represents optimal timing for integrating radiation

We categorized radiotherapy interventions into four cohorts based on their rationale: alleviation of symptoms such as bone pain or intracranial hypertension, concurrent radiotherapy initiated within 3 cycles of starting immunotherapy or chemoimmunotherapy, consolidation radiotherapy post disease stabilization or remission (following 3 cycles of systemic therapy) and addressing oligoprogression or oligoprogression trends. The patient distribution across these categories was 27, 19, 20, and 9, respectively. Comparing these intervention groups with patients who did not undergo radiotherapy, we found that concurrent radiotherapy (HR 0.41, 95% CI 0.20–0.84; p = 0.02) and consolidation radiotherapy post disease stabilization or remission (HR 0.32, 95% CI 0.15–0.66; p = 0.003) were associated with significant PFS benefits (Fig. 4C). Additionally, the concurrent radiotherapy cohort exhibited an extended OS (HR 0.32, 95% CI 0.12–0.90; p = 0.03; Fig. 4D). However, no PFS or OS advantage was observed for radiation aimed at symptom relief or addressing oligoprogression trends. We then conducted a detailed analysis of the concurrent and consolidation radiotherapy groups. Among the 19 patients in the concurrent group, 10 received lung radiotherapy and 9 received brain radiotherapy, initiated within 3 months before or up to 2.52 months after starting immunotherapy. The consolidation group, comprising 20 patients, primarily targeted lung or intrathoracic lymph node lesions, with radiotherapy administered from 2.63 months to 10.66 months after initiating immunotherapy (Fig. 4E). Despite variations in target lesions and timing of radiotherapy initiation between the concurrent and consolidation groups, both approaches demonstrated evidence of immune system activation.

Incidence of pneumonitis analysis

Lastly, we evaluated the incidence of pneumonitis in 68 patients who underwent radiotherapy (Fig. 4F). Among them, 15 patients experienced grade 1 pneumonitis, one had grade 2, and another had grade 3 pneumonitis. No pneumonitis was observed in 45 patients with available post-radiotherapy imaging. The remaining 6 patients (8.8%) lacked post-radiotherapy imaging and were therefore classified as not assessable for pneumonitis. Specifically, among the 29 patients who received lung radiotherapy (Fig. 4G), 14 developed pneumonitis, including 12 cases of grade 1, 1 case of grade 2, and 1 case of grade 3. Importantly, only one patient who did not receive radiotherapy experienced severe immune-related adverse reactions and ultimately died of immune-mediated hepatitis.

Discussion

Our analysis emphasizes key considerations for implementing combined radioimmunotherapy, including patient selection criteria, targeted anatomical sites, optimal timing of intervention, and tailored radiotherapeutic regimens. The conclusion shows that optimal radiotherapy administered to specific patients receiving immunotherapy significantly improves PFS and OS.

In the pre-immunotherapy era, radiotherapy’s role in advanced NSCLC primarily focused on patients with oligometastatic disease. Landmark trials such as SABR-COMET II and CURB demonstrated a survival advantage when SBRT was combined with standard care for patients with oligometastases, laying the foundation for radiotherapy in advanced lung cancer [18, 19]. In the current era of immunotherapy, the synergy between radiotherapy and immunotherapy is supported by solid theoretical foundations. Initial applications of this combined approach in advanced lung cancer, particularly in oligometastatic patients, have shown promise. While individual analyses like the Pembro-RT (Phase 2) study and the MD Anderson Cancer Center Trial (Phase 1/2) yielded negative results, pooled data have highlighted significantly improved response rates, control rates, PFS, and OS with the combination of immunotherapy and SBRT compared to immunotherapy alone.12 These findings underscore the potential of radiotherapy-combined therapies in optimizing immunotherapy outcomes for oligometastatic patients. However, given that approximately 74% of stage IV NSCLC patients present with multi-metastatic disease at diagnosis [20], expanding the application of radiotherapy-immunotherapy combinations to this population is urgently needed. In this context, our study focused on advanced NSCLC patients and drew on retrospective analyses of the efficacy and safety of radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy/chemoimmunotherapy.

Most previous investigations into radiotherapy and immunotherapy combinations were conducted in post-line treatments without chemotherapy intervention [8, 10–12]. However, in real-world clinical practice, first-line treatment often involves a combination of chemotherapy and immunotherapy, and the effectiveness of radiotherapy and immunotherapy following chemotherapy intervention remains unclear. A recent retrospective study advocated for the early introduction of thoracic radiotherapy in NSCLC patients undergoing immunotherapy [21, 22], emphasizing the need for further discussion on the advancement of radiotherapy. Building on these insights, our primary focus is on examining the benefits of adding radiotherapy to first-line immune-containing therapy for advanced NSCLC.

Numerous studies have demonstrated varied optimal time intervals between radiotherapy and immunotherapy. The Pacific study highlighted that commencing immunotherapy within two weeks after radiotherapy commencement improved survival outcomes. In contrast, Bryant et al. and Denault et al. did not identify a critical two-week timeframe [23, 24]. A retrospective analysis of advanced NSCLC patients undergoing immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment following radiotherapy suggested that could be delineated at 60 days post-radiotherapy cessation [25]. Secondary analysis of KEYNOTE-001 data indicated persistent synergistic effects despite a median interval of 9.5 months between the first pembrolizumab cycle and radiotherapy [26]. Due to ongoing uncertainty, we limited the timing of radiotherapy within the radiotherapy group to a more conservative range, initiating it at least 30 days before starting immunotherapy and concluding at the end of immunotherapy. Patients undergoing radiotherapy after treatment line changes were excluded from radiation group due to ambiguity about localized therapy’s role in systemic progression treatment.

Based on this background, we evaluated the survival outcomes of the Rad plus IO group compared to IO alone. Our study showed significant improvement in median PFS (16.79 vs. 8.49 months) and a favorable OS trend, supporting the synergistic potential of combining radiotherapy with immunotherapy in advanced NSCLC. However, because most patients in the combination group had not yet reached the OS endpoint at the time of analysis, the Kaplan‑Meier estimate at the 50th percentile may be unstable. To provide more robust measures of treatment effect, we also examined 1‑year and 2‑year survival rates. The significantly higher 1‑year PFS rate in the Rad plus IO group (59.29% vs. 37.00%, p = 0.01) confirms a synergistic benefit, suggesting that radiotherapy may enhance the response to immunotherapy. The numerical improvements in 2‑year PFS and OS rates, although not statistically significant, hint at a potential durable benefit that warrants longer follow‑up for confirmation.

The positive findings from our study stand in contrast to the recently reported PACIFIC‑2 trial, which failed to demonstrate a survival benefit with concurrent durvalumab initiation during curative‑intent chemoradiotherapy in unresectable stage III NSCLC and even observed increased early toxicity. In PACIFIC‑2, the synchronous addition of immunotherapy to intensive definitive local therapy may have overwhelmed immune regulatory mechanisms, reaffirming the sequential “PACIFIC regimen” as the standard for locally advanced disease. In contrast, our study focused on stage IV disease, where radiotherapy was delivered as focal palliative or consolidative treatment to limited metastatic sites. In this setting, localized radiation appears to act as an immunomodulatory catalyst, amplifying systemic anti‑tumor responses without the toxicity burden. These contrasting results highlight that the synergy between radiotherapy and immunotherapy is highly context‑dependent, requiring precise tailoring based on disease stage, treatment intent, and the scope of radiation fields.

In addition to the overall survival benefit, we also explored the impact of radiotherapy on different metastatic sites. Considering immunogenetic variations, Professor Zhang et al. advocated for multisite irradiation to optimize immunotherapy efficacy [27]. However, comprehensive irradiation across all sites presents challenges such as prolonged hospitalization, elevated treatment costs, and increased risk of side effects. Some studies suggest that brain radiotherapy yields optimal response rates compared to radiotherapy targeting lung and bone metastases, indicating varying degrees of immune sensitization across organs [28]. While our study observed a trend towards extended survival in subgroups with lung and brain metastases, no significant survival difference was noted with additional radiotherapy across various metastatic sites. We believe that further refinement in the number and location of targeted lesions may yield divergent results, which will be a focal point of our future research.

Additionally, consideration must be given to the modality of radiotherapy. Retrospective data from the National Cancer Database and result from MD Anderson Cancer Center research both indicated that NSCLC patients treated with SBRT combined with immunotherapy exhibit superior outcomes compared to those receiving conventional radiotherapy with immunotherapy [11, 29]. Conversely, a meta-analysis has shown that the synergistic use of radiotherapy and ICIs enhances OS in NSCLC compared to either treatment alone, with indications of improved survival observed with both SBRT and conventional radiotherapy [30]. This suggests that conventional radiotherapy also holds promise for synergizing with immune responses. Consistent with this analysis, our study found no significant difference in survival between SBRT and conventional fractionation, suggesting both dose fractions can be combined with immunotherapy.

The role of radiotherapy in activating immunity against advanced lung cancer is widely acknowledged. However, determining the optimal treatment regimen remains crucial. In our study, patients were stratified based on the intent and timing of radiation therapy to explore optimal treatment strategies. Subgroup analyses indicated that concurrent radiation during immunotherapy yields the most favorable synergistic survival benefits. This finding aligns with previous research and underscores the recommendation for early incorporation of radiation therapy at the onset of systemic treatment in advanced NSCLC patients [21]. Furthermore, in the radiation consolidation subgroup, adding radiotherapy after disease stabilization or reduction showed a tendency towards prolonged PFS but did not achieve statistical significance for OS. Additionally, our study categorized radiation therapy into consolidation versus synchronous cohorts based on a threshold of three cycles of systemic therapy, alternative timeframes for cohort classification may yield disparate outcomes.

Regarding adverse effects, meta-analyses have not identified any safety concerns associated with combining ICIs and radiotherapy [31]. The KEYNOTE-001 study noted an elevated overall incidence of pneumonia in patients with prior radiotherapy exposure, though no significant disparity was observed in grade 3 or higher pneumonia occurrence [26]. Our study statistically analyzed pneumonia incidence and found a similar incidence of grade 3/4 pneumonia as reported in the KEYNOTE-001 study, indicating an acceptable safety profile.

This study is not without limitations. Firstly, it is a single-center retrospective analysis with limited sample sizes, necessitating further validation through prospective studies. Secondly, due to limited data on PD-L1 expression, the constraints on PD-L1 analysis results remain apparent. Thirdly, this study did not perform detailed dosimetric analysis of radiotherapy regimens, given the substantial heterogeneity of protocols and limited subgroup sizes. However, expanding the sample size could lead to more detailed conclusions. Lastly, although over 50% of patients reached PFS endpoints, the majority did not achieve OS endpoints, highlighting the need for longer follow-up durations.

Conclusions

Our study explores the benefits of combining radiotherapy with first-line immunotherapy/chemoimmunotherapy for stage IV NSCLC patients, focusing on patient selection, targeted sites, timing of intervention, and radiotherapeutic regimens. Optimal candidates for radiotherapy combination included patients who do not undergo immunotherapy rechallenge and those with non-squamous cell carcinoma. Radiotherapy is more suitable for targeting the lung and brain. Concurrent radiotherapy during immunotherapy and consolidation radiotherapy post disease stabilization or remission represents optimal timing for integrating radiation. Notably, SBRT and conventional radiotherapy show comparable effects in sensitizing immune therapy. Importantly, our research explores the most effective radiotherapy strategy for integration into the treatment of metastatic NSCLC patients receiving immunotherapy. To validate these findings, a large-scale prospective trial is warranted.
