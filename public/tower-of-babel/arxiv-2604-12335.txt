All in One: A Unified Synthetic Data Pipeline for Multimodal Video Understanding
Tanzila Rahman, Renjie Liao, Leonid Sigal, [0.5em] University of British Columbia

Abstract
Training multimodal large language models (MLLMs) for video understanding requires large-scale annotated data spanning diverse tasks such as object counting, question answering, and segmentation. However, collecting and annotating multimodal video data in real-world is costly, slow, and inherently limited in diversity and coverage. To address this challenge, we propose a unified synthetic data generation pipeline capable of automatically producing unlimited multimodal video data with rich and diverse supervision. Our framework supports multiple task formats within a single pipeline, enabling scalable and consistent data creation across tasks. To further enhance reasoning ability, we introduce a VQA-based fine-tuning strategy that trains models to answer structured questions about visual content rather than relying solely on captions or simple instructions. This formulation encourages deeper visual grounding and reasoning. We evaluate our approach in three challenging tasks: video object counting, video-based visual question answering, and video object segmentation. Experimental results demonstrate that models trained predominantly on synthetic data generalize effectively to real-world datasets, often outperforming traditionally trained counterparts. Our findings highlight the potential of unified synthetic data pipelines as a scalable alternative to expensive real-world annotation for multimodal video understanding.

3.1
Overview

We propose a simple and efficient pipeline for generating synthetic multimodal data from a single image. The primary objective of this framework is to automatically expand one visual input into multiple related modalities, including text, video, and optionally audio. Rather than generating each modality independently, the proposed pipeline follows a sequential and connected design, where each stage conditions the outputs of the previous stage. This progressive generation strategy promotes semantic consistency and structural coherence across modalities, resulting in realistic and well-aligned multimodal data. The overall workflow of the proposed framework is illustrated in figure
2
.

The central idea of our approach is straightforward, yet effective. Starting from a single image, we first obtain a semantic description using a large language model. The generated caption, together with the original image, is then used as conditioning information for video synthesis. Finally, the generated video and its corresponding textual description can optionally serve as inputs for audio generation, enabling synchronized audiovisual output. Through this staged cross-modal conditioning process, a single visual input can be transformed into rich and semantically aligned multimodal content.

Image Modality:
The image is the starting point and is considered an important condition throughout the pipeline. It provides concrete visual details such as object shapes, colors, layout, and background structure. Moreover, based on the image and the objects associated with it, we can imagine a possible future scene that could emerge from it. By keeping the image as a reference during video generation, the system can maintain visual consistency. Using the image helps prevent unrealistic changes in object identity or scene structure. For example, if a person appears in the image, conditioning the image ensures that the same person remains visually consistent in the generated video. Therefore, the image provides strong grounding and improves the reliability of the synthetic data.

Text Modality:
The first step of the pipeline is caption generation. We take a single image and provide it to a large language model to describe what is happening in the scene. For this purpose, we use ChatGPT
92
. The model analyzes visual content and generates a natural and meaningful caption.
The generated caption explains the objects in the image, their relationships, and the overall scene or event. For example, it may describe actions, emotions, or environmental details visible in the image. This text plays an important role in the pipeline because it provides high-level semantic information that may not be directly available from the image alone. The caption later acts as guidance for video generation, helping the system understand what kind of motion or event progression should occur.

Video Modality:
After obtaining both the image and its generated caption, we proceed to video generation. We use Wan 2.2
84
to create a temporally coherent video sequence. Wan is an open suite of video foundation models based on the diffusion transformer paradigm, designed for high-quality video generation across multiple tasks. It introduces series of innovations such as a spatio-temporal variational autoencoder, scalable pretraining strategies, and large-scale curated datasets, enabling strong generative performance while remaining efficient enough for consumer-grade GPUs.
The model can generate video conditioned on the caption, the image, or both together. If the video is conditioned only on text, the caption guides the type of motion or event that should happen. If it is conditioned only on the image, the model focuses on preserving visual appearance and structure. When both image and text are used together, the system benefits from semantic guidance and visual grounding at the same time. This combined conditioning typically produces more consistent and meaningful video output.
The generated video extends the static image into a dynamic sequence. It introduces motion while keeping the scene content aligned with the original image and its description. In this way, a single frame is transformed into a short visual story.

Audio as an optional modality:
As an optional extension, the pipeline can also generate audio. We use VTA-LDM
43
to synthesize sound based on the generated video and caption. The audio model considers both the visual events and the textual description to produce synchronized and contextually appropriate sound.
For example, if the video shows ocean waves, the system may generate wave sounds. If people are talking, it may generate the corresponding background audio. However, in this paper, we focus mainly on visual modalities and do not conduct detailed experiments with audio generation. The audio component is included to demonstrate that the pipeline can be extended to full audiovisual generation if needed.

3.2
Mask Generation for Video Segmentation:

Accurate and temporally consistent segmentation masks are critical for advancing video understanding and generation. In addition to generating multiple modalities, we focus on mask generation as a key auxiliary modality for object-level segmentation, which provides high-quality synthetic data that can significantly enhance the training of video foundation models. By capturing both spatial and temporal object dynamics, these masks enable models to better understand motion, occlusion, and structural changes in videos, addressing one of the main challenges in video-centric learning.
Starting from a single input image, we first generate precise, high-resolution object masks using SAM2
54
. These masks are then propagated across generated video frames using MUG-VOS
62
, ensuring temporal consistency and preserving object structures over time. This transforms static masks into fully annotated video sequences that reflect object motion, appearance variation, and occlusion events.

The resulting video segmentation masks can be utilized for a wide range of downstream applications. They provide synthetic supervision for training video foundation models, enabling robust learning of spatio-temporal representations without manual annotation. They also improve video object tracking and action recognition by offering precise localization across frames, and facilitate video editing tasks such as selective object manipulation, background replacement, and scene-level compositing. Moreover, these masks support dataset augmentation and benchmarking, offering a scalable, automated approach to enrich video datasets while reducing dependence on costly human-labeled annotations.

3.3
Why It is an All-in-One Pipeline:

We call our framework an all-in-one pipeline because it integrates multiple modalities within a single unified process. Starting from just one image, the system can automatically generate text, video, masks and even audio. Each modality is connected and conditioned on the others, ensuring consistency across outputs.
Unlike separate systems that generate text, video, or audio independently, our approach allows all modalities to be created within the same structured workflow. This means that we can generate image–text pairs, image–video pairs, text–video pairs, or full audiovisual sequences following the pipeline.
Because of this flexibility and integration, the proposed framework serves as a general solution for multimodal synthetic data generation. It can be used to create diverse training data for multimodal foundation models while maintaining coherence and controllability across modalities.

4
Experimental Results

4.1
LLM-Guided Annotations:

To construct richer supervision signals, we generate three types of labels using a large language model (LLM): (1) image captions, (2) object counting annotations, and (3) visual question–answer (VQA) pairs. We begin by sampling images from the MSCOCO dataset
63
, which provides high-quality object-level annotations.

First, we employ ChatGPT to generate future-plausible captions conditioned on each input image. These captions extend beyond literal descriptions and capture potential future events or plausible scenarios associated with visual content.
Second, since MSCOCO includes annotations for object instances, we compute object counting labels by aggregating the number of instances for each object category present in the image.
Finally, leveraging both the input image and the generated captions, we require the LLM to produce three visual question–answer pairs per image. These VQA pairs encourage reasoning about visual content while incorporating contextual cues from the generated captions.
The resulting dataset therefore contains complementary multimodal supervision, including object counts, descriptive captions, and question–answer pairs (see figure
3
). Such diverse annotations provide rich semantic and reasoning signals that can be used to train or fine-tune vision–language foundation models.

4.2
Text and Image driven video generation:

After generating captions for each video, we employ a video generation model to synthesize videos conditioned on the textual description and the initial frame. Specifically, we utilize Wan2.2
84
, which is available in two configurations with 5B and 14B parameters. In our experiments, we adopt the 14B parameter model to achieve higher generation quality and better temporal consistency.

Using the generated captions and the initial frame as inputs, the model produces synthetic videos that follow the described scene dynamics. From these generated videos, we construct a synthetic multimodal dataset containing: (1) the initial image, (2) descriptive captions, (3) object-count annotations derived from the captions, and (4) visual question–answer (VQA) pairs.
This automatically generated dataset provides diverse multimodal supervision signals that can be used to fine-tune multimodal foundation models, enabling improved performance on downstream tasks such as visual question answering and object counting.

4.4
Effect of Synthetic Data on Segmentation

In addition to VQA and object counting tasks, we investigate the effectiveness of synthetic data for improving segmentation performance. To construct the training dataset, we first sample images from the MSCOCO dataset and use the Segment Anything Model (SAM)
54
to extract high-quality object-level segmentation masks. These masks serve as accurate instance-level annotations for each object in the images.

Next, we generate synthetic videos using our proposed video generation pipeline. To obtain temporally consistent segmentation annotations across video frames, we apply the MUG-VOS
62
pipeline to track the segmented objects throughout each video. Starting from the initial frame masks, the tracking propagates the segmentation masks to subsequent frames, producing frame-level segmentation labels for the entire video. The resulting synthetic videos are then used to fine-tune SAM.

Experimental results demonstrate that fine-tuning with synthetic videos significantly improves segmentation performance over the baseline model. We further study the effect of synthetic data scale by training models with 2K and 5K videos. As shown in Tables
3
and
4
, increasing the number of synthetic videos consistently improves performance, with the model trained on 5K videos achieving higher mean IoU than the one trained on 2K videos. Some classes exhibit minor degradations after fine-tuning. This can occur due to domain shift, overfitting to patterns present in the synthetic data, or occasional tracking errors in the MUG-VOS pipeline that propagate imperfect masks across frames. These degradations can be mitigated by combining synthetic videos with a small fraction of real annotated images, applying data augmentation to increase variability, or improving the diversity of synthetic object appearances and backgrounds. Despite these cases, the overall trend demonstrates clear performance gains, as reflected in the improved mean IoU.

These results highlight the scalability of our approach. Synthetic data can be generated at negligible cost compared to manual annotation, enabling the creation of large-scale training datasets that improve model performance while reducing annotation effort and cost.

We evaluate our models using task-specific metrics to comprehensively measure performance. For video object counting (in Table

), we report the mean absolute error (MAE) and the mean squared error (MSE)

, which quantify the deviation between the predicted and ground-truth object counts, where lower values indicate better performance. For video-VQA (in Table

These metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of our framework across counting, reasoning, and visual understanding tasks.

4.6
Effectiveness of Synthetic Videos Despite Imperfections:

Although the synthetic videos generated by our pipeline are not perfect and may contain minor artifacts, unrealistic object motions, or occasional misalignments, they still provide valuable supervision for model training. This is because the videos introduce a wide variety of objects, scenes, and actions that are often underrepresented in real datasets, allowing the model to learn more diverse visual-language correspondences. In addition, the temporally consistent annotations propagated across frames provide dense supervision that is rarely available in large-scale real videos, helping the model to capture stable object representations over time. Furthermore, the large scale of synthetic data ensures that the model is exposed to thousands of variations, which allows it to generalize well despite minor imperfections in individual samples. When used for fine-tuning, these synthetic videos complement the knowledge already learned from real datasets, enabling the model to improve performance on downstream tasks such as video captioning, VQA, object counting, and segmentation. Overall, even imperfect synthetic videos act as a cost-effective and scalable source of training data that consistently enhances model performance.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we propose a scalable pipeline for generating synthetic videos and systematically study how such data can be leveraged to train multimodal vision-language models. By producing temporally consistent videos with frame-level annotations, our approach provides a cost-effective alternative to manual data collection. Through extensive experiments on video-VQA, object counting, and segmentation, we show that fine-tuning with synthetic data improves overall performance across tasks. We further demonstrate that increasing the amount of synthetic data leads to additional performance gains, highlighting the scalability of our framework. These results suggest that synthetic data can serve as a practical and efficient means of augmenting real-world datasets and reducing reliance on expensive annotations.
Moreover, our analysis shows that even imperfect synthetic videos provide useful supervision by introducing diversity and temporal consistency often missing in real datasets. This highlights the potential of synthetic data not only as a supplement but also as a key component for training large-scale multimodal models.
In future work, we plan to extend this framework to audio-visual settings by incorporating synthetic audio to enable richer multimodal reasoning.
