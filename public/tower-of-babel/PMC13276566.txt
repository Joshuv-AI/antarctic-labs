Potential Years of Life Lost Due to Lip, Oral and Oropharyngeal Cancer in Brazil: Estimates and Forecasting
Authors: Luana Carla Salvi, Francine dos Santos Costa, Kauê Farias Collares, Otávio Pereira D’avilla, Luiz Felipe Pinto, Flávio Fernando Demarco, Ana Carolina Uchoa Vasconcelos, Luiz Alexandre Chisini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aims to evaluate the effects of age-period-cohort on potential years of life lost (PYLL) due to lip, oral, and oropharyngeal cancer (LOOPC) in Brazil from 1980 to 2024 and forecast PYLL for 2044.MethodsA retrospective ecological study was performed with all 5570 Brazilian municipalities. PYLL per 100,000 inhabitants and age-standardized-PYLL were calculated. Trends and the annual percent change (APC%) were assessed using the Prais-Winsten regression. Poisson regression was applied to assess age-period-cohort effects. Forecasts were conducted using the Lee-Carter model.ResultsA total of 165,467 deaths resulting in 1,574,693 PYLL were observed. Lip cancer accounted for 0.9%, oral cancer for 60.4%, and oropharyngeal 38.7% of the total PYLL. Men accounted for 85.6% of the total PYLL due to LOOPC. The analysis of trends revealed that lip cancer in men shows a significant decreasing trend (β = -0.13, 95% CI [-0.22 to -0.05]), with an APC% = -2.71% (95% CI: -4.09% to -1.33%). The age-period-cohort model demonstrated superior fit compared to alternative models (p<0.001). Projections showed a decrease in lip cancer in men between 5–45 years and 50–60 years, while oral and oropharyngeal cancers are projected to increase before 25 years and decrease between 25 and 70 years for men. In contrast, oral cancer in women is projected to increase before 40 years and from 45 to 65 years.ConclusionSignificant regional inequalities were observed in the PYLL projections. Although women have significantly less PYLL due to LOOPC, most projections show a reduction in PYLL for LOOPC in men and an increase in oral cancer in women.Keywords: Mortality rates, Potential years of life lost, Oral cavity cancer, Oropharyngeal cancer, Age-period-cohort analysis

Introduction

Cancer ranks globally as the second most common cause of mortality1 with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) being the most common form of lip, oral, and oropharyngeal cancer (LOOPC).2 The highest incidence of oral cancer has historically been reported in South and Southeast Asia, parts of Western and Central Europe, and South America.3,4 Despite being preventable, LOOPC remains a significant public health challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.2,5 These cancers develop through a complex process involving genetic and epigenetic alterations.6,7 Key risk factors include tobacco and alcohol use,8 infection with human papillomavirus (HPV)—particularly in the case of oropharyngeal cancer9,10— and prolonged exposure to ultraviolet radiation (UVR) — which is strongly linked to lip cancer.8,9,11 While global tobacco use has declined,12 HPV infections are rising, largely due to changes in sexual behaviors such as an increased number of partners and unprotected sex.13,14

Beyond mortality rates, the burden of LOOPC extends to its impact on premature mortality, as many affected individuals die at relatively young ages.15 Potential Years of Life Lost (PYLL) is a crucial metric for assessing the public health impact of early deaths, as it quantifies the years of life lost compared to an expected lifespan.16 Unlike crude mortality rates, PYLL provides insight into premature death's societal and economic consequences. For example, premature deaths from LOOPC17, 18, 19 will disproportionately affect working-age adults contributing to productivity losses and increased healthcare costs.20 Understanding PYLL trends can help policymakers prioritize prevention, early detection, and treatment strategies to mitigate the long-term consequences of LOOPC-related deaths.

Despite its significance, few studies have projected future trends in LOOPC mortality4,21,22 and no study has forecast the PYLL. Applying an age-period-cohort (APC) model in combination with forecasting methods allows for a more detailed assessment of the influence of age, historical periods, and birth cohorts on PYLL patterns. This approach not only enhances understanding of disease dynamics but also enables more precise projections of future mortality rates across different age groups.23,24 Thus, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of age, period, and birth cohort on PYLL due to LOOPC in Brazil from 1980 to 2024 and projected mortality rates for 2044 to provide insights into future disease burden.

Materials and methods

Study reporting and design

The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline was used to report this study.25 Given its ecological design, additional considerations relevant to studies using aggregated population-level data were taken into account.

Study design and setting

A retrospective time-series ecological study was carried out, encompassing all 5,570 municipalities in Brazil. The study examined the impact of age, period, and birth cohort on the PYLL due to LOOPC in Brazil from 1980 to 2024. Modeled projections were generated for 2044 at both the national and regional levels (South, Southeast, Midwest, North, and Northeast). Mortality data were obtained from the Mortality Information System (in Portuguese: Sistema de Informações sobre Mortalidade, SIM). Population demographic data for the study period were retrieved from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics. All data was collected in April 2026.

Study outcome

The primary outcome of this study was the PYLL due to LOOPC from 1980 to 2024, categorized by cancer location (lip, oral, and oropharyngeal) and sex (men and women). Cancer site classification followed the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 and ICD-10)19,26 codes: C00 and 140 (lip); C01-02 and 141 (tongue); C03 and 143 (gingiva); C04 and 144 (floor of the mouth); C05-06 and 145 (other parts of the mouth); and C09-10 and 146 (oropharynx).21 For analysis, codes C01/141 to C05/145 were grouped under oral cancer; while the tongue base is anatomically part of the oropharynx, DATA-SUS did not distinguish it separately between 1979 and 1995. Malignant neoplasms of the nasopharynx, hypopharynx, and major salivary glands were excluded.

PYLL was calculated based on the remaining years until reaching 69 years, as previously established.18,27 PYLL rates per 100,000 inhabitants were determined for each age group. Age-standardized calculations employed the direct method, considering the reference population as proposed by Segi and modified by Doll.28 Age-standardized PYLL (AS-PYLL) were also estimated by cancer type and sex for each period analysed.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses included the estimation of PYLL and AS-PYLL for lip, oral, and oropharyngeal cancer. Lexis diagrams were created for the outcome, presenting rates per 100,000 inhabitants. Outcomes were plotted by age, period, and birth cohort as proposed by Carstensen.23 Trends and the annual percent change (APC%) with 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) were assessed using the Prais-Winsten regression model for the period evaluated using as the outcome the log-transformed PYLL.29,30

Age-Period-Cohort (APC) effects were estimated using the method proposed by Holford31 to deal with the nonidentifiability problem, thus, APC parameters were estimated by deviations, curvatures, and drift. APC analysis was modeled using Poisson regression modeling. The APC model produced relative risk (RR) estimates for each period, using the year 2000 as the reference period and the 1960 birth cohort as the reference cohort. Therefore, nine 5-year periods (1980-1984, …, 2020-2024) and 22 five-year cohorts (1915-1919, 1920-1924, …, 2020-2024) were evaluated. Age was categorized into 5-year intervals.

Model fit was assessed using the deviance statistic, with lower deviance values indicating better fit. The contribution of each effect (age, period, and cohort) was evaluated by comparing the deviance of the full model to models with specific effects removed. Regression modeling employed the "ns" parameter, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using the "bs" parameters.

Projection analysis

Forecasts were generated using the Lee-Carter24 model, a non-linear age-time model designed for rates within a Lexis diagram. The model expresses log-transformed rates as a function of age and period, positioning knots based on event frequency. The reference age was set at 50 years, and the reference period at 2024. Projections for 2044 were generated using 1000 bootstrap simulations, with 95% confidence intervals calculated from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio version 4.3.2, employing the “dplyr” and “Epi” packages.

Results

A total of 165,467 deaths attributed to LOOPC cancer were registered in Brazil, resulting in 1,574,693 PYLL. Men accounted for 85.6% of the total PYLL due to LOOPC, equivalent to 1,348,059 PYLL, whereas women contributed to 14.4% (226,634 PYLL) of the total. Lip cancer accounted for 0.9%, oral cancer for 60.4%, and oropharyngeal 38.7% of the total PYLL. The analysis of trends revealed that lip cancer in men shows a significant decreasing trend (β = -0.13, 95% CI [-0.22 to -0.05]), with an APC% = -2.71% (95% CI: -4.09% to -1.33%) (Table 1). No other significant trend was observed in the Praiss-Winsten regression model. Age-period-cohort effect

Supplemental Table 2 shows the full adjustments in the APC models. Notably, the APC model demonstrated superior fit compared to alternative models: age, age–drift, age–cohort, and age–period. Thus, the age-period-cohort was the best model for all cancers and sexes, displaying the lowest deviance and significant p-values. The age-period-cohort model presented the best fit for all cancers and sexes in sensitivity analysis (Supplemental Table 3).

The age effect model (Figure 1) indicates that increasing age is associated with a marked rise in PYLL rates for LOOPC, peaking around 50 years and declining thereafter for both cancer types and sexes. A cohort effect is significant, with distinct patterns by cancer type and sex. For lip cancer in men, there is a consistent decline in PYLL rates across successive younger cohorts, whereas in women, this decline levels off from the 1980 birth cohorts onward. For oral cancer in men, a similar decreasing trend is observed across younger cohorts; in contrast, women exhibit an increase in PYLL in the youngest cohort. For oropharyngeal cancer, PYLL rate ratios increase among cohorts born between approximately 1915 and 1960, followed by a marked decline in cohorts born after 2000. The period effect varies across cancer types and sexes. For lip cancer, men show a decrease in PYLL rate ratios from 1980 to 2000, followed by an increase, whereas women exhibit an increase up to 1990, followed by a decline that stabilizes after 2000. For oral cancer, a decrease in rate ratios is observed from 1980 to 2000, followed by a slight increase and a subsequent decline after 2010 in both sexes. For oropharyngeal cancer, the period effect is relatively stable in men, while in women, there is a decrease pre 2000 era, followed by an increase in post 2020 era.

Projections

PYLL forecasting due LOOPC for Brazil in 2044 is displayed in Figure 2. For lip cancer, a statistically significant decrease in PYLL is projected for men between 5-45 years and 50-60 years, whereas no significant changes are forecast for women. For oral cancer, men show a significant increase in PYLL projections up to 25 years, followed by a significant decline from 25 to 70 years. In women, a significant increase is observed from 0 to 40 years and again from 45 to 65 years. For oropharyngeal cancer, men exhibit a significant projection increase in PYLL up to 25 years, followed by a decline from 25 to 70 years. In women, a modest but statistically significant decrease is projected between 55 and 65 years.

Significant regional inequalities were observed in PYLL projections (Figure 3), with distinct patterns across regions, cancer types, and sexes. In the North, an overall increase in PYLL is projected for lip, oral, and oropharyngeal cancers in both men and women, although the magnitude and age range of these increases vary by cancer type. The Northeast shows a more consistent pattern: no significant changes are projected for lip cancer in either sex, while a significant increase in PYLL is observed across most age groups for both oral and oropharyngeal cancers in men and women. In the Southeast, a significant decrease in PYLL is projected for lip cancer in men, with no meaningful changes in women. For oral cancer, PYLL is projected to increase before 20 years and 65 years and decline between 25 and 60 years in men, whereas a slight increase is observed in women before 30 years and around 55 years. For oropharyngeal cancer, PYLL is projected to decrease in most age groups for both sexes. In the South, PYLL is projected to decline in men across all cancer types. In women, the only significant change is observed for oropharyngeal cancer, with an increase around 45 years, followed by a decrease around 50 years. In the Midwest, lip cancer shows a decrease in PYLL among men before 30 years, followed by an increase between 30 and 45 years; in women, a slight decrease is observed around 60 years. For oral cancer, PYLL is projected to increase in men up to 30 years and again around 50 years, while remaining stable in women. For oropharyngeal cancer, PYLL is projected to increase in men from 0 to 15 years and after 65 years, with a decrease between 20 and 60 years; in women, a decline is observed from 10 to 60 years.

Discussion

Our findings provide new insights into the trends and future projections for PYLL due to LOOPC in Brazil. While age, period, and cohort effects help explain the historical distribution of PYLL, our projections for 2044 offer valuable perspectives on future directions, potentially informing public health strategies. Notably, our projections reveal significant differences by sex and cancer location: among men, PYLL rates for lip cancers are expected to decline substantially in Brazil between 5-45 years and 50-60 years, while oral and oropharyngeal cancers are projected to increase before 25 and decrease between 25 and 70 years. In contrast, oral cancer in women is projected to increase before 40 years and from 45 to 65 years.

Additionally, stark regional disparities were observed. Northern Brazil may experience increasing PYLL trends for lip cancer in both men and women. This region lies closer to the equator and is therefore exposed to higher levels of solar radiation, particularly ultraviolet (UV) rays, a well-established risk factor for lip cancer, reinforcing the association between UV exposure and disease burden.32 The North and Northeast regions may also face increasing PYLL trends for oral and oropharyngeal cancers. Although these regions report lower consumption of tobacco33 and alcohol,34 they are, together with the Midwest, the regions with a higher prevalence of heavy drinkers.35 Other factors may also contribute to the observed projections burden: socioeconomic inequalities, limited access to timely diagnosis and treatment, and differences in healthcare infrastructure in these regions36 can increase premature mortality in these regions. North and northeast regions also report consistently lower HPV vaccination coverage,37 which may also contribute to the higher burden of oropharyngeal cancer.38 In contrast, the southern and southeastern regions show marked reductions in PYLL, which could be explained by the best specialized dental public health observed in the southeast,39 likely reflecting better healthcare access,36 and more effective prevention strategies.

Despite the known role of typical non-genetic etiologic agents of LOOPC,7,15 epidemiological studies have shown that, even after adjusting for these risk factors, social conditions still have a residual effect on the frequency of these cancers.40 A recent survey on lifestyle perception in Brazil indicates that, in 2019, men had a higher percentage of tobacco users (16.2%) than women (9.8%).41 The Brazilian National Tobacco Control Policy, which ensures tobacco-free public environments and regulates advertisements, has contributed to a decrease in smoking rates from 35% in 1989 to 12.6% in 2019.42 Contrary to the trend observed for smoking, alcohol consumption has increased in recent decades among Brazilians. Between 2010 and 2019, the percentage of alcohol consumers increased by 6% among men and 9% among women. Slightly more than one-quarter of Brazilian adults consumed alcohol at least once a week in 2019, with the prevalence among men (37%) being more than twice the prevalence among women (17%).41 The traditional gender differences in smoking and alcohol habits could be a plausible explanation for the sex disparity in PYLL due to LOOPC.

Considering the prevention and screening strategies, it is important that findings should be interpreted in light of the most recent global framework for oral cancer prevention proposed by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC).43 This framework emphasizes that the primary drivers of oral cancer burden worldwide remain tobacco use and alcohol consumption, with strong evidence supporting risk reduction following cessation of these exposures.43 In addition, the IARC highlights the role of secondary prevention through targeted screening, particularly clinical oral examination in high-risk populations, as a potentially effective strategy to reduce mortality. Thus, prevention strategies must be tailored to population risk profiles and health system capacity, which aligns with the substantial regional disparities identified in our study. Therefore, integrating evidence-based primary prevention (e.g., reduction of tobacco, alcohol use, and the expansion of HPV vaccination) with targeted, risk-based screening approaches may be essential to mitigate the future burden of LOOPC in Brazil, particularly in regions projected to experience increasing premature mortality.

The age-period-cohort analysis of PYLL due to LOOPC in Brazil provides an overview of the dynamic nature of these cancers over time. The superior fit of the APC model compared to alternative models (age, age–drift, age–cohort, and age–period) emphasizes the importance of simultaneously considering all 3 factors when evaluating LOOPC trends. The strong age effect observed in this study, where PYLL peaked around age 50 for both sexes before declining, is consistent with established epidemiological patterns.18 This peak reflects the cumulative exposure to major risk factors such as UV rays, tobacco, and alcohol consumption, which typically exert their carcinogenic effects over decades.15 Additionally, in the case of oropharyngeal cancer, the role of HPV infection—particularly among younger populations—is increasingly recognized as a contributing factor.44

The period effect demonstrated notable fluctuations in both sexes and cancer types. For lip cancer, most of the period effect showed a wide confidence interval, with the lowest rates around the year 2000 for men and women. The period effect slight increase in oropharyngeal cancer PYLL among men and more strongly for women after the 2000s was observed. This period mark a well-documented shift in oropharyngeal cancer epidemiology due to HPV,38 and these trends align with global incidences,45,46 in which oropharyngeal cancer had increased in 19 (for men) and 23 (for women) countries of the 42 investigated between 1993 and 2012.47 Rates of oropharyngeal cancer are rising among older men, a trend strongly tied to human papillomavirus (HPV) exposure in specific birth cohorts.47 In contrast, female incidence patterns show greater variation across countries, reflecting diverse regional risk factors—with HPV likely influencing increases in some areas.47 Period effects for oral cancer were less pronounced, likely due to robust public policies against tobacco, which began in the 1980s.48 These measures gained momentum in 1985 with the establishment of Brazil's Advisory Group for Tobacco Control, followed by the launch of the National Program to Combat Smoking in 1986.48

Cohort effects highlight significant generational disparities in cancer risk. For lip cancer, with each more recent birth cohort, the PYLL rate ratio has decreased for both men, while for women continuing to decline until the 1980 cohort, after which it remains stable at very low levels. The decreasing risk in recent cohorts in oral cancer among men and its increase among women suggest shifting exposure patterns in more recent birth cohorts. Historically, men had higher rates of smoking and alcohol consumption, but as these habits have become more prevalent among women in recent decades,34,49,50 their risk of developing these cancers has risen. Conversely, the cohort effect observed in oropharyngeal cancer, particularly the decreasing risk in more recent cohorts, aligns with findings that younger generations are usually the target population of HPV vaccination programs.51

This study has certain limitations inherent to its design and data sources. As an ecological study, it relies on aggregated data, which prevents the establishment of direct causal relationships between risk factors and PYLL. However, such studies are crucial for public health as they help identify population-level trends and inform prevention policies. Variations in the classification of anatomical sites for oral and oropharyngeal cancer across different studies may limit comparability. Despite efforts to ensure data completeness, national mortality databases may still be subject to underreporting or misclassification of causes of death. Nevertheless, the Mortality Information System is the most comprehensive and widely used source for epidemiological research in Brazil, providing a robust basis for long-term mortality assessments. Additionally, the projections presented in this study are based on statistical modeling and cannot account for unforeseen changes in risk factors, healthcare interventions, or advancements in treatment.52 As a result, the estimates may over- or underestimate future trends. Despite this, forecasting studies are valuable tools for anticipating disease burden52 and guiding resource allocation. Finally, while the Age-Period-Cohort (APC) approach helps separate temporal influences, it does not fully resolve the intrinsic dependency between these effects.53 There is no consensus on the best solution for this linear dependency. This study estimates APC parameters using deviations, curvatures, and drift, following Holford’s (1983).31method. This approach analyses linear combinations and curvatures, decomposing the overall linear trend into 2 components: a primary age-related effect and drift, which captures the combined linear influence of period and cohort.

This study is based on aggregated data, and, therefore, is subject to ecological fallacy, meaning that assumptions observed at the population level may not reflect individual-level relationships. Regional estimates may also be affected by aggregation bias, masking important within-region heterogeneity. Furthermore, the forecasting approach relies on historical trends and assumptions that may not fully capture future changes in risk factor distribution, healthcare access, or public health interventions. As such, causal inferences cannot be established, and the results should be understood as indicative of population-level patterns rather than definitive explanations of underlying mechanisms.

Moreover, an additional limitation of this study is the absence of stage-at-diagnosis information, which restricts a more nuanced interpretation of PYLL trends. Because PYLL is strongly influenced by the timing of diagnosis, particularly the distinction between early- and late-stage disease, our findings cannot disentangle whether the observed patterns reflect changes in incidence, improvements in early detection, or advances in treatment and survival. For instance, reductions in PYLL in certain groups could be partially attributable to earlier diagnosis and improved management, whereas increases—especially among younger populations and in underserved regions—may reflect persistent late-stage presentation and barriers to timely care. This limitation is particularly relevant in the context of LOOPC cancers, where prognosis is highly stage-dependent and where delays in diagnosis remain common.54 Although staging data were not available in the mortality-based dataset used in this study, indirect evidence from a regional center in Brazil suggests that a substantial proportion of cases are still diagnosed at advanced stages,55 which may be still more pronounced in regions with limited access to specialized care. Future studies should address this gap by incorporating proxy indicators and complementary data sources. Linkage with population- and hospital-based cancer registries would enable the inclusion of staging data and improve the interpretation of PYLL trends.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. It is the first to project PYLL due to LOOPC, an essential metric for assessing the burden of premature mortality. Unlike crude mortality rates, PYLL highlights the societal and economic consequences of early deaths, emphasizing the need for preventive interventions.16 Moreover, this study analyses one of the longest time series for LOOPC mortality in Brazil, covering over 4 decades. This extended observation period enhances the reliability of trends and projections. By integrating APC modeling and forecasting techniques, the study provides a comprehensive assessment of mortality patterns and future risks, offering valuable insights for policymakers and healthcare planners.

In conclusion, our findings have critical implications for public health. LOOPC-related PYLL in Brazil demonstrates significant age, period, and cohort effects, forecasting a decrease of lip cancer in men, while oral and oropharyngeal cancers are projected to increase before 25 years and decrease between 25 and 70 years for men. In contrast, oral cancer in women is projected to increase before 40 years and from 45–65 years. We highlight substantial gender and regional disparities. The projected PYLL trends for 2044 emphasize the necessity for targeted public health policies to mitigate premature mortality and health inequities.
