Clinically actionable classification tool for childhood and adult atopic dermatitis via explainable AI: a retrospective cross-sectional study optimizing diagnostic accuracy
Authors: Rong Zhou, Weiming Zhang, Lingyue Zhou, Qin Zhu, Jinqing Zhang, Shiyu Wang, Chang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the critical role of early identification in managing atopic dermatitis (AD), current studies are limited by the absence of adult-specific risk stratification models and imbalanced cohort data, hindering effective risk profiling.ObjectiveWe aimed to address this deficiency by developing models for AD diagnostic classification through robust machine learning techniques, with a focus on integrating personalized risk factors to improve the identification and characterization of AD in both children and adults.MethodsThe study employed multiracial cross-sectional data from the National Health And Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) in which children (age < 20) and adults (age ≥ 20) with self-reported AD endpoints were extracted from 1999 to 2006. Comprehensive class balancing and machine learning techniques were employed to develop a set of AD classification models for different age groups. The models were assessed using five-fold cross-validation for internal validation and internal hold-out validations using a test set composed of raw data. The optimal model for each subgroup, with the best accuracy to classify AD presence, was determined and assessed by ACC, F1-score, MCC, and ROC curve. Key features and their contributions to each classification decision are interpreted by permutation feature importance (PFI) and SHapley Additive explanations (SHAP). We compared and further analyzed demographic and nutritional indicators that have significant associations.ResultsOf 22,635 qualified participants, 1118 (13.47%) children and 1732 (12.80%) adults were identified as having AD. The pronounced class imbalance within the dataset led to baseline models yielding accuracies in the range of merely 0.500–0.503. Following the implementation of class balancing techniques and diverse modeling methods, the refined model, utilizing accessible variables from both children and adults, achieved markedly enhanced performance (ACC: 0.835–0.885, F1-score: 0.834–0.884, AUC: 0.913–0.957). Explainable AI revealed opposing effects of dietary antioxidants between children and adults, highlighting age-specific associated mechanisms.ConclusionThe classification models for both childhood and adult AD have demonstrated robust discriminative ability and potential utility for case identification in both internal and internal hold-out validations. These models address the challenge in accurate, individualized classification for childhood and adult AD, demonstrating strong discriminative potential in our validation cohorts. We have developed a novel AD diagnostic identification system specifically for both children and adults based on readily accessible demographic, clinical, and nutritional features, which enable the characterization of individuals with a high probability of having AD and the pinpointing of their associated features. This provides a methodological foundation for future research into early management strategies for AD, which could ultimately aim to minimize adverse impacts and modify disease progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13040-026-00565-x.Keywords: Atopic dermatitis, Machine learning, Classification model, Class imbalance, Explainable artificial intelligence

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic pruritic skin disease that affects approximately 204.05 million people globally [1]. In the USA, the 12-month diagnosed AD prevalence rate is 15.8%-17% for children aged 6 months to 18 years and 7.3% for adults [2, 3], which poses a significant burden on personal quality of life and medical services. In recent years, corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, phototherapy, immunosuppressants, Janus kinase inhibitors, and biologics have become the main treatments for AD, but they may be accompanied by risks such as skin infections, blepharitis or conjunctivitis, head and neck dermatitis, exacerbation of acne, and even malignancies [4–7]. The economic burden, visible symptoms, and disability caused by AD lead to emotional issues that greatly affect the quality of life of patients and their families[8]. Furthermore, AD frequently progresses to other allergic diseases such as asthma and food allergies, a process known as the “atopic march”[9]. Early identification and intervention are crucial for improving treatment outcomes and patient quality of life [10]. However, the manifestations and course of AD are heterogeneous and often difficult to diagnose, particularly in different age groups [11, 12]. Therefore, accurately identifying AD based on currently available characteristics and understanding its associated factors is an urgent problem that needs to be addressed.

The clinical value of machine learning models in classifying disease status is well established, as they analyze large-scale health datasets—from large cohort studies and electronic health records—to extract key risk factors, including transcriptomics, lifestyle, and environmental exposures [13–15]. These models utilize both cross-sectional and longitudinal data to identify features associated with chronic diseases, cardiovascular diseases, and cancers, offering diagnostic probability eastimates for individuals. Although no universally accepted tool for assessing the risk of AD has been developed, many studies have quantified the correlation of one or a group of risk factors through odds ratios. These provide limited insights into the onset of AD. Nevertheless, the precise identification of individuals with AD necessitates the development of classification models that can efficiently consider multiple factors and generate numeric risk scores. Hallit et al. [16] developed the first risk assessment scale for allergic diseases in Lebanese children, considering the multifactorial interactions of environmental exposures and family history. Huang et al. [17] used machine learning methods to predict children’s atopic dermatitis and allergic rhinitis based on prenatal air pollutant exposures and their temporal variations. Dong et al. [18] collected demographic and lifestyle data from Asian birth cohorts through surveys and used machine learning to classify children’s atopic eczema, rhinitis, and wheezing. Landau et al. [19] constructed a predictive model for children AD using electronic health records from the Leumit Health System. These studies employed mathematical models or machine learning methods to predict allergic diseases in children, primarily using accessible variables, supporting personalized AD risk prediction. However, these models currently face several critical limitations: (1) suboptimal diagnostic accuracy, (2) classification imbalance between AD and non-AD classifications, (3) absence of interpretable decision-support frameworks for clinicians, and (4) failure to account for developmental pathophysiology differences between childhood and adult AD manifestations. These extremely skewed class distributions, which are common in clinical data, make it impossible for classifier to learn the intrinsic relationships between features and predictive variables. Although relying on simply synthesizing new data or selectively discarding observational data can generate good performance metrics, they often lead to overfitting and fail to make reliable predictions on real-world samples. More importantly, existing research lacks classification models for adult AD. Given the substantial differences between children and adults in immune system function [20], disease phenotype, lifestyle, and environmental exposures, it is imperative to develop and validate distinct diagnostic classification models to ensure accuracy and relevance. Critically, a dual-cohort approach is essential because AD manifests distinctly across the lifespan, characterized by differing lesion distributions and immune mechanisms. A unified analysis allows for the direct comparison of associated features, revealing age-specific divergences such as contrasting nutritional impacts that are vital for developing targeted management strategies for both children and adults.

In this study, our objective is to accurately classify AD status across diverse age groups using readily accessible variables. This enables the timely identification of individuals with a high probability of having AD and the pinpointing of their associated features, thereby providing a critical evidential foundation for future research into management strategies aimed at mitigating the disease’s impact on quality of life and altering its progression. First, we identify AD and non-AD participants among American children and adults from NHANES 1999–2006 survey and collect potentially relevant demographic, dietary, nutritional, and clinical variables. Given the differences in environmental and lifestyle factors between children and adults, we design specialized classification models for each group. Next, to address potential missing and imbalanced data, we implement a stringent data filtering and imputation scheme and employ diverse data balancing strategies. This approach retains as much of the true data structure as possible while developing and validating the AD classification models. Additionally, we perform multi-faceted model interpretation to analyze the key influencing factors of childhood and adult AD, further elucidating the disease’s prevalence. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use NHANES data to classify AD status in American children and adults and to internally hold-out validate the model on original raw data. Methods

Data source

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a significant survey targeting U.S. residents, aimed at comprehensively assessing the health and nutritional status of Americans. Conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), this survey focuses on two primary populations: children and adults. Utilizing a complex, multistage probability sampling method, NHANES ensures the representativeness of its samples, accurately reflecting the health status nationwide. The survey includes various forms of data collection, such as face-to-face interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests, to gather comprehensive health data. The publicly accessible data can be obtained from the National Center for Health Statistics (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/index.htm). Fig. S1 illustrates the workflow of the study cohort selection. Among the 41,474 participants surveyed between 1999 and 2006, 24,909 reported AD endpoints in dermatology or allergy surveys. This specific time period (1999–2006) was selected because it represents the only continuous NHANES cycles that included a comprehensive dermatology and allergy questionnaire modules that allow for a consistent and rigorous definition of atopic dermatitis, which is not fully available in later cycles. Additionally, this period offers a rich set of environmental and nutritional variables essential for our multi-factorial analysis.

After excluding 2,274 individuals who were pregnant or had a history of cancer, there were 8,297 children (age < 20), including 1,118 with AD and 7,179 without AD; and 14,338 adult participants (age ≥ 20), including 1,732 with AD and 12,606 without AD. For both children and adults, we collected potentially relevant variables from five sections of NHANES data, including demographics, nutritional status, inflammation and immunity, and health status, and incorporated them into the construction of the machine learning model.

Outcome definition

The assessment of AD was derived from the dermatology questionnaire (1999–2004) and the allergy questionnaire (2005–2006). These surveys provide individual household interview data on various skin conditions and common allergic diseases, including hay fever, allergies, and eczema, representing the latest NHANES data available for self-reported AD. In this study, participants who answered “yes” to either “Dermatitis, eczema, rash in past year” or “Have dermatitis, eczema, rash now” were classified as having AD; participants who answered “yes” to both “Ever had itchy rash at least 6 months” and “Itchy rash at any time in past 12 months” were also classified as having AD; and participants who answered “yes” to “Doctor told have eczema” were classified as having AD. Participants who reported “Missing”, “Don’t know”, or “Refused” to all questions were considered to lack AD data, while the remaining participants were classified as not having AD. While these outcomes are self-reported, the inclusion of the specific item ‘Doctor told you have eczema’ serves as a medically validated proxy widely used in epidemiological studies to minimize self-diagnosis error. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that without concurrent physical examination confirmation, this definition represents a history of diagnosis rather than current clinical activity. During the period from 1999 to 2006, 24,909 individuals completed the Dermatology and Allergy surveys, and 3,162 individuals were self-reported AD (12.69%).

Input feature

We extracted 1,175 variables from the Demographics, Dietary, Examination, Laboratory, and Questionnaire data released by NHANES, and 44 clinically accessible features were derived through unification and reconstruction. These features include age, gender, poverty income ratio (PIR), education level, birthplace, household annual income, race, marital status, military service, family members, composite dietary antioxidant index (CDAI), nutritional risk index (NRI), systemic immunity-inflammation index (SII), systemic immune response index (SIR), C-reactive protein, asthma, anemia, arthritis, heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, myocardial infarction, stroke, emphysema, chronic bronchitis, diabetes, depression, hypertension, thyroid health, hay fever, among others. Detailed variable definitions can be found in Table S1.

Unbalanced dataset processing techniques

To address class imbalance, we employed three strategies implemented in imbalanced-learn (0.12.0): (1) Oversampling using Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) [21] and its variants (Borderline-SMOTE [22], ADASYN [23], KMeansSMOTE [24], and SVMSMOTE [25]); (2) Under-sampling using NearMiss (NM) [26], InstanceHardnessThreshold (IHT) [27], and ClusterCentroids; and (3) Ensemble-sampling, utilizing cleaning methods (RepeatedEditedNearestNeighbours (RENN) [28], TomekLinks (TL) [29], AllKNN [28], NeighbourhoodCleaningRule (NCR) [30], and OneSidedSelection (OSS) [31]) to remove noise before synthesis. Baseline models using original data were constructed for comparison. In this study, we applied three class balancing strategies mentioned above to the training sets for children and adults and constructed a baseline model using the original data for comparison. All class balancing algorithms were implemented in imbalanced-learn (0.12.0) [32] and Python (3.10.6).

Crucially, to prevent data leakage, the dataset was randomly split into training and testing sets prior to the application of any data imputation or class balancing techniques. All preprocessing steps, including SMOTE variants and under-sampling, were applied exclusively to the training set. The test set remained composed entirely of unseen, original real-world samples to provide an unbiased evaluation of model performance.

Feature engineering

In this study, all participants were divided into two groups, each with its own unique features. We first removed features with more than 80% missing values from both datasets and performed data imputation on the remaining features. For categorical features, the most frequent value was used for imputation, while for numerical features, the arithmetic mean of the dataset was applied. This deterministic imputation strategy was chosen to maintain the transparency of the preprocessing pipeline. Unlike stochastic methods, which rely on complex assumptions about inter-variable dependencies that may not hold in heterogeneous NHANES data, this approach avoids introducing unverified synthetic variance prior to modeling. The resulting datasets served as the original datasets for constructing baseline models. Subsequently, class balancing strategies were employed to generate balanced datasets for model construction. Before model building, all datasets underwent checks for Pearson correlation coefficients, variance, and collinearity. Numerical features with a Pearson correlation coefficient greater than 0.95 or a variance less than 0.05 were removed, as were numerical features with a variance inflation factor (VIF) greater than 10.

Machine learning models

Ten classification models were constructed, including Decision Tree (DT) [33], Random Forest (RF) [34], Gradient Boosting Tree (GBT) [35], Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) [36], eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGB) [37], Logistic Regression (LR) [38], K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) [39], Support Vector Machine (SVM) [40], Categorical Boosting (CatBoost) [41], and Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost) [42]. We constructed ten classification models: DT, RF, GBT, LightGBM, XGB, LR, KNN, SVM, CatBoost, and AdaBoost. Hyperparameters were tuned for each model to identify the optimal classifier.

To identify the optimal machine learning model for each strategy, we constructed the aforementioned ten classification models for each dataset and performed hyperparameter tuning. The models were then internal hold-out validated using five-fold cross-validation and a test set to compare the best-performing model for each method. Initially, 300 positive and 300 negative samples were randomly selected from the original dataset, which included only real samples, to form a test set of 600 samples. The remaining data were used as the training set to build the Baseline model. After applying three class balancing strategies (oversampling, under-sampling, and ensemble-sampling), additional models were constructed. Thus, the Baseline represents the optimal model identified from ten modeling attempts using the original training set. Model-OS represents the optimal model identified from 60 modeling attempts after applying six oversampling methods. Model-US represents the optimal model identified from 40 modeling attempts after applying four under-sampling methods. Model-ES represents the optimal model identified from 300 modeling attempts after applying combinations of five under-sampling and six oversampling methods. This process was conducted for both the children and adult AD datasets, resulting in the construction of a total of 820 models. All machine learning algorithms were implemented in Python (3.10.6) equipped with scikit-learn (1.2.2), xgboost (2.0.3), catboost (1.1.1), and lightgbm (3.3.3).

Widely used classification performance metrics such as accuracy (ACC), area under the curve (AUC), recall, precision, F1-score, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) were employed for model performance comparison. Model performance was evaluated using ACC, AUC, recall, precision, F1-score, and MCC.

Explainable artificial intelligence

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [43] were used to evaluate the importance and impact of included features on classifying AD and to identify the most direct interactions between key features. The feature-based interpretation method SHAP calculates a Shapley value matrix, structured as samples × features, for new classifications of trained models, where the sign and magnitude of each element indicate the direction and extent of a feature’s influence on the classification of a particular sample. Shapley values are additive, allowing us to determine the relative importance of each feature by summing the absolute values across all samples. Permutation Feature Importance (PFI) [44] is another model-agnostic method for evaluating feature importance. PFI represents the importance of a feature by assessing the change in model performance caused by disrupting the relationship between the feature and the target variable. PFI provides global feature importance with relatively low computational complexity and resource consumption. By using both methods, we can discover the impact of features on AD from different perspectives.

Statistical analysis

Patient data were presented as numerical or categorical variables. Kolmogorov-smirnov test was used to assess whether the data followed a normal distribution. For non-normally distributed numerical variables, the data were described as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and frequencies, and either the Chi-square test (two groups) or Fisher’s exact test (multiple groups) was used for comparisons. All statistical tests were two-sided, with p-values < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed in Python (3.10.6).

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Class balancing strategies enhance the AD classification in children and adults

The Fig. 2 shows how class balancing strategies improved model performance by addressing the class imbalance inherent in the children and adults AD datasets. Baseline, Model-OS, Model-US, and Model-ES are respectively trained using the original training set, balanced training sets processed through oversampling, under-sampling, and ensemble-sampling. Tables 2 and 3 summarizes the results of the detailed performances. Each one has undergone extensive model construction and selection, including comparisons of several sampling algorithms, comparisons of ten classification algorithms, and model hyperparameter tuning. It was observed that in the original training set, due to the extreme class imbalance, the Baseline failed to capture the correct data patterns and intrinsic relationships between AD and the features, leading to the classifier almost entirely classifying the majority class for all test samples (Fig. 2(A and C)). Direct oversampling of the minority class (AD) had limited improvement on model performance, as the generated synthetic samples potentially introduced noise, resulting in models that, despite performing well in training and cross-validation, still misclassified a significant number of cases in the test set (children: FN = 275; adults: FN = 276). Reducing FN classifications could help in the timely and accurate identification of patients in early stages, laying the groundwork for potential future interventions, with the ultimate aim of improving patient quality of life. In contrast, the Model-US significantly enhanced model performance, with the RF and LightGBM models achieving a classification accuracy of 0.835–0.885 in children and adults on internal hold-out validation. Particularly, the Model-US for childhood AD achieved an F1-score of 0.884 in the test set, with precise and balanced classifications across both classes (recall = 0.877; precision = 0.892; MCC = 0.770), substantially reducing false negatives (children: FN = 37; adults: FN = 51), although at the cost of a slightly increased rate of false positive classifications.

In the ensemble-sampling method, the RENN technique was first used to remove 3,687 and 5,807 potential noise and confounding non-AD samples from the children and adult training set, respectively. These data might blur the decision boundary. Subsequently, BorderlineSMOTE and ADASYN were separately employed to generate additional samples in regions with difficult-to-distinguish classification boundaries, enhancing the classifiers’ ability to learn complex decision boundaries. This process resulted in a children AD dataset with 6,984 samples (AD: non-AD = 3,492: 3,492) and an adult AD dataset with 13,630 samples (AD: non-AD = 6,831: 6,799). Compared to Model-US, the ensemble-sampling approach similarly enhanced model performance, with the KNN model showing particularly strong performance. For childhood AD classification, the KNN model achieved 80.00% performance on the internal hold-out test set, correctly diagnosing 70.33% of positive samples, with a FP (misdiagnosis) rate comparable to that of the under-sampling method; in adult AD, it achieved an even lower FP (misdiagnosis) rate. Although the Model-ES still exhibited some classification errors compared to the Model-US, they preserved the maximum number of real samples by removing only a small fraction of outlier data from non-AD participants, resulting in a dataset size slightly smaller than that of the original dataset (Fig. 2B). This may aid in expanding the model’s applicability while ensuring its robustness in large-scale screening.

Interpretation of features associated with childhood AD

We interpreted the contribution and importance of model features in the best model to explain the model’s decisions and identify features associated with childhood AD. Fig. 3(A and B) shows the global impact ranking of PFI and SHAP in the Model-US for childhood AD. The top seven important features identified by both methods were completely consistent, including HR birthplace, PIR, Race, Asthma, Age, BMI, and CDAI. These features showed strong contributions to model classifications of childhood AD status. The most important feature, HR birthplace, represents the birthplace of adult family members of child participants and has multiple values in the dataset. By converting it into one-hot encoding and reconstructing the same model, the impact of each category on classification can be clearly observed (Fig. 3(C)). All three categories significantly impact the model classification. Specifically, “HR birthplace (Born in Mexico)” is associated with a lower probability of childhood AD, while “HR birthplace (Born in US)” and “HR birthplace (Born Elsewhere)” are associated with a higher probability. Similarly, Fig. 3(D) shows the SHAP values after one-hot encoding of Race. Consistent with previous results, “Race (Mexican American)” is an important feature negatively associated with childhood AD. This indicates that both adult family members born in Mexico and child participants of Mexican American ethnicity are associated with a lower probability of having childhood AD. Additionally, a history of asthma or hay fever, higher PIR, lower age, lower BMI, lower CDAI, and female gender are also important features associated with childhood AD. From the perspective of impact on a single sample, a history of asthma exhibited the most influential feature, followed by age and HR birthplace. Besides the aforementioned features, CRP, NRI, CDAI, SII, and SIR also significantly impact the classification of childhood AD. In Fig. 3 (E), the SHAP force diagrams illustrate the model’s classification output and interpretation for two NHANES child participants, showing how various variables interact and influence the final accurate classification of childhood AD. It is worth noting that despite potential conceptual overlaps between socioeconomic and household reference variables such as PIR and HR attributes, all retained features passed the collinearity screening (VIF < 10), ensuring that they provide distinct contributions to the model’s decision-making process

Interpretation of features associated with adult AD

Figure 4(A and B) shows the global impact ranking of PFI and SHAP in the Model-US for adult AD. There is an 80.00% overlap between the top 15 important features identified by PFI and SHAP, with feature PIR, CDAI, BMI, SIR, NRI, HR age, Age, CRP, SII, Family members, Race, and HR education being key features for classifying adult AD. Similar to childhood AD, SHAP analysis identifies the birthplace of adult participants as the most important feature, with “Birthplace (Born in Mexico)” being a significant factor associated with reduced adult AD (Fig. 4(C)). For the feature Race, “Race (Non-Hispanic White)” is the variable with the highest global impact according to SHAP after one-hot encoding, making it a key feature for adult AD (Fig. 4(D)). For the feature PIR and CDAI, higher values mostly correspond to positive SHAP values, indicating that higher PIR and CDAI were associated with higher probability of AD classification in the model. Extremely high or low BMI and lower NRI indicate poor nutritional status, while higher SIR, SII, and CRP levels suggest systemic inflammatory responses, all of which are associated with an increased probability of adult AD. The features with the greatest impact on individual samples are Depression and ChB, indicating that a history of depression and chronic bronchitis significantly correlates with a higher likelihood of AD. Additionally, a history of arthritis, asthma, thyroid issues, diabetes, military service, and stroke also contribute positively to adult AD classification, making them associated features of the disease. Conducted SHAP force analysis on adult AD classifications of two NHANES adult participants showcase the complex interplay between features with positive and negative associations that led to the final correct classification (Fig. 4 (E)). This analysis demonstrates the intricate balance between positively and negatively associated features, ultimately leading to accurate classifications.

To verify that the performance gains achieved by class balancing reflected meaningful feature learning rather than computational artifacts, we compared the SHAP summary plots of the Baseline models against the optimized Model-US (Fig. S2). As illustrated in Fig. S2A (Children-Baseline) and S2C (Adults-Baseline), the models trained on imbalanced data exhibited diffuse feature importance and relied heavily on generic features such as Age, Asthma, or broad socioeconomic proxies (PIR), failing to capture distinct feature profiles. In contrast, the optimized Model-US (Fig. S2B and S2D) displayed robust feature attribution with clear separation between positive and negative associated features. Crucially, the balancing strategy unmasked ‘HR Birthplace’ (Children) and ‘Birthplace’ (Adults) as the dominant indicators, aligning with the strong negative association of Mexican origin identified in epidemiological literature. This shift from generic to specific, clinically relevant associated features confirms that the under-sampling strategy successfully exposed latent clinical signals previously obscured by the majority class, rather than introducing synthetic noise.

Contribution and interaction of demographic characteristics on AD classification

NHANES conducted a comprehensive demographic survey on the study population. We included 23 demographic features in our study, such as age, gender, race, place of birth, education level, and economic level of participants and their family/household reference persons (HR). Some of these features were found to have a significant impact on AD in the model interpretation. Figure 5 (A and F) shows the relationship between important demographic features and endpoints in the original datasets of children and adult AD. SHAP analysis shows that the most important influencing factors in children and adult AD are HR birthplace and Birthplace, respectively, and“HR birthplace (born in Mexico)” and “Race (Hispanic)” are important features negatively associated with AD in children, while“Race (Non-Hispanic White)” is an important feature positively associated with AD in adult AD. This is consistent with the findings of the National Health Information System on the epidemiology of skin allergies in children from 1997 to 2011 [45]. However, SHAP analysis reveals that their impact on the probability of AD classification all participants is not entirely consistent, and in some cases can be opposite. This suggests the presence of interactions. Here, we further investigate the impact of the interaction between birthplace and race of childhood and adult AD through PIR, which has also been found to have a significant impact on AD. Dietary antioxidant intake affects childhood and adult AD differently

CDAI is an indicator that measures individual antioxidant capacity based on dietary antioxidant intake, and can be used to evaluate the comprehensive impact of dietary antioxidants on human health. Due to data limitations in the 1999–2006 survey, only Vitamin C, Zinc, and Selenium were used to calculate CDAI, referred to as CDAI_3. Model interpretation revealed that CDAI had a significant influence on AD classification in both children and adults, but the direction of influence was different. To explore the relationship between dietary antioxidant intake and AD, a more comprehensive CDAI, calculated based on the intake of six dietary antioxidants (Vitamin C, Zinc, Selenium, Vitamin E, Vitamin A, and β-Carotene) from 2001 to 2006, was used. After data curation, there were 808 AD and 3989 non-AD child participants, and 1250 AD and 8873 non-AD adult participants. The same models were constructed and interpreted using SHAP. Figure 6 (A and B) shows the density distribution of CDAI_3 and CDAI_6, respectively. It can be observed that compared to CDAI_3, the distribution of CDAI_3 shows greater differences. The CDAI_6 levels in adult AD and children non-AD are significantly higher than those in adult non-AD and children AD, respectively. With the increasing variety of antioxidants, the AD classification model still exhibits excellent performance, and even shows improvement in classifying childhood AD (Fig. 6 (C and D)). SHAP analysis kept consistent with the previous results, that higher dietary antioxidant intake is associated with a reduced probability of AD classification in children but an increased probability in adults (Fig. 6 (E and F)).

Discussion

Leveraging multiracial NHANES data, we developed and validated machine learning models that accurately classify AD status using accessible demographic, clinical, and nutritional features. These models demonstrated high discriminative ability in both internal and internal hold-out validations, addressing the limitations of previous studies regarding cohort imbalance and adult-specific classification. While the childhood AD model achieved slightly higher performance metrics than the adult model, both outperformed existing tools, likely reflecting the more complex environmental pathogenesis in adults.

We found that in the classification of childhood and adult AD, a wider dataset and higher classification performance do not occur simultaneously. The ensemble-sampling method removed a small portion of noisy or borderline data from non-AD patients, retaining most of the real samples. The size of the dataset was only slightly reduced compared to the original dataset (children: 8297; adults: 14,338). Although Model-ES still has some misclassifications compared to Model-US, it learned more diverse and extensive data relationships between AD and features, making it more robust and applicable in clinical practice. Overall, a reasonable data balancing strategy preserved as much as possible of the real samples while effectively filtering outlier data and adjusting the composition of the dataset. This approach not only optimized the discriminatory performance of the model but also enhanced its ability to accurately classify individuals by AD status. The development of such a performant diagnostic classification model establishes a methodological foundation for future research. These findings provide a robust methodological framework and analytical tool for AD diagnostic profiling and for similar analytical challenges in other medical domains. Notably, although the class imbalance (~1:7) was not extreme, baseline classifiers yielded negligible sensitivity. The superior performance of our under-sampling model (Model-US), which utilizes exclusively real patient data rather than synthetic generation, confirms that improvements were driven by the unmasking of true clinical signals rather than the learning of synthetic artifacts.

Recent research on factors influencing AD has highlighted the significance of prenatal exposure [46], nutritional status [47, 48], and family history [49] as key associated features. Yet, these studies often fall short in offering personalized insights for individuals with AD, which hampers effective prevention and early intervention strategies. To bridge this gap, our study leverages robust explainable artificial intelligence techniques, including PFI and SHAP analysis, to unravel how demographic, nutritional, and clinical features contribute to AD. In our exploration of the optimal model for childhood AD, both methods consistently pinpointed the same seven critical features, reinforcing their credibility. For adult AD, there is an impressive 80% overlap between SHAP and feature importance rankings. Our findings reveal that Race, HR birthplace, and PIR are the most influential features for model classifications, with significant contributions also coming from asthma, age, BMI, sex, CDAI, SIR, and SII. Additionally, factors such as family members [50] and education [51], previously linked to AD, were confirmed as influential. Notably, we discovered that including information about adult family members in the households of child participants provides a more valuable representation of familial genetic background. This comprehensive approach offers a more nuanced understanding of features associated with AD, which is crucial for improving the identification of individuals with a high probability of having AD and informing personalized clinical assessment. Model interpretation highlighted complex demographic interactions. Consistent with previous epidemiology, “Born in Mexico” and “Mexican American” ethnicity were associated with lower probability of AD classification in the model. Notably, we found that higher household income (PIR) modified the model attribution patterns associated with race in classifying AD status, suggesting that economic factors may modulate genetic or cultural associations.

Dietary composition and nutrient status have always been important components in AD-related research, including but not limited to analysis of associated features of maternal prenatal nutrition [52–54], child dietary nutrition [55], and dietary supplements [56], as well as prevention or treatment of AD through dietary adjustments. Given the role of ROS and oxidative stress in the development and treatment of AD [57–59], the impact of antioxidants on allergic outcomes has garnered significant attention. Regarding nutritional factors, expanding the Composite Dietary Antioxidant Index (CDAI) from three to six antioxidants improved model performance. Crucially, SHAP analysis revealed a divergence: higher antioxidant intake was associated with a lower probability of AD classification in children but a higher probability in adults within the model. Previous studies have primarily focused on the role of antioxidants in children AD [60, 61], finding that improving antioxidant nutritional status can reduce AD risk, consistent with our findings. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Huan Yang et al. (2022) [62] found that antioxidant therapy significantly reduced the severity of AD, but this benefit was observed only in children, with no additional benefit for adults. Simoni et al. (2024) [63] suggested that the differential maturity of the immune system and the varying prevalence of inflammatory cytokines in the pathogenesis of AD may make antioxidants more effective in children than in adults. The role of age and the efficacy of combined antioxidant therapy still require further evaluation. These findings suggest that antioxidants may play a more complex role in the onset and progression of atopic dermatitis in adults. Furthermore, the synergistic or antagonistic effects of different types of antioxidants on AD need to be further elucidated to identify the most suitable biomarkers for AD classification.

Despite promising findings, there are still some limitations of our research. First, a significant portion of adult AD may be attributed to occupational exposures [64–67], which are not captured in the NHANES dataset. Second, the AD status used as the outcome variable in our analysis was derived from self-reported questionnaire data. Although the definition relies on items reflecting physician diagnosis, it lacks direct clinical confirmation or biopsy at the time of survey. This reliance introduces potential recall bias and misclassification, particularly for adults recalling childhood onset. Consequently, the model should be interpreted as a screening tool for ‘probable AD presence’ rather than a substitute for clinical diagnosis. Incorporating concurrent allergic diseases such as asthma and allergic rhinitis into future model designs could further mitigate this and enhance clinical significance. In addition, the internal hold-out validation was conducted on a balanced dataset (300 AD vs. 300 non-AD) to strictly evaluate the model’s discriminative ability without majority class bias. Therefore, the reported performance metrics are conditional on this balanced sampling and do not directly reflect expected performance in real-world population screening, where AD prevalence is substantially lower (approximately 7–17%). In particular, precision (positive predictive value) would likely be lower in a general population setting, whereas sensitivity and specificity are prevalence-independent and remain valid indicators of model discriminative performance. Fourth, while we strictly excluded features with excessive missing data (>80%), the remaining variables were processed using mean/mode imputation. This deterministic strategy was selected to maintain transparency and avoid the unverified assumptions regarding inter-variable dependencies required by complex stochastic models. However, we acknowledge that this approach may alter the original distribution of features (e.g., reduce variance, shift central tendency) and could influence downstream feature importance estimates and model interpretation, particularly for nutritional and biomarker variables. Future iterations of this tool should explore advanced imputation techniques to better preserve distributional properties and refine feature fidelity. Fifth, we acknowledge that the use of 1999–2006 data warrants caution regarding temporal relevance. While this dataset was chosen because it includes comprehensive dermatology and allergy questionnaire modules that allow for a consistent and rigorous definition of AD. However, over the past two decades, numerous factors have evolved, including environmental exposures (e.g., air pollution), dietary patterns (e.g., ultra-processed food consumption), lifestyle factors (e.g., screen time, hygiene practices), and clinical diagnostic criteria for AD. Consequently, while stable biological features (e.g., genetic ancestry, immune markers) may remain relevant, the weight and importance of time-sensitive features—particularly nutritional and environmental variables—may differ in contemporary populations. Future studies are necessary to validate these models in contemporary cohorts to confirm their continued applicability. Sixth, we did not perform decision curve analysis (DCA) or formal calibration plots. As this is a retrospective exploratory study without defined clinical intervention thresholds (e.g., treatment initiation or referral), calculating net benefit would rely on arbitrary assumptions and potentially lead to overinterpretation. Furthermore, the models currently focus on feature association identification and relative probability estimation rather than absolute probability estimation. Future prospective studies are necessary to define actionable thresholds and validate calibration before clinical deployment. Finally, the cross-sectional design of NHANES limits our ability to infer causality. While we identified strong feature- AD associations, these should be interpreted as associations. Longitudinal studies are required to confirm whether these factors are truly causative related of AD.

Our study highlights the potential of machine learning models, trained on comprehensive data from nationwide surveys and physical examinations such as NHANES, to accurately classify AD status in both children and adults. These models enable diagnostic classification of AD, offering diagnostic probability estimates for individuals in primary care settings. The timely identification of individuals with a high probability of having AD through such tools represents a critical first step toward exploring strategies for current management. Validated prospectively, this approach could help minimize AD’s impact on quality of life and reduce the likelihood of progression to other atopic conditions. While our current classification models demonstrate robust internal performance within this U.S. cohort, future research is necessary to validate their discriminatory ability for AD and other allergic diseases across diverse populations, which is essential for assessing their generalizability and broader clinical utility.
