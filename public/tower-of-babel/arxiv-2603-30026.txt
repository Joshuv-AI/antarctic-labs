Geometric Properties of Level Sets for Domains under Geometric Normal Property
M. Barkatou

Abstract
This work is devoted to the study of the geometric properties of level sets for solutions of elliptic boundary value problems in domains satisfying the geometric normal property with respect to a convex set C C ( C C -GNP class). We prove that, for the classical Dirichlet problem as well as for the coupled system ℬ ⁡ ( f , g ) \mathcal{B}(f,g) (related to the biharmonic plate equation), the level sets inherit the C C -GNP structure. We establish their star-shapedness property, exact formulas for their mean curvature, and characterize their asymptotic behavior near singular contact points (cusps). We also study the stability of these level sets under the Hausdorff convergence of domains, establishing their convergence in the Hausdorff sense, in the compact sense, and in L 1 L^{1} . The analysis relies on adapted coarea formulas, leading to Faber-Krahn, Szegö-Weinberger, and Payne-Rayner type isoperimetric inequalities. In order to go beyond the purely qualitative framework of the C C -GNP class, we introduce and analyze two new quantitative geometric measures: the thickness function τ Ω \tau_{\Omega} and the convexity gap γ ⁡ ( Ω ) \gamma(\Omega) . We rigorously study their behavior, regularity, and continuity under Hausdorff convergence. These theoretical tools open up new perspectives for shape optimization under geometric constraints, the study of free boundary problems, and the geometric control of latent spaces in machine learning.



1
Introduction

The
C
-GNP class (geometric normal property with respect to a convex set
C
) was introduced by Barkatou
[
1
]
to guarantee good compactness and regularity properties for free boundary problems. A domain
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
has a radial structure with respect to
C
: each point of
\partial C
defines a normal ray along which the free boundary
\partial\Omega
is reached uniquely. A natural question is whether the level sets of the solutions
u_{\Omega}
(defined by
\{x\in\Omega:u_{\Omega}(x)>t\}
for
t\in[0,\max u_{\Omega}]
) inherit similar geometric properties. This question is important because the level sets are involved in the representation of the solution as a layer potential; their geometry controls the regularity of
u_{\Omega}
via coarea-type formulas; they appear naturally in super/subsolution methods for free boundary problems. In this work, we extend these results to the overdetermined problem
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
defined by the biharmonic system
[
8
]
with
f\geq 0
supported in a compact convex set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
g>0
on
\partial\Omega
. This problem, related to the biharmonic plate equation, has a rich structure that we analyze via the geometry of level sets. We analyze in particular the stability of the level sets under the Hausdorff convergence of domains, demonstrating that the convergence of domains implies that of their level sets in several topological and geometric senses. This stability property is essential for variational applications and shape optimization.

Furthermore, existing results on the
C
-GNP class are mainly qualitative. For a more detailed analysis, especially in applications where quantitative measures are necessary (shape optimization
[
5
,
6
]
, machine learning), it is useful to introduce geometric quantities measuring locally the deviation from convexity or the thickness of the domain. We therefore propose two new measures: the thickness function
\tau_{\Omega}
and the convexity gap
\gamma(\Omega)
, and we study their behavior under Hausdorff convergence.

2
Review of the
C
-GNP Class

Definition 2.1

(
C
-GNP)
.

Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a compact convex set with nonempty interior. An open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is said to satisfy the
C
-GNP property if:

1.

\Omega\supset\operatorname{int}(C)
;

2.

\partial\Omega\setminus C
is locally Lipschitz;

3.

for every
c\in\partial C
, there exists an outward normal ray
\Delta_{c}=\{c+r\nu(c):r\geq 0\}
such that
\Delta_{c}\cap\Omega
is connected;

4.

for every
x\in\partial\Omega\setminus C
, the inward normal ray to
\Omega
(if it exists) meets
C
.

We denote by
\mathcal{O}_{C}
the set of open sets satisfying these properties
[
1
]
.

Proposition 2.2

(Radial parametrization)
.

For every
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
, there exists a thickness function
d:\partial C\to(0,+\infty)
such that:

\Omega\setminus C=\{c+r\nu(c):c\in\partial C,\ 0<r<d(c)\},

(1)

where
\nu(c)
is the outward normal to
C
at point
c
. The function
d
is continuous and locally Lipschitz on
\partial C\setminus\partial\Omega
.

Proof.

For each
c\in\partial C
, by property (3) of the
C
-GNP definition, the ray
\Delta_{c}=\{c+r\nu(c):r\geq 0\}
intersects
\Omega
in a connected manner. Define
d(c)=\sup\{r>0:c+r\nu(c)\in\Omega\}
. By property (4), this supremum is finite because the ray meets
C
(hence
\Omega
is bounded in this direction). By construction, every point
x\in\Omega\setminus C
can be written as
x=c+r\nu(c)
with
c\in\partial C
and
0<r<d(c)
(uniqueness of
c
follows from the convexity of
C
and the fact that rays do not intersect). The continuity of
d
follows from the continuity of the function
c\mapsto d(c)
, which is a consequence of the continuity of
\partial\Omega
(property (2)). Finally, local Lipschitz property on
\partial C\setminus\partial\Omega
follows from the fact that
\partial\Omega
is locally Lipschitz and the projection onto
\partial C
along normals is Lipschitz.
∎

3
Structure of Level Sets

Let
u_{\Omega}
be the solution of
-\Delta u=f
in
\Omega
,
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
, with
f\geq 0
supported in
C
. By the strong maximum principle
[
7
]
,
u_{\Omega}>0
in
\Omega
.

Definition 3.1

(Level sets)
.

For
t\in[0,\max u_{\Omega}]
, we define:

\Omega^{t}=\{x\in\Omega:u_{\Omega}(x)>t\},\qquad\Gamma^{t}=\{x\in\Omega:u_{\Omega}(x)=t\}.

\Gamma^{t}
is the level line at height
t
.

3.1
Definition and First Properties

Theorem 3.2

(Radial structure of level sets)
.

For every
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
and for every
t\in[0,\max u_{\Omega}]
, the level set
\Omega^{t}
belongs to
\mathcal{O}_{C}
. More precisely:

1.

\Omega^{t}
contains a neighborhood of
C
;

2.

\partial\Omega^{t}\setminus C=\Gamma^{t}
is a Lipschitz hypersurface;

3.

for every
c\in\partial C
, the normal ray
\Delta_{c}
intersects
\Gamma^{t}
at a unique point
c+d_{t}(c)\nu(c)
with
d_{t}(c)\leq d(c)
;

4.

for every
x\in\Gamma^{t}
, the inward normal ray to
\Omega^{t}
meets
C
.

The thickness function
d_{t}
is given by
d_{t}(c)=\sup\{r>0:u_{\Omega}(c+r\nu(c))>t\}
.

Proof.

We prove each point separately.

1.

\Omega^{t}\supset\operatorname{int}(C)
: Since
f
is supported in
C
and
u_{\Omega}
is subharmonic in
\Omega
, we have
u_{\Omega}\geq\min_{\partial C}u_{\Omega}>0
on
C
by the maximum principle. Hence
C\subset\Omega^{t}
for all
t<\min_{\partial C}u_{\Omega}
. For larger
t
,
\Omega^{t}
may not contain all of
C
, but one can verify that property (1) of the definition is satisfied with
C
replaced by
C\cap\Omega^{t}
. In fact, one can show that
\Omega^{t}
contains a neighborhood of
C
for all
t
.

2.
Regularity of
\Gamma^{t}
: By the implicit function theorem, since
|\nabla u_{\Omega}|>0
on
\Omega\setminus C
(Hopf principle),
\Gamma^{t}
is a
C^{1,\alpha}
hypersurface for almost every
t
. Global Lipschitz property follows from the structure of
\Omega
.

3.
Intersection with normal rays: For
c\in\partial C
, consider the function
\phi_{c}(r)=u_{\Omega}(c+r\nu(c))
for
r\in[0,d(c)]
.
\phi_{c}
is strictly decreasing (by the Hopf principle along the normal), so for every
t
, there exists a unique
r=d_{t}(c)
such that
\phi_{c}(r)=t
. Moreover,
d_{t}(c)\leq d(c)
because
u_{\Omega}=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

4.
Inward normal condition: For
x\in\Gamma^{t}
, let
n(x)
be the inward normal to
\Omega^{t}
(direction of steepest increase of
u_{\Omega}
). Since
u_{\Omega}
is harmonic in
\Omega\setminus C
,
n(x)
is collinear with
\nabla u_{\Omega}(x)
. Following the descending gradient line from
x
, we necessarily reach
C
(because
u_{\Omega}
decreases and vanishes on
\partial\Omega
). This shows that condition (4) is satisfied.
∎

Corollary 3.3

(Monotonicity of thickness functions)
.

The functions
d_{t}
are decreasing in
t
: for
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq\max u_{\Omega}
, we have
d_{t_{1}}(c)\geq d_{t_{2}}(c)
for all
c\in\partial C
. Moreover,
d_{0}(c)=d(c)
(the free boundary) and
d_{\max u_{\Omega}}(c)=0
.

Proof.

If
t_{1}<t_{2}
, then
\{x:u_{\Omega}(x)>t_{2}\}\subset\{x:u_{\Omega}(x)>t_{1}\}
, hence
\Omega^{t_{2}}\subset\Omega^{t_{1}}
. By the radial parametrization, this implies
d_{t_{2}}(c)\leq d_{t_{1}}(c)
. Moreover, by definition
d_{0}(c)=d(c)
and
d_{\max u_{\Omega}}(c)=0
because
u_{\Omega}=\max u_{\Omega}
only at a point (or on a set of measure zero) and certainly not on an entire ray.
∎

3.2
Strict Inclusion Conditions

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
and
u_{\Omega}
be the solution of
-\Delta u=f
in
\Omega
, with
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
and
f\geq 0
supported in
C
. Then, for every
t\in[0,\max u_{\Omega}]
, the level set
\Omega^{t}
strictly contains
C
if and only if

t<\min_{c\in\partial C}u_{\Omega}(c).

Proof.

By the maximum principle,
u_{\Omega}
is strictly positive in
\Omega
and attains its minimum on
C
at a point of
\partial C
. Thus, if
t<\min_{\partial C}u_{\Omega}
, then for every
c\in C
,
u_{\Omega}(c)\geq\min_{\partial C}u_{\Omega}>t
, which implies
C\subset\Omega^{t}
. Conversely, if
C\subset\Omega^{t}
, then for every
c\in\partial C
,
u_{\Omega}(c)>t
, hence
t<\min_{\partial C}u_{\Omega}
.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
(\Omega,u,v)
be a solution of the coupled problem
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
with
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
. Then for the same
t>0
, we have both
C\subset\Omega_{u}^{t}
and
C\subset\Omega_{v}^{s(t)}
if and only if

t<\min_{\partial C}u,\quad\text{and}\quad s(t)<\min_{\partial C}v,

where
s(t)=\min_{c\in\partial C}v(c+d_{u}^{t}(c)\nu(c))
. In particular, if
\min_{\partial C}u>0
and
\min_{\partial C}v>0
, then there exists
t_{0}>0
such that for all
t\in(0,t_{0})
, both level sets strictly contain
C
.

Proof.

The condition for
u
is identical to that of the previous proposition. For
v
, we use the fact that
v
is strictly decreasing along normal rays to
C
(see
Theorem

3.13
). Thus, for every
c\in\partial C
, the value of
v
on
\Gamma_{u}^{t}
at the point
c+d_{u}^{t}(c)\nu(c)
is a strictly decreasing function of
t
. If
\min_{\partial C}v>0
, then for sufficiently small
t
, this value is still strictly positive and hence
C\subset\Omega_{v}^{s(t)}
.
∎

3.3
Structural Role of the Inclusion

Theorem 3.6

(Structural role of the inclusion)
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
be a candidate domain for a solution to an overdetermined problem. The existence of a
t>0
such that
C\subset\Omega^{t}
(or
C\subset\Omega_{u}^{t}
and
C\subset\Omega_{v}^{s(t)}
for
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
) is a necessary condition for the considered level set to inherit the
C
-GNP structure and for variational techniques of symmetrization and comparison to apply.

Proposition 3.7

(Preservation of the radial parametrization)
.

If
C\subset\Omega^{t}
, then
\Omega^{t}\setminus C
admits a radial parametrization analogous to that of
\Omega
. On the other hand, if
C\not\subset\Omega^{t}
, this property is lost.

Proof.

Let
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
. By
Proposition

2.2
, there exists a thickness function
d:\partial C\to(0,+\infty)
such that
Eq.

1
holds.

For the level set
\Omega^{t}
, we define:

d_{t}(c)=\sup\{r>0\mid u_{\Omega}(c+r\nu(c))>t\}.

Case 1:

C\subset\Omega^{t}
. Then for all
c\in\partial C
, we have
u_{\Omega}(c)>t
, so
d_{t}(c)>0
. The function
\phi_{c}(r)=u_{\Omega}(c+r\nu(c))
is strictly decreasing on
[0,d(c)]
because
\phi_{c}^{\prime}(r)=\nabla u_{\Omega}(c+r\nu(c))\cdot\nu(c)<0
by Hopf’s lemma. By continuity, there exists a unique
r=d_{t}(c)
such that
\phi_{c}(r)=t
. Thus, the ray
\Delta_{c}
intersects
\Gamma^{t}
at a unique point
c+d_{t}(c)\nu(c)
. Consequently:

\Omega^{t}\setminus C=\{c+r\nu(c)\mid c\in\partial C,\ 0<r<d_{t}(c)\},

which is exactly the desired radial parametrization.

Case 2:

C\not\subset\Omega^{t}
. There then exists
c\in\partial C
such that
u_{\Omega}(c)\leq t
. In this case,
d_{t}(c)=0
and the ray
\Delta_{c}
does not intersect
\Gamma^{t}
. The radial structure of
\Omega^{t}
is therefore broken.
∎

Proposition 3.8

(Validity of comparison principles)
.

The inclusion
C\subset\Omega^{t}
is necessary to be able to compare solutions of different domains along the same rays originating from
\partial C
.

Proof.

Comparison methods rely on the possibility of comparing solutions along the same rays originating from
\partial C
. Suppose that
C\subset\Omega^{t}
. Then for all
c\in\partial C
, the point
x_{c}^{t}=c+d_{t}(c)\nu(c)
is well-defined and belongs to
\Gamma^{t}
. We can then compare the solutions of two different domains
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
by comparing their thickness functions
d_{t}^{(1)}
and
d_{t}^{(2)}
along
\partial C
. More precisely, if
d_{t}^{(1)}(c)\leq d_{t}^{(2)}(c)
for all
c\in\partial C
, then
\Omega_{1}^{t}\subset\Omega_{2}^{t}
and by the comparison principles for elliptic equations, we obtain order relations between the solutions. If
C\not\subset\Omega^{t}
, some rays do not reach
\Gamma^{t}
. For these rays, the thickness function
d_{t}(c)
is not defined (or is zero), making systematic comparison along all rays impossible.
∎

Proposition 3.9

(Foundation of isoperimetric inequalities)
.

The inclusion
C\subset\Omega^{t}
is necessary for the Steiner symmetrization with respect to
C
to be well-defined and preserve the radial structure.

Proof.

Inequalities of Faber-Krahn, Szegö-Weinberger, or Payne-Rayner type use a Steiner symmetrization with respect to
C
. This operation consists of replacing each level set
\Omega^{t}
with a symmetric set
\Omega^{t*}
of the same measure, whose boundaries are regular perturbations of
\partial C
. For a domain
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
with thickness function
d_{t}
, the symmetrized
\Omega^{t*}
is defined by:

\Omega^{t*}\setminus C=\{c+r\nu(c)\mid c\in\partial C,\ 0<r<d_{t}^{*}(c)\},

where
d_{t}^{*}
is the constant function on
\partial C
defined by:

d_{t}^{*}(c)=\left(\frac{|\Omega^{t}\setminus C|}{|\partial C|}\right)^{\frac{1}{N-1}}.

For this symmetrization to be well-defined, it is necessary that each
\Omega^{t}
contains
C
. Indeed, if
C\subset\Omega^{t}
, then
\Omega^{t}\setminus C
is non-empty and admits the radial parametrization; the measure
|\Omega^{t}\setminus C|
is well-defined; the constant function
d_{t}^{*}
is positive. If
C\not\subset\Omega^{t}
, then
\Omega^{t}
is not star-shaped with respect to
C
and Steiner symmetrization is no longer well-defined.
∎

Proposition 3.10

(Regularity of level surfaces)
.

The inclusion
C\subset\Omega^{t}
guarantees that
|\nabla u_{\Omega}|>0
on
\Gamma^{t}
, an essential condition for the regularity of
\Gamma^{t}
via the implicit function theorem.

Proof.

The regularity of
\Gamma^{t}
is obtained via the implicit function theorem. At a point
x\in\Gamma^{t}
, we have
u_{\Omega}(x)=t
and
|\nabla u_{\Omega}(x)|>0
. The hypothesis
C\subset\Omega^{t}
guarantees that
\Gamma^{t}
is at a positive distance from
C
. Indeed, if
C\subset\Omega^{t}
, then for all
x\in\Gamma^{t}
, we have
x\notin C
and there exists
\delta>0
such that
d(x,C)\geq\delta
. In this region, far from
\partial C
, Hopf’s lemma ensures that the normal derivative is strictly negative and uniformly bounded from below in absolute value:
|\nabla u_{\Omega}(x)|\geq\alpha>0
for all
x\in\Gamma^{t}
. This uniform lower bound guarantees the application of the implicit function theorem and thus the
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity of
\Gamma^{t}
. If
C\not\subset\Omega^{t}
, there can exist points of
\Gamma^{t}
arbitrarily close to
\partial C
. At these points, the normal derivative can vanish or tend to zero, compromising regularity.
∎

Corollary 3.11
.

The strict inclusion
C\subset\Omega^{t}
is therefore a necessary and sufficient condition for the level set
\Omega^{t}
to:

1.

inherit the radial structure of
\Omega
;

2.

allow a systematic comparison of solutions;

3.

be symmetrizable in the Steiner sense;

4.

possess a regular boundary.

It thus constitutes a pillar for the application of variational and geometric methods in shape optimization and in the theory of overdetermined problems.

3.4
Differential Properties of Thickness Functions

Theorem 3.12

(Differential equation for
d_{t}
)
.

For almost every
c\in\partial C
and for every
t
, the function
d_{t}
satisfies:

\frac{\partial d_{t}}{\partial t}(c)=-\frac{1}{|\nabla u_{\Omega}(c+d_{t}(c)\nu(c))|}.

(2)

Proof.

By definition,
u_{\Omega}(c+d_{t}(c)\nu(c))=t
. Differentiating with respect to
t
:

\nabla u_{\Omega}(c+d_{t}(c)\nu(c))\cdot\nu(c)\frac{\partial d_{t}}{\partial t}(c)=1.

But
\nabla u_{\Omega}\cdot\nu=-\frac{\partial u_{\Omega}}{\partial n}=-|\nabla u_{\Omega}|
(since the normal derivative is negative). This yields the result.
∎

3.5
Extension to the Biharmonic Problem
\mathcal{B}(f,g)

Theorem 3.13

(Radial structure for the biharmonic problem)
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
be a solution of the problem
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
. Then:

1.

For every
t\in[0,\max u_{\Omega}]
,
\Omega_{u}^{t}=\{x\in\Omega:u(x)>t\}
belongs to
\mathcal{O}_{C}
with thickness function
d_{u}^{t}(c)=\sup\{r>0:u(c+r\nu(c))>t\}
.

2.

For every
s\in[0,\max v_{\Omega}]
,
\Omega_{v}^{s}=\{x\in\Omega:v(x)>s\}
belongs to
\mathcal{O}_{C}
with thickness function
d_{v}^{s}(c)=\sup\{r>0:v(c+r\nu(c))>s\}
.

3.

The thickness functions satisfy the differential relations:

\frac{\partial d_{u}^{t}}{\partial t}(c)=-\frac{1}{|\nabla u(c+d_{u}^{t}(c)\nu(c))|},\quad\frac{\partial d_{v}^{s}}{\partial s}(c)=-\frac{1}{|\nabla v(c+d_{v}^{s}(c)\nu(c))|}.

4.

On the boundary
\partial\Omega
(corresponding to
t=0
and
s=0
), we have the coupling relation:

g(c)=|\nabla u(c+d(c)\nu(c))|\cdot|\nabla v(c+d(c)\nu(c))|.

Proof.

(1) The proof for
u
is identical to that of
Theorem

3.2
, since
u
is subharmonic and satisfies the Hopf principle.

(2) For
v
, the situation is more delicate because
v
is superharmonic (
\Delta v=-u\leq 0
). We first show that
v
is strictly decreasing along normal rays to
\partial C
. For
c\in\partial C
, consider
\psi_{c}(r)=v(c+r\nu(c))
. We have:

\psi_{c}^{\prime\prime}(r)=\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial r^{2}}=\Delta v-\Delta_{\partial C}v=-u-\Delta_{\partial C}v.

The positivity of
u
and the regularity of
v
do not allow us to conclude strict monotonicity directly. We use a comparison argument. Let
0\leq r_{1}<r_{2}\leq d(c)
. By the maximum principle applied to the function
w(x)=v(x)-v(c+r_{2}\nu(c))
in the domain
\{c+r\nu(c):r\in[r_{1},r_{2}]\}
, one shows that
w
cannot vanish in the interior. The boundary condition
v=0
on
\partial\Omega
and the positivity of
u
ensure that
v
strictly decreases. Once strict decrease is established, uniqueness of
d_{v}^{s}(c)
follows from the implicit function theorem. Continuity and Lipschitz property are obtained as for
u
.

(3) The differential relations are obtained by differentiating
u(c+d_{u}^{t}(c)\nu(c))=t
and
v(c+d_{v}^{s}(c)\nu(c))=s
as in
Theorem

3.12
.

(4) On
\partial\Omega
, we have
d_{u}^{t}(c)=d_{v}^{s}(c)=d(c)
. The overdetermined condition gives the desired relation.
∎

4
Link Between Geometric Conditions and Existence Conditions

4.1
Review of Conditions

Consider the Quadrature Surfaces problem:
\mathcal{QS}(f,g)
: Find
\Omega\subset D
strictly containing
C
such that

\begin{cases}-\Delta u_{\Omega}=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u_{\Omega}=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\
|\nabla u_{\Omega}|=g&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

For the problem
\mathcal{QS}(f,g)
, the existence condition is

\int_{C}f>\int_{\partial C}g.

4.2
Fundamental Link for the Problem
\mathcal{QS}(f,g)

Theorem 4.1

(Fundamental link)
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
be a solution of
\mathcal{QS}(f,g)
. Then the strict inclusion condition (GL) for some
t>0
implies the integral condition (CS). Conversely, if (CS) is satisfied, then there exists a solution
\Omega
of
\mathcal{QS}(f,g)
whose level sets satisfy (GL) for all sufficiently small
t
.

Proof.

Direct direction.
Suppose
\Omega
is a solution of
\mathcal{QS}(f,g)
and that for some
t_{0}>0
, we have
C\subset\Omega^{t_{0}}
. By Green’s formula applied to
\Omega^{t_{0}}
:

\int_{\Omega^{t_{0}}}f=\int_{\Gamma^{t_{0}}}|\nabla u_{\Omega}|.

By the radial parametrization, every point
x\in\Gamma^{t_{0}}
can be written as
x=c+d_{t_{0}}(c)\nu(c)
with
c\in\partial C
and
d_{t_{0}}(c)>0
. The projection
\pi_{t_{0}}:\Gamma^{t_{0}}\to\partial C
,
x\mapsto c
, has a Jacobian
J_{t_{0}}(c)>1
because the level surfaces
\Gamma^{t_{0}}
are strictly convex outward relative to
C
, a consequence of the convexity of
C
and the strict convexity of the level sets of
u_{\Omega}
established by the maximum principle
[
4
]
. Hence:

\int_{\Gamma^{t_{0}}}|\nabla u_{\Omega}|=\int_{\partial C}|\nabla u_{\Omega}(c+d_{t_{0}}(c)\nu(c))|J_{t_{0}}(c)\,d\sigma(c).

By monotonicity of
u
along rays and the overdetermined condition
|\nabla u_{\Omega}|=g
on
\partial\Omega
, for every
c\in\partial C
:

|\nabla u_{\Omega}(c+d_{t_{0}}(c)\nu(c))|\geq|\nabla u_{\Omega}(c+d(c)\nu(c))|=g(c+d(c)\nu(c)).

Moreover, by the monotonicity hypothesis on
g
,
g(c+d(c)\nu(c))\geq g(c)
. Combining these inequalities:

\int_{\Omega^{t_{0}}}f>\int_{\partial C}g.

Since
\Omega^{t_{0}}\subset\Omega
and
f
is supported in
C
, we have
\int_{\Omega^{t_{0}}}f\leq\int_{C}f
. Letting
t_{0}\to 0^{+}
,
\Omega^{t_{0}}\to\Omega
and
\int_{\Omega^{t_{0}}}f\to\int_{C}f
. We finally obtain
\int_{C}f>\int_{\partial C}g
.

Converse direction.
Suppose (CS) holds. By
[
2
]
, there exists a solution
\Omega
of
\mathcal{QS}(f,g)
. We show that for sufficiently small
t
,
C\subset\Omega^{t}
. By continuity of
u_{\Omega}
on the compact set
C
, let
m=\min_{\partial C}u_{\Omega}>0
. Then for all
t<m
, we have
C\subset\Omega^{t}
. Thus (GL) is satisfied for all such
t
.
∎

4.3
Case of the Biharmonic Problem
\mathcal{B}(f,g)

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
be a solution of
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
. If for some
t>0
, we have both
C\subset\Omega_{u}^{t}
and
C\subset\Omega_{v}^{s(t)}
(with
s(t)=v|_{\Gamma_{u}^{t}}
), then condition (CB) is satisfied. Conversely, if (CB) is satisfied, there exists a solution
\Omega
of
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
whose level sets satisfy these inclusions for sufficiently small
t
.

Proof.

Direct direction.
Suppose
\Omega
is a solution of
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
and that for some
t_{0}>0
, we have
C\subset\Omega_{u}^{t_{0}}
and
C\subset\Omega_{v}^{s(t_{0})}
. By the co-area formula for
u
and
v
and the radial parametrization, we obtain:

\int_{C}f=\int_{\partial\Omega}|\nabla u|>\int_{\partial C}|\nabla u|,\quad\int_{\Omega}u=\int_{\partial\Omega}|\nabla v|>\int_{\partial C}|\nabla v|.

Then,

\left(\int_{\partial C}\sqrt{g}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\int_{\partial C}\sqrt{|\nabla u||\nabla v|}\right)^{2}\leq\int_{\partial C}|\nabla u|\int_{\partial C}|\nabla v|.

Combining with the previous inequalities and using the fact that
\int_{\Omega}u=\int_{C}u_{C}+\int_{\Omega\setminus C}u
(with an additional positive term), we obtain (CB).

Converse direction.
If (CB) holds,
[
2
]
ensures the existence of a solution
\Omega
. By continuity of the solutions
u
and
v
on
C
, we have
\min_{\partial C}u>0
and
\min_{\partial C}v>0
. For sufficiently small
t
, we thus have
t<\min_{\partial C}u
and
s(t)<\min_{\partial C}v
, which guarantee the inclusions.
∎

Corollary 4.3

(Hierarchy of conditions)
.

For a domain
\Omega
solving
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
, the strict inclusion condition for the level sets of
u
and
v
is equivalent to the integral condition (CB). Moreover, (CB) implies (CS), but the converse is false in general: there exist data satisfying (CS) but not (CB), for which
\mathcal{QS}(f,g)
has a solution but
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
does not.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from
Theorem

4.2
. For the second, we have already shown in
[
2
]
that (CB) is stronger than (CS).
∎

4.4
Convergence of Level Sets

Theorem 4.4

(Convergence of level sets)
.

Let
\{\Omega_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{O}_{C}
be a sequence converging to
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
in the Hausdorff sense. Let
u_{n}
and
u
be the associated solutions of
-\Delta u=f
with homogeneous Dirichlet condition. Then for every
t\in[0,\max u)
which is not a critical value of
u
, the level sets

\Omega_{n}^{t}=\{x\in\Omega_{n}:u_{n}(x)>t\},\quad\Omega^{t}=\{x\in\Omega:u(x)>t\}

satisfy:

1.

Hausdorff convergence:
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\dH(\overline{\Omega_{n}^{t}},\overline{\Omega^{t}})=0
;

2.

Compact convergence: For every compact set
K\subset D
,
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\overline{\Omega_{n}^{t}}\cap K=\overline{\Omega^{t}}\cap K
in the Kuratowski sense;

3.

L^{1}
convergence:
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}|\Omega_{n}^{t}\triangle\Omega^{t}|=0
, where
\triangle
denotes the symmetric difference.

Proof.

The proof relies on several key ingredients.

Step 1: Convergence of solutions.
By Theorem 3.1 of
[
1
]
, the solutions
u_{n}
converge strongly in
H_{0}^{1}(D)
to
u
. In particular,
u_{n}\to u
uniformly on every compact subset of
D
(by the Rellich-Kondrachov theorem and elliptic regularity).

Step 2: Characterization of level sets by level surfaces.
For every non-critical
t
, the level surface
\Gamma^{t}=\{x\in\Omega:u(x)=t\}
is a
C^{1}
hypersurface (
Theorem

3.2
). Moreover,
|\nabla u|>0
on
\Gamma^{t}
, which allows the application of the implicit function theorem.

Step 3: Convergence of level surfaces.
We first show that
\Gamma_{n}^{t}
converges to
\Gamma^{t}
in the Hausdorff sense. Let
x\in\Gamma^{t}
. By the implicit function theorem, there exists a neighborhood
U
of
x
and a local parametrization
\phi:B\subset\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\to\Gamma^{t}\cap U
. For sufficiently large
n
, the equation
u_{n}(y)=t
admits a unique solution
y_{n}
close to
x
(since
u_{n}\to u
uniformly and
|\nabla u|>0
). Thus, there exists
x_{n}\in\Gamma_{n}^{t}
such that
x_{n}\to x
. Conversely, if
x_{n}\in\Gamma_{n}^{t}
and
x_{n}\to x
, then by continuity of
u
,
u(x)=t
and
x\in\Gamma^{t}
(because
x\in\overline{\Omega}
and cannot be in the interior of
\Omega^{t}
; otherwise
u_{n}(x_{n})>t
would imply
u(x)>t
by uniform convergence). Hence
\Gamma_{n}^{t}\xrightarrow{H}\Gamma^{t}
.

Step 4: Convergence of level sets.
Let
x\in\Omega^{t}
. Then
u(x)>t
. By uniform convergence, for sufficiently large
n
,
u_{n}(x)>t
, so
x\in\Omega_{n}^{t}
. Thus,
\Omega^{t}\subset\liminf\Omega_{n}^{t}
.

Conversely, let
x_{n}\in\Omega_{n}^{t}
with
x_{n}\to x
. By uniform convergence,
u(x)=\lim u_{n}(x_{n})\geq t
. If
u(x)=t
, then
x\in\Gamma^{t}
. By Step 3, there exists
y_{n}\in\Gamma_{n}^{t}
with
y_{n}\to x
. But
x_{n}\in\Omega_{n}^{t}
and
y_{n}\in\Gamma_{n}^{t}
imply that the segment
[x_{n},y_{n}]
is contained in
\Omega_{n}^{t}
(by star-shapedness,
Theorem

5.1
). In the limit, this would contradict the fact that
x
lies on
\Gamma^{t}
(as we would obtain points on both sides). Hence necessarily
u(x)>t
and
x\in\Omega^{t}
. Thus,
\limsup\Omega_{n}^{t}\subset\Omega^{t}
.

Hausdorff and compact convergence then follow from classical properties of the convergence of open sets with convergent boundaries.
L^{1}
convergence is obtained by noting that
\Omega_{n}^{t}\triangle\Omega^{t}\subset\{x\in D:|u_{n}(x)-u(x)|\geq\delta\}
for some
\delta>0
(since
t
is non-critical), and using the
L^{2}
convergence of
u_{n}
.
∎

Corollary 4.5

(Convergence of thickness functions)
.

Let
d_{t}^{n}:\partial C\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
d_{t}:\partial C\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be the thickness functions associated respectively with
\Omega_{n}^{t}
and
\Omega^{t}
(defined by
d_{t}^{n}(c)=\sup\{r>0:u_{n}(c+r\nu(c))>t\}
and
d_{t}(c)=\sup\{r>0:u(c+r\nu(c))>t\}
). Then:

1.

For almost every
c\in\partial C
,
d_{t}^{n}(c)\to d_{t}(c)
.

2.

The convergence is uniform on every compact subset
K\subset\partial C
such that
\overline{K}\cap(\partial\Omega\cap\partial C)=\emptyset
(i.e., away from contact points).

3.

If
t<\min_{\partial C}u
(so that
\Omega^{t}
strictly contains
C
), then the convergence is uniform on the whole
\partial C
.

Proof.

(i) For a fixed
c\in\partial C
, consider the functions
\phi_{n}(r)=u_{n}(c+r\nu(c))
and
\phi(r)=u(c+r\nu(c))
. By uniform convergence of
u_{n}
on compact sets,
\phi_{n}\to\phi
uniformly on
[0,R]
for any
R>0
. Moreover,
\phi
is strictly decreasing (Hopf’s principle). Let
r_{t}=d_{t}(c)
be the unique solution of
\phi(r_{t})=t
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, we have
\phi(r_{t}+\varepsilon)<t
and
\phi(r_{t}-\varepsilon)>t
(if
r_{t}>0
). By uniform convergence, for sufficiently large
n
,
\phi_{n}(r_{t}+\varepsilon)<t
and
\phi_{n}(r_{t}-\varepsilon)>t
. Hence the unique solution
r_{t}^{n}
of
\phi_{n}(r)=t
satisfies
|r_{t}^{n}-r_{t}|<\varepsilon
. This yields pointwise convergence.

(ii) On a compact set
K
avoiding contact points, the functions
\phi_{n}
are uniformly Lipschitz in
c
(by regularity of
u_{n}
and the parametrization). Pointwise convergence together with equicontinuity implies uniform convergence.

(iii) If
t<\min_{\partial C}u
, then
d_{t}(c)>0
for all
c\in\partial C
and we have a uniform lower bound for
|\nabla u|
on the level surfaces. The previous argument then applies globally.
∎

Theorem 4.6

(
L^{p}
convergence of level sets)
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

4.4
, for any
p\geq 1
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{n}^{t}}-\mathbf{1}_{\Omega^{t}}\|_{L^{p}(D)}=0,

where
\mathbf{1}_{E}
denotes the characteristic function of
E
.

Proof.

L^{1}
convergence (i.e.,
|\Omega_{n}^{t}\triangle\Omega^{t}|\to 0
) has been established. Since characteristic functions are bounded by 1,
L^{1}
convergence implies
L^{p}
convergence for any
p\geq 1
.
∎

Remark 4.7

(Case of critical values)
.

If
t
is a critical value of
u
(i.e., there exists
x\in\Omega
with
u(x)=t
and
\nabla u(x)=0
), the convergence may be more delicate. The level set may then have singularities. However, by Sard’s theorem, the set of critical values has measure zero, so for almost every
t
, convergence holds in the strong sense.

Remark 4.8

(Stability of strict inclusion)
.

Under the convergence hypotheses, if
\Omega
strictly contains
C
(i.e.,
\min_{\partial C}d(c)>0
), then for sufficiently large
n
,
\Omega_{n}
also strictly contains
C
. Moreover, if for some
t>0
we have
C\subset\Omega^{t}
(i.e.,
\min_{\partial C}d_{t}>0
), then for sufficiently large
n
,
C\subset\Omega_{n}^{t}
.

5
Geometry of Level Lines

5.1
Star-shapedness with Respect to
C

Theorem 5.1

(Star-shapedness of level sets)
.

For every
t
, the set
\Omega^{t}
is star-shaped with respect to
C
, i.e., for every
x\in\Omega^{t}
and every
c\in C
, the segment
[c,x]
is contained in
\Omega^{t}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\Omega^{t}
and
c\in C
. Consider the parametrized segment
\gamma(s)=(1-s)c+sx
for
s\in[0,1]
. The function
h(s)=u_{\Omega}(\gamma(s))
is subharmonic along the segment. By the maximum principle, its minimum is attained at the endpoints. Since
u_{\Omega}(c)\geq\varepsilon>0
and
u_{\Omega}(x)>t
, we have
h(s)>t
for all
s
, hence
\gamma(s)\in\Omega^{t}
.
∎

5.2
Curvature of Level Lines

For a point
x\in\Gamma^{t}
, denote by
\kappa_{t}(x)
the mean curvature of
\Gamma^{t}
at
x
.

Theorem 5.2

(Curvature variation formula)
.

For almost every
t
, the mean curvature of
\Gamma^{t}
is given by
[
3
]
:

\kappa_{t}(x)=\frac{\Delta u_{\Omega}(x)}{(N-1)|\nabla u_{\Omega}(x)|}+\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\left(\frac{1}{|\nabla u_{\Omega}|}\right)(x),

(3)

where
n
is the unit normal to
\Gamma^{t}
pointing into
\Omega^{t}
.

Proof.

This is a consequence of the classical formula for the curvature of level lines of a harmonic function. For a
C^{2}
function
u
, the mean curvature of the level line
\{u=t\}
is given by:

\kappa=\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}\right).

A direct computation gives:

\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}\right)=\frac{\Delta u}{|\nabla u|}-\frac{\nabla^{2}u(\nabla u,\nabla u)}{|\nabla u|^{3}}.

Using the equation
-\Delta u=f
and the expression for the second normal derivative, we obtain the formula of the theorem.
∎

Corollary 5.3

(Bound on curvature)
.

Under the hypotheses of the main article (
f
bounded,
g
bounded below), the curvature of
\Gamma^{t}
is uniformly bounded for
t
in an interval
[\varepsilon,\max u_{\Omega}-\varepsilon]
.

Theorem 5.4

(Curvature of level lines of
v
)
.

For the problem
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
, the mean curvature
\kappa_{s}
of the level lines of
v
is given by:

\kappa_{s}(x)=-\frac{u(x)}{(N-1)|\nabla v(x)|}+\frac{\partial}{\partial n_{v}}\left(\frac{1}{|\nabla v|}\right)(x),

where
n_{v}
is the unit normal to
\{v=s\}
pointing into
\Omega_{v}^{s}
.

Proof.

Apply the general curvature formula to the function
v
:

\kappa_{s}=\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\nabla v}{|\nabla v|}\right)=\frac{\Delta v}{|\nabla v|}-\frac{\nabla^{2}v(\nabla v,\nabla v)}{|\nabla v|^{3}}.

Using the equation
-\Delta v=u
, i.e.,
\Delta v=-u
, we obtain:

\kappa_{s}=-\frac{u}{|\nabla v|}-\frac{\nabla^{2}v(\nabla v,\nabla v)}{|\nabla v|^{3}}.

The second term can be rewritten as
\frac{\partial}{\partial n_{v}}(1/|\nabla v|)
, whence the result.
∎

5.3
Asymptotic Behavior Near the Boundary of
C

Theorem 5.5

(Asymptotic expansion near
\partial C
)
.

Let
c_{0}\in\partial\Omega\cap\partial C
. Then for sufficiently small
t
, the level line
\Gamma^{t}
near
c_{0}
is given by:

d_{t}(c)=d(c)-\frac{t}{|\nabla u_{\Omega}(c_{0})|}+o(|c-c_{0}|^{2}+t).

More precisely, there exists a constant
K>0
such that:

\left|d_{t}(c)-d(c)+\frac{t}{|\nabla u_{\Omega}(c_{0})|}\right|\leq K(|c-c_{0}|^{2}+t^{2}).

Proof.

Use Taylor expansion of
u_{\Omega}
near
c_{0}
and the geometric confinement of cusps. Near
c_{0}
, we have:

u_{\Omega}(c+r\nu(c))=|\nabla u_{\Omega}(c_{0})|(d(c)-r)+O(|c-c_{0}|^{2}+(d(c)-r)^{2}).

Setting
u_{\Omega}=t
and
r=d_{t}(c)
, we obtain:

t=|\nabla u_{\Omega}(c_{0})|(d(c)-d_{t}(c))+O(|c-c_{0}|^{2}+(d(c)-d_{t}(c))^{2}).

Inverting this relation yields the announced expansion.
∎

Corollary 5.6

(Tangency of level lines)
.

The level lines
\Gamma^{t}
are tangent to
\partial C
at contact points with
\partial\Omega
, with quadratic detachment:

d_{t}(c)\sim A|c-c_{0}|^{2}+Bt.

Theorem 5.7

(Asymptotic behavior for
v
)
.

Let
c_{0}\in\partial\Omega\cap\partial C
. For the problem
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
, the level line
\{v=s\}
near
c_{0}
satisfies:

d_{v}^{s}(c)=d(c)-\frac{s}{|\nabla v(c_{0})|}+\frac{u(c_{0})}{2|\nabla v(c_{0})|^{3}}s^{2}+o(|c-c_{0}|^{2}+s^{2}).

Proof.

Use Taylor expansion of
v
near
c_{0}
. In normal coordinate
r
along the ray, we have:

v(c+r\nu(c))=|\nabla v(c_{0})|(d(c)-r)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial r^{2}}(d(c)-r)^{2}+O(|c-c_{0}|^{2}+(d(c)-r)^{3}).

But
\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial r^{2}}=\Delta v-\Delta_{\partial C}v=-u-\Delta_{\partial C}v
. At the point
c_{0}
(on
\partial C\cap\partial\Omega
), we have
\Delta_{\partial C}v=0
because
v=0
on
\partial\Omega
and
\partial C
is a regular surface. Hence
\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial r^{2}}(c_{0})=-u(c_{0})
. Thus:

v(c+r\nu(c))=|\nabla v(c_{0})|(d(c)-r)-\frac{u(c_{0})}{2}(d(c)-r)^{2}+O(|c-c_{0}|^{2}+(d(c)-r)^{3}).

Setting
v=s
and
r=d_{v}^{s}(c)
, we obtain a quadratic equation in
\delta=d(c)-d_{v}^{s}(c)
:

s=|\nabla v(c_{0})|\delta-\frac{u(c_{0})}{2}\delta^{2}+O(|c-c_{0}|^{2}+\delta^{3}).

Inverting this relation yields:

\delta=\frac{s}{|\nabla v(c_{0})|}+\frac{u(c_{0})}{2|\nabla v(c_{0})|^{3}}s^{2}+o(|c-c_{0}|^{2}+s^{2}).

∎

Co-area Formula and Consequences

Theorem 6.1

(Co-area formula)
.

For any measurable function
\phi
on
\Omega
, we have

\int_{\Omega}\phi(x)|\nabla u(x)|\,dx=\int_{0}^{\max u}\left(\int_{\Gamma^{t}}\phi(x)\,d\sigma(x)\right)dt.

(4)

Proof.

This is a standard co-area formula for Lipschitz functions; see
[
4
]
.
∎

Corollary 6.2

(Co-area for
f
)
.

We have

\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\max u}\left(\int_{\Gamma^{t}}\frac{f(x)}{|\nabla u(x)|}\,d\sigma(x)\right)dt.

Proof.

Apply the co-area formula with
\phi=f/|\nabla u|
and use that
f
vanishes outside
C
.
∎

Corollary 6.3

(Isoperimetric inequality for level lines)
.

For almost every
t
, we have:

|\Gamma^{t}|\geq\frac{\int_{\Omega^{t}}f}{\max_{\Gamma^{t}}|\nabla u_{\Omega}|}.

Proof.

By Green’s formula applied to
\Omega^{t}
:

\int_{\Omega^{t}}f=\int_{\Gamma^{t}}|\nabla u_{\Omega}|\geq|\Gamma^{t}|\min_{\Gamma^{t}}|\nabla u_{\Omega}|.

Since
\min_{\Gamma^{t}}|\nabla u_{\Omega}|\leq|\nabla u_{\Omega}(x)|\leq\max_{\Gamma^{t}}|\nabla u_{\Omega}|
for all
x\in\Gamma^{t}
, we have:

\int_{\Omega^{t}}f\leq|\Gamma^{t}|\max_{\Gamma^{t}}|\nabla u_{\Omega}|,

which gives the desired inequality.
∎

Theorem 6.4

(Co-area formula for the
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
system)
.

For the problem
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
, we have the two relations:

\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\max u}\left(\int_{\Gamma_{u}^{t}}\frac{f(x)}{|\nabla u(x)|}\,d\sigma(x)\right)dt,

\int_{\Omega}u(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\max v}\left(\int_{\Gamma_{v}^{s}}\frac{u(x)}{|\nabla v(x)|}\,d\sigma(x)\right)ds.

Proof.

The first relation is identical to
Corollary

6.2
. For the second, apply the co-area formula to the function
v
with
\phi=u/|\nabla v|
:

\int_{\Omega}u(x)\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\frac{u(x)}{|\nabla v(x)|}|\nabla v(x)|\,dx=\int_{0}^{\max v}\left(\int_{\Gamma_{v}^{s}}\frac{u(x)}{|\nabla v(x)|}\,d\sigma(x)\right)ds.

∎

7.2
Coupled Bernoulli Problems for
\mathcal{B}(f,g)

Theorem 7.3

(Estimate for
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
)
.

If
\Omega
is a solution of
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
with

constant on

and radial

, then:

d(c)=\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{u_{\Omega}(c)}\frac{ds}{|\nabla v|(c+d_{s}^{c}(c)\nu(c))}\cdot\int_{0}^{u_{\Omega}(c)}\frac{dt}{|\nabla u|(c+d_{u}^{t}(c)\nu(c))}.

Theorem 7.4

(Nested Bernoulli problem for
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
)
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
be a solution of the problem
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
. Then for every
t\in[0,\max u]
, the domain
\Omega_{u}^{t}
is a solution of a coupled Bernoulli problem:

\frac{\partial u}{\partial n_{t}}=g_{t}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{u}^{t},

where
n_{t}
is the unit outward normal to
\Omega_{u}^{t}
and
g_{t}
is defined by:

g_{t}(c+d_{t}^{u}(c)\nu(c))=\left(-\frac{\partial d_{t}^{u}}{\partial t}(c)\right)^{-1}\cdot\left(-\frac{\partial d_{s}^{u(t)}}{\partial s}(c)\right)^{-1},

with
s(t)=v(c+d_{t}^{u}(c)\nu(c))
.

Proof.

Let
x=c+d_{t}^{u}(c)\nu(c)\in\Gamma_{u}^{t}
. By construction,
u(x)=t
. Moreover,
v(x)=s
for some
s=s(t,c)
. The differential relations of
Theorem

3.13
give:

\frac{\partial d_{t}^{u}}{\partial t}(c)=-\frac{1}{|\nabla u(x)|},\quad\frac{\partial d_{s}^{v}}{\partial s}(c)=-\frac{1}{|\nabla v(x)|}.

Hence:

|\nabla u(x)|\cdot|\nabla v(x)|=\left(-\frac{\partial d_{t}^{u}}{\partial t}(c)\right)^{-1}\cdot\left(-\frac{\partial d_{s}^{v}}{\partial s}(c)\right)^{-1}=g_{t}(x).

This is exactly the overdetermined condition on
\Gamma_{u}^{t}
.
∎

Theorem 7.5

(Hierarchy of Bernoulli problems for
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
)
.

For the problem
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
, the domains
\Omega_{u}^{t}
and
\Omega_{v}^{s}
are solutions of coupled Bernoulli problems. More precisely, there exist functions
g_{t}^{u}
and
g_{s}^{v}
such that:

|\nabla u|=g_{t}^{u}\text{ on }\Gamma_{u}^{t},\quad|\nabla v|=g_{s}^{v}\text{ on }\Gamma_{s}^{v},

with the coupling relation
g_{t}^{u}\cdot g_{s}^{v}=h_{t,s}
where
h_{t,s}
is determined by the initial condition
g
and the geometry.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of
Theorem

7.4
. Setting
g_{t}^{u}=|\nabla u|
on
\Gamma_{u}^{t}
and
g_{s}^{v}=|\nabla v|
on
\Gamma_{v}^{s}
, we have
g_{t}^{u}\cdot g_{s}^{v}=g_{t}
where
g_{t}
is defined as in
Theorem

7.4
. This quantity is constant along rays, whence the result.
∎

Theorem 7.6

(Variational characterization for
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
)
.

\Omega
is a solution of
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
if and only if for every variation of the thickness function
d
that preserves
C
, we have:

\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}\mathcal{E}(d_{\varepsilon})=\int_{\partial C}g^{2}\delta d\,d\sigma,

where the energy
\mathcal{E}(d)
is defined by:

\mathcal{E}(d)=\int_{\partial C}\left(\int_{0}^{d(c)}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla v|^{2}-fu\right)dr\right)d\sigma(c),

and
(u,v)
is the solution of the Dirichlet system in the domain associated with
d
.

Proof.

The proof relies on the first variation of energy functionals with respect to boundary variations. For a variation
\delta d
of the thickness function, the variation of the total energy is:

\delta\mathcal{E}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla v|^{2}\right)\delta n\,d\sigma,

where
\delta n
is the normal displacement of the boundary. Parametrizing
\partial\Omega
by
(c,d(c))
and using the coupling condition
|\nabla u||\nabla v|=g
, we obtain after computation:

\delta\mathcal{E}=\int_{\partial C}g^{2}\delta d\,d\sigma.

The extremality condition
\delta\mathcal{E}=\int_{\partial C}g^{2}\delta d\,d\sigma
for all variations
\delta d
therefore characterizes critical points of the functional, which correspond to solutions of
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
.
∎

8
Isoperimetric Inequalities for Level Sets

8.1
Faber-Krahn Inequality for
\mathcal{QS}(f,g)

Theorem 8.1

(Faber-Krahn inequality for level sets)
.

Among all domains
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
with fixed volume, the domain that minimizes the first eigenvalue of the Dirichlet Laplacian is the one whose level lines are surfaces parallel to
\partial C
(i.e.,
d(c)=\text{const}
).

Proof.

Let
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
be a domain with fixed volume
V
. The proof relies on the principle of radial Steiner symmetrization with respect to
C
. We introduce the symmetrized domain
\Omega^{*}
parallel to
C
with the same volume. By the Polya-Szegö inequality, the Dirichlet integral decreases under symmetrization. Equality forces the level lines to be surfaces parallel to
C
, which proves the result for the minimizer.

More precisely, let
u_{\Omega}
be the first eigenfunction normalized in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. By Steiner symmetrization, we construct
u^{*}
radial on
\Omega^{*}
such that:

•

For all
t\geq 0
,
|\{x\in\Omega^{*}:u^{*}(x)>t\}|=|\{x\in\Omega:u_{\Omega}(x)>t\}|
;

•

u^{*}
is decreasing along normal rays to
\partial C
;

•

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{\Omega}|^{2}\,dx\geq\int_{\Omega^{*}}|\nabla u^{*}|^{2}\,dx
(Polya-Szegö inequality).

By construction,
u^{*}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega^{*})
with
\|u^{*}\|_{L^{2}}=\|u_{\Omega}\|_{L^{2}}=1
. Hence:

\lambda_{1}(\Omega)=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{\Omega}|^{2}\,dx\geq\int_{\Omega^{*}}|\nabla u^{*}|^{2}\,dx\geq\lambda_{1}(\Omega^{*}).

Thus, for every
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
with fixed volume,
\lambda_{1}(\Omega)\geq\lambda_{1}(\Omega^{*})
. Equality holds if and only if all intermediate inequalities are equalities, which requires that the level lines
\Gamma^{t}
be surfaces parallel to
\partial C
and that
u_{\Omega}
be radial, i.e.,
d(c)
constant on
\partial C
.
∎

Corollary 8.2

(Upper bound for
u
)
.

For every
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
, we have:

\int_{\Omega}u_{\Omega}\,dx\leq\frac{|\partial C|}{N}\int_{\partial C}d(c)\,d\sigma(c),

with equality if and only if
d(c)
is constant.

Proof.

By the co-area formula, we have:

\int_{\Omega}u_{\Omega}(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\max u_{\Omega}}|\Omega^{t}|\,dt.

For every
t
,
\Omega^{t}\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
with thickness function
d_{t}
. Using the radial parametrization, the volume of
\Omega^{t}
can be written as:

|\Omega^{t}|=\int_{\partial C}\int_{0}^{d_{t}(c)}\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}(1-\kappa_{i}(c)r)\,dr\,d\sigma(c)\leq\int_{\partial C}d_{t}(c)\,d\sigma(c)\leq|\partial C|\cdot\max_{c\in\partial C}d_{t}(c).

Integrating this inequality from
0
to
\max u_{\Omega}
, we obtain:

\int_{\Omega}u_{\Omega}(x)\,dx\leq|\partial C|\int_{0}^{\max u_{\Omega}}\max_{c\in\partial C}d_{t}(c)\,dt.

By
Theorem

3.12
, for each
c\in\partial C
, the function
t\mapsto d_{t}(c)
is strictly decreasing and satisfies
\frac{\partial d_{t}}{\partial t}\leq-1/M
where
M=\max_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{\Omega}|
. Moreover, using the radial parametrization and the fact that the curvature terms
\kappa_{i}(c)
are non-negative for convex
C
, a finer analysis using the Hardy-Littlewood-Pólya inequality yields:

\int_{0}^{\max u_{\Omega}}\max_{c\in\partial C}d_{t}(c)\,dt\leq\frac{1}{N}\int_{\partial C}d(c)\,d\sigma(c).

Combining these estimates gives the desired inequality. The equality case corresponds to
d(c)
constant.
∎

8.2
Inequalities for the Biharmonic Problem
\mathcal{B}(f,g)

Theorem 8.3

(Szegö-Weinberger type inequality for
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
)
.

Among all domains
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
with fixed volume, the domain that minimizes the first eigenvalue of the clamped plate bilaplacian:

\Lambda_{1}(\Omega)=\inf_{w\in H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\Delta w|^{2}\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}w^{2}\,dx},

is the one whose level lines of the eigenfunction are surfaces parallel to
\partial C
(i.e.,
d(c)=\text{const}
).

Proof.

Let
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
be a domain with fixed volume
V
. The first eigenfunction
w
of the clamped plate bilaplacian (
w=|\nabla w|=0
on
\partial\Omega
) satisfies
\Delta^{2}w=\Lambda_{1}(\Omega)w
in
\Omega
. We decompose this problem by setting
u=-\Delta w
. Then
(u,w)
satisfies

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\Lambda_{1}(\Omega)w&\text{in }\Omega,\\
-\Delta w=u&\text{in }\Omega,\\
w=|\nabla w|=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

This system is analogous to
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
with
f=\Lambda_{1}(\Omega)w
.

Let
\Omega^{*}
be the radial symmetrization of
\Omega
(domain parallel to
C
with the same volume). By Steiner symmetrization adapted to the biharmonic operator, we can construct radial functions
u^{*}
and
w^{*}
on
\Omega^{*}
such that:

•

For all
t\geq 0
,
|\{x\in\Omega^{*}:w^{*}(x)>t\}|=|\{x\in\Omega:w(x)>t\}|
;

•

w^{*}
is decreasing along normal rays to
\partial C
;

•

\int_{\Omega}|\Delta w|^{2}\,dx\geq\int_{\Omega^{*}}|\Delta w^{*}|^{2}\,dx
(Polya-Szegö inequality for the bilaplacian).

By construction,
w^{*}\in H_{0}^{2}(\Omega^{*})
with
\|w^{*}\|_{L^{2}}=\|w\|_{L^{2}}
. Hence:

\Lambda_{1}(\Omega)=\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\Delta w|^{2}\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}w^{2}\,dx}\geq\frac{\int_{\Omega^{*}}|\Delta w^{*}|^{2}\,dx}{\int_{\Omega^{*}}(w^{*})^{2}\,dx}\geq\Lambda_{1}(\Omega^{*}).

Equality holds if and only if all intermediate inequalities are equalities, which forces
w
to be radial and its level lines to be surfaces parallel to
\partial C
, i.e.,
d(c)
constant.
∎

Theorem 8.4

(Payne-Rayner type inequality for
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
)
.

For any solution
(u,v)
of the problem
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
with
f
constant on
C
, we have:

\int_{\Omega}v(x)\,dx\leq\frac{|\partial C|}{2N}\int_{\partial C}d(c)^{2}\,d\sigma(c),

with equality if and only if
d(c)
is constant.

Proof.

Use the co-area formula for
v
and the relation between
u
and
v
.

(1) By the co-area formula, we have:

\int_{\Omega}v\,dx=\int_{0}^{\max v}|\Omega_{v}^{s}|\,ds.

(2) By
Theorem

3.13
,
\Omega_{v}^{s}\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
with thickness function
d_{v}^{s}
. Using the radial parametrization:

|\Omega_{v}^{s}|=\int_{\partial C}\int_{0}^{d_{v}^{s}(c)}\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}(1-\kappa_{i}(c)r)\,dr\,d\sigma(c)\leq\int_{\partial C}d_{v}^{s}(c)\,d\sigma(c).

(3) The relation between
u
and
v
gives, for constant
f
:

u(c+r\nu(c))=f\int_{r}^{d(c)}\frac{d\rho}{|\nabla u(c+\rho\nu(c))|}\quad\text{(approximation)}.

Integrating and using monotonicity, one shows that:

\int_{0}^{\max v}\max_{c}d_{v}^{s}(c)\,ds\leq\frac{1}{2N}\int_{\partial C}d(c)^{2}\,d\sigma(c).

(4) Combining these estimates yields the desired inequality.

(5) The equality case corresponds to
d(c)
constant, which saturates all intermediate inequalities.
∎

9
Applications to Free Boundary Problems

9.1
Comparison Principles

Theorem 9.1

(Comparison principle via level sets)
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
with
\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega_{2}
. Then for every
t
,

\Gamma_{1}^{t}\subset\Gamma_{2}^{t^{\prime}}\quad\text{with }t^{\prime}\geq t,

where
\Gamma_{i}^{t}
is the level line
t
of
u_{\Omega_{i}}
.

Proof.

By the maximum principle,
u_{\Omega_{1}}\leq u_{\Omega_{2}}
in
\Omega_{1}
. Hence if
x\in\Gamma_{1}^{t}
, then
u_{\Omega_{1}}(x)=t
, so
u_{\Omega_{2}}(x)\geq t
, which means that
x\in\Omega_{2}^{t}
. Since
x
is in the interior of
\Omega_{2}^{t}
, there exists
t^{\prime}\geq t
such that
x\in\Gamma_{2}^{t^{\prime}}
.
∎

Theorem 9.2

(Comparison principle for
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
)
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
be two solutions of
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
with
\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega_{2}
. Then for the associated thickness functions, we have
d_{1}(c)\leq d_{2}(c)
for every
c\in\partial C
.

Proof.

By the maximum principle for the system, we have
u_{1}\leq u_{2}
and
v_{1}\leq v_{2}
in
\Omega_{1}
. On
\partial\Omega_{1}
, we have
u_{1}=0
and
u_{2}\geq 0
, so
u_{2}\geq 0=u_{1}
. By the Hopf lemma, we obtain
\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial n}\leq\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial n}
on
\partial\Omega_{1}
. Similarly for
v
. The overdetermined condition then gives a relation between the thickness functions, leading to
d_{1}\leq d_{2}
.
∎

10
Quantitative Geometric Measures for
C
-GNP Domains

10.1
Definitions and Properties

Definition 10.1

(Thickness function)
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
be a domain containing
C
. For any point
x\in\partial\Omega
where the interior normal
\nu(x)
exists, we define the thickness function
\tau_{\Omega}(x)
by:

\tau_{\Omega}(x)=\sup\{t>0:x+t\nu(x)\in\Omega\setminus C\}.

If the normal does not exist, we set
\tau_{\Omega}(x)=0
.

Remark 10.2
.

C
-GNP implies that the half-line
x+t\nu(x)
meets
C
. Thus,
\tau_{\Omega}(x)
is exactly the distance from
x
to
C
along the normal before entering
C
.

Proposition 10.3

(Properties of
\tau_{\Omega}
)
.

For
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
:

1.

\tau_{\Omega}
is defined almost everywhere on
\partial\Omega
.

2.

\tau_{\Omega}(x)\geq d(x,C)
(Euclidean distance).

3.

If
x\in\partial\Omega\setminus C
,
\tau_{\Omega}(x)
is finite and positive.

4.

If
x\in\partial\Omega\cap\partial C
,
\tau_{\Omega}(x)=0
(cusp points).

Proof.

(1) By Proposition 2.4 of
[
1
]
, the interior normal exists
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-almost everywhere on
\partial\Omega\setminus C
. On
\partial\Omega\cap\partial C
, although the normal may not exist, we set
\tau_{\Omega}(x)=0
by definition.

(2) For all
t<\tau_{\Omega}(x)
, we have
x+t\nu(x)\in\Omega\setminus C
. By the property of the normal, the Euclidean distance
d(x,C)
is the length of the segment from
x
to
C
along the normal. Since
C
is convex, the point
x+t\nu(x)
belongs to
C
only when
t\geq d(x,C)
. Hence
\tau_{\Omega}(x)\geq d(x,C)
.

(3) For
x\in\partial\Omega\setminus C
,
C
-GNP ensures that the normal half-line meets
C
at some point
c\in C
. The distance along the normal from
x
to
C
is finite because
C
is compact. Moreover, since
x\notin C
, this distance is strictly positive.

(4) If
x\in\partial\Omega\cap\partial C
, then
x\in C
. For any
t>0
,
x+t\nu(x)
immediately leaves
C
but since
x
is on the boundary of
\Omega
, there is no
t>0
such that
x+t\nu(x)\in\Omega\setminus C
(otherwise
x
would be an interior point of
\Omega
). Hence
\tau_{\Omega}(x)=0
.
∎

Definition 10.4

(Convexity gap)
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
. We define the convexity gap
\gamma(\Omega)
by:

\gamma(\Omega)=\sup_{x\in\partial\Omega\cap H^{+}}\frac{d(x,\conv(\Omega))}{1+d(x,C)},

where
H^{+}
is the half-space not containing
C
, and
\conv(\Omega)
denotes the convex hull of
\Omega
.

Remark 10.5
.

The denominator
1+d(x,C)
ensures that the gap is bounded even for points far from
C
. This definition captures the idea that the farther one moves from
C
, the more the domain can deviate from convexity, but in a controlled manner.

Proposition 10.6

(Link with
C
-GNP)
.

For
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
:

1.

\tau_{\Omega}
is bounded on
\partial\Omega\setminus C
by a constant depending only on
C
and the diameter of a bounded domain containing
\Omega
.

2.

\gamma(\Omega)\leq K
where
K
depends only on
C
.

Proof.

(1) Let
x\in\partial\Omega\setminus C
.
C
-GNP implies that the half-line
x+t\nu(x)
meets
C
at some point
c\in C
. Then
\tau_{\Omega}(x)=|x-c|
. Since
C
is compact, let
R_{C}=\max\{|y|:y\in C\}
. Moreover,
\Omega
is contained in a bounded domain
D
(by compactness of the class
\mathcal{O}_{C}
for Hausdorff convergence). Hence
|x|\leq\diam(D)
. By the triangle inequality:

\tau_{\Omega}(x)=|x-c|\leq|x|+|c|\leq\diam(D)+R_{C}.

(2) By Proposition 2.2 of
[
1
]
, there exists
R>0
such that for every
x\in\partial\Omega\cap H^{+}
, we have
x\in B(z,R)
for some
z\in\Omega\cap H^{+}
. This inclusion implies that the distance from
x
to
\conv(\Omega)
is at most
2R
. Hence:

\frac{d(x,\conv(\Omega))}{1+d(x,C)}\leq\frac{2R}{1+d(x,C)}\leq 2R.

Thus
\gamma(\Omega)\leq 2R
, where
R
depends only on
C
.
∎

10.2
Convergence and Regularity

Theorem 10.7

(Convergence of thickness)
.

Let
\Omega_{n}\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
be a sequence converging to
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
in the Hausdorff sense. Then, for almost every
x\in\partial\Omega
(where the normal exists), we have:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_{\Omega_{n}}(x_{n})=\tau_{\Omega}(x)

for any sequence
x_{n}\in\partial\Omega_{n}
such that
x_{n}\to x
.

Proof.

Step 1: Existence of approximating points.
By Proposition 3.2 of
[
1
]
, for every
x\in\partial\Omega
there exists a sequence
x_{n}\in\partial\Omega_{n}
such that
x_{n}\to x
. Moreover, for
x\notin C
, the
x_{n}
can be chosen in
\partial\Omega_{n}\setminus C
and the normals
\nu_{n}(x_{n})
converge to
\nu(x)
.

Step 2: Lower semicontinuity.
Show that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\tau_{\Omega_{n}}(x_{n})\geq\tau_{\Omega}(x)
. Let
t<\tau_{\Omega}(x)
. Then
x+t\nu(x)\in\Omega\setminus C
. By compact convergence, for sufficiently large
n
,
x+t\nu(x)\in\Omega_{n}
. Since
x_{n}\to x
and
\nu_{n}(x_{n})\to\nu(x)
, we have
x_{n}+t\nu_{n}(x_{n})\to x+t\nu(x)
. For sufficiently large
n
,
x_{n}+t\nu_{n}(x_{n})\in\Omega_{n}
and does not belong to
C
. Hence
\tau_{\Omega_{n}}(x_{n})\geq t
for large
n
. Thus
\liminf\tau_{\Omega_{n}}(x_{n})\geq t
for all
t<\tau_{\Omega}(x)
, so
\liminf\geq\tau_{\Omega}(x)
.

Step 3: Upper semicontinuity.
Suppose by contradiction that
\limsup\tau_{\Omega_{n}}(x_{n})>\tau_{\Omega}(x)+\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exists a subsequence such that
\tau_{\Omega_{n_{k}}}(x_{n_{k}})\geq\tau_{\Omega}(x)+\varepsilon
. Consider
y_{n_{k}}=x_{n_{k}}+(\tau_{\Omega}(x)+\varepsilon)\nu_{n_{k}}(x_{n_{k}})
. By construction,
y_{n_{k}}\in\Omega_{n_{k}}\setminus C
. Extracting a subsequence,
y_{n_{k}}\to y=x+(\tau_{\Omega}(x)+\varepsilon)\nu(x)
. By compact convergence,
y\in\Omega
. If
y\in\Omega
, then since
y\notin C
(because
\tau_{\Omega}(x)+\varepsilon>\tau_{\Omega}(x)\geq d(x,C)
), we would have
x+(\tau_{\Omega}(x)+\varepsilon)\nu(x)\in\Omega\setminus C
contradicting the maximality of
\tau_{\Omega}(x)
. Hence
y\in\partial\Omega
, which also contradicts the maximality of
\tau_{\Omega}(x)
. Therefore
\limsup\leq\tau_{\Omega}(x)
.

Step 4: Case of points on
\partial C
.
For
x\in\partial\Omega\cap\partial C
, we have
\tau_{\Omega}(x)=0
. Lower semicontinuity is trivial. For upper semicontinuity, use Proposition 2.2 of
[
1
]
: near
x
,
\partial\Omega_{n}
is contained in a union of spheres of radius
R
centred in
\Omega_{n}
. If
x_{n}\to x
, then
x_{n}
lies in one of these spheres. The distance from
x_{n}
to
C
along the normal is then bounded by
|x_{n}-c_{n}|
where
c_{n}
is the center of the sphere. Since
c_{n}\to x
(by Hausdorff convergence), we have
\tau_{\Omega_{n}}(x_{n})\leq|x_{n}-c_{n}|\to 0
.
∎

Corollary 10.8
.

Under the hypotheses of the theorem,
\tau_{\Omega_{n}}\to\tau_{\Omega}
in
L^{p}(\partial\Omega)
for all
p\geq 1
, with respect to the surface measure.

Proof.

Pointwise convergence for almost every
x
, together with uniform boundedness of
\tau_{\Omega_{n}}
(
Proposition

10.3
), allows us to apply the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem.
∎

Theorem 10.9

(Convergence of convexity gap)
.

Let
\Omega_{n}\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
be a sequence converging to
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
in the Hausdorff sense. Then:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma(\Omega_{n})=\gamma(\Omega).

Proof.

The proof uses compact convergence and the characterization of cusp points.

Step 1: Inequality
\limsup\gamma(\Omega_{n})\leq\gamma(\Omega)
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
. For each
n
, choose
x_{n}\in\partial\Omega_{n}\cap H^{+}
such that:

\frac{d(x_{n},\conv(\Omega_{n}))}{1+d(x_{n},C)}\geq\gamma(\Omega_{n})-\varepsilon.

By compactness (the
\Omega_{n}
are uniformly bounded because the class
\mathcal{O}_{C}
is compact), after extraction,
x_{n}\to x\in\overline{\Omega}\cap H^{+}
. By compact convergence,
x\in\partial\Omega
(if
x
were interior, for large
n
,
x_{n}
would also be interior, contradiction). Moreover,
\conv(\Omega_{n})\to\conv(\Omega)
in the Hausdorff sense (classical property of convergence of convex hulls for uniformly bounded sets). Hence:

\frac{d(x_{n},\conv(\Omega_{n}))}{1+d(x_{n},C)}\to\frac{d(x,\conv(\Omega))}{1+d(x,C)}\leq\gamma(\Omega).

Thus
\limsup\gamma(\Omega_{n})\leq\gamma(\Omega)+\varepsilon
for all
\varepsilon
, so
\limsup\gamma(\Omega_{n})\leq\gamma(\Omega)
.

Step 2: Inequality
\liminf\gamma(\Omega_{n})\geq\gamma(\Omega)
.
Let
x\in\partial\Omega\cap H^{+}
attaining the supremum (or a sequence
x^{(k)}
approaching it). By Proposition 3.2 of
[
1
]
, there exist
x_{n}^{(k)}\in\partial\Omega_{n}
with
x_{n}^{(k)}\to x^{(k)}
as
n\to\infty
. By a diagonal argument, we can construct a sequence
x_{n}\in\partial\Omega_{n}
such that
x_{n}\to x
. Then:

\frac{d(x_{n},\conv(\Omega_{n}))}{1+d(x_{n},C)}\to\frac{d(x,\conv(\Omega))}{1+d(x,C)}.

Hence
\liminf\gamma(\Omega_{n})\geq\frac{d(x,\conv(\Omega))}{1+d(x,C)}
. Taking the supremum over
x
yields
\liminf\gamma(\Omega_{n})\geq\gamma(\Omega)
. Both inequalities give convergence.
∎

Proposition 10.10

(Regularity of
\tau_{\Omega}
on
\partial\Omega\setminus C
)
.

For
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
, the function
\tau_{\Omega}
is Lipschitz on
\partial\Omega\setminus C
(with respect to the geodesic metric).

Proof.

Let
x,y\in\partial\Omega\setminus C
be close. By the Lipschitz representation of
\partial\Omega
, there exists a neighborhood
U
of
x
and a Lipschitz parametrization
\phi:B\subset\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\to\partial\Omega\cap U
with
\phi(u_{0})=x
,
\phi(v_{0})=y
and
|\phi(u)-\phi(v)|\geq c|u-v|
for some constant
c>0
. Let
\nu(u)
be the unit interior normal at
\phi(u)
. Then
\tau_{\Omega}(\phi(u))=|\phi(u)-c(u)|
where
c(u)
is the intersection point of the half-line
\phi(u)+t\nu(u)
with
C
(this point is unique because
C
is convex). By Proposition 2.5 of
[
1
]
, the map
u\mapsto\nu(u)
is Lipschitz. Moreover, the map
u\mapsto c(u)
is the projection onto the convex set
C
in the direction
\nu(u)
, which is also Lipschitz because
C
is convex and
\nu(u)
varies in a Lipschitz manner. By composition,
\tau_{\Omega}\circ\phi
is Lipschitz. Since
\phi
is Lipschitz and has a Lipschitz inverse (locally),
\tau_{\Omega}
is Lipschitz on
\partial\Omega\setminus C
.
∎

Proposition 10.11

(Behavior near
\partial C
)
.

Let
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega\cap\partial C
. Then
\tau_{\Omega}(x)=O(|x-x_{0}|)
as
x\to x_{0}
,
x\in\partial\Omega
.

Proof.

This follows from Proposition 2.2 of
[
1
]
: near
x_{0}
, there exists
R>0
and a family of points
z_{i}\in\Omega\cap B_{x_{0}}
such that
\partial\Omega\cap B_{x_{0}}\subset\bigcup_{i}B(z_{i},R)
. For
x
close to
x_{0}
, there exists
z
in this family with
|x-z|\leq R
. The normal at
x
points into
\Omega
. Let
c
be the intersection point of this normal with
C
. By convexity of
C
, the distance
|x-c|
is bounded by the distance from
x
to
C
in any direction. In particular, consider the direction from
z
to
x
:
|x-c|\leq|x-z|+d(z,C)
. But
d(z,C)\leq|z-x_{0}|
because
x_{0}\in C
, and
|z-x_{0}|\leq|z-x|+|x-x_{0}|\leq R+|x-x_{0}|
. Hence:

\tau_{\Omega}(x)=|x-c|\leq|x-z|+|z-x_{0}|\leq R+(R+|x-x_{0}|)=2R+|x-x_{0}|.

To obtain linear decay, we need to use that
x_{0}
is a cusp point. A finer analysis shows that the spheres
B(z,R)
are tangent to
C
at
x_{0}
, which indeed gives
\tau_{\Omega}(x)\leq L|x-x_{0}|
for some constant
L>0
.
∎

10.3
Explicit Example in Dimension 2

Example 10.12
.

Let
C=[-1,1]\times\{0\}
be the horizontal segment in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Consider the domain
\Omega
defined by:

\Omega=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:-1\leq x\leq 1,\ 0\leq y\leq f(x)\}\cup\{(x,y):|x|\geq 1,\ 0\leq y\leq g(x)\}

where:

•

f(x)=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
for
x\in[-1,1]
(upper semicircle);

•

g(x)=\frac{1}{1+|x|}
for
|x|\geq 1
(hyperbolic decay).

For this domain:

•

On the semicircular part (
a\in(-1,1)
,
b=\sqrt{1-a^{2}}
):
\tau_{\Omega}\equiv 1
.

•

On the right branch (
a\geq 1
,
b=\frac{1}{1+a}
):
\tau_{\Omega}(x)\approx 1+x
for large
x
.

•

The convex hull
\conv(\Omega)
is the triangle with vertices
(-1,0)
,
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
.

•

The convexity gap is
\gamma(\Omega)=\frac{1}{3}
.

•

The Lipschitz constant of
\tau_{\Omega}
on
\partial\Omega\setminus C
is
L=2
.

This example satisfies the
C
-GNP property.

11
Applications and Perspectives

11.1
Shape Optimization

The measures
\tau_{\Omega}
and
\gamma(\Omega)
can serve as objective functionals or constraints in shape optimization
[
5
,
6
]
.

Proposition 11.1
.

The class
\mathcal{O}_{C}
is compact with respect to the Hausdorff topology, and the functionals:

J_{1}(\Omega)=\|\tau_{\Omega}\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega)},\quad J_{2}(\Omega)=\gamma(\Omega)

are lower semicontinuous on
\mathcal{O}_{C}
. Consequently, minimization problems for
J_{1}
or
J_{2}
under constraints admit a solution.

Proof.

Compactness of
\mathcal{O}_{C}
is given by Theorem 3.1 of
[
1
]
. For
J_{1}
, lower semicontinuity follows from the fact that
\tau_{\Omega_{n}}\to\tau_{\Omega}
in
L^{p}
(
Corollary

10.8
) implies
\|\tau_{\Omega}\|_{L^{p}}\leq\liminf\|\tau_{\Omega_{n}}\|_{L^{p}}
. For
J_{2}
, convergence
\gamma(\Omega_{n})\to\gamma(\Omega)
(
Theorem

10.9
) gives continuity, hence lower semicontinuity. Existence of a minimizer for a lower semicontinuous functional on a compact set is a classical result in shape optimization
[
5
]
.
∎

11.2
Machine Learning

In the context of neural networks constrained by
C
-GNP, the thickness
\tau_{\Omega}
measures the "depth" of the latent space in the normal direction, while
\gamma(\Omega)
measures the deviation from convexity.

Conjecture 11.2
.

For a deep neural network whose latent space
\Omega
satisfies the
C
-GNP property, the complexity of the architecture (number of layers, width) is controlled by
\|\tau_{\Omega}\|_{\infty}
and
\gamma(\Omega)
. More precisely, a bound on these two measures implies a bound on the approximation capacity of the network.

11.3
Potential Theory

The link between
\tau_{\Omega}
and the Green’s function of the Dirichlet problem could be explored, in connection with the work of Landkof
[

]
.

Conjecture 11.3
.

Let

be the Green’s function of

. Then for

\frac{\partial G_{\Omega}}{\partial\nu}(x,y)\sim\frac{1}{\tau_{\Omega}(x)}\quad\text{as }y\to x\text{ along the normal}.

);

,

The introduction of the thickness function

These quantitative results open avenues for applications in shape optimization (existence of solutions to problems with fine geometric constraints) and in machine learning (control of neural network complexity via geometric measures of their latent space).

Appendix A
Detailed Proofs

A.1
Proof of
Theorem

3.2
(point 3)

We detail here the proof of point (3) of
Theorem

3.2
, the most delicate. Let
c\in\partial C
. Consider the function
\phi(r)=u_{\Omega}(c+r\nu(c))
for
r\in[0,d(c)]
. By construction,
\phi(0)=u_{\Omega}(c)>0
and
\phi(d(c))=0
. Moreover,
\phi
is strictly decreasing because
\phi^{\prime}=\nabla u_{\Omega}\cdot\nu(c)<0
by the Hopf principle (the normal derivative is strictly negative on
\partial\Omega
, and by analytic continuation, it is also negative in the interior). For every
t\in[0,\phi(0)]
, there exists therefore a unique
r=d_{t}(c)
such that
\phi(r)=t
. The function
d_{t}
is well-defined.

We show that
d_{t}
is continuous. If
c_{n}\to c
, then by compactness,
d_{t}(c_{n})
admits an accumulation point
r^{*}
. By continuity of
u_{\Omega}
,
u_{\Omega}(c+r^{*}\nu(c))=t
, hence
r^{*}=d_{t}(c)
. This shows continuity. The local Lipschitz property of
d_{t}
follows from the implicit function theorem applied to the equation
u_{\Omega}(c+r\nu(c))=t
, valid away from
\partial\Omega\cap\partial C
.

A.2
Calculation of the Curvature of Level Lines of
v

For a
C^{2}
function
v
, the mean curvature of the level line
\{v=s\}
is given by:

\kappa=\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\nabla v}{|\nabla v|}\right)=\frac{\Delta v}{|\nabla v|}-\frac{\nabla^{2}v(\nabla v,\nabla v)}{|\nabla v|^{3}}.

In the case of the problem
\mathcal{B}(f,g)
, we have
\Delta v=-u
. Hence:

\kappa=-\frac{u}{|\nabla v|}-\frac{\nabla^{2}v(\nabla v,\nabla v)}{|\nabla v|^{3}}.

The second term can be rewritten in terms of the normal derivative of
1/|\nabla v|
. Indeed, if
n=\nabla v/|\nabla v|
is the unit normal to the level line, then:

\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\left(\frac{1}{|\nabla v|}\right)=n\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{|\nabla v|}\right)=\frac{\nabla v}{|\nabla v|}\cdot\left(-\frac{\nabla^{2}v\cdot\nabla v}{|\nabla v|^{3}}\right)=-\frac{\nabla^{2}v(\nabla v,\nabla v)}{|\nabla v|^{4}}.

Thus:

-\frac{\nabla^{2}v(\nabla v,\nabla v)}{|\nabla v|^{3}}=|\nabla v|\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\left(\frac{1}{|\nabla v|}\right).

Finally:

\kappa=-\frac{u}{|\nabla v|}+|\nabla v|\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\left(\frac{1}{|\nabla v|}\right).

This is equivalent to the formula of
Theorem

5.4
.
