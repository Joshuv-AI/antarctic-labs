Predictors of psychological resilience among high-risk professionals: a two-year follow-up study in Dutch firefighters
Authors: Bert Lenaert, Tom J Barry, Ineke Ten Hertog, Miriam J J Lommen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Psychological resilience refers to positive adaptation in the face of adversity. As a dynamic process, resilient responding depends on the availability and use of biopsychosocial resources. More knowledge is needed about which resources contribute to resilience over time.Objective: To assess whether existential beliefs, sleep quality, and social support represent such resources and predict resilience in Dutch firefighters over a two-year follow-up period.Methods: At baseline, 528 firefighters from 11 regions in the Netherlands participated. Existential beliefs were operationalized as Sense of Coherence (SOC-R) and Meaning in Life (MLQ), sleep quality was assessed with Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and social support by colleagues wwith an Organizational Stress (VOS-D) questionnaire. Resilience was measured using the Connor-Davidson Resilience scale (CD-RISC). Follow-up measurements took place after 12 (N = 237) and 24 months (N = 203).Results: Multiple regression analysis showed that, after controlling for baseline resilience, sense of coherence at baseline significantly predicted resilience after 12 months. After 24 months, meaning in life significantly predicted resilience as well. Sleep quality and social support did not predict resilience at any time point, and neither did the covariates age, sex or experienced stressful events.Discussion: Existential beliefs that life is meaningful, comprehensible and manageable may boost resilience in high-risk professionals such as firefighters. Further research is needed to assess whether promoting sense of coherence and meaning in life can become intervention targets aimed at improving resilience.KEYWORDS: Resilience, sense of coherence, meaning in life, sleep quality, social support, firefighters

Introduction

Firefighters and other first responders such as police officers and frontline healthcare workers play a crucial societal role whenever the lives or health of individuals are in acute danger. Inherent to these professions is the exposure to work related hazards, including risk of physical injury, experiencing or witnessing traumatic life-threatening situations and events, and recurrent exposure to other stressors varying in nature. This exposure puts these professionals at greater risk of an array of health problems and may explain the high prevalence of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, suicidality, anxiety, alcohol dependence and sleep problems among first responders (Huang et al., 2022; Jones, 2017; Syed et al., 2020).

Prevalence rates of PTSD in firefighters have been estimated to range from 2% to 57%, depending on the study and measurement methods used (Obuobi-Donkor et al., 2022; Salleh et al., 2020). For police officers, the global pooled point prevalence of PTSD is estimated to be 14.2% (Syed et al., 2020). Further, the prevalence of PTSD in healthcare workers during the first years of the COVID-19 pandemic was estimated to be 22% (Li et al., 2021). Each of these rates exceeds those in the general population, although estimates vary greatly here too (Schein et al., 2021).

However, the majority of first responders do not develop PTSD or other mental health problems that require treatment (Berger et al., 2012). These individuals are often described as resilient. Despite the many available definitions, two elements shared by most definitions of resilience are positive adaptation in the face of adversity (Fletcher & Sarkar, 2013). Three main approaches have been used to conceptualize resilience (Troy et al., 2023). First, resilience can refer to a characteristic or trait which a person possesses in the absence of adversity but which may only be observed in the presence of adversity. Second, resilience can be described as a dynamic process that requires the use of and access to resources that promote resilient responding or coping when confronted with adversity. These resources may vary and can be biological (e.g. sleep), psychological (e.g. cognitive appraisal) or social (e.g. a supporting working culture) in nature. Third, resilience can be defined as an observable outcome such as maintained functioning or health in the face of adversity. This functioning can be reflected in various ways including well-being, societal participation, or the absence of psychopathology.

Although these approaches need not be mutually exclusive, we concur with the view of resilience as a dynamic process rather than a static trait that someone either has or does not have (Luthar et al., 2000). More precisely, the availability of personal and environmental resources for resilience may change throughout a person’s life and may vary across different contexts. For instance, a person may have learned to cope with challenging events in a more or less adaptive way over the years, or may experience more social support from others in certain social contexts but not in other contexts. As a consequence, whether a person shows resilience may be influenced by an ever-changing set of factors or resources. More knowledge of the longitudinal contribution to resilience of such resources in samples of at-risk professionals is needed.

The present longitudinal study therefore examines the contribution of a subset of biopsychosocial resources in predicting resilience in a sample of Dutch firefighters over a two-year follow-up period. More specifically, we investigated to what extent existential views and beliefs (i.e. meaning in life and sense of coherence), experienced social support by colleagues, and sleep quality may constitute promoting factors for resilience.

When confronted with adverse events, existential views and beliefs may co-determine whether an individual responds resiliently (Ostafin & Proulx, 2020; Pino et al., 2024). We have operationalized these beliefs in two ways. First, we assessed whether an individual’s sense of coherence predicts resilience. In Antonovsky’s original theory on salutogenesis (1979), sense of coherence refers to an enduring feeling of confidence about life as being comprehensible, manageable, and meaningful. Comprehensibility refers to the perception of stimuli or future events as structured, predictable and explicable (cognitive component), whereas manageability refers to a person’s perception about the resources that are available to deal with these events (behavioural component). Meaningfulness refers to whether life makes emotional sense and whether challenges are worth facing (motivational component) (Lindström & Eriksson, 2005). A higher sense of coherence has been associated with better mental health and well-being in different populations, including individuals with chronic illness and aid workers exposed to war trauma (Eriksson, 2022; Galletta et al., 2019; Veronese & Pepe, 2017). In the context of resilience, a sense of coherence may promote a person’s ability to comprehend the whole situation within a meaningful context and to use the resources that are available to cope with adversity. Hence, we hypothesized that having a higher sense of coherence would be related to higher levels of resilience at follow-up, taking into account baseline resilience and independent of experienced stressful events.

In addition, we investigated to what extent experiencing meaning in life may act as a promoting factor for resilience. Frankl (1985) originally argued that humans thrive best when they perceive a sense of meaning and have a purpose in life. Meaning in life can be defined as people’s judgement that their lives consist of purpose (feeling like one’s life is motivated by valued goals), coherence (feeling like one’s life makes sense), and significance (feeling that one’s life matters) (Ostafin & Proulx, 2020). In a series of cross-sectional and longitudinal studies, Ostafin and Proulx (2020) showed that meaning in life was inversely related to psychological distress and repetitive negative thinking. Moreover, it has been shown that experiencing meaning in life is associated with higher levels of well-being and is a protective factor against the development of psychopathology (Steger, 2022). We hypothesized that experiencing meaning in life would be related to higher levels of resilience at follow-up, taking into account baseline resilience and independent of experienced stressful events.

The constructs of sense of coherence and meaning in life have some related components. With respect to (predicting) resilience, both theoretical frameworks suggest that resilient responding is promoted by the ability to derive or maintain meaning in the face of suffering or adversity. Nevertheless, both constructs differ both in scope and theoretical emphasis. Although the motivational component of sense of coherence is linked with meaningfulness, it is part of a broader construct that reflects a global orientation towards life’s challenges as being comprehensible and manageable, and in that sense reflects how individuals perceive their ability to use resources necessary to cope with life events. In contrast, the concept of meaning in life is less focused on the task of managing life’s demands, but rather emphasizes the subjective experience that life makes sense and has a purpose (felt sense). Additionally, a meta-analysis about predictors of distress in cancer patients showed that both meaning in life and sense of coherence demonstrated significant negative associations with distress, and that these associations were independent of one another and differed in strength; with sense of coherence being more strongly related to distress (Winger et al., 2016). The authors concluded that meaning in life and sense of coherence are related but distinct constructs. Therefore, these constructs may capture distinct dimensions of psychological functioning that uniquely contribute to resilience over time.

Further, we investigated whether feeling supported by colleagues and supervisors predicts resilience among firefighters. Numerous studies have shown that social support is related to better health and well-being across the lifespan. For instance, Siedlecki and colleagues (2014) showed that perceived support predicted several indicators of well-being in a sample of 1,111 individuals aged 18–95, with similar effects across age groups. A meta-analysis of longitudinal data in 75 samples (N = 32,402) showed that social support was associated with lower risk of PTSD in different populations (Wang et al., 2021). In regard to social support in the workplace, Jolly et al. (2021) developed an integrative framework describing how job demands and social support from colleagues may shape motivational and stress reactions such as burnout. In a cross-sectional survey among Chinese firefighters, Wang et al. (2023) found that firefighters had a lower likelihood of reporting depressive symptoms if they experienced social support from coworkers. Further, a recent study in 53,980 South Korean firefighters (representing 92.6% of the workforce) showed that perceived support from colleagues moderated the impact of a colleague’s death on PTSD symptoms and suicide risk. Specifically, higher levels of support reduced the adverse effects of emotional damage and alcohol-related problems on PTSD symptoms and suicide risk (Kim et al., 2025).

With respect to resilience, it has been suggested that individual resilience is in a bidirectional relationship with socio-ecological or systems-level resilience, referring to the broader social context of partners, family members, colleagues, friends, neighbourhoods and communities (Sippel et al., 2015). In a comprehensive meta-analysis of resilience interventions, social support-based interventions yielded large and meaningful effects, underscoring the importance of supportive environments (Liu et al., 2020). Southwick and colleagues (2016) discuss a number of psychological and behavioural mechanisms through which social support may promote resilience, including enhanced sense of control or mastery, feelings of being understood, appraisal of events as being less threatening, or the use of more active coping strategies. In this study, we measured perceived social support from colleagues and supervisors as firefighters usually operate in teams when exposed to work hazards. We hypothesized that perceived social support would predict higher levels of resilience at follow-up, taking into account baseline resilience and independent of experienced stressful events.

Finally, we assessed whether perceived sleep quality constitutes a longitudinal predictor of resilience. Although good quality sleep is vital for our health and well-being, sleep problems are highly prevalent in all ages, with sleep disruption having negative health consequences in the short and long term (Grandner, 2022; Medic et al., 2017; Palagini et al., 2022). Several psychobiological mechanisms may underlie the relationship between sleep quality and resilience. For instance, sleep quality may promote resilience by facilitating adaptive regulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and enhancing recovery from stress, thereby reducing prolonged physiological stress activation (Lo Martire et al., 2024). In addition, sleep supports emotion regulation and executive functioning, including cognitive flexibility and inhibitory control, which are essential for effective coping with adversity (Choong et al., 2025; Palmer & Alfano, 2017). Sleep may therefore not merely represent a correlate of resilience, but rather a modifiable factor that may strengthen resilient responding through neuroendocrine, emotional, and cognitive pathways (Lo Martire et al., 2024). In a meta-analysis on the relationship between sleep, mental toughness and resilience amongst healthy individuals, resilience was positively but weakly related to (mainly self-report) indices of sleep duration (r = 0.11, p < .001), and sleep quality (r = 0.27, p < .001) (Arora et al., 2022). The authors stressed the need for prospective studies to verify directional relationships between sleep and resilience. In the current study, we hypothesized that higher perceived sleep quality predicted higher levels of resilience at follow-up, taking into account baseline resilience and independent of experienced stressful events.

Given the unpredictable nature of firefighters’ stress exposure, we could not assume a uniform pattern or direction of change in resilience across the follow-up period. Accordingly, our primary aim was not to model resilience trajectories over time, but to examine whether baseline predictors (sense of coherence, meaning in life, social support, and sleep quality) prospectively predicted resilience at subsequent timepoints. We therefore treated resilience at 12 and 24 months as separate outcomes and tested whether baseline predictors explained variance in later resilience in two separate regression models, accounting for baseline resilience and stressful events experienced during follow-up in each model.

Method

Participants and data

A total of 528 Dutch firefighters (501 males; 94.9%) completed the baseline assessment (T0) of the resilience scale (CD-RISC). Follow-up assessments included 338 (T1; approx. 6 months), 237 (T2; approx. 12 months), 222 (T3; approx. 18 months), and 203 (T4; approx. 24 months) participants with complete or partial data. Parts of these cohort data focusing on different research questions have been published previously (Ansah et al., 2024; de Haart et al., 2021; Egberink et al., 2020; Lommen et al., 2024; Lommen & Boddez, 2022; Sopp et al., 2021). The mean age of participants at T0 was 39.9 years (SD = 10.0). Participants had on average 15 years of work experience (SD = 9.5) and were either full-time firefighters (n = 223; 42.2%), volunteers (n = 137; 25.8%), or had experience as both (n = 169; 31.9%). We used the maximal sample size available given practical constraints and the limited population size. Within each fire brigade in the Netherlands that agreed to be included in the study, we included everyone who was willing to participate, thereby reaching the upper limit of recruitment in light of what was feasible (Lakens, 2022). Power analysis showed that a sample size of 108 or more was required to detect medium sized effects at a power level of 0.8 and a maximum total of 8 predictors.

Procedure

After contacting all 25 safety regions in the Netherlands, which include the fire departments, arrangements were made with 11 fire brigades to be included in the study. The heads of each of these brigades informed their firefighters about the study. Baseline assessment (T0) was held during the summer of 2017. Researchers visited firefighters at their respective bases where they again informed eligible participants about the study and the strictly voluntary nature of study participation without financial compensation. After providing written informed consent, participants completed the baseline assessment. Follow-up questionnaires were completed online. The primary outcome measure of resilience was administered at baseline (T0), after approximately 12 months follow-up (T2), and after approximately 24 months follow-up (T4), but not at T1 and T3. This was a design choice made to limit participant burden, as this research project had a broad scope with multiple aims, including unravelling long-term predictors of psychopathology and, as presented here, resilience. Therefore, the test battery consisted of multiple measures that were administered in an alternating fashion during follow-up. All predictors were measured at baseline (T0). The study protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee of Psychology of the University of Groningen, the Netherlands (16374-O).

Measures

Resilience was assessed using the 10-item version of the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), the most widely used scale to measure resilience in both clinical and non-clinical populations (Campbell-Sills & Stein, 2007). The 10-item unidimensional scale has shown to have excellent psychometric properties. Items consist of statements (e.g. ‘I tend to bounce back after illness or hardship’), rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from ‘Not true at all’ to ‘True nearly all the time’. Total scores range from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating higher resilience.

Sense of coherence

The Dutch revised version of the revised Sense of Coherence Scale (SOC-R) was used (Egberink et al., 2020). The SOC-R consists of 10 items measuring sense of coherence (e.g. ‘In my thoughts and actions, I take into account that things often have two sides: good and bad ones.’), which are scored on a 5-point Likert-scale ranging from 1 (‘not at all true’) to 5 (‘very true’). Total scores range from 10 to 50, with a higher score indicating a stronger sense of coherence.

Meaning in life

The Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ; Steger et al., 2006) consists of 10 items and has two 5-item subscales: Presence of Meaning (e.g. ‘I understand my life’s meaning) and Search for Meaning (e.g. I am seeking a purpose of mission for my life’). All items are assessed on a 7-point Likert scale. In this study, only the Presence subscale was administered to participants. Sumscores range from 5 to 35, with higher scores indicating stronger experienced meaning in life.

Social support at work

Perceived social support was assessed using a subscale of the Questionnaire on Organisational Stress (VOS-D; Bergers et al., 1986). It includes 5 questions that are rated on a 1 (very good) to 4 (bad) Likert-scale for social support from colleagues and supervisors separately (e.g. ‘How is your relationship with your direct supervisor in general?’; ‘To what extent can you count on your colleagues, when you have difficulties in your work?’). The sum score of these 10 items (range 10–40) was used as indicator of social support at work. Scores were mirrored so higher scores represent higher perceived social support.

Sleep Quality was measured with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI; Buysse et al., 1989), which evaluates self-reported sleep quality during the past month. A total score and seven component scores, such as subjective sleep quality, sleep efficiency, and daytime dysfunction can be derived from the scale. Each component is scored on a 4-point scale ranging from 0 (‘not during the past month’) to 3 (‘three or more times a week’). In this study, total scores were used, which range from 0–21 and with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality.

Stressful events

Experiencing firefighter-related stressful events was included as a covariate and was measured using a scale created in collaboration with several firefighters. These firefighters created a list of events specific to their work which they regarded, based on their experience as a firefighter or supervisor, as stressful and potentially traumatizing or with the potential of having an emotional impact (e.g. rescuing or witnessing a victim of a fire with severe burns, suicide, death of a colleague during an incident). The final questionnaire consisted of 21 predefined events and one open question for events that were not covered in the 21 items. For each event, participants indicated whether or not they had experienced the event in the past time period. The total sum score (range = 0–22) was used in the analyses.

Analysis

Our analysis strategy was not pre-registered but it mirrors that of other papers from our group in this area and in this specific dataset (de Haart et al., 2021; Lommen, 2024; Lommen & Boddez, 2022; Lommen et al., 2024). Two separate linear regression models were run, with resilience at T2 and resilience at T4 as outcomes respectively, which allowed us to assess to what extent resilience could be predicted after 12 and 24 months follow-up. In both regressions, baseline resilience (model 1), age, sex, and stressful events (model 2) were included as covariates. Next, sense of coherence, meaning in life, social support, and sleep quality were included as predictors (model 3). For the regression analysis with resilience at T2 as outcome variable, stressful events reported at T2 were used as covariate. For the regression with resilience at T4 as outcome, stressful events at T4 were used as covariate. To correct for multiple testing, Bonferroni-adjusted significance thresholds were applied. The final regression models contained 8 predictors, leading to a threshold of p < .00625 for statistical significance (.05/8). Each model contained only participants with complete data at each respective follow-up assessment.

Results

Descriptives

At 12 months follow-up (T2), participants (n = 251) reported that on average 5.5 (SD = 3.6; range = 0–20) stressful events had occurred. At 24 months follow-up (T4), participants (n = 215) reported on average 6.5 (SD = 3.6; range = 0–17) stressful events. At baseline (T0), there were 501 male and 27 female (5.1%) participants. Mean age was 39.9 years (SD = 10.0; range = 18–61). Because there was substantial drop-out during follow-up, we made a comparison of sex, age, and resilience at baseline between participants (n = 271) who completed at least one follow-up measurement (T2, T4, or both), and participants (n = 257) who completed no follow-up measurements. There was a significant difference in age, t(526) = −3.75, p < .001. Correlations

Correlational analyses revealed moderate to strong associations (r = .57–.72) between resilience at baseline (T0), after 12 months (T2), and 24 months (T4). Meaning in life and sense of coherence at baseline showed weak to moderate positive associations with resilience across all measurement occasions (r = .35–.47 and r = .43–.46, respectively). Perceived social support at baseline was positively associated with resilience, although these associations were relatively weak (r = .20–.23). Similarly, poorer sleep quality at baseline was weakly associated with lower resilience at baseline and follow-up (r = −.19 to −.24). Examination of the intercorrelations among the predictor variables showed that meaning in life and sense of coherence were only weakly related to one another (r = .29). Meaning in life was also weakly associated with perceived social support (r = .22) and sleep quality (r = −.19), whereas sense of coherence showed similarly weak associations with perceived social support (r = .16) and sleep quality (r = −.19). Perceived social support and sleep quality were also only weakly correlated (r = −.15).

Predicting resilience after 12 months

Multiple linear regression predicting resilience after 12 months (T2) showed that resilience at baseline was a significant predictor, β = .57, p < .001, explaining 32.4% of the variance in resilience 12 months later (Table 3). Neither experienced stressful events, nor age or sex were significant predictors, and adding these covariates to the model did not lead to a significant increase in explained variance, ΔR2 = .000, ΔF(3, 210) = 0.04, p = .988. With respect to the main predictors, sense of coherence at baseline significantly predicted resilience after 12 months, taking into account baseline resilience, age, sex and stressful events, β = .22, p < .001. Meaning in life was no longer a significant predictor after Bonferroni correction, β = .16, p = .013. Finally, perceived social support and sleep quality did not significantly predict resilience. Adding this set of predictors led to a significant increase in explained variance, ΔR2 = .063, ΔF(4, 206) = 5.25, p = <.001. The final model explained a total of 38.7% in the variance of resilience after 12 months.

Predicting resilience after 24 months

Multiple linear regression predicting resilience after 24 months (T4) showed that resilience at baseline was a significant predictor, β = .61, p < .001, explaining 37.1% of the variance in resilience 24 months later (Table 3). Again, neither reported stressful events, nor age or sex were significant predictors of resilience, and adding these covariates to the model did not lead to a significant increase in explained variance, ΔR2 = .010, ΔF(3, 184) = 0.98, p = .405. With respect to the main predictors, meaning in life at baseline significantly predicted resilience after 24 months, taking into account baseline resilience, age, sex and stressful events, β = .20, p = .003. Sense of coherence was no longer a significant predictor after Bonferroni correction, β = .15, p = .024. Finally, perceived social support and sleep quality did not significantly predict resilience. Adding this set of predictors led to a significant increase in explained variance, ΔR2 = .066, ΔF(4, 180) = 5.36, p = <.001. The final model explained a total of 44.7% in the variance of resilience after 24 months.

Discussion

In this study we investigated to what extent sense of coherence, meaning in life, social support, and sleep quality predicted psychological resilience 12 and 24 months later in Dutch firefighters. Results showed that a stronger sense of coherence significantly predicted higher levels of resilience after 12 months (β = .22, p < .001), controlling for age, sex, experienced stressful events, and baseline resilience. Experiencing meaning in life showed a similar relationship with resilience after 12 months, but was not a significant predictor after Bonferroni correction (β = .16, p = .013). After 24 months, a similar pattern occurred, although inverted in terms of predictor strength and statistical significance. Here, stronger meaning in life significantly predicted higher levels of resilience 24 months later (β = .20, p = .003), whereas sense of coherence was no longer significant after Bonferroni correction (β = .15, p = .024). Neither perceived social support, nor reported sleep quality at baseline significantly predicted resilience after 12 or 24 months.

Baseline resilience (T0) was a strong predictor of resilience at follow-up after 12 and 24 months. This may be interpreted as lending support to the notion of resilience as a stable trait, although two caveats have to be taken into account. First, it is plausible that personal and environmental resources for resilience also remained stable over the course of two years, resulting in similar resilience scores, without those scores necessarily reflecting a person trait or characteristic. Second, a large amount of variance in resilience at follow-up remained unexplained by resilience at baseline, underscoring the importance of research into the long-term predictors and determinants of psychological resilience.

This study showed that resilience can be predicted by sense of coherence and meaning in life over a two-year follow-up period, beyond what can be predicted based on baseline resilience alone. These personal resources may constitute protective and promotive factors for resilience. When confronted with adverse life events, having a sense that life is meaningful, comprehensible and manageable may act as a buffer against the potentially negative effects of such events. Notably, meaning in life and sense of coherence were only weakly associated (r = .29), supporting the notion that these measures capture different aspects of psychological functioning (Winger et al., 2016).

Sleep quality and social support did not predict resilience at follow-up. In the correlational analyses however, both predictors were weakly but significantly correlated with resilience at all time points in the hypothesized direction. Nevertheless, multiple regression analyses revealed that sleep quality and social support did not explain unique variance in resilience at follow-up beyond what could be predicted based on baseline resilience and the other included predictors. One possible explanation could lie in differences in predictor strength as a function of time. Whereas existential beliefs (i.e. meaning in life) may be more stable over time, predicting psychological functioning over many years – as supported by this study; sleep quality and social support may be more prone to variability. It is therefore possible that sleep and social support influence functioning over the short term but may lose some of their predictive strength over longer time periods. It should be noted, however, that sleep quality and social support were not more strongly associated with resilience measured at the same point in time (T0) relative to later time points (T2, T4) (Table 2).

Interestingly, a previous investigation on the same dataset that addressed the prediction of psychopathology found that lower levels of social support predicted more posttraumatic stress symptoms, and lower meaning in life predicted more depressive symptoms (Lommen, 2024). The observation that resilience and depressive and posttraumatic stress symptoms have both shared (e.g. meaning in life), and unique predictors (e.g. social support) within the same cohort underscores the complexity of the relationship between resilience and psychopathology. Moreover, resilience only showed weak negative correlations with psychological symptoms in that study (r range: −.09 to −.27). This supports the notion that resilience and psychopathology should not be regarded as two sides of the same coin, in that the presence of one does not imply the absence of the other, and with both having unique sets of predictors.

This study has some limitations. First, there were relatively high levels of drop-out. We found that participants who stayed in the study were slightly older (3.2 years on average) than participants who dropped out. It is also possible that some degree of self-selection bias took place during follow-up, where participants with more psychological complaints or lower levels of resilience dropped out more frequently than others. However, there were no significant differences in baseline resilience between participant who completed at least one follow-up measurement and participants who had no follow-up data. . Another possibility is that participants who dropped out experienced more stressful events or developed more psychological complaints after baseline measurement took place. Another limitation is that social support was operationalized with a scale that assesses perceived social support by work colleagues and supervisors. We did not measure social support from family or friends, which may be equally if not more important resources for resilience (Blais et al., 2021).

Major strengths of the current study are related to the study design and sample. First, researching longitudinal predictors of resilience in a sample of firefighters adds to our knowledge of factors that may promote resilience in high-risk professions over time. To the extent that resilience is a dynamic process that relies on the availability and utilization of personal and environmental resources, this knowledge can be used to develop prevention and treatment strategies that aim to train, exploit or make available such resources among first responders. Second, because frequent exposure to potentially traumatic events is inherent to the profession of firefighters, investigating this population offers unique opportunities to study longitudinal predictors of resilience in a valid way over relatively short time periods.

Future research should investigate predictors of resilience in new firefighters and individual differences in resilience trajectories. This may allow identifying personal and environmental resources that are of particular importance to firefighters early in their career. Further research with experimental designs, including controlled trials and single case experimental designs (SCED), is needed to assess whether promoting sense of coherence and meaning in life results in stronger resilience, making them potential intervention targets aimed at improving resilience. A meta-analysis by Liu and colleagues (2020) showed that interventions aimed at improving resilience can be effective, although effects are generally small to moderate. However, this meta-analysis also suggests that strengthening existential beliefs has not received much attention in resilience interventions, which predominantly focus on factors such as physical activity, social support, psychoeducation, or mindfulness. Promisingly, a meta-analysis by Manco and Hamby (2021) showed that several interventions exist that can effectively increase meaning in life, while also being brief and not always requiring licensed professionals. A future avenue for research lies in assessing whether such interventions may also benefit resilience.

This study showed that existential beliefs predicted resilience in a sample of Dutch firefighters over a two-year follow-up period. Due to their increased exposure to stressful and potentially traumatic events, it is important to gain insight into predictors of psychological resilience among firefighters and other frontline personnel. Having a sense that life is meaningful, comprehensible, and manageable may promote resilience in firefighters. We found no evidence for sleep quality or social support as predictors of resilience in the current study. Future studies should investigate whether strengthening sense of coherence and meaning in life may promote resilience in professions at increased risk of mental health problems.
