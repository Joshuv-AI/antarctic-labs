 ABSTRACT
The USA Structures (USS) foundational data layer remains a critical national dataset for supporting dam-
age assessment and disaster response across the United States. Maintaining its relevance requires regular
updates to reflect changes in the built environment and to ensure the accuracy of key structural attributes.
Building on the dataset update workflow and research outcomes from 2023-2024 Phase 3 Prototype ef-
forts (Yang, Hauser, et al. 2024), the Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) team has completed a new
and scaled data refresh, naming Phase 3 Scaling, that incorporates geometry updates and building codes
for 15 additional states: Arizona, Arkansas, California, Colorado, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Maryland, Mas-
sachusetts, Missouri, Nevada, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, and Pennsylvania. In parallel, the team ad-
vanced its population modeling approach, producing refined population estimates for all states and Wash-
ington, D.C., including updated estimates for the nine priority states covered in the previous refresh. With
this year’s refresh, the total structure count within USS dataset comes to 143.8 million. Additionally, there
are 28 attributes populated of the 32 within the schema.
This refresh expands coverage to a substantially larger spatial extent, necessitating further optimization of
the attribution data processing pipeline, as well as improvements in satellite image processing and deploy-
ment modules. ORNL has invested in automating feature extraction, vector data processing, and attribution
workflows to enable more rapid and regular dataset updates in the future. Additionally, the team presents a
preliminary synthetic aperture radar (SAR)-based change-detection dataset to support large-scale structure
monitoring and ongoing efforts to improve Automatic Feature Extraction (AFE) outcomes.

 1. STRUCTURE GEOMETRY UPDATES
Starting with Phase 3 (Yang, Hauser, et al. 2024), one of the key focuses of the project has been to estab-
lish a sustainable dataset-refresh framework, which includes mapping updated geometries using the latest
satellite imagery. During Phase 3 Scaling, we continued leveraging recent advances in computer vision for
structure mapping to update geometries across 15 states: Arizona, Arkansas, California, Colorado, Idaho,
Illinois, Indiana, Maryland, Massachusetts, Missouri, Nevada, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, and Pennsyl-
vania.

1.1 IMAGE CURATION AND COVERAGE
As with the original USA Structures (USS) inventory and the previous update, we used very high-resolution
commercial satellite imagery as the source for mapping structures. In total, this update used 16,728 WorldView-
2 and WorldView-3 scenes acquired between 2017 and 2024. Scene selection was performed with a new
multi-criteria suitability method implemented using open-source Python packages, increasing emphasis on
image quality (e.g., lower cloud cover, reduced off-nadir angle) and reducing dependence on proprietary
software within the workflow. Preprocessing steps followed those described in the previous data descriptor
(Yang, Laverdiere, et al. 2024).
Figure 1 shows the portion of area covered by updated imagery by year of acquisition, and Figure 2 maps
the acquisition year for all scenes used in the update. Gaps can be seen in the updated imagery set where
either no newer imagery was available or newer imagery did not meet our minimum quality standards in
terms of off-nadir angle and cloud coverage.
State-level geometry update outcomes of the Automatic Feature Extraction (AFE) deployment are summa-
rized in Table 1, which reports the percentage of each state updated and the count of potential new struc-
ture detections. For this analysis, a potential new structure is defined as a structure with area exceeding
450 ft2 and located more than 3 meters from any existing structure in the USS inventory. The 3 m thresh-
old was chosen to account for horizontal errors in geo-registration of the imagery.

1.2 AUTOMATED FEATURE EXTRACTION IMPROVEMENTS
In FY 2025, we substantially overhauled the AFE workflow to reduce or eliminate dependencies on com-
mercial off-the-shelf software by migrating to free and open-source software (e.g., QGIS), Python pack-
ages, and PostgreSQL/PostGIS. This redesign, coupled with the adoption of an advanced transformer-
based model as described in our previous update cycle, is referred to as AFE 2.0. The update delivers
notable improvements in the processing chain and building-feature outputs relative to the original USS
Phase 1 and Phase 2 efforts, streamlining the workflow and helping to ease maintenance of data prove-
nance.
AFE 2.0 moves from file-centric processing to a database-oriented design. Centralizing storage within
PostGIS database allows source imagery metadata, intermediate data layers, and final structure layers to
be managed in a single system of record. As a result of this effort, we established two databases, namely
AFE database (AFEDB) and USA database (USADB) for managing imagery derived outputs and support-
ing USS production, respectively. Attributes from source layers (e.g., scene-level acquisition metadata)
relate seamlessly to final outputs without extra file merges. Role-based access improves sharing among
multiple users without duplicating large files, while the reduction of intermediate layers and read/write cy-
cles lowers input/output overhead. Exploiting the spatial indexing and SQL operations accelerates several
postprocessing steps that address raw detected geometries, such as area calculation, dissolving features,
and calculating distance to reference features.
Although AFE 2.0 development is ongoing, work during FY 2025 involved eliminating prior dependencies
on ESRI algorithms for production and designing and deploying a robust open-source method for merging
and dissolving layers that resolved a critical error occurred in the former commercial approach. We intend
to expand this approach to embrace any large scale for future USS updates.

 2. ATTRIBUTION UPDATES
2.1 NEW ATTRIBUTION
In FY 2025, one change was made to the USS schema. PROP_CNTY is a new attribution column that
is the proper county name as defined by the Census 2020 tiger shapefile (US Census Bureau 2020b). The
county of a structure is determined by the intersection of the structure’s centroid and the county boundary
projected to World Geodetic System (WGS) 84, spatial reference identifier (SRID) 4326. The result is then
documented in the PROP_CNTY attribute column for the structure.

2.2 AUTOMATION OF ATTRIBUTION PROCESS
The attribution process received significant updates and restructuring with the goal of enabling full au-
tomation of the workflow, as illustrated in Figure 6. Many of these steps have been described in greater
detail in previous technical reports (Hauser et al. 2023; Yang, Hauser, et al. 2024). At the core of our au-
tomation approach is the combination of a PostgreSQL database and customized Python scripts, working
together to validate outputs and seamlessly drive the workflow from one processing stage to the next.
For example, Standard Attribution (Table 3) is the second conflation step in the attribution process (Figure
6). This step requires that the previous step, Filter with Solar Panel Layer at State Boundary, to have been
completed. A script will confirm the presence of a table of universally unique identifiers (UUIDs), which
represent the structures that intersect with a solar-array data layer for a specific state. Once intersections
are confirmed, spatially joining operations will start using filtered structures with the corresponding attri-
bution layers. In this case, those layers are census state, census tract, census county, and imagery coverage
data layers.
By confirming prerequisite outputs at each stage, the workflow can advance states as far as available data
allow in parallel. Some steps, such as Merge, pause until additional inputs (e.g., manual QA/QC results),
are provided. Once these results are loaded into the database, the system automatically recognizes the new
tables and seamlessly resumes the Merge step without requiring further manual engagement of the work-
flow .

2.2.1 Attribution Workflow Description
This section describes the processing steps in the order in which they occur within the workflow.
 • Raw Structure Detections: Newly extracted structures are loaded into the centralized multi-project
 PostgreSQL database AFEDB, as shown in Figure 6.
 • Manual QA/QC: Hot-spot areas are generated per the steps for manual review (Yang, Hauser, et
 al. 2024). These hot-spot areas are captured as geometry masks, which are ancillary data required by
 later processing steps referred to as qaqc_zones and qaqc_tables. Section 1.3 of the previous report
 (Yang, Hauser, et al. 2024) provides more details.
 • Regularization: This process simplifies and improves the visual quality of the raw structure detec-
 tions. Section 2.1 of the previous report (Yang, Hauser, et al. 2024) provides more details.
 • Ingest USADB: PostgreSQL foreign data wrappers are used to pull the raw and regularized structure
 detections from AFEDB to USADB.
 • Filter with Solar Panel Layer at State Boundary: Structure centroids that intersect with the US Large-
 Scale Solar Photovoltaic Database (Fujita et al. 2025) are removed from the final data layer.

• Addressing: This processing step assigns an address to the newly extracted structures via the process

• OCC: This processing step assigns OCC_CLS and PRIM_OCC (occupancy classes) to the newly

• Building Code: This processing step assigns the building code that was likely in effect when the

• Filter with False Positive Tables: When analysts finish their manual QA/QC, qaqc_tables tables are

  the QA/QC process.
• Merge: In this step, we combine the previous deliverable data with the new geometry detections.
  This work is described in more detail in Section 2.2 of the previous report (Yang, Hauser, et al. 2024).
• Validation Attribution: The step populates the VAL_METHOD attribute. If a structure falls within
  one of the qaqc_zones, then the VAL_METHOD equals “Manual,” indicating that the structure was
  manually validated. If a new structure detection intersected and replaced an older detection, then its
  VAL_METHOD attribute equals “Spatial Intersect,” indicating that the structure was not manually
  validated but intersected a previously validated structure.
• Generate Gauntlet: The Geographic Augmentation of Extracted Building Features Tool (Gauntlet) is
  an open-source code base (Hauser 2025) and recently updated to version 2.0. This step generates the
  structure-level morphologies of the newly created structure dataset using Gauntlet.
• ResType Modeling: The original ResType (v1) model is described in Section 2.5 of the previous
  report (Hauser et al. 2023). ResType has been updated to version 2.0 and is described in more detail
  in Section 2.3. This step generates the model inference using the Gauntlet v2.0 morphology features.
  These inferences are used only if no other ancillary data are available to derive the OCC_CLS or
  PRIM_OCC attribute.
• Population Estimation: Once the OCC_CLS and PRIM_OCC attributes are known, a population
  estimate can be generated for the structures that are defined as Residential in OCC_CLS. As previ-
  ously described (Yang, Hauser, et al. 2024), population is estimated by approximating household
  size for each structure, matching physical and dwelling characteristics to synthetic housing unit
  data—and multiplying the result by the estimated number of units where necessary—to yield me-
  dian and 95% confidence interval counts of people by structure. We have improved the population
  modeling method to provide more accurate estimates, as discussed in Section 2.4.
• Incorporate United States Geological Survey (USGS) Feedback: The USGS National Map Corps
  provides feedback on structure occupancy labels. This feedback is used to update occupancy attribu-
  tion. If an occupancy label comes from USGS, then the VAL_METHOD will be “USGS TNMC.”
  Unlike the previous update (Yang, Hauser, et al. 2024), this update does not include any additional
  feedback from our USGS partner; future updates will integrate USGS partner feedback when avail-
  able.

2.3 UPDATES TO RESTYPE MODEL
The ResType model has been upgraded from version 1 (Hauser et al. 2023) to version 2. The overall pur-
pose of the ResType model remains the same: to predict either residential or nonresidential use type of a
structure when no ancillary data describe the structure’s use as the attribution of OCC_CLS in the dataset.
A model inference of residential is documented as Residential in OCC_CLS, and a nonresidential infer-
ence is denoted as Unclassified in OCC_CLS.
The updates come in the form of three significant changes:
 • Use of Gauntlet version 2 features

 10

 • Updated sampling techniques on a larger pool of data
 • Model execution integrated with the updated attribution workflow, as described in Section 2.2.1 and
 Figure 6.
Gauntlet version 1 (v1), as described elsewhere (Hauser et al. 2023), generated 25 morphological fea-
tures of a given extracted structure. Gauntlet version 2 (v2) (Hauser 2025) generates 65 morphological
features of a given extracted structure, including a new set of contextual features. Contextual features de-
scribe how the structure relates to its neighboring structures within a certain radius. In Gauntlet v1, the
only radius used for the contextual features was 100 m. Gauntlet v2 significantly expands these features to
50 m, 100 m, 250 m, 500 m, and 1,000 m. These additional features have been demonstrated to be crucial
in other morphological approaches (Milojevic-Dupont et al. 2020; Biljecki and Chow 2022; Nachtigall et
al. 2023; Stipek et al. 2024; D. Adams et al. 2024).
In addition to incorporating Gauntlet v2 features into new ResType modeling, we reexamined how the
training data were originally sampled for model training (Hauser et al. 2023). Our findings (D. S. Adams
et al. 2024) highlight the value in maintaining some class imbalance (residential vs. nonresidential) when
the actual dataset has significant imbalance between the model classes. Using F-1 scores as the evaluating
metrics, we find that ResType v2 offers F1 scores (i.e. harmonic mean of the precision and recall) 97.5%
and 82.7% for residential and nonresidential, respectively. We modified our original sampling and training
methods by the following steps:
 1. Label Creation: The model labels are derived from the PRIM_OCC attribute field. If a structure’s
 PRIM_OCC equals any of the following values, then it is given a residential label:
 (a) Single-family dwelling
 (b) Multi-family dwelling
 (c) Manufactured home
 (d) Nursing home
 (e) Institutional dormitory
 All other structures with a PRIM_OCC attribute outside of the above values and Unclassified were
 given a nonresidential label.
 2. Stratified Random Sample: A random sample of 200,000 records from each state was generated,
 regardless of label, if a label was present. If a state or territory had less than 200,000 samples, then
 the entire state population was used.
 3. Iterative Hypertuning: Hyperparameters were tuned with iterative steps (using the grid Search cross-
 validation approach) to find the near-optimal parameters. Table 4 details the hyperparameters.
 4. Model Selection: We evaluated the model’s accuracy using 10 kfolds method.

2.4 POPULATION ESTIMATE UPDATES
The process of estimating the population for Residential structures (Yang, Hauser, et al. 2024) conflates
dwelling type (PRIM_OCC) and physical properties of structures with synthetic residences (housing
units and group quarters) generated for their containing census block group (Tuccillo et al. 2023). Popu-
lation estimates (median and 95% coverage interval) are then assigned to each structure based on the block

 12

group-level distribution of synthetic household sizes weighted by the conflation score. As with the previ-
ous version, we directly used the weighted household size distribution to estimate population for one-unit
residential structures (such as single-family detached and mobile homes). For multifamily Residential
structures, we multiplied this distribution by the estimated number of units in the structure by building
floor area (planar area × number of floors) and facility occupancy estimates (Stewart et al. 2016; Urban et
al. 2023). However, in contrast to the previous version, we now account for vacant housing (i.e., house-
hold size 0) when computing the weighted household size distribution. Accompanying this change for
one-unit structures is conflation of structure building floor area with synthetic housing unit room count
rather than household income, as in the previous version. This adjustment was necessary because income
is not reported for vacant housing units in the synthetic residence data. Additionally, room count as a con-
flation criterion is more conceptually related to observed building floor area than household income. The
improved population estimate approach has applied to 27 states and Washington D.C. where no geometry
updates were provided during Phase 3 (including 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 updates). We also updated
population estimates to the dataset covering the nine priority states we worked on during the Phase 3 Pro-
totype to ensure the estimation is more accurate and consistent across the datasets. Finally, population es-
timate is also added to the 15 states where geometries were updated using the latest satellite images (as
discussed in Section 1). Population estimates are not provided for five territories.
Similar to the previous version (Yang, Hauser, et al. 2024), we validated the population estimates by com-
paring the aggregate 95% coverage interval estimate for Residential structures by county to the official es-
timate from the American Community Survey (ACS) 2019 5-year estimates (table B01001, variable 001E)
(U.S. Census Bureau 2020a). At the county level, a 95% coverage interval of the estimate that bounds the
reported ACS population is considered accurate, whereas one that is either above or below the ACS esti-
mate may feature higher instances of estimation errors at the block group level.
Figure 8 compares the accuracy of Phase 2 county-level population estimates between the previous and
new methodologies. The updated accounting for vacant housing units substantially improves overall ac-
curacy rate, particularly for predominantly rural counties in the Upper Midwest for which the previous
method tended to overestimate total population.

 3. DATASET PREPARATION AND DELIVERABLE
The workflow that was used to merge new structure detections with Phase 2 detections was previously de-
scribed in Section 2.2 of the previous report (Yang, Hauser, et al. 2024). This workflow is referred to as
the established method. However, various scenarios were examined to improve the logic and methodol-
ogy applied in 2024. The main limitation of the established method is the assumption that the older lidar-
generated structures from 133 Cities should be prioritized over the new structure detections. In larger
cities, this assumption implies that many new geometries were not being included if 133 Cities have cover-
age over those.
This assumption was reevaluated. The goal was to create a more sophisticated and balanced merging strat-
egy that can effectively use the new geometries extracted from AFE using latest satellite images while
considering the benefits offered by lidar-derived 133 Cities data. The initial approach explored the po-
tential use of parcel data to refine the merging strategy. Specifically, we assumed that the “year built” at-
tribute within the parcel records could serve as an indicator of data vintage relative to the attribution IM-
AGE_DATE (Table 3, indicating the acquisition date) of the lidar-derived geometries. Under this assump-
tion, structures with a parcel year-built value more recent than the lidar acquisition would justify replacing
the lidar-derived geometry with the newly detected structure geometry. California was selected as the test
case for this evaluation. However, this approach proved ineffective because the year-built attribute in the
parcel data was too sparsely populated to support meaningful or large-scale updates to the structure layer.
A subsequent strategy focused on evaluating the spatial correspondence between Phase 3 structures and the
133 Cities structures. Three scenarios were considered. In cases of one-to-one correspondence, the Phase
3 structure was retained. In cases of one-to-many correspondence, where a single Phase 3 structure inter-
sected multiple 133 Cities structures, the Phase 3 structure did not replace the existing geometries. Con-
versely, in cases of many-to-one correspondence—where multiple Phase 3 structures intersected a single
133 Cities structure—the Phase 3 geometries were used to replace the existing structure. This methodol-
ogy substantially improved the inclusion rate of Phase 3 structures while reducing redundancies in the 133
Cities dataset for California.
Table 5 shows the difference in structure counts from the previously adapted merging logic and this newly
developed merging strategy. The merging approach increased the inclusion of Oak Ridge National Lab-
oratory (ORNL) Phase 3 structures by 44% (from 5.9 million to 8.4 million) and decreased 133 Cities
structures by 52% (from 4.9 million to 2.3 million). Note that we observe the quality of the AFE is com-
parable to lidar, except when structures are radially distorted, or so close that facades overlap with adjacent
building’s roofs (merged residential structures). This new merging logic was implemented and tested with
California data. With this year’s refresh, the total structure count within USSUSS data set comes to 143.8
million. Additionally, there are 28 attributes populated of the 32 within the schema. Table 6 describes the
progression of structure counts and attributes throughout the four major releases of this dataset.

 4. ONGOING WORK
There are two research and development threads ongoing aimed to support future project needs in efficient
and high-quality data refresh at scale : 1) changes-guided geometry updates and 2) AFE model improve-
ments.

4.1 CHANGE DETECTION WITH SYNTHETIC APERTURE RADAR
Building on earlier progress in synthetic aperture radar (SAR)-based change detection, this line of work is
geared toward enabling a tip-and-cue mechanism that will allow the AFE workflow to focus only on areas
where significant changes are observed. This mechanism aims to support annual geometry updates to the
USS dataset. The team has made significant progress in algorithmic improvements and benchmark dataset
creation for urban change detection using Sentinel 1 SAR data.

4.1.1 Change Detection Algorithmic Improvements
We built upon the pixel-wise t-test approach that was designed to detect persistent building damage de-
tection proposed by Ballinger (2024), which uses pre- and post-event Sentinel-1 SAR backscatter ampli-
tude to capture changes between two temporal windows. We repurposed this approach to conduct change
detection over urban areas to address our need. To prototype this capability, we leveraged Google Earth
Engine (Amani et al. 2020) Sentinel-1 ground-range detected time-series data for efficient computation of
the t-test and implemented a scalable postprocessing workflow to generate refined change maps. Based on
experimental results, we further enhanced the algorithm with adaptive orbital pass selection during data
acquisition and morphological operations during postprocessing to improve the quality of detected change
regions. By leveraging adaptive orbital pass selection, we obtained more images for the t-test, thereby in-
creasing true positives. Incorporating filtering components such as the Dynamic World Landcover (Brown
et al. 2022) product and the US Large-Scale Solar Photovoltaic Database (Fujita et al. 2025) further im-
proved performance by reducing vegetation-related and solar panel false positives, respectively. These
improvements have further refined the change maps, ensuring their relevance for updating the USS AFE
workflow.

4.1.2 Need for Benchmark Dataset
Monitoring urban built-up changes is essential for capturing the dynamics of urbanization, enabling timely
disaster response, and evaluating the environmental consequences of expanding urban areas. Although
SAR, particularly Sentinel-1, offers unique advantages for consistent, cloud-penetrating, all-weather mon-
itoring, only a handful of open datasets (Hafner et al. 2021) (Hafner, Ban, and Nascetti 2023) address
change detection in urban environments, and none establish a standardized benchmark for SAR-only ap-
proaches. The limited coverage, cross-modality, and broader urban environment focus, in addition to the
absence of systematic protocols in available datasets, have constrained methodological advances, repro-
ducibility, and inter-study comparability. By addressing this gap, we seek to establish a foundation for the
systematic development, evaluation, and application of SAR-focused approaches for urban change detec-
tion.
S1 SAR Benchmark Change Detection Dataset: This curated dataset focuses specifically on the task of
detecting appearing and disappearing buildings using Sentinel-1 SAR imagery. Ground truth labels, indi-
cating precise building-level changes over time, are derived from the USS dataset. The dataset is paired
with co-aligned Sentinel-1 SAR imagery for pre- and post-time (i.e. image date in the metadata) instances
corresponding to the USS data, enabling algorithm development for detecting building-level changes in

 17

complex urban environments. As part of this effort, four areas of interest in Florida have been identified,
with QA/QC currently in progress. Figure 9 depicts one of the four AOIs in Florida with appearing (red)
and disappearing (green) buildings overlayed on Google Earth basemap imagery. Additional areas of in-
terest across the United States are also being identified to further expand coverage and diversity. This re-
search thus aims to deliver a curated, quality-checked benchmark dataset with urban structure-focused
change labels aligned with Sentinel-1 SAR imagery. The dataset is designed to support the development
of robust, scalable models for urban change detection, enabling advances in disaster monitoring, urban
growth analysis, and SAR-based remote sensing to support USS annual updates.

4.1.3 Future Directions
A future direction of this work is to complement the pixel-wise t-test approach with methods that are less
reliant on multiple pre- and post-event images. Although the t-test framework is effective when sufficient
time-series data are available, such data may not always be accessible due to acquisition gaps or the added
cost of large-scale computation. To broaden applicability, deep learning–based change detection methods
that rely on a single pre-event and post-event image pair offer a promising alternative. Benchmarking these
approaches on the proposed dataset would provide useful comparisons with statistical methods and help
advance the state of SAR-based change detection. In addition, expanding the benchmark dataset to encom-
pass more diverse geographic regions and building types will enhance its representativeness and utility for
evaluating algorithms across varied real-world settings.

4.2 EXPANSION OF TRAINING DATA TO IMPROVE AFE MODEL PERFORMANCE
In the previous updates, we identified the potential value of leveraging false positives to enhance both
workflow efficiency and AFE algorithm development. Specifically, incorporating false positives into the
negative training set could improve model robustness while guiding future QA/QC efforts and informing
sample generation for additional training of building extraction and automated QA/QC models.
During this update, the pool of available false positives has been significantly expanded, providing a more
comprehensive foundation for model retraining and evaluation. This expanded example of false positives
introduces a broader range of error cases, including both systematic misclassifications and isolated anoma-
lies, thereby increasing the diversity of negative examples available for training.
Looking ahead, this enriched false positive pool is expected to directly improve model accuracy in several
ways:
 • Reduction of systematic errors: By exposing the model to recurring false positive patterns (e.g.,
 airports, solar panels, or boulders misclassified as structures), the likelihood of repeat misclassifica-
 tions will be reduced.
 • Improved model generalization: A more representative negative training set enables the model to
 better distinguish true building geometries from non-structure features across varied landscapes.
 • Enhanced QA/QC automation: Training automated QA/QC tools with a larger false positive in-
 ventory will strengthen their ability to detect and flag likely misclassifications, reducing manual re-
 view workload.
In parallel, efforts to generate additional training data are ongoing. These activities are focused on creat-
ing a balanced and well-curated dataset that integrates both newly identified false positives and validated
positive samples. The resulting dataset will support the development of more reliable building extraction
models and increasingly automated QA/QC frameworks. Together, these advancements represent a critical
step toward operationalizing the proposed methodology and improving the overall accuracy and efficiency
of structure mapping workflows.
