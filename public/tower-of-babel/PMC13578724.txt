Emetine dihydrochloride suppresses the invasiveness and motility of hepatocellular carcinoma cells through MAPK pathway inhibition and Twist1 protein destabilization
Authors: Haelim Yoon, Eunjeong Kang, Gyun Seok Park, Sayeon Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a crucial step in the development and metastasis of cancer. Emetine was previously proposed as an antiemetic and a protein synthesis inhibitor, but its effect on cancer metastasis has not been evaluated. Therefore, this study sought to investigate the mechanisms regulating cell migration in the context of EMT using emetine dihydrochloride (EDH) in various HCC cell lines (Huh7, Hep3B, SNU449, SNU886, and PLC-PRF-5). EDH inhibited the migration and invasion of HCC cell lines at non-toxic concentrations. EDH inhibited cancer cell motility by reducing Twist1 protein levels, a key transcription factor involved in EMT. This reduction in Twist1 suppressed the expression of mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin and vimentin) while increasing the expression of epithelial marker E-cadherin. MG132 rescue and cycloheximide chase assays suggested that EDH may reduce Twist1 protein stability. EDH also suppressed JNK and p38 MAPK signaling, pathways previously implicated in the regulation of Twist1 phosphorylation at Serine 68 (S68). Consistently, EDH-induced reduction in Twist1 stability was attenuated in cells expressing the phosphorylation-deficient Twist1 S68A mutant, supporting a possible contribution of S68-associated MAPK signaling to EDH-mediated Twist1 regulation. Collectively, these findings suggest that EDH attenuates mesenchymal characteristics and motility in HCC cells, potentially through EMT-related mechanisms associated with altered Twist1 stability. Introduction

Liver cancer is the third leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide1. Although surgical resection is the primary and most effective treatment for liver cancer patients, the prognosis for this disease remains poor due to its high 5-year recurrence rate2. Sorafenib, approved by the Food and Drug Administration in 2007, has been widely used for the treatment of advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)3. However, its clinical use is limited by adverse effects, drug resistance4 and toxicity5. Therefore, several studies have sought to enhance the anticancer activity of sorafenib by combining it with other chemicals such as doxorubicin6 and celecoxib7,8.

Most cancer-related deaths are caused by metastasis rather than the primary tumor9. Metastasis begins when cancer cells disseminate to distant organs through the circulation, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a crucial process in this progression10. During EMT, tumor cells lose epithelial properties such as cell polarity and cell–cell adhesion and acquire mesenchymal traits, including enhanced migration, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis10–12. Key EMT-inducing transcription factors (EMT-TFs) such as Twist1/2, Snail1/2, and ZEB1/2 are intricately regulated by a network of signaling pathways, including TGF-β, Wnt/β-catenin, and Notch12. The most studied intracellular signaling pathways involved in EMT-TF expression are the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 and nuclear factor kappa B12. AKT and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) including extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), and p38 pathways are known to regulate the activity of EMT-TFs13. The basic helix-loop-helix transcription factor Twist1 is a key regulator of EMT and is highly expressed in various malignancies14. In various cancer types, Twist1 has been reported to contribute to cancer invasion and metastasis by increasing intravascular migration and extravasation through blood vessel walls14. Twist1 has been shown to upregulate N-cadherin, a mesenchymal marker, and downregulate E-cadherin, an epithelial marker, at the transcriptional level through E-box elements located in the promoters of its target genes14. Additionally, Twist1 is associated with HCC metastasis, stimulating invasiveness through the expression of matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-2 and -915. Therefore, understanding the regulatory mechanism of Twist1 in the EMT process would provide valuable insights into the broader context of EMT and its impact on cancer metastasis.

Emetine, an active ingredient isolated from the ipecac, has traditionally been used as an oral emetic and expectorant, and is currently used as an antiprotozoal agent16. The mechanism of action of emetine, reported as a protein synthesis inhibitor, is to inhibit intracellular ribosomal and mitochondrial protein synthesis and to interfere with DNA and RNA synthesis17. Interestingly, emetine has recently been studied as an antiviral agent, considered a potential drug with anti-coronavirus effects by inhibiting the replication of SARS-CoV-216,18,19. Emetine is formulated as a hydrobromide or hydrochloride salt when used as a drug, which increases the solubility of insoluble amines and facilitates absorption from the gastrointestinal tract18. In the 1970s, emetine dihydrochloride (EDH) was used in clinical trials at the National Cancer Institute for its antitumor activity but was no longer developed due to severe toxicity20. Despite this, the apoptotic effect of emetine has been reported in various human cancer cell lines21,22. A recent study reported that emetine induces apoptosis not only by inhibiting protein synthesis but also by regulating anti-apoptotic genes such as Bcl-x21. This apoptosis-inducing effect makes emetine a potential anticancer drug candidate for combination therapy, with some observed effects in lung cancer patients23. Although emetine has been studied primarily for its apoptosis-inducing effects in various human cancer cells, this study aimed to identify a previously unrecognized mechanism by which emetine suppresses cancer metastasis. Here, we examined the effects of EDH on migration and invasion in HCC cell lines and investigated the underlying mechanism with a focus on EMT-related signaling.

Results

EDH regulates the motility and invasiveness of HCC cell lines

Using HCC Huh7 cells, wound healing assays were conducted as an initial screen of the drug repositioning library provided by the Korea Chemical Bank. EDH (Fig. 1a) was identified as a potential inhibitor of Huh7 cell migration. Given that cancer cells exhibit high motility and invasiveness, which contribute to metastasis, several HCC cell lines (Huh7, Hep3B, SNU449, SNU886, and PLC-PRF-5) were treated with EDH to evaluate its effects on wound healing. Treatment with increasing concentrations of EDH inhibited wound closure in a dose-dependent manner compared with that in untreated cells (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. S1a). Emodin, a reported inhibitor of cancer cell migration and invasion, was used as a positive control. Because wound closure reflects the combined effects of cell migration and proliferation, Transwell assays were performed to more specifically evaluate the effects of EDH on HCC cell motility and invasiveness. Figure 1c shows that cell migration decreased in Huh7 and Hep3B cells treated with EDH in a concentration-dependent manner. Furthermore, cell invasion assays using Matrigel-coated Transwell membranes indicated that EDH inhibited invasiveness in HCC cell lines (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. S1b). Notably, EDH inhibited the migration and invasion of HCC cells more effectively than the positive control, emodin, at lower concentrations. To further assess whether EDH affects EMT-associated cellular phenotypes, we examined morphological changes in Huh7 cells after EDH treatment. EDH-treated cells displayed a more rounded morphology compared with untreated control cells, suggesting a reduction in mesenchymal-like features (Supplementary Fig. S2). To confirm that the inhibitory effect of EDH on cancer cell migration in HCC cell lines was not due to cell death, the cytotoxicity of EDH on Huh7 and Hep3B cell lines was evaluated both under cell growth conditions (10% FBS in the medium) and growth inhibition conditions (1% FBS in the medium) (Supplementary Fig. S3a). EDH showed no significant cytotoxicity in HCC cells at concentrations up to 200 nM. Furthermore, when other HCC cell lines (SNU449, SNU886, and PLC-PRF-5) were treated with EDH, at least 80% of the cells survived at 200 nM EDH (Supplementary Fig. S3b). Therefore, in subsequent experiments, HCC cell lines were treated with EDH concentrations of 200 nM or less to investigate the mechanisms by which EDH regulates cancer cell migration and invasion.

Normal cells require extracellular matrix contact to grow and divide24. In contrast, metastatic cancer cells can grow in an anchorage-independent manner in soft agar. Soft agar colony formation assays were performed to determine whether EDH inhibits the anchorage-independent colony formation of cancer cells. When Huh7 cells were treated with EDH, colony formation was inhibited in a dose-dependent manner compared to the negative control group (Fig. 1e). Additionally, the invasiveness of multicellular tumor spheroids was evaluated through a 3D spheroid invasion assay mimicking the conditions occurring during cancer metastasis in vivo25. When spheroids derived from Huh7 cells were exposed to EDH for a duration of up to 96 h, a notable suppression in invasiveness was observed, even at a concentration of 50 nM EDH (Fig. 1f).

Under conventional two-dimensional culture conditions, EDH also reduced Huh7 cell proliferation, particularly at concentrations above 100 nM (Supplementary Fig. S4). Thus, although a contribution of growth inhibition to the wound-healing results cannot be completely excluded, particularly at higher EDH concentrations, the consistent inhibitory effects observed in the Transwell and 3D spheroid invasion assays support the conclusion that EDH attenuates the migratory and invasive properties of HCC cells.

EDH downregulates MMP activity in Huh7 cells

The progression of cancer promotes the degradation of the extracellular matrix at the primary tumor site, thereby accelerating invasion into adjacent tissues26. Given the pivotal role of MMP-2 and -9 in extracellular matrix breakdown and subsequent cancer metastasis, the impact of EDH on MMP activity was explored27. The activities of secreted MMP-2 and -9 from Huh7 cells treated with EDH were assessed using gelatin zymography assays. As the concentration of EDH increased, the activities of both MMP-2 and -9 decreased notably (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, MMP enzymatic activity assays indicated that EDH downregulated MMP collagenase activity in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2b). Because elevated MMP-2 and -9 expression is associated with increased tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis, the expression levels of MMP2 and 9 genes in response to EDH treatment were validated by qRT-PCR. EDH decreased the gene expression levels of MMP2 and 9 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2c). EDH downregulates protein levels of Twist1 in Huh7 cells

To investigate the regulatory mechanism of EDH, the expression level of Twist1 protein was evaluated. Twist1 plays a pivotal role as a transcription factor in orchestrating EMT and facilitating cancer invasion by modulating the expression of critical genes like E-cadherin and N-cadherin12. EDH decreased Twist1 protein levels in Huh7 cells in a dose-dependent manner, whereas the levels of other EMT-TFs, Snail and ZEB1, were not significantly affected (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. S5). At the highest concentration (200 nM), EDH treatment remarkably decreased Twist1 protein expression, not only in Huh7 cells but also in other HCC cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S6). Notably, EDH significantly reduced Twist1 expression at lower concentrations than pelitinib (Fig. 3b). Pelitinib, used as a positive control, is a compound reported to suppress HCC cell migration and invasion by inhibiting Twist1. EDH did not inhibit the transcriptional expression of TWIST1, indicating that its regulatory effect on Twist1 protein occurs post-transcriptionally (Fig. 3c). To further investigate whether EDH affects Twist1 protein stability, we performed MG132 and cycloheximide (CHX) chase experiments. When EDH-treated Huh7 cells were co-treated with MG132, a potent proteasome inhibitor, the reduction in Twist1 protein levels was prevented (Fig. 3d). Under conditions of translation inhibition by CHX, the decrease in Twist1 protein levels in the presence of EDH occurred more rapidly than when treated with CHX alone (Fig. 3e). EDH regulates target genes of Twist1

Given the inhibitory effect of EDH on Twist1 protein expression in Huh7 cells, both mRNA and protein levels of Twist1 target genes were investigated. EDH-treated Huh7 cells exhibited increased mRNA expression of the epithelial marker CDH1 (E-cadherin protein coding gene), whereas the mRNA levels of mesenchymal markers CDH2 (N-cadherin protein coding gene) and VIM (Vimentin protein coding gene) decreased (Fig. 4a). The protein levels of E-cadherin exhibited a dose-dependent increase upon EDH treatment, whereas those of N-cadherin and Vimentin decreased (Fig. 4b). To further assess whether EDH affects EMT-related transcriptional activity, we performed a Ferroptosis Suppressor Protein 1 (FSP1) reporter assay in Hep3B cells, using FSP1 promoter activity as a functional readout of transcriptional changes associated with metastatic potential28. Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) markedly increased FSP1 reporter activity, whereas EDH dose-dependently suppressed PMA-induced FSP1 activation (Supplementary Fig. S7). EDH inhibits MAPK signaling in HCC cell lines

Multiple studies have provided insights into the molecular mechanisms involved in the EMT-mediated metastasis process10,12, with the MAPK signaling pathway playing a key role in regulating the Twist1 protein levels12. Given that EDH treatment decreases Twist1 protein levels, the phosphorylation on the activation loop of MAPKs (ERK, JNK, and p38) was examined by immunoblot analysis in EDH-treated Huh7 cells (Fig. 5a). EDH decreased the activation loop phosphorylation of MAPKs, and EDH inhibited the phosphorylation of JNK and p38 more strongly than that of ERK. Treatment with either SB203580, a specific p38 inhibitor, or JNK inhibitor VIII decreased Twist1 protein levels (Fig. 5b). However, it is worth noting that the reduction of the Twist1 protein level by these inhibitors was much lower than that by EDH. Because MAPKs stabilize Twist1 through phosphorylation at serine 68 (S68) of Twist129, a phosphorylation-defective Twist1 mutant in which serine 68 was replaced with alanine (S68A) was overexpressed in Hep3B cells, after which the cells were treated with EDH. While EDH treatment decreased the protein level of wild-type Twist1, the protein level of Twist1 S68A remained unaffected (Fig. 5c). Overexpression of the Twist1 S68A mutant retained its ability to promote cell migration in Hep3B cells, while EDH-induced inhibition of cell migration was attenuated in cells expressing Twist1 S68A compared with empty vector- or Twist1 WT-transfected cells (Supplementary Fig. S8). Collectively, these results suggest that inhibition of MAPK phosphorylation may be involved, at least in part, in the EDH-associated reduction of Twist1 stability. Considering that EDH regulates JNK and p38 and sorafenib regulates ERK30, these findings suggest that EDH and sorafenib may exert complementary effects on MAPK signaling. When sorafenib and EDH were administered together, cell motility and invasiveness were further decreased (Supplementary Fig. S9a,b). Additionally, co-treatment of these drugs further decreased the expression of Twist1 in Huh7 cells without affecting cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. S9c,d). These results suggest that combined treatment with sorafenib and EDH further suppresses Huh7 cell migration, possibly through enhanced inhibition of Twist1. In summary, EDH treatment was associated with reduced Twist1 stability and suppression of MAPK signaling pathways (Fig. 5d). Discussion

The high mortality rate of HCC has been attributed to early metastasis, including local or portal vein invasion as well as distant metastasis, and resistance to drug therapy11. Multiple studies have demonstrated the growth-inhibitory and apoptosis-inducing effects of EDH. However, the mechanisms by which EDH regulates EMT remain largely unexplored18. In the present study, EDH was initially identified as a potential anti-metastatic compound based on its inhibitory effect on wound closure. Because wound-healing assays can be influenced by both cell migration and proliferation, Transwell and 3D spheroid invasion assays were performed to provide additional evidence that EDH attenuates the migratory and invasive properties of HCC cells. On the basis of these observations, we further explored the cellular mechanisms potentially underlying the anti-metastatic activity of EDH.

The relationship between cancer cell metastasis and EMT-TFs has been reported in numerous studies10–12. Although most experiments in this study were performed in Huh7 cells, EDH also decreased Twist1 protein levels in other tested HCC cell lines, including Hep3B, SNU449, SNU886, and PLC-PRF-5. EDH accelerated the reduction of Twist1 protein levels under conditions of protein synthesis inhibition, whereas the reduction in Twist1 protein levels was abolished by proteasome inhibition. Taken together, the finding that EDH does not regulate TWIST1 mRNA expression suggests that EDH regulates Twist1 stability at the post-translational level through proteasome-dependent degradation. However, the direct role of EDH in Twist1 regulation via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway remains to be elucidated. Furthermore, because EDH is a known inhibitor of protein synthesis, a contribution of translational inhibition cannot be fully excluded. Thus, the observed reduction in Twist1 protein levels may reflect a combination of decreased de novo synthesis and enhanced protein turnover. MAPKs are known to phosphorylate Twist1 at S68, thereby increasing its protein stability29. Given that EDH inhibited MAPK signaling, particularly JNK and p38, these findings suggest that suppression of MAPK activity may be involved, at least in part, in the EDH-associated reduction of Twist1 protein levels. Furthermore, the attenuated effect of EDH in cells expressing the Twist1 S68 mutant, together with reduced Twist1 levels following treatment with JNK and p38 inhibitors, suggests that MAPK signaling may contribute to the regulation of Twist1 stability. Since Twist1 S68 phosphorylation was not directly measured in this study, MAPK-dependent regulation of Twist1 through the S68 site should be considered a putative mechanism requiring further experimental verification. Interestingly, EDH decreased Twist1 expression more effectively at a lower concentration (200 nM) than JNK and p38 inhibitors (10 and 5 µM, respectively). Considering that EDH was used at low nanomolar concentrations (< 200 nM) and short exposure times (< 24 h), the observed suppression of Twist1 and modulation of JNK, ERK, and p38 signaling by EDH are more consistent with context-dependent signaling and post-translational regulation than with nonspecific translational inhibition, suggesting that EDH may act as an effective inhibitor of Twist1 at relatively low concentrations. Taken together, our findings are consistent with a model in which EDH suppresses EMT-associated migratory and invasive behavior, at least in part, through MAPK-dependent destabilization of Twist1. However, because functional rescue experiments were not performed in the present study, a definitive causal role of Twist1 in mediating these effects remains to be established. Twist1 is widely recognized as a regulator of cell migration and invasion, as well as a key mediator of EMT, which has been increasingly linked to therapeutic resistance across multiple cancer types. Previous studies have shown that mesenchymal-like pancreatic cancer cells exhibit enhanced metabolic activity, invasive capacity, and resistance to anticancer agents compared with epithelial-like cells, suggesting that EMT status may be closely associated with treatment responsiveness31,32. In this context, the suppressive effects of EDH on Twist1 expression and EMT-associated phenotypes may reflect broader regulation of EMT-related cellular states, which could potentially enhance therapeutic sensitivity in HCC33. Moreover, reduced drug responsiveness associated with specific cellular states has been reported to be overcome through combination strategies that target additional signaling pathways34. Considering that sorafenib is an anti-HCC drug that targets ERK-related signaling, the combined effects of EDH and sorafenib on Twist1 regulation warrant further investigation. Additionally, Twist1 overexpression in Huh7 cells has been reported to increase resistance to sorafenib4, suggesting that EDH may have potential as a complementary agent. However, our study examined the combined effects of EDH and sorafenib only at the cellular level, and the underlying mechanisms by which these two compounds jointly regulate Twist1 expression remain to be clarified.

Overexpression of Twist1 in HCC cell lines has been reported to upregulate CDH2 and VIM while downregulating CDH114. Matsuo et al. reported that Twist1 overexpression in HCC tissues is associated with metastasis and is inversely correlated with E-cadherin expression35. EDH regulates the expression of EMT markers at the mRNA level by reducing Twist1 protein levels. This suggests that EDH suppresses the motility of HCC cells by increasing E-cadherin expression and decreasing N-cadherin and vimentin expression. In addition, EDH suppressed PMA-induced FSP1 reporter activity, providing further support for its potential role in regulating EMT-associated phenotypes and mesenchymal-like cellular characteristics. Additionally, EDH affects the activity and expression of MMPs, which may further contribute to the reduced motility of HCC cells. More than 20 MMPs are involved in ECM degradation during cancer progression27, with MMP-2 and -9 serving as key mediators and biomarkers of metastasis36. Khales et al. reported that Twist1 upregulates MMP expression and activity in esophageal squamous-cell carcinoma cells37. Collectively, our findings suggest that the EDH-induced reduction in MMP activity, particularly that of MMP-2 and MMP-9, may contribute to the suppression of cell motility through Twist1 downregulation.

Because our study was conducted exclusively in vitro, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the role of EDH in EMT regulation. In this study, we demonstrated that EDH selectively reduced Twist1 protein levels without affecting other key EMT regulators, including Snail and ZEB1. However, the direct mechanism by which EDH regulates Twist1 remains to be elucidated. Although our findings suggest that EDH promotes proteasome-dependent reduction in Twist1 protein levels, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this process remain to be elucidated. Specifically, this study did not directly assess ubiquitin-mediated degradation or identify potential upstream E3 ligases. Further investigation will therefore be required to determine whether ubiquitination contributes to EDH-induced Twist1 turnover. Future transcriptomic analyses, such as RNA sequencing, are warranted to enable a broader evaluation of EDH-mediated regulation of EMT-related genes. Such studies would help exclude potential indirect or compensatory effects of EDH and allow assessment of whether these findings are generalizable across different cancer types. Emetine has previously been evaluated in clinical settings, including as an anticancer agent, but its clinical application has been limited by dose-dependent toxicity. In this context, direct comparison between the concentrations used in the present in vitro study and clinically administered doses remains challenging. Notably, the current study was performed at low nanomolar concentrations of EDH, which differ from the higher doses historically associated with cytotoxic effects. Therefore, further pharmacokinetic and in vivo studies are required to define clinically relevant and achievable concentrations and to determine whether the mechanistic effects observed at low doses can be translated into therapeutic applications. In particular, in vivo validation using appropriate animal models, such as HCC xenograft models, would further strengthen the clinical relevance of the inhibitory effects of EDH on migration and invasion. Such studies would also help validate the EMT-related molecular changes observed in vitro within a more complex biological context.

Our findings suggest that EDH may have potential as a candidate compound for attenuating metastatic characteristics of HCC cells, potentially through modulation of MAPK signaling and reduction of Twist1 protein levels. Since EDH was used at low nanomolar concentrations not exceeding 200 nM and for short exposure times of less than 24 h, the observed suppression of Twist1 and modulation of JNK, ERK, and p38 signaling are more consistent with context-dependent signaling and post-translational regulation than with nonspecific translational inhibition. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to investigate the anticancer effects of EDH in the context of EMT regulation. Although previous studies have shown that emetine inhibits cancer invasion by downregulating MAPK signaling in lung cancer cells38, its association with EMT-related transcription factors has not been well characterized. Given its inhibitory effects on cancer cell migration through Twist1 regulation, EDH may have potential as a candidate for HCC treatment. Although the mechanistic analysis of EDH in this study was primarily conducted in Huh7 cells, EDH consistently reduced Twist1 expression across multiple HCC cell lines. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to confirm the generalizability of the proposed mechanism and to evaluate the potential combined effects of EDH with other therapeutic agents.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

Emetine dihydrochloride (cat. No. 324693) used in the experiment was purchased as a powder from EMD Millipore Corp (Billerica, MA, USA) and dissolved in pure water. Pelitinib (HY-32718), JNK inhibitor VIII (JNK inhibitor, HY-107598) and SB203580 (p38 inhibitor, HY-10256) were purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). MG132 (474790), Sorafenib (SML2653), cycloheximide (CHX; C4859), and FLAG antibodies (F3165) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Twist1 (sc-81417), JNK (sc-7345), p38 (sc-7972), Vimentin (sc-32322), and β-actin (sc-47778) were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. (Dallas, TX, USA). ERK1/2 (#9102), p-ERK1/2 (#9106), p-p38 (Thr180/Tyr182, #9211), p-JNK (Thr183/Tyr185, #9251), ZEB1 (#70512), and Snail (#3879) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology, Inc. (Danvers, MA, USA). E-cadherin (610182) and N-cadherin (610720) antibodies were obtained from BD Biosciences (San Jose, CA, USA). Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (GTX100118) was obtained from GeneTex (Irvine, CA, USA). Polyclonal anti-mouse IgG Fc-tagged antibodies (LF-SA8001) and polyclonal anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (LF-SA8002) were purchased from AbFrontier (Young In Frontier Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea).

Cell culture

HCC cells (Huh7, Hep3B, PLC-PRF-5, SNU886, and SNU449) were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium or Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (WELGENE, Seoul, Korea) containing 1% penicillin–streptomycin (GIBCO, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; WELGENE, Seoul, Korea). All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Cell viability assay

Cells (4 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight in a 5% CO2 humidified air atmosphere. The cells were then treated with a mixture of EDH (50, 100, 200, and 400 nM) in a medium containing either 1% or 10% FBS. Cell viability and cell growth assays were performed using EZ-Cytox (DOGEN, Seoul, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions after EDH treatment.

Wound healing assay

The day after seeding HCC cells (Huh7, Hep3B, PLC-PRF-5, SNU886, and SNU449) into 24-well plates (at 90% confluency), wounds were created using a white pipette tip. The culture medium was then aspirated and replaced with fresh medium containing 1% FBS and various concentrations of emetine dihydrochloride (50, 100, and 200 nM). The scratched cells were incubated for 24 and 48 h, and cells that migrated to the wound surface were observed using a JuLI-Stage Real-Time Cell History Recorder (NanoEnTek Inc., Seoul, Korea). Wound closure was quantified using ImageJ software by measuring the wound area at each time point and expressing the values as the percentage of wound closure relative to the initial wound area. The change in wound closure is expressed as a percentage of wound healing in the treated groups compared to the control group. Each experiment was repeated three times, and the results were averaged.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration was analyzed using a 24-well Transwell plate (pore size: 8 µm, SPL, Pocheon, Korea) containing a polycarbonate membrane. Huh7 cells and Hep3B cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in the upper chamber. The cells were then treated with 250 μl of medium containing 1% FBS, with or without EDH. The lower chamber was filled with 500 μl of medium containing 10% FBS. Huh7 cells were incubated for 6 h and Hep3B cells for 12 h to allow migration through the membrane. The migrated cells on the membrane were then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. Cells attached to the upper surface of the membrane were removed using cotton wool. The migrated cells were visualized by photographing the membrane under a microscope at 100× magnification. To perform the Matrigel invasion assay, 30 μl of diluted Matrigel (0.5 mg/ml; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was coated in the upper chamber for 3 h. This procedure was similar to the migration assay, except that the Huh7 cells were incubated for 18 h and the Hep3B, PLC-PRF-5, SNU886, and SNU449 cells were incubated for 24 h. For quantification, migrated or invaded cells were counted in three randomly selected microscopic fields per membrane at 100× magnification, and the mean value was calculated for each insert. Data were obtained from three independent experiments. Representative images shown in the figures were selected from experiments yielding results consistent with the quantified data.

Analysis of soft agar colony formation

The bottom of a 12-well plate was coated with 2 × cell culture medium containing 0.5% agarose (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Huh7 cells (4 × 103 cells/well) were added to the coated upper layer by mixing with cell culture medium containing 0.3% agarose. Both the top and bottom layers were incubated at 4 °C for 30 min each to make them semi-solid. The uppermost layer was then supplemented with cell culture medium with or without EDH. Fresh medium was replaced every 3 days, and the cells were incubated for 14 days at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Colonies were stained with 0.05% crystal violet solution for 10 min at room temperature and decolorized using PBS. Each stained well was photographed using a digital camera.

Gelatin zymography

Huh7 cells (2 × 106 cells/well) were inoculated into a 12-well culture plate and cultured for one day. After washing with PBS, EDH was mixed in serum-free medium at each concentration (50, 100, and 200 nM), and 500 μl of each mixture was added to each well. After culturing for 24 h, the cultured medium was collected and mixed with sample buffer [2.5 mM Tris–Cl (pH 6.8), 5% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 50% glycerol, 0.05% bromophenol blue]. The samples were then subjected to electrophoresis on 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gel containing 0.1% gelatin (G1393, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The separated gel was washed with a wash buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 0.2 M NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2, 1 µM ZnCl2 and 2.5% Triton X-100] at room temperature for 30 min. Substrate buffer (0.016% NaN3 and 1% Triton X-100) was added to the wash buffer, and the gel was incubated on a shaker at 37 °C for 48 h. After fixing for 30 min with fixing solution (40% methanol and 10% acetic acid), the gel was stained with 0.25% Coomassie Brilliant Blue for 1 h. For decolorization, a decolorization solution (5% methanol and 8% acetic acid) was used.

MMP activity assay

Huh7 cells (2 × 106 cells/well) were seeded into 12-well culture plates and incubated for 24 h in serum-free medium with or without EDH. MMP activity was analyzed using the collected medium with the Gelatinase/Collagenase Assay Kit (E12055, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Three-dimensional (3D) Spheroid Invasion analysis

Huh7 cells (2 × 103 cells/well) were inoculated into a low-attachment 96-well plate (34896, SPL, Pocheon, Korea) and cultured for 72 h. After removing the medium, 3 mg/ml of Matrigel (356230, BD Biosciences, USA) was added, and the cells were further cultured for 24 h. The resulting spheroids were then treated with EDH in 10% FBS medium. Spheroids were recorded at 24-h intervals using a JuLI-Stage Real-Time Cell History Recorder (NanoEnTek Inc., Seoul, Korea).

Immunoblotting analysis

After Huh7 cells were treated with EDH, cells were lysed in a lysis buffer [20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Triton X-100, 0.5% IGEPAL CA-630, 1 mM EDTA, 1% glycerol; with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 10 mM NaF, and 1 mM Na3VO4]. EDH-treated and untreated protein samples were mixed with equal volumes of 5× SDS sample buffer [12 mM Tris–HCl (pH 6.8), 5% glycerol, 0.4% SDS, 0.02% bromophenol blue, and 1% β-mercaptoethanol]. After mixing, the samples were boiled at 100 °C for 5 min. The samples were then electrophoresed on 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The protein-containing membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dry skim milk and then incubated with specific primary antibodies (all antibodies diluted at 1:1000) in 3.3% BSA overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were washed several times with 1× Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 and then incubated with a secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescent immunoblotting detection reagent (Pierce; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). All experiments were performed at least in triplicate, and the band intensity of each protein was analyzed with ImageJ.

Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) analysis and quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Huh7 cells were seeded in a 12-well plate and treated with EDH for 24 h to extract total RNA (Labozol, COSMO Genetech, Seoul, Korea). Using the extracted RNA (1 µg), complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized with the TOPscript cDNA synthesis kit (Enzynomics, Daejeon, Korea). RT-PCR and qRT-PCR were performed using the synthesized cDNA. Fluorescence values of PCR amplicons were obtained by performing qRT-PCR with a CFXConnect real-time thermocycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Gene expression levels were normalized to the GAPDH gene. According to the 2−ΔΔCq method, gene expression levels were expressed compared to the control group. The RT-PCR and qRT-PCR primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Transfection

Huh7 cells were inoculated to 70% confluency in a 6-well plate and incubated overnight. For transfection, the plasmid and linear polyethyleneimine (PolySciences, Warrington, USA) were mixed at a 1:3 ratio and left at room temperature for 30 min. Then, the mixture was added to each well and further cultured for 48 h.

CHX chase assay

Huh7 cells were pretreated with CHX (10 µg/mL) for 1 h to inhibit de novo protein synthesis, followed by treatment with EDH (200 nM) in the presence of CHX. Cells were harvested at the indicated time points (0, 0.5, 1, and 3 h) after EDH treatment and then lysed using the same lysis buffer described for the immunoblotting. Equal amounts of protein were subjected to immunoblot analysis using anti-Twist1 antibody, and GAPDH used as the loading control. Twist1 protein levels were quantified by densitometry and normalized to GADPH levels to assess degradation kinetics over time.

Characterization of morphological alterations

To observe morphological changes in Huh7 cells following EDH treatment, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 103 cells per well. The cells were then treated with 50 nM of EDH in medium containing 10% FBS. Morphological alterations were monitored for 24 h using a JuLI-Stage Real-time Cell History Recorder (NanoEnTek Inc., Seoul, Korea).

Luciferase (Luc) reporter assay

Hep3B cells were seeded into 12-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 ℃. On the following day, cells were transfected with FSP1L-Luc reporter (0.5 µg) and gWIZ-green fluorescence protein (GFP; 0.1 µg) plasmids as detailed in the Transfection section. GFP served as an internal control to normalize the luciferase reporter activity. The cells were pre-treated with EDH (50 and 100 nM) for 2 h prior to 50 nM PMA stimulation for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were lysed using a 1× cell culture lysis reagent (Promega Madison, WI, USA) and analyzed with a luciferase assay reagent (Promega Madison, WI, USA). Luminescence and fluorescence were measured using a multi-well plate reader (Synergy H1: Luminometer filter).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Prism 3.0 statistical analysis program (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All data represent the mean value obtained from three or more independent experiments, and the error is presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Post hoc analysis was conducted using Holm-Šídák’s method, and statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. Non-significant differences are marked with “N.S.” in the graph.
