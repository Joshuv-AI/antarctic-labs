Abstract
This report summarizes analytical estimates of beam intensity limitations in the Beam
Accumulator Ring (BAR), a part of the Electron-Ion Collider injector complex under development
at Brookhaven National Laboratory. The BAR is designed to accumulate a polarized electron beam
with a single-bunch charge up to 28 nC for subsequent acceleration in the Rapid Cycling
Synchrotron and then injection into the Electron Storage Ring. Analytical models of longitudinal
and transverse collective effects are developed using a broadband impedance model calibrated
with the beam-based measurement results from NSLS VUV. The study evaluates the impact of
resistive-wall and geometric impedances, assessing microwave and transverse mode coupling
instabilities, bunch lengthening, and beam-induced heating.

Introduction
The Electron-Ion Collider (EIC) project is under development at Brookhaven National Laboratory
to explore the fundamental structure of matter [1]. The Beam Accumulator Ring (BAR) is a part
of the EIC injector. It is dedicated to accumulating a polarized electron beam with the high single-
bunch intensity required by the Electron Storage Ring (ESR). The beam with a charge of about
1 nC is injected from the linac into BAR with a repetition rate of 30 Hz. Once the single-bunch
charge of 28 nC is achieved, the beam is extracted from the BAR and injected into the Rapid
Cycling Synchrotron (RCS), where it is accelerated up to the full energy of 5 to 18 GeV for
injection into the ESR.
 The BAR magnet lattice is based on the NSLS VUV ring design [2]. The ring lattice
consists of four double-bend achromat arcs and four straight sections: one for injection and
extraction, one for the RF system, and two others can be used to accommodate damping wigglers
if necessary. The BAR machine and beam parameters are summarized in Table 1, its schematic
layout is shown in Fig. 1.

 A requirement for a high single-bunch charge of 28 nC at the extraction is a major
challenge for the BAR design. The interaction of such a high-intensity beam with the vacuum
chamber impedance can result in beam instabilities and local overheating of vacuum chamber
components if their impedance is not properly minimized. In the next sections, we discuss the
impedance model, longitudinal and transverse collective effects to assess the possibility of reliable
accumulation of a single bunch with the maximum charge of 32 nC (required charge of the
extracted beam with a safety margin).

chamber size looks quite risky.

Kicker ceramic chambers
The BAR injection/extraction system is based on using fast kickers, generating short (shorter than
the revolution period) pulses to kick the beam in a single turn. The length of the kicker is 30 cm
and the ceramic chamber cross-section is assumed the same as of the standard sections,
80 mm × 40 mm. The chamber is supposed to be coated by a 3 𝜇m titanium layer to conduct the
high-frequency beam-induced wall current and to be maximum transparent for the kicker pulse
field.
 Using the measured titanium resistivity 𝜌IQ = 3.85 × 10%R Ω.m obtained at NSLS-II as
discussed in Ref. [12], we estimated the beam-induced heating of the Ti-coated ceramic chamber
(6 mm ceramic wall) with the analytical formulas presented in that reference, which were
benchmarked against NSLS-II temperature measurements and showed good agreement. Fig. 10a
shows the loss factor (Eq. 17) of the kicker chamber as a function of the bunch current, taking the
intensity-dependent bunch lengthening into account.
 To explore the possibility of decreasing the vertical size of the kicker ceramic chamber,
which is beneficial for the pulser cost and reliability, we scaled the beam-induced power with the
chamber height 2𝑏 assuming the long-term average beam current to a half of the maximum value
corresponding to the charge of 32 nC. Fig. 10b shows the average beam-induced power as a
function of the vacuum chamber height 2𝑏. As one can see, the power increased by about 35%
when the vertical aperture is reduced from 40 mm to 30 mm.

Conclusion
This study provides an assessment of collective effects in the Beam Accumulator Ring (BAR) of
the EIC injector, focusing on impedance-driven instabilities and beam-induced heating. Using a
broadband impedance model calibrated with empirical data from NSLS VUV ring, the analysis
demonstrates that the BAR can accommodate the design goal of a 32 nC single-bunch charge.
With zero chromaticity, the transverse mode coupling instability threshold is higher than the design
beam current. Although the threshold of the longitudinal microwave instability is quite low, this
instability does not limit the beam intensity but results in significant bunch lengthening and energy
spread growth. The bunch length at 32 nC is about 85 mm exceeding the specifications by 20%.
The results also show that the reduction of the vacuum chamber height from 40 mm can lead to

elimination of the head-tail stability margins, which looks quite risky. These results support the
feasibility of the BAR design while highlighting critical constraints for future design iterations.
