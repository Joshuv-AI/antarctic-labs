Covariance-Guided Resource Adaptive Learning for Efficient Edge Inference
Ahmad N. L. Nabhaan 1 § , Zaki Sukma 2 § , Rakandhiya D. Rachmanto 2 , Muhammad Husni Santriaji 3 , Byungjin Cho 5 , Arief Setyanto 1 , In Kee Kim 2

Abstract
For deep learning inference on edge devices, hardware configurations achieving the same throughput can differ by 2 × \times in power consumption, yet operators often struggle to find the efficient ones without exhaustive profiling. Existing approaches often rely on inefficient static presets or require expensive offline profiling that must be repeated for each new model or device. To address this problem, we present Coral , an online optimization method that discovers near-optimal configurations without offline profiling. Coral leverages distance covariance to statistically capture the non-linear dependencies between hardware settings, e.g. , DVFS and concurrency levels, and performance metrics. Unlike prior work, we explicitly formulate the challenge as a throughput-power co-optimization problem to satisfy power budgets and throughput targets simultaneously. We evaluate Coral on two NVIDIA Jetson devices across three object detection models ranging from lightweight to heavyweight. In single-target scenarios, Coral achieves 96% – 100% of the optimal performance found by exhaustive search. In strict dual-constraint scenarios where baselines fail or exceed power budgets, Coral consistently finds proper configurations online with minimal exploration.

In battery-constrained deployments,

For real-time applications,

II-A
2
Distance Covariance

Distance covariance
[
18
]
measures statistical dependency between two variables without assuming a specific functional form.
Unlike Pearson correlation, which captures only linear relationships,
distance covariance detects arbitrary non-linear dependencies
.
Given
n
paired observations of metric
m
(e.g., throughput or power) and hardware setting
s
, we first compute the pairwise distance matrices:

a_{ij}=\|m_{i}-m_{j}\|,\quad b_{ij}=\|s_{i}-s_{j}\|

(1)

These matrices are then double-centered to remove marginal effects:

A_{ij}=a_{ij}-\bar{a}_{i\cdot}-\bar{a}_{\cdot j}+\bar{a}_{\cdot\cdot}

(2)

where
\bar{a}_{i\cdot}
,
\bar{a}_{\cdot j}
, and
\bar{a}_{\cdot\cdot}
denote the row mean, column mean, and grand mean, respectively. The distance covariance is then:

\text{dCov}^{2}(m,s)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ij}

(3)

Finally, distance correlation normalizes by the individual variances:

\text{dCor}(m,s)=\frac{\text{dCov}(m,s)}{\sqrt{\text{dCov}(m,m)\cdot\text{dCov}(s,s)}}

(4)

The resulting
dCor
ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates statistical independence and higher values indicate stronger dependency.
Throughout this paper, we use
distance covariance
to refer to this methodology, while the algorithm uses the normalized form (
dCor
) for comparability across parameters.
This property makes distance covariance well-suited for capturing the complex relationships between hardware settings and performance metrics. The relationship between hardware settings and performance is highly non-linear.
Increasing GPU frequency may boost throughput only when CPU frequency is sufficiently high.

II-B

Related Work

II-B
1
Hardware-Aware Optimization

A substantial body of work addresses hardware efficiency for DL inference.
DVFS and power management strategies have been extensively studied to navigate the fundamental trade-off between power and performance.

Profiling-based methods.

ALERT
[
19
]
demonstrated the effectiveness of coordinating application and system-level adaptation, achieving significant energy savings through probabilistic modeling with Kalman filters.
However, ALERT requires extensive offline profiling that must be repeated for each model-device combination.

Learning-based methods.

Zhang
et al.
[
22
]
proposed DVFO, using deep reinforcement learning (DRL) to co-optimize DVFS parameters for edge-cloud collaborative inference.
While effective, DRL-based methods require extensive training iterations and may struggle to converge quickly in new environments.
Yan
et al.
[
21
]
introduced PolyThrottle, applying constrained Bayesian optimization for energy-efficient inference, but such methods may still need offline exploration phases.

Online (or offline profiling-free) methods.
SHEEO
[
20
]
uses reinforcement learning to continuously optimize energy efficiency without prior profiling, monitoring runtime variations, and adapting power management strategies accordingly.

Coral
differs from these approaches by using distance covariance for lightweight online search, requiring no offline profiling, no training phase, and explicitly targeting dual-constraint scenarios where both power and throughput must be satisfied.

II-B
2
Concurrent Execution Frameworks

Recent frameworks address execution orchestration for edge inference. Miriam
[
23
]
proposes a contention-aware task coordination framework for multi-DNN inference on edge GPUs, achieving significant throughput improvements while maintaining low latency for critical tasks.
HaX-CoNN
[
1
]
enables heterogeneity-aware execution of concurrent DNNs by intelligently scheduling workloads across diverse accelerators within a System-on-Chip.
Hao et al.
[
3
]
explored maximizing inference throughput by leveraging AI multi-tenancy and dynamic model placement on heterogeneous edge devices.

Coral
complements these frameworks by functioning as a device-level optimizer. While frameworks like HaX-CoNN manage distributed scheduling,
Coral
dynamically tunes the underlying hardware parameters (concurrency level and DVFS settings) in real-time to meet specific throughput and power targets.

III
The Coral Method

III-A

Problem Formulation

We aim to find a hardware configuration that satisfies both throughput and power constraints.
We define the configuration space as:

\mathbf{s}=(s_{cpu},c_{cpu},s_{gpu},s_{mem},c)

(5)

where
s_{cpu}
is CPU frequency,
c_{cpu}
is the number of active CPU cores,
s_{gpu}
is GPU frequency,
s_{mem}
is memory frequency, and
c
is concurrency level. Each configuration
\mathbf{s}
yields a throughput
\tau(\mathbf{s})
and power consumption
p(\mathbf{s})
.

Throughput-Power Co-Optimization.

Given a throughput target
\tau_{target}
and power limit
p_{budget}
, we seek a configuration
\mathbf{s}^{*}
that satisfies both constraints:

\tau(\mathbf{s}^{*})\geq\tau_{target}\quad\text{and}\quad p(\mathbf{s}^{*})\leq p_{budget}

(6)

A configuration that violates either constraint is marked as infeasible and excluded from future exploration. Among all feasible configurations, we prefer those with higher efficiency
\eta(\mathbf{s})=\tau(\mathbf{s})/p(\mathbf{s})
.

Search Strategy.

Coral
adapts its search direction based on the current configuration’s performance.
If the throughput target is already satisfied,
Coral
attempts to reduce power consumption to improve efficiency.
Otherwise,
Coral
prioritizes increasing throughput to first reach the feasible region.
This adaptive strategy enables
Coral
to navigate toward the feasible region while optimizing for efficiency.

Challenges.
This problem has three challenges.
First, the configuration space can be large (over 2K combinations per device in our experiments).
Second, the functions
\tau(\mathbf{s})
and
p(\mathbf{s})
are non-linear and exhibit complex interactions among parameters as discussed in §
I
.
Third, the feasible region satisfying both constraints is often
narrow, making exhaustive search impractical.
These challenges motivate
Coral
’s distance covariance-based approach, which efficiently identifies high-impact parameters without exhaustive profiling.

III-B

Overview

Coral
operates in three steps at each iteration.

Step 1: Reward Evaluation (§
III-C
).

Coral
executes inference with the current configuration and measures throughput and power.
It then evaluates the results using Algorithm
1
to find 1) configurations satisfying both constraints receive a positive reward (efficiency) and 2) those violating constraints receive a penalty and are added to a prohibited list.
The best and second-best configurations are updated based on the reward.

Step 2: Correlation Analysis (§
III-D
).

Coral
computes distance correlation between each hardware parameter and both performance metrics (throughput and power) using recent observations.
This identifies which parameters have the strongest influence on performance.

Step 3: Configuration Search (§
III-E
).

Coral
uses the correlation weights to determine the next configuration to explore.
Parameters with higher correlation receive larger adjustments, while less influential parameters change minimally (Algorithm
2
).

These steps repeat for a fixed iteration budget (10 in our implementation).

III-C

Reward and Feasibility Check

Algorithm
1
evaluates each configuration by checking feasibility and computing a reward score.

Feasibility Check.

A configuration is feasible if it satisfies both constraints: throughput meets the target (
\tau\geq\tau_{target}
) and power stays within the budget (
p\leq p_{budget}
).
Configurations violating either constraint are marked as infeasible and added to a prohibited list (
PS
).
Before exploring a new configuration,
Coral
checks
PS
and skips any previously identified infeasible configurations to avoid redundant evaluations.

Reward Calculation.

For feasible configurations, the reward is the efficiency ratio:

r=\frac{\tau}{p}

(7)

Higher efficiency means more throughput per watt, which is desirable for energy-constrained edge deployments. For infeasible configurations, the algorithm returns a negative penalty:

r=-(\frac{p}{\tau})

(8)

We use this penalty to ensure that infeasible configurations always rank below feasible ones. The inverted ratio (power over throughput) further penalizes configurations that waste power without delivering throughput.

Step Size.

For each parameter dimension
i
,
Coral
computes a combined weight
\gamma_{i}=\max(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
and calculates a weighted step size:

\Delta_{i}=\frac{1}{2}|x_{i}-y_{i}|\cdot\gamma_{i}

(10)

where
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
are the values of parameter
i
in the best and second-best configurations, respectively. This step size is proportional to both the distance between good configurations and the correlation strength. Parameters with higher correlation receive larger adjustments, while less influential parameters change minimally.

Search Direction.

Coral
sets low and high bounds (

,

) from the best and second-best configurations, and adapts its search direction based on current performance. If throughput meets the target and power is above the floor,
Coral
moves toward lower values (
v_{i}=l-\Delta_{i}
) to reduce power consumption. Otherwise,
Coral
moves toward higher values (
v_{i}=h+\Delta_{i}
) to improve throughput. After computing each parameter value,
Coral
clamps it to the valid range to ensure the configuration is executable.

Power Optimization Heuristic.

When the throughput target is satisfied but power remains above the floor,
Coral
sets CPU frequency to minimum and concurrency to maximum. This leverages the insight that CPU is a dominant power consumer, and higher concurrency can compensate for reduced CPU performance.

Once the configuration is set, the object detection inference is executed.
The resulting performance metrics, throughput (fps) and power consumption (m
\mathrm{W}
), are measured and fed back to the optimizer for evaluation.
This feedback loop continues for a fixed number of iterations. We set the iteration budget to 10, which balances search effectiveness with practical deployment constraints.
This budget is sufficient for
Coral
to converge while remaining feasible for online adaptation scenarios.

We evaluate
Coral

V
Conclusion

We presented
Coral
, an online optimization method for deep learning inference on edge devices.

Coral
addresses the throughput-power co-optimization problem,
e.g.
, satisfying both throughput targets and power budgets simultaneously,
without requiring offline profiling
.
By leveraging distance covariance, it captures non-linear dependencies between hardware parameters and performance metrics, enabling near-optimal configurations quickly and with minimal overhead.

Our evaluation on two edge devices across three object detection models (
YOLO
,
FRCNN
, and
RetinaNet
) highlights
Coral
’s effectiveness.
In single-constraint scenarios,
Coral
achieves 96% – 100% of the optimal throughput found by offline exhaustive search.
In dual-constraint scenarios with strict power and throughput constraints,
Coral
consistently finds right configurations while baseline methods either exceed power budgets or fail to meet throughput targets.
