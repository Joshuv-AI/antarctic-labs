Navigating the Numerous Frameworks for Obesity Care
Authors: Priya Sumithran, Louise A Baur
Published: 2026

More than 30 years ago, the World Health Organization (WHO) defined obesity as “the degree of fat storage associated with clearly elevated health risks.” However, a lack of agreement about cutoff points for the percentage of body fat that defines obesity, and the difficulty of measuring fat mass under field conditions, led the WHO Expert Committee to propose classifying adults according to body mass index (BMI) [1]. While acknowledging that BMI does not measure fat mass or distribution, provide an indication of health, or account for the heterogeneity of obesity, it was considered “the most useful, albeit crude, population‐level measure” to estimate the prevalence of obesity and its associated risks [2] because height and weight are robust measurements and routinely included in population health surveys.

Decades later, BMI remains the most practicable measure for population descriptions and monitoring, especially in low‐resource settings, although appropriate cut points will vary with ethnicity. However, recent therapeutic advances have encouraged rethinking of our approach to obesity in individuals. A profusion of new frameworks for definition, categorization, staging, and management has been put forward [3, 4, 5], leading to understandable confusion about which to use and when. As practicing clinicians, we propose that some clarity may emerge from considering the scope of the issues each framework seeks to address and their common features.

Essential steps in obesity management include establishing the presence and impact of excess adiposity. Three prominent recent frameworks—the Lancet Commission on Clinical Obesity [3], the European Association for the Study of Obesity (EASO) framework for the diagnosis, staging, and management of obesity in adults [4], and the American Association of Clinical Endocrinology (AACE) 2025 algorithm for the evaluation and treatment of adults with obesity/adiposity‐based chronic disease [5]—concur that at an individual level, BMI alone is insufficient for these purposes. All three frameworks recommend confirmation of excess adiposity with an anthropometric measure, particularly to reduce the risk of under‐detection of abdominal fat accumulation (and consequently, under‐investigation and management of its sequelae) in adults with BMI < 30 kg/m2. The Lancet Commission framework also includes recommendations for children and adolescents.

All frameworks recommend thorough evaluation of obesity's impact on health and well‐being through history taking, physical examination, and investigations as relevant. Obesity is a major risk factor for many noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), such as type 2 diabetes, atherosclerotic disease, and several cancers. However, even in the absence of such NCDs, excess adiposity may impair a person's health or functioning (represented in the Lancet Commission's diagnostic criteria for “clinical obesity”) [3]. The EASO and AACE frameworks further recommend staging of obesity based on the presence and severity of obesity‐related complications and diseases [4, 5]. The Commission did not question the importance of staging, but it was outside the scope of its work. The AACE consensus proposes a simple three‐tier staging system [6], although several others exist [6, 7], all of which consider the cardiometabolic, mechanical, psychological, and functional impacts of obesity.

Regarding management, all three frameworks [3, 4, 5] advocate for an individualized, holistic, long‐term approach to care. Both current evidence of ill health and the prevention of future ill health are appropriate reasons to consider treatment. Formulation of specific treatment pathways was beyond the Lancet Commission's scope. Detailed recommendations, particularly with regard to choice of medications, are provided in both the AACE and the EASO frameworks for the pharmacological treatment of obesity and its complications [8], both of which recommend a complications‐centric approach.

As such, despite being generated by separate consensus groups and having different (though partially overlapping) scopes, the recommendations of each framework are largely complementary and remarkably consistent. Several major questions remain, on which it would be ideal to have a consensus view. These include how best to define obesity at both a population and individual level, prioritize access to treatment fairly and sustainably, and define treatment targets and outcomes.
