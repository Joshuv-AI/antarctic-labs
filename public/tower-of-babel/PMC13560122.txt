Endothelial–immune cells-on-chip for multiplex evaluation of drug responses through patient plasma screening
Authors: Khang Leng Lee, Hannah Su-Ann Wee, Zi Yi Teng, Hui Min Tay, Ying Jie Chee, Eunice Xiang Xuan Tan, Yock Young Dan, Mark D Muthiah, Rinkoo Dalan, Han Wei Hou, Christine Cheung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMost organ-on-chip models focus on parenchymal cell function, overlooking the critical role of endothelial–immune interactions in metabolic disease pathophysiology. Drugs for metabolic conditions, such as SGLT2 inhibitors, provide not only metabolic improvements but also vascular-protective effects, underscoring the need for human platforms that capture immunovascular dynamics. Here, we developed a human endothelial–immune cells-on-chip system integrating patient-derived blood outgrowth endothelial cells, T cells, and hepatocytes to model the liver sinusoidal microenvironment. Using plasma from type 2 diabetes patients treated with dapagliflozin, the platform revealed reduced endothelial inflammatory activation, attenuated T cell migration, and decreased hepatocyte lipid accumulation, indicating systemic modulation of vascular and hepatic phenotypes. Despite the presence of endothelial SGLT2 protein expression, glucose uptake in vitro was unaltered by dapagliflozin, suggesting these beneficial effects might occur indirectly via plasma factors. Our multiplexed, human-relevant cellular platform enables simultaneous assessment of endothelial, immune, and hepatic responses, offering a versatile tool for personalized plasma-based drug screening and preclinical evaluation of vascular-protective therapies in metabolic diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05130-1.

Introduction

The endothelium maintains vascular homeostasis by regulating vessel tone and exerting anti-inflammatory, anti-thrombotic, and antioxidant effects. Metabolic diseases such as diabetes and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease are increasingly recognized as major contributors to cardiovascular burden. Chronic systemic inflammation driven by metabolic diseases promotes endothelial dysfunction, a key driver of vascular complications [1, 2]. Furthermore, excess glucose, lipids, and reactive oxygen species disrupt endothelial function, leading to impaired vasodilation, inflammation, and loss of barrier integrity. These structural and functional changes promote atherosclerosis and exacerbate cardiometabolic disease [3]. The bidirectional relationship between metabolic syndrome and endothelial dysfunction underscores the need for therapies that provide both vascular and metabolic benefits [4].

A consistent finding across several clinical studies highlights residual inflammatory risk in patients despite being treated for cardiovascular diseases [5]. These observations support the ongoing development of pharmacological agents to reduce the inflammatory response in cardiometabolic conditions. Among the approved therapies or drugs with repurposing potential for anti-inflammatory effects, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors present as a potential candidate [6]. SGLT2 inhibitors is a class of anti-hyperglycemic drugs that act primarily on kidney tubule cells to inhibit reabsorption of renal filtered glucose [7]. Beyond its glucose control benefits, reduction in cardiovascular detrimental events and renal protection have been reported [8–11]. In clinical trials involving fatty liver disease patients, administration of SGLT2 inhibitors improve systemic liver function markers (alanine transaminase and aspartate transaminase), reduce the FIB-4 index, and have biopsy-proven benefits in attenuating hepatic inflammation and fibrosis [12–15]. While SGLT2 inhibitors are known to confer metabolic improvements, their role in attenuating vascular inflammation remains unclear. This highlights a need to facilitate investigations of anti-inflammatory strategies to preserve endothelial health.

Recently, the US FDA announced plans to phase out animal testing in the development of certain drug modalities. Instead, an integrated safety assessment using New Approach Methodologies, including in vitro and ex vivo human-based systems, will evaluate drug mechanisms. Numerous microengineered human cell-on-chip models (microphysiological systems) have advanced the field by incorporating controlled perfusion, mechanical stimulus and multicellular compartments to mimic the in vivo tissue architecture and functions [16, 17]. The designs of microfluidic systems are especially amenable to accommodate multiple cell layers to recapitulate the complex structure of native tissues and to facilitate cell-cell interaction studies. Most organotypic models primarily emphasize parenchymal cell function (e.g., hepatocytes in liver organoids, cardiomyocytes in cardiac tissues), while incorporating vascular and immune cell components remains a key challenge. Furthermore, many existing models rely on immortalized cell lines that lack patient-specific and disease-relevant phenotypes, limiting the robustness and translational relevance of assay readouts in clinical settings. We hypothesized that incorporating patient-derived endothelial cells, which retain disease-specific signatures, would enhance the pathophysiological validity of these platforms for therapeutic evaluation.

In this study, we leveraged the DMVascular clinical trial, in which newly diagnosed, drug-naïve patients with type 2 diabetes received 12 weeks of dapagliflozin in a randomized controlled design [18]. To investigate vascular protective effects, we profiled circulating biomarkers of endothelial dysfunction and established patient-derived blood outgrowth endothelial cells (BOECs) using our previously reported protocol [19]. These BOECs served as a critical cellular component for developing an endothelial–immune cells-on-chip platform incorporating T cells and a hepatic compartment for patient plasma screening. Plasma samples from patients treated with or without dapagliflozin were evaluated for endothelial activation, immune cell reactivity, hepatocyte lipid uptake, and endothelial cytokine secretion. This integrated platform enables mechanistic interrogation of the vascular-protective effects of dapagliflozin and provides a foundation for multiplex assessment of novel therapeutic modalities.

Results

SGLT2 inhibition ameliorates biomarkers associated with endothelial dysfunction in metabolic diseases

SGLT2 inhibitors, such as dapagliflozin, have been shown to confer metabolic and cardiovascular benefits, including improved glycemic control, reduced heart failure risk, and attenuation of atherosclerotic progression [20]. In this study, we leveraged the DMVascular clinical trial [18], a randomized controlled study in which newly diagnosed, drug-naïve patients with type 2 diabetes received dapagliflozin for 12 weeks (patient demographics shown in Supplemental Table S1). Consistent with prior reports, patients with metabolic diseases treated with dapagliflozin for three months exhibited significant improvements in glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and body mass index (BMI) (Fig. 1A).

Metabolic diseases and the associated metabolic inflammation increase susceptibility to vascular dysfunction [21–23]. To assess endothelial injury in this context, we employed circulating endothelial cells (CECs) as a surrogate biomarker. CECs, which are dislodged endothelial cells released into the bloodstream from damaged vessels, have been validated as a cell-based biomarker for vascular dysfunction across various conditions, including cardiovascular disease, COVID-19, metabolic disorders, preeclampsia, and sickle cell anemia [24]. Blood samples were collected from each patient, and the peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) fraction was isolated. CECs were then identified in PBMC samples via flow cytometry using the immunophenotypic profile CD45-/CD31+/CD133-/DNA+ (Fig. 1B, Supplemental Fig. S1A).

To investigate whether CECs reflect vascular injury in metabolic condition, drug-naïve patients were stratified according to vascular phenotyping and metabolic–inflammatory profiles. These included endothelium-dependent vasodilatory function, assessed by the natural logarithm of the reactive hyperemia index (LnRHI), systemic inflammation measured by high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), and conventional metabolic parameters (BMI and HbA1c). CEC levels were significantly elevated in patients with impaired endothelial function (LnRHI < 0.51) and in those with heightened systemic inflammation (hsCRP ≥ 2 mg/L), indicating a strong association between endothelial dysfunction, inflammatory burden, and endothelial cell injury (Fig. 1C). In contrast, CEC levels did not differ significantly across BMI or HbA1c categories, suggesting that endothelial injury captured by circulating CECs is more closely linked to vascular dysfunction and inflammatory activation than to global metabolic control alone.

We further assessed soluble endothelial activation markers, including soluble ICAM-1 and von Willebrand factor (VWF). While sICAM-1 and VWF did not show significant changes following dapagliflozin treatment, CECs were significantly reduced, suggesting that CECs may represent a more dynamic and responsive indicator of endothelial damage in this clinical context (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Fig. S1B and S1C). These results provide a broader evaluation of endothelial dysfunction and highlight the complementary value of CECs alongside vascular physiological measurements and conventional soluble markers.

SGLT2 inhibition does not disrupt glucose uptake in patient-derived endothelial cells despite presence of SGLT2 receptor

SGLT2 is primarily expressed in the kidney proximal tubule, where it facilitates the reabsorption of the glucose filtered by the glomerulus, contributing to its well-established metabolic benefits. Emerging evidence also suggests that SGLT2 and related transporters may be expressed in other cell types, potentially mediating additional physiological effects beyond renal glucose reabsorption. Reports have indicated SGLT2 expression in certain non-renal tissues, including pancreatic α-cells, adipocytes, and vascular smooth muscle cells, where its inhibition has been associated with anti-inflammatory, vasodilatory, and cardioprotective effects [25, 26]. To investigate whether dapagliflozin directly affects endothelial cells via SGLT2 inhibition, we sought to determine the expression of SGLT2 in endothelial cells. Since SGLT2 belongs to the solute carrier family 5 (SLC5) of transporters, which includes structurally similar receptors such as SGLT1 (SLC5A1), SGLT3 (SLC5A4), SMIT1 (SLC5A3), and SMVT (SLC5A6), we also considered the potential for off-target interactions with these transporters.

To assess the expression of SGLT2 in endothelial cells, we analyzed data from the publicly available Human Vascular Cell Atlas [27]. This resource integrates single-cell transcriptomic data from 19 human organs and tissues, providing a comprehensive gene expression profile across diverse vascular cell types. As expected, endothelial-specific markers such as PECAM1, CDH5, and CLDN5 were highly expressed across arterial, capillary, and venous endothelial cell clusters (Fig. 2A). However, transcript levels of SLC5A2 (encoding SGLT2) were low, as were those of other SLC5 family members, including SLC5A1, SLC5A3, SLC5A4, and SLC5A6. These findings suggest that while endothelial cells express key vascular markers, they exhibit minimal gene expression of SGLT2 and related transporters, raising questions about the direct endothelial-targeting effects of SGLT2 inhibitors such as dapagliflozin.

To establish patient relevance, we have generated blood outgrowth endothelial cells (BOECs) from individuals with metabolic disease and healthy controls [19], providing a physiologically relevant human cellular model. BOEC model characterization including endothelial marker expression (CD31, CD144, CD146), angiogenic capability (bead-based sprouting assay), baseline chemokine expression (CXCL10, CXCL11, CXCL12, CCL20, CX3CL1), and endothelial-immune interactions (chemotaxis assay) were validated in our previous work [19]. Consistent with other published data [28], gene profiling of BOECs revealed low but detectable expression of SLC5 family receptors, including SLC5A2 (encoding SGLT2), compared to the positive control, human kidney 2 (HK2) cells, and the negative control, human brain microvascular endothelial cells (hBMECs) (Fig. 2B). Despite the low SLC5A2 transcript levels, SGLT2 protein expression was readily detected in BOECs through immunofluorescence staining using an anti-human SGLT2 antibody (Fig. 2C). Quantification of fluorescence intensity per cell demonstrated no significant difference in SGLT2 protein levels between BOECs derived from healthy individuals and those from metabolic disease patients (Fig. 2D). Western blot analysis further substantiated immunofluorescence findings, revealing positive bands for SGLT2 in both healthy and disease patients derived BOECs (Fig. 2E). Densitometric quantification reveals similar SGLT2 protein levels in BOECs derived from healthy individuals and those from metabolic disease patients (Fig. 2E and Supplemental Fig. S2).

To assess the functional relevance of endothelial SGLT2, we performed a glucose uptake assay using 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG). Notably, treatment with dapagliflozin did not alter glucose uptake capacity in BOECs, despite confirmed SGLT2 protein expression (Fig. 2F). Whereas dapagliflozin significantly affected glucose uptake in the positive control, HK2 cells. These findings suggest that while BOECs express SGLT2 at the protein level, its functional role in endothelial glucose handling may be minimal. Furthermore, the vascular effects of dapagliflozin appear to be independent of direct modulation of endothelial glucose uptake, pointing to alternative mechanisms underlying its endothelial protective properties.

Perfused endothelial-immune cells-on-chip platform for assessing effects of SGLT2 inhibition

Endothelial cells play a critical immunomodulatory role by regulating immune cell trafficking, activation, and function, and are increasingly recognized as active participants in inflammatory regulation [29]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that adaptive T cell populations are altered in metabolic inflammation. In human obesity and type 2 diabetes, pro-inflammatory T-cell subsets, including Th1, Th17, and CD8⁺ T cells, are enriched in adipose tissue and contribute disproportionately to systemic inflammatory cytokine production compared with lean individuals [30, 31]. Consistent with this, we have previously reported T cell intensification in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease [19]. To determine whether pharmacological metabolic modulation alters systemic T cell profiles, peripheral blood T cell phenotyping was performed by flow cytometry in drug-naïve and dapagliflozin-treated patients. Notably, dapagliflozin treatment was associated with a marked reduction in circulating T cell abundance compared with untreated individuals (Supplemental Fig. S3A). Further analysis revealed no selective reduction in CD69⁺ activated T cells (Supplemental Fig. S3B), suggesting that dapagliflozin reduces overall circulating T cell numbers without overtly altering T cell activation status.

During chronic inflammation, activated endothelial cells upregulate adhesion molecules, promoting leukocyte recruitment, adhesion, and extravasation. This disrupts endothelial junctions, thereby compromising barrier integrity, leading to vascular leakage [32, 33]. To investigate the impact of dapagliflozin on endothelial-immune cell interactions and vascular barrier integrity, we utilized a dynamic flow system to mimic physiological flow conditions. Using the Ibidi perfusion system with a Y-shaped µ-slide, we simulated vessel branching to examine region-specific endothelial responses under varying flow conditions (uniform/ non-uniform flow, high/ low shear stress). Endothelial monolayers subjected to physiological shear stress (12 dyn/cm²) were perfused with CellTracker Deep Red-labeled Jurkat T cells to assess immune cell adhesion, while NeutrAvidin-FITC staining marked intercellular gaps as a measure of endothelial permeability (Supplemental Fig. S3C, D). This approach allowed us to dissect the functional consequences of SGLT2 inhibition on endothelial integrity under flow.

Patient-derived BOECs were cultured in a dynamic flow system to assess endothelial permeability with or without immune cell exposure. In the presence of immune cells, endothelial permeability markedly increased as indicated by elevated FITC stained regions (Supplemental Fig. S3E). Notably, in dapagliflozin-treated setup, addition of T cells led to pronounced reduction in endothelial permeability (Supplemental Fig. S3F, G). This highlights the importance of an immune-competent model to study drug-induced changes to immune-mediated damage to vascular integrity.

Further examining region-specific endothelial responses, in vehicle-treated controls, different flow regions did not significantly affect endothelial permeability (Supplemental Fig. S3F). However, dapagliflozin treatment led to a significant reduction in endothelial permeability, particularly in the non-uniform flow, low shear stress region, typically associated with pathological flow disturbances. Upon addition of Jurkat T cells, in the vehicle control group, endothelial barrier disruption was most pronounced in the non-uniform flow, low shear stress region (Supplemental Fig. S3G). Dapagliflozin treatment mitigated this effect, reducing intercellular FITC-labelled collagen exposure, which reflects improved endothelial barrier integrity. Consistently, Jurkat T cell adhesion to the BOEC monolayer was significantly higher in the non-uniform flow, low shear stress region in vehicle controls. Dapagliflozin treatment was associated with reduced T cell adhesion, suggesting a potential role in modulating endothelial-immune cell interactions, with possible implications for endothelial tight junction integrity.

Developing an endothelial-immune cells-on-chip microfluidic platform for multiplex functional assays

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is highly prevalent among individuals with metabolic syndromes and is associated with increased mortality driven largely by cardiovascular complications [34, 35]. Although MASLD is defined by hepatic steatosis, its progression and clinical outcomes are strongly influenced by systemic metabolic and vascular dysfunction, including insulin resistance and endothelial impairment [36]. These observations underscore the importance of vascular health in metabolic pathophysiology and provide a rationale for investigating therapeutic strategies that target vascular and inflammatory pathways in addition to metabolic control.

We constructed a liver-relevant endothelial-immune cells-on-chip to recapitulate the architecture of a liver sinusoid (Fig. 3A) by adapting our previously established arterial wall-on-a-chip model [37] (Fig. 3B). Briefly, the model enables compartmental culture of cells in 2D (monolayer) or 3D (hydrogel) with well-defined biointerfaces. BOECs derived from patients with MASLD were co-cultured on-chip with primary human hepatocytes in spatially defined vascular and hepatic compartments, respectively, with Jurkat T cells seeded into the vascular channel (Fig. 3C). Figure 3D shows representative immunostaining of VE-cadherin-expressing BOECs and albumin-positive hepatocytes within their respective channels, together with CD3-expressing T cells, illustrating the platform used to phenotype endothelial–immune interactions on-chip. To validate the multiplex functional readouts of the endothelial-immune cells-on-chip system, we first assessed whether the platform could simultaneously report endothelial inflammatory activation, endothelial-immune cell interactions, and hepatocyte lipid hallmarks across interconnected vascular and hepatic channels. We experimented with inflammatory stimulation of endothelial cells by TNF-α. As expected, TNF-α exposure significantly increased endothelial activation, evidenced by elevated ICAM-1 expression quantified by immunofluorescence staining of the BOECs (Fig. 4A). This inflammatory state was functionally accompanied by increased immune cell adhesion to the endothelial cells in the vascular channel (Fig. 4B), demonstrating that the chip reliably captured vascular-immune interactions.

In parallel, to establish disease relevance, hepatocytes were stimulated with free fatty acid (FFA) and fructose-containing media to mimic MASLD-associated liver dysfunction. A combination of palmitic acid and oleic acid is used as they are the two major diet-obtained circulating FFAs in vivo, and is commonly used to induce hepatic steatosis in in vitro chip studies for disease modelling [38–41]. Fructose is also added to recapitulate hyperglycemic systemic environment commonly seen in metabolic disease patients. Then we examined the trajectory of immune cells as they migrated from the vascular channel towards the hepatic compartment. Immune cells in the TNF-α-treated chips displayed greater migration distance relative to untreated controls (Fig. 4C), confirming that the system could resolve immune cell migratory dynamics.

Next, we profiled the hepatic channel. Hepatocytes exposed to FFA and fructose exhibit pronounced intracellular lipid accumulation characterized by higher triglyceride content, increased lipid droplet number and greater mean droplet volume, and remain viable as suggested by stable albumin expression (Fig. 4D and E). These findings verify successful induction of steatosis while ensuring hepatocyte viability within the hepatic compartment, enabling crosstalk studies between hepatocytes and endothelial-immune compartments.

Proof-of-concept of personalized plasma-based drug response screening

Distinct from the cohort analyzed in Fig. 1, a second, independent patient cohort (demographics in Supplemental Table S2) received dapagliflozin treatment for more than three months. In comparison to the first cohort, diabetic patients who received the longer-term dapagliflozin regime (second cohort) display improved lipid (LDL-C) and inflammatory profiles (hsCRP), while most metabolic traits remain comparable (Supplemental Fig. S4A). Importantly, dapagliflozin-treated patients exhibited significant improvements in systemic and vascular parameters, including reduced systemic inflammation (hsCRP), decreased arterial stiffness (as reflected by lower pulse wave velocity), and lower systolic and diastolic blood pressures (Supplemental Fig. S4B). In addition, our analysis of key inflammatory mediators associated with metabolic dysfunction [42] revealed a significant reduction in the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 following dapagliflozin treatment, whereas other measured factors (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-15, MCP-1, and CXCL10) did not show significant changes (Supplementary Fig. S4C).

To examine whether these systemic changes translate into altered endothelial inflammatory responses, plasma from dapagliflozin-treated and untreated patients in cohort 2 was introduced into the vascular channel of the chip platform (Fig. 5A). BOEC conditioned media from chips exposed to plasma of dapagliflozin-treated patients showed significantly reduced levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-15 compared with those exposed to plasma from untreated patients (Fig. 5B), consistent with an attenuated endothelial inflammatory response. Correspondingly, immunostaining revealed reduced expression of the endothelial activation marker ICAM-1 within the vascular channel (Fig. 5C).

Despite these molecular changes, the number of Jurkat T cells adhered to the endothelium did not differ significantly between the two plasma conditions (Fig. 5D). However, immune cell dynamics downstream of adhesion was altered, as evidenced by a shorter migration distance of T cells in chips treated with dapagliflozin-treated plasma (Fig. 5E), indicating that the drug-associated changes in systemic condition could be involved in modulating immune cell dynamics.

Across the hepatic compartment, exposure to dapagliflozin-treated plasma also led to decreased lipid accumulation, reflected by a lower percentage of lipid droplet-positive hepatocytes (Fig. 5F). The observed attenuation of endothelial activation and hepatic lipid accumulation may be, at least in part, mediated through modulation of IL-6–associated inflammatory signaling. We acknowledge that the effects are likely multifactorial and that additional unidentified plasma components may also contribute. Collectively, these multi-compartmental readouts demonstrated that the platform captured treatment-associated vascular and hepatic phenotypes, supporting its utility for personalized plasma-based drug response screening.

Discussion

Metabolic diseases are strongly associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [43], prompting contemporary diabetes care guidelines, including those from the American Diabetes Association, to prioritize therapies that deliver metabolic improvements together with proven cardiovascular benefit. For example, SGLT2 inhibitors have demonstrated vascular protective effects beyond their metabolic benefits in clinical studies [44]. Given their pleiotropic properties, exploring their potential to modulate vascular inflammation is of growing interest. This underscores the need for fit-for-purpose, human cell-based models to evaluate such therapeutic effects.

Endothelial dysfunction promotes aberrant immune cell adhesion and transmigration, amplifying inflammation and accelerating atherosclerosis [45, 46]. Beyond a marker of vascular injury, it actively drives immune dysregulation in metabolic disease, highlighting its therapeutic relevance. In this study, we established an endothelial–immune cell-on-chip platform that recapitulates key features of the liver sinusoidal microenvironment by integrating patient-derived endothelial cells, immune cells, and hepatocytes under disease-relevant conditions. The microfluidic design permits paracrine signaling across compartments, enabling interrogation of heterotypic cell interactions. We showed that systemic changes in dapagliflozin-treated patient plasma modulated endothelial activation and immune behavior, reflected by reduced endothelial ICAM-1 expression, attenuated immune migration toward the hepatic compartment, and decreased hepatic lipid accumulation. These findings support a role for SGLT2 inhibition in modulating cardiometabolic risk through endothelial–immune crosstalk. Importantly, this platform enables multiplexed assessment of vascular, immune, and hepatic phenotypes within a single system, providing a versatile approach for personalized plasma-based drug response screening.

Current liver-on-chip models have substantially advanced liver disease modeling and drug testing by integrating hepatocytes with non-parenchymal cells, including hepatic stellate cells, Kupffer cells, and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, to recapitulate key aspects of the native hepatic microenvironment [47, 48]. Existing models have certainly contributed to our understanding of hepatic biology too. Since most studies place emphasis on interpreting hepatocyte functions, these models may fall short of replicating the immunovascular dynamics of the liver sinusoid. On the other hand, vessel-on-chip devices focus on modelling the vascular endothelium, often to study cell adhesion, transmigration and blood vessel function without organotypic relevance [49].

Despite its promise, our endothelial–immune cell-on-chip platform has limitations inherent to microphysiological models of human vascular disease. For instance, the current design lacks active flow, precluding full recapitulation of physiologically relevant shear stress, a key regulator of endothelial function and immune cell trafficking in vivo [50]. While shear stress is an important regulator of endothelial behavior, the current system was configured under static conditions to enable controlled investigation of endothelial–parenchymal (e.g., hepatocyte) communication mediated primarily through diffusion. Our prior work has demonstrated that such diffusion-driven interactions in similar microfluidic platforms are reproducible and physiologically relevant [37]. Nonetheless, the device is inherently compatible with perfusion. Based on our earlier studies [51], the hydrogel structure remains stable under flow, allowing future integration of controlled shear stress to further enhance physiological relevance.

Another limitation of our current model is the use of Jurkat T cells in place of autologous or patient-derived primary T cells. Jurkat T cells are an immortalized human T lymphocyte cell line widely used to study T cell signaling, adhesion, and migration. While they recapitulate several fundamental aspects of T cell behavior, including responsiveness to inflammatory cues and the capacity for adhesion to endothelial cells [52, 53], they do not fully capture the phenotypic heterogeneity, activation states, and patient-specific variability inherent to primary human T cells. As such, the endothelial-immune interactions observed in this model can only be interpreted as reflecting generalized T cell behavior under inflammatory conditions rather than individualized immune responses. Further iterations of our platform incorporating autologous immune cells or more physiologically relevant immune cells subsets will further enhance its capacity for personalized immunovascular profiling.

Our model serves as a robust and informative setup for simultaneously probing endothelial–immune crosstalk and hepatic lipid signatures to assess vascular and hepatic effects of potential therapeutic candidates. The successful demonstration of SGLT2 inhibitor activity in modulating endothelial-immune cell interactions also illustrates the model’s capacity for revealing mechanistic insights into systemic vascular inflammation. With continued refinement, this platform offers potential to support the discovery and preclinical evaluation of vascular-protective therapies targeting vasculopathies associated with metabolic diseases.
