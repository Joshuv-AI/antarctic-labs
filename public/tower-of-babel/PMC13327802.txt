Feasibility of surface-guided radiotherapy for detecting patient instability during prostate radiotherapy
Authors: Yuichi Kato, Masaya Inohira, Masahiko Harada, Ryo Wakabayashi, Daisuke Watanabe, Keiritsu Sho, Yuto Takahashi, Yushi Suzuki, Masaki Someya, Yoshiko Oshiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMaintaining patient immobility during radiotherapy is essential for accurate dose delivery; however, patients may experience discomfort during treatment, leading to involuntary movement. Continuous imaging to detect intrafractional changes is not routinely feasible in conventional fractionation due to radiation exposure. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of knee-based surface-guided radiotherapy (SGRT) as a real-time method for detecting subtle patient motion during prostate radiotherapy.Material and MethodsA total of 125 patients undergoing prostate intensity-modulated radiation therapy (2,527 fractions) were monitored using an SGRT system with the knees as the region of interest. Mean and maximum displacement per fraction were analyzed. Clinical factors, including bladder volume, age, International Prostate Symptom Score, laxative use, and overactive bladder medication, were evaluated. A displacement threshold of ≥4 mm was defined as an operational level to identify notable motion events.ResultsDisplacement ≥4 mm was observed in 357 of 2,527 fractions (14.1%). Larger displacements were more frequent in the latter half of sessions and were often suggestive of urinary urgency. In a single case, motion improved following verbal instruction. Bladder volume was significantly associated with both mean displacement (p = 0.025) and the proportion of fractions with displacement ≥4 mm (p = 0.004), whereas other factors were not consistent predictors. Knee motion monitoring allowed real-time detection of patient movement requiring clinical attention.ConclusionKnee-based SGRT appears to be a feasible, non-invasive method for monitoring patient motion. While not a surrogate for prostate displacement, it may help identify patient instability and support timely clinical responses.

Introduction

Radiotherapy requires accurate and reproducible patient positioning to ensure precise dose delivery. Although it is generally assumed that patients remain immobile during irradiation, involuntary movement may occur due to various factors, including discomfort, anxiety, or physiological conditions. Such movement during treatment has the potential to compromise treatment accuracy, even when careful setup and image guidance are performed prior to irradiation.

In prostate radiotherapy, particular attention is given to organ motion, as the prostate may shift due to changes in bladder and rectal filling or pelvic muscle activity [1-7]. To minimize these effects, image-guided techniques such as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) or fiducial markers are commonly used for patient setup. However, continuous monitoring during irradiation is not routinely performed in conventional fractionation due to concerns regarding additional radiation exposure. As a result, unexpected patient movement during treatment may go undetected.

Surface-guided radiotherapy (SGRT) is a non-invasive technique that enables real-time monitoring of patient surface motion without additional radiation exposure. It is widely used for the management of respiratory motion, particularly in techniques such as deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH) for breast and thoracic radiotherapy [8-10]. Its application for monitoring non-respiratory body motion during irradiation remains limited, and the application of SGRT in prostate radiotherapy has not been well established. Several studies have reported the use of SGRT primarily for patient setup in prostate radiotherapy [11-13]. In clinical practice, however, patients undergoing prostate radiotherapy are often treated under full bladder conditions, which may lead to urinary urgency and associated discomfort during treatment. This may result in involuntary body movement that is difficult to detect using conventional monitoring methods such as closed-circuit television (CCTV) cameras. Initially, we attempted to track the pelvic region using the thermal surface camera. However, respiratory motion interfered with accurate tracking, resulting in unstable displacement signals (Fig. 1). In this study, we evaluated the feasibility of knee-based SGRT as a real-time method for detecting patient motion during prostate radiotherapy. Importantly, this approach is not intended to serve as a surrogate for prostate displacement, but rather as a practical tool to identify patient instability and detect clinically relevant motion during treatment.

Materials and Methods

1. Preparation for radiotherapy

Patients underwent radiotherapy under near full bladder conditions. They were instructed to drink a fixed volume of water at a consistent time and to maintain regular bowel movements. Bladder and rectal volumes were assessed using low-dose planning computed tomography (CT) scans employing CARE Dose 4D & CARE kV with a Tin filter (SOMATOM go Sim, Siemens AG, Munich, Germany). Once stable bladder and rectal conditions were confirmed, a full planning CT was acquired, and treatment plans were developed using volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT).

2. Radiation therapy

Patients were positioned supine on the treatment couch using a breast board and knee support. Initial positioning was performed using ExacTrac Dynamic (ETD)-SGRT system by aligning the reference contour from the planning CT with the real-time optical surface. Subsequently, CBCT was performed for precise anatomical alignment based on target and organ-at-risk structures. After CBCT verification, 6 MV VMAT beams were delivered. Standard prescriptions were 60 Gy in 20 fractions or 63 Gy in 21 fractions. Each treatment fraction lasted approximately 10 minutes. During treatment, patients were monitored using CCTV and the ETD system, which continuously tracked both knee motion. A displacement threshold of 4 mm was used to define clinically relevant motion. This threshold was selected based on commonly used tolerance levels in clinical SGRT practice, where motion thresholds of approximately 3–5 mm are widely applied. In this study, 4 mm was adopted as a practical cutoff to identify noticeable intrafraction body motion detectable by the external monitoring system [14].

3. Data analysis

The ETD system logs displacement values at each monitor unit. Displacement was defined as the magnitude of positional deviation detected by the SGRT system rather than directional components (e.g., vertical, longitudinal, and lateral). Using these data, we calculated the mean and maximum displacement per fraction for each patient. The proportion of fractions with displacements ≥4 mm was defined as the number of such fractions divided by the total number of evaluated fractions. Associations between displacement metrics (mean displacement, maximum displacement, and proportion of fractions with displacement ≥4 mm) and continuous variables (age, bladder volume on planning CT, and International Prostate Symptom Score [IPSS]) were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Categorical variables, including the use of urinary and bowel medications, were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analyses were included in multivariable linear regression models to identify independent predictors of displacement. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 140 patients received intensity-modulated radiation therapy for prostate cancer between October 2023 and November 2024, of whom 125 were monitored using ETD. Patient demographics are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 76 years, the median IPSS was 7, and the median bladder volume was 231.5 mL. Medications included α1 blockers (40 patients) and overactive bladder drugs (18 patients). We retrospectively reviewed log files from these 125 patients covering 2,527 treatment fractions. Displacement ≥4 mm was detected in 357 treatments (14.1%). Mean displacement per fraction was 0.9 mm (range, 0.06 to 9.52), while mean maximum displacement was 21.5 mm (range, 0.37 to 344.7). The extreme displacement of 344.7 mm occurred in a patient with anxiety and hearing impairment; treatment for this patient was continued after revising the immobilization method. Representative patterns of intrafraction motion are shown in Fig. 2, including stable displacement, late-session motion associated with urinary urgency, and motion reduction following clinical intervention. In univariate analysis, age, IPSS, and laxative use showed weak but statistically significant associations with mean displacement (p = 0.039, p = 0.026, and p = 0.040, respectively). Notably, IPSS demonstrated a weak inverse correlation (r = −0.20). Bladder volume was significantly associated with the proportion of fractions exhibiting displacement ≥4 mm (p = 0.044) (Table 2). Linear regression analysis further demonstrated a weak but statistically significant association between bladder volume and mean displacement (R2 = 0.033) (Fig. 3). All patient characteristics evaluated in the univariate analysis were subsequently included in the multivariable linear regression analysis. Bladder volume emerged as the only consistent predictor for both mean displacement (p = 0.025) and the proportion of fractions with displacement ≥4 mm (p = 0.004). No evaluated factors significantly predicted maximum displacement (Table 3).

Discussion and Conclusion

It is well recognized that the correlation between prostate position and external surface motion is limited; therefore, surface-based monitoring cannot be used as a direct surrogate for prostate localization [14]. For this reason, image-guided techniques such as CBCT or fiducial marker-based alignment remain the standard approaches for accurate target positioning prior to irradiation. Once treatment begins, however, continuous monitoring of prostate position is not routinely performed in conventional fractionation due to concerns regarding additional radiation exposure. As a result, unexpected patient movement during irradiation may compromise treatment accuracy despite careful initial setup and contouring.

In this study, we did not aim to establish knee motion as a surrogate for prostate displacement. Instead, we evaluated the feasibility of knee-based SGRT as a practical tool to detect patient instability or discomfort during treatment, which may indicate an increased risk of intrafractional uncertainty. While SGRT is commonly used for managing respiratory motion, such as in DIBH [8-10], the present findings suggest that it may also be useful for detecting subtle and non-respiratory body motion during irradiation.

In prostate radiotherapy, maintaining a full bladder and an empty rectum is commonly recommended to reduce internal organ motion and optimize dose distribution [15,16]. Malygina et al. [15] demonstrated that bladder volume was significantly correlated with dosimetric parameters such as V48Gy and V40Gy, suggesting a potential benefit in reducing urinary toxicity. However, bladder filling is also associated with urinary urgency, which may lead to involuntary patient movement. In our observations, increased knee displacement was frequently accompanied by signs of urinary urgency, such as patient-reported discomfort or an immediate need for voiding after treatment. These findings suggest that knee motion reflects changes in patient condition during irradiation, rather than target motion itself, and may serve as a practical indicator of patient status. Monitoring such involuntary patient movements may provide a practical approach to identifying clinically relevant motion during treatment. Although the pelvis was initially selected as the region of interest (ROI), respiratory motion produced substantial fluctuations in this area. Therefore, the ROI was shifted to the knee, which is less affected by respiration. Although the present study did not directly evaluate the relationship between knee and pelvic motion, the kinetic linkage between the pelvis and lower extremities has been well described [17,18]. From a biomechanical perspective, substantial movement of the lower extremities may reflect a broader pattern of whole-body instability rather than isolated limb motion. Therefore, knee displacement observed in this study is more appropriately interpreted as an indicator of patient instability or discomfort during treatment, rather than a direct surrogate for pelvic or prostate motion. Importantly, knee motion does not directly reflect prostate or pelvic displacement, and the two should be considered independent phenomena.

Detection of substantial knee motion may serve as a practical trigger for additional imaging. Such an approach could help identify potential positional deviations and support more accurate treatment delivery. Further studies are warranted to evaluate the clinical impact of this workflow.

Bladder volume was identified as a significant factor associated with mean displacement (p = 0.025), suggesting that urinary urgency due to a full bladder may contribute to increased patient motion during treatment. In contrast, no significant association was observed with the mean of maximum displacement, indicating that sudden and large motion may occur unpredictably. SGRT enables real-time detection of both types of patient motion, potentially facilitating appropriate and timely clinical intervention without additional radiation exposure. These findings may apply to patients who have difficulty remaining still, such as those experiencing severe pain or pediatric patients. For example, in pediatric radiotherapy, minimizing additional radiation exposure is important, and it is well recognized that maintaining complete immobility can be challenging [19]. In non-sedated patients, real-time detection of motion may allow timely verbal intervention, which could help reduce patient movement. In addition, even in sedated patients, continuous monitoring of body motion may provide an additional safety layer by enabling the detection of unexpected movement and facilitating appropriate actions, such as temporary beam interruption when necessary [20,21].

In this context, SGRT-based motion monitoring without additional radiation exposure may offer a clinically useful approach for detecting patient movement, even when direct visualization of the treatment region is limited, such as when immobilization devices are used. Therefore, this approach may have broader applicability beyond prostate radiotherapy, including pediatric settings and proton therapy, where motion management remains a critical challenge.

This study had several limitations. First, since the data were derived from routine clinical practice, CBCT re-setup was not performed for every detected motion event; therefore, the dosimetric impact of intrafractional motion could not be quantified. Second, although knee motion was consistently observed and used as a practical surrogate for trunk movement, it does not directly represent trunk or prostate motion and may not fully capture intrafractional body movement.

Despite these limitations, knee-based SGRT offers a simple and non-invasive method for monitoring patient condition during treatment. While it does not provide direct information on prostate position, it may serve as a useful adjunct tool to identify patients who require closer observation, reassurance, or intervention during irradiation.
