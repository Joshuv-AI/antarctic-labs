Expert knowledge elicitation to determine the relative importance of potentially foodborne parasitic diseases in Armenia
Authors: Heghine Papoyan, Lucy J Robertson, Hasmik Gevorgyan, Oleg Shcherbakov, Ahmad Daryani, Sargis A Aghayan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReliable prioritization of foodborne parasitic diseases is challenging in settings where routine surveillance and attribution data are limited. For example, In Armenia, the relative importance of different foodborne parasitoses has not previously been assessed. This study applied expert knowledge elicitation (EKE) to identify and rank foodborne parasitic diseases of public health relevance and to support national prioritization efforts.A structured questionnaire was administered to experts from human health, veterinary medicine, food safety, and biological sciences. Participants evaluated selected parasitic diseases according to predefined criteria including prevalence, geographic distribution, morbidity, mortality, diagnostic complexity, and environmental detectability. Responses were analysed using rank-based non-parametric methods, and regional patterns were visualized using geographic information systems.Ascariasis and echinococcosis were consistently ranked as the highest-priority foodborne parasitic diseases, clearly separated from all others. Giardiasis ranked third, while fascioliasis, toxoplasmosis, and trichinellosis formed a moderate-priority group. Cryptosporidiosis, taeniasis, and anisakiasis were assigned low priority. Professional background and geographic region had only minor influence on ranking outcomes, indicating strong national consensus.The results demonstrate a distinct hierarchy of the opinions of experts regarding foodborne parasitic risks in Armenia, driven by disease burden, environmental exposure, and diagnostic visibility. This study illustrates the value of EKE for evidence-based prioritization in data-limited settings and provides a foundation for targeted surveillance, control strategies, and One Health–oriented food safety policies. It would nevertheless be of value to investigate whether this prioritization is supported by medical records and reports from analysis of food matrices, soil samples, and water bodies for elucidating transmission routes.Keywords: Echinococcosis, Ascariasis, Giardiasis, One Health, Multicriteria ranking, Disease prioritization

Introduction

Foodborne and waterborne enteric diseases represent a persistent public-health challenge worldwide (Newell et al., 2010; Ogwu and Izah, 2026). However, their burden is disproportionately underestimated in regions where epidemiological surveillance and laboratory capacity are limited (Albert et al., 2024; Cissé, 2019). It is estimated that 7.7% of individuals all over the world suffer from foodborne illness every year with a mortality rate of 7.5% (Albert et al., 2024; Havelaar et al., 2015). While these global estimates provide an essential benchmark, they mask substantial regional heterogeneity in transmission pathways, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where food, water, and environmental exposures frequently overlap (Cissé, 2019).

A central challenge in food safety and public-health risk assessment is the attribution of enteric disease burden to specific transmission pathways, particularly distinguishing foodborne transmission from waterborne, environmental, and, for some, direct animal-contact or person-to-person transmission routes. This challenge is especially pronounced for parasitic infections, many of which are transmitted through multiple overlapping pathways, including contaminated food, drinking water, irrigation water, and environmental exposure, as well as directly from infected people or animals. In such contexts, conventional epidemiological surveillance often lacks the resolution required to disentangle these pathways, necessitating the use of structured expert knowledge elicitation (EKE) to estimate the relative contribution of foodborne and waterborne transmission routes (FAO and WHO, 2014; Havelaar et al., 2008; Kirk et al., 2015). By systematically capturing and quantifying specialized local and regional expertise, EKE offers a transparent and reproducible framework for generating probabilistic estimates suitable for risk modelling and evidence-informed policy development (EFSA, 2014; Trevisan et al., 2019).

Furthermore, unlike bacterial or viral agents that often cause acute outbreaks, parasitic infections are frequently chronic, underdiagnosed, and associated primarily with morbidity rather than mortality, and this also means that by the time an infection is diagnosed it is often too late to determine the most probable infection route and vehicle of infection.

Recognizing the lack of harmonized data, the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) developed a multicriteria-based global ranking of foodborne parasites to support evidence-based prioritization and risk management. This framework integrates health impact, likelihood of foodborne transmission, socioeconomic consequences, and feasibility of control measures, relying heavily on expert judgment to compensate for data gaps. Parasites such as Taenia solium, Echinococcus granulosus, Echinococcus multilocularis, Toxoplasma gondii, and Cryptosporidium spp. ranked among the highest priorities due to their severe health outcomes, long-term disability, and limited treatment possibilities (FAO and WHO, 2014).

In Armenia, systematic surveillance of foodborne and waterborne parasitic diseases has historically been limited, resulting in substantial uncertainty regarding dominant transmission pathways. Until recently, environmental monitoring of water sources for parasitic contamination was minimal, constraining the ability of public-health authorities to conduct robust risk assessments. This gap has begun to narrow following recent investigations demonstrating contamination of raw water sources with Cryptosporidium and Giardia across several rivers (Shcherbakov et al., 2024). These findings suggest that environmental and waterborne exposure may play a more significant role in parasite transmission than previously recognized, with potential downstream implications for food contamination through irrigation and washing of fresh produce.

Clinical data further reinforce the relevance of parasitic diseases in Armenia. Retrospective analyses document a sustained burden of human echinococcosis, with hundreds of cystic echinococcosis cases and confirmed alveolar echinococcosis cases recorded over the past decade, many diagnosed at advanced stages and associated with significant mortality (Manukyan et al., 2022). Additionally, E. multilocularis was recently identified in a free-roaming dog, marking the previously detection of the parasite in a definitive host in Armenia (Gevorgyan et al., 2025). Despite these reports, the true burden is likely underestimated due to asymptomatic infections, delayed diagnosis, and underreporting. Moreover, the relative contribution of foodborne, waterborne, and environmental transmission pathways for different parasites in Armenia remains largely unquantified.

The Armenian context exemplifies the broader challenge faced by many countries where empirical data are insufficient to support conventional quantitative risk assessment. Laboratory detection of parasites in food and environmental matrices is technically demanding, often relies on non-standardized methods, and is constrained by low sensitivity and matrix interference (Chalmers et al., 2020; EFSA, 2014). Armenia has been transitioning from informal backyard slaughter toward regulated slaughterhouse processing, including mandatory use of officially registered slaughterhouses for commercial meat sales introduced in 2020 under the Food Safety Inspection Body. This shift aims to strengthen veterinary inspection and hygiene controls and is relevant to zoonotic parasites in livestock such as Trichinella spp. and Echinococcus spp. Although E. granulosus is not transmitted to humans via meat consumption, slaughter practices contribute to its transmission cycle through infection of canids (CDC, 2019). Nevertheless, publicly accessible, systematically reported national data on slaughterhouse detection of these infections remain limited, constraining quantitative assessment and motivating the use of expert elicitation for prioritization. Within regulatory and scientific frameworks, EKE is not considered a substitute for empirical data, but a complementary tool explicitly designed to address irreducible uncertainty. By applying established elicitation protocols, such as Delphi or Sheffield methods, EKE minimizes cognitive bias while ensuring reproducibility and transparency, key requirements for evidence-based public-health decision-making (Hadjigeorgiou et al., 2022; Robertson et al., 2015).

Against this background, Armenia represents a pertinent case study for the application of EKE to foodborne disease attribution. The present study used a questionnaire-based EKR to prioritize parasitic diseases in Armenia with potential food- and/or water-associated transmission, based on structured scoring across predefined criteria. Materials and methods

Data collection

Our methodology to elucidate which potentially foodborne parasitosis were of most importance in Armenia consisted of three consecutive phases: (1) Expert Consultation and Disease Prioritization; (2) Questionnaire Development and Criteria Definition; and (3) Expert Recruitment and Data Collection.

Expert consultation and disease prioritization

The initial phase focused on qualitative data collection using a formal expert consultation process, drawing on elements of the Modified Delphi Technique (Sarafrazi et al., 2014). Three meetings were held in June 2025, during which leading subject-matter specialists from the human health, veterinary health, food safety, and biological sciences sectors met with the research team to discuss parasitic diseases of relevance in Armenia. All invited participants were Armenia-based experts. The consultation group included representatives from academia/research institutions, public-sector services (public health and food safety/regulatory), and clinical/veterinary practice (including private practice), to ensure multidisciplinary coverage of human health, animal health, and food-chain perspectives.

Based on parasites previously detected and recorded in Armenia, a preliminary list of parasitic agents was compiled and used as the basis for expert discussion. These parasites are listed in Appendix 1. As these parasites had been detected prior to the study, they were considered appropriate for structured discussion with specialists.

The consultations aimed to build professional consensus and were informed both by expert discussion and by previously published studies conducted in Armenia. On this basis, a list of parasitic diseases considered current or emerging public-health concerns and/or relevant to livestock production, with potential food- and/or waterborne transmission, was compiled for inclusion in the questionnaire. These diseases were: ascariasis, echinococcosis, giardiasis, fascioliasis, toxoplasmosis, trichinellosis, taeniasis, amoebiasis, balantidiasis, cryptosporidiosis, and anisakiasis.

To ensure clarity and maximize respondent engagement, diseases were listed at the clinical or disease level rather than by specifying individual parasite species. This approach was adopted to avoid potential confusion or reduced participation that might arise from the use of less familiar taxonomic names, while still capturing expert knowledge of the relevant disease entities.

The second phase focused on defining criteria of importance and developing a questionnaire for quantitative data collection. The questionnaire was developed by adapting established risk-ranking and prioritization methodologies previously applied by the World Health Organization and the Food and Agriculture Organization in a comparable global exercise (FAO and WHO, 2014).

Questionnaire development and criteria definition

The questionnaire comprised of three main sections.

The first section requested information on relevant demographic and professional background data from the experts, including their years of experience and primary sector of operation.

The second section of the questionnaire requested experts to select three parasitic diseases, from the predefined list derived in phase 1, that they considered to be the most common in Armenia.

The third section included predefined ranking criteria for the three selected diseases:

(i) prevalence of the parasitic disease in Armenia, based on diagnosis or parasite detection;

(ii) geographical distribution within the country;

(iii) morbidity, reflecting clinical severity and post-infectious sequelae;

(iv) mortality, indicating the relative fatality associated with the disease;

(v) clinical complexity, including challenges related to diagnosis and availability of treatment; and

(vi) environmental detection difficulty, assessing the feasibility of detecting the parasite in food, water, or soil matrices (Appendix 2).

Data collection from experts

The third stage of the procedure involved expert recruitment and data collection. A total of 122 stakeholder organizations and departments were invited to participate in the study. These included, but were not limited to, the National Institute of Health, branches of the National Center for Disease Control and Prevention, Reference Laboratory Centers, the National Center of Infectious Diseases, stationary medical service providers, regional branches of the Food Safety Inspection Body, and major universities and scientific research centers in the Republic of Armenia. Official invitation letters described the objectives of the study and emphasized the importance of expert judgment in prioritizing foodborne parasitic diseases. Each institution nominated qualified specialists based on professional expertise and experience.

Participants were asked to complete the questionnaire once, relying on their professional knowledge, observed cases, practical experience, and expectations regarding future trends in parasitic disease occurrence. The responses collected through this structured approach formed the basis for calculating relative disease importance and subsequent prioritization analyses.

For each criterion, experts assigned an ordinal score from 0 to 5 using criterion-specific qualitative descriptors provided for each score level (Appendix 2). A score of 0 indicated ‘not present/not relevant’ and 5 indicated the highest level of importance/impact for that criterion; intermediate levels (1–4) reflected increasing severity/relevance according to the predefined anchors. Blank responses were treated as missing (criterion not scored), not as zero. Thus, a score of 2 corresponds to a lower–moderate level, 3 to moderate, and 4 to high, as defined by the criterion-specific anchors in Appendix 2.

For analysis, Phase 2 (selection) and Phase 3 (scoring) were treated as separate outputs. Selection frequencies (how many experts selected each disease among their top three) were summarized descriptively. For the overall prioritization, an overall importance score was calculated for each selected disease by summing the criterion scores (0–5) across the 10 criteria. These overall scores were then rank-transformed to compute mean ranks (Table 1, Table 2). Criterion-level results are reported as median (IQR); n for each disease–criterion cell (Table 3).

The survey was administered using the secure online data collection platform Kobo Toolbox and was conducted between June 5, 2025, and August 8, 2025.

Following the collection phase, all raw data were exported, cleaned, and checked for comprehensibility and completeness. Initial data management, cleaning, and descriptive analyses were primarily performed using Microsoft Excel.

Experts based their scores on professional experience and evidence sources, including clinical and veterinary observations, laboratory diagnostic experience, and knowledge of local exposure and transmission contexts.

Statistics

Survey data were analysed using JASP (version 0.95.4.0). Because respondents had been asked to prioritize a maximum of three potentially food-borne parasitic diseases, the resulting importance scores were strongly zero-inflated and deviated from normality. Accordingly, all inferential analyses were conducted using rank-based, non-parametric approaches, which are appropriate for ordinal, non-normally distributed, and expert-elicitation data (Conover, 1999).

Disease importance ranking and comparison

Importance scores assigned by experts to each food-borne parasitosis were converted to ranks across the full dataset (Score_rank), with higher ranks indicating greater perceived public-health importance. Rank transformation was applied to enable robust comparison across diseases while minimizing the influence of non-normal score distributions and the high proportion of zero values arising from the study design. Rank-based approaches are widely recommended for expert-elicitation and prioritization exercises, as they preserve relative ordering while reducing sensitivity to scale effects and outliers (Conover, 1999).

Respondent identifiers were retained to preserve the long-format structure of the dataset, however, individual respondents were not treated as a factor in the models, as the primary objective was to assess group-level disease prioritization patterns rather than inter-individual variability.

Differences in perceived importance among food-borne diseases were evaluated using a rank-based one-way analysis of variance, equivalent to the Kruskal–Wallis test, with “Score_rank” as the dependent variable and Disease as the fixed factor. Effect sizes were quantified using omega squared (ω2) with 95% confidence intervals to estimate the proportion of variance explained by disease identity. Mean ranks were calculated for each disease and used to establish the overall national prioritization hierarchy.

Factors influencing disease prioritization

Effect of professional background

To examine whether professional background influenced prioritization patterns, a rank-based two-factor ANOVA was conducted with “Score_rank” as the dependent variable and Disease and Profession as fixed factors, including their interaction (Disease × Profession). This model tested whether different professional groups assigned systematically different ranks to specific diseases. Marginal mean ranks for each disease within professional groups were extracted for descriptive comparison. Interpretation focused on effect sizes (ω2) to assess practical relevance.

Effects of geographic region

An analogous rank-based two-factor ANOVA was used to evaluate potential geographic variation in prioritization, with Disease and Region as fixed factors and inclusion of the Disease × Region interaction. This analysis assessed whether regional background influenced overall ranking patterns or modified the relative importance assigned to specific diseases. As with the professional analysis, emphasis was placed on effect sizes rather than statistical significance alone. Spatial visualization of dominant diseases by region was performed using QGIS (3.40 Bratislava version). Regional maps were generated by assigning each administrative region the disease with the highest mean importance score.

Score distributions were visualized using boxplots with jittered individual data points, illustrating central tendency, dispersion, and the prevalence of zero scores. Mean ranks derived from rank-based models were summarized in tables, and conditional grayscale shading was applied to selected tables to facilitate visual comparison of relative importance across diseases and groups. Shading was used solely for descriptive purposes and did not imply statistical significance.

Throughout all analyses, statistical significance was evaluated at α = 0.05, but interpretation emphasized effect sizes, consistency of patterns, and robustness of the prioritization hierarchy, in line with best practices for expert-elicitation and prioritization studies.

For the disease-by-criterion analyses, we restricted calculations to disease records with at least one score. Specifically, entries in which a respondent selected a disease but provided all-zero scores across every criterion (interpreted as non-response rather than a true “zero importance” assessment) were excluded. In addition, diseases with no evaluable responses after filtering (e.g., only a single incomplete record or no non-missing criterion scores) were removed from the summarized table to avoid misleading estimates. For the remaining records, zeros were retained as valid scores when intentionally assigned to a criterion, whereas blank cells were treated as missing (i.e., the respondent did not score that criterion). Consequently, the sample size n varies by cell and reflects the number of respondents who provided a non-missing score for that specific disease-criterion combination.

Results

A total of 86 experts completed the online questionnaire. As institutional identifiers were not collected in the online survey, respondents cannot be stratified by specific organizations or departments. However, professional background was recorded. Participants represented multiple relevant fields, including human medicine/public health (n = 34), veterinary medicine (n = 20), food safety (n = 4), biological sciences (n = 8), and other related areas (n = 20).

Expert-based prioritization of food-borne parasitic diseases

In the first step, respondents selected up to three parasitic diseases based on how common they considered the diseases to be. The disease considered by most respondents to be most common was ascariasis (selected by 73 of 86 (85%) respondents), while none of the respondents selected anisakiasis.

Importance scores differed significantly among food-borne parasitic diseases (non-parametric analysis on ranked data, p < 0.001), with a large effect size (ω2 = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.28–0.37). Ascariasis and echinococcosis showed the highest mean ranks, indicating consistent prioritization by respondents, followed by giardiasis. In contrast, anisakiasis received uniformly low scores and exhibited no variability. Overall, the analysis revealed a clear hierarchy of food-borne parasitic disease threats as perceived by experts in Armenia. Effects of professional perspectives on ranking results

A rank-based two-factor analysis revealed a strong effect of parasite identity on importance scores (p < 0.001), while the main effect of professional background was not significant (p = 0.084; ω2 = 0.004). The interaction between parasite and professional background was statistically significant (p < 0.001) but accounted for a small proportion of variance (ω2 = 0.041), indicating minor profession-specific differences in scoring for some parasitic diseases. While there was broad agreement across professional groups on the highest-priority diseases, some profession-specific differences were observed, particularly for mid-ranked illnesses (Table 2). Because the respondent pool included limited representation from fisheries/seafood-related professions, marine-associated diseases such as anisakiasis may be under-represented in the selection and scoring.

Regional perspectives

A rank-based two-factor analysis including geographic region revealed a strong effect of parasitic disease identity on importance scores (p < 0.001; ω2 = 0.189), while the main effect of region was statistically significant but negligible in magnitude (p = 0.011; ω2 = 0.010). The Region × Disease interaction was statistically significant (p < 0.001) but accounted for a small proportion of variance (ω2 = 0.059), indicating limited regional variation in the prioritization of certain parasites. Overall, these results suggest that geographic regions have only a minor influence on expert-based rankings and do not alter the national hierarchy of food-borne parasitic disease threats.

Disease perspectives

Across evaluation criteria (Table 3), ascariasis and echinococcosis consistently received the strongest overall ratings. Because diseases were presented as group labels, ‘ascariasis’ reflects experts' perceptions of diseases caused by both A. lumbricoides and A. suum across relevant hosts, which may have contributed to high ratings on both human- and animal-related criteria. Toxoplasmosis and trichinellosis showed more criterion-specific concern: toxoplasmosis tended to score higher for geographic distribution and human/animal relevance, whereas trichinellosis stood out particularly for human severity and diagnostic challenges, despite lower perceived prevalence than the two leading diseases. Giardiasis ranked as a broad, moderate-to-high concern, with comparatively steady medians across prevalence and distribution-related indicators. In contrast, taeniasis and fascioliasis showed mixed and generally lower medians across several criteria.

Discussion

Disease prioritization patterns and public health implications

We used structured EKE to prioritize parasitic diseases with potential food- and water-borne transmission in Armenia, a setting where routine surveillance and standardized detection in food/environmental matrices remain limited. Using a simple disease-selection task complemented by criterion-specific scoring, we observed a clear hierarchy of perceived public-health importance across diseases, while also revealing which attributes (e.g., severity, diagnostic difficulty, environmental detectability) drive expert concern. This approach aligns with established applications of EKE and risk-ranking frameworks in food and feed safety, which are specifically recommended when empirical data are incomplete, but decisions on prioritization and surveillance are still needed (EFSA, 2014; Hadjigeorgiou et al., 2022; Ruzante et al., 2010; Van der Fels-Klerx et al., 2018).

Across respondents, ascariasis and echinococcosis emerged as the highest-priority diseases, with giardiasis ranked next. The prominence of echinococcosis is consistent with its recognized global burden and long-term clinical consequences, including chronic morbidity, advanced-stage presentation, and substantial healthcare costs (Torgerson et al., 2015; Tuergan et al., 2025). Importantly, Armenia-specific evidence supports ongoing transmission potential. The recent molecular surveys have documented E. multilocularis in dogs, E. ortleppi in wolvess and E. canadensis G6/7 (along with T. hydatigena, and T. laticollis) in lynx, reinforcing the relevance of One Health control strategies that integrate human, livestock, and wildlife interfaces (Gevorgyan et al., 2025; Horefti, 2023). While E. granulosus s.l. and related taxa are routinely ranked among high-priority foodborne parasites globally (Devleesschauwer et al., 2017), the high placement of ascariasis in our national elicitation likely reflects local perceptions of endemicity, exposure pathways linked to sanitation and environmental contamination, and the practical reality that “foodborne” and “waterborne” routes overlap in daily life, especially where irrigation water, household hygiene, and informal food chains intersect (Cissé, 2019; FAO and WHO, 2014; Newell et al., 2010; Torgerson et al., 2020). Importantly, “ascariasis” was presented as a disease-group label (not a species-defined entity), and respondents were not asked to distinguish A. lumbricoides (human) from pig ascariasis/A. suum. This possibly contributed to the relatively high animal-domain scores by capturing veterinary relevance and/or shared environmental exposure concerns, rather than implying A. lumbricoides infection in animals. It is important to note for ascariasis that direct transmission from another infected person does not occur due to the requirement for a prolonged period for embryonation of the egg transmission stage in the environment. Because of this, and because eggs are persistent in the environment, experts may perceive ascariasis as strongly linked to sanitation and to food−/water-associated exposure pathways. For echinococcosis also, person-to-person transmission does not occur, but people may be infected following direct contact with infected dogs as well as from environmental contamination. For protozoan enteric infections, experts assigned relatively high priority to giardiasis, consistent with its wide geographic distribution and frequent water-associated transmission, which might indirectly translate into food contamination via irrigation and produce handling (Albert et al., 2024; Cissé, 2019). In contrast, cryptosporidiosis did not feature strongly in the ranking outputs in our dataset, largely because only one respondent provided complete criterion scoring for this disease, limiting interpretability. This should not be taken as evidence of low relevance. It likely reflects differences in familiarity, diagnostic practices, and perceived visibility of the pathogen in routine clinical or veterinary settings. Cryptosporidium is well recognized as an important protozoan parasite with complex species/genotype patterns and host specificities, with major implications for public health and transmission routes. However, it is also a pathogen for which awareness and laboratory confirmation can be uneven across settings, particularly regarding diagnostic tests where some are more sensitive and specific than others (Xiao et al., 2004).

In this context, limited scoring may itself be informative, pointing to a potential knowledge or diagnostic gap that could be addressed through targeted training and improved laboratory capacity.

Comparison of our results with another foodborne-parasite prioritization exercise at the European level (Bouwknegt et al., 2018) is of interest. Although Armenia (in the Caucasus region of West Asia) was not included in this exercise, neighbouring Türkiye was included under South-East Europe. In this region (consisting of Bulgaria, Croatia, the Republic of Macedonia Greece, Serbia, and Türkiye), the highest ranked parasites (in positions 1 and 2) were E. granulosus and E. multilocularis, which is similar to our results. However, Ascaris was at position 8, and Giardia did not appear at all in the top 10 parasites for that region. This probably reflects methodological differences, as well as regional variations and the variety in the experts providing the data. Direct comparison should be interpreted cautiously because Bouwknegt et al. used a formal MCDA framework in a broader regional setting, whereas our study is a national EKE in which experts first selected only three diseases and then scored them using qualitative anchors, resulting in variable n per disease. In addition, a full MCDA parameterization comparable to that used in the FAO/WHO and European exercises was not feasible in the Armenian setting because consistent national data across diseases, including human case burden, animal surveillance, and food and environmental monitoring, remain limited. These differences in framework and context may partly explain why ascariasis and giardiasis were ranked higher in our Armenian elicitation.

The criterion-based results (Table 2) add an important layer beyond overall selection of most common parasites by showing why experts view certain diseases as priorities. For example, diseases perceived as severe and/or difficult to diagnose in humans or animals can remain under detected, even when transmission is ongoing, creating a risk of delayed recognition and missed opportunities for control. Likewise, high perceived difficulty in detecting pathogens in environmental samples may have direct implications for monitoring strategy and method selection, given that parasite detection in complex matrices often requires technically demanding workflows and suffers from sensitivity and matrix-interference constraints (EFSA, 2014; Van der Fels-Klerx et al., 2018). The pattern observed here supports a practical prioritization logic: diseases rise to the top not only because of perceived prevalence, but also because they combine health impact with “control difficulty” (diagnosis and environmental detection), which is precisely the type of multidimensional reasoning formalized in multicriteria ranking approaches (Bouwknegt et al., 2018; FAO and WHO, 2014; Ruzante et al., 2010).

We also observed differences by professional field and region, with interaction effects indicating that prioritization is not fully uniform across respondent groups. Such heterogeneity is expected in EKE exercises because experts weigh evidence through the lens of their professional experience (e.g., clinical caseloads, livestock production systems, regional exposure patterns, laboratory access). Structured elicitation frameworks explicitly recognize this as a feature rather than a flaw, provided that procedures are transparent and uncertainty is documented (EFSA, 2014; Hadjigeorgiou et al., 2022; O'Hagan et al., 2006). From a policy perspective, group differences can be actionable. Where priorities diverge, it may signal distinct regional transmission conditions or sector-specific concerns (human health vs. veterinary/economic impact), supporting tailored surveillance and communication.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this expert knowledge elicitation. The main limitation is that only three diseases were selected per expert, and these selections were based on which diseases were considered most common, without specifying whether commonness referred to occurrence in humans or animals. As a result, very severe diseases with high mortality and morbidity but relatively low occurrence may not have been selected and therefore were not included in the subsequent evaluation. Additionally, diseases selected by very few experts (e.g., cryptosporidiosis) therefore yield unstable criterion summaries and should not be interpreted as low true importance. This design may favour more familiar or clinically visible diseases, accordingly, we report n per disease/criterion and interpret low-n diseases cautiously.

In contrast to FAO/WHO expert ranking exercises, which relied on structured in-person panels and facilitated consensus-building, the present study was conducted using an online questionnaire. While this approach enabled broad national participation and reduced logistical constraints, it limited opportunities for real-time clarification, iterative feedback, and convergence of expert judgments through discussion, potentially contributing to greater variability in individual scores. Additionally, we did not include trade-related criteria (e.g., international market impact), as the elicitation focused on domestic prioritization of food- and/or waterborne parasitic diseases in Armenia.

The limited number of specialists with dedicated expertise in food parasitology in Armenia necessitated the inclusion of experts from related disciplines, including human medicine, veterinary health, food safety, and biological sciences. Although this interdisciplinary composition enhanced national representativeness and reflected the practical distribution of expertise within the country, it may have influenced prioritization outcomes. Parasites that are less visible outside specialized parasitological practice, particularly those requiring advanced diagnostic methods or targeted surveillance, may have been underrepresented in the rankings.

Several methodological considerations should also be taken into account when interpreting the disease-criteria tables. Response rows with SumScore = 0 (i.e., cases where a disease was selected but all criteria were scored as zero) were excluded, as these patterns are most consistent with non-response or disengaged completion rather than a meaningful “all criteria are zero” judgment. Some respondents left individual criteria blank and were treated as missing rather than assumed to be zero, and criterion-specific sample sizes (n) are reported to maintain transparency. Diseases with extremely sparse scoring cannot support stable summaries and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

More generally, expert elicitation is sensitive to framing effects, differential familiarity, and cognitive biases. Best-practice guidance recommends clear definitions, structured questionnaires, explicit handling of uncertainty, and transparent reporting of missingness and exclusions, principles followed here in line with EFSA expert knowledge elicitation guidance and the broader methodological literature (EFSA, 2014; Hadjigeorgiou et al., 2022; O'Hagan et al., 2006).

An additional limitation is that parasitic hazards were defined and presented at the disease level rather than at the species or subtype level. For example, echinococcosis was considered as a single disease entity without distinguishing between causative species, such as Echinococcus granulosus s.l. and E. multilocularis, which differ in epidemiology and public health significance. Similarly, toxoplasmosis was treated as a single hazard without differentiating between transmission contexts, such as congenital and acquired toxoplasmosis, despite their distinct epidemiological characteristics. This approach was adopted to ensure clarity and accessibility for respondents and to avoid potential confusion or disengagement associated with detailed taxonomic nomenclature. However, because parasite species differ substantially in life cycles, host range, transmission pathways, geographic distribution, and public-health impact, this aggregation may have obscured important species-specific differences relevant to risk assessment and prioritization.

Despite these limitations, the combined ranking and criterion scoring provide a practical, evidence-informed basis for national prioritization in Armenia. The findings support focusing near-term surveillance and control efforts on high-ranked helminth infections, notably echinococcosis and ascariasis, while using the criterion profiles to identify areas where improved diagnostics, environmental monitoring, and cross-sector coordination would yield the greatest benefit (FAO and WHO, 2014; Horefti, 2023; Newell et al., 2010).

Conclusion

This study presents the first systematic, expert-based prioritization of parasitic diseases with potential food- and water-borne transmission in Armenia. Using a structured EKE approach, we identified a clear national pattern in perceived public-health importance. Ascariasis and echinococcosis consistently emerged as the highest-priority diseases, followed by giardiasis and a group of infections of moderate concern. Importantly, this prioritization was largely consistent across professional backgrounds and geographic regions, suggesting a shared national perspective despite differences in expertise and local experience.

The criterion-based evaluation adds important nuance to the overall ranking by showing which disease characteristics most strongly influence expert concern. In addition to perceived prevalence, factors such as clinical severity, diagnostic difficulty, and challenges in environmental detection played a key role in shaping priorities. This highlights that diseases may be considered important not only because they are common, but also because they are difficult to detect, manage, or control once transmission is established.

While the study is subject to limitations inherent to expert elicitation and selection-based ranking, the results provide a practical and transparent basis for guiding national surveillance and control efforts. In particular, they support prioritizing helminth infections such as echinococcosis and ascariasis for near-term action, while also drawing attention to protozoan infections where limited scoring may reflect gaps in awareness, diagnostics, or monitoring rather than true absence of risk. However, these priorities should be verified using systematically collected empirical evidence, including human medical records, veterinary surveillance data, and targeted analyses of food matrices, soil, and water bodies to confirm burden and elucidate transmission routes.

Overall, this work demonstrates the value of EKE as a pragmatic tool for disease prioritization in settings where empirical data remain incomplete. The findings offer a baseline for future studies and can help inform One Health-oriented food safety policies in Armenia, as additional clinical, veterinary, environmental, and food-chain data become available.
