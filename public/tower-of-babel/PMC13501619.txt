Cataract after electrical injury: clinical characteristics and surgical management outcomes, a case report of three cases
Authors: Yue Wang, XiaoKe Chang, Li Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElectric cataract, a rare and vision-threatening complication, is frequently observed in patients following electrical injuries. Current knowledge of its clinical characteristics and treatment is largely derived from a limited number of case reports. This report presents three cases of distinct types of electric cataract, with detailed documentation of disease progression, clinical features, individualized surgical management, and corresponding outcomes.Case presentationThis case series included three patients: a 30-year-old female with indirect lightning-induced cataract (onset at 2 years), a 64-year-old male with cataract following domestic low-voltage electrical injury (onset at 50 years), and a 54-year-old male with high-voltage electric cataract (onset at 3 months). All three cases presented with characteristic anterior subcapsular opacities (snowflake, sunflower, or moss-like patterns), anterior subcapsular cortical opacities, and posterior subcapsular opacities. Intraoperative zonular laxity was observed in all three cases. Two patients had unilateral involvement, while one had bilateral cataracts. The young patient received implantation of an extended depth-of-focus (EDOF) intraocular lens (IOL), and the middle-aged patient received implantation of a toric monofocal IOL. The patient with bilateral cataracts also had simultaneous macular damage, as well as corneal leukoma; a monofocal IOL was implanted in one eye, while the fellow eye ultimately progressed to a mature cataract. Postoperative corrected distance visual acuity improved to 0.8 in two cases and 0.4 in the third. No significant postoperative complications were observed.ConclusionCataracts following electrical injury may be insidious at onset and can exhibit progressive worsening. Pupillary dilation examination is essential, and regular follow-up is required. Attention should be paid to concomitant injuries of the cornea, fundus, zonules, and corneal endothelium to guide individualized surgical planning, thereby maximizing patient benefits.Keywords: Electrical Injury, Lightning Injury, Cataract, Phacoemulsification, Intraocular Lens Implantation

Electrical and lightning injuries are infrequent in occupational and environmental settings. However, they can cause significant trauma. Electric cataract is a rare disorder characterized by lens degeneration and opacification following ocular exposure to an electric current or lightning. The severity depends on the current pathway, voltage, exposure duration, contact area, tissue resistance, and the type of current (alternating or direct current) [1]. Variations in these parameters, as well as the proximity of the contact point to the eyes, account for differences in cataract onset, degree of visual impairment, and treatment outcomes. The standard treatment for electric cataract is phacoemulsification with intraocular lens (IOL) implantation. Isolated lens injury is associated with more favourable postoperative visual recovery [2, 3], whereas poor outcomes are linked to concurrent damage to the retina, macula, choroid, or optic nerve [4]. Electrical injuries are often life-threatening, so ophthalmic evaluation is usually postponed. Early clinical manifestations of electric cataract are subtle. These signs may not be noticed until vision declines. As a result, current knowledge of clinical features, progression, and outcomes comes mainly from case reports and reviews. Most published reports do not cover IOL selection criteria or compare the effects of different electrical exposures on latency and morphological changes.

This paper presents three cases—indirect lightning-induced cataract, cataract resulting from domestic electrical injury, and high-voltage electric cataract—to illustrate the range of presentations and management challenges encountered in clinical practice. These cases were chosen to highlight differences in lens morphology, onset timing, and disease progression that inform treatment strategies. Surgical interventions were tailored to the extent of ocular injury and the patient’s needs. Key management strategies for electric cataract are synthesized from these illustrative cases.

Case 1

A 30-year-old female experienced a sudden loss of consciousness while washing dishes in a temporary shelter during a thunderstorm two years earlier. She had no memory of the incident. Upon regaining consciousness, the room was found to have blackened walls and scattered electrical wires. Immediate left ocular pain and redness prompted her to seek medical attention at a local hospital. Cranial computed tomography (CT) revealed no abnormalities, and intravenous anti-inflammatory medication was administered, resulting in the resolution of ocular discomfort. One year later, progressive visual decline in the left eye developed. A diagnosis of electric cataract of the left eye was established.

No skin redness, swelling, or burns were observed on the body following the indirect lightning strike. There was no significant past ocular or systemic history, and no history of ocular blunt trauma. Previous binocular visual acuity was reported as comparable. Physical examination confirmed the absence of cutaneous burn marks.

Surgical intervention was indicated for the left eye, with no contraindications identified. No intraoperative complications were encountered. Case 2

A 64-year-old male presented with a remote history of electrical injury from a broken household electrical wire sustained 50 years earlier. At the time of injury, he experienced numbness, fell to the ground, and was separated from the wire, but remained conscious and did not report additional symptoms. He did not experience ocular discomfort or visual decline and did not seek medical evaluation or assessment for burns. The patient had no history of systemic disease or previous ocular blunt trauma. Four months before presentation, the patient reported a gradual decline in visual acuity in the left eye. Physical examination showed no evidence of cutaneous burn scars.

The foveal reflex was absent in the macular region, with the retina attached.

Case 3

A 54-year-old male presented with progressive visual decline in both eyes, which began 3 months after a high-voltage electrical injury sustained at work. He was initially treated in the burn unit and received an ophthalmology consultation, where he was diagnosed with ‘bilateral cataracts’. He presented to our institution due to ongoing progressive visual deterioration. His vital signs were stable at the time of admission. The patient had no significant systemic disease or history of ocular blunt trauma.

Discussion

These three cases had different onset times and disease courses but shared similar clinical features. Cataract formation after electrical injury can be insidious, with different onset time. In the three cases, the interval between electrical injury and cataract detection ranged from 3 months to over 50 years. Typical lens changes include anterior subcapsular, anterior subcapsular cortical, and posterior subcapsular opacities. In Case 2, cataracts were discovered 50 years after injury. Although other etiologies are possible, the patient had no known risk factors for cataracts (such as diabetes, use of steroids or other medications, or ocular trauma), and the affected eye showed asymmetric anterior subcapsular sunflower-like opacities characteristic of electrical cataracts, while the contralateral eye had only mild nuclear opacity, we acknowledge that a causal relationship cannot be definitively established. Nevertheless, previous reports confirm similarly long intervals (several decades), suggesting that cataracts can be a long-term delayed complication of electrical and lightning injuries [5]. Another reported case described a female patient who developed bilateral cataracts 25 years after an indirect lightning injury [6]. The electrical injury remained the most likely etiology of the cataract in this case, while also presenting this as a particularity and limitation of Case 2.

Electrical burns are classified as low-voltage (< 1,000 V) or high-voltage (≥ 1,000 V) [7]. Lightning strikes often cause less destructive entry/exit due to brief contact [8]. In our series, Case 1 (indirect lightning) had no burns and unilateral cataract; Case 2 (childhood low-voltage, short contact, no burns) developed unilateral cataract 50 years later; Case 3 (high-voltage, entry hand/exit forehead) had bilateral cataracts, with the left eye (closer to forehead scar) affected earlier and progressing faster. Head and neck electrical injury is a risk factor for cataract development (3.7× higher risk) [9]. Higher voltage is likely to lead to an earlier onset and more severe cataracts; the eye closer to the entry point is often affected first and more rapidly [10, 11].

Cases 1 and 3 (lightning and high-voltage) shared snowflake anterior subcapsular, anterior subcapsular cortical, and posterior subcapsular opacities. Another bilateral lightning injury case was reported, presenting with anterior and posterior subcapsular opacities without special morphology, similar to our Case 1 [12]. Case 2 (low-voltage) had unique sunflower or starfish-shaped opacities, similar to a previous lightning-induced cataract case [13], but different from our lightning Case 1. Thus, distinctive cataract morphologies should prompt inquiry about possible electrical or lightning injury.

The earliest change is fine vacuoles under the anterior lens capsule [11], which are often difficult to see without dilating the pupil. This matches the progression observed in Case 3’s right eye over a period of 1.5 years. In Cases 1 and 2, the fellow eyes appeared clear lenses and mild nuclear opacity on slit-lamp. Pentacam Scheimpflug imaging showed no subcapsular opacities within the natural pupillary area. However, the literature suggests that the Pentacam Scheimpflug can detect subclinical lens opacities not visible by slit lamp [14]. Notably, in our three cases, the lenses were not examined with Pentacam under dilation. Therefore, we cannot rule out the presence of peripheral subclinical opacities.Lightning injuries often involve multiple systems, so ophthalmic attention may occur only after cataract-induced visual impairment, leading to a lack of data on timing, progression, and mechanisms.

A systematic review indicates that ocular manifestations of electric or lightning injuries can include: cataract, anterior uveitis, corneal edema, fundus damage [15], pupillary abnormalities, lens dislocation [16] and spontaneous posterior capsular rupture [17], etc. Case 1 had ocular redness/pain post-injury and synechiae suggesting possible prior uveitis, plus reduced corneal endothelial cell density (no similar reports found). P-VEP and visual field tests suggested slight impairment of optic nerve fibers and conduction, without functional impact; a similar lightning-induced cataract case with more severe damage had worse best-corrected visual acuity [18]. Case 3 had intraoperative floppy iris and iris prolapse, suggesting possible damage to the iris muscle fibers from an high-voltage electrical injury. Additionally, Case 3 had macular damage, with severely reduced P-VEP but normal F-VEP, indicating macular dysfunction despite intact conduction. Lack of visual field testing in this case is a limitation. Macular involvement following electrical injury may be due to its high melanin concentration, which increases resistance and reduces heat dissipation [15]. The macular neuroepithelial detachment and ellipsoid zone disruption in Case 3 improved spontaneously without specific treatment, consistent with literature [19], but without correspoding visual acuity gain.

Case 1, the young patient desired full visual range, and received an EDOF IOL despite intraoperative localized zonular laxity. Postoperative IOL centration was good, but an unexpected myopic shift occurred, possibly due to effective lens position (ELP) changes from partial zonular laxity. However, we are aware of a similar case in which a favorable outcome was achieved after EDOF IOL implantation [20]. Case 2 had lower near vision demands and received a monofocal toric IOL. Mild zonular laxity was observed intraoperatively but did not result in IOL malposition. In Cases 1 and 2, no phacodonesis or dilated signs of zonular abnormality were seen during preoperative examination, so UBM was not performed, lacking important evidence for the dynamic assessment of zonular function. The postoperative refractive surprise in Case 1 highlights that zonular abnormalities should be a key preoperative consideration, and great caution should be exercised when selecting functional IOLs for electrical cataract patients. In Case 3, mild phacodonesis was observed preoperatively, prompting UBM examination, which revealed no signs of zonular abnormalities, yet zonular laxity was also observed intraoperatively. This may suggest that electrical injury may primarily affect zonular function rather than inducing structural disruption. The zonules attach to the anterior and posterior surfaces of the lens capsule [21]. We hypothesize that the electrical current may also affect the zonular microstructure (e.g. proteins, branching of zonular fibers) or the neuromuscular regulatory function of the ciliary body–zonule complex, leading to loss of functional tension-that is more likely to be detected intraoperatively under dynamic conditions, and UBM may not be able to detect it.

Limitations of this study

Small sample size (n = 3), retrospective design with potential incomplete data, variable follow-up (months to 1.5 years post-surgery), inability to cover all electrical injury parameters (voltage, current, pathway), and single-center recruitment (possible selection bias). Multi-center collaboration will be essential to expand the cohort.

In conclusion, prevention of electrical injury is of paramount importance. Cataract onset after electrical injury is variable. In cases of lightning and electrical injury, characteristic lens changes may include various morphologies of anterior subcapsular opacities, anterior subcapsular cortical opacities, and posterior subcapsular opacities. Stellate or petaloid patterns should prompt inquiry about electrical/lightning history. Preoperative comprehensive evaluation of the fundus, cornea, endothelium, iris, lens capsule, zonular function, and dilated examination are essential to guide IOL selection.
