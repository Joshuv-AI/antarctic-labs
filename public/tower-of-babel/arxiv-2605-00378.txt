Explicit marginal distributions for permutations with prescribed Robinson–Schensted shape
William Q. Erickson

Abstract
. Given a permutation σ \sigma , the Robinson–Schensted correspondence determines a certain partition called the shape of σ \sigma . Famously, the shape measures the longest unions of increasing and decreasing subsequences, thus giving global information about σ \sigma . In this paper, by contrast, we ask how prescribing a shape collectively controls local behavior: if σ \sigma is a random permutation of shape λ \lambda , then what is P i ​ j λ ≔ P^{\lambda}_{ij}\coloneqq the probability that σ ⁡ ( i ) = j \sigma(i)=j ? Using tableau-theoretic methods, we derive explicit formulas for P i ​ j λ P^{\lambda}_{ij} when λ \lambda is a hook, two-row, or rectangular shape. We use these formulas to depict and analyze the intricate diffraction-like patterns in the matrices ( P i ​ j λ ) (P^{\lambda}_{ij}) . As a surprising application, we show that for both hook and two-row shapes, as the largest part of λ \lambda tends to infinity with the remaining parts fixed (summing to m m ), the expected proportion of fixed points in σ \sigma approaches the Wallis integral ∫ 0 π / 2 sin 2 ​ m + 1 ⁡ x ​ 𝑑 x = ( 2 ​ m ) !! / ( 2 ​ m + 1 ) !! \int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{2m+1}x\>dx=(2m)!!/(2m+1)!! .

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
denote the symmetric group on
n
letters.
In this paper, we solve the following problem in the case where the partition
\lambda
is a hook or two-row or rectangular shape:

Problem 1.1
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be chosen uniformly at random, and let
\lambda
be a partition of
n
.
For all
1\leq i,j\leq n
, find an explicit formula for the conditional probability

P^{\lambda}_{ij}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sigma(i)=j\;\Big|\;\sigma\text{ has Robinson--Schensted shape }\lambda\Big).

Programming our solution has led us to realize that the doubly stochastic matrix
P^{\lambda}=(P^{\lambda}_{ij})
exhibits intricate “diffraction”-like patterns that imitate certain aspects of the Young diagram of
\lambda
;
see Figures
1
–
3
, which show the heat maps of
P^{\lambda}
for hook, two-row, and rectangular shapes, respectively.
Our
P^{\lambda}_{ij}
formulas not only describe these patterns explicitly, but also enable us to generate their pictures in the first place, since a brute-force approach becomes computationally prohibitive even for relatively small
n
.
We are hopeful that the results and methods in the paper might provide a launch point in solving Problem
1.1
for other families of shapes, or ultimately for arbitrary
\lambda
.

At a less granular level, however, when we forget the tableau entries, the RS correspondence simply associates to each permutation in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
a certain
shape
, namely the partition of
n
given by the row lengths of either tableau.
For example, the permutation in (
1.1
) has shape
(3,2,1,1)
.
The shape of a permutation
\sigma
encodes global information about
\sigma
:
Greene’s theorem (
2.12
) states that the partial sums of the shape’s row (resp., column) lengths give the lengths of the longest unions of increasing (resp., decreasing) subsequences in
\sigma
.
Thus, since prescribing the shape of a random permutation
\sigma
controls the lengths of its monotone subsequences, this should presumably affect the expected
local
behavior of
\sigma
, meaning the marginal distribution of
\sigma(i)
for each
1\leq i\leq n
.
The effect of shape on the marginal distributions seems not to have been studied in the literature;
our desire to understand and quantify it led us to pose Problem
1.1
, the central problem in this project.

Problem
1.1
admits trivial solutions in the two extreme cases where
\lambda=(n)
is a single row or
\lambda=(1^{n})\coloneqq(1,\ldots,1)
is a single column.
In the one-row case, there is only one permutation of shape
\lambda=(n)
, namely the identity
(1,2,3,\ldots,n)
, and so we have
P^{\lambda}_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
.
Likewise, in the one-column case, there is only one permutation of shape
\lambda=(1^{n})
, namely the exchange permutation
(n,\ldots,3,2,1)
, and so we have
P^{\lambda}_{ij}=\delta_{i,n+1-j}
.
All other shapes interpolate between these two extreme cases in a deeply complicated way.

To gain some intuition for this, it is helpful to view the
heat map
of the matrix
P^{\lambda}
, where each entry
P^{\lambda}_{ij}
is given a shade between white (0) and black (the maximum matrix entry).
See Figure
4
for the heat maps of all matrices
P^{\lambda}
in the case
n=7
;
note the complicated way in which the mass spreads away from the diagonal, and then back toward the antidiagonal, as
\lambda
varies between the two extremes
(n)
and
(1^{n})
.
Even at such a small value
n=7
, one can see the very coarse beginnings of the diffraction-like patterns in Figures
1
–
3
.
Because
n=7
is so small, one can easily generate the heat maps in Figure
4
by brute force, by averaging the permutation matrices of all permutations with shape
\lambda
.
By contrast, the last heat map shown in Figure
3
would have required taking the average of approximately
5\times 10^{17}
permutation matrices.

A different perspective on Problem
1.1
comes from the representation theory of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, where the group algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]
acts on its defining representation
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, with each
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
acting via its corresponding permutation matrix.
Then
P^{\lambda}
is the matrix of the element in
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]
obtained by averaging over all permutations of shape
\lambda
.
Although these “shape classes” in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
lack the nice algebraic structure enjoyed by, say, a subgroup or a conjugacy class, nonetheless it would be interesting in future work to investigate the determinant, eigenvalues, eigenvectors, etc. of
P^{\lambda}
and to study their representation-theoretic interpretations.

Main result:
P^{\lambda}_{ij}
formulas

In this paper we focus on three families of shapes
\lambda
:
namely,
hook
shapes
(\ell,1^{m})
,
two-row
shapes
(\ell,m)
, and
rectangular
shapes
(\ell^{m})\coloneqq(\ell,\ldots,\ell)
.
(Due to the inherent symmetries of the RS correspondence (Lemma
2.3
), our solution for two-row shapes automatically yields the solution for two-
column
shapes as well, by reflecting
P^{\lambda}
horizontally or vertically.)
We chose these three families in part because they exhibit a natural and interesting contrast with respect to the complements of their first rows:
for hooks and two-row shapes, the complement is as thin as possible (a single column or row), whereas for rectangular shapes it is as thick as possible.
Immediately below, we preview our three main theorems, for hook, two-row, and rectangular shapes, respectively;
the reader will observe that the three
P^{\lambda}_{ij}
formulas differ significantly from each other, despite our best efforts to render them in a uniform manner.
This is a reflection of the various symmetries in the RS correspondence when restricted to certain shapes.
Recall that
f^{\mu}
denotes the number of standard Young tableaux of shape
\mu
.

Theorem 1.2

(see Theorem

)
.

Let
\lambda=(\ell,\>1^{n-\ell})

For all

\displaystyle\textstyle\left(\kern-2.79999pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a}{b}\right)\kern-2.79999pt\right)

Method of proof

Sections
3
,
4
, and
5
, devoted to hook and two-row and rectangular shapes, respectively, share a parallel four-part development:

(1)

Lemma
3.1
(resp.,
4.1
and
5.1
) is the theoretical heart of the argument.
We decompose the set of
\sigma
’s of shape
\lambda
according to the relative position of
\sigma(i)=j
:

•

For hook shapes,
\sigma
forms an “X” pattern, meaning the union of a maximal increasing subsequence and a maximal decreasing subsequence.
We decompose the
\sigma
’s according to whether
\sigma(i)
lies in the “crossroads” of the “X,” meaning the intersection of the two maximal subsequences.
If so, then
\sigma
contributes 1 to the
X
formula in Theorem
1.2
;
if not, then
\sigma
contributes 1 to one of the
Y
formulas, depending on which of the four legs of the “X” contains
\sigma(i)
.

•

For two-row shapes,
\sigma
is the disjoint union of a maximal increasing sequence and several descent pairs, determined by Viennot’s “light and shadow” construction (see Algorithm
2.2
).
We decompose the
\sigma
’s accordingly:
if
\sigma(i)
lies in the maximal increasing subsequence, then
\sigma
contributes 1 to the
V
formula in Theorem
1.3
, and otherwise to the
W
formula.

•

For rectangular shapes, which are especially amenable to the RS correspondence, there is no advantage in decomposing the
\sigma
’s into subtypes;
rather, we immediately characterize all
\sigma
’s in terms of the positions of the entries
i
and
j
in the corresponding RS tableaux.

(2)

Lemma
3.2
(resp.,
4.2
and
5.2
) is the technical heart of the argument.
For each subtype
\star
of
\sigma
’s described above, we exhibit a bijection
\Phi_{\star}
of the following form:

\{\text{$\sigma$'s of type $\star$}\}\xrightarrow{\;\;\operatorname{RS}\;\;}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{certain tableau pairs }\\
\text{$(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})$ of shape $\lambda$}\end{array}\right\}\xrightarrow{\;\;\Phi_{\star}\;\;}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{certain tableau}\\
\text{quadruples $(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D})$}\end{array}\right\},

where
\mathsf{A}
(resp.,
\mathsf{B}
) is the subtableau of
\mathsf{P}
(resp.,
\mathsf{Q}
) with entries up to
i
(resp.,
j
), and where
\mathsf{C}
(resp.,
\mathsf{D}
) is formed by rearranging and relabeling the remainder of
\mathsf{P}
(resp.,
\mathsf{Q})
.
This explains all the fourfold products of

1.2
–
1.4
.
The five bijections
\Phi_{\mathrm{X}}
,
\Phi_{\mathrm{Y}}
,

,

are defined in detail in (

(3)

Theorem

We combine the two previous lemmas to give the desired formula for

(4)

Remark

(resp.,

(The reader can quickly verify these observations by inspecting the main theorems to determine the

In particular, a two-row shape

Despite the uniform approach outlined above, the three families each required a surprising degree of special handling.
For instance, hooks (being conjugate to other hooks) admit symmetries via evacuation operators which two-row shapes do not have.
Moreover, for hooks and rectangular shapes it suffices to determine just the locations of

and

and

Having worked through the various shape-dependent details in this paper, we are increasingly curious whether Problem

Fixed points and Wallis integrals

Our formulas above led to an unexpected result concerning fixed point asymptotics.
Given a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, a key object of interest is its
fixed point set

\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)\coloneqq\Big\{i:\sigma(i)=i\Big\}.

Note that the trace of our matrix
P^{\lambda}
equals the expected number of fixed points in a permutation of shape
\lambda
.
Hence this expected value can be obtained directly from Theorems
1.2
–
1.4
simply by taking
\sum_{i}P^{\lambda}_{ii}
.
A more interesting problem is to determine an asymptotic for the
proportion
of fixed points:
in particular, for either hook or two-row shapes
\lambda
, one can fix the complement of the first row of
\lambda
at some size
m
, and then ask for the expected proportion of fixed points as the length
\ell
of the first row approaches infinity.
(The question does not make sense for rectangular shapes, of course, since letting only the first row grow destroys the rectangular shape.)

Our trace formulas from Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
yield the following curious answer to this problem — the same answer, in fact, for both hook and two-row shapes.
By way of background, the family of
Wallis integrals
was introduced in 1656 by John Wallis
[
26
]
in his development of an infinite product expression for
\pi
.
In particular, the
k
th Wallis integral is given by

(1.2)

W_{k}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{k}\!x\>dx=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\cos^{k}\!x\>dx,\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\lambda=(\ell,\>1^{m})
or
(\ell,\>m)
, and let
\sigma
with shape
\lambda
be chosen uniformly at random.
For
m\geq 0
fixed,

\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\left|\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)\right|}{n}\right]=\frac{(2m)!!}{(2m+1)!!}=W_{2m+1},

where
W_{2m+1}
is the
(2m+1)
th Wallis integral in (
1.2
).

We devote Section
6
to the proof of Theorem
1.5
.
It is striking that the result applies to both hooks and two-row shapes, since (below the first row) these two cases are opposite extremes.
This raises the question of whether the result in Theorem
1.5
is in fact shape-independent.

Problem 1.6
.

Fix a positive integer
m
, and let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be chosen uniformly at random from among those permutations whose longest increasing sequence has length
n-m
.
Is it true that as
n\rightarrow\infty
, the expected proportion of fixed points in
\sigma
equals
W_{2m+1}
?

Problem 1.7
.

Give a conceptual explanation (perhaps in terms of Beta functions?) for the appearance of the Wallis integral in Theorem
1.5
.

Lemma 2.1

\textstyle f^{(a,1^{b})}=\left(\kern-2.79999pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a}{b}\right)\kern-2.79999pt\right),\qquad f^{(a,b)}=\left\langle\kern-3.20007pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a}{b}\right\rangle\kern-3.20007pt\right\rangle,\qquad f^{(a+c,\>b)/(c)}=\left\langle\kern-3.20007pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a \rightarrow c}{b}\right\rangle\kern-3.20007pt\right\rangle.

Proof.

For a hook shape, each tableau in
{\rm SYT}(a,1^{b})
is uniquely determined by the entries in its first column below the 1.
Thus

equals the number of ways to choose these

\binom{a+b-1}{b}=\left(\kern-2.79999pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a}{b}\right)\kern-2.79999pt\right)

Next, it is well known

]
*Thm. 10.3.1 that

We exhibit the following bijection between the set of all such paths and the set

\mathsf{T}|_{\leq i}\coloneqq\text{the result obtained from $\mathsf{T}$ by deleting all boxes except those with entries $\leq i$}.

We define
\mathsf{T}|_{<i}
,
\mathsf{T}|_{>i}
, etc., in the same way.
Note that
\mathsf{T}|_{\leq i}\in{\rm SYT}(\mu)
, where
\mu
is the shape determined by those boxes of
\mathsf{T}
with entries
\leq i
.
Moreover, the result obtained by subtracting
i
from all entries of
\mathsf{T}|_{>i}
is an element of
{\rm SYT}(\lambda/\mu)
.

The
Robinson–Schensted
(
RS
)
correspondence
is a bijection

(2.10)

\operatorname{RS}:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\longrightarrow\coprod_{\lambda\vdash n}{\rm SYT}(\lambda)\times{\rm SYT}(\lambda),

to be defined explicitly below in Algorithm
2.2
.
In particular, we give the geometric “light and shadow” construction due to Viennot
[
25
]
;
the RS correspondence is also commonly defined via Schensted’s row insertion algorithm
[
21
]
, Schützenberger’s jeu de taquin
[
23
]
, or Fomin’s growth diagrams
[
7
,
8
]
.
In the following algorithm, we follow the exposition by Sagan
[
20
]
*§3.6, with the exception that we reverse the roles of the tableaux
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{Q}
.
(Thus our
\mathsf{P}
is the “recording tableau” and our
\mathsf{Q}
the “insertion tableau”;
we do this so that
\sigma(i)=j
corresponds to the entry
i
in
\mathsf{P}
and the entry
j
in
\mathsf{Q}
.
In light of Lemma
2.3
, this change in convention has no effect on our main result.)
See Figure
5
for an illustrated example of the following algorithm.

and

as a grid with matrix coordinates. Imagine a light source in the northwest corner.

Thus each point casts a

(1)

Initialize

.
(Each

of

(2)

to be the set of all points in

that do not lie in the shadow of another point.
The
shadow line
of

is the boundary of the combined shadow of its elements.

(3)

Recursively define

by applying step

for all

of
\sigma^{(r)}
.

(4)

Fill in row

of
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{Q}
as follows:

\mathsf{P}_{r,k}=\min\{i:(i,j)\in L_{k}^{(r)}\},\qquad\mathsf{Q}_{r,k}=\min\{j:(i,j)\in L_{k}^{(r)}\}.

(5)

Increment

\sigma^{(r+1)}
to be the set of points in
[n]\times[n]
corresponding to the

\lrcorner

-shapes in the shadow diagram of
\sigma^{(r)}
.
Repeat steps
2
–
4
.
The algorithm terminates when
\sigma^{(r)}=\varnothing

Schützenberger involutions

We denote the “complement” of
i\in[n]
by

(2.15)

\bar{\imath}\coloneqq n+1-i.

Consider the element
\omega_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
given by
\omega_{0}(i)=\bar{\imath}
, that is,
\omega_{0}=(n,\ldots,3,2,1)
.
The maps given by right- and left-multiplication by
\omega_{0}
are involutions of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
Along with inversion, these involutions generate the following group
G
acting naturally on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
:

(2.16)

G\coloneqq\Big\langle g_{{}_{\mathrm{T}}}:\sigma\mapsto\sigma^{-1},\quad g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}:\sigma\mapsto\sigma\omega_{0},\quad g_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}}:\sigma\mapsto\omega_{0}\sigma\Big\rangle.

The reason for our notation
g_{{}_{\mathrm{T}}}
,
g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}
,
g_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}}
is the fact that
G
is isomorphic to the dihedral group of order
8
, which is clear from its action
g:M_{\sigma}\mapsto M_{g\sigma}
on permutation matrices (
2.9
):
in particular,
g_{{}_{\mathrm{T}}}
is a reflection about the main diagonal (i.e., the transpose),
g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}
is a horizontal reflection, and
g_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}}
is a vertical reflection.
Note that the product

g_{{}_{\mathrm{R}}}\coloneqq g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}g_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}}=g_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}}g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}

is another involution acting on permutations via
g_{{}_{\mathrm{R}}}:\sigma\mapsto\omega_{0}\sigma\omega_{0}
, and on permutation matrices by a 180-degree rotation.
Explicitly, the following are equivalent:

(2.17)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sigma(i)&=j,\\
g_{{}_{\mathrm{T}}}\sigma(j)&=i,\\
g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}\sigma(i)&=\bar{\jmath},\\
g_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}}\sigma(\bar{\imath})&=j,\\
g_{{}_{\mathrm{R}}}\sigma(\bar{\imath})&=\bar{\jmath}.\end{split}

That is, if we write
\sigma
in one-line notation
\sigma=(\sigma(1),\ldots,\sigma(n))
, then
g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}\sigma
is obtained by taking the pointwise complement of
\sigma
, and
g_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}}\sigma
is obtained by reversing
\sigma
, and
g_{{}_{\mathrm{R}}}\sigma
is obtained by doing both.
Below we highlight the well-known interactions between the RS correspondence and the group
G
, due to Schensted
[
21
]
and Schützenberger
[
22
,
23
]
.
In particular, the following are equivalent
[
20
]
*Thms. 3.2.3, 3.6.6, 3.9.4:

(2.18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\operatorname{RS}(\sigma)&=(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})\text{ with }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda},\\
\operatorname{RS}(g_{{}_{\mathrm{T}}}\sigma)&=(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{P})\text{ with }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda},\\
\operatorname{RS}(g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}\sigma)&=(\mathsf{P}^{\prime},\epsilon(\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}))\text{ with }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda^{\prime}},\\
\operatorname{RS}(g_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}}\sigma)&=(\epsilon(\mathsf{P}^{\prime}),\mathsf{Q}^{\prime})\text{ with }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda^{\prime}},\\
\operatorname{RS}(g_{{}_{\mathrm{R}}}\sigma)&=(\epsilon(\mathsf{P}),\epsilon(\mathsf{Q}))\text{ with }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda},\\
\end{split}

where
\epsilon
denotes Schützenberger’s evacuation operator (itself an involution on
{\rm SYT}(\lambda)
; see
[
22
]
or
[
20
]
*§3.9), and where
\mathsf{P}^{\prime}
denotes the transpose of a tableau
\mathsf{P}
.
Combining each line in (
2.17
) with its corresponding line in (
2.18
), we obtain the following bijections with the set
\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}_{ij}
defined in (
2.14
):

(2.19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}_{ij}&\xleftrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447ptg_{{}_{\mathrm{T}}}\hskip 8.19447pt}\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}_{ji},\\
\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}_{ij}&\xleftrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447ptg_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}\hskip 8.19447pt}\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{i\bar{\jmath}},\\
\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}_{ij}&\xleftrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447ptg_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}}\hskip 8.19447pt}\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{\bar{\imath}j},\\
\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}_{ij}&\xleftrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447ptg_{{}_{\mathrm{R}}}\hskip 8.19447pt}\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}_{\bar{\imath}\bar{\jmath}}.\\
\end{split}

The matrix
P^{\lambda}

Our main object of interest is the
n\times n
matrix
P^{\lambda}
whose
(i,j)
entry gives the conditional probability

(2.20)

P^{\lambda}_{ij}\coloneqq\frac{|\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}_{ij}|}{|\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}|}=\mathbb{P}\Big(\sigma(i)=j\;\Big|\;\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}\Big),

where
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is chosen uniformly at random.
Note that
P^{\lambda}
is doubly stochastic.
Due to our combinatorial approach, we will also work with the nonnegative integer matrix
\check{P}^{\lambda}
defined via

(2.21)

\check{P}^{\lambda}_{ij}=\left|\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}_{ij}\right|.

(The notation is meant to suggest that
\check{P}^{\lambda}
is the “un-normalized” form of
P^{\lambda}
.)
In other words,

(2.22)

\check{P}^{\lambda}=\left|\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}\right|\cdot P^{\lambda}=\sum_{\mathclap{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}}}M_{\sigma},

where the second equality follows from the definitions (
2.9
) and (
2.14
).

A priori, the matrix
P^{\lambda}
inherits the symmetries from the action of the group
G
given in (
2.16
) and (
2.19
), which we record in the following lemma.
Recall that a square matrix is said to be
symmetric
if it is symmetric about its main diagonal;

persymmetric
if it is symmetric about its antidiagonal;
and
bisymmetric
if it is both symmetric and persymmetric.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, with
\lambda\vdash n
.
The matrix
P^{\lambda}
in (
2.20
) is bisymmetric, and is the vertical (equivalently, horizontal) reflection of the matrix
P^{\lambda^{\prime}}
.
More precisely, for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
,

P^{\lambda}_{ij}=P^{\lambda}_{ji}=P^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{i\bar{\jmath}}=P^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{\bar{\imath}j}=P^{\lambda}_{\bar{\imath}\bar{\jmath}}.

Proof.

By (
2.21
) we have
\check{P}^{\lambda}=|\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}_{ij}|
, and thus the bijections in (
2.19
) yield
\check{P}^{\lambda}_{ij}=\check{P}^{\lambda}_{ji}=\check{P}^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{i\bar{\jmath}}=\check{P}^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{\bar{\imath}j}=\check{P}^{\lambda}_{\bar{\imath}\bar{\jmath}}
.
(Note that bisymmetry means
\check{P}^{\lambda}_{ij}=\check{P}^{\lambda}_{\bar{\jmath}\,\bar{\imath}}
, which follows from combining the first and last equality.)
Since
P^{\lambda}
is a scalar multiple of
\check{P}^{\lambda}
, the result now follows.
∎

3.
Main result for hook shapes

Throughout this section, we fix a positive integer
n
, and we assume that
\lambda=(\ell,1^{n-\ell})
, where
1\leq\ell\leq n
.
By Greene’s theorem (
2.12
), we have
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}
if and only if
\sigma
contains an increasing subsequence of length
\ell
and a decreasing subsequence of length
\bar{\ell}\coloneqq n+1-\ell
.
Note that these two subsequences are not necessarily uniquely determined;
moreover, since
\ell+\bar{\ell}=n+1
, any two such subsequences intersect in exactly one term.
With this in mind, for all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}
we define

\displaystyle\mathrm{X}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{(\ell,i,j):\begin{array}[]{l}\text{$\sigma(i)=j$ is contained in both}\\
\text{an increasing subsequence of length $\ell$}\\
\text{and a decreasing subsequence of length $\bar{\ell}$}\end{array}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Y}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{(\ell,i,j):\begin{array}[]{l}\text{$\sigma(i)=j$, and}\\
\text{$i>i^{\prime}$ and $j>j^{\prime}$ for all $(\ell,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\mathrm{X}(\sigma)$}\end{array}\right\}.

In the triples
(\ell,i,j)
above, the
\ell
simply keeps track of the fact that
\sigma
has shape
(\ell,1^{n-\ell})
.
If we visualize the support of the permutation matrix
M_{\sigma}
, then we see that
(\ell,i,j)\in\mathrm{X}(\sigma)
precisely when
(i,j)
lies in the “crossroads” of this support, between a northwest-to-southeast pattern of length
\ell
and a northeast-to-southwest pattern of length
\bar{\ell}
.
(Hence the “X” notation, to evoke this crossroads.)
Meanwhile,
(\ell,i,j)\in\mathrm{Y}(\sigma)
precisely when
(i,j)
lies in the support of
M_{\sigma}
to the southeast of the crossroads.

Throughout this section, we adopt the shorthand

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathfrak{S}^{(\ell,1^{n-\ell})}_{ij},

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{X}}(\ell,i,j)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Big\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j):(\ell,i,j)\in\mathrm{X}(\sigma)\Big\},

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}(\ell,i,j)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Big\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j):(\ell,i,j)\in\mathrm{Y}(\sigma)\Big\}.

Recall the 8-element group
G
from (
2.16
).
Since
\lambda=(\ell,1^{n-\ell})
if and only if
\lambda^{\prime}=(\bar{\ell},1^{n-\bar{\ell}})
, with the bar notation as in (
2.15
), we can rewrite the bijections (
2.19
) as follows:

(3.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)&\xleftrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447ptg_{{}_{\mathrm{T}}}\hskip 8.19447pt}\mathfrak{S}(\ell,j,i),\\
\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)&\xleftrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447ptg_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}\hskip 8.19447pt}\mathfrak{S}(\bar{\ell},i,\bar{\jmath}),\\
\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)&\xleftrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447ptg_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}}\hskip 8.19447pt}\mathfrak{S}(\bar{\ell},\bar{\imath},j),\\
\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)&\xleftrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447ptg_{{}_{\mathrm{R}}}\hskip 8.19447pt}\mathfrak{S}(\ell,\bar{\imath},\bar{\jmath}).\end{split}

This induces an action by
G
on the set
\{\ell,\bar{\ell}\}\times\{i,\bar{\imath}\}\times\{j,\bar{\jmath}\}
, defined so that for all
g\in G
,

(3.10)

\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)\xleftrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447ptg\hskip 8.19447pt}\mathfrak{S}(g\cdot(\ell,i,j)).

From now on we deal primarily with the subgroup of involutions

(3.11)

I\coloneqq\Big\{e,\>g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}},\>g_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}},\>g_{{}_{\mathrm{R}}}\Big\}\subset G.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
1\leq\ell,i,j\leq n
, with all notation as in (
3.6
)–(
3.11
).
We have

\big|\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)\big|=\left|\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{X}}(\ell,i,j)\right|+\sum_{g\in I}\big|\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}(g\cdot(\ell,i,j))\big|.

Proof.

First observe from (
2.17
) and (
3
) that for all
g\in I
,

(3.12)

g\cdot\mathrm{X}(\sigma)=\mathrm{X}(g\sigma),

where the action on the left-hand side is the induced action in (
3.10
).
Next, observe from (
3
) and (
3.8
) and (
3.12
) that for each
g\in I
, the set
g\>\mathfrak{S}_{\rm{Y}}(g\cdot(\ell,i,j))
takes the form

\displaystyle e\>\mathfrak{S}_{\rm{Y}}\big(e\cdot(\ell,i,j)\big)

\displaystyle=\Big\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j):i>i^{\prime}\text{ and }j>j^{\prime}\text{ for all }(\ell,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\mathrm{X}(\sigma)\Big\},

\displaystyle g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}\>\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}\big(g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}\cdot(\ell,i,j)\big)

\displaystyle=\Big\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j):i>i^{\prime}\text{ and }j<j^{\prime}\text{ for all }(\ell,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\mathrm{X}(\sigma)\Big\},

\displaystyle g_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}}\>\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}\big(g_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}}\cdot(\ell,i,j)\big)

\displaystyle=\Big\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j):i<i^{\prime}\text{ and }j>j^{\prime}\text{ for all }(\ell,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\mathrm{X}(\sigma)\Big\},

\displaystyle g_{{}_{\mathrm{R}}}\>\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}\big(g_{{}_{\mathrm{R}}}\cdot(\ell,i,j)\big)

\displaystyle=\Big\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j):i<i^{\prime}\text{ and }j<j^{\prime}\text{ for all }(\ell,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\mathrm{X}(\sigma)\Big\}.

In words, each set above contains those
\sigma
’s for which
(i,j)
lies (respectively) southeast, southwest, northeast, and northwest of the “crossroads” of the support of
M_{\sigma}
.
Recalling from (
3.7
) that
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{X}}(\ell,i,j)
contains those
\sigma
’s for which
(i,j)
lies
in
the crossroads, it follows that every
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)
lies in exactly one of these five sets:

(3.13)

\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)=\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{X}}(\ell,i,j)\sqcup\coprod_{g\in I}g\>\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}\big(g\cdot(\ell,i,j)\big).

Since by (
3.10
) translation by
g
is a bijection, we have

\left|g\>\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}\big(g\cdot(\ell,i,j)\big)\right|=\left|\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}\big(g\cdot(\ell,i,j)\big)\right|,

and so the result follows from (
3.13
).
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
1\leq\ell\leq n
.
For all
1\leq i,j\leq n
,

\displaystyle X(\ell,i,j)\coloneqq\big|\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{X}}(\ell,i,j)\big|

\displaystyle=\textstyle\left(\kern-3.99994pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{K}{i - K}\right)\kern-3.99994pt\right)\left(\kern-3.99994pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{K}{j - K}\right)\kern-3.99994pt\right)\left(\kern-3.99994pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell-K+1}{n - \ell+ K - i}\right)\kern-3.99994pt\right)\left(\kern-3.99994pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell- K + 1}{n - \ell+ K - j}\right)\kern-3.99994pt\right),

\displaystyle Y(\ell,i,j)\coloneqq\big|\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}(\ell,i,j)\big|

\displaystyle=\sum_{1<k<K}\textstyle\left(\kern-3.99994pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k-1}{i-k}\right)\kern-3.99994pt\right)\left(\kern-3.99994pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k-1}{j-k}\right)\kern-3.99994pt\right)\left(\kern-3.99994pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell- k}{n - \ell+ k - i}\right)\kern-3.99994pt\right)\left(\kern-3.99994pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell- k}{n - \ell+ k - j}\right)\kern-3.99994pt\right),

where

and where
\left(\kern-2.79999pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a}{b}\right)\kern-2.79999pt\right)
is the multiset number defined in (

Proof.

We will prove each formula by exhibiting a bijection with a certain subset of
{\rm SYT}(\lambda)\times{\rm SYT}(\lambda)
.
First we prove the

formula.
Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)
, and suppose that
\operatorname{RS}(\sigma)=(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})
.
By (
3
) and (
3.7
), we have
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{X}}(\ell,i,j)
if and only if there exist indices

and
1\leq b_{1}<\cdots<b_{\bar{\ell}}\leq n
such that

(3.14)

\sigma(a_{1})<\cdots<\sigma(a_{\ell})\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma(b_{1})>\cdots>\sigma(b_{\bar{\ell}}),

along with indices
K
and

such that

a_{K}=b_{R}=i,\text{ and thus }\sigma(a_{K})=\sigma(b_{R})=j.

It follows that

(3.15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{R-1}\}&=\{1,\ldots,i-1\}\setminus\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{K-1}\},\\
\{\sigma(b_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(b_{R-1})\}&=\{j+1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{\sigma(a_{K+1}),\ldots,\sigma(a_{\ell})\}.\end{split}

Since the left-hand sides in (
3.15
) have the same cardinality
R-1
, the right-hand sets must as well, yielding

(3.16)

R-1=(i-1)-(K-1)=(n-j)-(\ell-K).

Solving for
K
, we obtain

K=(i+j+\ell-n)/2
,
which coincides with the
K
defined in the statement of the lemma.
Hence

(3.17)

\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{X}}(\ell,i,j)\text{ if and only if $\sigma(i)=j$ is the $K$th term in an increasing subsequence of length $\ell$}.

Hence if
K\notin[\ell]
, then automatically
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{X}}(\ell,i,j)=\varnothing
;
in this case, the
X(\ell,i,j)
formula in the lemma returns 0 by definition (
2.5
), so we are done.
We therefore may assume that
K\in[\ell]
.
By (
3.17
), we have
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{X}}(\ell,i,j)
if and only if
(i,j)\in L_{K}^{(1)}
in Algorithm
2.2
.
Thus we must have
\mathsf{P}_{1,K-1}<\mathsf{P}_{1,K}\leq i<\mathsf{P}_{1,K+1}
, assuming the entries in question exist.
Due to the hook shape of
\mathsf{P}
, it follows that the entry
i
occupies either box
K
in the first row, or box
i-K+1
in the first column.
By the same argument, in
\mathsf{Q}
the entry
j
occupies either box
K
in the first row, or box
j-K+1
in the first column.
Hence

\operatorname{RS}\left(\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{X}}(\ell,i,j)\right)=\left\{(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})\in{\rm SYT}(\lambda)\times{\rm SYT}(\lambda):\begin{aligned} i&=\,\mathsf{P}_{1,K}\text{ or }\mathsf{P}_{i-K+1,1}\,,\\[-2.15277pt]
j&=\mathsf{Q}_{1,K}\text{ or }\mathsf{Q}_{j-K+1,1}\end{aligned}\right\},

and, recalling the notation
\mathsf{P}|_{\leq i}
from (
2.8
), we have a bijection

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathrm{X}}:\operatorname{RS}\left(\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{X}}(\ell,i,j)\right)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\left(\begin{array}[]{r}{\rm SYT}(K,1^{i-K})\\
{}\times{\rm SYT}(K,1^{j-K})\end{array}\right)\times\left(\begin{array}[]{r}{\rm SYT}(\ell-K+1,\>1^{n-\ell+K-i})\\
{}\times{\rm SYT}(\ell-K+1,\>1^{n-\ell+K-j})\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})

\displaystyle\longmapsto\big((\mathsf{P}|_{\leq i},\mathsf{Q}|_{\leq j}),(\mathsf{R},\mathsf{S})\big)

where
\mathsf{R}
(resp.,
\mathsf{S}
) is formed from
\mathsf{P}|_{>i}
(resp.,
\mathsf{Q}|_{>j}
) by decreasing all entries by
i-1
(resp.,
j-1
), and then sliding the two pieces together to meet at a new upper-left box with entry 1.
This process is clearly invertible, so
\Phi_{\mathrm{X}}^{-1}
is well defined.
Since
\operatorname{RS}
is injective, the
X(\ell,i,j)
formula now follows by using Lemma
2.1
to take the cardinality of the right-hand side of (
3
).

Now we prove the
Y(\ell,i,j)
formula.
By (
3.8
), we have
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}(\ell,i,j)
if and only if there exist (not necessarily unique) sequences (
3.14
) along with indices
k
and
h
such that

a_{k}=i,\;\;\sigma(a_{k})=j,\qquad b_{h}<i,\;\;\sigma(b_{h})<j,\qquad b_{h}\notin\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell}\}.

It follows that

\{b_{m}:\sigma(b_{m})>j\}\subsetneq\Big(\{b_{m}:b_{m}<i\}\setminus\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell}\}\Big),

since the
\sigma(b_{m})
’s are decreasing, and since
b_{h}
is contained in the right-hand set but not the left-hand set.
Rewriting these two sets, we have

\sigma^{-1}\Big(\{j+1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{\sigma(a_{k+1}),\ldots,\sigma(a_{\ell})\}\Big)\subsetneq\Big(\{1,\ldots,i-1\}\setminus\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1\}}\Big),

and taking cardinalities we obtain

(n-j)-(\ell-k)<(i-1)-(k-1),

and solving for
k
we have

k<(i+j+\ell-n)/2=K.

Thus
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}(\ell,i,j)
if and only if
\sigma(i)=j
is the
k
th term in an increasing subsequence of length
\ell
, for some
k<K
.
Equivalently,
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}(\ell,i,j)
if and only if
L_{k}^{(1)}=\{(i,j)\}
for some
k<K
in Algorithm
2.2
;
note that
L_{k}^{(1)}
must be a singleton due to the fact (
3.8
) that
(i,j)
is southeast of every point in
\mathrm{X}(\sigma)
.
Consequently we have
\mathsf{P}_{1,k}=i
and
\mathsf{Q}_{1,k}=j
.
Note that
k=1
would force
i=j=1
and thus
k<K=(1+1+\ell-n)/2=1+(\ell-n)/2\leq 1
, which is a contradiction; therefore we may take
1<k<K
.
Hence

\operatorname{RS}\left(\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}(\ell,i,j)\right)=\coprod_{1<k<K}\Big\{(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})\in{\rm SYT}(\lambda)\times{\rm SYT}(\lambda):i=\mathsf{P}_{1,k}\text{ and }j=\mathsf{Q}_{1,k}\Big\}

and we have a bijection

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathrm{Y}}:\operatorname{RS}\left(\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}(\ell,i,j)\right)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\coprod_{1<k<K}\left(\begin{array}[]{r}{\rm SYT}(k-1,1^{i-k})\\
{}\times{\rm SYT}(k-1,1^{j-k})\end{array}\right)\times\left(\begin{array}[]{r}{\rm SYT}(\ell-k,\>1^{n-\ell+k-i})\\
{}\times{\rm SYT}(\ell-k,\>1^{n-\ell+k-j})\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})

\displaystyle\longmapsto\big((\mathsf{P}|_{<i},\mathsf{Q}|_{<j}),(\mathsf{R},\mathsf{S})\big)

where
\mathsf{R}
(resp.,
\mathsf{S}
) is formed from
\mathsf{P}|_{\geq i}
(resp.,
\mathsf{Q}|_{\geq j}
) by decreasing all entries by
i-1
(resp.,
j-1
), and then sliding the two pieces together so that the upper-left corner is the box now labeled 1, that is, the box originally labeled
i
(resp.,
j
).
The rest of the proof is the same as the
X(\ell,i,j)
case above.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\lambda=(\ell,1^{n-\ell})
be a hook shape, and recall
P^{\lambda}_{ij}
from (
2.20
).
For all
1\leq i,j\leq n
,

P^{\lambda}_{ij}=\frac{X(\ell,i,j)+Y(\ell,i,j)+Y(\bar{\ell},i,\bar{\jmath})+Y(\bar{\ell},\bar{\imath},j)+Y(\ell,\bar{\imath},\bar{\jmath})}{\left(\kern-3.99994pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell}{n-\ell}\right)\kern-3.99994pt\right)^{2}},

where the functions
X
and
Y
are those defined in Lemma
3.2
, where
\bar{\imath}\coloneqq n+1-i
, and where
\left(\kern-2.79999pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a}{b}\right)\kern-2.79999pt\right)
is the multiset number defined in (
2.5
).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\check{P}^{\lambda}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\left|\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)\right|

by (
2.21
) and (
3.6
)

\displaystyle=\left|\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{X}}(\ell,i,j)\right|+\sum_{g\in I}\left|\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{Y}}\big(g\cdot(\ell,i,j)\big)\right|

by Lemma
3.1

\displaystyle=X(\ell,i,j)+\sum_{g\in I}Y\big(g\cdot(\ell,i,j)\big)

\displaystyle\text{by Lemma~\ref{lemma:size SX SY}},

and thus by (
3.9
)–(
3.10
), the numerator in the theorem equals
\check{P}^{\lambda}_{ij}
.
The result now follows from (
2.13
) and Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Remark 3.4

(Diagrammatic interpretation)
.

The following construction gives a simple way to visualize the contribution to
\operatorname{supp}P^{\lambda}
coming from each of the five terms in Theorem
3.3
.
Throughout this remark, when we write “
\lambda
,” we refer to the result of rotating the Young diagram of
\lambda
clockwise by 45 degrees, then superimposing it to fit snugly inside a generic
n\times n
matrix.
(Note that
\lambda
is depicted in red in the equation at the end of this remark.)
In particular, the upper-left box of
\lambda
occupies matrix position
(1,\bar{\ell})
, the upper-right box occupies position
(\ell,n)
, and the lower-left box occupies position
(\bar{\ell},1)
.
By
column
k
of
\lambda
, we mean its
k
th column from the left;
to account for cases where
k
is not between
1
and
\ell
, we imagine the columns continuing in both directions past the edges of
\lambda
.
Similarly, by
row
k
of
\lambda
, we mean its
k
th row from the top (imagining the same conventions as described above when
k
is not between
1
and
\bar{\ell}
).
In the proof of Lemma
3.2
, by solving the equation (
3.16
) for
K\coloneqq K(\ell,i,j)
and
R\coloneqq R(\ell,i,j)
we obtained

\displaystyle K(\ell,i,j)

\displaystyle=(i+j-\bar{\ell}+1)/2,

\displaystyle R(\ell,i,j)

\displaystyle=(i+\bar{\jmath}-\ell+1)/2=K\big(g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}\cdot(\ell,i,j)\big)=i-K(\ell,i,j)+1.

The position
(i,j)
occupies column
K(\ell,i,j)
and row
R(\ell,i,j)
of
\lambda
.
To collect all the
(i,j)
’s together, define five
n\times n
matrices
P^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{X}}
and
P^{\lambda}_{g}
via

\displaystyle(P^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{X}})_{ij}

\displaystyle=X(\ell,i,j)\big/|\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}|,

\displaystyle(P^{\lambda}_{g})_{ij}

\displaystyle=Y\big(g\cdot(\ell,i,j)\big)\big/|\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}|\text{ for all $g\in I$}.

Then Theorem
3.3
can be rewritten as the matrix equation

(3.26)

P^{\lambda}=P^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{X}}+\sum_{g\in I}P^{\lambda}_{g}.

The matrix
P^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{X}}
is bisymmetric;
P^{\lambda}_{e}
and
P^{\lambda}_{g_{{}_{\mathrm{R}}}}
are symmetric only; and
P^{\lambda}_{g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}}
and
P^{\lambda}_{g_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}}}
are persymmetric only.
Moreover, upon inspecting where the multiset numbers vanish in Theorem
3.3
, one can determine the support of each matrix above:

•

The support of
P^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{X}}
is its central “checkerboard” region

\operatorname{supp}P^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{X}}=\left\{(i,j):\begin{array}[]{l}\text{$K(\ell,i,j)$ and $R(\ell,i,j)$ are integers, and}\\[2.15277pt]
1\leq K(\ell,i,j)\leq\ell\text{ and }1\leq R(\ell,i,j)\leq\bar{\ell}\end{array}\right\}.

•

The support of
P^{\lambda}_{e}
is its southeastern region

\operatorname{supp}P^{\lambda}_{e}=\Big\{(i,j):K(\ell,i,j)>2\text{ and }1<R(\ell,i,j)<\bar{\ell}\Big\}.

•

The support of
P^{\lambda}_{g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}}
is its southwestern region

\operatorname{supp}P^{\lambda}_{g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}}=\Big\{(i,j):1<K(\ell,i,j)<\ell\text{ and }R(\ell,i,j)>2\Big\}.

•

The matrix
P^{\lambda}_{g_{{}_{\mathrm{V}}}}
is the 180-degree rotation of
P^{\lambda}_{g_{{}_{\mathrm{H}}}}
, so its support is in its northeastern region.

•

The matrix
P^{\lambda}_{g_{{}_{\mathrm{R}}}}
is the 180-degree rotation of
P^{\lambda}_{e}
, so its support is in its northwestern region.

From the observations above, it follows that if
\lambda
is a hook shape, then the support of

is all of
[n]\times[n]
, except in the edge case

.
Thus (with the exception of that edge case), prescribing a hook shape
\lambda
never prevents
\sigma
from sending any

to any

.
This is in stark contrast with the two-row and rectangular cases, where prescribing
\lambda
forces the bandwidth of
\sigma
to equal
\ell
or

, respectively; see Remarks

and

.

4.
Main result for two-row shapes

Throughout this section, we fix a positive integer
n
, and we assume that
\lambda=(\ell,n-\ell)
is a two-row shape (which forces
n/2\leq\ell\leq n
).
For all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}
we define

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{V}(\sigma)&\coloneqq\Big\{(i,j)\in\operatorname{supp}M_{\sigma}:\text{$L_{k}^{(1)}=\{(i,j)\}$ for some $1\leq k\leq\ell$}\Big\},\\
\mathrm{W}(\sigma)&\coloneqq\Big\{(i,j)\in\operatorname{supp}M_{\sigma}:\text{$L_{k}^{(1)}=\{(i,j),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\}$ for some $1\leq k\leq\ell$}\Big\},\end{split}

with
L_{k}^{(1)}
as defined in Algorithm
2.2
, where
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\neq(i,j)
.
(The notation is meant to suggest the fact that, upon rotating the page 135 degrees counterclockwise, the points in
\mathrm{V}(\sigma)
lie on a V-shaped shadow line, while the points in
\mathrm{W}(\sigma)
lie on a W-shaped shadow line.)
Throughout this section, we adopt the shorthand

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)&\coloneqq\mathfrak{S}^{(\ell,n-\ell)}_{ij},\\
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{V}}(\ell,i,j)&\coloneqq\Big\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j):(i,j)\in\mathrm{V}(\sigma)\Big\},\\
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{W}}(\ell,i,j)&\coloneqq\Big\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j):(i,j)\in\mathrm{W}(\sigma)\Big\}.\end{split}

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(\ell,n-\ell)
be a two-row shape, with notation as in (
4.2
).
For all
1\leq i,j\leq n
,

\left|\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)\right|=\left|\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{V}}(\ell,i,j)\right|+\left|\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{W}}(\ell,i,j)\right|.

Proof.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)
.
Since
(\ell,\>n-\ell)
has at most two rows, Algorithm
2.2
terminates at
r\leq 2
.
Hence each
|L_{k}^{(1)}|\leq 2
, and so
\operatorname{supp}M_{\sigma}=\mathrm{V}(\sigma)\sqcup\mathrm{W}(\sigma)
.
Upon fixing
(i,j)
, we have
\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)=\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{V}}(\ell,i,j)\sqcup\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{W}}(\ell,i,j)
, which completes the proof.
∎

In the following lemma, we use the Iverson bracket
[P]
, defined to be 1 if
P
is true and 0 if
P
is false.
We also use the shorthand
[a]_{+}\coloneqq\max\{a,\>0\}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(\ell,\>n-\ell)
be a two-row shape.
For all
1\leq i,j\leq n
,

\displaystyle V(\ell,i,j)\coloneqq\left|\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{V}}(\ell,i,j)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\textstyle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k-1}{i-k}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k-1}{j-k}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell- k \: \rightarrow[j - i]_{_+}}{n - \ell+ k - i}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell- k \: \rightarrow[i-j]_{_+}}{n - \ell+ k - j}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle,

\displaystyle W(\ell,i,j)\coloneqq\left|\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{W}}(\ell,i,j)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathclap{(p,q)\in{L}}}\textstyle\;\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{p - [i<j]}{i - p - [i>j]}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\!\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{q-[i>j]}{j - q - [i<j]}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\!\left(\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{l}\phantom{-}\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell- p \: \rightarrow\: [j-i]_{_+} + p - q}{n - \ell+ p - i}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell- q \: \rightarrow\: [i-j]_{_+} + q - p}{n - \ell+ q - j}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\\[8.61108pt]
-\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell- p \: \rightarrow\: [j-i]_{_+} + p - q - 1}{n - \ell+ p - i}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell- q \: \rightarrow\: [i-j]_{_+} + q - p - 1}{n - \ell+ q - j}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\end{array}\!\!\right),

where

{L}\coloneqq\left\{(p,q)\in[\ell]\times[\ell]:\begin{array}[]{l}p\leq i,\;\;q\leq j,\\
p+q\geq\max\{i,j\},\\
0\leq(p-q)(i-j)<(i-j)^{2}\end{array}\right\},

and where
\left\langle\kern-3.20007pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a \rightarrow c}{b}\right\rangle\kern-3.20007pt\right\rangle
and
\left\langle\kern-3.20007pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a}{b}\right\rangle\kern-3.20007pt\right\rangle
are the ballot numbers defined in (
2.6
)–(
2.7
).

Proof.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)
.
By the argument in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, for all
1\leq k\leq\ell
in Algorithm
2.2
, we have
|L_{k}^{(1)}|=1
or
2
.
Moreover, there are exactly
\ell
many
k
-values such that
|L_{k}^{(1)}|=1
, and thus
n-\ell
many
k
-values such that
|L_{k}^{(1)}|=2
.
Let
a_{k}
,
b_{k}
,
A_{k}
,
B_{k}
be the indices uniquely determined so that

(4.3)

\text{either }L^{(1)}_{k}=\{(a_{k},b_{k})\}\text{ or }L^{(1)}_{k}=\{(a_{k},B_{k}),\>(A_{k},b_{k})\},

where in the second case
a_{k}<A_{k}
and
b_{k}<B_{k}
.
Note that
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
are defined only for
n-\ell
many values of
k
, and for these
k
-values,
(A_{k},B_{k})
is the location of the unique

\lrcorner

-shape in the shadow line of
L_{k}^{(1)}
.
Passing to the
r=2
iteration of Algorithm
2.2
, then, for all
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq n-\ell
there is a unique
k\geq k^{\prime}
such that

(4.4)

\{(a^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}},b^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}})\}\coloneqq L_{k^{\prime}}^{(2)}=\{(A_{k},B_{k})\}.

Thus
(a^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{1}),\ldots,(a^{\prime}_{n-\ell},b^{\prime}_{n-\ell})
are the locations of the

\lrcorner

-shapes in the shadow diagram of
\sigma^{(1)}
, and we have
a^{\prime}_{1}<\cdots<a^{\prime}_{n-\ell}
and
b^{\prime}_{1}<\cdots<b^{\prime}_{n-\ell}
.
By step
2
in Algorithm
2.2
, the unique
k
determined by
k^{\prime}
in (
4.4
) is given by taking

(4.5)

k\text{ maximal such that }a_{k+s}<a^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}+s}\text{ and }b_{k+s}<b^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}+s}\text{ for all }0\leq s\leq n-\ell-k^{\prime},

since the shadow lines of the
L^{(1)}_{k}
’s do not intersect each other.
En route to characterizing the two cases in (
4.3
), define

(4.6)

u(k)\coloneqq\big|\{k^{\prime}:a_{k}<a^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}\text{ and }b_{k}<b^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}\}\big|,

and for
1\leq t\leq u(k)
put

(4.7)

a^{\prime\prime}_{t}\coloneqq a^{\prime}_{n-\ell-u(k)+t},\qquad b^{\prime\prime}_{t}\coloneqq b^{\prime}_{n-\ell-u(k)+t}.

Thus
(a^{\prime\prime}_{1},b^{\prime\prime}_{1}),\ldots,(a^{\prime\prime}_{u(k)},b^{\prime\prime}_{u(k)})
are the locations of the

\lrcorner

-shapes in the shadow diagram of
\sigma^{(1)}
that lie strictly southeast of
(a_{k},b_{k})
, and we have
a_{k}<a^{\prime\prime}_{1}<\cdots<a^{\prime\prime}_{u(k)}
and
b_{k}<b^{\prime\prime}_{1}<\cdots<b^{\prime\prime}_{u(k)}
.
Note that
L_{k}^{(1)}=\{(a_{k},b_{k})\}
if and only if
(a^{\prime\prime}_{1},b^{\prime\prime}_{1})=(A_{K},B_{K})
for some
K>k
.
Equivalently, by (
4.5
),

(4.8)

L_{k}^{(1)}=\{(a_{k},b_{k})\}\text{ if and only if $a_{k+t}<a^{\prime\prime}_{t}$ and $b_{k+t}<b^{\prime\prime}_{t}$ for all $1\leq t\leq u(k)$}.

On the other hand, by (
4.3
)–(
4.5
), we have
L_{k}^{(1)}=\{(a_{k},B_{k}),\>(A_{k},b_{k})\}
if and only if there is some (necessarily unique)
k^{\prime}\leq k
such that
A_{k}=a^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}
and
B_{k}=b^{\prime}_{k}
;
in turn,

(4.9)

L_{k}^{(1)}=\{(a_{k},b^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}),\>(a^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}},b_{k})\}\text{ if and only if $k^{\prime}\leq k$ such that $k$ and $k^{\prime}$ satisfy~\eqref{k from k'}}.

Next we translate the preceding observations into tableaux.
Let
\operatorname{RS}(\sigma)=(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})
.
Note that in either case of (
4.3
) we have
a_{k}=\min\{i:(i,j)\in L^{(1)}_{k}\}
and
b_{k}=\min\{j:(i,j)\in L^{(1)}_{k}\}
;
likewise in (
4.4
) we have
a^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}=\min\{i:(i,j)\in L^{(2)}_{k^{\prime}}\}
and
b^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}=\min\{j:(i,j)\in L^{(2)}_{k^{\prime}}\}
.
Thus by step
4
of Algorithm
2.2
, along with (
4.7
),

(4.10)

\displaystyle a_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}_{1,k}\qquad

\displaystyle b_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Q}_{1,k}

\displaystyle a^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}_{2,k^{\prime}}\qquad

\displaystyle b^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Q}_{2,k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle a^{\prime\prime}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}_{2,n-\ell-u(k)+t}\qquad

\displaystyle b^{\prime\prime}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Q}_{2,n-\ell-u(k)+t}

for all
1\leq k\leq\ell
and
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq n-\ell
and
1\leq t\leq u(k)
.
Upon combining (
4.8
) and (
4.10
), we obtain the following for all
1\leq k\leq\ell
:

(4.11)

L^{(1)}_{k}=\{(a_{k},b_{k})\}\text{ if and only if }\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{P}_{1,k}=a_{k},\;\mathsf{Q}_{1,k}=b_{k},\\
\text{and for all $1\leq t\leq u(k)$},\\
\mathsf{P}_{1,k+t}<\mathsf{P}_{2,n-\ell-u(k)+t}\text{ and }\mathsf{Q}_{1,k+t}<\mathsf{Q}_{2,n-\ell-u(k)+t}\end{array}\right).

Likewise, upon combining (
4.9
) and (
4.10
), we obtain the following for all
1\leq k\leq\ell
:

(4.12)

L^{(1)}_{k}=\{(a_{k},b^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}),\>(a^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}},b_{k})\}\text{ if and only if }\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{P}_{1,k}=a_{k},\;\mathsf{Q}_{1,k}=b_{k},\>\mathsf{P}_{2,k^{\prime}}=a^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}},\;\mathsf{Q}_{2,k^{\prime}}=b^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}},\\
\text{$k^{\prime}\leq k$, and $k$ is maximal such that,}\\
\text{for all $0\leq s\leq n-\ell-k^{\prime}$},\\
\mathsf{P}_{1,k+s}<\mathsf{P}_{2,k^{\prime}+s}\text{ and }\mathsf{Q}_{1,k+s}<\mathsf{Q}_{2,k^{\prime}+s}\end{array}\right).

We will now use (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) to prove the
V(\ell,i,j)
and
W(\ell,i,j)
formulas, respectively, as stated in this lemma.
We begin with the
V(\ell,i,j)
formula.
By the definitions (
4.1
)–(
4.2
),

(4.13)

\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{V}}(\ell,i,j)\text{ if and only if }L_{k}^{(1)}=\{(i,j)\}\text{ for some }1\leq k\leq\ell.

In turn, by (
4.11
),

(4.14)

L_{k}^{(1)}=\{(i,j)\}\text{ if and only if }\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{P}_{1,k}=i,\;\mathsf{Q}_{1,k}=j,\\
\text{and for all $1\leq t\leq u(k)$},\\
\mathsf{P}_{1,k+t}<\mathsf{P}_{2,n-\ell-u(k)+t}\text{ and }\mathsf{Q}_{1,k+t}<\mathsf{Q}_{2,n-\ell-u(k)+t}\end{array}\right).

Combining (
4.13
) and (
4.14
), we obtain

(4.15)

\operatorname{RS}(\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{V}}(\ell,i,j))=\coprod_{k=1}^{\ell}\Big\{(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})\in{\rm SYT}(\lambda)\times{\rm SYT}(\lambda):(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})\text{ satisfies right-hand side of~\eqref{size 1 iff ij}}\Big\}.

Observe that
(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})
satisfies the right-hand side of (
4.14
) if and only if the following two conditions hold:

•

\mathsf{P}_{1,k}=i
and
\mathsf{Q}_{1,k}=j
;

•

each of the two-row arrangements below is column strict (where ellipses denote consecutive entries from a row of
\mathsf{P}
or
\mathsf{Q}
, respectively):

(4.16)

\begin{array}[]{r}\boxed{\begin{array}[]{lllllll}&&\mathsf{P}_{1,k+1}&\cdots\cdots\cdots&\mathsf{P}_{1,k+u(k)}&\cdots&\mathsf{P}_{1,\ell}\\
\mathsf{P}_{2,i-k+1}&\cdots\cdots\cdots&\mathsf{P}_{2,n-\ell-u(k)+1}&\cdots\cdots\cdots&\mathsf{P}_{2,n-\ell}&&\end{array}}\\[17.22217pt]
\boxed{\begin{array}[]{lllllll}&&\mathsf{Q}_{1,k+1}&\cdots\cdots\cdots&\mathsf{Q}_{1,k+u(k)}&\cdots&\mathsf{Q}_{1,\ell}\\
\mathsf{Q}_{2,j-k+1}&\cdots&\mathsf{Q}_{2,n-\ell-u(k)+1}&\cdots\cdots\cdots&\mathsf{Q}_{2,n-\ell}&&\end{array}}\end{array}

In each arrangement in (
4.16
), the top row consists of the last
\ell-k
entries from the top row of
\mathsf{P}
(resp.,
\mathsf{Q}
);
likewise, the bottom row ends with the last
u(k)
entries from the bottom row of
\mathsf{P}
(resp.,
\mathsf{Q}
), which are now left-justified with the top row.
By (
4.6
) we have

(4.17)

u(k)=n-\ell-\max\{i,j\}+k,

since the number of entries less than
i
(resp.,
j
) in the bottom row of
\mathsf{P}
(resp.,
\mathsf{Q}
) equals
i-k
(resp.,
j-k
);
thus by (
4.17
) the lengths of the left-hand “tails” in (
4.16
) are given by

(4.18)

\begin{split}(n-\ell-u(k)+1)-(i-k+1)&=\max\{i,j\}-i=[j-i]_{+},\\
(n-\ell-u(k)+1)-(j-k+1)&=\max\{i,j\}-j=[i-j]_{+}.\end{split}

Further observe that the entries in each arrangement in (
4.16
) are precisely the entries from
\mathsf{P}
(resp.,
\mathsf{Q}
) that are greater than
i
(resp.,
j
);
therefore the full bottom row has length
n-\ell+k-i
(resp.,
n-\ell+k-j
).
Thus by (
4.18
),

(4.19)

\begin{array}[]{l}\text{the $\mathsf{P}$ arrangement in~\eqref{subtableaux} has skew shape $(\ell-k+[j-i]_{+},\>n-\ell+k-i)/([j-i]_{+})$},\\
\text{the $\mathsf{Q}$ arrangement in~\eqref{subtableaux} has skew shape $(\ell-k+[i-j]_{+},\>n-\ell+k-j)/([i-j]_{+})$}.\end{array}

Upon subtracting
i
(resp.,
j
) from every entry, each arrangement in (
4.16
) becomes an SYT with the skew shape given in (
4.19
).
Hence starting from (
4.15
), and recalling the notation
\mathsf{P}|_{<i}
from (
2.8
), we have a bijection

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathrm{V}}:\operatorname{RS}(\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{V}}(\ell,i,j))

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\coprod_{k=1}^{\ell}\left(\!\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{r}{\rm SYT}(k\!-\!1,i\!-\!k)\\
{}\times{\rm SYT}(k\!-\!1,j\!-\!k)\end{array}\!\!\!\right)\times\left(\!\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{l}\phantom{{}\times{}}{\rm SYT}((\ell\!-\!k+\![j\!-\!i]_{+},\>n\!-\!\ell\!+\!k\!-\!i)/([j\!-\!i]_{+}))\\
{}\times{\rm SYT}((\ell\!-\!k\!+\![i\!-\!j]_{+},\>n\!-\!\ell\!+\!k\!-\!j)/([i\!-\!j]_{+}))\end{array}\!\!\right),

\displaystyle(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})

\displaystyle\longmapsto\big((\mathsf{P}|_{<i},\mathsf{Q}|_{<j}),(\mathsf{R},\mathsf{S})\big)

where
\mathsf{R}
(resp.,
\mathsf{S}
) is formed from
\mathsf{P}|_{>i}
(resp.,
\mathsf{Q}|_{>j}
) by sliding the top row to the left to obtain the alignment in (
4.16
), and then decreasing all entries by
i
(resp.,
j
).
This process is clearly invertible, so
\Phi_{\mathrm{V}}^{-1}
is well defined.
Since RS is injective, the
V(\ell,i,j)
formula follows by using Lemma
2.1
to take the cardinality of the right-hand side of (
4
).

It remains to prove the
W(\ell,i,j)
formula.
By the definitions (
4.1
)–(
4.2
),

(4.24)

\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{W}}(\ell,i,j)\text{ if and only if }L_{k}^{(1)}=\{(i,j),\>(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\}\text{ for some }1\leq k\leq\ell,

for some
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\neq(i,j)
.
Note that if
(i,j)
is northeast of
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
, then
i<j
;
to see this, observe that
i\geq j
forces at least one value
i^{\prime\prime}<i
such that
\sigma(i^{\prime\prime})\coloneqq j^{\prime\prime}>j
, but then
\sigma
would contain the decreasing subsequence
(j^{\prime\prime},j,j^{\prime})
which contradicts Greene’s theorem (
2.12
) since
\sigma
has two-row shape.
By the same argument, if
(i,j)
is southwest of
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
, then
i>j
.
Hence automatically we have
W(\ell,i,i)=0
, and moreover, we may as well assume that
i\leq j
and that
(i,j)
is northeast of
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
:

(4.25)

\text{Throughout this proof, we assume in~\eqref{doubleton} that $i\leq j$ and $i<i^{\prime}$ and $j>j^{\prime}$}.

At the end of the proof we will easily use the symmetry of Viennot’s construction to extend our result to all
(i,j)
.
By (
4.12
) and (
4.25
),

(4.26)

L_{k}^{(1)}=\{(i,j),\>(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\}\text{ if and only if }\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{P}_{1,k}=i,\;\mathsf{Q}_{1,k}=j^{\prime},\>\mathsf{P}_{2,k^{\prime}}=i^{\prime},\;\mathsf{Q}_{2,k^{\prime}}=j,\\
\text{and $k$ is maximal such that,}\\
\text{for all $0\leq s\leq n-\ell-k^{\prime}$},\\
\mathsf{P}_{1,k+s}<\mathsf{P}_{2,k^{\prime}+s}\text{ and }\mathsf{Q}_{1,k+s}<\mathsf{Q}_{2,k^{\prime}+s}\end{array}\right)

for some
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq\min\{k,\>n-\ell\}
.
Combining (
4.24
) and (
4.26
), we obtain

(4.27)

\operatorname{RS}(\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{W}}(\ell,i,j))=\coprod_{k=1}^{\ell}\;\coprod_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}\left\{(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})\in{\rm SYT}(\lambda)\times{\rm SYT}(\lambda):\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})\text{ satisfies}\\
\text{right-hand side of~\eqref{size 2 iff ij}}\\
\text{for some $i^{\prime}>i$ and $j^{\prime}<j$}\end{array}\right\}.

(Note that neither
\mathsf{P}_{2,k^{\prime}}
nor
\mathsf{Q}_{2,k^{\prime}}
exists when
k^{\prime}>n-\ell
, so in this range the corresponding set in (
4.27
) is automatically empty; hence there is no harm in letting
k^{\prime}
range all the way up to
k
.)
Observe that
(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})
satisfies the right-hand side of (
4.26
) for some
i^{\prime}>i
and
j^{\prime}<j
if and only if the following four conditions hold (where the fourth condition is required for the maximality of
k
in (
4.26
)):

•

\mathsf{P}_{1,k}=i
and
\mathsf{Q}_{2,k^{\prime}}=j
;

•

k+k^{\prime}>i
(in order to satisfy
\mathsf{P}_{1,k}<\mathsf{P}_{2,k^{\prime}}
, which is the
s=0
case in (
4.26
));

•

each of the two-row arrangements below is column strict (where ellipses denote consecutive entries from a row of
\mathsf{P}
or
\mathsf{Q}
, respectively):

(4.28)

\begin{array}[]{r}\boxed{\begin{array}[]{lllllll}&&\mathsf{P}_{1,k+1}&\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots&\mathsf{P}_{1,n-\ell+k-k^{\prime}}&\cdots&\mathsf{P}_{1,\ell}\\
\mathsf{P}_{2,i-k+1}&\cdots\cdots&\mathsf{P}_{2,k^{\prime}+1}&\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots&\mathsf{P}_{2,n-\ell}&&\end{array}}\\[17.22217pt]
\boxed{\begin{array}[]{lllllll}&&\mathsf{Q}_{1,j-k^{\prime}+1}&\cdots&\mathsf{Q}_{1,n-\ell+k-k^{\prime}}&\cdots&\mathsf{Q}_{1,\ell}\\
\mathsf{Q}_{2,k^{\prime}+1}&\cdots&\mathsf{Q}_{2,j-k+1}&\cdots&\mathsf{Q}_{2,n-\ell}&&\end{array}}\end{array}

•

it is
not
the case that both arrangements in (
4.28
) remain column-strict when the top row is shifted left by one entry.

Note that since
\mathsf{Q}_{2,k^{\prime}}=j
, we have
\mathsf{Q}_{1,1}<\cdots<\mathsf{Q}_{1,j-k^{\prime}}<j<\mathsf{Q}_{2,k^{\prime}+1}<\cdots<\mathsf{Q}_{2,n-\ell}
.
For this reason, in the top row of the
\mathsf{Q}
arrangement in (
4.28
), we suppressed the entries
\mathsf{Q}_{1,k+s}
for
s\leq j-k-k^{\prime}
, since these are precisely the top entries that are less than
j
, and therefore automatically less than the corresponding entries
\mathsf{Q}_{2,k^{\prime}+s}
, which are all greater than
j
.
We observe that the entries in each arrangement in (
4.28
) are precisely the entries from
\mathsf{P}
(resp.,
\mathsf{Q}
) that are greater than
i
(resp.,
j
).
Moreover,

(4.29)

\begin{array}[]{l}\text{the $\mathsf{P}$ arrangement in~\eqref{subtableauxW} has skew shape $(\ell+k^{\prime}-i,\>n-\ell+k-i)/(k+k^{\prime}-i)$},\\
\text{the $\mathsf{Q}$ arrangement in~\eqref{subtableauxW} has skew shape $(\ell-k^{\prime},\>n-\ell-k^{\prime})/(j-k-k^{\prime})$}.\end{array}

Now taking the skew shapes from (
4.29
), define the sets

(4.30)

\begin{split}{A}&\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\phantom{{}\times{}}{\rm SYT}\big((\ell+k^{\prime}-i,\>n-\ell+k-i)/(k+k^{\prime}-i)\big)\\[2.15277pt]
{}\times{\rm SYT}\big((\ell-k^{\prime},\>n-\ell-k^{\prime})/(j-k-k^{\prime})\big)\end{array}\right),\\[4.30554pt]
{A}^{\prime}&\coloneqq\left\{(\mathsf{R},\mathsf{S})\in{A}:\begin{array}[]{l}\text{both $\mathsf{R}$ and $\mathsf{S}$ remain column strict}\\
\text{upon sliding their top row one box to the left}\end{array}\right\},\\[4.30554pt]
{B}&\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\phantom{{}\times{}}{\rm SYT}\big((\ell+k^{\prime}-i-1,\>n-\ell+k-i)/(k+k^{\prime}-i-1)\big)\\[2.15277pt]
{}\times{\rm SYT}\big((\ell-k^{\prime}-1,\>n-\ell-k^{\prime})/(j-k-k^{\prime}-1)\big)\end{array}\right),\end{split}

and note the obvious bijection
{A}^{\prime}\longrightarrow{B}
given by sliding the top row of each tableau to the left by one box, yielding

(4.31)

\left|{A}^{\prime}\right|=\left|{B}\right|.

Hence starting from (
4.27
), we have a bijection

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathrm{W}}:\operatorname{RS}(\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{W}}(\ell,i,j))

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\coprod_{k=1}^{\ell}\>\coprod_{k^{\prime}=i-k+1}^{k}\left(\!\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{r}{\rm SYT}(k-1,\>i-k)\\
{}\times{\rm SYT}(j-k^{\prime},\>k^{\prime}-1)\end{array}\!\!\!\right)\times\big({A}\setminus{A}^{\prime}\big),

\displaystyle(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})

\displaystyle\longmapsto\big((\mathsf{P}|_{<i},\mathsf{Q}|_{<j}),(\mathsf{R},\mathsf{S}))

where
\mathsf{R}
(resp.,
\mathsf{S}
) is formed from
\mathsf{P}|_{>i}
(resp.,
\mathsf{Q}|_{>j}
) by sliding the top row to the left to obtain the alignment in (
4.28
), and then decreasing all entries by
i
(resp.,
j
).
This process is clearly invertible, so
\Phi_{\mathrm{W}}^{-1}
is well defined.
We now use Lemma
2.1
and (
4.30
)–(
4.31
) to take the cardinality of the right-hand side of (
4
):

(4.34)

W(\ell,i,j)=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\;\sum_{k^{\prime}=i-k+1}^{k}\textstyle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k-1}{i-k}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{j-k'}{k'-1}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\left[\begin{array}[]{l}\phantom{{}-{}}\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell-k \rightarrow k + k' - i}{n - \ell+ k - i}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell+ k' - j \rightarrow j - k - k'}{n - \ell- k'}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\\[8.61108pt]
{}-{}\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell-k \rightarrow k + k' - i - 1}{n - \ell+ k - i}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell+ k' - j \rightarrow j - k - k' - 1}{n - \ell- k'}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\end{array}\right].

To make the symmetry more apparent in (
4.34
), we reindex via the indices
p=k
and
q=j-k^{\prime}
, yielding

(4.35)

W(\ell,i,j)=\sum_{p=1}^{\ell}\>\sum_{q=j-p}^{p-i+j-1}\textstyle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{p-1}{i-p}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{q}{j-q-1}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\left[\begin{array}[]{l}\phantom{{}-{}}\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell-p \rightarrow j-i + p - q}{n - \ell+ p - i}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell- q \rightarrow q-p}{n - \ell+ q -j}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\\[8.61108pt]
{}-{}\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell-p \rightarrow j-i + p - q - 1}{n - \ell+ p - i}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell-q \rightarrow q-p-1}{n - \ell+ q -j}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle\end{array}\right].

We can rewrite (
4.35
) as a single sum over the set
\{(p,q)\in[\ell]\times[\ell]:p+q\geq j\text{ and }q-p<j-i\}
, but this includes many terms that vanish;
by inspecting the ballot numbers in (
4.35
) to see when their arguments become negative, we see that the effective range of the sum is the index set

{K}\coloneqq\Big\{(p,q)\in[\ell]\times[\ell]:p\leq i\text{ and }p+q\geq j\text{ and }0\leq q-p<j-i\Big\}.

Hence we arrive at the version of our
W(\ell,i,j)
formula presented in the introduction (see Theorem
1.3
), where we assumed
i\leq j
.
Indeed, we now recall our ongoing assumption (
4.25
) that
i\leq j
;
in particular, we proved then that
(i,j)
is the northeasternmost (resp., southwesternmost) of the two points on its shadow line if and only if
i<j
(resp.,
i>j
).
Hence the formula (
4.35
) is true for
i\leq j
(correctly returning zero for
i=j
), but returns zero otherwise.
Clearly the arguments in this proof are all identical for the symmetrical case where
(i,j)
is the southwesternmost of the two points on its shadow line and
i>j
;
thus one can extend (
4.35
) to all
(i,j)
by stipulating that
W(\ell,i,j)=W(\ell,j,i)
when
i>j
, as we did in the introduction.
In order to give a unified formula, however, we simply rewrite (
4.35
) to account for the possibility that
i>j
, which leads to the Iverson brackets and
[\;\;]_{+}
symbols in the statement of this lemma.
This new sum ranges over the symmetrized set

{L}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\{(p,q):(p,q)\in{K}\}&\text{if }i\leq j,\\
\{(q,p):(p,q)\in{K}\}&\text{if }i\geq j,\end{cases}

which coincides with the index set
{L}
given in the statement of this lemma;
note that there is no ambiguity between the two cases since
{K}=\varnothing
when
i=j
.
This completes the proof of Lemma
4.2
.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\lambda=(\ell,n-\ell)
be a two-row shape, and recall
P^{\lambda}_{ij}
from (
2.20
).
For all
1\leq i,j\leq n
,

P^{\lambda}_{ij}=\frac{V(\ell,i,j)+W(\ell,i,j)}{\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell}{n - \ell}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle^{2}},

where the functions
V
and
W
are those defined in Lemma
4.2
, and where
\left\langle\kern-3.20007pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a}{b}\right\rangle\kern-3.20007pt\right\rangle

2.7
).

We have
\check{P}^{\lambda}_{ij}=\left|\mathfrak{S}(\ell,i,j)\right|

4.1

,

) we include the constraint that

–

If
\ell=n/2
, then

.

•

–

If
\ell\neq n/2
, then

(4.38)

\operatorname{supp}P^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{W}}=\Big\{(i,j):1\leq|i-j|\leq\ell\Big\}.

Note that this region is just the band containing
\lambda
, and its reflection about the diagonal.

–

If
\ell=n/2
, then in (
4.38
) we include the constraint that if

5.
Main result for rectangular shapes

Throughout this section, we fix positive integers
\ell
and
m
, and we assume that
\lambda=(\ell^{m})
is a rectangular shape.
It is well known
[
11
]
*eqn. (1.1) that for rectangular shapes
\lambda
, the hook-length formula (
2.3
) reduces to the form

(5.1)

f^{\lambda}=f^{(\ell^{m})}=\left[\prod_{s=0}^{m-1}\frac{s!}{(\ell+s)!}\right](\ell m)!.

For any fixed
m\geq 2
, the terms of the sequence
f^{(1^{m})},f^{(2^{m})},f^{(3^{m})},\ldots
are called the
m
-dimensional Catalan numbers
, and the
m=2
case recovers the classical Catalan numbers; see entry A060854 in the OEIS
[
13
]
.

In the case of rectangular shapes, the RS correspondence exhibits an especially nice vertical reflection property.
Given a standard Young tableau
\mathsf{P}
, let

Before proving Theorem
1.5
, we record the following well-known binomial coefficient identities:

(6.2)

\displaystyle\textstyle\binom{a}{b}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{b}\textstyle\binom{a}{b+t}\binom{b+t}{t}\binom{b}{t}

\displaystyle\text{(see bijective argument below)},

(6.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{h=0}^{a}\textstyle\binom{h}{b}\binom{a-h}{c}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\binom{a+1}{b+c+1}

\displaystyle\text{special case of equation (5.26) in \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{GKP}{}{}]}*{p.~169}},

(6.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{p=0}^{m}\textstyle\binom{2p}{p}\binom{2m-2p}{m-p}

\displaystyle=4^{m}

\displaystyle\text{equation (5.39) in~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{GKP}{}{}]}*{p.~187}},

(6.5)

\displaystyle{\textstyle\binom{a+c}{b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{b}}{b!}+O(a^{b-1}),

(6.6)

\displaystyle{\textstyle\binom{a+c}{b}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{a^{b}}{b!}\text{ as }a\rightarrow\infty.

The identity (
6.2
) follows immediately from the following bijective argument.
In particular, the left-hand side of (
6.2
) is the number of ordered pairs
(X,Y)
of
b
-element subsets of an
a
-element set, while the right-hand side refines these ordered pairs by the quantity
t\coloneqq|X\setminus Y|=|Y\setminus X|
;
indeed, for fixed
t
, the number of such pairs
(X,Y)
is the number
\binom{a}{b+t}
of choices of
X\cup Y
, times the number
\binom{b+t}{t}
of choices of
X\setminus Y
, times the number
\binom{b}{t}
of remaining choices of
Y\setminus X
.
The fact (
6.5
) follows from the fact that
\binom{a+c}{b}=(a+c)!/(b!\cdot(a-b+c)!)
is a polynomial in
a
with leading term
a^{b}/b!
, and this in turn yields the asymptotic (
6.6
).

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

For a uniform random element
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}
, the definitions imply that the expected value of
\left|\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)\right|
equals the trace of
P^{\lambda}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\left|\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)\right|\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}\right|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}}\left|\{i:\sigma(i)=i\}\right|

(6.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}\right|}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}:\sigma(i)=i\}\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left|\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}_{ii}\right|}{\left|\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}\right|}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}P^{\lambda}_{ii}=\operatorname{tr}P^{\lambda}.

Hook case:
\lambda=(\ell,1^{m})
.
We adopt the notation of Section
3
.
Since
m=n-\ell
, note that
K(\ell,i,i)=i-(m/2)
.
Thus by Theorem
3.3
, upon converting the multiset numbers into binomial coefficients via (
2.5
), the trace of
\check{P}^{\lambda}
equals

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigg[\textstyle\binom{i-1}{m/2}^{2}\binom{n-i}{m/2}^{2}+\displaystyle\overbrace{\sum_{\mathclap{k<i-(m/2)}}\textstyle\binom{i-2}{i-k}^{2}\binom{n-i}{m-i+k}^{2}}^{F(\ell,i,i)}+\displaystyle\overbrace{\sum_{\mathclap{k<\bar{\imath}-(m/2)}}\textstyle\binom{\bar{\imath}-2}{\bar{\imath}-2}^{2}\binom{n-\bar{\imath}}{m-\bar{\imath}+k}^{2}}^{F(\ell,\bar{\imath},\bar{\imath})}+\displaystyle\overbrace{2\sum_{\mathclap{k<(m/2)+1}}\textstyle\binom{i-2}{k-2}\binom{\bar{\imath}-2}{k-2}\binom{n-i}{m+1-k}\binom{n-\bar{\imath}}{m+1-k}}^{F(\bar{\ell},i,\bar{\imath})+F(\bar{\ell},\bar{\imath},i)}\Bigg],

which equals the following upon reindexing:

(6.8)

\sum_{h=0}^{n-1}\Bigg[\overbrace{\textstyle\binom{h}{m/2}^{2}\binom{n-h-1}{m/2}^{2}}^{h=i-1}+\displaystyle\overbrace{\sum_{\mathclap{0\leq p<m/2}}\textstyle\binom{h}{m-p}^{2}\binom{n-h-2}{p}^{2}}^{\begin{subarray}{c}h=i-2,\\
p=m-i+k\end{subarray}}+\displaystyle\overbrace{\sum_{\mathclap{m/2<p\leq m}}\textstyle\binom{h}{m-p}^{2}\binom{n-h-2}{p}^{2}}^{\begin{subarray}{c}h=i-1,\\
p=\bar{\imath}-k\end{subarray}}+\displaystyle\overbrace{2\sum_{\mathclap{0\leq p<m/2}}\textstyle\binom{h-1}{p-1}\binom{n-h-2}{p-1}\binom{n-h-1}{m-p}\binom{h}{m-p}}^{\begin{subarray}{c}h=i-1,\\
p=k-1\end{subarray}}\Bigg].

Note that among the four terms in (
6.8
), term 1 vanishes if
m
is odd, and otherwise can be rewritten as follows:

term 1

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=0}^{n-1}\textstyle\binom{h}{m/2}^{2}\binom{n-h-1}{m/2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{t=0}^{m/2}{\textstyle\binom{i-1}{(m/2)+t}\binom{(m/2)+t}{t}\binom{m/2}{t}}\right)\left(\sum_{u=0}^{m/2}{\textstyle\binom{n-i}{(m/2)+u}\binom{(m/2)+u}{u}\binom{m/2}{u}}\right)

by (
6.2
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t,u=0}^{m/2}{\textstyle\binom{m/2}{t}\binom{(m/2)+t}{t}\binom{m/2}{u}\binom{(m/2)+u}{u}}\left(\sum_{h=0}^{n-1}{\textstyle\binom{i-1}{(m/2)+t}\binom{n-i}{(m/2)+u}}\right)

rearranging

\displaystyle=\sum_{t,u=0}^{m/2}{\textstyle\binom{m/2}{t}\binom{(m/2)+t}{t}\binom{m/2}{u}\binom{(m/2)+u}{u}}\cdot\textstyle\binom{n}{m+t+u+1}

by (
6.3
)

(6.9)

\displaystyle\sim{\textstyle\binom{m}{m/2}^{2}}\cdot\frac{n^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!}\text{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$}

\displaystyle\text{by~\eqref{ID asymptotic}},

where the last line is obtained by observing that
m+t+u+1
obtains its maximum if and only if
t=u=m/2
.
Next, note that terms 2 and 3 in (
6.8
) both vanish at
h=n-1
, and can be rewritten in the same way as term 1 above, upon putting
{P}\coloneqq\{0,\ldots,m\}\setminus\{m/2\}
:

term 2 + term 3

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=0}^{n-2}\>\sum_{p\in{P}}\textstyle\binom{h}{m-p}^{2}\binom{n-h-2}{p}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=0}^{n-2}\>\sum_{p\in{P}}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{m-p}{\textstyle\binom{h}{m-p+t}\binom{m-p+t}{t}\binom{m-p}{t}}\right)\left(\sum_{u=0}^{p}{\textstyle\binom{n-h-2}{p+u}\binom{p+u}{u}\binom{p}{u}}\right)

by (
6.2
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\in{P}}\sum_{t=0}^{m-p}\sum_{u=0}^{p}{\textstyle\binom{m-p}{t}\binom{m-p+t}{t}\binom{p}{u}\binom{p+u}{u}}\left(\sum_{h=0}^{n-2}{\textstyle\binom{h}{m-p+t}\binom{n-h-2}{p+u}}\right)

rearranging

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\in{P}}\sum_{t=0}^{m-p}\sum_{u=0}^{p}{\textstyle\binom{m-p}{t}\binom{m-p+t}{t}\binom{p}{u}\binom{p+u}{u}}\cdot\textstyle\binom{n-1}{m+t+u+1}

by (
6.3
)

\displaystyle\sim\left(\sum_{p\in{P}}{\textstyle\binom{2p}{p}\binom{2m-2p}{m-p}}\right)\cdot\frac{n^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!}\text{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$}

\displaystyle\text{by~\eqref{ID asymptotic}}.

Observe that if
m
is odd, then term 1 vanishes and
{P}=\{0,\ldots,m\}
, so there is no missing term in the sum over
p
.
On the other hand, if
m
is even, then the
\binom{m}{m/2}^{2}
in the term 1 asymptotic (
6.9
) is precisely the missing
p=m/2
term in the sum over
p
.
Hence in either case, in (
6.8
) as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

term 1 + term 2 + term 3

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=0}^{n-2}\>\sum_{p=0}^{m}\textstyle\binom{h}{m-p}^{2}\binom{n-h-2}{p}^{2}

(6.10)

\displaystyle\sim\left(\sum_{p=0}^{m}{\textstyle\binom{2p}{p}\binom{2m-2p}{m-p}}\right)\cdot\frac{n^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!}

(6.11)

\displaystyle=\frac{4^{m}}{(2m+1)!}\cdot n^{2m+1}

\displaystyle\text{by~\eqref{ID 4m}}.

Finally, by performing the same analysis, we find that term 4 in (
6.8
) is asymptotic to a constant multiple of
n^{2m-1}
, which can be seen immediately by observing that the sum of the four lower binomial coefficient indices equals
2m-2
, rather than
2m
(as in terms 1, 2, and 3).
Hence term 4 contributes nothing to the asymptotic behavior of (
6.8
) as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Thus by (
6.11
) we have

\eqref{big sum over h fix m}=\operatorname{tr}\check{P}^{(n-m,\>1^{m})}\sim\frac{4^{m}}{(2m+1)!}\cdot n^{2m+1}\text{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$},

and therefore since
\left|\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}\right|=(f^{\lambda})^{2}=\left(\kern-2.79999pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n-m}{m}\right)\kern-2.79999pt\right)^{2}=\binom{n-1}{m}^{2}\sim n^{2m}/(m!)^{2}
, we obtain

(6.12)

\operatorname{tr}P^{(n-m,\>1^{m})}=\frac{\operatorname{tr}\check{P}^{(n-m,\>1^{m})}}{\binom{n-1}{m}^{2}}\sim\frac{4^{m}(m!)^{2}}{(2m+1)!}\cdot n=W_{2m+1}\cdot n,

where
W_{2m+1}
is the Wallis integral in (
6.1
).
Finally, applying (
6.7
) to (
6.12
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\Big[\left|\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)\right|\Big]=\operatorname{tr}P^{(n-m,\>1^{m})}\sim W_{2m+1}\cdot n\text{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$},

and so dividing by
n
, and letting
\ell=n-m
approach infinity, we obtain

\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left|\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)\right|}{n}\right]=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left|\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)\right|}{n}\right]=W_{2m+1},

which completes the proof in the hook case.

Two-row case:
\lambda=(\ell,m)
.
We adopt the notation of Section
4
.
Recall from the proof of Lemma
4.2
that for all
1\leq i\leq n
, we have
W(\ell,i,i)=0
.
Therefore by Theorem
4.3
, the trace of
\check{P}^{\lambda}
equals
\sum_{i}V(\ell,i,i)
.
Substituting
m=\ell-n
and converting ballot numbers to binomial coefficients via (
2.6
)–(
2.7
), we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\check{P}^{\lambda}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(n-m,i,i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n-m}\textstyle\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k-1}{i-k}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle^{2}\left\langle\kern-4.49997pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n - m - k}{m + k - i}\right\rangle\kern-4.49997pt\right\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=i-m}^{i}\textstyle\left[\binom{i-1}{i-k}-\binom{i-1}{i-k-1}\right]^{2}\left[\binom{n-i}{m+k-i}-\binom{n-i}{m+k-i-1}\right]^{2}\right).

Making the substitutions
h=i-1
and
p=m+k-i
, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\check{P}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=0}^{n-1}\;\sum_{p=0}^{m}\textstyle\left[\binom{h}{m-p}-\binom{h}{m-p-1}\right]^{2}\left[\binom{n-h-1}{p}-\binom{n-i}{p-1}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=0}^{n-1}\;\sum_{p=0}^{m}\textstyle\left[\binom{h}{m-p}^{2}\binom{n-h-1}{p}^{2}+\text{lower-order terms in $n$}\right]

by (
6.5
)

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{h=0}^{n-1}\;\sum_{p=0}^{m}\textstyle\binom{h}{m-p}^{2}\binom{n-h-1}{p}^{2}\quad\text{as $n\rightarrow\infty$}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{4^{m}}{(2m+1)!}\cdot n^{2m+1}

\displaystyle\text{by~\eqref{main hook asymptotic} and~\eqref{123 asymptotic}}.

The rest of the proof is now identical to the hook case above, since in the two-row case we still have

\left|\mathfrak{S}^{\lambda}\right|=(f^{\lambda})^{2}=\textstyle\left\langle\kern-3.20007pt\left\langle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n-m}{m}\right\rangle\kern-3.20007pt\right\rangle^{2}=\left[\binom{n}{m}-\binom{n}{m-1}\right]^{2}\sim(n^{m}/m!)^{2}=n^{2m}/(m!)^{2}\text{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$},

just as in the hook case (
6.12
).
∎
