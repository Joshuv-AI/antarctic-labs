Comparative efficacy of platelet-rich plasma monotherapy versus combination therapies for knee osteoarthritis: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Dehao Luan, Xiaonan Zhang, Ziyi Zhang, Xi Man, Hai Bo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlatelet-rich plasma (PRP) is widely used as an intra-articular treatment for knee osteoarthritis (KOA), but whether combining PRP with hyaluronic acid (HA), mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), or ozone (O3) provides additional clinical benefit remains unclear. We conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) to compare the efficacy of PRP monotherapy with these three PRP-based combination strategies.MethodsTwelve electronic databases were searched from inception to April 2026 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that compared intra-articular PRP alone with PRP combined with HA, MSCs, or O3 in adults with KOA. Two reviewers independently performed study selection, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. The primary outcomes were changes in WOMAC total score and VAS pain score. A random-effects NMA was conducted within a frequentist framework in Stata 17.0. Treatment rankings were calculated using surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) values.ResultsEighteen RCTs involving 1,376 participants met the inclusion criteria. The evidence network was star-shaped, with PRP alone serving as the sole common comparator. For WOMAC function, PRP + HA was the only combination that demonstrated a statistically significant improvement over PRP alone (MD = − 6.16, 95% CI − 9.71 to − 2.61, P < 0.001). Differences were not significant for PRP + O3 (MD = − 9.65, 95% CI − 20.33 to 1.03) or PRP + MSC (MD = − 0.80, 95% CI − 12.85 to 11.25). For VAS pain, PRP + HA again showed superior pain relief (MD = − 1.01, 95% CI − 1.57 to − 0.44, P < 0.001). PRP + MSC (MD = − 0.77, 95% CI − 1.92 to 0.38) and PRP + O3 (MD = − 0.15, 95% CI − 1.87 to 1.57) did not differ significantly from PRP alone. SUCRA values ranked PRP + HA highest for VAS pain and second for WOMAC function. No significant publication bias was detected.ConclusionsAmong PRP-based combination therapies for KOA, the addition of HA to PRP is supported by the most consistent evidence for improving pain and function. Current data are insufficient to conclude that adding MSCs or O3 provides any additional benefit. Standardized, large-scale RCTs with longer follow-up are needed to clarify the role of these emerging combinations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-07042-5.Keywords: Knee osteoarthritis, Platelet-rich plasma, Hyaluronic acid, Mesenchymal stem cells, Ozone, Network meta-analysis

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a highly prevalent degenerative joint disease that constitutes a leading cause of chronic pain and disability worldwide [1]. The condition is characterized by progressive cartilage loss, subchondral bone remodeling, and synovial inflammation, which together produce persistent pain, stiffness, and functional impairment [2, 3]. With population aging, the burden of KOA continues to rise, particularly in countries such as China where prevalence among older adults exceeds 50% [4, 5].

Current non-surgical management options include oral analgesics such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and intra-articular injections of glucocorticoids or hyaluronic acid (HA) [6]. While these modalities can offer symptomatic relief, their capacity to arrest or reverse cartilage degeneration is limited, and long-term use of NSAIDs is associated with substantial gastrointestinal and cardiovascular risks [7, 8]. This therapeutic gap has driven considerable interest in biological interventions that may modify the intra-articular environment.

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is an autologous blood product that contains platelet concentrations several times higher than circulating blood [9]. Upon activation, platelets release a multitude of growth factors, including transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), as well as anti-inflammatory cytokines that can downregulate interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) [10–12]. These biological properties have made PRP an attractive option for treating KOA, and multiple randomized trials have reported superior or equivalent outcomes compared with HA or placebo [13, 14].

As PRP monotherapy has become more widely adopted, investigators have explored whether combining PRP with other intra-articular agents could produce synergistic effects. Three combination strategies have received particular attention. The first is PRP combined with HA, which pairs the immediate viscoelastic effects of HA with the sustained chondroprotective and anti-inflammatory actions of PRP growth factors. Preclinical evidence indicates that this combination more effectively suppresses cartilage degradation than either agent alone [15–18]. The second is PRP combined with mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), based on in vitro evidence that PRP enhances MSC proliferation and promotes chondrogenic differentiation [19–21]. The third is PRP combined with medical ozone (O₃), which has demonstrated anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects in animal models of arthritis through the modulation of reactive oxygen species [22–24].

Individual trials evaluating these combinations have produced conflicting results. Several studies have reported that PRP + HA yields greater improvements in pain and function than PRP alone [25, 26], while others have found no significant between-group difference [27]. Similarly, small trials of PRP + MSC have suggested potential benefits in pain reduction and cartilage preservation [28, 29], but the evidence base remains sparse and methodologically heterogeneous. The literature on PRP + O₃ is even more limited, with few controlled studies available.

Traditional pairwise meta-analyses cannot simultaneously compare multiple competing interventions, which limits their utility for determining which combination strategy, if any, should be preferred. Network meta-analysis (NMA) addresses this limitation by synthesizing both direct and indirect evidence, permitting comparative effectiveness estimates and probabilistic treatment rankings across three or more interventions that share a common comparator [30]. In the context of PRP-based combination therapies for KOA, all strategies share PRP monotherapy as the control arm, creating a star-shaped evidence network amenable to NMA.

Given the clinical relevance of the question and the conflicting signals in the literature, we undertook a systematic review and network meta-analysis to compare the efficacy of PRP alone against PRP combined with HA, PRP combined with MSCs, and PRP combined with O₃ for improving pain and function in adults with KOA.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and network meta-analysis adheres to the PRISMA extension statement for network meta-analyses (PRISMA-NMA) [31, 32]. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, registration number CRD420251118543).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they met the following PICOS criteria.

Population: Adults aged 18 years and older with a clinical or radiological diagnosis of KOA, regardless of disease severity (Kellgren-Lawrence grade I–IV). Trials that included patients with inflammatory arthritis, septic arthritis, or prior knee arthroplasty were excluded.

Intervention: Intra-articular injection of PRP combined with at least one other intra-articular agent, specifically HA, MSCs, or O₃. No restrictions were placed on PRP preparation methods (single- or double-spin centrifugation), platelet concentration, leukocyte content, activation status, or injection protocols (volume, frequency, timing).

Comparison: Intra-articular injection of PRP alone.

Outcomes: The primary outcomes were change from baseline in the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) total score and change from baseline in the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score for knee pain [33–35], both assessed at the longest available follow-up in each trial. When total WOMAC score was not reported, we used the pain or function subscale score as a proxy only if the total score could not be calculated. Secondary outcomes were prespecified but are not reported here due to insufficient data.

Study design: Randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Quasi-randomized trials, cohort studies, case–control studies, case series, and reviews were excluded. Only articles published in English or Chinese were considered, and full texts had to be available.

Search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Scopus, Springer Nature Link, EBSCO, CNKI, Wanfang, WeiPu, the Chinese Medical Journal Full-text Database, and the China Biomedical Literature Service System (CBM) from the inception of each database to April 30, 2026. No language filter was applied at the search stage. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to knee osteoarthritis, platelet-rich plasma, hyaluronic acid, stem cells, ozone, and randomized controlled trials. To identify additional studies, we also hand-searched reference lists of included articles and relevant systematic reviews. Although no language filter was applied during the database search, only articles published in English or Chinese were included in the final review. This language restriction may have introduced selection bias. The full search strategies for all databases are provided in Additional file 1.

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts of retrieved records after removing duplicates. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were obtained and independently assessed for eligibility. Disagreements at any stage were resolved by discussion or, when consensus could not be reached, by consultation with a third reviewer. All reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented.

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized, piloted form. The following information was recorded: first author, year of publication, country, study design, sample size, participant demographics (mean age, sex distribution, body mass index, Kellgren-Lawrence grade), details of PRP preparation (centrifugation protocol, platelet concentration, leukocyte classification, use of activators), injection regimen (volume per injection, number of injections, interval between injections, site of injection), details of the co-intervention (type, source, preparation), outcome measures (pre- and post-intervention means and standard deviations for WOMAC and VAS), and follow up duration.

When trials reported multiple follow-up points, we extracted data from the longest time point available, with a minimum of three months. If a study reported both an early and late outcome within the range typically considered “short term” (e.g., 6 and 12 months), we chose the later time point to represent the sustained effect of the intervention. The longest follow-up time point was selected because the durability of treatment effects is of primary clinical interest, and longer-term outcomes may better reflect the sustained benefits or limitations of combination therapies. When standard deviations of change scores were not directly available, they were calculated from pre- and post-intervention standard deviations using the formula provided in the Cochrane Handbook [36]. If only medians and interquartile ranges were reported, means and standard deviations were estimated using the method of Wan et al. [37] when sample size permitted. Authors were contacted for missing data when necessary, but no additional information was obtained for this review.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of each included RCT was assessed using the revised Cochrane risk of bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) [38]. This tool evaluates five domains: bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention), bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, and bias in selection of the reported result. Each study was classified as having a low risk of bias, some concerns, or a high risk of bias overall. Two reviewers performed the assessments independently, and disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

All network meta-analyses were conducted within a frequentist framework using the network suite of commands in Stata 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). A random-effects model was chosen a priori to account for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across trials. Treatment effects were expressed as mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for the primary treatment comparisons.

The network geometry was visualized in a network diagram, where nodes represented treatment arms weighted by the number of participants, and edges represented direct comparisons weighted by the number of studies. Because all included treatments shared PRP alone as the sole common comparator, the network was star-shaped with no closed loops. The consistency assumption, that direct and indirect evidence agree, could therefore not be formally assessed using the design-by-treatment interaction model or node-splitting methods. All estimates were derived under the assumption of transitivity, meaning that the distribution of potential effect modifiers (such as age, KOA severity, and PRP preparation method) was assumed to be balanced across the different comparisons. This is a limitation that must be considered when interpreting the results.

To rank the comparative efficacy of treatments, we calculated the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) for each outcome [39]. SUCRA values range from 0 to 1 (or 0% to 100%), with higher values indicating a greater probability that the intervention is among the best treatments.

Direct pairwise meta-analyses were also performed for each treatment comparison using DerSimonian–Laird random-effects models. Statistical heterogeneity within each direct comparison was quantified using the I2 statistic, with I2 > 50% taken to indicate substantial heterogeneity [40]. In addition, we calculated the overall I2 statistic from the network meta-analysis to quantify the proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity.

Comparison-adjusted funnel plots were constructed to evaluate potential small-study effects across the network. We further performed Egger’s linear regression test, where a P-value < 0.10 was considered suggestive of funnel plot asymmetry [41]. All analyses were conducted separately for WOMAC and VAS outcomes.

Two preplanned sensitivity analyses were attempted but could not be completed meaningfully because of the limited number of studies for the PRP + MSC and PRP + O₃ comparisons. We had intended to examine the influence of PRP leukocyte classification (leukocyte-rich versus leukocyte-poor) and the number of PRP injections (single versus multiple), but the small number of trials within each stratum prevented reliable subgroup analyses. This issue is addressed further in the Discussion.

Results

Literature search and study selection

The systematic search identified a total of 7,440 citations from the 12 databases, with an additional 17 records located through other sources. After removing 2,311 duplicates, 5,146 records were screened on the basis of title and abstract. Of these, 4,644 were excluded because they were clearly irrelevant. The full texts of the remaining 504 articles were sought for retrieval; two were excluded because the full text was available only in a language other than English or Chinese. After a detailed full-text review, 484 were excluded for the following reasons: wrong study design (e.g., reviews, conference abstracts, cohort studies), inappropriate interventions or comparators, irrelevant outcomes, duplicate publication, or inability to extract valid outcome data. Ultimately, 18 RCTs met all eligibility criteria and were included in the quantitative synthesis. The study selection process is summarized in Fig. 1.

PRISMA flow diagram of study selection

Characteristics of included studies

The 18 RCTs were published between 2014 and 2024, with more than half (10 studies, 55.6%) published from 2020 onward. Together, they enrolled 1,376 participants, with individual sample sizes ranging from 34 to 200. The mean age of participants ranged from 46.2 to 73.3 years. Body mass index (BMI), when reported, was between 21.5 and 28.9 kg/m2. The most frequent comparison was PRP alone versus PRP + HA, which was evaluated in 13 trials. Three trials compared PRP alone with PRP + MSC, one trial compared PRP alone with PRP + O₃, and one trial included both PRP + HA and PRP + MSC arms alongside a PRP-alone arm. No trial directly compared two different combination therapies against each other. Most studies used a single-spin or double-spin centrifugation method for PRP preparation, and the reported volume of PRP injected per session varied from 2 to 8 mL. The number of injections ranged from one to four, typically administered at weekly or biweekly intervals. HA was the most common co-intervention, used as a high-molecular-weight preparation in most studies, with intra-articular doses of 2–4 mL. MSCs were derived from autologous bone marrow, adipose tissue, or umbilical cord tissue, and the number of injected cells ranged widely. Details of the included trials are presented in Table 1.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias assessment for the 18 RCTs is summarized in Fig. 2. Overall, the methodological quality was moderate. The most frequent limitation was risk of bias in the measurement of the outcome, which was judged as having “some concerns” or “high risk” in the majority of trials. This was principally because outcome assessors were not blinded to treatment allocation, a common challenge in injection trials where interventions are difficult to mask. Blinding of participants was also frequently not described or was stated as infeasible due to differences in the color or consistency of the injectates. In the domain of selection of the reported result, a few studies were rated at high risk because no preregistered protocol or statistical analysis plan was available. By contrast, most trials were assessed as low risk for attrition bias, as follow-up rates were generally high and missing outcome data were balanced between groups.

Network meta-analysis results

WOMAC score

Twelve studies (1,030 patients) contributed to the WOMAC analysis. The pooled direct comparison between PRP + HA and PRP alone showed a significant advantage for the combination therapy (MD = − 6.16, 95% CI − 9.71 to − 2.61, P < 0.001). Substantial heterogeneity was present in this direct comparison (I2 = 78.4%). In the NMA, the estimated treatment effects for the other two combinations were not statistically significant: PRP + O₃ versus PRP alone (MD = − 9.65, 95% CI − 20.33 to 1.03, P = 0.076) and PRP + MSC versus PRP alone (MD = − 0.80, 95% CI − 12.85 to 11.25, P = 0.896). The point estimate for PRP + O₃ was large but accompanied by an extremely wide confidence interval, as it was informed by only a single small trial. VAS pain score

Thirteen studies (897 patients) contributed to the VAS analysis. In the direct pairwise comparison, PRP + HA demonstrated a statistically significant and clinically meaningful reduction in pain compared with PRP alone (MD = − 1.01, 95% CI − 1.57 to − 0.44, P < 0.001), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 64.1%). In the NMA, the differences were not significant for PRP + O₃ (MD = − 0.15, 95% CI − 1.87 to 1.57, P = 0.864) or PRP + MSC (MD = − 0.77, 95% CI − 1.92 to 0.38, P = 0.188) when compared with PRP alone. The point estimate for PRP + MSC favored the combination, but the 95% CI was wide and crossed the null value. The overall I2 from the network meta-analysis was approximately 60–70%, indicating that much of the variability across the network came from between-study differences.

Treatment rankings

SUCRA values for each outcome are displayed in Fig. 4. For WOMAC function, PRP + O₃ ranked first (SUCRA = 0.85), with PRP + HA (SUCRA = 0.69) second, PRP + MSC (SUCRA = 0.30) third, and PRP alone (SUCRA = 0.16) last. For VAS pain, PRP + HA had the highest probability of being the most effective intervention (SUCRA = 0.82), followed by PRP + MSC (SUCRA = 0.67). PRP + O₃ (SUCRA = 0.33) and PRP alone (SUCRA = 0.18) ranked third and fourth, respectively.

Publication bias

Comparison-adjusted funnel plots for the WOMAC and VAS outcomes did not exhibit marked asymmetry upon visual inspection (Fig. 5). Egger’s linear regression test results were consistent with no significant small-study effects: for WOMAC, t = 2.04, df = 10, P = 0.07; for VAS, t = − 0.86, df = 11, P = 0.41. These results must be interpreted with caution, given the relatively small number of studies in the network, which limits the power of funnel plot–based methods.

Discussion

Summary of principal findings

This systematic review and network meta-analysis of 18 RCTs, encompassing 1,376 participants, compared PRP monotherapy with three distinct PRP-based combination strategies for treating KOA. The analyses yielded two main observations. First, the addition of HA to PRP consistently and significantly improved both functional outcomes (WOMAC) and pain (VAS) compared with PRP alone, with effect estimates that are likely to be clinically meaningful. Second, neither the addition of MSCs nor O₃ achieved statistically significant superiority over PRP alone, and the effect estimates for these comparisons were accompanied by wide confidence intervals, indicating imprecise and uncertain treatment effects.

Interpretation of the PRP + HA finding

The superiority of PRP + HA over PRP alone is biologically plausible and clinically important. HA provides immediate mechanical benefits: by restoring synovial fluid viscosity and elasticity, it improves joint lubrication and attenuates nociceptive input from mechanically stressed tissues [56, 57]. In contrast, the effects of PRP are delayed and sustained, mediated by the gradual release of growth factors that suppress inflammatory pathways (e.g., inhibition of NF-κB signaling and reduction of IL-1β and TNF-α), promote chondrocyte anabolic activity, and potentially enhance matrix synthesis [58]. The combination therefore pairs a fast-acting mechanical effect with a slow-acting biological one, which likely explains the consistent benefit seen in both outcome domains. It should be noted that much of this mechanistic evidence derives from in vitro and animal studies rather than clinical trials in KOA populations, and direct extrapolation of these findings to clinical settings requires further validation.

Clinically, the magnitude of the difference we observed, approximately 1 point on the VAS and 6 points on the WOMAC total score, exceeds or approaches the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds reported for KOA. Meta-analyses of intra-articular HA and PRP have generally considered VAS improvements of 0.5–1.0 points and WOMAC improvements of 6–8 points as clinically meaningful. The results therefore suggest that, for clinicians who have already decided to use PRP for a patient with KOA, the addition of HA to the treatment regimen is a rational choice supported by moderate-quality evidence.

The uncertain role of MSC and O₃ add-on therapy

Despite the biological rationale for combining PRP with MSCs, our analysis did not detect a statistically significant benefit for this strategy. The SUCRA ranking for VAS placed PRP + MSC second behind PRP + HA, which might be interpreted as a signal of potential efficacy [59], but the direct treatment estimate was associated with a wide confidence interval that included the null. Several factors could account for this discrepancy.

First, the included MSC studies were heterogeneous in almost every respect. MSCs were harvested from different tissues, including bone marrow, adipose tissue, and umbilical cord, which are known to differ in proliferative capacity, differentiation potential, and secretory profiles [60]. Culture techniques, passage numbers, and cell doses also varied substantially. Second, the purported mechanism of action of MSCs in KOA is primarily paracrine, involving the secretion of extracellular vesicles, cytokines, and growth factors that exert immunomodulatory and anti-fibrotic effects [20, 61]. These downstream effects may evolve over months and could require follow-up periods of 24 months or longer to become fully manifest, yet almost all included RCTs reported outcomes at 6 or 12 months. As with the evidence for PRP + HA, much of the mechanistic understanding of MSC therapy in KOA is derived from preclinical studies, and the clinical relevance of these mechanisms remains to be established. Third, the small number of trials (only three studies with a PRP + MSC arm) means the analysis was underpowered to detect anything but very large treatment effects.

The evidence for PRP + O₃ is even weaker. Only one RCT contributed data to this comparison, and the resulting treatment estimates were extremely imprecise. While theoretical arguments regarding the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of ozone are plausible [62], the clinical evidence base is currently too thin to support any conclusions. In this context, the high SUCRA ranking of PRP + O₃ for WOMAC must be interpreted with caution: it reflects the large point estimate from a single small study, not consistent evidence from multiple trials.

Comparison with other systematic reviews

Our findings for PRP + HA are broadly consistent with the results of pairwise meta-analyses that have specifically compared this combination against PRP alone. For instance, a recent meta-analysis focusing on PRP and HA co-administration reported that combined treatment provided superior pain relief compared with PRP alone at six- and twelve-month follow-ups [63]. The network meta-analytic framework extends these findings by simultaneously incorporating all available direct evidence and by providing relative rankings that can inform treatment selection when multiple options are under consideration.

More generally, the heterogeneity observed in our analysis is consistent with other studies showing that the clinical effects of PRP depend heavily on preparation and administration protocols. A recent meta-analysis of microfracture surgery with PRP augmentation for knee cartilage lesions found that variation in PRP processing, including centrifugation speed, number of spins, use of activators, and injection timing, was a major source of inconsistency in pooled estimates [64]. The same issue likely affects the KOA injection literature, making it harder to detect genuine treatment effects, particularly for novel combinations whose signal may be modest. Until standardized reporting of PRP characteristics becomes the norm, substantial inter-study heterogeneity will remain an unavoidable limitation of syntheses in this field.

Strengths

This review has several notable strengths. First, it is the most comprehensive network meta-analysis to date comparing PRP alone with multiple combination therapies for KOA. The NMA framework provides not only comparative effect estimates but also probabilistic treatment rankings, which are clinically more intuitive than separate pairwise comparisons. Second, the search strategy was broad, covering 12 databases and including both English and Chinese literature, thereby minimizing the risk of language or regional bias. Third, we focused exclusively on RCTs categorized as having at least “some concerns” on the RoB 2 assessment, ensuring that the evidence base, although imperfect, was of a relatively high standard. Fourth, we extracted and analyzed data on two complementary outcome domains, pain and function, both of which are patient-centered endpoints directly relevant to clinical decision-making.

Limitations

Several important limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting these results.

Limited data for non-HA comparisons. The most significant limitation is the small number of studies for PRP + MSC and PRP + O₃. For PRP + MSC, three RCTs provided direct comparisons against PRP alone, but the pooled sample size remained modest. For PRP + O₃, only one small trial was available. These sample size constraints resulted in wide confidence intervals and low statistical power, meaning the absence of statistical significance cannot be interpreted as evidence of no effect. Larger, adequately powered trials are required before any claims of equivalence or inferiority can be made.

Clinical and methodological heterogeneity. There was marked variability across studies in patient baseline characteristics (age range, KOA severity as reflected by Kellgren-Lawrence grade distribution), PRP preparation protocols (single-spin vs. double-spin centrifugation, platelet concentration, leukocyte content), and injection regimens (number of injections, interval, and volume). Although we planned to explore these variables through subgroup analyses and meta-regression, the limited number of studies per comparison made these analyses infeasible. The high I2 values observed in the direct comparisons for PRP + HA confirm that heterogeneity was substantial, and its sources remain unquantified in this analysis. Comparable heterogeneity has been noted in other syntheses of PRP interventions, including a recent meta-analysis that found inconsistent benefit of PRP augmentation over microfracture alone, driven partly by differences in PRP formulation [64]. The unrestricted inclusion of diverse PRP preparation methods, including variations in centrifugation protocols, platelet concentration, and leukocyte content, may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity and should be considered when interpreting the pooled estimates.

Absence of closed loops and inconsistency assessment. Because the evidence network was star-shaped, lacking any closed loops of evidence, formal inconsistency testing could not be performed. The validity of NMA treatment rankings in this situation depends on the untestable assumption of transitivity, that the distribution of effect modifiers is balanced across studies with different comparators. Given the heterogeneity documented above, this assumption may not hold, and our estimates should be viewed as approximations rather than definitive comparative effectiveness measures. This limitation is inherent to the current evidence structure and can only be overcome by future trials that include direct head-to-head comparisons among the combination therapies.

Outcome measurement and time points. We used the longest available follow-up in each trial, which ranged from 3 to 24 months, with most trials reporting outcomes at 6 or 12 months. Combining results from different time points could introduce additional heterogeneity, especially if the relative efficacy of treatments changes over time. Selecting only the longest follow-up may favor treatments with delayed or cumulative effects and limit comparability with studies that reported only shorter follow-up durations. Also, the symptomatic benefit of HA may peak at 8–12 weeks, whereas PRP effects appear to increase and plateau around 6–12 months [65, 66]. The time point of outcome assessment may therefore be an effect modifier, but sparse data prevented us from performing a dose–response or time-course analysis.

Lack of structural outcome data. This review focused exclusively on patient-reported outcomes. Radiographic or MRI-based structural endpoints, such as joint space narrowing, cartilage thickness, or bone marrow lesion volume, were inconsistently reported and could not be synthesized. Consequently, the analysis speaks only to symptomatic efficacy and does not provide evidence regarding disease modification.

Collectively, these limitations, including small sample sizes for the non-HA comparisons, short follow-up durations in most trials, and substantial heterogeneity in both PRP preparation and MSC sourcing, limit the reliability and generalizability of the observed treatment effects. The findings, particularly for the PRP + MSC and PRP + O₃ comparisons, should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Implications for clinical practice and future research

From a clinical standpoint, the results of this NMA support the use of PRP + HA rather than PRP alone when the goal is to maximize short- to medium-term relief of pain and improvement of function in patients with KOA. This recommendation is based on consistent, statistically significant findings from multiple RCTs and is supported by a biologically coherent mechanistic rationale. It does not imply that PRP alone is ineffective; rather, the data suggest that the addition of HA incrementally improves outcomes.

For PRP + MSC and PRP + O₃, the evidence is insufficient to support routine clinical use outside a research setting. Clinicians who choose to offer these therapies should do so only in the context of ethically approved clinical trials with comprehensive informed consent that acknowledges the uncertainty regarding efficacy.

Several specific research priorities emerge from this review. First, large multicenter RCTs comparing PRP alone with PRP + MSC are urgently needed. Such trials should standardize MSC source and preparation, specify a minimum cell dose, and include follow-up periods of at least 24 months to capture the potential disease-modifying effects of MSC therapy. Second, adequately powered trials of PRP + O₃ are required before this combination can be deemed worth further investigation. Third, the research community must adopt minimum reporting standards for PRP preparation, as recommended by the Platelet-Rich Plasma Standardization Initiative, to reduce avoidable heterogeneity and facilitate future pooled analyses. Finally, future trials across all KOA injection therapies should consistently report both patient-reported outcomes and at least one imaging-based measure of structural progression, so that the relationship between symptomatic improvement and disease modification can be explored.

Conclusions

This systematic review and network meta-analysis of 18 RCTs involving 1,376 patients found that the combination of intra-articular PRP and HA is more effective than PRP alone for improving pain and function in adults with knee osteoarthritis. The addition of MSCs or O₃ did not yield statistically significant additional benefits, and the available evidence for these combinations is too limited to support their use in routine practice. High-quality, adequately powered, and methodologically standardized RCTs with longer follow-up are essential to determine the role of emerging PRP-based combination therapies and to guide evidence-based treatment selection for this common and disabling condition.
