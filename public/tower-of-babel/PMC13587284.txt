Molecular detection of extended-spectrum β-Lactamase genes in multi-drug-resistant bacteria isolated from drinking water supply and surface water systems in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia
Authors: Getachew Gezehagn Kussia, Bayable Atnafu Kassa, Tesfaye Admassu Abate, Tesfaye Sisay Tessema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe emergence of β-lactamase-producing bacteria is a critical global public health threat that undermines the efficacy of last-resort antibiotics. In Ethiopia, studies exploring resistance genes in drinking water systems are scarce. This study investigated bacterial profiles, antimicrobial susceptibility, and associated Extended-Spectrum β-Lactamase (ESBL) genes, including blaTEM, blaCTXM and blaSHV.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between March 2023 and April 2024 by sampling raw, finished, reservoir, tap, and storage tank water in Addis Ababa. The samples were processed aseptically with routine microbiological culture and characterization methods. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was conducted using standardized Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method as described by Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) version 33 guidelines. All data recorded were validated and then analyzed by Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25.0 Armonk. The associated parameters were compared using Fishers’ exact test to determine their correlation. In all cases, a p-value less than or equal to 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsFrom 45 samples, 94 bacterial species were characterized. Dominant isolates included E. coli (23.4%), Enterobacter spp. (16%), Citrobacter spp. (10.6%), K. pneumoniae (10.6%), Proteus spp. (8.5%), and Coagulase negative Staphylococcus species. Multi-drug resistant (MDR) is a non-susceptibility of an isolate to at least one antibiotic agent in three or more antimicrobial classes. Disc diffusion tests revealed that 63.8% (60/94) of isolates were MDR. The highest resistance levels were observed for ampicillin (86%), tetracycline (72.7%), cefuroxime (66.2%), and cefoxitin (53.5%). The principal MDR isolates were E. coli, Enterobacter, and Klebsiella species. Multiplex PCR on genomic DNA of MDR isolates showed a 26.6% (16/60) occurrence of ESBL genes; the combination of blaCTXM + blaTEM 11.7% (7/60) being the most frequent, followed by blaTEM (10%), blaCTXM (3.3%), and blaSHV (1.7%). In plasmid DNA of Gram-negative MDR isolates, ESBL genes occurred in 10.9% (6/54), with blaTEM (7.3%) being dominant, followed by blaCTXM (0.18%) and co-occurring blaSHV + blaTEM (0.18%).ConclusionThis study confirms the presence of ESBL genes in both the genomic and plasmid DNA of MDR bacteria in the water supply, particularly in storage tanks, tap and raw water. These findings indicate the transmission of β-lactamase-producing bacteria into drinking water supply systems. Regular monitoring, surveillance, and rigorous studies to identify contamination sources and mitigation strategies are urgently recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05314-y.Keywords: Bacteria, Drinking water, ESBLs genes, Multidrug resistant, Surface water

Introduction

Contamination of the natural environment, both the terrestrial and aquatic systems, impacts all life in the biosphere, disrupting the normal functioning of the ecosystem and altering natural processes [1]. Pollutants can be sourced from either natural phenomena (wildfires, dust storms, volcanoes, and decaying plants) or anthropogenic activities (industrial wastes, storm runoff, hospital discharges, and animal husbandry practices) [2]. Emerging contaminants, including pesticides, antimicrobial residues, resistant microbes and their genes, disinfectants, nanomaterials, and various dissolved and suspended wastes, are also carried into the natural ecosystem [3] and negatively impact vital environmental services, including the delivery of clean water, air, and food. Water sourced from both surface and groundwater systems is important for health, well-being, and societal and economic balance. However, approximately 2.1 billion people in the world do not have access to safely managed freshwater services, including 302 million people who rely on unimproved sources and 106 million people who rely on untreated surface water sources [4]. This impacts the attainment of Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 6.1, which targets ensuring universal and equitable access to safe drinking water by 2030. The challenge is doubled for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including those in Sub-Saharan Africa, where clean freshwater is still a scarce resource. Unsafe drinking water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) services increase the impact of waterborne diseases, which were attributed to approximately 1.4 million deaths globally, based on a 2019 report by the World Health Organization (WHO). Hence, protecting surface water quality is crucial to the well-being of the societies relying on it.

Antimicrobial resistance results from complex interacting networks linking clinical factors and environmental components, which comprise the natural phenomenon leading to genetic changes in particular microbial communities. In bacteria it primarily favors the emergence of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) [5–7], an event aggravated by human anthropogenic activities spanning abuse of antimicrobials in humans, animals, and agriculture [6, 8]. As a result, antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) are ubiquitous in environmental compartments, including water bodies (wastewater and surface water), soil, and industrial, clinical, and pharmaceutical effluents. Diverse literature across the world reports both Enterobacterales [9–13], and non-enterobacterales related [9, 14, 15] ARB in freshwater sources, implying the possible dissemination of drug-resistant strains into the drinking water supply system [12]. This signals public health risks, as resistance traits indicate a decline in the therapeutic efficacy of clinical antibiotics [16, 17].

Thus, AMR is included among the top 10 urgent global threats to public health and socioeconomic development [18], as the 700,000 deaths occurring each year are linked to it [19], a figure expected to rise significantly over the coming decades if no action is taken. This will make AMR a leading cause of death globally, potentially rising to 10 million annual deaths with a simultaneous cumulative economic loss of 100 trillion USD by 2050 [20, 21]. In Ethiopia, AMR is an emerging healthcare challenge, as the prevalence of MDR bacteria has reached as high as 70% [22]. Though Ethiopia has launched a stewardship program that principally surveys antimicrobial practices across all hospitals [23], it does not address the environmental aspect. Consequently, different studies on AMR prevalence are skewed toward samples from clinical environments, with limited focus on non-clinical samples, including freshwater sources. Additionally, several studies have demonstrated the poor bacteriological quality of the drinking water system in different parts of the country; however, reports on antibiotic resistance patterns and their linked resistance genes are sparse in those related studies [24–26]. Bacteriological testing of drinking water quality focuses only on indicator organisms, with no specific goal to address prevailing ARGs. Hence, it cannot reveal the overall safety of the water.

The presence of ARGs (for example marA, ermC, amp, QEP, and qEmarA) in bacterial strains such as E. coli isolated from the drinking water supply system may promote horizontal gene transfer (HGT) in the gut bacterial microbiome and result in asymptomatic colonization of the gut with resistant strains [27]. A prevalence of β-lactamase-producing E. coli ranging from 16.5% to 21.1% has been reported in healthy individuals at the community level [28], raising an alarm regarding the global spread of β-lactamase-mediated resistance via water systems [29]. Bacteria acquire ARGs via HGT mechanisms including conjugation, transduction, and transformation [30], where both chromosomal and plasmid-mediated distribution occur involving multiple mobile genetic elements (MGEs) such as plasmids, transposons, gene cassettes, and integrons [31–33]. Understanding these transmission pathways is crucial for water quality management and effective control strategies. In Ethiopia, phenotypic studies accompanied by molecular analysis have reported a high prevalence of β-lactamase genes among clinical isolates [34, 35]. However, there is no comprehensive report on the molecular distribution of various ESBL genes in both clinical and non-clinical settings, necessitating detailed investigations to provide crucial insights for scientific solutions. In Addis Ababa, a few studies report antibiotic resistance in drinking water, but papers characterizing ESBL producers phenotypically or molecularly are scarce [25]. Poor plumbing, weak waste management, inadequate regulation, and excessive antibiotic usage may amplify the distribution of antibiotic residues and corresponding ARGs through tap water. Local water treatment plants rely on conventional treatment technologies that have a limited capacity to remove all antibiotic residues, resistant bacteria, and their genes, thereby allowing these contaminants to survive treatment pathways and enter distribution networks [36]. This study, therefore, investigates the occurrence of ARB across drinking water supply systems and identifies chromosomal and plasmid-mediated β-lactamase genes using multiplex PCR to inform policymakers and support the development of effective preventive measures.

Materials and methods

Description of study area

This study was conducted in Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia, harboring population over 5.9 million. As the headquarters of the African Union and various international institutions, the city serves as a major hub for global visitors and diplomats. The raw water supply system for Addis Ababa’s domestic and industrial needs relies on both groundwater and surface water. Groundwater is sourced from the Akaki well field (located 22 km south of the city) alongside various internal and nearby springs and wells. Surface water is supplied by two main facilities: the Legedadi Drinking Water Treatment Plant (LDWTP), located 25 km east, and the Gefersa Drinking Water Treatment Plant (GDWTP), located 18 km west. Two major rivers flow through the heart of the city: the Little Akaki and the Big Akaki. Only the Big Akaki River serves as the source for the city’s drinking water system. LDWTP receives water from the Legedadi and Dire reservoirs within the Big Akaki river basin. These reservoirs are filled via runoff from the Akaki catchment area during the major rainy season (June to September) and the minor rainy season (mid-February to April). The Legedadi reservoir also receives water from the Kebena River, which originates from Entoto Mountain. Conversely, GDWTP sources its water from the Gefersa dam, which is fed primarily by local rainfall and watershed runoff. The Big Akaki River, along with its main tributaries (Kebena and Bulbula), passes through the city’s residential and commercial areas. The Little Akaki River passes through industrial zone flows downstream of the Gefersa reservoir, merges with the Gefersa effluent, and eventually joins the Big Akaki River. Together, they discharge into the Aba-Samuel reservoir, forming the largest tributary of the Awash River. Both rivers play significant socio-economic role as numerous industries, laboratories, health centers, and farms are situated along their banks. LDWTP is the larger plant possessing a capacity of 195,000 m³/day, and delivering approximately 47% of the city’s daily water need. Its distribution line connects to multiple residential service reservoirs including Kotebe, Karalo, Ankorcha, Jan Meda, Gebrial Palace, Teferi Mekonnen, Entoto, Belay Zeleke, Police Hospital, Army Hospital and Kasa Gebre supported by two pumping stations at Ureal and Mexico Square. GDWTP is the smaller with a capacity of 30,000 m3/day, providing 7% of the city’s distributed water [37, 38]. Its distribution line connects residential service reservoirs including Rufael, Saint Paul, and Ras Hailu Meda, thereby supplying water to the community of the surrounding vicinity. Both treatment plants rely on conventional treatment techniques: pre-chlorination, coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, sand filtration, and post-chlorination to maintain an average chlorine residual of 0.8 mg/l in the distribution system [36].

Inclusion and exclusion criterion

The inclusion criteria for this study consists raw water (sourced from dams and traced to treatment plants), finished water from treatment plants, service reservoirs, tap water, and public restaurant storage containers. Equally, the exclusion criteria included all forms of human and animal samples, water samples from household and institutional storage containers, and any samples sourced from groundwater systems.

Statement of consent and permission

Before sample collection, verbal consent was obtained from all household owners. They were informed about the aim and significance of the study, its no-risk nature, the types of samples to be collected, and the processing procedures. Participation was entirely voluntary, and the right of the owners to decline participation was fully respected.

Study design and sampling points

This cross-sectional study (March 2023 to April 2024) investigated antibiotic-resistant bacteria in Addis Ababa’s drinking water supply and surface water systems. Using a purposive sampling strategy, 45 water samples were collected along the Legedadi and Gefersa distribution lines spanning from source to tap across representative sub-cities (Arada, Gullele, Yeka, Addis Ketema, and Bole) following Standard Methods [39] and the WHO guidelines [40] (Fig. 1). The 45 samples comprised: raw source water from both Gefersa and Legedadi dams (n = 2); main service reservoirs (before distribution n = 2); residential service reservoirs (before distribution, n = 4); households tap water (n = 17 from Legedadi line and 8 from the Gefersa line); and Public restaurant storage tanks (n = 12, 4 from the Gefersa line and 8 from the Legedadi line) (Annex i). Pre-sampling observations around both treatment plants identified potential contamination sources, notably the presence of domestic animals and fecal dumping.

Sample collection and processing

All water sampling and preservation procedures followed Standard Methods [39], and WHO guidelines [40]. Approximately 300 mL of water was collected from each unit in sterile bottles, leaving a small airspace for shaking [41]. For quality control, a Falcon tube with 20 mL of autoclaved distilled water was carried and analyzed alongside field samples. Raw water was collected from treatment plant dams by immersion at 15–20 cm depth into sterile bottles containing 3–5 drops of 0.1% sodium azide. For chlorinated samples, two drops of 10% sodium thiosulfate were used. Tap tips were cleaned and flushed for 10 min before gentle sample collection. Duplicate samples from each site were labeled, stored in an ice box, and transported to the Health Biotechnology laboratory at Addis Ababa University for composite analysis. Bacteriological analysis was conducted within 6 to 10 h of collection to prevent unpredictable microbial population changes per WHO recommendations [40].

Isolation of bacteria from samples

Standard microbiological protocols were followed based on the presumed bacterial load. For raw water, the spread plate method was used: a tenfold serial dilution (10⁻² to 10⁻¹⁰), was performed in peptone water, and 0.1 mL aliquots were spread onto sheep blood agar. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, morphologically distinct, countable colonies from the 10⁻⁶ dilution were re-streaked onto the same medium. Resulting pure colonies underwent 3% KOH testing and Gram staining for differentiation before further purification. For chlorinated water, 100mL samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm cellulose acetate membrane using filtration apparatus. The membrane filters were placed on tryptic soya agar (TSA) and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24–48 h. Grown isolates were subcultured on TSA, and pure isolates tested for 3% KOH Gram reaction. All Gram-negative isolates were streaked onto MacConkey agar, and Gram-positive isolates onto Mannitol salt agar, then incubated overnight at 37 °C. For Salmonella and Shigella isolation, resuscitation steps preceded TSA inoculation. For Salmonella, the membrane filters from a filtered 100 mL sample were incubated in 20 mL buffered peptone water at 37 °C for 24 h; a 0.1 mL enriched aliquot was then transferred into 5 mL selenite F broth for another 24-hour incubation. A loopful enriched culture was then streaked onto Salmonella-Shigella (SS) agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Presumptive colorless colonies with black centers were subcultured onto MacConkey agar, then TSA, and confirmed via biochemical tests. For Shigella, the membrane filters from a filtered 100 mL sample were incubated in 20 mL selenite F broth at 37 °C for 24 h. Aliquots of enriched culture were then streaked onto xylose lysine deoxycholate (XLD) agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Presumptive red colonies without black centers were subcultured onto MacConkey agar, then TSA, and confirmed biochemically.

Identification of bacterial isolates

MacConkey agar differentiated Gram-negative isolates by lactose fermentation, followed by oxidase, catalase, and oxidative-fermentative tests. Isolates were analyzed for colony morphology, opacity, pigmentation, and consistency. Standard biochemical characterization included Gram staining, IMViC, hydrogen sulfide production, motility, Triple Sugar Iron, and urea hydrolysis (Additional file 1 Spreadsheet 1). Presumptive colonies were cultured on selective media: Eosin Methylene Blue (EMB) agar, SS agar, and Pseudomonas agars (HiMedia) for morphological identification. Gram-positive isolates were tested for mannitol fermentation on Mannitol Salt Agar and further differentiated using sheep blood agar hemolysis, alongside oxidative-fermentative, catalase, oxidase, and coagulase tests (Additional file 1 Spreadsheet 2). Results were compared with Bergey’s Manual of Systematic Bacteriology for genus and species identification. Finally, pure colonies were subcultured on Trypticase Soy Agar, inoculated into Tryptic Soy Broth, and 1 mL of the broth culture was transferred into a cryovial tube containing 1 mL of 50% glycerol for storage at -20 °C.

Quality control

Before the study, all reagents and media were verified for proper functioning. Media sterility was checked by incubating negative control plates with sterile distilled water overnight at 37 °C to rule out laboratory contamination. Biochemical tests were verified using reference strains E. coli ATCC 25,922, K. pneumoniae ATCC 700,603, and S. aureus ATCC 25,923. For antibiotic susceptibility testing, E. coli (ATCC 25922) and S. aureus (ATCC 25923) were used as quality control organisms to validate disk diffusion efficiency as per CLSI guidelines. The same protocol was followed consistently throughout the study.

Antibiotic Susceptibility Testing

Antibiotic susceptibility testing was conducted on 94 isolated strains (Additional file 1 Spreadsheet 3) using the standardized single-replicate disc diffusion (Kirby-Bauer) method following CLSI Version 33 guidelines. Pure colonies cultured on nutrient agar at 37 °C for 24 h were suspended in sterile normal saline to obtain turbidity equivalent to 0.5 McFarland standard. Sterile swabs were dipped into the standardized suspension, rotated against inner wall of tube and spread evenly over the entire surface of Mueller-Hinton agar plates in three directions at 90°. Susceptibility profiling was conducted for 16 clinically relevant antibiotics most commonly used in Ethiopian human and veterinary medicine, as well as against WHO priority pathogens (Table 1). Gram-negative bacteria were tested against 13 antibiotics: tetracycline (30 µg), cefuroxime (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), cefoxitin (30 µg), ampicillin (10 µg), meropenem (10 µg), doripenem (10 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), kanamycin (30 µg), sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (23.75/1.25 µg), and aztreonam (30 µg). Gram-positive bacteria were tested against 10 discs (Oxoid): clindamycin (2 µg), sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (23.75/1.25 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), tetracycline (30 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), kanamycin (30 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), oxacillin (1 µg), ampicillin (10 µg), and cefoxitin (30 µg) as per recommendation for a particular bacterial genus (Annex ii–iv). E. coli (ATCC 25922) and S. aureus (ATCC 25923) were used as quality control strains. After 18 h of incubation at 37 °C, inhibition zones were measured and interpreted as resistant (R), intermediate (I), or susceptible (S) per CLSI criteria. Multidrug resistance was defined according to Magiorakos et al. (2012) as non-susceptibility to at least one antibiotic agent in three or more antimicrobial classes [42]. Only MDR isolates were selected for subsequent molecular investigations.

Extraction of genomic and plasmid DNA from the bacterial isolates

Genomic DNA was extracted using the standard boiling method [43]. Briefly, 2000 µL of an overnight TSB bacterial culture (incubated at 37 °C) was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was washed by resuspending it in 200 µL of nuclease-free water and centrifuging at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The pellet was then resuspended in 150 µL of TE buffer, boiled at 100 °C for 10 min, and cooled on ice for 3 min. Following a final centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 1 min, the DNA-containing supernatant was collected and stored at -20 °C. Concentration and purity were verified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer.

For plasmid DNA extraction, selected single bacterial colonies were grown overnight in TSB under aerobic conditions. Plasmid DNA was isolated from characterized MDR Gram-negative isolates using the mini alkaline lysis method [44]. Briefly, 2 mL of overnight culture was centrifuged at 20,000 rpm for 1 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of ice-cold Solution (I50 mM glucose, 25 mM Tris-Cl [pH 8.0], and 10 mM EDTA [pH 8.0]), vortexed, and mixed gently with 200 µL of freshly prepared Solution II (0.2 N NaOH, 1% [w/v] SDS). Next, 150 µL of Solution III (3 M potassium acetate) was added, incubated on ice for 5 min, and centrifuged at 20,000 rpm for 10 min. To precipitate the DNA, 400 µL of the supernatant was mixed with 900 µL of 95% ethanol, incubated at -20 °C for 30 min, and centrifuged at 20,000 rpm for 30 min. The pellet was then washed with 1000 µL of 70% ethanol, centrifuged at 20,000 rpm for 3 min, and air-dried for 10 min at room temperature. Finally, the DNA was dissolved in 50 µL of TE buffer (pH 8.0), quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and stored at -20 °C.

Molecular detection of ESBLs genes in MDR bacterial isolates

A multiplex PCR assay was conducted to detect ESBL gene variants in genomic and plasmid DNA from MDR isolates using specific primers (Table 2) purchased from Macrogen Europe. The 25 µL reaction mixture, contained 0.4 µM of blaCTXM, 0.48 µM of blaSHV, and 0.08 µM blaTEM primers, 1× FIREPol buffer, 2 mM MgCl₂, 0.25 mM dNTPs, 0.04 U FIREPol® DNA polymerase, and 2 µL of genomic DNA (3 µL for plasmid DNA), with nuclease-free water added to adjust the final volume. Thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 4 min; 35 cycles of 94 °C for 25 s, 58 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 60 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min [45]. Amplified products were resolved on a 2% agarose gel in 1× TAE buffer at 120 V for 1 h and 20 min, then visualized using a gel documentation system. For the negative control, all PCR reagents except the DNA template were included in the reaction mixture.

Data analysis

Sample data (source, type, load, resistance, and ESBL genes) were recorded in Excel 2013 and analyzed using IBM SPSS Version 25.0. Descriptive statistics, frequencies, and cross-tabulations determined bacterial load and susceptibility proportions, visualized via tables and figures. Parameter associations were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than or equal to 0.05.

Results

Bacterial species isolated from sampling points

This study investigated a total of 45 water samples collected from different sampling points, with a positive culture growth rate of 71.1% (32/45) (Annex i). The sampling points included inlets of treatment plants (raw water), finished water from treatment plants, residential reservoirs, household tap water, and storage tank water from various public restaurants (Fig. 2). A total of 94 bacterial isolates were recovered, comprising both Gram-positive and Gram-negative species. Samples from raw water (n = 2) and storage tanks (n = 12) showed 100% growth, followed by tap water (68%, 17/25) and residential reservoirs (25%, 1/4) (Fig. 2); the differences were statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test = 13.78, p = 0.002, Cramer’s V = 0.57). These findings may be attributed to inefficient water management, inadequate cleaning, poor handling of storage containers, and/or prolonged storage periods, all of which can increase the risk of water contamination. Among the recovered isolates, E. coli was the dominant species, accounting for 23.4% (22/94), followed by Enterobacter spp. (16%, 15/94), Citrobacter spp. (10.6%, 10/94), K. pneumoniae (10.6%, 10/94), Proteus spp. (8.5%, 8/94), Coagulase negative Staphylococcus species (8.5%, 8/94) and P. aeruginosa (6.4%, 6/94) (Table 3). The majority of E. coli isolates were recovered from tap water (31.9%, 11/94), followed by storage tank water (17.8%, 8/94), with a single colony-forming unit (CFU/100 mL) detected in the Janmeda service reservoir. Regarding total bacterial distribution across sampling points, storage tanks (50%), tap water (31.9%), and raw water (17%) accounted for the majority, with no statistically significant difference (Fisher’s exact test = 36.56, p = 0.55, Cramer’s V = 0.31). All isolates, comprising eleven bacterial species (Table 3), were members of Enterobacteriaceae, except for Acinetobacter spp., P. aeruginosa, and Staphylococcus spp.

Antibiotic resistance patterns

Based on their clinical relevance and widespread use in Ethiopian human and veterinary healthcare, sixteen different antibiotic discs were selected for this study. These antibiotics were tested against both Gram-positive species (except for aztreonam, doripenem, meropenem, cefuroxime, ceftazidime, and cefotaxime) and Gram-negative isolates (except for erythromycin, oxacillin, and clindamycin) (Additional file 1 Spreadsheet 3). The highest levels of resistance were observed for ampicillin (86%, 74/94), followed by tetracycline (72.7%, 64/94), cefuroxime (66.2%, 49/94), cefoxitin (53.5%, 46/94), and gentamicin (46.8%, 44/94). Conversely, the lowest resistance levels were observed for aztreonam (12.5%), doripenem (14.6%), meropenem (14.6%), kanamycin (15.1%), and ciprofloxacin (21.3%) (Table 4). Among the isolates, Klebsiella spp., Enterobacter spp., and Citrobacter spp., exhibited complete resistance to ampicillin. Overall, relatively high and intermediate resistance levels were recorded for the most commonly prescribed antibiotics in the Ethiopian healthcare system, including tetracycline, ampicillin, cefuroxime, gentamicin, cefoxitin, ceftazidime, and sulfamethoxazole (Additional file 2).

Discussion

The emergence of β-lactam and carbapenem-resistant genes has become a global threat, as it leads to the occurrence and wider dissemination of MDR bacteria, which are resistant to empirical treatment strategies. Currently, global multidrug-resistant infections are predicted to increase by 43%. Specifically, healthcare-associated infections and community-acquired infections (such as methicillin-resistant S. aureus, and ESBL- and carbapenemase-producing Enterobacteriaceae) are projected to rise sharply by 68% and 38%, respectively, in regions with high antimicrobial misuse [46]. Understanding the molecular mechanisms responsible for the dissemination of drug-resistant genes in non-clinical isolates aids in active epidemiologic surveillance and provides baseline information that strengthens preventive and infection control strategies against the emergence of AMR. Although drinking water distribution systems have been identified as conducive hotspots for numerous ARGs and ARBs, they are often overlooked as potential sources [14].

This study explored the bacterial profile, antibiotic susceptibility pattern, and molecular detection of associated ESBLs genes in MDR isolates from drinking water supply systems in Addis Ababa city, Ethiopia. From a total of 45 samples, a 71.1% overall positive culture rate was recorded, which is comparable to another study done by Asfaw et al. (2016) in Jigjiga city (71.2%) [47]. However, a recent study conducted in the North Shoa Zone, Ethiopia, reported a positive culture rate of 29.7% [9], which was nearly fourfold less than our result. This may be attributed to the differences in sampling sources, water handling practices, and distribution pipeline systems of the drinking water. In comparison with our result, a higher growth rate (80%) was reported from a tap water sample of Shashemane town [48], and lower positive growth in Bule Hora (20%) [24]. The study carried out in Shashemane, however, was in line with our findings in reporting 100% growth in storage containers [48]. This implies that stored water is more prone to contamination than tap water, and differences in culture growth in water samples may be attributed to inefficiency of treatment methods, differences in handling of storage containers, inadequate cleaning, damage to pipelines, and prolonged storage periods, which may increase the risk of water contamination. Numerous studies in various towns of Ethiopia have shown the various degrees of microbial contamination of household tap water, storage water and protected drinking water sources [49–52]. These findings prove that there is always a high risk of water contamination by pathogenic microorganisms that may pose a serious threat to the consumers of this water.

In the present study, eleven bacterial species of public health importance were identified. The majority of isolates were from storage tanks, tap water, and raw water, which is in line with Bonso et al. (2023) who reported a higher number of isolates from storage container samples, followed by tap water samples. In our study, E. coli (22/94) was the most frequent isolate, followed by Enterobacter spp., (15/94), Staphylococcus species (12/94), and Citrobacter species (10/94). A similar study by Dhengesu et a. (2022) in Bule Hora; Bonso et al. (2023) in Shashemane; Mogessie et al. (2024) in Addis Ababa; Odonkor et al. (2022) from Ghana also reported a comparable frequency of E. coli along with other Enterobacteriaceae members from the household drinking water [24, 25, 48, 53]. This dominance of E. coli in drinking water sources may be attributed to recent fecal contamination of source water by human and animal wastes, depletion of residual chlorine in distribution lines, or leakages occurring at certain points in distribution systems. In Addis Ababa, Kassa et al. (2026) demonstrated that households with damaged pipes and latrines within a 10-meter proximity to water sources showed a significantly higher likelihood of E. coli contamination in their tap water [54]. This finding suggests a direct seepage of wastewater into the municipal freshwater supply system from the nearby sewers or drainage ditches.

On the other hand, a study from the North Shoa Zone reports P. vulgaris and P. mirabilis as the most frequently isolated bacteria in drinking water sources, along with S. aureus and members of Enterobacteriaceae [9]. A similar study from Nigeria also revealed that Proteus was the most frequent isolate (18.18%) from treated water samples [55]. Moreover, Acinetobacter species were characterized in the raw water sources collected from the dams of both Gefersa and Legedadi DWTPs. Notably, a study of Canadian aquatic systems with different uses, including raw drinking water source, recreational and agricultural purposes reported various species of Acinetobacter from each sample [56]. Similar other studies also reported Acinetobacter species from municipal tap water distribution systems in Oyo State, Nigeria [57] and in rivers, ponds, and lakes designated as major water hubs for domestic purposes by the inhabitants of Dhaka, in Bangladesh [58]. Several factors may be responsible for variation in the occurrence rate of bacterial contaminants across drinking water sources and distribution systems in Addis Ababa city, particularly, and some other study areas in general. Treatment ineffectiveness, long storage periods, intermittent water supply, old pipelines (formation of biofilms), leakage of sewage, septic tanks, poor maintenance of distribution lines, domestic animal grazing and pit latrines around water sources, sampling points, poor maintenance of residual disinfectants (e.g., short contact time), bacterial resistance to chlorine concentration and other domestic wastes are a few among many other factors [36, 57].

Although WHO guidelines and the Ethiopian standard agency established a zero CFU of E. coli and/or total coliforms (0 CFU/100 mL) in drinking water, the presence of one E. coli CFU/100 mL in water intended for drinking, as in the Janmeda service reservoir, exceeds the limit of WHO guidance values for quality of drinking water. Hence, the presence of E. coli and other members of coliforms in Addis Ababa drinking water sources and the supply system dictates a call for serious intervention and monitoring strategies, as contaminated water carries ARB and enhances the dissemination of resistance genes. In our study, the occurrence rate of MDR is 63.8%, which is relatively less than in a study by Bonso et al. (2023), where all isolates were reported as MDR. A similar study, conducted in the North Shoa zone by Abebe et al. (2024), also showed a higher pattern of MDR occurrence (65.3%) [9]. Moreover, several studies have reported different levels of MDR occurrences in bacteria from drinking water sources, including Ghana [53, 59], Nigeria [55], South Africa [60] and Kenya [61]. Overall, the occurrences of MDR bacterial isolates in drinking water sources call for policy interventions that enable effective design and implementation of water safety plan strategies to reduce the dissemination of antibiotic resistance and prevent waterborne infections. In our case, E. coli (25%) was the most frequent MDR isolate, followed by Enterobacter spp. (16.7%), K. pneumoniae (13.3%), Proteus spp. (10%), and Citrobacter spp. (8.3%). Similarly, a study by Ahmed et al. [59] and Yenew et al. [62] also reported E. coli as the most frequent MDR isolate in drinking water samples from household tap and storage containers. Another study, however, reported S. aureus (31.25%) and P. vulgaris (15.6%) as the most frequent MDR isolates, followed by P. mirabilis (12.5%) [9]. This variation in MDR principal isolates in drinking water supply systems across studies may be due to differences in the source of contamination, the number of antibiotic discs tested, the treatment techniques employed, and the sampling source used.

In this paper, a high degree of resistance was observed to ampicillin (86%), tetracycline (72.7%), cefuroxime (66.2%), cefoxitin (53.5%), and gentamicin (46.8%), in decreasing order. This finding was consistent with a study in Shashemane [48] which reported 98% to 100% resistance to ampicillin; in Bahirdar, which also reported 100% resistance to ampicillin and amoxicillin [63]; and in Addis Ababa [25], where 96% resistance to ampicillin was reported. A study from Bule Hora also aligns with our findings in showing complete resistance to ampicillin and tetracycline by Enterobacteriaceae (except E. coli, Salmonella, and Shigella species) isolated from drinking water sources [24]. A comparable investigation into Ghana’s drinking water sources revealed notably high resistance rates to cefuroxime (88.7%) and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (62.6%) [59]. A study by Odonkor et al. (2022) also reported higher resistance patterns of bacterial isolates towards cefuroxime (24.5%), meropenem, and cefotaxime, each exhibiting 21.3% resistance. These overall discrepancies may be attributed to significant variation in antibiotic usage within countries, high prescription rates without laboratory confirmations, improper handling and utilization of veterinary drugs, and inappropriate disposals of pharmaceuticals by residents and related industries. All these factors are responsible for the development and emergence of bacteria resistant to commonly prescribed antibiotics. On the other hand, low levels of resistance were exhibited for antibiotics, including meropenem, doripenem, aztreonam, ciprofloxacin, and kanamycin, in our findings. This is aligned with the report by Abebe et al. (2024), where a low level of resistance was observed towards ciprofloxacin and newly introduced antibiotics such as imipenem and meropenem.

In the present study, molecular detection with a multiplex PCR assay was conducted for all 60 MDR strains from the drinking water systems. Among targeted ESBL gene variants, the co-occurring blaCTXM + blaTEM (11.7%) was the most frequently detected gene, followed by blaTEM (9.8%), blaCTXM (3.3%), and blaSHV (1.6%) in the genomic DNA of MDR isolates. In contrast, Adesoji et al. (2016) reported blaTEM as the most frequently occurring gene and blaCTXM as the least gene in MDR isolates from drinking water distribution channels in Nigeria. This same study reports Klebsiella (18.03%) as the dominant genus harboring these ESBL genes, followed by the genus Proteus (14.75%) [55]. This differs from the present study, where E. coli (8.3%, 5/60) mainly harbored these ESBL genes, followed by K. pneumoniae (6.6%, 4/60), K. oxytoca (3.3%, 2/60), and Enterobacter spp. (3.3%, 2/60). In the current study, at least one co-occurring blaCTXM+ blaTEM gene was detected in E. coli, K. pneumoniae, K. oxytoca, Enterobacter, Citrobacter, and Proteus spp. In contrast to this, Adesoji et al. (2016), confirmed such a combination only in the genus Klebsiella. These discrepancies may be attributed to the difference in genetic transfer of ESBL genes involving MGEs and selective pressures in the study areas. Moreover, blaSHV (1.67%, 1/60) was solely detected in K. oxytoca, which is in line with a study in Türkiye that reported blaSHV gene in three Klebsiella strains isolated from the aquatic system [64]. In the present assay, blaTEM was dominantly detected in E. coli (6.5%), followed by blaCTXM (3.3%) in K. pneumoniae. In contrast to the present study, Mahmud et al. reported that E. coli isolated from drinking water harbored both blaCTXM−1 group and blaCTXM−15 gene variants (59%, 39/66), followed by blaTEM gene (35%, 23/66), blaCTX−M−1group and/or blaCTX−M−15 genes (4.5%, 3/66) [65]. Another study spotted the blaTEM gene dominantly in P. aeruginosa (94.1%), but the least CTX-M gene in the same isolate [66]. This variation may result from differences in target gene variants, seasonal changes, and involved genetic determinants.

In Ethiopia, studies that characterize ESBL genes in drug-resistant isolates of drinking water sources are very limited. The only study conducted in North Shoa reported the presence of blaCTXM gene variants including the blaCTXM-gp8/25 (6/33, 18.2%), the blaCTXM-gp9 (5/33, 15.2%), and the blaCTXM-gp1 (5/33, 15.2%) bacterial isolates from drinking water sources [9]. This presence of the bla genes, corresponding to the findings by Abebe et al. (2024), indicates the potential distribution of antibiotic-resistant genes via the drinking water supply system contaminated by antibiotic-resistant bacteria to the final consumers. This requires stringent intervention to assess the potential health risk and implement appropriate preventive measures, including evaluating the effectiveness of treatment technologies. The presence of bla genes in drinking water sources is also a challenge for developed countries like Canada, where the presence of blaTEM in water samples was reported from the tap in the treatment plant, taps in different households, a lake used as a source of water for DWTP, and lakes used as direct drinking water sources [67]. In line with our findings, these authors also reported both blaTEM and blaCTXM genes in samples of different storage containers at home used for cleaning purposes.

In addition to genomic DNA, we also investigated the presence of the above bla genes in plasmid DNA for the MDR isolates, except Staphylococcus species. Six isolates (10.9%, 6/55) were found to harbor those ESBL genes. The blaTEM (7.3%, 4/55) was the most frequently detected gene, where the two other genes were blaCTXM (detected in K. pneumoniae (1.18%)), and co-occurring blaCTXM + blaSHV gene (detected in E. coli (1.18%, 1/55)). In Ethiopia, no similar study targeting bla genes in plasmid DNA of drug-resistant bacteria from water sources was conducted. However, a study from France reported that the blaCTX−M−1 gene was carried on an IncI1/ST3 plasmid [68], which was found analogous to blaCTX−M−1 IncI1/ST3 plasmids previously characterized in E. coli isolated from domestic animals [69] and K. pneumoniae from the intensive care unit in Spain [70]. Another study also reported higher prevalence of plasmid-mediated bla genes, including blaTEM-1, blaCTX-M − 15, as well as qnrS1 and armA in commensal E. coli isolates from the Yamuna River, India [71]. This suggests the possible transfer of ESBL genes through drinking water contaminated by animal sources (e.g., feces), where E. coli has been identified as a potential reservoir for the persistence of bla genes and their spread from one setting to another, i.e., between hospitals, the environment, and animals. Though studies on the detection of bla genes in plasmid DNA isolates from non-clinical samples are limited, the highest occurrences of blaTEM (63.4%) and blaCTX−M (36.6%) were reported in E. coli from clinical samples in Nepal [72]. The CTX-M-15 and TEM-1 β-lactamase gene variants were also spotted from plasmid DNA of clinical Citrobacter spp., in Mexico, which also reported resistance to other antibiotic classes, including aminoglycosides, quinolones, and inhibitors of the folate pathway [73]. The macrolide-modifying enzyme gene blaCTX-M-14, as well as ermB, was also reported in clinical MDR E. coli carried on an IncI1 plasmid in Uruguay [74].

Overall, the variation of plasmid-mediated ESBL-1 gene occurrences across various study areas may be attributed to contamination of source water by hospital and domestic wastes, transferability of plasmid DNA, inefficient drinking water treatment technologies, improper maintenance of distribution pipelines, presence of selective pressure, type of water, and time of the year at sampling. Moreover, lack of initiatives in funding studies on non-clinical samples, lack of proper laboratory reagents, and lack of awareness by concerned sectors on the issue of AMR dynamics may influence the occurrence of bla genes in water. Finally, despite MDR phenotype in Acinetobacter or Pseudomonas species, none of targeted ESBL genes were detected in these species. We believe this may be attributed to the existence of diverse beta-lactamase gene variants beyond the classical blaTEM, blaCTXM and blaSHV that are commonly occurs in Enterobacteriaceae, reflecting their underlying genetic diversity, molecular structure, and functional complexity. Additionally, despite displaying MDR phenotypes, research indicates that these two species employ a distinct resistance mechanism. For example, Acinetobacter species rely potently on resistance driven by OXA-type carbapenemase genes (such as blaOXA-23, blaOXA-58) [75] whereas Pseudomonas species primarily utilizes resistance mediated by AmpC cephalosporinases or Metallo-beta-lactamases including such as VIM, IMP, PER, and VEB types [76]. We intend to target these specific resistance genes in future research.

Antibiotic-resistant bacteria can potentially escape from the wastewater discharges of clinical, industrial, and polluted environments into freshwater sources intended for human use. In Addis Ababa, a recent study revealed a 52.5% phenotypic prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolates from the wastewater effluents of two hospitals, where the magnitudes of ESBL and carbapenemase producers were 19% and 3.1%, respectively [77]. Likewise, a high occurrence of MDR Gram-negative bacterial isolates such as E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Salmonella, and Citrobacter has been reported in local rivers, indicating that wastewater discharges from healthcare facilities and abattoir environments contaminate downstream waterways [78, 79]. This poses high exposure risks to farming communities that rely on these rivers to irrigate leafy vegetables (e.g., lettuce and Swiss chard) and to final consumers that buy these raw vegetables from the city markets, creating a direct exposure route to drug resistant bacteria found on fresh produce [80]. The persistent environmental contamination resulting from inadequate wastewater management and improper disposal of antibiotic residues drives resistance traits in bacteria. These resistant strains ultimately enter freshwater sources, posing a serious public health risk. Although the city’s municipal water is disinfected at the Legedadi and Gefersa treatment plants, inefficiencies in removing all antimicrobial residues and ARB lead to the substantial persistence of ARGs within the water distribution network. The dissemination of these ARGs is further potentiated within biofilms that form inside water distribution facilities [81, 82]. In clinical settings, biofilms increase the resistance and pathogenicity margins of bacterial pathogens, complicating antibacterial treatment options [83]. Because the city’s municipal water supply is intermittent and has variable pressure, it can cause biofilm aggregates to slough off, introducing ARB and their potential resistance genes into household taps and point-of-use systems. Greater human exposure risks occur at the household level due to water supply gaps that force residents to rely on storage tanks, buckets, and jerrycans [25, 84]. Furthermore, poor hygiene practices during the handling and storage of water can lead to secondary bacterial contamination at the point of use [54]. This contamination has previously been linked to acute diarrhea among children under five in the slums of Addis Ababa [85], infantile diarrhea [86], and a cholera outbreak resulting from the Vibrio cholerae O1 serotype isolated from contaminated holy water and raw vegetables [87]. The present study provides baseline information regarding the movement of ARGs (specifically, ESBL) from the clinical environments to freshwater sources, and their potential mobilization to the human population in Addis Ababa, thereby linking aquatic environments with human health. Consequently, we strongly advocate for stringent regulatory strategies in wastewater management, alongside the rigorous monitoring of freshwater sources and supply systems. This should be achieved via an integrated One Health approach at the interface of the aquatic, human, and animal sectors. Moreover, innovative research involving anti-biofilm coatings that prevent bacterial adhesion to various water infrastructure surfaces [88] as well as advanced therapeutic approaches utilizing nanoparticles and their synergy with existing antimicrobial agents to tackle drug-resistant pathogens [89] should be endorsed.

Limitations of the study

Although this study identifies drinking-water sources and supply networks, including reservoirs, taps, and storage tank water, as a potential hotspot for the distribution of ARB and ARGs in Addis Ababa, it acknowledges several limitations. The study was limited to raw water samples collected from Gefersa and Legedadi drinking water treatment plants, which source water from surface water. Consequently, the results may not fully represent all drinking water sources. Likely, imbalance in sample sizes specifically, the small sample sizes for certain sub-groups (main reservoirs, n = 2; raw water, n = 2 and service reservoirs, n = 4) compared to the larger sub-groups (tap water, n = 25; storage water, n = 12) poses a challenge. Mathematically, these discrepancies restrict the statistical tests used to evaluate significant variations across sub-groups, forcing the analysis to rely on highly limited datasets for certain categories. Consequently, statistical comparisons across sampling points may be underpowered due to these incomparable sample sizes (particularly the small raw water and reservoir samples), potentially rendering the detection of differences less reliable. Another limitation for this study was the absence of validated reference strains for our targeted genes, although consistent PCR products were obtained for all isolated screened for putative ESBL genes. Molecular analysis for genetic determinants was limited to multiplex PCR assays, designed to detect resistance associated with target genes using specific primers. Future studies should consider sequencing technologies and updated microbiological protocols that reveal detailed HGT mechanisms, including plasmids, transposons, integrons, and gene cassettes. While the alkaline lysis method cannot distinguish between small and large plasmids or confirm their structural integrity, it is still the gold standard for extracting high-yield plasmid DNA in resource-limited settings. Unfortunately, resource constraints in our laboratory currently prevent us from conducting further experiments, such as transformation and conjugation assays, to confirm whether this plasmid-borne resistance is transferable. Moreover, due to the low number of samples enrolled, this study may not reveal the full picture of the city’s ARB prevalence in the drinking water distribution system; instead, it sheds some light on the topic for future attention. Testing more samples and nationwide surveillance should be considered in future research. Additionally, we are unable to assess MDR occurrences across seasonal differences due to sampling barriers, limited study time, and financial support. We hope these factors will be addressed in future studies. Lastly, phenotypic studies for β-lactamase-producing bacteria were not conducted due to financial constraints. We believe that AST conducted against selected antibiotics in the current study can potentially highlights the resistance profiles across various sampling points and serve as crucial baseline information for AMR surveillance within drinking water distribution systems.

Conclusion

Our study showed that water distribution systems and storage tanks at public restaurants in Addis Ababa are contaminated with various potential bacterial pathogens and indicator organisms. Among the MDR isolates screened for different bla genes, blaCTXM, blaTEM, co-occurring blaCTXM + blaTEM and blaSHV + blaTEM were detected in both genomic and plasmid DNA from water samples. The majority of these genes were found in isolates from storage tank water samples. The findings of this study have dual significance, as they reveal the drinking water supply system in the study area as a potential hub for bacterial proliferation and the spread of various ARGs, particularly ESBL genes. Our results further suggest the possible emission of β-lactamase-producing bacteria from the environment into drinking water sources and their distribution networks. Therefore, a system targeting critical points within distribution pipelines capable of providing quantitative and qualitative estimates of potential exposure risks to ARB and other pathogens via drinking water and storage tanks should be implemented in Addis Ababa. Moreover, the presence of a high number of MDR isolates and associated resistance genes in drinking water sources underscores the need for regular monitoring of all water sources and for operational strategies that include advanced water treatment technologies and proper waste management practices. Given the potential community exposure to bacterial pathogens via the drinking water supply, urgent interventions and preventive measures are warranted. We recommend the implementation of antimicrobial stewardship programs across all One Health sectors, active surveillance and monitoring of antibiotic residues, upgrading of drinking water treatment facilities, and pilot studies incorporating risk assessments and high-throughput analytical tools.
