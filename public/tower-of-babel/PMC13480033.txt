HEPACAM2 expression for prognostic stratification in rectal adenocarcinoma treated with neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy
Authors: Eric Yi-Liang Shen, Hung-Wei Yeh, Din-Li Tsan, Yu-Chen Chang, Chien-Feng Li, Ching-Chieh Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Colorectal cancer remains a substantial contributor to global cancer mortality, ranking as the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. Rectal tumors account for approximately one-third of all colorectal malignancies documented in population-based registries [2]. Current management of locally advanced rectal cancer employs a multimodality approach incorporating neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CRT) followed by total mesorectal excision. Recent landmark trials, including PRODIGE 23 [3, 4] and RAPIDO [5], have demonstrated improved survival outcomes with intensified total neoadjuvant therapy protocols. Despite advances in locoregional disease control, distant metastasis persists as the predominant cause of treatment failure, affecting 20%–30% of patients within 3 years [3–5].

Pathological tumor regression grade (TRG) following neoadjuvant therapy serves as a valuable surrogate for treatment efficacy and carries prognostic implications for long-term outcomes [6, 7]. However, owing to its inherently postoperative nature, TRG assessment cannot inform pretreatment decision-making. Molecular biomarkers capable of predicting treatment response and survival before neoadjuvant therapy could guide individualized treatment intensification or de-escalation strategies, representing an unmet clinical need.

HEPACAM2 (hepatocyte cell adhesion molecule 2), also known as MIKI (mitotic kinetics regulator), encodes an immunoglobulin superfamily protein with established roles in cell cycle regulation [8, 9]. HEPACAM2 undergoes poly(ADP-ribosyl)ation by tankyrase-1 during late G2 phase and prophase, subsequently translocating from the Golgi apparatus to mitotic centrosomes where it serves as an anchoring platform for CG-NAP/AKAP9, thereby facilitating γ-tubulin ring complex activity and microtubule nucleation [8]. In colorectal malignancies, single-cell RNA sequencing studies have identified HEPACAM2 as an epithelial-specific transcript that distinguishes neoplastic from normal tissue [10]. Computational analyses of the normal mucosa–adenoma–carcinoma sequence have documented stepwise decreases in HEPACAM2 expression during tumor evolution with associated prognostic implications [11].

Recent integrative bioinformatic analyses have linked reduced HEPACAM2 expression to advanced T classification and diminished overall survival, with mechanistic connections to WNT/TCF signaling and G2/M checkpoint regulation, supporting a potential tumor suppressor function [12]. Conversely, in small cell lung carcinoma, HEPACAM2 exhibits marked overexpression with elevated levels predicting worse progression-free and overall survival, suggesting context-dependent oncogenic properties [9]. The prognostic significance of HEPACAM2 expression in rectal cancer patients undergoing neoadjuvant CRT remains unexplored. This study aims to evaluate the association between HEPACAM2 expression and clinicopathological characteristics, treatment response, and survival outcomes in a retrospective cohort of patients with rectal adenocarcinoma treated with neoadjuvant CRT followed by surgical resection.

Methods

Transcriptomic database mining

To identify genes predictive of CRT response, we interrogated a publicly available gene expression dataset (GSE35452; Gene Expression Omnibus) containing pretreatment tumor samples from 46 rectal cancer patients who subsequently underwent CRT [13, 14]. Treatment response based on pathological response assessment was defined by Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum (JSCCR) criteria. JSCCR Grade 2–3 (n = 24) was classified as responders and Grade 0–1 (n = 22) as non-responders. Gene expression profiles were obtained using Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 microarrays and analyzed with Nexus Expression 3 software (BioDiscovery). The signal intensities were normalized using the robust multi-array average (RMA) algorithm. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering was performed using the Euclidean distance metric and the average linkage method to visualize the transcriptomic relationships between samples. In this dataset, 24 patients (52.2%) were classified as responders and 22 patients (47.8%) as non-responders, and the comparative analysis was performed between these two groups. Transcripts that meet the selection criteria of p < 0.01 and log2-transformed fold change ≥ 1.0 between responders and nonresponders were retained for further investigation.

Study population

We retrospectively analyzed tissue specimens from 343 patients with newly diagnosed rectal adenocarcinoma treated at Chi Mei Medical Center between 1998 and 2017. All patients received neoadjuvant CRT consisting of 5-fluorouracil–based chemotherapy administered concurrently with radiotherapy, followed by curative surgical resection incorporating total mesorectal excision. Patients presenting with clinical or pathological stage T3 and/or N1 or higher disease were considered eligible for adjuvant chemotherapy according to institutional treatment guidelines based on NCCN recommendations. Adjuvant chemotherapy was typically initiated approximately 3–4 weeks after surgery, most commonly using FOLFOX, CapOX, or 5-fluorouracil–based regimens, and administered for a duration of at least 4 months. Follow-up continued through December 31, 2022, or until death or last clinical contact [15].

All staging information was re-evaluated and updated according to the AJCC 8th edition TNM classification to ensure consistency across the study cohort, based on preoperative assessments including endoscopic ultrasonography, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging. Documented clinicopathological variables included demographic characteristics, pretreatment and posttreatment tumor and nodal classification, lymphovascular and perineural invasion status, and pathological regression assessment.

Pathological assessment and tumor regression grading

Histopathological evaluation of surgical specimens was performed independently by two pathologists (W.S.L. and T.J.C.). Pathological response to neoadjuvant CRT was quantified using the five-tier regression grading system proposed by Rödel et al. : grade 0, no regression; grade 1, minor regression (dominant tumor with fibrosis in ≤ 25% of specimen); grade 2, moderate regression (fibrosis in 26%–50%); grade 3, good regression (fibrosis dominant over residual tumor, > 50%); and grade 4, complete regression (no identifiable viable tumor cells) [6]. For the purpose of statistical analysis, TRG was further categorized into three groups: poor response (grades 0–1), intermediate response (grades 2–3), and complete response (grade 4). This regrouping was performed for both clinical and statistical considerations. Specifically, due to the uneven distribution of patients across the five original categories, combining adjacent grades helped ensure adequate sample size within each group and improved the stability of statistical estimates. In addition, we aimed to preserve the clinical significance of complete response (grade 4), which has been consistently associated with the most favorable prognosis, by maintaining it as a distinct category. The intermediate grouping (grades 2–3) reflects partial tumor regression, whereas grades 0–1 represent minimal or no response to treatment.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded pretreatment biopsy specimens. Tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with hydrogen peroxide. Sections were then incubated with a rabbit polyclonal anti-human HEPACAM2 antibody (IHC-plus™, LS-B8888; LSBio, Seattle, WA, USA) at a dilution of 1:200. HEPACAM2 protein expression was quantified using the H-score method, calculated as: H-score = ΣPi × (i + 1), where Pi represents the percentage of tumor cells demonstrating staining at each intensity level (0%–100%), and i represents staining intensity grade (0–3+). For survival analyses, HEPACAM2 expression was dichotomized at the median H-score into high (≥ median) and low (< median) categories.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM). Primary outcomes included 5-year disease-specific survival (DSS), locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRFS), and metastasis-free survival (MeFS). Time-to-event intervals were calculated from diagnosis to last follow-up, disease recurrence, metastatic progression, or death. Deaths attributable to rectal cancer were considered events; deaths from other causes were censored. Associations between HEPACAM2 expression and clinicopathological variables were examined using chi-square testing. Survival distributions were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using log-rank testing. Multivariable analyses employed Cox proportional hazards regression to identify independent prognostic factors. Variables with statistical significance in univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable models. All tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of HEPACAM2 as a CRT response–associated gene

Analysis of the GSE35452 gene expression dataset identified HEPACAM2 as significantly upregulated in CRT-resistant tumors compared with treatment-responsive cases. Among the 10 most differentially expressed transcripts, HEPACAM2 had a log2 expression ratio of 1.75 with a comparative p-value of 0.0001, clustering with REG4, PLA2G2A, CLCA1, SPINK4, MUC2, AGR3, PCSK1, CTSE, and HSD17B2 (Table 1; Fig. 1). These findings identified HEPACAM2 as a candidate CRT-resistance biomarker warranting clinical validation.

Notably, several of the co-identified transcripts, including REG4, CLCA1, SPINK4, PCSK1, and CTSE, have previously been investigated in colorectal cancer or the neoadjuvant CRT setting [16–20], and PCSK1 was specifically characterized as a prognostic biomarker in rectal cancer receiving preoperative CRT in a prior publication from our research group [21]. The remaining co-identified transcripts, PLA2G2A, AGR3, HSD17B2, and MUC2, encode secretory or metabolic proteins whose specific contributions to CRT resistance mechanisms in colorectal cancer remain less defined. HEPACAM2 therefore emerged as the candidate combining scientific novelty with the most direct mechanistic plausibility for a role in CRT resistance.

As a centrosomal scaffold protein whose poly(ADP-ribosyl)ation by tankyrase-1 during the G2/M transition facilitates microtubule nucleation [8], the overexpression of HEPACAM2 in genomically unstable tumor cells may promote centrosome amplification and aberrant spindle assembly, both of which have been mechanistically linked to cellular invasion [22] and have been increasingly implicated in resistance to genotoxic therapies [23]. Additionally, prior bioinformatic analyses have documented mechanistic connections between HEPACAM2, WNT/TCF signaling, and G2/M checkpoint regulation [12], pathways with well-established roles in CRT resistance in colorectal cancer [24, 25]. Taken together, the combination of scientific novelty, mechanistic plausibility within functionally relevant oncogenic pathways, and the absence of prior clinical validation in this treatment context provided the primary rationale for prioritizing HEPACAM2 for immunohistochemical validation.

Patient characteristics

As shown in Table 2, the study cohort included 343 patients with rectal adenocarcinoma, comprising 223 males (65.0%) and 120 females (35.0%), with a median age of 64 years (range, 22–88 years). Pretreatment clinical staging identified locally advanced tumor classification (T3–T4) in 210 patients (61.2%) and regional nodal involvement (N1–N2) in 144 patients (42.0%). Posttreatment pathological evaluation following CRT and surgical resection revealed T3–T4 residual disease in 188 patients (54.8%) and N1–N2 nodal involvement in 118 patients (34.4%). Vascular invasion was documented in 38 patients (11.1%), and perineural invasion in 38 patients (11.1%). Pathological regression was graded as poor (TRG 0–1) in 73 patients (21.3%), intermediate (TRG 2–3) in 248 patients (72.3%), and complete (TRG 4) in 22 patients (6.4%).

Association of HEPACAM2 expression with clinicopathological parameters

HEPACAM2 immunohistochemistry was successfully performed and quantified in all 343 tissue specimens (Fig. 2). Using the median H-score threshold, 171 patients (49.9%) were classified as having low HEPACAM2 expression and 172 patients (50.1%) as having high expression.

In Table 2, elevated HEPACAM2 expression was associated with adverse clinicopathological characteristics. At baseline, high expression was associated with advanced pretreatment tumor classification (T3–T4: 56.2% vs. 43.8%; p = 0.005) and pretreatment nodal involvement (N1–N2: 68.8% vs. 31.3%; p < 0.001). Following neoadjuvant treatment, elevated HEPACAM2 expression correlated significantly with posttreatment residual tumor (T3–T4: 67.6% vs. 32.4%; p < 0.001) and residual nodal disease (N1–N2: 73.7% vs. 26.3%; p < 0.001). High expression was also strongly associated with vascular invasion (97.4% vs. 2.6%; p < 0.001) and perineural invasion (84.2% vs. 15.8%; p < 0.001).

Moreover, HEPACAM2 expression was inversely associated with pathological tumor regression after CRT. Among patients with poor regression (TRG 0–1), 71.2% demonstrated high HEPACAM2 levels compared with 28.8% with low expression. Conversely, among patients achieving a complete pathological response (TRG 4), only 40.9% had high HEPACAM2 expression, compared with 59.1% with low expression (p < 0.001).

HEPACAM2 expression and survival outcomes

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that elevated HEPACAM2 expression was significantly associated with inferior outcomes across all three survival endpoints (Fig. 3). Patients with high HEPACAM2 expression had worse DSS (p < 0.0001), LRFS (p < 0.0001), and MeFS (p < 0.0001) compared with those with low expression. The results of univariable analysis are shown in Table 3. Among patients with elevated HEPACAM2 expression, 50 of 172 (29.1%) died of disease compared with 13 of 171 (7.6%) in the low-expression cohort. Locoregional failure occurred in 29 patients (16.9%) with high expression versus six patients (3.5%) with low expression. Distant metastases developed in 53 patients (30.8%) with high expression compared with nine patients (5.3%) with low expression. Cox proportional hazards multivariable regression was performed to identify independent prognostic factors (Table 4). After adjustment for clinicopathological covariates, elevated HEPACAM2 expression remained an independent predictor for all three survival endpoints: DSS (HR 2.663, 95% CI 1.280–4.203, p = 0.005), LRFS (HR 4.311, 95% CI 1.130–5.960, p = 0.003), and MeFS (HR 5.711, 95% CI 2.657–12.278, p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this cohort study, we demonstrate that elevated HEPACAM2 expression is significantly associated with adverse clinicopathological features, diminished response to neoadjuvant CRT, and inferior survival outcomes in patients with rectal adenocarcinoma. To our knowledge, this represents the first investigation of HEPACAM2 as a prognostic biomarker specifically in rectal cancer patients treated with neoadjuvant CRT. These findings provide a rationale for future mechanistic studies to elucidate the biological role of HEPACAM2 in tumor progression and treatment resistance, and may also support the development of HEPACAM2-guided therapeutic strategies or targeted interventions in rectal cancer.

In contrast to prior bioinformatic analyses reporting that reduced HEPACAM2 expression correlates with advanced disease and worse survival in colorectal cancer [12], our findings demonstrate an inverse association: elevated HEPACAM2 expression predicted poor outcomes across all survival endpoints. Several reasons may account for this apparent discrepancy. First, biological distinctions exist between colon and rectal cancers, including differences in molecular subtypes, anatomical microenvironment, and treatment sensitivities, which may partly account for divergent findings across studies. Second, our study specifically focused on rectal cancer patients treated with neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, whereas prior bioinformatic analyses may have included more heterogeneous colorectal cancer populations and treatment settings. Third, methodological differences between transcriptomic profiling and tissue-based immunohistochemistry may capture distinct aspects of HEPACAM2 biology, as post-transcriptional regulation can lead to discrepancies between mRNA and protein expression levels. In addition, the lack of comprehensive independent validation data for the polyclonal antibody used in this study should be acknowledged as a limitation when interpreting the observed protein expression patterns.

Importantly, our observations align with recent findings in small cell lung carcinoma, where elevated HEPACAM2 expression similarly predicted worse progression-free and overall survival [9]. In that investigation, HEPACAM2 correlated positively with ASCL1 expression and enhanced malignant cell proliferation in experimental models, supporting context-dependent oncogenic properties. These findings, together with ours, suggest that the prognostic implications of HEPACAM2 expression are tissue-specific and treatment-dependent.

From a mechanistic standpoint, HEPACAM2 functions as a centrosomal protein that facilitates microtubule nucleation during mitosis through poly(ADP-ribosyl)ation by tankyrase-1 [8]. In normal cells, this activity supports orderly cell division. In tumor cells harboring underlying genomic instability, however, overexpressed HEPACAM2 may enhance centrosome amplification and aberrant spindle formation—phenomena associated with chromosomal instability and therapeutic resistance. Additionally, HEPACAM2 has been mechanistically linked to WNT/TCF signaling and G2/M checkpoint regulation [12], pathways implicated in resistance to both chemotherapy and radiotherapy. These connections provide a biological framework for understanding why elevated HEPACAM2 expression might identify tumors refractory to CRT, although direct functional studies are required for confirmation.

The strong associations between elevated HEPACAM2 expression and both vascular invasion (97.4% of vascular invasion–positive cases harboring high expression; p < 0.001) and perineural invasion (84.2%; p < 0.001) carry important biological and clinical implications. Both features are well-established histopathological indicators of aggressive tumor biology and adverse long-term prognosis in rectal cancer, independently associated with increased risk of recurrence and diminished survival [26, 27]. The near-absolute enrichment of high HEPACAM2 expression among patients with vascular invasion in particular suggests that elevated HEPACAM2 may reflect a broadly invasive, locally aggressive tumor phenotype rather than solely CRT insensitivity, an interpretation further supported by the substantially higher rates of distant metastasis observed in the high-expression group (30.8% vs. 5.3%). These associations are biologically plausible given the centrosomal and WNT/TCF-related functions of HEPACAM2 described above, which, through overexpression, may promote invasive cellular behavior [22]. These explanations remain speculative in the absence of direct functional studies in rectal cancer models, and prospective investigations are warranted to elucidate the precise mechanisms linking HEPACAM2 overexpression to the invasive phenotype observed in our cohort.

The robust association between HEPACAM2 expression and pathological regression grade carries potential clinical implications. Achievement of complete pathological response following neoadjuvant CRT is an established prognostic indicator and provides the rationale for organ-preservation approaches in selected patients. The marked enrichment of high HEPACAM2 expression among poor responders (71.2% vs. 28.8%) suggests potential utility for pretreatment risk stratification. Although prospective validation is pending, HEPACAM2 assessment could inform treatment intensification decisions, including identifying patients who might benefit from total neoadjuvant therapy regimens rather than conventional CRT alone.

Patients in this cohort were treated during 1998–2017, preceding the widespread adoption of total neoadjuvant therapy protocols currently supported by randomized data from the PRODIGE 23 [3, 4] and RAPIDO [5] trials. Whether HEPACAM2 retains prognostic relevance among patients receiving intensified regimens incorporating induction or consolidation chemotherapy warrants evaluation in contemporary cohorts.

Although HEPACAM2 remained statistically significant in the multivariable analyses, the current findings should be interpreted cautiously. The strong association between HEPACAM2 expression and adverse pathological features, particularly vascular invasion, together with the limited number of events for certain endpoints such as LRFS, may have affected coefficient stability and reduced the interpretability of individual covariate effects in the Cox models. In addition, the retrospective database primarily recorded post-treatment T and N categories separately rather than complete integrated ypTNM stage groupings, which limited the feasibility of reconstructing standardized overall pathological stage classifications across the entire cohort. Further subdivision into detailed stage categories would also have resulted in small subgroup sizes and sparse event numbers, potentially further compromising model stability. Therefore, the present findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and HEPACAM2 should be interpreted as a potentially complementary biomarker requiring further validation in larger cohorts with more standardized pathological staging and prospective model development.

Despite these findings, our study has several limitations. First, given the close relationships between HEPACAM2 expression and established pathological factors (e.g., TRG and vascular invasion), some degree of interdependence may exist among variables included in the model. Accordingly, the independent prognostic value of HEPACAM2 should be interpreted with caution. Second, our institutional database lacks information on several potentially important prognostic factors, including tumor differentiation grade, circumferential resection margin status, detailed data on adjuvant chemotherapy (e.g., treatment regimens, intensity, and completion), and molecular profiles of key oncogenes (e.g., KRAS, BRAF, and mismatch repair status). Therefore, the potential impact of treatment heterogeneity could not be fully accounted for and may have introduced residual confounding [28]. Third, due to the retrospective design and use of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue specimens, direct assessment of concordance between mRNA and protein expression levels was not feasible. Therefore, potential discrepancies arising from post-transcriptional regulation should be considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, the median dichotomization approach used in this study was adopted primarily for pragmatic and exploratory purposes; however, we acknowledge that this strategy is not considered the methodologically preferred approach for prognostic biomarker cutoff determination, and that the robustness of the observed HEPACAM2 associations across alternative cutoff strategies or continuous-variable modeling has not been empirically validated in the current cohort. We agree that these issues warrant further investigation in future studies using independent validation datasets and more comprehensive biomarker modeling approaches.

Conclusions

In this cohort study, elevated HEPACAM2 expression was associated with aggressive clinicopathological features, poor pathological response to neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, and inferior survival outcomes in rectal adenocarcinoma. These findings suggest that HEPACAM2 serves as a potentially complementary biomarker to refine risk stratification and better capture tumor biological heterogeneity, rather than a replacement for established pathological staging. As a candidate adjunctive tool, its clinical utility requires further external and prospective validation. Future research is warranted to explore its role in guiding personalized treatment strategies, particularly for patients demonstrating a suboptimal response to chemoradiotherapy.
