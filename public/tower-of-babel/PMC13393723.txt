Pressure-targeted neurocritical care at the bedside: the emerging role of transcranial doppler
Authors: Rafael Hortêncio Melo, Chiara Robba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractManagement of acute brain injury (ABI) focuses on maintaining cerebral perfusion pressure through systemic blood pressure optimization. However, traditional approaches relying on fixed pressure thresholds may not fully capture the complex interaction between systemic hemodynamics, cerebrovascular resistance, and cerebral autoregulation. Transcranial Doppler (TCD) ultrasonography offers a noninvasive bedside method to assess cerebral blood flow velocity patterns and their response to hemodynamic interventions. Doppler-derived parameters such as diastolic flow velocity (FVd) and pulsatility index (PI) provide physiological information that may help interpret the relationship between systemic arterial pressure and cerebral circulation. Observing how these signals change during controlled adjustments of mean arterial pressure may allow clinicians to identify pressure ranges in which cerebral perfusion appears more favorable for an individual patient. Importantly, TCD findings should not be interpreted in isolation but integrated with systemic hemodynamic assessment, including cardiac output, fluid responsiveness, and the underlying mechanism of hypotension. When used within a multimodal monitoring strategy, TCD may serve as a practical physiological tool to complement traditional neurocritical care monitoring. This viewpoint discusses the physiological rationale, potential clinical applications, and current limitations of Doppler-guided blood pressure targeting in patients with ABI.Keywords: Transcranial doppler, Cerebral perfusion, Neurocritical care

Introduction

Neurocritical care increasingly relies on continuous physiological monitoring to guide individualized treatment in patients with acute brain injury (ABI). Traditionally, management has focused on invasive intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring and maintenance of cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) targets [1–4]. However, invasive monitoring is not universally available [5] and, even when present, provides only a partial view of cerebral hemodynamics. The interaction between systemic circulation, cerebrovascular resistance, and autoregulatory mechanisms often remains incompletely characterized at the bedside. In this context, transcranial Doppler (TCD) ultrasonography has emerged as a practical noninvasive technique capable of providing real-time information about cerebral blood flow dynamics [4].

Although TCD has historically been used to detect vasospasm after subarachnoid hemorrhage and to assist in the diagnosis of cerebral circulatory arrest, its role in neurocritical care has expanded over the past decade [6, 7]. Doppler-derived signals are increasingly explored as physiological markers that may help interpret the relationship between systemic arterial pressure and cerebral circulation. In this viewpoint, we discuss the physiological basis of Doppler-derived pressure assessment, its potential role in guiding blood pressure targets at the bedside, and the limitations that currently constrain its clinical application. Rather than providing a formal systematic evidence synthesis, this article aims to offer a physiologically oriented narrative perspective on the interpretation of TCD-derived hemodynamic parameters and their potential role in individualized neurocritical care.

Physiological basis of transcranial doppler

TCD is based on the Doppler effect and provides noninvasive assessment of cerebral blood flow velocity within the major intracranial arteries. Importantly, TCD measures flow velocity rather than volumetric cerebral blood flow itself, and Doppler-derived signals are influenced by several technical and physiological factors, including the acoustic window, angle of insonation, vascular tone, arterial carbon dioxide tension, and systemic hemodynamics. Reliable acquisition also depends on stable probe positioning and operator expertise, which may limit prolonged continuous monitoring in routine clinical practice. Although fixation headframes and emerging robotic systems may improve signal stability, TCD remains an operator-dependent technique requiring careful interpretation within the broader clinical context.

The PI is calculated from the difference between systolic and diastolic flow velocities (FVd) divided by the mean flow velocity (FVm). Physiologically, it reflects the relationship between pulsatile inflow and downstream vascular resistance. In many clinical situations, elevations in intracranial pressure or increases in cerebrovascular resistance reduce diastolic flow velocity, which leads to higher PI values. Several experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated correlations between PI and ICP, particularly during episodes of acute intracranial hypertension [8–10]. However, its interpretation requires careful physiological context. Multiple systemic and cerebral variables can influence Doppler waveforms, including arterial pressure, cardiac output (CO), vascular compliance, and carbon dioxide tension [11]. For this reason, PI should not be considered a direct surrogate for ICP.Instead, it reflects the interaction between perfusion pressure, vascular resistance, and downstream cerebrovascular conditions.

Although this approach may provide useful physiological insight when invasive monitoring is unavailable, it relies on several assumptions. The calculation presumes relative stability of arterial diameter, consistent insonation of the same vascular segment, and stable systemic conditions during measurement. Variations in vascular tone or systemic hemodynamics can therefore influence Doppler velocities and affect the accuracy of the estimate.

TCD-guided pressure targeting

TCD may also provide physiological information that helps interpret the effects of systemic blood pressure manipulation on cerebral circulation (Fig. 2). Changes in MAP can produce measurable alterations in Doppler flow velocity patterns, reflecting the interaction between systemic perfusion pressure and cerebral autoregulation. In patients with impaired autoregulation, increases in MAP achieved through vasopressor therapy may lead to proportional increases in FVd and reductions in PI, suggesting that cerebral blood flow has become pressure dependent [11, 15]. In contrast, when autoregulatory mechanisms remain intact, moderate MAP increases often produce little change in Doppler velocities, indicating that cerebral blood flow is maintained despite variations in perfusion pressure [16, 17]. Observing Doppler responses during controlled MAP adjustments may therefore help identify the pressure range at which cerebral circulation becomes pressure-passive. This concept supports the growing shift toward individualized perfusion targets in neurocritical care, where fixed MAP thresholds may not reflect the physiological requirements of a given patient.

During MAP titration, interpretation often focuses on the diastolic component of the Doppler waveform. FVd is particularly sensitive to changes in distal cerebrovascular resistance and perfusion pressure [18]. In the MCA, diastolic velocities in stable adults typically range between approximately 20 and 40 cm/s, although absolute values vary with age and systemic physiological conditions. When cerebral perfusion pressure becomes critically reduced, diastolic velocity may fall below 15 to 20 cm/s and in extreme cases may approach zero or even reverse. During stepwise MAP increases, restoration or augmentation of forward diastolic flow may therefore suggest improvement in downstream perfusion conditions.

PI provides complementary information in this setting. In adults, typical MCA PI values range from approximately 0.6 to 1.1. Persistently elevated values above 1.2 to 1.3 often reflect increased distal resistance or reduced CPP, whereas values exceeding 1.5 to 1.6 may be observed in severe intracranial hypertension. During MAP augmentation, a reduction in PI accompanied by increasing FVd generally indicates more favorable cerebral perfusion dynamics. For this reason, these parameters are best interpreted together, since PI is largely determined by the behavior of the diastolic component of the waveform [4].

FVm may also change during pressure manipulation, although its interpretation is less straightforward. Typical values in adults range between approximately 40 and 80 cm/s. When autoregulation is impaired, increases in MAP may lead to modest increases in mean velocity. However, FVm alone cannot reliably represent cerebral blood flow because it may be influenced by arterial diameter, metabolic demand, and arterial carbon dioxide tension. Trends in mean velocity are therefore most informative when interpreted combined with FVd and PI rather than in isolation.

An important limitation when using TCD derived indices for pressure titration is that waveform changes do not always correspond directly to changes in cerebral blood flow. For instance, elevations in PI may occur when cerebrovascular resistance increases without a true reduction in cerebral perfusion, such as during hyperventilation induced hypocapnia or marked increases in arterial pulsatility [19–21]. Conversely, relatively low PI values may be observed despite impaired cerebral perfusion in states of systemic hypotension or reduced CO, where global flow is diminished but distal vascular resistance remains low. These situations illustrate that PI primarily reflects the balance between pulsatile inflow and downstream resistance rather than cerebral blood flow itself.

Doppler signals can also be interpreted together with Doppler-derived estimates of CPP. During stepwise MAP adjustments, observing how FVd, PI, and estimated CPP evolve together may help determine whether waveform changes reflect genuine improvements in cerebral perfusion or simply alterations in vascular resistance. Although such estimates do not replace invasive monitoring and remain dependent on several physiological assumptions, integrating them with qualitative waveform interpretation may provide an additional perspective when titrating blood pressure at the bedside.

Another relevant concept is the identification of pressure passive circulation. When autoregulatory capacity is impaired, cerebral blood flow becomes increasingly dependent on systemic perfusion pressure. Under these conditions, incremental MAP increases, typically in 5 to 10 mmHg steps, may produce parallel increases in FVd and reductions in PI, suggesting improved downstream perfusion [22]. Conversely, minimal Doppler changes despite MAP augmentation may indicate preserved autoregulation or adequate perfusion at the current pressure level. The goal is therefore not to normalize absolute Doppler values but to identify the pressure range in which cerebral hemodynamics appear most favorable for the individual patient.

Hemodynamic strategies to achieve blood pressure targets

How a blood pressure target is achieved should not be determined by TCD alone. Doppler monitoring should instead be integrated into a broader hemodynamic assessment that considers CO, fluid responsiveness, fluid tolerance, and the underlying mechanism of hypotension or impaired cerebral perfusion. In patients with evidence of hypovolemia or preload responsiveness, a cautious isotonic fluid challenge may be appropriate. In contrast, when patients appear euvolemic or fluid unresponsive, vasopressor titration is usually the more appropriate strategy to increase mean arterial pressure.

This distinction is particularly important in neurocritical care, where excessive fluid administration may worsen pulmonary dysfunction or cerebral edema without necessarily improving cerebral perfusion [23]. Contemporary literature in traumatic brain injury (TBI) and subarachnoid hemorrhage supports a goal-directed strategy centered on euvolemia, avoidance of hypotension, and individualized manipulation of arterial pressure and CO rather than indiscriminate fluid loading [24–26].

Within this physiological context, TCD may provide a bedside indication of whether a given intervention is associated with more favorable cerebral flow dynamics (Fig. 3). Emerging venous ultrasound approaches may eventually complement this assessment, although their role in hemodynamic titration remains investigational [27, 28].

Clinical vignette

Consider a 52-year-old patient admitted to the neurocritical care unit after TBI following a motor vehicle accident. Initial cranial computed tomography demonstrated diffuse cerebral edema without a large surgical mass lesion. After initial stabilization, the patient remained deeply sedated with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 6 prior to sedation, bilaterally reactive pupils, and persistent MAP of 67 mmHg.TCD examination of the MCA shows reduced FVd, with a diastolic velocity of 14 cm/s and a PI of 1.5, suggesting an unfavorable downstream perfusion pattern.Bedside echocardiography demonstrates preserved ventricular function and no clear evidence of preload responsiveness, while lung ultrasound reveals diffuse B lines, arguing against additional fluid administration. Norepinephrine is therefore titrated in 5 mmHg increments of mean arterial pressure.

At a MAP of 77 mmHg, FVd increases to 19 cm/s and PI decreases to 1.2. At 83 mmHg, FVd rises further to 23 cm/s with a PI of 1.0, without meaningful additional improvement at higher pressures. In this scenario, TCD does not determine the choice of vasopressor and does not replace invasive monitoring. However, it helps identify a MAP range in which cerebral flow dynamics appear more favorable and supports prioritizing vasopressor titration rather than further fluid administration.

Multimodal integration, limitations, and future perspectives of Doppler-guided pressure management

TCD should be interpreted within a multimodal monitoring strategy that integrates neurological examination, systemic hemodynamics, neuroimaging, and advanced neuromonitoring techniques. In many neurocritical care units, Doppler findings are considered combined with ICP monitoring, brain tissue oxygenation (PbtO₂), continuous electroencephalography (EEG), and indices of cerebrovascular autoregulation. Each modality provides complementary information about different aspects of cerebral physiology, including perfusion pressure, oxygen delivery, metabolic activity, and neuronal function.

No single monitoring technique can fully characterize the complex pathophysiology of ABI. ICP monitoring informs intracranial dynamics, EEG detects cortical dysfunction and nonconvulsive seizures, and brain tissue oxygenation reflects regional oxygen delivery and consumption. Systemic hemodynamic monitoring provides information on upstream determinants of cerebral perfusion. Within this integrated approach, TCD contributes a dynamic perspective by providing real-time information about cerebral blood flow velocity patterns and their response to hemodynamic interventions.

Limitations and current evidence gaps

TCD also has important limitations that should be recognized. Doppler-derived indices do not provide direct measurements of ICP or cerebral blood flow and must therefore be interpreted within the broader physiological and clinical context.

Technical and practical constraints also exist. TCD remains an operator dependent technique, and reliable acquisition of Doppler signals requires adequate training and experience. In addition, adequate insonation of the MCA is not possible in all patients because of insufficient acoustic windows. This issue is particularly common in elderly individuals or in patients with thick temporal bones, where the absence of a suitable transtemporal window may prevent reliable Doppler assessment. Even when insonation is feasible, signal quality and reproducibility may vary depending on probe positioning and patient specific anatomical factors.

Important limitations also arise from the current evidence base. Although many studies have demonstrated physiological associations between Doppler parameters and cerebral hemodynamics, high quality prospective trials evaluating Doppler guided hemodynamic strategies remain limited. Much of the available literature focuses on diagnostic correlations or physiological observations rather than outcome driven therapeutic interventions. Consequently, although Doppler monitoring offers valuable insight into cerebral circulation, its role in guiding specific treatment targets has not yet been definitively established.

Future investigations should therefore focus on integrating Doppler derived signals with other forms of continuous neuromonitoring. One promising direction involves combining TCD parameters with additional physiological data, such as ICP derived indices including the pressure reactivity index (PRx) [29, 30] or Doppler based measures of autoregulation such as the mean flow index (Mx) [31, 32], to obtain a more comprehensive representation of cerebrovascular physiology. Emerging technologies, including automated waveform analysis and machine learning based modeling [33], may further improve the extraction of clinically meaningful information from noninvasive signals and refine estimation of cerebral perfusion dynamics.

Ultimately, the greatest potential of TCD lies not in replacing established monitoring modalities but in complementing them. By providing real time information on cerebral blood flow velocity patterns, TCD may help bridge the gap between systemic hemodynamic management and cerebral physiology. When integrated with other monitoring techniques, this approach may facilitate the development of individualized perfusion targets tailored to each patient’s cerebrovascular condition, moving beyond fixed pressure thresholds toward a more physiology-driven model of neurocritical care.

Conclusion

TCD offers a practical bedside method to observe how cerebral circulation responds to changes in systemic pressure. It can provide a physiological perspective on whether cerebral perfusion is likely to benefit from further MAP optimization. Although this approach remains primarily physiologically driven, it highlights the potential role of Doppler monitoring in refining individualized perfusion strategies in neurocritical care. Further research should determine whether incorporating these signals into clinical decision-making can translate into improved patient outcomes.
