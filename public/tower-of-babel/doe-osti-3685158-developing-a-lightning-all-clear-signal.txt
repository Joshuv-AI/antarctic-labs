 Executive Summary
Lightning safety is a major concern at the Savannah River Site (SRS). Notifications are provided when 3-strikes
occur onsite within a 15-minute period. It is generally left up to individual contractors at SRS to determine
when it is safe for workers to return to outside work, generally following a 3-hour period of no lightning, after
the last lightning strike occurs. This work tries to improve safe return to work decisions by developing a
reliable all-clear signal using an electric field mill. The best signal used thresholds from multiple electric field
values to provide an average delay for return to work of 2 hours and 13 minutes. This average was heavily
influenced by lingering storms in the area or missing data from power or network outages. One event out of
98 signaled the all-clear prior to another event occurring less than 60 minutes later. Regardless, 62% of the all-
clear signals had delay times less than the mean and 72% had delay times less than the current 3-hour delay
after the last strike. This provides better worker safety for returning to work than 30 minutes after the last
lightning strike (e.g. OSHA Lightning Safety) and improves the delay time from the current 3-hour delay.

1.0 Introduction
Lightning poses a risk to outdoor workers but is often difficult to predict. In general, forecasts
can provide information about when conditions will be favorable for thunderstorms but do not
accurately predict timing or location of lightning strikes. Conditions favorable to produce
lightning can also vary by location, season, and synoptic forcing. In the southeastern United
States, lightning is driven by two major processes: convective systems driven by localized
surface heating, mainly in the summertime [1]; and synoptic frontal systems that generate
strong vertical motions, mainly in the spring. Due to the general climate of the southeastern
United States, frontal induced and airmass generated convection are possible throughout the
year. This leads to work stoppages to ensure the safety of the workers, at a cost to the
employers and their efficiency towards the ability to complete projects. At the Savannah River
Site (SRS), companies have the capability to warn workers about incoming lightning storms but
often lack the ability to determine when it is safe to return to work. Recommended lightning
safety standards suggest waiting a minimum of 30 minutes after that last flash of lightning or
last sound of thunder before returning outside [2]. However, anecdotal experience indicates
this doesn’t guarantee another storm is not approaching.

SRS is a large complex with multiple companies working to fulfill government contracts, with
many of the workers completing outside tasks. SRS experiences more than 3000 cloud-to-
ground lightning strikes per year that put outside workers at risk [3]. Managing worker safety
while maintaining productivity is a priority for companies. Currently, SRS warns workers and
companies about the approach of lightning storms impacting SRS when three cloud-to-ground
strikes are detected onsite, or within a buffer area (typically 4.4 miles) around the site, within a
15-minute period. Presently, no true all-clear signal is provided but rather the companies advise
waiting three hours after the last cloud-to-ground lightning strike on site (or within the buffer
area) to return to work. The goal of this research is to develop a method using an electric field
mill that can provide an objective signal for safe conditions to return to work.

An electric field mill (EFM) measures the electric field present in the atmosphere [4]. It is a tool
for enhancing thunderstorm safety by providing real-time data on the atmospheric electric
field, crucial for monitoring and predicting lightning activity [5-7]. On a calm and clear day, the
atmospheric field is driven by the ionosphere. However, as storms develop, electrical charge
separates in the storm updraft through motions of ice particles and water droplets. As the
storm moves toward or builds over the EFM, the field becomes increasingly negative
(strengthens), while lightning discharges often flip the field to positive and back to negative. As
the storm dissipates or moves away from the area, the electric field eventually returns to a
stable state as existed before the storm. Generally, EFMs are used to determine increasing
potential for lightning with incoming storms [6, 8] or study the electrical discharge of lightning
into the electric field [9, 10].

 1

Previous work using EFM data at SRS investigated the use of the EFM to provide earlier (initial)
warnings for potential lightning events [11, 12]. The prior investigations were limited to electric
field averages, maxima and minima in each 15-minute period. Here, we investigate the use of
an EFM to identify a possible all-clear signal for safe return to work after electrified storms at
SRS. In 2022, electric field data collection (average, maximum and minimum) was reconfigured
to be recorded at 1-minute intervals. These changes provide a slightly different approach to the
thresholds used previously for detecting increased lightning potential.

2.0 Methods
This work uses an EFS 1000 Series Electric Field Mill [13, 14] (EFM) located near the
northwestern edge of SRS. While the EFM measures the state of the electric field at 10 Hz, only
summaries of these measurements were recorded at less frequent intervals, keeping the
maximum, minimum, and mean values instead of all the 10Hz values. Because the electric field
shows little variation over short distances, an EFM measurement at a given location could be
indicative of a lightning storm anywhere within a wide radius. The EFM manufacturer suggests
7 miles [13] to be an effective operating range of the instrument. Originally, this data was
recorded in 15-minute intervals which was used in previous studies. In 2022, the EFM was
repaired after a motor failure that occurred in 2021, and the data logger was adjusted to save
data at 1-minute intervals. Cloud to ground lightning information for comparison to the EFM
data is provided by the National Lightning Detection Network (NLDN) [15].
A 2009-2020 15-minute dataset of recorded EFM data was originally used by Noble et al. [11]
for prediction of lightning activity within storms at SRS. An earlier study by Kabela & Parker
when the field mill sensor was originally installed in 2007 did this for a shorter period [12]. Both
studies experimented with using thresholds on the EFM data to separate times where lightning
was expected from times where clear conditions were expected. The mean and the minimum
values were useful for determining when the electric field has a powerful inversion indicating a
building or approaching potential thunderstorm or if lightning has discharged. Additionally, the
differences between mean values and the difference between maximum and minimum values
between samples point to times when the electric field undergoes rapid changes. While this
approach was adopted for lightning detection, it is also effective for determining when lightning
events end.
This work uses data from November 2022 to June 2024. To ensure similarities between the 15-
minute and 1-minute data sets, Figure 1 shows average of the difference between maximum
and minimum values, and average of the minimum values associated with the NLDN location of
each lightning strike in the data sets. The 15-minute data set has more strikes due to the longer
period of record (2009 to 2020). However, similarities exist between the data sets, especially
within the manufacturer’s recommended 7-mile radius. This empirical evaluation provides
evidence for EFM utility beyond the 7-mile range perhaps closer to 10-miles.

 2

The increased granularity of the 1-minute data makes these thresholds more susceptible to
noise and false positive predictions. To respond to this issue, we employ a ‘sliding window’
technique over the time-series EFM data. Instead of considering one single 1-minute data point
at a time, a 10-minute window of multiple consecutive 1-minute data is used, where the
threshold would have to be met for all samples in the window.

To evaluate a reliable method for detecting when a lightning event had ended and clear
conditions had returned, we examined the EFM data coinciding with each lightning event. For
the purposes of this study, a lightning event is a continuous series of lightning strikes from the
NLDN data within a 7-mile radius of the EFM where a gap of one hour without lightning
constitutes a separate event. For the 1-minute data, this included 98 lightning events with
1,180 individual lightning strikes.
A tested predictor using the thresholds would have a delay time between the last time lightning
struck and the first indication of clear conditions for each lightning event. The predictors were
also tested on EFM data during the lightning event to measure false predictions. A clear
prediction before the last lightning strike in an event is an error, and if the predictor were used
in practice, it would be a dangerous mistake. Even a correct prediction can be problematic if a

 3

second event occurs shortly after the clear prediction. We employed thresholds on three of the
fields of EFM data: the minimum threshold where the minimum electric field (V/m) is greater
than some value; the maximum minus minimum threshold where the difference between those
electric fields is less than some value; and the difference threshold where the difference in the
mean electric field values in the window are less than some value. While these thresholds were
tested individually, utilizing multiple thresholds were tested concurrently to identify the most
useful combination for an all-clear signal.

3.0 Results and Discussion
As expected, these average EFM indicator values quickly approach zero, or normal conditions,
following the end of a lightning event as demonstrated in Figure 2. The minimum electric field
approaches zero faster but has the most variability when compared to the difference between
maximum and minimum and the 10-minute mean. This indicates possible uncertainty in using a
threshold based only on this value. The average 10-minute mean values show a more gradual
decline while the average difference is steeper. Regardless, all seem to drop to background
between 30 to 60 minutes after the last lightning strike.

For the 98 lightning events, each threshold type was tested with various levels. Key test
conditions for performance include the delay time from the last strike to the all-clear signal (all-

clear delay), the number of false positives (zero or negative delays), and the number of times
there was less than 60 minutes to the next event. Due to the gap of at least one hour between

although did not factor into the testing plays an important role as to not prematurely returning
to work.
3.1 Individual Thresholds
Each threshold using minimum value of the electric field (Table 1) provided short delay times
for 5 of the 6 tested values, around 75 to 90 minutes. The 6th threshold (minimum electric field
greater than 200 V/m) is longer than the current 3-hour all-clear notification. However, these
thresholds, when taken in isolation have a 9-13% false positive rate and multiple events where
the all-clear would have been issued less than 60 minutes before the next event. This is likely
due to the uncertainty in only using this value as demonstrated by Figure 2. These factors
would be detrimental to personnel returning to work making this threshold an ineffective
predictor for the all-clear.

3.2 Combined Thresholds
Better results were obtained by combining multiple thresholds (Table 4) instead of relying on
just one. Using some of the better values of the individual thresholds provides zero false
positives and only one case where the next event is less than 60 minutes after the all-clear. The
delay in all-clear is comparable to individual delays and below the current 3-hour delay.
Requiring three thresholds to all be met for the same 10-minute sliding window makes the
predictor more robust against false positives for future unseen data compared to using a single
threshold.

3.4 Details from Combined Threshold
Figure 3 provides a more detailed distribution of the all-clear delay times for the best combined
threshold (min > -200 V/m AND max – min < 200 V/m AND difference < 45 V/m for a 10-minute
window). The histogram indicates the large skew from very long all-clear times. The median of
the distribution of the all-clear delay is 82 minutes where 59% of all-clear delays are less than
two hours, 62% are less than the mean (133 minutes), and 72% are less than the current 3-hour
all-clear delay time. Thus, using this combined threshold from EFM data usually provides
improvement over current notifications. Long time periods for all-clear notifications are acting
to skew the mean time where the detailed data shows improvements beyond just the mean.
Longer time periods for the all-clear are likely due to continued precipitation in the area but
insufficient electric field to continue to produce lightning for the given event. Additionally, a
few times were due to missing data due to power outages from facility maintenance.

This combined threshold produced no false positives for when the notification should be sent
but electrified clouds were still in the vicinity. However, it did indicate one case (1%) where the
next event occurred less than 60 minutes after the all-clear. For lightning events occurring after
the all-clear, 5 (5%) events had the next event less than 90 minutes after the all-clear, 10 (10%)
events had them less than 2 hours after, and 15 (15%) events had them less than 3 hours after.
Overall, these are low percentages for the time until the next event compared to the high
percentage of events with decreased all-clear delay time.

4.0 Conclusions
Using data from an electric field mill at SRS, multiple threshold types and values for safe return
to work were tested. The minimum electric field value provides shorter delays but many false
positives. The difference between the maximum and minimum field values improved on the
false positives but had longer delay times. Using a 10-minute sliding window of the variability in
the mean value was similar to the difference between the maximum and minimum. A mixture
of multiple thresholds was found to provide the most reasonable signal with no dangerous false
positives (notifying while storms were still electrified), a limited number of all-clear notifications
where the next event occurred less than 60 minutes later, and better delay time to the all-clear
notification than the current 3-hour period used by SRS. The average delay time to the all-clear
was 133 minutes but this was skewed by long times where convection and precipitation
continued in the area. Thus, 62% of the all-clear signals had delay times less than the mean and
72% had delay times notably less than the current 3-hour delay.

5.0 Recommendations and Future Work
Results from this study using one-minute data from the field mill provided increased resolution
over the previous 15-minute data. However, the data set was much more limited in duration.
With additional years of measurements, this approach can be retested to ensure similar
statistics. Regardless, these results provide sufficient evidence for implementation. For full
coverage at SRS, an additional (three) field mills would need to be strategically located [16]. For
future improvements, the higher resolution data can provide enhancement to the early
notification signal presented by Noble et al. [11].
