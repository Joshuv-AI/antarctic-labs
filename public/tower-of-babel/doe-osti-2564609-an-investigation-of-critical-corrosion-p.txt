1.0 Introduction
This report is a summary of an investigation on the effects that corrosion pitting has on the drop
test performance of SAVY-4000 nuclear material storage containers. It is recognized that these
containers are susceptible to pitting corrosion, and it is postulated that these corrosion features
may have a negative impact on the performance during some accident scenarios.

Six 5-quart SAVY-4000 containers exposed to 6.0 M gaseous HCl for between 152 days and 159
days, all of which exhibited pitting corrosion, were dropped from the M441.1 qualification
criteria of the maximum working or storage height. This height was defined within the SAVY-
4000 Safety Analysis Report to be 3.66 m or 12 feet [1]. The total mass of the test package was
at least 18.1 kg which corresponds to the maximum package weight allowed in a 5Qt SAVY
container as well as that tested during initial design qualification [1]. Each container was
dropped once. The target impact location was selected for each container based on a visual
examination of the corrosion on or near the bottom radius of the containers. The bottom radius
was selected based on the expectation that maximum deformation would be achieved with this
impact location. Before each drop, a helium leak test was performed to confirm that the container
was sealed and did not have any leak paths before the container insult. After drop testing of each
container, helium leak testing was performed to evaluate the performance of the given container.

These drop tests were intended to evaluate containers that exhibited significant pit-type corrosion
and to attempt to determine a threshold for pit size that would result in a potential release of
nuclear material during an accident scenario drop. The concentration of hydrogen chloride (HCl)
used in these experiments is believed to be significantly greater than what is present inside of an
in-service nuclear material storage container and was meant to accelerate behavior seen during
container surveillance [2].

The drop test system used for these tests is a major component of the Respirable Release
Fraction Measurement Chamber (RRFMC), which gives the end-user the ability to record and
analyze high-speed video and photography and if needed to gather aerosol mass release during a
drop. In this report the principal issue is the physical deformation of the 5Qt SAVY-4000
container. Analytical test powder was not included in the experimental payloads and therefore
there were no aerosol mass release measurements made in this series of testing.

2.0 Methodology
 Atmospheric Exposure
The Science & Engineering team within the Materials Recovery & Recycle (PSD-4) group
performs experimental work in a cold lab environment allowing for non-radiological
experiments, without the risk of contamination. Within this lab, corrosive solutions can be
handled and stored allowing for prolonged exposures to specimens.

This test used six 5-quart SAVY-4000 containers exposed to the same environmental conditions
with slight differences in exposure duration between containers. A flask containing 200mL of
6M HCl was placed at the bottom of each container approximately in the center, as shown in

Figure 1. Each SAVY-4000 container filter was plugged using a hermetic cap, which allowed the
relative humidity to remain constant at approximately 60% RH. Each lid was then placed onto
the container body to seal the container with the flask inside.

In addition to the flask containing solution, a sample holder was placed inside of the bottom of
each SAVY. Each sample holder (Figure 1) had three SAVY wall samples attached to it, with the
intent to monitor the progress of corrosion. Samples were placed at varying elevations along the
holder to evaluate the stratification of corrosive gases. The corrosion exposure was conducted for
a minimum of 152 days (0.42 years) to a maximum of 159 days (0.44 years). The relative
humidity and temperature were recorded within one of the containers via a modified SAVY lid,
such that a probe could be inserted into the headspace. These measurements were made over the
duration of the exposure.

 Drop Testing
The RP-SVS Radiation Instrumentation and Calibration (RIC) team maintains an Aerosol
Engineering Facility (AEF) at TA-03-0130. The building contains a LANL-designed drop tower
system (Figure 2), known as the RRFMC (Respirable Release Fraction Measurement
Chamber), which uses a Lansmont PDT80 to raise test articles and release them remotely (
Figure 3). This chamber is a closed loop wind tunnel (Figure 4) capable of filtering air entering the
drop region of the system and measuring the aerosol concentration downstream from the drop
region. Although aerosol was not measured in this series of tests, the drop region was used to
facilitate the simulation of an accident scenario. The RRFMC system is operated at LANL in the
TA03-0130 building according to the Technical Procedure RP-SVS-RIC-TP-100 [3].

These experiments were performed according to “RP-SVS-RIC-TP-100 Rev 4.0 Respirable
Release Fraction Measurement Chamber RRFMC (drop tower) Procedure”. This provides
instruction for operating a facility to drop test packages in TA03-0130-103. The methods
described are based on ASTM D5276 (Standard Test Method for Drop Test of Loaded
Containers by Free Fall) and DOE-HDBK-3010-94 (Airborne release fractions/rates and
respirable fractions for nonreactor nuclear facilities). For each of the six drop tests, test data
summary sheets were generated where payload, gross weights, drop height and impact
orientations were recorded.

For this series of drop tests an orientation was selected that would maximize the strain to the thin
wall of the container body. The intent was to induce regions of high stress so that the effect of
the pits generated during corrosion exposure could be evaluated. Each container was loaded with
a payload of 14.7 kg (Figure 6), which brought the gross weight of each container being tested to
at least 18.1 kg, the maximum allowable weight in TA55-DOP-091 [4]. Due to rigging materials
and configuration, the total weights were slightly higher than the target weight (Figure 7).

The drop height for each of the six container drops was set to at least 12 feet. The containers
were raised to the drop height and allowed to come to rest at the top of the drop tower. Once the
containers were no longer swinging in the drop tower, they were released using a Sea Catch(R)
toggle release actuated pneumatically from a remote location.

Each of the containers being tested were loaded with the same payload and then a helium leak
test was performed. Once the drop test was complete a post-drop helium leak test was performed
to evaluate whether a breach in the container wall occurred or not.

 Post Drop Corrosion Characterization
Containers were visually inspected to locate suitable regions for destructive examination. The
center-of-gravity over bottom corner drop orientations induce deformation up to 2” from the
  Rev. 1, May 1, 2025                                                                    Page 8 of 17

impact location. A sample comprising the bottom surface and side wall up to 2” from the center
of the bottom radius was sectioned from the container using a handheld bandsaw. One sample
was taken from each of the 6 exposed containers for characterization.

Specimens were cleaned in a 2M nitric acid solution for one hour at 60 °C. Samples were
enclosed in a glass jar and placed in an ultrasonic bath to agitate specimens during the cleaning
process. The bottom surface and sidewall of each specimen were characterized using a Keyence
VK-X3000 laser confocal microscope (LCM). Surface height maps were obtained for each
coupon up for the entire region permitted by the optics on the system, following the practices
established in [5].

A separate set of specimens were extracted for metallographic examination. Coupons from the
deformed region and weld area of one container (091205159) were extracted using a similar
approach as above. These were used to examine the possible influence of both large transient
tensile stresses and residual stresses on the corrosion-induced defect morphology, similar to
observations in a previous study [6]. Images of the coupons sectioned from the deformed region
are provided in Figure 8. Note that only coupons A, B, and C were selected for further
examination.

 Figure 8 - (a) Marked locations for container sectioning from the deformed region of
 container 091205159 and (b) corresponding coupons.

Specimens were mounted in epoxy and ground using success SiC grit papers to a final grit of
P4000 (5 μm). Specimens were further polished using diamond solutions of 3 μm and 1 μm
particles. All specimens were examined using a Keyence VHX-7000 optical microscope to
obtain cross-section images of the coupons. It is important to note that the SAVY-4000 has a
large hoop and axial residual tensile stress near the weld. Weld coupons were oriented such that
the surface normal to the circumferential direction of the container was accessible during
polishing to better visualize cracking and registration relative to the weld [7,8].

3.0 Results
 Corrosion Observations
Of the six SAVYs used in the environmental corrosion exposures, three were exposed for 152
days, while the other three were exposed for 159 days. The exposures had to be stopped after
these periods as two of the containers had developed through wall pits. The resulting corrosion
from these exposures do have some differences from what is observed in container surveillance
(Figure 9). Based on the non-destructive visual examination it was evident that the corrosion had
a less dense distribution of larger diameter pits than what is seen in SAVY surveillance.

Figure 9. Image on the left shows the result of the corrosive environment during the cold lab
exposure, while the picture on the right shows typical corrosion seen during SAVY surveillance.

The coupons that were placed in the container were not used for characterization. Visual
observations between the coupons and corresponding locations on the container wall showed
significant discrepancies. The coupons showed significantly less corrosion features on the
surface in nearly all containers.

 Drop Testing
Due to the presence of through-wall pitting, two of the containers had to be inspected to locate
the leaks so that those leaks could be obstructed before testing. Epoxy resin was used to fill the
leak path such that the container could pass the helium leak test prior to drop testing. In some
cases, multiple through wall pits were present in one container. In one case, one of the containers
continued to leak after filling all the visible leak paths. To locate this, a positive pressure leak
test was conducted while the container was submersed in water. After a few minutes a single
bubble was seen developing on the side of the container wall (Figure 10). Once this leak was
identified T-REX® tape was placed over it and the helium leak test was conducted again on this
container. Ultimately, all six containers passed the pre-drop test leak test after being loaded with
the payload. The pre-drop helium leak rate report for 091205138 is missing from our dataset as
the report could not be found at the time of this report’s generation. All six containers passed the
post-drop helium leak rate, with leakage rates that were orders of magnitude smaller than the
allowable rate (Figure 11).

 Post Drop Corrosion Analysis

Pit Distributions

Regions that were pitted and undeformed were sectioned from each container and analyzed on
the VK-X3000 LCM. Shown below in Figure 12 is an overview of the data compiled from the
specimens across all six containers. In total, over 1,070 pits were recorded across the samples,
with a significant majority exhibiting max depths in the range of 20 to 60 μm. In addition,
highlighted by Figure 12(b) are the few outlier pits that have severely disproportionate pit aspect
ratios, where the diameters range from two to three times the depth. For the analysis it was
assumed that every pit had a direct line of sight to the deepest point and that undercutting did not
occur.

  Rev. 1, May 1, 2025                                                                   Page 12 of 17

Figure 12 - (a) Maximum pit depth distribution and (b) pit depth and diameter values for
all corroded containers.
Shown below in Table 2 are the maximum pit depths, average pit aspect ratio, and pit area
fraction as calculated for each analyzed container. The included error values were derived
following the recommendations outlined in a prior report [9]. The deepest pit measured during
the analysis was 493.057 ± 0.25 μm, which was observed on container body 091205159B. Of
note is that with the addition of the error value, the maximum possible pit depth would be 493.3
μm, which is approximately 82 % of the container’s nominal body thickness (~600 μm) relative
to the bottom wall [1]. Another highlight from Table 2 is the pit area fraction calculated for each
sample, which ranges from 0.0005 to 0.0064. This aligns with observations made that the
corrosion occurred in very low densities across the containers.

The averaged pit aspect ratio and area fraction align closely with pitting observations in Hagan
and SAVY surveillance containers examined to date [6,10-12]. We note that the 20x microscope
objective footprint precluded measurement of the region up to 5 mm from the bottom radius.
The statistics noted in Figure 12 and Table 2 are derived from measurements made on the region
15 mm to 30 mm from the bottom radius and fully contain the region subject to transient loading
during drop testing.

Metallography

Figure 13 provides an example of pitting observed in the deformed region of container. Without
a method to mark the location of pits, three sections were mounted in epoxy and polished
simultaneously. Specimens were periodically examined for the presence of pits in the deformed
region and further polished when visible. The higher magnification image of the pit shows a
complex structure within the pit with clear undercutting of the surface. The most salient
conclusion to derive from the micrograph is the absence of cracking at the bottom of the pit.
Additional pits observed in the deformed region, as shown in Figure 14 do not show cracking at
the bottom of the pits as well. It is reasonable to conclude from these images that the stress
intensity factor for each defect is not sufficiently large to induce cracking during a drop test.

a large pit present.

Figure 15 showcases four pits observed in the region near the weld of the SAVY container. Two
of the pits, (a) and (c) show no notable features beyond slight undercutting of the surface. The
pit in Figure 15(b) shows the presence of cracking at the bottom of the pit that is aligned with
observations made in a previous study [13], with cracks less than 25 μm deep. Note that the
nature of the feature (i.e. inter vs. transgranular) requires additional metallography to resolve.

The pit in Figure 15(d) shows a more extensive case of surface undercutting. Here, the line of
sight to the bottom of the pit is not the deepest part of the pit with up to a 15 μm depth
discrepancy. This is a potential concern with the current depth characterization modality as the
accuracy is contingent on an unobstructed line of sight to the deepest portion of the pit. Follow-
on efforts to establish a correlation between line of sight depth to true depth for pits of various
sizes and/or an evaluation of alternative approaches to characterizing pit depth will be
considered.

Figure 15 - Images of pits near the SAVY weld (<5 mm).

4.0 Conclusion
With the above summarized work, it can be concluded that pit depths up to 82% through the
SAVY-4000 wall, with the distribution noted in Figure 12 did not result in a failure during a
maximum postulated drop scenario. Furthermore, the distribution of pits (pit area, pit aspect

  Rev. 1, May 1, 2025                                                                   Page 15 of 17

ratio) averaged in the region between 15 mm and 30 mm from the bottom radius align closely
with the observation from container surveillance of storage containers. It can also be stated that
pits located in the deformation region of the container did not form cracks. This observation does
not imply that drop-induced cracking cannot form but does indicate that for the pits produced in
this study and the locations identified on the container, cracking does not appear post-drop.
