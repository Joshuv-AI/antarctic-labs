Small modular reactors (SMRs) and microreactors are entering the U.S. distribution grid as syn-
chronous generation on feeders designed for loads and inverter-based distributed energy resources
(DERs). No existing cybersecurity training program addresses this intersection of nuclear operations,
DER management, and operational technology security. As subcontractor to Iowa State University
on the CyDERMS Center, Argonne analyzed the relevant standards and training landscape, trans-
lated the resulting gaps into a twelve-objective competency framework across distribution-operator
and graduate-analyst role tracks, and built a containerized training lab using a ∼400-bus composite
grid model behind a realistically simulated Modbus TCP SCADA stack. The analysis isolates the
balance-of-plant / energy-management-system (BOP/EMS) boundary as the critical jurisdictional
seam where, as of March 2026, neither NRC nor NERC CIP cleanly claims cybersecurity responsi-
bility for distribution-connected SMRs. The framework maps each objective across NIST CSF 2.0,
ISA/IEC 62443, NIST NICE Task–Knowledge–Skill statements, and NRC RG 5.71 awareness-and-
training controls. The training lab implements operator-recognition assessment scenarios spanning
grid-side disturbances and telemetry-layer anomalies.

Executive Summary
The CyDERMS Center is a regional cybersecurity center led by Iowa State University with
Argonne National Laboratory as a subcontractor. Argonne entered the project late in the
original period of performance, and the scope of Argonne’s work was refocused on a sin-
gle coherent thread: the cybersecurity workforce implications of integrating small modular
reactors (SMRs) and microreactors into DER-rich distribution systems. This report docu-
ments that work, which corresponds to subtasks 5.1, 5.2, and 5.3 of the revised Argonne
Statement of Work (originally numbered 6.1 and 6.2 in the Iowa State Statement of Project
Objectives).
 The core finding is that the SMR/DER/microgrid intersection sits in a gap that no ex-
isting cybersecurity training program covers. Standards exist in four largely independent
domains (governance, OT/ICS, DER/grid, and nuclear), and existing training programs
are either sector-agnostic (NICE), engineering-oriented (CIE), or specialized to one do-
main (SANS, C2M2). None addresses the hybrid operator-cyber role that fleet-managed,
remotely-operated SMRs on distribution feeders will require. The most consequential oper-
ational gap is at the balance-of-plant / energy-management-system (BOP/EMS) boundary,
where as of March 2026 neither U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission cyber authority nor
NERC Critical Infrastructure Protection (CIP) authority unambiguously claims jurisdic-
tion for distribution-connected SMRs.
 To address this gap, the project produced three coordinated artifacts:

 (1) A structured survey of the cybersecurity standards and training landscape that
 names six workforce and curriculum gaps and analyzes their operational implica-
 tions at the BOP/EMS boundary (§4).
 (2) A twelve-objective competency framework organized across two role tracks (distri-
 bution grid operator, graduate-level analyst) and three proficiency tiers, with each
 objective mapped onto NIST CSF 2.0 functions, ISA/IEC 62443 zones and conduits,
 NIST NICE Task–Knowledge–Skill statements, and NRC RG 5.71 awareness-and-
 training controls (§5).
 (3) A containerized training lab (the CyDERMS Notebook) built on a ∼400-bus com-
 posite grid model with embedded SMR and microreactor generation, behind a re-
 alistically simulated Modbus TCP SCADA protocol stack, with twelve progressive
 Jupyter notebooks and a library of six grid-side disturbance and protocol-layer
 anomaly scenarios that directly implement the framework’s operator-recognition
 assessment objectives (§6).

1.3 What Is and Is Not in Scope
The work covered by this report explicitly addresses cybersecurity workforce competencies
for distribution grid operators and graduate-level cybersecurity analysts who interact with
SMR/microreactor generation on or near distribution feeders. It does not address:
 ∙ NRC-licensed reactor operator qualifications, reactor instrumentation and control
 (I&C) cybersecurity training, or any aspect of nuclear safety system cybersecurity.
 ∙ Classified threat intelligence; all attack scenarios are derived from publicly available
 vulnerability research and the project’s own protocol-stack analysis.
 ∙ Reactor physics, thermohydraulics, or balance-of-plant internals beyond the behav-
 ioral signatures that distribution-side instrumentation can observe.
 These boundaries are deliberate. The training audience is the distribution-side workforce
that will be operationally exposed to SMRs as they enter service, and the design constraint
is that the training must be deliverable without privileged access to reactor design data.

2 Background
This section establishes the three concept domains the rest of the report draws on: the
SMR and microreactor generation class, the operational reality of distribution-grid inte-
gration of synchronous nuclear sources, and the established threat model for cyber attacks
on industrial control systems. Readers familiar with any of these domains may skim the
corresponding subsection.

2.1 Small Modular Reactors and Microreactors
Small modular reactors (50–300 MW) and microreactors (1–20 MW) represent a departure
from the traditional commercial nuclear archetype on multiple operational axes simultane-
ously. Where conventional nuclear plants connect at transmission voltage, operate with ded-
icated onsite staffing models numbering in the hundreds, and exist within well-established
regulatory and cybersecurity boundaries built around large light-water reactors (LWRs),
SMRs and microreactors are designed to connect at sub-transmission and distribution levels,
operate in fleets with reduced or remote staffing, and integrate into microgrid architectures
alongside photovoltaic arrays, battery storage, and conventional DERs [1, 2]. Throughout
this report, “SMR” and “microreactor” are used in their literal size senses; where a claim
applies more strongly to one class than the other (e.g., distribution-voltage interconnection
or fully unattended operation, which are most directly contemplated for microreactors),
the text says so explicitly. Figures 1 and 2 contrast the conventional light-water reactor
(LWR) layout with the integral SMR layout. The structural difference is consequential: in

  Three characteristics of this generation class drive the cybersecurity training problem:

  Fleet operation and software monoculture. SMRs are only cost-effective when built in
volume; commercial deployments envision dozens to hundreds of identical units [4]. The
same software stack and firmware will run across the fleet, which reduces unit cost but
turns supply-chain firmware risk and patch management into safety-critical operational
concerns at fleet scale.

  Remote and reduced-staff operation. The remote-operation model applies most strongly
to microreactors, which generally envision remote monitoring with minimal or no onsite op-
erating staff (e.g., DoD’s Project Pele [5], Westinghouse’s eVinci [6]); the I&C and commu-
nication challenges this creates are an active area of nuclear research in their own right [7].
Larger SMR designs such as TerraPower’s Natrium [8] and GE Hitachi’s BWRX-300 [9]

2.2 Distribution-Grid Integration of Synchronous Nuclear Sources
From the distribution operator’s perspective, an SMR or microreactor is a synchronous
machine that behaves operationally unlike anything else on the feeder. Four operational
consequences matter for training design. (Throughout this section and the rest of the report,
the BOP/EMS interface refers to the control-system boundary between the plant’s I&C
system, which actuates the balance-of-plant equipment — turbine, generator, condenser,
feedwater — and the external utility energy management system or microgrid controller,
which sees the unit only as a dispatchable grid asset.)

  Asymmetric ramp behavior. Nuclear sources change power output slowly compared to gas
turbines and inverter-based DERs. Modern light-water reactors can operate flexibly once
or twice per day in the range of 100% to 50–25% of rated power with a ramp rate of up to
about 5% of rated power per minute [11]; a peaking gas turbine moves the same fraction
in seconds. When grid conditions require fast compensation, the SMR cannot provide it,
and the operator’s mental model for “expected response from generation” must adapt.

  Trip-recovery asymmetry. A reactor trip is not a fast-restart event. Recovery windows
range from minutes-to-hours for some microreactor designs whose design intent is rapid
restart, to days for larger SMRs subject to xenon-induced restart delay [12]; in either case
the unit is unavailable for the duration of the recovery window regardless of grid need. A
distribution operator who sees a microreactor go offline cannot assume it will be back in
the next dispatch cycle.

  Altered fault-current and protection coordination. A synchronous generator on a distri-
bution feeder contributes fault current of a magnitude and duration that the feeder’s pro-
tective relaying was not coordinated for. Reverse-power tripping, anti-islanding logic, and
bidirectional protection schemes become operative where they previously were not. Cyber
events that change SMR output, breaker state, or protection settings can interact with
existing feeder protection in ways that operators trained on conventional inverter-based
DER behavior would not anticipate.

  Cross-jurisdictional coordination. The SMR or microreactor on a distribution feeder may
be owned and operated by a non-utility entity (data center operator, military base, indus-
trial campus). The distribution operator’s normal coordination channels with bulk-power
generation owners may not exist; the balancing authority may have no direct line to the reac-
tor’s control room. Cyber events that cross the BOP/EMS boundary therefore traverse not
just a technical interface but also an organizational and regulatory one. As of the analysis

2.3 The ICS Cyber Threat Model
The cybersecurity threat to industrial control systems is no longer hypothetical. Stuxnet es-
tablished in 2010 that targeted, nation-state-grade malware can manipulate cyber-physical
systems through programmable logic controllers without immediate operator awareness,
masking its effects through false telemetry while inducing equipment-damaging behav-
ior [15, 16]. The 2015 Ukraine grid attack demonstrated that the same threat class applies to
electric power systems specifically: coordinated SCADA-level intrusion across three distri-
bution utilities resulted in roughly 225,000 customers losing power for hours, with operators
locked out of their consoles and breakers operated remotely by adversaries [17]. The follow-
on 2016 Industroyer / CRASHOVERRIDE attack on a Ukrainian transmission substation
demonstrated a fully automated, protocol-aware grid attack capability targeting multiple
ICS protocols simultaneously [18].
 The threat model relevant to SMRs on distribution feeders is the union of these capabili-
ties applied to a generation class with high consequence potential, geographically dispersed
fleet operation, and ambiguous jurisdictional ownership at the grid interface. It is the ab-
sence of training for the resulting operational scenarios that this project addresses.

3 Approach
The work was structured in four sequential phases:

  Phase 1: Orientation. The principal investigator delivered a structured orientation brief
to the team covering grid fundamentals, conventional and advanced reactor operation,
the SMR generation class, and the cybersecurity differences SMRs introduce relative to
traditional nuclear. The purpose was to establish a shared technical vocabulary between
team members with backgrounds spanning OT cybersecurity, power systems, and nuclear
engineering.

  Phase 2: Reading groups. Three internal reading-group sessions were held in early March
2026, each led by a different team member. Session 1 examined the cybersecurity regu-
latory landscape for SMRs (NRC, NIST, NERC, and CSA standards) and identified the
need to focus narrowly on distribution-connected SMR scenarios rather than attempting

14 Blakely et al.

full NERC CIP coverage. Session 2 reviewed publicly available cybersecurity training pro-
grams and workforce frameworks, identifying NIST NICE, NRC RG 5.71, INL CIE, and
NEI 13-10 as the four candidate foundations and noting that none addresses SMR-specific
operational scenarios. Session 3 examined existing cybersecurity strategies for advanced
reactors, including the NRC draft regulatory guide DG-5075/RG 5.96 [19] and Sandia Na-
tional Laboratories’ work on defensive architectures and the SMR Cybersecurity Exposure
Framework [20, 21]. Each session produced a written summary memo that fed directly into
D5.1.

  Phase 3: Whitepapers. The reading-group output was synthesized into two whitepapers.
D5.1 catalogued the standards landscape and existing training programs across four do-
mains and identified six workforce/curriculum gaps. D5.2 translated those gaps into a
twelve-objective competency framework organized by role and proficiency tier, with each
objective mapped onto multiple federal frameworks simultaneously to address the fragmen-
tation gap structurally.

 Phase 4: Prototype lab. The lab implementation built on the open-source CITADEL grid-
simulator (originally developed under the DOE CESER ZT-CARD project at Argonne) by
adding a composite grid topology with SMR and microreactor generation, a behavioral
reactor transient model, four containerized RTUs polling Modbus TCP registers, a ground-
truth bypass channel for anomaly detection, twelve progressive training notebooks, and a
library of disturbance and telemetry-anomaly scenarios implementing the D5.2 operator-
recognition assessment objectives.
 The remainder of this report describes the output of Phases 3 and 4 in detail.

4 Cybersecurity Landscape and Education Gaps
This section summarizes deliverable D5.1: a structured survey of the publicly available
cybersecurity standards, frameworks, and training programs relevant to SMR integration
into DER and microgrid environments, organized by what each artifact covers, what it
does not cover, and where the resulting gaps fall. The headline finding (§4.4) is that the
balance-of-plant / energy-management-system (BOP/EMS) boundary is the institutional
and technical crux at which six workforce and curriculum gaps converge.
  To set the stage: the U.S. nuclear cybersecurity regulatory landscape during this analysis
period was operating in two parallel regimes. The currently-binding regime is 10 CFR 73.54,
implemented through RG 5.71 as the staff-endorsed compliance pathway — a regime de-
signed around large LWRs with traditional staffing patterns and largely air-gapped digital
architectures. A second, emerging regime for advanced reactors comprises 10 CFR Part 53,

the proposed 10 CFR 73.110, and the draft regulatory guide DG-5075; none of these had
been finalized as of March 2026. The grid-side regulatory hooks are the NERC CIP stan-
dards, which by construction govern the bulk electric system rather than the distribution-
connected generation that the rest of this report discusses. The reader can keep these two
regimes in mind as the standards inventory below cites instruments from both.

4.1 The Standards Landscape Spans Four Independent Domains
Cybersecurity for an SMR-integrated microgrid cannot be governed by a single standard.
The relevant publicly available standards and frameworks fall into four domains that have
been developed largely independently:

  Governance and risk management. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 [22] provides
a cross-sector taxonomy of cybersecurity outcomes (Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond,
Recover, Govern) widely used as the organizing structure for energy-sector cybersecurity
programs. The DOE Cybersecurity Capability Maturity Model (C2M2) [23] narrows this to
the energy sector specifically, providing a four-level maturity indicator scale (MIL0–MIL3)
across ten cybersecurity domains.

  Operational technology / industrial control systems. NIST SP 800-82r3 [24] provides au-
thoritative guidance on securing OT environments including SCADA, EMS, and distributed
control systems. The ISA/IEC 62443 series [25, 26] defines security requirements for in-
dustrial automation and control systems across their lifecycle, and introduces the zone-
and-conduit architectural primitive that this work uses extensively. NIST SP 800-53r5 [27]
provides the underlying control catalog from which sector-specific requirements are derived.

  Distributed energy resources and bulk grid. IEEE 2030.5 [28] defines the application-
layer communication protocol for DERs interacting with utilities and aggregators. NERC
CIP [29, 30] is the mandatory cybersecurity standard set for the North American bulk
electric system; for the SMR-integrated distribution context, the most directly relevant
components are CIP-005 (electronic security perimeters) and CIP-007 (system security
management) at the generation–grid interconnection.

 Nuclear cybersecurity. NRC Regulatory Guide 5.71 [13] and the underlying 10 CFR
73.54 [14] establish binding cybersecurity requirements for NRC-licensed nuclear facilities,
including mandatory awareness, role-based, and insider-threat training (the AT-1 through
AT-4 controls). NEI 13-10 [31] is the industry-consensus methodology for assessing cyberse-
curity controls and identifying critical digital assets at nuclear plants. CSA N290.7:21 [32]
is the modern, lifecycle-based Canadian standard that, while not legally binding in the U.S.,

16 Blakely et al.

is the most current available framework specifically addressing cybersecurity for SMRs as
a generation class. The forthcoming risk-informed regime for advanced reactors comprises
10 CFR Part 53 [33], the proposed 10 CFR 73.110 [34], and the draft regulatory guide
DG-5075 [19]; as of the analysis period these had not been finalized.

  Why fragmentation is the actual problem. Each standard provides partial coverage, but no
single framework addresses the SMR + DER + microgrid intersection holistically. A train-
ing developer attempting to build a curriculum for that intersection must independently
synthesize requirements from multiple standards and reconcile their differing assumptions
about asset criticality, communication architecture, and operator role.

4.2 Existing Training Programs Are Either Too General or Too Specialized
Four publicly available training programs and workforce frameworks could plausibly serve
as the foundation for SMR-cyber training:

  Idaho National Laboratory’s Cyber-Informed Engineering (CIE) program [35]. integrates
cybersecurity into the engineering design process, emphasizing the physical consequences of
cyber events. CIE’s consequence-driven methodology and its digital literacy for engineers
pillar are uniquely relevant to SMRs because SMRs are still being designed; security-by-
design opportunities exist that are no longer available for legacy LWRs. CIE provides
methodology, but not operator-level training scenarios or SMR-specific scenario libraries.

 The SANS Institute ICS/OT cybersecurity training. provides hands-on training focused
on securing industrial control systems with realistic scenarios and incident response exer-
cises. It is widely recognized for developing practical skills in operational environments, but
it is not tailored to nuclear or SMR systems and does not address DER–SMR integration.
Cost is a significant accessibility barrier for individual operators.

  The NIST NICE Workforce Framework [36, 37]. defines standardized cybersecurity work
roles and the associated Task, Knowledge, and Skill (TKS) statements. It provides the struc-
tural template for any federal cybersecurity training program, but it is sector-agnostic.
Unlike the Department of Defense (which has a published DoD Cyber Workforce Frame-
work overlay [38]), the nuclear sector has no published mapping of NICE work roles to
nuclear-specific cyber positions.

  The DOE C2M2 program [23]. provides a structured organizational maturity assessment,
useful for utility cybersecurity program managers but not a training program for individual
practitioners.

 What is missing. None of these four addresses SMR cybersecurity specifically. None ad-
dresses the distribution grid operator interacting with SMR generation. None defines the hy-
brid operator-cyber role that SMR fleet operations will require. Two recent Sandia National
Laboratories reports begin to fill specific gaps in the broader advanced-reactor cybersecurity
literature: a defensive cybersecurity architecture for high-temperature gas-cooled reactors
(HTGRs, a class of advanced reactor) using event-tree analysis [20], and an SMR Cybersecu-
rity Exposure Framework that addresses modularity, cloud, and remote-operation risks [21].
Both are valuable inputs to training design but are not training programs themselves.

4.3 Six Workforce and Curriculum Gaps
The standards-and-training analysis identifies six workforce and curriculum deficiencies
that collectively define the problem space the rest of this work addresses:

 (1) No SMR-specific cybersecurity training programs. No existing program ad-
 dresses the operational context of SMRs and microreactors. RG 5.71 was written for
 large LWRs with traditional staffing and air-gapped architectures; it does not ad-
 dress fleet-operated SMRs at distribution voltage, remote monitoring configurations,
 or the software monoculture risk inherent in factory-built reactor fleets. The draft
 10 CFR Part 53 framework had not finalized cybersecurity training requirements
 as of the analysis period.
 (2) Lack of training for DER–SMR integration at the BOP/EMS boundary.
 Even where cybersecurity training exists for individual domains (nuclear operations,
 grid operations, OT/ICS security), no program addresses the integration boundary
 where these domains converge. Operators on both sides of this boundary lack train-
 ing in recognizing a cyber event on one side that manifests as an operational anomaly
 on the other.
 (3) Absence of hybrid operator–cybersecurity roles. NEI 13-10 assumes tradi-
 tional staffing where cybersecurity personnel and reactor operators are distinct roles
 with separate training pipelines. SMR and microreactor staffing models will require
 operators who can recognize cyber-induced anomalies in grid telemetry, execute
 incident response procedures at the BOP boundary, and coordinate with entities
 outside the traditional nuclear security perimeter. No training program prepares
 personnel for this hybrid function, and the NIST NICE framework does not include
 a nuclear-energy overlay or define roles specific to SMR operations.
 (4) Fragmentation between standards and training. The four-domain standards
 fragmentation extends to training: existing programs are either too general (NICE,

18 Blakely et al.

 C2M2) or too specialized in one domain (SANS for ICS, CIE for engineering design).
 A training developer must independently synthesize requirements with no reference
 architecture or competency model to guide the integration. This gap is structural
 rather than content-specific.
 (5) Lack of SMR-specific cyber incident scenarios. Effective training depends on
 realistic, domain-specific scenarios. Existing ICS attack taxonomies (e.g., MITRE
 ATT&CK for ICS [39]) cover general industrial control system threats but do not
 capture the nuance of synchronous nuclear generation on distribution feeders, where
 a compromised inverter setpoint on one feeder and an unscheduled reactor ramp
 on another may constitute a coordinated attack rather than independent faults [40].
 CIE methodology supports consequence reasoning but does not supply ready-to-use
 SMR/DER scenarios.
 (6) Limited training for remote and autonomous SMR operations. SMR and
 microreactor concepts envision geographically dispersed sites under remote monitor-
 ing or reduced onsite staffing. This hybridized model introduces remote communi-
 cation links, fleet management software, and centralized credential stores that have
 no LWR precedent. No current training program addresses how operators should
 verify generation status, authenticate communications, or coordinate incident re-
 sponse when the reactor operator is not physically collocated and may be managing
 multiple units simultaneously.

4.4 The BOP/EMS Boundary Is the Critical Jurisdictional Seam
The six gaps converge at one specific technical interface: the point at which reactor balance-
of-plant (BOP) controls hand off to the energy management system (EMS) or microgrid
controller. At this boundary, nuclear-domain cybersecurity requirements meet grid-domain
cybersecurity requirements, and as of March 2026 neither RG 5.71 nor NERC CIP unam-
biguously claims jurisdiction for distribution-connected SMRs. NRC regulatory authority
traditionally ends at the site boundary; NERC CIP applies to the bulk electric system; the
distribution-connected SMR sits in between. The result is a regulatory and training gap
that will need to be addressed administratively (through cooperative agreements between
NRC, NERC, and FERC) regardless of any technical solution.

4.5 Operational Implications at the Boundary
The workforce and curriculum gaps translate into a set of operational implications that
this project’s competency framework and training lab are designed to address:

Cybersecurity Workforce Training for SMR Integration into Distribution Grids 19

 ∙ Increased cyber-physical coupling. SMR integration creates tighter coupling
 between nuclear control systems and grid-connected assets. Disruptions in one do-
 main can propagate across others, affecting both operational reliability and safety
 functions.
 ∙ Expanded attack surface at system interfaces. The BOP/EMS boundary in-
 troduces additional communication pathways and data exchange interfaces between
 SMR systems and external grid or DER platforms, increasing exposure to external
 networks and interconnected services.
 ∙ Ambiguity in jurisdictional ownership. The misalignment between regulatory
 boundaries and system architecture creates unclear cybersecurity accountability,
 complicating incident detection, response, and coordination across domains.
 ∙ Cyber incidents manifest as operational anomalies. Cyber incidents in OT
 environments often appear as subtle changes in system behavior rather than ex-
 plicit security alerts. At the DER–SMR interface it becomes difficult to distinguish
 between equipment faults, communication issues, and malicious activity without
 explicit training in cross-domain anomaly recognition.
 ∙ Real-time decision-making under uncertainty. Interconnected SMR systems
 require operators to make rapid decisions during cyber events with limited or am-
 biguous information, balancing safety, reliability, and system stability under time
 constraints. Without clear cross-domain guidance the risk of delayed or suboptimal
 response is elevated.

5.2 Two Role Tracks
The framework defines two role tracks that reflect distinct workforce populations:

  Distribution grid operator (OPR). is the existing utility distribution operator who will en-
counter SMR and microreactor generation on their feeders as those units come into service.
The operator track emphasizes real-time recognition of cyber-induced anomalies, incident
response procedures, protocol-level interpretation skills (e.g., distinguishing a spoofed Mod-
bus register value from a real sensor fault), and cross-boundary coordination with reactor
BOP operators and balancing authorities under degraded communications.

 Graduate-level analyst (ANA). is the cybersecurity workforce population in academic
training pipelines (graduate students, junior analysts at utilities or vendors) who will design
and assess SMR-integrated cybersecurity programs. The analyst track emphasizes architec-
tural review (62443 zone/conduit design), consequence-driven risk ranking (CIE method-
ology), scenario development, workforce role design, and alternative-countermeasure justi-
fication when standard controls are impractical.
 A small foundational set of objectives applies to both tracks; the remaining objectives
are weighted toward one track or the other.

6 The CyDERMS Prototype Training Lab
This section documents deliverable D5.3: the CyDERMS Notebook, a containerized train-
ing environment in which trainees interact with a ∼400-bus composite grid model con-
taining SMR and microreactor generation, behind a realistically simulated Modbus TCP
SCADA protocol stack, through twelve progressive Jupyter notebooks. The lab implements
the assessment scenarios specified in the D5.2 competency framework (§5) and provides an
executable training delivery vehicle for an asset class not yet in operation on the U.S.
distribution grid.

6.1 Design Philosophy: A Grid-Visible Cyber Consequence Simulator
The CyDERMS Notebook is deliberately scoped as a grid-visible cyber consequence simula-
tor — not a reactor simulator, not a hardware-in-the-loop testbed, and not a high-fidelity
research platform. It is a small, self-contained training system designed to run on a single
workstation in containers, with the entire stack (grid simulator, RTUs, JupyterLab console)
brought up by a single command. The simulation boundary is the distribution operator’s
SCADA screen. Everything the operator can observe — voltage, power flow, breaker state,
regulator tap position, alarms — is modeled with engineering fidelity. Everything they can-
not observe — reactor thermohydraulics, neutron flux, coolant chemistry, balance-of-plant
internals — is abstracted to its electrical signature on the distribution side. The “EMS”
that appears throughout this report refers, depending on the operator’s architecture, ei-
ther to a traditional distribution EMS or to a distributed energy resource management
system (DERMS); the lab models the read/write semantics of that boundary rather than
a particular product.
  This boundary follows directly from the project scope. The training audience is the
distribution-side workforce; the training objective is grid resilience; and the operator’s
decision space is bounded by what they can see and control. Modeling reactor internals
would imply licensing-level fidelity that this tool does not (and should not) provide, and
that trainees would have no operational means to act upon.

6.2 Three-Layer Architecture
The implementation separates concerns across three layers:
 Layer 1 (electrical engine). The pandapower library [42] provides the power flow
 solver, network element data structures, and time-series simulation framework. pan-
 dapower is a Python-native, BSD-licensed tool that supports both transmission
 and distribution modeling and includes the OpenDSS converter used to import the

28 Blakely et al.

 IEEE 123-bus feeder model. It is treated as a pure numerical engine; notebooks
 never interact with it directly.
 Layer 2 (CyDERMS library). The project-specific Python library owns the map-
 ping between cyber events and observable grid consequences. It provides the reac-
 tor transient state machine, the symptom/mechanic event ontology, the operator-
 observable visualizations, the assessment scoring framework, and the scenario defi-
 nitions. This is where the training value lives.
 Layer 3 (notebooks). Twelve Jupyter notebooks form the pedagogical interface.
 Each notebook is a structured learning experience that uses the CyDERMS library’s
 high-level API. Trainees see grid concepts (feeders, voltages, generators, setpoints),
 not pandapower element indices.
  This separation is what makes the lab scenario-extensible: an instructor can add a new
disturbance or anomaly scenario by composing existing event primitives in Layer 2 without
touching either the electrical engine below or the notebook curriculum above.

6.3 Composite Grid Model
The grid model combines a transmission backbone with three differentiated distribution
feeders, yielding a ∼400-bus system that supports both local (feeder-level) and system-wide
(transmission-level) cyber event scenarios.

  Transmission backbone (13 buses). A deliberately asymmetric, mixed-voltage ring at
230 kV and 115 kV, rather than a symmetric reference network. This is a project-specific
composite, not a published reference network; it was constructed to produce realistic
voltage-weakness asymmetry between the 115 and 230 kV corridors so that the same distur-
bance produces visibly different operator-side consequences depending on where it lands.
A trip of the 77 MW SMR at the 115 kV bus produces a larger and more localized voltage
depression than a trip of the 300 MW SMR at the 230 kV bus, because the 115 kV corri-
dor is weaker. This teaches operators that grid topology determines impact severity. The
backbone includes:
 ∙ One 300 MW SMR (transmission-connected baseload nuclear)
 ∙ One 77 MW SMR behind a generator step-up transformer
 ∙ One 40 MW conventional gas generator (slow ramp, high inertia)
 ∙ One 25 MW peaking combustion turbine (fast ramp, moderate inertia)
 ∙ Transmission-level loads: 100 MW data center, 25 MW smelter, 32 MW industrial
 ∙ An external grid reference at the boundary providing voltage reference and absorb-
 ing power imbalance

 The conventional generators are essential to the training value of the model. Without
them, an SMR trip looks artificially catastrophic; with them, operators learn realistic com-
pensation dynamics — the gas unit picks up load quickly while the thermal unit responds
slowly, producing a characteristic recovery signature.

  Three differentiated IEEE 123 feeders. Each feeder is an instance of the IEEE PES 123-
bus radial distribution test feeder [43], imported from the OpenDSS-format release main-

The three instances are differentiated by load mix and DER portfolio so they exhibit dif-
ferent cyber-event response signatures:
 Feeder A (industrial campus + 2 MW microreactor). Industrial and commer-
 cial loads. A 2 MW microreactor placed behind-the-meter at a campus bus creates
 reverse-flow possibilities when output exceeds local load, and stresses the voltage
 regulators when power flow direction reverses. Models a dedicated industrial or
 military-base campus with on-site nuclear generation.
 Feeder B (DER-heavy, no nuclear). Mixed residential and commercial loads with
 three photovoltaic clusters at feeder-end buses (strong irradiance sensitivity) and a
 wind source mid-feeder. Serves as both a comparison feeder and a DER interaction
 testbed for inverter-related anomaly scenarios.
 Feeder C (residential + 1 MW behind-meter microreactor). Predominantly res-
 idential loads with longer weak laterals and a 1 MW microreactor placed behind-
 meter at a lateral bus. The weak lateral amplifies the local voltage effect of a reactor
 trip, testing the operator’s ability to distinguish a local lateral event from a system-
 wide event.

6.4 Reactor Transient Model
The reactor transient model captures the observable electrical behavior that distinguishes
nuclear generation from gas, hydro, or inverter-based DERs, without modeling reactor in-
ternals. It is implemented as a state machine over six reactor classes (1, 5, 20, 77, 200, and
300 MW) with class-parameterized ramp limits, thermal lag, minimum stable generation,
protection trip setpoints, and recovery delays. State transitions are AVAILABLE → RAMP-
ING → OPERATING, with TRIPPED and RECOVERY states reachable on protection
actuation and a class-dependent lockout window before the unit returns to AVAILABLE.
  What is modeled (because it has electrical consequences the operator can see):
 ∙ Class-parameterized ramp rate limits (nuclear sources move substantially slower
 than gas-turbine peakers, as discussed in §2)

6.5 SCADA Protocol Stack
A defining design decision is that the lab uses standard SCADA protocol implementations
rather than abstracting the protocol layer to behavioral mocks. Trainees interact with the

32 Blakely et al.

grid model through the same protocol layers (Modbus TCP, RTU polling, SCADA teleme-
try) used in production distribution systems, so that protocol-layer anomaly recognition
exercises produce evidence that matches what an operator would see on a fielded SCADA
system.
  The choice of Modbus TCP rather than DNP3 (which dominates U.S. distribution
SCADA between master and RTU) was deliberate: Modbus’s simpler register model is
more pedagogically accessible to trainees who are not already SCADA specialists, and
the prototype scope of this work prioritized that accessibility over protocol realism at the
master/RTU layer. The underlying architecture is protocol-agnostic and supports a DNP3
substitution as a follow-on extension.
  The stack is built atop the open-source CITADEL grid-simulator (originally developed
under the DOE CESER ZT-CARD project at Argonne) [47], extended with a CyDERMS-
specific composite topology, reactor state callbacks, a real-time UI, and an HTTP ground-
truth API. Around it sit four containerized Remote Terminal Units (RTUs) and a Jupyter-
Lab console, all orchestrated via Docker Compose:
 ∙ Grid-simulator runs continuous pandapower power flow with reactor state call-
 backs, publishing state via Modbus TCP (port 502) and an HTTP API (port
 8081). The HTTP API serves as the EMS-equivalent ground-truth view used by
 the GroundTruthClient.
 ∙ RTU-A, RTU-B, RTU-C each poll their feeder zone’s Modbus registers from the
 grid-simulator and serve zone-scoped state via REST and a north-facing Modbus
 interface. This mirrors the realistic distribution-SCADA polling pattern.
 ∙ RTU-T performs the same function for the transmission backbone (13 buses, gen-
 erators, transmission-level loads).
 ∙ Console runs JupyterLab and exposes the CyDERMS library, plus three client
 classes: SCADAClient for normal operations (queries RTUs via REST), RawModbusClient
 for direct protocol access used to inject the telemetry-layer anomaly signatures in
 the scenario library, and GroundTruthClient which bypasses the RTUs to read
 directly from the grid-simulator HTTP API. The GroundTruthClient is the ana-
 lytical primitive that lets trainees verify whether observed RTU values match the
 underlying grid state.

 Network condition simulation. Per-container Linux tc/netem link profiles allow the SCADA
links to be configured with realistic latency, loss, and jitter signatures representative of LAN,
fiber, microwave, cellular, and satellite backhaul. This supports the operator-track objective

6.6 Anomaly and Disturbance Scenario Library
The lab includes a library of ten scenarios that present the trainee with grid-side and
telemetry-side anomaly signatures, framed entirely from the distribution operator’s vantage
point. The training objective in every case is the same: recognize, classify, and respond to the
observable signature, regardless of whether the underlying cause is cyber attack, equipment
fault, or communication failure. The scenarios are constructed to exercise the discrimination
skill that the BOP/EMS jurisdictional gap (§4.4) makes operationally necessary, since an
SMR’s on-site reactor information is generally not visible to the distribution operator.
They are not, and are not intended as, attacker tradecraft documentation; the underlying
mechanic is described only at the level needed for the operator to recognize the signature
class.

  Six grid-side disturbance scenarios. present SMR- or DER-created anomalies that mani-
fest in SCADA-observable quantities (voltage, real and reactive power flow, breaker state,
generator output) without correlated evidence in protocol-layer telemetry. These are signa-
tures the operator must learn to recognize on the assumption that the cause may already
have moved into the physical layer:
 G1: SMR-created overgeneration anomaly. A reactor’s electrical output exceeds
 its safe operating envelope, producing visible voltage rise and reverse-flow conditions
 on the host feeder. Tests operator recognition of an out-of-envelope dispatch state
 and the appropriate response when the reactor is owned and operated by a non-
 utility entity.
 G2: Unscheduled generation loss during peak load. Generation drops at peak
 load with limited compensation capacity. Tests operator response to a generation
 gap that cannot be filled within the SCADA refresh cycle and that may originate
 inside an operator’s normal coordination boundary or outside it.
 G3: Reactive-power-driven voltage anomaly. Generator reactive output shifts,
 producing voltage inflation or depression without correlated active-power evidence.
 Tests operator ability to distinguish reactive-driven voltage events from active-power
 events.
 G4: Broad voltage-reference shift. An external-grid or generator bus voltage ref-
 erence shifts, producing a broad voltage shift across feeders. Tests recognition of a
 system-level event masquerading as multiple local events.
 G5: Unauthorized feeder-section isolation. A feeder section becomes unexpect-
 edly isolated, causing load transfer and downstream reconfiguration. Tests recogni-
 tion of an unauthorized isolation event versus a legitimate reconfiguration.

34 Blakely et al.

 G6: Coordinated SMR + DER instability signature. Combined inverter-DER
 and SMR output irregularities produce voltage and frequency oscillation. The head-
 line multi-asset disturbance signature exercised in analyst-track Objective 10.

  Four protocol-layer anomaly scenarios. present telemetry-versus-ground-truth divergence
at the Modbus TCP layer between RTUs and the grid-simulator, with no concurrent phys-
ical disturbance. These are signatures the operator must learn to recognize earlier, before
the underlying root cause has propagated into observable physical effects:

 P1: Telemetry-register inconsistency. RTU-cached Modbus register values diverge
 from underlying grid state. The operator’s SCADA display reports values that
 GroundTruthClient disconfirms. Headline signature for operator-track Objective 5.
 P2: In-flight measurement divergence. Polled values disagree with verified state
 in a way that is internally consistent at the RTU but not consistent with ground
 truth.
 P3: Polling-cycle stalls. Stale telemetry data with no explicit error indication. Tests
 operator ability to recognize stale data as a security signal rather than a transient
 communication fault.
 P4: Multi-zone degrade-and-divergence. Combined network-condition degrada-
 tion on one or more RTU zones (via tc/netem link profiles) and telemetry inconsis-
 tency on another. The headline multi-vector signature for analyst-track Objective 10
 in combination with G6.

 The scenarios are organized internally as a two-layer ontology: a symptom vocabulary
of what the operator observes (voltage shift, breaker trip, telemetry inconsistency) over a
mechanics vocabulary of what produced the symptom. Distinct mechanics that yield iden-
tical operator-observable symptoms are kept distinguishable internally so that scenarios
remain analytically separable for diagnostic scoring and instructor reference; trainees, how-
ever, work entirely against the symptom layer. This two-layer organization is conceptually
aligned with structured cyber-physical security ontologies for power grids such as Grid-
STIX [48], which extends STIX 2.1 to capture grid-specific assets and cyber-physical rela-
tionships. The CyDERMS library focuses on operator-visible disturbance signatures rather
than on the attacker-side coverage of generic ICS taxonomies such as MITRE ATT&CK
for ICS [39], which do not capture how synchronous nuclear generation on distribution
feeders changes the observable anomaly profile.

6.8 Detailed Walkthroughs: Notebooks 06–08
  Notebook 06: Reactor-Sourced Anomalies. Three reactor-side disturbance scenarios chained
sequentially against the 2 MW microreactor on Feeder A. The trainee runs an overgener-
ation scenario (where the reactor’s electrical output is driven beyond its safe envelope), a
forced-trip scenario at peak load, and a false-reactive-injection scenario that produces volt-
age inflation without active-power evidence. Each is rendered through the voltage heatmap
visualization (showing how the disturbance propagates spatially across Feeder A buses)
and through a side-by-side operator dashboard comparison between a pre-disturbance
timestep and a peak-disturbance timestep. The pedagogical objective is for the trainee to
learn the three distinct signature classes and the corresponding operator response asymme-
try: an overgeneration anomaly is recoverable by setpoint correction through normal coor-
dination, a forced trip removes the reactor from the dispatch picture for the trip-recovery
window [12], and a false reactive injection may not produce any clear active-power signature
at all.

  Notebook 07: Combined Scenarios. A multi-vector scenario combining alternating PV
curtailment with reactor output surges, deliberately tuned to drive the on-load tap chang-
ers into hunting behavior. The scenario is composed via the CyDERMS library’s general
scenario API, which accepts a list of timed events targeting different generation assets,
and the notebook demonstrates this composition pattern explicitly so that instructors and
analysts can construct novel scenarios from the existing event primitives. The notebook
adds an alarm-timeline analysis pass that walks through the active-event and alarm counts
at representative timesteps, surfacing the operator-confusion problem that tap hunting cre-
ates: the cumulative alarm count grows faster than the operator can triage, with no clear
single root cause. The pedagogical objective is recognition of coordinated multi-vector pat-
terns and the specific symptom signature (cascading alarms plus regulator instability) that
distinguishes a coordinated event from independent simultaneous faults.

  Notebook 08: Assessment (unknown-attribution). The capstone evaluation notebook. A
randomized scenario is selected from a pool (overgeneration, forced trip, and additional
classes as the library grows) with the attribution and event metadata hidden from the
trainee. The notebook integrates the reactor transient model so that during a setpoint-
spoof attack the reactor’s internal belief about its own electrical output diverges from
the externally-imposed generator setpoint — and that divergence, surfaced through the
operator dashboard’s reactor-status panel, is itself the signature the trainee must identify.
The trainee analysis space is structured around four directed questions: is an anomaly
occurring; if so, when did it begin; what type of anomaly is it (overgeneration, trip, reactive,
voltage-reference, switch, or combined); and which response action is appropriate. The
notebook is designed to feed an automated scoring engine in a follow-on phase, so the
analysis space includes the scaffolding for future scoring without yet implementing it.

7 Limitations and Next Steps
7.1 Limitations
The work documented here is a pilot construct. Five limitations bound the scope of any
conclusions drawn from it and define the engagement opportunities for follow-on work.

 Pre-deployment validation only. As of the analysis period, no SMR or microreactor was
operating on a U.S. distribution grid. The competency framework has therefore not been
validated with operational SMR personnel, and the training scenarios are derived from pub-
licly available vulnerability research and the project’s own protocol-stack analysis rather
than from observed operational incidents. The first operational SMR deployments will
produce training-relevant data that this work does not have.

  Speculative regulatory alignment. The framework’s regulatory compliance mapping as-
sumes that NRC awareness-and-training requirements for advanced reactors will follow the
RG 5.71 AT-1 through AT-4 structure. The forthcoming 10 CFR Part 53 framework had
not been finalized as of the analysis period [33], and the supporting regulatory guide DG-
5075 was in draft [19]. NRC final positions on SMR cybersecurity training requirements
may diverge from the assumptions embedded in this framework.

 Public-source threat intelligence only. No sensitive or classified threat intelligence in-
formed the scenario design. Attack scenarios are based on the publicly available vulnerabil-
ity research summarized in §4, the structural cyber-physical security ontologies referenced
in §6.6, and the protocol stack of the CyDERMS SCADA implementation itself. Scenarios
driven by classified adversary tradecraft are out of scope and would require a separate,
appropriately-cleared development effort.

  Behavioral, not physical, reactor model. The reactor transient model captures time-domain
electrical behavior observable on the grid (ramp rates, trip recovery, minimum stable gener-
ation) but does not model reactor internals. Claims about specific reactor designs’ behavior

Cybersecurity Workforce Training for SMR Integration into Distribution Grids 39

should not be derived from this model. The model is appropriate for training distribution-
side workforce on grid-observable consequences; it is not appropriate for any reactor-side
licensing, design, or operational analysis.

  Quasi-steady-state grid simulation. Each timestep is a power flow solution at SCADA
refresh intervals (2–10 seconds), not electromechanical transient simulation. Sub-second
dynamics — fault transients, relay timing, generator swing, true voltage and frequency
oscillation — are not modeled. The fidelity is appropriate for the operator-recognition
exercises this lab targets: trainees learn to read the SCADA-rendered signature of a dis-
turbance. They do not learn the underlying transient itself, and should not be expected to
apply this training to phenomena that require dynamic-simulation fidelity. A complemen-
tary tool would be needed for transient-stability training where that matters.

7.2 Deferred Deliverable: Pilot Training Delivery
A pilot training delivery and feedback collection phase (D5.4) was originally scoped within
the Argonne SOW but was deferred from this engagement due to the compressed period
of performance. The natural follow-on work has three components: pilot delivery of as-
sessment scenarios through the CyDERMS containerized lab to a representative trainee
population (distribution operators or graduate students); collection of trainee performance
data against the assessment criteria specified in the competency framework; and revision of
the objectives, scenarios, and instructor guidance based on observed gaps in comprehension
or scenario fidelity.

7.3 Engagement Opportunities
Three engagement paths would extend the value of this work:

  NICE overlay submission. Analyst objective 11 explicitly produces a draft NICE overlay
for nuclear-energy cybersecurity workforce roles. Engagement with NIST NICE program
staff and the DOE workforce development pipeline could explore formal adoption of this
overlay, which would provide federal-workforce-language compatibility for any subsequent
SMR cybersecurity training program.

  NRC RG 5.71 / 10 CFR Part 53 contribution. The framework is structured to map
cleanly onto RG 5.71 AT controls and to extend to the equivalent provisions of 10 CFR
Part 53 once finalized. NRC is the natural recipient of the framework as a reference input
to the cybersecurity training portion of advanced reactor cybersecurity rulemaking.

  Coordination with Sandia advanced reactor cybersecurity work. Sandia National Labora-

8 Conclusion
This report has documented Argonne National Laboratory’s contribution to the CyDERMS
Center under DOE CESER subcontract from Iowa State University: a structured analysis of
the cybersecurity standards and training landscape for SMR-integrated DER and microgrid
environments, a competency framework that translates the resulting six gaps into twelve
assessable learning objectives mapped across NIST CSF 2.0, ISA/IEC 62443, NIST NICE
TKS, and NRC RG 5.71, and a containerized training lab that implements the framework’s
assessment scenarios on a ∼400-bus composite grid model behind a realistically simulated
Modbus TCP SCADA stack.
  The headline analytical finding is that the balance-of-plant / energy-management-system
boundary on a distribution-connected SMR is the critical jurisdictional seam in the U.S.
cybersecurity regulatory landscape: as of March 2026, neither NRC RG 5.71 cyber authority
nor NERC CIP cleanly claims responsibility for it. Whatever administrative resolution
eventually addresses this gap, the operational and training problem it creates exists today
and will be operationally exposed as soon as the first distribution-connected SMRs come
into service. The competency framework and training lab provide a vehicle for preparing
the distribution-side workforce in advance of that operational exposure, on a generation
class for which no operational training data yet exists.
