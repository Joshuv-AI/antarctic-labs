A forecast-to-dispatch workflow for day-ahead operation of a regional natural gas transmission network based on station-level load prediction and linepack target setting

Ying Liu, Yong Xiao, Zhiping Tang, Simin Zhong, Chenguang Yong, Yuanyuan Tian

ABSTRACT
Day-ahead operation of regional natural gas transmission networks requires station demand forecasts and their translation into inventory-preparation references. This study evaluated a sequential forecast-to-dispatch workflow for the Chongqing regional gas network. Six forecasting models were assessed under a leakage-controlled 30-day walk-forward protocol. Across nine routine stations, mean station-level MAPE was 8.61% for SARIMAX, 8.95% for XGBoost, and 9.78% for GRU. The corresponding values were 10.93% for LSTM, 11.63% for BPNN, and 11.77% for CNN-BiLSTM-Attention. Station G was assessed separately as a data-quality boundary case; Station K was excluded because only 62 observations were available. An independent archived 18-day hourly dataset reported reconstruction MAPE of 6.7%−8.1% and peak-timing deviations of 1.0–1.5 h. A retrospective West-pipeline case for 22 April 2023 traced historical station forecasts through weighted aggregation, intermediate rounding, hourly reconstruction, and reduced-order linepack calculation. Its LFFX preparation target and the separate NGXDD and DLFX targets were 1.272, 4.702, and 1.571 × 10⁶ m³, respectively. All three targets passed case-specific deterministic inventory checks. The results support station-specific forecast-model selection and an explicit interface between daily prediction and segment-level inventory preparation.

1. Introduction Natural gas infrastructure provides dispatchable flexibility for balancing variable renewable generation, seasonal heating demand, and cross-sector energy interactions in low-carbon energy systems [ 1 ]. Pipeline linepack and gas-power coordination can support renewable accommodation, demand balancing, and system resilience. Growing gas consumption has also increased the operational demands placed on regional and urban transmission networks. China's apparent natural gas consumption reached 372.6 billion m³ in 2021, a year-on-year increase of 12.7% [ 2 , 3 ]. Accurate day-ahead load forecasts are therefore central to secure and economical pipeline operation [ 4 ]. The Chongqing regional transmission network is a ring-shaped source-network-load-storage system with a daily peak-valley difference exceeding 10 × 10⁶ m³. Gas transport depends mainly on inlet-pressure differences and dispatch adjustments because the network has no internal compressors. Its demand combines relatively regular urban and residential loads with volatile industrial and commercial loads. Temperature, holidays, weekly cycles, and operating-regime changes jointly shape these station-level series [ 4 – 7 ], requiring models that represent both persistent structure and short-term disturbances. Natural gas load forecasting has progressed from ARIMA- and SARIMA-type models for regular seasonal series to machine-learning and deep-learning methods for nonlinear, multivariate demand [ 8 , 9 ]. Comparative studies have evaluated LSTM, GRU, and hybrid architectures for local fluctuations and longer temporal dependencies [ 10 – 12 ]. Pipeline-operation research has concurrently developed optimization-based approaches for emergency scheduling, hourly day-ahead operation, and integrated gas-power systems with linepack flexibility [ 13 – 15 ]. Forecasting and dispatch nevertheless remain weakly connected in much of this literature: dispatch models often use assumed demand profiles, while forecasting studies commonly stop at predictive accuracy [ 16 – 20 ]. Closed-loop predict-and-optimize research links forecasting to the downstream decision objective. Chen et al. trained a cost-oriented renewable-generation predictor using the objective and constraints of network-constrained unit commitment [ 21 ]. Their work distinguishes predictive accuracy from operating cost. The present gas-network workflow uses sequential forecasting and inventory preparation with a linepack-deviation objective. Related industrial-monitoring research provides additional methodological context for field deployment. Multimodal signal fusion integrates complementary operating measurements for robust diagnosis [ 22 ]. Zero-sample and zero-fault-sample methods use physical constraints, digital twins, and virtual-real signal interaction when abnormal observations are scarce [ 23 , 24 ]. Lightweight explainable architectures address deployment and interpretation constraints [ 25 – 27 ], and prior-informed neural networks incorporate engineering knowledge into reliability prediction under uncertainty [ 28 ]. Regional gas-network applications require models that accommodate operational-data heterogeneity and station-dependent predictability while producing interpretable dispatch references. Daily station forecasts must be converted into hourly withdrawal profiles before they can support segment-level inventory preparation. Field evidence remains limited on the accuracy of this conversion and the resulting linepack and pressure references. This study evaluated a sequential forecast-to-dispatch workflow for the Chongqing regional natural gas transmission network. It connects leakage-controlled station forecasting, hourly nodal demand construction using historical intraday patterns, and reduced-order linepack and average-pressure target setting. Separate forecasting and temporal-disaggregation assessments characterize performance under heterogeneous field conditions, including a severely data-sparse station. A retrospective West-pipeline case traces historical forecasts through hourly reconstruction to an initial linepack preparation target. 2. Materials and methods 2.1 Problem definition and information flow Day-ahead operation of the Chongqing regional natural gas transmission network requires two sequential tasks: forecasting next-day station demand from historical loads and exogenous variables, and calculating linepack targets for key segments within prescribed pressure and inventory envelopes. Fig 1 shows the three-stage forecast-to-dispatch workflow. Layer 1 provides next-day station-level daily demand forecasts Q ^ s , d + 1 , where s is the station index and d + 1 is the next day. Layer 2 converts these forecasts into hourly profiles using historical intraday allocation factors and maps the withdrawals to the modeled offtake nodes. 10.1371/journal.pone.0349981.g001 Fig 1 Computational information flow of the forecast-guided operational framework. Layer 3 aggregates the hourly nodal withdrawals into segment-level demand-rate profiles. The reduced-order quasi-steady model combines these profiles with prescribed inlet supply and pressure and linepack envelopes. It returns the initial linepack preparation value x e , the corresponding average-pressure reference, and a packing or drafting recommendation for each segment. Forecasting was assessed over a common 30-day chronological hold-out period, and temporal disaggregation was evaluated using an independent archived 18-day hourly dataset. Retrospective engineering cases traced the subsequent linepack calculations, including the West-pipeline case for 22 April 2023 in Section 3.6. 2.2 Study network, data sources, and preprocessing strategy 2.2.1 Study network and station configuration. Validation and test observations did not contribute to transformation fitting. 2.3 Daily station-level forecasting model CNN-BiLSTM-Attention is the focal hybrid architecture because it formed the original engineering forecasting workflow and supports the architectural ablation analysis. The historical hybrid forecasts were retained to reconstruct the original engineering case. 2.3.1 Feature engineering and model inputs. The forecasting pipeline used a fixed eight-dimensional feature vector at each daily time step. It comprised the wavelet-denoised station load, temperature PC1, sine and cosine encodings of day of week and month, and weekend and holiday indicators. With a 14-day lookback, CNN-BiLSTM-Attention, LSTM, GRU, and the sequence-based ablation models received a 14 × 8 multivariate sequence. BPNN and XGBoost used the flattened 112-element window. SARIMAX used normalized load history and the seven transformed exogenous dimensions obtained by excluding contemporaneous load. The feature schema remained fixed across stations and forecast origins. MIC-style dependence scores served as an exploratory development-stage diagnostic and did not determine feature columns at individual forecast origins. For station s , z s , d ∈ ℝ m is the daily feature vector with m = 8. Complete date-level reconstruction inputs and metric calculations needed to rederive every summary entry are unavailable, as are balanced seasonal and abnormal-day statistics. 2.5 Linepack-oriented operational optimization model The linepack target-setting model uses prescribed inlet supply and hourly segment withdrawals to calculate preparation references within pressure and inventory envelopes. 2.5.1 Key pipeline segments and operating zones. The model represents LFFX as a low-pressure western buffering corridor and NGXDD and DLFX as higher-pressure eastern transmission corridors with larger static inventories. This partition captures the segments where linepack flexibility is concentrated at the resolution of the historical engineering model. For each segment e , the admissible linepack interval follows from prescribed pressure limits and throughput conditions. An interior allowable band applies conservative engineering margins to the minimum and maximum admissible linepack, V e m i n and V e m a x , respectively. Its lower and upper bounds are: V e L = ( 1 + β L ) V e m i n (21) V e U = ( 1 − β U ) V e m a x (22) where β L and β U are the dimensionless safety-margin factors, β L = 0.1 and β U = 0.2 . The initial preparation value x e is selected within [ V e L , V e U ] . 2.5.2 Reduced-order quasi-steady target-setting formulation. The target-setting model represents each segment by a lumped linepack state at hourly resolution. For segment e , x e is the initial preparation inventory for the next-day horizon, m³, with V e,0 ( x e ) = x e . Candidate values generate inventory trajectories under prescribed inlet-supply and withdrawal-rate profiles. Comparing x e with the currently observed linepack determines the preparation direction before the next-day horizon. A higher preparation value indicates packing, a lower value indicates drafting, and a negligible difference indicates no substantial adjustment. The horizon contains N h = 24 hourly intervals and 25 boundary states. V e,k is the segment linepack at boundary k = 0,..., N h , expressed as standard-condition gas volume, m³. Eq. Forecast accuracy was evaluated with MAPE, MedianAPE, MAE, and RMSE. MAPE supports scale-independent comparison across stations and with previous gas-demand studies. MedianAPE describes typical relative error when low loads or isolated large errors skew MAPE. MAE reports deviation in demand units, and RMSE assigns greater weight to large errors relevant to dispatch preparation. MAPE = 1 n ∑ i = 1 n | y ^ i − y i y i | × 100 % (36) MedianAPE = median ( | y ^ 1 − y 1 | | y 1 | , ⋯ , | y ^ n − y n | | y n | ) (37) MAE = 1 n ∑ i = 1 n | y ^ i − y i | (38) RMSE = 1 n ∑ i = 1 n ( y ^ i − y i ) 2 (39) where y i and y ^ i are the observed and predicted daily demand values for sample i , respectively, and n is the number of evaluated days. Stations A-F and H-J contained 531–540 consecutive daily observations; Station G contained 253 observations and was analyzed as a data-quality boundary case. The final 30 observations of each evaluable station formed the chronological hold-out period, with a 14-day lookback for one-day-ahead prediction. Station K contained 62 observations and was excluded from the common comparison because it could not support the shared lookback, validation, and hold-out design. At forecast origin t, the historical development window contained observations available at or before t. Its latest 10% formed the chronological validation block; earlier observations formed the model-fitting subset. Neural models were fitted on the model-fitting subset and used the validation block for early stopping. Baseline hyperparameters were fixed before hold-out evaluation. SARIMAX was refitted to all available transformed history at each origin, whereas XGBoost was fitted on the transformed model-fitting subset. After the observation at t + 1 became available, the origin advanced by one day and the historical window expanded. This process covered all 30 hold-out days, with identical chronological origins and leakage-control procedures across forecasting models. 2.6.2 Baseline and ablation design. The comparison evaluated accuracy, stability, and deployment suitability under a common operational forecasting protocol. The benchmark set comprised statistical, tree-based, neural-network, and ablation models. SARIMAX represented linear seasonal forecasting with exogenous meteorological and calendar inputs. Next-day calendar indicators were treated as known in advance, whereas the three temperature covariates used a one-day persistence forecast from the latest available observation. Observed test-day weather was not used. XGBoost represented nonlinear tabular regression using the flattened 14 × 8 feature window. BPNN provided a feed-forward neural reference using the flattened 112-element window; LSTM and GRU retained the 14 × 8 sequence representation. The ablation variants CNN-BiLSTM, BiLSTM-Attention, and CNN-Attention isolated local feature extraction, bidirectional temporal modeling, and temporal weighting. Baseline and ablation models used the same preprocessing, walk-forward origins, and metrics. For prospective deployment, candidate models should be compared by chronological validation within each station. The selected model would then generate that station's day-ahead forecast. 2.6.3 Data splitting, implementation settings, and reproducibility. The 11 station targets represent distinct demand regimes. Stations A, B, I, and J show comparatively regular urban or composite demand. Engineering implications and limitations The workflow links station forecasts to segment inventory preparation through explicit hourly withdrawal profiles. The historical targets imply packing for LFFX and drafting for NGXDD and DLFX. Station-dependent forecast errors and intraday profile shifts make model selection and coefficient updating central to prospective use. The selected linepack is a segment-level preparation reference for the start of the next-day horizon. The reduced-order calculation checks inventory and average-pressure envelopes under fixed boundary conditions. It omits actuator sequences, valve switching, compressor dispatch, flow reversal, nodal pressure redistribution, and transient dynamics. Compressor fuel, procurement cost, and pressure-fluctuation penalties are outside the objective. Operational implementation requires current boundary data, operator review, and transient hydraulic verification. MPC was not benchmarked because the formulation lacks the closed-loop dynamic states and control actions needed for a matched comparison. Forecast and reconstruction errors were evaluated separately, without quantifying their propagation into transient pressure and flow responses. The effects of the observed 1.0–1.5 h peak-timing errors on preparation linepack and average-pressure targets were not quantified. Broader multi-day and multi-season end-to-end validation requires synchronized forecast, withdrawal, supply, and linepack-state records. At data-impaired sites such as Station G, telemetry repair remains a priority for reliable forecasting. 5. Conclusions This study evaluated a forecast-to-dispatch workflow linking daily station forecasting, hourly withdrawal-profile construction, and reduced-order segment-level linepack target setting for the Chongqing regional gas network. Forecast accuracy varied substantially across stations. Among nine routine stations, SARIMAX achieved the lowest mean MAPE, 8.61%, compared with 11.77% for CNN-BiLSTM-Attention. Station G had a CNN-BiLSTM-Attention MAPE of 51.42%. Baseline and ablation results support station-specific model selection for prospective deployment and highlight the forecasting limits imposed by sparse telemetry. The independent archived 18-day reconstruction dataset reported hourly MAPE of 6.7%−8.1% and peak-timing deviations of 1.0–1.5 h. The hourly interface supplies the withdrawal profiles needed for segment-level inventory preparation. The retrospective LFFX case and the separate NGXDD and DLFX cases supported linepack preparation values of 1.272, 4.702, and 1.571 × 10⁶ m³, respectively. All three values passed case-specific deterministic inventory checks. Operational implementation requires current boundary data and transient hydraulic verification.
