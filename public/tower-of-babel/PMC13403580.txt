Non-albicans Candida vaginal infections in sub-Saharan Africa: a systematic review, meta-analysis, and narrative synthesis of prevalence, antifungal resistance, and clinical implications among women of reproductive age
Authors: Suleiman Ameir Idrissa, Prosper Akankwasa, Sara Liban, Yasmin Abdirashid Osman, John Katongole, Esther Namutosi, Jackson Kakooza, Catherine R. Lewis, Theoneste Hakizimana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC) is a pressing gynecological issue in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where non-albicans Candida (NAC) species are increasingly prevalent, challenging treatment due to variable antifungal resistance patterns. Despite this, species-level NAC data from SSA remain fragmented, and no prior meta-analysis has separately quantified the population-level prevalence of NAC infection alongside the species-specific proportions of NAC among confirmed Candida isolates. This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed to estimate the pooled prevalence and determinants of NAC vaginal infections among women of reproductive age.MethodsA PRISMA 2020-compliant systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted. Four electronic databases were searched for studies published from January 1, 2015 to December 31, 2025; all four databases were searched in February 2026. Eligible studies enrolled women of reproductive age (15–49 years) from SSA and confirmed Candida species identity using validated laboratory methods. Six random-effects meta-analyses were performed on logit-transformed proportions; pooled estimates were back-transformed for presentation. Publication bias was assessed using standard funnel-plot methods.ResultsFourteen cross-sectional studies from nine countries (N = 3,587 women) met inclusion criteria. Pooled NAC prevalence was 12.56% (95% CI 8.73–17.83%; I2 = 66.4%). Pooled NAC proportion among confirmed Candida isolates was 34.98% (95% CI 25.29–46.11%; I2 = 68.8%). Species-specific proportions were: C. glabrata 16.38% (95% CI 10.72–24.27%); C. krusei 4.52% (95% CI 2.07–9.56%); C. tropicalis 4.15% (95% CI 2.12–7.91%); and C. parapsilosis 1.46% (95% CI 0.61–3.46%). These four species collectively account for 26.51% of total Candida isolates. The remaining proportion reflects other, less common NAC taxa not individually meta-analyzed due to data sparsity. Pooled estimates suggested an appreciable burden of NAC species in the region, but the magnitude should be interpreted in light of substantial between-study heterogeneity and differences in laboratory identification methods.ConclusionsNAC species contribute substantially to VVC in women of reproductive age in SSA. However, the studies included varied in laboratory identification methods, so these findings should inform surveillance and diagnostic strengthening rather than immediate empirical treatment changes. Standardized species identification and antifungal susceptibility testing with isolate-level minimum inhibitory concentration reporting are needed.RegistrationPROSPERO CRD420251071725.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-01025-2.

Background

Vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC) is a common opportunistic fungal infection of the lower female reproductive tract and remains one of the leading causes of vaginitis among women of reproductive age. It is estimated that 70–75% of women experience at least one episode during their lifetime, and a substantial proportion develop recurrent disease, with important consequences for physical comfort, sexual health, psychosocial well-being, and healthcare use [1–3].

Clinically, VVC presents with vulvar pruritus, dysuria, dyspareunia, and abnormal vaginal discharge. In pregnancy, Candida infection has been linked to adverse obstetric and neonatal outcomes, including preterm birth, low birth weight, premature rupture of membranes, and neonatal mucocutaneous or invasive fungal infection, making the condition particularly relevant in settings with high maternal and newborn vulnerability [2, 4, 5].

Historically, Candida albicans has been regarded as the dominant etiologic agent of VVC, accounting for most laboratory-confirmed cases worldwide. However, this traditional view is increasingly challenged by the emergence of non-albicans Candida (NAC) species, particularly Nakaseomyces glabrata (formerly Candida glabrata), Pichia kudriavzevii (formerly Candida krusei), Candida tropicalis, and Candida parapsilosis [6–8]. For clinical consistency with the primary literature, in which most included studies (2015–2025) still use the legacy Candida nomenclature, the former species designations are used throughout this review, with current accepted names noted here.

This shift is clinically important because NAC species often demonstrate reduced susceptibility or intrinsic resistance to commonly used azole antifungals. C. krusei is intrinsically resistant to fluconazole, while C. glabrata frequently exhibits dose-dependent susceptibility or acquired resistance, creating a substantial risk of empirical treatment failure and persistent or recurrent infection [6, 7].

The challenge is especially acute in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where VVC is frequently managed syndromically, often without microbiological confirmation of Candida species or antifungal susceptibility. In many low-resource settings, women presenting with vaginal discharge are treated empirically with over-the-counter or first-line azole antifungals, despite evidence that symptoms alone do not reliably distinguish Candida infection from other genital tract conditions [4, 7].

At the same time, several behavioral and clinical factors common in SSA, including pregnancy, prior antibiotic exposure, contraceptive use, hygiene practices, and hot-climate clothing patterns may shape both Candida ecology and the risk of persistent or recurrent infection [9, 10].

Although individual studies from SSA have reported increasing isolation of NAC species, the regional evidence remains fragmented. Prior syntheses in SSA have largely focused on VVC overall rather than on NAC-specific vaginal infections, leaving important uncertainty regarding the true pooled burden of NAC infection, the relative distribution of major NAC species, the pattern of antifungal resistance, and the broader clinical implications for women of reproductive age [11]. This systematic review, meta-analysis, and narrative synthesis was therefore undertaken to estimate the pooled prevalence of NAC vaginal infections, quantify the contribution of major NAC species, synthesize available evidence on antifungal resistance patterns, and summarize clinical and public health implications in resource-limited settings across SSA. This review addressed the following questions: (1) what is the pooled prevalence of non-albicans Candida vaginal infection among women of reproductive age in sub-Saharan Africa?; (2) What proportion of laboratory-confirmed Candida vaginal isolates are non-albicans species?; (3) what are the pooled species-specific proportions of C. glabrata, C. krusei, C. tropicalis, and C. parapsilosis among confirmed Candida isolates?; and (4) what antifungal resistance patterns, diagnostic limitations, and clinical implications are reported in the included studies?

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [12]. The protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251071725).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they: (a) recruited women of reproductive age (15–49 years) from any SSA country; (b) reported Candida species identification using culture-based phenotypic, biochemical, or molecular methods; (c) reported NAC case counts with a clearly defined denominator, either total women examined or total Candida-positive isolates; and (d) were primary peer-reviewed research articles published between January 1, 2015 and December 31, 2025. Because conventional biochemical and chromogenic methods differ in their ability to distinguish cryptic or emerging species, identification platform was extracted as a study characteristic and treated as a potential source of misclassification bias. Cross-sectional studies were included because the primary objective was to estimate prevalence and species distribution at population or clinic levels. Cohort and case–control designs were not required for the primary prevalence objective unless they provided extractable baseline prevalence data using a clearly defined denominator. Studies were excluded if they were reviews, case reports, or conference abstracts; used morphological identification without species confirmation; or reported a duplicate population without separately extractable data.

Information sources and search strategy

Systematic searches were conducted in four electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Lens.org. The four databases were searched in February 2026 to capture all eligible records indexed up to the search date. In Scopus, the syntax PUBYEAR > 2014 AND PUBYEAR < 2026 was used to retrieve studies published from 2015 through 2025. Although the search was conducted in February 2026, this Scopus publication-year filter limits eligible records to the 2015–2025 publication window and does not retrieve records with a publication year of 2026.

The search strategy combined MeSH terms with free-text keywords in Boolean combinations. The search included Candida species terms (“Candida”, “non-albicans Candida”, “Candida glabrata”, “Nakaseomyces glabrata”, “Candida krusei”, “Pichia kudriavzevii”, “Candida tropicalis”, and “Candida parapsilosis”); disease terms (“vulvovaginal candidiasis”, “vaginal candidiasis”, “VVC”, “vaginitis”, and “vaginal infection”); and population/geography terms (“women”, “reproductive age”, “pregnant women”, “Sub-Saharan Africa”, and individual SSA country names). Reference lists of all included articles and relevant prior systematic reviews were also hand-searched. Full database-specific search strings are provided in Supplementary File S1.

No language restrictions were applied. Non-English titles, abstracts, and full texts were screened using reviewer language competence where available and translation tools where necessary, with eligibility decisions confirmed by at least two reviewers. Duplicate records were removed before screening.

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened all titles and abstracts using a pre-piloted standardized form, then reviewed full texts of potentially eligible records. Disagreements were resolved through structured discussion; unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by a third reviewer.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized form and reconciled discrepancies before analysis. Variables extracted included: first author and year, country, study setting, design, total women enrolled, women with confirmed vaginal Candida, total Candida isolates, species-specific isolate counts, and identification method. Where a study reported data from two clinically distinct arms, such as the antenatal care and labor wards in Sule-Odu et al. [5], each arm was treated as an independent data point for analyses using the isolate denominator.

Methodological quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for prevalence studies [13], adapted to five operational criteria: sample representativeness, adequacy of sample size, use of objective laboratory diagnostics, clinical and demographic description, and appropriateness of statistical analysis. Scores are reported in Supplementary Table S1. All eligible studies were retained regardless of score to avoid selection bias.

Statistical analysis

Effect size and data transformation

The effect size for each analysis was the proportion of interest. Raw proportions were logit-transformed before pooling to stabilize sampling variances and to ensure that back-transformed confidence intervals remained within the possible range of 0 to 1. The logit of a proportion p is defined as ln[p/(1 − p)], with sampling variance 1/[n·p·(1 − p)], where n is the relevant denominator. For studies in which a given NAC species was not observed, a continuity correction of 0.5 was added to both numerator and denominator [14]. This fixed correction is acknowledged to potentially inflate estimates for very low-prevalence species (i.e., C. parapsilosis, C. krusei) asymmetrically relative to arm-based alternative corrections; the magnitude of this effect was examined in the pre-specified continuity-correction exclusion sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S3).

Meta-analysis model and software

Six random-effects meta-analyses were run using the metafor package [15] within Jamovi v2.6.44 (The jamovi project, 2024) running R v4.4 (R Core Team, 2024). A random-effects model was pre-specified for all analyses because genuine between-study variability was expected from differences in patient populations, clinical settings, geographic Candida ecology, laboratory identification methods, and temporal patterns of antifungal use. Between-study variance (τ2) was estimated by restricted maximum-likelihood (REML). The six analyses were: Analysis 1—pooled NAC prevalence in the total study population; Analysis 2—pooled NAC proportion among confirmed Candida isolates; Analysis 3—C. glabrata; Analysis 4—C. krusei; Analysis 5—C. parapsilosis; Analysis 6—C. tropicalis.

Between-study heterogeneity

Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran’s Q, I2, H2, τ, and REML-estimated τ2. I2 was interpreted using established thresholds: < 25% = low; 25–50% = moderate; 50–75% = substantial; > 75% = high [16].

Sensitivity analyses

Two classes of sensitivity analysis were pre-specified: (i) leave-one-out influence analyses, systematically excluding each contributing study; and (ii) continuity-correction exclusion analyses, removing all zero-event studies and re-pooling. Full results are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Subgroup analyses

We planned stratified analyses by geographic subregion, identification method, and clinical setting. Where at least three studies were available per subgroup, subgroup-specific pooled estimates were calculated descriptively; where data were sparser, subgroup patterns were summarized narratively without formal between-group testing.

Publication bias

Publication bias was evaluated for each analysis using: Rosenthal’s Fail-Safe N (FSN; threshold 5 k + 10), Kendall’s rank correlation, and Egger’s weighted regression.

Narrative synthesis

A structured narrative synthesis was conducted in parallel with the meta-analyses, following the framework of Popay et al. [17]. Five thematic domains were defined a priori: (i) antifungal resistance profiles and over-the-counter antifungal misuse; (ii) validity of symptom-based syndromic management; (iii) adverse obstetric and neonatal outcomes; (iv) behavioral and sociodemographic risk determinants; and (v) diagnostic technologies for resource-limited settings. A structured summary is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1545 records were identified from four electronic databases: Lens.org (n = 735), Web of Science (n = 297), Scopus (n = 312), and PubMed/MEDLINE (n = 201). The eligible publication period was 1 January 2015 to 31 December 2025, and all four databases were searched in February 2026. After removal of 523 duplicate records, 1022 unique records were screened by title and abstract; 959 were excluded at this stage. Sixty-three full-text reports were sought for retrieval, of which 11 could not be obtained. The remaining 52 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility; 38 were excluded with reasons (Fig. 1). The 14 studies were published between 2015 and 2025 and came from nine SSA countries: Nigeria (n = 3), Ghana (n = 2), Cameroon (n = 2), Senegal (n = 2), Benin (n = 1), Uganda (n = 1), Ethiopia (n = 1), Tanzania (n = 1), and Namibia (n = 1). All were cross-sectional in design. The aggregate study population was 3587 women. Individual sample sizes ranged from 100 [8] to 456 [6]. Every study used at least one validated identification method: CHROMagar Candida, API-20C AUX, Vitek-2 yeast card, or an equivalent biochemical panel, and two studies additionally employed molecular confirmation. Between-study heterogeneity

Statistically significant heterogeneity was present across all six analyses (Table 4), confirming that the pre-specified random-effects model was appropriate throughout. For Analysis 1, I2 = 66.40% (τ2 = 0.4005, SE = 0.2388; τ = 0.633; H2 = 2.976; Q[13] = 39.318, p < 0.001), meaning approximately two-thirds of the observed variance reflected genuine between-study differences. Analysis 2 showed comparable heterogeneity (I2 = 68.77%; τ2 = 0.5305; Q[13] = 40.221, p < 0.001). Among the species-specific analyses, heterogeneity was moderate-to-substantial for C. glabrata (I2 = 54.94%) and high for C. krusei (I2 = 70.39%), largely attributable to Aboagye et al. [2]. Qualitative inspection of forest plots suggests that West African studies tended to report higher NAC proportions than East or Southern African studies. Studies using CHROMagar alone generally identified higher NAC counts than those relying exclusively on API panels, and antenatal care settings more often reported symptomatic NAC VVC than mixed or community settings. Formal subgroup meta-analyses were not conducted because no subgroup category reached the pre-specified minimum of three contributing studies: across geographic subregion, identification method, and clinical setting, each cell contained at most two studies. Conducting between-group tests with k < 3 per subgroup cell would produce unreliable variance estimates and carry a high risk of type I error from multiple comparisons. These directional patterns are therefore hypothesis-generating observations, to be tested in future primary studies with larger and more geographically diverse samples. Sensitivity analyses

Full leave-one-out results are in Supplementary Table S3. Across Analyses 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6, no single study removal produced an estimate outside the primary 95% CI; these five analyses are numerically stable. For Analysis 1, removing Fanou et al. [1] (39.84%) shifted the pooled NAC prevalence to 11.37% (95% CI [7.91–16.04%]). For Analysis 3, removing Waikhom et al. [4] (57.41%) produced a revised C. glabrata estimate of 14.72% (95% CI [10.01–21.17%])—still the dominant NAC species. For Analysis 4, removing Aboagye et al. [2] lowered the C. krusei estimate from 4.52 to 3.11% (95% CI [1.72–5.57%]); the conclusion of a real, non-trivial prevalence was maintained. Continuity-correction sensitivity analyses for Analyses 3–6 restricting pooling to studies with observed non-zero events yielded estimates directionally and numerically consistent with the primary values (Supplementary Table S3), suggesting the fixed 0.5 correction did not materially distort results, although mild upward bias in ultra-low-prevalence species (i.e., C. parapsilosis, C. krusei) cannot be entirely excluded. For Analysis 5 (C. parapsilosis), restricting to non-zero-event studies (k = 6) yielded 1.38% (95% CI [0.48–3.87%]), closely in line with the primary estimate of 1.46%.

Publication bias assessment

For Analysis 1, Egger’s regression was significant (intercept = − 2.177; p = 0.029) but Kendall’s τ was not (p = 0.233); Fail-Safe N = 1291 (threshold 80). For Analysis 2, neither test was significant; Fail-Safe N = 105 (threshold 80). For C. glabrata (Analysis 3), Egger’s regression was significant (p = 0.041); Fail-Safe N = 350 (threshold 60). For C. krusei (Analysis 4), all three tests indicated asymmetry, consistent with the outlier effect of Aboagye et al. [2]. For Analyses 5 and 6, Fail-Safe N values (584 and 837, respectively) argue against selective publication as a primary driver (Supplementary Table S5; Supplementary Figures S1-6).

Narrative synthesis: contextual evidence across five thematic domains

The 14 included studies report contextual evidence extending well beyond the pooled prevalence estimates. The full structured tabulation is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Domain 1: antifungal resistance profiles and over-the-counter antifungal misuse

Five included studies reported antifungal susceptibility data or linked NAC prevalence to antifungal prescribing patterns. Aboagye et al. [2] reported fluconazole resistance in 53.2% of C. albicans isolates and 59.6% of NAC isolates. C. dubliniensis showed complete fluconazole resistance and C. krusei showed 69% resistance. Mukasa et al. [6] linked increasing NAC detection in Uganda directly to widespread over-the-counter antimycotic use. C. krusei showed 71.43% fluconazole resistance and nystatin resistance remained below 1%. Waikhom et al. [4] found 48.1% of all Candida isolates resistant to fluconazole versus only 9.3% to nystatin. Sule-Odu et al. [5] reported 0% C. krusei isolates sensitive to fluconazole, attributing universal resistance to unregulated antifungal sales. Emeribe et al. [18] documented a statistically confirmed association between prior antibiotic exposure and VVC incidence (p < 0.05), positioning antibacterial stewardship as an additional prevention lever.

Domain 2: validity and limitations of syndromic management

Waikhom et al. [4] found that vaginal discharge showed no statistically significant association with confirmed VVC (p = 0.143). Dunaiski et al. [7] documented that 73% of Candida-positive women had at least one concurrent infection, including Trichomonas vaginalis (27%), Chlamydia trachomatis (6.4%), and bacterial vaginosis (15%). Emeribe et al. [18] found equal culture-positive rates in symptomatic and asymptomatic women (n = 14 each), confirming substantial silent carriage. These findings are consistent with the gap between Analysis 1 (12.56% in total population) and Analysis 2 (34.98% among confirmed isolates), reflecting women who carry Candida without symptoms that would trigger syndromic treatment.

Domain 3: adverse obstetric and neonatal outcomes

Sule-Odu et al. [5] found that vaginal Candida infection at labor onset was independently associated with low birth weight delivery (Adjusted OR = 2.8, 95% CI [1.1–6.8], p = 0.03). Waikhom et al. [4] reported that 81.5% of confirmed VVC cases were highly symptomatic during pregnancy. Aboagye et al. [2] reported that 44.2% of 43 women who experienced preterm birth or miscarriage tested VVC-positive, with NAC species disproportionately represented in adverse-outcome cases. Three independent cohort findings converging in the same direction provide a clinical rationale for routine antenatal VVC screening grounded in quantifiable patient harm.

Domain 4: behavioral and sociodemographic risk determinants

Kechia et al. [9] identified tight or synthetic insulating underwear (p = 0.005) and heavy sweating (p = 0.033) as statistically significant independent risk factors. Eskezia et al. [19] reported that replacing underwear more than once daily was significantly protective (AOR = 0.18, 95% CI [0.05–0.72], p = 0.02). Ndiaye et al. [10] documented a significant association between contraceptive use and VVC incidence (51.6%; p = 0.014), with IUD use linked through biofilm formation. Aboagye et al. [2] identified educational attainment as a positive determinant of protective hygiene behaviors.

Domain 5: diagnostic technologies for resource-limited settings

Diallo et al. [21] tested qPCR alongside culture in Senegal: qPCR achieved 94% specificity but generated 19 qPCR-positive, culture-negative samples, reflecting the limitation of detecting non-viable DNA. A hybrid approach, qPCR, for rapid screening followed by culture-based susceptibility testing for positive results was recommended. Chromogenic media may support low-cost presumptive differentiation of common Candida species in resource-limited laboratories, but these methods do not replace reference-standard identification when cryptic or emerging species are of concern. Conventional phenotypic and biochemical identification platforms may also miss or misclassify cryptic or emerging Candida species, including Candida auris, when reference-standard molecular confirmation is not available. Accordingly, findings from phenotypic or biochemical studies should be interpreted as reported species distributions rather than definitive taxonomy.

Discussion

This review is the first to provide species-specific pooled prevalence estimates for the four principal NAC species among women of reproductive age across SSA. Across 14 studies and 3,587 women, NAC species accounted for 12.56% of all vaginal infections and 34.98% of confirmed Candida isolates. C. glabrata was the dominant species at 16.38%, followed by C. krusei (4.52%), C. tropicalis (4.15%), and C. parapsilosis (1.46%).

Interpretation of main results

The finding that roughly one in three confirmed Candida infections in SSA women involves a NAC species is clinically significant because first-line empirical treatment oral fluconazole is pharmacologically inactive against C. krusei and has markedly reduced efficacy against C. glabrata, the dominant species [22]. C. glabrata’s dominance at 16.38% reflects its distinct biology: unlike C. albicans, it uses robust biofilm formation, efflux pump overexpression (Cdr1, Pdh1, Snq2), and rapid ERG11 mutations to establish and maintain azole resistance [22]. Even moderate antifungal pressure from repeated over-the-counter (OTC) fluconazole use is sufficient to drive C. glabrata selection over the more azole-susceptible C. albicans. The C. krusei estimate of 4.52% must be interpreted with caution: removal of one influential study reduced the estimate to 3.11%, and heterogeneity was the highest of all six analyses (I2 = 70.39%). C. parapsilosis at 1.46% was expected to be low given its primarily nosocomial ecology.

Comparison with previous studies

The most comparable published evidence is the systematic review by Mushi et al. [11], which pooled data from 41 SSA studies (N = 15,723 participants) and reported a pooled VVC prevalence of 33% (95% CI 28–38%), with C. glabrata accounting for 40.9% of NAC isolates across 17 species-reporting studies. Our NAC proportion among isolates (34.98%) is broadly consistent with that body of evidence, but our methodological advances (logit transformation, REML modeling, and independently verified back-transformations) provide explicit, quantified uncertainty estimates for each species for the first time. At the global level, Denning et al. [23] estimated that recurrent VVC affects 138 million women annually. Akinosoglou et al. [22] identified 25–45% NAC representation in high-resistance settings globally, consistent with our findings. Our data partly diverge from Schuster et al. [24] who found no significant association between asymptomatic Candida colonization and preterm birth (OR 1.10, 95% CI 0.99–1.22). The distinction between asymptomatic colonization and active symptomatic NAC infection in a context of treatment failure may explain this divergence.

Biological and clinical explanation of findings

The ecological shift toward NAC species is mechanistically coherent and is driven in part by patterns of antifungal use common in SSA. C. glabrata is haploid and reproduces clonally, meaning resistance mutations are rapidly fixed under antifungal selection [22]. Repeated or inappropriate azole exposure, whether from OTC fluconazole taken without laboratory diagnosis, at incorrect doses, or for incomplete treatment courses, provides precisely the selection pressure needed to favor C. glabrata over C. albicans. Emeribe et al. [18] found that all culture-positive VVC participants had received prior antibacterial therapy (p < 0.05); broad-spectrum antibiotics disrupt the lactobacillus-dominated vaginal flora, allowing NAC species already present as commensals to establish symptomatic infection. In SSA, where antibacterials are widely used empirically for undifferentiated vaginal discharge, this mechanism likely operates at scale [25]. The protective effect of changing underwear more than once daily (AOR 0.18) [19] is biologically plausible and points to an inexpensive, scalable prevention target.

Clinical implications

The present review suggests that non-albicans species contribute materially to the regional VVC burden and that reliance on syndromic management alone is problematic. However, prevalence data and heterogeneous susceptibility summaries are insufficient to support a uniform change in first-line treatment across SSA. Treatment decisions should remain guided by local guidelines, pregnancy status, recurrence history, species identification where available, and standardized susceptibility testing interpreted within recognized breakpoint frameworks.

Public health and policy implications

The clearest policy implication of these findings is the urgent need to strengthen mycology diagnostics and antifungal stewardship in SSA. Specifically, what is needed includes: species-level identification using validated chromogenic or molecular platforms; standardized antifungal susceptibility testing with MIC reporting against EUCAST or CLSI breakpoints; local and regional surveillance systems to track resistance trends over time; and integration of resistance data into national VVC treatment guidelines. These pooled data support diagnostic stewardship more strongly than they support a uniform change in empirical antifungal policy, but they provide a compelling evidence base for investment in laboratory capacity and antimicrobial stewardship programs at the national level.

Strengths and limitations

This review provides a focused synthesis of reported NAC burden among women of reproductive age in SSA and combines quantitative pooling with narrative contextualization. Its main strengths are transparent eligibility criteria, duplicated screening and extraction, and explicit reporting of heterogeneity and sensitivity analyses.

Several limitations warrant emphasis. First, included studies varied substantially in identification platforms, and several relied on conventional phenotypic or biochemical methods that may misclassify cryptic or emerging species. Of particular concern, biochemical panels such as API-20C, AUX and Vitek-2 yeast cards have well-documented limitations in resolving cryptic species complexes (e.g., the Nakaseomyces/glabrata complex) and cannot reliably identify emerging multidrug-resistant pathogens such as Candida auris, a species of growing public health importance in SSA that circulated in the region during the study period. Species distributions reported across included studies should therefore be interpreted as phenotypic approximations rather than definitive taxonomy, and the true burden of C. auris and related cryptic species in this population remains unknown. Second, and critically, no included study presented complete minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) data for each NAC species interpreted against current EUCAST or CLSI breakpoints. Susceptibility findings were reported as binary resistant/susceptible categories using variable or unstated thresholds, making cross-study comparison of resistance profiles unreliable. Accordingly, this review cannot support specific empirical antifungal treatment changes, and all susceptibility findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating observations requiring confirmation through standardized MIC testing. Third, the small number of studies constrained subgroup investigation. Finally, some studies reported NAC species incompletely, requiring careful reconciliation of species-specific counts and leaving uncertainty around less common taxa.

Implications for future research

All 14 included studies were cross-sectional, meaning incidence, recurrence, and treatment response cannot be assessed. Prospective cohort studies with species identification and antifungal susceptibility profiling at each episode are the most pressing methodological priority. Randomized controlled trials comparing nystatin versus fluconazole as empirical first-line VVC treatment in high-NAC prevalence SSA settings are conspicuously absent. Large-scale implementation studies evaluating chromogenic agar and hybrid qPCR-culture diagnostic pathways across SSA district laboratory networks would provide the evidence needed for national guideline development.

Conclusion

NAC species represent an important and growing component of VVC in women of reproductive age in SSA, with C. glabrata frequently reported among the dominant non-albicans taxa. These findings support the need for better species-level surveillance and standardized antifungal susceptibility testing. They should not, however, be interpreted as sufficient on their own to mandate a region-wide empirical treatment change.
