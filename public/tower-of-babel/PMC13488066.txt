Associations between diet, metabolome, gut microbiota and blood pressure in Australian adults
Authors: Jordan Stanford, Holly Supple, Clare E Collins, Erin D Clarke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEarly metabolomic and microbial markers of blood pressure (BP) dysregulation may be detectable before clinical hypertension develops. This exploratory study aimed to examine associations among dietary intake, BP, metabolomic profiles (plasma and urine), and gut microbiota composition. A secondary aim was to assess whether circulating metabolites mediate relationships between significant dietary factors and BP.MethodThis was a cross-sectional analysis of baseline data from a randomised cross-over trial. Usual dietary intake was assessed using the Australian Eating Survey (AES)® - Heart version Food Frequency Questionnaire. In-clinic BP measurements were measured and participants provided plasma, urine, and stool samples. Plasma and urine were analysed via untargeted metabolomics. Stool samples were collected for shotgun metagenomic sequencing, though metagenomic data was not included in this analysis. Associations between BP, individual metabolites, microbial taxa, and alpha diversity were assessed using linear regression with false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Causal mediation analysis was performed using nonparametric bootstrapping.ResultThirty-four Australian adults (mean age: 38.4 ± 18.1 years; 52.9% female) had complete data at baseline. Nut intake (servings/day and % energy) was the only dietary factor significantly associated with systolic BP (SBP), with higher intake linked to a 1.13 mmHg reduction. Twenty-nine plasma lipid metabolites were significantly associated with SBP after FDR correction. Of these, nine lipid-related metabolites, particularly 1,2-dilinoleoyl-GPC (18:2/18:2) and 1-linoleoyl-GPC (18:2), were observed to partially mediate the nut-SBP relationship. No urinary metabolites or microbial taxa were significantly associated with BP.ConclusionsIn this exploratory cross-sectional study, specific lipid metabolites were associated with SBP and partly accounted for the nut-SBP association. These hypothesis-generating findings suggest potential biomarkers of nut intake and BP regulation, warranting confirmation in larger longitudinal, interventional, and mechanistic studies.Trial RegistrationAustralian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (Registration number ACTRN12622001321730, Registration date 12/10/2022).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-026-01336-4.Keywords: Blood Pressure, Nut Intake, Lipid Metabolites, Plasma Metabolomics, Causal Mediation Analysis

Introduction

Hypertension is a major, yet preventable, contributor to global cardiovascular disease burden and premature mortality [1]. Although effective pharmacological treatments exist, only a third of those with hypertension receive effective treatment [2]. Therefore, prevention remains the most sustainable and scalable strategy. Diet is a well-established modifiable factor, with higher intakes of fruits, vegetables, and whole grains consistently associated with lower systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) across diverse populations [3]. However, there is great variability in how individuals respond to population-based dietary recommendations [4, 5]. This variability may be underpinned by limited understanding of biological mechanisms through which diet influences blood pressure (BP), limiting development of mechanistically targeted dietary interventions.

Metabolomics offers a promising avenue to elucidate these mechanisms. Through high-throughput profiling of small-molecule metabolites in biological samples, metabolomics provides an integrated snapshot of dietary exposures and host metabolic responses [6]. Emerging evidence suggests that circulating metabolites may not only serve as biomarkers of diet and disease risk but also function as mediators in the causal pathway between dietary intake and BP regulation. A recent systematic review highlighted that in people with, or at risk of high BP, more than 100 metabolites identified from feeding interventions were associated with BP [7]. Of these metabolites, only two (proline betaine and N-acetylneuraminate) were reproducible in more than one study [7]. Despite this potential, studies that concurrently assess dietary intake, untargeted metabolomic profiles, and BP within a single analytical framework in healthy, free-living populations remain limited. Undertaking integrative, hypothesis-generating approaches are crucial for identifying early biological signals, understanding the application of metabolomics and multi-omics approaches in practice, and informing the design of future interventions using reproducible methodologies [4].

Variation in gut microbial composition adds to the heterogeneity of individual responses to dietary interventions [8, 9]. In hypertensive populations, studies have reported consistent alterations in microbial composition, including reductions in short chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing taxa (Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Roseburia, Ruminococcus) and increases in pro-inflammatory or Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO)-associated genera (Prevotella, Klebsiella, Desulfovibrio) [10–12]. Australian research, notably from Marques and colleagues, has demonstrated that dietary fibre modulates gut microbiota composition and SCFA production, contributing to BP reduction in both animal models and hypertensive humans [13]. However, there remains an opportunity to move beyond dietary fibre as the only dietary focus and to consider a broader range of dietary components, such as vegetables, fruit, nuts, seeds and whole grains, that contain additional microbial-accessible substrates such as polyphenols. These food groups and associated compounds (i.e. polyphenols) may influence gut microbiota through distinct and complementary mechanisms beyond fibre fermentation [14, 15].

Existing studies have focused on individuals with clinically diagnosed hypertension, with limited investigation into microbial and metabolic variation across the full continuum of BP, particularly among normotensive or mildly hypertensive individuals. This represents a critical gap, as early microbial and metabolomic signatures of BP dysregulation may be detectable prior to the onset of clinical hypertension. Additionally, few studies within the Australian context have integrated multi-omics approaches including gut microbiota profiling, untargeted metabolomics, and detailed dietary assessments to characterise microbiota- and metabolite-BP relationships in self-identified healthy populations.

To address these gaps, this exploratory study leveraged available baseline data from a randomised, cross-over control trial to investigate associations among dietary intake, BP, plasma and urinary metabolomic profiles (using untargeted metabolomics), and gut microbiota composition in a small Australian adult cohort. An exploratory hypothesis that emerged prompted the application of causal mediation modelling to assess whether statistically significant metabolites might partially explain observed associations between key dietary components and BP.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study is a cross-sectional analysis of baseline data from a randomised, cross-over feeding study involving Australian adults (Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry; Registration number: ACTRN12622001321730; Registration date: 12/10/2022) [16]. The study was approved by the Hunter New England Human Research Ethics Committee, New Lambton Heights, Australia (Approval number: 2022/ETH01649) and the University of Newcastle’s Human Research Ethics Committee, Callaghan, Australia (Approval number: H-2022-0330). All participants provided informed, written consent prior to commencement.

Study population

The study population included participants aged 18 years and older living in the NSW Hunter region. Full details are provided in the study protocol published elsewhere [16]. Briefly, adults who had no known food allergies or intolerances, access to a smartphone with internet, or were taking no medication (or on stable medications not known to influence study outcomes) were eligible. Individuals with certain medical conditions were also excluded, such as type 1 or 2 diabetes, cancer receiving treatment, and those with uncontrolled hypertension. Volunteers were recruited using multiple strategies including flyers on public notice boards, media advertisements and social media platforms.

Study procedures

All assessments were collected during each participant’s first in-person visit at the Nutrition and Dietetics Clinical Research laboratory at the University of Newcastle, Callaghan, NSW, 2308 Australia, between December 2022 and July 2023. Demographic and lifestyle data recorded at baseline included self-reported medical history and current medication.

Dietary intake assessment

Dietary intake of all participants was assessed using the Australian Eating Survey (AES)® – Heart version, a validated Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) consisting of 177 self-administered questions that measure usual dietary intake over the past 3 months and has been validated against red blood cell fatty acids [17]. The AES-Heart provides data on both nutrient intake and food group intake, with a particular emphasis on heart healthy food and nutrients such as fat, sodium, legume, red meat and seafood intakes [17]. The AES-Heart uses data from the AUSNUT 2011-13 food nutrient database to calculate specific nutrient and food group intakes [18]. For this study, we selected to examine relevant nutrient and food group intakes commonly referenced in hypertension prevention and management guidelines, including fibre, sodium, potassium, magnesium, omega-3 and 6 fatty acids, fruits, vegetables, wholegrains, seafood and red meats [3, 19]. Food group intakes are reported as servings per day and/or percent energy.

Blood pressure assessment

SBP and DBP measurements were recorded in-clinic using standardised procedures with the Uscom BP+ supra-systolic oscillometric central BP device. Before measuring BP, participants were asked to sit comfortably for five minutes with their feet flat and legs uncrossed and to maintain even breathing. Three consecutive measurements were taken from the left arm with one-minute rests between each measure. The first measurement was discarded, and the average of the second two measurements was used.

Plasma and urinary metabolomics

Blood samples were collected by a trained phlebotomist following a minimum 12-hour fast. Participants were also advised to avoid vigorous physical activity and alcohol consumption for 24 h prior to their appointment. Spot urine samples (mid-stream) were self-collected by participants in sterile containers during the same data collection session. Blood samples were processed by centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and plasma was separated into aliquots, as were urine samples, and stored at -80 °C until batch shipment. All samples were shipped on dry ice for metabolomic analysis. Metabolite profiling of plasma and urine was performed by Metabolon Inc (Morrisville, NC, USA) using ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) as part of their Global Discovery Panel [20].

Gut microbiota

Faecal samples were collected within 3 days of individual clinic appointments using at-home collection kits, using Zymo Research FecesCatcher catchment paper [21] and DNA/RNA Shield™ faecal collection tubes [22]. Clear instructions, both verbal and written, were provided to participants regarding how to collect the samples correctly and they were instructed to store the samples at ambient temperature and return them either at their next clinic appointment or via prepaid reply post bags, whichever is more convenient and timelier. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, faecal samples can be viably stored at ambient temperatures for at least 30 days [22]. On receipt, faecal samples were stored at -80 °C until DNA extraction and sequencing.

Stool samples were thawed on ice, and 100–300 mg of each sample was used for genomic DNA (gDNA) extraction. Total gDNA was extracted using the Promega Faecal Microbiome DNA Kit in combination with the Maxwell RSC 48 System (Promega), following the manufacturer’s instructions with one modification: samples resuspended in lysis buffer were homogenized for 3 min using a Precellys 24 homogenizer (Bertin) at 5,000 rpm with 0.3 g of 0.1 mm and 0.1 g of 0.5 mm silica beads.

Library preparation was conducted using the Nextera DNA Flex Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, Cat# 20018705), and samples were indexed with IDT for Illumina Nextera DNA Unique Dual Indexes Sets A-D (Cat# 20027213-6). Indexed libraries were pooled in equimolar concentrations and sequenced using paired-end 150 bp chemistry on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at Microba Life Sciences (QLD, Australia), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was performed to a target depth of 10 Gb per sample, with a minimum sequencing output of 3 Gb per sample, corresponding to approximately 70–160 million paired-end reads.

Raw metagenomic sequencing (MGS) data were quality-filtered and trimmed using the KneadData pipeline (https://bitbucket.org/biobakery/kneaddata). Taxonomic profiling was then performed using MetaPhlAn3, applying default settings for microbial community classification.

Statistical methods

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics for study participants were presented as mean (standard deviation) or median with interquartile range for continuous variables, and as percentages for categorical variables. Associations between dietary factors and BP were assessed using multivariable linear regression adjusting for age, and sex. For dietary variables that were not already standardised to energy intake (i.e. percentage energy), such as food group servings or grams of specific nutrients, additional adjustments for total energy intake (kJ/day) were included to account for differences in total dietary consumption.

Urinary and plasma metabolomic data, along with faecal microbial species abundances, were filtered to retain only features detected in at least 20% of samples. Metabolite concentrations were log-transformed prior to analysis, while microbial relative abundances underwent arcsine square root transformation. Multiple linear regression models were used to examine associations between metabolites and both SBP and DBP, controlling for age and sex. Standardised beta coefficients were reported to indicate the strength and direction of these associations. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05. To account for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method within each sample type dataset.

Causal mediation analysis was conducted using the R package mediation [23] to explore whether metabolites that were significantly associated with BP statistically mediated the cross-sectional associations between significant dietary factors and BP measurements. For each metabolite identified as significantly associated with the BP measurement, two linear regression models were specified: one model predicting the metabolite from the dietary factor (mediator model), and another predicting BP from both the dietary factor and the metabolite (outcome model). Both models were adjusted for age and sex. The mediate() function was then used to estimate the average causal mediation effect (ACME), average direct effect (ADE), total effect, and the proportion mediated, with statistical inference based on nonparametric bootstrapping (boot = TRUE). This approach allowed for a preliminary investigation into the extent to which dietary effects on BP might be explained through specific metabolic pathways, while accounting for potential confounding. These findings are intended to generate hypotheses for future mechanistic and interventional research.

Results

Forty participants enrolled in the study, of whom 34 healthy Australian adults completed all study measures and were included in this analysis (Table 1). Approximately half of the sample (52.9%) were female, with a mean age of 38 years (range: 22–78 years). Mean body mass index (BMI) was 25.1 kg/m², with seven participants (21%) considered to be hypertensive, of whom three reported taking anti-hypertensive medications.

Dietary variables and blood pressure

Among the nutrients and food groups analysed, only the percentage of energy from nuts and the number of nut servings per day were statistically significantly inversely associated with SBP. This relationship remained significant after adjusting for age, sex (in both models), and total energy intake for nut servings (Fig. 1, Table S1).

Urinary metabolites

There were 116 statistically significant associations between urinary metabolites and BP, including 60 with SBP and 56 with DBP. Of these, 68 associations were negative (34 with SBP, 34 with DBP) and 48 were positive (26 with SBP, 22 with DBP) (Table S2). However, after applying a conservative multiple comparison correction, none remained statistically significant (Fig. 2).

Plasma metabolites

The study identified 188 statistically significant associations between plasma metabolites and BP, including 120 with SBP and 68 with DBP. Of these, 59 associations were negative (42 with SBP, 17 with DBP) and 129 were positive (78 with SBP, 51 with DBP) (Table S3). After multiple comparison corrections, associations between 29 plasma metabolites and SBP remained significant (none with DBP), with 15 showing a negative association and 14 showing a positive association (Fig. 2).

Gut microbiota diversity and composition

There was no significant difference in alpha-diversity indices (Figure S1, p > 0.05). However, 20 microbial species were associated with BP measures (Fig. 3). Among these, 13 species were associated with SBP; two of them, GGB74463 SGB54347 and Oscillospiraceae bacterium BX1, were positively associated, while the remaining species, including Anaerobutyricum hallii, Blautia obeum and Blautia faecicola showed a negative association. For DBP, seven species were identified: four were positively associated, including Bacteroides ovatus, and three negatively associated, including Parabacteroides goldsteinii. However, after adjusting for multiple comparisons, none of these associations remained statistically significant (Table S4).

Mediation analysis

Of the 29 metabolites significantly associated with SBP, nine demonstrated statistically significant indirect effects, indicating partial mediation of the nut intake-SBP relationship (Table S5). These included 1,2-dilinoleoyl-GPC (18:2/18:2), docosatrienoate (22:3n3), 1-linoleoyl-GPC (18:2), 2-linoleoyl-GPE (18:2), 1-(1-enyl-palmitoyl)-2-linoleoyl-GPC (P-16:0/18:2), 1-linoleoyl-2-linolenoyl-GPC (18:2/18:3), 1-margaroyl-2-linoleoyl-GPC (17:0/18:2), 1-stearoyl-2-dihomo-linolenoyl-GPC (18:0/20:3n3 or 6) and 1-(1-enyl-stearoyl)-2-linoleoyl-GPC (P-18:0/18:2). All mediating metabolites belonged to the lipid super-pathway. Two metabolites showed statistically significant mediation proportions and were identified as key mediators (Fig. 4). The metabolite 1,2-dilinoleoyl-GPC (18:2/18:2) exhibited an average causal mediation effect (ACME) of -1.04 mmHg (95% CI: -1.72 to -0.32, p = 0.004), mediating 92% of the total effect of nut intake on SBP. The metabolite 1-linoleoyl-GPC (18:2) showed an ACME of -0.57 mmHg (95% CI: -1.19 to -0.11, p = 0.012), accounting for 51% of the total effect (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This exploratory study identified that nut intake was the only dietary factor significantly associated with SBP in this sample of generally healthy Australian adults. Almonds were reported as the most frequently consumed nut, followed by cashews, mixed nuts, and walnuts. A higher intake of nuts (as a percentage of total energy) was associated with a 1.13 mmHg reduction in SBP. While this individual-level effect size may appear modest, even small reductions in SBP can lead to meaningful public health benefits. For instance, findings from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study (n = 15,744) suggest that a 1 mmHg decrease in SBP could prevent approximately 13 cases of heart failure per 100,000 person-years in Caucasians, and 20 cases per 100,000 person-years in African Americans [25].

This study offers insights into the potential mechanistic pathways linking nut intake to BP. Specifically, we discovered 29 circulating metabolites associated with SBP, with nine lipid-related metabolites partially mediating the relationship between nut intake and SBP. This is consistent with the broader cardioprotective profile of nuts [26, 27], which not only contain lipid-related compounds, are also naturally rich in dietary fibre, polyphenols, antioxidants, and potassium, while being low in sodium when unsalted. These co-occurring nutrients are known to support vascular function, aligning with existing literature demonstrating the cardiovascular benefits of nut consumption [19], including evidence specifically supporting SBP-lowering effects of cashews [28].

Two phospholipid metabolites, 1,2-dilinoleoyl-GPC (18:2/18:2) and 1-linoleoyl-GPC (18:2), were identified in exploratory causal mediation models as statistically accounting for 92% and 51% of the total nut-SBP association, respectively. These findings suggest that linoleoyl-containing phospholipids may represent metabolic correlates of BP or markers of nut intake but cannot be considered definitive mediators of BP regulation based on these cross-sectional data. Mechanistically, we hypothesise that linoleic‑acid-containing phospholipid incorporation into endothelial membranes enhances membrane fluidity and modulates endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS)-mediated signalling [29]. Furthermore, these phospholipids act as reservoirs for bioactive oxylipins that directly regulate vascular tone and inflammation [30]. We acknowledge, however, that these effects may be context-dependent, as free linoleic acid and its oxidised products have been linked to impaired insulin/Akt‑dependent eNOS activation and can influence nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, particularly under conditions of metabolic stress [31]. While neither metabolite has been directly linked to BP in previous studies, structurally related phospholipid metabolites, such as 1-linoleoyl-GPE (18:2) and 1-stearoyl-2-linoleoyl-GPC (18:0/18:2), were also associated with SBP in our study and have recently been associated with BP and hypertension risk [32]. This supports their potential relevance as candidate biomarkers of BP or cardiometabolic risk. Other metabolites identified in mediation models include ether-linked phospholipids (plasmalogens) such as 1-(1-enyl-palmitoyl)-2-linoleoyl-GPC (P-16:0/18:2), and 1-(1-enyl-stearoyl)-2-linoleoyl-GPC (P-18:0/18:2), which are of interest given emerging evidence for their roles in inflammation, oxidative stress, and membrane signalling [33, 34]. Notably, 1-(1-enyl-stearoyl)-2-linoleoyl-GPC (P-18:0/18:2) has been linked to altered heart rate in paediatric populations with orthostatic intolerance [35]. However, the causal relevance between this specific metabolite, and ether-phospholipids more broadly, with BP regulation in the general population remains to be elucidated and is an active area of investigation. Additionally, 2-linoleoyl-GPC (18:2), a lysophosphatidylcholine was recently proposed as a statistically significant but weakly discriminating biomarker for early coronary atherosclerosis [36]. Given that elevated BP is a major risk factor for atherosclerosis [36, 37], this metabolite may reflect early vascular changes relevant to the nut-BP relationship. Overall, these associations are intriguing but could reflect shared relationships with nut intake, BP, or underlying metabolic status rather than causal pathways, and require confirmation in longitudinal, mechanistic, and interventional studies.

Although associations between gut microbial taxa and SBP did not retain statistical significance after multiple comparison correction, which is likely due to limited statistical power, several noteworthy trends emerged that merit discussion and further research. Higher abundance of SCFA-producing taxa, such as Anaerobutyricum hallii (formerly Eubacterium hallii), were associated with lower SBP. A. hallii is a known butyrate producer that enhances gut barrier integrity and regulates immune responses, with its metabolite butyrate linked to improved metabolic profiles and reduced arterial pressure in both human and animal studies [38, 39]. Similarly, Pseudoflavonifractor capillosus, a flavonoid-metabolising bacterium, showed negative associations with DBP. Its capacity to convert polyphenols into bioactive compounds may support vascular health through anti-inflammatory and antioxidant pathways [40, 41]. While these findings are preliminary, they suggest that gut microbiota composition may modulate BP responses to dietary intake, including nuts, highlighting the need for investigation in larger, well-powered cohorts.

This study is strengthened by its integrative multi-omics approach, combining untargeted metabolomics and microbiome data with detailed dietary assessments and clinical BP measurements. Nevertheless, several important limitations warrant consideration. First, the small sample size and cross-sectional design limit statistical power, generalisability, and the ability to infer causal relationships from the observed associations. Instead, these findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating, guiding future longitudinal and interventional studies. Additionally, the identified metabolites may reflect coordinated physiological responses or shared metabolic states rather than discrete causal mediators of BP regulation. It is also important to interpret the estimated “proportion mediated” as a statistical measure of the extent to which the association between nut intake and blood pressure is explained by these metabolites, rather than as evidence of a definitive biological causal pathway. Collectively, these results highlight plausible diet-metabolite-BP pathways that merit replication in independent cohorts and experimental studies to assess their robustness and clinical significance. Owing to the small sample size, we did not adjust for additional potential confounders (e.g., BMI, physical activity, or medication use), and residual confounding by these unmeasured factors remains a study limitation. The observed interquartile range of nut intake (2.4–6.6% of total energy), and therefore the association with SBP, might not be attributable to nut consumption in isolation. Rather, it may reflect broader dietary patterns that commonly include nuts as components of nutrient-dense, minimally processed diets. Dietary patterns such as DASH and Mediterranean diets emphasise foods rich in unsaturated fat sources, including nuts, and are well-established for their favourable effects on cardiometabolic outcomes [19, 42]. Given that this population sample demonstrated generally high overall diet quality, nut intake may serve as a proxy for healthful dietary behaviours. Consequently, the observed metabolic signatures may represent not only direct biochemical effects of nut consumption but also capture indirect influences from co-consumed foods or associated dietary patterns. While gut microbiota associations with BP were not statistically significant after multiple comparison correction, it is possible that functional changes in microbial metabolism, rather than taxonomic abundance, may be more relevant and should be explored in the future.

Conclusion

This exploratory study found that higher nut intake was associated with lower SBP. Several lipid metabolites, particularly linoleoyl-containing phospholipids and ether-linked lipids, statistically explained part of this association, suggesting they may be metabolic correlates of nut intake or BP regulation, or candidate causal biomarkers. While the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, these findings are hypothesis-generating and warrant confirmation in larger longitudinal, interventional, and mechanistic studies.
