From Marginal to Central: Marginal Zone‐like B Cells as Critical Targets in Cladribine‐Treated Multiple Sclerosis
Authors: Marta Pirronello, Mario Picozza, Gisella Guerrera, Silvia Corbisiero, Andrea Misiti, Roberta C Placido, Alice Verdiani, Lorenzo de Marco, Manolo Sambucci, Elena Olivieri, Daniela F Angelini, Esmeralda Quartuccio, Luca Prosperini, Carla Tortorella, Maria C Buscarinu, Valeria Zancan, Francesca de Masi, Marco Salvetti, Claudio Gasperini, Giovanna Borsellino, Luca Battistini

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMultiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disease where B cells play a central pathogenic role. Cladribine, an oral therapy, provides durable benefits by reshaping lymphocyte populations, yet its specific long‐term impact on distinct B‐cell subsets is not fully understood. This study aimed to define cladribine's longitudinal effects on B‐cell subsets and characterize their inflammatory properties.MethodsWe conducted a 48‐month longitudinal study of 36 persons with relapsing–remitting MS treated with cladribine who had completed 2 annual treatment cycles, using high‐parameter flow cytometry to track B‐cell subset dynamics. In a parallel cross‐sectional analysis, B cells from 16 untreated patients and 16 healthy donors were profiled for polyfunctional cytokine production granulocyte‐macrophage colony stimulating factor (GM‐CSF), interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) and key surface markers.ResultsCladribine induced profound, sustained depletion of memory B cells, with marginal zone‐like (MZ‐like) cells showing the deepest and most persistent loss, alongside reconstitution of transitional and naïve populations. Functionally, MZ‐like B cells were the most potent polyfunctional producers of proinflammatory cytokines and immunoglobulin M (IgM), a capacity significantly enhanced in persons with MS compared to healthy donors, and expressed the highest levels of CD1c and CD1d, consistent with specialization in lipid antigen presentation.InterpretationMZ‐like B cells are a dominant, polyfunctional inflammatory subset in MS, equipped for non‐canonical lipid antigen presentation and IgM secretion. They represent a preferential pharmacological target of cladribine. Sustained reduction of this pathogenic memory pool, followed by repopulation with less inflammatory B cells, offers a mechanistic basis for cladribine's durable clinical benefit and nominates MZ‐like cells as biomarkers of response and therapeutic targets. Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disease of the central nervous system (CNS) characterized by demyelination and neurodegeneration. 1 In parallel with imaging, serum neurofilament light chain (NfL) has emerged as a sensitive marker of neuroaxonal injury and MS disease activity. 2 , 3 Immunoglobulin G (IgG) oligoclonal bands (OCBs) represent a hallmark of intrathecal immune activation. 4 , 5 Beyond diagnostic IgG OCBs, a subset of patients shows intrathecal immunoglobulin M (IgM) OCB, frequently directed against myelin lipids, which consistently mark a high‐risk phenotype with faster relapse accrual and disability progression. 6 , 7 , 8

The presence of OCBs implicates B lymphocytes in MS pathogenesis. The success of B cell‐targeted therapies has transformed MS treatment, underscoring the central role of these cells. 9 , 10 , 11 B cells are heterogeneous and newly generated B cells exit the bone marrow as transitional cells, mature into naïve cells, and, on antigen encounter, differentiate into memory B cells. Memory compartments include classical switched memory cells (IgD−IgM−CD27+) and “unconventional”, or rather alternative, memory cells, comprising unswitched or marginal zone‐like (MZ‐like) memory cells (IgMhiIgDloCD27+) and double‐negative (DN) memory B cells that lack CD27 (IgD−IgM−CD27−), which include the CD11c+ DN2 subset (IgD−CD27−CD11c+), an “alternative”/extrafollicular memory population that has been reported to expand in chronic inflammatory and autoimmune settings. 12 , 13

Monoclonal antibodies against CD20 rapidly deplete circulating B cells and reduce MS relapse rates and progression. 9 , 11 , 14 However, they spare plasma cells, so OCBs often persist even during clinical remission. 15 This persistence of antibody production, despite remission, suggests that the therapeutic benefit of B‐cell depletion arises from eliminating also non–antibody‐producing pathogenic B‐cell subsets that support CNS inflammation.

It has been proposed that effective MS treatments converge on the removal or functional inactivation of proinflammatory B cells, particularly memory B cells. 16 B cells can drive inflammation by presenting autoantigens to T cells 17 and/or by secreting proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), and GM‐CSF. 18 Critically, B cells organize into ectopic lymphoid follicles within the CNS during chronic autoimmunity, where they can support germinal‐center‐like responses and propagate local inflammation. 19 , 20 , 21

Cladribine efficacy is highly dependent on memory B‐lymphocyte depletion, 22 , 23 , 24 yet its specific impact on distinct memory B‐cell subsets and their role in MS deserves focused investigation. The preferential depletion of memory B cells, with relative sparing of naïve B cells, reshapes the B‐cell repertoire toward a population largely untested or only lightly imprinted by prior antigenic encounters. 25 , 26 , 27

Here, we investigate how cladribine modulates the composition and function of peripheral B‐cell subsets in MS. First, in a longitudinal cohort of cladribine‐treated MS patients, we delineate the dynamics of B‐cell subset reconstitution over several years, and we then profile the phenotypic features and the proinflammatory capabilities of different B‐cell subsets, identifying which subsets preferentially produce cytokines that can drive MS pathology. Our findings reveal that MZ‐like memory B cells have an outsized proinflammatory capacity in vitro and are preferentially depleted and slow to reconstitute in vivo after cladribine treatment. DN B cells, which also exhibit an activated phenotype with high antigen‐presenting potential, are affected but recover more rapidly. These results point to alternative memory B cells, especially the IgM‐producing MZ‐like subset, as key contributors to MS immunopathology and as selective targets of cladribine, potentially underpinning its durable efficacy.

Materials and Methods

Cohorts

Persons with MS (pwMS) (n = 36; age: median = 39 [18–57]; sex: 25 female, 11 male) were enrolled from the San Camillo and Sant'Andrea Hospitals, in Rome, to define the immune landscape before and during cladribine treatment (Tables 1 and 2). Sample size was defined by the availability of cladribine‐treated patients at recruiting hospitals between 2018 and 2025. Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Cladribine treatment involves 2 cycles over a period of 2 years. Each cycle consists of 2 treatment weeks: 1 at the beginning of the first month and 1 at the start of the second month of that treatment year. During each treatment week, the patient takes 1 or 2 tablets as a single daily dose for 4 or 5 days, depending on body weight. After completing the 2 cycles, no further treatment is required for the following 3 to 4 years. Blood samples were collected in heparinized tubes and processed immediately. Venous blood was drawn at baseline (0 months) and subsequently at 6, 12, 24, 36, and 48 months during therapy.

Healthy donors (HDs) were anonymous volunteer blood donors from the Italian Red Cross blood bank from whom buffy coats were obtained, and because samples were fully anonymized, individual demographic and clinical data are not available. All donors were screened and accepted according to Italian regulations and national guidelines for blood donation, which require adult age (18–65 years), adequate general health, and exclusion of donors with chronic inflammatory/autoimmune diseases, malignancies, active infections, or ongoing immunosuppressive therapies. For the MS validation experiments, we selected 15 untreated relapsing pwMS, comprising 8 clinically stable patients and 7 sampled during an acute clinical relapse. High‐parameter phenotypic and functional assays were prioritized for cohorts/time points with sufficient recovered B‐cell numbers and available cryopreserved material to ensure robust subset‐level analyses.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consent

The subjects' consent was obtained according to the Declaration of Helsinki and the study was approved by the ethical Committees of the recruiting Hospitals.

Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells Isolation

Blood samples were collected and processed immediately under standardized protocols to minimize variability. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from whole blood by density gradient centrifugation using standard procedures (Ficoll‐Paque Plus, GE Healthcare, Buc, France).

Absolute Cell Counts

Absolute counts of circulating B cells were determined by a lyse‐no‐wash method based on staining 50μL of peripheral blood with fluorescent‐monoclonal antibody aCD19 as previously described. 28 , 29 After 15 minutes at room temperature (RT), 450μl of FACS lyse solution (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, USA) 1:10 were added. Following 7 minutes of lysis, samples were acquired on a Cytoflex cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, USA).

Flow Cytometry

PBMCs were stained using a 19‐color flow cytometry panel designed to identify 7 distinct B cell subsets. For each subset, we measured the frequency (% of CD19+ cells) and the expression of functional markers. PBMCs were collected and placed in V‐bottom 96‐well plates. They were subsequently resuspended in 30μl of previously titrated antibodies (Table 3) diluted in Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences). Following a 20‐minute incubation at room temperature, the suspension was washed, and samples were subsequently acquired on a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Quality control beads (Beckman Coulter) were used daily to check and standardize instrument performances. For intracellular staining (ICS), post‐culture cells were pelleted in V‐bottom 96‐well plates and resuspended in 30μl of pre‐optimized antibody cocktails diluted in Brilliant Stain Buffer. Cells were incubated at room temperature for 15 minutes with a panel of antibodies for surface staining to identify the main circulating B cell subsets. Following this, cells were fixed with 3.6% formaldehyde in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). Intracellular staining was performed by incubating the fixed cells in 30μl of antibodies targeting cytokines (GM‐CSF PE, IL‐6 PE‐CF594, TNF‐α PE‐Cy7, IL‐10 APC Al700) diluted in 0.25% (w/v) saponin (Sigma, Buchs, Switzerland) solution for permeabilization. Samples were acquired on a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer within 6 hours of fixation. Flow cytometry data were analyzed in FlowJo ver. 10.10 (BD Biosciences). Cytokine positivity was defined using single‐cytokine gates for GM‐CSF, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, and cytokine combinations/polyfunctionality were quantified by Boolean combination analysis of these gates.

B Cell Stimulation

PBMCs (2 × 106/condition) were stimulated with cytosine‐phosphate‐guanine (CpG) (Invivogen, San Diego, USA; 1μg/ml) for 1 hour and then phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (Sigma; 10μg/ml), ionomycin (Sigma; 100μg/ml) and brefeldin A (Sigma; 10μg/ml) were added to the culture. After additional 5 hours, cells were harvested and directly stained for ICS.

Antibody Production

For assessment of antibody production, index‐sorted B‐cell subsets (naive, classical memory [cMem], MZ‐like, and double negative 2 [DN2]) from HDs and untreated pwMS were cultured at limiting dilution in 384‐well plates with irradiated CD40L‐expressing 3 T3 feeder cells, IL‐2, and IL‐21 for 14 days. Supernatants were then collected and IgG, IgM, and IgA concentrations were measured by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Wells with optical density above the prespecified threshold (OD 450nm ≥0.3) were scored as antibody‐producing, and the frequency and isotype distribution of responder wells were calculated for each subset and donor.

Serum NfL and Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein Dosage

Quantitative measurement of serum neurofilament light chain (sNfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (sGFAP) levels was performed in MS patients at baseline and after 6, 12, 24, and 36 months of treatment with Cladribine. Biomarker concentrations were determined using ultrasensitive single molecule array (SiMoA) immunoassays.

Frozen serum samples were stored at −80°C and thawed at room temperature before analysis. Samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 minutes to remove debris. Assays were performed on the fully automated SiMoA SR‐X Analyzer (Quanterix Corporation, Billerica, MA) using the commercially available NF‐Light Advantage Kit (item 104073) for sNfL and the GFAP Discovery Kit (item 102336) for sGFAP, following a 2‐step digital protocol.

All calibrators and serum samples were analyzed in duplicate, according to the manufacturers' instructions, including the use of appropriate internal controls and standard curves. Serum samples were diluted 1:4 with sample diluent before loading into individual wells. This dilution factor was validated for the accurate quantification of both sNfL and sGFAP.

To reduce intra‐assay variability, all longitudinal samples from the same patient were analyzed within the same analytical run. The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) and the limit of detection (LOD) for each assay were defined in accordance with the manufacturers' specifications.

Statistics

Statistical testing and graphing were performed with GraphPad Prism version 10.5. Friedman test with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons was performed to compare marker expression and cytokine production on different B cell subsets in HDs. Multiple Mann–Whitney tests with FDR correction were used to compare HDs and pwMS for cytokine production capabilities of the different B cell subsets. For the limiting‐dilution cloning assay, the positivity threshold for each ELISA plate was the 95th percentile of the negative‐control distribution, and wells above this threshold were scored as antibody‐producing clones. Inferential analysis was performed at the donor level. For each donor and subset, the percentage of responder wells secreting each isotype was the unit of analysis (n = 3 HD, n = 4 pwMS), and HD versus pwMS comparisons were performed by 2‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Because of missing data, changes in longitudinal sNfL levels, as well as in total B cells and B cell subsets' number after cladribine treatment initiation were compared by Kruskal‐Wallis test with Dunn's post hoc correction. Patients were stratified by baseline NfL (high vs low) and GFAP levels (elevated vs normal) to assess treatment effects. Random Forest regression (R package randomForest) was used to infer which B cell subset was more predictive of the unfolding of the overall B‐cell compartment over time.

Results

Cladribine Induces Long‐Term Reconfiguration of B Cell Subsets with Persistent MZ‐Like B Cell Depletion in MS

We longitudinally tracked neuroinflammatory biomarkers and peripheral B‐cell subsets in a cohort of 36 pwMS with the relapsing–remitting form of the disease treated with cladribine. Blood samples were collected at baseline (pre‐treatment) and at 6, 12, 24, 36, and 48 months after treatment initiation (Fig 1A). To assess neuroinflammation, we first quantified sNfL levels, a well‐established biomarker of neuronal injury, across all patients at baseline and during longitudinal follow‐up (see Fig. 1B). Given that not all patients in our cohort were treatment‐naïve, we observed overall lower levels of sNfL compared to naïve untreated relapsing MS cohorts. 3 , 30 To identify patients with potentially ongoing neuroinflammatory activity, we stratified the cohort based on NfL levels at baseline using the 75th percentile of values observed in our HD population as a threshold (not shown). Patients with baseline NfL levels above this cut‐off (n = 31) showed a significant and sustained decline in NfL levels following cladribine treatment, whereas patients below the threshold (n = 7) exhibited stable low levels over time (see Fig 1B). Because prior treatment exposure could contribute to lower baseline NfL, we compared baseline age, disability, and treatment history between the 2 NfL strata. The low‐NfL group consisted exclusively of younger patients who had all received at least 1 prior disease‐modifying therapy (DMT) (median age, 34 years; median EDSS, 0.5). In contrast, the high‐NfL stratum included all treatment‐naïve patients together with those switching therapy (median age, 43 years; median EDSS, 1.0) (Tables 1 and 2).

We next analyzed sGFAP levels as a biomarker of neurodegeneration. To explore treatment effects in patients with evidence of astrocytic injury, we stratified the cohort based on the upper interquartile range (IQR) of GFAP values reported in age‐matched healthy controls. 31 Among patients with elevated baseline GFAP, levels remained stable or showed a slight decreasing trend following cladribine treatment, whereas GFAP remained consistently low in the other subgroup (Fig S1).

Cladribine treatment was accompanied by a rapid and deep depletion of total circulating CD19+ B cells, which reached a nadir by 6 months and began a slow recovery after 12 months (see Fig 1C). This overall reconstitution, however, masked striking differences in the recovery kinetics of specific B‐cell subsets. Throughout the study, B‐cell subsets were defined by flow cytometry using the same stringent gating strategy applied consistently across all experiments, and which identified transitional B cells, naïve B cells, MZ‐like memory B cells, classical switched memory B cells, antibody‐secreting cells (ASC; comprising plasmablasts and plasma cells), and double negative (DN) B cells, stratified into DN2 and DN1/3 by CD11c expression (Fig S2). At baseline, absolute numbers of all memory B‐cell subsets, including classical switched memory, MZ‐like, and DN subsets, were comparable between the high‐ and low‐NfL groups, between treatment‐naïve patients, and those previously treated with DMTs, with no enrichment of any specific memory subset (not shown).

Proportional symbol plots illustrate the early and profound contraction of most B cell subsets following therapy, contrasted by the gradual expansion of naïve and transitional compartments, and sustained suppression of memory and MZ‐like B cells (see Fig. 1D), which is consistent with a significant remodeling and rejuvenation of the immune repertoire. In parallel, fold‐change analyses across major lineages show a transient reduction in total T cells with subsequent recovery, alongside relatively stable NK and monocyte compartments (Fig S3).

Extending the analysis to long‐term immune reconstitution, baseline‐to‐36‐month comparisons (see Fig 1E) indicate that only transitional B cells are significantly increased, whereas MZ‐like B cells remain significantly reduced relative to baseline, and all other subsets are not different from baseline at 36 months.

Fold‐change analysis demonstrated that B‐cell reconstitution differed markedly across subsets. Transitional and naïve B cells progressively expanded, whereas memory populations remained depleted, with MZ‐like B cells showing the most sustained reduction (Fig. 1F). At 36 months, log2 fold‐change values indicated that transitional and naïve B cells had increased relative to baseline, while cMem, DN, and MZ‐like B cells remained below baseline levels; the greatest reduction was observed in the MZ‐like subset (Fig. 1G). Random forest regression further identified MZ‐like and cMem B cells as the variables with the greatest contribution to predicting longitudinal changes in the total B‐cell compartment (Fig. 1H).

Together, these findings indicate that cladribine reduces neuroinflammatory biomarker levels in patients with biochemical evidence of neuroinflammation at baseline, and drives a profound, selective reshaping of the circulating B‐cell compartment, characterized by durable depletion of memory subsets, especially MZ‐like cells, alongside preferential expansion of immature and naïve populations.

MZ‐Like B Cells Exhibit a Distinct Antigen Presentation Profile Via the Non‐Canonical CD1 Pathway

Building on the finding that cladribine persistently depletes MZ‐like B cells, we next investigated the phenotypic and functional properties of human B cell subsets to understand the immunological significance of this durable and selective depletion. We immunophenotyped ex vivo B‐cell subsets in 3 cohorts analyzed in parallel: HDs, stable pwMS, and pwMS sampled during clinical relapse, using thawed cryopreserved PBMC and the same gating strategy and antibody panel across groups, enabling direct subset‐ and cohort‐level comparisons (Fig 2). Across antigen presenting cell (APC)‐related molecules, DN2 and MZ‐like cells carried the clearest signatures. DN2 cells were consistently MHC‐II high (HLA‐DR and HLA‐DP), but in MS they showed lower HLA‐ABC, and HLA‐DP was further reduced during relapse. In contrast, the CD1 axis was concentrated in MZ‐like cells, which had the highest CD1c + fraction and the highest CD1d expression, and DN1 + DN3 remained largely CD1 low. Notably, DN2 also showed a tendency toward higher CD1d in MS, particularly during relapse. Co‐stimulatory and checkpoint markers distributed in a subset‐specific manner with additional modulation by clinical state. CD86 was most prominent on ASCs and detectable on DN2. ASC CD86 was lower in stable MS than in controls, whereas DN2 CD86 was higher during relapse. MZ‐like cells were the dominant CD83‐positive subset and showed higher CD84 in MS, most evident in stable disease. CD80 was largely confined to MZ‐like and naïve/transitional compartments and was reduced in stable MS within naïve/transitional cells. CD40 was broadly expressed across non‐DN2 subsets and tended to be lower in stable MS with partial restoration in relapse. On the inhibitory axis, PD‐L1 (CD274) was strongly enriched in DN2 and was reduced in MS, with the lowest levels during relapse, and PD‐L2 (CD273) remained near background. CD200 was consistently highest on naïve and transitional cells, but in stable MS it was reduced across multiple compartments (including DN1 + DN3, MZ‐like, and naïve/transitional), whereas relapse was associated with higher CD200 in several differentiated subsets, most notably ASCs, cMem, and MZ‐like cells.

Collectively, these profiles position DN2 and MZ‐like B cells as dominant antigen‐presenting and immunomodulatory hubs among circulating B‐cell subsets through complementary, non‐overlapping mechanisms of T‐cell activation: DN2 via classical peptide presentation with strong co‐stimulation and MZ‐like via lipid antigen presentation and dual checkpoint engagement.

MZ‐Like B Cells Are Potent Producers of Proinflammatory Cytokines

To define cytokine production potential at the subset level, PBMC from HDs were stimulated under short‐term conditions that preserved subset identity. Dimensionality reduction with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) revealed 3 major islands within the B cell compartment (Fig 3A), each corresponding to a distinct immunophenotype based on CD38, IgD, IgM, CD24, CD27, and CD11c expression (see Fig 3B,C).

We measured GM‐CSF, IL6, and TNF‐α, 3 cytokines implicated in B cell‐driven inflammation in MS. 18 , 32 , 33 GM‐CSF production was almost entirely confined to memory subsets (i1 and i2), whereas naïve/transitional cells (i3) where weak producers. IL‐6 and TNF‐α were more broadly distributed, but still enriched in memory islands. Quantitative analysis showed that i2 (MZ‐like) cells had the highest proportion of cytokine‐positive cells for all 3 cytokines (see Fig 3D,E). Boolean combination analysis demonstrated that i2 B cells were the most polyfunctional, with a significantly greater fraction of GM‐CSF+IL‐6+TNF‐α+ triple‐producers than i1 or i3 (see Fig 3F,G).

These findings were independently confirmed by manual gating, which also showed that MZ‐like B cells had the highest IL‐6 production and were strong producers of GM‐CSF and TNF‐α (see Fig 3H). cMem B cells were also cytokine‐competent but produced less IL‐6, whereas naïve, transitional, and DN B cells produced low levels of all 3 cytokines. A direct comparison of cMem versus MZ‐like B cells (see Fig 3I) confirmed significantly higher polyfunctionality in the MZ‐like subset (Fig 3J, 3L).

Collectively, these data identify MZ‐like B cells as the most potent and polyfunctional cytokine producers among peripheral B‐cell subsets, highlighting their potential to drive CNS inflammation in MS and providing a mechanistic rationale for their selective and sustained depletion by cladribine.

Enhanced Proinflammatory Cytokine Profile in MZ‐Like Cells in MS Patients

To determine whether the cytokine‐producing capacity of B‐cell subsets differs between health and disease, we analyzed intracellular cytokine production in peripheral B cells from 16 HDs and 16 untreated relapsing pwMS after short‐term stimulation.

GM‐CSF‐producing B cells showed a non‐significant trend toward higher frequencies in MS patients across all subsets, with the highest levels in MZ‐like and cMem B cells (Fig 4). IL‐6 production showed a similar pattern, appearing higher in MS patients across several B‐cell subsets, particularly in MZ‐like, DN2, and cMem B cells, but again without statistical significance. In contrast, TNF‐α production was significantly increased in MS patients in the MZ and naïve B‐cell subsets, with a similar trend in all subsets. Representative data from a single donor, illustrating cytokine production across all B‐cell subsets, are shown in Figure S4. We next assessed the frequency of polyfunctional B cells (GM‐CSF+, IL‐6+, and TNF‐α+). These were more abundant in MS patients, especially in the MZ‐like subset, and remained rare in naïve and transitional B cells across both groups. Direct comparison of MZ‐like versus cMem B cells consistently showed higher frequencies of triple‐positive cells in MZ‐like B cells, a difference more pronounced in MS patients, suggesting disproportionate proinflammatory potential in disease.

MZ‐Like B Cells Are Dominant Antibody Producers and Skew toward IgM in MS

To characterize the humoral output of B‐cell subsets, naïve, cMem, MZ‐like, and DN2 B cells from 3 HDs and 4 untreated pwMS were index‐sorted at single‐cell density and cultured on CD40L‐expressing 3 T3 feeders with IL‐2 and IL‐21 for 14 days. Supernatants were assayed by ELISA for IgG, IgM, and IgA. The positivity threshold for each plate was the 95th percentile of the negative‐control distribution, and wells exceeding it were scored as antibody‐producing clones. For each donor and subset, the percentage of responder wells secreting each isotype was computed (Fig 5).

IgG‐secreting wells (see Fig 5, top) were the dominant output of DN2 cells in both cohorts and accounted for the majority of MZ‐like output in HDs. In pwMS, the fraction of IgG‐secreting MZ‐like well was reduced, whereas cMem and naïve fractions were comparable between cohorts. A trend toward increased IgM contribution by MZ‐like wells was observed in pwMS (see Fig 5, middle), and in HDs, cMem, and naïve compartments provided the largest fractions of IgM‐producing clones and the MZ‐like subset contributed only a minor share, whereas in pwMS this pattern was inverted, with MZ‐like cells reaching a several‐fold higher IgM‐secreting fraction than in HDs (2‐way ANOVA on donor‐level values, p = 0.094 vs HD). IgA‐secreting clones (see Fig 5, bottom) were a minor component across all subsets and showed no subset‐ or cohort‐specific pattern.

Clone‐level OD values stratified by donor (Fig S5) show the same subset‐specific patterns within individual donors of both cohorts. Compared with cMem B cells, MZ‐like cells in pwMS retain substantial IgG‐secreting capacity while preferentially contributing IgM‐producing clones, as visualized by mapping each antibody‐secreting clone back onto its original index‐sort position (Fig S6). All together, these findings support a role for MZ‐like B cells as IgM‐competent memory producers whose humoral output is redirected toward IgM in pwMS.

Discussion

B cells in MS are now understood as multi‐purpose instigators of neuroinflammation rather than mere antibody factories. Our longitudinal data add to this view by showing that cladribine does not simply deplete circulating lymphocytes; instead, it selectively prunes the B‐cell network, removing nodes with the greatest inflammatory leverage while permitting a measured regrowth of less experienced compartments. The dominant signature is a durable loss of memory subsets alongside expansion of transitional and naïve cells, a pattern consistent with an immunological reset, in agreement with previous studies. 22 , 25 , 26 , 34 Memory B cells are known to be particularly sensitive to cladribine because they show a high ratio of the enzyme deoxycytidine kinase (dCK) to 5′‐nucleotidase. This high enzyme ratio causes the preferential phosphorylation of cladribine, a deoxyadenosine analogue, within these cells. The process leads to the intracellular accumulation of its toxic form, 2‐chloro‐2'‐deoxyadenosine triphosphate (2‐CdATP), which in turn causes DNA damage and triggers apoptosis, resulting in a selective and long‐lasting depletion of the memory B cell pool. 22

Importantly, among memory populations the depletion is deepest and most persistent in the MZ‐like subset, with comparatively milder declines in cMem and DN cells. To place these longitudinal dynamics in mechanistic context, we profiled antigen‐presentation and co‐regulatory programs across HDs, untreated stable pwMS, and relapse pwMS using the same high‐parameter panel and obtaining a subset‐resolved reference map of antigen‐presentation and immunomodulatory wiring (HLA and CD1c/CD1d together with co‐stimulatory/checkpoint molecules) across circulating B‐cell compartments, establishing the baseline hierarchy in health and revealing selective, subset‐specific remodeling in MS.

Within this map, DN2 cells retain a strong MHC class II signature (high HLA‐DR/HLA‐DP), consistent with preserved capacity for CD4‐facing peptide presentation, yet in MS they show reduced HLA‐ABC and a tendency of higher CD1d. It is possible that, in an environment where demyelination and oxidative injury generate abundant myelin‐derived and modified lipids, the antigen‐presenting interface of effector‐like B cells may tilt toward lipid handling and display. Active MS lesions show prominent oxidative damage including oxidized lipids, and demyelination necessarily liberates large quantities of myelin lipids that can become immunologically salient. 35 In parallel, CD1 pathways are well positioned to sense and present such lipid species. CD1 expression is increased in CNS inflammatory demyelination in MS and in its murine model experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, and has been explicitly discussed as a route for myelin‐lipid autoimmunity, including sulfatide and related glycolipids presented by CD1 molecules, with demonstrable sulfatide‐CD1d‐restricted T‐cell reactivity in humans. 36 , 37 Importantly, intrathecal IgM oligoclonal responses against myelin lipids associate with more inflammatory disease courses, implying that lipid antigens can represent sustained immune targets in at least a subset of patients. 38 MS is associated not only with altered antigen‐presentation hardware, but also with tuning of the co‐stimulation/checkpoint balance in the same effector‐like compartments. MZ‐like cells combine an activated APC profile with increased interaction potential. In parallel, DN2 cells, already positioned as strong APCs, show a progressive reduction of PD‐L1 toward relapse, suggesting a relative loss of inhibitory tone precisely within the subset most enriched for antigen‐presentation markers. CD86 is reduced on patients' circulating ASCs, consistent with a pool skewed toward more terminal, highly secretory plasmablast/plasma‐cell states in which classical co‐stimulatory programs are physiologically attenuated. In contrast, the relative increase of CD86 on DN2 during relapse fits with these cells occupying an earlier, more recently activated extrafollicular effector stage that still retains robust T cell co‐stimulatory capacity.

Beyond surface phenotypes, these subsets diverge significantly in their inflammatory output. In functional assays, MZ‐like cells were the dominant producers of GM‐CSF, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, and this polyfunctionality was amplified in MS versus HDs. By contrast, DN2 cells retained high antigen‐presenting potential, but produced fewer proinflammatory cytokines and reconstituted more rapidly after cladribine, suggesting a more transient contribution to post‐treatment immune activation.

The pronounced and durable depletion of MZ‐like B cells is clinically notable given their link to aggressive MS pathology. Intrathecal IgM OCBs, especially myelin‐lipid–specific, mark a high‐risk MS phenotype, predicting ≈11‐fold higher second‐relapse hazard and greater EDSS. 6 , 7 Given that MZ‐like cells specialize in lipid‐antigen handling and proinflammatory output and produce IgM, a contribution of MZ‐like programs to the intrathecal IgM repertoire is plausible, and cladribine's durable depletion of circulating MZ‐like memory may reduce an upstream source of lipid‐reactive IgM and antigen‐presenting/cytokine activity. Furthermore, meningeal ectopic lymphoid follicles, which are linked to greater disease severity and cortical pathology, are populated by distinct B‐cell clusters, including a notable enrichment of B1/MZ‐like cells. 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 These structures host self‐perpetuating B‐cell circuits that sustain chronic, smoldering inflammation. The combination of robust antigen‐presentation machinery and polyfunctional cytokine release positions MZ‐like cells as plausible drivers of these CNS‐compartmentalized responses. Beyond MS, MZ/MZ‐like B cells have also been implicated as dysregulated innate‐like memory populations in systemic autoimmunity, including vasculitis and lupus, where they can amplify IgM‐driven and cytokine‐mediated inflammation. 43 Additionally, single cell profiling of rheumatoid arthritis synovium has identified a CD1c+ B‐cell subset transcriptionally consistent with recirculating MZ B cells that produces IL‐6 and TNF‐α and is enriched in T/B‐cell–rich inflammatory tissue states, underscoring the broader involvement of MZ‐type B cells in human autoimmunity. 44

Conversely, DN2 cells are strongly linked to extrafollicular responses and have been consistently associated with systemic autoimmunity and, in some reports, with MS. 13 , 45 DN cells can also be expanded by chronic viral triggers such as Epstein‐Barr virus (EBV). 46 Notably, in a humanized HLA‐DRB1*15:01 model, EBV expands T‐bet+CXCR3+ B cells that home to the CNS and colocalize with activated Th1/Th17 and effector‐memory CD8+ T cells. B‐cell depletion or CXCR3 blockade reduces CNS lymphocyte infiltration, and activated CD4+ T‐cell frequencies correlate with sNfL. 21 These data raise the possibility that EBV‐primed DN2 cells, alongside MZ‐like cells, may serve as long‐lived reservoirs of viral latency and potent inducers of T‐cell–driven CNS inflammation in MS. In our cohort, DN2 cells displayed high antigen‐presenting potential yet were less prominent producers of proinflammatory cytokines than MZ‐like cells and recovered more rapidly after cladribine, suggesting they may contribute transiently rather than persistently to post‐treatment immune activation.

Cladribine's historical precedent also supports selective action against memory‐biased programs. It was first deployed in hairy cell leukemia, a B‐cell malignancy whose transcriptional program resembles late post–germinal‐center memory, possibly of MZ origin. 47 , 48 Together with our observation of slow MZ‐like reconstitution and strong cytokine polyfunctionality, this precedent is consistent with preferential removal of pathogenic memory‐leaning states in MS. The ability of MZ‐like cells to arise via a Notch2‐dependent, germinal center–independent pathway may further underwrite their innate‐like, CD1c/CD1d‐high phenotype, and inflammatory potential. 49

Although several observed differences did not reach statistical significance, this is plausibly because of limited sample size rather than absence of effect. We have, therefore, initiated an independent multicenter expansion across 4 active MS clinics to increase power and validate these signals in a larger cohort.

Taken together, our data suggest that cladribine's long‐term therapeutic effect in MS may hinge on its capacity to selectively remove B‐cell subsets with the highest pathogenic potential, most prominently MZ‐like cells, while sparing or regenerating less experienced populations. This targeted reshaping of the B‐cell repertoire likely reduces the availability of inflammatory hubs in the immune network, limiting the capacity for sustained autoantigen presentation and pro‐inflammatory cytokine release. The resulting immune landscape, dominated by transitional and naïve cells, may represent a state less conducive to MS reactivation, offering a mechanistic explanation for cladribine's durable efficacy. More broadly, our findings support a unifying framework in which effective MS therapies converge on disabling cytokine‐competent, antigen‐presenting memory B‐cell hubs, via depletion (immune‐reconstitution, anti‐CD20) or functional silencing. Within this framework, the IgM‐memory/MZ‐like compartment emerges as a prioritized node whose depth and durability of suppression may underwrite long‐lasting disease control. These observations motivate subset‐informed immune monitoring to guide sequencing and retreatment, and they support trials stratified by intrathecal IgM/IgM OCB status to test whether high‐risk IgM signatures predict greater benefit from sustained suppression of IgM‐memory programs.
