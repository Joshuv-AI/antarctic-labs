Repurposing trifluridine as an anti-Staphylococcus aureus agent targeting virulence factor staphyloxanthin and attachment genes
Authors: Supriti Sandhu, Nidhin Poovathumkadavil Thambi, Niti Singh, Arjan Singh, Meenu Katoch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work explores the antibacterial and antibiofilm activities of trifluridine (TRFD) against S. aureus. The Minimum Inhibitory Concentrations (MIC) and Minimum Biofilm Inhibitory Concentrations (MBIC50) for TRFD were observed at 1.562 mg L−1 and 1 mg L−1 for ciprofloxacin-susceptible S. aureus (CSSA) and at 0.0625 mg L−1 and 0.14 mg L−1 for ciprofloxacin-resistant S. aureus (CRSA). Synergistic and partial synergistic antimicrobial effects of TRFD combined with ciprofloxacin (CIP) were observed against CSSA and CRSA, respectively, along with synergistic and indifferent antibiofilm effects. The TRFD + CIP significantly increased plasma membrane damage and generated ROS, affecting staphyloxanthin production, partially reducing slime production, and inhibiting the cell surface hydrophobicity and pellicle production. TRFD + CIP also reduced the carbohydrate and eDNA contents of extracellular polymeric substances. TRFD + CIP treatment decreased icaR, fnbA, and clfB expression and increased icaA, efb, and map expression. Scanning electron microscopy analysis revealed the TRFD + CIP-treated S. aureus biofilm and planktonic cells with deformities and debris.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-54841-1.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Staphylococcus aureus, Drug repurposing, Ciprofloxacin, TrifluridineSubject terms: Biochemistry, Microbiology

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a public health crisis worldwide, leading to the World Health Organisation (WHO) listing it as one of the top 10 global health hazards1. AMR presents with increased relapse infections and treatment failure2 The 2025 Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS) report shows an unevenly distributed high global antibiotic resistance level3. Recent WHO reports show that in 2019, AMR contributed to 4.95 million deaths globally, with 1.27 million directly associated with bacterial AMR, and that by 2050, it is projected to reach up to 39 million deaths4. The 2025 GLASS also reported that AMR disproportionally affects low and middle-income countries and countries with weak health systems3.

The pathogens bypass antibiotic effects primarily through biofilm formation, efflux pumps, target modifications, enzyme effects, and neutralisation5. Bacterial colonies in a biofilm are 100 to 1,000 times more resistant to antimicrobials than their planktonic cells6. 80% of chronic infectious diseases are biofilm-associated; furthermore, previous studies report that conventional antibiotics are ineffective at eradicating biofilms7. Eradication of biofilms is prioritised in future drug development to overcome drug resistance8. However, over the last few decades, few antimicrobial drugs have been approved5. Staphylococcus aureus is an extremely virulent pathogen capable of abruptly evolving and developing antibiotic resistance. It is estimated that about 40% of healthy humans are carriers of S. aureus on their skin, in their intestines, throats, and noses. S. aureus is versatile, quickly evolving, and tends to develop resistance to almost all the antibiotics used against it9,10. The 2024 WHO bacterial priority pathogen lists S. aureus in the high category, with an increased estimated global burden, and reports it as a leading cause of healthcare-associated and community-acquired infections globally11. S. aureus biofilms are most commonly reported in medical device associated infections, notably from implants; biofilms on implant surfaces can continuously release bacteria, causing persistent infections that lead to fatal shock in severe cases12. S. aureus biofilms are reported to suppress normal wound healing by keeping the wound in a prolonged inflammatory state for months or years; this critically affects the patient’s quality of life and overburdens healthcare by taking up substantial medical resources12.

The lack of antimicrobial drugs hinders the global initiative to curb AMR. It is concerning as the drug development pipeline for antimicrobials is relatively small compared to that for other therapeutic drugs, and very few novel compounds reach the therapeutic market13. This lack of initiative by the pharmaceutical industry to develop new antibiotics is mainly due to the high cost of discovery and development, combined with the low pricing of antibiotics, which leads to reduced economic incentives2,13.

The traditional drug discovery pipeline is lengthy, costly, and has a high failure rate14. Drug repurposing involves finding new therapeutic applications for already-approved drugs13. Drug repurposing can substantially mitigate the costs and risks associated with drug development by bringing the drugs straight to preclinical and clinical trials13,15. With their existing safety and efficacy data, repurposed drugs could respond more quickly to emerging resistant infections15. Compared to a traditional drug discovery program, which takes 10–17 years to develop a new drug, the drug repurposing strategy is advantageous, as the estimated time to develop a reprofiled drug is only 3–12 years14.

Nucleoside analogues represent a diverse, clinically relevant class of drugs16. They exert their activities by mimicking endogenous nucleosides and incorporating them into DNA and RNA, inhibiting replication and further arresting cell proliferation16. Previous studies have reported the antibacterial activities of nucleoside analogues, yet their antibacterial applications remain underexplored16.

Ciprofloxacin (CIP) is a broad-spectrum antibacterial agent, effective against various multidrug-resistant gram-positive and gram-negative pathogens. The prolonged widespread use of this antibiotic has led to the emergence of ciprofloxacin-resistant S. aureus17.

Trifluridine (TRFD), a thymidine-based nucleoside analogue, is already used as an antiviral drug against the herpes virus18. TRFD as an antimicrobial has not yet been studied, except that it was recently reported as a hit against Enterococcus faecium in a phenotypic high-throughput screening19. Despite previous reports of its antibacterial properties, its antibiofilm activity remains unexplored20. Moreover, its effects and mode of action against S. aureus have not yet been elucidated.

Hence, in the present study, we investigate the antimicrobial and antibiofilm effects and the mode of action of TRFD against CIP-susceptible and CIP-resistant S. aureus alone and in combination with CIP.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain and media

CIP susceptible S. aureus (CSSA) (MTCC737, MTCC, Chandigarh, India) and CIP resistant S. aureus (CRSA) (Government Medical College, Jammu, J&K, India) were used in the study. Experiments were performed at 37 °C in static/shaking conditions in Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA) (Himedia, India), Mueller-Hintonbroth (MHB), Luria Bertani broth (LBB) (Himedia, India), Luria Bertani agar (LBA) (Himedia, India), Tryptic soy broth (TSB) (tryptone type-I (17 g L−1), NaCl (5 g L−1), soya peptone (3 g L−1), K2HPO4 (2.5 g L−1), sucrose (20 g L−1)) and Congo red agar (CRA) (brain–heart infusion broth (37 g L−1), Congo red (0.8 g L−1), sucrose (36 g L−1), agar (15 g L−1)).

Antibacterial assay

Antibacterial assay was performed in MHB using the microdilution method21. CSSA and CRSA were grown shaking overnight in MHB at 37 °C, were re-inoculated in fresh MHB, and grown to mid-logarithmic phase to acquire 0.5 McFarland standard turbidity (1.5 × 108 CFU mL−1). The bacterial culture with 1.5 × 108 CFU mL−1 was diluted in MHB for the final concentration of 4 × 104 CFU mL−1. Stock solutions (1 mg L−1) of TRFD and CIP were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). DMSO was used as the negative control.

The experiments were carried out on a U-shaped 96-well plate. Two-fold serial dilutions of TRFD (100 mg L−1–0.0488 mg L−1) and CIP (100 mg L−1–0.0488 mg L−1) for CSSA and TRFD (128 mg L−1–0.0625 mg L−1) and CIP (100 mg L−1–0.0488 mg L−1) for CRSA were made in 150 μL media, and to each well, 50 μL of bacterial suspension (4 × 104 CFU mL−1) was added. The plate was incubated shaking at 37 °C for 24 h. The wells were visually inspected, and the lowest concentration, which resulted in clear wells with no visible growth, was considered the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC). 50 µL from wells without visible growth was spread on MHA and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C to determine the Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC). The lowest drug concentration that prevented any bacterial regrowth was considered the MBC.

Assessment of combinatorial antimicrobial effects between trifluridine and ciprofloxacin

The combined effect of TRFD and CIP on CSSA and CRSA was studied using the checkerboard microdilution assay22. This assay was performed using a U-bottom 96-well plate with MHB. Two-fold serial dilutions of MICs of CIP (0.39 mg L−1–0.003 mg L−1) and TRFD (3.125 mg L−1–0.024 mg L−1) for CSSA and CIP (25 mg L−1–0.195 mg L−1) and TRFD (0.125 mg L−1–0.0009 mg L−1) for CRSA were made vertically and horizontally in 150 μL MHB, respectively. Subsequently, 50 μL of a bacterial suspension containing 4 × 104 CFU mL−1, prepared as previously mentioned in Sect. 2.2, was added to each well. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h and were visually inspected for growth.

The experiments were performed in sterile 5 mL glass vials. MICs (1/2, 1, 2) of TRFD, CIP, and their synergistic combination were made in MHB. Staphyloxanthin inhibition assay

CSSA and CRSA bacterial suspensions were prepared according to Sect. 2.2 and added to achieve a final concentration of 1 × 104 CFU mL−1. The tubes were incubated by shaking at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the cells were collected by centrifugation at 15294g for 5 min. The cell pellets were subjected to methanol extraction (1 mL) and shaking at 37 °C overnight. Assessment of plasma membrane integrity

The effects of CIP, TRFD, and their synergistic combination on CSSA and CRSA plasma membranes were investigated through propidium iodide (PI) staining24. The experiments were performed in sterile 5 mL glass vials. MICs (1/4, 1/2, and 1) for TRFD, CIP, and their synergistic combination were made in MHB. CSSA and CRSA suspensions were prepared according to Sect. 2.2 and added to get a final concentration of 2 × 104 CFU mL−1. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, the cells were centrifuged and stained with PI (100 mg L−1) at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min. The cells were rinsed three times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove the excess stain. The cells were then mounted on glass slides and visualised under 20× magnification using a fluorescence microscope (FV1000, Olympus) (excitation 546 nm, emission 590 nm) with random field view selection.

PI uptake was quantified as follows: the PI-treated cells were vortexed, and 200 μL of the sample was transferred to a 96-well microtiter plate. Then, the fluorescence was measured (excitation at 546 nm, emission at 590 nm) with PBS as the blank using a microtiter plate reader (Infinite® M200 PRO, Tecan).

Evaluation of intracellular reactive oxygen species

CIP, TRFD, and their synergistic combined treatments with CSSA and CRSA were performed as mentioned in Sect. 2.6. The production of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) was detected via staining with 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFDA)(Sigma-Aldrich)24. The centrifuged and collected cells were stained with 20 μM DCFDA and incubated for 1 h in the dark at 37 °C. The cells were rinsed three times with sterile PBS to remove the excess stain. The cells were then mounted on glass slides and visualised at 20× magnification using a fluorescence microscope (excitation 485 nm, emission 535 nm) with random field view selection.

DCFDA uptake was quantified as follows: DCFDA stained PBS washed cells were disrupted using acid-washed glass beads in a cell disruptor in PBS for 3 min (FastPrep FP120, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The suspension was centrifuged at 10,621g at 4 °C for 5 min, and 200 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well microtiter plate. DCFDA fluorescence was measured using a microtiter plate reader (excitation at 485 nm, emission at 535 nm) with PBS as the blank.

DNA synthesis inhibition

The DNA synthesis inhibition effects of CIP, TRFD, and their synergistic combination on CSSA and CRSA were evaluated according to25. CSSA and CRSA cell suspensions were made as mentioned in Sect. 2.2. CSSA and CRSA suspensions (1 × 104 CFU mL−1) were incubated for 10 h in MHB at 37 °C, shaking at 120 rpm. Afterwards, treatments at MICs (1X, 2X, 5X) of CIP, TRFD, and the synergistic combination for 4 h. The cells were collected by centrifuging and were washed twice with PBS. The DNA from the whole cell was extracted using the Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA was visualised on a 0.8% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide (0.5 mg L−1).

Antibiofilm activity of trifluridine and ciprofloxacin

Anti-biofilm activity was performed in a flat-bottom 96-well plate (Tarsons, India)26. The CSSA and CRSA cultures were grown overnight with shaking in 5 mL TSB at 37 ℃. The overnight-grown bacteria were re-inoculated in fresh TSB, grown to the mid-logarithmic phase (0.5 McFarland standard turbidity) and diluted (1:1000) in TSB. Two-fold serial dilutions of TRFD (35 mg L−1–0.068 mg L−1) and CIP (10 mg L−1–0.039 mg L−1) for CSSA and TRFD (1 mg L−1–0.0039 mg L−1) and CIP (100 mg L−1–0.39 mg L−1) for CRSA were made in 150 μL TSB, and to each well, 50 μL of the diluted culture was added. Untreated TSB inoculated with inoculum was considered the control, and the plate was incubated statically at 37 ℃ for 24 h.

For biofilm quantification, the planktonic cells were removed by washing wells twice with distilled water. Afterwards, 100 µL crystal violet (0.4% (w/v) solution was added to the wells for staining and was incubated at room temperature on a dancing shaker (Tarsons, India) for 45 min. After incubation, the wells were washed three times with distilled water to remove the excess stain. Followingly, 200 µL of 95% ethanol was added and incubated for 1 h26.

The optical density (OD) was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Multiskan sky High Micro-plate, spectrophotometer, Thermo Scientific, India) 95% ethanol as the blank. Biofilm inhibition was calculated using the following formula:

The lowest drug concentration that results in 50% biofilm inhibition was considered the minimum biofilm inhibitory concentration (MBIC50), which was calculated through nonlinear regression analysis of individual concentration-effect data.

Slime production assay

Slime production by S. aureus (CSSA and CRSA) was evaluated using the CRA assay27 The bacterial suspension was prepared as mentioned in Sect. 2.9. The experiments were conducted on six-well tissue culture plates. The MBIC50 TRFD, CIP concentration, and synergistic combination were mixed with 1 mL of CRA. CRA without added antibiotics was taken as a control. Afterwards, the bacterial suspension was streaked. The plate was incubated at 37 ℃ for 24 h.

Pellicle formation assay

Pellicle assay was performed in sterile 5 mL glass vials with LBB supplemented with 2% glucose. Drug treatments and CSSA and CRSA inoculum were given according to Sect. 2.11. The vials were incubated statically for 72 h at 37 ℃. After incubation, the vials were washed thrice with distilled water, then 1 mL of crystal violet was added. The vials were incubated (45 min) for staining at room temperature. Afterwards, the excess stain was removed thrice with distilled water. Following that, 2 mL of 95% ethanol was added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h.

50 µL was drawn from each vial and transferred to 150 µL of 95% ethanol in a 96-well U-bottom plate. The plates were then incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Afterwards, OD was measured at 550 nm with 95% ethanol as the blank29.

Assessment of extracellular polymeric substances

Extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) were extracted with minor modifications30. The experiments were performed in 5 mL sterile glass vials with TSB. The drug treatments and CSSA and CRSA inoculum were given according to Sect. 2.11. The tubes were incubated statically for 24 h at 37 ºC. Afterwards, 20 mg EDTA was added and incubated in the dark for 4 h at 4 ºC. Afterwards, the vials were centrifuged (12,851g) for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected. Three volumes of ethanol were added to the supernatant and kept on ice for 2 h. The mixture was again centrifuged (12,851g) at 4 ºC for 20 min, and the pellets were air dried at room temperature.

The extracted EPS was chemically analysed for carbohydrate, protein and extracellular DNA (eDNA) content31,32. The carbohydrate content was quantified using the phenol–sulfuric acid method with d-glucose as a standard. The protein content was determined using the Lowry method, with bovine serum albumin as the standard. The eDNA content was quantified using a NanoDrop.

Isolation of RNA and real-time quantitative PCR

CSSA was treated with half MBIC50 of TRFD, CIP and their synergistic combinations, and CRSA was treated with half MBIC50 of TRFD and CIP for RNA extraction. Cells were grown, and approximately 2 × 109 cells were pelleted at 10,621 g. RNA was isolated from the pellet by the Trizol method33. To assess the anti-biofilm effects of TRFD, CIP and their synergistic combination on S. aureus, the expression levels of attachment factor encoding genes, namely icaA, icaR, fnbA, clfB, efb, and map, were assessed through real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR).

The RNA was treated with 2 μg DNase and used as a template to generate cDNA with the PrimeScript 1st strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Clonetech, Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. About 1 μl of generated cDNA was used for RT-qPCR using 5X Gotaq qPCR Mix Plus (SYBR Green; Promega) on the ABS system (USA). The quantity of cDNA measured by real-time PCR was normalised to the abundance of 16S cDNA. RT-qPCR was repeated three times in triplicate parallel experiments. The primer pairs used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

SEM

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) studies were conducted to examine the changes in the morphology of CSSA and CRSA treated with 1/2 MIC of TRFD, CIP, and their combination. CSSA and CRSA cell suspensions for 1/2 MIC treatments were prepared according to Sect. 2.2. The treatments were given for 4–6 h in sterile glass vials in MHB. CSSA and CRSA cell suspensions for drug treatments (1/2 MBIC50 of TRFD, CIP and their combination) were prepared according to Sect. 2.8. The treatments were given for 24 h in sterile glass vials. Then, the cells were centrifuged at 6797g and washed with sterile PBS.

Cell fixation was performed using 2.5% glutaraldehyde prepared in PBS (pH 7.4) for 24 h at 4 °C. After 24 h, cells were washed with sterile PBS and fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h at 4 °C. The samples were then dehydrated sequentially with increasing ethanol gradients (30%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 98% and 100%) for 20 min each. Subsequently, the samples were treated with iso-amyl acetate and ethanol (1:1) for 30 min. Finally, iso-amyl acetate (100%) was added to the samples for 1 h24. The samples were then vacuum-dried prior to inspection under the scanning electron microscope (JSM-IT300, JEOL).

Assessment of haemolysis

The haemolysis effect of TRFD and CIP was assessed on Red Blood Cells (RBCs)26. RBCs used in the experiment were voluntarily donated by one of the authors and collected by a trained technician at the institute’s dispensary upon the donor’s request. RBCs were separated after centrifugation (425 g) at 4 °C for 5 min. The cells were washed three times in sterile PBS. The RBCs were then suspended in PBS (5% v/v). The test drug concentrations (50 mg L−1–0.02 mg L−1) were then prepared in a 96-well plate at a volume of 150 µL, and 50 µL of the prepared RBC suspension was added to each well. PBS was taken as the negative control, and 0.1% Triton X-100 prepared in PBS was taken as the positive control. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h and centrifuged (425g) for 20 min. The supernatant was then transferred to a fresh 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at 360 nm with PBS as the blank to detect the released haemoglobin content. The experiment was repeated three times. The percentage of haemolysis was calculated with the given formula:

ODx = Absorbance of positive control at 360 nm.

ODy = Absorbance of negative control at 360 nm.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated three times, and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism 7.0 software was used for the statistical analyses, and ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used to assess statistically significant differences.

Results

Time kill kinetics

In CSSA, a time-dependent reduction in cell number was observed after drug treatment. All 1/2X treatments showed a significant reduction in cell number compared to the control (Fig. 1a). However, the highest cell decrease was observed with 1/2 CIP, followed by 1/2 TRFD + CIP (Fig. 1a). All 1X treatments showed a significant time-dependent reduction of cells. Like 1/2X treatments, the highest cell decrease was observed for 1 CIP, followed by 1 TRFD + CIP treatment (Fig. 1b). For 2X treatments, an effective reduction was observed with 2 CIP, resulting in complete elimination of cells by 30 min (Fig. 1c). 2 TRFD + CIP also exhibited a statistically significant time-dependent reduction in cell number (Fig. 1c).

The statistical analyses were performed using ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. Different letters indicate statistically significant differences (n = 3, P < 0.05).

For CRSA, like CSSA, a time-dependent reduction in cell number was observed. All 1/2X treatments showed a significant reduction in cells compared to the control. For 1X treatments, all treatments showed a significant reduction in cells compared to the control. A significant difference between all the treatments, 1 CIP, 1 TRFD, and 1 TRFD + CIP, was observed, with 1 TRFD being the treatment with the highest cell reduction (Fig. 1e). All 2X treatments showed a significant difference compared to the control. The 2 TRFD + CIP treatment exhibited the fastest complete eradication of cells, followed by 2 TRFD and 2 CIP. However, we observed no significant difference between 2 TRFD and 2 CIP and 2 TRFD + CIP and 2 TRFD (Fig. 1f).

Staphyloxanthin inhibition assay

For CSSA, the untreated control showed no inhibition of staphyloxanthin. For 1/4X, 1/2X, and 1X treatments, the highest inhibition was observed for the TRFD + CIP, followed by CIP, and TRFD treatment showed the least inhibition of staphyloxanthin (Fig. 2a–c). However, among 1/2X treatments, the highest staphyloxanthin inhibition was observed with 1/2 TRFD, followed by 1/2 CIP and 1/2 TRFD + CIP (Fig. 2e). Moreover, no significant difference was observed between treatments at their 1X concentrations (Fig. 2f).

Discussion

According to the CLSI 2020 guidelines, the breakpoint for CIP against S. aureus is MIC ≤ 1 mg L−1 for susceptible strains, MIC 2 mg L−1 for intermediate strains, and MIC ≥ 4 mg L−1 for resistant strains. In the present study, we observed CIP MICs of 0.195 mg L−1 against CSSA and 12.5 mg L−1 against CRSA, confirming their sensitivity and resistance towards CIP. Even though the antibacterial activities of fluorinated pyrimidine analogues have been reported previously against S. aureus, no previous studies have investigated the antibacterial effects of TRFD against S. aureus34. The antimicrobial activity of TRFD against Mycoplasma pneumoniae has been reported18. We observed a TRFD MIC of 1.562 mg L−1 against CSSA and 0.0625 mg L−1 against CRSA.

Previous reports suggest a higher rate of resistance when nucleoside analogues are used alone as antibiotics16. It is unlikely that certain pyrimidine analogues cause cross-resistance to any currently approved major antibiotic classes34. Additionally, synergistic combinations can overcome or reduce the development of resistance, as they can effectively clear the infection in a very short time, thereby preventing resistant mutations from passing on to the next population16,35. Previously, it has been observed that a combination of gentamicin and azidothymidine thoroughly stopped the regrowth of azidothymidine-resistant bacteria35. We observed a synergistic effect between TRFD and CIP against CSSA. However, against CRSA, a partial synergistic effect was observed. Recently, a synergistic combination of 5-fluoro-2-deoxyuridine, trimethoprim and uridine was reported to be active against S. aureus13.

Recent literature reports staphyloxanthin as a new promising target for anti-S. aureus agents36. More than 90% of S. aureus isolates harbour staphyloxanthin as one of their major virulence factors; staphyloxanthin is known for its antioxidant activity and protects the pathogen from ROS-mediated damage36. Similar to our findings, CIP has been previously reported to suppress staphyloxanthin production37. No previous information regarding the effect of TRFD on staphyloxanthin was available in our literature survey. We observed TRFD inhibition of staphyloxanthin in both CSSA and CRSA. However, we observed a higher level of inhibition in CRSA, which warrants further investigation to understand fully. Furthermore, the TRFD + CIP showed the highest staphyloxanthin inhibition for CSSA and CRSA, which could be attributed to the synergistic effect between CIP and TRFD.

PI penetrates cells with damaged plasma membranes and intercalates between DNA bases38. Similar to our observations, CIP has been previously reported to affect the S. aureus plasma membrane permeability39. TRFD and 5-fluorouracil share the same target, thymidylate synthase40,41. No studies have yet reported the effects of TRFD on the S.aureus plasma membrane. However, 5-fluorouracil has been reported to inhibit S. aureus cell-wall mucopeptide synthesis, rupturing and perforating the Pseudomonas aeruginosa membrane41.

As observed, CIP treatment has previously been reported to generate intracellular ROS in S. aureus42. Excessive intracellular ROS has been reported to irreparably damage proteins, lipids, and DNA, causing plasma membrane damage and eventual cell death38. Although TRFD has been reported to generate ROS in cancer cells, we found no previous reports of ROS generation in bacteria40. However, the antibacterial activity of 5-fluorouracil against E. coli has been reported to involve the generation of intracellular ROS43.

As in previous reports, we observed inhibition of S. aureus DNA synthesis by CIP44. No previous reports were available confirming the DNA synthesis inhibition by TRFD in S. aureus. This work is the first to report it. TRFD has previously been reported to target cancer and viral DNA synthesis45. Thus, in the present study, the synergistic interaction of TRFD + CIP also retained inhibition of DNA synthesis.

The clinical implications of S. aureus biofilms and the inefficacy of existing antibiotics have been extensively studied; a recent study reported the MBIC50 of CIP at 0.368 mg L−1 against S. aureus ATCC 2921346. Based on our knowledge, no previous studies have reported the antibiofilm effects of TRFD on S. aureus. The present study observed MBIC50 values of 1 ± 0.21 mg L−1 and 0.14 ± 0.018 mg L−1 for TRFD against the CSSA and CRSA biofilm.

Synergistic effects of CIP and 5-fluorouracil against S. epidermidis have been previously reported and were attributed to the shared DNA synthesis-inhibiting mode of action of 5-fluorouracil and CIP47. Considering these reports, the synergistic antibiofilm effects of TRFD and CIP against CSSA and CRSA were explored, and we observed a synergistic interaction against CSSA. However, an indifference effect was observed against CRSA.

5-fluorouracil inhibited S. epidermidis slime production and was ineffective against S. aureus47. However, we observed a partial suppression of CSSA slime production by MBIC50 TRFD and TRFD + CIP. Similar to previous reports, we observed complete slime production suppression by MBIC50 CIP48. Previous studies have reported the existence of non-slime-producing S. aureus populations49,50. In the present study, CRSA was identified to be a non-slime-producing strain.

Similar to our finding, CIP has been previously reported to disrupt the bacterial hydrophobic surface51. The antibiofilm activity of 5-fluorouracil is attributed to suppressing adhesion factors, which can, in turn, affect the CSH20. Due to the synergistic interaction, TRFD + CIP significantly suppressed CSSA CSH compared to CIP and TRFD MBIC50 treatments. Furthermore, the TRFD MBIC50 treatment exhibited a significant reduction in CSH % in CRSA.

Previous studies report that CIP disrupts pellicle formation, as observed in our results52. We observed a significantly reduced pellicle production by CSSA and CRSA under TRFD treatment. Interestingly, the TRFD + CIP reduced CSSA pellicle production significantly lower than their individual treatments, and this effect could be attributed to the synergistic interaction.

As observed in the present study, EPS protein, carbohydrate and eDNA destruction under CIP treatment have been reported previously53. However, in TRFD + CIP treatment against CSSA, even though EPS’s carbohydrate and eDNA contents reduced significantly compared to individual treatments, protein content increased significantly. The increased protein content of EPS could be attributed to the excessive protein production by microorganisms under antibiotic stress, highlighting the critical role of proteins in stress release rather than polysaccharides54. The significant reduction in carbohydrate and eDNA content under TRFD + CIP treatment against CSSA might be due to the synergistic effect of TRFD and CIP.

We observed that, under CSSA 1/2 MBIC50 CIP treatments, there was no increase in icaA expression levels, which reflected on the CRA agar with no slime formation (Fig. 7b). Against CRSA 1/2 MBIC50 CIP treatments also showed no increase in icaA expression levels. We observed a significantly increased icaA expression under 1/2 MBIC50 TRFD and 1/2 TRFD + CIP treatment against CSSA, which could also be responsible for the appearance of black colonies of the CRA (Fig. 7c,d). These results indicate that 1/2 TRFD and 1/2 TRFD + CIP promote biofilm formation through polysaccharide intercellular adhesin production, which may be due to lower drug concentrations triggering a stress response as a defence mechanism for survival of bacteria. Consequently, icaR expression was downregulated. However, against CRSA, 1/2 MBIC50 TRFD treatment showed no increase in icaA expression levels.

Previous studies observed that 1/2 MIC CIP treatments had increased the fnbA expression55. On the contrary, we observed no fnbA expression under treatment with 1/2 MBIC50 CIP against both CSSA and CRSA; this might be because, unlike previous reports, MBIC50 concentrations were used rather than MICs in the present study. No studies have yet investigated the effects of TRFD on fnbA expression. We observed a significantly increased fnbA expression under 1/2 MBIC50 TRFD treatment against CSSA and CRSA; fnbA expression significantly decreased. Interestingly, the 1/2 TRFD + CIP treatment significantly reduced fnbA expression compared to 1/2 MBIC50 TRFD in CSSA. It could be inferred from the results that the 1/2 TRFD + CIP impairs the fibronectin-binding of S. aureus.

We observed no clfB expression under treatment with 1/2 MBIC50 CIP against CSSA and CRSA. However, 1/2 MIC levofloxacin treatment reportedly increased clfB expression levels56. clfB expression reportedly upregulated under sub-MIC tigecycline treatment57. Our contrasting result might be attributed to using MBIC50 concentrations other than MICs. We observed a significantly increased clfB expression under 1/2 MBIC50 TRFD treatment against CSSA and CRSA. However, 1/2 TRFD + CIP treatment significantly reduced clfB expression. Therefore, 1/2 TRFD + CIP affects the fibrinogen binding of S. aureus. In the present study, 1/2 MBIC50 CIP treatment did not significantly increase efb expression in either CSSA or CRSA. However, efb expression reportedly increased under sub-MIC CIP58. We observed a significant increase in efb expression under treatment with 1/2 MBIC50 TRFD and 1/2 TRFD + CIP against CSSA, thus promoting the S. aureus biofilm formation through fibrinogen binding and platelet activation inhibition. However, against CRSA, 1/2 MBIC50 TRFD treatment showed no significant increase in efb expression.

The Map protein is also designated anchorless extracellular adherence protein (Eap), a product of eap gene59. Sub-MIC CIP treatment reportedly altered eap expression55. Our results showed no increase in map expression with 1/2 MBIC50 CIP treatment against CRSA and CSSA. Against CRSA, 1/2 MBIC50 TRFD showed no significant increase in map expression. However, 1/2 MBIC50 TRFD and 1/2 TRFD + CIP treatments significantly increased map expression against CSSA. Interestingly, in the case of CSSA 1/2 TRFD + CIP, the treatment map expression was significantly lower than 1/2 MBIC50 TRFD but higher than 1/2 MBIC50 CIP. Based on our results, it can be inferred that against CSSA 1/2 MBIC50 TRFD and 1/2 TRFD + CIP promote S. aureus biofilm formation by increasing map expression.

The concentrations corresponding to 1/2 MBIC50 (25% biofilm inhibition) of TRFD and TRFD + CIP were utilised for RT-qPCR analysis. Previously, it has been reported that sublethal concentrations of antimicrobials can upregulate adhesin production in S. aureus60. The antibiofilm effects of nucleoside analogues remain understudied, and no prior research has investigated the effects of TRFD on S. aureus. However, a recent study also indicated that treatment with 5-fluorouracil in Pseudomonas aeruginosa supported biofilm formation61.

No previous studies were available regarding the morphological impacts of TRFD on S. aureus planktonic cells or biofilms. However, similar to our observations in TRFD, 5-fluorouracil was reported to cause swelling and deformations in the Streptococcus suis membrane41. Similar to our results, CIP-treated S. aureus cells were reported to cluster39. SEM results show substantial evidence of TRFD + CIP targeting the plasma membrane, causing its distortion. Recently, sub-MIC CIP was reported not to disrupt the S. aureus biofilm morphology; similar observations were repeated in our results62.

The haemolytic effect on RBCs was assessed to assess the safety of TRFD and CIP. CIP treatment has been previously reported to induce interstitial nephritis and autoimmune haemolytic anaemia63. The incidence of haematological toxicity has been previously observed under TRFD treatment64. However, we observed negligible haemolysis on RBCs under treatment with CIP and TRFD at our active concentrations. Furthermore, previous studies reported that CIP at 0.194 mM (i.e., 64 mg L−1) and 50–75 mg L−1 is cytotoxic to human fibroblast cells65,66. TRFD was reported to show minimal toxicity against MCF-10A at a concentration of ≤ 20 µM (i.e., 5.92 mg L−1)67. The active concentrations of CIP and TRFD mentioned in the present study are significantly lower than the previous toxicity reports, thus highlighting the probable safety of the CIP-TRFD combination.

Overall, the antimicrobial activity of the TRFD + CIP combination appears to be multitargeted, involving the generation of intracellular ROS, plasma membrane damage, and DNA synthesis inhibition. Furthermore, TRFD and CIP exhibited significant antibiofilm activity. TRFD + CIP suppressed the CSH, slime, and pellicle production. TRFD + CIP showed a significant reduction in EPS carbohydrate and eDNA. The antibiofilm mode of action of TRFD + CIP is likely mediated by suppressing the adhesion factors FnbA and ClfB. However, 1/2 TRFD + CIP promoted biofilm formation by increasing production of PIA, Efb, and Map, warranting further functional evaluation to fully understand.

However, further investigations are required to confirm its antibacterial activity in vivo and ascertain its clinical relevance. Furthermore, this study could encourage more investigations into nucleoside analogue-antibiotic synergistic combinations, which are relatively under-investigated.
