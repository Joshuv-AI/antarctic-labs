Introduction

It is well-known that Sobolev spaces of domains and manifolds that admit exclusively metrically conical singularities at frontier points enjoy nicer properties than those having cusps.
In the 70’s of the preceding century, R. A. Adams
[
1
,
2
]
however achieved a satisfying version of Sobolev embedding theorem on spaces with cusps. Much more recently, the progress of Lipschitz geometry of subanalytic sets
[
24
]
made it possible to prove several results on Sobolev spaces of subanalytic manifolds and domains without any restriction on the metric geometry of singularities
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
26
]
, and some applications to PDEs were provided
[
22
,
25
]
.

We focus in this article on the Sobolev spaces relative to the push-forward of a globally subanalytic measure by a globally subanalytic mapping, prove a Sobolev embedding theorem (Theorem
3.3
), and, using the Morrey embedding achieved in
[
21
]
, derive a natural embedding of Sobolev spaces into the space of inner Lipschitz functions (Corollary
3.5
).

Analytic functions and mappings appear naturally in applied mathematics and statistics. Empirical observations are usually modelized by random variables that are distributed with respect to the same density and are often modified by some analytic functions. Our aim is to provide singular models for kernel methods, which requires adequate versions of Sobolev embedding theorems. Before giving more details on the content of the present article, let us make it more precise.

Extension of functions via kernel methods.
Machine learning often demands to find (see
[
5
]
), given a set of observations
\mathcal{X}=(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and some values
y_{1},\dots,y_{l}
, a function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies
f(x_{i})=y_{i}
for
i\leq l
, that will make a reasonable guess for the missing values at the
x_{i}
,
i>l
, called the unlabeled points. A natural way to have a formula for such an
f
is to search for a function of type
\sum\alpha_{i}f_{i}
,
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, where the
f_{i}
are some chosen functions called hypotheses, generally analytic or polynomial. This approach has the significant advantage to lead to a linear problem, which has a solution if the
f_{i}
are sufficiently independent and numerous. The main issue is then the so-called overfitting problem, i.e. to avoid spikes that might arise from the large number of required conditions on
f
. To this end, authors then generally introduce a loss function in order to penalize too complicated solutions. An efficient way to achieve it is to choose a positive semi-definite kernel
\mathbf{k}(x,x^{\prime})

[
10
,
15
]
, which has the advantage to provide both hypothesis functions and the loss function. The main issue is however often to find an adequate kernel. In the quest of the most relevant kernel, authors mainly pay attention to three features, considering that a good kernel should help to:
(i)
reduce redundancy of relevant information (sparsify),
(ii)
assess the complexity of a solution (eliminate overfitting)
(iii)
extract information from the unlabeled observations (manifold learning).

A positive semi-definite kernel is characterized by a mapping into a Hilbert space
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathscr{H}
, called a feature map

have close images, i.e. that

be uniformly continuous. Points in a cloud

are generally connected to neighbors, giving rise to a graph and hence to an inner metric

Most of the considered kernels, if not all, are Hölder continuous with respect to the inner metric, i.e.

||\phi(x)-\phi(x^{\prime})||_{\mathscr{H}}\leq Cd_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime})^{\theta},

for some

, and we then have on
\mathcal{X}

which means that
{\mathscr{H}}
embeds continuously into the space
\check{\mathscr{C}}^{0,\theta}(\mathcal{X})
of functions that are Hölder continuous with respect to the inner metric. It is worthy of notice that on the other hand, if we have a continuous linear map

for some constant

Hence, the problems of mapping Hölder continuously
\mathcal{X}
into a Hilbert space and the problem of finding a Hilbert space mapping continuously into
\check{\mathscr{C}}^{0,\theta}(\mathcal{X})
are completely equivalent.

An efficient way to address the point
(ii)
is to choose a Hilbert space whose norm estimates the size of the derivative. Many authors therefore choose to work in Sobolev spaces, often relative to the distribution measure of the observations, or to minimize the graph Laplacian
[

,
4
,

,

,

. If one regards
\mathcal{X}
as a graph, it is then a one-dimensional set, and
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{X})\hookrightarrow\check{\mathscr{C}}^{0,\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{X})
in this case.
Clouds being generally rather large, one-dimensional models are however often considered not enough accurate. In the setting where the data are dense in a higher dimensional set, such an embedding no longer exists. The space
W^{1,p}(\overline{\mathcal{X}})
,
p>m:=\dim\overline{\mathcal{X}}
(the closure of
\mathcal{X}
), which embeds into
\check{\mathscr{C}}^{0,1-\frac{m}{p}}(\overline{\mathcal{X}})
via Morrey’s embedding if
\overline{\mathcal{X}}
is a Lipschitz manifold, is then a relevant alternative
[
7
,
14
]
, with the drawback that it is not a Hilbert space but just a semi-Hilbert space
[
27
]
, and the resulting mathematical problem is of higher degree. This is why some authors work with higher order derivatives
[
9
,
13
,
28
]
. Sobolev’s embedding theorem ensures that
W^{k,2}(\overline{\mathcal{X}})
embeds into
\mathscr{C}^{0,1}(\mathcal{X})
for
k\geq\frac{\dim\overline{\mathcal{X}}}{2}+1
. This nevertheless requires the data to be concentrated on a metrically conical set, which has in general no reason to happen, or to work with the norm of the ambient space.

If we regard
\mathcal{X}
as a set of random variables
i.i.d.
with respect to a density measure
\mu
, this measure is natural to compute the
L^{2}
norm. First, it can be estimated from the unlabeled observations (see
(iii)
), which is clearly an asset since these data are much easier to get.
Secondly, the problem of estimating the
L^{2}
norm with respect to a measure which is supported outside the cloud would involve data with no real signification for our problem, and in addition the resulting problem may be regarded as ill-posed for impossible to be solved on the basis of the data only.

Our approach.
An obstacle is that
working with a singular measure requires to have an adequate version of Sobolev embedding theorem.
As already mentioned, if
X
has only metrically conical singularities then (see
[
2
, Theorem 4.12]
)
k\geq\frac{\dim X}{2}+1
suffices to embed
W^{k,2}(X)

into
\check{\mathscr{C}}^{0,1}(X)
(the inner Lipschitz functions, see (
3.26
)).
The situation is however more intricate when cusps arise. For instance, if

X=\{(x,y)\in(0,1)^{2}:|y|\leq e^{-1/x^{2}}\}

and
u(x,y)=\frac{1}{x}
then
u\in W^{k,2}(X)
for all
k
but is not inner Lipschitz. This is of course because the contact between the two branches of curve
y=\pm e^{-1/x^{2}}
is flat. On a non flat cusp
X=\{|y|\leq x^{l}\}
, one can show that
W^{k,p}(X)\hookrightarrow\check{\mathscr{C}}^{0,1}(X)
for
k
large. The smallest such
k
then depends on
l
and is no longer determined by the dimension of
X
. This was established by Adams on all spaces with cusps and horns
[
1
,
2
]
, and was proved in
[
21
]
to hold on an arbitrary bounded subanalytic manifold (with the Hausdorff measure).

Clouds are generally modelized by engineers as sets of random variables
i.i.d
with respect to the same probability measure
\mu
, and it is not rare to take the image of the data under a morphism
\Phi
(dimension reduction, neural networks), almost always subanalytic. These pushed-forward random variables are then equidistributed with respect to
\Phi_{*}\mu
.
In the present article, we thus generalize the results of
[
21
]
to measures that are push-forward of a globally subanalytic measure
\mu
(Definition
2.3
) under a globally subanalytic mapping. We restrict ourselves to globally subanalytic measures in order to avoid flat vanishing of the density, which would be prone to generate pathological behavior of Sobolev spaces. That
\mu
is globally subanalytic is however not definitely necessary, as it obviously suffices that the density be
\sim
to a globally subanalytic function (
f\sim g
if and only if
\frac{f}{C}\leq g\leq Cf
for some
C
), so that we cover all the measures that have a density bounded away from
0
and
\infty
, which is a current framework.

Dealing with push-forward measures significantly complicates our task as the density of
\Phi_{*}\mu
may tend to zero at some points even if
\Phi
is Lipschitz and
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure. For instance if
\Phi(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{4}x_{i}^{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
endowed with the Lebesgue measure then the density of
\Phi_{*}\mu
tends to
0
at
0
. This also forces us to work with non subanalytic density functions (see section
2.1
).

We show that, if
(A,\mu)
is a globally subanalytic measured space (Definition
2.3
) and
\Phi:(A,\mu)\to{\Omega}
a bounded globally subanalytic mapping (section
2
), then for
k
large enough
W^{k,2}_{\Phi_{*}\mu}({\Omega})
embeds continuously into
\check{\mathscr{C}}^{0,1}(X)
,
X
being the support of
\Phi_{*}\mu
(the closure of
\Phi(\mathcal{X})
, with probability
1
). As a byproduct, we can construct a positive Lipschitz kernel (Corollary
3.6
), definite if
\mu
is finite, with
W^{k,2}_{\Phi_{*}\mu}(X)
as representing Hilbert space.

On a discrete model, kernels are easy to produce. On the other hand, continuous models can be regarded as more accurate when the cloud is huge, and constructing on singular sets kernels having the above properties
(i-iii)
is, in the opinion of the author, a worthwhile challenge, as it can lead to qualitative information on solutions, valuable to ensure the consistency of algorithms.

2.
Globally subanalytic sets and measures

We provide basic definitions. We refer to
[
24
]
for more on globally subanalytic geometry.

Definition 2.1
.

A subset
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
semi-analytic
if it is
locally

defined by finitely many real analytic equalities and inequalities. Namely, for each
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there is
a neighborhood
U
of
a
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and real analytic functions
f_{ij},g_{ij}
on
U
, where
i=1,\dots,r,j=1,\dots,s_{i}
, such that

(2.1)

E\cap U=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\bigcap_{j=1}^{s_{i}}\{x\in U:g_{ij}(x)>0\mbox{ and }f_{ij}(x)=0\}.

The flaw of the semi-analytic category is that it is not preserved by analytic morphisms, even when they are proper. To overcome this problem, we prefer working with the subanalytic sets, which are defined as the projections of the semi-analytic sets.

A subset
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
subanalytic
if
each point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has a neighborhood
U
such that
U\cap E
is the image under the canonical projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
of some relatively compact semi-analytic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
(where
k
depends on
x
).

A subset
Z
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
globally subanalytic
if
\mathcal{V}_{n}(Z)
is a subanalytic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\mathcal{V}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-1,1)^{n}
is the homeomorphism defined by

(2.2)

\mathcal{V}_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):=(\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}}},\dots,\frac{x_{n}}{\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}}}).

We say that
a mapping (or a function)
f:A\to B
is globally subanalytic
,
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
globally subanalytic, if its graph is a globally subanalytic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
. We will allow the density of measures to be infinite, considering that
f:A\to[0,\infty]
is globally subanalytic if
f^{-1}(\infty)
is a globally subanalytic set and the restriction of
f
to
f^{-1}([0,\infty))
is a globally subanalytic function.

Of course, a bounded subanalytic set is globally subanalytic. For simplicity, globally subanalytic sets and mappings will often be referred as
definable
sets and mappings. We denote by
\mathcal{S}_{n}
the set of all the globally subanalytic subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definable families.
It is natural to regard a set

. Let us make it more precise.

Any family constructed in this way is called a

A definable family

on (the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of) a set
A\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
is said to be globally subanalytic if it is a finite sum of type
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i}\cdot\mu_{E_{i}}
where, for every
i
,
E_{i}\subset A
is a globally subanalytic manifold and
\xi_{i}:E_{i}\to[0,\infty]
is a globally subanalytic function. We then say that
(A,\mu)
is a
globally subanalytic measured space
.

We impose the
E_{i}
to be manifolds for convenience. Since definable sets can always be decomposed into finitely many definable manifolds, this is no loss of generality, and the measure
B\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{D_{i}\cap B}\xi_{i}\,d\mathcal{H}^{l_{i}}
,
l_{i}\leq n
, with
D_{i}
arbitrary definable subset of
A
and
\xi_{i}:D_{i}\to[0,\infty]
definable function for each
i
, is also globally subanalytic.

Given a Borel mapping
\Phi:(A,\mu)\to X
, we denote by
\Phi_{*}\mu
the push-forward of
\mu
under
\Phi
, i.e.
\Phi_{*}\mu(B):=\mu(\Phi^{-1}(B))
, if
B\subset X
is a Borel set.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
(A,\mu)
be a globally subanalytic measured space and let
\Phi:A\to X
be globally subanalytic. There is a finite collection
\mathcal{F}
of globally subanalytic manifolds included in
X
such that

\Phi_{*}\mu=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbf{f}_{M}\cdot\mu_{M},\quad\mbox{with}\quad\mathbf{f}_{M}(y):=\sum_{S\in\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{M}}\int_{\Phi_{S}^{-1}(y)}\zeta_{S}(x)\,d\mu_{\Phi_{S}^{-1}(y)}(x),

where
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{M}
is for each
M\in\mathcal{F}
a finite collection of globally subanalytic manifolds comprised in
\Phi^{-1}(M)
on each of which
\Phi
induces a
\mathscr{C}^{\infty}
submersion
\Phi_{S}:S\to M
and the
\zeta_{S}:S\to[0,\infty]
are globally subanalytic functions.

Proof.

If
\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i}\cdot\mu_{E_{i}}
, it clearly suffices to prove the desired fact for each
\xi_{i}\cdot\mu_{E_{i}}
, which means that we can suppose
\mu
to be of type
\xi\cdot\mu_{E}
,
E
definable manifold and
\xi:E\to[0,\infty]
definable function. Take a stratification
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})
of
\Phi
(see

[
24
, Definition
2.6.9
]
for the definition of a stratification of a mapping) such that
E
is a union of strata of
\mathcal{S}
. Since
\Phi_{*}\mu=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}(\Phi_{*}\mu)_{|T}
, we can assume that
\mathcal{T}
is reduced to one single stratum
T
. Since
\mu_{E}=\sum\mu_{S}

where the sum ranges over all the
S\in\mathcal{S}
satisfying
S\subset E
and
\dim S=\dim E
, it is clearly enough to prove the needed fact for the mapping
\Phi_{S}:S\to T
induced by
\Phi
on
S
, for every such
S
.

We are reduced to the case of a measure
\xi\cdot\mu_{S}
with
\Phi_{S}:S\to T
smooth submersion, in which case the claimed fact can be easily derived from the coarea formula (
1.1
), as follows. Note that since
\Phi_{S}
is a submersion, the generalized jacobian of
\Phi_{S}
does not vanish, and we can set

(2.3)

\zeta_{S}(y):=\int_{\Phi_{S}^{-1}(y)}\frac{\xi(x)}{\mbox{jac}\,\Phi_{S}(x)}\,d\mu_{\Phi_{S}^{-1}(y)}(x),\qquad y\in T.

Then, by definition of the push-forward, we have for every Borel set
B\subset T

\displaystyle\Phi_{S*}(\xi\cdot\mu_{S})(B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Phi_{S}^{-1}(B)}\xi(x)\,d\mu_{S}(x)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq_coarea_formula})}{=}

\displaystyle\int_{B}\int_{\Phi^{-1}_{S}(y)}\frac{\xi(x)}{\mbox{jac}\,\Phi_{S}(x)}\,d\mu_{\Phi_{S}^{-1}(y)}(x)\,d\mu_{T}(y)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq_dfn_zeta})}{=}

\displaystyle\int_{B}\zeta_{S}(y)\,d\mu_{T}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta_{S}\cdot\mu_{T}(B),

which is of the desired type.
∎

The sets
\mathcal{I}(M)
and
\overline{\mathcal{I}}(M)
.
We can summarize the above proposition by saying that the image of a definable measure under a definable morphism is a finite sum of measures of definable manifolds with densities that are integrals of definable functions on the fibers. It is well-known that integrals of definable functions may fail to be definable, which complicates our task. It can be seen that these are log-analytic functions
[
6
,
24
]
, i.e. finite polynomials in some globally subanalytic functions and their logarithms, but we shall not need it as it will be more convenient for us not to exit the subanalytic structure.

This leads us to introduce the following sets of nonnegative functions. Given a definable manifold
M
, we will denote by

\mathcal{I}(M)
the set of functions (not necessarily definable)
\mathbf{f}:M\to[0,\infty]
of type
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{Z_{i,y}}\zeta_{i,y}(x)d\mu_{Z_{i,y}}(x)
with
(Z_{i,y})_{y\in M}
definable family of manifolds and
\zeta_{i,y}:Z_{i,y}\to[0,\infty]
definable family of functions for each
i
.

We then denote by
\overline{\mathcal{I}}(M)
the subset of
\mathcal{I}(M)
constituted by the elements
\mathbf{f}
as above for which the families of sets
(Z_{i,y})_{y\in M}
and the families of functions
(\zeta_{i,y})_{y\in M}
can be chosen uniformly bounded.

Proposition 2.5
.

If
\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{I}(M)
then
\mathbf{f}^{-1}(0)
and
\mathbf{f}^{-1}(\infty)
are definable. Moreover, if
M
is bounded and
\dim M\geq 1
then
there is an
\mathbf{f}\cdot\mu_{M}
-negligible definable set
B\subset M
which is closed in
M
and such that we have on
M
for some positive constants
\alpha
and
c

(2.4)

\mathbf{f}(y)\geq c\,d(y,B\cup\delta M)^{\alpha}.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{I}(M)
, say
\mathbf{f}(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{Z_{i,y}}\zeta_{i,y}d\mu_{Z_{i,y}}
. Set then

\check{Z}_{i,y}:=\{(x,z)\in Z_{i,y}\times\mathbb{R}:0<z<\zeta_{i,y}(x)\},

and note that

(2.5)

\mathbf{f}(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\check{Z}_{i,y}|.

By
[
24
, Corollary
3.2.12
]
, there is a finite partition
\mathcal{P}
of
M
into definable sets such that
(\check{Z}_{i,y})_{y\in M}
is for each
i
definably bi-Lipschitz trivial
along each element of
\mathcal{P}
, which means that for each
S\in\mathcal{P}
there are
y_{S}
in
S
and a definable family of bi-Lipschitz homeomorphisms
h_{i,y}:\check{Z}_{i,y}\to\check{Z}_{i,y_{S}}
,
y\in S
. Since bi-Lipschitz mappings preserve both negligible and infinite measure sets,
\mathbf{f}^{-1}(0)
and
\mathbf{f}^{-1}(\infty)
are unions of elements of
\mathcal{P}
, and hence are definable.

Suppose now
M
to be bounded and at least one-dimensional. Let
C_{i,y}
denote the bi-Lipschitz constant of
h_{i,y}
, and observe that
y\mapsto C_{i,y}
is for each
i
a definable function. Refining the partition
\mathcal{P}
, we can assume that the frontier in
M
of an element of
\mathcal{P}
is a union of elements of
\mathcal{P}
and that
y\mapsto C_{i,y}
is continuous on each element of
\mathcal{P}
(for each
i
). Notice that for
y\in S\in\mathcal{P}

(2.6)

|\check{Z}_{i,y}|\geq\frac{|\check{Z}_{i,y_{S}}|}{(1+C_{i,y})^{l_{i,S}}},\qquad\mbox{where }\;\;l_{i,S}:=\dim\check{Z}_{i,y_{S}}.

Let
B
denote the union of all the nowhere dense elements of
\mathcal{P}
together with all the elements of
\mathcal{P}
on which
\mathbf{f}
(identically) vanishes. Possibly extracting a point from an element of
\mathcal{P}
(which is
\mu_{M}
-negligible, since
\dim M\geq 1
) we may assume
B
to be nonempty. Fix
S\in\mathcal{P}
disjoint from
B
. As
C_{i,y|S}
is continuous and
M
is bounded, it can only tend to infinity at points of
\delta S\subset B\cup\delta M
.
By Łojasiewicz’s inequality (Proposition
2.2
, applied to
g(y):=\frac{1}{1+C_{i,y}}
and
f(y):=d(y,B\cup\delta M)
), we deduce that there are constants
\kappa
and
\lambda
such that we have on
S
for each
i

(2.7)

d(y,B\cup\delta M)^{\kappa}\leq\frac{\lambda}{1+C_{i,y}}.

We thus conclude for
y\in S

\mathbf{f}(y)\overset{(\ref{eq_f_zc})}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\check{Z}_{i,y}|\overset{(\ref{eq_mu_zc})}{\geq}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{|\check{Z}_{i,y_{S}}|}{(1+C_{i,y})^{l_{i,S}}}\overset{(\ref{eq_cilambda})}{\geq}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{|\check{Z}_{i,y_{S}}|}{\lambda^{l_{i,S}}}d(y,B\cup\delta M)^{\kappa l_{i,S}}.

Since
l_{i,S}
takes only finitely many values, this yields the desired fact.
∎

2.2.
Lipschitz conic structure

Let us first recall the following theorem which is a Lipschitz version of the famous
\mathscr{C}^{0}
conic structure of definable sets. We refer to
[
24
, Theorem
3.4.1
]
for a proof.

Theorem 2.6

(Lipschitz Conic Structure)
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be subanalytic and
x_{0}\in X
. For each
\varepsilon>0
small enough, there exists a Lipschitz subanalytic homeomorphism

H:x_{0}*(\mathbf{S}(x_{0},\varepsilon)\cap X)\to\overline{\mathbf{B}}(x_{0},\varepsilon)\cap X,

satisfying
H_{|\mathbf{S}(x_{0},\varepsilon)\cap X}=Id
, preserving the distance to
x_{0}
, and having the following metric properties:

(i)

The natural retraction by deformation onto
x_{0}

r:[0,1]\times\overline{\mathbf{B}}(x_{0},\varepsilon)\cap X\to\overline{\mathbf{B}}(x_{0},\varepsilon)\cap X,

defined by

(2.8)

r(s,x):=H(sH^{-1}(x)+(1-s)x_{0}),

is Lipschitz. Indeed, there is a constant
C
such that for every fixed
s\in[0,1]
, the mapping
r_{s}
defined by
x\mapsto r_{s}(x):=r(s,x)
, is
Cs
-Lipschitz.

(ii)

For each
\delta>0
,
the restriction of
H^{-1}
to
\{x\in X:\delta\leq|x-x_{0}|\leq\varepsilon\}
is Lipschitz and, for each
s\in(0,1]
, the map
r_{s}^{-1}:\overline{\mathbf{B}}(x_{0},s\varepsilon)\cap X\to\overline{\mathbf{B}}(x_{0},\varepsilon)\cap X
is Lipschitz.

Any retraction
r
as in the above theorem will be called
a Lipschitz conic structure retraction of
X
at
{x_{0}}
.
It was established in the proof of the above theorem (see
[
24
, Remark 3.3.2]
) that given definable set-germs
X_{1},\dots,X_{l}
at
{x_{0}}\in\cap_{i=1}^{l}X_{i}
, we can find a mapping
r:[0,1]\times\overline{\mathbf{B}}(x_{0},\varepsilon)\to\overline{\mathbf{B}}(x_{0},\varepsilon)
that induces on every
[0,1]\times X_{i}
(by restriction) a Lipschitz conic structure retraction of
X_{i}
at
{x_{0}}
(i.e. we can handle several sets simultaneously). We will then say that
r
is a
Lipschitz conic structure retraction of
X_{1},\dots,X_{l}
at
{x_{0}}
.

We may require in addition that, given germs of nonnegative bounded definable functions
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{k}
at
{x_{0}}
, we can choose
r_{s}
such that
\eta_{i}(r_{s}(x))\leq C\eta_{i}(x)
for all
i
, for some constant
C
independent of
x
and
s
(see
[
24
, Remark
3.4.4
]
).
Unfortunately, as mentioned in the preceding section, the study of push-forward measures will involve non definable density functions and we need to establish such a fact for an element
\mathbf{f}\in\overline{\mathcal{I}}(M)
, which is the purpose of the proposition below whose rather technical proof is postponed to section

.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\mathbf{f}\in\overline{\mathcal{I}}(M)
,

definable manifold, and
x_{0}\in{\overline{M}}

\varepsilon>0

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

small there is a Lipschitz conic structure retraction

We first focus on

\displaystyle||u||_{L^{p}_{\mathbf{f}}(N^{\eta})}^{p}

\displaystyle||u||_{L^{p}_{\mathbf{f}}(N^{\eta})}^{p}\overset{(\ref{eq_u_neta})}{\lesssim}\eta^{m-p}\cdot\eta^{p}\int^{\varepsilon/\eta}_{1}||\nabla u||_{L^{p}_{\mathbf{f}}(N^{t\eta})}^{p}dt\overset{(\ref{eq_coarea_sph})}{\lesssim}\eta^{m-1}||\nabla u||_{L_{\mathbf{f}}^{p}(M)}^{p},

3.2.
Embedding theorems

Unlike the classical one, our Sobolev embedding theorem does not embed

into

(see just below). The number

depends on the geometry of the singularities of
\delta M
and on the rate of vanishing of

, and not only on

. The crucial point of the theorem below is however that

can be chosen independent of

Theorem 3.3
.

(Sobolev embedding)
Let

be a bounded
definable manifold. Given
\mathbf{f}\in\overline{\mathcal{I}}(M)

It suffices to establish such an inequality for an element

of
\mathscr{C}_{\mathbf{f}}^{1,p}(M)

to be supported in
\mathbf{B}({x_{0}},\varepsilon)

{x_{0}}\in{\overline{M}}
and
\varepsilon>0

We argue by induction on

Fix

Observe that for

\displaystyle||v\circ r_{s}||_{L^{p}_{\mathbf{f}}(N^{\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{N^{\varepsilon}}|v\circ r_{s}|^{p}\,\mathbf{f}\right)^{1/p}\overset{(\ref{eq_jacr_s})}{\lesssim}\left(\int_{N^{\varepsilon}}|v(r_{s}(x))|^{p}\,\frac{\mbox{jac}\,\,r_{s}^{\varepsilon}(x)}{s^{\nu}}\ \mathbf{f}(x)dx\right)^{1/p}

to

To estimate the

4.1.
Regular vectors.

Given a
definable set

stands for the set of points at which

is a definable manifold (of any dimension).

Definition 4.1

Let

We then also say that

It is not difficult to see that

(the last vector of the canonical basis of

if and only if this set can be comprised in the union of the graphs of some definable Lipschitz functions

(for more, see
