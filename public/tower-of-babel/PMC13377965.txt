The Tower Foraging Park: A paradigm for studying cognitive and motor processes underlying behavioral flexibility in freely moving mice
Authors: Maud Schaffhauser, Tom Orjollet-Lacomme, Kenza Amroune, Thomas Morvan, Aurélien Fortoul, Mathias Lechelon, David Robbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBalancing experimental control with ecological validity remains a central challenge for studying brain function. Here, we developed the Tower Foraging Park (TFP), a self-paced behavioral paradigm that emulates patch foraging and in which mice collect rewards by performing directional quarter-turns around square towers (exploit) and switching between them as they become depleted (explore). Mice rapidly learned the rewarded turning direction, increased movement speed, reduced trajectory variability, and typically abandoned towers before depletion. Reversal of the rewarded direction triggered rapid adaptation, accompanied by a dissociation between movement variability and speed. Repeated reversals progressively improved flexibility. Increasing the difficulty of locating rewarding towers prolonged exploitation, revealing adaptive regulation of patch-leaving decisions. Finally, mice trained under flexible contingencies ultimately outperformed those trained under stable contingencies in a challenging context. Altogether, the TFP reveals mechanisms underlying flexible foraging and provides a versatile, ecologically grounded platform for investigating the neural bases of adaptive behavior.

Introduction

One overarching aim of neuroscience is to understand how humans and other animals learn and flexibly adapt their behavior to satisfy needs, allowing them to strive and survive. It is largely accepted that adaptive behavior relies on separate functions, such as spatial navigation, motor control, memory, sensory-guided decision-making, or procedural learning, mapping onto distinct brain regions.1 To understand these brain functions and their neuronal underpinnings, a widely used strategy is to train humans and other animals on trial-based tasks that segment behavior into perceptual, cognitive, and motor epochs. This strategy reflects the necessity of simplifying the conditions under which brain functions are investigated2 and has led to major insights into the understanding of perception, cognition, and motor control.3,4,5,6,7 But it also faces practical challenges, especially when applied to non-human animals. Indeed, for trial-based tasks to yield statistically robust results, animals must perform numerous trials for small rewards delivered if they follow the task’s rules (e.g., move left if stimulus A appears) and respect their epoch-based temporal structure (e.g., wait for stimulus presentation and deliberation period before responding). Yet animals are driven to secure rewards as quickly as possible,8 and perception, cognition, and action spontaneously unfold in parallel rather than serially.9,10,11,12 Extensive training is therefore needed to counter their impulsivity and to enforce compliance with the abstract constraints of trial-based tasks. This challenge is particularly pronounced for natural foragers such as rodents, especially when they are head-fixed to enable neuronal recordings.13 Moreover, it has been increasingly recognized that rodents develop stereotyped uninstructed motor patterns (rituals) in such experimental contexts,14,15,16,17,18 sometimes bypassing the brain functions that the experimenters aimed to study.19,20,21,22,23,24,25 Such an experimental outcome is at odds with the original scientific objective: to understand the capacity of animals to exhibit adaptive and flexible behaviors. Efforts have been made to analytically separate the neuronal correlates of movements and cognition in trial-based tasks.16 Still, the highly constrained structure of these tasks raises epistemological concerns2,25,26,27,28 and limits the understanding of the behavioral and neuronal processes that enable animals to flexibly adjust their behavior both to their dynamic needs and to environmental changes.

An alternative experimental strategy has been to take advantage of the sustained and spontaneous exploratory behavior of rats and mice. Historically, this approach has been successful in the study of spatial representation29 or motor sequences.30,31,32 Combined with recent advances in movement quantification (e.g.,33) and its unsupervised decomposition into discrete actions (e.g.,34), it has increasingly been used to study neuronal correlates of so-called self-paced or innate movements and social interactions.35,36 One obvious advantage of this approach is that it does not require training and capitalizes on the animal's natural behavioral repertoire. A limitation, however, is that it may be difficult to obtain enough repetitions of complex behavioral situations to investigate higher-level functions such as sensory-guided decision-making, motor skill learning, and memory (but see37,38,39,40). In addition, if internal and external constraints shaping spontaneous exploration and social interactions are not manipulated, the observed behavioral and neuronal dynamics can be informative but remain limited in revealing underlying mechanisms.41

In summary, trial-based tasks provide analytical power at the cost of ecological validity, whereas self-paced exploration captures the richness of spontaneous behavioral activity but often remains descriptive. These approaches highlight the diversity of strategies for studying behavior and its underlying brain functions, but one may wonder how to combine them to harness their strengths. We propose that patch foraging in a self-paced, freely moving context provides a natural bridge between these approaches. Patch foraging consists of searching for and consuming resources that are concentrated in discrete locations.42,43,44 During patch foraging, animals must decide which patch to harvest (exploit), and after this initial commitment, must decide when to leave the patch to search for a new one (explore), hopefully in a better state than the one they left. This dilemma is referred to as the exploration-exploitation tradeoff. Patch foraging provides several interesting features to study adaptive behavior in a quantitative manner. First, foraging consists of a continuous cycle of decisions and actions that can be easily parsed into exploitative and exploratory phases, eliminating the need for the artificial segmentation of behavior while providing enough repetitions to yield statistically robust results. Second, the decisions involved do not require associations between abstract stimuli or actions and reward delivery, avoiding lengthy training to condition animals. Third, patch foraging is not simply a decision-making dilemma but also requires movement to exploit and explore the environment, allowing the investigation of motor control in a continuous and relatively unconstrained setting. Fourth, because patches eventually deplete, animals cannot repeat the same behavior to obtain rewards, creating a natural balance between structure and flexibility. Fifth, even if there is a theoretical optimal solution to the patch foraging problem,45 animals typically deviate from optimality.46 Indeed, the exploration-exploitation tradeoff is known to be sensitive to internal and external constraints (e.g., motivational state, environmental knowledge, and effort) that can be manipulated, providing a straightforward approach to probe the behavioral and neuronal mechanisms underlying adaptive behavior.

Here, we developed the Tower Foraging Park (TFP), a patch-foraging task in which four square towers deliver water drops from tubes protruding from each of their four faces (i.e., four water delivery tubes per tower). Mice can collect water drops by performing quarter-turns (QTs) around the towers in a given direction (patch exploitation), with all towers being potentially rewarding. Critically, each time a mouse begins exploiting a tower, the number of available drops is randomly set to a small value, requiring mice to regularly switch towers (patch exploration). We first examined whether and how an initial group of mice became proficient in this task, both in terms of choice (performing a majority of turns in the rewarded direction, exploit-explore strategy) and motor control (kinematics of QTs and runs between towers). We then tested how these well-trained mice adapted to a reversal of the rewarded turning direction, assessing both their ability to update their choices and the accompanying changes in movement kinematics. In a second phase, we trained a separate group of mice under a protocol in which the rewarded direction was reversed daily. In a more exploratory phase of the study, both groups were exposed to a substantially more challenging foraging protocol in which, contrary to the previous protocols, a single rewarding tower-direction combination was randomly selected each time a mouse departed from a depleted rewarding tower.

Results

To emulate the dilemmas faced by animals during patch foraging, we created an experimental apparatus consisting of a custom-made square arena in which four smaller square towers are positioned at the center of each quadrant (northwest, northeast, southwest, and southeast towers, Figures 1A and 1B). The four walls of each tower (north, east, south, and west) are equipped with a small tube located at its center (Figure 1B, right). Each tube (referred to as a water port in the rest of the manuscript) is connected to a water reservoir housed within the tower to deliver single water drops. The arena floor is transparent, allowing video tracking of the mice's position using an infrared camera positioned beneath the apparatus. A closed-loop system based on real-time video tracking of the animal positions controls the delivery of drops of water based on the trajectory of the mice (Figure 1C). To ensure that mice obtained a drop of water when moving from one water port to the next (i.e., when turning around a tower corner), we defined four trapezoidal regions (referred to as trapezes) around each tower, each containing a water port. A single drop of water was delivered each time a mouse crossed the boundary between two adjacent trapezes (referred to as a trapeze switch; Figure 1C, see inset on the computer screen). With this basic rule for obtaining rewards and the spatial configuration of the setup, mice can obtain a series of water drops by circling along the walls of a given tower, going from one water port to the next (i.e., they can harvest, or exploit a tower by performing QTs around it). A critical feature of the task is that, each time a mouse begins exploiting a tower, the number of available water drops is finite (i.e., towers become transiently depleted through a software-imposed limit). Therefore, the task discourages mice from circling only around a single tower and requires them to regularly switch towers (explore). For this, a Python-based control software integrates information about the mice trajectory to further control the water delivery based on: (1) the harvesting direction (e.g., whether the mouse performs QTs in clockwise (CW) or counterclockwise (CCW) direction); (2) which tower can potentially be harvested (e.g., all or a subset of towers deliver water with similar or distinct rules of depletion); (3) ongoing harvesting history (e.g., number of drops obtained or number of QTs performed between ports while harvesting a given tower); and (4) in-between towers history (e.g., which tower can be harvested after leaving a tower, how long after leaving a tower can reward be obtained in the other towers). The combination of these features allows experimenters to study decision-making in a variety of foraging contexts (e.g., different modes of patch depletion and exploration rules) while also engaging processes related to motor skills and spatial cognition.

To illustrate the potential of the TFP, we present two series of experiments conducted in two cohorts of mice larger than those typically used in mouse behavioral studies. In the first group (group 1, n = 19), we examined whether mice could forage under a fixed procedural constraint: Only one turning direction was rewarded. All towers could deliver water, and each time a mouse began harvesting from a tower, we randomly set the maximum number of drops available in that tower for that visit. Once this number was reached, no more drops could be obtained, forcing mice to explore. We quantified the mice’s ability to perform the right action (preference for the QTs in the rewarded direction), their exploration-exploitation tradeoff, and the kinematics of their reward-oriented movements. Then we examined how these mice reacted to a sudden reversal of the rewarded direction, both in terms of decision-making (direction preference) and motor control. In the second group (group 2, n = 32), we examined whether mice could forage while the rewarded direction was reversed every day. Lastly, both groups were exposed to a more uncertain foraging protocol in which the rewarded direction and tower changed continuously and randomly during the session.

Experiment 1: Stable unidirectional harvesting followed by reversal in group 1 mice

Mice (n = 19, 10 females) were first accustomed to the experimenter during two daily 5–10 min handling sessions over 5 days. They were then familiarized with the apparatus during two 12 min long sessions (morning and afternoon familiarization sessions, day 1). After the afternoon familiarization session, they were placed under a water-restriction schedule (see STAR Methods). Starting on day 2, they performed two daily 12 min-long foraging sessions (one in the morning and one in the afternoon, spaced by at least 4 h), during which they could obtain drops of water in the TFP. Critically, the mice had to adapt to the two following rules. First, only CCW (from the camera viewpoint) turns around the towers triggered reward delivery at the next water port. Second, each time mice approached a tower, a random number of rewards was assigned for that tower and visit (between 4 and 12, equiprobably). Mice could leave a tower at any time, even without obtaining a single reward. After leaving a tower, mice could visit any of the remaining three towers, as all of them would deliver rewards if harvested in the rewarded direction. Once the mouse began harvesting an alternative tower (i.e., transitioning between two contiguous trapezes), the previous tower was reset, and the number of rewards available would be determined only when the mice re-enter this tower. The rewarded direction was maintained for 6 days (i.e., 12 CCW sessions) after which it was reversed to CW for three days (i.e., 6 CW sessions).

After a few foraging sessions, mice spent more time turning around the towers (Figures 2A and 2B, center, right). We first examined how the number, direction, and kinematics of QTs evolved in Experiment 1 (CCW-rewarded sessions). Plotting the detected QTs for sessions 1 (AM, day 1), 6 (PM, day 3), and 13 (AM, day 7) revealed a selective increase in the number of CCW QTs, while CW QTs remained rare (Figure 3B). To quantify this at the group level, we computed, for each mouse and each session, the normalized difference between the number of CCW and CW QTs. This analysis showed that within just four sessions (across days 2 and 3), all mice developed a strong preference for performing CCW QTs (Figure 3C). If mice keep harvesting a tower after it is depleted, CCW QTs are no longer rewarded. We thus examined how the number of rewarded and unrewarded QTs evolved across sessions. This revealed that mice progressively obtained more rewards, while the number of unrewarded QTs (CW QTs or CCW QTs after depletion) increased only modestly (Figure 3D). Finally, we examined when QTs occurred during the sessions. The cumulative distribution of CCW and CW QTs performed by the example mouse across the three illustrative sessions confirmed the growing bias toward CCW QTs, as well as a faster accumulation of CCW QTs at the start of the session (Figure 3E). To quantify this trend at the group level, we computed during each session the time it took for each mouse to perform 80% of their QTs in the rewarded direction. This showed that, with training, mice accumulated rewards at a progressively higher rate (Figure 3F). Altogether, these results indicate that mice rapidly performed QTs selectively in the rewarded direction.

Aligning QTs to their starting points further illustrated mice's selective performance of CCW QTs and suggested that trajectories became less variable across sessions (Figure 3G). To quantify this, we realigned and rotated all QTs in a given direction (CCW or CW) from each session relative to a single corner (see STAR Methods). We then computed the Fréchet distance, a measure of similarity between two curves, between all QT pairs in the same direction within each session (see STAR Methods for details and rationale for using this specific metric). This analysis showed that CCW QT trajectories became progressively more similar (less variable) across sessions (Figure 3H, red). Noticeably, non-rewarded CW QTs did not become more similar across sessions (Figure 3H, blue). This difference was significant and was not due to the smaller number of CW QTs, compared to CCW ones (Figures S1A and S1B).

Next, we computed the speed profile of the QTs. Comparing the speed of all QTs between the 3 illustrative sessions revealed an increased peak speed with training (Figure 3I). Performing this analysis for CCW and CW QTs across all mice and sessions revealed a progressive increase in the speed of (reward-oriented) CCW QTs, while CW QTs speed did not display such an increase (Figure 3J). This difference was significant and not due to the difference in the number of CW and CCW QTs (Figures S1C and S1D). Finally, for the illustrative mouse, color coding the speed profiles of the CCW QTs according to their order of occurrence within a given session indicated that their peak speed progressively decreased, suggesting that it was influenced by satiety or fatigue (Figure 3I;47). Across animals, this effect was observed for a variable proportion of sessions (Figure S2). Altogether, these results indicate that mice quickly learned to execute QTs selectively in the rewarded direction, with trajectories becoming less variable and faster across sessions. Consequently, mice accumulated rewards more quickly during each session.

After examining how QTs evolved during learning, we conducted a similar analysis on RBTs. We first plotted the RBTs for a single mouse (same as in Figure 3) during sessions 1, 6, and 13. This mouse performed progressively more RBTs (Figure 4A). Aligning the RBTs at their origin for the illustrative sessions (Figure 4B) and plotting their speed profiles (Figure 4C) suggested that the kinematic parameters of these actions changed across sessions. Indeed, at the group level, we observed that not only did the number of RBTs increase (Figure 4D, left), but they were also performed faster (Figure 4D, center), and their distance decreased (Figure 4D, right). These results indicate that mice performed more RBTs in a faster and more direct manner. The results above indicate that mice became progressively more efficient at collecting water drops across sessions, performing more frequent, faster, and more stereotyped QTs in the CCW direction and switching towers more efficiently. For two illustrative mice, raster plots of QTs and their locations revealed large variability in the number of QTs per visit, with a significant fraction of exploitation bouts (consecutive QTs around a tower) ending with a tower switch before a non-rewarded QT occurred (Figure 5A). To quantify how many times mice harvested a tower before switching, we computed the number of trapeze switches per rewarded visit across mice and sessions (see STAR Methods). Although this number increased significantly at the group level, it remained below the theoretical average of available rewards per visit (i.e., 8; Figure 5B). Consistent with these results, the proportion of visits including at least one unrewarded QT while the tower was depleted was very low at the onset of training and, although it increased across sessions, remained low overall (on average ∼25%; Figure S4). This tendency for underexploitation was accompanied by a relatively homogeneous exploratory drive: mice, on average, spent similar amounts of time around each tower (Figure 5C). The observation that mice often stop harvesting towers before they are fully depleted raises questions about the strength of the acquired procedural rule. It is possible that this rule (i.e., making CCW QTs around any tower) is so simple that mice can rediscover it on the fly at the beginning of each session through rapid trial-and-error. In that case, task proficiency would reflect task familiarization rather than procedural learning per se, and should remain largely unaffected if the rewarded direction was suddenly switched from CCW to CW. To test this possibility, we switched the rewarded direction between sessions 14 and 15 (i.e., before the morning session of day 8; hereafter referred to as the directional reversal) and examined how mice adapted their behavior. We observed a transient drop in the number of rewarded QTs during this first session post directional reversal (Figure 6A, pink, see Figure S5 for statistical comparisons). In this session, mice continued to perform nearly as many CCW QTs as in the previous session, despite these actions no longer being rewarded (Figure 6A, violet; Figure S5). This persistence is consistent with the acquisition of a procedural memory. Nonetheless, mice did begin to perform CW QTs in this first session post directional reversal, albeit at a much lower rate than CCW QTs (Figures 6A and 6B, Figure S5). Strikingly, by the following afternoon session, nearly all mice had already reached a performance level comparable to that of previous days (Figure 6A; Figure S5). We also examined how the rule reversal affected the time required for mice to accumulate rewards. As expected, mice took much longer to obtain 80% of their rewards during the first session after the direction reversal (Figure 6C; Figure S5), but this time rapidly decreased in subsequent sessions. This post-reversal improvement occurred faster than during initial learning (Figure S6), suggesting that the speed of adaptation is experience-dependent. Next, we examined the impact of the rule reversal on the kinematics of CCW (previously rewarded, now unrewarded) and CW (previously unrewarded, now rewarded) QTs. During the first post-reversal session, mice performed only a few CW QTs (Figures 6A and 6B), but the variability of their trajectory was already much lower than in the pre-reversal sessions, quickly reaching its minimum in the following sessions (Figure 6D, blue). Conversely, the variability of CCW QTs increased after the rule reversal (Figure 6D, red; Figure S5). Together, these findings indicate that QT variability is highly sensitive to recent reward history. On the other hand, the speed of CW QTs did not increase during the first post-directional-switch session (Figure 6E, blue), and mice continued performing unrewarded CCW QTs with the same fast speeds as in the previous session (Figure 6E, red; Figure S5). In addition, during the following post-directional-switch sessions (sessions 16–20), the speed of CW QTs slowly increased (Figure 6E, blue) even though directional choice selectivity had already plateaued around 85% by session 17 (Figure 6B). During these same sessions, the speed of CCW QTs slowly declined (Figure 6E, red), suggesting that movement speed reflects the history of reward outcomes over several sessions. Overall, these results showed that the mice’s ability to flexibly update their preferred harvest direction (from CCW to CW) was accompanied by unexpectedly decoupled changes in the variability and speed of the QT trajectories upon reversal of rewarded direction.

Experiment 3: Adaptation of group 1 and group 2 mice to random foraging rules

In a final and more exploratory experiment, we examined how the two groups of mice behaved under a highly uncertain foraging protocol in which, contrary to Experiments 1 and 2, only one tower (NW, NE, SE, or SW)—direction (CW or CCW) combination was rewarding, randomly reselected each time a mouse left a depleted tower (hereafter referred to as the uncertain protocol; see STAR Methods). The number of rewarded QTs dropped when group 1 mice were exposed to the uncertain protocol (Figure 9A, left, pink). An opposite modulation was observed for unrewarded QTs, which abruptly increased during the first sessions of the uncertain foraging protocol (Figure 9A, left, purple). This inefficiency was not due to the mice persevering in the previously rewarded direction. Indeed, the CW bias inherited from previous foraging sessions was reduced during the first session post-protocol change and was totally nonexistent after three sessions in this new protocol, suggesting that mice from group 1 struggled to quickly find the rewarded tower and direction rather than flexibly performing CW or CCW QTs (Figure 9B, left). In contrast, the decrease in rewarded QTs during the first session under the uncertain rule was much less pronounced in group 2 than in group 1 (compare Figure 9A, left vs. right panels, pink lines). Thus, although the number of unrewarded QTs logically increased in this protocol (Figure 9A, right, purple), group 2 mice ultimately maintained a fair balance between rewarded and unrewarded QTs. Moreover, group 2 mice immediately reduced their CW directional bias from the previous session, performing as many CW as CCW turns as early as the first session under the uncertain protocol (Figure 9B, right). In the uncertain protocol, the time required to find water after leaving a depleted tower necessarily increases, as the rewarded tower and direction are randomly reset. According to the marginal value theorem,45 when foragers seeking to maximize their reward rate face longer exploration times, they should increase the time spent harvesting patches. While our uncertain protocol does not fully allow us to test the assumptions of the marginal value theorem, it nevertheless predicts that mice should prolong their tower exploitation under this condition. Indeed, we found that this was the case for both groups (Figure S15). Interestingly, group 1 mice displayed longer exploitation during unrewarded tower visits compared to group 2 mice, although a similar trend was already present before switching to the uncertain foraging protocol (Figure S16). More strikingly, group 2 mice displayed a much stronger increase in the number of RBTs in the uncertain condition compared to group 1 mice (Figures S17 and S18; see also Figure S19 for statistical comparison). Critically, this effect was not attributable to differences in the number of RBTs during the session preceding the switch in the uncertain foraging protocol (Figure S19). Altogether, these results indicate that, compared to group 1, group 2 mice were able to rapidly adopt an exploratory mode of foraging that is advantageous in such an uncertain environment.

Discussion

In this study, we present experiments conducted in a developed behavioral framework that recreates the explore-exploit dilemma of patch foraging while requiring procedural rule learning (harvesting patches in a specified direction). The results show that mice rapidly developed adaptive foraging strategies. Across sessions, they preferentially harvested the towers in the rewarded direction, and the speed and variability of their reward-oriented movements (but not of non-rewarded ones) increased and decreased, respectively. In the present foraging protocols, the number of rewards available during patch (tower) exploitation was limited to a small, randomly determined quantity, requiring mice to regularly switch (explore) towers. Interestingly, we found that mice tended to leave towers before they were fully depleted from the very beginning of training. Moreover, across sessions, they progressively departed from the inner faces of the towers, facilitating transition to the nearest alternatives in the absence of visual cues. Thus, the mice’s spontaneous exploratory tendency, combined with the close proximity of the towers, resulted in a strategy in which frequent early departures minimized unrewarded visits and maximized reward capture rate.

Our experimental paradigm not only allows us to study learning, decision-making, and motor control but also reveals the animals’ capacity to adapt to an unexpected change in procedural rule (reversal of harvesting direction). We found that mice quickly, though not instantaneously, switched their turning direction and displayed an intriguing decoupling of the two main kinematic features of their reward-oriented movements: They modified their trajectories faster than their speed in response to a change in rewarding outcome. In addition, mice subjected to several reversals of the rewarded direction became faster at adapting. Later in discussion, we first discuss why our task can be considered naturalistic and compare it to previous attempts to model the exploration-exploitation dilemma. We then elaborate on the two main mechanistic findings of the study: the decoupling of speed and trajectory, and experience-dependent improvement in adaptation to rule reversals, to highlight the potential of our experimental framework to understand how animals flexibly adapt to unexpected changes in their environment.

The Tower Foraging Park, a naturalistic behavioral task?

Here we present the Tower Foraging Park (TFP), an experimental framework designed to study learning, reward-guided decisions, and movements in freely moving mice. This experimental development should be seen in the context of a growing interest in studying behavior under more naturalistic conditions.43,48,49,50,51,52,53,54 Defining what qualifies as naturalistic is a complex issue.52 We take this challenge as a starting point to outline key features that, in our view, make our task ethologically sound, despite the inevitable constraints of studying behavior in a laboratory. First, mice were allowed to move freely in a large arena (relative to their body size), and the task was not divided into trials and intertrial periods. Second, the task requires mice to forage along walls in complete darkness, which aligns with their nocturnal nature and leverages their spontaneous tendency to perform thigmotaxis.55 Consistent with this, we found that mice rely on their whisker to fine-tune the kinematics of their reward-oriented QTs.56 Third, the task did not require mice to associate arbitrary instrumental responses (e.g., lever presses) or neutral stimuli (a sound, a light) with the remote delivery of rewards. Instead, mice directly checked for the presence of water drops at the tips of water ports located on the sides of the towers. Critically, our custom software continuously tracked the position and the history of reward-oriented actions to implement reward delivery rules according to the behavior of the mice and with built-in variability. This variability constitutes the fourth feature that makes our task ethological: After a small, randomly determined number of rewards, the towers became transiently depleted. This mimics two key features of natural environments, namely, that repeated actions do not always yield the same outcomes, as resources tend to deplete or fluctuate over time, and that mice have several options to obtain rewards.

As pointed out by Chrzanowska et al.,52 what qualifies as a “naturalistic” behavioral task is to some extent subjective, and it would be problematic if this term was used as a petitio principii or a virtue signal to implicitly dismiss other approaches, especially some that have already yielded key insights into the neural underpinning of behavior. Still, given the mounting societal pressure against animal experimentation, neuroscientists cannot avoid reflecting on how they approach animal behavior. Indeed, several potential issues can arise when studying brain function during non-naturalistic tasks. The first is that overly constrained experimental conditions may promote behaviors outside the animal’s natural repertoire, leading to the investigation of non-natural brain patterns.41 The second is that such conditions can create distress, an obvious major concern, both ethically and scientifically.26 The third is the risk that, in unnatural settings, animals may not comply with the anthropomorphic expectations of the experimenter and instead solve the task using idiosyncratic heuristics, or more generally by engaging brain functions other than those originally targeted.25 Our own experience in training mice and rats on trial-based tasks has led us to identify two key parameters that could serve as markers of non-naturalistic design: long training protocols involving multiple progressive phases, and the frequent observation of uninstructed stereotyped movements (rituals) that appear necessary for task completion and that animals have difficulties to unlearn despite a change in reward outcome.23,24,25,57 Several aspects of our data provide empirical grounding for describing the TFP as naturalistic. First, after spontaneously exploring the entire arena with a clear preference for the outer borders, mice rapidly developed a foraging strategy (circling around the appropriate tower), typically within just three to four 12-min long sessions.49 Second, the kinematics of their reward-oriented movements showed expected signs of utility-based modulation, both across and within sessions. Third, mice maintained a high level of exploration of the different towers interspersed with brief exploitation bouts, which matched the task’s geometry. They did not develop prolonged exploitation nor overly stereotyped sequences of exploration of specific towers. Fourth, despite clear procedural learning, mice quickly adjusted their choices and movements when the task rules changed. Fifth, mice exposed to regular rule changes learned to become more flexible and adapted quickly to drastic changes in foraging uncertainty. Finally, although there was clear individual variability in the behavioral features described above, the trends we observed were consistent across two groups of animals larger than those commonly used in mouse behavioral studies (19 and 32 mice in Groups 1 and 2, respectively), trained under distinct protocols, and remained robust across sessions (i.e., mice did not suddenly stop working or give up, as can be observed in head-fixed paradigms).

Comparison with multi-armed bandit tasks in operant boxes

Our task is inspired by patch foraging, a behavior shared by most animals that requires solving the so-called exploration-exploitation dilemma.42,44,58 In rodents, this dilemma is most often studied using multi-armed bandit tasks, in which animals must choose between several similar actions (typically choosing to press on one lever out of 2 or 3) to obtain a reward (typically delivered in a magazine placed on the wall opposite to the levers). Each action is associated with a distinct probability of reward delivery, and these probabilities fluctuate in unsignaled blocks. Exploitation and exploration are typically inferred from the ratio of responses to the high- and low-value action and from the latency to switch lever after reward contingencies are reversed. These tasks have provided major insights into the neural mechanisms that support flexible decision-making in uncertain environments (e.g.,59,60,61,62). Nonetheless, they may not fully capture the richness of the exploration-exploitation dilemma. First, bandit tasks for rodents are usually conducted in small operant boxes, whereas exploration and exploitation are inherently spatial behaviors, especially for animals such as mice and rats. This is important because time and effort are important determinants of economic decision-making.8,63 Second, in the bandit setup, the available options (typically pressing a lever or poking its nose into a hole) are nearly identical in spatial and motor requirements. This makes it difficult to dissociate exploration from exploitation based on overt behavior alone. For example, pressing the low-probability lever could reflect either an exploratory action (testing whether its value has changed) or an exploitative one (if the animal expects it to yield a reward). Still, pressing the low-reward lever is interpreted as exploration. In our experiments, when the rewarded direction was fixed, mice rarely performed QTs in the non-rewarded direction, and when they did, their movements were slower and more variable than in the rewarded direction. Yet mice were not overly exploitative, as they generally underharvested the patch. These observations highlight the analytical granularity of our experimental design, independent of theoretical models. Third, instrumental bandit tasks typically involve large differences in reward probability between the two levers at any given time. In contrast, real-world foraging dilemmas often involve deciding when to leave a still-rewarding source, not only choosing between a good and a bad one. In the bandit setting, when reward probabilities are swapped, the animal has no choice but to switch, making it unclear whether this reflects genuine exploration or merely the abandonment of an inferior option.64 Fourth, in operant versions of bandit tasks, the location of reward delivery is physically separated from the location of the choice (e.g., lever vs. magazine). This separation limits direct comparison to natural foraging problems, such as patch depletion, where exploitation and reward consumption are spatially and temporally coupled. To structure trials and encourage choice behavior, arbitrary cues such as lever retraction or trial initiation sensory cues are often introduced. This requires conditioning the animals during lengthy pre-training phases, which may bias behavior toward exploitation and reduce its natural flexibility. Finally, multi-armed bandit tasks rarely track for the kinematics of reward-oriented movements; they focus almost exclusively on the timing of lever-press and food magazine entry, ignoring the kinematics of the actions themselves, even though they convey important information about decision-making,63 including during foraging.65

In contrast, in our setup, mice rapidly engaged in foraging behavior without any pretraining, aside from two short familiarization sessions on day one. The foraging options (towers) are spatially segregated, making both exploration and exploitation kinematically tractable on a 2D plane (e.g., QTs vs. RBTs). Notably, we observed that mice often ceased exploiting a rewarding tower even when rewards were still available, likely an adaptive strategy due to the proximity of neighboring towers. We also found that exploitation behavior was modulated by the duration of prior exploration: Under the high-uncertainty protocol, all mice exhibited more prolonged exploitation once they located a rewarding tower/direction (Figure S9). This suggests that their behavior is sensitive to both uncertainty and time, in line with theoretical prediction.46 Finally, our task can provide a bridge with human and non-human primate patch-foraging paradigms.66,67 Indeed, foraging behavior has recently emerged as a valuable model for studying the exploration-exploitation tradeoff and its relationship to mental health.68

Rule reversal reveals separate effects of reward outcome history on movement speed and variability

The TFP provides an interesting playground to study motor learning and investigate how reward influences motor control. Indeed, mice not only learned which action to perform to obtain water (CW or CCW QTs) but also displayed pronounced modulation of how they execute these actions. Thus, our study allows us to probe both action selection and execution, two fundamental components of motor skills.69,70 As mice became more familiar with the task structure and expected to find water around the towers, we observed improvements in both action selection (e.g., animals from group 1 performed more CCW than CW rewarded trajectories) and movement execution. Specifically, QTs were progressively executed faster, and their trajectories displayed less trial-by-trial variability (Figure 3). This phenomenon is well documented for visual saccades or reaching movements in both humans and non-human primates (e.g.,71,72). In rodents, reduced variability and increased speed are typically interpreted as signs of automatization of reward-oriented movements or action sequences,23,73,74,75,76 reflecting a relatively unified motor learning process. Surprisingly, when mice from group 1 performed their first session following rule reversal (i.e., switching from six days of training with CCW QTs rewarded to CW QTs being rewarded), we observed an increase in the variability of CCW QTs, while their speed remained unchanged (i.e., fast). Analysis of CCW QTs across sessions confirmed that variability increased more rapidly than speed decreased. A similar dissociation was found for CW turns: Before the rule reversal, these unrewarded actions remained both slow and highly variable. Yet as early as the first session after reversal, their variability decreased while speed remained low. Over subsequent sessions, variability quickly reached a low plateau, whereas the speed of CW QTs increased more gradually. This pattern suggests that reward-related movement variability is highly sensitive to recent reward history,77 while speed integrates reward outcomes over a longer timescale spanning several sessions, potentially reflecting the degree of confidence animals have that the chosen actions will yield a positive outcome. The differential influence of reward history on QT speed and variability was robust and also evident in group 2 mice. For these animals, the rewarded direction was swapped each morning, and the variability of CCW or CW QTs immediately decreased when CCW or CW QTs were rewarded, respectively (Figure 6D). In contrast, in the same sessions, the speed of CCW or CW did not increase immediately, consistent with the idea that it reflects reward integration across multiple sessions (Figure 6E).

The above decoupling between the speed and variability of reward-oriented movements is interesting for several reasons. First, if reduced QT variability simply reflected the consolidation of a motor skill, it is unclear why movement variability would increase so quickly once the action became unrewarded. In the rodent literature, the stereotypy of movements is often associated with habitual or skilled action (i.e., robust to change in reward contingencies). Here, the reported high sensitivity of QT variability to reward history echoes works in humans78,79 and rats,77 and suggests that movement variability can also reflect an exploration-exploitation tradeoff enabling adaptive behavior when reward outcome fluctuates.80 In the case of a foraging animal under temporal pressure, increasing movement variability while maintaining their speed (instead of slowing down) may help animals to quickly find an alternative solution to a now obsolete kinematics. Second, reward-dependent movement invigoration is a well-known adaptive process allowing humans and other animals to reduce temporal discounting of rewards.63 However, this process poses a challenge for the nervous system: Stronger motor commands are more susceptible to noise, which can reduce both trajectory and endpoint accuracy, and necessitate error-reducing mechanisms.81,82 It has been proposed, for example, that reward may compensate not only for the energetic cost necessary to move quickly but also for the cost of maintaining precision, acting therefore on two separate aspects of motor control.83 The reduction of motor noise has been proposed to involve multiple mechanisms, including increased limb stiffness.84 By establishing a model in which locomotion speed and variability can decouple in response to a sudden change in reward history, our study opens the way to investigating the neural mechanisms underlying this dissociation (e.g., distinct brain circuits under the influence of dopamine). Here, for the sake of simplicity, all our analyses were based on tracking the center of mass of the mice. In the future, a better quantification of the entire body position will be incorporated.33,85 Indeed, while biomechanical costs have been shown to influence motor decisions in humans,86,87 the extent to which similar modulation contributes to foraging behavior is, to the best of our knowledge, largely unknown, despite obvious relevance in the context of a freely moving foraging task.11

Repetitive reversion of harvesting direction reveals functional meta-learning

An interesting observation from our experiments is that group 2 mice, which were exposed to daily reversals of the rewarded direction, became progressively faster at updating their choice in the morning sessions (Figure 8; Figure S8). In these sessions, group 2 mice began by harvesting in the direction rewarded on the previous day (see Figure 7C), showing that the animals relied on their past experience to forage. This persistence in performing QTs in a non-rewarded direction suggests that they had formed a “belief” that this direction should yield reward, and required repeated contradictory outcomes to revise their policy. This strategy is adaptive, since the absence of reward could result from transient noise, and it would be suboptimal to immediately abandon a previously successful strategy. However, excessive rigidity would also be detrimental, especially in a constantly changing world. Strikingly, across sessions, group 2 mice performed fewer unrewarded QTs and more rewarded QTs during the morning sessions (Figures 8A and 8B). This translated into progressively faster reward accumulation (Figure 8C) and less difference between morning and afternoon sessions’ performance (Figure S8), indicating that, with experience, mice adapted more quickly to the daily change in reward direction. This experience-dependent improvement in adaptation resembles what is referred to as meta-learning, the ability to learn to learn.88 Theoretically, such a process should provide an advantage in adapting to other highly volatile foraging conditions. In line with such a prediction, group 2 mice were more proficient than group 1 in a more challenging version of the task, where, after leaving a depleted tower, the next rewarding tower and direction were assigned randomly. Group 2 mice managed to maintain reward intake in this uncertain version, whereas group 1 mice showed reduced reward gains, although there was considerable individual variability (some group 2 mice performed poorly, and some group 1 mice performed relatively well). Still, a clear difference emerged: In the uncertain foraging task version, group 2 mice markedly increased their number of RBTs, whereas group 1 mice did not (compare Figures S11D and S12D). This indicates that group 2 mice were more explorative, most likely due to their prior experience with unrewarded outcomes when the rewarded direction was reversed every morning session. We acknowledge that the comparison between the two groups is not ideal, as they differed in genetic background and were involved in distinct experimental protocols (group 2 mice participated in chemogenetic manipulations not reported here; see STAR Methods). Although we cannot rule out that these differences influenced behavior, performance in the session preceding the switch to the uncertain foraging protocol was comparable across groups (Figure 9; compare session 20 in group 1 with session 34 in group 2). Group 2 mice also underwent longer training, which, if anything, strengthens our interpretation, as extended training under stable contingencies would be expected to reduce rather than enhance flexibility. Altogether, our data reveal a striking experience-dependent improvement in adaptation and pave the way to work aimed at testing its functional relevance and neuronal underpinnings. Whether this form of meta-learning reflects a high-level internal representation of the task environment and its directional rules89,90,91 is certainly possible, although it remains speculative at this stage. The relatively slow adaptation to rule reversals, both in terms of directional choice and movement speed, combined with the highly exploratory behavioral profile of mice, could also support a more embodied form of knowledge that may be updated at variable rates, without necessarily relying on the manipulation of abstract task representations.

In summary, the TFP offers a powerful framework for studying learning, flexible decision-making, and motor control. By training two groups of mice under distinct protocols, we showed that animals rapidly developed efficient strategies and adapted their choices and movements when confronted with sudden changes in foraging rules. Importantly, mice maintained a strong exploratory drive: They neither overexploited specific towers nor settled into stereotyped routines, which we interpret as evidence of naturalistic behavior. These experiments provide insights into how reward influences motor control, and uncovered a form of experience-dependent improvement in adaptation, whose behavioral relevance was explored in alternative task versions, an approach, to our knowledge, rarely applied in rodents. The paradigm generates statistically robust datasets that comply with FAIR principles (see quantification and statistical analysis), and both hardware and software will be made openly available. Combined with neurophysiological and computational approaches, the TFP could also prove valuable for translational research, including the study of disease models, since foraging principles extend naturally to human behavior.

Limitations of the study

Clearly, every scientific attempt to understand the living comes with inherent limitations. There will always be a gap between “real-life” experience and the simplifications (or abstractions) required to study brain functions scientifically.2 Our task is no exception to this limitation. First, mice were motivated to forage through a controlled water restriction schedule. This introduces an artificial element and some stress, although prolonged hunger and thirst are not unnatural states for wild animals. Thus, although thirst was experimentally induced, the resulting drive to forage reflects part of their innate behavioral repertoire. Second, and related to the first point, mice were placed twice per day in the arena by an experimenter, who also returned them to their home cage when the session ended (session duration was fixed at 12 min). This experimental condition implies a significant temporal pressure and a stress level that is likely to impact decision-making, motor control, learning, and adaptation. Our mid-term objective is to provide mice with on-demand access to the foraging area, rather than having experimenters handle them at fixed times of the day.92,93 This kind of approach would open the door to a wide range of questions, such as whether the urgency to drink during a limited number of short sessions and the potential physiological stresses induced by these protocols affect mice’s decision-making and movements.

Experimental model and study participant details

Group 1 included 19 mice (9 males; 10 females) from two genetic backgrounds: C57BL/6N wildtype mice (Charles River, 4 mice) and C57BL/6J Eml1fl/fl and RhoAfl/fl “control” mice (94,95, 8 and 7 mice, respectively). In these two transgenic lines, flox sequences were inserted around specific exons from the Eml1 or RhoA genes without interfering with the expression of these two genes. Eml1fl/fl and RhoAfl/fl “control” mice were therefore considered as “wild-type”.

Group 2 included 32 C57BL/6J (20 males; 12 females) mice expressing Cre recombinase under the DRD1 or A2A promoter (DRD1-Cre or A2A-Cre, 18 and 14 mice, respectively,96,97). Group 2 mice underwent a stereotaxic surgery under deep anesthesia (Isoflurane 2,5-3%) 1 week prior to the first familiarization session, to perform chemogenetic manipulations of striatal projection neurons (results not described in this study).

All mice were 8 to 12 weeks old at the start of the experiments and were group-housed (2-3 per cage) in temperature-controlled, ventilated racks. A reversed 12 h light/dark cycle was maintained, and all behavioral experiments were conducted during the dark phase. Food was available ad libitum throughout the experiments. To motivate active foraging, mice underwent a controlled water restriction protocol (see below for detail). The number of animals (19 and 32 mice in Group 1 and 2) was not determined by a formal power calculation but is similar if not superior to sample sizes commonly used in mouse behavioral studies. No significant sex-related differences were observed in the behavioral outcomes reported in this study. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional and national ethical guidelines for animal research (European Community Directive 86/609/EEC) and were approved by the French Ministry of Higher Education and Research (Ministère de l’enseignement supérieur et de la recherche, France, Authorization #46285).

Method details

Apparatus

The behavioral task was conducted in a custom-designed square arena (83 × 83 cm) enclosed by opaque 9-cm-high walls (Figure 1). At the center of each quadrant, a square tower (12 × 12 cm wide, 9 cm high) was placed, resulting in four fixed tower locations: northwest, northeast, southwest, and southeast. Each of the four vertical faces of every tower was equipped with a protruding water port (total of 16 ports), consisting of a 0.9 mm diameter tube centered on the wall and positioned 2 cm above the arena floor. The tube extended 2 cm outward and was connected to an internal water reservoir within the tower, which was covered and inaccessible to the animal. Water drops were delivered via solenoid valves (VAC-20 PSIG, 12 V, Parker), each controlled independently through digital pulses, enabling precise and localized closed-loop water delivery. All water ports were calibrated at the beginning of each experimental day to deliver 4 μL droplets that remained suspended at the tip of tube by surface tension, controlled via the valve opening duration. Water flow was driven by the hydrostatic pressure difference between the reservoir and the delivery tube. Thus, we cannot exclude the possibility of small, random trial-by-trial variations in droplet size. The floor of the arena was made of transparent Plexiglas and elevated 140 cm above the ground to allow for video tracking from below using an infrared-sensitive camera (Grasshopper3 GS3_U3_41C6NIR, Point Grey Research), operating at 25 Hz. The transparent Plexiglas floor was laser-cut into interlocking puzzle-shaped tiles to facilitate cleaning and modularity. Each tile included pre-drilled holes precisely positioned to accommodate vertical support pillars used to secure the tower walls and outer arena walls. This design allows for flexible reconfiguration of the arena layout, including change in the number, shape and position of the towers. All experiments were conducted in complete darkness, in accordance with the animals’ reversed light-dark cycle and to promote reliance on thigmotaxis. Upon publication, all the hardware components along with instructions to build the apparatus will be available in a public repository (see data and code availability section).

Closed-loop water delivery

Mouse position was tracked and video frames were processed in real time using a custom Python script using a background subtraction algorithm provided by the OpenCV library.99 The extracted positional data was processed online and communicated to a Raspberry Pi 3B+ to control water delivery. The system employed a custom Raspberry Pi HAT integrating a 16-bit input/output expander (MCP23017) and two Darlington transistor arrays (ULN2803A) to independently trigger the 16 solenoid valves.

Four trapezoidal regions (trapezes) were virtually defined around each tower, each containing a water port. A drop of water was delivered when the mouse crossed the boundary between two adjacent trapezes surrounding a given tower (depending on foraging rules). This crossing triggered (or not) a digital pulse to open the corresponding valve. Drops were delivered even if the mouse did not stop or lick at the port, allowing for passive accumulation of drops early in training sessions. The solenoid valve produced a brief sound when opening as mice crossed the border between trapezes; although this occurred after movement initiation and shortly before port arrival, we cannot rule out that animals used this cue.

General behavioral procedure

Prior to behavioral testing, all the mice were first accustomed to the experimenter through two daily “handling” sessions of 5 to 10 minutes for 4-5 consecutive days, with animals handled together with their cage mates. They then underwent two familiarization sessions (one in the morning and one in the afternoon), lasting 12 minutes for Group 1 and 15 minutes for Group 2, during which each mouse individually explored the arena. During these sessions, mice were not water-restricted and no water was delivered in the arena. Throughout the figures of this article, these familiarization sessions are referred to as sessions 1 and 2 (day 1, orange shading). Water restriction began after the second familiarization session and at least 20 hours before the first foraging session (i.e., session 3). From day 2 onward, mice were subjected to two daily foraging sessions lasting 12 minutes each, one in the morning and one in the afternoon, interspaced by at least 4 hours. Mice were always introduced into the arena from the same starting location.Body weight was monitored twice daily (before and after behavioral sessions). The number of sessions and their duration allowed mice to maintain their weight at about 90 to 95% of their body weight before water restriction. Well-trained mice stopped foraging for water after 5 to 8 minutes, most likely because they started looking for solid food. Throughout the entire protocol, water was only accessible during the foraging sessions, except for mice whose weight dropped below 85% of their pre-restriction weight, in which case supplemental water was provided (this occurred only for a minority of mice at the beginning of training).

Experiment 1 procedure

Starting on day 2, mice from Group 1 performed two daily 12 min-long foraging sessions (one in the morning and one in the afternoon, spaced by at least 4 hours), during which they could obtain drops of water by going from one water port to the next one. Critically, the mice had to adapt to the two following rules. First, only counterclockwise (CCW, from the camera viewpoint) turns around the towers triggered reward delivery at the arrival water port. Second, each time mice approached a tower, a random number of available drops was randomly assigned (between 4 and 12). Mice could leave a tower at any time, regardless of whether it was fully or partially depleted, or even without collecting a single reward. After leaving a tower, rewards could only be obtained at any of the remaining three towers. The previously depleted tower was replenished only if the mouse performed at least one rewarded or unrewarded transition between two contiguous trapezes in one of the other three towers. The rewarded direction was maintained for 6 days (i.e., 12 CCW sessions) after which it was reversed to clockwise (CW) for three days (i.e., 6 CW sessions).

Experiment 2 procedure

Starting on day 2, mice from Group 2 performed two daily 12-minute foraging sessions (one in the morning and one in the afternoon, spaced by at least 4 hours). On day 2, mice from Group 2 could obtain an unlimited number of rewards by performing either clockwise (CW) or counterclockwise (CCW) QTs, with both directions rewarded during both sessions. From day 3, the rewarded direction was fixed for the day and alternated each day (e.g., if CCW was rewarded on day 3, CW was rewarded on day 4, and so on). From day 5 to day 10, the maximum number of rewards available per tower visit was randomly set between 4 and 12, as in Experiment 1. From day 11 to day 17, as part of a separate study, mice followed the same foraging protocol (two daily sessions with the rewarded direction changing each morning) but received an intraperitoneal injection of clozapine-N-oxide (CNO, 1 mg/kg) 30 minutes before the morning sessions of day 11 and day 16 (to perturbate the activity of striatal projection neurons). Data from days 11 to 17 are not included in this study, but the behavioral perturbations induced by CNO, which will be reported in a separate article, were fully reversed by the afternoon sessions. Indeed, comparing the main behavioral parameters (preference for rewarded vs non rewarded QTs, number QTs, speed of QTs, variability of QTs) the day before the first CNO injection (Figure 8, lasts 2 sessions) versus the day after the second injection (Figure 9, first 2 sessions) indicated very similar performance. Although we cannot exclude the possibility that these chemogenetic manipulations affected covert aspects of behavior, using the same animals across multiple protocols (rather than training an additional group of animals) satisfies ethical recommendations to limit the number of animals.

Experiment 3 procedure

After adapting to the directional reversal (once for Group 1 or across several days for Group 2), mice were challenged in a protocol in which there was a unique rewarded direction (CW or CCW) and rewarded tower (northwest, northeast, southeast, or southwest) that were randomly reassigned each time a mouse left a fully depleted tower. The maximum number of rewards per tower visit was set as in Experiments 1 and 2 (i.e., randomly between 4 and 12). This procedure began on day 11 for Group 1 and day 18 for Group 2, lasting 3 and 4 days, respectively. We only report the results from the three first days for Group 2 so that comparison can be made with Group 1.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data processing

Behavioral data were saved as CSV files generated by the acquisition software (see above). Three primary data sources were used: (1) time-stamped x/y coordinates of the animal’s centroid, (2) information each time the animal just crossed the boundary between two adjacent trapezes (e.g., time of this crossing, cardinal identity of the tower [NW, NE, SW, or SE], cardinal identity of the trapezes where the animal is [N, E, S, or W], cardinal identity of the trapezes where the animal was on the previous video frame, information related to whether this trapeze switch will deliver a reward) and (3) session metadata. The first 15 seconds of each session were excluded to remove tracking inaccuracies due to background subtraction initialization. Additionally, positional data were filtered to retain only values within a 1–500 pixel range, discarding outliers. Spatial coordinates were converted from pixels to centimeters using a scaling factor derived from the physical dimensions of the arena. Positions were smoothed using a 1D Gaussian filter (σ = 1), and these smoothed trajectories were used to compute distances, instantaneous speeds, and angular velocities (in degrees per second).

Running epochs were automatically identified based on a speed threshold (> 7 cm/s), with a minimum duration of 0.3 s and separated when speed was below the threshold for at least 0.1s. To refine the temporal boundaries of each running epoch, the initial and final time points were further adjusted based on local acceleration dynamics. Specifically, for the onset of each run, the algorithm traced backward from the speed threshold-crossing point to identify the earliest time point at which acceleration dropped below 10% of the acceleration observed at the threshold. This allowed inclusion of pre-threshold data points that approximated better movement initiation. A similar procedure was applied at the end of the run, tracing forward from the point where speed dropped below the threshold to detect when deceleration fell below 10% of the prior peak, providing a better approximation of the moment when locomotor activity ceased. This correction ensured that each epoch more accurately reflected the full extent of runs rather than relying solely on fixed speed thresholds. Our definition of runs does not distinguish locomotion type, though videos showed that mice primarily walked.

Each running epoch was classified based on the animal’s position at the beginning and end of the detected run. Runs were categorized as exploratory runs, approaches toward a tower, runs between towers (RBTs), or runs around a tower. In this study, we focused on RBTs and runs around a tower. RBTs were defined as runs starting and ending in trapezes located around two different towers (i.e., when mice run from one water port of a given tower to a water port located around a different tower). Runs around a tower were defined as runs starting and ending in trapezes located around the same tower, with the mice crossing at least one border between two contiguous trapezes. Almost all runs around a tower consisted of QTs, in which mice moved from one water port to the next (either in a clockwise or counterclockwise direction). A very small number of runs around a tower corresponded to half-turns (e.g., a run from the south water port to the north water port of a given tower without stopping at the east or west port). In extremely rare cases, mice started and finished their run around the same tower but after a long detour. To avoid comparing heterogeneous turns around a tower, we restricted our analysis to QTs. Practically, QTs were runs around a tower during which mice performed only one trapeze switch and covered a distance shorter than 30 cm.

Foraging analyses

To quantify the extent of mice engagement in reward collection (Figure 2; Figure S7), we computed their time spent within different zones of the arena (near the peripheral walls vs in the trapezes surrounding the towers). To quantify how much mice exploited a given tower, we then defined a visit as a sequence of trapeze transitions occurring around a same tower and counting at least one QT (Figure 5; Figures S4, S15, and S16). We separated visits when a run started out of a trapeze or from another trapeze than the one the last QT ended in, or when the previous run was not a QT. A visit was rewarded if at least one of its QTs was rewarded. A visit was unrewarded when none of its QTs was rewarded. The beginning of a visit is defined as the time at which the first transition between two trapezes occurs. For each visit, we computed the number of trapeze transitions, the number of rewards obtained, the maximum number of rewards available and the starting time of the visit. We discarded the last trapeze transition of each visit when it occurs after the last QT, since the transition happens when the mouse is leaving the tower surroundings (e.g., performing a RBT), thus it does not correspond to an exploitation oriented movement. We define the exploitation score for a given mouse and a given session as the average number of trapeze transitions that occurred in a visit. For each visit, the relative exploitation score is calculated as the number of trapeze transitions minus the maximum number of rewards available.

Kinematic analyses

Basic kinematic parameters (duration, distance traveled, mean and peak speed) were extracted for all runs (Figures 3, 4, 6, 8, and 9; Figures S1, S2, S5, S9, S10, S17, and S18). For QTs, additional features were extracted: direction (CW or CCW), and reward status (rewarded or not) (Figures 3, 6, 7, 8, and 9; Figures S1, S2, S5, S6, S8, S9, and S11–S14). Concerning Group 2 mice (those for which the rewarded direction alternated every day), most of the animals started with CW QTs being rewarded on day 3 while a subgroup started with CCW QTs being rewarded. To examine whether, independently from the rewarded direction on day 3, all these mice updated their preference for rewarded and unrewarded QTs across sessions (Figure 8), we arbitrarily relabeled CCW QTs as CW in the small subgroup for which CCW QTs were rewarded on day 3.

To evaluate the similarity between QT trajectories, we used the Fréchet distance, which quantifies the similarity between two trajectories while preserving the ordering of points along them. Intuitively, it is the minimal possible maximal distance between two points on the trajectories when both are traversed from start to end, allowing variable speeds but preserving their order. One intuitive way to understand the Fréchet distance is the “human and dog” analogy. Imagine a person walking along one curve and a dog walking along the other. Both can vary their speed but cannot go backward. The Fréchet distance (between the trajectory of the dog and the man, or two QTs in our case) is the minimum leash length required for them to walk together from start to end among every possible travel speeds (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fréchet_distance). This distance is low for trajectories that are both close and similar in their shape, and high if they are different or distant. Fréchet distance was computed across all possible pairs of QTs performed during a given session, separately for CW and CCW QTs (Figures 3, 6, 8, and 9; Figures S1, S5, and S9). This involved translating and rotating trajectories relative to a single point (a single corner of a single tower). First, the coordinates of the positions forming each QT (initially expressed in the arena reference frame) are transformed to be expressed relative to the specific tower corner around which the QT occurs. This step ensures that all QTs share a common reference point. Then, QT trajectories around the SW, NW and NE corners are rotated CCW by 90, 180 and 270 degrees, respectively. Thus, all the CW and CCW QTs performed during a session around different corners of different towers became aligned, allowing the quantification of Fréchet distances between pairs of CCW QTs and pairs of CW QTs. We use the python library similaritymeasures (Jekel et al. 2019), which allows to compute Fréchet distances for trajectories with different numbers of points. We thus did not need to resample the QTs trajectories. We use the median of all the Fréchet distances computed from all the pairs of CW and CCW QTs as measure of the variability in their trajectory.

Statistical analyses

In all figures displaying behavioral metrics across sessions (or days) and mice, session-wise individual animal data are shown as thin colored lines, the group median as a thick line, and the interquartile range as error bars (Figures 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, and 9; Figures S1, S3, S4, S7, S8, S10, S13, and S15–S18). The number of mice in each group is indicated either in the figure panels or in the corresponding legends (Group 1: n = 19; Group 2: n = 32). Illustrative mice shown in the figures were randomly selected. Sessions were selected to illustrate three representative stages of training: early sessions (before stable learning), intermediate sessions (during learning), and late sessions (after performance had stabilized).

To assess whether a given behavioral metric exhibited a significant increase or decrease across sessions at the group level, we used a non-parametric permutation-based approach. For each metric and each mouse, we obtained a session-wise time series as described in the previous paragraph. We then computed the median across animals for each session, yielding a group-median time series. Monotonic changes across sessions and animals were quantified using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) between session number and the group-median metric as the observed statistic. Statistical significance was assessed via permutation testing, by permuting median values between sessions. Under the null hypothesis that session order does not influence the metric, session labels were independently permuted within each animal, thereby preserving each mouse’s distribution of values while disrupting any consistent relationship with session number (in the case of Figure S8 the same procedure is applied to days instead of sessions). For each permutation, the group-median time series and its corresponding Spearman correlation coefficient were recomputed. This procedure was repeated 1000 times to generate a null distribution of correlation coefficients (ρperm). The observed correlation coefficient (ρobs) was compared to the null distribution using either a one-sided or two-sided test, depending on the directional hypothesis. The p-value was calculated as the proportion of permuted correlation coefficients that were as extreme as or more extreme than ρobs. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

To assess the statistical significance of changes in behavioral performance across mice between pairs of sessions (Figure 9A; Figures S5, S6A, S9A, S9C, S9E, S9G, and S14A left, S19AC), we used a permutation-based paired comparison. For each metric and each pair of sessions, we computed the difference between the group median values of the two sessions, yielding an observed change for each pair. Statistical significance was assessed using a paired permutation procedure. Under the null hypothesis that there is no systematic variation of the metric between the two sessions of a pair, session labels are exchangeable within each animal. For each permutation, the session labels were randomly swapped within animals and the group-level median difference was recomputed. This procedure preserves the within-animal pairing and the distribution of each animal’s data. The permutation was repeated 1000 times to generate a null distribution of group-level median differences. The p-value was calculated as the proportion of permuted differences that were as extreme as or more extreme than the observed difference (one-sided or two-sided depending on the hypothesis). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

To compare the magnitudes of metric changes between two session pairs (Figure S6B, S9B, S9D, S9F, S9H, S14B, S19B, and S19D), we employed a permutation-based method. For each mouse and metric, we first calculated change scores for each session pair; first minus second session for Figure S6, first divided by second session for Figures S9 and S19, and first minus second normalized by first session difference for Figure S6; then computed medians for both pairs to quantify variation per animal. At the group level, the observed difference was determined by subtracting the median variation of the second session pair from that of the first. Under the null hypothesis that there is no systematic variation difference between session pairs, permutations were used to assess significance. Session pairs were paired within each animal when comparing same-group mice, but unpaired when comparing different groups. During permutation, labels were randomly reassigned while preserving the number of animals per pair. After labels permutation, group-level medians difference was recomputed, and this process was repeated 1000 times to generate a null distribution. The p-value was calculated as the proportion of permuted differences equal to or more extreme than the observed difference (one-sided or two-sided), with significance defined as p < 0.05.

To compare session-dependent changes in CW and CCW kinematics while controlling for unequal numbers of trajectories (Figure S1), we used a combined downsampling and permutation procedure. For each mouse and session, analyses were restricted to sessions containing at least five CW and five CCW trajectories. Within these sessions, CCW trajectories were randomly downsampled to match the number of CW trajectories. Kinematic metrics (either median Fréchet distance or median peak speed) were then computed separately for CW trajectories and downsampled CCW trajectories for each mouse and session. Then, we first computed, for each session, the median kinematic metric across mice separately for CW trajectories and downsampled CCW trajectories. Session-dependent changes were then summarized by computing the Spearman rank correlation coefficient (ρ) of sessions number with the session-wise group median, restricting the analysis to sessions from the third session onward. This yielded one ρ value for CW trajectories and one ρ value for downsampled CCW trajectories. The observed group-level statistic was defined as the difference between these two correlation coefficients (Δρ = ρCCW − ρCW). To assess statistical significance, we performed a permutation test that preserved within-animal structure. For each permutation, and for each downsampling realization, CW and CCW labels were randomly swapped within each mouse with equal probability, and session-wise group medians were recomputed to estimate Δρ. This procedure was repeated 1000 times to create a null distribution, each time using an independent random downsampling of CCW trajectories. Statistical significance was assessed by comparing the observed Δρ to the null distribution of permuted Δρ values, with the p-value defined as the proportion of permutations in which the permuted Δρ was greater than or equal to the observed Δρ and significance as when p < 0.05.

In Figures S13A–S13C top and S16B, to quantify the difference between Group 1 and 2 session-wise on several metrics, we used a permutation test on the group medians difference. We first computed a null distribution of the median difference for each session by randomly permuting the group labels 1000 times. We then pulled the null distributions of the two pre-change sessions together and the six post-change sessions together, creating two global null distributions with the null hypothesis that metric values from group 1 and 2 come from the same distribution in all sessions. Observed medians difference of each session was considered significantly different from its corresponding global null distribution if it was out of the 2.5% - 97.5% confidence interval (this corresponds to a two-sided test with a significance threshold of p < 0.05). By using global null distributions, we avoid multiple comparisons problems that can arise from testing several sessions by having a significance criterion accounting for the number of sessions studied.

To assess within-session evolution in turn speed, we focused on CCW turn speeds (Figure S2A). For each session containing at least 100 CCW QTs, we computed the slope of the linear regression between trial rank (i.e., position within the session) and maximal turn speed. A one-sided permutation test was performed to assess whether the observed slope was significantly more negative than expected under the null hypothesis of no temporal structure. At each iteration (n = 1000), CCW turn speeds were randomly shuffled, and the regression slope was recomputed to generate a null distribution. The p-value was calculated as the proportion of permuted slopes less than or equal to the observed slope. The evolution of slope values across sessions is shown for each mouse (Figure S2B). Sessions with significant negative slopes (p < 0.05) are marked with circles, non-significant ones with crosses. The same analysis was applied to the Pearson correlation coefficient ρ between trial rank and turn speed, highlighting sessions with significant effects (Figure S3C). For each mouse, we recorded the number of sessions showing a significant negative slope (p < 0.05), summarizing the prevalence of within-session effects at the individual level (Figure S3D).

In Figure 5C, we quantified tower preference by comparing the time mice spent around each tower to the chance level of 0.25 (equal time at each of the 4 towers). For each mouse, tower and session, we computed the proportion of time spent near that tower, resulting in a distribution of ratios. To assess whether mice preferred certain towers, we compared the median of each distribution to the chance level using a one-sample Wilcoxon test (after subtracting 0.25 from each value), with a significance criterion of p < 0.05.

FAIR principles

Our code and data comply with the Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reusability (FAIR) principles.

(F): Upon publication, our data and codes to generate the figures will be deposited in stable public repositories (see resource availability). The data are extensively described in a dedicated file on OSF.

(A): Both the data and code are open-source.

(I): The code is interoperable, as it was developed exclusively in Python 3.12 and can therefore be used on any computer and operating system that supports this version. Data files follow a clear nomenclature, and the code uses explicit variable names and is extensively commented, which facilitates reuse. Applying the code to new datasets requires minimal manual intervention.

(R): The data and code are free to use provided that this article is cited. The origins of the data sets are clearly stated.
