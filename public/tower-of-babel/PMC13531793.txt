Hesperidin attenuates gentamicin-induced testicular toxicity via modulation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway
Authors: Murat Celebi, Cagla Celebi, Hasan Susar, Ibrahim Halil Gungor, Ozhan Karatas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGentamicin is an aminoglycoside antibiotic widely used in the treatment of Gram-negative infections; however, one of its major limitations is testicular toxicity. Oxidative stress is considered to play a central role in gentamicin-induced testicular damage. Hesperidin is a natural flavonoid with potent antioxidant and cytoprotective properties and may exert protective effects on the reproductive system.MethodsThis study investigated the protective effects of hesperidin against gentamicin-induced testicular damage. Histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluations were performed alongside biochemical and hormonal analyses. In addition, sperm concentration, motility, and the rate of abnormal spermatozoa were assessed. To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, the gene expression levels of PD-1, PI3K, and AKT-1 were analyzed.ResultsGentamicin administration was associated with lower Johnsen scores, decreased testosterone levels and sperm motility, increased sperm abnormalities, elevated MDA concentrations, reduced CAT, SOD, GPx, and GSH levels, increased Caspase-3 and 8-OHdG immunoreactivity, and reduced AKT expression. Hesperidin co-administration significantly attenuated many of these alterations, being associated with improved histopathological scores, enhanced antioxidant status, reduced immunoreactivity of oxidative stress- and apoptosis-related markers, improved sperm quality parameters, and increased PI3K and AKT gene expression compared with the gentamicin-treated group.ConclusionThese findings indicate that hesperidin possesses a protective potential against gentamicin-induced testicular damage, likely mediated through its antioxidant and anti-apoptotic effects.Keywords: Gentamicin, Hesperidin, Testicular toxicity, Oxidative stress, Apoptosis, PI3K/AKT pathway

Introduction

Aminoglycosides are a well-established class of antibiotics widely used for the treatment of infections caused by Gram-negative bacteria. Among them, gentamicin (GEN) remains one of the most frequently prescribed agents owing to its broad bactericidal spectrum, cost-effectiveness, and efficacy against β-lactam–resistant microorganisms. However, the clinical use of gentamicin is significantly limited by its adverse effects, most notably nephrotoxicity, as well as ototoxicity and neuromuscular blockade. It has been reported that approximately 10–25% of patients receiving gentamicin develop reversible moderate renal dysfunction [1, 2].

While gentamicin-induced nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity have been extensively investigated, its effects on non-target tissues remain comparatively underexplored. The male reproductive system, particularly the testes, represents a critical yet insufficiently studied target, despite its essential role in fertility. Although gentamicin has been suggested to exhibit limited accumulation within the testes due to poor permeability across the blood–testis barrier, accumulating evidence indicates that gentamicin exposure may impair sperm motility, reduce reproductive organ weight, and induce testicular apoptosis, ultimately contributing to testicular dysfunction and infertility. Nevertheless, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying gentamicin-induced testicular toxicity remain incompletely understood. Current evidence suggests that oxidative stress plays a central role in gentamicin-induced testicular damage. Increased generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), lipid peroxidation, inhibition of germ cell proliferation and protein synthesis, and oxidative stress–mediated cellular injury have been identified as key contributors to testicular dysfunction [3, 4]. Testicular tissue and spermatozoa are particularly susceptible to ROS-mediated damage due to their high content of polyunsaturated fatty acids, which compromises membrane integrity and leads to impaired sperm motility and viability [5, 6]. These findings highlight the urgent need for effective therapeutic strategies capable of mitigating gentamicin-induced oxidative damage in the male reproductive system.

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward naturally derived compounds with antioxidant and cytoprotective properties. Among plant-derived flavonoids, hesperidin (Hes), a predominant flavanone glycoside abundant in citrus fruits, has been extensively investigated for its biological effects. Following oral administration, Hes undergoes intestinal and hepatic metabolism and is primarily excreted via urine [7]. Hes is particularly notable for its potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. The diverse biological and pharmacological activities of Hes are schematically illustrated in Fig. 1. Previous experimental studies have demonstrated that Hes alleviates oxidative stress by scavenging ROS, interrupting oxidative chain reactions, and protecting lipids, proteins, and DNA from oxidative damage, thereby strengthening cellular antioxidant defenses [8, 9].

Biological and pharmacological properties of hesperidin

In the context of male reproductive health, Hes has been shown to exert protective effects in experimental models of testicular toxicity induced by various chemical and pharmacological agents. These studies report that Hes reduces oxidative stress markers (MDA) while enhancing antioxidant defenses (CAT, SOD, GSH), improves histopathological alterations, and restores steroidogenic enzymes and reproductive hormones (FSH, LH, and testosterone) [10, 11]. In addition, Hes has been demonstrated to suppress apoptosis in testicular tissue by modulating key apoptotic markers, including Caspase-3, Bax, and Bcl-2, thereby contributing to the preservation of spermatogenic integrity [12, 13]. Importantly, recent evidence indicates that Hes may exert its protective effects through modulation of intracellular signaling pathways involved in cell survival. In particular, Hes has been reported to restore PI3K and AKT signaling, suppress caspase-mediated apoptosis, and improve steroidogenic function in models of drug-induced testicular toxicity. These findings suggest that Hes may directly target molecular pathways regulating testicular cell survival. Given that suppression of the PI3K/AKT pathway is closely associated with oxidative stress–induced apoptosis in testicular tissue, restoration of this pathway represents a key therapeutic target. Furthermore, considering that PI3K/AKT signaling plays a critical role in maintaining cellular integrity and survival in both male and female gonadal tissues, these findings provide additional mechanistic support for the potential protective effects of Hes in testicular toxicity models [14, 15].

However, despite accumulating evidence supporting the protective effects of hesperidin in various models of testicular injury, its role in gentamicin-induced testicular toxicity has not yet been elucidated. Therefore, the present study was designed to investigate the protective effects of hesperidin against gentamicin-induced testicular toxicity, with a particular focus on its potential to modulate oxidative stress, apoptosis, and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. To this end, histopathological, immunohistochemical, biochemical, and hormonal evaluations were performed in blood and testicular tissues. In addition, sperm concentration, motility, and abnormal spermatozoa rates were assessed, and the expression levels of PD-1, PI3K, and AKT-1 genes were analyzed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed effects.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental procedure

This study adhered to the ethical guidelines set forth in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals endorsed by the National Institutes of Health. Animal experiments complied with the Animals Scientific Procedures Act, UK of 1986 and EU Directive 2010/63/EU for animal experiments. In this study, a total of 28 male Wistar albino rats, aged 10–12 weeks and weighing 300–350 g, were used. The animals were obtained from the Balıkesir University Experimental Animal Research and Application Center (Balıkesir, Türkiye). The sample size was determined based on previous experimental studies investigating gentamicin-induced toxicity and related interventions, in which similar group sizes (n = 6–8) were sufficient to detect statistically significant differences [16]. The rats were housed under standard laboratory conditions at a constant temperature of 25 ± 2 °C, 60–65% humidity, and a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. Animals were provided with commercial rat chow, and water was supplied ad libitum. Prior to the initiation of the experimental treatments, all animals were allowed to acclimatize to the environment and the researcher for one week. Following the acclimatization period, animals were randomly assigned to the control and treatment groups using a computer-generated randomization schedule to minimize selection bias. The experimental protocol is presented in Fig. 2.

Overview of the experimental design and treatment protocol

The animals were randomly allocated into four experimental groups, each consisting of seven rats, as shown in Table 1. Rats in the control group (C, n = 7) received 0.5 mL/kg physiological saline. Rats in the GEN group (n = 7) were administered gentamicin at a dose of 100 mg/kg, dissolved in saline, via the intraperitoneal (IP) route (Sigma-Aldrich). Rats in the Hes group (n = 7) received hesperidin at a dose of 100 mg/kg/day, dissolved in saline, via oral gavage (Sigma-Aldrich). In the GEN + Hes group, rats were treated sequentially with gentamicin (100 mg/kg/day, IP) and hesperidin (100 mg/kg/day, oral gavage). The doses used in this study were selected based on previous studies [4, 17]. In addition, all treatment solutions were freshly prepared on a daily basis.

Anesthesia, euthanasia and sample collection

This study was approved by the Balıkesir University Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments (Approval date: 26 June 2025; Decision No: 2025/6 − 3). At the end of the experimental period, general anesthesia was induced using a ketamine/xylazine combination (ketamine 90 mg/kg and xylazine 10 mg/kg, IP) [18]. The depth of anesthesia was confirmed by the loss of the pedal withdrawal reflex. Under deep anesthesia, a midline incision was performed, and terminal exsanguination was carried out via intracardiac puncture, allowing the collection of an adequate volume of blood for biochemical analyses. Although exsanguination is not accepted as a sole method of euthanasia, it may be performed as a terminal procedure only in unconscious or deeply anesthetized animals, particularly when a large volume of blood is required. All procedures were conducted in accordance with animal welfare principles, the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals [19], and Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Union. Body weights of the animals were recorded on day 0 and on the day of termination. Following euthanasia, blood samples collected for biochemical analyses were allowed to clot, then centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to separate the serum. The obtained serum samples were transferred into Eppendorf tubes and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Animals were rapidly dissected through a midline incision in the lower abdominal region, and both testes and epididymides were excised. The testes were carefully cleared of surrounding fat and connective tissues, gently blotted to remove excess surface fluid, and weighed individually using a precision analytical balance. The testicular mass index (TMI) was calculated using the following formula (Testicular weight / Body weight) × 100 [20]. Subsequently, the testes were sectioned transversely; the same half of each testis was allocated for histopathological evaluation, while the remaining halves were prepared for biochemical analyses and stored at − 80 °C until further processing.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses (Caspase-3 and 8-OhdG)

Histopathological analyses

Testicular tissues collected from necropsied rats were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. Following routine alcohol–xylene processing, the samples were embedded in paraffin blocks. Sections of 4 μm thickness were mounted on poly-L-lysine–coated slides and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Histopathological alterations observed in the tissues were evaluated under a light microscope according to the Johnsen testicular scoring system [21].

Immunohistochemical evaluation

Sections (4 μm) mounted on poly-L-lysine–coated slides were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded alcohol series. After washing in PBS, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation in 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was performed using an antigen retrieval solution (2 × 5 min at 500 W). Following PBS washes, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against Caspase-3 (Affbiotech, Cat. No. AF6311, 1:200 dilution) and 8-OHdG (Bioss, Cat. No. bs-1278R, 1:200 dilution) at + 4 °C for 12 h.

As the secondary detection system, a Large Volume Detection System: anti-Polyvalent, HRP (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. TP-125-HL) was applied according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Immunoreactivity was visualized using DAB (3,3′-diaminobenzidine) as the chromogen. Sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin, mounted with Entellan, and examined under a light microscope. Immunopositivity in testicular tissues was semi-quantitatively scored as absent (0), mild (1), moderate (2), or severe (3), [22]. Histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluations were performed by an experienced pathologist blinded to the experimental groups.

Biochemical analyses (CAT, MDA, SOD, GPx, GSH and Testosterone, LH, FSH)

Half of one testis obtained from each rat was homogenized in ice-cold phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) using a glass–Teflon homogenizer. The resulting homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the obtained supernatants were used as the enzyme source for biochemical analyses. Oxidative stress and antioxidant defense parameters were evaluated in testicular tissues, while reproductive hormone levels were determined in serum samples. Levels of catalase (CAT; Cat. No: E0869Ra), malondialdehyde (MDA; Cat. No: E0156Ra), superoxide dismutase (SOD; Cat. No: E0168Ra), reduced glutathione (GSH; Cat. No: EA0113Ra), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx; Cat. No: E1172Ra), as well as testosterone (TES; Cat. No: EA0023Ra), luteinizing hormone (LH; Cat. No: EA0013Ra), and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH; Cat. No: EA0015Ra), were measured using commercially available ELISA kits from Bioassay Technology Laboratory (Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Accordingly, enzyme-related results were expressed as concentration values provided by the manufacturer’s ELISA system rather than conventional enzymatic activity units. For all biochemical measurements, the optical density of the assay plates was read at 450 nm using a Chromate 4300 microplate reader (Awareness Technology, Inc., Palm City, FL, USA). Results were expressed as ng/mL for CAT, SOD, and GPx, nmol/mL for MDA, mg/L for GSH, and mIU/mL for the hormones. The assay sensitivities were 0.52 ng/mL for CAT, 0.01 nmol/mL for MDA, 0.022 ng/mL for SOD, 10.25 mg/L for GSH, 0.23 ng/mL for GPx, 0.043 ng/mL for testosterone, 0.31 mIU/mL for LH, and 0.022 mIU/mL for FSH.

Spermatological analyses

Sperm concentration was determined using the right epididymis. Following the determination of epididymal weight, the tissue was minced and gently compressed in 1 mL of 0.9% NaCl solution for 2 min to allow the release of spermatozoa into the medium. The resulting suspension was diluted at a ratio of 1:200 using 1% eosin solution, and spermatozoa were counted in both chambers of a Neubauer hemocytometer under ×200 magnification. Sperm concentration was calculated accordingly. Sperm motility was assessed using a suspension prepared from the left cauda epididymis, which was gently homogenized in Tris buffer solution. A 5–10 µL aliquot of the suspension was placed onto a pre-warmed glass slide on a heated stage. Motility evaluation was performed by examining three randomly selected microscopic fields within a single drop, and the average of the three fields was recorded as the percentage of motile spermatozoa. To determine the percentage of abnormal spermatozoa, Eosin–Nigrosin staining was applied to the Tris buffer–sperm suspension used for motility assessment. Thin smears were prepared, air-dried, and examined under ×400 magnification. A total of 200 sperm cells per slide were evaluated, and the proportion of morphologically abnormal spermatozoa was expressed as a percentage [23, 24].

Gene expression analysis (RT-PCR)

In this study, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis was performed to evaluate changes in the mRNA expression levels of PD-1, PI3K, and AKT1 genes in all experimental groups. Total RNA was isolated from testicular tissues using the Tissue RNA Purification Kit (Cat No: I04-01-10, 100 preps, A.B.T.™ Laboratories, Turkey) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically, and samples with acceptable purity were used for subsequent analyses. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA using a reverse transcription protocol, and gene expression analyses were subsequently conducted. To normalize gene expression levels, β-actin was used as the internal control (housekeeping) gene. RT-qPCR reactions were prepared in 96-well plates under the reaction conditions specified in sealed with a transparent adhesive film (Applied Biosystems™ 96-Well Reaction Plate Seal), and loaded into a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. Primer sequences were designed and verified using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST), and the forward and reverse primer sequences are presented in Table 2. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

No animals, experimental units, or data points were excluded from the study. All data obtained from the experiments were included in the final statistical analysis without omission. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.1 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Prior to comparative analyses, data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. For datasets that met the assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison post hoc test. When the assumptions for parametric testing were not satisfied, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. All experimental results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05. In tables and figures, statistically significant differences among groups are indicated by different superscript letters within the same row or above bars. All graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism software, and figures were prepared as publication-quality bar graphs displaying mean ± SD values, with consistent color coding and patterning across experimental groups.

Biochemical findings

Serum testosterone levels differed significantly among the experimental groups (p < 0.001). Gentamicin administration caused a marked reduction in testosterone concentrations compared with the control group.

Hesperidin treatment alone partially preserved testosterone levels, while concomitant administration of hesperidin with gentamicin significantly attenuated the gentamicin-induced decrease; however, testosterone levels in the GEN + Hes group remained lower than those of the control group. Significant differences were also observed in LH levels among groups (p = 0.0057). Gentamicin treatment resulted in elevated LH concentrations relative to the control group, whereas hesperidin co-administration moderated this increase. LH levels in the GEN + Hes group were intermediate between the gentamicin and control groups. In contrast, FSH levels did not differ significantly among the groups (p = 0.170), indicating that neither gentamicin nor hesperidin treatment had a statistically significant effect on FSH concentrations under the present experimental conditions. Spermatological findings

Significant differences were observed among the experimental groups with respect to sperm motility (p = 0.018). Gentamicin administration resulted in a marked reduction in sperm motility compared with the control group.

Hesperidin treatment alone preserved sperm motility at levels comparable to controls, while concomitant administration of hesperidin with gentamicin partially attenuated the gentamicin-induced decrease; however, motility values in the GEN + Hes group remained lower than those of the control group. Sperm concentration did not differ significantly among groups (p = 0.279), indicating that neither gentamicin nor hesperidin treatment exerted a statistically significant effect on sperm count under the present experimental conditions. In contrast, sperm abnormality rates differed significantly among groups (p = 0.001). Gentamicin treatment significantly increased the percentage of abnormal spermatozoa compared with the control and hesperidin groups. Co-administration of hesperidin significantly reduced the gentamicin-induced increase in sperm abnormalities, although values did not fully return to control levels (Table 5).

Gene expression results

Significant differences were detected among the experimental groups for all analyzed genes (p < 0.001). Relative AKT expression was markedly reduced in the gentamicin-treated group compared with the control group. In contrast, co-administration of hesperidin with gentamicin resulted in a pronounced upregulation of AKT expression, with levels significantly higher than those observed in all other groups. A similar pattern was observed for PI3K expression. While gentamicin or hesperidin treatment alone did not induce a significant change relative to the control group, combined treatment with hesperidin and gentamicin significantly increased PI3K expression, yielding the highest levels among all experimental groups. Regarding PD-1, no significant differences were observed among the control, gentamicin, and hesperidin groups, whereas PD-1 expression was markedly and significantly elevated in the GEN + Hes group compared with all other groups, indicating a synergistic effect of combined treatment on PD-1 regulation (Table 7).

Discussion

The present study provides comprehensive evidence that gentamicin induces marked testicular toxicity in male Wistar rats and that hesperidin exerts a significant protective effect against this damage through its antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, and cytoprotective properties. To our knowledge, this is one of the limited experimental studies evaluating the protective role of hesperidin specifically against gentamicin-induced testicular injury, integrating histopathological, biochemical, spermatological, hormonal, and molecular endpoints. In the current study, gentamicin administration resulted in a significant reduction in gonadosomatic index and epididymal cauda weight, indicating impairment of testicular development and reproductive organ integrity. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting decreased testis and epididymal weights following gentamicin exposure, which reflect disrupted spermatogenesis and androgen deficiency [6, 25, 26]. Although body weight was not significantly affected, the selective reduction in reproductive organ mass suggests that gentamicin preferentially targets the male reproductive system, likely due to its high metabolic activity and susceptibility to oxidative stress. At the hormonal level, gentamicin caused a pronounced decline in serum testosterone concentrations, accompanied by an increase in LH levels, while FSH remained unchanged. The reduction in testosterone may be attributed to direct Leydig cell dysfunction or impaired steroidogenic enzyme activity induced by oxidative damage. The compensatory elevation in LH observed in the gentamicin group likely reflects negative feedback regulation at the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis. Similar hormonal alterations have been reported in previous gentamicin models, where decreased testosterone and dysregulated gonadotropin levels were associated with impaired spermatogenesis and reduced fertility potential [27, 28]. Importantly, hesperidin co-administration partially restored testosterone levels and normalized LH concentrations, suggesting a protective effect on Leydig cell function and endocrine homeostasis.

Oxidative stress appears to represent a central mechanism underlying gentamicin-induced testicular toxicity. In the present study, gentamicin significantly increased testicular MDA levels, while markedly decreasing the activities of major antioxidant enzymes, including CAT, SOD, GPx, and GSH. These findings confirm that gentamicin disrupts redox balance in testicular tissue, leading to lipid peroxidation and antioxidant depletion.

Similar redox disturbances have been consistently reported in previous studies, supporting the hypothesis that excessive ROS generation plays a key role in gentamicin-mediated germ cell injury [6, 25]. Testicular tissue and spermatozoa are particularly vulnerable to oxidative stress due to their high content of polyunsaturated fatty acids, which renders membrane structures highly susceptible to peroxidative damage.

Notably, hesperidin treatment effectively counteracted gentamicin-induced oxidative damage by significantly reducing MDA levels and restoring antioxidant enzyme activities toward control values. These results are in line with previous studies demonstrating that hesperidin enhances endogenous antioxidant defense systems and scavenges ROS in various models of testicular toxicity [11, 29]. The ability of hesperidin to restore CAT, SOD, GPx, and GSH levels indicates that its protective effect is mediated, at least in part, through reinforcement of the intrinsic antioxidant network. Importantly, hesperidin administration alone did not produce marked adverse alterations in the evaluated reproductive, biochemical, or histopathological parameters under the present experimental conditions. However, the long-term reproductive safety profile and potential dose-dependent effects of hesperidin require further investigation in dedicated toxicological studies.

The spermatological findings further support the protective role of hesperidin. Gentamicin significantly reduced sperm motility and increased the proportion of abnormal spermatozoa, reflecting impaired sperm maturation and structural integrity. Although sperm concentration was not significantly altered, the decline in motility and increase in morphological abnormalities suggest functional infertility rather than quantitative spermatogenic failure. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that gentamicin primarily affects sperm quality rather than sperm count [27, 30]. Hesperidin co-treatment significantly improved sperm motility and reduced abnormal sperm rates, indicating preservation of sperm membrane integrity and mitochondrial function.

At the molecular level, gentamicin markedly suppressed AKT expression, suggesting inhibition of survival and proliferation signaling pathways in testicular tissue. The PI3K/AKT pathway plays a critical role in regulating germ cell survival, spermatogonial proliferation, and resistance to apoptosis. Downregulation of AKT following gentamicin exposure implies increased susceptibility of germ cells to apoptosis and oxidative injury.

Interestingly, hesperidin co-administration resulted in a strong upregulation of both PI3K and AKT, with expression levels exceeding those of all other groups. These findings may suggest an association between hesperidin treatment and alterations in survival-related molecular signaling; however, the underlying mechanisms require further validation at the protein level. Recent experimental studies have similarly demonstrated that natural antioxidant-based interventions may attenuate testicular toxicity through modulation of oxidative stress, apoptosis-related pathways, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR-associated molecular responses. For example, Astragalus polysaccharides and curcumin-loaded nanoliposomes were reported to ameliorate chemically induced testicular damage while improving redox balance and apoptosis-related alterations in rat models [31, 32]. Similarly, recent evidence has shown that antioxidant-based therapeutic approaches targeting oxidative stress and survival-related molecular pathways may exert protective effects in experimental toxicological injury models, further supporting the potential role of intracellular signaling modulation in tissue protection against oxidative damage [33].

Furthermore, PD-1 expression was significantly elevated in the GEN + Hes group. Although PD-1 is primarily recognized as an immune checkpoint regulator, its potential role in testicular tissue responses under oxidative stress conditions remains insufficiently understood. Therefore, the observed increase in PD-1 expression in the combined treatment group should be considered a preliminary and exploratory finding rather than definitive evidence of a protective mechanistic response. Further mechanistic studies are required to clarify the biological significance of PD-1 modulation in gentamicin-induced testicular toxicity and hesperidin treatment. Similar molecular effects of hesperidin on PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling and apoptosis-related markers, including Caspase-3 and 8-OHdG, have been reported in other experimental models of testicular toxicity [15, 34].

Histopathological and immunohistochemical findings further corroborated the biochemical and molecular results. Gentamicin was associated with pronounced histological alterations in seminiferous tubules, accompanied by reduced seminiferous epithelial organization and increased immunoreactivity for Caspase-3 and 8-OhdG, indicating enhanced apoptosis and oxidative DNA damage. In contrast, hesperidin markedly attenuated these histopathological alterations and reduced apoptotic marker expression, preserving seminiferous tubule architecture and histological organization. These morphological improvements are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that hesperidin protects testicular tissue by suppressing oxidative stress-induced apoptosis and maintaining cellular homeostasis [11, 35]. While the present study demonstrates the pronounced protective effects of hesperidin as a single-agent intervention, future studies may explore combination strategies involving hesperidin and other antioxidants or therapeutic compounds in order to evaluate potential synergistic or additive effects on gentamicin-induced reproductive toxicity. In addition, further translational studies in human populations are warranted to clarify the clinical relevance of these findings, particularly with respect to bioavailability, pharmacokinetics, and potential drug–drug interactions.

Nevertheless, several limitations of the present study should be considered. First, the findings were obtained using an experimental rat model, which may limit direct extrapolation to human reproductive physiology.

Second, although significant alterations in PI3K/AKT-related gene expression were observed, protein-level validation methods such as Western blotting or immunofluorescence analyses were not performed. Moreover, since PI3K/AKT signaling activity is largely regulated at the post-translational level, the present findings should be interpreted as alterations in pathway-related gene expression rather than definitive evidence of pathway activation. In addition, detailed mechanistic analyses of downstream apoptotic signaling cascades and mitochondrial dysfunction were beyond the scope of the current study. Functional fertility outcomes including mating performance, fertilization capacity, or pregnancy success were also not evaluated. Finally, the relatively short experimental duration may not fully reflect the long-term reproductive consequences of gentamicin exposure or the sustained protective effects of hesperidin. Therefore, further long-term and mechanistic studies are warranted to better elucidate the molecular pathways underlying the protective effects of hesperidin in gentamicin-induced reproductive toxicity.

Collectively, the findings of the present study demonstrate that gentamicin induces testicular toxicity primarily through oxidative stress, endocrine disruption, and suppression of survival signaling pathways, which may contribute to impaired spermatogenic function and altered sperm quality. Hesperidin exerts a pronounced protective effect by restoring redox balance, improving hormonal profiles, enhancing sperm quality, and modulating PI3K/AKT-related molecular responses. These results highlight hesperidin as a promising natural therapeutic agent for mitigating antibiotic-induced reproductive toxicity and preserving male fertility.

Conclusion

In conclusion, gentamicin induces marked testicular toxicity in male rats, characterized by oxidative stress, impaired antioxidant defense, hormonal imbalance, and deterioration of sperm quality. These alterations are associated with histological alterations in seminiferous tubules and suppression of survival-related molecular responses. Hesperidin attenuated gentamicin-induced damage by restoring redox balance and improving hormonal and spermatological parameters, while also being associated with modulation of PI3K/AKT-related molecular responses linked to cytoprotective processes. Overall, the present findings suggest that hesperidin may have protective potential against antibiotic-induced reproductive toxicity in experimental settings.
