Personalized protein delivery in critical care: from phenotyping and endotyping to post-ICU recovery
Authors: Noortje Maria Petronella Overwater, Michelle Carmen Paulus, Lara Schepers, Arthur Raymond Hubert van Zanten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose of reviewThis review examines the importance of optimizing protein delivery during and in the convalescence of critical illness, with a focus on the challenges associated with individual protein dosing strategies.Recent findingsMuscle mass depletion often begins within the first days of intensive care unit (ICU) admission, emphasizing the need for adequate protein intake. However, many ICU patients fail to meet recommended targets. Large-scale studies have shown mixed results on high-protein intake, with some suggesting no added benefit or even harm in certain groups. This review highlights the need for individualized protein dosing guided by phenotyping and endotyping and discusses the challenges in assessing body composition, illustrated by a case study. The potential role of biomarkers in optimizing protein dosing is also explored, alongside factors that hinder adequate protein intake.SummaryFurther research is needed to determine the optimal protein strategy for critically ill patients, with a focus on enhancing functional outcomes and quality of life, particularly for survivors who face long-term physical and cognitive challenges. Future guidelines should incorporate individualized approaches, leveraging emerging technologies and patient-centred data to tailor protein delivery and enhance recovery outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

The rapid depletion of muscle protein during critical illness, resulting in an 18% loss of muscle mass within the first ten days of intensive care unit (ICU) admission, underscores the importance of addressing protein requirements [1]. Despite this clear need, critically ill patients often fail to meet recommended protein targets [2,3]. At the same time, the optimal protein dosage during critical illness and the subsequent recovery period remains unclear. This review examines protein dosing strategies from the acute phase through critical illness and post-ICU care, emphasizing personalized approaches using phenotyping and endotyping to optimize outcomes. A case patient example will illustrate the considerations for protein management in critically ill patients.

CURRENT GUIDELINES ON PROTEIN DOSING DURING THE ACUTE PHASE OF CRITICAL ILLNESS

Current guidelines from the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) and the American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ASPEN) emphasize early nutrition in critically ill patients and recommend a gradual increase in protein intake to avoid overfeeding [4,5]. ASPEN advises 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day, while ESPEN suggests 1.3 g/kg/day. For a 100 kg ICU patient, our case patient, we could start with 1.3 g/kg, providing 130 grams of protein daily. While these guidelines offer a foundation, recent randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have explored optimal protein dosages and their impact on clinical outcomes, offering insights into how specific protein interventions may affect recovery and long-term health.

RECENT RANDOMISED CONTROLLED TRIALS REGARDING PROTEIN DELIVERY AND INTAKE DURING CRITICAL ILLNESS

Recent large RCTs consistently show no clinical benefit of higher protein intake in critically ill patients, while several trials signal potential harm in vulnerable subgroups. In contrast, early protein restriction appears safe and may accelerate recovery.

NUTRIREA-3 (n = 3036) compared early protein restriction (0.2–0.4 g/kg/day) with standard dosing (1.0–1.3 g/kg/day) [6]. Although mortality was unchanged, the restricted-protein group had faster readiness for ICU discharge, earlier liberation from ventilation, and fewer gastrointestinal and hepatic complications, indicating that early protein restriction is not harmful and may confer short-term physiological advantages.

EFFORT Protein (n = 1301) evaluated high-dose (2.2 g/kg/day) vs. usual-dose (1.2 g/kg/day) protein [7]. High protein showed no benefit for time-to-discharge alive or mortality, but produced substantially higher urea levels. Importantly, patients with acute kidney injury (AKI) or high organ failure scores (SOFA ≥9) had signals of worse outcomes, suggesting intolerance of high exogenous amino acid loads in severe organ failure.

PRECISe (n = 935) also found no benefit of 2.0 vs. 1.3 g/kg/day protein [8▪▪]. High protein intake was associated with lower 6-month health-related quality of life (HRQoL), slower recovery, and greater gastrointestinal intolerance, with potential harm, particularly in female and medical patients, groups with typically lower fat-free mass (FFM) and potentially reduced metabolic capacity.

TARGET Protein (n = 3397) compared augmented protein delivery (1.12 g/kg/day achieved) with usual protein (0.69 g/kg/day) [9▪▪]. There was no difference in hospital-free days at day 90, but the high-protein group had higher urea concentrations and a longer ICU length of stay (LOS). Subgroup analyses suggested possible harm among mechanically ventilated patients and those receiving renal replacement therapy.

Smaller RCTs yield heterogeneous, nongeneralizable findings, and no clinical benefits [10▪,11▪]. Wang et al. (n = 173) found minor reductions in ICU LOS and mechanical ventilation [12▪], whereas Badpeyma et al. (n = 56) reported lower hospital mortality but no effect on 60-day survival [13▪]. These trials are underpowered and do not counter the neutral or harmful signals from the extensive multicentre studies.

In summary, across high-quality RCTs, higher early protein does not improve survival (Fig. 1), recovery, or HRQoL, and may be harmful in patients with impaired renal/metabolic tolerance. Early moderate protein restriction appears safe and may support faster clinical stabilization. These findings reinforce the need for individualized protein dosing (Table 1).

GUIDING OPTIMAL PROTEIN INTAKE DURING CRITICAL ILLNESS: PHENOTYPING

The ESPEN guidelines recommend tailoring protein delivery in patients with obesity, considering factors such as urinary nitrogen losses and lean body mass (LBM), as measured by computed tomography (CT) imaging [4]. LBM is considered equivalent to FFM [15], with FFM preferred nowadays and consistently used throughout this review. Determining body composition may benefit all patients, as differences in FFM can substantially influence protein requirements across the entire ICU population. For example, two patients of identical weight may have significantly different FFM, as demonstrated by the study of Moonen et al. (Fig. 2) [16]. For our 100 kg patient, the FFM is 52 kg, whereas another male patient with the same total body weight may have a FFM that is 30 kg higher. This discrepancy highlights the risk of overfeeding one patient while underfeeding another. Moreover, it may explain the primarily harmful effects observed in the PRECISe trial in females [8▪▪], who generally have a lower FFM relative to total body weight than males.

Protein dosing based on FFM is a promising approach [17], but accurately assessing FFM in critically ill patients remains challenging. CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are considered the most accurate methods for assessing FFM, particularly skeletal muscle [18,19], but are impractical in ICU settings due to logistical challenges. As a result, practical bedside methods are recommended, including bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), muscle ultrasonography (US), and anthropometric assessments (e.g., upper-arm or calf circumference) [20,21].

Multifrequency BIA can estimate FFM; however, its accuracy is limited in critically ill patients due to the complex fluid dynamics involved [22]. Therefore, it is important to calculate dry FFM by adjusting for extracellular fluid overload with the formula: dry FFM = FFM – (ECW – ((0.38 × ICW) / 0.62)), normalizing the ICW/ECW ratio to 0.62/0.38 (Fig. 3). Both patients, with a total body weight of 100 kg, show different protein requirements when calculated using FFM. For the patient with 50 kg FFM, the requirement is 92.5 g/day, much lower than the guideline recommendation of 130 g/day based on total body weight. For the patient with 82 kg FFM, the requirement would be 151.7 g/day. If protein intake were calculated based on total body weight (130 g/day), the first patient would be underfed (21.7 g). In contrast, the second would be overfed by 37.5 g, highlighting the importance of using FFM to estimate protein requirements accurately.

Ultrasound imaging is increasingly used in the ICU to assess skeletal muscle mass, but limitations limit its ability to guide protein prescriptions in critically ill patients [22]. Measurement protocols vary (e.g., patient positioning, probe angle, and timing), and the accuracy is often limited or unvalidated against gold standards [23]. Additionally, the heterogeneity of measured parameters and outcomes, combined with the effects of oedema, fluid shifts, and critical illness-related catabolism, complicates interpretation. Notably, the link between ultrasound-detected muscle changes, protein intake, and clinically meaningful outcomes remains unclear. Until standardization, validation studies, and interventions are completed, ultrasound-based skeletal muscle measurement remains a promising research tool, but not yet a reliable method for individualized protein prescriptions in the critically ill.

GUIDING OPTIMAL PROTEIN INTAKE DURING CRITICAL ILLNESS: ENDOTYPING

Monitoring the urea-to-creatinine ratio (UCR) is crucial during critical illness as it may reflect catabolic activity. Several studies have shown that urea and UCR levels tend to increase during ICU stay [7,9▪▪,10▪], potentially indicating muscle protein catabolism and the progression to persistent critical illness [24]. A high UCR may indicate an excess of exogenous amino acids, alongside increased endogenous catabolism [25], which cannot be effectively utilized. This mechanism could explain the higher mortality observed in the reanalysis of the EFFORT protein trial [25], in which a higher protein dose was associated with excessive ureagenesis. Therefore, UCR may serve as a marker of nutritional tolerance, with a low or declining UCR suggesting a better protein response in ICU patients. For instance, our patient exhibited spikes in UCR whenever C-reactive protein (CRP) levels increased, and for a longer duration than the CRP increase (Fig. 4). Such patterns in UCR could be leveraged to identify trends in catabolic responses and disease progression, offering a valuable tool for monitoring and adjusting nutritional interventions aimed at mitigating muscle wasting in critically ill patients.

Besides UCR and CRP levels, protein tolerance must be considered when personalizing protein intake. The study by Heyland et al. found that higher protein intake may be harmful to patients with AKI [7]. Additionally, caloric restriction is recommended in refeeding syndrome (RFS), which consequently limits protein dosing because many enteral formulas have fixed energy–protein ratios [26,27]. Moreover, our research group has shown that, in RFS, higher protein intake is associated with increased mortality, indicating that protein restriction may be warranted alongside caloric restriction [26]. Close monitoring of gastrointestinal intolerance is also required to ensure adequate nutritional tolerance [28].

RECENT INTERVENTION STUDIES REGARDING PROTEIN DOSING DURING CONVALESCENCE OF CRITICAL ILLNESS

No guidelines exist for protein dosing in post-ICU patients. However, van Zanten et al. recommend administering higher protein doses than during the ICU phase to promote recovery [29]. Nevertheless, it appears that protein targets are often not met even in the post-ICU phase [30,31,32▪]. Also, only a limited number of RCTs have been conducted on this topic.

Beumeler et al. (n = 81) investigated protein and energy intake in ICU survivors during the first year of recovery [33]. Only 10% of patients achieved the protein target of 1.2 g/kg/day, and 28% reached the energy target of 25 kcal/kg/day after 12 months of treatment. While dietary supplementation improved protein intake at 3, 6, and 12 months, and energy intake at 3 months, these improvements did not enhance physical health outcomes.

The CONFUCIUS trial (n = 15) compared the effects of 22 g of collagen peptides supplementation vs. an isocaloric carbohydrate on physical functioning in post-ICU patients with moderate ICU-acquired weakness [34▪]. However, due to early termination, no significant differences were observed between the two groups. Challenges in patient recruitment, adherence, and outcome assessment were identified, highlighting the need for further research on nutrition interventions for post-ICU recovery.

The PROSPECT-II study (n = 70) assessed the impact of a personalized, stepwise nutrition protocol on energy and protein intake in post-ICU patients [32▪]. Following the PROSPECT-I cohort (preimplementation), which had an energy adequacy of 82.3% (SD 18.3) and protein adequacy of 83.1% (SD 19.8) [35], PROSPECT-II (postimplementation) introduced a tailored protocol with stepwise nutrition, reducing enteral feeding only once adequate oral intake was confirmed. This strategy resulted in significant improvements, with energy and protein adequacy averaging 100.2 ± 28.8% and 97.1 ± 29.0%, respectively. However, no differences in physical function scores (MRC-sum, CPAx) were observed between the two cohorts from ICU discharge to hospital discharge.

The INTENT trial (n = 237) is a multicentre, unblinded, parallel-group phase trial to test whether a tailored nutrition intervention, delivered from ICU admission to hospital discharge, provides more energy than usual care in patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation [36▪▪]. The intervention resulted in a significant increase in both energy and protein delivery, with 72 ± 2 g protein/day in usual care vs. 93 ± 2 g protein/day in the tailored group, with an adjusted mean difference of 19 g/day (95% CI: 14–23 g/day). However, no differences were observed in duration of hospital stay, ventilator-free days, or bloodstream infection rates (Table 2).

OPTIMAL PROTEIN INTAKE DURING CONVALESCENCE OF CRITICAL ILLNESS

The recovery period following critical illness is crucial for survival, regaining muscle mass, and functional improvement. ICU survivors frequently experience postintensive care syndrome (PICS), characterized by new or worsening physical, cognitive, or mental health impairments following critical illness [37]. Our case patient faces not only organ failure and infections but also significant muscle loss, reduced mobility, and cognitive deficits, delaying recovery. Additionally, functional challenges like dysphagia, loss of smell and taste, and pain may complicate rehabilitation [2,3]. Given these multifaceted challenges, carefully managing protein intake during convalescence is essential for optimizing recovery and long-term outcomes [38].

One often-overlooked aspect of nutritional therapy is the timing of protein intake. During ICU stays, nutrition is typically provided continuously via enteral or parenteral routes. However, once patients transition to the ward and begin oral intake, aligning nutrition delivery with the body's circadian rhythm may improve metabolic efficiency and support recovery [39]. Distributing protein intake throughout the day, particularly during the morning and afternoon, could enhance protein utilization and stimulate muscle protein synthesis [34▪,40,41].

Determining the optimal protein dosing strategy remains challenging. While absolute body weight is commonly used in clinical practice and research, it may not accurately reflect individual variations in muscle mass. Our patient lost a significant amount of muscle mass following critical illness. In such instances, using body composition data, specifically FFM, at ICU admission or preferably before admission, could help establish more accurate protein targets aimed at restoring the patient's baseline muscle mass.

Various strategies can be employed to meet a patient's protein requirements, depending on their specific needs. Protein supplements are beneficial for patients who cannot meet their protein requirements, a common issue among critically ill patients transitioning from the ICU to the ward. For those unable to consume adequate amounts orally, enteral nutrition protocols are essential to provide the necessary protein [32▪]. This transition from enteral to oral intake must be carefully managed to prevent under- or overfeeding (Figure 5).

Potential determinants for protein targets during critical illness and beyond. The figure highlights phenotyping through body composition assessment, followed by endotyping, which evaluates the body's ability to utilize proteins. This evaluation includes factors such as disease severity, urea-to-creatinine ratio, refeeding syndrome, acute kidney failure, and gastrointestinal (in)tolerance. In the recovery phase, the focus shifts to how proteins promote healing, while also considering physical, cognitive, mental, and functional impairments associated with postintensive care syndrome.

Monitoring protein intake during the post-ICU phase is crucial for recovery. Schumacker et al. demonstrated that healthcare professionals can effectively quantify food consumption using food record charts and digital photography, which are valuable tools for documenting nutritional intake [42]. Involving patients and their family members in monitoring protein intake can promote self-care and empower patients in their recovery [43]. This patient-centred approach may improve adherence to nutritional protocols and enhance clinical outcomes [44].

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Future protein guidelines should prioritize individualized care by accounting for each patient's catabolic-anabolic balance. Moving from total body weight to FFM-based dosing requires a reliable assessment of skeletal muscle mass using imaging or bedside methods (CT, BIA, US), combined with metabolic biomarkers that provide real-time insight into protein utilization and catabolism. Adaptive feeding algorithms guided by UCR should be developed and tested in RCTs. Importantly, the field must now link protein provision to meaningful functional outcomes such as HRQoL and physical performance. Although a recent systematic review reported modest improvements in muscle strength with higher protein intake (mean difference ~2.36 kg), the available trials remain few, heterogeneous, and at moderate to high risk of bias [45].

Future trials must therefore evaluate personalized protein targets using functional and long-term outcomes, rather than focusing solely on muscle mass. Emerging technologies, including artificial intelligence (AI), may further enable personalized dosing across the ICU and post-ICU continuum. By integrating clinical data, body composition, metabolic biomarkers, and patient-centred outcomes, AI models could generate dynamic, real-time recommendations and support adaptive guidance via tele-nutrition platforms. Prospective validation studies are needed to determine whether such approaches improve muscle mass, function, and long-term recovery.

CONCLUSION

Current evidence does not support the routine use of high protein doses in critically ill patients. Large RCTs show that protein intake above ~1.2–1.3 g/kg/day does not improve survival or recovery, and may harm vulnerable patient subgroups. Protein prescriptions should be individualized, considering phenotyping and endotyping, including body composition (ideally FFM corrected for fluid overload), disease severity, catabolic state (e.g., UCR), organ dysfunction, refeeding syndrome, and gastrointestinal tolerance. Post-ICU, structured protocols, meal timing, and patient-family involvement are essential. Future trials should focus on FFM- and biomarker-guided algorithms with functional outcomes and HRQoL as primary endpoints.
