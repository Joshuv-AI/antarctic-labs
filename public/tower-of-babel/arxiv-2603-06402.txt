Transversal Rank, Conformality and Enumeration
Martin Schirneck

Abstract
The transversal rank of a hypergraph is the maximum size of its minimal hitting sets. Deciding, for an n n -vertex, m m -edge hypergraph and an integer k k , whether the transversal rank is at least k k takes time O ⁡ ( m k + 1 ​ n ) O(m^{k+1}\hskip 1.00006ptn) with an algorithm that is known since the 70s. It essentially matches an ( m + n ) Ω ⁡ ( k ) (m+n)^{\Omega(k)} ETH-lower bound by Araújo, Bougeret, Campos, and Sau [Algorithmica 2023] and Dublois, Lampis, and Paschos [TCS 2022]. Many hypergraphs seen in practice have much more edges than vertices, m ≫ n m\gg n . This raises the question whether an improvement of the run time dependency on m m can be traded for an increase in the dependency on n n . Our first result is an algorithm to recognize hypergraphs with transversal rank at least k k in time O ⁡ ( Δ k − 2 ​ m ​ n k − 1 ) O(\Delta^{k-2}\hskip 1.00006ptmn^{k-1}) , where Δ ⩽ m \Delta\leqslant m is the maximum degree. Our main technical contribution is a “look-ahead” method that allows us to find higher-order extensions , minimal hitting sets that augment a given set with at least two more vertices. We show that this method can also be used to enumerate all minimal hitting sets of a hypergraph with transversal rank k ∗ k^{*} with delay O ⁡ ( Δ k ∗ − 1 ​ m ​ n 2 ) O(\Delta^{k^{*}-1}\hskip 1.00006ptmn^{2}) . We then explore the possibility of further reducing the running time for computing the transversal rank to poly ​ ( m ) ⋅ n k + O ⁡ ( 1 ) \textup{{poly}}(m)\cdot n^{k+O(1)} . This turns out to be equivalent to several breakthroughs in combinatorial algorithms and enumeration. Among other things, such an improvement is possible if and only if k k -conformal hypergraphs can also be recognized in time poly ​ ( m ) ⋅ n k + O ⁡ ( 1 ) \textup{{poly}}(m)\cdot n^{k+O(1)} , and iff the maximal hypercliques/independent sets of a uniform hypergraph can be enumerated with incremental delay.

Introduction

A plethora of results in graph algorithms discuss methods to decide
whether a given input belongs to a certain graph class.
We have algorithms that recognize planar graphs, graphs of treewidth at most 67,
or graphs with independence number at least 42.
For hypergraphs, however, much fewer methods are known
and most of the literature focuses on uniform hypergraphs,
where all hyperedges have the same number of vertices
[
1
,
21
,
29
]
,
or, equivalently, binary matrices in which every column has the same number of 1s
[
17
]
.
Our goal is to advance the area of recognition algorithms for classes of
non-uniform
hypergraphs.
Specifically, we are interested in hypergraphs with large
transversal rank
,
which is the
maximum
size of any
inclusion-wise minimal
hitting set.
1
1

1

A
transversal
is another word for hitting set.
The
transversal hypergraph
is the collection of all minimal hitting sets
and the transversal rank is the maximum edge size of this hypergraph.

While our target are non-uniform hypergraphs,
our analysis leverages ideas from the uniform realm.
It also reveals deep connections to the problem of enumerating all minimal hitting sets.

The transversal rank was introduced to gauge the worst-case performance of “local improvement” methods for the hitting set problem.
It found applications in areas like parameterized algorithms and kernelization
[
31
,
42
]
,
formal concept analysis
[
16
]
,
and computational biology
[
19
]
.
Let
Transversal Rank
denote the problem of deciding,
for an
n
-vertex,
m
-edge hypergraph and a non-negative integer
k
,
whether there is a minimal hitting set with at least
k
vertices.
Fittingly, the problem is also known under the name
Maximum Minimal Hitting Set

[
19
]
.
It generalizes many other “max-min” variants of classical optimization problems
including
Maximum Minimal Vertex Cover

[
10
]
,

Maximum Minimal Feedback Vertex Set

[
23
]
,

Maximum Minimal Blocking Set
(where all maximum independent sets of a graph need to be hit
[
2
]
),
as well as
Upper Domination
(maximum minimal dominating set)
and its variant
Upper Total Domination

[
3
]
.

The max-min variants are often harder then their underlying minimization problems.
Known lower bounds on the computational complexity of the
Upper Domination
problem
immediately transfer to
Transversal Rank
.
A result by Cheston, Fricke, Hedetniemi, and Jacobs
[
15
]

thus shows that the problem is
NP
-complete.
Bazgan, Brankovic, Casel, Fernau, Jansen, Klein, Lampis, Liedloff, Monnot,
and Paschos
[
3
]

proved that it is
\textsf{W}[1]
-hard when parameterized by
k

and that even approximating the transversal rank
within a factor of
n^{1-\varepsilon}
is
NP
-hard.
They further showed that
there is no algorithm for
Transversal Rank
running in time
(m+n)^{o(\sqrt{k}\hskip 0.81949pt)}
,
unless the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) fails.
Recently, two independent works by Araújo, Bougeret, Campos, and Sau
[
2
]
as well as
Dublois, Lampis, and Paschos
[
24
]

improved this to
(m+n)^{o(k)}
.

Currently, the most efficient algorithm to recognize hypergraphs with large minimal hitting sets
follows from a result by Berge and Duchet
[
6
]
from the 70s.
Here, a
Sperner
hypergraph is one in which no two edges are contained in one another.
2
2

2

Sperner hypergraphs are sometime also called
simple
, e.g. in
[
5
]
,
but this term is not used consistently in the literature, see
[
30
]
.

Lemma 1

(Berge and Duchet
[
6
]
;
see Corollary 1, p. 58 of
[
5
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Sperner hypergraph and
k\geqslant 3
an integer.
Hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
has transversal rank at least
k
if and only if
there exists
k
distinct edges
E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{k}\in\mathcal{H}

such that every edge
E\in\mathcal{H}
has a vertex
x\in E

that appears in at most one of the
E_{i}
.
Moreover, if
D
denotes the set of all such
x
,
then
every
minimal hitting set
T\subseteq D
has size at least
k
.

For hitting sets, it does not make a difference whether the full hypergraph
\mathcal{H}

or only its inclusion-wise minimal edges are considered.
Finding those takes time
O(m^{2}\hskip 1.00006ptn)
and the resulting subhypergraph is Sperner,
allowing an application of
1
.
Given a collection
\{E_{i}\}_{i}
of
k
edges in
\mathcal{H}
,
checking whether the resulting set
D
is a hitting set for
\mathcal{H}
can be done in time
O(mn)
.
Cycling through all
k
-subhypergraphs of
\mathcal{H}
thus gives a total time of
O(m^{k+1}\hskip 1.00006ptn)
.
We show in
Appendix A

how a more careful preprocessing can reduce the running time to

O(m^{k+1}+m^{k}\hskip 1.00006ptn)
, which is never worse than
O(m^{k+1}\hskip 1.00006ptn)
.
Producing an actual witness for the large transversal rank,
namely, a
minimal
hitting set of size at least
k
,
hardly takes any additional effort.
Once a suitable set
D
is found,
an arbitrary minimal solution
T\subseteq D
can be computed in time
O(mn)

(see also
Appendix A
).

The algorithm matches the lower bound by Araújo et al.
[
2
]

as well as Dublois, Lampis, and Paschos
[
24
]

up to constant factors in the exponent.
However, its large complexity in terms of
m
makes it prohibitively
expensive for hypergraphs with many edges,
which is the typical setting in many practical applications
[
4
,
8
,
38
,
40
]
.
Note that in non-uniform hypergraphs (in constrast to graphs)

m
is not polynomially bounded in
n
.
ETH excludes the possibility that the exponents of
m
and
n

in the running time can be reduced to
o(k)

simultaneously
.
Notwithstanding, the fine-grained trade-offs between the two
are entirely unexplored.
Running times of, say,
m^{k-10}\cdot\textup{{poly}}(n)
and
O(m\cdot n^{3k/2})

are both consistent with our current knowledge,
but would result in drastically different behavior in practice.

We investigate whether one can reduce the dependency on the number of edges
m
,
even if this may increase the complexity in terms of the number of vertices
n
.
Our first result is a faster algorithm for hypergraphs with very many edges,
or instances in which every vertex has only small degree.
Let
\Delta\leqslant m
denote the maximum degree.

Theorem 2
.

Hypergraphs with transversal rank at least
k
can be recognized
in time
O(\Delta^{k-2}\hskip 1.00006ptmn^{k-1})
.
For
k<2
, the running time is
O(mn)
.

When comparing
1
and

Theorem 2
, the dependency on
m
drops
from
m^{k+1}
to
\Delta^{k-2}\hskip 1.00006ptm
,
which, however, increases the running time in terms of the number of vertices to
n^{k-1}
.
The trade-off is beneficial whenever
m=\Omega(\hskip 1.00006pt(\Delta n)^{\frac{k-2}{k}})
.

Consider the extension problem for minimal hitting sets (
Extension MinHS
),
where we have to check whether a given set of vertices
X
is contained in a minimal hitting set.
Boros, Gurvich, and Hammer
[
11
]
showed that the problem is
NP
-complete,
and Bläsius, Friedrich, Lischeid, Meeks, and Schirneck
[
9
]

proved that it is
\textsf{W}[3]
-complete when parameterized by the cardinality
|X|
of the partial solution.
We investigate the variant that decides
whether there exists a minimal hitting set that contains
X

and uses
at least two more
vertices.
We call this a
higher-order extension
.
The central contribution of the first part of our work is an algorithm
that allows us to
look ahead
in the search for higher-order extensions of
X
.
It runs in the same time as the best-known method for the original extension problem.
This is the key ingredient to establish
Theorem 2
.
Along the way, we identify the higher-order variant as the hard core of the extension problem.
This immediately shows that it is also
NP
- and
\textsf{W}[3]
-complete and implies several fine-grained lower bounds.
(See
section 2
for details.)

The look-ahead idea also has applications in the
enumeration
of minimal hitting sets.
Finding a
minimum-cardinality
hitting set is classically
NP
-hard
[
35
]
,
but a simple greedy algorithm can produce a minimal one.
Between the two extremes is the task of enumeration, that is, computing and listing

all
minimal hitting sets of a hypergraph.
There can be exponentially many minimal solutions,
so a polynomial algorithm is outright impossible.
The hope would be to achieve
output-polynomial
time,
scaling polynomially with the input size and the number of solutions.
It is
the
major open problem in enumeration
whether the so-called
transversal hypergraph problem

admits an output-polynomial algorithm
[
12
,
20
,
37
,
39
]
.

While answering this question seems to be out of reach of our current techniques,
a lot of work has been dedicated to identify classes of hypergraphs for which this problem
is tractable, see e.g.
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
36
]
.
For hypergraphs for which the transversal rank
k^{*}
is bounded,
even better than mere output-polynomial methods are possible.
The
delay
is the time between consecutive outputs.
This includes the preprocessing before the first solution and
the time after the last one until termination.
Bläsius et al.
[
9
]
used the
Extension MinHS
problem
to devise an algorithm with delay
O(m^{k^{*}+1}\hskip 1.00006ptn^{2})
,
The crucial property of the algorithm is
that it does
not
need to know
k^{*}
in advance,
3
3

3

If
k^{*}
were known and indeed a constant,
merely brute-forcing all sets up to size
k^{*}

would give a trivial polynomial (thus output-polynomial) algorithm.

only the analysis depends on
k^{*}
.
Araújo et al.
[
2
]

later observed that the bound can be tightened to
O(\Delta^{k^{*}}mn^{2})
,
which is more efficient if the degrees are small,
but gives the worst case running time if
\Delta=\Omega(m)
.
Our improvement regarding the higher-order extensions allows
us to break the
\Omega(m^{k^{*}+1})\cdot\textup{{poly}}(n)
delay barrier
for general hypergraphs.

Theorem 3
.

The minimal hitting sets of a hypergraph with transversal rank
k^{*}
can be enumerated
with delay
O(\Delta^{k^{*}-1}\hskip 1.00006ptmn^{2})
.
The algorithm uses space
O(mn)
.

The second part of this work explores the question whether the dependency on
m

in the running time to compute the transversal rank can be reduced beyond
Theorem 2
.
Ideally, we would like it to be polynomial in
m
,
with an exponent that is independent of
k
.
Of course, we cannot expect to achieve complexity
n^{o(k)}
at the same time.
Instead, we investigate the consequences of a method with running time
\textup{{poly}}(m)\cdot n^{k+O(1)}
.
As it turns out, such an improvement
is
equivalent
to several other breakthroughs in combinatorial algorithms
for conformal hypergraphs as well as the enumeration of maximal hypercliques.

Consider a set of vertices
S
for which there exists a hyperedge
E\,{\in}\,\mathcal{H}

with
S\,{\subseteq}\,E
.
Evidently, each
k
-subset of
S
is also contained in an edge of
\mathcal{H}
.
The hypergraph is called
k
-
conformal
if the opposite is true,
that is, all
k
-subsets of
S
being contained in some edge
is already sufficient for the existence of an edge that contains all of
S
.
The
conformal degree
of
\mathcal{H}
is the smallest
k
for which
\mathcal{H}
is
k
-conformal.
Already Berge and Duchet
[
6
]
observed
that there is a close connection between hypergraphs with large transversal rank
and large conformal degree (see
16
).
It is thus not surprising that recognizing
k
-conformal hypergraphs is
coNP
-complete,
and
\textsf{co}\textsf{W}[1]
-hard when parameterized by
k

[
3
,
16
]
.
A
hyperclique
in a
k
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{G}
is a set
S

such that each
k
-subset of
S
is
equal
to an edge of
\mathcal{G}
.

We extend the connection between transversal rank and conformal degree to enumeration.
We say the solutions to a combinatorial problem can be listed with
incremental delay

if the
i
-th delay, the time between the
i
-th and
(i{+}1)
-st output,
is bounded by a polynomial of the input size and
i
.
We reserve the variable
i
for the number of solutions seen so far throughout this work.
In general, the notion of incremental delay lies between output-polynomial time and polynomial delay,
but for hitting sets
it is known that any output-polynomial algorithm can be turned
into one with incremental delay
[
7
]
.
This includes hypergraphs whose
rank

r
(the maximum edge size) is bounded by a constant.
However, like for the transversal rank,
a fine-grained discussion of the
\textup{{poly}}(m,n,i)
-delay is missing from the literature.
A method with delay
i^{3}\cdot n^{r+O(1)}
would be preferred over
one with delay
O(i^{r+2}\cdot n)
,
the latter being the current state of the art
[
25
]
.
We prove a quantitative equivalence between the computation of the conformal degree,
the enumeration of minimal hitting sets in rank-bounded hypergraphs,
and the enumeration of maximal hypercliques.
Of course, everything that is said about hypercliques also applies to independent sets
by swapping the role of edges and non-edges.

Theorem 4
.

The following statements are equivalent.

•

Hypergraphs with transversal rank at least
k
can be recognized
in time
\textup{{poly}}(m)\cdot n^{k+O(1)}
.

•

Hypergraphs that are
k
-conformal can be recognized
in time
\textup{{poly}}(m)\cdot n^{k+O(1)}
.

•

The minimal hitting sets of hypergraphs with rank
r
can be enumerated
with incremental delay
\textup{{poly}}(i)\cdot n^{r+O(1)}
.

•

The maximal hypercliques/independent sets of uniform hypergraphs with rank
r
can be enumerated
with incremental delay
\textup{{poly}}(i)\cdot n^{r+O(1)}
.

On the one hand, the equivalence shows how faster enumeration algorithms in uniform hypergraphs
can help with decision problems also for non-uniform inputs.
On the other hand, it opens new inroads to prove (conditional) delay lower bounds,
a notoriously hard problem in enumeration complexity
[
12
]
.
Ruling out fast listing algorithms for minimal hitting sets/maximal hypercliques in uniform hypergraphs
now reduces to proving lower bounds on the
Transversal Rank
problem.
We point out that the hypergraphs in the first two items of
Theorem 4

do
not
need to have bounded rank.
Indeed, if
r
is constant, Araújo et al.
[
2
]

showed how to compute the transversal rank much faster
in time
2^{rk}\cdot\textup{{poly}}(m,n)
.

Traditionally, the enumeration of minimal hitting sets has been investigated
using a different kind of decision problem, namely,
given two hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
,
verifying whether
\mathcal{G}
consists precisely of the minimal hitting sets of
\mathcal{H}
.
This problem is known to be solvable in polynomial time (in the combined input size of
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
)
if and only if the minimal hitting sets can be enumerated
with incremental delay
[
7
]
.
We strengthen this equivalence in the case of rank-
r
hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a hypergraph with rank
r
.
The following statements are equivalent.

•

The minimal hitting sets of
\mathcal{H}
can be enumerated
with incremental delay
\textup{{poly}}(i)\cdot n^{r+O(1)}
.

•

For any hypergraph
\mathcal{G}
, deciding whether it contains all and only the minimal hitting sets of
\mathcal{H}
can be done in time
\textup{{poly}}(|\mathcal{G}|)\cdot n^{r+O(1)}
.

The heart of
Theorem 4
is to show
that recognizing hypergraphs with large transversal rank
is the same as quickly enumerating the maximal hypercliques
of a uniform hypergraph with suitably chosen rank.
For simple graphs (2-uniform hypergraphs),
there are non-trivial clique enumeration algorithms.
Tsukiyama, Ide, Ariyoshi, and Shirakawa
[
41
]

and later Johnson, Papadimitriou, and Yannakakis
[
34
]

showed how to enumerate the maximal cliques of a graph
G=(V,E)

with delay
O(|V|\,{\cdot}\,|E|)
.
We plug this into our framework
to obtain an algorithm to recognize conformal (i.e., 2-conformal) hypergraphs.
It improves over the previous
O(m^{4}\hskip 1.00006ptn)
-time procedure based on
1
.

Theorem 6
.

Conformal hypergraphs can be recognized in time
O(mn^{3})
.

Outline.

Next, we give a technical overview of our main contributions.
We fix the basic notation in
section 3
.

section 4
then describes the look ahead and its applications,
and
section 5
proves the equivalence between the decision and enumeration problems.

2
Overview and Discussion

Our first contribution is the look-ahead algorithm for the enumeration of minimal hitting sets
in hypergraphs of bounded transversal rank.
The same approach can be used to compute the transversal rank faster.
We then sketch the technical ideas behind the equivalence between the recognition of
hypergraphs with large transversal rank, those with large conformal degree,
and the enumeration of hypercliques and of hitting sets.
We also briefly discuss the possibility of further improvements
as well as open questions for future work.

2.1
Looking Ahead for Higher-Order Extensions

Our enumeration algorithm
improves the one by Bläsius et al.
[
9
]
.
It constructs a search tree pruned by a subroutine that decides,
for a pair of disjoint sets
X,Y\subseteq V
of vertices,
whether
X
can be extended to a minimal hitting set without using any elements from
Y
.
This extension subroutine runs in time
O(\Delta^{|X|}\hskip 1.00006ptmn)
for any
Y
.
Their main argument for the
O(\Delta^{k^{*}}mn^{2})
delay bound is that,
on hypergraphs with transversal rank
k^{*}
,
the subroutine is only ever invoked with sets
X
that satisfy
|X|\leqslant k^{*}
.
This holds even though
k^{*}
is a priori
unknown
to the algorithm.
The article
[
9
]
also contains several conditional lower bounds
showing that the extension algorithm is almost optimal.
A method running in time

O(m^{|X|-\varepsilon})\cdot\textup{{poly}}(n)
for any constants
|X|\geqslant 2
and
\varepsilon>0

would violate the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH).
The, arguably much stronger,
Non-deterministic Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (NSETH)
is a barrier to reduce the running time even to
O(m^{|X|+1-\varepsilon})\cdot\textup{{poly}}(n)

(see
15
for a precise statement).
So there is little hope for improvement from that side.

Instead, we tackle the bottleneck of the tree search.
In the root the two sets
X
and
Y
are both empty.
The search checks in each node whether
(X,Y)
is extendable.
If so, the algorithm branches on the decision whether to add the next vertex
in
V{\setminus}(X\cup Y)
to the partial solution
X
or the set of “forbidden” vertices
Y
.
The worst case occurs once a set
X
is reached
that has cardinality exactly equal to the transversal rank
k^{*}
.
If we were to know
k^{*}
in advance,
we could stop the search one level before the bottleneck at
|X|=k^{*}-1
.
We would quickly find all extending minimal hitting sets
since they have at most one more vertex.
All other supersets of
X
are irrelevant for the enumeration
as they are already too large to be minimal solutions.
The issue is that we cannot test for the right cardinality of
X

without computing the transversal rank
k^{*}
.

To solve the bottleneck,
we instead integrate a “look ahead” in
every
level of the search tree.
The main difficulty is to decide whether
X
still has higher-order extensions,
that is, a minimal hitting set
T\supseteq X
with
|T{\setminus}X|\geqslant 2
.
The search must then expand the current node
(X,Y)
to find the remaining solutions.
We prove a structural characterization of the higher-order extensions,
which implies that deciding the existence of such a
T

is at least as hard as the extension problem without size restrictions.
Notwithstanding, we give an algorithm running in the same
O(\Delta^{|X|}\hskip 1.00006ptmn)
time.
In other words, our look ahead is for free.
This is the reason for our delay improvement
and also helps with the original problem
of recognizing hypergraphs with transversal rank at least
k
.

One could hope that this approach can somehow be iterated.
Instead of considering only two more vertices,
one may want to check for minimal extension that have up to
\ell
more elements
and expect an enumeration delay of
\Delta^{k^{*}-\ell+1}\hskip 1.00006ptmn^{\ell+O(1)}

and similar improvements for computing the transversal rank.
If feasible, the approach would bypass the lower bounds on the extension problem
and, just by enhancing the tree search, enable a smooth trade-off
between the dependency on
\Delta
and
n
.
However, the iterated look ahead requires a complementing algorithm
to decide whether
X
is contained in a minimal hitting set of cardinality at least
|X|+\ell
.
For
\ell>2
, this seems to be much harder than extension.

2.2
Transversal Rank and Conformal Degree

It is conceivable that an entirely different approach lowers the running time
for recognizing hypergraphs with transversal rank at least
k

all the way to
\textup{{poly}}(m)\cdot n^{k+O(1)}
.
We show that this is equivalent to several other new results
involving hypercliques instead of hitting sets.
Consider a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
with vertex set
V

and a minimal hitting set
S\subseteq V
of at least
k
vertices.
Minimality implies that,
for each subset
S^{\prime}\subsetneq S
with
|S^{\prime}|=k-1
,
there exists an edge
E\in\mathcal{H}
such that
S^{\prime}\cap E=\emptyset
.
Thus,
S^{\prime}
is contained in the complement
\overline{E}=V{\setminus}E
.
Since
S
hits every edge of
\mathcal{H}
,
the set itself is
not
contained in any complement edge.
This shows that the hypergraph
\overline{\mathcal{H}}=\{V{\setminus}E\mid E\in\mathcal{H}\}

is not
(k{-}1)
-conformal.
In fact,
1
can be reformulated to
state that a transversal rank at least
k
is equivalent
to the conformal degree of
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
being at least
k

(see
16
).

We extend this equivalence to several enumeration problems
by using ideas from the analysis of uniform hypergraphs.
Given a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
with arbitrary edge sizes,
its
k
-
section

[\mathcal{H}]_{k}
is constructed
by including any set of
k
vertices
that appear together in some edge of
\mathcal{H}
as an edge of
[\mathcal{H}]_{k}
.
Clearly, the
k
-section is
k
-uniform.
(In the example above, each set
S^{\prime}
is an edge of the
(k{-}1)
-section of
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
.)
We characterize the conformal degree of
\mathcal{H}

in terms of the maximal hypercliques of
[\mathcal{H}]_{k}
.
The crucial observation is that we only need to know at most
|\mathcal{H}|
such hypercliques
to compute the conformal degree,
even if there are way more cliques than that.
This is where enumeration with incremental delay comes into play.
These algorithms allow for a non-trivial bound on the maximum time
between consecutive outputs,
and thus on the time until we see the
|\mathcal{H}|
-th solution.

Recall that the cliques of a graph
are in one-to-one correspondence with the independent sets of the complement graph.
Those, in turn, correspond to the vertex covers (hitting sets) by taking the set complement.
The same argument transfers any delay improvement for minimal hitting sets in
k
-uniform hypergraphs
to the enumeration of maximal hypercliques.
Now to close the circle of reductions,
we show that a faster algorithm for computing the transversal rank
can indeed speed up the enumeration of minimal hitting sets in uniform hypergraphs.
Our result even holds for the larger class of hypergraphs with bounded rank,
we do not need all edges to have the same size.
Consider an algorithm that has already found several minimal hitting sets of
\mathcal{H}
,
but has to decide whether there are still more solutions to be discovered.
The minimal hitting sets found so far form a hypergraph
\mathcal{G}
of their own.
Building on the work by Eiter and Gottlob
[
25
]
,
we show how to decide whether
\mathcal{G}
already contains all solutions,
using information about the transversal rank of
\mathcal{G}
.

As usual with fine-grained equivalences,
they can be seen as additional motivation to find better algorithms
or as conditional evidence of the impossibility of this endeavor.
Currently, there is no algorithm known that computes the transversal rank,
equivalently, the conformal degree,
in time
\textup{{poly}}(m)\cdot n^{k+O(1)}
.
We leave it as an open problem to obtain such a method or rule out its existence
under plausible complexity assumptions.
Arguably, a more modest research goal is
an algorithm running in time
m^{k+1-\varepsilon}\cdot n^{o(k)}
.

3
Preliminaries

Hypergraphs, rank, and complements.

A
hypergraph
is a finite set
V
together with a collection
\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{V}
of subsets.
The elements of
V
are the
vertices
,
\mathcal{H}
contains the (
hyper
-)
edges
.
We usually identify the hypergraph with its edge set.
It is safe to assume that all hypergraphs in this work share the same vertex set.
We use
n=|V|
to denote its size.
The hypergraphs may have different number of edges,
we use
m=|\mathcal{H}|
if this does not create ambiguities.
A vertex
v\in V
has
degree

\deg(v)=|\{E\in\mathcal{H}\mid v\in E\}|
,
i.e., the number of edges containing
v
.
The
maximum degree
of
\mathcal{H}
is
\Delta=\max_{v\in V}\deg(v)
.

The
rank

\rank(\mathcal{H})=\max_{E\in\mathcal{H}}|E|

is the maximum edge cardinality.
If the rank is bounded by some non-negative integer
r
,
we say that
\mathcal{H}
is an
r
-hypergraph
.
It is
r
-uniform
if all its edges have the same size
r
.
The
2
-uniform hypergraphs are the usual (undirected, simple) graphs.
A hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
is
Sperner
if no two of its edges are contained in each other.
Note that uniform hypergraphs (of any rank) are always Sperner.

We need two different notions of a
complement
of a hypergraph
(V,\mathcal{H})
.
We define
\overline{\mathcal{H}}=\{V{\setminus}E\mid E\in\mathcal{H}\}

to be the hypergraph containing the complement edges.
In the specific case where
\mathcal{H}
is
r
-uniform,
we let
\mathcal{H}^{\textsf{C}}=\{E\in\binom{V}{r}\mid E\notin\mathcal{H}\}

consist of all the
r
-subsets of vertices that are non-edges of
\mathcal{H}
.

Hitting sets and transversal rank.

Let
(V,\mathcal{H})
be a (not necessarily rank-bounded) hypergraph
and
X\subseteq V
a set of vertices.
The subhypergraph
\mathcal{H}(X)
consists of all edges
E\in\mathcal{H}

that contain an element of
X
.
The remaining edges are in
\mathcal{H}^{V{\setminus}X}=\mathcal{H}{\setminus}\mathcal{H}(X)
.
The notation alludes to the fact that
E\in\mathcal{H}^{V{\setminus}X}

is equivalent to
E\subseteq V{\setminus}X
.

We say a set
T\subseteq V
of vertices
is a
hitting set
, or
transversal
,
if it intersects every hyperedge,
meaning that
E\cap T\neq\emptyset
holds for all
E\in\mathcal{H}

or, equivalently,
\mathcal{H}(T)=\mathcal{H}
.
The hitting set is (
inclusion-wise
)
minimal

if none of its proper subsets is also a hitting set.
Equivalently, a hitting set is minimal iff
for each
t\in T
,
there exists a
private
edge
E\in\mathcal{H}
with
E\cap T=\{t\}
.
If some
t\in T
does not have a private edge,
then the set
T
is
redundant
.

The minimal hitting sets of
\mathcal{H}

form another hypergraph on the same vertex set
V
,
the
transversal hypergraph

\Tr(\mathcal{H})
.
It is Sperner,
regardless of whether
\mathcal{H}
has this property.
For two disjoint sets
X,Y\subseteq V
,
we use
\Tr(\mathcal{H})[X,Y]
for the subhypergraph of all those minimal hitting sets
T\in\Tr(\mathcal{H})

that satisfy
X\subseteq T\subseteq V{\setminus}Y
.

The
transversal rank
of
\mathcal{H}
is
\rank(\Tr(\mathcal{H}))
,
that is, the maximum cardinality of a minimal transversal.
We consider the corresponding search problem.

Transversal Rank

Input:

A hypergraph
(V,\mathcal{H})
and a non-negative integer
k
.

Output:

Either produce
T\in\Tr(\mathcal{H})
with
|T|\geqslant k

or correctly output that
\rank(\Tr(\mathcal{H}))<k
.

The problem is trivial for
k\leqslant 2
.
A hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
has transversal rank at least
1
if and only if
\emptyset\notin\mathcal{H}
.
It has transversal rank at least
2
iff it contains two edges
that are incomparable with respect to set-inclusion.

Hypercliques and conformality.

Let
(V,\mathcal{H})
be an
r
-uniform hypergraph.
A
hyperclique
in
\mathcal{H}
is a set
C\subseteq V
of at least
r
vertices
such that every subset
S\subseteq C
with
|S|=r

is a hyperedge,
S\in\mathcal{H}
.
A hyperclique is (
inclusion-wise
)
maximal
if all its proper supersets are no longer hypercliques.
The collection of all maximal hypercliques is denoted
\C(\mathcal{H})
.
Conversely, if
C
does not contain any edge of
\mathcal{H}
, it is an
independent set
.

Let now
(V,\mathcal{H})
be an arbitrary hypergraph and
k
a positive integer.

\mathcal{H}
is
k
-
conformal
if for every set
S\subseteq V
the following
two conditions are equivalent.

1.

There exists a hyperedge
E\in\mathcal{H}
such that
S\subseteq E
.

2.

For each subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
with
|S^{\prime}|\leqslant k
there exists an edge
E^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}

with
S^{\prime}\subseteq E^{\prime}
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is
k
-conformal, then it is also
k^{\prime}
-conformal for all
k^{\prime}\geqslant k
.
The
conformal degree
of
\mathcal{H}
is the smallest
k
such that
\mathcal{H}
is
k
-conformal.

Conformal Degree

Input:

A hypergraph
(V,\mathcal{H})
and a non-negative integer
k
.

Output:

Either correctly output that
\mathcal{H}
is
k
-conformal
or produce a set
S\subseteq V
such that

every subset in
\binom{S}{\leqslant k}
is contained
in some edge of
\mathcal{H}
, but
S\nsubseteq E
for all
E\in\mathcal{H}
.

Parameterized Complexity and the (Strong) Exponential Time Hypothesis.

We assume some familiarity with parameterized complexity
and only review the most important concepts.
For a more detailed exposition, we refer the reader to the textbooks
[
18
,
22
,
28
]
.
Consider pairs
(I,k)\in\{0,1\}^{*}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
where
I
is (a suitable encoding of) an input instance
and the non-negative integer
k
is the parameter.
A parameterized problem
\Pi
is a subset of
\{0,1\}^{*}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
The problem is
fixed parameter tractable
(
FPT
) if there exists a computable function
f

such that the inclusion
(I,k)\in\Pi
can be decided in time
f(k)\cdot\textup{{poly}}(|I|)
,
called
FPT
-
time
.
A parameterized reduction between parameterized problems
\Pi
and
\mathrm{P}

is a function computable in
FPT
-time that maps instances
(I,k)
for
\Pi

to equivalent instances
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})
for
\mathrm{P}

such that there exists a computable function
g
with
k^{\prime}\leqslant g(k)
.

These reductions give rise to a system of complexity classes called the
W
-
hierarchy
.
The
Weighted Circuit Satisfiability
problem is,
given a Boolean circuit
C
and a non-negative integer
k
,
to decide whether
C
has a satisfying assignment of Hamming weight
k
.
The problem parameter is
k
.
A large gate in
C
is one with fan-in strictly larger than
2
.
The
weft
of
C
is the maximum number of large gates on any input-output path through
C
.
For any positive integer
t
, the class
\textsf{W}[t]

is the collection of all parameterized problems reducable to

Weighted Circuit Satisfiability
when restricted to circuits of constant depth and weft
t
.
All inclusions in the
W
-hierarchy

\textsf{FPT}\subseteq\textsf{W}[1]\subseteq\textsf{W}[2]\subseteq\textsf{W}[3]\subseteq\dots

are conjectured to be strict.
The class
\textsf{co}\textsf{W}[t]
contains the complements of problems in
\textsf{W}[t]
.
Beyond the
W
-hierarchy lies the class
para
-
NP
which contains all parameterized problems that can be solved by a
nondeterministic
algorithm in
FPT
-time.
We have
\textsf{FPT}=\text{{para}-{NP} }
if and only if
\textsf{P}=\textsf{NP}
,
see
[
28
]
.
A parameterized problem is complete for
para
-
NP
if it is
NP
-complete
already for constant parameter values.
The class
para
-
coNP
is the analog using co-nondeterministic algorithms and the class
coNP
.

A stronger hardness assumption than the
W
-hierarchy being proper
is the
Exponential Time Hypothesis
(ETH)
[
32
,
33
]
.
It states that the
3
-
Sat
problem,
the satisfiability problem for Boolean formulas on
n
variables
in conjunctive normal form with clause length
3
, cannot be solved in time
2^{o(n)}
.
It is known that ETH implies
\textsf{W}[1]\neq\textsf{FPT}

[
14
]
.
In turn, ETH is implied by the
Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis
(SETH)
[
33
]

positing that, for every constant
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a clause length
\ell

such that
\ell
-
Sat
cannot be solved in time
O(2^{(1-\varepsilon)n})
.
Finally, the
Nondeterministic Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis
(NSETH)
is a common generalization of SETH and
\textsf{co}\textsf{NP}\neq\textsf{NP}

[
13
]
.
It denotes the conjecture that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an
\ell

such that no
co-nondeterministic algorithm without randomness
can decide
\ell
-
Sat

in time
O(2^{(1-\varepsilon)n})
.
Note that, while it formalizes the current frontier in worst-case
Sat
-solving,
NSETH is a very strong assumption and thus not entirely plausible.
Its value stems from the fact that both proving and refuting NSETH would have
interesting consequences in complexity theory
[
13
]
.

4
A Look Ahead for the Transversal Rank

In this section, we present an algorithm that,
given a non-empty hypergraph
\mathcal{H}\neq\emptyset
with (unknown) transversal rank
k^{*}

and maximum degree
\Delta
,
enumerates the minimal hitting sets of
\mathcal{H}
with delay

O(\Delta^{k^{*}-1}\hskip 1.00006ptmn^{2})

using
O(mn)
space.
We first review the method by Bläsius et al.
[
9
]
,
including the analysis of Araújo et al.
[
2
]
.

4.1
The Enumeration Algorithm by Bläsius et al.

The algorithm in
[
9
]

constructs a search tree which is pruned by repeated calls to an
extension oracle
,
that is, a subroutine that decides for set of vertices
X

whether it is contained in a minimal hitting set
T\subseteq X
.
We call such a set
T
a
minimal extension
of
X
.
The correctness of the algorithm hinges on the following lemma.

Lemma 7

(
[
9
,
11
]
)
.

Let
(V,\mathcal{H})
be a hypergraph and
X\subseteq V
a set of vertices.
The set
X
has a minimal extension if and only if
there exists a family of edges
\{E_{x}\}_{x\in X}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
such that

1.

for every vertex
x\in X
, we have
E_{x}\cap X=\{x\}
;

2.

for every edge
E\in\mathcal{H}
contained in
\bigcup_{x\in X}E_{x}
,
we have
E\cap X\neq\emptyset
.

Recall that every vertex
x
of a minimal hitting set needs a private edge only hit by
x
.
The first condition of
7
makes
E_{x}
a
candidate private edge

for
x
w.r.t. the partial solution
X
.
When
X
is clear from context, we use
\mathcal{H}_{x}=\{E\in\mathcal{H}\mid E\cap X=\{x\}\}

for the subhypergraph of all candidate private edges for
x
.
The second condition then ensures that the candidates
E_{x_{1}},E_{x_{2}},\dots

for different elements of
X
do not interfere with each other.

Besides the partial solution
X
, we also need to incorporate a set
Y
of vertices
that have already been excluded from the search.
With the notation
\Tr(\mathcal{H})[X,Y]
for all minimal hitting sets of
\mathcal{H}

that contain
X
but avoid
Y
,
we get the following.

Corollary 8
.

Let
(V,\mathcal{H})
be a hypergraph and
X,Y\subseteq V

two disjoint set of vertices.
There is a minimal hitting set
T\in\Tr(\mathcal{H})[X,Y]
if and only if
there exists a family of edges
\{E_{x}\}_{x\in X}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
such that

1.

for every vertex
x\in X
, we have
(E_{x}{\setminus}Y)\cap X=\{x\}
;

2.

for every edge
E\in\mathcal{H}
, if
(E{\setminus}Y)\cap X=\emptyset
,
then
(E{\setminus}Y)\nsubseteq\bigcup_{x\in X}(E_{x}{\setminus}Y)
.

For a subhypergraph
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
,
we use
\mathcal{G}^{*}=\{E{\setminus}Y\mid E\in\mathcal{G}\}

for the respective collection of edges that have
Y
removed.
For example,
\mathcal{H}^{*}_{x}=\{E{\setminus}Y\mid E\in\mathcal{H},E\cap X=\{x\}\}

are the reduced candidate private edges for
x
,
and
(\mathcal{H}^{V{\setminus}X})^{*}=\{E{\setminus}Y\mid E\in\mathcal{H},E\cap X=\emptyset\}

the reduced edges not yet hit by
X
.
We sometimes also use
E^{*}
for the edge
E{\setminus}Y
itself.

8
implies the following solution to the extension problem for a given pair
of disjoint sets
X
and
Y
.
If one of the hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}^{*}_{x}
is empty,
X
is redundant and thus has no minimal extension.
Otherwise, if
(\mathcal{H}^{V{\setminus}X})^{*}=\emptyset
(that is, all edges of
\mathcal{H}^{*}
are hit by
X
),
then
X
is a minimal hitting set for the
original
hypergraph
\mathcal{H}

of the non-reduced edges.
If those sanity checks do not already resolve the instance,
all combinations of edges

(E^{*}_{x_{1}},E^{*}_{x_{2}},\dots,E^{*}_{x_{|X|}})\in\prod_{x\in X}\mathcal{H}^{*}_{x}

in the Cartesian product are tested whether their union

\bigcup_{x\in X}E^{*}_{x}
completely contains some edge of
(\mathcal{H}^{V{\setminus}X})^{*}
.
There is a combination for which this is
not
the case if and only if

X
is extendable to a minimal hitting set for
\mathcal{H}
without using
Y\!
.

Fix an arbitrary total order
\leqslant
on the vertex set
V
.
The algorithm constructs a tree in which
each node is labeled with a disjoint pair
(X,Y)

such that
X\cup Y
is a prefix of
V
w.r.t. the order
\leqslant
.
The root is labeled
(\emptyset,\emptyset)
.
In any the node
(X,Y)
, it is tested whether
X
is extendable without
Y
.
If
X
has no minimal extension, the search in that branch halts and backtracks.
If the check determines that
X
itself is already a minimal hitting set,
the solution is output and the branch pruned as well.
Otherwise,
X
is indeed extendable without
Y
and
the search branches on the decision where to include the next vertex

v_{\min}=\min_{\leqslant}{V{\setminus}(X\cup Y)}

by creating two child nodes
(X\cup\{v_{\min}\},Y)
and
(X,Y\cup\{v_{\min}\})
.

The algorithm does not need to know the transversal rank.
By design, the cardinality of
X
never exceeds
k^{*}
.
Any set with
|X|=k^{*}
is either a (maximum) minimal hitting set itself
or already too large to be extendable.
The worst case occurs if a set with
k^{*}{-}1
vertices is verified to be extendable,
but the next vertex
v_{\min}
is such that

X\cup\{v_{\min}\}
is neither redundant nor minimal.
Then, all
\prod_{x\in X\cup\{v_{\min}\}}|\mathcal{H}^{*}_{x}|=O(\Delta^{k^{*}})
possible combinations
have to be checked only to verify that there is always some edge

E^{*}\in(\mathcal{H}^{V{\setminus}(X\cup\{v_{\min}\})})^{*}

with
E^{*}\subseteq\bigcup_{x\in X\cup\{v_{\min}\}}E^{*}_{x}
.
Each check takes
O(|(\mathcal{H}^{V{\setminus}(X\cup\{v_{\min}\})})^{*}|\cdot|V|)=O(mn)
time.
The
O(\Delta^{k^{*}}mn^{2})
delay follows from
O(n)
nodes sitting between two outputs.

4.2
Higher-Order Extensions

Our improvement stems from avoiding the bottleneck
by looking ahead and checking whether
X
has a minimal extension with at least 2 more vertices.
Consider disjoint sets
X
and
Y
of which we already know
that there exists a minimal hitting set
T\in\Tr(\mathcal{H})[X,Y]
.
It trivial to check whether
T=X
itself is already minimal,
we call this a
0-extension
.
It is also easy to find all
1-extensions
,
the minimal solutions that have exactly one more element than
X
.
However, the minimal extension
T
may be (much) larger than
|X|+1
.
Those are the
higher-order extensions
.
As it will turn out, deciding their existence is the hard core of the extension problem,
see
subsection 4.4
.
The following lemma is crucial both for understanding the computational complexity
of higher-order extensions as well as for implementing the look-ahead idea
without sacrificing running time.
We first discuss the case where
Y=\emptyset
.

Lemma 9
.

Let
(V,\mathcal{H})
be a hypergraph and
X\subseteq V
a set of vertices.
Define
S=\bigcap\,\mathcal{H}^{V{\setminus}X}
.
A minimal hitting set
T\in\Tr(\mathcal{H})
with
T\supseteq X

satisfies
|T{\setminus}X|>1
if and only if
T\subseteq V{\setminus}S
.

Proof.

The set
S=\bigcap\,\mathcal{H}^{V{\setminus}X}
contains those vertices
that appear in all edges that are not yet hit by
X
.
For any such
s\in S
, the set
X\cup\{s\}
is a (not necessarily minimal) hitting set of
\mathcal{H}
.
Thus, no proper superset
T\supsetneq X\cup\{s\}
is a
minimal
hitting set.
If
X
has a higher-order extension
T
at all, we thus have
s\notin T
.

Conversely, every
T\in\Tr(\mathcal{H})
with
X\subseteq T\subseteq V{\setminus}S

has at least two more elements than
X
.
It cannot use a single vertex to hit all edges in
\mathcal{H}^{V{\setminus}X}

since those are all in
S
.
∎

Regarding the general case that includes non-empty sets
Y
,
it is easy to verify that
T
is a minimal extension of
X
avoiding
Y

if and only if it is an (arbitrarily sized) minimal extension in the hypergraph of reduced edges

\mathcal{H}^{*}=\{E{\setminus}Y\mid E\in\mathcal{H}\}
.

Corollary 10
.

Let
(V,\mathcal{H})
be a hypergraph and
X,Y\subseteq V

two disjoint sets.
Let
S=\bigcap\,(\mathcal{H}^{V{\setminus}X})^{*}
be the
intersection of the
reduced
edges that are disjoint from
X
.
A minimal hitting set
T\in\Tr(\mathcal{H})
with
X\subseteq T\subseteq V{\setminus}Y

satisfies
|T{\setminus}X|>1

if and only if
T\subseteq V{\setminus}(Y\,{\cup}\,S)
.

We now implement the look ahead.
Instead of merely checking whether
X
is extendable without
Y
,
we devise an algorithm that outputs all 0- and 1-extensions of
X
avoiding
Y
,
and then decides whether there are any higher-order extension left in
\Tr(\mathcal{H})[X,Y]
.
Moreover, if there are such large extensions,
the algorithm computes a set
Y^{+}\supseteq Y
with the guarantee that all remaining extensions
also avoid
Y^{+}
.
This obviates the need to evaluate nodes
(X,Z)
with
Y\subseteq Z\subseteq Y^{+}

and thus allows us to short-cut the search.
We further include several sanity checks that allow for premature pruning on suitable inputs.
A full overview of the procedure is given in
.

Input:

Disjoint sets
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{|X|}\}
and
Y\subseteq V
.

Output:

All minimal extensions
T\in\Tr(\mathcal{H})[X,Y]
with
|T{\setminus}X|\leqslant 1
.

Return:

continue with

Y^{+}
if there exists a
T^{\prime}\in\Tr(\mathcal{H})[X,Y]

with
|T^{\prime}{\setminus}X|>1
,

where
Y\subseteq Y^{+}\subseteq V{\setminus}X

is such that all such
T^{\prime}
are in
\Tr(\mathcal{H})[X,Y^{+}]
.

halt
if there is no such
T^{\prime}
.

Procedure

extendable(
X,Y
)
:

/*
preprocessing empty sets
X
*/

if

X=\emptyset

then

1

initialize
\mathcal{H}^{*}=\emptyset
;

2

foreach

E\in\mathcal{H}

do

3

if

E\subseteq Y

then

Proof of

We consider a certain binary relation over hypergraphs.
We write
\mathcal{G}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{H}
if every edge of
\mathcal{G}

contains an edge of
\mathcal{H}
as a subset.
Note that
\preccurlyeq
is a pre-order in that it is reflexive and transitive.
Moreover,
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
implies
\mathcal{G}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{H}
.
When restricted to Sperner hypergraphs, the relation is even antisymmetric
(i.e., a partial order).
The key fact we use is that the relation is inverted when taking hitting sets

8
