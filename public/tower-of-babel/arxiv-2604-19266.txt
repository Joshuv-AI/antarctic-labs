Abstract
. We study constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs) where the constraint languages are defined by finite automata, giving rise to automata-based CSPs. The key notion is the concept of Automatic Constraint Satisfaction Problem ( AutCSP \mathrm{AutCSP} ) where constraint languages and instances are specified by finite automata. The AutCSP \mathrm{AutCSP} captures infinite yet finitely describable sets of relations, enabling concise representations of complex constraints. Studying the complexity of the AutCSP \mathrm{AutCSP} s illustrates the interplay between classical CSPs, automata, and logic, sharpening the boundary between tractable and intractable constraints. We show that checking whether an operation is a polymorphism of such a language can be done in polynomial time. Building on this, we establish several complexity classification results for the AutCSP \mathrm{AutCSP} . In particular, we prove that Schaefer’s Dichotomy Theorem extends to the AutCSP \mathrm{AutCSP} over the Boolean domain, and we provide algorithms that decide tractability of some classes of AutCSP \mathrm{AutCSP} s over arbitrary finite domains via automatic polymorphisms. An important part of our work is that our polynomial-time algorithms run on AutCSP \mathrm{AutCSP} instances that can be exponentially more succinct than their standard CSP counterparts.



1.
Introduction

The classical Constraint Satisfaction Problem (CSP) asks whether there exists an assignment to a set of variables that satisfies all given constraints. The CSP has found its applications across many areas of mathematics, computer science, and artificial intelligence. Many of its variants received much attention in the last several decades. In this paper we are concerned with the decision version of the problem, especially its ‘non-uniform’ kind, in which the type of allowed constraints is restricted by a collection of predicates often called a constraint language.

The study of non-uniform CSPs was initiated by the seminal paper of Feder and Vardi
(
17
)
. A major turning point was the introduction of the algebraic approach to CSPs by Jeavons, Cohen, and Gyssens
(
20
;
21
)
and by Bulatov, Jeavons, and Krokhin
(
9
)
. The study of the complexity of non-uniform CSPs has been a major research direction spanning four decades starting from the work by Schaefer
(
25
)
, through many intermediate results
(
11
;
13
;
2
;
19
)
, and finally a complete characterization of the complexity of non-uniform CSPs proved independently by Bulatov
(
14
)
and Zhuk
(
27
)
. Beyond the finite-domain setting, the study of CSPs over infinite domains was initialized by Bodirsky and Nešetřil
(
7
;
8
)

and came to a new layer of complexity and model-theoretic challenges
(
23
)
.

We aim to explore constraint satisfaction problems, in which constraint languages are described by finite automata. Our automata-based formulation of the CSPs naturally extends the classical non-uniform CSPs to the settings with infinite but finitely representable constraint languages. In particular, we initiate a program of studying the dichotomy problem for automata-based CSPs.

In order to put some meat on the bones, we recall the formal definition of the CSP. A
CSP instance
is a triple
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C})
, where
D
is a finite non-empty domain,
V
is a set of variables, and
\mathcal{C}
is a finite set of constraints. Each constraint is a pair
\langle\mathbf{s},R\rangle
, where
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
is a tuple of variables from
V
and
R\subseteq D^{k}
is a
k
-ary relation over
D
. A
solution
of
I
is a function
\varphi:V\to D
such that for each constraint
\langle\mathbf{s},R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}
,
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
, it holds that
(\varphi(s_{1}),\dots,\varphi(s_{k}))\in R
. An alternative view of the instance
I
is the conjunction of formulas of the form
R(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
.

The CSP is NP-complete, as it contains known NP-complete problems. Hence, the problem is often restricted either by imposing conditions on the way the variables interact, or by limiting the relations that appear in constraints. The latter is known as the non-uniform CSP parametrized by a
constraint language

\Gamma
, – a set (finite or infinite) of relations on the domain
D
. The problem
\mathrm{CSP}(\Gamma)
includes all instances in which constraint relations belong to the language
\Gamma
. The standard examples of non-uniform CSPs
include the well-known
Satisfiability
problem, in which the constraint language consists of relations over
\{0,1\}
expressible as clauses; the
Graph
k
-Coloring
problem, which can be translated into a CSP by using the constraint language consisting solely of the disequality relation on a
k
-element set; and
Linear Equations
, which encodes solving systems of linear equations through a CSP whose constraints are collections of solutions of a linear equation. These examples illustrate the overall research goal of delineating hard and easy non-uniform CSPs.

Although the complexity of non-uniform CSPs has been characterized in
(
14
;
27
)
, the algorithms in these papers assume a specific representation of instances. The choice of representation may make a dramatic difference in terms of the running time of algorithms and the complexity of the problems. This especially matters when dealing with infinite constraint languages. Note that the constraint languages in the
Satisfiability
and
Linear Equation
problems are infinite, while in the
Graph
k
-Coloring
problem, it is finite. The standard conventions to represent instances of the CSP are:

–
Uniform CSP representation
: In this case the representation of the instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C})
is
explicit
, that is, it lists all the variables in
V
, all domain elements in
D
, and all tuples that appear in relations that occur in
\mathcal{C}
. Hence, the size of
I
is
|V|+|D|+|\mathcal{C}|
, where
|\mathcal{C}|=\sum_{\langle\mathbf{s},R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}}(|\mathbf{s}|\cdot|R|)
.

–
Non-uniform CSP(
\Gamma
) representation,
\Gamma
is finite
: The language
\Gamma
is a parameter and is not part of input instances. For a given instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C})
,
constraints
\langle\mathbf{s},R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}
in the instance are represented by the symbol
R
and
\mathbf{s}
. Hence, the size of
I
is
|V|+|\mathcal{C}|
,
where
|\mathcal{C}|=\sum_{\langle\mathbf{s},R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}}|\mathbf{s}|
.

–
Non-uniform CSP(
\Gamma
) representation,
\Gamma
is infinite
: It is standard in the literature on non-uniform CSPs to represent relations
explicitly
by a complete list of tuples they contain.

Variations of these conventions are used in some cases. For example, in the
Satisfiability
problem, it suffices to indicate the literals occurring in a clause and their polarities, which is exponentially more concise than listing all the tuples satisfying the clause. In a similar way
Linear Equations
do not require listing all solutions of a linear equation. Both problems admit a representation exponentially more concise than that of the explicit one. Sometimes CSPs over an infinite language are replaced with an ‘equivalent’ CSP over a finite one. For example, rather than considering the
Satisfiability
problem, one considers the
3-Satisfiability
problem. A similar phenomenon occurs in
Linear Equations
, in which case a linear equation is replaced with several linear equations containing at most 3 variables; some auxiliary variables may be added as well.

If a CSP admits some algebraic structure, as in the case of
Linear Equations
, yet another way of representing constraints is possible.

–
Representation by a set of generators
:
This is studied in
(
15
;
16
;
19
)
, where
rather than listing all tuples in a relation, one finds a compact representation of instances via generators.
Constraint languages with such compact representations have an edge polymorphism
(
5
)
. While compact representations help design solution algorithms when the constraints are given explicitly, it is unclear if they can also be helpful to represent constraint relations. Indeed, building on the ideas of Berkholz and Grohe
(
4
)
, an algorithm capable of handling constraints with an edge polymorphism given by their compact representations would improve solving the
Graph Isomorphism
problem.

In this paper we study the complexity of CSPs, in which constraints are represented in some concise way. Clearly, the ultimate concise representation would be by a circuit that tests whether or not a tuple is accepted by a constraint. However, this seems to be too general, as even checking if a constraint accepts any tuple at all is NP-complete. Hence, a more tame framework would clearly be more of interest. In this paper, we propose such a framework based on finite automata. More precisely, we consider the
Automatic Constraint Satisfaction Problem
(AutCSP for short), whose instances are quadruples of the form
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
, where
V
is a set of variables,
D
a domain,
\mathcal{A}
a finite automaton with domain
D
, and
\mathcal{C}
a set of constraints of the form
\langle\mathbf{s},R\rangle
. Here,
\mathbf{s}
is a tuple of variables (of length
|\mathbf{s}|
) and
R=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})\cap D^{|\mathbf{s}|}
, where
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
is the language recognized by
\mathcal{A}
. If the automaton
\mathcal{A}
is fixed, we denote the corresponding problem AutCSP(
\mathcal{A}
). The overall goal is to determine the complexity of AutCSP(
\mathcal{A}
) depending on the automaton
\mathcal{A}
.

We now briefly describe our paper. We define the automatic constraint satisfaction problem, give examples of automatic CSPs,
recast polymorphisms in the
\mathrm{AutCSP}
setting, and prove some of their basic properties. We also introduce automatic constraint languages
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
that contain all the relations admissible in instances of AutCSP(
\mathcal{A}
), and observe that AutCSP(
\mathcal{A}
) is at least as difficult as the problem CSP(
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
), which means that we need to focus on algorithmic results about the AutCSP. Then, to help with this project, we turn to polymorphisms of constraint languages that are exceptionally important in the study of the classical CSP. We design algorithms for automata whose corresponding constraint language has one of a number of types of polymorphisms, such as polymorphisms on the Boolean domain
\{0,1\}
identified in Schaefer’s Dichotomy Theorem
(
25
)
, affine operations
x-y+z
, where
+
and
-
are operations of a finite field, and near-unanimity operations. We also prove that it is polynomial time to recognize, given an automaton
\mathcal{A}
and an operation

, whether or not

is a polymorphism of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
. Furthermore, we discuss width 1 automatic constraint languages. Finally, we prove Schaefer’s Dichotomy Theorem in the setting of
\mathrm{AutCSP}
.
A detailed account of our contributions is in Section
3
after we fully provide the necessary concepts in the next section.

2.
Preliminaries

For instance, in the CSP case, we present some known algorithms such as the 1-minimality algorithm and the decomposition of relations closed under the majority operation into binary relations; in the automata case, we explain finite automata and automatic relations borrowed from the theory of automatic structures. Definition 2.1.

A

The automaton

– The emptiness problem for finite automata, that is finding out if a given automaton accepts at least one string, can be decided in linear time by checking whether any of the accepting states are reachable from any of the initial states.

– Checking if there is a word of a specific length

– The infinity problem of checking if a given automaton accepts infinitely many strings, can be solved in linear time.

An important algebraic concept used in the study of the constraint satisfaction problem is the notion of polymorphism.

Definition 2.12.

In this section, we investigate polymorphisms of automatic constraint languages.
In particular, we design an algorithm that, given a finite family
\mathcal{F}
of operations and a finite automaton
\mathcal{A}
,
decides if at least one of the operations in
\mathcal{F}
is a polymorphism of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}

Let
f:D^{k}\rightarrow D
be an operation. Let us represent the operation

as a
|D|^{k}\times(k+1)

Lemma 4.1.

For any

f_{\omega}

on

Proof.

To prove that
f_{\omega}

(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k},u_{k+1})\in Graph(f_{\omega})

A deterministic finite automaton recognizing the graph of

has two states:
s
and

. The alphabet of the automaton is
D^{k+1}
.
The state

is the start state and
s
is the accepting state. The transitions from
s
to

to

We prove the first part. The second part of the theorem follows from the first part. Consider the automaton

We show that the problem of deciding whether

•

\Gamma\subseteq\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
such that the
CSP(\Gamma)
, and hence
AutCSP(\mathcal{A})
, are NP-complete.
∎

Theorem
4.3
and its corollaries motivate us to give the following definition that we will use in the rest of this paper.

Definition 4.6 (
The class
\mathcal{K}_{f}
).

Let
f:D^{k}\to D
be an operation. Define the
following class of automata:

\mathcal{K}_{f}\;=\;\{\mathcal{A}\mid\text{The language $\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}$ has $f$ as its polymorphism}\}.

5.
Automatic CSPs over Boolean domain

In this section, we investigate two cases of
the
\mathrm{AutCSP}
over the Boolean domain
D=\{0,1\}
.
The first case concerns the
\mathrm{AutCSP}
for instances
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
,
where
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{\land}
or
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{\lor}
.
The second case focuses on the
\mathrm{AutCSP}
for instances
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
,
where
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{Minor}
. By Theorem
4.3
, given a finite automaton
\mathcal{A}
, we can decide in polynomial time if
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{f}
for each
f\in\{\land,\lor,Minor\}
.

5.1.
The case of
\land
and
\vee
polymorphisms

We investigate
\mathrm{AutCSP}
instances
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
where
D=\{0,1\}
and
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{\land}
. The case of
\vee
is analogous. We show how the automaton
\mathcal{A}
can be used to derive a polynomial-time decision algorithm for the
\mathrm{AutCSP}
instance
I
.
We introduce the core combinatorial structure and the technical ideas in a clean way.
The next section extends these ideas to the class of automatic constraint languages of width 1.
Since tuples are functions, we will use a partial function to describe a tuple with some "pattern":

Definition 5.1.

A partial function
\tau:[n]\rightharpoonup D
is a
pattern
.
A function
\psi:[n]\rightarrow D

extends

\tau
with respect to an
n
-ary relation
R_{n}
on
D
if
\psi(i)=\tau(i)
for all
i\in dom(\tau)
and
(\psi(1),\dots,\psi(n))\in R_{n}
.
A pattern
\tau
is
extendable
if it has at least one extension.

Lemma 5.2.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(S,q_{0},\Delta,F)
be a finite automaton over the alphabet
D
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\tau:[n]\rightharpoonup D
be a pattern. Then the extendability of
\tau
can be checked in time
O(n\,|\mathcal{A}|)
.

Proof.

The extendability can be tested via a BFS on
\mathcal{A}
to depth
n
: at each position
i
, follow
\Delta(s_{i-1},\tau(i))
if
i\in\mathrm{dom}(\tau)
; otherwise, any outgoing transition may be used. ∎

Patterns represent partial solutions to a given instance. For a given pattern our task is to extend the pattern to a full solution.

Theorem 5.3.

There exists a polynomial-time algorithm that, given
an
\mathrm{AutCSP}
instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
where
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{\wedge}
, decides if the instance
I
has a solution. The algorithm runs in time
O(|V|\,|\mathcal{C}|^{2}\,|\mathcal{A}|)
where
|\mathcal{C}|=\sum_{\langle\mathbf{s},R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}}|\mathbf{s}|
.

Our proof proceeds in three steps:
First, we compute the minimal elements of
\land
-closed automatic relations (Lemma
5.4
). Second, we solve single-constraint instances (Lemma
5.5
). Finally, we extend the approach to instances with multiple constraints.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{\land}
be a finite automaton over
D=\{0,1\}
.
Recall that
R_{n}=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})\cap D^{n}
.
Since
R_{n}
is
\land
-closed, we introduce a partial order on
R_{n}
by declaring that
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
if and only if
r_{1}\land r_{2}=r_{1}
.

In particular,
R_{n}
has a unique minimal element
\bigwedge_{r\in R_{n}}r
, i.e., the tuple in
R_{n}
with the maximum number of zeros.
Explicitly computing
\bigwedge_{r\in R_{n}}r
may take exponential time. To address this, we introduce the concept of minimal extension, which allows us to find this minimal element efficiently.

Formally, given an extendable pattern
\tau:[n]\rightharpoonup D
, a tuple
r\in R_{n}
is called a
minimal extension
of
\tau
if
r
extends
\tau
and, for every other extension
r^{\prime}\in R_{n}
, we have
r\leq r^{\prime}
.

Lemma 5.4.

There exists an algorithm that, given a finite automaton
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{\land}
and a pattern
\tau:[n]\rightharpoonup D
, decides whether
\tau
is extendable and computes the minimal extension if such an extension exists. The algorithm runs in time
O(n^{2}|\mathcal{A}|)
.

Proof.

To compute the minimal extension of
\tau
, we start from any valid extension
v_{0}
that can be found using Lemma
5.2
and iterate through positions
i=1,\dots,n
.
Whenever
i\notin\mathrm{dom}(\tau)
and
v_{i-1}(i)=1
, we temporarily fix
\tau(i)=0
and test extendability again.
If extendable, we denote by
m_{i}
the new extension and update
v_{i}=v_{i-1}\land m_{i}
; otherwise, we keep
v_{i}=v_{i-1}
.

The resulting sequence
\{v_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
is a non-increasing sequence with respect to
\leq
. Hence, the sequence stabilizes at the minimal element consistent with
\tau
.
Thus, the final tuple
v_{n}
is the minimal extension.
At most
n
extendability tests are performed, each requiring
O(n|\mathcal{A}|)
time, so the overall complexity is
O(n^{2}|\mathcal{A}|)
.
In particular, when
\tau=\emptyset
we get the minimal element of
R_{n}
.
∎

We next compute minimal extensions for one constraint:

Lemma 5.5.

There is an algorithm running in time
O(|V|\,n^{2}\,|\mathcal{A}|)
that, given
an automaton
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{\land}
,
a constraint
C=\langle\mathbf{s},R_{n}\rangle
, and
a pattern

\tau:[n]\rightharpoonup D
,
decides if
\tau
can be extended to a tuple satisfying
C
,
and, if so, computes the minimal satisfying extension of
\tau
.

Proof.

Lemma
5.4
allows one to find a minimal extension of
\tau
in
R_{n}
. However, this extension may not satisfy the constraint
\langle\mathbf{s},R_{n}\rangle
, as some of the entries of
\mathbf{s}
may be equal. We describe a simple iterative procedure and argue correctness and complexity.

(1)

Using Lemma
5.4
, compute the minimal extension
r\in R_{n}
of the current
\tau
since
R_{n}
is closed under
\wedge
. If none exists, return "no extension".

(2)

If the relation
\{(s_{i},r_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
defines a consistent assignment to variables (i.e., it is a function
s_{i}\mapsto r_{i}
), then
r
is a satisfying tuple for
C
; output the induced extension, and stop.

(3)

Otherwise, there is a variable
v\in V
appearing at positions
i,j
with
r_{i}\neq r_{j}
. By minimality of
r
, any satisfying tuple must assign
v=1
. Fix all occurrences of
v
in the constraint to
1
by extending
\tau
accordingly, and go back to Step (1).

Each iteration either finds a minimal satisfying extension or fixes a variable that must be assigned
1
, so the algorithm is correct. Each iteration uses Lemma
5.4
once, and at most
|V|
iterations occur, giving total time
O(|V|\,n^{2}\,|\mathcal{A}|)
. Note that we used the polymorphism
\wedge
in the algorithm above through Lemma
5.4
.
∎

Similarly, setting
\tau=\emptyset
in Lemma
5.5
yields a solution to the single-constraint
\mathrm{AutCSP}
instances.

Proof of Theorem
5.3
.

We extend the approach above to handle instances with multiple constraints

\mathcal{C}=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{m}\}
, where each
C_{i}=\langle\mathbf{s}_{i},R_{k_{i}}\rangle
,
which leads to a polynomial-time algorithm as stated in Theorem
5.3
.

(1)

Initialize every partial assignment
\tau_{i}=\emptyset
.

(2)

For each
C_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
, compute its minimal consistent tuple
e_{i}
using Lemma
5.5
and let
\varphi_{i}
be the corresponding function. If
\varphi=\bigcup_{i}\varphi_{i}
is consistent, output
\varphi
as a solution.

(3)

If
\varphi
is inconsistent, find the first variable
v
witnessing inconsistency.
For each
C_{i}
containing
v
, extend
\tau_{i}
by fixing all coordinates of
v
in
\mathbf{s}_{i}
to
1
.

(4)

For each
C_{i}
, recompute the consistent minimal
e_{i}^{\prime}
under the new
\tau_{i}
.
If some
C_{i}
becomes unsatisfiable, output "no solution"; otherwise, return to step (2).

Each iteration fixes at least one variable, so the number of iterations is at most
|V|
.
Within each iteration, Lemma
5.4
is called for all constraints, taking time
O(|\mathbf{s}_{i}|^{2}|\mathcal{A}|)
per constraint.
Therefore, the total runtime is
O(|V|\,|\mathcal{C}|^{2}\,|\mathcal{A}|)
.
∎

5.2.
The case of minority polymorphism

We assume that the operation
Minor
is a polymorphism of the automatic constraint language
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
over the Boolean domain.
For this section, we expand the group
G_{D}=(\{0,1\},\oplus)
to the two-element field
GF(2)\cong(\{0,1\},\oplus,\land)
.

We prove that any
n
-ary relation
R\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
can be identified with an equivalent system of linear equations
Mx=b
, that is,
r\in R
if and only if
Mr^{\intercal}=b
. While this correspondence is well understood, we show how to extract the matrix
M
from the automaton
\mathcal{A}
only.

It is well-known that
R\subseteq D^{n}
admits
Minor
as its polymorphism if and only if
R
is a coset of some subgroup
S\leq G_{D}^{n}
. Moreover,
G^{n}_{D}
is a vector space over the field
GF(2)
. Hence,
S
is a subspace of the space
G^{n}_{D}
.
The subspace
S
is a kernel of some matrix
M
, that is
M\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}=0
iff
\mathbf{s}\in S
. In such a representation,
R
is equivalent to the set of solutions to the linear equation
Mx=b
for some vector
b
.

Theorem 5.6.

There exists an algorithm that, given an automaton
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{Minor}
over the Boolean domain
D
and an integer
n
, constructs in time
O(n^{3}|\mathcal{A}|)
a system of linear equations
Mx=b
such that
r\in R_{n}
if and only if
Mr^{\intercal}=b
.

Proof.

We fix a tuple
r\in R
and set
S=R-r
, a linear subspace of
D^{n}
of dimension
m
. We construct a basis
\mathcal{B}
of
S
in
m
steps via a strictly increasing chain of bases
\mathcal{B}_{1}\subsetneq\mathcal{B}_{2}\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq\mathcal{B}_{m}=\mathcal{B}
.
Once
\mathcal{B}
is constructed, choose a maximal set of linearly independent row vectors

\{\alpha\in D^{1\times n}:\forall b\in\mathcal{B}(\alpha\cdot b^{\intercal}=0)\}
, and let
M
be the matrix whose rows are these vectors. Then
\ker M=\mathrm{span}\,\mathcal{B}=S
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{0}=\emptyset
. At step
t(t\geq 1)
, given
\mathcal{B}_{t-1}=\{b_{k}:1\leq k\leq t-1\}
, we seek
b_{t}\in S
such that
\mathcal{B}_{t}=\mathcal{B}_{t-1}\cup\{b_{t}\}
is a basis of size
t
.

To that end, we normalize the matrix
\mathcal{B}_{t-1}
to a row canonical form. Let
L_{t-1}\subseteq[n]
be the
columns containing the leading
1

b_{t}
exists iff there exists a non-zero
b_{t}\in S
such that
b_{t}(k)=0
for all
k\in L_{t-1}
.
Given
L_{t-1}
, we can detect such a candidate by testing the extendability of the following patterns. For each column
p\in[n]\setminus L_{t-1}
, define a pattern
\phi_{p,L_{t-1}}:[n]\rightharpoonup\{0,1\}
:

\phi_{p,L_{t-1}}(k)=\begin{cases}r(k)\oplus 1,k=p\\
r(k),k\in L_{t-1}\\
\text{undefined},\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

There is an
x\in R
extending
\phi_{p,L_{t-1}}
iff
b=x-r\in S
satisfies
b(k)=0
for all
k\in L_{t-1}
and
b(p)=1
, in which case we set

.

The process is summarized in Algorithm

We now analyze the running time of this algorithm. The initialization takes time

to find a fixed vector

. The algorithm would iterate at most
n
times. During each iteration, we test the extendability of each pattern
\phi_{p,L_{t-1}}
on
\mathcal{A}
and it takes time
O(n^{2}|\mathcal{A}|)
in total. Therefore, the kernel

can be constructed in time
O(n^{3}|\mathcal{A}|)
for any relation
R\subseteq D^{n}
.
This concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Basis Construction

Theorem
5.6
shows that any automatic relation with the
Minor
polymorphism can be translated into an equivalent system of linear equations over
GF(2)
.
This leads directly to a polynomial-time algorithm for solving
\mathrm{AutCSP}
by reducing it to solving a single global system of linear equations.

Theorem 5.7.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that, given
an
\mathrm{AutCSP}
instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
where
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{Minor}
, decides if the instance
I
has a solution. The algorithm runs in time
O(|\mathcal{C}|^{3}|\mathcal{A}|+|V|^{3})
where
|\mathcal{C}|=\sum_{\langle\mathbf{s},R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}}|\mathbf{s}|
.

Proof.

For each constraint
\langle(v_{j_{1}},\ldots,v_{j_{n_{i}}}),R_{n_{i}}\rangle
,
by Theorem
5.6
,
we can build an equivalent system of linear equations
M_{i}x_{\mathbf{s}_{i}}=b_{i}
,
where
x_{\mathbf{s}_{i}}=(x_{v_{j_{1}}},\ldots,x_{v_{j_{n_{i}}}})^{\intercal}
. The matrix

is over the field

, and every equation in
M_{i}x_{\mathbf{s}_{i}}=b_{i}
cannot contain a variable more than once. Therefore, the length of equation is bounded by
|V|
.

All constraints

are combined into a global system

of linear equations, where

is the vector of all variables, and

and

are obtained by vertically concatenating all local matrices

Thus, a function

iff the corresponding Boolean vector

.
Each local translation takes

. Hence, the overall procedure runs in time
O(|\mathcal{C}|^{3}|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{C}||V|^{2})
.
∎

6.
Width 1 Automatic Constraint Languages

In this section, we focus on the CSP over automatic constraint languages of width
1
. So, let us fix a finite domain
D
, a finite automaton
\mathcal{A}=(S,q_{0},\Delta,F)
over
D
, and the language
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
.
It is known that languages closed under a semilattice operation have width
1
.

6.1.
Pattern-Driven Search

An important ingredient in finding a solution to a given constraint is a process that refines a given partial solution. We need to recast this process in automata-theoretic setting.

The notion of a pattern in Definition
5.1
is single-valued and partial, which is sufficient in the Boolean setting: each position either receives a fixed value from
\{0,1\}
or remains undefined, and our algorithm in Subsection
5.1
extends such partial assignments until a full solution is obtained.
However, this framework breaks down for width-
1
constraint languages over arbitrary finite domains.
In these settings positions may need to carry sets of possible values rather than a single fixed value. In addition, the algorithm in this section starts with the full domain
D
at every position and enforces consistency by shrinking these sets rather than extending a single-valued partial assignment as in the Boolean case.
This key difference motivates the notion of a set-valued pattern.

Definition 6.1.

Given a domain
D
, a
set-valued pattern
is a function
\phi:[n]\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(D)
.

Given a set-valued pattern
\phi
, we want to find a tuple
t
such that

t\in R_{n}\cap\phi(1)\times\ldots\times\phi(n)
. Finding such a tuple
can be exponential on
n
. The next lemma shows that exponential search is not necessary for automatic constraint languages. A proof is in Appendix
C
.

Lemma 6.2.

There exists an algorithm that, for a given pattern
\phi:[n]\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(D)
and an automaton
\mathcal{A}
, decides if there exists a tuple
t\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})\cap\prod_{i=1}^{n}\phi(i)
and, if so, constructs such a tuple
t
. The algorithm runs in time
O(n|\mathcal{A}|)
.
∎

6.2.
1-minimality in classical setting

We introduce the
1-minimality
concept and then define constraint languages of
width 1
. For an instance
I
of
\mathrm{CSP}(\Gamma_{H})
, the 1-minimality concept mimics the termination of the unit propagation process in
Horn-3-SAT
as presented below.

Example 6.3 (
Horn-3-SAT
).

The language for
Horn-3-SAT
is
\Gamma_{H}=\{S_{110},S_{111},C_{1}\}
, where
S_{xyz}=\{0,1\}^{3}\setminus\{(x,y,z)\}
and
C_{1}=\{1\}
.
The unit propagation procedure applied to any instance
I=(V,\{0,1\},\mathcal{C})\in\mathrm{CSP}(\Gamma_{H})
proceeds as follows:

(1)

For each
x\in V
, introduce the unary
domain
constraint
P_{x}\subseteq\{0,1\}
. Initially, set
P_{x}=\{0,1\}
.

(2)

For all
\langle\mathbf{s},R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}
(all constraints are at most ternary) and for all
s_{i}\in\mathbf{s}
, update
P_{s_{i}}
to
P_{s_{i}}^{\prime}
if
P_{s_{i}}^{\prime}\subsetneq P_{s_{i}}
, where

P_{s_{i}}^{\prime}=\pi_{i}\{t\in R:\pi_{j}t\in P_{s_{j}}\text{ for all }j\in[k]\}.

(3)

Return to Step (
2
) if any
s_{i}
is updated; else
terminate.

The unit propagation corresponds to step (
2
). When the algorithm terminates, there are only two possibilities:

•

If

P_{x}=\emptyset
for some
x
, then
I
is unsatisfiable.

•

Else,
I
has the solution
\psi:V\rightarrow\{0,1\}
:

\psi(x)=\begin{cases}a,\text{ if }P_{x}=\{a\}\\
0,\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

The notion of 1-minimality is based on the example above.

Definition 6.4 (
1
-minimality).

A CSP instance
I
is
1
-minimal
if for all

x\in V
it contains unary constraint
P_{x}
, and for all constraints
\langle\mathbf{s},R_{i}\rangle
of
I
and all
x\in\mathbf{s}
, we have
\pi_{x}(R_{i}\cap\prod_{v\in\mathbf{s}}P_{v})=P_{x}
.

For completeness, we provide the following known theorem since we need to recast it in the automata-theoretic setting:

Theorem 6.5.

(
12
;
3
)

There exists an algorithm that transforms every instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C})
to an
equivalent

1
-minimal instance

. The algorithm runs in time
O(|V||D|mr^{2})
, where

r
is the maximal arity of any constraint relation in
\mathcal{C}
, and

is the total number of tuples across all constraint relations.

Proof.

Below we present the desired algorithm known as the

:

(1)

Initialize
P_{x}=D
for all

.

(2)

For each constraint
\langle(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}),R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}
and

, set

If

, then let

.

(3)

If no set

where

, is updated, then output

.
∎

Remark 2.

I
, produces a 1-minimal CSP instance
I^{\prime}

that is equivalent to
I
. Because
I^{\prime}
is equivalent to
I
, it is clear that if some
P_{x}=\emptyset
in
I^{\prime}
, then
I
has no solution since
I^{\prime}
does not. In this case we say that the 1-minimality algorithm
refutes
the instance
I
. However, the converse is not always true.

Example 6.6.

Consider the
2
-coloring
\mathrm{CSP}(\neq_{2})
. The instance
I_{K_{3}}=(\{x,y,z\},\{0,1\},\{x\neq_{2}y,y\neq_{2}z,z\neq_{2}x\})
is unsatisfiable because
K_{3}
is not
2
-colorable. However, the
1
-minimality algorithm cannot update any domain constraint and returns the trivial
1
-minimal instance with
P_{v}=\{0,1\}
for all
v\in\{x,y,z\}
.

Now we single out the languages for which the 1-minimality algorithm provides a solution to the CSP instances:

Definition 6.7 (Width 1).

(
1
;
3
)

We say that a constraint language is of
width 1
if for any unsatisfiable instance
I
of the language the
1
-minimality algorithm always refutes
I
.

6.3.
1-minimality in automata setting

Inessential expansions:
Before we start this section, we slightly expand the definition of
\mathrm{AutCSP}
.
Consider the language
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is an automaton over
D
. Let

\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{k}\}
be a fixed set of relations on
D
.
The CSP instances over the language
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{Q})=\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}\cup\mathcal{Q}
can still be considered as instances of
\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
because the set
\mathcal{Q}
is fixed, and the representations of the relations
Q_{1}
,
\ldots
,
Q_{k}
can be given a priori.
Hence, we call the language

\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{Q})=\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}\cup\mathcal{Q}
an

inessential expansion of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, and refer to the CSP instances over
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{Q})
as the
\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
instances. This convention is assumed for the rest of this section.

The goal of this subsection is twofold. First, we prove Theorem
6.5
in the setting of automatic constraint languages
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
.
The key here is that the
1
-minimality algorithm in Theorem
6.5
iterates over all possible tuples in the constraint relations as the CSP instances in the theorem are given explicitly.
This might blow up the running time exponentially in the size of the representations of the
\mathrm{AutCSP}
constraints. To avoid this, we show an alternative way to establish
1
-minimality for any
\mathrm{AutCSP}
instance. Second, we show that the satisfiability of an instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
can be decided in polynomial time if
the constraint language
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
has width
1
.

The core of the
1
-minimality algorithm
is finding domains of individual variables.
Hence, we first handle projections in the setting of automatic constraint languages
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
.

Lemma 6.8.

There exists a polynomial-time algorithm that, given
an automaton
\mathcal{A}
over a finite domain
D
,
a constraint
\langle(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}),R_{k}\rangle
, the domain constraints
P_{s_{1}}
,
\ldots
,
P_{s_{k}}
, and
i\in[k]
, computes
the projection
\pi_{i}(R_{k}\cap\prod_{j\in[k]}P_{s_{j}})
.
The algorithm runs in time
O(k|D||\mathcal{A}|)
.

Proof.

Note that
\pi_{i}(R_{k}\cap\prod_{j\in[k]}P_{s_{j}})\subseteq P_{s_{i}}
. Since
P_{s_{i}}\subseteq D
, it suffices to check for every element
d\in P_{s_{i}}
the membership of the element
d
in the set
\pi_{i}(R_{k}\cap\prod_{j\in[k]}P_{s_{j}})
. In the 1-minimality algorithm, this is done by running through every tuple
t\in R_{k}
. In our case, we invoke the procedure from Lemma
6.2
. We construct the following pattern

\phi(j)=\begin{cases}\{d\},\text{ if }j=i\\
P_{s_{j}},\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

By using the procedure from Lemma
6.2
with parameters
\mathcal{A}
and
\phi
, one can find such a desired tuple if it exists. Note that we iterate over all elements in the set
P_{s_{i}}
, and each iteration requires
O(k|\mathcal{A}|)
time to answer the membership problem with pattern
\phi
. Thus, it takes time
O(k|P_{s_{i}}||\mathcal{A}|)=O(k|D||\mathcal{A}|)
to compute the full projection.
∎

With Lemma
6.8
in hand, we now show that the
1
-minimality algorithm runs in polynomial time for automatic constraint languages.
The theorem builds an inessential expansion of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
.

Theorem 6.9.

There exists an algorithm that, given an
\mathrm{AutCSP}
instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
, constructs an equivalent

1
-minimal
\mathrm{AutCSP}
instance
I^{\prime}=(V,D,\mathcal{C}\cup\{P_{x}:x\in V\},\mathcal{A})
in time

O(|V||D|^{2}|\mathcal{C}|^{2}|\mathcal{A}|)
.

Proof.

Our algorithm follows the same lines as the 1-minimality algorithm in Theorem
6.5
, where the projection procedure uses the process from Lemma
6.8
:

(1)

Initialize
P_{x}=D
for all
x\in V
.

(2)

For each constraint
\langle(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}),R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}
and each
i\in[k]
, compute

P^{\prime}_{s_{i}}=\pi_{i}\big(R\cap\textstyle\prod_{j\in[k]}P_{s_{j}}\big)

using Lemma
6.8
.

If
P^{\prime}_{s_{i}}\subsetneq P_{s_{i}}
, replace
P_{s_{i}}
with
P^{\prime}_{s_{i}}
.

(3)

If some
P_{x}
is updated, then return to Step (
2
); else, output

I^{\prime}=(V,D,\mathcal{C}\cup\{P_{x}:x\in V\},\mathcal{A})
.

The algorithm produces a sequence
I_{0}
,
I_{1}
,
\ldots
,
I_{w}
of instances while preserving equivalence throughout its execution.
Since each
P_{x}
can shrink at most
|D|
times, the total number of updates is bounded by
O(|V||D|)
. In addition, for each constraint
\langle\mathbf{s},R_{i}\rangle
, Lemma
6.8
is applied
|\mathbf{s}|
times, giving a total cost of
O(|\mathbf{s}|^{2}|D||\mathcal{A}|)
.
Processing all constraints in one update step costs
O(|\mathcal{C}|^{2}|D||\mathcal{A}|)
where
|\mathcal{C}|=\sum_{\langle\mathbf{s},R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}}|\mathbf{s}|
.
Multiplying the
O(|V||D|)
bound on the number of updates gives the runtime

O(|V||D|^{2}|\mathcal{C}|^{2}|\mathcal{A}|)
.
∎

Note that the instance
I^{\prime}
in the theorem is over an inessential expansion of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
. As an immediate corollary, we get the following:

Theorem 6.10.

There exists an algorithm, which on any instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
of width
1
language
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, decides the satisfiability of
I
in time
O(|V||D|^{2}|\mathcal{C}|^{2}|\mathcal{A}|)
. ∎

We now apply Theorem
6.10

to automatic constraint languages
that admit a semilattice operation, say
\wedge,
as polymorphisms.
Then after enforcing
1
-minimality, an automatic CSP instance is satisfiable precisely when every unary domain is nonempty.

Theorem 6.11.

Let
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
be an
\mathrm{AutCSP}
instance such that
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{\wedge}
, and let
I^{\prime}=(V,D,\mathcal{C}\cup\{P_{x}:x\in V\},\mathcal{A})
be an equivalent
1
-minimal
\mathrm{AutCSP}
instance.
Then
I
is satisfiable if and only if every unary domain constraint
P_{x}
in
I^{\prime}
is nonempty.

Proof.

Let us fix
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{\wedge}
.
If some
P_{x}=\emptyset
, then variable
x
has no admissible value in the
1
-minimal instance
I^{\prime}
, and then
I
is unsatisfiable.
Now assume that
P_{x}\neq\emptyset
for all
x\in V
. For each
x\in V
, define
a_{x}=\bigwedge P_{x}
, the meet of all elements in
P_{x}
under the semilattice operation. Then the function
x\mapsto a_{x}
satisfies every constraint of the instance
I^{\prime}
. Indeed, with this function, each of the constraints
\langle(s_{1},\dots,s_{k}),R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}
is satisfied by the tuple
(a_{s_{1}},\dots,a_{s_{k}})
, since
\wedge
is a polymorphism of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
.
∎

For this theorem, it is important that in the inessential expansion of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}

by
\{P_{x}:x\in V\}
the predicates
P_{x}
are closed under
\wedge
.

Corollary 6.12.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that, given
an
\mathrm{AutCSP}
instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
where
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{\wedge}
, decides if the instance
I
has a solution.
∎

7.
Languages with a majority polymorphism

In this section, we investigate the class of automata associated with a majority (near-unanimity) polymorphism. Such a class contains many problems (e.g.
2-SAT
) where the
1
-minimality algorithm in the classical setting is not enough to solve them.

7.1.
Majority polymorphism

We start with the following definition:

Definition 7.1.

An operation
g:D^{3}\rightarrow D
is a
majority operation
if for
all
x,y\in D
,
g(x,y,y)=g(y,x,y)=g(y,y,x)=y
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an automaton over
D
such that the language
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
has a majority polymorphism
g
.
For the operation
g
on
D
, consider the set
\mathrm{Inv}(g)
as in Definition

2.13
.
It is well-known that
\mathrm{CSP}(\mathrm{Inv}(g))
is decidable in polynomial time
(
2
;
3
)
. Our goal is to address this result for
\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
. To do so, we start with the following lemma.

Lemma 7.2.

Let
R\in\mathrm{Inv}(g)
and
R\subseteq D^{n}
. Then for all
r\in D^{n}
we have
r\in R
if and only if
\pi_{\{i,j\}}r\in\pi_{\{i,j\}}R
for any
i,j\in[n]
,
i\neq j
.

Proof.

The proof in the ‘only if’ direction is obvious.
We prove the other direction. Let
r=(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},\ldots,r_{n})
be a tuple such that for all distinct
i,j\in[n]
we have
(r_{i},r_{j})\in\pi_{\{i,j\}}R
. We want to show that
r\in R
. We use induction on the cardinality of
U\subseteq[n]
, where
|U|\geq 2
, and prove that
\pi_{U}r\in\pi_{U}R
.

•

Base case
: This is clear when
|U|=2
.

•

Inductive step
: Let
W=\{w_{i}:1\leq i\leq k+1\}\subseteq[n]
be a subset with
k+1
elements. Consider the following three
k
element sets of coordinates:
U_{i}=W\setminus\{w_{i}\}
, where
i=1,2,3
. By induction hypothesis, for each
U_{i}
there is a tuple
a_{i}\in R
such that
\pi_{U_{i}}a_{i}=\pi_{U_{i}}r
. Let us write

a_{i}=(a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,n})
. Note that for all
\ell\in U_{i}
,
r_{\ell}=a_{i,\ell}
. Since
g
is a polymorphism,
g(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\in R
.
For
b=g({a}_{1},{a}_{2},{a}_{3})
we have

b(i)=g(a_{1,i},a_{2,i},a_{3,i})=g(r_{i},r_{i},r_{i})=r_{i}
,
when
i\in U_{1}\cap U_{2}\cap U_{3}
; then, as
g
is a majority operation, we have

b(w_{1})=g(a_{1,w_{1}},a_{2,w_{1}},a_{3,w_{2}})=g(a_{1,w_{1}},r_{w_{1}},r_{w_{1}})=r_{w_{1}}
.
Similarly
b(w_{2})=r_{w_{2}},b(w_{3})=r_{w_{3}}
.
Since
g
is a polymorphism of the relation
R
, we conclude that
b
is the desired tuple, that is,
\pi_{W}b=\pi_{W}r
.

We proved the lemma.
∎

Lemma
7.2
implies the following. Let
R
be an
n
-ary relation that admits the majority operation
g
as a polymorphism. For each
i,j\in[n]
,
i<j
, introduce a binary predicate
R_{i,j}
defined by
\pi_{\{i,j\}}R
.
Then the relations
R_{i,j}
determine
R
in the following sense:

Corollary 7.3.

Let
R\subseteq D^{n}
be a relation invariant under
g
. For any tuple
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in D^{n}
, the following is true:

R(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\Longleftrightarrow\bigwedge_{1\leq i<j\leq n}R_{i,j}(a_{i},a_{j})

Assume that
R_{n}\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
. For a pair
(a,b)\in D
and the relation
R_{i,j}
, let us consider a partial function
\tau:[n]\rightharpoonup D
such that
\tau(i)=a
,
\tau(j)=b
, and
\tau(k)
is undefined for all other
k\in[n]
. By Lemma
6.2
, we can find if the partial function
\tau
has an extension in
R
in time
O(n|\mathcal{A}|)
. This implies that we can compute the relation
R_{i,j}
in time
O(n|\mathcal{A}||D|^{2})
by trying every possible pair
(a,b)\in D^{2}
. Hence:

Corollary 7.4.

There exists an algorithm that,
given a finite automaton
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{g}
and an integer
n
, outputs the relations
R_{i,j}
such that
for all
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in D^{n}
we have the following:

R_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\Longleftrightarrow\bigwedge_{i<j\leq n}R_{i,j}(a_{i},a_{j})
.

The algorithm runs in time
O(n^{3}|D|^{2}||\mathcal{A}|)
. ∎

Theorem 7.5.

There exists an algorithm that, given an
\mathrm{AutCSP}
instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
where
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{g}
, constructs a CSP instance
I^{\prime}=(V,D,\{\langle(x,y),P_{xy}\rangle:x,y\in V\})
such that

(1)

Each
P_{xy}\subseteq D^{2}
belongs to
\mathrm{Inv}(g)
.

(2)

A function
\psi:V\rightarrow D
satisfies
I
iff
\psi
satisfies
I^{\prime}
.

(3)

The algorithm runs in time
O(|\mathcal{C}|^{3}|\mathcal{A}||D|^{2})
.

Proof.

For each
x,y\in V
,
P_{xy}
is defined as follows:

P_{xy}=\bigcap_{\langle(s_{1},\dots,s_{k}),R\rangle\in\mathcal{C},\;s_{i}=x,\;s_{j}=y,\;i\neq j}R_{i,j}.

For the first part, clearly
P_{xy}\in Inv(g)
. For the second part, let
\psi:V\rightarrow D
be a function. By Corollary
7.3
, we have the following:

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\langle\mathbf{s},R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}}\psi(\mathbf{s})\in R\iff\bigwedge_{\langle\mathbf{s},R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}}\bigwedge_{(s_{i},s_{j})\in\mathbf{s}^{2}}(\psi(s_{i}),\psi(s_{j}))\in R_{i,j}

\displaystyle\iff\bigwedge_{(x,y)\in V^{2}}\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}\langle(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}),R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}\\
s_{i}=x,s_{j}=y\end{subarray}}(\psi(x),\psi(y))\in R_{i,j}

For each constraint
\langle(s_{1},\dots,s_{k}),R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}

and each distinct pair
(i,j)\in[k]^{2}
,
we compute
R_{i,j}\subseteq D^{2}
by enumerating all
(a,b)\in D^{2}

and testing membership using Corollary
7.4
.
Each projection
costs
O(k|D|^{2}|\mathcal{A}|)
time,
and there are
O(k^{2})
such pairs per constraint,
giving a total per-constraint cost of
O(k^{3}|\mathcal{A}||D|^{2})
.
Iterating over all constraints gives overall complexity
O(|\mathcal{C}|^{3}|\mathcal{A}||D|^{2})
.
Thus,
I^{\prime}
can be constructed in polynomial time and is equivalent to
I
.
∎

Since the constraints in the instance
I^{\prime}
in Theorem
7.6
are binary, we can use the explicit representation of these constraints:

Theorem 7.6.

(
1
)

There is an algorithm that, given a binary CSP instance
I=(V,D,\{\langle(x,y),P_{x,y}\rangle:x,y\in V\})
where all
P_{x,y}\subseteq D^{2}
are in
\mathrm{Inv}(g)
, decides the satisfiability of
I
in time
O(|V|^{3}|D|^{3})
.

Proof.

Iteratively prune the relations
P_{x,y}
as follows: for every triple
(x,y,z)
and every pair
(a,b)\in P_{x,y}
, check whether there exists
c\in D
such that
(a,c)\in P_{x,z}
and
(b,c)\in P_{y,z}
. If no such
c
exists, delete
(a,b)
from
P_{x,y}
. If any
P_{x,y}
becomes empty, report “unsatisfiable”; otherwise, after no more deletions are possible, report “satisfiable”. This process examines all triples and all values, taking time
O(|V|^{3}|D|^{3})
. Its correctness is proved in Appendix
E
.
∎

Thus, we obtain the following corollary:

Corollary 7.7.

There exists a polynomial-time algorithm that, given
an
\mathrm{AutCSP}
instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
where
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{K}_{g}
, decides if the instance
I
has a solution. The algorithm runs in time
O(|\mathcal{C}|^{3}|\mathcal{A}||D|^{2}+|V|^{3}|D|^{3})
where
|\mathcal{C}|=\sum_{\langle\mathbf{s},R\rangle\in\mathcal{C}}|\mathbf{s}|
. ∎

Second, Theorem

The proof is in Appendix

. Finally, we invoke the known fact that

)

Let

8.
Dichotomy for Boolean AutCSPs

We now combine the results above to obtain a full complexity dichotomy for AutCSPs over the Boolean domain.

Theorem 8.1.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an automaton over the Boolean domain
\{0,1\}
.
Then
\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})\in P
if and only if
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
admits one of the following polymorphisms:
\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{1}
,
\land
,
\lor
,

Maj
,
Minor
. Otherwise the
\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
is NP-complete.

Proof.

Assume that the automatic constraint language
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
admits none of the listed operations
\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{1}
,
\land
,
\lor
,

Maj
, and
Minor
as its polymorphisms.
For each of the operations
f
from the set
\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1},\land,\lor,Maj,Minor\}
build an automaton
\mathcal{A}_{f}
from Theorem
4.3
. Let us assume, for simplicity, that
f
is either
\land
or
\lor
. Since
f
is not a polymorphism of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, there is a triple
of strings
(x_{f},y_{f},z_{f})
accepted by
\mathcal{A}_{f}
. Hence,
x_{f},y_{f}
are accepted by
\mathcal{A}
, but
f(x_{f},y_{f})=z_{f}
is not accepted by
\mathcal{A}
.
Let
n_{f}=|x_{f}|
and
R_{n_{f}}\;=\;\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})\cap D^{n_{f}}
.
Hence, the collection of relations

\Gamma^{\prime}\;=\;\{\,R_{n_{f}}\mid f\in\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1},\land,\lor,Maj,Minor\}\,\}

forms a finite language over the Boolean domain that admits none of
the listed operations as its polymorphisms.
By Schaefer’s Dichotomy Theorem
(
25
)
,

\mathrm{CSP}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
is NP-complete. Indeed, given any instance

I=(V,D,\{\langle s_{1},R_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle s_{m},R_{m}\rangle\})

of
\mathrm{CSP}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
,
we construct the instance

I^{\prime}=(V,D,\{\langle s_{1},R_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle s_{m},R_{m}\rangle\})\in\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
in linear time.
Clearly
I
is satisfiable if and only if
I^{\prime}
is satisfiable.
Thus
\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
is NP-hard. By Proposition
2.10
,
\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
is in NP. Hence,
\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
is NP-complete.

If
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
admits one of the listed automatic polymorphisms, then tractability follows from the corresponding results:
semilattice operations (
\land
,
\lor
) by Theorem
5.3
;

Minor
by Theorem
5.6
;

Maj
by Theorem
7.5
;
and the unary operations
\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{1}
by trivial evaluation of constant assignments.
Hence
\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
is in P.
∎

Now combine Theorem
8
and Corollary
4.5

for the next statement:

Corollary 8.2.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm with the following properties:

(1)

Given a finite automaton
\mathcal{A}
over
D=\{0,1\}
as input, the algorithm decides if
\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
is in P.

(2)

If
\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
is not in P, then the algorithm produces a finite set
\Gamma^{\prime}
of relations from
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
witnessing that

\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
is NP-complete.

(3)

If
\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
is in P, then the algorithm produces a decision process for
\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
running in polynomial time on instances
I\in\mathrm{AutCSP}(\mathcal{A})
and the automaton
\mathcal{A}
. ∎

9.
Conclusion

Several questions remain open. First, while classical CSPs over finite domains are tractable when the language admits a Mal’tsev polymorphism
(
10
;
15
)
, it is unknown if Mal’tsev polymorphisms imply tractability for AutCSPs. Second, we lack an algorithm that converts an AutCSP instance into an equivalent
(2,3)
-minimal instance
(
1
;
3
)
, a central tool in classical relational width and local-consistency frameworks. Finally, we aim to extend our results beyond the Boolean case by establishing a full dichotomy for AutCSPs over the 3-element domain, further clarifying the structure and complexity landscape of automatic constraint languages.
