Abstract
The maximum score method ( Manski 1975 ; Manski 1985 ) is a powerful approach for binary choice models, yet it is known to face both practical and theoretical challenges. In particular, the estimator converges at a slower-than-root- n n rate to a nonstandard limiting distribution. We investigate conditions under which strictly concave surrogate score functions can be employed to achieve identification through a smooth criterion function. This criterion enables root- n n convergence to a normal limiting distribution. While the conditions to guarantee these desired properties are nontrivial, we characterize them in terms of primitive conditions. Extensive simulation studies support, the root- n n convergence rate, the asymptotic normality, and the validity of the standard inference methods. JEL classification: C14, C25 Keywords: binary choice, maximum score, root- n n asymptotic normality, surrogate method.

Extensive simulation studies further demonstrate that, under these conditions, our proposed method achieves root-

Thus, our claim does not contradict the existing knowledge in the literature.
We view the alternative approaches in the literature discussed above as complementary to our work.
They impose different restrictions on the data-generating processes, which in turn lead to different identification strategies, estimation methods, asymptotic properties, and/or research objectives.

In summary, we investigate conditions under which a strictly concave surrogate maximum score identifies a solution to the original maximum score problem, thereby enabling root-

Consequently, standard inference methods (such as the bootstrap) are valid, and there is no need to select tuning parameters for subsampling or smoothing, unlike in conventional maximum score methods.
An important advantage of the availability of standard asymptotic results is that they facilitate implementation using widely used statistical software packages, such as Stata, whose default output assumes a normal limiting distribution.

2
Maximum Score and Its Surrogate Counterpart

Consider the threshold-crossing binary choice model

Y=\1\{X^{\prime}b_{0}+\varepsilon\geq 0\}

(2.1)

where
Y
is a
\{0,1\}
-valued choice variable,
X
is a
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued explanatory variables,
b_{0}
is an unknown parameter vector, and
\varepsilon
is an unobserved error satisfying the conditional median restriction

\mathrm{Median}(\varepsilon|X)=0.

(2.2)

For this model, Manski develops the maximum score method to identify and estimate
b_{0}
.

The maximum score method solves

\max_{b\in B}Q_{0}(b)
, where
Q_{0}
is defined by

\displaystyle Q_{0}(b)=\mathbb{E}\big[Y\cdot\1\{X^{\prime}b\geq 0\}+(1-Y)\cdot\1\{X^{\prime}b<0\}\big].

(2.3)

When this problem is replaced by its sample counterpart, both practical and theoretical difficulties arise.
Because the score is defined through an indicator function, which is discontinuous, the resulting optimization problem is non-convex.
Moreover, the resulting estimator converges at a nonstandard rate that is slower than
\sqrt{n}
and has a nonstandard limiting distribution.

To circumvent these difficulties, the statistical learning literature proposes replacing the indicator function with a continuous surrogate loss.
Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable surrogate function.
Define the surrogate objective function
Q_{\phi}
by

\displaystyle Q_{\phi}(b)=\mathbb{E}\big[Y\cdot\phi(X^{\prime}b)+(1-Y)\cdot\phi(-X^{\prime}b)\big].

(2.4)

We then define the surrogate maximum score method as the solution to

\max_{b\in B}Q_{\phi}(b).

There is, however, a natural question at this point.
Are the solutions to the surrogate problem guaranteed to also solve the original maximum score problem?
If the answer to this question is ‘no,’ then the surrogate method would generally yield biased estimates and would therefore be of limited usefulness.
On the other hand, if the answer is ‘yes,’ then the surrogate approach could indeed help overcome the difficulties associated with the maximum score method.

We argue that, under certain conditions, the surrogate maximum score method indeed yields solutions to the original maximum score problem – Section
3.1
.
Furthermore, the surrogate problem allows for point identification without imposing additional restrictions – Section
3.2
.
The key conditions under which these desirable results hold are nontrivial.
Nevertheless, they accommodate a wide class of distributions for
X
– Section
4
.

3
Main Results

3.1
Validity of the Surrogate Maximum Score

Denoting the conditional choice probability by

\eta(x)=\Pr(Y=1|X=x),

consider the following assumption on the Bayes boundary.

Assumption 3.1
(Bayes Boundary):

Let
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be nonempty and compact.
There exists
b_{0}\in B
such that the following conditions hold.

(i)

\1\{\eta(X)\geq 1/2\}=\1\{X^{\prime}b_{0}\geq 0\}
a.s.

(ii)

\Pr(X^{\prime}b_{0}>0)>0
and
\Pr(X^{\prime}b_{0}<0)>0
.

Assumption
3.1
is the standard assumption employed in the literature on cost-sensitive binary classification, and in particular, maximum score estimation.
Assumption
3.1
(
i
) requires the linear Bayes boundary,
that is, the linear classification
x\mapsto x^{\prime}b
is correctly specified with a representative
true
parameter vector
b=b_{0}
.
In the literature on maximum score estimation, this assumption is essentially equivalent to what is known as the conditional median restriction (
2.2
).
2
2

2

Indeed, in the threshold crossing model (
2.1
), the conditional median restriction (
2.2
) implies Assumption
3.1
(
i
) under the regularity conditions that
\Pr(X^{\prime}b_{0}=0)=0
and
F_{\varepsilon|X}(\ \cdot\ |X)
is strictly increasing at 0 almost surely.

Assumption
3.1
(
ii
) requires nondegeneracy of the Bayes boundary, that is, there are non-trivial sub-populations on both sides across the classification boundary.

Let
Q_{0}
denote the maximum score objective function defined in (
2.3
).
Let
Q_{\phi}
denote the surrogate objective function defined in (
2.4
).
The following lemma shows that surrogate maximum score solutions can characterize the original maximum score solutions up to scale under two key conditions.

Lemma 1
(Validity of the Surroate Maximum Score):

Suppose that Assumption
3.1
and the following two conditions are satisfied.

(L.
1
.1)

If
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
are not parallel, then

\Pr(\1\{X^{\prime}b_{1}\geq 0\}\neq\1\{X^{\prime}b_{2}\geq 0\})>0
.

(L.
1
.2)

Every
b_{\phi}\in\arg\max_{b\in B}Q_{\phi}(b)
satisfies
\1\{X^{\prime}b_{\phi}\geq 0\}=\1\{\eta(X)\geq 1/2\}
a.s.

Then, for every
b_{\phi}\in\arg\max_{b\in B}Q_{\phi}(b)
, there exists a scalar
c>0
such that
b_{\phi}=cb_{0}
, and
\arg\max_{b\in B}Q_{\phi}(b)\subseteq\arg\max_{b\in B}Q_{0}(b)
.

Proof of this lemma is provided in Appendix
A.1
.
Here, in the main text, we sketch the essence of the proof focusing on the roles played by the two key conditions, (L.
1
.1) and (L.
1
.2).

By way of contradiction, suppose that
b_{\phi}\in\arg\max_{b\in B}Q_{\phi}(b)
does not parallel
b_{0}
.
Then, Condition (L.
1
.1) implies

\Pr(\1\{X^{\prime}b_{\phi}\geq 0\}\neq\1\{X^{\prime}b_{0}\geq 0\})>0.

On the other hand, Assumption
3.1
(
i
) and Condition (L.
1
.2) imply

\1\{X^{\prime}b_{\phi}\geq 0\}=\1\{X^{\prime}b_{0}\geq 0\}\qquad\text{a.s.},

a contradiction.
Hence,
b_{\phi}=cb_{0}
must hold for some scalar
c\neq 0
.
The formal proof in the appendix further establishes
c>0
.

That is, any surrogate solution
b_{\phi}\in\arg\max_{b\in B}Q_{\phi}
should coincide with the representative true parameter vector
b_{0}
up to scale
c
, and therefore, it should belong to the set of solutions to the original maximum score problem.

3.2
Existence and Uniqueness of the Surrogate Maximum Score Solution

Lemma
1
would be vacuous if the set
\arg\max_{b\in B}Q_{\phi}(b)
of the surrogate maxima were empty.
We are going to show that it is not empty under a suitable condition.
Furthermore, under additional conditions, it is not only nonempty, but also a singleton.
In other words, we etablish that the surrogate maximum score problem guarantees a unique solution, unlike the original maximum score problem.
To this goal, however, we need to choose a reasonable surrogate score function
\phi
as formally outlined in the following assumption.

Assumption 3.2
(Surrogate Score Function):

\phi
:
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is strictly concave, strictly increasing and differentiable at 0 with
\phi^{\prime}(0)>0
.

To satisfy Assumption
3.2
, we can take
\phi
to be the negative of a common loss function
\ell
, that is,
\phi(u)=-\ell(u)
, where
\ell
is strictly convex, strictly decreasing, and differentiable at
0
with
\ell^{\prime}(0)<0
.
Examples include the following:

(1)

Logistic loss
:
\ell(u)=\frac{1}{a}\log\left(1+e^{-au}\right),a>0
;

(2)

Pseudo-Huber loss
:
\ell(u)=\sqrt{a^{2}+u^{2}}-u,a>0
;

(3)

Probit loss
:
\ell(u)=-\log\Phi(au)
,
a>0
, where
\Phi(\cdot)
denotes the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.

On the other hand, our requirements rule out other popular loss functions, such as the hinge loss, the ReLU loss, and square loss functions.
Even though the exponential loss function satisfies Assumption
3.2
, it does not generally satisfy an additional assumption to be required later (Assumption
5.1
(
iv
)), and hence we desist from listing it as an example here.

In addition to the above requirements for surrogate score function
\phi
, we also impose the following regularity conditions.

Assumption 3.3
(Regularity Conditions):

The following conditions hold.

(i)

\E\left[\sup_{b\in B}(|\phi(X^{\prime}b)|+|\phi(-X^{\prime}b)|)\right]<\infty
.

(ii)

B=\{b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|b\|\leq R\}
for some
R\in(0,\infty)
.

(iii)

For every
v\neq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\Pr(X^{\prime}v\neq 0)>0
.

Assumption
3.3
(
i
) requires the bounded moments of the surrogate score, and is used along with Assumption
3.2
to ensure the continuity of the surrogate objective function
Q_{\phi}
.
Assumption
3.3
(
ii
) is under a researcher’s control, and implies the convexity of the parameter set
B
in particular.
For the sake of Lemma
2
below, only the concavity of
B
is needed.
We state this particular form of
B=\{b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|b\|\leq R\}
in Assumption
3.3
(
ii
) for the purpose of proving Proposition
2
in Section
4.2
later.
Assumption
3.3
(
iii
) requires that the classifier
X^{\prime}v
is not degenerate for any possible parameter vector
v\neq 0
.
Assumption
3.3
(
ii
)–(
iii
) serve to ensure the strict concavity of the surrogate objective function
Q_{\phi}
on
B
.
With the continuity and strict concavity of
Q_{\phi}
established as such, we can establish the following existence and uniqueness properties.

Lemma 2
(Existence and Uniqueness of the Surrogate Maximum):

If Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
, and
3.3
(
i
) hold, then a solution to
\max_{b\in B}Q_{\phi}(b)
exists.
If Assumption
3.3
(
ii
)–(
iii
) hold in addition, then
\max_{b\in B}Q_{\phi}(b)
admits a unique solution.

Proof of Lemma
2
is provided in Appendix
A.2
.
The continuity of
\phi
implied by Assumption
3.2
does not necessarily guarantee the continuity of
Q_{\phi}
.
To ensure the continuity of
Q_{\phi}
, we use the bounded moment condition of Assumption
3.3
(
i
) in addition so that score can be shown to be integrable to invoke the dominated convergence theorem.
Then, the standard argument via Weierstrass Theorem yields the existence result.
The uniqueness follows by the strict concavity of
Q_{\phi}
on

, which holds under Assumption
3.3
(
ii
)–(
iii
).

Summary of the Main Theoretical Results

Putting

Theorem 1

If Assumptions

,

, and

(T.

\1\{X^{\prime}b_{\phi}\geq 0\}=\1\{\eta(X)\geq 1/2\}

4.3

We introduce the following definition of elliptically symmetric distributions.

Definition 1

A random vector

f(x)=|\Sigma|^{-1/2}g\left((x-\mu)^{\prime}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right).

We write

If

\E\left[X|b_{0}^{\prime}X=t\right]=\frac{\Sigma b_{0}}{b_{0}^{\prime}\Sigma b_{0}}t,

Examples of elliptically symmetric distributions include the multivariate normal, multivariate

Furthermore, all these families of distributions satisfy Assumption

All these families of distribution also satisfy Assumption

Hence, these distributions serve as examples that satisfy the high-level conditions, (T.

Finally, we emphasize that these distribution families serve only as illustrative examples satisfying the sufficient conditions.

5
Surrogate Maximum Score Estimation & Standard Inference

5.1
Estimator

The conventional maximum score estimator is based on the sample counterpart of

\max_{b\in B}Q_{0}(b)
, where
Q_{0}
is defined in (
2.3
).
Because of the presence of the indicator function, this method presents both practical and theoretical challenges.
In particular, the resulting optimization problem is nonconvex, and the estimator converges at a slower-than-root-
n
rate to a nonstandard limiting distribution.

Under the conditions discussed in Sections
3
and
4
, we argued that the surrogate objective
Q_{\phi}
defined in (
2.4
) can be used in place of
Q_{0}
.
Motivated by this observation, we propose the surrogate maximum score estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{b}=\arg\max_{b\in B}Q_{\phi,n}(b),\qquad\text{where}

\displaystyle Q_{\phi,n}(b)=\mathbb{E}_{n}\big[Y\cdot\phi(X^{\prime}b)+(1-Y)\cdot\phi(-X^{\prime}b)\big],

(5.1)

with
\mathbb{E}_{n}
denoting the sample mean operator.
The equality “
=
” in this definition characterizes
\widehat{b}
uniquely, as the solution is shown to be unique in Section
3.2
.

The surrogate score function
\phi
is well-behaved, cf. Assumption
3.2
.
Therefore, unlike the conventional maximum score estimator, the surrogate method in (
5.1
) admits a convex optimization problem, a unique solution, and a root-
n
convergence rate to a standard limiting distribution.
Although the underlying theoretical arguments are largely standard, the following subsection presents the asymptotic properties of the estimator (
5.1
) for the sake of completeness.

5.2
Asymptotic Properties

The uniqueness of the solution
b_{\phi}
, as established in Theorem
1
, together with the local curvature of the population objective, as described below, provides the key ingredients for deriving its statistical properties.
For convenience of writing, we write

\ell_{\phi}(Z_{i},b):=Y_{i}\phi(X_{i}^{\prime}b)+(1-Y_{i})\phi(-X_{i}^{\prime}b)

and

\psi(Z_{i},b):=\nabla_{b}\ell_{\phi}(Z,b)
.

Assumption 5.1
:

(i)

Z_{i}:=(Y_{i},X^{\prime}_{i})^{\prime}
are i.i.d. and the support of
X_{i}
is a set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(ii)

The parameter
b_{\phi}
belongs to a compact set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

(iii)

\ell_{\phi}
is twice continuously differentiable;

H:=\nabla^{2}_{b}Q_{\phi}(b_{\phi})
is negative definite; and

\Omega:=\mathbb{E}[\psi(Z_{i},b_{\phi})\psi(Z_{i},b_{\phi})^{\prime}]
is finite and positive definite.

(iv)

\mathbb{E}[F_{0}(Z)^{2}]<\infty
,
\mathbb{E}[F_{1}(Z)^{2}]<\infty
, and
\mathbb{E}[F_{2}(Z)^{2}]<\infty
, where

F_{0}(Z):=\sup_{b\in B}|\ell_{\phi}(Z,b)|
,

F_{1}(Z):=\sup_{b\in B}\|\nabla_{b}\ell_{\phi}(Z,b)\|
, and

F_{2}(Z):=\sup_{b\in B}\|\nabla_{b}^{2}\ell_{\phi}(Z,b)\|
.

Assumption
5.1
(
i
) imposes an i.i.d. sampling framework.
This assumption is not essential and can be extended to accommodate time-series or spatial dependence.
Assumption
5.1
(
ii
) requires the compactness of the parameter set.
Assumption
5.1
(
iii
) is compatible with the smooth surrogate function
\phi
and the uniqueness of the maximizer as guaranteed by Lemma
2
. In particular, it can be satisfied by the specific loss functions introduced as examples in Section
3.2
.

The envelope conditions in Assumption
5.1
(
iv
) are stated at a high level to maintain generality. Lower-level sufficient conditions are provided in Section
5.3
. In particular, we show that the conventional bounded fourth-moment condition
\E\|X\|^{4}<\infty
is sufficient for Assumption
5.1
(
iv
) in the context of the specific loss functions presented as examples in Section
3.2
.

The following corollary formalizes the root-
n
consistency and asymptotic normality of the surrogate maximum score estimator
\widehat{b}
.

Corollary 1
(Root-
n
Asymptotic Normality):

If Assumption
5.1
holds in addition to the conditions stated in Theorem
1
, then

\displaystyle\widehat{b}\rightarrow_{p}b_{\phi}~~\text{and}~~\sqrt{n}(\widehat{b}-b_{\phi})\rightarrow_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,~H^{-1}\Omega H^{-1}).

Corollary
1
establishes that the surrogate maximum score estimator
\widehat{b}
attains the standard root-
n
rate of convergence and is asymptotically normal.
This stands in contrast to the classical maximum score estimator, which exhibits a cube-root convergence rate and a non-Gaussian limiting distribution due to the discontinuity of its objective function.

The key feature underlying this improvement is the smoothness of the surrogate loss
\phi(\cdot)
, which ensures that the population objective
Q_{\phi}(b)
is twice continuously differentiable and admits a local quadratic expansion around its maximizer
b_{\phi}
.
As a consequence, the asymptotic normality of
\widehat{b}
permits the use of conventional inference procedures, including bootstrap methods, thereby facilitating standard Gaussian-based inference.

Section
6
demonstrates these theoretical properties—namely, root-
n
consistency and asymptotic normality—through simulation studies.

5.3
Primitive Sufficient Conditions for Assumption
5.1
(
iv
)

The envelope conditions in Assumption
5.1
(
iv
) are stated at a high level to preserve generality. In this section, we provide lower-level sufficient conditions tailored to the three loss functions introduced in Section
3.2
.
Namely, it is enough to assume the conventional bounded fourth-moment condition
\E\|X\|^{4}<\infty
.

Let the parameter space
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact, and suppose that
\phi
and its derivatives satisfy polynomial growth:

\displaystyle|\phi(u)|\lesssim 1+|u|^{p},\quad|\phi^{\prime}(u)|\lesssim 1+|u|^{q},\quad\text{and}\quad|\phi^{\prime\prime}(u)|\lesssim 1+|u|^{r}

for some constants
p,q,r\geq 0
.
Then, the envelope functions satisfy the bounds

\displaystyle F_{0}(Z)\lesssim 1+\|X\|^{p},\quad F_{1}(Z)\lesssim\|X\|\bigl(1+\|X\|^{q}\bigr),\quad\text{and}\quad F_{2}(Z)\lesssim\|X\|^{2}\bigl(1+\|X\|^{r}\bigr).

In light of these bounds, we provide primitive sufficient conditions for Assumption
5.1
(
iv
) for each of the loss functions considered below.

(1)

Logistic loss:

\phi(u)=-\frac{1}{a}\log(1+e^{-au}),a>0
.
The polynomial growth conditions are satisfied with the exponents
p=q=r=0
, yielding

\displaystyle F_{0}(Z)\lesssim 1,\quad F_{1}(Z)\lesssim\|X\|,\quad\text{and}\quad F_{2}(Z)\lesssim\|X\|^{2}.

Hence, Assumption
5.1
(
iv
) is satisfied if
\mathbb{E}\|X\|^{4}<\infty
.

(2)

Pseudo-Huber loss:

\phi(u)=-\sqrt{a^{2}+u^{2}}+u
,
a>0
.
The polynomial growth conditions are satisfied with the exponents
p=1
and
q=r=0
, yielding

\displaystyle F_{0}(Z)\lesssim 1+\|X\|,\quad F_{1}(Z)\lesssim\|X\|,\quad\text{and}\quad F_{2}(Z)\lesssim\|X\|^{2}.

Hence, Assumption
5.1
(
iv
) is satisfied if
\mathbb{E}\|X\|^{4}<\infty
.

(3)

Probit loss:

\phi(u)=-\log\Phi(au)
.
The polynomial growth conditions are satisfied with the exponents
p=2
,
q=1
, and
r=0
, yielding

\displaystyle F_{0}(Z)\lesssim 1+\|X\|^{2},\quad F_{1}(Z)\lesssim\|X\|(1+\|X\|),\quad\text{and}\quad F_{2}(Z)\lesssim\|X\|^{2}.

Hence, Assumption
5.1
(
iv
) is satisfied if
\mathbb{E}\|X\|^{4}<\infty
.

All the three loss functions introduced in Section
3.2
as examples satisfying the high-level conditions in Assumption
3.2
also satisfy the high-level condition in Assumption
5.1
(
iv
).

On the other hand, not all loss functions that satisfy Assumption
3.2
meet the requirement in Assumption
5.1
(
iv
). For instance, the exponential loss satisfies Assumption
3.2
, but it cannot be bounded by any polynomial and therefore fails to satisfy Assumption
5.1
(
iv
). This is the primary reason why it was not included as an example in Section
3.2
.

5.4
Variance Estimation

In light of the root-
n
asymptotic normality established in Corollary
1
, we can conduct standard inference by computing the analytic variance estimate to obtain standard errors, constructing confidence intervals, comparing
t
-statistics with normal critical values, and computing
p
-values.

This feature of the surrogate maximum score method offers a substantial advantage: it can be readily implemented by empirical researchers familiar with standard asymptotics and is compatible with widely used statistical software packages, such as Stata, whose default output assumes a normal limiting distribution.

To this end, this subsection presents the estimation of the asymptotic variance
H^{-1}\Omega H^{-1}
and establishes its consistency.
This can be implemented via a straightforward plug-in approach.
Let
\widehat{b}
be the surrogate estimator of
b_{\phi}
, and define the sample Hessian and variance estimator as

\displaystyle\widehat{H}:=\nabla_{b}^{2}Q_{n}(\widehat{b})\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\Omega}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(Z_{i},\widehat{b})\,\psi(Z_{i},\widehat{b})^{\prime},

where
Q_{n}(b)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{\phi}(Z_{i},b)
and
\psi(Z_{i},b)=\nabla_{b}\ell_{\phi}(Z_{i},b)
.
Then, a natural estimator of the asymptotic variance is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{V}:=\widehat{H}^{-1}\widehat{\Omega}\widehat{H}^{-1}.

(5.2)

The following corollary establishes the consistency of this variance estimator.

Corollary 2
:

Suppose that the conditions stated in Corollary
1
hold. Then,

\displaystyle\widehat{V}\rightarrow_{p}H^{-1}\Omega H^{-1}.

The matrix
\widehat{V}
provides a consistent estimator of the asymptotic covariance of
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{b}-b_{\phi})
, and can thus be used to construct standard errors and confidence intervals.

In particular, the asymptotic normality result in Corollary
1
, together with the consistency of the variance estimator in Corollary
2
, implies that for any fixed vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle T_{n}:=\frac{\sqrt{n}\,a^{\prime}(\widehat{b}-b_{\phi})}{\sqrt{a^{\prime}\widehat{V}a}}\rightarrow_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

which justifies the use of
\widehat{V}
.

While these theoretical results and properties are standard, such “standard” results are rather unexpected in the context of maximum score estimation. We therefore dare to emphasize these points here.

5.5
Nonparametric Bootstrap with Asymptotic Refinement

A large literature on the maximum score problem has focused on resampling-based inference, motivated by the well-known failure of the standard nonparametric bootstrap; see Section
1
for details.

Once the maximum score problem is rendered “standard” via the surrogate score, however, the conventional nonparametric bootstrap becomes applicable and can deliver asymptotic refinements
(
Horowitz 2001
;
Horowitz 2019
)
, often leading to substantial finite-sample improvements. This section formalizes this bootstrap result.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a fixed vector, and define the scalar parameter
\theta:=a^{\prime}b_{\phi}
of interest with estimator
\widehat{\theta}:=a^{\prime}\widehat{b}
. Let
\widehat{V}
be the consistent variance estimator (
5.2
), and define the standard error

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}:=a^{\prime}\widehat{V}a.

The corresponding normalized test statistic is given by

\displaystyle T_{n}=\frac{\sqrt{n}\bigl(a^{\prime}\widehat{b}-a^{\prime}b_{\phi}\bigr)}{\widehat{\sigma}}.

To implement the bootstrap, draw
S
independent bootstrap samples
\{Z_{i}^{\ast(s)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with replacement from the empirical distribution. For each
s\in[S]
, compute the bootstrap estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{b}^{\ast(s)}:=\arg\max_{b\in B}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{\phi}(Z_{i}^{\ast(s)},b),

and define
\widehat{\theta}^{\ast(s)}:=a^{\prime}\widehat{b}^{\ast(s)}
. Let
\widehat{V}^{\ast(s)}
denote the bootstrap analogue of
\widehat{V}
, and define

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{\ast(s)2}:=a^{\prime}\widehat{V}^{\ast(s)}a.

The bootstrap studentized statistic is given by

\displaystyle T_{n}^{\ast(s)}:=\frac{\sqrt{n}\bigl(a^{\prime}\widehat{b}^{\ast(s)}-a^{\prime}\widehat{b}\bigr)}{\widehat{\sigma}^{\ast(s)}}.

Let
\widehat{q}_{\alpha}^{\ast}
denote the empirical
\alpha
-quantile of
\{T_{n}^{\ast(s)}\}_{s=1}^{S}
. A
(1-\alpha)
confidence interval for
\theta=a^{\prime}b_{\phi}
is then given by

\displaystyle\left[a^{\prime}\widehat{b}-\frac{\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}^{\ast}\,\widehat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}},\quad a^{\prime}\widehat{b}-\frac{\widehat{q}_{\alpha/2}^{\ast}\,\widehat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}}\right].

The following corollary establishes the asymptotic validity and higher-order accuracy of the proposed nonparametric bootstrap procedure.

Assumption 5.2
:

(i)

The Cramér’s condition,

\limsup_{\|t\|\to\infty}|\mathbb{E}e^{it^{\prime}W_{i}}|<1,

is satisfied for the characteristic function of the three-dimensional random vector

\displaystyle W_{i}:=(a^{\prime}H^{-1}\psi(Z_{i},b_{\phi}),a^{\prime}H^{-1}(\psi(Z_{i},b_{\phi})\psi(Z_{i},b_{\phi})^{\prime}-\Omega)H^{-1}a,a^{\prime}H^{-1}(\nabla_{b}^{2}\ell_{\phi}(Z_{i},b)-H)Va)^{\prime}.

(ii)

Assume that the surrogate loss
\phi
is three-times differentiable.

(iii)

Assume that
\mathbb{E}\|\psi(Z_{i},b_{\phi})\|^{6}<\infty
.

Corollary 3
:

Suppose that the conditions of Corollary
1
and Assumption
5.2
hold. Then,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}^{\ast}(T_{n}^{\ast}\leq t)-\mathbb{P}(T_{n}\leq t)\right|=o(n^{-1/2}),

where
\mathbb{P}^{*}(\cdot)
denotes the conditional bootstrap probability over data. In particular, the studentized bootstrap achieves an asymptotic refinement over the normal approximation of Subsection
5.4
, whose approximation error is of order
O(n^{-1/2})
.

6
Simulation Evidence

While we have established the theory of root-
n
asymptotic normality, the conventional theory for the maximum score estimator does not exhibit these desirable properties.
Therefore, it is useful to provide numerical evidence in support of our theoretical predictions.

This section presents simulation studies that demonstrate the root-
n
convergence rate (Section
6.2
), the normal limiting distribution (Section
6.3
), and the validity of inference methods based on these theoretical properties (Section
6.4
).
We begin by presenting our simulation design in Section
6.1
.

6.1
Simulation Design

We generate
n
independent copies of
(Y_{i},X_{i}^{\prime})^{\prime}
from the threshold-crossing model (
2.1
).
Let

\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.5\\
0.5&1\end{pmatrix},

and consider the following three designs for
X_{i}
in line with the discussion in Section
4.3
:

(i)

Normal distribution
:
X_{i}\sim N(0,\Sigma)
;

(ii)

t
distribution (
t_{5}
)
:
X_{i}=Z_{i}/\sqrt{U_{i}/5}
, where
Z_{i}\sim N(0,\Sigma)
and
U_{i}\sim\chi^{2}_{5}
independently;

(iii)

Laplace distribution
:
X_{i}=\sqrt{S_{i}}\,Z_{i}
, where
Z_{i}\sim N(0,\Sigma)
and
S_{i}\sim\text{Exp}(1)
independently.

Let
\varepsilon_{i}\sim\text{Logistic}(0,1)
be independent of
X_{i}
, and set
b_{0}=(1,1)^{\prime}/\sqrt{2}
.

Our parameter of interest is a scalar reparameterization
\theta
of the two-dimensional coefficient vector
b
under the unit-norm normalization. Specifically, we write

\displaystyle b(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad\|b(\theta)\|=1.

Thus, estimating
\theta
is equivalent to estimating the direction of
b
.
Given the true parameter values
b_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1)^{\prime}
, the corresponding true angle is
\theta_{0}=\frac{\pi}{4}
.
This parameterization is convenient because the threshold-crossing model (
2.1
) yields observationally equivalent distributions under scaling of
b
, whereas
\theta
provides a unique scalar target for reporting RMSE and plotting densities.
Note that this reparameterization is smooth, so the theoretical results established in Section
5
extend to
\theta
.

. These results therefore provide evidence for the validity of the standard inference procedure, as guaranteed by our theory in Sections
5.2
–
5.4
.

Next, we turn to the standard inference based on (B) the bootstrap method.
Overall, the bootstrap-based confidence intervals deliver coverage probabilities close to the nominal
95\%

than its analytic counterpart for for all three surrogate losses when

7
Conclusions

The maximum score method is a powerful tool for analyzing semiparametric binary choice models and has been highly influential in the econometrics literature.
At the same time, it faces a number of practical and theoretical challenges.
These challenges are part of the reason why the literature has remained active since the method’s introduction.

In particular, the lack of the standard root-
n
convergence rate and asymptotic normality has motivated many researchers to develop methods that either address or accommodate these limitations.
In this paper, we revisit this problem and, rather than addressing or accommodating these challenges, investigate how to achieve root-
n
asymptotic normality.

Specifically, we study the conditions under which these standard asymptotic properties hold for surrogate maximum score methods.
These conditions, stated as Conditions (T.
1
.1)–(T.
1
.2) in Theorem
1
are nontrivial.
Yet, they are sufficiently general to encompass a large class of distributions for
X
.

Under these conditions, we establish root-
n
asymptotic normality and the validity of standard inference methods, such as the use of normal critical values with analytic variance estimation, as well as bootstrap procedures for small-sample refinement.
Extensive simulation studies corroborate our theoretical claims regarding the root-
n
convergence rate, asymptotic normality, and the validity of these standard inference methods.
