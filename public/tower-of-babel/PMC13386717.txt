Association between pre-existing sleep disorders and post-extraction discomfort in adult patients: a comparative study
Authors: Arda Toptaş, Onur Koç, Semih Demirci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative pain and discomfort following tooth extraction are common complications that may negatively affect patients’ quality of life. The potential association between pre-existing sleep disturbances and postoperative discomfort remains insufficiently explored.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the association between pre-existing sleep disturbances and postoperative pain intensity, emotional impact, and limitations in daily activities in patients undergoing routine tooth extraction.Materials and methodsThis retrospective observational cohort study included 111 adult patients (18–65 years) who underwent tooth extraction at Hacettepe University. Preoperative sleep quality was assessed using the Jenkins Sleep Scale (JSS). Participants were classified into two groups: Group A (patients with sleep disturbances; JSS ≥ 12, n = 48) and Group B (patients without sleep disturbances; JSS < 12, n = 63). Postoperative outcomes were evaluated on postoperative day 1 using the American Pain Society Patient Outcomes Questionnaire–Revised (APS-POQ-R). The null hypothesis stated that there would be no difference in postoperative discomfort between the two groups. Statistical analyses included the Mann–Whitney U test and Chi-square test.ResultsPatients with pre-existing sleep disturbances (Group A) demonstrated significantly higher pain intensity scores (p < 0.001), greater emotional impact (p = 0.030), and increased limitations in daily activities (p = 0.002) compared with Group B. Satisfaction of pain management was significantly lower in Group A (p = 0.016). No statistically significant differences were observed between groups regarding nausea, numbness, itching, or dizziness (p > 0.05).ConclusionPre-existing sleep disturbances were significantly associated with increased postoperative discomfort and reduced satisfaction following tooth extraction. The null hypothesis was rejected. However, given the observational design and the lack of predictive analysis, these results should be interpreted cautiously, and further prospective studies are needed before clinical application can be recommended.Keywords: Sleep disturbances, Tooth extraction, Postoperative pain, Oral surgery, Jenkins Sleep Scale, APS-POQ-R

Introduction

Tooth extraction is a common procedure in oral and maxillofacial surgery. Although it is considered minor surgery, patients often experience pain, swelling, odor, and other symptoms afterward [1]. These can affect daily activities and quality of life, especially in the first postoperative days [1]. Postoperative pain, swelling, trismus, dizziness, and nausea may follow tooth extraction. Pre-existing sleep disturbances may further intensify these complaints [2, 3]. Predisposing factors that increase postoperative pain after tooth extraction should be fully disclosed.

Sleep quality is critical for postoperative recovery and health. The literature shows that poor sleep lowers the pain threshold and slows recovery [4, 5]. Sleep is key for physiological recovery; lack of sleep impairs cognitive and sensory functions, disrupts homeostasis, and slows healing [5]. Sleep disturbances are common and linked to delayed healing, prolonged fatigue, higher cardiovascular risk, and postoperative delirium in dental settings [5, 6]. Pain and sleep have a bidirectional relationship. Acute surgical pain disrupts sleep, and poor sleep increases pain perception [5]. Postoperative sleep disturbances can develop due to pain, surgical stress, and environmental factors. These disturbances may increase pain sensitivity and impair cognitive function [6]. However, the effect of pre-existing sleep disturbances on postoperative pain perception following dental extractions is poorly studied. Past studies focus on major procedures under general anesthesia or those requiring hospitalization [4, 5]. Few address sleep quality in relation to postoperative pain after routine tooth extractions performed with local anesthesia.

Validated instruments are available to assess sleep quality and postoperative pain outcomes. The Jenkins Sleep Scale (JSS) is a widely used and validated self-reported questionnaire for assessing sleep disturbances in clinical and population-based settings [7–10]. Similarly, the American Pain Society Patient-Reported Outcomes Questionnaire–Revised (APS-POQ-R) is a multidimensional tool designed to evaluate postoperative pain intensity, emotional impact, functional limitations, and patient satisfaction, and has demonstrated reliability in surgical populations [11, 12].

For this purpose, the present study evaluated whether an existing sleep disorder affects postoperative pain perception following tooth extraction. Identifying factors associated with postoperative discomfort may help clinicians better understand variations in perioperative patient experiences.

Methods

This study was designed as a retrospective, observational cohort study. The study population consisted of patients who had undergone routine tooth extraction at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Faculty of Dentistry, Hacettepe University. The study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki on Medical Research Involving Human Subjects. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Hacettepe University Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee (approval no: SBA 25/137). The required sample size was calculated as a minimum of 111 patients based on a power analysis using a 95% confidence level, a 5% alpha error rate, and an effect size of 0.3. This effect size was selected based on Cohen’s conventional criteria, representing a moderate effect size commonly used in clinical research when prior data are limited [13]. Accordingly, at least 111 patients who had undergone tooth extraction by the same surgeon and met the eligibility criteria during the study period were consecutively included from institutional records.

Patients aged 18–65 years who had undergone routine tooth extraction without requiring additional surgical procedures such as tooth sectioning or flap elevation were eligible for inclusion. Only individuals for whom at least 24 h had passed since the procedure and whose postoperative data were available in the clinical records were considered. Additionally, patients who were able to read and understand the consent form and had provided written informed consent at the time of treatment were included. Since the surveys used in the study were self-administered, the participants needed to be able to read and understand the consent form. Correct completion of these validated instruments requires sufficient literacy and comprehension to ensure the reliability of the data collected. Therefore, to ensure data validity, individuals who could not complete the surveys independently were not included in the analysis.

The age range of 18–65 years was selected to ensure a relatively homogeneous adult population. Pediatric patients were excluded due to developmental differences in pain perception, sleep architecture, and neurophysiological responses to stress and inflammation, which could confound the relationship between sleep quality and postoperative discomfort. Similarly, individuals older than 65 years were excluded because age-related alterations in sleep physiology, increased prevalence of systemic diseases, polypharmacy, and altered pain perception may independently influence postoperative pain outcomes. Limiting the sample to adults within this age range helped reduce potential confounding factors and improve internal validity.

Exclusion criteria included individuals with advanced cardiac or pulmonary disease, allergies to local anesthetics or NSAIDs, immunosuppression, history of antiresorptive medication or head and neck radiotherapy, pregnancy or breastfeeding, requirement of antibiotic prophylaxis, extraction due to jaw fracture, tumor, or cyst, and documented use of medication for sleep disorders or chronic pain treatment. Patients whose postoperative records were incomplete were also excluded. Individuals using pharmacological treatment for sleep disorders were excluded because sleep medications may independently influence sleep architecture, pain perception, and postoperative recovery, thereby acting as potential confounding factors [14–16].

All procedures involved single-tooth extractions performed under local anesthesia. The extracted teeth included both anterior and posterior teeth from the maxilla and mandible. The primary indications for extraction were extensive dental caries, periapical pathology, and periodontal disease. All extractions were classified as routine tooth extractions and were performed without flap elevation or tooth sectioning. Consequently, surgical extractions, including third molars, were not included in the study.

Postoperative analgesics consisted primarily of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), most commonly ibuprofen, according to the routine clinical practice of the department. A standardized postoperative analgesic protocol was applied to all patients to ensure consistency in pain management across the study population. However, individual analgesic consumption was not quantitatively recorded due to the retrospective design of the study.

Three instruments were used in the data collection process: Preoperative Jenkins Sleep Scale (JSS) scores previously recorded as part of routine clinical assessment were retrospectively retrieved from patient records. The JSS is a validated self-reported questionnaire consisting of four items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 20. Participants with a total score ≥ 12 were classified as Group A (sleep disturbance group), while those with a score < 12 were classified as Group B (non-sleep disturbance group). This cut-off value has been used in previous studies to indicate clinically relevant sleep disturbance and allows for the identification of individuals with impaired sleep quality in both clinical and population-based settings [7–10]. The use of a validated threshold helped reduce potential misclassification and ensured comparability with existing literature. These assessments were routinely performed during the preoperative evaluation on the day of the procedure under standardized clinical conditions using the same questionnaire format. All patients completed the JSS prior to the extraction procedure, ensuring temporal consistency between exposure assessment and surgical intervention.

Postoperative outcomes recorded on postoperative day 1 were retrospectively obtained from patient-reported APS-POQ-R forms collected during routine clinical follow-up visits. The APS-POQ-R has evaluated pain intensity, emotional impact, functional limitations, satisfaction with pain management, and adverse effects. The functional interference domain of the APS-POQ-R specifically assesses limitations in physical and sleep-related activities, including activities in bed, activities out of bed, falling asleep, and staying asleep, in accordance with the original validated instrument. Pain outcomes were assessed using patient self-reported APS-POQ-R questionnaires, and no investigator-based pain scoring was performed. Data were retrospectively retrieved from clinical records; therefore, the investigators did not influence the original pain assessment. The investigators who performed the data analysis extracted information from anonymized records, and pain scores had been recorded independently of the present study hypothesis.

The primary independent variable was sleep disturbance status, as determined by the Jenkins Sleep Scale (JSS) score (Group A: JSS ≥ 12; Group B: JSS < 12). Age, gender, and smoking status were included as additional independent variables. These questionnaires were part of the routine clinical follow-up protocol, and all data were retrospectively retrieved from existing clinical records without any additional patient contact for research purposes.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 21 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Since the continuous variables did not meet the assumptions of normal distribution, non-parametric statistical tests were used for between-group comparisons. Continuous variables were compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate. Due to the retrospective design and limited availability of standardized clinical records, certain procedure-related variables such as tooth type (anterior vs. posterior), jaw location (maxilla vs. mandible), and indication for extraction were not included in the statistical analysis.

In addition to group comparisons, Spearman correlation analysis was performed using the total study sample (n = 111) to evaluate the association between continuous Jenkins Sleep Scale (JSS) scores and APS-POQ-R outcomes. Spearman’s correlation was selected because the questionnaire-based outcome measures were ordinal in nature and did not meet the assumptions required for parametric correlation analysis. Age, gender, and smoking status were also compared between groups to evaluate baseline comparability and potential confounding factors. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median values for continuous variables, and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables.

The mean age was 27.98 years (SD = 10.0) in Group A and 30.54 years (SD = 12.3) in Group B. Group A consisted of 16 males and 32 females, while Group B consisted of 28 males and 35 females. Discussion

In the present study, patients with pre-existing sleep disturbances experienced greater postoperative discomfort. All patients received a standardized NSAID-based analgesic protocol, most commonly ibuprofen, according to the routine clinical practice. Although analgesic regimens and consumption were not directly evaluated, previous literature suggests a complex interaction between sleep quality, pain perception, and analgesic requirements [17].

The observed association between sleep disturbances and increased postoperative pain reflects a complex and potentially bidirectional interaction between sleep and pain. Although sleep quality was assessed preoperatively and postoperative outcomes were evaluated after the extraction procedure, the retrospective design and single postoperative assessment point limit the ability to establish a definitive temporal or causal relationship between sleep disturbance and postoperative discomfort. It is possible that early postoperative pain and discomfort may also contribute to worsening sleep quality, resulting in a self-reinforcing cycle between pain and sleep disturbance. However, no conclusions can be drawn regarding the optimal analgesic strategy based on the present findings.

The higher pain intensity observed in patients with impaired sleep quality following routine tooth extraction is consistent with the interaction between sleep and pain described in the literature [18]. The first postoperative day represents the period of highest pain intensity following tooth extraction, making it a critical and clinically sensitive time point for evaluating differences in patient-reported outcomes [19]. Although the absolute pain intensity scores corresponded to a moderate pain level, patients with sleep disturbances experienced significantly higher pain intensity compared with those without sleep disturbances.

In addition to statistical significance, the observed difference in pain intensity between the groups may also be clinically relevant, as even moderate increases in postoperative pain can negatively affect daily functioning, patient comfort, and overall recovery experience. However, given the absence of established minimal clinically important difference thresholds for APS-POQ-R in this specific setting, the clinical magnitude of this difference should be interpreted with caution. Increased postoperative pain may disrupt sleep continuity, while insufficient or fragmented sleep may heighten pain perception and increase analgesic requirements [17].

Although many studies have focused on postoperative sleep disturbances caused by surgical pain, fewer investigations have examined whether pre-existing sleep disturbances are associated with greater postoperative discomfort. The present findings suggest that pain and sleep may interact in a mutually reinforcing manner [18, 20].

Age is also recognized as a factor that may influence pain perception and postoperative recovery. However, in the present study, no significant difference in age was observed between the groups, suggesting that age was unlikely to have substantially influenced the observed differences in postoperative outcomes. Given the observational design of the present study, potential residual confounding factors cannot be completely excluded, although baseline characteristics between the groups were statistically comparable.

In terms of emotional outcomes, increased anxiety and stress symptoms have been noted in our patients with sleep disturbances. Postoperative pain and discomfort can increase tension, irritability, and even fear levels in patients [21]. The literature indicates that patients’ fear and anxiety levels rise with the decision to undergo surgery, and that this psychological state both increases postoperative pain perception and impairs sleep quality [22]. Especially in individuals with high levels of fear of surgery, it has been shown that sleep quality decreases more significantly, and more severe pain is experienced in the postoperative period [22].

Higher emotional impact scores were observed in patients with pre-existing sleep disturbances. This association may be related to the reported interaction between sleep quality, mood regulation, anxiety, and pain perception described in previous literature [23]. Indeed, a study reported that patients with high preoperative dental anxiety experienced more severe postoperative symptoms following dental extraction procedures [24]. In this context, monitoring patients’ emotional state during the postoperative period and providing psychological support, if necessary, may be beneficial in breaking the cycle of pain and insomnia.

Our findings indicate that sleep and pain management are decisive factors in patient satisfaction. It is noteworthy that patients with sleep problems generally report dissatisfaction with care. This is primarily because patients who are unable to sleep sufficiently and whose pain cannot be completely relieved evaluate their care experience negatively [25].

In a study, a bibliometric and clinical analysis demonstrated that poor sleep hygiene is strongly associated with increased pain interference and lower satisfaction with care, highlighting the bidirectional relationship between sleep and pain perception [26]. The literature reports that providing adequate information and education to patients in the post-extraction period increases their participation in pain management and satisfaction [27–29]. These findings may suggest a potential role for more comprehensive postoperative education and communication strategies in patients with sleep disturbances.

For example, in a survey study, more than 90% of patients stated that their dentists explained what to expect and how to manage post-extraction pain, and 74% reported feeling confident/prepared to cope with pain [30]. This finding highlights the importance of involving the patient in the care process for care perception.

Functional limitations following tooth extraction represent an important component of postoperative recovery, pain, and sleep quality. In our study, patients with insomnia reported greater limitations in daily activities as assessed by the APS-POQ-R functional interference domain. This domain specifically evaluates limitations in physical and sleep-related functions, including activities in bed, activities out of bed, and sleep-related interference, in accordance with the original APS-POQ-R structure. Although the present study evaluated routine tooth extraction, comparisons with more invasive procedures, such as third molar surgery, provide contextual insight into functional recovery patterns. Similarly, in the literature, patients report that functional problems such as difficulty chewing, limited mouth opening, disruption of nutrition and social life, and sleep disturbances are the most troublesome after third molar surgery, and that these are even more prominent than pain [31].

Sancar et al. found that although patients experienced a decrease in sleep duration on the first day after routine tooth extraction, there was no significant change in sleep quality. However, they reported that nighttime sleep duration was further reduced in patients who had multiple teeth extracted compared with those who had a single tooth extracted [31].

Authors have emphasized that, although surgeons generally consider pain to be the biggest problem, patients find functional limitations more important [31–33]. Similarly, a study reported that one-third of patients who underwent simple tooth extraction experienced a significant decline in quality of life in the early period, particularly in nutrition and speech [34].

Our findings also reveal that pain combined with sleep disturbances significantly disrupts patients’ daily lives. Especially in patients reporting impaired sleep, reduced daytime performance may be associated with limitations in social and occupational functioning. These findings suggest that functional recovery may be influenced by both pain intensity and sleep quality. Providing patients with proper guidance on issues such as a soft diet, oral hygiene, and rest will help minimize these functional losses.

The literature indicates that complications arising in the postoperative period can also trigger sleep disorders, which can delay recovery and reduce quality of life [3, 35]. As a result, there is a close interaction between sleep quality deterioration after tooth extraction, pain intensity, emotional state, analgesic use, satisfaction, and functional well-being. The findings suggest an association between preoperative sleep disturbances and greater postoperative burden following routine tooth extraction.

Although the correlation coefficients observed were weak to moderate, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The correlation analysis was consistent with the main findings of the study by demonstrating that higher Jenkins Sleep Scale scores were associated with increased pain intensity, greater functional limitations, and higher emotional impact, as well as lower satisfaction with pain management. These findings are consistent with the group-based comparisons and reinforce the observed relationship between sleep disturbance severity and postoperative outcomes.

The primary conclusions of this study are based on intergroup comparisons, while correlation analysis was included as a supplementary analysis to explore the relationship between continuous sleep scores and postoperative outcomes. Given the retrospective and observational nature of the study, causal inferences cannot be established. Further prospective research is needed to clarify the directionality and mechanisms underlying the relationship between sleep quality and postoperative discomfort.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective cohort design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between sleep disturbances and postoperative discomfort. Second, the study was conducted in a single academic center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. In addition, the relatively young age of the study population may further limit generalizability to older individuals, who are more likely to have a higher prevalence of sleep disturbances and altered pain perception. The inclusion of a restricted adult age range was intended to reduce heterogeneity; however, future studies including older populations are needed to better understand age-related differences in postoperative outcomes. Third, although a standardized NSAID-based postoperative analgesic protocol was applied to all patients, individual analgesic consumption was not quantitatively recorded. This represents an important potential confounding factor, as interindividual variability in analgesic consumption may have significantly influenced pain perception and patient-reported outcomes.

In addition, potentially relevant clinical variables such as tooth type (anterior vs. posterior), jaw location (maxilla vs. mandible), and indication for extraction were not included in the statistical analysis. Although all procedures were limited to routine, non-surgical extractions to reduce variability, these factors may still have influenced postoperative pain outcomes. Moreover, although all extractions were classified as routine procedures without flap elevation or tooth sectioning, detailed measures of procedural difficulty and extraction duration were not recorded. These factors may influence postoperative pain and could not be accounted for in the present analysis.

Furthermore, although baseline characteristics such as age, gender, and smoking status were comparable between groups, no multivariable analysis was performed to adjust for potential confounding factors or to account for interrelated outcomes. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. The reliance on univariate non-parametric analyses, while appropriate given the distribution of the data, may limit the ability to fully capture complex relationships between variables. In addition, the APS-POQ-R includes items related to sleep within its functional interference domain, which may introduce conceptual overlap with the exposure variable (sleep disturbance). This overlap may have partially influenced the observed associations; however, the APS-POQ-R is a multidimensional instrument that primarily captures pain intensity, emotional impact, and functional limitations beyond sleep-related aspects.

Moreover, all outcome measures were based on self-reported questionnaires (JSS and APS-POQ-R), which may be subject to reporting bias. Although both instruments are validated and widely used in clinical research, their reliability and internal consistency were not specifically assessed in the present study population.

In addition, sleep disturbances were assessed using a self-reported screening questionnaire rather than objective sleep assessment methods. Although the Jenkins Sleep Scale is a validated instrument for evaluating subjective sleep disturbances, it does not establish a clinical diagnosis of sleep disorders. Objective measures such as polysomnography were not performed and may provide a more comprehensive assessment of sleep physiology.

Additionally, no blinding was implemented due to the retrospective design of the study. However, all patient-reported outcomes were collected as part of routine clinical practice prior to the initiation of the study and were therefore not influenced by the study hypothesis or investigator expectations. Nevertheless, subjective perceptions related to sleep quality and pain may still have influenced the reported outcomes.

In addition, patients using pharmacological treatment for sleep disorders were excluded to reduce potential confounding effects on sleep architecture and pain perception. However, this exclusion may have resulted in a study population with relatively milder sleep disturbances and may limit the generalizability of the findings to patients with more severe or clinically treated sleep disorders.

Furthermore, psychological factors such as anxiety, stress, and dental fear, which are known to influence both sleep quality and pain perception, were not assessed or controlled for in the present study. These unmeasured psychological variables may have acted as important confounders and could potentially explain part of the observed association between sleep disturbances and postoperative discomfort. Future studies incorporating validated assessments of psychological status and dental anxiety may help clarify the independent contribution of sleep disturbances to postoperative outcomes.

Finally, postoperative outcomes were assessed only on the first postoperative day, which primarily reflects acute pain and early recovery. Although this time point is clinically relevant, as postoperative pain typically peaks within the first 24 h, it does not capture the full trajectory of recovery, persistence of symptoms, or longer-term functional outcomes during the postoperative period.

In addition, because postoperative outcomes were assessed at a single postoperative time point without longitudinal follow-up, temporal changes in the interaction between sleep quality and postoperative pain throughout the recovery period could not be evaluated. Therefore, future prospective studies including multiple postoperative assessment points and longitudinal follow-up are needed to better understand the long-term relationship between pre-existing sleep disturbances and postoperative recovery.

Conclusions

Patients with pre-existing sleep disturbances (Group A) were associated with higher postoperative pain intensity, greater emotional impact, and more pronounced limitations in daily activities compared with patients without sleep disturbances (Group B).

Based on these findings, the null hypothesis stating that no difference would exist between the two groups regarding postoperative outcomes was rejected.

The results suggest an association between preoperative sleep disturbances and increased postoperative discomfort following tooth extraction. Considering the bidirectional relationship between sleep and pain, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and do not imply a directional causal effect. Assessment of preoperative sleep quality may contribute to a broader understanding of postoperative patient-reported outcomes; however, further studies incorporating predictive modeling and risk stratification are needed before clinical implementation can be recommended. Given the the observational design of the study, causal inferences cannot be established, and further prospective studies are warranted.
