A biomimetic hybrid membrane vesicle nanoplatform attenuates tendinopathy through neuroinflammation modulation and tendon regeneration
Authors: Dongxu Li, Wei Huang, Feifei Ni, Wenbo Yang, Xiao Lv, Fashuai Wu, Fei Gao, Yu He, Tingfang Sun, Wei Yu, Zekang Xiong, Chunqing Meng, Hong Wang, Yizhong Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTendinopathy is a common condition that causes long-term pain. The inflammatory microenvironment (inflamed soil) promotes the ingrowth of sensory nerves (a key pain trigger) and induces functional impairments in tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs) (dysfunctional seeds). These interconnected processes collectively drive the progression of tendinopathy, which is frequently accompanied by persistent pain. Here, we develop a multifunctional nanoplatform (MSM-DTM) by integrating macrophage-sensory neuron hybrid membrane vesicles (MSM) and decellularized tendon matrix (DTM). MSM, fused from macrophage and sensory neuron membranes, efficiently binds multiple inflammatory cytokines and neuropeptides, thereby reprogramming the inflammatory microenvironment. Moreover, MSM breaks the vicious cycle of inflammation-induced senescence and prevents TSPCs aging. DTM promotes tenogenic differentiation and migration of TSPCs, improving tendon regeneration. In vivo, MSM-DTM improves collagen alignment, regulates macrophage polarization, alleviates pain, and enhances tendon functional recovery. MSM-DTM disrupts the inflammation-senescence positive feedback loop and reconstructs a pro-regenerative microenvironment, offering a promising strategy for tendinopathy.Keywords: tendinopathy, hybrid membrane vesicle, decellularized tendon matrix, tendon regeneration

Introduction

Tendinopathy is a degenerative tendon disorder caused by repetitive microtrauma and chronic mechanical stress.1,2 In recent years, the incidence of tendinopathy has steadily increased due to population aging and increased physical activity, with weight-bearing tendons such as the rotator cuff and patellar tendon being particularly vulnerable.3 Abnormalities in microstructure, composition, and cellular architecture are characteristic of tendinopathy.2 Although the pathogenesis of tendinopathy is multifactorial and complex, the inflammatory theory is widely accepted.4 Current clinical treatments are mainly aimed at improving symptoms rather than targeting the underlying cause of tendinopathy.5 These treatments usually provide only short-term relief of symptoms and do not stop tendinopathy progression.6,7,8 Therefore, there is an urgent need for a multifunctional treatment method that can simultaneously improve the symptoms of tendinopathy and prevent its progression.

A hallmark of tendinopathy is the formation of a hostile inflammatory microenvironment that acts as a pathological “soil,” impairing the regenerative capacity of tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs)—the “seeds” of tendon repair.9,10 Multiple inflammatory factors bind to the surface receptors of TSPCs to activate the intracellular pro-inflammatory switch, which triggers a series of phenotypic changes and leads to the progression of tendinopathy.11 In addition, chronic inflammation drives TSPCs into a premature senescent state, impairing their proliferation, migration, and tenogenic differentiation capacities.12 Senescent TSPCs not only lose function but also secrete large amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines and matrix-degrading enzymes, further amplifying the inflammatory response.13 This reciprocal interplay establishes a self-reinforcing inflammation-senescence feedback loop, exacerbating tissue degeneration and extracellular matrix (ECM) disorganization.14 Meanwhile, neuroinflammatory mediators act as persistent “triggers” of pain hypersensitivity, further aggravating the disease burden and hindering rehabilitation.15 Neuropeptides such as substance P (SP), derived from ingrown sensory nerves, sustain nociceptive signaling and promote neurogenic inflammation in the tendon.15 However, no method has yet been developed that simultaneously addresses the inflammatory microenvironment (inflamed soil), dysfunctional cells (seeds), and persistent pain (pain triggers).

Decellularized tendon matrix (DTM) has emerged as a promising scaffold in tissue engineering due to its retention of native tendon architecture and bioactive components, such as type I collagen and glycosaminoglycans (GAGs).16 By effectively removing immunogenic cellular elements, DTM minimizes the risk of immune rejection in allografts while promoting tenogenic differentiation of TSPCs and restoring tendon phenotype.17 However, in the highly inflammatory microenvironment of tendinopathy, abundant cytokines and immune cells not only impair TSPCs function but also accelerate ECM degradation, limiting the regenerative efficacy of DTM alone.18 Extracellular vesicles (EVs) with low immunogenicity, strong load capacity, easy modification, and a variety of natural biological features are playing an increasingly important role in biomedicine.19,20 Our previous work demonstrated that macrophage membrane vesicles (MM) express a variety of cytokine receptors, such as TNFR and IL-1R, which can function as “molecular sponges” to efficiently bind and neutralize pro-inflammatory factors, thereby modulating the immune status of the local niche.21,22 Meanwhile, sensory neuron membrane vesicles (SM) are enriched in neuropeptide receptors, such as those for SP and nerve growth factor (NGF), which are closely associated with pain triggers. However, the single function of natural simple EVs has greatly hindered their development for clinical translation.23 Therefore, establishing multifunctional hybrid membrane nanovesicles with good adaptability and flexibility has become the key to expanding the practical application of EVs.

In recent years, nanomaterial-based therapeutic platforms have shown great potential in the treatment of chronic musculoskeletal disorders, including tendinopathy, owing to their tunable biocompatibility, targeted delivery, and sustained release properties.24 In this study, we developed a hybrid membrane vesicle nanoplatform (MSM-DTM) to simultaneously reprogram the inflammatory microenvironment and the phenotype of dysfunctional TSPCs for the treatment of tendinopathy. As shown in Figure 1, macrophage-sensory neuron hybrid membrane vesicles (MSM) were obtained from macrophage and sensory neuron membranes by co-extrusion. MSM was subsequently loaded into DTM to obtain MSM-DTM. The resulting MSM-DTM exhibited prolonged local retention. MSM blocks the pro-inflammatory effects mediated by inflammatory factors to reprogram the inflammatory microenvironment by absorbing a variety of inflammatory factors. By adsorbing neuropeptides, MSM prevented nerve ingrowths and hyperalgesia in tendinopathy. DTM improves the local survival environment and helps to remodel the pathological phenotype of TSPCs in tendinopathy. This nanoplatform mitigated inflammation, cellular senescence, restored stem cell phenotype, and alleviated pain triggering through multiple effects. In summary, MSM-DTM has a significant effect on improving the microenvironment and promoting tendon functional regeneration and analgesia, providing a comprehensive and targeted strategy for the treatment of tendinopathy.

Results

Preparation and characterization of MSM-DTM

Macrophage membranes and sensory neuron membranes were extracted and processed via a co-extrusion method to prepare MM, SM, and MSM (Figure 1A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that MM, SM, and MSM exhibited oval or spherical morphologies (Figure 2A). Subsequently, the zeta potential and particle size of the vesicles were analyzed. The zeta potentials of MM, SM, and MSM were −24.3 ± 6.25, −28.4 ± 4.44, and −25.6 ± 8.41 mV, respectively (Figure 2B). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was used to assess particle size distribution, showing average diameters of 188.9 ± 4.7 (MM), 195.5 ± 4.4 (SM), and 201.6 ± 7.3 nm (MSM) (Figure 2C). The fusion of macrophage and neuron membranes in MSM was verified by western blot and flow cytometry. Western blot confirmed that MSM expressed both macrophage membrane markers (CD14 and F4/80) and sensory neuron membrane markers (TrkA and TRPV1) (Figure 2D). For flow cytometric analysis, macrophage membranes were labeled with 3,3′-dioctadecyloxacarbocyanine perchlorate (DiO) and sensory neuron membranes with 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate (DiI). MSM vesicles prepared from the labeled membranes were analyzed for colocalization. Flow cytometry showed that 80.8% of the vesicles were double-positive for DiO and DiI, confirming successful membrane fusion (Figure 2E). The biocompatibility of MSM was evaluated using a CCK-8 assay. TSPCs were co-cultured with MSM at concentrations of 20, 40, 80, 120, and 160 μg/mL. No significant cytotoxicity was observed at concentrations below 120 μg/mL, while a reduction in cell viability was noted at 160 μg/mL (Figure 2F). Thus, 120 μg/mL was selected as the working concentration for subsequent experiments.

DTM was obtained from fresh bovine tendons using a decellularization protocol (Figure 1A). The efficiency of decellularization was assessed by H&E and DAPI staining. Both staining methods confirmed the absence of nuclei or cellular residues in the DTM sections (Figure 2G). Compared with fresh tendon, DTM showed a significant reduction in DNA content (from 485.70 ± 54.41 to 41.83 ± 4.79 ng/mg), while major ECM components including collagen (from 758.84 ± 39.14 to 735.19 ± 32.19 μg/mg) and GAGs (from 0.95 ± 0.026 to 0.92 ± 0.026 μg/mg) were well preserved (Figure 2H), indicating successful removal of cellular components with retention of ECM integrity. After gelation and incorporation of MSM, the resulting MSM-DTM hydrogel appeared semi-transparent and stable (Figure 2I). Rheological measurements demonstrated that the storage modulus (G′) of the DTM hydrogel was significantly higher than the loss modulus (G″), suggesting dominant elastic behavior, which favors structural stability under physiological conditions (Figure 2J). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that MSM-DTM retained the fibrous architecture similar to DTM (Figure 2K). As shown in Figure 2L, the release time of MSM can reach up to 21 days. The aforementioned results indicate that through co-extrusion and decellularization processes, we have successfully obtained MSM with macrophage and sensory neuron membrane receptors on the surface, DTM that maintains the ultrastructure of the tendon and the main components of the ECM, as well as the nanoplatform MSM-DTM.

Cell-specific targeting is essential for achieving high therapeutic efficacy and low systemic toxicity in nanomaterial-based therapies. The intrinsic phagocytic activity of macrophages may facilitate the cellular uptake of MSM. As illustrated in Figure S1A, macrophages were labeled with Hoechst and 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine,4-chlorobenzenesulfonate salt (DiD), TSPCs with Hoechst and DiI, fibroblasts with Hoechst, endothelial cells with DiD, and sensory neurons with DiI. MSM were labeled with DiO. After mixing the different cell types, MSM was added and co-incubated for 6 h. Flow cytometry was used to distinguish the various cell populations and quantify the percentage of DiO-positive cells, indicating MSM uptake. The results showed that the DiO-positive rates in macrophages, fibroblasts, TSPCs, endothelial cells, and neuronal cells were 65.7%, 5.52%, 22.3%, 0.88%, and 56.7%, respectively (Figures S1B and S1C). These data suggest that MSM are preferentially internalized by macrophages and neuronal cells. To further observe MSM uptake dynamics, TSPCs were co-cultured with DiO-labeled MSM. After 6 h, DiO fluorescence was predominantly localized at the cell membrane, while at 24 h, a substantial increase in intracellular DiO signal was observed, indicating progressive endocytosis and cytoplasmic distribution of MSM (Figure S1D).

MSM-DTM attenuates inflammation- and senescence-induced dysfunction in TSPCs and promotes their tenogenic differentiation and migration

Macrophage and sensory neuron membranes are enriched with receptors for inflammatory cytokines and neuropeptides, respectively. MSM, derived from the fusion of these two membranes, inherits their surface properties, thus possessing dual capacities to adsorb pro-inflammatory cytokines and neuropeptides. To verify this, we incubated MSM with TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS, each at 500 pg/mL), as well as IFN-γ and NGF (each at 100 pg/mL), for 1 h at 37°C. After incubation, MSM was removed by centrifugation, and residual cytokine levels in the supernatant were measured by ELISA. Results showed that 120 μg of MSM could adsorb 253.96 pg TNF-α, 398.87 pg IL-1β, 264.10 pg IL-6, 48.77 pg IFN-γ, 253.70 pg LPS, and 41.53 pg NGF (Figure 3A), confirming MSM’s strong capacity to sequester multiple cytokines and neuropeptides. After confirming the binding capacity of MSM to multiple inflammatory factors, we further evaluated the ability of MSM-DTM to modulate macrophage phenotypes. We first added 200 ng/mL LPS to the culture medium, followed immediately by the addition of PBS or various nanomaterials. The mixture was then incubated for 24 h before undergoing further evaluation. Flow cytometric analysis revealed an increased proportion of CD86+ macrophages following LPS exposure, indicating a shift from M0 to M1 macrophage polarization. MM-DTM and MSM-DTM, but not DTM and SM-DTM, effectively reversed this effect (Figure S2A). RT-qPCR results indicated that co-culturing macrophages with LPS led to significant upregulation of the M1 polarization markers CD86 and iNOS (Figures S2B and S2C). LPS-induced upregulation of M1 markers was unaffected by DTM and SM-DTM but was significantly suppressed by MM-DTM and MSM-DTM (Figures S2B and S2C).

Localized inflammation is a hallmark of tendinopathy, and TSPCs play a pivotal role in its progression.10,25 Previous studies have shown that prolonged IL-1β exposure induces senescence in TSPCs.26 Here, we isolated and validated human TSPCs according to established protocols,27 with successful identification confirmed by flow cytometry (Figure S3). TSPCs were treated with IL-1β for 1 day and 7 days, respectively, to induce inflammatory and senescent phenotypes. After 1 day of treatment, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that therapy with MM-DTM and MSM-DTM significantly reduced the expression of inflammatory markers IL-6 and CCL-2 (Figures 3B and 3C). Western blot revealed that MM-DTM and MSM-DTM reduced MMP3 protein levels while increasing COL1A2 and TNMD protein levels, indicating their regulatory role in ECM remodeling (Figure 3D). ELISA of the culture supernatant further confirmed reductions in TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ levels following MM-DTM and MSM-DTM treatment (Figure 3E). IL-1β exposure for 7 days led to marked TSPCs senescence, as indicated by increased protein levels of p16 and p21 (Figure 3F). Compared with the IL-1β group, DTM group, and SM-DTM group, treatment with MM-DTM and MSM-DTM significantly suppressed the expression of p16 and p21 (Figure 3F). β-Galactosidase staining showed a notable decrease in senescent cells in the MM-DTM and MSM-DTM groups (Figure 3G), confirming their protective effect against inflammation-induced senescence.

The ability of TSPCs to migrate and differentiate is critical for tendon regeneration. Immunofluorescence analysis after 14 days of treatment with DTM or MSM-DTM revealed elevated expression of tenogenic markers TNMD and TNC (Figures 3H and 3I). RT-qPCR further confirmed upregulation of Scx and TNC mRNA levels, indicating enhanced tenogenic differentiation (Figure 3J). Both the Transwell (Figures 3K and 3L) and wound healing assays (Figures 3M and 3N) demonstrated significantly enhanced TSPCs migration following DTM and MSM-DTM treatment. These results indicated that MSM-DTM effectively reprogrammed the inflammatory microenvironment (inflamed soil) and restored the function of TSPCs (seeds).

MSM-DTM alleviates TSPCs senescence by inhibiting aberrant activation of the JAK-STAT pathway

To investigate the molecular mechanism underlying IL-1β-induced TSPCs senescence, we conducted transcriptome sequencing on TSPCs from the control, IL-1β-treated, and MSM-DTM-treated groups after 7 days of stimulation. Genes with a fold change >2 and adjusted p value <0.05 were defined as differentially expressed. Compared to the control, IL-1β treatment resulted in 1,967 differentially expressed genes (1,095 upregulated and 872 downregulated). In contrast, 142 differentially expressed genes were identified between the MSM-DTM and IL-1β groups (43 upregulated and 99 downregulated) (Figure 4A; Figure S4A). The heatmap of the differentially expressed genes showed that the gene patterns of the MSM-DTM group and the control group were similar, indicating that MSM-DTM significantly alleviated the effect of IL-1β on TSPCs (Figure 4B). A Venn diagram visualized the overlap of differentially expressed genes among groups (Figure S4B). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes were mainly involved in cellular components and biological processes, with relatively fewer in molecular functions (Figure S4C). The top enriched GO terms included “cell part,” “binding,” “cellular process,” “biological regulation,” and “membrane,” suggesting that MSM-DTM may exert its anti-senescence effects by preserving cellular integrity and modulating molecular interactions and metabolic activity. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis identified enrichment in “cell adhesion molecules,” “JAK-STAT signaling pathway,” and “mTOR signaling pathway” (Figure 4C), suggesting the involvement of these pathways in TSPCs senescence. Previous reports have linked aberrant JAK-STAT activation to TSPCs dysfunction.28 Based on transcriptomic data and previous studies, we hypothesized that MSM-DTM alleviates TSPCs senescence by alleviating the overactivation of the JAK-STAT pathway (Figure 4D). RT-qPCR results showed that IL-1β upregulated JAK-STAT downstream targets such as MYC, IL-6, and EGFR, while MSM-DTM treatment significantly suppressed their expression (Figure S4D). Western blot analysis demonstrated that while total JAK2 and STAT3 levels remained unchanged, IL-1β treatment markedly increased phosphorylated JAK2 and STAT3 (p-JAK2 and p-STAT3), which were significantly reduced upon MSM-DTM treatment (Figure 4E). To evaluate the dose-response behavior, we prepared MSM-DTM with reduced MSM input (1/4 or 1/2 of the original MSM dose during MSM-DTM preparation). Compared with reduced-dose formulations, full-dose MSM-DTM effectively suppressed the JAK-STAT axis, supporting a dose-response relationship (Figures S4E–S4G). These findings indicate that MSM-DTM mitigates inflammation-induced TSPCs senescence by suppressing JAK-STAT pathway activation, consistent with its ability to buffer upstream inflammatory cues. Furthermore, colivelin, a STAT3-specific activator, was utilized to strengthen causal inference beyond correlation. Western blot analysis showed that colivelin restored STAT3 phosphorylation under MSM-DTM treatment conditions (Figures 4E and 4H). Functionally, colivelin abrogated the anti-senescent effects of MSM-DTM, as evidenced by a significant increase in SA-β-gal-positive cells and elevated expression of senescence-associated proteins p16 and p21 compared with the MSM-DTM group (Figures 4E–4J). Collectively, these interference and dose-response data support a causal role for the JAK-STAT signaling pathway in TSPCs aging and suggest that MSM-DTM exerts its anti-aging effects by inhibiting abnormal JAK2-STAT3 activation.

MSM-DTM alleviates tendinopathic inflammation and promotes tendon tissue regeneration in vivo

To investigate the therapeutic efficacy of MSM-DTM in vivo, a collagenase I-induced rat tendinopathy model was established via local injection into the Achilles tendon, followed by treatment with PBS, DTM, or MSM-DTM (Figure 5A). Rats treated with PBS were used as the positive control group for tendinopathy. To assess the retention of MSM-DTM in vivo, a single injection of 1,1-dioctadecyl-3,3,3,3-tetramethylindotricarbocyaine iodide (DiR)-labeled MSM-DTM was administered locally to the tendon and then monitored with an in vivo imaging system. The intense fluorescence signal of DiR-MSM-DTM was observed to decrease gradually. Compared with MSM solution, MSM-DTM significantly prolonged the release time of local MSM, and the detection time could be up to 21 days (Figure S5). As shown in Figure 5B, healthy tendons appear white with no synovial hyperplasia, whereas tendons with tendinopathy exhibit yellow discoloration after 8 weeks, accompanied by synovial hyperplasia. Eight weeks after local administration, MSM-DTM treatment visibly improved tendon appearance, restoring a white color and reducing synovial hyperplasia, with superior effects compared to the DTM group (Figure 5B). Histological analysis was performed to further assess tendon architecture. At 4 weeks post-treatment, H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining revealed disorganized collagen fibers, increased cellularity, and uneven ECM distribution in the tendinopathy group (Figure 5C). These abnormalities were partially improved by DTM and significantly alleviated in the MSM-DTM group (Figure 5C). Modified Stoll scoring29 confirmed that MSM-DTM treatment markedly improved histological features compared to the tendinopathy group (Figures 5D and S6A). At 8 weeks, MSM-DTM continued to show sustained improvements in tissue architecture and collagen organization, consistent with durable remodeling (Figures 5C and 5D).

We next assessed ECM remodeling by immunofluorescence staining of collagen I (COL I) and collagen III (COL III). At both 4 and 8 weeks, tendinopathy induced an imbalanced collagen profile, characterized by an aberrant COL I/COL III distribution, whereas MSM-DTM treatment shifted collagen composition toward a more tendon-like matrix organization (Figure 5E). Quantification further demonstrated that MSM-DTM significantly restored the COL I/COL III balance at 4 weeks (Figure S6B) and that this effect persisted at 8 weeks (Figure 5F). In parallel, the M1 macrophage marker CD86 decreased and the M2 marker CD206 increased in the MSM-DTM group (Figure S6C), suggesting an immunomodulatory shift toward a pro-resolving phenotype.

Given the transcriptomic and in vitro evidence implicating JAK-STAT signaling in inflammation-driven TSPCs senescence, we next evaluated senescence and pathway activation in vivo. Immunofluorescence staining showed that senescence markers p16 and p21 were highly expressed in the CD90+ cell population in tendinopathic tendons, whereas MSM-DTM markedly reduced the proportion of p16+/CD90+ and p21+/CD90+ cells (Figures 5G–5I; Figures S6D–S6F). Furthermore, immunofluorescence showed elevated p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 expression in the tendinopathy group, which was suppressed in the MSM-DTM group, returning to near-normal levels (Figures 5J–5L; Figures S6G–S6I). Importantly, co-administration of colivelin at week 8 counteracted the anti-aging benefits conferred by MSM-DTM, indicating that sustained STAT3 activation negates the anti-aging effects of MSM-DTM in vivo (Figures 5G–5L).

Regarding biosafety, H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) at 8 weeks showed no overt pathological abnormalities (Figure S7A). Additionally, we measured representative systemic inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IFN-γ at 8 weeks and found no significant elevation (Figure S7B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that MSM-DTM attenuates tendinopathy-associated pathological remodeling, improves collagen organization, suppresses cell senescence, and inhibits aberrant STAT3 activation in vivo, with sustained improvements evident at both 4-week and 8-week time points.

MSM-DTM adsorbs neuropeptides to alleviate neuroinflammation and improve tendon function

Chronic pain is a primary symptom of tendinopathy and significantly impairs patients’ quality of life.2 In healthy tendons, nerve fibers are typically confined to surrounding structures such as the tendon sheath.15 However, under inflammatory conditions, peripheral nerve endings and tendon cells can release neurotrophic factors like NGF, which promote nerve sprouting and ingrowth into tendon tissues, contributing to pain.9 Moreover, NGF upregulates pain receptors (e.g., NMDA-R1) and pain mediators (e.g., SP and glutamate), creating a pro-nociceptive microenvironment. Given MSM-DTM’s demonstrated ability to adsorb inflammatory cytokines and neuropeptides, we next evaluated its effect on neural growth and pain-related mediator expression. Dorsal root ganglion cells (DRGCs) were isolated and cultured in vitro with NGF and the respective biomaterials. Immunofluorescence staining for neurofilament showed that NGF robustly promoted neurite outgrowth, whereas both SM-DTM and MSM-DTM significantly inhibited NGF-induced neurite extension (Figure 6A). Consistently, western blot and DRGCs immunofluorescence demonstrated that NGF increased CGRP and TAC1 expression, and this activation was comparably attenuated by SM-DTM and MSM-DTM (Figures 6B and 6C; Figure S8A). Importantly, to directly quantify changes in secreted neuropeptides, we collected DRGCs culture supernatants after NGF stimulation and performed ELISA. NGF markedly increased the concentrations of CGRP and SP in the supernatant, whereas both SM-DTM and MSM-DTM significantly reduced their levels (Figures S8B and S8C). These results indicate that the cellular membrane composition of sensory neurons is the primary driver of the neuroinflammatory regulatory effects observed in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) experiments, while MSM-DTM retains this capability within an integrated hybrid platform.

Pain thresholds and functional recovery were evaluated following tendinopathy induction. Mechanical and thermal pain thresholds were assessed before collagenase injection, 1 day before nanomaterial injection, and on days 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, and 28 post-treatment. The Achilles functional index (AFI) was also measured on days 28 and 56 (Figure S8D). Pain assessments revealed significantly reduced mechanical and thermal thresholds in tendinopathic rats. However, by days 3, 6, and 12 post-treatment, MSM-DTM-treated rats showed markedly increased pain thresholds compared to the tendinopathy group. By day 18, pain thresholds in the MSM-DTM group had fully recovered (Figures 6D and 6E). To strengthen the interpretation of nociceptive modulation beyond behavioral tests, we incorporated deeper neural readouts. Immunofluorescence of the ipsilateral spinal dorsal horn showed that tendinopathy increased c-Fos expression, whereas MSM-DTM reduced dorsal horn c-Fos activation (Figure 6F). In parallel, ipsilateral DRG immunofluorescence revealed elevated CGRP and TAC1 signals in tendinopathy, which were suppressed by MSM-DTM (Figure 6F). At the peripheral site, immunofluorescence staining of tendons collected on day 28 showed that CGRP and TAC1 expression remained high in the DTM group but was significantly reduced in the MSM-DTM group (Figures 6G and 6H), indicating that MSM-DTM mitigates aberrant sensory nerve ingrowth and alleviates tendinopathy-associated pain. In addition to pain-related behavioral and neural response data, we further assessed functional recovery and tissue-level regeneration. Gait-based assessment using the AFI revealed that tendinopathy significantly impaired motor function. MSM-DTM treatment significantly improved AFI at 4 weeks, with this improvement further enhanced at 8 weeks (Figures 6I and S8E). This indicates sustained recovery of tendon-related function beyond early analgesic effects. To directly investigate regenerative effects, biomechanical testing of the Achilles tendon was performed at 8 weeks (Figure 6J). Compared with the tendinopathy and DTM groups, MSM-DTM-treated tendons exhibited superior mechanical properties, manifested as increased load to failure, stiffness, elastic modulus, and tensile strength (Figures 6K and 6L; Figures S8F and S8G). Collectively, these findings support that MSM-DTM not only alleviates neuroinflammatory pain but also promotes sustained functional recovery and biomechanical repair in injured Achilles tendons.

Discussion

Tendinopathy is an extremely common disease, and the inflammatory response is a key component of tendinopathy.4 In this study, a composite nanoplatform (MSM-DTM) composed of hybrid membrane vesicles (MSM) and DTM was constructed and systematically verified. This multifunctional nanoplatform was designed to target the pathophysiological triad of tendinopathy: a hostile inflammatory microenvironment (inflamed soil), senescent and dysfunctional stem cells (seeds), and persistent nociceptive triggers. These results demonstrate that MSM-DTM effectively adsorbs various inflammatory cytokines and neuropeptides, alleviates local inflammation and pain, and attenuates TSPCs senescence by suppressing aberrant activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway. In a rat model of tendinopathy, MSM-DTM exhibited superior histological and functional recovery compared with DTM alone, with favorable biosafety. This strategy represents a promising approach for the comprehensive management of tendinopathy.

The JAK-STAT signaling pathway is involved in a variety of biological processes, including immune regulation, cell proliferation and differentiation, metabolic homeostasis, and cellular senescence.30 Dysregulation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway is a key mechanism driving tendinopathy, by promoting persistent inflammation and disrupting tissue homeostasis.28,31,32 Upon ligand-receptor engagement, JAK kinases are phosphorylated and activate STAT transcription factors, which translocate into the nucleus to modulate gene expression.30,33 Previous studies have linked aberrant JAK-STAT activation to TSPCs dysfunction and tendinopathy progression.28 For instance, Chen et al. developed WP1066-loaded microneedles targeting the JAK-STAT pathway, which enhanced TSPCs stemness and ameliorated tendinopathy.28 Furthermore, JAK-STAT activation has been implicated in TSPCs senescence, and pharmacological inhibition with AG490 alleviated the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) and cellular senescence in TSPCs.34 Consistent with these findings, this study revealed significant upregulation of p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 in senescent TSPCs following IL-1β stimulation. MSM-DTM treatment downregulated these phosphorylated proteins and reduced the levels of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IFN-γ) in culture supernatants. In addition, β-galactosidase staining and expression of p16 and p21 were markedly decreased, suggesting that MSM-DTM attenuates TSPCs senescence by blocking JAK-STAT signaling. Although other pathways such as mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK),35 NF-κB,36 IL-17,37 and Wnt38 are also implicated in tendinopathy, our transcriptomic analysis identified JAK-STAT as the most significantly suppressed pathway following MSM-DTM treatment. Taken together with previous studies, our results support that MSM-DTM attenuates TSPCs senescence by acting on the JAK-STAT axis.

DTM has been widely used in tendon repair due to its preservation of native ECM architecture, collagen alignment, and mechanical properties, which collectively support cell adhesion, migration, and remodeling.16,39 Previous literature reports indicate that factors associated with cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation such as TGF-β1, IGF-1, and VEGF have been detected in ECM derived from various tissues.40 This suggests that beyond providing structural support, the ECM possesses distinct biochemical functions. However, clinical and experimental studies have shown that under chronic inflammatory conditions, DTM alone fails to achieve satisfactory regeneration.39 This is largely due to its limited ability to actively modulate the inflammatory niche and cellular senescence, making it unable to interrupt the vicious cycle of inflammation and degeneration.18,41 To address this, we incorporated MSM into DTM hydrogels. MSM inherits membrane proteins and receptors from both immune and neural cells, enabling efficient adsorption of inflammatory cytokines and neuropeptides. This dual-action capability not only reduces inflammatory signaling but also alleviates neurogenic pain. In our study, MSM-DTM significantly downregulated inflammatory and senescence-related gene expression in IL-1β-treated TSPCs and enhanced their migratory and tenogenic differentiation capacities. Besides, cellular vesicles have been reported to highly express integrins, including subunits αv, β3, and β8.42,43 Specific integrins such as α5β1, α2β1, and α10β1 are known to interact with collagens and other ECM proteins.44 DTM contains an abundant component of ECM components, particularly type I collagen, which may achieve the anchoring and slow release of vesicles through the binding mechanism of integrins.45,46 Our study also found that the retention time of MSM was significantly prolonged after being loaded with DTM, and the signal of MSM was still observed at day 21. Mechanistically, our data support a working model of MSM-mediated extracellular interception-intracellular clearance. MSM first functions extracellularly as receptor-carrying biomimetic vesicles, intercepting soluble inflammatory cytokines and neuropeptide-related mediators via membrane receptors inherited from macrophages and sensory neurons. Our uptake experiments demonstrate that, in addition to extracellular trapping, a portion of ligand-bound MSMs may undergo endocytosis and intracellular processing. Previous studies on membrane-coated biomimetic nanoparticles further support the notion that membrane functionalization can enhance endocytosis.47 Following internalization, vesicles may enter the endosomal-lysosomal system, where ligands associated with the vesicles can be degraded.48,49,50 These findings underscore the potential of combining functional membrane vesicles with ECM scaffolds to expand the regulatory scope of biomaterials, particularly in degenerative diseases characterized by chronic inflammation and cellular aging.

Pain is one of the most prominent and debilitating symptoms of tendinopathy, with mechanisms involving not only mechanical injury and collagen disorganization but also sustained activation by inflammatory and neurogenic mediators.15 Repetitive mechanical loading can stimulate peripheral nerve endings and tendon cells to release neurotrophic and neuroinflammatory factors.9 Neurotrophic factors such as NGF and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) induce nerve sprouting and innervation in normal nerve tendon tissue, making this region sensitive to mechanical and chemical stimuli. Infiltrating sensory neurons not only transmit pain signals via neuropeptides but also promote immune cell recruitment and local inflammation.51,52 While current clinical approaches, including eccentric training and extracorporeal shockwave therapy, show partial efficacy in reducing inflammation and promoting structural recovery,53,54 their mechanisms remain unclear, and persistent or recurrent pain is commonly reported.54,55 Although several biomaterial-based strategies using chitosan, polylactic acid (PLA), or hyaluronic acid have been explored,56 most focus on structural repair and anti-inflammation, with limited direct efficacy in pain modulation. In this context, this study introduced MSM into DTM to achieve receptor-mediated adsorption of pain-related neuropeptides. This targeted sequestration reduced the source of pain triggers. In vivo, MSM-DTM significantly improved collagen alignment, suppressed inflammation, and, more importantly, reduced both mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity beyond the effects of DTM alone. These findings support attenuation of pain-related neurogenic signaling. This multi-pronged strategy integrating tissue repair, inflammation modulation, and pain relief provides a compelling therapeutic approach for tendinopathy.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. Firstly, the use of primary DRG neurons, while suitable for proof of concept, presents challenges for scale-up. Standardized cell sources such as immortalized lines or iPSC-derived sensory neurons may help address scalability in future studies, but their equivalence to primary sensory neuron membranes requires direct validation before GMP-compatible translation. Secondly, while JAK-STAT signaling was prominently involved in our findings, other important pathways such as the Wnt signal pathway were not enriched, possibly due to differences in modeling methodology or analysis sensitivity. Furthermore, although the collagenase-induced model is a reproducible and widely used preclinical model for studying the degradation of the tendon matrix and the subsequent inflammation-related remodeling process, it remains an acute enzymatic injury model and cannot fully replicate the spectrum of chronic overuse and degenerative changes seen in human tendon pathologies. Our study was conducted in rodent models, and further validation in large animal models is necessary to confirm safety and efficacy before clinical translation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal study

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, and conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines ([2025] IACUC Number: 4634). A total of 110 male Sprague-Dawley rats (8 weeks old) were used in the animal experiments. All rats were housed under SPF conditions in a controlled environment maintained at 22 ± 2°C with a 12-h light-dark cycle and provided with ad libitum access to food and water. All animals were purchased from Hubei BIONT Biological Technology Co., LTD. A total of 110 male Sprague-Dawley rats were assigned to five groups: intact group, tendinopathy group, DTM group, MSM-DTM group, and MSM-DTM + Colivelin group. All rats, except those in the intact group, received 30 μL type I collagenase (10 mg/mL) injected into the right Achilles tendon on days −7 and −3 to induce tendinopathy. On day 0, rats received local injections of 30 μL PBS, DTM, MSM-DTM, or MSM-DTM and Colivelin respectively. Colivelin (0.2 mg/kg) is administered as a local injection every other day for 2 consecutive weeks. Animals were grouped by body weight and randomly assigned to different groups using a computer-generated random sequence method. The assignment process was concealed by coded group labels prepared by independent researchers not involved in outcome assessment. All outcome assessments including histological scoring, immunofluorescence quantification, pain behavior testing, AFI, DiR imaging quantification, and biomechanical testing were conducted by researchers unaware of group assignments. Group codes were disclosed only after data analysis completion. Following treatment, these animals underwent follow-up observation for either 4 weeks or 8 weeks. The primary endpoint was the maximum load at 8 weeks. Secondary endpoints included overall morphology, histology, immunofluorescence readings, pain-related tests, and AFI. Rats that died from accidental causes unrelated to experimental procedures were excluded and no animals were excluded based on experimental results. In vivo experiments were conducted in two independent cohorts (Cohort 1 and Cohort 2) using animals from different batches on separate dates (total sample size n = 110; Cohort 1: n = 48; Cohort 2: n = 62). Crucially, biological materials for each group were independently prepared according to the same predefined protocol. Group allocation, treatment administration, and endpoint measurements followed identical pre-established procedures across cohorts. Data presented in the main figure represent combined results from both cohorts to validate consistency between cohorts.

Human tendon samples

Human tendon samples were obtained from the Achilles tendons of patients undergoing acute Achilles tendon repair surgery, collected from inpatients between July 2023 and July 2024. Tendons were used solely for TSPCs extraction. All experiments involving human tissue were approved by the Ethics Committee of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, and strictly adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (UHCT-IEC-SOP-016-03-01). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Cell culture

Macrophage cell line RAW 264.7 (Procell, China) was used as the macrophage source. Cell lines were analyzed by short tandem repeat DNA and tested for the absence of mycoplasma. Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S, Gibco, USA). Sensory neurons were obtained from dorsal root ganglia (DRGs) isolated from 2–6-day-old rats. DRGs were enzymatically digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA for 15 min, followed by 0.2% type II collagenase for 20 min. The digestion was terminated, and the cell suspension was cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S for 1 day. Subsequently, cells were cocultured in complete medium (Procell, China) containing 5μM cytarabine (MedChemExpress, China) for 2 days before dorsal root gangli cells (DRGCs) were obtained. TSPCs were isolated from surgically excised Achilles tendons. Briefly, tendon tissues were minced and digested with 3 mg/mL type I collagenase (Sigma, USA) and 4 mg/mL dispase (Solarbio, China) at 37°C for 40 min. The digested tissue was filtered through a 70-μm strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension, which was cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium containing 15% FBS and 1% P/S. When TSPCs formed colonies, they were verified by flow cytometry. Once confirmed as TSPCs, they were collected, expanded, and used for subsequent cell experiments. P3-P6 cells were selected for the experiments. All cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Method details

Membrane extraction

Cell numbers were counted using a hemocytometer, and equal numbers of macrophages and sensory neurons were used for membrane extraction. Cells were harvested using a cell scraper (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), resuspended in PBS, and subjected to ultrasonication (Sonics, Denmark) for 30 s. The lysates were sequentially centrifuged at 3000 ×g for 5 min, 20,000 ×g for 30 min, and 100,000 ×g for 40 min to isolate membrane fractions.

Preparation and characterization of MSM-DTM

Macrophage and sensory neuron membranes were collected, resuspended in equal volumes of PBS (Gibco, USA), mixed, and extruded using a Genizer HandExtruder-1 mL (USA) equipped with 200nm polycarbonate membranes. The extrusion was repeated 50 times to produce vesicles of uniform size, referred to as MSM. For morphological characterization, vesicles were negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid and imaged using transmission electron microscopy (HITACHI, Japan). Zeta potential and particle size distribution were analyzed using dynamic light scattering (DLS, Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90, UK) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA, NanoSight NS300, China), respectively. DTM was prepared from fresh Achilles tendons collected from the gastrocnemius-calcaneus region of 2–3-year-old cattle at a local abattoir. Tendons were cleaned of surrounding fat and soft tissues and cut into 10 mm3 pieces. Samples were treated with 2% Triton X-100, frozen in liquid nitrogen for 10 min, and thawed in a 37°C water bath for 10 min. This freeze–thaw cycle was repeated five times. Samples were then incubated with 750 mU/mL DNase (Beyotime, China) at 37°C with shaking for 48 h. The decellularized tendons were freeze-dried and ground into powder. Tendon powder was suspended in 0.01M HCl and digested with pepsin at a concentration of 1 mg/mL (1:10, w/w) at 37°C for 24 h to obtain a DTM solution. MSM was incorporated into the DTM solution to a final concentration of 120 μg/mL.The solution was then neutralized and incubated at 37°C for 30 min to form MSM-DTM.

The morphology of DTM and MSM-DTM was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, HITACHI, Japan). Collagen and GAGs contents were quantified using a hydroxyproline assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, China) and a DMMB colorimetric GAG quantification kit (Genmed, USA), respectively. Total DNA was extracted from fresh tendon and DTM, and DNA content was measured by spectrophotometer (Nano Drop 2000, USA). Rheological properties of the MSM-DTM hydrogel were assessed using a strain-controlled rheometer (HAAKE MARS III, Thermo Scientific, Germany) under 1 Hz frequency and 1% shear strain.

MSM-DTM release assay

MSM used for MSM-DTM fabrication was pre-labeled with DiI (Yeasen, China) fluorescent dye. The DiI-labeled MSM-DTM was then spread at the bottom of a cell culture plate, and an equal volume of PBS was added on top. The plate was placed in a humidified incubator at 37°C. At predefined time points (1d, 2d, 3d, 4d, 5d, 6d, 7d, 10d, 14d, 17d, and 21d), the supernatant was collected and the fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader at 580 ± 20 nm to calculate the amount of MSM released. After each sampling, the same volume of fresh PBS was replenished. All procedures were conducted in the dark to avoid photobleaching.

In vitro treatments

To evaluate the effect of the nanoplatform on the phenotype of TSPCs in vitro, TSPCs were stimulated with recombinant IL-1β (10ng/ml, MedChemExpress, China) and treated with the nanomaterial. TSPCs treated with PBS alone were set as negative controls. The medium was changed every two days while the nanomaterial was added. Cells or culture supernatants were collected after 1 or 7 days of treatment for further experiments. To activate STAT3, TSPCs was treated with 5 μM colivelin. After treating DRGCs with NGF (20 ng/mL, Peprotech, USA) and nanomaterials for one day, cells and supernatants were collected for subsequent experiments. The effects of this nanoplatform on macrophage polarization were evaluated through lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100ng/ml) stimulation of RAW264.7 cells and nanomaterial treatment. LPS treatment alone served as the positive control group. Cells were collected after 12 h of treatment for subsequent experiments.

Flow cytometry

To assess membrane fusion efficiency, macrophages and sensory neurons were pre-labeled with DiO and DiI, respectively. Following membrane extraction and co-extrusion, MSM vesicles were obtained and analyzed by flow cytometry. The percentage of double-positive vesicles (DiO+/DiI+) was used to quantify the success of membrane hybridization.

To evaluate the cell-type specificity of MSM uptake, macrophages were labeled with Hoechst and DiD, TSPCs with Hoechst and DiI, fibroblasts with Hoechst, endothelial cells with DiD, and sensory neurons with DiI. MSM vesicles were labeled with DiO. After mixing the different cell types, MSM was added and co-incubated for 6 h. Flow cytometry was performed to determine the percentage of DiO-positive cells in each population.

After 12 h of LPS and nanomaterial treatment, collected macrophages were centrifuged at 400×g for 5 min, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended at a concentration of 1×106 cells per tube in 100μL flow cytometry staining buffer (Proteintech, China). Cells were incubated with anti-CD86 antibody (Proteintech, China) for 20 min in the dark. Following incubation, cells were washed with PBS, resuspended in staining buffer, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACS, USA).

For surface marker analysis, primary cells were harvested, washed twice with 1× PBS by centrifugation at 400 ×g for 5 min, and resuspended at a density of 1 × 106 cells per tube in 100 μL of flow cytometry staining buffer (Proteintech, China). Cells were incubated with antibodies against CD90 (Proteintech, China), CD44 (Proteintech, China), CD45 (Proteintech, China), and CD34 (Proteintech, China) for 20 min in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in staining buffer. Add flow cytometer staining buffer and then analyze using a flow cytometer (BD FACS, USA). Data were processed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Nanomaterials were incubated with cytokines or neuropeptides—TNF-α (Peprotech, USA), IL-1β (Peprotech, USA), IL-6 (Peprotech, USA), IFN-γ (Peprotech, USA), LPS (MedChemExpress, China), and NGF (Peprotech, USA) for 1 h at 37°C. After incubation, the mixtures were centrifuged at 100,000 ×g for 40 min to remove the nanomaterials. The remaining cytokines in the supernatant were quantified using ELISA kits for TNF-α (ABclonal, China), IL-1β (ABclonal, China), IL-6 (ABclonal, China), IFN-γ (ABclonal, China), and NGF (ABclonal, China), and LPS (ABclonal, China), following the manufacturers’ protocols. ELISA assays were performed on the supernatants obtained after treating DRGCs with NGF and nanomaterials for one day. ELISA assays were performed on the supernatants obtained after treating DRGCs with NGF and nanomaterials for one day to detect NGF (ABclonal, China) and SP (ABclonal, China).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using the RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (Vazyme, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using a commercial reverse transcription kit (Yeasen, China). Primer sequences are listed in Table S1. Quantitative PCR was performed on a Bio-Rad real-time PCR system using SYBR Green Super Mix Plus (Yeasen, China). GAPDH was used as the internal control. Gene expression was quantified using the 2ˆ−ΔΔCT method.

Western blot

After removing the culture medium, cells were washed and lysed in lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease inhibitor PMSF (Beyotime, China) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail I (Sigma, USA). Lysates were sonicated using an ultrasonic disruptor (Sonics, Denmark) and centrifuged at 15,000 ×g for 15 min. The supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Boster, China). Samples were denatured in SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Boster, China) and subjected to SDS-PAGE and western blotting as previously described.45 The following primary antibodies were used: anti-F4/80 (Proteintech, 27044-A-AP), anti-CD14 (Proteintech, 17000-1-AP), anti-TRPV1 (ABclonal, A8564), anti-TrkA (ABclonal, A4147), anti-Na+/K+-ATPase (ABclonal, A11683), anti-COL1A2 (ABclonal, A21059), anti-MMP3 (ABclonal, A11418), anti-TNMD (MedChemExpress, HY-P81207), anti-p16 (ABclonal, A11651), anti-p21 (ABclonal, A19094), anti-JAK2 (Selleck, F0231), anti-STAT3 (Selleck, F0200), anti-p-JAK2 (Affinity, AF3042), anti-p-STAT3 (ABclonal, AP0705), anti-TAC1 (Proteintech, 13839-1-AP), anti-CGRP (ABclonal, A5542), anti-GAPDH (ABclonal, AC033).

Cell viability

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5×103 cells per well). After complete attachment, cells were co-cultured with nanomaterial for 1 day. The appropriate amount of reagent (10μL) from Cell Counting Kit-8 (Dojindo, Kumamoto, JAP) was added to the cells and the cells were further cultured for 4 h. OD450 values were analyzed using a multimode plate reader (PerkinElmer, USA). Cell viability was calculated using optical density and normalized to 1 for the control group.

Transwell assay and wound healing assay

To evaluate the ability of MSM-DTM to promote the migration of TSPCs, a co-culture system was established using a transwell apparatus (8μm well, Corning). TSPCs were seeded in the upper chamber, and DTM and MSM-DTM were coated on the lower chamber of the 24-well plate. The lower and upper chambers were filled with medium, respectively. After 6 h, the cells on the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China) and stained with 0.5% crystal violet dye (Macklin, China). Non-migrating cells in the upper part of the membrane were removed with a cotton swab, and migrating cells were imaged using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan). Wound healing assays were performed using Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size, Corning). TSPCs were seeded in the lower chamber, and straight scratches were artificially created when the cells reached 80–90% confluence. The hydrogel was then coated in the upper chamber. The medium was replaced with serum-free medium. At 0, 6, and 12 h, the wound closure was observed and imaged using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan). ImageJ software was used to analyze the healing area.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were seeded on glass coverslips and treated according to the corresponding experimental protocols. After removing the culture medium, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China) for 15 min. Following fixation, cells were permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 (Biosharp, China) in PBS for 15 min and blocked with goat serum (Boster, China) for 1 h. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti-Neurofilament (ABclonal, A19084), anti-CGRP (ABclonal, A5542), anti-PGP9.5 (Proteintech, 66230-1-Ig), anti-TAC1 (Proteintech, 13839-1-AP), anti-TNMD (MedChemExpress, HY-P81207), anti-TNC (Proteintech, 67710-1-Ig). Subsequently, cells were incubated with fluorescently conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h, followed by counterstaining with DAPI (Yeasen, China) for 15 min. Coverslips were mounted using antifade mounting medium (Solarbio, China), and images were captured under a fluorescence microscope.

For the assay of MSM uptake by TSPCs, MSM was first stained with DiI for 15 min at 37°C. Subsequently, the supernatant was discarded by centrifugation at 15000g for 15 min. MSM was resuspended using PBS and then added to TSPCs. After washing three times with PBS at 6h and 24h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China) for 15 min. Following fixation, cells were permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 (Biosharp, China) in PBS for 15 min and blocked with goat serum (Boster, China) for 1 h. This was followed by incubation with phalloidin for 1h. Cells were incubated with DAPI (Yeasen, China) for 15 min at room temperature. After incubation, the sealing agent (Solarbio, China) was added to the cell slides, and the cells were observed and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

For the immunofluorescence staining of the sections, sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and incubated with the following primary antibodies: anti-COL I (ABclonal, A5786), anti-Collagen III (Affinity, AF5457), anti-CD68 (Santa Cruz, SC-17832), anti-CD86 (Proteintech, 30691-1-AP), anti-CD206 (Proteintech, 18704-1-AP), anti-CD90 (Proteintech, China), anti-p16 (Abmart, PC23797), anti-p21 (Affinity, DF6423), anti-PGP9.5 (Proteintech, 66230-1-Ig), anti-CGRP (ABclonal, A5542), anti-TAC1 (Proteintech, 13839-1-AP), anti-c-Fos (ABclonal, A2444), anti-p-JAK2 (Affinity, AF3042), anti-p-STAT3 (ABclonal, AP0705). Fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (Proteintech, China) were used for detection, and DAPI (Beyotime, China) was used for nuclear counterstaining. Anti-fade mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was applied before imaging. Fluorescent signals were visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

β-Galactosidase staining

The accumulation of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) in the cytoplasm is a hallmark of cellular senescence. SA-β-gal staining was performed using a commercial kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and washed three times with PBS. Samples were incubated overnight with X-gal staining solution. Images were acquired using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan).

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). For transcriptome library preparation, 1 μg of total RNA was used. mRNA was isolated from total RNA by polyA-tail selection using magnetic beads coated with Oligo (dT). Libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq X Plus platform. Sequencing quality control and downstream data analysis were performed using the Majorbio Cloud Platform.

Tissue harvest

Rats were euthanized four weeks and eight weeks after the first injection of nanomaterials, and the right Achilles tendons were harvested for further analysis. In addition to harvesting the Achilles tendon, the lumbar spinal cord segments (L4–L6) corresponding to the Achilles tendon’s innervation were dissected. Simultaneously, the corresponding ipsilateral L4–L6 DRG was carefully isolated. All tissue collected at four weeks were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution. For the eight-week tendons, a portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, while the remaining fresh tendons were reserved for mechanical testing. Fixed Achilles tendons were decalcified using EDTA solution (Servicebio, China). Following paraffin embedding, 4.0-micron sections were obtained using a rotary microtome (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Modified Stoll scoring

Paraffin sections underwent dewaxing followed by rehydration with distilled water. They were then stained using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Marson’s trichrome staining according to standard operating procedures. Three blinded researchers not involved in this study independently performed histological scoring using the modified Stoll scoring system.29

In vivo retention imaging

After successful establishment of the tendinopathy model, the Achilles tendon was locally injected with equivalent DiR-MSM solution or nanoparticles of DiR-MSM-DTM. Images were acquired at the indicated times using an in vivo imaging system (Bruker, USA).

Pain behaviors were assessed by investigators using mechanical withdrawal threshold testing and Hargreaves testing. Rats were acclimated to the testing apparatus for at least 20 min before each measurement to minimize stress-induced analgesia. Tests were performed at baseline (before collagenase injection), one day before biomaterial injection, and on days 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, and 28 post-treatments. All pain assessments were conducted according to previously described protocols.21,22 The rats were placed alone in a cage with a wire mesh floor and a vented lid, allowing a 20-min acclimation period to reduce exploratory behavior. The experiment commenced by applying a 2-gram filament to the ventral base of the tail with sufficient force to cause slight bending, lasting a maximum of 6 s. If the rat exhibited an immediate or within-6-s response to the filament such as tail retraction, licking, or shaking this was recorded as a positive reaction. Conversely, the absence of such a response was considered a negative result. This procedure was repeated five times. If the filament elicited a response, testing continued with a lighter filament. Testing ceased when five consecutive attempts failed to elicit a response. If no response was observed with the 2-gram filament, testing proceeded with progressively heavier filaments until all five attempts elicited a response. A minimum interval of 2 min was maintained between each test to ensure accuracy. The mechanical pain threshold was determined by the weight of the spindle corresponding to the time at which a reaction was observed. Hargreaves Test: Rats were placed in an acrylic chamber and allowed to acclimate for 20 min. Heat was then applied using an infrared light source, and the latency time of the rat’s reaction was carefully recorded. These responses included tail retraction, licking, biting, and tail wagging. Each rat underwent four tests per experimental day, with a minimum 5-min interval between tests to ensure animal well-being and prevent sensitization. The withdrawal latency for each rat was calculated as the average of the four recorded latency values.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates (exact n is specified in figure legends). Data are presented as mean ± SD. For two-group comparisons, two-tailed Student’s t test was used. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used. Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 9 (Version 9.5.0). A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance is indicated by asterisks: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001. For sample size estimation, we pre-specified the 8-week Achilles tendon load to failure as the primary endpoint and employed a comparative analysis between MSM-DTM and DTM. This difference is relatively more challenging to detect, thereby providing a conservative basis for efficacy testing. Using G∗Power software with a two-tailed α value of 0.05 and 80% power, we calculated the required sample size for this primary endpoint. Secondary endpoints were assessed based on the study sample size determined for the primary endpoint, with the exact sample size for each endpoint reported in the legend. In addition to p values, we report effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for key in vivo endpoints. Effect size was expressed as mean difference (and 95% CI) for pairwise comparisons.
