Centralized resource allocation with technological progress under a meta-frontier framework: towards China’s “Energy-economy-emission” optimization
Authors: Fei Yang, Haitao Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efficient resource allocation is crucial for promoting economic growth and reducing carbon emissions in China. This study develops a new centralized resource allocation approach based on data envelopment analysis (DEA). It incorporates technological progress into the environmental production possibility set and further extends it to a technological heterogeneity setting under a meta-frontier framework. The environmental efficiency of each decision-making unit is encouraged to achieve its future group frontier. The advantage of the proposed method is that, it enhances the forward-looking nature of decision-making, explicitly captures technological heterogeneity within the allocation framework, and improves the incentive compatibility of target setting while maintaining feasibility. It is then applied to optimize China’s “energy–economy–emissions” system. The results show that the optimal interprovincial allocation of energy consumption and labor can increase the aggregate economic output of the 30 provinces by 6.95% relative to 2023, while generating a carbon reduction potential of 5,681.17 million tons. In addition, optimal allocation combined with appropriate incentives could further raise economic output in 2024 by 1.8% and reduce emissions by 47.19%. Severe labor misallocation is found between the eastern and central regions. Sensitivity analysis further suggests that policymakers should place moderately greater weight on economic development than on emission reduction. Several managerial implications are derived accordingly.

Introduction

In 2023, China remained the world’s largest carbon emitter, with total emission reaching 12.6 billion tons [26]. As the world’s second-largest economy country, China is facing unprecedented development challenges. On the one hand, global climate change has become increasingly severe, placing a particularly heavy mitigation responsibility on China [36]. On the other hand, China’s energy consumption structure remains highly unbalanced, with fossil fuels consistently accounting for more than 80% of total energy consumption [22]. This implies that, for a considerable period to come, China’s economic development will remain difficult to decouple from dependence on fossil energy and the associated carbon emissions. Against this backdrop, the rational allocation of resources such as energy has become an important means of promoting economic development under emission constraints [38]. For example, in 2013, China introduced policies on total energy consumption control and quota-based management [15]. In recent years, as international competition and geopolitical conflicts have intensified, global energy supply chains have come under growing threat, further highlighting the importance of the efficient allocation of energy consumption.

A substantial body of research has proposed various methods for addressing resource allocation problems. For example, Fang et al., [13] developed a multi-criteria framework incorporating fairness, efficiency, feasibility, and sustainability to study the allocation of carbon emission allowances in China. Xia et al., [37] proposed a multi-objective optimization model for the continuous allocation of carbon emission rights. Liao et al., [18] examined the optimal allocation of carbon emission rights in Shanghai using a Shapley value game approach. Zhang et al., [43] proposed a benchmark approach to allocate carbon emission responsibility, defined by the average carbon emission intensity of a specific type of product across the industry. Yang et al., [41] developed a method combining categorical data envelopment analysis (DEA) and zero-sum gains DEA to study the optimal allocation of energy-use rights across 18 manufacturing sectors in China. Among these approaches, DEA has distinct advantages: it does not require the prior specification of a production function or factor price weights, and it naturally accommodates multiple inputs and outputs. As a result, it has been widely applied in resource allocation studies.

There are many branches of DEA-based resource allocation optimization methods, such as fixed-cost allocation [7], zero-sum gains DEA [34], Nash bargaining DEA allocation [6], and equilibrium efficient frontier allocation [29]. Among them, centralized DEA also constitutes an important research stream. Adopting a system-wide perspective, it seeks to maximize overall benefits by jointly allocating resources and setting output targets for all DMUs under aggregate resource constraints. Lozano and Villa [23], and Asmild et al., [2] suggest all DMUs to be improved to best practice after allocation; while Korhonen and Syrjänen [16] proposed that the efficiency of all DMUs after centralized allocation should remain unchanged from their pre-allocation levels. Building on these foundational studies, subsequent research has considerably extended the centralized DEA framework several directions. Some studies have relaxed the structure of resource allocation. For example, Fang [10], proposed a generalized centralized DEA model that allows resources to flow only among selected DMUs rather than across the entire system. Xiong et al., [39] and Lin and Lu [21] introduced centralized DEA models under network structures, while Lu et al., [25] developed a centralized DEA approach for non-homogeneous environments. Other studies have focused on enriching the performance adjustment mechanism: Fang [11] developed a centralized DEA approach that supports gradual efficiency improvement of DMUs. Fang [12], introduced price uncertainty into the centralized DEA framework. Chen et al., [4] incorporated cross-efficiency evaluation; and Soltanifar et al., and [30] proposed a common-weights centralized DEA model.

In addition, some studies have also paid attention to the issue of technological evolution in resource allocation. Chen et al., [5] incorporated technological progress into a centralized DEA framework to examine China’s potential for carbon emission reduction, while requiring the efficiency of each DMU to remain unchanged. Using a similar approach, Lu et al., [24] investigated the optimal implementation path of carbon reduction in China, while a carbon emission pressure index was further developed by Liu et al., [22].

The above review shows that the existing literature has made substantial progress in developing centralized DEA models. Nevertheless, several important gaps remain. First, most existing studies still allocate resources with reference to an observed production frontier, implicitly treating production technology as fixed. In reality, however, production technology evolves continuously, with old technologies being replaced by new ones. Second, technological heterogeneity has not yet been adequately addressed. DMUs belonging to different groups often operate under different technological conditions and therefore may not reasonably share a common frontier. Resource allocation based on a common production frontier may consequently impose excessively demanding requirements on technologically disadvantaged DMUs, leading to unrealistic target setting and even resource misallocation. Third, although maintaining DMU efficiency unchanged before and after allocation helps avoid the potentially unrealistic efficiency improvements implied by directly projecting all DMUs onto the frontier, it also rules out the possibility that DMUs may improve their production efficiency through measures such as optimizing production and operational processes. As a result, the production targets set by the decision maker may place too much emphasis on feasibility at the expense of incentives for performance improvement.

Motivated by these gaps, this study proposes a new approach for centralized resource allocation and target setting. It extends the existing centralized DEA method by integrating the following four components into a unified analytical framework. First, it is developed for an environmental production setting that explicitly includes undesirable outputs. Second, it introduces the concept of technological progress, thereby extending the environmental production frontier into the future target period and improving the forward-looking relevance of the optimization results. Third, technological progress is modeled under technological heterogeneity, which allows the progress of different groups to be characterized separately. This is more consistent with the differentiated evolution of real-world technologies and also improves the feasibility of the resulting production targets. Finally, the environmental efficiency of each DMU is neither forced to improve to the common frontier nor required to remain unchanged; instead, each DMU is encouraged to improve toward its own group frontier, thereby enabling decision makers to use resource allocation more effectively as an instrument for promoting environmental efficiency improvement within the organization.

The main content is summarized as follows. First, we employ the meta-frontier DEA approach to characterize environmental production technology heterogeneity across different groups and construct group-specific environmental production possibility sets. Second, we extend a technological progress estimation procedure to a heterogeneous technology setting. Specifically, by observing the extent to which those DMUs that were once on the group frontier are surpassed per unit period after falling behind the current group frontier, we estimate the rates of technological progress in desirable and undesirable outputs for the DMUs currently located on each group frontier, and then construct the group environmental production possibility sets for the future period. Finally, we develop a new centralized DEA model to guide resource allocation and production target setting, under which each DMU is encouraged to improve toward its corresponding group frontier in the future period. The proposed approach is applied to China’s “energy–economy–emissions” system to examine how energy consumption and labor should be optimally allocated across provinces, and how much economic gain and emission reduction such an optimal allocation can generate. We further compare the optimal results with actual data to identify the current interprovincial misallocation of energy consumption and labor supply in China, as well as the resulting losses in economic growth and emission reduction. We also conduct a sensitivity analysis to explore how decision makers may choose appropriate policy preference weights to balance economic growth and emission reduction. Several managerial implications are then derived from the findings.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the methodological foundations and problem definition. Section 3 develops a new centralized resource allocation model with technological progress under a meta-frontier framework. Section 4 reports the validation of technological progress estimation and the empirical analysis. Section 5 concludes the paper and highlights its contributions.

Centralized resource allocation problem definition

In an organization, discretionary inputs are often limited. As the manager and decision maker of the organization, the CDM has the authority to allocate resources and to supervise each DMU in achieving its corresponding production targets. Accordingly, determining how to allocate limited resources and set production targets optimally so as to maximize the aggregate desirable outputs of the organization and minimize its aggregate undesirable outputs constitutes a crucial managerial decision problem.

For this reason, the inherent performance gaps among DMUs are retained, thereby reflecting DMU heterogeneity.

Constraint (5.6) represents the resource availability restriction. Centralized resource allocation with technological progress under a meta-frontier framework

Existing centralized allocation approaches, although they attempt to accommodate heterogeneity among DMUs, still commonly rely on a single common frontier and therefore fail to capture the essential technological heterogeneity across groups, where DMUs may operate under fundamentally different production technologies. Moreover, by assuming that efficiency remains unchanged before and after allocation, these approaches implicitly rule out the possibility that DMUs can improve performance through better management and operational adjustment, making the resulting production targets overly conservative and insufficiently incentive-compatible. In addition, from a dynamic perspective, production technology is not static but evolves over time. Thus, using historical frontiers constructed from past observations to guide resource allocation and target setting in future periods may undermine the forward-looking relevance and timeliness of the decision basis.

Motivated by the above limitations, this section shows how a new resource allocation approach can be developed by incorporating both technological heterogeneity and technological progress into the classical centralized allocation framework, thereby supporting more forward-looking and incentive-compatible allocation decisions and production target setting. Technological heterogeneity is first introduced using meta-frontier DEA; then, a measure of technological progress within the heterogeneous technology framework is proposed; finally, a new centralized resource allocation model is constructed.

Characterization of technological heterogeneity using meta-frontier DEA

Meta-frontier DEA is a commonly used approach for modeling technological heterogeneity [27, 33]. Exogenously, suppose that each DMU j belongs to a group h (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\forall\:h\in\:\mathcal{H}=\left\{1,\dots\:,H\right\}$$\end{document}). The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{n}_{h}$$\end{document} DMUs within the same group share the same environmental production technology, while the technologies across different groups differ. In this setting, each group h in period t independently forms a group-specific PPS, denoted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:PP{S}_{ht}$$\end{document}. It can be explicitly constructed as follows:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:PP{S}_{ht}=\left\{\left(\begin{array}{c}{x}_{it},{f}_{ct},{y}_{rt},{b}_{ut},\\\:\forall\:i,c,r,u\end{array}\right)|\begin{array}{c}{\sum\:}_{j\in\:h}\left({\mu\:}_{j}+{v}_{j}\right){x}_{ijt}\le\:{x}_{it},\forall\:i\in\:I\\\:{\sum\:}_{j\in\:h}\left({\mu\:}_{j}+{v}_{j}\right){f}_{cjt}={f}_{ct},\forall\:c\in\:F\\\:{\sum\:}_{j\in\:h}{\mu\:}_{j}{y}_{rjt}\ge\:{y}_{rt},\forall\:r\in\:O\\\:{\sum\:}_{j\in\:h}{\mu\:}_{j}{b}_{ujt}={b}_{ut},\forall\:u\in\:B\\\:{\sum\:}_{j\in\:h}\left({\mu\:}_{j}+{v}_{j}\right)=1\\\:{\mu\:}_{j},{v}_{j}\ge\:0,\forall\:j\in\:h\end{array}\right\}$$\end{document}

Accordingly, the meta PPS in period t, as described in formula (3), represents the convex hull of the union of all group PPSs, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:PP{S}_{t}=Conv\left({\bigcup\:}_{h\in\:\mathcal{H}}PP{S}_{ht}\right)$$\end{document}.

In this section, we formally introduce a dynamic perspective to capture the evolution of production technology over time. The proposed model (14) aims to guide the CDM how to optimally allocate resources and set production targets for DMUs in period \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:T+{\Delta\:}t$$\end{document}, denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left({\Delta\:}{x}_{ik},{\Delta\:}{y}_{rk},{\Delta\:}{b}_{uk},\forall\:i,r,u,k\right)$$\end{document}. Similarly, the objective represents the trade-off between maximizing the growth of total desirable outputs and minimizing the growth of total undesirable outputs.

However, constraints (14.1)-(14.5) no longer project DMUs towards the meta environmental PPS; instead, each DMU is projected to its corresponding group PPS, i.e., the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:GPP{S}_{h}^{T+{\Delta\:}t}$$\end{document}. At the same time, technological progress is considered for each group separately, thereby capturing the differentiated technological evolution across groups. In this setting, the potential technological progress expected by the CDM for each DMU is aligned with the progress level of its own group, which leads to more appropriate and forward-looking production targets.

Moreover, the environmental efficiency of each DMU is no longer assumed to remain unchanged before and after allocation; instead, it is allowed to achieve to its corresponding group frontier. Such improvement does not require a difficult breakthrough in environmental production technology. Rather, it depends on gains in production efficiency arising from better operational management and more effective internal resource utilization. Therefore, this constitutes a moderate and feasible form of improvement. At the same time, it highlights the role of resource allocation in promoting environmental performance improvement and indicates how the CDM can motivate DMUs to achieve such improvement, with the resulting resource constraints and production target assessments serving as the concrete instruments through which these incentives are implemented.

Application in China’s “Energy-Economy-Emission” system

China faces an exceptionally severe carbon emissions challenge and remains the largest carbon emitter in the world. Achieving economic development under the constraint of carbon reduction has become a defining theme of China’s future development, as well as a major policy challenge. At the same time, intensifying international competition and recurring geopolitical conflicts, such as the Russia–Ukraine conflict and the Iran–Israel conflict, have caused substantial disruptions to global energy supply, further reinforcing the scarcity of energy resources.

Against this backdrop, it is of great significance to investigate how energy resources, together with complementary inputs such as labor, should be optimally allocated across provinces so as to maximize China’s economic development while reducing carbon emissions to the greatest extent possible. Motivated by this, this study applies the proposed resource allocation model to China’s “Energy–Economy–Emissions” system, with the aim of providing a reference for future decision-making and production planning.

Data and parameters

Data

Each provincial-level administrative region in China is treated as a DMU. Hong Kong, Macao, Taiwan, and Tibet are excluded from the analysis because of substantial data unavailability, leaving a final sample of 30 provinces. Following Lu et al., [24] and Liu et al., [22], the 30 provinces are classified into three groups—eastern, central, and western China—according to their socioeconomic development conditions2. Drawing on the existing literature on carbon emission efficiency, energy efficiency, and productivity evaluation [32, 42, 44], and in line with the objective of this study, five indicators are selected, as shown in Table 1.

The study period spans 2015 to 2024. Data for 2005–2023 are used for model estimation in order to derive the theoretically optimal resource allocation and output levels for 2024. Validity of technological progress estimation

Figure 1 reports the estimated results of technological progress. Among the 180 sample observations up to 2023, 60 are identified as group-efficient, and it is these observations that drive the advancement of the technological frontier toward the 2024 level at their respective rates of technological progress. Panel (a) presents the estimated technological progress in desirable outputs. The mean is 1.0537, the median is 1.0321, and the upper and lower quartiles are 1.0567 and 1.0303, respectively. These results indicate that most DMUs are expected to achieve improvements in output capacity at a moderate pace, with technological progress generally ranging from 3% to 6%. Panel (b) reports the estimated technological progress in undesirable outputs. The mean is 0.9424, the median is 0.9636, and the upper and lower quartiles are 0.9666 and 0.9293, respectively, suggesting that carbon emission reductions of approximately 3% to 7% are technically attainable.

Table 2 also reports the estimation performance by group. Among them, the eastern region (19 DMUs) and the central region (25 DMUs) exhibit relatively strong forecasting performance, both outperforming the western region (16 DMUs). A closer examination shows that the weaker performance in the western region is mainly driven by an outlier, namely Chongqing in 2016. Although it still exhibits a 0.53% improvement potential in both output expansion and emission reduction, as a benchmark province in western China, Chongqing’s prospects for rapid development may be substantially constrained by its current industrial structure. This suggests that further progress would likely require a more fundamental transformation of its industrial structure.

Overall, the estimated technological progress rates exhibit good representational performance, with errors remaining within a reasonable and controllable range. This provides a solid basis for forward-looking resource allocation and production target setting. In other words, although 2024 is taken as the target year in this study, the proposed method for estimating technological progress and the forward-looking resource allocation model should remain applicable to future periods as well. However, a longer forecasting horizon may gradually reduce predictive accuracy, owing to the nonlinear growth of technology and the possibility of discontinuous technological shifts.

The main results

In this section, we apply the proposed Model (14) to examine the optimal allocation of energy and labor, as well as the potential for economic growth and emission reduction, in 2024. We further explore the trade-off between economic growth and carbon emission reduction through sensitivity analysis, and finally derive the corresponding managerial implications.

However, the actual total amount and distribution of energy consumption in 2024 deviate markedly from the optimum. A total of 1,124.9423 million tons of standard coal equivalent, or 19.45% of total energy consumption in 2024, could have been saved, but was instead wasted because of allocative distortions and insufficient incentives. Table 4 reports the optimal interprovincial allocation of labor. The optimal total labor demand exactly reaches the parameter threshold, indicating that there is no further room for labor saving. By contrast, the central region should increase labor input by 9.1779 million persons, or 4.38% of its 2023 labor force. At the provincial level, 12 provinces should reduce labor use, with the largest reductions occurring in Liaoning, Shandong, and Yunnan. Managerial implications

Based on the findings, we derive the following managerial implications to support China’s economic development under emission reduction restriction:

First, most provinces in the central and western regions still require further expansion in energy use, indicating that industrial development in these regions remains insufficient. This suggests the need to reaffirm major national strategies such as the Rise of Central China and the Western Development Program, and to consider relocating part of the excess industrial and manufacturing capacity from the overconcentrated eastern region to the central and western regions. In fact, provinces such as Yunnan, Xinjiang, and Qinghai have relatively abundant hydropower, wind power, and solar power resources. Industrial relocation to these areas would significantly promote local consumption of green electricity and effectively increase the share of clean energy use. Typical examples include the construction of data centers and computing hubs, as well as the production and processing of new energy materials.

Second, labor mismatch should be treated as a priority issue. With the continuous deployment of new technologies, the eastern region has gradually achieved greater automation in production and services, which has replaced a large number of jobs and led to excess labor supply. By contrast, many central provinces, such as Shanxi, Hunan, Jiangxi, Jilin, and Heilongjiang, as major resource and manufacturing bases in China, exhibit substantial labor demand. Policymakers and provincial governments in these regions should adopt strong talent-attraction policies to encourage workers to move to, and remain in, the central region. Institutional arrangements should be improved to reduce the practical costs of interregional labor mobility, with particular emphasis on strengthening place-of-residence-based public employment services and the continuity of basic public services, so as to facilitate geographically proximate labor mobility and better balance regional labor supply and demand.

Third, the improvement of environmental efficiency across provinces should be recognized as a major driver of both economic growth and emission reduction. Accordingly, China should further improve an incentive-and-constraint system oriented toward environmental efficiency enhancement by incorporating environmental efficiency improvement into the policy evaluation framework for local governments and key industries. Through fiscal rewards, differentiated resource allocation, green finance support, and demonstration projects, policymakers can more fully stimulate the endogenous motivation of different regions to improve environmental efficiency. In this process, the western region deserves particular attention because of its substantial potential for both economic growth and carbon reduction. Greater policy support should therefore be directed to this region in areas such as green infrastructure construction, low-carbon technological upgrading, fiscal transfer payments, and the layout of major development projects.

Fourth, in designing resource allocation policies, decision makers should avoid adopting either an “extreme emission-reduction priority” strategy or an “extreme economic-growth priority” strategy. A more appropriate approach is to keep the weight on the economic objective within a moderately high range, such that it at least exceeds the threshold at which the optimal solution shifts. According to the results of this study, assigning the weight of economic growth within the interval \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left[\mathrm{0.45,0.6}\right]$$\end{document} is more conducive to significantly improving economic output while maintaining a strong emission-reduction effect.

Conclusion

Centralized resource allocation constitutes an important class of managerial decision problems. This study proposes a new approach to achieve optimal centralized resource allocation and production target setting, addressing the limitations of existing methods in terms of insufficient forward-looking capability, the absence of technological heterogeneity, and the lack of incentive-compatible target setting. First, the meta-frontier DEA approach is employed to characterize heterogeneity in the environmental production technologies of DMUs. Then the concept of technological progress and its estimation are introduced from a dynamic perspective to capture the per-period output technological advancement of efficient DMUs on the production frontier, thereby constructing group environmental production possibility sets for future periods. Finally, a new centralized resource allocation model is proposed that allows each DMU to achieve its future group frontier in the future period, so that the resulting decisions are forward-looking and the production targets are both technically feasible and incentive-compatible.

The proposed approach is applied to the “Energy-Economy-Emission” system of China, focusing on how resources such as energy should be optimally allocated and how much potential exists for economic growth and emission reduction. Specifically, using data on inputs, economic output, and emissions for 30 Chinese provinces from 2015 to 2024, we first examine the validity of the estimated technological progress. We then investigate the optimal interprovincial allocation of energy consumption and labor in 2024, as well as the corresponding potential for economic growth and emission reduction. By comparing the results with the actual values observed in 2024, we assess the current state of resource misallocation in China and the economic and emission-reduction gains that could be achieved through optimal allocation. Finally, we analyze the trade-off between economic growth and emission reduction under different policy preferences. Managerial implications are derived for promoting China’s economic development under emission constraints.

This study contributes to the existing literature from both theoretical and practical perspectives. Theoretically, we integrate undesirable outputs, technological heterogeneity, and technological progress into a unified centralized resource allocation framework. On this basis, we propose a method for estimating technological progress under technological heterogeneity and further develop a new centralized allocation model. The proposed framework has four main advantages. First, it incorporates the weak disposability and null-jointness of undesirable outputs. Second, by explicitly characterizing the technological heterogeneity of DMUs, it improves the feasibility of resource allocation and target setting. Third, the introduction of technological progress gives the decision-making process a forward-looking nature. Fourth, it provides effective incentives for improving the environmental performance of DMUs. From a practical perspective, this study offers an operational ex ante decision tool for interregional resource optimization in real-world settings. It can not only identify regions with redundant resources and inefficient allocation, but also indicate the optimal direction of adjustment under the joint objectives of economic growth and carbon reduction, thereby providing a basis for governments to formulate differentiated and forward-looking regional resource allocation policies.

Future research may proceed in at least the following directions. First, the “energy–economy–emissions” process can be characterized from the perspective of a network production structure. Second, given the possibility of technological leaps in practice, future studies may explore how artificial intelligence methods can be adapted to estimate nonlinear technological progress. Third, the resource allocation results can be further evaluated in terms of their economic costs from the perspective of shadow prices.
