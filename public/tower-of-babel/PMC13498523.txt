De-risking Atrial Fibrillation: Refining Anticoagulation Decision-Making
Authors: Navid Radfar, Dhir Gala, Harsh Singh, Juan Bello, Joseph Allencherril
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditional stroke risk assessment in atrial fibrillation relies on clinical scoring tools such as CHA₂DS₂-VASc, which do not capture the full spectrum of individual thromboembolic risk. Contemporary trials have further challenged this traditional approach, highlighting the importance of atrial fibrillation burden and patient-specific factors in guiding therapy. Advanced echocardiographic markers, including left atrial volume index, strain, and appendage morphology, correlate with atrial remodeling, while biomarkers reflecting cardiac stress and inflammation may further refine risk stratification. These multimodal tools offer a path toward more precise “de-risking” strategies and may help identify patients in whom the net benefit of anticoagulation warrants further individualized assessment. Integrating structural, functional, biomarker, and AI-enabled data into stroke prevention models represents a meaningful step toward personalized care in atrial fibrillation management.

Key Points

Standard stroke risk calculators in atrial fibrillation were designed for broad clinical use but treat key variables as binary, failing to capture the spectrum of individual thromboembolic risk. Emerging evidence supports moving toward more dynamic, personalized stratification.Structural and functional atrial markers, including left atrial volume index, strain, and appendage morphology, alongside cardiac biomarkers, can meaningfully refine risk estimation, particularly in intermediate-risk or gray-zone patients.Landmark trials including ARTESIA, NOAH-AFNET 6, and ALONE-AF collectively reinforce that anticoagulation decisions should be guided by individualized risk-benefit assessment rather than arrhythmia detection alone, with artificial intelligence and wearable technology offering a scalable pathway toward equitable, precision-based stroke prevention pending prospective validation.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac arrhythmia, with an estimated prevalence projected to reach 12.1 million by 2030 [1]. AF accounts for nearly 30% of all ischemic strokes, placing patients at a 2.4-fold increased risk of stroke [1]. Stroke prevention in AF primarily involves using oral anticoagulation therapy, guided by risk stratification tools such as the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score. While this scoring system provides a standardized framework for evaluating stroke risk, emerging evidence highlights its limitations, particularly in patients with low to intermediate risk [2]. In the USA, Medicare Part D, which covers prescription drugs, spending on oral anticoagulants increased from around $0.5 billion in 2011 to over $7 billion by 2019. This spending accounts for nearly 6% of total Medicare Part D drug costs [3]. Secondly, as the US population ages, there is a predicted 1.6-fold increase in the number of individuals with AF by the year 2030 [4]. Given this rise, even small reductions in inappropriate oral anticoagulant use through improved stroke risk reclassification may yield substantial economic benefit.

The 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS guidelines for diagnosis and management of AF recognize that patients with intermediate stroke risk may benefit from consideration of additional risk variables and alternative risk scores to inform medical decision-making [5]. AF presents significant heterogeneity in etiology, burden, comorbidities, and stroke risk, leading to a growing consensus that management strategies must be individually tailored [6]. This narrative review aims to evaluate current stroke and bleeding risk prediction models in AF, highlight limitations of existing algorithms, and explore emerging risk modifiers with the potential to improve risk stratification and guide derisking strategies in patients with AF.

A broad literature search of PubMed, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, and relevant cardiovascular journals was conducted. Searches included studies evaluating stroke risk stratification, bleeding risk prediction, AF burden, structural and functional imaging markers, circulating biomarkers, and anticoagulation decision-making in AF. Studies were selected on the basis of clinical relevance to anticoagulation decision-making in AF, with emphasis on randomized controlled trials, prospective registries, large cohort studies, systematic reviews and meta-analyses, and major contemporary guidelines.

Limitations

Sex Category

While earlier studies suggested women with AF suffer higher stroke incidence and severity [9–11], more recent evidence suggests age dependency in this risk [12]. Specifically, the increased stroke risk in women is most pronounced in those aged 65 or older with > 1 nonsex-related stroke risk factor [13]. CHA₂DS₂-VA is a sex independent modification of CHA₂DS₂-VASc, excluding the sex category (Sc).

The FinACAF cohort study demonstrated that while women had a higher risk of ischemic stroke in 2007, this difference diminished by 2018, coinciding with increased oral anticoagulants (OAC) use among women. These findings challenged earlier assumptions that women were undertreated with OAC. In 2007, CHA₂DS₂-VASc outperformed the CHA₂DS₂-VA, but this difference attenuated over time. Toward the end of the study, CHA₂DS₂-VA emerged with marginal superiority, attributed to minimal sex differences in AF-related stroke observed in recent years [14]. Similarly, in a study of 78,852 patients from the UK (36.3% female), no statistically significant difference was found in performance between the CHA₂DS₂-VASc and CHA₂DS₂-VA scores. Female sex was not independently associated with increased risks of stroke, arterial thromboembolism, vascular dementia, or mortality. However, this cohort excluded patients aged ≥ 75 years and those with prior stroke, suggesting female sex does not increase stroke risk in younger patients without stroke risk factors [9].

A Danish observational study of 239,671 OAC-naive patients with AF with a CHA₂DS₂-VA score of 0, demonstrated no significant difference in 1-year thromboembolism risk between males and females [12]. However, female sex was associated with a significantly higher risk of thromboembolism at CHA₂DS₂-VA scores of 2, 4, and 5 (P < 0.0001). Over a 5-year follow-up, the absolute risk of thromboembolism increased progressively with higher CHA₂DS₂-VA scores in both sexes, but this increase was more pronounced in women. Importantly, the excess risk associated with female sex was evident only in the presence of ≥ 2 nonsex-related stroke risk factors, suggesting “Sc” acts as a risk modifier rather than a predictor of stroke risk.

Collectively, these data suggest that while female sex may still contribute to stroke risk, it functions primarily as a modifier that amplifies the effect of other comorbidities rather than an independent predictor. This nuance supports evolving guideline preferences toward sex-neutral stratification (e.g., CHA₂DS₂-VA) while maintaining openness to models such as ATRIA that incorporate sex within a recalibrated, multivariable context.

The 2024 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines recognize CHA₂DS₂-VASc as the most widely used risk stratification tool while noting that prior recommendations align more closely with CHA₂DS₂-VA, as score thresholds for anticoagulation initiation have historically differed between men and women. Thus, CHA₂DS₂-VA is recommended to guide decisions on anticoagulation. OAC is recommended in patients with CHA₂DS₂-VA score > 2 (Class I) and may be considered in patients with a score of 1, following a patient-centric, shared-decision-making approach [15].

Hypertension

Hypertension in the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score is defined as resting blood pressure > 140/90 mmHg on at least two occasions or current treatment with antihypertensive medication [16]. However, this threshold is now outdated, as current clinical guidelines define hypertension at > 130/80 mm Hg [17]. Moreover, the score treats hypertension as a binary variable, without considering disease severity, treatment response, or remission. In reality, uncontrolled hypertension may amplify both ischemic stroke and bleeding risk in patients with AF, whereas well-controlled or resolved hypertension may confer substantially lower risk [18]. Consequently, assigning a single point regardless of blood pressure control may overestimate stroke risk in optimally managed patients and underestimate risk in poorly controlled patients. A modest C-statistic (0.64) highlights the need for dynamic or continuous risk models that integrate longitudinal blood pressure control to better inform anticoagulation decisions in patients with borderline or low risk [19].

Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes Mellitus is similarly binary despite clear graduation in risk. The score defined diabetes as a fasting glucose > 126 mg/dL or active treatment [20]. However, in clinical practice, the risk varies significantly. For example, an individual with well-controlled diabetes and an HbA1c of 6% has a significantly lower risk for stroke compared with an individual with an HbA1c of 12% [21].

While thromboembolic risk in AF varies across the spectrum of glycemic control, with lower HbA1c values being associated with decreased stroke risk, applying this to clinical practice is more nuanced. Most patients with diabetes are on more than one medication, with fluctuating adherence and competing comorbidities, diminishing the practicability of attaining or sustaining normal glycemic targets.

ATRIA

As CHA₂DS₂-VASc has only moderate predictive ability for ischemic stroke, Fang et al. developed the Anticoagulation and Risk Factors in Atrial Fibrillation (ATRIA) bleeding risk score in 20111 (Table 1) using data from more than 9000 anticoagulated patients with AF [22]. Importantly, ATRIA was developed during the warfarin era, which limits its contemporary performance in the direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) era.

Bleeding Risk Scores

HEMORR2HAGES

In 2006, the HEMORR2HAGES score was developed to assess bleeding risk in patients with AF receiving OAC (Table 2) [23]. This score incorporates various bleeding risk factors but has practical limitations. For example, genetic factors such as the CYP2C9 single nucleotide polymorphism was included as a risk factor but was not evaluated in the original cohort due to the unavailability of DNA samples, raising concern about its practical relevance. HAS-BLED

Higher CHA₂DS₂-VASc scores have been associated with increased gastrointestinal and intracranial bleeding risks in nonanticoagulated patients [25]. Despite this association, the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score has limited predictive ability for bleeding events (pooled C-statistic 0.56) [26].

The HAS-BLED score (2010) was derived from a cohort of 3450 anticoagulated AF patients to evaluate the 1-year risk of major bleeding (Table 2) [27]. It has demonstrated stronger predictive performance than the HEMORR2HAGES and ATRIA scores [28–30]. In a systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 studies including more than 60,000 patients, HAS-BLED achieved a pooled C-statistic of 0.65, comparable to HEMORR2HAGES and ATRIA (both 0.63), but significantly outperforming CHADS₂2 (0.55) and CHA₂DS₂-VASc (0.56) for major bleeding prediction [31]. Net reclassification and integrated discrimination analyses further confirmed its superiority, with HAS-BLED correctly reclassifying up to 31% more bleeding events compared with ATRIA and HEMORR₂HAGES. Calibration analyses demonstrated accurate risk prediction in both low- and high-risk strata. Furthermore, HAS-BLED demonstrated higher sensitivity for identifying AF patients at risk of major bleeding compared with HEMORR2HAGES and ATRIA [32].

TRIALS Shaping Derisking Strategies

GARFIELD AF

Recognizing the need for greater precision in risk assessment, large-scale registries such as the Global Anticoagulant Registry in the FIELD-Atrial Fibrillation (GARFIELD-AF) have emerged, covering over 55,000 patients with newly diagnosed AF from 35 countries. This data facilitated the development of the GARFIELD-AF risk calculator, which evaluates risks for all-cause mortality, nonhemorrhagic stroke, systemic embolism, and major bleeding [33].

This score incorporates 16 variables, including age, sex, weight, history of heart failure, vascular disease, prior stroke, bleeding, carotid occlusive disease, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, dementia, smoking status, ethnicity, antiplatelet treatment, pulse, and diastolic blood pressure [34]. It also estimates treatment effects and risk over a 24-month period. The GARFIELD-AF risk model has outperformed CHA₂DS₂-VASc for risk assessment for major bleeding, stroke, and all-cause mortality. Additionally, patients classified as low-risk by GARFIELD-AF would have been categorized as high risk (CHA₂DS₂-VASc > 2). However, within this cohort, the incidence of stroke risk increased [33]. A meta-analysis including 176,082 AF patients found that the GARFIELD-AF risk tool provided superior stroke risk prediction compared with CHA₂DS₂-VASc. The ability to integrate thromboembolic and bleeding risks makes it a strong candidate for replacing CHA₂DS₂-VASc [35].

However, formal net reclassification (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) analyses have not been uniformly reported. The Danish validation study explicitly did not evaluate NRI. However, Proietti et al. did perform reclassification analyses in a warfarin-treated RCT cohort with adjudicated outcomes, revealing unfavorable results for GARFIELD-AF [36]. Using score quintiles, GARFIELD-AF was associated with a significant reduction in discriminatory capacity across all study outcomes, with relative IDI reductions ranging from −45.7% for any stroke/SE to −123.2% for ischemic stroke compared with CHA₂DS₂-VASc. These results suggest that substituting GARFIELD-AF for CHA₂DS₂-VASc may worsen patient reclassification [36]. Whether GARFIELD-AF’s higher c-index translates into reclassification across the actionable risk threshold remains unanswered, and potentially contradicted.

ARTESIA

AF burden can be assessed through various monitoring technologies, from Holter monitors and external event recorders to implantable loop recorders (ILR) and wearable devices. While wearable devices such as smartwatches hold promise in arrhythmia detection, arrhythmia duration, and burden may not be as reliably quantified [37]. Recent studies underline the dose-response relationship between AF burden and thromboembolic risk [38].

The Apixaban for the Reduction of Thrombo-Embolism in Patients with Device-Detected Sub-Clinical Atrial Fibrillation (ARTESIA) was a prospective, multicenter, double-blind, randomized controlled trial that evaluated the efficacy and safety of apixaban compared with aspirin in patients with sub-clinical AF (SCAF) detected by implanted cardiac devices [39]. The trial included 4012 patients with > 1 acute heart rate episodes (AHRE) lasting ≥ 6 min in duration with no single episode lasting more than 24 h and a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score ≥ 2 in men or 3 in women. Individuals with clinical AF documented on electrocardiogram (ECG) were excluded. Participants were randomized to receive either apixaban or aspirin. The primary outcome was a composite of stroke, transient ischemic attack (TIA) with diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) evidence of cerebral infarction, and systemic embolism. The benefits of apixaban in preventing stroke or systemic embolism were more pronounced in patients with a higher CHA₂DS₂-VASc score (> 4), preventing 1.28 events per 100 patient-years compared with aspirin. Stroke risk was not significantly associated with AHRE duration (< 1 h, 1–6 h, > 6 h). In patients with a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score < 4, the benefit was minimal. Competing risks from noncardiovascular mortality and progression to clinical AF were not formally accounted for in the primary analysis, which relied on standard Kaplan–Meier and Cox proportional hazards methods.

The TRENDS observational study suggested that an atrial tachycardia (AT)/AF burden ≥ 5.5 h was associated with a nearly double risk of thromboembolism (HR = 2.2, 95% CI 0.96–5.05, P = 0.06); however, this did not reach statistical significance [40].

The subsequent ASSERT trial may explain the modest benefits of apixaban noted in ARTESIA. In this study, patients with device-detected SCAF > 24 h had a higher risk of subsequent stroke or systemic embolism in comparison to those not experiencing SCAF (HR 5.37, CI 95% 2.08–13.87) [41]. This was the only group that demonstrated a significant increase in stroke risk, which was not represented in ARTESIA.

In the ARTESIA trial, apixaban significantly reduced annual rates of stroke or systemic embolism to 0.78%, compared with 1.24% with aspirin, a modest absolute risk reduction in a population with SCAF and lower baseline thromboembolic risk. However, this is notably lower than the traditional risk threshold used to justify anticoagulation in clinical AF. In contrast, enrolling patients with symptomatic AF unsuitable for vitamin K antagonists, the AVERROES trial observed significantly higher event rates with apixaban (1.6%) and aspirin (3.7%) groups [42]. This yielded a reduction in stroke or systemic embolism of 2.5% per year with apixaban and, with 8.3% per year on aspirin, resulting in a 5.8% absolute risk reduction per year. This comparison illustrates that the net clinical benefit of apixaban is more pronounced in patients with higher baseline risk, supporting the notion that anticoagulation initiation should be guided by individualized stroke risk stratification.

GUARD AF

The GUARD-AF (Reducing Stroke by Screening for Undiagnosed Atrial Fibrillation in Elderly Individuals) trial, involving 11,931 participants, evaluated AF screening in individuals aged 70 years and older using a 14-day continuous electrocardiographic (ECG) patch monitor [43]. The primary efficacy end point was hospitalization for stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic), and the primary safety end point was hospitalization for a bleeding event.

Incidence of stroke was not significantly different between the patch monitor screening group and the usual care group (0.7% versus 0.6%, respectively; HR 1.10; 95% CI: 0.69–1.75). The risk of bleeding was also comparable between the two groups (1.0% versus 1.1%; HR 0.87; 95% CI: 0.60–1.26). While the diagnosis of AF was higher in the screening group (5%) compared with the usual care group (3.3%), and initiation of oral anticoagulation was also higher in the screening group (4.2% versus 2.8%), the lack of significant stroke reduction suggests that short-term, one-time patch-based screening may be insufficient to affect outcomes. Notably, competing risks from noncardiovascular mortality were not formally accounted for, which may temper findings in this elderly cohort. These findings highlight the need for longer monitoring periods, targeted populations, or integrated management pathways rather than discontinuing screening efforts.

LOOP

In the LOOP trial, 6004 adults aged 70–90 years with ≥ 1 stroke risk factor were randomized to ILR monitoring or usual care to evaluate the detection of SCAF. Anticoagulation was recommended if AF episodes lasting > 6 min were detected in the ILR group. AF was diagnosed in 31.8% of participants in the ILR group, compared with 12.2% in the control group (hazard ratio, 3.17; 95% CI, 2.81–3.59; P < 0.0001). Consequently, OAC was initiated in 29.7% of participants in the ILR group, compared with 13.1% in the control group (HR 2.72, 95% CI 2.41–3.08; P < 0.0001). However, the primary outcome of time to first stroke or systemic arterial embolism was not significantly reduced (4.5% ILR group versus 5.6% in the control group). There was no statistically significant difference in major bleeding events between arms [44]. The primary analysis used standard Cox proportional hazards models without formal competing risk analyses, a relevant consideration given the noncardiovascular mortality burden in this elderly population.

The LOOP trial highlights the importance of AF burden in stroke risk assessment as it quantifies the cumulative duration of AF episodes over a specific time frame. In contrast to categorical classifications (e.g., paroxysmal, persistent, or permanent), this approach emphasizes the association between AF episode duration and clinical outcomes.

NOAH AFNET 6

While the LOOP trial conveyed the importance of AF burden in refining stroke risk assessment, the Non–Vitamin K Antagonist Oral Anticoagulants in Patients with Atrial High Rate Episodes (NAOH-AFNET 6) trial further challenged the notion that all device-detected atrial arrhythmias warrant anticoagulation [45]. The investigators studied the efficacy and safety of anticoagulation in patients 65 or older with AHRE of > 6 min duration with an atrial rate > 180 beats per min detected by an implanted cardiac device. Patients had a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score of at least 2 and no ECG documented AF.

The trial notably found no significant difference in the primary outcomes of stroke, systemic embolism, and cardiovascular death between participants with device-detected AF, regardless of the AHRE median duration of 2.8 h, who received anticoagulation and those who did not. Among patients with prior stroke or TIA, the rate of stroke was 1.6% per-patient year in the anticoagulation group and 2.3% per-patient year in the control group. Cumulative incidence curves were generated using the Aalen–Johansen estimator, accounting for competing events, providing a more rigorous representation of event rates than standard Kaplan–Meier analysis.

However, major bleeding events were more frequent in the anticoagulation group (3.2% per patient year) than in the control group (0.8% per patient year). Thus, in patients with device-detected AF and a history of stroke or TIA, the benefits of anticoagulation may be offset by an increased risk of bleeding. This highlights the need for careful risk-benefit assessment when considering anticoagulation in patients with device-detected AF and a history of stroke or TIA.

ALONE-AF

The Anticoagulation One Year After Ablation of AF (ALONE-AF) randomized clinical trial addressed whether long-term OAC remains necessary after successful AF ablation. This multicenter trial enlisted 840 patients with a mean CHA₂DS₂-VASc 2.1 with no documented atrial arrhythmia recurrence for at least 1 year after catheter ablation, and at least one nonsex-related stroke risk factor were randomized to either discontinue or continue DOACs [46].

At 2 years, the composite primary end point of stroke, systemic embolism, or major bleeding at 2 years occurred in 0.3% of patients in the discontinuation group versus 2.2% in the continuation group (95% CI −3.5 to −0.3; P = 0.02), driven primarily by fewer major bleeding events (0% versus 1.4%). Rates of ischemic stroke were low and similar (0.3% versus 0.8%), with no signal of excess thromboembolism among patients who stopped anticoagulation.

This trial suggests that discontinuing OACs may reduce the risk of clinical events in patients with documented freedom from atrial arrhythmia after ablation and modest CHA₂DS₂-VASc scores. These findings reinforce the idea that AF burden and post-ablation rhythm status, rather than AF history alone, may inform long-term anticoagulation decisions.

Provoked Atrial Fibrillation

Provoked AF refers to AF that appears in the setting of an identifiable, transient stressor such as sepsis, electrolyte disturbances, myocardial ischemia, stress, endocrine derangements, obstructive sleep apnea, uncontrolled hypertension, or other forms of medical illness. For example, postoperative AF has an incidence of 20–40% following cardiac surgery [47]. In most cases, the trigger for provoked AF is multifactorial and can include inflammation, ischemia, and autonomic imbalance [48]. Commonly, acute inflammatory responses, such as those caused by surgery or sepsis, lead to a surge in cytokines, which promotes atrial electrical remodeling and impairs gap junctions, thereby causing arrhythmias [49]. Acute stressors can also trigger activation in the sympathetic nervous system, which can subsequently increase atrial automaticity and alter atrial electrophysiology, ultimately leading to atrial tachyarrhythmias [50].

Provoked AF is often transient and may resolve with correction of the underlying cause; however, evidence suggests that these episodes are not entirely benign. There is an increased risk of thromboembolic events during this transient phase. However, the risks and benefits of continued anticoagulation need to be personalized as these patients are also at an increased risk of bleeding during an acute illness [51]. Current practice often includes short-term anticoagulation during the acute phase with re-evaluation once the transient state has resolved [5, 52]. However, the European Society of Cardiology acknowledges an unclear risk–benefit profile of anticoagulation in this context, considering long-term outcomes after resolution of the acute trigger, based on thromboembolic risk, bleeding risk, and patient preferences [15].

Overall, AF occurring in the context of critical illness or provoking factors may reflect a transient, potentially reversible phenomenon, meriting reassessment of AF burden after stabilization rather than reflexive initiation of long-term anticoagulation. Additionally, is also important to recognize that the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score does not differentiate between provoked and unprovoked AF.

Structural Parameters

Left Atrium Size and Left Atrial Volume Index (LAVI)

Nearly 30% of ischemic strokes are of cryptogenic etiology [53]. This substantial proportion of cryptogenic strokes has prompted investigations into structural and functional cardiac abnormalities, particularly those involving the left atrium (LA).

Left atrial enlargement (LAE) has shown a consistent association with increased risk of stroke as demonstrated in multiethnic [54, 55] and elderly cohorts [56]. LAE measured by TTE may reflect the degree of LA remodeling due to AF [57]. A single-center cohort involving 6602 patients with NVAF classified as low risk by CHA₂DS₂-VASc demonstrated that moderate-to-severe LAE was a strong predictor of thromboembolic events. However, measuring LA size may not accurately measure the extent of atrial cardiopathy given patient variability [58].

To address these limitations, the LA volume index (LAVI) emerged as a more robust metric of the LA [59]. Greater LAVI has been independently associated with cardioembolic stroke (CES) and an increased likelihood of AF detection in patients with embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS). In a cohort of 1020 patients with ischemic stroke, each 1 mL/m2 increase in LAVI correlated with a 7% higher odds of CES (P < 0.0001) [53]. Additionally, among ESUS patients undergoing cardiac monitoring, higher LAVI was an independent predictor of AF detection, suggesting LA remodeling may contribute to stroke risk beyond clinically apparent AF.

Furthermore, LAVI may help uncover atrial cardiopathy, which is thought to cause thromboembolism independent of (or preceding) AF. Yang et al. found that LAVI-based stratification was most beneficial in intermediate-risk patients (CHA₂DS₂-VASc 2–4). However, its predictive value was less pronounced in high-risk patients (scores > 5) [60].

LAVI has been integrated into novel risk stratification models to enhance stroke risk prediction in AF. One such model, the CLOTS-AF score, identifies patients with AF at high risk for left atrial appendage (LAA) thrombus before cardioversion by integrating clinical and echocardiographic parameters. Notably, LAVI > 34 mL/m2 was associated with an increased risk of LAA thrombus (OR 1.015, P < 0.001) [61]. While this model targets LAA clot formation rather than stroke risk, the presence of LAA thrombus in AF increases the risk for stroke. Incorporating LAVI, therefore, may be essential in refining stroke risk stratification and guiding derisking strategies in patients with or without AF.

LAA Morphology

The LAA is the source of over 90% of embolic strokes in NVAF and exhibits considerable anatomical variability in shape, size, and lobar complexity [62]. Wang et al. classified LAA morphology into four distinct types using cardiac CT and MRI: chicken wing, cactus, windsock, and cauliflower [63].

The cauliflower shape has been most strongly associated with history of stroke or TIA, whereas the chicken wing morphology was associated with the lowest [64]. The odds of TE in patients with chicken wing morphology were significantly lower in comparison with cauliflower (OR 0.38, 95% CI: 0.26–0.56), windsock (OR 0.48, 95% CI: 0.31–0.73), and cactus morphologies (OR 0.49, 95% CI: 0.36–0.66) [65].

Cardiac CT, cMRI, or 3D-TEE thus may help to guide anticoagulation decisions, especially identifying patients with chicken wing LAA morphology, who may be less likely to benefit from indefinite AC compared with patients with non-chicken wing LAA morphology [65].

Additionally, a greater number of LAA lobes has been independently associated with increased risk of LA thrombus or spontaneous echo contrast (SEC) in patients with NVAF (95% CI 1.37–4.09; P = 0.002). These findings suggest that the number of LAA lobes is a moderate but significant predictor of thromboembolic risk beyond conventional risk stratification methods [66].

LAA Function

Beyond morphology, LAA function has also been associated with increased risk of stroke. Low-risk patients with AF (mean CHA₂DS₂-VASc score 2.3) with chicken wing morphology exhibited higher LAA emptying velocities and a two-and-a-half-fold lower prevalence of SEC (OR 3.2, 95% CI 1.1–9.3, P = 0.025) in comparison with non-chicken wing morphologies, suggesting a lower likelihood of thrombus formation [67]. These findings support the value of LAA functional assessment, particularly in TEE-derived LAA emptying velocity as an accessible, radiation-free, cost-effective tool for refining thromboembolic risk.

LA Strain

Definition and Measurement Techniques

LA strain (LAε) and LA strain rate (LASR) are developing markers of left atrial function [68]. LAε is the percentage of deformation of the left atrial myocardium during the cardiac cycle. Similarly, LASR measures the deformation rate per unit of time, quantifying functional dynamics. Both LAε and LASR are assessed during three phases. First is the reservoir phase, which reflects the left atrial compliance during ventricular systole. Second, the conduit phase reflects passive left ventricular filling during early diastole. Lastly, the contraction phase reflects active atrial contraction during late diastole. The most studied metrics are the peak atrial longitudinal strain (PALS) and LASR during the reservoir phase (LASRr).

A reduced LAε and a reduced LASR are strongly associated with an increased risk of embolic stroke. These findings suggest subclinical atrial dysfunction and may occur before the formal diagnosis of AF [69]. A cohort study of 806 older adults without prior stroke or AF demonstrated that low positive longitudinal LAε and negative longitudinal LASR during LA contraction were independently associated with ischemic stroke over a mean follow-up of 10.9 years. Participants in the lowest quintile of LAε had more than threefold increased risk of stroke (HR: 3.12), while those in the lowest quintile of LASR during LA contraction had a similarly increased risk (HR: 2.89). Notably, these associations were even stronger in participants with normal LA size, with reduced LAε (HR: 4.64) and LASR (HR: 11.02) predicting significantly higher stroke risk [68]. Over a median follow-up of 28 months of patients with cryptogenic stroke or TIA, LASr and LAS conduit strain (LAScd) were both significantly reduced in patients suffering stroke recurrence or death [70]. Inversely, given the study findings, the absence of such findings on echocardiography may aid in derisking patients.

PALS was a significant predictor of both incident AF and ischemic stroke in a population-based study of 400 patients followed over a median of 16 years, with a higher risk of AF, composite outcome of AF, and ischemic stroke in individuals < 65 years old. After risk factor adjustment, a 5% reduction in PALS was linked to a 46% increased risk of developing AF (HR, 1.46) and a higher likelihood of the composite outcome. Although PALS was a significant predictor of both incident AF and ischemic stroke, the predictive value was diminished in participants aged > 65 years. This suggests that PALS may be a significant predictor of stroke risk stratification in younger, low-risk populations [71]. Although LAε parameters demonstrate independent associations with stroke risk after adjustment for CHA₂DS₂-VASc components in several studies, the shared pathophysiological basis linking atrial remodeling to conventional risk factors such as age and hypertension introduces a potential for collinearity that may limit the additive discriminatory value of these metrics beyond established risk scores [72].

Potential to Refine Stroke Risk Stratification and Derisking Strategies

Incorporating LAε and LASR into risk stratification models provides an innovative method for reducing patient risk and tailoring anticoagulation decisions. LA strain metrics may identify lower-risk patients who may not derive benefit from anticoagulation with borderline CHA₂DS₂-VASc scores or unclear indications for anticoagulation. In the setting of preserved LAε and LASR, blood stasis and thrombus formation are less likely.

Newer data support this approach. In a cryptogenic stroke study, patients with higher LASr values had significantly lower rates of recurrent stroke or death, suggesting that preserved LA strain may identify patients who have minimal thromboembolic risk [73]. Similarly, in a population-based study of 400 participants, PALS was a significant predictor of AF and ischemic stroke in patients below the age of 65, but the predictive value seemed to diminish in older patients [71].

A derisking strategy based on the LA strain could also be applied to monitoring the effectiveness of therapy. Patients pursuing rhythm control strategies with subsequently improved LAε and LASR may represent a population in whom prospective studies evaluating anticoagulation deescalation are warranted, though RCT evidence supporting this approach is currently lacking. Importantly, none of these imaging-based markers have been prospectively validated in RCTs as criteria for withholding or discontinuing OAC, and their role remains adjunctive and hypothesis-generating.

Biomarkers

Cardiac Biomarkers

The relationship between cardiac biomarkers and cardiovascular outcomes has been well-described [74–77]. There has been increasing data on the relationship between cardiac biomarkers and the risk of AF as well. Elevated NT-proBNP levels have been associated with an increased risk of AF in the older population, regardless of echocardiographic parameters [78, 79]. The Aristotle and RE-LY trials found that the stroke or thromboembolism risk was doubled and increased up to fivefold higher for cardiovascular mortality when assessing NT-proBNP levels independently [80, 81]. Cardiac troponin elevation in AF patients is associated with nearly a two-fold increase in stroke risk and a threefold increase in cardiovascular mortality compared with undetectable levels of troponin [80].

The ABC (Age, Biomarkers, Clinical History) stroke risk score, derived and internally validated in over 14,700 patients from the ARISTOTLE trial and externally validated in the RE-LY cohort, represents the most rigorously characterized biomarker-based risk model to date. Incorporating NT-proBNP, high-sensitivity cardiac troponin, and prior stroke history, the ABC-stroke score yielded a c-index of 0.68 versus 0.62 for CHA₂DS₂-VASc in the derivation cohort (P < 0.001), and 0.66 versus 0.59 in the external validation cohort [82].

This improvement extended beyond discrimination metrics. Among patients classified as high-risk by CHA₂DS₂-VASc, nearly 70% were reclassified as low-risk by the ABC-stroke score, suggesting meaningful clinical downgrading that could directly inform deescalation of anticoagulation [83]. Similarly, the biomarker-based ABCD score demonstrated significantly improved continuous NRI compared with the non-sex CHA₂DS₂-VASc score (NRI = 0.576, P = 0.040) in patients with CHA₂DS₂-VA scores of 0–1, identifying a low-risk group who might reasonably avoid anticoagulation [84]. Nevertheless, it must be acknowledged that in real-world cohorts of anticoagulated patients, the addition of multiple biomarkers to CHA₂DS₂-VASc produced only a modest increase in c-index and marginal NRI improvement, with decision curve analyses showing lower net benefit compared with the original scores. These findings highlight that biomarker integration shows the greatest incremental value in intermediate-risk or borderline populations rather than across the full AF spectrum [85].

Inflammatory Markers

Recent clinical studies have additionally hypothesized that inflammation plays a role in triggering AF. Pentraxin-3 (PTX3) is a novel inflammatory marker produced at sites of local inflammation that has gained attention. In a subgroup analysis of 382 patients investigating plasma concentrations of PTX3, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) and the first time of return to AF, IL-6 and PTX3 concentrations were significantly higher in patients with AF compared with those who were in sinus rhythm at 6- and 12-month follow-ups [86].

hsCRP has also been investigated with respect to the recurrence rates of AF. A meta-analysis concluded that higher hsCRP levels correlated with AF recurrence rates [87]. At 1-month follow-up, AF recurrence was associated with significantly higher levels of hsCRP before cardioversion (OR 1.84; 95% CI 1.14–2.98; P = 0.013) even after adjustments for age, gender, and duration of AF. It is important to note, however, that hsCRP is a nonspecific systemic marker influenced by various comorbidities, and its interpretation requires caution.

Impaired Renal Function

Impaired renal function has also been independently associated with both AF recurrence and thromboembolic risk. In a prospective study of 159 patients undergoing successful cardioversion for persistent AF, 64% of patients with known chronic kidney disease (CKD) experienced recurrence at 1 year. Lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) at baseline was associated with AF recurrence, with the highest risk seen in those with eGFR < 30 mL/min (HR 6.82, P < 0.001) [88]. This relationship may reflect the proarrhythmic effects of electrolyte imbalances, uremic toxins, and systemic inflammation commonly seen in advanced CKD.

CKD has also been implicated in thromboembolism risk. In a cohort of 10,908 AF patients off anticoagulation, proteinuria was associated with a 54% higher risk of thromboembolism, and stroke risk increased as eGFR declined, likely reflecting the elevated levels of procoagulant factors and systemic inflammation seen in CKD [89]. In the ROCKET-AF trial, reduced eGFR was the second strongest predictor of stroke after prior stroke or TIA [90]. Incorporating creatinine clearance into the R2CHADS2 score improved the net reclassification index by 6.2% compared with CHA₂DS₂-VASc.

Clinical and Economic Feasibility

Despite their promise, the clinical implementation of advanced imaging and biomarker-based strategies faces significant real-world barriers. Left atrial strain measurements are subject to considerable intervendor variability [91]. Differences between vendor software have been identified as a major barrier to the widespread use and clinical applicability of LA strain, with intervendor coefficients of variation ranging from 31 to 42%. Consistent use of the same software platform has been recommended for serial patient assessments.

Routine CT-based LAA morphology assessment also carries important real-word limitations. Cardiac CT requires iodinated contrast administration with recommended volumes ranging from 40 to 100 mL, depending on patient weight and renal function [92]. Radiation exposure varies considerably by protocol, from 2 mSv with optimized prospective acquisition to over 9 mSv with standard retrospective EKG-gated protocols [93]. Contrast-induced nephropathy risk is a particularly relevant concern given the high prevalence of CKD in the AF population, with expert consensus recommending careful evaluation before CT in patients with GFR < 30 mL/min and contrast volume minimization in those with GFR between 30 and 45 mL/min [94].

From a health economics perspective, formal cost-effectiveness analyses of imaging-guided multimodal derisking strategies are currently lacking. A Markov cohort modeling study demonstrated that biomarker-based precision medicine reduced per-patient costs by 7% ($94,932 versus $102,057 Canadian dollars [CAD] over 20 years) and improved quality-adjusted life-years by 12%, with a dominant incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of −$6,541 CAD per quality-adjusted life-year [95]. However, these findings were specific to established biomarkers such high-sensitivity troponin I, NT-proBNP, GDF-15, and hemoglobin—incorporated into validated ABC-AF risk scores within a hypothetical cohort. These results therefore cannot be extrapolated to novel inflammatory markers such as PTX3 and IL-6, which lack validated clinical thresholds and are not routinely measured in AF management.

Until prospective data demonstrate that imaging-guided anticoagulation decisions reduce thromboembolic events or bleeding complications in a cost-effective manner, these tools should be reserved for select cases where clinical uncertainty remains after conventional risk stratification, rather than applied broadly.

Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning offer a promising pathway to bridge the gap between resource-intensive advanced imaging and real-world clinical practice. AI models trained on routinely available data, including standard 12-lead EKGs and basic echocardiographic parameters, have demonstrated the ability to predict incident AF and estimate thromboembolic risk with accuracy comparable to established risk scores [96, 97]. Notably, explainable AI frameworks such as probabilistic graphical models have been validated on 1.6 million patients to incorporate social determinants of health alongside traditional clinical variables, capturing complex comorbidity interactions that point-based scoring systems cannot model and highlighting AI’s potential to deliver equitable, personalized risk stratification across diverse populations [98].

In resource-limited settings, validated AI-enabled algorithms deployed via smartphones and smartwatches, several of which have received FDA clearance for AF detection, may offer a more scalable and equitable pathway to arrhythmia screening and individualized risk stratification than cardiac CT or MRI [99]. This potential to democratize personalized AF care makes AI integration a particularly compelling direction for future risk stratification models. However, as demonstrated by ARTESIA and NOAH-AFNET 6, device-detected AF does not automatically warrant anticoagulation. Prospective validation, societal consensus, and agreement on patient selection criteria remain essential prerequisites before AI-enabled smartwatch-based screening can be broadly integrated into personalized stroke risk stratification and anticoagulation decision-making in AF [100].

Conclusions

Stroke prophylaxis in AF is rapidly evolving from a one-size-fits-all paradigm towards a more nuanced, individualized approach. While the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score remains a practical and widely adopted tool, its reliance on broad clinical risk factors, including variables such as female sex that act more as risk modifiers than independent predictors, limits its precision. The score omits atrial morphologic features such as LAVI, Laε, and LAA, which may more directly predict the risk of embolic stroke from AF. However, their adoption into clinical practice is constrained by the simplicity and accessibility of the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score. Despite its promise, the integration of advanced imaging into stroke risk assessment is not universally feasible. The CHA₂DS₂-VASc score was deliberately designed for broad use, including in settings with limited resources. Therefore, structural and functional atrial imaging should be regarded as an adjunctive refinement, employed when clinically appropriate and logistically possible, rather than a replacement for traditional scoring systems. Future risk models must strike a balance between precision and practicality to promote equitable and individualized stroke prevention.

Importantly, most existing risk scores prioritize clinical comorbidities associated with general stroke risk, rather than the anatomical and functional features unique to cardioembolic stroke. While much of the focus on LA structural parameters have centered on augmenting risk assessment, they also may clarify risk among patients in whom anticoagulation decisions are less straightforward. For example, patients with intermediate CHA₂DS₂-VASc scores or transient AF episodes in the setting of acute illness (e.g., sepsis or UTI) may be reflexively anticoagulated. However, in such “gray zone” cases, integrating LAVI, LA strain, and TEE findings, such as LAA morphology and high emptying velocities, may inform shared decision-making discussions and support closer outpatient rhythm monitoring and serial reassessment of stroke risk, though prospective data are needed before these structural parameters alone can be used to defer anticoagulation. Similarly, when AF arises in the context of a clearly identifiable and reversible trigger, the burden and persistence of arrhythmia following resolution of the acute insult, rather than the episode itself, should anchor the anticoagulation decision. This approach emphasizes shared decision-making and acknowledges that stroke prevention strategies should go beyond clinical risk scores, including structural and functional atrial evaluation.

Incorporating dynamic metrics including LA strain could allow clinicians to refine anticoagulation decisions, tailoring therapy to an individual’s actual stroke risk rather than a static risk estimate. Likewise, reevaluating variables such as female sex as risk modifiers could prevent overtreatment in specific populations, such as women whose only CHA₂DS₂-VASc point arises from sex alone. Until robust prospective data become available, these factors may be best viewed as risk modifiers, augmenting our current stratification strategies without replacing them.

As AF management evolves, integrating biomarkers, imaging-based atrial metrics, and insights from trials such as LOOP, NOAH-AFNET 6, ARTESIA, and ALONE-AF will be central to developing a multifaceted risk assessment approach. ALONE-AF further reinforces that anticoagulation decisions need not be permanent, in patients with documented freedom from arrhythmia following ablation, de-escalation may reduce net clinical harm. Emerging AI-enabled tools may offer a scalable complement to advanced imaging by integrating clinical, structural, and demographic variables that point-based scores cannot capture. This paradigm shift offers the potential to reduce thromboembolic events and avoid unnecessary anticoagulation and its attendant bleeding risks, ultimately improving patient outcomes through more personalized care.
