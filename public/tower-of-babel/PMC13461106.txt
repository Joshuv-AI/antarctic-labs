Cross-race vs. same-race collaboration in plastic surgery: AI-driven analysis of research and race-interaction dynamics
Authors: Georgios Karamitros, Heather J. Furnas, Gregory A. Lamaris, Michael P. Grant, Sofoklis Goulas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Racial diversity in plastic surgery research remains a challenge. This study employs artificial intelligence (AI)-driven big data analysis to assess racial representation, cross-race mentorship trends, and research productivity disparities among plastic surgery authors over 12 years (2010–2022).Methods:A retrospective, cross-sectional study was conducted using AI-based web scraping to extract authorship data from 24 PubMed-indexed plastic surgery journals. RaceAPI, a machine-learning-based name-classification tool trained on U.S. Census data, inferred racial identity. Chi-square tests were used to evaluate racial disparities in first and senior authorship, mentorship patterns, and research output.Results:Among 16 019 publications, White researchers remained the dominant group among first authors (70% in 2010 vs. 66.2% in 2022, P < 0.001) and senior authors (73.8% vs. 72.6%, P < 0.01). Black first and senior authorship declined (8.1% to 6.4%, P < 0.001; 9.2% to 6.1%, P < 0.001), while Asian representation increased (first: 16% to 18.8%, P = 0.027; senior: 11.7% to 16%, P < 0.001). White authors had significantly higher research productivity (2.15 papers per author) than Asian (1.97), Black (1.76), and Hispanic (1.68) researchers (P < 0.05). Black senior authors had the highest rate of cross-race mentorship (77.5%), while White (26.8%) and Asian (27.3%) senior authors exhibited strong same-race mentorship preferences.Conclusions:Persistent racial disparities in authorship and research participation among racial groups that are persistently underrepresented in medical school and plastic surgery residencies suggest the need to assess and address educational barriers prior to medical school. Strengthening mentorship networks and increasing access to research for minority trainees could foster a thriving plastic surgery workforce for all communities, including those currently underserved.Level of Evidence:Level III, cross-sectional study.

Introduction

Plastic surgery remains one of the most competitive medical specialties where academic productivity serves as a pivotal differentiator for aspiring surgeons[1–3]. Peer-reviewed publications, particularly first-author papers, have become an increasingly influential metric in securing residency positions, fellowships, and faculty appointments[4–7]. The racial and ethnic disparities that persist within surgical specialties[8–10] are frequently rooted in differential access to the mentorship that can facilitate career progression[11–15]. Minority trainees often struggle to find meaningful mentorship[16–29], potentially contributing to their landing few senior academic positions.

Although previous studies have examined gender diversity within the plastic surgery workforce at various academic levels[30–38], less attention has been devoted to racial and ethnic representation. Given that authorship in high-impact journals functions as both an academic currency and a pathway to leadership, understanding disparities may help uncover opportunities for professional growth. Prior studies have explored racial representation at the trainee and faculty levels, but not in plastic surgery research authorship.

To identify racial and ethnic representation in plastic surgery publications, we employed an artificial intelligence (AI)-driven web-scraping methodology to systematically analyze racial diversity within plastic surgery research over a 12-year period (2010–2022). Specifically, we sought to quantify racial representation among first and senior authors in plastic surgery journals and characterize patterns of cross-race collaboration between established and early-career researchers[10,39–42]. By leveraging big data analytics and AI methodologies, this study provides critical insights into the structural factors influencing research participation and mentorship accessibility are presented. These findings offer a data-driven foundation for future interventions aimed at fostering a more inclusive academic landscape within the field of plastic surgery.

Methods

Author and publication data collection

To accurately assess the contributions of early-career researchers in plastic surgery, we focused exclusively on first authorship, as junior researchers are more likely to be the primary drivers of research projects in which they are listed as first authors[43,44]. To evaluate the influence of mentorship, we also analyzed the senior (last) authors of these publications, given their role in guiding and supporting early-career researchers[45,46]. First and senior authorship positions were used as proxies for structured academic collaboration between early-career and more established researchers. In biomedical research, this authorship configuration commonly reflects a hierarchical relationship in which senior authors provide intellectual oversight, project direction, and guidance, while first authors lead the execution of the work. Although this structure does not directly measure mentorship, it represents a widely accepted approximation of collaborative dynamics that may include mentorship-related interactions[45,46].

Race and ethnicity were inferred using a name-based classification algorithm (RaceAPI) derived from U.S. Census data. This approach provides probabilistic estimates based on naming patterns and should not be interpreted as reflecting self-identified race or ethnicity at the individual level. Name-based inference methods are commonly used in large-scale population studies where self-reported demographic data are unavailable; however, they are subject to inherent limitations, including potential misclassification, reduced accuracy for certain groups, and challenges in capturing multiracial or culturally heterogeneous identities[10,39–42,47,48,52,53].

Given these constraints, all analyses were conducted and interpreted at the aggregate level.

Individual-level classifications were not used to make inferences about specific authors. The potential for differential misclassification across groups was considered when interpreting results.

Statistical analysis

Publication data for first and senior authors were categorized by race: Arab, Asian, Black, Hispanic, and non-Hispanic White. Statistical analyses were conducted using chi-square tests to evaluate differences in categorical variables across groups. Significance thresholds were set at α = 0.05 and α = 0.01 to distinguish between conventional and more stringent levels of statistical significance. Given the exploratory nature of this large-scale analysis, adjustments for multiple comparisons were not applied; results were interpreted with appropriate caution in this context. Where applicable, effect sizes and corresponding confidence intervals are reported to complement p-values and provide additional context for the magnitude of observed differences[54]. All analyses were conducted using Stata 17.0.

Discussion

Underserved communities have been found to have better patient outcomes with racially and culturally congruent healthcare providers[55], and minority physicians are more likely to practice in these underserved areas[56–61]. Racial and ethnic concordance between patients and physicians has been associated with enhanced patient trust, improved communication, and increased patient satisfaction, all of which likely contribute to better patient outcomes[33,56–58,61,62].

To identify possible bottlenecks to career progression, this study used AI-based methodology to quantify racially and ethnically-based authorship trends over a 12-year period. (1) Our findings revealed disparities that persisted over the 12 years, with only a slight decline in the majority status of non-Hispanic White researchers (first and senior authors) over time. Asian and Hispanic authorship showed slight increases, and Black authorship declined. (2) Non-Hispanic White researchers demonstrated significantly higher publication output per author compared to their Asian, Black, and Hispanic counterparts. (3) Race- or ethnicity-congruent authorship varied, with Black senior authors showing the lowest congruent co-authorship and Hispanic senior authors displaying the highest congruent authorship of all groups studied. (4) Lastly, Black researchers are not only underrepresented relative to their Hispanic and Asian peers but also demonstrate no significant author congruence. These points raise questions: Does race-based congruence in authorship demonstrate mentorship? If so, is race-based congruence in mentorship more effective than cross-racial mentorship? If racially congruent mentoring is more effective, does the scarcity of Black senior researchers inherently limit academic progression for Black junior researchers within the field? If race-congruent mentorship is particularly effective, having few Black plastic surgeons in senior positions may perpetuate disparities in achieving greater Black representation at every stage of professional development.

In interpreting these patterns, it is important to consider alternative explanations for observed differences in research participation. An additional consideration in interpreting these findings is the role of differential engagement with academic research pathways. Although variations in research participation are sometimes discussed in terms of individual preferences or interests, current evidence does not support inherent differences across racial or ethnic groups. Rather, observed disparities in authorship and productivity are more appropriately understood within the context of unequal access to opportunities across the educational continuum. Factors such as early exposure to research, availability of mentorship, institutional resources, and sustained academic support play a critical role in shaping participation in scholarly activities[63–65]. Differences in these structural and environmental conditions can influence both entry into research pathways and long-term academic productivity. Future studies incorporating longitudinal and survey-based approaches may further clarify how these factors interact over time to influence academic engagement.

A limited pipeline: identifying the points of attrition

Only 2% of plastic surgery program directors and department heads are Black, and fewer than 2.8% of academic plastic surgery faculty are Black, despite Black individuals comprising 12.7% of the U.S. population[66]. Hispanic representation follows a similar trend, with Hispanic physicians accounting for only 4.8% of plastic surgery faculty and 0% of program directors or division chiefs[66]. Once these drop-offs occur at the faculty level, there is little to no recovery at the national leadership level, with only one Black individual and no Hispanic individuals ever serving as presidents of major plastic surgery societies or as editors-in-chief of leading journals[67].

Possible causes of underrepresentation of Blacks and Hispanics in plastic surgery, particularly in leadership positions, may be related to the pipeline: high school graduation rates; college attendance and graduation rates; medical school application, acceptance, and graduation rates; and plastic surgery residency application, acceptance, and completion rates.

Examining the educational pipeline: where does the gap begin?

Understanding the persistent underrepresentation of Black and Hispanic researchers in academic plastic surgery requires an examination of the earlier stages of the educational pipeline. Data indicate that despite rising application rates to medical school, acceptance rates among Black and Hispanic students have declined[68,69]. This suggests that while interest in medicine is growing among underrepresented minorities, many applicants may not be academically prepared to meet the rigorous demands of medical education.

Recent data from the 2024–2025 medical school application cycle show that Black applicants increased by 2.8% and Hispanic applicants by 2.2%, yet their matriculation rates dropped by 11.6% and 10.8%, respectively. In contrast, Asian applicants increased by 3.8% with an 8.4% rise in matriculants, making them the only racial/ethnic group to see a net increase in enrollment. White applicants decreased by 5.2%, with a relatively small drop in matriculation (−0.3%), whereas American Indian/Alaska Native students experienced the largest declines in both applicants (−15.4%) and matriculates (−22.1%)[68]. These disparities highlight challenges beyond medical school admissions, reflecting deeper barriers in academic preparation and support.

Nationally, Black students graduate from high school at rates ranging from 68% to 88%, Hispanic students between 69% and 88%, Asian/Pacific Islander students from 78% to 97%, and White students achieve significantly higher rates, from 82% to 96%[70]. These early disparities persist at the undergraduate level – only 23% of Black students and 30% of Hispanic students complete college within 150% of the expected time, compared with 32% of White students and 36% of Asian students[71]. The long-term impact of these educational gaps is evident in the attainment of higher education: from 2012 to 2022, the percentage of adults aged 25 and older with a bachelor’s degree or higher increased from 34.5% to 41.8% for the non-Hispanic White population, from 21.2% to 27.6% for the Black population, from 51% to 59.3% for the Asian population, and from 14.5% to 20.9% for the Hispanic population[72].

These findings highlight barriers that extend beyond medical school admissions. The declining matriculation rates for Black and Hispanic students despite increased application numbers suggest that early educational disadvantages persist throughout the academic pipeline. If gaps in K-12 preparation and access to academic support are not addressed, increasing application numbers alone will not be sufficient to diversify the medical and surgical workforce. Strengthening early education and ensuring access to mentorship, enrichment programs, and structured academic resources at earlier stages could play a pivotal role in bridging this gap and expanding the pool of competitive applicants for plastic surgery and other competitive medical specialties.

The role of residency selection and research productivity

Racial disparities in plastic surgery persist beyond medical school admissions, extending into residency applications, selection, and research engagement. Data from 2010 to 2016 reveal that although Black applicants to integrated plastic surgery programs increased by 0.42% per year, their representation among residents declined at the same rate, from 4.5% to 2.2%, marking a statistically significant gap[36]. Similarly, while Hispanic applicants fluctuated around 10% annually, their representation among residents remained consistently lower, at approximately 6–7%[36].

These trends are further reflected in long-term residency demographics. While Asian and Hispanic representation in plastic surgery residencies has increased nearly threefold and twofold, respectively, from 1995 to 2014, Black representation has remained stagnant (3.0% in 1995 vs. 3.5% in 2014, P = 0.129)[33]. Additionally, relative to the U.S. population, Black (0.1–0.4-fold) and Hispanic (0.1–0.5-fold) individuals remain underrepresented, whereas Asians are overrepresented (2.2–5.3-fold). Despite an increasing number of Black and Hispanic applicants, their disproportionately lower acceptance rates suggest persistent challenges in pre-residency competitiveness and selection criteria. This discrepancy again likely reflects hurdles in early education, mentorship access, and institutional support, which compound over time to create a cycle of underrepresentation[36].

Our findings indicate that these trends extend to academic research productivity, with non-Hispanic White researchers producing significantly more publications per author compared with their Black, Hispanic, and Asian counterparts. While causation remains speculative, several structural advantages may contribute to this trend. Non-Hispanic White researchers are more likely to be affiliated with historically well-funded institutions that benefit from extensive research grants, dedicated mentorship programs, and established academic networks – factors that collectively facilitate higher publication output. Additionally, the age distribution of researchers may play a role, as senior academics, who have had more time to cultivate research portfolios and collaborative networks, are disproportionately represented among White faculty. Access to experienced mentors, who provide critical guidance in navigating the research landscape, further reinforces this advantage. While these factors do not apply to every individual, they collectively create an environment where non-Hispanic White researchers have greater access to resources, mentorship, and funding – key advantages that likely contribute to higher research productivity compared to their minority counterparts.

Strategies for strengthening the pipeline

Mentorship has been shown to be a powerful tool for fostering academic and professional success, particularly among underrepresented minorities[11–14]. However, without a sufficient pipeline of Black and Hispanic students progressing through each stage of education, mentorship alone may not be adequate to bridge the gap. Addressing educational disparities beginning in K-12 should enhance student success in college and ultimately improve their ability to thrive in medical school and residency. Outreach efforts in communities with limited academic resources, providing academic support, mentorship, and early exposure to the medical field at the high school and college levels, could help expand the pool of competitive applicants entering plastic surgery.

While increasing the number of minority faculty is a long-term goal, immediate steps can be taken to enhance cross-race mentorship opportunities. Given the limited representation of Black senior authors in our study, mentorship programs that connect underrepresented trainees with experienced faculty – regardless of racial background – may help fill this gap.

One initiative geared toward underrepresented minority students, Plastic Surgery Research, Education, and Preparation Promoting Equity and Diversity (PREPPED), is designed to identify and nurture medical students who might not otherwise have considered a career in plastic surgery[32,73]. Supported by the Plastic Surgery Foundation Diversity and Inclusion Grant, PREPPED offers a 2-day immersive experience aimed at equipping students from historically disadvantaged backgrounds with critical technical skills, mentorship, and early exposure to plastic surgery[73]. Participants engage in hands-on surgical training, interactive workshops, one-on-one mentorship with plastic surgeons, and guided research project development, culminating in residency application preparation. Beyond traditional mentorship at the residency level, PREPPED underscores the importance of early-stage intervention, ensuring that underrepresented students gain competitive experience before medical school, thereby increasing the likelihood of securing a plastic surgery residency. Programs like the University of California San Diego (UCSD) Diversity Mentorship Program support students from disadvantaged backgrounds by pairing them with mentors, providing research training, and offering technical skill-building sessions[74,75]. These structured initiatives increase the competitiveness of underrepresented students in the highly selective residency match process by ensuring consistent mentorship throughout medical school. However, to create a lasting impact, pipeline programs must extend further back in the educational trajectory.

Limitations

This study is a retrospective analysis evaluating racial representation in plastic surgery research over a 12-year period (2010–2022). While this provides valuable insights into long-term trends, it limits our ability to predict future shifts influenced by evolving policies or societal changes[15]. Additionally, the retrospective nature precludes causal inferences, and findings should be interpreted within the historical context analyzed rather than as a forecast of future trends.

A key limitation is the reliance on name-based inference methods due to the absence of self-reported racial data. While our AI-driven classification tool provides a systematic approach, race prediction remains an approximation. Name ambiguity, multiracial identities, and regional variations in naming conventions introduce inherent uncertainties. Moreover, race alone is an imprecise metric for assessing who is best equipped to serve underserved populations. While racial concordance between physicians and patients has been linked to improved trust and outcomes, factors such as linguistic fluency, shared experiences, and socioeconomic awareness may play a more pivotal role in addressing healthcare disparities.

Also, there is reliance on name-based inference to estimate race and ethnicity. While this approach enables large-scale analysis in the absence of self-reported demographic data, it is inherently probabilistic and subject to misclassification. Accuracy may vary across racial and ethnic groups, and the method may not adequately capture multiracial identities or variations in naming conventions. As a result, findings should be interpreted at the population level rather than as precise representations of individual identity. Future studies incorporating self-reported demographic data and validation of classification approaches are needed to further refine these analyses.

Race-inferring software such as RaceAPI relies on probabilistic models that associate personal names with racial and ethnic self-identification patterns derived from large datasets, including the U.S. Census. We recognize that race inference is a complex task and that individuals with the same name may identify with different racial or ethnic groups. Future research could improve accuracy by incorporating self-reported author demographic information – currently unavailable at scale. However, given the well-documented underrepresentation of minority groups in highly competitive and prestigious fields such as plastic surgery, we believe it is important to analyze the available

Input data to begin addressing these disparities – even if current methods are not perfectly precise.

Our use of publication data as a proxy for research representation also presents limitations. Bibliometric analyses do not account for contributions in clinical practice, mentorship, or industry roles[76,77]. Additionally, systemic biases in authorship – including disparities in publishing opportunities and senior author allocations – may skew observed trends, leading to potential misclassification of research productivity.

This study also assumes a correlation between first and senior authorship as a proxy for mentorship. While our data reveal strong tendencies for racial congruence in mentorship, they do not capture the complexity of these relationships[27,78,79]. Cross-race mentorship may be equally – if not more – effective, particularly when senior researchers foster inclusive mentorship environments. Additionally, informal sponsorship, networking, and direct career guidance are not captured in authorship-based analyses, making it difficult to infer the depth of these professional relationships. While first–senior author pairings are commonly used as a proxy for hierarchical collaboration and may reflect mentorship or guidance relationships, authorship order alone does not establish the presence, quality, or directionality of mentorship. Collaborative structures vary across institutions and cultural contexts, and mentorship may occur outside formal authorship roles. Therefore, findings should be interpreted as reflecting patterns of co-authorship that may, but do not necessarily, correspond to mentorship relationships.

Lastly, given the number of statistical comparisons performed, there is a potential risk of type I error[54,80]. Formal adjustments for multiple testing were not applied, as the analysis was primarily exploratory and descriptive in nature; however, findings should be interpreted with this consideration in mind. Additionally, while the present study focuses on descriptive comparisons across time, more advanced regression-based approaches (e.g., logistic or count models) may further elucidate temporal trends and should be considered in future work.

Despite these constraints, our study leverages AI-driven big data analytics[41] to quantify systemic trends that are otherwise difficult to measure, offering a data-driven foundation for future interventions. Future research should integrate self-reported demographic data, adopt prospective study designs, and employ advanced statistical techniques – such as interaction analysis and hierarchical modeling[15,81,82] – to refine interpretations and develop evidence-based strategies for fostering a more inclusive academic environment.

Conclusion

This assessment of racial diversity among U.S. plastic surgery researchers suggests the need to expand the pipeline of underrepresented minority researchers, which could potentially improve healthcare outcomes in minority communities. The time and funding to support outreach and mentorship programs at K-12 grade levels and through college, medical school, and residency would be a solid investment in cultivating a future surgical workforce with the talent needed to provide high-quality care to underserved communities.
