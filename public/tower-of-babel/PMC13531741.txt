Safety and feasibility of rotational atherectomy in chronic total occlusion: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ahmed Abdelaziz, Hanaa Elsayed, Aboalmagd Hamdaalah, Karim Atta, Ahmed Mechi, Hallas Kadhim, Aya Moustafa Aboutaleb, Ahmed Elaraby, Mohamed Hatem Ellabban, Fayed Mohamed rzk, Mahmoud Eid, Hadeer Elsaeed AboElfarh, Rahma AbdElfattah Ibrahim, Emad addin Zawaneh, Mahmoud Ezzat, Mohamed Abdelaziz, Shaimaa Fadel, Mohamed Yasser Elnaggar, Ahmed O. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is a lack of data available on the feasibility of rotational atherectomy (RA) in chronic total occlusion (CTO) lesions compared to RA in non-CTO lesions. So, we aimed to study the safety and feasibility of RA in patients with CTO lesions compared to non-CTO lesions.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science (WOS), and Ovid until March 2024. We included all relevant studies assessing RA in CTO lesions versus non-CTO lesions. The primary outcome of interest was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), while secondary outcomes were all-cause death, cardiac death, myocardial infarction (MI), and target-vessel revascularization (TVR). Other studied angiographic outcomes were vessel perforation, technical success, and procedural success. The pooled data was analyzed using odds ratio (OR) with its 95% CI in using a random-effect model.ResultsFour studies comprising 1925 patients were included in the analysis. The pooled analysis showed that RA in CTO lesions was associated with higher rates of vessel perforation (OR = 3.56, 95% CI: 1.59–7.98, p < 0.001) compared to the non-CTO lesions. On the other hand, no significant differences were observed regarding studied clinical outcomes and angiographic outcomes between CTO and non-CTO lesions.ConclusionRA for CTO lesions was associated with comparable clinical and angiographic outcomes. RA is a safe and feasible approach in CTO lesions, despite a higher incidence of vessel perforation. More investigations on the severity of each case should be guided in further studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-03866-8.

Introduction

Chronic total occlusion (CTO) occurs in approximately 18.4–26.4% of patients with significant coronary artery disease (CAD) [1, 2]. More than 50% of CTO lesions exhibit moderate to severe calcification [3]. Managing complex CTO lesions is often considered as the most challenging aspect of percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) [4], because it demands effective therapeutic methods, advanced expertise, specialized equipment and considerable patience. In addition, factors such as comorbidities as diabetes mellitus (DM) and renal failure, as well as an increasing number of patients developing CTOs after coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) failures, are expected to significantly increase the impact of CTOs in the future [1].

The success rate of PCI for CTO using standard procedures has recently been reported to be between 60 and 90% when performed by experienced operators [5, 6]. The primary reason for CTO PCI failure is the difficulty of the guidewire to pass through the lesions, followed by the inability of the smallest balloon to pass through heavily calcified lesions [7, 8]. Approximately 6% to 9% of all CTOs are classified as balloon uncrossable, while un-dilatable lesions account for around 12% of CTO lesions [9].

For over thirty years, rotational atherectomy (RA) has been has been used to treat severely calcified coronary lesions, including those in the left main, bifurcation, long lesions, and CTO lesions, however, it is not commonly used for elective treatment of calcific CTO and usually reserved for balloon-resistant lesions [10–12]. It is primarily used to prepare the entire lesion before stenting and to facilitate device passage [13]. Poor long-term outcomes are often associated with stents that are not fully expanded [14]. Clinical factors such as age, diabetes, renal function, left ventricular function, and acute coronary syndrome may affect the outcomes of RA for coronary lesions [15, 16].

While RA is established to be used for managing severely calcified lesions, its application in the challenging nature of CTOs raises specific efficacy and safety concerns. Individual studies on RA application in CTOs are limited and often report conflict results when compared to its use in non-CTO lesions [17, 18].

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to address the clinical conflict of the current evidence by direct comparation of safety and feasibility of RA in CTO lesions compared to RA in non-CTO lesions, and to provide valuable insights to address this gap of knowledge.

Methods

We conducted this meta-analysis in accordance with the recommendations provided in the PRISMA statement for systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Additionally, we followed the guidelines outlined in the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis of Interventions, version 5.1.0.

Eligibility criteria

During our analysis, we included all relevant studies, including both randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies, that examined the use of RA in patients with CTO lesions as the intervention group, and patients with non-CTO lesions as the control group. We reported outcomes of interest using an intention-to-treat analysis. We further excluded animal studies, conference abstracts, and unpublished data.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was the in-hospital rates of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), as defined by each individual study, shown in Supplementary Table 1. Furthermore, we examined various secondary outcomes, such as in-hospital all-cause death, cardiac death, myocardial infarction (MI), target-vessel revascularization (TVR). Other angiographic studied outcomes were the rates of vessel perforation, procedural success, and technical success. The definitions of these outcomes were based on the descriptions provided by the authors of the individual studies.

Literature search and data extraction

We performed an extensive electronic search of several databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science (WOS) and Ovid from inception until March 2024. Our search strategy was as follows: (“rotational atherectomy” OR RA) AND (“coronary artery disease” OR “chronic total occlusion” OR CTO OR “CTO lesions” OR “CTO-PCI”) AND (non- chronic total occlusion OR “non- CTO lesions”) AND (“percutaneous coronary intervention” OR “PCI”). The duplicates were identified and removed using EndNote. We screened all relevant included studies and prior meta-analyses for additional citations. The screening of the results was conducted in two stages: first, a title and abstract screening was performed, followed by a full-text screening of the relevant studies. To gather the required information, we utilized a specific data extraction sheet. The sheet covered four key areas: [1] Characteristics of the included studies [2], Characteristics of the population under each study [3], Risk of bias domains, and [4] Outcome measures.

Risk of bias assessment

Three authors (H.K., A.M.A. and M.E.) independently assessed the quality of the clinical studies included in our analysis, using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) which involves three domains (selection, comparability, and outcome).

Synthesis of results

To evaluate the statistical heterogeneity among the studies we used the Chi-square test (Cochrane Q test), applying the following equation: I2 = Q-dfQx100%. We considered a P-value of less than 0.1 in the Chi-square test as indicative of significant heterogeneity. High heterogeneity was defined as I-square values of 50% or higher. In scenarios of significant heterogeneity, we employed the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis model to address the reported heterogeneity. Moreover, we used the Galbraith plot to detect any heterogeneity across studies.

If studies presented data at various time intervals, we concentrated on the results obtained at the final follow-up point. To combine the frequency of events and the total number of patients for dichotomous outcomes, we employed the DerSimonian-Laird random-effect model and presented the results as odds ratios (OR). All analyses were performed using Stata MP Version 17 for Mac. Moreover, to assess the publication bias across studies, we employed a DOI plot with LFK index to analyze the relationship between effect size and standard error. We assessed evidence of publication bias using Egger’s regression test.

Clinical outcomes

MACE

All four studies assessed MACE in which the event rate was 8.29% (20 of 241) in the RA CTO group compared to 7.89% (133 of 1684) in the RA non-CTO group. The pooled OR showed that there was no statistical difference between the two studied groups according to MACE (OR = 1.22, 95% CI [0.73 to 2.04], p = 0.45); the pooled studies were homogenous (I2= 0.00%, p = 0.92), as shown in Fig. 2.

Secondary outcomes

All four studies assessed all secondary outcomes of studied clinical outcomes. The pooled analysis showed no statistical difference between RA CTO and RA non-CTO groups according to all-cause mortality (OR = 0.97, 95% CI [0.44 to 2.12], p = 0.94), cardiac mortality (OR = 1.06, 95% CI [0.48 to 2.32], p = 0.89), incidence of MI (OR = 1.79, 95% CI [0.84 to 3.82], p = 0.13), or incidence of TVR (OR = 1.37, 95% CI [0.56 to 3.36], p = 0.49). The pooled studies were homogenous with the following values, respectively; (I2= 0.00%, p = 0.57), (I2= 0.00%, p = 0.66), (I2= 0.00%, p = 0.64), and (I2= 0.00%, p = 0.99), as shown in Supplementary Figs. 1–4.

Angiographic outcomes

All included studies assessed vessel perforation with an incidence rate of 3.73% (9 of 241) in RA CTO group versus 1.6% (27 of 1684) in RA non-CTO group. The pooled OR showed a significant increase in vessel perforation in RA CTO group (OR = 3.56, 95% CI [1.59 to 7.98], p < 0.001); the pooled studies were homogenous (I2= 0.00%, p = 0.64), as show in Fig. 5.

Discussion

This meta-analysis of observational studies assessed the feasibility and complications of RA in the CTO lesion group compared to the non-CTO lesion group. Our results showed no significant differences in clinical outcomes, including MACE, all-cause death, cardiac death, the incidence of MI, and the incidence of TVR. Also, there were no significant differences in the angiographic outcomes, including technical and procedural success, except for the incidence of vessel perforation, which was significantly associated with the CTO lesion group compared to the non-CTO lesion group.

CTO lesions can pose challenges during coronary intervention [23]. Patients with moderate to severe calcifications are likely to have worse clinical outcomes during PCI [24]; Heavy calcium deposits may result in incomplete expansion of the stenotic lesion, increasing the risk of restenosis and stent thrombosis [25]. The high pressure needed to inflate the balloon against resistant arteries may also increase the risk of damage and rupture of the coronary arteries [26].

In the past 10 years, there have been significant advances in CTO PCI with improved devices, balloons, and revascularization techniques such as the retrograde approach [27]. However, balloon-uncrossable or un-dilatable lesions remain a challenge, as they have a lower success rate and a higher incidence of MACE. Moreover, these lesions require longer exposure time to fluoroscopy and contrast [28]. To overcome this challenge, excimer laser coronary atherectomy or rotational or orbital atherectomy can be effectively used [29].

The RA technique is a suitable option for preparing coronary arteries for optimal stent replacement because it dissolves and expands calcified plaque, causing lumen enlargement [18]. It has been shown to improve procedural success rates in cases with uncrossable and un-dilatable lesions, while also reducing residual plaque and improving the minimum lumen diameter post-procedure with minimal vascular injury, thereby reducing the risk of restenosis and stent thrombosis [30, 31]. In the published literature, the RA success rate ranged from 77% in a study on 35 CTO lesions [32], 95.6% on 45 CTO lesions [7], and 96.2% on 26 CTO lesions [33]. The slow flow caused by RA may exacerbate the poor antegrade blood flow or distal drainage. Additionally, the presence of heavy calcium plaque may cause burrs that might get trapped and cause vessel perforation [34, 35].

The overall analysis in our study revealed no significant differences between the CTO and non-CTO groups in terms of MACE, success rates, and complications, except for vessel perforation. The previous evidence showed the same results in success rate and complication incidence, and a propensity-score matching analysis by Tsai 2022 et al. showed that short- and intermediate-term outcomes in RA for CTO patients were not different from RA for non-CTO patients. Despite the high lesion complexity in the CTO group, the success rate in RA for CTO patients was high and comparable to RA for non-CTO (95.5% vs. 100%, p = 0.498); also, the complications and acute slow or no flow rates were comparable [22]. Elbasha et al. (2022) reported successful treatment of CTO lesions with RA, but noted higher complication rates compared to non-CTO RA, specifically slow flow which could be worsened by using a large burr at higher speeds. To avoid slow flow, it is recommended to use a smaller burr at the lowest speed possible. Furthermore, using a rotational atherectomy cocktail (nitroglycerine, verapamil, and heparin) with short runs (20–30 s) may also minimize the rate of slow flow [21]. On the contrary, Lee 2021 et al. reported that the success rate was lower in the CTO group, which may be due to the nature of the patients included in this study, who have previous coronary revascularization and multivessel disease [20]. Brinkmann 2018 et al. reported that complications were lower in RA with the CTO group compared to RA with the non-CTO group, due to higher lesion complexity, heavily calcified lesions, and longer stented segment lengths [19].

According to previous publications, RA for CTO lesions was associated with cardiac tamponade (2.6%) and vascular injury (4%) [36]. Most complications related to RA were not directly caused by procedure itself. Coronary perforation, for instance, often resulted from high-pressure balloon inflation, stent replacement, or perforation by PCI wire, particularly in older patients with heavily calcified lesions. Therefore, it is advisable to avoid very high-pressure inflation of the balloons or stents after RA to avoid perforation [37]. Furthermore, temporary pacemaker implantation before RA was found to be the second most common cause of cardiac tamponade after coronary perforation [38].

Intravascular imaging is also an important consideration when performing CTO PCI, especially in cases where rotational atherectomy is needed. IVUS or OCT can help clarify plaque characteristics, vessel size, and the pattern of calcium, all of which guide burr selection and the overall atherectomy strategy. Imaging is often useful for identifying areas of thin vessel wall or eccentric disease where the risk of perforation may be higher, allowing operators to adjust their technique accordingly [39]. Recent evidence has suggested that imaging-guided atherectomy can lead to more appropriate sizing and more controlled lesion modification, with fewer complications. In practice, using IVUS or OCT in these complex cases may simply make the procedure safer and more predictable, particularly when dealing with heavy or irregular calcification in CTO segments.

Additionally, when considering RA in CTO interventions, it is also important to put it in the context of available calcium-modification strategies, particularly intravascular lithotripsy (IVL). Although IVL has gained popularity for treating heavily calcified lesions because of its ease of use and favorable safety profile, its performance in CTOs is still somewhat limited by the need for balloon delivery across the occlusion [40]. In contrast, RA can be used earlier in the procedure to facilitate wire passage, modify tight or nodular calcium, and create a channel for subsequent devices. IVL tends to be more effective for concentric or deep calcium once a balloon can be delivered, whereas RA remains the more practical option for severe superficial calcium or when device crossing is the main limitation. Both techniques can be complementary, and in some cases a sequential approach is required. Nonetheless, in the CTO population, RA continues to play a central role because it addresses the fundamental issue of device deliverability, while IVL is often reserved for later plaque modification once access to the lesion has been established [41].

Advances in clinical practice

This study adds to the current literature, mainly in the context of CTO lesions. A previous meta-analysis reported the outcomes of PCI in CTO lesions compared to non-CTO lesions, of which it was associated with higher rates of vessel perforation and cardiac tamponade at short-term assessment, while it was associated with higher rates of MACE at long-term assessment [42]. Moreover, comparing RA with PCI in CTO lesions demonstrated comparable efficacy and safety, with no context in the efficacy of RA in non-CTO lesions [43].

Study limitations

Nevertheless, the study has some limitations, such as only including observational studies, which makes it difficult to establish cause-effect associations between RA-CTO as a risk factor and complications. Additionally, there were a low number of included studies and the interpretation of publication bias is significantly limited. Although we employed a DOI plot with LKF index, which is more suitable for small datasets than regression-based tests, any assessment of asymmetry remains underpowered. Therefore, we recommended more prospective studies with a large number of patients to establish confident scientific evidence. It is also important to compare conventional PCI against RA in older patients with complex lesions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current study reported a safety and feasibility of RA in CTO patients, mainly patients with uncrossable or un-dilatable lesions. CTO cannot be considered a direct risk factor for patients undergoing RA. Complications may occur due to the nature of CTO patients, who are mostly older and have many comorbidities. Additionally, the operator can avoid vessel perforation by avoiding high pressure during balloon inflation or stent replacement. Moreover, the outcomes of this meta-analysis provide evidence that RA is effective and safe in CTO patients compared to non-CTO patients. There was no significant difference in the use of the RA procedure in CTO patients versus non-CTO patients. Perforation was significantly more prevalent in CTO patients; however, it can be avoided with certain operator methods.
