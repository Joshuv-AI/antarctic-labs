Distributional Uncertainty and Adaptive Decision-Making in System Co-design
Yujun Huang, Gioele Zardini

Abstract
Complex engineered systems require coordinated design choices across heterogeneous components under multiple conflicting objectives and uncertain specifications. Monotone co-design provides a compositional framework for such problems by modeling each subsystem as a design problem: a feasible relation between provided functionalities and required resources in partially ordered sets. Existing uncertain co-design models rely on interval bounds, which support worst-case reasoning but cannot represent probabilistic risk or multi-stage adaptive decisions. We develop a distributional extension of co-design that models uncertain design outcomes as distributions over design problems and supports adaptive decision processes through Markov-kernel re-parameterizations. Using quasi-measurable and quasi-universal spaces, we show that the standard co-design interconnection operations remain compositional under this richer notion of uncertainty. We further introduce queries and observations that extract probabilistic design trade-offs, including feasibility probabilities, confidence bounds, and distributions of minimal required resources. A task-driven unmanned aerial vehicle case study illustrates how the framework captures risk-sensitive and information-dependent design choices that interval-based models cannot express.



I
Introduction

Embodied intelligence and
cyber-physical systems (CPS) tightly couple heterogeneous hardware (sensors, actuators, processors) with software (perception, planning, control), and must be evaluated against multiple, often incomparable, metrics.
As a result, designers face nontrivial
trade-offs
across components, subsystems, and system-level objectives, while coordinating stakeholders with diverse expertise.
For safety- and mission-critical applications, uncertainty cannot be treated purely adversarially: designers must quantify
risk
(e.g., probability of meeting specifications) and reason about how robustness trades against performance.
Moreover, design is rarely one-shot: decisions are made in stages as information becomes available, so later choices should be
adaptive
to earlier commitments and intermediate observations.

Monotone co-design provides a compositional framework for such multiobjective system design
[
7
,
28
,
6
]
.
Each component is modeled as a
design problem (DP): a feasible relation between provided functionalities and required resources, ordered in
partially ordered sets.
Systems are constructed by interconnecting
DPsin series, parallel, and feedback, supported by traced and symmetric monoidal categorical structure
[
28
]
.
This common language has enabled joint optimization of heterogeneous hardware/software architectures across robotics and control
[
25
,
27
,
20
]
, transportation
[
26
]
, and automotive
[
21
]
.

Existing co-design tools, however, treat uncertainty primarily via interval bounds on feasible sets
[
8
,
28
]
.
Intervals yield worst-case robustness but are too coarse to encode probabilistic risk (chance constraints, confidence levels, quantiles) and do not natively express multi-stage adaptive decision policies.
In particular, they cannot distinguish “almost-always safe” designs from uniformly conservative ones, nor represent strategies that react to intermediate observations (e.g., test outcomes, demand realizations, partial measurements).

This paper extends monotone co-design to a
distributional
and
adaptive
uncertainty framework.
We model uncertain specifications and outcomes via probability measures (and parametric families) over spaces of
DPs, built on quasi-measurable structures that avoid classical measurability pathologies while preserving co-design compositionality.
This enables: (i) distributional uncertainty over specifications and implementations, (ii) multi-stage adaptive design policies that condition later decisions on earlier realized outcomes, and (iii) queries that return probabilistic trade-offs and risk metrics (e.g., expected resource use, quantiles), rather than only deterministic feasible sets.
This is the first framework that simultaneously handles general distributional uncertainty over compositional design models, supports multi-stage adaptivity, and remains closed under the standard co-design compositions.

I-A

Related work

This work lies at the intersection of system-level design for
CPS, compositional specification formalisms, and robust and stochastic optimization.
A recurring gap in the literature is that methods with rich probabilistic and adaptive uncertainty models typically lack a modular, multiobjective, compositional language for heterogeneous subsystems, while compositional design frameworks often rely on coarse uncertainty descriptions that do not support quantitative risk or staged policies.

I-B

Statement of Contribution

The main contributions of this paper are fourfold.
First, we introduce probability measures and parametric uncertainty over
DPsusing quasi-measurable spaces, yielding a distributional modeling layer that is closed under the algebra of co-design compositions.
Second, we formalize multi-stage design processes in which implementations and specifications can depend on intermediate observations and earlier choices, generalizing interval uncertainty toward an adaptive stochastic-design setting while retaining compositionality.
Third, we define query operators that map distributional co-design models to probabilistic performance characterizations (e.g., feasibility probabilities, distributions of minimal required resources), enabling risk-aware system design.
Finally, we illustrate the framework on
unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)co-design with uncertain task profiles and component specifications, showing how distributional and adaptive modeling reveals trade-offs and robustness properties that interval methods cannot capture.

III-A

Monotone co-design theory

Co-design provides a compositional framework for modeling and analyzing complex engineering systems with heterogeneous components and tightly coupled design choices.
The basic objects are
functionalities
and
resources
, each modeled as a
poset.
The order expresses when one functionality (or resource) is better or worse than another.
As a running example, consider the design of
UAVs, where perception is an important sub-system.
Fixing all other subsystems, perception can be viewed as providing a certain level of
detection accuracy
at the cost of
computation power
, under given
weather conditions
.
Detection accuracy and computation power can be naturally modeled as positive real numbers, while weather conditions form a more complex poset with non-comparable elements.
For instance, a clear night and a foggy day pose qualitatively different challenges; designs optimized for one case need not perform well in the other.
Monotone co-design formalizes such trade-offs as
DPsrelating
functionalities
to
resources
.

IV
Distributional uncertainty and adaptive decisions in co-design

Real design processes are more complex than one-shot
implementation
choices under best– and worst–case uncertainty.
Different
implementations
come with parameters implying various system
specifications
, and these parameters are uncertain in ways that cannot be captured by intervals alone.
Moreover, some implementation decisions can be postponed until after part of this uncertainty has been resolved.

Consider again the perception sub-system of a
UAV with sensor and algorithm components.
The designer chooses a sensor from a catalogue (e.g., different camera variants), each posing
specifications
such as field of view, noise characteristics, and failure modes of the system.
Manufacturers typically guarantee only
distributions
over parameters; when a sensor is purchased, one obtains a random sample from these distributions.
Similarly, perception algorithms with tunable hyperparameters are characterized by distributions over performance metrics (e.g., detection accuracy), and their performance may depend on the chosen sensor.
Crucially, the algorithm and its parameters can often be selected
after
the actual sensor instance and part of the environment have been observed.
Thus, the resulting design process is inherently
adaptive
and involves full probability distributions, not only interval bounds.

One could try to reduce distributions to interval bounds by taking confidence intervals, but this loses the quantitative trade-offs between
functionality
,
resources
, and risk that practitioners care about.
To support such trade-offs in a compositional way, we move from intervals to
distributions over
DPs
, and from static
MDPIs to
MDPIs with
distributional uncertainty and adaptive decisions
.

In this section we: (a) Formalize
distributions over system performance
as distributions over
DPs; (b) introduce
MDPIs with distributional uncertainty
and an adaptive framework based on Markov kernels and re-parameterization; (c) describe
queries
and
observations
for these models, including probabilistic versions of classical co-design queries.

IV-A

Distributions over design problems as uncertain design results

In deterministic co-design, the performance of components and systems, i.e., the design results for fixed
implementations
, is described by
DPs, encoding trade-offs between
functionalities
and
resources
(
Definition

15
).
To model distributional uncertain design, we wish to talk about

DPs, i.e.,

•

Compositional

•

Semantically expressive
: for a random design problem, we can ask for the probability that a given

pair (or countable family of pairs) is feasible;

•

Compatible with co-design
: deterministic

A naive approach is to assign a sigma-algebra

to

and consider ordinary probability measures on it, requiring at least that (i) the composition operations in

are measurable, (ii) for each

\text{ev}_{x_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}},x_{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}}}\colon\mathsf{DP}\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}},{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}\}\to\mathrm{Bool},\ {\mathrm{dp}}\mapsto\big(\langle x_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}},x_{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}}\rangle\in{\mathrm{dp}}\big)

is measurable, and (iii) the

{\text{const}}:{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}\times{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}}\to{\mathsf{DP}\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}},{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}\}},\ {x_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}}\times x_{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}}}\mapsto{\downarrow\!\left\{x_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}}\right\}\times\uparrow\!\left\{x_{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}}\right\}}

Note that (iii) is implied by compatibility with co-design by considering

Counterexample 1

(Impossibility result)
.

Consider

Each measurable map

Write

IV-A
1
Quasi Measurable Spaces and Quasi Universal Spaces

In probability theory, we first fix a sample space
\Omega
with a sigma algebra
\Sigma_{\Omega}
of random events, then talk about
random variables
from this sample space to other
measure spaces
, such as real numbers with the common Borel sigma algebra.

Quasi Measurable Spaceprovide another perspective of probability theory to resolve this issue, where random variables are viewed as first-class citizens and sigma algebras are derived from them.
Let’s fix a sample space with random events
\langle\Omega,\Sigma_{\Omega}\rangle
, and denote
{\Omega}^{\Omega}
the set of measurable maps from
\Omega
to
\Omega
.

Definition 21

(Quasi-measurable spaces, quasi-measurable maps, and induced sigma algebras)
.

A
Quasi Measurable Space (QMS)
is a pair
\langle A,{A}^{\Omega}\rangle
where
A
is a set and
{A}^{\Omega}
is a distinguished set of maps
\Omega\to A
, called
random variables
on
A
such that:

1.

For every
m_{A}\in A
, the constant map
\text{const}_{m_{A}}\colon\omega\mapsto m_{A}
belongs to
{A}^{\Omega}
.

2.

For every
\alpha\in{A}^{\Omega}
and every measurable map
\varphi\in{\Omega}^{\Omega}
, the composite
\alpha\mathbin{\circ}\varphi
belongs to
{A}^{\Omega}
.

If the set of random variables is clear, we simply write “
QMS
A
”.
The sample space
\Omega
with all measurable maps also forms a
QMS
\langle\Omega,{\Omega}^{\Omega}\rangle
.
A map
f\colon A\to B
between two
QMSs
\langle A,{A}^{\Omega}\rangle
and
\langle B,{B}^{\Omega}\rangle
is
quasi-measurable
if, for every
\alpha\in{A}^{\Omega}
, the composite
f\mathbin{\circ}\alpha
lies in
{B}^{\Omega}
.
The set of all quasi-measurable maps from
A
to
B
is denoted
{B}^{A}
.

Any
QMS
\langle A,{A}^{\Omega}\rangle
induces a sigma algebra:

\mathcal{B}(A)\mathrel{\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}$}}\{X_{A}\subseteq A\mid\forall\alpha\in{A}^{\Omega},\alpha^{-1}(X_{A})\in\Sigma_{\Omega}\}.

We call
\mathcal{B}(A)
the
induced sigma algebra
on
A
.

Connecting back to common probability theory, when
A
comes with a natural sigma algebra, for instance the Borel sigma algebra of a Polish space, taking all the measurable maps as random variables is a good choice.
Further assuming
\Omega
being Polish, the induced sigma algebra will be the same as the completion of Borel sigma algebra.
One can be tempted to make a
QMS
A
very general by taking all maps from
\Omega
to
A
as random variables, but this makes very few maps from
A
to be quasi-measurable, just as taking the power set as sigma algebra makes very few maps into
A
measurable.

Definition 22

(Product of
QMSs)
.

Given
QMSs
\langle A,{A}^{\Omega}\rangle
and
\langle B,{B}^{\Omega}\rangle
, their
product
is the
QMS
\langle A\times B,{\left(A\times B\right)}^{\Omega}\rangle
, where:

{\left(A\times B\right)}^{\Omega}\mathrel{\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}$}}\{\langle\alpha_{a},\alpha_{b}\rangle\mid\alpha_{a}\in{A}^{\Omega},\alpha_{b}\in{B}^{\Omega}\}.

Thus a random variable on
A\times B
is simply a pair of random variables, one on
A
and one on
B
.

A significant result is that function spaces are now equipped with a natural set of random variables, and the evaluation map is quasi-measurable.

Definition 23

(
QMS of quasi-measurable maps and evaluation)
.

To define random variables for
{B}^{A}
,
we pick those maps
\beta\colon\Omega\to{B}^{A}
that make the map

{\beta\big(\varphi(-)\big)\big(\alpha(-)\big)}:{\Omega}\to{B},\ {\omega}\mapsto{\beta\big(\varphi(\omega)\big)\big(\alpha(\omega)\big)}

a random variable in
{B}^{\Omega}
for each
\alpha\in{A}^{\Omega}
and
\varphi\in{\Omega}^{\Omega}
.
The
evaluation
map

{\text{ev}}:{A\times{B}^{A}}\to{B},\ {\langle m_{A},f\rangle}\mapsto{f(m_{A})}

is then quasi-measurable.

Remark 5
.

Together with the terminal
QMS (a singleton), products, and the

of

[
9
]
.
Consequences are extremely rich.
The following are used in this paper:

1.

For each quasi-measurable map

{\bar{f}}:{A}\to{{C}^{B}},\ {m_{A}}\mapsto{f(m_{A},-),}

is quasi-measurable.

2.

For each quasi-measurable map
f\colon A\to B
, its pre-composing and post composing maps

\displaystyle{(-)\mathbin{\circ}f}:{{C}^{B}}\to{{C}^{A}},\ {g}\mapsto{g\mathbin{\circ}f}

\displaystyle{f\mathbin{\circ}(-)}:{{A}^{C}}\to{{B}^{C}},\ {h}\mapsto{f\mathbin{\circ}h}

are quasi-measurable.

To connect
QMSs with standard probability theory and Markov kernels in a compositional way, we restrict to a class of “good” sample spaces.

Definition 24

(Quasi-universal spaces)
.

A
QMS
\langle A,{A}^{\Omega}\rangle
is a
Quasi Universal Space (QUS)
if the sample space
\Omega
is a Polish space.
Typical choices are
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the usual topology.

Remark 6
.

This restriction is not computationally limiting: randomness that is computable or from countably many random experiments originates from such sample spaces.
All constructions for
QMSs restrict straightforwardly to
QUSs.

We adopt push-forward of distributions over
QUS
[
9
]
.

Definition 25

(Distributions over
QUSs)
.

Let
\langle A,{A}^{\Omega}\rangle
be a
QUS.
A
distribution over

A
is an equivalence class
\left\{\langle\mu,\alpha\rangle\right\}/\simeq
where
\mu
is a probability distribution on
\Omega
, and
\alpha
is a random variable in
{A}^{\Omega}
.
Two pairs
\langle\mu,\alpha\rangle
and
\langle\mu^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}\rangle
are equivalent if they induce the same probability measure on the induced sigma algebra
\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
, i.e.,

\mu(\alpha^{-1}(X_{A}))=\mu^{\prime}(\alpha^{\prime{-1}}(X_{A}))\text{ for all }X_{A}\in\mathcal{B}(A).

The set of equivalence classes is denoted
\mathcal{P}(A)
.
When no confusion can arise, we write
\langle\mu,\alpha\rangle
for the corresponding class.
To make
\mathcal{P}(A)
itself a
QUS, we take as random variables precisely those maps of the form

\displaystyle\alpha{}_{*}\kappa\colon\Omega

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(A),\,\omega

\displaystyle{\mapsto}

\displaystyle\langle\kappa(\omega),\alpha\rangle,

where
\alpha\in{A}^{\Omega}
and
\kappa\in{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}^{\Omega}
is a Markov kernel (in the classical sense) assigning to each
\omega
a distribution
\kappa(\omega)
on
\Omega
.
One can check that
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
(with the induced sigma algebra
\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
) is the usual space of probability measures on
\Omega
.

One can define
Markov kernels
and their compositions as in classic measure theory.

Definition 26

(Markov kernels for
QUSs and their compositions)
.

Given
QUSs
A
to
B
, a
Markov kernel

A\Rightarrow B
is simply a quasi-measurable map
a\colon A\to\mathcal{P}(B)
.
Given
a\colon A\Rightarrow B
and
b\colon B\Rightarrow C
, their
Kleisli composition

a\mathbin{\mathbin{\circ}_{\mathcal{P}}}b\colon A\Rightarrow C
is the Markov kernel defined as follows.
For
m_{A}\in A
, write
a(m_{A})=\langle\mu,\alpha\rangle
.
Since
b
is quasi-measurable and
\alpha\in{B}^{\Omega}
, the composite
\alpha\mathbin{\circ}b
is a random variable in
{\mathcal{P}(C)}^{\Omega}
.
Thus there exist
\kappa\in{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}^{\Omega}
and
\beta\in{C}^{\Omega}
such that
\alpha\mathbin{\circ}b=\beta{}_{*}\kappa
.
We then set
\left(a\mathbin{\mathbin{\circ}_{\mathcal{P}}}b\right)(m_{A})\mathrel{\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}$}}\langle\mu\mathbin{\circ}\kappa,\beta\rangle\in\mathcal{P}(C).

Remark 7
.

Equipped with suitable structural quasi-measurable maps (e.g., duplication and deletion),
\mathcal{P}
extends to a strong monad on the category of
\mathsf{QMS}
.
Restricted to
QUSs,
\mathcal{P}
becomes commutative, so that the associated Kleisli category is symmetric monoidal, and we can describe arbitrary compositional diagrams of Markov kernels.
This Kleisli category is not, in general, a full Markov category: conditional kernels and disintegrations are not part of our basic structure.
For the purposes of this paper, the monoidal structure suffices to model compositional, adaptive design processes.
Learning and conditioning are object of future work.

IV-A
2
Sample space and assumptions on posets

Following
Section

IV-A
, we fix a sample space
\Omega
(a
QUS) that aggregates all sources of randomness.
For instance, in perception, these could include manufacturing variability, environmental disturbances, algorithmic randomness, and randomness in
functionality
and
resource
requirements.
Random variables into any space
A
are maps
\Omega\to A
in the sense of
QMSs/
QUSs.

With this sample space fixed, we ask
what are the proper random variables mapping into
\mathsf{DP}\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}},{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}\}
,
DPs representing component and system performance, that are compositional, computable, and rich enough for real applications?

We assume mild regularity on the
posets used as functionality and resource spaces.

Assumption 1
.

All the
posets under consideration are
Souslin Spaces
, and the order relation
\{\langle x_{P},x_{P}^{\prime}\rangle\mid x_{P}\preceq_{P}x_{P}^{\prime}\}\subseteq P\times P

is a Borel set.

Remark 8
.

Finite
posets with the discrete topology,
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the usual topology and either the product or the discrete order, and complete separable metric spaces whose less-or-equal relation is preserved under limits (equivalently, whose order relation is closed) all satisfy
1
.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose
posets
P
and
Q
satisfy
1
.
Then the opposite
poset
P^{\mathrm{op}}
and the product
poset
P\times Q
also satisfy
1
.

Proof.

Since
\{\langle x_{P},x_{P}^{\prime}\rangle\mid x_{P}\succeq x_{P}^{\prime}\}=\text{perm}_{1\leftrightarrow 2}(\{\langle x_{P}^{\prime},x_{P}\rangle\mid x_{P}^{\prime}\preceq x_{P}\})
and permutations are homomorphisms, the opposite order set
\{\langle x_{P},x_{P}^{\prime}\rangle\mid x_{P}\succeq x_{P}^{\prime}\}
is a Borel set.
This proves the opposite
poset satisfies
1
.
Then we prove the product
poset also satisfies
1
.
The product of two Souslin Spaces is a Souslin Space,
so we only need to prove that the set
\{\langle x_{P},x_{Q},x_{P}^{\prime},x_{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\mid x_{P}\preceq x_{P}^{\prime},x_{Q}\preceq x_{Q}^{\prime}\}
is a Borel set.
Observe that

\displaystyle\{\langle x_{P},x_{Q},x_{P}^{\prime},x_{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\mid x_{P}\preceq x_{P}^{\prime},x_{Q}\preceq x_{Q}^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{perm}_{2\leftrightarrow 3}(\{\langle x_{P},x_{P}^{\prime},x_{Q},x_{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\mid x_{P}\preceq x_{P}^{\prime},x_{Q}\preceq x_{Q}^{\prime}\})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\text{perm}_{2\leftrightarrow 3}(\left\{x_{P}\preceq x_{P}^{\prime}\right\}\times\left\{x_{Q}\preceq x_{Q}^{\prime}\right\}).\end{multlined}

Thus, the statement holds since the product of two Borel sets is Borel, and the permutation map is a homomorphism.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
poset be a
P
satisfying
1
.
If
A\subseteq P
is analytic, then its upper and lower closures
\uparrow\!A
and
\downarrow\!A
are also analytic.

Proof.

Let
O=\{\langle x_{P},x_{P}^{\prime}\rangle\mid x_{P}\preceq x_{P}^{\prime}\}\subseteq P\times P
and let
\text{proj}_{1}\colon\langle x_{P},x_{P}^{\prime}\rangle\mapsto x_{P}
and
\text{proj}_{2}\colon\langle x_{P},x_{P}^{\prime}\rangle\mapsto x_{P}^{\prime}
denote two projections.
Then
\uparrow\!A=\text{proj}_{2}(\text{proj}_{1}^{-1}(A)\cap O)
, which analytic because analytic sets are closed under inverse images of continuous maps, finite intersections, and projections.
The claim for
\downarrow\!A
follows by applying the same argument to the opposite order on
P
.
∎

Lemma 7
.

\langle\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}},{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}\},{\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}},{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}\}}^{\Omega}\rangle

Proof.

Since
\Omega
is Polish by definition of

\Omega
.
For (i), let
{\mathrm{dp}}=\uparrow\!A
with
A
analytic in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}^{\mathrm{op}}\times{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}
.
By
Lemma

6
,
{\mathrm{dp}}
is analytic, hence
F_{\alpha}=\{\langle\omega,x_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}},x_{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}}\rangle\mid\langle x_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}},x_{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}}\rangle\in{\mathrm{dp}}\}=\Omega\times{\mathrm{dp}}

is analytic.
Thus the constant map
\alpha(\omega)={\mathrm{dp}}
lies in
{\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}},{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}\}}^{\Omega}
.
For (ii), let
\alpha\in{\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}},{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}\}}^{\Omega}

\langle\omega,x_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}},x_{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}}\rangle\mapsto\langle\varphi(\omega),x_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}},x_{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}}\rangle.

\displaystyle F_{\varphi\mathbin{\fatsemi}\alpha}

\displaystyle=\{\langle\omega,x_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}},x_{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}}\rangle\mid\langle x_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}},x_{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}}\rangle\in\varphi\mathbin{\fatsemi}\alpha(\omega)\}

\displaystyle=\{\langle\omega,x_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}},x_{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}}\rangle\mid\langle\varphi(\omega),x_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}},x_{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}}\rangle\in F_{\alpha}\}=\tilde{\varphi}^{-1}(F_{\alpha}),

∎

contains all constant

In practice, designers usually have

.

IV-A
4
Compositionality of the QUS of design problems

We now show that all co-design operations on
DPs are quasi-measurable on
\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}},{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}\}
, so that they lift to distributions via the distribution monad
\mathcal{P}
on
\mathsf{QUS}
.

Theorem 4
.

All the composition operations in
Definition

16
(series, parallel, feedback, union, intersection) are quasi-measurable as maps between the corresponding
QUSs of
DPs.
Consequently,
these operations lift to distributions over
DPs in
\mathsf{QUS}
.
Furthermore, the lifted union
\mathcal{P}(\vee)
represents the design result when the designer can choose which design outcome to implement after observing the random outcome, while the lifted intersection
\mathcal{P}(\wedge)
applies when the designer has to be robust against the random outcomes being composed with it.

Proof.

We must show that if we start from random variables whose
outcome feasible set are analytic, then post-composing with any of the operations in
Definition

16
yields a new random variable with analytic
outcome feasible set.

Series
: Let
\alpha_{a}\in{\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{P,Q\}}^{\Omega}
and
\alpha_{b}\in{\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{Q,R\}}^{\Omega}
with feasible sets
F_{\alpha_{a}}\subseteq\Omega\times P\times Q
and
F_{\alpha_{b}}\subseteq\Omega\times Q\times R
.
Post-composing with
\fatsemi
gives

\displaystyle\fatsemi\mathbin{\circ}\langle\alpha_{a},\alpha_{b}\rangle\colon\Omega

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{P,R\},\,\omega

\displaystyle{\mapsto}

\displaystyle\alpha_{a}(\omega)\mathbin{\fatsemi}\alpha_{b}(\omega)

with the
outcome feasible set (with
\star=\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{R}\rangle
)

\displaystyle\{\star\mid\langle x_{P},x_{R}\rangle\in\alpha_{a}(\omega)\mathbin{\fatsemi}\alpha_{b}(\omega)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\star\mid\exists x_{Q}\in Q,\langle x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\in\alpha_{a}(\omega),\langle x_{Q},x_{R}\rangle\in\alpha_{b}(\omega)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\star\mid\exists x_{Q}\in Q,\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\in F_{\alpha_{a}},\langle\omega,x_{Q},x_{R}\rangle\in F_{\alpha_{b}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\{\star\mid\exists x_{Q}\in Q,\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{Q},x_{R}\rangle\\
\in\text{proj}_{1,2,3}^{-1}(F_{\alpha_{a}}),\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{Q},x_{R}\rangle\in\text{proj}_{1,3,4}^{-1}(F_{\alpha_{b}})\}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{proj}_{1,2,4}\big(\text{proj}_{1,2,3}^{-1}(F_{\alpha_{a}})\cap\text{proj}_{1,3,4}^{-1}(F_{\alpha_{b}})\big),

being analytic.

Parallel
:
For two random variables
\alpha_{a}\in{\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{P,Q\}}^{\Omega}
and
\alpha_{a}^{\prime}\in{\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{P^{\prime},Q^{\prime}\}}^{\Omega}
, we similarly have the
outcome feasible set of
\otimes\mathbin{\circ}\langle\alpha_{a},\alpha_{a}^{\prime}\rangle
being analytic, with
\star=\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{P}^{\prime},x_{Q},x_{Q}^{\prime}\rangle
:

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\{\star\mid\langle\langle x_{P},x_{P}^{\prime}\rangle,\langle x_{Q},x_{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\rangle\in\alpha_{a}(\omega)\mathbin{\otimes}\alpha_{a}^{\prime}(\omega)\}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\{\star\mid\langle x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\in\alpha_{a}(\omega),\langle x_{P}^{\prime},x_{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\in\alpha_{a}^{\prime}(\omega)\}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\{\star\mid\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\in F_{\alpha_{a}},\langle\omega,x_{P}^{\prime},x_{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\in F_{\alpha_{a}^{\prime})}\}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\{\star\mid\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{P}^{\prime},x_{Q},x_{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\in\text{proj}_{1,2,4}^{-1}(F_{\alpha_{a}}),\\
\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{P}^{\prime},x_{Q},x_{Q}^{\prime}\rangle\in\text{proj}_{1,3,5}^{-1}(F_{\alpha_{a}^{\prime}})\}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{proj}_{1,2,4}^{-1}(F_{\alpha_{a}})\cap\text{proj}_{1,3,5}^{-1}(F_{\alpha_{a}^{\prime}}).

Feedback/Trace
:
Consider a random variable with proper
functionality
and
resource
:
\alpha\in{\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{P\times R,Q\times R\}}^{\Omega}
, taking the trace operator gives
outcome feasible set

\displaystyle\{\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\mid\langle x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\in\text{Tr}(\alpha(\omega))\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\mid\exists x_{R},\langle\langle x_{P},x_{R}\rangle,\langle x_{Q},x_{R}\rangle\rangle\in\alpha(\omega)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\mid\exists x_{R},\langle\omega,\langle x_{P},x_{R}\rangle,\langle x_{Q},x_{R}\rangle\rangle\in F_{\alpha}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\{\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\mid\exists x_{R},x_{R}^{\prime},\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{R},x_{Q},x_{R}^{\prime}\rangle\\
\in F_{\alpha}\cap\{\langle\tilde{\omega},\tilde{x}_{P},\tilde{x}_{R},\tilde{x}_{Q},\tilde{x}_{R}^{\prime}\rangle\mid\tilde{x}_{R}=\tilde{x}_{R}^{\prime}\}\}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\{\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\mid\exists x_{R},x_{R}^{\prime},\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{R},x_{Q},x_{R}^{\prime}\rangle\\
\in F_{\alpha}\cap\text{proj}_{3,5}^{-1}(\left\{\tilde{x}_{R},\tilde{x}_{R}\right\})\}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{proj}_{1,2,4}(F_{\alpha}\cap\text{proj}_{3,5}^{-1}(\left\{\tilde{x}_{R},\tilde{x}_{R}\right\})).

It is an analytic set, since the diagonal set
\left\{\tilde{x}_{R},\tilde{x}_{R}\right\}\subseteq R\times R
is a Borel set (thus analytic).

Union and Intersection
:
For two random variables
\alpha_{a},\alpha_{b}\in{\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{P,Q\}}^{\Omega}
, the resulting
outcome feasible sets for union and intersection are (with
\star=\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle
)

\displaystyle\{\star\mid\langle x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\in\alpha_{a}(\omega)\text{ or/and }\langle x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\in\alpha_{b}(\omega)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\star\mid\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\in F_{\alpha_{a}}\text{ or/and }\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\in F_{\alpha_{b}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\star\mid\langle\omega,x_{P},x_{Q}\rangle\in F_{\alpha_{a}}\cup/\cap F_{\alpha_{b}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{\alpha_{a}}\cup/\cap F_{\alpha_{b}},

both being analytic.
Their semantics of choosing after observing the outcome, and being robust against two different outcomes, are self-explanatory in the expressions.
∎

Remark 9
.

With
posets satisfying
1
as objects and

yields a symmetric monoidal category enriched in

.
Using the additional structure from

, but we do not need this level of abstraction in the present paper.

Remark 10
.

Theorem

4
is not constructive in terms of how to compose two distributions, only guaranteeing that such lifting can be performed.
Taking the series composition
\fatsemi
as an example, to compose two distributions
\langle\mu_{a},\alpha_{a}\rangle\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{P,Q\})
and
\langle\mu_{b},\alpha_{b}\rangle\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{Q,R\})
, we need to compute:

\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{P,Q\})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{Q,R\})\\
\xrightarrow{\mathbin{\otimes_{\mathcal{P}}}}\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{P,Q\}\times\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{Q,R\})\xrightarrow{\mathcal{P}(\fatsemi)}\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{P,R\}),

with element-wise evaluations:

\langle\mu_{a},\alpha_{a}\rangle\times\langle\mu_{b},\alpha_{b}\rangle\xmapsto{\mathbin{\otimes_{\mathcal{P}}}}\langle\mu,\alpha\rangle\xmapsto{\mathcal{P}(\fatsemi)}\langle\mu,\alpha\mathbin{\circ}\fatsemi\rangle,

with

inducing a probability measure on the product space
\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{P,Q\}\times\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{Q,R\}
, which is the product measure of those induced by
\langle\mu_{a},\alpha_{a}\rangle
and
\langle\mu_{b},\alpha_{b}\rangle
.
Such
\langle\mu,\alpha\rangle
exists guaranteed by
[

, and resembles the product distribution in usual probability theory.

However, if one has a decomposition of the sample space

, and the random variables can be represented by

\alpha_{b}(\langle\omega_{a},\omega_{b}\rangle)=\alpha_{b,b}(\omega_{b})
for some random variables

and

can be computed by
\langle\mu_{a,a}\times\mu_{b,b},\alpha_{a,a}\times\alpha_{b,b}\rangle
where
\mu_{a,a}
and
\mu_{b,b}
are the corresponding marginal distributions.

Recall that
\Omega
is usually the product of all sources of randomness for all the components in the system, so such a decomposition commonly exists (e.g.
Section

).

IV-A
5
Feasibility evaluation and constant injection

With all the
implementations
fixed, e.g. fixed choice of sensors, algorithms, and tunable parameters, the designer will get the design result as a distribution over the
QUS of
DPs,
\langle\mu,\alpha\rangle\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}},{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}\})
.
They will wonder:
what practical questions can I ask with this design result
?
It turns out that the feasibility evaluation map is quasi-measurable.

Theorem 5
.

The evaluation map

\displaystyle\text{ev}\colon\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}},{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}\}\times{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}}^{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Bool}

\displaystyle\langle{\mathrm{dp}},\left\{x_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}},i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\left\{x_{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}},i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\rangle

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\left(\forall i\in\mathbb{N},\langle x_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}},i},x_{{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}},i}\rangle\in{\mathrm{dp}}\right)

is quasi-measurable.

Proof.

Consider a random variable
\alpha
for

The constant injection map

IV-A
6
Confidence bounds on distributions over design problems

Distributions over
DPs can be summarized by
confidence bounds
, which generalize interval uncertainty.
Because
DPs are rarely totally ordered, we distinguish between confidence levels for sampling
inside
a bound and confidence levels for sampling
outside
a bound.

Definition 28

(Inner confidence bounds)
.

Let
\langle\mu,\alpha\rangle\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}},{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}\})
be a distribution on
DPs and
p\in[0,1]
.
An interval
[{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L}},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U}}]
of
DPs is an
inner confidence bound
of level
p
if there exists universally measurable set
X\subseteq\Omega
satisfying:
X\subseteq\alpha^{-1}([{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L}},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U}}])
and
\mu(X)\geq p
.
If both equalities hold, we call the bound
strict
.

Definition 29

(Outer confidence bounds)
.

Let
\langle\mu,\alpha\rangle\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{DP}_{\text{A}}\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}},{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}\})
and
p,q\in[0,1]
.
An interval
[{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L}},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U}}]
of
DPs is an
outer confidence bound
of level
p,q
if there exist two universally measurable subsets of
\Omega
,
X
and
Y
, satisfying:

X\supseteq\alpha^{-1}([\varnothing,{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L}}])
and
\mu(X)\leq p
;
Y\supseteq\alpha^{-1}([{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}^{\mathrm{op}}\times{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}])
and
\mu(Y)\leq p
.
If the four equalities hold, the bound is called
strict
.

Informally, an inner bound guarantees the design result to stay between the bounds with
at least
probability
p
.
An outer bound instead guarantees the probabilities of having design results worse or better than the boundaries will be
at most

p
and
q
, respectively.
The bounds are strict when the probabilities are exact.

Remark 11
.

For a given distribution
\langle\mu,\alpha\rangle
, inner and outer confidence bounds need not exist, and if they exist they are in general not unique, even when restricted to strict bounds.
Under additional structure, for instance, when
\Omega
carries an order and
\alpha
is monotone, confidence bounds can often be constructed from suitable subsets of
\Omega
(e.g., intervals).

Remark 12
.

Although intervals of
DPs can always be composed by composing their bounds (
Lemma

3
), the resulting interval need not be a valid confidence bound due to the non-constructive nature of lifted compositional operations in
Theorem

4
.
However, whenever composition does yield a valid bound, its confidence levels can only deteriorate, by monotonicity of the co-design operations.
For instance, when the decomposition of sample spaces in
Remark

10
holds, inner confidence bounds always compose to new bounds.
Outer confidence bounds are left for future research.

Lemma 9
.

Suppose we have a diagram of
DPs such as
Figure

4
, with
f
being the deterministic map taking the component
DPs and returning the composed
DP.
Denote the distributions over
DPs for each component as
\langle\mu_{i},\alpha_{i}\rangle
, indexed by a finite set
I
.
Suppose we have a decomposition of the sample space, namely:
\Omega=\times_{i\in I}\Omega_{i}
, and
\alpha_{i}(\langle\omega_{i}\rangle_{i\in I})=\alpha_{i,i}(\omega_{i})
.
Given an inner confident bound
[{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}]
for each distribution, together with
X_{i}\subseteq\Omega
and
p_{i}
satisfying conditions in
Definition

28
, the interval composition

\mathcal{I}(f)(\langle[{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}]\rangle_{i\in I})=[f(\langle{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i}\rangle),f(\langle{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}\rangle)]

is an inner confidence bound of level
\times_{i}p_{i}
for the composed distribution.

Proof.

The composed distribution reads:
\langle\times_{i}\mu_{i,i},f\mathbin{\circ}\times_{i}\alpha_{i,i}\rangle
.
Let
X_{i,i}\mathrel{\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}$}}\text{proj}_{i}(X_{i})
.
We will prove the set
\times_{i}X_{i,i}\subseteq\Omega
satisfies conditions in
Definition

28
.
First observe:

\alpha_{i}^{-1}([{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}])=\text{proj}_{i}^{-1}(\alpha_{i,i}^{-1}([{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}]))

and since
X_{i}\subseteq\alpha_{i}^{-1}([{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}])
,

X_{i,i}=\text{proj}_{i}(X_{i})\subseteq\text{proj}_{i}(\alpha_{i}^{-1}([{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}]))\\
=\text{proj}_{i}(\text{proj}_{i}^{-1}(\alpha_{i,i}^{-1}([{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}])))\\
=\alpha_{i,i}^{-1}([{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}]).

Then we have:

X_{i}\subseteq\text{proj}_{i}^{-1}(X_{i,i})\subseteq\text{proj}_{i}^{-1}(\alpha_{i,i}^{-1}([{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}]))\\
=\alpha_{i}^{-1}([{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}])

and consequently

\mu_{i,i}(X_{i,i})=\mu_{i}(\text{proj}_{i}^{-1}(X_{i,i}))\geq\mu_{i}(X_{i})\geq p_{i}.

Since all the compositional operations are monotone, we have

f(\times_{i}[{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}])\subseteq[f(\langle{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i}\rangle),f(\langle{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}\rangle)],

thus

\times_{i}[{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}]\subseteq f^{-1}(f(\times_{i}[{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}]))\\
\subseteq f^{-1}([f(\langle{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i}\rangle),f(\langle{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}\rangle)]),

and then

\times_{i}X_{i,i}\subseteq\times_{i}\alpha_{i,i}^{-1}([{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}])\\
=\left(\times_{i}\alpha_{i,i}\right)^{-1}(\times_{i}[{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i},{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}])\\
\subseteq\left(\times_{i}\alpha_{i,i}\right)^{-1}\left(f^{-1}([f(\langle{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i}\rangle),f(\langle{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}\rangle)])\right)\\
=\left(f\mathbin{\circ}\times_{i}\alpha_{i,i}\right)^{-1}\left([f(\langle{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i}\rangle),f(\langle{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}\rangle)]\right).

Together with

\displaystyle\times_{i}\mu_{i,i}(\times_{i}X_{i,i})=\mu_{1,1}(X_{1,1})\times\mu_{2,2}(X_{2,2})\cdots\geq\times_{i}p_{i},

we have that
[f(\langle{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{L},i}\rangle),f(\langle{\mathrm{dp}}_{\mathrm{U},i}\rangle)]
is an inner confidence bound of level
\times_{i}p_{i}
.
∎

IV-B

Implementations, specifications, and adaptive decisions

In deterministic co-design,
MDPIs represent design processes: choosing
implementations
yields deterministic
DPs (
Definition

17
).
With distributional uncertainty, we extend this to
MDPIs with distributional uncertainty
, where design results are distributions over the
QUS of
DPs, and adaptivity is modeled by Markov kernels and re-parameterization.

Definition 30

(
MDPIs with distributional uncertainty)
.

Let
{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}F}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0.7,0,0}R}}
satisfy
1
.
An
MDPI with distributional uncertainty
is a tuple
\langle{\color[rgb]{0.258,0.527,0.957}S},{\color[rgb]{1,0.58,0.063}I},a\rangle

{\color[rgb]{0.258,0.527,0.957}S}

and

Even though all uncertainty, including uncertainty in

explicit in

.
This allows us to

(Re-parameterization of distributional uncertain

Given a distributional uncertain

Diagram of

.

{\color[rgb]{0.258,0.527,0.957}S}_{k}

Remark 13
.

Beyond parameterized policies, one can also consider higher-order objects: the space of Markov kernels
\mathsf{QUS}\{A,\mathcal{P}(B)\}
is itself a
QUS, and the evaluation map is quasi-measurable.
Thus, policies can themselves be included among
implementations
.
In practice, however, parameterized policies (e.g., controllers or design rules with a finite-dimensional parameter vector) are more convenient.

Remark 14
.

Since
\mathcal{P}
forms a symmetric strong monad on
\mathsf{QUS}

[
9
]
, its Kleisli category is monoidal.
Re-parameterizations can thus be represented as arbitrary string diagrams in this monoidal Kleisli category, like those in
Figure

7
, and categorical coherence guarantees that any such diagram corresponds to a well-defined composite Markov kernel.

Remark 15
.

The lifted co-design operations of
Theorem

4
and the re-parameterizations of
Definition

31
interact to provide two layers of adaptivity: (1) at the
policy level
, via the kernels
\pi_{k}
; and (2) at the
co-design level
, via the fact that co-design composition is performed after all relevant uncertainties for the composite have been observed.
In interval uncertain co-design (
Section

III-B
), the optimistic and pessimistic
DPs are solved independently, so the design choices are allowed to be different in the two cases and are all made on the
co-design level
, namely after observing all the relevant uncertainties.
Formally, these structures can be organized into a 2-category whose 1-cells are uncertain co-design problems and whose 2-cells are re-parameterizations
[
11
]
.

Overall, compositionality and re-parameterization give designers a quantitative, compositional, and adaptive language for uncertain system design: uncertain
MDPIs for different components can be defined locally, and then composed and re-parameterized using Markov kernels that represent the adaptive design process.
Diagrams like
Figure

8
represent such compositional adaptive design processes compactly.

Lemma 11

Proof.

Policies used for comparison

The framework allows general (including randomized) design policies, important when chance constraints or explicit risk measures are imposed
[
24
]
.
Here we further allow policies in
Figures

7(a)
and
7(b)
to depend on
payload
, and use a simple deterministic policy template:
given observations, choose the
implementation
that is most likely to be optimal for minimal
lifetime cost
.
Operationally, this policy is obtained from Monte Carlo sampling where, for each observation, we estimate the probability that each implementation attains the minimal lifetime cost, and then selecting the maximum a posteriori (MAP) option.
This is meant to (approximately) optimize expected minimal lifetime cost under the corresponding information structure, while keeping the comparison across adaptivity levels transparent.

VI
Conclusions and future work

We developed a compositional framework for co-design under
distributional
uncertainty with explicit
adaptive
multi-stage decision-making.
Building on monotone co-design, we used
Quasi Measurable Space/
Quasi Universal Spaceto define distributions over
design problemsthat avoid classical measurability pathologies while preserving a tractable notion of feasibility.
In this setting, the standard co-design interconnection operations (series, parallel, feedback, union, and intersection) remain compositional and lift naturally to distributions over design problems.
We also introduced adaptive design processes via Markov-kernel re-parameterizations, supporting uncountable specification spaces, dependence between specifications and design choices, and risk-aware objectives and constraints.
Finally, we formalized
queries
and
observations
as the interface between the mathematical model and engineering questions, including probability-of-feasibility, confidence bounds, and minimal-resource observations, and illustrated the framework on an uncertain task-driven unmanned aerial vehicle co-design study.

Several directions remain.
On the algorithmic side, we currently rely on Monte Carlo estimation; an important next step is to develop
compositional
estimators and probability bounds that exploit the co-design graph structure, including variance-reduced sampling, concentration-based certificates, and deterministic inner/outer bounds when monotonicity is available.
For modeling, we will incorporate standard risk measures and constraints, such as chance constraints, CVaR-type objectives, and distributionally robust variants, directly into the query layer, and to study policy synthesis under partial observations.
More broadly, the framework suggests an end-to-end learning loop: infer and update specification distributions from data, propagate uncertainty compositionally, and optimize adaptive policies at the system level.
