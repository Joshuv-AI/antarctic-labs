A cephalometric analysis of underlying components of deep overbite malocclusion
Authors: Avinash Chaudhary, Sushant Pandey, Samikshya Sangroula, Jamal Giri, Prabhat Ranjan Pokharel, Rajesh Gyawali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeep overbite is a critical parameter in orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning, reflecting the excessive vertical overlap of anterior teeth. It not only affects facial esthetics but may also contribute to functional disturbances. It is influenced by a complex interplay of skeletal, dental, and angular factors. Understanding the relative association of these components is critical for accurate diagnosis and effective treatment planning. So, this study aims to determine which skeletal and dental components most significantly affect deep overbite.Materials and methodsThe study was carried out on 252 pre-treatment lateral cephalograms of Nepalese patients of mixed ethnic backgrounds (Indo-Nepali, Tibeto-Nepali, and Indigenous Nepali) who presented with an overbite of greater than 4 mm measured on the lateral cephalogram, in the Department of Orthodontics, BPKIHS, Nepal. Among them, 130 were males and 122 were females. The mean age of the subjects was 22.26 ± 3.85 years (range:16 to 41 years). Lateral cephalograms meeting the inclusion/exclusion criteria were digitally traced using Onyxceph3TM Pro (version 3.2.67) software. Twelve vertical and angular, skeletal and dental parameters from Burstone’s Cephalometrics for Orthognathic Surgery (COGS) analysis were measured. Descriptive analysis was performed for each variable. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed performed to evaluate the association between skeletal and dentoalveolar variables and deep overbite. Iterative backward elimination regression approach was used to assess the association between the variables and deep over bite after considering multicollinearity.ResultsThe final model explained 71.4% of the variance in deep overbite (R²=0.714, p < 0.001). Lower anterior dental height (L1-MP) had the strongest effect on deep overbite. Other significant predictors included lower anterior facial height (ANSGn), mandibular plane angle (MP-HP), upper anterior dental height (U1-NF), upper anterior inclination (U1NFang), Lower anterior inclination (L1MPang) and lower posterior dental height (LM-MP).ConclusionOverbite is predominantly influenced by dentoalveolar factors, particularly incisor position and inclination, while skeletal variables play a secondary role.

Introduction

Malocclusion constitutes a significant deviation from ideal occlusion, encompassing irregularities in tooth alignment and jaw relations that may compromise function, esthetics, and oral health. It represents one of the most common oral health problems encountered in clinical practice worldwide [1]. Among the various forms of malocclusion, deep overbite — a vertical discrepancy where the mandibular incisors are excessively overlapped by maxillary incisors, has been documented in approximately 8.4–51.5% of population globally. The wide variation in reported prevalence of deep overbite may be attributed to differences in diagnostic thresholds, ethnic variation, age distribution, and methodological heterogeneity among epidemiological studies [2, 3].

The etiology of deep bite is multifactorial, involving combination of genetic and environmental components. Cuspal height of molars and premolars, ramus length, molar height, interincisal angle, incisor dimensions and facial type, have all been reported to influence the development of deep overbite [4–7] From etiological perspective, deep overbite may be attributed to hereditary and developmental factors. Based on whether the deep bite is skeletal or dentoalveolar, it may present with increased overjet, wear of incisors, palatal impingement, compromised esthetics, and temporomandibular joint disorder in extreme cases [8–11].

Correction of overbite is critical in orthodontics. It is suggested that it should be corrected prior to the correction of overjet [12]. The amount of overbite, irrespective of sagittal maxilla-mandibular relation, shows individual variation. Understanding these relationships is essential for effective treatment planning. Orthodontic management of deep bite often involves intrusion of anterior teeth, extrusion of posterior teeth or combination of both. In severe cases, treatment with both the braces and corrective jaw surgery may be required [13, 14]. Decisions regarding modality, retention strategies, and prediction of stability are better informed when the clinician has a clear picture of the patient’s craniofacial architecture [8, 15–18]. Accurate assessment of dentofacial morphology in individuals with deep bite extends beyond routine dental measurements. Cephalometric analysis offers valuable insight into underlying skeletal and dental patterns that may contribute to deep bite. Evaluation of cephalometric data assists in differentiating deep bite cases with inherent skeletal or dental abnormalities, need of conventional orthodontic treatment or in extreme case orthognathic surgery, that may affect treatment responsiveness and long-term outcomes. Therefore, the objective of this study was to determine which skeletal and dental components most significantly affect deep overbite.

Materials and methods

A retrospective study was conducted on 252 pre-orthodontic lateral cephalograms obtained from the Department of Orthodontics, BPKIHS, Dharan after getting ethical clearance from the Institutional review Committee, BPKIHS [IRC/2447/023].

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was determined based on the availability of pretreatment lateral cephalograms that fulfilled the predefined eligibility criteria during the study period, using a convenience sampling approach. A total of 252 subjects were included in the final analysis. For the planned multiple linear regression analysis, sample size adequacy was assessed in relation to the number of predictor variables included in the model. The study incorporated 12 predictor variables, resulting in a subject to predictor ratio of 21:1 (252/12). This exceeds the commonly recommended minimum ratio of 15 subjects per predictor variable for obtaining stable and reliable regression estimates. Therefore, the available sample was considered sufficient to support the planned statistical analyses [19].

The selection of pretreatment cephalograms was based on certain inclusion and exclusion criteria. These included an age of 16 years or greater, presence of complete set of natural permanent dentition up to second molars, good-quality radiograph with clearly identifiable landmarks, and radiographs taken from the same machine and technique with similar calibration. Subjects with cephalometrically measured overbite greater than 4 mm were included in the study. Lateral cephalograms of subjects who had undergone orthodontic, orthopedic, orthognathic surgical treatment, splint therapy, dental prosthesis, plastic surgery; with history of trauma to orofacial region; with temporomandibular joint disorders, medical condition that affects the growth of the mandible and maxilla, systemic syndromes and craniofacial anomalies were excluded.

The cephalograms were digitally traced using Onyxceph3TM Pro (version 3.2.67, Image instruments GmbH, Germany). Landmarks were manually located by one trained orthodontist to minimize inter-examiner variations. To prevent the measurement error and to test the magnitude of intra-examiner error, 51 randomly selected samples were retraced and re-measured by the same researcher two weeks after the initial measurements. Radiographic landmarks for the study were in accordance to the hard tissue vertical parameters used in Cephalometrics for Orthognathic Surgery (COGS) by Burstone et al. [20] (Fig. 1).

The obtained data were entered in to Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 24.0 (SPSS 24.0; SPSS, Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA) for statistical analysis and testing. Obtained data were then tested for normality distribution using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Descriptive statistics derived were means, median, standard deviations and interquartile range for each measured parameter based on the normality distribution. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between skeletal and dentoalveolar variables and deep overbite. Initially, a full regression model including all the 12 predictor variables was constructed. The statistical significance of individual predictors was evaluated using regression coefficients and associated t-tests. Variables that did not demonstrate a statistically significant contribution to the model were removed, and the regression analysis was repeated with the remaining variables. This iterative process continued until all retained predictors showed a statistically significant independent contribution to the model. At each step, multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) values. The final regression model included only statistically significant predictors with VIF values less than 10. Additionally, tolerance values and condition indices were evaluated to assess multicollinearity among predictors.

Regression coefficients with corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

Results

The present study was conducted on 252 lateral cephalograms of pre-orthodontic subjects with deep overbite. 51 random samples were retraced and measured after an interval of 2 weeks. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) showed a high correlation between the two measurements. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for overbite (OB) was 0.997, while the ICC values for the independent variables ranged from 0.917 for upper anterior dental height (U1–NF) to 0.999 for the gonial angle (ArGoGn). Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to determine the distribution of data. Except for the overbite and U1-NF all other variables were normally distributed.

Discussion

This retrospective study evaluated 252 lateral Cephalograms of pre-orthodontic patients with an overbite greater than 4 mm, to determine the skeletal and dental parameters associated to the development of deep overbite. Since the data were collected from existing records, it was not possible to control for all the potential confounding variables. To minimize selection bias, we applied predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria consistently across all eligible patients during the study period. Consecutive cases meeting eligibility criteria were included to reduce investigator-driven selection. A total of 12 cephalometric parameters, five skeletal and seven dental, were digitally measured using Onyxceph3TM Pro (version 3.2.67), a system with established accuracy [21].

Deep overbite has been studied from cephalometric measurements to evaluate different skeletal and dentoalveolar presentations with heterogenous measurement methods and statistical approaches [4, 22, 23]. While some of the studies done have focused on evaluating the contribution of specific component on overbite, others have evaluated relative contribution of underlying components without giving due consideration to mutual impact among the underlying variables. The present study addresses this gap by evaluating the association of skeletal and dental variables to deep overbite using multiple regression analysis.

The present study employed multiple linear regression analysis to evaluate the relationship between skeletal and dental parameters and deep overbite. The overall regression model was found to be statistically significant, indicating that the selected variables when considered collectively, are meaningful in predicting deep overbite. The model showed a strong overall fit with R² of 0.722, suggesting that the model explained approximately 72.2% of the variance in deep overbite. All variables except N-ANS, PNS-N, ArGoGn and OPHP showed significant association to deep overbite. So, these variables were excluded in the next regression model.

On analysis of the next model, UM-NF showed insignificant effect on deep overbite. Final regression analysis (model 2) was conducted by excluding N-ANS, PNS-N, ArGoGn, OPHP and UM-NF. Model 2 explained approximately 71.4% of the variance in deep overbite. The unexplained variance of 28.6% may be attributable to non-significant predictors that were removed from the analysis and factors such as genetic makeup, parafunctional habits, curve of Spee, and ramus length, which were not included in the present analysis. Exclusion of 5 variables barely decreased the explained variance by 0.8%, but decreased the multicollinearity between the variables improving the overall model. On comparison of the two models, no significant difference in Sig. F change was seen, suggesting that the model 2 was sufficient enough to explain the variance in deep overbite.

Based on Model 2, L1-MP and U1-NF showed positive association with deep overbite which was statistically significant. Whereas, ANSGN, MPHP, U1NF angle, L1MP angle and LM-MP demonstrated significant negative associations, indicating that increases in these parameters are associated with reduced overbite magnitude.

Among all variables, L1-MP emerged as the strongest predictor of deep overbite (β = 1.754), indicating that for every 1 mm increase in lower anterior dental height, overbite increased by 0.691 mm after accounting for all other predictors. ANSGn demonstrated the strongest negative association (β = −1.450), with each 1 mm increase in lower anterior facial height associated with a 0.379 mm decrease in overbite. Overeruption of lower incisors have been found as one of the major contributor to deep overbite (37%) by El-Dawlatly et al. [22]. Naini et al. too has mentioned lower incisor extrusion as one of the major cause of deep overbite [24]. An accentuated Curve of Spee has been identified as a principal dental contributor to deep bite by El-Daawlatly et al., Fattahi et al., and Bhateja et al., however, these studies did not account for collinearity [22, 23, 25]. Given that the continued eruption of the mandibular incisors has been implicated as the primary etiological factor in the development of the Curve of Spee, it is reasonable to regard lower incisor extrusion as the predominant predictor in the manifestation of deep bite [26–28].

Upper incisor vertical position as measured by U1-NF too showed positive association suggesting that extrusion of upper incisors is associated with deep overbite. This finding is in agreement with the findings of Prakash and Margolis, Barman, El-Dawlatly et al., and, Fattahi et al. [4, 22, 23, 29]

ANSGn, LM-MP, MPHP, L1MP angle and U1NF angle showed significant negative association with deep overbite. Among the negative predictors, ANSGn showed strongest negative effect. The finding that ANSGn exhibited the strongest negative effect indicates that an increase in lower anterior facial height is associated with a decrease in overbite. Similarly, reduced LM-MP indicates insufficient posterior dentoalveolar height or infraerupted molars. This decreases posterior vertical support, causing the anterior teeth to overlap excessively and resulting in a deeper Curve of Spee. An accentuated Curve of Spee is a characteristic feature of deep bite malocclusion [30, 31]. These findings are consistent with the observation made by Proffit et al., who described deep overbite as a consequence of reduced lower anterior facial height, decreased mandibular plane angle, infraocclusion of posterior teeth, and retroclination of incisors along with other skeletal and dental components [1]. Similarly, Graber, and Nanda have emphasized the role of decreased lower anterior facial height, decreased posterior dental height and lower mandibular plane in the development of deep overbite [32, 33] These findings are also in complete agreement with the findings of Fattahi et al., El-Dawlatly et al., and Watted et al. [22, 23, 34] Bhateja et al., and Al-Attar et al. too found negative association between lower anterior facial height, mandibular plane and deep overbite [25, 35]. It should be noted that ANSGn retained a VIF of 7.96 in the final model, reflecting its structural correlation with other vertical variables. While this value falls within the acceptable threshold of VIF < 10 and tolerance > 0.1 applied in this study, [36] it suggests that its regression coefficient should be interpreted with appropriate caution, as its apparent association may partially reflect shared variance with MPHP and L1-MP.

In contrast, upper anterior facial height (N-ANS), upper posterior facial height (PNS-N), gonial angle (ArGoGn), occlusal plane angle (OPHP), and upper posterior dental height (UM-NF) did not demonstrate significant independent associations in the final regression model. This may be because their effects were partially represented through correlated vertical skeletal and dentoalveolar variables included in the model. Furthermore, the association of these variables to deep overbite may be indirect or dependent on interactions with other craniofacial characteristics.

While the condition index was above 30 in the collinearity diagnostic table with elevated variance-decomposition proportions for ANSGn (0.85) and L1-MP (0.65), their corresponding VIF values (7.96 and 6.35, respectively) remain well below the critical threshold of 10, indicating that although these two variables share variance, their individual coefficients are not severely destabilized. Elevated condition index with moderate, sub-threshold VIFs indicates residual but non-critical multicollinearity. Furthermore, it is well established that condition indices computed in SPSS — incorporate the intercept column, which can artificially elevate the condition number without reflecting true predictor-to-predictor collinearity among the study variables. The near-equivalence of R² (0.714) and Adjusted R² (0.706), along with statistical significance of all predictors, further supports the stability of the model [36, 37].

The present findings may have important implications for orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning. Since lower anterior dental height and incisor inclination demonstrated stronger associations with deep overbite than skeletal variables, careful control of incisor position during treatment may be particularly important in deep bite correction. The results support the biomechanical rationale for incisor intrusion and maintenance of appropriate incisor inclination during treatment and retention [38]. Furthermore, the significant association of lower anterior facial height and mandibular plane angle suggests that vertical skeletal pattern should still be considered during treatment planning, especially in patients with reduced lower facial height and horizontal growth tendencies.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective cross-sectional design limited the control over potential confounding variables and did not permit causal inference. Second, the sample was derived from a single institutional population in Nepal, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic or geographic populations. In addition, some degree of residual multicollinearity may have persisted despite the sequential removal of highly collinear variables. Although variance inflation factor and tolerance values in the final model remained within acceptable limits, elevated condition indices indicated underlying correlations among certain predictors, which may influence the stability and interpretation of individual regression coefficients.

The present study was designed primarily to evaluate the association of skeletal and dentoalveolar variables associated with deep overbite. Although age and sex distribution was recorded descriptively, they were not included as covariates in the regression model. Consequently, the potential influence of age and gender related differences on the observed associations could not be evaluated. This should be considered a limitation of the study and may restrict the generalizability of the findings to populations with different age and sex distributions.

Future longitudinal studies with stratified sampling, control group, larger subgroup sizes and inclusion of 3D imaging modalities may further clarify whether these associations differ according to sex, skeletal classification, growth pattern, associated parafunctional habits or deep bite severity.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this retrospective cephalometric study, the final regression model showed that dentoalveolar parameters explained a greater proportion of overbite variance than skeletal parameters. Lower anterior dental height and incisor inclination showed the strongest associations with overbite magnitude, while lower anterior facial height and mandibular plane angle demonstrated significant inverse associations. Among all variables, the lower anterior dental height (L1-MP) was the strongest independent predictor of deep overbite (standardized β = 1.754), highlighting the predominant role of dentoalveolar compensation in the Nepalese orthodontic population.
