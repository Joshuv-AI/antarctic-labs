The YTHDC1/HECW1/SMAD7 axis drives cisplatin resistance in bladder cancer
Authors: Weigang Ren, Jiaxiang Yi, Mingqiang Zeng, Hongtao Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCisplatin-based combination chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of treatment for advanced bladder cancer (BC). However, primary or acquired resistance following long-term use remains a critical unresolved issue in BC treatment. While accumulating evidence implicates epitranscriptomic modifications in driving chemoresistance, the precise underlying mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated. Exploring the potential mechanisms underlying cisplatin resistance in BC is crucial for identifying novel therapeutic targets and prolonging patient survival. This study aims to identify the key N6-methyladenosine (m6A) regulatory factors related to chemotherapy resistance in BC and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsIntegrated bioinformatic analyses were performed to screen and identify critical m6A readers associated with cisplatin resistance in BC. Gene expression and molecular interactions were analyzed using reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), Western blotting, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP), and co-immunoprecipitation. Cell proliferation and apoptosis assays were employed to evaluate chemosensitivity and therapeutic interventions.ResultsIn this study, we identified the m6A reader YTHDC1 as a critical regulator of cisplatin resistance in BC. Mechanistically, YTHDC1 expression was significantly downregulated in cisplatin-resistant BC cells. The reduced YTHDC1 expression enhanced the stability and inhibited the degradation of HECW1 messenger RNA (mRNA). Consequently, the upregulated E3 ubiquitin ligase HECW1 directly interacted with SMAD7 and promoted its ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation. The diminished SMAD7 protein levels ultimately reduced the sensitivity of BC cells to cisplatin treatment.ConclusionsThis study identifies a YTHDC1-HECW1-SMAD7 axis that regulates cisplatin resistance in BC. Targeting this axis with HDAC2 inhibitors or curcumin potentiates the anti-tumor efficacy of cisplatin, offering a promising therapeutic strategy to overcome chemoresistance in BC.Keywords: Bladder cancer (BC), cisplatin, N6-methyladenosine reader (m6A reader), ubiquitination

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study identifies a YTHDC1-HECW1-SMAD7 axis that regulates cisplatin resistance in bladder cancer (BC). Targeting this axis with HDAC2 inhibitors or curcumin effectively resensitizes BC cells to cisplatin, potentiating its anti-tumor efficacy.

What is known and what is new?

• The N6-methyladenosine (m6A) readers were involved in regulating cisplatin sensitivity.

• Through integrated screening, we identified the m6A reader YTHDC1 as a critical suppressor of cisplatin resistance. Mechanistically, downregulated YTHDC1 stabilizes HECW1 mRNA, which consequently promotes the ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation of SMAD7 to induce chemoresistance.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Our findings elucidate a novel mechanistic crosstalk between RNA epitranscriptomic modification and the ubiquitin-proteasome system in driving BC chemoresistance.

• Future clinical and translational research should explore the combination of cisplatin with HDAC2 inhibitors or curcumin as a promising therapeutic strategy to overcome acquired chemoresistance in patients with advanced BC.

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC) is one of the most common malignant tumors of the urinary system, with more than 500,000 new cases and approximately 200,000 deaths reported annually (1,2). Although enfortumab vedotin (EV) combined with immunotherapy has been recommended as a first-line standard of care for the majority of eligible patients with advanced BC, the high cost imposes a substantial economic burden on both patients and society (3,4). Cisplatin-based combination chemotherapy remains the mainstay of treatment for advanced BC (5). However, the development of cisplatin resistance following long-term administration severely compromises patient prognosis (6). Therefore, further investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying cisplatin sensitivity in BC patients is crucial for the identification and development of novel therapeutic targets.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) represents the most abundant internal modification of eukaryotic RNA. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that m6A is involved in multiple aspects of RNA regulation, including messenger RNA (mRNA) splicing, nuclear export, mRNA stability, and translational control (7). m6A methylation is a dynamically reversible process: it is installed by m6A methyltransferases, erased by demethylases, and recognized by m6A -binding proteins (8). m6A -binding proteins, including YTHDF1/2/3, YTHDC1/2, and IGF2BP1/2/3, etc., are a class of proteins that specifically recognize and bind to m6A-modified sites on RNA. They directly participate in the regulation of gene expression and exert critical roles in tumorigenesis, tumor progression, and therapeutic resistance (9). A growing body of research has confirmed that m6A readers play important roles in modulating cisplatin treatment sensitivity. Hao et al. showed that YTHDF1-mediated upregulation of TRIM29 promotes cisplatin resistance and cancer cell stemness in ovarian cancer (10). Huang et al. revealed that YTHDF2 accelerates intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma progression and impairs cisplatin sensitivity by enhancing the degradation of CDKN1B mRNA (11). In addition, Zhu et al. demonstrated that YTHDF1 modulates cell proliferation and cisplatin sensitivity via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis in BC cells (12). Although previous studies have reported that the m6A readers were involved in regulating cisplatin sensitivity, the complex and diverse regulatory system of epigenetic transcriptional regulation remains unclear, and its underlying mechanism still needs to be further explored.

In this study, we demonstrated that the expression of the m6A reader YTHDC1 was significantly downregulated in cisplatin-resistant BC cells. The reduced YTHDC1 expression inhibited the degradation of HECW1 mRNA. The upregulated HECW1 bound to SMAD7 and mediated its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby decreasing cisplatin sensitivity in BC cells. The combined use of HDAC2 inhibitor CAY10683 to enhance the transcription of YTHDC1 or Curcumin to increase the expression of SMAD7 can enhance the anti-tumor effect of cisplatin in BC. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2026-0204/rc).

Methods

Bioinformatics analysis of public datasets

The transcriptomic data for the GSE231835 dataset were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Data normalization and differential expression analysis were performed using the DESeq2 package in R software. The thresholds for identifying significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were set as an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) >1.0 and an adjusted P value <0.05. For clinical cohort validation, the transcriptomic analysis and data visualization of the IMvigor210 cohort were conducted utilizing the BEST web server hosted on the Hiplot platform (https://rookieutopia.hiplot.com.cn/app_direct/BEST/#PageHomeAnalysisModuleSelection). To explore the underlying epitranscriptomic mechanisms, the m6A modification sites within the HECW1 transcript were predicted and visualized using the RMBase v2.0 database (https://rna.sysu.edu.cn/rmbase/index.php). Subsequently, the potential RNA-protein binding interaction between the m6A reader YTHDC1 and HECW1 mRNA was evaluated utilizing the ENCORI (Encyclopedia of RNA Interactomes) platform (https://rnasysu.com/encori/index.php). Finally, to identify downstream ubiquitination targets, the protein-protein interaction network of HECW1 was constructed and analyzed using the BioGRID database (https://thebiogrid.org/). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Cell lines

The human BC cell lines 5637 (RRID: CVCL_0126) and T24 (RRID: CVCL_0554), alongside the cisplatin-resistant T24 R variant, were obtained from Jinyuan Biology Technology (Shanghai, China). Routine STR profiling and PlasmoTest™ (InvivoGen) assays ensured lineage validity and mycoplasma-free status. Cells were incubated at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2 in designated media (McCoy’s 5A for T24; RPMI-1640 for 5637 and T24 R), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotics. T24 R cells were maintained under selective pressure using 0.5 mg/L cisplatin.

Transient transfections (for knockdown or overexpression) were executed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) with 2 µg/mL of the respective vectors, strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Post-transfection (24-72 hours), cells were collected for downstream evaluation. For the generation of stable cell lines, transfected cells harboring indicator plasmids were continuously screened utilizing puromycin until stable clones were established.

The cDNA sequences for YTHDC1 (#P44598), HECW1 (#P76007), and SMAD7 (#P70795) were provided by Miaolingbio (Wuhan, China). Following standard protocols, both full-length and truncated versions of these genes were generated and subsequently inserted into PCDH-3×Flag or PCDH-3×HA backbone vectors. For gene silencing, short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting the respective genes were synthesized by GeneCopoeia (Guangzhou, China). Detailed shRNA sequences are provided in Table 1.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Cells were seeded into 96-well microplates, with all experimental conditions tested in triplicate. After a 24-hour incubation with specified drug concentrations, each well was supplemented with 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent. The microplates were subsequently incubated for an additional 2 hours at 37 ℃ in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Optical density was measured at 450 nm utilizing a microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were computed from the dose-response curves according to the manufacturer’s analytical instructions.

Colony formation assay staining

After the termination of colony formation culture, cell colonies were fixed with 4% neutral paraformaldehyde fix solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China, G1101) for 15 min at room temperature. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), colonies were stained with 0.1% crystal violet staining solution for colony assay (Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China, G1014) for 10 min. Excess staining solution was washed off with ultrapure water, and stained cell colonies were photographed under a bright-field microscope to obtain the cell images.

Antibodies and reagents

YTHDC1 (1:1000; 14392-1-AP, RRID: AB_2218881), HECW1 (1:1500; PA5-115349, RRID: AB_2874135), SMAD7 (1:2,000; 25840-1-AP, RRID: AB_2877239), and cleaved caspase-3 (1:1,000; 19677-1-AP, RRID: AB_10693526). Tagged proteins were detected utilizing anti-Flag (1:20,000; 66008-4-Ig, RRID: AB_2881699) or anti-HA (1:5,000; 51064-2-AP, RRID: AB_2910459) antibodies.

Cisplatin (#S1166), MG132 (#S2619), CAY10683 (#S7595), and curcumin (#S1848) were obtained from Selleck (Shanghai, China).

Western blot analysis and coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Total cellular proteins were extracted utilizing RIPA lysis buffer (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, G2002) supplemented with protease (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, P1005) and phosphatase (Beyotime, P1081) inhibitors. Protein yields were quantified via the BCA protein assay. Equivalent amounts of denatured protein lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and electrophoretically transferred onto 0.45 µm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk, followed by overnight incubation at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies formulated in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Post-washing with Tris Buffered Saline with Tween 20 (TBST; three times), membranes were probed with corresponding secondary antibodies for one hour at ambient temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an ECL detection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and captured on a ChemiDoc XRS imaging platform (Bio-Rad) after three terminal TBST washes.

For Co-IP assays, treated cell lysates were prepared in the aforementioned inhibitor-supplemented RIPA buffer. The clarified lysates obtained post-centrifugation were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with target-specific primary antibodies and magnetic beads. Following extensive washing (six times), the immunocomplexes were eluted and subjected to standard immunoblotting analysis.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Following total RNA extraction with TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), RNA concentration was determined using the NanoDrop 2000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was carried out using the #AG11728 reverse transcription kit (Accurate Biotechnology, Changsha, China). For the qPCR assay, cDNA was amplified using the Evo M-MLV one-step RT-qPCR kit (#AG11732, Accurate Biology, China) alongside specific primers, the sequences of which are detailed in Table 2.

mRNA stability assay

Cells were cultured to 70–80% confluency in 6-well plates and treated with actinomycin D (ActD, #50-76-0, MedChemExpress) at a final concentration of 5 µg/mL [diluted from 5 mg/mL stock solution in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)] to inhibit de novo transcription. Cells without ActD treatment served as the negative control. At designated time points (0, 1, 2, 3 h after ActD addition), cells were harvested immediately, and total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed to detect the relative expression level of target mRNA and internal reference mRNA (GAPDH).

Apoptosis assay

To quantify the extent of cellular apoptosis, an Annexin V-FITC/PI dual-labeling approach was applied. The cell suspensions were treated with the appropriate fluorochromes supplied in the Vazyme Apoptosis Detection Kit (A211-02) strictly following the provided guidelines. After being allowed to incubate for 15 min at ambient temperature, the samples were subjected to flow cytometric analysis, with subsequent data interpretation conducted using FlowJo.

Statistical analysis

All statistical evaluations were conducted employing GraphPad Prism 8 software. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) derived from a minimum of three independent biological replicates. Statistical comparisons between two independent cohorts were performed using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test. For multigroup comparisons, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied. The number of biological replicates (n) is detailed in the respective figure legends. The threshold for statistical significance was established at P<0.05. Significance levels are denoted as follows: *, P<0.05; **, P<0.01; ***, P<0.001; and ns, not significant, P>0.05.

Results

YTHDC1 is a key molecule regulating cisplatin sensitivity in BC

By integrating analyses of DEGs associated with cisplatin resistance in BC with reported m6A reader-related genes, we identified YTHDC1 as the only gene exhibiting significant differential expression (Figure 1A). Our previous study demonstrated that the downregulation of YTHDC1 in BC promotes tumor progression by regulating glucose metabolism via the GLUT3-RNF183 axis (13). Consistently, analysis of the IMvigor210 public dataset revealed that YTHDC1 expression was significantly reduced following platinum-based chemotherapy (Figure 1B). To further validate these findings, we established T24 cell lines with normal cisplatin sensitivity and cisplatin resistance (Figure 1C). Both mRNA and protein analyses confirmed that YTHDC1 expression was markedly decreased in cisplatin-resistant BC cells (Figure 1D,1E). To verify the role of YTHDC1 in cisplatin sensitivity of BC, we found that the IC50 of cisplatin was significantly increased after YTHDC1 knockdown (Figure 1F,1G). Conversely, YTHDC1 overexpression markedly decreased the IC50 of cisplatin (Figure 1H,1I). Consistent results were verified in colony formation and apoptosis-related assays (Figure 1J-1N). Collectively, these findings suggest that downregulation of YTHDC1 attenuates cisplatin sensitivity in BC.

YTHDC1 promotes the degradation of HECW1 mRNA

The subsequent question to be addressed was how YTHDC1 regulates cisplatin resistance in BC. By integrating the analysis of YTHDC1-bound genes, genes upregulated in cisplatin-resistance BC cells, and genes upregulated following YTHDC1 knockdown, we identified that HECW1 might be the potential key gene through which YTHDC1 regulates cisplatin sensitivity in BC (Figure 2A). Analysis using RMBase v2.0 (https://rna.sysu.edu.cn/rmbase/index.php) revealed that the mRNA of HECW1 harbors abundant m6A modifications (Figure 2B). Results of RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays with an anti-m6A antibody demonstrated that HECW1 undergoes m6A modification in both T24 and 5637 cell lines (Figure 2C). Furthermore, analysis via the ENCORI web tool (https://rnasysu.com/encori/index.php) indicated that three YTHDC1-binding regions are present within HECW1 (Figure 2D) which was subsequently validated by RIP assays using an anti-YTHDC1 antibody (Figure 2E). YTHDC1 has been reported to modulate the splicing, subcellular localization and stability of its target mRNAs (14), we next investigated the mechanism by which YTHDC1 regulates HECW1 expression. Our results showed that HECW1 expression was increased following YTHDC1 knockdown, whereas YTHDC1 overexpression led to a decrease in HECW1 expression (Figure 2F,2G). Furthermore, our findings indicated that altered YTHDC1 expression did not affect the subcellular localization of HECW1 (Figure 2H,2I), but alter the stability of HECW1 mRNA (Figure 2J,2K). Functionally, CCK-8 and colony formation assays demonstrated that HECW1 knockdown potentiated the anti-tumor effects of cisplatin in BC cells and partially reversed the cisplatin resistance induced by YTHDC1 downregulation (Figure 2L,2M). These results suggest that YTHDC1 downregulation impairs the anti-tumor efficacy of cisplatin by stabilizing HECW1 mRNA in BC.

HECW1 promotes SMAD7 degradation through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway

As an E3 ubiquitin ligase, HECW1 may play a critical role in regulating the ubiquitination and degradation of target proteins. High-throughput screening via the BioGRID database identified SMAD7 as a potential HECW1-interacting protein (Figure 3A). SMAD7 is an endogenous negative regulator of the TGFβ-SMAD signaling pathway that inhibits TGFβ receptor-mediated SMAD phosphorylation. Previous studies have shown that SMAD7 may play an important role in the malignant progression of BC (15). Our results demonstrated that HECW1 is capable of interacting with SMAD7 (Figure 3B). HECW1 knockdown did not affect the mRNA expression level of SMAD7, but significantly increased its protein expression level (Figure 3C,3D). In contrast, HECW1 overexpression markedly suppressed SMAD7 protein expression, and this effect could be reversed by MG132, a proteasome pathway inhibitor (Figure 3E,3F). Further studies revealed that HECW1 knockdown induced the half-life of SMAD7 (Figure 3G) and inhibited its polyubiquitination (Figure 3H). In contrast, HECW1 overexpression enhanced the polyubiquitination of SMAD7 (Figure 3I). Consistently, YTHDC1 knockdown did not affect the mRNA expression of SMAD7, but promoted the polyubiquitination level and protein degradation of SMAD7. However, the effect of YTHDC1 knockdown could be reversed by HECW1 silencing (Figure 3J-3L). This suggests that HECW1 enhances the degradation of SMAD7 via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway.

The YTHDC1-HECW1-SMAD7 axis regulates cisplatin sensitivity in BC

Having established that the YTHDC1/HECW1 axis negatively regulates SMAD7 protein stability, we next sought to investigate whether this specific signaling cascade is functionally responsible for the altered cisplatin sensitivity observed in BC cells. Our findings demonstrated that SMAD7 knockdown elevated the IC50 of cisplatin in BC cells, and this effect was independent of altered YTHDC1 and HECW1 expression (Figure 4A,4B). Consistently, colony formation assays demonstrated that the enhanced anti-tumor efficacy of cisplatin induced by YTHDC1 overexpression or HECW1 knockdown was largely abolished upon SMAD7 depletion. In contrast, cisplatin resistance resulting from YTHDC1 downregulation or HECW1 overexpression was further exacerbated by SMAD7 knockdown (Figure 4C-4F). These results were verified in apoptosis-related experiments (Figure 4G,4H). Collectively, these results indicate that the YTHDC1-HECW1-SMAD7 axis plays a crucial role in regulating cisplatin sensitivity in BC.

Combination with CAY10683 or curcumin enhances cisplatin sensitivity in BC

These findings indicate that the YTHDC1-HECW1-SMAD7 axis plays an important role in regulating cisplatin resistance in BC, suggesting that targeting this axis may serve as a potential therapeutic target for BC. Previous studies have demonstrated that CAY10683 can upregulate the expression of YTHDC1 via the inhibition of HDAC2. Consistent with these findings, our present study further validated this regulatory effect (Figure 5A,5B). Importantly, combination treatment with CAY10683 enhanced cisplatin-mediated proliferation inhibition and apoptosis promotion in BC cells (Figure 5C-5E).

Curcumin is a naturally occurring polyphenol, and studies have shown that it exhibits a wide range of physiological and pharmacological properties, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer activities (16). Rutz et al. demonstrated that curcumin can improve the therapeutic efficacy of BC through multiple pathways (17). Moreover, Guo et al. reported that curcumin can upregulate the expression of SMAD7 (18), a finding that was further confirmed in our study (Figure 5F,5G). Notably, combined use of curcumin enhanced the antitumor effect of cisplatin in BC (Figure 5H-5J). These results suggest that YTHDC1 was significantly downregulated in cisplatin-resistant BC cells. The reduced YTHDC1 expression inhibited the degradation of HECW1 mRNA. Upregulated HECW1 promotes proteasome-mediated degradation of SMAD7, thereby conferring cisplatin resistance in BC. Combined use of CAY10683 (which promotes the transcriptional activation of YTHDC1) or curcumin (which upregulates the expression of SMAD7) can effectively restore the sensitivity of BC to cisplatin (Figure 5K).

Discussion

YTHDC1 is a member of the YTH domain-containing protein family and harbors a conserved m6A-binding domain that enables the selective recognition of m6A-modified RNAs (19). Accumulating evidence indicates that YTHDC1 participates in diverse aspects of RNA metabolism, including transcriptional regulation, pre-mRNA splicing, nuclear export, and the regulation of mRNA stability and degradation (20). However, the role of YTHDC1 in tumorigenesis remains controversial. Our previous work demonstrated that YTHDC1 is downregulated in BC and promotes malignant progression by modulating glucose metabolism through the GLUT3-RNF183 axis (13). In contrast, Tan showed that elevated YTHDC1 expression is positively correlated with poor prognosis in breast cancer patients. Mechanistically, YTHDC1 enhances TGF-β signaling by promoting the nuclear export and expression of SMAD3, thereby driving metastasis in triple-negative breast cancer (21). These findings suggest that YTHDC1 exerts context-dependent functions in different types of tumors. In addition to its role in tumor progression, emerging evidence suggests that YTHDC1 is critically involved in therapeutic resistance in BC. Xing et al. reported that reduced YTHDC1 expression suppresses NECTIN4 expression, thereby diminishing sensitivity to EV (22). Moreover, Su demonstrated that YTHDC1 positively regulates PTEN expression and plays a pivotal role in determining responsiveness to cisplatin-based chemotherapy in BC (23). In this study, we further reveal that YTHDC1 downregulation in cisplatin-resistant BC cells promotes cisplatin resistance through the HECW1-SMAD7 axis, thereby providing a novel mechanistic framework for understanding how YTHDC1 governs cisplatin sensitivity in BC.

Ubiquitination is one of the most abundant and evolutionarily conserved post-translational modifications of proteins (24). It governs the degradation, subcellular localization, and functional activation of target proteins, thereby regulating a wide range of fundamental biological processes. Dysregulation of ubiquitination is therefore critically implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of numerous human diseases (25). The process of protein ubiquitination is mediated through a sequential enzymatic cascade involving three distinct components: ubiquitin-activating (E1), ubiquitin-conjugating (E2), and ubiquitin ligase (E3) enzymes. Among these, E3 ubiquitin ligases are responsible for substrate recognition and recruitment, rendering them the key determinants of specificity within the ubiquitination machinery (26). HECW1 is a HECT-type E3 ubiquitin ligase characterized by a catalytic HECT domain and an N-terminal WW domain that recognizes and binds polyproline-rich motifs in substrate proteins (27). Owing to the diversity of its substrates, HECW1 exhibits distinct and sometimes opposing biological functions across different tumor types. Zhang et al. demonstrated that HECW1-mediated ubiquitination of HIPK2 promotes gastric cancer metastasis through activation of the AKT signaling pathway (28). In contrast, Xu et al. showed that HECW1 suppresses cervical cancer cell proliferation by ubiquitinating DVL1 and attenuating Wnt/β-catenin signaling (29). Our study further extended the functional repertoire of HECW1 by demonstrating that HECW1 directly interacts with SMAD7 and promotes its ubiquitination-dependent proteasomal degradation, thereby contributing to cisplatin resistance in BC.

The TGF-β signaling pathway is a highly conserved and intricately regulated signaling cascade that exerts a context-dependent, biphasic role in cancer development. During the early stages of tumorigenesis, TGF-β signaling functions as a tumor suppressor by inducing apoptosis and cell-cycle arrest, thereby restraining cancer cell proliferation. However, as tumors progress, cancer cells frequently evade or hijack TGF-β signaling through adaptive alterations, leading to the activation of tumor-promoting programs, including enhanced invasion, angiogenesis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-driven metastasis (30). Su et al. demonstrated that circRIP2 accelerates BC progression through the miR-1305/ TGF-β2/SMAD3 axis (31). Similarly, Xie et al. reported that ADNP induces cisplatin resistance in BC by promoting TGF-β-mediated EMT (32). These studies collectively highlight a critical role for TGF-β signaling in the development of cisplatin resistance in BC. SMAD7 is a well-established endogenous inhibitor of the TGF-β signaling pathway, acting through multiple mechanisms in both the cytoplasm and nucleus to suppress TGF-β signal transduction (33). Chen et al. reported that Circ_0003266 inhibits BC progression by modulating the miR-503-5p/SMAD7/TGF-β signaling axis (34). In the present study, we demonstrate that HECW1 directly interacts with SMAD7 and promotes its ubiquitination-dependent proteasomal degradation, resulting in reduced SMAD7 expression and subsequent cisplatin resistance in BC. Notably, pharmacological upregulation of SMAD7 using curcumin significantly enhances the antitumor efficacy of cisplatin. Together, these findings establish SMAD7 as a critical downstream effector linking the YTHDC1-HECW1 axis to TGF-β-mediated cisplatin resistance in BC cell models.

Conclusions

Collectively, our results demonstrate that YTHDC1 expression is downregulated in cisplatin-resistant BC. The reduced YTHDC1 expression inhibited the degradation of HECW1 mRNA. Elevated HECW1 promotes ubiquitin-dependent degradation of SMAD7, ultimately reducing the sensitivity of BC to cisplatin treatment. Therefore, we identified a novel YTHDC1-HECW1-SMAD7 axis that regulates cisplatin resistance in BC. Targeting this axis with HDAC2 inhibitors or curcumin may offer therapeutic strategies to enhance the sensitivity of BC cells to cisplatin.
