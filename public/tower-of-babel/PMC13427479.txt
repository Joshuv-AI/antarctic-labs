Neonatal cervical teratoma with critical airway compression: A case report
Authors: Abdishakur Mohamed Abdi, Ismail Mohamoud Abdullahi, Abdullahi Yusuf Ali, Abdisalam Ismail Hassan, Abdirahman Ahmed Omar Alasso, Ismail Gedi Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neonatal cervical teratomas are rare congenital tumors that may cause life-threatening airway compromise despite their predominantly benign histology. We report a male neonate born at 38 weeks of gestation by elective Caesarean section due to a large prenatal neck mass and polyhydramnios. Shortly after birth, the infant developed severe respiratory distress requiring endotracheal intubation following transfer to our institution. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrated a 10 × 12 × 13.5 cm heterogeneous mass centered within the oropharyngeal/oral cavity and submandibular spaces with extension into the anterior cervical region and upper chest wall, containing cystic and solid components, fat, and calcifications, causing marked tracheal compression and displacement without intracranial extension. Preoperative serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) was 14,432.78 ng/mL. Complete surgical excision was performed on day 4 of life through a transverse cervical incision while preserving major cervical structures. Histopathological examination confirmed mature cystic teratoma composed of mature glial tissue, cartilage, adipose tissue and squamous epithelium without immature elements. The patient had a good postoperative recovery and was discharged on the 35th postoperative day. Follow-up evaluation revealed normal serum calcium and parathyroid hormone levels, while thyroid function tests indicated subclinical hypothyroidism needing levothyroxine replacement therapy. At 5 months, there was no recurrence and AFP had decreased to 44.64 ng/mL. This case underscores the importance of early airway stabilization, detailed imaging, complete surgical excision, and multidisciplinary postoperative surveillance in neonatal cervical teratomas.

Introduction

Teratomas are rare congenital tumors derived from pluripotent germ cells and contain tissues originating from all three embryonic germ layers. They occur most commonly in the sacrococcygeal region, while only approximately 5% arise in the head and neck region. Although cervical teratomas are usually histologically benign, they may become life-threatening because of compression of the upper aerodigestive tract, resulting in airway obstruction and respiratory failure immediately after birth [1,3].

Prenatal diagnosis has improved significantly with routine obstetric ultrasonography, allowing identification of fetal neck masses and associated findings such as polyhydramnios caused by impaired fetal swallowing. Early recognition facilitates delivery planning and immediate airway management, which are crucial determinants of survival [2,4]. Radiologic imaging plays a pivotal role in defining lesion extent, tissue composition, airway involvement, and surgical planning.

We report a giant neonatal cervical teratoma causing critical airway compression that was successfully managed with urgent airway stabilization, detailed computed tomography evaluation, and complete surgical excision.

Case presentation

A 2-day-old male neonate was referred to our hospital because of a large congenital neck mass associated with severe respiratory distress. The infant was born at 38 weeks of gestation by elective Caesarean section because the large cervical mass was considered likely to obstruct vaginal delivery. Birth weight was 3.8 kg. Antenatal ultrasonography performed during the third trimester demonstrated polyhydramnios and a large fetal cervical mass.

Immediately after birth, the neonate developed severe respiratory distress. Multiple unsuccessful attempts at endotracheal intubation were performed at the referring hospital due to significant airway distortion from the mass. Following urgent transfer, airway management was undertaken by the pediatric and anesthesia teams jointly, and endotracheal intubation was successfully achieved. The infant was then admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit. Prenatal EXIT (Ex Utero Intrapartum Treatment) planning was not feasible because a definitive prenatal diagnosis had not been established.

Physical examination demonstrated a massive cervical swelling extending from the mandibular region to the supraclavicular area, predominantly involving the midline and left side of the neck. Serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) level was 14,432.78 ng/mL. Chest radiography, abdominal ultrasonography, and echocardiography revealed no associated congenital anomalies.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the neck was performed in the venous phase. Imaging demonstrated a 10 × 12 × 13.5 cm heterogeneous multiloculated mass centered within the oropharyngeal/oral cavity and submandibular spaces, extending inferiorly into the anterior cervical region and suprasternal upper chest wall. The lesion contained multiple cystic areas, enhancing solid components, internal fat attenuation, and scattered punctate calcifications. Marked displacement and severe compression of the trachea and upper aerodigestive tract were present. The carotid sheath structures were displaced but not encased. Postoperative course was uneventful and the infant was discharged at the 35th postoperative day in good clinical conditions.

At follow-up, serum calcium was 8.3 mg/dL and parathyroid hormone level was 16.3 pg/mL, both normal for age. Thyroid function tests revealed an elevated TSH of 24.09 mIU/L (reference range 0.35–5.1 mIU/L) with normal free T4 (0.92 ng/dL) and free T3 (2.68 pg/mL), consistent with subclinical primary hypothyroidism. Levothyroxine replacement therapy was started. Subsequent testing demonstrated improvement, with TSH decreasing to 8.91 mIU/L, free T4 of 1.02 ng/dL, and free T3 of 2.27 pg/mL. At five months after surgery, the infant showed normal growth and development without clinical or imaging evidence of recurrence. Discussion

Congenital cervical teratomas are uncommon tumors accounting for only a small proportion of neonatal teratomas. Despite their generally benign histology, they are clinically significant because of their proximity to the upper airway and major cervical structures [5,6].

Prenatal detection is important because polyhydramnios and a fetal neck mass may suggest the diagnosis. Early recognition allows multidisciplinary delivery planning and airway preparation. In advanced centers, the EXIT procedure may be considered when severe airway obstruction is anticipated. In our setting, definitive prenatal diagnosis was unavailable and airway management was performed after birth.

Radiologic imaging is central to diagnosis and treatment planning. The typical imaging features include a heterogeneous lesion with variable proportions of cystic components, soft tissue, fat, and calcifications. These features help distinguish cervical teratomas from lymphatic malformations and infantile hemangiomas, which typically do not have calcified and fat-containing elements. Contrast-enhanced CT in our patient clearly demonstrated the extent of the lesion, its internal composition, and the severe tracheal compression, thereby facilitating surgical planning [[7], [8]].

Complete surgical excision remains the treatment of choice. Most cervical teratomas are encapsulated and can be removed safely while preserving adjacent vital structures. In the present case, complete excision was achieved without injury to the aerodigestive tract or major vascular structures [9].

Interpretation of AFP levels in neonates requires caution because physiological AFP concentrations are naturally elevated during the first weeks of life and may reach approximately 16,000 ng/mL during the first postnatal week. Therefore, postoperative trends are more informative than a single measurement. In our patient, AFP decreased from 14,432.78 ng/mL preoperatively to 44.64 ng/mL at five months, supporting complete tumor excision and the absence of recurrent disease [10,11].

An important aspect of this case was the inability to identify the thyroid and parathyroid glands during surgery because of distortion by the giant mass. Postoperative endocrine assessment demonstrated preserved parathyroid function but evidence of hypothyroidism requiring thyroid hormone replacement. This finding highlights the importance of endocrine surveillance in patients undergoing resection of large congenital cervical masses.

Airway compromise remains the principal determinant of early morbidity and mortality. Most reported cases are diagnosed prenatally through ultrasonography and fetal MRI, often in association with polyhydramnios. Imaging typically reveals heterogeneous lesions containing cystic, solid, fatty, and calcified components. Complete surgical excision remains the standard treatment and is generally associated with excellent outcomes when airway stabilization can be achieved. Postoperative surveillance using serial AFP measurements and imaging studies is recommended to detect recurrence. Our case shares many of these characteristics but is notable for the exceptionally large size of the tumor, marked tracheal compression, and postoperative endocrine evaluation following inability to identify the thyroid and parathyroid glands intraoperatively.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that neonatal cervical teratomas can cause critical airway compromise requiring urgent multidisciplinary management. Prenatal recognition, prompt airway stabilization, detailed imaging assessment, complete surgical excision, and postoperative endocrine surveillance contributed to a favorable short-term outcome in our patient. Continued long-term follow-up remains necessary to monitor growth, endocrine function, and tumor recurrence.
