Oral GLP‐1RA TERN‐601 for Adults With Obesity/Overweight: Placebo‐Controlled, Multiple‐Ascending‐Dose, Phase 1 and 2 Studies
Authors: W Timothy Garvey, Julio Rosenstock, Harold E Bays, Douglas Denham, Anita Kohli, Melanie Haines, Christopher Jones, Ellen Kwan, Erin Castelloe, Tonya Marmon, Emil Kuriakose, Cara Nelson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and efficacy of TERN‐601, a once‐daily, oral small‐molecule glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonist, were studied in Phase 1 and 2 clinical studies.MethodsThe Phase 1 (28‐day) and Phase 2 (12‐week), randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, multiple‐ascending‐dose studies evaluated the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and efficacy (change in weight, appetite, and gastric emptying) of TERN‐601 for adults with obesity or overweight.ResultsIn the Phase 1 study, TERN‐601 produced dose‐dependent weight loss over 28 days, with statistically significant reductions versus placebo at all doses. Treatment was associated with GLP‐1–consistent pharmacodynamic effects, including reduced appetite and delayed gastric emptying. In the Phase 2 study, TERN‐601 doses ≥ 500 mg resulted in significantly greater mean percentage weight loss at Week 12 versus placebo. Gastrointestinal treatment‐emergent adverse events were consistent with the GLP‐1 receptor agonist class and dose‐related. Three participants in the Phase 2 study experienced transaminase elevations consistent with potential drug‐induced liver injury.ConclusionsTERN‐601 demonstrated modest, dose‐dependent weight loss with a gastrointestinal tolerability profile consistent with GLP‐1 receptor agonists. However, liver safety findings observed in the Phase 2 study led to clinical development discontinuation. These data contribute to the overall understanding of oral small‐molecule GLP‐1 receptor agonists in obesity.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06854952Keywords: GLP‐1 receptor agonist, obesity, oral GLP‐1 agonist, randomized controlled trial, TERN‐601

Introduction

Pharmacologic treatments for obesity have advanced to more effective glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonists [1]. GLP‐1 receptor agonists have transformed treatment for people living with obesity, emerging as the most effective pharmacologic intervention because of robust clinical trial efficacy data, favorable effects on metabolism, and sustained weight loss through centrally mediated appetite suppression, enhanced satiety, and delayed gastric emptying [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. The four GLP‐1–based agents currently approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for long‐term weight management for adults living with obesity or overweight with comorbidities (i.e., liraglutide, semaglutide [injectable and oral versions], tirzepatide [a dual‐action glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide/GLP‐1 receptor co‐agonist], and orforglipron [a once‐daily, oral small‐molecule (non‐peptide) GLP‐1 receptor agonist]) have demonstrated benefits for obesity complications and related diseases, including type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular events and heart failure, steatotic liver disease, obstructive sleep apnea, and knee osteoarthritis [5, 6, 7].

These treatments have established GLP‐1 receptor signaling as a validated therapeutic pathway for long‐term weight management [3, 6]. Semaglutide and tirzepatide have produced degrees of weight loss in clinical trials (≥ 15%) that can effectively prevent or treat a broad array of obesity‐related complications and diseases, and for this reason, they have been termed second‐generation obesity medications [5, 8]. Despite these benefits, three of the four approved treatments are peptide‐based therapies requiring fasting and high daily dosages with oral administration because of low oral bioavailability [9, 10, 11] or necessitating subcutaneous administration because of several physiologic and biochemical factors that hinder the absorption of peptide therapeutics in the gastrointestinal tract [6, 12, 13, 14]. Subcutaneous administration may limit patient acceptance and long‐term adherence for some patients who are disinclined toward injections given the need for chronic obesity pharmacotherapy as well as limited accessibility and high costs associated with production of peptides and cold‐chain storage [6, 13, 15, 16, 17].

The limitations associated with peptide GLP‐1 receptor agonists have heightened interest in the development of orally administered, nonpeptide GLP‐1 receptor agonists, which aim to preserve the established efficacy of GLP‐1 receptor activation while offering potential advantages in convenience, manufacturing, access, and tolerability [5, 17]. Two oral GLP‐1 receptor agonists have been approved by the FDA for the treatment of obesity: oral semaglutide in December 2025 [18] and orforglipron in April 2026 [19]. Phase 2 and Phase 3 clinical trials for adults living with obesity demonstrated a > 10% weight loss within 36 weeks and significantly greater reductions in body weight with orforglipron compared with placebo [7, 19, 20, 21]. The adverse event (AE) profile is consistent with that of other GLP‐1 receptor agonists, with the most common AEs with orforglipron being gastrointestinal and mostly mild to moderate (as reported in approximately 44% to 70% of participants across studies) [7, 19, 20, 21].

Lotiglipron and danuglipron, two oral small‐molecule GLP‐1 receptor agonists with a similar core scaffold/pharmacophore, were discontinued as a result of liver toxicity findings in clinical studies [5]. Pharmacokinetic (PK) data and laboratory observations of elevated transaminases in Phase 1 and Phase 2 clinical studies contributed to the discontinuation of the clinical development of lotiglipron [22, 23]. Danuglipron demonstrated efficacy in reducing hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), fasting blood glucose, and body weight (least‐squares mean [LSM] percentage change from baseline ranging from −5% to −12.9% in a Phase 2 study for adults living with obesity without diabetes) [24, 25, 26]. However, its twice‐daily dosing was associated with significant gastrointestinal AEs (reported for 77.4% of participants) as well as potential drug‐induced liver injury (DILI) [25, 26, 27]. Therefore, development of danuglipron was also discontinued [28].

TERN‐601 is an orally bioavailable small‐molecule agonist of the human GLP‐1 receptor that has a similar core scaffold/pharmacophore to danuglipron and lotiglipron, with notable differences in pharmacologic properties, specifically lower solubility and higher plasma protein binding. TERN‐601 was in development for long‐term weight management for adults [29], with in vitro studies demonstrating potent and selective activation of human GLP‐1 receptors, minimal activity against closely related G protein‐coupled receptors or a broad panel of targets of potential safety concern, and limited activity at nonprimate GLP‐1 receptors [29, 30, 31]. In transgenic C57BL/6J mice expressing human GLP‐1 receptors, single oral doses of TERN‐601 led to dose‐dependent suppression of ad libitum food intake, slowed gastric emptying, lowered blood glucose, and induced weight loss with associated improvements in body composition in diet‐induced obesity models [29, 30]. In cynomolgus monkeys, intravenous administration of TERN‐601 increased glucose disposal and insulin secretion [32]. Together, these nonclinical data supported the use of TERN‐601 for long‐term weight management for humans [29, 30, 31, 32, 33].

In a Phase 1 first‐in‐human study, the single‐ascending‐dose (SAD) portion of the study demonstrated TERN‐601 exposures increased approximately linearly with dose escalation from 30 mg to 1000 mg [29, 32]. At doses greater than 100 mg, the PK profile indicated prolonged absorption of TERN‐601, resulting in sustained target coverage during 16 to 24 h despite a terminal half‐life (t½) of 3 to 5 h. Overall, the PK data supported once‐daily (QD) dosing of TERN‐601. Participant demographics and baseline clinical characteristics, safety and tolerability data, and PK results for the SAD portion of the Phase 1 study are provided in Tables S1 and S2 and Figure S1. In addition, 14‐day multiple‐ascending‐dose (MAD) nontitration cohorts were conducted at doses of 30 mg and 100 mg QD to identify a well‐tolerated starting dose of TERN‐601 for future studies.

A Phase 1, 28‐day MAD study and a Phase 2, 12‐week dose‐titration study (FALCON) were conducted for adults living with obesity or overweight as part of the clinical development program for TERN‐601. We report data addressing the efficacy, safety, tolerability, PK, and pharmacodynamic (PD) characteristics of TERN‐601 from both studies.

Methods

A Phase 1, randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial was conducted to assess the safety, tolerability, PK, and PD of TERN‐601 for otherwise healthy adults living with obesity or overweight. The trial consisted of two parts: Part 1 SAD and Part 2a 14‐day MAD and Part 2b 28‐day MAD. The 28‐day titration MAD part of the Phase 1 study and a Phase 2, 12‐week dose‐titration study, both described in this manuscript, were conducted to assess the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of several doses and dosage‐titration schedules, progressing from TERN‐601 treatment of 4‐week duration in an inpatient setting to a 12‐week duration in an outpatient setting.

These studies were conducted in compliance with the ethical principles originating in or derived from the Declaration of Helsinki and in compliance with all International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidance. The protocols were approved by a central institutional review board (Advarra, Columbia, MD, USA). All participants provided written informed consent. A list of clinical study sites and principal investigators is provided in Table S3.

Study Design

The Phase 1 first‐in‐human inpatient study (TERN601‐1013) was followed by a Phase 2 outpatient, multicenter, dose‐ranging, randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled study of TERN‐601 for adults living with obesity or overweight without diabetes (TERN601‐2003; NCT06854952). In the 28‐day titration cohorts of the Phase 1 MAD study, participants were randomized in a 3:1 ratio (TERN‐601:placebo) across three cohorts with different target doses and dosage‐titration schedules (Figure 1A). Phase 1 participants were confined to the clinical research unit from Day −2 until the completion of assessments on Day 30. In the Phase 2 study, participants were randomly assigned to one of four TERN‐601 dose/titration schedule arms or placebo in a 1:1:1:1:1 manner. Randomization was stratified by body mass index (BMI [≤ 35, > 35 kg/m2]) and sex. Participants received treatment for 12 weeks, including the titration period, with a 4‐week, post‐treatment follow up (Figure 1B).

Dosage Titration

In both studies, dose‐titration approaches were used, with TERN‐601 up‐titrated to each cohort's target dose over time (Figure 2). In the Phase 1, 28‐day MAD cohorts, TERN‐601 was titrated from a starting dosage of 100 mg QD to maintenance dosages of 240 mg, 500 mg, or 740 mg QD. The maintenance dosages were reached on Day 9 for the 500‐mg cohort, Day 12 for the 740‐mg cohort, and Day 15 for the 240‐mg cohort. All treatments were administered to participants in a fasting state. In the Phase 2 study, TERN‐601 was titrated from a starting dosage of 100 mg QD to maintenance dosages of 250 mg (achieved at Week 3), 500 mg (achieved at Week 6 for the 500‐mg arm or Week 9 for the 500‐mg slow titration [ST] arm), or 750 mg QD (achieved at Week 9). All treatments were administered to participants without regard to food. In both studies, supportive therapies, dosage interruptions, and/or dosage reductions were allowed for the management of AEs.

Participants

The main criteria for inclusion were similar for both studies: adults, aged ≥ 18 to ≤ 65 (Phase 1) or ≥ 18 to ≤ 75 years (Phase 2), with HbA1c < 6.5% at screening, BMI ≥ 27 to < 40 kg/m2 (Phase 1) or ≥ 27 to < 50 kg/m2 (Phase 2), and stable body weight by history for ≥ 3 months prior to screening and randomization. Phase 2 participants with BMI ≥ 27 to < 30 kg/m2 were also required to have at least one of the following weight‐related comorbidities: hypertension, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disease, or obstructive sleep apnea (obstructive sleep apnea only applicable to participants > 30 years of age). Participants were excluded if they had obesity induced by medication or other diagnosed endocrinologic disorders, had been diagnosed with or had laboratory evidence of diabetes, had or were planning surgical or device‐based therapy for obesity, or had a history of suicidal ideation or attempt. Potential participants with clinically significant liver laboratory abnormalities at baseline (i.e., alanine aminotransferase [ALT] or aspartate aminotransferase [AST] > 3.0× upper limit of normal [ULN], alkaline phosphatase [ALP] > 1.5× ULN, total bilirubin [TBL] > 1.5× ULN [except known Gilbert's syndrome], or TBL 1–1.5× ULN and direct bilirubin > ULN) were excluded from study participation. A full list of exclusion criteria can be found in Table S4.

In the Phase 1 study, all participants must have abstained from taking prescription and over‐the‐counter medications, herbal products, or dietary supplements, unless the product was for short‐term use and, in the opinion of the investigator and sponsor, would not interfere with the study. In the Phase 2 study, medications to treat long‐term conditions were allowed, provided they had been at stable dosages for at least 3 months and were not restricted because of the potential to either cause weight gain/loss or a drug–drug interaction that could impact the PK of TERN‐601.

Endpoints and Assessments

The primary objective of the 28‐day MAD cohorts of the Phase 1 study was to assess safety and tolerability of TERN‐601 (treatment‐emergent AEs [TEAEs]) following multiple‐dose administration with exploratory endpoints to assess the PD effects of TERN‐601, including change in body weight, appetite score, and gastric emptying. The primary endpoint of the Phase 2 study was percentage change in body weight from baseline at Week 12. Secondary efficacy endpoints of the Phase 2 study included change in body weight from baseline at Week 12 and percentage of participants achieving a ≥ 5% weight loss. Both studies assessed the steady‐state PK of TERN‐601 in plasma, including area under the time‐concentration curve (AUC) and maximum observed concentration (C max).

Appetite Questionnaire

In the Phase 1 study, the effect of TERN‐601 on appetite was assessed using a visual analog scale (VAS) (0–100 mm) at multiple time points during a 4‐h period at baseline and on Day 27 of treatment [34]. VAS appetite questionnaires included questions assessing strength of feelings of hunger, fullness, and desire to eat from “not (much) at all” to “extremely/an extreme amount” [34]. Participants were given a standardized mixed meal (Ensure Plus) at 2 h post dose (or planned dosing time on Day −1), which was consumed entirely within 10 min. The appetite questionnaire was administered 30 min prior to the mixed meal, within 30 min after the mixed meal (0‐h time point), and at multiple time points up to 4 h after consumption of the mixed meal.

Gastric Emptying

In the Phase 1 study, the effect of TERN‐601 on gastric emptying was assessed at baseline and Day 27 using plasma concentrations of acetaminophen (APAP) as a marker [35, 36]. For this assessment, 1.5 g of APAP as a liquid solution was administered orally 2 h after dosing (or planned dosing time on Day −1) and within 5 min prior to the mixed meal (Ensure Plus). Plasma samples to measure APAP were collected before the meal (prior to start of APAP administration) and 15 min after the start of the meal up to 5 h after APAP administration. Plasma concentrations of APAP were determined using a validated bioanalytical assay (ICON Bioanalytical Laboratories, Assen, The Netherlands).

Safety and Tolerability

Safety and tolerability were assessed, including incidence and severity of TEAEs and of abnormal clinical and laboratory findings. The protocol provided specific guidance on the grading of the severity of gastrointestinal AEs, summarized in Table S5. The criteria for increased monitoring of liver function were defined as ALT or AST values > 3× ULN if baseline was normal or > 2× baseline if baseline was > ULN, TBL > 2× ULN if baseline was normal or > 1.5× baseline if baseline was > ULN, and ALP > 2× ULN if baseline was normal or > 1.5× baseline if baseline was > ULN, consistent with FDA guidance [37]. The protocol‐provided specific guidance for the increased monitoring of liver function for participants meeting the criteria is described in Table S6.

Statistical Methods

Statistical analyses tested whether mean percentage change, mean change from baseline (kg) in body weight, and percentage of weight loss responders (decrease of ≥ 5% in the Phase 2 study) were different between placebo and each TERN‐601 dose. The baseline value for statistical analyses of quantitative laboratory parameters was defined as the mean of all available evaluations, including any unscheduled or repeat assessments, prior to first administration of study drug. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4. Statistical tests comparing groups were generally conducted using either analysis of variance (ANOVA [percentage change]) or analysis of covariance (ANCOVA [change]) model, including treatment group and randomization strata (sex [male or female] and BMI [≤ 35 kg/m2 or > 35 kg/m2]) as fixed effects and percentage change or change from baseline as the dependent variable. LSM, LSM difference, associated standard errors (SE) and 90% confidence intervals (CI), and p value using a two‐sided α = 0.1 were used. Efficacy analyses were conducted on the intention‐to‐treat analysis set (i.e., all randomized participants receiving any study drug) using an available case analysis approach and evaluating only available data with no imputation for missing data. Statistical methods for the Phase 1 and 2 studies are detailed in Table S7.

Medical history and AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA). Overall summaries of TEAEs are presented by study, dosage, and severity.

Results

The Phase 1 clinical trial was conducted between October 5, 2023, and July 10, 2024, at a single inpatient study center in the United States. The Phase 2 clinical trial was conducted between March 7, 2025, and September 19, 2025, at 17 outpatient research centers in the United States (Table S3).

Participants

Disposition

In the 28‐day, Phase 1 MAD cohorts, 37 participants were enrolled and received at least one dose of study drug (28 participants received TERN‐601 [10 in the 240‐mg dose group, 9 each in the 500‐mg and 740‐mg dose groups]), and 9 participants received placebo (Figure 3A). One participant in the 240‐mg dose group discontinued as a result of a TEAE considered unrelated to study drug. This participant was replaced (as allowed per protocol) at the same treatment assignment. In the Phase 2 study, 167 participants were randomized (TERN‐601, n = 134 in one of four titration schedules; placebo, n = 33), and 129 (77.2%) completed treatment (Figure 3B). A total of 38 participants discontinued study treatment early (TERN‐601, n = 33/134 [24.6%]; placebo, n = 5/33 [15.2%]).

Demographics and Baseline Clinical Characteristics

Demographics and baseline clinical characteristics were well balanced between treatment groups within the respective studies (Table 1). In the 28‐day, Phase 1 MAD cohorts, most participants were male (78.6% overall TERN‐601; 77.8% placebo). Mean (standard deviation, SD) baseline BMI was 30.6 (2.3) kg/m2 in the overall TERN‐601 group and 29.7 (1.6) kg/m2 in the placebo group. In contrast, in the Phase 2 study, most participants were female (79.0% overall TERN‐601; 78.8% placebo). Mean (SD) baseline BMI was 36.3 (4.8) kg/m2 in the overall TERN‐601 group and 36.2 (4.8) kg/m2 in the placebo group.

Weight Loss

All three Phase 1, 28‐day MAD TERN‐601 groups exhibited steady weight loss during the treatment period, whereas body weight in the placebo group remained relatively stable. Statistically significant differences from placebo were observed as early as Day 8 in the TERN‐601 500‐mg and 740‐mg groups and Day 22 in the 240‐mg group. These differences were maintained through Day 29. On Day 29, the LSM (SE [90% CI]) changes from baseline in body weight were −2.5 kg (0.7 [−3.8, −1.2]), −4.0 kg (0.7 [−5.3, −2.7]), and −5.1 kg (0.7 [−6.3, −3.8]) in the TERN‐601 240‐mg, 500‐mg, and 740‐mg groups, respectively, and −0.7 kg (0.7 [−1.9, 0.6]) in the placebo group (Figure 4A). The LSM (SE [90% CI]) percent changes from baseline on Day 29 were −2.5% (0.8% [−3.7%, −1.2%]), −4.4% (0.8% [−5.7%, −3.1%]), and −5.5% (0.8% [−6.8%, −4.3%]) in the TERN‐601 240‐mg, 500‐mg, and 740‐mg groups, respectively, and −0.6% (0.8% [−1.9%, −0.7%]) in the placebo group, resulting in placebo‐adjusted LSM (SE) differences of −1.9% (1.1% [p < 0.1]), −3.8% (1.1% [p < 0.01]), and −4.9% (1.1% [p < 0.0001]), respectively.

In the Phase 2 study, participants in the TERN‐601 500‐mg cohorts had the greatest percentage of participants achieving ≥ 5% weight loss at Week 12. In the 750‐mg group, 25.0% (p = 0.4019) of participants achieved this response compared with 46.4% (p = 0.0104) of participants in the 500‐mg cohort, 39.3% (p = 0.0700) in the 500‐mg ST cohort, and 14.8% (p = 0.9054) in the 250‐mg cohort, compared with 15.4% in the placebo group.

Appetite Questionnaire

On Day 27 of treatment in the 28‐day MAD cohorts of the Phase 1 study, there was a dose‐dependent decrease from baseline in the hunger and appetite AUC from time 0 to 4 h (AUC0–4 h) and a corresponding dose‐dependent increase in fullness, satiety, and composite overall appetite score AUC0–4 h (Figure S2).

Gastric Emptying

A delay in gastric emptying (not observed in the placebo group) was observed on Day 27 in all three TERN‐601 treatment groups within the 28‐day MAD cohorts of the Phase 1 study. There did not appear to be a dose‐dependent response on gastric emptying, as all three TERN‐601 doses had similar values for APAP AUC0–3 h on Day 27, all of which were lower than baseline (Day −1) values (Figure S3).

Safety and Tolerability

Mean Duration of Treatment

In the Phase 1, 28‐day MAD cohorts, the mean durations of treatment were 25.9 days in the TERN‐601 240‐mg dose group and 28.0 days in the remaining groups. No participant in any dose group had a dose reduction or dose interruption.

In the Phase 2 study, mean durations of treatment were 10.7 weeks for participants who received TERN‐601 and 11.2 weeks for participants who received placebo. Treatment was permanently discontinued prior to completion of planned treatment for 33 participants (24.6%) who received TERN‐601 and 5 participants (15.2%) who received placebo. The numbers of participants who permanently discontinued treatment were similar across all TERN‐601 dosages. Dosage interruptions occurred for 12 participants (9.0%) who received TERN‐601 and 1 participant (3.0%) who received placebo. Single missed doses occurred for 33 participants (24.6%) who received TERN‐601 and 8 participants (24.2%) who received placebo. Dosage modifications were requested by the investigator for 12 participants (9.0%) who received TERN‐601 and no participants who received placebo. Modifications included dosage reduction by one dose level for eight participants, return to Week 2 dosage for three participants, and remaining on current dosage (no titration) for one participant.

Treatment‐Emergent Adverse Events

In the Phase 1, 28‐day MAD cohorts, TEAEs occurred for 60.0% of participants in the TERN‐601 240‐mg group, 100% of participants in the TERN‐601 500‐mg and 740‐mg groups, and 55.6% of participants in the placebo group (Table 2). There were no treatment‐related TEAEs leading to study drug interruption, reduction, or discontinuation in the 28‐day MAD cohorts, and there were no serious AEs (SAEs).

Gastrointestinal TEAEs were the most common TEAEs in both studies (Table 2). In the Phase 1, 28‐day MAD cohorts, the overall incidence and severity of gastrointestinal TEAEs increased with increasing dose of TERN‐601 (Table 3; Figure S4A), with increases in gastrointestinal TEAEs in the 500‐mg and 740‐mg groups appearing to be closely associated with the more rapid titration scheme. Many of the events were of short duration and/or intermittent, with initial onset during the dose‐titration period. The overall incidence of nausea and vomiting increased with dose. No gastrointestinal TEAEs were graded as severe or considered to be SAEs in either study. In all except one participant, gastrointestinal TEAEs were considered to be related to study treatment. There did not appear to be an abatement of nausea or vomiting rates over time and there was no obvious difference in the rates of nausea or vomiting over time between the 500‐mg ST group, which titrated every 4 weeks, and the 500‐mg group, which titrated every 2 to 3 weeks (Figure S4B).

Hepatobiliary Safety

Hepatic and Biliary Laboratory Assessments

In both the Phase 1, 28‐day MAD and Phase 2 studies, serum chemistry values and changes from baseline over time remained stable for most participants (Figure 5). Increased Monitoring of Liver Function

No participants in the 28‐day MAD cohorts met the criteria for increased monitoring of liver function. In the Phase 2 study, criteria for increased monitoring of liver function were met in 9.1%, 2.9%, 5.9%, and 9.1% of participants who received TERN‐601 250 mg, 500 mg ST, 500 mg, and 750 mg, respectively, compared with no participants who received placebo. Of the nine participants who met the criteria for increased monitoring of liver function, five met the criteria based on TBL alone without a corresponding increase in ALT or AST. One participant (Participant 4 in Figure 6) in the 500‐mg ST group had rising TBL and AST as well as elevated creatinine kinase (CK > 10× ULN) on Day 1 before first dose administration (attributed to an adverse reaction to a varicella zoster vaccine); CK continued to rise at post‐baseline. This participant was subsequently discontinued at the investigator's discretion after 5 days of dosing.

Hepatotoxicity

There was no evidence of hepatotoxicity in the Phase 1, 28‐day MAD study. In the 12‐week, Phase 2 study, three participants (Participants 1, 2, and 3 in Figure 6) experienced increases in ALT and/or AST starting at Week 8 or later, with peak values occurring several weeks after the last dose of TERN‐601, and one of which was reported as an SAE of DILI. All transaminase and bilirubin increases resolved without treatment. All three cases were reviewed by an independent DILI expert from the DILI Network [39]. Two of the cases had profiles consistent with DILI, one of which was supported by findings on liver biopsy. The third participant had a possible alternate etiology of gallstones, although DILI could not be definitively ruled out. Figure S5 and Table S9 provide details on the three cases of transaminase elevations/DILI. Note that two participants who experienced AEs of increased transaminases and/or DILI participated in an optional PK substudy (Figure S6), and both had TERN‐601 exposures (C max and area under the plasma concentration‐time curve over the dosing interval [AUC tau]) similar to mean exposures.

Discussion

TERN‐601 produced dose‐dependent weight loss over 28 days in the Phase 1 MAD study, with statistically significant reductions in all TERN‐601 treatment groups versus placebo (up to −5.5% in the 740‐mg group [−4.9% placebo‐adjusted]). In the Phase 2 study, significantly greater mean percent weight loss was observed at Week 12 at doses ≥ 500 mg versus placebo (up to −5.0% in the 500‐mg group [−4.6% placebo‐adjusted]), with weight loss observed for the 750‐mg dose cohort being nominally less than both the 500‐mg and 500‐mg ST dose cohorts. Treatment was associated with PD effects consistent with GLP‐1 receptor agonists, including reduced appetite and delayed gastric emptying. Gastrointestinal TEAEs were common and appeared to be dose‐related.

The LSM weight loss demonstrated in these Phase 1 and 2 TERN‐601 studies reflected data from other short‐term, early clinical trials of oral, nonpeptide GLP‐1 receptor agonists. In a Phase 2a trial of aleniglipron, a significant reduction in body weight was observed over 8 weeks compared with placebo (−4.7%) in participants living with obesity [40]. In a Phase 2a trial of RGT‐075, a −5% placebo‐adjusted weight loss was observed at 12 weeks for adults living with obesity [41]. In a Phase 2 trial of orforglipron, mean change from baseline in body weight ranged from −8.6% to −12.6% at 26 weeks, followed by a mean change from baseline in body weight ranging from −5.1% to −9.6% at 72 weeks in a Phase 3 study [21, 40]. In addition, longer‐duration Phase 3 data have demonstrated further incremental weight loss with treatment beyond 1 year with orforglipron [20, 21]. Taken together, these data suggest that the early weight loss effect of TERN‐601 was generally consistent with the efficacy of established oral, nonpeptide GLP‐1 receptor agonists, albeit on the lower end of the magnitude of weight loss expected at the beginning of the Phase 2 trial.

Although clinically meaningful weight loss has been observed with GLP‐1 receptor agonists for both sexes, recent research demonstrates greater weight loss for women compared with men, especially for the treatment of obesity [42, 43]. This difference has been observed at both lesser and greater dosages of GLP‐1 receptor agonists, with studies resulting in substantial weight reduction (i.e., > 5% of baseline body weight) demonstrating an even greater difference in weight loss between sexes [42]. In the Phase 2 TERN‐601 study, most participants were female (79.0% overall TERN‐601; 78.8% placebo). In contrast, mostly male participants were enrolled in the 28‐day, Phase 1 MAD cohorts (78.6% overall TERN‐601; 77.8% placebo). Based on the recent research yielding differences between the sexes with GLP‐1 receptor agonists [42, 43], the populations for the TERN‐601 studies not being evenly distributed between male and female participants may have biased the weight loss efficacy toward greater weight loss in the Phase 2 study and toward lesser weight loss in the Phase 1 MAD study.

Gastrointestinal TEAEs were most frequently reported with TERN‐601, with greater incidence compared with placebo. Nausea was the most commonly reported TEAE, and the majority of TEAEs were mild to moderate. Although all titration schedules in the Phase 2 study were slower than the Phase 1 study, overall dose reduction and discontinuation rates due to TEAEs were greater in the Phase 2 study, with 12% of participants randomized to TERN‐601 discontinuing treatment early because of (primarily gastrointestinal) TEAEs. These discontinuation rates are similar to those observed in clinical trials of other GLP‐1 receptor agonists studied for obesity [44, 45, 46]. The two cohorts with the same target dosage of 500 mg QD but different titration speeds demonstrated similar safety and tolerability, indicating that an even slower, every‐4‐week titration schedule did not improve tolerability.

Unexpectedly, three participants had AST and/or ALT elevations starting at Week 8 or later, with peak elevations occurring several weeks after last dose. All three cases were reviewed by an independent expert from the DILI network [39], and no obvious differentiating or contributing factors were noted. Two of these cases were deemed consistent with potential DILI, and DILI could not be definitively ruled out in the third case. Two participants in the Phase 2 study who experienced AEs of increased transaminases and/or DILI both had TERN‐601 plasma exposures (C max and AUC tau) similar to mean exposures. Neither the available nonclinical data nor the Phase 1 data for TERN‐601 predicted the liver toxicity observed in the Phase 2 study. In fact, the Phase 1 study demonstrated a downward trend in transaminases in the 28‐day MAD cohorts.

Interest in GLP‐1 receptor agonist medications has been unprecedented, with an estimated 700% increase in GLP‐1–based prescriptions from 2019 to 2023 for people without diabetes [47]. As of May 2025, an estimated 12% of all Americans were receiving GLP‐1–based therapies [48]. The increased use of GLP‐1 receptor agonists has led to heightened interest in the development of orally administered, nonpeptide GLP‐1 receptor agonists [5, 17]. TERN‐601 is the third oral small‐molecule GLP‐1 receptor agonist with liver toxicity findings reported during or after Phase 2 studies [23, 28], following danuglipron and lotiglipron, which share a similar core scaffold/pharmacophore with TERN‐601. Laboratory observations of elevated transaminases were demonstrated in lotiglipron clinical studies [22, 23], and at least one participant on danuglipron experienced potential DILI [28]. As there are multiple, highly effective GLP‐1 pharmacotherapies without evidence of liver toxicity that are approved and widely used for the treatment of obesity [40, 45, 49], it is likely that the observed hepatotoxicity with TERN‐601 and other small‐molecule GLP‐1 receptor agonists is an off‐target effect. At the conclusion of the Phase 2 study, the TERN‐601 clinical development program was discontinued based primarily on the findings of liver toxicity in three participants [33]. The overall benefit–risk profile did not support continued development of TERN‐601 for the treatment of obesity.

The Phase 1 MAD and Phase 2 studies had similar methodologies but differed in some respects, including treatment setting (in‐clinic versus free‐living), duration, dosage‐titration schedules, and efficacy measures. These studies had limitations, including their short duration and rapid dosage titration, which differ from real‐world clinical practice. The Phase 1 study was also limited by its small sample size, and the Phase 2 study was not powered for direct comparisons between active dosage groups. Early treatment discontinuations, primarily because of gastrointestinal AEs, may have negatively impacted observed efficacy estimates. In addition, the short exposure duration and limited cumulative safety experience constrain mechanistic interpretation of the observed liver enzyme elevations.

Conclusion

Once‐daily administration of multiple doses of the oral small‐molecule GLP‐1 receptor agonist TERN‐601 in Phase 1 and 2 studies demonstrated modest, dose‐dependent weight loss along with a mechanism of action and safety profile largely consistent with other GLP‐1 receptor agonists. However, three participants in the Phase 2 study experienced a similar pattern of transaminase elevations. Two cases were consistent with DILI, and DILI could not be excluded in the third. These liver safety findings led to clinical development discontinuation for TERN‐601. It will be important to consider structural elements common to TERN‐601, danuglipron, and lotiglipron that could contribute to hepatotoxicity in future drug development. Nevertheless, with careful monitoring, well‐tolerated, oral small‐molecule GLP‐1 receptor agonists remain essential to address limitations of peptide‐based therapies.
