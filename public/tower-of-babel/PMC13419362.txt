Analysis of predictive factors and development of a nomogram for complicated acute appendicitis in adults
Authors: Jin Yu, Shixing Li, Yulin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe number of patients receiving conservative treatment for acute appendicitis is currently increasing. However, for complicated acute appendicitis (CAA), surgical treatment yields more definitive outcomes, and delayed surgery increases the risk of complications. Therefore, this study aimed to analyze the predictive factors for CAA and establish a nomogram.MethodsPatients who underwent appendectomy between January 2023 and October 2025 were included. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to identify predictive factors and independent predictive factors, respectively. A nomogram prediction model was constructed to visualize these factors. The performance of the prediction model was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, calibration curve, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsThe incidence of CAA was 25.24%. Analysis revealed six factors as independent predictors associated with CAA in acute appendicitis: age [OR 3.697 (95% CI 1.774–7.704)], body temperature [OR 3.235 (95% CI 1.367–7.656)], neutrophil percentage [OR 2.317 (95% CI 1.123–4.782)], C-reactive protein (CRP) [OR 12.434 (95% CI 5.525–27.982)], appendix diameter [OR 30.731 (95% CI 15.189–62.177)], and appendiceal fecalith [OR 6.968 (95% CI 3.544–13.698)]. A risk prediction model was developed on the basis of these factors and presented as a nomogram. The area under the ROC curve was 0.959, with a sensitivity of 87.3% and specificity of 90.3%. The calibration curve showed a mean absolute error of 0.014, and the DCA indicated favorable clinical net benefit.ConclusionAge, body temperature, neutrophil percentage, CRP level, appendix diameter, and the presence of an appendiceal fecalith are predictive factors for CAA. The established nomogram can effectively predict the risk of CAA.Keywords: Complicated acute appendicitis, Appendectomy, Nomogram, Prediction

Background

The treatment of acute appendicitis can be divided into surgical treatment and nonsurgical treatment, with nonsurgical treatment consisting of antibiotic therapy. During the COVID-19 pandemic, antibiotic treatment for acute appendicitis was incorporated into general surgery guidelines, and the number of patients treated with antibiotics for appendicitis increased [1, 2]. According to the pathological type, appendicitis can be classified into simple appendicitis and complicated appendicitis. Uncomplicated appendicitis is associated with mild infection and few complications and can be treated with antibiotics. In contrast, surgery is the main treatment for complicated appendicitis. Early diagnosis and timely management are key to reducing the incidence of complications and the length of hospital stay [3, 4]. Complicated acute appendicitis (CAA) is a subtype characterized by rapid disease progression, requiring prompt emergency surgical intervention [5–7]. Delayed surgical treatment increases the risk of complications, including intra-abdominal abscesses and appendiceal perforation [8]. Accurate discrimination between uncomplicated acute appendicitis (UAA) and CAA is crucial for guiding clinical treatment planning.

At present, numerous studies have analyzed CAA. The white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, C-reactive protein (CRP) level, D-dimer level, duration of symptoms, appendiceal diameter, and presence of an appendicolith have been identified as independent predictive factors of CAA [9–12]. But our extensive research shows that some controversies still remain.A study by Jia-hui Cai et al. revealed that there was no statistically significant association between white blood cell (WBC) count and CAA, and multivariate analysis further revealed that the neutrophil percentage was not significantly associated with CAA. In contrast, a study by Min et al. [13] reported that although the WBC count was not statistically significant in multivariate analysis, the neutrophil percentage was significantly associated with CAA. Controversy also exists over symptom duration. While et al. [11] and Tianlei Xu et al. [14] found no statistically significant difference between symptom duration and complicated appendicitis, Badhaasaa et al. [15] and Yosuke et al. [16] reported a statistically significant difference.

In this study, we investigated the factors associated with CAA by incorporating readily available clinical and imaging features and evaluated the diagnostic value of each factor for CAA. Furthermore, a nomogram was ultimately constructed to predict the risk of CAA.

Methods

General data

This retrospective observational study retrieved data from the electronic medical records of patients who underwent appendectomy between January 2023 and October 2025. Surgical and pathological findings served as the definitive diagnostic criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathologically confirmed acute appendicitis and (2) preoperative diagnosis on the basis of abdominal computed tomography (CT). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) concomitant appendiceal conditions, such as tumors, inflammatory bowel disease, or intestinal perforation; (2) appendiceal abscess; and (3) incomplete clinical data.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongxiang People’s Hospital (KY2025033) and strictly adheres to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived because of its retrospective nature. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this study.

CAA was defined as appendiceal necrosis, perforation, or abscess formation. Patient information, including sex, age, duration of symptoms, body mass index (BMI), history of hypertension, history of diabetes mellitus, preoperative WBC count, preoperative neutrophil percentage, preoperative C-reactive protein (CRP) level, preoperative blood glucose level, preoperative D-dimer level, surgical findings, and pathological results, was collected. For cases in which the surgical and pathological diagnoses were inconsistent, a consensus was reached through expert consultation.

Computed tomography (CT) was used for radiological assessment. The imaging variables included the presence of an appendicolith and the appendiceal diameter, with the maximum appendiceal diameter selected for measurement. Two radiologists with 5–6 years of experience, who were blinded to the clinical information, independently reviewed the radiological images to evaluate the imaging features of acute appendicitis. Any discrepancies between the two radiologists were resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

By organizing the relevant data of the research subjects, statistical analysis was performed via SPSS 27.0 and R 4.4.2. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages) (n(%)) and were analyzed via the chi-square test, with Fisher’s exact test applied when necessary. For continuous variables, the t test was used for normally distributed data, whereas the rank-sum test was applied for nonnormally distributed data. For variables with statistical significance, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to determine the cutoff values of the independent variables, and the sensitivity and specificity were calculated. The meaningful independent variables were subsequently selected for multivariate logistic regression analysis. The nomogram was formulated based on the results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis with the rms package in R. The score for each variable in the nomogram is derived by proportionally and linearly scaling the logistic regression coefficient to a 0–100 scale, and the sum of the corresponding scores for each variable corresponds to the final predicted probability (transformed via the plogis function).The performance of the nomogram was measured by the area under the ROC curve (AUC) and the calibration curve. To evaluate the sampling variability and stability of the AUC, the Bootstrap resampling method (1000 resamples with replacement) was used to construct 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Specifically, Bootstrap samples of the same size as the original dataset were randomly drawn from the original data, the model was fitted, and the AUC was calculated. This process was repeated 1000 times, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles were taken as the 95% CI for the AUC. Additionally, 1000 ROC curves from the Bootstrap samples (depicted as semi-transparent lines) were plotted to visualize the concentration of their distribution.Internal validation of the nomogram calibration was performed using 1000 Bootstrap resamples to correct for overfitting bias.Its clinical application value was evaluated via decision curve analysis (DCA). The collinearity of independent factors from the logistic regression model was tested by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF).A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Univariate analysis of CAA

The results of the univariate analysis comparing the UAA and CAA groups are presented in Table 1. A total of 465 patients were included in the UAA group, and 157 were included in the CAA group, yielding an incidence of CAA of 25.24%. Compared with those in the UAA group, patients in the CAA group were older (52 vs. 59 years, P < 0.001), had a longer duration from symptom onset to presentation (1.62 vs. 1.94 days, P < 0.001), and had a higher body temperature at presentation (36.2 vs. 36.8 °C, P < 0.001). In addition, the CAA group had higher blood glucose levels at admission (6.43 vs. 7.23 mmol/L, P < 0.001), white blood cell counts (11.31 vs. 13.00 × 10⁹/L, P < 0.001), neutrophil percentage (80.4% vs. 86.3%, P < 0.001), and CRP levels (17.2 vs. 84.74 mg/L, P < 0.001). Furthermore, the appendiceal diameter was greater (9.51 vs. 13.26 mm, P < 0.001), and the prevalence of appendicolith was significantly greater in the CAA group than in the UAA group (19.33% vs. 59.87%, P < 0.001).

Multivariate analysis of CAA

To identify independent factors associated with CAA in patients with acute appendicitis, binary logistic regression analysis was performed. CAA was included as the dependent variable, while variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were entered as independent variables. The results are shown in Table 3. Multivariate analysis revealed six variables as independent factors associated with CAA: age [odds ratio (OR) 3.697, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.774–7.704], body temperature [OR 3.235, 95% CI 1.367–7.656], neutrophil percentage [OR 2.317, 95% CI 1.123–4.782], CRP level [OR 12.434, 95% CI 5.525–27.982], appendiceal diameter [OR 30.731, 95% CI 15.189–62.177], and presence of an appendicolith [OR 6.968, 95% CI 3.544–13.698].

Nomogram construction and validation

Through multivariate logistic regression analysis, six independent predictors were identified and incorporated into a nomogram via R software (Fig. 2). A nomogram for predicting the probability of CAA was developed by integrating multiple prognostic variables, including age, body temperature, neutrophil percentage, CRP level, appendiceal diameter, and the presence of an appendicolith. Each variable was assigned a specific score to quantify its contribution to the risk of CAA. The individual scores were summed to generate a total score, with higher total scores indicating a poorer prognosis. The total score was subsequently transformed into the predicted probability of CAA through a linear predictor.

(To use the nomogram, located your patient’s risk factors, draw lines from their values to the “Points” axis, sum the points, then draw a line down from the total points to find the predicted outcome on the “Predicted Value” axis), Note: CRP: C-reactive protein

This nomogram provides a personalized risk assessment tool that enables clinicians to evaluate the probability of CAA based on individual patient characteristics and imaging features.ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of the nomogram (Fig. 3). The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.959 (95% CI: 0.943–0.975), Mean bootstrap apparent AUC: 0.960 (95% CI: 0.943–0.974), Mean optimism: 0.003 (95% CI: −0.015–0.017), Optimism-corrected AUC: 0.956 (95% CI: 0.926–0.990), with a sensitivity of 87.3% and a specificity of 90.3%, indicating excellent discriminatory ability in our dataset. The optimism value is close to 0, indicating a very low degree of overfitting, with minimal difference between the pre- and post-correction AUC, and the model’s performance has not been overestimated.

The calibration curve shows a high degree of agreement between predicted probabilities and actual probabilities, with the bias-corrected curve closely aligning with the diagonal. The mean absolute error was 0.014, indicating good calibration performance, with high precision and stability (Fig. 4).

Calibration plots of the nomogram. The apparent line almost coincides with the diagonal, but the bias-corrected line (after Bootstrap bias correction) still maintains good agreement with the diagonal

Figure 5 presents the decision curve analysis (DCA) evaluating the model’s net benefit at different risk thresholds. The threshold probability range for the DCA was set from 0 to 1 (step size 0.01). Within the threshold probability range of 0.01–0.98, the model’s net benefit was higher than both the “treat all” and “treat none” baseline strategies, indicating that the prediction model provides clinical net benefit across the above threshold range.

Collinearity

All VIF values of each independent predictor in the nomogram were ranged from 1.03 to 1.22 (< 5) (age, 1.03, body temperature,1.13, neutrophil percentage, 1.08, CRP, 1.19, Appendiceal diameter, 1.22, appendicolith, 1.07), indicating no collinearity. The discriminatory ability of the model is credible.

Discussion

Acute appendicitis is one of the most common general surgical emergencies in the world, with an estimated lifetime risk of 7–8% [17]. Appendectomy is one of the most common surgical procedures and, as such, has become one of the burdens on modern healthcare systems. Many studies currently suggest that antibiotic treatment for UAA is a safe treatment option [1, 6, 18]. However, in regard to CAA, the risk of treatment failure with antibiotics is greater. The aim of this study was to identify CAA in adult patients, avoid conservative treatment, and opt for surgical treatment, which offers more definitive efficacy.

To date, several studies have analyzed the identification of CAA in acute appendicitis patients. These studies suggest that CAA is associated with certain clinical factors (such as age, duration of symptoms, and the neutrophil ratio) [10, 13, 19, 20]. However, the predictive models developed have shown poor predictive ability [11, 13, 20].

Our study included 622 patients with acute appendicitis, of whom 157 had CAA, for an incidence rate of 25.24%. Our research revealed that age, body temperature, the neutrophil ratio, the CRP level, the maximum diameter of the appendix, and the presence of appendiceal fecaliths were significant factors. We used the ROC curve to determine the optimal cutoff point and constructed a nomogram model on the basis of these six factors. The advantage of our model lies in its simplicity: by performing abdominal CT, routine blood tests, and CRP examination in the outpatient setting, along with asking about the patient’s age and body temperature, we can predict CAA with good performance. Moreover, the CT imaging data we included can be quickly interpreted. Our nomogram achieves the highest predictive ability in the current literature, but it lacks external validation.

As patients age, they tend to have more underlying diseases and a greater degree of vascular damage, making them more prone to progressive microvascular transmural ischemia (independent lumen occlusion), which can lead to gangrene, perforation, and abscess formation. Age has been validated as a risk factor for CAA in many studies [10, 13, 21]; however, some studies do not support this view [11, 20]. Currently, there is a lack of meta-analyses on this topic.

In most studies, the duration of symptoms has been identified as an independent risk factor for CAA [10, 13]. In our study, it was significant only in the univariate analysis. The authors believe the main reason is that, in our case system, the duration of symptoms was recorded in days, which was not sufficiently precise. This lack of precision may have led to symptom duration not being identified as an independent risk factor in the final regression analysis. In the studies by Maxim [10] and Jia-Hui [20], body temperature was identified as an independent predictor of acute complicated appendicitis. In Maxim’s study, the temperature cutoff value was 37.5 °C, whereas in Jia-Hui’s study, it was 37.2 °C. The cutoff value included in our study was higher than those reported in previous studies. This is because we did not use the temperature at admission; instead, we selected the highest body temperature recorded from symptom onset to the time before surgery. As a result, our cutoff value was higher. A cutoff of 38 °C resulted in the highest specificity, reaching 93.1%. Therefore, when a patient’s fever exceeds 38 °C, CAA should be strongly suspected, and timely surgical intervention is warranted.

Some studies have reported that the median WBC count in patients with uncomplicated acute appendicitis ranges from 12.9 × 10³/µL to 14.6 × 10³/µL, whereas in patients with complicated acute appendicitis, the median leukocyte count ranges from 13.3 × 10³/µL to 17.4 × 10³/µL [9]. The literature indicates that the WBC count has a sensitivity of 79% to 93% for acute appendicitis, but its specificity is relatively low. In our study, as well as in the studies by Min [13] and Ana [19], an association between WBC count and CAA was not demonstrated, nor was it confirmed to be a strong predictive factor. In our study, the neutrophil percentage and CRP level were identified as independent risk factors for CAA. Among the laboratory markers, the ROC curve for CRP had the largest AUC, indicating a strong predictive ability, which is consistent with previous research [9, 19, 22]. In the study by Ana [19], the cutoff value for CRP was 63.3 mg/L, with a sensitivity of 73.65% and a specificity of 85.1%. In the study by Hyoung-Min [9], the cutoff value for CRP was 70.5 mg/L, with a sensitivity of 57.6% and a specificity of 97.3%. In comparison, the cutoff value in our study was 64.73 mg/L, which is close to theirs. Our study revealed a sensitivity of 66.2% and a specificity of 88.75%.

In our study, we included only two CT imaging indicators, as appendiceal fecaliths and the appendiceal diameter are more easily observed. In the study by Toshimichi [23], appendiceal fecaliths and the appendiceal diameter were identified as independent risk factors for the failure of nonsurgical treatment for appendicitis. The main cause of acute appendicitis is obstruction of the appendix outlet [24], with appendiceal fecaliths being one of the causes of this obstruction. Unlike lymphoid, appendiceal fecaliths cause persistent blockage of the appendiceal lumen, leading to increased intraluminal pressure and promoting the progression of the inflammatory process. Moreover, as the diameter of the appendix increases, increased intraluminal pressure can lead to complications such as gangrenous appendicitis and perforation [25]. Our study is consistent with the findings of Yun [11]. The presence of appendiceal fecaliths was found to be associated with acute complicated appendicitis in almost all the studies that included it as a factor. However, the association with the appendiceal diameter remains controversial [10, 11, 20], and further meta-analyses are needed to clarify this phenomenon.

In the study by Yun [11], glucose and D-dimer were included as predictive factors for appendiceal gangrene or perforation. There is a connection between the coagulation system and the inflammatory response [26]. D-dimer is a degradation product of fibrin and has been studied in the context of nontraumatic acute abdomen, showing the potential to replace the WBC count in the diagnosis of this condition [27]. Previous studies have also suggested a potential correlation between D-dimer levels and the severity of appendicitis [12]. Elevated blood glucose levels are considered a response to inflammatory stimuli and physical stress, creating a favorable environment for bacterial growth and exacerbating inflammation in the appendix [28]. Although D-dimer and glucose are linked to inflammation, their predictive value is relatively lower than that of the neutrophil ratio and C-reactive protein.

CAA should include appendiceal abscess, but this manuscript excludes it. The aim of our study is to predict CAA to guide clinicians toward surgical rather than conservative treatment for such patients. However, the treatment for appendiceal abscess tends to be conservative rather than surgical. According to clinical guidelines, conservative treatment is preferred for appendiceal abscess because there is a 14.3% probability of underlying appendiceal neoplasm. Additionally, surgery for appendiceal abscess often involves severe adhesions and carries a risk of bowel resection. Therefore, guidelines recommend initial conservative treatment, followed by surgical intervention 6–12 weeks later [29]. For the reasons outlined above, we have set appendiceal abscess as an exclusion criterion.

We converted continuous variables into binary variables using cutoff points to construct the nomogram, which makes the nomogram simpler. Using continuous variables would be relatively cumbersome for clinicians to calculate, so this approach is also intended to facilitate clinical practice. However, dichotomization may lead to loss of information and imprecision in effect estimation, which is another limitation of this manuscript.

The dataset included in this article consists of patients who underwent appendectomy, with the aim of predicting CAA. For CAA, current treatment guidelines recommend surgical intervention [10]. Therefore, the purpose of our manuscript is to advocate for active surgery in such patients by predicting CAA. However, when analyzing the decision between conservative management and surgical treatment, our manuscript has certain limitations.

This study employed a retrospective case-control design, and the participants were from a single center, which may reduce the generalizability of the conclusions. The lack of external validation is therefore a limitation of this manuscript. Furthermore, this study required abdominal CT for the assessment of complicated acute appendicitis, which increases outpatient costs for patients and also adds constraints to the clinical application of the findings. In future research, we plan to conduct a large-scale, multicenter prospective study with external validation to confirm our findings.

Conclusion

Age, body temperature, neutrophil percentage, CRP, appendix diameter, and appendiceal fecalith are predictive factors for CAA. We developed a simple clinical prediction model aimed at identifying CAA in acute appendicitis patients. This model performs well in identifying CAA, and for such patients, active surgical treatment should be promptly considered.
