Abstract
. State-space models (SSMs) are effective architectures for sequential modeling, but a rigorous theoretical understanding of their training dynamics is still lacking. In this work, we formulate the training of SSMs as an ensemble optimal control problem, where a shared control law governs a population of input-dependent dynamical systems. We derive Pontryagin’s maximum principle (PMP) for this ensemble control formulation, providing necessary conditions for optimality. Motivated by these conditions, we introduce an algorithm based on the method of successive approximations. We prove convergence of this iterative scheme along a subsequence and establish sufficient conditions for global optimality. The resulting framework provides a control-theoretic perspective on SSM training.



1.
Introduction

State-space models (SSMs) are a classical and highly versatile mathematical framework for modeling dynamical systems, in which observable sequences are generated by the evolution of latent states governed by differential or difference equations. In the discrete setting, SSMs are also referred to as hidden Markov models. Originating in control systems engineering, SSMs have long served as a foundational tool in time series analysis. Their simple yet expressive formulation, consisting of a latent state dynamics equation coupled with an observation model, has enabled widespread adoption across disciplines including economics
[
21
]
, neuroscience
[
12
,
23
]
, ecology
[
4
,
16
]
, robotics
[
13
,
14
]
, and weather forecasting
[
19
]
.

In recent years, SSMs have re-emerged as powerful architectures for sequence modeling in machine learning, driven by advances that integrate neural parameterizations with structured dynamical systems. Modern SSM-based models demonstrate strong empirical performance and excellent scalability on long sequences, making them compelling alternatives to attention-based architectures. In particular, selective SSMs
[
6
]
introduce input-dependent gating mechanisms that dynamically modulate state transitions, enabling selective information propagation over long temporal horizons with linear computational complexity. Building on this line of work, the Mamba architecture
[
5
]
employs parameter-efficient structured recurrences that achieve transformer-level accuracy while maintaining favorable scaling, stability, and memory efficiency. More recently, Mamba-3
[
9
]
further enhances expressivity through complex-valued state models and higher-order discretization, and improves inference efficiency via a multi-input multi-output (MIMO) design. Compared to transformers, which rely on pairwise attention with quadratic complexity in sequence length, SSM-based models represent sequences through dynamical systems with structured evolution laws, providing a principled and efficient mechanism for long-range dependency modeling and an interpretable state representation.

Although compelling empirical evidence confirms the remarkable performance of SSM-based architectures, there remains a pressing need for a rigorous mathematical framework to analyze their fundamental properties, particularly the mechanisms underlying their training and learning dynamics. In the existing literature, the analogy between deep learning and optimal control has become a well-established and fruitful viewpoint. In this framework, the forward propagation of features is interpreted as the trajectory of a dynamical system governed by parameterized equations, while the learning process corresponds to solving an optimal control problem that minimizes a cost functional defined by the training objective.

This control-theoretic interpretation has been developed for several major architectures, including residual networks
[
7
,
11
]
, neural ordinary differential equations (neural ODEs)
[
10
,
17
]
, and more recently, transformers
[
8
,
22
]
. In these works, the state dynamics in the control problem correspond to the forward pass through the layers of a neural network

\dot{x}(t)=b(t,x(t),u(t)),\quad x(0)\sim\mu_{0},

starting from an initial distribution
\mu_{0}
of data inputs. The control variables correspond to the network parameters or weights that influence the state evolution.
This framework has proven useful for understanding the training of deep neural networks and continuous-depth models at scale. Within this setting, Pontryagin’s maximum principle (PMP) arises as a continuous-time analogue of backpropagation: the adjoint states correspond to Lagrange multipliers enforcing the dynamic constraints, and updating the control corresponds to moving along the Hamiltonian gradient. This duality between control and learning has inspired a wide range of theoretical analyses, including continuous-depth and mean-field formulations, as well as the design of training algorithms grounded in optimal control principles.

The main motivation for the current study is to generalize the control-theoretic viewpoint to the analysis of state-space models. Compared with the formulation discussed above, the main difference for (selective) SSMs is that the state evolution depends on an input signal (the input sequence) in addition to the current latent state and the control. Thus, it is most natural to model the state evolution as

\dot{x}_{\omega}(t)=b(t,x_{\omega}(t),u(t),\omega),\qquad x_{\omega}(0)=x_{0}(\omega),

where
\omega
denotes the input signal (see Table
1
for the correspondence with the standard SSM terminology). Such state dynamics, and thus the associated control problem, correspond to what is known as
ensemble control
in the control literature.

In an ensemble control problem, one seeks a single control signal that simultaneously governs a continuum of systems indexed by a parameter
\omega\in\Omega
. In the present context, this parameter
\omega
is naturally interpreted as the input sequence of the SSM (cf. Table
1
). The overall cost functional therefore aggregates performance across an ensemble of input signals. In particular, only a single control signal is allowed, as we require a single SSM to generate (approximate) output sequences for multiple diverse input sequences.

The training of structured state-space architectures naturally fits within this ensemble control framework. The heterogeneity of SSMs arises from input-dependent system matrices: different input signals induce distinct dynamical flows even when the model parameters are shared. In particular, selective or input-dependent SSMs exhibit dynamics that evolve as functions of the input signal, making the ensemble interpretation especially relevant: each input sequence induces a distinct dynamical flow, yet the model parameters (controls) are shared across all trajectories. Consequently, analyzing the training dynamics of SSMs requires tools that extend beyond single-system or mean-field formulations, motivating a general ensemble optimal control framework that can capture their structural and statistical properties in a unified manner.

In this work, we develop such a unified mathematical framework for analyzing the training of SSMs through the lens of ensemble optimal control. Specifically, Section
2
introduces a general class of ensemble control problems characterized by parameter-dependent dynamics and cost functionals integrated over a parameter space, encompassing state-space model training as a particular instance. Section
3
derives the necessary optimality conditions in the form of PMP adapted to ensemble systems and establishes its specialization to the SSM training problem. Building upon the analytical structure of this ensemble PMP, Section
4
proposes an iterative algorithm that constructs approximate optimal controls and proves its convergence to a stationary point satisfying the necessary conditions under suitable assumptions. Finally, Section
5
provides a set of sufficient conditions for optimality and demonstrates that the limiting control produced by the proposed algorithm indeed achieves optimality for the class of ensemble control problems under consideration.

Related Work

Recent studies have increasingly applied optimal control theory to understand, train, and enhance deep learning models. Li et al.
[
11
]
formulate neural network training as a discrete-time optimal control problem and introduce a Pontryagin-based method of successive approximations that enables gradient-free and stable optimization, even for networks with discrete or ternary weights. Extending this formulation, Li et al.
[
10
]
treat deep learning in continuous time and employ Pontryagin’s maximum principle (PMP) to derive an alternative training algorithm with provable convergence that alleviates common issues of gradient-based methods such as slow convergence and saddle-point trapping. Han et al.
[
7
]
generalize this perspective to population risk minimization by introducing a mean-field optimal control framework governed by the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman and Pontryagin principles, thereby establishing a rigorous mathematical link between optimal control and learning dynamics. More recently, Ruiz et al.
[
17
]
analyze neural ordinary differential equations (neural ODEs) through a control-theoretic lens, demonstrating that data classification and universal approximation can be achieved by simultaneously controlling multiple neural ODEs with a shared control law. Furthermore, Chen et al.
[
3
]
exploit a closed-loop control formulation to construct self-healing neural networks capable of autonomously detecting and correcting adversarial perturbations, thus improving robustness.

Recent advances have extended these control-theoretic insights to modern architectures. Zhang et al.
[
22
]
cast vision transformer (ViT) fine-tuning as an optimal control problem solved via the PMP, revealing that low-precision (binary) adaptation matrices can preserve the controllability of their full-precision counterparts. Kan et al.
[
8
]
apply optimal control theory to both training and architecture design of transformers, proposing a continuous-time framework that improves performance, generalization, and parameter efficiency while offering theoretical grounding for model design and training improvements.

Starting from a similar control-theoretic viewpoint, our work focuses specifically on state-space models (SSMs). While earlier methods apply optimal control ideas to feedforward networks, neural ODEs, or transformers, we develop an optimal control formulation tailored to the dynamics of SSMs. Closely related to our work, recent theoretical studies on selective state-space models, such as
[
15
]
, formulate input-dependent SSMs as linear controlled differential equations and analyze their expressive power using tools from rough path theory, establishing universality and theoretical representation guarantees. In contrast, our approach adopts an ensemble control perspective, in which selectivity is not modeled as an input-dependent state evolution along a single trajectory, but rather as control over a family of dynamical systems indexed by different inputs. This formulation emphasizes controllability, structural properties, and system-level behavior of selective SSMs, and thus allows us to derive explicit optimality conditions, approximation schemes, and convergence results, offering theoretical and practical insights not captured in previous work.

2.
Formulation

The central motivation of this work lies in the observation that the training dynamics of modern structured state-space models can be naturally interpreted as an ensemble optimal control problem. In this section, we first present a general mathematical framework for ensemble control, describing a family of systems driven by a common control input. Subsequently, we demonstrate that the training of state-space models fits within this framework as a specific instance of ensemble optimal control, thereby establishing the foundation for the analytical developments in subsequent sections.

2.1.
Ensemble Optimal Controls

We introduce the formulation of a general ensemble optimal control problem. Let
T>0
be the terminal time,
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
the control set, and
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a probability space representing the underlying uncertainty or parameter variation in the system. Each point
\omega\in\Omega
labels a distinct member of the ensemble, such as a different instance of a system with varying parameters.

The dynamics for each individual system in the ensemble are given by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{x}(t,\omega)=b(t,x(t,\omega),u(t),\omega),&t\in[0,T],\ \omega\in\Omega,\\
x(0,\omega)=x_{0}(\omega),&\omega\in\Omega,\end{cases}

where
b:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times U\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
describes the system dynamics, which may vary with
\omega
,
x_{0}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the (possibly random or distributed) initial condition, and
u:[0,T]\to U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the control field, assumed measurable in time. Crucially, the control
u(\cdot)
is independent of
\omega
, meaning the same control acts simultaneously on all systems in the ensemble, though their responses differ due to their dependence on
\omega
.

The goal is to find a control
u(\cdot)
that optimizes the performance of the entire ensemble, typically measured through an expected or average cost. The cost functional is defined as

(2.2)

\displaystyle J(u(\cdot)):=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[L(t,x(t,\omega),u(t),\omega)]\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[g(x(T,\omega),\omega)],

where

L:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times U\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is the running cost,
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is the terminal cost.
The expectation over
\omega\in\Omega
represents the averaging of costs across the ensemble with respect to the measure
\mu
. The admissible control set is given by

\mathcal{U}[0,T]:=\{\,u:[0,T]\to U\mid u(\cdot)\ \text{is measurable}\,\}.

A pair
(u(\cdot),\{x(\cdot,\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega})
is called
admissible
if
u(\cdot)\in\mathcal{U}[0,T]
and for each
\omega\in\Omega
, the trajectory
x(\cdot,\omega)
is the unique solution of (
2.1
). An admissible pair
(u^{*}(\cdot),\{x^{*}(\cdot,\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega})
is called
optimal
if

\displaystyle J(u^{*}(\cdot))\leq J(u(\cdot)),

for any admissible
(u(\cdot),\{x(\cdot,\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega})
. Formally, the ensemble control problem is to find an optimal control
u^{*}(\cdot)
by solving the following minimization problem:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{P})\quad\inf_{u(\cdot)\in\mathcal{U}[0,T]}J(u(\cdot)).

Conceptually, this involves a single control
u(\cdot)
steering an infinite collection of systems, indexed by
\omega
, toward a desired collective behavior. The ensemble’s performance is measured in an average sense via the expected cost
J(u(\cdot))
.

For convenience, define the ensemble-averaged running and terminal costs:

\widebar{L}(t,x(t,\cdot),u(t)):=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[L(t,x(t,\omega),u(t),\omega)],\qquad\widebar{g}(x(T,\cdot)):=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[g(x(T,\omega),\omega)].

Then the cost functional (
2.2
) can be written more compactly as

J(u(\cdot))=\int_{0}^{T}\widebar{L}(t,x(t,\cdot),u(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+\widebar{g}(x(T,\cdot)).

This representation resembles the standard optimal control formulation, except that at each time
t
the state variable
x(t,\cdot)
is now a function
\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, describing the entire ensemble’s state rather than a single deterministic vector. In particular, the ensemble problem is infinite-dimensional, and the goal is to choose a common control
u(\cdot)
that achieves the best trade-off for all members of the ensemble.

2.2.
State-space Model Training as an Ensemble Control Problem

We interpret the training of state-space models within the framework of ensemble optimal control. In this formulation, the objective is to approximate a target mapping

\mathcal{F}:\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime},\qquad\omega\mapsto\omega^{\prime},

which associates each input trajectory
\omega:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a corresponding output trajectory
\omega^{\prime}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
. The sets
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
are assumed to be Borel subsets of
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
, respectively, endowed with the topology induced by the supremum norm.

To formalize the ensemble setting, we need to equip
\Omega
with a probability measure. Let
\Sigma
denote the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega
generated by the norm topology. The ambient space
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
admits various probability measures, including Dirac and Wiener measures. If
\Omega
is a Borel subset of positive measure, then any such measure on
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
can be restricted to
\Omega
, thereby inducing a probability measure
\mu
on
(\Omega,\Sigma)
. In what follows, we fix the probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
.

Given an input function
\omega
, a state-space model produces an output trajectory through an underlying latent (hidden) state
x(\cdot,\omega):[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
governed by a dynamical system that is linear in the state variable
x
:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{x}(t,\omega)=A(t,\omega;u(t))\,x(t,\omega)+B(t,\omega;u(t)),&t\in[0,T],\\[4.0pt]
x(0,\omega)=x_{0}(\omega).\end{cases}

The matrix function
A(t,\omega;u(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and vector field
B(t,\omega;u(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are parametrized by a measurable control function
u:[0,T]\to U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
U
represents the parameter space. For each time
t
and input
\omega
, the choice of
u(t)
determines the effective system dynamics.

The model output is obtained by decoding the hidden state via a fixed linear readout:

y(t,\omega)=Cx(t,\omega),\qquad t\in[0,T],

where
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times n}
is a prescribed decoding matrix. The decoding matrix
C
can be extended to an input-dependent and trainable form,
C=C(t,\omega;u(t))
, allowing adaptive readout dynamics. However, for simplicity, we assume
C
to be a constant matrix in the present formulation.

The objective is to choose the time-dependent control parameters
u(\cdot)
so that the predicted output of the model
y(\cdot,\omega)
closely matches the target output
\mathcal{F}(\omega)(\cdot)
for all inputs
\omega\in\Omega
.

We define the admissible set of controls by

\mathcal{U}[0,T]:=\bigl\{u:[0,T]\to U\,\big|\,u(\cdot)\ \text{is measurable}\bigr\}.

For each admissible control
u(\cdot)\in\mathcal{U}[0,T]
, let
\{x(\cdot,\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
be the corresponding state trajectories solving the state equation (
2.3
). We then define the cost functional

\displaystyle J(u(\cdot))

\displaystyle:=\alpha\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\lvert Cx(t,\omega)-\mathcal{F}(\omega)(t)\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\beta\int_{0}^{T}\lvert u(t)\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\big[\,\alpha\lvert Cx(t,\omega)-\mathcal{F}(\omega)(t)\rvert^{2}+\beta\lvert u(t)\rvert^{2}\,\big]\,\mathrm{d}t.

The positive constants
\alpha
and
\beta
balance two objectives: data fitting and regularization. The first enforces agreement between model output
Cx(t,\omega)
and the target
\mathcal{F}(\omega)(t)
, while the second penalizes excessive control effort to keep
u(\cdot)
bounded in
L^{2}([0,T];U)
. With these definitions in place, the ensemble optimal control formulation of training can be stated as:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{PS})\quad\inf_{u(\cdot)\in\mathcal{U}[0,T]}J(u(\cdot)).

It is evident that problem
(\mathbf{PS})
is a particular instance of the general ensemble optimal control formulation
(\mathbf{P})
with the following correspondences: for every
t\in[0,T]
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
u\in U
and
\omega\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle b(t,x,u,\omega)

\displaystyle=A(t,\omega;u)\,x+B(t,\omega;u),

\displaystyle L(t,x,u,\omega)

\displaystyle=\alpha\lvert Cx-\mathcal{F}(\omega)(t)\rvert^{2}+\beta\lvert u\rvert^{2},

\displaystyle g(x,\omega)

\displaystyle=0.

In this interpretation, the collection of input signals
\{\omega\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
plays the role of an ensemble of systems being driven by a common control
u(\cdot)
. The objective is to design a single control trajectory
u(\cdot)
that achieves the best performance, measured by output alignment with
\mathcal{F}(\omega)
, across the entire ensemble rather than for any single input.

To ensure that problem
(\mathbf{PS})
is well-posed and mathematically tractable, we impose the following structural and regularity conditions on the input-output spaces, the control set, and the system dynamics.

Assumption 2.1
.

We make the following assumptions regarding problem
(\mathbf{PS})
:

(1)

The input set
\Omega
is a compact and convex subset of
\tilde{\Omega}
, a bounded open subset of
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
equipped with the supremum norm, and
\Sigma
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega
generated by the supremum norm topology;
\mu
is an arbitrary fixed probability measure on
(\Omega,\Sigma)
.

(2)

The output set
\Omega^{\prime}
is a compact subset of
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
equipped with the supremum norm. In particular, this implies that there exists
M_{\Omega^{\prime}}>0
such that for any
\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega^{\prime}
,
\|\omega^{\prime}\|\leq M_{\Omega^{\prime}}
.

(3)

The input-output map
\mathcal{F}:\tilde{\Omega}\to\Omega^{\prime}
and the initial condition
x_{0}:\tilde{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
are both continuously Fréchet differentiable.

(4)

The set
U
is a nonempty, convex and compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

(5)

The maps
A:[0,T]\times\tilde{\Omega}\times U\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
B:[0,T]\times\tilde{\Omega}\times U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
are (jointly) continuous and bounded, i.e. there exist constants
M_{A},M_{B}>0
such that

\displaystyle\lvert A(t,\omega;u)\rvert\leq M_{A},\quad\lvert B(t,\omega;u)\rvert\leq M_{B}

for any
(t,\omega,u)\in[0,T]\times\tilde{\Omega}\times U
;

(6)

The maps
A(t,\omega;u)
and
B(t,\omega;u)
are twice continuously differentiable with respect to
u
.
Assume that there exist finite constants
G_{A}
,
G_{B}
,
H_{A}
and
H_{B}
such that

\displaystyle G_{A}:=\sup_{(t,\omega,u)}\|D_{u}A(t,\omega;u)\|_{\mathrm{op}},\qquad G_{B}:=\sup_{(t,\omega,u)}\|D_{u}B(t,\omega;u)\|_{\mathrm{op}},

\displaystyle H_{A}:=\sup_{(t,\omega,u)}\|D_{u}^{2}A(t,\omega;u)\|_{\mathrm{op}},\qquad H_{B}:=\sup_{(t,\omega,u)}\|D_{u}^{2}B(t,\omega;u)\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

where
(t,\omega,u)
is taken over
[0,T]\times\tilde{\Omega}\times U
.
We further assume that
D_{u}A(t,\omega;u)
and
D_{u}B(t,\omega;u)
are equicontinuous in
t
, that is, there exist moduli of continuity
\theta_{A}(\cdot)
and
\theta_{B}(\cdot)
such that for any
t,s\in[0,T]
,

\sup_{(\omega,u)}\|D_{u}A(t,\omega;u)-D_{u}A(s,\omega;u)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\theta_{A}(\lvert t-s\rvert),

and

\sup_{(\omega,u)}\|D_{u}B(t,\omega;u)-D_{u}B(s,\omega;u)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\theta_{B}(\lvert t-s\rvert).

where
(\omega,u)
is taken over
\tilde{\Omega}\times U
. Here
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}
is the operator norm induced by the matrix 2-norm and the vector Euclidean norm used in this paper (see Remark
2.1
).

(7)

A(t,\omega;u)
and
B(t,\omega;u)
are Fréchet differentiable with respect to
\omega
, and
\partial_{\omega}A(t,\omega;u)
and
\partial_{\omega}B(t,\omega;u)
are continuous. In view of (1) and (4), this implies

\displaystyle\sup_{(t,\omega,u)\in[0,T]\times\tilde{\Omega}\times U}\|\partial_{\omega}A(t,\omega;u)\|_{\mathrm{op}}<\infty,\quad\sup_{(t,\omega,u)\in[0,T]\times\tilde{\Omega}\times U}\|\partial_{\omega}B(t,\omega;u)\|_{\mathrm{op}}<\infty.

(8)

The constant matrix
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times n}
has full column rank,
d^{\prime}\geq n
.

Remark 2.1
.

We explain the operator norm introduced in Assumption
2.1
(6) and give a method of estimating the constants
G_{A},G_{B},H_{A},H_{B}
. For any
(t,\omega,u)\in[0,T]\times\tilde{\Omega}\times U
, the first-order derivative

D_{u}A(t,\omega;u):\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the linear map

D_{u}A(t,\omega;u)[v]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\dfrac{\partial A(t,\omega;u)}{\partial u_{i}},\qquad\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

and the second-order derivative

D_{u}^{2}A(t,\omega;u):\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

is the bilinear map

D_{u}^{2}A(t,\omega;u)[v,w]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}v_{i}w_{j}\,\frac{\partial^{2}A(t,\omega;u)}{\partial u_{i}\,\partial u_{j}},\qquad\forall v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

The associated operator norm of the linear and bilinear maps are defined by

\|D_{u}A(t,\omega;u)\|_{\mathrm{op}}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\\
\lvert v\rvert=1\end{subarray}}\lvert D_{u}A(t,\omega;u)[v]\rvert.

and

\|D_{u}^{2}A(t,\omega;u)\|_{\mathrm{op}}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\\
\lvert v\rvert=\lvert w\rvert=1\end{subarray}}\lvert D_{u}^{2}A(t,\omega;u)[v,w]\rvert.

The operator norms of
D_{u}B(t,\omega;u)
and
D_{u}^{2}B(t,\omega;u)
are defined likewise.
Suppose one has the elementwise bounds

\left|\frac{\partial A(t,\omega;u)}{\partial u_{i}}\right|\leq\bar{G}_{A},\quad\left|\frac{\partial^{2}A(t,\omega;u)}{\partial u_{i}\,\partial u_{j}}\right|\leq\bar{H}_{A},\qquad\forall 1\leq i,j\leq m,\ \forall(t,\omega,u)\in[0,T]\times\tilde{\Omega}\times U.

Then, for any
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, by the triangle inequality, we have

\lvert D_{u}A(t,\omega;u)[v]\rvert\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lvert v_{i}\rvert\left|\dfrac{\partial A(t,\omega;u)}{\partial u_{i}}\right|\leq\bar{G}_{A}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lvert v_{i}\rvert\leq\sqrt{m}\,\bar{G}_{A}\,\lvert v\rvert,

and

\lvert D_{u}^{2}A(t,\omega;u)[v,w]\rvert\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\lvert v_{i}\rvert\,\lvert w_{j}\rvert\,\left|\frac{\partial^{2}A(t,\omega;u)}{\partial u_{i}\,\partial u_{j}}\right|\leq\bar{H}_{A}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lvert v_{i}\rvert\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lvert w_{j}\rvert\Bigr)\leq m\,\bar{H}_{A}\,\lvert v\rvert\,\lvert w\rvert.

Hence,

\|D_{u}A(t,\omega;u)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\sqrt{m}\,\bar{G}_{A},\qquad\|D_{u}^{2}A(t,\omega;u)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq m\,\bar{H}_{A}.

Consequently, we have
G_{A}\leq\sqrt{m}\,\bar{G}_{A}
and
H_{A}\leq m\,\bar{H}_{A}
. The estimation for
G_{B}
and
H_{B}
is similar.

2.3.
Examples

In practice, one often considers matrix-valued functions of the form

A(t,\omega;u)=A(\omega(t);u),\quad B(t,\omega;u)=B(\omega(t);u),

so that the matrices
A
and
B
depend only on the current value of
\omega
at time
t
, rather than on its entire trajectory. In this setting, the mappings
A
and
B
are jointly continuous in
(t,\omega,u)
provided that the functions
(\xi,u)\mapsto A(\xi;u)
and
(\xi,u)\mapsto B(\xi;u)
are continuous. By the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, the compactness of
\Omega
in
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
implies that the elements of
\Omega
are equicontinuous. Therefore,
D_{u}A
and
D_{u}B
are equicontinuous in
t
provided that
D_{u}A(\xi;u)
and
D_{u}B(\xi;u)
are equicontinuous in
\xi
.

Let us survey several representative examples arising in practice. A basic and widely used model is the standard linear state-space system

(2.4)

\dot{x}(t,\omega)=\mathbb{A}\,x(t,\omega)+\mathbb{B}\,\omega(t),

where
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbb{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. The input signal is
\omega=(\omega_{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}
, where
\omega_{i}
denotes the
i
-th input channel. If we regard
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
as trainable parameters, i.e., as the control variables of the model, then (
2.4
) clearly fits into the structural framework described in Assumption
2.1
.

In many structured state-space models (SSMs), the dynamics operate independently across input channels. A canonical example is the structured state-space sequence model (S4)
[
6
,
15
]
, which can be viewed as a special case of (
2.4
) with both
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
block-diagonal. More precisely, let
n=Nd
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
and write

\mathbb{A}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&&&\\
&A_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&A_{d}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd\times Nd},\qquad\mathbb{B}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{B}&&&\\
&\tilde{B}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\tilde{B}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd\times d},

where each
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is diagonal and
\tilde{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}
. In this case, the system decomposes into
d
independent subsystems,

(2.5)

\dot{x}_{i}(t,\omega)=A_{i}x_{i}(t,\omega)+\tilde{B}\omega_{i}(t),\qquad 1\leq i\leq d,

so that
x_{i}(t,\omega)
depends only on the
i
-th channel
\omega_{i}
, i.e.,
x_{i}(t,\omega)=x_{i}(t,\omega_{i})
.

In practice, SSMs are implemented in discrete time via a suitable discretization of (
2.5
). For instance, under a zero-order hold (ZOH) scheme
[
1
, Chapter 2]
with channel-dependent step size
\Delta_{i}
, one obtains the recurrence

(2.6)

x_{i,l}=\bar{A}_{i}\,x_{i,l-1}+\bar{B}_{i}\omega_{i,l},\qquad 1\leq l\leq L,\;1\leq i\leq d,

where
\bar{A}_{i}=\exp(\Delta_{i}A_{i})
and
\bar{B}_{i}
is determined accordingly by the discretization formula. Here, the gates
\bar{A}_{i}
and
\bar{B}_{i}
are channel-specific but constant across time. The selective state-space model (S6)
[
5
,
15
]
extends (S4) by allowing the discretization to depend on the current input. Specifically, the recurrence becomes

(2.7)

x_{i,l}=\bar{A}_{i}(\omega_{l})\,x_{i,l-1}+\bar{B}_{i}(\omega_{l})\,\omega_{i,l},\qquad 1\leq l\leq L,\;1\leq i\leq d,

where
\omega_{l}=(\omega_{1,l},\dots,\omega_{d,l})
denotes the full input vector at time step
l
. Unlike (
2.6
), the matrices
\bar{A}_{i}(\omega_{l})
and
\bar{B}_{i}(\omega_{l})
now depend on all input channels at time
l
. This input dependence is typically introduced through an input-controlled step size

\Delta_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\Delta_{i}(y)=\operatorname{softplus}(\alpha_{i}\cdot y+\beta_{i}),

with trainable parameters
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. The discrete gates are then defined by

\bar{A}_{i}(y)=\exp(\Delta_{i}(y)A_{i}),\qquad\bar{B}_{i}(y)=(B\,y)\,\Delta_{i}(y),

where each
A_{i}
is diagonal and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
is a shared linear projection across channels.

As shown in
[
15
]
, by introducing a small parameter
\delta>0
and parameterizing the step size as
\Delta_{i}(y)=\delta\operatorname{softplus}\big((\alpha_{i}\cdot y+\beta_{i})/\delta\big)
, one finds that to leading order in
\delta
, the recurrence (
2.7
) reduces to

x_{i,l}=x_{i,l-1}+A_{i}\,x_{i,l-1}\sigma(\alpha_{i}\cdot\omega_{l}+\beta_{i})\,\delta+(B\,\omega_{l})\,\omega_{i,l}\,\sigma(\alpha_{i}\cdot\omega_{l}+\beta_{i})\,\delta,

which is precisely the forward Euler discretization of the controlled ODE

(2.8)

\dot{x}_{i}(t,\omega)=A_{i}\,x_{i}(t,\omega)\,\sigma(\alpha_{i}\cdot\omega(t)+\beta_{i})+(B\,\omega(t))\,\omega_{i}(t)\,\sigma(\alpha_{i}\cdot\omega(t)+\beta_{i}),

where
\sigma(z)=\operatorname{softplus}(z/\delta)
.

In this formulation, the trainable parameters are
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
,
B
, and
\{\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
, so the control variable
u
can be identified with the vectorization of all these parameters. The continuous-time dynamics (
2.8
) therefore take the general form

\dot{x}(t,\omega)=A(\omega(t);u)\,x(t,\omega)+B(\omega(t);u),

with a block-diagonal gate

A(\omega(t);u)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}\,\sigma({\alpha}_{1}\cdot\omega(t)+{\beta}_{1})&&&\\
&A_{2}\,\sigma({\alpha}_{2}\cdot\omega(t)+{\beta}_{2})&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&A_{d}\,\sigma({\alpha}_{d}\cdot\omega(t)+{\beta}_{d})\end{pmatrix},

and a channel-wise gated projection

B(\omega(t);u)=\begin{pmatrix}(B\,\omega(t))\,\omega_{1}(t)\,\sigma({\alpha}_{1}\cdot\omega(t)+{\beta}_{1})\\[3.99994pt]
(B\,\omega(t))\,\omega_{2}(t)\,\sigma({\alpha}_{2}\cdot\omega(t)+{\beta}_{2})\\
\vdots\\
(B\,\omega(t))\,\omega_{d}(t)\,\sigma({\alpha}_{d}\cdot\omega(t)+{\beta}_{d})\end{pmatrix}.

The resulting coefficients
A(\omega(t);u)
and
B(\omega(t);u)
satisfy the regularity and structural conditions required in Assumption
2.1
. In particular, the equicontinuity of
D_{u}A(\xi;u)
and
D_{u}B(\xi;u)
follows from the smoothness of the softplus activation and the fact that the affine maps
\xi\mapsto\alpha_{i}\cdot\xi+\beta_{i}
form a uniformly Lipschitz family when the parameters are restricted to a compact set
U
.

3.
Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle

In this section, we present a set of necessary conditions for optimality in the ensemble control problems (
P
) and (
PS
), known as Pontryagin’s maximum principle. We begin by formalizing the notion of a local minimizer for the control problem, and then state Pontryagin’s maximum principle first for the general ensemble problem (
P
), followed by the specific problem (
PS
), which arises as a corollary under Assumption
2.1
.

Definition 3.1
.

An admissible pair
(u^{*}(\cdot),\{x^{*}(\cdot,\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega})
is called a
W^{1,1}
-local minimizer for problem (
P
) or (
PS
) if there exists
\epsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle J(u^{*}(\cdot))\leq J(u(\cdot)),

for any admissible
(u(\cdot),\{x(\cdot,\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega})
that satisfies

\displaystyle\|x^{*}(\cdot,\omega)-x(\cdot,\omega)\|_{W^{1,1}}\leq\epsilon,\quad\forall\,\omega\in\Omega.

We state Pontryagin’s maximum principle for problem (
P
) as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(u^{*}(\cdot),\{x^{*}(\cdot,\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega})
be a
W^{1,1}
-local minimizer for problem (
P
). Suppose that there exists
\delta>0
such that

(A1)

(\Omega,\rho_{\Omega})
is a complete separable metric space.

(A2)

U
is a nonempty and closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

(A3)

The maps
b:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times U\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
L:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times U\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
are measurable, and there exists a constant
M>0
such that for
\varphi(t,x,u,\omega)=b(t,x,u,\omega),L(t,x,u,\omega),g(x,\omega)
, we have

\displaystyle\lvert\varphi(t,x,u,\omega)-\varphi(t,x^{\prime},u^{\prime},\omega)\rvert\leq M(\lvert x-x^{\prime}\rvert+\lvert u-u^{\prime}\rvert),\quad\lvert\varphi(t,x,u,\omega)\rvert\leq M,

for any
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
,
x,x^{\prime}\in B(x^{*}(t,\omega),\delta)
, and
u,u^{\prime}\in U
.

(A4)

There exists a modulus of continuity
\theta:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that for
\varphi(t,x,u,\omega)=b(t,x,u,\omega),L(t,x,u,\omega)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{x\in B(x^{*}(t,\omega),\delta),u\in U}\lvert\varphi(t,x,u,\omega^{\prime})-\varphi(t,x,u,\omega^{\prime\prime})\rvert\mathrm{d}t\leq\theta(\rho_{\Omega}(\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime})),

and

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in B(x^{*}(T,\omega),\delta)}\lvert g(x,\omega^{\prime})-g(x,\omega^{\prime\prime})\rvert\leq\theta(\rho_{\Omega}(\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime})),

for any
\omega,\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime}\in\Omega
.

(A5)

The map
x\mapsto g(x,\omega)
is differentiable in
B(x^{*}(T,\omega),\delta)
for every
\omega\in\Omega
, and the map
(x,\omega)\mapsto\nabla_{x}g(x,\omega)
is continuous.

(A6)

For
\varphi=b,L
, the map
x\mapsto\varphi(t,x,u,\omega)
is continuously differentiable on
B(x^{*}(t,\omega),\delta)
, and there exists a constant
M>0
such that

\displaystyle\lvert\nabla_{x}\varphi(t,x,u,\omega)-\nabla_{x}\varphi(t,x^{\prime},u^{\prime},\omega)\rvert\leq M(\lvert x-x^{\prime}\rvert+\lvert u-u^{\prime}\rvert),\quad\lvert\nabla_{x}\varphi(t,x,u,\omega)\rvert\leq M,

for any
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
,
x,x^{\prime}\in B(x^{*}(t,\omega),\delta)
, and
u,u^{\prime}\in U
.

Then there exists
p(\cdot,\cdot):[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

(i)

p(\cdot,\omega)\in W^{1,1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
\omega\in\Omega
,

(ii)

For a.e.
t\in[0,T]
,

(3.1)

\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[p(t,\omega)\cdot b(t,x^{*}(t,\omega),u^{*}(t),\omega)-L(t,x^{*}(t,\omega),u^{*}(t),\omega)\right]\\
=\max_{u\in U}\,\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[p(t,\omega)\cdot b(t,x^{*}(t,\omega),u,\omega)-L(t,x^{*}(t,\omega),u,\omega)\right].

(iii)

For all
\omega\in\Omega
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{p}(t,\omega)=-\nabla_{x}b(t,x^{*}(t,\omega),u^{*}(t),\omega)^{\top}p(t,\omega)+\nabla_{x}L(t,x^{*}(t,\omega),u^{*}(t),\omega)\quad\text{a.e. }t\in[0,T],\\
p(T,\omega)=-\nabla_{x}g(x^{*}(T,\omega),\omega).\end{cases}

We call
\{p(\cdot,\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
the
adjoint variables
, (
3.2
) the
adjoint equation
(corresponding to the pair
(u^{*}(\cdot),\{x^{*}(\cdot,\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega})
), and (
3.1
) the
maximum condition
. The maximum condition can be written as

H(x^{*}(t,\cdot),p(t,\cdot),u^{*}(t),t)=\max_{u\in U}H(x^{*}(t,\cdot),p(t,\cdot),u,t),\quad\forall t\in[0,T],

where the
Hamiltonian
functional
H
is given by

\displaystyle H(x(t,\cdot),p(t,\cdot),u,t):=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[p(t,\omega)\cdot b(t,x(t,\omega),u,\omega)-L(t,x(t,\omega),u,\omega)\right].

Compared with the state equation (
2.1
), the adjoint equation (
3.2
) evolves backward in time; hence we also call (
2.1
) the
forward problem (equation)
and (
3.2
) the
backward problem (equation)
.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
is a direct adaptation of
[
2
, Theorem 3.3]
. The argument first treats the case where
\Omega
is finite, so that the ensemble problem reduces to a standard optimal control problem. One then approximates a general separable space by finite ensembles, uses uniform bounds to obtain compactness, and passes to the limit in the corresponding optimality conditions.

It is easy to verify that under Assumption
2.1
, any
W^{1,1}
-local minimizer of the problem (
PS
) satisfies conditions (A1)–(A6) of Theorem
3.1
for some
\delta>0
. Therefore, as a direct corollary of Theorem
3.1
, we state the following version of Pontryagin’s maximum principle for problem (
PS
).

Theorem 3.2
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, let
(u^{*}(\cdot),\{x^{*}(\cdot,\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega})
be a
W^{1,1}
-local minimizer for problem (
PS
). Then there exists
p(\cdot,\cdot):[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

(i)

p(\cdot,\omega)\in W^{1,1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
\omega\in\Omega
;

(ii)

For a.e.
t\in[0,T]
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[p(t,\omega)\cdot\bigg(A(t,\omega;u^{*}(t))x^{*}(t,\omega)+B(t,\omega;u^{*}(t))\bigg)-\alpha\lvert Cx^{*}(t,\omega)-\mathcal{F}(\omega)(t)\rvert^{2}-\beta\lvert u^{*}(t)\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\max_{u\in U}\,\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[p(t,\omega)\cdot\bigg(A(t,\omega;u)x^{*}(t,\omega)+B(t,\omega;u)\bigg)-\alpha\lvert Cx^{*}(t,\omega)-\mathcal{F}(\omega)(t)\rvert^{2}-\beta\lvert u\rvert^{2}\right];

(iii)

For all
\omega\in\Omega
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{p}(t,\omega)=-A(t,\omega;u^{*}(t))^{\top}p(t,\omega)+2\alpha C^{\top}(Cx^{*}(t,\omega)-\mathcal{F}(\omega)(t)),\quad\text{a.e. }t\in[0,T],\\
p(T,\omega)=0.\end{cases}

Remark 3.1
.

Although stated here, the maximum principle (Theorem

.

4.
Method of Successive Approximations

In this section, we introduce an iterative algorithm inspired by Pontryagin’s maximum principle to approximate an optimal solution of (
PS
). The proposed scheme, known as the method of successive approximations, constructs a sequence of admissible controls by recursively updating the control through the associated state and adjoint equations together with the maximum condition. We show that, for sufficiently large penalty parameter
\beta
, the generated sequence is precompact in the uniform topology and that every limit point satisfies the necessary optimality conditions. The detailed procedure is outlined in Algorithm
1
, and its convergence properties are established in Theorem
4.1
below.

Before presenting Algorithm
1
and analyzing its convergence properties, we give some a priori estimates for admissible pairs for (
PS
), which are summarized in the following Lemma
4.1
. The proof is deferred to Appendix
A
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, define constants

\displaystyle M_{X}:=(\|x_{0}\|+TM_{B})e^{TM_{A}},\quad M_{P}:=2\alpha T\lvert C\rvert(\lvert C\rvert M_{X}+M_{\Omega^{\prime}})e^{TM_{A}}.

For any continuous
u(\cdot)
, there exist a unique solution
x(\cdot,\omega)
to the state equation (
2.3
) and a unique solution
p(\cdot,\omega)
to the adjoint equation (
3.4
) for each
\omega\in\Omega
,
and the following hold:

(1)

\lvert x(t,\omega)\rvert\leq M_{X}
and
\lvert p(t,\omega)\rvert\leq M_{P}
for all
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
;

(2)

The state variable
x(t,\omega)\in C^{1}([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and
\partial_{\omega}x(t,\omega)
satisfies the following first-order variational equation:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{z}(t,\omega)=A(t,\omega;u(t))z(t,\omega)+\partial_{\omega}A(t,\omega;u(t))x(t,\omega)+\partial_{\omega}B(t,\omega;u(t)),\quad t\in[0,T],\\
z(0,\omega)=\partial_{\omega}x_{0}(\omega).\end{cases}

Recall that
\partial_{\omega}A
and
\partial_{\omega}B
are Fréchet derivatives (see Assumption
2.1
).

(3)

The adjoint variable
p(t,\omega)\in C^{1}([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
as well, and
\partial_{\omega}p(t,\omega)
satisfies the following first-order variational equation:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{z}(t,\omega)=-A(t,\omega;u(t))^{\top}z(t,\omega)-\partial_{\omega}A(t,\omega;u(t))^{\top}p(t,\omega)\\
\hskip 60.00009pt+2\alpha C^{\top}(C\partial_{\omega}x(t,\omega)-\partial_{\omega}\mathcal{F}(\omega)(t)),\quad t\in[0,T],\\
z(T,\omega)=0.\end{cases}

to approximate an optimal control for (

), which produces a sequence of control functions
\{u^{(k)}\}
by successively solving the associated forward and backward problems and updating the control according to the maximum condition.

Input:

Initial continuous control
u^{(0)}

Output:

Updated control sequence
\{u^{(k)}\}

for

k=0,1,2,\ldots

Solve the forward problem for

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{x}^{(k)}(t,\omega)=A(t,\omega;u^{(k)}(t))x^{(k)}(t,\omega)+B(t,\omega;u^{(k)}(t)),\quad t\in[0,T],\\
x^{(k)}(0,\omega)=x_{0}(\omega).\end{cases}

p^{(k)}(T,\omega)=0.\end{cases}

according to the maximum condition

(4.4)

\displaystyle H(x^{(k)}(t,\cdot),p^{(k)}(t,\cdot),u^{(k+1)}(t),t)=\max_{u\in U}H(x^{(k)}(t,\cdot),p^{(k)}(t,\cdot),u,t),\quad t\in[0,T],

where

(4.5)

\displaystyle H(x(t,\cdot),p(t,\cdot),u,t)

\displaystyle\quad:=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[p(t,\omega)\cdot\left(A(t,\omega;u)x(t,\omega)+B(t,\omega;u)\right)-\alpha\lvert Cx(t,\omega)-\mathcal{F}(\omega)(t)\rvert^{2}-\beta\lvert u\rvert^{2}\right].

Algorithm 1

Method of Successive Approximations

Algorithm
1
can be interpreted as a continuous-time analogue of iterative training procedures commonly used in machine learning. In particular, the state trajectory
x^{(k)}(t,\omega)
corresponds to the forward pass through the model, obtained by solving the forward equation (
4.2
), while the adjoint variable
p^{(k)}(t,\omega)
plays a role analogous to the back-propagated error signal and is obtained by solving the backward equation (
4.3
). The update step based on the maximum condition parallels a parameter update step in training, where the current control
u^{(k)}
is adjusted using gradient-related information encoded in maximizing the Hamiltonian (
4.5
). From this perspective, the method of successive approximations can be viewed as a functional analogue of gradient-based optimization, with the key difference that updates are obtained by solving forward–backward systems rather than performing explicit gradient descent steps. Moreover, once the forward trajectory is known, the backward equation becomes linear in the adjoint variable and has a dynamical structure similar to the forward equation. As a result, both forward and backward solves can be carried out independently across input trajectories and implemented efficiently in parallel.

To ensure that this iterative process is well-posed, it is necessary to establish conditions under which its limit points satisfy the Pontryagin maximum principle. The following theorem formalizes this result by proving that, under Assumption
2.1
, the sequence produced by Algorithm
1
is precompact in the uniform topology and that every uniform limit point fulfills the necessary optimality conditions of (
PS
) when the penalty parameter
\beta
is large enough.

Theorem 4.1
.

Define
C_{X}:=(G_{A}M_{X}+G_{B})e^{M_{A}T}
,
C_{E}:=\frac{1}{2}C_{X}T(M_{P}G_{A}+\alpha\lvert C\rvert^{2}C_{X}T)
and

\beta_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}M_{P}(H_{A}M_{X}+H_{B})+C_{E}.

Under Assumption
2.1
, if

\beta>\beta_{0}
, then Algorithm
1
generates a sequence of continuous controls
\{u^{(k)}\}
and the sequences of state and adjoint variables
\{x^{(k)}\}
and
\{p^{(k)}\}
that correspond to
\{u^{(k)}\}
. Moreover, there exists a subsequence indexed by
\{k_{j}\}
such that
u^{(k_{j})}\to u^{*}
,
x^{(k_{j})}\to x^{*}
, and
p^{(k_{j})}\to p^{*}
uniformly on
[0,T]\times\Omega
, and the triple
(x^{*},p^{*},u^{*})
satisfies the maximum condition (
3.3
) in Pontryagin’s maximum principle (Theorem
3.2
) for (
PS
), where
x^{*}
and
p^{*}
are the state and adjoint variables that correspond to
u^{*}
.

Proof.

We divide the proof into several steps.

1. Well-posedness of the Algorithm
1
.
We prove by induction that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist unique solutions
x^{(k)}
and
p^{(k)}
to the forward and backward problems (
4.2
) and (
4.3
). The initial control
u^{(0)}(\cdot)
is continuous by selection. By Lemma
4.1
, corresponding to
u^{(0)}(\cdot)
, there exist unique solutions
\{x^{(0)}(\cdot,\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
and
\{p^{(0)}(\cdot,\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
to the forward and backward problems (
4.2
), (
4.3
) respectively. Moreover, the state and adjoint variables
\{x^{(0)}(\cdot,\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
and
\{p^{(0)}(\cdot,\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
satisfy the boundedness estimate in Lemma
4.1
, that is,

\displaystyle\lvert x^{(0)}(t,\omega)\rvert\leq M_{X},\quad\lvert p^{(0)}(t,\omega)\rvert\leq M_{P},\quad\forall(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega.

We now show that
u^{(1)}(\cdot)
is continuous. Observe that the function

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{(0)}(t,u):=H(x^{(0)}(t,\cdot),p^{(0)}(t,\cdot),u,t)

is continuous on
[0,T]\times U
. Moreover,
\mathcal{H}^{(0)}(t,u)
is twice differentiable with respect to
u
. For each
(t,u)\in[0,T]\times U
, the Hessian of
\mathcal{H}^{(0)}
with respect to
u
is

\displaystyle\nabla_{u}^{2}\mathcal{H}^{(0)}(t,u)=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[p^{(0)}(t,\omega)\cdot\left(D_{u}^{2}A(t,\omega;u)x^{(0)}(t,\omega)+D_{u}^{2}B(t,\omega;u)\right)\right]-2\beta\mathbb{I}.

We compute the quadratic form of
\nabla_{u}^{2}\mathcal{H}^{(0)}(t,u)
along the direction
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{u}^{2}\mathcal{H}^{(0)}(t,u)[v,v]=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[p^{(0)}(t,\omega)\cdot\left(D_{u}^{2}A(t,\omega;u)[v,v]x^{(0)}(t,\omega)+D_{u}^{2}B(t,\omega;u)[v,v]\right)\right]-2\beta\lvert v\rvert^{2}.

By Assumption
2.1
(6), we have

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(0)}(t,u)[v,v]\leq-\lambda\lvert v\rvert^{2},\qquad\lambda:=2\beta-M_{P}(H_{A}M_{X}+H_{B}).

Since

Recall that

2.
L^{2}
-convergence
\|u^{(k+1)}-u^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
.
Define

\delta u^{(k)}:=u^{(k+1)}-u^{(k)},\qquad\xi^{(k)}:=x^{(k+1)}-x^{(k)}

and

A^{(k)}(t,\omega)=A(t,\omega;u^{(k)}(t)),\quad B^{(k)}(t,\omega)=B(t,\omega;u^{(k)}(t)),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

By Assumption
2.1
(6), for every
\omega\in\Omega
,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\lvert(A^{(k+1)}-A^{(k)})(t,\omega)\rvert\leq G_{A}\lvert\delta u^{(k)}(t)\rvert,\quad\lvert(B^{(k+1)}-B^{(k)})(t,\omega)\rvert\leq G_{B}\lvert\delta u^{(k)}(t)\rvert.

Since
\xi^{(k)}
satisfies the difference of the forward equations and
\xi^{(k)}(0,\omega)=0
,

\displaystyle\xi^{(k)}(t,\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\big(A^{(k+1)}x^{(k+1)}-A^{(k)}x^{(k)}+B^{(k+1)}-B^{(k)}\big)(s,\omega)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\big((A^{(k+1)}-A^{(k)})x^{(k+1)}+A^{(k)}\xi^{(k)}+B^{(k+1)}-B^{(k)}\big)(s,\omega)\,\mathrm{d}s,

considering the uniform boundedness of
\{A^{(k)}\}
and
\{x^{(k)}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\lvert\xi^{(k)}(t,\omega)\rvert\leq\int_{0}^{t}(G_{A}M_{X}+G_{B})\lvert\delta u^{(k)}(s)\rvert+M_{A}\lvert\xi^{(k)}(s,\omega)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}s.

By Grönwall’s inequality,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\lvert\xi^{(k)}(t,\omega)\rvert\leq C_{X}\int_{0}^{t}\lvert\delta u^{(k)}(s)\rvert\mathrm{d}s,\quad\text{where }C_{X}=(G_{A}M_{X}+G_{B})e^{M_{A}T}.

We now compute
J(u^{(k+1)})-J(u^{(k)})
:

\displaystyle J(u^{(k+1)})-J(u^{(k)})={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[2\alpha(Cx^{(k)}(t,\omega)-\mathcal{F}(\omega)(t))^{\top}C\xi^{(k)}(t,\omega)+\alpha\lvert C\xi^{(k)}(t,\omega)\rvert^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\beta\int_{0}^{T}\big(\lvert u^{(k+1)}(t)\rvert^{2}-\lvert u^{(k)}(t)\rvert^{2}\big)\mathrm{d}t.

Using the adjoint equation (
4.3
), the terminal condition
p^{(k)}(T)=0
,
\xi^{(k)}(0)=0
, and integration by parts, the first-order term becomes

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[2\alpha(Cx^{(k)}(t,\omega)-\mathcal{F}(\omega)(t))^{\top}C\xi^{(k)}(t,\omega)\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[p^{(k)}\cdot\big((A^{(k+1)}-A^{(k)})x^{(k+1)}+(B^{(k+1)}-B^{(k)})\big)(t,\omega)\right]\mathrm{d}t.

Using

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[p^{(k)}\cdot\big((A^{(k+1)}-A^{(k)})x^{(k)}+(B^{(k+1)}-B^{(k)})\big)(t,\omega)\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(t))-\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k)}(t))+\beta(\lvert u^{(k+1)}(t)\rvert^{2}-\lvert u^{(k)}(t)\rvert^{2}),

and
x^{(k+1)}=x^{(k)}+\xi^{(k)}
, the
\beta
-terms cancel:

\displaystyle J(u^{(k+1)})-J(u^{(k)})={}

\displaystyle{-}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(t))-\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k)}(t))\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle{-}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[p^{(k)}\cdot(A^{(k+1)}-A^{(k)})\xi^{(k)}(t,\omega)\right]\mathrm{d}t}_{E_{1}}

\displaystyle{+}\underbrace{\alpha\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\lvert C\xi^{(k)}(t,\omega)\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}t}_{E_{2}}.

By the
\lambda
-strong concavity of
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u)
in
u
(with
\lambda=2\beta-M_{P}(H_{A}M_{X}+H_{B})
), for each
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(t))-\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k)}(t))\geq\langle\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(t)),\delta u^{(k)}(t)\rangle+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lvert\delta u^{(k)}(t)\rvert^{2}.

Since
u^{(k+1)}(t)=\argmax_{u\in U}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u)
and
u^{(k)}(t)\in U
, the first-order optimality condition gives
\langle\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(t)),u^{(k)}(t)-u^{(k+1)}(t)\rangle\leq 0
, so the gradient term is non-negative. Integrating over
[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(t))-\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k)}(t))\right)\mathrm{d}t\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\delta u^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

For
E_{1}
, by the uniform bound of
\{p^{(k)}\}
, (
4.6
), and (
4.7
):

\displaystyle\lvert E_{1}\rvert\leq M_{P}G_{A}C_{X}\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\delta u^{(k)}(t)\rvert\int_{0}^{t}\lvert\delta u^{(k)}(s)\rvert\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t.

Setting
F(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\lvert\delta u^{(k)}(s)\rvert\mathrm{d}s
, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives
\int_{0}^{T}F^{\prime}(t)F(t)\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{2}F(T)^{2}
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
F(T)^{2}\leq T\|\delta u^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
, hence

\displaystyle\lvert E_{1}\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2}M_{P}G_{A}C_{X}T\|\delta u^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

For

,

E_{2}\leq\alpha\lvert C\rvert^{2}C_{X}^{2}\frac{T^{2}}{2}\|\delta u^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Thus, we get

(4.8)

J(u^{(k+1)})-J(u^{(k)})\leq-\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}-C_{E}\right)\|\delta u^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\quad C_{E}=\frac{1}{2}M_{P}G_{A}C_{X}T+\frac{1}{2}\alpha\lvert C\rvert^{2}C_{X}^{2}T^{2}

By assumption,

\displaystyle\beta>\beta_{0}=\frac{1}{2}M_{P}(H_{A}M_{X}+H_{B})+C_{E},

then
c:=\lambda/2-C_{E}>0
. Since
J\geq 0
, summing (
4.8
) gives

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|\delta u^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq J(u^{(0)})/c<\infty,

therefore,
\|\delta u^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
.

3. Uniform convergence
\|u^{(k+1)}-u^{(k)}\|\to 0
.
We begin by showing that
\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u)
is equicontinuous in
t
, that is, there exists a modulus of continuity
\theta:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that

(4.9)

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{N}\\
u\in U\end{subarray}}\lvert\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u)-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(s,u)\rvert\leq\theta(\lvert t-s\rvert),\qquad\forall t,s\in[0,T].

Recall that the gradient of
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u)
with respect to

\displaystyle\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u)=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[p^{(k)}(t,\omega)\cdot\left(D_{u}A(t,\omega;u)x^{(k)}(t,\omega)+D_{u}B(t,\omega;u)\right)\right]-2\beta u.

By the forward and backward equations (
4.2
)–(
4.3
), we know that both
x^{(k)}(t,\omega)
and

are uniformly Lipschitz in
t
, that is, for any

,

\lvert x^{(k)}(t,\omega)-x^{(k)}(s,\omega)\rvert\leq L_{X}\lvert t-s\rvert,\quad\lvert p^{(k)}(t,\omega)-p^{(k)}(s,\omega)\rvert\leq L_{P}\lvert t-s\rvert,

where
L_{X}=M_{A}M_{X}+M_{B}
and
L_{P}=M_{A}M_{P}+2\alpha\lvert C\rvert(\lvert C\rvert M_{X}+M_{\Omega^{\prime}})
. Now we fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
u\in U
. For any
t,s\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u)-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(s,u)=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\bigg[\left(p^{(k)}(t,\omega)-p^{(k)}(s,\omega)\right)\cdot D_{u}A(t,\omega;u)x^{(k)}(t,\omega)\,+

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+p^{(k)}(s,\omega)\cdot D_{u}A(t,\omega;u)\left(x^{(k)}(t,\omega)-x^{(k)}(s,\omega)\right)+

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+p^{(k)}(s,\omega)\cdot(D_{u}A(t,\omega;u)-D_{u}A(s,\omega;u))x^{(k)}(s,\omega)\,+

\displaystyle+\left(p^{(k)}(t,\omega)-p^{(k)}(s,\omega)\right)\cdot D_{u}B(t,\omega;u)+p^{(k)}(s,\omega)\cdot(D_{u}B(t,\omega;u)-D_{u}B(s,\omega;u))\bigg].

By the uniform boundedness and equicontinuity of
D_{u}A(\cdot,\omega;u)
,
D_{u}B(\cdot,\omega;u)
,
x^{(k)}(\cdot,\omega)
,
p^{(k)}(\cdot,\omega)
,

\displaystyle\lvert\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u)-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(s,u)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq L_{P}\lvert t-s\rvert G_{A}M_{X}+M_{P}G_{A}L_{X}\lvert t-s\rvert+

\displaystyle+M_{P}\theta_{A}(\lvert t-s\rvert)M_{X}+L_{P}\lvert t-s\rvert G_{B}+M_{P}\theta_{B}(\lvert t-s\rvert).

Then (
4.9
) is proved by defining
\theta(\lvert t-s\rvert)
as the right-hand side of the above inequality, which does not depend on
k
and
u
.

Next, we show that
\{u^{(k)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is equicontinuous on
[0,T]
. Take
t,s\in[0,T]
. Since

u^{(k+1)}(t)=\argmax_{u\in U}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u),

the first-order optimality condition gives

\langle\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(t)),v-u^{(k+1)}(t)\rangle\leq 0,\quad\forall v\in U.

Taking

, we have

(4.10)

\langle\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(t)),u^{(k+1)}(t)-u^{(k+1)}(s)\rangle\geq 0.

Interchanging

and
t
in (
4.10
) gives

(4.11)

-\langle\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(s,u^{(k+1)}(s)),u^{(k+1)}(t)-u^{(k+1)}(s)\rangle\geq 0.

Adding (
4.10
) with (
4.11
), we obtain

\langle\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(t))-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(s,u^{(k+1)}(s)),u^{(k+1)}(t)-u^{(k+1)}(s)\rangle\geq 0.

Rearranging the above inequality,

(4.12)

\displaystyle-\langle\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(t))-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(s)),u^{(k+1)}(t)-u^{(k+1)}(s)\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\langle\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(s))-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(s,u^{(k+1)}(s)),u^{(k+1)}(t)-u^{(k+1)}(s)\rangle.

By the uniform
\lambda
-strong concavity of
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u)
, the left-hand side of (
4.12
) becomes

\displaystyle-\langle\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(t))-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(s)),u^{(k+1)}(t)-u^{(k+1)}(s)\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\lambda\lvert u^{(k+1)}(t)-u^{(k+1)}(s)\rvert^{2}.

By (
4.9
) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the right-hand side of (
4.12
) becomes

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(t,u^{(k+1)}(s))-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}^{(k)}(s,u^{(k+1)}(s)),u^{(k+1)}(t)-u^{(k+1)}(s)\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\theta(\lvert t-s\rvert)\,\lvert u^{(k+1)}(t)-u^{(k+1)}(s)\rvert.

Combining the above inequalities, we reach

\lambda\lvert u^{(k+1)}(t)-u^{(k+1)}(s)\rvert\leq\theta(\lvert t-s\rvert).

As
\lambda>0
, this implies
\{u^{(k+1)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is equicontinuous. Since the first element
u^{(0)}
is uniformly continuous on
[0,T]
by the Heine-Cantor theorem, the entire sequence
\{u^{(k)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is equicontinuous.

Now we prove by contradiction that
\|\delta u^{(k)}\|\to 0
. Suppose there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
, a subsequence
\{\delta u^{(k_{j})}\}
and
\{t_{j}\}\subseteq[0,T]
such that

\displaystyle\lvert\delta u^{(k_{j})}(t_{j})\rvert\geq\epsilon_{0},\qquad\forall j\in\mathbb{N}.

The equicontinuity of
\{u^{(k)}\}
implies the equicontinuity of
\{\delta u^{(k)}\}
, thus there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
\lvert t-s\rvert<\delta
,

\displaystyle\lvert\delta u^{(k)}(t)-\delta u^{(k)}(s)\rvert<\epsilon_{0}/2,\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence, if
\lvert t-t_{j}\rvert<\delta
, then

\displaystyle\lvert\delta u^{(k_{j})}(t)\rvert\geq\lvert\delta u^{(k_{j})}(t_{j})\rvert-\lvert\delta u^{(k_{j})}(t)-\delta u^{(k_{j})}(t_{j})\rvert\geq\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{0}/2=\epsilon_{0}/2,

that is to say, on the interval

I_{j}:=[0,T]\cap(t_{j}-\delta,t_{j}+\delta),

we have
\lvert\delta u^{(k_{j})}(t)\rvert\geq\epsilon_{0}/2
. Note that
\lvert I_{j}\rvert\geq\delta
, then

\displaystyle\|\delta u^{(k_{j})}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\delta u^{(k_{j})}(t)\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}t\geq\int_{I_{j}}\lvert\delta u^{(k_{j})}(t)\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}t\geq\delta(\epsilon_{0}/2)^{2}>0,\qquad\forall j\in\mathbb{N},

a contradiction with
\|\delta u^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
. Therefore,
\|\delta u^{(k)}\|\to 0
.

4. Extraction of convergent subsequences.
Since
U
is compact, which implies that
\{u^{(k)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded on
[0,T]
, by the equicontinuity of
\{u^{(k)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, there exists a subsequence
\{u^{(k_{j})}\}
of
\{u^{(k)}\}
that converges uniformly to a continuous limit
u^{*}
on
[0,T]
. Since
\|u^{(k_{j}+1)}-u^{(k_{j})}\|\to 0
from Step 3, we also have that
\{u^{(k_{j}+1)}\}
converges uniformly to
u^{*}
.

Next we show that the sequence
\{x^{(k_{j})}\}
admits a further subsequence that converges to some
x^{*}
in
C([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Previously, we have shown that
\{x^{(k_{j})}\}
is uniformly bounded by
M_{X}
. By the forward equation (
4.2
),
\{\partial_{t}x^{(k_{j})}\}
is also uniformly bounded. Further, by Lemma
4.1
(2), each
x^{(k_{j})}
is continuously Fréchet differentiable with respect to
\omega
, and
\partial_{\omega}x^{(k_{j})}(t,\omega)
satisfies the first-order variational equation

(4.13)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{z}(t,\omega)=A(t,\omega;u^{(k_{j})}(t))z(t,\omega)+\partial_{\omega}A(t,\omega;u^{(k_{j})}(t))x^{(k_{j})}(t,\omega)+\partial_{\omega}B(t,\omega;u^{(k_{j})}(t)),\\
z(0,\omega)=\partial_{\omega}x_{0}(\omega).\end{cases}

By Assumption
2.1
(7), the functions
(t,\omega)\mapsto\partial_{\omega}A(t,\omega;u^{(k_{j})}(t))
and
(t,\omega)\mapsto\partial_{\omega}B(t,\omega;u^{(k_{j})}(t))
are uniformly bounded. By applying Grönwall’s inequality for the variational equation (
4.13
), we see that
\{\partial_{\omega}x^{(k_{j})}\}
is uniformly bounded. Therefore,
\{x^{(k_{j})}\}
is equicontinuous. By the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, there exists a further subsequence
\{x^{(k_{j_{\ell}})}\}
of
\{x^{(k_{j})}\}
and a function
x^{*}\in C([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

x^{(k_{j_{\ell}})}\to x^{*}\quad\text{uniformly on }[0,T]\times\Omega.

For notational simplicity, we relabel the subsequence
\{x^{(k_{j_{\ell}})}\}
as
\{x^{(k_{j})}\}
.

Similarly,
\{p^{(k_{j})}\}
is uniformly bounded by
M_{P}
, and by the backward equation (
4.3
),
\{\partial_{t}p^{(k_{j})}\}
is also uniformly bounded. By Lemma
4.1
(3), each
p^{(k_{j})}
is continuously Fréchet differentiable with respect to
\omega
, and
\partial_{\omega}p^{(k_{j})}(t,\omega)
satisfies the first-order variational equation

(4.14)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{z}(t,\omega)=-A(t,\omega;u^{(k_{j})}(t))^{\top}z(t,\omega)-\partial_{\omega}A(t,\omega;u^{(k_{j})}(t))^{\top}p^{(k_{j})}(t,\omega)\\
\hskip 60.00009pt+2\alpha C^{\top}(C\partial_{\omega}x^{(k_{j})}(t,\omega)-\partial_{\omega}\mathcal{F}(\omega)(t)),\\
z(T,\omega)=0.\end{cases}

Invoking Grönwall’s inequality for (
4.14
), we know that
\{\partial_{\omega}p^{(k_{j})}\}
is uniformly bounded. Again, using the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, we may assert that, by extracting a subsequence,
p^{(k_{j})}
converges to some
p^{*}\in C([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
uniformly.

5. Passing limit in the maximum condition and forward-backward system.
We show that
(x^{*},p^{*})
solves the forward-backward system corresponding to
u^{*}
and the triple
(x^{*},p^{*},u^{*})
satisfies the maximum condition (
3.3
) in Theorem
3.2
. It is straightforward to show by definition that for any fixed
t\in[0,T]
, the following map

\displaystyle C(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times C(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{m}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(x(\cdot),p(\cdot),u)

\displaystyle\mapsto H(x(\cdot),p(\cdot),u,t)

is continuous, hence we can take the limit
j\to\infty
on the left-hand side of (
4.4
) and get

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}H(x^{(k_{j})}(t,\cdot),p^{(k_{j})}(t,\cdot),u^{(k_{j}+1)}(t),t)=H(x^{*}(t,\cdot),p^{*}(t,\cdot),u^{*}(t),t),\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Since
U
is a compact and convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the map

\displaystyle C(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times C(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(x(\cdot),p(\cdot))

\displaystyle\mapsto\max_{u\in U}H(x(\cdot),p(\cdot),u,t)

is also continuous for any fixed
t\in[0,T]
. We can then take the limit
j\to\infty
on the right-hand side of (
4.4
) and this gives

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\max_{u\in U}H(x^{(k_{j})}(t,\cdot),p^{(k_{j})}(t,\cdot),u,t)=\max_{u\in U}H(x^{*}(t,\cdot),p^{*}(t,\cdot),u,t),\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Combining the above two equalities, we have

\displaystyle H(x^{*}(t,\cdot),p^{*}(t,\cdot),u^{*}(t),t)=\max_{u\in U}H(x^{*}(t,\cdot),p^{*}(t,\cdot),u,t),\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

By taking the limit
j\to\infty
in the integral form of the forward and backward equations and invoking the bounded convergence theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x^{*}(t,\omega)=x_{0}(\omega)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(A(s,\omega;u^{*}(s))x^{*}(s,\omega)+B(s,\omega;u^{*}(s))\right)\mathrm{d}s,\\
p^{*}(t,\omega)=\int_{T}^{t}\left(-A(s,\omega;u^{*}(s))^{\top}p^{*}(s,\omega)+2\alpha C^{\top}(Cx^{*}(s,\omega)-\mathcal{F}(\omega)(s))\right)\mathrm{d}s,\\
\end{cases}\forall t\in[0,T].

Since the integrands on the right-hand side of the above equations are integrable on
[0,T]
, it follows that
x^{*}
and
p^{*}
are differentiable a.e. in
t
. By taking the derivative, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{x}^{*}(t,\omega)=A(t,\omega;u^{*}(t))x^{*}(t,\omega)+B(t,\omega;u^{*}(t)),\text{ a.e. }t\in[0,T],\\
x^{*}(0,\omega)=x_{0}(\omega),\\
\dot{p}^{*}(t,\omega)=-A(t,\omega;u^{*}(t))^{\top}p^{*}(t,\omega)+2\alpha C^{\top}(Cx^{*}(t,\omega)-\mathcal{F}(\omega)(t)),\text{ a.e. }t\in[0,T],\\
p^{*}(T,\omega)=0.\end{cases}

Therefore, we conclude that
x^{*}
and
p^{*}
are the state and adjoint variables that correspond to
u^{*}
, and that the triple
(x^{*},p^{*},u^{*})
satisfies the necessary conditions given by Pontryagin’s maximum principle.
∎

5.
Sufficient Conditions for Optimality

In this section, we establish sufficient conditions under which an admissible control constitutes an optimal solution to both problems (
P
) and (
PS
). In particular, we prove that the limit control generated by Algorithm
1
in Theorem
4.1
, which satisfies the necessary conditions stated in Theorem
3.2
, is indeed optimal when both the weighting parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
are chosen to be sufficiently large. These results provide a theoretical justification for the efficacy of the proposed iterative scheme and ensure that, under appropriate weighting, the resulting control achieves global optimality.

The following lemma is adapted from
[
20
, Chapter 3, Lemma 2.4]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\varphi
be a convex or concave function on
X\times U
where
(X,\|\cdot\|)
is a Banach space and
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a convex body. Assume that
\varphi(x,u)
is differentiable in
x
and
\varphi_{x}(x,u)
is continuous in
(x,u)
. Then

\displaystyle\bigg\{(\varphi_{x}(x^{*},u^{*}),r)\mid r\in\partial_{u}\varphi(x^{*},u^{*})\bigg\}\subseteq\partial_{x,u}\varphi(x^{*},u^{*}),\quad\forall(x^{*},u^{*})\in X\times U,

where
\partial_{u}\varphi(x^{*},u^{*})
and
\partial_{x,u}\varphi(x^{*},u^{*})
are Clarke’s generalized (partial) gradients of
\varphi
at
(x^{*},u^{*})
.

Proof.

First, we assume that
\varphi
is convex. For any
\xi\in X
and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we choose a sequence
\{(x_{i},h_{i})\}\subseteq X\times\mathbb{R}
in the following way:

\displaystyle(x_{i},u^{*})\in X\times U,\quad(x_{i}+h_{i}\xi,u^{*}+h_{i}u)\in X\times U,

\displaystyle h_{i}\downarrow 0\text{ as }i\to\infty,\text{ and }\|x_{i}-x^{*}\|\leq h_{i}^{2}.

By the convexity of
\varphi
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}

\displaystyle\dfrac{\varphi(x_{i}+h_{i}\xi,u^{*}+h_{i}u)-\varphi(x^{*},u^{*}+h_{i}u)}{h_{i}}

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{i\to\infty}\dfrac{\langle\varphi_{x}(x^{*},u^{*}+h_{i}u),x_{i}-x^{*}+h_{i}\xi\rangle}{h_{i}}=\langle\varphi_{x}(x^{*},u^{*}),\xi\rangle.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\dfrac{\varphi(x^{*},u^{*}+h_{i}u)-\varphi(x^{*},u^{*})}{h_{i}}\geq\langle r,u\rangle.

Also,

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\dfrac{\varphi(x^{*},u^{*})-\varphi(x_{i},u^{*})}{h_{i}}\geq\lim_{i\to\infty}\dfrac{\langle\varphi_{x}(x_{i},u^{*}),x^{*}-x_{i}\rangle}{h_{i}}=0.

Adding up the above three inequalities, we get

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\dfrac{\varphi(x_{i}+h_{i}\xi,u^{*}+h_{i}u)-\varphi(x_{i},u^{*})}{h_{i}}\geq\langle\varphi_{x}(x^{*},u^{*}),\xi\rangle+\langle r,u\rangle.

Thus
(\varphi_{x}(x^{*},u^{*}),r)\in\partial_{x,u}\varphi(x^{*},u^{*})
by the definition of the generalized gradient. In the case where
\varphi
is concave, the desired result follows immediately by noting that
-\varphi
is convex.
∎

\displaystyle\leq\lvert x_{0}(\omega+h)-x_{0}(\omega)-\partial_{\omega}x_{0}(\omega)[h]\rvert+

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle\lvert B(s,\omega+h;u(s))-B(s,\omega;u(s))-\partial_{\omega}B(s,\omega;u(s))[h]\rvert\,\mathrm{d}s+

\displaystyle+M_{X}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\lvert A(s,\omega+h;u(s))-A(s,\omega;u(s))-\partial_{\omega}A(s,\omega;u(s))[h]\rvert\,\mathrm{d}s+

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle\lvert A(s,\omega+h;u(s))-A(s,\omega;u(s))\rvert\cdot\lvert x(s,\omega+h)-x(s,\omega)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}s+

\displaystyle+M_{A}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\lvert x(s,\omega+h)-x(s,\omega)-z(s,\omega)[h]\rvert\,\mathrm{d}s.

Dividing both sides by
\|h\|
and by Grönwall’s inequality,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\lvert x(t,\omega+h)-x(t,\omega)-z(t,\omega)[h]\rvert}{\|h\|}

\displaystyle\leq e^{M_{A}t}\bigg(\dfrac{\lvert x_{0}(\omega+h)-x_{0}(\omega)-\partial_{\omega}x_{0}(\omega)[h]\rvert}{\|h\|}+

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle\dfrac{\lvert B(s,\omega+h;u(s))-B(s,\omega;u(s))-\partial_{\omega}B(s,\omega;u(s))[h]\rvert}{\|h\|}\,\mathrm{d}s+

\displaystyle+M_{X}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{\lvert A(s,\omega+h;u(s))-A(s,\omega;u(s))-\partial_{\omega}A(s,\omega;u(s))[h]\rvert}{\|h\|}\,\mathrm{d}s+

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle\dfrac{\lvert A(s,\omega+h;u(s))-A(s,\omega;u(s))\rvert}{\|h\|}\cdot\lvert x(s,\omega+h)-x(s,\omega)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg).

Since
x_{0}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Fréchet differentiable,

\displaystyle\lim_{\|h\|\to 0}\dfrac{\lvert x_{0}(\omega+h)-x_{0}(\omega)-\partial_{\omega}x_{0}(\omega)[h]\rvert}{\|h\|}=0.

By Assumption
2.1
(7) and the dominated convergence theorem, as
\|h\|\to 0
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{\lvert B(s,\omega+h;u(s))-B(s,\omega;u(s))-\partial_{\omega}B(s,\omega;u(s))[h]\rvert}{\|h\|}\,\mathrm{d}s\to 0,

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{\lvert A(s,\omega+h;u(s))-A(s,\omega;u(s))-\partial_{\omega}A(s,\omega;u(s))[h]\rvert}{\|h\|}\,\mathrm{d}s\to 0.

Finally, notice that,

\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\dfrac{\lvert A(s,\omega+h;u(s))-A(s,\omega;u(s))\rvert}{\|h\|}\leq\sup_{s\in[0,t],\tau\in[0,1]}\|\partial_{\omega}A(s,\omega+\tau h;u(s))\|_{\mathrm{op}},

and, by Assumption
2.1
(7), the right-hand side of the inequality is bounded by

\sup_{(s,\omega^{\prime},u)\in[0,T]\times\tilde{\Omega}\times U}\|\partial_{\omega}A(s,\omega^{\prime};u)\|_{\mathrm{op}}<\infty

whenever
\omega+h\in B
. In the previous step, we have shown that

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\lvert x(s,\omega+h)-x(s,\omega)\rvert\to 0\text{ as }\|h\|\to 0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{\lvert A(s,\omega+h;u(s))-A(s,\omega;u(s))\rvert}{\|h\|}\cdot\lvert x(s,\omega+h)-x(s,\omega)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}s\to 0\text{ as }\|h\|\to 0.

Combining the above results gives (
A.2
). This means
x(t,\omega)
is differentiable with respect to
\omega\in\Omega
and
\partial_{\omega}x(t,\omega)
satisfies the first-order variational equation (
4.1
). As in the first step, by applying the Grönwall’s inequality to (
4.1
) and using the fact the Fréchet derivative
\partial_{\omega}x_{0}
is continuous, one may show that
\partial_{\omega}x(t,\omega)
is continuous with respect to
\omega
uniformly in
t
. Moreover, for each
\omega

\partial_{\omega}x(t,\omega)
is continuous with respect to
t
as the solution of (
4.1
). Therefore,
\partial_{\omega}x(t,\omega)
is jointly continuous in
(t,\omega)
. This completes the proof of (2) since all partial derivatives, including the one with respect to
t
