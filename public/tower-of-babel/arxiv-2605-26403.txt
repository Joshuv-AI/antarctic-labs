From Static Context to Calibrated Interactive RL: Mitigating Distribution Shift in Multi-turn Dialogue with Aligned Simulator
Xiaohua Wang Jiakang Yuan Zisu Huang Muzhao Tian Changze Lv Kaitao Song Chen Tao Xiaoqing Zheng Fudan University xhwang24@m.fudan.edu.cn

Abstract
A long-standing goal of the research community is to develop highly interactive LLM-based dialogue agents. Recent research focuses on optimizing policies based on fixed offline logs (Static Context RL) or using a prompt-based simulator (Interactive RL). In this work, we theoretically show that both paradigms are fundamentally limited by context distribution shift—a mismatch between dialogue histories observed during training and those encountered in real conversations. This shift compounds quadratically over turns and severely degrades dialogue quality. Specifically, we attribute this shift to two distinct sources: (i) policy-induced shift, arising from training on static histories rather than self-generated trajectories; and (ii) simulator-induced shift, stemming from discrepancies between simulated and real human behaviors. To address these challenges, we propose Calibrated Interactive RL, a unified framework that couples interactive RL with simulator alignment. By aligning the simulator with human interaction patterns, our approach reduces the sim-to-real gap and mitigates compounding distribution shifts. Experiments across multiple dialogue tasks confirm our theoretical analysis: (i) Interactive RL significantly outperforms the Static Context baseline by mitigating policy distribution shift; and (ii) calibrating simulators with our alignment method further bridges the sim-to-real gap, yielding state-of-the-art downstream performance.

In this work, we establish the theoretical foundations for addressing these challenges. We formally demonstrate that existing paradigms are fundamentally constrained by context distribution shift—a discrepancy between dialogue histories observed during training and those encountered in real conversations. We show that this shift compounds quadratically over turns, severely degrading dialogue quality. Specifically, we decompose this phenomenon into two distinct sources: (i)
Policy-induced shift

Simulator-induced shift
arises from the structural divergence between prompt-based simulators and real human behaviors.

To this end, we introduce
Calibrated Interactive RL
, a unified framework that couples interactive RL with the aligned simulator. Specifically, we first align the user simulator using Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on real human multi-turn interactions, enabling it to capture realistic human behaviors such as clarification-seeking, stubbornness, and strict goal-adherence. Building upon this calibrated simulator, we leverage interactive RL to optimize the policy on self-generated trajectories, thereby explicitly addressing the policy-induced distribution shift and allowing the agent to learn essential error-recovery skills.

Empirically, our analysis reveals that standard prompt-based simulators exhibit severe behavioral artifacts, most notably

This effectively trivializes the RL interaction loop and inevitably leads to reward hacking, where the policy learns to exploit the simulator’s permissiveness rather than solving the actual task. By treating the simulator as a trainable agent and performing behavioral cloning on high-quality offline logs, our Simulator Alignment framework rigorously grounds the simulation in human reality.
Our experiments demonstrate that Interactive RL consistently outperforms the Static Context baseline and that training with our Aligned Simulators yields further significant gains. These results highlight the need for both closing the interaction loop and explicitly grounding simulations.

2.2
Distribution Shift and Exposure Bias

The discrepancy between training on ground-truth history (
i.e.
, teacher forcing) and generating on the model’s own history (
i.e.
, student forcing) is a fundamental challenge in sequence generation, known as exposure bias

21
.
In the context of decision-making, this phenomenon parallels the distribution shift analyzed in imitation learning. Ross et al.
22

i.e.
, offline training) results in errors that accumulate quadratically (
O(H^{2})

i.e.

Dagger

While recent works have analyzed this shift in the context of single-turn reasoning chains

, our work formally extends this analysis to the multi-turn dialogue setting. We derive a rigorous bound illustrating how static context training fails to generalize to the dynamic distribution induced by the policy’s own actions during prolonged interactions.

In this section, we formally analyze the distribution shifts inherent in multi-turn dialogue training. We derive two fundamental bounds that motivate our proposed
Calibrated Interactive RL
framework. First, we show that standard offline training (
i.e.
,
Static Context RL
) induces a biased objective due to compounding policy deviations. Second, we demonstrate that while
Interactive RL
addresses this by closing the interaction loop, it relies on simulator fidelity, as simulator errors also accumulate quadratically. These insights underscore the necessity of our Simulator Alignment approach. Detailed proofs are provided in Appendix

.

3.1
Setup and Notation

We consider an episodic dialogue environment with a finite horizon
H
. Let
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
denote the environment state and
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
the dialogue agent’s action at time
t
. A history
h_{t}=(s_{0},a_{0},\dots,s_{t})
represents the trajectory up to time
t
. Policies map histories to distributions over actions; we denote the behavior policy that generated the offline logs as
\pi_{\text{data}}
, and the learned policy as
\pi
.

Let
d^{\pi}_{t}(h)
be the marginal distribution over histories at time
t
induced by policy
\pi
. To analyze the simulation gap, we distinguish between the transition dynamics of the real user, denoted by the operator
\mathcal{T}_{\text{real}}
, and the user simulator,
\mathcal{T}_{\text{sim}}
. We assume rewards are bounded such that
|r(s,a)|\leq R_{\max}
. The objective is to maximize the finite-horizon return
J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_{\pi}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}r_{t}\right]
.

Based on the notation,
Static Context RL
decouples policy optimization from state generation. Its updates and gradient estimators are computed using contexts
h\sim d^{\text{data}}_{t}
sampled from the offline distribution
d^{\text{data}}_{t}
, rather than the on-policy distribution
d^{\pi}_{t}
. Consequently, the optimization is fundamentally off-policy with respect to the state distribution, as it ignores how
\pi
influences future histories.
On the contrary,
Interactive RL
maintains the closed interaction loop. The objective is evaluated under the distribution
h\sim d^{\pi}_{t}
induced by the policy’s interaction with the environment, thereby capturing the consequences of its own actions.

3.2
Error Source I: Compounding Policy Shift in Static Context RL

We first quantify the performance degradation caused by training on static contexts. Let
\pi^{\prime}
denote a reference policy (
e.g.
, the data-generating policy
\pi_{\text{data}}
) and
\pi
denote the learned policy. We define two divergence terms: the
history distribution mismatch

\Delta_{t}=\|d^{\pi}_{t}-d^{\pi^{\prime}}_{t}\|_{1}
, and the
per-step policy deviation

\varepsilon_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{h\sim d^{\pi^{\prime}}_{t}}\|\pi(\cdot\mid h)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot\mid h)\|_{1}
.

Theorem 3.1

(Performance Difference via Policy Shift)
.

For any two policies
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
and finite horizon
H
, the difference in expected return is bounded by:

\small|J(\pi)-J(\pi^{\prime})|\leq R_{\max}\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}(\Delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}).

(1)

Furthermore, since
\Delta_{t}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\varepsilon_{i}
, the total error satisfies:

\small|J(\pi)-J(\pi^{\prime})|\leq R_{\max}\sum_{i=0}^{H-1}(H-i)\varepsilon_{i}.

(2)

Proof Sketch.
We decompose the return difference into a sum over time of divergences in the joint (history, action) distributions. As the environment transition operator is non-expansive in the
\ell_{1}
-norm, distribution errors propagate linearly, yielding
\Delta_{t+1}\leq\Delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}
. Summing this recurrence yields the quadratic bound. Detailed proofs are provided in
Appendix

A
.

Theoretical Implication.
Theorem
3.1
establishes that errors in Static Context RL compound quadratically (
O(H^{2})
). Small deviations
\varepsilon_{t}
in the policy at early turns accumulate into a large distribution shift
\Delta_{t}
, pushing the agent into out-of-distribution states where it has never been trained. Static Context RL ignores
\Delta_{t}
, optimizing only for local accuracy, and thus fails to guarantee long-horizon interactive performance.

Intuitive Explanation & Running Example.
To intuitively understand this quadratic compounding effect, consider a conversational math tutoring scenario. Suppose the agent makes a minor logical error at turn
t=1
(a small policy deviation
\varepsilon_{1}
). In a real interaction, the subsequent dialogue history will fundamentally diverge from the ground-truth offline logs (creating a large history mismatch
\Delta_{2}
), because the human user will likely point out the mistake or express confusion. However, a model trained purely via Static Context RL has only seen perfect, expert-guided trajectory histories. When thrust into this self-generated, flawed context during deployment, the agent lacks the essential
error-recovery
skills. Instead of correcting itself, it is prone to hallucinate further (yielding large
\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3},\dots
). Consequently, an initially negligible mistake cascades dynamically over the horizon
H
, leading to catastrophic failure. Interactive RL mitigates this by explicitly exposing the agent to its own mistakes during training, thereby teaching it how to navigate back to the correct path.

3.3
Error Source II: Simulator-Real Distribution Shift

While Interactive RL resolves the policy distribution shift by training on
d^{\pi}_{t}
, it introduces a new source of error: the discrepancy between the simulator
\mathcal{T}_{\text{sim}}
and the real user
\mathcal{T}_{\text{real}}
. To isolate the impact of simulator fidelity, we analyze the gap for a fixed policy
\pi
. We define the
simulator error
at time
t
as the expected divergence in transition dynamics under the real joint distribution:
\delta_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{(h,a)\sim q^{\text{real}}_{t}}\|\mathcal{T}_{\text{real}}(\cdot|h,a)-\mathcal{T}_{\text{sim}}(\cdot|h,a)\|_{1}
, where
q^{\text{real}}_{t}(h,a)=d^{\pi}_{\text{real},t}(h)\pi(a|h)
.

Theorem 3.2

(Sim-to-Real Gap)
.

For any policy
\pi
and horizon
H
, the performance gap caused by the simulator is bounded by:

\small|J_{\text{real}}(\pi)-J_{\text{sim}}(\pi)|\leq R_{\max}\sum_{i=0}^{H-1}(H-i)\delta_{i}.

(3)

Proof Sketch
We upper-bound the performance gap between the real and simulated environments by aggregating the marginal distribution shifts over the horizon. Leveraging the non-expansive nature of stochastic transition operators under the
\ell_{1}
-norm, we show that the distribution divergence at any step is bounded by the sum of the propagated error from the previous step and the immediate local simulation error. This leads to the recurrence
\Delta_{t+1}^{\text{env}}\leq\Delta_{t}^{\text{env}}+\delta_{t}
, which telescopes to yield the final cumulative bound. The complete derivation is detailed in
Appendix

A
.

Theoretical Implication.
This result highlights that simulator misalignment is as detrimental as policy misalignment. Even if interactive training is used, a simulator that deviates from human behavior (i.e.,
\delta_{t}>0
) causes the training objective to diverge from the real-world objective at a rate of
O(H^{2}\delta)
. This formally justifies our
Simulator Alignment
approach: minimizing
\delta_{t}
is a theoretical prerequisite for robust interactive learning.

Intuitive Explanation & Running Example.
To see why standard interactive training is insufficient without simulator calibration, consider the well-documented behavioral artifacts of uncalibrated LLM simulators, such as “sycophancy” (blind agreement)
28
. Suppose the dialogue agent proposes a highly flawed document edit. A real human user would reject the edit and demand a revision (representing a specific transition in
\mathcal{T}_{\text{real}}
). In contrast, a naive, uncalibrated simulator might enthusiastically accept the flawed edit, complimenting the agent (a severely divergent transition in
\mathcal{T}_{\text{sim}}
, yielding a large local error
\delta_{t}
). By interacting with this flawed simulator over multiple turns, the agent receives false-positive feedback and learns a degenerate policy that exploits the simulator’s permissiveness rather than actually solving the task—a phenomenon closely related to
reward hacking

36
. As shown by our bound, this simulator-induced delusion accumulates quadratically over the conversation length
H
. Therefore, aligning the simulator to accurately reflect human stubbornness, constraints, and feedback patterns is not merely an engineering trick, but a fundamental mathematical requirement to ensure that the surrogate objective
J_{\text{sim}}(\pi)
faithfully proxies the true real-world objective
J_{\text{real}}(\pi)
.

4
Calibrated Interactive RL

Motivated by the compounding distribution shifts identified in
Theorem

3.1
and
Theorem

3.2
, it is evident that a robust multi-turn dialogue agent must simultaneously address both policy-induced and simulator-induced errors. To this end, we propose
Calibrated Interactive RL
. As shown in
Figure

1
(b), our method operates in two distinct phases: (1)
Simulator Calibration
, where we train a goal-guided user simulator via Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) to mimic human interaction patterns; and (2)
Interactive Policy Optimization
, where the dialogue agent is trained within this calibrated environment using memory-efficient RL to maximize task success.

4.1
Phase I: Simulator Calibration via Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

The primary bottleneck in standard interactive RL is the
simulator-induced shift
(
\delta_{t}
). Conventional prompt-based simulators frequently fail to exhibit the specific intent, cognitive blind spots, or "stubbornness" of real users. To minimize this sim-to-real gap, we formulate the simulator as a trainable agent and explicitly align it with human behavioral distributions using historical multi-turn data.

Goal-Guided Simulation Context.
Unlike the dialogue policy, which must infer the user’s intent dynamically, the user simulator functions as an environmental "Teacher" and thus requires access to the full ground truth. Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y^{*},h_{\text{real}})\}
denote a dataset containing the initial instruction
x
, the ground-truth solution
y^{*}
, and the reference human history
h_{\text{real}}
. We condition the simulator
\pi_{\phi}^{\text{sim}}
on an oracle context
I_{t}^{\text{sim}}=[x,y^{*},h_{t},a_{t}]
. This privileged information allows the simulator to consistently verify whether the agent’s current action
a_{t}
aligns with the target
y^{*}
and to provide feedback or corrections consistent with the reference intent, without hallucinating diverging goals.

Calibration via Behavioral Cloning.
To ensure the simulator accurately reflects human transition dynamics
\mathcal{T}_{\text{real}}
, we optimize the simulator parameters
\phi
using Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on the real user turns from
\mathcal{D}
. Specifically, we minimize the negative log-likelihood (NLL) of the reference human responses
u_{t}
given the oracle context and the dialogue history up to turn
t
:

\small\mathcal{L}_{\text{Calibration}}(\phi)=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{*},h_{\text{real}},a_{\text{real}})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\sum_{t}\log\pi_{\phi}^{\text{sim}}(u_{t}\mid x,y^{*},h_{t},a_{t})\right]

(4)

By conditioning on both the ground-truth and the agent’s actual output the simulator learns to provide grounded feedback, such as asking for clarification, expressing partial understanding, or persistently rejecting incorrect solutions.

Mitigating Simulator Artifacts and Reward Hacking.
The motivation for SFT calibration extends beyond mere behavioral realism; it also provides a critical defense against
reward hacking
36
. Uncalibrated simulators frequently exhibit "sycophancy" (blindly agreeing with flawed logic
28
), which RL agents rapidly exploit by generating confident but incorrect responses to trick the simulator into emitting unearned success signals. By rigorously grounding the simulator in actual human data, we eliminate these exploitable artifacts, ensuring the policy must make genuine progress to achieve high rewards.

4.2
Phase II: Interactive Policy Optimization

With the calibrated simulator
\pi^{\text{sim}}_{\phi^{*}}
frozen, we proceed to optimize the dialogue policy
\pi_{\theta}
. This phase closes the interaction loop to address the
policy-induced shift
(
\Delta_{t}
).

On-Policy Trajectory Generation.

In contrast to Static Context RL, our agent generates full multi-turn trajectories by interacting dynamically with the aligned simulator. For an episode of length
H
, the joint trajectory
\tau=(s_{0},a_{0},u_{0},\dots,a_{H-1},u_{H-1})
is generated via alternating execution, as summarized in Algorithm
1
. This ensures that the policy
\pi_{\theta}
is trained on the exact state distribution
d^{\pi_{\theta}}_{t}
it dynamically visits, allowing it to naturally develop robust error-recovery and long-horizon planning strategies that are fundamentally unlearnable from static, off-policy logs.

Sparse Task Rewards.

The quality of the interaction is measured solely by outcome success. We utilize a sparse reward signal
R_{\text{task}}
administered only at the final turn
H
. Let
\hat{y}
be the final output produced by the agent (e.g., the refined document or final code):

\small R(\tau)=\begin{cases}\text{Score}(\hat{y},y^{*})&\text{at }t=H\\
0&\text{at }t<H\end{cases}

(5)

For document editing tasks,
\text{Score}(\cdot)
is the BLEU metric; for math and coding tasks, it is a binary correctness indicator. This outcome-based supervision forces the agent to engage in meaningful information seeking. The policy
\pi_{\theta}
is then optimized using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
27
to maximize the expected return
\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[R(\tau)]
.

5
Experiments

We conduct extensive experiments to validate two core hypotheses: (1)
Interactive RL
significantly outperforms Static Context RL by mitigating the distribution shift between offline training contexts and on-policy generation; and (2)
Simulator Alignment
is critical for interactive training—optimizing the simulator to mimic human intent and natural interaction patterns leads to superior downstream policy performance.

5.1
Experimental Setup

Datasets and Benchmarks.

To rigorously evaluate the dynamic interaction capabilities of our framework, we employ two challenging multi-turn dialogue benchmarks inspired by recent collaborative agent evaluations
37
. These datasets are explicitly designed to test long-horizon planning, ambiguity resolution, and error recovery:

•

MediumDocEdit-Chat (Collaborative Editing):
Derived from document editing corpora, this task requires the agent to help a user iteratively refine a document. Unlike single-turn instruction following, user intents in this dataset are frequently ambiguous or incomplete. The agent must proactively ask clarifying questions, propose partial edits, and adapt to shifting user feedback over multiple turns. The primary objective evaluation metric is
BLEU
, calculated between the final model-refined document and the ground-truth target text.

•

MATH-Chat (Conversational Reasoning):
Adapted from the MATH dataset, this benchmark evaluates the agent’s ability to act as an interactive tutor. Instead of directly outputting the final answer, the agent must guide a simulated student (who may make logical errors or express confusion) step-by-step toward the correct solution. This requires robust reasoning scaffolding and the ability to correct trajectory deviations dynamically. The task success is measured by the final answer
Accuracy (Acc.)
.

Models and Baselines.

To ensure a fair and controlled comparison, we utilize
Gemma-3-4B-IT

9
as the foundational backbone for both the policy agent and the user simulator across all experiments. We systematically compare our full
Calibrated Interactive RL
framework against established paradigms and internal ablations, seamlessly mirroring the theoretical sources of distribution shift (as summarized in Table
2
):

•

Gemma-3-4B-IT (Base Model):
The original instruction-tuned model without any multi-turn RL fine-tuning, acting as our zero-shot baseline.

•

CollabLLM
(Static RL):
The state-of-the-art offline multi-turn RL method
37
. We faithfully reproduce this baseline using the official codebase, optimizing the Gemma-3 policy on static conversational logs using Multi-turn-aware Rewards. This represents the upper bound of the
Static Context RL
paradigm.

•

Static Context:
An ablation of our method where the policy is trained exclusively on fixed offline history distributions (
h\sim d^{\text{data}}
) without dynamic simulator rollouts.

•

Ours (Naive Interactive):
An ablation utilizing the full Interactive RL loop, but employing a basic, uncalibrated user simulator (prompting the Base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
19
model without Phase I SFT). This isolates the impact of closing the interaction loop from the simulator’s behavioral fidelity.

•

Ours (Calibrated Interactive):
Our complete proposed framework, featuring on-policy trajectory generation against the Phase I SFT-aligned user simulator.

•

Oracle (Proxy Human):
An evaluation upper-bound representing the performance of the base policy when interacting with a privileged proxy human simulator that has direct access to the ground-truth targets.

Conclusion

In this work, we established the theoretical foundations for the limitations of Static Context RL, formally proving that training on fixed offline logs induces a context distribution shift where errors compound quadratically over turns. To address this fundamental bottleneck, we proposed
Calibrated Interactive RL
, a unified framework that couples interactive training with rigorous simulator alignment. We demonstrated that simply closing the interaction loop is insufficient; the success of interactive learning critically depends on minimizing the sim-to-real gap. By treating the user simulator as a trainable agent and optimizing it to maximize a discriminator-derived realism reward, we effectively aligned the simulation environment with human interaction patterns. Our extensive experiments confirm that our framework not only mitigates policy-induced distribution shifts but also fosters robust information-seeking behaviors, significantly outperforming baselines relying on static contexts or uncalibrated simulators. This work highlights the necessity of high-fidelity, aligned simulation as a prerequisite for the next generation of interactive dialogue agents.

Appendix A
Theoretical Proofs and Derivations

In this appendix, we provide full proofs for the theoretical claims presented in
Section

3
. We first restate the necessary notation and assumptions, then establish auxiliary lemmas regarding distributional divergence, and finally derive the bounds for Policy Mismatch (
Theorem

3.1
) and the Sim-to-Real Gap (
Theorem

3.2

A.1
Notation and Preliminaries

We consider a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP) defined over histories to account for the non-Markovian nature of dialogue when viewed from the perspective of state-only transitions.

•

Horizon:

H\in\mathbb{N}
. Time steps are indexed by
t=0,\dots,H-1
.

•

History:
Let
h_{t}=(s_{0},a_{0},\dots,s_{t})\in\mathcal{H}
denote the interaction history up to time
t
.

•

Distributions:
Let
d^{\pi}_{t}(h)
denote the marginal distribution over histories at time
t
induced by policy
\pi
and the environment dynamics.

•

Norms:
Unless otherwise specified,
\|\cdot\|_{1}
denotes the
\ell_{1}
-norm (total variation distance) for probability distributions. For a function
f
,
\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x}|f(x)|
.

•

Rewards:
We assume a bounded reward function
r(h,a)
such that
|r(h,a)|\leq R_{\max}
for all
h,a
.

A.2
Auxiliary Lemmas

We begin by establishing a decomposition lemma that bounds the divergence of a joint distribution in terms of the marginal distribution divergence and the conditional distribution divergence.

Lemma A.1

(Joint Distribution Decomposition)
.

Consider two joint distributions
p(x,y)=p(x)p(y|x)
and
q(x,y)=q(x)q(y|x)
over discrete domains
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
. Then:

\|p-q\|_{1}\leq\|p(x)-q(x)\|_{1}+\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q(x)}[\|p(y|x)-q(y|x)\|_{1}].

(6)

Proof.

By definition of the
\ell_{1}
-norm:

\displaystyle\|p-q\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}|p(x)p(y|x)-q(x)q(y|x)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}|p(x)p(y|x)-q(x)p(y|x)+q(x)p(y|x)-q(x)q(y|x)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x,y}|p(x)-q(x)|p(y|x)+\sum_{x,y}q(x)|p(y|x)-q(y|x)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}|p(x)-q(x)|\underbrace{\sum_{y}p(y|x)}_{=1}+\sum_{x}q(x)\sum_{y}|p(y|x)-q(y|x)|

\displaystyle=\|p(x)-q(x)\|_{1}+\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q(x)}[\|p(y|x)-q(y|x)\|_{1}].

∎

Lemma A.2

(Error Propagation Dynamics)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be the environment transition operator mapping the joint distribution at time
t
to the marginal history distribution at
t+1
. Let
\Delta_{t}=\|d^{\pi}_{t}-d^{\pi^{\prime}}_{t}\|_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{h\sim d^{\pi^{\prime}}_{t}}\|\pi(\cdot|h)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|h)\|_{1}
. Assuming the start state distribution is fixed (
\Delta_{0}=0
), then for all
t\geq 0
:

\Delta_{t+1}\leq\Delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}\quad\implies\quad\Delta_{t}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\varepsilon_{i}.

(7)

Proof.

Let
q^{\pi}_{t}(h,a)=d^{\pi}_{t}(h)\pi(a|h)
be the joint distribution at time
t
. The transition to the next history
h_{t+1}
is deterministic given
h_{t},a_{t}
and the stochastic environment transition
s_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})
. The operator mapping
q_{t}\to d_{t+1}
is a linear map represented by a stochastic matrix, making it non-expansive in the
\ell_{1}
-norm. Thus:

\Delta_{t+1}=\|d^{\pi}_{t+1}-d^{\pi^{\prime}}_{t+1}\|_{1}\leq\|q^{\pi}_{t}-q^{\pi^{\prime}}_{t}\|_{1}.

Applying Lemma
A.1
to the RHS:

\|q^{\pi}_{t}-q^{\pi^{\prime}}_{t}\|_{1}\leq\|d^{\pi}_{t}-d^{\pi^{\prime}}_{t}\|_{1}+\mathbb{E}_{h\sim d^{\pi^{\prime}}_{t}}\|\pi(\cdot|h)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|h)\|_{1}=\Delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}.

Combining these gives the recurrence
\Delta_{t+1}\leq\Delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}
. Telescoping the sum from
i=0
to
t-1
with
\Delta_{0}=0
yields the result.
∎

A.3
Proof of Theorem
3.1
(Performance Difference via Policy Shift)

Theorem
3.1
.

For any two policies
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
over finite horizon
H
, the difference in expected return is bounded by:

|J(\pi)-J(\pi^{\prime})|\leq R_{\max}\sum_{i=0}^{H-1}(H-i)\varepsilon_{i}.

Proof.

The difference in expected return can be written as the sum of differences in expected rewards at each time step. Let
q^{\pi}_{t}
denote the joint distribution of
(h,a)
at time
t
under policy
\pi
.

\displaystyle|J(\pi)-J(\pi^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{(h,a)\sim q^{\pi}_{t}}[r(h,a)]-\mathbb{E}_{(h,a)\sim q^{\pi^{\prime}}_{t}}[r(h,a)]\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\left|\sum_{h,a}(q^{\pi}_{t}(h,a)-q^{\pi^{\prime}}_{t}(h,a))r(h,a)\right|.

Using Hölder’s inequality (bounding the expectation difference by the
\ell_{1}
distance and max reward):

\left|\sum_{h,a}(q^{\pi}_{t}(h,a)-q^{\pi^{\prime}}_{t}(h,a))r(h,a)\right|\leq R_{\max}\|q^{\pi}_{t}-q^{\pi^{\prime}}_{t}\|_{1}.

From the proof of Lemma
A.2
, we know
\|q^{\pi}_{t}-q^{\pi^{\prime}}_{t}\|_{1}\leq\Delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}
. Thus:

|J(\pi)-J(\pi^{\prime})|\leq R_{\max}\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}(\Delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}).

Substituting
\Delta_{t}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\varepsilon_{j}
:

\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}(\Delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t})=\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\varepsilon_{j}+\varepsilon_{t}\right)=\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\sum_{j=0}^{t}\varepsilon_{j}.

By changing the order of summation, the term
\varepsilon_{i}
appears for every
t\geq i
:

\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\sum_{j=0}^{t}\varepsilon_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{H-1}(H-i)\varepsilon_{i}.

Multiplying by
R_{\max}
concludes the proof.
∎

A.4
Proof of Theorem
3.2
(Sim-to-Real Gap)

Theorem
3.2
.

For any fixed policy
\pi
, let
\delta_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{(h,a)\sim q^{\text{real}}_{t}}\|\mathcal{T}_{\text{real}}(\cdot|h,a)-\mathcal{T}_{\text{sim}}(\cdot|h,a)\|_{1}
. Then:

|J_{\text{real}}(\pi)-J_{\text{sim}}(\pi)|\leq R_{\max}\sum_{i=0}^{H-1}(H-i)\delta_{i}.

Proof.

Let
d^{\text{real}}_{t}
and
d^{\text{sim}}_{t}
denote the history distributions induced by
\pi
in the real and simulated environments, respectively. Let
\Delta^{\text{env}}_{t}=\|d^{\text{real}}_{t}-d^{\text{sim}}_{t}\|_{1}
.

Since the policy
\pi
is fixed in both environments, the joint distributions satisfy
q^{\text{real}}_{t}(h,a)=d^{\text{real}}_{t}(h)\pi(a|h)
and
q^{\text{sim}}_{t}(h,a)=d^{\text{sim}}_{t}(h)\pi(a|h)
, so
\|q^{\text{real}}_{t}-q^{\text{sim}}_{t}\|_{1}=\Delta^{\text{env}}_{t}
.

The value difference is bounded by the cumulative divergence in joint distributions plus the local transition errors:

|J_{\text{real}}(\pi)-J_{\text{sim}}(\pi)|\leq R_{\max}\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}(\Delta^{\text{env}}_{t}+\delta_{t}).

This follows because at each step
t
, the reward difference depends on the state at
t+1
, which is influenced by both the history mismatch
\Delta^{\text{env}}_{t}
and the current transition error
\delta_{t}
.

We now analyze the recurrence of
\Delta^{\text{env}}_{t}
. The history distribution at
t+1
is obtained by applying the respective transition operators.

\displaystyle\Delta^{\text{env}}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{T}_{\text{real}}(q^{\text{real}}_{t})-\mathcal{T}_{\text{sim}}(q^{\text{sim}}_{t})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{T}_{\text{real}}(q^{\text{real}}_{t})-\mathcal{T}_{\text{sim}}(q^{\text{real}}_{t})+\mathcal{T}_{\text{sim}}(q^{\text{real}}_{t})-\mathcal{T}_{\text{sim}}(q^{\text{sim}}_{t})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\|\mathcal{T}_{\text{real}}(q^{\text{real}}_{t})-\mathcal{T}_{\text{sim}}(q^{\text{real}}_{t})\|_{1}}_{(A)}+\underbrace{\|\mathcal{T}_{\text{sim}}(q^{\text{real}}_{t})-\mathcal{T}_{\text{sim}}(q^{\text{sim}}_{t})\|_{1}}_{(B)}.

Term (A) - Local Simulator Error:
This measures the error of the simulator on the
real
distribution.

\displaystyle(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{h^{\prime},h,a}|P_{\text{real}}(h^{\prime}|h,a)q^{\text{real}}_{t}(h,a)-P_{\text{sim}}(h^{\prime}|h,a)q^{\text{real}}_{t}(h,a)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{h,a}q^{\text{real}}_{t}(h,a)\|P_{\text{real}}(\cdot|h,a)-P_{\text{sim}}(\cdot|h,a)\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(h,a)\sim q^{\text{real}}_{t}}[\|\mathcal{T}_{\text{real}}(\cdot|h,a)-\mathcal{T}_{\text{sim}}(\cdot|h,a)\|_{1}]=\delta_{t}.

Term (B) - Error Propagation:
Since
\mathcal{T}_{\text{sim}}
is a stochastic map, it is non-expansive.

\displaystyle(B)

\displaystyle\leq\|q^{\text{real}}_{t}-q^{\text{sim}}_{t}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{h,a}|d^{\text{real}}_{t}(h)\pi(a|h)-d^{\text{sim}}_{t}(h)\pi(a|h)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{h}|d^{\text{real}}_{t}(h)-d^{\text{sim}}_{t}(h)|\underbrace{\sum_{a}\pi(a|h)}_{1}=\Delta^{\text{env}}_{t}.

Thus, we have the recurrence
\Delta^{\text{env}}_{t+1}\leq\Delta^{\text{env}}_{t}+\delta_{t}
. Assuming
\Delta^{\text{env}}_{0}=0
, this unfolds to
\Delta^{\text{env}}_{t}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\delta_{i}
. Substituting this back:

\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}(\Delta^{\text{env}}_{t}+\delta_{t})=\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\delta_{i}+\delta_{t}\right)=\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\sum_{j=0}^{t}\delta_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{H-1}(H-i)\delta_{i}.

Multiplying by
R_{\max}
concludes the proof.
∎

B.1
Datasets and Benchmarks

We evaluate our proposed framework on two distinct multi-turn dialogue domains that require long-horizon planning and precise information seeking.

•

The user provides ambiguous or incomplete editing instructions, requiring the agent to proactively ask clarifying questions and propose partial edits across multiple turns.

Sentence-level BLEU score between the agent’s final produced document and the ground-truth reference

B.2
Model Architectures

Policy Agent (
\pi_{\theta}
).

We use
Gemma-3-4B-IT

9
as the backbone for the dialogue policy across all experiments. This model is chosen for its strong instruction-following capabilities at a compact 4B parameter scale, which enables efficient on-policy rollout generation during interactive RL training. The model is loaded in

User Simulator (
\pi_{\phi}^{\text{sim}}

We use

B.3
Training Phase I: Simulator Calibration (SFT)

In the first phase, we calibrate the user simulator via Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on multi-turn interaction traces collected from the Qwen3-235B oracle simulator interacting with the base policy model. This is a behavioral cloning approach—the simulator learns to replicate realistic human-like interaction patterns.

Data Collection.

We deploy the Qwen3-235B model as an oracle user simulator (conditioned on ground-truth goals) and collect multi-turn trajectories by having it interact with the Gemma-3-4B-IT base model. This produces:

•

MATH-Chat:
1,860 multi-turn trajectories (max sequence length: 4,096 tokens).

•

MediumDocEdit-Chat:
16,028 multi-turn trajectories (max sequence length: 6,144 tokens).

Training Setup.

We fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct using standard causal language modeling loss on the user turns only (masking assistant turns). Training uses FSDP on
8\times
NVIDIA A800-80GB GPUs. Hyperparameters are detailed in Appendix
D
, Table
3
.

B.4
Training Phase II: Interactive Policy Optimization (GRPO)

In the second phase, the SFT-calibrated simulator is frozen, and we optimize the policy agent
\pi_{\theta}
via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
27
. GRPO eliminates the need for a separate value network by normalizing rewards within a group of sampled trajectories, making it memory-efficient for long-context multi-turn training.

Interaction Protocol.

For each training prompt
x
, the agent generates
G=32
independent multi-turn trajectories by interacting with the aligned simulator. Each episode runs for up to
H=7
dialogue rounds (14 total turns). The simulator generates the first user message and the episode terminates upon the simulator emitting a
[[TERMINATE CHAT]]
signal or reaching the maximum turn count.

Reward Function.

We use sparse outcome rewards
R(\tau)
administered only at episode completion:

•

MATH-Chat:
Binary accuracy (0/1) determined by an LLM judge comparing the final answer to ground truth.

•

MediumDocEdit-Chat:
BLEU score between the final document and the reference.

Training Setup.

We train with Ray-based distributed FSDP on 32 GPUs (4 nodes
\times
8 A800-80GB) with SGLang for efficient rollout generation. The policy and rollout engine are co-located on the same GPUs (
--colocate
mode). Full hyperparameters are in Appendix
D
, Table
4
.

The original instruction-tuned model without any multi-turn RL fine-tuning, serving as the zero-shot baseline. We evaluate it with the same proactive system prompt used during RL training.

CollabLLM (Static RL).

We reproduce CollabLLM
37
using the official codebase. CollabLLM represents the state-of-the-art in

: it trains the policy on fixed offline conversation logs using Multi-turn-aware Rewards (MR). To ensure a fair comparison, we adapt CollabLLM to use Gemma-3-4B-IT as the backbone (matching our method), rather than the models used in their original paper. The reward model and training procedure follow their official implementation.

Ours (Static Context).

An ablation of our framework where the policy is trained via GRPO on static offline trajectories
without
dynamic simulator interaction. The policy optimizes against fixed conversation logs collected from the oracle simulator, conditioning on offline history
h\sim d^{\text{data}}_{t}
rather than self-generated trajectories. This isolates the effect of closing the interaction loop.

Ours (Naive Interactive).

An ablation using the full interactive RL loop but with an uncalibrated user simulator. Specifically, we use the base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model (without Phase I SFT) prompted to act as a user. This isolates the impact of simulator calibration from the benefit of interactive training.

Appendix E
Limitations and Future Work

While our work establishes a theoretical framework for mitigating distribution shift in interactive dialogue and demonstrates strong empirical results, we acknowledge certain limitations that point towards future research directions.

Computational Overhead.
A primary limitation of the Calibrated Interactive RL framework is its computational demand during the training phase. Unlike Static Context RL, which only requires a single forward-backward pass over fixed offline datasets, our closed-loop paradigm necessitates running generation steps for both the policy agent and the user simulator during the rollout phase. This substantially increases the inference overhead and memory footprint, especially for long-horizon conversations. Future work could address this bottleneck by distilling the aligned simulator into a much smaller parameter-efficient model, or by exploring offline-to-online RL paradigms where the agent is pre-trained heavily on static logs and only fine-tuned interactively for a few critical turns to rectify specific distribution shifts.

Advanced Simulator Calibration.
A second limitation lies in our current reliance on Supervised Fine-Tuning for Phase I simulator calibration. While behavioral cloning is highly effective for matching human distributions within the support of the training data, it may struggle to handle heavily out-of-distribution dialogue states dynamically induced by a rapidly exploring policy agent. In future work, we plan to extend simulator training beyond SFT by formulating it as a Reinforcement Learning problem. By optimizing the simulator to maximize a discriminator-derived "Realism Reward," it can proactively learn to maintain human-like stubbornness and constraints even in unobserved scenarios. Furthermore, drawing inspiration from Generative Adversarial Networks and adversarial imitation learning, a highly promising direction is to introduce an adversarial co-evolution framework. Instead of relying on a fixed, rule-based or static reward model, we could dynamically co-train a discriminator to distinguish between real human interaction logs and simulated trajectories. The simulator would be trained to actively fool this continuously evolving discriminator, establishing an auto-curriculum that mutually pushes both the dialogue policy and the simulator toward a higher level of behavioral fidelity and robustness.
