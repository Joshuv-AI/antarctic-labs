INTRODUCTION AND CONTEXT
This document is the self-assessment of the High Energy Physics activities at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory for FY2026. On a budget authority of about $2.362M to date this fiscal year, ORNL has
continued to leverage the Spallation Neutron Source (SNS) as a landmark low-energy neutrino facility, the
Oak Ridge Leadership Computing Facility (OLCF) and the Center for Nanophase Materials Science
(CNMS), all DOE user facilities, to advance the high energy physics program. The Physics Division
supports a broad user community at these facilities with a high scientific output, providing an excellent
training ground for the next generation of high energy physicists. Staff are committed to mentoring students
with most of them continuing their career in physics after completing their studies at the Laboratory.
Collaboration across directorates enables an impactful detector R&D and quantum sensing program, with
this year world-first results. The Research Accelerator Division that powers the SNS operates the world’s
most intense proton driver that enables studies for future high intensity applications. The following sections,
structured along the PEMP goals, highlight the ORNL achievements for fiscal year 2026.

Objective 1.1: Science or Technology Highlights and Achievements
Intensity Frontier

COHERENT: In a new study, the COHERENT collaboration reported the most precise measurement of
the coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering cross section to date. This measurement was performed
with the germanium detector array accumulating an exposure of 4.68 × 1022 protons on target and yielding
a total number of observed counts of 124$"%
 !"# and a flux-averaged cross section of 1.00 ± 0.10 (statistical)
± 0.10 (systematic) relative to the standard-model expectation of 5.9× 10−39 cm2. This marks a new
milestone for CEvNS science with the statistical significance now matching the systematic uncertainties
dominated by the SNS neutrino flux uncertainty to be addressed by the ORNL-designed heavy water
detector. The well-understood energy and timing distributions of the neutrino source allow for independent
measurements of muon- and electron-neutrino scattering rates and improves constraints on non-standard
neutrino interactions mediated by heavy particles. The manuscript has been submitted for publication in
Physical Review Letters.

The COHERENT Argon 750 detector construction project made significant advancements this year. ORNL
completed the ASME engineering analysis, pressure tests, and laboratory review of the Seoul National
University 750 kg argon cryostat for operation in the U.S. ORNL led the design and fabrication of the argon
circulation and purification system and oversaw the full integration of the cryostat and circulation system
through a complete cryogenic test to demonstrate stable operation and condensing 718 kgs of liquid argon
from May 8 to June 28. ORNL neutrino science staff supervise the onsite contributions of three early career
scientists from SNU and Indiana University stationed at ORNL for the Argon 750 deployment in addition
to approximately ten students routinely working in Neutrino Alley during weeks long visits. ORNL
oversees the local operation of six COHERENT instruments collecting CEvNS on Na and Ge targets as
well as inelastic neutrino interactions on deuterium, oxygen, lead and thorium targets.

AI/ML, Computation

Celeritas: The ORNL-led GPU-optimal Monte Carlo (MC) particle transport code Celeritas continued its
focus on the development of optical photon physics capabilities on GPU and its use on HEP and NP
experiments. The initial uses of this capability are CalVision and the Deep Underground Neutrino
Experiment (DUNE), with other collaborations being either on their early stages or discussed. These include
LUX-ZEPLIN (LZ), Large Enriched Germanium Experiment (LEGEND), and COHERENT. Performance
optimizations across the code, from geometry navigation to improving asynchronous execution between
CPU and GPU during particle transport are also ongoing. The cumulative improvements in geometry
navigation with ORANGE have led to 700% event throughput, with a 128 CPU to GPU equivalence.
Collaboration with DUNE is now official, with a limited membership agreement for Celeritas team
members. The preliminary results from the integration effort show that Celeritas can generate full, high-
fidelity, MC optical particle transport 10% faster than the existing approximate, semi-analytical method
used by DUNE. These results currently limited to the Nvidia Tesla V100, which is almost 10 years old, due
to old DUNESW container infrastructure, which is not built for newer hardware. Considerable performance
improvements are expected once those challenges are overcome. From a proof-of-principle perspective,
Celeritas is now integrated with LArSoft, despite not yet being a core component of the framework. The
current Celeritas-LArSoft integration also considers the planned transition of DUNE from the art
Framework (in which LArSoft is based on) to Phlex. As the current Celeritas-LArSoft integration design
uses data products as the required dependencies, instead of art Framework specific classes, the conversion
to Phlex should be trivial. The Celeritas team is also aiming to make Celeritas part of the next DUNE
Technical Design Report (TDR), cementing it as a core component of the DUNE computational framework.
Beyond optical photon physics, the collaboration between Celeritas and AdePT has led to a joint plan to
use Celeritas core components to underpin the development of both VecGeom and AdePT over the next
year. With respect to Celeritas’ electromagnetic (EM) physics capabilities, the U.S. Muon Collider
Collaboration (USMCC) is interested in using Celeritas to speed-up their workflow, where the simulation
of beam-induced background (BIB) caused by EM showers at the TeV scale is currently the largest
simulation bottleneck. Lastly, the Genesis Mission award “Enabling Rare Event Discovery with Surrogate
Modeling and Simulation AI Agents” will use Celeritas for generating fast, high-fidelity data for surrogate
models for the LEGEND-1000 detector.

QIS

Opto-mechanical Devices: During FY26, our focus for the QuantISED program centered around
optimizing the capabilities and technology established during FY25 to enable quantum enhanced impulse
detection for ultraheavy dark matter (UHDM) detection and entanglement-enhanced distributed sensing for
ultralight dark matter (ULDM) detection. As part of these efforts, we designed, fabricated, and
characterized an array of novel optomechanical (MEMS) devices with higher quality factors. Much of the
efforts focused on the interface of these devices with our source of squeezed light. We have observed a
quantum noise reduction of around 1 dB with the configuration that will be used for impulse detection. We

 2

are finalizing the integration of the MEMS with the system to impart an optical pulse to the MEMS to
emulate the impulse from a UHDM signal and demonstrate quantum-enhanced impulse detection. In
parallel, we are exploring configurations for probing a small array of optomechanical devices to test
entangled distributed sensing approaches we previously proposed as part of this program. We have designed
and implemented a compact system to simultaneously probe 4 MEMS with independent alignment to
establish a stable and scalable configuration. As part of our initial tests, the response of several of the
MEMS has been characterized and it has been determined that, while they all have similar resonance
frequencies, they do not fully overlap. We are currently considering techniques, such as stress or thermal
approaches, for independently tunning the resonance frequency of each device in the array.
We have also participated in the Quantum Outposts on the Energy and Intensity Frontiers NOFO,
submitting a proposal titled “Toward Direct Neutrino Detection with an Entangled Optomechanical Sensor
Array”. Using neutrons as a proxy to establish the principle for detection was proposed. Although the
proposal reviewed very well, it was not funded.
On the theory side, we refined our previous models for using an array of optomechanical impulse sensors
for UHDM searches and showed that such a system can cover significant new parameter space compared
to existing experimental efforts. In addition to the work on quantum sensing for dark matter detection,
ORNL continues to contribute to DRD5 of the ECFA Detector R&D roadmap in the framework of the
European Strategy for Particle Physics Update. The focus of DRD5 is to establish a quantum sensing and
emerging technologies roadmap for particle physics. As it relates to the QuantISED program, ORNL is one
of the leads for Work Package 5: Quantum Techniques for Sensing and chairs the Collaboration Board.
Publications: 1 published with ORNL authors; 2 published by non-ORNL team members.

General Accelerator Research and Development

The goal of this research is to improve particle accelerator modeling for high-power hadron linacs to predict
beam halo formation, with the broader goal of improving accelerator performance by lowering uncontrolled
beam loss. This is done through careful benchmarking of the predictive model with advanced phase space
diagnostics on an accelerator test stand (the Beam Test Facility at SNS).
The focus in FY26 has been continuing systematic studies of beam halo growth under mismatch conditions
– the first systematic study was conducted in FY25. Since then, we have developed additional cases for
study: 4 different input beam distributions (controlled using an attenuator and collimator at the beginning
of the test beamline), and, for each input beam, 5 different mismatch levels. For each case (4x5), we
characterize the output beam distribution with 2 independent measurements (of horizontal and vertical
phase space distribution), for a total of 40 measurements. Each measurement requires 4 hours of
uninterrupted beam time, in addition to in-situ tuning of the solution. Therefore, the focus of this year has
been on a large experimental push. At this point at the end of Q3, we have completed approximately half
the planned measurements. Unexpected downtime due to technical issues has delayed this progress.
We have continued our simulation program. The main effort has been in learning other implementations of
accelerator particle-in-cell codes, to extend our benchmark beyond our in-house code PyORBIT. We have

 3

also started an investigation into the dependence of halo on mismatch launch condition (previous studies
only considered amplitude) – this effort was initiated by a SULI project.
Equipment upgrades have been studied to support continued progress. We have tested an NVIDIA DGX
SPARK computer as a shared, high-performance simulation resource, and plan to purchase a few more. We
have also invested in adding more steering correction in the test beamline, motivated by the FY25 results
that suggest that larger-than-expected amounts of the beam halo distribution are scraped off by the pipe
aperture.

Detector R&D and Microelectronics

Photon Digital Converters: This project has seen a major achievement in FY26. Following the successful
fabrication of the first fully integrated three-dimensional Photon Digital Converters (3D-PDCs) at Teledyne
DALSA in FY25 and the subsequent delivery of the first prototype devices, the collaboration initiated an
extensive characterization campaign at ORNL and the University of Sherbrooke. The teams are now
routinely acquiring data from 3D-PDCs coupled to a variety of scintillator materials, evaluating detector
performance, timing resolution, photon detection efficiency, and integration strategies for future radiation
imaging systems.
A significant accomplishment during FY26 was the successful scheduling and production of multiple wafer
runs ahead of the planned modernization of the Teledyne DALSA fabrication facility. The foundry is
expected to undergo a major upgrade that will temporarily suspend production for up to six months while
transitioning from 150 mm to 200 mm wafer processing. By leveraging the maturity of the fabrication
process, the collaboration secured sufficient production capacity before the shutdown and anticipates
receiving approximately 6,000 3D-PDC chips. This inventory will provide ample supply of devices to
support characterization, packaging, system integration, and application development throughout the
foundry downtime.
During this fabrication hiatus, the project will focus on advancing the next generation of the technology.
Key activities include completing the design and implementation of scalable tile-controller electronics
required to construct large-area detector arrays, as well as developing improved SPAD array architectures
with a target fill factor of approximately 60%, representing a substantial increase over the current
generation. These developments are expected to enhance photon detection efficiency, improve overall
detector sensitivity, and further strengthen the scalability of PDC-based imaging systems.
The workforce has been strengthened by fully integrating an early-career research staff member into the
team. This researcher has assumed responsibility for identifying and developing new application areas for
PDC technology and broadening the impact of the project beyond its initial objectives. This work has been
integrated into the EIC, who supports radiation hardness studies, and is presented with the ECFA DRD4
collaboration on photodetectors.
Publications: 1; Conference Proceedings: 4; Invited talks: 8; Total talks: 11.

Detector Development – Timepix4: ORNL is the only US national laboratory that is a member of the
CERN Timepix4 collaboration, a position secured through a partnership with UTK, and FY26 saw this
investment translate into hardware milestones of international significance. The first single-chip Timepix4
assemblies with through-silicon-via (TSV) architecture were received and characterized at ORNL, a world
premiere for this device. In parallel, ORNL installed and commissioned the first Timepix4 event-based
camera in North America on the Nion UltraSTEM 200 microscope at CNMS, giving the user community
the world's fastest scanning transmission electron microscopy (4D-STEM) camera, with 200 ps time
resolution and 160 Gbps of readout bandwidth. The camera is already producing publication-quality science

 4

on quantum materials, including studies of charge density waves and chirality in 1T-TaS2 relevant to phase-
change memory devices. A second deployment in optical-readout mode was characterized in a cold neutron
beam at HFIR, taking data continuously at 8–9 Gbps, which establishes the path toward the large-area (100
x 100 mm2) neutron imaging detectors required by VENUS, MARS, and the proposed CUPI2D instrument.
To exploit these data rates, ORNL developed LibTPX4, an in-house, pipelined C++20 analysis toolkit with
Python bindings that currently processes 20M pixel hits per second per thread and delivers data reduction
factors of 3–5 through hit clustering, with a roadmap to 100 million hits/s and further deployment for speed
and low latency to FPGA via classical or neuromorphic algorithms.
Advanced Interconnect and 3D Integration: A distinguishing strength of the ORNL detector R&D
program is its end-to-end capability in advanced packaging, from wafer-level processing to device stacking
and high-density interconnection. In FY26, ORNL and the Timepix4 collaboration launched a TSV
processing run on Timepix4v3 wafers at a US vendor, pursuing two innovations: substantially lowering the
cost for 300 mm wafer processing than the available European options, and the addition of copper pillars
on the pixel side so that wafers arrive ready for flip-chip bonding. TSV-processed ASICs remove the wire-
bond periphery and allow ASICs to be tiled seamlessly into large sensors with no dead area outside the
acceptance. Working in partnership with Advafab, ORNL has carried thick (1 mm) and thin (250 µm)
bumped sensors with thin entrance windows through UBM metallization, with the process deliberately split
to compare bump-on-sensor and bump-on-ASIC approaches and is preparing the flip-chip assembly of
single, quad (5x5 cm2), and nonuplet (7.5x7.5 cm2) devices. The nonuplet assembly, called BEAST (Big
Experimental Area Sensor for Timepix4), will be a two-megapixel hybrid pixel detector and the largest
device of its kind. ORNL is qualifying low-temperature anisotropic conductive paste as a low-cost
alternative to BGA balling for flex-to-ASIC attachment, a process compatible with pre-existing bump
bonds, executable in-house and done in collaboration with CERN through ENIG plating of two Timepix4
ASICs for AC-coupling to the first SMALLGAD sensors for Timepix4 delivered from BNL. These sensors
aim at capacitive coupling using a thin-layer of adhesive to establish the AC-Coupling between ASIC and
sensor. This new method for AC-LGAD hybridization to ASIC has the potential to provide low-cost
methods for large-area deployments of AC-LGADs in future experiments.
Neuropix: ORNL is leading NEUROPix, a neuromorphic computing framework for the processing of
pixelated detector data at the edge, addressing a growing mismatch between the hundreds of Gbps generated
by a single modern pixel ASIC and what readout, powering, and cooling technology can sustain. The project
applies spiking neural networks, which are event-driven, massively parallel, and inherently low power, to
perform clustering, particle identification, anomaly detection, and interpolation directly on the detector data
stream, using Timepix4 as a test case. During FY26 the team assembled an interdisciplinary collaboration
spanning the Physics Division, Computational Sciences and Engineering, UTK, and external partners, and
hired two postdoctoral computer scientists with additional engineering and graduate positions forthcoming.
A complete simulation-to-training pipeline was built, coupling Allpix-Squared detector simulation to a
flexible dataloader with interchangeable spike-train encoding schemes, allowing candidate encodings of
the Timepix4 time-of-arrival, charge, and position outputs to be evaluated against one another during
ensemble training. The first trained networks achieved up to 98% clustering and classification efficiency
on synthetic data, and studies are under way to determine how the networks weigh cluster shape against the
timing information available in fast-timing pixel data. Results were presented at the ULITIMA 2026
conference at Fermilab in May 2026 and at the VERTEX conference in July 2026.

GOAL 3: S&T RESEARCH PROJECT/PROGRAM MANAGEMENT

Objective 3.1: Program Planning and Vision

The Division delivered a 10-year strategic plan that identified four grand challenges with the research
objective of “Revealing Broken Symmetries in Nature with Neutrinos and Neutrons” relevant to the HEP
program. This science thrust expands on our existing neutrino program at the SNS and HFIR and targets
future integration of quantum sensors, leveraging investments from national security. Discussions
continued with the project management of the Second Target Station to explore opportunities for
incorporation of a neutrino experimental program at the new facility.

The low-energy neutrino program at the SNS continues to deliver impactful physics that informs the DUNE
physics program and ORNL provides full support to the user community to host experiments at the SNS.
The commissioning the 750 kg liquid argon detector in Neutrino Alley is nearing completion in
collaboration with Seoul University and is expected to deliver ~5000 CEvNS interactions per year.
Discussions are ongoing with the University of Hawaii to deploy directional dark matter detectors and with
UC Berkeley to deploy the water-based liquid scintillator EOS experiment. Following the guidance from
the office, the PROSPECT program is being completed.

 6

In collaboration with DARPA and universities, a quantum sensing program is being established at HFIR
supported by institutional funding for equipment, with a potential goal of detecting reactor neutrinos.
Synergies with the quantum information science efforts at the laboratory are being leveraged.

ORNL staff is actively developing the HPC-based code Celeritas for photon transport in noble liquid
detectors and for calorimetry applications and anticipate providing multiple factors of speedup of the Monte
Carlo simulations and enhancing their fidelity. The code has been integrated in the DUNE software
framework.

Several projects within the Detector R&D realm aim at tackling some of the most challenging technical
challenges for future detectors for nuclear and high energy physics and, using the technology developed
within the high energy physics community, are applying those techniques to other areas of science that
breaks existing barriers and have the potential to open up new areas of research.

Objective 3.2: Effective and Efficient Science and Technology Program
Management

The ORNL high energy physics program effectively leverages the multi-disciplinary nature of the
laboratory and its many user facilities. Its program is well-aligned with the P5 science priorities. The
Celeritas particle transport code has pivoted towards photon tracking and has been integrated with the
DUNE software framework. In neutrino alley a liquid argon detector will be deployed that will provide
relevant cross section information for low-energy neutrinos relevant to the DUNE physics program. The
instrumentation program targets new technologies for next-generation collider experiment, including the
integration of AI in the front-end.

Objective 3.3: Efficient and Effective Communications and Responsiveness

During the monthly meeting with the Office of High Energy Physics the status of the various programs is
discussed. Regular discussion take place with the various program managers to ensure that our program is
aligned with the priorities of the office. The computational high energy physics program communicates on
a regular basis with HEP-CCE management and the ASCR program managers to ensures all activities are
aligned with the different program priorities. Regular in-depth reports are given at the detector R&D forum
that takes place quarterly.
