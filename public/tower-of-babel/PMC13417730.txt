Scientific Opinion on waiving of the dog studies in the regulatory process of agrochemicals approval
Authors: EFSA Panel on Plant Protection Products and their Residues (PPR), Tamara Coja, Pauline Adriaanse, Antonio Finizio, Maeva Giraudo, Thomas Kuhl, Emily McVey, Francesca Metruccio, Martin Paparella, Silvia Pieper, Eugenio Scanziani, Ivana Teodorovic, Paul Van der Brink, Martin Wilks, Antonio Hernandez‐Jerez, Olavi Pelkonen, Andrea Terron, Jochem Louisse, Martina Panzarea, Adriana Scattareggia Marchese, Judy Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommission Regulation (EU) No. 283/2013 sets out the data requirements for pesticide active substances and includes short‐term oral toxicity testing in rodents (90‐day rat study) and non‐rodents (90‐day dog study) to support human health hazard assessment for pesticide active substances in the European Union (EU). However, the relevance of the 90‐day dog study for such assessments has been increasingly questioned. In this context, the EFSA Panel on Plant Protection Products and their Residues (PPR Panel) initiated a self‐task mandate to evaluate the contribution of the 90‐day dog study to hazard assessment and to explore whether dog studies may be waived from a scientific perspective. This work included a retrospective analysis for the role of dog data in the derivation of health‐based guidance values (HBGVs, i.e. acceptable daily intake (ADI) and acceptable operator exposure level (AOEL)) for pesticide active substances in the EU and the development of a tiered assessment strategy to support regulatory decision‐making for new active substances. The evidence obtained in the retrospective analysis showed that, in 97% of the cases for ADI and 95% for AOEL, observed interspecies differences in established no observed adverse effect levels (NOAELs) were attributable to experimental and biological variability, differences in study design and/or allometric scaling, rather than reflecting a genuine dog‐specific sensitivity. Further analyses of modes of action (MoA); absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME); and toxicokinetic (TK) profiles across species were conducted. Based on the retrospective analysis, a tiered assessment strategy was defined to decide whether for a new active substance, the 90‐day dog study can be waived. A minimum data set and scientific criteria were defined for this assessment strategy, which includes integration of physiologically based kinetic (PBK) modelling and human‐relevant data. The PPR Panel concludes that this strategy provides a robust basis to support the waiving of dog studies when scientifically justified and supports the implementation of new approach methodologies (NAMs) in the regulatory risk assessment of pesticide active substances.

SUMMARY

Commission Regulation (EU) No. 283/2013 sets out the data requirements for pesticide active substances. Among others, short‐term oral toxicity testing in rodents (90‐day rat study) and in non‐rodents (90‐day dog study) are required to address human health hazard assessment of pesticide active substances to support their approval in the European Union (EU).

The added value of the 90‐day dog study for these hazard assessments has been questioned. A retrospective analysis of the active substance approval process previously conducted by EFSA underlined that, in 95% of the evaluated cases (96 out of 101 active substances), the dog was not the most sensitive species used for setting the health‐based guidance values (HBGVs).

In this context, the EFSA Panel on Plant Protection Products and their Residues (PPR Panel) was self‐tasked to develop a Scientific Opinion with two objectives (‘Terms of Reference’ or ToR): [1] to review and update the aforementioned retrospective analysis (ToR1) with a broader database of active substances and [2] to develop a tiered assessment strategy that can support the decision‐making of whether waiving of the dog studies in the regulatory process can be scientifically justified (ToR2). To this end, a dedicated Working Group of the PPR Panel (hereafter referred to as ‘PPR Panel WG’) was established.

For ToR1, the retrospective analysis was expanded to 461 active substances registered in Europe. Among these 461 active substances, dog studies were considered relevant for the setting of HBGVs in 288 cases: 107 (23%) for acceptable daily intake (ADI) and 183 (40%) for acceptable operator exposure level (AOEL), where dogs showed greater sensitivity to toxicity than other species tested.

For these cases, an in‐depth analysis was performed to assess whether differences are due to genuine dog‐specific sensitivity or whether they could be attributed to other factors, including experimental and biological variability, differences in study design (e.g. dose spacing or selection) or whether they could be explained by allometric scaling.

Higher sensitivity of dog due to experimental variability was considered for cases when the NOAELother species/NOAELdog is ≤ 3 (ADI: 83 cases; AOEL 183 cases). Higher sensitivity of dog due to dose spacing was considered for cases when the ratio of NOAELother species/NOAELdog > 3 but the NOAELother species < LOAELdog (a further 7 cases for ADI and 17 cases for AOEL). For cases where the lower NOAEL in dog is based on a 1‐year or 2‐year toxicity study, the NOAEL from 90‐day dog study (when available) was retrieved. If the NOAEL from the 90‐day dog study was in the same range as the lower NOAEL from other species (i.e. ratio of NOAELother species/NOAEL90‐d dog ≤ 3), no further analyses were considered needed as only the 90‐day study in dog is required according to Regulation (EU) No. 283/2013 (a further 7 cases for ADI and 6 cases for AOEL). Human equivalent doses (HEDs) were calculated to evaluate whether differences between dogs and other species could be explained by allometric scaling (a further 7 cases for ADI and 7 cases for AOEL). Overall, in 97% of the cases for ADI and 95% for AOEL, the observed interspecies differences in the established no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) between dog and other species such as rat were not reflecting genuine dog‐specific sensitivity.

This outcome served as a basis for further investigations into the mode of action (MoA); absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME) characteristics; and toxicokinetic (TK) profiles of the relevant active substances across species. The findings from the updated retrospective analysis supported the setting of a minimum data set and scientific criteria required to structure a tiered, weight‐of‐evidence (WoE)‐based approach to determine whether dog toxicity data are needed from a scientific perspective. Based on this retrospective analysis, a tiered assessment strategy (ToR2) was developed to support the decision‐making process for pesticide active substances, with the aim of ensuring that HBGVs remain protective while avoiding unnecessary dog toxicity testing. The assessment strategy consists of two preliminary phases and a flowchart. Preliminary phase 1 considers in vitro comparative metabolism studies and the toxicological mode of action of the active substance and allows an early decision on the possible waiving of dog toxicity studies. A dog study may be waived in case the dog shows a dissimilar in vitro metabolite profile compared to humans and/or if the active substance belongs to a well‐defined chemical class for which sufficient toxicological MoA data are available. For substances with an MoA for which the dog is known to be less sensitive than rats (rodents), a dog study may be waived. For substances with an MoA for which the dog is more sensitive than rodents, dog toxicity studies may also be waived but with further consideration of an extra uncertainty factor, depending on the available data and context of the assessment. Preliminary phase 2 evaluates whether the rat is considered an adequate test species for the human health hazard assessment regarding internal exposure upon oral intake based on the information available from in vivo ADME studies in rats and in vitro human Caco‐2 permeability data. If waiving of the dog study cannot be concluded in preliminary phase 1 and the rat is identified as an adequate test species in preliminary phase 2, the flowchart needs to be followed, focusing on interspecies comparison of internal exposure supported by physiologically based kinetic (PBK) modelling. If it can be concluded from the analysis that the dog does not provide added value for hazard assessment based on internal exposure considerations, the requirement for a dog toxicity study may be waived.

To assess the feasibility of employing the assessment strategy, data for four active substances were available for case studies. In three case studies, it could be demonstrated with medium to high confidence that hazard assessment using primarily rat toxicity data without dog toxicity testing could be performed. Limitations and uncertainties were identified while testing the assessment strategy and are discussed in the Scientific Opinion.

Key recommendations are made in this Scientific Opinion to facilitate the fulfilment of the necessary conditions to effectively run the assessment strategy for waiving purposes. The proposed tiered assessment strategy fosters the use of New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) in the regulatory risk assessment of pesticide active substances, which is fully in line with EFSA's Strategy 2027 1 and the European Commission's roadmap towards phasing out animal testing. 2

INTRODUCTION

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

Background

Under Regulation (EU) 283/2013 (European Commission, 2013), setting out the data requirements for pesticides active substances, short‐term oral toxicity testing in rodents (90‐day rat study) and non‐rodents (90‐day dog study) species are required to address hazard identification and human safety of plant protection products (PPPs) and to support the active substance approval in the European Union (EU).

The necessity to use a rodent species (‘first’ species) in parallel with a non‐rodent species (‘second’ species) for assessing potential hazardous effects of chemicals used as drugs, pesticides and consumer products, dates back to the 1960s (Box & Spielmann, 2005). The main reason why a second mammalian species phylogenetically different from rodents is used, was to identify interspecies differences in sensitivity and therefore for the derivation of the health‐based guidance values (HBGVs), e.g. acceptable daily intake (ADI), etc.

For the approval of PPPs, the scientific rationale of using the dog as ‘second’ species in the regulatory process has been debated for long time and culminated in the elimination of the one‐year dog study (OECD TG 452; OECD, 2018) from the data requirements in the EU, leaving the 90‐day study (OECD TG 409; OECD, 1998) as the only study available in the data set for the short‐term repeated dose toxicity study in a non‐rodent species.

PREV Unit and Plant Protection Products and their Residues (PPR) Panel members have reviewed the existing data on dog studies conducted with the pesticide active substances previously marketed in the EU and/or currently approved by the European legislation. A retrospective analysis of the results on setting of HBGVs (i.e. ADI) for pesticides was performed. An overview of the work is published as editorial in the EFSA Journal (Panzarea et al., 2022).

The current project aims to continue contributing to the debate by reviewing/updating existing data to develop a tiered testing strategy, recommending the possible waiving of dog studies in the regulatory process when deemed scientifically justifiable. In fact, from the retrospective analysis (Panzarea et al., 2022), for the 95% of cases in a database of 101 substances, the dog was not the most sensitive species used for setting the HBGVs. However, this project aims to go into the details of the previous analysis and explain the relevance of the dog for the cases where this species was the most sensitive one.

The outcome of the project will contribute to identifying which critical scientific and regulatory questions dog studies are expected to answer, if any. In a broad perspective, the project will provide evidence to assess the economic cost/benefits of the dog studies contextualizing the ethical use of animals in experimental toxicology. It will also assess the impact of dog studies on the paradigm shift from observational endpoints to a more mechanistic reasoning, using human‐relevant test systems to evaluate biological processes.

This project aligns with broader objectives, including complementing the European Commission's roadmap 3 to accelerate phasing out of animal studies in chemical risk assessment, integrating the use of New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) in the regulatory process as per the EFSA Strategy 2027 and, fostering collaborations with EFSA's stakeholders involved in analogous projects.

Interpretation of the Terms of Reference

In its interpretation of the Terms of Reference (ToR), the PPR Panel WG determined that the main objective of this mandate is to advise risk managers and EFSA stakeholders on the conditions under which toxicity studies in dogs might be considered dispensable in the approval process of pesticide active substances and under what circumstances their inclusion might be justified. Therefore, the intended goal of this work is to provide an assessment strategy to determine whether dog toxicity studies are necessary for the human health hazard assessment of pesticide active substances or whether they can be waived with scientifically robust justification.

In addressing ToR1, the PPR Panel WG reviewed and updated existing evaluations, starting with the previously conducted retrospective analysis on this topic (Panzarea et al., 2022) to ensure that current regulatory assessments are based on the latest scientific evidence. The emphasis of the updated analysis was placed on assessments of the effects of repeated exposure because it is widely understood that identifying toxicity from repeated exposure is the main objective of using dog toxicity studies. Occasionally, effects in repeated dose oral toxicity studies are used as a surrogate for acute effects but do not necessarily reflect acute toxicity. In particular, the acute effects most commonly observed in dogs (e.g. diarrhoea, vomiting, local gastrointestinal irritation) are considered to be a result of dog‐specific induced emesis/local gastrointestinal toxicity rather than being effects of the systemic toxicity of the test substance, meaning these acute effects in dogs are not suitable for extrapolation to humans. Furthermore, these effects are dose‐limiting as less of the substance potentially reaches systemic circulation. The outcome of ToR1 is addressed in Section 4.1.

Regarding ToR2, the PPR Panel WG interpreted the development of a tiered testing strategy, as mentioned above in Section 1.1.2, keeping in mind the broader context of reducing or phasing out animal studies. Specifically, for this mandate, the PPR Panel WG developed a tiered assessment strategy to support the potential waiving of dog studies in the regulatory approval process of pesticide active substances while ensuring robust human health safety assessments. This approach critically evaluates existing and relevant data to determine if dog toxicity studies are truly necessary to adequately identify and characterise human health hazards. To achieve this, the strategy defines the minimum set of data required for assessment, prioritising the incorporation of NAMs. The outcome of ToR2 is addressed in Section 4.2.

To test the tiered assessment strategy, proof‐of‐concept case studies were performed, using empirical data to substantiate and/or refine the decision framework. The Scientific Opinion defined the conditions under which dog toxicity studies might not be required based on a critical analysis of the available scientific data.

In the context of this Scientific Opinion, it should be emphasised that the term ‘agrochemicals’ strictly refers to the active substances undergoing or having undergone approval review process in the EU. The developed Protocol (Annex A) and the resulting Scientific Opinion for this mandate are independent of the pesticide peer review process and are not intended to modify regulatory decisions.

The economic cost/benefit of waiving dog studies, as mentioned in the Background (Section 1.1.1) of this Scientific Opinion, is not elaborated in this document, as the PPR Panel WG deemed it of foremost importance to focus on scientific and regulatory argumentations for waiving dog studies.

Abbreviations used in this Scientific Opinion can be found in a separate chapter at the end of the document. Additional background information on specific scientific topics (e.g. toxicokinetics and toxicodynamics) relevant for this mandate can be found in Appendices A and B, and protocols as well as outputs generated/provided by the PPR Panel WG can be found as Annexes as supplementary documents.

DOG AS AN EXPERIMENTAL ANIMAL MODEL

This section provides background and context considered pertinent to the ToRs addressed in the current Scientific Opinion. In particular, it outlines the historical reasons for using Beagle dogs as animal models in toxicity testing and highlights some critical elements that have led to the re‐evaluation of their continued use in toxicology, focusing on relevant aspects of canine physiology and pathology.

First and foremost, it is important to acknowledge that no commonly used laboratory species (e.g. dogs and rodents) can fully represent the complexity and variability of the human population and that every tested mammalian species has apparent physiological and/or anatomical differences from humans.

Notwithstanding, under the pesticide regulatory framework, the rat is the elective primary animal model to cover and address the key hazard endpoints, e.g. carcinogenicity, reproductive and developmental toxicity, neurotoxicity, of an active substance and its use is regulatorily justified for both scientific and practical reasons. The interspecies differences between humans and any mammalian species used for toxicity testing (in this case, rat) are critically considered by risk assessors when assessing the potential human health hazard of a substance.

The use of a second mammalian species for hazard assessment of chemicals has traditionally aimed to evaluate and address uncertainties regarding interspecies variability. However, selecting two species for toxicity testing does not necessarily reduce uncertainty regarding potential human health risks and there is emerging evidence to suggest that a second species may not be required in many cases for the hazard assessment (Bishop, Brescia, et al., 2023; Bishop, Dellarco, & Wolf, 2023; Janer et al., 2008; Linke et al., 2017). Nevertheless, there have been some circumstances, such as those in drug development, that justify the use of a second species like dog for safety assessment, but the decision is based on scientific a priori knowledge of the species differences in kinetics or toxicity (Bower et al., 2022).

The OECD Test Guideline (TG) 409 (Repeated Dose 90‐day Oral Toxicity Study in Non‐Rodents), which is the test protocol relevant for the implementation of Commission Regulation (EU) No 283/2013, indicates that the 90‐day study in non‐rodent species provides information on the possible health hazards likely to arise from repeated exposure over a period of rapid growth and into young adulthood (although most of the time dogs would still be in the peripubertal/pubertal sexual maturity stage at the end of the study). The study can also provide information on potential toxic (adverse) effects, target organs and the possibility of accumulation and can identify a no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of exposure for establishing HBGVs. It is of note that, in paragraph 4 of the OECD TG 409, it specifies the following conditions, in which the second species should be used: ‘where effects observed in other studies indicate a need for clarification/characterisation in a second, non‐rodent species, orwhere toxicokinetic studies indicate that the use of a specific non‐rodent species is the most relevant choice of laboratory animal, orwhere other specific reasons justify the use of a non‐rodent species’.

History and traits of the Beagle dog

The dog, Canis familiaris, is likely to be one of the oldest domesticated animals with skeletal remains discovered dating back to the middle Stone Age (6000–8000 B.C.) (Scott, 1964). Man discovered that some dogs had specific abilities that could better serve his needs, and by selective breeding, the desired traits were fixed and consistently transmitted to the progeny. Today, approximately 400 different canine breeds are recognised. They have remarkable genetic variations, underpinning the broad spectrum of physical, physiological and behavioural traits that are typical of the contemporary canine breeds.

Even though the dog has a long history of use in research, researchers only became cognizant during the latter half of the 20th century that the lack of standardisation of using dogs as experimental model, particularly regarding the health status, was an issue. The use of non‐standardised animals was considered a relevant hurdle to define what can be considered the ‘normal’ state of health. The consequence was that researchers were obliged to select a breed, originally selected for other purposes, but acceptable to experimental procedures. Among the various breeds considered for research, one survey indicated the Beagle as the breed of choice by a factor of 20:1 6 on selected characteristics (e.g. weight, size, temperament, etc.) (Scott, 1960).

The name Beagle is thought to derive from the French word beagle meaning bugler. However, the English were the main breeders of the Beagle as a hunting dog with an excellent scenting ability, and the breed standard for the Beagle was first described in the early 19th century (Taplin, 1803). Modern‐day Beagles are thought to have descended from Talbot Hounds, large white scent‐hounds that accompanied William the Conqueror into Britain during the 11th century. Another now‐extinct breed that may have contributed some DNA to the modern Beagle is the Irish Kerry Beagle, small black‐and‐tan dogs similar to a Bloodhound. The name Beagle first appeared in English literature in 1475. It was mentioned in the works of no lesser literary lights than Chaucer and Shakespeare. ‘She's a Beagle, true‐bred, and one that adores me’, says Sir Toby Belch in Twelfth Night. Queen Elizabeth I was among the many Beagle enthusiasts who owned one of the variations on the basic design. Her ‘pocket Beagles’ stood about 9 inches tall. These tiny hounds were also known as ‘singing Beagles’ or ‘glove Beagles’. Beagle‐like dogs appeared in America sometime before the Civil War. However, the breed was not firmly established until the 1880s. In 1885, a Beagle named Blunder became the first to be registered by the American Kennel Club (AKC). In the 1890s, the recently formed National Beagle Club held its first field trials. The Beagle therefore became popular in the United States at the end of 19th century and was one of the most popular dog breeds in the United States around the 1950s.

The most desirable qualities of the Beagle as an experimental breed are its medium size, moderate length coat in two or more colours, even temperament, adaptability to living in groups and representative conformation, as well as the lack of need for cosmetic surgery. 7 The Beagle's excellent disposition and friendly personality are great assets of the breed because special handling is often necessary with minimal restraint in several experimental procedures. They rarely show aggressiveness, timidity or shyness. In the past, variations in size and body weight, shedding and loud bark were initially recognised as faults; while shedding and barking remain unresolved issues, the size of the Beagle male and female dogs has been much standardised nowadays, and these issues are no longer perceived as faults.

The dog (namely the Beagle) is considered the elective second mammalian species and has been extensively used for hazard assessment for regulatory applications. Considering all in vivo animal models, using the dog for toxicity testing has both advantages and limitations, the latter including small sample size resulting in low statistical power and a lack of suitability for the assessment of biological products (e.g. antibodies, for which the elective species would be the non‐human primate). This section is not intended to list the advantages and the limitations of using the dog in experimental studies, as several seminal publications and books have already discussed this (Morton, 1998; Namdari et al., 2021), but rather to focus on some critical elements that should be considered when thinking about any potential added value of including the dog in toxicity studies for the purpose of characterising the toxicological profile of a substance. Two critical elements worth discussing here are (1) the dog's gastrointestinal (GI) physiology and its impact on the internal exposure and (2) certain specific histopathological characteristics of the Beagle dog that can affect hazard characterisation.

Discussions on species‐specific enzymatic activities (e.g. cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYPs)) and renal clearance (e.g. glomerular filtration rate, transporters activities) are not described in this chapter because these are not considered to be unique characteristics of the dog, but rather general factors impacting species and individual toxic sensitivity via toxicokinetic differences.

A comparison of dog and human gastrointestinal (GI) physiology

The acute effects most commonly observed in dogs (e.g. diarrhoea, vomiting, local gastrointestinal irritation) are considered to be a result of dog‐specific induced emesis/local gastrointestinal toxicity rather than being effects of the systemic toxicity of the test substance (Clark & Steger‐Hartmann, 2018; Elwood et al., 2010) and are considered of limited relevance to humans.

The digestive tract of the dog is adapted for a diet typical of carnivores, focused on the rapid breaking down of proteins. Dogs have a shorter small intestine and a longer large intestine relative to their size; this is different from humans, where the longer small intestine and the presence of specialised enzymes (e.g. amylase) allow for an efficient absorption of nutrients represented by a mix of plant and animal components. Although anatomical differences among the two species exist, an advantage of including dog studies in hazard assessment is the relatively comparable ADME characteristics between humans and dogs, making the evaluation easier compared to other animal species. However, despite the large number of studies performed, significant discrepancies still exist between the oral bioavailability observed in dogs and that in humans. The two main aspects of the GI physiology expected to affect the absorption and bioavailability of chemical substances are the transit time/motility of the substance and the pH of the GI tract, which are further elaborated in the section below. Species‐specific differences in GI physiology are expected to influence the time profile and/or the extent of absorption of a chemical substance, e.g. showing differences between dogs and humans (Dressman, 1986; Smeets‐Peeters et al., 1998). It should additionally be noted that variations among breeds that can influence absorption of chemical substances also exist in canine gastrointestinal physiology (Bourreau et al., 2004; Oswald et al., 2015).

Residence time in the stomach

Most of the absorption of a substance in dogs occurs in the small intestine, with some exceptions related to absorption in the colon (e.g. short‐ and medium‐chain fatty acids and octanoic acid). However, before entering the small intestine, feed and chemical substances must transit through the stomach, where several physiological processes occur, noting that the dog does not have amylase in the saliva. The residence time of a chemical substance in the stomach depends on several factors. These include the administered form (liquid vs. solid), the volume of liquid administration, the particle size and the fasted vs. the postprandial (recently fed) states. In the fasted state, the motility of the upper GI tract consists of three main phases (phase I with no contractions, phase II characterised by intermittent contractions and phase III characterised by regular rhythmic contractions) and feeding results in changes in the motility pattern. In the stomach, motility is characterised by regular tonic contractions, pushing food towards the antrum while mixing it with the gastric secretion. The fasting motility pattern is resumed when the meal has been emptied from the stomach.

These patterns are qualitatively similar between dogs and humans, but quantitative differences exist, affecting absorption. Fine particles are emptied at a similar rate to digestible solid in the fed state and the dependency of emptying on the particle size in the two species is relevant. The motility patterns of the GI tract are thought to be similar between the two species and monolithic dosage forms are expected to be similar in terms of residency time. Indeed, fine particles will empty from the stomach with fluids, whereas particles too large to empty with fluid will exit in conjunction with Phase III activity. There is a relationship between particle size/density and rate of emptying relative to the meal. Overall, this general trend is similar between the two species, meaning particles of less than 0.5 mm should empty with fluid, particles between 0.5 and 3 mm should empty during the course of the meal and clearance of particles larger than 4.5 mm is expected to be delayed until the meal has emptied (Gruber et al., 1987; Meyer et al., 1985).

Assessing the transit time along the intestine is more complex than in the stomach and data are less consistent (Koziolek et al., 2019). In humans, transit generally takes 3–5 h, independent of the particle size or whether in a fasted state, but transit can take longer after ingesting a heavy meal. The effect of a heavy meal is not known in dogs, but it is known that particle size can affect the transit rate. This relationship has not been confirmed in human. A mean small intestine residence time of 238 ± 14 min was reported for human, consistent with the transit time under fasting or light meal conditions (Dressman, 1986). Transit time in dog is less than half that in humans and more variable, ranging from 15 to 206 min, possibly indicating that absorption of chemical substances in dogs is more variable and less complete, but exceptions can exist (Koziolek et al., 2019).

Role of pH in the GI tract in absorption

The pH of the GI tract is the second relevant factor affecting absorption of chemical substances. For passively absorbed substances, the non‐ionised form is generally better absorbed than the ionised form. The pH of the GI tract can, therefore, dictate the rate of absorption of chemical substances with dissociation constraints in the physiological pH range. For lipophilic chemical substances, the pH of half‐maximal absorption should be considered instead of the pKa. For non‐lipophilic chemical substances, the pH of half maximal absorption and the pKa are expected to be similar. If the pH of half‐maximal absorption is in the range of the pH observed in the GI tract, changes in pH due to feeding, a disease state or species differences will affect bioavailability. Variations in the GI pH can also affect the ionised status of actively absorbed chemical substances, and they can influence the dissolution state, particularly affecting poorly soluble chemical substances.

Gastric pH in the fasted dog is about 1.5 ± 0.04 (ranging 0.9–2.5), whereas, in humans, it ranges from below 1 to 3.2, with a mean maximum pH of 2.1 and a mean minimum pH of 1.3. After meal ingestion, there is a rise in pH in humans due to the buffering effect of food, followed by a rise in gastric secretion with a gradual return to pre‐meal values in 60–90 min. The acid peak in humans occurs around an hour after meal and can possibly also be affected by the order of fluid vs. solid administration. The buffering effect is not observed in dogs and there is no change in pH after the first postprandial hour, which can be explained by the higher acid peak observed in dogs, minimising the buffering effect of feed. Therefore, while the gastric pH is similar between humans and dogs in the fasted state, the initial postprandial pH peak is absent in dogs, making the pH response to meals less predictable and more variable.

The pH in the intestinal lumen of dogs is consistently 1 unit higher than in humans when normalised to gastric emptying. This is mainly due to the lower basal gastric secretion while the concentration of pancreatic bicarbonate (representing the main buffering system in the small intestine) is similar between the two species. The pancreatic bicarbonate output is, however, more variable in dogs, and in this species, the rate of bicarbonate secretion in the fasted state is higher than the basal acid secretion. For humans, the opposite is true. Therefore, it is likely that chemical substances with a pH at half‐maximal absorption in the pH range between 5 and 7 may be absorbed at different rates when comparing the two species. The intestinal pH of dogs has a wider pH range than that of humans, and this would affect the variability in absorption of poorly absorbed chemical substances with a half maximal absorption pH in this range (Table 2). These aspects are expected to affect the absorption and bioavailability of chemical substances, limiting the suitability of the dog as a protective model species (Martinez & Papich, 2009).

Histopathological traits

The spontaneous (i.e. not experimentally induced) background incidence of histopathological changes in dogs has been widely described in several dedicated papers and, more recently, collected in an INHAND publication (Woicke et al., 2021) with the purpose of providing a standardised nomenclature for classifying lesions observed in most tissues and organs from the dog that are examined in toxicological studies. A more comprehensive understanding of the spontaneous histopathological changes in Beagle dog can be retrieved from the abovementioned INHAND publication.

Two conditions commonly observed in the Beagle dog as an experimental species, namely idiopathic canine polyarteritis and particular features of the male reproductive system, must be interpreted with caution when evaluating dog toxicity studies for the hazard characterisation of pesticide active substances as they can confound histopathological diagnosis of effects of the substance being tested. This consideration comes primarily from the regulatory experience. For example, the presence of isolated cases of idiopathic canine polyarteritis could represent a relevant confounding factor when a distinction between background pathology and treatment‐related findings is necessary, or, as in the case of the histological characteristics of the dog reproductive system (when matched with the peripubertal status), these cases could seriously limit the use of the dog in the assessment of male reproductive toxicants.

Idiopathic canine polyarteritis is also known as Beagle pain syndrome or canine juvenile polyarteritis syndrome. Understanding and morphologically differentiating the spontaneous occurrence of this disease from chemically induced vascular changes is relevant in toxicological studies because chemically induced vascular pathology can have some common traits with the Beagle idiopathic canine polyarteritis. The aetiology and pathogenesis of this disease remain unknown and the site predilection (i.e. affected vessels), dose–response pattern and clinical and changes in laboratory clinical chemistry and haematological parameters (e.g. neutrophilic leucocytosis, hyperfibrinogenaemia and hypoalbuminemia) should be considered to differentiate the spontaneous vascular syndrome from a treatment‐related effect. Idiopathic canine polyarteritis usually shows prominent transmural to periarterial inflammation and haemorrhages are seldomly observed; it will typically affect arteries of the heart and of many other tissues and it is not associated with haemodynamic changes, whereas a constellation of clinical signs like pyrexia, anorexia, reluctance to move, cervical rigidity and pain are variably reported. Elevated polymorphonuclear cell count and concentration of immunoglobulin A (IgA) and acute phase proteins are frequent concomitant changes at haematological and clinical chemistry analysis. However, the background condition can be exacerbated following treatment with vasoactive or immune modulators substances (Clemo et al., 2003; Hogenesch et al., 1995; Son, 2004).

The other relevant condition refers to the male reproductive system of dogs used in toxicological studies. Dogs used in toxicity studies can be 5–6 months of age at the start of the test, but sexual maturity occurs from 7 to 12 months of age, depending on the source of the animals. The use of animals with sexually immature or peripubertal reproductive organs is not a sensitive means to detect potential treatment‐related effects on adult reproductive tissues. Dog spermatogenesis is less organised and less synchronised than in rodents, and special staining (i.e. PAS staining) of the acrosome is less specific in classifying canine spermatogenesis. In addition, the canine male reproductive tract is not synchronised when considering the rate of maturation of the testes, epididymides and prostate. Indeed, maturation of the prostate may be delayed in relation to the state of sexual maturation in the testes or vice versa. In addition, detailed stage‐dependent evaluation is also challenging in the dog because the release of mature spermatids may occur over two stages and dogs have a higher incidence of spontaneous findings (e.g. hypospermatogenesis, swollen spermatocytes, multinucleated giant cells or changes associated with peripubertal development) compared to the rat. Germ cells in dogs seem to lack androgen and follicle stimulating hormone (FSH) receptors, and therefore, the target for the hormonal control is likely via the stromal cells. Consequently, capturing testicular toxicants in dogs can be challenging or even impossible, and the use of male dog for the assessment of the endocrine‐disrupting properties of active substances has often posed major limitations in the assessment and using the dog for this purpose should be taken with caution (Greaves, 2011; Soares et al., 2009).

Data interpretation and evaluation

Based on the Excel data set, pesticide active substances (hereafter referred as ‘cases’), for which dog toxicity studies were used for the derivation of HBGVs, were identified and highlighted. In particular, comparative analyses were performed and data on lower NOAELs/LOAELs from available studies in other species from the data set were collected.

Although information on the four main types of HBGVs (ADI, AOEL, ARfD and AAOEL) was retrieved and collected, the PPR Panel WG agreed to focus the critical investigations of HBGVs in which dog toxicity studies were used to set ADIs and AOELs. The ARfDs and AAOELs were not analysed because the overall main objective of including dog toxicity studies in hazard assessment of pesticide active substances is to identify target organ(s) after repeated exposure rather than effects of a single (acute) exposure. In general, the derivation of ARfD and AAOEL relies on expert judgement on whether the critical effect selected for setting acute HBGVs is truly of an acute nature. The updated retrospective analysis showed that dog data were used in the derivation of ARfD and AAOEL in 36 and 12 cases, respectively. Most of the observed critical effects were related to general toxicity, gastrointestinal effects (vomiting and diarrhoea), neurological signs (excessive salivation and vomiting when recognised as signs of central nervous system toxicity), effects associated with methemoglobinaemia, blood ChE inhibition and local irritation, which are generally considered inadequate for extrapolation to humans for hazard characterisation. Moreover, in alignment with the 3R principle, it is neither advisable nor recommended to conduct dog toxicity studies solely for identifying acute effects. Metrics considered for data interpretation

Although dog toxicity studies have been commonly used as a basis for deriving HBGVs, a number of considerations must be taken into account to truly identify potential cases of dog‐specific toxicity. To achieve this, a number of metrics and methods were employed by the WG (see Section 3.4 ) and described more in detail in this section.

Factor of 3

For the retrospective analysis, a factor of 3 between NOAELdog and NOAELother species was used as a screening threshold to account for the intrinsic experimental design limitations and biological variability from in vivo toxicological studies. The rationale behind this is that this factor is considered narrow, i.e. in the context of the dose‐spacing intervals set in repeated dose toxicity studies (as the dose‐spacing interval is often set between 3 and 10 in such studies); it also covers the standard toxicokinetic interspecies variability (i.e. a factor of 4.0; see EFSA Scientific Committee, 2012) and aligns with other retrospective analyses (Ono et al., 2018). This factor is not an uncertainty factor for risk characterisation but serves to distinguish differences attributable to study design (e.g. dose spacing, duration) or biological variation from true species‐specific sensitivity.

Dose spacing

A review and a comparison of the dosing regimens used in the toxicological studies were performed in order to evaluate whether the dose spacing could have influenced the selection of the lower NOAEL. If the NOAEL from another species was lower than the LOAEL in dogs, the observed difference was likely due to dose spacing rather than true species‐specific sensitivity (refer to Section 3.4 ). Indeed, the dose levels used to define the NOAEL and LOAEL are discrete values established during the study design phase. The true LOAEL could lie anywhere between these tested doses, and the actual threshold for adverse effects may therefore overlap between the species.

Other metrics: HED

Allometric scaling is a well‐known method used in regulatory toxicology that applies mathematical relationships between biological parameters, such as body weight (bw) and body surface area (BSA), to predict and support the comparison of doses and toxicokinetic parameters across species. Although HED is commonly used in pharmaceutical development, it is a general toxicological concept based on physiological scaling principles and is also recognised in regulatory toxicology for risk assessment of chemical substances, including pesticides (EFSA, 2023; Rhomberg & Lewandowski, 2004). The concept is based on the observation that metabolic rate and physiological parameters scale predictably with body size, following power laws. Scaling based on BSA is more closely correlated with physiological parameters, such as cardiac output and renal clearance, than scaling based on body weight alone (van Valkengoed et al., 2025). BSA can be calculated using the DuBois and DuBois formula (Burton, 2008): BSA=0.007184×Weight inkg0.425×Height incm0.725

A key component of this method is the Km value, 9 which represents the ratio of BSA to bw and varies across species (Reagan‐Shaw et al., 2008). It is used to convert the mg/kg dose used in a study to an mg/m2 dose. Km=bwkgBSAm2kg

The Km values are derived from average BSA calculations for both human and animal species. For instance, the BSA of a 70 kg human (assuming a height of 170 cm) is approximately 1.8 m2, which corresponds to a Km value of 38.7. For animals, the typical K m values are approximately 3 for mice, 6 for rats, 12 for rabbits and 20 for dogs. The Km value simplifies conversion to HED through the following formula (Reagan‐Shaw et al., 2008): HEDmgkg=Animal DosemgkgxAnimalKmHumanKm

Because dogs have a lower surface area‐to‐body mass ratio relative to rodents, dose extrapolations were normalised using allometric principles to facilitate the interpretation of apparent interspecies differences in NOAELs. In this assessment, such scaling (including the calculation of the HED) was used solely as a comparative and interpretative screening tool and not for the derivation of HBGVs. The application of standard regulatory uncertainty factors remains unchanged and fully protective of human health.

BMD modelling

The consideration of using and comparing BMD values between species is driven by the fact that the identification of the NOAEL is highly dependent on the selection of spacing between the tested doses in the toxicological studies (Niemann et al., 2023). Since the NOAEL is defined as the highest dose tested in a study without evidence of an adverse effect, higher NOAELs are often observed when the statistical power of the study is low (e.g. small group size). In contrast, the BMD approach evaluates the full dose–response relationship, allowing for a more refined and probabilistic analysis of the critical effect. It also allows estimation of the probability of response at any dose level.

The BMD represents a dose level associated with a predefined change in response relative to the background response that can be reliably measured, the so‐called benchmark response (BMR). The BMDL is the lower bound of the BMD's credible interval and this value is normally used as a reference point. The concept of BMD modelling offers a more refined analysis of toxicological data and has been described in further detail in several publications, including an EFSA guidance on the use of the BMD approach in risk assessment (Haber et al., 2018; EFSA Scientific Committee, 2022). Nevertheless, in the context of pesticide risk assessment, the NOAEL is the reference point most often used to derive HBGVs (and also required in Commission Regulation (EU) No. 283/2013).

Basis for the development of the tiered assessment strategy

The tiered assessment strategy was developed in response to findings from the critical investigations of cases, where experimental and biological variability could not adequately explain the differences in NOAELs or HEDs across species. These unexplained differences highlighted the need for a structured and scientifically robust approach to assess interspecies differences in toxicological hazard assessment. A central motivation for developing the strategy was the need to systematically address cases where dogs appeared to show greater sensitivity to target organ toxicity. This led to the identification of the specific data required to evaluate whether such sensitivity was truly species‐specific or instead attributable to other factors such as study design, dose spacing or toxicokinetics. The resulting tiered assessment strategy provides a set of proposed methods for evaluating higher sensitivity of dogs, together with clearly defined data requirements necessary to reach scientifically sound conclusions.

The PPR Panel WG first considered several known scientific factors, such as species‐specific differences in ADME processes and physiological characteristics that may contribute to the increased dogs' sensitivity to exposure to certain pesticides. In particular, the type and quality of information required to enable a scientifically reliable comparison of these factors across different species was discussed, and such information ultimately served as the conceptual and data‐driven basis for developing the tiered assessment strategy.

Next, the potential strategies to mitigate the uncertainties and/or resolve data gaps identified during the case investigations were addressed. In some cases, the critical investigations precluded conclusive interpretations due to the presence of unspecific effects (e.g. reduced body weight or body weight gain) and/or limited scientific understanding on the toxicological mode of action for certain active substances. Consequently, the PPR Panel WG discussed not only the type of information required to resolve these uncertainties and fill the data gaps but also explored alternative or other (new) approaches for handling situations where uncertainties/data gaps could not be fully eliminated. Finally, the tiered assessment strategy was drafted based on these foundational considerations and envisioned a stepwise process grounded in scientific evidence to justify whether dog toxicity studies are necessary on a case‐by‐case basis. The strategy is designed to ensure that decisions are based on clearly defined criteria and supported by relevant, high‐quality data.

ASSESSMENT

Outcome of the updated retrospective analysis (ToR 1)

Among the total of 461 active substances with published EFSA Conclusions and European Commission Review Reports (up to November 2024), dog toxicity studies were found to be relevant for the setting of ADI and AOEL in 107 (23%) and 183 cases (40%), respectively (see Figure 1).

Following the successive screening phase to identify and exclude cases for which the differences between the NOAEL in dog and the next lower NOAEL in other species were a consequence of experimental design and/or biological variability, only three cases for ADI (cyclaniliprole, methiocarb and sodium 5‐nitroguaiacolate) and nine cases for AOEL (amidosulfuron, cyclaniliprole, florasulam, fluoxastrobin, imazaquin, iprovalicarb, meptyldinocap, myclobutanil and sodium 5‐nitroguaiacolate) remained open for further analysis (see Figure 1 and Annex B.2 for further details).

Retrospective analysis of the relevance of regulatory accepted 90‐day and 1‐year repeated‐dose oral toxicity studies in dogs for the derivation of ADI and AOEL values of 461 active substances.

Common observations and patterns of toxicity identified

Of the 12 cases included for further analysis (3 for ADI and 9 for AOEL), the setting of the NOAEL in the 90‐day dog studies was mainly based on unspecific effects. These cases were related to general physiological responses, such as changes in body weight or body weight gain, and did not indicate any target organ toxicity or a toxicological mode of action but rather tolerability limitations associated with high dose administration (see Appendix B). Unspecific effects arise from general physiological or cellular disruption, such as oxidative stress, energy depletion or inflammation, and typically appear across species at higher doses because they are not driven by a particular molecular target. In contrast, specific effects result from defined interactions with particular biological targets (e.g. receptors, enzymes, ion channels), often occurring at lower doses and showing clearer species differences due to variations in target structure and function. In the cases where only unspecific effects were identified, the PPR Panel WG highlighted that the absence of toxicokinetic data in dogs precludes an assessment of whether dogs show any unique species‐specific features relative to other test species.

In three cases only (methiocarb, imazaquin, florasulam), the effects observed in dogs were on specific organs (methiocarb: reduction in erythrocyte and retinal cholinesterase; imazaquin: anaemia and skeletal myopathy; florasulam; anaemia, increased liver weight, and adrenal and liver vacuolation) and possibly associated with a specific mechanism of action of the active substance. Of these three, only florasulam is currently approved in the EU.

Although not identified in the retrospective analysis as substances where dog studies were critical for hazard characterisation, for some pesticide active substance classes, some recurrent patterns of toxicity were identified, namely: In the case of imazaquin, anaemia and skeletal myopathy were observed in 1‐year dog toxicity studies, and it seemed that dog was the only species in the data set able to predict this hazard. It is noteworthy that similar effects, i.e. anaemia and/or skeletal myopathies, were also reported after 1‐year exposure to other imidazolinone herbicides such as imazamethabenz‐methyl, imazapic and imazethapyr.In the cases of phenoxyacetic acids (e.g. 2,4‐D and 2,4‐DB) and pyridyloxyacetic‐acid (e.g. triclopyr) herbicides, lower NOAELs were consistently observed in dog repeated dose studies compared to respective studies in other species. These NOAELs were based on kidney toxicity and a possible MoA was related to the fact that dogs have lower capacity to actively secrete organic acids via the renal organic acid secretory system compared to rats, allowing organic acids to accumulate in the proximal renal tubular cells. The human relevance of such an effect remains unclear.Dog may be more sensitive to the effects of macrocyclic lactones (e.g. milbemectin). One study suggests that dogs of certain breeds are more sensitive to this class of compounds due to mutations in the ABCB1 gene. In addition, the PPR Panel WG performed an evaluation of the ECHA's existing Classification, Labelling and Packaging (CLP) Regulation's database 10 in order to explore the number of incidences of which the dog was used as the only species for the classification of pesticide active substances as STOT RE (single target organ toxicity after repeated exposure). A total of 84 pesticidal active substances have been listed in Annex VI of Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008 (up to ATP 22 11 ) harmonised classification of STOT RE, among which 40 substances have a Risk Assessment Committee (RAC) Opinion that is publicly available. For these 40 substances, seven (18%) revealed organ‐specific effects only observed in dogs, namely flupyradifurone, ipconazole, metosulam, picolinafen, spiroxamine, spirodiclofen and tebufenpyrad.

The eye was the most frequently affected organ (3 substances: ipconazole, metosulam, spiroxamine). For metosulam, notably higher concentrations in dog eyes compared with rodents suggest that the findings are likely driven by toxicokinetic differences rather than by a dog‐specific toxicodynamic mechanism. Effects on muscle, skin, thyroid, blood, liver and the gastrointestinal tract each occurred only once and each for different substances, and no investigation of specific mechanism of action was identified in any of the RAC opinions.

For two substances (flupyradifurone and tebufenpyrad), the ADI and AOEL values established during EFSA peer review were based on rat studies, indicating that dogs were not the most sensitive species. For the remaining five substances, HBGVs were derived from dog NOAELs; however, the observed interspecies differences can be explained by normal experimental and biological variability, dose spacing, or allometric scaling, rather than by genuine dog‐specific sensitivity (for details on applied metrics please see Chapter 3.5).

Overall, no evidence of a dog‐specific mechanism of action was identified. Notably, although the mechanism underlying the observed toxicity in dogs remains unclear, this does not affect the overall hazard characterisation of these substances.

Human Equivalent Dose (HED)

Scaling factors may play a relevant role in the lower NOAEL observed in dogs as compared to that observed in rats. Dogs have a lower surface area‐to‐body mass ratio relative to rodents, which can influence systemic exposure when doses are expressed on a mg/kg bw basis. To support interpretation of apparent interspecies differences, HED was therefore explored as a comparative screening metric.

First, for each case not excluded after the consideration of the aforementioned metrics, the critical NOAELs observed for the second species and for dogs were normalised to body surface area as described in Section 3.5 using the Km value reported in the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance (FDA, 2005). NOAELs expressed by body mass were also reported for these cases (Tables 5 and 6). Second, the corresponding HED was calculated accordingly, using the conversion factors reported in this FDA guidance.

A ratio HEDother species/HEDdog within the range of threefold was considered indicative of no meaningful difference, assuming that the standard UF of 100 (comprising 10 for interspecies and 10 for intraspecies variability) remains fully applicable and protective of human health (Tables 5 and 6).

Identification of criteria for determining the inclusion of dog toxicity studies for active substance approval process

Following the critical investigation on the 12 specific cases (for both ADI and AOEL), the PPR Panel WG elaborated criteria to robustly determine whether and under which conditions dog toxicity studies may be waived for the approval process of pesticide active substances, addressing the assessment question posed during problem formulation (‘What are the criteria to determine whether dog toxicity studies should be included as part of the data requirement for the approval of active substances included in plant protection products?’). It is important to note that, while setting the criteria, the PPR Panel WG considered the data requirements currently set out in Commission Regulation (EU) No. 283/2013 for pesticide active substances.

First and foremost, to allow a clear and robust assessment of short‐term toxicity (and potentially of other toxicological endpoints) without the inclusion of dog toxicity studies, it is critical that a comprehensive toxicological data set in one species (namely rat) is available, in line with the current data requirements listed in the Commission Regulation (EU) 283/2013, e.g. including ADME, in vitro and in vivo toxicokinetic data on rat (or on other rodent species like mouse, if justified). In addition, if available, it is relevant to retrieve information and gain understanding on the MoA (pesticidal and toxicological) of the substance (and potentially substances belonging to the same chemical class) to consider its toxicodynamics, as this information may provide sufficient justification to waive dog toxicity studies (see Section 4.2.1 for further elaboration).

For nine out of the 12 cases identified in the retrospective analysis, unspecific effects in dog toxicity studies were selected as the critical effects for deriving ADIs or AOELs. In the remaining three cases, i.e. methiocarb, florasulam and imazaquin, the effects observed in dogs involved specific target organs and were possibly associated with a specific mechanism of action of the active substance. These cases were documented and taken into account in the assessment strategy flowchart. A data deficiency consistently observed in the critical investigation that precluded complete evaluation of the cases was the lack of reliable ADME or toxicokinetic (TK) data across species. Although the retrospective analysis cannot fully exclude the possibility of a dog‐specific target organ toxicity, it reinforced the importance of a robust ADME strategy to validate and justify the relevance of selecting the rat (or mouse, if justified) as the suitable species for the hazard assessment. In particular, a CIVM study using dog, rat (or mouse, if justified) and human hepatocytes (microsomes may be used on a case‐by‐case basis) could facilitate a scientifically robust comparison of ADME properties among species.

Finally, understanding the internal exposure to an active substance across species, especially in dogs, rats (or mice, if justified) and humans, could further provide justification for potentially excluding dogs from toxicity testing. For example, if rats were shown to have a higher body burden or slower excretion of the active substance than dogs, this would support the rat as a more adequate test system to detect and quantify hazards for the substance of interest, thus justifying the waiving of dog toxicity studies. Emerging methodologies in hazard assessment, such as PBK modelling, could be employed to simulate and compare the internal exposure across species. Obtaining comprehensive ADME information in multiple species would be essential to appropriately generate the models to accurately predict internal exposure levels in different species. Consequently, this information supports the assessment of the added value of including the dog as a second species or, alternatively, supports the quantification of the level of uncertainty when a dog study is absent. The minimal information required for such PBK modelling could be obtained using various in vitro and in silico methods (refer to the Table in Section 4.2.1 for more details).

Overall, these three aspects (i.e. a comprehensive toxicological data set in rats, a proper ADME strategy and an understanding of the internal exposure across species) served as the foundation for establishing the minimum information required for applying the tiered assessment strategy described further in the section below.

Development of a tiered assessment strategy (ToR 2)

A tiered assessment strategy for justifying the inclusion or exclusion of the dog as a second species for evaluating repeated‐dose toxicity of a pesticide active substance was conceived considering the three main criteria (i.e. a comprehensive toxicological data set in rats, a proper ADME strategy, and an understanding of the internal exposure across species) and the sub‐question of the problem formulation for ToR2 (‘How can these criteria be applied and integrated into a tiered assessment strategy to determine whether dog studies should be retained or waived during the approval process of pesticide active substances?’).

To develop the strategy, the PPR Panel WG first identified the minimum information required (Table 9) and then specified acceptable test types, applicable OECD guidelines, and other published protocols for conducting the strategy (see Section 4.2.1 ). Based on the established information requirement, a workflow was created to evaluate different possible outcomes, also depending on the quality, reliability and completeness of available data. The PPR Panel WG acknowledged that there are a few outcomes in which waiving of dog toxicity studies could be scientifically and readily justified without running the entire strategy and thus established an initial step (‘preliminary phase 1’) to determine whether a (new) active substance would meet one of these outcomes and allow an early ‘exit’ from the assessment strategy. Next, in order to substantiate the scientific justification of waiving dog toxicity studies, it is pertinent to demonstrate that rat (or mouse, if justified) is a suitable model for hazard assessment; thus, this substantiation forms another early step (‘preliminary phase 2’) before entering the full flowchart of comparing data and information, including PBK modelling predictions and/or in vivo TK data, between rats (or mice, if justified) and dogs.

Finally, to test the feasibility of employing this assessment strategy, proof‐of‐concept case studies were developed by the PPR Panel WG using the currently available information (i.e. existing evidence) for the selected substances.

Development of the tiered assessment strategy

Following the collection of data on the active substances, the workflow reported in Figure 2 was designed to evaluate the information in a prioritised sequence. This workflow serves to determine and justify the selection of the species to adequately identify and characterise hazards to human health. This assessment strategy is primarily developed for new active substances (NAS) to be placed on the EU market as an approach to determine whether dog toxicity testing is truly necessary for the hazard identification and characterisation of the NAS. The proposed strategy is meant to guide applicants and risk assessors to provide information to support the waiving of the dog as a second species for the assessment of pesticide active substances. In this context, additional information not included in the strategy, but supporting the waiving decision, can be considered.

It should be specified that the tiered nature of the assessment strategy refers to a series of steps (see paragraph below for details) to be followed in sequence, and, depending on the outcome of each step, the next suitable course of action is taken, eventually leading to the final conclusion that would determine if a dog toxicity study is truly necessary for the hazard assessment.

The PBK model should allow prediction of the internal plasma exposure at least in dog, rat (or mouse) and human to enable cross‐species comparison.

Flowchart

When the results of both preliminary phases (phase 1 and 2) subsequently lead to the main flowchart, i.e. preliminary phase 1 does not exclude dog toxicity testing, and preliminary phase 2 confirms the rat (or mouse) as a suitable animal species, the assessment strategy moves to the PBK modelling. The different steps of the flowchart are outlined in the following paragraphs.

Check the acceptability of PBK model: If the measured and PBK model‐predicted internal exposures (Cmax and AUC) in rats, under both single and repeated dose conditions, do not differ by more than fivefold, 15 the PBK model predictions for the tested substance are considered acceptable and the flowchart will proceed to the next step, i.e. comparison of PBK models between rat and dog. Otherwise, in vivo TK data in dog should be generated to allow comparison of internal exposure between dog and rat.

Compare PBK models between rat and dog: If the PBK model‐predicted internal exposure in dogs is not more than threefold 16 higher than that in rats, the dog toxicity study can be waived. Otherwise, the flowchart proceeds to the next step, i.e. comparison of PBK models between rat and human.

Compare PBK models between rat and human: If the PBK model‐predicted internal exposure in dog is higher than in rats, but the PBK model‐predicted internal exposure in rats is equal to or higher than that predicted in human, then dog toxicity study can still be waived because the rat remains a suitable animal model to capture the potential human hazard. Otherwise, further in vivo TK data in dogs need to be generated and internal exposure in dogs and rats needs to be compared.

Generate in vivo TK data in dog: If the internal exposure (Cmax and AUC) measured from the TK study in dogs is more than 10‐fold 17 higher than that in rats, then the dog toxicity study cannot be waived. Otherwise, no dog toxicity study is considered necessary (i.e., the study can be waived). The PPR Panel WG acknowledges that it might be possible to still waive the dog study considering an additional uncertainty factor. It is noted that TK data in dog can be used to update the PBK model results and confirm the predictions in dog and the process can be reiterated.

Outcome of the proof‐of‐concept case studies

Four proof‐of‐concept case studies were conducted to examine whether, for a given active substance, rat data supported by mechanistic and kinetic evidence can provide a sufficiently conservative basis for human health hazard assessment such that toxicity studies in dogs as a second species are not considered necessary for the assessment, or whether dog studies would be considered to provide added value.

DISCUSSION

The use of two species (typically one rodent and one non‐rodent, the latter often being the dog) has traditionally been the standard practice for deriving HBGVs of pesticide active substances. However, advances in toxicological science, refinement of in vitro and in silico approaches and evolving ethical considerations have called into question the necessity of maintaining the dog as a default second species. The use of dogs in regulatory testing entails specialised housing, handling and high resource demands. Moreover, under the remit of chemical legislations worldwide, the dog is used as a second species only for the approval process of pesticide active substances. This Scientific Opinion presents an opportunity to critically evaluate the relevance of dog toxicity studies in the context of regulatory assessment for pesticide active substances.

Retrospective analysis (ToR1)

The update of the retrospective analysis was built upon the work previously reported by Panzarea et al. (2022), which provides contextual insight and basis for the methodology and evaluation of the substances.

In nearly all cases where dog toxicity studies were selected for the setting of the HBGVs, the differences were attributed to experimental and biological variability, differences in study design (e.g. dose spacing or selection) or allometric scaling rather than true dog‐specific sensitivity. In some other cases, the differences were attributable to unspecific effects, such as body weight changes, which are not mechanistically linked to human‐relevant toxicological pathways. After accounting for study design, variability, scaling factors and specificity of toxicity, dog toxicity studies were deemed critical for setting HBGVs in only a few cases (see Section 4.1 , Figure 1 and Annex B.2), closely matching the findings reported by Panzarea et al. (2022) and Bishop, Dellarco, and Wolf (2023). The retrospective analysis performed by Health Canada's Pest Management Regulatory Agency (PMRA) came to a similar conclusion that, for most of the pesticides, effects levels in the 1‐year dog study did not substantially differ from those in other toxicity studies after considering factors like dose spacing and experimental variability (Linke et al., 2017).

The outcome from this retrospective analysis challenges the value of dog toxicity studies in most regulatory assessments and supports the development of a more targeted, evidence‐based approach in determining when dog toxicity data are scientifically necessary. However, the updated retrospective analysis also revealed important gaps during the data evaluation, particularly the absence of standardised in vitro metabolism studies and limited availability of robust in vivo TK data sets in both dogs and rodents, which reduces the confidence in the conclusions and highlights the need for improved study design and reporting standards. The gaps provided the basis and served as criteria for developing the minimum data requirements for the tiered assessment strategy, as discussed below.

Tiered assessment strategy (ToR2)

In response to the aforementioned findings and observations, a tiered assessment strategy was developed as means to provide a structured framework to evaluate on a case‐by‐case basis whether the performance of dog toxicity studies is scientifically justified.

The interpretation of this assessment strategy, summarised in a decision flowchart (Figure 2), aims to support a nuanced, tiered decision‐making process that balances scientific rigour with pragmatic constraints. Rather than prescribing a rigid, uniform protocol with determinate course of outcomes, the strategy encourages WoE reasoning, where available information on TK, comparative metabolism and information on systemic exposure across species using PBK modelling is integrated to allow better informed decision‐making as to whether dog toxicity testing for a pesticide active substance should be included or excluded (i.e. waived). The incorporation of PBK models is particularly important, as it allows a quantitative comparison of internal exposure among rats, dogs and humans, thereby improving interspecies extrapolation (Paini et al., 2019). The rationale‐based assessment strategy proposed in this Scientific Opinion is aligned with broader regulatory trends towards reducing animal testing, e.g. Bishop, Brescia, et al. (2023) and Bishop, Dellarco, and Wolf (2023); developing integrated approaches to testing and assessment (IATAs) in order to support chemical safety (OECD, 2020); European Commission's roadmap (European Commission, 2023). EFSA has increasingly advocated for the integration of new approach methodologies (NAMs) (Cattaneo et al., 2023) and the pharmaceutical sector has similarly advanced efforts to waive the second species requirements under specific conditions as reflected in the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) S6(R1) guidance (Prior et al., 2020). However, the present Scientific Opinion extends these principles by systematically incorporating PBK modelling and human‐relevant data into a tiered regulatory decision‐making process for pesticide active substances.

In addition, the National Centre for the Replacement, Refinement and Reduction of Animals in Research (NC3R) in the UK is conducting a comparable evaluation in drug development to determine how often dogs have been critical in selecting the starting and stopping dose in early clinical studies. Although results are still preliminary (Passini et al., work in progress 18 ), NC3R suggests that the use of dogs is rarely critical to predict human toxicity. This reinforces the concept applied in this Scientific Opinion that, for environmental toxicants, the regulatory toxicology is focussed on protection rather than predicting toxicity.

Case studies

The four proof‐of‐concept case studies presented in this Scientific Opinion collectively illustrate that a WoE approach, i.e. integrating and evaluating MoA considerations and metabolite profiles as well as comparing TK data and PBK modelling, allows to assess whether human health hazard assessment for a given pesticide active substance can be performed in the absence of dog toxicity data with medium to high confidence in the assessment. While the outcomes consistently supported that rat data suffice for the hazard assessment, the degree of certainty varied across case studies. In particular, in the case of spinosad, the confidence in the outcomes was considered medium due to high uncertainty and limited data transparency (i.e. uncertainty in levels of spinosyn A and spinosyn D in spinosad), which hampered species comparison in internal exposure (see Annex C.1. for more details).

Two key challenges were identified during this exercise. First, the minimum data requirements specified in the assessment strategy (Table 9) were generally not fulfilled for the case studies. Several dossiers lacked critical data elements, such as in vitro metabolism or robust in vivo TK data in dogs and rodents, which constrained the ability to reach definitive conclusions. This gap highlights the importance of standardised study design and data reporting. Furthermore, the inherent limitations of animal models in predicting human outcomes, especially in the context of toxicodynamics, cannot be fully overcome by kinetic modelling alone (Schmeisser et al., 2023). Second, the PBK modelling tool used for the case studies (TK Plate; version 1.0.24) was most suitable only for substances with simple kinetics, as shown in the case of sulfoxaflor.

Notwithstanding the uncertainties and limitations, the proof‐of‐concept case studies clearly demonstrate the practical applicability of the assessment strategy under real‐world regulatory constraints. In nearly all cases, the waiving of dog toxicity studies could be scientifically justified using the available data set. In the case of spinosad, the lack of reliable data on the test item composition (a critical uncertainty with high impact on the assessment outcome) precluded the justification of waiving the dog study, resulting in the final outcome that waiving could not be supported. However, it was shown with the available 90‐d toxicity studies that the NOAELs between rat and dog were similar, highlighting that the dog toxicity study did not provide added value for hazard assessment of spinosad.

Limitations and challenges

There are some caveats to using the proposed assessment strategy and interpreting its outcomes. It is important to note that the strategy can be best used when the minimum data requirements, especially those needed for PBK modelling, are met. The paucity of reliable data was consistently observed during both the review of the updated retrospective analysis (resulting in identification of gaps as an outcome of the review) and the case studies (leading to a number of uncertainties, especially regarding the validity of the PBK models). For the latter, having the minimum data required for the assessment strategy could have resulted in different outcomes.

The minimal PBK modelling approach applied here was also not fully capable or suitable for handling substances with complex kinetic behaviours. For such substances, more advanced PBK models with further developed functionalities together with more elaborated kinetic data, which were not available within the scope of this project, would be required to enable a more robust interpretation of the assessment. However, the main objective of this work was to demonstrate the feasibility of the proposed assessment strategy in supporting a scientifically grounded decision to waive dog toxicity testing. More broadly, the approach illustrates how integrated evidence, particularly PBK modelling, can inform regulatory decision‐making on the need for dog studies. To ensure regulatory acceptance of waiving dog studies based on PBK modelling, the level of confidence in the model's predictive capacity (as established through the characterisation and validation criteria in OECD GD No. 331) must be sufficiently high to outweigh the uncertainty associated with not conducting in vivo dog studies.

Future considerations/forward strategy

This Scientific Opinion is part of a broader paradigm shift in toxicological risk assessment, moving away from the sole reliance upon in vivo animal studies towards human‐relevant, non‐animal methodologies. These include advanced in vitro and ex vivo systems, computational modelling and integrated testing strategies, which enhance the human relevance of toxicological assessments while reducing animal use and maintaining the same level of safety. Similar initiatives are underway in other major regulatory bodies, such as the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA, 2023), the European Medicines Agency (EMA, 2025) and the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA, 2025), along with those already mentioned above from ICH.

Regulatory toxicology ultimately aims to protect human health and animal models do not always capture human sensitivities. Advances in molecular and cellular sciences, such as omics technologies and mechanistic pathway mapping (e.g. the adverse outcome pathway (AOP) framework), enable the integration of human‐relevant animal data as well as actual human (i.e. epidemiological) data into regulatory evaluations. These approaches are already reducing the reliance on animal studies in specific areas of toxicological testing and hold promise for broader applications across other toxicological endpoints.

While this Scientific Opinion focuses on the use of dog in the toxicology evaluation of pesticide active substances, similar considerations could be applied to other higher vertebrates, such as mice and rabbits, where human‐relevant mechanistic data should ultimately form the basis of hazard and risk assessment. For example, a retrospective analysis of developmental toxicity studies in rats and rabbits was conducted by Janer et al. (2008), and the authors reached a similar conclusion as this project that there were no clear indications of specifically requiring the rabbit as an additional test species for the evaluation of developmental toxicity. Lower NOAELs in animals or derived HBGVs may provide default protection, but regulatory decisions are most scientifically justified when based on mechanisms relevant to humans. The long‐term objective is a regulatory framework that should rely entirely on validated human‐relevant evidence, representing a fundamental paradigm shift in toxicological assessment.

CONCLUSIONS

The work undertaken by the PPR Panel WG demonstrates that dog toxicity studies generally do not provide added value to the hazard assessment of pesticide active substances.

However, the available evidence indicates that a small number of cases may remain where uncertainties related to species‐specific TK, mode of action or data availability do not allow a definitive conclusion on waiving the dog as a potentially informative species. Therefore, the conclusion on limited added value should be interpreted within the scope and limitations of the current data set and associated uncertainties. This evidence‐based assessment approach is scientifically substantiated and could be further supported by integrating robust in vitro ADME and TK studies as well as PBK modelling into the future standard data requirement for the approval process of pesticide active substances.

Incorporating these elements would ensure that all relevant data, particularly those needed for PBK modelling, are generated in standardised manner aligned with established guidelines, thereby improving data quality, consistency and reliable reporting. Strengthening these methodological elements will not only enhance the scientific justification for waiving short‐term oral toxicity testing in non‐rodent species (90‐day dog study) but will also improve the quality of the risk assessment by enhancing the understanding of the toxicity of active substance and its human relevance, while fostering the use of NAMs in the approval process of pesticide active substances.

Moreover, whenever the assessment strategy indicates that dog studies do not provide a meaningful additional value to the hazard assessment of pesticide active substance, these studies should not be performed for ethical reasons.
