Don’t ignore the effects of paternalistic medical culture on maternal grief when their baby is ‘born sleeping’

Context

Stillbirth is a unique phenomenon in that the grief it brings is often overlooked. This experience challenges bereaved parents across multiple dimensions of identity, including motherhood, the role of being a patient, full citizenship and the identity of the baby. It significantly impacts their psychological well-being, subsequent pregnancies, existing relationships (such as marital and community relationships) and their attachments to living children.1 Cultural contexts and spiritual beliefs often shape how these parents experience and express grief. While much of the research on perinatal loss and grief has been conducted in Western cultures, the grief experiences of bereaved mothers in Chinese culture may differ from those observed in Western contexts.2

Methods

This descriptive qualitative study explored the impact of culture and spirituality on the grieving process of Chinese mothers who experienced stillbirth. Data were collected through in-depth interviews with bereaved mothers, focusing on their emotional responses, coping mechanisms and cultural or spiritual practices. Thematic analysis was used to identify key patterns and themes in the data.

In-depth interviews were conducted with 28 mothers who had experienced stillbirth within the past year. The study was conducted at a public hospital, the largest maternal and child health centre in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province. A purposive sampling approach and multidimensional sampling were used to select participants. Inclusion criteria included primiparous women aged 20 years or older with a diagnosis of stillbirth (fetal gestational age ≥24 weeks), while women participating in other clinical research or psychological interventions were excluded. The data were analysed using thematic analysis.

Findings

From interviews with 28 mothers diagnosed with stillbirth, three key themes and 12 subthemes were identified: (1) the influence of culture on grief expression, with subthemes: restrained expressions of grief, unattainable mourning ceremonies, hospital policy as a barrier to grieving and others—oriented self; (2) cultural characteristics of post-stillbirth experiences, with subthemes: paternalistic medical culture, ‘Kong yuezi’, embarrassment during postpartum visits and cultural taboos on dealing with deceased babies’ possessions; and (3) finding significance in the spiritual healing process, with subthemes: seeking meaning in the event, accepting and reconciling with the event, reshaping beliefs and views about life and death and achieving personal growth.2

Commentary

This study explores how culture and spirituality shape the maternal grief process after stillbirth, particularly in China. Cultural norms influence grief expression and coping mechanisms. Western bereavement support models may not suit all contexts. For example, Chinese mothers often prefer private mourning, while Western practices focus on memory creation and emotional expression. These differences may underscore the need for culturally and spiritually sensitive interventions.2

A key strength of the study is its emphasis on how hospital policies and paternalistic medical culture hinder maternal grief expression after stillbirth. Such practices, through unilateral decision-making, ignore bereaved mothers’ needs, creating a disconnect between medical procedures and emotional well-being.

Hospital policies often exacerbate the issue, as healthcare providers’ (HCP) paternalistic decisions disregard mothers’ emotional and psychological needs. Mothers are forced to follow protocols that may prevent them from seeing their deceased infants or holding mourning rituals. This experience complicates how grief is expressed and intensifies feelings of failure and inadequacy. Many mothers also express frustration over the lack of private mourning spaces, as sharing rooms with mothers celebrating live births further hinders their ability to process loss.1

The term ‘stillbirth’, commonly used in medical settings, can evoke guilt, incompleteness and personal failure in mothers.3 In studies and counselling sessions of the first author (EK) with bereaved mothers in Gorgan, Iran, the term ‘born sleeping’ was introduced as an alternative. This change received positive feedback, helping mothers shift from negative to positive emotions and better adapt to their loss. One mother shared:

“This was the most beautiful words I heard. It was the most beautiful feeling. It allowed me to think positively despite my profound sadness. There’s no blame or insult. It is not my fault the story, unlike when my doctor calls me a ‘stillbirth mother’. I wish they had said it like you did: I am a mother of a baby born sleeping.”

Culturally sensitive policies, ongoing training for HCPs and supportive systems are essential4 to the continuity of care for the stillbirth experience.5 Strategies include effective communication, involving mothers in decision-making, doing something for their deceased child, helping relieve guilt and letting cope with the stillbirth and creating supportive mourning environments.3 These measures can enhance care quality and reduce the emotional burden on grieving mothers.
