Early‐Onset Herpes Zoster in a 10‐Month‐Old Infant Following Nonvenomous Snake Exposure: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Seyedeh Mahla Hoseini, Maryam Saghafi, Fatemeh Saghafi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHerpes zoster can rarely occur in immunocompetent infants without prior varicella history. This case highlights the importance of considering herpes zoster in atypical dermatomal vesicular eruptions during infancy and suggests that physical stress or local trauma, including nonvenomous snake exposure, may act as a possible trigger for viral reactivation.

Introduction

Herpes zoster (HZ), commonly known as shingles, is a viral disease primarily affecting older adults and immunocompromised individuals [1]. It is caused by the varicella‐zoster virus (VZV), which initially manifests as varicella (chickenpox). After resolution of the primary infection, the virus remains latent in the dorsal root ganglia and can later reactivate as herpes zoster [2]. Clinically, HZ presents as a painful, unilateral, vesicular or maculopapular rash confined to a dermatome [3].

The incidence of HZ in children is extremely low, with rates below 2 cases per 1000 person‐years, compared to 4–10 cases per 1000 in adults, particularly those over 50 or immunocompromised [4]. Although HZ is associated with well‐established risk factors such as immunosuppression and chronic disease, the role of environmental triggers remains largely unexplored [5].

Here, we report a 10‐month‐old previously healthy infant who developed HZ shortly after contact with a nonvenomous snake, representing a rare temporal association with an unusual environmental exposure. To our knowledge, similar associations have not previously been reported in the literature.

Case Presentation

A 10‐month‐old previously healthy male infant, born at full term following an uncomplicated pregnancy and delivery, was brought to the clinic with irritability and localized skin eruption. Initially, the lesions were non‐specific and not clinically characteristic of herpes zoster. According to the parents, the infant had developed erythematous maculopapular lesions over the left upper back, which appeared 3 days after contact with a nonvenomous snake. The lesions were localized to the left upper thoracic region, most consistent with approximately T4–T5 dermatomal involvement.

The parents reported that the snake had crawled over the infant's exposed skin; however, no bite marks, puncture wounds, or visible skin trauma were observed. The child had no prior history of varicella infection, immunodeficiency, recent vaccination, or significant systemic illness. He had not received the varicella vaccine due to age‐related immunization scheduling. There was no maternal history of varicella or herpes zoster during pregnancy, making congenital or perinatal transmission unlikely.

The initial clinical impression was contact dermatitis, and topical mometasone was prescribed. However, within 48 h, the lesions evolved into grouped vesicles on an erythematous base with a clear unilateral dermatomal distribution over the left upper back (Figure 1), becoming more consistent with herpes zoster. The infant also exhibited irritability and discomfort during this period, although postherpetic neuralgia could not be clearly assessed because of age. Topical corticosteroid therapy was discontinued accordingly.

On physical examination, clustered vesicular eruptions on an erythematous base were observed in a unilateral dermatomal distribution over the left upper back (Figure 1). The remainder of the systemic examination was unremarkable, and no fever or lymphadenopathy was noted. Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count and inflammatory markers, were within normal limits. The diagnosis of herpes zoster was made clinically and was independently confirmed by two pediatric infectious disease specialists and three dermatologists. Virological confirmation using VZV PCR, Tzanck smear, or serological testing was not performed because of limited diagnostic availability at the time of presentation.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included herpes simplex virus infection, contact dermatitis, impetigo, and insect bite reaction. However, the dermatomal distribution and characteristic grouped vesicular morphology supported the diagnosis of herpes zoster.

The infant was treated with oral acyclovir (20 mg/kg per dose), administered three times daily for 10 days. In addition, symptomatic management with oral cetirizine, calamine lotion, and ibuprofen syrup was provided to control pruritus and discomfort. Progressive improvement was observed, and complete resolution of lesions occurred within 10 days without complications.

Further immunological assessment, including serum immunoglobulin levels and lymphocyte subsets, was suggested to exclude underlying immunodeficiency; however, considering the child's normal clinical findings, these tests were not conducted based on parental decision.

Discussion

This report describes a rare observation of a previously healthy 10‐month‐old infant who developed herpes zoster shortly after reported contact with a nonvenomous snake, representing an unusual environmental exposure not previously documented in the literature.

Although herpes zoster is primarily observed in older adults, its occurrence in pediatric populations remains uncommon, particularly in regions with widespread varicella immunization programs. The risk of HZ is higher among children with impaired immune function; however, this patient had no clinical evidence suggestive of immunodeficiency [4].

Herpes zoster reactivation has been associated with aging, immunosuppressive conditions, malignancies, emotional stress, fatigue, and radiation exposure [6]. In the present case, the temporal association with snake contact raises the possibility of a physical or emotional stress‐related trigger, although a causal relationship cannot be established. Previous population‐based studies in adults have suggested associations between physical trauma or mechanical injury and VZV reactivation [7, 8]. Similarly, isolated reports have proposed that localized stressors, including thermal injury, may precipitate HZ [9].

This patient had not received varicella vaccination, and there was no maternal history of varicella or herpes zoster during pregnancy, making vertical transmission unlikely. In addition, the parents reported no known history of active chickenpox or herpes zoster among household contacts or caregivers prior to presentation. Nevertheless, subclinical primary VZV infection or unrecognized exposure to infected individuals remains possible and may explain the establishment before latent infection prior to reactivation.

Although the child had recently experienced a mild infectious episode, there was no evidence of chronic or severe immunosuppression, consistent with the patient's otherwise healthy clinical status.

Reactivation of latent VZV depends largely on cell‐mediated immunity, which is still developing during early infancy. It is hypothesized that stressful sensory stimulation or emotional stimuli following snake exposure could transiently influence immune regulation through activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and subsequent modulation of T‐cell responses [10, 11]. However, this proposed mechanism remains speculative and cannot be inferred from a single observational case.

Limitations

This report describes a single case, and no comparative or mechanistic data are available to support a causal relationship between nonvenomous snake exposure and early‐onset herpes zoster. Therefore, a definitive association cannot be established. Although immunological evaluation was recommended, further testing was not performed because the child had no clinical evidence suggestive of immunodeficiency and the parents declined additional investigations. In addition, virological confirmation of varicella‐zoster virus infection using PCR, Tzanck smear, or serological assays was not available at the time of presentation; therefore, the diagnosis remains clinically presumptive rather than laboratory‐confirmed.

Clinical Implications

Clinicians should consider herpes zoster in infants presenting with unilateral dermatomal vesicular eruptions, even in the absence of typical risk factors or known immunodeficiency. This case also highlights the need for cautious interpretation of unusual temporal associations and the importance of considering potential environmental or stress‐related triggers as hypotheses requiring further investigation.

Future studies are needed to clarify whether atypical environmental or stress‐related exposures may contribute to VZV reactivation in otherwise healthy infants.

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare occurrence of herpes zoster in a previously healthy 10‐month‐old infant and emphasizes that dermatomal vesicular rashes in infancy should prompt consideration of HZ even in the absence of classic risk factors. Although a temporal association with nonvenomous snake exposure was observed, causality cannot be established, and the finding should be interpreted cautiously. Greater awareness of atypical presentations and potential triggering factors may facilitate earlier recognition and appropriate management of pediatric herpes zoster.
