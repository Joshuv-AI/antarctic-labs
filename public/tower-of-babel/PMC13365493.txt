Application of solid modeling techniques for geometric simulation of surface topography in boring operations
Authors: Mohammad Mehrabinasab, Behnam Moetakef-imani, Mohsen Fallah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe quality of the machined surface and the dynamic stability of the cutting process are directly correlated to the dynamic cutting force, which itself depends on the geometry of engagement between the cutting tool and workpiece that defines the dynamic chip thickness. The surface topography is one of the most important figures of merit for the evaluation of performance in machining processes, which is influenced by the geometry of the cutting edge, the kinematics of the cutting operation, the flexibility of the machine tool structure, and the resulting structural vibrations during the chip formation process. In order to define the instantaneous engagement between the cutting tool and workpiece precisely, all these factors should be taken into account. In this paper, the mechanics, dynamics, and geometry of boring operations are considered for the development of a virtual simulation model by using the solid modeling techniques. The dynamic parameters of the boring bar are defined by modal analysis experiments, and the cutting force coefficients are experimentally identified by conducting mechanistic cutting tests. The experimental cutting tests are conducted in both absolutely stable and unstable cutting conditions. In order to validate the developed model, firstly, the simulated cutting forces are compared with the corresponding experimental results. Secondly, the simulated topography of the machined surface in both stable and unstable cutting conditions is compared with the SEM images from real cut surfaces. The presented geometric simulation model shows a remarkable potential for exact simulation of boring operations in stable and unstable conditions.Keywords: Geometric simulation, Boring bar, ACIS solid modeler, Surface topography, ChatterSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The term “virtual machining” means to use digital computers for the purpose of simulating the geometry, mechanics, and dynamics of machining processes, in order to improve the performance and stability of cutting operations during the production of industrial products. Various researchers have presented machining simulation models that predict the behavior of the cutting process in different conditions. The accurate estimation of dynamic cutting force is one of the main objectives in these models, which helps to optimize the performance of cutting processes. The cutting force can be computed by analytical, numerical, or mechanistic approaches1. The mechanistic force model has remarkable advantages over other methods for modeling the mechanics of cutting processes in various cutting operations with different cutting edge geometries2. The cutting coefficients and the dynamic chip thickness are the two main variables that should be properly computed for the estimation of dynamic cutting forces. The cutting force in machining processes should be appropriately calculated to study the regenerative chatter phenomenon and its impact on the topography of the cut surface.

The dynamic cutting force is influenced by not only the cutting parameters such as spindle speed, feed rate, depth of cut, and geometric parameters of the cutting edge, but also it highly depends on the cutter workpiece engagements (CWE), which is affected by the structural response of the cutting tool. In fact, the CWE engagement is directly influenced by the structural vibrations imposed on the tool’s tip. The instantaneous engagement geometry will change due to the cutting vibrations, which change the instantaneous dynamic chip thickness accordingly. Various geometric simulation models are proposed for the exact computation of CWE, which can be classified as solid modeling approaches, discrete methods, and analytical approaches.

The solid modeling method was first developed almost six decades ago, when the CAD/CAM systems started to represent models with geometric dimensions3. Today, the acceptable solid modeling approaches are constructive solid geometry (CGS)45 and boundary representative (B-rep)6. In the B-rep method, the vertices, edges, and faces are used in order to describe the boundaries of solid geometries7, in which various parametric representations such as Bezier, B-spline, and NURBS can be used in order to model the curves, surfaces, and volumes. It uses parametric representation and topological information to determine the connectivity and adjacency between the geometric elements8. The B-rep technique provides an exact and continuous model for the cutter swept volume during the CWE, but computing the intersections between surfaces is rather time-consuming9. The idea behind the CGS method is to combine simple geometric volumes, such as a cube or a sphere, by using Boolean operations, in order to provide a simple representation of more complex solid geometries. This method was used for the computation of CWE and the simulation of cutting forces in machining processes45. However, the B-rep method has better capabilities for modeling of CWE in multi-axis machining processes of complex sculptured surfaces, and most of the recent research in this field was conducted by using the B-rep method. By integrating cutting edge geometry and trochoidal motion within the ACIS environment, researchers were able to simulate semi-roughing operations6. Another study concluded that theoretical surface roughness models could be successfully validated against real micro-milling experiments, proving that solid modeling is a robust tool for predicting finish quality10. Further developments expanded these capabilities to 2.5-axis milling and utilized Boolean operators to update workpiece volumes dynamically.11. There are some other studyies in litrature1213, have used a B-rep-based model to compute the chip volume in other machining processes. Further research have used the triangular mesh model as a simpler B-rep based method to model the geometry of machining processes1415.The disadvantage was computation of intersections that required the updating of a NURBS-based B-rep model, which was computationally demanding. In another study16, the application of B-rep solid modeling was suggested without continuous updating of the workpiece surface, but it was only performed once after the completion of each tool path.While these investigations confirmed the high geometric fidelity of B-rep models, they also highlighted a persistent challenge: the heavy computational load required to calculate surface intersections in real-time. Consequently, discrete methods like Z-mapping and Z-buffer became the preferred choice for defining engagement in sculptured surface machining due to their speed.

Z-map is the most adopted approach in which the workpiece boundary is shown with the intersection points of the Z direction vectors (ZDV) with the upper surface of the part17. But, only one intersection between the model and ZDV is allowed. In order to solve this issue, later research allowed the storage of multiple intersections and gaps between the intersection points. This method was suitable for shapes that do not have overhangs. The dexcel representation was based on the Z-map approach that was specifically developed for workpieces with overhang18. Nie and Feng introduce Adaptive voxel-update schemes, which are a uniform voxel framework with a direct voxel-tracing algorithm19. In order to generate a “ functional cutter surface,” they trace the tool geometry, then, perform Boolean subtraction. The advantage is avoiding scanning the whole workpiece, and the CWE map is calculated by voxelizing cross-sectional circles of the cutter. The discrete methods have lower accuracy compared to solid modeling techniques due to the rasterization problem, which diminishes by sharpening the resolution at the cost of higher computational cost. In order to overcome this issue, different analytical approaches were suggested by the researchers. By using the B-spline theory20, the geometry of cutting edge was modeled in boring operations as well as the kinematics and dynamics of the cutting tool. They used a geometric algorithm for exact computation of the boundaries of the removed chip, the contact length of the cutting edge as well as the dynamic chip load distribution on the cutting edge.The advantages of the method was considering the tool tip radious in computing the CWE engagement.

Recent research combined analytic geometry and discrete models to achieve high accuracy and efficiency8.Nie and Zhao suggest a tri-level grid (tri-dexel) method21. In fact, the discrete voxel output is optimized with analytic envelop computation, reaching high precision. With the advent of 4D geometric models, researchers represent the continuous evolution of the tool and workpiece in space-time. Zhang et al.22 to compute the instantaneous engagement and perform Boolean set operations in 4D construct two 4D meshes: one for the time-dependent simulation (WOR) and one for the tool’s swept volume (TOR) in 4D. Building these models is computationally heavy;This model is able to capture CWE with a continuous spatio-temporal representation.

In the machining era, CWE modeling in turning and boring is simpler than milling because the engagement area remains continuous. By contrast, in milling due to rotating multi-edge cutters and complex multi-axis toolpath calculating, CWE are far more challenging. Prior researchers have studied the engagement geometry and tool vibration in boring operations separately. This study bridges the gap, calculating the CWE by considering machining vibration in a solid modeling environment. Exact simulation of the cut surface in machining operations is a very complicated task, especially in unstable cutting conditions, in which the surface topography is affected by the vibration marks left on the cut surface during the previous revolutions of the spindle. In this article, a virtual simulation model of the boring process is developed within the ACIS solid modeling environment to predict surface topography in both stable and unstable cutting conditions Henceforth, the article is organized as follows: First, the dynamic parameters of the boring bar and the mechanistic cutting force coefficients are experimentally identified through modal analysis and mechanistic cutting tests. Next, the kinematics of the boring operation and the exact geometry of the cutting edge are modeled using a boundary representation (B-rep) approach. The model is then extended to include the dynamics of the process by solving the equations of motion for the flexible tool in the radial direction and adding the resulting instantaneous displacements to the kinematic motion. The proposed model is validated by comparing simulated cutting forces and acceleration signals with experimental results in stable and unstable conditions. Finally, the simulated topography of the machined surface is compared with SEM images of real cut surfaces to evaluate the model’s accuracy in predicting feed marks and chatter grooves.

Experimental setup

The cutting tests are conducted on a general-purpose lathe machine tool. The boring bar is the most flexible component in the machine tool structure. It is highly susceptible to cutting vibrations in the radial direction. The dynamics of boring bar are directly affected by the length-to-diameter ratio (LD) of the cutting tool. In order to measure the tool’s vibrations, IEPE accelerometer sensors are installed near the cutting head. The experimental setup for identification of cutting force coefficients is shown in Fig. 1. The Kistler 9255-B dynamometer is used as the clamping support of the boring bar, in order to measure the cutting forces in three mutually perpendicular directions. The experimental setup for modal analysis tests is also illustrated in Fig. 2. The experimental vibration data was gathered using Tenlee IEPE accelerometers mounted at the tool tip. The primary sensor used was the TL122A100 model, which provides a frequency range of 0.5 to 10 kHz and a shock limit of ±2000g peak. These sensors facilitate measurements in both single-axis and triaxial configurations. For the models employed in this study, the acceleration measurement range is rated at 100g, ensuring high-fidelity signal capture within a linear response zone below 5 kHz.

Parameters of boring operations

The chip formation process in boring operations is a three-dimensional, geometrically intricate phenomenon that is affected by various parameters. For instance, it is influenced by geometric parameters (such as tool working angles and cutting edge radius), mechanical parameters (the cutting coefficient of workpiece material), dynamical parameters (the model parameters of the cutting tool), and the cutting parameters (cutting speed, cutting depth, and feed rate). The influential parameters of the boring process are briefly introduced in this section.

Dynamic parameters of boring bar

Experimental modal analysis is a well-adopted approach for obtaining the response of flexible structures in the frequency domain. The impact applied by the instrumented hammer excites a broad range of the system’s frequencies, and the frequency response function (FRF) of the system is computed by dividing the discrete fourier transform (DFT) of the output signal (displacement, velocity, or acceleration) by the DFT of the input force signal. The modal instruments used in this study are shown in Fig. 3. The modal tests are conducted for two different LD of the steel boring bar. The experimental and analytical FRFs of boring bar in the radial direction for LD 4 and 8 are illustrated in Fig. 6 and Fig. 7, respectively. The dynamics of the boring bar is only studied in the radial direction, as the radial deflections have a dominant effect on the dynamic chip thickness and dimensional accuracy compared to the tangential direction27. The peak picking method is used to extract the modal parameters of boring bar structure around the dominant fundamental frequency. The obtained parameters are reported in Table 2. The differences between the experimental data and the fitted FRF are not significant, particularly considering that the FRF amplitude is plotted on a dB scale, which visually amplifies the discrepancies between the two curves compared with a linear scale representation. Nevertheless, as shown in Figs. 6 and 7, the experimental data and the fitted FRF exhibit very good agreement near the fundamental frequency, where the two curves almost completely overlap. Therefore, the modal parameters extracted from the fitted curves accurately represent the dynamic behavior of the boring bar in the vicinity of the fundamental frequency. According to the reported values, the natural frequency and damping of boring bar structure with LD 4 are much higher than the boring bar with LD 8. In fact, in real cutting tests, it is observed that the cutting process is highly stable with the cutting tool that has the LD 4. But, due to a lack of dynamic stiffness, the cutting process is unstable in almost every cutting condition by using the boring bar with LD 8.

Geometric simulation

The boring operation is simulated inside the environment of ACIS. All geometric dimensions, kinematic parameters, and dynamic factors are defined in the programming module of ACIS. Examples of geometric parameters include workpiece dimensions, cutting edge geometry, and tool path. The kinematics of motion is defined by feed rate, depth of cut, and spindle speed. The dynamic parameters include the natural frequency, modal damping, and static stiffness for the fundamental mode of the boring bar.

Kinematics of cutting process

In this section, the kinematics and geometry of the cutting process are used to formulate the motion of the cutting edge and present the surface generation algorithm for boring operations. The geometry of the cutting edge, including the cutting edge radius, side cutting edge angle () and back cutting edge angle (), is clearly depicted in Fig. 8. In boring operations, the cutting edge constantly removes chips from the workpiece surface. The spindle rotation is required for the continuation of the chip formation process, while the cutting tool moves into the workpiece hole. The kinematics of boring operation directly affect the geometry, accuracy, and quality of the machined surface topography.

The profile of cutting edge at initial condition (-) and ready for cutting operation () by considering the machining angles.

The kinematics of boring operation are implemented based on the theory of homogeneous transformation matrix (HTM). The incremental movements of the cutting edge and workpiece can be represented by the combination of individual motions, i.e., rotation around the workpiece axis and translation along the tangential direction. The profile of cutting edge is transformed by linear transformation matrices in order to generate the envelope of the machined surface. In addition to the kinematics of motion, the radial vibrations of cutting tool are also considered in the proposed model. Two different coordinate systems are defined in order to describe the surface generation mechanism in boring operations, which are shown in Fig. 9.

Dynamics of cutting process

Regenerative chatter is an unwanted vibration phenomenon that occurs in most of metal cutting processes, and if not properly controlled, it will lead to deterioration of surface quality, dimensional accuracy, and generation of noise or damage to the machine tool structure. The regeneration of waviness and coupling of structural modes during the cutting process are the primary mechanisms for generation of chatter vibrations. It is a very complicated task to define the individual effect of each the two mechanisms on the process stability, as these two phenomena are inherently correlated with each other, especially in rough cutting conditions28. This paper only includes the regenerative chatter phenomenon by considering the dynamics of the cutting tool in the radial direction. The regeneration effect is due to the presence of phase shift between the subsequent vibrations. ACIS simulation environment

3D ACIS® Modeler provides a powerful environment for modeling of complicated geometries. ACIS is a geometric molder developed based upon the B-rep technique with Open C++ object oriented architecture. It can be used to model complex analytical geometries as well as modeling highly intricate free-form curves and surfaces by using the theories of B-spline and NURBS. This software is utilized by a verity of software developers in a diverse range of engineering software packages including computer-aided-design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM), computer-aided-engineering (CAE), architecture engineering18. In this paper, ACIS environment is used for the purpose of simulating the boring process with a geometric approach in order to extract the exact geometry of the machined surface topography. The “scheme” language is used in order to perform the mathematical computations on arrays, lists and vectors as well as creating analytical geometries in the ACIS environment. It allows for the development of a variety of platforms through the development of script programs, optimization compilers, graphic packages and virtual reality systems as well as supporting the object-oriented programming approach11.

Simulation of boring process in ACIS environment

The kinematics of boring bar is included in the first simulation stage and the geometry of machined surface is generated without including the dynamic vibrations at tool’s tip. In this stage, only the geometry of cutting edge and workpiece is included as well as the kinematic parameters of the cutting process. The boring simulation is executed by using the appropriate coordinate transformations. In the next stage, the instantaneous dynamic vibrations of the boring bar during the chip formation process is calculated and also added to the geometric simulation algorithm in order to model the surface topography in the presence of dynamic vibrations

Kinematic simulation in scheme

The basic idea of geometric simulation is based upon the fact that the cutting edge sweeps a volume during its kinematic motion relative to the workpiece. The surface of workpiece is formed by the subtraction of this swept volume form the workpiece volume. The engagement volume also defines the removed chip geometry. Thus, the cutting edge is incrementally rotated and translated with respect to the workpiece at each time step. The swept volume at each increment is defined by specifying the instantaneous sweep direction. During each increment, the center of the cutting edge is transformed by a fraction of the feed. The position of the cutting edge also transforms due to the incremental rotation of cutting edge relative to workpiece. In order to detect the cutting edge sweep volume, the algorithmic module developed in Section "Geometric parameters of boring bar" is used. The removed chip is modeled by knowing the two subsequent positions of the cutting edge. At each position, the vectors tangent to the trajectory of cutting edge should be properly modeled. The delay in the measured signal’s growth can be attributed to the physical time required for the regenerative effect to build up sufficient energy in the actual machine-tool system, whereas the simulation responds more rapidly. The eventual surpassing of the simulated signal by the measured one highlights the non-linear nature of real-world chatter. As the vibration amplitude increases, factors such as tool-flank interference (process damping) and non-linearities in the cutting force–which are simplified in the linear model–can lead to a more rapid escalation of instability in the experimental setup.

The dynamic stiffness of the boring bar greatly reduces in the radial direction with an increase of LD from 4 to 8. The topography of machined surface radically changes due to the transition of cutting dynamics from stable state to unstable one. The geometry of chatter grooves is directly correlated with the chatter frequency, spindle speed and regenerative phase shift. By using the radial deflections of cutting tool as well as the cutting geometry and mechanics, the presented model can simulate the topography of cut surface is both stable and unstable cutting conditions.

Conclusions

The topography of machined surface in boring operations is affected by the kinematics, geometry and dynamics of the cutting process. In this article, a geometric simulation model is developed in the ACIS solid modeler environment for the prediction of surface topography in stable and unstable cutting conditions. The solid modeling approach is the more exact and precise method for computation of the CWE in machining processes. The proposed model includes the dynamics of boring bar into the geometric simulation model as opposed to the previous studies. The topography of machined surface in stable cutting conditions is characterized by the feed marks which are generated by the tip of cutting edge with finite radius. On the other hand, the dynamics of boring bar in mutual interaction with the dynamics of cutting process plays a primary role on the topography of the machined surface in unstable cutting conditions. The chatter grooves or channels are thus the main feature of cut surface in unstable cutting conditions as shown by the results of this research.

A comparison between Figs. 30 and 27 indicates that feed marks constitute the dominant surface feature in Fig. 27, whereas surface tearing becomes the dominant characteristic in Fig. 30. The tearing marks are localized and clearly observable at the bottom of the grooves. Fig. 29 corresponds to the conditions presented in Fig. 26. As shown in Fig. 26, under stable cutting conditions, the vibrations induced by the cutting process exhibit a damped response. In contrast, under unstable cutting conditions, the acceleration amplitude remains nearly constant and undamped due to the regenerative chatter mechanism. In the stable regime, an impulsive vibration with a relatively large amplitude occurs once per spindle revolution, which subsequently decays over time. However, in the unstable regime, the tool acceleration amplitude does not decay and instead maintains a significant and nearly constant magnitude.

The proposed model can reasonably predict the surface topography of workpiece in both stable and unstable cutting conditions. Because it includes the exact geometry of cutting edge, as well as the modal parameters of the boring bar related to its fundamental mode and the cutting force coefficients of the workpiece material. The presented dynamic geometric simulation model can be extended to other cutting processes as well in order to provide a deeper understanding of the impact of cutting mechanics and dynamics on the topography of the cut surface, especially in unstable cutting conditions.

The spindle frequency in this study is equal to 8.33 Hz which is far from the fundamental structural frequency of the boring bar with LD4 (in stable cutting condition with 600 Hz) as well as boring bar with LD8 (in unstable cutting condition with 200 Hz). The root cause of sever vibrations with LD8 is the regenerative chatter mechanism, which is a self-excited vibration phenomenon rather than forced structural vibration phenomenon, caused by matching of the spindle frequency with the fundamental structural resonance of the boring bar.

In boring, the tool is a cantilever beam. Its stiffness decreases with the cube of the length (L3). Even a slightly flexible workpiece is often 10–100 times stiffer than a boring bar with LD8. If the workpiece frequency is much higher than the tool frequency, it doesn’t participate significantly in the vibration. The Kistler dynamometer used in our setup provides a very stiff and stable base, supporting the “rigid clamping” assumption for this specific experiment. While this study focuses on the radial vibrations of the flexible boring bar, future developments could incorporate the structural dynamics of the workpiece and clamping system. In cases with low workpiece stiffness, the interaction between the natural frequencies of the tool and the part would further influence the global dynamic behavior and the resulting cutter-workpiece engagement.
