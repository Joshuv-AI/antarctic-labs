Optimization of direct organogenesis from immature inflorescence explants of coconut (Cocos nucifera L.)
Authors: Shareefa Mohammedali, Regi Jacob Thomas, J S Sreelekshmi, Maya Lekshmi, M K Rajesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mass propagation of elite, high-yielding coconut palms is a primary objective of many coconut improvement programs, as it enables the clonal multiplication of disease-resistant genotypes with superior productivity and other valuable agronomic traits. The present study aimed to establish a protocol for direct organogenesis from immature inflorescences, bypassing an intermediary callus phase, to minimize the risk of somaclonal variation among regenerated plantlets. Immature inflorescences were collected from 25–30-year-old West Coast Tall palms. Those with an outer spathe length of 2–12 cm were selected, and rachillae segments 1.0 mm long were excised and cultivated under dark conditions on three culture initiation media (WPM, Y3, and MS) containing various plant growth regulator combinations. After eight months of culture, WPM supplemented with 5 mg L⁻1 TDZ proved most effective for culture initiation, yielding the highest number of vegetative buds and the least amount of tissue browning. The greatest number of multiple shoots and the most pronounced shoot elongation were achieved on WPM medium containing NAA (1 mg L⁻1), BAP (1 mg L⁻1), and TDZ (5 mg L⁻1). Subsequent shoot elongation was further optimized on Y3 medium supplemented with NAA (2 mg L⁻1) and BAP (5 mg L⁻1), which also promoted the maximum number of leaves. For rooting, a reduction in the basal salt concentration of the medium, combined with the addition of NAA and IBA, proved to be the most effective treatment. Rooted plantlets were successfully hardened under controlled conditions before being acclimatized in a greenhouse. Genetic fidelity assessment using SSR marker analysis confirmed a high level of genetic uniformity between the regenerated plantlets and their respective mother palms. This protocol describes a reliable and efficient method for producing true-to-type, healthy, and high-yielding coconut plants, particularly for tall cultivars and hybrids. Introduction

Coconut (Cocos nucifera L.) is a vital tropical palm that supports the livelihoods of more than 50 million smallholder farmers worldwide. It is cultivated across over 12 million hectares in approximately 94 countries, predominantly in the Asia–Pacific region1. Indonesia is the leading global producer, followed by the Philippines and India, reflecting the crop’s significant socio-economic importance in these regions. Despite its importance, the global coconut sector faces numerous challenges that hinder its growth and sustainability. The sector is vulnerable to climatic fluctuations, natural disasters, droughts, and recurrent pest and disease outbreaks. One of the most critical constraints affecting coconut productivity is the shortage of high-quality planting stock, which severely hampers efforts to enhance productivity. This scarcity limits the large-scale dissemination of improved varieties with superior yield potential and resilience to biotic (pests and diseases) and abiotic (drought and salinity) stresses. Although conventional breeding programs have contributed significantly by identifying elite genotypes and parental palms with desirable traits, the large-scale multiplication of these improved varieties remains a major bottleneck2. In this context, plant tissue culture offers a promising approach for mass propagation of superior genotypes. However, coconut is considered a recalcitrant species for in vitro regeneration, with low regeneration frequencies, phenolic exudation, and strong genotype dependence limiting its efficiency3. Therefore, further refinement of culture protocols is essential to enhance the efficiency and applicability of biotechnological approaches in coconut improvement.

To date, a range of explants, such as immature leaves, plumules, zygotic embryos, immature inflorescences, immature ovaries, anthers, and roots, among others, have been explored for in vitro regeneration of coconut, yielding variable success in callus induction, somatic embryogenesis, maturation, and plantlet regeneration4. Among these, plumules from zygotic embryos5 and rachillae from immature inflorescences6 have shown the most promising results7 and, as a result, have been the most extensively studied. However, plumule explants do not guarantee the propagation of palms with proven field performance. At such an early developmental stage, especially for tall varieties and hybrids, the agronomic potential of progenies cannot be reliably predicted. Consequently, plantlets regenerated from plumules of zygotic embryos may exhibit considerable variability in yield, disease resistance, and stress tolerance, which makes them less suitable for the clonal propagation of elite genotypes.

In contrast, rachillae explants obtained from immature inflorescences of adult palms offer a more reliable alternative. These explants enable clonal propagation of high-performing palms with well-established traits, such as superior yield potential and resistance or tolerance to major pests, diseases, and abiotic stresses8. Consequently, the use of rachillae from immature inflorescences offers significant potential for the mass multiplication of elite genotypes with known phenotypic and agronomic attributes, which is essential for the genetic improvement and commercial expansion of coconut plantations.

Until recently, somatic embryogenesis was considered the only viable pathway for achieving clonal propagation of coconut plants9. However, direct organogenesis is emerging as a promising alternative10, this approach involves developing vegetative buds directly from the meristematic tissues of immature inflorescences, bypassing the intermediate callus phase. The elimination of the callus stage is particularly advantageous because it minimizes the risk of somaclonal variation, thereby ensuring greater genetic fidelity in regenerated plants. Consequently, the successful regeneration of plantlets from immature inflorescence explants via direct organogenesis is of significant importance for coconut improvement programs. This method provides a practical solution for the clonal propagation of tall varieties and hybrids, which are otherwise extremely difficult to propagate clonally due to the cross-pollinated and genetically heterozygous nature of coconut palms. Efficient clonal propagation methods are urgently needed to overcome the limitations of seed-based propagation, in which genetic variability among progeny poses challenges to uniformity and large-scale cultivation.

The present study aimed to develop a refined regeneration protocol for coconut using immature inflorescences, to produce true-to-type, root (wilt) disease-free, and high-yielding palms of the West Coast Tall cultivar.

Materials and methods

Explant preparation

Immature inflorescences were collected by destructive sampling from 25- to 30-year-old healthy, high-yielding West Coast Tall coconut (Cocos nucifera var. typica) palms maintained at the farm of ICAR-Central Plantation Crops Research Institute, Regional Station, Krishnapuram, Kerala, India (9° 08′ N and 76° 30′ E). After removing the leaves and bunches, the stem apex cylinder containing the crown portion was brought to the laboratory (Fig. 1a). Unopened immature inflorescences with an outer spathe length ranging from 2 to 12 cm were carefully collected (Fig. 1b). The inflorescences were wiped with 70% alcohol and placed under aseptic conditions. Rachillae were sliced into 1 mm explants using a surgical blade and inoculated into conical flasks containing 100 mL of culture initiation medium. For the culture initiation stage, five media combinations were evaluated. The basal medium was supplemented with 100 mg L⁻1 L-glutamine, 40 g L⁻1 sucrose, 1 g L⁻1 activated charcoal, and 6 g L⁻1 agar. The pH of the media was adjusted to 5.8 using 0.1 N NaOH or 1 N HCl before autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min.

The cultures were initially incubated under diffused light conditions of 100 lx light intensity for 16 h and, after one month, were transferred to a 16-h photoperiod provided by cool white fluorescent lamps (Philips) with light intensity of 2500 lx. In vitro rooting

For in vitro rooting, individual plantlets with 3–4 well-expanded leaves were transferred to rooting media consisting of either full-strength or half-strength MS or Y3 basal medium formulations, fortified with different concentrations of auxins (NAA, IAA, and IBA), applied individually or in combination. In addition, the influence of varying sucrose concentrations (10 g L⁻1, 20 g L⁻1, and 40 g L⁻1) and activated charcoal levels (0, 0.5, and 1.0 g L⁻1) on root induction and development was systematically evaluated. To minimize photodegradation of auxins and enhance their stability and availability during root induction, the lower portion of the culture tubes was wrapped with aluminium foil. This modification was intended to reduce light exposure to the rooting zone, thereby improving auxin stability and creating more favourable conditions for adventitious root formation.

Hardening and acclimatization

Healthy, well-rooted plantlets were selected for hardening. The process was initiated by carefully removing the plantlets from the culture tubes and gently rinsing the root systems under running tap water to eliminate all traces of adhering medium. The cleaned plantlets were then immersed in a 0.2% (w/v) Carbendazim solution for 10 min as a prophylactic treatment against microbial infection. Subsequently, the plantlets were transplanted into small disposable cups containing a sterilized potting mixture of sand, cocopeat, and perlite in a 2:1:1 ratio. The cups were placed inside storage containers and sealed tightly with cling film to maintain high humidity. After two months of initial hardening, the plantlets were transferred to small pots, covered with transparent polythene covers, and kept at room temperature. Gradual acclimatization to ambient conditions was achieved by making small holes in the polythene covers to facilitate a progressive reduction in humidity. Fully hardened plantlets were finally transplanted into polybags and maintained in the greenhouse for further establishment.

Clonal fidelity analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from spindle leaves of the mother palms and from young leaves of respective in vitro–derived plantlets sampled during the hardening stage. The extraction was carried out using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Genetic fidelity was evaluated by PCR amplification using a set of 10 coconut-specific simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers. The marker panel comprised five previously reported SSR primers (CAC71, CAC72, CAC84, CAC39, and CAC52) developed by Perera et al.14 and five in-house SSR primers (DSSR1, DSSR2, DSSR3, DSSR4, and DSSR5) developed at ICAR-CPCRI for coconut genetic studies (unpublished). Details of the SSR primers and their sequences are provided in Table 1. Each PCR reaction was performed in a final volume of 10 µl, containing 10 ng of template DNA and 30 ng each of forward and reverse primers in 1X PCR master mix comprising 10X Taq buffer with MgCl₂, 200 µM dNTPs, and 1 U of Taq DNA polymerase. The amplification was carried out under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min; followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, primer annealing at 50–60 °C (depending on the Tm of the primers used) for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min; with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. All PCR amplifications were performed in a thermal cycler (SureCycler 8800, Agilent Technologies, USA). The amplified products were resolved by electrophoresis on 2% high-resolution agarose gel and documented using Gel Doc (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were laid out in a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications per treatment. Each replication consisted of 10 culture flasks or test tubes, which served as the experimental units. To ensure reproducibility, the entire experiment was conducted twice under identical conditions, and the pooled data from the two independent experiments were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical analysis was performed using GRAPES 1.1.0 software, developed by Kerala Agricultural University15, and treatment means were compared using an appropriate test to assess statistical significance.

Results and discussion

Three basal media Y3, MS, and WPM- were evaluated in preliminary experiments to assess their effectiveness in minimizing tissue browning. The influence of different treatments on the extent and progression of browning was monitored at regular intervals until 240 days after inoculation (DAI). Considerable variation was observed among treatments in both the onset and severity of browning (Fig. 2). In most treatments, browning was initiated within seven days after inoculation and progressively increased, affecting 100% of explants by 140 DAI. In contrast, rachilla explants cultured on Y3 medium supplemented with 1 mg L⁻1 2,4-D, and on WPM containing 5 mg L⁻1 TDZ plus 7 mg L⁻1 2,4-D, showed no browning up to 45 DAI, with only a gradual increase thereafter. Even at 240 DAI, tissue browning in these treatments remained below 20%, indicating their effectiveness for successful culture initiation and establishment.

Response of rachille explants to various media in dark condition. Media I: Y3 + 2,4-D 1 mg L⁻1, Media II: MS + 2,4-D 100 mg L⁻1 + 2ip 3 mg L⁻1, Media III: Y3 + Picloram 72 mg L⁻1, Media IV: Y3 + Picloram 48 mg L⁻1, Media V: WPM + TDZ 5 mg L⁻1 + 2,4-D 7 mg L⁻1.

Browning observed in treatments II, III, and IV may be attributed to the high concentrations of 2,4-D and picloram in the initial medium. Their herbicidal nature at elevated levels can induce physiological stress, promoting the release of phenolic compounds. Subsequent oxidation by polyphenol oxidase and peroxidase forms toxic quinones, leading to tissue browning and necrosis. This response is more pronounced in coconut due to its high endogenous phenolic content. Studies have shown that lower concentrations of auxins are generally associated with reduced browning compared to higher doses, highlighting the importance of precise hormonal balance during culture initiation. In coconut inflorescence culture, media supplemented with high levels of 2,4-D (e.g., ≥ 50–100 mg L⁻1) have been associated with severe browning within a few days of inoculation (-16).

Regeneration of coconut plantlets through direct organogenesis using immature inflorescence explants remains one of the least explored tissue culture pathways in coconut. Only a limited number of studies have attempted this approach7,10,17–19. The significance of this regeneration route is particularly high for clonal propagation in coconut, a crop characterized by high cross-pollination and extreme heterozygosity. The findings of the present study confirm that TDZ a potent cytokinin-like plant growth regulator, influences morphogenesis by modulating the endogenous auxin–cytokinin balance, often enhancing cytokinin-dominant conditions that favour shoot induction and organogenic responses in coconut. Earlier reports also support the effectiveness of TDZ in enhancing morphogenic response, although the optimal concentration varies with the nature of the explant. Chandrakala et al.20 reported a 75% shoot-induction rate when coconut zygotic embryos were treated with 150 μM TDZ. Similarly, Raju17 reported that immature inflorescence tissues responded favourably to relatively low TDZ levels, and the combination of TDZ (4.54 μM) with picloram (8.28 μM) induced up to 14 shoots per explant. Comparable observations have been made in other perennial palms. Taha et al.21 emphasized the positive effect of TDZ for organogenesis in date palm cultivars, while Hassan et al.22 obtained the highest percentage of shoot bud formation on MS medium supplemented with 1.0 mg L⁻1 TDZ in combination with an auxin. The inclusion of benzyladenine (BA) along with TDZ has further been shown to enhance bud proliferation and organ number, as evidenced by Al-Mayahi23, who achieved a maximum average of 18.2 buds per culture on medium supplemented with 1.0 mg L⁻1 BA and 0.5 mg L⁻1 TDZ. In a study conducted by Elaziem et al.24 on direct organogenesis in date palm (Phoenix dactylifera L.) using immature inflorescence explants, the highest organogenic response was achieved on MS medium supplemented with 1.0 mg L⁻1 zeatin in combination with 1.0 mg L⁻1 thidiazuron (TDZ), highlighting the synergistic effect of these cytokinins in promoting shoot induction. The effectiveness of TDZ in inducing both direct and indirect organogenesis has also been reported in several woody perennial tree species. Successful TDZ-mediated organogenic responses have been documented in Korean pine25, Neolamarckia cadamba (2020), Gymnocladus assamicus (2020), Tree peony26 and Capparis decidua27, highlighting the broad applicability of TDZ in promoting organogenic regeneration in woody perennials.

The physiological basis of TDZ-induced morphogenesis is largely attributed to its strong influence on endogenous hormonal balance. TDZ stimulates rapid cell division and bud differentiation in apical meristematic regions28 and may promote organogenesis through modulation of calcium signaling, with subsequent ethylene production acting as a downstream mediator29. Several studies have shown that TDZ elevates endogenous auxin, ethylene, and abscisic acid levels30,31, thereby creating a favourable biochemical environment for shoot bud formation. These reports collectively suggest that TDZ acts not only as a synthetic cytokinin-like compound but also as a potent modulator of internal growth regulators. The results of this study, together with earlier evidence, indicate that successful direct shoot organogenesis in coconut depends on an appropriate TDZ concentration and its interaction with other growth regulators, which in turn is determined by the explant type and developmental stage.

Apart from the influence of plant growth regulators, the selection of an appropriate basal medium is critical for minimizing tissue browning and promoting vegetative bud induction in coconut tissue culture. The in vitro response of explants is strongly governed by the ionic composition, total salt concentration, and nitrogen balance of the medium, which together influence osmotic status, nutrient uptake, and cellular metabolism. Among the available formulations, Y3 medium has been widely adopted for coconut due to its relatively high salt strength and suitability for supporting somatic embryogenesis14,19,32.

In contrast, WPM, characterized by lower total salt concentration and a balanced nutrient profile tailored for woody perennials, appears to create a more favourable physiological environment for culture establishment for direct organogenesis. In the present investigation, WPM supplemented with TDZ proved more effective for culture initiation, as evidenced by healthier explant growth and enhanced organogenic responses. Similar observations have been reported in other perennial species; for instance, Al-Najm et al.12 identified WPM as superior for adventitious bud induction in date palm, while Romyanon et al.33 demonstrated its effectiveness in promoting compact shoot formation in oil palm, where Y3 medium was less responsive.

Shoot multiplication

The white vegetative bud clusters were initially transferred to a multiple shoot induction medium and maintained under diffused light conditions at an intensity of approximately 100 lx. Under these low-light conditions, the bud clusters gradually developed a green coloration within one month, indicating the initiation of chlorophyll synthesis. Following chlorophyll development, the cultures were transferred to a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod with a higher light intensity of 2500 lx to promote further shoot proliferation and elongation. The number of vegetative shoots formed and the average shoot length were recorded after six months of culture. The treatments exhibited significant differences with respect to both the number of shoots produced and shoot length (Fig. 4). The maximum shoot proliferation (8.3 shoots per explant) was recorded on MSI-I, which is WPM supplemented with 1 mg L⁻1 each of NAA and BAP + 5 mg L⁻1 TDZ, which was statistically similar to ½ MS medium supplemented with 1 mg L⁻1 NAA and BAP + 5 mg L⁻1 2-iP (Table 3). Similarly, the highest average shoot length between the two media was not significantly different (6.83 cm vs 6.38 cm respectively). The superior response observed in MSI-I cannot be attributed to TDZ alone, as the treatments differ in both basal medium composition and plant growth regulator combinations. However, it is noteworthy that MSI-I, which included TDZ along with NAA and BAP, produced significantly higher shoot proliferation and elongation. TDZ is widely reported to exhibit strong cytokinin-like activity and has been shown to enhance shoot induction and multiplication in several woody and recalcitrant species, including palms. A direct comparison between MSI-III and MSI-IV, which differ only in the inclusion of 2-iP (5 mg L⁻1), indicates that the addition of 2-iP was associated with a significant increase in both shoot number and shoot length. This suggests a positive role of 2-iP in enhancing shoot proliferation and elongation under the present conditions. Overall, shoot length ranged from 1.0 to 12.0 cm. The shoot clusters separated naturally into individual shoots upon gentle manipulation with forceps, enabling easy isolation without causing mechanical damage.

Taha et al.21 reported that, in date palm, the incorporation of BA along with TDZ and other growth regulators in the culture medium enhanced shoot proliferation, as the initiation of new shoots was markedly accelerated during the multiplication stage. The observed increase in shoot proliferation may be attributed to the physiological role of BAP, which is recognized as one of the most effective and widely used cytokinins in plant micropropagation owing to its high efficiency and low cost34. BAP plays a key role in stimulating cell division, promoting differentiation of adventitious buds, and accelerating overall morphogenic responses in vitro. In the current study, the media compositions were evaluated as combined treatments, the independent effect of BAP could not be assessed due to confounding among growth regulators. Future studies using factorial designs are required to elucidate the specific role of individual hormones.

Shoot elongation

Individual, healthy shoots were transferred to shoot elongation media containing Y3 as the basal formulation and supplemented with three different combinations of growth regulators. Analysis of variance revealed significant differences among treatments in average shoot length and the number of leaves formed per shoot (Table 4). The greatest shoot elongation (8.03 cm) was achieved on Y3 medium fortified with 2 mg L⁻1 NAA and 5 mg L⁻1 BAP, followed by Y3 medium supplemented with 1 mg L⁻1 NAA and 2 mg L⁻1 2-iP, which also supported appreciable elongation (Fig. 5). A similar trend was observed for leaf initiation, with the highest number of leaves per shoot recorded in the Y3 + NAA + BAP treatment, demonstrating the synergistic interaction between auxin and cytokinin in promoting both shoot elongation and leaf formation.

The superior shoot growth and leaf proliferation observed on Y3 medium containing 2 mg L⁻1 NAA and 5 mg L⁻1 BAP can be attributed to the complementary physiological roles of these regulators. NAA, a synthetic auxin, enhances cell expansion, vascular differentiation, and overall tissue organization, while BAP, as a potent cytokinin, stimulates active cell division and shoot development. The balanced auxin–cytokinin interaction in these medium likely optimized endogenous hormonal levels, resulting in improved shoot vigour, enhanced morphological development, and better shoot establishment during the elongation phase.

Rooting and acclimatization

Shoots bearing more than three well-developed leaves were selected and transferred to rooting media consisting of either full-strength or half-strength Y3 or MS basal formulations supplemented with different auxins. Root induction was not achieved consistently following shoot development, despite testing a range of treatments involving auxins, sucrose concentrations, activated charcoal, and modifications in basal medium strength. The responses were generally low and variable, and no reproducible pattern could be established; therefore, detailed data are not presented. However, certain trends were observed.

Root initiation was primarily observed on half-strength media supplemented with NAA and IBA, highlighting the suitability of reduced nutrient concentrations for efficient rooting (Fig. 6). Among the basal formulations tested, the half-strength Murashige and Skoog (½ MS) medium proved to be the most effective in promoting root induction and subsequent root elongation. These results suggest that a lower salt concentration provides a more favourable in vitro environment for adventitious root initiation and growth.

Root initiation in ½ MS medium with NAA 5 mgL-1+ IBA 1 mgL-1 + Sucrose 40 gL-1.

The addition of activated charcoal enhanced rooting performance. This improvement may be attributed to its ability to adsorb inhibitory phenolic exudates released and probably by modifying the medium environment in a manner that partially mimics natural soil conditions. Activated charcoal also helps maintain auxin stability by reducing light penetration into the rooting zone, thereby preventing photodegradation and preserving the physiological activity of the applied auxins. Furthermore, increased sucrose concentration positively influenced rooting, as root induction and development are highly energy-dependent processes. The greater availability of carbon sources likely provided adequate metabolic energy for sustained cell division and elongation, resulting in improved root formation.

In date palm, it has been reported that half-strength MS medium supplemented with 0.5 mg L⁻1 each of NAA and IBA, along with 1.0 mg L⁻1 IAA, was more effective and suitable for inducing rooting in plantlets regenerated from immature inflorescences21.

Healthy, vigorously rooted plantlets were utilized for hardening. Successful hardening and acclimatization of in vitro derived plantlets were achieved through a stepwise transition from in vitro to ex vitro conditions. The initial transfer from semi-solid culture media to a sterilized cocopeat: soil: perlite substrate and incubation under controlled temperature and light conditions facilitated early establishment by minimizing transplant shock. Subsequent transfer to pots covered with transparent polythene enabled the maintenance of high humidity while allowing gradual exposure to ambient conditions. Final establishment in polybags under greenhouse conditions further supported adaptation to external environments. As the emergence of a new leaf typically requires approximately one month, each hardening stage was maintained for a minimum period of two months to ensure adequate adaptation. Although the acclimatization process was time-intensive, it proved effective in ensuring high survival and establishment of plantlets.

Clonal fidelity testing

PCR-amplified products were resolved by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel and visualized to generate distinct SSR banding profiles. The resulting banding patterns were examined for each marker across all samples. Comparative analysis of SSR fingerprints revealed that the tissue culture–derived plantlets displayed monomorphic and identical allele patterns corresponding to their respective mother palm. This complete concordance between the progeny and the source palm confirms the genetic uniformity and clonal fidelity of the regenerated plants. Fernando et al.35 used microsatellite markers to assess genetic uniformity among their regenerated coconut plants, thereby validating the genetic stability of tissue culture–derived coconut plantlets. These results demonstrate that SSR marker–based molecular analysis is a robust and reliable approach for assessing the genetic integrity of micropropagated coconut plantlets, thereby ensuring their true-to-type nature, quality, and suitability for field planting and commercial distribution.

Conclusion

To promote direct organogenesis from immature inflorescences, WPM supplemented with TDZ emerged as the most effective treatment, exhibiting a high level of responsiveness. Combinations of TDZ with other plant growth regulators produced comparable responses, with no substantial advantage over TDZ alone, indicating its dominant role. This pronounced effect of TDZ may be attributed to its dual mode of action, exhibiting both cytokinin- and auxin-like properties], which enhance cell division, stimulate meristematic activity, and promote vegetative bud differentiation, thereby enabling direct organogenesis without additional exogenous regulators. The present study demonstrates successful induction of direct organogenesis from immature inflorescence explants of coconut, leading to efficient regeneration of true-to-type plantlets. This pathway minimizes somaclonal variation and ensures genetic stability. The consistent morphogenic response highlights the developmental competence of immature inflorescences as a reliable explant source for coconut micropropagation. Although further refinement of root induction is required to enhance regeneration frequency and reproducibility, the protocol provides a robust framework for rapid clonal multiplication of elite, high-yielding, and disease-tolerant coconut genotypes, with strong potential for large-scale production of quality planting material.
