Mechanisms, optimization strategies, and salvage options for CAR-T cell therapy
Authors: Bijing Wu, Jiahui Wang, Qihua Zou, Jun Cai, Yuchen Zhang, Qian Zhong, Bing Bai, Fang Liu, Ye Cao, Peng Li, Dan-Ling Dai, Qingqing Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has transformed the treatment landscape for relapsed or refractory hematologic malignancies, producing high remission rates in otherwise treatment-resistant patients. However, primary resistance and disease relapse remain common, particularly in solid tumors, limiting long-term benefit and broader clinical applicability. As the population of patients failing therapy grows, there is an urgent need for an integrated understanding of resistance mechanisms and a structured approach to salvage therapy. This review proposes a conceptual “Why-How-What if” framework to navigate the complexities of treatment failure. We first address “Why” therapy fails, identifying multifactorial drivers including tumor-intrinsic factors like antigen loss and immune evasion, T cell-intrinsic dysfunction such as exhaustion and limited persistence, and extrinsic constraints imposed by an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. We then explore “How” to enhance efficacy through mechanism-based strategies. These include rational combination approaches with immune checkpoint inhibitors or small molecule inhibitors, and next-generation engineering such as dual-target, armored, and in vivo generated CAR-T cells aimed at overcoming metabolic and physical barriers. Finally, we address the “What if” of treatment failure by summarizing individualized salvage options, for which current clinical evidence is derived predominantly from hematologic malignancies. These strategies range from target-switching and bispecific antibodies to emerging cellular platforms like CAR-natural killer cells and consolidation via allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. By integrating mechanisms of failure with evolving optimization and salvage strategies, this framework provides a practical roadmap for clinical and translational progress. Future success will depend on biomarker-guided combinations and the continued diversification of adoptive cell therapy platforms.Keywords: CAR-T cell therapy, Resistance mechanisms, Tumor microenvironment, Combination therapy, Salvage therapy

Background

Over the past decade, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has profoundly transformed the management of relapsed or refractory hematologic malignancies and has demonstrated promising potential in solid tumors and autoimmune diseases. Since the first product approvals in 2017 [1], accumulating data from clinical trials and real-world cohorts have established CAR-T therapy as a treatment capable of inducing deep and durable remissions in patients who are refractory to conventional therapies. In particular, CD19-directed CAR-T therapy has achieved an overall response rate (ORR) exceeding 80% in relapsed or refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) [2], and has produced long-term remission in approximately one-third of patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [3], representing an unprecedented therapeutic advance in populations with historically poor outcomes. The recent approval of CLDN18.2-directed satricabtagene autoleucel (satri-cel) for advanced gastric or gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma marks the first regulatory approval of CAR-T therapy for a solid tumor [16]. Early clinical studies have also suggested that CD19-directed CAR-T cells may induce drug-free remission in selected patients with refractory B-cell-mediated autoimmune diseases [17].

Despite these remarkable achievements, substantial barriers limit broader and more consistent clinical application. Due to target-related biological constraints and the complexity of the tumor microenvironment (TME), a considerable proportion of patients, particularly those with solid tumors, experience primary resistance or relapse [18]. Meanwhile, treatment-associated toxicities, including cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), necessitate standardized recognition and management strategies [19]. The mechanisms underlying limited efficacy are multifactorial and include antigen heterogeneity or loss, immunosuppressive features of the tumor microenvironment, T-cell dysfunction characterized by insufficient persistence or exhaustion, variability in manufacturing processes and product attributes, and host-related factors. To overcome these challenges, extensive preclinical studies have elucidated key mechanisms of CAR-T failure, and parallel clinical investigations are evaluating rational optimization strategies. Combination approaches incorporating immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) or epigenetic modulators may reshape the tumor microenvironment and mitigate T-cell exhaustion [20]. At the level of construct design, dual-target CARs and armored CAR-T cells are being developed to prevent antigen escape and enhance persistence [21]. Alternative cellular platforms, including CAR-natural killer (CAR-NK) cells, CAR-macrophages (CAR-M), and allogeneic ‘off-the-shelf’ products, are being developed to mitigate CRS risk while bypassing the prolonged manufacturing timelines and high costs inherent to autologous therapies [22].

Furthermore, therapeutic options for patients experiencing CAR-T failure remain limited and are typically characterized by poor prognosis, leaving the optimal salvage strategy a subject of ongoing debate. Emerging salvage approaches are currently undergoing clinical evaluation, with a growing emphasis on individualized decision-making informed by relapse kinetics and specific resistance mechanisms to improve overall survival (OS).

In this review, we propose a structured “Why (mechanisms)–How (strategies)–What if (salvage)” framework. We first delineate the principal biological and clinical mechanisms that contribute to limited efficacy and treatment resistance. We then focus on mechanism-driven optimization strategies designed to enhance therapeutic potency, durability, and safety. Finally, we summarize current and emerging salvage approaches for patients who experience treatment failure. Through this integrated perspective, we aim to provide a practical roadmap to inform clinical decision-making and to guide future translational and therapeutic development in CAR-T therapy.

Mechanisms of CAR-T cell therapy failure

Antigen-related mechanisms

Tumor cells may evade CAR-T–mediated recognition through antigen loss or downregulation [23], antigen heterogeneity, particularly in solid tumors, where many tumor-associated antigens lack strict tumor specificity, and lineage switching [24, 25] (Fig. 1A). In hematologic malignancies, CD19-negative relapse accounts for approximately 30%–50% of post–CD19 CAR-T relapses [26], underscoring antigen loss as a predominant mechanism of resistance. In solid tumors, this challenge is further intensified by highly variable and often patchy antigen expression, such as mesothelin, HER2, and EGFR, which enables the selective expansion of antigen-low or antigen-negative tumor clones. Importantly, antigen density is a critical determinant of CAR-T cytotoxic activity. Low target density, as observed with CD20 in B-cell malignancies and EGFRvIII in glioblastoma, is associated with diminished therapeutic efficacy [24, 27]. In addition, tumor cells may acquire CAR molecules through trogocytosis, resulting in antigen masking and subsequent CAR-T dysfunction [28]. These findings highlight that durable CAR-T efficacy depends on rational antigen selection and the development of multi-target and evolution-resilient CAR constructs capable of adapting to dynamic tumor evolution.

Mechanisms of CAR-T cell therapy failure. (A) Antigen escape: loss or downregulation of target antigens, together with clonal diversity, prevents effective recognition and killing. (B) Immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment: Tregs, MDSCs, TAMs, fibroblasts, and soluble factors (IL-4, IL-10, TGF-β, PGE2) restrict CAR-T trafficking, survival, and cytotoxicity. (C) T-cell exhaustion: dysfunctional CAR-T cells upregulate inhibitory receptors (PD-1, CTLA-4, TIM-3) and exhibit reduced effector function. (D) Safety and host factors: cytokine release syndrome and host immune rejection, driven by inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α), constrain dose intensity, persistence, and therapeutic durability

TME-related mechanisms

The TME represents a major constraint on CAR -T efficacy, particularly in solid tumors, where physical exclusion and metabolic stress synergistically impair trafficking, persistence, and effector function. Dense stromal components, including collagen and fibronectin, restrict CAR-T infiltration, while hypoxia and acidosis suppress oxidative phosphorylation and promote T-cell exhaustion [29]. In hematologic malignancies, dynamic alterations within the bone marrow and lymph node microenvironments also influence therapeutic outcomes [30]. Complete responders are characterized by reduced myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and restoration of CXCL9/CXCL10 chemokine gradients, whereas partial responders exhibit aberrant recruitment of regulatory T cells (Tregs) [31]. These observations underscore that the spatial heterogeneity and temporal evolution of the TME critically shape CAR-T activity (Fig. 1B).

Immunosuppressive cellular and soluble components within the TME further attenuate CAR-T function. MDSCs impair cytotoxicity through arginase release and upregulation of exhaustion markers such as PD-1 and TIM-3, whereas Tregs consume IL-2 and secrete TGF-β, thereby inducing a suppressive phenotype in CAR-T cells [32]. Arginine deprivation impairs CAR-T proliferation, whereas arginine-resynthesis enzymes such as argininosuccinate synthase (ASS) or ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) expression enhance CAR-T fitness under arginine-limited conditions [33]; other nutrient-deprivation pressures, including limited tryptophan or cystine availability, also constrain CAR-T expansion within the TME [34]. In multiple myeloma (MM), M2-polarized macrophages further inhibit CAR-T cytotoxicity through aberrant metabolic reprogramming, including S100A8/A9 upregulation [35]. Soluble mediators, such as IL-10 and PGE2, impair memory CAR-T cell formation [36], while defects in IFNγ signaling pathways, including JAK1/2 alterations, confer tumor-intrinsic resistance [37, 38]. Preclinical evidence indicates that blockade of TIGIT or PD-L1 can partially reverse TME-induced suppression [39]. In solid tumors, however, additional matrix-remodeling strategies may be required, such as CAR-T targeting cancer-associated fibroblasts [40]. In MM, bone marrow cancer-associated fibroblasts promote functional suppression and therapeutic resistance; dual-compartment strategies co-targeting malignant plasma cells and fibroblasts have restored B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)-directed CAR-T activity in preclinical models [41]. These findings highlight that effective reprogramming of the immunosuppressive TME is essential to fully unlock the therapeutic potential of CAR-T therapy.

T-cell-intrinsic mechanisms

Persistent antigen stimulation drives CAR-T cells toward a state of functional exhaustion, characterized by the co-expression of inhibitory receptors including PD-1, CTLA-4, TIM-3, LAG-3, and TIGIT, together with reduced production of effector cytokines such as IFN-γ and IL-2 (Fig. 1C). This state is accompanied by exhaustion-associated transcriptional reprogramming [42]. This transcriptional program compromises cytotoxic capacity and contributes to early disease relapse. Clinically, T-cell exhaustion represents a major barrier across malignancies. In chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), non-responders to tisagenlecleucel demonstrated enhanced exhaustion signatures, including co-expression of PD-1, LAG-3, and TIM-3 [43]. In pancreatic cancer, sustained antigen exposure has been shown to drive CAR-T cells from a CD8+ phenotype toward NK-like differentiation, accompanied by upregulation of transcription factors such as ID3 and SOX4, ultimately resulting in diminished effector function [44]. Combination strategies incorporating PD-1 blockade, such as pembrolizumab, can partially reverse exhaustion and enhance tumor control [45].

Beyond exhaustion, CAR construct design and product composition further influence cellular fate. CD28 and 4-1BB costimulatory domains confer distinct metabolic and functional programs. CD28-based constructs promote rapid effector activity but are associated with earlier exhaustion, whereas 4-1BB-based constructs support greater persistence, albeit with delayed cytotoxic kinetics [46]. Cellular senescence has also emerged as a resistance mechanism and appears more frequently in 4-1BB-based products [47]. Moreover, the intrinsic quality of the infused T cells is critical. Products derived from elderly or heavily pretreated patients often demonstrate limited expansion and reduced durability [48, 49]. In contrast, CAR-T products enriched for naïve and memory subsets, as well as multifunctional T cells, exhibit superior proliferative capacity and long-term persistence [50, 51]. Overall, mitigating T-cell exhaustion while optimizing CAR design and input T-cell quality is fundamental to achieving durable and sustained CAR-T efficacy.

Host and disease-related factors

Host immunity represents a substantial barrier to the success of allogeneic CAR-T therapy. Anti-mouse scFv antibodies may trigger complement-dependent cytotoxicity, resulting in rapid clearance of infused CAR-T cells [52]. In addition, allogeneic CAR-T products are susceptible to recognition and elimination by host T cells and NK cells due to HLA mismatch [52]. Gene-editing strategies aimed at disrupting HLA-A/B and β2-microglobulin expression can reduce immunogenicity; however, these approaches remain insufficient to fully prevent NK cell–mediated rejection [53, 54]. The development of fully humanized CAR constructs and strategies that promote immune tolerance may provide more durable solutions to mitigate host immune rejection.

Inflammatory toxicities further constrain therapeutic intensity and durability. CRS and ICANS remain major safety concerns in CAR-T therapy [55] (Fig. 1D). Approximately 70% of adult patients with B-cell lymphoma (BCL) develop CRS following CD19 CAR-T infusion [56], and severe cases may progress to life-threatening hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis [57]. Construct design also influences toxicity profiles: CD28-based CARs are more frequently associated with early-onset CRS, whereas 4-1BB-based CARs are linked to delayed neurotoxicity [58], underscoring the need to carefully balance potency and safety. The Penn grading scale has been established to standardize the assessment and management of CRS [59], and early administration of tocilizumab has been shown to reduce mortality without compromising CAR-T efficacy [60]. Furthermore, CRISPR-mediated knockout of GM-CSF or pharmacologic neutralization with lenzilumab has demonstrated potential in attenuating CRS and neurotoxicity [61, 62]. Taken together, host immune rejection and treatment-related inflammatory toxicities remain critical challenges, highlighting the importance of immune-compatible CAR design and refined toxicity management strategies to optimize the therapeutic index of CAR-T therapy.

Strategies to enhance the efficacy of CAR-T therapy

Combination therapy

Targeting the tumor immune microenvironment

The TME restricts CAR-T infiltration and effector function through checkpoint signaling, suppressive immune subsets, and physical barriers, thereby increasing the risk of primary resistance and relapse [63]. ICIs targeting PD-1/PD-L1, TIGIT, and CTLA-4 can alleviate T-cell dysfunction and partially reprogram the TME (Fig. 2A). Preclinical studies consistently demonstrate that ICIs restore CAR-T effector programs and enhance antitumor activity [64–66]. In hematologic malignancies, early clinical investigations in r/r B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (B-NHL) evaluated CAR-T combined with PD-1 blockade, reporting ORRs of 81.8% with nivolumab and 66% with pembrolizumab, with corresponding complete response (CR) rates of 45.5% and 48.9%. The pembrolizumab cohort further demonstrated 1-year OS and progression-free survival (PFS) rates of 53.7% and 25.8%, respectively [67, 68]. A phase 2 prospective study of CD19 CAR-T combined with sintilimab (NCT07077512) is ongoing. Additionally, engineered strategies such as anti-PD-1 autocrine CAR-T cells aim to enhance functional persistence and scalability [69–71]. Beyond systemic checkpoint blockade, intrinsic redirection of CAR-T cells to the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, either through direct targeting of PD-L1-expressing tumors [72] or via co-expression of a PD-L1–specific chimeric switch receptor that converts inhibitory signaling into CD70–CD27–mediated costimulation, has demonstrated improved persistence and antitumor efficacy in preclinical models [73].

Combination strategies to enhance CAR-T efficacy. (A) TME-targeted approaches: immune checkpoint blockade (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1), radiotherapy, and chemotherapy recondition the tumor microenvironment, improving CAR-T trafficking, survival, and resistance to exhaustion. (B) Tumor-targeting antibodies: monoclonal antibodies (e.g., anti-CD20 rituximab) synergize with CAR-T to debulk disease and mitigate exhaustion, while CD20×CD3 bispecific antibodies (e.g., glofitamab) serve as bridging or salvage therapies after CAR-T and may promote peripheral CAR-T re-expansion. (C) Small molecule inhibitors: BTK inhibitors (e.g., ibrutinib, zanubrutinib) enhance CAR-T expansion, persistence, and antitumor activity, whereas TKIs such as dasatinib may attenuate CRS-related cytokine release. (D) Novel therapies: mRNA vaccines (e.g., CLDN6 CARVac) augment in vivo CAR-T expansion and promote antigen spreading, and CAR-macrophages (CAR-M) cooperate with CAR-T, with M1 polarization enhancing phagocytosis and tumor control

In solid tumors, however, outcomes of CAR-T combined with checkpoint blockade remain heterogeneous. In a study of 18 patients with malignant pleural disease, mesothelin-targeted CAR-T plus pembrolizumab achieved a median OS of 23.9 months and a 1-year OS rate of 83% [74]. In contrast, a phase 1 trial of EGFRvIII-targeted CAR-T combined with pembrolizumab in newly diagnosed glioblastoma demonstrated acceptable safety but no significant efficacy improvement [75]. These findings suggest that, in solid tumors, optimization of antigen selection, dosing, sequencing, and microenvironmental modulation is required to overcome TME-specific resistance. Consistent with this concept, tumor-targeted delivery of IFN-α and orthogonal IL-2 by TIE2-expressing macrophages restored B7-H3-directed CAR-T cell activity and engaged host T-cell responses in an immunocompetent glioblastoma model, highlighting cytokine-mediated microenvironmental modulation as a strategy to overcome local immunosuppression [76].

Conventional modalities may further recondition the TME. Radiotherapy enhances tumor vascular normalization and facilitates immune cell trafficking [77]. Clinically, radiotherapy has been used mainly as a bridging strategy before CAR-T infusion in aggressive B-cell lymphomas, where it may reduce tumor burden, although optimal dose, field, and timing remain undefined [78, 79]. Immunomodulatory agents such as lenalidomide promote CAR-T expansion and cytokine production and may attenuate exhaustion programs [80–82]. Clinically, a patient with r/r multiple myeloma (RRMM) treated with lenalidomide plus BCMA-targeted CAR-T achieved a very good partial response lasting over eight months [83]. In a separate cohort of 10 newly diagnosed high-risk MM patients, lenalidomide maintenance following CAR-T therapy and autologous stem-cell transplantation (ASCT) was well tolerated and yielded a 100% ORR, with seven patients sustaining minimal residual disease (MRD) negativity for more than two years [84]. Furthermore, pharmacologic strategies that increase antigen density, such as all-trans retinoic acid-mediated upregulation of BCMA or CD38, provide additional rationale for combination approaches in MM [85, 86].

Tumor-targeting antibodies and bispecific antibodies

Tumor-targeting monoclonal antibodies can reduce tumor burden and mitigate T-cell exhaustion, thereby providing a strong rationale for combination strategies with CAR-T therapy in B-cell malignancies (Fig. 2B). In the ZUMA-14 study, CD19 CAR-T combined with rituximab in patients with r/r large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL) achieved an ORR of 88% and a CR rate of 65% [87]. In r/r B-ALL, the addition of rituximab to CD19/CD22 CAR-T was associated with improved long-term outcomes, and mechanistic analyses indicated attenuation of exhaustion-related transcriptional programs in CAR-T cells [88]. These findings suggest that antibody-mediated tumor debulking and immunomodulation may enhance CAR-T persistence and functionality.

Bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) have been evaluated primarily in B-cell malignancies and multiple myeloma, both as bridging therapy prior to CAR-T infusion and as combinatorial partners. A retrospective analysis demonstrated that BsAbs provided a safe and effective bridging strategy for patients with RRMM undergoing BCMA-targeted CAR-T therapy [89]. In the post–CAR-T setting, the CD20×CD3 BsAb glofitamab improved survival outcomes in patients with relapsed DLBCL and mantle cell lymphoma (MCL). Specifically, increased circulating CAR-T cell levels were observed in peripheral blood following BsAb administration, suggesting that part of its clinical activity may derive from promoting CAR-T re-expansion or reactivation, although the precise mechanisms underlying this synergy remain to be fully elucidated [90, 91].

Small molecule inhibitors

Small molecule inhibitors may enhance CAR-T efficacy by suppressing tumor survival pathways, reshaping the TME, and modulating treatment-related toxicity. Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors (BTKis) inhibit B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling, thereby reducing aberrant activation and survival of malignant B cells. In addition, they can attenuate immunosuppressive features within the TME, ultimately promoting CAR-T survival, expansion, and antitumor activity [92] (Fig. 2C). Preclinical studies demonstrated that the addition of ibrutinib reduced proinflammatory cytokine production and mitigated CRS risk without compromising CAR-T cytotoxicity, providing a strong rationale for combination therapy [93]. Clinical data further support this strategy. In a phase 2 cohort of patients with CLL, ibrutinib combined with CD19 CAR-T therapy achieved a 3-month CR rate of 44%, and 2-year OS and PFS rates of 84% and 70%, respectively [94]. In the phase 2 TARMAC study involving 20 patients with r/r MCL, short-term ibrutinib was initiated before leukapheresis and continued for at least six months after CD19-targeted CAR-T infusion. The 4-month CR rate reached 80%, with 1-year OS and PFS rates of 100% and 75%, respectively [95]. Similarly, zanubrutinib combined with CD19-targeted CAR-T in a small r/r DLBCL cohort produced a 100% ORR at 3 months, with durable remissions and sustained CAR-T expansion during follow-up [96].

Beyond BTKis, dasatinib has been investigated as a pharmacologic modulator to control cytokine release during CAR-T therapy, given its ability to suppress inflammatory mediators implicated in CRS [97, 98]. In a single-arm phase 2 trial, 28 adults with newly diagnosed philadelphia chromosome-positive ALL (Ph + ALL) received dasatinib combined with VP induction, followed by sequential CD19- and CD22-targeted CAR-T therapy and maintenance dasatinib. This regimen achieved a complete molecular remission rate of 76%, with favorable safety and a 2-year OS rate of 92%, suggesting a potential chemotherapy- and hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation (HSCT)-sparing strategy [99]. Additional targets are also under exploration. Inhibition of XPO1 with selinexor has been shown to alleviate macrophage-driven immunosuppression and enhance CAR-T function [100]. These findings highlight small-molecule agents as pragmatic and biologically synergistic partners that can improve both the efficacy and tolerability of CAR-T therapy through tumor-directed effects and immune modulation.

RNA vaccines and CAR-M

Emerging combinatorial strategies aim to overcome antigen heterogeneity and limited infiltration in solid tumors (Fig. 2D). mRNA vaccines encoding tumor antigens, such as CLDN6-targeted CARVac, enhance CAR-T efficacy through complementary mechanisms. First, by expressing antigens shared with CAR-T targets, these vaccines promote in vivo CAR-T expansion and functional plasticity, thereby amplifying antitumor responses. Second, they induce antigen spreading, activating endogenous T cells to recognize diverse tumor-associated antigens and counteracting antigen loss–mediated resistance in solid tumors [101]. A phase 1 trial in patients with r/r advanced solid tumors demonstrated the safety of CLDN6-targeted CAR-T combined with CARVac. Among 21 evaluable patients, the ORR and disease control rate (DCR) were 33% and 67%, respectively. Notably, in patients with germ cell tumors, the ORR reached 57%, with 4 of 7 patients achieving objective responses [102].

CAR-M represents another innovative platform designed to address TME-associated barriers. By integrating the intrinsic tumor-infiltrating and phagocytic properties of macrophages with CAR specificity, CAR-M expands the functional scope of adoptive cell therapy [103]. In vitro studies have demonstrated synergistic cytotoxicity when CAR-M and CAR-T are combined, supporting cooperative interactions between innate and adaptive immune compartments [104]. Furthermore, promoting M1 polarization enhances CAR-M-mediated antitumor activity, offering a strategy to improve therapeutic efficacy in solid tumors [105]. By leveraging complementary immune mechanisms, this approach holds particular promise for overcoming the limited trafficking and microenvironmental resistance that constrain CAR-T therapy in solid malignancies.

Optimization of CAR-T design

Dual and multi-targeted CAR-T

Dual- and multi-targeted CAR-T strategies expand tumor antigen recognition to mitigate antigen escape (Fig. 3A). These platforms can be implemented through several approaches, including sequential administration of two single-target products, co-transduction of T cells with two distinct CAR constructs, bicistronic expression within a single vector, or the design of tandem or loop CAR architectures [106]. In hematologic malignancies, commonly explored dual targets include CD19/CD22, CD19/CD20, CD19/BCMA, and BCMA/CD38 in B-cell malignancies and MM (Table 2), while combinations such as CD5/CD7 [120] and CD3/CD7 [121] remain under preclinical evaluation. In solid tumors, dual-target constructs including EGFR/IL13Rα2, HER2/IL13Rα2, IL13Rα2/TGF-β, and GD2/B7-H3 have been investigated predominantly in preclinical or early-phase studies.

Optimization of CAR-T design to enhance performance. (A) Dual- and multi-target CAR-T: simultaneous recognition of two or more antigens (e.g., CD19/CD22, CD19/BCMA, EGFR/IL13Rα2) mitigates antigen loss and improves durability. (B) Armored CAR-T: CAR-T cells engineered to secrete or express cytokines or cytokine receptors (e.g., IL-12, IL-15, IL-18) resist TME-mediated suppression, recruit immune partners, and sustain effector function. (C) CAR structure engineering: optimization of scFv affinity and humanization, hinge length and composition, and transmembrane domains regulates receptor density and activation thresholds, while tuning intracellular signaling modules (e.g., CD28 versus 4-1BB costimulation and ITAM number or position) balances rapid cytotoxicity with long-term persistence. (D) T-cell subset selection: defined cellular composition (balanced CD4:CD8 ratio) and enrichment of stem-like or memory phenotypes (e.g., TSCM, TCM) enhance self-renewal, reduce exhaustion, and improve antitumor activity and safety

Clinical data in hematologic malignancies support the efficacy of dual targeting. Sequential or simultaneous infusion of CD19 and CD22 CAR-T cells has demonstrated favorable safety and enhanced durability in pediatric patients with r/r B-ALL [107–110]. In adult B-ALL, CD19/CD22 bispecific CAR-T achieved an ORR of 100% and an MRD-negative CR rate of 88%, without dose-limiting toxicities [111]. In r/r B-NHL, CD19/CD20 dual-target CAR-T showed acceptable tolerability, with ORRs ranging from 79% to 100% and CR rates between 70% and 88% [112–114]. In RRMM, humanized anti-CD19 and anti-BCMA CAR-T combination therapy achieved ORRs exceeding 90% with manageable toxicity profiles [115]. In a study of 50 patients with RRMM treated with BCMA/CD19 bispecific CAR-T, the ORR reached 92%, with 1-year PFS and OS rates of 55% and 85%, respectively [116]. Similarly, BCMA/CD38 dual-target CAR-T demonstrated encouraging activity in MM, with ORRs approaching 90% and high stringent complete response (sCR) rates [117–119].

In solid tumors, dual-target CAR-T may address intratumoral heterogeneity and antigen loss. In recurrent glioblastoma, locoregional administration of EGFR/IL13Rα2 bispecific CAR-T induced rapid tumor regression on MRI within 24–48 h, with sustained responses observed in selected cases [122, 123]. Preclinical studies further support the efficacy of HER2/IL13Rα2 and IL13Rα2/TGF-β dual-target constructs in glioblastoma, while GD2/B7-H3 CAR-T reduced antigen escape and remodeled the immunosuppressive TME in neuroblastoma models [124–126].

Beyond dual targeting, trispecific CAR-T cells directed against CD19/CD20/CD22 have demonstrated robust antitumor activity in preclinical models of antigen-heterogeneous B-cell malignancies, underscoring their potential to further reduce relapse driven by antigen loss or downregulation [127].

Armored CAR-T

Armored CAR-T cells are engineered to co-express immunomodulatory cytokines (e.g., IL-12, IL-15, IL-18, IL-7) or cytokine receptors in addition to conventional second- or third-generation CAR constructs (Fig. 3B).

Preclinical studies have demonstrated that cytokine armoring can enhance CAR-T function and counteract local immunosuppression. IL-12 is a potent inducer of Th1 polarization and IFN-γ–amplifying feedback loops; however, its systemic toxicity necessitates strategies that confine activity locally, such as intratumoral administration, membrane anchoring, or autocrine single-chain IL-12 secretion [128–131]. IL-18 similarly augments IFN-γ signaling and immune activation and has improved tumor control in preclinical models, including BCMA/BAFF-R dual-target CAR-T in low-antigen MM and delta-like ligand 3 (DLL3)-targeted CAR-T in small-cell lung cancer, where it reduced exhaustion and supported durable responses [132–134]. CAR-T cells co-expressing IL-7 and the chemokine CCL19 (7 × 19 CAR-T) recruit peripheral T cells and dendritic cells, resulting in sustained antitumor activity in preclinical models [21, 135, 136]. Metabolic armoring represents another promising avenue; for example, IL-10 secretion has been shown to preserve mitochondrial function through MPC-dependent oxidative phosphorylation, thereby mitigating exhaustion and promoting durable immunity [137]. Functional armoring can also be accomplished by neutralizing suppressive pathways, as illustrated by a dual-compartment strategy combining CD4+ anti–TGF-β helper CAR-T with CD8+ conventional CAR-T to enhance persistence and antitumor activity in solid tumors [138].

Early clinical translation of cytokine-armored CAR-T cells has been reported in both solid and hematologic malignancies. In a phase 1 study, IL-15 armored glypican-3 (GPC3)-targeted CAR-T cells achieved a DCR of 66% and an ORR of 33% in patients with solid tumors, with manageable toxicities [139]. In a multicenter phase 1 trial involving adults with r/r LBCL, 7 × 19 CAR-T infusion achieved an ORR of 79.5% and a CR rate of 56.4% at 3 months. After a median follow-up of 32 months, median PFS was 13 months, with no dose-limiting toxicities reported [140]. Clinical activity has also been reported for IL-18-secreting CD19 CAR-T cells in patients with r/r B-cell lymphoma after prior CAR-T failure [141]. CAR-T structure engineering

CAR-T function is governed not only by the extracellular antigen-recognition domain but also by the hinge, transmembrane, and intracellular signaling components (Fig. 3C). The antigen-binding domain typically consists of a scFv formed by linked variable light (VL) and heavy (VH) chains. Its binding affinity critically influences both efficacy and safety: excessive affinity may increase on-target toxicities, whereas insufficient affinity risks suboptimal activation and antigen escape [142, 143]. Interestingly, emerging evidence suggests that lower-affinity scFvs may reduce trogocytosis and rapid antigen loss, thereby improving therapeutic durability [144]. In addition, humanization of murine-derived scFvs decreases immunogenicity and has demonstrated favorable safety and activity in ALL [145, 146].

The hinge region connects the scFv to the transmembrane domain via a flexible linker and commonly incorporates sequences derived from CD8, CD28, IgG1, or IgG4. The transmembrane domain, typically derived from CD4, CD8, or CD28, influences receptor stability, membrane localization, and signal propagation. Together, these structural elements determine receptor density, spatial configuration, and activation thresholds [147]. Variations in hinge length and composition can substantially affect in vivo antitumor activity [148, 149], although further refinement is required to optimize these parameters for clinical application.

The intracellular signaling module consists of CD3ζ coupled with one or more costimulatory domains, most commonly CD28 or 4-1BB. CD28-based CAR-T cells confer rapid activation and potent cytotoxicity but are associated with limited persistence, whereas 4-1BB-based constructs promote enhanced durability, often with slower initial tumor clearance. Rational modification [150, 151] or strategic combination of these signaling domains [152] may harness complementary strengths to optimize overall efficacy. Incorporation of additional signaling modules, such as a Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) intracellular domain, has been shown to enhance CAR-T expansion and antitumor activity, supporting TLR2 costimulation as a viable design strategy [153]. Furthermore, fine-tuning the number and positioning of immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM) within the CD3ζ domain, through selective deletion or inactivation, can modulate T-cell differentiation and functional output [154, 155]. Overall, precise coordination of antigen-binding affinity, hinge–transmembrane architecture, and intracellular signaling configuration is fundamental to next-generation CAR engineering and the optimization of therapeutic performance.

Selection of T cell subsets

The composition and differentiation state of the input T-cell product are critical determinants of CAR-T expansion, persistence, and antitumor potency (Fig. 3D). These properties are shaped by complex interactions among subset proportions, phenotypic characteristics, differentiation trajectories, and intracellular signaling pathways [156]. CD4+ and CD8+ T cells encompass diverse functional and differentiation states, ranging from naïve and stem cell–like memory populations to terminally differentiated effector cells. Imbalances in subset composition or excessive differentiation can adversely affect therapeutic durability and functional performance.

Functional synergy between CD4+ and CD8+ compartments is particularly important. Although many clinical CAR-T products are enriched for CD8+ cells, a more balanced CD4:CD8 ratio, approximately 1:1, has been associated with enhanced expansion, persistence, and antitumor activity [157, 158]. Enrichment for naïve or memory phenotypes further improves therapeutic performance, as these subsets demonstrate greater self-renewal capacity, metabolic fitness, and resistance to exhaustion. This principle has been validated in patient-derived samples and in clinical studies of hematologic malignancies [112, 159]. Moreover, TSCM-derived CAR-T cells targeting BCMA or CD19 have shown superior efficacy and favorable safety profiles in MM models and in patients with B-ALL [160, 161].

In conclusion, rational selection and enrichment of stem-like or memory T-cell subsets represent a personalized and biologically grounded strategy to optimize CAR-T function and improve the consistency and durability of clinical outcomes.

Optimization of CAR-T manufacturing

Optimization of T cell sources

T-cell sources for CAR-T therapy include autologous peripheral blood, allogeneic donors, umbilical cord blood, and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) (Fig. 4A). Although most approved and investigational products remain autologous, patient-derived manufacturing is limited by variability in starting-cell quality, prolonged production timelines, non-negligible manufacturing failure rates, and high cost. These challenges have accelerated the development of off-the-shelf, universally available CAR-T platforms.

Optimization of CAR-T cell manufacturing. (A) T-cell sources: autologous peripheral blood remains the standard but is limited by variable quality and prolonged lead times; allogeneic platforms (donor-derived, umbilical cord blood, or iPSC-derived) aim to provide off-the-shelf products through genome editing to prevent GvHD and enhance function. (B) In vitro transduction: gene delivery methods include γ-retroviral and lentiviral vectors, transposon systems, and mRNA electroporation, which seek to improve efficiency, reduce cost, and enhance safety. (C) In vitro expansion: optimization of cytokine supplementation, including IL-4, IL-2, IL-7, and IL-15, improves CAR-T fitness and preserves less-differentiated phenotypes. (D) In vivo CAR-T: direct in vivo gene delivery using viral vectors or non-viral nanoparticles enables in situ CAR-T generation, shortens manufacturing timelines, reduces costs, and demonstrates early clinical feasibility

Universal CAR-T products are generated through gene-editing strategies that disrupt T cell receptor (TCR)-associated genes and modify major histocompatibility complex (MHC) expression to reduce the risks of graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) and host immune rejection while enhancing functional performance. Additional modifications targeting inhibitory checkpoints such as PD-1 or CTLA-4 may further augment antitumor activity [162, 163]. Early clinical studies employing CRISPR/Cas9 or zinc finger nuclease-edited, CD19/CD22- and IL13Rα2-targeted CAR-T cells have demonstrated encouraging efficacy with manageable safety in r/r ALL and glioblastoma, respectively [164, 165]. Similar gene-edited cellular platforms have also shown feasibility in refractory autoimmune diseases, including cases achieving sustained drug-free remission in SLE [166, 167].

Umbilical cord blood provides an alternative source of less-differentiated T cells with lower GvHD risk and substantial proliferative capacity [168]. Preclinical studies indicate that cord blood–derived CAR-T cells exhibit enhanced antitumor activity compared with patient-derived products in murine models [169]. For example, CD123-targeted CAR-T cells generated from cord blood have demonstrated favorable differentiation profiles and potent cytotoxicity both in vitro and in vivo [170]. However, the limited absolute number of T cells in cord blood necessitates more complex expansion protocols to achieve therapeutic doses.

iPSC-derived T cells are emerging as a promising platform as gene-editing and differentiation technologies continue to advance. FT819, an iPSC-derived anti-CD19 CAR-T product, expresses uniform CAR constructs and lacks TCR expression, thereby minimizing GvHD risk while retaining robust proliferative capacity. In preclinical B-ALL models, FT819 effectively controlled tumor growth [171] and has subsequently advanced into clinical trials for B-cell malignancies [172]. Overall, allogeneic and stem cell–derived CAR-T platforms hold significant potential to streamline manufacturing, reduce cost, and expand patient access. Continued preclinical and clinical investigation will be essential to optimize their safety, persistence, and therapeutic durability.

Optimization of in vitro CAR transduction methods

Efficient gene transfer of the CAR construct into purified T cells is a critical step in CAR-T manufacturing, requiring high transduction efficiency, genomic stability, and minimal immunogenicity. Current approaches are broadly categorized into viral (e.g., γ-retroviral and lentiviral vectors) and non-viral methods (e.g., transposon systems and electroporation) (Fig. 4B). Viral vector–mediated transduction remains the standard for most commercial CAR-T products; however, it carries risks of insertional mutagenesis, high production costs, and limited scalability. To address these limitations, optimization strategies such as suspension HEK293-based lentiviral production platforms and the identification of high-yield producer cell lines have been developed to enhance efficiency and reduce manufacturing expenses [173, 174]. Recent advances include the development of CD3-targeted lentiviral vectors compatible with both human and non-human primate (NHP) models. These vectors efficiently transduce T cells, support CAR-T expansion in vitro, and exhibit resistance to serum complement–mediated inactivation, thereby facilitating evaluation of biodistribution, safety, and efficacy in translational models [175].

Non-viral approaches offer additional flexibility. Transposon systems enable stable genomic integration of CAR constructs, whereas mRNA electroporation allows transient CAR expression lasting several days, providing a favorable safety profile and reversible expression kinetics [176, 177]. The CARAMBA trial, for example, utilizes the Sleeping Beauty transposon system to generate non-viral CAR-T products in MM [178]. Genome editing technologies have further enabled precise, virus-free CAR integration. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated site-specific insertion of CAR sequences into defined genomic loci simplifies manufacturing and reduces production time. PD1-19bbz, a CD19-targeted CAR-T construct inserted at the PD-1 locus, represents a notable example. Preclinical studies demonstrated potent tumor cytotoxicity [179], and in a phase 1 clinical trial (NCT04213469), 21 patients with r/r B-NHL achieved an ORR of 100% and a CR rate of 85.7%, with no CRS or neurotoxicity exceeding grade 2 [180]. Overall, continued refinement of viral and non-viral gene transfer platforms is essential to improve manufacturing efficiency, safety, scalability, and clinical consistency of CAR-T products.

Optimization of in vitro expansion conditions for CAR-T cells

Following successful transduction, CAR-T cells must undergo ex vivo expansion to achieve therapeutic cell doses. This phase is both time- and labor-intensive, and large-scale expansion may promote differentiation, exhaustion, and reduced persistence after infusion. Therefore, optimizing culture conditions to preserve functional fitness while improving expansion efficiency is critical for generating high-quality CAR-T products and maximizing clinical benefit (Fig. 4C). Prolonged in vitro culture can drive excessive differentiation and exhaustion. Although cytokines such as IL-2, IL-7, and IL-15 are commonly used to support expansion, IL-7 and IL-15 are generally preferred over IL-2 because they better maintain less-differentiated phenotypes while sustaining proliferation [181, 182]. IL-21 has also been shown to enrich low-differentiation CAR-T populations and enhance cytotoxic capacity, thereby improving the functional quality of the final product [183].

The role of IL-4 in CAR-T expansion and persistence appears context dependent. Exposure to IL-4 induces features of exhaustion in CD8+ CAR-T cells, whereas IL-4 pathway editing or neutralizing antibodies enhance antitumor activity and reduce exhaustion in MCL xenograft models [184]. However, multi-omics analyses in pediatric leukemia have suggested that type 2 T-cell programs may correlate with durable remission, indicating that selective enhancement of Th2-like features could support long-term persistence. Indeed, an IL-4–IgG Fc fusion protein (Fc-IL-4) was shown to act directly on CD8+ T cells, enrich functional terminally exhausted subsets within tumors, and augment type 1–dominant CAR-T responses through metabolic reprogramming [185, 186]. These findings underscore the nuanced and context-specific effects of cytokine modulation during ex vivo expansion.

In addition, because in vivo T-cell expansion relies on interactions with antigen-presenting cells (APCs), biomaterial-based platforms that mimic APC-mediated activation have been developed. These systems enhance in vitro expansion efficiency and may shorten manufacturing timelines while preserving functional potency [187]. Taken together, precise optimization of cytokine supplementation, culture duration, and activation platforms is essential to balance proliferative expansion with preservation of stem-like and functional characteristics in CAR-T products.

In vivo CAR-T

All currently approved CAR-T products require autologous T cells to be genetically engineered and expanded ex vivo before reinfusion. In contrast, in vivo CAR-T therapy delivers CAR-encoding gene constructs directly into the patient using viral or non-viral vectors to modify T cells in situ (Fig. 4D). This strategy eliminates the need for ex vivo isolation, expansion, and reinfusion, thereby reducing production timelines from weeks to days and lowering manufacturing costs. Importantly, it addresses the risk of clinical deterioration during the manufacturing interval and represents a promising evolution in CAR-T development [188].

Lentiviral vector-based in vivo delivery platforms, such as VivoVec, have demonstrated feasibility in preclinical models. These systems successfully generated functional CAR-T cells in humanized mice and NHPs, leading to measurable antitumor activity and sustained B-cell depletion [189, 190]. Early clinical signals are emerging in hematologic malignancies. A BCMA-targeted in vivo CAR-T product (ESO-T01) achieved an ORR of 100% in four patients with RRMM, including two sCRs [191]. In addition, lentiviral in vivo CAR-T candidates targeting CD19 (UB-VV111) and CD20 (INT2104) are currently being evaluated in clinical trials for B-cell malignancies [192].

Nanotechnology-based delivery systems provide an alternative non-viral approach. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) can deliver CAR-encoding mRNA directly to T cells following systemic administration. A targeted LNP-mediated system achieved CAR expression in more than 80% of CD8+ T cells in humanized mouse models, substantially simplifying manufacturing and enhancing scalability [193]. Peptide-based, non-lipid nanoparticle platforms have also been developed to deliver CAR mRNA efficiently in murine models, offering a virus-free strategy for in vivo engineering [194]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that LNP-mediated delivery enables efficient and safe CAR expression in CD8+ T cells in both oncologic and autoimmune settings [195]. Phase I clinical trials evaluating in vivo CAR-T approaches targeting TROP2 in advanced epithelial tumors and GPC3 in hepatocellular carcinoma are currently underway [196, 197] (Table 4). Although significant challenges remain, particularly with respect to delivery specificity, safety monitoring, and long-term durability, continued advances in vector engineering and nanoparticle design may facilitate broader clinical implementation of in vivo CAR-T therapies.

Potential preclinical strategies

Epigenetic regulation

Integration of epigenetic modulators with CAR-T therapy offers a promising strategy to enhance T-cell function and remodel the TME, thereby augmenting antitumor efficacy (Fig. 5A). High-throughput screening has identified class I histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi), including M344 and chidamide, as agents capable of increasing chromatin accessibility, modulating transcription factor networks, and activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. These effects support the maintenance of memory phenotypes, limit exhaustion, and enhance antitumor activity both in vitro and in vivo [198].

Potential strategies to potentiate CAR-T therapy. (A) Epigenetic regulation: class I HDAC inhibitors and EZH2 or dual EZH1/2 inhibition increase chromatin accessibility and promote memory programming, thereby mitigating exhaustion and enhancing antitumor activity. (B) Metabolic reprogramming: enhancing metabolic fitness through FOXO1 overexpression, Foxp3-mediated mitochondrial modulation, or IDH2 inhibition with enasidenib promotes mitochondrial biogenesis, memory differentiation, and durable cytotoxicity within the TME. (C) Oncolytic viruses: genetically modified OVs deliver immunomodulatory transgenes or ectopic antigens to augment CAR-T recognition and reverse local immunosuppression. (D) Emerging technologies: photothermal control strategies enhance safety and efficacy, while ultrasound-responsive systems (e.g., EchoBack-CAR) enable spatiotemporal regulation of CAR expression with limited off-tumor effects. CRISPR-based functional screening further identifies cell-intrinsic targets that can be leveraged to improve CAR-T fitness, persistence, and antitumor activity

Inhibition of EZH2 provides an additional epigenetic approach. Through transcriptional reprogramming, EZH2 inhibitors enhance tumor immunogenicity, promote T-cell infiltration, and facilitate differentiation of CD8+ memory T cells, thereby counteracting exhaustion. Based on these mechanistic insights, a clinical trial evaluating the EZH2 inhibitor tazemetostat in combination with CD19 CAR-T therapy for r/r B-NHL (NCT05934838) has been initiated [199]. Moreover, dual inhibition of EZH1 and EZH2, as achieved with valemetostat, has demonstrated broader and more potent antitumor activity than selective EZH2 inhibition in both hematologic and solid tumor models, suggesting expanded therapeutic potential [200].

To address impaired linker for activation of T cells (LAT) phosphorylation in second-generation CAR-T cells under conditions of low antigen stimulation, investigators have developed a bicistronic CAR platform incorporating a LAT-activating CAR (LAT-CART). This design restores LAT signaling and significantly improves sensitivity, persistence, and cytotoxicity against antigen-low tumors [201]. These findings indicate that coordinated epigenetic modulation and intracellular signaling optimization may represent a rational strategy to overcome antigen-low resistance and enhance CAR-T durability.

Metabolic reprogramming

Metabolic reprogramming represents a promising strategy to enhance CAR-T adaptability within the TME by improving mitochondrial fitness and sustaining antitumor activity (Fig. 5B). Transcriptomic analyses have identified forkhead box O1 (FOXO1) as a critical regulator of memory potential and metabolic resilience. Enforced FOXO1 expression promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and proliferative capacity, thereby enhancing antitumor efficacy and long-term persistence of CAR-T cells [202, 203]. Beyond FOXO1, modulation of mitochondrial dynamics has emerged as another avenue for metabolic optimization. The regulatory T-cell transcription factor Foxp3 has been shown to reprogram CAR-T metabolism through a non-transcriptional mechanism involving mitochondrial remodeling. CAR-T cells engineered to express Foxp3 demonstrated improved efficacy and persistence against solid tumors in preclinical models, suggesting a metabolically driven and potentially low-toxicity strategy for enhancing therapeutic durability [204]. Pharmacologic targeting of metabolic enzymes offers additional opportunities. Screening of mitochondrial-directed compounds identified enasidenib (ENA), an inhibitor of isocitrate dehydrogenase 2 (IDH2), as a promoter of memory CAR-T differentiation and antileukemic cytotoxicity. Mechanistically, IDH2 activity restricts glucose utilization through the pentose phosphate pathway, thereby limiting oxidative stress adaptation under nutrient-deprived conditions. Inhibition of IDH2 reverses this constraint, enhancing metabolic flexibility and functional persistence of CAR-T cells [205]. These findings underscore the importance of metabolic fitness in CAR-T performance and highlight mitochondrial and metabolic pathways as actionable targets to overcome TME-imposed constraints. Continued exploration of metabolic modulators may further refine CAR-T durability and efficacy.

Oncolytic viruses (OVs)

OVs selectively infect and lyse tumor cells while sparing normal tissues. When combined with CAR-T therapy, OVs can function as intratumoral delivery platforms for immunomodulatory transgenes, enhance CAR-T activation, reverse TME-mediated immunosuppression, and directly induce tumor lysis with subsequent release of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), thereby broadening antitumor immunity. This combinatorial strategy is particularly attractive in solid tumors, where antigen heterogeneity and immune exclusion limit CAR-T efficacy [206].

One approach employs OVs engineered to express truncated, non-signaling CD19 (CD19t), enabling CD19 CAR-T cells to recognize and eliminate CD19-negative tumors. In murine models, OV-mediated CD19t delivery significantly enhanced the antitumor activity of CD19 CAR-T cells [207]. However, prior studies indicate that OV pretreatment can produce both beneficial and detrimental effects. While OVs may remodel the TME and promote CAR-T recruitment, OV-induced type I interferon responses can also trigger CAR-T apoptosis and reduce cellular persistence [208]. To address this limitation, investigators have developed strategies in which OVs are preloaded onto CAR-T cells in vitro, allowing viral antigens to activate endogenous TCR signaling before infusion. In murine models of melanoma and glioma, systemic administration of this combination approach improved tumor control and prolonged survival [209]. Taken together, these findings suggest that OVs represent a versatile platform to enhance CAR-T efficacy in solid tumors. However, further mechanistic studies are required to delineate the bidirectional interactions between OVs and CAR-T cells within the TME and to optimize the timing, dosing, and delivery of combination regimens (Fig. 5C).

Other emerging technologies

In recent years, CAR-T therapy has increasingly integrated interdisciplinary innovations, including photothermal regulation and ultrasound-based activation strategies, and systematic CRISPR-based functional screening, all of which remain in preclinical development (Fig. 5D). One approach combines systemically administered CAR-T cells with synthetic thermal gene switches that enable localized intratumoral expression of immunostimulatory payloads, such as an IL-15 superagonist or an NKG2D-based bispecific engager. Photothermal activation allows spatially restricted control of transgene expression, thereby enhancing antitumor efficacy, reducing antigen escape, and potentially improving safety profiles [210]. Ultrasound-guided activation systems represent another promising platform. The EchoBack-CAR T system utilizes a highly sensitive heat shock promoter identified through library screening, enabling focused ultrasound-triggered CAR expression. This system incorporates a CAR signaling–driven positive feedback loop to sustain activation. In preclinical models, EchoBack-hGD2 CAR-T cells targeting GD2 demonstrated potent cytotoxicity and prolonged activity in three-dimensional glioblastoma systems. Similarly, EchoBack-PSMA CAR-T cells targeting PSMA achieved durable tumor suppression with limited off-target toxicity in prostate cancer models [211]. These technologies illustrate how external physical stimuli can be harnessed to achieve spatially and temporally controlled CAR-T activation.

In parallel, systematic CRISPR-based functional screening provides a complementary preclinical approach to identify intrinsic regulators of CAR-T function. A genome-wide CRISPR screening platform in primary human CAR-T cells identified RHOG deletion, alone or in combination with FAS deletion, as a strategy that enhanced CAR-T activity across multiple preclinical models [212]. Similarly, an in vivo loss-of-function CRISPR screen of BCMA-targeted CAR-T cells in a myeloma model identified CDKN1B as a regulator of late CAR-T fitness, and its deletion improved CAR-T proliferation, effector function, tumor control, and survival [213]. These findings provide a framework for the rational discovery of next-generation CAR-T designs, although clinical translation remains to be established.

Continued interdisciplinary collaboration will be required to translate these emerging approaches into next-generation CAR-T platforms, particularly in solid tumors in which precision targeting and safety remain paramount.

Biomarker-guided patient selection and monitoring

Biomarker-guided assessment may complement therapeutic optimization by informing patient selection and longitudinal monitoring across the CAR-T treatment course (Table 5). Before lymphodepletion, disease burden and systemic inflammation provide readily accessible measures of risk. In r/r LBCL, high metabolic tumor volume (MTV), elevated LDH, and elevated CRP defined a clinical profile enriched for early progression and shorter survival [214]. Building on these variables, an adverse preinfusion inflammatory profile defined by InflaMix has also been associated with CAR-T treatment failure and inferior survival in non-Hodgkin lymphoma [215]. PET-based risk models, including CAR-IMPI, may further refine pretreatment risk stratification [216].

Dynamic biomarkers may facilitate early assessment of treatment response and emerging resistance. Early FDG-PET response and changes in MTV after bridging therapy correlate with subsequent outcomes in LBCL [217, 218]. Molecular monitoring using ctDNA-based measurable residual disease (MRD) assessment may provide an early signal of treatment response after CAR-T therapy [219]. Assessment of target-antigen expression at baseline and relapse also remains important, as low antigen density and antigen loss can contribute to primary resistance and antigen-negative relapse [23, 154].

CAR-T expansion, persistence, and infusion-product T-cell fitness provide complementary information on treatment durability [43, 220]. Biomarkers of treatment-related toxicity are also clinically relevant. Early increases in inflammatory cytokines and endothelial activation markers have been associated with severe CRS and ICANS [56, 221]. In addition, the CAR-HEMATOTOX score, which integrates baseline hematopoietic reserve and inflammation, predicts prolonged hematotoxicity and infectious complications [222, 223]. Although these biomarkers are increasingly informative, most evidence is derived from CD19-directed CAR-T therapy in B-cell malignancies, and prospective validation across products and disease settings is required before they can guide standardized treatment adaptation.

Strategies after CAR-T therapy failure

Although CAR-T therapy has substantially improved outcomes for patients with relapsed or refractory hematologic malignancies, real-world data indicate that more than 60% of patients experience disease progression or relapse within two years. Prognosis following relapse remains poor, with median OS often less than six months, particularly among those who relapse early within 30 days of infusion [224]. Resistance is multifactorial, reflecting tumor-intrinsic evolution, compromised T-cell fitness, and persistent immunosuppressive pressure within the TME [225]. A range of post-CAR-T therapeutic strategies has been investigated, including repeat CAR-T infusion, target switching, multi-target CAR-T approaches, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT), BsAbs, ICIs, and rational combination regimens (Fig. 6). However, standardized treatment algorithms are lacking, and clinical decision-making remains largely individualized. Therefore, a structured, evidence-based framework that incorporates relapse timing, antigen expression status, disease burden, and prior response characteristics is urgently needed to guide subsequent therapeutic selection and optimize patient outcomes.

Retreatment with target-switching CAR-T

Target switching represents a pragmatic approach to overcoming antigen escape after CD19-directed therapy, most commonly by redirecting specificity toward CD20 or CD22 (Table 6). A phase 1 clinical trial evaluating CD20-targeted CAR-T (e.g., C-CAR066) in patients with DLBCL who relapsed after CD19 CAR-T reported an ORR of 92.9% and a CR rate of 57.1%, with a median PFS of 9.4 months and a 7.1% incidence of grade ≥ 3 CRS [226]. A large retrospective analysis further demonstrated that salvage CD20-directed CART2 significantly improved CR rate (27.8% vs. 7.9%, p = 0.03) and median OS (8.15 vs. 3.02 months, p < 0.001) compared with non–CAR-T salvage therapies [233]. Similarly, CD22-targeted CAR-T has achieved ORRs of approximately 85.7% with encouraging survival outcomes in patients with DLBCL following CD19 CAR-T failure [227, 228]. However, several factors have been identified as significantly associated with CART2 outcomes, including an infusion interval of ≤ 100 days from CART1, the presence of bulky disease at retreatment (tumor mass > 7 cm), and TP53 mutations [234]. These findings underscore the importance of patient selection and disease biology in determining the efficacy of second-round CAR-T strategies.

Retreatment with dual-target CAR-T

Dual-target CAR-T cells are designed to simultaneously recognize two tumor-associated antigens, thereby broadening target coverage and reducing the likelihood of escape driven by single-antigen selection pressure. Preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated the superiority of this approach in preventing antigen loss–mediated resistance [235, 236], and early clinical data are encouraging. KITE-363, a dual-target CAR-T product directed against CD19 and CD20, achieved an ORR of 80% and a CR rate of 67% in a phase 1 study of 37 patients with r/r BCL,including 19% who had previously received CD19-directed CAR-T therapy [229]. Similarly, a phase 1/2 trial evaluating CD19/CD20 CAR-T in r/r BCL reported a CR rate of 67%, with 6 of 9 patients achieving complete remission [230]. Dual-target constructs directed against CD19/CD22 have also shown meaningful activity in patients with B-NHL who relapsed after CD19 CAR-T, with 2 of 4 patients achieving CR [231]. In MM, BCMA/CD19 dual-target CAR-T achieved a 60% response rate in a phase 1 trial enrolling patients who had relapsed following prior CAR-T therapy [116]. Moreover, tandem CD19/CD22 CAR-T demonstrated significant efficacy in high-risk r/r B-ALL, with 18-month OS rates approaching 70% [232]. These findings support dual targeting as a rational rescue strategy after CAR-T failure. However, larger prospective studies are required to determine optimal construct design, patient selection criteria, and treatment sequencing in the post-CAR-T setting.

Retreatment with armored CAR-T

Armored CAR-T cells are engineered to secrete immunomodulatory cytokines or chemokines, with the goal of reversing T-cell exhaustion and overcoming TME-mediated immunosuppression. The first IL-18–secreting armored construct (huCART19-IL18) demonstrated notable efficacy in patients with lymphoma who relapsed after CD19 CAR-T therapy, achieving an ORR of 81% and a CR rate of 52%, with a median remission duration of 9.6 months. Enhanced CAR-T expansion was observed in selected subgroups, including patients with prior CD28 costimulatory signaling failure [141]. An alternative armoring strategy involves secretion of supportive cytokines and chemokines to promote immune recruitment and persistence. IL-7/CCL19–secreting (7 × 19) CAR-T achieved an ORR of approximately 79.5% and a CR rate of 56.4% in patients with r/r DLBCL, with a relatively low incidence of grade ≥ 3 CRS (approximately 12.8%) [140]. Comparable approaches have shown promising activity in RRMM and cutaneous T-cell lymphoma [237, 238]. Although these data suggest that armored CAR-T constructs may help restore efficacy after CAR-T failure, evidence specifically in the post–CAR-T relapse setting remains limited. Larger, prospective studies are needed to clarify their role, optimal timing, and patient selection criteria within salvage treatment algorithms.

Monoclonal antibodies

Following CAR-T failure, impaired T-cell fitness and exhaustion may limit secondary immune responses [239–241]. Although checkpoint blockade can restore T-cell function in preclinical models [242, 243], clinical efficacy in the post–CAR-T setting has been modest. PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy in patients who relapse after CAR-T has yielded a CR rate of only approximately 10%, with a median PFS of less than 2 months [244, 245]. Rational combination strategies may offer greater therapeutic benefit. Preclinical evidence indicates that dual inhibition of PD-1 and LAG3 enhances T-cell proliferation and improves tumor clearance compared with single-agent blockade [246]. However, timing appears critical. Early administration of PD-1 inhibitors may disrupt T-cell activation dynamics, whereas delayed intervention may be insufficient to reverse established exhaustion in terminally dysfunctional clones [247]. These findings suggest that checkpoint inhibition alone is unlikely to provide durable control after CAR-T failure. Optimizing combination strategies and defining appropriate timing of intervention will be essential to improve clinical outcomes in this setting.

BsAbs

BsAbs that engage CD3 and a tumor-associated antigen provide an immediately available, off-the-shelf therapeutic option after CAR-T failure. These agents are generally associated with low rates of severe CRS or neurotoxicity and offer flexibility for combination strategies (Table 7) [262]. In DLBCL, treatment outcomes appear closely related to the timing of relapse. Patients who relapse within three months of CAR-T infusion tend to have poor responses and short PFS, whereas those relapsing beyond six months demonstrate higher response rates and more durable disease control [239]. Several CD20×CD3 BsAbs have shown meaningful activity in the post–CAR-T setting. In a phase 1/2 study, glofitamab achieved a CR rate of 35%, with a 12-month duration of CR of 78% [248]. Epcoritamab, administered subcutaneously, produced a CR rate of 34.4% and a median remission duration of 9.7 months in the EPCORE NHL-1 trial [251]. Odronextamab and mosunetuzumab have also demonstrated activity; however, efficacy appears attenuated in patients with very early refractory relapse after CAR-T therapy [253, 254].

Combination regimens may further enhance depth and durability of response. In the STARGLO trial, glofitamab combined with GemOx achieved a CR rate of 58.5% and a median PFS of 13.8 months [250, 263]. Similarly, epcoritamab plus GemOx yielded a CR rate of 45% in a phase 2 study [252]. In a phase 1b/2 trial, mosunetuzumab combined with polatuzumab vedotin achieved a CR rate of 40% and a median PFS of 9.6 months; in the post–CAR-T subgroup, the CR rate was 31% with a median PFS of 5.7 months [255]. Additional studies are warranted to define its role specifically in the post–CAR-T relapse population.

In MM, BsAbs have become a central salvage strategy after CAR-T failure, particularly when directed against non-BCMA antigens that bypass prior selection pressure. Talquetamab (GPRC5D×CD3) demonstrated an ORR of 72% and a median PFS of 7.7 months in the MonumenTAL-1 study, with notable efficacy in extramedullary disease [256]. Teclistamab (BCMA×CD3) achieved an ORR of 53.3% and a CR rate of 26.7% in the MajesTEC-1 trial, although median PFS was relatively short at 4.4 months [259]. Cevostamab (FcRH5×CD3) produced an ORR of 44.4% in BCMA-resistant patients [260] and 73% in a phase 1/2 study of RRMM [261]. Combination approaches may further deepen responses. In the RedirecTT-1 study, talquetamab combined with teclistamab achieved an ORR of 78% in patients with RRMM [257]. Additionally, talquetamab plus daratumumab achieved an ORR of 78%, potentially through Treg depletion and TME reprogramming [258, 264].

Overall, BsAbs represent a readily deployable salvage option after CAR-T failure, although response depth and durability remain influenced by relapse timing and disease biology.

Trispecific antibodies (TsAbs)

TsAbs simultaneously engage CD3 while targeting two tumor-associated epitopes or incorporating costimulatory domains such as CD28. This configuration enhances immune synapse formation, broadens antigen coverage, and may partially overcome dysfunctional T-cell states [265–267]. In lymphoma, the CD3/CD19/CD20-targeting TsAb BR110 optimized epitope configuration to improve T-cell recruitment and became the first TsAb to enter clinical evaluation [268]. In MM, a CD3/BCMA/GPRC5D-targeting TsAb achieved an ORR of 55% in patients previously refractory to BCMA- or GPRC5D-directed therapies [269]. SAR442257 (CD38/CD28/CD3), which incorporates CD28-mediated costimulation, demonstrated DCRs of 60% in RRMM and 28.6% in r/r NHL [270, 271]. Additional investigational candidates, including PIT565 (CD3/CD19/CD2), SIM0500 (CD3×BCMA×GPRC5D), and MBS314 (CD3×BCMA×GPRC5D), are currently under clinical evaluation [272–274]. As clinical experience with TsAbs expands, these multi-target platforms may provide enhanced protection against antigen escape and more durable immune activation, although comparative efficacy data remain limited.

In the post–CAR-T setting, TsAb-based strategies may be particularly relevant when rapid disease control or broader antigen coverage is required. Target selection should be guided by antigen expression, with non–cross-reactive targets preferred when antigen escape is suspected.

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs)

ADCs deliver antigen-directed cytotoxic payloads independently of endogenous T-cell fitness, rendering them particularly attractive in the post–CAR-T setting, where relapse is often driven by antigen escape and/or T-cell exhaustion. Polatuzumab vedotin is a CD79b-directed ADC that releases monomethyl auristatin E upon internalization, inducing tumor apoptosis and potentially enhancing immune-mediated cytotoxicity within the TME. Because CD79b expression is frequently preserved in DLBCL and is less prone to downregulation than CD19, it represents a rational alternative target after CD19-directed CAR-T relapse [275, 276].

Pola, administered either as monotherapy or in combination regimens such as Pola-BR, has demonstrated meaningful clinical activity in patients with CAR-T failure. Reported ORRs range from 62% to 73%, with a median PFS of 6.1 months. Notably, an ORR of 53% has also been observed in patients with early progression within three months after CAR-T therapy [277–279]. In addition, Pola has been employed as bridging therapy to stem cell transplantation and may facilitate subsequent consolidation in carefully selected patients [277, 280]. Future studies should explore rational combinations of ADCs with BsAbs or epigenetic modulators to deepen responses and prolong durability. Incorporation of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)-based dynamic monitoring may further refine therapeutic sequencing and timing, particularly in patients with high tumor burden, in whom earlier intervention may meaningfully alter disease trajectory.

CAR-NK

CAR-NK therapy offers a distinct safety profile characterized by low rates of severe CRS, neurotoxicity, and GvHD [281]. The absence of clonal T-cell expansion further renders CAR-NK particularly suitable for patients with poor performance status or a history of severe treatment-related toxicities [281]. In addition, the off-the-shelf nature of CAR-NK products addresses the prolonged manufacturing timelines and high costs associated with autologous CAR-T production, which typically requires three to four weeks. A single umbilical cord blood donor can generate multiple cryopreserved CAR-NK doses, substantially improving treatment accessibility. Importantly, CAR-NK cells employ diverse tumor-recognition mechanisms. Beyond CAR-mediated cytotoxicity, they retain native NK cell receptor–mediated activity (e.g., via NKG2D and natural cytotoxicity receptors), enabling antigen-independent killing and potentially reducing the risk of antigen escape [281].

In hematologic malignancies, CAR-NK therapy has demonstrated encouraging activity in patients relapsing after CAR-T (Table 8). TAK-007, an allogeneic CD19-targeted CAR-NK derived from cord blood, achieved an ORR of 42.9% in nine BCL patients previously treated with CAR-T [282]. Similarly, the iPSC-derived CAR-NK product FT596 combined with rituximab produced an ORR of 45% in 27 patients with prior CAR-T exposure, without unexpected immune toxicity [283]. An IL-15–armored CD19-BBz CAR-NK incorporating 4-1BB demonstrated an ORR of 62.5% and a CR rate of 50% in refractory LBCL, with a median PFS of 9.5 months and OS not reached. Single-cell analyses further associated memory-like NK subsets with durable remission [284].

To enhance persistence and efficacy, current strategies emphasize genetic optimization and rational combinations. Incorporation of costimulatory domains such as 4-1BB has been shown to extend in vivo persistence, with CD19-BBz CAR-NK detectable for more than 15 months in some cases [284]. Dual-target constructs, including BCMA/GPRC5D designs, have reduced relapse risk associated with antigen escape in preclinical models [285]. Ongoing investigations are evaluating additional dual-target platforms, such as CD19/BCMA CAR-NK and CD19/CD22 CAR-NK. Combination approaches with PD-1 blockade have also demonstrated the capacity to reverse TME-mediated suppression and enhance CAR-NK infiltration and activity in preclinical studies [286].

Despite these advances, important challenges remain. CAR-NK persistence is generally limited, with peak activity occurring four to seven days after infusion. Moreover, the TME continues to impose barriers through immunosuppressive cytokines and antigen heterogeneity. Future research will likely focus on improving cryopreservation and reconstitution protocols, as well as engineering CAR constructs capable of targeting tumor stroma and resisting TME-mediated inhibition.

Allo-HSCT

Allo-HSCT represents a potentially curative strategy for patients who relapse after CAR-T therapy by harnessing graft-versus-leukemia (GVL) effects to eradicate drug-resistant clones. Its therapeutic mechanisms include reversal of the immunosuppressive TME through reconstitution of donor-derived immunity, elimination of antigen-escape variants via GVL responses that are not restricted to a single target, and restoration of long-term immune surveillance to sustain antitumor memory [287]. This approach may be particularly advantageous in patients with early relapse, high tumor burden, or genetically heterogeneous resistant disease.

Accumulating evidence indicates that allo-HSCT can improve long-term survival in selected patients after CAR-T failure, although outcomes are strongly influenced by transplantation timing and disease status at the time of intervention. In a study from the German Lymphoma Alliance, high-risk DLBCL patients, most of whom had not achieved CR prior to transplantation, had 1-year OS rates ranging from 28% to 36% [288]. Additional retrospective analyses have demonstrated that earlier transplantation, particularly in patients achieving CR before allo-HSCT, is associated with superior survival, with reported 1-year OS rates of 53% and 59% and 1-year PFS rates of 34% and 45%, respectively [289, 290].

Overall, allo-HSCT remains a valuable consolidation strategy for selected patients after CAR-T failure, particularly those achieving CR with limited bridging therapy. Earlier transplantation may be associated with more favorable outcomes, whereas delayed transplantation and multiple bridging lines portend a higher risk of treatment failure [288, 289]. Future strategies should integrate TME profiling and dynamic ctDNA monitoring to refine risk stratification and optimize transplantation timing, thereby improving individualized post–CAR-T management [219, 291].

Framework for post–CAR-T decision-making

Post–CAR-T salvage strategies for hematologic malignancies should be individualized according to relapse timing, tumor burden, antigen status, and the predominant resistance mechanisms. Early relapse within three months of infusion typically reflects aggressive disease biology and often necessitates rapidly deployable therapies. In this setting, T-cell–redirecting antibodies, administered alone or in combination with cytotoxic agents, may provide prompt disease control while additional options are evaluated. In contrast, patients with later relapse may be candidates for more complex cellular strategies, including target-switched or multi-target CAR-T approaches. In appropriately selected patients who achieve meaningful disease control, consolidation with allo-HSCT may be considered to leverage GVL effects and prolong remission. When antigen escape is suspected, therapeutic redirection toward non–cross-reactive targets or the use of multi-antigen antibody platforms may broaden coverage and mitigate repeated selective pressure on a single epitope. Taken together, a mechanism-informed and timing-adapted strategy is essential to optimize outcomes after CAR-T failure, balancing rapid disease control with opportunities for durable immune reconstitution.

Conclusion

CAR-T therapy has delivered meaningful and durable clinical benefit in selected hematologic malignancies, whereas clinical translation in solid tumors remains at an earlier stage. In hematologic malignancies, long-term benefit is constrained primarily by antigen escape, limited in vivo persistence, and product- and host-related factors; in solid tumors, these challenges are compounded by intratumoral heterogeneity, impaired trafficking, and an immunosuppressive TME. Beyond oncology, early clinical studies in B-cell-mediated autoimmune diseases suggest the potential for durable immune reset, although longer follow-up and prospective validation are needed. Within the proposed “Why–How–What if” framework (Fig. 7), overcoming resistance requires coordinated advances across multiple domains. Mechanism-driven combination strategies with targeted agents and immunomodulators may enhance CAR-T fitness and reshape the TME. Dual- and multi-target constructs can mitigate antigen escape, while armored CAR-T platforms provide local immune modulation to counteract suppressive signals. Optimization of manufacturing platforms, including in vivo CAR-T approaches, may further expand accessibility and improve product consistency. For patients who experience CAR-T failure, salvage strategies should be individualized and guided by relapse timing, antigen status, and disease biology. Rapidly deployable antibody-based therapies, second-generation cellular approaches, and consolidation with allo-HSCT in appropriate candidates represent complementary options within a structured decision-making paradigm.

Summary of failure mechanisms, enhancement strategies, and post-failure options for CAR-T therapy. This review constructs a “Why–How–What if” framework that links resistance mechanisms to corresponding optimization strategies and structured post-failure management pathways

Looking forward, sustained progress will depend on mechanism-based cellular engineering, biomarker-guided therapeutic sequencing, and integration of diversified platforms, including allogeneic “off-the-shelf” CAR-T, CAR-NK, CAR-M, BsAbs, TsAbs, and ADCs. Through iterative innovation and personalized treatment strategies, CAR-based immunotherapy may achieve broader durability, safety, and global accessibility across hematologic and solid malignancies, with emerging applications in autoimmune diseases.
