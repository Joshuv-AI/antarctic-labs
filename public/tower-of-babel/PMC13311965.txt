Occupational and environmental risk factors for salivary gland cancer: a systematic literature review
Authors: Denny Choi, Khayati Patel, Andrew Zarnke, David Boyce-Fappiano, Donald Cole, Sarah McCormick Rhodes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOccupational and environmental risk factors for salivary gland cancer (SGC) are largely unrecognised. This systematic review is the first to characterise the relationship between employment in specific industries or occupations and risk of SGC, as well as exposure to key occupational and environmental hazards.Studies published up to September 2023 were collected from three electronic databases and systematically screened. The Health Assessment Workplace Collaborative programme was used to tag studies based on pre-established inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies eligible for inclusion (n=24) underwent an in-depth review to extract key study characteristics and findings, including effect estimates and 95% CIs. Studies were evaluated for influence of key biases. To identify priority sectors and hazards for intervention, effect direction plots were created to analyse the number and quality of studies reporting elevated rates of SGC across industry, occupation and exposure groups.24 articles were included for analysis. Exposure misclassification and confounding bias were a common concern across studies. Industry and occupation groups with the strongest relationship to SGC included cleaning services, material handling, food services, rubber/plastics production, engineering and construction/painting. Exposures with the strongest relationship to SGC included silica dust, cement dust, chlorinated solvents, formaldehyde and white spirits.Several occupational exposures were associated with an elevated risk of SGC, whereas environmental evidence was more limited. Additional research involving larger cohorts with quantitative exposure data is needed to further establish relationships between occupational and environmental exposures and often-overlooked rare cancers like SGC.PROSPERO registration number: CRD42023468037.Keywords: Occupational Health, Environmental Exposure, Dust, Solvents, Nickel

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Our review identified multiple hazards associated with an increased occurrence of SGC, namely silica dust, cement dust, chlorinated solvents, formaldehyde and white spirits. Specific industries and occupations were also implicated, including the following: cleaning services, material handling, food services, rubber/plastics production, engineering and construction/painting.

Introduction

Salivary gland cancer (SGC) encompasses a heterogenous group of rare malignancies most often located in the head and neck (HN) region, with an estimated annual incidence rate ranging from 0.64 to 3 cases per 100 000 people.1 2 Almost 80% of salivary gland tumours occur in the parotid gland and the majority of these are benign in nature, whereas salivary tumours arising from the submandibular, sublingual or minor salivary glands have a higher propensity to be malignant.3 The most common histological types, representing over 50% of all SGCs, include adenoid cystic carcinoma and mucoepidermoid carcinoma.3 Malignant salivary gland tumours can present with pain and facial nerve involvement, and often carry an increased risk of local reoccurrence, which can be debilitating for the patient.4 SGC impacts more men than women and is most common among elderly populations.2

Established risk factors for SGC are medical or behavioural, whereas occupational and environmental factors are largely unrecognised. To date, the relationship between major SGCs and common cancer risk factors like smoking and alcohol consumption is mixed. However, heavy smoking above 40 pack-years has been more consistently associated with SGCs,5 and elevated SGC among smokers has focused largely on Warthin tumour, a type of benign salivary gland tumour.4 Other risk factors potentially associated with SGC include several infectious disease agents, such as Epstein-Barr virus, HIV and human papillomavirus.5 By comparison, occupational and environmental risk factors for SGC remain largely unknown. Radiation exposure is the only well-established risk factor of this kind, as demonstrated by reports of elevated SGC rates observed among atomic bomb survivors, those with occupational radiation exposure and those exposed to therapeutic radiation to the HN region.6 7

The National Cancer Institute estimates that rare cancers, including SGC, account for 25% of all new cancer cases in the USA, which highlights the growing need to identify key risk factors for these illnesses.8 The authors of this study are employed at the Occupational Health Clinics for Ontario Workers (OHCOW), a network of clinics funded by the Ontario Ministry of Labour, Immigration, Training and Skills Development. OHCOW has observed an unexpectedly high rate of SGC among plastics and rubber workers across our clinics over the last decade. To support prevention initiatives, this systematic literature review aims to elucidate our understanding of occupational and environmental risk factors for SGC by identifying: (1) industries, occupations or job types that have been associated with elevated rates of SGC among workers, (2) occupational/workplace exposures that have been associated with elevated rates of SGC among workers and (3) environmental risk factors (eg, non-occupational exposures experienced via contact with air, soil, water, food, etc) that have been associated with elevated rates of SGC among community members.

Methods

Study design

We used the Health Assessment Workplace Collaborative (HAWC) programme to conduct literature searches using predefined search terms (available in online supplemental table S1). Literature searches (completed on 13 September 2023) were conducted on three databases: PubMed, Web of Science and OVID Embase. The protocol for this systematic review was registered in the international database of prospectively registered systematic reviews (PROSPERO) on 9 October 2023 (registration ID: CRD42023468037). No new studies were included after this date.

The abstract and title screening process (phase 1) involved two reviewers blinded to each other’s results, who tagged studies based on pre-established inclusion and exclusion criteria, along with other relevant study characteristics (eg, study type, outcome type). Any discrepancies were discussed in a consensus meeting with an additional reviewer to provide a separate appraisal and reach majority consensus. The reviewers did not review articles they consulted on, authored or coauthored. The comprehensive inclusion and exclusion criteria for this review are summarised in table 1 using the Population (including animal species), Exposure, Comparator and Outcomes framework. Studies were included only if they reported primary relative risk (RR) data for SGC (major or minor), salivary duct cancer or tracheal cancer, and studies reporting primary risk data for broader cancer groupings were excluded (eg, HN) (table 2).

At the end of phase 1, occupational health physicians (DB-F, DCo) reviewed all papers reporting solely on salivary duct cancer (n=1) and tracheal cancer (n=21). These studies were excluded from analysis, as many papers grouped tracheal with lung/bronchial tumours, which were mainly squamous cell carcinoma and not SGC.9 Studies included after phase 1 proceeded to a full-text review and detailed data extraction (phase 2), where an extensive list of variables was extracted from each study (eg, study type, exposure assessment, SGC effect estimates and 95% CI, etc, with a complete list presented in online supplemental table S2). A Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) diagram of our study inclusion and exclusion process during phase 1 and phase 2 is illustrated in online supplemental figure S1.

Bias assessment

Four study authors with training in exposure assessment and/or epidemiology, and blinded to others’ results, assessed the level of bias present in each study following methods used by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC).10 11 The authors commented on the following bias domains: misclassification of exposure, misclassification of outcome, healthy worker bias, confounding by lifestyle factors (eg, smoking), selection bias and recall bias. These biases are common in occupational and environmental studies. In particular, exposure misclassification is a well-recognised occurrence, where study participants are incorrectly categorised in relation to their true exposure level. This often occurs when there are incomplete or inaccurate job titles, lack of personal or environmental measurement data, use of large geographical scale as proxy for environmental exposure (eg, county of residence), or inaccurate questionnaire or job exposure matrix data. Healthy worker bias was included given it is a common form of bias in occupational studies (eg, cohort, nested case–control); however, this form of bias was not applicable to environmental studies.

Additional collaborators with training in occupational medicine and epidemiology were consulted if consensus on the level of bias was not reached for a given study. Studies were classified as having a ‘no to minimal’ level of concern or ‘moderate to major’ level of concern for each bias domain, and then later classified into levels of high, medium and low bias at the study level (examples in online supplemental table S3; figures1 2).

Level of evidence criteria

To support results synthesis, occupational hygienists grouped the effect estimates extracted from each study into broad categories of industry and occupation using related industry or occupation codes (eg, International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO)), where possible (online supplemental table S4), followed by occupational and environmental exposure categories (table 3; online supplemental table S5). For example, in online supplemental table S4 and figure 1, the broad category titled ‘Agriculture’ includes all studies reporting SGC effect estimates for occupation/industry categories related to agriculture, such as ‘General farmers (ISCO 6-11)’, ‘Agricultural production: crops (SIC 010)’ and ‘General farm workers (ISCO 6-21)’.

Due to the limited number of studies captured by our review, as well as the heterogeneity of studies, it was not feasible to conduct a meta-analysis. Thus, we completed the Synthesis Without Meta-Analysis (SWiM) checklist and created effect direction plots to increase the transparency of our results synthesis process12 (online supplemental table S6). Through an assessment of multiple factors, including the direction and precision of estimates, total number of SGC cases and level of bias present in each study, the industry/occupation/exposure groups were categorised as having a strong, moderate or limited level of evidence (LOE). Results were then visually synthesised in effect direction plots (figures1 2), where the top left quadrant (boxed in red) indicates the industry, occupation or exposure groups with the strongest LOE.

In the effect direction plots, the number of arrows in each cell (up to 3) symbolises the total number of SGC cases across each broad category of industry/occupation/exposure; the direction of the arrows indicates either: (arrows up)=elevated SGC occurrence observed across all subcategories OR (arrows facing away from one another)=mixed results, with positive associations observed for some subcategories and null or negative associations observed for others. We assessed the magnitude and precision of effect estimates, indicating where estimates were moderately precise (represented by superscripts) to highlight studies with more reliable estimates. Although effect direction plots often highlight statistical significance (ie, p value <0.05), we did not present this in our plot given p values are largely misinterpreted in non-randomised studies.13 14 Studies were also grouped according to high (beige), medium (yellow) and low (green) levels of bias to identify the most valid studies.

In figure 1, an industry/occupation group was assigned to the ‘strong LOE’ category if two or more studies reported a positive association with SGC for that group (with no to moderate bias present for at least two of those studies and with at least one of those studies reporting n>20 total SGC cases). A ‘moderate LOE’ was assigned for groups where at least two studies reported a positive association (and with at least one of those studies having minimal to moderate bias). The ‘limited LOE’ category includes industry/occupation groups where only one study (with no to minimal bias present) reported a positive association with SGC.

In figure 2, exposure groups were assigned to the ‘strong LOE’ category if at least one study (with no to minimal bias and n>20 total SGC cases) reported a positive association with SGC and no other studies showed conflicting evidence for that exposure group. A ‘moderate LOE’ was assigned for exposure groups where at least one study reported a positive association, but other studies showed mixed evidence or had moderate to high levels of bias. The ‘limited LOE’ category includes industry/occupation groups where one study with low bias reported mixed associations, or one study with moderate to high bias reported a positive association with SGC.

Results

After all exclusions were applied, a total of 24 studies were included in table 2.515,37 There were 12 cohort studies, nine case–control studies, two ecological studies and one case report. Most studies were conducted in the USA, but study locations ranged from Australia to Canada, China, Denmark, France, Germany, Republic of Korea and the UK. Studies more frequently measured cancer incidence than cancer mortality, and incidence studies covered more recent time periods.

Confounding, exposure misclassification and healthy worker bias were common concerns. Studies with high compared with low levels of bias had a higher frequency of mixed results across broad industry/occupation categories (figures1 2). 11 studies had no to minimal concerns for confounding1617 19 20 24 26 29 32,35; nine for exposure misclassification bias1517 19 22 24 29,32; and nine for healthy worker bias.516 17 20 24 26 27 29 33,35

Exposure classification methods varied across studies, with only a small portion of studies using job exposure matrices (n=3 studies) and under half of the studies conducting work/exposure history questionnaires or interviews as part of their exposure assessment (n=8 studies). Only one study specified that trained professionals (eg, retired nurses) with occupational health experience administered the questionnaires.26

Online supplemental table S4 presents the comprehensive results extracted from all studies reporting on SGC among workers employed in specific industries and occupations. Key results are described below, as well as visually synthesised in an effect direction plot (figure 1). The industry and/or occupation categories with the strongest LOE were: cleaning services (eg, ‘private household cleaners’: OR=1.7, 95% CI 1.04 to 2.85),24 material handling (eg, ‘material handling equipment operator’: OR=5.05, 95% CI 1.71 to 14.84),17 food services (eg, ‘Cooks’: OR=2.04, 95% CI 0.79 to 5.27),17 rubber and plastics production (‘Rubber and Plastics Products Industries’: HR=1.34, 95% CI 0.91 to 1.97),38 engineering and architecture (eg, ‘Engineering, architectural, and surveying services’: OR=2.2, 95% CI 1.09 to 4.26)24 and construction and painting (eg, ‘Construction’: OR=1.35, 95% CI 0.71 to 2.57).17

Industries and/or occupations with a moderate LOE included agriculture (eg, ‘Agricultural Production: Crops’: OR=4.6, 95% CI 1.18 to 17.68)24; transportation (eg, ‘Trucking service’: OR=2.8, 95% CI 0.85 to 9.04)24; hairdressing (‘Hairdressers’: OR=2.7, 95% CI 1.1 to 6.5)33; metalwork and welding (eg, ‘Welders and flame-cutters’: OR=4.55, 95% CI 1.86 to 11.12); and ‘sheet metal workers’ (OR=2.89, 95% CI 1.01 to 8.32).17 Two industry/occupation groups were classified as having a limited LOE, including Agent Orange-exposed veterans (adjusted HR (aHR)=6.96, 95% CI 1.5 to 32.3; RR=1.07, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.14),18 19 plumbing (‘Plumbers and pipefitters’: OR=3.51, 95% CI 1.33 to 9.29),17 among others (eg, chemistry, theatre, etc).

Results were mixed across some industry and occupation categories. In one study, multiple education-related categories yielded positive associations with SGC (eg, ‘Colleges and universities’: OR=2.0, 95% CI 1.18 to 3.52, ‘Teachers: Elementary School’: OR=1.8, 95% CI 1.23 to 2.63, among others),24 whereas another study showed no clear association.17 Similar findings were observed in healthcare, air transport and assembly, public administration, sales and typing fields.17 24 33 In rare cases, reduced rates of SGC were observed across categories, such as for bookkeepers.17

Table 3 presents occupational exposure agents associated with strong to moderate LOE for an association with SGC, while online supplemental table S5 presents occupational and environmental exposure agents associated with limited and mixed LOE for an association with SGC. Key exposure results are presented in an effect direction plot (figure 2). Only four occupational studies and one environmental study presented more detailed analyses of intensity and duration of exposure.16 17 24 29 35 Exposures with the strongest LOE for an association with SGC were: silica dust (ORs=up to 3.1, 95% CI 1.1 to 8.9),17 26 cement dust (eg, ‘<10 years of exposure’: OR=2.61, 95% CI 1.29 to 5.2717 as well as ‘females living within 3 km radius of Portland cement plants’: standardised incidence ratio (SIR)=3.03, 95% CI 0.98 to 7.07),22 chlorinated solvents (‘>10 years of exposure’: OR=1.4, 95% CI 0.77 to 2.78),17 formaldehyde (‘Mid-high probability/mid-high intensity’: OR=1.6, 95% CI 1.30 to 2.00)24 and white spirits (‘>10 years exposure’: OR=1.17, 95% CI 0.65 to 2.08).17

Exposures with a moderate LOE include nickel compounds or alloys (‘ever-exposed’: OR=6.0, 95% CI 1.6 to 22.0),16 petroleum products, including solvents (ORs=up to 1.47, 95% CI 0.86 to 2.52)17 and herbicides/dioxin (aHR=6.96, 95% CI 1.5 to 32.3).19 Further, exposures with a limited LOE included hair products (‘ever-exposed’: OR=4.1, 95% CI 1.5 to 11.5),35 aflatoxins in animal feed (‘Employment on cattle farm’: standardised proportional incidence ratio=4.80, 95% CI 1.52 to 11.57),28 dinitrotoluene (‘ever-exposed’: SIR=1.73, 95% CI 0.72 to 5.1630), refractory ceramic fibres (‘>10 years of exposure’: OR=1.85, 95% CI 0.71 to 4.80)17 and wood/straw as fuel (OR=1.6, 95% CI 0.6 to 4.4).26 For these exposure categories, elevated rates of SGC were reported in only one study, the effect estimates were imprecise and multiple studies had moderate to high bias.

Mixed results were reported for certain exposure agents, including asbestos, benzene and wood dust. Graham et al27 reported elevated rates among males and females living in asbestos-mining counties, whereas Radoï et al17 reported reduced rates among ever-exposed workers and workers with ≥10 years of exposure. For benzene, Wilson et al and Radoï et al both reported positive associations for some exposure groups, whereas Zheng et al reported no clear association.17 24 26 For wood dust, Zheng et al found a positive association with SGC in environmental settings (ie, emissions from wood and straw burning for cooking), but a negative association for occupational settings.26 Both studies had low case counts yielding imprecise estimates. Results were inconclusive for coal dust, use of coal for cooking and mineral wool, and negative associations were observed for oxygenated solvents, polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) and textile dust.17 26 29

Despite imprecise effect estimates, several environmental agents were associated with SGC, such as exposure to fuel emissions (eg, cooking with kerosene: OR=3.5, 95% CI 1.6 to 7.4) for males and females combined)26 and environmental exposure to asbestos based on residence in asbestos-mining counties (RR=2.11 (male); RR=3.35 (female); no CIs reported).27 Notably, as seen in occupational settings, environmental cement dust exposure was also associated with SGCs among women in one study (SIR=3.03, 95% CI 0.98 to 7.07); however, this trend was not observed among men.22 Environmental evidence for SGC is relatively limited, as existing studies had multiple methodological shortcomings, including lack of incorporation of environmental monitoring data, lack of control for confounding by occupation and use of broad geographical catchments as a proxy for exposure (eg, residence in asbestos-mining counties).

Discussion

Summary of evidence

This is the first systematic literature review to investigate occupational and environmental risk factors for SGC. Many studies captured by our review were flagged for key biases (eg, exposure misclassification, confounding) and lacked rigorous exposure assessment. To account for these limitations, we established criteria to weigh studies based on quality and validity. The hazards most strongly associated with SGC were silica dust, cement dust, chlorinated solvents, formaldehyde and white spirits. Specific industries and occupations were also implicated, including employment in cleaning services, material handling, food services, rubber/plastics production, engineering and construction/painting.

Silica dust

In a study conducted by Zheng et al, the odds of SGC among males ever exposed to silica dust were three times that observed among unexposed males. Radoï et al found 55% increased odds of SGC among ever-exposed relative to unexposed workers.17 26 These findings align with the elevated occurrence of SGC observed among workers in the construction and engineering industries, where silica is prevalent.39 40 Silica, asbestos and diesel exhaust are the most prevalent occupational carcinogens associated with trachea, bronchus and lung (TBL) cancers, which are distinct but closely related to SGCs.41 While evidence specific to salivary glands is lacking, silica dust is presumed to deposit in salivary glands in a similar manner to airways and promote an inflammatory response, subsequent cell necrosis and eventual carcinogenesis.42

Cement dust

As reported in table 3, cement dust was positively associated with SGC in occupational and environmental settings, although estimates were relatively imprecise.17 22 The construction industry, where cement dust exposure is common, was ranked as having a strong LOE in this review (figure 1; online supplemental table S4).43 Like silica dust, exposure to cement dust in occupational settings may occur primarily through inhalation (eg, nose, mouth breathing), with mouth breathing bypassing the nose’s natural filtration role. Cement dust may enter the oral cavity and pose a risk for minor salivary glands. The minor salivary glands are unencapsulated and sit beneath the epithelium, which makes them more vulnerable to carcinogens and localised inflammation.44 Cement dust exposure has also been associated with other head and neck cancers (HNCs), namely laryngeal cancer.45

Chlorinated solvents

Chlorinated solvents, such as trichloroethylene (TCE), are widely used in industries as effective degreasers, dry cleaning agents and chemical intermediates.46 Radoï et al reported a 51% increased rate of SGC among workers exposed to TCE for >10 years compared with unexposed workers.17 The primary route of exposure for TCE is inhalation and dermal contact, with industries such as manufacturing and plastics/rubber production having historically high exposures to TCE.46

Nickel compounds/alloys

Nickel compounds are classified as an IARC group 1 carcinogen.47 As reported in table 3, elevated rates of SGC were observed among workers ever compared with never exposed to nickel compounds (OR=6.0, 95% CI 1.6 to 22.0).16 Early evidence suggests that exposure to closely related nickel compounds and alloys in the rubber industry (eg, p-nitrosodimethylaniline, N,4-dinitroso-N-methylaniline) may lead to elevated occurrence of SGC.37 Other industrial groups with documented high nickel exposure are the following: (1) commercial and industrial machinery and equipment, (2) architectural and structural metal manufacturing, (3) automotive repair and maintenance and (4) building equipment contractor and machine shops,48 which overlap with industries and occupations connected to SGC in this review: ‘Material Handling’ (strong LOE), ‘Metalwork/Welding’ (moderate LOE) and ‘Plumbing’ (limited LOE).

Petroleum products and solvents, white spirits

Three studies report elevated rates of SGC among workers exposed to petroleum products and solvents.17 24 26 Radoï et al reported 32% increased odds of SGC among workers ever exposed to white spirits, 47% increased odds for workers exposed to at least one type of petroleum solvent for >10 years and 53% increased odds for those ever exposed to ‘diesel, fuels, and kerosene’, all compared against never-exposed workers.17 The study authors acknowledged that statistical imprecision in their job exposure matrix (JEM)-based estimates of exposure probability could have introduced non-differential misclassification bias, which generally results in an estimation of the risk biased towards the null for dichotomised exposures and a distortion of exposure-response trends.17 In this review, many of the industry/occupation categories ranked as having a strong or moderate LOE towards a relationship with SGC involve well-documented exposure to petroleum products and/or solvents, including but not limited to ‘Rubber/Plastics Production’, ‘Food Services’, ‘Material Handling’ and ‘Transportation’.49 50

Mechanistic evidence for SGC development through solvent exposure is lacking. However, for solvents to be excreted, they require activation through metabolism. The major salivary glands have found to carry similar metabolic enzymes (eg, cytochrome P450s like CYP1A2), which may result in the major salivary glands having a high risk of intense exposure to these toxic metabolites. Additionally, the role of the salivary glands is to release saliva and flush toxins from the body.51 Accordingly, it is hypothesised that salivary cells will concentrate and release these chemicals and metabolites into the oral cavity, leading to repeated high concentration exposures. Over time, this can result in chronic DNA damage and oxidative stress, which may result in cancer.

Cleaning services

Cleaning professionals represent another occupation exposed to many of the chemicals mentioned above in cleaners and degreasers. In our analysis, cleaning professionals (eg, cleaners, janitors) consistently exhibited a positive association with SGC.17 24 33 Radoï et al estimated an OR of 2.47 (95% CI 1.16 to 5.22) for ‘Charworkers, cleaners and related workers’ and 3.02 (95% CI 1.38 to 6.60) for ‘Charworker’, a relatively high level of risk among all occupation groups studied.17

Rubber and plastics production

This review identified multiple studies where workers in the rubber and plastics manufacturing industry,16 32 as well as those exposed to agents commonly found in this industry, such as solvents, nickel alloys and compounds, and N-nitrosamines, had an elevated occurrence of SGC (ORs ranging from 3.7 to 9.0).16 These results are in line with elevated rates of SGC reported among rubber/plastics workers in the Ontario Occupational Disease Surveillance System (pooled (n=27): HR=1.34, 95% CI 0.91 to 1.97; plastics only (n=21): HR=1.44, 95% CI 0.93 to 2.22).38

Earlier studies have pointed to the role of N-nitrosamines in SGC development.16 52 Pan et al discussed the potential association between N-nitroso compounds (NOCs) and SGC and the role of NOCs in tumour development in the parotid gland among mice, but their discussions were limited to nutritional exposures.5 While it is difficult to attribute cancer cases to a specific group of exposure agents within the industry, researchers have highlighted the presence of several known and suspected carcinogens, such as a styrene-butadiene mixture.38 Workers in facilities manufacturing styrene-butadiene rubber reported an increase in TBL cancers but evidence specific to SGCs is limited.53 Other chemicals associated with rubber manufacturing, such as N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone, may be of concern, but knowledge of their carcinogenicity is limited.54

Hairdressing and formaldehyde

Multiple studies identified a positive association between hairdressing and SGC, with potential ties to traditional hair dye products containing formaldehyde and ortho-anisidine.33,35 In a recent Brazilian study, researchers analysed 23 557 cancer deaths among hairdressers between 1996 and 2020 using the Brazilian Mortality Information System.55 The SGC mortality rate for female hairdressers was modestly elevated at 1.45 (95% CI 0.51 to 4.09) compared with other service workers, although this estimate is largely imprecise. This study is noteworthy given its size and the fact that results align with the previous findings on female hairdressers experiencing an increased odds for multiple cancers compared with their male counterparts.56

Formaldehyde and ortho-anisidine are suspect chemicals in hair dyes55 57 classified by IARC as group 1 and group 2A carcinogens, respectively.58 59 Formaldehyde has a well-known biological mechanism for cancer causation, with evidence of global DNA methylation changes shown through a biological monitoring study among hairdressers.57 Literature documenting the cancer risks from occupational exposures to ortho-anisidine is limited; one study analysing 10 male workers who were exposed to rubber industry emissions found an elevated risk for bladder cancer but not SGC.60 Diethanolamine (IARC group 2B), a chemical often found in cleaning and personal care products, is also of concern, but evidence remains insufficient for SGCs.59 Given remaining uncertainty, further research is needed to confirm the effects of specific hair dye chemicals on SGC risk.

Agriculture

Workers in the agricultural industry are often exposed to multiple carcinogens. Our results indicate that subgroups of farmers may be at an elevated risk of SGC, such as workers with high exposure to endotoxins, like aflatoxin.28 However, this study reporting a relationship between aflatoxin and SGC did not account for potential confounding factors (eg, smoking), which may have influenced findings.

Environmental exposures

Burning of biofuel and exposure to biofuel emissions were identified as potential risk factors for SGC in one study.26 In addition, environmental exposure to Portland cement dust, which contains crystalline silica and hexavalent chromium, has been associated with increased rates of SGC among women, supporting trends observed in occupational studies.22 However, existing evidence is limited, as only one study has been conducted on this topic. Graham et al identified elevated rates of SGC among people living in asbestos-mining counties relative to other rural counties; however, this study had multiple methodological issues, including a lack of environmental sampling data and a lack of control for confounding by occupation.27 Environmental monitoring data are needed to elucidate the environmental exposure pathway(s) for these hazards and to quantify the extent of risk associated with environmental exposures, specifically as levels tend to be lower than in occupational settings.

Strengths and weaknesses

This systematic review followed a rigorous, registered protocol, including the use of (1) the HAWC programme, (2) a bias assessment protocol used by IARC, (3) the SWiM checklist and (4) effect direction plots for evidence synthesis. Our methodology provides a holistic, transparent framework for synthesising studies on rare illnesses like SGC that may have a limited number of studies lacking quantitative exposure data.

The primary limitation of our review is the potential for exposure misclassification. To synthesise results across a wide range of industry and occupation categories, we grouped study results by broad industry/occupation categories based on common industry and occupation codes (eg, ISCO), where possible (online supplemental table S4). In cases where the industry/occupation code was not declared or not easily categorised, two occupational hygienists on our team reached consensus to finalise the grouping based on knowledge of shared job tasks and exposures. Therefore, our synthesis rankings could be impacted if exposure categories were commonly misclassified.

Challenges and recommendations

As highlighted throughout, the reviewed studies had multiple limitations, including low case numbers, a lack of occupational hygiene and environmental monitoring data and a lack of comprehensive work history information (eg, job title, task). Additional research involving larger cohorts with quantitative exposure data, as well as more harmonised cancer registries, is needed to further establish relationships between occupational and environmental exposures and often-overlooked rare cancers like SGC.

A plethora of existing studies focus on HNC broadly but do not present results for subgroups. For example, a recent study on a cohort of non-metal mining reported on mortality from ‘TBL’ cancer as opposed to reporting site-specific results (eg, for SGC alone), despite having access to finer level International Classification of Diseases codes via the American National Death Index.61 While it may be possible for the strong link found between metal manufacturing and TBL cancers to translate to SGCs, site-specific results are needed to assess this association. Often, the choice to present pooled results may be due to low case numbers observed for individual cancer types.

Existing literature also lacks histology data. SGC is a heterogenous group of cancers and biologically, adenoid cystic carcinoma, mucoepidermoid carcinoma, SGC, etc are all very different from each other. Thus, one would expect the occupational/environmental risk factors to vary based on the suspected histology. However, different histologies are grouped together and are not typically studied independently, diluting any histology-specific risk factor relationships that may exist.

In summary, future studies should present findings for rare cancers in the main text or supplementary material to prevent under-reporting. This is especially important because ‘rare’ cancers make up a large percentage of total cancer diagnoses. The Surveillance of Rare Cancers in Europe project defines rare cancers as those with an incidence rate of <6 cases per 100 000 population per year and has reported that rare cancers amount to 22% of all cancer diagnoses in Europe.62 The health burden of rare cancers is not negligible, and these cancers should continue to be monitored in population databases such as The Cancer Genome Atlas.63

Conclusion

This review illuminates emerging occupational and environmental risk factors for rare SGC, a life-altering disease. It is challenging to identify such risk factors due to limitations present in existing literature, including limited exposure data, low statistical power, under-reporting and grouping SGC cases with other HNCs. Despite these challenges, we found SGC was most consistently associated with the following industry and occupation categories: ‘cleaning services’, ‘material handling’, ‘food services’, ‘rubber/plastics production’, ‘engineering’ and ‘construction/painting’. Specific exposures implicated were silica dust, cement, chlorinated solvents, formaldehyde and white spirits. Prevention initiatives targeting the occupations and exposures highlighted by this review could protect workers and community members from SGC in the future.
