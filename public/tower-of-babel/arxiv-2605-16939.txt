Continuity properties of the Laguerre operator and its propagator
Smiljana Jakšić, Nenad Teofanov, Đorđe Vučković

Abstract
We study the well-posedness of a Cauchy problem associated with the general form of the Laguerre operator and relate it to the corresponding global problem for the harmonic oscillator. To this end, we carry out a detailed analysis of the continuity properties of the associated propagator. Furthermore, we establish connections between several integral transforms, including the fractional Fourier transform and the fractional Hankel transform. Our results highlight the role of Pilipović spaces on positive orthants when studying problems involving the Laguerre operator.

Introduction

The continuity properties of general forms of harmonic oscillator propagators,
are recently studied by Toft, Bhimani, and Manna in
[
19
]
.
They showed that certain classes of time–dependent equations are ill–posed in the framework of classical Gelfand–Shilov spaces, but well–posed in the framework of
suitable global Pilipović spaces (i.e. spaces on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
).
In this work, we study the Cauchy problem on the positive orthants for the Schrödinger type equation involving the
general form of the Laguerre operator.
We also reveal the connection
between radial elements from global Pilipović spaces and suitable spaces on positive orthants to show that our results
coincide with the ones from
[
19
]

when considering the radial solutions.

In addition, we establish connections between several integral transforms,
including the fractional Fourier transform and the fractional Hankel transform.
For example, let

denote
the Laguerre operator:

E=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}(x_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}})-\frac{x_{j}}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\Big)=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(x_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{x_{j}}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\Big),

(1)

,

, and let

where
\mathbf{e^{it}}=(e^{it},\dots,e^{it})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{e^{it}},0}

denotes the fractional power of the Hankel-Clifford transform, see (
32
).
This connection leads to the continuity of the fractional power of the Hankel-Clifford transform, Corollary
3.6
, and its extension to spaces of ultradistibutions, Remark
3.7
.
In contrast to
[
19
]
where the Bargmann transform is used
to prove the relation between the harmonic oscillator propagator and the fractional Fourier transform,
here we use a different approach based on kernel representations and suitable change of variables.

To give a flavor of our results, we proceed
with a particular conclusion included in Theorem
3.5
.
As a preparation, we introduce necessary notation,

The contents of the paper can be briefly summarized as follows.

We collect the necessary notions related to test function spaces and their distribution spaces in Section

We also prove a topological isomorphism between suitable spaces of radially symmetric ultradistributions; see Proposition

Section

In Section

\rho\neq 0

and

In Section

We conclude the paper with formulas that emerge from our investigations and that reveal connections between the propagator of the Laguerre operator and several fractional transforms. These links open up further possibilities for transferring various properties between these operators.

Notation

The sets of positive

,
\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}

\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,

The upper index

The standard multi-index notation is used: for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}

and

(or

x^{\alpha}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}...x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}

\partial^{\alpha}=\partial^{\alpha_{1}}/{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}}...\partial^{\alpha_{d}}/\partial x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}

The length of
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
is given by
\displaystyle|\alpha|=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}
.

The matrix
A=[a_{i,j}]_{1\leq i,j\leq d}

with real entries
is orthogonal if it is regular and additionally,

A^{T}\cdot A=E_{d\times d}
, where

A^{T}=[a_{j,i}]_{1\leq i,j\leq d}
, and

E_{d\times d}
denotes the identity matrix of order
d
. The space of all orthogonal matrices is denoted by
O(d)
. Since every

could be represented as a column-matrix, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}^{2}=x^{T}\cdot x
and therefore, for every

we infer

\sum_{j=1}^{d}y_{j}^{2}=(Ax)^{T}\cdot(Ax)=x^{T}(A^{T}\cdot A)x=x^{T}x=\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}^{2}.

(3)

In other words, orthogonality preserves the
2-
norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We write
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\preceq\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
when both sequences diverge to infinity, i.e
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{n}=\infty
and
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}=0
.

We also use the common notation for standard spaces of functions, distributions, and sequences.
For example,
\ell^{p}
,
p\geq 1
, denotes the Banach space of
p-
summable sequences, endowed with the usual norm:

\{a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}\in\ell^{p}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\|a_{n}\|_{\ell^{p}}=(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}|a_{n}|^{p})^{1/p}<\infty,

and
\displaystyle\{a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}\in\ell^{\infty}
if and only if
\|a_{n}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}|a_{n}|<\infty
.

A measurable function

f(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
,
belongs to the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
if

\|f\|_{L^{2}}:=\displaystyle(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}|f(x)|^{2}dx)^{1/2}<\infty,

and the scalar product is given in the usual way, i.e.

\displaystyle(f,g)=

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}f(x)\overline{g(x)}dx,

f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}).

By
{\cal S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d})
we denote the space of all smooth functions,
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
such that all of its derivatives
\partial^{\alpha}f
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
, extend to continuous functions on

\overline{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}
, and

\mu_{\alpha,\beta}\;:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}x^{\beta}|\partial^{\alpha}f(x)|<\infty,\qquad\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}.

The system of seminorms
\mu_{\alpha,\beta}
,

\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, endows

with the structure of Frechét space ((F)-space, for short), see e.g.
[
4
]
.
Its strong dual, the space of tempered distributions on orthant,
is denoted by
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}).

Let
I
be a directed set and let
\{X_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in I}
be a family of normed spaces.
By
\bigcup_{\alpha\in I}X_{\alpha}
we denote
the union of
\{X_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in I}
, endowed with the inductive limit topology, and by

\bigcap_{\alpha\in I}X_{\alpha}
we denote
the intersection of
\{X_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in I}
, endowed with
the projective limit topology.

A Fréchet space

, where
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are Banach spaces, is called a Fréchet–Schwartz space (
(FS)
-space) if the inclusions

are compact operators. On the other hand, if we have

Banach spaces and compact inclusions

then the space
X=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}X_{n}
is called dual Fréchet–Schwartz space
(
(DFS)
-space).

If
\mathcal{A}
is a space of test functions, then
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
denotes its dual space of (ultra)distributions, and
{}_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle

denotes the dual pairing.

2.3
Laguerre and Hermite functions and operators

For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\gamma\geq 0
, the
n
-th Laguerre
polynomial of order
\gamma
is defined by

L_{n}^{\gamma}(x)=\frac{x^{-\gamma}e^{x}}{n!}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}(e^{-x}x^{\gamma+n}),\quad x\geq 0,

and the
n
-th Laguerre function is defined by

l_{n}^{\gamma}(x)=L_{n}^{\gamma}(x)e^{-x/2}
,
x\geq 0
.
The
d
-dimensional Laguerre polynomials and Laguerre
functions are given by the corresponding tensor products:

L_{n}^{\gamma}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}L_{n_{j}}^{\gamma}(x_{k})\qquad\text{ and}\qquad l_{n}^{\gamma}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}l_{n_{j}}^{\gamma}(x_{k}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}.

(9)

We are interested in the case
\gamma=0
, and write
L_{n}
and
l_{n}
instead of
L_{n}^{0}
and
l_{n}^{0}
, respectively.

The Laguerre functions
\{l_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}
form an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
(see e.g.
[
21
]
). Thus every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
can be expressed via its Laguerre series expansion,
and by
a_{n}(f)

we denote the corresponding Laguerre coefficients:

a_{n}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}f(x)l_{n}(x)dx,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}.

Therefore,

f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}a_{n}(f)l_{n},\qquad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}),

where the equality holds in
L^{2}-
sense.

Recall, the Laguerre operator
E
is given by

E=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}(x_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}})-\frac{x_{j}}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\Big)=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(x_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{x_{j}}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\Big),

x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}.
The Laguerre functions are the eigenfunctions of the operator
E
:

E(l_{n}(x))=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{j}\Big)\cdot l_{n}(x)=|n|\cdot l_{n}(x),

see
[
2
, (11), p.188]
, and for
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
we have

E^{N}(l_{n}(x))=|n|^{N}l_{n}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}.

If
f,Ef\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
, by using the integration by parts we get

\langle Ef,l_{n}\rangle=\langle f,El_{n}\rangle=|n|\langle f,l_{n}\rangle=|n|\cdot a_{n}(f),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},

i.e. the operator
E
is self-adjoint.

The Hermite polynomials
H_{n}
and the corresponding Hermite functions
h_{n}
are defined by

H_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}e^{x^{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}(e^{-x^{2}}),\qquad h_{n}(x)=(2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi})^{-1/2}e^{-x^{2}/2}H_{n}(x),

(10)

x\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

respectively. The
d
-dimensional Hermite polynomials
H_{n}
and the Hermite functions
h_{n}
are given by the tensor products

H_{n}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}H_{n_{j}}(x_{j}),\quad\mbox{and}\quad h_{n}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}h_{n_{j}}(x_{j}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},

respectively.

The harmonic oscillator
(the Hermite operator)
H
is given by:

H=-\Delta+|x|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}_{j}}+x_{j}^{2}),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\Delta
denotes the Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then the Hermite functions are eigenfunctions of
H
:

H(h_{n}(x))=(2|n|+d)h_{n}(x),

see
[
18
, pp. 8]
, and

H^{N}(h_{n}(x))=(2|n|+d)^{N}h_{n}(x)\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},N\in\mathbb{N}.

The Hermite functions
\{h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}
form an orthonormal basis in

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, thus every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

can be expressed via its Hermite series expansion,

f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}a_{n}(f)h_{n},

where the equality holds in
L^{2}-
sense, and

a_{n}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)h_{n}(x)dx,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}.

The Hermite and Laguerre polynomials are related in the following way:

H_{2n}(x)=(-1)^{n}2^{2n}n!L_{n}^{-1/2}(x^{2}),\qquad H_{2n+1}(x)=(-1)^{n}2^{2n}n!L_{n}^{1/2}(x^{2})x,

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,
see (1.1.52) and (1.1.53) in
[
17
]
.

Lemma 2.1

Let

Proof.

Let

(
\mathcal{H}_{0,\alpha/2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Every even function

Throughout this section, we restrict our attention mainly to the case

Let us show that if in (

(

Lemma 2.7
.

Let there be given
\alpha>0
and

(

(
\mathcal{H}_{0,\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

Proof.

We will prove just the Roumieu case. Proof of the Beurling counterpart is similar.
We also observe that
\accentset{\sim}{g}
is obviously even.

Suppose that

By

the function
f
defined by

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).
Now we have

From
[

(ii)

\displaystyle f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}(f)\cdot l_{n},
such that

(18)

for some

(ii)

From Lemma

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}(f)(-1)^{n}l_{n}(x^{2}+y^{2})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}a_{n}(f)\cdot\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{n,k}h_{2k}(x_{1})h_{2n-2k}(x_{2})\right)

is an absolutely convergent series. Thus, we can rearrange the order of summation
to obtain

From the estimate (

wherefrom

Lemma 2.8

From

(20)

Lemma 2.11

3
The Laguerre operator and its propagator

We first introduce the general form of the Laguerre operator propagator, and then study its integral representation.
This reveals the close connection between the propagator and
the fractional powers of the Hankel-Clifford transform. In Subsection
3.2
we study its continuity properties in the context of test function spaces on positive orthants and their duals spaces.
In particular, we obtain Theorem
1.1

from the introduction as a special case of Theorem
3.5
.

Let
E
be the Laguerre operator given by (
1
).
We define the general form of the propagator
e^{zE}
,
z\in\mathbb{C}
, as the operator on the space of all formal series
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
as follows.
Let
\displaystyle f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}a_{n}(f)l_{n}\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
. Then

\displaystyle e^{zE}f\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
is the formal series

e^{zE}f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}e^{z|n|}a_{n}(f)l_{n},\quad\mbox{i.e.}\quad a_{n}\left(e^{zE}f\right)=e^{z|n|}\cdot a_{n}(f),\ n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}.

(23)

The injectivity of the mapping follows directly from its definition.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
and
\operatorname{Re}(z)\leq 0
. Since
\{a_{n}(f)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d})
and since
|e^{zn}\cdot a_{n}|\leq|a_{n}|
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, it follows that

e^{zE}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
, i.e. mapping
e^{zE}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
is well defined.

In particular, when
\operatorname{Re}(z)=0
, the operator
\displaystyle e^{itE}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, is unitary and
(e^{itE})^{-1}=e^{-itE}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
(cf.
[
15
, Section 3, p.527]
).
The operator
E
can therefore be interpreted as the infinitesimal generator of the unitary one-parameter group
(e^{itE})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
.

3.1
The Laguere operator propagator and the fractional power
of the Hankel-Clifford transform

Let us now suppose that
\operatorname{Re}(z)<0
.
Since
e^{zE}l_{n}=e^{z|n|}l_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, and
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\|e^{z|n|}l_{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}<\infty
when
\operatorname{Re}(z)<0
, it follows that
the operator
e^{zE}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt operator
[
10
, Prop. 16.8]
, and therefore admits an integral kernel,
[
10
, Prop 16.12]
.

We now make this statement precise.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
z=\operatorname{Re}(z)+i\operatorname{Im}(z)
,
\operatorname{Re}(z)<0
.
Then,
e^{zE}
is an integral operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
of the form

e^{zE}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}f(y)K_{z}(x,y)dy,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},

where

K_{z}(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{zk}\sum_{|n|=k}l_{n}(x)l_{n}(y),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}.

Proof.

By (
23
) it follows that

\displaystyle e^{zE}f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{zk}\sum_{|n|=k}a_{n}(f)l_{n}(x)

(24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{zk}\sum_{|n|=k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}f(y)l_{n}(x)l_{n}(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}f(y)\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{zk}\sum_{|n|=k}l_{n}(x)l_{n}(y)\Big)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}f(y)K_{z}(x,y)dy,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},

and the calculation is justified since the last integral absolutely converges in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
.
∎

To extend Lemma
3.1
to the integral representation of

when

\operatorname{Re}(z)=0
, we rewrite the kernel in terms of a modified Bessel function.
We follow the main ideas of the proof of
[
15
, Lemma 3.2]
,
making suitable modifications where necessary.
First, observe that

\displaystyle K_{z}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{zk}\sum_{|n|=k}\prod_{j=1}^{d}l_{n_{j}}(x_{j})l_{n_{j}}(y_{j})

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{n_{j}=0}^{\infty}l_{n_{j}}(x_{j})l_{n_{j}}(y_{j})e^{zn_{j}}\right),

n=(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},

x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}),

y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})

\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
,
since the series in (
25
) is absolutely convergent.

From
\operatorname{Re}(z)<0
, it follows that
|e^{z}|<1
,
and we can use the Hille–Hardy formula (see
[
2
, (20), p.189]
)
to write the

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}l_{n}(x)l_{n}(y)e^{zn}=(1-e^{z})^{-1}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(1+e^{z})(x+y)}{1-e^{z}}}I_{0}\Big(2\frac{(xye^{z})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{1-e^{z}}\Big),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

(26)

where
I_{0}
denotes the modified Bessel function of the first kind and the order zero, i.e.

I_{0}(\omega)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{2n}}{(n!)^{2}},\qquad\omega\in\mathbb{C},

cf.
[
2
, Chapter VII]
. Next we apply (
26
)
d
times,
for each respective series on the right hand side of (

K_{z}(x,y)=(1-e^{z})^{-d}\prod_{j=1}^{d}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1+e^{z}}{1-e^{z}}(x_{j}+y_{j})}\cdot I_{0}\Big(2\frac{(x_{j}y_{j}e^{z})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{1-e^{z}}\Big),

(27)

x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
,

y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
.

We now modify the result of Lemma
3.1
for the case

\operatorname{Re}(z)=0
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
z=it
,
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

\displaystyle e^{zE}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}f(y)K_{z}(x,y)dy

Proof.

The series on the right-hand side of (

is absolutely convergent, hence

and Abel’s theorem for power series yields:

\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}e^{z_{r}E}f=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}a_{n}(f)r^{|n|}e^{it|n|}l_{n}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}a_{n}(f)e^{it|n|}l_{n}=e^{itE}f.

(28)

Since
|e^{z_{r}}|=r<1
, equation
(\ref{expE})
holds for every

that is

\displaystyle e^{z_{r}E}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}f(y)K_{z_{r}}(x,y)dy,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}.

From

On the other hand, it is easy to verify that

is continuous.
Moreover, the transposed mappings

J^{\prime}_{\rho,c}:\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\;\text{ and }\;J^{\prime}_{\rho,c}:\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

are continuous as well.

Since we are interested in the dual counterpart of (
47
), we proceed below with
d=1
.

Let there be given
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. From (
53
)
and the fact that the operator
E_{\rho,c}
is self-adjoint,
we conclude that

e^{-itJ_{\rho,c}}=\mathcal{J}^{-1}\circ e^{-itE_{\rho,c}}\circ\mathcal{J}

on
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha/2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{H}_{0,\alpha/2}(\mathbb{R})
,
with the dual counterpart

e^{-itJ^{\prime}_{\rho,c}}=\mathcal{J^{\prime}}\circ e^{-itE_{\rho,c}}\circ\mathcal{(J^{\prime}})^{-1}

on
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\alpha/2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\alpha/2}(\mathbb{R})
.

The solution of the Cauchy problem (
43
) with the initial datum
f\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
is formally given by

u(t,\cdot)=e^{-itE_{\rho,c}}f
,
t\in[0,T]
.
Note that
g=\mathcal{J}^{\prime}f\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R})
.
Now let
v_{t}(\cdot)=v(t,\cdot)=e^{-itJ^{\prime}_{\rho,c}}\left(\mathcal{J}^{\prime}f\right)
,

t\in[0,T]
. Thus we obtain the family of generalized functions

v_{t}(\cdot)=v(t,\cdot)=e^{-itJ^{\prime}_{\rho,c}}\left(\mathcal{J}^{\prime}f\right)=\left(\mathcal{J^{\prime}}\circ e^{-itE_{\rho,c}}\right)f

wherefrom

(\mathcal{J^{\prime}})^{-1}v(t,\cdot)=e^{-itE_{\rho,c}}f=u(t,\cdot),

so that the family of ultradistributions
v_{t}(\cdot)=v(t,\cdot)=\mathcal{J}^{\prime}u(t,\cdot)

solves the initial value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}&i\partial_{t}v(t,r)-J^{\prime}_{\rho,c}v(t,\cdot)=0,\quad t\in[0,T]\\
&v(0,\cdot)=g,\end{array}\right.

(56)

Therefore the following is true.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
\alpha>0
,
\rho\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\},c\in\mathbb{C}
.
If the family of ultradistributions
u(t,\cdot)
solves the Cauchy problem (
43
) with the initial datum
f\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, (
f\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
) then the family of ultradistributions
v(t,\cdot)=\mathcal{J}^{\prime}u(t,\cdot)
solves the initial value problem (
56
) with initial datum
g=\mathcal{J}^{\prime}f\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R})
(
g=\mathcal{J}^{\prime}f\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R})
).

If, on the other hand, the family of ultradistributions
v(t,\cdot)
solves the Cauchy problem (
56
) with the initial datum
g\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R})
(
g\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R})
) then the family of ultradistributions
u(t,\cdot)=({\mathcal{J}^{\prime}})^{-1}v(t,\cdot)
solves the Cauchy problem (
43
) with the initial datum
f\in({\mathcal{J}^{\prime}})^{-1}g\in\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
(in the space
\mathcal{G}_{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}_{+}))
.

Finally, we present dual counterparts of Propositions
5.2
and
5.3
. In order to proceed,
we rewrite (
51
) in terms of
\mathcal{J}
and
\Phi
. Namely, for
\rho\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
c\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

(H_{\rho,c}\circ\Phi)g=\left(\mathcal{J}^{-1}\circ\Phi\circ E_{4\rho,c+\frac{1}{2}}\circ\mathcal{J}\right)g,\qquad g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),

so that

H_{\rho,c}=\mathcal{J}^{-1}\circ\Phi\circ E_{4\rho,c+\frac{1}{2}}\circ\mathcal{J}\circ\Phi^{-1}\quad\mbox{on}\quad\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}),\ \mathcal{G}_{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}),\alpha>0,

(57)

and

H_{\rho,c}=(\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\circ\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\circ E_{4\rho,c+\frac{1}{2}}\circ\Phi^{\prime}\circ(\mathcal{J^{\prime}})^{-1}\quad\mbox{on}\quad\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}),\ \mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}),\alpha>0.

Note that for
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
r>0
we have

\left((\mathcal{J}^{-1}\circ\Phi\right)f)(r)=(\mathcal{J}^{-1}f)(\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}})=f(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})=\left((\Phi\circ\mathcal{J}^{-1})f\right)(r)

i.e.

\mathcal{J}^{-1}\circ\Phi=\Phi\circ\mathcal{J}^{-1}\ \mbox{on}\ \mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}),\ \mathcal{G}_{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}),\alpha>0,

which also holds for the transpose mappings, and therefore

(\mathcal{J}^{\prime})^{-1}\circ\Phi^{\prime}=\Phi^{\prime}\circ(\mathcal{J})^{\prime-1}\ \mbox{on}\ \mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}),\ \mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}),\alpha>0.

(58)

Let us consider the problem (
43
) with initial datum
f\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
and the (formal)
solution
u(t,\cdot)=e^{-itH_{\rho,c}}f
,
t\in[0,T]
. It now follows that
g=\mathcal{J}^{\prime}f\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R})
.
From Proposition
2.13
and commutativity relation
(
58
)
we conclude that

\accentset{\sim}{g}=\left((\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\circ\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\right)f=\left(\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\circ(\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\right)f\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Let
w(t,\cdot)=e^{-itH_{\frac{\rho}{4},c-\frac{1}{2}}}\accentset{\sim}{g}
,

t\in[0,T]
.
Since

H_{\frac{\rho}{4},c-\frac{1}{2}}=((\mathcal{J}^{-1})^{\prime}\circ\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\circ E_{\rho,c}\circ((\mathcal{J}^{-1})^{\prime}\circ\Phi^{\prime})

we get

e^{-itH_{\frac{\rho}{4},c-\frac{1}{2}}}=(\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\circ\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\circ e^{-itE_{\rho,c}}\circ\Phi^{\prime}\circ(\mathcal{J^{\prime}})^{-1},

and therefore

\displaystyle w(t,\cdot)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-itH_{\frac{\rho}{4},c-\frac{1}{2}}}\accentset{\sim}{g}=\left((\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\circ\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\circ e^{-itE_{\rho,c}}\circ\Phi^{\prime}\circ(\mathcal{J^{\prime}})^{-1}\circ\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\circ(\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\right)f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left((\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\circ\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\circ e^{-itE_{\rho,c}}\right)f.

Finally we have

\left(\Phi^{\prime}\circ(\mathcal{J}^{\prime})^{-1}\right)w(t,\cdot)=\left((\mathcal{J}^{\prime})^{-1}\circ\Phi^{\prime}\right)w(t,x)=e^{-itH_{\rho,c}}f=u(t,\cdot),

which gives

w(t,\cdot)=\left(\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\circ(\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\right)u(t,\cdot)
.
The mappings
\Phi^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}
are bijections (moreover, topological isomorphisms) on suitable spaces, so we have the following
result concerning the dual spaces.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
\alpha>0
. If
u(t,\cdot)
solves the Cauchy problem (
43
) with initial datum
f\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
(
f\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
),
then the family of ultradistributions

w_{t}(\cdot)=w(t,\cdot)=\left(\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\circ(\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\right)u(t,\cdot),\qquad t\in[0,T],

solves the Cauchy problem (
55
)
with the initial datum
\accentset{\sim}{g}=\left(\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\circ(\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\right)f\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
(
\accentset{\sim}{g}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
).

If, on the other hand, the family
w_{t}(\cdot)
,
t\in[0,T]
,
solves the Cauchy problem (
50
) with initial datum

\accentset{\sim}{g}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

(
\accentset{\sim}{g}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0,\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
), then the family
u(t,\cdot)=(\Phi^{\prime}\circ(\mathcal{J}^{\prime})^{-1})w(t,\cdot)
solves the initial value problem
(
55
) with initial datum
f=(\Phi^{\prime}\circ(\mathcal{J}^{\prime})^{-1})\accentset{\sim}{g}\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
(
f\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}))
.

6
Relations between fractional transforms

Based on the results from previous sections, we are able to present a relation between certain fractional
integral transforms.
Let
\rho\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\},c\in\mathbb{C}
.
Note that

e^{-itJ_{\rho,c}}=\mathcal{J}^{-1}\circ e^{-itE_{\rho,c}}\circ\mathcal{J}

on
{\cal S}(\mathbb{R})
.
Thus,
for every even function
g
such that the function
f(x)=g(\sqrt{x})

admits Laguerre expansion of the form

f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}a_{n}(f)l_{n}

we yield

\left(e^{J_{\rho,c}}\right)g(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}a_{n}(f)e^{\rho n+c}l_{n}(x^{2}),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

When
\rho=1
and
c=0
, we set
e^{J_{1,0}}=e^{J}
.

Following the approach from
[
12
, Section 3, eq. (3.4)]
where the fractional Hankel transform

\mathscr{H}_{t}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
is defined
as follows:

\mathscr{H}_{t}g(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}(f)e^{int}l_{n}(x^{2})=\left(\left(\mathcal{J}^{-1}\circ e^{itE}\circ\mathcal{J}\right)g\right)(x),\qquad x\geq 0,

(59)

g\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
f=\mathcal{J}g
.
We remark that the fractional Hankel transform can
also be introduced in a different way, see for example
[
9
]
.

We proceed with the Roumieu case, and note that the same conclusions hold for the Beurling case.

For
g\in\mathcal{H}_{\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R})
we have
f=\mathcal{J}g\in\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and the following equalities hold:

\mathscr{H}_{t}g(x)=\left(e^{itJ}g\right)(x)=(e^{itE}f)(x^{2})=\left(\mathcal{I}_{e^{it},0}f\right)(x^{2}),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(60)

We note that when
g,\phi\in{\cal S}_{even}(\mathbb{R})
the following relation can be deduced by using (
59
) and the fact that

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}l_{n}(x^{2})l_{m}(x^{2})xdx=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{m,n}:

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(\mathscr{H}_{t}g)(x^{2})\cdot\phi(x^{2})xdx=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}g(x^{2})\cdot(\mathscr{H}_{t}\phi)(x^{2})xdx=\langle g,\mathscr{H}_{t}\phi\rangle.

(61)

We aim to extend (
61
) to the duality relation as follows.
If
g\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R})
,
then we define the ultradistribution
\mathscr{H}_{t}g
,
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
in the following way:

(\mathscr{H}_{t}g)(\varphi)=\langle g,\left(\mathcal{J}^{-1}\circ e^{-itE}\circ\mathcal{J}\right)\varphi)\rangle,\qquad\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R}),

i.e.

\mathscr{H}_{t}g=\left(\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\circ e^{-itE}\circ(\mathcal{J^{\prime}})^{-1}\right)g.

Let
f=\mathcal{(J^{\prime}})^{-1}(g)\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}).

Then, by using
(
39
) we obtain

\mathscr{H}_{t}g=\left(\mathcal{J^{\prime}}\circ\mathcal{I}_{e^{it},0}\right)(f)

(62)

which is the dual counterpart of (
60
).

We proceed by establishing connections between
the Laguerre operator propagator, the fractional Fourier transform
and the fractional power of Hankel-Clifford transform.

Let now
\rho\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
c=0
. We utilize the relation between the propagator of harmonic oscillator
and the fractional Fourier transform on
\mathbb{R}^{d}:

e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}H_{\rho,0}}=e^{-i\frac{\pi\rho d}{4}}{\mathcal{F}}_{\rho}

given in
[
19
, Thm. 3.2]
, to obtain the following interpretations.

Let
d=2
, and
\accentset{\sim}{g}\in\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},radial}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
\alpha>0
.
From Proposition
2.9
we have
g=\Phi^{-1}\accentset{\sim}{g}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha/2,even}(\mathbb{R}).

Moreover, the function
f
defined by
f=\mathcal{J}g=\mathcal{J}\circ\Phi^{-1}\accentset{\sim}{g}
is in
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
From (
48
) we yield

\displaystyle\left(e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}H_{\rho,0}}\right)\accentset{\sim}{g}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}J_{4\rho,\frac{1}{2}}}g\right)(\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}})

(63)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}E_{4\rho,\frac{1}{2}}}f\right)(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}).

We will also use the identity

e^{-itJ_{\rho,c}}=\mathcal{J}^{-1}\circ e^{-it\rho E_{\rho,c}}\circ\mathcal{J}=e^{-itc}\cdot(\mathcal{J}^{-1}\circ e^{-itE_{\rho,0}}\circ\mathcal{J}),t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},c\in\mathbb{C}.

(64)

Finally, from (
63
), (
64
) and the definitions of fractional Hankel transform, fractional powers of the Hankel–Clifford transform, and the fractional Fourier transform, we have the following relation:

\displaystyle({\mathcal{F}}_{\rho}\accentset{\sim}{g})(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\frac{\pi\cdot 2\rho}{4}}\cdot\left(e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}H_{\rho,0}}\accentset{\sim}{g}\right)(x_{1},x_{2})

(65)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\frac{\rho\cdot\pi}{2}}\cdot\left(e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}E_{4\rho,\frac{1}{2}}}(\mathcal{J}\circ\Phi^{-1}\accentset{\sim}{g})\right)(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}(2\rho-\frac{1}{2})}\left(\mathscr{H}_{-\rho\pi}(\Phi^{-1}\accentset{\sim}{g})\right)(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}(2\rho-\frac{1}{2})}\left(e^{-i\rho\pi E}(\mathcal{J}\circ\Phi^{-1}\accentset{\sim}{g})\right)(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}(2\rho-\frac{1}{2})}\left(\mathcal{I}_{e^{-i\rho\pi},0}(\mathcal{J}\circ\Phi^{-1}\accentset{\sim}{g})\right)(x_{1},x_{2}),

where
\accentset{\sim}{g}\in\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},radial}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
\alpha>0
.

By the continuity of
e^{-i\rho\pi E}
, see Theorem
3.5
,
it follows that all of the above mentioned transforms are continuous on their respective domains. We conlcude the paper with the dual counterpart of (
65
).

Let
\accentset{\sim}{g}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},radial}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Then
g=\Phi^{\prime}\accentset{\sim}{g}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},even}(\mathbb{R})
,
f=\mathcal{(J^{\prime}})^{-1}(g)\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
, and

\left(e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}H_{\rho,0}}\right)\accentset{\sim}{g}=\left((\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\circ\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\circ e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}E_{4\rho,\frac{1}{2}}}\circ\Phi^{\prime}\circ(\mathcal{J^{\prime}})^{-1}\right)\accentset{\sim}{g}.

From (
58
) we have

\left(\Phi^{\prime}\circ(\mathcal{J^{\prime}})^{-1}\right)\accentset{\sim}{g}=\left((\mathcal{J^{\prime}})^{-1}\circ\Phi^{\prime}\right)\accentset{\sim}{g}=f

and therefore

{\mathcal{F}}_{\rho}\accentset{\sim}{g}=e^{i\frac{\pi\cdot 2\rho}{4}}\cdot e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}H_{\rho,0}}\accentset{\sim}{g}=e^{i\frac{\pi}{8}(4\rho-1)}\cdot\left((\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\circ\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{I}_{e^{-i\rho\pi},0}\right)f.

From (
62
) we have that
\mathscr{H}_{-\rho\pi}g=\left(\mathcal{J^{\prime}}\circ\mathcal{I}_{e^{-\rho\pi i},0}\right)(f)

and, by using (
58
) once again,
we obtain

{\mathcal{F}}_{\rho}\accentset{\sim}{g}=e^{i\frac{\pi}{8}(4\rho-1)}\cdot\left((\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\circ\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{I}_{e^{-i\rho\pi},0}\right)f=e^{i\frac{\pi}{8}(4\rho-1)}\cdot\left((\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}\circ\mathscr{H}_{-\rho\pi}\right)(g).
