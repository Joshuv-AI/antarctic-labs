Robust Deep FOSLS for Transmission Problems
Alejandro Duque-Salazar, Paulina Sepúlveda, Carlos Uriarte, Jamie M. Taylor, David Pardo

Abstract
This work presents a robust, energy-based deep learning framework for solving transmission problems in heterogeneous media, including cases with discontinuous material scenarios. We introduce a weighted First-Order System Least-Squares (FOSLS) formulation involving an energy-norm Poincaré constant and prove its equivalence to a natural energy norm of the underlying equations, with constants independent of material parameters. As a result, the optimization landscape remains aligned with a meaningful error approximation even under high material contrast, where standard neural network losses often deteriorate. We further prove that the FOSLS formulation, together with its integral-loss representation, exhibits a passive variance reduction property, whereby the gradient variance progressively decreases as the loss diminishes, in contrast to methods such as VPINNs and Deep Ritz. From a numerical standpoint, we adopt a reduced-order perspective by constructing a low-dimensional space described by a neural network. The optimal coefficients are computed via a least-squares solver, and the space is subsequently improved through gradient-based updates. By selecting the activation function ReQU \operatorname{ReQU} , the method mitigates the spurious overshoots typically observed in smooth networks when approximating discontinuities. Numerical experiments in 1D and 2D interface settings corroborate these findings.



1
Introduction

Transmission problems in heterogeneous media occur in many applications, including heat conduction and wave propagation. Partial differential equations (PDEs) describe these phenomena, and their coefficients encode media properties. Where materials are discontinuous, coefficients vary and reduce PDE solution regularity, creating challenges for numerical methods
[
11
]
. Capturing the solution accurately near material interfaces is essential. Discontinuous material coefficients may cause jumps in the gradient or tangential flux component, leading standard numerical approaches to spurious oscillations or accuracy loss
[
22
]
. These challenges make alternative methods necessary to solve such PDEs accurately.

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)
[
31
,
24
,
12
]
approximate the solution of the PDE via a neural network by minimizing a loss function that inherently relies on the strong form of the equation. However, this strong formulation imposes restrictive regularity requirements on the target solution. In problems with discontinuous coefficients, the residual can exhibit Dirac delta-type singularities, violating the
L^{2}
-regularity assumption of the residual and making the strong form unsuitable
[
36
]
. Consequently, these problems naturally motivate the use of weak or variational formulations.

A mathematically natural way to relax these regularity requirements is to move from strong to weak formulations based on variational principles. This has motivated the development of Variational Physics-Informed Neural Networks (VPINNs)
[
25
,
7
,
33
]
and Deep Ritz methods
[
18
,
40
]
, which seek approximate solutions by minimizing functionals arising from weak formulations of the PDE. These approaches are attractive because they are consistent with classical finite element theory and naturally accommodate discontinuous coefficients and weak solutions. However, a major practical challenge remains in optimizing the resulting ariational integrals: existing VPINNs and Deep Ritz formulations lack a
passive variance reduction
property during training. In practice, the variance of the stochastic loss gradient often remains large even as the approximation improves, since quadrature noise is not inherently coupled to the PDE error. This can affect convergence unless an excessively large number of quadrature points are used for approximating the gradient.

To overcome these challenges, we turn to the First-Order System Least Squares (FOSLS) methodology, which reformulates the second-order PDE as an equivalent system of first-order equations
[
13
,
10
,
28
]
.
As a result, each equation contributes an
L^{2}
-residual, avoiding the need to evaluate higher derivatives of the solution. Its components remain in their natural spaces
H^{1}
for the primal variable and
H(\Div)
for the flux.
From a physical standpoint, the first-order formulation is especially meaningful because it treats the primary variable and its associated flux as independent functions. This separation aligns with fundamental physics laws such as Fourier’s law of heat conduction
[
20
]
or Darcy’s law in porous media
[
9
]
, where the flux represents a measurable physical quantity. Furthermore, the first-order system makes conservation principles explicit through balance equations, providing a more transparent representation of the underlying physics. In heterogeneous and composite materials, this formulation naturally accommodates discontinuities in material properties and allows for a physically consistent treatment of interface conditions across material boundaries
[
4
,
5
]
. Another advantage of the FOSLS formulation is that it exhibits a passive variance reduction property: as the neural approximation approaches the exact solution, the variance in the stochastic approximation of the gradient of the loss naturally decreases, which accelerates stochastic gradient descent-based optimization.

While recent “Deep FOSLS” methods have explored this approach
[
14
,
8
,
27
,
29
,
2
,
17
]
, a limitation remains: the equivalence constants relating the loss functional to the true error are influenced by physical coefficients, which may reduce accuracy in problems with high material contrast: the loss and the true error effectively decouple, so that a small loss no longer guarantees an accurate solution. To overcome this, we introduce a physically meaningful energy norm for the solution and its flux, along with a loss functional for the residual that is both physically motivated and mathematically balanced through the use of the energy-norm Poincaré constant. As a result, we prove that the equivalence constants between the error and the loss become independent of the physical coefficients, ensuring robust convergence even in the presence of extreme parameter discontinuities.

Our method proposes a hybrid solver-in-the-loop strategy that employs a vector-valued neural network as an adaptive generator of low-dimensional approximation spaces in the spirit of reduced-order modeling
[
30
]
. The optimal solution is computed on these spaces via a linear least-squares solver, while the spaces themselves are updated using gradient-based optimization. That is, we aim to find a low-dimensional subspace so that its least-squares minimizer —which may be readily computed— is an accurate solution to our PDE.

Discrete neural approximations of PDE solutions may suffer from the quasi-Gibbs phenomenon, namely, localized oscillations that appear near discontinuities in the trained approximation when globally smooth neural networks attempt to represent non-smooth solutions through extremely steep transition layers
[
22
,
15
]
. Although the network remains smooth, these narrow layers can produce spurious overshoots or undershoots in the solution or its gradient near material interfaces, an effect that becomes particularly pronounced in high-contrast transmission problems and when highly regular activation functions such as
\tanh
are employed. Several approaches have been proposed to alleviate this behavior. Adaptive sampling and integration-based strategies concentrate collocation points in regions with sharp variations, but they do not modify the intrinsic approximation space of the network and may therefore remain susceptible to quasi-Gibbs oscillations
[
21
]
.
Other strategies rely on localized refinement or material-dependent activations, mimicking adaptive finite-element techniques, yet they often require additional error estimators and may compromise the scalability of neural network approaches
[
35
,
3
,
34
]
. In contrast, our work addresses quasi-Gibbs behavior by choosing
\operatorname{ReQU}
as the activation function. This choice is informed by approximation results showing that ReLU networks can represent discontinuities without exhibiting Gibbs-type oscillations
[
15
,
16
]
. In the one-dimensional, single-hidden-layer case, this property admits an equivalent interpretation in terms of approximating jump discontinuities in the derivative. Guided by this observation, we improve the approximation of transmission problems without resorting to domain decomposition or local refinement strategies.

Despite the advantages, the current approach presents several challenges. First, the formulation introduces a new vector variable, increasing the number of unknowns. In practice, however, this extra cost is moderate in neural network settings, since the solution is represented through low-dimensional parametric representations.
Second, we have not explored the use of different neural networks for the solution and the flux, and since these variables belong to different functional spaces, they may exhibit different approximation requirements near discontinuities. We leave the possibility of employing multiple networks for future work.
Finally, other architectural choices, hyperparameter settings, and the hybrid use of different losses for updating the spaces remain to be studied. Addressing these aspects represents a natural direction for future research.

2
Problem Statement

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for
d\in\{1,2,3\}
, be a bounded domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
. We consider the diffusion equation with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions given by the following formulation: find
u
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}-\Div(\kappa\nabla u)&=&f&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&=&0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

(1)

where
\kappa\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
satisfies
\kappa\geq\kappa_{0}>0
in
\Omega
and
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Remark 1
.

In the case of inhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, let
{u}
denote the corresponding solution. Introducing a lift
u_{D}
of the boundary data, i.e., a sufficiently regular function coinciding with the prescribed values on
\partial\Omega
, the decomposition
u-u_{D}
reduces the problem to the homogeneous form (
1
), with modified source term.

Let
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega):=\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega):\nabla u\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{d},u|_{\partial\Omega}=0\}
. The weak formulation of problem (
1
) reads as: find
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
such that

b(u,v):=\int_{\Omega}\kappa\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,=\int_{\Omega}fv=:\ell(v),\qquad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

(2)

The Lax-Milgram theorem
[
19
]
guarantees the existence and uniqueness of the solution of (
2
). Since the bilinear form
b
is symmetric and positive definite, it induces an
energy-type
inner-product and norm in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
defined as

(u,v)_{H^{1}_{0,\kappa}}:=b(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}\kappa\nabla u\cdot\nabla v,\qquad\|u\|_{H^{1}_{0,\kappa}}^{2}:=b(u,u)=\int_{\Omega}\kappa|\nabla u|^{2},\qquad u,v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

(3)

This norm is more physically and mathematically meaningful than the standard
\kappa
-independent norms in
H^{1}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, namely,

\displaystyle\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}:=(\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\cdot\|_{L^{2}}^{2})^{1/2},\qquad\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}_{0}}:=\|\nabla\cdot\|_{L^{2}}.

For instance, when Eq. (
1
) models the steady heat equation, where
u
denotes the temperature field and
\kappa
the thermal conductivity, the energy norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}_{0,\kappa}}
represents the total energy dissipated by heat conduction in maintaining the steady state. In this sense, it plays an analogous role to mechanical work in continuum mechanics, where the relevant energy norm quantifies the work required to sustain a deformation.

2.1
First-Order System Least Squares (FOSLS)

Let
\boldsymbol{q}:=-\kappa\nabla u
denote the flux. The diffusion problem (
1
) can be equivalently rewritten as the first-order system of equations

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\Div\boldsymbol{q}&=&f,&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa\nabla u&=&0,&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&=&0,&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(4)

Since
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, the search spaces are naturally given by

u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\qquad\boldsymbol{q}\in H(\Div;\Omega):=\{\boldsymbol{q}\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{d}:\Div\boldsymbol{q}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}.

For any
(u,\boldsymbol{q})
in the product space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times H(\Div;\Omega)
, the mathematical norm is often given by (see, e.g.,
[
13
]
)

\|(u,\boldsymbol{q})\|_{H^{1}_{0}\times H(\Div)}:=\left(\|u\|^{2}_{H_{0}^{1}}+\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_{(L^{2})^{d}}^{2}+\|\Div\boldsymbol{q}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(5)

However, this norm has two limitations. First, because
it does not incorporate the energy norm introduced in Eq. (
3
). Second, for the exact solution
(u^{*},\boldsymbol{q}^{*})
of (
4
),
the equivalence constants between
\|u^{*}\|_{H_{0}^{1}}
and
\|\boldsymbol{q}^{*}\|_{L^{2}}
are
\kappa
-dependent and possibly large.

To overcome this, we introduce the following weighted norm in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times H(\Div;\Omega)
:

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(u,\boldsymbol{q})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\kappa}:=\left(\|u\|^{2}_{H_{0,\kappa}^{1}}+\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_{(L_{\kappa}^{2})^{d}}^{2}+C\|\Div\boldsymbol{q}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

(6)

where
C>0
is a weighting coefficient that we will determine,
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}_{0,\kappa}}
is the energy norm defined in (
3
), and
\|\cdot\|_{(L_{\kappa}^{2})^{d}}
is a weighted
L^{2}
-norm for the flux defined by

\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_{(L_{\kappa}^{2})^{d}}:=\|\kappa^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{q}\|_{(L^{2})^{d}}.

2.2
Continuous loss functional

We propose the following weighted functional associated with the first-order system given by Eq. (
4
):

\mathcal{L}(u,\boldsymbol{q}):=\|\kappa^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{1/2}\nabla u\|^{2}_{(L^{2})^{d}}+C_{\mathcal{L}}\|\Div\boldsymbol{q}-f\|^{2}_{L^{2}},

(7)

for some constant
C_{\mathcal{L}}>0
to be determined. For
C_{\mathcal{L}}=1
, this reduces to the standard loss proposed in
[
13
,
14
]
, where the authors show that it is equivalent to the standard norm given by Eq. (
5
), with
\kappa
-dependent equivalence constants.

Herein, we will show an equivalence of the loss in Eq. (
7
) with the error measured by the
physically meaningful
norm in Eq. (
6
), and choose
C
and
C_{\mathcal{L}}
to ensure that the upper- and lower-bound constants are
\kappa
-independent, therefore leading to a robust method. We state and prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
C>0
and
C_{\mathcal{L}}>0
be the constants defined in (
6
) and (
7
), respectively. Then, the loss function in Eq. (
7
) satisfies:

c_{1}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(u,\boldsymbol{q})-(u^{*},\boldsymbol{q}^{*})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\kappa}^{2}\leq\mathcal{L}(u,\boldsymbol{q})\leq c_{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(u,\boldsymbol{q})-(u^{*},\boldsymbol{q}^{*})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\kappa}^{2},

with

c_{1}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{8},\frac{C_{\mathcal{L}}}{6(C^{P}_{\kappa})^{2}+{C}}\right\},\qquad c_{2}=2\max\left\{1,\frac{C_{\mathcal{L}}}{2C}\right\},

where
C_{\kappa}^{P}
is the energy-norm Poincaré constant
defined as:

C_{\kappa}^{P}:=\sup_{0\neq v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\frac{\|v\|_{L^{2}}}{\|v\|_{H_{0,\kappa}^{1}}}.

Proof.

We first establish the upper bound. Applying Young’s inequality together with the definition of the weighted norm, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u,\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\kappa^{1/2}\nabla u-\kappa^{1/2}\nabla u^{*}\|_{(L^{2})^{d}}^{2}+2\|\kappa^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{1/2}\nabla u^{*}\|_{(L^{2})^{d}}^{2}+C_{\mathcal{L}}\|\Div\boldsymbol{q}-f\|^{2}_{L^{2}},

\displaystyle=2\|u-u^{*}\|_{H^{1}_{0,\kappa}}^{2}+2\|\kappa^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{1/2}\nabla u^{*}\|_{(L^{2})^{d}}^{2}+C_{\mathcal{L}}\|\Div\boldsymbol{q}-f\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

The exact solution satisfies
\kappa^{1/2}\nabla u^{*}=-\kappa^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{q}^{*}
and
\Div\boldsymbol{q}^{*}=f
. Thus,

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(u,\boldsymbol{q})&\leq 2\|u-u^{*}\|_{H^{1}_{0,\kappa}}^{2}+2\|\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{q}^{*}\|_{(L^{2}_{\kappa})^{d}}^{2}+C_{\mathcal{L}}\|\Div\boldsymbol{q}-\Div\boldsymbol{q}^{*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}},\\
&\leq 2\max\left\{1,\frac{C_{\mathcal{L}}}{2C}\right\}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(u,\boldsymbol{q})-(u^{*},\boldsymbol{q}^{*})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\kappa}^{2}.\end{split}

As a result, we consider
c_{2}=2\max\left\{1,\tfrac{C_{\mathcal{L}}}{2C}\right\}
.

Next, for computing
c_{1}
we consider bounds for each term of the energy norm of the error. First, we recall the equivalence between the energy norm of the error
u-u^{*}
and the norm of the residual
r_{u}\in H^{-1}(\Omega):=(H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))^{*}
defined by

r_{u}(v):=b(u,v)-\ell(v),\qquad v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

Specifically,

\|u-u^{*}\|_{H^{1}_{0,\kappa}}=\|r_{u}\|_{({H^{1}_{0,\kappa}})^{*}}=\sup\limits_{0\neq v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}{\frac{1}{\|v\|_{H^{1}_{0,\kappa}}}\int_{\Omega}\kappa\nabla u\cdot\nabla v-fv\,}.

(8)

We emphasize that we are equipping
H^{-1}(\Omega)
with the dual norm induced via the energy norm in (
3
). An estimate of this term can be obtained by bounding the last integral in (
8
). To this end, we add and subtract
\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\nabla v
and then integrate by parts. Then, for all
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,

\begin{split}\left(\int_{\Omega}\kappa\nabla u\cdot\nabla v-fv\,\right)^{2}&=\left(\int_{\Omega}(\kappa^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{1/2}\nabla u)\cdot\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla v-\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\nabla v-fv\,\right)^{2}\\
&=\left(\int_{\Omega}(\kappa^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{1/2}\nabla u)\cdot\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla v\,+\Div(\boldsymbol{q})v-fv\,\right)^{2}.\end{split}

Applying Young’s inequality, followed by Cauchy-Schwarz’s and the energy-norm Poincaré (
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}\leq C^{P}_{\kappa}\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}_{0,\kappa}}
) inequalities, we obtain: for all
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\Omega}\kappa\nabla u\cdot\nabla v-fv\,\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\int_{\Omega}(\kappa^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{1/2}\nabla u)\cdot\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla v\,\right)^{2}+2\left(\int_{\Omega}\Div(\boldsymbol{q})v-fv\,\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\kappa^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{1/2}\nabla u\|_{(L^{2})^{d}}^{2}\|v\|_{H^{1}_{0,\kappa}}^{2}+2\|\Div(\boldsymbol{q})-f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\|v\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\kappa^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{1/2}\nabla u\|_{(L^{2})^{d}}^{2}\|v\|_{H^{1}_{0,\kappa}}^{2}+2(C^{P}_{\kappa})^{2}\|\Div(\boldsymbol{q})-f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\|v\|_{H^{1}_{0,\kappa}}^{2}.

(9)

Using the upper bound (
2.2
) in (
8
), we arrive at

\|u-u^{*}\|_{H^{1}_{0,\kappa}}^{2}\leq 2\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla u\|_{(L^{2})^{d}}^{2}+2(C^{P}_{\kappa})^{2}\|\Div(\boldsymbol{q})-f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(10)

Analogously, we estimate the error of the flux in the corresponding weighted norm. Applying Young’s inequality together with (
10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{q}^{*}\|_{(L_{\kappa}^{2})^{d}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa\nabla u^{*}\|_{(L_{\kappa}^{2})^{d}}^{2},

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa\nabla u\|_{(L_{\kappa}^{2})^{d}}^{2}+2\|\kappa\nabla u-\kappa\nabla u^{*}\|_{(L_{\kappa}^{2})^{d}}^{2},

\displaystyle=2\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla u\|_{(L^{2})^{d}}^{2}+2\|u-u^{*}\|_{H^{1}_{0,\kappa}}^{2},

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla u\|_{(L^{2})^{d}}^{2}+4\left(\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla u\|_{(L^{2})^{d}}^{2}+(C^{P}_{\kappa})^{2}\|\Div(\boldsymbol{q})-f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle=6\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla u\|_{(L^{2})^{d}}^{2}+4(C^{P}_{\kappa})^{2}\|\Div(\boldsymbol{q})-f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(11)

Combining (
10
) and (
2.2
), the bound for the energy norm of the error reads as follows:

\begin{split}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(u,\boldsymbol{q})-(u^{*},\boldsymbol{q}^{*})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\kappa}^{2}&\leq 8\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+(6(C^{P}_{\kappa})^{2}+{C})\|\Div(\boldsymbol{q})-f\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\\
&\leq\max\left\{8,\frac{6(C^{P}_{\kappa})^{2}+C}{C_{\mathcal{L}}}\right\}\mathcal{L}(u,\boldsymbol{q}).\end{split}

Therefore, we consider:

c_{1}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{8},\frac{C_{\mathcal{L}}}{6(C^{P}_{\kappa})^{2}+C}\right\}.

∎

functions such that
\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
where the Neumann condition is imposed.

.

Let

) and (

c_{1}
and

in Theorem

are independent of
\Omega
and

. Moreover,
c_{1}=1/8
and
c_{2}=2
, so that
c_{2}/c_{1}=16
.

The corresponding norm and loss take the explicit form

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(u,\boldsymbol{q})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\kappa}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\|u\|^{2}_{H_{0,\kappa}^{1}}+\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_{(L_{\kappa}^{2})^{d}}^{2}+(C^{P}_{\kappa})^{2}\|\Div\boldsymbol{q}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big),

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u,\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\|\kappa^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa^{1/2}\nabla u\|^{2}_{(L^{2})^{d}}+2(C^{P}_{\kappa})^{2}\|\Div\boldsymbol{q}-f\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

.

From now on, we define the following norm in

as

\|\boldsymbol{q}\|^{2}_{H(\Div,\kappa)}:=\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_{(L_{\kappa}^{2})^{d}}^{2}+(C_{\kappa}^{P})^{2}\|\Div\boldsymbol{q}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

3
Discrete Subspace Approximation

We seek an approximation of the solution of the continuous problem in
finite-dimensional subspaces

V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}\times V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}\;\subset\;H_{0}^{1}\times H(\mathrm{div}).

Here,

V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}
and
V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{q}
are the discrete subspaces of
H^{1}_{0}
and
H(\operatorname{div})
where the approximations
u
and
\boldsymbol{q}
are correspondingly sought. The mapping
\boldsymbol{\theta}\mapsto V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}\times V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{q}
denotes a parametrization that defines this space.
The spaces
V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}
,
V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}
are defined via a given number of parametrised spanning functions,

V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}:=\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{u}},\qquad V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}:=\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{q}}.

Notice that for notational convenience we are omitting the dependence of these functions on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Elements
(u,\boldsymbol{q})\in V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}\times V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}
are therefore represented as

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}c_{i}^{u}\,{\varphi}_{i},\qquad\boldsymbol{q}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{q}}c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{q}}\,{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{i},

(14)

where we collect the coefficients into the vector

\boldsymbol{c}:=(\boldsymbol{c}^{u},\boldsymbol{c}^{\boldsymbol{q}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

n:=n_{u}+n_{q}.

3.1
Continuous loss over a discrete subspace

In terms of the coefficients
{\boldsymbol{c}}
, the quadratic loss of Eq. (
13
) can be written as

\mathcal{L}(u,\boldsymbol{q})={\boldsymbol{c}}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{c}}-2\,{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

(15)

where
\mathbf{H}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is symmetric and
positive semidefinite and with the following block decomposition

\mathbf{H}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{H}^{uu}&\mathbf{H}^{u\boldsymbol{q}}\\
(\mathbf{H}^{u\boldsymbol{q}})^{\top}&\mathbf{H}^{\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{q}}\end{pmatrix},

with blocks defined by

\displaystyle(\mathbf{H}^{uu})_{ij}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\kappa\,\nabla{\varphi}_{i}\cdot\nabla{\varphi}_{j},\qquad(\mathbf{H}^{u\boldsymbol{q}})_{ij}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla{\varphi}_{i}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{j},

(16)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{H}^{\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{q}})_{ij}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{-1}\,{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{i}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{j}+2\bigl(C_{\kappa}^{P}\bigr)^{2}\int_{\Omega}(\Div{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{i})(\Div{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{j}).

The linear and constant terms are given by

(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})_{j}=\begin{cases}0,\qquad 1\leq j\leq n_{u},\\
2\bigl(C_{\kappa}^{P}\bigr)^{2}\int_{\Omega}f\,\Div{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{j-n_{u}},\qquad n_{u}+1\leq j\leq n\end{cases},\qquad\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=2\bigl(C_{\kappa}^{P}\bigr)^{2}\int_{\Omega}f^{2}.

Least-Squares formulation

We are interested in minimizing the loss functional over the discrete spaces, i.e,

(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}):=\underset{(u,\boldsymbol{q})\in V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}\times V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\mathcal{L}(u,\boldsymbol{q}),

where
\mathcal{L}
denotes the least–squares functional in (
15
), and
(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
represents a function that minimizes it over
V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}\times V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}
.

Least-Squares solver

To gain stability for the least-squares solver, we apply a change of variables to the discrete system and introduce a simple diagonal scaling
based on the block entries of
\mathbf{H}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, which provides an effective
spectral normalization at negligible additional cost. For that, we define:

d_{i}:=\sqrt{(\mathbf{H}^{uu})_{ii}+\varepsilon}\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,n_{u},\qquad d_{n_{u}+j}:=\sqrt{(\mathbf{H}^{\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{q}})_{jj}+\varepsilon}\quad\text{for }j=1,\dots,n_{q},

and collect these values into a diagonal matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n}).

(17)

In the above, we add a small
\varepsilon>0

to stabilize the discrete system when
\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
becomes singular or near singular, for instance, when some spanning functions have negligible support inside
\Omega
.

Then, we perform the change of variables

\boldsymbol{c}:=\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}.

Then, (
15
) becomes:

\mathcal{L}(u,\boldsymbol{q})={\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}}-2\,{\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}}^{\top}{\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

(18)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}
, and
{\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{f}}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

Remark 3
.

The scaled matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is symmetric and
positive semidefinite, with nonnegative diagonal entries bounded by one.
Hence,

\lambda_{\max}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\leq n.

Since the dimension of the matrix is moderate, we can safely select a Tikhonov parameter
\mu>0
independent of the particular spanning set.

Thus:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}^{*}=\arg\min_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\mu\mathbf{I})\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}-2\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)=\left(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\mu\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

The physical coefficients associated with the original (non-normalized) spanning functions
are then recovered by

\boldsymbol{c}^{*}=\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}^{*},

and the corresponding solution
(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

is reconstructed using the spanning function expansion in (
14
).

3.2
Approximation of the energy-norm Poincaré constant

Our framework involves the Poincaré constant
C_{\kappa}^{P}
, whose exact value is generally unknown
a priori
. To estimate it, we consider its approximation over the finite-dimensional subspace
V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}
. Let

C_{\kappa,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{P}:=\max_{0\neq v\in V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}}\frac{\|v\|_{L^{2}}}{\|v\|_{H_{0,\kappa}^{1}}}\leq\sup_{0\neq v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\frac{\|v\|_{L^{2}}}{\|v\|_{H_{0,\kappa}^{1}}}=C_{\kappa}^{P}.

This constant is computed from the underlying eigenvalue problem associated to the second-order PDE: find
(u_{\lambda},\lambda)\in V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}\times\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\kappa\nabla u_{\lambda}\cdot\nabla v=\lambda\int_{\Omega}u_{\lambda}v,\qquad\forall v\in V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}.

Letting
\lambda_{\min,\boldsymbol{\theta}}
be the smallest positive eigenvalue, we then set
C_{\kappa,\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{P}=(\lambda_{\min,\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{-1/2}
.

The quality of the approximation is dependent on how well the subspace
V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}
approximates the first eigenfunction.
Expanding
u_{\lambda}
in terms of a chosen finite-dimensional set
\{{\varphi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{u}}
of
V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}
, the computation of
\lambda_{\min,\boldsymbol{\theta}}
reduces to solving the generalized eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{uu}\boldsymbol{a}=\lambda\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{a},

(19)

where
\boldsymbol{a}
is the coordinate vector of the eigenfunction

u_{\lambda}\in V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}
with respect to the spanning functions

\{{\varphi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{u}}
,
\mathbf{H}^{uu}
is defined as in Eq. (
16
) and

\mathbf{M}
denotes the mass matrix, i.e.,

\mathbf{M}_{ij}:=\int_{\Omega}{\varphi}_{i}{\varphi}_{j},\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq n_{u}.

Both matrices are symmetric and positive semidefinite. In particular,
\mathbf{M}
may fail to be positive definite if the spanning functions are linearly dependent in
L^{2}(\Omega)
or have negligible or vanishing support within
\Omega
.

As in the least-squares solver, we perform a change of variables and regularization for numerical stability: we first perform a change of variables and then regularize the resulting generalized eigenvalue problem. Instead of solving Eq. (
19
), we consider

\displaystyle(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{uu}+\alpha_{1}\mathbf{I})\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}=\lambda\,(\tilde{\mathbf{M}}+\alpha_{2}\mathbf{I})\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}},

(20)

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}>0
are regularization parameters,

{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}}^{uu}:=\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},u}^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}^{uu}\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},u}^{-1}
,
{\tilde{\mathbf{M}}}:=\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},u}^{-1}{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},u}^{-1}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}:=\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},u}^{-1}\boldsymbol{a}
, and
\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},u}
correspond to the upper block of size
n_{u}
of the matrix
\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
defined in Eq. (
17
).

Remark 4
.

The parameter
\alpha_{2}>0
ensures that

\tilde{\mathbf{M}}+\alpha_{2}\mathbf{I}
is invertible and
\alpha_{1}>0
regularizes the stiffness matrix

\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{uu}
, which is generally only positive semidefinite and may have a null eigenvalue.
In practice, both parameters are chosen to be small and fixed, just large enough to obtain an accurate approximation of the smallest eigenvalue, which is the primary quantity of interest. Although the regularization perturbs the exact discrete eigenvalues,
standard eigenvalue perturbation theory implies that, for sufficiently
small
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
, the smallest eigenvalue of
(
20
) remains a consistent approximation of

\lambda_{\min,\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

Remark 5
.

In practice,
\mathbf{H}^{uu}
is assembled as part of the least–squares loss evaluation, since it depends only on the discrete space
V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}
. Because the loss itself involves the Poincaré constant, this computation is performed once per update of the spaces. The extra cost for solving the energy-norm Poincaré constant is limited to the solution of the small generalized eigenvalue problem, which remains inexpensive as long as the dimension of the parameter space is moderate.

3.3
Update of the parametric spaces

We define the loss functional

\mathcal{J}:\boldsymbol{\theta}\longmapsto\mathcal{L}(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}):=\min_{(u,\boldsymbol{q})\in V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}\times V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}}\mathcal{L}(u,\boldsymbol{q}),

(21)

which measures the optimal value of the functional attainable within the parametrized approximation spaces. The approximate solution

(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is sought within these spaces by minimizing
\mathcal{L}
as described in Section
3.1
.
The update of the space
V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}\times V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}
is then formulated as the following optimization problem:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

which aims to obtain optimal discrete spaces to approximate the solution.

Remark 6
.

The proposed formulation can be interpreted as a reduced–order modeling
strategy in which the discrete spaces are optimized through the minimization of a
variational residual, with linear dependence on the reduced coefficients and
nonlinear dependence on the space parametrization.

Remark 7
.

The framework involves two conceptually distinct minimization processes based on the same continuum loss functional. One may generalize these ideas by considering

(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{(u,\boldsymbol{q})\in V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\mathcal{L}_{1}(u,\boldsymbol{q}),

and the minimization of

\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{L}_{2}\bigl(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\bigr).

Notice that our framework corresponds to the particular choice
\mathcal{L}_{1}=\mathcal{L}_{2}=\mathcal{L}
.

Alternative definitions of
\mathcal{J}
could be adopted, for example based on
a mixture of PDE and data-driven objectives. Similarly,

\mathcal{L}
could be approximated using different discretization strategies, such as a finite element method over a mesh dependent on the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
(see, e.g.,
[
1
]
). This perspective suggests the possibility of introducing hybrid schemes employing different losses.

4
Neural Network-based Parametrization

We construct a finite-dimensional space

V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}\times V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}

using a feedforward neural network with
L
hidden layers, defined recursively as

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\sigma_{l}\!\left(\mathbf{W}_{l}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}(\boldsymbol{x})+\mathbf{b}_{l}\right),\quad 1\leq l\leq L,

where

\mathbf{W}_{l}
,
\mathbf{b}_{l}
are trainable parameters,
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l}=(\Phi_{l}^{(1)},\dots,\Phi_{l}^{(n_{l})})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}
and
\sigma_{l}\in C^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
is an activation function that acts component-wise. This regularity is essential for a consistent numerical evaluation of the gradient of the variational loss functional. In particular, activations with lower regularity, such as ReLU, lead to distributional derivatives with respect to the trainable parameters, introducing Dirac delta-type singularities that hinder a stable and consistent computation of gradients (see
[
37
, Section 2.2]
).

The output of the last hidden layer,

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{L}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{L}},\qquad\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{L}=(\Phi_{L}^{(1)},\dots,\Phi_{L}^{(n_{L})}),

is a vector-valued function, with each component
\Phi_{L}^{(i)}\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)
.
These functions provide a
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-dependent parametric spanning set of the form

\boldsymbol{\theta}:=\{\mathbf{W}_{1},\mathbf{b}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{W}_{L},\mathbf{b}_{L}\}.

To enforce homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on
V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}
, we introduce a smooth cutoff function

g_{D}\in C^{1,1}(\Omega)
, that vanishes on
\partial\Omega
and is strictly positive in the interior.
The spanning set is then defined via a multiplicative ansatz:

\displaystyle\varphi_{j}(x):=g_{D}(x)\,\Phi_{L}^{(j)}(x),\qquad\boldsymbol{\tau}_{j+(k-1)n_{L}}(x):=\Phi_{L}^{(j)}(x)\,\mathbf{e}_{k},\quad j=1,\dots,n_{L},\;k=1,\dots,d,

where
\{\mathbf{e}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This multiplicative ansatz is standard in variational formulations and ensures conformity of the discrete spaces within

H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times H(\mathrm{div};\Omega)
independently of
\boldsymbol{\theta}

(see e.g.,
[
6
]
).

4.1
Initialization of the discrete space

This subsection describes the construction of the initial discrete spaces

V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}\times V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}
.
We focus on the choice of activation function and the resulting functional setting.

Activation function and functional setting

We select the Rectified Quadratic Unit (ReQU) activation function, defined by

\operatorname{ReQU}(x):=(\max\{0,x\})^{2}.

Together with the strongly imposed boundary conditions
g_{D}\in C^{1,1}(\Omega)
, and the selected architecture, this choice yields an ansatz space

V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}\times V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}\subset H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times H(\Div;\Omega),

which is conforming with the functional setting of the problem.
Among admissible
C^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
activations,
\mathrm{ReQU}
is particularly appealing because it has comparatively low regularity while remaining in this class, which helps mitigate the quasi-Gibbs phenomenon.

Geometric structure of the ansatz space

The initial distribution of the network parameters determines the initial geometric structure of the ansatz space.
Following
[
16
]
, we characterize this structure in terms of the support of the spanning functions,
defined through
breaking hypersurfaces
.
For the
l
-th hidden layer, these hypersurfaces (breaking points in one dimension and breaking curves in two dimensions)
are defined as

\mathcal{S}^{(l)}_{j}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\big(\mathbf{W}_{l}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{l-1}(\boldsymbol{x})+\mathbf{b}_{l}\big)^{(j)}=0\right\},\qquad j=1,\ldots,n_{l}.

(22)

Each hypersurface
\mathcal{S}^{(l)}_{j}
separates the input domain into regions where the corresponding
network component is either active or inactive, thereby inducing a piecewise-polynomial structure of the ansatz.

Remark 8
.

When considering more than one hidden layer, the breaking hypersurfaces associated with

\mathcal{S}^{(l)}_{j}
can be described by piecewise-polynomial equations of degree up to
2l
.
In particular, in 2D these hypersurfaces appear as
breaking curves
rather than straight lines. This contrasts with the case of standard ReLU networks, where the activation introduces linear partition boundaries.

Initialization in one dimension

In the one-dimensional case,
\Omega=(0,1)
, the weights and biases of the first hidden layer
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{1}

are chosen as

\mathbf{W}^{(i)}_{1}=(-1)^{i+1},\qquad\mathbf{b}^{(i)}_{1}=(-1)^{i}\frac{i}{n_{1}+1},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n_{1}.

This initialization produces
n_{1}
equispaced breaking points located strictly inside the domain. Figure

V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}\times V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}

5
Stochastic Quadrature

Since for arbitrary parametric spaces
V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{u}\times V^{\boldsymbol{q}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
the integrals present in the loss (
18
) and in the energy-norm Poincaré approximation (
20
) cannot be computed exactly, we resort to a quadrature rule
Q_{N}
with
N
points. The integral of a function

\mathcal{I}
over
\Omega
is then approximated by

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{I}\;\approx\;Q_{N}[\mathcal{I}]\;:=\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}\,\mathcal{I}(x_{i}),

where
\{(w_{i},x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
are the corresponding quadrature weights and points.

Classical deterministic rules (e.g., Gaussian quadrature) may lead to overfitting in neural network spaces
[
32
]
.
To overcome this overfitting, the Monte Carlo (MC) estimator samples
x_{i}
uniformly in
\Omega
with weights
w_{i}=|\Omega|/N
,

Q_{N}^{\mathrm{MC}}[\mathcal{I}]:=\frac{|\Omega|}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{I}(x_{i}),

which is unbiased, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[Q_{N}^{\mathrm{MC}}[\mathcal{I}]]=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{I}
, and satisfies

\operatorname{Var}\!\left(Q_{N}^{\mathrm{MC}}[\mathcal{I}]\right)=\frac{|\Omega|^{2}}{N}\left(\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{I}^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{I}\right)^{2}\right),

yielding a root mean-squared error of order
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})
.

As the variance decays slowly with
N
, a large number of integration points may be required to reduce integration errors. We therefore adopt
a first-order stratified strategy. Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}=\{K\}
be a partition of
\Omega
into disjoint hyperrectangles and denote by
F_{K}:\widehat{K}=(-1,1)^{d}\to K
the associated affine maps with Jacobian
|J_{F_{K}}|
. Using the unbiased first-order rule of
[
23
]
, the quadrature formula reads:

For each
K
, let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{K}
be uniformly distributed in
\widehat{K}
and define
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}^{K}=-\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{K}
, with weights
\widehat{w}_{1}^{K}=\widehat{w}_{2}^{K}=2^{d-1}
. The global approximation is given by

Q_{N}^{P1}[\mathcal{I}]:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|J_{F_{K}}|\sum_{j=1}^{2}\widehat{w}_{j}^{K}\,\mathcal{I}\circ F_{K}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}^{K}).

When employing a uniform partition, the root mean-squared error of this rule behaves as
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2-2/d})
for sufficiently smooth integrands, see
[
38
]
.

5.1
Passive variance reduction

The functional
(
21
) is discretized as

\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\approx Q^{P1}_{N}[\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|J_{F_{K}}|\sum_{j=1}^{2}\widehat{w}_{j}^{K}\left(\|\kappa^{1/2}\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\kappa^{-1/2}\nabla u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}+2(C^{P}_{\kappa})^{2}|\Div\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-f|^{2}\right)\circ F_{K}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}^{K}),

(23)

where
\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the integrand of the loss function.

The parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are updated by means of a gradient-based optimization algorithm.
The update requires the explicit computation of the gradient of the functional (
13
).
Replacing the exact loss by its quadrature approximation leads to

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{J}\approx\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}Q_{N}^{P_{1}}[\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}].

The resulting discrete parameter update can be interpreted as a Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) scheme.
The following theorem demonstrates that as
(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
approaches the exact solution, the variance of the gradient approximation naturally decreases, which accelerates the convergence of the optimization strategy
1
1

1

This is not the case for other deep variational approaches as VPINNs
[
25
,
39
]
and its variants (see, e.g.
[

]
), or Deep Ritz

.
.

Theorem 2

(Passive variance reduction of the loss gradient)
.

Assume that the neural network parametrizations

(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is sufficiently smooth with respect to the trainable parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
Furthermore, for each component
\theta
of the parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, assume that there exists a constant
C_{\rm grad}>0
such that, for almost every

\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega
,

\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\kappa^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{x})\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})+\kappa^{-1/2}(\boldsymbol{x})\dfrac{\partial\nabla u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})\\[4.30554pt]
\sqrt{2}\,C^{P}_{\kappa}\,\dfrac{\partial\Div\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\leq C_{\rm grad}.

(24)

Then, for the unbiased quadrature approximation

Q^{P1}_{N}[\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]
of the loss functional in Eq. (
23
),
the variance of its gradient satisfies

\operatorname{Var}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}Q_{N}^{P1}[\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]\right]\leq 4C_{\rm grad}^{2}\,|\Omega|\,\mathcal{L}(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),

(25)

where the constant
C_{\rm grad}>0
depends only on the bound
(
24
) and is independent of the quadrature rule.

Proof.

Introduce the
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
-valued residual

\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\kappa^{1/2}\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\kappa^{-1/2}\nabla u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\\[2.15277pt]
\sqrt{2}\,C^{P}_{\kappa}\,(\Div\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-f)\end{pmatrix}.

Since

\|\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}^{2}=\|\kappa^{1/2}\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\kappa^{-1/2}\nabla u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{2}+2(C^{P}_{\kappa})^{2}|\Div\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-f|^{2},

we have

\mathcal{L}(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\int_{\Omega}\|\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

Differentiating the quadrature-based loss (
23
) with respect to a single parameter
\theta
of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and using the chain rule, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}Q^{P1}_{N}[\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]

\displaystyle=2\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|J_{F_{K}}|\sum_{j=1}^{2}\widehat{w}_{j}^{K}\Big\langle\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\theta}\Big\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\circ F_{K}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}^{K}),

where

\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\kappa^{1/2}\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\theta}+\kappa^{-1/2}\dfrac{\partial\nabla u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\theta}\\[4.30554pt]
\sqrt{2}\,C^{P}_{\kappa}\,\dfrac{\partial\Div\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\theta}\end{pmatrix}.

Using the bound (
24
), the positivity of the quadrature weights, and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}Q^{P1}_{N}[\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{\rm grad}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|J_{F_{K}}|\sum_{j=1}^{2}\widehat{w}_{j}^{K}\|\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\circ F_{K}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}^{K})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}.

(26)

Applying now the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the quadrature sum gives

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|J_{F_{K}}|\sum_{j=1}^{2}\widehat{w}_{j}^{K}\|\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\circ F_{K}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}^{K})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|J_{F_{K}}|\sum_{j=1}^{2}\widehat{w}_{j}^{K}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|J_{F_{K}}|\sum_{j=1}^{2}\widehat{w}_{j}^{K}\|\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\circ F_{K}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}^{K})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}^{2}\right).

Since the quadrature is unbiased and exactly integrates constants, the first factor equals
|\Omega|
. Therefore, from (
26
),

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}Q^{P1}_{N}[\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4C_{\rm grad}^{2}|\Omega|\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|J_{F_{K}}|\sum_{j=1}^{2}\widehat{w}_{j}^{K}\|\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\circ F_{K}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}^{K})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}^{2}.

Taking expectations and using again the unbiasedness of the quadrature rule, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}Q^{P1}_{N}[\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4C_{\rm grad}^{2}|\Omega|\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|J_{F_{K}}|\sum_{j=1}^{2}\widehat{w}_{j}^{K}\|\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\circ F_{K}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}^{K})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=4C_{\rm grad}^{2}|\Omega|\int_{\Omega}\|\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=4C_{\rm grad}^{2}|\Omega|\,\mathcal{L}(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

Since

\operatorname{Var}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}Q_{N}^{P1}[\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}Q_{N}^{P1}[\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]\right|^{2}\right],

the result follows.

6.2.3
Quasi-Gibbs phenomenon

In
[
16
]
, the authors provide a constructive proof showing that ReLU networks (
\sigma(x)=\mathrm{ReLU}(x)
) can approximate characteristic functions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
over sufficiently regular domains without exhibiting Gibbs-type oscillations. This construction assumes that the discontinuity set (location and geometry) is known
a priori
. However, herein we allow the neural network to
discover
the discontinuity location. While the network can approximate this location, we often observe a quasi-Gibbs phenomenon due to the misalignment between the derivative discontinuity and its neural network approximation, an effect that is magnified when using a smooth activation function
\sigma
. This is particularly relevant because such oscillations enter directly into the loss functional, thereby requiring a more careful integration to control the variance.

To see this quasi-Gibbs phenomenon, we compare our
\operatorname{ReQU}
-based discrete space versus a smooth neural network using
\sigma(x)=\tanh(mx)\in C^{\infty},\,m>0,
in the problem (
27
) with
\kappa_{0}=3
. For the former space, we consider two cases: (a) the network defined in Section
6.2
and (b) a neural network with
L=2
hidden layers and
n_{1}=n_{2}=16
. For the latter, we consider
m\in\boldsymbol{\theta}
, i.e., a parameter to be optimized, initialized with
m=50
. Figure
8
shows the results. The smooth network exhibits a noticeable quasi-Gibbs phenomenon localized near the interface. This behavior was observed throughout all iterations, a pattern also observed with other
\sigma\in C^{\infty}
functions. In contrast, the
\operatorname{ReQU}
network exhibits only mild oscillations beyond those inherent to its piecewise structure. Even with more hidden layers, after some iterations, the quasi-Gibbs phenomenon is dampened considerably. To quantify this phenomenon near the interface, we measure the Total Variation (TV) of the gradient error,
\|\nabla(\nabla(u^{*}-u_{\mathrm{NN}}))\|_{L^{1}}
restricted to the interval
[0.4,0.6]
in the final iteration. The neural network with
\operatorname{ReQU}
activation and
L=1
layer has a TV of
4.76
, whereas the network with
L=2
layers has a TV of
4.67
. The
\tanh
network with
m\in\boldsymbol{\theta}
has a TV of
9.29
. A simple calculation helps us interpret the TV–error. Since we are measuring the total variation of the gradient error, we model the jump in the gradient by considering:

g_{\varepsilon}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x<-\varepsilon,\\[3.0pt]
\dfrac{A}{2\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon),&-\varepsilon\leq x\leq\varepsilon,\\[6.0pt]
A,&x>\varepsilon,\end{cases}

which represents an
ideal
piecewise linear approximation of a step function with jump
A
. The total variation of this approximation is

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\nabla g_{\varepsilon}|=\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}\frac{A}{2\varepsilon}\,dx=A.

Thus, even an ideal non-oscillatory approximation contributes a nonzero amount
A
to the TV norm. In our case, the jump in the gradient is
2\pi\left(1-1/3\right)\approx 4.188
, which we can interpret as an excess error, with the excess error in the tanh case being around nine times larger than the ReQU implementation. Moreover, since the exact gradient is discontinuous whereas the approximation has a continuous gradient, the
L^{\infty}-
error is expected to be at least of order
A/2
. Hence, the errors observed near the discontinuity should not be interpreted as unexpectedly large.

7
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced a novel FOSLS functional and an associated robust energy norm for transmission problems. We also proposed a neural network training methodology that combines the optimality properties of least-squares solvers with the flexibility of gradient-based methods for searching over optimal trial spaces. Moreover, we showed that the gradient-based loss induced by the FOSLS functional yields a passive reduction in gradient variance, a property not satisfied by other standard weak formulations commonly used in the literature. We further observed that the neural approximation spaces accurately reproduce the associated Poincaré-type approximation constant, showing that the learned spanning functions capture the relevant structure of the solution space.

Our numerical experiments indicate that passive variance reduction plays an important role in stabilizing neural network training. We further illustrated the robustness of the proposed formulation for transmission problems, contrasting it with the standard FOSLS formulation. In addition, the use of the
\operatorname{ReQU}
activation in neural networks was observed to reduce the quasi-Gibbs phenomenon compared to smoother activation functions.

The present study was restricted to problems without singularities, as these introduce additional analytical and numerical challenges. We leave as future work incorporating prior information about the singularity into the approximation space, for example, through tailored architectures following the ideas in
[
37
]
. In the experiments reported in this work, the numerical integration was performed on uniform meshes. The use of adaptive grids for more accurate integration is another interesting direction for future investigation.

Appendix A
Derivation of first eigenvalue constant for planar geometries

We consider the one-dimensional problem with
\Omega=(0,1)
,
x_{0}\in\Omega
, and homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, where

\kappa(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\kappa_{1},&0\leq x\leq x_{0},\\
\kappa_{2},&x_{0}\leq x\leq 1,\end{array}\right.

with
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>0
. Since
\kappa
is constant on each subinterval, the eigenfunctions are given piecewise by

u(x)=\begin{cases}A\sin\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{1}}}\,x\right),&0\leq x\leq x_{0},\\
B\sin\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{2}}}\,(1-x)\right),&x_{0}\leq x\leq 1.\end{cases}

Imposing continuity of
u
and of the flux
-\kappa u^{\prime}
at
x=x_{0}
yields

A\sin\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{1}}}\,x_{0}\right)=B\sin\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{2}}}\,(1-x_{0})\right),

and

\kappa_{1}A\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{1}}}\cos\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{1}}}\,x_{0}\right)=-\kappa_{2}B\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{2}}}\cos\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{2}}}\,(1-x_{0})\right).

Eliminating
A
and
B
leads to the transcendental equation:

\sqrt{\kappa_{2}}\tan\left(\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{1}}}x_{0}\right)+\sqrt{\kappa_{1}}\tan\left(\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{2}}}(1-x_{0})\right)=0.

This equation characterizes a discrete sequence of positive eigenvalues. In particular, the smallest positive eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
is uniquely defined.
