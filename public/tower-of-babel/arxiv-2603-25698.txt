Notes on Diagrammatic Coaction for Cosmological Wavefunction Coefficients: A Two-Site Prelude
Yuhan Fu (付雨涵), Jiahao Liu (刘家昊)

Abstract
We study the coaction of cosmological wavefunction coefficients of conformally coupled scalars in FRW background of a two-site example, which turns out to have an elegant diagrammatic interpretation. We show how the coaction acts on the twisted integrals for wavefunction coefficients, decomposing them into contributions associated with subtopologies and cuts, with the subtopologies admitting an interpretation as time-ordered integrals. This provides a clear interpretation of their analytic structure and suggests a broader applicability to more general cosmological diagrams.

The self-interacting conformal scalar theory in (
d+1

S\left[\phi\right]=-\int{\rm d}^{d}x\,{\rm d}\tau\sqrt{-g}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\partial\phi)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\xi R\phi^{2}+\sum_{k>2}\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{k}}{k!}\phi^{k}\right),

(1)

{\rm d}^{2}s=a(\tau)^{2}(-{\rm d}\tau^{2}+{\rm d}x^{2})=\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau_{0}}\right)^{-2\epsilon}(-{\rm d}\tau^{2}+{\rm d}x^{2}).

(2)

3
Coaction on Multipolylogarithms and Hypergeometric Functions

In this subsection, we briefly review the coaction on multipolylogarithms (MPLs) and hypergeometric functions. We only provide the needed information and set up our notation. For more detailed discussion, one can refer to
41
and references therein.

Like many cases of multi-loop Feynman integrals, the twisted integral can be evaluated in terms of MPLs, defined by the iterated integrals

G(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n};z)=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{{\rm d}t}{(t-a_{1})}G(a_{2},\ldots,a_{n};t),

(18)

where the
a_{i}
and
z
are either constants or functions of certain variables. If all
a_{i}=0
, the integral diverges, and we define

G(\vec{0}_{n};z)=\frac{1}{n!}\log^{n}z\,,\qquad\vec{0}_{n}=(\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{n\textrm{ times}})\,.

(19)

We call an MPL is of weight-
n
if it is defined by an
n
-fold iterated integral. The space of all weight-
n
MPL is
\mathcal{H}_{n}
, including lower weight MPLs multiplied by transcendental constants,
e.g.

\pi
and
\zeta_{n}
. Roughly speaking, the coaction of an MPL is a map

\mathcal{H}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\longrightarrow}}\bigoplus_{p+q=n}\mathcal{H}_{p}\otimes(\mathcal{H}_{q}\text{ mod }\pi),

(20)

which takes an MPL to a linear combination of tensor products of lower weight MPLs. Note that the second entry is defined modulo
\pi
. Two simple examples are as follows,

\displaystyle\Delta(\log^{n}z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\log^{n-k}z\otimes\log^{k}z,

(21)

\displaystyle\Delta(\textrm{Li}_{n}(z))=1\otimes\textrm{Li}_{n}(z)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\textrm{Li}_{n-k}(z)\otimes\frac{\log^{k}z}{k!}.

(22)

For the transcendental constants
\zeta_{n}
and
\pi
, we also have the following definition of coaction:

\Delta(\zeta_{n})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\zeta_{n}\otimes 1+1\otimes\zeta_{n}\,,\qquad&n\textrm{ odd}\,,\\
&\zeta_{n}\otimes 1\,,\qquad&n\textrm{ even}\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(23)

and

\Delta(\text{i}\,\pi)=(\text{i}\,\pi)\otimes 1.

(24)

For later convenience, we write

\Delta=\sum_{p+q=n}\Delta_{p,q},

(25)

where
\Delta_{p,q}
denotes the
\mathcal{H}_{p}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{q}
part in the coaction. And we can further take coaction of a coaction. There are two seemingly different ways to do so,
i.e.

(\Delta\otimes\text{id})
and
(\text{id}\otimes\Delta)
, but coassociativity ensures that their results are the same. So we have a unique way “decompose” a weight-
n
MPL. We will denote a particular decomposition into weight
\{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{j}\}
by
\Delta_{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{j}}
. Note that the total differential is the coaction
\Delta_{n,1}
modulo
\pi
, and the symbol is simply
\Delta_{1,1,\ldots,1}
modulo
\pi
with
n
subscripts is the symbol.

At each order of
\epsilon
in the perturbative solution of DE (
17
), the coaction is calculated as mentioned above. We can also resum the result, and come to the notion of coaction on the resumed function. As one can check order by order:
\Delta(m^{-2\epsilon})\,=m^{-2\epsilon}\otimes m^{-2\epsilon}
. We then give a more non-trivial example for hypergeometric functions
{}_{2}F_{1}
, defined by

{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;x)=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\gamma-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{1}du\,u^{\alpha-1}(1-u)^{\gamma-\alpha-1}(1-ux)^{-\beta}\,.

(26)

We can expand an explicit hypergeometric function as

{}_{2}F_{1}(-\epsilon,1;1-\epsilon;x)=1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\epsilon^{n}\,\textrm{Li}_{n}(x)=1-F(\epsilon,x)\,.

(27)

Considering Eq. (
22
), the resummed coaction can be written as

\Delta\left[F(\epsilon,x)\right]=1\otimes F(\epsilon,x)+F(\epsilon,x)\otimes x^{\epsilon}\,.

(28)

Then it is easy to see that

\Delta\left[{}_{2}F_{1}(-\epsilon,1;1-\epsilon;x)\right]=1\otimes{}_{2}F_{1}(-\epsilon,1;1-\epsilon;x)-F(\epsilon,x)\otimes x^{\epsilon}\,.

(29)

More generally, the coaction on a twisted integral can be written as

(30)

where the left and right entry are given by master forms
\vec{\Omega}
(which can be chosen as the canonical forms of the bounded regions) and the dual contours
\vec{\gamma}
respectively. And
\mathbf{C}(\vec{\Omega},\Omega(\vec{\gamma});u)
is the intersection number matrix, where the intersection number is

\mathbf{C}(\vec{\Omega},\Omega(\vec{\gamma});u)_{ij}=\langle\Omega(\gamma_{j})|\Omega_{i}\rangle,

(31)

and
\Omega(\gamma_{j})
denotes a member of the dual cohomology or the relative twisted cohomology, defined as the direct sum of the twisted cohomology on boundaries.

H^{n}(M,B,\nabla_{-\omega})=\overset{n}{\underset{p=0}{\bigoplus}}\overset{}{\underset{|J|=p}{\bigoplus}}\ H^{n-|J|}(M\cap B_{J},\nabla_{-\omega}|_{J}),

(32)

where

, and

denotes a collection of

boundaries with index set

as in

The intersection number in relative twisted cohomology can be defined as that in ordinary twisted cohomology on boundary

5
Outlook

In this work, using a two-site example, we study the coaction of cosmological wavefunction coefficients of conformally coupled scalars in FRW background, which turns out to have a diagrammatic interpretation. We also clarified the physical interpretation of the various terms appearing in the coaction, identifying them with time ordered integrals and cuts.

More broadly, the coaction encodes structured information associated with the integrated function. In this sense, it complements both the direct series expansions studied in
43
;
44
and the differential-equation approach developed in
49
. In particular, it provides a systematic and general framework for studying the singularity of FRW integrals, generalizing the notion of symbols.

In this work, we only present a two-site example, then a natural question to ask is whether the diagrammatic coaction can be constructed for arbitrary trees and loop integrands of conformally coupled scalars, and whether a similar structure can also be developed for massive scalars. For the massless cases, we leave it to a future work
46
. And for the massive cases, one may replace the Hankel functions of the second kind appearing in massive propagators by their integral representations, thereby rewriting the problem in terms of a higher-dimensional twisted integral. For example, for the single massive exchange diagram in dS, in close analogy with the twisted-integral representation in (
13
) and (
14
), one obtains

\begin{array}[]{l}B_{1}=X_{1}+x_{1}+Ys_{1},\quad B_{2}=X_{2}+x_{2}+Ys_{2},\\
B_{3}=X_{1}+x_{1}+X_{2}+x_{2}+Y(s_{1}-s_{2}),\quad B_{4}=X_{1}+x_{1}+X_{2}+x_{2}+Y(s_{2}-s_{1}),\end{array}

(60)

and the integrand can be written as

\psi^{(\nu)}=\psi_{+}^{(\nu)}+\psi_{-}^{(\nu)}+\psi_{0}^{(\nu)},

(61)

where the three terms correspond respectively to the time-ordered, anti-time-ordered, and boundary contributions:

\displaystyle\psi_{+}^{(\nu)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{1}\land\mathrm{d}x_{2}\land\mathrm{d}s_{1}\land\mathrm{d}s_{2}}{2YB_{1}B_{3}},

(62)

\displaystyle\psi_{-}^{(\nu)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{1}\land\mathrm{d}x_{2}\land\mathrm{d}s_{1}\land\mathrm{d}s_{2}}{2YB_{2}B_{4}},

\displaystyle\psi_{0}^{(\nu)}

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi i\nu}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{1}\land\mathrm{d}x_{2}\land\mathrm{d}s_{1}\land\mathrm{d}s_{2}}{2YB_{1}B_{2}}.

Here,

u=(x_{1}x_{2})^{-\epsilon}\bigl(s_{1}s_{2}(s_{1}+2)(s_{2}+2)\bigr)^{\epsilon},

(63)

and the integral is defined by

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{4}}u\cdot\psi^{(\nu)}.

(64)

However, the increase in the dimension of the integral for also introduces a much larger amount of redundancy in the basis. Naively, the hyperplanes in this four-dimensional integration space intersect to produce five boundaries
B_{J}
, with
J=\{1,2\},\{1,3\},\{2,4\},\{3\},\{4\}
. This would lead to a
20
-dimensional cohomology basis, with four basis elements supported on each boundary. By contrast, the fact that the integral satisfies second-order partial differential equations strongly suggests that the true physical basis should be much smaller.

The main challenge, therefore, is to identify a systematic reduction from this redundant cohomology basis to the true physical one. This is closely related to the mechanism discussed in
39
, but the present setting is more subtle: in addition to redundant cohomology elements, one also encounters unphysical integrals whose symmetries generate further equivalence relations among the integrands. Understanding how to quotient out these redundancies will be an important step toward extending the coaction framework to massive fields.
