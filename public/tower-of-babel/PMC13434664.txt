Collaboration With Clinicians in Inpatient Mental Healthcare: A Qualitative Investigation of Consumer Perspectives
Authors: Joel Sebastian Zugai, Katherine Gill, Grant Kinghorn, Lucie Ramjan, Yenna Salamonson, Luke Molloy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCollaboration is a foundational characteristic of ethical mental healthcare. However, inpatient mental healthcare often fails to espouse the practices and principles that would establish meaningful collaborative care, with coercion and restrictive practices an ongoing aspect of consumers' lived experience. This qualitative descriptive study aimed to understand consumers' perspectives of collaboration with clinicians in the inpatient mental health setting. One‐on‐one semi‐structured interviews were conducted with 12 people who have experienced involuntary inpatient mental healthcare; data were analysed by thematic analysis. The participants of this study valued collaborative inpatient mental healthcare. However, they reported that their own perspectives were not sufficiently sought or considered. Clinicians' default scepticism compounded participants' experiences of inconsideration. In contrast, genuinely caring interpersonal relationships with clinicians were valued, establishing a sense of safety and collaboration. The failure to establish collaboration requires systemic reform and the resolution of micro‐cultures that disenfranchise the perspectives of people receiving inpatient mental healthcare. Notably, the participants of this study reported that interpersonal relationships with clinicians facilitated meaningful collaboration. Collaboration relies on the presence of strong interpersonal care; the culture, policy and structures of inpatient mental healthcare must be oriented to be interpersonal.Keywords: communication, health care ethics, hospitals, mental health, psychiatric, qualitative research

Introduction

Inpatient mental health services provide care to individuals experiencing acute mental health crises and are intended to establish safety and therapeutic support. However, the imperative of maintaining safety and mitigating risk often conflicts with consumers' autonomy and preferences (Reid et al. 2018). Furthermore, coercive practices such as seclusion and restraint persist in the inpatient setting (Hawsawi et al. 2020). Eschewing paternalism, contemporary philosophy of mental healthcare has shifted toward partnership, active consumer participation and recovery‐focused models of care (Davidson et al. 2017; Jørgensen et al. 2024). Internationally, mental health policy and professional standards consistently promote consumer collaboration as a cornerstone of effective and ethical care; guidelines and frameworks emphasise principles such as shared decision‐making, recovery orientation, and active consumer participation in treatment planning and service evaluation (Australian Government 2022; National Institute for Health and Care Excellence 2021; Sunderji et al. 2022; World Health Organization and United Nations 2023). To ensure ethical and evidence‐based mental healthcare, the inpatient setting must adopt these principles into practice. To enhance care, the dynamics of collaboration in the inpatient mental health setting must be better understood.

Background

Collaboration in mental healthcare encompasses partnership, respect, and shared purpose between consumers and clinicians (Olasoji et al. 2020). Recovery‐oriented practice can only be delivered with a collaborative approach, with consumers being active agents in their care, supported to make decisions consistent with their values and goals (Lau and Hutchinson 2021). Collaboration has been shown to enhance empowerment, treatment engagement, and adherence (James and Quirk 2017; Olasoji et al. 2020). Shared decision‐making (SDM), where consumers are actively involved in treatment choices and their preferences are valued, is a key strategy for advancing collaborative and consumer‐centred care (Elwyn et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2020). This practice enables consumers and clinicians to exchange information, discuss preferences and reach consensus on treatment decisions (Sagen et al. 2023). SDM enhances therapeutic relationships, fosters engagement and improves outcomes (Huang et al. 2020; James and Quirk 2017). Also of significance, consumers emphasise the importance of interpersonal connection and care in fostering collaborative relationships. Being treated with dignity, respect, and attentiveness provides a sense of safety and trust, which is essential for collaboration to occur (Olasoji et al. 2020; Oxelmark et al. 2018). Studies highlight the importance of nurses and other clinicians providing time, presence and attentiveness to establish continuity and genuine care (Oxelmark et al. 2018; Wolf et al. 2017). Such interpersonal engagement helps consumers feel valued and safe, providing the foundation for trust and collaboration.

Despite developments in research, philosophy and policy, collaboration in mental healthcare is unrealised. Meaningful collaboration is constrained by entrenched cultural and structural barriers (Billsten and Benderix 2021; Faerden et al. 2023; Sagen et al. 2023). For example, institutional micro‐cultures may constrain the adoption of practices that espouse ideal principles of collaboration in mental healthcare (Carr 2024). Consumers are seldom consulted or meaningfully involved with treatment decisions, especially decisions related to medications (Laitila et al. 2018; Olasoji et al. 2020; Sagen et al. 2023). Furthermore, evidence suggests that consumer perspectives are often undermined, with their accounts dismissed as expressions of illness, leading to the marginalisation of their contributions into the planning of their own care (Laitila et al. 2018). Inpatient mental healthcare often involves physical restraint, involuntary detainment, non‐negotiated administration of medication and seclusion (Zugai 2023). There is a clear inconsistency between idealised collaboration in mental healthcare and practice. The aim of this study is to establish consumers' lived experiences of collaboration with clinicians within the inpatient mental health setting. Specifically, this study will focus on the lived experiences of consumers who were involuntarily hospitalised for psychotic mental illness.

Methods

Design

This study is focused on understanding the subjective lived experiences of people within a specific context; the aims of this study are best achieved through a qualitative descriptive approach. A qualitative descriptive approach gives a voice to consumers and contributes to the utilisation of research findings in nursing practice (Doyle et al. 2020). The COREQ guidelines were used to ensure transparency in reporting (Tong et al. 2007).

Procedures, Participants and Data Collection

In accordance with the aims of the study, recruitment relied on purposive, non‐probability sampling. All participants were required to be at least 18 years of age, reside in Australia, and have experienced mandated inpatient mental healthcare for the treatment of psychotic illness. People who were currently receiving inpatient care were excluded. Recruitment and data collection occurred between February and May 2024. Participants were recruited from two social media community sites for people in Australia with psychotic illness. On these sites, a flyer was posted introducing the study and the PI's (Principal Investigator) contact details. On contact, potential participants were provided with an information sheet that detailed requirements of participation and other details related to the study. Participants liaised with the PI to organise a mutually convenient time to meet for an interview. Interviews were conducted over an online conferencing service. The authors had no pre‐existing relationships with participants.

Upon commencing the interview, participants were asked a series of screening questions to confirm suitability for inclusion and were then asked to provide verbal consent. All interviews were audio recorded. Files generated from interviews were stored directly in Cloudstor hosted by AARNET. The interview schedule prompted participants to describe their experiences related to collaboration with clinicians. Being semi‐structured, the direction of the interview varied according to the unique experiences reported by each participant. Upon review of the transcripts, the authors concluded that 12 interviews were sufficient for data redundancy, with no new themes emerging (Guest et al. 2006). This number of interviews is consistent with other qualitative investigations of comparable focus and methodology (Hem et al. 2018). Transcripts were not returned to participants.

Analysis

The data were analysed through an inductive thematic analysis in accordance with the steps described by Braun and Clarke, which is compatible with a qualitative descriptive approach (Braun and Clarke 2006, 2019; Doyle et al. 2020). The data were analysed through a systematic coding process, involving peer debriefing and reflexive journaling to ensure rigour (Braun and Clarke 2019; Morse 2015), and the thick descriptions within the interviews secured transferability of findings (Younas et al. 2023). Two authors conducted the initial coding and analysis, and all authors were involved in the finalisation of analysis and reporting. Quotes are used in the presentation of results and are attributed to pseudonyms to protect participants' identities. The authors consisted of three men and three women. All authors are PhD qualified and have experience in qualitative research. Five of the authors are full‐time nursing academics and one of the authors is a consumer representative, with an established track record of participation in consumer perspective research. The composition of the research team invariably requires consideration for reflexivity, as our own lived experiences cannot be quarantined from our interpretation of the data (Dodgson 2019), and most of the research team consists of clinicians. For this reason, the consumer representative (an adult female who has experienced involuntary inpatient hospitalisation) provided an important and informed contribution to the interpretation of the data.

Ethical Considerations and Data Management

This study was approved by the University of Wollongong Human Research Ethics Committee (2023/207). There were no reported adverse events from this study, and fully informed consent was obtained from all participants. All audio recordings were deidentified with audio software (Audacity). The deidentified audio files were then forwarded to a transcription service adherent with the Australian Privacy Principles within the Australian Privacy Act 1988 and the Guidelines on Privacy in the Private Health Sector issued by the Federal Privacy Commissioner under the National Health Act 1953 (Pacific Transcription 2023). Data will be stored for 5 years post‐publication and will then be permanently deleted in accordance with university institutional ethical guidelines.

Results

Twelve participants were interviewed for this study, four men and eight women, with an average age of 40.3 years. Interviews lasted 33 min on average. Eight participants reported being diagnosed with schizophrenia, 3 with schizo‐affective disorder and 1 with type 1 bipolar disorder. All participants were born in Australia, except 1. Participants reported various total durations of involuntary hospitalisation, with 23 days and 18 months being the range. The participants in this study were asked questions that specifically examined their perception of clinicians' power in the inpatient setting, and participants provided insight into the implications of their interactions with clinicians.

Participants of this study provided rich descriptions of their inpatient experiences, and their interactions with clinicians. Inpatient mental healthcare routinely involved the suspension of autonomy and limited the capacity for independent decision making. Clinicians had the power to permit or deny ordinary freedoms and were able to mandate treatments. Being so vulnerable, participants felt safer when their perspectives were genuinely considered, and when care was determined and delivered collaboratively. All too frequently, participants reported that care in the inpatient setting failed to establish meaningful collaboration. Three themes were established: Shared Decision Making, Default Dismissal, and Care and Interpersonal Presence. Each theme represents a distinct category of interaction that influenced participants' perception of collaboration with clinicians.

Shared Decision Making: ‘Open to My Own Suggestions and Listens to What I Want’

The participants of this study valued interactions that fostered a collaborative approach to their care. The participants expressed a strong preference for close consultation and shared decision‐making regarding the treatments they were to receive. Tina described the importance of care that is determined through a consultative process.

I think that was really good and they let me eat in my room, which I really needed. So they were very good about that, but these days I've got friends who've been hospitalised and they have to participate in groups… So that's a big thing for me, being forced to participate in groups. But what they could do is they could say, well, if you don't want to do this mindfulness group‐ which I've done a million times before‐ maybe you could go for a walk, like a guided walk or something or go swimming or do some exercise, Tina.

Overall, inpatient care was not sufficiently consultative, and clinician led decision making with medications was a particularly salient point of criticism. Many participants felt that psychiatrists made unilateral decisions with medications with a casual indifference for their health and preferences.

I think the medications—they need to give you all the side effects, like all down up‐front and not just put you on a medication and say, okay, this is yours. Stick with it, and if it doesn't work, we'll put you on another medication without telling you what it's actually doing to you, Stanley.

Well, you never get told really what you're being prescribed or why. You never get told the risks and the benefits. I was considered to be a very difficult customer because I read up on the drugs that I was being forced to take, Stella.

The participants of this study wanted to make substantial contributions to their own care plans, exercising autonomy over their own outcomes. Clinicians' use of medication presented the greatest incursion to their sense of agency over their own body and mind, invoking strong rebukes of psychiatric prescribing. Contrasting most reports, Tina shared her experience with a psychiatrist who engaged with her in a duly consultative manner.

I'm lucky with my doctor. He's very open to my own suggestions and listens to what I want to do. So he will say to me, do you want to try this medication here? It's supposedly good for X, Y and Z. So he will give me the choice—when I was in hospital, he did ‐ as to what medication I wanted to be on… So I feel during my time of hospitalisation, I was given—I was involved in decision‐making, Tina.

Default Dismissal: ‘Everything This Person is Saying That Comes Out of Their Mouth is Psychosis’

A common and major hindrance to effective collaboration in the inpatient setting was the tendency for clinicians to readily dismiss the participants' expressed views and perspectives. Receiving inpatient mental healthcare was grounds for a clinician's prima facie conclusion that mental illness contaminated the veracity of any interaction. For example, Gemma described how simply having a diagnosis led to a prevailing incredulity.

it seems like they're analysing every single thing you say and they just don't believe anything that's coming out of your mouth. I think once you have that schizophrenia schizoaffective type diagnosis it's just everything this person is saying that comes out of their mouth is psychosis…, Gemma.

Similarly, Tom describes a benign interaction wherein a clinician wrongly concluded that he was under the influence of a delusion, and consequently made erroneous documentation.

I was talking to my mother who was there and I was explaining to her it was hard where I was living because the drain went from the sink to the garage underneath the house and the food built up and it smelled. Then the doctor wrote in the notes, the discharge note, that I was talking about how [my] mind was being controlled by gases that were being pumped—He said, is that what you were saying? I said, ‘No, I was just talking about the smelly compost’… I believe it flags on your file, the mental health diagnosis… sometimes, some of these younger doctors‐ or probably doesn't matter how old they are‐ they miss the mark a bit, Tom.

Participants described numerous interactions where clinicians conveyed a predictable dismissal. Clinicians were unable to distinguish statements that were mediated from mental illness, and statements that were authentic. This undermined the sense of cooperation that participants were so keen to establish, and clinicians claimed licence to make unduly paternalistic or ill‐informed decisions. Clinicians' pervasive stance of incredulity and scepticism compromised the potential for collaboration.

Care and Interpersonal Presence: ‘So You Don't Feel Like Just a Number’

Authentic collaboration occurred in the context of interpersonal, genuinely caring relationships. To feel genuinely cared for led to a sense of safety and partnership. Jane described a relationship she had with a nurse who made extraordinary efforts to bridge an interpersonal connection.

she organised these massive cook up breakfasts every weekend, and I know that was extra to what her role was. She would take me for a walk down behind the hospital, down the river, this bushy walk, and she would show me this kingfisher bird that she would whistle to, and it would whistle back. She obviously did this walk many times and had become friends with the kingfisher. She was a really lovely lady. That experience, like I wish I could find her and give her a hug and say thank you, because I don't know if nurses like her realise the impact of that. Surely she does, that's why she does it, but I would love for her to feel great, like the gratitude that I have for her, Jane.

To be treated with consideration and compassion fostered a sense of comfort, safety and collaboration. Whilst many examples of collaborative relationships were described, participants also described experiences where they felt that clinicians failed to engage interpersonally, compromising any sense of collaboration.

when you tried to get someone's attention to talk to and you're in that fishbowl environment of the mental health ward and you're experiencing a personal crisis, you need to talk to someone, you need to see someone, whatever you need to do, you tap on the glass and they walk off or they talk to each other, yeah, it's just feeling disconnected from the people in power. Yeah, I experienced that, Tom.

Doctors just feel like God and I think it's like a power complex that they have…I always find the nurses encouraging and sympathetic and like they cared about what I was saying. Whereas doctors I find that I just always felt belittled and unimportant and I felt like a patient… They're impossible to get a hold of. They—they're like untouchable, you know? There's very much a big power division between you and them, Gemma.

To be denied interpersonal warmth or attention was a harmful privation, leading to a sense of abandonment and neglect. The absence of an interpersonal connection limited the extent to which collaboration could occur.

Discussion

Despite a well‐defined policy emphasis and corresponding professional expectations for collaborative mental health care (Australian Government 2017; World Health Organization and United Nations 2023), the findings of this study indicate that consumers continue to experience barriers to meaningful collaboration in their treatment and care. Participants described systems in which power was concentrated in the hands of clinicians, with psychiatrists occupying the dominant positions of authority. Within these systems, decision‐making, particularly those related to medications, would often occur without adequate consultation. The findings are similar to previous research highlighting that despite collaborative mental health care now being a widely expected standard of mental health care, consumers experience systematic failures in this regard, particularly in inpatient settings (Huang et al. 2020; Pappa et al. 2021; Stead et al. 2017). Slade (2017) notes that institutional structures can harbour micro‐cultures that can be out of step with more broadly held cultural values. These cultures privilege clinicians' expertise and authority over consumers' values and treatment preferences (Slade 2017).

The pervasive nature of these oppressive micro‐cultures is explicable when considering the extensive history of immodest harmful practices within psychiatry (Zugai 2023). Dominated by a historically established medical model approach, institutional micro‐cultures lay emphasis on symptom management, coercive practices and the use of medication as the central aspects of treatment (Carr 2024). The dominance of this model over policy is reinforced by a powerful legal framework that dictates key elements within these services and privileges the power of some professional groups over others (Hyde et al. 2015; Pullman et al. 2025). Effective translation of policy rhetoric into culture change requires both organisational and clinician commitment to systemic reform (Pappa et al. 2021; Tse 2017). However, the findings of this research highlight that, from the perspective of consumer participants, this reform has yet to be fully realised. Entrenched institutional micro‐cultures, characterised by clinician‐dominated decision‐making, continue to hinder meaningful consumer collaboration and make systemic change elusive.

A specific barrier to collaboration identified by consumers was the scepticism with which their perspectives were received by clinicians. This disbelief is not simply the product of individual biases but reflects belief systems deeply embedded within micro‐cultures in inpatient mental health settings, where clinical authority overpowers the lived experience of consumers (Bennetts et al. 2011). Parker (2014) observes that how mental health clinicians understand and interpret consumer experiences is shaped by their training and professional socialisation. These experiences can be shaped by stigmatising discourses and practices which can exist in the mental health services, among mental health clinicians (Scholz et al. 2019). Clinicians can adopt the assumption that mental illness distorts the reality of consumer narratives (Parker 2014), which in our findings led to pervasive patterns of dismissal of the consumer's voice. Consumers described how such assumptions were enacted within everyday interactions with clinicians. Clinicians would question their credibility and reframe their concerns as symptoms of mental illness. Fricker (2007) developed the concept of epistemic injustice to explain this phenomenon, where people, often marginalised in society, are routinely dismissed or disbelieved.

Epistemic injustice was evident in our findings as consumers were determined by clinicians to be less credible knowers of their own realities. For consumers, this created experiences in mental health services that were characterised by exclusion, disempowerment and mischaracterisation. Dotson (2014) notes that epistemic injustice “can only begin to be addressed through recognition of the limits of one's overall epistemological frameworks” (2014, 116). For clinicians seeking to foster collaborative relationships with consumers, this underscores the need to critically reflect on the assumptions that guide their practice (Molloy et al. 2019). Addressing epistemic injustice requires systemic integration of consumer perspectives in the delivery, appraisal and development of mental health services. To this end, the authentic integration of peer‐support workers, shared decision making, recovery planning, advanced directives and co‐production of research and training are strategies for the meaningful integration of consumer perspectives (Chamberlin 2005; Happell et al. 2018; Slade et al. 2014; Woltmann and Whitley 2010).

The findings of this research emphasise the centrality of interpersonal relationships in shaping consumers' perceptions of collaborative care. As noted in previous research, the development of relationships, characterised by genuine trust, is crucial to establishing collaboration in mental health care (Scholz et al. 2019; Ventosa‐Ruiz et al. 2025). Within inpatient settings, even though clinicians' authority may be privileged, moments of authentic engagement stood out for consumers, helping restore trust and a sense of partnership. Conversely, those clinicians who were perceived as distant or indifferent undermined the possibilities of collaboration and reinforced patterns of exclusion and disempowerment in inpatient mental health care. Authentic engagement with compassion and without judgement can allow clinicians to understand what matters to the person, including their values, beliefs and preferred approach to care (Pratt et al. 2021). The findings reinforce that collaboration in mental health care is not solely a policy‐mandated process, but a meaningful interpersonal practice. The process of collaborating is beneficial, supporting recovery and informing wider care (Reid et al. 2018). Clinicians' willingness to engage with consumers as credible knowers of their own experiences is central to whether consumers feel genuine collaboration within their care.

Limitations

The use of purposive recruitment via social media offered an efficient means of engaging participants who met the study criteria; however, this approach is not without limitations. Participants who are active on digital platforms may be disproportionately represented, favouring individuals with higher digital literacy, stronger online social networks or greater confidence in virtual engagement. Consequently, the sample may not fully capture the experiences of those who are less technologically engaged or who occupy more marginalised positions, thereby constraining the representativeness of the findings. Furthermore, the data were derived exclusively from self‐reported accounts provided by individuals with psychotic illness residing in a single national context. The findings are then contextually bound and should be interpreted as reflecting experiences within that specific setting. While the Australian focus enables a detailed exploration of the local mental health system, this geographic and systemic specificity may limit the applicability of the findings to jurisdictions with differing legal frameworks, models of care or cultural understandings of mental health and involuntary treatment.

Furthermore, expanding future research to include multiple countries and culturally diverse contexts would enhance the broader relevance and transferability of insights into mandated mental healthcare. The study's reliance on English‐language participation further constrained inclusivity, excluding perspectives from individuals from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds. This omission is particularly important given well‐documented inequities in mental health service engagement and outcomes among these populations (Primm et al. 2010; Sanchez et al. 2016). Future investigations should prioritise inclusive research designs that enable participation across linguistic and cultural groups, as well as consideration for the implications of race ethnicity, sex or gender identity in interactions with clinicians. Additionally, integrating the perspectives of both consumers and mental health professionals in future work may offer a more comprehensive understanding of the ethical challenges associated with involuntary inpatient mental healthcare identified in this study.

Conclusion

While contemporary mental health philosophy and professional standards mandate collaboration, participants' descriptions of inpatient care were characterised by clinician‐dominated decision‐making, limited consultation and constrained autonomy. Shared decision‐making was frequently absent, particularly in relation to medication prescribing, where consumers felt excluded from dialogue about treatment rationale, risks and alternatives. Participants also described pervasive scepticism toward their perspectives, with clinicians frequently dismissing their accounts as manifestations of illness. This tendency toward a reflexive dismissal compromised trust, damaged therapeutic relationships and further impeded collaboration. Conversely, when clinicians engaged consumers with respect, compassion and genuine interpersonal presence, participants reported feeling safe, valued and able to collaborate in their care. Despite policy commitments to collaboration, entrenched institutional micro‐cultures continue to privilege clinical authority. By centring consumer perspectives, this study contributes important insights into the ethical and practical conditions required for meaningful collaboration in inpatient mental health settings.

Relevance for Clinical Practice

This study demonstrates the need for mental health clinicians and services to prioritise collaboration as a necessary aspect of care in the inpatient setting. Clinicians and services can enhance collaboration by advocating for measures that maximise autonomy, such as supported decision making and advance planning. Supported decision making is the inclusion of a chosen representative/advocate who will assist the consumer with making care related decisions, and advanced planning is the process of recording pre‐determined decisions in preparation for times of illness that would otherwise compromise the capacity for autonomous decision making (World Health Organization and United Nations 2023). These strategies enable clinicians and consumers to collaborate in such a way that consumers can exercise autonomy to a greater extent, limiting clinicians' control. Furthermore, the findings of this study demonstrate a need for an approach to inpatient mental healthcare that is duly considerate of risks, whilst also being interpersonally sound. Paternalistic measures are ethically problematic, compromising consumers' autonomy. However, there are circumstances that warrant care that is not negotiated. To minimise the ethical problems of paternalism, a ‘maternalistic’ approach should be adopted. Maternalism is distinct from paternalism, in that maternalism involves the presence of a trusting knowledgeable relationship as a guide to determining care (Specker Sullivan and Niker 2018; Zugai 2023).
