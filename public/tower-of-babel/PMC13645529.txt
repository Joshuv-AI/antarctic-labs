Music listening patterns and their associations with mood regulation, emotional processing, and cognitive performance: an intensive longitudinal questionnaire study

Yanyan Wang, Ge Cai, Tingting Wang

Abstract

BackgroundMusic is often played when university students are studying and during their daily activities, but most of the research to date in the field of music listening and academic performance has used self-report techniques. Implications for student wellbeing and academic success are summarized where listening patterns are used to understand mood regulation and academic cognitive performance in real-world settings.MethodThe design of this study was intensive longitudinal (IL) using experience sampling methodology (ESM) with event-contingent sampling over the 14-day period. Hundred and twenty-eight university students filled out questionnaires that measured musical sophistication, alexithymia, personality and trait mood regulation at baseline, and then answered short questions on 5,448 music listening episodes. Within-person and between-person associations in relation to academic versus non-academic contexts were examined using multilevel modeling.ResultsUsing music for mood regulation was the strongest predictor of mood improvement (β = 0.87, p < 0.001), followed by active enjoyment (β = 0.54, p < 0.001), while focus purposes predicted perceived academic cognitive performance (β = 0.56, p < 0.001). Musical sophistication moderated the mood regulation-mood change relationship (β = 0.22, p = 0.006), while alexithymia reduced its effectiveness (β = −0.19, p = 0.035). Lyrics presence was detrimental to cognitive performance during high-demand academic tasks (β = −0.28, p = 0.005), whereas faster tempos enhanced performance (β = 0.19, p = 0.035). Conscientiousness moderated the focus-cognitive performance relationship (β = 0.21, p = 0.018).ConclusionMusic listening patterns among university students are dynamically associated with mood regulation and academic cognitive performance in daily life, with effects moderated by individual differences including musical sophistication, alexithymia, and conscientiousness. These findings inform the development of personalized music-based interventions for student wellbeing and academic success.

Introduction

Music listening is a pervasive, goal-directed human behavior that engages auditory, affective, cognitive, and reward-related systems, making it a scientifically important context for studying mood regulation, emotional processing, and cognitive performance (; ; ; ; ). Mood regulation refers to the use of music to modulate, sustain, or regulate affective states, whereas emotional processing encompasses the perception, appraisal, and neural encoding of emotionally salient musical stimuli. Cognitive performance includes functions such as attention, working memory, learning, and executive control. Musical sophistication is defined here in terms of self-reported musical abilities, training, engagement, emotional response to music, and perceptual abilities assessed using the Goldsmiths Musical Sophistication Index (Gold-MSI) (). Alexithymia refers to difficulties in identifying, describing, and processing emotions and is assessed using the Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20).

Recent reviews indicate that listening is the dominant mode of music-based emotion regulation research, accounting for 85.1% of reviewed studies, and that the field has expanded rapidly since 2021, reflecting broad international interest in music as an everyday self-regulatory resource (). This is a global phenomenon as music listening is not only cheap and scalable, but also embedded in culture and widely accepted during periods of stress, social disruptions, and academic demands, such as university students and community sample across multiple countries (; ; ).

Self-report, experience sampling, psychophysiology, and neuroimaging techniques have been used together in recent studies to measurably advance our understanding of the impact of music listening and music cognition. While previous research has demonstrated the engagement of distributed emotion-related networks during music processing, the temporal dynamics and functional interplay among these neural systems underlying effective responses to music remain incompletely understood (). Neurophysiological evidence has also reported stronger and more consistent amygdala responses to live compared with recorded music, together with more widespread functional coupling across emotion-related systems during live music listening. These findings illustrate that emotional responses to music may depend on characteristics of the listening experience and its context.

Experimental and theoretical findings suggest that music influences cognition in a variety of ways: arousal processes may alter physiological and psychological activation, mood processes may influence positive mood states that can facilitate cognitive processing, and attentional processes may facilitate or interfere with limited cognitive resources depending on musical characteristics and task demands. These mechanisms are in line with arousal-mood and resource allocation theories (). Although music listening has been linked to various positive effects in student populations at the behavioral level calm, happy music has been found to decrease induced negativity compared to sad music (; ). In parallel, clinical and non-clinical studies have been reviewed to show that music activities can influence mood, arousal, attention, memory, executive processes, and well-being, but this influence is dependent on the context, and the musical form (; ).

Notwithstanding this development, literature is conceptually and methodologically disunited. While music emotion regulation is often considered synonymous with music emotion research, there is still definitional ambiguity linking these terms, inconsistent operationalization of ‘emotion’, and music treated as a single type of exposure in contrast to the potential heterogeneity of music listening characteristics such as genre, valence, tempo, familiarity, context, intentionality or self-selection (; ; ). Diverse motives and strategies for listening have been identified in questionnaire surveys, such as diversion, solace, discharge, memory, calmness, and mood matching, but some of these constructs are not empirically distinct, making it difficult to determine which listening characteristics may be associated with mental health outcomes (; ). Additional inconsistencies with regard to outcomes are also found: Overall music use to regulate emotions is not consistently related to depression or anxiety symptoms, however; mood-congruent music discharge is related to worse outcomes, and success in everyday affect regulation occurs in less than half of the listening episodes that were sampled and is strongly dependent on context and initial emotional state (; ). Furthermore, many studies utilize cross-sectional or retrospective self-report measures, which may lack representativeness and may not allow for causal inferences and ecological precision (; ; ).

There is a significant lack in the literature on the simultaneous investigation of music listening patterns, with regard to mood regulation, emotional processing and cognitive performance, in an ecologically valid framework. Past studies usually focus on a single outcome domain, are conducted in laboratory environments, employ retrospective recall techniques, and fail to consider individual difference moderators. To fill these gaps, the present study adopts an intensive longitudinal questionnaire design based on the Experience Sampling Methodology (ESM) over 14 days to measure the real-time music listening experience of university students. This methodological approach reduces reliance on retrospective recall, captures intra-individual variability, and allows examination of time-varying relationships. The novelty is the simultaneous investigation of several outcome domains in one analysis and testing of the role of the various factors of musical sophistication, alexithymia and personality traits as moderators.

The associations between personality traits and emotional regulation strategies and academic performance have been consistently found - particularly with the Big Five dimensions (Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism) (; ). Of these factors, conscientiousness is noteworthy, as it is a personality trait that reflects self-discipline, organization and goal-setting behaviors that can have a significant impact on the use of music for improving academic concentration and achievement. On the other hand, neuroticism might affect the use of music for emotional regulation, as individuals with high levels of neuroticism may experience greater emotional instability and may differ in their use of music to regulate emotions ().

This study aims to create a more in-depth understanding of the dynamic process in which university students use music to shape their emotions and thinking process within their daily lives. To achieve this objective, we used an intensive longitudinal experience sampling design over a 14-day period to capture music listening experiences in real time. Specifically, this study seeks to: (1) explore intra-personal relationships between listening goals, music features and effects (mood change and perceived cognitive performance in the classroom); (2) determine whether effects are moderated by individual differences (musical sophistication, alexithymia, personality traits); and (3) examine whether effects vary depending on academic versus non-academic settings.

Rationale

In individuals with higher musical sophistication, music’s ability to enhance mood will be dampened as they are more emotionally sensitive to music, have better perceptual and analytical processing skills to evaluate musical structures, and will have more of a history with using music as an emotional medium. These attributes may facilitate the ability of music-savvy individuals to more accurately listen, interpret, and react to the emotional content of music, which can lead to increased emotional benefits from listening to music (; ). On the other hand, alexithymia (defined as difficulties in emotional awareness and processing) can get in the way of the effective use of music to regulate mood. People with high alexithymia may not be able to identify how music impacts their emotional state or may be less likely to choose music that aligns with the way they need to regulate their emotions, leading to less effective music emotion regulation (; ).

Hypothesis

The following directional hypotheses are proposed based on the theoretical framework of the arousal-mood hypothesis, resource allocation theory, and individual differences models.

H1: Music listening for mood regulation will be positively correlated with mood improvement, while background listening will have weaker or non-significant correlations with mood change. This forecast is based on evidence showing that music listening with a purpose and intention leads to more emotional benefits than passive or incidental listening (; ).

H2: Results indicated that music use for mood regulation and mood improvement would be positively moderated by musical sophistication, such that those with higher level of musical sophistication would demonstrate greater mood-enhancing effects from music listening. This forecast is based on studies that musical expertise improves emotional sensitivity and the capacity to experience emotional benefits from music (; ).

H3: Music use for mood regulation will be negatively moderated by alexithymia scores, such that the more alexithymic (difficulty identifying and describing emotions) the less beneficial the mood regulation from music listening. This prediction is based on theoretical research indicating that dysfunction in emotional processing could hinder therapeutic outcomes of music (; ).

H4: The relationship between music listening and perceived academic cognitive performance will be mediated by music characteristics (lyrics presence and tempo) and academic context. Specifically, we hypothesize that listening to music with words will have a negative effect on cognitive functioning during tasks of high cognitive demand (consistent with the resource allocation theory), and that faster music tempos will be beneficial for cognitive functioning during tasks of high cognitive demand (consistent with the arousal-mood hypothesis). Additionally, we hypothesize that focus-purpose listening will be more predictive of cognitive functioning in academic situations than in non-academic situations.

Literature review

The relevance of music as a model of emotional-cognitive interactions was confirmed by describing music listening as a multi-regional process in the brain that is relevant to emotional and cognitive functions (). A review of 63 studies found that music activities, ranging from receptive listening to intentional listening, are correlated with various psychosocial processes, including arousal, mood, attention, memory, and social connection, thus creating a generalised framework for contemporary music-related well-being research (). Music listening has been the dominant theme in the emotion-regulation literature, and publications in the field jumped significantly from 2021 to 2024, reflecting both conceptual momentum and diversification of the methods used to study emotion regulation strategies (). Music listening was situated in the broader emotion-regulation literature, and it was highlighted that the Music in Mood Regulation paradigm encompasses multiple different ways of regulating emotions, rather than just a single one (). This position was extended by showing that some purported emotion regulation strategies are only partially distinct empirically, while qualitative data identified additional regulatory functions such as remembering, calming oneself, and matching mood ().

Real-time fMRI demonstrated that live music elicited stronger and more consistent amygdala activation than recorded music and engaged a denser affective neural network, suggesting that ecological musical interaction can shape emotional processing more strongly than static stimuli (). Calm and happy music more effectively reduced experimentally induced negative emotion than sad music, and further showed that negative mood interfered with conflict control performance on a Stroop task (). Brief exposure to music affected visuospatial working memory, and it was argued that emotional activation and central executive attention are involved in shaping downstream cognitive performance, although the observed advantage was composer-specific rather than uniform across musical conditions ().

Findings were nuanced by showing that in general there was no main effect of music on regulation intensity across strategies, but music decreased the perceived difficulty only in the reappraisal strategy, and effects were moderated by music sophistication and executive functioning (). It was observed that 92.3% of surveyed students listened to music while studying and that many perceived benefits for concentration, relaxation, and study persistence, yet those findings remained descriptive and self-reported rather than performance-verified ().

Experience sampling found that affect-regulation goals were achieved in less than half of everyday listening episodes and that success was strongly shaped by context and baseline affect, challenging overly general claims that music listening is uniformly effective (). Overall use of music as emotion regulation was not directly associated with depression and anxiety symptoms, whereas discharge-oriented listening showed positive associations with those symptoms through maladaptive regulation pathways (). Similarly, it was noted that distressed individuals tended to choose negatively valenced music that was not particularly effective for achieving well-being goals, even though music use overall was associated with positive affective change across countries (). Several limitations across the field were highlighted, namely, the lack of a good theoretical separation between music emotion and music emotion regulation, the use of inconsistently defined terms, as well as the fact that only a few of the intra-musical elements that could explain the differential effects were drawn (). Moreover, music preferences and listening contexts were associated with different psychological and behavioral patterns in students, thereby highlighting the importance of a multidimensional approach to listening behavior beyond simply counting the number of times students listen to music ().

To extend the definition of music’s role in regulation beyond mood improvement, the qualitative findings of university students were integrated with their synthesized coping model, which included three additional values: emotional awareness, perceived control and interoceptive engagement (). A large Brazilian cross-sectional sample showed that people with severe depressive symptoms more often used music for comfort, anxiety reduction, boredom relief, and cognitive stimulation, and their factor analysis distinguished negative mood management, positive mood management, cognitive functioning, and physical involvement as separable listening functions ().

Convergent evidence from systematic reviews indicated that music-based interventions can support attention, executive function, and episodic memory, but those findings derive largely from therapeutic or cognitively impaired populations and therefore cannot be directly generalized to healthy everyday listeners (; ). It was further concluded that music-related benefits for executive functions are heterogeneous across interventions and cognitive domains, which strengthens the rationale for population-specific, ecologically grounded questionnaire studies of ordinary listening patterns (). A cross-sectional framework was outlined in which frequency, duration, genre, and listening context can be linked to perceived focus, efficiency, retention, and qualitative motives, supporting the present study’s integrative design while leaving the empirical question open ().

Taken together, the recent literature supports the view that music listening is a multifaceted self-regulatory behavior with measurable associations across emotional and cognitive domains, but it also shows that outcomes depend on strategy, listener characteristics, context, and the specific listening pattern under study (; ; ). The perspective of an active listener as an active regulator of internal states, as advocated by Reybrouck et al., is a more encompassing homeostatic one that is congruent with contemporary research on contextual and goal-dependent variability (). The importance of designs that reflect the natural contexts of auditory perception and separation of beneficial and maladaptive regulatory uses of media was emphasized, particularly in the face of interindividual differences in symptoms, goals, and demands of the context (; ). This study fills this gap by exploring music listening behaviors, as well as their relationships with mood regulation, emotional processing, and cognitive performance under a single framework, and expands previous research that has tended to study these fields in isolation or with more limited measures of music-related behaviors (; ; ).

Methodology

Study design

This study employed an intensive longitudinal design using Experience Sampling Methodology (ESM) with event-contingent sampling. Participants completed an initial baseline questionnaire followed by brief in-the-moment surveys triggered by music listening episodes over a 14-day period. This design is selected for its ability to capture the dynamic, context-dependent nature of everyday music listening, minimize retrospective recall bias, enable multilevel modelling to distinguish within-person from between-person effects, and provide ecological validity through data collection in natural environments. Event-contingent sampling ensures that data are collected specifically for music listening episodes rather than at random intervals, maximizing the relevance and efficiency of data collection. Data collection spanned 14 consecutive days, including both weekdays and weekends to capture variability in listening patterns across different academic and social contexts.

Participants

A target sample of 120–150 university students was planned for this study. With an expected completion of approximately 3–5 listening episodes per participant per day over 14 days, this yielded approximately 5,040–10,500 listening episodes for analysis, providing sufficient power for multilevel modelling. Participants must be currently enrolled university students aged 18 years or older, be regular music listeners who listen to music at least once per day, own a smartphone capable of receiving survey notifications, be willing to complete brief surveys during or immediately after music listening episodes, and be proficient in the language of the survey materials. Students currently receiving treatment for serious mental health conditions that may affect music listening experiences were excluded. Participants were recruited through university research participation programs, campus-wide email lists, student organization announcements, and social media platforms targeting university student populations.

Measures

Baseline questionnaire

Testing for the 14-day ESM period occurred after the baseline questionnaire is completed, which assessed stable individual difference variables. Academic information (i.e., year of study, field of study, grade point average (GPA), typical amount of study hours/week) was gathered with custom items. Demographic data (such as age, gender and music listening history) also was gathered. Musical sophistication was measured using the Goldsmiths Musical Sophistication Index (Gold-MSI), a 39-item self-report inventory that assesses musical skills, training, engagement, emotions, and perceptual abilities. The Gold-MSI has demonstrated good internal consistency in previous research (Cronbach’s α = 0.85–0.92 for subscales) and correlates with objective measures of musical ability.

Emotional processing was assessed using the Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20), a 20-item measure of difficulty identifying and describing feelings, and externally oriented thinking. The TAS-20 has shown good psychometric properties across diverse samples, with established three-factor structure and internal consistency typically exceeding α = 0.80. Personality traits were measured using the Ten-Item Personality Inventory (TIPI), a brief measure of the Big Five personality dimensions. Despite its brevity, the TIPI has demonstrated adequate test–retest reliability and convergent validity with full-length personality measures in university student samples. Trait mood regulation using music was assessed with the Music in Mood Regulation (MMR) Scale, a 21-item measure of specific mood regulation strategies employed through music listening. The MMR scale has demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency across its subscales (α = 0.74–0.79) and has been validated in multiple cultural contexts. A comprehensive summary of all study measures and instruments is presented in .

Experience sampling questionnaire

The experience sampling questionnaire was administered each time a participant reports a music listening episode. Items are brief to minimize participant burden while capturing key variables. Contextual information included the time of the listening episode, location with categories including home, campus or library, commuting, exercise, social setting, and other, primary academic-related activity with categories including studying, attending lectures, completing assignments, commuting, exercising, relaxing, socializing, and other, and companionship status with categories including alone, with friends, with classmates, with family, with partner, in public, and other. An additional item assessed whether music listening occurred during academic-related activities versus non-academic activities.

Music characteristics were assessed through items on genre selected from a list of common music genres, familiarity rated on a scale from 1 (not at all familiar) to 7 (very familiar), enjoyment rated on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much), presence of lyrics with response options of yes, no, or instrumental, tempo rated on a scale from 1 (very slow) to 7 (very fast), and source of music selection with categories including playlist created by the participant, algorithm recommendation, friend or other person, radio, and other.

Listening purpose and intentionality were assessed by asking participants why they chose to listen to music, with the option to select all that applied from mood regulation, background, active enjoyment, focus or concentration (including studying), social or cultural, and other. Intentionality was rated on a scale from 1 (not at all deliberate) to 7 (very deliberate). Mood assessment was conducted both before and after listening to capture mood changes. Pre-listening mood was assessed for valence with the question “Before listening, how did you feel?” rated on a scale from 1 (not at all positive) to 7 (very positive) and for arousal with the question “Before listening, how did you feel?” rated on a scale from 1 (not at all energetic) to 7 (very energetic). Post-listening mood was assessed similarly with items asking “Right now, how do you feel?” for both positivity and energy levels.

Perceived academic cognitive performance was assessed through items on perceived focus during academic tasks with the question “During this listening episode, how focused were you on your academic task?” rated on a scale from 1 (very distracted) to 7 (very focused), perceived task performance with the question “How well were you performing your academic task while listening?” rated on a scale from 1 (very poorly) to 7 (very well), and cognitive demand of academic activities with the question “How cognitively demanding was your primary academic activity?” rated on a scale from 1 (not at all demanding) to 7 (extremely demanding). Emotional regulation was assessed through items on mood regulation goal asking whether participants intended to change their mood through music with response options of yes, no, or unsure, and mood regulation success rated on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 7 (completely) in response to the question “To what extent did music help you achieve your mood goal?”

Procedure

Pre-study phase

Potential participants completed a brief screening survey to assess eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria including current university student status. Eligible participants were provided with detailed information about the study and asked to provide informed consent electronically. Following consent, participants completed the baseline questionnaire online, which took approximately 20–30 min. After completion of the baseline questionnaire, participants received training and orientation on the ESM procedure, covering how to access the online survey platform, when to complete listening episode surveys, best practices for timely completion, and common troubleshooting issues. Finally, participants were shown how to set up the system, putting the selected ESM platform on their phones or saving the link to the web-based survey for quick access.

The complete list of ESM survey items and response options was enlisted in , and the survey flow diagram was shown in . All ESM surveys were conducted on the Qualtrics survey platform (Qualtrics, Provo, UT) via a mobile-friendly web link participants saved to their mobile phone to complete as many daily as possible at the same time (morning and evening).

Experience sampling phase

The experience sampling phase lasted for 14 days and used event-contingent sampling. After starting a music listening session, the participants were directed to the survey on the designated platform to fill out the first part of the survey, which included measures for initial mood, contextual and expectancy measures. Following the listening episode, participants filled out a short post-listening questionnaire, which measured post-listening mood, listening goals, music characteristics, and perceived academic cognitive outcomes. The first survey section measured initial mood, context and expectations. Participants filled out a short follow-up questionnaire after the listening episode to evaluate their mood after listening, the goal of the listening activity, the characteristics of the music, and their perceived academic cognitive outcomes after listening. The survey was designed for completion within 5 min of the listening episode. Participants were encouraged to complete surveys for all music listening episodes throughout the 14-day period. Regular reminder notifications were sent to participants, but these were not excessive to avoid burden. Participants received clear instructions and training, incentives for consistent participation such as course credit or monetary compensation, and prompt support for technical issues.

Post-study phase

Following the 14-day ESM period, participants completed a short debrief questionnaire asking about their experience with the ESM procedure, including items assessing whether the surveys influenced their listening habits as a reactivity check. Participants received compensation proportional to their completion rate, either course credit or monetary compensation depending on the recruitment channel.

Data analysis

Compliance criteria

A priori, a compliance rate of 20% of the expected number of surveys (based on the number of days participants were active in the study and an expected number of surveys of 3–5 per day) was set for inclusion in the final analytical sample, which was based on the criterion that compliance rates in previous ESM studies usually range between 10 and 20% of expected surveys, and thus yield reliable multilevel estimates ().

Missing data handling

To deal with missing data, multiple imputation with chained equations (MICE) was used for baseline variables where less than 5% of data were missing. For ESM variables, missing data were addressed at the level of the multilevel models by full information maximum likelihood estimation (FIML), which provided an estimation of the model parameters using all available data without needing to delete individual observations. To assess the robustness of the results to handling of missing data, the models were compared with and without imputation, via sensitivity analyses.

Preliminary and multilevel analyses

The data had a hierarchical structure with listening episodes at Level 1 nested within participants at Level 2. Preliminary analyses included descriptive statistics and scale reliability calculated using Cronbach’s alpha for multi-item measures at baseline. Intraclass correlation coefficients were calculated to quantify the variance at Level 1 (within person) compared to the variance at Level 2 (between persons). Data were analyzed using a multilevel or mixed effects model, which allowed for the partitioning of within-person from between-person effects and took account of the nested nature of the data. Within-person changes in mood and perceived academic cognitive performance were the primary focus of analysis, with listening purpose, music characteristics, and context as predictors. All continuous Level 1 predictors were person-mean centered to separate within-person from between-person effects. Cross-level interactions were tested to examine whether individual differences including musical sophistication, alexithymia, personality, and academic year moderated the within-person associations between listening patterns and outcomes.

Contextual and temporal analyses

Contextual effects were examined to determine whether associations differed by academic versus non-academic activities. Temporal dynamics were modeled to examine time trends in listening patterns and outcomes across the 14-day period, with particular attention to patterns during examination or high-stakes academic periods.

Model estimation and secondary analyses

Models were estimated using Restricted Maximum Likelihood or Maximum Likelihood estimation in statistical software such as R using the lme4 package or SPSS using the MIXED or GENLINMIXED procedures. Secondary analyses included exploratory factor analysis to examine the factor structure of listening purposes and music characteristics, mediation analysis to test whether mood change mediated the relationship between listening purpose and perceived academic cognitive performance, and growth curve modeling to examine whether the effect of music listening on outcomes changed over the course of the study period.

Statistical analyses

The multilevel models testing our hypotheses are the key analyses, but supplementary exploratory analyses were also carried out to gain more insights into the data structure. In particular, exploratory factor analysis was performed to validate our measurement approach by analyzing the factor structure of listening purposes and music characteristics. As exploratory follow up analyses, mediation analyses were performed to assess whether the relationship between listening purpose and perceived academic cognitive performance is mediated by mood change. Such analyses were not focus of the hypothesis testing, but were complementary to the primary analyses in order to gain additional insights into the mechanisms underlying the observed effects. All exploratory analyses are separated from primary hypothesis-testing analyses in the result section.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 128 university students completed the baseline questionnaire and commenced the 14-day experience sampling period. The final analytical sample consisted of 124 participants who completed at least 20% of expected ESM episodes, yielding 5,024 listening episodes for analysis. The CONSORT flow diagram () depicts participant recruitment, enrolment and completion across the study. Participant ages ranged from 18 to 35 years with a mean age of 21.4 years (SD = 3.2). The sample was approximately balanced by gender, with 58 males (45.3%), 65 females (50.8%), and 5 individuals identifying as non-binary or other (3.9%). Regarding year of study, 25.0% were first-year students, 21.9% were second-year students, 18.8% were third-year students, 17.2% were fourth-year students, and 17.2% were graduate or postgraduate students. In terms of field of study, 32.8% were enrolled in humanities or social sciences, 29.7% in STEM fields, 21.9% in business or economics, 9.4% in arts or music, and 6.3% in other fields. The mean grade point average was 3.2 (SD = 0.5) out of 4.0, and participants reported studying an average of 18.4 h per week (SD = 8.7). The baseline characteristics are presented in .

Participants reported a mean daily music listening duration of 3.2 h (SD = 2.1) and a mean of 4.2 years (SD = 5.1) of formal musical training. The Goldsmiths Musical Sophistication Index total score averaged 81.8 (SD = 18.6) out of a possible 126, indicating moderate to high levels of musical sophistication in the student sample. The mean score for the Toronto Alexithymia Scale was 49.2 (SD = 11.5) indicating relatively low alexithymia scores. Overall, the personality trait scores were not extreme or high, with openness (M = 5.3, SD = 1.2) and agreeableness (M = 5.0, SD = 1.3) having the highest mean scores. The most common mood regulation strategies with music measured with the Music in Mood Regulation Scale were using music for solace (M = 3.8, SD = 0.9) and entertainment (M = 3.9, SD = 0.9). provides the conceptual framework for the study.

Descriptive statistics of ESM variables

Descriptive statistics and intraclass correlation coefficients for all variables from the experience sampling method for the 14-day study period are reported in . The mean number of listening episodes participants completed per day was 4.3 (SD = 1.9 between-person; SD = 1.6 within-person) and the mean length of the listening episodes was 27.8 min per episode (SD = 13.8 between-person; SD = 11.5 within-person). Participants reported moderate to high familiarity of musical characteristics (M = 5.3, SD = 1.3), high enjoyment (M = 5.7, SD = 1.1) and moderate tempo (M = 4.5, SD = 1.4). On the intentionality of music choice scale, the mean score was 4.1 (SD = 1.6) which represents moderate deliberation in music selection. As seen in , listening episodes were evenly distributed across the 14 days of the study with more on the weekdays than the weekends of the study.

Before listening, the mood valence was moderately positive (M = 4.2, SD = 1.3) and the mood arousal was moderately positive (M = 4.0, SD = 1.4). After music listening, valence (M = 5.1, SD = 1.2) and arousal (M = 4.7, SD = 1.3) both significantly increased compared to pre-listening levels [valence: t(123) = 12.45, p < 0.001; arousal: t(123) = 8.92, p < 0.001]. The mean scores of perceived academic cognitive performance variables were in the moderate to high range; perceived academic focus (4.3, SD = 1.5), academic task performance (4.5, SD = 1.4), and academic cognitive demand (4.6, SD = 1.5). Mood regulation success averaged 4.5 (SD = 1.5), suggesting moderate perceived effectiveness of music for mood regulation among university students.

Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) represent the proportion of total variance attributable to between-person differences. To express this as a percentage of variance, the ICC value must be squared (ICC2 × 100). For example, an ICC of 0.41 indicates that 16.8% (0.412 × 100) of the total variance is attributable to between-person differences, while the remaining 83.2% is attributable to within-person fluctuations. In the present study, ICCs showed that enjoyment (ICC = 0.37) and familiarity (ICC = 0.41) were relatively more trait-like, whereas perceived academic focus (ICC = 0.26) and intentionality (ICC = 0.28) exhibited greater state-like fluctuations, with between-person variance accounting for only 6.8–16.8% of total variance and the remainder due to within-person changes. This substantial within-person variability justified the use of multilevel modeling to separately examine within-person and between-person effects on outcomes such as mood and perceived academic cognitive performance. The ICC values thus confirmed that meaningful variation in outcomes was statistically explained by fluctuations in listening patterns and contexts within individuals over time.

Academic versus non-academic listening patterns

Multilevel model predicting perceived academic cognitive performance

The multilevel model results for perceived academic cognitive performance are shown in . The model included Level 1 predictors including listening purposes, music characteristics, academic cognitive demand, pre-listening mood, and academic context, as well as Level 2 predictors including musical sophistication, conscientiousness, and grade point average, and cross-level interactions. The model accounted for significant variance in perceived academic cognitive performance, with random effects showing that 6.25% of the variance was attributable to between-person differences (ICC = 0.0625).

At the within-person level, using music for focus purposes was the strongest positive predictor of perceived academic cognitive performance (β = 0.54, p < 0.001). In addition, more positive intentions (β = 0.16, p = 0.008) and enjoyment (β = 0.20, p = 0.004) significantly predicted higher perceived academic cognitive performance. The academic task difficulty was negatively correlated with perceived academic cognitive performance (β = −0.43, p < 0.001), which was in line with the results indicating that task difficulty is an important contextual factor affecting students’ academic experiences. Most important, the effects of music characteristics were also strongly moderated by cognitive demand (see cross-level interactions): Lyrics presence was especially harmful and faster tempo was especially helpful when the cognitive load of the academic task was high. Higher pre-listening mood valence (β = 0.22, p < 0.001) and arousal (β = 0.17, p = 0.005) also significantly predicted better perceived academic cognitive performance. Importantly, academic context was a significant predictor of lower perceived academic cognitive performance (β = −0.26, p = 0.004) which suggested that students perceived cognitive performance as lower during academic context than non-academic context. Background listening (β = −0.07, p = 0.379), active enjoyment (β = 0.09, p = 0.317), tempo (β = 0.03, p = 0.617), and lyrics presence (β = −0.01, p = 0.912) did not significantly predict perceived academic cognitive performance.

At the between-person level, musical sophistication (β = 0.20, p = 0.013), conscientiousness (β = 0.30, p = 0.001), and grade point average (β = 0.24, p = 0.017) significantly predicted better perceived academic cognitive performance. The results suggest that overall, university students with higher musical sophistication, higher conscientiousness, and higher GPAs positively perceived their cognitive performance in most of the academic listening episodes.

There were large moderation effects for academic cognitive demand and music characteristics found in the cross-level interactions. Music with lyrics significantly interacted with academic cognitive task difficulty (β = −0.26, p = 0.009), such that academic cognitive task difficulty was especially associated with academic cognitive performance of music with lyrics. In contrast, an interaction between tempo and the academic cognitive demand was significant in the opposite direction (β = 0.18, p = 0.046), faster tempo was associated with improved academic cognitive performance under the high- vs. low-demand task. The grouped bar chart in indicates that music without lyrics was perceived as significantly lower than instrumental music in terms of perceived academic cognitive performance during studying and academic activities. The scatterplot in indicated that perceived academic task performance was related to tempo for low, medium and high cognitive demand academic tasks, and that optimal tempo for academic cognitive performance was different across each of these three task types.

Temporal dynamics of mood change

The trajectory of mood-change scores across the 14-day study period indicated that the effectiveness of mood regulation remained relatively stable over time, with no evidence of habituation or adaptation. This stability extended across both weekdays and weekends, suggesting that the positive effect of music on mood regulation does not diminish with repeated use and is not strongly dependent on day of the week or academic scheduling.

Discussion

Significant findings

The present study used an intensive longitudinal design to examine the bidirectional connections of music listening habits, mood regulation, emotional processing and perceived academic cognitive functioning in students’ everyday lives. The results are very clear and confirm that music listening has a number of psychological roles and that listening to music for different purposes yields different effects (). In particular, the use of music for mood regulation and active enjoyment was most strongly related to improvements in mood and the use of music for concentration and focus was related to an improvement in perceived academic cognitive performance. These findings corroborate previous cross-sectional studies that have shown that these relationships occur across the individual, by demonstrating that these relationships occur at the within-person level, which reflects the changing levels of mood and cognitive functioning within the natural setting of academic tasks (; ). The large intra-person variability of all ESM variables (from 83.2 to 93.2%) provides evidence of the dynamism of the music listening behavior and its psychological effects, thus suggesting that the processes occurring in music listening should be captured in real time instead of relying solely on retrospective recall (; ; ; ).

One of the contributions of the current study is the determination of individual difference variables that moderate the effectiveness of music listening in university students. Additionally, greater mood improvements occurred when using music for emotion regulation (β = 0.21, p = 0.009), such that musical sophistication emerged as an important moderator of the mood regulation benefits of music. This discovery is consistent with theoretical arguments that expert musicians might be more adept at experiencing emotional reactions to music and extracting emotional benefits from it, which could be due to a higher level of emotional sensitivity, perceptual skills, and cognitive involvement in music structures. In contrast, higher alexithymia was correlated with lower effectiveness in mood regulation (β = −0.18, p = 0.046), suggesting that impaired emotional processing could hinder therapeutic benefits of listening to music. These findings are important for the university student population, as emotional regulation issues could affect academic performance and mental health. Furthermore, the link between focus purpose and academic cognitive performance was significantly moderated by conscientiousness (β = 0.20, p = 0.020), with students scoring higher in conscientiousness deriving greater cognitive benefits from using music to focus on academic tasks. The results are consistent with self-regulation theories, and indicate that individual differences are important to take into account in research as well as applied interventions.

A surprising result was that students felt their cognitive functioning to be poorer in academic contexts than in non-academic contexts (β = −0.26, p = 0.004). This paradoxical finding is that students feel they perform worse when listening to music during an academic task, compared to when listening to music during a non-academic task, could indicate that students are more aware of their performances and more critical of them during the academic task. Students may be more aware of their academic output when engaged in academic activities, and more likely to feel any distraction (such as music) is a negative effect. The finding may alternatively be because academic work is more cognitively demanding and this could lead to less attentional resources available for one’s own performance. This is in line with resource allocation theories which argue that when tasks are challenging, people might not be able to rate themselves favorably.

This study also uncovered important context-specific effects on academic cognitive performance with implications for understanding how music is utilized in academic contexts by university students (). Music with lyrics was especially harmful for perceived academic cognitive performance during high-demand academic tasks (β = −0.26, p = 0.009), and faster tempo was associated with better academic cognitive performance during high-demand tasks (β = 0.18, p = 0.046). The present results are in line with the arousal-mood hypothesis that posits that music can facilitate cognitive performance when arousal is optimized, and with resource allocation theories that propose that the number of cognitive resources allocated to a task is limited and can be allocated to music lyrics during such tasks. The results of this study suggest that there is a significant association between academic context and lower perceived cognitive performance (β = −0.26, p = 0.004), suggesting that pupils know the cognitive demands of what they are doing in their studies and are likely to adapt their listening behaviors accordingly. The results showed that listening time was longer when engaging in non-academic tasks, and intentionality was higher when listening to music during academic tasks, indicating that students’ music listening behaviors are strategically adjusted depending on the cognitive demands of their tasks, listening longer and more relaxed when engaging in non-academic tasks and more attentively when engaging in academic tasks ().

This study is considered to have advanced a methodology in music psychology and educational research: intensive longitudinal methodology. The study piloted the capture of music listening experiences in real-time over a period of 14 days to reduce retrospective recall bias and to offer ecologically valid data with regard to music listening behaviors in the everyday lives of university students. The authors employed the multilevel modeling approach to distinguish within-person from between-person relationships, and identified mostly within-person relationships between listening patterns and outcomes (i.e., listening patterns and mood and cognitive performance varied within persons across listening episodes and days). Stable differences between individuals (such as musical sophistication, alexithymia and conscientiousness) moderate the strength of within-person relationships, which does not contradict the importance of individual differences. The results have implications for theory and practice because they indicate that music listening interventions should be directed at immediate decision-making choices (within-person effects) while also accounting for stable individual characteristics (between-person differences) that moderate these effects, rather than assuming universal effects across all individuals (). The study focuses on university students, as this is an environment where music is extensively used, and provides a much-needed evidence-based advice on how to use music optimally for academic achievement and wellbeing.

Theoretical implications

The results have implications for a number of theoretical frameworks in music psychology and educational psychology. First, the present study extends and confirms the arousal-mood hypothesis by showing that music effects on cognitive performance are context-dependent: optimal music features can differ depending on task requirements. The effects of lyrics presence and tempo on perceived academic cognitive performance are consistent with the resource allocation models that suggest that lyrics during difficult tasks may take up limited cognitive resources, and music tempo may influence arousal state that may support or impair cognitive performance depending on the nature of the task. Given that the current study measures perceived rather than actual cognitive performance, however, the results are tentative support for the resource allocation theory. To determine if these effects carry over to actual academic performance, future research with objective measures of cognitive performance (e.g., standardized test scores, reaction time tasks, or real-time accuracy assessments) is warranted (; ). Second, the results are applicable to self-regulation theories because of the ability to demonstrate how students are adjusting their music listening behaviors strategically in relation to the academic requirements. The findings revealed that in the academic activities, students’ music selection was more intentional while listening time was longer during non-academic activities, suggesting that students’ music listening patterns were “strategically” adjusted depending on the cognitive demands of their task.

Third, musical sophistication, alexithymia, and conscientiousness further expand individual differences theories by pinpointing certain traits that can impact mood regulation and cognitive functioning by music (). Fourth, the difference in the meanings for mood regulating purposes (predicting mood outcomes) versus focus purposes (predicting cognitive outcomes) is consistent with a functional view of music listening, indicating that the various uses of music can have different psychological functions. Lastly, the study contributes to theoretical research on the dynamic and context-dependent nature of music listening by showing that music listening variability within a person is an important predictor of mood and cognitive performance variability, underscoring the importance of capturing music listening in naturalistic contexts.

It is important to discuss the moderation of the relationship between focus-purpose listening and academic cognitive performance by conscientiousness. Conscientious individuals are known for their greater self-discipline, organization, and persistence with goals (; ). These are the characteristics that could account for why highly conscientious students do better with music as a tool to help them concentrate and focus on academic work. Conscientious students may be more likely to (1) strategically choose music to enhance rather than detract from their academic objectives, (2) keep focus on the task at hand while there is music present, (3) use music monitoring as a strategy to facilitate their task completion, and (4) stop listening to music when it hinders their work. Less conscientious students, however, may be more prone to be distracted by music or less likely to develop self-regulatory skills needed to maximize listening for learning. This is consistent with the wider self-regulation literature, which posits that self-control and goal-directed behavior vary among individuals and moderate the impact of environmental cues on performance outcomes.

Practical implications

The results have some significant implications for research, practice and student wellbeing. First, differential prediction of mood and academic cognitive outcomes by listening to music reveals that interventions should be tailored to their goals. For students who are looking to improve their mood, they can listen and enjoy music for their mood; for students who are aiming to improve their academic cognitive performance, they may listen to music for focus, but consider musical qualities like avoiding lyrics during cognitively demanding tasks. Second, the moderating effect of the factors of musical sophistication, alexithymia, and conscientiousness indicates that music-based interventions should take individual differences into account when it comes to musical background, emotional processing skills, and personality traits. The more alexithymic students might need extra guidance to gain advantages from music use for mood regulation; the more conscientious students might be able to employ the cognitive advantages of music for academic tasks.

Third, lyrics and tempo effects were found to be context dependent, which has practical consequences for the creation of optimal listening environments in educational contexts; that is, the type of music used may improve concentration and academic performance. Fourth, the difference between academic and non-academic contexts in terms of listening patterns and outcomes underscores the importance of making recommendations for the use of music that are specific to the context. Finally, the study findings over the 14 days suggest that music listening continued to be effective at sustaining mood throughout the study period, meaning that music is a reliable, accessible emotion regulation strategy that does not become less effective with repeated use, which can be a helpful tool for students’ wellbeing and mental health (; ; ).

Limitations

There are some restrictions to be noted. First of all, the sample was composed of university students only and the results may not be generalizable to other groups, such as non-student young adults, older adults, children, and clinical samples. Secondly, the study was entirely self-report and may have been subject to common method bias, although this was minimized by the intensive longitudinal design, which also minimizes retrospective recall bias. Third, the event-contingent sampling method may have led to selection bias, as it is possible that students may have been more likely to complete surveys on more salient and intentional listening episodes, potentially oversampling the more salient and intentional listening experiences. Fourth, the 14-day study duration was sufficient to capture within-person variability but may not have captured longer term patterns or seasonal variation in music listening, by including examination periods or breaks in academic schedule (; ).

Fifth, only perceived academic cognitive performance, not objective cognitive performance, was assessed, so the conclusions reached about the actual effects on cognitive functions are limited. Sixth, findings might not be generalizable to other cultural settings and educational environments, given differences in music listening practices and school environments between different cultures. Seventh, the study did not evaluate the quality or content of academic tasks (e.g., whether tasks required deep processing, rote memorization, creative thinking, or problem-solving). The nature of the academic task could moderate the impact of music listening on cognitive performance, as different task types may have different attentional requirements. For example, music with lyrics might be particularly detrimental to reading comprehension tasks but less so to mathematical calculations. Future research should incorporate task quality and content assessments to better understand these nuanced relationships. Eighth, the sample size was sufficient for the multilevel modeling, but might have been too small to detect smaller effect sizes and more complex interaction effects.

Future work

There were a number of important directions for future research based on these findings. First, studies should use objective measures of academic cognitive performance (standardized test score, reaction time task, or real-time assessment of task accuracy) in addition to self-report measures and get more objective measures to support the cognitive effect of music listening. Second, passive smartphone sensing could be combined with ESM to obtain objective listening information such as song metadata, listening time, and listening patterns through passive measurements, which would experience less sampling error and distortion in listening behavior data than self-initiated surveys (; ; ). Third, all connections should be explored in clinical populations that might benefit from music-based interventions (students with depression, anxiety, attention disorders, etc.) and that may be most relevant for emotional processing and personality (as moderators).

Fourth, greater longitudinal studies are needed to explore changes in patterns of music listening and psychological impact over longer time scales, since this would provide a better understanding of whether such patterns are stable or fluctuate over longer time periods, such as across academic semesters or examination periods, or across different phases of academic life. Fifth, the findings of this study which relate listening purposes and musical characteristics to one another naturally could be used in experimental studies as a basis for establishing causal relations. Sixth, cross-cultural studies would be useful to see if these findings would apply to other cultural contexts, educational systems, and musical traditions, and if the impact of music on mood and cognition is different in different cultures. Seventh, studies should focus on elucidating the mechanisms responsible for the effects observed, such as understanding the neural correlates, physiological reactions, and cognitive processes that underlie the link between music listening and psychological outcomes. Lastly, intervention studies could be developed and tested that would provide personalized music-based interventions for student wellbeing and academic success, with a focus on specific listening strategies and music characteristics that are based on individual differences and context.

Conclusion

This is an intensive longitudinal study showing the dynamic relationship of music listening to mood regulation, emotional processing, and the perceived academic cognitive performance in everyday life among university students. Music use for mood regulation and active enjoyment was most strongly associated with mood improvement and music use for focus was associated with academic cognitive performance. The more musically sophisticated, the more effective the mood regulation was, whereas the more alexithymic, the less effective it was. The relationship between music listening and academic cognitive performance varied in accordance to the task demand and lyrics presence and tempo.

Conscientiousness and grade point average moderated the cognitive benefits of using music for focus purposes. The academic versus non-academic setting was a significant factor in the listening patterns and the resulting outcomes, as students listened strategically to tasks. The results of this study indicate that in order to better understand the psychological effects of music listening at the university level, both context and individual differences should be taken into account, and that interventions focused on music can be developed to promote student wellbeing and academic achievement at the individual level. The study helps to further understand how music is listened to, and changes with the context, and offers evidence-based recommendations for students to maximize their listening experiences to regulate mood and enhance academic studying.
