Estimating the hazard ratio of excess mortality using a microsimulation framework with golden section search calibration: a methodological primer
Authors: Reda Rhellab, Maximiliaan L Notenboom, Jonathan RG Etnel, Jolanda Kluin, Ewout W Steyerberg, Johanna JM Takkenberg, Kevin M Veen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExcess mortality in a population with a disease can be defined as extra mortality observed compared to the matched-general population, and expressed as a hazard ratio (HR). While excess hazard models and cure models exist, these commonly need patient-level data. In this paper, we show a method for estimation of excess mortality with aggregated data.MethodsWe detail the estimation of excess mortality in a microsimulation framework using the golden section search algorithm. This iterative algorithm identifies a local minimum within a predefined search interval. The overall excess mortality and residual excess (remaining mortality after accounting for modelled events) is estimated by calibrating microsimulation-derived survival curves to observed patient-sample survival curves. R code is provided. We apply the method to a study of aortic valve repair in non-elderly adults.ResultsThe method enabled efficient estimation of excess mortality, expressed as overall and time-dependent hazard ratios through a piecewise function. The estimated excess mortality HR in the case-study was 2.70 without accounting from modelled events and 2.43 while accounting for modelled events, and the HR increased over time (1.23; 2.32; 5.50 at 0–60 months, 61–120 months and 121–188 months, respectively).ConclusionsThis microsimulation framework provides an accessible, efficient method for estimating time-varying excess mortality hazard ratios, allowing for estimation of residual excess mortality, which excludes mortality due to modelled events, using only aggregated input data.Keywords: Microsimulation, Calibration, Golden section search, Optimization, Excess mortality

Introduction

Excess mortality is typically defined as the number of deaths observed beyond what would be expected in a given population [1]. While it is commonly used to assess excess deaths following acute mortality crises (e.g. pandemics or natural disasters), it can also be applied in the context of (chronic) diseases. In such settings, it helps answer questions like: What is the excess mortality in a population with a disease compared to a matched-general population? Is excess mortality diminishing following an intervention, essentially establishing cure of the disease?

Over time, excess mortality in a population with a disease may increase or decrease due to factors such as disease progression, treatment failure, or recovery, either spontaneous or due to intervention(s). Several cure models have been developed to capture these dynamics, but they often require detailed patient-level data [2, 3].

In another application, researchers may be interested in quantifying the residual excess mortality after accounting for deaths attributable to specific events. This is particularly relevant when trying to determine whether observed excess mortality in a population with a disease can be fully explained by those events and their downstream effects. A microsimulation framework can be especially useful in addressing these complex scenarios because of its ability to simulate patient cohorts based on aggregate data inputs, and ability to capture the downstream effects of modelled events.

In the current report, we aim to explore estimation of the excess mortality hazard ratio (HR) with aggregated data only. Hereto, we use a microsimulation framework, with and without direct mortality due to modelled events. We demonstrate the application of the golden section search in a microsimulation model based on a previous study in non-elderly adults with aortic valve interventions [4, 5]. It should be noted that this work serves as a methodological primer for applied researchers and does not seek to identify or advocate for an optimal methodology.

Methodology

Microsimulation framework

In microsimulation, or individualized state-transition models, a transition probability refers to the probability to transition from one predefined health-state to another. These states can represent various conditions, such as health, sickness, and death. Tutorials to understand and implement microsimulation in R-language have been published previously [6, 7]. A transition probability for death can described by the general-population mortality rate (also known as background mortality) multiplied by a disease-specific HR (also known as the excess mortality HR) (Eq. 1), which is subsequently transformed to a probability (Eq. 2). In a microsimulation framework, mortality due to other modelled events can be modelled separately, excluding these deaths from the estimated excess mortality, which we will call residual excess mortality here for defining purposes [7]. In the current example the separately modelled events refer to postoperative complications (endocarditis and prosthetic valve dysfunction). Estimating the excess mortality HR is commonly done by generating multiple microsimulation-based survival curves using different HRs of excess mortality and calibrating these to observed patient-sample survival curves. The calibration is based on minimizing the distance between the patient-sample survival curves and microsimulation-based survival curves by a link function (i.e. the sum of squared distances).

Golden section search

The golden section search is an existing methodology originally published by Kiefer [5]. In a golden section search a set of values are iteratively divided into two sections, based on the golden ratio. This mathematical constant, where the ratio of the whole to the larger part is the same as the ratio of the larger part to the smaller part, allows one to efficiently determine the minimum value of the set. First, the lower and upper bounds of a function f(X) are defined, e.g. a and b, respectively. The value of the interval between a and b (c) is divided according to the golden ratio (≈ 1.618) into a larger interval (d) and a smaller interval (e). Subsequently d is added to a, yielding a value of X1 and e is added to a, yielding a value of X2. The values of f(X) at X1 and X2 are then compared. If the value of f(X1) > f(X2) all values larger than X1 are eliminated and X1 becomes the new upper bound (Fig. 1). If the value of f(X1) < f(X2) all values lower than X2 are eliminated and X2 becomes the new lower bound. By iteratively performing this process the range becomes increasingly narrow until convergence and a minimum is found.

Golden section search in microsimulation calibration

To apply the golden section search to the calibration of clinical microsimulation modelling, two steps need to be undertaken. First, the function to be minimized should be defined. In the case of clinical microsimulation, the minimization object is the sum of squared distances between a microsimulation-based survival curve and an observed mortality curve. To generate this function, the microsimulation is run iteratively, while varying the HR within pre-specified bounds (Fig. 2). The algorithm will find a local minimum within the pre-specified bounds. In each iteration the sum of all squared differences for each time cycle/interval between the microsimulation-based curve compared to the observed mortality curve is calculated and stored. A range of estimators or predicted survival curves can be used as reference (target) curves. Second, a golden section search can be performed on this function. Moreover, by separating the observed survival curve into segments, a piecewise-constant HR can be estimated. Confidence interval

The golden section search generates point estimates. However, the uncertainty around the input that is being calibrated to can be used to generate posterior distributions for the estimated HR. For example, sampling from beta distributions (proportions), lognormal distributions (rates). This would amount to a probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA), of which tutorials have been previously published [9]. If individual patient data is available bootstrapping can be used, where a large number of parametric survival models can be generated and for each model a HR of excess mortality can be estimated.

Practical illustration

To illustrate the method, we use a microsimulation model aimed at estimating long-term outcomes after heart valve intervention, based on a previously published microsimulation framework from a study of aortic valve repair (AVr) in non-elderly adults [4, 7]. This model was developed according to the coding principles proposed by the Decision Analysis in R for Technologies in Health group [4, 6]. The input parameters are analogous to the study data and are provided in Table 1. In the microsimulation model including separately modelled events, the microsimulation contains two health states (Alive and Dead) and includes constant risks of early mortality, endocarditis, and constant rates of late reintervention, endocarditis and valve dysfunction. In the model without separately modelled events the late events are set to zero (Table 1). The excess mortality was derived by calibrating the microsimulation to a pooled Kaplan-Meier (KM) generated according the method developed by Guyot et al. [10]. The general-population mortality data is directly obtained from the Human Mortality Database lifetables [10]. In the practical example we focus on the point estimate estimation and leave the PSA out of scope. The codes are shared on GitHub(https://github.com/kevinmveen/excessmortmicrosim) and contain notes for future users.

Validation and efficiency golden section search algorithm

To illustrate that the golden section search yields appropriate estimates, it was compared with an incremental search. In this simple approach, the lower and upper bounds of the HR search are defined together with the step size of the search. The microsimulation is then run for each candidate value between the lower and upper bound. The candidate value associated with the lowest sum of squares is taken as the optimal calibrated HR within the prespecified bounds. Because all candidate values within the search range are evaluated, the incremental search will, by definition, identify the optimum at the chosen resolution (step size) and therefore provides a useful reference for validating the estimates obtained with the golden section search. However, because all possible values must be assessed, runtimes are expected to be considerably longer. For the incremental search, runtimes depend on both the step size used in the calibration and the width of the predefined bounds. For the golden section search, runtimes are determined by the stopping tolerance, which can be specified manually in the R code. In the practical illustration, we report the difference in runtime between the two approaches. The configurations of both search methods are specified in Table 2. To allow for a fair comparison, all analyses were performed on a dedicated computer optimized for R analyses (Intel Core i9-13900KS, 128 GB RAM, Nvidia RTX 4070).

Validation and running times

The estimated excess mortality HRs between the incremental search and golden section search were similar, confirming that the golden section search yields appropriate estimates (Table 2). In the used configurations, runtimes were considerably shorter for the golden section search, despite wider bounds and higher resolution.

Discussion

In epidemiological studies estimation of excess mortality is essential to assess burden of disease in relation to the matched-general population. The current study provides an effective approach in R using the established golden section search when only aggregated inputs are available, with code and guidance for applied researchers.

Effectiveness and use-cases

The golden section search can be efficiently integrated with existing microsimulation frameworks for estimating the HR of excess mortality. As expected, the golden section search yields results that are similar to those of incremental calibration in much shorter computation time. In most contemporary microsimulation studies, the HR of excess mortality was assumed to be constant over time [4, 11, 12]. However, in practice, this HR may often be time-varying.

The golden section search can be used to efficiently calibrate the HR of excess mortality for multiple segments of a curve. An example was demonstrated in Fig. 4. The flexibility of the R software functions in choosing a different number of segments with their respective cut-offs, allows for easy estimation of the HR for an extensive range of patient samples. This method might be especially useful for diseases for which a cure is possible and for simulating survival after invasive interventions, which are known to have varying excess mortality over time [13]. The patterns of excess mortality observed over time can help researchers determine underlying driving factors of mortality. In this practical example, the HR of excess mortality increases over time, which, as mentioned in Sect. 3.2, may be due to recurrent aortic valve disease increasing in a non-linear fashion and being accompanied by other valve-related events that are not modelled, or to declining heart function in this population over time.

Additionally, the difference between the excess mortality and residual excess mortality, (i.e. between the estimated excess with and without modelled events), may be of interest in epidemiological studies. This difference can help provide an insight into the part of all mortality ascribed indirectly and directly to the disease/intervention versus the residual excess where the events generally associated with disease/intervention are modelled separately. In the practical example we see that the excess mortality HR was 2.70 whereas the residual excess mortality HR was 2.43. This indicates that although the modelled events explain a small proportion of the excess mortality, the majority is not explained by these events and thus comes from different underlying causes. Quantifying this difference may be especially important in health technology assessment, where innovations are often designed to reduce specific events, while in practice these events may not contribute substantially to observed excess mortality.

The comparison with the incremental search in Table 2 further illustrates the practical advantages of this approach. Because the incremental search evaluates every candidate value, it identifies the optimum at the chosen resolution and therefore provides a reference against which the golden section search can be validated; the golden section search did this far more efficiently, reaching the optimum in 14 microsimulation runs and under two minutes, compared with up to 500 runs and almost two hours for the incremental search at the same resolution, and while using wider bounds and an equal or finer tolerance. This efficiency makes fine-resolution calibration feasible even when it must be repeated many times, for example within a probabilistic sensitivity analysis.

Other methods for estimating excess mortality

Other methods for estimating excess mortality that rely on Esteve models (Cox-type models) are typically fitted using individual patient data, whereas the golden section search method can also be applied to aggregated data (i.e. meta-analysis data) [14, 15]. With the advent of methods to reconstruct approximate individual patient data from published Kaplan–Meier curves, however, relative survival and other Cox-related survival models can in some cases also be applied to data that were originally available only in aggregated form [16]. Cox-type models are widely used because they allow for estimation of the effect of covariates on the hazard function, providing interpretable hazard ratios associated with patient characteristics such as age, sex, disease stage, or treatment. In the present study, a single estimated excess mortality parameter is applied to the simulated population. However, microsimulation frameworks naturally allow transition probabilities to vary according to patient characteristics. Therefore, the proposed calibration approach could in principle be extended to estimate the excess mortality stratified for different patient groups. If individual patient data is available for example, this could be done by fitting a parametric survival model with a number of relevant covariates to the survival data. The microsimulation can subsequently be calibrated to the survival curves of each combination of relevant covariates using the golden section search. In this way such extensions could allow calibration of multiple excess mortality parameters corresponding to different risk groups, thereby combining the flexibility of microsimulation modelling with the ability to reflect heterogeneity in patient populations. Moreover, by using the golden section search within the microsimulation framework, mortality due to specific events relevant to the disease/intervention can be modelled separately. The calibration using the golden section search then yields a residual excess mortality. This is not possible when using cox-type models to estimate excess mortality.

A major advantage of (more recent) cox-type models, such as Touraine models [15], is their ability to account for potential misspecification of the background mortality derived from population life tables. In these models, the general population mortality can be adjusted through additional parameters, allowing the analysis to account for differences between the study population and the general population used to construct the life tables. This is particularly relevant when patients with a given disease differ systematically from the general population with respect to characteristics associated with mortality risk, such as socioeconomic status, smoking behaviour, or comorbidities. By jointly modelling excess mortality and potential deviations in background mortality, these approaches can provide more accurate estimates of disease-related excess risk. While the microsimulation framework allows considerable flexibility in modelling disease progression and competing risks, the joint estimation of excess mortality and background mortality correction parameters, as implemented in Touraine-type models, is not yet developed within this framework. Extending microsimulation calibration methods to incorporate such adjustments represents a potential direction for future methodological development.

Other optimization methods

Additionally, other optimization methods besides the golden section search exist (including: Newton’s method, Brent’s method, Secant Method, Nelder-Mead, Gradient Based, Genetic Algorithms, Simulated Annealing). The golden section was chosen since only a single parameter is to be optimized (excess mortality), it can handle noisy data, has minimal assumptions about functional form and does not require derivatives.

Limitations

Importantly, when performing a golden section search, the upper and lower bound of the function, i.e. the interval between the minimal and maximum HR that one expects the optimized value to be in, has to be defined. If the true HR value falls outside of the predefined bounds, the estimation will not be valid. This can be bypassed by increasing the interval between the upper and lower bound to such a degree that it contains all realistic values for the HR. However, this can result in longer run times of the algorithm. Additionally, the golden section search can only be used for single parameter optimization and generates a point estimate.

Also, we optimized the squared distance between survival probabilities. Other loss functions might be used, e.g. log-based loss function. Finally, the limitations for the general microsimulation framework, e.g. misspecification of matched-general-population mortality apply for this method [8].We used a multiplicative model for excess hazard, while an additive model could also be explored [17, 18].

Conclusion

Excess mortality in populations with a disease can be estimated using a microsimulation framework based on aggregated data using golden section search based calibration. The time-varying estimation of excess mortality and residual excess mortality after accounting for modelled events may answer several relevant epidemiological research questions.
