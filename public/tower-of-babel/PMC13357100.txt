A LASSO-based nomogram for predicting breast cancer-specific survival in elderly patients after surgery: a large retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ziqiang Wang, Haojie Zhang, Qianqian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate prognostic assessment for elderly patients with breast cancer remains challenging, as the conventional tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system is limited in capturing individual heterogeneity. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-Cox-based nomogram for predicting breast cancer-specific survival (BCSS) in elderly patients.MethodsA total of 69,137 eligible patients diagnosed between 2010 and 2015 were identified from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database and randomly divided into training (70%) and validation (30%) cohorts. LASSO regression was applied for variable selection, followed by multivariable Cox proportional hazards modeling to construct a nomogram. Model performance was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and net reclassification improvement (NRI) were used to compare predictive accuracy with the TNM staging system.Results11 variables were identified by LASSO selection and entered into the multivariable Cox model, including surgery, age, tumor grade, TNM stage, radiotherapy, hormone receptor status, primary site and marital status. The nomogram demonstrated acceptable discrimination (training C-index: 0.807) and satisfactory calibration. DCA suggested a higher net clinical benefit of the nomogram compared with TNM staging across a range of threshold probabilities.ConclusionsThis LASSO-Cox-based nomogram provides a prognostic model for estimating BCSS in elderly patients and may assist postoperative risk stratification in elderly patients.

Highlight box

Key findings

• We developed and internally validated a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-Cox prognostic nomogram to predict breast cancer-specific survival (BCSS) in 69,137 elderly patients. The model (C-index: 0.807) integrates 11 key predictors including surgery, age, tumor grade, tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage, radiotherapy, hormone receptor status, primary site and marital status, and shows improved performance compared with TNM staging across multiple evaluation metrics (decision curve analysis, integrated discrimination improvement, and net reclassification improvement).

What is known and what is new?

• Traditional TNM staging relies strictly on anatomical features, inadequately capturing the physiological heterogeneity, treatment modalities, and sociodemographic disparities unique to older adults.

• This study adds a robust, multidimensional prognostic tool tailored specifically for the elderly demographic. Utilizing LASSO regression on a large Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results cohort reduces overfitting and enables construction of an interpretable scoring system.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The nomogram provides a practical, point-based tool that may support postoperative risk stratification in elderly breast cancer patients. It may help integrate multiple clinicopathological factors for individualized clinical assessment. Future studies should focus on multicenter external validation and incorporate competing risk analyses to further refine prognostic evaluation in geriatric oncology.

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most common malignancies and a leading cause of cancer-related incidence and mortality among women worldwide (1). With the exacerbation of global population aging, the incidence of breast cancer in the elderly (typically defined as ≥65 or 70 years of age) continues to rise (2), making the disease management and prognostic evaluation of this specific population particularly crucial (3). Compared with their younger counterparts, elderly patients with breast cancer exhibit high physiological heterogeneity, often presenting with multiple comorbidities, declined organ functional reserves, and potentially poorer tolerance to antineoplastic treatments (4). These collective factors contribute to significant individual variations in postoperative survival outcomes. Therefore, developing an accurate, interpretable, and clinically practical prognostic tool is of great significance for assisting individualized treatment decision-making, risk stratification management, and the optimization of follow-up strategies.

Currently, the prognostic assessment of breast cancer patients heavily relies on the traditional tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system. However, earlier editions primarily depended on anatomical staging methods, which have demonstrated limited predictive accuracy, particularly in elderly cohorts (5). While the Cox proportional hazards model is widely applied in survival analysis, it is susceptible to issues such as multicollinearity, high-dimensional covariates, and overfitting in small samples. When handling complex clinical data, these limitations may lead to inadequate model stability and generalizability. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, as a robust regularization method, offers a reliable approach for prognostic modeling. By shrinking coefficients and conducting automated variable selection, it effectively reduces model complexity, prevents overfitting, and enhances generalizability, making it particularly suitable for large-scale clinical datasets with high-dimensional features.

In recent years, screening key prognostic factors via LASSO regression and subsequently constructing a nomogram have emerged as an important paradigm in cancer prognostic modeling (6). Nomograms visualize multifactorial regression results into an intuitive, quantifiable scoring system, enabling clinicians to rapidly estimate the probability of cancer-specific survival (CSS) for individual patients. This graphical calculator translates complex statistical models into a clinician-friendly tool, achieving an optimal balance between precision and ease of use.

However, there is still a relative scarcity of LASSO-Cox-based nomogram models specifically targeting breast cancer-specific survival (BCSS) as the primary endpoint in elderly patients. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a prognostic model for predicting BCSS in elderly postoperative breast cancer patients using data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Independent prognostic factors were identified using LASSO-Cox regression, and a nomogram was constructed to estimate individualized survival probabilities. The model’s discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility were systematically evaluated to assess its potential value in risk stratification and personalized postoperative management. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0530/rc).

Methods

Data source and population

Data for elderly patients (aged ≥70 years) diagnosed with breast cancer between 2010 and 2015 were extracted from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program database (www.seer.cancer.gov) using SEER*Stat software (version 9.0.42.2). The specific dataset accessed was the Incidence - SEER Research Data, 17 Registries, Nov 2024 Sub (2000-2022). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Patients were included based on the following criteria: (I) female sex; (II) age at diagnosis ≥70 years; (III) year of diagnosis between 2010 and 2015; (IV) histologically confirmed breast cancer; (V) primary tumor site coded as C50.0–C50.9 per the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O); (VI) specific histology codes including 8500/3 (infiltrating duct carcinoma, NOS), 8520/3 (lobular carcinoma, NOS), 8522/3 (infiltrating duct and lobular carcinoma), and 8523/3 (infiltrating duct mixed with other types of carcinoma); and (VII) complete follow-up information. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) lack of surgical treatment; (II) missing key clinical variables; and (III) a survival time of 0 months.

The sample size for this study was strictly determined by the total number of eligible patients available in the SEER database between 2010 and 2015 who met the prespecified inclusion criteria. In the training cohort, a total of 5,315 breast cancer-specific mortality events were observed against 15 candidate predictors. This yielded an events-per-variable (EPV) ratio of approximately 354, which exceeds the conventional minimum threshold of EPV recommended for developing robust multivariable prediction models.

A detailed flowchart illustrating the patient selection process is presented in Figure 1. Ultimately, a total of 69,137 eligible patients were enrolled in this study. To establish and validate the prognostic nomograms for BCSS, the final cohort was randomly allocated into a training cohort and an internal validation cohort at a 7:3 ratio.

Variables and outcome

The characteristics of elderly patients with breast cancer were described using demographic, clinicopathological, treatment, and survival variables extracted from the SEER database. All variables were defined and categorized according to SEER coding standards.

Demographic variables included age at diagnosis, race, and marital status. Age was stratified into five groups: 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, 85–89, and ≥90 years. Race was classified as White, Black, and other (which includes American Indian/Alaska Native and Asian/Pacific Islander).

Tumor-related characteristics encompassed the primary tumor site, laterality, histologic subtype, tumor grade, and molecular marker status (ER, PR, and HER2). Primary tumor sites were categorized as the upper-inner quadrant (UIQ), lower-inner quadrant (LIQ), upper-outer quadrant (UOQ), lower-outer quadrant (LOQ), axillary tail, overlapping lesions, central portion, and breast, NOS. Histologic subtypes were identified using ICD-O-3 codes (8500/3, 8520/3, 8522/3, and 8523/3). Tumor grade was classified well differentiated (Grade I), moderately differentiated (Grade II), and poorly differentiated/undifferentiated (Grade III/IV).

Treatment variables included surgical management, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. Surgical treatment was categorized based on SEER codes into partial mastectomy, total mastectomy, and modified radical mastectomy. Radiotherapy and chemotherapy were recorded as yes or no. All candidate predictors were handled as categorical variables. For the LASSO and multivariate Cox regression models, all categorical variables were entered using dummy coding, with the lowest clinical risk category or the most frequent category designated as the reference group. Predictor assessment was based on routinely collected registry data, and blinding of predictor assessment was not applicable.

The primary study endpoint was BCSS, defined as the time from diagnosis to death attributable to breast cancer. Patients alive at the last follow-up or those who died from other causes were censored. Blinding of outcome assessment was not applicable in this study because the outcome was an objective endpoint directly extracted from the SEER registry.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2). Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages, with group comparisons evaluated using the chi-square test. Missing data were addressed via complete-case analysis. Because of the large initial SEER cohort, excluding patients with incomplete records did not adversely affect statistical power.

For variable selection, a LASSO-Cox regression analysis was applied with 10-fold cross-validation. The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was defined using the 1-standard-error (lambda.1se) criterion to ensure a parsimonious and robust model. Variables with non-zero coefficients were subsequently incorporated into a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional hazards assumption was verified for all variables using Schoenfeld residual tests. Based on this final Cox model, a prognostic nomogram was developed to predict 3- and 5-year BCSS. The exact regression coefficients and mathematical formulas are provided in the Appendix 1.

Model performance was comprehensively evaluated in both the training and validation cohorts. Discrimination was measured using the concordance index (C-index) with 1,000 bootstrap resamples. Calibration was assessed using 3- and 5-year calibration curves, while clinical utility was examined through decision curve analysis (DCA). To benchmark the nomogram against the conventional TNM staging system, the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and net reclassification improvement (NRI) were calculated.

For risk stratification, the optimal cutoff value for the nomogram score was determined using maximally selected rank statistics (via the ‘surv_cutpoint’ function). Patients were then categorized into low- and high-risk groups, with survival differences compared using Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curves and the log-rank test. The validation cohort was generated by randomly splitting the entire dataset, ensuring that candidate predictors and outcome definitions were identical to the development cohort. Prognostic predictions for the validation cohort were derived directly from the training cohort’s mathematical equation without recalibration. All statistical tests were two-sided, with P<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

A total of 69,137 eligible elderly patients with breast cancer were included in this study, with 7,604 breast cancer-specific deaths observed during the follow-up period. The patients were randomly allocated into a training cohort (n=48,395; 5,315 BCSS events) and a validation cohort (n=20,742; 2,289 BCSS events). To provide broader clinical context regarding the baseline mortality burden in this elderly population, we evaluated overall survival (OS) and median survival time across age strata (Table S1). The median survival time for the entire cohort was 94 months, which steadily declined from 102 months in the 70–74 years age group to 43 months in the ≥90 years age group (P<0.001). Notably, non-cancer mortality increased substantially with advancing age. While the breast cancer-specific mortality rate modestly increased from 8.72% (ages 70–74 years) to 18.61% (ages ≥90 years), the all-cause mortality rate increased from 30.56% to 91.17% across the same age spectrum (P<0.001). The two cohorts were well-balanced, with no statistically significant differences observed across all variables (all P>0.05). Variable selection and model construction

In the training cohort, the LASSO-Cox regression was used to screen candidate variables and reduce overfitting. The coefficient profiles of these variables are illustrated in Figure 2A. Using 10-fold cross-validation, the optimal penalization parameter (λ) was determined based on the minimum partial likelihood deviance (Figure 2B). From this process, 11 variables with non-zero coefficients were identified as potential predictors, including age, surgery, marital status, histological grade, T stage, N stage, M stage, radiotherapy, primary site, ER status and PR status. These selected variables were subsequently entered into a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model (Figure 3). In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, age, histological grade, T stage, N stage, M stage, ER status, PR status, radiotherapy and marital status showed statistical significance (P<0.05). Regarding the primary site, while most quadrants (UIQ, LIQ, LOQ, and Central portion) did not differ significantly from the UOQ, the axillary tail (HR =1.408, 95% CI: 1.157–1.714, P<0.001) and breast, NOS (HR =1.258, 95% CI: 1.129–1.401, P<0.001) were identified as independent risk factors for poorer BCSS. Notably, while surgery did not reach statistical significance (P>0.05) in the multivariable model, it was retained in the final nomogram. This decision was based on the LASSO selection and its established clinical importance in breast cancer management.

Nomogram construction and validation

A prognostic nomogram was constructed to estimate the 3- and 5-year BCSS probabilities for elderly breast cancer patients by integrating the independent prognostic factors identified in the multivariable Cox model (Figure 4). Each predictor was assigned a weighted point value proportional to its regression coefficient. The cumulative score for an individual patient, calculated by summing the points of all variables, corresponds to predicted survival probabilities.

The developed nomogram for predicting 3- and 5-year BCSS in elderly patients. To use the nomogram, locate the patient’s value on each variable axis, draw a vertical line upwards to the “Points” axis to determine the score for each variable, sum all the points, and draw a vertical line downwards from the “Total Points” axis to estimate the 3- and 5-year BCSS probabilities. BCSS, breast cancer-specific survival; ER, estrogen receptor; LIQ, lower-inner quadrant; M, metastasis; N, node; NOS, not otherwise specified; PR, progesterone receptor; T, tumor; UIQ, upper-inner quadrant; UOQ, upper-outer quadrant.

The nomogram demonstrated good discrimination, with an apparent C-index of 0.807. Internal validation via 1,000 bootstrap resamples yielded an identical optimism-corrected C-index of 0.807, suggesting relatively stable internal performance. Calibration performance was evaluated using bootstrap resampling with 1,000 repetitions (Figure 5). The calibration curves for both 3- and 5-year BCSS demonstrated high concordance between the nomogram-predicted probabilities and the actual outcomes observed by the K-M method. To further assess the predictive performance of the nomogram, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed and compared with the traditional TNM staging system (Figure 6). This analysis indicated that the nomogram showed improved AUC values compared with TNM staging. Specifically, the 3-year AUC was 0.853 (95% CI: 0.835–0.871) for the nomogram versus 0.790 for TNM stage. Similarly, the 5-year AUC was 0.845 (95% CI: 0.831–0.861) compared with 0.784 for TNM staging.

Clinical utility and risk stratification

The clinical utility of the newly developed nomogram was evaluated using DCA and compared with the TNM staging system. As illustrated in the 3- and 5-year DCA plots (Figure 7), the nomogram (red line) showed a higher net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities compared to the TNM staging system (blue line). To further quantify this incremental value, IDI and continuous NRI were calculated. Compared to the TNM staging system, the nomogram was associated with improved predictive performance at 3 years (IDI: 0.050, 95% CI: 0.045–0.057; NRI: 0.351, 95% CI: 0.332–0.379; P<0.001) and at 5 years (IDI: 0.055, 95% CI: 0.050–0.063; NRI: 0.324, 95% CI: 0.304–0.345; P<0.001). These improvements indicate that the nomogram provided better risk estimation and improved risk reclassification performance.

The x-axis indicates the threshold probability, and the y-axis indicates the net benefit. The grey line represents the “treat-all” strategy, the horizontal black line represents the “treat-none” strategy, the red line represents the novel nomogram, and the blue line represents the traditional TNM stage. Based on the clinical utility established above, a risk stratification system was developed. The selected cut-off value for the risk score was identified as 0.93 using maximally selected rank statistics, corresponding to a total score threshold of 181.1 on the nomogram. Accordingly, patients were stratified into low-risk (total points ≤181.1) and high-risk groups (total points >181.1). K-M survival analysis confirmed that patients in the high-risk group experienced poorer BCSS compared to those in the low-risk group (P<0.001, Figure 8). This risk-stratification capability underscores the potential of the nomogram to guide personalized follow-up and treatment intensity in elderly breast cancer patients.

Validation of the nomogram in the internal validation cohort

To verify the stability and reliability of the constructed nomogram, its predictive performance was further evaluated in the internal validation cohort (n=20,742). As depicted in Figure 9, the 3- and 5-year calibration curves demonstrated excellent agreement between the nomogram-predicted survival probabilities and the actual observations, indicating optimal and reproducible calibration. Furthermore, applying the established risk stratification criteria, patients in the validation cohort were stratified into low-risk and high-risk groups. K-M survival analysis (Figure 10) revealed a significant difference in BCSS between these risk groups (log-rank P<0.0001), confirming the model’s prognostic stratification capability. Finally, DCA in the validation set corroborated the findings from the training cohort, showing that the nomogram provided higher net clinical benefit than the default ‘treat-all’ and ‘treat-none’ strategies across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Figure 11). Collectively, these results indicate the robustness, reproducibility, and clinical practicality of the proposed predictive model.

Discussion

Key findings

We developed and internally validated a prognostic nomogram for predicting BCSS in 69,137 elderly patients using the LASSO-Cox regression method. By integrating demographic, clinicopathological, and treatment-related variables, our model demonstrated acceptable discrimination and calibration across both training and validation cohorts (C-index: 0.807). DCA suggested that the nomogram offers additional clinical net benefit across a broad range of threshold probabilities, showing improved net benefit compared with the traditional TNM staging system. Furthermore, the score-based risk stratification system categorized patients into high- and low-risk groups with clearly separated prognostic outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several methodological strengths and limitations. Utilizing the large-scale SEER database ensures adequate statistical power. Applying LASSO regression minimized multicollinearity and prevented overfitting. Furthermore, internal validation using an independent cohort and multiple metrics (C-index, DCA, IDI, NRI) demonstrated the model’s performance.

However, important limitations must be acknowledged. First, an independent, multicenter external validation cohort is lacking, which is required to evaluate the model’s generalizability. Second, the retrospective nature of the SEER database results in a lack of detailed clinical data relevant to geriatric oncology. Specifically, the database does not include information on comorbidity indices, detailed systemic therapy regimens, or baseline functional status. These omissions limit the clinical applicability of the model, as physicians must manually integrate these unmeasured frailty metrics into real-world clinical decision-making. Finally, by exclusively predicting BCSS, the model does not account for the elevated risk of non-cancer-related mortality in the elderly. As our supplementary data demonstrates, this competing risk is substantial; therefore, clinicians must carefully contextualize the nomogram score with the patient’s overall life expectancy to avoid overtreatment.

Comparison with similar research

Currently, prognostic evaluation for breast cancer relies heavily on the TNM staging system. Because this anatomical-based system does not account for patients’ underlying biological characteristics, treatment modalities, and sociodemographic disparities, its predictive utility may be limited in the elderly population. While recent efforts have employed machine learning or multivariable modeling to improve prognostic accuracy (7), most of these studies have been limited by small sample sizes, a lack of independent validation, or poor model interpretability. In contrast to previous research, our model focuses exclusively on the elderly demographic and incorporates a multidimensional array of treatment and sociodemographic factors alongside tumor stage. By integrating multiple clinicopathological variables, the nomogram demonstrated improved discrimination compared with conventional TNM staging system in this cohort.

Explanations of findings

The prognostic indicators identified in our study align with established biological and clinical paradigms. Advancing age is significantly correlated with poorer prognosis, likely due to diminished physiological reserve, a higher burden of comorbidities, and reduced treatment tolerance in older patients (8). Similarly, elevated tumor grade and advanced TNM stage reflect heightened tumor aggressiveness and metastatic potential, leading to inferior survival outcomes. In terms of anatomical location, our multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that patients with primary tumors located in the axillary tail faced a significantly increased risk of breast cancer-specific mortality, which was 40.8% higher compared to those with tumors in the upper outer quadrant (UOQ) (HR =1.408, 95% CI: 1.157–1.714, P<0.001). This finding is consistent with previous research (9). A previous study has demonstrated that axillary tail tumors exhibit aggressive biological behavior, accounting for the highest proportions of Grade III histology (39.5%) and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) subtypes (16.9%) across all primary breast sites(9). This suggests that the poorer prognosis associated with axillary tail tumors may be driven by their elevated malignancy and the lack of effective therapeutic targets. Consequently, tailored systemic treatment regimens may warrant consideration in clinical practice for patients presenting with primary breast cancers in the axillary tail. Conversely, radiotherapy emerged as a significant protective factor. This aligns with previous studies demonstrating that appropriately selected elderly breast cancer patients who undergo radiotherapy show different 5-year survival rates compared to those who do not (10). This finding underscores that elderly patients can still derive substantial survival benefits from standard local therapies, and chronological age alone should not dictate the omission or de-escalation of radiotherapy. Furthermore, marital status, which has been consistently identified as an independent protective factor across multiple studies (11), may serve as a proxy for robust social support, better treatment adherence, and timely healthcare utilization (12). Collectively, these findings suggest that survival in elderly breast cancer patients is governed not solely by tumor biology, but is intricately tied to treatment paradigms and psychosocial dynamics.

Implications and actions needed

Clinically, the proposed nomogram may provide additional support for risk stratification in elderly breast cancer patients. By converting regression coefficients into a point-based tool, clinicians can estimate an individual patient’s 3- and 5-year BCSS probabilities at the bedside. This capability may support shared decision-making and follow-up planning in conjunction with established clinical factors. For elderly patients, optimizing the balance between treatment efficacy and tolerance is important. Our model aids in pinpointing high-risk individuals who may benefit from tailored, intensive management within existing treatment guidelines, while potentially sparing low-risk patients from the toxicities of overtreatment, thereby supporting personalized care and the judicious allocation of healthcare resources.

To translate these findings into broader clinical practice, future research must prioritize external validation across multicenter cohorts. Additionally, future iterations of the model should incorporate competing risk analyses to better account for the high rate of non-cancer-related mortality in older adults. The integration of detailed clinical variables, including comorbidity profiles and specific targeted therapy data, may help further refine personalized prognostic evaluations.

Conclusions

In summary, our study provides an internally validated prognostic model that may assist postoperative risk assessment in elderly breast cancer patients.
