Abstract
Recent studies at high redshift have revealed an enigmatic class of Little Red Dots (LRDs) with extreme Balmer breaks, stronger than in any stellar atmosphere. However, it is unclear whether such objects exist at lower redshift, especially given the low number of LRDs reported at z ≲ 2 z\lesssim 2 . Here we report the discovery of PAN-BH*-1, an LRD with an extreme Balmer break at z = 1.73 z=1.73 , identified from JWST/NIRCam pure-parallel imaging taken by the PANORAMIC survey, and confirmed by deep VLT/X-Shooter spectroscopy. The rest-optical to near-infrared spectral energy distribution of PAN-BH*-1 is consistent with a photospheric continuum with effective temperature T eff ≈ 4800 T_{\rm eff}\approx 4800 K. The broad H α \alpha emission line shows remarkably deep absorption, stronger than previously measured in any LRD. The absorption trough spans from − 520 -520 km/s to + 267 +267 km/s with respect to the systemic redshift. The presence of blue- and red-shifted absorption suggests complex dynamics of the obscuring gas along the line of sight. We speculate that the absorption trough can be produced by a thick wind launched from a thick, rotating photospheric disk, the latter being the source of the red optical continuum. While the source is unresolved in the rest-optical JWST data ( r eff < 47 r_{\rm eff}<47 pc), the rest-NUV HST imaging shows an extended morphology with r eff = 1.0 − 0.3 + 0.5 r_{\rm eff}=1.0^{+0.5}_{-0.3} kpc, that we interpret as a host galaxy with a stellar mass ∼ 10 8 \sim 10^{8} M ⊙ M_{\odot} , in line with the narrow H α \alpha emission. The discovery of this object at cosmic noon highlights the feasibility of systematic searches for extreme LRDs with wide-area facilities such as Euclid and Roman.

\sim
53
\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}

The reduction of the X-Shooter data uses a combination of

2
2

2

IV
Absorber kinematics

As described in Sect.
III.2
, the velocity distribution of the absorber is modeled with a Gaussian, which we find has a central velocity of
-94\pm 4

\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
relative to redshift of the narrow emission component (adopted as systemic). The absorption trough extends from negative to positive velocities with respect to the redshift of the narrow component, but also with respect to the center of the symmetric exponential wings. We define the maximum and minimum absorber velocity as the values where the transmission increases to 99%,
v_{99}^{\rm blue}=-520\pm 13

\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
and
v_{99}^{\rm red}=267\pm 11

\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
. The absorbing trough extends over
787\pm 17

\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
under this definition.
In Fig.
5
we illustrate three proposed configurations of the velocity distribution of the absorbing gas that could explain the shape of the observed Balmer absorption that we discuss below.

IV.1
Unstable gas flows?

The fact that there is significant absorption at both negative and positive velocities with respect to the systemic redshift cannot be simply explained by an axisymmetric outflowing or inflowing wind. In the case of observing a compact object through a spherically symmetric, non-turbulent bulk flow, a classical P Cygni profile is expected, with a purely blueshifted absorption (or redshifted if the wind is infalling). The fact that we also see redshifted absorption rules out this simple scenario. In principle, turbulent motions could also produce broadening of the absorbing medium (scenario
a
in Fig.
5
). However, the required turbulent velocity dispersion
\sigma_{\rm turb}\approx 120

\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
(from the Gaussian fit in Sect.
III.2
) is comparable to the mean velocity of the absorption trough, meaning that turbulence dominates the gas flow. In such a case, strong variability of the absorption profile would be expected, given the typical dynamical crossing times
(
20
, see Sect. 4.1 in)
. For example, for a radius of
10^{16}
cm
(
90
, e.g.,)
and a mass of
10^{6}

M_{\odot}
, the dynamical free fall time is
t_{\rm ff}=\sqrt{\frac{R^{3}}{GM}}\approx 2.75
yr.
Moreover, the turbulent velocity would be highly supersonic, and the dissipation timescale would be comparable to the dynamical time
(
55
, e.g.,)
.
Alternatively, in the context of a strong Balmer absorber at
z\sim 7
,
19
recently discussed a “breathing mode” scenario with cyclic inflows and outflows along the same line of sight, with the gas being in different phases at different depths
(
68
, scenario
b
in Fig.
5
; see also)
. In this case, the same arguments regarding the stability of the absorber would apply, and absorber variability is expected on observed timescales of
\sim 5
yr (for a source at
z=1.7
), which is testable with future observations.

IV.2
The case for the disk wind hypothesis

Figure 5:
Geometric configurations for the absorber. We illustrate three scenarios that could give rise to the observed Balmer absorption in
PAN-BH*-1
. In scenario (a), the obscuring agent is a thick screen of gas with a certain bulk velocity, and turbulent motions produce the broadening of the absorption trough. In (b), there are two (or more) absorbers with opposite velocities in the line-of-sight. These first two scenarios are dynamically unstable, therefore variability is expected in the absorption. Lastly, in (c) we observed an extended source through a disk wind with a rotational component (
\mathbf{v}_{\phi}
) in addition to the poloidal (non-azimuthal) velocity (
\mathbf{v}_{p}
). In the last scenario, the redshifted absorption is produced by stream lines that oppose the observer when projected along the line-of-sight, despite the fact that the gas is outflowing from the central source.

An alternative, dynamically stable scenario is a disk wind configuration (scenario
c
in Fig.
5
).
Here, the wind would be launched from a thick disk near the central engine which we speculate could be the source of the optical continuum emission
(
53
;
52
;
13
;
108
, e.g.,)
. A rotating disk would imprint to the wind an azimuthal velocity component (
\mathbf{v}_{\phi}
).
Observations at specific lines of sight, particularly for high inclination angles (close to edge-on) where the rotational component dominates the poloidal velocity, can give rise to both blueshifted and redshifted absorption features
(
74
;
31
;
30
;
73
;
26
)
.
Most observed LRDs have blueshifted P Cygni-like absorbers
(
59
)
, that can be naively interpreted as a uniformly expanding shell. The low incidence of redshifted Balmer absorbers in LRD spectra
(
17
;
47
;
20
;
19
;
54
, e.g.,)
can therefore be explained by the requirement of high inclination angles to observe such features
(
84
, see also)
. Such a picture is broadly in line with disk wind models for AGN with broad absorption lines
(
31
;
107
, e.g.,)
and around stars with circumstellar disks
(
23
, e.g.,)
, such as accreting T Tauri stars
(
22
)
or cataclysmic variables
(
75
)
.

IV.3
Implications of rotating winds for the emission lines of LRDs

The disk wind hypothesis would imply that a photosphere in the shape of a rotating disk is the source of the optical continuum emission, and drives winds that can explain the observed absorption trough. Emission lines originating in a thin rotating disk would have a double-peaked profile in the idealized case (for most inclination angles), but this is not necessarily true if the disk is not sufficiently thin
(
61
, e.g.,)
, for instance, in the case of a puffed-up disk associated with super-Eddington accretion
(
52
, e.g.,)
. In addition, most line emission would not be produced directly at the base of the disk, but slightly outside
(
90
, e.g., via collisional cooling or residual recombination;)
, where the rotational velocity is lower, and the dynamics are complex
(
82
, e.g.,)
.

The Balmer lines of most LRDs are dominated by broad, symmetric exponential components that are associated with broadening by electron scattering
(
77
;
59
, e.g.,)
. For
PAN-BH*-1
, the
\text{H}{\alpha}
line profile of
PAN-BH*-1
is compatible with a broad exponential profile emerging through a dense wind where the absorption trough is produced.
In dense gas with a large column density of neutral hydrogen, and optically thick to Balmer transitions (
N_{{\rm H\,\text{\sc i},}2s}\gtrsim 10^{14}

\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}
), resonant scattering effects become important. Crucially, resonant scattering impacts
\text{H}{\alpha}
and
\text{H}{\beta}
differently
(
12
, e.g.,)
, hence the 3D radiative transfer and photon redistribution of both lines may produce different profiles (see e.g., Fig. 2 in
75
). Therefore, the empirical fitting and interpretation of the absorption profiles becomes non-trivial.
Dedicated radiative-transfer modeling is necessary to study such effects, and they can be tested in other emission lines with high optical depth such as He
i

\lambda 10830
Å, or resonant lines like C
iv

\lambda 1550
.

V
Implications for the galaxy and black hole masses

V.1
Properties of the host galaxy

Assuming that the narrow component of
\text{H}{\alpha}
corresponds to ISM emission in the host galaxy, we compute the associated star formation rate using the local calibration from
39
and assuming no dust attenuation. We obtain
\rm SFR({\text{H}{\alpha}}{})=3.3\pm 0.3

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}
. A somewhat lower value of
\rm SFR({\text{H}{\alpha}}{})=2.1\pm 0.2

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}
is obtained using the high-redshift (
z\gtrsim 4
) calibrations in
45
, which might be more appropriate for a young dwarf galaxy with a bursty star formation history. The star-formation rates are low, but in line with a main-sequence galaxy with
\log_{10}(M_{*}/M_{\odot})\approx 8

(
85
, extrapolating the relation from)
. Assuming zero dust attenuation, the UV absolute magnitude (
M_{\rm UV}=-16.7\pm 0.7
; Sect.
III.1
) would imply
\rm SFR(UV)=0.18\pm 0.12

M_{\odot}\rm\,yr^{-1}

(
39
)
. The discrepancy between the UV and
\text{H}{\alpha}
inferred SFR suggests there is some amount of dust attenuation in the host galaxy.

We derive a dynamical mass from the width of the line and the estimated UV size as
\log_{10}(M_{\rm dyn}/M_{\odot})=9.9\pm 0.2
, adopting a virial correction
K(n)K(q)
from
94
, where
K(n)
and
K(q)
are functions of the best-fit ellipticity and Sérsic index (see Appendix
B
). Adopting a
M_{\rm dyn}/M_{*}
factor of 40 as found by
16
for dwarf galaxies at high redshift, we infer a stellar mass of
\log_{10}(M_{*}/M_{\odot})=8.3\pm 0.2
. However, the
M_{\rm dyn}/M_{*}
is very uncertain in this regime, and the uncertainty can span over 1 dex
(
78
)
.

As discussed in Sect.
IV
, the absorption profile is compatible with broadening by a rotating disk wind, and numerical modeling of such configurations often predicts a narrow component arising from increased transmission due to purely kinematic effects in the wind geometry
(
74
;
73
;
75
)
. This would be an alternative explanation for at least part of the narrow component flux. On the other hand, most LRDs present narrow
[O
iii
]
emission that is often associated with the host galaxy. Indeed, the ionized gas producing
[O
iii
]
emission should have associated emission in the
\text{H}{\alpha}
and higher order Balmer lines. However, constraining this component largely depends on the assumptions on dust attenuation or ISM conditions, and requires very high S/N and resolution data. Deep, space-based follow-up observations of
PAN-BH*-1
would be very constraining for the wind kinematics (e.g., by the joint analysis of
\text{H}{\beta}
) and to assess whether a narrow component comes from a host galaxy (e.g., by comparing to a narrow
\text{H}{\beta}
component or
[O
iii
]

\lambda\lambda 4960,5008
).

V.2
Black hole mass from photosphere models

The general physical setup of LRDs is an open debate, and their masses a major unknown. Due to the multiple differences with respect to the classical AGN population, the validity of standard virial calibrations has been questioned (e.g.,
77
;
29
;
90
;
84
, although see e.g.,
9
;
8
;
79
for an alternative interpretation).

One can obtain a mass estimate assuming a system in radiative equilibrium with
L_{\rm bol}/L_{\rm Edd}=1

(
92
, e.g.,)
; this yields a total mass of
\approx 10^{6}

M_{\odot}
, using the bolometric luminosity from integrating the best-fit blackbody in Sect.
III.1
.
Recently,
52
developed a synthetic spectral library of LRD atmosphere models. In these models, the density of the photosphere is regulated by the net surface gravity of an optically thick atmosphere, enabling constraints on the mass of the system. We fit the JWST photometry of
PAN-BH*-1
using the models from
52
, assuming a negligible contribution from a host galaxy to the optical continuum. The best-fit model has effective temperature
T_{\rm eff}=4800
K, surface gravity
\log_{10}(g/\rm cm\,s^{-2})=-1.5
, and metallicity
\log_{10}(Z/Z_{\odot})=-1
(
\chi^{2}_{\nu}=1.7
; see Fig.
1
).
The best-fit
\log_{10}g
implies a total mass of the system (BH plus gas) of
\log_{10}(M_{\rm tot}/M_{\odot})\approx 5

(Eq. 6 in
52
, assuming hydrostatic equilibrium)
. For the second and third best fits we obtain
\log_{10}(g/\rm cm\,s^{-2})=-1
and
-2
, respectively (
\chi^{2}_{\nu}=2,3
, respectively; with the same metallicity and effective temperature), which would imply lower limits to the system mass between
\log_{10}(M_{\rm tot}/M_{\odot})\gtrsim 6
and 4.
The bolometric luminosity of
PAN-BH*-1
(from the integral of the best-fit green curve in Fig.
1
) implies an Eddington luminosity ratio of
L/L_{\rm Edd}\lesssim 13
, assuming the best-fit mass from the
52
models. The elevated Eddington ratio is in line with the hypothesis of a radiation-driven wind discussed in Sect.
IV
, and allowing for somewhat larger system masses. The low masses obtained with this model, combined with the stellar mass infered from dynamical arguments for the host galaxy (Sect.
III.3
) set lower limits to the BH-to-stellar mass ratio of
M_{\rm BH}/M_{*}\gtrsim 10^{-4}
–
10^{-2}
, comparable to the relations observed in the Local Universe, within the large uncertainties
(
76
)
.

VI
Conclusions

In this letter we presented the discovery and spectroscopic confirmation of
PAN-BH*-1
, an LRD with an extreme Balmer break at
z=1.731
. The strenght of the Balmer break (
\rm F115W/F814W=7\pm 1
) is comparable to the most extreme LRDs known,
The Cliff

(
17
)
and
MoM-BH*

(
62
)
. We summarize the observations and our main conclusions as follows.

1.

We obtained deep VLT/X-Shooter spectroscopy of
PAN-BH*-1
. The
\text{H}{\alpha}
emission line is luminous and broad (
L_{\text{H}{\alpha}}{}=10^{43}

\mathrm{e}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
), and has an unusually strong absorption.
\text{H}{\beta}
is detected with
\rm S/N\approx 6
, and we conservatively estimate a lower limit for the Balmer decrement of
{\text{H}{\alpha}}{}/{\text{H}{\beta}}{}>9.4
(at a 3
\sigma
confidence level), in line with other LRDs in the literature
(
15
;
66
, e.g.,)
.

2.

The absorption trough spans from
-520

\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
to
267

\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
(at a transmission level of 99%). We interpret the presence of blue- and red-shifted absorption as produced by a disk wind, analogous to those analysed in the context of broad absorption line quasars or accreting stars. This hypothesis would imply that the source of the optical continuum is likely a thick photospheric disk.

3.

We detect a narrow
\text{H}{\alpha}
component (
\rm FWHM=184\pm 12

\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
) which we interpret as probing a host galaxy with
M_{*}\approx 10^{8}

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
and
\rm SFR=2
–3
M_{\odot}
. This interpretation is in line with the extended rest-NUV morphology measured in the HST bands (
r_{\rm eff}=1^{+0.5}_{-0.3}
kpc).

4.

By fitting the synthetic atmosphere models of
52
, we estimate a system mass (BH+envelope) of
10^{4}
–
10^{6}

M_{\odot}
. The inferred masses, together with the stellar mass inferred from morphology and narrow-emission line dynamics implie BH-to-stellar mass ratios of
10^{-2}
–
10^{-4}
, close to the extrapolated trend in the local Universe
(
76
)
.

5.

The confirmation of this source at cosmic noon (magnitude of
\approx 22
in the
K
band, H
\alpha
flux
\approx 5\times 10^{-16}
erg s
-1
cm
-2
) proves the feasibility of detecting extreme LRDs at such epochs with wide-area spectroscopic surveys like Euclid or the forthcoming Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope.
