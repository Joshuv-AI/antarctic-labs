A fetus with paternal uniparental isodisomy of chromosome 3: genetic analysis and prenatal diagnosis following a positive NIPS with IUGR
Authors: Fang Zhang, ShanShan Ma, Yongan Wang, Shuai Men, Rong Zhang, Ting Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo report a rare prenatal case of paternal uniparental isodisomy of chromosome 3 (upd(3)pat) associated with isolated intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR).MethodsExpanded non‑invasive prenatal screening (NIPS) in a 19‑year‑old woman indicated a duplication on chromosome 3q. Amniocentesis was performed for karyotyping, chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA), and trio whole‑exome sequencing (WES). Copy number variation sequencing (CNV-seq) was performed for the validation of the placental tissue postpartum.ResultsKaryotype was normal (46,XY). CMA revealed a whole-chromosome region of homozygosity (ROH) on chromosome 3, and WES confirmed upd(3)pat with no recessive pathogenic variants. Third‑trimester ultrasound showed IUGR. A male infant weighing 2,450 g was delivered at 38 weeks with normal birth examination. CNV-seq confirmed trisomy 3 on the fetal side of the placenta. Follow-up assessment at 2.5 years of age showed unremarkable neurodevelopmental outcomes.ConclusionNIPS is a screening rather than a diagnostic test and all positive findings must be confirmed by invasive diagnostic procedures. Trisomy rescue can lead to coexistence of UPD3 with confined placental mosaicism (CPM). A multimodal approach incorporating karyotyping, CMA, trio‑WES, and CNV‑seq is essential for resolving discordant findings. This study expands the limited prenatal literature on upd(3)pat and highlights the critical importance of comprehensive genetic testing for accurate diagnosis and genetic counseling.

Introduction

Uniparental disomy (UPD) is a rare genetic condition characterized by the inheritance of both homologous chromosomes, or entire homologous segments, from a single parent, with the complete absence of a corresponding chromosome copy from the other parent [1]. Based on its mechanisms of formation, UPD can be categorized into three main types: heterodisomy, arising from nondisjunction of homologous chromosomes; isodisomy, derived from nondisjunction of sister chromatids; and mixed heterodisomy/isodisomy [1, 2]. To date, the exact population frequency of UPD has not been definitively established and reported prevalence rates in the literature range from 0.05% to 0.6%, with chromosome 16 UPD being the most frequently observed [3].The first clinical case of UPD, presenting with cystic fibrosis and short stature, was reported by Spence JE et al. in 1988 [4]. UPD is not inherently pathogenic, and clinical manifestations only ensue when chromosomal aberrations, genomic imprinting perturbations, or homozygosity for autosomal recessive pathogenic variants are present [5, 6].

Non-invasive prenatal screening (NIPS) analyzes cell-free DNA (cfDNA) fragments from maternal plasma using massively parallel sequencing to assess the risk of common fetal chromosomal aneuploidies as well as segmental chromosomal imbalances [7, 8]. Despite its capability to detect copy number variations (CNVs), NIPS is associated with a high false-positive rate for such findings and cannot identify conditions like UPD or imprinting disorders [9]. In addition, NIPS has been suggested to be more sensitive to detect confined placental mosaicism (CPM) as the entire placental trophoblast sheds cfDNA into the maternal circulation [10]. Notably, the coexistence of CPM and UPD has been increasingly reported, as both conditions can arise from the same underlying mechanism-trisomy rescue [11, 12].

Herein, we present a prenatal case of paternal UPD3 (upd(3)pat) in a Chinese pregnant woman, following an incidental NIPS finding and complicated by third-trimester intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR). The diagnosis was subsequently confirmed through a comprehensive genetic workup, including karyotype analysis, chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA), trio whole-exome sequencing (WES) and copy number variation sequencing (CNV-seq).

Materials and methods

Ethics statements

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Lianyungang Maternal and Child Health Hospital (Number: LYG-XM 2923026). Written informed consent for genetic testing and publication of the results was obtained from the patient.

Clinical case report

The patient was a 19-year-old Chinese pregnant woman (gravida 1, para 0). The woman and her spouse were healthy, nonconsanguineous, and reported no family history of genetic disorders or congenital anomalies. During the first trimester of pregnancy, the fetal nuchal translucency (NT) measured 1.2 mm, and the nasal bone (NB) was visualized. In the second trimester, the woman received prenatal serological triple screening after genetic counseling and obtaining their informed consent at 15 + 2 weeks. After further genetic counseling, the pregnant woman refused amniocentesis and opted for expanded NIPS. While the expanded NIPS showed a low risk for chromosomes 21, 18, and 13, it identified a 104.18 Mb duplication spanning 3q11.1-3q29 on the long arm of chromosome 3, accompanied by a mean count-based Z-score of 2.5. Following genetic counseling, the patient underwent amniocentesis at 18 + 2 weeks for diagnostic testing. Conventional cytogenetic studies, quantitative fluorescent polymerase chain reaction (QF-PCR), and CMA were performed.

Prenatal serological testing

At 15 + 2 weeks of gestation, a 3 mL fasting blood sample was collected from the pregnant woman. Serum concentrations of alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), free β-subunit of human chorionic gonadotropin (f-β-hCG), and unconjugated estriol (uE3) were determined using time-resolved fluoroimmunoassay (TRFIA) on a Wallac 1235 AutoDELFIA analyzer (DELFIA 1235; PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Risk assessments for neural tube defects (NTD), trisomy 21 (T21), and trisomy 18 (T18) were calculated using Lifecycle software (version 5.0), incorporating maternal age, gestational age, maternal weight, and insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus. High-risk cutoffs were defined as a T21 risk > 1:270 and a T18 risk > 1:350.

NIPS

Expanded NIPS was performed by massive-parallel whole-genome sequencing method. Approximately 5 mL of maternal peripheral blood sample was collected in EDTA anticoagulant tubes. Plasma was separated through centrifugation at 1600 g for 10 min at 4 ℃, followed by re-centrifugation at 16,000 g for 10 min at 4 ℃ within 8 h. Cell-free DNA isolation, enrichment, library construction, and massive parallel shotgun sequencing were performed on a MGISEQ-2000 sequencing platform, producing 35 single-end reads (BGI, Shenzhen, China). Sequencing data were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19, NCBI build 36), and Z-scores were calculated for each chromosome. Fetal aneuploidy was evaluated based on chromosomal Z-scores using Halos-NIFTY software (BGI-Shenzhen, Shenzhen, China).

Conventional cytogenetic studies

Amniocentesis was performed to obtain fetal samples for karyotyping. Amniotic fluid cells were cultured, and metaphase chromosomes were harvested and stained using standard G-banding techniques at the 350–450 band level. From two independent primary cultures, a total of thirty metaphase spreads were obtained, of which five were selected for detailed analysis. The karyotype was described in accordance with the latest International System for Human Cytogenomic Nomenclature (ISCN 2024).

Genomic DNA extraction

Approximately 20 mL of amniotic fluid was collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to obtain the cell pellet. Genomic DNA was extracted from uncultured amniotic fluid using a QIAGEN kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was then used for both QF-PCR and CMA.

QF-PCR

To detect maternal cell contamination and polyploidy, QF-PCR was performed using a set of STR markers on chromosomes 13, 18, 21, X, and Y.

CMA

Affymetrix CytoScan 750 K (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used for chromosomal aneuploidy analysis. DNA digestion, amplification, segmentation, labeling, and hybridization of the arrays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Affymetrix, USA). The results were analyzed using the Chromosome Analysis Suite software (ChAS). The following public databases were used for data analysis and interpretation: Database of genomic variants (DGV; https://dgv.tcag.ca/dgv/app/home) [13], database of Chromosomal Imbalances and Phenotypes in humans using Ensembl Resources (DECIPHER; https://www.deciphergenomics.org/) [14], online mendelian inheritance in man (OMIM; https://www.omim.org/) [15], ISCA search (https://www.iscaconsortium.org/) [16], ClinVar of NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) [17] and ClinGen Dosage Sensitivity Map (https://dosage.clinicalgenome.org) [18].

Trio Whole-Exome Sequencing

After genetic counseling, trio whole exome sequencing (WES) of fetal (uncultured amniocytes) and parental blood DNA was performed for whole exome capture using the Universal Kit for sequencing reaction (BGI, Shenzhen, China). According to the manufacturer’s recommendations, the resulting libraries were sequenced on a BGISEQ-500 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China). Reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19) using a Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA V0.7.15). All genomic variations, including single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions/deletions (InDels) were detected and filtered by GATK HaplotypeCaller (v3.3.0) (Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA, USA. Potential disease-causing mutations were predicted using the sorting intolerant from tolerant (SIFT) algorithm. Data were filtered with several variant databases, including dbSNP (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp/) [19], the 1000 Genomes Project (https://www.internationalgenome.org/) [20], and genomAD database (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) [21]. Candidate mutations were expected to be absent from these databases. The conservation analysis of amino acid sequences was aligned using ClustalW2 (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalw2/).

CNV-seq

To examine the reason behind the false positive of NIPS, CNV-seq was performed with low read-depth (1×) on placental tissues. Four samples were collected: two from the maternal side of the center of the placenta, two from the fetal side of the edge of the placenta. CNV-seq was performed as previously described [22].

Results

Prenatal serological testing

For this patient, the maternal serum multiple of the median (MoM) values were 0.56 (reference range: 0.4–2.5) for AFP, 2.8 for f-β-hCG (reference range: 0.3–2.5), and 0.59 (reference range: 0.7–2.5) for uE3, yielding a T21 risk of 1:75.

Expanded NIPS results

An expanded version of NIPS was performed at 17 weeks of gestation, generating 19.15 Mb of valid data and showing a fetal fraction of 28.5%. The results indicated elevated amounts of DNA from chromosome 3q with a mean count-based Z-score of 2.5, suggesting a gain of approximately 104.18 Mb in size at 3q11.1-q29 (Fig. 1).

QF-PCR and CMA results

No abnormality was detected by QF-PCR for chromosomes 21, 18, 13, X, and Y. CMA identified no pathogenic CNVs but revealed a complete regions of homozygosity (ROH) spanning the entire chromosome 3, indicating copy neutral loss of heterozygosity (Fig. 3).

Trio-WES results

The result of WES revealed no homozygous pathogenic variants in recessive genes on chromosome 3. WES analysis of SNP patterns across chromosome 3 confirmed the absence of maternal haplotypes, establishing a diagnosis of upd(3)pat (Fig. 4).

Pregnancy outcome and Clinical follow-up assessment

Based on the results of prenatal genetic diagnosis and ultrasound examination, the parents decided to continue the pregnancy. During the second trimester, prenatal ultrasound examinations revealed no significant abnormalities in fetal growth or anatomical structures. However, at 32 weeks, ultrasound screening revealed signs of IUGR, indicated by Biparietal Diameter (BPD): 7.5 cm (-1.705 SD), Head Circumference (HC): 27.6 cm (-2.025 SD), Abdominal Circumference (AC): 25.8 cm (− 1.537 SD), Femur Length (FL): 5.4 cm (− 1.99 SD), and an estimated fetal weight of 1414 g (-2.315 SD). IUGR was still persistent at full term. At 38 weeks and 3 days of gestation, a male infant weighing 2,450 g was delivered by cesarean section. Apgar scores were 10 and 10 at 1 and 5 min, respectively. The neonate had normal physical findings at birth. The infant has been enrolled in a structured follow-up program. Now, aged 2.5 years, his developmental assessment revealed no abnormalities. His gross motor, fine motor, language, and social skills were all consistent with established norms for his chronological age.

CNV-seq results

The CNV-seq analysis of the fetal side of placenta verified three copies of chromosome 3 (Fig. 5). In contrast, no abnormality was detected by CNV-seq at the maternal side of placenta.

Discussion

In the present case, NIPS suggested a rare autosomal trisomy involving chromosome 3, while conventional karyotyping, CMA and Trio-WES of amniocytes revealed a normal fetal karyotype with upd(3)pat. Subsequent CNV-seq analysis of placental tissue detected trisomy 3, providing direct evidence of CPM, which is defined as the presence of a chromosomal abnormality restricted to the placenta while the chromosomes of the fetus itself is normal [23]. Based on these findings, we speculate that the mechanism underlying this case is consistent with a meiotic origin of CPM. Specifically, a meiotic error leading to trisomy 3 in the trophoblast lineage, followed by postzygotic loss of the supernumerary chromosome in the epiblast (fetal lineage) through trisomy rescue [24]. However, The presence of CPM also explains the positive NIPS result, as the cell-free DNA in maternal plasma is predominantly derived from the trophoblast [25]. Accumulating evidence suggests that CPM represents a primary contributor to NIPS false-positive results [25, 26].

Furthermore, insufficient placental fusion following the trisomy rescue phenomenon arising from abnormal trophoblast proliferation and function may impair nutrient and oxygen delivery to the fetus, leading to fetal growth restriction (FGR) [27]. Although pregnancies with CPM are typically uneventful during the first trimester-or at least until the time of prenatal testing-CPM has been associated with an increased risk of FGR [12, 13]. However, a recent review indicated that CPM involving chromosomes 2, 3, 7, 13, 15, 16, or 22 is associated with a high-risk condition [25]. In this case, the placental findings support CPM as a plausible contributor to the observed IUGR.

Trisomy rescue, monosomy rescue, and gametic complementation represent the primary mechanisms underlying the correction of aneuploid zygotes to euploidy, a process that frequently results in the emergence of UPD [28]. In this study, the detection of trisomy 3 in placental tissue combined with upd(3)pat in the fetus strongly supports trisomy rescue as the underlying mechanism for both CPM and UPD formation. According to the proposed model, a meiotic error in gametogenesis resulted in a disomic gamete, followed by fertilization with a normal gamete to form a trisomic zygote. Subsequent postzygotic loss of the supernumerary chromosome in the epiblast lineage restored euploidy in the fetus, while the trophoblast lineage retained trisomy 3, giving rise to CPM [29]. This process simultaneously led to the formation of uniparental disomy in the fetal lineage, with the retained homologous chromosomes both originating from the same parent. In the present case, the paternal origin of the UPD further suggests that the trisomy rescue event occurred following the loss of the maternal chromosome 3.

To date, 33 cases of complete UPD3 with normal or unreported karyotypes have been reported, including 13 cases of unknown origin, 13 cases of maternal origin, and 7 cases of paternal origin [30]. Among upd(3)mat cases, 10 of 13 were associated with recessive disorders. Of the seven upd(3)pat cases, three presented with monogenic disorders, whereas the remaining four exhibited no obvious phenotypic abnormalities including two normal prenatal cases [31–33]. Unlike these prenatal cases, our index case presented with third-trimester IUGR. CMA and Trio-WES detected no pathogenic mutations or homozygous recessive pathogenic variants. Although several cases of upd(3)mat/pat associated with autosomal recessive disorders have been documented, genomic imprinting effects have not been implicated in UPD3-associated pathological phenotypes [34, 35]. While ALDH1L and ZIC1 on chromosome 3 have been predicted by bioinformatic analysis to be paternally imprinted, their clinical relevance in human imprinting disorders remains to be elucidated [33]. Accordingly, a definitive causal link between upd(3)pat and IUGR cannot be conclusively established based on the available data.

Limitations

Although we made a comprehensive analysis of the fetuses with upd(3)pat in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, owing to the technical limitations of CMA and karyotype analysis, the possibility of low-level mosaicism for partial trisomy 3q could not be completely excluded. Second, the potential effects of other undetected genetic or epigenetic modifiers on fetal growth and neurodevelopment could not be fully evaluated in the present single-case study. Therefore, further accumulation of well-characterized cases and extended longitudinal studies are warranted to clarify the clinical implications and underlying mechanisms of upd(3)pat.

In conclusion, NIPS is a screening rather than a diagnostic test and all positive findings must be confirmed by invasive diagnostic procedures such as chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis. Trisomy rescue can lead to the coexistence of UPD3 and CPM. An integrated multimodal approach-incorporating karyotyping, CMA, trio-WES, and CNV-seq, successfully resolved the discordant findings in this pregnancy. Our study expands the limited prenatal literature on upd(3)pat and emphasizes the critical importance of comprehensive genetic testing for accurate diagnosis and genetic counseling. Continued accumulation of well-characterized cases will further enhance our understanding of the clinical implications of UPD3 and improve management strategies for affected pregnancies.
