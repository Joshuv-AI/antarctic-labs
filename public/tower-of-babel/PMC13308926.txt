Introduction

Anetoderma is an elastolytic dermatosis characterized clinically by circumscribed areas of wrinkled skin and histologically by focal loss of elastic fibers in the dermis.1 The typical clinical presentation consists of round to oval, atrophic, wrinkled macules or patches, or herniated sac-like protrusions surrounded by normal skin. Five types of anetoderma have been described: primary, secondary, drug-induced, familial, and prematurity-associated.2 Primary anetoderma itself is further divided into inflammatory (Jadassohn-Pellizzari) and noninflammatory (Schweninger-Buzzi).3 Secondary anetoderma has been reported in cutaneous tuberculosis, sarcoidosis, leprosy, mastocytosis, xanthomas, and lichen planus.2,3

Case presentation

A 36-year-old woman presented with an asymptomatic lesion over her left lower cheek for the past 3 years (Fig. 1). The lesion became herniated, sac-like on tilting her face toward the left (Fig. 2). There was no history of medications, trauma, inflammation, or systemic complaints. She had two full-term, uncomplicated pregnancies with no miscarriages. There was no personal or family history suggestive of a connective tissue disorder.

Clinical photograph of the lesion with the patient’s head in the neutral position. The skin coloured papule appears less conspicuous but remains visible on the left lower cheek.

Clinical photograph showing the lesion on the left lower face becoming more prominent when the patient turns her head to the left. A solitary, well-demarcated, skin-colored papule with a sac-like herniation. The lesion demonstrates a characteristic “button-hole” sign on palpation, consistent with primary anetoderma.

On cutaneous examination, a skin-colored papule measuring approximately 2 × 8 mm2 was noted on the left lower cheek. There was no erythema, scaling, or induration.

The lesion was completely excised. Histopathology revealed normal epidermis with focal thinning of the dermis (Fig. 3). Verhoeff-Van Gieson staining demonstrated marked loss of elastic fibers in the papillary and reticular dermis, confirming the diagnosis of anetoderma (Fig. 4).

Histopathological section of skin (hematoxylin & eosin stain, ×40) showing normal epidermis with marked loss of elastic tissue in the upper and mid-dermis. The dermis exhibits loosely arranged, thin, fragmented collagen bundles with widened spaces.

Elastic tissue stain (Verhoeff–Van Gieson, ×10) showing complete loss and fragmentation of elastic fibers in the upper and mid-dermis. The epidermis appears unremarkable.

A comprehensive laboratory workup, including antinuclear antibody, antiphospholipid antibodies, anti-double-stranded DNA, HIV, Venereal Disease Research Laboratory, and thyroid function tests, was negative. On the 3-month follow-up, there was no recurrence.

Discussion

Anetoderma is characterized by localized areas of dermal elastolysis, presenting clinically as soft, wrinkled, or sac-like lesions. Verhoeff-Van Gieson staining is essential to demonstrate elastic fiber loss and differentiate anetoderma from other elastolytic disorders.4 Pathogenesis involves increased matrix metalloproteinase activity with reduced tissue inhibitors, leading to dermal elastolysis.5

There is growing evidence linking primary anetoderma to autoimmune conditions, including systemic lupus erythematosus, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, and autoimmune thyroiditis.6,7 Therefore, testing for antiphospholipid antibody is recommended in all cases.8,9

Facial involvement, as seen in our patient, is an uncommon presentation. The trunk, upper arms, and thighs remain the most frequently affected sites.2,9,10

Several elastolytic disorders were considered in the differential diagnosis. Acquired cutis laxa and granulomatous slack skin disease were excluded because of the absence of generalized laxity or flexural plaques. Other entities, including mid-dermal elastolysis, papular elastorrhexis, perifollicular elastolysis, pseudoxanthoma elasticum-like papillary dermal elastolysis, and atrophoderma of Pasini and Pierini, were ruled out based on clinical features and histopathology.2 Acne scars were excluded as they are typically preceded by inflammatory papules or pustules and histologically demonstrate dermal fibrosis with collagen remodeling.11

In our case, the absence of widespread involvement, lack of systemic findings, and characteristic histopathological features confirmed the diagnosis of Schweninger-Buzzi variant of primary anetoderma.

Conclusion

Primary anetoderma presenting on the face is rare and may pose diagnostic challenges. Recognition of characteristic clinical findings and confirmation with elastic tissue staining are essential. Evaluation for associated autoimmune conditions is recommended for comprehensive management.
