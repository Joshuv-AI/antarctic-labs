Trends and predictors of mortality among children and adolescents with congenital heart disease in Ethiopia: A retrospective follow-up study
Authors: Mohammed Nasir, Sura Markos, Shibikom Tamrat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Congenital heart disease (CHD) is a leading noninfectious cause of mortality during childhood and adolescence, particularly in developing countries where timely diagnosis and treatment are limited. Despite its public health significance, data on mortality trends and their predictors in resource-limited settings remain scarce. This study aimed to assess mortality trends and identify predictors among children with CHD in a resource-limited setting.Materials and Methods:We conducted a retrospective follow-up study on 6228 children and adolescents with CHD who received care at the Cardiac Center of Ethiopia, either as outpatients or inpatients, with follow-up between 2015 and 2024. Data were collected through medical record reviews and phone interviews with caregivers from January 1 to August 1, 2025. Joinpoint was used to assess trends in CHD-related mortality by calculating the annual percent change (APC), and the average APC (AAPC) was used to evaluate overall mortality trends. Predictors of CHD-related mortality were identified using a Cox proportional hazards model.Results:The median age of patients was 0.7 years (interquartile range, 0.3–3.8 years), with a female-to-male ratio of 1.42:1. During follow-up, 827 patients (13.3%) died. The overall AAPC in mortality per 1000 CHD cases was −8.19% (95% confidence interval [CI]: −11.2–−5.47, P < 0.001), indicating a statistically significant annual decline. A marked decrease occurred between 2019 and 2022 (APC: −40.94%; 95% CI: −47.41–−27.50; P = 0.04). Malnutrition increased mortality risk by 2.9-fold (adjusted hazard ratio [AHR]: 2.9; 95% CI: 1.9–3.9; P < 0.001), pulmonary hypertension (PH) by 3.5-fold (AHR: 3.5; 95% CI: 2.2–6.6; P < 0.001), and the presence of syndromic associations (with or without extracardiac anomalies) by 30% (AHR: 1.3; 95% CI: 1.1–2.2; P = 0.04). Conversely, undergoing surgery or an interventional procedure reduced mortality risk by 80% (AHR: 0.2; 95% CI: 0.1–0.8; P < 0.001).Conclusion:Mortality among children and adolescents with CHD in Ethiopia has shown a significant decline in recent years. However, malnutrition, PH, syndromic associations, and lack of surgical or interventional care remain key predictors of poor outcomes. Strengthening timely access to interventions, enhancing nutritional and multidisciplinary support, and prioritizing early identification of high-risk patients are essential to further reduce CHD-related mortality in resource-limited settings.

INTRODUCTION

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is identified as one of the most common major congenital anomalies, constituting 28% of all such anomalies.[1] It is not only highly prevalent but also contributes significantly to childhood mortality.[12] This is attributed to its association with multisystem complications, including pulmonary complications such as pulmonary edema, infections, atelectasis, and pulmonary hypertension (PH), as well as renal and liver dysfunction, neurological complications, and protein-losing enteropathy as a gastrointestinal tract complication.[3] Additionally, the association of malnutrition with CHD, particularly in developing countries where there is delayed diagnosis, also contributed to increased mortality.[4] An isolated study from Malaysia showed that the complexity of CHD increases the risk of mortality further.[25]

Advancements in medical, surgical, and percutaneous interventions have greatly improved survival rates in patients with CHD. Nevertheless, significant regional disparities persist, with patients in the best centers now reaching life expectancy beyond 65 years.[36] In the US, for instance, age-adjusted CHD mortality dropped from 3.2 to 0.8/100,000 population from 1968 to 2019.[7] Europe, high-income Asia Pacific, tropical Latin America, and Australasia showed a 60% decrement in infant mortality from 1960 to 2017. However, central sub-Saharan Africa was one of the regions that reported the highest infant mortality due to CHD in 2017.[6]

Since CHD-related mortality reflects the quality and accessibility of cardiology care, this study aimed to evaluate the trends and identify the predictors of CHD-related mortality in Ethiopia, a resource-limited setting with limited data on this topic.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design, period, and population

This study employed a retrospective follow-up design. The source population included all pediatric patients who received care in the outpatient or inpatient cardiac units of Cardiac Center of Ethiopia (CCE). The study population comprised all pediatric cardiovascular patients managed at the center between January 1, 2015, and January 1, 2024. Data were collected through medical record reviews and phone interviews with caregivers from January 1 to August 1, 2025.

Eligibility criteria

This study included all pediatric CHD patients seen in the outpatient or inpatient cardiac units of CCE during the study period, excluding patients with missing data on the outcome variable (mortality) and those who had no registered or nonfunctional phone numbers in their medical charts.

Variables

The dependent variable in this study was cardiovascular mortality. The independent variables included the age at presentation in years, gender, place of residence, weight-for-height or body mass index (BMI) status, presence of syndromic association, categories of CHDs, presence of PH, medication status, and whether a surgical or percutaneous procedure was undertaken.

Data collection tool, technique, and quality

A structured questionnaire was formulated based on a comprehensive review of the literature. Data collection was carried out using methods such as medical chart reviews and phone interviews with caregivers of patients, with clinical diagnoses categorized using ICD-10. Medical residents gathered the data after participating in a 2-day training on data collection techniques, and they were closely supervised to improve their understanding and interpretation of medical records. To maintain data quality and consistency, a pretest was conducted on 10% of the population in this study from the medical records of CHD patients at Tikur Anbessa Hospital.

Data entry and analysis

Initially, data were assessed for completeness and consistency. Subsequently, cleaning and coding were conducted before transferring it into the Kobo Toolbox software. Then, it was exported into Python version 3.5 for statistical analysis. After performing a normality test, the continuous variable (age in years) was described, and absolute frequency and percentage were used to describe categorical variables. Tables and graphs were used to present the data. Joinpoint regression was employed to analyze trends by calculating the annual percent change (APC), and the average APC (AAPC) was used to check the overall trend in CHD-related mortality for the whole follow-up. A Cox proportional hazard model was applied to assess the predictors of CHD-related mortality. The hazard ratio with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was used to see the strength of the association between dependent and independent variables, with statistical significance established at P < 0.05.

Operational definitions

PH was defined as having a peak tricuspid regurgitation gradient that surpasses 36 mmHg on echocardiography. For patients with ventricular septal defect (VSD) or patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), the systolic pulmonary pressure is calculated by deducting the trans-VSD or PDA systolic gradient from the systemic systolic blood pressure. In cases of CHD with pulmonary stenosis, the gradient across the pulmonary valve was subtracted from the right ventricular systolic pressure, which was determined by the tricuspid regurgitation gradient by Bernoulli’s formula, to calculate the actual systolic pulmonary pressure.[8] In children under 5 years, the associated malnutrition was diagnosed using the World Health Organization weight-for-height Z score of <−2, while for those aged 5 years and older, a BMI-for-age of <−2 was applied.[9] For syndromic patients, the specific curve established by the CDC for syndromic children was used.[10] The diagnosis of syndromes was made solely based on phenotypic traits of the patient. The assessment of operability was carried out following Indian guidelines for the indications and timing of interventions for CHDs.[11]

Syndromic associations were identified in 354 (5.7%) patients [Table 1]. All extracardiac anomalies in our cohort were observed among patients with syndromic associations, with 43.2% of those patients presenting with at least one extracardiac anomaly. Neurological anomalies were the most common extracardiac anomalies and found in 39 (11.0%) patients, of which 56.4% had microcephaly, and myelomeningocele was present in 43.6%. Musculoskeletal anomalies were the second most common anomalies and were seen in 29 (8.2%) patients, of which club feet comprised 51.7%, polydactyly 31.0%, and absent radial bone 17.2%. Craniofacial anomalies were revealed in 25 (7.1%) patients, all of whom were patients with cleft lip and palate. Ocular anomalies were present in 23 (6.5%) patients, including cataracts (65.2%), strabismus (17.4%), and nystagmus (17.4%). Abdominal wall anomalies were observed in 25 (7.1%) patients, with omphalocele in 64.0% and diaphragmatic hernia in 36.0%. Anorectal malformations were found in 8 (2.3%) patients. Renal anomalies were identified in 4 (1.1%) patients, all with unilateral renal agenesis.

Table 2 presents the distribution of CHDs in each category. PH was documented in 17% of the cases. Spontaneous closure occurred in 62 patients with small secundum atrial septal defects (ASD), 15 patients with small VSDs, and 5 patients with small PDA. Additionally, spontaneous resolution was observed in 6 patients with mild pulmonary stenosis during follow-up. More than two-thirds of the patients were managed medically, with furosemide and spironolactone used in 2600 patients, furosemide and enalapril in 2326 patients, digoxin in 40 patients, and propranolol or metoprolol in 35 patients. Of the total patients, surgical or interventional procedures were indicated in 5055 (81.2%), but only 676 patients, accounting for 13.4% of those with an indication, underwent a surgical or interventional procedure. Over a median follow-up duration of 7.1 years (IQR: 5.6–11.2 years), 13.2% of the patients had died.

Trends in the number of congenital heart disease cases and mortality per 1000 cases

As demonstrated in Figure 2, the number of CHD cases rose steadily from 2013 to 2018, showed a decrease in 2019, and then increased consistently until 2024. The mortality rate per 1000 CHD cases rose until 2019, fell between 2020 and 2022, and remained constant from 2022 to 2024. The lowest mortality rate was documented in 2024 at 47.7/1000 cases, with 2023 at 57.7/1000 cases.

Trends in the number of congenital heart disease cases and mortality across time. CHD: Congenital heart disease

As depicted in Figure 3, the APC in mortality displayed three distinct trends: from 2013 to 2019, there was a statistically significant increase in mortality with an APC of + 12.75% (95% CI: 6.61–21.75, P = 0.01). Between 2019 and 2022, a significant decline was observed, with an APC of −40.94% (95% CI: −47.41–−27.50, P = 0.04). From 2022 to 2024, the change in mortality showed a decrease in trend, but it was not statistically significant, with an APC of −3.94% (95% CI: −24.71–15.80, P = 0.67). The overall AAPC throughout the whole period was −8.19% (95% CI: −11.2–−5.47, P < 0.001), signifying a statistically significant overall decline in mortality per 1000 CHD cases.

Survival of patients by type of congenital heart disease

Overall survival was 99.6% at 1 year, 95.4% at 5 years, and 75.7% at 10 years. Figure 4 illustrates that survival rates varied significantly with the severity of CHD (log-rank test, P < 0.001). At 1 year, the survival rates were 23.6% for severe univentricular CHD, 76.3% for severe biventricular CHD, and 86.2% for nonsevere CHD. After 5 years, these rates fell to 10.4%, 44.5%, and 79.3%, respectively. By the 10-year mark, only 4.7% of patients with severe univentricular CHD remained alive, while 38.8% and 44.0% of those with severe biventricular and nonsevere CHD survived, respectively. The median survival periods were 0.75 years, 4.5 years, and 9.29 years, respectively.

Predictors of mortality

Severe acute malnutrition increased the risk of mortality by 2.9 times (adjusted hazard ratio [AHR]: 2.9, 95% CI: 1.9–3.9, P < 0.001). Additionally, PH was associated with a 3.5-fold increase in mortality risk (AHR: 3.5, 95% CI: 2.2–6.6, P < 0.001). Children with syndromic associations with or without extracardiac anomaly had a 30% higher risk of death (AHR: 1.3, 95% CI: 1.1–2.2, P = 0.04). On the other hand, undergoing surgery or an interventional procedure had a statistically significant positive effect, resulting in an 80% decrease in mortality risk (AHR: 0.2, 95% CI: 0.1–0.8, P < 0.001) [Table 3].

Survival status based on the presence and absence of predictors in congenital heart disease patients

In this study, 10.9% of patients underwent intervention, with an overall median time delay to intervention of 2.9 years (IQR, 1.83–2.97 years). Children who received surgical or interventional treatment had survival rates of 99.8% at 1 year, 99.6% at 5 years, and 97.5% at 10 years, compared to 99.5%, 95.5%, and 67.1%, respectively, among those who did not. The median survival of the nonintervention group was not reached within the follow-up period. Regarding nutritional status, children with malnutrition had lower survival rates of 99.1% at 1 year, 89.2% at 5 years, and 66.3% at 10 years. Children with syndromic associations had a median survival of 8 years, with survival rates of 98.3% at 1 year, 90.3% at 5 years, and 47.4% at 10 years, while those without syndromic associations had survival rates of 99.6%, 96.4%, and 79.4% at the same time points, with median survival not reached within the follow-up period. Among children without PH, survival was 100% at 1, 5, and 10 years. Those with PH had a median survival of 7 years, with rates of 97.4% at 1 year, 79.3% at 5 years, and 31.1% at 10 years [Figure 7].

DISCUSSION

CHD-related mortality among children and adolescents in Ethiopia declined significantly over the study period, despite a concurrent rise in CHD incidence, reflecting improving diagnostic capacity and growing clinical expertise at a dedicated cardiac center. Four independent predictors of mortality were identified: PH, severe acute malnutrition, syndromic associations, and absence of surgical or interventional care. Absence of intervention was associated with an 80% increase in mortality risk, underscoring the critical role of timely surgical and interventional care in this resource-limited setting.

Our study revealed a consistent increase in the prevalence of CHDs, reflecting the global trend. Recent studies have attributed this increase mainly to severe biventricular CHDs as well as nonsevere defects such as ASD, VSD, and PDA, which together accounted for 93.4% of the overall rise in prevalence.[12] Similarly, in our study, more than 90% of CHD cases were comprised of severe biventricular or nonsevere CHDs. This can be attributed to improved diagnostic modalities, notably echocardiography. However, because our patients were not referred to CCE at a neonatal age and no prenatal ultrasounds were performed, factors such as poor survival of severe univentricular lesions after birth and the high risk of spontaneous abortion may have contributed to the low prevalence of severe univentricular CHDs observed in our study.

The mortality trend for CHD decreased by 34.5% between 1990 and 2017, even though it is still among the main causes of mortality in children.[13] Despite this significant global progress, the disparity in mortality rates between low- and high-income countries has continued to widen between 1990 and 2019.[14] The decrease in mortality from CHD has been slower in low-income countries.[61516] For example, in 2019, an estimated 217,000 deaths attributed to CHD occurred worldwide, with India, China, Pakistan, and Nigeria together accounting for 39.7% of these deaths.[6]

In Africa, CHD ranks as the second leading cardiovascular cause of death among children and adolescents.[17] As of 2019, CHD-related mortality in Nigeria and Sudan was reported to be higher than the global average, with Nigeria showing an increasing trend in CHD-related deaths.[6] Similarly, the trend in CHD-related mortality in our study showed an increase from 2013 to 2019; however, it declined from 2020 to 2024. The reduction in mortality from 2020 to 2024 may be attributed to several factors, including improvements in diagnostic services, increased clinical expertise at the center, the initiation of percutaneous procedures by the local team, and the commencement of surgical management for syndromic children starting in 2022.

Regarding long-term survival, the 10-year rate in our study was 75.7%, significantly lower than Korea’s 98.1% and Malaysia’s 84%, yet higher than Benin’s reported rate of 50%.[518] This disparity in survival may be attributed to variations in healthcare infrastructure, limited access to advanced interventions, as only a few patients underwent surgery or percutaneous procedures, case mix, and delays in early diagnosis and referral.

Similar to our finding, studies conducted in both developed and developing nations, such as Sweden, Spain, Norway, South Korea, Peru, French Guiana, Brazil, and Malaysia, revealed that children with complex CHD had lower survival time.[518192021222324] This might be because patients with complex CHDs have fewer treatment options, require more sophisticated surgical or interventional care, and are more likely to experience complications before, during, and after surgery.

The prevalence of malnutrition among children with CHD in developing countries, where surgical or interventional procedures are delayed, is high.[2526] In our study, it was found to be 42.5%, which falls within the range reported in previous studies conducted in developing countries, varying from 24.1% in Iran to 63% in Hawassa, Ethiopia.[2627282930] The high prevalence of malnutrition might be explained by the fact that most patients in our study had acyanotic CHDs, such as ASD, VSD, and PDA. This is consistent with findings from a study conducted in Hawassa, which reported that the presence of acyanotic CHDs was significantly associated with an increased risk of malnutrition.[28] This can be attributed to the higher prevalence of recurrent pneumonia, recurrent heart failure, and severe PH associated with significant left-to-right shunt lesions, particularly large VSDs and PDA, that can negatively impact a child’s growth.

Consistent with findings from studies in Uganda and Indonesia, we found that the presence of malnutrition among children with CHD was a predictor of mortality.[293132] This might be attributed to the high prevalence of anemia, the increased risk of postoperative infections, the increased risk of congestive heart failure, and the prolongation of the length of hospital stay in children with severe acute malnutrition.

PH prevalence has decreased in developed countries due to early diagnosis and timely intervention; it remains a significant problem in the developing world.[333435] One study conducted in Southern Africa reported that 8.4% of patients developed fixed PH before surgery.[36] In our study, 1056 patients (17%) developed PH, of whom 7 patients (0.7%) had fixed PH. Consistent with studies from Korea and Malaysia, our study also identified PH as a predictor of mortality in children with CHD.[518] This is likely because PH causes an increase in right ventricular afterload that eventually leads to right heart failure, which can cause death.

Previous studies from developed and developing countries have reported a wide range in the prevalence of syndromic or extracardiac anomalies among patients with CHD, with estimates varying from 7% to as high as 50%. In comparison, our study found a lower prevalence of 5.7% patients with syndromic associations with or without extracardiac anomalies. This disparity might be due to differences in study population characteristics, the availability, and use of advanced imaging and genetic testing.[3738394041] The presence of extracardiac anomalies or syndromic associations was associated with increased mortality in our study, which is consistent with findings from studies conducted in Malaysia and Ethiopia.[540] This may be due to the added physiological burden of multiple anomalies on existing physiological stress in patients with CHD, the additional complications arising from coexisting structural or genetic abnormalities, and the higher risk of postoperative complications in syndromic patients with or without extracardiac anomalies.

CHD is associated with reduced long-term survival compared to the general population. Surgical and interventional procedures have been shown to improve survival significantly, with a Danish study reporting a 10-year overall survival of 93% following surgical or catheter-based intervention.[42] Evidence from India and other low- and middle-income countries confirms meaningful survival improvements following CHD surgery, though outcomes remain influenced by case complexity, institutional volume, and resource availability.[43] Direct comparisons with high-income Western countries are limited by differences in patient presentation, disease spectrum, and healthcare infrastructure, making such comparisons a case of apples and oranges.[44] Late presentation is a well-recognized challenge in these settings and has been shown to independently worsen surgical outcomes and long-term survival.[45] Expanding access through public–private partnership models has shown promise in improving case volumes and outcomes in resource-limited settings,[46] while neonatal cardiac surgery continues to evolve, though outcomes remain resource-dependent and institutionally variable.[47]

Access to care remains a critical barrier across low-income countries, with up to 90% of children unable to receive essential cardiac surgery.[14] In our study, the 10-year survival rate was 97.5% among patients who received surgical or interventional treatment, compared to 67.1% among those who did not. This survival advantage is likely attributable to the timely correction of structural defects, reduction of complication risk, and improved cardiac function, whereas the absence of intervention is associated with progressive heart failure, recurrent infections, and other fatal outcomes.

CONCLUSION

Mortality among children and adolescents with CHD in Ethiopia has declined, yet significant gaps remain. Malnutrition, PH, syndromic associations, and limited access to surgical or interventional care continue to drive adverse outcomes. Patients who received timely interventions showed markedly better survival, underscoring the critical role of access to definitive care. Addressing these challenges requires expanding timely interventions, providing targeted nutritional and clinical support, integrating multidisciplinary care, and prioritizing early identification of high-risk patients, particularly those with complex CHDs, to further improve long-term outcomes in resource-limited settings.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. Follow-up did not consistently begin at birth, potentially underestimating early mortality, especially in patients with univentricular CHDs. There is a chance for the introduction of misclassification bias due to the retrospective nature of the study, which relies on chart reviews. Although the number of patients excluded from the study due to missing outcome data was relatively small, their exclusion may affect the estimated mortality rate. PH was diagnosed only by echocardiography, without cardiac catheterization confirmation, which may affect accuracy. Syndromic associations were identified solely based on phenotypic features, without genetic testing, possibly leading to misclassification. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable baseline data on CHD-related mortality, informing quality improvement initiatives and demonstrating the need to address both cardiac and extracardiac risk factors through targeted interventions in resource-limited settings.
