Preliminary evaluation of the impact of a simulation-based training program on needlestick injuries among nursing students: a quasi-experimental study guided by the Kirkpatrick model
Authors: Poonam Joshi, Madhu Madhu, Biju V., Rashmita Devi, Grace Steffy Roseben, Suvashri Sasmal, Smita Ranjan, Surya Kant Tiwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeedlestick injuries (NSIs) pose a significant occupational threat to nursing students. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of a simulation-based training (SBT) program for NSI prevention among nursing students using the Kirkpatrick model.MethodsA quasi-experimental, one-group pre-test/post-test study was conducted with 206 nursing students from a government college in Eastern India. The participants underwent a two-day blended SBT program that incorporated didactic sessions and scenario-based simulations with debriefing using Rapid Cycle Deliberate Practice. Outcomes were evaluated across four Kirkpatrick levels: reaction (satisfaction), learning (knowledge and self-reported practices), behavior (observed practices), and results (NSI incidence). Data were collected at baseline, immediately post-intervention, and at one- and three-month follow-ups.ResultsThe mean satisfaction score was high (17.6 ± 2.8/20). Knowledge scores significantly improved from 13.6 ± 3.4 to 16.0 ± 2.9 (mean difference: 2.4; 95% CI: 1.90–2.90; p < 0.001; d = 0.78). Self-reported practice scores showed a large increase from 13.3 ± 1.7 to 17.3 ± 3.0 (mean difference: 4.0; 95% CI: 3.54–4.46; p < 0.001; d = 1.20). The observed behavior scores at three months were 16.1 ± 2.0 (n = 108). The number of reported NSI exposures fell from 48 (23.3%) in the year before training to 6 (2.9%) in the three months after training (an 87.5% relative reduction). Given the unequal recall periods (12 months vs. three months), this reduction should be considered exploratory. Attitude scores also showed significant and sustained improvements from baseline to three months (p < 0.01).ConclusionsThe SBT program was associated with improvements in knowledge, self-reported practices, attitudes, and observed behaviors; it was well accepted and accompanied by a reduction in reported NSIs. However, the lack of a control group and the single-center design limit causal inference. Incorporating SBT into nursing education appears promising for enhancing occupational safety; however, multicenter controlled trials are needed to establish causal evidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04897-1.

Introduction

Needlestick injuries (NSIs) represent a significant occupational hazard for healthcare workers globally, with nursing students being particularly vulnerable during clinical training [1, 2]. A recent meta-analysis indicated that 35% of nursing students experience at least one NSI during their education, a rate comparable to or even exceeding that of practicing nurses [1, 3]. The consequences of NSIs extend beyond immediate physical trauma and the risk of bloodborne pathogen transmission, potentially affecting students’ psychological well-being, clinical confidence, and long-term career choices [4–6].

Effective prevention requires a multifaceted approach that addresses both systemic factors and individual knowledge and behaviors. Although didactic education on safety protocols is common in nursing curricula [7], evidence shows that knowledge alone does not reliably translate into consistently safe practice in high-pressure clinical environments [8].

Simulation-based training (SBT) offers a powerful pedagogical alternative that allows learners to develop and refine psychomotor skills and clinical reasoning in a realistic yet consequence-free setting [9]. SBT is posited to enhance cognitive control over acute, unforeseen situations like NSIs, thereby bridging the theory-practice gap [9].

However, the evidence on SBT specifically for NSI prevention among nursing students remains limited in three critical areas. First, existing studies have largely focused on knowledge acquisition alone without measuring actual behavioral changes or injury outcomes [10–12]. Second, no prior study has applied the full Kirkpatrick evaluation framework to an NSI prevention SBT program for nursing students. Third, previous interventions rarely included observed clinical behavior or post-intervention NSI incidence as endpoints. A preliminary survey at our institution revealed an annual NSI incidence of 8.6%, underscoring the urgent need for effective evidence-based interventions.

Theoretical framework

The study was guided by two complementary frameworks. First, the Kirkpatrick Model of training evaluation provided the overarching structure for assessing program impact across four levels: reaction, learning, behavior, and results [13]. Second, Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory informed the simulation design by emphasizing that deep, transferable learning occurs through cycles of concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation [14]. Together, these frameworks supported a program evaluation approach that moves beyond measuring immediate knowledge gains to capturing behavioral transfer and clinical outcomes.

To address these gaps, we developed, implemented, and evaluated a comprehensive SBT program for NSI prevention, guided by the Kirkpatrick model. To our knowledge, based on a literature search conducted up to December 2024, this appears to be the first study to evaluate an SBT program for NSI prevention in nursing students using all four Kirkpatrick levels (reaction, learning, behavior, and results). It also included observed behavior and actual injury outcomes as endpoints. Our aim was to assess the program’s impact not only on immediate knowledge and skill gains but also on participants’ satisfaction, observed clinical behavior, and ultimately NSI incidence.

Materials and methods

Study design

This quasi-experimental, one-group, pre-test/post-test study was conducted between November 2024 and April 2025. The study followed the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Non-randomized Designs (TREND) guidelines [15].

Setting and participants

The study was conducted at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, Kalyani, West Bengal, India. We used total enumeration sampling to recruit nursing students for the degree programme. The eligibility criteria were as follows: (1) students enrolled in a nursing degree program and (2) aged 17 years or older. We excluded students who were unavailable during the data collection period or who declined to provide informed consent.

Sample size

An a priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software. Based on a previous study [2], we needed a minimum of 179 participants to achieve 95% power at a two-tailed alpha level of 0.05. The calculation assumed a mean pre–post change of 1.0 points, SD = 4.12, and a pre–post correlation of 0.6. After allowing for a 10% attrition rate, the final target sample size was 199.

Intervention: SBT program

The research team developed the program using a blended learning approach that combined didactic instruction with hands-on simulation. Development drew on a needs assessment and NSI prevention guidelines from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the World Health Organization. Two certified simulation trainers (nurse educators with > 5 years of simulation experience) delivered the program over two days (12 h total).

Before implementation, we pilot tested the program with 10 nursing students not included in the main study sample. Pilot testing identified minor timing issues (scenarios ran 5–10 min longer than planned) and clarified two ambiguous checklist items. No changes in the content were required. Structure and content of the simulation-based training program for NSI prevention

Day 1

Didactic Sessions

By end of Day 1, participants will be able to:

(1) describe NSI epidemiology and risks;

(2) list standard precautions for sharps safety;

(3) explain step-by-step post-exposure management.

Six interactive lectures:

(1) NSI epidemiology and burden (1 h);

(2) Bloodborne pathogens (HIV, HBV, HCV) – transmission risks (1 h);

(3) Standard precautions and sharps safety (1 h);

(4) Biomedical waste management (1 h);

(5) Post-exposure prophylaxis protocols (1 h);

(6) Reporting systems and legal aspects (1 h).

Teaching methods: PowerPoint with case vignettes, Q&A, and group discussion.

Day 2

Simulation Sessions

By end of Day 2, participants will be able to:

(1) demonstrate correct first aid after an NSI;

(2) demonstrate proper reporting procedure;

(3) initiate PEP steps correctly;

(4) demonstrate safe sharps handling during clinical procedures.

Pre-briefing (30 min): Orientation to simulation environment, psychological safety, roles, and “stop the line” principle.

Three scenario-based simulations: Each scenario runs 20–25 min per small group, plus 15–20 min for RCDP feedback and repetition. Students rotate through all three scenarios.

• Scenario 1 (Venipuncture with NSI) – 80 min (including RCDP cycles)

• Scenario 2 (Random blood sugar monitoring with NSI) – 80 min

• Scenario 3 (IM injection with NSI during recapping) – 80 min

Debriefing (90 min total): Using the PEARLS framework – includes plus/delta, advocacy-inquiry, and summary.

Themes of simulation scenarios:

The simulation session included three scenarios delivered in the following sequence.

Theme 1: Venipuncture with accidental NSI

The student performs venipuncture on a manikin, and a needlestick occurs during withdrawal. Required actions: stop procedure, first aid (wash, squeeze, do not suck), report to supervisor, and initiate post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP).

Theme 2: Random blood sugar monitoring with NSI

The student uses a lancet on a simulated finger, and lancet sticks occur. Required actions: same as above, plus proper disposal of the lancet into a sharp container.

Theme 3: Intramuscular injection (IM) with NSI during recapping

The student administers an IM injection and then attempts to recap the needle using a two‑hand technique, resulting in a stick. Required actions: same as above, plus demonstration of the one‑handed scoop recapping method.

Sequence rationale: Scenarios progress from a common clinical procedure (venipuncture) to a simpler task (RBS) to a higher risk behavior (recapping). This allows students to build confidence before addressing the most preventable cause of NSIs.

Trainer qualifications and standardization: Both trainers completed a 2‑day train‑the‑trainer workshop on Rapid Cycle Deliberate Practice (RCDP) and Promoting Excellence and Reflective Learning in Simulation (PEARLS) debriefing [16, 17]. Trainers used a standardised script and checklist for each scenario.

Participants’ roles during the simulation: The students actively participated as primary providers in rotation. Non‑acting students observed the simulation in the same room and participated in debriefing. All the students completed all three scenarios.

Fidelity and safety: No sharps or blood were used. Simulated blood (corn syrup with red food coloring) and blunt training needles were also used. Trainers informed students of this before they participated.

No booster sessions were scheduled during the three-month follow-up period, enabling assessment of the standalone program’s durability.

Tool development and validation

We developed a comprehensive data collection kit comprising five primary instruments; the complete set of data collection tools is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Sociodemographic and NSI history proforma

This tool collects data on age, year of study, and detailed NSI histories. The NSI history section captured the number of lifetime injuries, type of injury (superficial, moderate, severe), location of injury, device involved, and immunization status against Hepatitis B. It also recorded whether the injury was reported and whether medical attention was received within 2 h.

Knowledge questionnaire

We used a 20-item multiple-choice questionnaire to assess participants’ knowledge of NSIs. The content covered the domains of causes, risks, immediate actions, prevention strategies, reporting protocols, and PEP. Each item had one correct answer and three distractors each. The scoring system awarded 1 point for a correct answer and 0 points for an incorrect or unanswered question, yielding a maximum possible score of 20. Higher scores indicate greater levels of knowledge.

Attitude scale

We measured attitudes towards NSIs using an 11-item 5-point Likert-type scale. The response options ranged from ‘Strongly Disagree’ [1] to ‘Strongly Agree’ [5]. Items assessed constructs such as perceived susceptibility and severity, beliefs about preventability, confidence in preventive measures, and emotional responses to NSIs. Negatively worded items were reverse-scored. The total score ranges from 11 to 55, with higher scores indicating a more positive and safety-oriented attitude.

Practices scale

We assessed self-reported practices related to NSI prevention using a 20-item binary-response questionnaire (Yes = 1, No = 0). Items covered safe sharps disposal, consistent PPE use, one-handed recapping technique, first aid after a needlestick injury, post-exposure prophylaxis initiation, sharps container management, and NSI reporting. The total score ranged from 0 to 20, with higher scores indicating safer self-reported practices.

Satisfaction scale

We evaluated participants’ immediate reactions to the SBT using a 4-item scale. The items assessed the perceived usefulness, relevance, realism of the simulation, and quality of the debriefing. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert-type scale, yielding total scores ranging from 4 to 20.

Observed behavior checklist

We developed a checklist to assess adherence to safe injection and sharp-handling practices in clinical settings. The checklist included critical steps such as hand hygiene, equipment preparation, safe manipulation of sharps, immediate disposal in puncture-proof containers, and post-procedure actions. It was scored as ‘done’ (1) or ‘Not Done’ (0), yielding a maximum possible score of 17.

Observer training

Three nurse educators (not involved in delivering the training program) served as observers. They reviewed the checklist together, discussed the operational definitions for each item, and practiced scoring two mock clinical observations.

Validity and reliability of instruments

Content validity for all five primary instruments was established through review by a panel of five subject matter experts (two senior nursing educators, two infection control practitioners, and one biostatistician). Items with a Content Validity Index (CVI) below 0.78 were revised or discarded. Scale-level CVI scores were: Knowledge Questionnaire (0.91), Attitude Scale (0.89), Practices Scale (0.88), and Satisfaction Scale (face validity confirmed by expert consensus). The Observed Behavior Checklist was reviewed by the same panel for content coverage and operational clarity. Internal consistency was assessed using baseline data. For knowledge and practices instruments, items are binary-scored (0/1), making KR-20 the appropriate reliability coefficient. KR-20 = 0.756 (Knowledge Questionnaire) and 0.79 (Practices Scale); Cronbach’s α = 0.81 (Attitude Scale) and 0.80 (Satisfaction Scale).

Selection of the observed subset

We conducted the observed behavior assessment three months post-intervention in a subset of 108 students. This subset consisted of all students who were on clinical rotation in the inpatient departments during the two-week observation window (convenience sampling based on clinical schedules).

Outcome measures and variables

The study evaluated the intervention using the Kirkpatrick model across four levels:

Reaction: Participant satisfaction with the training was measured immediately after the intervention using a satisfaction scale.

Learning: Change in knowledge (assessed using a knowledge questionnaire) and self-reported practices (assessed using a practices scale).

Behavior: Observed clinical behavior was assessed at three months post‑intervention in a subset of 108 students using an observed behavior checklist during clinical practicum.

Results: The primary clinical outcome was the change in the number of self-reported NSI incidents collected at baseline (referring to the past year) and at the three-month follow-up.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected across four time points. At baseline (T1), prior to the intervention, all participants completed a sociodemographic proforma, knowledge questionnaire, attitude scale, and practices scale. Immediately post-intervention (T2), participants completed the knowledge questionnaire, practices scale, and satisfaction scale. At one-month follow-up (T3), the attitude scale was re-administered to assess medium-term retention of attitudinal changes. At three-month follow-up (T4), the attitude scale was re-administered to assess sustained attitudinal change; the observed behavior checklist was used to evaluate a subset of 108 students during their routine clinical practice; concurrently, NSI incidence data for the three months since the training were collected via self-report.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were used to summarize the participant characteristics and outcome variables. A paired t-test was used to compare pre- and post-intervention knowledge and self-reported practice scores. Repeated Measures Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post-hoc tests was used to analyze attitude scores across three time points (baseline [T1], one month [T3], and three months [T4] post-intervention). Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d for t-tests and partial eta-squared (η²p) for ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC-250/16.05.2023). This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after a detailed explanation of the study’s procedures. Anonymity and confidentiality were maintained throughout the study.

Results

Participant characteristics and baseline NSI history

All 206 enrolled nursing students (mean age 21.5 ± 1.7 years) completed the baseline assessments. The majority (79.1%, n = 163) reported no history of NSI, while 20.9% (n = 43) reported at least one incident. Among these 43 students with injuries (who collectively reported 48 NSI events), most were superficial (65.1%, n = 28) and involved hypodermic needles (44.2%, n = 19). The inpatient department was the most common location (76.7%, n = 33). Critically, only 34.9% (n = 15/43) of the students who experienced an NSI formally reported it (Table 2).

Kirkpatrick level 1: Reaction

Participant satisfaction with the SBT was high, with a mean score of 17.6 ± 2.8 on a 4-20-point scale.

Kirkpatrick level 2: Learning

The intervention led to significant improvements in learning outcomes. Knowledge scores increased from 13.6 ± 3.4 to 16.0 ± 2.9 (mean difference: 2.4, 95% CI: 1.90–2.90, p < 0.001, d = 0.78). Self-reported practice scores showed a larger increase, from 13.3 ± 1.7 to 17.3 ± 3.0 (out of a maximum of 20; mean difference: 4.0, 95% CI: 3.54–4.46, p < 0.001, d = 1.20) (Table 3).

Kirkpatrick level 3: Behavior

Direct observation of a subset of 108 students three months post-intervention demonstrated high adherence to safe practices, with a mean score of 16.1 ± 2.0 on a 17-point checklist. Baseline observed behavior scores were not collected because the checklist was designed for post-intervention use only, which limits the before-after comparison.

Kirkpatrick level 4: Results

At baseline, participants reported NSIs occurring in the 12 months before the intervention. At three months post‑intervention, participants reported NSIs that occurred during the three months following the training. Reported exposures fell from 48 (23.3%) in the pre‑intervention year to 6 (2.9%) in the post‑intervention three months, representing an absolute reduction of 42 cases and a relative reduction of 87.5%. These figures come from different recall periods (12 months vs. 3 months) and should not be directly compared. When expressed as person-time rates, the pre-intervention exposure was approximately 0.019 per person-month (48 events/206 students/12 months) and the post-intervention rate was approximately 0.010 events per person-month (6 events/206 students/3 months). Given the unequal recall periods, these rates should be interpreted with caution, and the observed reduction should be considered exploratory. Hepatitis B immunization coverage also improved among all participants (Table 3).

Longitudinal change in attitude

Among the 201 participants with complete follow-up data, a repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant effect of time on attitude scores (F(2,400) = 14.2, p < 0.001, partial η ² = 0.066). Scores improved from baseline (T1:34.1 ± 4.74) to one month (T3:36.0 ± 4.59) and three months (T4:37.0 ± 4.24) post-intervention. Post-hoc tests confirmed significant improvements from T1 to T3 (p < 0.002) and T1 to T4 (p < 0.01), but not between T3 and T4 (p = 0.329), indicating a sustained improvement (Table 4).

Discussion

This quasi-experimental study found that a structured SBT program was associated with improvements across all four Kirkpatrick levels and with a subsequent reduction in self-reported NSIs among nursing students. However, the lack of a control group and different recall periods for NSI incidence require cautious interpretation of these findings.

Baseline data confirmed significant occupational hazards in this population, with nearly one in five students reporting at least one injury. The high rate of under-reporting (only 34.9% of injured students formally reported their injury) is a critical finding. This aligns with the global literature that identifies fear, stigma, and lack of knowledge about protocols as major barriers [18, 19]. This underscores the necessity of interventions that not only teach technical skills but also actively foster a culture of safety and open reporting. The elevated under-reporting rate at baseline also represents a significant confounding variable for the NSI incidence outcome. Training-induced awareness of the duty to report may have increased formal reporting at follow-up, which would paradoxically reduce the apparent post-training incidence without reflecting a true fall in events. Addressing systemic under-reporting therefore requires broader organizational and structural changes beyond individual-level education.

The SBT program successfully addressed the “Learning” level of the Kirkpatrick model. The significant improvement in knowledge scores (p < 0.001, d = 0.78) is consistent with studies that used educational interventions for NSI prevention [20, 21]. The large improvement in self-reported practices (p < 0.001, d = 1.20) suggests that simulation scenarios and deliberate practice are particularly effective in building procedural confidence and competence. However, the substantially larger effect size for self-reported practices compared with objective knowledge scores (d = 1.20 vs. d = 0.78) warrants caution. This discrepancy may partly reflect social desirability bias, as participants may have over-reported their likelihood of engaging in safe behaviors after training. Objective behavioral assessment and blinded observation are needed to confirm these gains. This translation of knowledge into practical skills is a key advantage of SBT over traditional didactic methods and is essential for lasting behavioral change [9]. Our previous work in India demonstrated that simulation-based training significantly improves nurses’ knowledge, skills, and confidence in pediatric airway management [22]. It was also as effective as traditional methods for neonatal resuscitation [23]. These findings support the broader applicability of simulations across different clinical domains in nursing education.

Our findings at the “Behavior” and “Results” levels are promising but require further clarification to be useful. The high observed behavior scores three months post-intervention (mean 16.1/17) suggest that the learned skills were transferred to the clinical setting, at least among the subset assessed. However, baseline observational data were not collected, and the subset was selected by convenience, which limits the before-after comparison and may introduce selection bias.

Regarding NSI incidence, the reported exposures fell from 48 (23.3%) in the year before training to 6 (2.9%) in the three months after training, an 87.5% relative reduction. When expressed as person-time rates, this corresponds to approximately 0.019 per person-month before training and 0.010 per person-month after training. Several factors warrant caution in interpreting these findings. First, the recall periods were unequal (12 months vs. 3 months), which preclude valid direct comparison. Second, the absence of a control group means that we cannot rule out history effects (e.g., changes in clinical supervision), maturation, or concurrent learning from routine clinical rotations. Third, improved reporting behavior after training could have increased baseline reporting (i.e., students recalled past injuries more accurately) or reduced under-reporting at follow-up, potentially inflating the apparent reduction. Fourth, regression to the mean is possible, given the high baseline incidence. Therefore, this finding should be considered exploratory rather than definitive evidence of a causal effect.

The sustained improvement in attitude scores over three months, with significant gains from baseline to both follow-ups, indicates a positive shift in students’ underlying safety culture and perceptions. However, the effect size was small (partial η² = 0.066), suggesting that attitudes may be more resistant to change than knowledge or skills. The fact that scores plateaued between one and three months without declining is encouraging, indicating that the modest attitudinal shift was retained in the medium term.

Our results support the potential of experiential learning for NSI prevention training. While some studies have found that theoretical training alone fails to reduce NSI rates [24, 25], our blended SBT program, which integrates theory with repetitive and feedback-driven practice, yielded promising results. These findings align with those of meta-analyses confirming that simulation is superior to problem-based learning for acquiring critical procedural and management skills [26]. However, studies with stronger designs are needed to confirm whether SBT causally reduces the incidence of NSIs.

Strengths and limitations

This study had several strengths, including an adequate sample size, the use of a validated evaluation framework (Kirkpatrick model), and the measurement of both proximal (knowledge and skills) and distal outcomes (observed behavior and NSI incidence). The inclusion of a three-month follow-up for behavior and attitude provided valuable insight into the sustainability of the intervention’s effects.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the single-group pre-test/post-test design without a control group is a major limitation. We cannot definitively attribute the observed improvements to the intervention; history, maturation, testing effects, and concurrent clinical training may have contributed. Second, the study was conducted at a single government nursing college in Eastern India. The results may not be generalizable to other settings (private institutions, different regions, or countries with different healthcare systems and NSI reporting cultures). Multicenter replication is needed before broader application. Third, the use of self-reported data on NSI history, practices, and satisfaction is susceptible to recall and social desirability bias. Improved reporting after training could explain the apparent reduction in the NSI incidence. Fourth, observed behavior was assessed only in a convenience subset with no baseline observation. The absence of a pre-observation baseline and the convenience-based selection of the subset preclude a valid before–after behavioral comparison and may overestimate post-training adherence through selection bias. Fifth, formal inter-rater reliability for the observed behavior checklist was not calculated; no intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) or kappa statistic was computed for observer agreement, which limits the reproducibility of the observed behavior score. Sixth, the three-month follow-up period, while valuable, was insufficient to assess long-term retention over the entirety of students’ training. Seventh, the training duration was short (two days). Future studies should examine booster sessions or longer programs to determine the optimal dosage. Finally, the construct validity of the study instruments was not formally assessed beyond content validity and internal consistency.

Implications for practice and research

Implications for Nursing Education. Integrating similar SBT modules into undergraduate nursing curricula can be a promising strategy for protecting students from preventable occupational injuries. Nurse educators and program directors should consider embedding structured simulation sessions on sharps safety—incorporating RCDP-based practice and structured debriefing—into both pre-clinical orientation and ongoing training. The program’s high satisfaction scores indicate that it is an engaging and acceptable pedagogical method that can complement existing curricula without replacing established safe-practice instruction.

Implications for Hospital Administrators and Infection Control Teams. Hospital administrators and infection control practitioners should work alongside nursing colleges to establish mandatory pre-clinical placement training on sharps safety and NSI reporting protocols. Equally important is addressing organizational barriers to reporting: the baseline under-reporting rate of 65.1% highlights that institutional culture, stigma reduction, and simplified reporting pathways must accompany any educational intervention. Training alone is insufficient without a supportive reporting environment.

Implications for Future Research. Future research should prioritize multicenter randomized controlled trials with longer follow-up periods and active control groups to establish causal evidence. Future work could also evaluate the cost-effectiveness of SBT programs and investigate which simulation components (e.g., debriefing style, number of RCDP repetitions, scenario complexity) drive the most durable behavioral change. Additionally, qualitative and mixed-methods approaches—such as Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR), Online Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (OIPA), and Online Photovoice (OPV)—would complement quantitative findings. These methods could capture nursing students’ lived NSI experiences, barriers to reporting, and perceptions of safety culture from their own perspectives, thereby informing more grounded and context-sensitive interventions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides preliminary evidence that a simulation-based training program is associated with improvements in knowledge, self-reported practices, attitudes, and observed safe behaviors, alongside a reduction in self-reported NSI incidence among nursing students. However, the lack of a control group and other methodological limitations preclude causal inferences. Pending confirmation from randomized controlled trials, integrating SBT into nursing curricula appears to be a promising strategy for enhancing occupational safety.
