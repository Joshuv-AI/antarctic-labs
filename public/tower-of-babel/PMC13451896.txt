Exercise attenuates neuropathic pain and neuroinflammation in an obese rat model exhibiting diabetes-related metabolic dysfunction and features of type 2 diabetes
Authors: Megumi Sumizono, Shogo Kakimoto, Kazuki Nakanishi, Ryoma Matsuzaki, Yuki Kato, Yushin Yoshizato, Takaki Imai, Harutoshi Sakakima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the effects and mechanisms of exercise intervention on neuropathic pain in an obese rat model exhibiting diabetes-related metabolic dysfunction. Chronic constriction injury (CCI) was induced in Otsuka Long-Evans Tokushima Fatty (OLETF) rats, and the animals were randomly assigned to three groups: exercise (Ex), no-exercise (No-Ex), and normal controls. The 50% pain response threshold was significantly higher in the Ex group than in the No-Ex group 5 weeks post-CCI (p < 0.05). Monocyte chemoattractant protein-1(MCP-1), C-C chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2), P2X purinoceptor 4 (P2X4R), and tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6(TRAF6) which are related to microglia in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, were significantly decreased in the Ex group compared to the No-Ex group (p < 0.05). In the hippocampal dentate gyrus, tumor necrosis factor-α(TNF-α) and interleukin 6(IL-6) were significantly decreased in the Ex group compared with the No-Ex group (p < 0.05). However, no significant differences were observed in doublecortin expression, the Y-maze, or the alternation rate in the passive avoidance test (p > 0.05). In neuropathic pain in diabetes, targets of interest include not only P2X4R but also CCR2 and TRAF6. Furthermore, the synergistic effects of chronic pain and neuroinflammation, such as neuropathic pain with a diabetic background, may accelerate the progression of dysfunction in memory-related regions and require continued validation.Keywords: CCR2, metabolic dysfunction, neuropathic pain, hippocampal neurogenesis, TRAF6

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a metabolic disorder characterized by chronic hyperglycemia resulting from a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors. The global prevalence of diabetes, particularly type 2 diabetes, continues to rise because of population aging, lifestyle changes, and increasing rates of obesity and physical inactivity. As the number of individuals with type 2 diabetes increases, diabetes-related complications, including neuropathic pain, have become a major clinical and socioeconomic burden (Bellary et al. 2021). Several studies have suggested that both type 1 and 2 diabetes are associated with an increased risk of dementia and reduced cognitive function (Wium-Andersen et al. 2019). The risk of developing dementia is approximately 60% higher in patients with type 2 diabetes than in individuals without diabetes (Chatterjee et al. 2016). Furthermore, the comorbidity of diabetic neuropathic pain and depression is more difficult to treat than diabetic neuropathic pain alone, and patients with both conditions experience a lower quality of life (Wei et al. 2020).

Neuropathic pain is triggered by damage to the central or peripheral nervous system and is associated with the activation of microglia and astrocytes in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord following nerve injury, resulting in the production of inflammatory mediators and abnormal neuronal function (Inoue and Tsuda, 2009, Tsuda, 2016, Malcangio and Sideris-Lampretsas, 2025). Peripheral nerve injury associated with neuropathic pain can induce pathological inflammation in the dentate gyrus, leading to reduced neurogenesis and memory impairment (Egorova et al., 2019, Sumizono et al., 2022). Inflammatory pain is characterized by persistent nociceptor activation, and anti-inflammatory responses contribute to pain relief. In contrast, nerve injury has been reported to induce hypersensitivity (central sensitization) and pain memory, resulting in the reorganization of the nervous system following the loss of peripheral afferent input after peripheral nerve damage (Lavado et al., 2010).

Pharmacological treatment options for neuropathic pain remain limited, with 40–60% of patients failing to achieve even partial pain relief (Dworkin et al., 2007, Bannister et al., 2020, Sumizono et al., 2022). Numerous studies have investigated the therapeutic effects and underlying mechanisms of treatment in both humans and animal models; however, many aspects remain unclear. For example, exercise has been shown to reduce neuropathic pain, but the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. We previously reported that the suppression of microglia and astrocytes in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, activation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) released from microglia, and activation of the descending inhibitory system through endogenous opioids were associated with the analgesic effects of exercise therapy on neuropathic pain (Sumizono et al., 2018). Furthermore, we found that changes in the expression of spinal glial cells, C-C chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2), tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6), and the dynamics of doublecortin (DCX) and Prox1 in the hippocampal dentate gyrus associated with neuropathic pain were related to the effects of regular exercise therapy. These findings indicate that TRAF6 is a promising therapeutic target in glial cells because of its role in the maintenance of neuropathic pain (Sumizono et al., 2022).

Exercise is also a strategy that can both prevent the development of pain and alleviate it after injury. Among 64 published studies on exercise-induced analgesia, 58 (∼90%) reported positive effects of exercise using a single activity modality. Activation of the descending pain inhibitory system in the midbrain has also been reported, suggesting that neuropathic pain relief may be mediated through this mechanism (Lesnak and Sluka, 2020, Sumizono et al., 2024). The benefits of exercise include delaying the onset of diabetic pain and tactile hyperalgesia, as well as reducing functional allodynia and hyperalgesia following peripheral nerve injury. Furthermore, regular exercise has been shown to decrease neuropathic pain in humans, improve cutaneous sensation, and reduce inflammatory cytokine levels (Holmes and Hastings, 2021, Luo et al., 2022). Regarding the prevention of diabetes onset, training once every 2–3 days has been reported to be as effective as daily training in preventing the development of non-insulin-dependent diabetes and associated islet morphological changes (Shima et al., 1994). However, the effects of exercise on peripheral neuropathy remain poorly understood. The pathogenesis of pain in diabetic neuropathy (DN) involves multiple factors, including hyperglycemia, advanced glycation end products (AGEs), glycogen synthase kinase−3 (GSK−3), glial activity, BDNF, and the inflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin−1β (IL-1β), and interleukin−6 (IL-6). Some reports suggest that exercise may delay, although not necessarily reverse, the progression of symptoms (Dobson, McMillan and Li, 2014). Furthermore, activation of P2X purinoceptor 4 (P2X4R) has been reported to be essential for the maintenance of hyperalgesia associated with diabetes-induced neuropathy (Teixeira et al., 2019).

Neuropathic pain in patients with diabetes is a significant complication that markedly impairs quality of life. However, the mechanisms underlying its development in type 2 diabetes remain poorly understood, partly because conventional animal models do not adequately reflect human pathology. To address this limitation, we employed Otsuka Long-Evans Tokushima Fatty (OLETF) rats, a spontaneous animal model widely used to study obesity and diabetes-related metabolic dysfunction. OLETF rats are characterized by progressive obesity and impaired glucose and lipid metabolism and have been reported to exhibit several features of type 2 diabetes, including impaired glucose tolerance and age-related hyperinsulinemia (Kawano et al., 1992, Gao and Zheng, 2014, Shima et al., 2017, Oshima et al., 2024). In the present study, we investigated the effects of exercise intervention on neuropathic pain and focused on key immune-related molecules in the spinal dorsal horn (P2X4R, MCP−1, CCR2, and TRAF6) and the hippocampal dentate gyrus (TNF-α, IL-6, and DCX).

Experimental procedures

Animals

Eight-week-old male diabetic model rats (Otsuka Long-Evans Tokushima Fatty: OLETF, n = 15) and male Long-Evans Tokushima Otsuka (LETO) rats (n = 5), used as controls for the OLETF rat model, were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. (Hamamatsu, Japan). The rats were maintained at room temperature (23.0 ± 1.0°C) under a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to solid food and water. Experiments were designed to use the minimum number of animals necessary to obtain equivalent or maximum information, including in studies that required long-term follow-up. This study was approved by the Kagoshima University Faculty of Medicine Animal Experimentation Committee (approval number: M23001). The protocol conformed to the Classification of Biomedical Experimental Processes Based on Ethical Considerations for Nonhuman Species (Consensus Recommendations for Effective Institutional Animal Experimentation), and all experiments were conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines. In addition, all animal procedures adhered to the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Only male rats were used to minimize sex-related variability in metabolic and neuropathic pain phenotypes; therefore, sex- and gender-based analyses were not performed.

Chronic constriction injury model (CCI)

Rats were anesthetized with 2–3% isoflurane using a small-animal inhalation anesthesia apparatus (MK-A110D, Muromachi Kikai Co., Ltd., Japan). The skin covering the right thigh was incised and carefully dissected to avoid injury to the sciatic nerve; four ligature threads (4–0 silk) were loosely tied around the right sciatic nerve at approximately 1 mm intervals. The length of the nerve affected by the ligatures was approximately 6 mm. The incision was closed with 4–0 sutures, and the animals were returned to their cages for recovery. This procedure was a modification of the method proposed by Bennett and Xie (Bennett and Xie, 1988).

Regular treadmill exercise and the experimental group

Exercise was performed on a treadmill (MK−680, MUROMACHI KIKAI CO., LTD., Japan), and all rats were subjected to environmental adaptation at a speed of 20 m/min for 15 min with a 1.0 A voltage for 3 days. After environmental adaptation, the rats were randomly divided into three groups: CCI Exercise group (Ex, n = 5), CCI Non-exercise group (No-Ex, n = 5), and OLETF Normal group (Normal, n = 5). The exercise routine comprised treadmill exercise once per day at a speed of 20 m/min; after CCI surgery, free-cage activity was allowed for 2 days during recovery. The exercise intervention was initiated 2–3 days after CCI at a speed of 20 m/min for 15 min. The exercise duration was increased to 30 min after 1 day of exercise and maintained for up to 5 weeks after CCI (Sumizono et al., 2018, Sumizono et al., 2022). The workload of rats at this training intensity was approximately 55% of their maximum oxygen consumption (Véras-Silva et al., 1997, Bement and Sluka, 2005). Body weights were periodically measured, and stress induced by treadmill exercise was monitored. None of the animals were excluded from the study.

Change in the mechanical pain range value

Mechanical sensitivity was evaluated as the withdrawal response frequency using von Frey filaments (MUROMACHI KIKAI CO., Ltd., Japan) applied to both hind limbs. Measurements were taken before CCI and at 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 weeks after CCI (between 1:00 PM and 5:00 PM). Rats were placed in individual clear plastic cages on wire mesh grids to allow full access to the ventral aspect of the hind limbs. Measurements were obtained before the exercise intervention, and animals were allowed to acclimate to the experimental environment (room and apparatus) for at least 20 min. A logarithmic series of 11 filaments (0.41–28.84 g) was pressed vertically onto the plantar surface of the hind paw until the filament bent. The test was initiated with a 3.63 g filament. A up-down method was used in this study. Tactile stimuli producing a 50% probability of hind limb withdrawal responses were calculated using the following formula (Dixon, 1980, Chaplan et al., 1994, Mills et al., 2012, Christensen et al., 2020). The cut-off value was set at 28.84 g.

Warm pain hypersensitivity

Using a plantar-type analgesia measuring device (Model 37570; Ugo Basile, Gemonio, Italy), thermal stimuli were applied to the plantar surface of the hind paws, and escape-response latency was measured using focused radiant heat. Withdrawal latency was defined as the time between the onset of thermal stimulation and hind limb withdrawal. To avoid tissue damage, the cut-off time was set at 25 s, and measurements were performed twice, with at least 5 min between measurements (Guo et al., 2022, Mwobobia et al., 2021). The mean value of the two trials was used for data analysis. The tests were conducted preoperatively and at 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks, 3 weeks, 4 weeks, and 5 weeks post-CCI.

Y-Maze test

The Y-maze spontaneous alternation test was used to assess working memory, a function associated with the hippocampus. The apparatus consisted of three identical arms (51.5 × 10 × 25 cm) symmetrically separated by 120° (YM−03R; MUROMACHI KIKAI Co., Ltd., Japan). A video camera (Logitech HD Pro Webcam C920r; Logitech Co., Ltd., Lausanne, Switzerland) was mounted above the Y-maze to capture exploratory behavior. The rats were placed at the end of one arm and allowed to freely explore all three arms. Each rat was allowed to explore the maze for 6 min. An arm entry was recorded when the entire body, including the tail, entered an arm. The total number of arm entries (N) and the number of correct alternations (M; entries into all three arms consecutively without re-entry were recorded (Kambe et al., 2022). The alternation rate was calculated using the following formula: R (%) = M× 100/(N−2). The alternation rate was analyzed using SMART 3.0 (Panlab, Barcelona, Spain). The brightness of the room lighting during the test ranged from 150 to 300 lx (Nakanishi et al., 2021, Otsuka et al., 2022, Otsuka et al., 2024). The test was conducted between 9:00 AM and 2:00 PM.

Passive avoidance test

To examine the effects of chronic neuropathic pain on long-term memory, each rat was placed in the larger light compartment of a step-through passive avoidance apparatus (MST−01SR, MUROMACHI KIKAI Co., Ltd., Japan) and allowed to explore. Exploratory behavior was recorded for 10 min using a video camera (Logitech HD Pro Webcam C920r; Logitech Co., Ltd., Lausanne, Switzerland) mounted above the apparatus. When the rats entered the dark compartment, they received a mild electric shock (0.4 mA, 5 s) as an aversive stimulus. After 24 h, each rat was placed in the same apparatus, and the number of entries into the dark compartment and the time spent in the dark compartment were recorded (Dimitrova and Getova-Spassova, 2006). The brightness of the room lighting during the test ranged from 150 to 300 lx (Nakanishi et al., 2021, Otsuka et al., 2022, Otsuka et al., 2024). The test was conducted between 9:00 AM and 2:00 PM.

Open field test

An open-field apparatus (55 cm × 60 cm × 40 cm) was placed in a quiet environment. The apparatus was cleaned with a 70% ethanol solution between tests. A video camera (Logitech HD Pro Webcam C920r; Logitech Co., Ltd., Lausanne, Switzerland) was mounted above the open field. The rats were placed in the apparatus, and the total distance traveled during exploratory behavior was measured for 10 min using a video-tracking system (SMART Ver. 3.0; Panlab, Barcelona, Spain). SMART 3.0 (Panlab, Barcelona, Spain) was used for behavioral data analysis.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

The rats were sacrificed, and histological and immunohistochemical analyses were performed at 5 weeks (Ex: n = 5, No-Ex: n = 5, Normal: n = 5). Every effort was made to reduce the number of animals used in the study. The rats were deeply anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of 5% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg). Subsequently, blood was collected from the abdominal aorta, and the animals were euthanized by cardiac perfusion with heparinized saline.

The spinal cord and cerebrum were then removed and immersed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 4°C. After fixation, the tissues were dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. Sections (4 μm thick) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) to observe histological changes. Furthermore, immunohistochemical changes after CCI were analyzed.

After deparaffinization, the sections were immersed in 3% H₂O₂ solution for 10 min to inactivate endogenous peroxidase.

After washing three times for 5 min each in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.6), the sections were incubated with 10% skim milk in PBS to block nonspecific binding sites. The sections were then rinsed three times with PBS for 5 min each and incubated individually at 4°C with one of the following primary antibodies: anti-P2X4R antibody (1:400, rabbit polyclonal to P2X4R, Proteintech Group, Inc.), anti-MCP−1 antibody (1:400, rabbit polyclonal, Abcam plc; ab25124), a marker for C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2); anti-CCR2 antibody (1:1500, goat polyclonal, Novus Biologicals, USA); anti-TRAF6 antibody (1:600, mouse monoclonal, Proteintech Group, Inc., USA); anti-TNF-α antibody (1:100, rabbit polyclonal, Abcam plc; ab6671); anti-IL-6 antibody (1:800, Abcam plc; ab9324); and anti-DCX antibody (1:1500, rabbit polyclonal, Abcam plc; ab18723). After primary antibody incubation, sections stained for P2X4R, MCP−1, TRAF6, TNF-α, and IL-6 were rinsed three times in PBS for 5 min each. The sections were then incubated for 60 min with goat anti-rabbit IgG or anti-mouse IgG conjugated to a peroxidase-labeled dextran polymer (EnVision; Dako, CA, USA). The incubated sections were washed three times in PBS for 5 min each, followed by visualization of immunoreactivity using diaminobenzidine (DAB). For CCR2 staining, an ABC kit (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions. After three 5-min washes in PBS, immunoreactivity was visualized using DAB.

Blood analysis

Blood samples were collected in plastic tubes containing 3.2% sodium citrate (Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Serum was separated by centrifugation and stored at −80°C. Measurements were outsourced to the Nagahama Life Science Laboratory (Nagahama, Shiga Prefecture, Japan), Nagahama Plant, Oriental Yeast Co., Ltd., and were performed using an automatic analyzer (JCA-BM6050; Hitachi High-Tech Corporation). The facility is ISO 9001 certified. Serum glucose (GLU), glycoalbumin (GA), total ketone bodies (T-KB), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were measured. Each parameter was assessed using the following methods: the HK-G6PDH method for GLU, the enzymatic method for GA, the enzymatic cycling method for T-KB, the enzymatic method for TG, the direct method for HDL-C, and the JSCC standardized method for AST and ALT. For blood analysis, the OLETF and LETO groups were evaluated as indicators of diabetes onset and progression.

Quantitative analysis of immunostaining

A digital camera (DP21; Olympus Optical Co., Tokyo, Japan) attached to an optical microscope was used to capture images of stained sections of the spinal cord and cerebrum at 10 × magnification. The ratios of P2X4R-, MCP−1-, CCR2-, and TRAF6-immunoreactive areas within laminae I–III of the spinal dorsal horn, as well as the ratio of immunoreactive areas within the hippocampal dentate gyrus, were quantitatively measured using ImageJ software (NIH, USA) (Hochman, 2007, Sumizono et al., 2018, Sumizono et al., 2024, Sumizono et al., 2022). Immunostained areas were measured in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord ipsilateral to the injury. Quantitative analysis of each immunostained section was performed independently by three individuals blinded to the treatment groups, and the average value was used. This method was employed to quantify the percentage of immunostained area.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, followed by parametric or nonparametric tests as appropriate. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to examine the role of TRAF6. Analyses of the 50% pain response threshold, total distance traveled in the open field test, Y-maze alternation rate, and passive avoidance test were subjected to Bonferroni post hoc testing when a significant interaction was identified by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Other data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Student's t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error (SE). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM, USA). GraphPad Prism 9.3.1 (471) (GraphPad Holdings, LLC, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for graphing.

Results

Changes in biochemical tests and body weight in a spontaneous diabetes model

For GLU, an independent-samples t-test showed a significant difference in means between the groups (t(13) = 2.82, p = 0.015), with a mean difference of 28.80 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 6.71, 50.89) and an effect size (Cohen's d) of 1.54 (95% CI: 0.30, 2.74). After small-sample correction, Hedges' g was 1.45 and Glass' delta was 1.52, both showing similar trends.

For T-KB, an independent-samples t-test showed a significant difference in means between the groups (t(13) = 2.48, p = 0.027). The mean difference was 32.70 (95% CI: 4.24, 61.16), with a large effect size (Cohen's d) of 1.36 (95% CI: 0.15, 2.53). After small-sample correction, Hedges' g was 1.28 and Glass' delta was 1.12, both showing similar trends.

GLU and T-KB levels were significantly higher in the OLETF group than in the LETO group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1A, B).

Compared to the OLETF and LETO groups, the OLETF group, a type 2 diabetes model, show significantly higher values for GLU (A), T-KB (B), TG (C), and HDL-C (D). For TG, an independent-samples t-test showed a significant difference in means between the groups (t(13) = 3.47, p = 0.004). The mean difference was 83.90 (95% CI: 31.60, 136.20), with an effect size (Cohen's d) of 1.90 (95% CI: 0.58, 3.17), indicating a large effect size. After small-sample correction, Hedges' g was 1.79 and Glass' delta was 3.65, both suggesting strong effects.

For HDL-C, an independent-samples t-test showed a significant difference in means between the groups (t (13) = 3.16, p = 0.008). The mean difference was 5.20 (95% CI: 1.64, 8.76), with an effect size (Cohen's d) of 1.73 (95% CI: 0.45, 2.96), indicating a large effect size. After small-sample correction, Hedges' g was 1.63 and Glass' delta was 1.48, both suggesting strong effects.

The OLETF group showed significantly higher levels of TG and HDL-C than the LETO group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1C, D). No significant difference was observed in GA (p > 0.05) (Fig. 1E).

For AST, an independent-samples t-test showed no significant difference in means between the groups (t (13) = 0.30, p = 0.770). The mean difference was 4.30 (95% CI: −26.80, 35.40), with a small effect size (Cohen's d) of 0.16 (95% CI: −0.92, 1.24), indicating limited differences between the groups.

For ALT, an independent-samples t-test showed a significant difference in means between the groups (t (13) = 3.75, p = 0.002). The mean difference was 11.70 (95% CI: 4.96, 18.44), and the effect size (Cohen's d) was 2.06 (95% CI: 0.70, 3.36), indicating a large effect size. After small-sample correction, Hedges' g was 1.93 and Glass' delta was 4.49, both suggesting strong effects.

Regarding liver function-related parameters, no significant difference was observed in AST. However, ALT values were significantly higher in the OLETF group than in the LETO group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1F, G).

An independent-samples t-test revealed no significant difference in the AST/ALT ratio between the groups (t (13) = −1.70, p = 0.11). The mean difference was −0.62 (95% CI: −1.41–0.17), indicating no significant difference between the groups (Fig. 1H).

For body weight, the main effect of group was statistically significant (F (2,72) = 189.16, p < 0.001, η²= 0.565). The main effect of time was also significant (F (5,72) = 42.80, p < 0.001, η²= 0.320), with both showing large effect sizes. In contrast, the interaction between group and time was not significant (F (10,72) = 0.48, p = 0.897, η²= 0.007), indicating few differences in the temporal patterns among the groups.

The body weights of the OLETF group (Ex + No-Ex groups) and the LETO group (Normal group) were measured weekly. Both groups showed gradual increases in body weight, with the Ex and No-Ex groups exhibiting significantly greater body weights than the Normal group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1). No significant adverse events were observed in any experimental group during the study.

Changes in hyperalgesia and hyperthermia after CCI

The 50% pain response threshold (g), measured using the von Frey test, was evaluated before CCI and at 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 weeks after CCI in the Ex, No-Ex, and Normal groups (n = 5 per group at each time point) (Fig. 2A).

Regarding warm pain hypersensitivity (B), other groups are as variable as the Normal group (n = 5), and no abnormal findings are identified. A two-way analysis of variance showed a significant main effect of group (F(2,10) = 13.99, p = 0.001, η²= 0.737). The main effect of time did not reach statistical significance (F (5,10) = 2.998, p = 0.066, η²= 0.600). In contrast, the interaction between group and time was significant (F (10,72) = 3.61, p = 0.001, η²= 0.334).

The pain response thresholds in both the Ex and No-Ex groups improved at 2 weeks compared with those at 1 week after CCI. The Ex group showed a trend toward improvement at 4 weeks after CCI compared with the No-Ex group; however, the difference was not significant. At 5 weeks after CCI, the Ex group showed a significant improvement in pain response threshold compared with the No-Ex group (p < 0.05).

No significant changes were observed in hind paw withdrawal latency in response to thermal stimulation (p > 0.05) (Fig. 2B).

For P2X4R, one-way analysis of variance showed a significant main effect among groups (F (2,12) = 9.47, p = 0.003). The effect size was large, with an eta-square (η²) of 0.612 (95% CI: 0.124, 0.757). Similar trends were observed for epsilon-square (ε²=0.548) and omega-square (fixed effect: ω²=0.530, random effect: ω²=0.361).

For MCP−1, the main effect among groups was significant (F (2,12) = 5.53, p < 0.05). The effect size was moderate, with an eta-square (η²) of 0.480 (95% CI: 0.012, 0.671). Epsilon-square (ε²=0.393) and omega-square (fixed effect: ω²=0.377, random effect: ω²=0.232) showed similar trends.

For CCR2, one-way analysis of variance showed a significant main effect among groups (F (2,12) = 20.62, p < 0.001). The effect size was very large, with an eta-square (η²) of 0.775 (95% CI: 0.379, 0.858). Epsilon-square (ε²=0.737) and omega-square (fixed effect: ω²=0.723, random effect: ω²=0.567) were similarly large.

For TRAF6, the Kruskal–Wallis test showed a significant difference among groups (H (2) = 6.08, p = 0.048). The effect size (η²) was 0.34, indicating a moderate effect.

Quantification of TNF-α-, IL-6-, and DCX-positive cell area ratios in the hippocampal dentate gyrus was performed at 5 weeks post-CCI (Fig. 4J).

Epsilon-square (ε²=−0.149) and omega-square (fixed effect: ω²=−0.137, random effect: ω²=−0.064) were also below zero, indicating very limited explanatory power for differences among groups.

Changes in short-term and long-term memory

For the alternation rate in the Y-maze, a behavioral assessment of short-term memory, two-way analysis of variance showed that the main effect of group was not significant (F (2,4) = 4.76, p = 0.087, η²= 0.704). The main effect of time was also not significant (F (2,4) = 0.13, p = 0.881, η²= 0.062). The interaction between group and time was likewise not significant (F (4,36) = 0.46, p = 0.764, η²= 0.049).

No significant difference was observed in the Y-maze alternation rate at 5 weeks after CCI (p > 0.05) (Fig. 5B), although the values differed among the Ex group (52 ± 6.4%), No-Ex group (54 ± 4.0%), and Normal group (62 ± 3.2%).

In the passive avoidance test, which evaluates long-term memory, two-way analysis of variance showed a significant main effect of group for the number of transitions from the light compartment to the dark compartment (F (2,36) = 5.51, p = 0.008, η²= 0.204). The main effect of time was not significant (F (2,36) = 2.37, p = 0.108, η²= 0.088). The interaction between group and time was also not significant (F (4,36) = 0.59, p = 0.673, η²= 0.044).

For time spent in the dark compartment, two-way analysis of variance showed that the main effect of time was significant (F (2,36) = 25.20, p < 0.001, η²= 0.479), as was the interaction between group and time (F (4,36) = 3.98, p = 0.009, η²= 0.151). In contrast, the main effect of group was not significant (F (2,36) = 1.44, p = 0.249, η²= 0.027). Notably, η² was large for time (0.479) and moderate for the interaction (0.151).

In the passive avoidance test, there was no significant difference in the number of transitions from the light compartment to the dark compartment (p > 0.05). Compared with the No-Ex group (15.2 ± 1.4 times), the Ex group (6.8 ± 1.7 times) showed significantly less time spent in the dark compartment (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5C, D).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that regular exercise alleviated neuropathic pain in OLETF rats exhibiting diabetes-related metabolic dysfunction. Exercise suppressed microglia-associated P2X4R activation and the CCL2–CCR2 signaling pathway in the spinal dorsal horn, both of which are key contributors to the development and maintenance of neuropathic pain. In the hippocampal dentate gyrus, exercise reduced inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) and enhanced neurogenesis-related markers (DCX and Prox1). These findings suggest that exercise modulates both spinal glial signaling and hippocampal plasticity, providing a mechanistic basis for its analgesic effects in metabolic dysfunction-associated neuropathic pain.

OLETF rats, originally established in 1992, are widely used as a spontaneous model of obesity and diabetes-related metabolic dysfunction. Previous studies have reported age-dependent metabolic changes in this strain, including impaired glucose tolerance, progressive hyperglycemia, and the development of diabetes-like features in adulthood. Although the relationship between neuroinflammation and cognitive function in this model remains unclear, OLETF rats have been described as exhibiting metabolic abnormalities relevant to human pathology (Gao and Zheng, 2014, Kawano et al., 1992, Oshima et al., 2024, Shima et al., 2017). LETO rats were used as controls.

In the biochemical tests, the OLETF group showed significantly higher GLU and T-KB levels than the LETO group at 5 weeks post-CCI in this study. TG and HDL-C levels were also significantly higher in the OLETF group. No significant difference was observed in GA. However, the presence of differences in GLU and TG, but not in GA, may indicate that abnormalities in glucose metabolism were developing but were not reflected by GA. The significant increase in body weight in the OLETF group, together with the total distance traveled in the open-field test, may reflect increased GLU levels associated with overeating and hyperactivity. Furthermore, lipid abnormalities, including elevated TG and HDL-C levels, were observed. These findings suggest that the OLETF rats in this study were in the early stages of diabetes. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports (Hong et al., 2002, Kawagoe et al., 2016).

Regarding temporal changes in the 50% pain response threshold, the Ex and No-Ex groups showed a significant decrease in threshold compared with the Normal group at 1 week post-CCI, indicating the development of neuropathic pain. Both groups showed improvement from 2 to 3 weeks after CCI; however, the No-Ex group showed a slight decline at 4 weeks post-CCI. Conversely, the Ex group showed greater improvement than the No-Ex group from 4 to 5 weeks post-CCI, with a significant improvement observed at 5 weeks post-CCI. At 4 weeks after CCI, the rats were 12 weeks old. When considered together with the biochemical findings obtained at 5 weeks post-CCI, the effects of exercise intervention may have contributed to this improvement; however, the early stage of diabetes may also have influenced the results. In our previous studies, neuropathic pain was induced in Sprague–Dawley rats and exercise intervention was applied. However, the delayed improvement in pain thresholds observed in the present study differed from our previous findings (Sumizono et al., 2024, Sumizono et al., 2022).

Microglia are involved in the activation and maintenance of astrocytes during neuropathic pain. CCR2 is expressed on activated microglia. CCL2–CCR2 signaling has been reported to contribute to the modulation of N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR)-mediated central sensitization, and CCL2/CCR2-dependent mechanisms play a crucial role in the development of neuropathic pain (Piotrowska et al., 2016, Xian et al., 2020, Zhang et al., 2007). Similarly, P2X4R has been associated with neuropathic pain and is considered essential for diabetic neuropathic pain (Teixeira et al., 2019). In reactive astrocytes, TRAF6 has been reported to maintain neuropathic pain through interactions with TNF-α and IL-1β signaling and activation of the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)/CCL2 pathway. CCR2 and TRAF6 have also been reported to contribute to central sensitization (Sumizono et al., 2024). However, few studies have examined the involvement of CCR2 and TRAF6 in diabetic neuropathic pain. DN is a major microvascular complication of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes. In type 2 diabetes, it may already be present at the time of diagnosis, whereas in type 1 diabetes, it typically develops approximately 10 years after disease onset (Galiero et al., 2023). Therefore, the combination of diabetes-related metabolic dysfunction and CCI-induced neuropathic pain in OLETF rats may reflect a clinical condition in which early neuropathy and chronic pain coexist, potentially contributing to both reduced pain thresholds and an increased susceptibility to memory-related dysfunction. In the present study, CCI was induced at 8 weeks of age. Additionally, this model has been reported to show a gradual increase in GLU levels after 10 weeks of age (Shima et al., 2017). In OLETF rats, mechanical hypersensitivity persisted for up to 3 weeks after CCI, whereas the Ex group showed gradual recovery beginning at 2 weeks post-CCI. These time points correspond to 10–11 weeks of age in OLETF rats, a period considered to represent an early stage of metabolic dysfunction. At 4 weeks post-CCI (12 weeks of age), no significant difference in pain thresholds was observed between the Ex and No-Ex groups; however, the Ex group exhibited a clearer recovery trend. This pattern may reflect the relatively early stage of metabolic abnormalities in OLETF rats and is consistent with the biochemical findings obtained at 5 weeks post-CCI.

Changes in the spinal dorsal horn at 5 weeks post-CCI showed that MCP−1, CCR2, P2X4R, and TRAF6 expression levels were significantly lower in the Ex group than in the No-Ex group. CCR2-dependent mechanisms regulate downstream microglial activation without directly affecting initial microglial proliferation, migration, or morphological activation. The cellular sources of CCL2, the cellular targets expressing CCR2, and the infiltration of blood-derived CCR2-positive cells differ between the dorsal and ventral horns of the spinal cord and depend on the type of injury and the time elapsed after injury (Pottorf et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2007). In the present study, exercise reduced the expression of MCP−1 (CCL2) and its receptor CCR2 in the spinal dorsal horn, indicating that exercise attenuates chemokine-mediated microglial activation associated with neuropathic pain. Exercise also suppressed P2X4R expression, a receptor considered essential for the development of hyperalgesia in diabetes-related neuropathy.

TRAF6 expression in astrocytes is important for the maintenance of neuropathic pain. TRAF6 is regulated by cytokines and Toll-like receptors (Wang et al., 2020, Wang et al., 2018). TRAF6 may contribute to the development of inflammation-related diseases, including diabetes, through the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathway, and forced expression of miR−125a−5p in astrocytes has been shown to reduce TRAF6, p-JNK1, and p-JNK2 levels and suppress astrocyte activation (Kasimu et al., 2022, Liu et al., 2018, Wang et al., 2021). Significant differences were observed between the Ex and No-Ex groups at 5 weeks post-CCI, suggesting that exercise intervention suppressed TRAF6 expression.

Second, regarding changes in the hippocampal dentate gyrus, the Ex group showed significantly lower levels of TNF-α and IL-6, markers of inflammatory cytokines, than the No-Ex group at 5 weeks post-CCI, suggesting that exercise reduced pathological inflammation in the hippocampus. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in the expression of DCX, a marker of immature neurons. Recent studies have reported that neuropathic pain induces pathological inflammation in the hippocampal dentate gyrus, resulting in reduced neurogenesis and decreased DCX expression, which are associated with memory impairment (Egorova et al., 2019, Sumizono et al., 2022). OLETF rats have been reported to develop memory impairment after 8 weeks of age (Shima et al., 2017). The Y-maze test, a behavioral assessment of short-term memory, showed no significant differences among the Normal, No-Ex, and Ex groups. However, the No-Ex group showed a decreasing trend in the alternation rate at 3 weeks post-CCI, corresponding to 11 weeks of age. At 5 weeks post-CCI, the Ex group showed a similar decreasing trend. Regarding long-term memory, the Normal group showed an increase in exploratory behavior, whereas the Ex and No-Ex groups showed no change compared with pre-CCI values, although transient changes were observed at 3 weeks post-CCI. This finding may indicate that avoidance behavior was not adequately formed. The Normal group (LETO rats) was used as a model with preserved memory function (Shimoda et al., 2021, Tanaka and Taniuchi, 2024).

In the passive avoidance test, the prolonged step-through latency observed in OLETF rats may reflect the absence of a repeated aversive stimulus after 24 h and the consequent lack of updating of the learned association. Both the Ex and No-Ex groups were in an early stage of metabolic dysfunction, and the No-Ex group, in particular, exhibited elevated hippocampal TNF-α and IL-6 levels, consistent with chronic pain-related neuroinflammation. When considered together with the exaggerated avoidance behavior, these findings suggest that inflammation-associated impairment of memory consolidation and extinction learning may have contributed to the observed behavioral phenotype (Magrinelli et al., 2015). Although no significant differences in passive avoidance performance were observed among the groups, this does not exclude subtle impairments in memory-related processes. Passive avoidance is a relatively coarse behavioral measure, and early inflammation-related changes in the hippocampus may precede detectable behavioral deficits. In the present study, the No-Ex group exhibited elevated hippocampal TNF-α and IL-6 levels despite comparable behavioral performance, suggesting that neuroinflammatory alterations may represent an early stage of dysfunction that has not yet manifested as overt memory impairment.

One limitation of this study is that the OLETF rat model differs from humans in that nearly all males develop diabetes by 25 weeks of age, whereas the incidence of diabetes in human males is substantially lower and is strongly influenced by sex hormones. Furthermore, a definitive diagnosis cannot be established on the basis of fasting blood glucose or blood glucose levels alone, and an oral glucose tolerance test is required (Gao and Zheng, 2014, Kawano et al., 1992, Oshima et al., 2024, Shima et al., 2017). In this study, an oral glucose tolerance test was not performed; therefore, a definitive diagnosis of diabetes was not established. These rats are also highly sensitive to environmental stress and require careful husbandry.

Furthermore, since the effects of exercise intervention following CCI in rats with normal metabolism have already been reported in our previous studies using Sprague–Dawley rats, we did not include a CCI plus exercise intervention group using LETO rats in this study. This should be considered an additional limitation of the present work (Sumizono et al., 2018, Sumizono et al., 2024, Sumizono et al., 2022).

This study demonstrated that regular exercise alleviated neuropathic pain in OLETF rats exhibiting diabetes-related metabolic dysfunction. Exercise reduced the expression of MCP−1 (CCL2), CCR2, P2X4R, and TRAF6 in the spinal dorsal horn, indicating attenuation of microglia-associated inflammatory signaling. However, because cell-type–specific quantification and detailed co-localization analyses were not performed, the reductions in P2X4R, MCP−1, CCR2, and TRAF6 expression observed in this study should be interpreted as overall changes within the dorsal horn region rather than definitive microglial or astrocytic mechanisms. In the hippocampal dentate gyrus, exercise suppressed inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6), suggesting that exercise mitigates pain-related neuroinflammation and contributes to its analgesic effects.

In conclusion, diabetic neuropathy has been reported to develop even before the clinical diagnosis of diabetes, suggesting that early metabolic abnormalities can contribute to neural dysfunction. In this study, suppression of CCL2 (MCP−1) and CCR2 by exercise may reflect attenuation of central sensitization, whereas reduced TRAF6 expression suggests modulation of TNF-α- and IL-1β-mediated signaling pathways. These findings indicate that, in addition to P2X4R, CCR2 and TRAF6 may represent potential therapeutic targets for diabetes-related neuropathy. Furthermore, the combined effects of a diabetic metabolic background, chronic neuropathic pain, and neuroinflammation may accelerate dysfunction in memory-related brain regions, highlighting the need for further investigation.
