Abstract
Analyzing large volumes of sensor network data, such as electricity consumption measurements from smart meters, is essential for modern applications but raises significant privacy concerns. Privacy-enhancing technologies like z z -anonymity offer efficient anonymization for continuous data streams by suppressing rare values that could lead to re-identification, making it particularly suited for resource-constrained environments. Originally designed for centralized architectures, z z -anonymity assumes a trusted central entity. In this paper, we introduce de ℤ \mathbb{Z} ent, a decentralized implementation of z z -anonymity that minimizes trust in the central entity by realizing local z z -anonymity with lightweight coordination. We develop de ℤ \mathbb{Z} ent using a stochastic counting structure and secure sum to coordinate private anonymization across the network. Our results show that de ℤ \mathbb{Z} ent achieves comparable performance to centralized z z -anonymity in terms of publication ratio, while reducing the communication overhead towards the central entity. Thus, de ℤ \mathbb{Z} ent presents a promising approach for enhancing privacy in sensor networks while preserving system efficiency.

More precisely,

This method effectively suppresses rare values from smaller groups of users and ensures an implicit anonymity set.
Each published data point also includes a timestamp and a client pseudonym.
The parameters

As shown in

\Delta t

Generally, the higher the value of

k

At present, the implementations are confined to a centralized setting.
That is,
z
-anonymity could be applied at a

ce

This raises trust concerns, as the

As a result, implementing
z
-anonymity on the

III-B

Attacker Model

We assume an
hbc
(
hbc
) who follows the protocol but attempts to infer sensitive information about clients.
While stronger attacker models certainly exist, an
hbc
is reasonable in our use case, as smart energy metering operates in a highly regulated environment with controlled infrastructure.
Extending de
\mathbb{Z}
ent to defend against stronger, active attackers would broaden its applicability, but would likely incur performance penalties.

Therefore, our main goal with de
\mathbb{Z}
ent is to prevent re-identification and the inference of personal characteristics, e.g., linking consumption patterns to specific clients.
We focus on protecting against a corrupt
ce
that can combine and analyze data from all
sn
.
At the same time, we seek to deploy a solution that is lightweight and can be applied in sensor networks.
We further assume secure communication channels between entities, which excludes eavesdroppers from our threat model.
The adversary’s inference capabilities are therefore limited to the data available through protocol-compliant interaction and published outputs, not through side channels such as traffic analysis.
We further assume that
sn
trust their associated
gw
and that widespread collusion among
gw
is unlikely.
Under this assumption, de
\mathbb{Z}
ent offers probabilistic protection against colluding attackers: since neighboring
gw
are selected pseudo-randomly when establishing the ring topology, an attacker cannot reliably position colluding
gw
next to a target.
We analyze the collusion probability in Sec.
V-D
.

To avoid placing trust in the

This approach enhances client privacy by limiting trust to local components and reducing global data visibility.

To address this, we develop the privacy-preserving coordination protocol de

The coordination offered by de

In each cycle, one

is designated as

2.

Through de

During setup, connections and ring topology between

At the beginning of each clock cycle, a

Several strategies can be implemented to assign the

The

To prepare the data collection, the

(

The

The

Once the

IV-
2
Decentralized Collection Round

Upon receiving
cnt
, each
gw
adds the counts of its data points to the
cnt
, and forwards it to the succeeding
gw
to continue the data collection process.
As in
[
Jha2023zAnon
]
, each

remembers values reported by their

and the number of its occurrences within
\Delta t
.
First, the
gw
ensures that only measurements with timestamps within
\Delta t
are considered while older data points are deleted (
apply_delta_t()
).
Next, the
gw
requests new measurements from its associated
sn
and increases the respective counters in the
cnt
.
Additionally, the
gw
stores the new records containing measured values and the corresponding
sn
IDs for the duration of the clock cycle because the counted values from all
gw
need to be exchanged first before potentially publishing data points.
As long as the ring has not been completely traversed the current

forwards the updated

to its successor, which then repeats the process.
When the
ccc
receives the

, the round has been completed, and all data points from
gw
have been processed.

IV-
3
Ensure minimum count (
z
-anonymity)

Once the collection round is completed and
ccc
has received the updated
cnt
, it proceeds with the post-processing to prepare the data publication.
First, the
ccc
reads
cnt
, and ensures that only data points meeting the required minimum count
z
are considered for publication.
To this end, it subtracts
z-1
from all the value counts, those counts lower than
z-1
are set to zero.
This cleaned

is then used for the publication round.

IV-
4
Decentralized Publication Round

In the publication round, the
cnt
is used to exchange information among
gw
about whether a value has been counted at least
z
times during the collection round.
The
ccc
initiates the publication round by forwarding the cleaned
cnt
to its successor.
Upon receiving the
cnt
, each
gw
can check if the counters in the
cnt
are positive for the measurement values reported by its
sn
during data collection.
This means the value occurred more than
z
times, and the
gw
may publish it by sending a tuple with value, ID, and timestamp to the
ce
.
It then decrements the counter in the
cnt
by 1, ensuring no more occurrences are published than permitted.
Last, the updated
cnt
is forwarded to the successor.

When publication proceeds in the order defined by the system’s ring structure, a bias may arise, as the
gw
immediately following the
ccc
are more likely to publish.
To mitigate this bias, we introduce a publication probability
p_{pub}
, which the
ccc
sets.
The
gw
publish a value with probability
p_{pub}
, reducing the publication bias.
Since this step is probabilistic, not all values may be published after the round completes.
Therefore, the
ccc
can initiate an additional round with
p_{pub}=1
to guarantee publication of all remaining values.

If the bias is acceptable, publication may be enforced in the first round, thus, omitting additional publication rounds.
It is important to note that, in general, it is not necessary for the
cnt
to be empty to stop the publication round, since all values that occurred within
\Delta t
are counted, however, only the data points of the current clock cycle are available for publication.
The entire procedure is repeated in the next clock cycle with a new designated
ccc
.

V
Privacy-Preserving Aggregation

de
\mathbb{Z}
ent realizes
z
-anonymity in a distributed system by adding minimal coordination between
gw
.
We consider a network with a set
\mathcal{S}
of
sn
, and a set
\mathcal{N}=\{N_{1},\dots,N_{n}\}
of
gw
arranged in a ring, where
N_{1}
is the
ccc
, without loss of generality.
Each node
N_{i}
holds a local multiset
\mathcal{M}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
with measurement values of its associated
\{\acs{sn}_{i}\}
, where
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
.

The basic functionality of de
\mathbb{Z}
ent does not offer protection against an
hbc
, since the adversary can observe counts for reported measurements during data collection.
To add privacy protection, we propose the combination of a
stochastic
counting structure and add a secure sum strategy.
While cryptographic solutions could also provide privacy, we focus on lightweight mechanisms that remain viable for sensor networks with low-power entities and high measurement frequency.
In line with this design goal, the stochastic
cnt
is a suitable choice, as it reduces the space complexity for message transmission between
gw
.

V-A

Stochastic Counting Structure

Specifically, we choose a
cbf
(
cbf
)
[
Fan2000CBF
,
Gakhov2019CBF
]
to represent the aggregated multiset.
However, the data structure can be replaced with any solution that supports distributed counting and information exchange regarding the existence of values.
Given their memory and computing efficiency that seem reasonable for implementation in sensor networks,
cbf
are a suitable option that we have chosen deliberately for our implementation of the protocol.

A
cbf
is an array of counters to record the frequency of value occurrences with the help of independent hash functions.
More precisely, a
cbf
is defined as a tuple
\mathsf{CBF}=\langle m,k,b,H,C\rangle
, where
m\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the length of the counter array
C\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the number of hash functions
H=\{h_{1},\dots,h_{k}\}
with
h_{j}:\mathcal{U}\to\{0,\dots,m-1\}
.
Each counter can represent up to
2^{b}
occurrences, where
b
is the number of allocated bits per counter.

To add an element
x\in\mathcal{U}
, all
k
hash functions are evaluated, and the corresponding counters are incremented by 1:

\displaystyle\texttt{CBFAdd:}\forall j\in\{1,\dots,k\}:\;C[h_{j}(x)]\leftarrow C[h_{j}(x)]+1

(1)

Deletion follows the same procedure as addition,
but decrements the corresponding counters by 1.
The frequency of
x
can then be estimated as:

\displaystyle\texttt{CBFCount:}\hat{f}(x)=\min_{j=1}^{k}C[h_{j}(x)].

(2)

Due to potential hash collisions, false positives are possible, meaning counts could be greater than 0 even if the element was never added.
For a given expected number of elements
\mathbb{X}
, the parameters
m
and
k
can be adjusted according to the accepted false positive probability
P_{fp}

[
Gakhov2019CBF
]
:

\displaystyle m=-\frac{\mathbb{X}\cdot ln(P_{fp})}{(ln2)^{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad k=\frac{m}{\mathbb{X}}\cdot ln2

(3)

The parameter estimation should be performed based on the specific use case, taking into consideration the required privacy and utility guarantees.

Implementing a stochastic data structure also offers the opportunity to enhance privacy, because only the number of value occurrences needs to be shared for
z
-anonymity and no additional details of the data points are required.
However, directly applying a
cbf
carries the risk of reverse-engineering measurement values from the stored counts
[
Reviriego2023CBFPriv
]
.
To address this limitation, we introduce an additional layer of protection through secure summation.

V-B

Secure Summation

We propose the use of a secure sum algorithm to ensure that intermediate nodes cannot infer the exact number of occurrences during data collection.
Our design builds upon the
bss
(
bss
)
[
Clifton2002SSum
,
Ranbaduge2020SMC
]
, which can be seamlessly integrated in de
\mathbb{Z}
ent.
Here, a perturbation masks partial sums during aggregation, ensuring that no intermediate node learns individual contributions
[
Tschorsch2013distCount
]
.
This is particularly important for values that occur fewer than
z
times and must remain private.

In our design, secure sum is achieved by having
N_{1}
add a random perturbation
R\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
to the initial counter array and removing it at the end.
In
[
Clifton2002SSum
]
,
R
is uniformly chosen from
[1,\cdots,n]
.
Note that, with domain knowledge, the maximum required noise may be reduced, which can further reduce the counter size
b
.
We accordingly adapt the initialization and publication preparation by having the
ccc
add the perturbation to all bits of the
cbf
during initialization and remove it again during publication preparation.

Initialization

N_{1}
samples
R^{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
, and initializes the
cnt
with
C\leftarrow

CBFAdd
(\{R^{m}\})
, and forwards
C
to
N_{2}
.

Aggregation

When
N_{2}
receives
C
, the decentralized collection round is executed.
At each
N_{i}
, with
2\leq i\leq n
, the node updates
C
by adding each element
x\in\mathcal{M}_{i}
to the
cnt
, i.e.,
C\leftarrow\texttt{CBFAdd}(x)
.
Finally, the updated
cnt
is forwarded to the next node, with
N_{n}
sending it back to
N_{1}
.
Due to the perturbation
R
, privacy protection against an
hbc
is provided during data collection.

Finalization

Before initiating the publication phase,
N_{1}
receives the final
cnt

C
and removes the initial perturbation
R
, yielding
\langle m,k,b,H,C\rangle
, a
cbf
representing the aggregated multiset
\mathcal{M}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{M}_{i}
.
For any
x\in\mathcal{U}
, the estimated frequency is given by
\hat{f}(x)=\texttt{CBFCount}(x)
.
Correctness of the distributed counts follows, since
R
cancels out in this finalization step.

V-C

ID Masking

Even though the stochastic
cnt
and secure sum offer protection during the publication process, knowledge can still be inferred based on the published data tuples.
In
z
-anonymity, published tuples comprise a timestamp, a measurement value, and a client ID.
To avoid a direct link between IDs and client ID,
[
Jha2023zAnon
]
assign rotating pseudonyms.
For de
\mathbb{Z}
ent, this could be implemented by using rotating pseudonyms for the
gw
.
Presumably, this comes at a cost to data utility; an implementation and detailed analysis are left for future work.

To further strengthen privacy, the IDs of individual
sn
can be replaced with the ID of their coordinating
gw
.
This masks client identities and eliminates the need for rotating
sn
pseudonyms.
The approach is conceptually similar to Network Address Translation
[
rfc3022
]
, where multiple devices share a common identifier.
The approach changes linking and tracing possibilities between clock cycles.
However, the
ce
can still infer that each
sn
contributes exactly one measurement per cycle, so data utility is preserved.

V-D

Privacy Analysis of de
\mathbb{Z}
ent

By combining a stochastic data structure with a lightweight secure summation strategy, de
\mathbb{Z}
ent achieves local
z
-anonymity in a distributed network with minimal coordination.
It provides the same output privacy as centralized
z
-anonymity
[
Jha2023zAnon
]
, while drastically reducing the trust placed in the
ce
.
In de
\mathbb{Z}
ent, the
ce
only learns information about data tuples that are published, since the anonymization process is only performed on the
gw
.
Accordingly, the
ce
does not receive any information about measurements that occur fewer than
z
times, unlike when the
ce
applies
z
-anonymity centrally, which would allow the
ce
to analyze information from all tuples.

Currently, the
ce
can link messages and the sending
gw
since it is aware of the message origin.
Potential mitigation techniques include publication via proxy, but the performance and privacy impact of this needs further analyses.
As
sn
can be masked by their associated
gw
, we expect our approach to provide sufficient privacy for most practical use cases.

When removing the perturbation
R
, the
ccc
is the only
gw
that is capable of identifying values that occurred fewer than
z
times,
We therefore propose to rotate the
ccc
each clock cycle to reduce the capability of a single
gw
to correlate values across multiple clock cycles.

Beyond the
ccc
, each
gw
only observes perturbed intermediate aggregates during secure sum and thus cannot distinguish genuine measurements from injected noise.
However, if two
gw
collude and happen to be neighbors of an honest
gw
in the ring, they can compare the incoming and outgoing values and thereby extract information about that
gw
’s values.
That is, de
\mathbb{Z}
ent building upon
bss
does not provide full collusion resistance
[
Ranbaduge2020SMC
]
, but offers probabilistic protection:
in a pseudo-random ring, the probability of a successful targeted collusion attack is
p_{att}=\frac{k}{(n-1)}\cdot\frac{(k-1)}{(n-2)}
for
k
attackers among
n
nodes.

I

VI
Methodology

In the following, we outline our simulation methodology.
We base our simulation on an exemplary smart metering use case and consider three main scenarios: centralized, fully decentralized, and de
\mathbb{Z}
ent.

Simulation Data

To get a data basis, we synthetically generate energy consumption data for different client types based on publicly available standard load profiles.
3
3

3

https://www.bdew.de/energie/standardlastprofile-strom/

These profiles include representative consumption patterns, e.g., for households, farms, and local businesses, each exhibiting different usage behaviors.
The profiles provide values for every 15 minutes and are normalized to a maximum annual consumption of 1,000 kWh/a.
Even tough we do not aim to realize
z
-anonymity for electricity metering in particular, we want to utilize realistic data.
We therefore scale the values to typical consumption ranges for each client type, using publicly available statistics from the Federal Statistic Office of Germany.
4
4

4

https://www.destatis.de/DE/Themen/Gesellschaft-Umwelt/Umwelt/UGR/private-haushalte/Tabellen/stromverbrauch-haushalte.html

The references for the scaling values are documented in our GitHub project.
Using these profiles, we model an exemplary district in Berlin, Germany.
We first define a representative distribution of various client type profiles.
Then, for each simulation, the type of each
sn
can be randomly determined based on the underlying profile distribution, and the measurement values are generated based on the corresponding standard load profile.

Note that the measurement data can take arbitrary floating-point values, which can distort the anonymization.
To address this, we discretize the measurements by rounding them to key values (i.e., buckets), which are then used to count occurrences.
In our simulation, the rounding level increases with larger values, under the assumption that larger measurements exhibit greater variability.

Simulation Scenarios

For our simulation, we implement three scenarios: centralized, fully decentralized, and de
\mathbb{Z}
ent (i.e., decentralized with coordination).
The centralized and fully decentralized scenarios serve as reference points.
In the centralized setup,
z
-anonymity is applied at the
ce
, while in the fully decentralized, each
gw
applies local
z
-anonymity without coordination, i.e., on the locally available data only.

In all scenarios, the number of
gw
and the maximum number of
sn
connected to each
gw
can be adjusted.
The number of
sn
is drawn from a normal distribution, with the peak occurring at half of the maximum number.
We also evaluate other parameters, such as the value of
z
and the client types.
This enables us to examine how the network architecture influences the achievable level of anonymity.

We validated our centralized implementation against the original implementation of
z
-anonymity
[
Jha2023zAnon
]
.
Interested readers can find the results in Appendix
.
