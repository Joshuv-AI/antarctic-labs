Momentum–gapped quasiparticles in disordered metals
Miguel-Ángel Sánchez-Martínez, Blaise Goutéraux, Louk Rademaker, Felix Flicker

Abstract
Nature contains massless particles with linear dispersions, and massive particles whose energies depend quadratically on their momenta with finite mass gaps. Both have equivalents in condensed matter physics in the form of collective modes and quasiparticles , measurable excitations with well-defined energy-momentum relations. A hypothesised third particle type – the super-luminal tachyon – would have an undefined energy at low momentum. A similar collective mode – long hypothesised within the hydrodynamic theory of matter – would have a purely imaginary energy at low momentum, corresponding to a finite lifetime. This third possibility has never been directly observed in a quantum system. Through a careful comparison of hydrodynamics with microscopic models of metals, we establish that this previously unseen third dispersion occurs in correlated quantum matter whenever the electronic fluid undergoes momentum relaxation due to explicit breaking of translation by impurities. As a specific example of these momentum-relaxed modes we consider the recent discovery of an acoustic plasmon – dubbed Pines’ demon – in Sr 2 RuO 4 . The observed dispersion of this neutral mode differed significantly from the massless linear behaviour predicted by the random phase approximation. We demonstrate that the observed dispersion corresponds, in fact, to a momentum-gapped quasiparticle.

The central object by which to study Pines’ demon’s dispersion is the dielectric function

II.1
Hydrodynamics

Pines’ demon is an acoustic electronic collective mode that can be viewed as an out-of-phase combination of two electronic densities, producing a neutral ‘sound’-like excitation. To describe its long-wavelength dynamics in the presence of disorder we use momentum-relaxed hydrodynamics

Goutéraux and Shukla 2024

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\pi^{i}+\partial_{i}(n\partial^{i}\mu)

\displaystyle=-\Gamma nv^{i}-e\frac{n}{q}E^{i},

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}n+\partial_{i}(nv^{i})

\displaystyle=0.

is the total momentum density along the

spatial direction,

is the collective velocity of the electronic fluid, and

\partial_{t}

(\partial_{j})

\displaystyle\omega_{\pm}(q)=-i\frac{\Gamma}{2}\pm\sqrt{q^{2}v_{s}^{2}-\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{4}}.

II.2
Microscopic quantum calculation

Accurate incorporation of momentum relaxation into microscopic response functions has been sought for decades owing to its importance to realistic materials modelling. Often this is done phenomenologically by modifying RPA-based dielectric functions
Mermin 1970
;
Mahan 2000
;
Vos and Grande 2025
. However, at the small
q
and small
\omega

The origin of the problem is that disorder does not simply broaden single-particle propagators: it also generates essential vertex corrections required by conservation laws
Mahan 2000
.

Here we treat the disorder vertex correction in the polarizability in a controlled way. We start from the full polarizability in terms of fermionic Matsubara frequencies
\omega_{n}

\displaystyle P({\bf q},i\omega_{n})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{m}\sum_{\bf k}\Lambda({\bf q},i\omega_{n};{\bf k},i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{G}({\bf k},i\omega_{m})\mathcal{G}({\bf k}+{\bf q},i\omega_{m}+i\omega_{n}),

(4)

where

is the polarizability,

is the 3D momentum,
\mathcal{G}
is the fully dressed electron Green’s function, and

is the vertex function

\epsilon({\bf q},\omega)=1-\sum_{\nu=1}^{2}\frac{\omega_{\textrm{pl},\nu}^{2}}{\omega(\omega+i\Gamma)-\frac{1}{3}v_{\textrm{F},\nu}^{2}q^{2}},

(5)

is the plasma frequency, and

III
Conclusions

Hydrodynamics and its extension to slowly-relaxing momentum has become a vital tool in the study of electronic systems in which entanglement and strong correlations cause the breakdown of the quasiparticle picture and the failure of standard microscopic theories. Notable examples include the ‘strange metals’ from which many cuprate high-Tc superconductors form, ‘bad metals’ which include Sr
2
RuO
4
, and graphene
Hartnoll et al. 2018
;
Lucas and Fong 2018b
;
Phillips et al. 2022
. Degree-of-freedom counting often suggests a connection between macroscopic hydrodynamic modes and microscopic quasiparticle dispersions, but the direct demonstration of an adiabatic connection between the two has so far proven elusive even in simple models. This makes the exact analytic agreement we report here – between hydrodynamics on the one hand, and quasiparticle dispersions of disordered multi-band metals on the other – all the more remarkable. It seems reasonable to hope that this connection may provide clues in the search for microscopic descriptions of systems in which hydrodynamics provides the best current understanding.

Our results also reveal a general feature of quasiparticle dispersions. Any real material has momentum relaxation – be it from defects, disorder, or finite crystal size – and so
any
putative gapless bosonic quasiparticle will feature a momentum gap energy scale.
In this sense, the detailed dispersion of Pines’ demon we report in Fig.
2
is generic. There are two main reasons momentum-gapped dispersions had not previously been measured. First, emergent bosons’ masslessness is often symmetry–protected, such as when they arise from Goldstone’s theorem (as in the case of acoustic phonons in crystals). Second, a mass gap will also typically be present; if the mass gap is larger than the momentum gap, the familiar massive-particle dispersion will result. Importantly, a mass gap will always open in the presence of Coulomb interactions: the standard optical plasmon is an example. The special feature of Pines’ demon that allowed its momentum gap to be revealed is that it is charge neutral. This made its experimental observation all the more remarkable
Husain et al. 2023
.

Crucially, acoustic plasmons could play a role in the superconducting pairing of exotic and high-Tc superconductors, such as metal hydrides
Akashi and Arita 2014
;
Pashitskii et al. 2022
, by lowering the Coulomb repulsion between electrons
Ruvalds and Kahn 1979
;
Ruvalds and Soukoulis 1979
;
Tüttö and Ruvalds 1979
;
Kliewer and Fuchs 1969
;
Ihm et al. 1981
. In this regard, it is relevant to ask if the momentum-gapped Pines’ demon plays a role in the exotic but not yet fully understood multiband superconductivity of Sr
2
RuO
4

Tyler et al. 1998
;
Mackenzie and Maeno 2003
;
Wang et al. 2004
;
Stricker et al. 2014
;
Wagner et al. 2021
;
Røising et al. 2019
, and the high-T
c
superconductivity of multiband metals.

IV.1
Hydrodynamics

In this section, we show how two independent bands of charged particles give rise to a neutral acoustic mode when treated hydrodynamically. We model the bands by two densities coupling only through the external electric fields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\pi_{a}^{i}+\partial_{i}(n_{a}\partial^{i}\mu_{a})

\displaystyle=-\Gamma_{a}n_{a}v_{a}^{i}-e\frac{n_{a}}{m_{a}}E^{i},

(8)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}n_{a}+\partial_{i}(n_{a}v_{a}^{i})

\displaystyle=0,

where

(no sum over

We perturb the hydrodynamic fields

\Psi=\bar{\Psi}+\delta\Psi(\omega,q)e^{-i\omega t+iqx}

\bar{\Psi}=(\bar{n}_{a},\bar{\mu}_{a},\vec{0})
the equilibrium values. To close the system of equations, we use

\displaystyle\epsilon(\omega,q)=1-\frac{\sigma(\omega,q)}{i\omega\epsilon_{0}}.

optical
plasmon,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\rm RPA}(q\ll\omega)=1-\frac{ne^{2}}{m\epsilon_{0}\omega^{2}}=1-\frac{\omega_{\rm pl}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}.

(19)

Let us now try to include the effects of disorder. On the level of the electron Greens function, disorder yields (using the self-consistent Born approximation) an imaginary part of the electron self-energy, which is conventionally written as
\text{Im}\Sigma=-\Gamma/2
. For the polarization, this suggests we can replace
\omega\rightarrow\omega+i\Gamma
. The long-wavelength limit of the dielectric function in this ‘RPA+
\Gamma
’ approximation is

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\rm RPA+\Gamma}(q\ll\omega;\Gamma)=1-\frac{\omega_{\rm pl}^{2}}{(\omega+i\Gamma)^{2}}.

(20)

This is result is
incorrect
. This can be seen by relating the long-wavelength dielectric function to the optical conductivity
\sigma(\omega)

\displaystyle\epsilon(q=0,\omega)=1-\frac{\sigma(\omega)}{i\omega\epsilon_{0}}

(21)

and provided that the optical conductivity has the Drude form

\displaystyle\sigma(\omega)=\frac{ne^{2}}{m}\frac{1}{\Gamma-i\omega}

(22)

the dielectric function in the limit
q\rightarrow 0
should have the shape

\epsilon(\omega)=1-\frac{\omega_{\rm pl}^{2}}{\omega(\omega+i\Gamma)}.

(23)

Physically, the problem of the approximated form Eq. (
20
) is that in the limit
\omega\rightarrow 0
the inverse
1/\epsilon
should vanish for a metal
Mermin 1970
. Note that Eq. (
23
) does satisfy this physical constraint.

Since the ‘RPA+
\Gamma
’ approximation is incorrect, we need to go beyond RPA by including vertex corrections.
Formally, the full polarization in terms of Matsubara frequencies
\omega_{n}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle P({\bf q},i\omega_{n})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{m}\sum_{\bf k}\Lambda({\bf q},i\omega_{n};{\bf k},i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{G}({\bf k},i\omega_{m})\mathcal{G}({\bf k}+{\bf q},i\omega_{m}+i\omega_{n})

(24)

where
\mathcal{G}
is the fully dressed electron Greens function and
\Lambda
is the vertex function. We use the common approximation to express the vertex function self-consistently using the Bethe-Salpeter equation. For momentum-independent impurity scattering, the vertex has no dependence on the electron momentum and the Bethe-Salpeter equation reads

\displaystyle\Lambda({\bf q},i\omega_{n};i\omega_{m})=

\displaystyle 1+\Lambda({\bf q},i\omega_{n};i\omega_{m})\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi N_{0}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\bf k^{\prime}}\mathcal{G}({\bf k^{\prime}},i\omega_{m})\mathcal{G}({\bf k^{\prime}}+{\bf q},i\omega_{m}+i\omega_{n})

(25)

where
N_{0}
is the bare electron density of states at the Fermi level and
\Gamma
is the momentum relaxation rate due to disorder. The vertex function is thus

\displaystyle\Lambda({\bf q},i\omega_{n};i\omega_{m})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi N_{0}}\sum_{\bf k^{\prime}}\mathcal{G}({\bf k^{\prime}},i\omega_{m})\mathcal{G}({\bf k^{\prime}}+{\bf q},i\omega_{m}+i\omega_{n})}.

(26)

Let us define the part with summation over
{\bf k^{\prime}}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{P}({\bf q},i\omega_{n};i\omega_{m})=\sum_{\bf k}\mathcal{G}({\bf k},i\omega_{m})\mathcal{G}({\bf k}+{\bf q},i\omega_{m}+i\omega_{n}).

(27)

For a parabolic dispersion
\xi_{\bf k}=\frac{k^{2}}{2m}-E_{F}
, with
k=|\mathbf{k}|
, in
d=3
dimensions, this becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{P}=\int\frac{\textrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{i\omega_{m}-\xi_{\bf k}+i\frac{\Gamma}{2}{\rm sgn}(\omega_{m})}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{i\omega_{m}+i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\bf k}-\frac{kq}{m}\cos\theta-\frac{q^{2}}{2m}+i\frac{\Gamma}{2}{\rm sgn}(\omega_{m}+\omega_{n}).}

(28)

The integral over
|{\bf k}|
can be replaced with an integral over the dispersion
\xi_{\bf k}
. Since the Greens functions are highly peaked near the Fermi level, we extend the integral over
\xi
to all values of
\xi
, set the density of states
N(\xi)
equal to that at the Fermi level
N(\xi)\rightarrow N_{0}
, and replace any instance of
|{\bf k}|\equiv k
with
k_{F}
. This approximation, valid for
\omega,\Gamma\ll E_{F}
, yields

\displaystyle\tilde{P}=N_{0}\int\frac{\sin\theta\textrm{d}\theta\textrm{d}\phi}{4\pi}\int d\xi\frac{1}{i\omega_{m}-\xi+i\frac{\Gamma}{2}{\rm sgn}(\omega_{m})}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{i\omega_{m}+i\omega_{n}-\xi-v_{F}q\cos\theta-\frac{q^{2}}{2m}+i\frac{\Gamma}{2}{\rm sgn}(\omega_{m}+\omega_{n})}

(29)

where
v_{F}=k_{F}/m
. The integral over
\xi
can now be performed using standard contour integration methods. For
\omega_{n}>0
, the integral only yields a nonzero result when
\omega_{m}+\omega_{n}<0
, because then the two poles are in the opposite half-planes. We thus get

\displaystyle\tilde{P}=

\displaystyle\Theta(-\omega_{n}-\omega_{m})N_{0}\int\frac{\sin\theta\textrm{d}\theta\textrm{d}\phi}{4\pi}

\displaystyle\times\frac{2\pi i}{i\omega_{n}-v_{F}q\cos\theta-\frac{q^{2}}{2m}+i\Gamma}.

(30)

Finally, we expand the integrand quadratically in momentum
q
, ignore the imaginary component of the vertex, and insert the result for
\tilde{P}
into Eq. (
26
) to obtain the vertex function

\displaystyle\Lambda=\left\{\begin{matrix}\left(1-\frac{\Gamma}{\Gamma+\omega_{n}}+\frac{v_{F}^{2}\Gamma}{3(\Gamma+\omega_{n})^{3}}q^{2}\right)^{-1}&{\rm if}-\omega_{n}<\omega_{m}<0,\\
1&{\rm otherwise}.\end{matrix}\right.

(31)

We have a vertex correction
\Lambda({\bf q},i\omega_{n};i\omega_{m})
that does not depend on
\omega_{m}
, with the exception of the Heaviside functions. We thus approximate our final polarization function by the vertex correction times the Lindhard function corrected by disorder,

\displaystyle P_{\rm RPA+\Gamma+\Lambda}({\bf q},i\omega_{n})\approx\Lambda({\bf q},i\omega_{n})P_{0}({\bf q},i\omega_{n}+\Gamma).

(32)

In the optical range
q\ll\omega_{n}
, the ‘shifted’ Lindhard function is

\displaystyle P_{0}({\bf q},i\omega_{n}+\Gamma)=-\frac{nq^{2}}{m(\omega_{n}+\Gamma)^{2}}.

(33)

Including the vertex correction of Eq. (
31
) yields

\displaystyle P_{\rm RPA+\Gamma+\Lambda}({\bf q},i\omega_{n})=-\frac{nq^{2}/m}{\omega_{n}(\omega_{n}+\Gamma)+\frac{v_{F}^{2}\Gamma}{3(\Gamma+\omega_{n})}q^{2}}.

(34)

Expanding for small
q,\omega_{n}
, we find
\frac{q^{2}}{\Gamma+\omega_{n}}\approx q^{2}/\Gamma
and thus

\displaystyle P_{\rm RPA+\Gamma+\Lambda}({\bf q},i\omega_{n})=-\frac{nq^{2}/m}{\omega_{n}(\omega_{n}+\Gamma)+\frac{1}{3}v_{F}^{2}q^{2}}.

(35)

Analytical continuation to real frequencies,
i\omega_{n}\rightarrow\omega+i0^{+}
, yields

\displaystyle P_{\rm RPA+\Gamma+\Lambda}({\bf q},\omega)=\frac{nq^{2}/m}{\omega(\omega+i\Gamma)-\frac{1}{3}v_{F}^{2}q^{2}}

(36)

and the corresponding dielectric function reads

\displaystyle\epsilon({\bf q},\omega)=1-\frac{\omega_{\rm pl}^{2}}{\omega(\omega+i\Gamma)-\frac{1}{3}v_{F}^{2}q^{2}}.

(37)

The static limit of the dielectric function, which is unaffected by the vertex corrections and disorder, is recovered by the above expression for
\epsilon(q,\omega)
.

The case of a multi-band system can be described by combining Eq. (
37
) for each band. For each band, we just need to know the momentum relaxation rate
\Gamma
, the Fermi velocity
v_{F}
, and the optical plasmon frequency
\omega_{\rm pl}
.
For example, an acoustic plasmon is captured by the standard Thomas-Fermi screening result for the light band,
\omega\rightarrow 0
, and the long-wavelength limit
q\rightarrow 0
of Eq. (
37
) for the heavy band. Combining, this yields for a two-band system

\epsilon_{\text{2}-band}(q,\omega)=1+\underbrace{\frac{q_{{\rm TF},L}^{2}}{q^{2}}}_{\rm light\,band}-\underbrace{\frac{\omega_{{\rm pl},H}^{2}}{\omega(\omega+i\Gamma)}}_{\rm heavy\,band}.

(38)

Solving for
\epsilon(q,\omega)=0
yields the momentum-gapped acoustic plasmon mode.

IV.4
Estimation of
\Gamma

Momentum relaxation in metallic systems contributes to the optical conductivity at low frequencies through the Drude conductivity, which for a parabolic band structure reads
Bruus and Flensberg 2004
:

\sigma(\omega)=\epsilon_{0}\frac{\omega_{pl}^{2}}{\Gamma-i\omega},

(44)

where
\epsilon_{0}
is the vacuum permittivity,
\omega_{pl}
is the plasma frequency,
\Gamma
is the momentum relaxation rate
\Gamma=1/\tau
,
\tau_{mr}
is the momentum relaxation time, and
\omega
is the frequency of the applied field.

The optical conductivity of Sr
2
RuO
4
has been measured over a wide range of temperatures
Stricker et al. 2014
, where the low-energy response is well captured with two Drude oscillators. At small frequencies and temperatures above
40
K, the conductivity can be fitted with a single Drude oscillator
Wang et al. 2021
. Therefore, to obtain the momentum relaxation scale at
T=300
K used in momentum-relaxed hydrodynamics in Sec.
II.1
and in the microscopic theory in Sec.
II.2
, we fit the real part of the measured optical conductivity reported in Ref.
Stricker et al. 2014
with a Drude function of the form:

\sigma^{D}(\omega)=\frac{\epsilon_{0}\omega_{pl}^{2}\Gamma}{\omega^{2}+\Gamma^{2}}+\sigma^{0},

(45)

where
\sigma_{0}
is the background contribution from the tails of other Lorentz oscillators at finite frequencies
Stricker et al. 2014
. The resulting value of
\Gamma
obtained from the experimental optical data is shown in Fig.
5
.
