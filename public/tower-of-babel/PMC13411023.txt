Synergistic Design of a SiO2–CNT–Amorphous Carbon 3D Host for Stable SEI Formation and Long‐Term Cycling Stability of Lithium Metal Anodes
Authors: Eunhye Kim, Jae Hun Choi, Dae Soo Jung, Yun Chan Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLithium metal batteries (LMBs) offer exceptionally high energy density but suffer from dendritic lithium growth and large volume changes. Employing three‐dimensional (3D) host electrodes is a promising strategy to mitigate these challenges. Here, a 3D composite host comprising conductive carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and amorphous carbon with uniformly distributed SiO2 (SiO2@AC‐CNT) is fabricated through spray pyrolysis followed by carbonization. The synergistic integration of SiO2, CNTs, and amorphous carbon optimizes electrical conductivity and lithiophilicity, enabling uniform, dendrite‐free lithium deposition, while the amorphous carbon domains promote a Li2CO3‐rich solid electrolyte interphase that facilitates lithium‐ion transport and suppresses parasitic reactions. In asymmetric cell tests, the SiO2@AC‐CNT electrode delivers an average coulombic efficiency of 99.5% for 100 cycles at 2.0 mA cm−2. With a lithium loading of 3.0 mAh cm−2, symmetric cell tests of the SiO2@AC‐CNT sustain more than 1300 h at 1.0 mA cm−2 with stable voltage hysteresis and a low overpotential of 18 mV. Paired with a commercial LiFePO4 cathode, the full cell exhibits excellent capacity retention over 600 cycles at 2.0 C, highlighting its potential as a stable and high‐performance 3D host for practical LMB.

Introduction

Lithium‐ion batteries (LIBs) have attracted considerable attention due to the growing demand for electric vehicles and mobile devices. However, the conventional graphite anode, with a limited capacity of 372 mAh g−1, cannot meet the demand for high energy density, despite ongoing efforts to enhance performance by developing high‐capacity cathode materials and incorporating solid electrolytes [1]. In response, researchers have investigated alternative anode materials to improve energy density. Lithium metal anodes (LMAs) have emerged as promising candidates due to their exceptionally high theoretical capacity (3860 mAh g−1), low gravimetric density (0.534 g cm−3), and low redox potential (−3.04 V vs. the standard hydrogen electrode) [2, 3, 4]. Nevertheless, several critical challenges—such as dendritic lithium growth, uneven lithium plating/stripping, and volume expansion during cycling—must be resolved before LMAs can be practically applied in lithium metal batteries (LMBs). These issues can lead to low Coulombic efficiency (CE), internal short circuits, capacity fading, and poor cycling stability, which hinder the commercial viability of LMAs [5, 6, 7].

Several studies have sought to achieve uniform lithium deposition and stripping while mitigating volume expansion. In particular, three‐dimensional (3D) host structure have proven to be an effective strategy, as they provide sufficient space for lithium deposition and help suppress dendritic lithium growth [8, 9]. Additionally, the high surface area of 3D hosts lowers the local current density, thereby reducing volume fluctuations during cycling [10, 11, 12, 13]. Incorporating lithiophilic materials into 3D hosts is especially effective, as it decreases the nucleation overpotential and inhibits lithium dendrite formation [14, 15]. Various lithiophilic materials, such as TiO2, Al2O3, and SiO2, have been explored for constructing stable LMAs [16, 17, 18]. Among them, SiO2 is one of the most widely used materials due to its ability to be electrochemically reduced to Si and form lithiophilic LiSix seeds, which promote selective nucleation and deposition [19, 20]. W. Chen et al. demonstrated that laser‐induced SiO2 can serve as an effective lithiophilic material by promoting uniform lithium nucleation and enabling stable lithium deposition behavior. Such silicon oxide species can be electrochemically transformed into Li–Si alloys and Li2O, which act as favorable nucleation seeds and lower the energy barrier for lithium deposition [21]. Despite these advantages, the insulating nature of SiO2 can hinder electrochemical reaction kinetics and ultimately limit battery performance. Therefore, incorporating conductive materials is essential to enhance charge transport. Carbon‐based materials are particularly attractive because of their high electrical conductivity, low density, and chemical stability [22, 23, 24]. Among them, carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are especially promising for lithium metal deposition due to their large surface area, entangled porous network, and robust mechanical strength [25, 26, 27]. Zhang et al. reported that CNT sponge structures provide interconnected conductive networks and large internal space, enabling uniform lithium deposition and suppressing dendrite growth [28]. These features provide abundant nucleation sites for lithium, accelerate electron mobility, and accommodate volume changes during repeated cycling [27, 29]. Amorphous carbon, another useful carbon material, helps equalize the local current density, leading to a more stable solid‐electrolyte interphase (SEI) that suppresses side reactions between the electrolyte and lithium, thereby improving battery performance [22, 30, 31, 32]. Therefore, integrating SiO2 with a CNT network in the presence of amorphous carbon is expected to synergistically combine uniform lithium nucleation, efficient electron transport, and interfacial stabilization. Such a rational design enables stable and dendrite‐free lithium deposition within the 3D host structure, providing a comprehensive strategy to address the critical challenges of lithium metal anodes.

This study proposes SiO2‐embedded amorphous carbon and CNT microspheres (SiO2@AC‐CNT) as a 3D host for LMAs, synthesized through a facile spray pyrolysis process. During synthesis, NaCl and Fe nanoparticles serve as a sacrificial template and carbonization catalyst, respectively. Their removal during washing and acid etching generates interconnected pores that increase the surface area and provide internal space for lithium storage. The incorporated SiO2 functions as a lithiophilic component while simultaneously optimizing the electrical conductivity of the carbon composite, suppressing top‐surface lithium accumulation and promoting uniform deposition throughout the electrode. Asymmetric and symmetric cell tests reveal highly reversible lithium plating and stripping with excellent electrochemical stability over prolonged cycling, maintaining high CE. Ex situ XPS analysis further reveals that the amorphous carbon domains promote the formation of a Li2CO3‐rich SEI, which stabilizes the lithium–electrolyte interface and contributes to uniform and stable lithium deposition. Notably, when paired with a commercial LiFePO4 (LFP) cathode, the full cell retains 96.5% of its initial capacity for 600 cycles, demonstrating the long‐term durability and practical applicability of the SiO2@AC‐CNT host.

Results and Discussions

Synthesis and Characterization of Materials

The formation mechanism of SiO2@AC‐CNT microspheres is illustrated in Scheme 1. The multi‐step synthetic process was deliberately designed to achieve a hierarchical structure with synergistic functionalities. An aqueous spray solution containing silica sol, dextrin, Fe nitrate, CNTs, and NaCl was ultrasonically nebulized (Scheme 1a). The resulting droplets were dried in a thermal reactor, forming SiO2/Fe3O4/AC/CNT/NaCl composite microspheres (Scheme 1b). Spray pyrolysis enables the formation of uniform composite microspheres, ensuring homogeneous distribution of SiO2, CNTs, and carbon precursors, along with Fe species and NaCl that serve as catalytic and templating agents. Because the residence time during spray pyrolysis is short, a post‐heat treatment at 700°C under an Ar atmosphere was performed to promote NaCl crystallite growth, which in turn enlarged the internal voids. Under these conditions, Fe3O4 was transformed into metallic Fe and iron carbide (C0.12Fe1.88) through carbothermal reduction, which promotes carbonization and the formation of conductive networks, yielding SiO2/Fe/C0.12Fe1.88/NaCl@AC‐CNT composites (Scheme 1c) [33]. After post‐heat treatment, NaCl was completely removed by deionized‐water washing, and Fe/C0.12Fe1.88 was removed by acid etching with aqueous HCl, producing SiO2@AC‐CNT microspheres with abundant mesopores, providing sufficient space for lithium accommodation (Scheme 1d) [34, 35]. As a result, the obtained SiO2@AC‐CNT microspheres integrate lithiophilic sites from SiO2, efficient electron transport pathways from the CNT network, and interfacial stabilization from amorphous carbon. This structural design is expected to facilitate uniform lithium nucleation, reduce local current density, and enhance cycling stability. To elucidate the morphological and structural roles of each component in the SiO2@AC‐CNT composite microspheres, three control samples were prepared by selectively omitting one constituent from the precursor solution: SiO2@AC, SiO2@CNT, and AC‐CNT.

Schematic illustration of the formation mechanism of SiO2@AC‐CNT microsphere.

The morphological evolution of SiO2@AC‐CNT during synthesis was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figure 1; Figure S1). The as‐sprayed powder consisted of spherical microspheres containing embedded NaCl crystallites (Figure S1a,b). After the post‐heat treatment (Figure S1c,d), crystal growth caused some NaCl to protrude from the microspheres, and the images showed that spherical SiO2 and Fe nanoparticles had assembled into the structural framework. Following deionized (DI)‐water washing and HCl etching, the resulting SiO2@AC‐CNT composite microspheres exhibited a porous architecture, in which the removal of NaCl and Fe species created voids between the CNT network and SiO2 particles, as shown in the SEM and TEM images (Figure 1a–c). The high‐resolution TEM (HR‐TEM) image in Figure 1d shows the coexistence of SiO2 with CNT bundles and amorphous carbon; clear lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.35 nm correspond to the graphitic walls of CNTs, while the surrounding amorphous phase is consistent with SiO2 uniformly wrapped by amorphous carbon [36]. The elemental mapping images (Figure 1e) demonstrate the uniform distribution of SiO2 throughout the carbon matrix, confirming the successful synthesis of SiO2@AC‐CNT composite microspheres.

Morphologies of the SiO2@AC‐CNT microsphere observed by SEM and TEM. (a) SEM image, (b,c) TEM images, (d) HR‐TEM image, and (e) Elemental mapping images.

X‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Figure S2) reveals distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to NaCl and iron oxide (Fe3O4) phases in the as‐sprayed powder. After post‐heat treatment, Fe3O4 is reduced via carbothermal reduction to metallic Fe and iron carbide (C0.12Fe1.88), while NaCl remains crystalline. Subsequent HCl etching and DI‐water washing completely remove both NaCl and Fe‐related phases, as evidenced by the disappearance of their characteristic peaks. Consistent with the amorphous nature of SiO2, no distinct diffraction peaks are observed in the final SiO2@AC‐CNT composite [37].

The SiO2@AC sample exhibited a morphology similar to that of SiO2@AC‐CNT, as shown in Figure S3a,b. In contrast, the HR‐TEM images (Figure S3c,d) revealed that the SiO2 nanoparticles were encapsulated within an amorphous carbon matrix. In SiO2@CNT (Figure S3e), spherical SiO2 particles were not clearly resolved in SEM, suggesting that the SiO2 domains are significantly smaller than those in SiO2@AC‐CNT and SiO2@AC. TEM images (Figure S3f,g) further confirmed this trend, showing larger internal pores and smaller SiO2 particles relative to the other microspheres. This difference is attributed to the absence of carbon, which allowed unrestricted NaCl crystal growth during spray pyrolysis and post‐heat treatment, resulting in a larger pore network within the SiO2@CNT structure. HR‐TEM (Figure S3h) further confirms amorphous SiO2 interfaced with multiwalled CNTs, exhibited a with 0.35 nm lattice fringe. AC‐CNT (Figure S3i–k) displays open surface pores generated by NaCl washing and Fe‐related etching, and its interior shows smaller internal voids with a more uniform, hierarchical pore distribution. This finer, more regular porosity can be attributed to the absence of SiO2, which allowed NaCl to grow more uniformly and in a more confined manner within the carbon framework during spray pyrolysis and post‐heat treatment. HR‐TEM analysis (Figure S3l) reveals that the amorphous carbon and CNTs in the AC‐CNT structure are intimately interconnected, forming a unified composite rather than existing as distinct phases.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was used to examine the carbon content of SiO2@AC‐CNT and the control samples under an air atmosphere, as shown in Figure 2a. The sharp weight loss observed above 350°C corresponds to the combustion of amorphous carbon and CNTs, while only SiO2 remains beyond 600°C. Accordingly, the carbon content in each sample was calculated from the weight difference between the mass at approximately 350°C and the residual mass at temperatures above 600°C. The carbon contents of SiO2@AC‐CNT, SiO2@AC, and SiO2@CNT were determined to be 26.4, 20.9, and 21.3 wt%, respectively, with no significant differences observed among the three samples. The temperature of maximum mass loss rate (Tmax), obtained from the derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) trace, is shown in Figure S4. SiO2@AC exhibits a sharp DTG maximum at 376°C, whereas SiO2@CNT shows a higher Tmax at 567°C. The lower combustion temperature of SiO2@AC indicates that less energy is required to oxidize the defect‐rich, more sp3‐like amorphous carbon. By contrast, SiO2@CNT forms a robust, percolated sp2 carbon network provided by the CNT scaffold, which increases structural order, reduces defect and edge sites, and shifts combustion to higher temperatures [38]. The SiO2@AC‐CNT composite displays two distinct Tmax values at 433 and 496°C, with broadened profiles rather than sharp peaks, indicating the coexistence of amorphous and graphitic carbon domains. The surface area and pore structures of SiO2@AC‐CNT and the control samples were characterized using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method (Figure 2b). The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms of all samples display typical hysteresis loops characteristic of IUPAC Type IV isotherms, indicating mesoporous structures [39]. The mesopores primarily originate from the amorphous carbon framework, with additional void generation resulting from the removal of NaCl and Fe‐related components during the washing and etching steps. The specific surface areas are 206, 231, 81, and 817 m2 g−1 for SiO2@AC‐CNT, SiO2@AC, SiO2@CNT, and AC‐CNT, respectively. The AC‐CNT sample, which lacks SiO2, exhibits a markedly higher specific surface area than the other composites because the incorporation of SiO2 reduces the accessible surface area by diluting the carbon fraction and partially blocking the pore structure. Among the SiO2–carbon composites, SiO2@CNT shows the lowest specific surface area, which can be attributed to the absence of amorphous carbon. The mesopore size distribution determined by the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method (Figure 2c) shows a narrow peak centered at 3–4 nm for all samples, indicating a consistent mesoporous framework. The total pore volumes were determined to be 0.45, 0.85, 0.36, and 0.34 cm3 g−1 for SiO2@AC‐CNT, SiO2@AC, SiO2@CNT, and AC‐CNT, respectively. Considering an electrode loading of 15 mg cm−2, these pore volumes are sufficient to accommodate 5.0 mAh cm−2 of Li, indicating that all four host structures possess adequate pore volume for high‐capacity lithium deposition.

X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to analyze the surface chemical states of SiO2@AC‐CNT (Figure 2d–f). The survey spectrum shows C 1s, O 1s, and Si 2p signals, confirming the successful formation of the SiO2–carbon composite (Figure S5). The C 1s spectrum in Figure 2d is deconvoluted into five peaks corresponding to C─Si (283.8 eV), C─C (sp2) (284.5 eV), C─C (sp3) (285.4 eV), C═O (287.8 eV), and O─C═O (290.4 eV) [40, 41, 42, 43]. The dominant C─C (sp2) and C─C (sp3) peaks arise from the CNT scaffold and amorphous carbon derived from dextrin, respectively, confirming their coexistence in the SiO2@AC‐CNT structure. The presence of C─Si bonding further confirms interfacial interactions between SiO2 and the carbon matrix [19]. The O 1s spectrum in Figure 2e shows peaks assigned to C─O (531.3 eV), SiO2 (532.4 eV), and Si─O (533.1 eV)bonding, indicating that SiO2 is embedded within and surrounded by carbon, providing strong evidence of robust SiO2–carbon interfacial bonding [44, 45]. The Si 2p spectrum in Figure 2f exhibits peaks corresponding to Si─O (103.0 eV) and SiO2 (103.5 eV), confirming the presence of silica within the composite [19]. Additionally, a Si–C signal at 101.0 eV is observed, indicating strong interfacial coupling between SiO2 and the surrounding carbon matrix [46]. Raman spectroscopy was conducted to investigate the structural ordering of carbon in the SiO2@AC‐CNT and control samples (Figure S6). All samples exhibit the characteristic D band (1350 cm−1) and G band (1580 cm−1), corresponding to disordered and graphitic carbon structures, respectively [47]. In addition, a peak at 2987 cm−1 is assigned to the 2D band, which arises from the overtone of the D band and reflects the double‐resonance effect associated with the sp2‐bonded graphitic structure. The intensity ratio of the D to G bands (ID/IG), representing the relative abundance of amorphous to graphitic carbon, was 1.02, 0.97, 0.95, and 0.97 for SiO2@AC‐CNT, SiO2@AC, SiO2@CNT, and AC‐CNT, respectively. Electrochemical Characterization and Interfacial Stability

Electrochemical characterization was conducted using asymmetric, symmetric, and full cell tests using electrolyte as lithium bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonamide (LiTFSI, 1.0 M) dissolved in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of 1,2‐dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3‐dioxolane (DOL) with 0.3 M LiNO3 was used as the electrolyte. The reversibility of repeated lithium deposition and stripping for SiO2@AC‐CNT and the control samples was evaluated using asymmetric cells, in which lithium foil served as the counter electrode and the synthesized hosts were used as the working electrodes. CEs, defined as the ratio of stripped to plated lithium, were measured at current densities of 2.0 and 5.0 mA cm−2 with a fixed deposition capacity of 1.0 mAh cm−2 (Figure 3a,b). At the current density of 2.0 mA cm−2, SiO2@AC‐CNT exhibited the most stable cycling behavior, maintaining a high CE of 98.6% for more than 200 cycles. SiO2@CNT and SiO2@AC showed moderate stability, sustaining CEs of 96.5% for 100 cycles and 95.5% for 70 cycles, respectively. AC‐CNT demonstrated the poorest durability, retaining only 60 stable cycles despite an initial CE of 96.7%. At the higher current density of 5.0 mA cm−2, SiO2@AC‐CNT again showed superior stability, maintaining a CE of 96.5% for 90 cycles. SiO2@CNT and SiO2@AC showed lower CEs of 95.7% and 94.1%, respectively, and both began to degrade after 70 cycles. AC‐CNT failed much more rapidly, exhibiting stable cycling for only 35 cycles. These results highlight the role of lithiophilic SiO2 in promoting uniform lithium nucleation, as well as the importance of integrating SiO2 with a conductive CNT framework and an amorphous carbon matrix. Overall, SiO2@AC‐CNT delivered the most reversible and durable lithium plating/stripping behavior across all tested current densities. To further verify the role of SiO2 in enhancing lithiophilicity, the nucleation overpotential of each sample was evaluated from the same asymmetric cell tests (Figure S7). The nucleation overpotential was determined from the lithium deposition profile as the voltage difference between the initial nucleation dip and the subsequent steady‐state plating plateau [49]. Based on this analysis, AC‐CNT exhibited the highest nucleation overpotential of 9.9 mV, whereas SiO2@AC‐CNT, SiO2@AC, and SiO2@CNT showed lower values of 8.6, 8.7, and 9.1 mV, respectively. This trend indicates that the low lithium affinity of carbon elevates the nucleation barrier and promotes non‐uniform, dendritic lithium growth. Incorporating lithiophilic SiO2 into the carbon scaffold reduces the nucleation overpotential, homogenizes the interfacial current distribution, and enables dense, uniform lithium deposition within the structure.

Post‐mortem SEM analysis was carried out to evaluate the structural stability of the SiO2@AC‐CNT host during repeated cycling. The asymmetric cell was cycled at 2.0 mA cm−2 with an areal capacity of 1.0 mAh cm−2 for 100 cycles. Subsequently, lithium corresponding to 1.0 mAh cm−2 was completely stripped, and the morphology of the resulting electrode was observed by SEM (Figure S8). Both SEM images show that the SiO2@AC‐CNT host remains structurally stable during repeated lithium deposition/stripping. The cross‐sectional SEM image (Figure S8a) indicates that the electrode structure is well preserved after cycling, without obvious cracks, structural collapse, or delamination throughout the electrode. In particular, the individual microspheres are clearly observed with their rounded morphology retained, indicating that the host framework is maintained without significant deformation. The top‐view image (Figure S8b) further confirms that the electrode surface remains relatively uniform after cycling, with no obvious dendritic lithium growth or dead lithium accumulation. These results demonstrate that robust structural stability of the SiO2@AC‐CNT host, showing that it effectively maintains its original during repeated cycling.

To further evaluate electrode stability under practical conditions, the average CE was measured in an asymmetric cell after a lithium pre‐deposition step. Unlike the previous asymmetric cell tests performed on pristine electrodes, commercial LMBs operate after lithium has already been plated onto the host. Therefore, CE was evaluated with lithium pre‐deposited on the working electrode. Using lithium metal as the counter electrode, 5.0 mAh cm−2 of lithium was initially deposited onto the host, stripped to 1.0 V, and subsequently re‐deposited at the same capacity before cycling. The subsequent 100 cycles were conducted at a current density of 2.0 mA cm−2, repeatedly stripping and depositing 1.0 mAh cm−2 of lithium in each cycle. After 100 cycles, the cell was finally stripped to 1.0 V to quantify the residual lithium, which was then used to calculate the average CE; the results are shown in Figure 3c [50]. The stripping profile from the 100th cycle (Figure 3d) shows that the amount of residual lithium is largest for SiO2@AC‐CNT, followed by AC‐CNT, SiO2@CNT, and SiO2@AC. Based on these residual lithium values, the average CEs were determined to be 99.5% for SiO2@AC‐CNT, followed by 99.0% for SiO2@AC, 99.1% for SiO2@CNT, and 99.4% for AC‐CNT, respectively. The high CE reflects improved lithium cycling reversibility due to suppressed parasitic reactions enabled by the combined effects of amorphous carbon and CNT. A detailed view in Figure 3e shows the potential difference between deposition and stripping, which corresponds to polarization. AC‐CNT, which does not contain SiO2, exhibits the largest voltage hysteresis of 114 mV, consistent with its low lithiophilicity. The voltage hysteresis values of the SiO2‐containing composites were 61, 57, and 70 mV for SiO2@AC‐CNT, SiO2@AC, and SiO2@CNT, respectively. These results indicate that lithiophilic SiO2, combined with the balanced contributions of amorphous carbon and CNTs, promotes highly reversible lithium deposition and stripping, thereby sustaining stable cycling over extended operation. Furthermore, this reduced polarization is consistent with the decreased nucleation overpotential observed for SiO2‐containing hosts (Figure S7), reinforcing the role of lithiophilic SiO2 in regulating uniform lithium nucleation.

The reasons for the improved cycling performance and enhanced stability observed in the asymmetric cell and CE tests were further examined using SEM to analyze lithium deposition morphology. After deposition of lithium to 3.0 mAh cm−2 on SiO2@AC‐CNT and the control electrodes, top‐view and cross‐sectional SEM images were collected, as shown in Figure 4. Cross‐sectional SEM images of SiO2@CNT (Figure 4a) and AC‐CNT (Figure 4b) show that lithium is not uniformly deposited throughout the electrode but instead accumulates predominantly near the upper region, as indicated by the dotted lines. The corresponding high‐magnification images (Figure 4e,f) further reveal dendritic lithium growth on the exposed surfaces of these structures. Consistently, the top‐view images (Figure 4i,j) confirm substantial lithium accumulation on the upper electrode surface. For SiO2@AC, lithium is densely deposited starting from the lower region of the electrode, as shown in the cross‐sectional image (Figure 4c). However, the high‐magnification image (Figure 4g) reveals partially dendritic lithium growth on the surfaces of the SiO2@AC framework, along with additional lithium deposits accumulated above the electrode surface, as indicated by the dotted line. The top‐view image (Figure 4k) also shows noticeable lithium accumulation on the upper surface, though to a lesser extent than in SiO2@CNT and AC‐CNT. This behavior suggests that, although lithium nucleation and initial growth occur within the host structure, the deposited lithium is not fully confined during continued deposition. The absence of a continuous conductive network in the SiO2@AC electrode may limit effective electron transport, causing lithium growth to extend along the framework surface and accumulate above the electrode. SiO2@AC‐CNT exhibits the most uniform and densely packed lithium deposition, with no upper‐surface accumulation and no dendritic features (Figure 4d,h,l). This morphology indicates that lithium was deposited not only between the host particles but also within the internal pores of the 3D host structure. An optimized ratio of amorphous carbon, CNTs, and lithiophilic SiO2 provides a balanced combination of ionic transport and electronic conductivity, enabling uniform and dense lithium deposition. Electrical conductivity was measured for each electrode using the four‐point probe method, and the results are summarized in Table S1. As expected, SiO2@AC exhibits the lowest conductivity (1.8 × 10−3 mS cm−1), whereas SiO2@CNT and AC‐CNT show high conductivities of 52.0 and 53.9 mS cm−1, respectively. SiO2@AC‐CNT displays an intermediate conductivity (14.4 mS cm−1), consistent with its lithium deposition behavior, as this moderate electronic conductivity mitigates top‐side accumulation and promotes uniform lithium distribution throughout the electrode. The electrode thickness after depositing 3.0 mAh cm−2 of lithium was also measured: 34, 26, 42, and 47 µm for SiO2@AC‐CNT, SiO2@AC, SiO2@CNT, and AC‐CNT, respectively. SiO2@AC‐CNT and SiO2@AC exhibit the smallest volume expansion, while SiO2@CNT and AC‐CNT show larger expansion due to top‐side lithium accumulation. When the deposition amount increased to 7.0 mAh cm−2 (Figure S9), SiO2@AC‐CNT preserved a dense and compact morphology, whereas pronounced top‐accumulation of lithium was observed on the top surface of AC‐CNT. For the SiO2@AC‐CNT electrode, the magnified cross‐sectional SEM image (Figure S9b) revealed densely and uniformly deposited lithium throughout the electrode thickness, whereas the corresponding top‐view image (Figure S9c) showed that lithium did not completely cover the upper surface of the electrode. In contrast, the magnified cross‐sectional image of the AC‐CNT electrode (Figure S9e) indicated that lithium was predominantly deposited near the top surface, with minimal lithium observed within the interior framework. Consistently, the top‐view image (Figure S9f) showed a substantial accumulation of lithium on the electrode surface. This behavior originates from the excessively high electrical conductivity of AC‐CNT, which favors surface‐localized lithium deposition, whereas the moderated conductivity and lithiophilic SiO2 in SiO2@AC‐CNT enable uniform and dense lithium deposition. Overall, the asymmetric cell performance and SEM observations demonstrate that the SiO2@AC‐CNT composite microspheres deliver superior CE and stable lithium deposition.

To further clarify the interfacial mechanisms responsible for these behaviors, ex situ XPS analysis was conducted to compare the SEI compositions formed on the different carbon structures. For sample preparation, 1.0 mAh cm−2 of lithium was first deposited and completely stripped to ensure stable SEI formation on the host. An additional 0.1 mAh cm−2 of lithium was then re‐deposited, corresponding to the charge below the nucleation point inferred from the deposition profile, thereby capturing the surface state immediately prior to lithium nucleation. The C 1s spectra in Figure S10a–c can be deconvoluted into five peaks located at 284.0, 285.0, 286.5, 288.9, and 290.0 eV, attributed to C─C (sp2), C─C (sp3), C─O, O─C═O, and CO3, respectively [51]. SiO2@AC‐CNT and SiO2@AC exhibit all five peaks, whereas SiO2@CNT shows only the C─C (sp2) and C─C (sp3) peaks, with C─O and O─C═O nearly absent. The stronger oxygenated carbon signals in SiO2@AC‐CNT and SiO2@AC indicate electrolyte (DOL/DME) decomposition and the formation of lithium carbonate within the SEI [52]. The O 1s spectra shown in Figure S10d–f include components centered at 528.0, 530.5, 532.0, 533.6, and 534.0 eV, assigned to Li2O, LixSiOy, C═O, O─C═O, and C─O, respectively [51, 53]. Notably, the C═O contribution is markedly lower for the SiO2@CNT electrode compared with the other samples, indicating a smaller amount of Li2CO3 in its SEI. Importantly, the LixSiOy peak is consistently observed across all SiO2‐containing electrodes, directly confirming that SiO2 undergoes electrochemical lithiation to form lithium silicate species. These LixSiOy species serve as lithiophilic sites that facilitate lithium nucleation and interfacial stability. The Li 1s spectra (Figure S10g–i) exhibit peaks at 54.1, 54.9, 55.3, and 55.8 eV, corresponding to Li2O, Li2CO3, LixSiOy, and LiF, respectively [52, 54]. The Li2CO3 peak is most intense for SiO2@AC and weakest for SiO2@CNT. In contrast, the SiO2@AC‐CNT electrode displays the strongest LiF signal, indicating the coexistence of LiF and Li2CO3 within the SEI. The ex situ XPS results highlight the critical role of the amorphous carbon phase in promoting the in situ formation of Li2CO3. The disordered, defect‐rich surface of amorphous carbon facilitates electrolyte reduction to Li2CO3 and yields a more uniform charge distribution, thereby lowering the local current density and promoting uniform SEI formation. LiF, generated through LiTFSI decomposition, is mechanically robust and electronically insulating, effectively blocking electron transport and suppressing lithium dendrite growth; however, its low lithium‐ion permeability can increase interfacial resistance [55, 56]. Li2CO3, a major SEI component, exhibits higher lithium‐ion conductivity than Li2O and LiF, enabling more efficient ionic transport through the SEI and reducing inactive lithium accumulation, ultimately improving CE [57]. Overall, the synergistic effect of the fast lithium‐ion transport kinetics of Li2CO3 and the strong electron passivation provided by LiF in SiO2@AC‐CNT effectively suppresses lithium dendrite growth and enables uniform, dense lithium deposition.

To further examine the evolution of the SEI during prolonged cycling, additional ex situ XPS analysis was conducted after 100 cycles, and the results are shown in Figure S11. After prolonged cycling, additional features emerged in the high‐binding‐energy region of the C 1s spectra, indicating the formation of electrolyte‐derived SEI species. Notably, the SiO2@AC‐CNT electrode exhibited broadened and overlapping C 1s features (Figure S11a), suggesting the coexistence of multiple SEI species. In comparison, the SiO2@AC electrode shows the development of distinct peaks in the 289–290 and 292–293 eV regions (Figure S11b), corresponding to carbonate‐like species and fluorinated decomposition products, respectively. However, these features appear more separated and less overlapped than those observed for the SiO2@AC‐CNT electrode. For the SiO2@CNT electrode (Figure S11c), although carbonate‐like species are formed after cycling, the high‐binding‐energy peak, typically associated with fluorinated decomposition products, is much less pronounced compared to the other electrodes. These results indicate that, while all electrodes undergo electrolyte decomposition during cycling, the SiO2@AC‐CNT electrode develops a more compositionally diverse and integrated multi‐component SEI. Such a multi‐component interphase, consisting of both inorganic and organic species, is known to provide synergistic advantages, combining mechanical robustness with interfacial flexibility and ionic transport capability. This balanced interphase can help mitigate continuous SEI breakdown and reconstruction during cycling, thereby contributing to improved electrochemical stability.

Based on the ex situ XPS results confirming that the SEI composition varies with the carbon configuration, in situ EIS was further performed to investigate how these SEI differences influence interfacial resistance during lithium plating and stripping. Each plating and stripping step lasted for 1 h, with impedance spectra recorded every 10 min to monitor resistance evolution in real time, as supported by the voltage profile shown in Figure 5a. In a Nyquist plot, the high‐frequency intercept typically represents the electrolyte resistance (Rele), the high‐frequency semicircle corresponds to the SEI resistance (RSEI), and the mid‐frequency semicircle reflects the charge‐transfer resistance (Rct). In order to compare across electrodes, the overall resistance (Rtot) was defined as the sum of Rele, RSEI, and Rct and was used as a metric to compare their interfacial behavior [58], Analysis of the acquired spectra shows that the SiO2@AC‐CNT electrode exhibits the lowest overall impedance, remaining below 10.0 Ω throughout the lithium plating process and decreasing from approximately 9.3 to 7.7 Ω during stripping, as shown in Figure 5b,c. The small impedance values were maintained with minimal change throughout the plating and stripping steps, which indicates that a stable SEI layer was rapidly established, allowing smooth lithium exchange at the interface and preventing fluctuations in interfacial resistance during cycling. For SiO2@AC, the impedance during the initial plating began at approximately 18.3 Ω and decreased to about 13.6 Ω, but increased again to nearly 20.1 Ω after 1.0 mAh cm−2 of lithium had been plated (Figure 5d). During stripping, the resistance remained relatively stable at around 14.8 Ω, as shown in Figure 5e. In the case of SiO2@CNT, the electrode initially exhibited a very high impedance exceeding 30.0 Ω, which gradually decreased as lithium deposition progressed and stabilized at approximately 14.2 Ω by the end of plating. Upon stripping, the resistance again decreased but rose sharply at the final stage, indicating interfacial instability (Figure 5f,g). These in situ EIS results, consistent with the ex situ XPS findings, demonstrate that the synergistic combination of amorphous carbon and CNTs, which stems from the fast lithium‐ion transport kinetics of Li2CO3 and the strong electron‐passivating effect of LiF, plays a crucial role in regulating interfacial resistance during lithium deposition and stripping.

The capacitance was evaluated using cyclic voltammetry (CV) for the SiO2@AC‐CNT and control electrodes at scan rates ranging from 0.1 to 3.0 mV s−1 within a voltage window of 0.01–3.0 V, as shown in Figure S12. At a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1, the capacitances of SiO2@AC‐CNT, SiO2@AC, SiO2@CNT, and AC‐CNT were 0.48, 0.37, 0.13, and 0.86 F cm−2, respectively. At 3.0 mV s−1, the corresponding values decreased to 0.08, 0.05, 0.04, and 0.31 F cm−2. Across all scan rates, AC‐CNT exhibited the highest capacitance due to its lack of insulating SiO2 and its highest specific surface area. Among the SiO2‐containing electrodes, SiO2@AC‐CNT displayed the highest capacitance. Capacitance is typically proportional to electronic conductivity and surface area; however, SiO2@AC‐CNT is not the highest in either parameter [59]. Its superior capacitance instead arises from synergistic structural and interfacial characteristics. In particular, lithiophilic SiO2 plays a key role by promoting lithium‐ion accumulation at the interface, while the interconnected CNT network provides efficient electron pathways, and defect‐rich amorphous carbon supports uniform and stable SEI formation. The enhanced interfacial capacitance indicates a higher near‐surface lithium‐ion concentration, enabling more uniform lithium plating and stripping, suppressing dendritic growth, and thereby improving cycling stability.

To evaluate the electrochemical performance of SiO2@AC‐CNT as a lithium host for LMAs, symmetric cell tests were conducted after pre‐depositing 3.0 mAh cm−2 of lithium on each electrode, denoted Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT, Li‐SiO2@AC, Li‐SiO2@CNT, and Li‐AC‐CNT, as shown in Figure 6a. Rate capability was assessed by measuring voltage hysteresis while stepping the current density from 0.5 to 10.0 mA cm−2. At 0.5 mA cm−2, the voltage hysteresis values were 14, 32, 33, and 22 mV for Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT, @AC, @CNT, and AC‐CNT, respectively. At 10.0 mA cm−2, the hysteresis increased to 108, 400, 356, and 277 mV. Across all rates, Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT exhibited the lowest polarization, particularly under high‐current operation, where a pronounced resistance difference was observed. After the high‐rate sequence, returning to 1.0 mA cm−2 resulted in a voltage hysteresis of 18 mV, identical to its initial value at 1.0 mA cm−2, indicating reversible polarization. These results show that the SiO2@AC‐CNT scaffold promotes uniform and dense lithium plating and stripping and provides a low‐resistance interface suitable for LMAs. Long‐term cycling stability of the symmetric cell is shown in Figure 6b. At a current density of 1.0 mA cm−2 with a capacity of 1.0 mAh cm−2, the Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT symmetric cell maintained a voltage hysteresis of approximately 18 mV for more than 1100 h, which slightly increased to 26 mV and remained stable beyond 1300 h. This minimal change in polarization indicates that SiO2@AC‐CNT serves as a stable host for LMAs. In contrast, the Li‐Cu symmetric cell without a 3D host structure exhibited an initial hysteresis of 24 mV that gradually increased to 40 mV at 250 h, followed by an abrupt rise in overpotential after 300 h, confirming the superior stability of the SiO2@AC‐CNT hosts. To further validate the exceptional symmetric cell performance of the SiO2@AC‐CNT electrode, including its low voltage hysteresis and long‐term cycling stability, a comparative analysis was performed against previously reported lithium metal hosts with similar structural designs (Table S2). Notably, while many reference symmetric cells exhibited significant increases in hysteresis voltage and rapid degradation, the SiO2@AC‐CNT electrode maintained stable voltage profile and sustained reversible cycling over extended operation.

The charge‐transfer behavior and interfacial reaction kinetics of the Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT and Li‐Cu foil electrodes were analyzed using Tafel plots, as shown in Figure S13. The Tafel slopes were obtained from symmetric cell tests conducted at a current density of 1.0 mA cm−2 with a capacity of 1.0 mAh cm−2. The Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT electrode exhibited a higher exchange current density (j0 = 2.28 mA cm−2) than the Li‐Cu foil (j0 = 1.4 mA cm−2), indicating accelerated interfacial charge transfer and faster redox kinetics at the electrode–electrolyte interface [60]. The larger exchange current density suggests that the SiO2@AC‐CNT host significantly reduces kinetic barriers for lithium plating and stripping [61]. This enhancement could be attributed to the synergistic effect of the amorphous carbon–CNT conductive framework and the SiO2 layer, which together provide numerous electrochemically active sites and uniform electron pathways. The highly dispersed CNT network enables efficient redox reactions, while the amorphous carbon and SiO2 increase the local lithium‐ion concentration, facilitating homogeneous nucleation and stable SEI formation during cycling. Based on the Tafel‐derived j0 values, the overpotential (η) was calculated using the Butler—Volmer equation as follows (Equation 1): (1) j=j0expαnFηRT−exp1−αnFηRT where j is the applied current density, α is the charge transfer coefficient (0.5 for lithium), and F, R, T are the Faraday constant, gas constant, and temperature, respectively [62]. By substituting the experimental values (j = 1.0 mA cm−2, j0 = 2.28 mA cm−2), the calculated overpotential for a single electrode is approximately 11 mV, yielding a total overpotential of 22 mV for the symmetric configuration. This theoretical value is consistent with the experimentally observed overpotential of 18 mV, indicating that polarization in the Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT cell primarily originates from intrinsic charge‐transfer kinetics rather than mass‐transport limitations. Additionally, the lithiophilic SiO2 domains lower the lithium nucleation barrier, while the highly conductive CNT network provides continuous electron pathways that decrease charge‐transfer resistance across the electrode. The amorphous carbon matrix further minimizes local current density and promotes uniform lithium deposition.

Full‐Cell Application in Lithium Metal Batteries

To evaluate the practical potential of SiO2@AC‐CNT as a lithium metal host, full‐cell tests were conducted using commercial LiFePO4 (LFP) cathodes paired with SiO2@AC‐CNT and gCu foil, denoted as Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT|LFP and Li‐Cu|LFP, respectively (Figure 7). Long‐term cycling was performed with an areal capacity of 1.3 mAh cm−2 for the LFP cathode and 7.0 mAh cm−2 of lithium to assess the durability and feasibility of the SiO2@AC‐CNT host (Figure 7a). The initial two cycles at 0.1 C were applied to stabilize the cathode‐electrolyte interface (CEI) prior to long‐term cycling at 2.0 C. At 0.1 C, the initial discharge capacities were 158.7 mAh g−1 for Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT|LFP and 154.8 mAh g−1 for Li‐Cu|LFP. When the current density was increased to 2.0 C, the Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT|LFP cell delivered 131.7 mAh g−1, while the Li‐Cu|LFP cell showed 132.1 mAh g−1. In the Li‐Cu|LFP cell, the capacity rose to 137.5 mAh g−1 and stabilized at 132.0 mAh g−1 after 300 cycles, followed by rapid fading after 350 cycles, resulting in a capacity retention of 96.0%. In contrast, the Li–SiO2@AC‐CNT|LFP cell delivered an initial capacity of 140.9 mAh g−1 and retained 136.0 mAh g−1 after 600 cycles, corresponding to a capacity retention of 96.5%. Additionally, the average CE of Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT|LFP was 99.99% over 600 cycles, substantially higher than the 99.59% observed for Li‐Cu|LFP. These results demonstrate that the optimized combination of lithiophilic SiO2 with conductive amorphous and graphitic carbon, together with a stable SEI layer, effectively suppresses inactive lithium formation and promotes uniform lithium plating/stripping, resulting in superior cycling stability, capacity retention, and CE. To further assess cycling performance under limited lithium conditions, additional tests were conducted with lithium areal capacities of 5.0 and 3.0 mAh cm−2. With 5.0 mAh cm−2 of lithium (Figure 7b), the Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT|LFP cell maintained a high CE of 99.97% and stable cycling over 500 cycles at 2.0 C, whereas the Li‐Cu|LFP cell showed a gradual decline after 200 cycles and rapid fading at 320 cycles. When the lithium capacity was reduced to 3.0 mAh cm−2, the cell maintained a CE of 99.99% for 350 cycles at 1.0 C and 99.97% for 200 cycles at 2.0 C (Figure S14a,b). These observations confirm that the SiO2@AC‐CNT host effectively suppresses dead lithium accumulation and preserves reversibility even under reduced N/P ratio conditions. To further demonstrate the practical feasbility of the Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT|LFP full cell, a comparative summary of recently reported LFP‐based lithium metal full cells with varying cathode loadings and lithium inventories is provided in Table S3. Compared with these systems, the Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT|LFP full cells exhibited superior capacity retention even under low lithium excess. These results confirm that the advantages of the SiO2@AC‐CNT host are preserved under practical full‐cell conditions with a commercial cathode, underscoring its strong potential for next‐generation LMBs applications. The rate performance of Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT|LFP and Li‐Cu|LFP cells was compared by increasing the current density from 0.1 C to 5.0 C, with 1.0 C defined as 170 mAh g−1, as shown in Figure 7c. The electrolyte used for full‐cell rate tests was 1.0 M LiPF6 dissolved in a mixed solvent of ethylene carbonate (EC), dimethyl carbonate (DMC), and ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) in a volume ratio of 1:2:1, with 2 wt% vinylene carbonate (VC) as an additive. Carbonate‐based electrolytes were used to evaluate the rate capability of full cells, as they are compatible with LFP cathodes and impose more demanding conditions for assessing lithium plating/stripping kinetics. Given the limited oxidative stability of ether electrolytes at the operating voltages of full cells, carbonate formulations provide a more suitable platform for high‐rate evaluation with commercial LFP cathodes. The Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT|LFP full cell delivered discharge capacities of 163, 150, 141, 134, 129, and 124 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C, 1.0 C, 2.0 C, 3.0 C, 4.0 C, and 5.0 C, respectively, whereas the Li‐Cu|LFP cell delivered 155, 131, 118, 106, 94, and 81 mAh g−1. Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT|LFP exhibited higher capacities at all current densities, with lower degradation at high rates. Moreover, after returning to 0.1C and 1.0 C, the discharge capacities of Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT|LFP were 166 and 153 mAh g−1, demonstrating excellent stability. The voltage profiles from the rate‐capability tests were analyzed to understand the differences between the two full cells (Figure S15). The gap between the charge and discharge curves reflects cell polarization. At 0.1 C, Li‐Cu|LFP and Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT|LFP showed nearly identical profiles, indicating similar polarization (0.05 V). As the rate increased to 1.0 C, the polarization difference became pronounced: Li‐SiO2@AC‐CNT|LFP exhibited 90 mV, whereas Li‐Cu|LFP reached 340 mV. At 3.0 C and 5.0 C, Li‐Cu|LFP experienced severe polarization, with the charge plateau nearly disappearing. These results indicate that the SiO2@AC‐CNT host reduces resistance, particularly at high current densities, thereby enhancing rate performance.

Conclusion

In this study, SiO2@AC‐CNT was synthesized via spray pyrolysis as a 3D host for LMAs. Comprehensive structural and electrochemical analyses confirmed that the composite features a porous architecture, well‐distributed lithiophilic sites, and adequate electrical conductivity. These characteristics enable uniform lithium nucleation and deposition while effectively suppressing dendritic growth. Ex situ XPS analysis showed that the amorphous carbon component promotes the formation of a Li2CO3‐rich SEI, enhancing lithium‐ion transport kinetics, whereas LiF provides strong electronic passivation. As a result, the SiO2@AC‐CNT electrode supports stable and dense lithium deposition, exhibiting a low voltage hysteresis of 18 mV maintained over 1300 h in symmetric cells. Full cells paired with LFP cathodes demonstrated superior capacity retention and rate capability compared to bare Cu electrodes. These findings highlight that integrating lithiophilic SiO2, amorphous carbon, and conductive CNTs into a 3D framework offers a promising strategy for stable, high‐performance LMAs, with practical potential for next‐generation LMBs.
