Utility of the scope retractor for adhesiolysis in unilateral biportal endoscopic lumbar surgery: illustrative cases
Authors: Changik Lee, Young-Jin Kim, Jin-Sung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDDuring unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) lumbar surgery, adhesiolysis can be technically challenging compared with microscopic lumbar surgery because of the constraints imposed by single-handed manipulation. The authors evaluated the use of a scope retractor as an adjunctive tool to facilitate bimanual-like tissue handling.OBSERVATIONSTwo illustrative cases with severe epidural adhesions—one related to postoperative scarring and the other associated with chronic inflammation from a synovial cyst—were treated with the aid of a scope retractor, which was used either to secure adhesive tissue or as a dissecting instrument. Successful adhesiolysis and adequate neural decompression were achieved in both cases.LESSONSUBE surgery provides high-quality visualization that enhances differentiation between the dura mater and surrounding soft tissue. Incorporation of a scope retractor facilitates precise tissue handling by providing an effect analogous to bimanual manipulation, improving surgical outcomes in cases with severe epidural adhesions. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26223

As an increasing number of spine surgeons adopt the unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) technique, its indications have expanded, progressively replacing the role of conventional microscopic spine surgery in procedures such as central canal decompression, foraminal decompression, and interbody fusion.1–4 However, because the surgeon must hold the endoscope with one hand, operative maneuvers are inherently limited to the remaining hand. Consequently, procedures that require stable control of the target tissue, such as dural suturing or placement of hemostatic agents, are technically more demanding with UBE surgery than with microscopic surgery, which allows the use of both hands.5

This limitation becomes more pronounced in the presence of severe epidural adhesions. During conventional microscopic surgery, adhesive tissue can be secured with one hand while adhesiolysis is performed along the plane between the adhesive tissue and the dura mater using the other hand. In contrast, during UBE surgery, securing adhesive tissue while performing adhesiolysis is more challenging because of the constraints imposed by single-handed manipulation.

The scope retractor was originally devised to protect the dura mater and nerve roots during UBE surgery.5 However, its role has expanded to address some of the technical limitations inherent to single-handed surgery. Beyond dural protection, the use of a scope retractor for handling adhesive tissue may facilitate adhesiolysis by providing an effect analogous to bimanual manipulation. In this report, we describe an adhesiolysis technique utilizing the scope retractor.

Illustrative Cases

Surgical Technique

Under epidural or general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the prone position. Using fluoroscopic guidance, we identified and marked the medial pedicle line. In the lateral view, a working portal was established parallel to the intervertebral disc, and a viewing portal was created approximately 2.5 cm cranial or caudal to the working portal for a left- or right-sided approach, respectively. The multifidus muscle was detached from the lamina using a Cobb periosteal elevator. In revision cases, in which a prior laminotomy had already been performed, it is recommended to first identify the isthmus and proceed in a caudal direction to identify the inferior border of the cranial lamina while maintaining continuous contact with the bony surface.

When adhesive tissue obscures the distinction between the lamina and the interlaminar space, limited drilling to remove adhesive tissue adherent to the bone can clarify the bony margins and facilitate accurate identification of the anatomical boundaries (Fig. 1). Starting at the spinolaminar junction, the cranial lamina is drilled. Using a curette, we detached the ligamentum flavum or adhesive tissue from the lamina to create a safe epidural working space. If necessary, additional bony removal is performed using a Kerrison punch. By scraping beneath the lamina and proceeding in a caudal direction, the medial facet joint and caudal lamina can be identified. Partial drilling and selective removal of bony structures allow exposure of the epidural space at the lateral and caudal aspects. FIG. 1. Dissection should be initiated from an area of dura mater where adhesive tissue is not attached, proceeding along the plane between the dura mater and the adhesive tissue using a curette or Penfield dissector. Because UBE surgery is inherently a single-handed technique, detachment of adhesive tissue that is mobile in the saline environment can be technically challenging. By securing the adhesive tissue with a scope retractor, adhesiolysis can be performed in a more efficient manner (Fig. 2A). Alternatively, adhesive tissue may be grasped and gently pulled using pituitary forceps for detachment. However, in the absence of countertraction to stabilize the dura mater, this maneuver may increase the risk of dural injury. The scope retractor can be used as a dissecting instrument by gently pushing or sweeping the dura mater while simultaneously pulling the adhesive tissue (Fig. 2B). This approach replicates bimanual manipulation typically achieved under microscopic surgery. Complete removal of adhesive tissue is not required. Because excessive manipulation may increase the risk of dural injury, selective removal limited to the area required for decompression is recommended.6 FIG. 2. Case 1

A 78-year-old male with a history of total laminectomy and transpedicular screw fixation at the L3–5 levels performed 10 years earlier, as well as discectomy at the L2–3 level 6 months prior, presented to the outpatient clinic with recurrent right anterior thigh pain. Plain lateral radiographs demonstrated disc height loss and retrolisthesis of the L2 vertebral body consistent with adjacent segment degeneration (Fig. 3A). MRI revealed recurrent herniated nucleus pulposus at the L2–3 level (Fig. 3B). To prevent further recurrence and potential progression of iatrogenic instability, fusion at the L2–3 level was recommended. However, the patient declined fusion because of concerns regarding potential complications. Therefore, revisional UBE discectomy was performed using the technique described above (Video 1). After identification of the cranial lamina, a curette was used to scrape beneath the bony structures to expose the medial facet joint and caudal lamina. Following partial laminotomy, the dura mater was identified at the caudal aspect, and adhesive tissue was partially detached using a curette. Through the working portal, the partially detached adhesive tissue was grasped and gently pulled using pituitary forceps, while the plane between the dura mater and the adhesive tissue was simultaneously swept using the scope retractor. Adhesive tissue was efficiently removed using this technique. Following adhesiolysis, herniated disc material was exposed along the lateral border of the dura mater. During discectomy, the scope retractor was used to protect the dura mater and maintain a secure working space. The patient’s severe pain markedly improved after the operation. Case 2

A 55-year-old male patient presented to the outpatient clinic with low back pain and bilateral leg radiating pain. He also complained of neurogenic claudication occurring after walking less than 500 m. MRI revealed spinal stenosis at the L4–5 level with an associated left-sided synovial cyst (Fig. 4A and B). Based on the radiological findings and clinical symptoms, unilateral laminotomy with bilateral decompression and synovial cyst removal at the left side of L4–5 level was planned (Video 2). After drilling of the ipsilateral and contralateral laminae, the insertion of the ligamentum flavum was detached using a curette, and the epidural space was exposed. Dense adhesion between the ligamentum flavum and the dura mater was observed, presumably due to chronic inflammation associated with the synovial cyst. Using the scope retractor, we gently elevated and stabilized the cranial end of the ligamentum flavum while adhesiolysis was attempted using a Penfield dissector and a hook-type dissector. However, detachment of the ligamentum flavum was limited due to the dense adhesion. Pituitary forceps were then used to gently pull the ligamentum flavum while counterforce was applied to the dura mater using the scope retractor. The ligamentum flavum was gradually detached and ultimately removed en bloc. Residual adhesive tissue on the dura mater was also removed using the same technique. The dura mater was fully exposed, and bilateral traversing nerve roots were sufficiently decompressed. Postoperative MRI demonstrated adequate decompression, and the patient’s symptoms improved significantly (Fig. 4C).

Discussion

Observations

With advances in surgical techniques, UBE lumbar surgery has been increasingly applied to revision cases.6 Previous studies have reported that UBE revision lumbar discectomy yielded long-term outcomes comparable to those of microscopic revision surgery, while providing less immediate postoperative pain and shorter hospitalized days.7,8 In addition, UBE revisional lumbar discectomy has been reported to demonstrate equivalent outcomes to those of primary UBE lumbar discectomy.9

The advantages of UBE surgery for adhesiolysis include the provision of magnified high-resolution real-time visualization, which facilitates accurate discrimination between neural structures and adhesive tissue.10 In revision surgery, paraspinal muscle elasticity is often reduced because of postoperative fibrosis, making muscle retraction difficult and necessitating a longer incision to obtain an adequate operative field during microscopic surgery. In contrast, UBE surgery allows direct access to the surgical target through muscle-splitting portals, eliminating the need for muscle retraction.11,12 Nevertheless, because UBE surgery is inherently a single-handed technique, adhesiolysis is technically more demanding than in microscopic surgery, which allows the use of both hands.

Since Hong et al. introduced the scope retractor to protect the dura mater and traversing nerve root during laminectomy, it has been adopted by many surgeons, with its clinical applications continuing to expand.5 Zhang et al. utilized a translucent retractor for lumbar discectomy and reported lower postoperative numeric rating scale and Oswestry Disability Index scores compared with procedures performed using a metallic scope retractor.13 Park and Oh utilized a transparent scope retractor to retract the detached ligamentum flavum and maintain the surgical plane between the remnant ligamentum flavum and the underlying bony structures.14 In addition, Kim and Heo reported the use of a scope retractor to elevate the lamina flap from the ipsilateral edge of the laminectomy during UBE cervical laminoplasty.15 The scope retractor helps address the inherent limitation of UBE surgery related to single-handed manipulation. We first applied a scope retractor for adhesiolysis; it can be used to hold tissue in a manner similar to forceps, or it can be used as a dissector over the dura mater. In the second method, adhesive tissue can be gently pulled with pituitary forceps while the scope retractor is used to sweep along the surface of the dura mater, allowing more effective adhesiolysis than the simple tissue holding maneuver (Fig. 5). Lessons

Adequate adhesiolysis facilitates clear differentiation between neural structures and compressive lesions such as herniated disc, enabling precise removal and reliable confirmation of sufficient decompression. However, the scope retractor is not applicable in all cases of adhesiolysis. In the presence of dense epidural adhesions, excessive manipulation may increase the risk of neural tissue injury. When traction on the adhesive tissue resulted in elevation of the underlying dura mater, we avoided applying additional tension, as this was considered indicative of excessive manipulation and an increased risk of dural injury. Furthermore, the scope retractor cannot be used in the initial stage of adhesiolysis, as identification of normal neural structures through laminotomy or sublaminar dissection must precede. Its application becomes feasible only after the plane between the dura mater and the adhesive tissue has been adequately identified.

Complete removal of all adhesive tissue is not required. Adhesiolysis should be limited to the extent necessary to achieve sufficient decompression, such as exposure of the lateral border of the dural sac to identify the intervertebral disc space, because aggressive dissection may increase the risk of neural injury.6 Even with magnified endoscopic visualization, differentiation between neural structures and adhesive tissue may remain challenging in certain cases. Therefore, adhesiolysis requires careful judgment, meticulous technique, and adequate surgical experience.

In this report, we described a technique for adhesiolysis using a scope retractor in the presence of severe epidural adhesions. Application of the scope retractor may overcome the inherent limitation of single-handed manipulation in UBE surgery by enabling bimanual-like tissue handling, allowing the surgeon to secure adhesive tissue and facilitate more efficient adhesiolysis.
