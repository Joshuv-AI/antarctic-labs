Unilateral diffuse alveolar haemorrhage as an atypical presentation of PR3-ANCA-associated vasculitis: A case report
Authors: Poonam Patil, Babaji Ghewade, Ulhas Jadhav, Sanket Ghute, Beeravolu Harshith Reddy, Amisha Harish Soni, Shristi Jain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diffuse alveolar haemorrhage (DAH) is a life-threatening pulmonary manifestation of small-vessel vasculitis, most commonly associated with anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis. Unilateral DAH has been infrequently reported and may pose diagnostic challenges, as its radiological presentation can mimic pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, or localized haemorrhage. This atypical presentation may delay recognition of the underlying vasculitis and appropriate immunosuppressive therapy.We present a case of a woman in her thirties with previously diagnosed PR3-ANCA-associated vasculitis who presented with acute worsening of dyspnea (mMRC grade IV) and hemoptysis. Radiological assessments revealed extensive right-sided ground-glass opacities with areas of consolidation and minimal left lower lobe involvement. Bronchoscopy with sequential bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) showed progressively haemorrhagic aliquots consistent with DAH. Laboratory investigations revealed anemia (Hb 7.1 mg/dL) and elevated serum creatinine (5.02 mg/dL). The patient developed type I respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation and hemodialysis. She was treated with pulse intravenous methylprednisolone followed by rituximab therapy, along with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis. The patient improved clinically, radiologically, and biochemically and was discharged on supplemental oxygen.

Introduction

Unilateral DAH is an uncommon manifestation of PR3-ANCA-associated vasculitis and may delay diagnosis. This case highlights the importance of recognizing atypical unilateral imaging patterns to prompt early bronchoscopy and immunosuppressive therapy. ANCA-associated vasculitis is a necrotizing small-vessel vasculitis typically affecting the kidneys and lungs. DAH is a serious pulmonary complication of ANCA-mediated capillaritis. Unilateral radiographic appearance is rarely documented [1] and can mimic pneumonia or pulmonary oedema, delaying diagnosis [2].

Case presentation

A woman in her thirties presented to the respiratory medicine department with dyspnea of mMRC grade II–III for one year, which acutely progressed to grade IV over two days. This was associated with two to three daily episodes of blood-tinged sputum for four days. She was a nonsmoker with no significant occupational exposure, no history of tuberculosis, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. She had been diagnosed with PR3-ANCA vasculitis during evaluation for sensorineural hearing loss nine months earlier.

General examination revealed a well-built, well-nourished, afebrile patient. She was conscious and oriented. Pallor and bilateral pitting pedal oedema were present. Icterus, cyanosis, clubbing, and lymphadenopathy were absent. Pulse rate was 100 beats per minute, respiratory rate 30 cycles per minute, blood pressure 110/70 mmHg, and oxygen saturation 94% on 6 L of oxygen. Diagnostic assessment

Haemoglobin was 7.1 mg/dL, and serum creatinine was 5.02 mg/dL. PR3-ANCA was positive by ELISA. Arterial blood gas showed type I respiratory failure. Sputum Gene Xpert and culture were negative for acid-fast bacilli and other organisms.

Chest radiography showed a heterogeneous opacity with air bronchogram in the right hemithorax and left costophrenic angle blunting [Fig. 2]. The combination of haemoptysis, anemia, positive PR3-ANCA, renal dysfunction, and progressive haemorrhagic BAL confirmed DAH secondary to PR3-ANCA vasculitis. Unilateral involvement was an atypical feature that initially suggested alternative diagnoses such as pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, or pulmonary embolism.

Hospital course

The patient developed tachypnoea, desaturation, and type I respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation. A multidisciplinary team (pulmonology, rheumatology, nephrology) was formed. After stabilization, induction therapy with pulse steroids and rituximab was given, along with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis. Hemodialysis was required for renal dysfunction. Plasmapheresis was considered but declined by the patient. The patient gradually improved, was weaned from ventilation, and discharged on supplemental oxygen with improving renal parameters.

Discussion

Unilateral DAH is a rare but important atypical presentation of PR3-ANCA-associated vasculitis. While DAH classically presents with bilateral ground-glass opacities [2], unilateral involvement can mislead clinicians toward more common diagnoses such as pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, or malignancy [1]. This diagnostic pitfall may delay life-saving immunosuppressive therapy [3].

In this case, bronchoscopy with sequential BAL was critical in confirming DAH despite unilateral imaging. The progressively haemorrhagic aliquots provided definitive evidence of alveolar bleeding. This finding underscores the importance of early bronchoscopy in any patient with known or suspected ANCA-associated vasculitis presenting with acute respiratory symptoms and hemoptysis, regardless of radiographic distribution [2].

The prognosis of ANCA-associated vasculitis has improved substantially with modern immunosuppression [4], but relapse remains common [5]. The addition of cyclophosphamide to corticosteroids lowered 5-year mortality to 12% [3]. Remission rates now reach 90% with aggressive therapy, and more than 50% of patients recover renal function and become dialysis independent [4]. Unilateral DAH as a first presentation or flare is not well characterized in the literature, and this case expands the recognized radiological spectrum of the disease [6,7]. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for DAH even when imaging is unilateral [1,2].

Plasma exchange is not routinely indicated for DAH but may be considered in select cases [8,9]. Current practice supports individualized decision-making rather than routine use [5,8].

Conclusion

Unilateral diffuse alveolar haemorrhage is an uncommon and diagnostically challenging manifestation of PR3-ANCA-associated vasculitis [6]. It may mimic more common pulmonary conditions, delaying diagnosis and treatment [2]. Early bronchoscopy with sequential BAL is essential for confirmation [2]. A multidisciplinary approach and prompt initiation of immunosuppressive therapy are critical for favourable outcomes [4,5]. This case broadens the recognized imaging presentations of PR3-ANCA-associated vasculitis [6].
