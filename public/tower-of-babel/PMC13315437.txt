Whole mitogenome profile of Pelung and Sentul chickens to reveal potency of Indonesian livestock genetic resources
Authors: Nena Hilmia, Indrawati Yudha Asmara, Dani Garnida, Muhammad Ihsan Andi Dagong, Sri Rachma Aprilita Bugiwati, Sutopo Sutopo, Dela Ayu Lestari, Asep Setiaji, Fajrin Shidiq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Native chickens are essential genetic resources in Indonesia, providing economic, cultural, and nutritional value with strong adaptability to local environments. Among these native chickens, Sentul and Pelung are recognized as national genetic resources due to dual-purpose and ornamental traits, respectively, but their whole mitogenome characterization remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to assemble and analyze the whole mitogenome of Sentul and Pelung chickens as well as perform a comparison with 35 additional genomes from domestic chickens and jungle fowls across Asia. The experiment was carried out using next-generation sequencing and bioinformatics-based genome assembly and analysis. The results showed that both Sentul and Pelung genomes were 16,784 bp in length and contained the typical mitochondrial gene composition, including 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 22 transfer ribonucleic acids (tRNAs), two ribosomal ribonucleic acids (rRNAs), and a control region (D-loop). Furthermore, comparative analysis identified five single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) distinguishing the two breeds, located in ND1, COX1, COX2, ND4, and the D-loop region. Phylogenetic reconstruction based on whole mitochondrial sequences showed that Sentul and Pelung chickens belong to haplogroup D, alongside other native breeds and red jungle fowls from Indonesia and the Philippines.

Introduction

Native chickens are essential genetic resources with social, cultural, and economic value, serving as a sustainable source of animal protein for the future. In Indonesia, poultry contributes the largest share of animal protein, showing the importance of native chickens due to their adaptability to tropical environments and resilience against diverse diseases.1

In West Java, Sentul and Pelung chickens represent unique native genetic resources with strong development potential. Sentul is valued for superior growth and higher egg production compared to other local breeds, showing high suitability as dual purpose chickens.2 Meanwhile, Pelung is renowned for distinctive crowing, which confers socio cultural and economic value as an ornamental and companion animal.3 Recognizing the importance, the Indonesian Ministry of Agriculture formally designated these breeds as national genetic resources (Pelung: Decree No. 2918/Kpts/OT.140/6/2011; Sentul: Decree No. 698/Kpts/PD.410/2/2013).

The conservation and sustainable use of native chicken genetic resources depend critically on a comprehensive understanding of their genetic characteristics. This information provides a foundation for conservation strategies that integrate economic, sociological, and political considerations to prevent the irreversible loss of animal genetic diversity.4 Therefore, advances in conservation efforts have significantly enhanced the understanding of the structures of livestock by identifying wild progenitors, exploring their genetic contributions to domestic breeds, and uncovering the evolutionary processes underlying domestication.5

At the DNA level, molecular characterization offers a powerful method for defining genetic diversity. Among several molecular markers, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is particularly valuable due to its small size (11–50 kb), high copy number, maternal inheritance, absence of recombination, and rapid evolutionary rate.6, 7 These features, along with the conserved gene organization and relatively simple molecular architecture, make mtDNA an ideal marker for studying phylogeny, population structure, and domestication history.8 The compact and evolutionary dynamic nature of the mitogenome has made it an indispensable tool in evolutionary and population genetics studies.6

Mitochondrial diversity is closely related to adaptive and physiological traits. For example, mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in aging and age-related diseases through disruptions in ATP synthesis, calcium homeostasis, and reactive oxygen species generation.9 Adaptive evolution in mtDNA genes such as ND2, ND4, and ATP6 has also been associated with altitude adaptation in avian species.10 Furthermore, mitochondria govern essential physiochemical and biochemical in both animals and humans, including their involvement in diseases such as cancer11

In Indonesia, studies of native chickens have predominantly depended on microsatellites12, 13, 14 or partial mtDNA fragments such as COX115 and the D-loop.16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22 Currently, only one whole mitogenome from the Cemani chicken,23 two from Indonesian jungle fowls,24 and 13 additional unknown breeds of indigenous chicken mitogenomes are available in GenBank.25 Consequently, most Indonesian native breeds remain uncharacterized at the whole mitogenome level.

Recent advances in next-generation sequencing enable whole mitogenome assembly, providing deeper resolution of genetic diversity, evolutionary origins, and phylogenetic relationships. Therefore, establishing the complete mtDNA sequences of Sentul and Pelung chickens addresses a critical gap and provides a genomic foundation for their conservation and sustainable utilization. This study contributes to the sustainability of animal-based food systems, facilitating community food security and improved nutritional outcomes in alignment with the Sustainable Development Goals, specifically Zero Hunger.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Sentul and Pelung chickens sample collection was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Padjadjaran University (Approval No: 218/UN6.KEP/EC/2024). Liver tissue samples were obtained from Sentul chickens at the Nucleus Breeding Center and Development of Indigenous Chicken (BPPTU) in Jatiwangi, Majalengka District. Meanwhile, samples of Pelung chickens were obtained through the Association of Indonesian Pelung Chicken Fans and Breeders (HIPPAPI), Bandung District. Approximately 10 mg of liver tissue was collected from each individual, preserved in DNA/RNA Shield (Zymo Research, USA), and stored at −20 °C until further processing.

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted using the gSYNC DNA Extraction Kit (Geneaid, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer's protocol. DNA concentration and purity were initially assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and DNA fragment size was visualized on a 0.8% agarose gel. Accurate quantification was performed using a Qubit Fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA), and DNA integrity was further evaluated using the 4150 TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, USA).

Library preparation & sequencing

High-quality gDNA was applied for library preparation using the MGIEasy FS DNA Library Prep Set (MGI, China). Subsequently, DNA was enzymatically fragmented to obtain the desired insert size, followed by ligation of MGI-compatible adapters containing unique sample barcodes. The adapter sequences used were Forward Adapter — AAGTCGGAGGCCAAGCGGTCTTAGGAAGACAA, and Reverse Adapter — AAGTCGGATCGTAGCCATGTCGTTCTGTGAGCCAAGGAGTTG.

Following adapter ligation, the fragments were amplified through polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Library quality and concentration were assessed using the 4150 TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, USA) and Qubit Fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA), respectively. Amplified libraries were circularized to form single-stranded circular DNA, which served as templates for DNA nanoball (DNB) generation.

The DNBs obtained were loaded onto flow cells and sequenced on the MGI DNBSEQ-G400 platform (MGI, China). Sentul chicken libraries were sequenced using 312 cycles (PE150), while Pelung was performed through 612 cycles (PE300). Mitochondrial de novo assembly

Raw sequencing reads generated from the MGI DNBSEQ-G400 platform were preprocessed using fastp (v0.23.2) for adapter trimming and quality filtering.26 Read quality before and after filtering was evaluated with FastQC (v0.11.9). High-quality reads were correlated with Gallus gallus mitochondrial reference genome (NC_053523) using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17-r1188).27 Meanwhile, only reads mapping to mitogenome were retained for downstream analysis. Post-alignment processing, including format conversion and sorting, was performed using SAMtools (v1.6).28

De novo mitogenome assembly was performed with SPAdes (v3.15.5),29 and the assembled contigs were visualized with Bandage (v0.8.1).30 Low-coverage (less than 2×) and short-length (less than 1000 bp) contigs were discarded from the genome sequence, followed by three iterative rounds of error correction using Pilon (v1.24).31 Assembly quality metrics were assessed using QUAST (v5.0.2)32 and Qualimap (v2.2.2-dev), with all quality control reports consolidated through MultiQC (v1.13).33

To confirm taxonomic identity and assess potential contamination, the assembled mitogenomes were queried against the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) nr/nt database using BLASTn (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi).34 Genome annotation was conducted using MitoZ (v3.4).35 Finalized assemblies were reoriented to match the standard G. gallus mitogenome starting position (NC_053523) and visualized using Geneious Prime® (v2025.1.3). A comprehensive overview of the bioinformatics workflow is provided in Fig. 1 (blue box).

Data analysis

Nucleotide composition skewness was calculated following Perna & Kocher (1995),36 with formulas: AT-skew = (A − T)/(A + T) and GC-skew = (G − C)/(G + C). Secondary structures of transfer ribonucleic acids (tRNAs) genes were predicted and visualized using the MITOS2 web server (http://mitos2.bioinf.uni-leipzig.de/index.py).37

Comparative and phylogenetic analysis

A phylogenetic tree was constructed using complete mitogenome sequences to clarify the evolutionary position of Sentul and Pelung chickens within the G. genus. In total, 37 complete chicken mitogenomes representing diverse breeds were retrieved from GenBank (Table 1). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MUSCLE v5.38 The best-fit nucleotide substitution model was selected with the Find Best DNA Substitution Model implemented in MEGA12,39 under Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), which identified GTR + G + I as the optimal model. Phylogenetic reconstruction was carried out using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method with 1000 bootstrap replicates. Meleagris gallopavo (Turkey, NC_010195) was used as the outgroup. Results and discussion

High-quality mitogenome assemblies were generated for Sentul and Pelung chickens using MGI sequencing technology. For the Sentul chicken, sequencing produced 20,564,260 raw reads, with 19,874,244 passing quality filtering. A total of 59,152 reads mapped to mitogenome, leading to a complete circular assembly of 16,784 bp with a mean coverage depth of 501.57× (±118.08). Similarly, sequencing of Pelung chickens yielded 22,829,666 raw reads, with 22,566,268 being retained after filtering. Among these reads, 63,784 were mapped to mitogenome, producing an identical circular assembly of 16,784 bp with a higher mean coverage of 973.52× (±294.8). Both assemblies were gap-free, confirming the reliability of the reconstructed genomes (Fig. 2). BLAST analysis showed that both genomes share 99.9% sequence identity with Daninghe Chicken (OR187300), Huxu Chicken (CP115610), and 20230224_TCRDL_Ggal Chicken (LC880097).41, 42Fig. 2The whole mitogenome of Sentul and Pelung chickens. The assembled mitogenome lengths of Sentul and Pelung chickens (16,784 bp) are consistent with the values reported for most domestic breeds, such as Kedu, Garut, and Manado, measuring 16,785 bp. Meanwhile, the Green Jungle Fowl genome is slightly shorter at 16,783 bp.24 Red Jungle Fowl, the primary ancestor of domestic chickens, shows minimal variation, ranging from 16,784 to 16,785 bp across regions.24, 40, 43 Chinese native breeds show a similarly narrow range between 16,784 and 16,786 bp.,40, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48 while several Indian breeds, including Kadaknath and Aseel, are shorter at 16,775 bp.49 In comparison, the Ceylon and Gray Jungle Fowl possess the longest at 16,841 bp,24 representing a 57 bp increase relative to Sentul and Pelung.

The results show that mitogenome lengths in Sentul and Pelung chickens are highly conserved, falling within the narrow range of variation (1–2 bp) observed across Gallus lineages (Table 1). Based on the results, both Sentul and Pelung chickens showed the typical avian mitochondrial architecture,23 comprising 13 PCGs, 22 transfer RNAs (tRNAs), two ribosomal RNAs (12S and 16S rRNA), and the non-coding control region (D-loop). Gene order and orientation were fully conserved with the Gallus reference, where the majority of genes were encoded on the heavy (H) strand. The 13 PCGs spanned 11,396 bp, accounting for ∼68% of the mitogenome. Gene lengths ranged from 165 bp (ATP8) to 1818 bp (ND5). The largest genes were ND5 (1818 bp), COX1 (1551 bp), and ND4 (1378 bp), while ATP8 was the smallest (165 bp). The PCGs were AT-rich, with overall A + T contents of 53.06% and 53.05% for the Sentul and Pelung chickens, respectively (Fig. 3). Among individual genes, A + T content ranged from 50.51% (COX3) to 54.70% (ND3) in both breeds. Initiation codons were predominantly ATG, consistent with the most common start codon across metazoans.,52 except COX1, which utilizes GTG, a start codon reported only in some vertebrates and invertebrates.53, 54

In line with the results, stop codons showed higher variation. A total of nine genes (ATP8, ATP6, ND1, ND3, ND4L, ND5, COX2, CYTB, and ND6) terminated with TAA, the most frequent stop codon in vertebrate mitogenomes.55, 56, 57, 58 COX1 ended with AGG, which, although originally encoding arginine in the standard genetic code, has been reassigned as a stop codon in metazoan mtDNA.59 Three genes, namely ND2, COX3, and ND4, contained incomplete termination codons (T--), a typical feature of vertebrate mitogenomes that is resolved through post-transcriptional polyadenylation.60

Based on the observation in other avian species, both breeds showed an additional cytosine in ND3 at position 9726 bp. This extra base is not translated but induces a frameshift, a phenomenon previously reported in avian mitogenomes, although the underlying mechanism remains unresolved.61 The ribosomal RNA genes measured 975 bp (12S rRNA) and 1622 bp (16S rRNA), flanked by tRNAPhe, tRNAVal, and tRNALeu2. The 22 tRNA genes ranged from 65 to 76 bp and were scattered across the genome, interspersed among PCGs, rRNAs, and the control region. The D-loop was 1231 bp in both breeds, located between tRNAGlu and tRNAPhe. This length is consistent with previous studies of native chickens (1231–1232 bp), showing the potential as the most variable region of the mitogenome, commonly used in population and phylogenetic studies. Substantial diversity was also reported by Sulandari et al. (2008b)20 who identified 54 polymorphic regions forming 69 haplotypes in Indonesian chickens and Jungle Fowl. Osman and Nishibori (2014)62 reported 23–38 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in Southeast Asian populations,63 Siriwadee et al. (2023) observed nine polymorphic and 18 haplotypes in Thai chickens, while22 Ulfah et al. (2015) described 76 polymorphic regions and 93 haplotypes in Indonesian breeds and wild fowls.

When comparing the mitogenomes of Sentul and Pelung chickens, five SNPs were identified, including ND1 (3207 A > G), COX1 (6657 T > A), COX2 (7608 T > C), ND4 (11,349C > T), and the D-loop (15,774 T > C). Comparative analysis across a broader dataset, including 35 additional chicken breeds and local populations, showed that the ND1 substitution (3207 A > G) was shared exclusively between Pelung and Goma chickens, while the COX1 (6657 A > T) and COX2 (7608C > T) variants were unique to the Sentul breed. These lineage-specific polymorphisms represent potential diagnostic markers for breed differentiation, requiring further validation in larger populations.

The complete sequences of Sentul and Pelung chickens were aligned with 35 additional mitogenomes representing diverse chicken breeds retrieved from the NCBI database, as presented in Table 3. Phylogenetic reconstruction based on the complete mitochondrial sequences provided a clearer resolution of the evolutionary position of Sentul and Pelung chickens within the G. genus. Haplogroup classification followed the framework of40 Miao et al. (2013), which defined haplogroups A–I and W–Z.

As shown in Fig. 5, both Sentul and Pelung chickens clustered within the common haplogroup D, alongside several other Indonesian native breeds, including Garut, Maluku, Manado, Nunukan, Goma, Kedu, Tarakan, and Lombok.25 The Daweishan Mini chickens from China were also positioned within this haplogroup. Previous studies have shown that the Cemani chickens are closely related to both Daweishan Mini and Kedu breeds. Sutopo et al., 202423 suggested that Sentul and Pelung shared a close matrilineal lineage with Cemani. In Asia, haplogroup D is predominantly distributed in the Philippines, Indonesia, Guam, the Solomon Islands, Vanuatu, and Easter Island. Meanwhile, smaller proportions have also been reported in China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam, Laos, Thailand, India, and Sri Lanka.64 These results show the hypothesis of a shared maternal origin among Southeast Asian native breeds, suggesting that Indonesian domestic chickens have retained strong genetic continuity with their wild ancestors.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the whole mitogenome profile of Sentul and Pelung chickens is identical in length (16,784 bp), containing the typical avian mitochondrial gene composition. These include 13 PCGs, 22 tRNAs, two rRNAs, and a control region (D-loop). A comparative analysis identifies five SNPs that differentiate the two breeds, located in ND1, COX1, COX2, ND4, and the D-loop region. Among these, the ND1 substitution (3207 A > G) was shared exclusively between Pelung and Goma chickens, while the COX1 (6657 A > T) and COX2 (7608C > T) substitutions were unique to Sentul.

Phylogenetic analysis places both breeds within haplogroup D, along with other Indonesian indigenous chickens. These results show their shared maternal ancestry and underscore the close genetic relationship between Indonesian native breeds and their wild progenitors. The whole mitogenome sequences reported in this study provide valuable genomic resources for future analysis on the diversity, evolution, and conservation of Indonesia's native chicken genetic resources.
