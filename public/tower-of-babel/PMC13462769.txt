Reply to Kluger: Tattoo-induced inflammation and immune responses: Mechanistic insights and considerations
Authors: Santiago F. Gonzalez
Published: 2026

We thank Kluger (1) for the opportunity to clarify some aspects of our study. While we agree that caution is required when extrapolating preclinical findings to clinical settings, these limitations were explicitly acknowledged in our original manuscript (2) and do not detract from the mechanistic insights provided.

Our experimental design was not intended to replicate aesthetic tattooing practices but rather to establish a controlled, reproducible exposure model. The selected tattoo area ensured consistent pigment delivery and sufficient signal for quantitative and mechanistic analyses. While variability in tattoo size and density exists in real-world settings, pigment transport to and accumulation in draining lymph nodes is well documented across species, including humans. Our study builds on this foundation by addressing the functional immunological consequences of such exposure.

Importantly, in addition to the murine model, we performed in vitro experiments using human macrophages that recapitulated key findings, including the capture of tattoo pigment and induction of cell death or reduced antigen expression under tattoo-related conditions. Furthermore, analysis of human lymph node samples confirmed pigment accumulation in anatomical regions consistent with our experimental observations. Together, these data strengthen the biological relevance of our findings.

Regarding vaccination timing, the intervals used were intentionally selected to probe distinct phases of the tattoo-induced inflammatory response. Specifically, we examined both an acute phase and a later, chronic stage. This design was not meant to reflect clinical recommendations, but rather to enable mechanistic insight into how different inflammatory contexts influence vaccine responses. Consistent with this objective, we show that tattoo-associated inflammation can affect the mechanism of action of messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccines. Both in vivo and in vitro data indicate that pigment-laden antigen-presenting cells exhibit reduced antigen expression, supporting a direct effect on vaccine responsiveness at the cellular level.

We also acknowledge the differences in outcomes observed between the COVID-19 (mRNA) and influenza (inactivated) vaccines. We interpret this divergence as reflecting fundamental differences in vaccine technology and immune activation mechanisms, rather than an inconsistency. These findings highlight that immune modulation is context- and platform-dependent.

We respectfully disagree with the suggestion that our interpretation is selective. Our conclusions are consistently grounded in the observation that tattooing induces a persistent and evolving inflammatory environment. We demonstrate sustained inflammatory signaling and progressive pigment accumulation in draining lymph nodes. This results in a chronic inflammatory state associated with incomplete clearance and prolonged immune engagement. In this context, the introduction of additional immune stimuli, such as vaccination, may produce effects that are not readily predictable. Accordingly, our interpretation is intentionally cautious and not restricted to a specific vaccine type. Rather than advancing prescriptive conclusions, we emphasize the need for further investigation into how chronic inflammatory niches in lymph nodes from tattooed individuals might influence immune responses.

In summary, our study demonstrates that tattoo ink persists in lymphoid tissues and can modulate immune function in a biologically meaningful way. We agree that further experimental and clinical studies will be essential to define the extent and clinical relevance of these effects and to inform evidence-based recommendations.
