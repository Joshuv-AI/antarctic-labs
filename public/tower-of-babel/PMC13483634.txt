Associations between adiposity indices and liver steatosis and stiffness assessed by two-dimensional shear wave elastography in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Asieh Mansour, Mehrnam Amouei, Amir Hossein Esmaeili, Atie Sadat Khorasanian, Hadis Gerami, Amir Reza Radmard, Arina Afshinpour, Fateme Sadat Fani Sadrabadi, Elham Madreseh, Mohammad Reza Mohajeri-Tehrani, Sayed Mahmoud Sajjadi-Jazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study investigated associations between adiposity indices and non-invasive markers of liver fibrosis and steatosis in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).MethodsWe assessed adiposity using body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and visceral adiposity index (VAI), along with body composition and fat distribution measured by bioimpedance analysis and abdominal ultrasonography, respectively. Liver steatosis and fibrosis were evaluated using non-invasive imaging methods, including two-dimensional shear wave elastography (2D-SWE). Multivariable regression was used to examine independent associations between adiposity indices and liver outcomes after adjustment for confounders, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis with area under the curve (AUC) values was performed to assess discriminative ability.ResultsAmong 164 adults with T2DM (86 men, 78 women), 27 (16.5%) had hepatic steatosis (≥ S2; attenuation coefficient > 0.6 dB/cm/MHz), and 19 (11.6%) had fibrosis (≥ F1; liver stiffness > 7.1 kPa). In fully adjusted for relevant confounders, central adiposity indices including visceral fat thickness (VFT) (B = 0.023; p = 0.012), WC (B = 0.049; p < 0.001), WHtR (B = 6.532; p = 0.005), and WHR (B = 5.155; p = 0.037) were independently associated with increased liver stiffness. Hepatic steatosis showed associations with BMI (B = 0.012; p < 0.001), total fat mass (TFM) (B = 0.007; p < 0.001), WHtR (B = 0.642; p < 0.001), WC (B = 0.005; p < 0.001), and VFT (B = 0.002; p = 0.014). None were associated with the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) score, and ROC analyses revealed sex-specific differences in association-based discrimination. For liver fibrosis (≥ F1), among females, VFT (AUC = 0.902; 95% CI: 0.787–1.000), WC (AUC = 0.891; 95% CI: 0.789–0.999), WHtR (AUC = 0.853; 95% CI: 0.732–0.973), and WHR (AUC = 0.839; 95% CI: 0.701–0.978) showed strong discrimination. In males, only WC demonstrated statistically significant discrimination (AUC = 0.718; 95% CI: 0.519–0.918). For moderate or greater steatosis (≥ S2), WC performed best in males (AUC = 0.818; 95% CI: 0.695–0.941), while TFM was most accurate in females (AUC = 0.751; 95% CI: 0.622–0.880).ConclusionsCentral adiposity indices were independently associated with increased liver stiffness, while both general and central adiposity measures were significantly related to hepatic steatosis, independent of confounders. Their discriminative performance showed distinct sex-specific patterns: for both fibrosis and steatosis, WC was the best-performing index in men, whereas VFT performed best for fibrosis in women and TFM was the best discriminator for steatosis in women.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Central adiposity, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, Adiposity indices, Two-dimensional shear wave elastography

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), has evolved into a global public-health challenge, affecting roughly one-third of the adult population, with its incidence steadily rising over recent decades [1, 2]. In 2023, an international expert consensus redefined NAFLD as MASLD to better reflect its underlying metabolic basis and to establish positive diagnostic criteria centered on the presence of hepatic steatosis in conjunction with metabolic dysfunction, including overweight/obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [3].

This condition is not merely a benign accumulation of adipose tissue; it can progress to more aggressive forms, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), which carries a significant risk of advancing to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. The strong epidemiological links between MASLD, metabolic syndrome, obesity, and insulin resistance highlight its systemic nature and broad health implications [4].

Obesity remains a major driver of MASLD progression [5] and is commonly defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) using body mass index (BMI): normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), and obesity (≥ 30.0 kg/m²), calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²) [6]. While BMI is widely used in clinical settings due to its simplicity and correlation with body fat percentage, it has notable limitations. It cannot differentiate between fat mass and lean mass and does not account for sex or ethnic differences in body composition, which can lead to potential misclassification [7].

Reflecting these limitations, the 2025 Lancet Commission redefined obesity as a chronic, systemic disease caused by excess adiposity that impairs tissue or organ function, distinguishing it from preclinical obesity, in which function remains intact despite elevated fat mass. It emphasized that BMI alone is insufficient for diagnosing individual health status and recommended confirmation with direct or anthropometric fat measures (e.g., waist circumference [WC], waist-to-hip ratio [WHR] or waist-to-height ratio [WHtR]) [8].

This limitation becomes particularly relevant in the context of liver health, as not all adipose tissue is metabolically equivalent. Clinical observations and research findings increasingly indicate that the distribution of body fat, particularly the accumulation of visceral adiposity, holds greater significance in the pathogenesis of metabolic diseases, including MASLD [9, 10].

The reliance on general adiposity measures like BMI for assessing liver health can be insufficient. While BMI may show an initial association with hepatic steatosis in preliminary analyses, this relationship often diminishes or becomes non-significant in more comprehensive multivariate models that account for visceral fat [10].

Consistent with recent consensus statements indicating that BMI is an imprecise marker of adiposity and should not be relied upon as a primary indicator of metabolic risk [11, 12], investigating precise adiposity indices in individuals with T2DM is crucial for understanding liver health. MASLD and T2DM have a bidirectional relationship driven by insulin resistance, lipotoxicity, and systemic inflammation. Patients with T2DM have a higher prevalence of MASLD and an increased risk of progression to advanced fibrosis [13].

Accordingly, the primary objective of the current study was to evaluate and compare the discriminative ability of various adiposity indices, including visceral fat thickness (VFT), visceral-to-subcutaneous fat ratio (V/S), and the visceral adiposity index (VAI), against conventional anthropometric measures (BMI, WC, WHR, and WHtR) and total fat mass (TFM) in determining liver disease severity among patients with T2DM.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted at a specialized diabetes clinic in Tehran, Iran. The research protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.TUMS.EMRI.REC.1402.090) and was performed in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent prior to their inclusion in the study.

Participants

Eligible male and female participants, aged 20 to 75 years, were included if they were diagnosed with T2DM as outlined by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) guideline. T2DM was defined by any of the following criteria: fasting plasma glucose (FPG) level of at least 126 mg/dL; hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level of at least 6.5%; 2-hour plasma glucose level of at least 200 mg/dL following a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test; or a random plasma glucose level of at least 200 mg/dL in the presence of symptoms of hyperglycemia. Participants with a documented prior clinical diagnosis of T2DM or those receiving glucose-lowering medications were also eligible for inclusion [14]. Exclusion criteria encompassed evidence of other chronic liver pathologies, specifically viral hepatitis (indicated by positive hepatitis B surface antigen [HBsAg] or hepatitis C virus [HCV] antibody), autoimmune hepatitis, hereditary liver conditions such as Wilson’s disease, or documented drug-induced liver injury based on medical records, drug history or self-reports. Furthermore, individuals presenting with active liver disease or significant alcohol consumption (exceeding 20 g/day for women and 30 g/day for men) were deemed ineligible via structured self-report questionnaire. Because thyroid dysfunction can influence liver function, thyrotropin (TSH) and free thyroxine (FT4) levels were assessed to exclude participants with overt thyroid dysfunction [15, 16]. Additional exclusions included a current diagnosis or history of cancer within the past five years. Participants were also ineligible if they had heart disease or impaired kidney function, defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 30 mL/min/1.73 m² or lower. These exclusion criteria were applied to reduce clinical heterogeneity and minimize confounding, as these comorbidities independently alter liver metabolism and could obscure the primary study outcomes. Lastly, women who were pregnant, breastfeeding, or planning pregnancy were also excluded.

Methodology for biochemical analysis

Blood samples were obtained following an overnight fasting period. Serum levels of FPG, alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), creatinine, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and triglycerides were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) on the Cobas c 311 system (Roche). Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) concentrations were calculated from the lipid panel using the Friedewald formula [17]. LDL-C was calculated only when triglycerides < 400 mg/dL and samples were fasting.

The platelet count was determined using a Sysmex KX-21 automated hematology analyzer. HbA1c was measured via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a Tosoh analyzer (Tosoh, Tokyo, Japan). Thyroid function tests, including TSH and FT4, were performed using ELISA; TSH measurements were conducted on the Cobas e 411 (Roche), while FT4 was tested using kits from Pishtaz Teb (Tehran, Iran).

The eGFR was determined using the chronic kidney disease epidemiology collaboration (CKD-EPI) Eq [18]. To calculate the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) score for liver fibrosis, we used the following formula: FIB-4 = (Age × AST) / (Platelets × √ALT) [19]. Per ADA Standards of Care, FIB-4 was used as the first-line noninvasive fibrosis tool for risk stratification in individuals with type 2 diabetes [20].

Methodology for clinical analysis

Liver steatosis and stiffness were assessed using two-dimensional shear wave elastography (2D-SWE) [21], performed by a single experienced radiologist to ensure consistency. Before the examination, participants fasted for at least six hours to minimize hepatic blood flow variability. Imaging was conducted with the SuperSonic MACH 30 ultrasound system and a C6-1X convex probe (frequency range: 1–6 MHz). With the patient in the supine position and the right arm elevated above the head, liver measurements were obtained through an intercostal space during a brief breath-hold. The 2D-SWE provided real-time quantitative measurements of liver stiffness, expressed in kilopascals (kPa) as a surrogate marker for fibrosis, and the ultrasound attenuation coefficient (ATT), measured in decibels per centimeter per megahertz (dB/cm/MHz), which correlates directly with hepatic fat content (steatosis). Using the same probe, subcutaneous fat thickness and VFT were also measured at the anterior midline of the abdomen at the level of the superior mesenteric artery origin [22]. V/S ratio was calculated as VFT divided by subcutaneous fat thickness measured at the same anatomical site.

Body composition, specifically TFM, was evaluated using a body composition analyzer (Jawon Medical Company, Korea) [23]. Participants prepared for the assessment by fasting for at least 4–5 h, avoiding strenuous physical activity for 12 h, and abstaining from caffeine for 24 h prior to the measurement.

Height was measured using a stadiometer, and body weight was recorded using a calibrated digital scale.

WC was measured at the midpoint between the lower margin of the last palpable rib and the superior border of the iliac crest, while hip circumference was measured at the widest portion of the buttocks, around the greater trochanters. The WHR was calculated by dividing WC by hip circumference, and the WHtR was derived as WC divided by height.

Blood pressure was manually assessed using a calibrated sphygmomanometer and stethoscope after a 5-minute seated rest, adhering to standardized procedures. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 80 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive therapy [24]. Physical activity levels were determined using the short-form International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), a validated instrument for estimating physical activity in epidemiological studies [25]. Participants reporting use of any tobacco product within the previous 30 days were categorized as current smokers.

We calculated the VAI using the sex-specific formulas originally proposed by Amato et al. [26].

Females: VAI = (WC / [36.58 + (1.89 × BMI)]) × (TG / 0.81) × (1.52 / HDL-C).

Males: VAI = (WC / [39.68 + (1.88 × BMI)]) × (TG / 1.03) × (1.31 / HDL-C).

Sample size calculation

Based on Hernández-Conde et al. [27], who reported that visceral fat accounted for 11.2% of the variance in liver fibrosis (R² = 0.112, P < 0.01) in a multivariable model, we conducted an a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1.9.4 software. For a linear multiple-regression model with 10 predictor variables, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and power of 0.80, the analysis indicated a required sample size of approximately 144 participants. To accommodate a potential 15% dropout rate, we increased the planned sample size to 164 participants.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS for Windows (version 24.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R 4.6.0, with statistical significance set at a p-value of less than 0.05. Group differences between sexes were analyzed for a range of parameters, including age, diabetes duration, anthropometric data, 2D-SWE results, metabolic biomarkers, and medication use. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous variables, while the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was applied to categorical variables.

To assess the association between adiposity and liver health, multivariable linear regression analyses were performed. The models evaluated the relationship between adiposity markers such as VFT, V/S, VAI, BMI, TFM, WC, WHtR, and WHR and key liver factors including liver stiffness, ATT, and FIB-4.The analysis was structured into two sequential models: model 1, adjusted for age and sex; and model 2, further adjusted for the variables in model 1 plus smoking status, physical activity, hypertension, diabetes duration, HbA1c, triglycerides, cholesterol, eGFR, and use of specific medications (pioglitazone and sodium glucose cotransporter 2 [SGLT2] inhibitors). Only SGLT2 inhibitors and pioglitazone were included as medication covariates, as these agents have well-documented and clinically meaningful effects on hepatic steatosis and fibrosis progression, whereas other antidiabetic medications primarily affect glycemic control without established direct effects on liver fibrosis.

To evaluate the ability of various anthropometric indices to discriminate between the presence or absence of liver fibrosis and steatosis, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed. The area under the curve (AUC), with 95% confidence interval (CI), was calculated to quantify discriminative ability. We compared ROC curves and their AUCs between adiposity markers using the paired DeLong nonparametric method to test differences in AUCs.

Youden’s index was used to determine the optimal cut-off value for each indicator, along with its corresponding sensitivity and specificity. In addition to these metrics, we have included negative predictive value (NPV), positive predictive value (PPV), number needed to diagnosis (NND), and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) in the tables.

We applied cut-off values of > 7.1 kPa for liver stiffness and > 0.6 dB/cm/MHz for ATT using 2D-SWE to detect the presence of any fibrosis (≥ F1) and moderate or greater hepatic steatosis (≥ S2), respectively [21, 28]. The FIB-4 score was also evaluated and categorized, with values > 2.67 indicating a high-risk group [29].

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 164 adults with T2DM were enrolled between July 4, 2022, and February 9, 2024, including 86 males and 78 females. Hepatic steatosis (≥ S2; ATT > 0.6 dB/cm/MHz) was identified in 27 participants (16.5%), while liver fibrosis defined by 2D-SWE (≥ F1; liver stiffness > 7.1 kPa) was found in 19 participants (11.6%). Based on FIB-4, 5 participants (3.0%) had scores > 2.67, consistent with a high risk of advanced fibrosis. Of the study population, 24 (14.6%) were normal weight, 89 (54.3%) were overweight, and 51 (31.1%) were obese.

The median age was comparable between sexes, with no statistically significant difference observed (males: 62.00 years, interquartile range [IQR]: 55.00–67.25 vs. females: 61.50 years, IQR: 55.75–66.00; p = 0.380). Smoking was significantly higher among males (24.40%) compared to females (6.40%; p = 0.002). No significant sex-based differences were identified in diabetes duration, physical activity levels, or prevalence of hypertension.

Males exhibited significantly higher liver stiffness (5.70 kPa [IQR: 5.00–6.52]) compared with females (4.80 kPa [IQR: 4.50–6.00]; p = 0.001), whereas females showed higher hepatic ATT values (0.56 dB/cm/MHz [IQR: 0.40–0.60] vs. 0.42 dB/cm/MHz [IQR: 0.36–0.55] in males; p = 0.001) suggesting a pattern of greater hepatic fibrosis risk in males but greater hepatic steatosis in females.

Marked differences in body composition were also evident. Males had significantly greater visceral adiposity, reflected by higher VFT with a median of 56.00 mm (IQR: 45.75–65.00) compared to 50.00 mm (IQR: 45.00–60.00) in females (p < 0.001) and V/S with a median of 3.66 (IQR: 2.99–4.51) compared to 2.78 (IQR: 2.26–2.30) in females (p < 0.001). In contrast, females showed higher overall adiposity indicators, including BMI (29.40 [IQR: 27.08–32.48] vs. 26.65 [IQR: 24.97–28.78] kg/m²; p < 0.001), TFM (27.55 [IQR: 23.32–31.97] vs. 18.50 [IQR: 15.40–23.40] kg; p < 0.001), and WHtR (0.64 [IQR: 0.59–0.68] vs. 0.59 [IQR: 0.56–0.62]; p < 0.001). The VAI was significantly higher in females, with a median of 7.35 (IQR: 4.66–10.95) compared to 4.04 (IQR: 2.84–6.12) in males (p < 0.001). WC was similar between sexes, but males had a higher WHR (0.98 [IQR: 0.95–1.04] vs. 0.95 [IQR: 0.90–0.99]; p < 0.001), indicating relatively greater central fat distribution.

In terms of metabolic profile, females demonstrated a less favorable lipid pattern, with significantly higher triglyceride and total cholesterol levels compared with males. Platelet counts were also higher among females. However, glycemic markers, liver enzymes, eGFR and thyroid tests did not differ significantly between sexes.

There were no significant sex-based differences in the use of antidiabetic medications, including metformin, insulin, sulfonylureas/glinides, SGLT2 inhibitors, pioglitazone, or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP4) inhibitors. However, the use of antihypertensive drugs was significantly higher among females (70.50% vs. 52.30%; p = 0.017). Full data are presented in Table 1.

Measures of central adiposity exhibited the strongest and most statistically significant associations with fibrosis in patients with T2DM. Specifically, VFT (B = 0.023, SE = 0.009; p = 0.012) and WC (B = 0.049, SE = 0.014; p < 0.001) were associated with higher liver stiffness. Additionally, WHtR (B = 6.532, SE = 2.294; p = 0.005) and WHR (B = 5.155, SE = 2.444; p = 0.037) also showed significant positive associations.

In contrast, ATT, a validated quantitative measure of hepatic steatosis, showed significant associations with a broad range of adiposity indicators, highlighting the impact of both overall and central fat accumulation on liver fat content. The strongest associations were with BMI (B = 0.012, SE = 0.003; p < 0.001), TFM (B = 0.007, SE = 0.002; p < 0.001), WC (B = 0.005, SE = 0.001; p < 0.001), and WHtR (B = 0.642, SE = 0.173; p < 0.001). Importantly, VFT, a direct measure of visceral adiposity, was also independently linked to ATT (B = 0.002, SE = 0.001; p = 0.014).

In the fully adjusted model, none of the anthropometric or body composition variables were significantly associated with the FIB-4 score.

Among female participants (n = 78), fibrosis was observed in 5 (6.4%) and steatosis in 18 (23.1%), whereas among male participants (n = 86), fibrosis was present in 14 (16.3%) and steatosis in 9 (10.5%).

In males, WC was the only significant predictor of liver fibrosis, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.718 (95% CI: 0.519–0.918; p = 0.032). At the optimal cut-off value of 112.50 cm, WC offered a sensitivity of 50.00% and a specificity of 91.20% (Fig. 1A).

In women, VFT emerged as the strongest non-invasive predictor of liver fibrosis, achieving exceptional discriminative performance (AUC = 0.902, 95% CI: 0.787–1.000; p = < 0.001). WC (AUC = 0.891, 95% CI: 0.789–0.999; p < 0.001), WHtR (AUC = 0.853, 95% CI: 0.732–0.973; p < 0.001), and WHR (AUC = 0.839, 95% CI: 0.701–0.978; p < 0.001) also demonstrated strong discrimination. At their respective optimal cut-off values, 59.00 mm for VFT, 107.00 cm for WC, 0.67 for WHtR, and 0.98 for WHR, all four measures achieved a sensitivity of 100%, with specificities ranging from 71% to 75%. (Fig. 1B). These near-perfect sensitivity values, however, likely reflect the limited number of fibrosis cases in sex-specific strata and should be interpreted cautiously.

In male patients, WC demonstrated the highest utility for liver steatosis, with an AUC of 0.818 (95% CI: 0.695–0.941; p < 0.001). Other measures showing good and statistically significant discriminative power included BMI (AUC = 0.765; p < 0.001), TFM (AUC = 0.761; p = 0.005), VFT (AUC = 0.737; p = 0.001), and WHtR (AUC = 0.722; p = 0.010). In females, TFM was the most discriminator of steatosis, with an AUC of 0.751 (95% CI: 0.622–0.880; p < 0.001), followed by BMI (AUC = 0.718; p = 0.002), VFT (AUC = 0.696; p = 0.015) and WC (AUC = 00.657; p = 0.031) (Fig. 2A and B).

At their respective optimal cut-off values in male patients with T2DM, the discriminative ability for steatosis was as follows: VFT 57.50 mm (sensitivity 81.80%, specificity 57.10%), BMI 27.40 kg/m² (sensitivity 81.80%, specificity 62.50%), TFM 24.75 kg (sensitivity 63.60%, specificity 87.50%), WC 109.50 cm (sensitivity 72.70%, specificity 83.90%), and WHtR 0.615 (sensitivity 81.80%, specificity 69.60%). In females, discriminative performance for detecting liver steatosis at their respective optimal cut-off values was as follows: VFT 51.50 mm (sensitivity 76.50%, specificity 55.80%), BMI 30.21 kg/m² (sensitivity 70.60%, specificity 69.80%), WC 95.50 cm (sensitivity 100.00% and specificity 37.20%) and TFM 28.30 kg (sensitivity 76.50%, specificity 67.40%) (Fig. 2A and B).

FIB-4 was excluded from the ROC analyses because it was not significantly associated with any adiposity index in either Model 1 or Model 2.

ROC comparison of adiposity indices

Among men, the discriminative performance of the evaluated adiposity indices for liver fibrosis did not differ significantly on pairwise ROC comparison, suggesting comparable diagnostic accuracy across markers. This contrasts with women, VFT differed significantly from both V/S (p < 0.001) and BMI (p = 0.014). V/S also showed significant performance differences compared to WC (p = 0.005), WHtR (p = 0.004), and WHR (p = 0.039).

In women, the discriminative performance of the evaluated markers for liver steatosis was largely comparable. However, the WHtR showed significantly different performance compared to BMI (p = 0.026), TFM (p = 0.009), and WC (p = 0.033). In men, more extensive differences were observed. The VAI performed significantly differently than VFT (p = 0.041), TFM (p = 0.040), and WC (p = 0.001). Additionally, the V/S showed significant performance differences compared to BMI (p = 0.038) and WC (p = 0.017). WC also showed significant differences when compared with WHtR (p = 0.049) and the WHR (p = 0.015).

Discussion

Central fat measures, including VFT, WC, WHtR, and WHR, are strongly linked to increased liver fibrosis in T2DM patients. This highlights the critical role of central and visceral fat accumulation in worsening liver fibrosis. Additionally, ATT is strongly associated with both overall and central adiposity, indicating that increased body fat, especially visceral fat, significantly contributes to liver fat accumulation. In fully adjusted analyses, however, none of the anthropometric or body composition measures, including VFT, BMI, or WC, were significantly associated with the FIB-4 score. Overall, among the body composition measures, WC and VFT emerged as the strongest discriminator of liver steatosis and fibrosis.

The analysis also reveals sex differences in the discriminative performance of anthropometric measures for liver fibrosis. In men, WC was the only measure showing a significant association, with moderate discrimination and sensitivity, whereas other measures showed no meaningful utility. In contrast, women exhibited much stronger associations: VFT was the most accurate discriminator, followed closely by WC, WHtR, and WHR. Regarding liver steatosis, in males, WC demonstrated the highest discrimination, followed by BMI, TFM, VFT, and WHtR, all showing statistically significant associations. Conversely, in females, TFM was the most accurate discriminator, while BMI, VFT and WC also showed appreciable discriminative performance.

Studies consistently demonstrate a significant association between visceral fat area and hepatic steatosis, regardless of gender or overall obesity status [10, 30]. This suggests that even individuals who appear metabolically healthy with lower BMIs (below 23 kg/m²) may be at risk due to excessive visceral fat accumulation [10]. Interestingly, the mediating effect of visceral fat area on the relationship between overall obesity (BMI) and hepatic steatosis is considerably stronger in females than in males, despite males often having higher average BMI and visceral fat area levels. This underscores an important sex-specific difference in how visceral fat contributes to liver fat accumulation and explains why the WC cut-off for abdominal obesity is lower in females than in males [31].

Beyond the initial onset of steatosis, visceral fat also significantly drives the progression to MASH and liver fibrosis [32], which are key indicators of severe liver disease. Our study also shows that visceral fat emerges as a critical, independent discriminator of liver fibrosis, overriding traditional obesity metrics like BMI. In addition, findings demonstrating a J-shaped relationship between BMI and elevated liver stiffness [33, 34], where individuals with lower BMIs (specifically below an inflection point of 23.05 kg/m2) still show an increased risk of liver fibrosis [34]. In a large CT-based study of 324 biopsy-proven MAFLD patients, Yu et al. (2015) demonstrated that each 10 cm² increase in visceral fat area independently increased the odds of significant fibrosis by 21% (OR = 1.21) and MASH by 17% (OR = 1.17) [32], while van der Poorten et al. (2008), in a smaller MRI-based study, found that each 1% rise in visceral fat was linked to an OR of 2.4 for inflammation and an OR of 3.5 for fibrosis, even after adjusting for insulin resistance and steatosis [35]. This progressive trend of increasing steatosis and liver stiffness with rising visceral fat is observed in both diabetic and non-diabetic populations [35, 36]. Collectively, these findings emphasize the need for broader screening strategies for MAFLD that consider visceral fat distribution rather than solely relying on BMI.

Consistent with these findings, multiple longitudinal cohort studies demonstrate that markers of central obesity such WHR and WHtR are significantly superior to BMI in predicting severe liver disease, MAFLD, and liver-related mortality [9, 37–40]. Notably, this predictive value of WHR remained consistent across different BMI categories, with absolute liver related outcomes (LRO) risks showing a stronger correlation with WHR than with BMI. Moreover, WHR demonstrated a significant synergistic interaction with harmful alcohol consumption in predicting LROs, an effect not observed with BMI [38]. Similarly, WHtR serves as a stronger predictor, especially in non-obese individuals (BMI < 24 kg/m²), and generally outperforms BMI in disease prediction [40].

In T2DM, insulin resistance in visceral adipose tissue impairs insulin’s ability to suppress lipolysis, leading to the excessive release of free fatty acids (FFAs). These FFAs accumulate in the liver, causing hepatic steatosis and lipotoxicity, which contribute to the progression of liver disease [41]. Visceral adipose tissue also creates a pro-inflammatory environment, characterized by reduced adiponectin levels and elevated concentrations of leptin, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6). This inflammatory milieu promotes the transition from steatosis to MASH and drives fibrogenesis. Together, these mechanisms underscore the strong discriminative ability of visceral obesity measures, such as WC and VFT, for both hepatic steatosis and fibrosis [35].

Our finding that the VAI did not independently associate with liver stiffness (a proxy for fibrosis) or hepatic steatosis (measured by ATT) contributes to a conflicting body of evidence. Although some studies suggest that VAI can accurately predict MAFLD and liver fibrosis [42–45], several investigations align with our results and indicate that it does not outperform conventional anthropometric indices such as BMI and waist circumference [46, 47]. Furthermore, it showed no association with the severity of liver fibrosis and failed to discriminate between histological stages in biopsy-proven MAFLD [47, 48].

Because VAI relies on lipid profiles (triglycerides and HDL-C), it may better reflect cardiovascular risk, due to the role of dyslipidemia in atherogenesis, rather than liver-specific risk [47, 49]. Liver fibrosis, however, is primarily a wound-healing response driven by chronic hepatic injury, oxidative stress, and inflammation [50], where visceral fat volume and metabolic activity exert a more direct influence [46]. Additionally, an important consideration in VAI accuracy is the study population, specifically patients with T2DM who have advanced metabolic dysregulation. In this group, the lipid components of the VAI are significantly affected by both the disease itself and common medications like statins and fibrates. This can reduce VAI’s accuracy in reflecting visceral fat or predicting liver fibrosis [46]. In contrast, anthropometric measures such as VFT and WC remain reliable indicators of central adiposity, as they are less influenced by such treatments, maintaining their clinical value for risk assessment.

In individuals with T2DM, adiposity measures were strongly associated with elastography-derived liver stiffness and steatosis, but not with the FIB-4 score. Since FIB-4 is primarily designed to detect advanced fibrosis [51], this lack of association may be explained by the low prevalence of advanced fibrosis in the study population (only 3.0% had FIB-4 > 2.67), as well as its moderate discriminative ability in patients with diabetes [52].

For VFT, we identified cut points of 5.75 cm for males and 5.15 cm for females as predictive of moderate or greater steatosis, along with a 5.90 cm threshold for liver fibrosis in females with T2DM. Prior studies report markedly lower thresholds in obese adolescents (4.47 cm for girls and 4.21 cm for boys) [53], human immunodeficiency viruses (HIV)-infected men (> 3.20 cm) [54], and higher values in general adult population (≥ 7–8 cm for mild and ≥ 9–10 cm for moderate-to-severe steatosis) [55]. Another ultrasonography-based study involving 352 subjects found that mean VFT increased progressively with liver steatosis severity, measuring 3.53 cm in individuals with normal liver, 4.29 cm in Grade 1, 5.04 cm in Grade 2, and 5.78 cm in Grade 3 steatosis. The authors concluded that VFT may serve as an adjunct parameter for grading liver steatosis [56].

The WC thresholds identified in our study (ranging from 95.50 to 112.50 cm) surpassed both the international high-risk criteria, such as the Adult Treatment Panel III (ATP III) thresholds of ≥ 102 cm for men and ≥ 88 cm for women [57], and the regional standard established by Iran’s national guidelines of ≥ 95 cm for both sexes [58]. This finding is consistent with research demonstrating significant variability in WC cut-offs across different populations and metabolic conditions. For example, in Iranian adults with MAFLD, optimal WC thresholds were 102.25 cm for men and 101.45 cm for women [59], which is similar to the 101.5 cm reported in white obese adolescents with MAFLD [60]. Lower thresholds have been observed in South Asians (Indian) (89 cm for men and 84 cm for women) [61], and even lower in East Asians, with Korean men and women having thresholds of 81 cm and 78.5 cm, respectively [62], and Chinese adults at 82.5 cm [63]. These disparities illustrate how VFT and WC thresholds are influenced by both population characteristics such as age group, sex, and disease status, as well as the measurement method employed (ultrasound vs. computed tomography [CT] vs. dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry [DXA]). Together, these findings underscore the importance of establishing T2DM-specific, sex-stratified cut-offs for accurate clinical risk stratification.

Finally, clinical practice should move beyond relying solely on BMI to include visceral fat assessment, using simple and cost-effective tools such as WC for evaluating liver health. This approach enables earlier detection and timely intervention. Recognizing that not all fat is metabolically equivalent calls for a precision medicine approach to obesity management for liver health, emphasizing targeted interventions such as tailored exercise programs and dietary modifications aimed specifically at reducing visceral fat. This focus is particularly important for women, given the sex-specific differences in the metabolic effects of visceral fat.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. It specifically targets patients with T2DM, a population for whom effective liver disease screening is a clinical priority. The comparison of multiple simple and accessible anthropometric indices offers valuable insights for clinicians. Moreover, the use of quantitative noninvasive markers for detecting steatosis and fibrosis improves the accuracy of liver assessment.

However, the findings should be interpreted in light of certain limitations. First, the cross-sectional design of this study limits our ability to establish causality, precluding any causal inferences regarding the relationships between visceral adiposity and liver parameters. Second, there is a potential for measurement coupling bias; because visceral fat thickness, ATT, and liver stiffness were all assessed simultaneously during the same ultrasound examination, this methodological proximity may artificially strengthen the observed associations and should be interpreted with caution. Third, the study relied on ultrasound-based and other noninvasive markers without histological confirmation. Although these methods are validated and widely used, they do not fully substitute for liver biopsy, the gold standard for staging fibrosis and grading steatosis [64].

Additionally, as a single-center study, our results may be influenced by population-specific factors. The relatively older age of our study also limits generalizability to younger T2DM patients and may inherently inflate FIB-4 scores, potentially overestimating advanced liver fibrosis. Exclusion of participants with advanced CKD and established cardiovascular disease may limit generalizability to higher-risk populations. Moreover, the absence of external validation further restricts the clinical applicability of the proposed diagnostic thresholds, which require confirmation in larger, multicenter cohorts. Moreover, the lack of multivariable diagnostic modeling may have limited the robustness of the results.

Although sex-related differences were observed in the ROC analyses, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The study did not include sex-stratified regression models, and the modest sample size may have limited the statistical power to perform such analyses. Therefore, the observed differences between men and women should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of sex-specific effects.

Conclusion

These findings highlight the central role of visceral and central adiposity in liver steatosis and fibrosis among individuals with T2DM. WC and VFT were the strongest overall predictors, though their discriminative ability differed by sex. In women, VFT, WC, WHtR, and WHR were most informative for fibrosis, whereas in men, only WC showed moderate association. For steatosis, WC was most accurate in men, while TFM was the strongest discriminator in women. Notably, no adiposity measure was associated with the FIB-4 score after full adjustment, underscoring limitations of this index in older diabetic populations. Future research should evaluate these sex-specific patterns in larger, diverse cohorts and explore whether tailored anthropometric cutoffs can improve non-invasive liver disease screening in T2DM.
