Dual roles of the DHX9 RGG domain in hybrid-dependent recruitment and suppression of 53BP1
Authors: Yuina Tsuchiya, Yudai Hiwatashi, Hayaki Ikegame, Saaya Matsuya, Yurina Abe, Ryotaro Nishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) are repaired mainly by non-homologous end-joining (NHEJ) and homologous recombination repair (HR) in mammalian cells. Recent studies highlight the importance of RNA in regulating these pathways and influencing pathway choice, particularly through the formation and resolution of DNA-RNA hybrids. Here, we show that DNA-RNA hybrids are formed at DSB sites in a PARP1- and poly (ADP-ribosyl)ation-dependent manner and are resolved by DHX9 dependent manner. The carboxyl-terminal RGG domain of DHX9 is necessary and sufficient for its recruitment and is required for efficient DNA end-resection. We further identify methylated arginine residues within this domain that are required for suppressing loading of the NHEJ-promoting factor 53BP1 to the DSB sites. Our findings highlight a central role of the RGG domain of DHX9 in DNA-RNA hybrid regulation and DSB repair pathway choice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-59280-6.

Introduction

DNA insults that are generated by endogenous and exogenous factors interfere normal cellular activities by disturbing DNA replication, transcription and chromosome segregation. To cope with DNA insults, livings have acquired DNA repair mechanisms that are specialized for various DNA lesions1–3. Repair of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) that are generated by the collapse of DNA replication fork, exposure to ionizing radiation and anti-cancer drugs is paramount important, since defective repair of DSBs are known to be causative for some human hereditary diseases that are manifested with a high predisposition to cancer4. In mammalian cells, DSBs are mainly repaired either by non-homologous end-joining (NHEJ) or homologous recombination repair (HR) that have distinct characters5–7. In view of cell cycle, NHEJ functions throughout cell cycle except M phase, whereas HR occurs in S and G2 phases due to its requirement of sister chromatid. These two pathways are also different in the accuracy of the repair. In NHEJ, two DNA ends are directly ligated with minor processing, which results in insertion or deletion of a few bases in some cases. Contrary, in the process of HR, homologous DNA sequence is copied from sister chromatid, which makes HR basically error-free. Although NHEJ is the primary DSB repair mechanism even in the S and G2 phases8, the choice of DSB repair mechanism is critical for maintaining genome stability7,9.

Canonically, the choice of DSB repair is understood by the competitive recruitment of 53BP1 or BRCA1 and their accompanying factors to chromatin around DSBs. While 53BP1 protects DNA ends from degradation10,11, BRCA1 promotes DNA-end resection that generates 3’ overhang single-strand DNA, which is critical for initiating HR12–15. The key events that regulate recruitment of 53BP1 and BRCA1 are histone modifications. DSB provokes signal cascade by the formation of γH2AX which is a phosphorylated form of H2AX at S139 and functions as a platform for downstream factors16,17; RNF168, a ubiquitin E3 ligase, is recruited to DSB sites via γH2AX-MDC1-RNF8 axis and conjugates ubiquitin to various substrates including histone H2A/X on K13/1518–21. Ubiquitylated H2A/X is recognized by both 53BP1, and BARD1 that forms complex with BRCA122,23. RNF168 also ubiquitylates and promotes proteasomal degradation of KDM4A/JMJD2 that binds to histone H4K20me224. Whereas H4K20me2 are recognized by Tudor domain of 53BP1, BARD1 binds to unmethylated-H4K20 that increases during DNA replication, which makes cell cycle-specific BRCA1-BARD1 complex binding to DSB sites22. In addition, RNF8-mediated removal of L3MBTL1 that also binds to H4K20me2 contributes to expose H4K20me225.

Recently, a large repertoire of RNA molecules such as microRNA (miRNA), long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) and damage-induced lncRNA (dilncRNA) have been implicated not only in DSB repair but also the choice of DSB repair pathway directly or indirectly26. In some cases, RNA molecules bind to genomic DNA and forms DNA-RNA hybrid structure, however how DNA-RNA structure is formed depends on the context. Whereas dilncRNAs transcribed near DSB sites by RNA polymerase II are processed by Dicer and functions to promote recruitment of DSB repair factors including MDC1 and 53BP127–31, RNA polymerase III also generates DNA-RNA hybrid32. In addition, DSB-induced inhibition of transcription results in DNA-RNA hybrid formation33, indicating that revealing the molecular mechanism underlying DNA-RNA hybrid formation is crucial to understand DSB repair. The formed DNA-RNA hybrid affects both positively and negatively on the recruitment of DSB repair factors, especially ones involved in DNA-end resection30,34–40. The thing is, temporary formed these structures containing DNA-RNA hybrids should be removed properly, otherwise residual DNA-RNA hybrid is known to cause genome instability41. Thus far, several helicases are reported to remove or resolve DNA-RNA hybrid formed around DSB in maintaining the genome integrity36,40,42. Previously, we also found that a DNA-RNA helicase ,DHX9, promotes HR by facilitating BRCA1 recruitment to DSB sites36, however it has remained unclear whether DHX9 recognizes and resolves DNA-RNA hybrid at DSB sites, how these hybrids are generated, and by what mechanism DHX9 exactly promotes the recruitment of BRCA1. In this study, we have revealed the molecular mechanism of DHX9 recruitment to DSB sites and the importance of arginine methylation in the carboxy-terminal RGG motif of DHX9 in antagonizing 53BP1 recruitment to DSB sites.

Results

DNA-RNA hybrids formed at DSB sites were resolved in DHX9 dependent manner

Although we recently reported the recruitment of DHX9 to DSB sites43, it remains unclear whether this process requires DNA-RNA hybrids and whether DHX9 resolves them. To address this, cells stably expressing GFP-fused DHX9 (GFP-DHX9) were transfected with a plasmid encoding mCherry-fused RNaseH1(mCherry-RNaseH1) and subjected to local induction of DSBs by 405 nm laser micro-irradiation (Fig. 1a and b, Figs. S1 and S2). As described in our previous report, GFP-DHX9 accumulated at laser-irradiated sites within 2 min, and this accumulation was significantly reduced by RNaseH1 expression, suggesting that DHX9 recruitment to DSB sites was dependent on DNA-RNA hybrids. To assess the role of DHX9 in resolving DSB-induced DNA-RNA hybrid, we attempted to generate a DHX9 knockout cell line using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. We obtained a heterozygous DHX9 knockout cell line (DHX9 het KO), in which both mRNA and protein levels were reduced to approximately half of those in parental U2OS cells (Fig. S3a and b). Because DHX9 knockout caused embryonic lethality in mice44, this may explain why only a heterozygous, rather than a homozygous, DHX9 knockout cell line was obtained. This DHX9 het KO cell line recapitulated the phenotypes observed upon DHX9 knockdown36, showing reduced phosphorylation of RPA2 at S4/S8 and 5-bromo-2'-deoxyuridine (BrdU) foci formation after camptothecin (CPT) treatment without significant effects on cell proliferation or cell-cycle profiles (Fig. S3c–f). We next investigated the accumulation of GFP-fused hybrid binding domain of RNaseH1 (GFP-HBD), which specifically binds to DNA-RNA hybrids, at laser-induced DSB sites in the S phase cells. GFP-HBD accumulated at DSB sites within 2–3 min after laser-irradiation in U2OS cells. The RGG domain of DHX9 was necessary and sufficient for its DNA-RNA hybrid-dependent recruitment to DSB sites

To identify the domain required for DHX9 recruitment to DSB sites, a series of GFP-DHX9 deletion mutants were examined for their localization to DSB sites (Fig. 2a–c). Deletion of the N-terminal double-strand RNA binding domains (ΔRBD1 and ΔRBD2) resulted in faster and stronger accumulation than the wild type (WT). By contrast, the GFP signal of OB-fold deletion mutant (ΔOB-fold) at the laser micro-irradiation site was reduced compared to undamaged sample. In contrast to these mutants, the RGG domain deleted mutant (ΔRGG) failed to accumulate at the irradiated sites, whereas the mutant containing only the RGG domain (RGG) accumulated at the damaged sites (Fig. 2b and c). In addition, accumulation of the RGG domain at irradiated sites was significantly suppressed by RNaseH1 expression (Fig. 2d and e). DHX9 recruitment to DSB sites was dependent on PARylation catalyzed by PARP1

Although we revealed that DHX9 recruitment to DSB sites depends on DNA-RNA hybrid formation, the involvement of other DSB repair factors in this process has not been investigated. Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1), which is recruited to laser-induced DNA lesions within seconds, regulates the recruitment of various DSB repair proteins including MRE11, NBS1 and BRCA145,46. In addition, poly (ADP-ribosyl)ation (PARylation) of BRCA1 is required for fine-tuning of HR47. These findings prompted us to investigate the involvement of PARPs and PARylation in DHX9 recruitment to DSB sites. First, we examined the effects of a PARP inhibitor (Olaparib) on DHX9 recruitment (Fig. 3a and b). GFP-DHX9 accumulation at irradiated sites was markedly reduced upon Olaparib treatment, suggesting that PARylation at DSB sites is required for the recruitment of DHX9. Among PARP family members, PARP1, PARP2 and PARP3 have been implicated in the DSB response, and PARP1 accounts for the majority of PAR synthesis in mammalian cells48–53. We therefore examined the GFP-DHX9 dynamics in PARP1-depleted cells (Fig. 3c and d, Fig. S4). GFP-DHX9 recruitment to DSB sites was significantly reduced in PARP1 depleted cells, suggesting that PARP1 and/or PARP1-dependent PARylation is required for recruiting DHX9 to DSB sites. Because the interaction between PARP1 and DHX9 had been reported previously54,55, we investigated the effect of Olaparib on this interaction and on PARP1 recruitment to DSB sites. The PARP1-DHX9 interaction was reduced but still detectable in the presence of Olaparib (Fig. 3e). Notably, the DHX9 mutant lacking the RGG domain also interacted with PARP1 (Fig. 3f and g). Furthermore, PARP1-GFP accumulated efficiently at DSB sites even in the presence of Olaparib (Fig. 3h and i), consistent with a recent report showing prolonged retention of PARP1 at DSB induced foci upon Olaparib treatment56. DNA-RNA hybrids formed in a manner dependent on PARylation were required for recruiting the RGG domain of DHX9

Since DHX9 recruitment to DSB sites depends on PARylation (Fig. 3), we next examined whether DNA-RNA hybrid formation is also PARylation-dependent. Accumulation of GFP-HBD at laser-irradiated sites was markedly reduced in PARP1-depleted cells (Fig. 4a and b). Similarly, Olaparib treatment also significantly decreased GFP-HBD recruitment to DSB sites (Fig. 4c and d). We also investigated whether accumulation of the RGG domain at DSB sites requires PARylation. RGG domain accumulation at DSB sites was significantly suppressed upon Olaparib treatment (Fig. 4e and f). Finally, we tested the importance of RGG domain in DNA-end resection. As described above, DHX9 het KO cells showed reduced phosphorylation of RPA2 at S4/S8 upon CPT treatment (Figs. S3c and 4g). This defect was rescued by expression of WT DHX9, but not ΔRGG mutants (Fig. 4g). Together, these results suggest that PARylation catalyzed by PARP1 is required for DNA-RNA hybrid formation at DSB sites, which is required for recruiting the RGG domain of DHX9. Arginine methylation of the DHX9 RGG domain antagonized 53BP1 recruitment to DSB sites

Thus far, we have shown that DHX9 is required to resolve DNA-RNA hybrids at DSB sites and promotes DNA-end resection through its RGG domain. However, how the RGG domain mediates this function in DNA-end resection remains elusive. The RGG domain is a well-known target of arginine methylation, which can modulate protein–protein interactions57–59. In addition, because 53BP1 competes with the BRCA1-BARD1 complex for chromatin binding and inhibits DNA-end resection, we next examined whether arginine methylation of DHX9 affects 53BP1 accumulation to DSB sites. Immunopurified GFP-DHX9 was detected with an antibody against mono- and di-methyl arginine, whereas the ΔRGG mutant was not (Fig. 5a), suggesting that DHX9 is arginine methylated within its RGG domain. The RGG domain of DHX9 contains nine arginine residues arranged in RG or RGG motifs, which are potential methylation sites for protein arginine N-methyltransferases (PRMTs). To identify the methylated residues, we analyzed a series of DHX9 deletion mutants (Fig. 5b and c). Almost no arginine methylation signal was detected in truncated mutants lacking residues beyond amino acid 1203 (1–1203), whereas a weak signal was still observed in GFP-DHX9 (1–1241) (Fig. 5c). To further define the methylated arginine residues, we examined two GFP-DHX9 mutants (2RK and 4RK), in which R1174/R1175 and R1242/R1249/R1253/R1265 were substituted with lysine, respectively. While arginine methylation signals were reduced in both mutants compared with WT, GFP-DHX9 (4RK) showed a markedly reduced methylation signal, indicating that R1242/R1249/R1253/R1265 represent the major methylation sites (Fig. 5d). Since 53BP1 competes with BRCA1-BARD1 complex at DSB sites, we speculated that DHX9 suppresses 53BP1 recruitment to facilitate BRCA1 accumulation, and that arginine methylation plays important roles for this process. To test this, we examined the accumulation of mCherry-fused 53BP1 (mCherry-53BP1) at DSB sites upon overexpression of GFP-DHX9 (WT or 4RK) (Fig. 5e and f). Notably, overexpression of GFP-DHX9 WT antagonized 53BP1 accumulation at DSB sites, whereas GFP-DHX9 (4RK) did not. Because the DHX9 mutant lacking the RGG domain failed to accumulate at DSB sites (Fig. 2b and c), we next tested whether GFP-DHX9 (4RK) is recruited to DSB sites (Fig. 5g and h), and found that the 4RK mutant accumulated with efficiency similar to WT. Consistent with these results, GFP-DHX9 (ΔRGG) did not suppress 53BP1 accumulation at DSB sites (Fig. S5). We further investigated the interaction between DHX9 and 53BP1 (Fig. S6), indicating that, at lease under our experimental conditions, we did not observe the interaction between these proteins. Discussion

In this study, we have revealed the molecular mechanism by which DHX9 promotes HR. First, DHX9 is recruited to DSB sites at a relatively early stage (within 2 min after DSB induction) in a manner dependent on PARP1-mediated PARylation, indicating that DHX9 is directly involved in HR. Second, DHX9 is required for resolving DNA-RNA hybrids that are also formed in a PARP1 dependent manner upon DSB induction. Furthermore, the RGG domain of DHX9 is necessary and sufficient for its recruitment to DSB sites. Finally, we show that DHX9 antagonizes 53BP1 loading to DSB sites in a manner dependent on arginine methylation of the RGG domain. These results suggest that the RGG domain of DHX9 has two distinct functions during HR: DNA-RNA hybrid-dependent DHX9 recruitment and arginine methylation-mediated suppression of 53BP1 loading.

Although the formation of DNA-RNA hybrids or R-loops at DSB sites is a well-established phenomenon, how these structures are generated remains under debate. Several studies have suggested that RNA polymerase II recruited to DSB sites is responsible for de novo RNA synthesis and DNA-RNA hybrid formation30,31,37,60,61, whereas RNA polymerase III has also been implicated in this process32. In addition, recent work has shown that transcription shut down can induce DNA-RNA hybrid accumulation at DSB sites62. Although PARP1 was recruited to DSB sites extremely rapidly46 and was known to interact with DNA-RNA hybrids63, its involvement in DNA-RNA hybrid formation has not been investigated. In the present study, we show that DNA-RNA hybrids form within a few minutes after DSB induction in a PARP1-dependent PARylation-dependent manner. Therefore, how PARP1-mediated PARylation influences the localization and activity of RNA polymerases will be an important question for future investigation.

As discussed above, we have shown that PARylation-dependent DNA-RNA hybrid formation is crucial for DHX9 recruitment; however, this does not exclude additional mechanisms. PARylation induces liquid–liquid phase separation, which promotes condensation of DSB repair proteins at DSB sites64. In addition, RGG repeats facilitates liquid demixing of proteins65. Furthermore, several DSB repair or response proteins are recruited to damage sites through mechanisms involving their RGG domains and PARylation. In particular, the RGG domain of MRE11 interacts with PAR chain46. Moreover, although various post-translational modifications of DHX9 have been reported66–68, PARylation of DHX9 remains to be tested. Together, these observations suggest that DHX9 recruitment to DSB sites may involve multiple mechanisms.

The RGG domain is defined as a domain containing repeated RG or RGG motifs separated by 0–4 amino acid residues59. RGG domains of various proteins bind to diverse nucleic acid structures, including single-strand DNA69, single-strand RNA69, double-strand RNA70 and G-quadruplex71,72. Given that DHX9 is recruited to DSB sites depending on DNA-RNA hybrid formation (Figs. 1 and 4) and that the RGG domain of DHX9 is required for its recruitment (Fig. 2), these findings suggest that DHX9 RGG domain may recognize DNA-RNA hybrids at DSB sites, in addition to the above-mentioned nucleic acid structures. Although DHX9 preferentially unwind nucleic acid structures such as DNA-RNA hybrids and R-loops, its nucleic acid binding properties remain poorly understood, especially with respect to the RGG domain. Since the RGG domain of DHX9 binds more strongly to ssDNA than to ssRNA or dsRNA73, it will be important to determine whether it binds to the displaced ssDNA in R-loops or to DNA-RNA hybrids during DSB repair. Interestingly, deletion of the OB-fold abolished DHX9 recruitment to DSB sites (Fig. 2b and c). As the OB-fold is known to mediate nucleic acid binding, this finding suggests that OB-fold domain may contribute to DHX9 recruitment, potentially in concert with the RGG domain. Further in vitro studies will be required to determine the precise contribution of the OB-fold to this process. Among DNA repair proteins, RGG domains exhibit considerable diversity, and their functions are likely to vary depending on the context. For example, DDX5, which, like DHX9, possesses DNA–RNA helicase activity and has been implicated in DSB repair, also contains an RGG domain. However, this domain has been reported to be important not for recruitment to DSB sites, but rather for the exclusion of DDX5 from these sites40. These observations suggest that while the RGG domain itself plays an important role, its coordinated function with other domains, such as the OB-fold, may be critical for DSB repair.

We also found that DHX9 is arginine methylated within its RGG domain (R1242/R1249/R1253/R1265), and that this modification is important for antagonizing 53BP1 loading to DSB sites (Fig. 5). DHX9 has previously been reported to be methylated by PRMT1; however the proposed methylation sites that located upstream of residue 1221 and were involved in nuclear localisation of DHX974 differ from those identified in this study. Thus, our results may be a novel regulatory mechanism of DHX9 through arginine methylation. Although the underlying mechanism remains to be clarified, several possibilities can be considered. Arginine methylation is known to promote protein–protein interactions58. In particular, the interaction between DHX9 and TDRD3 is mediated by arginine methylation75, suggesting that arginine methylation may also facilitate the interaction between DHX9 and BRCA1. Identifying PRMTs that target DHX9 and elucidating how arginine methylation of DHX9 suppress 53BP1 loading will be important topics for future studies. Overall, our results highlight the importance of the RGG domain of DHX9 in DSB repair.

Together with our previous study36, these findings indicate that DHX9 plays a critical role in the choice of DSB repair mechanism, thereby favouring HR. Because DNA-RNA hybrids formation and DHX9 recruitment occur rapidly, which approximately 2 min after DSB induction, these events may regulate histone modifications that orchestrate 53BP1 and BRCA1 accumulation. It is also possible that DSBs which recruits DHX9 are pre-marked by hitherto unidentified mechanism to undergo HR. Investigating these possibilities may help clarify the key steps that determine the choice of DSB repair mechanism. In the current study, we primarily used laser micro-irradiation to induce DSBs. Compared with IR, this approach generates more spatially confined DSBs, which may influence the choice of DSB repair pathways. Further investigation using IR would help to extend the importance of DHX9 and its arginine methylation in DSB repair.

Methods

Cell culture and cell lines

All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Nacalai tesque) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, SIGMA-Aldrich, Funakoshi), 100 Units/ml penicillin (Nacalai tesque), 100 µg/ml streptomycin (Nacalai tesque) and in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Where indicated, cells were continuously treated with the inhibitor at the specified concentrations and for the designated durations.

U2OS cell was kindly provided by Sir Stephen P. Jackson (University of Cambridge, United Kingdom). DHX9 heterozygous knockout U2OS cells were generated by co-transfecting pCas-Guide (Origene) encoding Cas9 and gRNA (5′-GGGGTAGAATCTGATACCGA-3′), and pDonor-D09 (GeneCopoeia), containing homology arms and a puromycin resistant gene. To clone homology arms, following primers were used; Left Homology arm forward (5′-GGTTAATTAATAAATGAACAGCAGATTAGAAAATTTAG-3′), Left homology arm reverse (5′-GGGGTACCCCTATATAACAATAAAGAACAATCAGG-3′), Right homology arm forward (5′-GGTCTAGACCCTGCATTCTCAGATTCCTCGAGA-3′) and right homology arm reverse (5′-GGGTCGACAAGGTAGGATATTTGTCATGTGCTAAA-3′). After transfection of the plasmids, cells were cultured in the presence of 1 µg/ ml puromycin (Invivogen) for two weeks to establish a monoclonal cell line. Expr

ession of DHX9 was examined by RT-qPCR and immunoblotting. Monoclonal-stable cell lines expressing GFP-DHX9 were established by transfecting U2OS or DHX9 het KO with the pEGFP-C1 (Takara Bio) vector encoding DHX9 followed by selection with 1 µg/ml Geneticin (Nacalai tesque) for 2 weeks. DHX9 expression levels were verified by immunoblotting. A U2OS cell line stably expressing mCherry-53BP1 was established by transfecting pmCherry-C1-53BP1, followed by selection with 1 µg/ml Geneticin for 2 weeks76.

RT-qPCR

Total RNAs were extracted from cells cultured in 60 mm dishes using the RNA Basic Kit (Nippon Genetics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Genomic DNA and DNase were subsequently removed using RQ1 RNase-free DNase (Promega) according to the provided protocol. RNA concentration was measured with a NanoPhotometer (IMPLEN), and 1 µg of RNA was used as a template for reverse transcription. cDNA was synthesized using the SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis System for RT-PCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and qPCR reaction was performed with Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). DHX9 mRNA expression levels were normalized to the GAPDH mRNA expression. Primers used for qPCR: DHX9 forward 5′-CGGTATGGAGATGGTCCACG-3′; DHX9 reverse 5′-CGCTACCATAGCCTCCACTG-3′; GAPDH forward 5′-GTCAGCCGCATCTTCTTTTG-3′; GAPDH reverse 5′-GCGCCCAATACGACCAAATC-3′.

Transfection of plasmid DNA and siRNA

Plasmids were transfected to cells using 1 mg/ml polyethyleneimine “Max” (PEI-MAX, MW 40,000, Polyscience) as described below. PEI-MAX (2 volumes of used plasmid) was diluted in OPTI-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then incubated for 5 min at room temperature. The PEI-MAX containing media was mixed with OPTI-MEM supplemented with the plasmid, and was further incubated for 20 min at room temperature. Finally, the mixture was added to the cells cultured with DMEM supplemented only with 10% FBS. Cells in 35, 60 or 100 mm dish were transfected with 1, 4 or 12 µg of plasmid, respectively. Knockdown experiments were performed by transfecting siRNAs with HiperFect (Qiagen) at a final concentration of 40 nM. The siRNA was transfected 48 h before the experiment. The siRNA sequences used in this sutudy: sicontrol (siCtrl) 5′-AACGUACGCGGAAUACUUCGATT-3′; siPARP1 5′-CCAUCGAUGUCAACUAUGA-3′.

Laser micro-irradiation and live-cell imaging

U2OS cells were seeded into 35 mm glass bottom dishes (Matsunami) and transfected with plasmids on the next day. Forty-eight hours after transfection, cells were incubated with 10 µg/ml Hoechst33342 (Nacalai tesque) for 15 min. Where indicated, cells were treated with DMSO or Olaparib (1 µM, 1 h) prior to the incubation with Hoechst33342. For detecting DNA-RNA hybrids, U2OS or DHX9 het KO cells were transfected with GFP-HBD and synchronized to the S phase of the cell cycle. For investigating mCherry-53BP1 localization, U2OS cells stably expressing mCherry-53BP1 were transfected with the plasmid coding GFP-DHX9s, and 24 h after transfection, the cells were cultured with 10 μM 5-Bromo-2'-deoxyuridine (BrdU) for 24 h prior to laser micro-irradiation.

On the day of analysis, the medium was replaced with phenol Red free DMEM (Nacalai tesque) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 µg/ml streptomycin and 584 µg/ml L-glutamine before laser micro-irradiation. Cells were irradiated with 405 nm laser and images were taken every 30 s by the confocal microscope FV3000 (Olympus) equipped with heat stage (TOKAI HIT). Objective used with this microscope: Olympus UPLSAPO 60x, 1.35 numerical aperture. Magnification was 3 times. Pixel dimensions: 1024 × 1024. Image bit depth: 16. Quantification of GFP or mCherry signal was carried out with ImageJ or cellSens software.

Cell extract preparation, immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting (IB)

Cells were washed twice with ice-cold phosphate buffered saline (PBS, Nacalai tesque) and lysed in CSK buffer [10 mM PIPES (pH6.8), 3 mM MgCl2, 1 mM ethylene glycol bis (2-aminoethyl ether)-N, N, N’, N’-tetraacetic acid, 0.1% Triton X-100 and 300 mM sucrose] containing 300 mM NaCl, 1 × Protease Inhibitor cocktail ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA)-free (PI, Nacalai tesque), 10 mM NaF (Nacalai tesque), 20 mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM, Nacalai tesque) and 0.25 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, SIGMA-Aldrich) for 30 min on ice with occasional mixing. The soluble fraction was obtained by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Residual chromatin-bound fractions were washed twice, and then resuspended with the identical buffer, followed by sonication (UD-100, 40% output, 30 s, TOMY). Where indicated, cells were incubated with 1 µM Camptothecin for 1 h prior to the cell extract preparation. The protein concentrations of cell extracts were determined with Protein Assay CBB Solution (Nacalai tesque) with bovine serum albumin as a standard (Takara Bio). The soluble fraction was subjected to immunoprecipitation with an anti-GFP antibody coupled with magnetic agarose beads (GFP-Trap_MA, Proteintech) by rotating overnight at 4 °C. The beads were washed six times with the CSK buffer and bound proteins were eluted by boiling at 95 °C for 10 min with 1 × Laemmli buffer [62.5 mM Tris–HCl (pH6.8), 2% sodium dodecyl sulphate, 10% glycerol, 0.02% bromophenol blue and 6.25% β-mercaptoethanol]. Cell lysates and immunoprecipitated samples were analyzed by immunoblotting according to the Laemmli’s method.

Immunofluorescent staining (IF)

Cells were cultured with 30 µM BrdU for 24 h prior to the treatment with 1 µM camptothecin for 1 h. Cells were pre-extracted with pre-extraction buffer [10 mM PIPES (pH6.8), 3 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% Triton X-100, 300 mM sucrose, and 100 mM NaCl] for 10 min on ice, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were then permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min at room temperature. Samples were subsequently washed twice with 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS. After incubating cells with blocking buffer [5% FBS, 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS] for 30 min, cells were incubated sequentially with primary antibodies for 1 h and secondary antibodies for 30 min, both diluted in blocking buffer. Following nuclei staining with 1 µg/ml of 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) solution for 10 min, samples were sealed with VECTASHIELD (Vector Laboratories) and images were taken with confocal microscope FV3000. When Cyclin A was investigated, cells were fixed as described above without pre-extracton. Objective used with this microscope: Olympus UPLSAPO 60x, 1.35 numerical aperture. Magnification was 3 times. Pixel dimensions: 1024 × 1024. Image bit depth: 16.

Cell proliferation assay

Cells were seeded into 96 well plates (Greiner Bio-One) at a density of 2,000 cells/well. On the day of analysis, 10 µl of Cell Count Reagent SF (Nacalai tesque) was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad).

S phase synchronization by double-thymidine block

Cell cycle synchronization was carried out by double-thymidine block method. Cells were incubated with 2 mM thymidine (Nacalai tesque) for 16 h. Thymidine was removed by washing cells with PBS twice, and then cells were cultured for 10 h without thymidine. Cells were synchronized at G1/S boundary by incubating with 2 mM thymidine for 16 h. For S phase synchronization, after removal of thymidine cells were cultured for 3 h.

Cell cycle profiling

Cells were collected with appropriate volume of PBS and spin downed at 500 × g, for 5 min, followed by PBS removal. Then cells re-suspended with 200 µl of PBS were fixed with 5 ml of ice-cold 70% ethanol and kept at − 30 °C for overnight. After washing with 1% BSA/PBS twice, cells were incubated with a solution containing 50 µg/ml propidium iodide (Nacalai tesque), 0.1 mg/ml RNase A (MACHEREY–NAGEL) and 0.05% Triton X-100 for 30 min at 37 °C. Samples were analyzed by flow cytometer (Sony, SH800S).
