 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Given its chemical structure, polyethylene (PE) waste may be a viable feedstock for the
 production of linear fatty alcohols (FALCs), a major surfactant produced either from palm kernel
 oil, vegetable oils, or via synthetic pathways that are associated with high carbon footprint. The
 BOTTLE Consortium will collaborate with Procter & Gamble (P&G) to develop FALCs from
 waste PE feedstocks.

 Summary of Research Results:

 PHASE 1

 Task 1: Selective Carboxylation of Model Substrates

 Discussion: In Task 1, the BOTTLE Consortium will synthesize a series of Ni-catalysts with
 varying ligand structures to target C10-C18 carbons on model n-paraffin compounds. The
 carboxylation selectivity will be evaluated, and catalyst design optimized. A mid-project stage-
 gate meeting will be conducted in March 2023 to evaluate catalyst selectivity on controlled PE
 substrates. This will serve as a mid-point check and Task 1 will continue. Task 1 will be broken
 down into the following subtasks:

 Task 1.1 – Synthesize a series of Ni-catalysts with varying ligand structures to target
 C10-C18 carbons on model n-paraffin compounds (C20-C24 mixtures or larger).

 Task 1.2 – Evaluate carboxylation selectivity of catalysts synthesized in Task 1.1 and
 measure carboxylated C10-C18 yield on model substrates in CO2 using GC-MS.

 Task 1.3 – Test catalyst in pressurized oxygen on model substrates and characterize
 products (i.e., can we avoid carboxylation and impart selective COOH groups directly
 with oxidation and reduce steps?).

 Task 1.4 – (Stretch Target) In March of 2023, evaluate catalyst selectivity on control
 HDPE substrates.

 Contractor Deliverables:

 The BOTTLE team will provide to P&G:

 1. monthly updates (verbal discussions and written presentations) to the P&G team;
 2. a March 2023 stage/gate update on catalyst selectivity on control PE substrates (stretch
 target);
 3. a detailed report of catalyst design and carboxylation experiments to present at a stage
 gate meeting at 12 months; and
 4. at least 1 g of mono carboxylated C10 – C18 compounds from the best test procedure.

 2

 The BOTTLE team will target the following milestones:

 1. minimum yield of 80% mono-carboxylated C10-C18 compounds;
 2. at least 80% conversion of the model n-paraffin compounds used as a feedstock;
 3. relatively mild reaction conditions (T and P);
 4. relatively short time-on-stream (TOS); and
 5. reaction scale of at least 5 g of the model n-paraffin compounds.

 Participant Deliverables: P&G will provide analytical data/access when needed including mass

 Task 1 Results:

 The objectives for Phase 1 of this collaboration were to determine if it was possible to employ an
 oxidative approach to selectively convert polyethylene (PE) waste into primarily C12-C18
 diacids or asymmetric ester-acids that could be selectively reduced or converted to P&G
 surfactant products in as few steps as possible with the lowest environmental impact and costs. In
 Phase 1 (12 months), the following methods were explored:

 1. Selective placement of ketones on PE backbones with ~C10-C16 carbon spacing
 followed by conversion to ester groups via a Bayer-Villiger reaction and hydrolysis to
 ester-acid products for further reduction to monoalcohols for surfactant applications
           (Scheme 1).
 2. Oxidative deconstruction of PE into a distribution of dicarboxylic acids (DCAs) followed
 by separations to target diacid chain length for further reduction to monoalcohols for
 surfactant applications (Scheme 2).

 3

 Phase 1 was an iterative process with several pivots/adaptations based on feedback from the
 P&G team and key learnings. At the current stage-gate point, the primary
 accomplishments/findings from Phase 1 were:

 • Captured Intellectual Property (IP): Filed a provisional patent on conversion of PE to
 polyesters (prior to any relevant publications).
 • PE to Polyketones: Successfully converted PE to polyketones with greater than 5%
 conversion without a loss in molecular weight, thermal properties, or mechanical
 properties in non-chlorinated solvents (target conversion for surfactant length was 6%).
 • Polyketones to Polyesters: Demonstrated that it was possible to further convert
 polyketones to polyesters that were hydrolysable (and potentially biodegradable). This
 process still needs optimization and yield improvement. Once optimized, these polyesters
 could be hydrolyzed to ester-acids within the target C12-C18 chain length for
 downstream conversion to fatty alcohol surfactants. Converting PE to polyesters could
 also be a potential pathway to yield a more recyclable (i.e., depolymerizable) and/or
 biodegradable form of PE.
 • Oxidative Deconstruction of PE to DCAs: Demonstrated that DCA yields from PE
 oxidation could be tailored to favor C4-C6 DCAs for potential conversion to surfactants
 (verified by P&G team). Also demonstrated that DCA mixtures can be separated to target
 carbon lengths via low energy liquid-liquid extraction processes.
 • Technoeconomic Analysis (TEA) and Life Cycle Assessment (LCA): Preliminary
 TEA and LCA models showed that PE oxidation to upcyclable intermediates could be
 economical with minimal environmental impact.

 The team’s initial approach focused on incorporating ketone groups into low-density
 polyethylene (LDPE) and medium-density polyethylene (MDPE) via a benzaldehyde mediated
 oxidation platform in various chlorinated solvents (Scheme 3). The highest functionalization
 achieved was 2% (the target oxidation extent was 6%) and the use of chlorinated solvents was
 not ideal. When the oxidation extent yields were above 2% chain scission occurred. The
 substrates, conditions, and results of this first approach are displayed in Table 1.

 We also explored the oxidative deconstruction of high-density polyethylene (HDPE) to a
 distribution of DCAs originally targeting longer chain lengths in the C12-C18 DCA range. We
 found that switching from N-hydroxyphthalimide (NHPI)] to a sodium bromide (NaBr) initiator
 led to slower kinetics (i.e., lower overall DCA yields in the 1-hour screenings) and higher
 selectivity to C4-C6 DCA range (Figure 6A) which the P&G team confirmed could be converted
 to useful surfactants. Increasing the Cobalt catalyst loadings improved overall DCA yields with
 approximately 25% greater than C11 chain lengths (Figure 6B). This target range could
 potentially be separated via low energy solvent separations (Figure 7). In Phase 2 we planned to
 explore feeding back in the liquid fraction (i.e., yellow stripped bar in Figure 6A) of longer
 carbons (either functionalized PE or longer DCAs) back into the oxidation reaction for further
 oxidation to either the C12-C18 or C4-C6 target ranges.

 PHASE 2

 Task 2: Oxidation of PE to target DCAs for further conversion to fatty alcohols

 Discussion: The task 2 work plan will focus only on the DCA processes and targets the PE
 conversion to primarily: 1) C4 – C6 and 2) C12 – C16 DCAs for subsequent chemical
 transformations into both new and commercial surfactants by P&G (Figure 12):

 Figure 12. Task 2 work plan process flow.

 Task 2.1: Oxidation of PE to C4-C6 dicarboxylic acids (DCAs), separations, and process scale
 up Optimize the C4 – C6 DCA process conditions with target yield greater than about 50%;

 1. Establish the separation pathway to remove C4 - C6 DCAs from the reaction product
 mixture using preferably commercialized or new techniques (such as solvent extraction);
 2. Deliver 50 - 100 g of succinic (C4) and glutaric (C5) acids (in total) with > 90% purity
 from PE;
 3. Achieve a full carbon and mass balance to quantify all reaction products (i.e., quantify
 the 41 - 46% of products not shown in Figure 6 of Phase 1 final report); and
 4. Compare the TEA and LCA to current manufacturing processes for production of C4 and
 C5 DCAs from petroleum.

 Contractor deliverables: The BOTTLE team will provide at least 50 g of purified C4 and C5
 DCAs to P&G.

 Participant deliverables: P&G will attempt to convert the purified C4 and C5 DCAs received
 from NLR to new surfactants at P&G facilities.

 Task 2.2: Oxidation of PE to C12-C16 DCAs

 1. Optimize the C12 – C16 DCA process conditions and benchmark maximum possible
 yields using mid-century oxidation process on PE;
 2. Establish the separation pathway to remove C12 – C16 DCAs from the reaction product
 mixture using preferably commercialized or new techniques (such as solvent extraction);
 3. Deliver 50 – 100 g of C12 0 C 16 SDCAs from the reaction product mixture using
 preferrabley commercialized or new techniques (such as solvent extraction);
 4. Achieve a full carbon and mass balance to quantify all reaction products;
 5. Deliver the TEA and LCA models of scaled-up C12 – C16 production process.

 Contractor deliverables: Provide a written report on the C12-C16 benchmarking studies along
 with quantified reaction products and an experimentally validated separations strategy.

 Participant deliverables: P&G will attempt to convert the purified C12 – C16 DCAs received
 from NLR to commercial surfactants at P&G facilities.

 Task 2.3: PE oxidation process studies - In addition, P&G would like to see the following studies
 on both C4 – C6 and C12 – C16 process studies performed to ensure process scalability:

 1. Impact of O2/inert ratio and pressure on product yields, and the replacement of O2 with
 air;
 2. Impact of increasing PE feed concentration on product yields (e.g., 700 mg PE feed);
 3. Impact of reduced Co(OAc)2 and Mn(OAc)2 catalyst concentration (i.e., is 9.5 wt%
 Mn(OAc)2 really needed) on product yields, and the impact of Co/Mn ratio on product
 yields;
 4. Impact of AcOH concentration on product yields; and
 5. Recycle study of the unreacted C11 – C22 product fraction (i.e., will it further react if
 subjected to a second round of the reaction conditions?).

 Contractor deliverables: A final report on in depth process analysis with optimized conditions
 reported.

 Participant deliverables: None.

 Task 2 Results:

 The team was unable to achieve the target yields for C12–C14 DCAs; however, a downstream
 separations process was identified via either liquid–liquid extraction (see patent application) or
 counter-current chromatography (CCC). Based on yield and carbon balance, the Phase 2 focus
 shifted to C4–C6 DCAs. The process was optimized to maximize C4–C6 DCA yields, and a
 downstream separations process using CCC was established. Scale-up to a 1-L reactor enabled
 production of larger DCA quantities, and a process model was developed that incorporated
 recycling of the oxidation stream.

 All Phase 2 data, including process optimization studies and catalyst screenings, were
 summarized in the following report: https://www.nlr.gov/docs/fy26osti/98497.pdf
