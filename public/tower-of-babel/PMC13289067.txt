Help-seeking and barriers to emergency care during out-of-hospital cardiac arrests in public housing areas: a qualitative study
Authors: Anne Juul Grabmayr, Astrid Marie Rolin Kragh, Sofie Kjærholm, Annam Pervez Sheikh, Doris Østergaard, Christian Hassager, Fredrik Folke, Tine Tjørnhøj-Thomsen, Carolina Malta Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionUnderstanding whether socioeconomic and ethnic backgrounds influence help-seeking and communication with emergency medical services (EMS) is essential to mitigate disparities.ObjectiveWe explored how public housing residents seek help and communicate with EMS, focusing on barriers and enablers to out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) recognition and swift EMS responses.DesignExplorative qualitative analysis of audio recordings of emergency service calls regarding non-EMS witnessed OHCAs. Audio recordings were screened and selected to ensure call variety—then transcribed and analysed using thematic analysis inspired by the concepts of social wrongs and social orders. We also analysed the dispatch protocol, a tool for managing emergency service calls.SettingFour public housing areas with >50% ethnic minority residents in Copenhagen, Denmark.ParticipantsNon-EMS witnessed OHCAs from 2017 to 2022.ResultsWe included 17 calls regarding 13 OHCAs and defined two themes. The effective call aligned with the dispatch protocol, leading to swift OHCA recognition and dispatcher-assisted cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). In contrast, the challenging call, which did not match the framework of the dispatch protocol, entailed one or more of the following characteristics, leading to delayed or missed OHCA recognition: (1) multiple people involved (multiple callers, third-party caller or bystanders communicating with caller), (2) language barriers, (3) emotionally unstable callers and (4) lack of emergency medical competencies. Across themes, witnesses contacted someone in their network instead of calling emergency services immediately. This was a barrier to OHCA recognition in the challenging calls, which some dispatchers managed using calm intonation and simple inquiries.ConclusionsWe identified two themes concerning communication in emergency service calls from public housing areas: the effective call and the challenging call, resulting in swift or delayed/missed OHCA recognition, respectively. The dispatch protocol enabled swift recognition in the effective calls but was a barrier in the challenging calls. Across themes, witnesses used network-based help-seeking before calling emergency services, delaying EMS responses and dispatcher-assisted CPR.Keywords: Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest, ACCIDENT & EMERGENCY MEDICINE, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Early recognition and swift emergency medical services (EMS) activation are crucial for survival following out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA).1 The American Heart Association has underlined the importance of understanding how laypeople act when facing an OHCA and what may hinder or enable a correct EMS response.2 To improve dispatch recognition and management of OHCA, many communities have implemented detailed dispatch protocols that guide dispatchers’ questions and instructions to facilitate quick triage, appropriate EMS response and bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR).3 No randomised-controlled trials have tested the current dispatch protocols.4 5 Furthermore, how these protocols work among callers of varying demographics—for instance, people with lower socioeconomic status or ethnic minority backgrounds—is not fully understood.

Public housing areas in Europe typically have a higher proportion of residents with low socioeconomic status and ethnic minorities,6 both of which have been associated with poorer cardiovascular outcomes.7 A recent study found that public housing areas have a 1.5-fold higher incidence per 100 000 inhabitants than other residential areas.8 Furthermore, OHCAs in these areas had longer EMS response times and a lower probability of initial shockable rhythm and survival, even among patients below 30.8 This could indicate that people in public housing areas encounter barriers in the first step of the survival chain, namely, when recognising OHCA and calling EMS.8 The skills and competencies needed to seek medical assistance and communicate with healthcare professionals are not evenly distributed in the population; those with the fewest resources also encounter more barriers in the healthcare system than the resourceful ones.9 Socioeconomic and ethnic disparities in EMS responses and OHCA have been reported in many different communities,10 11 and current strategies for training the population in CPR might not meet the needs of the public housing population.8 Understanding how these barriers arise and how they relate to OHCA recognition, help-seeking, caller–dispatcher communication and the design of dispatch protocols could enable the design of interventions to mitigate these disparities.2

We sought to investigate how barriers to or enablers of OHCA recognition and swift EMS responses arise in emergency service calls from public housing areas in Copenhagen, how barriers are intertwined with the context of the calls, and how the communication between caller and dispatcher affects OHCA recognition and the dispatch of an appropriate EMS response.

Methods

This exploratory qualitative study of emergency service calls regarding OHCAs in public housing areas provides unique insights into the occurrences in and around the OHCA scene before EMS arrival. Please see online supplemental material for a description of the research team.

Setting

Public housing areas

Public housing is managed through non-profit organisations, ensuring low rent and accounting for 20% of the Danish housing stock.6 Residents are generally older, more likely female, have a lower income and educational level and are more likely to have ethnic minority status than the remaining population.6 More than 50% of the residents in the included public housing areas have an ethnic minority background.

Emergency medical dispatch centre

The Danish emergency medical dispatch centres (EMDCs) are call centres where dispatchers, experienced nurses or paramedics with additional communication and emergency care training handle incoming emergency service calls.12 No translational services are available, and calls are generally answered in Danish or English. Dispatchers can establish video streaming from the scene through a text-message link; however, this is encouraged in OHCA cases only after CPR has been initiated and more than two people are present.13 According to perceived urgency, dispatchers choose responses ranging from self-care to a physician-staffed helicopter, physician-staffed vehicle and a paramedic-staffed ambulance.

Dispatch protocol

The dispatch protocol is The Danish Index for Emergency Care, which is used in all Danish EMDCs. Implemented in 2011, this tool is used to manage calls to the EMDC (figure 1).14 It is displayed on one of several screens from which the dispatcher operates. The protocol suggests EMS responses and which instructions should be provided to the caller, including CPR instructions, through a criteria-based click-and-select system.12 It assumes that there is one caller who is by the patient’s side. It starts with an open-ended question (‘This is emergency services, what is your problem?’), to which a short answer is expected; otherwise, the dispatcher may interrupt the caller. When the caller answers that the patient is not awake, the remaining questions can be answered with yes or no.

Study population and data sources

Emergency service calls

We included audio recordings of emergency service calls regarding non-EMS witnessed OHCAs occurring in major public housing areas with more than 50% of residents having an ethnic minority background in Greater Copenhagen, between 2017 and 2022. We excluded cases where the patients were awake during emergency service calls, with missing audio recordings, and where the caller was an on-duty healthcare professional. By geomapping OHCA cases, we identified all OHCAs from four major public housing areas in Copenhagen from 2017 to 2020. The four areas are part of the cluster-randomised trial Cardiac Arrest in Residential Areas with Volunteer Responder Activation (The CARAMBA Trial, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04446585).15 AJG screened audio recordings from 48 cases from the four public housing areas by listening to them and excluded 18 cases according to the exclusion criteria. Thereafter, cases were selected following purposive sampling where cases were selected to inform the research question with the broadest possible variety rather than being a representative sample.16 The criteria used for selecting cases were patient characteristics (age and sex), caller characteristics (gender, relation to patient, language, number of callers and callers’ location), and both dispatcher recognised and unrecognised OHCAs. Additional cases from 2022 were included while validating OHCA data for the Danish OHCA Registry; they were from similar major public housing areas and provided new information about the call situation. The sample size was determined based on the concept of information power where a narrow research question, a homogenous population of cardiac arrest patients in specific residential areas, and a theoretically based analysis allowed for a smaller sample.17 In addition, the study was embedded in a larger community intervention (the CARAMBA trial) where the main author had interactions, conversations and held focus groups with the target population which further strengthens the choice of a smaller sample. There were no paediatric OHCAs among the screened audio recordings.

The dispatch protocol

The dispatch protocol serves as a tool for the dispatcher to understand and triage the call. Thus, it is imperative that the dispatcher can rely on the protocol to conduct quick triaging of the patient to activate the appropriate EMS response. The part of the dispatch protocol concerning OHCA recognition and dispatch-assisted CPR has been included as a data source, as illustrated in figure 1.

Theoretical framework

We drew on Fairclough’s critical discourse analysis to understand the communication between the caller and the dispatcher and how the context around the call influenced the communication involved.18 Fairclough’s concept of social wrongs and social orders provides a broader understanding of barriers that include the context and how they can be mitigated. We defined social wrongs as aspects of emergency service calls that deviated from dispatch protocol or the EMS system and that were detrimental to patients’ outcomes.18 Social orders are the structures or procedures that prevent social wrongs from being mitigated.18

To further deepen the understanding of the social wrongs and orders, we drew on Shim’s concept of cultural health capital, which entails verbal and nonverbal skills, competencies and resources that callers and dispatchers obtain through their social interactions that may help callers achieve optimal healthcare services and dispatchers deliver suitable services.9 These competencies are not evenly distributed across the population; rather, those with a low socioeconomic status or ethnic minority background tend to be less able to navigate the healthcare system, and the providers tend to have less knowledge regarding this population’s needs and customs potentially leading to misunderstandings and misdiagnoses.9 In contrast, improving cultural health capital can overcome barriers by enabling the caller or the provider to adjust to the situation.9

Analysis

AJG transcribed the calls and performed the analysis using the six phases of reflexive thematic analysis.19 20 Intonation, emotional outbursts and concurrent speech are difficult to transcribe; therefore, AJG listened to the calls during the transcription phase and again during the analysis to grasp the full content of the communication. In the data familiarisation phase, the transcripts were read and reread to gain insight into the material. In the next phase, AJG, AMRK and SK generated and discussed initial codes. The dispatch protocol was then coded and compared with the emergency service calls to analyse where the calls followed the script of the dispatch protocol and where the calls deviated from the dispatch protocol. We realised that the dispatch protocol could serve as a framework for understanding the communication within emergency service calls as it outlined the assumptions of an ‘effective call’. During the coding process, we searched the literature for theories to help broaden our understanding and developed the theoretical framework described above.18 We performed a second coding round, applying social orders, social wrongs and the dispatch protocol as lenses through which to understand the communication within the calls.18 In the third phase, the codes were sorted into themes related to barriers to an appropriate EMS response. In addition to the themes presented in this paper, we found that agonal breathing was a barrier to recognition; this has been described in multiple studies.4 We decided to focus on the themes that provided new knowledge of barriers to EMS among residents in public housing areas. The concept of cultural health capital was applied to analyse how disparities intertwine with social wrongs and how they might be mitigated.9 In the fourth phase, the themes were reviewed to reflect all the relevant social wrongs and social orders, named and defined.20 In the fifth phase, the themes were named and defined from the perspective of the dispatchers, and the contrast between them emphasises what is at stake in some of the calls. During the analysis, we drew on our background knowledge of the EMS system and public housing residents (see online supplemental material) to include the context of the calls and broaden our understanding of the themes.18 Finally, in the sixth phase, we finalised the writing of the paper.

Network-based help-seeking

As an overarching theme, witnesses used network-based help-seeking rather than calling emergency service directly in 7 out of 13 cases across age, gender and language skills (table 1). First, the witness contacted one or more helpers—predominantly younger family members—in their network. Thereafter, four different scenarios played out: (1) the helper called emergency service from a location that differed from the patient’s, (2) multiple people called emergency service regarding the same incident, (3) the helper travelled to the location of the OHCA and then called emergency service and (4) the witness called emergency service after calling a helper.

The effective call and the challenging call

We constructed two themes, encapsulating two types of calls concerning communication and OHCA recognition. The effective call was when dispatchers quickly recognised OHCA, and the caller and dispatcher communicated effectively. The effective call served as a contrast to the challenging call, where barriers within the call or the EMS system hindered or delayed recognition and a swift EMS response. The effective call

The effective call represented a situation in which the caller and the OHCA scene matched the assumptions in the dispatch protocol, leading to effective communication, whereby OHCA was quickly recognised, and CPR initiated.

Caller follows the lead of the dispatcher

In case C, a caller found his partner collapsed in their apartment. In the emergency service call, the caller was calm, answered precisely, and acted according to the dispatcher’s instructions (table 3, Quote 1). Although the dispatcher asked questions beyond the dispatch protocol regarding temperature and rigour mortis, the OHCA was quickly recognised, and CPR initiated. By following the dispatcher’s lead, providing short and accurate answers, and remaining calm, the caller can be viewed as being in line with the social order of the dispatch protocol.

In case I, a caller who spoke fluent Danish, witnessed a younger person collapse. The caller followed the dispatcher’s instructions and quickly started CPR (table 3, quote 2). While the caller performed CPR, a bystander arrived and became audible in the background. The bystander immediately began to help. Based on the audio recording, the bystander does not appear to be surprised and immediately starts to assist the caller in providing CPR. The bystander’s question to the caller, ‘Who is calling now?’ subtly implied that the witness and the bystander had been in contact before emergency service was called (table 3, quote 3). The call to emergency service was delayed due to network-based help-seeking. However, this did not hinder dispatcher recognition or CPR once contact with emergency service was established.

Challenging calls

The challenging call deviated from the dispatch protocol, leading to miscommunication between the caller and dispatcher in some cases. We conceptualised the deviations as social wrongs and grouped them into calls with third-party callers, multiple callers, bystanders, language barriers, emotionally unstable callers and lack of emergency medical competencies (see table 4 for definitions).

Multiple callers

In case A, an older person had collapsed in an apartment. Owing to language skills, the witnesses of the OHCA had called the patient’s grandchildren. Two relatives then called emergency service from different locations concurrently. The first caller was by the patient’s side, with bystanders audibly grieving in the background. The caller communicated with them in a language other than Danish while on the phone with the dispatcher. The second caller was calling from her car on the way to the scene. The phrase “…that is why I have arrived” implies that the caller has not witnessed the OHCA; rather he arrived at the scene after being contacted by the witness. The dispatcher was audibly irritated by the two callers and the bystanders, which shifted focus away from the patient. The multiple callers can be interpreted as a social wrong that led to the termination of the first call due to resource distribution since the social order assumes only one caller per incident. This was also evident in another case where there were multiple callers. This social order emerges from the EMS organisation. Another social wrong further complicated the situation: the caller talked to the bystanders in another language than Danish, which the dispatcher could not understand. The social order of the dispatch protocol is that the communication is between the dispatcher and the caller. When callers talked to bystanders in a language other than Danish, in multiple cases, the dispatchers concurrently talked to the caller. In the quote above, the caller talked with bystanders in a language other than Danish while the dispatcher was giving instructions to the caller. This led to the caller not hearing the dispatcher explain why the dispatcher was ending the call. The caller was then presumably left in a situation where the relative was in OHCA, but did not know whether an ambulance was on the way or why the call was ended.

Language barriers

In case K, a younger caller who spoke fluent Danish called emergency service because a relative had collapsed. During the call, another relative was crying and wailing in the background, and the caller was talking to the other relative in a language other than Danish. The OHCA was recognised at the beginning of the call, and the caller was initially relieved to would get help. At the beginning of the call, the caller said they had received CPR training. However, the caller was not able to follow the CPR instructions that were repeatedly given by the dispatcher because the caller did not understand the specific instructions. The more the dispatcher repeated the same instructions, the more frustrated the caller became, and started doubting whether an ambulance was on the way. The language barrier can be viewed as a social wrong, as the caller could not understand and follow the dispatcher’s CPR instructions (table 4, quote 6.2). The caller sounded upset, which seemed to be exaggerated by the caller’s inability to follow CPR instructions (table 4, quote 9.2). At this point in the conversation, the dispatcher had told the caller multiple times that the ambulance was on the way. However, when the caller could not answer questions or follow instructions, the caller started yelling desperately, asking whether the ambulance was on the way, and did not comprehend the dispatcher’s reassurances. The stringent CPR instructions from the dispatch protocol can be viewed as the social order that reinforces these social wrongs in this call. The call also showed a way to mitigate the social order. When the dispatcher asked the caller concise and straightforward questions (eg, ‘How many people are present?’), he collaborated and answered calmly.

The role of dispatchers’ competencies in handling challenging calls

Most dispatchers handled challenging calls by insisting on following the protocol. They came across as annoyed or impatient when the callers could not answer questions or follow instructions. This further exacerbated challenging calls with emotionally unstable callers who reacted with anger, cried and demanded help, unable to hear and comprehend the dispatchers’ answers such as ‘The ambulance is on the way’ in case K. However, two dispatchers acted differently. As the following case shows, they came across as empathic and calm and asked the callers elementary questions, thereby calming them and enabling collaboration.

Emotional instability

In case D, an elder person has collapsed in the hallway of an apartment. The person’s partner, who spoke fluent Danish, was in panic and cried while talking with emergency service. When asked to start CPR, the partner refused and capitulated. This call can be viewed as having two social wrongs: the caller was emotionally unstable and unable to follow the dispatcher’s instructions. The dispatcher mitigated this by being calm and empathetic and breaking instructions and questions into small steps (‘Feel her chest’ and ‘Is she warm?’). When the caller was able to follow the instructions or answer questions, the caller calmed down and collaborated (table 4, quote 8).

Third-party callers

In case N, a middle-aged person collapsed while visiting a younger relative in Denmark. The relative did not speak Danish and called a helper instead of EMS. The helper called emergency service from another location to request an ambulance to the witness’s address (table 4, quote 4). The third-party caller started the call by saying that a person was dying. This led to a negotiation between dispatcher and caller, wherein the dispatcher, audibly annoyed, repeatedly asked about symptoms and wanted to contact the witness at the scene. The caller kept saying that the patient was dead and that the witness did not speak Danish. The dispatcher initially sent an A2 response, which was later upgraded to an A1 response (see table 2 for response definitions). The third-party caller can be viewed as the social wrong in this call. The social order of the dispatch protocol is that the caller must be by the patient’s side to describe symptoms and that questions regarding breathing and consciousness must be answered before OHCA can be assumed. In cases with third-party callers, the dispatchers typically attempted to call witnesses directly. However, when the witness had language barriers, the caller refused this strategy, referring to the witness’s inability to communicate in Danish. In the majority of the calls in this study, an ambulance was sent very early in the call. However, the callers were left feeling unheard. This experience will form the next time they need EMS assistance, shaping their help-seeking and communication with potentially detrimental results.9

Network-based help-seeking generally delayed first contact with emergency service. During the effective calls, the dispatcher-caller interaction seemed unaffected by network-based help-seeking, while it seemed to delay dispatcher recognition during the challenging calls.

Discussion

In this qualitative study of OHCA emergency service calls from public housing areas, we found two lines of barriers to early recognition and EMS responses. The first line of barriers was the use of network-based help-seeking instead of an immediate call to emergency service. The second line of barriers concerned communication during the emergency service call. It included third-party callers, multiple callers, bystanders, language barriers, emotionally unstable callers and a lack of emergency medical competencies. These barriers led to challenging calls that collided with the assumptions and social order of the dispatch protocol and the EMS system. Patients with challenging calls risked OHCA being unrecognised and receiving a delayed EMS response. Notably, some dispatchers overcame the challengingness through a calm and empathetic intonation combined with elementary questions or instructions. These results originated from public housing areas where most residents had an ethnic minority background. However, these results may be relevant beyond this context as we have not restricted the inclusion of calls to callers of ethnic minority backgrounds.

This study provides novel information regarding how laypeople seek help and navigate the EMS when facing an OHCA, as has been warranted by the American Heart Association.2 The dispatch protocol can be an effective tool for ensuring a swift and appropriate EMS response.14 However, when the caller does not fit the dispatch protocol’s social order, the dispatchers lack the tools to navigate the call situation. Many other communities have similar dispatch protocols, such as medical priority or criteria-based dispatch.3 These systems are similar to Denmark’s dispatch protocol and do not account for the barriers addressed in this study. Furthermore, the barriers identified in the challenging calls have been reported in studies from many other communities,4 and it is common practice to exclude third-party calls in studies of emergency service calls.4 Consequently, the specific intervention points in the public housing areas and the EMS system might translate well to other EMS systems and lay the foundation for future interventions to reduce socioeconomic and ethnic disparities in OHCA and improve survival.

We found network-based help-seeking across caller demographics, even among people trained in CPR, implying that network-based help-seeking could be a praxis beyond the public housing population. To develop interventions that minimise the use of network-based help-seeking, it is essential to investigate who and how many use this strategy, as it is detrimental to survival.1 Studies from the USA (data from 1993 to 1996)21 and Australia (data from 2002 to 2012)22 have reported that 15% and 6% of callers, respectively, used network-based help-seeking, which are associated with a lower survival probability. Barriers to calling emergency services in ethnic minority neighbourhoods with low socioeconomic status have also been reported in the United States.23 Current basic life support training and education guidelines contain little and simple information regarding calling emergency services and do not account for network-based help-seeking.24 25 Teaching people that network-based help-seeking is common but unfavourable might broaden the witnesses’ emergency medical competencies and prepare them to call emergency services directly.9 However, it is unknown whether the current strategy for training the population in CPR reaches people across socioeconomic and ethnic minority status, such as those who commonly live in public housing. Public housing areas offer a framework for community interventions to improve OHCA survival, from national organisations with political influence and budgets to local social workers, volunteers, communication channels and communal spaces.6 15 In the cluster-randomised trial Cardiac Arrests in Residential Areas with Volunteer Responder Activation (The CARAMBA trial), we successfully implemented a community intervention in public housing areas to train the residents in CPR.15 The study has yet to show whether this strategy improves bystander defibrillation and survival.

Unfortunately, teaching people to call emergency services would not benefit those without the needed language competencies. These callers depended on their networks to obtain access. However, patients risk delayed EMS response because of multiple or third-party callers related to network-based help-seeking. This issue will likely increase with Denmark’s growing number of refugees and immigrants.26 Some communities offer translation services at the EMS; however, it takes time to establish contact with an appropriate translator,23 they are expensive, and the wide variety of minority languages in Denmark might prove impossible to accommodate.26 New developments in artificial intelligence and language models could provide live translations from any language. Nevertheless, the dispatch protocol and the EMS system must accommodate network-based help-seeking. This could be done by improving the cultural health capital of the EMS system through changes to the dispatch protocol to include third-party or multiple callers and strengthening the dispatchers’ knowledge of network-based help-seeking.9 An alternative pathway to the EMS has been developed for the estimated 4000 people in Denmark with impaired hearing who can contact the EMS through text messages. Although this is not relevant to language barriers, it shows that alternative access pathways exist. To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore communication with EMS, including the dispatch protocol within a context of low socioeconomic status and ethnic minorities

Cultural health capital is an essential concept for mitigating the social wrongs identified in this study, as it encompasses teaching the witnesses and callers to navigate the EMS system and the dispatchers to navigate the challenging calls.9 The dispatch protocol follows biomedical and hierarchical language, as outlined in international resuscitation guidelines,25 which sometimes collides with the callers’ circumstances and competencies. The callers may be familiar with the discourse of the dispatch protocol through prior experience with calling emergency services, first aid training or campaigns.27 Some callers could not navigate the emergency service call or were in situations that deviated from the dispatch protocol, making it difficult to fulfil the role of the caller, leading to miscommunication. This may be mitigated by providing a broader understanding of callers and call situations in the dispatch protocol and the dispatchers’ scenario training. For example, when a caller communicated with bystanders in the background in a language other than Danish, the dispatchers continued asking questions or giving instructions that the caller did not hear. Allowing for these translations would improve communication and ensure that the caller understood the information or instructions provided. When dealing with a challenging call, most dispatchers reacted by adhering to the dispatch protocol—for example, demanding to know the patient’s conscious level or breathing status and not accepting the answer that the patient had died. Dispatchers’ annoyance and impatience in challenging calls are valid feelings; however, they become problematic when directed towards the caller. Instead, the dispatchers could use these feelings to question whether the caller might be in a situation in which they risk receiving a delayed EMS response. Importantly, some dispatchers overcame challenging calls by being empathic, using a calm voice and using elementary questions or instructions.

Limitations

The fact that this study was conducted in major public housing areas in Copenhagen with low socioeconomic status and a high proportion of ethnic minorities limits its transferability to other contexts. However, our results might still be transferable, as dispatch protocols are widespread3–and public housing residents share characteristics and OHCA incidences across countries.6 8 Using the concepts of social order and social wrongs strengthens the findings and their transferability.28

The study included 17 calls regarding 13 patients from four public housing areas. This qualitative analysis aimed to achieve information richness rather than a representative sample. For this reason, the number of participants is small compared with quantitative research. A larger sample size or a sample that included additional housing areas might have led to further insight. However, data saturation was reached with the current sample. Future studies could further evaluate the prevalence of the identified barriers and facilitators in larger cohorts through EMS call databases.

We aimed to include people with challenges accessing EMS, making inclusive recruitment essential. Audio recordings enabled the broad inclusion of OHCAs without recollection or selection bias. However, further elaborations have not been accessible from witnesses, callers or dispatchers. To accommodate this, AJG has conducted participant observations and informal dialogue among dispatchers, as well as focus groups and interviews with public housing residents discussing this study’s findings. More research is needed to understand why some dispatchers follow the dispatch protocol strictly while others do not and what other consequences these actions may have.

Conclusions

We defined two themes concerning the communication between dispatchers and callers in emergency service calls regarding OHCAs in public housing areas: the effective call, resulting in swift OHCA recognition and EMS response, and the challenging call, resulting in delayed or missed OHCA recognition and an inappropriate EMS response. The dispatch protocol enabled effective communication in the effective calls but was a barrier to OHCA recognition and swift EMS responses in the challenging calls. Across themes and caller characteristics, witnesses used network-based help-seeking instead of calling emergency services immediately, which delayed contact with the EMS, OHCA recognition, dispatcher-assisted CPR and EMS responses.
