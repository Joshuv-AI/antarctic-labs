Nonlocal Fourier Laws for Heat Propagation via Fractional powers of Vector Operators Thanks: F. Colombo is supported by MUR grant Dipartimento di Eccellenza 2023-2027.
Fabrizio Colombo, Francesco Mantovani, Peter Schlosser

Abstract
. The present work is devoted to the study of fractional powers of vector operators, with particular emphasis on the gradient operator with non-constant coefficients. Within the setting of Clifford algebra ℝ n \mathbb{R}_{n} , this operator turns out to have bisectorial properties. By applying the spectral theory on the S S -spectrum, we address a fundamental mathematical challenge: unlike sectorial operators, bisectorial operators involve fractional powers that are not analytic on the negative real line. To circumvent this, we introduce a novel definition of the fractional power function in this setting. Building upon previous works on bisectorial vector operators and weak solutions, we extend the definition of fractional powers to abstract vector operators. The core contribution of this work is the application of the functional calculus for vector operators to the gradient operator, showing that these fractional powers provide a rigorous mathematical foundation for nonlocal Fourier laws in heat propagation.

S
-spectrum.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we study the gradient operator with non-constant
coefficients
a_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, acting within the Clifford algebra

\mathbb{R}_{n}
of dimension
n\geq 3
.
Specifically, let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
denote the standard imaginary units of

\mathbb{R}_{n}
satisfying the anticommutation relations

e_{i}e_{j}+e_{j}e_{i}=-2\delta_{ij}
;
the gradient operator is then formally defined as

(1.1)

\nabla_{a}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\,a_{i}(x)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}.

Applying the spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum, we use the

H^{\infty}
-functional calculus associated with the operator
\nabla_{a}

in the Clifford-algebra setting.
This functional calculus provides a rigorous framework for defining the
fractional powers of vector operators such as
\nabla_{a}
.

The key observation is that the fractional powers
of
\nabla_{a}
, constructed via the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus
on the
S
-spectrum, provide a natural operator-theoretic model for the
nonlocal Fourier law.
This constitutive relation generalises Fourier’s classical local law
to a setting in which heat conduction at a given point depends on the
temperature distribution over an extended spatial region.
In particular, when all coefficients are constant and equal to one,
i.e.,
a_{i}\equiv 1
for every
i=1,\dots,n
, the operator
\nabla_{a}

reduces to the standard gradient

\nabla:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},

and the fractional powers of
\nabla
constructed within the
proposed framework, when we apply the divergence operator, recover the classical fractional Laplacian. This consistency result confirms that the theory developed here is a
genuine extension of the well established constant coefficient theory to
the variable coefficient in the Clifford algebra settings.

Spectral analysis of operators in a Banach module over
\mathbb{R}_{n}
is significantly more complicated than in the complex case, due to the non-commutativity of
\mathbb{R}_{n}
. The natural notion of spectrum in this setting is the
S
-spectrum, see Definition
2.1
, which is closely connected to hypercomplex analysis. For comprehensive discussions, the reader is referred to the books
[
7
,
8
,
16
]
. This spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum also applies to operators in Vector Analysis and Differential Geometry, particularly for the study of Dirac operators on manifolds, which are specific instances of Clifford operators.

This theory originated to provide a rigorous mathematical foundation for Quaternionic Quantum Mechanics, but it was subsequently generalized to the setting of Clifford operators. This foundational framework was established by Birkhoff and von Neumann
[
5
]
and expanded upon in works such as
[
19
,
20
,
27
]
and Adler’s book
[
1
]
.

In our previous work
[
14
]
, we study the
S
-spectral problem of the gradient operator (
1.1
) and concluded its bisectorial nature. In particular,
\nabla_{a}
has
S
-spectrum on the negative as well as on the positive real line. This makes it hard to give meaning to fractional powers
\nabla_{a}^{\alpha}
(where
\alpha
is a suitable real number) of the gradient, since the classical fractional power function
s^{\alpha}
is not defined (not holomorphic) on the negative real axis. Compared to complex fractional powers, in the hypercomplex setting of
\mathbb{R}_{n}
it is also not possible to rotate this axis of non-holomorphicity into another direction, it is fixed on the negative real line. Our solution to this problem are the functions

(1.2)

p_{\alpha}(s):=\begin{cases}s^{\alpha},&\operatorname{Sc}(s)>0,\\
-(-s)^{\alpha},&\operatorname{Sc}(s)<0.\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_{\alpha}(s):=\begin{cases}s^{\alpha},&\operatorname{Sc}(s)>0,\\
(-s)^{\alpha},&\operatorname{Sc}(s)<0,\end{cases}

where the precise definition of the term
s^{\alpha}
is

(1.3)

s^{\alpha}:=e^{\alpha\ln(s)},\qquad\operatorname{Sc}(s)>0,

using the logarithm of a Clifford number

(1.4)

\ln(s):=\ln|s|+J\arg(s),\qquad\operatorname{Sc}(s)>0.

Here
J\in\mathbb{S}
is such that
s\in\mathbb{C}_{J}
, and
\arg(s)\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})
is the argument of
s
, treated as complex number in
\mathbb{C}_{J}
. Since the function
q_{\alpha}
can also be written as

q_{\alpha}(s)=(s^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}},

this function is simply a rewriting of the classical fractional power of the square
s^{2}
. However, the function
p_{\alpha}
is much more natural as a definition of fractional powers of real valued numbers, in the sense that for
\operatorname{Sc}(s)>0
, the angle between
p_{\alpha}(s)
and the positive real axis changes by the factor
\alpha
, while for
\operatorname{Sc}(s)<0
, the angle between
p_{\alpha}(s)
and the negative real axis changes with the factor
\alpha
.

We remark that the theory of fractional powers of complex operators has been developed by several authors. Without claiming completeness, we mention the early works
[

,
23
,
24
,
25
]
and also
[
30
,

,
32
,
33
,

,

]
. The literature is now very wide and it has developed in several directions.

In order to give meaning to the fractional powers
p_{\alpha}(T)
and
q_{\alpha}(T)
of any bisectorial Clifford operator
T
, we will use the so called
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus. The
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus, see
[
26
,
28
,
29
]
, extends the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus
[
18
,
35
]
to sectorial and bisectorial operators. It is established via a three-step procedure: First, the calculus is defined for functions that exhibit decay at zero and infinity, see Definition
2.3
. In the second step it will be extended to functions which are continuous at zero and at infinity, see Definition
2.4
. In the third step, via a regularization procedure, the calculus is extended to functions with polynomial growth, see Definition
2.5
and Remark
2.6
. This theory is of fundamental importance for linear operators on Banach spaces, and it is used to study partial differential equations and time-dependent evolution processes. In our previous works
[
34
]
, there is already a study of bisectorial operators in the Clifford setting and a detailed investigation of the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus is provided.

Our approach to fractional diffusion problems, based on the
S
-spectrum, extends beyond the Fourier law with constant coefficients. It applies to the general variable-coefficient case (
1.1
) and generates the associated non-local diffusion operator.

To illustrate this, let
v:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denote the temperature and
q
the heat flow. For
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
, the classical heat equation is deduced from the following two laws:

\displaystyle q(x,t)+\nabla_{a}v(x,t)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad\text{(Fourier's law)}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}v(x,t)+\operatorname{div}q(x,t)

\displaystyle=0.\qquad\text{(Conservation of energy)}

In our framework, we define a fractional Fourier law using fractional powers
p_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})
of the generalized gradient. Subsequently, the fractional Fourier law
q(x,t)=-p_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})v(x,t)
, is replaced into the energy conservation law. In doing so we can also formulate the associated time-independent spectral problem of the fractional heat equation

(1.5)

\lambda v(x)-\operatorname{div}p_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})v(x)=f(x),\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

This approach based on the theory on the
S
-spectrum has advantages that include the following.

i)

It modifies the Fourier law while preserving the conservation of energy.

ii)

It is applicable to a large class of operators, including gradients with non-constant coefficients. In the specific case of the standard gradient
\nabla
, we recover the fractional heat equation with the fractional Laplacian defined via Fourier transform or the spectral theorem.

iii)

Keeping the divergence form of the evolution equation allows for an immediate and natural definition of the weak solution to the fractional evolution problem.

The content of the paper
: In Section
2
, we present preliminary material regarding the spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum and recall basic facts concerning bisectorial operators and the functional calculi we will use in this article. In Section
3
, we define the fractional powers of abstract bisectorial operators.
First, in Section
3.1
we define positive fractional powers for operators which are not necessarily injective. We prove classical properties like the power rule or the decomposition rule of these exponents.
Then in Section
3.2
we also introduce negative powers, but only for injective operators. Starting from Section
4
, we apply the abstract results to concrete problems that lead to new fractional equations for heat propagation. Specifically, we establish bisectorial estimates for the gradient operator with non-constant coefficients. In particular, we derive regularity results of weak solutions in Theorem
4.4
, injectivity of the gradient in Proposition
4.6
, and the fact that
\nabla_{a}
is a bisectorial operator in Theorem
4.7
. All these results allow us to define fractional powers of the gradient, and as a central result, we prove that the fractional power acts on real function as a vector valued function, see Theorem
4.9
.

2.
Preliminaries on Clifford Algebras and Clifford modules

In this section we will fix the algebraic and functional analytic setting of this paper. The underlying algebra in this article will be the real
Clifford algebra

\mathbb{R}_{n}
over
n

imaginary units

e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
, which satisfy the relations

e_{i}^{2}=-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad e_{i}e_{j}=-e_{j}e_{i},\qquad i\neq j\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

In this context we will denote

\circ

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:=\big\{x_{0}+x_{1}e_{1}+\dots+x_{n}e_{n}\;\big|\;x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\big\}
the subset of all
paravectors
,

\circ

\mathbb{S}:=\big\{x_{1}e_{1}+\dots+x_{n}e_{n}\;\big|\;x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}=1\big\}
the set of all imaginary units,

\circ

\mathbb{C}_{J}:=\big\{x+Jy\;\big|\;x,y\in\mathbb{R}\}
the complex hyperplane of the imaginary unit
J\in\mathbb{S}
.

In particular, we will consider
Hilbert modules
over
\mathbb{R}_{n}
, which are real Hilbert spaces
V
where the linearity of the space and the inner product is compatible with
\mathbb{R}_{n}
. The space of bounded, everywhere defined operators
T:V\rightarrow V
will be denoted by
\mathcal{B}(V)
and the set of closed, unbounded operators by
\mathcal{K}(V)
. An overview over the notation and special objects related to the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
can be found in
[
34
, Section 2]
.

Differently from complex Hilbert spaces, in Cliffordian Hilbert modules the spectrum of an operator
T
is connected to the bounded invertibility of the operator

(2.1)

Q_{s}[T]:=T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2},\qquad\text{with }\operatorname{dom}(Q_{s}[T]):=\operatorname{dom}(T^{2}).

This suggests the following definition of
S
-spectrum.

Definition 2.1

(
S
-Spectrum)
.

For every
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
, its
S
-resolvent set and
S
-sepctrum are defined as

\rho_{S}(T):=\big\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\;\big|\;Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)\big\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{S}(T):=\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\rho_{S}(T),

where
\mathcal{B}(V)
is the space of bounded everywhere defined operators. Moreover, for every
s\in\rho_{S}(T)
we define the left and the right
S
-resolvent operators

(2.2)

S_{L}^{-1}(s,T):=Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\overline{s}-TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{R}^{-1}(s,T):=(\overline{s}-T)Q_{s}[T]^{-1}.

In this article we will consider the important special class of bisectorial operators. In order to introduce them, we define for every
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
the open
double sector

D_{\omega}:=\big\{re^{J\phi}\;\big|\;r>0,\,J\in\mathbb{S},\,\phi\in(-\omega,\omega)\cup(\pi-\omega,\pi+\omega)\big\},

Definition 2.2

(Bisectorial operator)
.

An operator
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
is called bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, if its
S
-spectrum is contained in the closed double sector

(2.3)

\sigma_{S}(T)\subseteq\overline{D_{\omega}},

and for every
\varphi\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
there exists some
C_{\varphi}\geq 0
such that the left
S
-resolvent (
2.2
) satisfies

(2.4)

\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)\|\leq\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|s|},\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\}).

For these operators, it is possible to define
f(T)
via the so called
\omega
-functional calculus
[
34
, Definition 3.5]
if
f
is holomorphic and decays at zero and at infinity, and more importantly via the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus
[
34
, Definition 5.3]
if
f
is holomorphic and polynomially growing at zero and infinity. Following early explorations in
[
2
,
6
]
, the calculus was recently established for unbounded bisectorial operators in Clifford modules
[
34
]
, thanks to the universality property described in
[
9
]
. While for left slice holomorphic functions, this calculus is considered already in
[
7
, Theorem 7.2.6]
,
f(T)
for right holomorphic functions was a bigger challenge and only recently in
[
12
, Definition 4.7]
it was fully understood and developed.

More precisely, if we denote by
\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})
the space of left slice-hyperholomorphic functions
f:D_{\theta}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{n}
, see also
[
34
, Definition 2.1]
, we will consider the following two functional calculi in this paper.

Definition 2.3

(
\omega
-functional calculus)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then, for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})
, which satisfies the estimate

(2.5)

|f(s)|\leq\frac{C_{\alpha}|s|^{\alpha}}{1+|s|^{2\alpha}},\qquad s\in D_{\theta},

we define the
\omega
-functional calculus

\omega
\varphi
\theta
\mathbb{C}_{J}

(2.6)

f(T):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{\varphi}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}f(s),

where
\varphi\in(\omega,\theta)
and
J\in\mathbb{S}
are arbitrary, and (
2.6
) is independent of their choice.

We will denote the space of all
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})
which satisfy (
2.5
) by
\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
. If
f
is also intrinsic, we will call the space
\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
.

Definition 2.4

(Extended
\omega
-functional calculus)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then, for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, which admits the decomposition

(2.7)

f(s)=f_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(s),\qquad s\in D_{\theta},

for some
f_{0},f_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
and
\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
, we define the extended
\omega
-functional calculus

(2.8)

f(T):=f_{\infty}+(1+T^{2})^{-1}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(T).

The space of functions
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})
which satisfy the decomposition (
2.7
) is denoted by
\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\text{bnd}}(D_{\theta})
. If
f
is also intrinsic, we call the space
\mathcal{N}^{\text{bnd}}(D_{\theta})
.

Definition 2.5

(
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, for which there exists a function
e\in\mathcal{N}^{\text{bnd}}(D_{\theta})
with the properties

ef\in\mathcal{N}^{\text{bnd}}(D_{\theta})\qquad\text{and}\qquad e(T)\text{ is injective},

we define the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus

(2.9)

f(T):=e(T)^{-1}(ef)(T).

This definition is independent of the choice of the regularizer function
e
.

Remark 2.6
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, and
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})
, for some
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
.

i)

If there exists some
f_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
such that

|f(s)-f_{0}|\leq C\max\{|s|^{\gamma},|s|^{\delta}\},\qquad s\in D_{\theta},

for some
0<\gamma\leq\delta
and
C\geq 0
, then one can apply the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus with the regularizer function
e(s)=\frac{1}{(1+s^{2})^{n}}
, for some
n>\frac{\delta}{2}
.

ii)

If the operator
T
is injective, we can also allow the function
f
to be polynomially growing at
0
and at
\infty
, i.e.,

|f(s)|\leq C\Big(\frac{1}{|s|^{\gamma}}+|s|^{\gamma}\Big),\qquad s\in D_{\theta},

for some
\gamma>0
and
C\geq 0
. Then we can apply the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus with the regularizer function
e(s)=\frac{s^{n}}{(1+s^{2})^{n}}
, for some
n>\gamma
.

3.
Fractional powers of bi-sectorial operators

In this section, we define and analyse two versions of fractional powers of bisectorial operators
T
with real exponent.
More precisely, with the functions in (
1.2
), we investigate the operators
p_{\alpha}(T)
and
q_{\alpha}(T)
for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Since the logarithm
\ln(s)
in (
1.4
) and hence the fractional power
s^{\alpha}
in (
1.3
) are intrinsic functions for
\operatorname{Sc}(s)>0
, both functions

p_{\alpha}(s)\text{ and }q_{\alpha}(s)\text{ are intrinsic for }\operatorname{Sc}(s)\neq 0.

Moreover, the absolute values of
p_{\alpha}
and
q_{\alpha}
are given by

|p_{\alpha}(s)|=|q_{\alpha}(s)|=|s|^{\alpha},\qquad\operatorname{Sc}(s)\neq 0.

This shows that the functions
p_{\alpha},q_{\alpha}
are polynomially growing, and it will be possible to give meaning to
p_{\alpha}(T)
and
q_{\alpha}(T)
via the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus, Definition
2.5
.

More precisely, for
\alpha>0
the functions
p_{\alpha}
and
q_{\alpha}
are vanishing as

s\rightarrow 0
and are polynomially growing as
s\rightarrow\infty
. In this case,
p_{\alpha}(T)
and
q_{\alpha}(T)
can be defined for every bisectorial operator
T
, see Definition
3.1
. Conversely, for
\alpha\leq 0
, both functions
p_{\alpha}
and
q_{\alpha}
are not vanishing as
s\rightarrow 0
, they are bounded for
\alpha=0
or even polynomially growing for
\alpha<0
. Hence, we need to regularize the function at
s=0
, which makes it necessary to assume
T
injective, see Definition
3.12
.

For this reason, we will split the considerations of positive and negative powers into the two distinct Subsection
3.1
and Subsection
3.2
.

3.1.
Positive fractional powers

In this subsection we define the operators
p_{\alpha}(T)
and
q_{\alpha}(T)
for positive powers
\alpha>0
, for bisectorial operators
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
, without the need of
T
being injective. As central properties, we will find the product rule in Theorem
3.5
as well as the composition rule of fractional powers in Theorem
3.11
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial. Then for every
\alpha>0
, we define

p_{\alpha}(T)\text{ and }q_{\alpha}(T)\text{ via the }H^{\infty}\text{-functional calculus \eqref{Eq_Hinfty}}.

A regularizer, is for every integer
n>\frac{\alpha}{2}
, is given by

(3.1)

e(s)=\frac{1}{(1+s^{2})^{n}}.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that the ’trivial’ case
\alpha=0
is not covered by Definition
3.1
. Since the functions
p_{0}(s)
and
q_{0}(s)
do not decay at zero but are only bounded, the definition of
p_{0}(T)
and
q_{0}(T)
is subtle.

On the one hand, the function
q_{0}(s)=1
in (
1.2
) is the constant function. This means, also the operator

q_{0}(T)=1

can be realized as the identity operator, with the help of the extended
\omega
-functional calculus (
2.8
). On the other hand, the function
p_{0}(s)=\operatorname{sgn}(s)
in (
1.2
) reduces to the sign function (understood as the sign function of the scalar part of
s
). Hence, it is constant on both sectors, but the respective limit
s\rightarrow 0
and
s\rightarrow\infty
depend from which direction one approaches
0
and
\infty
. Hence, the extended
\omega
-functional calculus is not applicable for
p_{0}
, and we need to use the full
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus and regularize the function at
0
as well as at
\infty
. This in particular means that we need to assume that
T
is injective in order to define

p_{0}(T)=\operatorname{sgn}(T),

see Definition
3.12
. We also a priori do not know if the resulting operator is bounded. Only if we additionally assume that the operator
T
satsify quadratic estimates, see
[
13
]
, we know that
p_{0}(T)
is a bounded operator.

Remark 3.3
.

Consider the special case
\alpha=n
being an odd positive integer, or
\alpha=m
being an even positive integer. Then there is
p_{n}(s)=s^{n}
and
q_{m}(s)=s^{m}
, which by
[
34
, Theorem 5.9]
means

p_{n}(T)=T^{n},\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_{m}(T)=T^{m},

in the sense that action as well as domains of both sides of the equation coincide.

In order to find the first central property of fractional powers, the product rule in Theorem
3.5
, we have to improve the well known product rule
[
34
, Theorem 5.7]
of the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus, which in general only gives an operator inclusion. It is already known that if for example the second operator
f(T)
is bounded, it is satisfied with equality. However, for both operators
f(T)
and
g(T)
being unbounded,
there is no existing result which ensures equality. Roughly speaking, the following Proposition
3.4
shows that for functions
f,g
which, up to some constant, are both vanishing at
0
and are polynomially bounded at
\infty
, and for which
1/g
vanishes at
\infty
and polynomially grows at
0
, the product rule holds with equality.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, and
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})
,
g\in\mathcal{N}(D_{\theta})
, for some
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
. If there exist
0<\gamma\leq\delta
, as well as constants
0<C_{1}\leq C_{2}
, such that for some
f_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
,
g_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
there hold the estimates

(3.2a)

\displaystyle C_{1}\min\{|s|^{\delta},|s|^{\gamma}\}\leq|g(s)-g_{0}|\leq C_{2}\max\{|s|^{\delta},|s|^{\gamma}\},\qquad s\in D_{\theta},

(3.2b)

\displaystyle|f(s)-f_{0}|\leq C_{2}\max\{|s|^{\delta},|s|^{\gamma}\},\qquad s\in D_{\theta}.

Then, we have the product rule

(gf)(T)=g(T)f(T).

Proof.

From the product rule
[
34
, Theorem 5.7]
of the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus, we already know the operator inclusion

(3.3)

(gf)(T)\supseteq g(T)f(T),

with the domains connected by

\operatorname{dom}((gf)(T))\cap\operatorname{dom}(f(T))=\operatorname{dom}(g(T)f(T)).

This means, in order to show equality in (
3.3
), we have to verify

(3.4)

\operatorname{dom}((gf)(T))\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(f(T)).

To do so, let
v\in\operatorname{dom}((gf)(T))
. Due to the upper bounds (
3.2a
) and (
3.2b
), it is possible to consider the regularizer function
e
in (
3.1

-functional calculus, the condition

Next, due to the lower bound (

), and by the choice

a function for which we can apply the

However, with the product rule

∎

The product rule of Proposition

can now be applied to the functions

and

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial. Then for

i)

p_{\alpha+\beta}(T)=p_{\alpha}(T)q_{\beta}(T)=q_{\alpha}(T)p_{\beta}(T)
;

ii)

q_{\alpha+\beta}(T)=p_{\alpha}(T)p_{\beta}(T)=q_{\alpha}(T)q_{\beta}(T)

Proof.

It is immediate that for every
\operatorname{Sc}(s)\neq 0

(3.5)

\begin{split}p_{\alpha+\beta}(s)=&p_{\alpha}(s)q_{\beta}(s)=q_{\alpha}(s)p_{\beta}(s),\\
q_{\alpha+\beta}(s)=&p_{\alpha}(s)p_{\beta}(s)=q_{\alpha}(s)q_{\beta}(s).\end{split}

The stated product rules are then a consequence of Proposition
3.4
.
∎

This product rule of fractional powers can now also be used to get a domain inclusion in between different order fractional powers.

Definition 3.12

Let

be injective and bisectorial. Then for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

In our current setting, where we assume that the operator

-functional calculus holds with equality. Namely, in the injective setting we can also allow functions

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be injective and bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})

(3.24a)

\displaystyle C_{1}\min\Big\{\frac{1}{|s|^{\delta}},\frac{1}{|s|^{\gamma}}\Big\}\leq|g(s)-g_{0}|\leq C_{2}\max\Big\{\frac{1}{|s|^{\delta}},\frac{1}{|s|^{\gamma}}\Big\},\qquad s\in D_{\theta},

(3.24b)

\displaystyle|f(s)-f_{0}|\leq C_{2}\max\Big\{\frac{1}{|s|^{\delta}},\frac{1}{|s|^{\gamma}}\Big\},\qquad s\in D_{\theta}.

Then we have the product rule

(gf)(T)=g(T)f(T).

Proof.

For the same reason as in (

), it is sufficient to verify the domain inclusion

(3.25)

\operatorname{dom}((gf)(T))\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(f(T)).

To do so, let

. Due to the upper bound of

in (

f
in (

with

is a regularizer of the product

Next, due to the lower bound (

) of

v\in\operatorname{dom}(f(T))
. Then by Lemma
3.21
, there exists a sequence
(v_{n})_{n}\in\operatorname{ran}(e(T))=\operatorname{dom}(T^{2k+1})\cap\operatorname{ran}(T^{2k+1})
, such that

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}v_{n}=v\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}f(T)v_{n}=f(T)v.

Since
v_{n}\in\operatorname{ran}(e(T))
, we can apply it to (
3.34
), and write the second limit as

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}v_{n}=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}f(T)v_{n}=f(T)v.

This shows that
v\in\operatorname{dom}\big(\overline{(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}}\big)
and
f(T)v=\overline{(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}}v
.

Now that we have proven (
3.33
), we can combine this result with the operator
g(T)
. First, since
(gfe)(T)
is a bounded operator, we get

(gfe)(T)e(T)^{-1}=g(T)(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}\subseteq g(T)\overline{(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}}=g(T)f(T),

where in the last equality we used (
3.33
). If we now take the closure of this inclusion, and use again (
3.33
) with
gf
instead of
f
, we obtain the remaining operator inclusion

(gf)(T)=\overline{(gfe)(T)e(T)^{-1}}\subseteq\overline{g(T)f(T)}.\qed

The next theorem is the remaining third one, finalizing together with Theorem
3.5
and Theorem
3.14
the power rule of the fractional powers. It gives the power rule for all possible exponents
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, but since for
\alpha
and
\beta
both being positive or both being negative we have already stronger results in Theorem
3.5
and Theorem
3.14
, the real application for the following result is for mixed positive and negative powers.

Theorem 3.23
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be injective and bisectorial, with
\overline{\operatorname{dom}}(T)=\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T)=V
. Then for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
there hold the power rules

i)

p_{\alpha+\beta}(T)=\overline{p_{\alpha}(T)q_{\beta}(T)}=\overline{q_{\alpha}(T)p_{\beta}(T)}

ii)

q_{\alpha+\beta}(T)=\overline{p_{\alpha}(T)p_{\beta}(T)}=\overline{q_{\alpha}(T)q_{\beta}(T)}

Proof.

Since for every
\operatorname{Sc}(s)\neq 0
, there hold the function identities (
3.5
), the stated product rules are a consequence of the product rule in Proposition
3.22
.
∎

Remark 3.24
.

If the Banach module
V
is
\mathbb{R}
-reflexive (or even a Hilbert space), the fact that
T
is injective and bisectorial, already implies that
\overline{\operatorname{dom}}(T)=\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T)=V
, see
[
12
, Theorem 3.3]
. In particular, if
V
is
\mathbb{R}
-reflexive, the statements of Lemma
3.21
, Proposition
3.22
and Theorem
3.23
hold without the explicit assumption of dense domain and range.

3.3.
Connection to fractional powers of
T^{2}

In this subsection, we will find a connection between the two versions
p_{\alpha}(T)
and
q_{\alpha}(T)
of fractional powers, to the classical fractional powers of the squared operator
T^{2}
. As classical fractional power we understand in this context the function
f(s)=s^{\alpha}
, defined for every
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(-\infty,0]
, and the corresponding operator
f(T)
defined via the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus of sectorial operators. Consequently, we first show that for any bisectorial operator
T
, the squared operator
T^{2}
is sectorial.

Lemma 3.25
.

For every
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
with
\rho_{S}(T)\neq\emptyset
. Then
T^{2}\in\mathcal{K}(V)
and its
S
-spectrum is given by

(3.35)

\sigma_{S}(T^{2})=\sigma_{S}(T)^{2}.

Moreover, for every
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with
s^{2}\in\rho_{S}(T^{2})
, we have

(3.36a)

\displaystyle 2S_{L}^{-1}(s^{2},T^{2})s

\displaystyle=S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)-S_{L}^{-1}(-s,T),\qquad\text{and}

(3.36b)

\displaystyle 2sS_{R}^{-1}(s^{2},T^{2})

\displaystyle=S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)-S_{R}^{-1}(-s,T).

Proof.

First of all, since
\rho_{S}(T)\neq\emptyset
, it follows from
[
15
, Proposition 2.7]
with
s=0
that
T^{2}\in\mathcal{K}(V)
. Furthermore, for every
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, the operator (
2.1
) satisfies

\displaystyle Q_{s}[T]Q_{-s}[T]

\displaystyle=(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})(T^{2}+2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})

(3.37)

\displaystyle=T^{4}+2(|s|^{2}-2s_{0}^{2})T^{2}+|s|^{4}=Q_{s^{2}}[T^{2}].

Using this equation with
s
and once with
s
replaced by
-s
, we have

Q_{s^{2}}[T^{2}]\text{ is bijective}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad Q_{s}[T],Q_{-s}[T]\text{ are bijective}.

By
[
15
, Remark 2.6]
this equivalence can now also be written as

s^{2}\in\rho_{S}(T^{2})\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad s,-s\in\rho_{S}(T).

This proves the accordance of the
S
-spectra in (
3.35
). In order to show the relation between the
S
-resolvents (
3.36a
), let
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with
s^{2}\in\rho_{S}(T^{2})
. Then there is
s,-s\in\rho_{S}(T)
and all the pseudo-resolvents in (
3.37
) are invertible, with

Q_{-s}[T]^{-1}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}=Q_{s^{2}}[T^{2}]^{-1}.

Then, by the definition (
2.2
) of the left
S
-resolvent, we get

\displaystyle S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)-S_{L}^{-1}(-s,T)

\displaystyle=\big(Q_{s}[T]^{-1}+Q_{-s}[T]^{-1}\big)\overline{s}-T\big(Q_{s}[T]^{-1}-Q_{-s}[T]^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=2Q_{s}[T]^{-1}(T^{2}+|s|^{2})Q_{-s}[T]^{-1}\overline{s}-TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}4s_{0}TQ_{-s}[T]^{-1}

\displaystyle=2(T^{2}+|s|^{2})Q_{s^{2}}[T^{2}]^{-1}\overline{s}-4s_{0}T^{2}Q_{s^{2}}[T^{2}]^{-1}

\displaystyle=2|s|^{2}Q_{s^{2}}[T^{2}]^{-1}\overline{s}-2T^{2}Q_{s^{2}}[T^{2}]^{-1}s

\displaystyle=\big(Q_{s^{2}}[T^{2}]^{-1}\overline{s}^{2}-T^{2}Q_{s^{2}}[T^{2}]^{-1}\big)2s=S_{L}^{-1}(s^{2},T^{2})2s.

The similar relation (
3.36b
) for the right
S
-resolvent operator follows analogously.
∎

With Lemma
3.25
we are now in the position to prove that
T^{2}
is sectorial, if
T
is bisectorial.

Proposition 3.26
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then
T^{2}\in\mathcal{K}(V)
is sectorial of angle
2\omega
.

Proof.

Since
T
is bisectorial, there is in particular
\rho_{S}(T)\neq\emptyset
and hence
T^{2}\in\mathcal{K}(V)
by Lemma
3.25
.
Since
\sigma_{S}(T)\subseteq\overline{D_{\omega}}
, we deduce from Lemma
3.25
, that

\sigma_{S}(T^{2})=\sigma_{S}(T)^{2}\subseteq(\overline{D_{\omega}})^{2}=\overline{S_{2\omega}}.

Moreover, let
\varphi\in(2\omega,\pi)
and consider
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(S_{2\varphi}\cup\{0\})
. Then
q=s^{2}
, for some
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\})
, and using the representation (
3.36a
) of the left
S
-resolvent operator of
T^{2}
, we get the estimate

\|S_{L}^{-1}(q,T^{2})\|\leq\frac{\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)\|+\|S_{L}^{-1}(-s,T)\|}{2|s|}\leq\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|s|^{2}}=\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|q|}.

This proves that
T^{2}
is a sectorial operator of angle
2\omega
.
∎

Theorem 3.27
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be injective and bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Then we have the relations

q_{\alpha}(T)=(T^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{\alpha}(T)=\overline{\operatorname{sgn}(T)(T^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}}

between
p_{\alpha}(T)
and
q_{\alpha}(T)
from Definition
3.12
and the fractional power
(T^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
understood as the sectorial
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus of the operator
T^{2}
and the function
f(s)=s^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
, see
[
7
, Definition 7.2.5]
,
where the sign-function is understood as the sign of the scalar part.

Proof.

Let us first consider the operator
q_{\alpha}(T)
. Consider the regularizer
e(s)=\frac{s^{2n}}{(1+s^{2})^{2n}}
, for some integer
n>\frac{|\alpha|}{2}
. Then the
\omega
-functional calculus (
2.6
) of the product
(eq_{\alpha})(T)
can for any
J\in\mathbb{S}
and
\varphi\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
be written as

\displaystyle(eq_{\alpha})(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{\varphi}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}e(s)q_{\alpha}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial S_{\varphi}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}e(s)q_{\alpha}(s)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial S_{\varphi}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(-s,T)ds_{J}e(-s)q_{\alpha}(-s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial S_{\varphi}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}\big(S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)-S_{L}^{-1}(-s,T)\big)ds_{J}e(s)q_{\alpha}(s),

where in the last line we used that
e(-s)=e(s)
and
q_{\alpha}(-s)=q_{\alpha}(s)
. Using now the connection (
3.36a
) between the
S
-resolvents of
T
and
T^{2}
, we can substitute
\xi=s^{2}
to rewrite this integral as

\displaystyle(eq_{\alpha})(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\partial S_{\varphi}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s^{2},T^{2})sds_{J}\frac{s^{2n}s^{\alpha}}{(1+s^{2})^{2n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial S_{2\varphi}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(\xi,T^{2})d\xi_{J}\frac{\xi^{n}\xi^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}}{(1+\xi)^{2n}}=\big(\widetilde{e}\,\xi^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big)(T^{2}),

where in the last equation we interpreted the integral as the sectorial
\omega
-functional calculus
[
7
, Definition 7.1.5]
with the regularizer function
\widetilde{e}(\xi):=\frac{\xi^{n}}{(1+\xi)^{2n}}
and the fractional power
\xi^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
.

Using now the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
2.9
), we get the stated result

q_{\alpha}(T)=e(T)^{-1}(eq_{\alpha})(T)=\widetilde{e}(T^{2})^{-1}\big(\widetilde{e}\,\xi^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big)(T^{2})=(\xi^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})(T^{2})=(T^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}.

The second identity
p_{\alpha}(T)=\overline{\operatorname{sgn}(T)(T^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}}
now follows immediately from the power rule Theorem
3.23
i) and the obvious fact that
p_{0}(s)=\operatorname{sgn}(s)
.
∎

4.
The gradient with nonconstant coefficients

In this section we want to apply the abstract results of the previous Section
3
to the specific case of the gradient operator with non-constant coefficients in
n\geq 3
dimensions. Precisely, if
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
are the imaginary units of the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
, we consider the hypercomplex representation of the gradient

(4.1)

\nabla_{a}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}a_{i}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},\qquad\text{with }\operatorname{dom}(\nabla_{a}):=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

This is an operator in the Clifford module of square integrable functions with values in
\mathbb{R}_{n}
,

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\Big\{f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{n}\;\Big|\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(x)|^{2}dx<\infty\Big\}.

Our goal is to define the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus of
\nabla_{a}
, and in particular apply the fractional powers of Section
3
.

For the coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
of
\nabla_{a}
in (
4.1
), we require the following assumptions.

Assumption 4.1
.

The coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the gradient (
4.1
) are continuously differentiable and belong to one of following two cases:

I)

If the functions
a_{i}
depend on all variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, we assume that

(4.2a)

\displaystyle m_{a}:=

\displaystyle\min\limits_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\inf\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}a_{i}(x)>0,

(4.2b)

\displaystyle M_{a}:=

\displaystyle\Big(\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\|a_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty,

(4.2c)

\displaystyle M_{a}^{\prime}:=

\displaystyle\Big(\sum\nolimits_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big\|a_{j}\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\Big\|^{2}_{L^{n}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty,

(4.2d)

\displaystyle M_{a}^{\prime\prime}:=

\displaystyle\Big(\sum\nolimits_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big\|\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty,

with bounds which satisfy the condition

(4.3)

m_{a}^{2}>C_{S}M_{a}^{\prime},

with

C_{S}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n(n-2)}}\Big(\frac{\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}\Big)^{\frac{1}{n}}

the constant from Sobolev embedding theorem, see
[
36
]
.

II)

If
a_{i}(x)=a_{i}(x_{i})
only depends on the variable
x_{i}
, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we only assume the bounds (
4.2a
), (
4.2b
), and (
4.2d
).

Remark 4.2
.

In case I) of Assumption
4.1
, the conditions (
4.2a
), (
4.2b
) and (
4.2c
) are needed for the existence of a weak solution of the spectral problem, while the condition (
4.2d
) leads to
H^{2}
-regularity of this solution. The inequality (
4.3
) additionally ensures that the
S
-spectrum is contained in a double sector. Case II) on the other hand will be reduced to a gradient with constant coefficients using a transformation of variables.

In order to derive the
S
-spectrum of the operator
\nabla_{a}
, we need to investigate the bounded invertibility of the operator
Q_{s}[\nabla_{a}]
in (
2.1
). As calculated in
[
14
, Eq.(1.7)]
, the corresponding form to this operator is given by

(4.4)

q_{s}(u,v):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},a_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}+(2s_{0}a_{i}-B_{i})e_{i}v\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}+|s|^{2}\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}},

with
\operatorname{dom}(q_{s}):=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and where we have set

(4.5)

B_{i}:=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}a_{j}\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{j}},\qquad i\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

Under the Assumption
4.1
on the coefficients, we now prove the unique solvability of the weak formulation.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let the coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
satisfy the Assumption
4.1
case I) or case II), and let us set

(4.6)

K_{a}:=\begin{cases}\frac{M_{a}}{\sqrt{m_{a}^{2}-C_{S}M_{a}^{\prime}}},&\text{in case I)},\\
1,&\text{in case II)}.\end{cases}

Then, for every
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with

|s|>K_{a}|s_{0}|,

and every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, there exists a unique
u_{f}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, such that

q_{s}(u_{f},v)=\langle f,v\rangle_{L^{2}},\qquad v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Moreover, with the notion

\|u\|_{D}^{2}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

this solution satisfies the bounds

(4.7)

\|u_{f}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\Big(\frac{M_{a}}{m_{a}\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{\|f\|_{L^{2}}}{|s|^{2}-K_{a}^{2}s_{0}^{2}},\quad\text{and}\quad\|u_{f}\|_{D}\leq\Big(\frac{M_{a}}{m_{a}\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{K_{a}|s|\|f\|_{L^{2}}}{m_{a}(|s|^{2}-K_{a}^{2}s_{0}^{2})}.

Proof.

In the case where the coefficients
a_{i}
satisfy the Assumption
4.1
case I), this result is already proven in
[
14
, Theorem 3.2]
. Note that there is always
M_{a}\geq\sqrt{n}\,m_{a}
for the constants in (
4.2a
) and (
4.2b
). In the case the coefficients satisfy the Assumption
4.1
case II), let us define for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
the function

\xi_{i}(x_{i}):=\int_{0}^{x_{i}}\frac{1}{a_{i}(u)}du,\qquad x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
\frac{1}{a_{i}(u)}\leq\frac{1}{m_{a}}
is bounded from above by (
4.2a
), the integral exists. Since
\frac{1}{a_{i}(u)}\geq\frac{1}{M_{a}}
is also bounded from below by (
4.2b
), the function
\xi_{i}
is strictly monotone increasing, with
\lim_{x_{i}\rightarrow\pm\infty}\xi_{i}(x_{i})=\pm\infty
. Hence
\xi_{i}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is bijective, and differentiable with derivative

\xi_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})=\frac{1}{a_{i}(x_{i})}.

We now use the change of variables

(4.8)

y_{i}=\xi_{i}(x_{i}),\qquad i\in\{1,\dots,n\},

to reduce the gradient
\nabla_{a}
with nonconstant coefficients, to the gradient with all coefficients equal to one

\nabla_{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}a_{i}(x_{i})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}a_{i}(x_{i})\xi_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}=:\nabla.

The form
\widetilde{q}_{s}
, which corresponds to the operator
Q_{s}[\nabla]
, is given by (
4.4
) when all coefficients
a_{i}
are set to
1
, i.e.

\widetilde{q}_{s}(u,v):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial y_{i}},\frac{\partial v}{\partial y_{i}}\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}+|s|^{2}\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}},\qquad u,v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

For functions
u,v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
let us now consider the functions

(4.9)

\widetilde{u}(y):=u(x),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{v}(y):=a_{1}(x_{1})\dots a_{n}(x_{n})v(x),

with the connection (
4.8
) between the coordinates
y
and
x
. Then there is

(4.10)

u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\Leftrightarrow\widetilde{u}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\qquad\text{as well as}\qquad v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\Leftrightarrow\widetilde{v}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Moreover, the partial derivatives of these functions are given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}\widetilde{u}(y)

\displaystyle=a_{i}(x_{i})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}u(x),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}\widehat{v}(y)

\displaystyle=a_{i}(x_{i})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\big(a_{1}(x_{1})\dots a_{n}(x_{n})v(x)\big)=a_{1}(x_{1})\dots a_{n}(x_{n})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\big(a_{i}(x_{i})v(x)\big).

With this notion of
\widetilde{u}
and
\widehat{v}
there exists the following connection between the forms
q_{s}
and
\widetilde{q}_{s}

\displaystyle q_{s}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},a_{i}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}(va_{i})+2s_{0}e_{i}v\Big)\Big\rangle_{L^{2},dx}+|s|^{2}\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2},dx}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\frac{1}{a_{i}}\frac{\partial\widetilde{u}}{\partial y_{i}},\frac{a_{i}}{a_{1}\dots a_{n}}\Big(\frac{\partial\widehat{v}}{\partial y_{i}}+2s_{0}e_{i}\widehat{v}\Big)\Big\rangle_{L^{2},dx}+|s|^{2}\Big\langle\widetilde{u},\frac{\widehat{v}}{a_{1}\dots a_{n}}\Big\rangle_{L^{2},dx}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\frac{\partial\widetilde{u}}{\partial y_{i}},\frac{\partial\widehat{v}}{\partial y_{i}}+2s_{0}e_{i}\widehat{v}\Big\rangle_{L^{2},dy}+|s|^{2}\langle\widetilde{u},\widehat{v}\rangle_{L^{2},dy}=\widetilde{q}_{s}(\widetilde{u},\widehat{v}).

Note that in the index of the
L^{2}
-scalar products we indicate by
dx
or
dy
with respect to what variable the
L^{2}
-integral is understood. This connection between the forms means that for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the problem

(4.11)

q_{s}(u,v)=\langle f,v\rangle_{L^{2},dx},\qquad v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

admits a unique weak solution
u_{f}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if and only if the problem

(4.12)

\widetilde{q}_{s}(\widetilde{u},\widehat{v})=\langle\widetilde{f},\widehat{v}\rangle_{L^{2},dy},\qquad\widehat{v}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

admits a weak solution
\widetilde{u}_{\widetilde{f}}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with respect to the function

(4.13)

\widetilde{f}(y):=f(x).

However, since the constant coefficients of the form
\widetilde{q}_{s}
are covered by Assumption
4.1
case I), there exists for every
\widetilde{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
a unique solution
\widetilde{u}_{\widetilde{f}}
, which satisfies the estimate

\|\widetilde{u}_{\widetilde{f}}\|_{L^{2},dy}\leq\frac{\|\widetilde{f}\|_{L^{2},dy}}{|\operatorname{Im}(s)|^{2}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\widetilde{u}_{\widetilde{f}}\|_{D,dy}\leq\frac{|s|\|\widetilde{f}\|_{L^{2},dy}}{|\operatorname{Im}(s)|^{2}}.

Plugging in the connection
\widetilde{u}_{\widetilde{f}}(y)=u_{f}(x)
from (
4.9
), the respective connection (
4.13
) between
f
and
\widetilde{f}
as well as using the transformation
dx=a_{1}\dots a_{n}dy
between the
dy
- and the
dx
-norms, gives

\displaystyle\|u_{f}\|_{L^{2},dx}

\displaystyle=\big\|\sqrt{a_{1}\dots a_{n}}\,\widetilde{u}_{\widetilde{f}}\big\|_{L^{2},dy}\leq\Big(\frac{M_{a}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\|\widetilde{u}_{\widetilde{f}}\|_{L^{2},dy}\leq\Big(\frac{M_{a}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{\|\widetilde{f}\|_{L^{2},dy}}{|\operatorname{Im}(s)|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{M_{a}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{1}{|\operatorname{Im}(s)|^{2}}\Big\|\frac{f}{\sqrt{a_{1}\dots a_{n}}}\Big\|_{L^{2},dx}\leq\Big(\frac{M_{a}}{m_{a}\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{\|f\|_{L^{2},dx}}{|\operatorname{Im}(s)|^{2}},

where in the first line we used the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means, to estimate the term
\sqrt{a_{1}\dots a_{n}}
. Similarly, for the derivatives, the estimate on
\widetilde{u}_{\widetilde{f}}
transforms into the estimate for
u_{f}
as

\|u_{f}\|_{D,dx}\leq\Big(\frac{M_{a}}{m_{a}\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{|s|\|f\|_{L^{2},dx}}{m_{a}|\operatorname{Im}(s)|^{2}}.\qed

Next we prove that the unique weak solution of the form
q_{s}
from Theorem
4.3
is actually a strong solution of the operator
Q_{s}[\nabla_{a}]
. The proof of this statement is highly motivated by
[
21
, Theorem 8.8]
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let the coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
satisfy Assumption
4.1
. If for some
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the function
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a solution of the weak problem

(4.14)

q_{s}(u,v)=\langle f,v\rangle_{L^{2}},\qquad v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

with the form
q_{s}
in (
4.4
), then
u\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a solution of

Q_{s}[\nabla_{a}]u=f.

Proof.

In order to show that
u\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have to show that
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. However, by
[
21
, Lemma 7.24]
it is sufficient to verify

(4.15)

\sup\limits_{h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\Big\|\Delta_{h}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\|_{L^{2}}<\infty,\qquad i\in\{1,\dots,n\},

where for every
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, the difference quotient operator
\Delta_{h}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is defined as

\Delta_{h}v(x):=\frac{v(x+h)-v(x)}{|h|},\qquad v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Since
u
is a solution of (
4.14
), we can in particular test (
4.14
) with
\Delta_{-h}v
, for some arbitrary
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Plugging in the explicit form (
4.4
) of
q_{s}
, then gives

\displaystyle\langle f,\Delta_{-h}v\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},a_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial\Delta_{-h}v}{\partial x_{i}}+(2s_{0}a_{i}-B_{i})e_{i}\Delta_{-h}v\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}+|s|^{2}\langle u,\Delta_{-h}v\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle a_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},\frac{\partial\Delta_{-h}v}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}+\Big\langle|s|^{2}u-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}(2s_{0}a_{i}+B_{i})\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},\Delta_{-h}v\Big\rangle_{L^{2}},

where in the second line we used
\overline{B_{i}}=-B_{i}
, see (
4.5
). This equation can be rearranged to

(4.16)

\Big\langle\underbrace{f-|s|^{2}u+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}(2s_{0}a_{i}+B_{i})\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}}_{=:g},\Delta_{-h}v\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle a_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},\frac{\partial\Delta_{-h}v}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}.

It is straight forward to verify that the adjoint difference quotient is given by
\Delta_{-h}^{*}=-\Delta_{h}
. Using also the product rule

\Delta_{h}(uv)(x)=u(x+h)\Delta_{h}v(x)+\Delta_{h}u(x)v(x),

allows us to rewrite (
4.16
) as

\langle g,\Delta_{-h}v\rangle_{L^{2}}=-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\Delta_{h}\Big(a_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\Big),\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}=-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle a_{i}(\,\cdot\,+h)^{2}\frac{\partial\Delta_{h}u}{\partial x_{i}}+\Delta_{h}a_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Rewriting this equation once more, and using the inequalities

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{-h}v\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|h|}\Big\|\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}h_{j}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{j}}\Big\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|v\|_{D},\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{h}a_{i}^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|h|}\Big\|\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}h_{j}\frac{\partial a_{i}^{2}}{\partial x_{j}}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{2}{|h|}\Big\|\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}h_{j}a_{i}\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 2M_{a}M_{a}^{\prime\prime},

from
[
21
, Lemma 7.23]
, we can now estimate

\displaystyle\Big|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle a_{i}(\,\cdot\,+h)^{2}\frac{\partial\Delta_{h}u}{\partial x_{i}},\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\Delta_{h}a_{i}^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\Big\|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\|_{L^{2}}\Big\|\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\|_{L^{2}}+\|g\|_{L^{2}}\|\Delta_{-h}v\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2M_{a}M_{a}^{\prime\prime}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big\|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\|_{L^{2}}\Big\|\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\|_{L^{2}}+\|g\|_{L^{2}}\|\Delta_{-h}v\|_{L^{2}}

(4.17)

\displaystyle\leq 2M_{a}M_{a}^{\prime\prime}\|u\|_{D}\|v\|_{D}+\|g\|_{L^{2}}\|v\|_{D}.

Next, we will further estimate the
L^{2}
-norm of the function
g
in (
4.16
), namely

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}}+|s|^{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big(2|s_{0}|\|a_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|B_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}}\Big)\Big\|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}}+|s|^{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}}+\bigg(2|s_{0}|\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|a_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|B_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg)\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big\|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(4.18)

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}}+|s|^{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}}+(2|s_{0}|M_{a}+M_{a}M_{a}^{\prime\prime})\|u\|_{D},

where in the last line we used that the bounds (
4.2b
) and (
4.2d
), to estimate the
B_{i}
-term, given in (
4.5
), by

\|B_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|a_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}}\Big\|\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|a_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\Big\|\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}=M_{a}\Big(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\Big\|\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Plugging now (
4.18
) back into (
4.17
), gives

\Big|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle a_{i}(\,\cdot\,+h)^{2}\frac{\partial\Delta_{h}u}{\partial x_{i}},\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}\Big|\leq\Big(\|f\|_{L^{2}}+|s|^{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}}+(3M_{a}^{\prime\prime}+2|s_{0}|)M_{a}\|u\|_{D}\Big)\|v\|_{D}.

With the special choice
v=\Delta_{h}u
, this inequality then becomes

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle a_{i}(\,\cdot\,+h)^{2}\frac{\partial\Delta_{h}u}{\partial x_{i}},\frac{\partial\Delta_{h}u}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}\leq\Big(\|f\|_{L^{2}}+|s|^{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}}+(3M_{a}^{\prime\prime}+2|s_{0}|)M_{a}\|u\|_{D}\Big)\|\Delta_{h}u\|_{D}.

Note that we were allowed to remove the absolute value on the left hand side, since every inner product is nonnegative. Using now also the lower bound (
4.2a
) of the coefficients
a_{i}
, then gives

m_{a}^{2}\|\Delta_{h}u\|_{D}^{2}\leq\Big(\|f\|_{L^{2}}+|s|^{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}}+(3M_{a}^{\prime\prime}+2|s_{0}|)M_{a}\|u\|_{D}\Big)\|\Delta_{h}u\|_{D}.

Simplifying
\|\Delta_{h}u\|_{D}
on both sides of this inequality, we have the desired uniform boundedness (
4.15
). By
[
21
, Lemma 7.24]
this then implies
u\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Now that we know that
u\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we can undo the integration by parts in
[
14
, Eq.(1.8)]
, to rewrite the weak solution as

\langle f,v\rangle_{L^{2}}=q_{s}(u,v)=\langle Q_{s}[\nabla_{a}]u,v\rangle_{L^{2}},\qquad v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Since
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is arbitrary, we conclude that
u
is a strong solution of
Q_{s}[\nabla_{a}]u=f
.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

Let the coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
satisfy the Assumption
4.1
. Then

\operatorname{dom}(\nabla_{a}^{2})=H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

and
\nabla_{a}^{2}
acts as on any
u\in\operatorname{dom}(\nabla_{a}^{2})
as

(4.19)

\nabla_{a}^{2}u=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}e_{i}e_{j}a_{i}\frac{\partial a_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}.

Proof.

It is clear that
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(\nabla_{a}^{2})
. For the inverse inclusion, let
u\in\operatorname{dom}(\nabla_{a}^{2})
. Then with the form (
4.4
) with
s=0
, we can write for every test function
v\in D(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle q_{0}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},a_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}-B_{i}e_{i}v\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle a_{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}(a_{i}v)\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},\Big(B_{i}-e_{i}a_{i}\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}\Big)e_{i}v\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}

(4.20)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle a_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\Big(a_{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\Big),v\Big\rangle_{D^{\prime}\times D}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle e_{i}\Big(B_{i}-e_{i}a_{i}\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}\Big)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},v\Big\rangle_{D^{\prime}\times D},

where in the second last line we used integration by parts in the sense of distributions, since we a priori do not know if
a_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}(a_{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}})\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Using now also the explicit representation

\nabla_{a}^{2}u=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\Big(a_{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\Big)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\Big(B_{i}-e_{i}a_{i}\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}\Big)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

from
[
11
, Eq.(1.6)]
, we can rewrite (
4.20
) as

q_{0}(u,v)=\langle\nabla_{a}^{2}u,v\rangle_{D^{\prime}\times D}=\langle\nabla_{a}^{2}u,v\rangle_{L^{2}},\qquad v\in D(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

Note that in the second equation we again replaced the
D^{\prime}\times D
-dual pairing by the
L^{2}
-inner product, since we know that
\nabla_{a}^{2}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. However, since
D(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is dense in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we can extend this identity to

q_{0}(u,v)=\langle\nabla_{a}^{2}u,v\rangle_{L^{2}},\qquad v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

This means, the function
u
is a weak solution of (
4.14
), with the function
f=\nabla^{2}_{a}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Consequently, Theorem
4.4
then states
u\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

In order to show the explicit action of
\nabla_{a}^{2}
in (
4.19
), let
u\in\operatorname{dom}(\nabla_{a}^{2})=H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Applying (
4.1
) twice onto this function, and using the product rule gives

\displaystyle\nabla_{a}^{2}u

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}a_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}a_{j}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}e_{i}e_{j}a_{i}\Big(\frac{\partial a_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}+a_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}e_{i}e_{j}a_{i}\frac{\partial a_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}^{2}},

where in the last line we used that all summands with
i\neq j
cancel because of the anticommutativity
e_{i}e_{j}=-e_{j}e_{i}
of the Clifford imaginary units.
∎

The last main result which is missing to apply the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus to the gradient, is the injectivity.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let the coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
satisfy the Assumption
4.1
. Then, the gradient operator
\nabla_{a}
in (
4.1
) is injective.

Proof.

We distinguish whether the coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
satisfy Assumption
4.1
I) or II).

In case I), let
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\nabla_{a}u=0
. Then clearly
u\in\operatorname{dom}(\nabla_{a}^{2})
, with
Q_{0}[\nabla_{a}]u=0
. Hence
u
must also be a weak solution of the equation

q_{0}(u,v)=0,\qquad v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

In particular, for the special choice
v=u
, we have

(4.21)

q_{0}(u,u)=0.

However, from equation
[
14
, Eq.(3.7)]
we know that the form
q_{0}
is elliptic, i.e.

(4.22)

\operatorname{Sc}q_{0}(u,u)\geq(m_{a}^{2}-C_{S}M_{a}^{\prime})\|u\|_{D}^{2}.

Combining now (
4.21
) and (
4.22
), gives us
\|u\|_{D}=0
and consequently
u
is constant, which on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can only be the case if
u\equiv 0
.

In case II), let again
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\nabla_{a}u=0
. Using the transformation of variables (
4.8
), as well as the function
\widetilde{u}(y)=u(x)
from (
4.9
), we can write

0=\nabla_{a}u(x)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}a_{i}(x_{i})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}u(x)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}\widetilde{u}(y)=\nabla\widetilde{u}(y).

Since we know that
\nabla
is injective by case I), we conclude that
\widetilde{u}(y)=0
, for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and hence also
u(x)=0
, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
∎

Now we are in the position to combine the above results and prove that the gradient
\nabla_{a}
is an injective bisectorial operator, and hence we are allowed to apply the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus to it.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let the coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
satisfy Assumption
4.1
, and set
K_{a}
as in (
4.6
). Then the gradient operator
\nabla_{a}
in (
4.1
) is densely defined, injective and bisectorial of any angle

(4.23)

\arctan\big(\sqrt{K_{a}^{2}-1}\big)<\omega<\frac{\pi}{2}.

Proof.

The domain
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of
\nabla_{a}
is clearly dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and the injectivity is proven in Proposition
4.6
. In order to show that
\nabla_{a}
is bisectorial, we have to check the Definition
2.2
. To do so, let
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\overline{D_{\omega}}
, i.e.
|\operatorname{Im}(s)|>\tan(\omega)|\operatorname{Sc}(s)|
. By the choice of
\omega
in (
4.23
), this implies

|s|>K_{a}|s_{0}|.

Hence, by Theorem
4.3
and Theorem
4.4
; there exists for every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
a unique solution
u_{f}\in\operatorname{dom}(\nabla_{a}^{2})
, of

Q_{s}[\nabla_{a}]u_{f}=f.

This shows that the operator
Q_{s}[\nabla_{a}]
is bijective, and consequently, we have proven that

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\overline{D_{\omega}}\subseteq\rho_{S}(\nabla_{a}),

which is exactly (
2.3
). Moreover, by (
4.7
), the inverse operator satisfies the estimate

(4.24)

\|Q_{s}[\nabla_{a}]^{-1}f\|_{L^{2}}=\|u_{f}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\Big(\frac{M_{a}}{m_{a}\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{\|f\|_{L^{2}}}{|s|^{2}-K_{a}^{2}s_{0}^{2}}.

Also by (
4.7
), the gradient of the pseudo
S
-resolvent admits the upper bound

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{a}Q_{s}[\nabla_{a}]^{-1}f\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big\|a_{i}\frac{\partial u_{f}}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\|_{L^{2}}\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|a_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}}\Big\|\frac{\partial u_{f}}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|a_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big\|\frac{\partial u_{f}}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(4.25)

\displaystyle\leq M_{a}\Big(\frac{M_{a}}{m_{a}\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{K_{a}|s|\|f\|_{L^{2}}}{m_{a}(|s|^{2}-K_{a}^{2}s_{0}^{2})}.

Combining now (
4.24
) and (
4.25
), we can estimate the the left
S
-resolvent (
2.2
) by

\displaystyle\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,\nabla_{a})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|Q_{s}[\nabla_{a}]^{-1}\overline{s}\|+\|\nabla_{a}Q_{s}[\nabla_{a}]^{-1}\|

(4.26)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{M_{a}}{m_{a}\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\Big(1+\frac{M_{a}K_{a}}{m_{a}}\Big)\frac{|s|}{|s|^{2}-K_{a}^{2}s_{0}^{2}},\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\overline{D_{\omega}}.

For the estimate (
2.4
), let us fix now
\varphi\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\})
, there is
|\operatorname{Im}(s)|\geq\tan(\varphi)|s_{0}|
and consequently

|s|^{2}\geq\big(1+\tan^{2}(\varphi)\big)^{2}s_{0}^{2}.

Taking into account (
4.26
), we then have

\displaystyle\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,\nabla_{a})\|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{M_{a}}{m_{a}\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\Big(1+\frac{M_{a}K_{a}}{m_{a}}\Big)\frac{|s|}{|s|^{2}-K_{a}^{2}\frac{|s|^{2}}{1+\tan^{2}(\varphi)}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{M_{a}}{m_{a}\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\Big(1+\frac{M_{a}K_{a}}{m_{a}}\Big)\frac{1+\tan^{2}(\varphi)}{1+\tan^{2}(\varphi)-K_{a}^{2}}\frac{1}{|s|}.

Finally, let
J\in\mathbb{S}
. We have just established that
J\in\rho_{S}(\nabla_{a})
and, therefore, by
[
15
, Theorem 2.4]
, the operator
\nabla_{a}-\mathcal{I}^{R}J
admits a bounded inverse. In particular,
\nabla_{a}-\mathcal{I}^{R}J
is closed and, since
\mathcal{I}^{R}J
is a bounded operator, it follows that
\nabla_{a}
is also a closed operator.
∎

Next we want to prove that for the gradient operator with coefficients which satisfy the case II) of Assumption
4.1
, the
S
-spectrum is the whole real line.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let the coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
satisfy Assumption
4.1
case II). Then

\sigma_{S}(\nabla_{a})=\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

With the connection (
4.10
) between
u
and
\widetilde{u}
as well as between
v
and
\widehat{v}
, as well as the equivalence (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) of the solvability of
q_{s}
and
\widetilde{q}_{s}
, we conclude from Theorem
4.4
, that for every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
there holds the equivalence

\displaystyle Q_{s}[\nabla_{a}]u=f\quad

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad q_{s}(u,v)=\langle f,v\rangle_{L^{2}},\quad\text{for all }v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\widetilde{q}_{s}(\widetilde{u},\widehat{v})=\langle\widetilde{f},\widetilde{v}\rangle_{L^{2}},\quad\text{for all }\widehat{v}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad Q_{s}[\nabla]\widetilde{u}=\widetilde{f}.

This equivalence proves that
Q_{s}[\nabla_{a}]
is bijective if and only if
Q_{s}[\nabla]
is bijective. Consequently, there is
\sigma_{S}(\nabla_{a})=\sigma_{S}(\nabla)
, and since we already know from
[
7
, Theorem 9.1.1]
that
\sigma_{S}(\nabla)=\mathbb{R}
, the statement of the proposition is proven.
∎

The previous Theorem
4.7
has shown that the gradient operator
\nabla_{a}
is well suited to apply the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus from
[
34
, Definition 5.3]
. In particular, the fractional powers
p_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})
and
q_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})
from Definition
3.1
and Definition
3.12
of the gradient are well defined for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. By its definition in (
4.1
), the gradient operator
\nabla_{a}
consists of partial derivatives which are multiplied with the imaginary units. As a direct consequence of this definition, for every real valued
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, there is

\nabla_{a}f\text{ has values in }\mathbb{R}^{n}:=\big\{s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{n}e_{n}\;\big|\;s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\big\}.

The following theorem proves that the fractional powers
p_{\alpha}
preserve this property and that the fractional powers
q_{\alpha}
is doing the opposite, it gives back a real valued function. This property is for example crucial if we consider the heat equation (
1.5
). Namely, if
p_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})u
is not a vector function, it is not possible to apply the divergence to it.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let the coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
satisfy Assumption
4.1
case II), and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary.

i)

If
u\in\operatorname{dom}(p_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a}))
is real valued, then
p_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})u
has values in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

ii)

If
u\in\operatorname{dom}(q_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a}))
is real valued, then
q_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})u
is real valued as well.

Proof.

Let us start with
q_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})
in ii). First, it is already shown in Theorem
3.27
, that

q_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})=(\nabla_{a}^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}},

where the right hand side is understood as the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus of sectorial operators. Since we have assumed that the coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
satisfy the Assumption
4.1
case II), they only depend on
a_{i}(x)=a_{i}(x_{i})
. In particular, in the explicit action (
4.19
) of
\nabla_{a}^{2}
all the mixed derivatives with
i\neq j
in the first sum vanish, and we get

(4.27)

\nabla_{a}^{2}=-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}a_{i}^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}.

Clearly, if we apply it on a real valued function, it gives back a real valued function.

In order to show the same property for
(\nabla_{a}^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
, let us choose the regularizer

e(s)=\frac{s^{k}}{(1+s)^{2k}},

for some
k>\frac{|\alpha|}{2}
. Then the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus is given by

(4.28)

q_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})=(\nabla_{a}^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}=e(\nabla_{a}^{2})^{-1}\big(es^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big)(\nabla_{a}^{2}).

Starting with the part
\big(es^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big)(T)
, we can use the
\omega
-functional calculus of sectorial operators, to write for every
\varphi\in(0,\pi)
, and any
J\in\mathbb{S}

\big(es^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big)(\nabla_{a}^{2})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial S_{\varphi}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,\nabla_{a}^{2})ds_{J}e(s)s^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}.

As in the proof of
[
34
, Proposition 3.7]
, only considering the path
\gamma_{+}
, we can rewrite this integral as

\big(es^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big)(\nabla_{a}^{2})=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(A(r)x(r)-B(r)y(r)\big)dr,

using the operators and the functions

\displaystyle A(r)

\displaystyle=\big(r\cos(\varphi)-\nabla_{a}^{2}\big)Q_{re^{J\varphi}}[\nabla_{a}^{2}]^{-1},

\displaystyle B(t)

\displaystyle=r\sin(\varphi)Q_{re^{J\varphi}}[\nabla_{a}^{2}]^{-1},

\displaystyle x(r)

\displaystyle=-\cos(\varphi)\operatorname{Im}\big((es^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})(re^{J\varphi})\big)-\sin(\varphi)\operatorname{Sc}\big((es^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})(re^{J\varphi})\big),

\displaystyle y(r)

\displaystyle=\sin(\varphi)\operatorname{Im}\big((es^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})(re^{J\varphi})\big)-\cos(\varphi)\operatorname{Sc}\big((es^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})(re^{J\varphi})\big).

Plugging this representation into (
4.28
) and using the explicit representation of the regularizer
e
, gives

(4.29)

q_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})=\frac{1}{\pi}(1+\nabla_{a}^{2})^{2k}(\nabla_{a}^{2})^{-k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(A(r)x(r)-B(r)y(r)\big)dr.

Since
\nabla_{a}^{2}
in (
4.27
) maps real valued function to real valued functions, all the elements in this representation have the same property. This means, if we apply
q_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})
onto some real valued
u\in\operatorname{dom}(q_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a}))
, then
q_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})u
is again real valued.

Turning now our attention to the mapping properties of
p_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})
in part i) of this theorem, we know from Proposition
4.6
that
\nabla_{a}
is injective. Moreover, since the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is reflexive, because it is a Hilbert space, we get from
[
12
, Theorem 3.3 ii)]
that
\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(\nabla_{a})=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Hence we are allowed to use the power rule for mixed powers in Theorem
3.23
i) and the representation of
p_{1}(\nabla_{a})
in Remark
3.3
, to write

p_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})=\overline{q_{\alpha-1}(\nabla_{a})\nabla_{a}}.

Let now
u\in\operatorname{dom}(p_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a}))
be real valued. Then there exists
(u_{n})_{n}\in\operatorname{dom}\big(q_{\alpha-1}(\nabla_{a})\nabla_{a}\big)
, such that

(4.30)

u=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})u=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}q_{\alpha-1}(\nabla_{a})\nabla_{a}u_{n}.

Note that, according to Lemma
3.21
, the sequence
u_{n}
is explicitly given by

u_{n}:=n^{2m}\nabla_{a}^{2m}(\nabla_{a}^{2}+n^{2})^{-m}\left(\nabla_{a}^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)^{-m}u

where
m
is an integer such that
m\geq\frac{\alpha+1}{2}
; therefore, since it has already been established that
\nabla_{a}^{2}
gives back a real-valued function whenever it is applied to a real-valued function and
u
itself is real-valued, we conclude that each
u_{n}
is real-valued as well. This means that
\nabla_{a}u_{n}
is a pure vector valued function, and using the representation (
4.29
) of
q_{\alpha-1}(\nabla_{a})
, one sees that also
q_{\alpha-1}(\nabla_{a})\nabla_{a}u_{n}
is a vector valued function again. Consequently, also
p_{\alpha}(\nabla_{a})u
in the second limit in (
4.30
) is a pure vector valued function.
∎

5.
Concluding remarks

Quaternionic and Clifford operators are fundamental in mathematics and physics, appearing in quaternionic quantum mechanics
[
1
]
, vector analysis, differential geometry, and hypercomplex analysis.

The application in
vector analysis
is illustrated by the gradient operator with nonconstant coefficients, see also
[
11
]
.

In
differential geometry
, the Dirac operator on a Riemannian manifold is expressed as

\mathcal{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\nabla_{E_{i}}^{\tau},

using covariant derivatives
[
22
]
. Specific examples include the Dirac operators in hyperbolic and spherical spaces, denoted as
\mathcal{D}_{H}
and
\mathcal{D}_{S}
respectively, which are explored in
[
4
]
.

In
hypercomplex analysis
, the standard Dirac operator
D
and its conjugate
\overline{D}
are extensively studied
[
17
,
22
]
. Furthermore, slice hyperholomorphic functions are connected to the kernel of the global operator
G
introduced in
[
10
]
. Recent developments include the Dirac fine structure on the
S
-spectrum, involving operators of the form

T_{\alpha,m}=D^{\alpha}(D\overline{D})^{m}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{T}_{\beta,m}=\overline{D}^{\beta}(D\overline{D})^{m},

which link specific function spaces to functional calculi.
