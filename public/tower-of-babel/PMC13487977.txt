Modelling the impacts of meteorological factors on herpangina: a Bayesian spatiotemporal analysis
Authors: Sophearen Ith, Shu Tamano, Yui Tomo, Fangyu Yan, Yu Takizawa, Kanako Otani, Taro Kamigaki, Motoi Suzuki, Daisuke Yoneoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHerpangina is a common enteroviral illness in young children with recurrent seasonal surges. Although meteorological conditions influence transmission, evidence capturing non-linear and delayed effects across space remains limited. We aimed to quantify the exposure–lag–response associations of temperature, precipitation, and relative humidity with herpangina risk in Japan using a Bayesian spatiotemporal model.MethodsWeekly sentinel surveillance herpangina cases and meteorological variables were obtained for the 47 prefectures from January 2001 to December 2024. We applied a Bayesian hierarchical spatiotemporal model with distributed lag non-linear functions to estimate the associations between meteorological variables and herpangina risk (RR with 95% credible intervals, CrIs).ResultsThe analysis included 2,556,514 herpangina cases. Compared with the overall mean, weekly temperature and relative humidity showed inverted U-shaped associations, with peak risks at approximately 23 °C (RR = 1.57; 95%CrI 1.40–1.75) and 87% (RR = 1.50; 95% CrI 1.40–1.60), respectively. Compared with the minimum-risk level (44 mm), weekly total precipitation exhibited a U-shaped association, with increased risks at both low (2 mm; RR = 1.11; 95% CrI 1.03–1.19) and high levels (106 mm; RR = 1.08; 95% CrI 1.02–1.14).ConclusionsMeteorological conditions were associated with distinct non-linear and lagged effects on herpangina risk. The estimated exposure–lag–response functions provide empirically grounded risk ranges and lead times that can be integrated into routine surveillance to support timely risk communication, paediatric healthcare planning, and targeted prevention in childcare and school settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-01000-x.

Background

Herpangina is an acute viral infection predominantly affecting children, characterised by the sudden onset of fever, sore throat, and vesicular lesions in the posterior oropharynx [1]. Herpangina is most commonly caused by enteroviruses, particularly Coxsackie A viruses, transmitted primarily via the faecal–oral routes, including contact transmission, and respiratory droplets [2]. Although herpangina is generally self-limiting, recurrent seasonal surges can place substantial pressure on paediatric outpatient services and disrupt childcare and educational settings [3].

In East Asia, herpangina and related enteroviral infections exhibit marked seasonality and recurrent epidemics, suggesting an important role of meteorological factors in shaping the transmission dynamics [4, 5]. Temperature may influence viral replication, environmental persistence, and human contact behaviour, while humidity affects viral stability in aerosols and on surfaces [6, 7]. Rainfall may provide a natural reservoir for the virus and facilitate its attachment to suspended particles, thereby further prolonging virus survival time [8, 9].

Previous epidemiological studies have linked meteorological factors to enterovirus-related diseases [7, 10]. In China, temperature demonstrated an inverted V-shaped association with hand-foot-mouth disease, with a peak at 28 °C, whereas relative humidity showed the highest risk at both 20% and above 85% [6, 11]. A non-linear exposure–response relationship has also been observed between daily rainfall and hand-foot-mouth disease, with the highest risk at 21 mm. [12] However, most existing studies focus on short timeframes or single locations, rely on simplified linear assumptions, or do not account for lagged effects and spatial heterogeneity—factors that are particularly relevant for diseases with strong seasonal patterns and marked interannual variability. Moreover, although herpangina and hand-foot-mouth diseases are caused by viruses within the same family, they are not interchangeable outcomes because of differences in clinical presentation, healthcare-seeking behaviour, and dominant circulating strains across settings and over time [13, 14]. Consequently, inferences derived from related enteroviral syndromes may not accurately characterise herpangina-specific risk patterns or temporal dynamics.

In addition to seasonal recurrence, climate change is expected to influence infectious disease risk through changes in pathogen ecology, transmission efficiency, and human exposure [15]. Rising temperatures, increased frequency of extreme heat events, altered precipitation patterns, and shifting humidity regimes may reshape the seasonal timing, geographic distribution, and intensity of enteroviral transmission [10]. This context underscores the importance of long-term analyses that characterise meteorological associations over multiple decades and across diverse climatic zones, thereby providing empirically grounded risk ranges and delayed-effect profiles relevant for public health planning under climate variability and longer-term climatic change.

Japan provides a valuable setting to examine associations between meteorological factors and herpangina, given its long-standing, high-quality nationwide surveillance system and pronounced north-to-south climatic gradients [16, 17]. Herpangina is routinely monitored in Japan and is characterised by recurrent summer epidemics and substantial year-to-year variability [18]. Following the COVID-19 pandemic period, a surge in herpangina incidence and an apparent shift in seasonality were observed, highlighting the need to better understand the impact of meteorological factors on herpangina risk [3]. By integrating long-term meteorological data with nationwide herpangina surveillance data from all 47 prefectures, this study aimed to quantify the non-linear and delayed effects of temperature, precipitation, and relative humidity on herpangina risk using Bayesian spatiotemporal modelling.

Methods

Study design

We employed a retrospective ecological time-series study design to examine the association between meteorological factors and herpangina risk in Japan between 2001 and 2024.

Study area and herpangina data

Japan spans a wide range of climatic zones, from subtropical regions in the south to cool temperate regions in the north. The country comprises 47 prefectures, which can be classified into eight major geographical regions or into 11 distinct climatic regions (Supplementary Fig. S1) [16]. We obtained weekly sentinel-based surveillance case counts and the number of reporting sentinel sites for each prefecture from epidemiological week 1 of 2001 to week 52 of 2024 from the Annual Surveillance Data of the Infectious Disease Weekly Report of the National Institute of Infectious Diseases, Japan Institute for Health Security (Appendix 1, p. 3) [17]. To ensure comparability across prefectures and account for temporal fluctuations in the number of reporting sentinel sites, we calculated the weekly herpangina cases per sentinel site by dividing the total weekly reported cases by the number of reporting sentinel sites in each prefecture.

Meteorological data

Meteorological data were obtained from the fifth-generation European Centre for Medium-range Weather Forecasts atmospheric reanalysis of the global climate (ERA5-Land) [19]. ERA5-Land is a reanalysis data set providing climate information at a fine spatiotemporal resolution (0·1° × 0·1° grid) for the whole global land surface. Hourly data were aggregated into daily mean temperature (°C), mean relative humidity (%), and total precipitation (mm), and then further summarised into weekly prefecture-level (Appendix 1, p. 3). Precipitation often deviates from a normal distribution because frequent no-rain periods produce many near-zero values, while extreme precipitation events create a right-skewed tail. To decrease the model unstability, the value of precipitation was transformed using the natural logarithm: ln(1 + precipitation). The transformed estimates were then back-transformed and reported on the original scale (mm) for interpretability. Pairwise Pearson correlations among all meteorological variables were assessed to evaluate potential collinearity, and no severe collinearity was observed with correlation coefficients below 0.6 (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Spatiotemporal statistical analyses

We applied a Bayesian hierarchical spatiotemporal framework incorporating a distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) to examine the non-linear and delayed effects of each meteorological factor on herpangina risk [20]. The Bayesian spatiotemporal DLNM framework allows flexible estimation of non-linear and delayed associations between meteorological exposures and disease risk while accounting for spatial dependence and temporal variation. This approach is particularly relevant for climate-sensitive infectious diseases, for which meteorological effects may vary across geographic areas and over time. The DLNM uses a cross-basis—a bi-dimensional representation of exposure–lag–response functions—allowing flexible estimation of both the intensity and timing of exposures. A conceptual diagram of the modelling framework and detailed descriptions of the model formulation are provided in supplementary Figure S3 and Appendix 2.

Weekly reported herpangina case counts were assumed to follow a negative binomial distribution to account for potential overdispersion. First, a baseline model was constructed by including spatiotemporal random effects to capture unobserved and unmeasured sources of heterogeneity. Specifically, autocorrelation random effects were modelled using a cyclic first-order random walk (cRW1) to account for seasonality. Likewise, spatial and independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random effects were incorporated via a modified Besag–York–Mollié (mBYM) model to account for dependency structures between prefectures (e.g., shared similar environmental and socioeconomic characteristics and human mobility across neighbouring areas) and unknown and unmeasured factors (e.g., health care disparities) [21]. Spatial effects were replicated yearly to capture unmeasured interannual variability (e.g., shifting outbreaks, dominant viral strains, or reporting practices). The weekly number of reporting sentinel sites was included as an offset to adjust for temporal variation in the reporting sentinel sites.

Then, using the Widely Applicable Information Criterion (WAIC) [22], single-exposure models were evaluated to determine the optimal cross-basis specification for each meteorological factor (Table S1). The selected cross-basis specifications were subsequently incorporated into multi-exposure models (Table S2). The final model included cross-basis functions for mean temperature, mean relative humidity, and the log-transformed total precipitation. Specifically, the exposure–response dimension was modelled using natural cubic splines with three internal knots at the 10th, 75th, and 90th percentiles for temperature and relative humidity, and two internal knots at the 33rd and 66th percentiles for the log-transformed precipitation. The lag–response dimension of each meteorological variable was specified using a natural cubic spline with one internal knot at equally spaced positioned on the log scale over a four-week lag period. A maximum lag of four weeks was selected to capture delayed associations between meteorological conditions and herpangina incidence within the weekly surveillance framework. Although herpangina has a short incubation period of approximately 3–5 days, lagged effects reflect delays from exposure to reporting, secondary transmission within schools, and childcare settings, and the influence of meteorological conditions on viral survival and contact patterns.

Posterior distributions were approximated using Integrated Nested Laplace Approximations (INLA) in R version 4.5.2 [23]. Relative risks (RRs) were derived from posterior mean estimates with 95% posterior credible intervals (CrIs). Statistical significance was evaluated by checking whether the 95% CrI overlapped with the null value. Reference values were set at the average value for weekly mean temperature (13.1 °C) and relative humidity (77%), and at the minimum-risk level for the log-transformed weekly total precipitation (44 mm).

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the selected model, multiple sensitivity analyses were performed. First, the sensitivity of the results to different maximum lag periods (6 weeks and 8 weeks) were examined. Second, alternative specifications of the cross-basis functions at the exposure dimension were evaluated, including two equally spaced knots, the 33rd and 66th percentiles, the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles, and the 10th, 75th and 90th percentiles. Third, models were compared using alternative temperature metrics, including mean weekly minimum and maximum temperatures. Finally, models were re-fitted after excluding data from 2020 to 2024 to account for the potential impact of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Results

The analysis included 2,556,514 herpangina cases reported from 47 prefectures in Japan between 2001 and 2024 (Table 1). The herpangina case per sentinel site was generally higher in southern Japan than in northern regions, with Miyazaki Prefecture recording the highest mean case per sentinel (Fig. 1a). A clear seasonal pattern of herpangina was observed, peaking in summer around epidemiological weeks 26–32, with later peaks in Hokkaido and the Tohoku region, and near year-round activity in Okinawa (Fig. 2). During the study period, the average weekly mean temperature, total precipitation, and mean relative humidity were 13.1 °C, 37 mm, and 77%, respectively (Table 1). Weekly mean temperature exhibited a pronounced latitudinal gradient, decreasing from south to north (Fig. 1b). Higher precipitation was observed in the southern and western regions compared with the northern region, while relative humidity was greater in the north than in the south (Fig. 1c, d). Distinct seasonal patterns of each meteorological variable were also observed across climate regions (Supplementary Fig. S5–S7).

Association between weekly mean temperature and herpangina

Figure 3a shows that the overall cumulative association between weekly mean temperature and herpangina risk followed an inverted U-shaped pattern, peaking at around 23 °C (86th percentile; RR = 1.57; 95% CrI 1.40–1.75). Compared with the average weekly mean temperature at 13.1 °C (48th percentile), extreme cold temperature at the 5th percentile (− 0.9 °C) was associated with a lower risk (RR = 0.63; 95% CrI 0.56–0.70), whereas extreme high temperature at the 95th percentile (25.8 °C) was associated with an increased risk (RR = 1.36; 95% CrI 1.21–1.54) (Table 2). Extreme cold was associated with reduced risk across all lags, while extreme high temperature increased risk for up to 3 weeks, peaking at a 2-week lag (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. S10). Association between weekly mean relative humidity and herpangina

The overall cumulative association between weekly mean relative humidity and herpangina risk exhibited an inverted U-shaped pattern, with the highest risk observed at approximately 87% (92th percentile; RR = 1.50; 95% CrI 1.40–1.60) (Fig. 5a). Compared with the average weekly mean relative humidity (77%; 48th percentile), extreme low relative humidity at the 5th percentile (65%) was associated with reduced risk (RR = 0.67; 95% CrI 0.62–0.71), whereas extreme high relative humidity at the 95th percentile (88%) was associated with increased risk (RR = 1.48; 95% CrI 1.38–1.60). Extreme low relative humidity was associated with reduced herpangina risk across all lags, while extreme high relative humidity increased risk across all lags, peaking at a 2-week lag (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. S10). Sensitivity analysis results

Sensitivity analyses indicated that the selected model was generally robust. Allowing longer maximum lag periods of six and eight weeks produced shapes similar to those in the main analysis (Supplementary Fig. S12 and S13). Altering the cross-basis specification at the exposure dimension yielded comparable association patterns for all meteorological variables (Supplementary Fig. S14–S16). However, when using two equally spaced knots or knots placed at the 33rd and 66th percentiles, the magnitude of the temperature effect was attenuated, while increased risk at the higher end was observed for relative humidity. Using minimum or maximum temperatures instead of mean temperature produced similar shapes, with the highest cumulative risk observed at 20 °C and 27 °C, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S17). Sensitivity analyses restricted to the pre-COVID-19 period yielded results consistent with the main analysis, except for a decreased risk at the extreme low precipitation and an increased risk at the high end of relative humidity (Supplementary Fig. S18).

Discussion

This nationwide spatiotemporal analysis of 24 years of weekly surveillance data shows that herpangina risk in Japan is associated with distinct non-linear and delayed effects of key meteorological conditions. Temperature and relative humidity displayed inverted U-shaped associations, with peak risks at approximately 23 °C and 87%, respectively, whereas precipitation showed a U-shaped relationship, with increased risk at both low and high levels. Importantly, these associations were not confined to the same week: the effects of elevated temperature persisted for up to three weeks (strongest at 1–2-week lags), those of high relative humidity persisted for up to four weeks (strongest at a 2-week lag), and precipitation exerted its impact across all the lags. By integrating distributed lag non-linear functions within a Bayesian spatiotemporal framework, this study provides empirically grounded risk ranges and lead-time profiles that can be incorporated into routine surveillance to support better-timed risk communication and anticipatory planning for paediatric service demand during seasonal surges. Although derived from Japan, the long-term and geographically comprehensive design suggests that the observed exposure–lag–response patterns may also be relevant to other East Asian settings with recurrent herpangina epidemics under similar climatic conditions. However, the generalisability to other East Asian settings should be interpreted cautiously because surveillance systems, healthcare-seeking behaviour, reporting practices, population immunity, circulating strain composition, and local climatic conditions may differ across countries.

An inverted U-shaped relationship was observed between weekly mean temperature and herpangina risk, with the highest risk occurring at approximately 23 °C. Direct comparison with previous studies is challenging due to limited evidence specific to herpangina. Nevertheless, similar non-linear climate-related associations have been reported for enterovirus-related diseases in East Asian settings, including China, Hong Kong, and South Korea [7, 24]. Mechanistically, the increased risk associated with rising temperature may reflect environmental conditions that favour enterovirus survival and transmission. Moderately warm temperatures may provide optimal conditions for viral persistence on surfaces and facilitate efficient person-to-person transmission, whereas extremely high temperatures may reduce viral viability through heat sensitivity, increased ultraviolet exposure, and accelerated environmental degradation [25]. Another plausible explanation is that warm temperatures may increase outdoor activities, childcare interactions, and social mixing among children, thereby increasing opportunities for viral transmission [26]. Conversely, extremely high temperatures may increase indoor cooling use or reduce outdoor activity, potentially altering transmission dynamics [26]. From a public health perspective, these findings suggest a potential threshold and lead time for healthcare preparedness during warm periods, particularly one to two weeks after favourable temperature conditions.

Weekly total precipitation and herpangina risk showed a U-shaped association, with minimum risk observed at approximately 44 mm. Although the estimated effect size for precipitation was smaller than those observed for temperature and relative humidity, precipitation may still provide useful epidemiological information for understanding herpangina transmission as even modest risks may be relevant at the population level when rainfall-related exposure conditions are widespread. Consistent with previous evidence, a modest increased risk was observed at both low and high rainfall across the lag periods [12, 27]. This pattern suggests distinct mechanisms at the extremes of the precipitation distribution. During dry periods, increased outdoor activity and intensified social mixing among children may increase contact opportunities, thereby raising transmission risk [28]. At the other extreme, extremely high rainfall often coincides with the seasonal weather disruptions, including the typhoon season [29]. Such conditions may shift activities indoors, increase close-contact mixing, and reduce ventilation, thereby facilitating viral transmission and accelerating observed case counts within the same week. However, these behavioural mechanisms were not directly measured in this study. Future studies incorporating behavioural, childcare, and indoor environmental data, as well as examining extreme weather events such as typhoons, would be valuable for clarifying the pathways linking meteorological conditions to herpangina transmission.

Relative humidity also exhibited an inverted U-shaped association with herpangina risk, peaking around 87%, and showed a prolonged influence persisting for up to four weeks. These findings align with evidence suggesting increased enteroviral disease risk under high-humidity conditions, although the humidity level at which risk peaks varies across studies and settings [6, 30]. Interpretation of the independent effect of relative humidity is complex, as the observed association may reflect the combined influence of humid and warm environments that are favour viral transmission rather than the isolated effect of relative humidity alone [30]. Under warm conditions, higher relative humidity may enhance the environmental stability of enteroviruses and promote their persistence on surfaces, increasing the likelihood of indirect transmission [25]. The prolonged effect observed suggests that relative humidity may contribute to maintaining environmental conditions conducive to sustained enteroviral circulation. From an operational perspective, this extended humidity-associated window indicates that persistent humid conditions may sustain elevated risk across multiple weeks and contribute to prolonged seasonal waves.

Although COVID-19 pandemic-related disruptions may have been partially captured by the year-specific random effects included in the main model, residual confounding due to these temporal disruptions cannot be completely excluded. The pandemic may have disrupted the usual seasonal circulation of enteroviruses and influenced both the magnitude and timing of reported herpangina incidence. Related changes, including altered contact patterns among children, changes in school or childcare attendance, enhanced infection control practices, and changes in healthcare-seeking behaviour, may have affected transmission dynamics and reporting patterns during 2020 to 2024.

This study has several limitations. First, the use of sentinel-based surveillance data may limit the representativeness of the true herpangina burden. In Japan, the number of sentinel sites is determined based on population size. However, as the actual establishment of sentinel sites is left to the discretion of prefectural governments, variation in the number of sentinel sites per population may occur across prefectures. Herpangina case data were derived from a passive surveillance system, which depends on healthcare-seeking behaviour and reporting practices. Consequently, mild or asymptomatic infections may be underreported, potentially leading to underestimation of associated risks. Second, herpangina cases were primarily diagnosed clinically rather than by laboratory confirmation, potentially leading to misclassification with other enteroviral syndromes, including hand-foot-mouth disease, which may present with similar clinical features. Third, this study estimated the effects of individual meteorological variables; however, these factors are interrelated, strongly seasonal, and often co-occur. Although the multi-exposure model included temperature, relative humidity, and precipitation simultaneously, and no severe pairwise collinearity was observed, these variables may still share overlapping temporal patterns, making it challenging to fully disentangle their independent effects. Consequently, the observed associations may partly reflect combined, interacting, or seasonally correlated effects rather than the fully isolated effect of a single exposure. The estimated associations should therefore be interpreted cautiously as conditional associations within the fitted model. Fourth, residual temporal confounding from unmeasured seasonal drivers may remain despite the inclusion of temporal random effects, as factors such as school calendars, circulating strain dynamics, behavioural changes, and non-pharmaceutical interventions could influence the temporal patterns of herpangina transmission. Fifth, the use of reanalysis meteorological data may have introduced exposure misclassification, as these gridded estimates may not fully capture local meteorological variability, especially in regions with complex topography or limited observational data. Future research should examine joint effects and project herpangina risk under different climate change scenarios to inform early warning systems, public health preparedness, and targeted prevention strategies.

Conclusions

Temperature, precipitation, and relative humidity are associated with herpangina risk, with distinct lagged effects. These findings provide insight into potential lead times for implementing targeted control measures and preparing healthcare systems for anticipated increases in herpangina incidence under favourable meteorological conditions. As climate change is expected to influence the transmission dynamics of infectious diseases, this study highlights the importance of integrating climate monitoring into herpangina surveillance to support timely preventive action, efficient resource allocation, and improved healthcare preparedness.
