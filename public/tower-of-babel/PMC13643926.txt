Variation in microbial and plant associations across wild bee families, functional groups, and urban environments

Katherine D Chau, Trevor J L Sless, Phuong N Nguyen, Sandra M Rehan, Paolina Garbeva

Abstract

Bees harbour diverse microbiomes and floral associations, which may shift in response to several biological and environmental factors. We examined the diversity of bacterial, fungal, and plant associates (i.e. from foraging or nesting material) across 32 bee species from five families (Apidae, Megachilidae, Halictidae, Colletidae, and Andrenidae) using 16S rRNA, ITS1, and rbcL amplicon sequencing. The included bee species represent multiple axes of variation across functional traits including body size, nesting behaviour, sociality, diet, and native status, and response to urbanization. Apidae, Colletidae, and Andrenidae bee families had richer communities of bacterial and plant associates than Halictidae and Megachilidae, while fungal diversity and composition were similar across families. Common microbes and plant associations were detected across the bee community, but certain taxa were common only to specific bee species. Plant associations more often shifted significantly in diversity depending on functional trait and urban intensity, whereas bacterial and fungal diversity were largely consistent across bee families, functional traits, and urban intensities. Our findings show that while microbial communities are fairly stable across bee lineages and functional groups, bee-associated plant communities vary with urbanization and are the primary factor differentiating bee species in urban landscapes.
Microbial communities are stable across bee lineages and functional groups, bee-associated plants are sensitive to environmental variation and serve as a primary axis that differentiates bee species in urban landscapes.

Background

With over 20 000 described species spanning seven families, bees (Hymenoptera: Anthophila) are an incredibly diverse group of insects that provide essential pollination services which contribute to global food security (Khalifa et al. ). Bees exhibit remarkable ecological and evolutionary diversity, encompassing multiple distinct functional guilds that reflect the unique roles bees play in many ecosystems. Correspondingly, bees possess diverse microbiomes (e.g. bacteria and fungi that reside within or on the host) which can vary according to the host’s taxonomic affinities, their social behaviour, or even their ecological niche (Kwong et al. , Hammer et al. ). Increasing evidence show that bee-associated microbial communities influence host health and fitness, thus, understanding the factors that structure these microbiomes has become an important area of research. Acquisition of microbes differs greatly depending on various characteristics of bees. For example, the social behaviour of bees has been shown to impact acquisition of microbes, such that eusocial bee microbiomes are influenced primarily by social interactions with colony members (Kwong and Moran ), whereas solitary and subsocial bee microbiomes are shaped by interactions with the environment (McFrederick and Rehan , Dew et al. ). Owing to their reduced exposure with conspecifics, solitary bees host more diverse bacteria compared to their eusocial counterparts and their microbiomes are typically dominated by environmental symbionts found in soil or flowers (Thamm et al. , Hettiarachchi et al. ).

Convergently evolved characteristics allow certain bee species to exploit niche plant resources and maximize their nutritional gain. For example, buzz pollination helps extract inaccessible pollen from flowers with poricidal anthers and evolved almost 45 times independently in bee genera in almost all bee families, thus leading to plant associations due to foraging on plant families with poricidal anthers such as Solanaceae, Melastomataceae, and Fabaceae (Cardinal et al. , Mesquita-Neto et al. ). Some bee species are more specialized (i.e. pollen specialist) and have reduced plant associations as a result of exclusively feeding on specific flower families or genera. These relationships have critical implications for maintaining healthy bee populations, especially for specialists when their limited nutritional resources are more easily altered by habitat changes (Anderson et al. ).

In addition to sociality and diet, other bee traits such as body size, nesting behaviour, and whether the bee is native or introduced also contribute to variation of microbiota and floral associations. The influence of body size of the bee species on microbial communities remains understudied, but some studied suggest that body size could play a role in shaping microbial communities. For instance, in some regions larger bee species were found to have a more specialized diet (Raiol et al. ), and would therefore have reduced floral associations than the smaller, generalist bee species. In contrast, larger bees are capable of foraging longer distances and, in some cases, will forage farther for better floral rewards (Greenleaf et al. ), thus increasing their contact with diverse floral resources and environmental microbes. Differences in nesting substrate may also shape microbial acquisition, as ground-nesting bees are exposed to soil-associated microbes, while cavity nesting bees are exposed to plant-associated microbes either through the phyllosphere (i.e. exposed plant tissue) or endosphere (i.e. internal plant tissue) (Turner et al. ). Variation within the nesting substrate, such as differences in soil physicochemical properties or type of plant nesting material, also impact the microbial community composition and abundance (de Sousa , Bogati et al. ). Native status of bees also influences the susceptibility and tolerance of microbial exposure as well as the preference of foraging patterns. Introduced bees have not coevolved with the native flora nor native environmental symbionts, and would thus harbor a different microbiome composition and plant-association compared to native species in the same area (Pei et al. ). Native status was also found to impact the prevalence of parasitic microbes in bees in which introduced bee species (often managed species) act as parasite reservoirs, transmitting parasitic microbes onto plants that spillover onto native bee populations, often as a detriment to the native species (Melanson et al. , Motta et al. ).

Bee functional traits further interact with varying degrees of urbanization to alter host–taxon relationships. Major land use shifts caused by human activity disrupt natural bee habitats and native floral resources and show no signs of slowing down (Matteson et al. , Ayers and Rehan ). Nonetheless, certain traits can increase bee species’ robustness against the negative effects of urbanization. Some studies have generalized that small-bodied bees, cavity-nesters, social bees, generalists, and introduced bee species thrive better in cities due to their greater tolerance to urban phenomena, such as elevated temperatures, phenological mismatch, and reduced or altered plant diversity (Fitch et al. , Wilson and Jamieson , Stemkovski et al. , Ferrari et al. ). Thus, it is no surprise that urbanization impacts bee microbiomes and bee foraging preferences, since urbanization itself imposes stringent environmental filtering for bees with disadvantaged traits. In some instances, urbanization increased microbial richness in urban bees but with an increased risk of exposure to potential pathogenic microbes (Chau et al. , Nguyen and Rehan ). In other instances, increasing urbanization decreases bacterial diversity in bee larvae (Peters et al. ).

Change in urban floral diversity alters bee–plant networks, which may lead to reduced bee abundance in cities for certain species whereas other species may exploit novel resources and better tolerate urbanization (Ayers and Rehan , Brasil et al. ). Host–microbiota and host–plant interactions across bee families and bee traits serve as an important framework for highlighting ecological relationships that shape bee health and their resilience to environmental change.

Utilizing bee functional traits to understand how bee microbiomes and their preferred plant resources vary has been useful to discern trait-based patterns observed in an urban context (Wilson and Jamieson , Ostwald et al. ). Here, we characterize the bacterial and fungal microbiome, and plant associations for bee species from five different bee families (Apidae, Megachilidae, Andrenidae, Colletidae, and Halictidae) sampled across an urban gradient and collected bees varying in functional traits. This study has two aims, with our first to assess the diversity of bacteria, fungi, and plant associates across bee species and bee functional traits, with the goal of determining both shared taxa among bees and unique taxa associated with specific bee species or functional traits. We predicted that taxa previously identified as common in bees such as the bacteria Apilactobacillus (Todorov et al. ), yeasts such as Starmerella and Metschnikowia (Rutkowski et al. ), or highly abundant plant genera in our sample region such as Trifolium will be shared across all our sampled bee species and traits. We also predicted that there will be host-specific and trait-specific taxa—taxa that are not as prevalent in the study but would be abundant in certain bee species or distinct functional traits—that contribute to variation in bee microbiome communities and plant-associate compositions. Second, we sought to determine how urbanization impacts the microbiome and plant associations across bee species and functional traits. Based on previous research, we expect increasing urbanization to decrease plant associations across bee species due to decreased floral resources, with a greater impact on specialists due to their restricted foraging patterns in urban environments.

Methods

Sample sites and bee sampling

We collected bees from 2022 to 2023 during late spring to late summer (May–September), sampling between 10:00 and 16:00. We established sampling across a gradient of urbanization levels within the Greater Toronto Area, Canada by selecting sites that covered a broad range of impervious surfaces within a 500 m radius buffer zone, which included ‘high’ urban sites (>80% impervious surface coverage), ‘medium’ urban sites (40%–80% impervious surface coverage), and ‘low’ urban sites (<40% impervious surface coverage). We calculated the impervious surface coverage using the Southern Ontario Land Resource Information System (SOLRIS) version 3.0 dataset (https://www.arcgis.com/home/item.html?id=0279f65b82314121b5b5ec93d76bc6ba) within QGIS version 3.36–2-Maidenhead (QGIS.org ). Impervious surfaces included transportation and built-up area-impervious. Other land type features included open water (open water), pervious surfaces (pervious built-up, tallgrass woodland, forest, coniferous forest, mixed forest, deciduous forest, treed swamp, thicket swamp, and hedge rows), and unclassified (undifferentiated). In total, 17 sites were chosen (6 high, 6 medium, and 5 low) (Fig. , ). We collected bees from flowers via hand-netting and stored them in cryotubes in a cooler while in the field. We identified bees with keys (Mitchell , , Ascher and Pickering ) prior to flash-freezing them in liquid nitrogen for long-term storage at −80°C. Bee specimen species as well as their functional traits were recorded (see ). We used the mean of the lower and upper female body size ranges obtained from literature to determine bee species’ body sizes; species are classified as ‘small’ if the mean length was 2–6 mm, ‘medium’ if the mean length was >6–10 mm, or ‘large’ if the mean length was >10 mm. Additional classifications included nesting behaviour (ground-nesters or cavity-nesters, which include stem nesting and snail shell nesting bee species), diet (generalists or specialists), sociality [solitary, subsocial (the mother remains at the nest and exhibit extended parental care), or eusocial], and native status (native or introduced).

DNA extraction and sequencing

We homogenized whole bee specimens, to ensure we captured all potential microbial and plant DNA that interact with the bee (internally and externally), using mortar and pestle and a homogenizer with ~0.1 ml of glass beads and one 1 mm steel bead. Extraction of DNA extraction followed the Qiagen DNeasy Blood & Tissue (catalogue number 69506) for purification of total DNA from whole-body animal tissue (DNeasy 96 protocol). DNA concentration was checked with a QuBit Fluorometer prior to sequencing. Library preparation, sequencing, and demultiplexing was done at the Canadian Centre for DNA Barcoding (Guelph, Canada). We included blank samples, which consisted of wells filled only with sterile water. We left the last well (H12) of our submitted plates empty as an additional control.

Paired-end amplicon sequencing was performed using Illumina MiSeq targeting 300 bp regions for three loci: the 16S rRNA gene (hereafter 16S) to identify bacteria, the internal transcribed spacer (hereafter ITS1) for fungi, and the RuBisCo large subunit gene (hereafter rbcL) to amplify plant DNA. We used the 16S forward primer 799F-mod3 (CMGGATTAGATACCCKGG) and the reverse primer 1115R (AGGGTTGCGCTCGTTG) to amplify the V5–V6 region for bacteria, the ITS1F forward primer (CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA) and ITS2 reverse primer (GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC) to amplify the ITS1 region in fungi, and the rbcL7 forward primer (CTCCTGAMTAYGAAACCAAAGA) and rbcL8 reverse primer (GTAGCAGCGCCCTTTGTAAC) for rbcL7 fragment amplification. Samples were sequenced in triplicates, and all library preparation, sequencing, and demultiplexing of reads was completed by the Canadian Centre for DNA Barcoding (Guelph, Canada) and were provided as raw FASTQ reads for further analysis.

Taxonomy classification and decontamination

We checked quality of the raw reads and trimmed them using the DADA2 pipeline (Callahan et al. ) in Qiime2 version 2023.9 (Bolyen et al. ). Reads were trimmed if Phred quality scores were below 40, and reads were truncated to keep lengths of 249 bp for 16S and 150 bp for rbcL. Due to variable lengths of ITS1 in fungi, we trimmed ITS1 adaptors using cutadapt version 4.9 (Martin ), following the DADA2 1.8 workflow for ITS sequences (https://benjjneb.github.io/dada2/ITS_workflow.html). We did not prefilter reads to remove sequences with ambiguous bases to maintain as many reads as possible and used the suggested minimum length of 50 bp to remove spurious low-length reads. In total, we obtained 9185 amplicon sequene variants (ASVs) for bacteria, 10 629 ASVs for fungi (read length range 52–474 bp, mean length of 220.52 bp), and 3214 ASVs for plants. Next, we obtained feature tables containing ASV sequences and abundances for each gene, with ASV sequences used for taxonomy classification via blastn version 2.14.1 (Altschul et al. ) and the core nucleotide core_nt database (last updated 23 September 2024). We kept the top 10 alignment hits for each gene using 10 maximum targeted sequences (–max_target_seqs 10). We then extracted the taxonomy identifiers (i.e. ‘taxids’) for all blast hits and processed them through Entrez E-utilities via efetch (version 15.3) to obtain full lineage information for each taxid (Sayers ). Simultaneously, the SILVA (version 138 full length sequences with 99% sequence similarity) database was used for taxonomy classification of bacterial ASVs (Quast et al. , Yilmaz et al. , Glöckner et al. ), the UNITE v9.0 database (version 18.07.2023 with global and 3% distance singletons) for fungal ASV classification (Abarenkov et al. ), and the nondereplicated rbcL database for plant ASV classification (Dubois et al. ). The majority of the hits for bacteria and fungi were labelled as ‘unclassified’ or ‘uncultured’ but had identical % identity and e-value scores to other taxonomy hits with full lineage information, so we filtered out these hits from our output. We only kept ‘unclassified’ and ‘uncultured’ hits if they at least maintained taxonomy identification up to family level. Next, to determine the most likely taxonomy up to genus level for each ASV, we used the remaining top alignment hits to identify the most common taxonomic classification. In cases where there were multiple likely classifications, the classification with the highest e-value score was used, followed by the highest % identity. If the e-value and % identities were identical for multiple top classifications, ASVs were classified up to the lowest common ancestor. Due to high purifying selection of the rbcL gene, discrimination of vascular plants is often poorer resulting in identical hits for many diverse plant species (Braukmann et al. ). Thus, we performed a filtering process by eliminating hits that identified a plant species, genus, or family which is not found in Ontario. To do this, we created a reference database by extracting all plant species (vascular and nonvascular) found in Ontario using the NatureServe Explorer database (3987 species records obtained) (NatureServe ). We also extracted all plant species found in Ontario from the Database of Vascular Plants of Canada (VASCAN; 3153 species records obtained) (Brouillet et al. ) to ensure we did not miss Ontario plants due to plant name synonyms which could occur. We finally used the Qiime2 databases to corroborate blast identifications or classify any ASVs that did not produce a blast hit or full taxonomic classification.

Next, we further filtered the bacterial, fungal, and plant datasets by entirely removing ASVs identified to be a true contaminant in blank samples using phyloseq (McMurdie and Holmes ) and decontam (Davis et al. ) packages in R version 4.2.1 (R Core Team ) using the prevalence method with a threshold of 0.1. If an ASV was significantly detected as a contaminant in a blank, that ASV was removed. Additionally, ASVs matching to domains not belonging to the gene were removed. For example, in bacteria we removed ASVs classified to Eukaryota. We also removed environmentally ubiquitous symbionts such as Sodalis, Arsenophonus, Rickettsia, Spiroplasma, and Wolbachia (Gerth et al. , Graystock et al. , Sless et al. ), and family classified to ‘Mitochondria’ or ‘Chloroplast’. For fungi, we removed ASVs classified to Bacteria or belonged to nonfungal phyla. For plants, we removed ASVs if they matched to phyla other than Streptophyta. After removal of blank samples and sample replicates with 0 reads, remaining sample replicates were merged together by sample and ASVs with abundances below a relative threshold abundance of 0.01% within a sample was converted to 0. Finally, we removed ASVs or samples with 0 total abundance, resulting in 6423 bacterial ASVs in 502 samples, 5694 fungal ASVs in 506 samples, and 2785 plant ASVs in 506 samples.

Diversity analyses

For alpha and beta diversity analyses, we used both the regular ASV datasets and rarefied versions. We rarefied each sample to a minimum depth of 1000 reads using the rarefy_even_depth function from the phyloseq package. After rarefying, this resulted in 331 bacterial samples with 3620 ASVs, 506 fungal samples with 5694 ASVs, and 505 plant samples with 3309 ASVs. We compared diversity across bee species, bee genera, families, bee functional traits (bee body size, nesting behaviour, sociality, diet, and native status) and urbanization levels. First, to determine if taxa richness varied significantly across sample groups, we calculated alpha diversity using the Shannon–Wiener diversity metric in the vegan R package (Oksanen et al. ). Next, a Shapiro–Wilk test via the shapiro.test function checked for normality in alpha diversity. For nonparametric analyses, we used Kruskal–Wallis testing via kruskal.test followed by Dunn post hoc testing with dunnTEST from the fsa package with Bonferroni correction (Ogle et al. ) for significant Kruskal–Wallis tests. Otherwise, we performed parametric tests with ANOVA using the aov function followed by Tukey’s Honest Significance Difference tests using HSD.test from the R package agricolae (de Mendiburu ) for significant ANOVA results. To test if taxa composition varied significantly across sample groups, we calculated beta diversity using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity on the full ASV and rarefied datasets that we first transformed using the ‘Hellinger’ method from the decostand function within vegan, followed by creating a distance matrix using vegdist with method set to ‘bray’. Next, we used PERMANOVA using the adonis2 method from vegan to check for significant differences in microbiome and plant composition for the different bee species, genera, families, traits, and urban intensities. This was followed by checks for beta dispersion using anova with betadisper; if ANOVA was significant, post hoc testing was done using Tukey HSD. To visualize Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, we created principal coordinate analysis plots by extracting the eigenvalues from weighted classical multidimensional scaling (wcmdscale function from vegan) to preserve Euclidean distances. Due to similar results between rarefied and non-rarefied datasets, the results and discussion will focus on results from the nonrarefied datasets.

Next, we merged ASVs with identical taxonomy classifications to assess differences in microbiome and plant composition at the taxon genus level. Prior to this, we removed ASVs not assigned to genus level to ensure focus on well classified taxa. For bacterial ASVs classified with the SILVA database, genera or families labelled with database codes (e.g. AD3) instead of being blank were treated as unique genus or family names to maintain as many unique taxonomy lineages as possible. To determine which taxa contributed the most to observed differences between groups, we implemented a similarity percentage (SIMPER) analysis using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity in the PAST program (Hammer et al. ). This analysis calculated a contribution value for each taxon to the overall dissimilarity between groups e.g. bee species, bee genus, bee family, bee functional traits, and urban intensity). To identify taxa shared across all analyzed groups or taxa associated to a particular group, we extracted bacteria, fungi, and plants using the genus merged datasets if they had ≥1.0% relative abundance and ≥50% sample prevalence within the sample type. We used the thresholds discussed in Graystock et al. () to classify a core taxon for a sample type (e.g. samples belonging to a specific bee species).

Taxa overrepresentation and co-expression

We incorporated random forest classification (RFC) to determine if bacterial, fungal, or plant compositions can predict bee species, bee genus, bee family, bee functional trait, or urban intensity. To do this, we used the R package randomforest (Liaw and Wiener ) for nine different training set sizes ranging from 10% to 90%, repeating the model 10 times to obtain an average accuracy. We used the genus merged datasets with unidentified genera removed and ran the tuneRF method to determine optimal ‘mtry’ values for each dataset—which calculates the minimum number of taxa to select to minimize error rates—for each dataset for 5000 runs (ntree = 5000). We used the caret package (Kuhn ) to generate a confusion matrix which summarized the RFC model performance, and we used the package randomforestexplainer (Paluszynska et al. ) to extract the top 10 important features (i.e. taxa) contributing to the RFC model accuracy. We focused on the top 10 taxa for all runs using the 80% training set size as using an 80/20 split for training/testing is standard practise and offers enough data for model training and predicting (Gholamy et al. ). Taxa that showed up all 10 times as an important feature were considered highly influential for the RFC model and likely play a role in taxa community differences for the analysed group. Finally, to assess if bees with specific functional traits experienced significant shifts in taxa alpha diversity with increasing urbanization, we implemented mixed-effects models. We used the lmer function from the lme4 package (Bates et al. ), keeping sites as the random effect and urban intensity as an interacting factor with each functional trait. We included bee species, bee genus, and bee family as a fixed effect to account for natural microbial or plant association variation across bee taxonomic groups and repeated this model for each functional trait individually to avoid multicollinearity.

Results

Sequencing and sample output

A total of 506 bees were collected from low (222 individuals), medium (226), and high (58) urban intensities. This included bees from five families encompassing 16 genera and 32 species, with the most specimens belonging to Halictidae (130 individuals; 8 species), then Megachilidae (122; 7), Apidae (111; 9), Colletidae (76; 3), and the least from Andrenidae (67; 5). All bee species were sampled at all levels of urban intensity except for andrenid or colletid individuals which were not found in high urban intensities (Fig. , and ). All bee functional groups were collected at each urban intensity except small bee species, which were absent from high urban intensities (Fig. , and ).

Amplicon sequencing resulted in a total of 35 526 785 reads for 16S (average 20 560 reads/sample replicate), 33 900 993 reads for ITS1 (average 19 630 reads/sample replicate), and 61 503 775 reads for rbcL (average 35 613 reads/sample replicate). Average read count of ASVs after filtering and using a 0.01% relative abundance threshold was 5555 reads/sample replicate for bacteria (8 300 320 reads; ), 19 111 reads/sample replicate for fungi (28 991 482 read; ), and 31 966 reads/sample replicate for plants (48 519 958 reads; ). Merging of ASVs by genus resulted in 598 bacterial genera (292 families + 48 unclassified families), 830 fungal genera (308families + 33 unclassified families), and 252 plant genera (89 families + 5 unclassified families) (, respectively).

Microbiome and plant diversity across bee taxonomic groups and traits

Across all bee taxonomic levels, bacterial alpha diversity significantly differed across all levels whereas fungal alpha diversity only differed at the bee species level (). At the family level, Apidae and Andrenidae had significantly richer bacterial microbiomes compared to Halictidae (Kruskal–Wallis ꭓ2 = 31.2, df = 4, P < .001; Fig. , ). However, bacterial richness varied greatly within families at the genus level, where instead the megachilid genus Heriades showed the highest bacterial richness, which was significantly greater when compared to the other megachilid genera Megachile, Anthidium, and Pseudoanthidium (, ). Even within genera there was high variation, with Heriades leavitti exhibiting the highest bacterial richness (mean Shannon index = 2.7) whereas H. carinata had the lowest (mean Shannon index = 0.06) (). Fungal diversity was similar across bee families (Fig. , ) and bee genera (, ) but showed variation in species even within the same genus (, ). For example, Andrena helianthi had significantly greater fungal diversity compared to all other Andrena species (). There were significant differences in bacterial and fungal diversity at all bee taxonomic levels, however, composition was largely similar across bee groups (Fig. , and , ). Differences in microbial communities were attributed to within-group variation of taxon compositions (, ).

Examination of bees by functional trait resulted in significantly higher bacterial alpha diversity higher in subsocial and native bees when compared to eusocial and introduced species (, ). Fungal diversity was instead found to be significantly higher in large bee species and solitary species when compared to medium-sized species and subsocial species (, ). Beta diversity of bacteria and fungi was significant across all examined functional traits, but clustering was only evident for a portion of solitary bees for bacteria ().

Plant diversity differed at all bee taxonomic levels, with plant diversity significantly richer in Apidae compared to Andrenidae, Colletidae, and Megachilidae (Kruskal–Wallis ꭓ2 = 78.1, df = 4, P < .001; Fig. , ). The halictid species Augochlorella aurata had the highest plant diversity (mean Shannon index = 2.7) whereas the colletid Hylaeus modestus had the lowest (mean Shannon index = 0.58; ). Plant beta diversity significantly differed across all bee taxonomic groups (), with a portion of Megachilidae forming a cluster (Fig. ), which consisted of Megachile rotundata and Anthidium oblongatum ( and ). Colletidae species and Andrenidae species also formed distinct clusters due to highly diverse plant associations (). For instance, a portion of Andrenidae were highly associated with the plant genera Xanthium and Tragopogon, whereas Colletidae specimens were dominated by either Frangula or Daucus. Halictidae and Apidae were quite varied in their plant associations, whereas a large majority of Megachilidae were associated with Lotus (). By bee functional trait, plant diversity significantly differed in all groups except for diet, where there were no differences in plant richness between specialists and generalists (, ). Instead, plant richness was significantly higher in large and medium-sized bees, ground nesters, subsocial and eusocial bees, and native bees compared to small bee species, cavity-nesters, solitary bees, and introduced species. Plant composition within bees significantly differed across all examined groups with pronounced clustering between native and introduced bees (, ).

Across all bee samples, we identified 13 common (i.e. ≥1% abundance and ≥50% prevalence across the sample group) microbes and plant associates; two bacteria, five fungi, and six plants (). Bacteria Apilactobacillus and Acinetobacter occurred in 72% and 62% of all bee samples respectively and made up 31% and 15% of all bacterial reads (). Together these two bacteria contributed over 41% of the variation observed across all bee taxonomic levels and functional traits () and were consistently identified as important taxa in our RFC models when predicting bee family, bee genus, or bee species (). The fungi Cladosporium, Alternaria, Metschnikowia, Ascosphaera, and Epicoccum were common in our bees, but only Cladosporium and Alternaria were 100% prevalent in all bee samples (). These two fungi also contributed over 50% of the community variation observed across most bee groups, and both fungi were always identified as an important taxon in distinguishing between bee taxonomic groups (). In the case of sociality, Cladosporium and Hemimycena contributed to 50% of the community composition variation between social groups (). The plant genera Trifolium, Erigeron, Daucus, Cirsium, Lotus, and Medicago were common across all bees, with Trifolium as the most prevalent (75%) and abundant (14%) (). Overall, due to the more diverse compositions of plant genera across bee families and species (), plant diversity generally better predicted bee taxonomy compared to bacteria and fungi in RFCs (Fig. , ). For example, using an 80% training data set size plants had an average accuracy of 82%, 71%, and 69% in predicting bee family, bee genus, and bee species, respectively, which was higher than bacteria (58%, 53%, and 47%) and fungi (69%, 62%, and 60%). Plant diversity also better categorized bees based on functional traits, except for sociality where it was comparable where it had similar average accuracy (82%) to fungi (84%) and bacteria (83%) (Fig. , ).

Across all taxonomic levels and functional traits, we found several microbes and plant associations that were predominantly associated to a specific bee species; no taxa were found common for a single bee family, bee genus, or functional trait (Fig. ). For example, the bacteria Commensalibacter and Gluconobacter were most prevalent in A. helianthi, Lactobacillus, and Fructobacillus were primarily detected in Bombus impatiens and Bifidobacterium was most prevalent in Bombus griseocollis (). For fungi, focusing on species with more than two individuals, Andrena carataegi and A. wilkella associated with the fungi Sawadaea and Cystofilobasidium, respectively. Among the many common plant genera, several genera were common primarily to Apidae species, such as Impatiens and Securigera found primarily in B. impatiens or Silphium and Centaurea primarily found in Melissodes trinodis.

Urbanization impacts on microbiome and plant associations

Overall, bees sampled along an urban gradient did not significantly differ in fungal richness, but bacterial richness was higher in bees sampled from low urban intensities compared to high intensities (Kruskal–Wallis ꭓ2 = 8.6, df = 2, P = .014, Fig. , ). Bacterial and fungal composition significantly differed across urban intensities and at all bee taxonomic levels primarily due to within-group variation, as clustering was not evident (Fig. , ). For bee species with representation across the urban gradient, we found that urbanization did not impact bacterial diversity but fungal richness were found to be elevated in Halictus rubicundus individuals from medium intensities relative to high intensities, and in M. rotundata from medium and high urban intensities relative to low urban intensities (). We found that the bacterium Rosenbergiella and fungus Filobasidium was common in bees only from high urban intensities, whereas the bacterium Saccharibacter and fungi Ascosphaera and Epicoccum were common to bees from low intensities; bees from medium urban intensities were instead associated with microbiota that were common across the urban landscape (). Furthermore we found no significant interaction between urban intensity and any of the bee functional traits for bacterial and fungal richness ().

With regards to plant associations, bees from low urban intensities had significantly higher plant diversity compared to bees from medium urban intensities (Kruskal–Wallis ꭓ2 = 18.0, df = 2, P < .001; Fig. , ) but composition was largely similar across the urban gradient (Fig. ). Conversely, plant diversity in bee species represented across the urban gradient showed no significant differences (). Regardless, plant composition still performed better at predicting the urban intensity the bees were collected from (average accuracy = 77%) compared to bacteria (64%) or fungi (71%) (Fig. , ). Our mixed-effects models distinguished a single significant interaction with urbanization and native status, where introduced bees from high urban intensities showed increased plant richness compared to native bees from low urban intensities (estimate = 0.5, standard error = 0.22, df = 281.2, P = .029; ). We found several plant genera common in bees from specific urban intensities, such as Lotus common in bees from high urban intensities, Hypericum to medium urban intensity bees, and Xanthium, Frangula, Cirsium, and Taraxacum for low urban intensity bees ().

Discussion

This study examines the microbiome and plant associations for 32 bee species from five families (Apidae, Megachilidae, Colletidae, Andrenidae, and Halictidae) across different bee taxonomic levels, functional traits, and urbanization. We found differences in bacterial and plant diversity at all bee taxonomic levels, whereas fungal diversity was different between bee species. Bacterial diversity was highest in subsocial bees and native bees, whereas fungal diversity was higher in large species and solitary species. All functional traits except diet impacted plant diversity, where bee-associated plant diversity was highest in large species, ground-nesters, subsocial species, and native species. We identified two bacteria, five fungi, and six bee-associated plant taxa that were highly common across the bee community, as well as several distinct bee-taxon associations for certain bee species. Bees from low urban intensities had increased bacterial richness when compared against bees collected from highly urbanized areas. Increasing urbanization had little impact on fungal richness for most wild bees. The impacts of urbanization were most notable for bee-plant associations with both increased and decreased plant richness at higher urban levels depending on functional trait. Plant composition data was also better at predicting bee taxonomic level or functional trait than microbial data but was comparable when predicting sociality.

Distinct microbial and plant associations with bee species

Five bee families shared many common bacterial and fungal microbes, most of which are well known core taxa in bees such as the bacteria Acinetobacter and Apilactobacillus (Graystock et al. , Caesar et al. , Nguyen et al. ), fungi Cladosporium and Alternaria (Nguyen and Rehan 2023, Rutkowski et al. ), and plant resources such as Trifolium, Erigeron, and Taraxacum (Nguyen et al. ). However, there were several microbial and plant taxa associations to specific bee species which suggests important microbial influences on specific bee species.

Although our study focuses on environmental symbionts across the whole bee specimen whereas most microbiome studies focus solely on the gut, we found well-known bee symbionts based on previous gut microbiome studies to also be common in our bee species (Kwong and Moran , Graystock et al. , Motta and Moran ). For instance, Commensalibacter, a noncore, environmentally acquired acetic-acid symbiont commonly found in the guts of insects that specialize on resources high in carbohydrates (i.e. Apidae species such as honey bees, bumble bees, and small carpenter bees) (Botero et al. ), was mainly common in A. helianthi (a nonapid bee) but was not highly prevalent in the Apidae species in our study (e.g. Bombus spp. and Ceratina spp.). Andrena helianthi, an Asteraceae specialist, associated with three plant genera two of which are asters (i.e. Xanthium and Tragopogon) whereas Bombus and Ceratina in our study associated with several more Asteraceae genera (). However, we speculate the high abundance and prevalence of Commensalibacter in A. helianthi may be less a result of diet and more to do with immunity. One study found that Commensalibacter suppressed the spread of Gluconobacter morbifer, a pathogenic sugar-loving environmental bacterium, in Drosophila through competition (Crotti et al. ). Commensalibacter intestini’s relative abundance also increased in the guts of honey bees infested with the mite Varroa, further suggesting a role in immune defense for bees (Hubert et al. ). In our study, Gluconobacter was also identified as a common endosymbiont in A. helianthi, with identical prevalence as Commensalibacter (69% prevalence for both) but a lower abundance (Gluconobacter: 1.1%, Commensalibacter: 2.6%) (). It is possible that Commensalibacter may be playing an important role in immunity for A. helianthi by outcompeting with Gluconobacter, however further testing of these specific microbes in A. helianthi is warranted to confirm if this is the case. Interestingly, we identified Bifidobacterium with at least 50% prevalence and 1% abundance primarily in B. griseocollis and not the other Bombus species in our study. Instead, it had 0% prevalence in B. rufocinctus but 80% prevalence in B. impatiens (<1% relative abundance in both species) in our study. Bifidobacterium is considered a core honey bee and bumble bee microbe along with other symbionts such as Gilliamella, Lactobacillus, and Snodgrassella, all of which were also prevalent in all our Bombus species, except for Lactobacillus which was mainly detected in in B. impatiens. Bifidobacterium is vertically transmitted through the interactions of colony members, and its varied prevalence may be the result of natural variation in the transmission of this bacterium within different colonies, as observed in other studies where Bifidobacterium is present in most Bombus species but not all of them (Kwong et al. , Zhang et al. ). Another aspect that impacts the variation of core phylotypes is the presence of noncore microbes which can displace core symbionts especially in wild-caught bumble bees with greater, natural exposure to noncore microbes (Hotchkiss et al. ). However, future focused research examining microbial functions in different wild bee species will be necessary to elucidate the direct biological and functional roles of microbes across diverse bee taxa.

Meanwhile, there were fewer unique associations of specific bee species with fungi due in part to the largely similar fungal microbiome across wild bees (Fig. ). Unlike bacterial symbionts that show strong host associations, fungal communities are shaped by local environment which have previously been shown to be resilient towards urbanization (Hui et al. , Abrego et al. ). The fungal communities in wild bees likely represent transient environmental associates rather than obligate bee symbionts. Within this context, two Andrenidae species, Andrena crataegi and A. wilkella had unique associations with the fungi Sawadaea and Cystofilobasidium, respectively. Sawadaea is a powdery mildew fungus and an obligate parasite of maple (Acer) trees (Hirose et al. ). Acer trees were not common in A. crataegi, nor were there any ASVs assigned to Acer present in any A. crataegi sample. However, other trees such as Gleditsia and Gymnocladus were common in A. crataegi but there are no studies to suggest that Sawadaea can exist on nonmaple woody trees. Our result may reflect the specific site of A. crataegi samples, which was only from Site 4, a medium urban intensity site. We speculate that while Acer is not a main floral resource for A. crataegi, Acer trees infected with Sawadaea in the area could come into contact with A. crataegi individuals that nest in the area from wind-borne conidia (Qin et al. ). Cystofilobasidium is a yeast that partakes in breakdown of polysaccharides like pectin, which is present in pollen grain walls (Ye et al. ). Little is known in terms of this fungus’ relationship with solitary bees, but its association only with A. wilkella suggests an increased nutrient uptake from pectin-rich pollen grains which may be the type of pollen it encounters in its floral preferences, all of which are Fabaceae genera. Further investigation into the pollen structure of its legume-associated floral resources is needed to determine if the grain walls are rich in pectin, which would support the idea that Cystofilobasidium partakes in its breakdown.

There were many distinct plant associations with a single bee species, which were mainly from generalist bee species. Our findings show that diet specialization played no role in altering the microbiome, nor did it produce any significant difference in plant associations between specialists and generalists (). We attribute this lack of difference in plant diversity between the diet types because of floral constancy—the preference of foraging on one flower—exhibited by generalist bees (Kelly and Elle , Grüter et al. ). Rather, our distinct bee–plant associations may be attributed to other factors such as nesting behaviour. For instance, Ceratina mikmaqi, a stem-nesting bee, associated with Linaria, a woody plant that can offer pithy stem material for nesting (Mikát et al. ). Bombus impatiens associated with, unsurprisingly, Impatiens emphasizing its floral preference (and namesake) despite being capable of a generalist diet. Nevertheless, two specialist Melissodes species in our study did have distinct plant associations. Melissodes subillatus is an Asteraceae specialist (Gibbs et al. ) but did associate with Syringa and Ligustrum plant genera belonging to the Oleaceae plant family suggesting that this specialist has flexibility in floral choice, which sometimes occurs in specialists when other resources are present and abundant (Burger et al. ). Melissodes trinodis is also an Asteraceae specialist (Gibbs et al. ) and its associations with Helianthus, Silphium, and Centaurea (Asteraceae genera) corroborate its diet specialization.

Urbanization impacts bee–plant and bee–bacteria associations more so than bee–fungi interactions

While fungal alpha diversity across bee species was unaffected by urban intensity, bacterial diversity was higher in bees from low urban intensities compared to high intensities. This finding generally agrees with previous studies that show bee bacterial and fungal communities change with urbanization (Nguyen and Rehan , 2023, Chau et al. , Peters et al. ). Although, focusing on five species with representation across the urban gradient revealed that urbanization did not impact bacterial communities at the species level, but rather, increasing urbanization impacts the bacterial community at the bee community level or only for some bacteria. For example, one study found that urbanization significantly decreased the bacteria Snodgrassella and Nocardioides in solitary bees whereas other bacteria were unaffected (Fernandez De Landa et al. ). As such, bee community-level shifts in bacterial communities are nuanced but reveal complex high-level patterns that are important for broader ecological impacts on urban bee communities. These changes may represent novel challenges for wild bees because host-associated bacteria contribute to nutrient acquisition, digestion, immune defense, and overall host fitness (Engel et al. , Kwong and Moran , Dharampal et al. ). On the contrary, it could be that our five representative bees are robust species that are unaffected by urbanization whereas other bee species might be influenced more readily by urbanization.

Instead, urbanization had a profound impact on plant diversity. Bees from low urban intensities had high plant richness, which was similar to bees collected from high urban areas. Furthermore, plant beta diversity varied significantly across the urban landscape, but clustering of plant communities by urban intensity was not evident. This suggests that while low urbanization may allow bees to access more floral resources, available plant species across the urban landscape appear homogenous. This may be attributed to pollinator programs where cities such as Toronto partake in initiatives to increase floral diversity in parks and urban gardens to boost pollinators richness, which may be one reason we observe similar plant-associated compositions in across the bee community (MacIvor ). Indeed, when assessing changes in plant richness in bee species with individuals sampled along the urban gradient, we observed no significant differences likely due to similar resources present at all urban intensities. While plant diversity was impacted by differences in functional traits, the impacts of urbanization were negligible when accounting for trait-based variation. This finding indicates that while bees from different functional guilds show floral preferences, increasing urbanization does not appear to hinder their floral choice.

Conclusion

This study highlights the diverse relationships between bee families and their microbial and plant associations. We showed that integrating a trait-based approach, urbanization, and phylogeny can reveal many unique interactions both at a bee community level and at finer resolutions. Bacterial diversity was higher in Andrenidae and Apidae relative to other families, and also higher in subsocial and native bees. Fungal diversity remained consistent across all bee families but was higher in large-bodied species and solitary species. Bee-associated plant diversity was higher in Apidae and Halictidae, and in large-bodied species, ground-nesters, subsocial and eusocial species, and native bees. Diet had no effect on microbial diversity or plant diversity in this study. Common microbes and plant-associations were already well-known core bee microbes and common plants in the sample region, but we also detected unique taxon associations for several bee species providing early indications of potential host-specific interactions that warrant further investigation. Overall, increasing urbanization greatly impacted plant diversity across focal bees, with little to no impact on microbial diversity suggesting that, despite common plants throughout the sample region, the effect of urbanization alters bee foraging behaviours and that higher urban intensities may be beneficial to certain bee groups via increased plant richness.
