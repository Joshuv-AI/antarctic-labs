Nonlinear Relativistic Effects on Cosmological Redshift Drift
and M. R. Medeiros Silva

Abstract
Using a fully gauge-invariant approach, we compute for the first time in the literature relativistic effects on the redshift drift up to second order in cosmological perturbation theory. This is achieved by employing a set of light-cone coordinates that simplify the description of light propagation in an inhomogeneous and anisotropic universe. We show that redshift-space distortion occurs only as a second-order effect whereas, as known, it is not present among the linear perturbations. We then derive analytical expressions of the bispectrum for the leading-order perturbative contributions on sub-Hubble scales, providing some numerical evaluations. Our finding is that, at low redshift and for large momenta, the non-linearities in the bispectrum are enhanced more than the squared power spectrum.

2
Redshift Drift: Background and Fully Non-linear Expressions

The redshift drift is defined as the ratio between the variation of the redshift
\Delta z
of a given source and the time interval
\Delta\tau_{\text{o}}
measured in the observer’s rest frame needed for the measurement. In a flat Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) universe, it has the simple expression
[
46
]

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta z}{\Delta\tau_{\text{o}}}=H_{\text{o}}(1+z)-H_{\text{s}}\,,

(2.1)

where the subscripts “s” and “o” stand for evaluation at the source and observer position.

To model the redshift drift in a generic inhomogeneous and anisotropic universe, the use of the so-called
Geodesic Light-Cone
(GLC) coordinates is particularly convenient
[
56
]
. Thus, in the following we will first define the GLC gauge and then provide the fully non-linear formula the the redshift drift in this gauge.

Fully non-linear Geodesic Light-Cone gauge.

Since light signals propagate along the past light-cone of an observer, their physical properties can be easily described using the GLC coordinates
x^{\mu}=(\tau,w,\tilde{\theta}^{a})
, first introduced in
[
56
]
. These coordinates are intrinsically defined by the line element

3
Second-Order Perturbation Theory on the Light-Cone

In this section, following the works
[
59
,
60
,
29
]
, we will introduce the formalism necessary to compute the first- and second-order relativistic corrections to the redshift drift. In particular, by building a perturbation theory on top of a background light-cone geometry, we will provide a set of gauge-invariant variables up to second order corresponding to the values that perturbations acquire in the perturbative counterparts of the fully non-linear GLC gauge.

3.1
Standard and light-cone metric perturbations

Standard perturbations.

In standard perturbation theory, using the conformal time and spherical spatial coordinates,
y^{\mu}=(\eta,r,\theta^{a})
, the perturbed FLRW metric is

\displaystyle\text{d}s^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv g_{\mu\nu}\text{d}y^{\mu}\text{d}y^{\nu}=(\bar{g}_{\mu\nu}+g^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}+g^{(2)}_{\mu\nu})\text{d}y^{\mu}\text{d}y^{\nu}

\displaystyle=a^{2}(\eta)\Big[-\big(1+2(\phi^{(1)}+\phi^{(2)})\big)\text{d}\eta^{2}-2(\mathcal{B}^{(1)}_{r}+\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{r})\text{d}\eta\text{d}r-2(\mathcal{B}^{(1)}_{a}+\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{a})\text{d}\eta\text{d}\theta^{a}

\displaystyle\quad+(1+\mathcal{C}^{(1)}_{rr}+\mathcal{C}^{(2)}_{rr})\text{d}r^{2}+2(\mathcal{C}^{(1)}_{ra}+\mathcal{C}^{(2)}_{ra})\text{d}r\text{d}\theta^{a}+(\bar{\gamma}_{ab}+\mathcal{C}^{(1)}_{ab}+\mathcal{C}^{(2)}_{ab})\text{d}\theta^{a}\text{d}\theta^{b}\Big]\,,

(3.1)

where
\bar{g}_{\mu\nu}
is the homogeneous and isotropic background metric, the 2-dimensional angular metric is
\bar{\gamma}_{ab}=\text{diag}\left(r^{2},r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right)
, while
g^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}
and
g^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}
respectively stand for first- and second-order fluctuations.
Using an index
i=(r,\theta,\varphi)
to label spatial coordinates and adopting from now on the superscript
n=1,2
to indicate first- and second-order perturbations, one can split the fluctuations according to the Scalar-Vector-Tensor (SVT) decomposition,
i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{(n)}_{i}

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}B^{(n)}+B^{(n)}_{i}\qquad,\qquad\mathcal{C}^{(n)}_{ij}=-2\psi^{(n)}\bar{\gamma}_{ij}+2D_{ij}E^{(n)}+2\nabla_{(i}F^{(n)}_{j)}+2h^{(n)}_{ij}\,,

(3.2)

where

\displaystyle\bar{\gamma}_{ij}=\text{diag}\left(1,\bar{\gamma}_{ab}\right)\qquad,\qquad D_{ij}\equiv\nabla_{(i}\nabla_{j)}-\frac{1}{3}\bar{\gamma}_{ij}\Delta_{3}\,,

(3.3)

and the symmetrization between the two lower indices of two vectors is defined as
X_{(\mu}Y_{\nu)}\equiv(1/2)(X_{\mu}Y_{\nu}+X_{\nu}Y_{\mu})
. We recall that, in the SVT decomposition, the vectors
B^{(n)}_{i}
and
F^{(n)}_{i}
are divergenceless, and the tensor
h^{(n)}_{ij}
is traceless and divergenceless, namely

\nabla^{i}B^{(n)}_{i}=0\qquad,\qquad\nabla^{i}F^{(n)}_{i}=0\qquad,\qquad h^{(n)}_{ii}=0\qquad,\qquad\nabla^{i}h^{(n)}_{ij}=0\,.

(3.4)

Light-cone perturbations.

We can now move to the set of background light-cone coordinates indicated as
x^{\mu}=(\tau,w,\tilde{\theta}^{a})
, where
\tau
is the proper time of a time-like particle,
w
defines the background light-cone of an observer and the angles
\tilde{\theta}^{a}
coincide with the two polar angles we observe in the sky.
These coordinates are related to
y^{\mu}
through the relations

\eta(\tau)-\eta_{\text{in}}=\int_{\tau_{\text{in}}}^{\tau}\frac{\text{d}\tau^{\prime}}{a(\tau^{\prime})}\qquad,\qquad r=w-\eta(\tau)\qquad,\qquad\theta^{a}=\tilde{\theta}^{a}\,,

(3.5)

where the initial condition
\eta_{\text{in}}
is chosen to cancel with the integral on the r.h.s. evaluated at
\tau_{\text{in}}
. Using the transformation rule under diffeomorphisms,
i.e.

f_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\partial y^{\rho}}{\partial x^{\mu}}\frac{\partial y^{\sigma}}{\partial x^{\nu}}\,g_{\rho\sigma}\,,

(3.6)

we can compute the background light-cone geometry
\bar{f}_{\mu\nu}
and then add first- and second-order perturbations on top of it:

\displaystyle\text{d}s^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv f_{\mu\nu}\text{d}x^{\mu}\text{d}x^{\nu}=(\bar{f}_{\mu\nu}+f^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}+f^{(2)}_{\mu\nu})\text{d}x^{\mu}\text{d}x^{\nu}

\displaystyle=a^{2}(\tau)\bigg[(L^{(1)}+L^{(2)})\text{d}\tau^{2}-\frac{2}{a}\big(1-a(M^{(1)}+M^{(2)})\big)\text{d}\tau\text{d}w

\displaystyle\quad+2(V^{(1)}_{a}+V^{(2)}_{a})\text{d}\tau\text{d}\tilde{\theta}^{a}+(1+N^{(1)}+N^{(2)})\text{d}w^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2(U^{(1)}_{a}+U^{(2)}_{a})\text{d}w\text{d}\tilde{\theta}^{a}+(\bar{\gamma}_{ab}+\gamma^{(1)}_{ab}+\gamma^{(2)}_{ab})\text{d}\tilde{\theta}^{a}\text{d}\tilde{\theta}^{b}\bigg]\,.

(3.7)

Here, we are using

\bar{\gamma}_{ab}=\big[w-\eta(\tau)\big]^{2}\text{diag}(1,\sin^{2}\tilde{\theta}^{1})\equiv\big[w-\eta(\tau)\big]^{2}q_{ab}\,,

(3.8)

where
q_{ab}
is the metric induced on the unit 2-dimensional sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Then, we indicate with
D_{a}
the covariant derivative on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and with
\tilde{D}_{a}\equiv\epsilon^{b}_{a}D_{b}
its dual, where
\epsilon^{b}_{a}
is the covariant volume form on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
defined as

\epsilon_{ab}\equiv\sqrt{\text{det}[q_{cd}]}\,\varepsilon_{ab}=\sin\tilde{\theta}^{1}\,\varepsilon_{ab}\,,

(3.9)

\varepsilon_{ab}
being the totally anti-symmetric Levi-Civita symbol.

The perturbations in Eq. (
3.7
) can be split into scalars and pseudo-scalars (indicated with a “hat”) as

\begin{split}V^{(n)}_{a}&=r^{2}\big[D_{a}v^{(n)}+\tilde{D}_{a}\hat{v}^{(n)}\big]\,,\\[4.30554pt]
U^{(n)}_{a}&=r^{2}\big[D_{a}u^{(n)}+\tilde{D}_{a}\hat{u}^{(n)}\big]\,,\\[4.30554pt]
\gamma^{(n)}_{ab}&=2r^{2}\big[q_{ab}\nu^{(n)}+D_{ab}\mu^{(n)}+\tilde{D}_{ab}\hat{\mu}^{(n)}\big]\,,\end{split}

(3.10)

where we have defined the traceless derivative operators

D_{ab}\equiv D_{(a}D_{b)}-\frac{1}{2}q_{ab}D^{2}\qquad,\qquad\tilde{D}_{ab}\equiv\tilde{D}_{(a}D_{b)}=D_{(a}\tilde{D}_{b)}\,

(3.11)

with

the angular Laplacian. This is the so-called

(SPS) decomposition, because the perturbations

,

,

,

,

, while
\hat{v}^{(n)}
,
\hat{u}^{(n)}
and

Gauge fixing at the observer position.

As we have already pointed out, the functions
w^{(n)}_{\text{o}}(w,\tilde{\theta}^{a})
and

appearing in Eqs. (
3.26

) reflect the fact that the GLC gauge is defined up to the residual transformations of Eqs. (
2.8
). This residual gauge freedom around the observer position is removed once a choice of the free functions
w^{(n)}_{\text{o}}(w,\tilde{\theta}^{a})
and
\xi^{a}_{(n)\text{o}}(w,\tilde{\theta}^{a})
is specified.

We choose the observer to be set at the center of the polar frame,
i.e.

r_{\text{o}}=w_{\text{o}}-\eta(\tau_{\text{o}})=0
. Then, in order to preserve the condition
w_{\text{o}}=\eta(\tau_{\text{o}})
in any other gauge, by taking the first-order gauge transformations

\tau\rightarrow\tilde{\tau}=\tau+\xi^{\tau}_{(1)}\qquad,\qquad w\rightarrow\tilde{w}=w+\xi^{w}_{(1)}\,,

(3.28)

we have to demand that, locally around the observer, it holds

\left(\xi^{w}_{(1)}\right)_{\text{o}}\equiv w^{(1)}_{\text{o}}=\frac{\left(\xi^{\tau}_{(1)}\right)_{\text{o}}}{a_{\text{o}}}\,,

(3.29)

so that
w^{(1)}_{\text{o}}
is a pure monopole, namely
w^{(1)}_{\text{o}}=w^{(1)}_{\text{o}}(w)
.

Furthermore, the residual angular gauge mode
\xi^{a}_{(1)\text{o}}(w,\tilde{\theta}^{a})
can be fixed by requiring that the angles
\tilde{\theta}^{a}
coincide with the
observed
angular directions of incoming photons in the sky, as in the so-called Fermi Normal Coordinates
[
58
]
. Let us consider the first-order gauge transformation mapping the angles as
\tilde{\theta}^{a}_{\text{o}}\rightarrow\tilde{\theta}^{a}_{\text{o}}+\xi^{a}_{(1)\text{o}}
. In the GLC gauge, the angles are precisely the background ones of the polar frame, namely
\tilde{\theta}^{a}=\theta^{a}_{\text{o}}
(see also
[
65
]
), hence

\displaystyle\xi^{a}_{(1)}(w_{\text{o}},\tilde{\theta}^{a}_{\text{o}})=0\,.

(3.30)

More in general, in this “observational gauge”, since no preferable angular direction at the center of the polar frame is allowed, any quantity
X
evaluated at the observer position must satisfy
D_{a}X_{\text{o}}\equiv 0
.

As we have discussed in
[
29
]
, the first-order gauge conditions (
3.29

3.30

\displaystyle w^{(1,2)}_{\text{o}}(w)=\frac{\xi^{\tau}_{(1,2)}(w_{\text{o}})}{a_{\text{o}}}\qquad,\qquad\xi^{a}_{(1,2)\text{o}}(w,\tilde{\theta}^{a})=0\,.

(3.31)

We refer to
[
29
]
for a more in-depth discussion about such a gauge fixing.

Discussion about the term
(\partial_{r}v_{||z})^{2}
.

The presence of the term
(\partial_{r}v_{||z})^{2}
in the second of Eqs. (
4.43
) deserves further clarifications.

In the first-order redshift drift, the terms involving
\partial_{r}v_{||z}
cancel out. On the contrary, this is not
the case for similar terms in the second-order expression, where the term

(\partial_{r}v_{||z})^{2}
is present. Indeed, at first order, considering Eqs. (
2.13
) and (
4.26
)-(
4.27
), we see that one term of the form
\partial_{r}v_{||z}
stems from the light-cone derivative of the redshift,
\partial_{w}(1+z)
, and another one from its time derivative,
\partial_{\tau}(1+z)
. These two terms cancel among each others because they are contained in the combination

\bigg(\partial_{\tau}+\frac{\partial_{w}}{a}\bigg){N}=\left(\bar{u}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\right){N}\,,

(4.44)

where
\bar{u}^{\mu}
is the background counterpart of the 4-velocity of a free-falling observer (see Eqs. (
2.5
)). In general, the quantity
u^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}
is the evolution operator along a given geodesic connecting source and observer belonging to the same light-cone.

Thus, this cancellation is physically due to the fact that the observer measuring the redshift drift of some given source always lies on the same light-cone of the source, and this is valid at any perturbative order. This point was not emphasized in
[
51
]
, and the use of GLC coordinates to compute the redshift drift helps in highlighting this subtlety in a natural and clear way. At the same time, there is a second source of this kind of contribution. Indeed, terms proportional to
\partial_{r}v_{||z}
can occur in the redshift expansion of
H_{\text{s}}
. However, we can easily see, from the second of Eqs. (
4.17
), that
\partial_{r}v_{||z}
in
\dot{\tau}^{(1)}_{z}
cancels out at first order.

The same cancellation occurs also at second order for the terms
\partial_{r}v^{(2)}_{||z}
, while the term
(\partial_{r}v_{||z})^{2}
survives in the redshift expansion of
H_{\text{s}}
, where a term proportional to
(\dot{\tau}^{(1)}_{z})^{2}
has no counterpart to be canceled with (see Eqs. (
4.17
)).

This is a very important result, which can be emphasized when compared against what happens
e.g.
in the galaxy number counts
[
33
,
34
]
. Indeed, the leading terms in the number counts have two radial/angular derivatives at first order and four at second order. Thus, the leading terms in the bispectrum and in the squared spectrum are of the same order in the sub-Hubble regime, since both of them contain eight radial/angular derivatives. On the contrary, for the redshift drift at leading order the bispectrum has six radial derivatives, while the square spectrum has only four radial derivatives. This seems to suggest that, on sub-Hubble scales, the magnitude of the three-point function for the redshift drift is significantly enhanced compared to the magnitude of the two-point function. This fact would make it easier to observe the three-point function w.r.t. what one could naively expect from the two-point function, with real future galaxy surveys or in relativistic
N
-body simulation (see
e.g.

[
73
,
74
,
75
]
for numerical simulations of the power spectrum of the redshift drift). We will further address this point with some numerical estimates in the next section.

5.1
General mathematical formulae for the bispectrum

The bispectrum of the redshift drift is defined as the connected part of the three-point correlation function,
i.e.

\displaystyle B(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})\equiv\left\langle\frac{\widetilde{\Delta z}}{\Delta\tau_{\text{o}}}(z_{1},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1})\frac{\widetilde{\Delta z}}{\Delta\tau_{\text{o}}}(z_{2},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2})\frac{\widetilde{\Delta z}}{\Delta\tau_{\text{o}}}(z_{3},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})\right\rangle_{\text{c}}\,,

(5.3)

where, following the notation of
[
34
]
, the observation direction is defined as the unit vector
-\hat{\mathbf{n}}
, while
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
represents the photon propagation direction in the sky. We can then expand the redshift drift in spherical harmonics as

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{\Delta z}}{\Delta\tau_{\text{o}}}(z,\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell m}(z)Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})\qquad,\qquad a_{\ell m}(z)=\int\text{d}\Omega_{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\,\frac{\widetilde{\Delta z}}{\Delta\tau_{\text{o}}}(z,\hat{\mathbf{n}})Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})

(5.4)

and the bispectrum as

\displaystyle B(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})=\underset{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}{\sum_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}}B^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}Y_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1})Y_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2})Y_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})\,,

(5.5)

having defined the coefficients
B^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}
as

\displaystyle B^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})

\displaystyle\equiv\left\langle a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(z_{1})a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(z_{2})a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(z_{3})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\text{d}\Omega_{1}\text{d}\Omega_{2}\text{d}\Omega_{3}\,B(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})Y^{*}_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1})Y^{*}_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2})Y^{*}_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})\,.

(5.6)

The statistical isotropy of the background geometry enforces the bispectrum to be of the form

\displaystyle B^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=\mathcal{G}^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\,b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\,.

(5.7)

The quantity
\mathcal{G}^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}
is the so-called Gaunt integral, defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}

\displaystyle\equiv\int\text{d}\Omega\,Y_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})Y_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})Y_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}\sqrt{\frac{(2\ell_{1}+1)(2\ell_{2}+1)(2\ell_{3}+1)}{4\pi}}\,,

(5.8)

where we have introduced the Wigner
3j
symbols (see, for example,
[
77
,
78
]
). Note that the Gaunt integral is non-zero only for

m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}=0\quad,\quad|\ell_{2}-\ell_{3}|\leqslant\ell_{1}\leqslant\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}\quad,\quad\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}=2n\,\,\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\,,

(5.9)

where the last two conditions are just the triangular inequality.
Assuming Gaussian initial conditions, namely

\left\langle\bigg(\frac{\widetilde{\Delta z}}{\Delta\tau_{\text{o}}}\bigg)^{(1)}(z_{1},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1})\bigg(\frac{\widetilde{\Delta z}}{\Delta\tau_{\text{o}}}\bigg)^{(1)}(z_{2},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2})\bigg(\frac{\widetilde{\Delta z}}{\Delta\tau_{\text{o}}}\bigg)^{(1)}(z_{3},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})\right\rangle_{\text{c}}=0\,,

(5.10)

we are then interested in computing the tree-level bispectrum given by

\left\langle\bigg(\frac{\widetilde{\Delta z}}{\Delta\tau_{\text{o}}}\bigg)^{(2)}(z_{1},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1})\bigg(\frac{\widetilde{\Delta z}}{\Delta\tau_{\text{o}}}\bigg)^{(1)}(z_{2},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2})\bigg(\frac{\widetilde{\Delta z}}{\Delta\tau_{\text{o}}}\bigg)^{(1)}(z_{3},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})\right\rangle_{\text{c}}+\text{perms.}\,,

(5.11)

that is,

\displaystyle B^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})

\displaystyle=\left\langle a^{(2)}_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(z_{1})a^{(1)}_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(z_{2})a^{(1)}_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(z_{3})\right\rangle_{\text{c}}+\left\langle a^{(1)}_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(z_{1})a^{(2)}_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(z_{2})a^{(1)}_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(z_{3})\right\rangle_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\langle a^{(1)}_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(z_{1})a^{(1)}_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(z_{2})a^{(2)}_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(z_{3})\right\rangle_{\text{c}}\,.

(5.12)

Next, following the notation of
[
34
]
, we redefine
v_{||}\equiv\partial_{r}v
,
v
being the velocity potential previously indicated as the time gauge mode
\xi^{\tau}
. Thus, because of Eqs. (
4.43
) (and dropping from now on the subscript “

”), Eq. (
5.11
) amounts to computing the following three correlators (we omit the dependence of
B
on the 6 variables
(z_{1},\dots\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})
):

\displaystyle B^{v^{\prime^{2}}vv}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\left(\partial^{2}_{r}v(z_{1},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1})\right)^{2}}{\mathcal{H}(z_{1})\left[\mathcal{H}_{\text{o}}-\mathcal{H}(z_{1})\right]}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(z_{2})\partial_{r}v(z_{2},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2})}{\mathcal{H}(z_{2})\left[\mathcal{H}_{\text{o}}-\mathcal{H}(z_{2})\right]}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(z_{3})\partial_{r}v(z_{3},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})}{\mathcal{H}(z_{3})\left[\mathcal{H}_{\text{o}}-\mathcal{H}(z_{3})\right]}\right\rangle_{\text{c}}+\text{perms.}\,,

(5.13)

\displaystyle B^{v^{\prime^{2}}\Phi^{\prime}\Phi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\left(\partial^{2}_{r}v(z_{1},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1})\right)^{2}}{\mathcal{H}(z_{1})\left[\mathcal{H}_{\text{o}}-\mathcal{H}(z_{1})\right]}\frac{\partial_{r}\Phi(z_{2},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2})}{\mathcal{H}_{\text{o}}-\mathcal{H}(z_{2})}\frac{\partial_{r}\Phi(z_{3},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})}{\mathcal{H}_{\text{o}}-\mathcal{H}(z_{3})}\right\rangle_{\text{c}}+\text{perms.}\,,

(5.14)

\displaystyle B^{v^{\prime^{2}}v\Phi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-2\left\langle\frac{\left(\partial^{2}_{r}v(z_{1},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1})\right)^{2}}{\mathcal{H}(z_{1})\left[\mathcal{H}_{\text{o}}-\mathcal{H}(z_{1})\right]}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(z_{2})\partial_{r}v(z_{2},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2})}{\mathcal{H}(z_{2})\left[\mathcal{H}_{\text{o}}-\mathcal{H}(z_{2})\right]}\frac{\partial_{r}\Phi(z_{3},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})}{\mathcal{H}_{\text{o}}-\mathcal{H}(z_{3})}\right\rangle_{\text{c}}+\text{perms.}\,,

(5.15)

and we then indicate the related reduced bispectra as

b^{v^{\prime^{2}}vv}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\qquad,\qquad b^{v^{\prime^{2}}\Phi^{\prime}\Phi^{\prime}}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\qquad,\qquad b^{v^{\prime^{2}}v\Phi^{\prime}}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\,.

(5.16)

By working in Fourier space, any function
f
can be written as

\displaystyle f(z,\mathbf{x})=\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,f(z,\mathbf{k})e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}=\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,f(z,\mathbf{k})e^{-ir(z)\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{n}}\,.

(5.17)

Therefore, the first-order Newtonian potential and velocity potential are expressed as

\displaystyle\Phi(z,\hat{\mathbf{n}})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\Phi\left(z,\mathbf{k}\right)e^{-ir(z)\mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\qquad,\qquad v(z,\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,v\left(z,\mathbf{k}\right)e^{-ir(z)\mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\,,

(5.18)

From now on, for brevity, we will adopt the shortcut
r(z)\equiv\eta_{\text{o}}-\eta(z)=r
.

To compute the quantities in Eqs. (
5.13
)-(
5.15
), we have to recall that, in linear perturbation theory, the power spectrum of the initial curvature perturbation is expressed as

\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{k})\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\right\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime})P_{\mathcal{R}}(k)\,,

(5.19)

where
\delta^{(3)}
is the 3-dimensional Dirac-delta function and
k\equiv|\mathbf{k}|
.
Furthermore, assuming adiabatic initial conditions, the transfer function
T_{A}(z,k)
of any perturbation
A
is defined through

A(z,\mathbf{k})=T_{A}(z,k)\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{k})\,,

(5.20)

and the angular power spectra of two quantities
A
and
B
in multipole space is

\displaystyle c_{\ell}^{AB}(z_{1},z_{2})\equiv 4\pi\int\frac{\text{d}k}{k}\,\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k)\Delta^{A}_{\ell}(z_{1},k)\Delta^{B}_{\ell}(z_{2},k)=\frac{2}{\pi}\int\text{d}k\,k^{2}P_{\mathcal{R}}(k)\Delta^{A}_{\ell}(z_{1},k)\Delta^{B}_{\ell}(z_{2},k)\,.

(5.21)

Here we have introduced the dimensionless primordial power spectrum of scalar perturbations as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k)\equiv\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}P_{\mathcal{R}}(k)=\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{R}}\left(\frac{k}{k_{*}}\right)^{n_{\mathcal{R}}-1}\,,

(5.22)

where
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{R}}
is the amplitude,
n_{\mathcal{R}}
is the spectral index and
k_{*}
is the pivot scale. Moreover,
\Delta^{A}_{\ell}(z,k)
represents the transfer function in multipole and redshift space associated with
A
. For
v_{||}
,
\partial_{r}\Phi
and
\partial_{r}v_{||}
, these functions are written as (see also
[
79
]
)

\displaystyle\Delta^{v}_{\ell}(z,k)

\displaystyle=j^{\prime}_{\ell}(kr)T_{v}(z,k)\,,

\displaystyle\Delta^{\Phi^{\prime}}_{\ell}(z,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{\mathcal{H}(z)}j^{\prime}_{\ell}(kr)T_{\Phi}(z,k)\,,

\displaystyle\Delta^{v^{\prime}}_{\ell}(z,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{\mathcal{H}(z)}j^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}(kr)T_{v}(z,k)\,,

(5.23)

where
j_{\ell}(kr)
is the spherical Bessel functions of order
\ell
, and the “prime” denotes the derivative w.r.t. its argument
kr
. In this notation, we indicate with
T_{v}
the transfer function of the module of the velocity field, given by
T_{v}=T_{\theta}/k
, where
T_{\theta}
is the transfer function of the divergence of the velocity, whose numerical values can be obtained from

5.2
Detailed computations of the bispectrum

Let us now proceed with an in-depth evaluation of Eqs. (
5.13
)-(
5.15
), using that

\displaystyle v_{||}(z,\hat{\mathbf{n}})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}T_{v}(z,k)\left[\partial_{(kr)}e^{-ir\mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\right]\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{k})\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\Phi(z,\hat{\mathbf{n}})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}kT_{\Phi}(z,k)\left[\partial_{(kr)}e^{-ir\mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\right]\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{k})\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{r}v_{||}(z,\hat{\mathbf{n}})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}kT_{v}(z,k)\left[\partial^{2}_{(kr)}e^{-ir\mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\right]\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{k})\,.

(5.24)

In the following computations, we will make use of the harmonic expansion of the exponential, given by

\displaystyle e^{-ir\mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}

\displaystyle=4\pi\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}(-i)^{\ell}j_{\ell}(kr)Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})\,,

(5.25)

where
Y_{\ell m}
are the spherical harmonics.

5.2.1
Term
b^{v^{\prime^{2}}vv}

Defining for brevity

\displaystyle{H}_{v^{\prime^{2}}vv}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\equiv\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(z_{2})\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(z_{3})}{\mathcal{H}(z_{1})\left[\mathcal{H}_{\text{o}}-\mathcal{H}(z_{1})\right]\mathcal{H}(z_{2})\left[\mathcal{H}_{\text{o}}-\mathcal{H}(z_{2})\right]\mathcal{H}(z_{3})\left[\mathcal{H}_{\text{o}}-\mathcal{H}(z_{3})\right]}\,,

(5.26)

we can use the Wick theorem to compute the correlation function in Eq. (
5.13

\displaystyle\langle\cdots\rangle_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle={H}_{v^{\prime^{2}}vv}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}k\,\text{d}^{3}k_{1}\,\text{d}^{3}k_{2}\,\text{d}^{3}k_{3}}{(2\pi)^{12}}\,\left[\partial^{2}_{(kr_{1})}e^{-ir_{1}\mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1}}\right]\left[\partial^{2}_{(k_{1}r_{1})}e^{-ir_{1}\mathbf{k}_{1}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\partial_{(k_{2}r_{2})}e^{-ir_{2}\mathbf{k}_{2}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2}}\right]\left[\partial_{(k_{3}r_{3})}e^{-ir_{3}\mathbf{k}_{3}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3}}\right]kk_{1}T_{v}(z_{1},k)T_{v}(z_{1},k_{1})T_{v}(z_{2},k_{2})T_{v}(z_{3},k_{3})

\displaystyle\quad\times\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{k})\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{k}_{1})\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{k}_{2})\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{k}_{3})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2{H}_{v^{\prime^{2}}vv}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}k\,\text{d}^{3}k_{1}\,}{(2\pi)^{6}}\,\left[\partial^{2}_{(kr_{1})}e^{-ir_{1}\mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1}}\right]\left[\partial^{2}_{(k_{1}r_{1})}e^{-ir_{1}\mathbf{k}_{1}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\partial_{(kr_{2})}e^{ir_{2}\mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2}}\right]\left[\partial_{(k_{1}r_{3})}e^{ir_{3}\mathbf{k}_{1}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3}}\right]kk_{1}T_{v}(z_{1},k)T_{v}(z_{1},k_{1})T_{v}(z_{2},k)T_{v}(z_{3},k_{1})

(5.27)

Next, using Eq. (
5.25

\int\text{d}\Omega_{\hat{\mathbf{k}}}\,Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})Y^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})=\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\,,

(5.28)

the correlator in Eq. (
5.27

\displaystyle\langle\cdots\rangle_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle=2{H}_{v^{\prime^{2}}vv}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell m}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\int\text{d}k\,\text{d}k_{1}k^{3}k_{1}^{3}T_{v}(z_{1},k)T_{v}(z_{1},k_{1})T_{v}(z_{2},k)T_{v}(z_{3},k_{1})

\displaystyle\quad\times P_{\mathcal{R}}(k)P_{\mathcal{R}}(k_{1})j^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}(kr_{1})j^{\prime}_{\ell}(kr_{2})j^{\prime\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}}(k_{1}r_{1})j^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}}(k_{1}r_{3})Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1})Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2})Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1})Y^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})\,,

(5.29)

which shows that

\displaystyle B^{v^{\prime^{2}}vv}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2},\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\underset{mm^{\prime}}{\sum_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}}Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1})Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{1})Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{2})Y^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{3})Z^{v^{\prime^{2}}vv}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})

where

\displaystyle b^{v^{\prime^{2}}vv}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}=\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}Z^{v^{\prime^{2}}vv}_{\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})+\text{perms.}\,

6
Summary and Conclusions

In this paper we have presented for the first time in the literature the
expression of the cosmological redshift drift up to the second perturbative order, including
all the physical effects and expressed as a function of the observed redshift, the observed past light-cone and observed angles.

This has been achieved by evaluating the redshift drift in the Geodesic Light-Cone (GLC) gauge, which naturally provides fully non-linear expressions for late-time cosmological observables. In this gauge, the observational angles remain constant along trajectories connecting the source and the observer on the same light-cone. As a consequence, the contributions to the redshift drift can be factorized into products of local perturbations evaluated at the source and observer positions. Resorting to the approach developed in
[
29
]
, we have then promoted each perturbation to its gauge-invariant counterpart, thereby obtaining manifestly gauge-invariant formulae for the redshift drift on the light-cone. Finally, we have
recast these results in terms of standard gravitational potentials, obtaining the usual perturbative effects – such as Shapiro time-delay, Doppler, SW, ISW, and angular deflections – to be manifest.

Our formulae are fully gauge invariant not only at the source but also at the observer position. Indeed, the GLC gauge admits a residual gauge freedom along the observer world-line, which we have consistently fixed following the analyses of
[
59
,
29
]
. This procedure gives observables as measured by a free-falling observer and generates original observer terms in the redshift drift already at first order, which were not properly accounted for in previous studies
[
51
]
. Also at second order, monopole and higher-multipole contributions arise at the observer position, ensuring full gauge invariance. Furthermore, since our methodology does not rely on any specific gravitational theory and allows for non-vanishing anisotropic stress, our formulae are also applicable to (dynamical) dark energies scenarios and modified gravity theories.

In particular, from the full formulae we have isolated the leading terms on sub-Hubble scales, namely those involving the highest number of radial or angular derivatives. At first order, these leading contributions are given by the peculiar velocity of the source and local variations of the gravitational potential along the radial direction. At second order, we have found that, among hundreds of terms, the leading contribution is simply proportional to
(\partial_{r}v_{||z})^{2}
. This RSD contribution represents a genuinely new second-order effect, since at first order terms proportional to
\partial_{r}v_{||z}
cancel out.

We have then used these leading terms to compute the correspondent contributions to the three-point correlation function of the redshift drift in Fourier space. By working directly in the observable spherical-harmonics-redshift space, we have provided analytical expressions for the bispectrum, and showed how the leading terms of the latter can be expressed as products of the angular power spectrum coefficients. We have also reported some numerical evaluations of the so-called
reduced
bispectrum, and compared its magnitude with that of the power spectrum squared. In particular, the plot of the reduced bispectrum normalized to the power spectrum squared indicates that, on sub-Hubble scales, non-linear physics has more impact on the redshift drift signal than what we might naively expect considering the square of the first-order terms. This suggests that measuring the bispectrum of the redshift drift using real data, or in
N
-body relativistic numerical simulations, may be easier than currently thought.

In summary, we have computed the fully gauge-invariant non-linear relativistic corrections to the redshift drift, including source and observer perturbations, as seen by a free-falling observer. We have provided analytical evaluations of the bispectrum for the dominant terms on sub-Hubble scales, and given some numerical estimates, discussing about their potential as probes of alternative models of Gravity and Cosmology.
It would be interesting to compare our analytical results with
N
-body numerical simulations
[
73
,
74
,
75
]
, and to understand the contamination that primordial non-Gaussianity evaluated through this observable could
be subject to because of the peculiar non-linearities of the redshift drift
(similarly, for example, to what done in
[
89
]
for the galaxy number count). Moreover, the investigation of the role of vector and tensor perturbations at first and second order will be addressed in a future work.
