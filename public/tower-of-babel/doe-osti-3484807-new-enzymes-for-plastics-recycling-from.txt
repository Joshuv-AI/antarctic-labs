Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

The Agile BioFoundry (ABF) is a consortium of national laboratories dedicated to accelerating
biomanufacturing and enabling the bioeconomy. The ABF operates a flexible biotechnology
platform that can adjust to the needs of numerous government, academic, and industrial partners,
thus enabling them to rapidly develop and optimize the production of a wide range of
bioproducts. In this project, the ABF partnered with Birch Biosciences, Inc. to use ABF
technology to assist in the development of high-performance enzymes that enable circular,
sustainable recycling of poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET) plastics. A primary goal of this
project is to enable cost-effective, high yield, sustainable recycling of PET plastic packaging
products.

Summary of Research Results:

Task 1: TEA and LCA to inform protein design strategies and pilot plant scale-up

Work in this task will define the main enzyme engineering objectives from analysis and update
the models as new enzymes are developed in the program. The process modeling data and
process models will be provided by NLR to Birch Biosciences. The economics and
environmental impacts data, especially around life cycle inventory impacts relative to primary
polymer manufacturing, will be of critical importance for Birch Biosciences to build a
compelling business case for investments in scale-up facilities.

In collaboration with the team from Birch Biosciences, a plan for the techno-economic analysis
(TEA) and life cycle analysis (LCA) of enzymatic hydrolysis was developed. The analysis
consisted of an update to existing models (Singh et al. Joule 2021, Uekert et al., Green Chemistry
2022) and results with a list of optimistic parameters that could be feasible in the short term. This
analysis would also be extended to a best-case scenario with ideal improvements across process
and economic parameters.

To support the ongoing development of PET enzymatic hydrolysis, the existing detailed process
model previously developed by the team at NLR for the enzymatic hydrolysis of PET (Singh et
al., 2021) was adapted to understand impactful areas for improvement under two future design
scenarios relevant to Birch Biosciences: the first case was a “realistic” case scenario. This is an
optimistic but likely-to-be-achieved process based largely on best-in-class engineered enzymes
and process design. The process model and analysis was updated to align with economic and
process parameters as described below. Sensitivity analyses for the major cost and emissions
drivers were performed to understand where the primary opportunities lie for enzyme/process
improvements. The second case represented a “holy grail” scenario. This involved a reinvented
terephthalic acid (TPA) and ethylene glycol (EG) recovery process in particular using an in-tank
TPA precipitation model and reactive extraction EG recovery. Under these conditions, we
clarified the primary drivers of process cost and carbon emissions and Birch worked to develop
enzymes that meet these criteria.

The process model for enzymatic hydrolysis was updated in accordance with the input
parameters from the Birch team outlined in the TEA and LCA plan. The updated minimum
selling price (MSP) for rPET from the process was found to be $1.77/kg as shown in Figure 1.

Task 2: ML-based design of process-advantaged PET hydrolase

This task aimed to train deep-learning models to map sequence data to enzyme function, and
iteratively employed these models to engineer PETases with markedly enhanced functional
properties, including activity on crystalline substrates, thermostability, and acid tolerance.

In initial project stages, a learning-driven sequence selection of ~500 sequences from genomic
databases combined with high-throughput expression and purification (Norton-Baker et al.,
Scientific Reports 2024) allowed for the identification of >100 novel PETases that have not been
previously reported in the literature. A scale-up expression and purification of the top performers
from these active enzymes was performed to produce sufficient quantities to enable validation of
their thermostability and activity. Additionally, the samples were prepared for X-ray
crystallographic analysis with one novel crystal structure fully determined. Scale-up of enzyme
production in E. coli revealed challenges in translating the small-scale well-plate expression to
liter-scale flask expressions using autoinduction media. We hypothesize that lower aeration in
the flasks reduced the expression yields and found IPTG-induction to perform better.

Using data from the screening of ~200 putative PETases, regions of the protein sequence that
were found to impact temperature and pH tolerance were identified with statistical significance
and a small number of new designs were suggested and tested. The approach used was
multifaceted and probed data on the following axes:

 1. Sequence conservation differences between groups of PETases with non-zero activity at
 standard conditions and those at conditions of interest. This strategy directly provides
 important mutations to apply to one of the scaffolds in the dataset for pH tolerance and
 high temperature tolerance. The amount of data comparing substrates was too low to
 repeat this methodology.
 2. Surface properties of the same performance groups as above were compared and regions
 with statistically significant differences identified. These were then mapped back to
 residues of scaffolds within the dataset. This analysis does not directly provide mutations,
 but instead regions of focus for variation exploration.
 3. Globally trained Topt and pHopt predictors were used to identify regions of attention
 important for these models to make predictions. Like surface properties, this analysis
 provides regions of focus as opposed to suggested mutations.
 4. pKa of titratable residues in the dataset long thought to be critical for pH tolerance were
 compared between enzymes active in acidic conditions and those active at standard
 conditions. Statistical comparison of predicted pKa revealed a few locations on the
 scaffold with significant differences between the performance groups, including the
 catalytic histidine. Further, when a position on the sequence was statistically more likely
 to observe a titratable residue for low pH activity, its most common amino acid was
 identified. This includes the position directly upstream from the catalytic histadine, where
 an aspartic acid was observed in many low pH active PETases.
 5. Additional domains observed within the dataset were clustered based on homology and
 correlated to performance conditions, where it was found that proteins exhibiting a Ricin
 B Lectin carbohydrate binding module were correlated with low pH performance. Other

 carbohydrate binding modules were observed in the dataset but not correlated with any
 conditions of interest.

With these findings, a set of 21 designs were suggested for testing in house at NLR.

The significant positions identified within this analysis were mapped back to the scaffolds of
interest for testing in Birch’s platform (ESM047, TEP015, ESM052, ESM050, TEP0008,
TEP179, DP090, TEP013, ESM065, ESP169, ESM128, TEP015, ESM047, ESM052). To
expand these findings into a larger library for experiment, a pipeline was created to identify an
82-residue length region within the protein was identified for each scaffold to be critical for pH
and temperature tolerance. Within this window, positions are weighted by the number of factors
associated with that residue, and some max depth number of positions sampled according to this
weight vector. A number of variants are sampled from this space by conditionally sampling the
gradients of ESM, which should produce far fewer extreme missense variants than random
sampling. This procedure can be repeated by sampling different positions at different mutation
depths, for each of Birch’s scaffolds.

A small library of variants was extracted based on direct measurement of factors important for
low pH tolerance and high temperature tolerance, pending experimental analysis. Larger libraries
of variants for the independent targeting of low pH activity and high temperature activity are
ready to be suggested for each scaffold of interest. Too few enzymes were tested against
amorphous film to repeat these strategies to compare substrate activity. This can instead be done
by looking at raw activity value instead of comparing groups, but this has yet to be done.

Using collected activity data from 213 enzymes from natural diversity screening, 116 active
PETases were identified. These PETases demonstrated moderate to low activity compared with
the highly engineered benchmarks, but with varying preference for PET substrates with higher
crystallinity and differing pH optima. Enzymes with unique activity profiles were shared with
Birch to enable their concurrent engineering campaigns. Statistical analysis of the enzyme
sequences paired with their activity data with different substrates and pH levels allowed for the
identification of hotspots (important amino acid positions in the sequence) that influence low pH
and crystalline activity. This predictor of activity achieves 0.7 AUROC (metric defining ability
to correctly differentiate active and inactive). These hotspots will be targeted in future work for
mutations according to maximal cumulative likelihood from ESM2. In screening a large number
of naturally derived putative PETases, we discovered that many demonstrate higher activity on
crystalline PET than amorphous PET. This finding was contrary to our expectation that the
amorphous PET would be more amenable to depolymerization due to superior enzyme
accessibility to the hydrolysable sites. However, we hypothesize the surface interactions between
the enzyme and the substrate may be influencing the observed activities.

In late project stages, we began training a large (>1 bil) parameter multimodal neural network for
conditional design of protein towards target pH and temperature. As a first step, we conducted
pure supervised temperature prediction training in order to benchmark against existing scorers
(without conditional MLM training). Multimodal model architecture must meet approximately
the same scores as benchmarks to move on. The first training (4 H100 gpus, 8 hours, 10% data
subset) did not achieve this, which was expected. Future work will include hyperparameter
optimization and scale up to the full dataset with the goal of achieving below 3 MAE (Figure 6).

If benchmarks are reached, a second test will be conducted—pure MLM training without
supervised task—to confirm this method of incorporating temperature information to improve
masked filling compared to the sequence-only model. After this test, the full proposed training
can be conducted.

 Figure 6. Validation MAE of temperature predictions over the course of the first trial training
 (supervised only loss) on a 50000 data subset.

Finally, Project 740 has been published. We have trained the ML model to predict activity at low
pH of Project 740 enzymes that uses only charge distribution in space features, achieving the
same performance as the 650M parameter pretrained protein language model embeddings, as
reported in the paper, with classification AUROC of 0.80 vs 0.79. This suggests that PET
hydrolase activity (of PETase homologs) at low pH is largely a function of the charge
distribution near the catalytic histidine; -charge mutational engineering may reduce pH optima.

Task 3: Application of biosensors and microfluidics tools to quantify multi-parameter
performance of up to 106-member enzyme variant libraries and feedback to improve ML
algorithms.

For microfluidics droplet-based screening, Birch Biosciences will fully perform this task.
Therein, they will employ PET substrates that can be tuned for crystallinity ranges (including in
the 25-40% crystallinity range needed in the proposed work) prepared through a protocol that is
currently under development.

The PETase activity of the library will be measured by the enzymatic hydrolysis of homogenous
PET substrates. Since PETases do not penetrate polymer particles, only the surface-exposed PET
ester bonds are accessible. Increasing the surface by decreasing particle size will accelerate the
rate of hydrolysis. The PETase library will be expressed in droplet-encapsulated E. coli and the
enzyme secreted using the native protein secretion system or cytoplasmically produced and
released using an in-droplet lysis method. After enzyme production and secretion, the droplets
will be heated to the target temperature (65-70°C) to identify active enzymes. Birch has already
developed this system and will apply it as part of this work.

In order to validate and synchronize results from microfluidics and related screening campaigns
in the participant and laboratory facilities, NREL will provide their Opentrons-2 based workflow
to Birch Biosciences for 96 deepwell plate expression, induction, and lysis of PET hydrolase
enzymes.

Deliverables (Participant):

 • 3.A. Manufacture and fully characterize plastic nanoparticles from 0-40% crystallinity to
 be uniformly packaged within a droplet-based system.
 • 3.B. Develop a biosensor with high dynamic range for TPA (10 nM to 2.5 mM).
 • 3.C. Use this enzyme screening tool to test new enzyme variants and provide feedback to
 Task 2.

This task focused on optimal biosensor and microfluidics tools for HTP characterization of the
library of engineered PETases. This work was fully performed by Birch Biosciences.

Birch made significant advancements in the development and optimization of its enzyme
engineering platform for poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET) degradation. A key achievement
was the acquisition of new analytical instrumentation that allows precise measurement of
polymer crystallinity, crucial for tuning PET nanoparticles to specific crystallinity ranges (e.g.,
25–40%) required in experimental scopes. They improved nanoparticle generation techniques,
enabling uniform packaging of PET nanoparticles into polyacrylamide beads used in
microfluidics assays. Furthermore, they identified and sourced low-cost PET bottle flakes to
serve as the primary substrate for laboratory testing, offering a sustainable and economical
option for experimentation.

Birch enhanced the sensitivity of its fluorescence-based PET degradation assay, improving
accuracy and validation of screening results from microfluidics and 96-well plate campaigns.
Through process optimization, they operationalized their high-throughput droplet-based
screening assay by employing a fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) instrument for
enzymatic activity sorting. This milestone was coupled with the development of chemistries that
conjugate single enzyme coding sequences to PET beads infused with fluorescent dyes. The dyes
respond to plastic degradation by adjusting fluorescence levels, enabling ultra-high-throughput
screening of large libraries of PETases based on activity level.

Further improvements included developing an automated sequencing pipeline using Oxford
Nanopore technology to analyze full-length coding sequences from enzyme variants obtained in
droplet assays. This system combines wet lab protocols and computational analysis software to
generate robust and structured genotype-to-phenotype datasets critical for enzyme engineering.
The pipeline integrates improved DNA amplification protocols, optimized cloning workflows,
and the capability to effectively process and analyze millions of variants. These refinements
support the screening of top-performing enzyme variants identified from droplet assays in
secondary plate-based screens.

Birch demonstrated successful screening of approximately 25,000 protein variants through the
high-throughput droplet assay, correlating activity data with DNA sequence-derived enrichment
scores. They finalized workflows that enable rapid downstream testing and evaluation of
enriched enzyme variants, leveraging improved DNA recovery and cloning methodologies.
Enhancements in post-FACS DNA retrieval protocols, including nuclease inhibitor proteins,
improved the DNA yield for sequencing and re-transformation, resulting in highly measurable
activity enrichment levels (e.g., threefold increase comparing pre- and post-sorting activity).
These workflows are now being applied to explore mutational spaces for optimized PETase
variants.

On the computational side, Birch refined the NextFlow-based sequencing pipeline operating on
AWS cloud systems. They improved file transfer handling, adjusted memory and compute
availability, and implemented batch scripting models to reduce operational costs. Previously
cumbersome processing techniques for barcode information associated with enzyme variant
sequencing were streamlined, reducing computational complexity and improving pipeline
reliability for large sequencing files (>10GB). Sensitivity analyses of the pipeline revealed read
depth as the most critical factor for accurate DNA variant calls, offering guidelines for the
required depth during future experiments.

Birch has implemented updates to droplet microfluidics protocols that integrate all recent process
improvements, ensuring replicability and reliability as operations expand and new team members
are onboarded. Insights gained during this reporting period highlight the trade-offs between
managing computational development and advancing experimental efforts, emphasizing the
importance of addressing pipeline improvements early in the project lifecycle. Birch continues to
refine and expand the droplet pipeline, leveraging machine learning-guided engineering and
structured datasets to optimize PETase enzyme variants for high activity and efficiency in PET
degradation.

Task 4: Development of a thermo-tolerant MHETase enzyme.

We will use ML-guided protein design, rational enzyme engineering, and ML-guided directed
evolution to develop new MHETase enzymes that are able to function at the optimal conditions
for PET depolymerization. This will include starting from various enzyme parents, such as the
PET046 enzyme recently reported by Streit et al. (bioRXiv, 2023). We will develop evolutionary
libraries for MHETase enzymes, and screen their activity in plate reader-based assays.

Deliverables (Participant and Contractor):

 • 4.A. Develop an evolutionary library of MHETase sequences based on an active chassis
       (e.g., PET046).
 • 4.B. Use this library to identify improved variants that are able to perform at optimal T
 and pH values, and provide sequences of both improved and not-improved variants to
 ML (Task 2) for ML-guided directed evolution.
 • 4.C. Test the optimal MHETase enzyme in a bioreactor with an optimal PET hydrolase at
 the end of the project.

The goal of this task was to use ML-guided protein design, rational enzyme engineering, and
ML-guided directed evolution to develop new mono(2-hydroxyethyl) terephthalate hydrolase
(MHETase) enzymes that are able to function at the optimal conditions for PET
depolymerization. This work started from various enzyme parents, such as the PET046 enzyme
recently reported by Streit et al. (bioRXiv, 2023). We will develop evolutionary libraries for
MHETase enzymes, and screen their activity in plate reader-based assays.

NLR successfully engineered a thermo-tolerant MHETase, with a combination of natural
diversity screening and directed evolution, which will be reported in a peer-reviewed manuscript
and corresponding patent application imminently. With the top engineered variant, we
demonstrated the compatibility of thermostable MHETase addition with a benchmark PETase,
LCCICCG. A statistically significant reduction in MHET accumulation was measured in
bioreactor deconstructions of post-consumer PET waste.
