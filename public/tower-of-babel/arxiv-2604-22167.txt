Estimating Tail Risks in Language Model Output Distributions
Rico Angell, Raghav Singhal, Zachary Horvitz, Zhou Yu, Rajesh Ranganath, Kathleen McKeown, He He

Abstract
Language models are increasingly capable and are being rapidly deployed on a population-level scale. As a result, the safety of these models is increasingly high-stakes. Fortunately, advances in alignment have significantly reduced the likelihood of harmful model outputs. However, when models are queried billions of times in a day, even rare worst-case behaviors will occur. Current safety evaluations focus on capturing the distribution of inputs that yield harmful outputs. These evaluations disregard the probabilistic nature of models and their tail output behavior. To measure this tail risk, we propose a method to efficiently estimate the probability of harmful outputs for any input query . Instead of naive brute-force sampling from the target model, where harmful outputs could be rare, we operationalize importance sampling by creating unsafe versions of the target model. These unsafe versions enable sample-efficient estimation by making harmful outputs more probable. On benchmarks measuring misuse and misalignment, these estimates match brute-force Monte Carlo estimates using 10 10 – 20 × 20\times fewer samples. For example, we can estimate probability of harmful outputs on the order of 10 − 4 10^{-4} with just 500 500 samples. Additionally, we find that these harmfulness estimates can reveal the sensitivity of models to perturbations in model input and predict deployment risks. Our work demonstrates that accurate rare-event estimation is both critical and feasible for safety evaluations. Code available here .

Introduction

Language models are queried billions of times every day
1
1

1

https://www.theverge.com/news/710867/openai-chatgpt-daily-prompts-2-billion
and have become integrated into daily life and workflows for millions of users.
The combination of increased capabilities exhibited by these models and population-level deployment scenarios elevates the potential for harmful outcomes.
Hence, accurately evaluating model safety is imperative to mitigating potential harm.
While alignment approaches have significantly improved the safety of these models, even rare unsafe behaviors will inevitably occur at deployment-level scale.
2

If there are a
n=1,000,000

\varepsilon=10^{-6}
probability of resulting in a harmful output, deploying such a model, on average, would produce at least one harmful output each day,
1-(1-\varepsilon)^{n}\approx n\varepsilon=1
.

There are two axes to evaluating model safety: the
distribution of inputs
that elicit harmful outputs, and the
distribution of outputs
generated by the model for any query. Existing works primarily focus on the distribution of input contexts, by either creating a diverse sets of prompts or searching for adversarial inputs to surface harmful outputs
(
Wu & Hilton 2024
;
Mazeika et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2025
;

;

;
Hughes et al. 2024
;
Andriushchenko et al. 2025
)
. For a given input, these evaluations sample only a single (or a few) output(s) from the language model. Evaluations with a small number of model samples are noisy, may provide a false sense of security that a model will safely handle a particular context, and may fail to surface rare outputs that would emerge after deployment.

In this work, we focus on estimating harmful behaviors exhibited in the output distribution. Concretely, we estimate the probability of the target model producing a harmful outputs in response to any given input query.
For some queries, harmful outputs appear only after
10,000 or more
samples. Hence, the probability of harmful outputs is small, but non-zero.
Estimating these probabilities by naive repeated sampling is computationally infeasible at scale.
Instead, we propose a sample-efficient method for estimating probabilities of harmful outputs, even when such instances are rare.

The core of our approach is importance sampling
(
Robert et al. 1999
)
, a well-studied method for rare-event estimation. Rather than sampling from the target model, importance sampling leverages outputs from another model, called the
proposal model
, that is more likely to produce the desired behavior and yield low-variance estimates. The key challenge of applying importance sampling to estimate harmfulness is designing an unsafe model that will generate the same harmful behaviors as the target.

In our work, we construct highly controllable proposal models using activation steering
(
Arditi et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
and automatically optimize for an unsafe model configuration that yields low-variance estimates. We demonstrate that our proposed method enables estimation of both the probability of unsafe responses to harmful queries, along with hallucination and sycophancy rates, using 5% of the samples required for Monte Carlo estimates.

Our estimates also reveal the sensitivity of model output distributions to perturbations in model input, and that even non-adversarial rewrites of queries can significantly shift harmfulness probabilities by multiple orders of magnitude. Therefore, a single query-level estimate is a poor proxy for deployment behavior. Fortunately, using extreme value theory
(
De Haan & Ferreira 2006
)
, we find that query-level estimates on an evaluation set can enable accurately predicting model average and worst-case harm on
unseen queries
. Our efficient estimates address a limitation with previous dataset-level forecasting work, which was limited to hand-crafted proxies for query-level risk
(
Jones et al. 2025
)
.

Contributions.
Our contributions are as follows:

•

We find that instruction fine-tuned models respond harmfully to many queries with repeated sampling, even without

•

\mathbf{c}

3
Efficient Rare Unsafe Event Estimation

Given a target language model
p_{\textrm{target}}
, an input context
\mathbf{c}
, and a judge of harm

h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})
, we seek to estimate
query-risk
which takes the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}):=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\text{target}}(\cdot~|~\mathbf{c})}[h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})=1].

(1)

Where
h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})
is a binary classifier for harm. When the likelihood of producing harmful outputs is low, estimation will require a large number of samples, making model evaluations prohibitively expensive. Instead, we propose a method to efficiently estimate
Equation

1
using importance sampling
(
Owen & Zhou 2000
)
.

3.1
Importance Sampling

Importance sampling generates samples from a
proposal
model
q_{\phi}
and then re-weights those samples using the ratio of likelihoods between the proposal and target model to efficiently estimate
Equation

1
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\text{target}}(\cdot~|~\mathbf{c})}\left[h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{x}}p_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{x}}q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})\frac{p_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})}{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})}h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q_{\phi}(\cdot~|~\mathbf{c})}\left[\frac{p_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})}{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})}h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})\right]

(2)

where it is required that the proposal model and the target share support.
More specifically, given an input context
\mathbf{c}
and any output
\mathbf{x}
with
p_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})>0
, it is required that
q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})>0
, in other words
\textsc{KL}\!\left(p_{\text{target}}\,||\,q_{\phi}\right)<\infty
.
In practice,
k
outputs are sampled
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}\sim q_{\phi}(\cdot~|~\mathbf{c})
and then we can estimate
Equation

2
by:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{p_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}~|~\mathbf{c})}{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{i}~|~\mathbf{c})}h(\mathbf{x}_{i};\mathbf{c}).

(3)

The estimate is unbiased, i.e.
\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c})\right]=\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c})
, and converges to the true value as the number of samples increases, i.e.
\lim_{k\to\infty}\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c})=\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c})
.

The choice of the proposal distribution
q_{\phi}
is critical to the sample-efficiency and reliability of importance sampling.
Since importance sampling is unbiased, the estimation error is determined by the variance of the estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c})
.
The optimal proposal distribution, with the lowest variance estimates, is
q_{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})\propto p_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})
, see
Chopin et al. 2020
. Unfortunately, sampling from this distribution is generally intractable.

3.2
Constructing Unsafe Proposals

In this section, we describe a practical method for constructing and refining unsafe proposal models that assign a higher likelihood to harmful outputs under the support of the target model. We first define a family of unsafe proposal models, and then directly optimize for configurations that yield low-variance estimators.

Activation Steering.

To increase the probability of harmful outputs, we leverage prior work on activation steering
(
Arditi et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
. The core idea of these methods is to construct a vector that, when added to the residual stream of the transformer, shifts the output distribution of the model.
Constructing these steering vectors is relatively simple and training-free.

To construct a steering vector for harmfulness, we collect unpaired sets of safe and unsafe examples, and take the difference-in-means of the hidden activations across these two sets at some hidden layer of the transformer
(
Marks & Tegmark 2024
;
Panickssery et al. 2024
)
.
At inference time, we can control the degree to which the output distribution is shifted by scaling the steering vector by some scaler
\lambda_{\text{steer}}\geq 0
before adding it to the residual stream.
Steering vectors enable modulating the harmfulness of the resulting model via steering vector intensity. See
Appendix

D
for more details.

Additionally, we note that there are several other possible methods for constructing unsafe models including sparse autoencoders
(
Cunningham et al. 2023
;
O’Brien et al. 2025
)
, prompting
(
Neumann et al. 2025
)
, and fine-tuning
(
Wallace et al. 2025
)
.

Refining Proposal Models.

Next, given an unsafe steered model, we discuss two techniques to bring the proposal model closer to the target model to reduce the variance of the importance sampling estimator:

•

Mixture of models:
At each step, we can define a mixture model
q_{\text{mixed}}

(
Owen & Zhou 2000
;
Hesterberg 1988
)
which interpolates between an unsafe model
q_{\phi}
and the target model
p_{\text{target}}
modulated by a mixing parameter
\alpha_{\text{mix}}\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle q_{\text{mix}}(x_{t}~|~\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1},\mathbf{c};\alpha)

\displaystyle:=(1-\alpha_{\text{mix}})q_{\phi}(x_{t}~|~\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1},\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha_{\text{mix}}\,p_{\text{target}}(x_{t}~|~\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1},\mathbf{c}).

Setting
\alpha_{\text{mix}}>0
ensures that
\textsc{KL}\!\left(p_{\text{target}}\,||\,q_{\text{mix}}\right)<\infty
.

•

Model switching:
For generating an output sequence
\mathbf{x}
of length
T
, we can sample the first
t_{\text{switch}}
tokens from an unsafe model, and then
switch
to the target model:

\displaystyle q_{\text{switch}}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}~|~\mathbf{c};t_{\text{switch}})

\displaystyle\qquad:=\underbrace{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t_{\text{switch}}}~|~\mathbf{c})}_{\text{First $t_{\text{switch}}$ tokens}}\underbrace{p_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{\text{switch}}+1:T}~|~\mathbf{x}_{1:t_{\text{switch}}},\mathbf{c})}_{\text{Remaining $T-t_{\text{switch}}$ tokens}}.

Both these choices reduce the estimator variance (i.e. error) and ensure that
\textsc{KL}\!\left(p_{\text{target}}\,||\,q_{\textsc{proposal}}\right)<\infty
.

Proposal Selection.

The optimal proposal for importance sampling is
q_{\text{opt}}\propto p_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})
, however, sampling from this distribution is infeasible. Instead, we select the closest approximation
q_{\phi}
to the optimal distribution in our family of our unsafe models,

\displaystyle q_{\text{unsafe}}:=\argmin_{\phi}\textsc{KL}\!\left(q_{\text{opt}}\Big\|q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})\right),

(4)

where
\phi=(\lambda_{\text{steer}},t_{\text{switch}},\alpha_{\text{mix}})
. However, estimating
Equation

4
is again infeasible since sampling from the optimal distribution directly is intractable.

However, we can estimate
Equation

4
using importance sampling over the family of proposal models that we have defined.
We adapt the
cross-entropy method

(
De Boer et al. 2005
)
to search for the proposal hyperparameters (i.e.
\lambda_{\text{steer}}
,
\alpha_{\text{mix}}
, and
t_{\text{switch}}
) that minimize the variance of our estimate. Formally, we generate data using a model
q_{\text{rand}}
, which ensembles
k
proposal models by selecting a
random
set of hyper-parameters
\phi_{i}
for each sample
\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim q_{\phi_{i}}(\cdot~|~\mathbf{c})
.
Then, to evaluate any hyperparameter setting
\phi=(\lambda_{\text{steer}},t_{\text{switch}},\alpha_{\text{mix}})
, we compute the following cross-entropy objective:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\textsc{ce}}(\phi)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}w(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{c})h(\mathbf{x}_{i};\mathbf{c})\log q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{i}~|~\mathbf{c}).

(5)

where
w(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c}):={p_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}~|~\mathbf{c})}/{q_{\text{rand}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}~|~\mathbf{c})}
.
We select the proposal model within our predefined family that minimizes
Equation

5
and denote this proposal model
q_{\text{unsafe}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})
.

In practice, we select the proposal model by estimating
Equation

5
using a subset of queries from the dataset, and then select a
single
proposal model that is used for all the importance sampling estimates.

Our full end-to-end pipeline takes a target model
p_{\text{target}}
, a dataset of queries
\{\mathbf{c}_{i}\}
, and judge
h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})
, and produces estimates of the probability of harmful outputs for each query. In summary, our proposed estimation pipeline is as follows:

1.

Construct a family of unsafe proposal models using activation steering. This family of proposal models is defined by a grid over possible hyperparameters,
\phi=(\lambda_{\text{steer}},t_{\text{switch}},\alpha_{\text{mix}})\in\Phi
.

2.

Optimize the proposal model over
\Phi
using a subset of the input contexts in the given dataset by selecting the
\phi\in\Phi
that minimizes
Equation

5
:

q_{\text{unsafe}}:=q_{\phi^{*}}\quad\text{where}\quad\phi^{*}=\argmin_{\phi\in\Phi}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\textsc{ce}}(\phi).

3.

For all input queries
\mathbf{c}_{i}
, use (
3
) to estimate
\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})
.

From Query-Level to Deployment Risks

Prior to deployment, frontier labs perform extensive model red-teaming to catch harmful behaviors
(
Anthropic 2025
;
OpenAI 2025a
)
.
Previous sections evaluated the probability of harmful outputs given specific queries. However, the purpose of risk evaluations is to infer how models will behave after deployment on new,
unseen queries
.

In this section, we utilize our efficient query-level estimates to study model behavior over a distribution of input queries. First, we find that these estimates reveal the sensitivity of model output distributions to small, yet semantically equivalent, changes in the input context. Our findings demonstrate that, in isolation, a single query-level harm estimate is not indicative of model behavior. However, we show that efficient estimates over multiple queries can enable
accurately approximating risk on unseen queries.

6.2
Predicting Worst-Case Risk on Unseen Queries

Given access to a distribution of queries
\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}
, we can sample
n_{\text{eval}}
queries,
\mathbf{c}_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}
, and estimate individual query-risks
\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})
for each
\mathbf{c}_{i}
using the our proposed method (
Section

3
). These estimates of
\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})
enable forecasting risks for unseen queries. The field of
extreme value theory

(
De Haan & Ferreira 2006
)
has developed a statistical framework to make such forecasts.

For instance, one measure of risk is: if we see
n
queries at deployment, what is the
probability that there will be a worst-query probability
above a threshold
\tau
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})>\tau\right]

(6)

\displaystyle\quad=1-\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})\leq\tau\right]

\displaystyle\quad=1-\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})\leq\tau\right]

\displaystyle\quad=1-\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{c}_{1}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{1})\leq\tau\right]^{n}

(7)

Here, we note that using
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})
estimated on an
evaluation set
can be used to approximate the cumulative density function
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{c}_{1}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{1})\leq\tau\right]
, enabling approximation of
Equation

6
for
unseen queries
. We include additional details in
Appendix

F
.

We randomly split the
StrongReject
paraphrase data, consisting of 7725 (309 queries, 25 paraphrases each) queries into an evaluation (30%) and deployment (70%) query set. In
Figure

7
, we observe that using the harm estimates on the evaluation set enables a reliable approximation of
Equation

6
at different harmfulness thresholds. This result demonstrates that efficient query-level harm estimates can be used to model the risk over unseen queries.

Related Work

Jones et al. 2025
use extreme value theory to forecast worst-case risk on unseen queries, given access to the
distribution of inputs
after deployment. Their forecasting method requires an estimate for the probability of harm for evaluation queries. However, the authors note the expensiveness of estimating this quantity by sampling, and instead use the sequence probability of a handcrafted
fixed
target output sequence as a proxy. Our work addresses this limitation and complements their framework by computing reliable and compute-efficient estimates of harmful output probability. We include additional details in
appendix

F
.

Wu & Hilton 2024
propose an estimation framework for the probability of sampling a query from a distribution of inputs that elicits a
target output
,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{c}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}}[\text{harm}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c})=1]
where
\mathbf{x}
is sampled
deterministically
from a target model. Our work focuses on estimating the harm for any query
\mathbf{c}
. Our harm estimates for individual queries can be combined with approaches similar to those proposed by
Wu & Hilton 2024
, which compute estimates over distributions of queries.

8
Conclusion

Current safety evaluations focus on surfacing
input contexts
that elicit harmful outputs, but neglect the probabilistic nature of language models. Critically, we show that in many cases harmful outputs are rare under a “safe” model’s output distribution, but would occur at deployment-level scale. Our results are a call-to-action:
evaluations must account for tail risks over both inputs and outputs
.
While catching these rare behaviors is daunting, we show that it is feasible. We provide a method for efficient estimation that can be incorporated into safety pipelines to better evaluate safeguards
(
Sharma et al. 2025
)
and guide deployment decisions.

9
Limitations

Our method requires constructing an unsafe model to estimate the probability of worst-case harmful outputs. By using steering vectors, we assume access to the model weights, who could use our method. Future work could explore methods for constructing proposals that only require access to model logits. We use an LLM-as-a-judge approach, which can make mistakes. Furthermore, a sufficiently strong misaligned system can produce harmful behaviors that evade detection.

Impact Statement

As models grow increasingly capable and are being deployed to hundreds of millions of users, robust safety evaluations are critical to avoid harm. In this work, we propose an efficient and reliable method for estimating the probability of harmful outputs from a model. This estimation method can be a useful tool for model evaluations and guide deployment decisions.

Appendix A
The Safety Evaluation Status Quo

Existing AI safety and security evaluations focus on exploring diverse input contexts that attempt to cover the distribution of possible inputs
(
Wu & Hilton 2024
;
Mazeika et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Chao et al. 2024
;
Zou et al. 2023
;
Hughes et al. 2024
;
Andriushchenko et al. 2025
)
. They make an implicit assumption that certain input contexts elicit harmful outputs, and others do not. Often times only a single output is sampled from the language model to test its behavior, especially in the case of jailbreaking. This neglects the probabilistic nature of AI models.
In fact,
Huang et al. 2023
showed that models could be manipulated into responding harmfully to unsafe prompts by simply searching over the hyperparameters used for decoding.

Other AI safety evaluations sample on the order of hundreds of samples per input context
(
Greenblatt et al. 2024a
;
MacDiarmid et al. 2025
;
Greenblatt et al. 2024b
;
Betley et al. 2025
)
, but as we have shown, this is still probably not enough to fully understand the model’s propensity to misbehave.
Additionally, many of these safety evaluations require extreme unrealistic contexts to elicit misaligned behavior. In contrast, our approach does not require such harsh prompting and can estimate the probability of misaligned behavior for any input context (see
Section

5
).

Appendix D
Steering Vector Computation Details

In this section, we detail how steering vectors are computed and used to steer the target model towards harmful and misaligned behaviors. The methodology presented in this section is based on prior work
(
Arditi et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
, and is the foundation of creating unsafe proposal models critical to the success of our proposed methodology.

D.1
Refusal Direction

We follow
Arditi et al. 2024
when computing the “refusal direction” for each target model.
This refusal direction is used to “turn-off” safeguards and create as close to a helpful-only version of the target model as possible.

Data.

The process begins by collecting sets of harmful and harmless input contexts, denoted
\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmful}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmless}}
, respectively. We construct a training-validation split of both of these sets as follows.
\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmful}}^{\text{(train)}}
is comprised of 128 harmful input context from
AdvBench

(
Zou et al. 2023
)
,
MaliciousInstruct

(
Huang et al. 2024
)
, and TDC2023
(
Mazeika et al. 2023
)
.
\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmful}}^{\text{(val)}}
is comprised of 32 instructions from
HarmBench

(
Mazeika et al. 2024
)
.

\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmless}}^{\text{(train)}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmless}}^{\text{(val)}}
are comprised of 128 and 32 input contexts from
ALPACA

(
Taori et al. 2023
)
, respectively.
We use the train sets to compute potential refusal directions and validation sets to select from the potential refusal directions.
Moreover, this method to compute the refusal direction is
training-free
.

Computing Candidate Refusal Directions.

Let
\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(l)}(\mathbf{c})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represent the residual stream activation at the
t^{\text{th}}
token position after layer
\ell
of the transformer has been applied to input context
\mathbf{c}
.
We start by computing the mean activations for each training input context for all
\ell\in\{0,1,...,\lfloor 0.8*L\rfloor\}
and each post-instruction token position
t
(i.e. the shared subsequence of tokens in the chat template that appear directly before model responds, but after the query)

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}^{(\ell)}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmful}}^{\text{(train)}}|}\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmful}}}\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(l)}(\mathbf{c}),\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\nu}_{t}^{(\ell)}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmless}}^{\text{(train)}}|}\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmless}}}\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(l)}(\mathbf{c}).

Then, we compute the candidate refusal directions using the
difference-in-means

(
Marks & Tegmark 2024
;
Panickssery et al. 2024
)
approach, namely the refusal direction at layer
\ell
and token position
t
is defined as

\mathbf{r}_{t}^{(\ell)}:=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}^{(\ell)}-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{t}^{(\ell)}}{\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}^{(\ell)}-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{t}^{(\ell)}\|}.

Selecting a Single Refusal Direction.

We use the validation sets
\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmful}}^{\text{(val)}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmless}}^{\text{(val)}}
to select a single direction
\mathbf{r}_{\star}
. The refusal direction is selected such that when ablated from the residual stream, the target model is helpful and harmful on input contexts from
\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmful}}^{(val)}
and does not affect the output logits significantly on input contexts from
\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmless}}^{\text{(val)}}
. In addition, adding the refusal direction to the residual stream causes the model to refuse on
\mathcal{D}_{\text{harmless}}^{\text{(val)}}
.

Refusal Ablation for Activation Steering.

To construct the harmful proposal model, we (partially) ablate the refusal direction from the residual stream at all token positions
t
and layers
\ell
,

\tilde{\mathbf{h}}\leftarrow\mathbf{h}-\lambda_{\text{steer}}\mathbf{r}_{\star}\mathbf{r}_{\star}^{\top}\mathbf{h},\quad\lambda_{\text{steer}}\in[0,1].

The steered residual stream activations
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}
are then passed to the next layer of the transformer as the forward pass proceeds. For importance sampling, we often set
\lambda_{\text{steer}}<1
.

D.2
Misaligned Persona Vectors

We follow
Chen et al. 2025
in constructing steering vectors for misaligned traits (i.e.
personas
), namely, sycophancy and hallucinating. For both traits we consider, this work provides a dataset of contrastive system prompts, evaluation prompts, and judge prompts (one for judging coherence and one for judging the presence of the misaligned trait in the sampled response). Throughout this section, we use
trait
to mean either sycophancy or hallucination, interchangeably.

Contrastive Model Response Pairs.

The process starts by sampling responses from the target model for every evaluation prompt using both the positive and negative system prompts, respectively. The positive system prompt encourages the model to express the misaligned trait and the negative system prompt simply encourages the model to be helpful (i.e. a standard system prompt). The target model’s responses are then filtered based on whether or not the desired misaligned trait was expressed (judge score
\geq 50
for positive examples, judge score
<50
for negative examples) or not as well as whether or not the response was coherent (judge score
\geq 50
for all examples). This creates two sets of prompt-response pairs: one set demonstrating the misaligned trait and one set not.

Extracting Persona Vectors.

After filtering the the prompt-response pairs, the residual stream activations are extracted at every layer of the target model. The residual stream activations are then averaged across all response token positions. Again, the steering vector is computed using a difference-in-means approach between the residual stream activates from the responses that exhibit the misaligned trait and those that do not. The result is one steering vector per layer for both traits.

Controlling Misaligned Personas with Activation Steering.

After the steering vectors
\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)}
for each layer
\ell
have been computed, we can elicit misaligned traits using the following activation steering:

\tilde{\mathbf{h}}^{(\ell)}\leftarrow\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}+\lambda_{\text{steer}}\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)},\quad\lambda_{\text{steer}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

During proposal optimization for estimating misaligned traits, we optimize over both the layer
\ell
and the steering coefficient
\lambda_{\text{steer}}
in addition to the model mixing and switching hyperparameters
\alpha_{\text{mix}}
and
t_{\text{switch}}
.

Appendix E
Proposal Selection

As we vary the steering vector intensity
\lambda_{\text{steer}}
, the proposal model produces samples that get higher average risk scores. However, this increase in average risk induces a greater mismatch between the target and proposal model leading to an increased variance in the estimates. Additionally, with the choice of mixing co-efficient,
\alpha_{\text{mix}}
and switch index,
t_{\text{switch}}
, we have a large set of proposals to select. Additionally, we see in
fig.

8
, the choice of the proposal model determines the variance of the importance sampling estimator. Therefore, selecting the proposal model is crucial to having reliable estimates.

Robert et al. 1999
;
Chatterjee & Diaconis 2018
show that the optimal proposal choice for importance sampling is:

\displaystyle q_{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})=\frac{1}{Z}p_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})

(8)

where
h\geq 0
and
Z=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\text{target}}(\cdot~|~\mathbf{c})}[h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})]
is the normalization constant. Sampling from
q_{\text{opt}}
for general functions
h
is not feasible. Instead, we propose to find the closest hyper-parameters,
\phi=(\lambda_{\text{steer}},\alpha_{\text{mix}},t_{\text{steer}})
that define
q_{\phi}
, in our family of proposal models, by minimizing the following objective:

\displaystyle\argmin_{\phi}\textsc{KL}\!\left(q_{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})\,||\,q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})\right).

(9)

This is a commonly used objective in rare-event estimation, called the cross-entropy method
De Boer et al. 2005
. As sampling from
p_{\text{opt}}
is infeasible, we can instead use another proposal
q_{\phi}^{\prime}
to estimate
eq.

9
using importance sampling:

\displaystyle\textsc{KL}\!\left(q_{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})~|~q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})\right)

\displaystyle=-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{q_{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})}\log q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{q_{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})}\log p_{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})

(10)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{Z}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})}h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})\log q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})+C

(11)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{Z}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{q_{\phi}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})}\frac{p_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})}{q_{\phi}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})}h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})\log q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})+C

(12)

where
C=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{q_{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})}\log q_{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})
and
Z
are constants independent of
\phi,\phi^{\prime}
. Now, note that to find
\phi
that minimize
eq.

12
does not require knowledge of
Z
or samples from
q_{\text{opt}}
, therefore, we define the objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\phi)=-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{q_{\phi^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})}\frac{p_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})}{q_{\phi^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})}h(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c})\log q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}~|~\mathbf{c})

(13)

Now, as to the choice of
q_{\phi}^{\prime}
, we use ensemble
k
proposal models by selecting a
random
set of hyperparameters
\phi_{i}
for each sample
\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim q_{\phi_{i}}(\cdot~|~\mathbf{c})
. Then, to evaluate any hyper-parameter setting
\phi=(\lambda_{\text{steer}},t_{\text{switch}},\alpha_{\text{mix}})
, we compute the following cross-entropy objective:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\textsc{ce}}(\phi)

\displaystyle:=-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{p_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}~|~\mathbf{c})}{q_{\text{rand}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}~|~\mathbf{c})}h(\mathbf{x}_{i};\mathbf{c})\log q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{i}~|~\mathbf{c}).

(14)

Computing
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\textsc{ce}}(\phi)
for any choice of
\phi
requires no sampling from
q_{\phi}
and can be done in a single forward pass per sample.

Appendix F
Predicting Worse-Case Risk on Unseen Queries

In this section, we detail how estimates for the probability of harmful outputs at the query-level can be used to predict the risk of harmful outputs on unseen queries using extreme value theory
(
De Haan & Ferreira 2006
)
.

With access to a distribution of
\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}
, we can sample
n_{\text{eval}}
queries,
\mathbf{c}_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}
, and estimate individual query-risks
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})
for each
\mathbf{c}_{i}
using the method detailed in
Section

3
. We then can then utilize the empirical cumulative density function of
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})
to estimate risks of new unseen queries.

There are several possible definitions of risk over unseen queries that can be estimated using query-level
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})
. We consider the following measure of risk:

If we see
n
new queries at deployment,

what is the probability that there will be a worst-query probability above a threshold
\tau
?

We can define this formally as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})>\tau\right].

(15)

Note that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})>\tau\right]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})\leq\tau\right],\qquad\text{ reversing the inequality}

(16)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}}\left[\forall i\leq n,\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})\leq\tau\right]\qquad\text{ as all $\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})$ are bounded their max}

(17)

\displaystyle=1-\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})\leq\tau\right],\qquad\text{ as $\mathbf{c}_{i}$ are sampled independently}

(18)

\displaystyle=1-\left[\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})\leq\tau\right]\right]^{n},\qquad\text{ as $\mathbf{c}_{i}$ are identically distributed}

(19)

where the last term
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})\leq\tau\right]
is the cumulative density function of the random variable
\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})
where
\mathbf{c}_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{query}}
. As a result, we can use the estimates
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\textsc{risk}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})
computed on the evaluation set to form an empirical
cdf
to approximate
eq.

15
. Furthermore, the extreme value theorem shows that
eq.

15
converge to
3
known limiting distributions, Gumbel, Frechet or Weibull family.

We do not rely on these asymptotic limits for our analysis. However,
Jones et al. 2025
show that these limiting distributions can be used to forecast the risks of unsafe behaviors over queries. Notably, rather than estimating
\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}
,
Jones et al. 2025
use a proxy for query harmfulness: the likelihood of
specific hand-crafted completions
.

Jones et al. 2025
note that this is due to the expensiveness of estimating the probability of query-level harmfulness probability with naive repeated sampling.
Therefore, our efficient estimates for
\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{risk}}
could potentially be used to further improve their forecasting methodology.
