Syndromic diabetes and hypergonadotropic hypogonadism in a boy with a 12q24.31–32 chromosomal deletion involving HNF1A, DHX37, and SETD1B
Authors: Tomohiro Nakagawa, Dai Saito, Hirohito Shima, Sayaka Kawashima, Dai Suzuki, Noriko Togashi, Atsuo Kikuchi, Junko Kanno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract.Chromosomal deletions involving several disease-associated genes exhibit diverse phenotypes. HNF1A is one of the causative genes of maturity-onset diabetes of the young. Furthermore, heterozygous variants in DHX37 and SETD1B, located near HNF1A, cause 46,XY disorders of sex development and syndromic neurodevelopmental disorders, respectively. This study describes a 12-yr-old boy with a 12q24.31–32 chromosomal deletion involving HNF1A, DHX37, and SETD1B. The boy was born small-for-gestational age, and experienced seizures, autism spectrum disorder, hearing loss, short stature, and intellectual disability. At five years of age, an 8.6-Mb chromosomal deletion at 12q24.31–32 was identified by array-based comparative genomic hybridization. At 12 yr of age, he developed islet autoantibody-negative diabetes. As the deletion region identified included HNF1A, this suggested that it is a cause of diabetes. Furthermore, SETD1B was considered to be a cause of intellectual disability and autism spectrum disorder. Owing to his intellectual disability, self-management of hypoglycemia was challenging; thus, nateglinide and insulin therapy were deemed effective for diabetes management rather than sulfonylurea drugs. Furthermore, DHX37 haploinsufficiency was considered a cause of hypogonadism. These findings emphasize the importance of diabetes management tailored to the characteristics of children with intellectual disabilities and the potential for hypogonadism due to DHX37 haploinsufficiency.Keywords: HNF1A, DHX37, SETD1B, MODY, chromosomal deletion

Highlights

● A boy with autism and a 12q24.31–32 deletion was diagnosed with diabetes.

● The deletion region in this patient included HNF1A, DHX37, and SETD1B.

● DHX37 haploinsufficiency might be involved in hypergonadotropic hypogonadism.

Introduction

Diverse phenotypes are displayed by patients with chromosomal deletions involving multiple disease-associated genes. Diabetes can arise in patients with deletions of genes responsible for insulin secretion (1). Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) exhibits an autosomal dominant inheritance form and manifests before 25 yr of age, and it is characterized by deficiency in insulin secretion attributable to pathogenic variants in transcription factors necessary for β-cell differentiation and function (2). Most cases of MODY are caused by variants in multiple genes, including HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, and HNF1B (3). Hepatocyte nuclear factor 1 homeobox A (HNF1A) is an essential transcriptional factor for tumor suppression in hepatic tissue, lipid metabolism, insulin secretion in pancreatic cells, protein synthesis, water absorption in the gastrointestinal tract, and glucose reabsorption in renal tissue (4). HNF1A plays a principal role in development, proliferation and insulin secretion in β-cells through regulating different genes responsible for pancreatic functions (5,6,7). Low-dose sulfonylureas are effective for HNF1A-MODY, and incretin-based agents are highly recommended as an adjunctive therapy (8,9,10,11). Genetic testing has clinical importance, as such treatment measures for specific monogenic diabetes subtypes have been established.

As an RNA helicase, DEAH-box helicase 37 (DHX37) plays a major role in ribosome biogenesis (12). Homozygous DHX37 variants lead to severe developmental disorders, whereas heterozygous variants cause isolated 46,XY disorders of sex development (DSD) (13, 14). Recently, reports of DHX37-related 46,XY DSD have increased (13, 15, 16). Pathogenic variants of DHX37 can also cause incomplete penetration in 46,XY individuals that range from asymptomatic to DSD, whereas 46,XX carriers remain nonsymptomatic with normal fertility (15). However, no reports have described the clinical features of DHX37 gene deletion.

SETD1B encodes a lysine-specific histone methyltransferase that methylates histone H3 at lysine-4 (17, 18). Reports have indicated that pathogenic variants in SETD1B are associated with intellectual developmental disorder associated with seizures and delays in language development (17, 19). Several studies have reported individuals with microdeletions involving SETD1B, in addition to those with pathogenic SETD1B variants (17, 19,20,21).

This case report describes a patient with hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, syndromic diabetes, and autism associated with a chromosomal deletion involving HNF1A, DHX37, and SETD1B.

Case Report

A 12-yr-old Japanese boy was born healthy from unrelated parents, and he had no family disease history of early-onset diabetes. Consistent with his small-for-gestational-age (SGA) status, he was 42 cm (−3.81 SD) in height and 2,007 g (−3.75 SD) in weight at birth. He was diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder, conductive hearing loss, short stature, afebrile seizures, and severe intellectual disability postnatally. With no meaningful speech, at 5 yr old, his tested intelligence yielded a DQ of 28, representing a level below 1 yr old. Array-based comparative genomic hybridization (array-CGH) at this time revealed an 8.6-Mb chromosomal deletion at 12q24.31–32 (Fig. 1). During routine school urinalysis at 11 yr old, urinary glucose was detected. At this age, he displayed no disease symptoms, but he exhibited polydipsia at 12 yr old. Then, he was referred to our department to evaluate suspected diabetes. At the beginning of the visit, he was 129.0 cm (−2.76 SD) in height and 31.5 kg (−1.51 SD) in weight, giving him a body mass index of 18.9 (44.0%tile, –0.15SD). The boy had a round face, microcephaly, mild hypertelorism, brachycephaly, synophrys, and brachydactyly. His pubic hair Tanner stage was 3, his testicular volume was 3 mL, and his penile length was 55 mm stretched. He did not have acanthosis nigricans. His communication was only nodding and pointing, and intelligence testing was not possible. The laboratory results demonstrated that his HbA1c level was 10.8%, and his fasting blood glucose level was 261 mg/dL (Table 1). No pancreatic islet autoantibodies (anti-GAD, anti-IA-2, and anti-insulin antibodies) were detected. The glucagon stimulation test revealed a peak C-peptide level of 3.35 ng/mL, indicating deficiencies in insulin secretion. Fasting blood glucose, insulin, and homeostasis model assessment-insulin resistance were 221 mg/dL, 4.9 µU/mL, and 2.67 (> 2.5), respectively, leading to suspicion of insulin resistance. Further testing indicated elevated follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels. A human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) stimulation test was performed by administering hCG at a dose of 3,000 IU/m2 daily for three consecutive days. Before the first injection and 24 hours after the final dose, blood samples were obtained. After stimulation, serum testosterone levels exhibited an increment from 85 ng/dL at baseline to 498 ng/dL. Clinical diagnosis of diabetes and hypergonadotropic hypogonadism prompted re-evaluation of the array-CGH results, revealing that the deleted region included three genes: mainly HNF1A,DHX37, and SETD1B. No other genes within the deletion region appeared to be directly associated with the patient’s phenotype (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1). Considering his early-onset, nonobese diabetes and HNF1A haploinsufficiency, a diagnosis of syndromic diabetes caused by chromosomal deletion involving the HNF1A region was rendered. He had intellectual disability and autism spectrum disorder; thus, exercise and dietary therapy were difficult. And he was deemed unable to manage hypoglycemia, a side effect attributed to sulfonylurea drugs. Consequently, sulfonylurea drugs were avoided despite their usefulness for treating HNF1A-MODY. Treatment with nateglinide was initiated given its lower risk of hypoglycemia than sulfonylureas. However, after 1 mo of oral treatment, his HbA1c level remained unchanged (10.7%); thus, basal insulin therapy was initiated, starting with insulin glargine at 9 U/d. Finally, his HbA1c level declined to 7.4% following treatment with nateglinide 360 mg/d and insulin glargine 27 U/d (Fig. 2). Hypergonadotropic hypogonadism was suggested because of the patient’s elevated FSH level. His testicular volume was small, with no observed abnormalities in the morphology of the external genitalia. He did not require testosterone replacement therapy, as indicated by results of hCG stimulation testing, which confirmed the preservation of testosterone production. The patient’s age and developmental disability precluded the performance of a semen analysis for the evaluation of spermatogenic function. At 13 yr and 10 mo old, his height and weight were 132.0 cm and 42.8 kg, respectively. His HbA1c level was 7.4% with treatment with nateglinide 360 mg/d and insulin glargine 35 U/d. Meanwhile, no diabetic complications were observed.

Discussion

This patient experienced autism, syndromic diabetes, and hypergonadotropic hypogonadism had a 12q24.31–32 chromosomal deletion. The deleted region included three main genes: HNF1A, DHX37, and SETD1B.

In previous cases with reported chromosomal deletions involving HNF1A, the age of diabetes onset was the early teens for all patients, although some patients had not yet developed or been diagnosed with diabetes (22,23,24,25,26,27). In patients with intellectual disability, the present case highlights the importance of individualizing glucose control. Our patient had intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, and syndromic diabetes at 12 yr old. The cause of intellectual disability and autism spectrum disorder might have been the chromosomal deletion that included SETD1B, as this gene deletion was previously reported as the cause of autism spectrum disorder and intellectual disability (17, 19,20,21). Establishing a consistent treatment routine is critical to children with autism spectrum disorder (28). HNF1A-MODY responds to low doses of sulfonylurea, allowing a potential switch from insulin therapy (8, 29, 30). Treating with sulfonylurea had been avoided in this patient; instead, treatment with nateglinide was initiated because he was unable to manage hypoglycemia as a side effect attributable to sulfonylurea due to his autism and intellectual disability. Insulin glargine was added as the basal insulin, and treatment with nateglinide was continued for additional secretions. In this case, the patient presents with a high insulin requirement and demonstrates impaired insulin secretion and insulin resistance. His SGA status at birth may have contributed to the development of insulin resistance. Nateglinide treatment has been reported to be effective for glycemic control without hypoglycemia in patients with HNF1A-MODY (31). The current case report revealed that the treatment and management of diabetes for children with intellectual disabilities or autism should differ from the general recommendations.

Deletion of DHX37 may lead to a hypergonadotropic hypogonadism phenotype. The present patient had elevated levels of FSH, and the chromosomal deletion encompassed DHX37. Phenotypes caused by DHX37 missense variants in XY individuals range from asymptomatic to DSD (13, 15, 32). A previous study of 60 XY cases with DHX37 abnormalities affecting testicular function identified missense variants in 59 cases presenting with DSD, whereas a patient carrying frameshift variant displayed normal external genitalia, albeit with azoospermia (15). This latter phenotype was similar to that observed in the current patient. In the present case, DHX37 haploinsufficiency existed without external genital abnormalities. The obtained analyses results indicated that FSH levels were elevated and testosterone production was preserved, suggesting that DHX37 deletion can cause a hypergonadotropic hypogonadism phenotype. Similar to the current case, several previous chromosomal deletions involving HNF1A and DHX37 have been reported. In a prior report, a 7-yr-old girl carrying a 6.6-Mb deletion at 12q24.23–24.31 involving HNF1A and DHX37 displayed complex congenital heart disease, global developmental delay, and refractory epilepsy, but she did not exhibit external genital abnormalities or diabetes (27). Heterozygous DHX37 abnormalities are not associated with impaired fertility or external genital deformations in females (15). In a recent analysis of 17 Japanese individuals with 46, XY DSD, DHX37 abnormalities were identified in three cases. All variants detected in this report were missense variants (33). A previous report identified reduced protein expression attributable to missense variants (34), but it unclear whether DHX37 variants lead to loss or gain of function. On the contrary, external genitalia abnormality was reported in a male child with a 12q24.31q24.33 gene deletion (35). DHX37 is localized within the 12q24.31 region (13), suggesting that this gene was included within the deletion range observed in the patient. This observation indicates that DHX37 haploinsufficiency might affect male gonadal functions.

Conclusion

The present patient had a 12q24.31–32 chromosomal deletion involving HNF1A, DHX37, and SETD1B, and he displayed hypogonadism, syndromic diabetes, and intellectual disability. This case report emphasizes the usefulness of managing diabetes based on the characteristics of children with intellectual disabilities and highlights the potential for DHX37 haploinsufficiency to cause hypogonadism.
