Intravascular Ultrasound–Guided and Angiography-Guided Chimney Stenting for Proactive Coronary Protection in High-Risk TAVR
Authors: Phan Thao Nguyen, Nguyen The Huy, Luong Hoang Long, Ly Duc Ngoc, Nguyen Cong Huu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe report a proactive coronary protection strategy during transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) using intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)–guided decision making and chimney stenting in patients with high-risk anatomy.Case SummaryTwo patients with severe symptomatic aortic stenosis and unfavorable coronary anatomy on preprocedural computed tomography underwent TAVR with planned coronary protection. IVUS-Guided and Angiography-Guided Chimney Stenting as Proactive Coronary Protection Strategies to Prevent Coronary Obstruction in High-Risk TAVR Patients

IVUS = intravascular ultrasound; TAVR = transcatheter aortic valve replacement.

Severe aortic stenosis is the most common valvular heart disease in developed countries and is increasing globally with aging populations.1 Once symptoms develop, mortality approaches 50% within 2 years if untreated.2 Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has transformed management across all risk groups but has serious complications, including coronary obstruction (0.5%-1%), which carries high mortality (>40%).3,4 This complication is more frequent in patients with unfavorable anatomy and is primarily caused by displacement of calcified leaflets toward the coronary ostia.5

Take-Home Messages

•Coronary obstruction during or after TAVR is a rare but life-threatening complication that requires early recognition and proactive prevention.

To mitigate this risk, strategies such as chimney stenting and bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA) have been developed. Whereas both strategies aim to preserve coronary flow through different mechanisms, determining the optimal timing of intervention remains challenging, as angiography may underestimate risk.6,7 Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) provides real-time assessment of leaflet position and leaflet-to-ostium distance, enabling more accurate evaluation of dynamic risk.8,9 We report 2 high-risk TAVR cases in which an IVUS-guided proactive coronary protection strategy with chimney stenting was successfully applied, highlighting the limitations of angiography and the value of imaging-guided intervention.

Case Presentations

Case 1

An 88-year-old woman presented with progressive dyspnea and NYHA functional class IV heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography confirmed severe aortic stenosis with a critically reduced valve area. Preprocedural multislice computed tomography demonstrated high-risk anatomical features, including a low left main coronary ostial height (11 mm), small sinuses of Valsalva, and severe leaflet calcification, indicating a high risk of coronary obstruction (Figure 1A).

Case 1: IVUS-Guided Chimney Stenting for Coronary Protection During TAVR

(A) Preprocedural multislice computed tomography (MSCT) demonstrating high-risk anatomical features, including low left main coronary height, narrow sinuses of Valsalva, and severe leaflet calcification. (B) Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) showing close leaflet-to-coronary ostium proximity, with measurement of a reduced leaflet-to-ostium distance indicating a high-risk configuration. (C) Fluoroscopic sequence of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) with coronary protection, including guidewire and undeployed stent positioning, valve deployment, and chimney stent implantation with intentional protrusion into the aorta to maintain coronary patency. LM = left main coronary artery; PCI = percutaneous coronary intervention.

A transfemoral TAVR was performed using a 23-mm self-expanding Evolut FX valve (Medtronic), with a planned coronary protection strategy. A guiding catheter with guide extension support (Guidezilla; Boston Scientific) was positioned in the left main coronary artery, and a 4.0 × 20 mm drug-eluting stent was advanced and secured for potential deployment. Following valve deployment, angiography demonstrated preserved coronary flow (TIMI flow grade 3) (Figure 1C). However, fluoroscopic imaging raised suspicion that the native leaflet was displaced toward the coronary ostium. IVUS was therefore performed and revealed a leaflet-to-ostium distance of 4.5 mm, representing a high-risk configuration despite preserved flow (Figure 1B). Based on these findings, chimney stenting of the left main coronary artery was performed, with intentional protrusion into the aorta to maintain patency. Final angiography confirmed preserved coronary flow, and IVUS demonstrated adequate stent expansion.

The patient remained hemodynamically stable, recovered well, and was discharged after 7 days. At subsequent follow-up at 1 year, normal prosthetic valve function was maintained, and the patient was clinically stable.

Case 2

An 80-year-old man with a history of coronary artery disease and prior percutaneous coronary intervention presented with symptomatic severe aortic stenosis. Preprocedural multislice computed tomography revealed an extremely high-risk anatomical profile, including low coronary ostial heights (12 mm for the left main and 11 mm for the right coronary artery), narrow sinuses of Valsalva, and severe leaflet calcification (Figure 2A).

Case 2: Multimodality Imaging and Coronary Protection During TAVR

(A) Preprocedural multislice computed tomography (MSCT) demonstrating high-risk anatomical features, including low coronary ostial height, narrow sinuses of Valsalva, and severe leaflet calcification, with detailed annular and sinus measurements and procedural planning. (B) Fluoroscopic sequence of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) with coronary protection, including coronary wiring, valve deployment, and chimney stent implantation with intentional protrusion into the aorta to maintain coronary patency. LCC = left coronary cusp; NCC = non-coronary cusp; RCA= right coronary artery; RCC = right coronary cusp.

A transfemoral TAVR using a 23-mm self-expanding Evolut FX valve was planned, with a dual coronary protection strategy involving both the left main and the right coronary arteries (Video 1). During the procedure, guidewires and undeployed stents were positioned in both coronary arteries before valve deployment (Videos 2 and 3). Following valve deployment, angiography demonstrated preserved coronary flow in both vessels (Video 4). However, significant narrowing (approximately 50%-60%) was observed at the right coronary artery ostium due to leaflet displacement. Given the risk of delayed coronary obstruction related to the continued expansion of the self-expanding valve, chimney stenting with a 4.0 × 22 mm stent was performed in the right coronary artery (Figure 2B, Video 5). The left main coronary artery was reassessed and considered safe, allowing removal of the protection system without intervention. Final angiography confirmed satisfactory coronary perfusion (Video 6). The patient remained hemodynamically stable, experienced no complications, and was discharged after 5 days; follow-up after 8 months showed no major adverse cardiac events.

Discussion

The present report provides important insights into coronary protection during TAVR, highlighting a shift toward a proactive, imaging-guided strategy that integrates preprocedural assessment with real-time intraprocedural decision making. Both cases represent a typical high anatomical risk phenotype, characterized by the convergence of critical features, including low coronary ostial height (<12 mm) and narrow sinuses of Valsalva. In such settings, displaced native leaflets lack sufficient space to accommodate within the sinus and are forced toward the coronary ostia, markedly increasing the risk of obstruction. Prior studies have shown that the incidence of coronary obstruction is significantly higher in this subgroup compared with patients with favorable anatomy.4 These observations underscore the essential role of meticulous preprocedural assessment using multislice computed tomography, as reliance on intraprocedural findings alone may result in a reactive approach and increased procedural risk.5 Chimney stenting is the more commonly used and simpler coronary protection strategy, involving stent deployment from the coronary ostium to prevent obstruction, whereas BASILICA is a more complex technique that uses electrosurgical leaflet laceration to preserve coronary flow and access.10 Recent comparative data demonstrated that both BASILICA and chimney stenting are feasible coronary protection strategies with high procedural success and comparable short-term clinical outcomes, with a trend toward lower cardiovascular mortality observed in the BASILICA group.11 At our institution, there are currently no operators formally trained in performing the BASILICA technique. Therefore, chimney stenting was selected based on operator expertise and immediate procedural feasibility.

A key finding from case 1 is the limitation of angiography in assessing true anatomical risk. Despite preserved coronary flow (TIMI flow grade 3), IVUS revealed a leaflet-to-ostium distance of 4.5 mm, within the high-risk range (<6 mm) for delayed coronary obstruction.8,12 This highlights the inability of angiography to capture the three-dimensional spatial relationship between the displaced leaflet and the coronary ostium. In the setting of self-expanding valves, which continue to expand after deployment, such borderline clearance may progressively narrow, increasing the risk of delayed obstruction. IVUS therefore plays a critical role in resolving this gray zone, enabling timely intervention. The decision to proceed with chimney stenting based on IVUS findings, despite preserved flow, reflects a proactive strategy that likely prevented a catastrophic event.

In case 2, angiography demonstrated preserved coronary flow but revealed significant ostial narrowing (50%-60%) of the right coronary artery due to leaflet displacement. This finding indicated evolving mechanical compromise and justified immediate intervention. The use of dual coronary protection with prepositioned stents highlights the importance of procedural planning in high-risk anatomy. Chimney stenting of the right coronary artery was performed to prevent delayed obstruction, particularly given the continued expansion of the self-expanding valve. Registry data support this approach, demonstrating high procedural success and effective immediate coronary protection.7,13 Importantly, these cases also highlight the need for selective intervention. Not all high-risk anatomical features mandate immediate stenting, and decisions should be individualized based on intraprocedural imaging. IVUS plays a central role in this process, enabling tailored decision making and helping to avoid both undertreatment and overtreatment.14

However, chimney stenting has some limitations. Potential concerns include stent thrombosis, the need for prolonged dual antiplatelet therapy, and challenges in future coronary access due to stent protrusion into the aorta.7,12 These issues must be carefully weighed, particularly in elderly patients at increased bleeding risk. Nevertheless, in patients with advanced age and high risk of coronary obstruction, the immediate life-saving benefit of maintaining coronary perfusion outweighs these long-term considerations.

Conclusions

Coronary obstruction remains a rare but life-threatening complication of TAVR. These cases demonstrate that an IVUS-guided, proactive coronary protection strategy with chimney stenting is feasible and effective in a middle-resource setting. This approach enables better assessment of dynamic risk and supports timely intervention. Further studies are needed to validate its role and define standardized criteria.
