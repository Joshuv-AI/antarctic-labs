Cambrian lattices are fractionally Calabi-Yau via 2-cluster combinatorics Thanks: Supported by the Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft (DFG, German Research Foundation) under Germany’s Excellence Strategy - GZ 2047/1, Projekt-ID 390685813
Markus Kleinau

Abstract
. Reading constructed a Cambrian lattice C Γ C_{\Gamma} for each oriented finite type Coxeter diagram Γ \Gamma . We show that the derived category of representations of C Γ C_{\Gamma} is fractionally Calabi-Yau for any Γ \Gamma , confirming a conjecture of Chapoton. This extends a result of Rognerud for Cambrian lattices of type A A with linear orientation, better known as Tamari lattices. If Γ \Gamma is crystallographic, then C Γ C_{\Gamma} is given by the lattice of torsion classes of any hereditary algebra Λ \Lambda of type Γ \Gamma . In this case we introduce and study a class of intervals in C Γ C_{\Gamma} whose combinatorics matches the combinatorics of 2 2 -cluster tilting objects in the 2-cluster category of Λ \Lambda . This allows us to compute the Calabi-Yau dimension of C Γ C_{\Gamma} .

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Fractionally Calabi-Yau lattices

A
Serre functor
on a category
\mathcal{C}
is an autoequivalence
\mathbb{S}
that admits a natural isomorphism
\Hom_{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)\cong\Hom_{\mathcal{C}}(Y,\mathbb{S}X)^{*}
. If a Serre functor exists then it is unique up to natural isomorphism. A triangulated category is called
d
-Calabi-Yau
for
d\in\mathbb{Z}
if the
d
-fold shift
[d]
is a Serre functor. Examples are given by the derived category of coherent sheaves on a Calabi-Yau variety and cluster categories of Dynkin quivers. More generally a category is called
fractionally Calabi-Yau of dimension

d/c
if
\mathbb{S}^{c}\cong[d]
. Examples include the derived categories of Dynkin quivers and some components of semiorthogonal decompositions of Fano varieties
[
25
]
. A recent conjecture of Chapoton in
[
13
]
predicts that the Fukaya-Seidel categories of certain quasi-homogeneous singularities are equivalent to derived categories of lattices. This should imply that the latter are fractionally Calabi-Yau. Motivated by this the fractionally Calabi-Yau property has been shown for Tamari lattices by Rognerud in
[
32
]
and for the lattices of order ideals of a product of two chains by Gottesman in
[
18
]
. In both cases the Calabi-Yau dimension matches the one predicted by Chapoton and in the latter the equivalence to a Fukaya-Seidel category has been shown by combining results of Gottesman
[
18
]
and Di Dedda
[
15
]
. In this article we show that the derived categories of Cambrian lattices are fractionally Calabi-Yau, further corroborating the conjecture of Chapoton.

Let
L
be a lattice and
k
a field. A
representation of

L
is given by assigning a
k
-vector space
M_{a}
to each element
a\in L
and a linear map
M_{a<b}\colon M_{a}\rightarrow M_{b}
to each relation
a<b
in
L
such that
M_{b<c}M_{a<b}=M_{a<c}
. A number of connections between the combinatorics of
L
and the homological algebra of representations of
L
have recently been discovered, see for example
[
21
]
and
[
23
]
. We call a lattice
L

fractionally Calabi-Yau
if
\mathcal{D}^{b}(kL)
, the derived category of the category of representations of
L
, is fractionally Calabi-Yau.

Given an interval
I=[a\leq b]
in
L
the
interval module

M_{I}
is given by setting
M_{I,x}=k
for
x\in I
and
0
else. The maps are
\textnormal{id}_{k}
where possible. The interval representations are often well behaved homologically. One approach to show that a lattice is fractionally Calabi-Yau is to exhibit a subset of the interval modules which the Serre functor permutes up to shift. If this subset is big enough then one can apply results of Rognerud
[
32
, Theorem 1.2]
and Gottesman
[
18
, Theorem B]
to conclude that the lattice is fractionally Calabi-Yau. Often one can construct a combinatorial model for this subset of interval modules and the permutation on them given by the Serre functor. Computing the Calabi-Yau dimension then becomes a purely combinatorial problem. This approach has been successfully applied to the Tamari Lattices by Rognerud
[
32
]
and to the lattice of order ideals of a product of two chains by Gottesman
[
18
]
. In this article we apply it to the Cambrian lattices. The set of representations we use are given by a new class of intervals we call mutable intervals and the Serre functor on this set can be modeled using the combinatorics of 2-cluster tilting objects in 2-cluster categories.

1.2.
Cambrian lattices

Reading
[
28
]
introduced a Cambrian lattice for each oriented finite type Coxeter diagram
\Gamma
. They provide orientations on the 1-skeleton of the type
\Gamma
-associahedron and hence the mutation graph of type
\Gamma
-cluster algebras. We refer to
[
29
]
for an overview. The Cambrian lattices of type
A
with linear orientation are exactly the Tamari lattices. For crystallographic
\Gamma
the Cambrian lattice of type
\Gamma
is the lattice of torsion classes of any hereditary algebra of type
\Gamma
as show by Ingalls and Thomas in
[
20
]
. This is the perspective we will use in this paper. Let
\Lambda
be a finite dimensional connected hereditary representation finite algebra. A full additive subcategory
\mathcal{T}
of
\modulecat\Lambda
is a
torsion class
if it is closed under extensions and quotient objects. The torsion classes of
\modulecat\Lambda
form a lattice under inclusion denoted by
\tors\Lambda
. A second important class of subcategories are the
wide subcategories
. That is the full additive subcategories
W
closed under extensions, kernels and cokernels. In the language of Coxeter groups they correspond to noncrossing partitions. The paper
[
20
]
established a bijection between the wide subcategories of
\modulecat\Lambda
and the torsion classes in
\modulecat\Lambda
given by sending a wide subcategory
W
to the torsion class
\Gen W
it generates. We call an interval
[\mathcal{T}\leq\mathcal{T}^{\prime}]
in
\tors\Lambda

mutable
if it is of the form
[\Gen W\leq\Gen W^{\prime}]
for two wide subcategories
W\subseteq W^{\prime}
. For example all intervals of the form
[0\leq\mathcal{T}],[\mathcal{T}\leq\mathcal{T}]
or
[\mathcal{T}\leq\modulecat\Lambda]
are mutable. For Tamari lattices, that is the lattices of torsion classes of linear type
A
algebras, the mutable intervals agree with Rognerud’s notion of exceptional intervals. This is the desired class of intervals that the Serre functor permutes up to shift.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finite dimensional connected hereditary representation finite algebra and let
I
be a mutable interval in
\tors(\Lambda)
. Then there exists another mutable interval
\mathbb{S}I
and an integer
K_{I}
such that
\mathbb{S}M_{I}\cong M_{\mathbb{S}I}[K_{I}]
.

A theorem of Rognerud allows us to glue these objectwise isomorphisms into an equivalence of functors and hence show that
\tors\Lambda
is fractionally Calabi-Yau. This is our first main result:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finite dimensional connected hereditary representation finite algebra. Let
h
be the Coxeter number of
\Lambda
and
N
the number of indecomposable representations of
\Lambda
.
Then
\tors\Lambda
is
(2N,2h+2)
-fractionally Calabi-Yau.

Further if
\Lambda
is of Dynkin type
A_{1}
,
B_{n}
,
C_{n}
,
D_{n}
(
n
even),
E_{7}
,
E_{8}
or
G_{2}
then
\tors\Lambda
is
(N,h+1)
-fractionally Calabi-Yau.

In particular all crystallographic Cambrian lattices are fractionally Calabi-Yau. By considering the non-crystallographic types separately, in part by computer, we can extend
1.2
to all Cambrian lattices.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
L
be an irreducible Cambrian lattice of Coxeter type
\Gamma
. Let
h
be the Coxeter number of
\Gamma
and
N
the number of hyperplanes of the type
\Gamma
reflection group.
Then
L
is
(2N,2h+2)
-fractionally Calabi-Yau.

Further if
\Gamma
is of Coxeter type
A_{1}
,
B_{n}
,
C_{n}
,
D_{n}
(
n
even),
E_{7}
,
E_{8}
,
H_{3}
,
H_{4}
or
I(m)
(
m
even) then
L
is
(N,h+1)
-fractionally Calabi-Yau.

The fractionally Calabi-Yau dimensions claimed here will be explained in the next subsection. For now we just note that they agree with the ones predicted by Chapoton in
[
13
]
. Rognerud computed the action of the Serre functor on exceptional interval modules over the Tamari lattice using explicit projective resolutions. For mutable intervals these resolutions are only available for intervals of the form
[\mathcal{T}\leq\mathcal{T}]
. To compute the Serre functor for larger intervals we introduce interval mutations. An interval mutation is a triple of mutable intervals
(B,I,A)
such that

I=A\sqcup B
,
\max B=\max I
, and
\min A=\min I
. In terms of modules they correspond to short exact sequences

0\rightarrow M_{B}\rightarrow M_{I}\rightarrow M_{A}\rightarrow 0

and hence to triangles in the derived category. Note that any two parts of an interval mutation determine the third. We can use this and the following proposition to compute the Serre functor inductively.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
I=[\mathcal{T}\lneq\mathcal{T}^{\prime}]
be a proper mutable interval in
\tors(\Lambda)
. Then there is an interval mutation
(B,I,A)
. In addition for any interval mutation
(B,I,A)
either
(\mathbb{S}I,\mathbb{S}A,\mathbb{S}B)
or
(\mathbb{S}A,\mathbb{S}B,\mathbb{S}I)
is also an interval mutation.

In order to explain this phenomenon and to compute the fractionally Calabi-Yau dimension we need a combinatorial model for the set of mutable intervals. This is given by the set of 2-cluster tilting objects in the 2-cluster category of
\Lambda
.

1.3.
2-cluster combinatorics

The cluster category
\mathcal{C}_{1}(\Lambda)
was introduced in
[
7
]
as an additive categorification of the cluster algebra corresponding to
\Lambda
. It is constructed as the orbit category of the derived category
\mathcal{D}^{b}(\Lambda)
under the functor
\tau^{-1}[1]
. An object
T\in\mathcal{C}_{1}(\Lambda)
is called
cluster tilting
if it is maximal with the property
\Ext^{1}(T,T)=0
. The set of cluster tilting objects categorifies the clusters of the corresponding cluster algebra in the following way: indecomposable summands correspond to cluster variables, clusters correspond to cluster-tilting objects and mutation corresponds to replacing an indecomposable direct summands with a different one. A generalization of the set of clusters of a cluster algebra to the set of
m
-clusters was introduced by Fomin and Reading in
[
16
]
. The
m
-clusters also admit an additive categorification using
m
-cluster categories
\mathcal{C}_{m}(\Lambda)
as shown by Thomas in
[
34
]
and Zhu in
[
36
]
. To construct them one replaces the functor
\tau^{-1}[1]
in the construction of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(\Lambda)
by
\tau^{-1}[m]
. We refer to
[
6
]
for an introduction. An object
T\in\mathcal{C}_{m}(\Lambda)
is called
m
-cluster tilting
if it is maximal with the property
\Ext^{i}(T,T)=0
for
1\leq i\leq m
. The
m
-cluster tilting objects also admit a notion of mutation: if one removes an indecomposable direct summand
T^{\prime}
from an
m
-cluster tilting object
T
then there are exactly
m
other indecomposable objects
T_{i}
such that
(T/T^{\prime})\oplus T_{i}
is again an
m
-cluster tilting object. Relevant for this paper is only the case
m=2
. The set of 2-cluster tilting objects of a 2-cluster category has two relevant combinatorial structures: The first is a permutation given by sending a 2-cluster tilting object
T
to
T[1]
and the second is the mutation mentioned above relating sets of three 2-cluster tilting objects. We construct a bijection between the set of 2-cluster tilting objects in
\mathcal{C}_{m}(\Lambda)
and the set of mutable intervals in
\tors\Lambda
which identifies the combinatorial structures on both sets. This is our second main result.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finite dimensional connected hereditary representation finite algebra. There is a bijection

\displaystyle\interval\colon\{\textnormal{2-cluster tilting objects in }\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)\}\rightarrow\{\textnormal{mutable intervals in }\tors(\Lambda)\}

such that for any 2-cluster tilting object
T
we have
\mathbb{S}\interval(T)=\interval(T[1])
. Further let
T_{1}
be a 2-cluster tilting object with a distinguished indecomposable direct summand
T^{\prime}
and let
T_{2}
and
T_{3}
be the two 2-cluster tilting objects obtained by mutating
T_{1}
at
T^{\prime}
. Then there is a permutation
\sigma\in S_{3}
such that
(\interval T_{\sigma(1)},\interval T_{\sigma(2)},\interval T_{\sigma(3)})
is an interval mutation. All interval mutations arise in this way.

This reduces the combinatorics of the Serre functor of a Cambrian lattice to the study of the shift functor in 2-cluster categories. The coefficients in
1.2
are much easier to compute in this setting and
1.4
can be explained by observing that if we have three 2-cluster tilting objects
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
and
T_{3}
connected by mutation then also
T_{1}[1]
,
T_{2}[1]
and
T_{3}[1]
are connected by mutation.

A geometric model for mutable intervals in linear type
A
has been given by Rognerud in
[
32
]
in terms of noncrossing trees while a model for 2-cluster tilting objects has been given by Baur and Marsh in
[
5
]
in terms of quadrangulations. The bijection
\interval
also has a simple geometric interpretation in this case.

In the appendix we describe and motivate the problem of classifying periodic Serre formal incidence algebras of lattices, which was one of the original goals when this project started. This problem seems to be a much more tractable problem compared to the classification problem of twisted fractional Calabi-Yau algebras among incidence algebras of lattices. We will give a conjecture for a combinatorial classification via the Coxeter matrix and give an example of a periodic Serre formal incidence algebra of a lattice that is not semidistributive and therefore not a Cambrian lattice.

Section 4

Section 5

Section 6

Section 7
completes the proof that Cambrian lattices are periodic Serre formal.

Section 8
contains a detailed example in type
A_{3}
.

Section 9

The appendix
discusses and gives a combinatorial conjecture for the classification of periodic Serre formal incidence algebras of lattices via the Coxeter matrix.

Definition 2.1
.

Let

(3)

Examples of these subcategories can be constructed using orthogonal categories:

Definition 2.2

For a subcategory

(1)

\mathcal{C}^{\perp_{0}}=\{Y\in\modulecat\Lambda\mid\Hom_{\Lambda}(X,Y)=0\ \forall X\in\mathcal{C}\}

2.2.
Support tilting modules

Definition 2.14
.

The
support
of a module
M\in\modulecat\Lambda
is the set of indecomposable projectives
P\in\modulecat\Lambda
with
\Hom_{\Lambda}(P,M)\neq 0
.

Definition 2.15
.

A
partial tilting module

M\in\modulecat\Lambda
is a basic module satisfying
\Ext_{\Lambda}^{1}(M,M)=0
. A partial tilting module
M
is
support tilting
if its rank is the same size as its support and
tilting
if it has the same rank as
\Lambda
.
In this case the
support tilting pair
associated to
M
is the pair
(M,P_{M})
, where
P_{M}
is a maximal basic projective module with
\Hom_{\Lambda}(P_{M},M)=0
.

In particular for any support tilting pair
(M,P_{M})
we have
|M|+|P_{M}|=|\Lambda|
. There are two canonical ways to complete a partial tilting module to a support tilting module.

Proposition 2.16

(Bongartz complement)
.

Let
M
be a partial tilting modules. Then there exist basic modules
\beta(M)\in\Cogen M
and
\gamma(M)\in\Gen M
such that
M\oplus\beta(M)
and
M\oplus\gamma(M^{\prime\prime})
are support tilting modules. The module
\beta(M)
is called the
Bongartz complement
and the module
\gamma(M)
is called the
co-Bongartz complement
.

The 2-cluster category

Cluster categories were introduced in
[
7
]
as an additive categorification of cluster algebras. They were generalised by Thomas
[
34
]
and Zhu
[

]
to

[

In this section we recall this theory specialised to 2-cluster categories.

Definition 3.1
.

The 2-cluster category is the orbit category
\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)=\mathcal{D}^{b}(\Lambda)/(\tau^{-1}[2])
. It has a triangulated structure such that the projection functor
\mathcal{D}^{b}(\Lambda)\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)
is triangulated.

That
\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)
admits a triangulated structure compatible with the projection was shown by Keller in
[
22
]
. We can describe objects in the 2-cluster category as follows:

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\Fund
be the smallest full additive subcategory of
\mathcal{D}^{b}(\Lambda)
containing
\modulecat\Lambda
,
(\modulecat\Lambda)[1]
and the object
P[2]
for each projective object

. Then
\Fund
is a fundamental region for the action of
\tau^{-1}[2]
. We use the same notation for objects in
\Fund
and their images in
\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)
.

Unlike the case of 1-cluster categories,
\Hom
and
\Ext^{1}
do not change when passing from the module category to the 2-cluster category. This is not true for higher or negative
\Ext^{i}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
A,B\in\modulecat\Lambda\subset\Fund
. Then

\Hom_{\Lambda}(A,B)=\Hom_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(A,B)

and

\Ext^{1}_{\Lambda}(A,B)=\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(A,B).

Proof.

By definition we have

\Hom_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(A,B)=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Hom_{\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)}(A,\tau^{-n}B[2n])

and

\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(A,B)=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Hom_{\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)}(A,\tau^{-n}B[2n+1]).

Consider an indecomposable object
M\in\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
. Standard result about the derived category of representation finite hereditary algebras (see
[
19
]
) imply

(1)

M\in\modulecat\Lambda[k]
for some

,

(2)

\tau^{-1}M\in(\modulecat\Lambda[k]\cup\modulecat\Lambda[k+1])
for

as above and

(3)

if
\Hom_{\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)}(A,M)\neq 0
, then
M\in(\modulecat\Lambda\cup\modulecat\Lambda[1])
.

In particular the family of objects
\{\tau^{-n}B[2n]\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
contains only one element in
(\modulecat\Lambda\cup\modulecat\Lambda[1])
, namely
B
itself. Similarly
B[1]
is the only element of the family
\{\tau^{-n}B[2n+1]\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
in
(\modulecat\Lambda\cup\modulecat\Lambda[1])
. The Proposition follows.
∎

The identification of
\tau
with
[2]
turns the Auslander-Reiten formula into a Calabi-Yau property:

(
[
22
]
)

The 2-cluster category is 3-Calabi-Yau.
That is for any

\Ext_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}^{i}(X,Y)\cong\Ext_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}^{3-i}(Y,X)^{*}

In particular

is zero if and only if

is zero. This motivates the next definition.

Definition 3.5

A basic object

(3)

An example of a 2-cluster category and (almost) 2-cluster tilting objects can be found in

.
The set of 2-cluster tilting objects and its combinatorics play a central role in this paper. There are two important structures on this set: a canonical permutation and a notion of mutation.

\displaystyle[1]\colon\{\text{2-cluster tilting objects}\}

Next we introduce mutation. In the 1-cluster category every almost cluster tilting object is a direct summand of exactly two cluster tilting object and hence defines a ’mutation’ between them. This can be adapted to the 2-cluster category as follows:

4.
Mutable intervals and 2-cluster tilting objects

In the next two sections we will recover the combinatorics of 2-cluster tilting objects within a class of intervals of the lattice of torsion classes called mutable intervals. As a first step we describe 2-cluster tilting objects as triples of partial tilting modules in
\modulecat\Lambda
satisfying certain orthogonality conditions.

Any 2-cluster tilting object
T\in\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)
can be decomposed as
T=T_{free}\oplus T_{tors}[1]\oplus T_{supp}[2]
where
T_{free},T_{tors}\in\modulecat\Lambda
and
T_{supp}\in\proj(\modulecat\Lambda)
. The next Proposition describes when three such modules form a 2-cluster tilting object.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
T_{free}
and
T_{tors}
be two partial tilting modules in
\modulecat\Lambda
and
T_{supp}
a basic projective module. Then
T=T_{free}\oplus T_{tors}[1]\oplus T_{supp}[2]

\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)
if and only if all of the following hold:

(1)

\Hom_{\Lambda}(T_{tors},T_{free})=0=\Ext^{1}_{\Lambda}(T_{tors},T_{free})

(2)

\Hom_{\Lambda}(T_{supp},T_{tors})=0=\Hom_{\Lambda}(T_{supp},T_{free})

Further
T
is 2-cluster tilting if and only if
|\Lambda|=|T_{free}|+|T_{tors}|+|T_{supp}|
.

Proof.

The object
T
is 2-rigid if and only if
\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(T,T)=0=\Ext^{2}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(T,T)
. Because
\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)
is 3-Calabi-Yau (
3.4
), we only need to check one of the two. Combining
3.3
and
3.4
yields the following isomorphisms:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(T_{free},T_{free})

\displaystyle\cong\Ext^{1}_{\Lambda}(T_{free},T_{free})

(4.2)

\displaystyle\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(T_{free},T_{tors}[1])

\displaystyle\cong\Ext^{1}_{\Lambda}(T_{tors},T_{free})^{*}

(4.3)

\displaystyle\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(T_{free},T_{supp}[2])

\displaystyle\cong\Hom_{\Lambda}(T_{supp},T_{free})^{*}

(4.4)

\displaystyle\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(T_{tors}[1],T_{free})

\displaystyle\cong\Hom_{\Lambda}(T_{tors},T_{free})

(4.5)

\displaystyle\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(T_{tors}[1],T_{tors}[1])

\displaystyle\cong\Ext^{1}_{\Lambda}(T_{tors},T_{tors})

(4.6)

\displaystyle\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(T_{tors}[1],T_{supp}[2])

\displaystyle\cong\Ext^{1}_{\Lambda}(T_{supp},T_{tors})^{*}

(4.7)

\displaystyle\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(T_{supp}[2],T_{free})

\displaystyle\cong\Ext^{1}_{\Lambda}(T_{free},\nu_{\Lambda}T_{supp})^{*}

(4.8)

\displaystyle\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(T_{supp}[2],T_{tors}[1])

\displaystyle\cong\Hom_{\Lambda}(T_{supp},T_{tors})

(4.9)

\displaystyle\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)}(T_{supp}[2],T_{supp}[2])

\displaystyle\cong\Ext^{1}_{\Lambda}(T_{supp},T_{supp}).

In (

is the Nakayama functor and we used the identity

Cataland remark 4.6

Mutable intervals are a generalization of the exceptional intervals Rognerud defined for Tamari lattices in
[
32
]
. We use a different name here to avoid confusion with exceptional sequences. While the lattice of torsion classes forms a Cambrian lattice, the lattice of wide subcategories corresponds to noncrossing partitions. From this perspective mutable intervals correspond to intervals in the lattice of noncrossing partitions. In the Tamari case this connection was already observed by Rognerud in
[
31

. As part of m-eralized Fuß-Catalan combinatorics intervals of noncrossing partitions are better known as 2-noncrossing partitions. Both the 2-noncrossing partitions and the 2-clusters are counted by 2-

-Catalan numbers and bijections between them are known.

Our next goal is to show that

defines a bijection between 2-cluster tilting objects and mutable intervals.

|\{\textnormal{2-cluster tilting objects in }\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)\}|=|\{\textnormal{mutable intervals in }\tors(\Lambda)\}|.

-Catalan number where

is the Coxeter type of
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Mutable intervals are by definition in bijection with intervals in the lattice of wide subcategories. The lattice of wide subcategories of
\Lambda
is isomorphic to the lattice of noncrossing partitions of

and intervals in that lattice, better known as 2-noncrossing partitions, are counted by the 2-
W
-Catalan number.

The 2-cluster tilting objects in
\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)
are in bijection with 2-clusters of type

, which are also counted by 2-

-Catalan number. For more details on the underlying Fuß-Catalan combinatorics see
[

]
.
∎

\displaystyle\interval\colon\{\textnormal{2-cluster tilting objects in }\mathcal{C}_{2}(\Lambda)\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\{\textnormal{intervals in }\tors(\Lambda)\}

\displaystyle T

5.

In this section we will interpret mutation of 2-cluster tilting objects in terms of mutable intervals. Any almost 2-cluster tilting object admits three completions to 2-cluster tilting objects. The mutable intervals corresponding to those completions form an interval mutation which we introduce next.

Definition 5.1
.

A triple

of mutable intervals is called an

if

(1)

I=A\sqcup B

(2)

\max B=\max I

(3)

\min A=\min I

Interval mutations relate a bigger interval to two smaller ones. This forms the basis for an induction argument for the main theorem. We study interval mutations on their own first, then relate them to mutation of 2-cluster tilting objects at the end of this section.

To construct interval mutations we need to associate another wide subcategory to a mutable interval

Under the identification of wide subcategories with noncrossing partitions this is the

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
n
be the rank of
\Lambda
. Then we have

|\{\textnormal{augmented intervals over }\Lambda\}|=\frac{n}{3}|\{\textnormal{mutable intervals in }\tors\Lambda\}|

Proof.

Let
I
be a mutable interval with delta sequence
(W_{1},W_{2},W_{3})
. Then the number of augmented intervals of the form
(I,X)
is the rank of
W_{2}
. We consider the following permutation on the set of mutable intervals interpreted as delta sequences:

\displaystyle\krew_{2}\colon\{\delta\text{-sequences}\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\{\delta\text{-sequences}\}

\displaystyle(W_{1},W_{2},W_{3})

\displaystyle\mapsto(W_{2},W_{3},W_{2}^{\perp_{0,1}}\cap W_{2}^{\perp_{0,1}}).

Its inverse is given by

\displaystyle\krew_{2}^{-1}\colon\{\delta\text{-sequences}\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\{\delta\text{-sequences}\}

\displaystyle(W_{1},W_{2},W_{3})

\displaystyle\mapsto({}^{\perp_{0,1}}W_{1}\cap{}^{\perp_{0,1}}W_{2},W_{1},W_{2}).

In particular there are in total
\rank(W_{1})+\rank(W_{2})+\rank(W_{3})=n
augmented intervals of one of the forms
(\krew_{2}^{-1}(I),X)
,
(I,X)
or
(\krew_{2}(I),X)
. Summing over all mutable intervals and accounting for the fact that each augmented interval is counted thrice yields the result.
∎

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
(I,X)
be an augmented interval and
(B,I,A)

with completions

and

Proof.

We write

I=[(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})\leq(\mathcal{T}^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})]

and recall

A=[(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})\leq(\mathcal{T}^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\wedge({}^{\perp_{0}}X,\Cogen X)]

and

B=[(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})\vee(\Gen X,X^{\perp_{0}})\leq(\mathcal{T}^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})].

For each

we let

be the corresponding 2-cluster tilting object and decompose it as

. We distinguish three cases which will turn out to correspond to whether the complement of
T^{(I,X)}
in
T^{I}
is a summand of
T^{I}_{free}
,
T^{I}_{tors}[1]
or
T^{I}_{supp}[2]

Case 1:

X\in W(T^{I}_{free})

In this case the complement of
T^{(I,X)}
in
T^{I}
will turn out to be a summand of
T^{I}_{free}
.

Step 1.1:

T^{B}_{supp}\cong T^{I}_{supp}\cong T^{A}_{supp}

The assumption
X\in W(T^{I}_{free})
implies
\Hom_{\Lambda}(T^{I}_{supp},X)=0
In particular we get

\Hom_{\Lambda}(T^{I}_{supp},\Gen X\vee\Gen T^{I}_{tors})=0=\Hom_{\Lambda}(T^{I}_{supp},\Cogen X\wedge\Cogen T^{I}_{free}).

The claim follows.

Step 1.2:

W(T^{I}_{free})=W(T^{A}_{free})

The objects
T^{I}_{free}
and
T^{A}_{free}
are the
\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{F})
-support tilting modules corresponding to the torsion free class
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\cap\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{F})

(\Cogen X\wedge\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\cap\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{F})

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq(\Cogen X\wedge\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
shows

Step 1.3:

From

|T_{free}^{I}|+|T_{tors}^{I}|+|T^{I}_{supp}|=|\Lambda|
and the analogous statement for

In particular
T^{A}_{tors}
and
T^{I}_{tors}
are both given by the tilting module of
\mathcal{T}\cap W(T^{I}_{tors})
in
W(T^{I}_{tors})

.

Step 1.4:

T^{A}_{free}
is a mutation of
T^{I}_{free}
as an
\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{F})
-support tilting module

We consider the torsion pair
(\tilde{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}},\tilde{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}):=(\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\cap\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{F}),\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\cap\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{F}))
in
\tors\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{F}).
The wide subcategories of
\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{F})
corresponding to
(\tilde{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}},\tilde{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}})
are
\mathfrak{a}(\tilde{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}})=\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{F})\cap\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})
and
\mathfrak{a}(\tilde{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}})=\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})

is a simple object in
\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{F})\cap\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})
. In particular
X
is a downlabel of
(\tilde{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}},\tilde{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}})
in
\tors\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{F})

and
\Cogen T^{I}_{free}
are adjacent in
\tors\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{F})
and hence

is a mutation of

as an
\mathfrak{a}(\mathcal{F})
-support tilting module.

Step 1.5:
description of the almost 2-cluster tilting object

From Step 1.2 we get that
T^{I}_{free}
and
T^{A}_{free}
have the same rank. By the previous step we can decompose

and

are both completions of the almost 2-cluster tilting object

Step 1.6:

T^{I}\prec T^{A}
among the completions of

T^{B}_{tors}

Shifting an almost 2-cluster tilting object yields another almost 2-cluster tilting object. Applying the Serre permutation to each element of an interval mutation should therefore yield another interval mutation. The next Proposition makes this precise. It also collects some results about the ranks of the categories

Proposition 5.12

Let
(B,I,A)

if

(\mathbb{S}A,\mathbb{S}B,\mathbb{S}I)

We let
T^{(I,X)}
be the almost cluster tilting module corresponding to

and let
T_{B},T_{I}
and
T_{A}
be the three complements of

labeled such that

and similar for

and

. We consider the three cases from the proof of

separately.

In Case 1

6.
The Serre functor and representations of incidence algebras

In this section we recall the definition and basic properties of incidence algebras and of the Serre functor. Then we show how to compute the Serre functor for certain modules of incidence algebras called boolean antichain modules.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
k
-linear and Hom-finite additive category, where
k
is a field. A
Serre functor
is an equivalence
\mathbb{S}\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, together with natural isomorphisms

\Hom_{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)\cong\Hom_{\mathcal{C}}(Y,\mathbb{S}X)^{*}

for all objects
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
.
If
\mathcal{C}
admits a Serre functor than it is unique up to natural isomorphism.

We refer for example to
[
24
, Chapter 6.3]
for more information on Serre functors. The bounded derived category of finite dimensional algebras of finite global dimension provides a large class of examples of categories with a Serre functor.

Proposition 6.2
.

[
24
, Theorem 6.4.13]

Let
R
be a finite dimensional algebra with finite global dimension. Then
D^{b}(R)
admits a Serre functor
\mathbb{S}
given by the derived Nakayama functor.

For the rest of this section let
L
be a finite lattice and
k
a field.

Definition 6.3
.

The
incidence algebra

kL
has as basis the set of pairs
\{(a,b)\in L^{2}\mid a\leq b\}
. The multiplication is given by

(a,b)*(c,d)=\begin{cases}(a,d)&\text{if }b=c\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}

Definition 6.4
.

A
representation

M=(M_{a\in L},M_{a<b\in L^{2}})
of
L
consists of a vector space
M_{i}
for each
i\in L
and a linear map
M_{a<b}\colon M_{a}\rightarrow M_{b}
for each comparable pair
a\lneq b\in L^{2}
such that for each chain
a<b<c\in L^{3}
we have
M_{a<c}=M_{b<c}\circ M_{a<b}
. A
homomorphism

f=(f_{a\in L})\colon M\rightarrow N
of representations of
L
is a collection of linear maps
f_{a}\colon M_{a}\rightarrow N_{a}
such that
f_{b}\circ M_{a<b}=N_{a<b}\circ f_{a}
for each pair
a<b\in L^{2}
.

Warning 6.5
.

There is a different notion of a representation of a lattice which requires the maps
M_{a<b}
to be injective. We do not require this here.

The category of representations of
L
is equivalent to the category of right-modules
\modulecat kL
and we will identify the two. The most important modules of
L
for us are the interval modules which we introduce next.

Definition 6.6
.

Given an interval
I=[a,b]\subset L
we define the
interval module

M_{I}
by

M_{I,x}=k
if
x\in I
and
f_{x<y}=\textnormal{id}_{k}
if
x,y\in I
.

Proposition 6.7
.

The simple modules in
\modulecat kL
are exactly the modules of the form
M_{[a,a]}
for
a\in L
. The projectives are the
M_{[a,\max L]}
and the injectives are the
M_{[\min L,a]}
for
a\in L
.

To simplify notation we write
P_{a}=M_{[a,\max L]}
,
I_{a}=M_{[\min L,a]}
and
S_{a}=M_{[a,a]}
. Morphisms between interval modules are easy to describe:

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
M_{[a,b]}
and
M_{[c,d]}
be two interval modules then

\dim\Hom_{kL}(M_{[a,b]},M_{[c,d]})=\begin{cases}1&\textnormal{if }c\leq a\leq d\leq b\\
0&\textnormal{else}\end{cases}

Let
a,b\in L
then we get for the projectives and injectives:

\displaystyle\dim\Hom_{kL}(P_{a},P_{b})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\textnormal{if }b\leq a\\
0&\textnormal{else}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\dim\Hom_{kL}(I_{a},I_{b})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\textnormal{if }b\leq a\\
0&\textnormal{else}\end{cases}

The incidence algebras of lattices always have finite global dimension and hence admit a Serre functor
\mathbb{S}
. Because
\mathbb{S}
is given by the derived Nakayama functor we can assume without loss of generality that
\mathbb{S}P_{a}=I_{a}
and that for
b\leq a
the canonical embedding
P_{b}\hookrightarrow P_{a}
gets sent to the canonical projection
I_{b}\twoheadrightarrow I_{a}
. To compute the image of more general interval modules under the Serre functor we need to consider two bigger classes of modules: The antichain modules and the dual antichain modules. The intersection of these two classes recovers exactly the class of interval modules. We refer to
[
18
]
for more information on antichain modules.

Definition 6.9
.

A subset
C\subset L
is called an
antichain
if the elements of
C
are pairwise incomparable. We speak of an antichain
C
over
\alpha\in l
if
\alpha
is a strict lower bound for all elements of
C
. Dually an antichain
D
is below
\beta
if
\beta
is a strict upper bound for all elements in
D
. Given a, possibly empty, antichain
C
over
\alpha
the antichain module
M^{C}_{\alpha}
is given by
M^{C}_{\alpha,x}=k
if
x\in\{y\in L\mid\alpha\leq y,\ c\nleq y\ \forall c\in C\}
. Further given an antichain
D
below
\beta
the dual antichain module
M^{\beta}_{D}
is given by
M^{\beta}_{D,x}=k
if
x\in\{y\in L\mid\beta\geq y,\ d\ngeq y\ \forall d\in D\}
. In both cases the maps are given by
\textnormal{id}_{k}
wherever possible and
0
else.

We can write an interval module
M_{[a,b]}
as an antichain module as follows: Let
C=\min([a,\max L]\setminus[a,b])
. Then
M_{[a,b]}=M^{C}_{a}
. The main advantage of antichain modules is that they admit a canonical projective resolution called the antichain resolution. They were first described in general in
[
21
]
.

Definition 6.10
.

Let
C
be an antichain over
\alpha
and
M^{C}_{\alpha}
the corresponding antichain module. The
antichain resolution
of
M^{C}_{\alpha}
is a projective resolution of
M^{C}_{\alpha}
of the form

0\rightarrow P^{|C|}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow P^{i}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow P^{1}\rightarrow P_{\alpha}\rightarrow M^{C}_{\alpha}\rightarrow 0

where

P^{i}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}C^{\prime}\subseteq C\\
|C^{\prime}|=i\end{subarray}}P_{\vee C^{\prime}}

and the maps are given by the Koszul sign rule.

In particular every antichain module has a finite projective resolution. Since all simple modules are antichain modules we recover
\globaldim kL<\infty
. Dually we get an antichain coresolution for dual antichain modules:

Definition 6.11
.

Let
D
be an antichain under
\beta
and
M^{\beta}_{D}
the corresponding dual antichain module. The
antichain coresolution
of
M^{\beta}_{D}
is an injective resolution of
M^{\beta}_{D}
of the form

0\rightarrow M^{\beta}_{D}\rightarrow I_{\beta}\rightarrow I^{1}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow I^{i}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow I^{|D|}\rightarrow 0

where

I^{i}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}D^{\prime}\subseteq D\\
|D^{\prime}|=i\end{subarray}}I_{\wedge D^{\prime}}

and the maps are given by the Koszul sign rule.

Applying the Serre functor to the antichain resolution of an antichain
C
over
\alpha
sometimes yields an antichain coresolution for a different antichain
D
below
\beta
. In this case we obtain
\mathbb{S}M^{C}_{\alpha}=M^{\beta}_{D}[|C|]
. The antichains for which this happens are called boolean, respectively dual boolean.

Definition 6.12
.

Let
C
be an antichain over
\alpha
. We consider the map of join semilattices

\displaystyle\gamma^{C}_{\alpha}\colon\mathcal{P}(C)

\displaystyle\rightarrow L

\displaystyle\emptyset

\displaystyle\mapsto\alpha

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigvee A\text{ for }A\neq\emptyset

where
\mathcal{P}(C)
is the boolean lattice of subsets of
C
ordered by inclusion. We call the antichain
C
over
\alpha

boolean
if
\gamma^{C}_{\alpha}
is an embedding of lattices.

Dually let
D
be an antichain under
\beta
. We consider the map of meet semilattices

\displaystyle\gamma^{\beta}_{D}\colon\mathcal{P}(C)^{op}

\displaystyle\rightarrow L

\displaystyle\emptyset

\displaystyle\mapsto\beta

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigwedge A\text{ for }A\neq\emptyset

where
\mathcal{P}(C)^{op}
is the boolean lattice of subsets of
C
ordered by reverse inclusion. We call the antichain
D
under
\beta

dual boolean
if
\gamma^{\beta}_{D}
is an embedding of lattices.

There are bijections

\{\text{boolean }C\text{ over }\alpha\}\leftrightarrow\{\text{boolean sublattices of }L\}\leftrightarrow\{\text{dual boolean }D\text{ under }\beta\}

where we associate to a boolean sublattice
B\subset L
the antichain consisting of its atoms over
\min B
and the antichain consisting of its coatoms under
\max B
. The following result is folklore. Special cases have appeared in
[
32
]
,
[
18
]
and
[

]
, but we could not find the general statement in the literature.

Proposition 6.13
.

Let
C
be a boolean antichain over
\alpha
. Then
\mathbb{S}M^{C}_{\alpha}=M^{\beta}_{D}[|C|]
, where
D=\{\bigvee C^{\prime}\mid C^{\prime}\subset C,|C^{\prime}|=|C|-1\}
under
\beta=\bigvee C
is the corresponding dual boolean antichain.

Proof.

We note that with this choice for
D
and
\beta
the images of the maps
\gamma^{C}_{\alpha}
and
\gamma^{\beta}_{D}
agree. We call the boolean sublattice given by the images of these maps
B
and for
x\in B
we use
|x|
to refer to the rank of

with respect to
B
. In
\mathcal{D}^{b}(kL)
we use the antichain resolution to obtain

M^{C}_{\alpha}\cong(P^{|C|}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow P^{i}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow P^{1}\rightarrow P_{\alpha})

where

P^{i}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}C^{\prime}\subseteq C\\
|C^{\prime}|=i\end{subarray}}P_{\vee C^{\prime}}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in B\\
|x|=i\end{subarray}}P_{x}.

Applying the Serre functor to each

we get

\mathbb{S}P^{i}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in B\\
|x|=i\end{subarray}}\mathbb{S}P_{x}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in B\\

and hence using the antichain coresolution

7.
Cambrian lattices are periodic Serre formal

We now combine the results from the previous chapters to prove that the lattice
\tors\Lambda
is periodic Serre formal and hence fractionally Calabi-Yau. The lattices of the form
\tors\Lambda
belong to the class of Cambrian lattices and at the end of the section we show how to generalise this result to all Cambrian lattices. We start by introducing periodic Serre formal and fractionally Calabi-Yau algebras and lattices.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
R
be a finite dimensional algebra with finite global dimension. We call
R

Serre formal
if the orbits of the indecomposable injectives under the Serre functor consist of stalk modules. That is for each indecomposable injective
I
and each

and

with
\mathbb{S}^{n}I\cong M[k]
. Further we call

periodic Serre formal
if there is

with
\mathbb{S}^{n}I\cong I[k]
for each indecomposable injective
I
.
We call a finite lattice
L

periodic Serre formal
if
kL
is for every field
k
.

.

Let
R
be a finite dimensional algebra with finite global dimension and

R
is
(m,n)
-fractionally Calabi-Yau
if

\mathbb{S}^{n}\cong[m]

in
\mathcal{D}^{b}(R)
.
A finite lattice

is
(m,n)
-fractionally Calab–Yau
if
kL
is for every field
k
.

We do not know whether either property can depend on the field. For lattices the two properties were connected by Rognerud.

.

[

The antichain

We first note that the interval

Now assume

and
\mathbb{S}M_{A}=M_{\mathbb{S}A}[k_{A}]
by induction. Applying the Serre functor and substituting these identities yields a second triangle

(7.1)

M_{\mathbb{S}B}[k_{B}]\rightarrow\mathbb{S}M_{I}\rightarrow M_{\mathbb{S}A}[k_{A}]\rightarrow M_{\mathbb{S}B}[k_{B}+1].

By
5.12
we know that either
k_{I}=k_{B}
and
(\mathbb{S}A,\mathbb{S}B,\mathbb{S}I)
is an interval mutation or
k_{I}=k_{A}
and
(\mathbb{S}I,\mathbb{S}A,\mathbb{S}B)
is an interval mutation. We consider the former case, the latter can be treated similarly.
We again obtain a triangle

M_{\mathbb{S}A}\rightarrow M_{\mathbb{S}B}\rightarrow M_{\mathbb{S}I}\rightarrow M_{\mathbb{S}A}[1].

Shifting this triangle by
k_{I}
and rotating once yields a triangle

(7.2)

M_{\mathbb{S}B}[k_{B}]\rightarrow M_{\mathbb{S}I}[k_{I}]\rightarrow M_{\mathbb{S}A}[k_{A}]\rightarrow M_{\mathbb{S}B}[k_{B}+1],

where we have already used
k_{I}=k_{B}=k_{A}-1
. Finally since

\dim\Ext^{1}(M_{\mathbb{S}A}[k_{A}],M_{\mathbb{S}B}[k_{B}])=\dim\Ext^{1}(\mathbb{S}M_{A},\mathbb{S}M_{B})=\dim\Ext^{1}(M_{A},M_{B})=1

and neither of the two triangles (
7.1
) and (
7.2
) split, we must have
\mathbb{S}M_{I}\cong M_{\mathbb{S}I}[k]
.
∎

Corollary 7.6
.

The lattice
\tors\Lambda
is periodic Serre formal.

Proof.

By
7.4
all indecomposable injectives are of the form
M_{I}
for some mutable interval
I
. Now iterated application of
7.5
shows that
k\tors\Lambda

is Serre formal.
∎

Theorem 7.7
.

Let
h
be the Coxeter number of
\Lambda
and
N
the number of indecomposable representations of
\Lambda
.
Then
\tors\Lambda
is
(2N,2h+2)
-fractionally Calabi-Yau.

Further if
\Lambda
is of Dynkin type
A_{1}
,
B_{n}
,
C_{n}
,
D_{n}
(
n
even),
E_{7}
,
E_{8}
or
G_{2}
then
\tors\Lambda
is
(N,h+1)
-fractionally Calabi-Yau.

Proof.

Applying
7.5
to the results of
4.17
yields
\mathbb{S}^{2h+2}M_{I}\cong M_{I}[2N]
(respectively
\mathbb{S}^{h+1}M_{I}\cong M_{I}[N]
) for every mutable interval
I
. By
7.4
the indecomposable injectives
I
are among the
M_{I}
and hence we can apply
7.3
to complete the proof.
∎

We note that the Calabi-Yau dimension only depends on the Dynkin type of
\Lambda
. In fact Ladkani has shown in
[
26
]
that for
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
two different algebras of the same Dynkin type we have
\mathcal{D}^{b}(k\tors\Lambda)\cong\mathcal{D}^{b}(k\tors\Lambda^{\prime})
.

9.
Tamari lattices and geometric models

Tamari lattices are the lattices of torsion classes of linear type
A
path algebras. They were proven to be fractionally Calabi-Yau by Rognerud in
[
32
]
. Our proof is partially based on his approach. We want to use this section to compare the two arguments and to relate the geometric model used by Rognerud to the geometric model Baur and Marsh introduced for the 2-cluster category in type
A
in
[
5
]
.

We start by giving an overview of Rognerud’s proof. For more details see
[
32
]
and
[
31
]
. Rognerud introduced a family of intervals called
exceptional intervals
and showed that the Serre functor permutes them up to shift. The exceptional intervals are exactly the mutable intervals in this case since both are given by intervals in the lattice of noncrossing partitions. There are two main differences between our proof and Rognerud’s: the way the Serre functor is computed and the way the exceptional intervals are parametrised. The interval modules of all exceptional intervals have boolean antichains and this allowed Rognerud to compute the Serre functor applied to each of them using a version of
6.13
. Every Cambrian lattice that is not Tamari or type I admits a mutable interval with a non-boolean antichain and hence this part of Rognerud’s argument can not be generalised directly. To parametrise the exceptional intervals Rognerud used noncrossing trees.

Definition 9.1
.

A
noncrossing tree
of type
A_{n}
is a subset of the diagonals of an
n+2
gon such that

(1)

no two diagonals cross,

(2)

every vertex is incident to at least one diagonal and

(3)

the diagonals do not include a cycle.

Note that boundary edges are considered diagonals for this definition.

Noncrossing trees admit a canonical permutation called planar duality. Rognerud showed that it corresponds to the Serre functor on exceptional interval. Note that the planar dual of the planar dual of a noncrossing tree is not the original tree, but a rotation of it by
2\pi/(n+2)

Definition 9.2
.

The
planar dual
of a noncrossing tree
T
is constructed as follows: We add a new vertex on each boundary segment of the
n+2
-gon. Then
T
cuts the
n+2
-gon into regions with each regions containing exactly one of the new vertices. Each edge of
T
separates two regions. For each edge in
T
we consider a new edge connecting the vertices in the regions it separates. The planar dual of
T
consists of all of these new edges after rotation by
2\pi/2(n+2)
. The rotation moves the new vertices onto the old ones.

Appendix by Rene Marczinzik: Periodic Serre formal algebras for incidence algebras of lattices

In this appendix we want to motivate the classification of periodic Serre formal incidence algebras of lattices, which was one of the motivating factors for this article. It seems the classification of periodic Serre formal incidence algebras is a much more tractable problem compared to the classification of fractionally Calabi-Yau incidence algebras of lattices as computer experiments suggest.
We will introduce a combinatorial version of periodic Serre formal algebras and pose the conjecture that for incidence algebras of lattices those two notions coincide.
Let
A=KQ/I
be a quiver algebra of finite global dimension with
n
simple modules. Let
\omega
be the Cartan matrix of
A
given as the
n\times n
-matrix with entries
\omega_{i,j}=\dim e_{j}Ae_{i}
.
The Coxeter matrix
C
of
A
is given by
-\omega^{T}\omega^{-1}
and we have that
C
sends the dimension vector of indecomposable projective modules
P_{i}
to minus the dimension vector of the indecomposable injective module
I_{i}
. We refer for example to
[
2
, Chapter III.3]
for more information and properties of the Coxeter matrix.
We denote by
[M]
elements in the Grothendieck group
K_{0}(A)
given by a module
M
.
Recall that an
A
-module
M
is called
perfect
if it has finite projective dimension
n
and
\Ext_{A}^{i}(M,A)\neq 0
iff
i=n
.

Proposition 10.1
.

Let
A
be an algebra of finite global dimension and
M
a perfect
A
-module of projective dimension
n
. Then
C([M])=(-1)^{n+1}[\tau_{n}(M)]
, where we set here
\tau_{0}:=\nu
the Nakayama functor.

Proof.

For
n=0
, by definition of the Coxeter matrix we have
C[P]=-[\nu(P)]
for a projective module
P
and the Nakayama functor
\nu
. Thus assume
n>0
and let

(10.1)

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow P_{n}\rightarrow P_{n-1}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow P_{0}\rightarrow M\rightarrow 0

be a minimal projective resolution of
M
.
Applying first the functor
\Hom_{A}(-,A)
and then the duality
D
and using that
M
is perfect gives the exact sequence:

(10.2)

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow D\Ext_{A}^{n}(M,A)\rightarrow D\Hom_{A}(P_{n},A)\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow\Hom_{A}(P_{0},A)\rightarrow 0.

Since this also exact sequence coincides with the exact sequence coming from the definition of
\tau_{n}(M)=\tau\Omega^{n-1}(M)
, we have
D\Ext_{A}^{n}(M,A)\cong\tau_{n}(M)
.
From the exact sequence
10.1
we obtain the following equality in the Grothendieck group:

[M]=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}{(-1)^{i}[P_{i}]}

and from the exact sequence
10.2
we obtain the equality:

[D\Ext_{A}^{n}(M,A)]=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}{(-1)^{i}[D\Hom_{A}(P_{i},A)]}.

Now using the definition of the Coxeter matrix and combining those identities gives:

C([M])=C(\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}{(-1)^{i}[P_{i}]})=-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}{(-1)^{i}[D\Hom_{A}(P_{i},A)]}

=-(-1)^{n}\sum\limits_{r=0}^{n}{(-1)^{r}[D\Hom_{A}(P_{n-r},A)]}=(-1)^{n+1}[D\Ext_{A}^{n}(M,A)].

∎

Motivated by the previous proposition, we give the following definition:

Definition 10.2
.

Let
A
be an algebra of finite global dimension with Coxeter matrix
C
and
n
points labeled by
1,...,n
.
We call an indecomposable injective
A
-module
I_{i}

combinatorially Serre formal
if
there is a minimal
n_{i}\geq 0
such that
C^{n_{i}}([I_{i}])=[P_{\pi(i)}]
, that is the
n_{i}
-th power of
C
applied to
[I_{i}]
gives the dimension vector of an indecomposable projective module
P_{\pi(i)},
and furthermore
C^{k}[I_{i}]
is always a weakly positive dimension vector or a weakly negative dimension vector for all
0\leq k\leq n_{i}
. We call
A

combinatorially Serre formal
if every indecomposable injective
A
-modules are combinatorially Serre formal and the map
\pi:\{1,..,n\}\rightarrow\{1,...,n\}
is a bijection, which we all the
Serre permutation
in that case.

The next corollary is a direct consequence of the definition of periodic Serre formal algebras, Proposition
10.1
and the fact that the Coxeter transformation is up to a sign the linear algebraic version of the derived Nakayama functor:

Corollary 10.3
.

A periodic Serre formal algebra of finite global dimension is combinatorially Serre formal.

We pose the following conjecture:

Conjecture 10.4
.

Let
A=kL
be the incidence algebra of a lattice
L
.
Then
A
is combinatorially Serre formal if and only if
A
is periodic Serre formal.

We verified this conjecture for all lattices with at most 12 elements.
The main result in this article showed that Cambrian lattices are periodic Serre formal. We pose it as a problem to give a general classification:

Problem 10.5
.

Classify the incidence algebras
KL
with
L
a lattice such that
KL
is periodic Serre formal.

The next result gives the classification in the distributive case using the main results of
[
17
]
:

Proposition 10.6
.

Let
KL
be the incidence algebra of a distributive lattice
L
. Then
KL
is periodic Serre formal if and only if
L
is a divisor lattice.

Proof.

A divisor lattice is periodic Serre formal as periodic Serre formal is invariant under taking tensor products and divisor lattices are by definition tensor products of hereditary linear Nakayama algebras.
Conversely, note that in a periodic Serre formal algebra
A
the indecomposable injective (left and right) modules
I
are all perfect, that is
Ext_{A}^{i}(I,A)
iff
i=\pdim I
. Now by
[
17
, Theorem 5.4]
a distributive lattice with all indecomposable injective (left and right) modules being perfect is a divisor lattice.
∎
