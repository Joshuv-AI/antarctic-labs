Parallel execution of nonlinear logic circuits using reconfigurable free-space diffractive optics
Authors: Gaurang R. Bhatt, Elliot J. Fuller, François Léonard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
All-optical computing promises fast, energy-efficient processing and has regained momentum as artificial intelligence workloads strain electronic hardware. Among optical approaches, free-space diffractive optics enables rapid, multidimensional information processing with high parallelism, but advancing these systems requires a fundamental understanding of their computational capacity and how to exploit it. Whereas electronic computation relies on cascaded linear and nonlinear operations to implement digital circuits, neural networks, and machine vision, we show that diffractive optical systems can implement complex logic circuits by collapsing serial chains of nonlinear functions into a single optical stage. We first realize all basic logic gates and then demonstrate parallel half-/full adders and subtractors with one-stage readout. We further demonstrate an 8-bit ripple-carry adder by cascading eight one-shot optical full adders. Finally, we show scalability to hundreds of parallel inputs and direct two-dimensional image processing using spatially localized nonlinear optical logic functions.

Introduction

Over the years, optical computing systems have attracted the interest of the scientific community due to their promise for fast, high throughput implementations of various algorithms1. Recently, a new-found interest in optical computing systems has emerged to address the demands of artificial intelligence for high computational resources, fast response times, and low power consumption2. Targeting both traditional and neuromorphic algorithms, a variety of on-chip integrated photonic processors have been demonstrated1,2. Photonic implementations have been shown using integrated waveguide-based approaches, where interaction effects at waveguide or photonic crystal junctions are used to generate basic logic functions which are then cascaded to realize more complex circuits3–11. However, these waveguide-based photonic processors rely on confinement of light into one or a few guided modes, with each node performing only one local boolean or mathematical function. Further, scaling these integrated photonic processors to perform multidimensional (e.g., 2D images) information processing requires complex architectures with monolithically integrated electronics12–14.

Free-space optical processors relying on diffractive optics offer the ability to perform fast, multidimensional information processing with a high degree of parallelism and minimal electronics15,16. Creation of engineered light-fields using free-space diffractive optics has attracted interest for processing multidimensional data in computer vision17,18, medical imaging19, optical processors20, neural networks21–24, as well as quantum and optical computing25–29. Moreover, encoding and processing information in free space allows for higher data bandwidth and a density of diffractive elements determined by the wavelength of operation30. With the aim of realizing basic transformations, several works using free-space diffractive optics have demonstrated various mathematical operations31,32, discrete Fourier transforms20, matrix multiplications33, permutation operations34, as well as linear logic functions35–37.

However, realizing complex and nonlinear functions using free-space diffractive optics remains an important challenge. While free-space optical diffractive systems have been explored as neural networks21–24, the architectural dissimilarities make it challenging to compare directly to conventional electronic computation35,38,39. Therefore, approaches where comparisons to conventional functions can be made are needed to help determine the advantage of optical parallelism. The execution of logic functions as computational primitives provides an excellent starting point. First, logic gates have clearly articulated digital analogs executed over a well-defined number of clock cycles and second, these logical primitives can be cascaded to generate more complex functions. In addition, nonlinear logic functions such as exclusive-OR (XOR) are often used to illustrate the attributes of neural networks40 and have recently served as the fundamental building blocks of deep logic gate networks41.

Our experimental approach for a free-space and reconfigurable optical system to realize non-linear digital logic functions employs simple phase patterns in one layer, operates at visible wavelengths, and can realize normally cascaded nonlinear functions in one stage. This marks a significant step beyond previous free-space diffractive approaches, which required multiple material layers, each with thousands of meta-atoms needing to be computationally designed35,38,39. In addition, previous work focused on the microwave regime with the field read-out requiring spatial scanning of a single-point probe38. In contrast, our scheme at visible wavelengths relies upon simple readout from a photodetector.

Results

Experimental approach

Our approach relies on the interference of multiple identical (in amplitude, frequency, spatial extent) and coherent optical beams that represent the digital inputs. Figure 1A illustrates the interference pattern of two identical beams of light as viewed in the far-field. The digital logic functionality is realized by measuring the optical intensity at different spatial locations of the interference pattern; by appropriately choosing the optical detection threshold level to represent logic 0 and logic 1, the fundamental maximum (anti-node) of the interference pattern follows a logical OR function, whereas the first-order maxima follow a logical AND function. The OR function can be simultaneously realized by changing the threshold intensity either at the same pixel (as AND) or at the green pixel, as shown. The non-linear XOR function is realized at the location of first-order minima of the interference pattern, where the relative phase difference between the two beams is ππ. Our experimental approach is presented in Fig. 1B–D and Supplementary Note 1, wherein we generate identical optical spots (Fig. 1B) using a laser and a digital micro-mirror device (DMD). The optical spots impinge on a spatial light modulator (SLM) programmed with a phase pattern, as shown in Fig. 1C, that provides beam deflections to ensure that the optical spots interfere at the detector plane in the far-field (Fig. 1D). We use a Charged Coupled Device camera to capture the full 2D image purely for representational purposes, but only measure the optical intensity at fixed, predetermined pixels to realize a given logic function. In our experiments, the optical inputs are either OFF (logical 00) or ON (logical 11), created by programming a square region on the DMD to direct the light away from the SLM (logical 0) or towards the SLM (logical 1). Simultaneous basic logic functions

We demonstrate that all basic logic operations are simultaneously present in the same optical field using only two inputs, one fixed diffractive surface, and three read-out pixels. Figure 1C shows the pattern programmed in the two halves of the SLM optically aligned to the two input spots; the blazed grating patterns in the two halves are identical in amplitude and period but have a phase difference of\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,\pi$$\end{document}π and different blaze directions. This allows us to coincide the two input spots at the same spatial location in the far-field, thus creating a mode-matched interference with high fringe-visibility (Near-unity dynamic control of the fringe visibility using the relative SLM phase is detailed in Supplementary Note 3). When either the 01 or 10 inputs are sent, a homogeneous optical intensity is observed, and the intensity on the camera pixel (represented by the blue dot in Fig. 1D) is above the threshold, and it is perceived as logical 1. However, for the 11 input, the interference pattern emerges, and the intensity at the pixel (same blue dot) is below the predetermined threshold level and is inferred as logical 0. The static phase profile thus directly produces the truth table for the nonlinear XOR function (Fig. 1A-inset). We dynamically test the speed of the system by sending 1000 random inputs chosen from the set {00, 01, 10, 11} and record the pixel intensity for each, reducing the camera acquisition frame to 16× 1616×16 pixels. As shown in Fig. 1C, a clear threshold can be defined delimiting logical output 0 and 1, leading to correct output for all the random input sets. The measurement results also indicate that our system can function at very low optical power, as the variation in output intensity is only ~10 counts (about 100 photons) on the camera pixel. More importantly, we demonstrate that all the Boolean functions are present in the same light field (as explained in Fig. 1A), including OR and AND, using the same static single diffractive surface by appropriately choosing the read-out pixel position and intensity threshold (a discussion of the optical patterns and the methodology to select the read-out pixels and thresholds is presented in the Supplementary Note 4). In this case, we monitor the optical intensity at a different pixel location represented by the red dot in Fig. 1D. A homogeneous intensity for the 01 and 10 inputs is high enough to produce a logical 1, while the 11 input produces the interference pattern with a maximum at this pixel, also giving a logical 1. The resulting statistical distribution of recorded optical intensities for a sequence of 1000 random inputs from {0000, 0101, 1010, 1111} is shown in Fig. 1F. The resulting AND logic function obtained using the horizontal line as threshold can be verified with the provided truth table. In Fig. 1F, we also present the case for the OR function that is simultaneously realized by changing the threshold intensity either at the same pixel or at the green pixel shown in Fig. 1D. The negative logic functions (NOT, NOR, NAND, XNOR) are obtained by simply reversing the threshold conditions of the positive gates, i.e., logical output equal to 1 if signal is below the threshold and 0 if above. The point here is that one light field generated from the interference of only two optical beams contains multiple logic functions, i.e., there is parallelism that is distinct from the traditional spatial parallelism for multiple input pairs (discussed below) (The work presented here uses a camera and a computer to illustrate the concepts. In that case, the pixel read-out and thresholding require digital operations that surpass the simplicity of the optical operations; however, as discussed in the Supplementary Note 2, a practical implementation would use fast, low-energy single pixels/detectors and analog thresholders to circumvent this issue.)

Higher complexity nonlinear digital circuits with one-shot parallelism

We take our system to the next level of computing complexity to make one-shot nonlinear circuits. The simplest of these is the half-adder (addition of two binary digits), which produces the binary SUM and CARRY bits (See Fig. 2A). In electronics, this is realized by combining two separate logic gates, XOR for SUM, and AND for CARRY. In our case, we realize this by simultaneously reading two different pixels, one for XOR and the other representing AND, thus achieving one-shot performance (see Figs. 1D and 2A). We perform a bit error measurement as a function of optical power on the camera to characterize the robustness of our optical system. In Fig. 2B we show the number of erroneous logic outputs as a function of photon counts for the {10} case. The error follows a Gaussian dependence on the {10} intensity (see Supplementary Note 5), going below 1 in 10,000 for as little as 8 photons for SUM and 4 photons for CARRY, the low floor being limited by the number of frames that can be uploaded to the DMD. Thus, we anticipate that error rates below 10−12 can be achieved with less than 15 counts per pixel for XOR, and even less for AND.

We also optically realize non-commuting operations such as the half-subtractor by creating a mode-mismatched interference between two Gaussian input spots. In Fig. 2C, we show the procedure for a binary half-subtractor along with its equivalent logic circuit. One can see that the electronic implementation of the half-subtractor requires an additional NOT gate for one of the inputs, along with an AND gate, which makes it a multi-stage sequential circuit. To optically realize the asymmetry between the two inputs (i.e., non-commuting), we offset the optical beams while maintaining some overlap and create an optical pattern composed of an interference bleeding over the individual beams (see Fig. 2D and Supplementary Note 4)42. The asymmetry is thus obtained by putting the BORROW read-out pixel where the interference pattern merges with one of the input beams. Figure 2E shows the captured light field image at the detection plane along with highlighted readout pixels representing the BORROW and DIFFERENCE bits. We ensure the reproducibility of our results by measuring system performance for a sequence of 200 random inputs {0000, 0101, 1010, 1111}. The statistical distribution of measured optical intensity at the two readout pixels is shown in Fig. 2F, G. One can see that both the XOR functionality for the DIFFERENCE and the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{A}*B$$\end{document}Ā*B combinatorial function for the BORROW can be concomitantly achieved. In our case, we defocus the out-coupling lens in the second 4f system (see setup in Supplementary Note 1) to create Gaussian beams (the pin-hole is kept at focal length f from the input coupling lens). Detailed explanations regarding this requirement to generate Gaussian beams are provided in Supplementary Note 4 and Supplementary Fig. 4.

We extend the above implementation to realize a full adder and a full subtractor using a single fixed diffractive surface and three input beams representing the three logical inputs (see Supplementary Note 4 for simulations). An electronic circuit schematic along with the truth table is provided for the full-adder in Fig. 3A. In electronics, the full-adder circuit is normally formed by cascading/serializing several logic gates to obtain the resulting truth table for the eight input combinations. Here, we show that we can realize this normally serial nonlinear circuit with one-stage parallelism (i.e., with all operations obtained from one light field). We achieve this by generating three identical beams with the DMD (representing the three digital inputs), equally spaced from each other and co-propagating in a triangular arrangement as shown in Fig. 3B. Corresponding to this arrangement, we program a pattern on the SLM as shown in Fig. 3B, which has three distinct regions with different blazed gratings to produce interference of the three beams at the camera. The interference patterns generated for two inputs at logical 1 are similar to the previous interference patterns but rotated according to the relative position of the input beams (Fig. 3C). However, when all three beams are logical 1 we obtain a triangular lattice pattern. The properties of the interference patterns can be controlled with the relative phase between them, as described earlier and in the Supplementary Note 3. However, since there are only two independent relative phases possible between three beams (as shown in Fig. 3B), it prevents us from using only two pixels to realize the full-adder in one stage. We circumvent this issue by using three read-out pixels; two pixels are used to determine the SUM bit and one pixel is used to determine the CARRY bit (see Fig. 3C). The SUM value is taken from the lowest of the two SUM pixels and compared to the SUM threshold while for the CARRY the measured optical intensity is simply compared with the threshold value to determine its logical state. In Supplementary Fig. 9, we show the statistical distribution of measured optical intensities for different combinations of inputs {000, 001, 010, …110, 111} sent through the system in a random sequence. One can see that a good separation between the logical 0 and 1 for the CARRY and SUM is achieved clearly representing the logical truth-table of a full-adder (Fig. 3A). Cascaded complex nonlinear circuits

We extend the implementation of our one-stage functions to show higher complexity circuits through optical cascading. Figure 3D shows the schematic of a Ripple Carry Adder (RCA) formed by cascading multiple full-adders that is used to add two multi-bit binary numbers. As shown in Fig. 3D, the addition process proceeds column-by-column, with each column adding the carry from the previous column and the two inputs, producing the SUM as well as the CARRY for the next column. The process is repeated from right-to-left until all the columns have been completed. The RCA can be achieved using a sequence of full adders as shown in Fig. 3D, with the result of the addition being the sequence of SUMs. Figure 3E shows a specific example for the addition of two 8-bit numbers. We realize the RCA by setting the SLM to the full-adder configuration and sequentially performing the column additions by sending inputs to the DMD based on the previous column results. Figure 3F shows the process for the addition, with each stage producing the column result at the bottom of the images.

As shown in Fig. 3A, the electronic system requires three stages to realize the full adder, so to a first approximation, realizing the circuit in one stage would be three times as fast, notwithstanding the differences in speed between optics and electronics. Alternatively, a cascaded operation can also be performed optically by cascading multiple (three in this case) diffractive layers or recycling light back into a single diffractive layer. This would still be beneficial since the stage time is not limited by the response time of electronic components.

Spatial parallelism and image processing

The results presented in the previous sections showed a new type of parallelism where normally cascaded logic circuits are realized in one shot. The optical system can also be used for standard spatial parallelism by projecting two-dimensional light fields containing many input pairs onto the SLM, as illustrated in Fig. 4A. In this case, we prepare 100 input pairs from the DMD (5 columns of 20 pairs) and project them onto a SLM pattern consisting of five separate regions each programmed with a pair of gratings as described earlier in Fig. 1C. While scaling to even more logic functions in parallel for computation is an obvious next step (requiring detailed optical engineering), the system can also directly process two-dimensional light fields for image processing, which is a challenge for on-chip approaches. Here, we utilize a parallel array of optical XORs to realize edge detection in one shot. The concept is illustrated in Fig. 4B for a simple example image containing one edge between dark and bright regions. In the dark region, two adjacent pixels both have 0 input, and the XOR output is 0. For two pixels that straddle the edge, the XOR input is either 01 or 10, and the XOR output is 1. In the bulk of the pattern, where both inputs are 1, the destructive interference gives an XOR output of 0. Thus, high values of the output are only seen at the location of edges. To illustrate this in practice, we considered the square annulus of Fig. 4B and performed an XOR operation in software, on each pair of pixels in the image, in the horizontal and vertical directions, to yield a combined output with the detected edges.

We implemented this approach in optical hardware using the SLM grating and pixel locations used for the 100 XORs. Figure 4C shows the input image on the DMD and the resulting light field on the camera for both the horizontal and vertical SLM gratings. In these images, interference patterns appear in the bulk of the pattern, while edges do not show such interference. While this simple demonstration has a resolution limited by the 5 SLM columns, we are not limited to the 20 read-out pixels in each column; we read out 100 pixels along each column/row to improve the resolution and obtain an edge-detected image that is faithful to the original image (right-most panel in Fig. 4C).

Discussion

In this work, we present an optical approach to realize complex nonlinear logic circuits with one-stage parallelism, using a single reconfigurable diffractive surface. Because of its implementation in free space, the method allows for direct processing of spatial parallel inputs, including two-dimensional images. Our approach offers an alternative to on-chip systems that can process one-dimensional inputs but require cascading of optical gates, often with scanning electronics to perform operations on multidimensional datasets. Tiling of the SLM to create a local function in each tile allows computing directly on two-dimensional inputs, which increases the capacity and/or removes the need to convert two-dimensional fields to one dimension. This could be used either to perform multiple operations in parallel, where each SLM tile performs an operation on the light incident on that tile, or to analyze 2D images where correlations between parts of the image are needed43.

In the case of parallel operations where each SLM tile has logical inputs, the capacity and size of the system depend on the wavelength of light, the ability to generate good quality inputs through the use of tightly packed emitters/reflectors, achieving good diffraction, and obtaining a non-linear intensity distribution at the detector plane30. This would require the generation of input spots of size ~20 µm separated by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d \sim \,50{{\rm{\mu }}}{{\rm{m}}}$$\end{document}d~50μm which is achievable with DMDs. Such a system would allow processing of 104 operations in parallel. Another implication for the architecture is in terms of the complexity and number of layers needed to achieve high classification accuracy. Existing work on optical diffractive neural networks has often focused on multiple layers with tens of thousands of trained phases per layer22,38, but it has been suggested that significantly fewer layers and a smaller number of trained features are needed to obtain good accuracy44,45. The results presented here suggest that even one simple diffractive surface can produce complex transformations, perhaps opening a path to much simpler designs.

Beyond these standard parallelization considerations, our work also identifies a different parallelism: the unraveling of normally cascaded (i.e., sequential) nonlinear functions (e.g., full adder) onto a single diffraction pattern, allowing for one-shot readout. This increases the number of effective operations that are performed in one pass and can be further improved by eliminating the backend electronics using fast optoelectronic devices46. Combined with the above parallelism, this could lead to even further performance improvements.

Finally, we note that the work presented here uses relatively slow input generation (i.e., DMD) and read-out methods (via camera) purely for demonstration purposes. For training and inference of neural networks as well as realizing parallel computation, faster image generation using novel electro-optic technologies, arrayed waveguides/fibers, and detector-array based data acquisition can be used to significantly increase the operational speeds46–48 (see Supplementary Note 2). With these enhancements, a reconfigurable element-based diffractive neural network would allow one to train the system in real time by tuning the phases on the SLM. We believe our work will open avenues for optical processors towards applications in image correlations, image classifications, and optical neural networks that are accurate, robust, and offer one-stage operation.

Methods

Generation of optical input

Light from a Spectra-Physics laser (CW, 532 nm, 20 mW, beam waist 2 mm) was attenuated and fed into a beam expander to create a circular beam of size ~20 mm. The expanded beam impinges on a digital micro-mirror device (DMD, Texas Instruments DLP700) at a normal incidence angle. The reflected light representing logical inputs is routed towards the SLM by using a combination of a quarter-wave plate and a polarization beam splitter. The logical inputs are programmed on the pixels of the DMD (1024× 7681024×768 px) and consist of square regions of size 70× 7070×70 pixels (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$957.6\,{{\rm{\mu m}}}\times 957.6\,{{\rm{\mu m}}}$$\end{document}957.6μm×957.6μm). The separation between any two inputs is 200 pixels (2.736 mm). The dark pixels on the DMD divert the light away from the optical axis while the programmed pixels reflect the light at normal incidence, thus creating an image of the reflected optical beam49. The reflected propagating beams are then passed through a 4f configuration with a 50 µm pinhole to remove higher-order diffraction modes arising from the DMD reflection.

Detection of light field

The modulated light field was imaged with an Andor Zyla 4.2 camera (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2048\times \,2048$$\end{document}2048×2048 pixels, pixel size 6.5 µm) located ~3 m away from the SLM. For faster acquisition, frames as small as 16× 1616×16 pixels were acquired, with an integration time as small as 100 µs, and 12-bit output. The counts from only two or three of these pixels are used for the logic functions. Images are acquired by triggering the camera directly from the DMD. Conversion from counts to number of photons was performed using the camera quantum efficiency of 82% and the sensitivity of 7.5 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e}^{-}/{{\rm{ADU}}}$$\end{document}e−/ADU (Analog-to-Digital Units).

Computer control of system

The system was controlled through a Python code that manages the DMD, SLM, and camera. The DMD was controlled through a Vialux V4395 unit using the ALP 4.3 application programming interface. Images sent to the DMD were 1-bit with one image display period consisting of equal illumination and dark times. The SLM was controlled through the Meadowlark dynamic-link libraries. 12-bit camera frames were captured using the Andor software development kit.
