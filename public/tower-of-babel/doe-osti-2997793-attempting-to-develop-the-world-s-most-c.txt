 ABSTRACT

This project aimed to make a practical system capable of sustained metal deposition in air
engineered with industrial integration and controls. The Al-Ce wire feedstock was tailored with
appropriate deposition and solidification properties for direct reactive interface printing (DRIP),
and the goal was to integrate onto a Hybrid Manufacturing Technologies system for producing
test parts without a controlled environment.

 1. OBECTIVES AND BACKGROUND

1.1 OBJECTIVES

The main objective of this Cooperative Research and Development Agreement (CRADA)
project between Hybrid Manufacturing Technologies (HMT) and Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL) was to develop one of the world’s most cost-effective metal part production
technologies. This work built off previous collaborations where the proof-of-concept was
developed, combining a novel high-temperature heat source with a uniquely engineered metal
material feedstock suitable for extrusion in open-air conditions. The new, efficient energy source
coupled with the feedstock material properties will unlock cost effectiveness in near-net metal
part creation. Additionally, a key element of this work is that the resulting deposition system will
be deployable on computer numerical control (CNC) machines (and robots), providing an easy
adoption path for machine shops and a practical means for part finishing in a single setup.

This Technical Collaboration aimed to deliver a technology that:
 • Is a cost-effective investment for machine shops with <1 year pay-back
 • Has running costs that are more competitive than machining alone
 • Enables existing CNC machines to be dual purposed as AM systems for metal (without
 any structural or guarding changes)
 • Is easily adopted by machinists (requiring <2 days of training)
 • Provides machine shops with technical capabilities to enjoy the freedoms of AM with the
 precision of CNC.
 • Is industry 4.0 ready
This Technical Collaboration resulted in the following technical achievements:
 • Novel wire fabrication method for tailored feedstock production
 • Induction heating modeling of wire feedstock to aid in system design
 • Properties of acceptable feedstock for reliable deposition
 • Adequate material properties for heat sink material. Mechanical properties were not of
 concern for this particular application
 • Progress towards commercialization

1.2 BACKGROUND

ORNL and PrintSpace 3D recently completed a Phase 1 Technical Collaboration (NFE- 17-
06531). The final report detailing the results of this project was submitted to AMO on
12/10/2019. Despite the promise shown during this Phase I Technical Collaboration, PrintSpace

3D has changed its trading structure and is not able to continue research in this area. PrintSpace
3D transferred its equipment and know-how developed during the Phase I project to HMT, and
HMT now continues progressing the research.
HMT’s current product line of AM heads and ancillaries is based on a unique and patented
integration methodology. It features deposition techniques including laser-based directed energy
deposition and pellet-fed material extrusion in CNC machines.
The envisaged product resulting from this Phase II Technical Collaboration will be a deposition
head for metal to be used inside of a CNC machine, gantry, or robotic platform. This product
format and integration style is best illustrated by the results from the Phase 1 Technical
Collaboration with HMT (NFE-16-06052: Link to report in OSTI:
https://info.ornl.gov/sites/publications/Files/Pub109240.pdf; See also Figure 3 below), except
that it will leverage the underlying materials and physics demonstrated in NFE-17-06531.
HMT is interested in teaming with ORNL researchers at the MDF in their effort to develop a
specific direct metal write (DMW) technology, termed Direct Reactive Interface Printing
(DRIP), which uses a reactive aluminum alloy chemistry and induction heating to produce 3D
prints with layer-to-layer cohesion at high volumetric deposition rates.
Fundamentally, DMW is analogous to most thermoplastic extrusion 3D printers - as it feeds wire
feedstock through a heated nozzle. The nozzle melts the wire feedstock into molten metal, which
is deposited in a controlled fashion to build cast structures in free space. This approach can be
used with a wide variety of metals including aluminum, titanium, stainless steel, and nickel-
based superalloys including Inconel. Initial development of this process has been focused on the
use of an aluminum-based alloy due to the practicality of handling the lower temperature.
Induction heating simplifies the nozzle and systems, which control it by heating the material on
demand immediately before extrusion through the nozzle. Direct heating of a metal, or the use of
a susceptor to transfer heat to a material can accomplish this. Beyond simplifying control and
expanding temperature capability, induction heating has the benefit of dramatically reducing
energy expenditure compared to a liquid pool design. The current prototype uses simple cast
ingots; however, for this project we will be incorporating wire feedstock with a metal inert gas
(MIG)-style delivery system. Equally important is the prototype, an Al-Ce alloy will be used as
an input material. This alloy has a reactive surface that stabilizes filaments such that they solidify
in the shape that they are printed rather than solidifying in the shape that they are flowing when
molten [1]. In general, the alloy has excellent fluidity and develops a strong and thermally stable
cast structure, making it a superior candidate for additive manufacturing. The surface
stabilization highlights another practical benefit of this technology: the ability for printing in
open atmosphere. This, combined with the low-cost feedstock and localized heating, makes
DRIP with Al-Ce alloys potentially the least expensive and least complex metallic additive
method yet developed.

2.1.5 Modeling Summary and Future Work

Numerical simulations for the induction heating were conducted with a 1D model that takes into
account the latent heat of solidification. The developed model capabilities include the prediction
of alloy temperature, alloy melting, required power distribution, and coil density. The induction
heating simulations were used to identify a valid power distribution required from the induction
coils. Based on the numerical simulation results several design changes were also recommended,
such as moving the insulator next to susceptor to reduce heat loss and use a thinner insulator.

Possible plans for future collaboration in the near future include the use of the combined physical
modeling and machine learning techniques for the data analysis, as follows:

 1. Another phase I for the HPC4Mfg program, would investigate the fluid dynamics effects
 on the solidification microstructure for the DC casting.

 2. A phase II for the HPC4Mfg program. This second option would extend the current DC
 casting model to include the fluid dynamics effects on the solidification microstructure
 for the DC casting.

2.2.1 Alloy Selection and Procurement

Six binary Al-Ce compositions were selected to explore
rheology, microstructure, extrudability, and printability in
an open-air environment. The first application of interest for Commercially
HMT was to use direct metal write to 3D print cooling fins Pure Al
onto an existing part. The goal was to maximize thermal
conductivity and strength could be neglected. For this C D
reason, binary alloys of Al and Ce were selected because
printability in the 6%Ce (wt%) composition has already
been demonstrated on a bench scale system [1]. Avoiding
all other alloying elements minimizes phonon scattering,
thus, maximizing thermal conductivity of the alloys. The
seven compositions were commercially pure Al, 1%Ce, 1%Ce 4%Ce
4%Ce, 6%Ce, 8%Ce, 10%Ce, 13%Ce, 16%Ce (all in wt%).
Three compositions (Commercially pure Al, 1%Ce, and Figure 8. Al and Al-Ce plates
4%Ce) were cast into a total of 15 plates (5 of each cast at Loukus Tech.
composition), by Loukus Technologies, with the following
nominal dimensions: 11 x 15 x 0.75 (inches) (Figure 8). Four plates of each composition were
sent to HMT to use as the precursor to their innovative wire fabrication technique, as well as for
use as baseplates for printing. The remaining plates were kept at ORNL for characterization. Six
of the compositions were arc melted by Max Neveau, a graduate student at UTK, for a
systematic evaluation of rheological properties (sans 4%Ce) using electrostatic levitation (ESL).
The rheology study is ongoing and contingent on the availability of ESL.

2.3 SYSTEM DEVELOPMENT

2.3.1 Wire Making

The drawing of wire from the various aluminum compositions was undertaken by Eck Industries
using traditional means and provided to HMT for evaluation on multiple occasions throughout
the project. See section 2.3.4.1 for further information on wire development via friction stir
channeling

2.3.2 Feedstock Wire Delivery System

In order to achieve the throughput of a metal extrusion system that would be commercially
attractive, it was determined that using off the shelf 0.45” (~1.1 mm diameter) wire would be
difficult to feed at a high enough rate to keep pace with desired deposition rates. As a result,
~1/8” or ~3 mm diameter wire was targeted. Although there are push-pull wire feeders that can
be used for relatively soft wire, these compositions proved so soft that it was anticipated to be

 11

challenging to get consistent feeding with conventional wire feeders. Additionally coupling the
federate of the wire to the process was also anticipated to be a key parameter to control, therefore
a customer wire feeder system was deemed necessary in the scope of this project. HMT designed
a system for 3 mm diameter soft metal composition wire delivery ([2]0) since the off the shelf
options were focused on smaller wire diameters and stiffer composition metals.

Figure 10. Custom large-scale soft wire feeder initial prototype

While awaiting the results of the induction modeling, project efforts were focused on advancing
initiative experimental testing. This involved utilizing a new wire feeder to evaluate various wire
compositions. It was discovered that while the prepared wire compositions started off soft, they
experienced significant work hardening after being repeatedly passed through the wire feed
mechanism. Note that, due to limited feedstock availability, HMT repeatedly feed the same
pieces of wire through the feeder. Annealing the wire was found to restore the material nearly to
its original as-drawn state and allowed for appropriate testing conditions.

In tandem with the wire feeder testing, a control architecture (Figure 11) was designed to ensure
that the wire feed force was synchronized with the nozzle and could be tuned to suit various
applications.

2.3.3.1 Oxidation at the Nozzle Tip

Prior attempts to extrude aluminum experienced significant clogging challenges due to oxidation
at the nozzle tip.

 12

Although HMT has a portfolio of local shielding nozzle designs for laser-based metal deposition,

2.3.4 Applications Exploration

The wire compositions tested, and the Al-Ce cast plates produced resulted in relatively low
mechanical properties. This was anticipated, however the practical implications of this became
much clearer after testing wire feeding and CNC machining the cast plate with these
compositions. These results led to the focus on non-structural components. Since the desire was
to aim at a specific commercial application using the existing material compositions, the new
application focus became the production of “cold plates,” which help thermally manage
electronics mounted to them. This application was selected to leverage the high thermal
conductivity of aluminum and achieve a commercially relevant result with relatively low
mechanical requirements.

2.3.4.1 Wire Drawing Out of Solid Feedstock using Friction Stir Channeling

As part of the shift to focus on cold plate manufacturing, a new suite of aluminum alloys became
available for testing with a novel wire drawing process called "CoreFlow™" (See Figure 12),
which was first developed by The Welding Institute (TWI) and further matured by HMT. The
results from testing these plates demonstrated that it is now possible to extrude wire in small
lengths for trials without the need for going through conventional wire drawing steps as shown
below.

Figure 12. AMBIT™ CoreFlow™ head enables subsurface friction stir channeling process to
extrude wire & leave a void
AMBIT™ CoreFlow™ heads extract metal from below the surface of a plate and extrude it as
wire (See Figure 12 middle). This leaves behind an empty channel (See Figure 12 right) in the
plate which can be used as a passage for liquid. The leaving of a hollow channel enables the
production of cold plates (Figure 13 left) and the extrusion of wire provides a potential feedstock
(Figure 13 right) to build up plate or billet to be used for planar or even non-planar cold plates.

 13

Figure 13. Extruded wire from Al-Ce compositions made using friction stir channeling

In the investigation of wire extrusion from three distinct aluminum-cerium alloys (commercially
pure Al, Al-1%Ce, and Al-4%Ce) using the CoreFlow™ system, several significant challenges
and observations were recorded. Some of these related to the relatively soft nature of these
compositions which led to clogging rather than efficient extrusion. A primary issue involved
material traveling up the probe taper and into the collet, leading to a jammed probe and tool
holder assembly upon process completion.

Regarding material performance, the Al-1%Ce alloy displayed poor stir welding characteristics
but ultimately yielded successful extrusions. In contrast, the Al-4%Ce alloy failed to undergo stir
welding, resulting in no extrusions. Two of the three compositions (commercially pure Al & Al-
1%Ce) were ultimately extruded into wire from plate. The results of the wire extrusion from the
new allows provided continuous lengths of wire in excess of 3 meters long which a total of 24
and 10 meters for the respective compositions. This was positive proof that it is possible to
extrude wire from Al-Ce alloys, albeit with some future adaptation of the equipment.

This provides an opportunity to have some of this material stay in a circular loop for reuse. Thus
producing a new alloy, drawing wire, and then depositing that wire onto the plate that it came
from is an interesting concept with a variety of commercial and environmental advantages. This
represents a unique and novel opportunity for further development, which moves the system
close to ideal material efficiency use in these compositions.

 3. OUTLOOK

3.1 LESSONS LEARNED

COVID slowed things and caused us to use resources less efficiently than desired. Resource
allocation for this project at HMT was available in the early period following the COVID-19
pandemic. However, misalignment in timing between various dependent tasks (including a lapse
in modeling licensing/compatibility) which caused misalignment of nearly one year that
prevented the project from progressing on the schedule when the time and energy were allocated
for it. Unfortunately, as the secondary and tertiary effects of the pandemic continued to reshape
the commercial landscape, attention at HMT needed to be focused on navigating it was not
possible to create the same scale of window originally envisaged for this project, which
precluded the depth of exploration desired.

 14

During the course of this project a new product introduction signaled the first commercialization
of an induction-based aluminum melting process from a European company in Belgium named
ValCUN. As part of the due diligence for this project, HMT met with ValCUN at an exhibition
to discuss the state of their technology. After seeing the parts, it appears that the ValCUN team
has advanced considerably beyond the R&D phase of this research. ValCUN’s approach is called
Molten Metal Deposition (MMD) technology and is amenable to use conventional aluminum
wire alloys including 2000 and 5000 alloys (with line of sight to 6000 series alloys as well) as
feedstock material. While the existence of a formerly unknown competitor was not welcome
news, it commercially validates the desirability of this type of technology. It also escalates the
amount of resource needed to be dedicated to this project to see it achieve commercial viability
and outcompete the first commercial mover in the marketplace. The ability of this competing
technology to print with compositions that already exceed the performance of the compositions
explored here make it difficult to complete, even in focused application areas. As the project
progressed, this began to loom as the largest hurdle to progress this project, despite the early
feasibility demonstrations and the IP protection held jointly between ORNL and HMT. The
limitation of the ValCUN technology is it currently only operates in an inert environment, like a
glovebox, which does leave many practical applications open, as we are targeting open-air
printing with a cover gas. Furthermore, the layer-to-layer adhesion of the 2000 and 5000 series
alloys is not surprising in inert environments, though likely does not translate to air. The novelty
of printing Al-Ce alloys still remains, as this alloy series can achieve layer-to-layer adhesion in
air as previously demonstrated.

3.2 PATH FORWARD

As noted in the above paragraph, ensuring that there is a commercially viable adoption path with
unique benefits is the key to progressing this concept. The selected application of cold plates
remains a relevant initial opportunity, but more application areas are desirable that leverage
stronger mechanical performance aluminum alloys.

These binary compositions remain relevant for cold plate applications as do Al-Ce die casting
alloys developed at ORNL, which will provide an alloy with enhanced mechanical properties.
Wire drawing of die casting alloy compositions with the aforementioned friction stir channeling
technology and open-air nozzle design and fabrication are the clear next steps for accessing a
wide array of potential printing applications. The use of local shielding to prevent oxidation at
the nozzle exit point will allow deposition outside of a closed inert chamber, enabling the
progress of this technology.
