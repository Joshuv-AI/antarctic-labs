Preventing Safety Drift in Large Language Models via Coupled Weight and Activation Constraints
Songping Peng, Zhiheng Zhang, Daojian Zeng, Lincheng Jiang, Xieping Gao

Abstract
Safety alignment in Large Language Models (LLMs) remains highly fragile during fine-tuning, where even benign adaptation can degrade pre-trained refusal behaviors and enable harmful responses. Existing defenses typically constrain either weights or activations in isolation, without considering their coupled effects on safety. In this paper, we first theoretically demonstrate that constraining either weights or activations alone is insufficient for safety preservation. To robustly preserve safety alignment, we propose Coupled Weight and Activation Constraints (CWAC) , a novel approach that simultaneously enforces a precomputed safety subspace on weight updates and applies targeted regularization to safety-critical features identified by sparse autoencoders. Extensive experiments across four widely used LLMs and diverse downstream tasks show that CWAC consistently achieves the lowest harmful scores with minimal impact on fine-tuning accuracy, substantially outperforming strong baselines even under high harmful data ratios.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in a wide range of natural language understanding and generation tasks
(
Achiam et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Qin et al. 2023
;
Grattafiori et al. 2024
)
. As LLMs are increasingly deployed in real-world applications, ensuring their safety alignment has received growing attention. To achieve this, LLMs are typically aligned before deployment using methods like supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
, or direct preference optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
. Meanwhile, adapting LLMs to specific downstream applications through fine-tuning has become a core paradigm, exemplified by services like OpenAI’s fine-tuning API
1
1

1

OpenAI fine-tuning API:
https://platform.openai.com/docs/guides/fine-tuning
and open-source LLMs (e.g., Llama, Mistral, Gemma). However, recent studies have revealed that current safety alignment techniques remain highly fragile under subsequent fine-tuning. Even a few epochs of training on completely benign data can fully erase refusal behaviors
(
Qi et al. 2024
;
Wei et al. 2024
;
Ponkshe et al. 2025a
;
Li et al. 2024
)
.

This brittleness has motivated a growing line of research on preserving the safety alignment of LLMs during downstream fine-tuning
(
Huang et al.
)
. Existing approaches can be categorized into alignment-stage, fine-tuning-stage, and post-tuning-stage methods. Alignment-stage methods strengthen initial safety through latent-space perturbations or targeted representation noising
(
Huang et al. 2024d
;
Rosati et al. 2024
)
, but remain vulnerable during downstream fine-tuning. Fine-tuning-stage defenses typically constrain weight-level updates through techniques such as low-rank safety projection
(
Hsu et al. 2024
)
and safety basin anchoring
(
Yang et al. 2025a
)
, or regularize activation-level critical pathways
(
Mukhoti et al. 2023
)
, while post-tuning methods attempt recovery by pruning harmful parameters or realigning neurons
(
Huang et al. 2024a
;
Yi et al. 2025
)
. However, these methods often focus on a single-level intervention (either weight-level or activation-level), which is insufficient to prevent safety degradation during fine-tuning. As illustrated in Figure
1
, harmful outputs can arise when weight drift or activation drift occurs. Constraining weight updates alone cannot prevent shifts in internal activations, as safety-related representations are not linearly distinct and may reorganize under fine-tuning
(
Ponkshe et al. 2025b
;
Kim et al. 2025
)
. On the other hand, activation-level regularization alone leaves model weights unconstrained, allowing the underlying computation to change
(
Shen et al. 2025
)
. This enables the model to bypass safety pathways, producing unsafe outputs even under a single-level constraint.

Among these stages, fine-tuning-stage methods offer a favorable trade-off between safety preservation and downstream task performance by imposing constraints directly during training. Nevertheless, current fine-tuning-stage approaches overwhelmingly constrain only weights and do not sufficiently address the interplay between weight updates and activation changes. This leads to a natural question:

Can we simultaneously constrain both weights and activations during fine-tuning to more robustly close these bypass pathways?

In this paper, we first theoretically demonstrate that constraining either weights or activations alone is insufficient for safety preservation. To more robustly preserve safety alignment while adapting to downstream tasks, we propose
Coupled Weight and Activation Constraints (CWAC)
, a novel approach that simultaneously constrains weight updates and internal activations. To address weight-level drift, we construct a weight safety subspace derived from a precomputed projection matrix using singular value decomposition on covariance matrices of harmful inputs, ensuring perturbations do not alter outputs on harmful prompts. Correspondingly, we introduce an activation regularization term to preserve safety-critical latent features identified by a sparse autoencoder trained on mixed benign and harmful data, minimizing deviations from baseline activations on held-out harmful prompts. This approach provides complementary protection against safety degradation that single-level methods cannot achieve. In summary, our contributions are as follows:

•

We theoretically demonstrate the insufficiency of single-level constraints by decomposing safety drift revealing that weight-only or activation-only constraints fail to block bypass pathways leading to safety degradation.

•

We propose CWAC, the first approach to couple weight subspace constraints with activation regularization, providing complementary protection against safety bypass mechanisms.

•

Extensive experiments validate CWAC across four LLMs, diverse downstream tasks, and varying harmful data ratios, demonstrating significant reductions in harmful scores with minimal impact on task performance compared to single-level constraints.

2
Related Work

Weight-Level.

Recent work has shown that LLMs are highly vulnerable to safety degradation during fine-tuning, and a number of defenses constrain parameter updates
\Delta W
to prevent alignment drift. Low-rank adaptation methods have been extensively explored: Safe LoRA
(
Hsu et al. 2024
)
projects LoRA updates into safety-aligned subspaces, SaLoRA
(
Li et al. 2025a
)
preserves alignment through orthogonal projections, and SC-LoRA
(
Luo et al. 2025
)
balances task performance with knowledge preservation via subspace constraints. Another line of work leverages safety basin geometry, where AsFT
(
Yang et al. 2025b
)
anchors updates within a narrow safety basin defined by alignment directions. Additionally, Lisa
(
Huang et al. 2024b
)
introduces proximal terms to constrain parameter drift during bi-state optimization. Post-fine-tuning interventions also operate on weights: Antidote
(
Huang et al. 2024a
)
prunes harmful parameters in a one-shot fashion after fine-tuning, SafeMERGE
(
Djuhera et al. 2025
)
selectively merges task-tuned and safety-tuned LoRA layers based on subspace similarity, and LoX
(
Perin et al. 2025
)
extrapolates parameters to flatter, more robust regions. Furthermore, Panacea
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
optimizes adaptive perturbations post-hoc to counteract harmful shifts while maintaining task performance. Despite their effectiveness in constraining weight drift, these methods assume that weight-level control is sufficient, leaving models vulnerable to safety degradation through unconstrained activation pathways
(
Zou et al. 2024
;
Ponkshe et al. 2025b
;
Kim et al. 2025
)
.

Activation-Level.

Methods at this level operate by manipulating internal representations to maintain or enhance safety. Inference-time interventions shift activations in targeted attention heads
(
Li et al. 2023
)
, learn refusal steering vectors under null-space constraints
(
Sheng et al. 2025
)
, or identify safety-sensitive layers for targeted steering
(
Cao et al. 2025
)
. Circuit Breakers
(
Zou et al. 2024
)
rewrite harmful representations into harmless directions at specific layers, while SCAR
(
Härle et al. 2024
)
employs sparse autoencoders to isolate and steer toxicity-related features. Evidence also indicates that activation degradation can emerge during fine-tuning: LDIFS
(
Mukhoti et al. 2023
)
shows that supervised fine-tuning distorts core latent features and weakens neuron selectivity, while NeuRel-Attack
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
demonstrates that adversarial fine-tuning can retrain safety-critical neurons to undermine refusal behavior. However, existing activation-level methods operate independently of weight constraints and are mostly applied at inference time or post-tuning, leaving gaps that allow weight drift to bypass activation-level control mechanisms during fine-tuning. Unlike prior work that addresses only a single-level intervention, our approach simultaneously constrains both weights and activations during fine-tuning to close these complementary bypass pathways.

3
Motivation

Existing safety preservation methods constrain either weight updates or internal activations, but rarely consider coupled constraints across both levels. In this section, we demonstrate that single-level constraints are insufficient to prevent safety drift during fine-tuning.

3.1
Safety Drift Decomposition

Consider a layer with weight matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}}
and input activation
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}
. The layer output is given by:

\mathbf{y}=f(W\mathbf{h})

(1)

where
f(\cdot)
denotes the elementwise activation function. Bias terms are omitted as they contribute only an additive constant and do not affect the drift structure.

During fine-tuning, both weights and activations change from their safe configurations. Let
W_{0}
and
\mathbf{h}_{0}
denote the initial safe values, and
W_{t}
and
\mathbf{h}_{t}
denote values after fine-tuning. The drift in layer output can be expressed as:

\Delta\mathbf{y}=f(W_{t}\mathbf{h}_{t})-f(W_{0}\mathbf{h}_{0})

(2)

Figure 2:
Illustration of CWAC’s coupled constraints: activation-level regularization (top) and weight-level safety subspace construction (bottom).

Applying a first-order Taylor approximation around the safe point
(W_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})
yields:

\Delta\mathbf{y}\approx f^{\prime}(W_{0}\mathbf{h}_{0})\odot(W_{0}\Delta\mathbf{h}+\Delta W\mathbf{h}_{0}+\Delta W\Delta\mathbf{h})

(3)

where
\Delta W=W_{t}-W_{0}
and
\Delta\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{h}_{t}-\mathbf{h}_{0}
represent the weight and activation changes, respectively, and
\odot
denotes elementwise multiplication (Hadamard product).

Since first-order Taylor expansion discards all second-order differentials by definition,
we omit the interaction term
\Delta W\Delta\mathbf{h}
and obtain the drift decomposition:

\Delta\mathbf{y}\approx f^{\prime}(W_{0}\mathbf{h}_{0})\odot(W_{0}\Delta\mathbf{h}+\Delta W\mathbf{h}_{0})

(4)

This decomposition reveals two independent sources of safety degradation: changes in activations (
\Delta\mathbf{h}
) and changes in weights (
\Delta W
).

3.2
Insufficiency of Weight-Only Constraints

Methods that constrain weight updates aim to minimize
\|\Delta W\|_{2}
through regularization or projection onto safe subspaces. Under such constraints, the drift simplifies to:

\Delta\mathbf{y}\approx f^{\prime}(W_{0}\mathbf{h}_{0})\odot W_{0}\Delta\mathbf{h}

(5)

Even with
\|\Delta W\|_{2}\approx 0
, if activations shift significantly (
\|\Delta\mathbf{h}\|_{2}\gg 0
), the preserved weights
W_{0}
will amplify this shift (via
\|\Delta\mathbf{y}\|_{2}\leq\|f^{\prime}(W_{0}\mathbf{h}_{0})\|_{\infty}\|W_{0}\|_{2}\|\Delta\mathbf{h}\|_{2}
). The model can thus produce harmful outputs by rerouting through alternative activation pathways, despite bounded weight changes.

3.3
Insufficiency of Activation-Only Constraints

Methods that constrain activations enforce
\|\Delta\mathbf{h}\|_{2}\approx 0
on reference data. Under such constraints, the drift simplifies to:

\Delta\mathbf{y}\approx f^{\prime}(W_{0}\mathbf{h}_{0})\odot\Delta W\mathbf{h}_{0}

(6)

However, weights remain free to drift. For unseen inputs with different activation patterns
\mathbf{h}^{\prime}
, the unconstrained weights can produce arbitrary drift:

\Delta\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\approx f^{\prime}(W_{0}\mathbf{h}^{\prime})\odot\Delta W\mathbf{h}^{\prime}

(7)

Since
\|\Delta\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\|f^{\prime}(W_{0}\mathbf{h}^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\|\Delta W\|_{2}\|\mathbf{h}^{\prime}\|_{2}
and
\|\Delta W\|_{2}
is unbounded, this leads to safety failures on out-of-distribution harmful queries.

3.4
The Necessity of Coupled Constraints

To bound the total drift, we apply the Holder’s inequality (for Hadamard product) and triangle inequality to Equation (
4
):

\begin{split}\|\Delta\mathbf{y}\|_{2}&\leq\|f^{\prime}(W_{0}\mathbf{h}_{0})\|_{\infty}\cdot\|W_{0}\|_{2}\|\Delta\mathbf{h}\|_{2}\\
&\quad+\|f^{\prime}(W_{0}\mathbf{h}_{0})\|_{\infty}\cdot\|\Delta W\|_{2}\|\mathbf{h}_{0}\|_{2}\end{split}

(8)

Minimizing this bound requires simultaneously constraining both
\|\Delta W\|_{2}
and
\|\Delta\mathbf{h}\|_{2}
. Neither constraint alone is sufficient.

4
Methodology

This section presents the proposed CWAC framework for preventing safety drift
during fine-tuning. As illustrated in Figure
2
, CWAC enforces
coupled constraints at two levels, where activation-level regularization preserves safety-critical latent features and weight-level constraints restrict parameter updates to a safety subspace.

4.1
Activation Safety Constraint

To address the activation-level drift, we introduce an activation constraint to preserve the model’s internal representations responsible for safety alignment.

Identifying safety-critical features.

We identify safety-critical latent features using a Sparse Autoencoder (SAE) trained on residual stream activations (see Appendix
A.1
for SAE preliminaries). Sparse autoencoders are employed because they decompose dense and entangled activation vectors into sparse and interpretable feature dimensions. This enables precise identification and selective preservation of only the features responsible for safety alignment, unlike prior methods that constrain entire activation vectors and thereby limit adaptation on downstream tasks. Following RouteSAE
(
Shi et al. 2025
)
, we employ a TopK SAE that decomposes activations
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from multiple layers into a sparse high-dimensional feature space
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
(where
M\gg d
):

\mathbf{z}=\text{TopK}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{enc}}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{b}_{\text{pre}})),\quad\hat{\mathbf{r}}=\mathbf{W}_{\text{dec}}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}_{\text{pre}}

(9)

where
\mathbf{W}_{\text{enc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
and
\mathbf{W}_{\text{dec}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}
are encoder and decoder matrices,
\mathbf{b}_{\text{pre}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bias term, and TopK selects the top
K
largest values to enforce sparsity. The SAE is trained to minimize reconstruction error:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}=\|\mathbf{r}-\hat{\mathbf{r}}\|_{2}^{2}

(10)

The training corpus consists of approximately 100 million tokens, with 30% from OpenWebText2
(
Gao et al. 2020
)
and 70% from harmful prompts where the base aligned model correctly refuses. This distribution ensures the SAE learns features
\mathbf{z}
that are strongly tied to refusal behavior and serve as proxies for the model’s internal safety mechanisms.

Activation Constraint.

Before fine-tuning, we compute baseline activations
\{\mathbf{z}_{b}^{\text{baseline}}\}
of these safety-critical latent features on a held-out set of harmful prompts using the base aligned model. During fine-tuning on
\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}
, for each training batch we sample a batch of harmful prompts , forward them through the current model and extract the corresponding safety-critical features. We add a regularization term that minimizes the mean squared error from the baseline:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{safety}}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\|\mathbf{z}_{b}^{\text{current}}-\mathbf{z}_{b}^{\text{baseline}}\|^{2}

(11)

where
B
is the batch size of harmful prompts,
\mathbf{z}_{b}^{\text{current}}
and
\mathbf{z}_{b}^{\text{baseline}}
denote the current and baseline safety-critical feature activations for harmful prompt
b
, respectively.

4.2
Weight Safety Subspace

In addition to the activation safety constraint, we construct a safety subspace in which weight updates can adapt to downstream tasks without disrupting the original safety alignment of the model on harmful prompts. We achieve this through a projection matrix that is pre-computed once on the base aligned model and applied throughout fine-tuning.

Subspace Construction.

Consider a layer
\ell
with FFN output projection weight
\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\text{ffn}}}
. When the base aligned model processes a set of harmful prompts
\mathcal{H}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{N}\}
and correctly refuses, we collect the inputs to this projection layer to form the matrix
\mathbf{X}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ffn}}\times N}
. Our goal is to constrain weight perturbations
\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\ell}
such that the model’s outputs on these safety-critical inputs remain unchanged:

(\mathbf{W}_{\ell}+\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\ell})\mathbf{X}_{\ell}=\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\mathbf{X}_{\ell}

(12)

which requires
\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\mathbf{X}_{\ell}=\mathbf{0}
.

Projection Matrix.

Directly identifying a suitable subspace for
\mathbf{X}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ffn}}\times N}
is computationally challenging due to the large number of columns
N
. In light of this, we use the covariance matrix
\mathbf{C}_{\ell}=\mathbf{X}_{\ell}\mathbf{X}_{\ell}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ffn}}\times d_{\text{ffn}}}
, which has much smaller dimensionality. This substitution is valid because
\mathbf{X}_{\ell}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\ell}
share the same left safety space (see Appendix
D.1
for detailed proof). To extract the desired safety subspace, we perform Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) on
\mathbf{C}_{\ell}
:

\{\mathbf{U}_{\ell},\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\ell},\mathbf{U}_{\ell}^{\top}\}=\text{SVD}(\mathbf{C}_{\ell})

(13)

where the columns of
\mathbf{U}_{\ell}
are the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{C}_{\ell}
. We retain only the eigenvectors corresponding to eigenvalues below a small threshold
\tau
and assemble them into the submatrix
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{\ell}
. The intuition is that small eigenvalues indicate directions where
\mathbf{X}_{\ell}
has minimal variance, meaning weight updates in these directions have negligible impact on the model’s outputs for harmful prompts. Based on this, the projection matrix is then defined as:

\mathbf{\Pi}_{\ell}=\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{\ell}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{\ell}^{\top}

(14)

This matrix satisfies
\mathbf{\Pi}_{\ell}\mathbf{C}_{\ell}\approx\mathbf{0}
and, consequently,
\mathbf{\Pi}_{\ell}\mathbf{X}_{\ell}\approx\mathbf{0}
. The detailed derivation is exhibited in Appendix
D.2
. Thus, weight updates projected into this safety subspace do not change the model’s outputs on harmful prompts:

(\mathbf{W}_{\ell}+\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\mathbf{\Pi}_{\ell})\mathbf{X}_{\ell}\approx\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\mathbf{X}_{\ell}

(15)

During fine-tuning, each weight update
\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\ell}
is projected via right-multiplication:
\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\leftarrow\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\mathbf{\Pi}_{\ell}
. This operation introduces negligible overhead while ensuring that the original safety alignment of the base model is preserved throughout fine-tuning.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we present CWAC, a novel approach that couples weight and activation constraints to robustly preserve safety alignment in large language models during downstream adaptation. We theoretically demonstrate that single-level constraints are insufficient due to the interplay between weight drift and activation drift, which enables bypass pathways for safety degradation. Extensive experiments across four widely used LLMs and diverse tasks show that CWAC consistently achieves the lowest harmful scores while maintaining or improving fine-tuning accuracy compared to strong baselines such as ASFT and SPPFT. Even under challenging conditions including high harmful data ratios and varying learning rates, CWAC exhibits superior robustness and stability, confirming the effectiveness of jointly addressing both drift mechanisms for reliable safety preservation.

7
Limitations

We discuss three main limitations of our work.
First, CWAC requires white-box access to the model. The method relies on direct manipulation of weights through gradient projection and internal activations via sparse autoencoder features. This design makes it applicable only to open-source models where full parameter and activation access is available. Extending CWAC to black-box proprietary models remains challenging, as these constraints cannot be applied without internal access. Second, the activation constraint depends on the quality of the trained sparse autoencoder. Identifying accurate safety-critical features requires a well-trained SAE with high reconstruction fidelity and meaningful sparse representations. Training such SAEs demands considerable computational resources and careful hyperparameter selection. Variations in SAE quality across layers or architectures may affect the precision and effectiveness of the activation regularization. Third, our evaluation focuses on instruction-tuned models in the 7B to 9B parameter range and primarily assesses refusal robustness against explicit harmful prompts. The performance of CWAC on substantially larger models or under different initial alignment techniques has not been explored. Additionally, resistance against more advanced jailbreak methods or subtle indirect safety violations requires further investigation in future work.

8
Ethics Statement

Our experiments involve evaluating model responses to harmful prompts sourced from public safety benchmarks, including JailbreakBench, HarmBench, AdvBench, and BeaverTails. These benchmarks contain prompts that intentionally probe for unsafe behaviors, such as requests for illegal activities, misinformation, or dangerous instructions. Although these prompts are standard in safety research and are used solely to measure refusal capabilities, they may include offensive or disturbing content.
We advise caution when reviewing examples from these datasets or qualitative responses in this work, particularly in Table
6
. Readers should be aware that some prompts and baseline model outputs (e.g., from standard SFT) contain explicit harmful or unethical content.
All models evaluated are publicly available open-source large language models accessed via the Hugging Face Hub under their respective licenses, which permit research use. No proprietary models or private data are involved. Our method is designed to enhance safety alignment and reduce the generation of harmful outputs, contributing positively to the responsible development of large language models.

Appendix A
Preliminaries

A.1
Sparse Autoencoders for Interpretability

Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) decompose neural network activations into interpretable features. Given a hidden state
\mathbf{r}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
at layer
l
, an SAE learns an overcomplete representation
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
where
M\gg d
:

\mathbf{z}=\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{enc}}\mathbf{r}^{l}+\mathbf{b}_{\text{enc}}),\quad\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{l}=\mathbf{W}_{\text{dec}}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}_{\text{dec}}

(17)

where
\mathbf{W}_{\text{enc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
and
\mathbf{W}_{\text{dec}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}
are encoder and decoder matrices. The SAE is trained to minimize reconstruction error while enforcing sparsity:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SAE}}=\|\mathbf{r}^{l}-\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{l}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{z}\|_{1}

(18)

Each feature
z_{i}
activates for specific semantic concepts, enabling identification of safety-critical features such as refusal patterns in aligned LLMs.

A.2
Safety Preservation during Fine-tuning

Given a safety-aligned model
f_{\theta}
with parameters
\theta
and a task dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}
, supervised fine-tuning updates parameters to minimize task loss:

\theta^{*}=\arg\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}}[-\log p_{\theta}(y|x)]

(19)

However, this optimization can shift model representations away from safe regions learned during alignment. To preserve safety while achieving task performance, the model should maintain robust refusal behavior on harmful prompts
\mathcal{H}
. This can be formulated as a constrained optimization:

\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\theta),\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\text{ASR}(\theta;\mathcal{H})\leq\epsilon

(20)

where
\text{ASR}(\theta;\mathcal{H})
measures the attack success rate on harmful queries and
\epsilon
is a safety threshold.

B.3
Choice of Output Projection Layer

CWAC applies the weight safety subspace constraint specifically to the FFN output projection layer (down_proj), which maps the FFN hidden dimension back to the residual stream. This design follows a minimal intervention principle. The down_proj layer is the unique linear bottleneck through which all FFN computations write back to the residual stream, making it the most direct point at which to enforce the invariance condition
\Delta W_{\ell}X_{\ell}=0
in Eq.
12
without interfering with intermediate nonlinear transformations in the gate and up projections. Furthermore, the FFN hidden dimension
d_{\text{ffn}}
is typically larger than the residual dimension
d
, providing a high-dimensional space for SVD decomposition and enabling fine grained geometric separation of task relevant and safety critical directions. While the attention output projection also writes to the residual stream, it primarily aggregates cross token contextual information, whereas the FFN performs token wise nonlinear feature recombination whose final contribution to the residual stream is more directly linked to content generation. Constraining down_proj therefore achieves precise, output level control over FFN contributions while leaving internal FFN computations largely unconstrained, preserving the model’s capacity for downstream task adaptation.

B.4
Training Details and Hyperparameters

In the default setting, experiments use full fine-tuning with the AdamW optimizer on aligned large language models, without LoRA or other parameter-efficient techniques. We train for 3 epochs with a batch size of 1, maximum sequence length of 512, and learning rate of
2\times 10^{-5}
, on a single NVIDIA A800 GPU. Our approach incorporates two constraints during fine-tuning on benign tasks: (1) a weight-space projection onto a pre-computed weight safety subspace targeting intermediate layers, and (2) an activation constraint that preserves safety-related sparse autoencoder (SAE) features via top-
k
gating (
k
ranging from 64 to 256 across experiments). We preserve the top 100 safety-critical latents identified from harmful prompts, with the activation preservation loss
\lambda=0.5
. By default, fine-tuning uses purely benign task-specific data (
n=5,000
examples), simulating standard supervised fine-tuning with only benign supervision. To assess robustness under more challenging conditions, we also conduct experiments with training mixtures containing a proportion
p
of harmful prompt-compliant response pairs (and
1-p
benign samples). These harmful prompts (corresponding to the top 100 safety-critical latents) are used solely to compute baseline activations and the preservation loss, without contributing to the main training objective.

B.5
Computational Cost

CWAC introduces three sources of computational cost including SAE training, SVD-based safety subspace construction, and fine-tuning overhead. SAE training is performed offline once, taking approximately 6.5 hours on a single NVIDIA A800 GPU. SVD precomputation is performed once per fixed backbone model and takes approximately 18 minutes on Llama-2-7B. As both are one-time offline operations, their overhead becomes negligible when amortized over multiple fine-tuning runs. During fine-tuning on Llama-2-7B using benign task-specific data, CWAC requires approximately 44–46 minutes per epoch compared to 42 minutes for standard SFT, corresponding to less than 10% overhead, with GPU memory increasing slightly from 44.2 GB to 46 GB. To further reduce complexity, we perform SVD on the covariance matrix
\mathbf{C}_{\ell}
rather than the full activation matrix
\mathbf{X}_{\ell}
, significantly lowering decomposition cost without affecting the resulting safety subspace.

C.3
Qualitative Examples

To provide deeper insight into the safety benefits of our proposed CWAC method, we present several representative examples of model responses to highly harmful prompts in Table

Importantly, CWAC maintains this robust safety boundary without resorting to overly verbose or evasive responses, demonstrating that strong alignment can coexist with clear and natural communication.
These qualitative illustrations complement our quantitative results by showing that CWAC not only reduces harmful scores in aggregate evaluations but also reliably preserves and strengthens refusal capabilities in real-world adversarial scenarios. By jointly constraining weights and activations, CWAC ensures that safety-critical representations remain intact during task-specific adaptation, enabling the model to perform downstream tasks effectively while refusing to generate dangerous or misleading outputs.

Appendix D
Proof Details

D.1
Proof for the Equivalent Safety Subspace

Theorem:
Let
\mathbf{X}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ffn}}\times N}
be a matrix. Then
\mathbf{X}_{\ell}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\ell}=\mathbf{X}_{\ell}\mathbf{X}_{\ell}^{\top}
have the same safety subspace, where the safety subspace is defined as the set of all vectors
\mathbf{v}
such that
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\ell}=\mathbf{0}
.

Proof:
We need to show that if
\mathbf{v}
satisfies
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\ell}=\mathbf{0}
, then
\mathbf{v}
also satisfies
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{\ell}=\mathbf{0}
, and vice versa.

Direction 1: If
