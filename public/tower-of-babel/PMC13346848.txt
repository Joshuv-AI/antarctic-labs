Influence of endocrown-retained fixed dental prosthesis design on marginal integrity and fracture resistance
Authors: Rasha Sayed Asaad, Marwa Beleidy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSETo investigate the effect of zirconia endocrown-retained fixed dental prosthesis (FDP) designs on marginal integrity and fracture resistance after thermomechanical aging, compared with full-coverage-retained FDPs.MATERIALS AND METHODSThree extracted mandibular second premolars and second molars were randomly assigned to three abutment designs: full-coverage retainers (A), butt-joint endocrowns (B), and endocrowns with shoulder finish line and ferrule-like extensions (C). All teeth were prepared as abutments for three-unit zirconia FDPs replacing a missing mandibular first molar, and each FDP was duplicated into 12 epoxy resin models. High translucent zirconia FDPs were fabricated using Computer-Aided Design-Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAD-CAM) and cemented using self-adhesive resin cement. Marginal gaps were measured under a stereomicroscope (× 35) before and after thermomechanical aging (1.2 × 105 cycles, 49 N, 5 – 55℃). Fracture resistance was assessed after aging under compressive loading until failure, and failure mode was recorded. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with post hoc tests (α = 0.05).RESULTSAll groups showed a significant increase in marginal gap after aging (P < .05). Group B had the smallest mean marginal gap before and after aging (P < .001). Group A exhibited the highest fracture resistance, significantly exceeding Groups B and C, with Group B showing the lowest values (P < .001). Failure mode analysis indicated that fractures were confined to the connector regions, mainly the distal connectors, with occlusal extension observed in Groups B and C.CONCLUSIONAbutment design significantly affected the zirconia endocrown-retained FDPs performance. Marginal gaps increased after thermomechanical aging, but remained within clinically acceptable limits. The butt-joint design demonstrated superior marginal adaptation, while the full-coverage design exhibited the highest fracture resistance, exceeding physiologic posterior masticatory forces.

INTRODUCTION

Managing the restoration of endodontically treated teeth presents ongoing clinical and biomechanical challenges due to the substantial loss of coronal structure, the altered biomechanical properties of the remaining dentin, and the consequent increase in fracture susceptibility.1 Conventional restorative strategies often employ full-coverage crowns supported by posts and cores; however, these approaches necessitate extensive removal of sound dental tissue, potentially compromising the structural integrity of the root and increasing the risk of mechanical failure.2

In response to the demand for more conservative and biomimetic restorative modalities, endocrown restorations have been introduced as a viable alternative.3 Endocrowns are monolithic restorations that achieve macromechanical retention from the pulp chamber while relying on adhesive cementation for micromechanical bonding, thereby eliminating the need for intraradicular posts and preserving the remaining tooth structure. The preparation design is minimally invasive and guided by the extent of existing caries or structural damage, in accordance with contemporary principles of adhesive dentistry and tissue conservation.4

Endocrowns are particularly indicated for posterior teeth, where the pulp chamber volume provides sufficient retention and stability. Their increasing clinical acceptance can be attributed to their ability to achieve favorable esthetic outcomes, enhance biomechanical performance, and reduce operative time while maintaining maximal preservation of the natural tooth structure.5 Nonetheless, the biomechanical behavior of endocrowns, particularly when employed as abutments in fixed dental prostheses (FDPs) replacing missing posterior teeth, remains a subject of ongoing investigation.67 The success of these restorations, grounded in the principle of structural conservation, has also prompted the development of novel strategies for employing endodontically treated teeth as abutments in adhesive prosthodontic rehabilitation.8

Monolithic zirconia represents an emerging material of interest for the endocrown fabrication owing to its superior mechanical strength, wear behavior, biocompatibility, and satisfactory esthetic outcomes. Nevertheless, although zirconia is widely employed in fixed prosthodontic restorations, evidence regarding its clinical behavior and marginal integrity in multi-unit endocrown-retained FDPs remains limited.579

Given these considerations, this research was conducted to examine the marginal integrity and fracture resistance of two different monolithic zirconia endocrown-retained FDP designs, butt-joint and shoulder finish line with ferrule-like extension, in comparison to a conventional full-coverage FDP, under simulated oral conditions. The first null hypothesis assumed that the marginal integrity would not differ among the evaluated FDP designs. The second hypothesis proposed that full-coverage retained FDPs and shoulder finish line with ferrule-like extension designed endocrown-retained FDPs would have higher fracture resistance than butt-joint designed endocrown-retained FDPs, but without a statistically significant difference.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical Approval

Approval from the Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Dentistry, October 6 University, (No. 17/2024) was secured before study commencement.

Sample size calculation

According to the findings reported by Hussein MM and Mohsen CA, the estimated effect size for the marginal gap was substantial (Cohen’s f = 2.20), indicating that six specimens per group would achieve 80% power at α = 0.05. For fracture resistance, the effect size was even larger (Cohen’s f = 8.80), suggesting that fewer than 10 specimens per group would provide > 99% power.9 Twelve specimens per group were included to compensate for possible losses during accelerated artificial aging and to preserve statistical validity. The sample size was computed using software for sample size calculation (G*Power 3.1.9.7 for Windows, HHUD, Germany).

Teeth Selection and Preparation

Three mandibular second premolars and mandibular second molars, recently extracted for periodontal or orthodontic reasons, were collected from patients attending the Faculty of Dentistry Hospital, October 6 University, after obtaining informed consent following the Declaration of Helsinki principles. The selected teeth were free from caries, restorations, and visible cracks, as confirmed under light magnification (Stemi DV4, 8 ×; Carl Zeiss MicroImaging, Jena, Germany) and by radiographic examination to exclude cracks or internal root resorption. The buccolingual and mesiodistal dimensions of the mandibular second premolars (approximately 8.2 ± 0.5 mm and 7.1 ± 0.5 mm, respectively) and second molars (approximately 10.8 ± 0.5 mm and 10.1 ± 0.5 mm, respectively) were selected to be comparable across all specimens and verified using a digital caliper (150 mm Digital Caliper, Mitutoyo Corporation, Kanagawa, Japan). Plaque and calculus were eliminated via ultrasonic scaling (UDS-A LED; Woodpecker, Guilin, China), and the teeth were disinfected in 0.5% sodium hypochlorite solution for 30 min. Following disinfection, the specimens were maintained in deionized water at room temperature until testing commenced.10

Conventional root canal treatment was performed for all teeth. Canal instrumentation was carried out using the crown-down technique with a rotary NiTi filing system (ProTaper SX-F3; Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) by the same operator to ensure procedural consistency. Following instrumentation, obturation was completed using thermoplasticized gutta-percha (Calamus Dual, Dentsply Maillefer, Woodinville, WA, USA) in combination with a resin-based sealer (AH 26, Dentsply Maillefer, Tulsa, OK, USA) as specified by the manufacturer.

All endodontically treated teeth were decoronated parallel to the occlusal plane, leaving 2 mm of coronal structure above the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) using a diamond disc in conjunction with abundant water irrigation to prevent overheating. Each premolar and molar was then vertically embedded in epoxy resin within metal molds (3 × 1.5 cm) (Kemapoxy 150 3D; CMB, Giza, Egypt) using a parallometer (BEGO, Paraflex, Bremen, Germany) to establish a uniform common path of insertion and long-axis orientation. The teeth were positioned 11 mm apart, corresponding to the approximate mesiodistal width of a mandibular first molar.11 The resin level was adjusted to be 2 mm apical to the CEJ to simulate bone level.12 The three prepared tooth blocks were randomly divided into three groups according to the FDP standardized preparation per design: Group A: full-coverage retainers, Group B: butt-joint endocrown retainers, and Group C: shoulder finish line with ferrule-like extension endocrown retainers.

For a full-coverage designed group (Fig. 1A), both abutments were prepared with a 1.5 mm axial wall thickness, 6° convergence, 1.5 mm occlusal reduction, and a 1 mm thickness shoulder finish line.13 For both endocrown-designed groups (Fig. 1B, C), the occlusal surface was prepared to achieve a 2 mm axial wall height with a 2 mm cervical sidewalk. The internal walls of the cavity were adjusted to eliminate any undercuts within the pulp chamber, providing an internal coronal divergence of approximately 7°.14 A thin (~0.5 mm thickness) layer of flowable composite resin was applied to the cavity floor to smooth the surface and eliminate residual irregularities without altering the planned cavity depth. The thickness was controlled using a calibrated periodontal probe and leveled with a flat-ended instrument before light curing. Intracoronal retention was achieved by extending the axial walls 4 mm into the pulp chamber, with the cavity base positioned 2 mm below the CEJ.15 For the shoulder finish line with the ferrule-like extension group, an additional 1 mm ferrule was established on the coronal surface below the endocrown occlusal table, with a shoulder finish line that is 1 mm wide. Internal line angles were uniformly rounded and smoothed.

Preparation parameters were standardized under magnification using dental loupes (AIR-X, 4.5 ×, Univet Loupes Spa, Rezzato, Italy). Depth-cutting burs were used to control reduction, and dimensions were verified with a digital caliper (± 0.01 mm; 150 mm Digital Caliper, Mitutoyo, Kanagawa, Japan). The external and internal axial walls of the prepared abutments were aligned parallel to the predetermined path of insertion to ensure consistent convergence and preparation geometry. Convergence angles were further confirmed through digital analysis (Identica Hybrid T500, MEDIT, Seoul, Korea), and specimens exhibiting deviations exceeding ± 0.1 mm in dimensions or ± 1° in angulation were corrected or excluded.

Model Duplications

After standardized preparation of the three FDPs on extracted natural abutment teeth (Fig. 1), twelve identical high-precision polyvinylsiloxane impressions (President Coltène, Coltène/Whaledent GmbH, Langenau, Germany) were taken for each prepared FDP abutments using a custom-made impression tray. These impressions were then used as molds to fabricate 12 duplicated models for each group using an epoxy resin (Kemapoxy 150 3D; CMB, Giza, Egypt).16 Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the epoxy resin was mixed and poured carefully into the silicone impressions while applying vibration to minimize air entrapment. After complete polymerization, the epoxy models were removed, inspected for defects, and stored at room temperature for a week to ensure uniformity of specimen dimensions and internal geometry across all test groups.

FDP’s Construction

All resin models were scanned using a 3D dental scanner (AutoScan-DS-EX Pro; SHINING 3D, Hangzhou, China). For standardization, full coverage and different endocrown configurations were designed using computer-aided design (CAD) software (exocad Dental CAD v3.0; exocad GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany). All retainers were designed with the same occlusal anatomy, a minimum thickness of 1.5 mm at the center of the occlusal surface for the full-coverage design, and the endocrown-designed retainer had 7 mm in the same area. Connector dimensions were set to be 4 × 4 mm for Group A, 2.25 × 4 mm for Group B, and 3.5 × 4 mm for Group C with 0.5 mm connector notch radius.1718 The cement thickness was set at 50 µm.1920 Monolithic high translucent zirconia blanks (Shade HT13; KATANA Zirconia HT, Kuraray Europe GmbH, Hattersheim, Germany) were milled (5X-300 Pro; ARUM Dentistry, Daejeon, Korea) and subsequently sintered in a furnace (LHT 02/17 LB Speed; Nabertherm GmbH, Lilienthal, Germany) for 7 h in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Each FDP was examined for its fit on the corresponding resin model after milling. Then, all FDPs were polished (Fig. 1) using silicone points containing diamond particles (Celtra Tw’s Tec Polishing kit, Dentsply, Konstanz, Germany) (coarse, regular, and fine, respectively) and a diamond-containing polishing paste (Pearl Surface Z; Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc., Tokyo, Japan) to achieve optimal luster as per the manufacturer’s guidelines, applied at low speed and minimum pressure.

FDPs Cementation

The internal surfaces of each zirconia FDP retainers were air-abraded using 110 µm Al2O3 particles at 2.8 bar for 10 s, maintaining a vertical distance of 10 mm.13 Then, they were put in an ultrasonic bath (L&R Transistor Ultrasonic T14, L&R, Kearny, NJ, USA) for 5 min. Afterward, a thin layer of Z-Prime Plus (Z-Prime Plus, BISCO Inc., Schaumburg, IL, USA) containing 10-methacryloyloxydecyl dihydrogen phosphate (10-MDP) was applied, followed by a 30-second wait and air-drying for 3 – 5 s. Each FDP was subsequently cemented to its respective resin model with a self-adhesive resin cement (Breeze, Pentron, Orange, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s specifications, followed by a 10 kg static loading application which was verified using a digital weighing scale prior to each cementation session. The load was directed along the long axis of the FDP using a custom-fabricated metal loading jig to achieve uniform force distribution and standardized seating. This load was maintained for 10 min to allow complete restoration seating and initial polymerization of the resin cement, exceeding the manufacturer-reported initial setting time to minimize restoration displacement and enhance standardization across specimens.13 Excess cement was removed, and each surface was light-cured for 20 s.

Marginal gap distance assessment before aging

Each specimen was imaged using a stereomicroscope with an integrated three-megapixel camera (Nikon Eclipse E600, Tokyo, Japan) positioned vertically at 2.5 cm from the specimen at 90° to the light source. Images were captured at 35 × magnification with a resolution of 2272 × 1704 pixels (minimum 1280 × 1024 pixels) and transferred to a computer. The marginal gap was quantified and analyzed using a computer-assisted image analysis system (ImageJ 1.43U, National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). All parameters were initially expressed in pixels, and then calibrated to real-world units using a ruler of known dimensions.

Each specimen was photographed by capturing shots of its margins (Fig. 2). For each image, morphometric measurements were conducted using four equally spaced reference points along the cervical circumference of each surface (buccal, lingual, and mesial) for premolar abutment and (buccal, lingual, and distal) for molar abutment, as both the distal of the premolar and the mesial of the molar were inaccessible, with a total of 24 readings for each FDP. The measurement at every point was conducted three times.

Thermomechanical aging

A dual-axis chewing simulator (four-station multimodal ROBOTA chewing simulator; ROBOTA Co., Alexandria, Egypt) performed the artificial aging on the FDPs. A servomotor (model ACH-09075DC-T; AdTech Technology Co., Shenzhen, China) was equipped with an in-built thermo-cyclic protocol to run this simulator. All specimens were mounted and underwent sequential loading under a 5 kg weight and a 49 N chewing force for 120,000 cycles, simulating a 6-month clinical service period under controlled wear testing.1321 Simultaneously, thermocycling was performed ranging from 5℃ to 55℃ to emulate intraoral temperature fluctuations. The specimens were exposed to each temperature bath for a dwell time of 30 s, with a transfer time of 10 s between baths. The specimens were then cleaned ultrasonically in distilled water for 3 min.

Marginal gap distance assessment after aging

As mentioned, the specimens were assessed for marginal gap distance (Fig. 2).

Fracture Resistance and Mode of Failure

All specimens were evaluated using a computer-controlled materials testing machine, fitted with a 5 kN load cell (Model 3345; Instron, Norwood, MA, USA) to accurately record applied forces. The specimens were mounted on the testing machine’s lower fixed compartment. A compressive load mode was applied vertically along the long axis of the FDP through a rounded tip metallic rod (8.6 mm diameter) fixed to the upper movable part of the testing machine. A sheet of tin foil was positioned between the rod and specimen to guarantee equally distributed stress and lessen the transmission of local force peaks. The rod was moved at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min, centered on the pontic occlusal surface and contacting both cuspal inclines to simulate functional occlusal loading. Failure was identified by an audible crack, visible fracture, and an abrupt decrease in the load-deflection curve. Failure load was measured in Newtons (N), as all data were collected by computer software (Bluehill Universal; Instron, Norwood, MA, USA).

The fractured specimens and fragments were collected, and the failure mode was examined visually and under a stereomicroscope (U500x Digital Microscope, Guangdong, China) at ×35 magnification (Figs. 3, 4, and 5). Images were transferred to a computer with Image Tool software (ImageJ 1.43U, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) for analysis. The failure mode patterns were classified according to the fracture origin and propagation pathway as follows: (1) fractures confined to the connector area, (2) fractures initiating at the connector and extending occlusally, and (3) catastrophic fractures involving complete separation of the restoration.

Statistical Analysis

Mean and standard deviation values were calculated for each group in all tests. Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests, confirming a parametric distribution. Comparisons among more than two independent groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, while paired sample t-tests were used for related specimens. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was applied to assess relationships across the variables. Statistical significance was set at P ≤ .05. All analyses were conducted using a statistical software (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows: Version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Impact of Experimental Variables on Marginal Gap Measurements

According to the three-way ANOVA results (Table 1), tooth type did not significantly influence the outcome variable (P = .859). However, thermomechanical aging and preparation design had significant effects (P < .001). The interaction among tooth type, aging, and preparation design was statistically non-significant (P = .220), denoting that the effects of aging and group were independent of tooth type.

Marginal gap before thermomechanical aging

For the premolar measurements, significant differences were detected among Groups A, B, and C (P < .001). Pairwise comparisons revealed that Group A differed significantly from both Group B (P < .001) and Group C (P = .001), whereas no significant difference was detected between Groups B and C (P = .056). In the molar measurements, a statistically significant difference was also found among the three groups (P = .001), with Group B differing significantly from Groups A (P = .004) and C (P = .004), while Groups A and C showed no significant difference (P = .998). When considering the entire FDP specimen, significant differences were observed among Groups A, B, and C (P < .001) as observed in Table 2.

Marginal gap after thermomechanical aging

For the premolar measurements, significant differences were detected across Groups A, B, and C (P = .005). Pairwise comparisons showed that Group A differed significantly from both Group B (P = .005) and Group C (P = .038), whereas no significant variation was found between Groups B and C (P = .646). Conversely, no significant variations were detected among the three groups for the molar measurements (P = .363) or for the entire FDP specimen (P = .082) as noted in Table 2.

Effect of thermomechanical aging on the marginal gap

All groups showed significant differences in marginal gap measurements before and after aging: Group A (P = .006), Group B (P < .001), and Group C (P < .001) as noted in Table 2.

Fracture resistance

A statistically significant variation was noted among tested groups (P < .001). Pairwise comparisons indicated significant variations between Group A and Groups B and C (P < .001), and that a significant difference also existed between Groups B and C (P = .001), as shown in Table 3.

Mode of failure

As shown in Table 4 and Figs. 3, 4, 5, most fractures occurred at the distal connector (between teeth #6 and #7), originating at the occlusal portion of the connector, with several specimens showing propagation toward the pontic, particularly in Groups A and C. Only one specimen in Group C exhibited a mesial connector fracture (between teeth #5 and #6).

Correlation between the marginal gap and fracture resistance

Pearson correlation analysis showed no statistically significant relationship between the variables in any group. Group A revealed a marked negative correlation (r = -0.779, P = .068), Group B showed a weak positive correlation (r = 0.328, P = .525), and Group C had a weak negative correlation (r = -0.263, P = .614). When considering all groups collectively, a very weak positive correlation was noted between marginal gap and fracture resistance (r = 0.178, P = .480), indicating no significant correlation.

DISCUSSION

The adoption of different preparation designs in the current research was guided by the need to balance preservation of sound tooth structure with the mechanical performance of endocrown-retained FDPs. The full-coverage design represents the traditional approach, providing substantial support and reinforcement, though at the cost of considerable tooth reduction. In contrast, the butt-joint endocrown design offers a more conservative preparation, preserving coronal tooth structure, but potentially compromising retention and marginal adaptation. The shoulder finish line with a ferrule-like extension serves as an intermediate strategy, aiming to improve marginal sealing and optimize stress distribution while minimizing unnecessary tissue removal. Comparing these designs provides valuable insight into how conservative preparation approaches may achieve clinical outcomes comparable to traditional full-coverage restorations.

Abutment preparations were carried out on extracted mandibular second premolars and second molars to produce standardized cavity designs for endocrown restorations, preserving key anatomical features such as pulp chamber depth, internal geometry, and occlusal morphology. High-precision epoxy resin dies were then fabricated from silicone impressions of these preparations, combining the anatomical fidelity of natural teeth with the mechanical consistency of synthetic materials, ensuring uniform dimensions across all test specimens. Epoxy resin provides reproducible mechanical properties suitable for in vitro testing, minimizing variability from natural tooth heterogeneity and enabling more reliable comparisons of restorative materials and designs in the assessment of marginal gaps and fracture resistance.16

The connector dimensions evaluated (4 × 4 mm for group A, 2.25 × 4 mm for group B, and 3.5 × 4 mm for group C) were selected based on the recommended minimum for zirconia connector cross-sectional area and the manufacturer recommendation (≥ 9 mm2).1718

Monolithic zirconia restorations demonstrate higher strength compared with bi-layered restorations, allowing for the preservation of sound tooth structure during preparation.22 The mechanical properties of zirconia make it an appropriate material for load-bearing restorations, particularly in long-span FDPs and for individuals prone to elevated occlusal forces such as those with bruxism.2324 KATANA HT zirconia, such as 3 mol% yttria-stabilized tetragonal zirconia polycrystal (3Y-TZP), consist predominantly of the tetragonal phase (≈85 – 90%), with a smaller proportion of cubic phase (≈10 – 15%) and minimal monoclinic content (< 1%) after sintering. The tetragonal phase provides transformation toughening and high strength required for FDPs subjected to posterior occlusal loads, while the cubic phase improves translucency. The very low monoclinic content ensures minimal phase-related microcracking, contributing to the material’s dimensional stability and long-term clinical performance. At the same time, microstructural modifications enhance translucency compared with conventional 3Y-TZP, allowing acceptable esthetic outcomes without the strength reduction associated with higher yttria (4Y-5Y) zirconia.25 However, information is scarce regarding their application in conservative treatment concepts.23

The large effect sizes observed in Hussein MM and Mohsen CA’ study were likely due to the highly standardized in vitro conditions and the use of epoxy resin models, which minimized biological variability and allowed isolation of preparation design effects.9 However, these results might not fully translate to clinical situations, where patient- and tooth-specific factors introduce additional variability.

Long-term success of restorations relies on optimal marginal adaptation, which minimizes the risk of failure due to poor fit. Inadequate adaptation can lead to microleakage, secondary caries, periodontitis, cement dissolution, plaque accumulation, and ultimately restoration failure.26 Factors affecting marginal fit include preparation design, dimensional parameters, and cement type.27 While horizontal discrepancies, such as over-contoured margins, can often be corrected clinically, vertical marginal gaps are more detrimental and challenging to manage. As noted by Holmes et al., vertical discrepancies may result in gradual cement dissolution and subsequent biological or mechanical complications. Consequently, this study focused exclusively on measuring vertical marginal gaps.

Several in vitro methods have been used to evaluate marginal adaptation, including direct inspection, cross-sectioning, and the silicone replica technique. However, each approach presents inherent limitations.212829 In this study, direct stereomicroscopic observation was chosen. It allows repeated, non-destructive measurements of marginal gaps, preserving the specimens for subsequent testing and enabling longitudinal comparisons before and after thermomechanical aging. Furthermore, by measuring multiple standardized points around each restoration and using digital calibration to minimize observer bias, this approach captures variations in marginal adaptation that closely reflect clinical conditions, consistent with previously published in vitro studies.1929

The first research hypothesis was rejected, as the shoulder finish line with a ferrule-like extension showed a significantly higher vertical marginal gap. This may be due to the complex geometry and multiple internal angles of the endocrown preparation, which can limit the precision of CAD-CAM milling because of bur size and tool path constraints.29 The intricate finish line and irregular surface topography may further contribute to the increased discrepancy. Since the marginal fit of CAD-CAM restorations depends heavily on the accuracy of digital scanning and milling precision, these results align with previous studies showing that shoulder margins often exhibit poorer adaptation and higher microleakage compared to butt-joint margins or post-and-core crowns.3031 However, the influence of a ferrule on marginal integrity remains inconclusive.31

In contrast, the butt-joint margin design demonstrated the lowest vertical marginal gap values. Its simpler geometry and uniform margin thickness allow for better seating of the restoration and a more consistent marginal seal. Additionally, the increased ceramic thickness at the butt-joint interface may enhance adhesive bonding and minimize marginal discrepancies.32 These findings indicate that margin preparation influences marginal integrity, although some previous studies have reported that margin design does not significantly affect the vertical gap of endocrown restorations.31

The full-coverage FDP group showed significantly better marginal fit than the shoulder-ferrule with a ferrule-like extension design but lower fit than the butt-joint endocrown design. The results may be attributed to inadequate retainers seating. During cementation of the full-coverage retainer, the axial wall engages the preparation, narrowing the cement escape pathway and increasing hydrostatic pressure within the retainer. This aligns with research indicating that partial crown seating results from the thickened resin cement layer, resulting in accumulation on the occlusal surface of the prepared tooth.16

Regarding the thermomechanical aging effect, an enlargement of the vertical marginal gap was noted. Frequent masticatory forces, along with thermal expansion incompatibility between the restoration and cement, can result in significant stress on the adhesive interface, leading to deterioration of marginal integrity.33 The findings of this study corroborate those reported in earlier investigations., which recorded an adverse effect of mechanical and thermocycling on the marginal fit of cemented crowns.3435 The results indicate that the initial statistical differences in marginal gaps observed among the tested groups for molar and whole FDP specimens disappeared following thermomechanical aging. This change may be attributed to cement fatigue and thermal degradation, mechanical settling during cyclic loading, and stress redistribution over time, with aging acting as an equalizing factor across the different preparation designs. These findings suggest that the initial advantages of specific designs may diminish under simulated clinical conditions, highlighting the importance of long-term evaluation when assessing marginal adaptation. However, the marginal gap of all groups before and after aging lies within the clinically accepted values of 120 µm.16

A key consideration in restorative design is that average occlusal forces approximate 250 N in the anterior segment and 350 N in the posterior segment but can increase to 720 – 900 N in patients with bruxism.19 Consequently, restorations must possess sufficient strength to resist both physiologic and parafunctional masticatory loads.

In the current study, restoration design significantly influenced fracture resistance, partially supporting the second hypothesis. The full-coverage design exhibited the highest fracture strength (1403.33 ± 223.97 N) among the tested groups. This superior performance can be attributed to circumferential coverage and increased bonding surface area, which enhance structural integrity, distribute occlusal forces more evenly along the axial walls, and reduce stress concentration, thereby delaying crack propagation.36 Larger connector cross-section and smooth embrasure curvature might promote uniform stress distribution and minimize tensile stress accumulation. Finite-element analysis further supports this observation, demonstrating lower stress concentrations in extra-coronal full-coverage designs compared with intracoronal anchorage systems.5 The fracture resistance results observed in the present investigation are consistent with previous reports.3738 However, full-contour zirconia restorations have been reported to withstand fracture loads ranging from 1607 to 3499.9 N.3940 Variations among studies may result from differences in preparation design, restorative configuration, fabrication techniques, aging protocols (including the number of thermocycling and cyclic loading cycles), and zirconia type or crystallinity.

In contrast, the butt-joint endocrown-retained FDPs had reduced connector height and sharper gingival embrasures due to the intracoronal configuration, producing localized tensile stress concentrations and earlier crack initiation. This confirms that connector behavior is strongly influenced by the marginal configuration of the abutment.

The shoulder finish line with a ferrule-like extension endocrown design (803.8 ± 178.3 N) exhibited lower fracture resistance than the full-coverage design but higher than the butt-joint endocrown. It was designed with a 90° butt joint to provide a flat, stable surface that enhances resistance to compressive forces. Adding a short axial wall or ferrule enhances the bonding surface and provides additional bracing, although the removal of residual enamel may slightly compromise fracture resistance. This design facilitates more even distribution of occlusal forces, reduces the resin cement layer thickness, minimizes polymerization shrinkage and internal stress, lowers the risk of catastrophic failure, and improves overall restoration durability.14 The added axial wall support and rounded embrasure transition might improve load transfer from the pontic to the retainers and delayed crack initiation. These results suggest that a shoulder finish line with a ferrule-like extension is a reliable option for short-span posterior fixed dental prostheses. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing higher fracture resistance for shoulder finish line designs compared to butt-joint configurations, despite similar failure modes.31 Einhorn et al.41 reported that specimens with 1 – 2 mm ferrules exhibited significantly greater fracture resistance than those without. However, Alamin et al.42 reported that the type of finish line, whether shoulder or deep chamfer, did not significantly influence fracture resistance. The inclusion of the ferrule led to a reduction in the dentin thickness between the cervical portion of the endocrown and the ferrule component. Excessive machining of the intricate intaglio surface would compromise the integrity of the complete endocrown-ferrule assembly.31

The butt-joint endocrown design exhibited the lowest fracture resistance (379.33 ± 39.39 N) due to its limited mechanical reinforcement and reliance on adhesive bonding. With no axial wall engagement, the restoration depends primarily on the pulpal floor for support, which restricts its ability to resist occlusal and lateral forces. The flat butt-joint margin also creates areas of stress concentration at the tooth-restoration interface, as proved by a previous finite element analysis study that showed significantly higher von Mises stresses on teeth in the model of a butt-joint endocrown-retained FDP.43 This is likely due to the more complex oblique and lateral forces acting on FDPs compared with single restorations, which necessitate extracoronal coverage to withstand these forces rather than relying solely on intracoronal anchorage. However, connector dimensions met minimum zirconia thickness requirements while accommodating anatomical constraints. Variations in connector size may have influenced fracture resistance and represent a potential confounding factor. Moreover, the absence of a ferrule effect eliminates the encircling bracing that helps distribute functional loads more evenly, leaving the restoration vulnerable to failure, implying that butt-joint margins can lead to less favorable stress patterns. This outcome corresponds with prior research demonstrating that the butt-joint design exhibits lower fracture resistance than both heavy chamfer and heavy shoulder finish line preparation designs.44 However, Fages and Bennasar highlighted the importance of the butt-joint margin, as it provides a broad, uniform flat surface capable of withstanding the compressive forces commonly experienced by posterior teeth.21 The prepared surface is aligned with the occlusal plane, facilitating enhanced stress distribution throughout the tooth’s long axis, resulting in increased resistance.

Failure analysis showed consistent localization of fractures at connector zones, most frequently at the connector between the first and second molars (distal connector), with butt-joint specimens showing occlusal propagation from the connector and shoulder/ferrule specimens exhibiting somewhat more variable mesial/distal involvement; fracture zones were therefore categorizable into mesial and distal connector sites as previously described.4546 Biomechanically, occlusal loading generates tensile stresses at the gingival aspect of the connector (with occlusal compression), and when tensile stress exceeds the material’s flexural strength, a crack initiates and propagates toward the occlusal surface; higher bending moments transmitted through the distal abutment likely explain the distal connector predominance. However, small number of fractures in the mesial connector region was observed limiting direct statistical comparison between groups for this variable. As horizontally elliptical connectors were designed, the current study findings align with previous studies reporting that horizontally elliptical connectors are more prone to failure than vertically oriented designs.47 However, these findings support previous researches indicating that connector design is a significant factor in FDP longevity and should be carefully altered to reduce stress concentration and improve structural integrity.4849

This study has several limitations. The epoxy resin does not fully replicate the biomechanical behavior of natural dentin under dynamic loading, which may have influenced the fracture resistance measurements of adhesive restorations such as endocrowns. This study primarily addressed marginal discrepancies and did not examine the ferrule design impact on internal adaptation. Factors beyond abutment geometry, including CAD-CAM milling accuracy, may affect restoration performance. Additionally, the experimental setup did not replicate critical intraoral conditions, such as saliva, limited access, long-term mastication, or potential scanning inaccuracies. The loading protocol represented a simplified occlusal model, without accounting for cumulative fatigue or parafunctional forces, and the absence of a simulated periodontal ligament may have altered stress distribution. The small sample size and limited material selection further constrain the relevance of the findings beyond the study context. The applied marginal fit measurement technique assessed external vertical marginal discrepancy and may not fully reflect internal adaptation along axial walls or deeper finish line regions. Future studies using alternative or three-dimensional analysis methods, such as micro-CT, could provide a more comprehensive evaluation of internal and marginal fit. Additionally, incorporating fatigue testing, more realistic oral simulations, and long-term clinical trials would help validate and extend these in vitro findings.

CONCLUSION

Abutment design significantly affected the zirconia endocrown-retained FDPs performance. Marginal gaps increased after thermomechanical aging, but remained within clinically acceptable limits. The butt-joint design demonstrated superior marginal adaptation, while the full-coverage design exhibited the highest fracture resistance, exceeding physiologic posterior masticatory forces.
