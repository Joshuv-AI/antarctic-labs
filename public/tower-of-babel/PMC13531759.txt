A hybrid approach via video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery and limited sternal incision for resection of invasive thymoma with superior vena cava prosthetic replacement: a case report
Authors: Huanqi Mo, Yinggao Li, Shasha Yao, Yuan Zheng, Kan Wang, Wei Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWhen an invasive thymoma involves major vessels such as the superior vena cava (SVC), conventional management requires median sternotomy with en bloc resection and vascular reconstruction, which carries substantial surgical trauma and delayed recovery. This report presents a case of invasive thymoma invading the SVC and left innominate vein treated using a hybrid approach combining video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) with a limited sternal incision, in which the technical highlight was a Y-shaped reconstruction of the SVC and left innominate vein.MethodsA 39-year-old woman presented with an incidentally discovered anterior mediastinal mass. Contrast-enhanced CT showed an invasive thymoma(3.7 cm×3.0 cm×5.6 cm) invading the SVC and left innominate vein confluence. After multidisciplinary evaluation, preoperative biopsy was deemed too hazardous owing to the tumor’s intimate proximity to the great vessels, and upfront surgery was recommended. Thoracoscopic exploration via a 3 cm subxiphoid incision confirmed resectability, followed by partial upper sternotomy (≈ 12 cm). Under temporary venous shunting and clamping of the SVC and left innominate vein, the tumor and invaded vessels were resected en bloc. The SVC was replaced with an 18 F PTFE (Goretex) graft, and the left innominate vein was reconstructed with a tubularized bovine pericardial conduit anastomosed end-to-side to the SVC graft, creating a Y-shaped reconstruction.ResultsOperative time was 315 min with 800mL blood loss. Recovery was uneventful. Pathology confirmed WHO type B2 thymoma, Masaoka–Koga stage III, and adjuvant radiotherapy was given. During follow-up, the prosthetic graft remained patent and no recurrence was detected.ConclusionA hybrid VATS-assisted approach with limited sternal incision enables reliable vascular reconstruction while reducing invasiveness, representing a feasible and safe option for invasive thymomas involving the SVC. Multidisciplinary evaluation, appropriate patient selection and proficiency in both minimally invasive techniques and open vascular reconstruction are essential to achieve R0 resection and favorable outcomes.

Introduction

Thymoma is the most common primary tumor of the anterior mediastinum, accounting for approximately 35% of adult anterior mediastinal tumors [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification and Masaoka-Koga staging system, about 10% of thymomas exhibit invasive growth. The biological behavior of invasive thymomas lies between benign thymomas and thymic carcinomas, and may invade adjacent critical structures such as the pericardium, major vessels, and lungs [2]. When the tumor invades major vessels such as the superior vena cava (SVC), surgical difficulty increases markedly. The traditional treatment is tumor resection and vascular reconstruction via median sternotomy. This approach is highly invasive, associated with slow recovery, and carries a relatively high incidence of postoperative complications, substantially impairing quality of life.

The evolution of minimally invasive thoracic surgery has transformed the management of mediastinal tumors. For non-invasive thymomas, complete video-assisted thoracoscopic thymectomy has become the standard approach with well-established oncological safety and perioperative advantages [3–5]. However, when tumors invade critical structures-particularly major vessels such as the SVC-the role of minimally invasive approaches remains debated. Recent publications have begun to challenge the dogma that vascular invasion mandates full sternotomy. Xu et al. reported successful thoracoscopic thymectomy with partial SVC resection and direct repair in selected cases [6]. However, when > 30% of SVC circumference is invaded, prosthetic replacement becomes necessary [7], which has been considered beyond the scope of pure thoracoscopic approaches due to technical complexity.

Hybrid strategies combining thoracoscopic assessment with limited-incision open reconstruction represent a pragmatic compromise, potentially offering the benefits of both paradigms. This report presents our experience with such an approach and discusses its implications for future practice.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old woman was admitted on September 26, 2025, for “anterior mediastinal mass detected for more than 2 months”. The mass was incidentally discovered on July 3, 2025, during surgery for acute appendicitis. She had no specific symptoms and received no further management at that time. On September 8, 2025, chest CT revealed a right anterior mediastinal mass (36 mm×21 mm). Contrast-enhanced CT on September 18 suggested invasion of the SVC, and invasive thymoma was suspected. Upon admission, she was conscious without facial or upper extremity edema, dyspnea, or cough. Physical examination revealed no significant abnormalities. Her medical history included appendectomy and two cesarean sections.

After admission, the patient reported no symptoms suggestive of myasthenia gravis (MG), and serum anti-acetylcholine receptor antibody testing was negative, excluding associated MG. Laboratory evaluations—including complete blood count, biochemistry, hepatic and renal function, coagulation profile, tumor markers, and cardiopulmonary function tests—were unremarkable. A repeat contrast-enhanced chest CT on September 28, 2025, showed an irregular anterior superior mediastinal mass measuring 3.7 × 3.0 × 5.6 cm with calcifications and moderate enhancement. The lesion invaded the SVC, and its interface with the ascending aorta and left innominate vein was indistinct. The diagnosis was invasive thymoma (Masaoka–Koga stage III) (Fig. 1).

The case was presented at a multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting involving thoracic surgery, medical oncology, diagnostic radiology, and anesthesiology. The imaging features—including a well-defined anterior mediastinal mass with calcification, moderate enhancement, and apparent vascular invasion—were considered highly suggestive of invasive thymoma. The question of preoperative percutaneous biopsy was specifically discussed; however, the tumor was in direct contact with the SVC, ascending aorta, and bilateral innominate veins, and the MDT consensus was that biopsy carried an unacceptably high risk of major vascular injury and hemorrhage. Given that the clinical and radiological diagnosis was considered sufficiently reliable, and that upfront surgical resection without prior biopsy is an accepted approach for resectable thymomas per current guidelines [8], the MDT recommended proceeding directly with surgery.

Surgery was performed on September 29, 2025. With the patient in supine position, a 3-cm subxiphoid incision was made and a 30-degree thoracoscope was introduced for initial exploration of the anterior mediastinum (Phase 1: thoracoscopic assessment). An anterior mediastinal mass measuring 3.7 × 5.6 × 3.0 cm was found, firm in consistency, invading the SVC (approximately 50% of the SVC circumference), the confluence of the left innominate vein, partial pericardium, right phrenic nerve, and right upper lobe. No pleural dissemination or occult metastases were identified. Thoracoscopic assessment confirmed that the tumor was resectable but that complete thoracoscopic resection was not feasible due to the extent of SVC and left innominate vein invasion requiring complex vascular reconstruction. The procedure was therefore converted to a limited open approach (Phase 2: limited sternotomy with thoracoscopic assistance). Partial upper sternotomy (approximately 12 cm) was performed, extending from the suprasternal notch to approximately the level of the fourth intercostal space. During the subsequent mediastinal dissection, the thoracoscope was repositioned through the subxiphoid port to provide supplementary visualization of the posterior dissection plane—particularly the interface between the tumor and the ascending aorta—which was not directly accessible through the limited sternotomy alone(Phase 3: thoracoscopic-assisted dissection). The vascular reconstruction itself was performed entirely under direct vision through the sternotomy incision.

Before vascular clamping, a temporary venous shunt was established to maintain cerebral venous drainage during the period of caval occlusion. After systemic heparinization, cannulae were inserted into the cephalad segment of the SVC and left innominate vein and into the caudal SVC segment near the right atrium; the two cannulae were connected externally by a shunt tube, creating a temporary bypass that diverted upper-body venous return to the right atrium. Only after the shunt was confirmed to be functioning were the proximal and distal SVC and the left innominate vein clamped. Complete tumor resection was then achieved including partial SVC, the left innominate vein, and partial right upper lung. An 18 F Goretex prosthetic graft was used for end-to-end anastomosis to replace the resected SVC, and a bovine pericardial patch was fashioned into a conduit approximately 1 cm in diameter and 3 cm in length to replace the resected portion of the left innominate vein. The bovine pericardial conduit was then anastomosed in an end-to-side fashion to the mid-portion of the SVC graft, thereby reconstructing the venous drainage in a Y-shaped configuration (Fig. 2). The operation lasted 315 min, with blood loss of 800 ml, requiring transfusion of 1.5 units of packed red blood cells and 400 ml of plasma. The patient recovered uneventfully without serious complications. Postoperative pathology confirmed invasive thymoma (WHO type B2, Masaoka–Koga stage III) (Fig. 3). Adjuvant radiotherapy was administered postoperatively. During follow-up, the prosthetic graft remained patent with no evidence of tumor recurrence.

Discussion

According to the 2025 NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines, surgery is the preferred treatment for resectable thymoma, and complete (R0) resection is key to achieving favorable prognosis [8]. However, thymomas invading the SVC represent one of the most challenging scenarios in thoracic surgery. Generally, if invasion is limited to the lateral wall of the SVC, partial resection with direct suturing or patch repair can be considered; if invasion exceeds 30% of the vessel circumference, vascular replacement is required7. In this case, the tumor invaded more than half of the SVC circumference and extended to the confluence of the left innominate veins, the pericardium, the right phrenic nerve, and the right upper lobe. Such extensive invasion necessitates complete SVC clamping and en bloc resection, traditionally performed via full median sternotomy. However, full sternotomy requires complete sternal division, leading to potential serious complications such as sternal nonunion or infection [5]. Another important consideration is whether preoperative tissue diagnosis should be obtained before definitive surgery. Current guidelines recommend biopsy when the diagnosis is uncertain or when neoadjuvant therapy is being considered [8]. However, when imaging findings are strongly suggestive of thymoma and the tumor is deemed resectable, upfront surgical resection without prior biopsy remains an accepted approach. In our case, the tumor was in intimate contact with the SVC, ascending aorta, and bilateral innominate veins, making percutaneous biopsy hazardous. Following MDT evaluation, the clinical and radiological diagnosis was considered sufficiently reliable to proceed directly with surgery—a decision validated by postoperative histopathological confirmation of WHO type B2 thymoma.

The role of VATS in this hybrid procedure can be delineated into three phases. In Phase 1 (thoracoscopic exploration), the subxiphoid thoracoscopic approach served as a critical decision-making tool, allowing comprehensive assessment of the extent of invasion and resectability with the following advantages: (1) The subxiphoid approach provides excellent visualization of the entire anterior mediastinum, particularly the junction area of the SVC and innominate veins; (2) The magnification of thoracoscopy allows clearer visualization of the tumor-vessel relationship, facilitating precise surgical planning; (3) If exploration reveals unresectable tumor, unnecessary thoracotomy trauma can be avoided; (4) Thoracoscopic exploration can also detect pleural seeding or other metastases not shown on preoperative imaging. In Phase 2, upon confirming that purely thoracoscopic resection was not feasible, the procedure was converted to limited sternotomy. In Phase 3, the thoracoscope was repositioned through the subxiphoid port to assist mediastinal dissection, providing supplementary visualization of the posterior tumor–aortic interface that was not directly accessible through the limited sternotomy alone. The vascular reconstruction was performed entirely under direct vision, as this step requires tactile feedback and immediate hemostatic control afforded by open technique.

The advantage of partial over full sternotomy warrants clarification. In this patient, a full median sternotomy would have required an incision of approximately 22–24 cm with complete sternal division. Our partial upper sternotomy (approximately 12 cm) extended only to the fourth intercostal space, preserving the lower sternum and xiphoid process. Although the 3-cm subxiphoid incision was in relative proximity, it involved only soft tissue for port access without sternal division, and the two incisions were not contiguous. The key benefit lies not in skin incision length alone, but in preservation of lower sternal continuity, which maintains chest wall mechanical stability and is associated with reduced pain, lower rates of sternal dehiscence, faster respiratory recovery, and shorter hospital stay. Our patient’s uneventful recovery without chest wall instability supports this advantage. Although robotic surgical systems with three-dimensional vision and flexible mechanical arms partially address these issues, the equipment is expensive and not available in all medical institutions [9]. Our case extends this experience by demonstrating that even when > 50% SVC circumference is invaded—traditionally considered an absolute indication for full sternotomy—a hybrid VATS-assisted approach with prosthetic replacement remains feasible and safe.

Technical details of vascular reconstruction are critical to success. Several key points were applied in this case: (1) Adequate mobilization of the proximal and distal SVC to ensure a tension-free anastomosis; (2) Selection of an appropriately sized graft-in this case, an 18 F Goretex graft matched the native SVC diameter well; (3) Continuous suturing using 5 − 0 Prolene to ensure anastomotic sealing; (4) Reconstruction of the innominate vein using a rolled bovine pericardial conduit, which allows flexible adjustment to the vessel diameter; (5) Careful inspection for stenosis or kinking after completion of reconstruction.

Prosthetic graft selection and long-term patency are determinants of long-term prognosis. Currently, PTFE material is mainly used for SVC reconstruction, with Goretex grafts being most widely applied. Multiple studies show PTFE graft long-term patency rates of 70–93% [10, 11]. Factors affecting prosthetic graft patency include: (1) Graft diameter - generally, diameters > 8 mm are associated with better patency; (2) Blood flow velocity - the high flow of the SVC favors patency; (3) Anticoagulation therapy - standardized postoperative anticoagulation significantly reduces thrombosis risk; (4) Anastomotic technique - precise anastomosis reduces turbulence and thrombosis. The bovine pericardial conduit reconstruction of the innominate vein used in this case has been rarely reported in domestic and international literature, and its long-term effects require further observation.

Based on our experience, the hybrid approach combining VATS with a limited sternal incision for invasive thymoma involving major vessels has several advantages: (1) Relatively less trauma with faster postoperative recovery; (2) Thoracoscopic exploration allows more accurate assessment of lesion extent; (3) Preservation of most thoracic integrity with minimal respiratory function impact. However, this approach also has limitations: (1) High technical demands requiring proficiency in both thoracoscopic and open surgical techniques; (2) Potentially prolonged operative time; (3) Increased instrument-related costs; (4) A prolonged learning curve.

Looking to the future, with continuous advances in surgical techniques and instruments, more complex mediastinal tumors will be amenable to minimally invasive approaches. 3D thoracoscopic systems provide stereoscopic vision that may facilitate endoscopic vascular anastomosis. Novel vascular anastomotic devices and staplers can simplify operative steps. Development of bioengineered vessels may solve some inherent defects of prosthetic grafts. Individualized 3D-printed vessels can provide optimal anatomical matching. These technical advances will benefit more patients through minimally invasive surgery.
