ABSTRACT: Micro- and nano-plastics (MNPs) pose a significant hazard to both
environmental and human health due to their complex interfacial interactions with their
surroundings, however there is a lack of techniques available to study the composition of MNPs
at their surface. To fill this gap, representative MNPs were coated with matrix that enabled
detection via matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization mass spectrometry (MALDI), a technique
that has been shown to be surface specific but had not been applied to MNPs previously. Three
different coating techniques were tested; evaporation and condensation, drop-casting, and mixed
nebulization. Mixed nebulization provided the best balance of results, which was a strong MALDI
signal in combination with even coating on the MNP surface from MNPs ≥20 µm. This work
provides a new technique for the measurement of MNP surface composition that will help advance
the field and enable more thorough studies of the chemical transformations that MNPs undergo in
the environment.

1. INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF RESULTS
Micro- and nano-plastics (MNPs) are a pollutant generated by the physical breakdown of larger
plastics, such as tire wear,1, 2 trash burning,3-5 and water erosion.6, 7 Once small enough, they can
become lofted into the atmosphere, with several studies finding high concentrations in urban areas8
and lower concentration in locations as far remote as the arctic.7, 9 A majority of these MNPs are
within the “accumulation mode” size regime, meaning they can persist in the atmosphere for days
to weeks.10 MNP presence in air represents a new exposure route for humans, with a recent study
finding that humans inhale up to 70,000 MNPs per day.11 This high exposure to MNPs is not
benign, with multiple studies finding that exposure to MNPs causes higher rates of pulmonary and
cardiovascular disease in humans.12 Furthermore, as MNPs exist in the environment, they can
adsorb pollutants13, 14 and undergo oxidation processes,15 both which increase their overall
negative health impacts. It is therefore critical to understand their changing surface composition
to better inform their impacts on human health and the environment. However, prior to this
proposal, the techniques available either lacked chemical speciation16 or were not surface specific.5

To fill this gap, we proposed a new technique to measure MNP composition with matrix assisted
laser desorption ionization mass spectrometry (MALDI).

MALDI is a compositional imaging technique which utilizes a chemical matrix to ionize the
sample upon contact with a laser before measurement by a mass spectrometer.17 Prior
measurements of MNPs with MALDI required either large amounts of matrix18 or solvation prior
to measurement,19 as MNPs are generally too small to be measured effectively by MALDI. To this
end, we proposed using a particle coating system to coat polystyrene MNPs with matrix prior to
measurement, to answer the question: Can coating MNPs in the accumulation size range with
matrix enable their composition measurement by MALDI? Our hypothesis is that the addition of
matrix on the surface of the MNPs will increase their size enough to allow for detection via
MALDI without further sample preparation, and our success metric was to detect the mass
signature for polystyrene with a signal-to-noise ratio > 5 (a widely accepted standard for compound
identification in mass spectrometry) for a 1 µm particle. While we were unable to meet this specific
metric, we were able to develop a measurement technique which creates a depth profile of MNP
composition for particles ≥20 µm, which can be applied to any type of polymeric particle. This
work has led to an article in preparation for a high-impact journal, five presentations to the broader
scientific community, career development for two postdocs (including the PI), one student intern,
and four early-career staff, as well as two new particle preparation and measurement capabilities
for Sandia, with one submitted as a technical advance. Further details of these results can be found
in Section 4 as well as the end-of-year Exploratory Express presentation tri-chart in the addendum,
Section 7.1.

2. DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT AND
METHODOLOGY
2.1 Materials
Materials for sample preparation included water (18Ω, Millipore), methanol (MeOH, ≥98%,
Sigma-Aldrich), 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (2,5-DHB, 98%, Sigma-Aldrich), retinoic acid (RA,
≥98%, Sigma-Aldrich), silver trifluoroacetate (AgTFA, 98%, Sigma-Aldrich) and various sizes of
polystyrene latex beads (PSL, Sigma-Aldrich) between 1-25µm.

2.2 Sample Preparation
2.2.1 Saturator/Condenser Coating
Solutions of PSL beads (1% v/v) were prepared in water and sonicated for 20 minutes before use.
They were atomized by a collison-based aerosol generator (TSI, Model 3076) operated at 20 psig
with dry, particle-free air from a compressor (Kobalt, Model #0300842), generating a particle-
laden flow of air at ~2 liters per minute (lpm). These aerosolized beads were dried using a silica-
based desiccant dryer (TSI, Model 3062). After the dryer, a tee attached the flow from the
sublimated matrix to the PSL flow. The sublimated matrix flow was generated by heating a pear-

shaped flask of 2,5-DHB in a sand bath on top of a hot plate set to 150 C. 0.1 lpm of dry, particle-
free air was flowed over the top of the 2,5-DHB using a mass flow controller (Alicat, MC-5SLPM-
D), and the outlet of the flask was teed into the PSL flow. After the tee, the 2,5-DHB vapor and
PSL flow mixed in a one-foot-long stainless steel tube, which cooled the flow sufficiently at room
temperature before sampling. Samples were either collected in the non-rotating multi-orifice
uniform deposition impactor (MOUDI, TSI, Model 100S4) with aluminum foil substrates for
analysis by MALDI, or their diameter was measured by a scanning electrical mobility particle sizer
(SMPS, TSI, Model 3938) to measure the change in particle diameter after coating. Samples from
this technique were taken from the third impactor stage of the MOUDI.

2.2.2 Drop-Casting Coating
Solutions of 15 µm PSL beads (1%) were prepared in water and sonicated for 20 minutes before
use. Matrix solutions of 5 mg/mL RA and 5 mg/mL AgTFA were prepared in methanol. Samples
were prepared by first pipetting 1 µL of matrix onto an aluminum foil substrate. Then, 1 µL of
PSL solution was pipetted on top of the matrix solution. Then, 1-4 layers of 1 µL of matrix were
pipetted on top of the PSL’s. In between each layering step, the sample was allowed to fully dry.

2.2.3 Mixed Nebulization Coating
Solutions of RA and AgTFA (200, 500, and 1000 ppm) were prepared in methanol and spiked
with 15 µm and 20 µm PSL beads to contain 1% PSL v/v before being sonicated for 20 minutes.
After preparation, the solutions were gravity-fed into a 4-fluid nozzle Blaustein atomizing module
(CH Technologies) operated at 20 psig of dry, particle-free air (Kobalt, Model #0300842) to
generate a flow of ~ 2 lpm. This flow entered an 8” long tube before connecting to the MOUDI
equipped with aluminum foil substrates. Samples from these collections were taken from the first
impactor stage.

2.3 Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization Mass Spectrometry (MALDI)
The MALDI analysis was performed using a Waters quadrupole time-of-flight (QToF) mass
spectrometer, Synapt HDMS XS (Waters Corporation, MA, USA) equipped with a MALDI
source. The samples were first taped onto a MALDI imaging plate before analysis. The Nd:YAG
laser source (355 nm, pulsed at ≥100 μJ/pulse) was operated at energies between 250 and 450. The
analysis was collected in positive mode and Waters sensitivity mode from 100 m/z to 2000 m/z.
Data acquisition was performed in MassLynx Version 4.2 (Waters Corporation, MA, USA).

2.4 Digital Microscopy
Digital microscopic images of the samples were taken on a Keyence VHX-7000 digital optical
microscope that is equipped with lens having following magnification: 20-100x, 100-500x, 500-
2500x and 2500-6000x. In a typical experiment, the aluminum foil contains sample was put on
microscope base under suitable lens and then focused to get clear images. The direction,
brightness, contrast and intensity of light was adjusted accordingly to acquire clear image.

2.5 Scanning Electron Microscopy-Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy
Scanning Electron Microscopy-Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) of the
samples were carried out on a Tescan Vega3 SEM, equipped with an Oxford Instruments® EDS
detector with Aztec® 6.0 SP1 software. Prior to analysis, a small piece of aluminum foil that
contain sample was attached on top of carbon tape on a SEM stub (Ted Pella, Inc.). Then the
sample was sputter coated with gold/palladium (60%/40%) to increase conductivity and reduce
sample charging during analysis. The SEM images were obtained using secondary electron (SE).
An accelerating voltage of 10 keV was used for Imaging. EDS elemental analysis was done using
20 keV accelerating voltage.

2.6 Atomic Force Microscopy
Particle measurements were performed via a Bruker Dimension Icon Atomic Force Microscope
(AFM) equipped with a Nanoscope V controller. Each as-received sample was adhered to an AFM
specimen disc using double-sided carbon tape prior to imaging. All images were captured while
operated in a tapping mode configuration using RTESPA-300 AFM cantilevers. The images were
acquired in air using a standard dimension AFM cantilever holder (DAFMCH). Measured sample
images were analyzed using the opensource AFM software gwyddion (https://gwyddion.net/).

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1 Saturator/Condenser Coating
Initial efforts for this project focused on developing and characterizing an aerosol coating system,
as was included in the original proposal and detailed in the methods section 2.2.1. For these tests,
2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (2,5-DHB) was used as the matrix due to its low chemical hazards and
demonstrated effectiveness at detecting polymers with MALDI.20 Initial tests with beads larger
than 1 µm showed poor transmission efficiency through the setup, with no beads collected in the
MOUDI after 15 minutes of collection; therefore, system testing continued using 1 µm beads.
Figure 1A demonstrates the change in particle diameter of 1 µm PSL beads measured by a SMPS
as a function of elapsed time, indicating that the particles were being coated with 2,5-DHB to a
thickness of about 5 nm after 25 minutes. This increase over time is attributed to the increase in
temperature of the solid 2,5-DHB, as the mixing time of the PSL beads and the 2,5-DHB flow did
not increase or change over time. The before and after samples from this experiment were analyzed
using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) as shown in Figures 1B and 1C. Due to the long
collection time, there is significant aggregation of the particles, with large amorphous chunks of
2,5-DHB completely obscuring the PSL beads.

Figure 1: (A) Change in particle diameter as a function of time elapsed as the 2,5-DHB reached
higher temperatures (B) 1 µm PSL beads before coating with 2,5-DHB (C) 1 µm PSL beads after
coating with 2,5-DHB (D) Mass spectra of 1 µm PSL beads coated with 2,5-DHB (E) mass spectra
of 1 µm PSL beads mixed with RA and AgTFA, with blue denoting the peaks corresponding to
polystyrene and silver, or [(C8H8)NAg]+

With the MALDI, the experiment is considered successful in detecting polystyrene if there are
observed peaks which have a separation of 104 m/z, which is equivalent to one unit of polystyrene,
or C8H8. The mass spectra with 2,5-DHB as the matrix is shown in Figure 1D. In this mass spectra,
there are no clear peaks that can be attributed to polystyrene, and there are also no peaks with a
repeating unit of 104 m/z. To confirm that the MALDI would be able to detect the polystyrene
from the beads at all, a sample of the beads were directly mixed with retinoic acid (RA) and silver
trifluoroacetate (AgTFA), which is another standard MALDI matrix which has been shown in the
literature to effectively detect polystyrene.21 These results are shown in Figure 1E, where there is
a clear repeating pattern of 104 starting at m/z 731.3 and continuing through m/z 835.3, 939.4,
1043.4, and 1147.5. These masses also correspond to the repeating units of polystyrene clustered
to silver, (C8H8)NAg+, where N=6, 7, 8, 9, and 10. With these positive results using RA and AgTFA
as the matrix, the decision was made to move on with the project using them rather than 2,5-DHB.
However, both RA and AgTFA come with health hazards more significant than 2,5-DHB, which
makes sublimating them a more significant safety concern in the laboratory. To this end, we
decided to change our coating strategy to one that wouldn’t involve heating the matrix.

3.2 Drop-Casting Coating
In the second phase of experiments, drop-casting was tested as a method to coat the PSL beads,
where thin films of material are formed by dropping solution onto a substrate and allowing the
solvent to fully evaporate, as described in Section 2.2.2. A box plot of the AFM results from these
samples are shown in Figure 2A, where each box represents eight bead measurements, and the
“coating amount” is the number of thin films that were drop-cast on top of the beads. All four
samples are within the 5th and 95th percentile of the other samples, and there is no clear upward
trend with the increasing number of thin films. The SEM results of these samples are shown in
Figure 2B and 2C, where 2B shows the “coffee ring” result of the thin film formation where there
is significant crystallization of the AgTFA at the outside of the circle, and 2C shows the significant
particle aggregation and uneven nature of the AgTFA crystals. Figure 2D is another SEM image
of these samples with two points indicated that were chosen for EDS measurement, where one is
directly on top of a bead and the second is in-between the beads. The EDS measurement of these
two points is shown in Figure 2E and 2F, where 2E shows the low quantity of silver present on top
of the bead (point 1), and 2F shows the high amount of silver present in-between the beads (point
2).

This method of sample preparation led to two significant problems. The first issue is that the PSL
beads would tend to form coagulated “islands” when drop-cast as indicated in Figure 2C, so
measurements of individual beads could not be conducted on the MALDI. The second, potentially
larger issue was that the matrix solution would not evenly coat the beads, leading to significant
crystallization of the silver trifluoroacetate at the edges of where the drop was placed, which is
also called a “coffee ring” in the drop-casting technique, which leads to an unevenness in the thin
film. The AgTFA that did manage to stay near the beads formed large crystals, which can lead to

irregular ionization in the MALDI. This was further demonstrated by EDS measurements shown
in Figure 2E and 2F, where the surface of the beads had many points where there was little to no
silver at all. While these samples showed positive polystyrene signal in the MALDI, there was no
indication that these samples had a uniform coating. Because the proposed technique relies on an
even coating of the PSL beads, a third coating technique was tested.

number of thin films drop-cast on the beads. (B, C) SEM image of drop-cast sample (D) SEM of
drop-cast sample, with points chosen for EDS in red (E) EDS spectrum of point 1 (F) EDS
spectrum of point 2

3.3 Mixed Nebulization Coating
To provide an even coating of matrix on the PSL beads, a top-down oriented nebulizer was chosen.
With the top-down orientation, larger PSL’s would be able to be nebulized and collected,
mitigating one of the issues from the first phase of the project, and would in theory have more
consistent coating thickness as the solvent could evaporate before impaction in the MOUDI,
mitigating the issues in phase two.

In this phase 15 and 20 µm PSL beads were tested. Initial MALDI results showed positive
polystyrene signal for both samples, so these samples were also tested with AFM and SEM, and
the samples were imaged using MALDI. The data produced from the MALDI was processed by
first confirming the presence of five polymer peaks (m/z 731.28, 835.34, 939.40, 1043.46, and
1147.53 +/- 0.001) in each pixel, and if all five were not present, that pixel was removed from
analysis. Next, the median, 25th and 75th percentiles were calculated from the reduced dataset.
These results were plotted against the AFM bead height results, as shown in Figure 3A for the 15
µm beads and 3C for the 20 µm beads. Additionally, the images from representative samples of
each are shown in Figure 3B for the 15um beads and 3D for the 20 µm beads.

Figure 3: (A) Plot of the median average intensity of all five polymer peaks from the 15 µm bead
samples plotted against the coated bead height, with the intensity error bars representing the 25th
and 75th percentile of the data and the height error bars representing the standard deviation of the

AFM measurements (B) AFM images of the coated 15 µm beads (C) Plot of the median average
intensity of all five polymer peaks from the 20 µm bead samples plotted against the coated bead
height, including the dashed line linear fit, with the intensity error bars representing the 25th and
75th percentile of the data and the height error bars representing the standard deviation of the AFM
measurements (D) AFM images of the coated 20 µm beads

Based on the hypothesis of this project, if the coating thickness increases, then the polystyrene
signal should decrease as less and less of the bead is sampled. For the 15 µm beads, this trend is
not apparent, with two samples entirely overlapping each other in both intensity and bead height,
and the third point showing separation based on bead height but not with intensity. It is theorized
that a contributor to this is the significant coagulation of the beads demonstrated in Figure 3B. If
the beads are significantly coagulated, there will be increased coating of the matrix in the wells
inside the beads, which was shown in Figure 2F. This would cause uneven ionization of the beads
at the edges, and overall have higher variability in the ion intensity from the beads. The results
were more positive for the 20 µm bead samples. These results are shown in Figure 3C, where there
is a clear decreasing linear fit to the data with an r2 value of 0.880. In contrast to the 15 µm beads,
while there are some neighboring beads shown in Figure 3D, there is clear separation between
them and little clustering.

5. CONCLUSION
In this work, multiple types of aerosol coating techniques were tested to measure the composition
of MNPs using MALDI. The saturator/condenser provided adequate coating of matrix; however,
the matrix was not effective at detecting polystyrene. Drop-cast coating with RA and AgTFA
provided excellent polystyrene signal on the MALDI, however the coating was very uneven,
leading to instable MALDI signal. Finally, the in-situ mixing of matrix and PSL beads and
atomization in a commercial, top-down oriented nebulizer led to both even coating as well as
strong MALDI signal. With this advance, depth profiles of MNP composition can be created to
better understand the overall impact of MNPs on environmental and human health. This is a
significant technical improvement to the field of MNP composition measurements, as previous
techniques could not provide as much chemical detail at the surface of the MNPs studied. From
this work, two major aerosol generation and measurement capabilities were added to the labs. It
also led to six presentations and one publication in preparation, as well as significant career
development for two postdocs, one student intern, and four early-career staff members.
