Time-to-progression ratio as a potential study endpoint in early-phase oncology trials: pooled analysis of phase II trials from the Australian MoST program
Authors: Derrick H. W. Siu, Frank P. Y. Lin, David M. Thomas, Subotheni Thavaneswaran, David Espinoza, Sarah Chinchen, Mandy L. Ballinger, R. John Simes, Chee Khoon Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTime-to-progression ratio (TTPr) measures intra-patient treatment benefit by comparing disease control on study treatment with that of the immediately preceding therapy. Its prognostic validity in the immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) era remains uncertain.MethodsWe performed a pooled patient-level analysis of eight phase II trials. Associations between TTPr, TTP on prior therapy (TTP1), and on-study TTP (TTP2/ PFS), objective response rate (ORR), with overall survival (OS) were evaluated using Cox regression. Landmark analyses at 6-months were used to mitigate guarantee-time bias.ResultsAmong 246 evaluable participants, median TTP1 and TTP2 were 3.9 and 2.1 months, respectively, with poor correlation (r = 0.02). TTPr was not associated with OS (TTPr ≥1.3 vs <1.3, HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.57–1.26). In contrast, TTP2 and ORR were strongly prognostic (TTP2 ≥2.1 months vs <2.1 months: HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.35–0.76, p < 0.001); (response vs non-response: HR 0.19, 95% CI 0.07–0.52, p = 0.001) and remained independently associated with OS in multivariable analyses. Findings were consistent across ICI and non-ICI trials.ConclusionsTTPr is not a robust prognostic measure in platform trials. Although this raises concern regarding its validity as a surrogate endpoint for OS, formal evaluation requires randomized treatment comparisons.

Introduction

Drug development in oncology faces growing challenges as cancers are progressively stratified into biomarker-defined subgroups. For rare cancers or those characterized by uncommon biomarkers, conducting randomized controlled trials (RCTs) based on overall survival (OS) may be infeasible. In these contexts, non-comparative study designs may play a crucial role in evaluating drug activity and generating evidence to support regulatory approval [1]. Basket and platform trials are now widely adopted in oncology, these adaptive trial designs enable early identification of promising drug candidates in biomarker-matched populations, offering more efficient development pathways [2,3]. Such trials are also often powered based on surrogate endpoints, where objective response rate (ORR) and progression-free survival (PFS) are accepted as substantial evidence to support accelerated drug approval [4]. However, the correlation between these surrogate endpoints and OS is often weak [5]. Therefore, identifying surrogate endpoints that more reliably reflect preliminary efficacy is essential to inform go/ no-go decisions in drug development.

Time to progression (TTP) ratio [6], also referred to as the progression-free survival ratio or growth modulation index, is recognized as a useful endpoint for assessing preliminary activity of novel cytotoxic chemotherapies and molecular targeted agents in early phase trials [7–15]. TTP ratio (TTPr) compares the TTP of current line of systemic therapy (TTP2) with that on the immediately preceding line of systemic therapy (TTP1). TTP generally shortens with each successive line of treatment, a TTPr threshold of ≥1.3 has been proposed to indicate promising drug activity, suggesting that the investigational therapy achieves a clinically meaningful extension of disease control relative to the prior treatment [6]. A key advantage of TTPr lies in its intra-patient comparison, where each patient serves as their own control. This approach mitigates inter-patient variability in tumor biology and prognostic factors, thereby enhancing the interpretability of efficacy signals in heterogeneous populations—a feature particularly valuable in basket and platform trials.

TTPr was mostly validated in the era of cytotoxic chemotherapy, the appropriateness of using TTPr to assess the activity of new classes of therapies remains unknown despite its adoption by several precision oncology platform studies. Of interest, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) exhibit distinct response dynamics and progression patterns that may differ from those observed with chemotherapies or molecular targeted agents. The role of TTPr has not been adequately studied in this context.

While formal evaluation of surrogate endpoint validity and predictive utility is ideally conducted using data from randomized trials, important insights can still be gained by examining the prognostic association between candidate endpoints and OS. In this pooled analysis of individual patient data (IPD) from single-arm phase II studies, we aimed to: (1) evaluate the prognostic association of TTPr on OS across a range of different thresholds; (2) dissect the individual associations of TTP1,TTP2, and ORR with OS and quantify the relative contribution of TTP1 to TTPr to isolate its independent prognostic effect; and (3) compare the performance of TTPr in ICI versus non-ICI studies, to assess its validity across therapeutic modalities.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a pooled analysis of IPD from the Australian Molecular Screening and Therapeutics (MoST) precision oncology program (ACTRN12616000908437) [16]. MoST is a master protocol that screens adults with refractory solid cancers using comprehensive genomic profiling. Eligible participants were enrolled in one of the 19 single-arm phase II trials investigating novel therapies matched to genomic alterations. TTPr was one of the pre-specified endpoints and was collected consistently across these trials. For the current analysis, we included eight studies with mature OS data. These were (1) MoST 2 (ACTRN12616001019493) – durvalumab plus tremelimumab in rare or neglected cancers [17], (2) MoST 7 (ACTRN 12620000,918921) – tremelimumab in advanced rare or neglected cancers [18], (3) MoST10 (ACTRN12620000568910) – palbociclib and avelumab in tumors with gain of function mutation in CDK4 and CCND1-3, or CDKN2A deletion or loss of function mutations [19], (4) MoST 6–8 (ACTRN 12617001000392) – olaparib and durvalumab in cancers with defects in the homologous recombination repair pathway [20], (5) MoST 11 (ACTRN12620000984998) – tildrakizumab in advanced osteosarcomas or soft tissue sarcomas [21], (6) MoST 5 (ACTRN12618000357257) – eribulin in advanced CD31+ angiosarcoma or other selected CD31+ sarcomas [22], (7) MoST 4 (ACTRN12618000281291) – vismodegib in tumors harboring PTCH1 or SMO mutations [23], and (8) MoST 8 (ACTRN12619001265167) – trastuzumab emtansine in tumors harboring HER2 amplifications or mutations [24]. The MoST program was approved by the St Vincent’s Hospital Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (reference, HREC/16/SVH/23).

Data analysis

TTP1 was obtained through central adjudication of medical records. TTP2, equivalent to PFS, was calculated from the date of study enrollment to the date of progression or death of any cause. TTPr was determined to be non-evaluable for the following reasons: (1) if there was no prior systemic therapy, (2) if the prior systemic therapy was an adjuvant or neoadjuvant therapy, (3) prior therapy was interpolated with a palliative radiotherapy or surgery to the target lesion, (4) prior therapy was stopped due to toxicity or withdrawal of consent, or (5) had incomplete medical record. OS was defined as the time from study enrollment to death from any cause, with participants alive at last follow-up censored at that date.

We assessed the association between TTPr status (≥1.3 vs <1.3) and OS using Cox proportional hazards regression. This association was further explored across varying TTPr thresholds. Additionally, the relationship between TTP1 and OS was also examined: participants were initially categorized by the median TTP1 distribution, and Cox regression analysis was performed to evaluate for prognostic differences, followed by sensitivity analyses across alternative TTP1 cut-points.

Landmark analyses were conducted at 6 months to evaluate the influence of TTPr, TTP1, and TTP2, up to the landmark time on subsequent OS. Landmark at 6 months was selected based on our prior work which demonstrated 6-months PFS rate was a valid surrogate for OS in phase II ICI studies [25]. Participants who died before or at 6 months were excluded as they were no longer assessable for OS at the landmark. For those with TTP2 of ≥6 months, TTP2 was truncated at 6 months, termed modified TTP26m (mTTP26m), and survival beyond the landmark was analyzed. Similarly, modified TTPr (mTTPr6m) was calculated using mTTP26m. Sensitivity analyses were performed using a 3-month landmark (mTTP23m). The landmark approach was used to mitigate guarantee-time bias associated with temporal overlap of survival time between TTP2/ PFS and OS. Partitioning survival time using landmark analysis ensure comparability among participants who survived beyond the landmark [26]. Given its established role as a marker of treatment activity, ORR was included to adjust for differences in efficacy across the investigational agents evaluated in this analysis. Univariable and multivariable landmark analyses were used to assess associations of TTP1, TTP2, ORR with OS. Finally, we compared the performance of TTPr and TTP2 between ICI and non-ICI subgroups. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.0).

Results

Participant characteristics

Out of 343 participants, 246 (72%) with evaluable data for TTPr were analyzed. Ninety-seven participants were excluded due to non-evaluable TTP1, key reasons included (i) no prior systemic therapy (n = 40, 41%), (ii) prior systemic therapy stopped due to completion of treatment (n = 30, 31%), and (iii) prior systemic therapy stopped due to adverse events (n = 9%) (Figure 1). The evaluable cohort consisted of 131 females (53%). The median age was 59 years (range 18–85). The most common tumor types were sarcoma (n = 87; 35%), upper gastrointestinal (n = 42; 17%), colorectal (n = 25; 10%), and gynecological cancers (n = 19; 8%). In a sensitivity analysis at the 3-month landmark, bivariate analysis showed that both mTTP23m (HR 0.39, 95% CI 0.28–0.52, p < 0.001) and TTP1 (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.46–0.87, p = 0.005) were significantly associated with improved OS (Supplementary Table S2a). In multivariate analysis, mTTP23m (HR 0.46, 95% CI 0.33–0.64, p < 0.001), TTP1 (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.44–0.84, p = 0.003), and ORR (HR 0.20, 95% CI 0.07–0.54, p = 0.002) were significantly associated with OS.

When TTP1 and TTP2 were analyzed as continuous variables in a bivariate model (Supplementary Table S2b), both remained significantly associated with OS; however, the effect size for TTP1 was modest (HR 0.97; p = 0.04). In multivariate analysis, mTTP23 m (HR 0.54, 95% CI 0.42–0.70; p < 0.001) and ORR (HR 0.20, 95% CI 0.07–0.56, p = 0.002) were strongly associated with OS, whereas the effect size for TTP1 and OS remained modest (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.93–0.99; p = 0.02).

Immunotherapy vs non-immunotherapy studies

There was no significant difference in 6-month landmark OS between participants who received ICI vs non-ICI treatments (HR 1.06, 95% CI 0.70–1.62, p = 0.78) (Supplementary Figure 3). HR for OS also did not vary significantly across different TTPr thresholds regardless of whether the participant received ICI or not (Supplementary Figure S4a, b, respectively).

Discussion

In this IPD analysis of 246 participants from eight phase II MoST studies, OS was not meaningfully associated with TTPr across a broad range of thresholds. Similarly, TTP1 was not consistently associated with OS. By contrast, our landmark analyses showed that longer on-study disease control during the first 6 months, as measured by mTTP26m, was associated with improved subsequent OS. These findings indicate that proportional delay in progression captured by TTPr does not appear to be prognostic, whereas absolute disease control achieved on the current investigational therapy, is a clinically relevant determinant of OS.

Prior studies using chemotherapy or targeted therapy reported a positive association between TTPr and OS [7–9,11]. In contrast, our analysis did not demonstrate TTPr to be prognostic regardless of treatment types. This discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in (i) analytical methodology, (ii) diverse cancer types and treatments with wide variations in TTP1, and (iii) the rigor in data collection for TTP1.

In all prior studies, TTPr had been evaluated in relation to OS without appropriate temporal separation between the assessment of TTPr and the survival outcome. As TTPr is mathematically dominated by TTP2, any association between on-study disease control and OS may be inflated due to the shared survival time rather than treatment-related benefit, resulting in a misleading prognostic signal. In contrast, our study employed landmark survival analyses by anchoring progression status at predefined timepoints and explicitly separating the intervals used to define TTPr from subsequent OS. Landmark analysis mitigates guarantee-time bias which arises when a time-dependent variable—such as TTP2—can only be observed in patients who survive long enough to experience the defining event. In such settings, patients with longer TTPr must, by definition, survive to reach progression, artificially inducing a correlation between TTPr and OS due to shared survival time rather than true prognostic effect. Although landmark approaches necessarily exclude patients who die prior to the landmark, this restriction is intrinsic to the methodological objective of the present analysis. Specifically, because our aim was to characterize disease-response trajectories on prior therapy (TTP1) and on-study treatment (mTTP2) that inform subsequent OS, meaningful interpretation requires restriction to patients who survive long enough for disease control to be observed and quantified. Accordingly, the resulting estimates represent conditional survival effects among patients alive at the landmark, rather than unconditional treatment effects from study initiation.

TTPr is sensitive to extreme values of either TTP1 or TTP2, which can be influenced by tumor biology or relative treatment effects. TTPr may appear high when TTP1 is very short due to aggressive disease biology or in the setting of an ineffective prior line of treatment, even if absolute disease control on study is modest (e.g., TTP1 1.1 months, TTP2 3.5 months, yielding TTPr 3.3). Conversely, TTPr may be low when TTP1 and TTP2 are of similar duration, despite favorable tumor biology and prolonged disease control with the investigational therapy (e.g., TTP1 16.1 months and TTP2 11.2 months, yielding TTPr 0.70). These examples illustrate that while TTPr may capture relative improvement over prior therapy, it does not necessarily reflect absolute treatment benefit or correlate consistently with survival. Additionally, our data demonstrated that TTP1 was significantly shorter in the TTPr ≥1.3 cohort, and vice versa, and TTP1 correlated poorly with TTP2. Our findings were consistent with other studies where there is no definitive correlation in the duration of disease control between successive lines of therapy [8,12,27], this observation suggests that disease trajectory is dynamic, and challenges the assumption that TTPr adequately accounts for the natural history of cancer to enable robust intra-patient comparison.

In our analysis, TTP2 consistently demonstrated significant association with improved OS, irrespective of landmark or modeling methods. Similarly, ORR was also significantly associated with improved OS, however the small sample size limited the robustness of its interpretation. In contrast, the interpretation of TTP1 was more nuanced. TTP1 was not consistently associated with improved OS. In scenarios where both TTP1 and TTP2 were associated with improved OS, the effect size of TTP1 was consistently smaller than that of TTP2 across multiple analyses. Taken together, these data suggest that TTP1 is a less robust prognostic marker than TTP2, which consistently showed a significant association with OS, with larger effect sizes and greater reproducibility across landmark times and modeling approaches. TTP2 reflects more contemporaneous disease behavior and treatment effect, and is therefore likely to exert a greater influence on subsequent survival than the more temporally distant TTP1. This finding was also consistent across both ICI and non-ICI trials, and aligns with our prior work demonstrating that 6-month PFS is a valid surrogate for 12-month OS in ICI studies [25].

Identifying endpoints suitable for earlier read-out in single-arm studies remains challenging. Although our findings demonstrated that TTP2/PFS correlated more strongly with OS than TTP1, these endpoints are less informative in single-arm settings, where progression rates vary substantially across heterogeneous patient populations and tumor types. Further, our study was unable to definitively address the predictive performance of TTPr. Future research that aims to further determine the prognostic and predictive properties of TTPr should consider comparing the performance of TTPr against other measures of treatment activity (e.g., PFS). These are ideally performed using randomized trial data. Further, as the use of real-world data has gained significant renewed interest, high quality external synthetic control data, together with thoughtful statistical methodology on indirect comparison, may provide valuable insights on comparative effectiveness analyses and endpoint evaluation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, our analysis was conducted using single-arm studies and thus our analyses are limited to evaluation of the prognostic, but not the surrogate, properties of these endpoints. Second, the sample size of fewer than 250 participants was modest. Third, subgroup analyses restricted to a single histology or treatment type were limited by small patient numbers. Histology is known to influence survival even when treated with the same biomarker-directed therapy [28,29], a larger sample size would allow greater insight into the influence of histology on survival. Fourth, TTP1 data were collected outside clinical trial settings and retrospectively obtained prior to study enrollment, with no standardization of imaging frequency or measurement methodology. The potential inaccuracies and variability introduced may hinder TTP1’s true capability to capture disease natural history. Fifth, the upper limit of TTP2 is fixed at the respective landmarks (i.e., at 3 or 6 months), this inherently restricts the range of possible TTPr values, especially for patients with short TTP1. Finally, this analysis was subject to cohort selection bias. Clinical trial participants were more likely to have favorable prognostic features compared to the real-world population, therefore limiting the generalizability of our results. Despite these limitations, our exploratory study provides important insights into the prognostic value of TTPr and related endpoints.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the findings from our study indicate that TTPr is not prognostic once temporal bias is addressed. Its potential predictive utility in comparative treatment settings warrants further investigation in randomized trials where relative treatment effects can be evaluated. Although the lack of prognostic association raises concern regarding its validity as a surrogate endpoint, formal surrogate endpoint validation would require analyses within randomized trial settings.
