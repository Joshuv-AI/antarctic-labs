Role of Coordination Motifs and Cluster Sizes in the Dehydration Thermodynamics of MgSO4·nH2O
Authors: Belaynew Teshome, Jolla Kullgren, Ahmed Mustefa Mohammed, Girum A. Tiruye, Kersti Hermansson, Getachew G. Kebede
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dehydration of MgSO4·7H2O to anhydrous MgSO4 proceeds through nonstoichiometric or amorphous intermediates, making finite-cluster models essential for describing stepwise dehydration thermodynamics. Here, we present a cluster-based density functional theory (DFT) study of MgSO4·nH2O (n = 0–7) that directly links local coordination motifs to dehydration energetics. Low-energy cluster isomers were generated by global optimization and refined at the DFT level. Proton transfer from water to sulfate is observed in both small clusters (n = 1–2) and bulk-derived high-hydration clusters (n = 6–7) lacking direct Mg2+···SO4 2– contact. Reaction energies were calculated and equilibrium PH2O–T relationships were then constructed. Successive dehydration steps (MgSO4·7H2O ⇌ MgSO4·6H2O ⇌ lower hydrates ⇌ MgSO4) show a systematic increase in dehydration free energy with decreasing hydration, due to an increase of the ion–ion (Mg2+···SO4 2–) interactions over water···water hydrogen bonding. Monomeric clusters overestimate dehydration energies at low hydration due to undercoordinated Mg2+ centers, while dimer clusters partially restore Mg coordination and improve agreement with experimental trends. These results demonstrate that cluster-based DFT essentially captures structure–thermodynamics relationships in MgSO4 dehydration thermodynamics and clarify the strengths and limitations of the cluster approach.

Introduction

Magnesium sulfate forms a variety of hydrated phases (MgSO4·nH2O) over wide ranges of temperature (T) and relative humidity or water vapor pressure (PH2O ). The reversible dehydration–rehydration behavior of these hydrates is relevant to planetary mineralogy − and thermochemical energy storage (TES), where MgSO4·7H2O is considered a promising low-cost heat-storage material. −

Dehydration of MgSO4·7H2O proceeds through distinct stages. The first step involves loss of one water molecule to form MgSO4·6H2O between 25 and 55 °C, with a reported reaction enthalpy of Δ r H ≈ 50.2 kJ mol–1. A dominant second stage follows at ≈ 60–265 °C, during which ≈5.9 water molecules are removed, yielding an amorphous MgSO4·0.1H2O phase (Δ r H ≈ 318.9 kJ mol–1). Crystalline anhydrous MgSO4 forms at temperatures above ∼265 °C. More recent experiment performed at higher water vapor pressures (100 hPa) demonstrates that PH2O strongly influences dehydration pathways and crystal structures, yielding metastable intermediate phases with n = 4, 2.5, and 2.14 Dehydration of MgSO4·6H2O at low PH2O (<20 hPa) frequently yields amorphous and nonstoichiometric products. , Under planetary conditions, particularly those relevant to Mars, dehydration of MgSO4·7H2O leads to layered assemblages of stable monohydrated MgSO4·H2O (LH-1w, distinct from kieserite) and metastable intermediates such as MgSO4·4H2O (starkeyite). ,

In both terrestrial and planetary environments, dehydration proceeds through a sequence of crystalline-to-crystalline and crystalline-to-amorphous transformations, with pathways controlled byPH2O–T conditions. These processes can be summarized by 1 MgSO4·7H2O(cr)+heat⁣⇌MgSO4·nH2O(croram)+(7−n)H2O(g) where n < 7 and “cr” and “am” denote crystalline and amorphous intermediates, respectively. The formation of amorphous or non-stoichiometric intermediates, combined with weak or absent diffraction signatures, limits direct structural characterization. Consequently, the thermodynamics and mechanisms of MgSO4 dehydration, and particularly the roles of local Mg2+ coordination, Mg2+···SO4 2– connectivity, and hydrogen-bond networks, remain difficult to interpret.

Computational modeling, in particular density functional theory (DFT) and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, have provided insight into the structures, bonding and hydration dynamics of crystalline MgSO4 hydrates. − Previous DFT cluster studies have identified proton transfer and coordination effects in selected MgSO4·nH2O clusters, , while periodic DFT and MD investigations have explored bulk structures and pressure- or temperature-induced dehydration. ,,, However, a systematic thermodynamic description of stepwise dehydration including electronic, enthalpic, and entropic contributions and their dependence on local coordination motifs remains lacking. Such analyses are particularly challenging for periodic models due to the need for large unit cells, the presence of extensive hydrogen-bond networks, and the absence of well-defined structures for amorphous intermediates.

Cluster-based DFT modeling, which we use in this study, provides a tractable framework for isolating the local Mg–SO4–H2O coordination environments and directly link to dehydration energetics. Finite clusters can capture changes in Mg2+ coordination number, contact versus solvent-separated ion pairing, hydrogen-bond rearrangements and localized amorphous-like motifs. Within this approach, reaction enthalpies, entropies, and Gibbs free energies can be evaluated directly, enabling construction of equilibrium PH2O–T relationships and systematic analysis of structure–thermodynamics correlations. Similar strategies have successfully described dehydration trends in related hydrated salts − but have not yet been comprehensively applied to MgSO4 across a broad hydration range.

In this work, we present a cluster-based DFT study of MgSO4·nH2O (n = 0–7), combining global structure optimization with vibrational thermochemistry to investigate stepwise dehydration reactions. We focus on: (i) the cluster structure evolution, in particular evolution of Mg2+ coordination, Mg–SO4 connectivity, and hydrogen-bond networks with hydration; (ii) the thermodynamic driving forces governing individual dehydration steps and their associated equilibrium PH2O–T relations; and (iii) the role of cluster size and coordination topology in reproducing experimental dehydration trends. The results provide molecular-level insight into the structural origins of MgSO4 dehydration thermodynamics, with implications for thermochemical energy storage and planetary sulfate mineralogy.

Computational Details

All electronic structure calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) − with the optPBE-vdW-DF exchange–correlation functional. In our previous work, this functional was shown to reproduce equilibrium hydrogen-bond distances and cell volumes for ionic crystalline hydrates in good agreement with experimental data. Although its quantitative performance for dehydration thermodynamics has not been systematically benchmarked, optPBE-vdW was used here because of its reliable description of bulk hydrate structures.

Cluster Generation and Structural Optimization

Low-energy MgSO4·nH2O (n = 0–7) clusters were generated in a two-step procedure. First, approximately 30 candidate structures were generated for each hydration level using an Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) global optimization algorithm in combination with the CHARMM force field. From these candidates, 5 (for low n) to 10 (for higher n) unique low-energy structures were manually selected for DFT refinement.

Each selected cluster was placed in a cubic periodic simulation cell with at least 12 Å of vacuum along the x, y, and z directions to ensure negligible interaction between periodic images. This approach was applied consistently to all clusters from n = 0 to n = 7, as well as to dimer clusters. The largest system considered was the (MgSO4·6H2O)2 dimer. Larger aggregates would provide a more bulk-like environment but would increase the cost of DFT global refinement and vibrational thermochemistry calculations. Gas-phase H2O was treated analogously as an isolated molecule using the same vacuum separation.

Initial geometry optimizations were carried out using a plane-wave energy cutoff of 400 eV and an electronic convergence criterion of 1 × 10–5 eV. This procedure yielded the set of unique low-energy isomers shown in Figures S1 and S2. The most stable non–proton-transferred isomers (along with selected slightly higher-energy isomers for n = 6 and 7) were then reoptimized using tighter convergence criteria: a plane-wave cutoff of 520 eV, an electronic convergence threshold of 1 × 10–6 eV, and a force convergence criterion of 5 × 10–3 eV Å–1. These refined structures are shown in Figure and were used for all dehydration thermodynamic analyses.

In our cluster models, hydrated MgSO4 phases were approximated as finite supramolecular aggregates (denoted as “g” in the reaction schemes), and their thermodynamic properties were evaluated in the same manner as gas-phase molecules. Importantly, while the hydrated clusters are treated as isolated species, the water vapor pressure is the only pressure-dependent term entering the reaction free energy. This reflects the experimental situation, in which hydration–dehydration equilibria are governed by the partial pressure of H2O and its temperature dependence.

Temperature-dependent Gibbs free energies were used to construct equilibrium PH2O–T diagrams. The equilibrium vapor pressure of water was obtained from P(H2O)/p°=exp{−ΔrG(T)/[(n−m)·(RT)]} 4 where p° = 1 atm and R is the gas constant and (n–m) is the number of water molecules released. From these relations, characteristic dehydration temperature windows were identified, defined by T onset (P­(H2O) = 10–3 atm) and T offset (P­(H2O) = 1 atm). Results and Discussions

Cluster Structures

Structural Evolution with Hydration (n = 0–7)

Figure shows representative low-energy MgSO4·nH2O (n = 0–7) clusters used for the thermodynamic analysis in Section . Full sets of monomeric and dimeric isomers are given in Figures S1,S2. Monomers are labeled na, nb, nc, etc., and dimers as n(2)­a, n(2)­b, etc., in order of increasing energy. Selected dimers (n = 0, 1, 6) were included to assess finite-size and coordination effects. The selected geometric parameters are given in Table S1.

n = 0–2: Onset of Coordination and Proton Transfer

For anhydrous MgSO4 (0a), Mg2+ binds two sulfate oxygens (Mg···O = 1.86 Å), leaving two terminal oxygens. This results in two distinct S–O environments: coordinated (1.62 Å) and terminal (1.44 Å). Dimerization (0a­(2)) increases coordination to four with an average Mg···O = 2.00 Å, indicating strong stabilization from bridging sulfate ligands.

At n = 1–2, water initially forms H-bonds with sulfate (1a, 2a), but proton transfer to SO4 2– occurs under tight optimization (1b, 2b). Because such proton-transferred structures are not representative of bulk hydrates, we excluded from the dehydration thermodynamic analysis. Instead, isomers 1c & 2c were generated by rotating the H2O molecule to disrupt hydrogen bonding prior to reoptimization. In 1c, Mg center is tricoordinated with < Mg–O> = 1.93 Å, while in 2c it reaches tetracoordinated with Mg–O distances expanding to 1.97 Å. These small clusters lack extended hydrogen-bond networks and show strong local polarization, consistent with their sensitivity to proton transfer.

n = 3: Emergence of Stable Hydrogen Bonding

At n = 3 well-defined, nonproton-transferred water–sulfate hydrogen bonds first appear (3a), with an Ow–H···Os distance of 1.527 Å and angle of Ow–H···Os = 161.32°. Mg becomes five-coordinate (⟨Mg–O⟩ ≈ 2.04 Å), and sulfate oxygens are in three different environments: Mg-coordinated, H-bonded accepting and dangling with the corresponding S–O distance of 1.550, 1.503,1.437 Å and average < S–O> = 1.510 Å.

n = 4: Competition between Coordination and Hydrogen Bonding

At n = 4, structural diversity increases markedly, with competing tetra-, penta-, and hexa-coordinated Mg2+ environments. Interstingly, the lowest-energy isomer (4a) is undercoordinated (tetra-coordinated Mg) but it is stabilized by a network where two coordinated waters donate hydrogen bonds to the sulfate while two noncoordinated waters act as bridges. Higher-energy isomers increase Mg coordination to five (4b–4d) but introduce dangling sulfate oxygens or weaker H-bonding patterns. The fully hexacoordinated structure (4e) lacks hydrogen bonding and is least stable.

This balance shows that hydrogen-bond topologies are competitive with coordination numbers in determining stability. It also explains the moderate dehydration energies for n ≥ 4 in Section , where removal of outer-sphere, H-bonded water is energetically accessible.

n = 6–7: Convergence toward Octahedral Coordination

From n = 4 onward, Mg coordination approaches six, with ⟨Mg–O⟩ increasing to ∼2.10 Å (Table S1). Simultaneously, sulfate distortion decreases (lower Δd and σ2), indicating a more symmetric environment. Hydrogen bonding becomes dominant: the number of H-bonds increases (for example 5 in 6a, 6 in 6c, 8 in 7c), with typical Ow–H···O distances of ∼1.7–1.8 Å and near-linear angles.

Two coordination motifs dominate at n = 6–7: Type I (contact ion pair): Mg2+ coordinated to two sulfate oxygens (6a, 7a) and Type II (partially solvent-separated): Mg2+ coordinated to one sulfate oxygen (6c, 7c). Both retain Mg coordination number ≈6 but differ in Mg–SO4 connectivity and hydrogen-bond networks. Type I clusters have fewer but stronger ion–ion interactions, while Type II clusters show denser hydrogen-bonding networks (shorter ⟨H···O⟩, more bonds), consistent with their distinct thermodynamic behavior in Section .

A third category of clusters was generated by cleaving fragments directly from bulk MgSO4·6H2O and MgSO4·7H2O, yielding bulk-like coordination environments without direct Mg–SO4 contacts (isomers 6e and 7e in Figure S2). However, these structures underwent spontaneous proton transfer upon optimization (forming 6f, 7f and 7g) and were therefore excluded from the dehydration thermodynamic analysis.

Dimer Clusters and Coordination/Cooperative Effects

Dimer clusters partially restore bulk-like coordination and hydrogen-bond cooperativity. For example, (MgSO4·6H2O)2 (6a­(2)) features sulfate-bridged Mg centers and an extended hydrogen-bond network (13 H-bonds vs 5–6 in monomers). In the (MgSO4·6H2O)2 structure, two Mg centers are bridged by sulfate oxygens, forming a double-bridged motif in which each sulfate connects the two Mg2+ ions from opposite directions. The remaining sulfate oxygens participate in hydrogen bonding with water molecules, while additional intercluster water–water hydrogen bonds form a semicyclic network around the Mg coordination spheres. This leads to more uniform Mg–O distances and reduced structural distortion. As shown in Section , these features significantly lower dehydration enthalpies and free energies by stabilizing hydrated states and weakening individual Mg–H2O interactions.

Overall, structural evolution is governed by a balance between Mg coordination, Mg–SO4 connectivity, and hydrogen-bond networks. These factors directly control dehydration energetics and equilibrium behavior discussed in Section .

Proton Transfer in MgSO4·nH2O

We observe proton transfer from H2O to SO4 2– in both small clusters (n = 1–2; isomers 1b and 2b in Figure S1) and bulk-derived clusters at high hydration (n = 6–7; isomers 6f, 7f, and 7g in Figure S2). Two distinct proton-transfer regimes were identified.

First, in small clusters (n = 1–2), proton transfer occurs only under tight geometry optimization. Under looser convergence criteria, intact hydrogen-bonded structures (1a and 2a) persist, indicating that proton transfer in these cases is sensitive to optimization protocol.

Second, proton transfer in bulk-derived clusters (n = 6–7) occurs spontaneously and independently of optimization settings. This behavior is absent in Type I and Type II clusters that retain direct Mg–SO4 coordination. In MgSO4·7H2O, the extent of proton transfer depends on the role of the seventh water molecule: when acting as a hydrogen-bond acceptor, two protons are transferred (protonated isomer 7f), whereas as a donor, only one is (protonated isomer 7g). The lack of stabilizing ionic coordination and the presence of strong directional H-bonds promote spontaneous proton transfer in these configurations.

These findings are consistent with earlier reports: Iype et al. observed proton transfer in MgSO4·6H2O, while Fernández et al. demonstrated geometry-dependent transfer in contact versus solvent-shared ion pairs. Our results further demonstrate that proton transfer can also occur in very small clusters under strong hydrogen bonding and tight optimization. The phenomenon may also depend on the choice of exchange–correlation functional and other DFT parameters, although this aspect was not explored here. It is worth noting that the present calculations were performed for isolated clusters to represent dehydration equilibria under a water-vapor pressure. Implicit-solvent approaches such as VASPsol were not considered because they correspond to a different physical environment, namely aqueous solvation. However, dielectric stabilization from an implicit medium could influence the relative stability of solvent-separated ion pairs and proton-transferred structures, which may be useful in future investigations of hydration and proton-transfer phenomena.

Because proton-transferred structures do not correspond to experimentally observed crystalline hydrates, only non–proton-transferred clusters were used in the dehydration thermodynamics.

Thermodynamics of Dehydration Reactions

In this section, we discuss the thermodynamics of the dehydration of MgSO4·7H2O to anhydrous MgSO4. We present the calculated reaction energetics (Tables ,,), including electronic reaction energies (Δ r E, 0 K), reaction enthalpies (Δ r H), entropic contributions (−TΔ r S), and Gibbs free energies (Δ r G) at 25 °C and P­(H2O) = 1 atm, along with the corresponding PH2O–T relationships (Figures ,,) and structure–thermodynamics correlations. The predictive capabilities and the limitations of finite cluster models for describing bulk dehydration are also discussed.

Dehydration of MgSO4·7H2O to MgSO4·6H2O

The initial dehydration of magnesium sulfate heptahydrate involves the loss of one water molecule. To assess how local structure influences this process, we examined four pathways using low-energy cluster isomers (7a, 7c, 6a, and 6c from Figure ): (i) 7a ⇌ 6a, (ii) 7c ⇌ 6c, (iii) 7a ⇌ 6c, and (iv) 7c ⇌ 6a. Calculated energies are listed in Table , with corresponding equilibrium PH2O–T curves shown in Figure . Standard reactions thermodynamic data derived from standard tables (Δ r H°, −TΔ r S° and Δ r G° at 298.2 K) and available experimental values , are included for reference.

Across these pathways, calculated thermodynamic quantities span a wide range. For example, Δ r G varies from ∼0 to ∼26 kJ·mol–1, corresponding to equilibrium temperatures of ∼270–510 K. This spread indicates the impact of the Mg2+ coordination, Mg–SO4 2– connectivity and hydrogen-bond topology on dehydration energetics. Experimentally, dehydration of MgSO4·7H2O to MgSO4·6H2O occurs between 25 and 60 °C. , Reported thermodynamic parameters include Δ r H =50.2 kJ·mol–1 from thermal analysis and Δ r G = 10.13 ± 0.07 kJ·mol–1 obtained from phase boundary measurements using the humidity-buffer technique at 0.1 MPa.

Among our models, the 7a ⇌ 6a reaction pathway (Δ r H = 51.8 kJ·mol–1, Δ r G = 9.6 kJ·mol–1; onset ∼310 K) agrees the best with the experimental values. The 7c ⇌ 6c pathway is also reasonable but slightly overestimates both values. Mixed-isomer pathways deviate more: 7c ⇌ 6a underestimates Δ r G and defines the lowest-temperature boundary (∼274 K at PH2O = 10–3 atm), whereas 7a ⇌ 6c overestimates the dehydration cost and shifts equilibrium to higher temperatures.

The PH2O–T curves (Figure ) show that not all pathways match experimental phase behavior (gray shaded area in the range 25–60 °C), indicating that individual cluster reactions reflect trends rather than exact phase boundaries. The 7c ⇌ 6a reaction covers the high vapor pressure/low-temperature (left) region, 7a ⇌ 6a occupies the center and 7c ⇌ 6c aligns with the low-pressure/high-temperature (right) region. The 7a ⇌ 6c pathway lies far to the right, outside the experimental range.

Electronic and enthalpic trends are largely governed by isomer stability. For a fixed heptahydrate, differences in Δ r E and Δ r H (∼15 kJ·mol–1) reflect the energy gap between 6a and 6c; for a fixed hexahydrate, smaller differences (∼3 kJ·mol–1) arise from 7a vs 7c. Local coordination further modulates Δ r H: in 7a, Mg2+ already forms two strong Mg2+···SO4 2– contacts, weakening the departing water; in 7c, water contributes more to stabilization, making removal less favorable. Entropy depends on structural change upon dehydration. When Mg2+···SO4 2– connectivity is preserved (7a ⇌ 6a, 7c ⇌ 6c), −TΔ r S is similar (−42 to −44 kJ·mol–1). Dehydration pathways that alter connectivity (7c ⇌ 6a, 7a ⇌ 6c) show larger deviations. Δ r G reflects the balance between enthalpic cost and entropic gain. Pathways forming stronger Mg2+···SO4 2– coordination in the product (notably 6a) yield lower Δ r H and Δ r G and thus dehydrate at lower temperatures.

In bulk MgSO4·7H2O, one water molecule is not directly coordinated to Mg2+, whereas all six in MgSO4·6H2O are coordinated. The first dehydration step therefore removes interstitial water, largely preserving the Mg2+ coordination environment while shifting interactions from water–water to stronger water–ion and ion–ion interactions. The cluster results capture this trend: pathways that enhance Mg2+···SO4 2– coordination are favored, whereas those that disrupt it are disfavored and yield unrealistic equilibria.

Dehydration of MgSO4·6H2O to Anhydrous MgSO4

Stepwise Dehydration Thermodynamics

Experimentally, dehydration of MgSO4·7H2O proceeds through multiple stages, with the second stage involving MgSO4·6H2O and the formation of nonstoichiometric, partially amorphous intermediates. Thermodynamically stable crystalline hydrates have been observed experimentally primarily for n = 7, 6, and 1, while other hydration states are metastable and depend on temperature and relative humidity.

We first analyzed stepwise dehydration of MgSO4·6H2O through intermediate hydrates (Table ; Figure ). The calculated reaction free energy per released H2O increases systematically as hydration decreases (6 ⇌ 4 ⇌ 3 ⇌ 2 ⇌ 1), increasing from ∼14 kJ per mol of released H2O·for the 6 ⇌ 4 step to over 100 kJ per mol of H2O (g) for the 2 ⇌ 1 transition. Correspondingly, the PH2O–T curves shift progressively to higher temperatures. This trend is expected as the hydration number n in MgSO4·nH2O decreases, water–water interactions progressively diminish while ion–ion interactions strengthen, leading to more tightly bound remaining water and higher dehydration temperatures. In our cluster model (Figure ), water molecules in clusters with n ≥ 4 often occupy outer-sphere positions; their removal is relatively low-energy. Below n = 4, water coordinates directly to the Mg2+ inner sphere, making dehydration significantly more endothermic.

Recalculating these steps using dimer clusters, (MgSO4·nH2O)2 (n = 6, 1, 0), improved agreement. In MgSO4·1H2O, dimerization increases Mg coordination from three to five, lowering Δ r G and T onset. Although still overestimated, results are closer to experiment and would likely improve with larger aggregates. Entropy changes are similar; the improvement stems mainly from reduced enthalpy due to enhanced Mg2+···SO4 2– connectivity.

A similar effect appears for 6 ⇌ 1: Δ r G per H2O decreases from ∼39 kJ·mol–1 (monomer) to ∼11 kJ·mol–1 (dimer), shifting equilibrium to lower temperatures but deteriorating the agreement. Structurally, (MgSO4·6H2O)2 features Mg centers bridged by sulfate oxygens and extensive hydrogen bonding, placing more waters in hydrogen-bonded rather than directly coordinated environments.

These results have important implications for cluster modeling. Accurate dehydration thermodynamics requires capturing coordination topology, hydrogen-bond networks, and ion–ion connectivity. Monomer clusters reproduce trends for higher hydrates (n ≥ 4) but overbind water at low hydration, exaggerating Δ r G and T onset. Increasing cluster size restores Mg coordination toward six and introduces stabilizing hydrogen-bond networks, lowering dehydration barriers. For higher hydrates, however, larger clusters may overcorrect and reduce agreement.

Thus, results are best interpreted in terms of local coordination motifs rather than direct mapping to bulk phases. The cluster approach neglects long-range electrostatics, so quantitative deviations are expected. More rigorous treatment would require embedded clusters or periodic models to capture the Madelung field, which is beyond the scope of this study.

Conclusions

In this work, we have carried out a systematic cluster-based DFT investigation of MgSO4·nH2O (n = 0–7) to elucidate the structural evolution and thermodynamics of dehydration reactions of magnesium sulfate hydrates. By combining global structure optimization, vibrational thermochemistry and equilibrium PH2O–T analysis, we establish links between local coordination motifs, hydrogen-bond topology, and dehydration energetics.

Structurally, the clusters exhibit a progressive increase in Mg2+ coordination number with hydration. Direct Mg–SO4 contact ion pairing persists in most low-energy clusters, even at high hydration, reflecting the absence of long-range electrostatic stabilization present in bulk crystals. Proton transfer from water to sulfate is observed in small clusters (n = 1–2) and in bulk-cleaved high-hydration clusters lacking direct Mg–SO4 coordination. Because such proton-transferred structures are not representative of experimentally observed crystalline hydrates, only non–proton-transferred clusters were used for dehydration thermodynamics.

Our thermodynamic analysis in the first dehydration step (MgSO4·7H2O ⇌ MgSO4·6H2O) shows that cluster pathways that strengthen Mg–SO4 interactions upon dehydration give lower reaction energetics and equilibrium dehydration temperatures, whereas pathways that disrupt Mg–SO4 connectivity lead to unrealistic reaction thermodyanmics. This highlights the importance of selecting physically meaningful local motifs when using finite clusters to model bulk dehydration behavior.

For subsequent dehydration steps, the calculated free energy per released H2O increases systematically as hydration decreases, consistent with the progressive replacement of water–water hydrogen bonding by stronger Mg–SO4 ion–ion interactions. Monomeric clusters reproduce experimental trends reasonably well for higher hydrates (n ≥ 4), but significantly overestimate dehydration energies for low-hydration states due to undercoordinated Mg2+ centers. Introducing dimer clusters partially restores Mg coordination and cooperative hydrogen bonding and then improving dehydration energetics and equilibrium temperatures for 1 ⇌ 0 reactions.

We conclude that dehydration thermodynamics of MgSO4 hydrates is governed primarily by local coordination topology rather than hydration number alone. While finite cluster models neglect long-range periodic interactions and therefore cannot reproduce absolute bulk phase boundaries, they provide a physically meaningful framework for identifying structure–thermodynamics relationships and dehydration trends. These insights are directly relevant to the interpretation of dehydration experiments under controlled PH2O–T conditions, to the design of MgSO4-based thermochemical energy storage materials and to understanding the stability of sulfate hydrates in planetary environments.
