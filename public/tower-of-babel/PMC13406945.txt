Toward Structured Transparency: A Governance Framework for AI Use in Biomedical Publishing
Authors: Jong-Min Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid adoption of large language models and generative artificial intelligence (AI) is transforming biomedical research and publishing. Although international organizations such as the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) and the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) have established the principle that AI cannot be recognized as an author, and the ICMJE, in its January 2026 revision, has introduced a dedicated section addressing AI use by authors, peer reviewers, and editors, journal-level Instructions for Authors still require further operational detail to apply these principles consistently throughout the publication process. This review critically examines current AI-related policies in the Journal of Korean Medical Science (JKMS) and the Korean Association of Medical Journal Editors (KAMJE) and identifies three major challenges: the practical limitations of the AI non-authorship principle, the inadequacy of current AI disclosure practices, and the emergence of AI-specific conflicts of interest that extends beyond conventional financial disclosures. We argue that medical publishing should move from a restrictive approach toward a framework of structured transparency that systematically documents, evaluates, and verifies AI use. To achieve this goal, we propose a practical governance framework that includes a three-tiered AI disclosure system, strengthened accountability for corresponding authors, expanded institutional conflict-of-interest disclosures, transparent reporting of AI use by peer reviewers, and formal editorial policies governing AI-assisted editorial activities. Future revisions of the KAMJE and JKMS Instructions for Authors should prioritize transparent and accountable AI governance rather than restricting AI use. Adoption of a structured, publication-wide framework encompassing authors, peer reviewers, and editors would strengthen research integrity while supporting the responsible integration of AI into biomedical publishing.

INTRODUCTION

The rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) and generative artificial intelligence (AI) is transforming medical research and academic writing. From literature retrieval and data synthesis to manuscript drafting, these technologies offer unprecedented opportunities to improve research efficiency. At the same time, their widespread adoption raises fundamental concerns regarding authorship, transparency, research integrity, and conflicts of interest (COI), challenging long-established principles of scientific publishing.

International organizations that establish standards for scholarly publishing, including the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) and the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE), have reached a clear consensus that AI cannot be recognized as an author because it cannot assume legal or ethical responsibility for the content of a scientific manuscript.12 In Republic of Korea (= South Korea), the Korean Association of Medical Journal Editors (KAMJE) and the Journal of Korean Medical Science (JKMS), the official journal of the Korean Academy of Medical Sciences, have likewise incorporated these principles into their editorial policies.3

Although these recommendations provide an important ethical foundation, current Instructions for Authors in most medical journals remain largely principle-based and, until recently, offered limited operational guidance on the appropriate use of AI throughout the research and publication process. In its January 2026 revision, the ICMJE added a dedicated section on the use of AI in publishing that separately addresses authors, peer reviewers, and editors, establishing that nondisclosure may, in some circumstances, be treated as misconduct and that journals should maintain a policy on the use of AI in the review process. These provisions supply an essential ethical and procedural foundation; the outstanding task is therefore not to fill a normative vacuum but to translate these principles into operational detail suited to the local publishing environment. Consequently, editorial decisions regarding AI-assisted research are often made on a case-by-case basis rather than through standardized and transparent criteria. As generative AI continues to evolve from general-purpose conversational models to specialized institutional and domain-specific systems, journals require more detailed guidance that clearly defines acceptable AI use across different stages of research and manuscript preparation. For example, a brief acknowledgment stating that “AI assistance” was used provides little information about the extent or nature of AI involvement and is insufficient for meaningful editorial assessment or peer review. These limitations highlight the need to reconsider how peer review should evaluate AI-assisted research.

The primary objectives of peer review are to assess the originality, scientific validity, methodological rigor, and potential contribution of a study. Traditionally, reviewers have focused on identifying errors arising from human judgment, methodological weaknesses, or inappropriate interpretation of results. With the increasing integration of AI into scientific research, however, reviewers must also evaluate whether AI-assisted outputs have been appropriately verified and whether any inaccuracies, hallucinations, or algorithmic biases have influenced the scientific conclusions. Likewise, journal editors must oversee not only authors’ responsible use of AI but also the appropriate use of AI by peer reviewers during the evaluation process.

In this review, we examine the current AI-related Instructions for Authors adopted by JKMS and propose a practical framework for future guideline development. Our recommendations are intended to strengthen editorial accountability, standardize AI disclosure, and expand existing COI policies to address AI-specific ethical challenges. Rather than focusing solely on authors, the proposed framework encompasses the entire publication process—including authors, peer reviewers, and journal editors—and outlines the minimum governance standards required to ensure the transparent and responsible integration of AI into biomedical publishing.

ANALYSIS OF CURRENT GUIDELINES AND EMERGING LIMITATIONS

Practical limitations of the AI non-authorship principle

The principle that AI cannot be recognized as an author has become widely accepted in scholarly publishing. However, translating this principle into day-to-day research and publishing practices remains challenging. As AI becomes increasingly integrated into scientific workflows, the distinction between technical assistance and substantive intellectual contribution is becoming progressively less clear, raising important questions about the conventional definition of authorship.

For example, an LLM may substantially reorganize the logical structure of a Discussion section, synthesize evidence for a systematic review, or generate analytical code. In such situations, the role of the human researcher may shift from that of the primary creator of scholarly content to that of a reviewer, editor, and supervisor of AI-generated outputs. Nevertheless, current editorial policies provide little guidance on the threshold at which AI involvement begins to compromise meaningful human intellectual contribution. The absence of operational criteria inevitably leads to inconsistent interpretations of acceptable AI use and authorship eligibility across journals. Future guidelines should therefore move beyond the simple assertion that AI cannot be an author and instead define practical boundaries for acceptable AI-assisted research.

Limitations of current AI disclosure practices

Most biomedical journals now require authors to disclose whether AI tools were used during research or manuscript preparation, typically through metadata fields or statements in the Acknowledgments section. Although these policies represent an important step toward greater transparency, they remain overly simplistic. A binary declaration indicating whether AI was “used” or “not used” fails to convey the purpose, extent, and significance of AI involvement throughout the research process.

Current disclosure practices also fail to distinguish between fundamentally different applications of AI. Routine language editing or grammatical correction is treated equivalently to far more substantive uses, such as literature synthesis, statistical code generation, data interpretation, or scientific writing. These activities differ considerably in both their intellectual contribution and their potential influence on research outcomes.

Moreover, without standardized reporting requirements, peer reviewers cannot adequately evaluate whether AI-generated content has been independently verified or whether potential hallucinations, algorithmic bias, or factual inaccuracies have been appropriately addressed. Consequently, existing disclosure policies promote transparency only at a superficial level and provide insufficient information for assessing the reliability and scientific integrity of AI-assisted research. A more structured and standardized reporting framework is therefore needed to document both the purpose and extent of AI utilization.

Expanding the concept of conflicts of interest in the AI era

Historically, COIs have been defined primarily in terms of direct financial relationships, including research funding, consulting fees, honoraria, or equity ownership. The widespread adoption of generative AI, however, introduces new forms of competing interests that extend beyond traditional financial disclosures and are not adequately addressed by existing COI frameworks. This topic is also described in a very detailed and extensive manner in the previous paper of this special issue.4

Proprietary AI models and institutional conflicts of interest

Healthcare institutions are increasingly developing proprietary LLMs through collaborations with commercial technology companies. Consequently, investigators may evaluate AI systems that were developed, funded, or deployed by their own institutions. In these circumstances, institutional affiliations may introduce competing interests that extend beyond the personal financial interests of individual researchers. Such relationships have the potential to influence study design, data interpretation, and reporting, yet they are seldom captured by conventional COI disclosure forms. Future disclosure policies should therefore recognize institutional ownership, development, and commercial partnerships involving AI systems as potential sources of COI.

Data governance, privacy, and indirect economic interests

The use of generative AI also raises important concerns regarding data governance. Uploading proprietary patient information or unpublished clinical research data to commercial AI platforms may violate applicable data protection regulations, including the Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA) in Korea, while simultaneously jeopardizing intellectual property rights and research confidentiality.

In addition, when proprietary datasets are incorporated into commercial AI systems, they may contribute to the continued development and commercial value of those models, even in the absence of direct financial compensation to researchers or their institutions. Although these indirect exchanges of value are rarely recognized as financial relationships, they nevertheless represent potential competing interests that may influence research conduct and should be considered within future COI disclosure frameworks.

DIRECTIVES FOR GUIDELINE REFORM: TOWARD A FRAMEWORK OF STRUCTURED TRANSPARENCY

Medical publishing policies should move beyond simply restricting the use of AI and instead adopt a framework of structured transparency that enables the systematic documentation, evaluation, and verification of AI use throughout the research and publication process. The central challenge is not the use of AI itself, but the absence of transparent and traceable records describing how AI contributed to scientific work.

As LLMs assume increasingly substantive roles in manuscript preparation, the chain of accountability for scientific content becomes more difficult to establish. Without adequate documentation of AI involvement, reviewers and editors may be unable to determine whether questionable findings, unsupported interpretations, or factual inaccuracies originated from human judgment or AI-generated outputs. Consequently, meaningful oversight depends not only on disclosure of AI use but also on sufficient information to evaluate its scope, purpose, and potential influence on the scientific conclusions. This topic is also discussed in a very detailed and extensive manner in the previous paper of this special issue.5

Guideline development should also take into account the diversity of the global research community. Although international recommendations, including those of the ICMJE, provide an essential ethical foundation, their implementation should be adapted to local publishing environments. For many researchers whose first language is not English, LLMs have become valuable tools for overcoming linguistic barriers in an English-dominated scientific publishing system. Excessively restrictive policies or burdensome reporting requirements may therefore create unintended disadvantages for these researchers and inadvertently undermine equity in international scholarly communication.

Accordingly, future revisions of the KAMJE and JKMS Instructions for Authors should emphasize responsible and transparent AI use rather than its prohibition. Clear, practical guidance should define acceptable levels of AI involvement while preserving human accountability for all scientific content. At the same time, journals should establish explicit policies for addressing intentional nondisclosure, inaccurate reporting, or misuse of AI, thereby reinforcing both transparency and accountability throughout the publication process.

RECOMMENDATIONS

Implement a three-tiered AI disclosure framework

To strengthen transparency while preserving scientific integrity, JKMS and KAMJE should adopt a structured three-tiered framework for reporting AI use in future revisions of their Instructions for Authors. Rather than relying on binary declarations or free-text descriptions, journals should incorporate a standardized AI disclosure matrix into the manuscript submission system. AI use should be classified according to both its purpose and its level of influence on the research and manuscript preparation process. This topic is also fully discussed in the previous paper of this special issue.6 Now, we propose the following guidelines for each tier.

Tier 1: Assistive (functional and linguistic support)

Scope: Routine language-related tasks, including grammar correction, translation, copy-editing, and formatting.

Requirement: Authors should disclose these activities in the Acknowledgments section using a standardized disclosure statement.

Tier 2: Augmentative (structural and analytical assistance)

Scope: AI-assisted drafting of manuscript sections, literature synthesis, statistical programming, or other analytical support.

Requirement: Authors should describe these activities, following the established distinction adopted by the ICMJE and major journals: AI-assisted drafting of manuscript text should be reported in the Acknowledgments section, whereas AI used for data collection, analysis, or figure generation should be described in the Methods section. In either case, authors should specify the AI model and version used, the purpose of the prompts, and the dates of access. Sufficient information should be provided to enable editors and reviewers to understand the scope of AI involvement.

Tier 3: Substantive (core scientific contribution)

Scope: Use of AI as a substitute for human intellectual authorship—that is, cases in which scientific hypotheses, interpretations of study findings, or scientific arguments are generated and adopted without independent human verification. This category does not include the legitimate use of AI as a research tool (for example, generating candidate hypotheses that are subsequently tested against independent data, or evaluating the performance of an AI method itself), which constitutes ordinary human-supervised science and should be reported in the Methods section under Tier 2.

Requirement: Because such use is fundamentally incompatible with current authorship standards, it should not be permitted under routine editorial practice. Any exceptional circumstances should require formal editorial review and clear evidence that all AI-generated content has been independently verified by the authors. Manuscripts lacking adequate human oversight should not be considered for publication.

Strengthen corresponding author accountability

Corresponding authors should formally certify that all AI-assisted content has been independently reviewed and verified before submission. We recommend incorporating an Accountability Clause into the copyright transfer and author agreement, affirming that the authors have verified all AI-generated references, analyses, interpretations, and scientific arguments against their original sources. The agreement should further state that the authors retain full scientific, ethical, and legal responsibility for the accuracy and integrity of all published content, regardless of whether AI tools were used during its preparation.

Expand COI disclosure to include institutional AI interests

Current COI disclosure policies should be expanded beyond traditional financial relationships to encompass AI-related institutional interests. Authors should disclose whether the AI systems used in the study were developed, funded, licensed, or operated by their own institution or by organizations with a financial or commercial interest in the study outcomes.

Disclosure should also include relevant collaborations, licensing arrangements, or data-sharing agreements between research institutions and AI service providers whenever these relationships could reasonably influence the conduct, interpretation, or reporting of the research.

Require transparent disclosure of AI use by peer reviewers

Journal policies should explicitly permit the responsible use of AI by peer reviewers while requiring transparent disclosure of such use. A tiered reporting framework analogous to that used for authors can be applied.

Tier 1: Assistive (functional and linguistic support)

Scope: Language editing and stylistic refinement of reviewers’ comments.

Tier 2: Augmentative (structural and analytical assistance)

Scope: Drafting review comments, checking references, summarizing manuscripts, or reanalyzing statistical methods.

Requirement: Reviewers should disclose these activities when submitting their evaluations. Consistent with the ICMJE recommendation on the use of AI by reviewers, reviewers must maintain the confidentiality of the manuscript, which may prohibit the uploading of confidential manuscripts, unpublished datasets, or identifiable research materials to software or other AI technologies where confidentiality cannot be assured, unless such use is explicitly permitted by the journal. Where reviewers rely on AI for the activities in this tier, they should do so only within environments in which confidentiality is contractually or technically guaranteed. Reviewers should also confirm that confidentiality requirements have been fully maintained.

Tier 3: Substantive (core scientific evaluation)

Scope: AI-generated peer-review reports or AI-based editorial recommendations.

Requirement: Such use should be prohibited because scientific judgment and editorial evaluation remain the responsibility of qualified human reviewers.

To facilitate compliance, reviewer submission systems should include a standardized checklist that allows reviewers to report AI use in a consistent and transparent manner.

Establish institutional policies for editorial AI use

Editors may appropriately use AI tools to improve editorial efficiency, including summarizing reviewer comments, checking references, identifying inconsistencies across manuscript revisions, and supporting administrative editorial tasks. However, editorial boards should establish formal internal policies defining the permissible scope of AI use and ensuring that editorial decision-making remains under direct human oversight.

CONCLUSION

The responsible integration of generative AI into biomedical publishing requires governance frameworks that prioritize transparency, accountability, and scientific integrity rather than simply restricting AI use. We propose a practical framework that extends across the entire publication lifecycle—from authors and peer reviewers to journal editors—and addresses emerging challenges in AI disclosure, authorship, and COIs. Adoption of this framework by JKMS and KAMJE could not only strengthen editorial governance within Korean medical publishing but also provide a practical reference for biomedical journals worldwide as they adapt to the rapidly evolving AI era. Ultimately, the challenge is not whether AI should be used in biomedical research, but whether its use is sufficiently transparent, traceable, and accountable to preserve scientific integrity.
