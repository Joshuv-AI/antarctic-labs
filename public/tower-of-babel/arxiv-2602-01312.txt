Imperfect Influence, Preserved Rankings: A Theory of TRAK for Data Attribution
Han Tong, Shubhangi Ghosh, Haolin Zou, Arian Maleki

Abstract
Data attribution, tracing a model’s prediction back to specific training data, is an important tool for interpreting sophisticated AI models. The widely used TRAK algorithm addresses this challenge by first approximating the underlying model with a kernel machine and then leveraging techniques developed for approximating the leave-one-out (ALO) risk. Despite its strong empirical performance, the theoretical conditions under which the TRAK approximations are accurate as well as the regimes in which they break down remain largely unexplored. In this paper, we provide a theoretical analysis of the TRAK algorithm, characterizing its performance and quantifying the errors introduced by the approximations on which the method relies. We show that although the approximations incur significant errors, TRAK’s estimated influence remains highly correlated with the original influence and therefore largely preserves the relative ranking of data points. We corroborate our theoretical results through extensive simulations and empirical studies.

Introduction

1.1
Related work and contributions

Modern machine learning systems, including large language models, rely heavily on massive training datasets, where data quality significantly impacts model performance. This dependence has motivated growing interest in
data attribution
, the task of tracing a model’s predictions back to individual training samples, to better understand model behavior, identify harmful or redundant data, and improve data efficiency.

A popular class of data attribution methods is based on counterfactual reasoning: asking how a model’s prediction would change if a particular training point were removed or reweighted. Popular approaches rely on quantities such as the Shapley value
(
11
;
4
;
18
)
and influence functions
(
8
;
16
;
21
;
9
;
6
;
10
;
14
;
5
;
7
)
, whose computation is often prohibitively expensive or even intractable for modern AI models. Recently, the TRAK algorithm
(
14
)
was proposed as a scalable data attribution method tailored to modern neural networks and has since gained popularity
(
20
;
19
;
7
)
. Its computational efficiency stems from three key approximations of the influence functions: linearization, random projection, and Approximate-Leave-one-Out (ALO) corrections
(
15
;
2
)
. TRAK has demonstrated strong empirical performance on large models and datasets.

Despite its practical success, little is known about the theoretical conditions under which TRAK provides accurate attributions, or about the error introduced by its multiple approximation steps. This lack of theoretical understanding limits our ability to determine when and for what tasks TRAK can be reliably used, and what information is lost by its approximations.

This paper aims to close this gap.
We provide the first systematic theoretical analysis of TRAK by evaluating the errors each approximation step of TRAK introduces. To give a high-level overview of our contributions, let
\mathcal{I}^{\rm True}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new})
denote the true influence of datapoint
\mathbf{z}_{i}
on a test data point
\mathbf{z}_{\rm new}

(
13
)
. This quantity will be defined more formally in (
2
). Furthermore, let
\mathcal{I}^{\rm TRAK}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new})
denote the TRAK approximation of
\mathcal{I}^{\rm True}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new})
.

Our analysis reveals the following facts about TRAK:

1.

Negative result:
While the error introduced by the ALO approximation is typically small and benign, both the linearization step and the projection step, especially when the number of projections is much smaller than the number of parameters, can introduce substantial errors. As a result, the exact ranking of training data points induced by
\mathcal{I}^{\rm TRAK}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new})
can differ significantly from that induced by
\mathcal{I}^{\rm True}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new})
.

2.

Positive result:
Despite the large errors TRAK introduces, it can detect datapoints that have large influences from the ones that have small influences. Informally speaking, if the influence of datapoint
\mathbf{z}_{i}
, i.e.
\mathcal{I}^{\rm True}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new})
is much larger than the
\mathcal{I}^{\rm True}(\mathbf{z}_{j},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new})
, then the
\mathcal{I}^{\rm TRAK}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new})
remains much larger than
\mathcal{I}^{\rm True}(\mathbf{z}_{j},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new})
as well. Hence, when the objective is to identify the most influential data points, TRAK provides accurate and reliable information.

1.2
TRAK Influence function

In this section, we formally present the three approximations employed by the TRAK algorithm. In the next section, we study the errors introduced by each approximation step to shed light on the performance of TRAK.
Consider the standard supervised learning setting, in which we are given a dataset

\mathcal{D}=\{(y_{1},\mathbf{x}_{1}),(y_{2},\mathbf{x}_{2}),\ldots,(y_{n},\mathbf{x}_{n})\},

wherre
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
represents the response, and
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes the corresponding feature vector. The goal is to learn a predictor that, given a new feature vector
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}}
, can accurately predict its associated response. Many successful approaches in AI and machine learning, including generalized linear models and neural networks, use predictors of the form
f(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})
, where
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are parameters (to be learned from data), and
f(\cdot;\bm{\beta})
is a linear or nonlinear function that produces the predictions. In most applications, the model parameters are estimated using empirical risk minimization:

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{\beta}}\triangleq\underset{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\arg\min}\Biggl\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}))\Biggr\},

(1)

where
\ell(y,f(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta}))
denotes the loss function, such as the squared loss or cross-entropy loss, depending on the application and the nature of the response variable.

To interpret model predictions, many data attribution techniques seek to quantify the influence of individual training samples on a model’s output. Specifically, the influence of a training datapoint
\mathbf{z}_{i}=(y_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})
on the model’s prediction at a new datapoint
\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}}=(y_{\mathrm{new}},\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}})
is defined as

\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{True}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}})\triangleq f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}};\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i})-f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}};\bm{\hat{\beta}}).

(2)

where

\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}\triangleq\arg\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\sum_{j\neq i}\ell\bigl(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j};\bm{\beta})\bigr)\right\}.

(3)

A fundamental challenge in applying (
2
) for data valuation is its prohibitive computational cost. Computing the influence of a single training sample requires solving the leave-one-out optimization problem in (
3
). In modern machine learning and AI applications, where both the sample size
n
and the model dimension
d
are large, the exact computation of
\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}
becomes computationally prohibitive, especially when it must be performed repeatedly for many training data points. The
TRAK
algorithm mitigates this issue by approximating
\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}
using the following sequence of steps:

Step 1: Linearization.

The first step in TRAK is to linearly approximate
f(\mathbf{x},\bm{\beta})
around
\bm{\hat{\beta}}
. Define

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{j}

\displaystyle=\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\hat{\beta}}),

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{j}

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\hat{\beta}})-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\hat{\beta}})^{\top}\bm{\hat{\beta}}.

Instead of solving the nonlinear optimization problem (
3
), TRAK solves the following linearized version:

\displaystyle\breve{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\arg\min}\Biggl\{\sum_{j\neq i}\ell\big(y_{j},\mathbf{g}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}+\mathbf{b}_{j}\big)\Biggr\}.

(4)

The intuition is that the linear approximation of
f(\mathbf{x},\bm{\beta})
around
\bm{\hat{\beta}}
provides a sufficiently accurate surrogate, and the corresponding optimization problem changes only slightly. As a result, we can use the linear function of
\{\mathbf{g}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
as an approximation of
f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\beta})
when
\bm{\beta}
is close to
\bm{\hat{\beta}}
.

Step 2: Random projection.

To further reduce computational cost, TRAK applies random projection to the gradients
\mathbf{g}_{i}
. The idea is to reduce dimensionality while approximately preserving inner products. More specifically, we define the gradient matrix used in the TRAK algorithm as:

\mathbf{G}(\bm{\beta}):=\big(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\bm{\beta}),\dots,\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{n},\bm{\beta})\big)^{\top},

(5)

and for notational simiplicty, we write
\mathbf{G}:=\mathbf{G}(\bm{\hat{\beta}})
.
Using a random projection matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times k}
with
k\ll p
, TRAK defines the feature map
\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})
, where
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla f(\mathbf{x},\bm{\hat{\beta}})
. Accordingly, we define
\Phi=(\bm{\phi}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\phi}_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
, where
\bm{\phi}_{i}=\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{g}_{i}
and
\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{new}}=\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new}}
.

Step 3: Approximate Leave-One-Out (ALO).

Building on the ideas of approximate leave-one-out
15
;
3
, TRAK estimates the influence via the ALO formula
1
1

1

In the TRAK paper, the authors note that replacing
\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits[\bm{\ddot{\ell}}(\bm{\breve{\beta}})]
with the identity matrix and ignoring the denominator in (
6
) does not significantly affect empirical performance. As a result, they use the simplified expression

\dot{\ell}_{i}(\bm{\breve{\beta}})\,\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{new}}^{\top}\bigl(\Phi^{\top}\Phi\bigr)^{-1}\bm{\phi}_{i}.

However, for theoretical analysis, we will consider the complete form in (
6
).
, which provides the following approximation for
\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{True}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}})
:

\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{TRAK}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}};k)\\
\triangleq\frac{\dot{\ell}_{i}(\bm{\breve{\beta}})\,\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{new}}^{\top}\bigl(\Phi^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits[\bm{\ddot{\ell}}(\bm{\breve{\beta}})]\Phi\bigr)^{-1}\bm{\phi}_{i}}{1-\ddot{\ell}_{i}(\bm{\breve{\beta}})\,\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\top}\bigl(\Phi^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits[\bm{\ddot{\ell}}(\bm{\breve{\beta}})]\Phi\bigr)^{-1}\bm{\phi}_{i}},

(6)

where
k
is the projection dimension,
\dot{\ell}(y,z)
and
\ddot{\ell}(y,z)
denote the first and second order derivative of the loss function with respect to
z
respectively,
\dot{\ell}_{i}(\bm{\breve{\beta}})=\dot{\ell}(y_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\breve{\beta}})
, and
\bm{\dot{\ell}}(\bm{\breve{\beta}})=[\dot{\ell}_{1}(\bm{\breve{\beta}}),\dot{\ell}_{2}(\bm{\breve{\beta}}),\ldots,\dot{\ell}_{n}(\bm{\breve{\beta}})]^{\top}
. Finally,
\bm{\breve{\beta}}
is defined by

\breve{\bm{\beta}}=\underset{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\arg\min}\Biggl\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell\big(y_{i},\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}+\mathbf{b}_{i}\big)\Biggr\}.

(7)

We should also note that TRAK includes two additional steps: (i) Ensemble averaging, which mitigates variability arising from random initialization and the inherent randomness of optimization algorithms used to find minima of the training loss, and (ii) the application of soft thresholding to the influence estimates, introducing shrinkage under the assumption that only a small subset of training points meaningfully affects the prediction for a given test example. However, since we only study the accuracy of approximations involved in TRAK, we skip these two steps.

Main theoretical contributions

2.1
Our theoretical framework

Throughout the theoretial part of the paper we assume that the elements of
\mathcal{D}
are independent draws from the joint distribution

(y_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})\sim q(y_{i}\mid f(\mathbf{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*}))\,p(\mathbf{x}_{i}).

(8)

Here,
\bm{\beta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the true parameter vector. Unlike classical linear models, the parameter dimension
d
may differ from the feature dimension
p
; for example, in neural networks,
d
corresponds to the total number of weights.

We define the loss function as the negative log-likelihood

\ell(y,f(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta}))=-\log q(y\mid f(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})).

Motivated by modern machine learning and AI applications, where the models have billions or even trillions of parameters and are trained on gigabytes of date, in our theoretical studies we focus on the regime that the sample size
n
, the number of features
p
, and number of parameters
d
are all large. In other words, in our analysis we will ignore the errors that are negligible for large values of
n,p
and
d
.

In addition to this assumption, to characterize the scaling behavior of the approximation errors introduced by TRAK, we impose the following structural assumptions. Their precise formal statements are deferred to Appendix
B.1.2
.

Assumption 2.1

(Data and Model Regularity)
.

We make the following structural assumptions throughout the paper.

(A1) Sub-Gaussian design.

Let
\bm{X}_{n}
denote the matrix with rows
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}

The rows of
\bm{X}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
are mean-zero sub-Gaussian random vectors with covariance matrix

. Moreover, there exist constants

such that

\frac{c}{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}\;\leq\;\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma}_{p})\;\leq\;\lambda_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma}_{p})\;\leq\;\frac{C}{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}.

(A2) Model and loss regularity.

In addition to assumptions about feature vectors we require some regularity conditions on the loss function and
f(\cdot,\cdot)
. Since the precise technical statements of these assumptions require additional space, we provide a brief and informal description here and defer the exact definitions to the supplementary material.

\bullet
The norm of
\nabla f(\mathbf{x},\bm{\beta}^{*})
is sub-Gaussian with a bounded second-moment. See Assumptions
B.2
and
B.7
for formal statement.

\bullet
The Hessian of the empirical risk in (
1
) and (
7

\bm{\hat{\beta}}
. See Assumptions
B.3
and
B.4
for formal statement.

\bullet
The loss
\ell

B.5
for the formal definition.

In the online supplement, we provide additional intuition for the chosen scalings and discuss why the underlying assumptions are satisfied. For instance, we verify in Appendix

2.2.3
Linearization step

In this section, we aim to understand the error that is introduced in the influence function because of the linearization step of TRAK. If the projection and ALO are removed from the TRAK, then the approximate influence function is given by

\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{Linear}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}})\triangleq\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new}}^{\top}(\bm{\breve{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\breve{\beta}}),

\mathbf{z}_{\rm new}
, Theorem
2.9
implies that
\mathcal{I}^{\rm Linear}(\mathbf{z}_{j},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new})
is of much smaller order. As a result, even though the TRAK approximation may incur substantial absolute error, it can still reliably distinguish data points with large true influence
\mathcal{I}^{\rm True}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new})
from those with negligible influence. This is consistent with the “positive result” mentioned in the introduction.

2.2.4
ALO step

In this section, we characterize the error introduced by the ALO approximation. If we ignore the projection step of the TRAK method, the influence function produced by TRAK reduces to

\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{ALO}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}})\triangleq\frac{\dot{\ell}_{i}(\bm{\breve{\beta}})\,\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new}}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{g}_{i}}{1-\ddot{\ell}_{i}(\bm{\breve{\beta}})\,\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{g}_{i}},

(13)

where
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{G}^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits[\bm{\ddot{\ell}}(\bm{\breve{\beta}})]\mathbf{G}
,
\dot{\ell}_{i}(\bm{\beta})=\dot{\ell}(y_{i},\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}+\mathbf{b}_{i})
and
\ddot{\ell}_{i}(\bm{\beta})=\ddot{\ell}(y_{i},\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}+\mathbf{b}_{i})
. In this section we would like to characterize the difference between
\mathcal{I}^{\rm ALO}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new})-\mathcal{I}^{\rm Linear}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new})
. Our first theorem shows that in case
\mathbf{z}_{\rm new}
has strong correlation with
\mathbf{z}_{i}
, the error is much smaller than
\mathcal{I}^{\rm Linear}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new})
. Hence, the error of this step is negligible.

Theorem 2.12
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
(formally, Assumptions
B.1
–
B.7
in the Appendix), for any
\epsilon>0
such that
\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}\leq n^{1-\epsilon}
, there exists an absolute constant
\tilde{C}^{\rm{ALO}}
such that, with probability tending to
1
, the ALO step error satisfies:

\begin{split}\sup_{i\in[n]}\biggl|&\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{Linear}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i})-\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{ALO}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i})\biggr|\leq\tilde{C}^{\rm ALO}\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}{n^{1.5-\epsilon}}.\end{split}

The proof of Theorem
2.12
is postponed to Appendix
B.2.5
.

It turns out that the error introduced by the ALO step is negligible even in the cases where
{\bm{z}}_{\rm new}
is independent of
\bm{z}_{i}
.

Theorem 2.13
.

Let
\mathbf{z}_{\rm new}
be independent of the dataset
\mathbf{z}_{i}
. Under Assumptions
2.1
(formally, Assumptions
B.1
–
B.7
in the Appendix), for any
\epsilon>0
such that
\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}\leq n^{1-\epsilon}
, there exists an absolute constant
C^{\rm{ALO}}
such that, with probability tending to
1
, the ALO step error satisfies:

\begin{split}\sup_{i\in[n]}\biggl|&\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{Linear}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}})-\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{ALO}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}})\biggr|\leq C^{\rm ALO}\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|}{n^{1.5-\epsilon}}.\end{split}

The proof of Theorem
2.13
is postponed to Appendix
B.2.6
.

Combining the above two theorems we can conclude that in both cases, we have

\frac{\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{ALO}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}})}{\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{Linear}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}})}=1+o_{p}(1.)

Hence, the error introduced by the ALO step is much smaller than the magnitude of the influence, and is therefore negligible.

2.2.5
Projection step

Finally, in this section, we study the impact of the projection step on the accuracy of the TRAK algorithm..

Theorem 2.14
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
(formally, Assumptions
B.1
–
B.7
in the Appendix), if
\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}}
and
\mathbf{z}_{i}
are independent, then for any
\epsilon>0
such that
\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}\leq n^{1-\epsilon}
, there exists absolute constant
C^{\rm TRAK}
such that, with probability tending to
1
, the projection error satisfies

\bigl|\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{TRAK}}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}},\mathbf{z}_{i};k)\bigr|\;\leq\;C^{\rm TRAK}\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|\sqrt{k}}{n^{1-\epsilon}\sqrt{d}}.

Furthermore, for
\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}}=\mathbf{z}_{i}
, there is an absolute constant
c^{\rm TRAK}
such that with probability tending to
1
,

\biggl|\frac{\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{TRAK}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i};k)}{\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{ALO}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i};k)}\biggr|\;\geq\;c^{\rm TRAK}\frac{k}{d}.

The proof of this result is presented in Section
B.2.7
of the Appendix.

Remark 2.15
.

Note that according to
proposition

2.4

\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{ALO}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i};k)\geq c\,\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}{n}
for some absolute constant
c>0
. Consequently,

\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{TRAK}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i};k)\;\geq\;c\,\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}\,k}{nd}.

The key question is whether this influence is larger than
\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{TRAK}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\rm new};k)
in the case where
\mathbf{z}_{\rm new}
is independent of
\mathbf{z}_{i}
. If so, this separation allows us to reliably distinguish data points that are strongly dependent on
\mathbf{z}_{\rm new}
, and therefore meaningfully affect the model’s prediction at
\mathbf{x}_{\rm new}
, from those that are effectively independent of
\mathbf{z}_{\rm new}
.

In Appendix
Figure

12
, we further show that the obtained order is sharp. Thus according to Theorem
2.14
, if
\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|\sqrt{k/d}\ll 1
, then
\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{TRAK}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i};k)
can become smaller than
\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{TRAK}}(\mathbf{z}_{\rm new},\mathbf{z}_{i};k)
for an independent
\mathbf{z}_{\rm new}
. Hence, the TRAK method may fail to reliably detect influential data points. This observation implies that, in order for TRAK to successfully identify such points, the number of projections
k
must satisfy

k\;\gg\;\frac{d}{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}
.

Hence, Theorem
2.14
demonstrates that the projection step reduces the separation between
\mathcal{I}^{\rm TRAK}
values for data points that are highly dependent on the test point and those that are independent of it.

5
Conclusion

TRAK is a widely used data attribution method that attains scalability through three approximations: linearization, approximate leave-one-out (ALO), and projection. In this work, we theoretically analyze the errors introduced by each step. We show that the ALO approximation incurs negligible error, while the linearization step introduces substantial error but still preserves the ability to distinguish highly influential data points from uninfluential ones. In contrast, the projection step can introduce significant additional error, and using too few projections may prevent TRAK from reliably identifying influential points. Overall, our results clarify both the strengths and limitations of TRAK and provide guidance for its effective use at scale.

Impact Statement

This work provides a theoretical analysis of the TRAK algorithm, a widely used method for data attribution in modern machine learning systems. By clarifying when TRAK produces reliable influence estimates and identifying regimes in which its approximations may break down, our results help practitioners use data attribution tools more responsibly and interpret their outputs with appropriate caution. Improved understanding of data attribution methods can support transparency, debugging, and accountability in machine learning pipelines. At the same time, the techniques studied here do not introduce new capabilities for misuse, and we do not anticipate direct negative societal impacts beyond those already associated with data attribution and interpretability methods.

A.1
Multi-class Classification Extension

The goal of this section is to extend our analysis to the multiclass classification setting. We consider a dataset with
K
classes and employ the standard cross-entropy loss,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(\mathbf{z},\bm{\beta})=-\log p_{y}(\mathbf{z},\bm{\beta}),

where
p_{y}(\mathbf{z},\bm{\beta})
denotes the model’s predicted probability for the true class label
y
associated with the data point
\mathbf{z}
.
Since we consider a multiclass classification problem, the model must produce class probabilities for all
K
classes. Even in the simplest setting, the model parameters therefore take the form

\mathbf{W}=(\mathbf{W}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{W}_{K-1},\mathbf{W}_{K})^{\top},

where each
\mathbf{W}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
corresponds to the weight vector for class
k
. For identifiability, we fix
\mathbf{W}_{K}=\mathbf{0}_{p}
. We then define

\bm{\beta}:=\mathrm{vec}\!\big(\mathbf{W}_{[1:(K-1),:]}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where the parameter dimension is
d=(K-1)p>p
; thus, this model is no longer a simple linear model, unlike the binary classification case.
To address this complexity, we employ a method similar to that used in
(
14
)
. Specifically, we define:

f(\mathbf{z},\bm{\beta})=\log\left(\frac{p(\mathbf{z},\bm{\beta})}{1-p(\mathbf{z},\bm{\beta})}\right),\ \ p(\mathbf{z},\bm{\beta})=\frac{e^{\mathbf{W}_{y}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{\mathbf{W}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}},

where we set
\mathbf{W}_{K}=\mathbf{0}_{p}
. The loss function can then be expressed as:

\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{z},\bm{\beta})=\log(1+e^{-f(\mathbf{z},\bm{\beta})})=l(f(\mathbf{z},\bm{\beta})),

where
l(s)=\log(1+e^{-s})
.
We can further express the gradient as:

\mathbf{g}=\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}f(\mathbf{z},\bm{\hat{\beta}})=\frac{(\mathbf{1}_{y}-\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{z},\bm{\hat{\beta}}))}{1-p(\mathbf{z},\bm{\hat{\beta}})}\otimes\mathbf{x},

where
\mathbf{1}_{y}
is a vector of length
K-1
with entries
{\mathbf{1}_{y}}_{[k]}=1
if
y=k
and
0
otherwise (note that if
y=K
,
\mathbf{1}_{y}
is a zero vector). Similarly,
\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{z},\bm{\beta})=\left(\frac{\exp(\mathbf{W}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x})}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(\mathbf{W}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x})},\dots,\frac{\exp(\mathbf{W}_{K-1}^{\top}\mathbf{x})}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(\mathbf{W}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x})}\right)^{\top}
is also a vector of length
K-1
. Finally, we obtain
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d=(K-1)p
.

is well conditioned, i.e.,

, where
\rho_{\max}
and
\rho_{\min}
denote the extremal eigenvalues.

Justification of Scaling.

When
d>p
and
\bm{\beta}^{*}=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{W})
with
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times p}
, we typically have
f(\mathbf{x},\bm{\beta}^{*})\asymp\|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}\|=O_{p}(1)
. Assuming that both
\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{W}
and
\bm{\Sigma}
are well-conditioned, we obtain

\|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\asymp\frac{\|\mathbf{W}\|^{2}_{F}}{p}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\asymp\frac{\|\mathbf{W}\|^{2}_{F}}{p}\cdot p\|\bm{\Sigma}\|=\|\mathbf{W}\|^{2}_{F}\|\bm{\Sigma}\|,

which implies
\|\bm{\Sigma}\|\asymp 1/\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}
.

B.2.1
Proof of Proposition
2.4

By using the mean value theorem we have

\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{True}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}})\triangleq f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}};\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i})-f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}};\bm{\hat{\beta}})=\nabla^{\top}f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}},\tilde{\bm{\beta}})(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}}),

(14)

where
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}
is a point on the line segment that connects
\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}
and
\bm{\hat{\beta}}
. Using the first order optimality conditions for
\bm{\hat{\beta}}
and
\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}
we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla l(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}))

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla l(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\hat{\beta}}))+\frac{1}{n}\nabla l(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}})).

Hence, by using the integral form of the mean value theorem we have

\displaystyle(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}})=\Big(\int_{t=0}^{1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla^{2}l(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j},t\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}+(1-t)\bm{\hat{\beta}}))dt\Big)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\nabla l(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}}))\right).

(16)

By combing (
14
) and (
16
) we obtain

\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{True}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}})=\nabla^{\top}f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}},\tilde{\bm{\beta}})\Big(\int_{t=0}^{1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla^{2}l(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j},t\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}+(1-t)\bm{\hat{\beta}}))dt\Big)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\nabla l(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}}))\right).

(17)

Therefore we can use the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and Assumption
B.3
to prove

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{True}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}})|\leq\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}{c_{1}n}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}},\tilde{\bm{\beta}})\|\|\nabla l(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}}))\|=O_{\rm P}\Big(\frac{\mathrm{poly}(\log n)\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}{n}\Big),

(18)

where to obtaint the last equality we have used Assumptions
B.2
and
B.5
.

To prove the second part of the theorem, i.e. (
9
), again we use (
17
). Note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{True}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i})=\nabla^{\top}f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}},\tilde{\bm{\beta}})\Big(\int_{t=0}^{1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla^{2}l(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j},t\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}+(1-t)\bm{\hat{\beta}}))dt\Big)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\nabla l(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}}))\right)

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla^{\top}f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}},\bm{\beta}^{*})\Big(\int_{t=0}^{1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla^{2}l(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j},t\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}+(1-t)\bm{\hat{\beta}}))dt\Big)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\nabla l(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}}))

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Big(\nabla^{\top}f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}},\bm{\hat{\beta}})-\nabla^{\top}f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}},\bm{\beta}^{*})\Big)\Big(\int_{t=0}^{1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla^{2}l(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j},t\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}+(1-t)\bm{\hat{\beta}}))dt\Big)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\nabla l(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla^{\top}f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}},\bm{\beta}^{*})\Big(\int_{t=0}^{1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla^{2}l(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j},t\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}+(1-t)\bm{\hat{\beta}}))dt\Big)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\nabla l(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}}))+O_{\rm P}\left(\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}{n^{1.5-\epsilon}}\right),

where to obtain the last inequality we have used the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and Lemma
B.10
. With a similar reasoning we can further simplify (

) in the following way:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{True}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i})

(20)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle O_{\rm P}\left(\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}{n^{1.5-\epsilon}}\right).

Furthermore, using Assumption
B.3
we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{True}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i})|\geq\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}{\tilde{c}_{2}n}|\nabla^{\top}f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\beta}^{*})\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\beta}^{*})||\dot{\ell}(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\beta}^{*}))|-O_{\rm P}\left(\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}{n^{1.5-\epsilon}}\right).

(21)

Assumption
B.8
then implies (

).

B.2.2
Proof of Proposition
2.5

First note that

\displaystyle|f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}},\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i})-f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}},\bm{\hat{\beta}})|\leq\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new}}^{\top}(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}})+(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}})^{\top}\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{\text{new}},(1-t)\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}+t\bm{\hat{\beta}})(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{\text{new}},(1-t)\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}+t\bm{\hat{\beta}})\|\|\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}}\|^{2}+\|\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new},*}^{\top}(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}})\|+\|(\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new}}-\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new},*})(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{2}\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{-2}\|\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}}\|^{2}+\|\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new},*}^{\top}(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}})\|+\|\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new}}-\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new},*}\|\|\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}}\|.

The first inequality follows from the mean value theorem. The second inequality uses the triangle inequality. The third inequality follows from Assumption
B.4
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Thus, it suffices to bound the three terms in the last expression.

1. Bounding
\|\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}}\|
.

Using the mean value theorem and the first-order optimality conditions in (
1
) and (
3
), we have:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla l(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}))-\frac{1}{n}\nabla l(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla l(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\hat{\beta}}))+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla^{2}l(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\tilde{\beta}}_{/i}))(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}})-\frac{1}{n}\nabla l(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla^{2}l(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\tilde{\beta}}_{/i}))(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}})-\frac{1}{n}\nabla l(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i})),

where
\bm{\tilde{\beta}}_{/i}=(1-t_{i})\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}+t_{i}\bm{\hat{\beta}}
for some
t_{i}\in[0,1]
. This implies:

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[n]}\|\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[n]}\Big\|\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla^{2}l(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\tilde{\beta}}_{/i}))\Big)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\nabla l(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}))\Big\|

(22)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[n]}\sigma_{\max}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla^{2}l(y_{j},f(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\tilde{\beta}}_{/i}))\Big)^{-1}\Big)\sup_{i\in[n]}\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\nabla l(y_{i},f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}))\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{C}_{1}\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}{c_{1}}\frac{\rm{poly}(\log n)}{n},\qquad\text{\rm\ \ with probability\ }\geq 1-q_{n},

where to obtain the last inequality we use Assumption
B.2
and
B.5
.

2. Bounding
\|\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new}}-\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new},*}\|
.

From Lemma
B.10
, for any
\epsilon>0
, with probability at least
1-q_{n}-n^{-\epsilon}
, there exists an absolute constant
s_{g}^{\ast}
such that

\|\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new}}-\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new},*}\|\leq s_{g}^{\ast}(\log n)^{0.5}n^{\epsilon-0.5}.

(23)

3. Bounding
\|\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new},*}^{\top}(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}})\|
.

By Assumption
B.7
,
\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new},*}
is sub-Gaussian. Using (
22
) and the independence of
\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new},*}
from
\bm{\hat{\beta}}
,
\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}
,
\bm{\breve{\beta}}
, and
\bm{\breve{\beta}}_{/i}
, there exists an absolute constant
s_{g}
such that
\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new},*}^{\top}(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}})
is a
s_{g}\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|\rm{poly}(\log n)}{n}
-sub-Gaussian random variable. Hence, for any
\epsilon>0
, with probability at least
1-2n^{-\epsilon}
, there exists an absolute constant
\hat{s}_{g}
such that

\|\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{new},*}^{\top}(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}})\|\leq\hat{s}_{g}\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|}{n^{1-\epsilon}}.

(24)

Final bound.

Combining the bounds above, there exists a constant
s_{1}
such that, for any
\epsilon>0
,

|f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}},\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i})-f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}},\bm{\hat{\beta}})|\leq s_{1}\Big(\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}{n^{2}}{\rm{poly}(\log n)}+\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}{n^{1.5-\epsilon}}+\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|}{n^{1-\epsilon}}\Big),

with probability at least

. The final result follows from

.

B.2.3
Proof of Theorem
2.6

By the first-order optimality conditions, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\dot{\ell}(y_{j},\mathbf{g}_{j}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\beta}}+\mathbf{b}_{j})\mathbf{g}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\dot{\ell}(y_{j},\mathbf{g}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\breve{\beta}}+\mathbf{b}_{j})\mathbf{g}_{j}

\displaystyle 0.

,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\ell(y_{j},\mathbf{g}_{j}^{\top}{\bm{\beta}}+\mathbf{b}_{j})\mathbf{g}_{j}

Next, we bound the difference:

\displaystyle|f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i})-(\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\breve{\beta}}_{/i}+\mathbf{b}_{i})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i})-f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\hat{\beta}})|+|\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{\hat{\beta}}-\bm{\breve{\beta}})|+|\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{\breve{\beta}}-\bm{\breve{\beta}}_{/i})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{i},(1-t)\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}+t\bm{\hat{\beta}})\|\|\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\mathbf{g}_{i,*}^{\top}(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}})\|+\|\mathbf{g}_{i,*}^{\top}(\bm{\breve{\beta}}-\bm{\breve{\beta}}_{/i})\|

\displaystyle C_{2}\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{-2}\|\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\mathbf{g}_{i,*}^{\top}(\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}})\|+\|\mathbf{g}_{i,*}^{\top}(\bm{\breve{\beta}}-\bm{\breve{\beta}}_{/i})\|

\displaystyle+\|\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{i,*}\|\|\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}}\|+\|\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{i,*}\|\|\bm{\breve{\beta}}-\bm{\breve{\beta}}_{/i}\|.

The first inequality follows from the mean value theorem and the fact that
\bm{\breve{\beta}}=\bm{\hat{\beta}}
. The second inequality uses the triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities. The third inequality follows from Assumption
B.4
and Cauchy-Schwarz. Thus, it suffices to bound the three terms in the last three lines.
Using the same method as the one used in deriving (
22
) we have

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[n]}\|\bm{\breve{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\breve{\beta}}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{\rm P}\Big(\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}\rm{poly}(\log n)}{n}\Big).

Furthermore, using the mean value theorem we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{i,*}^{\top}(\bm{\breve{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\breve{\beta}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{\ell}_{i}(\bm{\breve{\beta}})\mathbf{g}_{i,*}^{\top}\Bigl(\int_{t=0}^{1}\bm{G}_{/i}^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits[\bm{\ddot{\ell}}_{/i}(\bm{\breve{\beta}}_{/i}-t\bm{\breve{\Delta}}_{/i})]\bm{G}_{/i}dt\Bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{g}_{i}

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{\ell}_{i}(\bm{\breve{\beta}})\mathbf{g}_{i,*}^{\top}\Bigl(\int_{t=0}^{1}\bm{G}_{/i}^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits[\bm{\ddot{\ell}}_{/i}(\bm{\breve{\beta}}_{/i}-t\bm{\breve{\Delta}}_{/i})]\bm{G}_{/i}dt\Bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{g}_{i,*}+O_{\rm P}\left(\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}{n^{1.5-\epsilon}}\right),

where to obtain the last equality we used an argument similar to the one we used for deriving (
21
). Hence, we have

Lemma B.9
.

Assume that
\bm{G}^{\top}(\bm{D+\Gamma})\bm{G}
and
\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{DG}
are positive definite, and both
\bm{D}
and
\bm{\Gamma}
are diagonal matrices. Let
\mathbf{g}
be a vector independent of
\mathbf{G}
,
\bm{\Gamma}
, and
\bm{D}
. If
\|(\mathbf{G}^{\top}\bm{D}\mathbf{G})^{-1}(\mathbf{G}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{G})\|<1
, then there exists an absolute constant
M_{0}
such that

\left\|\left((\bm{G}^{\top}(\bm{D}+\bm{\Gamma})\bm{G})^{-1}-(\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{DG})^{-1}\right)\mathbf{g}\right\|\leq M_{0}\left\|(\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{DG})^{-1}\mathbf{G}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{G}(\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{DG})^{-1}\mathbf{g}\right\|.

Proof of Lemma
B.9
.

Let
A=\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{G}
and
\Delta=\bm{G}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{G}
. By the Neumann series expansion, since
\|A^{-1}\Delta\|<1
,

(A+\Delta)^{-1}=A^{-1}-A^{-1}\Delta A^{-1}+A^{-1}\Delta A^{-1}\Delta A^{-1}-\cdots.

Thus,

(A+\Delta)^{-1}-A^{-1}=-A^{-1}\Delta A^{-1}+A^{-1}\Delta A^{-1}\Delta A^{-1}-\cdots.

Multiplying by
\mathbf{g}
and taking norms,

\displaystyle\|((A+\Delta)^{-1}-A^{-1})\mathbf{g}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{-1}\Delta A^{-1}\mathbf{g}\|+\|A^{-1}\Delta A^{-1}\Delta A^{-1}\mathbf{g}\|+\cdots

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{-1}\Delta A^{-1}\mathbf{g}\|\left(1+\|A^{-1}\Delta\|+\|A^{-1}\Delta\|^{2}+\cdots\right)

\displaystyle=\|A^{-1}\Delta A^{-1}\mathbf{g}\|\cdot\frac{1}{1-\|A^{-1}\Delta\|}.

Since
\|A^{-1}\Delta\|<1
by assumption, the geometric series converges. Taking
M_{0}=\frac{1}{1-\|A^{-1}\Delta\|}
completes the proof.
∎

B\preceq C\implies j^{\top}(C-B)j\geq 0\implies j^{T}D^{\top}(C-B)Dj\geq 0

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\|\Pi h\|^{2}\leq\|P_{V}w\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{d}}\|\Pi h\|^{2}

(58)

Also it is clear that we have the following bounds for
\|h\|^{2}=w^{\top}\Lambda w
,
\lambda_{d}\|w\|^{2}\leq\|h\|^{2}\leq\lambda_{1}\|w\|^{2}
. Combining with
Equation

58
, we have shown
Equation

57

∎

Using the fact that the quadratic forms of a Gaussian vector projected onto an independent random subspace follow a Beta distribution, Chapter 7
(
1
)
, we have
\frac{\|\Pi h\|^{2}}{\|h\|^{2}}\sim\text{Beta}\left(k/2,(d-k)/2\right)
. By concentration of Beta distribution (using standard concentration inequalities from
(
17
)
), we have,

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\frac{\|\Pi h\|^{2}}{\|h\|^{2}}-\frac{k}{d}\right|>\epsilon\right)\leq C\exp(-ck\epsilon^{2})

(59)

By
Equation

57
, we have almost surely
\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{\|\Pi Q^{T}h\|^{2}}{\|Q^{T}h\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\|P_{V}Q^{T}w\|^{2}}{\|Q^{T}w\|^{2}}\leq\kappa\frac{\|\Pi Q^{T}h\|^{2}}{\|Q^{T}h\|^{2}}
. Thus since
P=QP_{V}Q^{\top}
and by
Equation

59
, we have shown
Lemma

B.12
.

B.4
Confirming our assumptions on the loss function

In this section, we examine Assumption
B.5
in the context of logistic regression, linear regression, and Poisson regression. Our goal is to justify that the orders are reasonable.

B.4.1
Logistic Regression

Since

\dot{\ell}_{i}\!\left(\bm{\beta}\right)=-y_{i}+\frac{e^{\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}}}{1+e^{\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}}},\quad\ddot{\ell}_{i}\!\left(\bm{\beta}\right)=\frac{e^{\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}}}{\left(1+e^{\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}}\right)^{2}},\quad\dddot{\ell}_{i}\!\left(\bm{\beta}\right)=\frac{e^{\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}}\left(1-e^{\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}}\right)}{\left(1+e^{\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}}\right)^{3}},

using simple algebra, it is straightforward to show that for any
\bm{\beta}
, we have

\|\dot{\bm{\ell}}\!\left(\bm{\beta}\right)\|_{\infty}\leq 1,\quad\|\ddot{\bm{\ell}}\!\left(\bm{\beta}\right)\|_{\infty}\leq\tfrac{1}{4},\quad\|\dddot{\bm{\ell}}\!\left(\bm{\beta}\right)\|_{\infty}\leq\tfrac{1}{10}.

Hence, the first inequality of Assumption
B.5
holds.
Note that we can assume
\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}=O_{p}(1)
, so the second inequality of Assumption
B.5
also holds.
oreover,

\|\bm{\ddot{\ell}}_{/i}\!\left(\bm{\beta}+\delta\right)-\bm{\ddot{\ell}}_{/i}\!\left(\bm{\beta}\right)\|_{2}\leq\|\ddot{\bm{\ell}}\!\left(\bm{\beta}+\delta\right)-\ddot{\bm{\ell}}\!\left(\bm{\beta}\right)\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i}\left(\ddot{\ell}_{i}\!\left(\bm{\beta}+\delta\right)-\ddot{\ell}_{i}\!\left(\bm{\beta}\right)\right)^{2}}.

Using the mean-value theorem, where
\epsilon_{i}\in[0,\delta_{i}]
, we have

=\sqrt{\sum_{i}\dddot{\ell}_{i}\!\left(\bm{\beta}+\epsilon_{i}\right)^{2}\left(\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\delta\right)^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\delta^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\delta}\leq\sqrt{\sigma_{\max}\!\left(\mathbf{G}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\right)}\,\|\delta\|_{2}.

Based on the inequality above, we obtain

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\frac{\|\bm{\ddot{\ell}}_{/i}\!\left((1-t)\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}+t\hat{\bm{\beta}}\right)-\bm{\ddot{\ell}}_{/i}\!\left(\hat{\bm{\beta}}\right)\|_{2}}{\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}-\hat{\bm{\beta}}\|_{2}}\;\leq\;\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\frac{(1-t)\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}-\hat{\bm{\beta}}\|_{2}\sqrt{\sigma_{\max}\!\left(\mathbf{G}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\right)}}{\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}-\hat{\bm{\beta}}\|_{2}}\;\leq\;\sqrt{\sigma_{\max}\!\left(\mathbf{G}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\right)}.

By Assumption
B.3
, we know that
\sigma_{\max}\!\left(\mathbf{G}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\right)=O_{p}\!\left({n/\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}}\right)
, thus the third inequality in Assumption
B.5
holds.

B.4.2
Linear Regression

Considering
\ell\!\left(y\mid\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(y-\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right)^{2}
, we have
\dot{\ell}_{i}\!\left(\bm{\beta}\right)=-\!\left(y_{i}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right)
and
\ddot{\ell}_{i}\!\left(\bm{\beta}\right)=1
. Therefore, the second and third inequalities in Assumption
B.5
hold directly.
Moreover, since
y\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*},\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\right)
, by properties of sub-Gaussian random variables we obtain

\max_{i\in[n]}\big|\dot{\ell}_{i}\!\left(\bm{\beta}\right)\big|\preceq\sqrt{\log n}.

Hence, the first inequality in Assumption
B.5
holds.

B.4.3
Poisson Regression

Consider

\ell\!\left(y\mid\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right)=h\!\left(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right)-y\log h\!\left(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right)+\log y!,

with the conditional mean
\mathbb{E}\!\left[y\mid\bm{x},\bm{\beta}\right]=h\!\left(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right)
where

h(s)=\log\!\left(1+e^{s}\right)
. Then the first and second derivatives of the loss with respect to
s:=\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}
are

\dot{\ell}(y,s)=h^{\prime}(s)\Bigl(1-\frac{y}{h(s)}\Bigr)=\sigma(s)\Bigl(1-\frac{y}{h(s)}\Bigr),

and

\ddot{\ell}(y,s)=\sigma(s)\bigl(1-\sigma(s)\bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{y}{h(s)}\Bigr)+y\frac{\sigma(s)^{2}}{h(s)^{2}}.

By Lemma 8 of
15
,
we have

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\frac{\|\bm{\ddot{\ell}}_{/i}\!\left((1-t)\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}+t\bm{\hat{\beta}}\right)-\bm{\ddot{\ell}}_{/i}\!\left(\bm{\hat{\beta}}\right)\|_{2}}{\|\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{/i}-\bm{\hat{\beta}}\|_{2}}\;\;\leq\;\;\left(1+6\|y\|_{\infty}\right)\sqrt{\sigma_{\max}\!\left(\bm{\mathbf{G}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{G}}\right)},

and

\|\bm{\dot{\ell}}\!\left(\bm{\beta}\right)\|_{\infty}\;\;\leq\;\;1+\|y\|_{\infty}.

where
\sigma
is sigmoid function. Since
\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}\right)
, we have

\max_{i\in[n]}\left|\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right|=O\!\left(\log n\right)
and

\|y\|_{\infty}=O\!\left(\mathrm{poly}\log n\right)
, both holding with high probability.
Therefore, using Assumption
B.3
, the three inequalities in Assumption
B.5
hold.

B.5
Confirming our assumptions on the nonlinear function
f(\cdot,\cdot)

B.5.1
Verification of Example
2.2
.

Consider the GLM where

. We verify Assumptions
B.2
,
B.3
,
B.4
, and
B.7
.

First, note that
p=d
and
\nabla f(\mathbf{x},\bm{\beta})=\mathbf{x}
. Since we assume

, we have
p\asymp\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}
. Combined with Assumption
B.1
, this implies that Assumptions
B.2
and
B.7
hold, due to standard properties of sub-Gaussian vectors. Next, because

B.4
holds automatically.

Furthermore, since

\mathbf{G}(\bm{\beta})^{\top}\mathbf{G}(\bm{\beta})=\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X},

.

B.5.2
Verification of Example
2.3
.

Setting and Assumptions.

We verify Assumptions
B.2
,
B.3
,
B.4
, and
B.7
in a linear-layer setting.
Let the parameter vector be

\bm{\beta}=(\text{\rm vec}(\mathbf{W})^{\top},\mathbf{v}^{\top})^{\top},

where
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times p}
satisfies

\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{W})\asymp\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{W}).

We further assume
|v_{l}|=O(1)
; that is, there exist constants
v,V>0
such that

v\leq|v_{l}|\leq V,\qquad\forall l\in[h].

We also assume the initialization satisfies

p\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\asymp\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}^{2},

which holds for common isotropic initializations.

Let
\sigma
be an activation function with

0\leq\sigma^{\prime}(x)\leq E.

For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, define
\sigma(\mathbf{x})
element-wise.
Define the function

f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{v}^{\top}\sigma(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}).

The gradients are given by

\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{v})=\sigma(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{h},

\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{v})=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits\big(\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x})\big)\,\mathbf{v}\,\mathbf{x}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times p}.

For notational convenience, for
l\in[h]
and
i\in B\subset[n]

A^{B}_{li}=\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{W}_{l}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}),\qquad D^{B}_{li}=v_{l}\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{W}_{l}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}).

Then the relationship between the Gram matrices is

D^{B}(D^{B})^{\top}=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(\mathbf{v})\,A^{B}(A^{B})^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(\mathbf{v}).

Assume that for any
B\subset[n]
with
|B|\gg h
, with high probability we can find constants
\underline{\alpha},\bar{\alpha}>0
such that

\bar{\alpha}|B|\;\geq\;\lambda_{\max}(A^{B}A^{B,\top})\;\geq\;\lambda_{\min}(A^{B}A^{B,\top})\;\geq\;\underline{\alpha}|B|.

Intuitively, if for all
l\in[h]
,

\sum_{i\in B}\sigma^{\prime 2}(\mathbf{W}_{l}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i})\asymp|B|,

then

\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(A^{B}A^{B,\top})=\sum_{l=1}^{h}\sum_{i\in B}\sigma^{\prime 2}(\mathbf{W}_{l}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i})\asymp h|B|.

If
A^{B}A^{B,\top}
is well-conditioned, this implies the eigenvalue bounds above.
Consequently, utilizing the bounds on
v_{l}
,

V^{2}\,\bar{\alpha}\,|B|\;\geq\;\lambda_{\max}(D^{B}D^{B,\top})\;\geq\;\lambda_{\min}(D^{B}D^{B,\top})\;\geq\;v^{2}\,\underline{\alpha}\,|B|.

We denote
A^{[n]}=A
and
D^{[n]}=D
for simplicity.

We further assume
n>d
and
n\gg h^{2}
.
Intuitively, if
n<d
where
d
is the parameter dimension, the empirical Fisher

\mathbf{G}^{\top}\mathbf{G}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{g}_{i}\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}

cannot be invertible.
Similarly, in very wide networks, the sample size must dominate the width to obtain stable approximations.

Verification of Assumption
B.2
.

First, we verify Assumption
B.2
. Note that

\|\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{v})\|=\|\sigma(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x})\|\leq E\|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}\|=O_{\text{\rm HP}}(\sqrt{\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\mathbf{W}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}^{\top})})=O_{\text{\rm HP}}(\sqrt{\|\mathbf{W}\|^{2}_{F}/\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}})=O_{\text{\rm HP}}(1).

using the scaling assumption
p\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\asymp\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}^{2}
.

Verification of Assumption

Next, we verify Assumption
B.3

\mathbf{g}_{i}=\text{\rm vec}(\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{v}))=c_{i}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{i},

To analyze the eigenvalues of
M
, consider any
q\in\mathbb{R}^{ph}
such that
q=\text{\rm vec}(Q)
and
\|q\|=\|Q\|_{F}=1
. We only need to bound

q^{\top}Mq=q^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{i})(c_{i}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{i})^{\top}\right)q=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}Qc_{i})^{2}.

Note that
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times h}
. We consider two cases separately:
\text{rank}(Q)=1
and
\text{rank}(Q)>1

First, consider the case when

\displaystyle q^{\top}Mq

\displaystyle=a^{\top}\Big[\tfrac{1}{n}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits\big((b^{\top}c_{1})^{2},\ldots,(b^{\top}c_{n})^{2}\big)(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}\Big]a

\displaystyle\geq\|a\|^{2}\,\lambda_{\min}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits\big((b^{\top}c_{1})^{2},\ldots,(b^{\top}c_{n})^{2}\big)(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}\right).

(60)

For any
b\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
with
\|b\|=1
, denote
S_{b}=\{i:\ (b^{\top}c_{i})^{2}\geq\frac{\underline{\alpha}v^{2}}{2}\}
and let
S_{b}^{c}=[n]\setminus S_{b}

\displaystyle\|b\|^{2}\underline{\alpha}nv^{2}

\displaystyle\leq b^{\top}DD^{\top}b=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b^{\top}c_{i})^{2}=\sum_{i\in S_{b}}(b^{\top}c_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i\notin S_{b}}(b^{\top}c_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|b\|^{2}E^{2}V^{2}h|S_{b}|+(n-|S_{b}|)\frac{\underline{\alpha}v^{2}}{2}

where in the second inequality we used
|c_{i}|^{2}\leq E^{2}V^{2}h
.
Thus, we get

|S_{b}|\geq\frac{\underline{\alpha}v^{2}}{2E^{2}V^{2}h-\underline{\alpha}v^{2}}n\gg h,

using
n\gg h^{2}
. We can further refine the inequality:

\displaystyle\|b\|^{2}\underline{\alpha}nv^{2}

\displaystyle\leq b^{\top}DD^{\top}b=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b^{\top}c_{i})^{2}=\sum_{i\in S_{b}}(b^{\top}c_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i\notin S_{b}}(b^{\top}c_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\underline{\alpha}v^{2}}{2}n+|S_{b}|\left(\bar{\alpha}V^{2}-\frac{\underline{\alpha}v^{2}}{2}\right),

where we used
b^{\top}D^{S_{b}}D^{S_{b},\top}b\leq\|b\|^{2}|S_{b}|\bar{\alpha}V^{2}
for
|S_{b}|\gg h

|S_{b}|\geq\frac{\underline{\alpha}v^{2}}{2\bar{\alpha}V^{2}-\underline{\alpha}v^{2}}n:=C(\underline{\alpha},\bar{\alpha},v,V)n.

Returning to (
60

\displaystyle q^{\top}Mq

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{\min}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits\big((b^{\top}c_{1})^{2},\ldots,(b^{\top}c_{n})^{2}\big)(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in S_{b}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}(b^{\top}c_{i})^{2}\right)\geq\lambda_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in S_{b}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\frac{\underline{\alpha}v^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\underline{\alpha}v^{2}}{2}\frac{|S_{b}|}{n}\lambda_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{|S_{b}|}\sum_{i\in S_{b}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right).

By the sub-Gaussianity of i.i.d
\mathbf{x}_{i}

\lambda_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{|S_{b}|}\sum_{i\in S_{b}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)-\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma})\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{p}{|S_{b}|}}\right)^{2}\to 0,\quad{\rm a.s.},

where
\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma})(1-\sqrt{\frac{p}{|S_{b}|}})^{2}\asymp\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|^{-2}

Finally, we get

Next, we consider the upper bound in the rank-1 case. Similar to (
60

\displaystyle\noindent q^{\top}Mq

\displaystyle=a^{\top}\Big[\tfrac{1}{n}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits\big((b^{\top}c_{1})^{2},\ldots,(b^{\top}c_{n})^{2}\big)(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}\Big]a

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(\tfrac{1}{n}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits\big((b_{r}^{\top}c_{1})^{2},\ldots,(b_{r}^{\top}c_{n})^{2}\big)(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top})

Verification of Assumption
B.7
.

For simplicity, we consider
\sigma(x)=x
.
We have

\mathbf{g}_{i,*}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{W}\\[3.0pt]
\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{p}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{i},

\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{W}\\[3.0pt]

\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|}{\sqrt{p}}\mathbf{x}_{i}

\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}

\|\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}\|\asymp\frac{1}{p}
. Thus
\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|}{\sqrt{p}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim\text{nSG}(\sigma)

\sigma>0

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{W}\\[3.0pt]
\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{p}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbb{E}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{W}\\[3.0pt]
\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{p}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\|\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{W}\\[3.0pt]
\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{p}\end{pmatrix}\right\|\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\asymp\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|}{\sqrt{p}}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

Therefore,

\mathbf{g}_{i,*}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\text{nSG}(\sigma).
