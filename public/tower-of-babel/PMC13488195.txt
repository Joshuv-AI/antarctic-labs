Exploring the relationship between potential hepatotoxicity and novel hormonal therapies in prostate cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Liang Ma, Liwei Fan, Xiaoli Luo, Zhuangcheng Huang, Jiacheng Dai, Yuejun Wu, Wenhua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNovel hormonal therapies (NHTs) have substantially improved outcomes in prostate cancer across multiple disease states. However, their hepatic safety remains clinically relevant because treatment is often prolonged and may be combined with androgen-deprivation therapy, corticosteroids, or chemotherapy. We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to quantify the risk of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) elevations associated with NHT-based therapy.MethodsThis study was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Randomized controlled trials comparing NHT-containing regimens with control regimens in patients with prostate cancer were eligible if they reported extractable ALT and/or AST adverse-event data. Pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for four prespecified outcomes: all-grade ALT elevation, grade ≥ 3 ALT elevation, all-grade AST elevation, and grade ≥ 3 AST elevation.ResultsTen randomized studies reported in 11 publications were included, involving 10,173 participants treated with abiraterone, enzalutamide, darolutamide, or abiraterone plus enzalutamide combination regimens. Compared with controls, NHT-based therapy significantly increased the risk of all-grade ALT elevation (RR 1.49, 95% CI 1.12–2.00; I2 = 82.8%), grade ≥ 3 ALT elevation (RR 5.91, 95% CI 2.86–12.20; I2 = 67.5%), all-grade AST elevation (RR 1.58, 95% CI 1.02–2.45; I2 = 84.5%), and grade ≥ 3 AST elevation (RR 3.32, 95% CI 2.13–5.18; I2 = 0%). Subgroup analyses suggested that the hepatic safety signal was more evident for abiraterone-containing and abiraterone plus enzalutamide combination regimens, whereas estimates for enzalutamide and darolutamide varied across outcomes and should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of available comparisons.ConclusionsNHT-based therapy for prostate cancer was associated with an increased risk of hepatic enzyme abnormalities, particularly severe ALT elevation. The hepatic safety signal appeared more evident for abiraterone-containing and combination regimens, whereas drug-specific estimates for enzalutamide and darolutamide remained limited. Because this analysis focused on laboratory-defined ALT and AST elevations, careful liver function monitoring remains warranted during NHT-based therapy, especially in patients receiving abiraterone-containing or prolonged combination regimens.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16339-x.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) represents a major global health burden, being the second most frequently diagnosed cancer in men [1–3]. Over the past decade, the standard of care for metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC) and castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) has evolved from simple androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) to intensified combinations involving Novel Hormonal Therapies (NHTs), also referred to as androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) [4, 5]. These agents, including the CYP17A1 inhibitor abiraterone acetate and second-generation androgen receptor inhibitors such as enzalutamide, apalutamide, and darolutamide, have demonstrated significant improvements in overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) [6].

However, the expansion of NHT use into earlier disease stages, such as non-metastatic HSPC(nmHSPC) and biochemically recurrent PCa, necessitates a more granular understanding of their long-term safety profiles [7]. Hepatotoxicity is one of the most frequently reported non-target adverse events (AEs) [8, 9]. The liver is the primary site of steroid metabolism and the clearance of these small-molecule inhibitors. Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) can manifest as transient elevations in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) or, in rare cases, progress to fulminant hepatic failure [10, 11]. Abiraterone acetate, in particular, requires co-administration with low-dose corticosteroids to prevent mineralocorticoid excess, but its hepatic metabolism can lead to idiosyncratic injury [12]. Furthermore, the trend toward “triple therapy” (NHT + ADT + Docetaxel) adds another layer of complexity, as taxane-based chemotherapy is independently associated with hepatotoxicity [13].

Although neutropenia has been reported in multiple phase 3 trials of NHT agents, a synthesized quantification of the relative risk (RR) across different NHT agents is lacking. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to fill this gap, providing clinicians with evidence-based risk estimates and monitoring recommendations to optimize patient safety while maintaining oncological efficacy.

Methods

Search strategy and study selection

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [14]. A comprehensive literature search of PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science Core Collection, the Cochrane Library/CENTRAL, and ClinicalTrials.gov was performed from database inception to Jan, 2026. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms for: (1) prostate cancer (“prostatic neoplasms”, “prostate cancer”, “prostate adenocarcinoma”); (2) novel hormonal therapies or androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (“abiraterone”, “enzalutamide”, “darolutamide”, “apalutamide”, “androgen receptor antagonist”, “CYP17 inhibitor”); and (3) randomized controlled trials (“randomized controlled trial”, “randomised”).

Eligible studies met the following inclusion criteria: (1) randomized controlled phase 2 or 3 trials; (2) evaluated a novel hormonal therapy or androgen receptor pathway inhibitor of interest, including abiraterone, enzalutamide, apalutamide, darolutamide, or their combinations, in patients with prostate cancer; (3) included a control arm receiving placebo or standard-of-care therapy without the investigational NHT; and (4) reported extractable arm-level data on hepatic adverse events, specifically elevations in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and/or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of any grade or grade ≥ 3 according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) criteria [15]. Exclusion criteria were: (1) non-randomized studies, single-arm trials, or observational cohorts; (2) trials evaluating NHTs in non-prostate malignancies; (3) studies not reporting extractable hepatotoxicity data; (4) duplicate publications or subset analyses of previously reported trials. Two investigators independently screened titles and abstracts for potential eligibility, followed by full-text review of selected articles. Disagreements were resolved through consensus discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer.

Data extraction and quality assessment

A standardized data extraction form was developed and piloted prior to use. For each included trial, the following information was extracted: first author name, publication year, trial acronym, study phase, specific NHT evaluated, comparator regimen, total number of randomized patients, allocation numbers per treatment arm, disease stage (nmHSPC, mHSPC, mCRPC), median patient age, baseline prostate-specific antigen (PSA) level, Gleason score distribution, and median follow-up duration. The primary safety outcomes of interest were the number of patients experiencing: (1) all-grade ALT elevation; (2) grade ≥ 3 ALT elevation; (3) all-grade AST elevation; and (4) grade ≥ 3 AST elevation. Events were extracted from published safety summaries, supplementary appendices, and ClinicalTrials.gov records when available.

Risk of bias for each included trial was assessed using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) [16]. Domains evaluated included: bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, and bias in selection of the reported result. Each domain was rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk,” and an overall risk of bias judgment was assigned. The overall methodological quality of this systematic review and meta-analysis was evaluated using the AMSTAR 2 (Assessing the Methodological Quality of Systematic Reviews) tool [17]. Each AMSTAR 2 item was judged as “Yes,” “Partial Yes,” or “No,” and the overall confidence rating was determined according to the AMSTAR 2 guidance.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates. A leave-one-out approach was used, in which each study was sequentially omitted and the meta-analysis was repeated for each of the four predefined outcomes. Additional analyses were conducted by excluding the study with concerns related to open-label design and treatment-exposure imbalance, and by excluding the only phase 2 trial. Fixed-effect and random-effects models were also compared to evaluate the consistency of the overall findings.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the meta package in R software (version 4.2.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For dichotomous safety outcomes (the incidence of all-grade and grade ≥ 3 ALT/AST elevations), the pooled Risk Ratios (RRs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated utilizing the Mantel–Haenszel (M-H) method. For studies with zero events in one treatment arm, the comparison was retained in the corresponding meta-analysis, and a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to zero cells for calculation of the log RR and its standard error. Studies with unavailable or non-extractable ALT/AST event counts were excluded only from the specific outcome analysis for which the relevant data were unavailable. No study was excluded solely because of a single zero-event arm. Comparisons with zero events in both arms, if present, were not included in RR-based pooling because they did not provide information on relative treatment effect. Given the anticipated clinical and methodological diversity across the included trials—such as variations in prostate cancer stages (mHSPC, nmHSPC, mCRPC) and differing baseline characteristics—a random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird method) was a priori selected for all pooled analyses to provide more conservative and generalizable estimates.

Statistical heterogeneity across studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test (with a significance level of p < 0.10) and quantified by the I2 statistic. I2 values of < 25%, 25%–50%, and > 50% were defined as indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [18]. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, predefined subgroup analyses were conducted based on the specific type of novel hormonal therapy (e.g., Abiraterone, Enzalutamide, Darolutamide, and combination regimens). Potential publication bias was evaluated visually via the symmetry of funnel plots and quantitatively assessed using Egger’s linear regression test. A two-sided p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all overall effect estimates and publication bias assessments. For outcomes showing funnel plot asymmetry or borderline/significant Egger’s test results, Duval and Tweedie’s trim-and-fill analysis was further performed to evaluate the potential influence of small-study effects. The adjusted pooled RR and 95% CI were estimated under a random-effects model. This analysis was interpreted as a sensitivity analysis rather than as a definitive correction, particularly when the number of included studies was limited or between-study heterogeneity was substantial.

Results

Study selection and study characteristics

The study selection process is presented in Fig. 1. A total of 7,680 records were identified through database and registry searches, including PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov. After removal of 2,882 duplicate records, 4,798 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 4,620 records were excluded. A total of 178 reports were assessed for full-text eligibility, and 168 were excluded, including 119 that did not report relevant outcomes, 35 non-randomized controlled trials, and 13 reviews. Apalutamide-related randomized trials were also retrieved and assessed during full-text screening. However, these reports did not provide extractable arm-level event counts for any of the four prespecified hepatic outcomes, namely all-grade ALT elevation, grade ≥ 3 ALT elevation, all-grade AST elevation, or grade ≥ 3 AST elevation, in the published articles, supplementary materials, or ClinicalTrials.gov records available within our prespecified data sources. Therefore, these apalutamide-related reports were classified as studies with no relevant extractable outcomes rather than being excluded because of the intervention type. Ultimately, 10 studies reported in 11 publications [19–29] were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis.Fig. 1PRISMA flow diagram of study selection. Risk of bias

Risk-of-bias assessments are summarized in Supplemental Table 1. Overall, six trials were judged to be at low risk of bias, and four trials were rated as having some concerns. No trial was judged to be at high risk of bias for the prespecified hepatic adverse-event outcomes. The concerns mainly arose from open-label designs, treatment-exposure differences, or limited reporting detail for safety ascertainment in STAMPEDE, Spetsieris et al., ENZAMET, and PEACE-1. Across the evidence base, the risk arising from the randomization process, missing outcome data, and selection of the reported result was generally low. Because ALT and AST elevations are laboratory-based outcomes, the risk in outcome measurement was judged to be low in double-blind placebo-controlled trials and to raise some concerns only in open-label studies where safety ascertainment or reporting could have been influenced by trial design.

Methodological quality of the review according to AMSTAR 2

The methodological quality of this systematic review and meta-analysis was assessed using AMSTAR 2. The item-level assessment is presented in Supplemental Material 2. Overall, 13 of the 16 AMSTAR 2 items were rated as “Yes,” and 3 items were rated as “Partial Yes.” No item was rated as “No.” The items rated as “Partial Yes” were item 2, because the review protocol was not prospectively registered; item 4, because although multiple databases and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched, some additional sources such as grey literature and expert consultation were not fully covered; and item 8, because the included studies were described in detail for the variables relevant to this meta-analysis, but not all intervention and comparator details, such as complete dosing information, were available across reports. Based on the AMSTAR 2 framework, the overall confidence in the methodological quality of this review was rated as moderate.

Meta-analysis of all-grade ALT elevation

The pooled analysis showed that NHT-based therapy was associated with a higher risk of all-grade ALT elevation than control treatment (RR 1.49, 95% CI 1.12–2.00; Fig. 2). Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 82.8%, p < 0.0001). In subgroup analysis, the pooled RR was 1.49 (95% CI 1.09–2.04) for abiraterone-containing regimens, 0.70 (95% CI 0.45–1.09) for enzalutamide, 2.42 (95% CI 1.75–3.35) for abiraterone plus enzalutamide, and 1.10 (95% CI 0.65–1.88) for darolutamide. Exploratory assessment of heterogeneity

Because substantial heterogeneity was observed for all-grade ALT and AST elevations, potential trial-level sources were assessed descriptively. The included trials differed in disease stage, treatment backbone, trial design, and follow-up length, with median follow-up ranging from 3.9 to 96 months. These factors were closely related to drug type: abiraterone-containing regimens were evaluated across several disease stages and were generally combined with prednisone or prednisolone, whereas darolutamide data mainly came from mHSPC trials. Docetaxel exposure was also unevenly distributed, being used in ARASENS and in a subset of PEACE-1 patients, but ALT/AST event counts stratified by docetaxel use were not consistently available. Therefore, reliable pooled subgroup estimates by disease stage, corticosteroid use, docetaxel exposure, trial design, or follow-up length could not be generated. Overall, the high heterogeneity in all-grade ALT and AST outcomes may reflect the combined influence of drug type, disease stage, treatment duration, concomitant therapy, trial design, follow-up length, and adverse-event ascertainment. These trial-level factors are summarized in Supplemental Table 2.

Sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses are shown in Supplemental Fig. 1A–D. The pooled estimates for all-grade ALT elevation, grade ≥ 3 ALT elevation, and grade ≥ 3 AST elevation remained statistically significant after sequential omission of each individual study, indicating good robustness of these findings. Across leave-one-out analyses, pooled RRs ranged from 1.39 to 1.64 for all-grade ALT elevation, 4.99 to 7.48 for grade ≥ 3 ALT elevation, and 3.11 to 3.71 for grade ≥ 3 AST elevation. In contrast, the result for all-grade AST elevation was less stable; although the direction of effect remained unchanged, exclusion of several individual studies resulted in confidence intervals crossing 1.0. Additional sensitivity analyses did not materially alter the overall conclusions. Exclusion of ENZAMET, which had some concerns related to open-label design and treatment-exposure imbalance, yielded pooled RRs of 1.64 for all-grade ALT elevation, 7.12 for grade ≥ 3 ALT elevation, 1.85 for all-grade AST elevation, and 3.43 for grade ≥ 3 AST elevation, while exclusion of the only phase 2 trial yielded corresponding pooled RRs of 1.50, 5.86, 1.60, and 3.24, respectively. Comparisons between common-effect and random-effects models also showed consistent directions of association across all four outcomes. These analyses, together with the subgroup-based sensitivity plots, are presented in Supplemental Fig. 2A–D.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test. The funnel plot for all-grade ALT elevation (Supplemental Fig. 3) showed asymmetry, and Egger’s test reached borderline statistical significance (p = 0.05). Therefore, Duval and Tweedie’s trim-and-fill analysis was further performed for this outcome. The trim-and-fill analysis imputed four potentially missing studies. After adjustment, the pooled estimate for all-grade ALT elevation remained statistically significant and was not attenuated (adjusted RR 2.00, 95% CI 1.49–2.69), compared with the original pooled estimate reported in Fig. 2 (RR 1.49, 95% CI 1.12–2.00; Supplemental Fig. 4). This result suggests that the association between NHT-based therapy and all-grade ALT elevation was robust to potential funnel plot asymmetry. By contrast, funnel plots for grade ≥ 3 ALT elevation (Supplemental Fig. 5), all-grade AST elevation (Supplemental Fig. 6), and grade ≥ 3 AST elevation (Supplemental Fig. 7) were generally symmetrical, with non-significant Egger’s test results (p = 0.347, p = 0.840, and p = 0.171, respectively).

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 10 randomized studies reported in 11 publications, NHT-containing therapy for prostate cancer was associated with a higher risk of hepatic enzyme abnormalities than control treatment. Significant associations were observed for all-grade ALT elevation, grade ≥ 3 ALT elevation, all-grade AST elevation, and grade ≥ 3 AST elevation, with pooled RRs of 1.49, 5.91, 1.58, and 3.32, respectively. Subgroup analyses suggested that the hepatic safety signal was most apparent for abiraterone-containing regimens and abiraterone plus enzalutamide combination regimens, although drug-specific estimates varied across outcomes. Because several subgroups, especially those for enzalutamide and darolutamide, included only a limited number of comparisons and had wide confidence intervals, these results should be interpreted cautiously. Non-significant subgroup findings should not be taken as definitive evidence of no hepatic risk.

The mechanisms underlying NHT-associated hepatotoxicity are likely heterogeneous and may differ across agents. For abiraterone, the mechanism of hepatic injury remains incompletely defined, but available evidence suggests that it may relate both to CYP17 inhibition and to hepatic metabolism through cytochrome P450 pathways, particularly CYP3A4 and CYP2D6, with possible formation of toxic or immunogenic intermediates [30–32]. By contrast, enzalutamide has generally been associated with mild, transient aminotransferase elevations, and clinically apparent liver injury appears to be uncommon [33]. Darolutamide showed less consistent subgroup patterns across outcomes in the present analysis, and the available estimates should be interpreted cautiously because they were based on a limited number of comparisons. Post-marketing and regulatory documents also indicate that idiosyncratic drug-induced liver injury should still be considered in the presence of relevant liver test abnormalities [34]. These differences are consistent with the nonuniform subgroup patterns observed in the present meta-analysis.

The clinical relevance of these findings lies in the fact that NHTs are frequently administered for prolonged durations and are often combined with androgen-deprivation therapy, corticosteroids, or chemotherapy. Under these circumstances, even laboratory-based hepatic adverse events may influence treatment continuation, dose modification, or the need for closer biochemical surveillance. This is particularly relevant for abiraterone, which has a recognized hepatic safety signal and has been associated with clinically relevant elevations in liver enzymes in both trial-based and post-treatment safety analyses [32]. In routine practice, these findings support regular liver function monitoring during NHT-based therapy, especially in patients receiving abiraterone-containing regimens, combination therapy, or long-term treatment [35].

Substantial heterogeneity was observed for all-grade ALT and AST elevations. Although subgroup analyses suggested that abiraterone-containing regimens contributed substantially to the hepatic signal, drug type alone may not fully explain the heterogeneity. Other trial-level factors may also have contributed, including disease stage, treatment duration, follow-up length, concomitant therapies, trial design, and adverse-event ascertainment. The included trials enrolled nmHSPC, mHSPC, and mCRPC populations, which may differ in disease burden, prior treatment exposure, baseline health status, and concomitant medications. Longer follow-up may increase the likelihood of detecting laboratory abnormalities, whereas short follow-up may underestimate delayed or recurrent events.

Concomitant therapies were another potential source of heterogeneity. Prednisone or prednisolone was co-administered with abiraterone-containing regimens, and docetaxel was used in ARASENS and in a subset of PEACE-1 patients. However, ALT/AST event counts stratified by corticosteroid or docetaxel use were not consistently available. Therefore, the independent contributions of these factors could not be estimated from aggregate data. Future trials should report hepatic adverse events stratified by disease stage, treatment backbone, corticosteroid use, docetaxel exposure, treatment duration, and follow-up interval.

Several limitations should be noted. First, this meta-analysis relied on published aggregate data rather than individual patient data, which precluded detailed analyses of time-to-event hepatotoxicity, dose–response relationships, and patient-level risk factors. Second, substantial heterogeneity was present for all-grade hepatic enzyme elevations, and residual heterogeneity from unmeasured trial-level factors cannot be excluded. Third, although funnel plot asymmetry and a borderline Egger’s test result were observed for all-grade ALT elevation, trim-and-fill analysis imputed four potentially missing studies and showed that the adjusted pooled estimate remained statistically significant. This supports the robustness of the all-grade ALT finding, although the trim-and-fill result should still be interpreted as exploratory because of the limited number of studies and substantial heterogeneity. Fourth, adverse-event ascertainment, reporting practices, and follow-up duration varied across trials. Fifth, the number of studies contributing to certain drug-specific subgroups was limited, which reduced the precision of subgroup estimates. Sixth, this review was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO; nevertheless, we followed PRISMA guidance and used a predefined search and analysis strategy. Finally, this meta-analysis evaluated laboratory-defined ALT and AST elevations rather than clinically apparent liver injury, recovery after hepatotoxicity, or outcomes after dose interruption, dose reduction, or treatment discontinuation. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of increased hepatic enzyme abnormality risk rather than direct evidence regarding reversibility or management outcomes.

Conclusion

NHT-based therapy was associated with an increased risk of ALT and AST elevations in patients with prostate cancer, particularly severe ALT elevation. The hepatic safety signal appeared more evident for abiraterone-containing and combination regimens, although drug-specific estimates for enzalutamide and darolutamide remained limited and should be interpreted cautiously. Because this meta-analysis assessed laboratory-defined ALT and AST elevations rather than reversibility, management outcomes, or clinically apparent liver injury, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of increased hepatic enzyme abnormality risk. Careful liver function monitoring remains warranted during NHT-based therapy, especially in patients receiving abiraterone-containing or prolonged combination regimens.
