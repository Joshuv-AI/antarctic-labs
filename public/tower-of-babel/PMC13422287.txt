Harnessing immunity against breast cancer: from checkpoints to cell therapies
Authors: Ali Mussa, Mahasin Hamid, Mustafa Talib, Nor Hayati Ismail, Fatmawati Lambuk, Abubakar Daku Bishir, Maaz Anwer Memon, Yusuf Lukman, Ruaa Suliman, Alaldeen Yagoub, Khalid Hajisa, Noor Fatmawati Mokhtar, Francesco M. Marincola, Rohimah Mohamud, Rosline Hassan, Mohammad A. I. Al-Hatamleh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Breast cancer (BC) remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality and a major contributor to disease burden among women worldwide. Although PD‑1/PD‑L1‑targeting immune checkpoint inhibitors have become a standard treatment for certain triple‑negative breast cancer subgroups, their use across the broader BC population is limited by intrinsic resistance, an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), and treatment‑related adverse effects. The present review offers a comprehensive synthesis of the immunotherapeutic paradigm in BC, spanning ICIs, adoptive cellular therapies (CAR-T, CAR-NK, CAR-M, TIL, TCR-T, and CIK cells), and emerging immune modulators. We critically evaluate pivotal clinical trials, discuss cross-cutting challenges—including biomarker development, therapy resistance, and the practical limitations of cellular products—and highlight rational combinatorial strategies. By contrasting the translational readiness of diverse modalities and outlining a framework for personalized therapy, this review aims to inform future research and clinical practice in harnessing immunity against BC.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide among women and the leading cause of cancer-associated deaths, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases (11% of all malignant tumors) and 666,000 deaths annually (6.9% of all malignant tumor mortalities) [1]. This disease encompasses a spectrum of molecular subtypes—luminal (hormone receptor-positive [HR+]/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 [HER2-negative (-)]), HER2-positive (+), and triple-negative (TNBC)—which exhibit distinct biological behaviors and therapeutic responses. Conventional treatments—surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy—are the mainstay of care, but their effectiveness is limited by resistance, recurrence, and systemic toxicities [2]. The high prevalence of therapeutic resistance is a primary driver for the development of novel strategies. In HR+ BC, intrinsic or acquired resistance to endocrine therapies occurs in approximately 30% of patients, limiting long-term efficacy [2]. The challenge of treatment failure extends across all major subtypes, with reported rates of resistance ranging from 20 to 50% in Luminal B, 10–40% in HER2-enriched disease, and up to 70% in TNBC [2]. This particularly severe resistance profile in TNBC exacerbates its poor prognosis [2, 3]. Collectively, this challenge underscores the urgent need for therapeutic alternatives, including immunotherapies

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is now recognized as a critical orchestrator of BC progression and treatment response. It is a complex ecosystem comprising immune cells, fibroblasts, vasculature and extracellular matrix [4]. Crucially, the tumor microenvironment in BC does not remain a passive observer. Instead, it actively contributes to the suppression of immune responses. Several immunosuppressive cell types infiltrate the TME to mediate this effect. These include cancer‑associated fibroblasts (CAFs), tumor‑associated macrophages (TAMs; also known as M2‑polarized macrophages), myeloid‑derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and regulatory T‑cells (Tregs) [5].

These cells promote immunosuppression through three main mechanisms. First, they secrete inhibitory mediators such as interleukin‑10 (IL‑10), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), hypoxia‑inducible factor 1‑alpha (HIF‑1α), and transforming growth factor‑beta (TGF‑β). Second, they upregulate certain inhibitory enzymes, including cyclooxygenase‑2 (COX‑2) and its product prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), indoleamine 2,3‑dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), and arginase-1 (ARG‑1). Third, they increase the expression of immune checkpoints like programmed death‑ligand 1 (PD‑L1). The net consequence is the inhibition of NK cells, type 1 conventional dendritic cells (c-DC1s) and CD8+ T cells inhibition/ exhaustion. Ultimately, these processes allow BC cells to escape anti-tumor immunity (Fig. 1) [5]. Historically, BC, particularly the HR+ subtype, has been immunologically classified as “cold” tumor, with low tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and a low mutational burden as well as more frequent PD-L1 expression in TNBC and HER2-positive disease compared to the more quiescent immune landscape of HR+/HER2- tumors [7]. However, this view has been dramatically revised. Significant heterogeneity in immune infiltration exists across BC subtypes, with TNBC and HER2+ often exhibiting an “inflamed or hot” phenotype. The number and composition of immune cells within the TME are now established powerful prognostic and predictive biomarkers [7].

Clinical advances achieved with ICIs directed at programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1)/PD-L1 have irrevocably transformed the landscape of TNBC treatment, validating the principle of reactivating the host immune system [8]. The field is now advancing into a new era of highly personalized cellular immunotherapies. This strategy involves engaging both innate and adaptive immunity via adoptive transfer approaches, including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells, CAR-engineered NK cells, CAR-modified macrophages (CAR-M), T cell receptor (TCR)-engineered T cells and cytokine-induced killer (CIK) cells, as well as leveraging the intrinsic anti-tumor response through TILs.

This review examines the clinical evolution of BC immunotherapy, from the current role of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to the emerging potential of cellular therapies. We critically evaluate strategies to synergize these modalities to overcome the immunosuppressive TME and achieve durable patient remission. This review distinguishes itself from prior summaries by providing an integrated, critical appraisal of both checkpoint and cellular therapies, with a specific focus on translational readiness, practical challenges (e.g., manufacturing, toxicity, cost), and biomarker-driven personalization. We synthesize data from pivotal clinical trials and highlight emerging preclinical-to-clinical bridges to outline a roadmap for the next generation of BC immunotherapy.

Methods and scope

This narrative review synthesizes evidence from key clinical trials, seminal preclinical studies, and published meta-analyses to provide a comprehensive overview of immunotherapy in BC. A systematic literature search was conducted using PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov databases for articles published between January 2010 and August 2025. Search terms included combinations of: “breast cancer,” “immunotherapy,” “immune checkpoint inhibitor,” “PD-1,” “PD-L1,” “CTLA-4,” “CAR-T,” “CAR-NK,” “CAR-macrophage,” “tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte,” “TCR-T,” and “cytokine-induced killer cell.” Emphasis was placed on phase I/II/III clinical trial results, practice-changing studies, and high-impact reviews. Conference abstracts and ahead-of-print manuscripts are cited with explicit caveats regarding their preliminary nature. The scope encompasses U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved therapies, investigational agents in advanced clinical development, and illustrative preclinical proof-of-concept studies that highlight novel mechanistic directions.

Immunotherapeutic strategies for tackling BC

The emergence of immunotherapy and cellular therapy have profoundly transformed the approach to BC treatment, presenting remarkable possibilities to leverage the body’s immune response in combating cancer. In this section, we explore the different therapeutic strategies that target BC.

Breaking immune silence in BC via ICIs

TNBC as an early-stage ICI target

Neoadjuvant therapy for early-stage TNBC seeks to establish a pathological complete response (pCR), which is characterized by the complete absence of invasive cancer in both the breast and lymph nodes at the time of surgery and it’s also strong prognostic indicator for improved overall survival (OS) and disease free survival (DFS) [9]. In early-stage TNBC, combining ICIs with chemotherapy improves pCR rates. Clinical trials such as KEYNOTE 522 (NCT03036488) show benefit across all PD-L1 expression subgroups. However, the degree of benefit, especially regarding pCR rates, tends to be greater in PD-L1 positive tumors, as demonstrated in the IMpassion031 trial. The use of specific cutoff values, for example a combined positive score (CPS) of 10 or higher, reveals a major challenge in selecting patients for therapy. Because these thresholds were defined after the fact (post hoc analysis), they demand complex companion diagnostic tests. They may also rule out patients with intermediate CPS scores, such as 1 to 9, who might still gain some benefit. Thus, whether PD-L1 testing is truly clinically necessary in the neoadjuvant setting remains an open question under active research and debate. Current guidelines tend to use PD-L1 testing mainly for prognostic purposes rather than as an absolute requirement to decide whether to start treatment [10]. Pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1 antibody) is FDA-approved for neoadjuvant early-stage TNBC. Early data from KEYNOTE-173 [11] and I-SPY2 trials [12, 13] established its efficacy with neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The definitive pivotal phase III KEYNOTE-522 trial revealed a notable increase in the pCR rate (64.8% compared to 51.2%) and a reduced risk of recurrence (HR 0.63) when utilizing neoadjuvant and adjuvant pembrolizumab when combined with chemotherapy [10]. The advantage was notably more significant in cases of node-positive disease, irrespective of the status of PD-L1. Updated 5-year event-free survival and overall survival data continue to show benefit for pembrolizumab [14, 15], despite some complex findings in patients with high residual cancer burden [16]. Adverse events related to immune activation were observed in about one-third of patients, reflecting immune activation against normal tissues, but the overall benefit of neoadjuvant pembrolizumab remains substantial [17]. The KEYNOTE-522 regimen defines the current standard of care for early-stage TNBC. However, this benefit comes with an increased incidence of immune-related adverse events (irAEs), necessitating vigilant monitoring and management, which may limit its application in patients with underlying autoimmune conditions [10]. Although, efforts to de-escalate anthracyclines while maintaining efficacy are ongoing (e.g., NeoPACT trial [18] and SWOG2212/SCARLET trial [NCT05929768]). While this regimen sets a new standard, its widespread adoption is tempered by the increased incidence of irAEs, necessitating careful patient selection and monitoring. Similarly, neoadjuvant atezolizumab also shows promise. In IMpassion031, patients receiving nab-paclitaxel with atezolizumab followed by ddAC achieved a pCR rate of 58%, compared to 41% with chemotherapy alone, while PD-L1–positive patients showed a higher response, with 68.8% reaching pCR compared to 49.3% in the PD-L1–negative group [19]. However, the NeoTRIPaPDL1/Michelangelo study did not show a pCR benefit with atezolizumab [20], possibly due to a higher proportion of node-positive patients. The GeparNuevo trial investigated neoadjuvant durvalumab, which did not yield a significant increase in pCR; however, it did demonstrate an enhanced 3-year DFS of 85.6% compared to 77.2% (HR 0.48), indicating potential long-term advantages of early ICI administration in the absence of adjuvant ICI [21, 22].

Several biomarkers like DetermaIO [23] and ImPrintTN [24] are being investigated to predict immunotherapy response. New avenues include neoadjuvant antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) like sacituzumab govitecan (NeoSTAR trial, 30% pCR [25]) and patritumab deruxtecan (SOLTI TOT-HER3 trial, 35% overall response rate (ORR) [26]). Studies are also evaluating optimal sequencing of neoadjuvant immunotherapy, with early results from the Neo-N trial which show similar pCR rates for lead-in versus concurrent nivolumab with chemotherapy [27]. Phase II trials such as BELLINI (NCT02755597) [28] and CHARIOT (ACTRN12617000651381) [29, 30] have investigated two neoadjuvant ICI therapy (PD-1 plus CTLA-4), demonstrating encouraging event-free and OS outcomes, particularly in PD-L1–positive and/or TIL-high subgroups, despite variability in pCR rates. In contrast, adjuvant ICIs have shown limited efficacy; the phase III IMpassion030/ALEXANDRIA trial (NCT03498716), evaluating adjuvant atezolizumab, was discontinued for futility, as shown in their early investigation [31]. Collectively, current evidence favors initial systemic therapy, including immunotherapy, for early-stage TNBC, whereas adjuvant use is more targeted.

TNBC as an advanced (aTNBC)/metastatic (mTNBC) target for ICI

Early clinical efforts in BC immunotherapy centered on using single agents in individuals with refractory, PD-L1–expressing metastatic or advanced TNBC. KEYNOTE-012 (phase 1b, 2016; NCT01848834) demonstrated a promising ORR of 18.5% with single-agent pembrolizumab in metastatic TNBC patients whose tumors expressed PD-L1 at or above 1% [32]. The phase II KEYNOTE-086 studies (NCT02447003) further evaluated single-agent pembrolizumab, reporting an overall response rate of 21% in treatment-naïve, PD-L1–positive mTNBC. In contrast, subjects receiving pembrolizumab beyond the first line demonstrated a lower ORR of 5.7%, regardless of PD-L1 status [33, 34]. These results suggest that ICIs may provide greater clinical benefit in PD-L1–expressing tumors when used as prior treatment regimen. Meanwhile, the phase III KEYNOTE-119 trial (NCT02555657), which compared pembrolizumab monotherapy with standard chemotherapeutic medicines in second- or third-line settings, did not demonstrate improvements in progression-free or overall survival. However, response rates were higher in patients with elevated PD-L1 combined positive scores, reaching their peak in those with combined positive score ≥ 10 [35, 36].

Likewise, according to the significant findings from the phase III KEYNOTE-355 trial (NCT02819518), pembrolizumab together with chemotherapy (nanoparticle albumin-bound paclitaxel, paclitaxel, or gemcitabine–carboplatin) has become the standard first-line therapy for metastatic TNBC or PD-L1–positive advanced [10, 37]. In this study of 847 patients, those with PD-L1 CPS ≥ 10 who received pembrolizumab demonstrated notable improvements in OS (23.0 vs. 16.1 months, HR 0.73) and progression-free survival (PFS; 9.7 vs. 5.6 months, HR 0.65) [10, 37]. A more pronounced PFS benefit was seen with the use of paclitaxel or nab-paclitaxel as fundamental treatments. Nonetheless, an exploratory analysis revealed diminished advantages of immunotherapy when paired with chemotherapy for early recurrence patients (6−12 months following curative-intent treatment) [37]. Furthermore, the superior PFS advantage observed with taxane backbones over gemcitabine/carboplatin suggests that the immunomodulatory effects of the specific chemotherapy partner are a key determinant of success, underscoring that ‘chemotherapy’ is not a monolithic partner for immunotherapy.

More specifically, Atezolizumab exhibited activity, achieving an ORR of 24% when used as a monotherapy first-line treatment [38] and 39.4% when combined with nab-paclitaxel in patients who had received prior treatment [39]. Importantly, 902 patients were randomized in the phase III IMpassion130 trial which revealed that the PFS advantage with the combination of nab-paclitaxel and atezolizumab (PFS extended to 7.5 months from 5.5 months) in the intention-to-treat cohort, with a more pronounced benefit among patients with PD-L1 expression > 1% (PFS extended from 5.0 months to 7.5 months) [40]. An overall survival benefit was also detected in the PD-L1–positive individuals [40]. However, later trials involving atezolizumab alongside various chemotherapy regimens demonstrated reduced efficacy. The IMpassion131 trial, which investigated the combination of paclitaxel and atezolizumab, revealed no significant differences in PFS or OS [41, 42], potentially attributable to variations in chemotherapy manufacturing or pre-treatment protocols. The discordant outcomes between IMpassion130 (nab-paclitaxel) and IMpassion131 (paclitaxel) underscore the critical, yet incompletely understood, influence of chemotherapy backbone and premedication (e.g., corticosteroids) on ICI efficacy, highlighting that not all chemotherapies synergize equally with immunotherapy. On the other hand, the IMpassion132 study, evaluating atezolizumab in early-relapsing TNBC (relapse < 12 months after prior treatment), also showed no improvement in median disease-free survival or overall survival [43], suggesting potential intrinsic resistance in this rapid-relapse population.

Beyond PD-L1, the Atract1B phase II trial challenged the necessity of PD-L1 positivity for ICI benefit. Preliminary data from this trial showed an 11.0-month median PFS and 63% ORR with paclitaxel, atezolizumab and bevacizumab in largely PD-L1 negative advanced TNBC [44]. Dual ICI therapy (e.g., durvalumab + tremelimumab (anti-CTLA-4) (Fig. 1) [45], ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4) + nivolumab (anti-PD-1) [46]) has shown some clinical activity but with augmented toxicity concerns (Fig. 1).

The combined administration of PARP inhibitors with ICIs demonstrates potential in conditions such as mutated BRCA. The combination of olaparib plus durvalumab resulted in a 63.3% RR in BC individuals with severely pretreated, BRCA-mutant, metastatic tumors of HER2-negative origin [47]. The TOPACIO/KEYNOTE-162 trial (NCT02657889) revealed that the objective response rates were significantly elevated with the combination of pembrolizumab and niraparib in cases of BRCA-mutated a/mTNBC, achieving 47%. However, BRCA-wild type cases only 11% ORR was observed [48]. The optimized execution of maintenance regimens represents a dynamic field of inquiry, as evidenced by studies such as DORA [49] and KEYLYNK-009 [50, 51], which investigate chemotherapy-free alternatives following patient response. Recently, preliminary results from the Phase 2 ATRACTIB trial (NCT04408118), conducted across multiple centers as a single-arm study, investigated the first-line combination of atezolizumab (840 mg), paclitaxel (90 mg/m2), and bevacizumab (10 mg/kg) in subjects with aTNBC, notably regardless of their PD-L1 status [52]. A total of one hundred female patients participated in the study, with a significant majority of the evaluable tumors (97.6%) exhibiting a PD-L1-negative status. The primary endpoint, assessed by the investigator, was met, with findings showing a median PFS of 11.0 months (95% CI 9.0–13.4; p < 0.001). A response rate of 63.0% (95% CI 52.8–72.4) was observed, alongside a median OS of 27.4 months (95% CI 23.4–37.4). Furthermore, 80% (95% CI 70.8–87.3) was observed to be the clinical benefit rate (CBR), while the median duration of response (DoR) was recorded at 10.1 months (95% CI 7.3–13.9) [52]. Although all patients experienced treatment emergent adverse events (TEAEs), with 61% demonstrating grade 3 or 4 TEAEs; however, throughout the trial, there were no fatalities associated with the treatment, nor were any new safety concerns identified. This combination exhibited notable antitumor efficacy as a primary treatment for patients with aTNBC [52]. Future studies must validate long-term efficacy, identify synergistic combination therapies, and decipher the mechanisms of response to enable refined treatment paradigms for aTNBC.

LAG-3 stands out as a novel immune-inhibitory receptor, predominantly up-regulated on T-regs and exhausted T cells. Unlike PD-1/PD-L1 or CTLA-4 immuno-inhibitors, LAG-3 inhibition uniquely offers a dual benefit; it can both suppress T-reg function and directly augment effector T-cell activity. IMP321, also known as eftilagimod alpha, represents a prominent drug directed at this pathway. It is a soluble fusion protein based on LAG‑3 and immunoglobulin. As an agonist, IMP321 functions by binding to major histocompatibility complex II (MHC class II) molecules on antigen-presenting cells (APCs), thereby stimulating them leading to subsequent anti-tumor CD8+ T-cell activation. Early clinical investigations into IMP321 showed promising results (Fig. 2). In a Phase I/II study (NCT00349934), the combination of IMP321 with paclitaxel given as first‑line therapy produced an encouraging OR rate of 50%. This efficacy was notably superior, significantly outperforming the 25% response rate typically observed in historical control groups [53]. Conversely, the AIPAC study (Phase IIb trial, NCT02614833), specifically examining IMP321 plus weekly paclitaxel in HR+/HER2- metastatic BC patients, failed to achieve its objective of extending median PFS [54]. Although a prolongation of median progression-free survival was not observed, IMP321 exhibited a tolerable safety profile in preliminary revealed data. The agent induced a prolonged elevation of CD8+ T cells in the bloodstream and produced clinical advantages for specific patient groups [54]. In light of previous findings, the ongoing Phase III AIPAC-003 trial (NCT05747794) is currently comparing IMP321 in combination with paclitaxel against placebo plus paclitaxel. The HR+/HER2-negative or low and endocrine therapy-resistant metastatic BC patients was specifically targeted in this study [55].

Initial investigations into another LAG-3 inhibitor, the NCT02460224 trial, evaluated ieramilimab (LAG525), a LAG-3 inhibitor, both as a monotherapy and in combination with spartalizumab (PDR001; anti-PD-1) in patients with advanced refractory solid tumors (Fig. 2) [56]. Ieramilimab was well-tolerated, with no maximum tolerated dose reached and a toxicity profile similar to spartalizumab monotherapy. Although the overall response rate was modest (3 complete and 10 partial responses), responses occurred in tumor types typically resistant to immunotherapy, including PD-L1–negative TNBC. Notably, some responses were remarkably durable, exceeding 4 years [56], suggesting the long-term tolerability and augmented therapeutic benefit of combined LAG-3 and PD-1 blockade. A Phase II trial of ieramilimab (anti-LAG-3) and spartalizumab (anti-PD-1) in advanced solid tumors confirmed a manageable safety profile and durable responses in anti-PD-1/L1–naïve patients [57]. In TNBC cohorts, the efficacy was confined to naive patients (ORR 14.3%), with no responses observed in the pretreated group. Biomarker analysis linked response to a pre-existing T-cell-inflamed TME, not baseline LAG-3 protein levels [57]. This study reinforces the tolerability of LAG-3/PD-1 co-blockade and underscores efficacy differences between naive and pretreated populations.

The LAG-3 pathway is a key focus in precision oncology, exemplified by trials like NCT02465060 (The MATCH Screening Trial), a Phase II study investigating therapy directed by genetic testing in advanced refractory cancers, which includes the LAG-3 inhibitor relatlimab. Extensive clinical investigations are actively exploring regimens such as anti-LAG-3 (NCT03250832) in conjunction with anti-PD-1, dual blockade with anti-LAG-3 plus anti-PD-1 (NCT03628677) and anti-TIGIT, and the administration of anti-LAG-3 with anti-PD-L1 (NCT03514121) and anti-B7–H4.

Comparative efficacy and toxicity of ICIs across breast cancer subtypes

The comparative efficacy and toxicity of ICIs across the various BC subtypes—such as TNBC, HER2–positive BC, and HR+ BC—demonstrate unique trends dependent upon tumor biology, immunogenicity, and TME characteristics. Among all BC subtypes, TNBC exhibits the highest immunogenicity due to a higher tumor mutational burden (TMB) and elevated expression of PD-L1 (PD-L1-positive subset). These characteristics provide a favorable environment for ICIs to function effectively compared to other subtypes [58–60]. TNBC has shown the most clinically significant responses to ICIs, particularly in both advanced metastatic and early-stage (neoadjuvant) settings. For example, the combination of ICIs like pembrolizumab or atezolizumab with standard chemotherapy has shown improved pCR rates [37, 61, 62]. Consequently, pembrolizumab, an anti-PD-1 inhibitor, has received FDA approval for TNBC in combination with chemotherapy, underscoring its role in improving PFS and OS in this aggressive subtype. Despite these survival benefits, TNBC-associated ICI treatments demonstrate typical irAEs, including rash, pneumonitis, colitis, inflammatory arthritis diarrhea, and hypothyroidism. To mitigate these risks, predictive biomarkers, such as PD-L1 and TMB, help identify patients more likely to respond to therapy while minimizing unnecessary toxicity risks [63].

While HER2-positive BC is moderately immunogenic, it relies significantly on HER2-targeted therapies (trastuzumab, pertuzumab). Combining ICIs with HER2-targeted agents has shown potential, particularly in HER2-enriched subtypes with greater levels of PD-L1 expression in the TME [59]. Clinical trials are beginning to explore ICIs in combination with trastuzumab deruxtecan or other HER2-directed antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs). Early evidence indicates modest improvements in immune activation, though results remain less substantial than in TNBC [64]. Compounding this modest efficacy, HER2-directed therapy introduces overlapping toxicities, notably cardiotoxicity and pneumonitis, which can compound irAEs induced by ICIs. This necessitates close cardiovascular monitoring in combination settings [65]. Similarly, HR+/HER2- BC particularly luminal subtypes, generally exhibits lower TMB and decreased immune infiltration but also it is largely resistant to single-agent ICIs, reflecting its immunologically “cold” phenotype making it less responsive to immunotherapies compared to TNBC [66]. Attempts to enhance therapeutic efficacy by adding ICIs to endocrine therapy (e.g., aromatase inhibitors) or CDK4/6 inhibitors are under investigation. Preliminary studies highlight limited success due to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in HR+ subtypes [59, 67]. The combination of ICIs with endocrine agents does not significantly escalate toxicity when compared to ICI monotherapy, but immune-related endocrinopathies, such as autoimmune thyroiditis, are important considerations [68]. This underscores the critical need for better predictive biomarkers to mandates a personalized approach to ICI use, balancing potential benefit against the risk of toxicity.

Indeed, patients treated with ICIs may experience late-onset irAEs, particularly after treatment cessation [68, 69]. These events emphasize the importance of long-term monitoring strategies [68]. Toxicity severity may vary across subtypes due to differences in the TME and pre-existing immune states. Notably, TNBC and HER2-positive tumors tend to exhibit slightly more inflammation-related side effects, consistent with their higher baseline immune activation [70]. Future studies should focus on optimizing ICI combinations for lower-responding subtypes like HR+ and HER2-positive cancers, potentially integrating novel immunotherapeutics or TME-targeting agents. To improve patient selection and mitigate toxicity, further research into robust predictive biomarkers is essential. Combining molecular and immune profiling (e.g., genomics, epigenomics, transcriptomics) could enable accurate responder stratification. Strategies to better predict and manage irAEs in at-risk patients, such as biomarker-guided risk stratification models, could optimize the therapeutic index for ICIs in BC.

Adoptive cellular therapies in BC: advancing immunotherapeutic frontiers

In cancer immunotherapy, adoptive cellular therapies (ACTs) occupy a key position by directly equipping and deploying a patient’s own or genetically engineered immune cells against malignancy. Through varied mechanisms, these strategies target and eliminate BC cells, thereby meeting diverse clinical needs and matching distinct tumor characteristics.

CAR cell therapy

CAR cells exemplify a sophisticated approach to adoptive immunotherapy, wherein T lymphocytes, NK cells and macrophages are meticulously modified to express synthetic CARs (Figs. 3a and b). These engineered receptors empower these cells to precisely identify and eradicate tumor cells without reliance on MHC [71, 72]. In particular, CAR-T cells, highly effective in hematological cancers, recognize specific tumor antigens, proliferate, and exert cytotoxicity through the release of perforin/granzyme and pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2) [73]. However, excessive and dysregulated release of these and other cytokines (such as IL-6, IL-1β, IL-8, and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF)) can lead to cytokine release syndrome (CRS) (Fig. 4) [74]. Their activity can be hindered by a TME rich in factors such as TGF-β, IL-10, PGE2, and VEGF, which promote T-cell exhaustion and upregulate enzymes like ARG-1, MMPs and COX-2 (Fig. 4) [75, 76]. It should be noted that all the cells of the TME can assist in the secretion of these cytokines, amplifying the development of CRS. CAR-T cells may also lead to the development of a severe immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) (Fig. 4). This syndrome can manifest as confusion, seizures, and other neurological symptoms, complicating the clinical management of patients undergoing CAR-T cell therapy [74]. CAR-NK cells, conversely, offer a favorable safety profile with lower CRS/ ICANS) incidence, leveraging their natural cytotoxicity and engineered specificity via IFN-γ and TNF-α production, while also boasting off-the-shelf potential [77]. Meanwhile, CAR macrophages (CAR-Ms) uniquely infiltrate dense solid tumors, mediating phagocytosis, antigen presentation and TME remodeling, with early data suggesting a potentially safer toxicity profile than CAR-T cells [72]. These diverse cell therapy modalities aim to overcome cancer evasion by distinct mechanisms, but ongoing research is crucial to optimize their efficacy and manage associated toxicities.

A standard CAR has three primary structural modules. These include an extracellular domain responsible for antigen recognition, a transmembrane segment, and one or more intracellular signaling domains [78]. A extracellular region (a single-peptide chain variable fragment (scFv)) rants target specificity and is often connected by a flexible hinge/spacer region [79]. Stability of the CAR is ensured by its transmembrane domain, which attaches the receptor to the cell membrane. This domain also serves as a connector between the extracellular and intracellular regions [79]. Lastly, the intracellular signaling region, typically containing the CD3ζ chain and co-stimulatory fragments (e.g., CD28, 4-1BB), orchestrates cell activation, proliferation and effector functions upon antigen binding [80].

The efficacy of CAR-T cells has significantly evolved through successive structural generations, primarily defined by their intracellular signaling domains [76]. First-generation CARs incorporated only the CD3ζ signaling domain, providing antigen-specific activation but lacking robust proliferation and long-term persistence [81]. Second-generation CARs, currently the basis for FDA-approved products, introduced an additional co-stimulatory region (e.g., CD28 or 4-1BB), significantly enhancing T cell activation, proliferation, survival and sustained anti-tumor activity [81]. Third-generation CAR constructs aim to amplify intracellular signaling by integrating dual costimulatory endodomains—most commonly pairing CD28 or ICOS with TNF receptor superfamily members like 4-1BB, OX40, or CD27 with CD3ζ, aiming for even greater potency and persistence [82, 83]. Similarly, through the integration of an inducible transgene under the control of a nuclear factor of activated T-cells (NFAT)-derived minimal promoter, fourth-generation CAR-T cells (TRUCKs; T cells redirected for universal cytokine-mediated killing) can initiate IL-12 transcription upon TCR/CD3 pathway signaling. This modification of a second-generation CAR allows for the regulated release of cytokines—including IL-12, IL-15, IL-18, or IL-23—to effectively remodel the TME and enhance the recruitment of endogenous immune cells (Fig. 4a) [82, 84–86]. The emerging fifth-generation CARs aim to further refine activation and persistence by incorporating additional intracellular signaling motifs, such as those from cytokine receptors (e.g., IL-2 R β chain), directly linking to pathways like JAK-STAT to enhance T cell function and persistence [82].

Complementary to generational advances in CAR architecture, recent clinical evidence has highlighted that the differentiation state of the infused T cell product is itself a critical determinant of therapeutic outcome. In a first-in-human Phase I trial (NCT01087294), Gattinoni et al. demonstrated that CAR-T cells enriched for the stem cell memory T (TSCM) subset (a minimally differentiated population endowed with self-renewal and multipotency, generated using a 7-day clinical-grade protocol incorporating IL-7, IL-21, and the GSK-3β inhibitor TWS119) exhibited superior in vivo expansion, achieved complete responses at significantly lower doses, and reconstituted the TSCM compartment over time through sequential waves of clonal succession [87]. While this work was conducted in B-cell malignancies post-allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), the authors propose that the TSCM-based manufacturing approach is applicable to autologous CAR-T therapies and may play a critical role in overcoming the persistence and exhaustion barriers that currently hinder CAR-T efficacy in solid tumors, including BC.

CAR-(T) cell therapy

Although CAR-T cell therapy has made remarkable advancements and successes in the treatment of hematologic malignancies (Fig. 4) [88], its utilization in solid tumors, including BC, faces numerous substantial challenges. The challenges at hand predominantly encompass tumor heterogeneity, which may result in antigen escape, restricted T cell homing to solid tumor locations and the typically immunosuppressive characteristics of the TME [89].

Despite these challenges, clinical trials are presently investigating the ability of CAR-T cells to target multiple BC-associated antigens (Fig. 4A). Promising targets presently being evaluated include various cell surface proteins and receptors such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), folate receptor alpha (FRα), HER2, HER3/4, MUC1, mesothelin (MSLN), trophoblast cell surface protein 2 (TROP2) and receptor tyrosine-like orphan receptor 1 (ROR1), among others (Fig. 4) [89]. A principal challenge for these therapies in solid tumors remains the identification of truly tumor-specific antigens to minimize the risk of ON-target/OFF-tumor toxicity, a hurdle less prominent in hematologic malignancies. Initial clinical trials focusing on BC have typically shown the safety of these CAR-T cell methodologies and have offered preliminary indications of their effectiveness. While promising preclinical, in vivo studies [90] and in vitro studies [91] form the foundation and numerous clinical trials are presently underway for CAR-T cell therapy in BC, the majority are in early phases (e.g., Phase I/II) focusing on safety and preliminary efficacy.

For example, a Phase 1 trial (NCT02414269) evaluated intrapleurally administered MSLN-targeted CAR-T cells in 20 patients, including one with BC, primarily focusing on malignant pleural mesothelioma. The study reported no CAR-T cell-related toxicities higher than grade 1, with careful monitoring showing no indication of ON-target/OFF-tumor toxicity [91]. Encouraging anti-tumor activity was detected, particularly when combined with anti-PD1 therapy, with some patients achieving complete metabolic response, partial responses, or stable disease. This trial highlights the safety and potential benefit of MSLN-targeted CAR-T cells, especially in combination with checkpoint blockade [91]. The Phase 1 trial NCT04020575 is evaluating the safety of huMNC2–CAR44 CAR-T cells targeting MUC1 in patients with MUC1-positive metastatic BC. Using lymphodepletion and dose escalation, the trial aims to determine the maximum tolerated dose (MTD). Secondary endpoints assess CAR-T cell persistence, phenotype, and preliminary antitumor activity across luminal, HER2-positive, and TNBC subtypes [92]. The Phase 1 trial NCT02706392 is assessing ROR1-targeting CAR-T cells in patients with advanced, ROR1-positive NSCLC or TNBC. Using lymphodepletion and dose escalation to determine the dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs), preliminary data report an absence of severe cytokine release syndrome, dose-limiting toxicities, or severe neurotoxicity. Some patients have observed early evidence of antitumor activity, including stable disease and partial responses, along with CAR-T cell expansion [93]. A fundamental challenge for these therapies in solid tumors remains the identification of tumor-specific antigens with minimal expression on vital healthy tissues, a hurdle less prominent in hematologic malignancies and a key driver of On-target/OFF-tumor toxicity. In addition, substantial research efforts remain focused on refining multiple components of CAR-T cell treatment to enhance their potency and persistence within the immunosuppressive TME. A critical priority is devising strategies to mitigate cytokine release syndrome, ON-target/OFF-tumor and neurotoxicity, a significant challenge when targeting antigens like HER2 that are expressed on some healthy tissues [90, 94, 95].

In this context, a particularly compelling advance has been the clinical demonstration that T cell product composition can simultaneously address both the persistence deficit and the CRS burden inherent to conventional CAR-T therapy. Gattinoni et al. recently reported that donor-derived CD8+ CAR-T cells enriched for the TSCM subset, achieved a 4.5-fold higher peak expansion and a 6.8-fold greater area under the expansion curve relative to standard products, while inducing only grade 1–2 CRS driven by IFN-γ rather than the IL-6–mediated inflammatory surge characteristic of conventional products without lymphodepletion [87]. Mechanistically, long-term CAR-TSCM persistence was sustained through sequential clonal succession waves analogous to hematopoietic stem cell repopulation, rather than through simple maintenance of initially expanded clones, a finding with direct implications for the development of durable CAR-T products in BC [87]. While this work was conducted in post-transplant B-cell malignancies, adapting the TSCM manufacturing platform to BC-relevant CAR constructs, including those targeting HER2, MUC1, B7–H3, and mesothelin, represents a compelling and clinically actionable strategy to overcome the exhaustion and persistence barriers that have historically limited CAR-T efficacy in solid tumors.

Likewise, a major problem in BC therapy is the phenomenon of acquired resistance, frequently resulting from tumor cells’ loss of the antigens that therapies aim to target. For instance, HER2, a prevalent target, may be released from the cell surface yet still permit the cancer to proliferate [96]. To address these challenges, dual-targeting CAR-T cells combat antigen escape and enhance anti-tumor activity through mechanisms like cytokine production (e.g., IL-12) and costimulatory ligands (e.g., 4-1BBL), promoting epitope spreading are being developed. To systematically capture the rapidly evolving clinical landscape, a growing number of clinical trials are evaluating the safety and preliminary efficacy of CAR‑based therapies in BC. This includes investigational CAR‑T cells targeting antigens such as HER2 (including in brain metastases), MSLN, MUC1 (with PD‑1 knockout), ROR1, CEA, EpCAM, B7‑H3, and EGFR/B7H3, as well as multi‑targeted approaches. Early-phase trials of CAR‑NK cell products (e.g., targeting MUC1) and pioneering CAR‑macrophage (CAR‑M) studies targeting HER2 are also underway. Together, these studies reflect the ongoing translational effort to overcome the unique challenges of solid tumors, such as antigen heterogeneity, immunosuppressive TME, and ON‑target/OFF‑tumor toxicity. CAR-NK cell therapy

In the innate immune system, NK cells are fundamental component, functioning as lymphocytes capable of initiating anti-tumor immunity without the necessity of prior antigen presentation or MHC restriction [97]. The adoptive transfer of activated exogenous NK cells has demonstrated notable therapeutic effects, including BC cell proliferation inhibition and the triggering of apoptosis within these cells [98, 99]. Notably, the emergence of CAR-NK cells represents a particularly promising development in this field. These cells are specifically engineered NK cells that express CARs, presenting unique benefits by having wider applicability due to the absence of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) matching requirements, rendering them appealing for the advancement of off-the-shelf immunotherapies as demonstrated in several in vitro, in vivo cancers as well as in cancer patients (Fig. 4B) [99–102].

Importantly, CAR-NK cells are specifically designed to target multiple BC-associated antigens. Promising targets presently being evaluated include various cell surface proteins and receptors such as EGFR, TROP2, MSLN, HER2, CEA, PD-L1, CD276, EpCAM, HER3/4, CD19 and MUC1, among others (Fig. 4) [99–103]. The CAR-NK cells mechanism of action involves the secretion of cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ, IL-12 and IL-15), direct killing by the secretion of perforin and granzymes as well as antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC). CAR-NK cells also use the death receptor pathways (such as TRAIL/FasL), which is further enhanced by the CAR for precise antigen recognition (Fig. 4B). This enables the surmounting of tumor heterogeneity and the evasion mechanisms of antigens commonly observed in BC TME [102, 104].

Preclinical development

The TME significantly impedes immune cell function, necessitating innovative strategies to enhance cellular therapy. A key challenge is oxidative stress; for instance, peroxiredoxin-1 (PRDX1) deficiency functionally impairs NK cells. Notably, IL-15 can restore NK cell function by upregulating PRDX1 expression, highlighting a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention. Klopotowska et al. developed PD-L1-CAR-NK cells to consistently overexpress PRDX1. The altered cells exhibited significant antitumor efficacy against BC cells, even in the presence of oxidative stress typical of the TME [105]. This methodical engineering approach significantly enhances CAR-NK cell efficacy, enabling robust cancer cell targeting and elimination even within hostile, oxidative TME conditions. Beyond conferring resilience, CAR-NK cells can be further engineered to actively counteract tumor-induced immunosuppression mechanisms. Chia-Ing et al. revealed that NK cells engineered with HLA-G CAR exhibit strong cytotoxicity against in vitro models of breast, ovarian, brain and pancreatic cancer cells, while also significantly inhibiting tumor growth and prolonging survival in orthotopic mouse xenograft [106]. Mechanistically, the anti-HLA-G scFv activated Syk/Zap70, reversing HLA-G-mediated immunosuppression to restore cytotoxicity. Combining this CAR-NK therapy with chemotherapy (in low-dose) synergistically enhances tumor killing in vitro and in vivo, as chemotherapy upregulates HLA-G expression via DNMT1 inhibition and TAP1 promoter demethylation in tumors [106].

Another compelling example in BC targeting involves HER2-CAR-NK cells. Data indicate that NK cells-expressing HER2 CARs, whether from BC patients or healthy donors, significantly enhances their anti-tumor efficacy toward various HER2-expressing cancer cells, regardless of the expression of MHC class I [107]. Notably, HER2-CAR-NK cells show enhanced cytotoxicity in targeting tumors over donor-matched HER2 CAR-T cells [107], suggesting that, these NK cells retain high cytotoxicity within immunosuppressive solid tumor microenvironments, highlighting their potential for safe and effective solid tumor immunotherapy. For instance, EGFR-CAR-NK cells are directed at EGFR-overexpressing TNBC. In vitro, they exhibit strong, EGFR-specific activity and killing of TNBC lines, marked by a substantial rise in IFN-γ, granzyme B, and perforin [108]. This targeted activation results in potent in vivo anti-tumor efficacy, where EGFR-CAR-NK cells markedly suppressed TNBC tumor growth and prolonged survival in xenograft and PDX models, without causing systemic toxicity [108].

Chen’s team further validated EGFR-CAR-NK-92 cells’ potential, showing suppressed tumor growth in murine models alongside elevated cytolysis and IFN-γ secretion in MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, and MCF-7 lines [109]. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of EGFR-CAR-NK cells as a safe and effective therapeutic strategy for EGFR-positive TNBC. Similarly, Hu and his colleagues developed and tested a second-generation tissue factor (TF)-CAR-NK construct that co-expressed CD16 (FcγIII) for enhanced ADCC [110]. (TF)-CAR-NK cells demonstrated elevated cytotoxicity against TNBC cells in vitro, an effect that was further enhanced through L-ICON–mediated ADCC. In vivo, these engineered NK cells showed therapeutic efficacy in TNBC models, including both cell line–derived and patient-derived xenografts [110]. This work substantiates targeting tissue factors with CAR-NK cells as a pioneering and efficacious framework for TNBC treatment.

Exemplifying this framework, the CAT-179 CAR-NK platform targets HER2, demonstrating both CAR-dependent and innate NK receptor killing, thereby reducing antigen escape. This off-the-shelf therapy is engineered for TGF-β resistance, preventing immune receptor downregulation and maintaining cytotoxicity, leading to significant cancer reduction and prolonged survival in xenograft models with prolonged cell persistence [111]. As evident by a notable 75% decrease in TGF-β-induced SMAD2 phosphorylation relative to controls. This alteration concurrently blocked TGF-β-mediated suppression of NK-activating receptors and fully restored their tumor-killing function [111]. The advantage of dual targeting is further highlighted by PD-L1/ErbB2-CAR-NK-92 cells, which significantly increased cytotoxic activity in vitro against tumor cell lines co-expressing both markers [112]. This enhanced killing, mediated by synergistic PLCγ/MAPK activation, effectively overcomes immune escape due to single antigen loss, representing a strategy to counteract potential resistance and enhance CAR-NK efficacy in difficult-to-treat tumors [112]. CAR-NK cells demonstrate the ability to circumvent BC resistance mechanisms, by intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) downregulation, which limits trastuzumab-mediated ADCC. Unlike ADCC, CAR-targeted NK cell cytotoxicity and activation remained robust, independent of lymphocyte function-associated antigen 1 (LFA-1) co-stimulation or the expression of ICAM-1 [113]. This highlights CAR-NK cells’ unique advantage in overcoming immune escape mechanisms by ensuring efficient degranulation despite inhibitory signals or altered adhesion molecule expression. Recent advancements encompass anti-HER2-CAR-NK cells (CAR-SNK02) produced through the SNK02 platform, exhibiting billion-fold expansion alongside sustained CAR expression [114]. These cells demonstrate significant anti-HER2 activity, effectively targeting low HER2-expressing cells, overcoming the constraints of trastuzumab-induced ADCC and retaining functionality post freezing/thawing [114]. Their high expandability and persistent activity highlight their considerable potential as an off-the-shelf immune cell therapy as well as their innate biology may confer a lower risk of CRS and neurotoxicity compared to CAR-T cells. These compelling preclinical findings must now be validated in clinical trials, where challenges such as achieving sufficient in vivo persistence, preventing host rejection of allogeneic products, and scaling up manufacturing present significant translational barriers.

Clinical Translation

Reflecting these encouraging preclinical findings, several early-phase clinical studies are now exploring the safety and efficacy of CAR-NK cell therapies. For instance, NCT02839954 is investigating anti-MUC1 CAR-pNK cell in various MUC1-positive refractory or relapsed solid tumors, including TNBC. Another significant ongoing trial, NCT05686720, focuses specifically on advanced TNBC, assessing the SZ011 CAR-NK cells efficacy (Table 1).

CAR-macrophage (CAR-M) therapy represents a viable immunotherapeutic strategy for BC, utilizing the distinctive characteristics of macrophages to effectively target and eradicate tumor cells (Fig. 5) [115]. This groundbreaking approach to ACT utilizes genetically modified macrophages to precisely identify and eradicate tumor cells via phagocytosis [115]. Preclinical rationale and proof-of-concept

Preclinical studies demonstrate compelling efficacy for CAR-M therapy. In a HER2-positive murine model (4T1), HER2-targeting CAR-Ms (CAR-147) significantly inhibited tumor growth. This was accompanied by extracellular matrix remodeling, evidenced by reduced collagen deposition, and enhanced T-cell infiltration. Crucially, these CAR-Ms also markedly reduced systemic levels of TNF-α and IL-6, suggesting a potential mitigation of cytokine-related toxicities like CRS (Fig. 5) [118]. Another study demonstrated that engineered CAR-monocytes (CAR-Mono) efficiently differentiate into anti-tumor M1-polarized macrophages in vivo. In various HER2-positive solid tumor xenograft models, these CAR-Mono exhibited potent anti-tumor efficacy, effectively trafficked to tumor sites and maintained durable CAR expression and persistence for over 100 days [119]. A novel HER2-targeting CAR-THP1 cell was engineered with a CD3ζ/CD147/iCasp9 backbone, demonstrating robust, T cell-independent anti-tumor activity. The iCasp9 suicide switch enabled controlled proliferation. Transcriptomics confirmed M1 polarization, bridging innate and adaptive immunity. Combined with retronectin-activated killers (RAKs), CAR-THP1 enhanced therapy by secreting MMPs leading to tumor stroma degradation, which in turn, facilitate T-cell infiltration [120]. These findings suggest that targeting the ECM and modulating the TME with engineered macrophages could be an effective treatment strategy for solid tumors, including BC.

In vitro, CAR-M phagocytosed and eliminated HER2+ tumor cells. Furthermore, they enhanced antigen presentation by upregulating MHC-I on malignant cells, and subsequent CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity stimulation. In vivo, CAR-M monotherapy induced significant tumor repression and prolonged survival in several syngeneic models via increased infiltration of intra-tumoral NK cells CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and dendritic cells (Fig. 6) [72]. These findings establish the standalone efficacy of CAR-M in controlling solid cancers. In HER2+ cancers resistant to anti-PD1 monotherapy, combining CAR-M with αPD1 significantly improved tumor control and survival over either agent alone. This synergy was associated with beneficial TME reprogramming, effectively sensitizing non-responsive tumors to checkpoint blockade [72]. Thus, CAR-M therapy represents a promising complement or alternative to CAR-T cells in solid malignancies. First-in-human clinical trials

Initial clinical translation of this preclinical promise is underway. The Phase I, NCT04660929 trial is the first-in-human trial evaluating the anti-HER2 CAR-macrophage, CT-0508 in advanced HER2-overexpressing solid cancer patients, including BC [121]. Preliminary results from seven participants (two with BC) demonstrated successful manufacturing and a satisfactory safety profile, with no DLTs or major organ damage. Only mild, self-resolving cytokine release syndrome was observed. Correlative analyses confirmed CT-0508 infiltration into tumors and TME remodeling, evidenced by increased myeloid and T-cell activity. Early signs of efficacy were noted, with three patients achieving stable disease at Week eight [121]. Parallel to clinical trials, the CARMA observational study (NCT05007379) aims to evaluate the anti-cancer efficacy of novel CAR-macrophages against patient-derived BC organoids representing HER2-negative, -low and -positive subtypes (Table 1).

The promising potential of myeloid cell therapies has spurred further clinical translation. A first-in-human study (NCT06254807) is assessing the safety and feasibility of CT-0525, an anti-HER2 CAR-monocyte product, in patients with advanced HER2-overexpressing solid tumors who have progressed on standard therapy. The phase I trial NCT04616248 is evaluating a multi-component regimen―combining radiotherapy, CDX-301 (anti-Flt3L agonist), CDX-1140 (anti-CD40 agonist) and Poly-ICLC―in patients with metastatic solid tumors including HER2-negative BC (Table 1). This combination aims to induce dendritic cell cross-presentation, release TAAs and activate tumor-specific T cells. An expansion cohort incorporates pembrolizumab and tocilizumab, reflecting a multifaceted strategy to remodel the immune landscape and overcome resistance.

TIL therapy

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy is a type of adoptive cell transfer that utilizes the body’s natural antitumor immune response. This approach involves resecting a patient’s tumor, isolating TILs from the tissue and expanding these tumor-reactive T cells ex vivo to quantities often exceeding billions before reinfusion (Fig. 6A1 and 6A2) [122, 123]. This highly amplified, polyclonal TIL product is then reinfused into the patient following a lymphodepleting chemotherapy regimen. High IL-2 dose is often administered afterward to support T-cell engraftment and persistence (Fig. 6A1). TIL therapy effectively targets diverse tumor antigens and neoantigens, showing efficacy in heterogeneous tumors like melanoma and emerging promise in other cancers [122, 123]. Although TILs are typically well-tolerated, the associated toxicities mainly arise from the required lymphodepletion and IL-2 administration, which may lead to myelosuppression and systemic inflammatory responses [122, 123].

Observations from numerous studies indicating the prognostic significance of TILs in BC have provided a compelling rationale for their development as an adoptive cellular therapy [124]. TIL therapy harnesses endogenous T lymphocytes extracted directly from tumor tissue, capitalizing on their inherent ability to recognize tumor [125]. Following ex vivo expansion and selective reinfusion, these activated TILs aim to enhance anti-tumor responses [126]. In trials for advanced HER2-positive and metastatic BC, TILs achieved notable objective response rates (ORR) ranging from 27.3% to 45.5% and, in some cases, durable remissions, including one patient achieving complete disappearance of metastases and remaining disease-free for over 5 years [125]. Despite this promise, technical complexities in isolation, amplification and cost remain significant hurdles.

Beyond direct therapeutic potential, TILs provide significant prognostic value. A large-scale analysis of 481 BCs with >10-year follow-up revealed that higher stromal TIL (sTIL) scores independently predicted improved outcomes. For every 10% increase in sTILs, researchers observed a 14–19% reduction in recurrence risk, distant recurrence and mortality (p ≤ 0.04), establishing sTILs as a sole prognostic marker for DFS and distant recurrence-free survival (DRFS) [127]. Examination of TIL distribution across HER2-0, HER2-low and HER2-positive cancers revealed heterogeneous patterns. For these respective classifications, TILs were categorized as absent (16.1%, 17.6%, 8.7%), low (70.2%, 52.9%, 34.8%), intermediate (11.3%, 25.5%, 47.8%) and high (2.4%, 3.9%, 8.7%) [128]. This quantitative assessment revealed a statistically significant disparity in TIL infiltration when comparing the aggregated HER2-negative cohort to HER2-positive tumors (p < 0.001); however, no such statistical difference was observed when comparing HER2-low to HER2-positive or HER2-negative to HER2-low categories [128]. This distribution confirms that HER2-positive tumors, like TNBC, exhibit significantly higher TIL infiltration than luminal–HER2-negative subtypes. This aligns with the recognized immunogenicity of both HER2-positive and TNBC diseases, explaining the stronger prognostic and predictive value of TILs in these contexts.

Reflecting this subtype-specific immunogenicity, TILs demonstrate distinct predictive value for pCR and long-term survival. A dose-dependent increase in pCR rates was observed with higher TIL levels across all subtypes: in luminal–HER2-negative BC, pCR rose from 6% (low TILs) to 28% (high TILs); in HER2-positive BC, from 32% to 48%; and in TNBC, from 31% to 50% (p < 0.0001 for all) [129]. Despite this strong prognostic association, the translation of TILs into a predictive biomarker for specific therapies beyond ICIs requires standardization of assessment methods and validation in prospective interventional trials. A study specifically examining pCR predictors in TNBC patients treated with neoadjuvant KEYNOTE-522 chemoimmunotherapy further underscored the paramount predictive value of TILs. In this cohort, pretreatment TIL levels emerged as the sole significant predictor for pCR (p = 0.014) [130]. Patients achieving pCR displayed notably higher mean pretreatment TILs (28.5% ± 19.1%) compared to those with residual disease (9.73% ± 9.40%) [130]. Similarly, a pivotal clinical trial (NCT01174121) involving a patient with refractory ER+/HER2- metastatic BC demonstrated the remarkable potential of personalized TIL therapy. This highly tailored approach utilized a prospective mutation analysis to identify and target neoantigen-reactive TILs specific to the patient’s unique somatic mutations [131]. The treatment induced durable complete regression of all tumor lesions. Analysis confirmed polyclonal T-cell responses targeting four nonsynonymous mutation-derived neoepitopes, with the profound regression primarily attributed to these mutation-specific TILs rather than concurrent pembrolizumab [131]. This case demonstrates that personalized TIL therapy can achieve potent efficacy in refractory metastatic BC, including typically less immunogenic ER+/HER2- subtypes, by targeting patient-specific neoantigens. It further suggests that neoantigen-specific TILs can overcome TME immunosuppression, as evidenced by the limited contribution of checkpoint inhibition, and supports the development of engineered T-cell therapies based on identified neoantigen-reactive TCRs.

A study of 1966 TNBC patients confirmed that high TIL levels (≥50%) significantly improved 5-year DRFI (94% vs. 78% with <30% TILs) and OS (95% vs. 82%). Each 10% increase in TILs correlated independently with improved survival outcomes, including invasive DFS, recurrence-free, DRFS, and OS (HRs: 0.87–0.92), underscoring TILs as a reliable prognostic biomarker in TNBC and a predictor of patient prognosis [132]. A meta-analysis of 2,148 patients from nine trials confirmed that elevated stromal TILs independently predict superior outcomes in early-stage TNBC [133]. Each 10% increase in sTILs corresponded to a likelihood of recurrence and death reduction, with substantial improvements observed in OS, distant DFS, and invasive DFS. The prognostic value was particularly strong; node-negative patients with sTILs ≥ 30% had 3-year overall survival of 99%. [133]. These observations reinforce sTILs as a key factor in predicting patient prognosis. While stromal TILs are a robust prognostic biomarker, their translation into a predictive biomarker for specific therapies (beyond general prognosis) requires standardization of assessment methods and validation in prospective, biomarker-stratified trials

The ShortHER trial (NCT00629278) revealed TILs as a key biomarker for personalizing therapy duration in HER2-positive BC [134]. A significant treatment interaction was observed: patients with lower TILs (<20%) derived greater benefit from extended therapy, while those with higher TIL levels (≥20%) excelled with a shorter regimen (DRFS P-interaction = 0.01) [134]. This predictive role complements strong prognostic value of TIL, where each 5% increment was associated with significantly improved long-term DRFS and OS, and levels ≥ 50% were linked to a 98.1% 10-year OS [134].

However, the impact on long-term survival exhibited more nuanced, subtype-specific patterns with a 10% increase in TILs consistently portended improved DFS in TNBC (HR 0.93) and HER2-positive BC (HR 0.94), but showed no significant association in luminal–HER2-negative tumors [129]. For OS, higher TILs predicted better outcomes exclusively in TNBC (HR 0.92). Intriguingly, while no association was found in HER2-positive disease, increased TILs in luminal–HER2-negative tumors paradoxically correlated with reduced OS (HR 1.10 [1.02–1.19]) [129], underscoring the need for context-dependent interpretation. A study of 944 patients (71% with low TILs) identified radiotherapy (RT) and high TILs as independent factors reducing ipsilateral breast tumor recurrence (IBTR) risk, while high grade and younger age were risk factors [135]. A key finding was that RT provided a strong, significant benefit for patients with low TILs (HR 0.37, p < 0.001). While patients with high TILs also exhibited a trend toward RT benefit, it was not significant (HR 0.58, p = 0.138). Though the formal interaction test was not significant (p = 0.317) [135], this suggests the relative benefit of RT is greatest for patients with low TIL levels in the primary tumor.

Despite their therapeutic potential, TIL-based therapies face significant barriers to widespread clinical implementation. A major limitation is the laborious and time-consuming manufacturing process, which can critically delay treatment for patients with advanced disease. Additionally, variable patient responses hinder reliable prediction of efficacy and complicate broader clinical translation [136]. In response to these challenges, the Phase II clinical trial (NCT01174121) is ongoing to meticulously assess the efficacy and safety of TILs in metastatic BC [125]. Further research is critical to optimizing TIL therapy for advanced BC. Despite the well-established prognostic and predictive role of TILs, translating this promise into reliable clinical application requires overcoming persistent obstacles, including lengthy manufacturing and heterogeneous patient responses, through continued scientific and procedural innovation.

TCR-based therapy

T cell receptor (TCR)-based treatment necessitates the modification of T cells by expressing particular TCRs that identify intracellular tumor antigens displayed on MHC proteins (Fig. 6C) [137]. The primary benefit of TCR-T cell therapy is its capacity to recognize a broader spectrum of antigens. TCR-engineered T cells can identify peptides from intracellular and membrane-bound proteins presented on MHC, unlike CAR-T cells, which are limited to surface epitopes (Fig. 6C) [138, 139]. However, this approach is HLA-restricted, limiting its use to patients with matching HLA alleles. Additionally, TCR-T cells can exhibit high sensitivity, capable of recognizing intracellular antigens presented by just a few peptide-MHC complexes per cell. In contrast, CAR-T cells typically require higher densities of surface antigen for optimal activation, a distinction that influences their respective target landscapes [138, 139]. This enhanced sensitivity holds the potential to significantly improve the precision of tumor cell detection and subsequent elimination.

The broad applicability of TCR-engineered T cells is evidenced by the diverse range of targetable antigens being explored across various cancers. These include well-known TAAs and cancer-testis antigens (CTAs) with their specific epitopes, such as MART-1 (e.g., AAGIGILTV, EAAGIGILTV) and gp100 (e.g., KTWGQYWQV) in melanoma; MAGE family members like MAGE-A3 (e.g., KVAELVHFL, EVDPIGHLY) across melanoma and metastatic solid tumors, MAGE-A4 (e.g., NYKRCFPVI, GVYDGREHTV) in esophageal cancer and advanced solid tumors and MAGE-A10 (e.g., GLYDGMEHL) in lung cancer, and CEA (e.g., IMIGVLVGV) is targeted in colorectal cancer (Fig. 6C) [139].

Clinical applications of TCR-based therapies are associated with specific toxicities and limitations. Toxic effects can occur when therapies engage their intended antigen on healthy tissues, leading to ON-target/OFF-tumor damage, as seen in severe colitis following CEA-directed TCR therapy due to antigen presence in normal intestine [139]. Additionally, off-target toxicity (cross-reactivity) arises when TCRs recognize unrelated epitopes on healthy cells. Therapeutic efficacy is further limited by primary resistance mechanisms, including low or heterogeneous target antigen expression and inherent tumor resistance to T-cell killing [139]. Finally, the tendency of ex vivo-expanded T cells to adopt a late memory phenotype often leads to poor persistence and accelerated exhaustion, underscoring the need for stem cell-like or early memory T-cell phenotypes to sustain long-term responses.

CIK cell therapy

Cytokine-induced killer (CIK) cell therapy is a form of adoptive cellular immunotherapy utilizing ex vivo stimulated and proliferating CD3+CD56+ T lymphocytes [140]. These cells exhibit hybrid phenotypic and functional characteristics representing both T cells and NK cells (Fig. 6D) [141, 142]. Their clinical appeal stems from several key attributes including MHC-unrestricted tumor recognition eliminating the need for antigen priming, inherent capacity for immune memory, elevated granzyme B production and strong tumor homing potential. Furthermore, CIK cells can be rapidly expanded in vitro, enhancing their practical applicability in clinical settings (Fig. 6D) [141, 142].

Clinical studies of CIK cell therapy in BC, particularly in advanced or high-risk settings, have demonstrated promising outcomes. Early research in metastatic TNBC, the addition of DC/CIK therapy to chemotherapy significantly extended median PFS and OS in retrospective analyses, with benefits pronounced in younger patients and those with visceral metastases [143]. A retrospective study in post-mastectomy TNBC patients showed that adjuvant CIK therapy combined with chemotherapy significantly improved both DFS and OS (p = 0.0382 and p = 0.0046, respectively). CIK treatment was a sole prognostic factor for OS, with greatest benefit in patients with grade 3 tumors or advanced TNM stages (N1–N3, IIB–III) [144].

A meta-analysis of 11 studies confirmed that adjunctive DC/CIK therapy improves objective response rates without augmenting classic chemotherapy toxicities [145]. Another prospective study, with data collected from 2003 to 2013 on Stage IV BC patients, further supported these findings. It reported enhanced immune function, a reduced risk of disease progression (p < 0.01) and improved OS (p < 0.01) following DC-CIK immunotherapy administered after low-dose chemotherapy [146]. More recently, a retrospective study in 2019 focusing on postoperative BC patients highlighted the prognostic value of PD-L1 expression. This research showed that CIK therapy significantly improved OS and recurrence-free survival (RFS), with benefits primarily observed in patients whose tumors expressed PD-L1 [147]. The suggests its potential as a predictive biomarker for adjuvant CIK therapy.

To counteract the suppression of CIK signaling within the TME, researchers have explored blocking immune checkpoints. Poh et al., found that, inhibitors targeting molecules like LAG-3, PD-L1, TIM-3, PD-1and KIR have been utilized and studies suggest these blockades can boost the anti-tumor activity of CIK cells against various malignancies, including advanced NSCLC, gastrointestinal cancer, and myeloid leukemia [148]. The CIK cell therapy combination with immune-modulating agents, such as ICIs or bispecific antibodies (BsAbs), demonstrates a potential approach to circumvent resistance and enhance anti-tumor activity in BC, providing a strong rationale for clinical exploration [149]. BsAbs are engineered to simultaneously bind two distinct targets, enabling them to effectively bridge CIK cells to tumor cells and potentiate CIK-mediated cytotoxicity. This approach has shown promise across malignancies; CD19/CD5 BsAbs enhanced CIK activity in B-cell lymphoma, EGFR/CD3 BsAbs in gastric cancer and CD3/CD133 BsAbs against CD133-high cancer cells [149].

Despite their advantages, CIK cell therapies face significant limitations, including unsatisfactory monotherapy results against solid tumors due to immunosuppressive TMEs [150]. A major challenge is the inherent heterogeneity of ex vivo expanded CIK populations, with variable proportions of key subsets like Treg, CD4+ T cells, and potent CD3+CD56+ effector T cells [151]. The rates of these CD3+CD56+ effector cells, for instance, vary substantially (6% to over 60%) across different studies [152, 153]. Furthermore, CD4-CIK cells exhibit reduced cytotoxic activity and are associated with exhausted T cells, though their function can be restored by rIL-17A, while CD4+ T cells (Th1/Th17) can enhance CIK function [151]. Treg cells pose another significant drawback by inducing CD8+ T cell exhaustion, with their development influenced by IL-2, necessitating novel induction methods [154]. Lastly, the diverse array of CIK expansion protocols across studies complicates the standardization and comparability of research findings [155]. Consequently, while meta-analyses of earlier-phase trials suggest that adding CIK therapy to standard regimens can improve clinical responses and survival in selected settings, the absence of standardized products and pivotal phase III data precludes its current integration into mainstream treatment algorithms. Future development requires rigorous, randomized trials.

Synthesis and comparative analysis of cellular modalities

The diverse adoptive cellular therapy platforms under investigation for BC each present a distinct profile of advantages and challenges. CAR-T cells offer potent, engineered specificity for surface antigens and have demonstrated remarkable success in hematologic malignancies, but their application in solid tumors is hindered by poor tumor infiltration, ON-target/OFF-tumor toxicity, and the logistical complexity of autologous manufacturing [75, 76, 89]. TCR-T cells can target a broader repertoire of intracellular antigens, including neoantigens, providing a key advantage for tumors with suitable HLA presentation. However, they are restricted by patient HLA type and carry inherent risks of off-target cross-reactivity [138, 139]. TIL therapy leverages a polyclonal population of endogenous, tumor-reactive T cells, which inherently possess tumor-homing capability and can target unique neoantigens. Its major limitations include a lengthy manufacturing process, dependency on resectable tumor tissue, and high variability in product composition [122, 123, 125]. CAR-NK cells provide innate cytotoxicity mechanisms and a generally favorable safety profile with a lower risk of severe CRS, making them strong candidates for off-the-shelf allogeneic therapy. Their clinical development is challenged by limited in vivo persistence and difficulties in achieving robust ex vivo expansion [77, 99]. CAR-M therapies represent a novel approach focused on remodeling the immunosuppressive TME and enhancing antigen presentation. While highly promising in preclinical models, this platform is at the earliest stage of clinical translation, with questions remaining about optimal cell sourcing and long-term functionality [72, 115]. The choice of platform is thus contingent on the clinical context, target antigen profile, and urgency of treatment. Cross-cutting challenges, Translational hurdles, and future directions

Despite remarkable progress, significant biological, logistical, and clinical obstacles impede the broader application and efficacy of immunotherapy in BC.

The immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and therapeutic resistance

The BC TME is a major orchestrator of treatment failure. Key resistance mechanisms include: (i) Metabolic and Structural Barriers: BC cells drive metabolic exhaustion by stabilizing HIF-1α, which facilitates adaptation to low-oxygen conditions and promotes the secretion of VEGF. This leads to the formation of a dysfunctional, leaky vasculature that hinders the efficient trafficking of therapeutic cells into the tumor site [156, 157]. Furthermore, BC cells contribute to the formation of a dense, impenetrable ECM by secreting MMPs and promoting the activation of CAFs [157]. This resulting collagen-rich architecture increases interstitial fluid pressure, creating a rigid mechanical barrier that physically obstructs the infiltration and migration of CAR-T, CAR-NK, CAR-M, TIL, TCR-T, and CIK cells into the tumor core. (ii) Cellular and Soluble Suppression: BC cells secrete a potent array of soluble mediators, including immunosuppressive cytokines like TGF-β, IL-6, and IL-10, which act to inhibit effector cell cytotoxicity [157]. These factors simultaneously drive the recruitment and stability of Tregs, M2-polarized TAMs, and MDSCs [158]. Once localized within the TME, these MDSCs facilitate nutrient depletion by producing IDO1 and ARG1. These enzymes deplete essential tryptophan and arginine, respectively, creating a metabolic blockade that effectively cripples the function of infiltrating adoptive and endogenous cellular therapies (Fig. 1) [158]. (iv) Antigenic Escape: Downregulation or loss of target antigens (e.g., transporter associated with antigen processing (TAP1/2), TAP-binding protein (TAPBP)) following selective pressure from antigen-directed therapies [159].

In parallel, resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in BC is driven by genomic alterations and aberrant signaling that facilitate immune evasion. Key mechanisms include CD274 (PD-L1) gene amplification on chromosome 9p24.1, loss of PTEN, and MYC-mediated upregulation of PD-L1 expression [160]. Tumor cells also exploit the IFN-γ/JAK/STAT1 pathway and the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 axis to boost PD-L1 levels, effectively inactivating CD8+ T cells. Additionally, post-translational modifications like N-glycosylation stabilize PD-L1, further hindering anti-tumor immune responses [106]. Sustained IFN-γ signaling, while initially stimulatory, eventually triggers immunosuppressive molecules and PD-L1 ligands, leading to a feedback inhibition that suppresses the anti-tumor immune response [106]. Consequently, these mechanisms suggest that PD-1/PD-L1 resistance in BC is not a static failure but a dynamic regulatory circumvention, where the tumor exploits genetic amplification and post-translational stabilization to maintain an immunosuppressive threshold that current monotherapies cannot easily overcome.

Several alternative immune checkpoints are being investigated as critical mediators of resistance within the BC TME. These pathways often serve as compensatory escape mechanisms that allow tumor cells to evade immune detection even when the primary PD-1 pathway is inhibited. One of the most extensively studied targets is CTLA-4, which functions during the initial stages of T-cell priming by competing with the costimulatory receptor CD28 for binding to B7 ligands on antigen-presenting cells [161]. While CTLA-4 inhibitors have shown limited efficacy as monotherapy in BC, they are increasingly utilized in combination with PD-1 blockades to provide a dual-stage release of the immune [161]. Another significant checkpoint is LAG-3, which is frequently co-expressed with PD-1 on exhausted T cells, particularly in TNBC [162]. LAG-3 binds to MHC class II molecules with a higher affinity than CD4, thereby delivering inhibitory signals that suppress T-cell proliferation and effector functions. Recent clinical characterization suggests that targeting LAG-3 may be essential for reversing terminal T-cell exhaustion [162, 163]. Similarly, TIGIT has emerged as a novel prognostic marker in invasive BC. It binds to PVR (CD155) on tumor cells, which not only inhibits the cytotoxic activity of NK cells but also enhances the suppressive stability of Tregs. Consequently, TIGIT blockade is being explored as a strategy to simultaneously reinvigorate both adaptive and innate anti-tumor responses [163, 164]. Finally, TIM-3 acts as a marker of terminal exhaustion, where its interaction with galectin-9 leads to the apoptosis of T helper 1 (Th1) cells and a reduction in IFN-γ production. The presence of TIM-3-positive infiltrating lymphocytes often correlates with advanced tumor stages, indicating its role in maintaining a deeply immunosuppressive TME (Fig. 2) [163]. Therefore, a multi-targeted therapeutic approach using these new ICIs may be necessary to overcome the redundant layers of immune suppression characteristic of the BC TME.

The collective synergy of these factors facilitates a profound immune escape for BC cells, while simultaneously fortifying the suppressive architecture of the TME. This multifaceted evasion strategy effectively neutralizes a broad spectrum of adoptive and endogenous cellular therapies, leading to systemic resistance against ICIs, as well as chimeric antigen receptor-based platforms (CAR-T, CAR-NK, CAR-M) and other cell-mediated interventions, including TILs, TCR-T, and CIK therapies. Practical and manufacturing limitations of cellular therapies

The translation of adoptive cellular therapies faces formidable logistical and economic hurdles. One of the most substantial barriers to the widespread implementation of cellular therapies is the difficulty in scaling up or scaling out production processes [165]. Specifically, the manufacturing processes for autologous cell therapies (CAR-T, TIL, TCR-T), are patient-specific and rely on individualized production chains. This approach inherently limits production capacity since cells must be harvested, engineered, and expanded for each patient, resulting in low yield and high cost [165–169]. For allogeneic “off-the-shelf” platforms (CAR-NK, pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)-derived cells), which use cells from healthy donors to treat multiple patients, scalability is less patient-specific, but challenges persist in generating large cell banks while maintaining therapeutic functionality and avoiding immunogenicity. Ensuring batch-to-batch consistency also becomes critical in this context [170].

The complexity of producing living therapeutic agents complicates rigorous adherence to Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP). The need for sterile environments, contamination risk management, and monitoring cell functionality across multiple steps in the production process demands sophisticated equipment and personnel. Quality control (QC) measures, such as assessing cell viability, purity, and potency, are labor- and resource-intensive, especially with personalized therapies. Adding to this operational challenge, the heterogeneity in different regulatory standards across countries makes global commercialization even more complicated [165, 166]. Additionally, maintaining compliance with regulatory requirements is complicated by the diversity of processes employed in creating cell therapies. This is especially problematic in decentralized, point-of-care models that aim to bring manufacturing closer to the patient [171, 172].

The financial demands of cell therapy manufacturing are a limiting factor for broader accessibility. High costs arise due to individualized production, expensive reagents, specialized instruments (e.g., for cell isolation, expansion, and genetic engineering), and GMP compliance. Autologous therapies are particularly impacted by costs associated with collecting patient cells, modifying them, and returning them in a timely manner [166, 167]. Efforts to implement automated and closed-system manufacturing solutions can reduce costs, but such technologies require substantial upfront investment and ongoing evaluation during clinical translation [167, 173].

Many manufactories for cell-based products still rely on cumbersome, manual techniques that introduce variability between batches. Despite advances like 3D bioprinting and microfluidic approaches (e.g., to enhance primary cell culture or streamline quality tests), implementing these technologies into routine practice remains slow due to their novelty and associated costs [167, 174]. A significant limitation arises in integrating new bioprocessing technologies, such as artificial intelligence, to customize processes, optimize efficiency, and minimize manual intervention. Efforts to align innovation with regulatory requirements in a manufacturing context remain slow [173, 175]. In addition, many other problems can also affect the production of cellular therapies, including logistical bottlenecks and ethical and accessibility issues [171, 175, 176]. Addressing these challenges requires collaboration among researchers, regulatory bodies, and healthcare providers to streamline processes and ensure equitable access to these innovative treatments. By fostering a supportive environment, we can enhance the development and distribution of cellular therapies for patients in need.

Toxicity management

Safety remains a paramount concern. For ICIs, irAEs can affect any organ system and require prompt recognition and management with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants [177]. For engineered cell therapies, risks include cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), which are managed with tocilizumab and steroids [178–180]. The most severe risk is “On-target/ Off-tumor” toxicity, where effector cells attack healthy tissues expressing low levels of the target antigen (e.g., HER2 on lung epithelial cells) [181]. Mitigation strategies include safety switches (e.g., inducible caspase-9 (iCasp9) suicide genes), logic-gated CARs requiring multiple antigens, and tuning of CAR affinity [182].

Biomarker development and patient selection

The precise selection of patients most likely to benefit from immunotherapy remains a central challenge. Current biomarkers—including PD-L1 expression, sTILs, TMB, and microsatellite instability (MSI)—have proven useful but insufficient, leading to imprecise patient selection and suboptimal therapeutic indices [183]. Advancements are anticipated from the integration of multi-omics profiling (genomic, transcriptomic, epigenomic, proteomic) and spatial biology technologies, which can capture the complex interplay between tumor cells and the immune microenvironment. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning models are poised to analyze these high-dimensional datasets to identify novel composite biomarkers and predictive signatures. Furthermore, liquid biopsies—analyzing circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), circulating tumor cells (CTCs), exosomes, and soluble immune factors—offer a promising non-invasive strategy for dynamic response monitoring and early detection of resistance.

Translating these approaches into clinical practice faces significant hurdles. Tumors exhibit profound spatial and temporal heterogeneity, both between patients and within individual lesions, complicating the identification of universal biomarkers. Technical and analytical variability across diagnostic platforms (e.g., PD-L1 immunohistochemistry assays, TMB scoring thresholds) impedes standardization. Critically, many promising biomarkers reflect a dynamic biological state (e.g., immune cell infiltration, cytokine fluxes) that requires serial assessment, for which repeated tissue biopsies are impractical. Overcoming these barriers is essential to move beyond static, single-parameter biomarkers towards dynamic, multi-parametric models that can guide personalized immunotherapy selection and combination strategies.

Future efforts must integrate single cell and multi-omics approaches—including genomic (TMB, specific mutational signatures), transcriptomic (immune gene signatures), and spatial proteomic profiling—to develop composite biomarkers that accurately predict response to specific immunotherapeutic modalities. AI and machine learning technologies can process complex multi-omics data and identify novel predictive biomarkers, improving patient stratification. The integration of biomarker-guided combination therapies can help design combination strategies, such as ICIs with anti-angiogenic therapies, to overcome resistance mechanisms.

Complementing these upfront stratification strategies, liquid biopsies analyzing CTCs, exosomal RNA/proteins, and other soluble biomarkers (e.g., cytokines, chemokines) may also hold promise for non-invasive response monitoring.

The success of CAR-T therapy depends on the expression of target antigens like CD19 (in hematologic cancers). However, antigen heterogeneity and loss pose challenges in solid tumors. In addition, long-term persistence of CAR cells is essential for sustained tumor control [184]. Biomarkers such as CAR cell expansion, activation markers (e.g., CD69, CD25), and memory cell subsets provide critical prognostic information. Similarly, pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IFN-γ) predict treatment efficacy and toxicity, including CRS. Cytokine levels guide therapeutic interventions aimed at mitigating severe adverse events. Finaly, solid tumors often present an immunosuppressive environment, characterized by high levels of Tregs, M2 macrophages, and inhibitory molecules like TGF-β. Biomarkers targeting these mechanisms are under investigation to improve CAR efficacy.

While these strategies may offer provisional solutions, they are impeded by deep-seated and persistent challenges. Tumors exhibit profound genetic and epigenetic heterogeneity, not merely between patients but within a single lesion, making the discovery of universally applicable biomarkers across cancer subtypes exceptionally difficult. Clinical implementation is further complicated by stark variability in diagnostic platforms, such as inconsistencies among PD-L1 immunohistochemistry assays and a lack of consensus on TMB scoring thresholds. The biological reality of many promising biomarkers, including the composition of immune infiltration and fluctuating cytokine levels, is that they are inherently dynamic, requiring serial assessment rather than a single measurement. Crucially, the invasive nature of tissue biopsies presents a fundamental logistical barrier, limiting the practical and repeated evaluation of tissue-based markers and underscoring the urgent necessity for reliable liquid biopsy methodologies.

Even with this tissue‑based limitation, tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) in BC remain clinically relevant immune niches that cannot be overlooked. Once dismissed as incidental infiltrates, TLSs are now recognized as organized immune niches whose clinical weight in BC proves strongly subtype dependent rather than uniform [185]. They distribute unevenly across the disease, concentrating in the immunologically active subtypes and thinning out in HR+ tumors, which typically assemble an immune-cold microenvironment1. In TNBC, the presence and maturity of TLSs track with favorable clinicopathological features, richer tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte content, and longer survival [186], and a parallel pattern surfaces in HER2+ disease, where TLS presence accompanies better outcome in patients receiving adjuvant trastuzumab [187]. Consistent with this, mature TLSs remain strikingly uncommon overall, identified in only roughly 5.6% of unselected BCs, yet they cluster in the aggressive subtypes, arising in about 15% of TNBC and 11% of HER2+ tumors but under 1% of HR+/HER2-negative disease [188]. This gradient discourages a single pan-subtype threshold and instead favors maturation-aware scoring, since mature germinal-center-bearing structures, rather than mere presence, appear to carry the prognostic signal, remaining independently associated with recurrence-free survival after adjustment for subtype, TIL level, and tumor stage.

The predictive potential of TLSs emerges most clearly in the neoadjuvant setting, where a pretreatment biopsy captures the immune contexture before therapy remodels it. This question has acquired urgency because checkpoint blockade now anchors early TNBC care: in the phase 3 KEYNOTE-522 trial (NCT03036488), which randomized 1,174 patients 2:1, adding pembrolizumab to platinum-based chemotherapy lifted the pCR rate from 51.2% to 64.8% and improved event-free survival (HR 0.63) and, at final analysis, OS (HR 0.66; 95% CI: 0.50–0.87), yet the benefit accrued largely regardless of PD-L1 status, leaving clinicians without a reliable tissue biomarker for patient selection; notably, pembrolizumab also shifted patients toward lower residual-cancer-burden categories across the spectrum of residual disease [189]. TLSs may help fill that gap. Applying a TLS-associated gene-expression signature to the I-SPY2 platform trial (NCT01042379), investigators showed that the signature independently predicted pathological response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in HER2-negative disease and, tellingly, singled out the patients who gained most from adding pembrolizumab, with the effect concentrated in signature-high tumors [190]. Histology corroborates this TLS heterogeneity and maturity, more than bulk CD8+ T-cell density, correlate with neoadjuvant response and with the surrounding immune microenvironment in TNBC [191]. These observations reframe TLSs as functional correlates of a therapy-responsive state, though a post-treatment rise in TLS abundance need not signal genuine induction rather than selective survival of pre-existing immune-active regions.

The practical challenge lies in positioning TLSs alongside the biomarkers already in use. The limits of any single marker are instructive. In the phase 3 IMpassion130 trial (NCT02425891), for instance, atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel prolonged survival only in the PD-L1 immune-cell-positive subgroup, where median OS reached 25.4 versus 17.9 months, whereas PD-L1-negative patients derived no benefit [40]. Rather than competing with TILs or PD-L1, TLSs contribute orthogonal information, capturing the spatial organization of the immune response that density-based or single-marker readouts overlook; in early TNBC, layering CD8 and PD-L1 onto a multivariable model already containing TILs sharpened prognostic stratification, so that patients with high CD8 and absent PD-L1 approached uniformly excellent survival while the low-CD8, PD-L1-positive group fared markedly worse [192].

Spatial approaches now push this integration further. A pan-cancer atlas of 340 spatial-transcriptomic samples across 12 cancer types resolved TLSs into early (E-TLS), primary-follicle-like (P-TLS), and secondary-follicle-like (S-TLS) states and derived a maturation-aware composite score that stratified patients more robustly than presence-or-absence or dominant-state metrics, remaining independently prognostic after adjustment for tumor stage [193]. BC emerged as one of the TLS-rich tumor types in this atlas, yet its structures were characteristically peritumoral and skewed toward the least mature state, with breast tumors enriched for peritumoral E-TLSs rather than the germinal-center-bearing S-TLSs that predominated in cancers such as gastric adenocarcinoma [193]. Crucially for the neoadjuvant question raised above, the same framework linked greater abundance of mature TLS states, particularly P-TLSs, to favorable response in BC treated with neoadjuvant combination chemotherapy and with anti-HER2 targeted therapy, reinforcing that maturation composition, not mere TLS presence, carries the predictive signal [193].

Even so, none of these spatial or compositional readouts escapes a fundamental interpretive constraint, because TLS status, TIL abundance, and IgG-based signatures interrelate. In this suggests TLS positivity should not be read as an independent variable unless analyses adjust for bulk TIL content [185]. Taken together, a composite strategy that integrates TILs, PD-L1, and TLS maturation promises to refine patient selection more precisely than any single parameter, provided standardized, reproducible scoring becomes established across laboratories. Delivering on that promise will demand prospective validation in uniformly treated, biomarker-stratified cohorts and consensus on quantification, yet the trajectory looks clear. TLSs are steadily earning a defined place within the BC immune-biomarker landscape.

Future perspectives: integration and Next-generation engineering

The future lies in rationally designed, multimodal regimens. This includes: (i) Sequential TME conditioning using ICIs, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy prior to cellular therapy infusion. (ii) Next-generation cellular products engineered for enhanced homing (e.g., chemokine receptor expression), resistance to suppression (e.g., dominant-negative TGF-β receptors), and controlled proliferation. (iii) Novel combinatorial targets simultaneously engaging multiple immune axes (e.g., PD-1 + LAG-3, TIGIT) or combining immunotherapy with ADCs and CDK4/6 inhibitors. The ultimate goal is a personalized immunotherapy roadmap, guided by the patient’s unique tumor biology and immune contexture.
