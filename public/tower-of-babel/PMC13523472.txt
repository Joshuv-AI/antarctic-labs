Boosting the immune response and protective efficacy of inactivated PRV vaccine using cGAMP as a mucosal adjuvant
Authors: Chen Chang, Haifeng Sun, Tao Hua, Qianwen Li, Xingyu Yan, Haoming Zhang, Wenzhu Yin, Haiyan Wang, Bo Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe respiratory mucosa serves as the primary site for Pseudorabies Virus (PRV) invasion, which can elicit both local mucosal and systemic immune responses. However, due to the presence of mucosal barrier, nasal immunization with inactivated vaccines typically results in a limited immune response; thus, a mucosal adjuvant is necessary to enhance this response. Cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) acts as a potent agonist of STING, exhibiting significant mucosal adjuvant properties. It plays a vital role in the regulation of innate immune responses to DNA viruses.ResultsIn this study, we developed a nasal vaccine for PRV by integrating cGAMP with PRV antigen. Both mucosal and systemic antibody responses were enhanced in a dose-dependent manner by PRV antigen or cGAMP adjuvant administration. cGAMP adjuvanted PRV vaccine induced a persistent IgG but resulted in a transient IgA mucosal antibody response. Intranasal immunization with cGAMP effectively enhanced CD3+CD4+T cell responses in vivo. cGAMP adjuvanted vaccine conferred effective protection against PRV challenge. Furthermore, we also established a well differentiated air-liquid cultured mice bronchial epithelial cell (Ali-MBECs). Utilizing this model, it was demonstrated that cGAMP activates IRF3 signaling, which subsequently induced the expression of MX1, ISG56, and IFN-β.ConclusionThis study found that cGAMP significantly enhanced the nasal immunological efficacy of the PRV inactivated vaccine by activating the STING signaling pathway within respiratory mucosa. Therefore, cGAMP demonstrated significant potential as an effective mucosal immune adjuvant to be employed in inactivated PRV vaccine. This approach offered novel technical support for the immunization and prevention against PRV infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05636-z.

Background

Pseudorabies Virus (PRV) is classified as an alphaherpesvirus, responsible for causing an acute febrile infectious disease. This condition is characterized by a markedly increased mortality rate and the presence of neurological disorders in piglets, along with a diminished reproductive capacity in sows [1]. The primary natural host of PRV is swine; however, it possesses the ability to infect a diverse array of mammalian species. There are no effective pharmacological treatments available for animals infected with PRV. The primary strategy for preventing this disease is through immunization using an effective vaccine [1, 2]. The attenuated PRV vaccine strain Bartha-K61 has been shown to provide protection against pseudorabies (PR) prior to 2011. However, since the end of 2011, PR has reemerged in several swine farms in China that utilize Bartha-K61 vaccination. This resurgence has led to significantly elevated mortality rates among newborn piglets, which can reach as high as 50%. Additionally, growing and finishing pigs have been adversely affected by the disease, with mortality rates ranging from 10% to 30% [3, 4]. Compared to previous isolates, the variant strain exhibits insertions, deletions, or substitutions in several viral proteins. These genetic variations contribute to the failure of traditional PR vaccines to provide effective protection.

PRV can be transmitted through various routes. The mucosal surfaces of the nasal cavity serve as the primary entry points [2]. After the infection of nasal epithelial cells, PRV has the capability to swiftly invade the olfactory nerve, subsequently allowing it to penetrate the nervous system. Consequently, nasal immunity plays a crucial role in effectively preventing viral colonization and infection [5]. Intranasal administration represents a highly effective approach for immunization against respiratory tract infections [3, 4, 6]. Intranasal administration effectively simulates the natural infection of the etiology and stimulates the production of mucosal antibodies in the nasal mucosa [7]. Intranasal vaccination holds significant promise for disease control and prevention.

cGAMP is an endogenous cyclic dinucleotide that serves as a second messenger, synthesized by cyclic-GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) in the cGAS-STING signaling pathway. This enzyme is activated by double-stranded DNA derived from pathogens in the cytosol [4]. cGAMP functions as a second messenger that activates the STING/TBK1/IRF3 signaling pathway. It binds to the STING, thereby inducing the release of type I interferons and cytokines, which subsequently activate the innate immune response [7–10]. Therefore, cGAMP serves a crucial function in the initiation of type I interferon production and the modulation of innate immune responses against DNA viruses [11, 12].

Numerous studies have shown that PRV has evolved various strategies to evade the host’s innate immune response. PRV UL13 inhibits IFN-β production by directly affecting IRF3, requiring its kinase activity [13, 14]. PRV gE plays a crucial role in inhibiting cGAS-STING induced interferon production through the degradation of CBP [15]. It has been demonstrated that cGAMP is an effective adjuvant for the vaccine, such as influenza and PRRSV virus-like particles vaccines [12, 16–18]. Therefore, cGAMP was employed as a mucosal adjuvant for the inactivated PRV vaccine in this study. This selection was designed to promote the activation of type I interferon (IFN) production and to enhance the innate immune response of the respiratory mucosa, which could not be achieved with inactivated PRV vaccine. We demonstrated that cGAMP significantly enhanced both serum and mucosal antibody responses, as well as T cell activation induced by the PRV vaccine in mice. Furthermore, our findings indicate that cGAMP markedly improves protective efficacy against PRV infection. Thus, cGAMP may serve as a promising mucosal adjuvant for inactivated PRV vaccine.

Results

cGAMP-adjuvanted vaccination induced a dose-dependent antibody response in mice

Mice were randomly assigned to seven or five groups, with six mice in each group. The mice were intranasally immunized with varying doses of PRV antigen combined with a consistent dose of cGAMP adjuvant, or alternatively, with a uniform dose of PRV antigen paired with varying doses of cGAMP adjuvant (Fig. 1A). Antibody levels of IgA and IgG in serum and BALF at 28dpi were quantified using ELISA. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the antibody responses elicited by the combination of PRV and cGAMP (PG) were significantly greater than those observed in the groups receiving PRV alone. Furthermore, as shown in Figs. 1B-C, the antibody levels within PG groups exhibiting different antigen doses demonstrated a clear dose-dependent relationship corresponding to the amount of antigen administered. Additionally, an increase in adjuvant dosage resulted in elevated antibody levels within the PG group, indicating a similar dose-dependent relationship concerning cGAMP adjuvant dosages (Figs. 1D-E). These findings suggest that both mucosal and systemic antibody responses are enhanced through a dose-dependent mechanism involving either PRV antigen or cGAMP adjuvant administration. Consequently, these results indicate that cGAMP is capable of inducing a more robust immune response.

cGAMP-adjuvanted PRV vaccine induced persistent IgG and transitory mucosal IgA antibody response

Mice were immunized twice as shown in Fig. 2A. Serum and BALF samples were collected at different time points, and antibody levels (IgA, IgG, IgG1, IgG2a, IgG2c) were measured by ELISA using serially diluted samples. As illustrated in Fig. 2B–F, the PG40 group induced pronounced serum antibody responses from day 14 to day 49. Notably, compared to the P40 group, the PG40 group induced significantly higher levels of serum IgA (except at days 14 and 49), IgG (except at day 14), IgG1 (all the time points), IgG2a (all the time points), and IgG2c (except at day 14) (Fig. 2B–F). In addition, the corresponding BALF antibody results (Fig. 2G–K) showed that PG40 elicited a significantly higher level of IgA only at days 28 and 35, and significantly higher levels of total IgG, IgG1, IgG2a, and IgG2c to the P40 group. Overall, these findings indicate that the cGAMP-adjuvanted PRV vaccine (PG40) induces a robust and persistent systemic IgG response, but only a transient mucosal IgA response in the respiratory tract.

cGAMP adjuvant vaccine induced responses of CD3+CD4+ T cells

The cellular immune response elicited by various vaccines was assessed through the analysis of splenocytes derived from the immunized mice. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the percentage of CD3+CD4+T cells in mice vaccinated intranasally with PG40 was approximately 20.8%, significantly surpassing the levels observed in the P40 group (14.4%) and the PBS group (11.70%) (Fig. 3A, B). Additionally, the proportion of CD3+CD8+T cells was 7.50% in PG40 group, 6.98% in P40 group and 5.88% in PBS group (Fig. 3C). There was no significant difference between PG40 and P40 group (Fig. 3D). These findings indicated that intranasal immunization with cGAMP effectively enhanced CD3+CD4+T cells cell responses in vivo.

The cGAMP-adjuvanted vaccine provided effective protection against homologous viral challenges

Mice were immunized intranasally and challenged as described in Fig. 4A. As shown in Fig. 4B, the control group exposed to the virus had a survival rate of 0%. The double-immunization group provided sufficient immune protection, with a survival rate of 90%, while the single immunization group had a survival rate of only 70% (Fig. 4B). At the same time, cGAMP increased the survival rate of the P20 group from 40% to 70% and raised the survival rate of the P40 group from 70% to 90% (Fig. 4C). The body weight of all the groups began to decrease from 2dpc. The P40 and PG40 groups started to recover from 2dpc, while the P20 and PG20 groups gradually recovered from 3dpc. The rate of weight loss was ordered as follows: PBS > P20 > PG20> P40 > PG40 (Fig. 4D). Both the P40 and PBS groups exhibited alveolar wall thickening, infiltration of lymphocytes and neutrophils, and localized bleeding in the lungs. These findings were significantly more severe than those observed in the PG40 group (Fig. 4E). In addition, viral titers in the brain, spinal cord, and lung of the PG40 group were lower than other groups (Fig. 4F). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance is indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ns = not significant.

Discussion

The PRV variant has been prevalent in China for over a decade, leading to substantial economic losses within the country’s pig industry. Furthermore, it poses a potential threat to public health [19]. Vaccination of swine using either attenuated live or inactivated vaccines is a common practice on the majority of swine farms. However, incomplete protection may be attributed to suboptimal immune responses elicited by the Bartha-K61 vaccine. In addition to the imported Bartha-K61 vaccine, several other licensed live attenuated and inactivated PRV vaccine strains derived from Chinese isolates are available [20–23]. Intramuscular injection is the most widely used method of immunization for the prevention of this disease.

The respiratory tract mucosa serves as the primary site of infection for respiratory virus. The nasal cavity is the body’s initial contact area for entering antigens [24, 25]. It is abundant in nasal-associated lymphoid tissue (NALT), which integrates humoral and cellular immune responses, enhancing both systemic and mucosal immunity [25]. Nasal vaccines additionally stimulate immune cells in the mucosal tissues of both upper and lower respiratory tracts. This dual immune system stimulation, along with needle-free delivery, facilitates the creation of new nasal vaccines for long-lasting immunity against respiratory infections. Local immune responses on mucosal surfaces are vital for defending against viral infections [25–27]. Therefore, the development of nasal vaccines may represent a viable alternative for achieving more effective immunization [28].

Due to the presence of the mucosal barrier, non-replicating vaccines tend to elicit suboptimal T cell immunity within the respiratory tract. Consequently, there is a necessity for potent mucosal adjuvants to effectively counteract the immunoregulatory mechanisms present in the respiratory mucosa [7, 24]. The inactivated influenza vaccines, when combined with an appropriate adjuvant, induce robust protection as well as cross-protection against infections caused by heterosubtypic or homologous viruses [29, 30].

The antiviral innate immune response in host cells begins with pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that detect invading viruses [31, 32]. The cGAS-STING pathway activates when viral or cellular DNA binds in the cytoplasm, resulting in cGAMP production. Then, cGAMP functions as a signaling molecule that initiates the activation of STING1, along with downstream components such as TBK1 and IRF3 pathways. Thereby inducing the release of type I interferons and interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), which in turn activate the innate immune response. The DNA sensing pathway activated by innate immunity is crucial in regulating PRV infection [31, 33]. However, PRV proteins, including UL13, UL24, and gE, have evolved sophisticated strategies to inhibit the type I interferons and ISGs production by targeting key factors such as IRF3 or IRF7 [14, 15, 34]. cGAMP functions as an agonist which stimulates the STING pathway. This activation leads to cytokine production, which enhances adaptive immune responses through multiple mechanisms [18]. Therefore, we used the cGAMP as an adjuvant for inactivated PRV vaccine in this study.

cGAMP was chosen among various STING agonists (c-di-GMP, c-di-AMP, ADU-S100, DMXAA, E7766) because it is naturally occurring, active in both mice and pigs, and has a well-established safety profile for mucosal delivery. Unlike DMXAA, which is species-restricted to mice [35], or c-di-GMP/c-di-AMP, which are less potent or trigger inflammasome activation [36], cGAMP effectively activates airway epithelial cells and induces balanced immune responses. Thus, cGAMP is the most suitable STING agonist for intranasal PRV vaccination in this study.

cGAMP is a well‑established STING agonist adjuvant, and its mechanisms-such as activating dendritic cells/macrophages, promoting CD8⁺T cell responses and antibody production, and inducing type I IFNs-are largely known [9, 12, 33]. However, the present study offers several novel insights that go beyond a simple application of cGAMP to a PRV vaccine. First, we provide a systematic, dose‑dependent characterization of both antigen and cGAMP in an intranasal PRV vaccine-a level of detail often missing in adjuvant studies but critical for real‑world vaccine development. Second, we uncover an unexpected kinetic divergence: cGAMP induced a robust and persistent systemic IgG response but only a transient mucosal IgA response in BALF. This challenges the assumption that STING agonists are inherently strong mucosal adjuvants and has direct implications for PRV control, which requires both systemic and respiratory protection. Third, this is the first study to characterize cGAMP’s innate immune effects using ALI cultured mouse bronchial epithelial cells, directly showing that airway epithelial cells are active participants in cGAMP sensing-not merely passive barriers. Fourth, we address a real‑world problem – the failure of traditional PRV vaccines (e.g., Bartha K61) against emerging Chinese variants-by demonstrating that a cGAMP‑adjuvanted inactivated antigen restores protective immunity without requiring live virus or complex reformulation [19]. Fifth, our intranasal delivery data provide the first direct evidence that the route of administration profoundly influences the quality and duration of cGAMP‑induced mucosal immunity [23]. Sixth, combining cGAMP with an intranasal inactivated vaccine offers practical advantages beyond adjuvant activity: no risk of reversion to virulence or shedding, suitability for PRV‑free farms and young animals, bypass of maternal antibody interference, direct engagement of the respiratory mucosa, and rapid “plug‑and‑play” updating to match emerging field variants. These contributions are neither trivial nor fully predictable from previous literature.

We acknowledge that cGAMP is currently more expensive than conventional adjuvants such as aluminum salts or oil emulsions. However, several factors support its potential economic feasibility in the context of PRV vaccination. First, the dose required for intranasal immunization in mice was 5 µg per mouse, and by extrapolation, the effective dose in piglets would likely be in the range of 1–5 mg per animal (based on body weight and mucosal surface area). With the decreasing cost of synthetic cyclic dinucleotides due to improved manufacturing processes (e.g., enzymatic synthesis), the cost per dose is expected to become competitive. Second, the needle-free intranasal route reduces labor costs and eliminates risks associated with needle injuries, which is particularly beneficial for large-scale pig farms. Third, cGAMP may allow for lower antigen doses (dose-sparing effect), as shown in our dose-dependent study, potentially reducing overall vaccine production costs. Fourth, the added value of protecting piglets against emerging PRV variants-which cause mortality rates of 10–50%-far outweighs the incremental cost of a more effective adjuvant. Nevertheless, we agree that a formal cost-benefit analysis using large-animal data is necessary before commercialization. Future work should evaluate cost-effectiveness using pig models.

IgA, constituting over 15% of total immunoglobulins, is more prevalent in mucosal secretions than in serum [25]. IgA present in the nasal mucosa is essential for protecting the mucosal surface from pathogens, preventing their attachment and entry through the nasal epithelium. The induction of both local IgA and serum IgG can enhance the effectiveness of intranasal immunization, potentially leading to cross-reactive antibodies. In this study, we observed that inactivated PRV antigen alone elicited only a limited respiratory mucosal immune response. However, intranasal administration of an inactivated PRV vaccine formulated with cGAMP induced persistent serum IgG levels, transient IgA antibodies in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), as well as T cell responses. These findings indicate that cGAMP may facilitate the activation of respiratory mucosal immune cells by PRV, thereby eliciting both local respiratory mucosal and systemic immune responses. This mechanism likely contributes to protection against PRV challenge. It should be noted that these findings specifically reflect immune responses in the respiratory mucosa (as assessed by BALF IgA); whether cGAMP induces broader mucosal immunity in other compartments such as the oral cavity or intestine remains to be investigated in future studies.

Ali systems offer a study environment that more accurately mimics in vivo conditions compared to submerged epithelial models [37]. To gain a clearer understanding of how cGAMP exerts its effects, we established Ali-cultured MBECs system in this study. The system is well-differentiated and polarized. To simulate the conduction of intranasal immunization in vivo, Ali-MBECs were exposed to PG40 or P40 from the apical side for varying durations. cGAMP effectively activated the cGAS-STING pathway, induced the mRNA expression of ISGs, which in turn activate the innate immune response.

Mice serve as an established alternative model for PRV efficacy studies [29, 38, 39]. Specifically, mice are a well-established, low-cost, and ethically convenient model for the initial screening of adjuvant activity and dose optimization [40]. Moreover, the cGAS-STING pathway is highly conserved between mice and pigs, supporting the translational relevance of murine data [41–43]. In addition, the murine intranasal immunization model is widely used for evaluating mucosal vaccines against respiratory viruses [7]. We fully agree that follow-up studies in pigs are essential, and ongoing work in our laboratory is testing the cGAMP-adjuvanted PRV vaccine in piglets, including challenge studies with the virulent PRV variant. These results will be reported in a separate manuscript. Therefore, the present study should be viewed as a necessary foundational step that provides critical proof-of-concept data to enable subsequent large-animal studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that cGAMP significantly enhanced the nasal immunological efficacy of the inactivated PRV vaccine by activating the STING signaling pathway. Consequently, cGAMP exhibited considerable potential as an effective mucosal immune adjuvant for use in inactivated PRV vaccine. This approach provides novel technical support for immunization and prevention against PRV infection. Future studies in pigs, cost-effectiveness analyses, and mechanistic investigations using STING-deficient models will further validate the translational value of this approach.

Methods

Animals

Female ICR mice (6-8weeks old) were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Centre of Yangzhou University (Yangzhou, China). Euthanasia was performed by introducing 100% CO2 into a sealed chamber at a rate of 50% VDR/min, following the approved IACUC protocol. It was necessary to expose the mice to carbon dioxide for a minimum of 5 min to guarantee irreversible euthanasia [44]. All animal experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of JAAS (Approval No. IACUC-PA-2023-06-003).

Virus

The PRV strain SQ was sourced from JAAS and cultured using methods outlined in our previous study. The virus was then inactivated and purified by ultracentrifugation through a 20% sucrose cushion (100,000×g, 2 h, 4 °C).The resulting pellet was resuspended in sterile PBS, and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay (Beyotime Biotechnology). The purified inactivated PRV antigen was stored at -80 °C until use [9].

Preparation and immunization of vaccines

cGAMP (lot BLV028) was purchased from Bidepharm Bio-Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China), and diluted with PBS to a concentration of 2 mg/mL. cGAMP was combined with inactivated PRV antigen (108 TCID50/mL, 1 mg/ml) to formulate PRV nasal vaccine.

For the immune dose study, mice (n = 6 per group) were randomly assigned to seven groups. Three groups received intranasal immunization with 20 µL (20 µg), 30 µL (30 µg), or 40 µL (40 µg) of the inactivated PRV antigen (2 mg/ml, 10⁸ TCID₅₀ /mL) alone (designated P20, P30, and P40, respectively). Three additional groups received the same volumes of antigen combined with 5 µg of cGAMP (designated PG20, PG30, and PG40, respectively). The total volume for each immunization was adjusted to 50 µL with PBS. A separate set of groups (n = 6 per group) received a fixed volume of inactivated PRV antigen (40 µL) alone or in combination with varying doses of cGAMP (1 µg, 3 µg, or 5 µg), also in a total volume of 50 µL. For the antibody duration study, mice (n = 6 per group) received intranasal immunization on days 0 and 14 with 40 µL of inactivated PRV antigen combined with 5 µg of cGAMP in a total volume of 50 µL. The vaccine formulation was administered intranasally using a sterile pipette tip attached to a standard micropipette (20–200 µL). The volume was delivered dropwise (5–10 µL per drop) over 30–60 s, alternating between nostrils (25 µL per nostril). Each drop was fully inhaled before the next was added. Mice that sneezed, expelled liquid from the nares, or showed visible swallowing were excluded from the study. BALF and serum samples were collected at different time points according to the experimental purpose: at 28 days post the first immunization for the immune dose study, and 14, 28, 35, 42, and 49 days for the antibody duration study. A PBS intranasal control group was included in all studies.

Viral challenge

For the immune protocol study (single vs. double immunization), mice were randomly assigned to 6 groups (n = 16 per group). For single immunization, PG40 group received a single intranasal immunization on day 0. For dual immunization, four groups received two intranasal immunizations on days 0 and 14: P20, PG20, P40, and PG40. The total volume for each immunization was adjusted to 50 µL with PBS. All mice were intranasally challenged with PRV (100 LD₅₀) at 28 days after the first immunization. Among the 16 mice in each group, 10 mice were monitored daily for body weight changes and clinical symptoms for 14 days post challenge (dpc) to determine survival rates. The remaining 6 mice in each group were euthanized at 4 dpc, and their brains, spinal cords, and lungs were harvested for viral load measurement and histological analysis.

Detection of serological antibodies

PRV-specific IgG, IgG1, IgG2a, IgG2c or IgA antibody levels were measured by indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) as previously described [10]. In brief, 20 µg/mL inactivated and purified PRV antigen was coated onto plates in NaHCO3 buffer, pH9.6 overnight. Then the plates were washed with PBST and blocked with 2% BSA for 2 h at room temperature. After incubation with diluted serum or BALF samples, HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG, IgG1, IgG2a, IgG2c or IgA antibody (1:20000, Bethyl, USA) was added to measure specific subtypes. Finally, 100 µL of enzyme-substrate solution (TMB, Beyotime Biotech, Nantong, China) was added to each well and incubated under dark conditions for 15 min at room temperature [38].

Flow cytometry

To assess the cellular immune response, mice (n = 5 per group) were intranasally immunized twice on days 0 and 14 with PBS, P40, or PG40 as described above. At 7 days after the second immunization, the splenocytes were then blocked with anti-CD16/32 antibody (Biolegend, USA), stained with FITC-labelled anti-mouse CD3, PE-labelled anti-mouse CD8a, and APC-labelled anti-mouse CD4 (Elabscience, USA). Following 1 h of incubation, the cells were washed and subsequently analyzed using flow cytometry [38].

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis

To quantify the viral loads of PRV in the tissues, mice were euthanized and necropsied at 4dpc. Lungs, brains and spinal cords were collected after euthanasia for quantitative PCR analysis to determine PRV DNA loads. DNA was extracted from the samples using a DNA mini kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) per the manufacturer’s instructions. To quantify the PRV viral loads, extracted DNA was analysed in triplicate via real-time PCR as previously described [38].

Well-differentiated Ali-MBECs were treated with 50 µL PG40 and P40 vaccines at the apical side for different times (0, 6hpi). Total RNA was extracted from the different treated MBECS samples by using Super FastPure Cell RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme Biotech). Reverse transcription was carried out using HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme Biotech). RT reaction mixture was submitted to qRT-PCR using Mx1, ISG56, IFN-β, GAPDH specific primers (Table 1), and ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech). The reaction was conducted at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The GAPDH gene served as an internal reference, normalizing the target gene mRNA levels to that of GAPDH mRNA in the same sample. Melting curve analysis of the RT-PCR products confirmed specific amplification.

Isolation and Ali culture of primary mice bronchial epithelial cells (MBECs)

The isolation and culture of primary mouse bronchial epithelial cells were conducted as previously described [24]. The bronchi from mice were carefully dissected and transversely sectioned into 1-mm thick rings. Subsequently, the bronchi were washed in chilled D-Hank’s solution and incubated in a pronase/DNase solution for 24 h at 4 °C. The bronchi cells were collected, resuspended, and fibroblasts were removed through differential adherence to plastic surfaces. Epithelial cells were then resuspended in Pneuma Cult™ Ex Plus Medium (StemCell Technologies, BC, Canada). Following this step, the cells were cultured on collagen-coated Trans well permeable supports (Corning, NY, USA) at a density of 3–5 × 106 cells/mL.0.6mL of Pneuma Cult™ Ex Plus Medium (StemCell Technologies) was added to the lower chambers while 0.2 mL of cell suspension was introduced into the upper chambers. Upon reaching confluence, the apical medium was removed to establish an Ali culture; subsequently, Pneuma Cult™ Ali Medium (StemCell Technologies) replaced the previous medium. The differentiation medium was refreshed every 2 days while simultaneously washing the surface of the cells with HBSS to eliminate mucus accumulation. The duration of culture days is defined in relation to the start of Ali culture on Day 1 (D1).

Histological examination

At 4dpc, three mice from each group were euthanized, and their lungs were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime Biotechnology, Nantong, China). The lung tissues were then processed for paraffin embedding, sectioned into slices, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) [24].

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

The filter membranes containing MBECs were rinsed with PBS and subsequently fixed using 2.5% glutaraldehyde in a 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH7.4). Specimens underwent dehydration through a series of graded acetone solutions and were then dried using hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS) solution (Sigma-Aldrich). Following drying, the specimens were coated with a thin platinum layer via an ion beam coater as part of a precision etching and coating system (PECS; Gatan France, Evry, France). Finally, the samples were examined using a Zeiss ultra+ field emission gun scanning electron microscope (FEGSEM; Carl Zeiss S.A.S., Le Pecq, France).

Indirect immunofluorescence assays (IFAs)

To detect ZO-1 and β-tubulin protein on the surfaces of MBECs, the MBECs were first blocked and then washed with PBS. They were subsequently incubated overnight at 2–8 °C with β-tubulin (1:200 dilution; Novus Biologicals, Littleton, USA) or ZO-1 antibodies (1:100 dilution; Abcam) in a solution containing 1% BSA. Following this, MBECs were washed and incubated for 1 h with goat anti-mouse IgG (Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated; Beyotime Biotechnology) or goat anti-rabbit IgG (Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated; Beyotime Biotechnology). For nuclear staining, MBECs were treated with 2,4-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Beyotime Biotechnology) for 5 min. Finally, the cells were washed and imaged using an Ultra View VOX laser scanning confocal microscope (PerkinElmer, USA) [24].

Western blot analysis

Ali-MBECs, subjected to various treatments, were lysed using a cell lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology) at indicated times. The protein concentration was measured using the BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology). Equivalent amounts of the lysates were heated in SDS loading buffer for 5 min and then separated on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels. Subsequently, the proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore, MA, USA) via a semi-dry transfer technique. The membranes were incubated with PBST containing 5% BSA at 37 °C for 45 min to block non-specific binding sites. Following three washes with PBST, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: rabbit monoclonal antibodies against pIRF3 and IRF3, as well as β-actin (Beyotime Biotechnology). After another three washes with PBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Beyotime Biotechnology) at 37 °C for 1 h. The membranes were then washed five times with PBST before being visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Beyotime Biotechnology). The protein band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software [45].

Statistical analysis

The results were presented as individual data points or means±standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons, and Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons, using GraphPad Prism software. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
