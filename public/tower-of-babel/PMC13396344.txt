Krüppel-like factor 4 downregulation during esophageal cancer formation facilitates immune evasion and impairs immunotherapy
Authors: Xiao Hu, Chenying Li, Dongxu Li, Ying Cao, Ziyi He, Zhenghao Dong, Xiaoqi Jiang, Tao Xiang, Yonglin Yi, Hanzhang Yi, Dongxin Lin, Chen Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Impaired antigen processing and presentation are critical for cancer immune evasion, but the underlying mechanism is not fully known. Here, we show that downregulation of Krüppel-like factor 4 (KLF4) expression during the development of cancer is essential in this immuno-decisive process. We find that suppressed KLF4 expression causes diminished MHC class I production in esophageal cancer, forming immunosuppressive niches that limit CD8⁺ T cell infiltration. Mechanistically, KLF4 loss reduces chromatin accessibility at the MHC class I loci and the enhanceosome complex formation, suppressing MHC class I expression. In mouse allografts, KLF4 loss promotes tumor progression and immunotherapy resistance. Extending beyond esophageal cancer, we reveal that low KLF4 levels are significantly correlated with high immunotherapy failure rates across multiple human cancer types. We demonstrate that pharmacological induction of KLF4 results in enhanced antigen presentation, increases activity of CD8⁺ T cells and improves therapeutic efficacy to PD-1 blockade in mouse models. These findings extend our knowledge of cancer immunology and provide a novel insight for improving cancer immunotherapy.

Introduction

Squamous-cell carcinoma (SCC) is a predominant histological subtype of esophageal cancer, which itself ranks as the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide.1,2 Dysphagia is often the initial clinical manifestation, typically reflecting locally advanced disease and carrying an unfavorable prognosis at diagnosis.3 Over the past decade, therapies that block immune checkpoints, especially inhibitors of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, have revolutionized cancer therapy, transforming the treatment landscape for several malignancies.4,5 In esophageal SCC (ESCC), immune checkpoint inhibitors have been integrated into first-line regimens for advanced or unresectable disease, offering renewed hope for improved clinical outcomes.3,6–10 However, clinical responses remain highly heterogeneous, with up to 60%−70% of patients exhibiting either primary resistance or acquired resistance.11–13 This variability underscores the urgent need to unravel the mechanisms underlying immune evasion and resistance in ESCC to improve the therapeutic outcomes.

Antigen presentation via the major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC class I) is central to antitumor immunity, enabling cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells to recognize and eliminate transformed cells.14–16 Nevertheless, malignant cells commonly downregulate MHC class I expression to escape immune surveillance, which represents a major process driving tumor progression and therapeutic resistance to immunotherapy.17–20 Notably, more than half of ESCC cases demonstrate reduced levels of MHC class I expression,21 suggesting that it may be a critical immune-evasive mechanism that directly impacts the efficacy of checkpoint blockade. Therefore, deciphering the molecular mechanism driving MHC class I suppression in ESCC and exploiting drug-based approaches to reinstate its expression are essential toward improving anticancer immunity and immunotherapy outcomes in this cancer.

The zinc-finger transcription factor krüppel-like factor 4 (KLF4) is well-known as one of the Yamanaka reprogramming factors.22–24 Beyond its role in cellular plasticity, KLF4 also regulates epithelial homeostasis, differentiation and apoptosis.25–28 KLF4 is increasingly recognized as a tumor suppressor across various cancer types, such as esophageal,29,30 lung,31–33 liver,34–36 pancreatic,37,38 gastric39,40 and colorectal cancer.41–43 We have previously reported that in ESCC cells, KLF4 depletion disrupts ANXA1-FPR2 signaling, which subsequently triggers fibroblast activation to promote tumor progression.44 However, whether KLF4 directly governs tumor-immune interactions, particularly through antigen presentation, remains unexplored.

Here, we combined single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, and proteomics of human and murine ESCC to uncover a previously unappreciated immunoregulatory function of KLF4. Our results show that KLF4 expression in basal epithelial cells sustains MHC class I gene expression and facilitates productive cross-talk with CD8⁺ T cells. Loss of KLF4 expression impairs antigen presentation, drives expansion of immunosuppressive cell populations, and dampens cytotoxic T cell responses. Mechanistically, we uncover that KLF4 maintains chromatin accessibility and orchestrates enhanceosome assembly via CREB/ATF1 recruitment to activate MHC class I gene transcription. Moreover, inducing KLF4 expression by APTO-253 restores antigen presentation and renders tumor cells across multiple cancer types more sensitive to immune checkpoint blockade. Taken together, we have identified KLF4 as an epithelial-intrinsic master regulator of antigen presentation and as a candidate target to overcome immune evasion and resistance to immunotherapy. Furthermore, the results from pan-cancer analysis suggest that this regulatory axis may extend to other malignancies.

Results

KLF4 is critical in immune surveillance of esophageal cancer

Our previous spatial transcriptomics data showed that the homeostasis of esophageal epithelium and T cells was progressively disrupted during ESCC development. Compared to normal epithelium (NOR) and low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (LGIN), high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (HGIN) and ESCC exhibited multiple shifted pathological structure hallmarks, including the depletion of basal cell populations, expansion of invasive cell populations and skewed T cell composition featuring depletion of effector T lymphocytes (Teff) alongside accumulation of exhausted T lymphocytes (Tex) and regulatory T lymphocytes (Tregs).45 These observations prompted us to investigate the underlying mechanisms. Our initial assessment of eight functional programs across epithelial cells44 revealed that the invasive cell subpopulation exhibited a markedly lower antigen-presenting score than the basal cell subpopulation (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Moreover, we found that epithelial cell-intrinsic antigen processing and presentation activity progressively declined from premalignant lesions to invasive carcinoma in the single-cell RNA sequencing data from multi-stage human and murine ESCC samples44,46 (Fig. 1a), suggesting that defective antigen presentation may drive immune evasion during ESCC development and progression.Fig. 1KLF4 is critical for immune surveillance against esophageal cancer. a Scores of antigen processing and presentation pathway across pathological stages in human (left) and mouse (right) esophageal samples. Because antigen processing/presentation pathways are central to immune regulation, we performed proteomic analysis of human ESCC samples and found that compared with basal epithelial cells, invasive epithelial cells expressed significantly reduced levels of antigen-presentation pathway components (Fig. 1b). Subsequently, we aimed to identify key regulators responsible for the observed downregulation of the antigen presentation pathway. By integrating scRNA-seq data with proteomic profiles, we identified KLF4 as the most significantly altered regulator (Supplementary Fig. 1b). In the previous studies, we have identified KLF4 as a tumor suppressor that maintains mucosal defense mechanism in the esophagus, with its RNA levels being significantly decreased during development and progression of ESCC in human and mouse models44,46 (Supplementary Fig. 1c). KLF4 RNA levels showed a strong positive correlation with the RNA levels of classical MHC class I molecules HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C (Fig. 1c), indicating its potential role in regulating antigen presentation. These results implicate basal epithelial cells with high KLF4 expression as key mediators of antigen presentation capacity, potentially sustaining immune surveillance. By further analysis of these scRNA-seq data, we observed that effector CD8+ T cells decreased substantially while Tex and Treg cells expanded (Supplementary Fig. 1d, 1e). Notably, the KLF4 RNA levels in epithelial cells showed a positive association with effector T cell function and an inverse association with markers of exhausted T cells (Supplementary Fig. 1f, 1g). Analysis of cell-cell communications indicated that KLF4-high epithelial cells sustained a stronger interaction of their MHC class I with CD8+ T cells than KLF4-low epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 1h and 1i), suggesting a role for KLF4 in sustaining antigen-specific T cell response.

We thus validated these findings in our other published dataset from 60 ESCC samples47 and the results showed that KLF4-low ESCC had significantly suppressed expression levels of the antigen processing/presentation and MHC protein complex pathways compared with KLF4-high ESCC (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1j). In addition, we found that KLF4-low ESCC displayed a more immunosuppressive microenvironment than KLF4-high ESCC, characterized by significantly reduced Teff cells but significantly increased Tex, Treg cells and SPP1+ macrophages (Figs. 1e, f and Supplementary Fig. 1k). We also examined the differences in spatial distributions of various CD8+ T cell subtypes between KLF4-high or -low ESCC samples in our previously reported spatial transcriptomic data45 and the results demonstrated that consistent with the KLF4 level decline from NOR, LGIN, HGIN to ESCC, the numbers of infiltrating Tex cells, Treg cells and SPP1+ macrophages gradually increased alongside a gradual decline of Teff cells (Fig. 1g). We partitioned 127 fields of view (FOVs) into square grids of 100 ×100 μm and classified them as high or low KLF4 level based on the mean KLF4 level across all grids. Low KLF4 grid regions, relative to high KLF4 ones, had significantly fewer Teff cells but significantly greater fractions of Tex cells, Treg cells and SPP1+ macrophages (Fig. 1h).

Since KLF4 is implicated in antigen presentation pathway, we next examined Teff cells by multiplex immunofluorescence. We observed that during ESCC progression, KLF4, MHC class I protein, and infiltrating GZMB+CD8+ T cells gradually and significantly declined (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Moreover, KLF4 levels positively correlated with the levels of MHC class I, the density of CD8+ T cells and the density of GZMB+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Together, these results suggest that diminished KLF4 expression in basal cells of the esophageal epithelium may suppress cancer immune surveillance by disrupting the epithelial-T cell homeostasis.

KLF4-deficiency reduces MHC class I expression to promote immune evasion of ESCC

We next investigated whether KLF4 regulates MHC class I expression in ESCC cells from mice and human. Relative to control cells, KLF4/Klf4-KO cells had markedly reduced MHC class I levels (Figs. 2a–c), whereas KLF4-OE cells showed significantly increased MHC class I levels (Fig. 2d). In mouse allograft tumor models, tumors derived from Klf4-KO mEC25 cells had markedly accelerated growth and decreased MHC class I protein, diminished CD8⁺ and GZMB⁺CD8⁺ T cells infiltration compared with those derived from control mEC25 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3a–3c). To test whether these effects were dependent on impaired MHC class I related cytotoxicity, we established OVA-expressing mEC25 cell lines with or without Klf4 KO. The flow cytometry analysis showed that compared with Klf4-non-KO cells, Klf4-KO cells had significantly diminished expression levels of H-2Kb/D,b a mouse MHC class I molecule, and H-2Kb: OVA, an MHC class I and OVA complex (Fig. 2e, f). These OVA+ mEC25 cells were then cocultured with OT-I CD8⁺ T cells. When OT-I CD8⁺ T cells were present, Klf4-KO OVA+ mEC25 cells had significantly reduced cell death compared with Klf4-non-KO mEC25 cells. Moreover, OT-I CD8⁺ T cells cocultured with Klf4-KO OVA+ mEC25 cells produced significantly lower IFN-γ, GZMB and TNF-α compared with those cocultured with Klf4-non-KO OVA+ mEC25 cells (Figs. 2g–i). We then tested these KLF4 effects in the mouse allograft tumor model and found that the growth rate of tumors derived from Klf4-KO mEC25 cells was significantly faster than those from Klf4-non-KO mEC25 cells. However, when CD8⁺ T cells were depleted using anti-CD8β antibody, the growth rates of Klf4-KO and Klf4-non-KO tumors became similar and both grew much faster than tumors in mice without CD8+ T cell depletion (Fig. 2j, k). Flow cytometric analysis of allograft tumor tissues revealed that Klf4-KO tumors had significantly lower MHC class I and CD8+ T cell levels than Klf4-non-KO tumors, whereas the percentage of CD45+ cells and CD3+ T cells did not differ significantly between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 3d–3f). Multiplex immunofluorescence analysis also indicated that Klf4-KO tumors had a significantly lower percentage of GZMB+CD8+ T cells among total CD8+ T cells compared to Klf4-non-KO tumors (Fig. 2l and Supplementary Fig. 3g). Since MHC class I is required and important in cancer immunity, we next wanted to explore whether the KLF4-MHC class I axis may also play a role in various tumor types other than ESCC. We knocked down KLF4 expression in multiple human cell lines, including NCI-H1299 (lung cancer), HepG2 (liver cancer) and JF-305 (pancreatic cancer). Interestingly, all these cancer cell lines with KLF4 silenced by small-interfering RNA (siRNA) had significantly reduced MHC class I expression levels compared with controls (Supplementary Fig. 4a–4c). Conversely, Klf4 overexpression in murine MC38 (colorectal cancer) and B16F10 (melanoma) cells significantly increased the level of MHC class I (Supplementary Fig. 4d, 4e). We then generated both Klf4 and OVAL overexpressing MC38 cells and B16F10 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4f–4i) and observed that upon coculture with OT-I CD8+ T cells, these double-overexpressing cancer cells were killed more efficiently than control cells (Supplementary Fig. 4j, 4k). Meanwhile, OT-I CD8+ T cells cocultured with both Klf4 and OVAL overexpressing cancer cells generated significantly higher frequencies of IFN-γ⁺, GZMB⁺ and TNF-α⁺ CD8⁺ T cells, relative to those cocultured with control cancer cells (Supplementary Fig. 4l, 4m).

We next turned to immunocompetent mouse allograft models using MC38 and B16F10 cells to examine the in vivo relevance of these findings in other cancer type models. Interestingly, overexpressing Klf4 markedly suppressed MC38- and B16F10-derived tumor growth in mice; however, this effect could be completely abrogated by depleting mouse CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 5a, 5b). Flow cytometric analysis of tumor tissues demonstrated that Klf4 overexpressed allograft tumors produced a coordinated immunogenic response, i.e., significantly elevated MHC class I expression on tumor cells, which in turn resulted in increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells relative to allograft tumors derived from Klf4 non-overexpressed cancer cells (Supplementary Fig. 5c–5f). The immunofluorescent analysis results of tumor tissues were consistent with the flow cytometric analysis results and further confirmed an expanded population of cytotoxic GZMB+CD8+ T cells in Klf4-overexpressed tumors compared to Klf4 non-overexpressed tumors (Supplementary Fig. 5g, 5h). Collectively, these experimental results from ESCC and other cancer models suggest that KLF4 may be a conserved regulator of MHC class I-mediated tumor immunity across multiple cancer types.

KLF4 is crucial in enhanceosome complex formation and MHC class I transcription activation

We next explored how KLF4 mediates MHC class I expression at the molecular level. We found that in all investigated human and mouse cell lines, KLF4/Klf4 KO or knockdown (KD) significantly reduced MHC class I expression at both transcript and total protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 6a–6e) but KLF4/Klf4 overexpression had opposite effects (Supplementary Fig. 6f–6h), suggesting that KLF4 regulates MHC class I expression at the transcriptional level. We analyzed the publicly available ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq data48 and the data indicated that KLF4 does not directly bind to the HLA-A/B/C promoter regions (Supplementary Fig. 6i) but KLF4 overexpression enhanced the chromatin accessibility at these loci (Supplementary Fig. 6j); however, KLF4 KD had the converse results (Supplementary Fig. 6k). Notably, these chromatin regions located within 300-bp upstream of the transcription start site, encompassing enhancer A (NF-κB-responsive) and the enhanceosome complex, both are critical for MHC class I transcription.49–51 Since KLF4 is known to interact with RELA to activate NF-κB signaling,52,53 which can regulate MHC class I expression,54,55 we focused particularly on the less-characterized enhanceosome complex for examining KLF4-mediated regulation of MHC class I.

Previous reports indicate that KLF4 can interact with CREB,56,57 a transcription factor that can form a heterodimer with ATF1 to perform its transcriptional activity.58–61 We first excluded the possibility that changes in KLF4 expression alter CREB and ATF1 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 6l, 6m) and thus hypothesized that KLF4 might interact with CREB/ATF1, the core components of the complex. To test this, we performed reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation assays, and the results demonstrated physical interactions among these 3 proteins (Fig. 3a–c). We also conducted immunofluorescence staining and further confirmed that these 3 proteins co-localize in the nuclei of multiple cancer cells (Fig. 3d, e). More importantly, knockdown of CREB1 or ATF1 in KLF4-overexpressed KYSE410, MC38 and B16F10 cells, in which the KLF4 level was high, significantly diminished the levels of both total and surface MHC class I in these cells (Fig. 3f–k). Pharmacological Induction of KLF4 enhances antigen presentation and anticancer immunity

Based on the results described above, we examined whether pharmacologically inducing KLF4 may rescue KLF4 loss-caused antigen presentation and CD8+ T cell-mediated anticancer immunity. To do this, we utilized APTO-253, a small molecule KLF4 inducer that has completed Phase I clinical trials with a favorable safety profile.62 We found that APTO-253 treatment robustly upregulated KLF4 and MHC class I transcripts as well as protein levels in a range of human cancer cell lines, including ESCC (KYSE150 and KYSE450), lung adenocarcinoma (NCI-H1299), hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG2) and pancreatic cancer (JF-305). Notably, surface MHC class I expression was also significantly enhanced (Figs. 4a–c and Supplementary Fig. 7a–7l). Consistent effects were also observed in OVA-expressed mouse cancer cells such as mEC25, MC38 and B16F10 cells (Figs. 4d–f and Supplementary Fig. 7m–7r). Furthermore, coculture experiments showed that APTO-253-treated OVA+ cancer cells (mEC25, MC38 and B16F10) were killed more efficiently by OT-I CD8+ T cells as indicated by increased cancer cell death and higher percentages of IFN-γ,+ GZMB,+ and TNF-α+ CD8+ T cells among OT-I CD8+ T cells (Figs. 4g–i and Supplementary Fig. 7s–7v). Using immunocompetent C57BL/6 J mice bearing allograft tumors, we found that APTO-253 administration markedly suppressed tumor growth compared with control (Figs. 4j, k and Supplementary Fig. 8a, 8b). Flow cytometric analysis of tumor tissues revealed that APTO-253 treated tumors, relative to controls, had significantly upregulated MHC class I on CD45⁻ cells and significantly enhanced intratumoral infiltration of CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 8c–8h). Multiplex immunofluorescence analysis also corroborated these findings. In APTO-253 treated tumors, we detected higher KLF4 and MHC class I levels on cancer cells, as well as increased numbers of CD8+ T cells and GZMB⁺ cytotoxic CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment relative to controls (Fig. 4l and Supplementary Fig. 8i, 8j). These results suggest that KLF4 induction by APTO-253 improves MHC class I antigen presentation, which in turn triggers CD8+ T cell-dependent anticancer immunity.

Finally, we assessed how KLF4 levels in tumors correlate with clinical response of patients to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. First, we examined two public scRNA-seq datasets of ESCC samples taken prior to ICB plus platin/paclitaxel neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy that meet our requirements63,64 and the results showed that responders (N = 3 and N = 8, respectively) had significantly higher KLF4 RNA levels and antigen processing/presentation scores within their cancer cells relative to non-responders (N = 4 and N = 4, respectively) (Figs. 5e, f). Beyond ESCC, we further explored whether the association between KLF4 and ICB response might also exist in other cancer types. Analysis of a spatial transcriptomic dataset of liver cancer samples obtained before ICB therapy65 showed that responders to the therapy had significantly higher KLF4 RNA levels in cancer cells than non-responders (Fig. 5g). KLF4 deficiency is caused by its promoter hypermethylation and copy number loss

We next explored why KLF4 is progressively downregulated during tumorigenesis and progression of ESCC, which was observed in both present and our previous studies.44 By reanalyzing the whole-genome sequencing data of multi-stage ESCC samples,71 we found that significant KLF4 copy number reduction occurred in HGIN samples, but the copy number reduction was significantly prevalent only in the status of TP53 biallelic loss but not in the status of TP53 mutations (Figs. 6a, b). These results suggest that during the ESCC development, TP53 biallelic loss may cause KLF4 copy number deletion but TP53 mutations have limited effect on KLF4 expression. We therefore asked if the gradual downregulation of KLF4 expression during ESCC progression results from epigenetic mechanisms, specifically, promoter DNA methylation. By analyzing our previously published dataset,44 we found that HGIN and ESCC samples had significantly increased methylation levels within the 1,000 base-pair regions upstream of the KLF4 transcription start site compared to NOR and LGIN samples (Fig. 6c). Meanwhile, we downloaded and analyzed the methylome and transcriptome data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) for the common digestive system cancers, including esophageal (ESCA), gastric (STAD), colorectal (COAD and READ), biliary (CHOL), hepatic (LIHC) and pancreatic (PAAD).72 Specifically, we identified significantly higher KLF4 promoter methylation levels in colon cancer, cholangiocarcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma relative to matched normal tissues (Fig. 6d) but not in the rest tumor types. However, across all investigated tumor types, KLF4 RNA levels showed a strong negative correlation with its promoter methylation levels (Fig. 6e). We then treated several cancer cell lines with the demethylase inhibitor Bobcat339. This treatment led to a time-dependent decrease in both KLF4 RNA and protein expression levels (Fig. 6f–k). Discussion

A fundamental hallmark of human cancer is the ability to evade immune destruction, which frequently involves disrupted antigen processing and presentation. While this dysfunction is a common feature across many tumor types, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain intricate and incompletely resolved. In this study, we used esophageal squamous cell carcinoma as the primary model system and identified the master transcription factor KLF4 as a pivotal regulator of the intrinsic immunogenicity of cancer cells. This previously unrecognized role for KLF4 provides a novel mechanistic framework for understanding how its loss drives immune evasion in ESCC and potentially other malignancies. We demonstrate that normal KLF4 expression in epithelial cells is crucial for maintaining MHC class I-mediated antigen processing and presentation pathway, which sustains a homeostatic equilibrium between epithelial cells and surveillant T cells. Conversely, the progressive KLF4 loss during ESCC development fosters the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor niche, enabling cancer cells to escape immune killing and resist immunotherapy.

Our study further shows that declining KLF4 expression throughout the multistage pathogenesis of ESCC, an event that may be due to copy number deletion71 or other mechanisms such as promoter methylation, strongly correlates with key immunological deficits. These include marked reduction of surface MHC class I molecule levels in transformed epithelial cells, significantly impaired infiltration and activity of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T lymphocytes, as well as significant expansion of highly immunosuppressive subsets, such as Tex cells, Treg cells and SPP1+ macrophages. This immunological profile aligns precisely with clinical observations where tumors exhibiting low MHC class I expression often present with an immune-excluded phenotype and demonstrate inherent resistance to immunotherapy, including immune checkpoint blockade.17,73–75 Our spatial transcriptomic analysis has demonstrated that the tumor regions with low KLF4 expression levels are enriched with Tex cells, but contained rare Teff cells compared with the regions having high KLF4 expression level. These results with spatial resolution have provided further and direct evidence supporting that the effect of KLF4 dysregulation on tumor immunosuppressive states is a causal effect, not merely a correlation.

A central novelty of our work is the recognition that the loss of KLF4 affects not only the malignant phenotypes of tumor cells themselves but also governs the communication between tumor cells and their immune microenvironment. Previous studies on the role of KLF4 in cancer have focused primarily on its involvement in cell apoptosis, differentiation, proliferation, metastasis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition.30 These investigations have suggested KLF4 as a tumor suppressor or as an oncogene depending on the cancer context. Notably, a recent study revealed that caffeine improves CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor effects in colorectal cancer through upregulation of KLF4 in epithelial cells, which in turn suppresses COL12A1 expression and subsequent kynurenine production.76 However, this study did not specifically examine KLF4’s own role in the communication between epithelial cells and CD8⁺ T cells. Our present study has directly addressed this issue and revealed that the expression states of KLF4 in cancer cells determine the degree of immune engagement, dictating whether cancer cells may be recognized by T cells or evade immune detection. This perspective shifts KLF4 research from being limited to tumor-intrinsic biology toward a broader framework in which KLF4 orchestrates tumor-immune dynamics.

Equally important is our identification of a KLF4-regulated transcriptional mechanism for MHC class I expression. The majority of prior studies on MHC class I regulation have emphasized post-transcriptional processes, particularly through the autophagic-lysosomal pathway.77–79 While these regulatory layers are critical, relatively little attention has been given to the transcriptional machinery that initiates MHC class I expression. The present study has filled this void, showing that although KLF4 does not directly bind to the promoter regions of MHC class I to function as a classical transcriptional activator, it governs their expression by maintaining an open, accessible chromatin conformation at these key gene loci and facilitating the assembly of the enhanceosome complex through CREB/ATF1 recruitment to drive the transcription. This mode of epigenetic regulation represents a distinct and sophisticated mechanism of control over tumor immunogenicity that operates independently of direct DNA binding, thereby adding an upstream regulatory tier that determines the transcriptional initiation of antigen presentation genes and offers a new conceptual entry point for therapeutic intervention.

While we have focused primarily on ESCC to perform mechanistic and functional validation, we further examined whether the KLF4-MHC class I axis operates in other cancer types. Interestingly, we have revealed that the effect of KLF4 deficiency on tumor immune suppression is not only in ESCC but also in other malignancies, including lung, liver and pancreatic cancer. Across these cancer models, KLF4 knockdown universally suppresses the MHC class I levels and significantly promotes tumor cells to evade immune destruction. However, ectopic KLF4 overexpression can significantly enhance the MHC class I expression and effectively restore CD8⁺ T cell-mediated immunity within the tumor microenvironment. The results observed in cancer types other than ESCC may highlight the fact that KLF4 is a significant anticancer immunity regulator in pan-cancers and, therefore, is a potential therapeutic target with broad applicability. Indeed, by analyzing tumor samples taken before chemoimmunotherapy from patients with several different cancer types, we have revealed that negative response rates are significantly correlated with low KLF4 expression levels. These results suggest that KLF4 could function as both a predictive biomarker and therapeutic target to guide patient stratification for ICB therapy. Furthermore, we have demonstrated that a clinical trial-stage KLF4 inducer APTO-253 can robustly upregulate expression levels of MHC class I on tumor cells and enhance CD8⁺ T cell killing ability in an in vitro co-culture system. More importantly, in mouse allograft models of various cancers including ESCC, colon cancer and melanoma, APTO-253 treatment significantly improved ICB efficacy even though, as a single agent, it also suppressed tumor growth. These results provide compelling preclinical evidence that pharmacological induction of KLF4 may overcome primary and acquired ICB resistance. Since APTO-253 has already been demonstrated to be safe in clinical trials, it is interesting and warranted to explore its benefit in improving clinical cancer immunotherapy.

We acknowledge that, despite these advances, the present study has some limitations. We did not uniformly observe significant promoter hypermethylation in tumors versus normal tissues across all digestive system cancers from TCGA. The reduced KLF4 RNA levels in some investigated cancers might be caused by genetic changes such as copy number alterations, as we found in ESCC. We did not explore the functions of KLF4 in the non-canonical antigen presentation pathways, such as MHC class II or cross-presentation, which warrants further investigate. In addition, although we have established a mechanistic link between KLF4 and CREB/ATF1-mediated enhanceosome formation, we cannot exclude the possibility that additional cofactors may also participate in this regulatory process.

In conclusion, the present study identifies KLF4 as an important regulator for the epithelial-immune interactions in ESCC and provides evidence suggesting that this regulatory mechanism may also exist in other cancer types. Loss of KLF4 function in cancer cells not only promotes their own malignant phenotypes but also undermines the anticancer immunity, leading to immune exemption of cancer cells and resistance to ICB therapy. Materials and methods

Human tissue sample collection

We used formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded tissue sections from our previously established and published cohort of ESCC patients in this study,44 consisting of normal, dysplasia, and tumor tissues of the esophagus collected from the same individuals who underwent surgical resection at Linzhou Esophageal Cancer Hospital (Henan Province, China) and Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, between 2020 and 2021.

Cell lines and cell culture

KYSE150, KYSE410 and KYSE450 (human ESCC) were generously provided by Dr. Y. Shimada (Hyogo College of Medicine, Japan). NCI-H1299 (non-small cell lung cancer) was obtained from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, USA). HepG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma), MCF7 (breast cancer) and HCT-8 (ileocecal adenocarcinoma) were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, and the School of Basic Medicine, Peking Union Medical College (Beijing, China). JF-305 (pancreatic cancer) was acquired from the China Medical University Cancer Research Institute (Shenyang, China). mEC25 (mouse ESCC) was a kind gift from Dr. Li Fu at Shenzhen University International Cancer Center. MC38 (colorectal cancer) was purchased from Meisen Cell Technology (Zhejiang, China). B16F10 (melanoma) was obtained from Pricella Life Science & Technology (Wuhan, China). We maintained cells at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. We cultured KYSE150, KYSE450, HepG2, MCF7, JF-305, mEC25 and MC38 cells in DMEM (Gibco) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), while KYSE410, NCI-H1299, HCT-8 and B16F10 cells were in RPMI 1640 (Gibco). For pharmacological assays, cells were treated with APTO-253 (MCE), Bobcat339 (MCE) or DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich) as a control for 48 to 72 h.

Establishment of cell lines with lentivirus transfection

Lentiviral particles for genetic modifications including KLF4/Klf4 overexpression constructs, KLF4/Klf4 knockout systems and OVAL overexpression vectors were obtained from GeneChem (Supplementary Table 1). We infected cells with the viruses and cultured them in complete medium for 48 h. Then we selected the cells using puromycin (Gibco) for 1 week or hygromycin (APExBIO) for 2 weeks. Western blotting was performed to verify the efficiency of either overexpression or knockout.

Mouse allograft model establishment and treatment

We conducted all animal experiments in accordance with the protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (NCC2021A271). We acclimatized five-week-old C57BL/6 J mice (Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc.) for 7 days under standard housing conditions prior to the experiment. For tumor inoculation, mEC25 (8 × 106), MC38 (2 × 106), or B16F10 (1.5 × 106) cells resuspended in 100 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were subcutaneously injected into the right hind legs of mice. To deplete mouse CD8+ T cells, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 200 μg of anti-CD8β antibody (BioXCell) beginning 3 days before tumor cell inoculation, followed by repeated doses administered every 3 days throughout the experiment. Mice injected with same amount of anti-IgG isotype (BioXCell) were served as control. For APTO-253 (MCE) treatment experiment, the agent dissolved in a solution containing 10% DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich), 40% PEG300 (MCE), 5% Tween-80 (MCE) and 45% saline (Biosharp) was intraperitoneally injected into mice at a dose of 25 mg/kg every 3 days. Mice treated with the same amount of vehicle were served as controls. For anti-PD-1 treatment, 200 μg of anti-PD-1 antibody (BioXCell) was intraperitoneally injected into each mouse (4 total doses administered every 3 days), and mice given the same amount of anti-IgG isotype (BioXCell) were served as control. We also performed the combined therapy with the same doses of anti-PD-1 antibody and APTO-253. The dosing of anti-PD-1 antibody was temporally staggered by 12 h from dosing APTO-253 to minimize potential drug interactions. Moreover, mice in vehicle group of Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 9 received both the anti-IgG isotype and vehicle for APTO‑253, ensuring consistency with the treatment groups. We monitored tumor growth by measuring tumors with digital calipers every 3 days and calculated volume as 0.5 × length × width.2 A tumor volume exceeding 2000 mm3 or completion of treatment was considered the endpoint of the experiment. At endpoint, tumors were harvested. Fresh tumor tissues were immediately processed for flow cytometry, while the rest were fixed in 4% formaldehyde and paraffin-embedded for histological analysis.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

Our previously published scRNA-seq data of ESCC were reanalyzed.44,47 For cell type annotation, T cell and myeloid cell subtypes were identified based on canonical marker genes expression levels (Supplementary Table 2). For pathway enrichment analysis, we stratified 44,547 epithelial cells derived from 60 ESCC tumors into KLF4-high and KLF4-low groups according to their normalized KLF4 expression levels. We identified differentially expressed genes between the KLF4-high and KLF4-low groups using the FindMarkers function in Seurat (v5.0.2) with default parameters. We selected genes with an adjusted P < 0.05 and absolute log₂ fold change > 0 for further analysis. Enrichment analysis was then performed with the enrichKEGG and enrichGO functions from the clusterProfiler R package (v4.6.2), referencing the org.Hs.eg.db annotation. CellChat (version 2.1.2) was primarily used to examine the communication patterns between epithelial cells and T cells in cell-cell communication. We performed all preprocessing steps according to the package’s default parameters. The analysis was conducted using the CellChatDB.human database as the reference for known ligand-receptor interactions. For gene signature scoring, we quantified immune activation and dysfunction signatures across epithelial cell and T cell subsets by calculating module scores for curated gene sets. The antigen processing and presentation score was based on the KEGG-defined gene set for this pathway. The effector function score was derived from the GO biological process term ‘positive regulation of immune effector process’. The exhaustion score was computed using canonical exhaustion markers including PDCD1, TIGIT, CTLA4, LAG3 and HAVCR2. We computed all scores using the AddModuleScore with default parameters.

Single-cell spatial transcriptomic data analysis

We investigated the local spatial relationships between epithelial KLF4 expression level and immune cell composition in ESCC using our previously published single-cell spatial transcriptomic data.45 For each FOV, single-cell coordinates were partitioned into square grids of 100 × 100 μm. Grids containing fewer than three epithelial cells were discarded to avoid sparsity artifacts. For each retained grid, the average KLF4 expression level of epithelial cells was calculated, and grids were stratified into KLF4-high or KLF4-low categories using the global mean KLF4 value across all grids. Immune cell subsets (Teff, Tex, Treg and SPP1⁺ macrophages) were enumerated within each grid. To normalize for differences in immune infiltration, cell-type frequencies were expressed as a fraction of the corresponding immune parent population (e.g., Teff/total CD8+ T cells). We compared groups using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and visualized the distribution with violin plots overlaid with single-tile values.

Acquisition and processing of TCGA methylation and expression data

We obtained DNA methylation data and RNA-seq expression data for The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) normal and tumor samples from the UCSC Xena browser (http://xena.ucsc.edu/). Based on the GENCODE v36 (hg38) gene annotation, we defined promoter regions as 1.5 kb upstream to 1.0 kb downstream of the transcription start site (TSS). We calculated the promoter methylation level as the mean β-value of all CpG probes located within the defined promoter region.

Assessment of KLF4 as the biomarker for predicting immunotherapy response

We integrated single-cell RNA-seq datasets of six cancer types to build logistic regression models for distinguishing responders from non-responders, including esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (N = 19),63,64 non-small cell lung cancer (N = 7),66 gastric cancer (N = 5),67 colorectal cancer (N = 10),68 melanoma (N = 20)69 and head and neck carcinoma (N = 20).70

To validate the impact of KLF4 expression on immunotherapy outcome, we extracted tumor cell subsets from pre‑immunotherapy samples after applying a standardized and rigorous single‑cell data processing pipeline. Considering the limited number of immunotherapy samples in certain datasets, we examined KLF4 expression levels and antigen processing and presentation scores across all tumor cells from the corresponding patient samples. Statistical significance was determined by Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

To construct logistic regression models for evaluating the predictive potential of KLF4 across diverse malignancies, we calculated the average gene expression levels per patient (patient‑level pseudobulk) to mitigate cell‑number bias derived from the seven scRNA-seq datasets. Model 1 incorporated the KLF4/MHC class I signature (KLF4, HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C), representing the epithelial antigen-presentation program. Model 2 was based solely on PD-L1 (CD274) expression to demonstrate the superior predictive accuracy of the KLF4/MHC class I signature. We used R package pROC (version 1.18.5) to calculate the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values for each model to compare their discriminative performance.

RNA interference of gene expression

We purchased small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting human CREB1/ATF1/KLF4 and mouse Creb1/Atf1 (Supplementary Table 1) from JTSBIO Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). Briefly, cells were seeded in antibiotic-free medium and transfected with 50 nM siRNA per well using jetPRIME® (Polyplus). All cells were harvested to evaluate the gene silencing efficiency by RT-qPCR or western blot assays after incubation for 48 to 72 h.

Real-time quantitative PCR

We isolated total RNA using the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (ES Science). The RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (TaKaRa). We detected mRNA levels on Quantagene q900 qPCR system (Kubo Technology, Beijing) with TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa). We normalized gene expression levels to β-ACTIN/β-Actin and quantified them via the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. All primers (Supplementary Table 1) were purchased from TSINGKE Biological Technology (Beijing, China).

Western blot assay

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Solarbio, R0010) supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (NCM Biotech) to obtain total protein. Samples (20 μg protein) were resolved by SDS-PAGE and then electrotransferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking with 5% skim milk (Solarbio, LP0033B) for 1.5 h, membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies of anti-KLF4 (ab215036), anti-human MHC class I (ab70328), anti-mouse MHC class I (ab281904), anti-ATF1 (ab181569), anti-Vinculin (ab219649) from Abcam, or anti-CREB (9197) from CST overnight at 4 °C. Following washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Proteintech) for 1.5 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using super sensitive ECL luminescence reagent (MeilunBio) on a chemiluminescence imaging analysis system (Beijing Baygene Biotech). Vinculin was used as the loading control.

Immunoprecipitation assay

Protein lysate was prepared using IP lysis buffer (Thermo-Fisher Scientific) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (NCM Biotech). Immunoprecipitation was performed by incubating 800 μg of lysate with primary antibodies: anti-KLF4, anti-ATF1, anti-CREB or rabbit IgG (Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C. Protein complexes were eluted using Classic Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit (Thermo-Fisher Scientific) and then analyzed by western blotting. To ensure signal specificity, detection was performed using a light chain-specific secondary antibody (Proteintech).

Immunofluorescence analysis

Approximately 60,000 cells were plated onto tissue culture-treated coverslips (Aoqing Biotechnology) in 24-well plates and cultured overnight. After PBS washing, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Aoqing Biotechnology) for 1 h, permeabilized in 0.2% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, T8200) for 30 min and then blocked with goat serum (ZSGB-BIO) for 1 h. Cells were then incubated with primary antibody against KLF4 (sc-393462), ATF1 and CREB overnight at 4 °C. After washing, secondary antibody Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Invitrogen) and tetramethylrhodamine (Invitrogen) were applied for 1 h. DAPI (Invitrogen) counterstaining was applied to visualize nuclei before mounting with antifading mounting medium (Solarbio, S2130). The images were captured on an Olympus FV4000 confocal microscope.

OT-I CD8+ T cell killing assay

We isolated naive CD8+ T cells from spleens of OT-I transgenic mice (Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc.) using the EasySep™ Mouse CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL). The isolated T cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS, 25 mM HEPES (Gibco), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco), 55 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (NCM Biotech). T cell activation was achieved by culturing for 3 days with 5 μg/mL SIINFEKL peptide (MCE) and 10 ng/ mL IL-2 (Proteintech). For cytotoxicity assays, GFP+ tumor cells stably expressing OVAL were seeded and cocultured with activated OT-I CD8+ T cells at an T cell-to-ESCC cell ratio of 4:1 for 24 h. After the incubation, all cells were collected and analyzed via flow cytometry.

Multiplexed immunofluorescence assay

After deparaffinization in two changes of Histo-Clear II (National diagnostics), formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded tissue sections were rehydrated in a graded ethanol series for 2 min each, and then treated with peroxidase blocking solution (ZSGB-BIO) for 20 min. EDTA buffer (ZSGB-BIO) was used for antigen retrieval. Sections were then blocked with goat serum (ZSGB-BIO) for 30 min, followed by incubation with primary antibody against KLF4, human MHC I, mouse MHC I, KRT6A (Proteintech), EpCAM (Proteintech), Melanoma gp100 (ab137078), human CD8a (Proteintech), mouse CD8a (ab217344) or GZMB (ab255598) for 2 h or overnight at 4 °C. Multiplexed fluorescence detection was achieved using the Opal 7-Color Manual IHC Kit (PANOVUE). DAPI (Invitrogen) counterstaining was applied to visualize nuclei before mounting with antifading mounting medium (Solarbio, S2130). Whole-slide imaging was performed via NanoZoomer S60 (HAMAMATSU) and images were analyzed with NDP.view2 software.

Flow cytometry analysis

Tumor cells were detached with trypsin (Gibco) and rinsed twice in PBS. Approximately 1 × 106 cells were suspended in 100 μL staining buffer and incubated with 5 μL of either APC anti-human MHC I (BioLegend) or APC anti-mouse MHC I (BioLegend) antibody for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Following two washes, cells were resuspended in 500 μL staining buffer for flow cytometric acquisition. To analyze T cell effector functions after cocultured with tumor cells, APC anti-mouse CD8α (BioLegend) was surface-stained as described above. Following surface labeling, cells were placed in Fixation Buffer (BioLegend) and then permeabilized with Permeabilization Wash Buffer (BioLegend). For intracellular staining, cells were incubated for 30 min at room temperature in permeabilization buffer with APC/Cyanine7 anti-IFN-γ (BioLegend), BV421 anti-GZMB (BioLegend) or BV711 anti-TNF-α (BioLegend). Cells were then washed twice and resuspended in 500 μL staining buffer for flow cytometric acquisition. For detection of dead tumor cells following coculture with T cells, all cells were collected, washed twice in PBS and resuspended in 500 μL staining buffer. Samples were then stained with 10 μL propidium iodide solution (BioLegend) for 15 min in the dark and analyzed by flow cytometry. For analyzing mouse subcutaneous allograft tumor, fresh samples were mechanically dissociated with sterile scissors in RPMI-1640 medium containing collagenase I (Gibco), collagenase IV (Gibco), and DNase I (Roche). The mixture was then shaken continuously at 37 °C for 30 min to allow enzymatic digestion. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through 100-μm cell strainer (FALCON), subjected to RBC lysis using lysing buffer (BD Bioscience) and washed twice in staining buffer. Approximately 1 × 106 cells were then suspended in 100 μL staining buffer and incubated for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark with PE anti-CD45 (BioLegend), APC anti-MHC I (BioLegend), BV711 anti-CD3e (BD Bioscience), BV421 anti-EpCAM (BD Bioscience) and APC anti-CD8α (BioLegend). Immediately prior to flow cytometry acquisition, 10 μL of 7-AAD (BD Bioscience) was added into each cell suspension for dead cell exclusion. BD LSRFortessa™ X-20 Flow Cytometer (BD Bioscience) was used for sample analysis and FlowJo 10.8.1 software for data analysis.

Statistical analysis

For all statistical analyses, we used R (version 4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.4). Statistical significance was determined by either Student’s t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Specific statistical test used for examining the difference of data in each experiment was detailed in corresponding figure legends.
