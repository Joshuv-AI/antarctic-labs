 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report summarizes key accomplishments from a 2 year Laboratory Directed Research and
Development (LDRD) project focused on advancing nanoscale characterization of metallic particulates
and synthesis of laboratory-scale analogs. We successfully developed and refined nanoscale
crystallography techniques with significantly improved spatial resolution and throughput compared to
traditional transmission electron microscopy. We also established nanotomography capabilities for 3D
particle reconstruction and began optimizing throughput for broader application. Additionally, we
demonstrated the ability to relocate and characterize individual particles using atomic force microscopy
(AFM), providing insights into hardness, conductivity, and magnetic behavior. On the synthesis front, we
leveraged controlled and combustion-based methods to investigate annealing effects and rapid oxidation
behavior of U–Fe–Ni compounds. These efforts collectively enhance our ability to characterize and
interpret particulate formation and transformation under relevant conditions.

 1. BACKGROUND

Particulates are pervasive across fuel cycle operations and can provide valuable insights into conversion
and production activities. For isotopically unperturbed particles, analysis must rely on chemical and
physical characterization—often requiring micron- or submicron-scale spatial resolution. These properties
are governed by the conditions under which the particles formed, offering constraints on the
thermodynamic environment at their origin. Understanding the stability ranges of specific chemical
phases can significantly narrow the parameter space for simulations that aim to reconstruct formation
conditions. Building this foundational knowledge not only clarifies formation mechanisms but also
strengthens attribution efforts, even as operational and environmental contexts become increasingly
complex. Metallic particulates present unique challenges. MicroRaman spectroscopy is widely used for
micron-scale chemical analysis, but it is ineffective for submicron features and metallic phases. In such
cases, characterization beyond mass spectrometry is typically limited to electron microscopy.

This Laboratory Directed Research and Development (LDRD) project was initiated to advance the high-
resolution characterization of metallic particulates and analog synthesis, with the goal of enabling more
robust attribution and supporting future programmatic needs in nuclear security. At the outset of this
project, routine analysis of metallic particulates primarily relied on scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
and mass spectrometry to determine elemental composition and morphology. Occasionally, focused ion
beam (FIB) sectioning and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were used to probe internal structure
and identify crystallographic phases. The following sections describe the development and demonstration
of advanced characterization techniques, as well as novel synthesis approaches. Together, these efforts
represent a significant step forward in the analysis and interpretation of metallic particulates.

 2. ADVANCING NANOSCALE PARTICULATE CHARACTERIZATION

2.1 NANOCRYSTALLOGRAPHY: TRANSMISSION KIKUCHI DIFFRACTION

Crystallography is a powerful tool for understanding particle formation history, but traditional approaches
using TEM are slow, expensive, and limited in spatial coverage. To address these limitations, we explored
the use of transmission Kikuchi diffraction (TKD) for nanoscale crystallography. In TKD, particles are
thinned similarly to TEM preparation but analyzed in a scanning electron microscope (SEM) by
positioning the sample above the electron backscatter diffraction detector. This approach significantly
increases the mapping throughput compared to TEM and improves spatial resolution by approximately an
order of magnitude over conventional electron backscatter diffraction.

This capability has been successfully demonstrated and matured to the point of transition into more
applied projects with high particulate analysis demands. A representative example is shown in Figure 1,
and a comprehensive summary of advanced microscopy techniques is available in [Parish2024].

2.2 NANOTOMOGRAPHY

Conventional electron microscopy provides only 2D views of particles, focusing either on the particle
surface for as-collected particles or the particle interior for FIB-sectioned samples. Although effective for
homogeneous materials, these methods can oversimplify the structure of heterogeneous particles.
Additionally, they do not support volumetric quantification of crystal phases or void content.

To overcome these limitations, we developed nanotomography capabilities that enable 3D reconstruction
of micron-scale particles with nanoscale resolution. This is achieved by serial sectioning with nanometer-
scale steps and reconstructing the slices using Avizo software. The process is optimized for use with a
plasma-FIB system, recently acquired through Institutional Capital Equipment investment, and includes
fiducial marking for accurate alignment.

Nanotomography has been successfully demonstrated on particles from applied projects. Using
backscatter electron image contrast correlated with atomic number, we reconstructed particles to reveal
compositional heterogeneity. For example, one particle exhibited a low-Z amorphous matrix, Ta-rich
crystalline inclusions, and internal voids. This technique enables quantitative assessment of crystal phase
fractions and void content, which are critical for understanding particle transport behavior. More detailed
descriptions are available in [Moseley2025b] and [Parish2025b].

2.3 PARTICLE RELOCATION AND MULTIPARTICLE SECTIONING

Nanocrystallography and nanotomography offer powerful characterization capabilities, but their
throughput is limited by the need to individually locate, extract, and prepare each particle for TEM
analysis. Preparing and analyzing a single particle typically requires 1 day of full-time effort and costs
$4,000–$5,000 per particle. To address this bottleneck, we developed methods for particle relocation and
simultaneous sectioning, initially focusing on 316L steel particulates. Using either a probe tip or

 2

microgripper, particles were repositioned into engineered clusters based on substrate and particle
characteristics. These clusters were then lifted out and sectioned together.

We successfully demonstrated relocation of up to seven particles. This approach significantly reduces
sample handling and registration time, resulting in five times better sectioning throughput and two to
three times better TEM diffraction throughput for 316L steel. Full details are being prepared for
publication [Parish2025b].

2.4 ATOMIC FORCE MICROSCOPY

AFM was evaluated using multiple imaging modes, including magnetic force microscopy, amplitude
modulation–frequency modulation, and phase imaging. Although technically successful, AFM was found
to be too slow, costly, and preparation-intensive for routine particle analysis. Moreover, it primarily
yielded qualitative contrasts between particle regions. As a result, we conclude that AFM is best suited as
a research tool rather than a production-level characterization method. However, its application for 3D
particle sizing is comparatively mature and may offer value in specific contexts. A complete summary can
be found in [Neu2025].

 3. ANALOG SYNTHESIS

A key objective of this LDRD was to mature laboratory-scale synthesis capabilities for uranium-based
alloys, enabling more routine and tunable production of analog materials. These efforts supported both
fundamental understanding and the development of materials relevant to applied programs. Two
complementary synthesis approaches were pursued: controlled annealing and combustion synthesis.

3.1 CONTROLLED ANNEALING

This effort focused on maturing early-stage synthesis capabilities into reliable, repeatable methods for
producing and characterizing uranium-based alloys. Controlled synthesis was demonstrated on several U-
alloy systems, and UNi₂ and UFe₂+Ni are highlighted here as representative examples. The work on both
materials has either been published or is currently undergoing peer review [Brubaker2025a,
Moseley2025a].

In the case of UNi₂, high-quality samples were produced through a carefully optimized combination of
arc melting and annealing. These samples were characterized at the Spallation Neutron Source and using
a physical property measurement system capable of handling radiological materials, which was recently
commissioned as part of an internal investment at ORNL. This combination of advanced synthesis and
characterization enabled direct comparison to computational models and demonstrated excellent
agreement with prior computational predictions.

In the case of UFe₂+Ni, gram-scale ingots were produced via arc melting and subjected to systematic
annealing to investigate the effects of thermal treatment on structural and physical properties. Multiple
annealing protocols were explored to assess their influence on crystal structure, lattice constants,
magnetic behavior, and phase fractions. This study helped establish processing–structure–property
relationships critical for interpreting phase stability and material history.

In both cases, characterization leveraged cross-directorate collaborations, representing a significant step
toward routine, tunable synthesis and characterization of U alloys.

3.2 COMBUSTION SYNTHESIS

To complement controlled synthesis, we extended combustion synthesis techniques from uranium oxide
systems to U-steel compounds, enhancing their relevancy for applied programs. Precursor materials from
the annealing studies were used as inputs, enabling us to target specific compositions and observe their
oxidation behavior under extreme conditions. This capability allows us to identify oxide phases that may
form under rapid or high-temperature formation environments, ultimately informing material history and
degradation pathways.

For example, combustion synthesis targeting U₂NiCrFe₄ resulted in rapid surface oxidation, which was
confirmed by electron microscopy and Raman spectroscopy. Notably, the resulting oxides were
predominantly Fe-based, with no detectable UO₂—an unexpected outcome that highlights the complexity
of multicomponent oxidation behavior. This capability has already informed a tasking proposal for a more
applied project, underscoring its potential to bridge the gap between controlled laboratory-scale synthesis
and operationally relevant experiments. A complete description of this work can be found in
[Brubaker2025b].

 4. CONCLUSIONS

This LDRD successfully advanced several nanoscale characterization and synthesis capabilities critical
for understanding and interpreting metallic particulates. Key achievements include the development of

 4

high-throughput nanocrystallography using TKD, the implementation of nanotomography for 3D particle
reconstruction, and the demonstration of AFM modalities for physical property mapping—each
contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of particle formation and behavior.

To address the throughput limitations of advanced characterization techniques, we developed and
demonstrated particle relocation and multiparticle sectioning workflows. These methods enable
simultaneous preparation and analysis of multiple particles, significantly reducing time and cost per
sample. For 316L steel, we achieved five times better sectioning throughput and two to three times
electron diffraction throughput. These capabilities are essential for scaling high-resolution analysis to
meet the demands of applied programs.

In parallel, analog synthesis capabilities were significantly matured. Controlled synthesis methods now
enable the production of high-fidelity U-alloy samples, and combustion synthesis provides a flexible
platform for studying oxidation behavior under extreme conditions. These capabilities are already
informing follow-on proposals and are positioned for integration into more applied programs.

Collectively, these efforts represent a substantial step forward in the ability to characterize, interpret, and
replicate the formation conditions of metallic particulates. The tools and workflows developed under this
project are now poised to support broader mission needs in nonproliferation, forensics, and materials
attribution.
