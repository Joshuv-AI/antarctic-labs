Mechanism of persister formation in response to nitrogen starvation
Authors: Raleb Taher, Sanika Vaidya, Pankaj Patil, Mi-Hyun Lee, Bryson A. Hawkins, Nandini Agrawal, Aanchal A. Srivastava, Kim Lewis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial resistance to antibiotics is a major problem, but most chronic infections that are recalcitrant to therapy are caused by drug-susceptible pathogens. This paradox is explained by the presence of a small population of quiescent persister cells that are tolerant of killing by antibiotics. Persisters typically have a decreased level of ATP, and diminished activity of antibiotic targets. This leads to persister tolerance of antibiotics that kill by corrupting their targets. We reasoned that starvation for nutrients that do not affect the energy level may represent an additional general mechanism of persister formation. In a culture of E. coli experiencing nitrogen starvation, persister levels sharply rise without a change in ATP. Here we show that PyrBI, a key enzyme in nitrogen assimilation and de novo nucleotide synthesis, is largely responsible for persister formation under nitrogen-limiting conditions. Tracking individual cells in a mother machine shows that persisters have low levels of PyrBI. Similarly, PyrBI is low in persisters present in a biofilm. Overexpression of PyrBI quenches the noise in its expression, decreasing persistence. Starvation for other essential nutrients could similarly induce persistence, with stochastic fluctuation in persister gene expression serving as a general mechanism of persister formation.

Introduction

Bacterial populations contain persister cells that are difficult to eradicate by antibiotics and are largely responsible for recalcitrance of chronic infections1–5. We proposed that tolerance of persisters results from inactivity of antibiotic targets6,7. Bactericidal antibiotics kill not by inhibiting their targets, but by corrupting them8. Aminoglycosides cause mistranslation, producing aberrant toxic peptides with multiple amino acid replacements9–11. Fluoroquinolones such as ciprofloxacin inhibit DNA religation, converting DNA gyrase and topoisomerase into endonucleases12–14. Cell-wall-acting antibiotics inhibit peptidoglycan synthesis, which causes cell lysis, although the precise mechanism of killing by this type of compound is still unclear15. Cells with diminished activity of targets will be tolerant to antibiotic killing. However, persisters are not totally inert as they maintain essential molecular functions necessary to support regrowth upon antibiotic removal16,17.

The mechanistic understanding of persister formation is advancing, with target shutdown as the main, if not the only, cause of antibiotic tolerance. There also appears to be a common reason for target shutdown: loss of available energy effected by a variety of mechanisms. A population of cells entering stationary phase experiences nutrient starvation and a drop in ATP18. Tolerance of antibiotics increases for the bulk of a stationary population, but some antibiotics, such as fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides, are still able to kill the majority of these non-growing cells, leaving a fraction of surviving persisters. What makes targets in persisters less active than in the rest of the population is an intriguing question that has attracted considerable attention.

In Escherichia coli experiencing DNA damage, the SOS response induces repair systems, but also expression of the TisB toxin that forms an ion channel, dissipating the proton motive force and ATP19,20. This leads to the formation of persisters21. This specialized mechanism pointed towards ATP depletion as a likely general cause of persister formation. We subsequently showed that stochastic fluctuation in the levels of expression of Krebs cycle enzymes causes persister formation in Staphylococcus aureus and E. coli18,22,23. Apart from stochastic processes, two recent studies describe direct inhibition of Krebs cycle enzymes in vivo resulting in antibiotic tolerance. Macrophages produce toxic reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS, RNS) that poison the Fe-S-containing aconitase of phagocytosed S. aureus, blocking the Krebs cycle and generating persisters24. Similarly, RNS poison the Krebs cycle in intracellular Salmonella, sharply increasing the population of persisters25. An unexpected case of energy depletion comes from a study of nutrient starvation in Bacillus subtilis26. Starved cells accumulate the alarmone pp(p)Gpp that orchestrates a shift from division to survival, but this also leads to a marked depletion of GTP from which it is formed. GTP depletion produces persisters, most likely due to its requirement for protein synthesis. This is an interesting case of energy depletion, but how widespread this mechanism may be is unclear—in S. aureus, deletion of the (p)ppGpp synthetases has no effect on the level of persisters18. Another interesting link to persisters involves protein aggregates. Typically formed from denatured proteins, aggregates are present in a small subpopulation of cells, and were shown to precede formation of persisters27. Disaggregation precedes the exit of persisters from dormancy. While many different types of proteins may be present in aggregates, the first to assemble are energy-producing, such as Krebs cycle enzymes28. One possibility is that a stochastic decrease in an energy-producing enzyme will cause a drop in ATP, diminishing activity of ATP-dependent chaperones, leading to protein misfolding and aggregation. Sequestering energy-producing proteins in aggregates will prevent their renaturation, exacerbating energy depletion and the entrance of a cell into a dormant state.

We reason that apart from a decreased energy level, there may be another general mechanism of persister formation causing target shutdown – depletion of essential nutrients such as nitrogen. Indeed, nitrogen starvation is not expected to affect energy levels, but diminishes available precursors of the major biosynthetic pathways – protein, DNA, and peptidoglycan synthesis that will result in a decrease of target activity. We show that persisters in a cell population experiencing nitrogen starvation have low levels of PyrBI, a key enzyme in the pathway leading from ammonium to amino acids and nucleotides. Stochastic variation in expression of PyrBI, recorded at a single cell level, underlies persister formation.

Results

Nitrogen depletion leads to formation of multi-drug tolerant persisters

To test the impact of nitrogen starvation on persister cell formation, we inoculated E. coli into a minimal medium with glucose and low nitrogen, M9n (Fig. 1a). After 3 h, the low NH4 culture clearly deviated from exponential growth (Fig. 1b), indicating that nitrogen is being depleted. This culture experiencing N limitation was then treated with ciprofloxacin, and time-dependent killing was monitored by CFU count (Fig. 1c). Ciprofloxacin exposure results in biphasic killing, highlighting the subpopulation of persister cells surviving antibiotic treatment. The surviving persister fraction in M9n was 100-fold higher as compared to that in M9N medium (Fig. 1c). Death from ciprofloxacin continues due to accumulated damage after cells are plated (post-antibiotic effect). It was suggested that slow growth on poor medium favors repair, while rapid growth of cells plated on rich medium will diminish repair and may affect accuracy of survival measurements29. We therefore tested recovery by plating cells after exposure to ciprofloxacin on rich LB medium and minimal M9 medium and observed no difference in CFU counts (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Time-dependent killing by meropenem was also biphasic, with a 4-log higher survival of the nitrogen-starved E. coli (Fig. 1d). To determine the kinetics of persister formation in nitrogen-starved conditions, we assayed antibiotic tolerance of cultures grown in M9N or M9n at hourly intervals following media switch. After 4 h of ciprofloxacin or meropenem exposure, the survival was assessed by CFU count (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Ciprofloxacin exposure results in a sharp increase in the persister population (100-fold) after 3 h of growth in nitrogen-limited conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Similarly, the meropenem tolerant population was increased by 3-logs after 2 h of growth in M9n (Supplementary Fig. 2d), suggesting that the low amount of NH4 present in the M9n medium gets assimilated by growing bacteria while the remaining non-growing population turns into persisters. Previous studies linked multidrug tolerance to diminished activity of antibiotic targets caused by a reduced energy level. To assess the impact of nitrogen starvation on bacterial energy sources, we compared ATP concentration in bulk population grown in nitrogen-rich or nitrogen-limited conditions using the firefly luciferase assay (Fig. 1e). Despite having reduced nitrogen access, ATP concentration was similar in M9N and M9n cultures. This is not surprising since a carbon source remains saturating under nitrogen starvation conditions. In order to examine the effect of nitrogen starvation on target activity, we measured the rate of DNA replication that determines the activity of DNA gyrase and topoisomerase, the targets of ciprofloxacin; and peptidoglycan synthesis, targeted by meropenem, under nitrogen starvation. We used 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU), a thymidine analog, which incorporates into bacterial DNA, allowing to quantify DNA synthesis using flow cytometry. As the culture deviates from exponential in M9n medium, DNA synthesis was reduced (Fig. 1f,g). Similarly, we measured incorporation of fluorescently labeled peptidoglycan (PG) precursor (TAMRA-3-amino-D-alanine) to monitor PG synthesis. Upon nitrogen starvation, there was a 40% decrease in TAMRA-3-amino-D-alanine incorporation into E. coli compared to nitrogen-enriched conditions (Fig. 1h,i). These results suggest that upon nitrogen depletion, a decrease in DNA synthesis and PG synthesis underlie tolerance to fluoroquinolones and beta-lactams.

E. coli was reported to accumulate (p)ppGpp under conditions of nitrogen starvation30,31, which could contribute to persister formation. In that study, cells exhausted nitrogen and entered into stationary phase, upon which (p)ppGpp levels increased32. In order to clarify whether (p)ppGpp increases due to nitrogen starvation or because cells enter into stationary phase, we monitored the stringent response using a fluorescent (p)ppGpp-dependent riboswitch sensor pUC-RbSW-mCha in which mChartreuse fluorescence increases upon binding of (p)ppGpp (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Induction of stringent response by serine hydroxamate (SHX) increased fluorescence, serving as a positive control. We also confirmed the specificity of pUC-RbSW-mCha reporter in MG1655 ΔrelAΔspoT ((p)ppGpp0) strain, unable to synthesize (p)ppGpp. No fluorescence accumulation was detected in (p)ppGpp0-pUC-RbSW-mCha strain in M9N including upon SHX-induced stringent response (Supplementary Fig. 3b,c). This confirmed the specificity of pUC-RbSW-mCha reporter for (p)ppGpp, although no quantification by thin layer chromatography (TLC) was performed. In M9n medium (p)ppGpp levels remained the same over 5 h in a culture growing in M9n medium. Note that the level of persisters increases sharply in M9n medium after 3 h of growth (Fig. 1c,d). This suggests that (p)ppGpp does not contribute to persister formation under these conditions.

Stochastic fluctuation in the levels of expression of a critical factor likely causes persister formation. For example, cells with high levels of expression of the HipA toxin are tolerant of antibiotics33, as are cells with low levels of expression of Krebs cycle enzymes23,34. We therefore sought to identify the critical components whose fluctuation in expression may determine formation of persisters in a nitrogen limited cell population. We reasoned that such persister genes are likely to be found among those whose expression is most affected by nitrogen starvation. We therefore performed a transcriptome analysis of E. coli responding to nitrogen starvation. An equivalent of 4 × 108 bacteria were collected before (N) and 5 h after nitrogen starvation (n), and total RNA was isolated and sequenced. Analysis of cells at N versus n growth conditions revealed 696 down- and 833 up-regulated genes (Fig. 2a, and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Differentially expressed genes were then compared to the ones obtained in published transcriptomic studies of nitrogen-depleted E. coli30,35,36. Experimental conditions and the E. coli strain in our study were somewhat different, but there was a considerable overlap, especially with the genes whose changes were particularly distinct. Among the common genes, 519 were downregulated, and 256 upregulated (Fig. 2b,c, and Supplementary Data 4 and 5). Gene ontology analysis indicates that E. coli accelerates catabolism (Fig. 2d, left panel) and reduces biosynthesis (Fig. 2d, right panel) of nitrogen-rich metabolites (purine, pyrimidine, arginine and histidine) in response to nitrogen starvation. The NH4+ transporter AmtB was highly expressed in response to nitrogen starvation. Carbamoyl-phosphate synthetase (carAB) and aspartate carbamoyltransferase (pyrBI) expression were the most reduced in nitrogen-depleted E. coli (Fig. 2b, and Supplementary Fig. 4a). CarAB and PyrBI are both involved in the histidine, purine and pyrimidine synthesis super pathway (Supplementary Fig. 4c), making them key metabolic effectors of nitrogen metabolism. Notably, pyrBI and carAB were among the most downregulated genes in both our study and in that of Switzer and co-authors. We next tested the possible involvement of these differentially expressed genes, amtB, glnA, carAB and pyrBI, in persister formation. Overexpression of a gene quenches the noise in its expression and will abolish its ability to control persister formation37. Therefore, we cloned glnA, amtB, carAB and pyrBI into a low-copy pMMB67EH vector under the control of anhydrotetracycline-inducible tetR. Overexpression of pyrBI in nitrogen-limiting medium resulted in a sharp 1.5-log decrease in persisters surviving treatment with ciprofloxacin, while overexpressing glnA or carAB had no effect (Fig. 2e, and Supplementary Fig. 5a). Overexpression of pyrBI also increased bacterial killing by meropenem in nitrogen-starved conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Overexpression of amtB led to a distinct 1-log decrease in persisters but also partially impaired growth (Supplementary Fig. 6). This is fairly typical for overexpressing membrane proteins. In contrast, induced expression of pyrBI, glnA and carAB by Atet did not affect growth of the recombinant strains. PyrBI (aspartate transcarbamoylase, ATCase) is required for nitrogen assimilation; overproduction quenches the noise in its expression, decreasing persisters under conditions of N starvation; individual persister cells have low levels of pyrBI expression. These observations point to pyrBI as a persister gene. To test for causality more directly, we took advantage of a well-characterized ATCase inhibitor, N-(phosphonacetyl)-L-aspartate (PALA). PALA is a non-hydrolysable transition-state analog that binds specifically to the catalytic subunit of PyrBI and blocks pyrimidine synthesis38–40 (Supplementary Fig. 7a). We determined that PALA diminishes cell density upon entering stationary phase in nitrogen-rich medium. Inversely, PALA exposure did not impair growth in LB medium, a condition in which exogenous nucleotides render PyrBI activity non-essential (Supplementary Fig. 7b,c). Adding PALA to a growing culture in M9N sharply increased the level of persisters surviving treatment with meropenem or ciprofloxacin, recapitulating the phenotype observed in nitrogen-starved conditions (Supplementary Fig. 7c,d). This means that inhibiting ATCase is directly causal to persister formation in this model experiment. Since ATCase levels are diminished in persisters, we can establish causality between this enzyme and persistence.

Noise quenching experiments suggest that stochastic variation in expression produces rare cells with low levels of PyrBI that become persisters. We then used single-cell analysis to directly test this hypothesis. To monitor the expression of proteins, we first cloned pyrBI translational fusion with mVenus and replaced it with the native alleles. However, the fluorescence was too low, and we therefore cloned these gene fusions in low-copy plasmids and transformed them into E. coli DE32 constitutively expressing mKate. The resulting PyrBI-mVenus reporter displayed foci formation upon increased PyrBI expression (Supplementary Fig. 7a). It is known that mVenus dimerizes when fused with multimeric proteins, causing foci formation41. In E. coli, aspartate carbamoyltransferase assembles in two PyrB trimers associated with three dimers of PyrI, positioning the C-terminal domains of PyrI dimers in proximity and favoring mVenus dimerization42. We therefore cloned pyrBI fused to mChartreuse (Supplementary Fig. 7b), a fluorophore engineered for improved monomericity43. We then tested PyrBI-mChartreuse for functionality. Since PyrB is essential for growth in minimal media, functionality of the reporter was validated by complementation of the E. coli BW25113 ΔΔpyrB strain. Plasmid-borne pyrBI-mChartreuse enabled growth of the E. coli ΔΔpyrB strain in M9N (Supplementary Fig. 7c).

To detect PyrBI levels in individual cells, we used a mother machine, a microfluidics device containing micro-trenches connected to a main feeding channel allowing constant flow and control of media23,44,45. After loading the device with a cell suspension, bacteria at the dead end of the trenches become “mother cells” whose progeny is pushed down the trenches (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Time-lapse microscopy then allows tracking of a large number of individual cells. Tracking the mKate signal allowed to identify single cells in trenches from which the intensity of pyrBI-mChartreuse can be extracted. DE32 pyrBI-mChartreuse cells in M9N medium were loaded into the mother machine and incubated for 3 h, after which the medium was switched to M9n for another 3 h. Cells were then exposed to meropenem at 8 µg/ml for 4 h (or ciprofloxacin at 5 µg/mL for 3 h) in M9n medium, after which the antibiotic was replaced with M9N medium, and bacteria were allowed to recover for 15 h. Fluorescence of cells in 3,000 trenches (10-15 cells per trench) was imaged every hour (Fig. 3a,b). Meropenem treatment caused lysis of regular cells in a span of 1 to 7 h (Fig. 3a right panel, white circles), leaving 0.02% of rare persister cells (Fig. 3a left panel, blue circles), which started dividing after removal of the antibiotic. Division of a persister cell can be seen 9 h following antibiotic wash-out, producing the first daughter cell (Fig. 3a, left panel, orange circles). The majority of cells exposed to ciprofloxacin elongated, a consequence of SOS-stress response, and either lysed (Fig. 3b, left panel, green circles) or died without lysis (Fig. 3b, right panel, white circles), since they were not able to propagate in fresh medium (Fig. 3b, right panel). Rare persister cells, making up 0.02% of the population, were able to divide after the antibiotic was washed away (Fig. 3b, left panel, blue circles). Mother cell division was observed 9 h after the end of antibiotic exposure and produced the first daughter cell (Fig. 3b, left panel, orange circles). A second division happened 3 h later, and a newly produced daughter cell pushed down the first daughter cell (Fig. 3b, left panel, purple circles). Subsequent divisions occurred every next hour, with the first daughter cell exiting from the trench by the end of the experiment. Looking back to time zero, these cells were not growing and did not elongate after antibiotic exposure (Supplementary Fig. 10). Strikingly, persisters had a 2.5-fold lower level of PyrBI-mChartreuse expression as compared to regular cells (Fig. 3c,d). Upon recovery in M9N medium, expression of PyrBI-mChartreuse in persisters sharply increased to the level of regular cells. Notably, 3 additional persister cells displayed PyrBI-mChartreuse puncti formation. (Supplementary Fig. 10). Upon recovery from meropenem exposure, the puncti resolved while PyrBI-mChartreuse expression increased as mother cells resumed divisions (Supplementary Fig. 11). Aggregate formation has been previously described in persisters, and resolving aggregates preceded their resuscitation27,28,46. It appears that PyrBI aggregate formation is associated with a small fraction of persisters under conditions of nitrogen limitation.

Nitrogen starvation induces formation of biofilms containing drug-tolerant persisters

Biofilm formation is an efficient strategy that various bacteria use to adapt to a hostile environment and persist in the presence of antibiotics47. We sought to determine whether PyrBI-dependent persisters are present in biofilms formed under nitrogen starvation. For this, E. coli biofilms were formed in microfluidic devices. To do so, we used a 0.35 mm3 (height = 0.1 mm, width = 0.5 mm, and length = 7 mm) microfluidic chamber connected to an inlet and an outlet to allow for constant slow medium flow. E. coli cells expressing a translational fusion of PyrBI-mVenus, along with constitutive mKate expression, were inoculated in microfluidic chambers with constant flow of M9N or M9n medium for 5 days. Using confocal microscopy, we observed that biofilms grown in M9N are flat (4–8 µm height on average), while biofilms grown in M9n have a characteristic polyp three-dimensional (3D) shape (20 µm height on average) (Fig. 4a, and Supplementary Fig. 12). During nutrient depletion stress, E. coli produces matrix components, which provide a scaffold for biofilms to expand outwards and upwards, creating 3D structures with distinct cellular architecture48. Biofilm cells in M9N have constant access to a nitrogen source and have more uniform cellular PyrBI-mVenus levels compared to the biofilm formed in M9n (Fig. 4c,d bottom). This was confirmed by the higher average PyrBI-mVenus signal per biovolume quantified in biofilms grown in M9N compared to M9n (Fig. 4e,f). PyrBI-mVenus expression is inherently heterogenous in planktonic cultures, and this cell-to-cell variability is further amplified in the biofilm environment (Supplementary Fig. 13). In M9n, PyrBI levels were high in the biofilm core, decreasing towards the periphery (Fig. 4b,d top). Given that pyrBI expression decreases in nitrogen-limiting conditions, it was surprising to see increased PyrBI levels in the biofilm interior where cells are expected to be nitrogen-starved. However, a similar pattern was observed in a previous study that reported increased expression of pyrB and pyrI in the biofilm interior compared to the periphery, using spatial transcriptomics49. Nitrogen-rich metabolites such as pyrimidine and arginine were enriched in the biofilm interior compared to periphery50. According to these studies, the biofilm periphery assimilates available nitrogen from the media into arginine, which is supplied to the interior cells as a nitrogen source. We next examined the tolerance of nitrogen-starved bacteria to ciprofloxacin in biofilms grown in M9N or M9n. Biofilms were formed by E. coli pyrBI-mVenus, and stained with impermeable ethidium homodimer III (EthD III) that binds to nucleic acids and reports cell death51. Five-day-old biofilms grown in M9n were treated with ciprofloxacin and EthD III for 24 h. Dead cells stained with EthD III represented 2% of the population of the biofilm. Notably, cells stained with EthD III were located in the core of the biofilm, and had high levels of PyrBI-mVenus fluorescence (Fig. 4g–j). This suggests that cells actively synthesizing pyrimidines remain susceptible to ciprofloxacin, while bacteria at the periphery with low levels of PyrBI expression are tolerant of antibiotic killing. These results are consistent with the noise quenching and mother machine experiments, showing that PyrBI is largely responsible for persister formation in cells experiencing nitrogen starvation.

Discussion

Persister formation is governed by both specific and general mechanisms. A low-energy state, arrived through various pathways, represents the known general mechanism of persistence. Here we show that depletion of essential biosynthetic pathways under nitrogen starvation drives target shutdown and persistence without a drop in ATP levels. Our study adds to the existing paradigm, suggesting that there are two general mechanisms of persister formation – a decrease in the energy level and a decrease in essential nutrients. Ultimately, tolerance of bactericidal antibiotics stems from diminished activity of their targets52. All known, and yet to be discovered, mechanisms of tolerance likely fall within this general principle. At the same time, resistance has been shown to contribute to persister survival and viewed as a tolerance mechanism. We suggest that the terms “resistance” and “tolerance” should be based on mechanism, not on the type of cells they operate in. Tolerance describes mechanisms that lead to dormancy and diminished target activity, while resistance prevents an antibiotic from engaging its target. For example, efflux, a classical resistance mechanism, helps persisters survive53,54, which is not surprising, since persisters are slowly killed by antibiotics, and any mechanism that decreases their engagement with the target will aid survival. A less obvious case is that of DNA repair (and repair in general) increasing persister survival55. Cells with diminished repair, such as recA mutants, have a decreased fluoroquinolone minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), thus belonging to resistance56,57. Repair will facilitate exit from dormancy, but not entrance into dormancy (tolerance). We therefore conclude that repair belongs to resistance, not tolerance. The conclusion from this analysis is that persisters benefit mainly from antibiotic tolerance stemming from dormancy and diminished activity of targets, while their survival can be aided by resistance and repair.

Investigating the mechanism of nitrogen limitation uncovered PyrBI as the main persister gene. PyrBI (ATCase) is the second enzyme in the pyrimidine synthesis pathway, catalyzing the condensation of carbamoyl phosphate into N-carbamoyl-L-aspartate (Supplementary Fig. 3d). pyrBI is one of the most highly downregulated loci under nitrogen starvation; ectopic overexpression of PyrBI sharply reduces persisters (noise quenching), and single-cell analysis shows that PyrBI expression is low in persisters. At the same time, CarAB (carbamoyl-phosphate synthetase), the first enzyme in the pathway, is also highly downregulated under N starvation. However, overexpression of CarAB has no effect on persistence. This makes sense upon considering the kinetics of the two reactions. The affinity of PyrBI to carbamoyl-phosphate is 86-fold lower as compared to the affinity of CarAB to glutamine. This shows that PyrBI is the rate-limiting step in pyrimidine synthesis (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Noise in the expression of pyrBI will then produce rare cells with an impaired ability to produce pyrimidines. This in turn will prevent DNA replication. Reduced DNA synthesis leads to decreased activity of gyrase and topoisomerase IV, targets of fluoroquinolones58,59, which will result in tolerance. In turn, cell elongation and septal formation depend on completion of DNA replication60–62. Without cell elongation, synthesis of peptidoglycan and activity of transglycolyases, targets of β-lactam antibiotics, will diminish. Indeed, persisters formed during N starvation are non-growing and tolerant of β-lactams.

Noise in expression of persister genes is a likely general underlying principle of persister formation. Notably, even when persistence is driven by specialized persister proteins such as TisB or HipA, only part of the population becomes persistent. HipA-derived persisters in particular are cells with higher levels of hipA expression33. In this study, we show that noise quenching by overexpressing pyrBI sharply decreases persistence. Noise quenching was also shown to diminish persistence driven by components of energy-generating metabolic pathways ß-galactosidase (LacZ) in E. coli, or acetate kinase AckA of Mycobacterium tuberculosis in the acetate catabolism pathway37,63. However, to our knowledge, a “noise generator” has not been identified for any persister gene. The heterogenous expression of pyrBI observed in nitrogen-starved persisters may stem from a transcription factor acting as a noise generator, introducing bursty, stochastic gene expression in bacteria64–66. Identifying the specific transcription factor responsible for pyrBI expression heterogeneity in this context will require further investigation.

Biofilms represent an environment where nitrogen-starved persisters are likely to be particularly relevant. Abundant carbon is required to build the extracellular matrix, while access to nitrogen in the environment is typically limited. Studies on E. coli biofilms show that environments with a high carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, where nitrogen is limiting, lead to increased production of extracellular polysaccharides48,67,68. This is consistent with our observation that nitrogen limitation enhances biofilm formation.

The level of persisters increases sharply when nutrients become limiting, and a growing culture deviates from strictly exponential growth. The main driver of persister formation will likely depend on the nature of the limiting growth factor. If this is carbon or oxygen, then persistence will be determined by cells with low levels of expression of energy-generating components. If nitrogen is limiting, then persisters will be formed by cells with low levels of PyrBI. Similarly, persisters are likely to be formed if other essential elements such as S and P are limiting, but the genes controlling their formation are yet to be discovered.

Methods

Bacterial strains and media

E. coli MG1655 and derived strains were cultivated in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth (Fisher Scientific), M9 minimal medium N (Na2HPO4 42 mM, KH2PO4 22 mM, NaCl 8.5 mM, NH4Cl 9.35 mM) or M9 minimal medium n (Na2HPO4 42 mM, KH2PO4 22 mM, NaCl 8.5 mM, NH4Cl 0.935 mM) supplemented with 0.4% glucose, 2 mM MgSO4 and 100 µM CaCl2. Bacteria were incubated aerobically at 37 °C with 220 rpm shaking. Strains carrying pMMB67EH-based plasmids were grown in media containing 50 μg/mL carbenicillin for plasmid maintenance. Expression of Anhydrotetracycline-dependent promoters was induced by 200 nM of anhydrotetracycline. E. coli DE2 used for microfluidics experiments is an MG1655 derivative built by motA deletion and insertion of mKate2 under control of a synthetic promoter (pRNA1) in CPS-53 prophage site (lambda-red recombination). E. coli MG1655 ΔrelAΔspoT ((p)ppGpp0) was constructed by successive phage transductions of ΔrelA and ΔspoT alleles from the KEIO collection into MG1655 background22.

Plasmid construction

All plasmid constructs were generated through Gibson assembly (New England Biolabs) fragment end-joining following established protocols, utilizing commercially available enzymes for DNA amplification (New England Biolabs). Primer pairs (Supplementary Data 2) were designed using SnapGene v7.02 (Insightful Science) and synthesized by IDT DNA or Genewiz. Assembled plasmids were transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells (New England Biolabs), with clones validated via Sanger sequencing (Plasmidsaurus).

Nitrogen starvation

E. coli MG1655 and derivative strains were cultured in 50 mL of M9 minimal medium N (NH4Cl 9.35 mM) at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm until mid-exponential phase (OD600nm = 0.2). To induce nitrogen starvation, cells were harvested by centrifugation (8500 × g, 10 min, room temperature), washed twice with M9 medium n (identical composition but NH4Cl 0.935 mM), and resuspended in M9 medium n at the original cell density. Cultures were then incubated at 37 °C with shaking for the desired starvation period. Growth was monitored by measuring OD600nm. All experiments were performed with biological triplicates, and appropriate controls included cultures maintained in complete M9 medium throughout.

Survival assays

Nitrogen-rich or starved cultures were incubated for 3 h at 37 °C with 220 rpm shaking and 4 mL were aliquoted into 14 mL conical tubes (VWR International). Antibiotic challenges included ciprofloxacin (5 μg/mL, 10× MIC) or meropenem (8 μg/mL, 10× MIC) before incubation at 37 °C with shaking. Survivors were quantified by pelleting 100 μL aliquots (4000 × g, RT, 5 min) at different time points, washing twice in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), performing tenfold serial dilutions, and plating on antibiotic-free LB or M9 agar supplemented with glucose, MgSO4 and CaCl2. Colonies were enumerated after 18 h incubation (37 °C), with survival frequencies calculated as (post-treatment CFU)/(initial CFU) × 100.

ATP quantification of bulk cultures

Intracellular ATP levels were quantified using the BacTiter-Glo™ Microbial Cell Viability Assay (Promega) following manufacturer protocols. Briefly, 100 μL aliquots of nitrogen-rich or starved cultures incubated for 3, 5 or 24 h in M9N or M9n were mixed at a 1:1 ratio with reconstituted reagent in white-walled 96-well plates (Corning). Luminescence was measured after 5 min incubation at 22 °C using a Biotek microplate reader (Agilent BioTek Synergy H1 multimode reader) with 1 s integration time. ATP standards (0.1–10 μM) provided calibration curves. Results are products of duplicates from three independent experiments with data normalized to OD600nm.

Flow cytometry analysis of peptidoglycan and DNA synthesis

Cultures of nitrogen-rich or starved E. coli were pre-incubated with 50 µM of TAMRA-3-amino-D-alanine (Tocris) for 1 h before the determined timepoints. 1 mL aliquots were pelleted (4000 × g, RT, 5 min) and resuspended in PBS before flow cytometry analysis. Similarly, 1 mL aliquots of cultures grown for 1 h 30 min and 2 h 30 min in nitrogen-limited and rich cultures were pre-incubated with 20 µg/mL of 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) for 30 min before fixation with 0.4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilization with 0.1% TritonX-100 for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were then washed with PBS and EdU incorporation was detected using the Click-iT EdU Flow Cytometry Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After a final wash, the cells were resuspended in PBS before flow cytometry analysis. The fluorescence intensity of cells was analyzed using a FACS ARIAII flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Cells were passed through a 70μm nozzle and detected by forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) parameters. TADA and EdU were detected with bandpass filters of 530/30 (PE). Data were analyzed with FlowJo 10 software.

RNA seq analysis

E. coli MG1655 cultures were subjected to nitrogen starvation as described, with duplicate samples harvested at 0 (N+ control) or 5 h (n) post-media switch for transcriptomic analysis. We commissioned RNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing to SeqCenter and retrieved raw sequences. Using the Kbase online platform, reads were trimmed (Trimmomatic v0.39), aligned to the E. coli MG1655 genome (NCBI ASM584v2) using STAR v2.7.10b, and quantified via featureCounts v2.0.3. Differential expression analysis (DESeq2 v1.38.3) identified genes with log2(fold change) ≥1 and adjusted pvalue < 0.05 (FDR correction). Differentially expressed genes were compared to nitrogen-starved cultures from Switzer et al.35 defining a list of 256 upregulated and 519 commonly downregulated genes. Gene ontology analysis using ShinyGO v0.741 (South Dakota State University). The RNA-seq data can be accessed under the accession number GSE300493 in the Gene Expression Omnibus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/).

Microfluidic chip fabrication

Mother machine chips were fabricated by mixing Sylgard™ 184 silicone elastomer base and curing agent (10:1 w/w ratio), followed by vacuum degassing (3 h) to remove bubbles before pouring onto silicon wafers featuring micron-scale growth channels. Curing at 65 °C for 3 h produced polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) casts that were peeled from wafers, with individual chips cut using a laser-guided template. Inlet/outlet ports (0.75 mm diameter) were punched using sterile biopsy punches, after which chips underwent sequential cleaning via 30 min isopropanol sonication, air-jet drying, and 65 °C annealing (3 h). Cleaned glass coverslips (length = 40 mm, width 24 mm thickness = 0.17 mm) were plasma-bonded (Harrick Plasma, 50 W, 170 mTorr, 60 s) to PDMS chips under optical alignment, followed by 65 °C post-bake (overnight) to strengthen adhesion.

PDMS chips were fabricated via soft lithography using SU-8 master molds (kindly provided by Johan Paulsson, Harvard Medical School) designed with 1024 parallel growth channels (channel length = 25 µm, channel width = 1.5 µm, channel height = 1.28 µm) interconnected by 150 μm-wide nutrient delivery channels.

Microfluidic chip loading

E. coli DE2-pyrBI-mVenus from overnight M9N (with 50 µg/mL carbenicillin) cultures supplemented with F108 surfactant (0.08%) were loaded via injection and the chips were centrifuged at 0.5 × g for 5 min to ensure single-cell occupancy of microchannels. Prior to imaging, loaded chips were connected to a peristaltic pump (Pharmacia Biotech) and a bubble trap. Flowing M9N medium at 1 μL/min for 5 min allowed to wash away leftover cultures from the loading step. Continuous flow was maintained at 1 µL/h for the entirety of the experiment. Media switch to M9n or M9n supplemented with antibiotics was performed by closing the circuit before purge of remaining media in the bubble trap.

Time-lapse microscopy of microfluidics chips

Loaded microfluidics chips were placed on a stage adapter of a Nikon Ti2-E automatic stage for rapid scanning of 40 fields of view per timepoint, and frames were captured (Apochromat 40× air objective) every hour for 23 h. Focal drift from the Nikon Perfect Focus system was used to eliminate minor thermal fluctuations. The setup was housed in a temperature-controlled incubator (OKO lab) set at 37 °C for the entirety of the experiment. mKate2 constitutive reporter and mVenus reporter fluorescence were captured using the mCherry filter (excitation at 575 nm for 300 ms) and YFP filter (excitation at 511 nm for 600 ms), respectively. Using Nikon Elements software, ND2 files were converted into individual TIFF files prior to image analysis.

Image analysis pipeline of microfluidics chips experiments

A computational framework processed time-lapse TIFF files to reconstruct single-cell lineages, employing a three-stage workflow adapted from prior methodology44: Cell segmentation based on mKate2 fluorescence generating a binary mask of single cells using the watershed mark algorithm implemented in FIJI; Fluorescence quantification by integrating pixel intensities within segmented regions across channels to extract mKate2 and mVenus/mChartreuse intensities; Trace curation removing artifacts through size, keeping only objects from 0.5 to 5 μm. This pipeline was used to quantify mVenus fluorescence of persisters and non-persisters.

Flow chamber fabrication

E. coli biofilms were grown in microfluidic flow chambers, adapted from prior methodology69. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) chips were plasma-bonded to ethanol-cleaned glass coverslips using oxygen plasma treatment (50 W, 30 s), creating flow chambers with standardized dimensions of 7 mm (length) × 0.5 mm (width) × 0.1 mm (height). Each chip incorporated four or eight parallel fluidic channels of identical geometry, with individual inlet/outlet ports (1.5 mm diameter) to enable independent experimental conditions per channel. Channel architecture was replicated via soft lithography from custom-made wafers (Atrandi Biosciences), similar to the microfluidics chips described previously.

Flow chamber biofilm experiments

E. coli DE2-pyrBI-mVenus or MG1655-pyrBI-mVenus cells were grown in M9N supplemented with carbenicillin (50 µg/mL) at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm overnight. The cells were washed with PBS, diluted 1:10 in fresh M9N or M9n media supplemented with carbenicillin (50 µg/mL), and inoculated into the flow channels. Syringes containing M9N or M9n were connected to the inlets of the channels using polyethylene tubing, with separate tubing connected to the outlets for waste collection. Following inoculation, the cells were given 1 h to attach to the glass surface of the chambers at room temperature, before a flow of 6 mL/h was initiated for 45 s through each channel to wash away non-adherent cells. The flow rate was set to 6 µL/h, regulated using a syringe pump (Harvard apparatus), for 5 days and the whole setup was kept at room temperature. Antibiotic treatment was performed by flowing 2.5 µg/mL of ciprofloxacin along with 2 µg/mL of Ethidium homodimer III in M9N or M9n media supplemented with carbenicillin for 24 h prior to imaging.

Biofilm imaging by confocal microscopy

Five days after flow initiation of M9N or M9n media, E. coli biofilms in flow channels were imaged on an inverted microscope through the bottom glass coverslip. Imaging was performed with a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal laser scanning microscope using 63X oil objective with a numerical aperture 1.4 (Zeiss). Three fields of view per flow channel were captured for each replicate. Fluorescent proteins mKate2 and mVenus were excited with 561 nm or 488 nm lasers, respectively. The microscope hardware was controlled by ZEN - Blue Edition (Zeiss). Images were acquired at low excitation light intensities with 60 ms exposure time while amplifying the readout using the EM-gain of the camera. Image z-stacks were acquired from bottom to top, at a spatial resolution of 99.035 nm in the xy-plane and 1 µm in the z-direction.

Biofilm image analysis

Three-dimensional biofilm architecture was quantified using BiofilmQ v1.0.115, leveraging mKate2 constitutive fluorescence or PyrBI-mVenus fluorescence to differentiate cellular biovolume from background. Raw z-stacks underwent preprocessing by applying a mean filter with the kernel size 5 px (xy), 3 px (z) to reduce the photon shot noise followed by application of the software’s floating cell suppression algorithm to reduce artefacts during segmentation. Signal enhancement employed a disk-shaped top-hat filter (25 pixel diameter, 5.5 μm actual size) applied per xy-plane to accentuate bacterial clusters while suppressing uneven illumination artifacts. Segmentation was performed by an Otsu threshold multiplied by 0.2 or 0.8 for mKate2 or PyrBI-mVenus, respectively, to capture low-intensity cells at biofilm peripheries, maintaining consistent discount factors across all samples. For the spatial quantification of the PyrBI-mVenus reporter (segmented using mKate2 fluorescence values) or ethidium homodimer III fluorescence (segmented using PyrBI-mVenus fluorescence values), images were further segmented into cubes with a side length of approximately 2 µm. For each cube, the mean fluorescence intensity (mean intensity per object) of PyrBI-mVenus or ethidium homodimer III was calculated. Heatmaps of the fluorescence intensities of all cubes by height of the biofilm were plotted, averaging four replicates.

Statistical analysis and data presentation

Bar graphs and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.3.1, one-way ANOVA/Tukey’s multiple comparison (Fig. 1g,h/Fig. 2e/Fig. 4b,c,h) or Student’s t-test (Fig. 1e/Fig. 4e–g). Three-dimensional rendering of confocal microscopy images of E. coli biofilms was performed using Paraview v5.7 based on segmentation results and output from BiofilmQ analyses.
