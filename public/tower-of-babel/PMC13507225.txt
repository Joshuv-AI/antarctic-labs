CDC7 and APC/CCdh1 gate distinct routes to initiate DNA replication
Authors: Samsara W. Upadhya, Nalin Ratnayeke, Katherine R. Ferrick, David L. Rosenthal, Tobias Meyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precise temporal control of DNA replication initiation is essential for faithful cell division, yet there are conflicting prevailing models for how cells trigger origin firing. Here, we demonstrate that human origins are not fired at fixed thresholds of cell cycle regulators E2F, APC/CCdh1, CDK2/1, and CDC7. Instead, origin firing is triggered at a tunable CDK2/1 threshold that is gated by either CDC7 or APC/CCdh1 activity. CDC7 phosphorylates MCM helicase, enabling Cyclin E-CDK2 to trigger origin firing at low CDK2/1 activity, independent of APC/CCdh1 inactivation. In contrast, APC/CCdh1 inactivation enables Cyclin A-CDK1 to phosphorylate MCM at distinct CDK2/1-specific sites and trigger origin firing, independent of CDC7 activity. Strikingly, this requires much higher CDK2/1 activity, which CDK2 cannot normally reach alone. Thus, having two distinct routes necessitates blocking both routes to prevent S phase: either by inhibiting CDC7 or CDK2 while also preventing APC/CCdh1 inactivation or inhibiting CDK1.

Introduction

The start of DNA replication occurs at S phase entry and is an irreversible step in the cell cycle that progresses cells towards cell division. Before this critical juncture, inactive MCM helicase complexes are loaded onto origins of replication across the genome in a process called origin licensing1,2. These licensed origins are held inactive until S phase entry, when MCM helicases are activated, and replisomes are assembled at origins across the genome to initiate DNA synthesis in a process called origin firing1–3. Premature S phase entry causes DNA replication stress and genome instability due to inadequate preparation for DNA replication4–7. Furthermore, since cells can exit the cell cycle throughout G1 phase in response to stress or loss of mitogen signaling8–11, the inadvertent firing of origins followed by cell cycle exit would similarly result in genome instability from partial genome duplication4,12,13. For these reasons, origin firing must only begin at the intended S phase entry time and continue until all DNA is replicated, requiring precise temporal regulation.

Despite the importance of the mechanism regulating the timing of S phase entry, previous studies in various organisms have come to different conclusions on how the start of origin firing is triggered. A major challenge has been the presence of multiple required regulatory factors that all change in parallel as cells transition from G1 to S phase. This is further confounded by the heterogeneous timing of when individual cells enter S phase14–16. Despite this complexity, it is well established that DNA replication is regulated by the transcription factor E2F, the cell cycle kinases CDC7 and cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK), and the E3 ubiquitin ligase APC/CCdh1 14,17 (Fig. 1a). In G1, the E2F transcriptional program expresses essential proteins for DNA replication, including origin licensing and firing factors and replisome components18,19. E2F also induces the expression of cell cycle kinases Cyclin E-CDK2, Cyclin A-CDK2, Cyclin A-CDK1, and DBF4-CDC718–21, which promote DNA replication by phosphorylating proteins that regulate origin firing22–28. In an additional layer of regulation in G1, APC/CCdh1 degrades S phase regulators such as Cyclin A29–31, SKP232, CDC25A33, and, reportedly, DBF434–36, restricting their presence until after APC/CCdh1 is inactivated near the end of G1.Fig. 1Cells can enter S phase before APC/CCdh1 inactivation.a Schematic depicting regulation of the G1/S transition and origin firing. b Schematic depicting the hypothesized mechanisms of triggering origin firing. c Example fluorescence intensity traces from single MCF10A cell depicting CRL4Cdt2 (S phase entry) and APC/CCdh1 reporters at different cell cycle phases. d Empirical cumulative distribution functions (ECDF) of S phase entry times and APC/CCdh1 inactivation times relative to mitosis in MCF10A cells, measured using reporters (n = 1441 cells). e Distribution of times between APC/CCdh1 inactivation and S phase entry in MCF10A cells showing heterogeneity at the single cell level (n = 5030 cells). While there is no general agreement on how origin firing is triggered in human cells, there are several plausible models for what specific activities are rate-limiting (Fig. 1b). The switch-like inactivation of APC/CCdh1 towards the end of G1 has been thought to be required for S phase entry and could initiate origin firing by starting the accumulation of rate-limiting APC/CCdh1 substrates17. Consistent with this model, premature APC/CCdh1 inactivation promotes S phase entry, and the timing of APC/CCdh1 inactivation is correlated with the start of S phase2,37–40. Alternatively, the activation of E2F could initiate origin firing by increasing the synthesis of multiple replisome components and kinase regulators. In this model, origin firing could be triggered when cells reach a critical E2F activity threshold (Fig. 1b).

Alternate kinase-centric models for S phase entry have also been proposed. In human cells, Cyclin E-CDK2, Cyclin A-CDK2, and Cyclin A-CDK1 complexes are the primary CDK-type S phase kinases and act in concert with DBF4-CDC7 to phosphorylate replication components22,40,41. Qualitative models posit that a rapid increase of one of these kinases initiates S phase. Nevertheless, this model has been challenged by gene deletion studies in mammalian cells showing cells lacking CDC7, CDK1, or CDK2 individually can still enter S phase, demonstrating functional redundancies42–45. Alternative quantitative models posit that cells fire origins at a threshold kinase activity. While a CDK threshold is thought to regulate passage through the restriction point, which governs cell cycle irreversibility in response to growth factor withdrawal46, whether a CDK or CDC7 activity threshold could trigger S phase entry is not known. For example, a combined CDK2 and CDK1 activity could reach an aggregate CDK2/1 threshold47 to begin origin firing and enter S phase. However, this model has been challenged by a recent study that showed CDK1 is uniquely required to fire origins when CDC7 is absent45.

Here, after testing these four models of S phase entry, we unexpectedly find that there is neither a singular route nor a fixed kinase activity threshold to trigger S phase entry. Instead, cells fire origins through two distinct routes gated by CDC7 activation or APC/CCdh1 inactivation, with both routes converging on a tunable CDK2/1 threshold that triggers the start of origin firing. The CDC7-gated route does not require APC/CCdh1 inactivation, Cyclin A, or CDK1 activation. Instead, CDC7-mediated phosphorylation of MCMs allows increasing Cyclin E-CDK2 activity to trigger origin firing at a low CDK2/1 activity threshold. We show that CDC7 is active in G1 since human DBF4, unlike the homologous yeast protein, is present in G1 when APC/CCdh1 is active. In contrast, the APC/CCdh1-gated route requires neither CDC7 nor CDK2 activity, but instead requires a much higher CDK2/1 activity that is essential to phosphorylate MCMs at CDK-selective sites and trigger origin firing. In this route, cells must first inactivate APC/CCdh1 to activate Cyclin A-CDK1 to reach the higher CDK2/1 threshold. The dependency on CDK1 in cells lacking CDC7 activity is due to the inability of CDK2 to reach the higher CDK2/1 threshold alone, rather than a unique catalytic role of CDK1. Together, these findings are clinically relevant and demonstrate that origin firing during S phase entry is prevented by blocking both routes, combining either CDC7 inhibition or CDK2 inhibition with either APC/CCdh1 activity stabilization or CDK1 inhibition.

Results

Cells can enter S phase before APC/CCdh1 inactivation

We first tested the model that the switch-like inactivation of APC/CCdh1 is the trigger initiating S-phase entry. The hypothesis that APC/CCdh1 inactivation is required for origin firing originates from studies showing that APC/CCdh1 inactivation can stabilize proteins critical for DNA replication, such as Cyclin A and DBF417,31,35,36,48, and that the deletion of the APC/C activating subunit Cdh1 (FZR1) causes premature S phase entry30,49,50. We reasoned that if APC/CCdh1 inactivation is the trigger initiating origin firing, its inactivation must precede S phase entry.

To measure the precise time of origin firing in single cells, we used a CRL4Cdt2 reporter that is rapidly degraded when the E3 ligase CRL4Cdt2 is activated by origin firing51 (Fig. 1c). We use the term “S phase entry” to refer to the initiation of origin firing as measured by the degradation of the reporter by CRL4Cdt2. To measure the time of APC/CCdh1 inactivation, we used an APC/CCdh1 reporter that is degraded when APC/CCdh1 is active and starts to accumulate when APC/CCdh1 is inactivated in a switch-like manner (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1a; this reporter pair is referred to as the FUCCI-CA system51). A computational detection algorithm was used to identify the precise timing of APC/CCdh1 inactivation, as done previously30,52 (Supplementary Fig. 1b, see also “Methods”). We used high-throughput automated fluorescence microscopy to measure both the time of APC/CCdh1 inactivation and S phase entry in thousands of asynchronously cycling non-transformed MCF10A and RPE-1 cells, as well as the cancer cell line U2OS. Consistent with a coupling between APC/CCdh1 inactivation and S phase entry, the timing of S phase entry and APC/CCdh1 inactivation were closely correlated (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1c). Furthermore, every cell that inactivated APC/CCdh1 also entered S phase (Supplementary Fig. 1d).

However, joint analysis of both reporters in the same cells revealed heterogeneity of S phase entry relative to APC/CCdh1 inactivation, as has been previously reported50. Some cells entered S phase up to 3 h before APC/CCdh1 inactivation, suggesting that APC/CCdh1 inactivation may not be necessary for S phase entry (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1e). Notably, cells that inactivated APC/CCdh1 after S phase entry still completed the cell cycle at a similar rate to those that inactivated APC/CCdh1 before S phase entry (Supplementary Fig. 1f).

To directly test whether APC/CCdh1 inactivation initiates origin firing, we used acute siRNA knockdown of the APC/CCdh1 inhibitor Emi1 (FBXO5)53,54 to prevent APC/CCdh1 inactivation in U2OS cells (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 1g). If APC/CCdh1 inactivation triggers S phase entry, preventing it should prevent S phase entry. Strikingly, cells that kept APC/CCdh1 active still entered S phase, as determined by both the CRL4Cdt2 reporter and the incorporation of 5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU), a marker of DNA synthesis (Fig. 1g, h, Supplementary Fig. 1h, i, and Supplementary Video 1). Similar results were observed in RPE-1 and MCF10A cells, where Emi1 knockdown delayed APC/CCdh1 inactivation and caused cells to fire origins long before APC/CCdh1 inactivation (Supplementary Fig. 1j, k). Thus, cells can fire origins and enter S phase without inactivating APC/CCdh1, demonstrating that APC/CCdh1 inactivation is not a general trigger for S phase entry.

Cells in G1 are poised to enter S phase with active E2F, CDC7, and CDK2

Since cells can enter S phase before inactivating APC/CCdh1, cells must have the necessary components to fire origins even if APC/CCdh1 is active. We used quantitative image-based cytometry (QIBC)52,55 to examine the levels and phosphorylation status of critical replication components. We live-imaged cells prior to fixation and staining to retrospectively calculate the time since each cell exited mitosis or entered S phase (RT-QIBC52; see “Methods”). Origin firing occurs on licensed origins and licensed origins, as measured by immunofluorescence staining of chromatin-bound MCM256,57, were present throughout G1 (Supplementary Fig. 2a), consistent with previous studies4,52,58,59. Origin firing also requires E2F-induced expression of replisome components18. Immunofluorescence staining of phospho-RB(Ser807/811), which is a marker of RB hyperphosphorylation and E2F activation10,11,60, confirmed that E2F is active during G1, well before S phase entry (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, the levels of the replisome components CDC45, PCNA, TIMELESS, POLA2, POLD2, and POLE2 (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 2b) were high in G1. We next examined the activity of the kinases regulating origin firing in S phase. We first determined whether DBF4, the activator of CDC7, is absent in G1 phase, since APC/CCdh1 degrades DBF4 in budding yeast and only becomes stabilized when APC/CCdh1 is inactivated34,35,61. In contrast to yeast, when we live-imaged fluorescently tagged human DBF4, we found that DBF4 levels are high in G1 when APC/CCdh1 is active (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2c–f). As a control, fluorescently tagged APC/CCdh1 substrate Geminin was almost completely degraded in G1 and was only stabilized after APC/CCdh1 inactivation (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2c–f).

Since the overexpressed DBF4 was present in G1, we next determined whether endogenous DBF4 was present in G1. Immunofluorescence staining of endogenous DBF4 showed that DBF4 signal was detectable in G1 and could be reduced by siRNA-mediated knockdown of DBF4, indicating that DBF4 protein is present while APC/CCdh1 is still on (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 2g, h). The primary role of DBF4-CDC7 in origin firing is the phosphorylation of MCM complexes41,62. Therefore, we used immunofluorescence to measure the relative ratio of CDC7-selective phosphorylation of MCM2(Ser53)63 to total MCM2 in individual cells to test whether DBF4-CDC7 was functionally active during G1. Since MCM phosphorylation occurs on the licensed chromatin-bound MCM complex64, we monitored phospho-MCM2(Ser53) on chromatin-bound MCM257–59. Unexpectedly, the phospho-MCM2(Ser53) ratio was high throughout G1 (Fig. 2e), demonstrating that DBF4-CDC7 is active in G1. Control experiments showed that the phosphorylation was inhibited by the addition of a CDC7-specific kinase inhibitor (TAK-931)65, or knockdown of DBF4 (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 2i). We conclude that human DBF4 is present in G1 despite APC/CCdh1 being on, DBF4-CDC7 is active in G1, and licensed MCMs are already phosphorylated by CDC7 long before APC/CCdh1 inactivation and S phase entry.

Since CDK2 and CDK1 both regulate origin firing42,45,66,67, we determined when CDK2/1 is activated by using a CDK2/1 activity reporter that measures the activity of Cyclin E or Cyclin A complexed with CDK2 or CDK1 (Supplementary Fig. 3a)9,47. We confirmed previous observations that the CDK2/1 reporter signal increases well before S phase entry (Fig. 2f)9,30,47. When APC/CCdh1 is active, this CDK2/1 activity reporter reflects Cyclin E-CDK2 activity9,52, since APC/CCdh1 degrades Cyclin A48 and Cyclin E has a strong preference to complex with CDK2 over CDK168. Indeed, Cyclin A knockdown only reduced CDK2/1 activity after S phase entry in these cells, coinciding with the time of APC/CCdh1 inactivation and Cyclin A stabilization (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c).

Our previous work showed that cells entering the cell cycle from quiescence spend many hours in a reversible state of partial E2F and Cyclin E-CDK2 activation before S phase entry11. To measure E2F and Cyclin E-CDK2 activity changes in cycling cells relative to the time of mitosis or S phase entry, we utilized a fluorescent transcriptional reporter for E2F activity11 (Supplementary Fig. 3d) together with the CDK2/1 reporter. Markedly, E2F and Cyclin E-CDK2 are also active in cycling cells for variable durations in G1 without triggering S phase entry (Fig. 2f, g).

We conclude that cells with active APC/CCdh1 spend time in a variable poised G1 state, where necessary components for origin firing are already present, and E2F, Cyclin E-CDK2, and DBF4-CDC7 are active. This raises the question of how cells transition out of the poised state to enter S phase (Fig. 2h).

Origin firing is triggered by CDK2/1 activity in cells with active CDC7

Since we found that both Cyclin E-CDK2 and DBF4-CDC7 are already active in G1, we hypothesized that a further increase in CDC7 or CDK2 activity could fire origins. To determine whether CDC7 or CDK2 is rate-limiting, we generated cell lines with doxycycline(Dox)-inducible expression of DBF4 or Cyclin E1 to increase DBF4-CDC7 or Cyclin E-CDK2 activity and measured whether S phase entry is accelerated.

We found that overexpression of DBF4 did not accelerate S phase entry (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 4a), despite increasing the CDC7-mediated phosphorylation of MCM2(Ser53) in G1. To increase CDC7-mediated MCM phosphorylation in an independent way, we knocked down RIF1, a phosphatase adapter that antagonizes kinase activity by directing the phosphatase PP1 to dephosphorylate MCMs69–72. In contrast, overexpressing Cyclin E1, which increased CDK2/1 reporter activity in G1 (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 4d), strongly accelerated S phase entry, as seen by the shortened G1 (Fig. 3b). This shortened G1 also resulted in most cells firing origins before they inactivated APC/CCdh1 (Fig. 3c). To increase the activity of CDK2 in an independent way, we inhibited WEE1, a kinase which inhibits CDK2 and CDK1 activity73. WEE1 inhibition not only increased CDK2/1 activity but also accelerated S phase entry, further supporting that CDK2/1 activity is rate-limiting for origin firing (Supplementary Fig. 4e). These results show that increased CDK2/1 activity but not CDC7 activity accelerates origin firing, supporting that Cyclin E-CDK2 but not DBF4-CDC7 activity triggers S phase entry.

The quantitative model of cell cycle progression posits that cells enter S phase at a CDK activity threshold. In human cells, such a CDK activity threshold could be based on combined phosphorylation by redundant CDK2 and CDK142,43. Two predictions from this model are that (1) origins should fire while CDK2/1 activity is increasing, and (2) there should be a fixed CDK2/1 activity threshold for firing origins independent of the greatly variable G1 length.

To test whether CDK2/1 activity triggers origin firing, we first measured the change in CDK2/1 activity relative to the CDK2/1 activity at the time of S phase entry. Indeed, cells invariably increased their CDK2/1 activity as they initiated origin firing (Fig. 3d). To determine whether the observed CDK2/1 activity level when origins are fired is independent of G1 length, we accelerated and delayed CDK2/1 activation and measured whether CDK2/1 activity at S phase entry changed. Consistent with a CDK2/1 activity level triggering origin firing, overexpression of Cyclin E1 or knockdown of Cyclin E1/E2, which accelerates or delays S phase entry, respectively, did not change the mean CDK2/1 activity at S phase entry (Fig. 3e, f and Supplementary Fig. 4f, g). In additional experiments, knocking down Cyclin A2 (CCNA2, the primary Cyclin A gene in somatic cells74), prolonging activation of APC/CCdh1 by knocking down its inhibitor Emi1 (FBOX5), or inhibiting WEE1 resulted in cells firing origins at the same mean CDK2/1 activity. (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 4h, i).

Since E2F activity increases alongside Cyclin E-CDK2 when cells enter S phase (Fig. 2f, g), these experiments left open the possibility that the CDK2/1 activity level is correlative, and it is rather the parallel increase in E2F activity to a threshold that triggers origin firing by increasing the expression of multiple rate-limiting factors. To test for a potential E2F threshold for origin firing, we used CDK4/6 (Palbociclib) and CDK2 (PF-07104091, Tagtociclib) inhibitors to lengthen G1 in two different ways11,75–78. CDK2 inhibition increased E2F activity at the time of S phase entry, while CDK4/6 inhibition decreased E2F activity (Fig. 3h, i). Overexpressing Cyclin E in CDK4/6 inhibited cells caused cells to enter S phase earlier, but the E2F activity at S phase entry remained low (Supplementary Fig. 4j). Despite the higher or lower E2F levels, cells entered S phase at a similar CDK2/1 activity threshold, supporting that there is no conserved E2F threshold and that E2F activity is generally not rate-limiting for S phase entry (Fig. 3h, i and Supplementary Fig. 4j). We conclude that cells with active APC/CCdh1 spend variable times in a poised G1 state where E2F, CDC7, and CDK2 are already active, but origin firing remains suppressed until cells enter S phase by increasing Cyclin E-CDK2 activity to reach a CDK2/1 activity threshold that triggers origin firing (Fig. 3j).

To determine whether the high CDK2/1 activity level at S phase entry in the absence of CDC7 activity is due to an increase in a CDK2/1 activity threshold, rather than a correlation with G1 length, we overexpressed Cyclin E1 to accelerate S phase entry (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). Markedly, these cells still fired origins at the same high CDK2/1 activity (Fig. 4b). Conversely, treating cells with siRNA targeting Cyclin E1/E2 delayed the increase in CDK2/1 activity and delayed S phase entry (Supplementary Fig. 5e, f), but the cells still started S phase at the high CDK2/1 activity (Fig. 4c). Thus, CDK2/1 activity triggers the start of origin firing at a low or high CDK2/1 activity threshold, depending on whether CDC7 is active, contrary to previously proposed solely qualitative or quantitative models of S phase entry.

Origin firing has been proposed to be initiated by phosphorylation of MCMs at multiple CDC7-selective sites63,64. In support of a critical role of CDC7, we found that MCM2 can already be phosphorylated by CDC7 early in G1 (Fig. 2e). This raises the question of how replisome assembly can be initiated without CDC7 activity. As MCMs also have CDK-selective phosphorylation sites63,79, CDK-selective phosphorylation of MCMs could be a functionally redundant mechanism to prime MCMs in preparation for origin firing (Fig. 4d)45. To test for a potential redundant MCM phosphorylation by CDK, we used a phospho-specific antibody to monitor the relative phosphorylation of a CDK-selective phospho-MCM2(Ser27)63 site, ratioed by the total MCM2. This site was phosphorylated at a higher level at the time of S phase entry in cells treated with CDC7 inhibitor (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 5g). Control experiments showed that dual CDK1 (RO-3306)80 and CDK2 inhibition using small molecule inhibitors decreased this signal (Fig. 4e). Thus, cells without CDC7 activity enter S phase with high CDK2/1 activity and high CDK-selective MCM2(Ser27) phosphorylation. In contrast, cells with CDC7 activity enter S phase with low CDK2/1 activity and low CDK-selective MCM2(Ser27) phosphorylation and instead have the CDC7-selective site MCM2(Ser53) phosphorylated (Fig. 4e).

To determine whether the CDK2/1 phosphorylation of MCMs is rate-limiting in CDC7-inhibited cells, we knocked down RIF1, which directs dephosphorylation of both CDC7 and CDK-selective MCM phosphorylation sites (Fig. 4d)69,71,81. Strikingly, in CDC7-inhibited cells, RIF1 knockdown shortened the G1 length and lowered the high CDK activity threshold for S phase entry (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 5h, i). Control experiments confirmed that the RIF1 knockdown did not increase the CDC7-selective MCM2 site in CDC7-inhibited cells (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 5j) but increased the phosphorylation of the CDK-specific site in G1 phase (Fig. 4e). Together, these results show that MCM phosphorylation by CDK2/1 is a rate-limiting step in origin firing in cells lacking CDC7 activity, providing cells with an alternate mechanism to initiate origin firing.

We conclude that the S phase entry mechanism in human cells is not based on a fixed CDK2/1, E2F, or APC/CCdh1 activity threshold. Instead, origin firing is controlled by two separate routes in which CDK2/1 triggers S phase entry at low or high CDK2/1 activity when CDC7 is present or absent, respectively (Fig. 4h). In the absence of CDC7-selective MCM phosphorylation, cells must phosphorylate CDK-selective MCM residues to initiate origin firing, which requires much higher CDK2/1 activity (Fig. 4i).

However, the conclusion that CDK1 and CDK2 activities have redundant roles for S phase entry was recently challenged by the finding that in CDC7-deficient cells, only CDK1, and not CDK2 activity could initiate DNA replication45. Our original interpretation of this result was that CDK1 provides a unique catalytic activity in the absence of CDC7. Consistent with the previous results, combining CDC7 with CDK1 inhibition prevented S phase entry (Fig. 5d). In contrast, combining CDC7 with CDK2 inhibition reduced the number of cells entering S phase but did not prevent S phase entry (Fig. 5d).

We considered whether the requirement for CDK1 activity to start S phase in CDC7-inhibited cells could be the result of the high CDK2/1 activity threshold needed for origin firing. Indeed, in cells with combined CDC7 and CDK1 inhibition, CDK2 activity still increased but could not reach the high CDK2/1 activity threshold (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 6e). In contrast, in cells with combined CDC7 and CDK2 inhibition, CDK1 activity could reach the high CDK2/1 activity threshold (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 6e).

The primary regulators of CDK1 activity are Cyclin A and Cyclin B. We therefore tested whether cells with inhibited CDC7 require Cyclin A or Cyclin B to reach the high CDK2/1 activity threshold and enter S phase. In support of an essential role for Cyclin A, CDC7-inhibited cells had elevated Cyclin A levels when they entered S phase (Fig. 5e). Moreover, knockdown of Cyclin A or inhibition of CDC7 alone only delayed EdU incorporation, but the combined knockdown of Cyclin A2 and inhibition of CDC7 strongly inhibited origin firing as shown by the inhibited EdU incorporation (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Thus, while Cyclin A is not required for cells to enter S phase when CDC7 is active, it is required for S phase entry in cells lacking CDC7 activity. Furthermore, overexpression of Cyclin A2 in CDC7-inhibited cells accelerated the CDK2/1 activity increase and accelerated origin firing (Fig. 5g, h and Supplementary Fig. 7b), demonstrating that Cyclin A is not only necessary but also rate-limiting.

Since Cyclin B has been proposed to also regulate the G1/S transition45,83, we knocked down Cyclin B1/B2 or overexpressed Cyclin B1 in CDC7-inhibited cells (Supplementary Fig. 7c–e). However, Cyclin B1/B2 knockdown did not delay origin firing, and overexpression of Cyclin B1 did not shorten G1 length (Supplementary Fig. 7d, e). As a control, we showed that the overexpressed Cyclin B1 was functional since it caused premature mitotic entry after S phase (Supplementary Fig. 7e).

As both CDK2 and CDK1 can trigger S phase entry when CDC7 is active, we hypothesized that the reason CDK2 activity alone is unable to reach the high CDK2/1 level is due to insufficient synthesis of Cyclin E rather than a unique catalytic role of CDK1. Indeed, when we overexpressed additional Cyclin E1 in cells with combined CDK1 and CDC7 inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 7f–g), CDK2/1 activation was accelerated, and most cells could reach the high CDK2/1 activity threshold and enter S phase (Fig. 5i). Thus, the reason why CDK1 is necessary for S phase entry in CDC7-inhibited cells is not a unique catalytic role for CDK1, but rather a difference in the amplitude of the physiological CDK1 and CDK2 activation.

Finally, we determined whether increasing CDC7 activity alone could trigger S phase entry when CDK2 and CDK1 are both inactive. However, overexpression of DBF4 in CDK1 and CDK2 co-inhibited cells failed to trigger S phase entry (Supplementary Fig. 7h), indicating that increased CDC7 activity cannot compensate for the loss of CDK activity.

We conclude that in cells with CDC7 activity, CDK1 and CDK2 are redundant and start origin firing at a low CDK2/1 activity threshold. In contrast, in cells lacking CDC7 activity, Cyclin A and CDK1 activity are both required for cells to reach the high CDK2/1 activity threshold, which CDK2 activity alone cannot normally reach (Fig. 5j).

To determine whether APC/CCdh1 inactivation is required, we knocked down the APC/CCdh1 inhibitor Emi1 to prevent APC/CCdh1 inactivation (Supplementary Fig. 8c). Knocking down Emi1 alone, like inhibition of CDC7 alone, still allowed for S phase entry (Fig. 6b). However, combining CDC7 inhibition and siEmi1 treatment prevented S phase entry (Fig. 6b). These cells could not fire origins since CDK2/1 activity could not reach a high enough level (Fig. 6c). Moreover, prematurely inactivating APC/CCdh1 by overexpressing Emi1 in cells with active CDC7 caused cells to increase CDK2/1 activity faster and enter S phase earlier (Fig. 6d, e and Supplementary Fig. 8d, e). In CDC7-inhibited cells, Emi1 overexpression also caused cells to enter S phase earlier at the same CDK2/1 threshold, since APC/CCdh1 inactivation also occurred earlier (Supplementary Fig. 8e). We conclude that APC/CCdh1 inactivation promotes S phase entry independently of whether CDC7 is active. However, APC/CCdh1 inactivation is required for firing origins only when CDC7 is inhibited, resulting in a suppressive synergy between CDC7 inhibition and stabilization of APC/CCdh1 activity.

CDK2-inhibited cells must inactivate APC/CCdh1 to enter S phase

Since Cyclin A activates CDK1 and is degraded in G1 by APC/CCdh1, we considered whether cells may have an additional suppressive synergy between CDK2 inhibition and APC/CCdh1 activity stabilization. Such a synergy seemed plausible since CDK2-inhibited cells with active CDC7 may require APC/CCdh1 inactivation to activate Cyclin A-CDK1 to fire origins. Indeed, like CDC7-inhibited cells, CDK2-inhibited cells also fired origins only after inactivating APC/CCdh1 (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 8f, g). Moreover, while neither siEmi1 treatment nor CDK2 inhibition alone prevented S phase entry, in combination these treatments suppressed origin firing, demonstrating a suppressive synergy for origin firing between APC/CCdh1 stabilization and CDK2 inhibition (Fig. 6g and Supplementary Fig. 8h). This synergy was exclusive for CDK2 inhibition, as combining CDK1 inhibition and siEmi1 did not prevent origin firing (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Fig. 8i). Accordingly, cells treated with CDK1inhibitor and siEmi1—but not cells treated with CDK2 inhibitor and siEmi1—were able to reach the low CDK2/1 threshold (Fig. 6i). Thus, the synergistic suppression of S phase entry by combining the stabilization of APC/CCdh1 with inhibition of either CDC7 or CDK2 results from the need for APC/CCdh1 inactivation to increase CDK1 activity to trigger S phase entry.

We conclude that human cells have unexpected pairwise synergies to prevent S phase entry (Fig. 6j). Origin firing can be suppressed by combining (1) CDC7 inactivation and APC/CCdh1 stabilization, (2) CDK2 inactivation and APC/CCdh1 stabilization, (3) CDK1 and CDC7 inactivation, or (4) CDK1 and CDK2 inactivation. However, inactivating the kinases CDC7, CDK1, or CDK2 alone did not prevent origin firing, nor did the stabilization of APC/CCdh1 activity alone. Furthermore, pairwise CDC7 and CDK2 inhibition or APC/CCdh1 stabilization and CDK1 inhibition did not prevent origin firing. This finding that select pairwise perturbation combinations suppress S phase entry (Fig. 6j) argues that there are only two distinct routes for S phase entry: one mediated by CDC7 and CDK2 activation and the second by APC/CCdh1 inactivation and CDK1 activation. S phase entry is only blocked by simultaneously targeting enzymes from both routes.

Initiating S phase entry by combining the CDC7 and APC/CCdh1-gated routes

Finally, given that unperturbed cells show heterogeneity in the timing of APC/CCdh1 inactivation (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1e) and in the level of DBF4 and CDC7-directed MCM2 Ser53 phosphorylation (Fig. 2d, e and Supplementary Fig. 2g), we considered whether cells can fire origins by combining the APC/CCdh1- and CDC7-gated routes. We therefore examined how the CDK2/1 activity threshold varies across a range of CDC7 activities. We started with the condition where CDC7 is active, the CDK2/1 threshold is low, and APC/CCdh1 inactivation is not needed for origin firing. Since Cyclin A steadily accumulates after APC/CCdh1 inactivation (Supplementary Fig. 6e), we next investigated how CDC7 inhibition, and thus the CDK2/1 threshold, affected the time between APC/C inactivation and origin firing at varying doses. Indeed, there was a close correspondence between the amplitude of the CDK2/1 activity threshold and the time between APC/CCdh1 inactivation and origin firing (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Fig. 9d). This correlation implies that, as the relative CDC7 activity is reduced in G1, cells have a corresponding need for more Cyclin A to accumulate to increase CDK2/1 activity and trigger S phase entry.

Thus, CDC7 activation and APC/CCdh1 inactivation initiate distinct routes to S phase entry but can also jointly promote S phase entry. If the two routes are combined, Cyclin E-CDK2, Cyclin A-CDK2, and Cyclin A-CDK1 activities are additive, and cells fire origins when the joint CDK activity reaches the specific CDK2/1 activity level set by CDC7 activity. Notably, even at maximal CDC7 activity, there is a minimal low CDK2/1 activity level below which origins cannot be fired.

Discussion

Our study shows that human cells do not have a fixed CDK2/1, E2F, or APC/CCdh1 threshold for firing origins. Instead, cells take two routes to enter S phase that both converge on CDK2/1, triggering origin firing at a tunable CDK2/1 activity threshold. Furthermore, we show that CDC7 is essential for firing origins in cells with active APC/CCdh1. Moreover, rather than having different catalytic roles, CDK1 and CDK2 are redundant in firing origins, but CDK2 normally fails to reach the heightened CDK2/1 activity needed to fire origins when CDC7 is inactive.

Instead of the different prevailing models that sought to identify a single general mechanism for origin firing, our study found that cells can take two routes to enter S phase initiated by CDC7 activation or APC/CCdh1 inactivation with different mechanistic requirements (Fig. 7d). In the CDC7-gated route, cells do not need to inactivate APC/CCdh1, synthesize Cyclin A, or activate CDK1. Instead, they steadily increase Cyclin E-CDK2 activity to trigger origin firing at a low CDK2/1 activity threshold. In the APC/CCdh1-gated route, APC/CCdh1 inactivation allows Cyclin A-CDK1 activity to fire origins by increasing to a much higher CDK2/1 activity when CDC7 is inactive. This second route does not require CDK2 or CDC7 activity but does require APC/CCdh1 inactivation and Cyclin A-CDK1 activation. We conclude that the CDC7 and APC/CCdh1-gated routes have low or high CDK2/1 activity requirements, respectively, with different enzymatic requirements for entering S phase. In this way, the human S phase entry mechanism has both qualitative and quantitative features, in contrast to previously described models of S phase entry.

The existence of two routes to enter S phase argues that cell cycle arrest by prevention of origin firing can only be enforced by blocking both routes. Indeed, we show that inhibiting CDC7, CDK2, or CDK1 alone, or stabilizing APC/CCdh1 alone, fails to prevent S phase entry. Also, neither combined CDC7 and CDK2 inhibition nor combined APC/CCdh1 stabilization and CDK1 inhibition prevents S phase entry. However, four combinations targeting both routes blocked origin firing and forced cell cycle arrest (Fig. 6j): combining CDC7 inhibition with either APC/CCdh1 stabilization or CDK1 inhibition and combining CDK2 inhibition with either APC/CCdh1 stabilization or CDK1 inhibition. Since specific cancer and normal cells rely to different relative degrees on these regulators85, dual targeting of specific enzymes in each route could be therapeutically useful for personalized cancer treatments.

Even though the CDK2/1 activity threshold differs between cells, there is a minimal required CDK2/1 activity, even in cells with full CDC7 activity. Surprisingly, this CDC7 activity does not trigger S phase entry itself, and either CDK2 or CDK1 is required. Thus, cells can remain in G1 with active CDC7 without triggering S phase entry as long as CDK2/1 activity remains below this level. This CDK2/1 threshold allows cells to spend time in a poised G1 state where origin firing remains suppressed, even though E2F and CDK2/1 are already active, replisome components are present, and CDC7 has already phosphorylated licensed chromatin-bound MCMs. Thus, our study provides a mechanistic basis for a previously postulated transition period for signal integration in G1 when cells both prepare for DNA replication and also decide whether to enter S phase or reverse to quiescence10,11.

Our study further shows that the molecular underpinning of the low and high CDK2/1 activity thresholds is the phosphorylation of licensed MCMs by either CDC7 or CDK2/1, respectively. While CDC7 and CDK-specific phosphorylation sites on MCM have previously been identified63, here we characterized their distinct temporal dynamics and functional relevance. Cells with active CDC7 start to phosphorylate MCMs during G1, which subsequently allows CDK2/1 activity to trigger origin firing at the low CDK2/1 activity threshold. Since MCM phosphorylation is not rate-limiting in cells with active CDC7 (Fig. 3a), origin firing is likely triggered by CDK2/1-mediated phosphorylation of other replisome factors such as Treslin22. We further found that cells with inactive CDC7 prepare for origin firing by activating CDK2/1 to phosphorylate MCMs at CDK2/1 selective sites. To phosphorylate these sites, CDK2/1 activity must increase far beyond the level required for S-phase entry in cells with inactive CDC7, which requires CDK1 activity. Furthermore, we show that phosphorylation of CDK sites on MCMs is rate-limiting for S phase entry. Thus, MCMs must be primed prior to origin firing through either CDC7 or CDK-mediated phosphorylation at distinct sites. Having two mechanisms to prime MCMs may allow cells flexibility in regulating the start of S phase. In addition, CDC7 and CDK-regulated phosphorylation of MCMs remains relevant later in S phase, where continued CDC7 activity and rising CDK2/1 activity contribute to the timely completion of DNA replication4,86,87.

Our finding that CDC7 is activated early in G1 was unexpected since DBF4 is an APC/CCdh1 substrate in budding yeast34,35. However, we now show that human DBF4 is present in G1 and DBF4-CDC7 kinase is active in G1 before APC/CCdh1 is inactivated. Furthermore, although we and others previously proposed that APC/CCdh1 inactivation must precede origin firing17,30,54, we found that cycling cells frequently enter S phase without inactivating APC/CCdh1. We show that such cells enter S phase by activating CDC7 and CDK2. However, we also show that APC/CCdh1 inactivation is required for origin firing in cells lacking CDC7 activity.

In conclusion, our study identifies an intricate control mechanism for S phase entry based on two routes driven by CDC7 and CDK2 activation or APC/CCdh1 inactivation and CDK1 activation with low or high CDK2/1 activity thresholds for S phase entry, respectively. This dual control system for cell cycle entry generates clinically relevant pairwise synergies for arresting the cell cycle. Selective CDC7, CDK2, and CDK1 kinase inhibitors have already been developed88, but to stabilize APC/CCdh1 activity, we used siRNA targeting Emi1, since small molecules that stabilize APC/CCdh1 activity by preventing Emi1 binding are not yet available. Our study suggests that such stabilizers of APC/CCdh1 activity would have unique therapeutic potential. Our mechanistic insights into why only select combinatorial treatments are effective can guide future therapeutic strategies to force cell cycle arrest in specific cancers.

Methods

Experimental model

Cell culture

The experiments involved MCF10A human mammary epithelial cells (ATCC Cat# CRL-10317, RRID:CVCL_0598) as the primary model unless otherwise specified. MCF10A cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 growth medium supplemented with HEPES (Gibco Cat# 11039047), 5% horse serum (Gibco Cat# 16050122), 20 ng/mL EGF (PeproTech Cat# AF-100-15), 100 ng/mL cholera toxin (Sigma Cat# C8052), 0.5 μg/mL hydrocortisone (Sigma: H0888), and 10 μg/mL insulin (Sigma Cat# I1882). Cells were passaged using trypsin-EDTA (0.05%, Gibco Cat# 25300054) and neutralized in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 20% horse serum. hTert-RPE-1 (ATCC Cat#CRL-4000, RRID:CVCL_4388) cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 with HEPES supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma Cat# TMS-013-B). U2OS cells (ATCC Cat#HTB-96, RRID:CVCL_0042) were cultured in DMEM growth media (Gibco Cat# 11995065) with 10% FBS. All cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For microscopy experiments, cells were seeded in collagen-coated (Advanced Biomatrix Cat# 5005-B, 60 μg/mL dilution 2–24 h) or fibronectin-coated (Sigma Cat# F1141 60 μg/mL dilution 2–24 h) 96-well glass-bottomed plates (Cellvis Cat# P96-1.5H-N). Cells were seeded 12–24 h before each experiment.

Cell line generation

Cell cycle reporter cell lines were generated via third-generation lentiviral transduction. Lentivirus was produced in HEK-293T cells by co-transfecting packaging plasmids pMDLg/pRRE (Addgene # 12251, RRID:Addgene_12251), pRSV-rev (Addgene # 12253, RRID:Addgene_12253), and pCMV-VSV-G (Addgene # 8454, RRID:Addgene_8454) with the lentiviral plasmid, using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# 11668019) in Opti-MEM (Fisher Scientific, Cat# 31-985-070). After 72 h, virus was harvested from the supernatant, filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Millipore Cat# SCGP00525), and concentrated using 100 kDa centrifugal filters (Millipore Cat# UFC910024). Subsequently, the virus was transduced into cells cultured in growth media.

Cell lines containing doxycycline-inducible constructs were made with a pCW backbone with a puromycin selection marker and utilized a TetOn system. Cells expressing the construct were selected with 1 μg/mL puromycin until control cells died. TetOn cells were cultured without doxycycline until the experiment began. Cell lines constitutively expressing fluorescent reporters were sorted for positive fluorescence.

MCF10A cell lines were generated from a base cell line transduced with CSII-pEF1a-H2B-mTurquoise or pLV-H2B-miRFP670 as a nuclear tracking marker. Cells with both APC/CCdh1 and Crl4Cdt2 reporters were generated by either transducing H2B-miRFP or H2B-mTurquoise cells with tFucci(CA)2/pCSII-EF. Cells with the Cyclin E/A-CDK reporter together with CRL4Cdt2 and APC/CCdh1 reporters were created by transduction of H2B-miRFP670 cells with CSII-pEF1a-hDHB(994–1087)-mTurquoise and tFucci(CA)2/pCSII-EF, or alternatively by transduction of H2B-mTurquoise cells with CSII-pEF1a-hDHB(994–1087)-mVenus, pLV-miRFP670-hCDT1(1–100)ΔCy and CSII-pEF1a-mCherry-hGeminin(1–110). U2OS cells with the Cyclin E/A-CDK, APC/CCdh1, and CRL4Cdt2 reporters were generated by transduction of H2B-miRFP670 cells with pLV- hDHB(994–1087)-mTurquoise and tFucci(CA)2/pCSII-EF.

MCF10A cell lines with the E2F transcriptional reporter were generated from a base cell line co-transduced with pLV-H2B-miRFP670 and pLV-hCDC6(−1057 to 239)-mVenus-NLS-PEST. These cells were transduced with pLV-mCherry–hCDT1(1–100)ΔCy (Crl4Cdt2 reporter) and pLV-EF1a-DHB–mTurquoise (Cyclin E/A-CDK reporter). mVenus-positive cells were single-cell cloned to establish cell lines expressing the E2F reporter.

TetOn Dox-inducible pCW constructs expressing HA, mVenus-tagged DBF4 or mVenus-tagged full-length Geminin were introduced into these fluorescent reporter cell lines with the APC/CCdh1 reporter and H2B-mTurquoise. RPE1 cells had DHB-mTurquoise, APC-mCherry, H2B-miRFP670.

Method details

Plasmid generation

All plasmids generated in this study were assembled using Gibson assembly of PCR amplified or synthesized inserts (IDT) and restriction enzyme-digested plasmid backbones.

siRNA transfection

MCF10A, U2OS, and RPE-1 cells were transfected with siRNA using DharmaFECT (Dharmacon Cat# T-2001-03) according to the manufacturer’s protocol using 20 nM siRNA and 1:500 diluted DharmaFECT 1. Cells were incubated in transfection media for 6–12 h in full growth media, followed by a media change before imaging. 4 siRNA oligos were pooled for siCtrl, siCdh1, siEmi1 (siFBXO5), siCyclinA (siCCNA2), siCyclinB1 (siCCNB1), siCyclinB2 (siCCNB2), siCyclinE1 (siCCNE1), siCyclinE2 (siCCNE2), and siRif1. CCNA2 was knocked down as CCNA1 is not expressed in our cell lines. Shorter incubation times can be used for our siRNA due to the rapid protein degradation that occurs during anaphase. A list of siRNA oligos can be found in Supplementary Data 1.

Drug treatments

Stock solutions of drugs were dissolved in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# D2650) and used at the given working concentration unless otherwise stated: 10 μM CDK1 inhibitor RO-3306 (Cayman Chemical, Cat#15149), 500 nM CDC7 inhibitor Simurosertib TAK-931 (MedChemExpress Cat# HY-100888), 1 μM CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib (Selleck Chemicals, Cat#S1116), 1 μM CDK2 inhibitor PF-07104091 (ChemieTek, Cat# CT-PF0710), 500 nM MK-1775 (WEE1i, Cayman Chemical Cat# 21266), 1 μg/mL Doxycycline hyclate (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# D9891). For all experiments where drugs or Doxycycline were added to cells, DMSO (vehicle) was added to control cells.

Live-cell reporters

The cell cycle reporters used in this study were originally developed as the two components of the Fucci(CA) reporter system58. This reporter system utilizes human mCherry-fused Cdt1(aa 1–100) with a mutated cyclin binding domain (ΔCy), which is rapidly degraded by the E3 ubiquitin ligase CRL4Cdt2 in response to the start of origin firing and is therefore a precise marker for S phase entry and exit. The APC/C reporter is mVenus-fused Geminin (aa 1–110) that contains an APC/C mediated ubiquitination domain that becomes stabilized at the time of APC/C inactivation at the G1/S transition. We used time-lapse microscopy to measure the precise time of CRL4Cdt2 activation (the start of degradation of the CRL4Cdt2 reporter) and APC/CCdh1 inactivation (the stabilization of the APC/CCdh1 reporter). These precise time points are also used for RT-QIBC to calculate IF signals after mitosis, S phase entry, and APC/C inactivation.

The Cyclin E/A-CDK activity reporter is a translocation-based reporter phosphorylated by Cyclin E or A complexed with CDK2 or CDK1 and is based on a fragment of human DNA helicase B (aa 944–1087)9. When CDK activity is low, the reporter is unphosphorylated and is nuclear localized. When the kinase activity increases and the reporter is increasingly phosphorylated, it translocates from the nucleus to the cytoplasm. The activity of the reporter is measured as a ratio of cytoplasmic to nuclear intensity. For calculation specifics, see Segmentation and Signal Quantification.

In this study, we used one version of the DBF4 protein dynamic reporter, which is a C-terminal mVenus-tagged full-length DBF4 (DBF4-mVenus). The control full-length tagged Geminin used was also tagged on the C-terminus (Geminin-mVenus).

Fixed-cell immunofluorescence

General high-throughput immunofluorescence protocol

All imaging and staining were done in 96-well glass-bottomed plates (Cellvis Cat# P96-1.5H-N). Cells were seeded in collagen/fibronectin-coated plates and incubated 12–18 h before experiments were performed. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS (Fisher Cat# NC1537886) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by PBS wash. Cells expressing fluorescent live cell reporters have the potential for spectral overlap with secondary fluorophores. If these signals were needed for quantification, cells were imaged before fluorescent proteins were chemically bleached. Cells were bleached in 3% H2O2 + 20 mM HCl in PBS for 1 h, washed in PBS and checked under a microscope for residual fluorescent signal. Cells were permeabilized in 0.2% Triton ×-100 in PBS for 10 min then blocked in blocking buffer A containing 10% FBS, 1% BSA, 0.1% Triton ×-100 and 0.01% NaN3 in PBS for 1 h. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight in blocking buffer A at 4 °C, washed in PBS, and then incubated with secondary antibodies in blocking buffer A for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were washed with PBS and then incubated in 1 μg/mL DAPI (Thermo Cat#62248) in PBS for 10 min, followed by a final PBS wash prior to imaging in PBS. Unless otherwise stated, all washes were done with an automated plate washer (aspirate to 50 μL, dispense 250 μL, repeated 9 times, BioTek 405 LS) or by hand (for pre-extracted samples, 3 washes aspirating all liquid).

High-throughput iterative immunofluorescence protocol

To stain for targets in the same cells with multiple antibodies from the same species, the iterative indirect immunofluorescence imaging (4i)89 technique was used. The first round of staining was identical to the general immunofluorescence protocol, however after the final PBS wash, cells were washed in ddH2O and imaged in imaging buffer containing 700 mM N-acetyl cysteine in ddH2O at pH 7.4 (Sigma-Aldrich A7250). Antibodies were then eluted by 30-min incubation in elution buffer, which consists of 0.5 M glycine (Sigma-Aldrich), 3 M urea (Sigma-Aldrich), 3 M guanidinium chloride (Sigma-Aldrich) and 70 mM TCEP-HCl (Goldbio Cat#TCEP50) in ddH2O at pH 2.5, followed by a PBS wash. Cells were then checked under a fluorescence microscope to ensure proper elution, blocked with blocking buffer consisting of 1% BSA in PBS supplemented with 150 mM maleimide (dissolved just prior to use, Sigma-Aldrich Cat# 129585) for 1 h and then washed in PBS. Cells were then blocked with blocking buffer A for 30 min followed by the next primary antibody incubation. All subsequent steps were the same as the first round and repeated as needed. Control wells without primary antibodies were checked to ensure the absence of residual signal from prior imaging rounds.

Pre-extraction for chromatin-bound protein

To stain for chromatin-bound proteins, soluble proteins were extracted from cells just prior to fixation. Cells seeded in 96-well plates were placed on ice and media was aspirated. Cells were then incubated in ice-cold pre-extraction buffer containing. 2% Triton ×-100 in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# ×100) + 1× Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Cat# 78439). After 4 to 5 min, 8% PFA in H2O was added to wells 1:1 with wide-orifice tips to minimize cell detachment, and cells were fixed for 25 min at room temperature. All subsequent steps were performed as in the general staining protocol.

EdU incorporation and labeling

To measure 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation, cells were pulsed with 10 μM EdU (Cayman Chemical Cat# 20518) in growth media for 8–12 min prior to fixation and pre-extraction. After fixing and blocking, cells were washed once with PBS and a click reaction was performed in 2 mM CuSO4, 20 mg/mL sodium ascorbate (dissolved just prior to use) in TBS (Tris 50 mM, NaCl 150 mM pH 8.3) with 3 μM AFDye 488 picolyl azide (Vector Laboratories Cat# CCT-1276), AFDye 647 picolyl azide (Vector Laboratories Cat# CCT-1300) or AFDye 568 picolyl azide (Vector Laboratories Cat# CCT-1292) for 30 min, followed by a PBS wash.

Microscopy

A Ti2-E inverted microscope (Nikon) was used for automated epifluorescence microscopy. 10× (Nikon CFI Plan Apo Lambda, NA 0.45) or 20× (Nikon CFI Plan Apo Lambda, 0.75 NA) objectives were used to acquire images. Multichannel fluorescent images were captured with Hamamatsu ORCA-Fusion BT Digital CMOS camera and acquired using quinta-band filter sets (ET- BV421/BV480/AF488/AF568/AF647, Chroma: 89903) and an LED light source (Lumencor Spectra X or Lumencor Spectra III). All images were acquired in 16-bit mode and acquisition settings were chosen to not saturate any signal. Fluorophores and channels were chosen to minimize bleedthrough.

Time-lapse imaging

Live-cell time-lapse imaging was conducted within an enclosed 37 °C, 5% CO2 and humidity-controlled environmental chamber in 200 μL of growth media. 9 stitched sites were imaged in each well every 12 min. The number of wells imaged varied depending on the experiment. Light exposure was minimized to prevent cell stress. Exposure time and intensity per channel were set based on the minimum exposure necessary to maintain suitable signal-to-noise ratios. Maximum light exposure time was limited to 100 ms per site at each timepoint. Both 10 and 20× objectives were used for capturing images.

RT-QIBC and QIBC

Cells used for RT-QIBC analysis were immediately taken off the microscope following the final live-cell time point and fixed. Fixed-cell imaging for RT-QIBC and QIBC was done using the 20× objective and 9–36 tiled images were captured per well. During multiple rounds of imaging, the position of the plate was physically and computationally aligned to the same approximate position to ensure error-free cell tracking.

Experimental details

Quantification and statistical analysis

Image analysis

Analysis of time-lapse imaging of cell cycle reporters, quantitative image-based cytometry (QIBC) and Retrospective Time-lapse Synchronized QIBC (RT-QIBC) were performed using a custom automated MATLAB (2023b, 2024a, MathWorks) pipeline based on previously published work. We use QIBC for the high-throughput single-cell quantification of fluorescent signals in fixed samples. RT-QIBC requires the live cell reporters to track the timing of different cell cycle phases at the single-cell level. Cells are then fixed and QIBC measurements can be assigned to specific time points based on H2B, the APC/CCdh1 reporter and the CRL4Cdt2 reporter. This allows for the expression dynamics of any protein with an available IF antibody to be reconstructed over time. The signal can be retraced to 12-min intervals based on our 12-min live-cell imaging interval. The image processing pipeline and code used to generate all figures in this study have been deposited on GitHub and data at Zenodo.

Segmentation and signal quantification

To correct for illumination bias, we conducted computational flat-field correction on all raw images. Each image was divided by an empirically determined illumination profile calculated based on autofluorescence in areas without cells and averaged over many sites. For fixed IF imaging, illumination profiles were calculated using a sample-free well with autofluorescent blocking buffer A.

Automated nuclei segmentation for live-cell imaging was performed using the H2B signal using a Laplacian of Gaussian blob detector and refined by active contour. Segmentation for fixed-cell imaging was performed using the DAPI stain and a threshold determined by histogram curvature. Nuclei larger than the median object size were checked using a curvature-based object splitting algorithm to split cells if necessary. For plates imaged multiple times, the first round was used as the primary mask, and proceeding rounds segmentations were matched to the first rounds segmentations.

Nuclear signals were quantified after subtracting the background signal. Background signal was calculated based on the 25th percentile of pixel intensity outside of a dilated nuclear mask (dilated 7.8 μm for predominantly nuclear signals, 15.6 μm for signals with cytoplasmic components). For IF images, further analysis was conducted to calculate non-specific binding on antibodies based on positive controls. Mean and median nuclear signals were then calculated. For cytoplasmic signals, the mean and median signal within a ring outside the nucleus was calculated (region 0.65 to 3.25 μm outside the edge of the nucleus).

Time-lapse tracking

Nuclei were tracked during time-lapse imaging using a nearest-neighbor algorithm between one frame and its previous frame, combined with a linear assignment problem-based tracking algorithm to decrease the number of incorrect identity assignments. The sum of nuclear intensity was used as a constant variable between frames to correct for aberrant merging and splitting of nuclei during segmentation. Mitotic events were assigned to two daughter cells detected within the vicinity of a previous nucleus and had a sum nuclear intensity approximately equal to the previous single total nuclear signal. For live-fixed cell tracking, fixed-cells were computationally matched to live-cell images using a 2D cross-correlation, and cells were assigned to their nearest live-cell neighbor. Bicubic interpolation was used to match 20× fixed-cell images to 10× live-cell images, or 20× fixed images were mean-value binned.

Cell cycle annotation of live-cell data

Mitotic events were annotated during the tracking process and were marked by the detection of two distinct nuclei during anaphase. S phase was annotated using the CRL4Cdt2 reporter. The activation of CRL4Cdt2 during S phase entry causes the start of the degradation of the reporter based on Cdt1, a target of CRL4Cdt2-mediated degradation. Nuclear intensity of the reporter was measured over time following mitosis and a drop detection algorithm was used to detect the point of S phase entry. The sliding-window algorithm detects the degradation in reporter at a given timepoint based on a set number of frames following it, typically ~4 frames, corresponding to 48 min. Each point was checked based on the slope being low and the curvature of the slope being high within the 4-frame window. During this window, the normalized reporter signal must drop below a threshold. The inactivation of APC/CCdh1 was annotated based on the accumulation of the reporter, which is based on Geminin, a target of APC/CCdh1-mediated degradation. A similar sliding-window detection algorithm was used to calculate the point where the slope increases to a threshold level, and the reporter increases from a low to a threshold-value of persistent increase. APC/CCdh1 inactivation was identified at a point where the following conditions are met across the window: (1) the rate of change of the APC/CCdh1 reporter intensity increases to a threshold level, and (2) the reporter increases from a low value (representing noise) to a threshold-value and remains high. This algorithm was chosen to suppress spurious detection from noise at low reporter intensities while also allowing for detection in cells with limited measurements post APC/CCdh1 inactivation. The sliding window was typically 4–5 frames, corresponding to 48–60 min. Threshold values for both algorithms were empirically determined and validated based on at least 200 traces by eye. For both CRL4Cdt2 and APC/CCdh1 reporters, the integrated nuclear intensity was quantified for trace analysis.

RT-QIBC

Following the quantification of reporters and annotation of cell cycle phases, each fixed-cell was matched to a corresponding live-cell. Thus, the time of alignment to a cell cycle event is representative of time elapsed from that event and multidimensional fixed-cell measurements were arranged based on these timepoints.

Cell gating

For RT-QIBC analysis, cells were gated based on live-cell reporters to identify time of mitosis, G1, S phase, and APC/CCdh1 inactivation. For cells treated with inhibitors that cause mitotic defects or prevent mitosis (CDK1 inhibitor etc.), only cells that received the drug in G1 were used for subsequent analyses. Dox-inducible DBF4-mVenus and Geminin-mVenus were gated based on the presence of an mVenus signal during imaging.

Cells that received with siRNA targeting Emi1 were gated based on the suppression of the APC/CCdh1 reporter signal after mitosis and during S phase entry. Cells treated with siRNA targeting Cyclin A2 or Cyclin B1/B2 were fixed at the end of live-cell imaging and stained for respective knocked down proteins and gated based on low relative immunofluorescence signals compared to untreated cells.

Quantification corrections

Under instances where live-cell reporters were bleached to image fixed immunofluorescence signals, the residual signaling post-bleaching was measured. Cells with empirically determined incomplete bleaching were removed from analysis. In experiments where IF signal was measured in multiple rounds, the cells were imaged post-antibody elution, before the second round of IF imaging to empirically ensure sufficient elution. Cells with incomplete elution were removed from analysis. For pre-extracted cells with chromatin-bound proteins, pre-extraction efficiency was determined based on the absence of APC/CCdh1 and Cyclin E/A-CDK reporters, which are soluble and thus absent from pre-extracted samples. Cells with high reporter signals after pre-extraction were excluded from analysis.

When equal numbers of cells were used for analyses between conditions, cells were randomly subsampled without replacement.

Data normalization

APC/CCdh1 reporter, CLR4Cdt2 reporter, and DBF4/Geminin-mVenus signals were normalized to the value at mitosis, which is when the signal peaks. To normalize MCM stains, background values were calculated based on the fluorescence intensity at G2 when chromatin-bound MCM2 and pMCM2 are absent. Values were subtracted from MCM2 and pMCM2 signals and the pMCM2:MCM2 ratio was calculated for each cell. To normalize Cyclin A2 levels before and after APC/CCdh1 inactivation, background signal was determined in cells that received siRNA targeting Cyclin A2, had not inactivated APC/CCdh1 and had 2 N DNA content.
