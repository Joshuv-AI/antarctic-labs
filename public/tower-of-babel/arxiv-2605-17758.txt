Memisis: Orchestrating and Evaluating Synthetic Data for Tabular Health Datasets
Nitish Nagesh, Mahdi Bagheri, Arshia Harish Puthran, Pengbao Zhou, Muhjaazee Love, Aadi Sharma, Ian Harris, Amir M. Rahmani

Abstract
Synthetic data is widely used in healthcare to create datasets that are similar to original data but without the privacy concerns. Generating and evaluating synthetic data across privacy, utility and fairness is crucial for facilitating high quality data availability for downstream prediction tasks and clinical decision making. We present Memisis, a tool that orchestrates and evaluates synthetic data by leveraging existing synthetic data tools, the power of large language models and state-of-the-art evaluation metrics. Our tool creates a unified workflow for data generation, validation and evaluation. Users have control over the training size, training epochs and the number of synthetic rows to sample. Instead of knobs to tune synthetic data, the interactive agent allows users to specify their synthetic data generation goals and the tool will orchestrate the workflow by leveraging existing tools while performing the requisite evaluation. For the demo, we use an open source schizophrenia dataset with protected attributes related to race and gender, three different synthesizers and a local language model to orchestrate the workflow. We observe that CTGAN, TVAE and GaussianCopula have comparable performance across fairness and utility metrics. The workflow allows users flexibility and control over the data generation and evaluation process.

2
Method

The tool coordinates with different generative models in the backend, plans and
orchestrates analysis, presents the requisite data and shares reports.

Workflow.

We leverage existing synthesizers such as GANs, VAEs and GaussianCopula by
connecting to the Synthetic Data Vault (SDV)
[
16
]
. A raw dataset
including metadata is given as input. The generators use samples of the
original data and are trained for a certain number of epochs, using a subset of
the data. Using TSTR (Train Synthetic Test Real) paradigm, the validation is
performed for evaluating distribution of data, which is a sign of utility.
Going one step further, we evaluate fairness metrics, to investigate balance in
the dataset. In the current version, the user exercises control through the
generation knobs, as implemented in current data generators. Going beyond, the
Memisis agent mode allows users agency to generate synthetic data through
natural language while taking into consideration information about protected
attributes. The agent orchestration is via a LangGraph ReACT (Reasoning and
Acting) framework where a generator and evaluator coordinate to determine
optimum generation mechanism based on designated distribution and fairness
thresholds. In the multi-agent configuration, a supervisor partitions a
generator subgraph from an evaluator subgraph, ensuring that scoring cannot
silently influence synthesis. After evaluation, the supervisor compares outputs
against parity thresholds and either routes back to the generator with a
targeted refinement instruction or surfaces results to the user.

Deployment stack.

The architecture comprises of a python-based server deployed on the Streamlit
cloud connected to GitHub. The chat model used is Llama3.2 (connected locally
through Ollama). An NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU is used for training the synthesizers.

Evaluation and composite scoring.

The orchestrator refines the responses based on user inputs, preferences and
performance. The generated synthetic data is evaluated for utility using
SDMetrics, a sister repository of SDV
[
5
]
. We evaluate fairness
through false positive rates as illustrated in Fairlearn
[
2
]
.
To jointly capture fairness–utility trade-offs, we devise a composite score
that combines an established distributional fidelity score with an established
fairness disparity measure into a single supervisor signal, using a
proportional penalty above an empirically motivated parity threshold.

\mathrm{synth\_score}=\mathrm{quality}\times\mathrm{fairness\_mult}

where

\mathrm{fairness\_mult}=\begin{cases}1.0&\text{if }\max_{\text{group}}\,\mathrm{FPR}/\min_{\text{group}}\,\mathrm{FPR}\leq 2\\[4.0pt]
\min\!\left(1,\,\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{max\_rel\_FPR}}\right)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Here,
\mathrm{quality}
is the
SingleTableQualityReport
overall score
from SDMetrics, averaging marginal column-shape fidelity and
pairwise-correlation fidelity into a single
[0,1]
value.

\mathrm{max\_rel\_FPR}
is the ratio of the highest to lowest group-wise false
positive rate, computed via Fairlearn’s
MetricFrame
under a
train-on-synthetic, test-on-real (TSTR) paradigm. A logistic regression is
trained exclusively on synthetic rows and tested on held-out real data, so the
FPR reflects how faithfully the synthetic distribution transfers to downstream
classification across demographic groups. A ratio at or below
2\times
is
treated as acceptable parity, and beyond that the multiplier discounts quality
proportionally. This ensures that a synthesizer cannot score well on fidelity
alone if the synthetic data encodes unequal downstream error across groups.

4
Discussion

The benchmark exercises workflow and reporting in a domain where intersectional
bias in diagnosis is clinically
documented
[
7
,
8
,
15
]
, not
clinical validity. GaussianCopula’s high distributional fidelity (
0.91
) does
not translate to a superior composite score when the downstream classifier
exposes FPR disparity across racial groups (
\mathrm{synth\_score}=0.68
).
CTGAN’s balanced result (
0.77
) illustrates that fidelity and fairness must be
evaluated jointly—particularly for datasets where demographic representation
directly affects diagnostic outcomes.

Impact.

Our proposed tool,
Memisis
, allows for a natural language-based
mechanism for generating synthetic data where the heavy lifting of curation,
processing and validation is by an agent with appropriate human-in-the-loop
oversight. The tool is currently used by a small community of researchers.

Challenges.

During development, we had a hard time finalizing the architecture since there
were many moving pieces. During deployment, limited tokens for cloud providers
and coordination challenges with multiple agents exist. During tool operation,
there is scope for improvement in response design and validity. In hindsight,
we would have shipped faster, tested and built architecture in a modular
fashion.

Future plans.

We are working towards improving interoperability and reliability of the tool.
Our short-term goal is to deploy the website on a persistent server, ensure the
agent performs well through a combination of data curation, prompt engineering
and tool orchestration. In the future, we will conduct experiments with more
datasets, explore HL7/HIPAA compliance standards and work closely with users to
solve pain points.
