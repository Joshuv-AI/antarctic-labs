Cerebriform Ridges on the Scalp Signaling a Tumor in the Brain
Authors: Ariel Park, Sydney Kenney, Caitlyn Kellogg, Jason Robert Castillo, Terese Bergheim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCutis verticis gyrata is an overgrowth of scalp tissue that manifests as symmetric cerebriform skin folds that can be primary or secondary due to an underlying endocrine, infectious, genetic, or inflammatory condition. The authors have reported a case of 62-year-old man presenting with longitudinal ridging of the scalp and acromegalic features who was found to have a pituitary tumor causing acromegaly and the rare cutaneous manifestation of cutis verticis gyrata. This case highlighted how initial detection by dermatology led to multidisciplinary care involving several specialties and the eventual neurosurgical intervention leading to biochemical cure and symptom improvement.

Introduction

Cutis verticis gyrata (CVG) is an overgrowth of scalp tissue that manifests as symmetric cerebriform skin folds and is categorized into primary vs secondary CVG. Primary CVG is further subdivided into essential primary CVG and nonessential primary CVG. Essential primary CVG commonly affects adolescent males and is related to unknown genetic mutations thought to be relevant in fibroblasts or dermal cell migration. This form has no associated neurologic, ophthalmologic, or systemic conditions. Cutaneous findings of essential primary CVG include isolated deep, symmetric, cerebriform scalp folds that run anterior-posteriorly and lack superficial features such as erythema or scaling. Nonessential primary CVG is the rarest form and is associated with neurologic and ophthalmologic conditions such as epilepsy, mental retardation, cerebral palsy, cataracts, and blindness. 1 Secondary CVG is the most common form of CVG. It may be the result of an underlying endocrine condition such as acromegaly, metabolic syndrome, or myxedema; infectious conditions such as syphilis or HIV; genetic disease such as neurofibromatosis, Darier syndrome, or tuberous sclerosis; and inflammatory conditions such as psoriasis and pemphigus. 2 Over 20 causes of secondary CVG have been reported in the literature with additional clinical findings and symptoms dependent on the unique CVG etiology. 2 The authors have presented a case of secondary CVG from an underlying pituitary tumor causing acromegaly, prompting multidisciplinary care including neurosurgical intervention.

Case Patient Presentation

A 62-year-old man presented to outpatient dermatology clinic with longitudinal ridging of the scalp and forehead, frontal bossing, lower jaw prominence, widening of the teeth, malocclusion, difficulty eating, and enlargement of the hands and feet (Figures 1, 2, 3, and 4). The patient was diagnosed with CVG changes likely secondary to an underlying endocrine disorder, given the acromegalic features. Laboratory workup revealed markedly elevated insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) levels at 573 ng/mL (typical range: 71–290 ng/mL). The patient was subsequently referred to endocrinology, and further workup demonstrated elevated growth hormone (GH) levels at 24.6 ng/mL (typical, fasting: 0.02–0.97, random typical < 5) and a 1.2-cm mass in the anterior pituitary, consistent with a macroadenoma, on magnetic resonance imaging (Figure 5). The patient was ultimately diagnosed with secondary CVG from a pituitary macroadenoma with acromegaly.

Treatment Pathway

After evaluation by neurosurgery, the patient underwent an endoscopic transsphenoidal craniectomy for gross total removal of the pituitary tumor with neurosurgery and otolaryngology. Surgical pathology confirmed a pituitary adenoma.

Outcomes

At 2 weeks postoperation, the patient reported substantial improvement in facial swelling, joint pain, and joint swelling. The patient achieved a biochemical cure with normalization of IGF-1 levels collected at 3 weeks postoperation and has been symptom free for over 6 months.

Discussion

Acromegaly is caused by excessive production of GH and IGF-1 by the pituitary gland, most often due to a benign pituitary adenoma. 2 Dental complaints, such as tooth pain and malocclusion due to mandibular prognathism, increased interdental spacing, and acral enlargement resulting from soft tissue and bone overgrowth, are hallmarks of acromegaly. 3 Skin manifestations of acromegaly commonly include hirsutism, acne, acrochordons, oily skin, acanthosis nigricans, and hidradenitis suppurativa. 4,5 Less commonly, patients can develop CVG, which is driven by the trophic effects of GH and IGF-1 on dermal fibroblasts, leading to increased synthesis of collagen, glycosaminoglycans, and extracellular matrix components that result in progressive thickening and hypertrophy of the dermis and subcutaneous tissue. 4,6

The prevalence of CVG among individuals with acromegaly is variable and not well established. 4 Although Resende et al found 0 out of 15 acromegaly patients had CVG, Akoglu et al and Kolawole et al found prevalence rates of > 24% among 49 and 17 patients, respectively. A comprehensive literature review of acromegaly-associated CVG by Roque et al found that although primary CVG tends to present with symmetrical furrows along the sagittal axis of the scalp, the CVG patterns in acromegaly patients are more variable and may affect any region of the scalp, forehead, and face. Roque et al additionally suggested that the degree of CVG in acromegaly patients does not directly correspond to the size of the pituitary adenoma, but CVG does tend to be more common in patients with greater disease burden.

Diagnosis of acromegaly is confirmed by elevated serum GH and IGF-1 levels and imaging studies, such as magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography, that can confirm the thickening of the scalp and subcutaneous tissues and identify the presence of a pituitary tumor. 7,8 If acromegaly is excluded as the cause of CVG, workup might be performed to exclude other entities in the differential diagnosis of secondary CVG, including pachydermoperiostosis, severe insulin resistance, hypothyroid myxedema, HIV-related lipodystrophy, and sex chromosome abnormalities. Biopsy is rarely recommended for CVG. However, it might be performed if after additional workup, the CVG etiology is still unclear. Pathology of acromegaly-related CVG is nonspecific and demonstrates dermal thickening and edema, increased collagen and glycosaminoglycans, and pilosebaceous unit hypertrophy. 4

The primary treatment goal of CVG secondary to acromegaly is to normalize GH and IGF-1 levels, typically through excision by transsphenoidal surgery, medical therapy with somatostatin analogs, GH receptor antagonists, or, rarely, radiation therapy. 9,10 CVG develops gradually and can regress with normalization of GH and IGF-1 levels, particularly with surgical endocrine remission, although complete reversal is uncommon if the changes are long-standing. 6 Surgical skin procedures, such as excisions and resections with or without grafts or flap reconstructions, are reserved for aesthetic distress after achieving optimal hormonal control. 4

This unique case demonstrated a pituitary tumor causing acromegaly and the rare cutaneous manifestation of CVG. In this patient’s situation, the collaboration of dermatology, endocrinology, neurosurgery, and otolaryngology led to prompt identification, diagnosis, intervention, and treatment, illustrating the importance of multidisciplinary care.
