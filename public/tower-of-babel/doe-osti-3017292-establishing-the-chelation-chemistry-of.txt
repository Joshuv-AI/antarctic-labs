Narrative Final Report for DE-SC0023012
Title: Establishing the Chelation Chemistry of Antimony-119 for Targeted Auger Therapy
PI: Nikki Thiele, Oak Ridge National Laboratory
Co-II: Jonathan Engle

Abstract/Summary: No chelators have been identified to stabilize the promising low energy electron
emitting radionuclide 119Sb in vivo, halting its clinical advancement. The project objective was to develop
bifunctional chelating agents for 119Sb that can be implemented to deliver this therapeutic radionuclide to
micrometastases and single-cell disease. The Project was conceived with three specific aims:
 1) Synthesize chelating agent candidates for Sb(III) or Sb(V)
 2) Evaluate radiolabeling and kinetic stability of chelators with 1XXSb
 3) Synthesize and evaluate bifunctional chelators for targeted delivery of 1XXSb in vivo
This report summarizes the success of the project, which include providing primary support for the
completed doctoral dissertation research of a University of Wisconsin Medical Physics researcher, three
peer-reviewed publications substantively advancing the field of antimony chelation for biomedical
applications, one conference presentation, and one submitted patent describing newly synthesized ligands
purpose built for this task.

Introduction: Targeted radionuclide therapy (TRT) is an emerging cancer treatment strategy that harnesses
the radioactive emissions of beta (β) particles, alpha (α) particles, or Auger electrons in combination with
tumor-seeking biological targeting vectors to selectively deliver a lethal dose of ionizing radiation to cancer
cells. Each of these radioactive emissions possess distinct physical and radiobiological properties that
render them uniquely suited for treating different types of cancers. β particles travel up to a millimeter from
the site of nuclear decay and have low linear energy transfer, making them best suited for treating large
solid tumors, whereas α particles have high linear energy transfer and micrometer pathlengths, rendering
them well-matched for the treatment of smaller tumor burdens. Auger electrons, on the other hand, possess
ultra-short pathlengths of only nanometers to a few micrometers, confining their deposited energy to a
single cell or subcellular dimensions. Thus, in contrast to α and β particles, Auger electrons have the
potential to give rise to highly targeted radionuclide therapies that are unparalleled for the treatment of
micrometastases and single-cell disease. The development of treatments for these types of cancers
represents an unmet clinical need in oncology medicine. Among the few Auger-emitting radioisotopes that
possess properties suitable for use in targeted Auger therapy applications, antimony-119 (119Sb) has
emerged as a highly promising candidate whose production is currently being supported by the DOE Isotope
Program. 119Sb emits an average of 23.7 low-energy electrons per decay and only a single gamma photon
of low energy. These emissions are ideal for maximizing therapeutic destruction of single cells while
minimizing off-target irradiation of neighboring healthy tissue. A key challenge hindering the
implementation of 119Sb in targeted Auger therapy, however, is the lack of bifunctional chelators that can
rapidly bind and stably retain this radioisotope to targeting vectors in vivo. The objective of this proposal
were to develop bifunctional chelating agents for 119Sb that can be implemented to deliver this Auger emitter
to micrometastases and single-cell disease.

Six Milestones were developed to scaffold progress towards completion of the three objectives described
above:
1) Synthesize at least 3 ligands for Sb chelation
2) Characterize solution and solid-state chemistry of ≥1 chelator w/ natSb
3) Execute the first radiolabeling of a ligand with 1XXSb in the +5-oxidation state and develop optimal
labeling conditions.
4) Identify 1 chelator that can stably retain >90% of 1XXSb over one half-life
5) Synthesize 1 bifunctional chelator and conjugate it to a tumor-targeting vector
6) Execute in vivo studies to demonstrate delivery of radio-Sb to tumors for the first time.

Results: Each of these milestones were completed and shared with the scientific community and their
results summarized in publications summarized below. In brief, the TREN-CAM family of chelators was
found to exhibit marked stability for Sb complexes with both stable and radioactive workups following
cyclotron production. These stabilities diminished in physiological media, but the collaboration was
nevertheless able to demonstrate the first non-invasive imaging of no-carrier-added antimony complexes in
vivo with both single photon computed tomography (SPECT) and positron emission tomography (PET)
imaging experiments.
