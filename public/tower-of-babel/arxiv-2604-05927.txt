Quantitative stability of constant equilibria in a non-linear alignment model of self-propelled particles
Émeric Bouin, Amic Frouvelle

Abstract
. We are interested in the long-time behaviour of the kinetic Vicsek equation, rigorously derived as the mean-field limit [ 3 ] of a coupled system of N N stochastic differential equations describing particles moving at unit velocity and aligning with their neighbours. We focus on the local-in-space version (that may for instance appear as a moderate interaction limit instead of mean-field), which is not a priori globally well-posed and could explode in finite time. Despite its simple expression, little is rigorously established about the behaviour of its solutions. We use hypocoercivity methods to show that finite time explosion does not occur in the vicinity of uniform and homogeneous equilibria in space below the critical threshold. We recast the now-classic [ 23 ] approach of modifying Sobolev-type norms by adding cross-terms, linked to commutators between the different operators appearing in the kinetic equation. However, the fact that the velocity space is the sphere adds significant subtleties and requires to develop an adapted algebraic framework of operators. Taking advantage of this new framework, we manage to perform an approach à la Hérau [ 16 ] to show the nonlinear stability. Our main results are a quantitative decay estimate in the case of the whole space, despite the absence of control of the L 1 L^{1} norm of the perturbation, and a gain in regularity at the nonlinear level which allows to have well-posedness and stability in the space H s , 0 ​ ( ℝ d × 𝕊 ) H^{s,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}) for some s < d 2 s<\frac{d}{2} (that is to say without a priori uniform bound in space on the L 2 L^{2} norm in velocity, that would come with Sobolev injection in the case s > d 2 s>\frac{d}{2} ).



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we are interested in qualitative properties of the following Vlasov–Fokker–Planck type model for alignment of self-propelled particles

(1.1)

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\mathsf{\nabla}_{v}\cdot(\mathsf{\nabla}_{v}(\mathbb{J}[f]\cdot v)\,f)=\mathsf{\Delta}_{v}f,

where
f(t,x,v)
represents the density at time
t\geqslant 0
of particles at position
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(or in a flat torus of dimension
d
, with
d>1
) having velocity
v\in\mathbb{S}
. There,
\mathbb{S}
denotes the unit sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the operators
\mathsf{\nabla}_{v}
,
\mathsf{\nabla}_{v}\cdot
and
\mathsf{\Delta}_{v}
stand for the gradient, the divergence and the Laplacian of a function on the sphere, respectively. Finally
\mathbb{J}[f]
denotes the first moment in velocity of
f
, given by

(1.2)

\mathbb{J}[f]=\int_{\mathbb{S}}vf\,\mathrm{d}v,

where the measure on the sphere is the Lebesgue measure normalized to be of total mass one.

This evolution equation is the spatially localized version of the kinetic Vicsek equation, which models the alignment of self-propelled particles moving at constant speed, aligning their velocities with neighbors by dipolar potential and subject to angular noise.

More precisely, let us consider the following stochastic differential system of
N
interacting particles

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}X_{k}=c\,V_{k}\,\mathrm{d}t,\\
\mathrm{d}V_{k}=\frac{\nu}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{R^{d}}K\big(\frac{1}{R}(X_{i}-X_{j})\big)\nabla_{V_{k}}(V_{i}\cdot V_{k})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\sigma}\,P_{V_{k}^{\perp}}\circ\mathrm{d}B_{t,k},\end{cases}

in which the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
particle, at position
X_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(or in a flat torus of dimension
d
), moves at constant speed
c
in the direction of its orientation
V_{k}\in\mathbb{S}
; this orientation
V_{k}
has a drift of intensity
\nu
towards the orientations of other neighbouring particles (the potential
V_{i}\cdot V_{k}
is maximized when
V_{k}=V_{i}
) weighted by the observation kernel
K
(a probability measure) which emphasizes the contributions of particles located within distance of order
R
; finally the orientation is subject to an angular noise of intensity
\sqrt{2\sigma}
, under the form of the contribution
P_{V_{k}^{\perp}}\circ\mathrm{d}B_{t,k}
of a Brownian motion on the sphere (here
P_{V_{k}^{\perp}}
is the projection on the orthogonal of
V_{k}
,
B_{t,k}
are independent brownian motions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\circ
denotes the fact that the stochastic differential equation must be understood in Stratonovich formulation, see
[
17
]
for more on this topic). This is a time-continuous version (see
[
11
]
for instance) of the Vicsek model
[
22
]
, which was originally designed as a discrete in time synchronous jump process for the velocities.

When the number
N
of particles is large, the empirical distribution of the system of stochastic differential equations (
1.3
) converges to the solution of the following equation
[
3
]
:

(1.4)

\partial_{t}f+c\,v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\nu\,\mathsf{\nabla}_{v}\cdot(\mathsf{\nabla}_{v}((K_{R}*_{x}\mathbb{J}[f])\cdot v)\,f)=\sigma\mathsf{\Delta}_{v}f,

where
K_{R}*_{x}\mathbb{J}[f]
is the spatial convolution between the scaled kernel
\frac{1}{R^{d}}K(\frac{1}{R}\cdot)
and the moment
\mathbb{J}[f]
defined in (
1.2
).

With a careful change of scale in time and space, and by multiplying
f
by a positive scalar
\rho
(not considering it as a probability density anymore), we may take without loss of generality
c=\nu=\sigma=1
, and therefore the evolution (
1.1
) is simply the formal limit of (
1.4
) as
R\to 0
.

Both limits
N\to\infty
and
R\to 0
may also be taken simultaneously, by replacing
R
by
R_{N}
in (
1.3
), and letting
R_{N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. In the “moderate” interaction regime, when
R_{N}^{d}\cdot N\to\infty
(which means that each particle interacts with a large number of particles), we expect that the empirical distribution still converges to a solution of (
1.1
). The methods used to obtain this kind of limit to a local equation in space require a well-posed limit system (see for example
[
21
,
18
,
13
,
8
,
7
]
).

Equation (
1.1
) also naturally appears in the formal derivation of macroscopic limits of the kinetic Vicsek equation
[
10
]
. Indeed, a hydrodynamic scaling by a parameter
\varepsilon
in space and time leads to a rescaled equation with
R_{\varepsilon}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, leading at the main order to the localized version of the operator.

The well-posedness and the long time behaviour of the spatial-homogeneous version of (
1.1
) is now well understood
[
14
]
. There is a phase transition depending on the density
\rho=\int_{\mathbb{S}}f\mathrm{d}v
. When
\rho\leqslant d
(or if we keep the parameters as in (
1.4
), when
\frac{\nu\rho}{\sigma}\leqslant d
, which means either a low alignment strength or a high level of noise), all solutions converge towards the isotropic distribution on the sphere, exponentially fast if
\rho<d
. When
\rho>d
, except for the initial conditions
f^{0}
such that
\mathbb{J}[f^{0}]=0
, the solutions all converge towards a von Mises equilibrium on the sphere, concentrated around a given orientation, which depends on the initial condition, but in a non-explicit way.

Regarding the space-inhomogeneous model (
1.1
), to our knowledge, until recently, few results were available for this equation. The recent work
[
5
]
deals with a more generic class of kinetic equations for self-propelled particles. It can be straightly adapted to obtain the local in time well-posedness of the model in
\mathsf{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S})
, that is, existence and uniqueness of a bounded weak solution to (
1.1
). We also provide a quantitative pointwise (in space) estimate on the
L^{\infty}
norm which allows to observe the finite speed of propagation.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given any nonnegative initial condition
f^{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S})
, there exists a unique weak solution to (
1.1
) in
\mathcal{C}([0,T],L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}))
for all
T<\frac{\sqrt{d}}{(d-1)\|f^{0}\|_{\infty}}
. It is nonnegative and satisfies for all
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the following estimate,

(1.5)

\|f(t,x,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S})}\leqslant\|f^{0}\|_{\infty,B(x,t)\times\mathbb{S}}+(d-1)\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathbb{J}[f](s)\|_{\infty,B(x,s-t)}\|f(s)\|_{\infty,B(x,s-t)\times\mathbb{S}}\,\mathrm{d}s,

where the left-hand side denotes the limit of
\|f(t)\|_{\infty,B(x,r)\times\mathbb{S}}
as
r\to 0
.

In our discussion, the density
f
is not assumed to have finite total mass (if
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
). Observe that
f\equiv\rho
is a stationary solution to (
1.1
). We are interested in the behaviour around this constant stationary solution. As said before, when
\rho<d
, this is the only homogeneous stationary solution in space, since it is then a stationary solution to the spatial-homogeneous version of (
1.1
). The main goal of the present paper is to prove that the stability result of this isotropic distribution is also true in the space-inhomogeneous framework. Our aim is to show an energy – energy-dissipation estimate on
g
, implying that the solution is global for small perturbations. Our strategy is to follow an hypocoercive-hypoelliptic approach based on high order Sobolev norms.

As we can see in the estimate (
1.5
), we need a
L^{\infty}
bound on
\mathbb{J}[f]
, which is itself controlled by the
L^{2}
norm in velocity, so we will not actually need to control derivatives in
v
. And regarding the
L^{\infty}
norm in
x
, a natural Sobolev space would therefore be
H^{s,0}
(defined through
\|f\|_{H^{s,0}}^{2}=\|f\|_{2}^{2}+\|(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{s}{2}}f\|_{2}^{2})
, with
s>\frac{d}{2}
, thanks to Sobolev injections. However, the regularizing properties of the equation (of hypoelliptic type), and the fact that the nonlinearity is governed by moments in velocity allows us to gain some derivatives in space and prove the local well-posedness in a larger space, which is one of the main results of the present paper.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
s>\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{4}
for
d\geqslant 3
, or
s>\frac{7}{8}
in the case
d=2
. For any
M>0
, there exists a time
T_{M}>0
such that for any
f^{0}\in H^{s,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S})
satisfying
\|f^{0}\|_{H^{s,0}}\leqslant M
, there exists a unique weak solution
f
to (
1.1
) in
C([0,T_{M}),H^{s,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}))
with initial condition
f^{0}
. Furthermore the solution depends continuously on the initial condition
f^{0}
, and for any
T<T_{M}
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
t\in(0,T]
, we have,

\|\nabla_{v}f\|_{H^{s,0}}\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{t}}\|f^{0}\|_{H^{s,0}},\quad\|P_{v^{\perp}}\nabla_{x}f\|_{H^{s,0}}\leqslant\frac{C}{t^{\frac{3}{2}}}\|f^{0}\|_{H^{s,0}},\quad\|v\cdot\nabla_{x}f\|_{H^{s,0}}\leqslant\frac{C}{t^{2}}\|f^{0}\|_{H^{s,0}}.

The time
T_{M}
can be explicitly computed (depending on
d
,
s
and
M
), as well as the constant
C
(which depends only on
d
,
s
and
\frac{T}{T_{M}}
).

The solutions are then actually strong solutions, which are smooth for arbitrary small times, and the finite speed of propagation allows then to define solutions for
f^{0}\in H^{s,0}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S})
, as soon as the local time of existence is bounded below, which is for instance the case if
\|f^{0}\|_{H^{s,0}(B(x,1)\times\mathbb{S})}
is uniformly bounded in
x
. Therefore we look at a small perturbation of the constant solution
\rho
by looking at an initial condition
f^{0}=\rho+g^{0}
where
g^{0}\in H^{s,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S})
, which ensures local in time existence and uniqueness of a solution
f=\rho+g
. The second main result of our work is a quantitative stability result up to the threshold of phase transition, with explicit decay rates.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
s>\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{4}
for
d\geqslant 3
, or
s>\frac{7}{8}
in the case
d=2
, and let
\rho<d
. There exist positive constants
\eta
,
C
and
\epsilon
(and
\lambda
in the cas of a flat torus), only depending on
d
,
\rho
and
s
, such that for any initial condition
g^{0}
such that
\|g^{0}\|_{H^{s,0}}\leqslant\eta
, the solution
f
of equation (
1.1
) with initial condition
f^{0}=\rho+g^{0}
is global, and satisfies the following decay estimates:

(i)

When the domain is a flat torus (and
g
has zero average), exponential decay in large time: for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\|g(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{s,0}}\leqslant C\|g^{0}\|_{H^{s,0}}e^{-\lambda t}.

(ii)

Algebraic decay controlled by spatial
L^{2}
moments in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
: for all
t\geqslant 0

\|g(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{s,0}}\leqslant C\frac{\|g^{0}\|_{H^{s,0}}}{\big(1+\frac{\|g^{0}\|^{2}_{H^{s,0}}}{\|g^{0}\|^{2}_{H^{s,0}}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}|x|^{2}(g^{0}(x,v))^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}v}\,t\big)^{\epsilon}}.

It is worth noticing that one important structural difficulty that we have manage to overcome is the fact that the nonlinear operator (and even the linearized counterpart) driving the solution
g
(see (
6.1
)) does not conserve positivity nor the
\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}}
norm. This makes a significant difference with,
e.g.
,
[
4
]
where Nash inequalities and mode-by-mode estimates could be used in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. One may also observe this difficulty in a even more recent paper by Carrapatoso and Gervais
[
6
]
on the Boltzmann equation in the full space, where rates of decay in
L^{2}
are not obtained without using higher
L^{p}
spaces, see
[
6
, Theorem 1.1]
. We shall now comment on the way we prove these results. The main difficulty to construct an hypocoercivity and hypoellipticity functional
à la
Villani
[
23
]
is due to the fact that the velocities lie on a strict submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. One can be convinced, taking a simple example in dimension
2
, that the standard chain of commutators of
[
23
]
is not enough. Let us consider the law of the kinetic Brownian motion (also called velocity spherical Brownian motion), corresponding to (
1.3
) with
\nu=0
and
\sigma=1
: a single particle moving at constant unit speed, with velocity given by a Brownian motion on the sphere. Writing in a comfortable way for the sequel

, with
\theta\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathsf{T}=\binom{\cos{\theta}}{\sin\theta}\cdot\nabla_{x}
and
\mathsf{A}=\partial_{\theta}
, we obtain

\partial_{t}f+\mathsf{T}f=\mathsf{A}^{2}f.

Villani’s chain of commutators would be to write
\mathsf{C}_{0}=\mathsf{A}
,
\mathsf{C}_{i+1}=[\mathsf{C}_{i},\mathsf{T}]
and hope to obtain the missing directions. But by noting
\mathsf{S}=[\mathsf{A},\mathsf{T}]=\binom{-\sin{\theta}}{\cos\theta}\cdot\nabla_{x}
, we would then obtain
\mathsf{C}_{1}=\mathsf{S}
and
\mathsf{C}_{2}=0
. This chain of commutators does not cover all the directions required to satisfy the parabolic hypoellipticity condition of Hörmander; to satisfy it we must not consider
[\mathsf{S},\mathsf{T}]
(which is zero) but use again a commutation with
\mathsf{A}
, getting that
[\mathsf{A},\mathsf{S}]=-\mathsf{T}
covers the missing direction.

In dimensions greater than

Theorem 1.4

There exist positive constants

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\mathsf{\Delta}_{v}f

(i)

Short-time regularizing estimates: for all

\|\nabla_{v}f\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{t}}\|f^{0}\|_{L^{2}},\quad\|P_{v^{\perp}}\nabla_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\frac{C}{t^{\frac{3}{2}}}\|f^{0}\|_{L^{2}},\quad\|v\cdot\nabla_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\frac{C}{t^{2}}\|f^{0}\|_{L^{2}}.

(ii)

When the domain is a flat torus (and

\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}}\leqslant C\|f^{0}\|_{H^{1}}e^{-\lambda t}.

(iii)

Algebraic decay controlled by

: for all

,

\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}}\leqslant C\frac{\|f^{0}\|_{H^{1}}}{\big(1+\min(1,\frac{\|f^{0}\|_{H^{1}}}{\|f^{0}\|_{L^{1}}})^{\frac{4}{d}}\,t\big)^{\frac{d}{4}}}.

(iv)

Let us now comment on two very recent works that are related to the present paper.

In
[
19
]
, the authors consider the localized Vicsek-BGK equation on the torus, for which the phase transition behaviour at the homogeneous level is similar to the case of Fokker–Planck (though the orientation of
\mathbb{J}
is preserved in time, which helps to describe the long-time behaviour). They prove that it is well-posed in
H^{s,0}
with
s>\frac{d}{2}
, which is natural since we do not expect any regularizing effect from the BGK relaxation operator. Regarding the long-time behaviour, they prove nonlinear stability of the homegeneous states up to the threshold and even a little bit after the threshold (by a perturbation argument). However a crucial assumption is that they need a sufficiently large speed of the particle (or equivalently, a sufficiently small torus). We believe that our method can be used to prove similar results on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, when
\rho<d
. Some preliminary computations indicate that, at least at the linearized level, we have a nonincreasing energy
\mathcal{F}
equivalent to the
H^{1}
norm, and that a quantitative decay in time such as in
Theorem

1.4
-
(iv)
is available. A more precise study in this direction is left to a future work.

In the very recent paper
[
15
]
, the authors consider the kinetic Vicsek equation on the torus with a slightly more general (but smooth) kernel of observation. They obtain similar results as
[
19
]
, that may persist a little bit after the threshold of phase transition. They also study effects of enhanced diffusion, which corresponds to the regime where the interaction operator is very weak compared to the free transport (or equivalently, with a small torus or a high speed). The kernel of observation may be compactly supported in space, but the constants depend on this kernel, so the intensity of the collision operator have to be very small as the kernel is close to a Dirac mass, and we do not believe their approach may be easily extended to our localized equation.

1.1.
Useful formulas on the sphere

In order to help a reader that would not be familiar with differential calculus on the sphere, we found relevant to recall some useful formulas (see
[
14
]
for proofs) that we may use a lot without further notice. For smooth
f,g:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
,
F:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and any
\mathcal{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one has:

(1.6)

\nabla_{v}(v\cdot\mathcal{J})=P_{v^{\perp}}\mathcal{J},\qquad\nabla_{v}\cdot(P_{v^{\perp}}\mathcal{J})=-(d-1)v\cdot\mathcal{J},

and

\int_{\mathbb{S}}F(v)\cdot\nabla_{v}f\mathrm{d}v=-\int_{\mathbb{S}}\nabla_{v}\cdot(P_{v^{\perp}}F(v))f(v)\,\mathrm{d}v,

From there, one gets very important identities for what follows:

(1.7)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}}\nabla_{v}g\,\mathrm{d}v=(d-1)\int_{\mathbb{S}}v\,g\,\mathrm{d}v,

(1.8)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}}f\,\nabla_{v}g\,\mathrm{d}v=\int_{\mathbb{S}}g\,[(d-1)vf-\nabla_{v}f]\,\mathrm{d}v.

We also have (for instance from (
1.7
) with
g=v\cdot\mathcal{J}
):

(1.9)

|\mathcal{J}|^{2}=d\int_{\mathbb{S}}(v\cdot\mathcal{J})^{2}\mathrm{d}v=\frac{d}{d-1}\int_{\mathbb{S}}|P_{v^{\perp}}\mathcal{J}|^{2}\mathrm{d}v,

and therefore we obtain that
f-d\,v\cdot\mathbb{J}[f]
and
d\,v\cdot\mathbb{J}[f]
are orthogonal in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
, which means that the orthogonal projection
\mathsf{\Pi}_{1}f
on spherical harmonics of degree
1
(that is to say functions of the form
v\mapsto\mathcal{J}\cdot v
) is given by

(1.10)

\mathsf{\Pi}_{1}f(v)=d\,v\cdot\mathbb{J}[f],

and providing

(1.11)

|\mathbb{J}[f]|^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\int(d\,v\cdot\mathbb{J}[f])^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v\leqslant\frac{1}{d}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}.

2.
Cauchy problem in
L^{\infty}
: existence, uniqueness, and finite speed propagation

This section is devoted to show how the strategy of the recent work
[
5
]
can be directly adapted to obtain
Theorem

1.1
. The main addition (which were not explicitly written in
[
5
]
but quite straightforward from their proofs) are the explicit estimation (
1.5
) and the fact that the initial condition does not need to be in
L^{2}
.

We recall their strategy here. First of all, using the same method as in
[
12
, Appendix A]
, one may solve, for a prescribed
\mathcal{J}\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and initial condition
f^{0}\in L^{2}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S})
, the following linear equation:

(2.1)

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\nabla_{v}\cdot(P_{v^{\perp}}\mathcal{J}f)=\mathsf{\Delta}_{v}f.

Writing this equation as

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\mathcal{J}\cdot\nabla_{v}f-\mathsf{\Delta}_{v}f=(d-1)\left(v\cdot\mathcal{J}\right)\,f,

and considering the right-hand side as a source term, we obtain the following estimate that comes from the maximum principle:

(2.2)

\|f(t)\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|f^{0}\|_{\infty}+(d-1)\int_{0}^{t}\||\mathcal{J}(s)|f(s)\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}s,

which gives, after using the Grönwall lemma (integral form),

\|f(t)\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|f^{0}\|_{\infty}\exp\Big((d-1)\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathcal{J}(s)\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big).

Furthermore if
f^{0}
is nonnegative, we obtain that
f
is also nonnegative.

From there, we shall use a fixed-point iteration. Indeed, since we have existence and uniqueness of a solution to (
2.1
) in
C([0,T],L^{2}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}))
, a weak solution to our nonlinear model (
1.1
) corresponds to a weak solution
f
of (
2.1
) satisfying furthermore
\mathcal{J}=\mathbb{J}[f]
.

We therefore denote

\Omega=\Big\{f\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}),\quad\forall t\in[0,T],\qquad\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{1}{\|f^{0}\|_{\infty}^{-1}-\frac{d-1}{\sqrt{d}}t}\Big\}.

From there, and using (
1.11
), we get that if
f_{n}\in\Omega
, then
f_{n+1}
solving (
2.1
) with
\mathcal{J}=\mathbb{J}[f_{n}]
satisfies

\displaystyle\|f_{n+1}(t)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|f^{0}\|_{\infty}\exp\left((d-1)\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathbb{J}[f_{n}](s)\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\|f^{0}\|_{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{d-1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\Big[\frac{\sqrt{d}}{d-1}\ln\big(\|f^{0}\|_{\infty}^{-1}-\frac{d-1}{\sqrt{d}}s\big)\Big]_{0}^{t}\right)=\frac{1}{\|f^{0}\|_{\infty}^{-1}-\frac{d-1}{\sqrt{d}}t},

and thus we get that
f_{n+1}\in\Omega
. By careful weak compactness, one may then extract a subsequence that converges to a weak solution
f
, thus proving the existence.

For uniqueness, let us improve a little bit the result of
[
5
]
, using the fact that information propagates at speed less than
1
, in order to prove furthermore the existence when we do not suppose
f^{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S})
. We suppose
f
and
\widetilde{f}
are two solutions in
C([0,T],L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}))
, with initial conditions
f^{0}
and
\widetilde{f}^{0}
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S})
. Denoting
u=f-\widetilde{f}
their difference, we obtain

\partial_{t}u+v\cdot\nabla_{x}u=\mathsf{\Delta}_{v}u-\nabla_{v}\cdot(P_{v^{\perp}}\mathbb{J}[f]u)-\nabla_{v}\cdot(P_{v^{\perp}}\mathbb{J}[u]\widetilde{f}).

Let
\varphi
be a smooth nonnegative compactly supported function of
x
and
t
, we compute

(2.3)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}\varphi u^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}(\partial_{t}\varphi+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi)u^{2}-\varphi\Big(|\nabla_{v}u|^{2}+\tfrac{d-1}{2}u^{2}\mathbb{J}[f]\cdot v-\widetilde{f}\mathbb{J}[u]\cdot\nabla_{v}u\Big).

We now take
\varphi(x,t)=\chi(|x-x_{0}|+t)
where
\chi
is a smooth non-increasing and nonnegative function, in order to obtain
\partial_{t}\varphi+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi=(1+v\cdot\frac{x-x_{0}}{|x-x_{0}|})\chi^{\prime}(|x-x_{0}|+t)\leqslant 0
, since
|v|\leqslant 1
.

By denoting
C(t)=\max\big(\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty},\|\widetilde{f}(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\big)
, we have that
\widetilde{f}\mathbb{J}[u]\cdot\nabla_{v}u\leqslant\frac{1}{4}C(t)^{2}|\mathbb{J}[u]|^{2}+|\nabla_{v}u|^{2}
, and therefore using (
1.11
), we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}\varphi u^{2}\leqslant(\tfrac{d-1}{2}C(t)+\tfrac{1}{4d}C(t)^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}\varphi u^{2}.

Solving this Grönwall estimate, we get
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}\varphi u^{2}\leqslant\widetilde{C}(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}\varphi^{0}(u^{0})^{2}
. Taking now
\chi(r)=0
for
r\geqslant R
and
\chi(r)=1
for
r\in[0,R-\varepsilon]
, we have in the limit

(2.4)

\int_{B(x_{0},R-t)\times\mathbb{S}}|f-\widetilde{f}|^{2}\leqslant\widetilde{C}(t)\int_{B(x_{0},R)\times\mathbb{S}}|f^{0}-\widetilde{f}^{0}|^{2}.

This gives the uniqueness of the solution, and furthermore provides a way to define a solution for any initial condition in
L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S})
. We proceed by truncating: if
\widetilde{f}^{0}=\mathbf{1}_{|x-x_{0}|\leqslant R+T}f^{0}
, we set
\widetilde{f}
the solution with initial condition
\widetilde{f}^{0}
and we get that if
f
is a solution with initial condition
f^{0}
, it should satisfy
f(t,v,x)=\widetilde{f}(t,v,x)
for
|x-x_{0}|\leqslant R
. This gives the definition of
f
for any
x
on
[0,T]
by taking
R
sufficiently large (we recall that
T
only depends on the initial
L^{\infty}
norm of
f^{0}
and therefore
\widetilde{f}
is well-defined on
[0,T]
for any
R
). We directly check that it gives a weak solution, since the test functions have compact support, using the fact that
\widetilde{f}
is itself a weak solution.

Finally, to prove the estimate (
1.5
), we start by proving that if
\widetilde{f}
is the solution to the linear equation (
2.1
) with initial condition
\widetilde{f}^{0}=\mathbf{1}_{|x-x_{0}|\leqslant t_{0}}f^{0}
and with
\mathcal{J}(t,x)=\mathbf{1}_{|x-x_{0}|+t\leqslant t_{0}}\mathbb{J}[f](t,x)
, then
\widetilde{f}
and the solution
f
to the nonlinear problem (
1.1
) agree on the set
\{|x-x_{0}|+t\leqslant t_{0}\}=B_{x_{0},t_{0}-t}\times\mathbb{S}
. To this aim, we proceed very similarly as for the uniqueness. The computations are similar, still denoting
u=f-\widetilde{f}
, and
\varphi=\chi(|x-x_{0}|+t)
, with
\chi
smooth, nonincreasing, such that
\chi(r)=0
for
r\geqslant t_{0}
and
\chi(r)=1
for
r\in[0,t_{0}-\varepsilon]
. We end up with the same equation as (
2.3
) where
\mathbb{J}[u]
is replaced by
(\mathbb{J}[f]-\mathcal{J})
. But since this last quantity is zero on the support of
\varphi
, we do not even have to worry about this term and the same strategy leads to the same estimate as (
2.4
) with
t_{0}
instead of
R
.

We can then directly use the estimate (
2.2
) for
\widetilde{f}
, and we get

\displaystyle\|f(t)\|_{\infty,B(x_{0},t_{0}-t)\times\mathbb{S}}

\displaystyle=\|\widetilde{f}(t)\|_{\infty,B(x_{0},t_{0}-t)\times\mathbb{S}}\leqslant\|\widetilde{f}(t)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\widetilde{f}^{0}\|_{\infty}+(d-1)\int_{0}^{t}\||\mathcal{J}(s)|\widetilde{f}(s)\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle\leqslant\|f^{0}\|_{\infty,B(x_{0},t_{0})\times\mathbb{S}}+(d-1)\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathbb{J}[f](s)\|_{\infty,B(x_{0},t_{0}-s)\times\mathbb{S}}\|f(s)\|_{\infty,B(x_{0},t_{0}-s)\times\mathbb{S}}\,\mathrm{d}s,

therefore letting
t\to t_{0}
and using the continuity of
t\mapsto\|f(t)\|_{\infty}
, we obtain the desired estimate (
1.5
), which ends the proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

3.
Algebraic framework: angular momentum operator and
2
-vectors

The object of this section is to rewrite our kinetic equation (
1.1
) under the following condensed form:

(3.1)

\partial_{t}f+\mathsf{T}f+\frac{1}{d}\,\mathsf{A}(f\mathsf{U}f)=\mathsf{A}^{2}f,

where
\mathsf{T}=v\cdot\nabla_{x}
is the transport operator,
\mathsf{A}
is an angular momentum operator and
\mathsf{U}
is a linear operator related to the alignment term. The reason we introduce these operators
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{U}
rely on their useful properties in terms of antisymmetry and commutation behaviour, summarized in the forthcoming
Lemmas

3.3
and
3.4
.

These operators act on functions from
\mathbb{S}
to
\mathbb{R}
and return functions from
\mathbb{S}
to
\bigwedge^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the space of so-called
2
-vectors, which is simply a euclidean space with orthonormal basis denoted
(e_{i}\wedge e_{j})_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant d}
, where
(e_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. There is a natural bilinear and antisymmetric map called exterior product, from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\bigwedge^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, given, for two vectors
u=(u_{i})
and
w=(w_{i})
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, by

u\wedge w=\sum_{i<j}(u_{i}w_{j}-u_{j}w_{i})\,e_{i}\wedge e_{j},

which is compatible with the notation
e_{i}\wedge e_{j}
for the elements of the basis.

Remark 3.1
.

An useful observation with respect to the (bilinear, antisymmetric) exterior product is the following: if
v,w,\tilde{v},\tilde{w}
are four vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a direct computation shows that

(v\wedge w)\cdot(\tilde{v}\wedge\tilde{w})=\sum_{i<j}(v_{i}w_{j}-v_{j}w_{i})(\tilde{v}_{i}\tilde{w}_{j}-\tilde{v}_{j}\tilde{w}_{i})=\sum_{i\neq j}v_{i}\tilde{v}_{i}w_{j}\tilde{w}_{j}-v_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}\tilde{v}_{j}w_{j}=(v\cdot\tilde{v})(w\cdot\tilde{w})-(v\cdot\tilde{w})(\tilde{v}\cdot w).

Consequently, if
v\in\mathbb{S}
and
v\cdot w=0
or
v\cdot\tilde{w}=0
, then
(v\wedge w)\cdot(v\wedge\tilde{w})=w\cdot\tilde{w}
.

We are now ready to define our linear operators
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{U}
, which have very concise forms once we have introduced this convenient framework.

Definition 3.2
.

For a smooth function
f
on
\mathbb{S}
, we define
\mathsf{A}f
and
\mathsf{U}f
by

\forall v\in\mathbb{S},\qquad(\mathsf{A}f)(v)=v\wedge\nabla_{v}f(v),\qquad(\mathsf{U}f)(v)=d\,v\wedge\mathbb{J}[f].

We may describe
\mathsf{A}
component-wise as follows. We denote
v_{i}
the function
v\in\mathbb{S}\mapsto v\cdot e_{i}
. Then, for a smooth function
f
on
\mathbb{S}
, we have
\mathsf{A}f=\sum_{i<j}\mathsf{A}_{i,j}f\,(e_{i}\wedge e_{j}),
where for
i\neq j
,
\mathsf{A}_{i,j}
is the following linear operator of degree
1
on the sphere:

(3.2)

\mathsf{A}_{i,j}=v_{i}\,e_{j}\cdot\nabla_{v}-v_{j}\,e_{i}\cdot\nabla_{v},

which corresponds to the infinitesimal generator of the rotation in the plane generated by
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
, sending
e_{i}
onto
e_{j}
. Said differently, if we write
f(v)=\widetilde{f}(\varphi,w)
when
v=\sin\theta(\cos\varphi e_{i}+\sin\varphi e_{j})+\cos\theta\,w
where
w\in\mathbb{S}\cap\{e_{i},e_{j}\}^{\perp}
, then
\mathsf{A}_{i,j}f(v)=\partial_{\varphi}\widetilde{f}(\varphi,w)
(in particular in dimension
d=2
, we recover the setting given in the introduction, with
\mathsf{A}=\partial_{\theta}
for
v=\binom{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}
).

In all what follows, we will only consider scalar operators or operators with values in
\bigwedge^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(such as
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{U}
), and any composition
\mathsf{B}\widetilde{\mathsf{B}}
between two such objects has to be understood as their (scalar) contraction
1
1

1

When more than one composition is involved, to avoid ambiguity, we use parentheses to depict the order of contractions; for instance
\mathsf{B}\widetilde{\mathsf{B}}^{2}\mathsf{B}=\sum_{i<j}\sum_{k<\ell}\mathsf{B}_{i,j}\widetilde{\mathsf{B}}_{k,\ell}^{2}\mathsf{B}_{i,j}
while
(\mathsf{B}\widetilde{\mathsf{B}})(\widetilde{\mathsf{B}}\mathsf{B})=\sum_{i<j}\sum_{k<\ell}\mathsf{B}_{i,j}\widetilde{\mathsf{B}}_{i,j}\widetilde{\mathsf{B}}_{k,\ell}\mathsf{B}_{k,\ell}
.

\sum_{i<j}\mathsf{B}_{i,j}\widetilde{\mathsf{B}}_{i,j}
. In particular, the terms appearing in (
3.1
) have to be understood as

\mathsf{A}^{2}f=\sum_{i<j}\mathsf{A}_{i,j}^{2}f,\qquad\mathsf{A}(f\mathsf{U}f)=\sum_{i<j}\mathsf{A}_{i,j}(f\mathsf{U}_{i,j}f).

The useful properties regarding
\mathsf{A}
, the gradient and the Laplace–Beltrami operators on the sphere are given in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

Fixing
1\leqslant i,j,k,\ell\leqslant d
with
i\neq j
and
k\neq\ell
, we have the following results.

(i)

The operator
\mathsf{A}_{i,j}
is antiselfadjoint on
\mathbb{S}
.

(ii)

If
f,g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{S})
, then
\nabla_{v}f\cdot\nabla_{v}g=\mathsf{A}f\cdot\mathsf{A}g
. Consequently if
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{S})
, then
\mathsf{\Delta}_{v}f=\mathsf{A}^{2}f
.

(iii)

We have
\mathsf{A}_{i,j}v_{k}=\delta_{jk}v_{i}-\delta_{ik}v_{j}
.

(iv)

If
\{i,j\}\cap\{k,\ell\}=\emptyset
, then
[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}]=0
. If
k\notin\{i,j\}
, then
[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{k,j}]=-\mathsf{A}_{i,k}
. As a consequence, we have that
[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}^{2}]=0
, which means that
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{\Delta}_{v}
commute.

Before proving this lemma, let us remark that the first two properties
(i)
–
(ii)
(together with
Remark

3.1
) directly justify the condensed formula (
3.1
) of our kinetic equation (
1.1
), as for any smooth function
g
and
f
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}}g\sum_{i<j}\frac{1}{d}\,

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{i,j}(f\mathsf{U_{i,j}}f)\,\mathrm{d}v=-\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{1}{d}\,\sum_{i<j}\mathsf{A}_{i,j}gf\mathsf{U}_{i,j}f\,\mathrm{d}v=-\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{1}{d}\,\mathsf{A}g\cdot f\mathsf{U}f\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{S}}(v\wedge\nabla_{v}g)\cdot(v\wedge\mathbb{J}[f]f)\,\mathrm{d}v=-\int_{\mathbb{S}}\nabla_{v}g\cdot\mathbb{J}[f]f\,\mathrm{d}v=\int_{\mathbb{S}}g\nabla_{v}\cdot(P_{v^{\perp}}\mathbb{J}[f]f)\,\mathrm{d}v.

Proof of
Lemma

3.3
.

We start with the proof of
(i)
. For smooth
f
and
g
, using the integration by parts (
1.8
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}}fv_{i}e_{j}\cdot\nabla_{v}g\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=e_{j}\cdot\int_{\mathbb{S}}g\,[(d-1)vfv_{i}-\nabla_{v}(fv_{i})]\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}}(d-1)(v_{i}v_{j}-e_{j}\cdot P_{v^{\perp}}e_{i})fg\,\mathrm{d}v-\int_{\mathbb{S}}gv_{i}e_{j}\cdot\nabla_{v}f\,\mathrm{d}v.

And therefore we get, using the same computation and exchanging
i
and
j
,

\int_{\mathbb{S}}f\,\mathsf{A}_{i,j}g\,\mathrm{d}v=-\int_{\mathbb{S}}g\,v_{i}e_{j}\cdot\nabla_{v}f\,\mathrm{d}v+\int_{\mathbb{S}}g\,v_{j}e_{i}\cdot\nabla_{v}f\,\mathrm{d}v=-\int_{\mathbb{S}}g\,\mathsf{A}_{i,j}f\,\mathrm{d}v.

The first part of
(ii)
comes from
Definition

3.2
and
Remark

3.1
. The next part comes from
(i)
, as we then have, for all smooth
g
,

\int_{\mathbb{S}}g\mathsf{\Delta}_{v}f\,\mathrm{d}v=-\int_{\mathbb{S}}\nabla_{v}g\cdot\nabla_{v}f\,\mathrm{d}v=-\int_{\mathbb{S}}\mathsf{A}g\cdot\mathsf{A}f\,\mathrm{d}v=\int_{\mathbb{S}}g\cdot\mathsf{A}^{2}f\,\mathrm{d}v.

The next point
(iii)
, using the fact thanks to (
1.6
) that
e_{i}\cdot\nabla_{v}(v_{j})=e_{i}\cdot P_{v^{\perp}}e_{i}=\delta_{ij}-v_{i}v_{j}
, is a direct computation from (
3.2
).
To prove the first part of
(iv)
, which are equalities on first order operators, we only need to prove their validity when applied to the functions
(v_{m})_{1\leqslant m\leqslant d}
. Indeed if two first order linear operators
\mathsf{B}
and
\widetilde{\mathsf{B}}
agree on such functions, then by induction they agree on any polynomial
P(v)
of these functions thanks to the fact that

\mathsf{B}(v_{m}P(v))=(\mathsf{B}v_{m})P(v)+v_{m}\mathsf{B}(P(v))=(\widetilde{\mathsf{B}}v_{m})P(v)+v_{m}\widetilde{\mathsf{B}}(P(v))=\widetilde{\mathsf{B}}(v_{m}P(v)).

Therefore by density they agree on any smooth function.

Finally, we have, thanks to
(iii)
,

[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}]v_{m}=\mathsf{A}_{i,j}(\delta_{\ell m}v_{k}-\delta_{km}v_{\ell})-\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}(\delta_{jm}v_{i}-\delta_{im}v_{j}),

which is zero thanks to
(iii)
again whenever
\{i,j\}\cap\{k,\ell\}=\emptyset
. Taking
\ell=j
and
k\notin\{i,j\}
, we get

[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{k,j}]v_{m}=-\delta_{km}v_{i}+\delta_{im}v_{k}=-\mathsf{A}_{i,k}v_{m},

which proves the first part of
(iv)
. We then have, for any
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant d
and
1\leqslant k<\ell\leqslant d
,

[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}^{2}]=\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}]+[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}]\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell},

which gives immediately that
[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}^{2}]=0
when
\{k,\ell\}\cap\{i,j\}=\emptyset
, and we therefore get, since
\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}^{2}=\mathsf{A}_{\ell,k}^{2}
,

\displaystyle[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k<\ell}\,[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}^{2}]=\sum_{m\notin\{i,j\}}[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{i,m}^{2}]+[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{m,j}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\notin\{i,j\}}\mathsf{A}_{i,m}[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{i,m}]+[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{i,m}]\mathsf{A}_{i,m}+\mathsf{A}_{m,j}[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{m,j}]+[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},\mathsf{A}_{m,j}]\mathsf{A}_{m,j},

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\notin\{i,j\}}-\mathsf{A}_{i,m}\mathsf{A}_{j,m}-\mathsf{A}_{j,m}\mathsf{A}_{i,m}-\mathsf{A}_{m,j}\mathsf{A}_{i,m}-\mathsf{A}_{i,m}\mathsf{A}_{m,j}=0.\qed

We now turn to properties related to the linear operator
\mathsf{U}
, which may also be described component-wise, by

\mathsf{U}_{i,j}f=d\left(v_{i}\,e_{j}\cdot\mathbb{J}[f]-v_{j}\,e_{i}\cdot\mathbb{J}[f]\right).

Lemma 3.4
.

Fixing
1\leqslant i,j,k\leqslant d
with
i\neq j
, we have the following properties.

(i)

The operator
\mathsf{U}_{i,j}
is antiselfadjoint on
\mathbb{S}
.

(ii)

We have
\mathsf{U}_{i,j}v_{k}=\delta_{jk}v_{i}-\delta_{ik}v_{j}
.

(iii)

If
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{S})
, then
\mathsf{U}^{2}f=\mathsf{AU}f=\mathsf{UA}f=-(d-1)d\,v\cdot\mathbb{J}[f]
, and
d(d-1)|\mathbb{J}[f]|^{2}=\|\mathsf{U}f\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\|\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}^{2}
.

(iv)

The operator
\mathsf{U}_{i,j}
commutes with
\mathsf{U}^{2}
and we have

\mathsf{AU}^{2}=\mathsf{U}^{2}\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{A}^{2}\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{UA}^{2}=\mathsf{U}^{3}=-(d-1)\mathsf{U}.

Before the proof, let us remark that the property
(ii)
corresponds exactly to
Lemma

3.3
–
(iii)
. This motivates the introduction of the factor
d
in
Definition

3.2
of
\mathsf{U}
. Since
\mathbb{J}[f]=0
for any spherical harmonic of degree different from
1
, this operator
\mathsf{U}
is therefore the composition of
\mathsf{A}
and the projection
\mathsf{\Pi}_{1}
on spherical harmonics of degree
1
, which also enlightens the last two properties of this lemma.

Proof of
Lemma

3.4
.

For
f,g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{S})
, we have
\int f\mathsf{U}g=d\,\mathbb{J}[f]\wedge\mathbb{J}[g]
, which gives
(i)
.
From (
1.9
), since
\int_{\mathbb{S}}v_{k}^{2}\mathrm{d}v=\frac{1}{d}
, one obtains
\mathbb{J}[v_{k}]=\frac{1}{d}e_{k}
, and we get
(ii)
. Therefore
\mathbb{J}[\mathsf{U}_{i,j}f]=(e_{j}\cdot\mathbb{J}[f])e_{i}-(e_{i}\cdot\mathbb{J}[f])e_{j}
, so we get

\mathsf{U}_{i,j}^{2}f=-d\,(v_{i}(e_{i}\cdot\mathbb{J}[f])+v_{j}(e_{j}\cdot\mathbb{J}[f])),

and summing over
i<j
we obtain

\mathsf{U}^{2}f=-(d-1)d\,v\cdot\mathbb{J}[f].

In fact, by
Lemma

3.3
–
(iii)
, we also have
\mathsf{A}_{i,j}\mathsf{U}_{i,j}f=-d(v_{i}e_{i}\cdot\mathbb{J}[f]+v_{j}e_{j}\cdot\mathbb{J}[f])
, so by summing over
i<j
we also get
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{U}^{2}
, and by antiselfadjointness this proves the first part of
(iii)
. From there we obtain
\|\mathsf{U}f\|_{2}^{2}=\langle\mathsf{A}f,\mathsf{U}f\rangle\leqslant\|\mathsf{U}f\|_{2}\|\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}
, and, thanks to (
1.9
),
d(d-1)|\mathbb{J}[f]|^{2}=\langle-f,\mathsf{U}^{2}f\rangle=\|\mathsf{U}f\|_{2}^{2}
, which proves the last part of
(iii)
.

Similarly, still using
Lemma

3.3
–
(iii)
, we obtain that
\mathsf{A}_{i,j}\mathsf{U}^{2}=-(d-1)\mathsf{U}_{i,j}
. Then, since
\mathsf{A}^{2}
is the Laplace–Beltrami operator, from (
1.6
) we get
\mathsf{A}^{2}v_{k}=-(d-1)v_{k}
, and therefore
\mathsf{A}^{2}\mathsf{U}_{i,j}=-(d-1)\mathsf{U}_{i,j}
. Finally, a direct computation of
\mathsf{U}_{i,j}\mathsf{U}^{2}
and
\mathsf{U}^{2}\mathsf{U}_{i,j}
using
(ii)
and
(iii)
, together with the antiselfadjoint character of
\mathsf{U}
and
\mathsf{A}
, gives
(iv)
.
∎

4.
The kinetic Brownian motion: hypoellipticity and hypocoercivity.

As a starter to study (
1.1
), we leave aside the nonlinear alignment term for the moment. We are therefore interested in the Kolmogorov equation on the sphere, given by

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\mathsf{\nabla}_{x}f=\mathsf{\Delta}_{v}f,

resulting of being the law of kinetic Brownian motion in velocity, which corresponds to the following stochastic process:

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}X=V\mathrm{d}t,\\
\mathrm{d}V=P_{V^{\perp}}\circ\mathrm{d}B_{t},\end{cases}

studied by Baudoin and Tardif
[
2
]
(in a more general framework of Riemannian manifold in space as well). Using estimates based on
\Gamma
-generalized calculus, they obtain a quantitative estimate of hypoelliptic (non-time-optimal) regularity. The goal of this section is to show that we can in fact remain in the classical case of Sobolev-type quadratic functionals, without deploying the machinery of the
\Gamma
-calculus, and that it may provides precise and constructive decay estimates, both in short and long time.

As before, we note
\mathsf{T}=v\cdot\nabla_{x}
and the equation is thus written

(4.1)

\partial_{t}f+\mathsf{T}f=\mathsf{A}^{2}f.

4.1.
The (quadratic) dissipation associated to the kinetic Brownian motion.

For a linear (scalar) operator
\mathsf{X}
and a real
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
function
f
with compact support in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}
, we define the quadratic quantity

Q_{\mathsf{X}}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}f\,\mathsf{X}f\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}v,

which we will simply denote
Q_{\mathsf{X}}
if the context is clear (and which therefore depends only on the self-adjoint part of
\mathsf{X}
). If
f
is a solution to (
4.1
), we have, after a direct computation,

(4.2)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}Q_{\mathsf{X}}(f)=Q_{\Phi(\mathsf{X})}(f),

where the operator
\Phi(\mathsf{X})
(which is self-adjoint when
X
is self-adjoint) goes as follows:

(4.3)

\Phi(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{A}^{2}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{X}\mathsf{A}^{2}+[\mathsf{T},\mathsf{X}]=2\mathsf{A}\mathsf{X}\mathsf{A}+[\mathsf{A},[\mathsf{A},\mathsf{X}]]+[\mathsf{T},\mathsf{X}],

where in the last expression we recall that
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{X}\mathsf{A}
stands for
\sum_{i<j}\mathsf{A}_{i,j}\mathsf{X}\mathsf{A}_{i,j}
.

We now introduce
\mathsf{S}=[\mathsf{A},\mathsf{T}]
. Thanks to
Lemma

3.3
–
(iii)
, we obtain
\mathsf{S}_{i,j}=v_{i}\partial_{x_{j}}-v_{j}\partial_{x_{i}}
, which we can write
\mathsf{S}=v\wedge\nabla_{x}=v\wedge P_{v^{\perp}}\nabla_{x}
.
By direct calculation, we obtain:

(4.4)

\mathsf{S}^{2}+\mathsf{T}^{2}=\mathsf{\Delta}_{x},

which is the main interest of our framework.
The operators
\mathsf{S}
and
\mathsf{T}
commute, but thanks again to
Lemma

3.3
–
(iii)
, we have
\mathsf{[\mathsf{A}_{i,j},S_{i,j}]}=-v_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}-v_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}
and summing over
i<j
we find that
[\mathsf{A},\mathsf{S}]=-(d-1)\mathsf{T}
.

To improve readability, we first calculate
\Phi(\mathsf{X})
for all the operators that will be of interest.

Proposition 4.1
.

We have

(4.5)

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathsf{Id})=2\mathsf{A}^{2},

(4.6)

\displaystyle\Phi(-\mathsf{A}^{2})=-2\mathsf{A}^{4}+\mathsf{(AS+SA)},

(4.7)

\displaystyle\Phi(-(\mathsf{AS+SA}))=2\mathsf{S}^{2}-2(\mathsf{A}^{2}\mathsf{SA}+\mathsf{AS}\mathsf{A}^{2})+(d-1)(\mathsf{AS+SA}),

(4.8)

\displaystyle\Phi(-\mathsf{S}^{2})=-2\mathsf{AS}^{2}\mathsf{A}-2(d-1)\mathsf{T}^{2}+2\,\mathsf{S}^{2}.

(4.9)

\displaystyle\Phi(-\mathsf{T}^{2})=-2\mathsf{AT}^{2}\mathsf{A}+2(d-1)\mathsf{T}^{2}-2\,\mathsf{S}^{2}.

Proof of
Proposition

4.1
.
.

Since
[\mathsf{T},\mathsf{Id}]=0
, we immediately get (
4.5
). We combine
\mathsf{[A,T]=S}
(by definition) and
[\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}^{2}]=\mathsf{A[T,A]+[T,A]A}
to obtain (
4.6
). With the first expression of
\Phi
in (
4.3
), we use
\mathsf{[A,S]}=-(d-1)\mathsf{T}
and the fact that
\mathsf{S}
and
\mathsf{T}
commute to write

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathsf{AS+SA})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{A}^{2}(2\mathsf{SA}-(d-1)\mathsf{T})+(2\mathsf{AS}+(d-1)\mathsf{T})\mathsf{A}^{2}+[\mathsf{T},\mathsf{AS}]+[\mathsf{T},\mathsf{SA}]

\displaystyle=2(\mathsf{A}^{2}\mathsf{SA}+\mathsf{ASA}^{2})+(d-1)[\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}^{2}]+[\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{S}+\mathsf{S}[\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}]

\displaystyle=2(\mathsf{A}^{2}\mathsf{SA}+\mathsf{ASA}^{2})-(d-1)(\mathsf{AS+SA})-2\mathsf{S}^{2},

which gives (
4.7
). Finally, to get (
4.8
), we first proceed to prove (
4.9
). Indeed, since
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{\Delta}_{x}
commute, we have
\Phi_{-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x}}=-2\mathsf{A\Delta}_{x}\mathsf{A}
, which proves (
4.8
) from (
4.9
) thanks to (
4.4
).

We have, thanks to the fact that
\mathsf{S}
and
\mathsf{T}
commute:

(4.10)

[\mathsf{A},\mathsf{T}^{2}]=\mathsf{T[A,T]+[A,T]T}=2\mathsf{TS}.

We then have

(4.11)

\mathsf{[A,TS]=T[A,S]+[A,T]S}=-(d-1)\mathsf{T}^{2}+\mathsf{S}^{2},

and with the second expression of
\Phi
in (
4.3
), this gives (
4.9
).
∎

4.2.
Constructing suitable hypocoercivity functionals on the sphere.

Definition 4.2
.

For
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta
some positive parameters that we will be adapted later on, we define two energy functionals
\mathcal{F}_{0}
,
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and two dissipation functionals
\mathcal{D}_{0}
,
\mathcal{D}_{1}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{0}

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathsf{Id}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}

\displaystyle=\alpha Q_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}}+\beta Q_{-\mathsf{SA-AS}}+\gamma Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}+\delta Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{0}

\displaystyle=Q_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{1}

\displaystyle=\alpha Q_{\mathsf{A}^{4}}+\beta Q_{\mathsf{A}^{2}\mathsf{SA+ASA}^{2}}+\beta Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}+\gamma Q_{\mathsf{AS}^{2}\mathsf{A}}+\delta Q_{\mathsf{AT}^{2}\mathsf{A}}.

Furthermore, we define the total energy and dissipation functionals as

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{0}+\mathcal{F}_{1},\quad\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{0}+\mathcal{D}_{1}.

The main interest of the definition is the following energy – energy-dissipation identity, which proof is immediate from (
4.2
)–(
4.3
) and
Proposition

4.1
.

Proposition 4.3
.

For any smooth and compactly supported solution
f
of equation (
4.1
) (which is the case if
f^{0}
is compactly supported), we have
\forall t>0
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}_{0}(f(t,\cdot))=-2\mathcal{D}_{0}(f(t,\cdot)),\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}_{1}(f(t,\cdot))=-2\mathcal{D}_{1}(f(t,\cdot))+\mathcal{B}(f(t,\cdot)),

where the functional
\mathcal{B}
(corresponding to “bad” terms without sign which will be controlled by the dissipative ones) is defined as follows:

(4.12)

\mathcal{B}=-(\alpha+\beta(d-1))Q_{-\mathsf{AS-SA}}+2(\gamma-\delta)[(d-1)Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}-\,Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}].

An interested reader would point out that
\mathcal{F}_{0}(f)=\|f\|_{2}^{2}
and,

\mathcal{F}_{1}(f)=\alpha\|\nabla_{v}f\|_{2}^{2}+2\beta\langle\nabla_{v}f,\nabla_{x}f\rangle+\gamma\|P_{v^{\perp}}\nabla_{x}f\|^{2}_{2}+\delta\|v\cdot\nabla_{x}f\|_{2}^{2},

and from there it seems clear that
\mathcal{F}_{0}+\mathcal{F}_{1}
is equivalent to the square of the
H^{1}
norm of
f
, as long as
\beta<\alpha\gamma
.
Similar terms appearing in the dissipation functionals can also be put under the form of standard Sobolev semi-norms, such as
\mathcal{D}_{0}(f)=Q_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}}=\|\nabla_{v}f\|^{2}_{2}=\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{0,1}}^{2}
, or
Q_{\mathsf{A}^{4}}=\|\mathsf{\Delta}_{v}f\|^{2}_{2}=\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{0,2}}^{2}
appearing in
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, but the meaning of some other terms is somewhat more intricate. The next proposition quantifies more precisely these equivalences with Sobolev semi-norms, and provides the main estimates of this paragraph.

Proposition 4.4
.

Assume
\beta^{2}<\alpha\gamma
. Then the functional
\mathcal{F}_{1}
is equivalent to the square of the
\dot{H}^{1}
semi-norm, and more precisely, for any function
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S})
, we have

(4.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}(f)

\displaystyle\leqslant(1+\tfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{\alpha\gamma}})\big(\alpha\|\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{2}^{2}\big)+\delta\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{2}^{2},

(4.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}(f)

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-\tfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{\alpha\gamma}})\big(\alpha\|\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{2}^{2}\big)+\delta\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{2}^{2},

and we recall that
\|f\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1}}=\|f\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{0,1}}+\|f\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1,0}}=\|\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{2}^{2}
.

Similarly
\mathcal{D}_{1}
is equivalent to the square of a Sobolev-type semi-norm given by
\|\cdot\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{0,2}}+\|\cdot\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1,0}}+\|\cdot\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1,1}}
through the following estimates:

(4.15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{1}(f)

\displaystyle\leqslant(1+\tfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{\alpha\gamma}})(\alpha\|\mathsf{A}^{2}f\|^{2}_{2}+\gamma\|\mathsf{S}\otimes\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}^{2})+\beta\|\mathsf{S}f\|^{2}_{2}+\delta\|\mathsf{TA}f\|^{2}_{2},

(4.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{1}(f)

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-\tfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{\alpha\gamma}})\big(\alpha\|\mathsf{A}^{2}f\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma\|\mathsf{S}\otimes\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}^{2}\big)+\beta\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{2}^{2}+\delta\|\mathsf{TA}f\|^{2}_{2}

(4.17)

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-\tfrac{\beta}{\sqrt{\alpha\gamma}})\big(\alpha\|\mathsf{A}^{2}f\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma\|\mathsf{S}\otimes\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}^{2}\big)+\tfrac{d-1}{2}\min(\beta,\sqrt{\beta\delta})\|\mathsf{T}f\|^{2}_{2}

where
\|\mathsf{S}\otimes\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}^{2}=Q_{\mathsf{AS}^{2}\mathsf{A}}=\sum_{i<j}\sum_{k<\ell}\|\mathsf{S}_{i,j}\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}f\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k<\ell}\|\mathsf{S}\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}f\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i<j}\|\mathsf{S}_{i,j}\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}^{2}
, therefore giving

\|f\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{0,2}}+\|f\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1,0}}+\|f\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1,1}}=\|\mathsf{A}^{2}f\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathsf{S}\otimes\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathsf{TA}f\|^{2}_{2}.

Finally, the “bad” term
\mathcal{B}(f)
given by
Proposition

4.3
is controlled by the dissipation: for any function
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S})
,

(4.18)

\mathcal{B}(f)\leqslant\frac{\alpha+(d-1)\beta}{\sqrt{\beta}}\big(\mathcal{D}_{0}(f)+\mathcal{D}_{1}(f)\big)+\frac{2|\gamma-\delta|}{\sqrt{\beta\delta}}\mathcal{D}_{1}(f).

In particular, we obtain
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}(t)\leqslant-(2-\varepsilon)\mathcal{D}(t)
for
\varepsilon
as small as we want, as soon as we have

(4.19)

\alpha\ll\sqrt{\beta},\quad\beta\ll 1,\quad|\gamma-\delta|\ll\sqrt{\beta\delta},

where we use the symbol
\ll
to denote inequality up to a small positive constant
C_{\varepsilon}
that may depend only on
\varepsilon
and
d
.

Proof of
Proposition

4.4
.
.

We start with the estimation of
\mathcal{F}_{1}
. Let us recall that we have
\mathsf{A}=v\wedge\nabla_{v}
and
\mathsf{S}=v\wedge P_{v^{\perp}}\nabla_{x}
, hence we get:

Q_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}}=\|\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}^{2}=\|\nabla_{v}f\|_{2}^{2}=\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{0,1}}^{2},\quad Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}+Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}=\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{2}^{2}=Q_{-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x}}=\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1,0}}^{2}.

We therefore only need to estimate
Q_{\mathsf{SA+AS}}
. By Cauchy–Schwarz and (anti)selfadjointness of
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{S}
, we have
|Q_{\mathsf{SA+AS}}|\leqslant 2\sqrt{Q_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}}Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}}Q_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\alpha}}Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}
by Young’s inequality, and this provides the upper and lower bounds (
4.13
)–(
4.14
).

We now estimate
\mathcal{D}_{1}
. We only have to estimate
Q_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}\mathsf{SA-ASA}^{2}}
, since
Q_{\mathsf{A}^{4}}
,
Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}
,
Q_{\mathsf{AS}^{2}\mathsf{A}}
and
Q_{\mathsf{A\Delta}_{x}\mathsf{A}}
are good terms. Using same type of arguments as above, we write, by Cauchy–Schwarz,

|Q_{\mathsf{A}^{2}\mathsf{SA}}|=|Q_{\mathsf{ASA}^{2}}|\leqslant\sqrt{Q_{\mathsf{A}^{4}}Q_{\mathsf{(AS)(SA)}}}.

To estimate
Q_{\mathsf{(AS)(SA)}}
one needs to pay attention to the subtleties of parentheses (and use the fact that
\mathsf{S}_{i,j}
and
\mathsf{S}_{k,\ell}
commute, and again Cauchy–Schwarz and Young). We write

\displaystyle Q_{\mathsf{(AS)(SA)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\sum_{k<\ell}Q_{\mathsf{A}_{i,j}\mathsf{S}_{i,j}\mathsf{S}_{k,\ell}\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}}=\sum_{i<j}\sum_{k<\ell}Q_{\mathsf{A}_{i,j}\mathsf{S}_{k,\ell}\mathsf{S}_{i,j}\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}}

(4.20)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i<j}\sum_{k<\ell}\sqrt{Q_{\mathsf{A}_{i,j}\mathsf{S}_{k,\ell}^{2}\mathsf{A}_{i,j}}}\sqrt{Q_{\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}\mathsf{S}_{i,j}^{2}\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}}}\leqslant\sum_{i<j}\sum_{k<\ell}\frac{1}{2}(Q_{\mathsf{A}_{i,j}\mathsf{S}_{k,\ell}^{2}\mathsf{A}_{i,j}}+Q_{\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}\mathsf{S}_{i,j}^{2}\mathsf{A}_{k,\ell}})=Q_{\mathsf{AS}^{2}\mathsf{A}}.

As a consequence,
|Q_{\mathsf{A}^{2}\mathsf{SA+ASA}^{2}}|\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}}Q_{\mathsf{A}^{4}}+\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\alpha}}Q_{\mathsf{AS}^{2}\mathsf{A}}
, and thus we obtain the upper bound and the first lower bound for
\mathcal{D}_{1}
given in (
4.15
)–(
4.16
).

To prove the last lower bound for
\mathcal{D}_{1}
given in (
4.16
), let us show how this combination of
\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{2}^{2}=Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}
and
\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{\dot{H}^{1,0}}^{2}=Q_{\mathsf{A\Delta}_{x}\mathsf{A}}
allow to control
Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}=\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{2}^{2}
.
Recall that
(\mathsf{STA})^{\star}=\mathsf{A}^{\star}\mathsf{T}^{\star}\mathsf{S}^{\star}=-\mathsf{ATS}
, and thus, by Cauchy–Schwarz
|Q_{\mathsf{ATS}}|=|Q_{\mathsf{STA}}|\leqslant\sqrt{Q_{\mathsf{AT}^{2}\mathsf{A}}Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}}
. Using the commutator calculated previously in (
4.11
), whe have
(d-1)\mathsf{T}^{2}-\mathsf{S}^{2}=\mathsf{STA-ATS}
, and using Young’s inequality we obtain

(4.21)

|(d-1)Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}-Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}|\leqslant 2\sqrt{Q_{\mathsf{AT}^{2}\mathsf{A}}Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\delta}}Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\beta}}Q_{\mathsf{AT}^{2}\mathsf{A}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta\delta}}(\beta\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{2}^{2}+\delta\|\mathsf{TA}f\|_{2}^{2}),

and therefore

\displaystyle(d-1)Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta\delta}}(\beta\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{2}^{2}+\delta\|\mathsf{TA}f\|^{2}_{2})

(4.22)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{\beta}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta\delta}}\right)(\beta\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{2}^{2}+\delta\|\mathsf{TA}f\|_{2}^{2})\leqslant\frac{2}{\min(\beta,\sqrt{\delta\beta})}(\beta\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{2}^{2}+\delta\|\mathsf{TA}f\|_{2}^{2}),

which completes the proof of the estimate (
4.17
).

To estimate
\mathcal{B}
, we have

(4.23)

|Q_{-\mathsf{AS-SA}}|\leqslant 2\sqrt{Q_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}}Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}}\leqslant\frac{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{0}\mathcal{D}_{1}}}{\sqrt{\beta}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}(\mathcal{D}_{0}+\mathcal{D}_{1}),\qquad|(d-1)Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}-Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}|\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta\delta}}\mathcal{D}_{1},

thanks to (
4.21
) and (
4.16
). Therefore, with the definition (
4.12
) of
\mathcal{B}
, it gives (
4.18
) and this ends the proof.
∎

These estimates ensure the decay of the solution to a constant isotropic state, when the initial condition belongs to
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S})
, as stated in the next proposition.

Proposition 4.5
.

Given a
d
-dimensional flat torus, there exist positive constants
C
and
\lambda
such that for any smooth solution
f
of (
4.1
) with zero average, we have for all positive time
t
:

\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}}\leqslant C\|f(0,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}}e^{-\lambda t}.

On
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists a positive constant
C
such that for any smooth and compactly supported solution
f
of (
4.1
), we have for all positive time
t
:

\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}}\leqslant C\frac{\|f(0,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}}}{\big(1+\min(1,\frac{\|f(0,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}}}{\|f(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}})^{\frac{4}{d}}\,t\big)^{\frac{d}{4}}}.

The constants in this statement can be constructed explicitly and only depend on the dimension
d
and the shape of the domain.

Proof of
Proposition

4.5
.
.

We fix coefficients
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
and
\delta
satisfying (
4.19
), ensuring for instance that
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}\leqslant-\mathcal{D}
. This is doable by taking for instance
(\beta,\gamma,\delta)=(\alpha^{\frac{3}{2}},4\alpha^{2},4\alpha^{2})
with
\alpha
sufficiently small (remember we must ensure
\beta^{2}<\alpha\gamma
).

Using the Poincaré inequalities on the torus and on the sphere we obtain, for a smooth function
f
with zero average (denoting
\mathsf{\Pi}_{0}f
its local average
\int_{\mathbb{S}}f(\cdot,v)\mathrm{d}v
):

\mathcal{F}_{0}=\|f\|_{2}^{2}=\|f-\mathsf{\Pi}_{0}f\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathsf{\Pi}_{0}f\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{d-1}\|\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\Lambda}\|\mathsf{\Pi}_{0}f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{d-1}\|\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\Lambda}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1,0}}^{2}.

From there, we use
Proposition

4.4
to get that
\mathcal{D}\geqslant\widetilde{\Lambda}\mathcal{F}
for some positive constant
\widetilde{\Lambda}
(only depending on the shape of the torus and the chosen coefficients
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
,
\delta
). By Gronwal’s inequality and equivalence of
\mathcal{F}
with the
H^{1}
squared norm, we obtain the first claim of the proposition.

Let us now deal with the case of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We use the fact that the
L^{1}
norm of
f
is decreasing, as can be checked by multiplying the equation by the sign of
f
and integrating by parts. By Nash inequality on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we obtain

\|\mathsf{\Pi}_{0}f\|_{2}^{2+\frac{4}{d}}\leqslant C_{d}\|\mathsf{\Pi}_{0}f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\|\mathsf{\Pi}_{0}f\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{4}{d}}\leqslant C_{d}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\|f(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{4}{d}}.

Therefore we get, as before (using
\lesssim
to denote inequality up to an explicit constant only depending on
d
),

\mathcal{F}_{0}\lesssim\|\mathsf{A}f\|_{2}^{2}+\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1,0}}^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}\|f(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{4}{d+2}}.

Thanks to
Proposition

4.4
, we therefore get
\mathcal{F}\lesssim\mathcal{D}+\mathcal{D}^{\frac{d}{d+2}}\|f(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{4}{d+2}}
. Using the fact that
\mathcal{F}
is nondecreasing, this leads (by separating the cases
\mathcal{D}\leqslant\|f(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}^{2}
and
\mathcal{D}\geqslant\|f(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}^{2}
) to

\mathcal{F}^{1+\frac{2}{d}}\lesssim\max(\|f(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{4}{d}},\mathcal{F}(0)^{\frac{2}{d}})\mathcal{D}.

Solving the corresponding differential inequality and using the equivalence of
\mathcal{F}
with the
H^{1}
squared norm ends the proof.
∎

From there, we now present some improvements regarding the short and long time behaviour, in view of the study of the nonlinear equation (
3.1
). In short time, we want to obtain regularizing estimates (of hypoellipticity type à la Hérau
[
16
]
) allowing to have local well-posedness in a larger space. And in long time, we would like to obtain a constructive decay estimate in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
without relying on the
L^{1}
norm, which is unfortunately not controlled in the presence of the alignment term. Those improvements are easier to present in the simple case of the kinetic Brownian motion, which is done in the next two subsections.

4.3.
Short-time hypoelliptic estimates.

The regularizing estimation à la Hérau
[
16
]
consists in letting the coefficients of the energy functional depend on time. By chosing appropriate powers of time, we obtain a decreasing quantity which, at time
0
, is the
L^{2}
norm. In order to keep light notations, we now suppose that the coefficients
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
,
\delta
are functions of a parameter
\tau
, so we may write
\mathcal{F}(\tau,f)=\mathcal{F}_{0}(f)+\mathcal{F}_{1}(\tau,f)
to emphasize this dependence. We therefore have, after
Definition

4.2
,

\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{F}=\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{F}_{1}=\alpha^{\prime}Q_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}}+\beta^{\prime}Q_{-\mathsf{SA-AS}}+\gamma^{\prime}Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}+\delta^{\prime}Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}.

However, the functionals will be always evaluated for
\tau=t
, so we will still denote
\mathcal{F}(t)
as a shortcut for
\mathcal{F}(t,f(t,\cdot))
. We proceed similarly for the other functionals that involve those coefficients, such as
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{B}
. Therefore we get from
Proposition

4.3
, still denoting
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{0}+\mathcal{D}_{1}

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}(t)=-2\mathcal{D}(t)+\mathcal{B}(t)+\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{F}_{1}(t).

We can then estimate
\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{F}_{1}
as was done for
\mathcal{B}
in (
4.18
), in order to obtain conditions ensuring decay of the total energy.

Proposition 4.6
.

We have

\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{F}_{1}\leqslant|\alpha^{\prime}|\mathcal{D}_{0}+\frac{|\beta^{\prime}|}{\sqrt{\beta}}(\mathcal{D}_{0}+\mathcal{D}_{1})+\Big(\frac{|\gamma^{\prime}|}{\beta}+\frac{2|\delta^{\prime}|}{(d-1)\min(\beta,\sqrt{\beta\delta})}\Big)\mathcal{D}_{1}.

Therefore we obtain
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}(t)\leqslant-(2-\varepsilon)\mathcal{D}(t)
for
\varepsilon
as small as we want, as soon as
\beta<\sqrt{\alpha\gamma}
and

(4.24)

\alpha\ll\sqrt{\beta},\quad\alpha^{\prime}\ll 1,\quad\beta\ll 1,\quad\beta^{\prime}\ll\sqrt{\beta},\quad|\gamma-\delta|\ll\sqrt{\beta\delta},\quad|\gamma^{\prime}|\ll\beta,\quad|\delta^{\prime}|\ll\min(\beta,\sqrt{\beta\delta}).

Proof of
Proposition

4.6
.
.

The estimate is directly obtained from the proof of
Proposition

4.4
, since we have
Q_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}}\leqslant\mathcal{D}_{0}
,
\beta Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}\leqslant\mathcal{D}_{1}
, and the estimates for
Q_{-\mathsf{AS-SA}}
and
Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}
are given in (
4.23
)–(
4.22
). The new conditions on the coefficients, compared to (
4.19
), therefore follow.
∎

Let us now focus on the case of powers of time, therefore we write
\alpha=\alpha_{0}\tau^{p_{\alpha}}
,
\beta=\beta_{0}\tau^{p_{\beta}}
,
\gamma=\gamma_{0}\tau^{p_{\gamma}}
, and
\delta=\delta_{0}\tau^{p_{\delta}}
. We want to satisfy the conditions (
4.24
) on
\tau\in[0,1]
. First of all, for
\tau=1
, we only need the following for the coefficients
\alpha_{0}
,
\beta_{0}
,
\gamma_{0}
, and
\delta_{0}
(remember that we also need
\beta_{0}<\sqrt{\alpha_{0}\gamma_{0}}
):

(4.25)

\alpha_{0}\ll\sqrt{\beta_{0}},\quad\beta_{0}\ll 1,\quad\gamma_{0}\ll\sqrt{\beta_{0}\delta_{0}},\quad\delta_{0}\ll\beta_{0}.

Indeed the conditions
\alpha_{0}\ll 1
,
\beta_{0}\ll\sqrt{\beta_{0}}
,
\gamma_{0}\ll\beta_{0}
are then implied. As before in the beginning of the proof of
Proposition

4.5
, taking
(\beta_{0},\gamma_{0},\delta_{0})=(\alpha_{0}^{\frac{3}{2}},4\alpha_{0}^{2},4\alpha_{0}^{2})
with
\alpha_{0}\ll 1
allows to satisfy these conditions.

And then the following conditions on the exponents
p_{\alpha}
,
p_{\beta}
,
p_{\gamma}
and
p_{\delta}
ensure that the conditions (
4.24
) (as long as
\beta<\sqrt{\alpha\gamma}
) are satisfied for all
\tau\in(0,1]
:

2p_{\beta}\geqslant p_{\alpha}+p_{\gamma},\quad 2p_{\alpha}\geqslant p_{\beta},\quad p_{\alpha}\geqslant 1,\quad p_{\beta}\geqslant 2,\quad 2p_{\gamma}\geqslant p_{\beta}+p_{\delta},\quad p_{\gamma}\geqslant p_{\beta}+1,\quad p_{\delta}\geqslant p_{\beta}+2.

Indeed the remaining conditions
p_{\beta}\geqslant 0
,
p_{\delta}\geqslant p_{\beta}
,
p_{\delta}\geqslant p_{\beta}+1
are then implied. And from here, it is clear that the minimal exponents that we can take are
(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta},p_{\gamma},p_{\delta})=(1,2,3,4)
. We therefore have
\mathcal{F}
nonincreasing on
[0,1]
. In the following proposition, we summarize the consequences in terms of short time estimates for the components of the
H^{1}
norm of
f
, and we also present a small improvement in weight regarding for averaged quantities such as
\mathbb{J}[f]
.

Proposition 4.7
.

There exists a constant
C
(only depending on the dimension
d
) such that for any smooth and compactly supported solution
f
of (
4.1
), we have for all
t\in(0,1]
:

\|\nabla_{v}f\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{t}}\|f^{0}\|_{L^{2}},\quad\|P_{v^{\perp}}\nabla_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\frac{C}{t^{\frac{3}{2}}}\|f^{0}\|_{L^{2}},\quad\|v\cdot\nabla_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\frac{C}{t^{2}}\|f^{0}\|_{L^{2}}.

Furthermore, if we fix
\mu=1
when
d=2
,
\mu\in(0,1]
when
d=3
or
\mu=0
for
d\geqslant 4
, there exists a constant
C
(depending on
\mu
for
d=3
) such that for all
t\in(0,1]
:

\|\nabla_{x}\mathbb{J}[f]\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant\frac{C}{t^{\frac{3}{2}+\frac{\mu}{4}}}\|f^{0}\|_{L^{2}}.

Proof of
Proposition

4.7
.
.

First of all, once we have chosen
(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta},p_{\gamma},p_{\delta})=(1,2,3,4)
, and the coefficients
\alpha_{0}
,
\beta_{0}
,
\gamma_{0}
and
\delta_{0}
as in (
4.24
) (with
\beta_{0}<\sqrt{\alpha_{0}\gamma_{0}}
), the nonincreasing quantity
\mathcal{F}
is equivalent to
\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+t\|\nabla_{v}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+t^{3}\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+t^{4}\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
.
Since
\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|P_{v^{\perp}}\nabla_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
and
\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|v\cdot\nabla_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
, this gives the first part of the proposition.
The second part comes from
Lemma

A.1
given in appendix, which implies that
\|\nabla_{x}\mathbb{J}[f]\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leqslant C(\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2-\mu}\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{L^{2}}^{\mu}
). Notice that, still using
Lemma

A.1
, we would obtain the same estimates for the local density, or any local quantity only involving a finite number of modes in
v
.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

To obtain the regularity estimates in
[
2
]
, if we were to re-translate their
\Gamma
-generalized-calculus approach into our framework, it would amount to using the following functional:

Q_{\mathsf{Id}}+at^{2}Q_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}}+bt^{4}Q_{\mathsf{-SA-AS}}+ct^{6}Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}+\hat{a}t^{4}Q_{\mathsf{A}^{4}}+\hat{b}t^{6}Q_{[\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}^{2}]}+\hat{c}t^{8}Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}.

In our case, we have used exponents two times smaller, and our functional is written with fewer terms:

\mathcal{F}=Q_{\mathsf{Id}}+\alpha_{0}tQ_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}}+\beta_{0}t^{2}Q_{\mathsf{-SA-AS}}+\gamma_{0}t^{3}Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}+\delta_{0}t^{4}Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}.

As we understand it, the presence of the term
Q_{\mathsf{A}^{4}}
is there in
[
2
]
simply to ensure that the functional is positive by taking as criterion
\hat{b}^{2}<\hat{a}\hat{c}
. But we’ve actually seen in the beginning of the proof of
Proposition

4.1
that
Q_{[\mathsf{T},\mathsf{A}^{2}]}=Q_{\mathsf{-SA-AS}}
, so we don’t need this term (which is the same as an already existing term, with a lower weight). We don’t know if in the context of a Riemannian manifold also in space, which is the case in
[
2
]
, our simple approach would work (there may be more terms with the curvature of the variety, in our case we used for example that
\mathsf{\Delta}_{v}
and
\mathsf{\Delta}_{x}
commute).

Remark 4.9
.

The enhanced dissipation estimates from
[
9
]
come with hypocoercive estimates on the torus, derived through an energy, mode by mode in space. By reconstructing the total energy, we would recover an energy of the form to
\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+at\|\nabla_{v}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+bt^{2}\langle\nabla_{x}f,\nabla_{v}f\rangle+ct^{3}\|P_{v^{\perp}}\nabla_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
, which corresponds to the first terms of our functional. Even if in
[
9
]
this energy is not proven to be decreasing, its derivative is controlled mode by mode, and it allows the authors to obtain hypocoercive decay in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
for any mode. If this energy is bounded, then it would be actually possible (after some work using the fact that
v\cdot\nabla_{x}f
can be seen as a commutator) to recover the control of the form
\|v\cdot\nabla_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leqslant\frac{C}{t^{2}}\|f^{0}\|_{L^{2}}
. However on the whole space, since the amplitude of the modes is not bounded below, we did not manage to control such an energy without adding the term
\delta t^{4}\|v\cdot\nabla_{x}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
directly into the functional, except in the case
d\geqslant 6
. In that case, the estimate (
4.21
) can be written differently, as

(d-1)Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}-Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}\leqslant 2\sqrt{Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}Q_{\mathsf{AS}^{2}\mathsf{A}}}\leqslant\sqrt{d-1}Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}Q_{\mathsf{AS}^{2}\mathsf{A}},

which gives, since
Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}\geqslant 0
that
(d-1)Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}-Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}-1}Q_{\mathsf{AS}^{2}\mathsf{A}}
. When
d>5
we have
\sqrt{d-1}-1>1
and therefore the last term in the expression (
4.12
) of
\mathcal{B}
can now be controlled by
c\mathcal{D}
with
c<2
(compared to
\varepsilon\mathcal{D}
for
\varepsilon
as small as we want when we include the term
\delta_{0}t^{4}Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}}
), which is sufficient to get the decay.

Let us also remark that, even if in our work we are not interested in the case of small diffusion
\sigma
, our functional gives another related approach to obtain their enhanced dissipation result (which therefore is valid in any dimension
d\geqslant 2
). Indeed, by keeping
\sigma
in front of
\mathsf{A}^{2}
in (
4.1
), and rewriting our functional as

Q_{\mathsf{Id}}+\sigma\alpha_{0}tQ_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}}+\sigma\beta_{0}t^{2}Q_{\mathsf{-SA-AS}}+\sigma\gamma_{0}t^{3}Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}+\sigma^{2}\delta_{0}t^{4}Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}},

we still obtain that it is nonincreasing under the same conditions (
4.24
) on the coefficients
\alpha_{0}
,
\beta_{0}
,
\gamma_{0}
,
\delta_{0}
(for
\sigma t
bounded). Together with the Poincaré inequality, this directly provides
(1+C\sigma^{2}t^{4})\|f\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leqslant\|f^{0}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}
, thus giving exponential decay with rate controlled by
\sqrt{\sigma}
when
\sigma
is small. In our approach, what plays the role of their interpolation between
\|P_{v^{\perp}}ef\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{v}}
(for a unit vector
e
) and
\|\nabla_{v}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{v}}
to control
\|f\|_{L^{2}_{v}}
, is our inequality (
4.22
) which allows to control
\|v\cdot\nabla_{x}f\|^{2}
by terms in the dissipation functional
\mathcal{D}
. Let us mention that the interplay between index of hypoellipticity and time scale of enhanced dissipation has already been noticed in the literature, see for instance
[
1
]
.

4.4.
Long-time estimates.

In this section, we are interested in deriving a long time estimate without using any information on the mass, that is, forgetting about the conservation of
\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S})}
. Indeed, this latter conservation will no longer be available for the nonlinear Vicsek equation (and neither for its linearised version) on the whole space. The next proposition is therefore quite similar to the second part of
Proposition

4.5
, but we control the
H^{1}
norm thanks to
L^{2}
moments in
x
instead of the
L^{1}
norm (using Heisenberg uncertainty principle instead of the Nash inequality), at the price of a worse exponent in time.

Proposition 4.10
.

There exist positive constants
C,\epsilon
(which can be computed explicitly and only depend on the dimension
d
) such that for any smooth and compactly supported solution
f
of (
4.1
) on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoting
\mathcal{M}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}|x|^{2}\,f^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}v
, we have for all positive time
t
:

\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}}\leqslant C\frac{\|f(0,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}}}{\big(1+\frac{\|f(0,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{1}}}{\mathcal{M}(0)+\|f(0,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{1}}}\,t\big)^{\epsilon}}.

Proof of
Proposition

4.10
.

We proceed as in the proof of
Proposition

4.5
, by first fixing the coefficients in order to have
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}\leqslant-\mathcal{D}
. Now we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathcal{M}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}|x|^{2}\,f\left(-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\Delta_{v}f\right)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}\left(v\cdot x\right)f^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}v-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}|x|^{2}\,\left|\nabla_{v}f\right|^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{d-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}(x\cdot\nabla_{v}f)\,f\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}v-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}|x|^{2}\,\left|\nabla_{v}f\right|^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}v

(4.26)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}-\big(x\cdot\nabla_{v}f-\frac{f}{d-1}\big)^{2}+\frac{f^{2}}{(d-1)^{2}}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq\frac{1}{(d-1)^{2}}\mathcal{F}_{0}(t).

where we have used that
\int_{\mathbb{S}}x\cdot\nabla_{v}(f^{2})\,\mathrm{d}v=(d-1)\int_{\mathbb{S}}(v\cdot x)\,f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v
after what has been stated in (
1.7
).

By Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle,

\mathcal{F}_{0}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}f^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}v\leq\frac{2}{d}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}|x|^{2}f^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}|\nabla_{x}f|^{2}\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}v\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

Combining with the hypocoercive estimate of
Proposition

4.4
, we obtain

\mathcal{F}(t)=\mathcal{F}_{0}(t)+\mathcal{F}_{1}(t)\leqslant C(\sqrt{\mathcal{M}(t)}\sqrt{\mathcal{D}(t)}+\mathcal{D}(t)),

from which we get (by separating the cases
\mathcal{D}\leqslant\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{D}\geqslant\mathcal{F}
):

\mathcal{F}(t)^{2}\leqslant\widetilde{C}(\mathcal{M}(t)+\mathcal{F}(t))\mathcal{D}(t).

We therefore have

(4.27)

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathcal{F}(t)

\displaystyle\leq-c\,\frac{\mathcal{F}(t)^{2}}{\mathcal{F}(t)+\mathcal{M}(t)},

(4.28)

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathcal{M}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{(d-1)^{2}}\mathcal{F}(t).

The functional
\mathcal{U}(t)=\mathcal{F}(0)+\mathcal{M}(0)+\frac{2}{(d-1)^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{F}(s)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}s
satisfies,

\mathcal{U}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{2\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(t)}{(d-1)^{2}}\leq-\frac{(d-1)^{2}c}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(t)^{2}}{\mathcal{F}(t)+\mathcal{M}(t)},

and by using (
4.28
) and the fact that
\mathcal{F}(t)
is nondecreasing, we get

(4.29)

\mathcal{F}(t)+\mathcal{M}(t)\leq\mathcal{F}(0)+\mathcal{M}(0)+\frac{2}{(d-1)^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{F}(s)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}s=\mathcal{U}(t).

Thus we finally obtain
\mathcal{U}^{\prime\prime}(t)\mathcal{U}(t)\leq-p\,\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(t)^{2}
for
p=\frac{(d-1)^{2}c}{2}
, from which one deduces after integration that

\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(t)\mathcal{U}(t)^{p}\leqslant\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(0)\mathcal{U}(0)^{p}=\tfrac{2}{(d-1)^{2}}\mathcal{F}(0)\mathcal{U}(0)^{p},

and therefore by a second integration

\mathcal{U}(t)\leqslant\Big(\mathcal{U}(0)^{p+1}+\tfrac{2(p+1)}{(d-1)^{2}}\mathcal{F}(0)\mathcal{U}(0)^{p}t\Big)^{\frac{1}{p+1}}=\mathcal{U}(0)\Big(1+\tfrac{2(p+1)\mathcal{F}(0)}{(d-1)^{2}\mathcal{U}(0)}t\Big)^{\frac{1}{p+1}}.

Using (
4.27
) and (
4.29
), we therefore get, after another integration

\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}(t)}\geqslant\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}(0)}+\frac{c}{\mathcal{U}(0)}\int_{0}^{t}\Big(1+\tfrac{2(p+1)\mathcal{F}(0)}{(d-1)^{2}\mathcal{U}(0)}s\Big)^{\frac{-1}{p+1}}\mathrm{d}s\geqslant\frac{\widetilde{c}}{\mathcal{F}(0)}\Big(1+\tfrac{\mathcal{F}(0)}{\mathcal{U}(0)}t\Big)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}.

Using
\mathcal{U}(0)=\mathcal{F}(0)+\mathcal{M}(0)
and the equivalence of
\mathcal{F}
with the
H^{1}
squared norm ends the proof.
∎

5.
Well-posedness and continuity with respect to initial condition in
H^{s,0}
.

5.1.
Higher order functionals and their derivatives in time

We now study the nonlinear kinetic equation under its condensed form (

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}Q_{\mathsf{X}}(f)=Q_{\Phi(\mathsf{X})}(f)+\frac{2}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}(\mathsf{A}\mathsf{X}f)\,(\mathsf{U}f)\,f\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}v.

where

), we will also be interested in the difference between solutions to the equation, and in solutions to a similar equation when some of the terms have been frozen, as in (
2.1
), in order to interpret the solution as a fixed point. Therefore, the cubic term in the right-hand side may be taken with different functions instead of three times the function

.

For

,
g
,

in

We start by describing the main components of our higher-order energy functionals.

, and

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{A}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\,\mathcal{D}_{0,s}},

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{A}^{2}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha}\,\mathcal{D}_{1,s}},

(5.6)

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma}\,\mathcal{F}_{1,s}},

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{1}{\beta}\,\mathcal{D}_{1,s}},

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{S}\otimes\mathsf{A}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma}\,\mathcal{F}_{1,s}},

(5.7)

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{1}{\delta}\,\mathcal{F}_{1,s}},

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{2}{(d-1)\sqrt{\beta\delta}}\,\mathcal{D}_{1,s}},

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{TA}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{1}{\delta}\,\mathcal{F}_{1,s}}.

Finally, letting
\mu=1
for

,
\mu\in(0,1]
for

, or
\mu=0
for

, we have

(5.8)

\|(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{J}[f]\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{1}{\gamma^{1-\frac{\mu}{2}}\delta^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}\,\mathcal{F}_{1,s}},\quad\|(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{J}[f]\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{1}{\beta^{1-\frac{\mu}{4}}\delta^{\frac{\mu}{4}}}\,\mathcal{D}_{1,s}},

where the symbol
\lesssim
depicts inequality up to a multiplicative constant which only depends on
\mu
and
d
.

Proof of
Lemma

5.4
.

We have thanks to
5.3
:

\nu Q_{-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x}}=\nu Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}-\mathsf{T}^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\gamma}{2}Q_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}}+\delta Q_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}},\quad\nu Q_{\mathsf{A}^{2}\mathsf{\Delta}_{x}}=\nu Q_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{S}^{2}\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{A}\mathsf{T}^{2}\mathsf{A}}\leqslant\frac{\gamma}{2}Q_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{S}^{2}\mathsf{A}}+\delta Q_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{T}^{2}\mathsf{A}},

and therefore we get
\nu\mathcal{F}_{0,1}\leqslant\mathcal{F}_{1,0}
and
\nu\mathcal{D}_{0,1}\leqslant\mathcal{D}_{1,0}
thanks to (

) and (
4.16
), which applied to

give (
5.4
). The next inequalities (
5.5
), (
5.6
) and (
5.7
) directly come from

4.4
applied to

, since

implies that

and therefore

. Finally, the last inequalities come from

applied to

, giving

\|(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{J}[f]\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}+\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}^{1-\frac{\mu}{2}}\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}^{\frac{\mu}{2}}.

Using (

), (

) and

, we obtain (

).
∎

We finally define the main functionals, combining the different orders.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
m>\frac{d}{2}
,
\theta\in[0,1]
and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta,\nu
be given as positive parameters (once more, they may depend on a parameter
\tau
). For a smooth function
f
, we define its total energy functional
\overline{\mathcal{F}}(f)
as

\overline{\mathcal{F}}(f)=\mathcal{F}_{0,0}(f)+\mathcal{F}_{0,m-\theta}(f)+\mathcal{F}_{1,0}(f)+\mathcal{F}_{1,m-\theta}(f)+\nu\,\mathcal{F}_{1,m-\theta+1}(f).

Similarly, we define the total energy dissipation, total “bad” term, and total remainder (for smooth functions
g
and
h
) as

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{D}}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{0,0}(f)+\mathcal{D}_{0,m-\theta}(f)+\mathcal{D}_{1,0}(f)+\mathcal{D}_{1,m-\theta}(f)+\nu\,\mathcal{D}_{1,m-\theta+1}(f),

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{B}}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{0}(f)+\mathcal{B}_{m-\theta}(f)+\nu\,\mathcal{B}_{m-\theta+1}(f),

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{R}}(f,g,h)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{0,0}(f,g,h)+\mathcal{R}_{0,m-\theta}(f,g,h)+\mathcal{R}_{1,0}(f,g,h)+\mathcal{R}_{1,m-\theta}(f,g,h)+\nu\,\mathcal{R}_{1,m-\theta+1}(f,g,h).

In virtue of (
5.2
)–(
5.3
), we therefore have, when
f
is a smooth solution of (
3.1
):

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\mathcal{F}}(f)=-2\overline{\mathcal{D}}(f)+\overline{\mathcal{B}}(f)+\partial_{\tau}\overline{\mathcal{F}}(f)+\frac{2}{d}\overline{\mathcal{R}}(f,f,f).

We thus want to control the terms
\overline{\mathcal{B}}
,
\partial_{\tau}\overline{\mathcal{F}}(f)
and
\overline{\mathcal{R}}(f,f,f)
in terms of the dissipation
\overline{\mathcal{D}}
.
The first two (quadratic terms) are directly estimated as in
Section

4
for the kinetic Brownian motion, as stated in the following proposition. The study of the cubic quantities, to control
\overline{\mathcal{R}}(f,g,h)
, is the object of the next subsection.

Proposition 5.6
.

For any smooth function
f
, we have

(5.9)

\overline{\mathcal{B}}(f)\leqslant\Big(\frac{2(\alpha+(d-1)\beta)}{\sqrt{\beta}}+\frac{2|\gamma-\delta|}{\sqrt{\beta\delta}}\Big)\overline{\mathcal{D}}(f),

(5.10)

\partial_{\tau}\overline{\mathcal{F}}(f)\leqslant\Big(|\alpha^{\prime}|+\frac{|(\nu\alpha)^{\prime}|}{\nu}+\frac{|\beta^{\prime}|+\frac{|(\nu\beta)^{\prime}|}{\nu}}{\sqrt{\beta}}+\frac{\max(|\gamma^{\prime}|,\frac{|(\nu\gamma)^{\prime}|}{\nu})}{\beta}+\frac{2\max(|\delta^{\prime}|,\frac{|(\nu\delta)^{\prime}|}{\nu})}{(d-1)\sqrt{\beta\delta}}\Big)\overline{\mathcal{D}}(f).

Proof of

5.6
.

From

5.2
, thanks to

we directly obtain

(5.11)

\mathcal{B}_{s}(f)\leqslant\frac{\alpha+(d-1)\beta}{\sqrt{\beta}}\big(\mathcal{D}_{0,s}(f)+\mathcal{D}_{1,s}(f)\big)+\frac{2|\gamma-\delta|}{\sqrt{\beta\delta}}\mathcal{D}_{1,s}(f).

For

, we use (

) to get

\nu\mathcal{B}_{m-\theta+1}(f)\leqslant\frac{\alpha+(d-1)\beta}{\sqrt{\beta}}\big(\mathcal{D}_{1,m-\theta}(f)+\nu\mathcal{D}_{1,m-\theta+1}(f)\big)+\frac{2|\gamma-\delta|}{\sqrt{\beta\delta}}\nu\mathcal{D}_{1,m-\theta+1}(f),

to which we add (
5.11
) (for

and

), thus obtaining (

) thanks to
Definition

5.5
.

Similarly, thanks to

, we have

\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{F}_{1,s}\leqslant|\alpha^{\prime}|\mathcal{D}_{0,s}+\frac{|\beta^{\prime}|}{\sqrt{\beta}}(\mathcal{D}_{0,s}+\mathcal{D}_{1,s})+\Big(\frac{|\gamma^{\prime}|}{\beta}+\frac{2|\delta^{\prime}|}{(d-1)\sqrt{\beta\delta}}\Big)\mathcal{D}_{1,s},

Since

, we use (

) and add (

.

5.2.
Estimates for the cubic terms.

We want to provide estimates for the term

, and a first preliminary step consists in deriving some estimates for the cubic quantities

given by
Definition

5.1
, for the different operators
\mathsf{X}
that will be needed for our energy functionals.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
m>\frac{d}{2}
, and
s\geqslant 0
(not necessarily integers). We use the symbol
\lesssim
to denote inequalities up to a multiplicative constant, which may only depend on
m
,
s
and the dimension
d
.
To have lighter notation, we denote

N_{m,s}(g,h)=\|g\|_{H^{m,0}}\,\|\mathbb{J}[h]\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|g\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\,\|\mathbb{J}[h]\|_{H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

and we then have the following estimates:

(5.13)

\displaystyle R_{(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{s}}(f,g,h)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\mathsf{A}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\,N_{m,s}(g,h),

(5.14)

\displaystyle R_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{s}}(f,g,h)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\mathsf{A}^{2}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\big(N_{m,s}(g,h)+N_{m,s}(\mathsf{A}g,h)\big),

(5.15)

\displaystyle R_{-(\mathsf{AS+SA})(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{s}}(f,g,h)

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\|\mathsf{S}\otimes\mathsf{A}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}+\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\Big)\big(N_{m,s}(g,h)+N_{m,s}(\mathsf{A}g,h)\big),

(5.16)

\displaystyle R_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{s}}(f,g,h)

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(\|\mathsf{S}\otimes\mathsf{A}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}+\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\big)\Big(N_{m,s}(\mathsf{S}g,h)+N_{m,s}(g,(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}h)\big),

(5.17)

\displaystyle R_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{s}}(f,g,h)

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(\|\mathsf{T}\mathsf{A}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}+\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\big)\Big(N_{m,s}(\mathsf{T}g,h)+N_{m,s}(g,(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}h)\big).

Proof of
Lemma

5.7
.

We use the following useful estimate, when
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are functions of the space variable
x
only, which can be obtained by Fourier decomposition thanks to Young’s convolution inequality, and the fact that
(\widehat{u_{1}}*\widehat{u_{2}})(\cdot)|\cdot|^{s}\leqslant 2^{s-1}(\widehat{u_{1}(\cdot)|\cdot|^{s}}*\widehat{u_{2}}+\widehat{u_{1}}*\widehat{u_{2}(\cdot)|\cdot|^{s}})
:

\|u_{1}u_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}\lesssim\|\widehat{u_{1}}\|_{L^{1}}\|u_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}+\|\widehat{u_{2}}\|_{L^{1}}\|u_{1}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}\lesssim\|u_{1}\|_{H^{m}}\|u_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}+\|u_{2}\|_{H^{m}}\|u_{1}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}.

From there, we can then estimate products in
\dot{H}^{s,0}
as long as we have a uniform bound in
v
for one of the two terms, which, as we will see, is the case for any term involving
\mathsf{U}
. For instance we have

\|g\mathsf{U}h\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\|g(v,\cdot)\mathsf{U}h(v,\cdot)\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}\mathrm{d}v\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{S}}\|g(v,\cdot)\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}\|\mathsf{U}h(v,\cdot)\|_{H^{m}}^{2}+\|g(v,\cdot)\|_{H^{m}}^{2}\|\mathsf{U}h(v,\cdot)\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v.

Now we fix
v\in\mathbb{S}
. Recalling that
|v\wedge\mathcal{J}|^{2}=|P_{v^{\perp}}\mathcal{J}|^{2}\leqslant|\mathcal{J}|^{2}
for all
\mathcal{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\|\mathsf{U}h(v,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=d^{2}\|P_{v^{\perp}}\mathbb{J}[h]\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leqslant d^{2}\|\mathbb{J}[h]\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

Therefore, using the fact that
\mathsf{U}
and
\mathbb{J}
commute with any (fractional) derivative in
x
, we may replace
h
by
(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{s}{2}}h
in the previous inequality to get

\|\mathsf{U}h(v,\cdot)\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim\|\mathbb{J}[h]\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

and combining both similarly to get
H^{m}
instead of
\dot{H}^{s}
. We obtain

(5.18)

\|g\mathsf{U}h\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\lesssim\big(\|g\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\|\mathbb{J}[h]\|^{2}_{H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|g\|^{2}_{H^{m,0}}\|\mathbb{J}[h]\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim N_{m,s}(g,h),

and this provides the first estimate (
5.13
), as we have

R_{(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{s}}(f,g,h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{s}{2}}\mathsf{A}f(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{s}{2}}(g\mathsf{U}h)\leqslant\|\mathsf{A}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\|g\mathsf{U}h\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}.

For the next estimates, we begin similarly, estimating
\|\mathsf{A}(g\mathsf{U}h)\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\leqslant\|\mathsf{A}g\mathsf{U}h\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}+\|g\mathsf{AU}h\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}
first. We have

\|\mathsf{A}g\mathsf{U}h\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{S}}\|\mathsf{A}g(v,\cdot)\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}\|\mathsf{U}h(v,\cdot)\|_{H^{m}}^{2}+\|\mathsf{A}g(v,\cdot)\|_{H^{m}}^{2}\|\mathsf{U}h(v,\cdot)\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v\leqslant N_{m,s}(\mathsf{A}g,h)^{2}.

Using
Lemma

3.4
–
(iii)
, we also get as before that for all
v\in\mathbb{S}
,

\|\mathsf{AU}h(v,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=d^{2}(d-1)^{2}\|v\cdot\mathbb{J}[h]\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leqslant d^{2}(d-1)^{2}\|\mathbb{J}[h]\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2},

and we proceed the same (replacing
h
with
(-\Delta_{x})^{\frac{s}{2}}h
) to get

\|g\mathsf{AU}h\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{S}}\|g(v,\cdot)\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}\|\mathsf{AU}h(v,\cdot)\|_{H^{m}}^{2}+\|g(v,\cdot)\|_{H^{m}}^{2}\|\mathsf{AU}h(v,\cdot)\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v\leqslant N_{m,s}(g,h)^{2}.

This provides the second estimate (
5.14
), as we have

R_{-\mathsf{A}^{2}(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{s}}(f,g,h)\leqslant\|\mathsf{A}^{2}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\|\mathsf{A}(g\mathsf{U}h)\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\lesssim\|\mathsf{A}^{2}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\big(N_{m,s}(\mathsf{A}g,h)+N_{m,s}(g,\mathsf{A}h)\big).

To get the third estimate (
5.15
), we use the fact that that
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{S}+\mathsf{S}\mathsf{A}=2\mathsf{S}\mathsf{A}-(d-1)\mathsf{T}
. We obtain

R_{-(\mathsf{A}\mathsf{S}+\mathsf{S}\mathsf{A})(-\mathsf{\Delta}^{s})}(f,g,h)\leqslant\|(2\mathsf{SA}-(d-1)\mathsf{T})f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\|\mathsf{A}(g\mathsf{U}h)\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}.

and we use (
4.20
) to get
\|\mathsf{SA}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}=Q_{(\mathsf{AS})(\mathsf{SA})}((-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{s}{2}}f)\leqslant Q_{\mathsf{AS}^{2}\mathsf{A}}((-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{s}{2}}f)=\|\mathsf{S}\otimes\mathsf{A}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}
, which gives (
5.15
).
We treat the fourth estimate (
5.14
) similarly, using the commutator (
4.10
) and the fact that
\mathsf{A}
commutes with
\mathsf{\Delta}_{x}=\mathsf{S}^{2}+\mathsf{T}^{2}
, which gives
[\mathsf{A},\mathsf{S^{2}}]=-[\mathsf{A},\mathsf{T}^{2}]=-2\mathsf{TS}
:

\displaystyle R_{-\mathsf{S}^{2}(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{s}}

\displaystyle(f,g,h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{s}{2}}(-2\mathsf{ST}+\mathsf{S}^{2}\mathsf{A})f(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{s}{2}}(g\mathsf{U}h)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\,\|\mathsf{S}(g\mathsf{U}h)\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}+\sum_{i<j}\|\mathsf{S}\mathsf{A}_{i,j}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\,\|\mathsf{S}(g\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h)\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}

(5.19)

\displaystyle\leqslant(2\|\mathsf{T}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}+\|\mathsf{S}\otimes\mathsf{A}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}})\Bigg(\Big(\sum_{i<j}\|\mathsf{S}g\,\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\Big(\sum_{i<j}\|g\,\mathsf{S}\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigg),

where we have proceeded as in (
4.20
) (using the fact that
S_{i,j}
and
S_{k,\ell}
commute) to get

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{S}(g\mathsf{U}h)\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\sum_{k<\ell}\langle\mathsf{S}_{i,j}(g\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h),\mathsf{S}_{k,\ell}(g\mathsf{U}_{k,\ell}h)\rangle_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}=\sum_{i<j}\sum_{k<l}\langle\mathsf{S}_{k,\ell}(g\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h),\mathsf{S}_{i,j}(g\mathsf{U}_{k,\ell}h)\rangle_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i<j}\sum_{k<\ell}\frac{1}{2}\big(\|\mathsf{S}_{k,\ell}(g\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h)\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}+\|\mathsf{S}_{i,j}(g\mathsf{U}_{k,\ell}h)\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\big)=\sum_{i<j}\|\mathsf{S}(g\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h)\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}^{2},

and then used Cauchy–Schwarz. We then have, proceeding as usual:

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j}\|\mathsf{S}g\,\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{i<j}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\big(\|\mathsf{S}g(v,\cdot)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s}}\|\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h(v,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{m}}+\|\mathsf{S}g(v,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{m}}\|\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h(v,\cdot)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s}}\big)\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{S}}\big(\|\mathsf{S}g(v,\cdot)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s}}\|\mathsf{U}h(v,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{m}}+\|\mathsf{S}g(v,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{m}}\|\mathsf{U}h(v,\cdot)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s}}\big)\mathrm{d}v\lesssim N_{m,s}(\mathsf{S}g,h)^{2}.

Similarly, we have

\sum_{i<j}\|g\,\mathsf{S}\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\lesssim\sum_{i<j}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\big(\|g(v,\cdot)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s}}\|\mathsf{S}\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h(v,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{m}}+\|g(v,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{m}}\|\mathsf{S}\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h(v,\cdot)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s}}\big)\mathrm{d}v.

Fixing
v\in\mathbb{S}
and recalling that
\mathsf{S}=v\wedge\nabla_{x}
, we have

\|\mathsf{S}\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h(v,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\|v\wedge\nabla_{x}\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h(v,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leqslant\|\nabla_{x}\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h(v,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=\|\mathsf{U}_{i,j}(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}h(v,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2},

and therefore using (
1.9
), we obtain

\sum_{i<j}\|\mathsf{S}\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h(v,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant\|\mathsf{U}(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}h(v,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leqslant d^{2}\|\mathbb{J}[(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}h]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

As usual, we replace
h
by
(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x}h)^{\frac{s}{2}}
, and we obtain

\sum_{i<j}\|g\,\mathsf{S}\mathsf{U}_{i,j}h\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\lesssim\|g\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\|\mathbb{J}[(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}h]\|_{H^{m}}^{2}+\|g\|^{2}_{H^{m}}\|\mathbb{J}[(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}h]\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}\lesssim N_{m,s}(g,(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}h)^{2}.

Thanks to (
5.19
), that gives (
5.16
).
Finally, we also use the commutator (
4.10
) to get

R_{-\mathsf{T}^{2}(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{s}}(f,g,h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{s}{2}}(2\mathsf{TS}+\mathsf{T}^{2}\mathsf{A})f(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{s}{2}}(g\mathsf{U}h)\leqslant\big(\|\mathsf{S}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}+\|\mathsf{TA}f\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\big)\,\|\mathsf{T}(g\mathsf{U}h)\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}.

As before, we have
\|\mathsf{T}g\mathsf{U}h\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\leqslant N_{m,s}(\mathsf{T}g,h)
, and

\|\mathsf{TU}h(v,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=\|v\cdot\nabla_{x}\mathsf{U}h(v,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leqslant\|\nabla_{x}\mathsf{U}h(v,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leqslant d^{2}\|\mathbb{J}[(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}h]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

which gives that
\|g\mathsf{TU}h\|_{\dot{H}^{s,0}}\leqslant N_{m,s}(g,(-\mathsf{\Delta}_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}h)
. We apply therefore the same method to get (
5.17
), and this ends the proof.
∎

only for

and

, which will be used to have short time bounds, while the second one uses a mix of

and

for

Proposition 5.8
.

We let
\mu=1
when
d=2
,
\mu\in(0,1]
for
d=3
, or
\mu=0
for

. Here we use the symbol
\lesssim
to denote inequalities up to a multiplicative constant that only depends on
m
,
\theta
,
\mu
and the dimension
d
(and in particular this constant does not depend on the choice of the coefficients, as soon as they satisfy
5.3
). Then for any smooth functions
f
,
g
and
h

(5.20)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{R}}(f,g,h)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\nu^{-\theta}}\,\Big(\frac{\gamma}{\delta}\Big)^{\frac{\mu}{4}}\,\sqrt{\,\overline{\mathcal{D}}(f)\overline{\mathcal{F}}(g)\overline{\mathcal{F}}(h)},

(5.21)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{R}}(f,g,h)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\nu^{-\theta}}{\alpha}}\,\sqrt{\,\overline{\mathcal{D}}(f)}\Big(\sqrt{\,\overline{\mathcal{D}}(g)\overline{\mathcal{F}}(h)}+\sqrt{\,\overline{\mathcal{F}}(g)\overline{\mathcal{D}}(h)}\Big).

Proof of
Proposition

5.8
.

We start with interpolation estimates, in order to control the
H^{m,0}
norms appearing in
Lemma

5.7

\overline{\mathcal{F}}(t,g(t,\cdot))\leqslant\frac{1}{1-(\frac{t}{T_{M}})^{\kappa}}\bigg(\overline{\mathcal{F}}(0,g^{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\tfrac{\kappa}{MT_{M}}(\tfrac{s}{T_{M}})^{\kappa-1}\Big(1-(\tfrac{s}{T_{M}})^{\kappa}\Big)\overline{\mathcal{F}}(s,\widetilde{f}(s,\cdot))\overline{\mathcal{F}}(s,u(s,\cdot))\mathrm{d}s\bigg).

For the case
\widetilde{f}=0
, we get
g=f
and so we have

(5.34)

\overline{\mathcal{F}}(t,f(t,\cdot))\leqslant\frac{\overline{\mathcal{F}}(0,f^{0})}{1-(\frac{t}{T_{M}})^{\kappa}},

thus proving
f=\Psi_{f^{0}}(h)\in\Omega_{M}
, which gives the stability. If now
\widetilde{h}\in\Omega_{M}
, we get
\widetilde{f}=\Psi_{f^{0}}(\widetilde{h})\in\Omega_{M}
, and the estimate (
5.33
) becomes

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{F}}(t,g(t,\cdot))

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{1-(\frac{t}{T_{M}})^{\kappa}}\bigg(\overline{\mathcal{F}}(0,g^{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\tfrac{\kappa}{T_{M}}(\tfrac{s}{T_{M}})^{\kappa-1}\overline{\mathcal{F}}(s,u(s,\cdot))\mathrm{d}s\bigg)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\overline{\mathcal{F}}(0,g^{0})}{1-(\frac{t}{T_{M}})^{\kappa}}+\frac{\tfrac{\kappa}{T_{M}}(\tfrac{T}{T_{M}})^{\kappa-1}}{1-(\frac{T}{T_{M}})^{\kappa}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{Ls}\|u\|_{\mathcal{X}_{T}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s,

which gives

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{f^{0}}(h)-\Psi_{\widetilde{f}^{0}}(\widetilde{h})\|_{\mathcal{X}_{T}}^{2}=\|g\|_{\mathcal{X}_{T}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\overline{\mathcal{F}}(0,g^{0})}{1-(\frac{T}{T_{M}})^{\kappa}}+\frac{\tfrac{\kappa}{T_{M}}(\tfrac{T}{T_{M}})^{\kappa-1}}{L\Big(1-(\frac{T}{T_{M}})^{\kappa}\Big)}\|u\|_{\mathcal{X}_{T}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\|f^{0}-\widetilde{f}^{0}\|_{H^{s,0}}^{2}}{1-(\frac{T}{T_{M}})^{\kappa}}+\frac{\tfrac{\kappa}{T_{M}}(\tfrac{T}{T_{M}})^{\kappa-1}}{L\Big(1-(\frac{T}{T_{M}})^{\kappa}\Big)}\|h-\widetilde{h}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{T}}^{2},

thus providing, for

sufficiently large, both the contraction property (when
\widetilde{f}^{0}=f^{0}
) and the continuity in
\mathcal{X}_{T}
of the unique fixed point with respect to the initial condition. This ends the proof of
Theorem

1.2
, since the estimates in time come from the bound (
5.34
) (remember
\overline{\mathcal{F}}(0,f^{0})=\|f\|_{H^{m-\theta,0}}^{2}
and

) and the estimates (
5.5
)–(
5.7
).

\displaystyle\leqslant-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}\Big((1-\tfrac{\rho}{d})|x|^{2}|\mathsf{A}g|^{2}-v\cdot x\,g^{2}\Big)\,{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}v+R_{|x|^{2}\mathsf{Id}}(g,g,g)

v
). For instance we have
\mathsf{\Pi}_{0}g=\int_{\mathbb{S}}g(\cdot,v)\mathrm{d}v
and
\mathsf{\Pi}_{1}g=d\,v\cdot\mathbb{J}[g]
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\ell\geqslant 0
and
g\in H^{1,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S})
(the result is also true for a flat torus of dimension
d
instead of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
). Recall that
\|\mathsf{S}g\|_{2}^{2}=\|P_{v^{\perp}}\nabla_{x}g\|_{2}^{2}
and
\|\mathsf{S}g\|_{2}^{2}=\|P_{v^{\perp}}\nabla_{x}g\|_{2}^{2}
. We have

\|\nabla_{x}\mathsf{\Pi}_{\ell}g\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\begin{cases}\|\mathsf{S}g\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathsf{S}g\|_{2}\|\mathsf{T}g\|_{2}&\text{if }d=2,\\
\|\mathsf{S}g\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathsf{S}g\|_{2}^{2-\mu}\|\mathsf{T}g\|_{2}^{\mu}&\text{if }d=3\quad(\text{for all }\mu\in(0,1]),\\
\|\mathsf{S}g\|_{2}^{2}&\text{if }d\geqslant 4,\\
\end{cases}

where the symbol
\lesssim
denotes an inequality up to a multiplicative constant only depending on
d
,
\mu
and
\ell
.

Proof of
Lemma

A.1
.
.

We work in Fourier variable in space, writing
g(x,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{g}(\xi,v)e^{ix\cdot\xi}\mathrm{d}\xi
, and proceed mode by mode. Therefore we have
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}}|P_{v^{\perp}}\nabla_{x}g|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}}(1-(e\cdot v)^{2})|\hat{g}(\xi,v)|^{2}\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}\xi
, where we have denoted
e=\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}
, for
\xi\neq 0
.
We therefore fix
e\in\mathbb{S}
and want to study integrals of the form

\mathcal{I}_{e}(h)=\int_{\mathbb{S}}(1-(e\cdot v)^{2})h(v)^{2}\mathrm{d}v.

We therefore have

\|\mathsf{S}g\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{2}\mathcal{I}_{e}(\hat{g}(\xi,\cdot))\mathrm{d}\xi,\ \|\mathsf{S}g\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathsf{T}g\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{2}\|\hat{g}(\xi,\cdot)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi,\ \|\nabla_{x}\mathsf{\Pi}_{\ell}g\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{2}\|\mathsf{\Pi}_{\ell}\hat{g}(\xi,\cdot)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi.

In virtue of Hölder inequality,
Lemma

A.1
will be proven if we manage to prove that for any
h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
,

(A.1)

\|\mathsf{\Pi}_{\ell}h\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\begin{cases}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{e}(h)}\|h\|_{2}&\text{if }d=2,\\
\big(\mathcal{I}_{e}(h)\big)^{1-\frac{\mu}{2}}\|h\|_{2}^{\mu}&\text{if }d=3\quad(\text{for all }\mu\in(0,1]),\\
\mathcal{I}_{e}(h)&\text{if }d\geqslant 4.\end{cases}

We decompose
h
in spherical harmonics (see for instance
[
14
, Appendix A.1]
):

h(v)=\sum_{k,\ell,m}c_{\ell,m,k}(h)Y_{\ell,m,k}(v),

where
(Y_{\ell,m,k})_{0\leqslant m\leqslant\ell,1\leqslant k\leqslant n_{m}=\binom{d+m-2}{d-2}-\binom{d+m-4}{d-2}}
is an orthonormal basis of spherical harmonics
(of degree
\ell
, implying that
\mathsf{\Delta}_{v}Y_{\ell,m,k}(v)=-\ell(\ell+d-2)Y_{\ell,m,k}(v)
) for which we precise next the construction. Our goal is then to show that for any
\ell,m,k
, we have (with
\mu=1
for
d=2
and
\mu=0
for
d=4
):

(A.2)

c_{\ell,m,k}(h)^{2}\lesssim\big(\mathcal{I}_{e}(h)\big)^{1-\frac{\mu}{2}}\|h\|_{2}^{\mu}.

We write
v=\cos\theta\,e+\sin\theta\,w
with
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and
w
seen as a vector of
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
(the unit vectors orthogonal to
e
). We suppose we are given
(Z_{m,k})_{1\leqslant k\leqslant n_{m}}
an orthogonal basis of the spherical harmonics of degree
m
on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
. We then have
Y_{\ell,m,k}(v)=Q_{\ell,m}(\cos\theta)\sin^{m}\theta Z_{m,k}(w)
where
Q_{\ell,m}
is a polynomial on degree
\ell-m
, obtained from the so called Gegenbauer polynomials, but we will not need their specific expression here. Indeed, we write
h_{m,k}(\theta)=\sum_{\ell\geqslant m}c_{\ell,m,k}Q_{\ell,m}(\cos\theta)
and therefore we have

h(v)=\sum_{m,k}h_{m,k}(\theta)\sin^{m}\theta Z_{m,k}(w).

To simplify notations, we suppose that the normalization of the basis
(Z_{m,k})
is done in such a way that

\int_{\mathbb{S}}\sin^{2m}\theta Z^{2}_{m,k}(w)\mathrm{d}v=\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{2m+d-2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

and therefore we have

(A.3)

\|h\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{m,k}\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{m,k}(\theta)^{2}\sin^{2m+d-2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta,\quad\mathcal{I}_{e}(h)=\sum_{m,k}\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{m,k}(\theta)^{2}\sin^{2m+d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

We now have, using Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\begin{split}c_{\ell,m,k}(h)^{2}=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{S}}h(v)Y_{\ell,m,k}(v)\mathrm{d}v\Big)&=\Big(\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{m,k}(\theta)Q_{\ell,m}(\cos\theta)\sin^{2m+d-2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\Big)\\
&\lesssim\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{m,k}(\theta)^{2}\sin^{4m+2d-4}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta,\end{split}

and therefore when
4m+2d-4\geqslant 2m+d
(that is to say
2m+d\geqslant 4
), we obtain
c_{\ell,m,k}(h)^{2}\lesssim\mathcal{I}_{e}(h)
. So the only remaining cases to treat are
m=0
for
d\in\{2,3\}
(for which the only possibility is
k=1
).

For the case
d=3
and
m=0
, we have, since
\sin^{-1+\mu}{\theta}
is integrable on
[0,\pi]
,

\begin{split}c_{\ell,0,1}(h)^{2}&=\Big(\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{0,1}(\theta)Q_{\ell,0}(\cos\theta)\sin^{\frac{3-\mu}{2}}\theta\sin^{\frac{-1+\mu}{2}}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\Big)\\
&\lesssim\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{0,1}(\theta)^{2}\sin^{3-\mu}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\leqslant\Big(\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{0,1}(\theta)^{2}\sin^{3}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\Big)^{1-\frac{\mu}{2}}\Big(\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{0,1}(\theta)^{2}\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\Big)^{\frac{\mu}{2}},\end{split}

thanks to Holder’s inequality, which gives (
A.2
) for
m=0
and
d=3
.

For the case
d=2
(and
m=0
), the same strategy would lead to (
A.2
) with any
\mu>1
, so to refine it a little bit and get the case
\mu=1
, we explicitly write the decomposition. Indeed we have
Q_{0,0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}
and
Q_{\ell,0}(\cos\theta)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cos\ell\theta
(the Chebyshev polynomials), and therefore we obtain
h_{0,1}
under the form of its Fourier series, writing
a_{0}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}c_{0,0,1}
and
a_{\ell}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}c_{\ell,0,1}
for
\ell\geqslant 1
:

h_{0,1}(\theta)=\frac{a_{0}}{2}+\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}a_{\ell}\cos(\ell\theta)

giving for instance
\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{0,1}(\theta)^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{\ell\geqslant 0}a_{\ell}^{2}
. To compute
\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{0,1}(\theta)^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta
, we know the Fourier decomposition of
\theta\mapsto\sin\theta h(\theta)
:

\sin(\theta)h(\theta)=\frac{a_{0}}{2}\sin\theta+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}a_{\ell}(\sin((\ell+1)\theta)-\sin(\ell-1)\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}(a_{\ell-1}-a_{\ell+1})\sin(\ell\theta)

We therefore obtain

(A.4)

\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{0,1}(\theta)^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{\pi}{8}\sum_{\ell\geqslant 0}(a_{\ell}-a_{\ell+2})^{2}.

Now we have, for any
\ell_{0}\geqslant 0
,

\frac{\pi}{8}a_{\ell_{0}}^{2}+\frac{\pi}{8}a_{\ell_{0}+1}^{2}=\frac{\pi}{8}\sum_{\ell\geqslant\ell_{0}}a_{\ell}^{2}-a_{\ell+2}^{2}=\frac{\pi}{8}\sum_{\ell\geqslant\ell_{0}}(a_{\ell}-a_{\ell+2})^{2}-2a_{\ell+2}(a_{\ell}-a_{\ell+2}),

and thanks to Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and (
A.4
), we get

\begin{split}\frac{\pi}{8}a_{\ell_{0}}^{2}+\frac{\pi}{8}a_{\ell_{0}+1}^{2}&\leqslant\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{0,1}(\theta)^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta+\sqrt{\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{0,1}(\theta)^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{\ell\geqslant\ell_{0}+2}a_{\ell}^{2}}\\
&\leqslant 2\sqrt{\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{0,1}(\theta)^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta}\sqrt{\int_{0}^{\pi}h_{0,1}(\theta)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta},\end{split}

which finally proves the case
d=2
of (
A.1
), thanks to (
A.3
).
∎
