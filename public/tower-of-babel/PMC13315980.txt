Sodium bicarbonate in critical illness: Correcting acidaemia without changing outcomes?
Authors: Ary Serpa Neto
Published: 2026

Metabolic acidosis is a common biochemical abnormalities encountered in critically ill patients and is associated with organ dysfunction, acute kidney injury (AKI), vasopressor dependence, and death.[1], [2], [3], [4], [5] Despite decades of widespread use, sodium bicarbonate therapy continues to generate controversy.[6], [7], [8] The physiological rationale for bicarbonate administration appears compelling: severe acidaemia may impair myocardial contractility, reduce catecholamine responsiveness, alter vascular tone, and worsen kidney injury.6,8,9 However, whether correcting arterial pH translates into improved patient-centred outcomes has remained uncertain. The SODium BICarbonate for Metabolic Acidosis in the ICU (SODa-BIC) trial10 adds important new evidence. After a pilot trial showed that protocolised bicarbonate administration could rapidly correct acidaemia in vasopressor-dependent intensive care unit (ICU) patients,11 SODa-BIC tested whether this physiological effect translated into improved clinical outcomes. In this multicentre, adaptive, double-blind randomised trial, critically ill patients with metabolic acidosis receiving vasopressors were assigned to sodium bicarbonate or placebo. Despite rapid and protocolised correction of acid-base abnormalities, sodium bicarbonate neither reduced the primary outcome of major adverse kidney events within 30 days nor reduced mortality. These findings reinforce an increasingly consistent message in the literature: biochemical correction of metabolic acidosis does not necessarily improve clinical outcomes.

Importantly, SODa-BIC differs from previous randomised trials in several ways. Unlike the Sodium Bicarbonate in the Intensive Care Unit (BICAR-ICU) trial12 and BICARICU-2,13 which focused on patients with severe acidaemia (pH ≤ 7.20) and particularly those with moderate-to-severe AKI, SODa-BIC enrolled a broader population with less severe metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.30) but requiring vasopressor support. Moreover, it used a blinded placebo-controlled design, reducing the risk that clinician awareness of bicarbonate allocation might influence decisions regarding renal replacement therapy (RRT). This is particularly relevant because previous studies consistently suggested reductions in RRT use despite neutral mortality findings.

BICAR-ICU first generated enthusiasm for bicarbonate therapy after demonstrating improved outcomes in the prespecified subgroup with AKI stage 2–3,12 including lower mortality and reduced use of RRT. This signal prompted BICARICU-2,13 which specifically targeted patients with severe acidaemia and AKI. However, although BICARICU-2 again showed reduced RRT use, it failed to demonstrate a mortality benefit. SODa-BIC now extends these findings into a broader shock population and suggests that even when bicarbonate administration is protocolised, blinded, and physiologically effective, meaningful improvements in kidney outcomes remain elusive.

These findings have important implications for clinical practice (Fig. 1). First, the routine administration of sodium bicarbonate to critically ill patients with metabolic acidosis cannot be justified solely to improve survival or prevent major kidney-related outcomes. Across randomised trials, mortality has remained remarkably consistent between treatment groups despite successful correction of pH and bicarbonate levels. Clinicians should therefore resist the common assumption that normalising arterial pH alters the trajectory of critical illness.

Second, sodium bicarbonate should not be viewed as a universal treatment for metabolic acidosis. Metabolic acidosis is not a disease itself but rather a physiological consequence of diverse underlying processes including lactate accumulation, hyperchloraemia, renal dysfunction, ketoacidosis, and tissue hypoperfusion.6,8,9 It is increasingly clear that bicarbonate therapy likely behaves differently across these phenotypes. Patients with severe AKI, impaired renal acid excretion, or hyperkalaemia may derive more benefit than patients with predominantly lactic acidosis driven by uncontrolled shock. Indeed, the most consistent signal across trials has been in patients with concomitant AKI and severe acidaemia.

Third, SODa-BIC highlights the distinction between correcting physiology and improving outcomes. The intervention clearly worked biologically: bicarbonate rapidly increased pH and reduced acidaemia. Yet this biochemical success did not translate into fewer deaths or fewer major kidney events. Critical care history repeatedly reminds us that normalisation of physiological variables does not guarantee improved patient outcomes.14,15 Like many ICU interventions before it, bicarbonate therapy may improve surrogate markers without substantially altering the underlying disease process.

However, these results should not be interpreted as evidence that bicarbonate should never be used. The trial does not challenge the role of bicarbonate in specific situations where treatment is physiologically or clinically justified, including severe acidaemia with life-threatening hyperkalaemia, bicarbonate loss states, certain toxicological emergencies, or severe AKI-associated acidaemia where clinicians are attempting to avoid or delay immediate RRT. Instead, the findings suggest that bicarbonate should be used selectively and thoughtfully rather than reflexively.

One particularly important unresolved issue is whether bicarbonate meaningfully prevents RRT or simply delays it. Previous trials, including SODa-BIC, consistently suggested lower RRT use with bicarbonate administration,10,12,13 but RRT initiation is inherently subjective and strongly influenced by pH. Because clinicians were unblinded in earlier studies, bicarbonate-treated patients may simply have appeared “less acidotic,” reducing the perceived urgency for RRT initiation. SODa-BIC’s double-blind design partially mitigates this concern and strengthens confidence that any observed effects are less likely to be driven by clinician behaviour alone. In particular, in SODa-BIC, the time to initiation of RRT was similar between groups, arguing against the hypothesis that bicarbonate merely delayed clinician decision-making regarding RRT initiation.

A recent target trial emulation further complicates interpretation.16 Using advanced causal inference methods in more than 6000 ICU patients, the authors reported a small but statistically significant mortality reduction associated with bicarbonate administration. Such findings suggest that clinically important treatment effects may exist in selected subgroups but are likely modest and difficult to detect in conventional randomised trials. If true, future studies may require very large sample sizes or highly enriched populations to demonstrate benefit.

Future research should move beyond asking whether bicarbonate works in all patients with metabolic acidosis and instead focus on identifying the patients most likely to benefit. Severe acidaemia with AKI remains the most biologically plausible target population. Similarly, patients with hyperchloremic or uraemic acidosis may differ fundamentally from patients with profound lactic acidosis due to refractory shock. Mechanistic phenotyping, incorporating acid-base profiles, chloride burden, renal function, ventilatory reserve, and CO2 handling, may help define responders more precisely.

Ultimately, SODa-BIC provides a timely and clinically relevant message. Sodium bicarbonate reliably corrects acidaemia but correction of acidaemia alone is insufficient to improve major clinical outcomes in a broad population of critically ill patients with shock and metabolic acidosis. Clinicians should therefore prioritise treatment of the underlying cause of acidosis and reserve bicarbonate therapy for carefully selected situations where its physiological effects appear most likely to translate into meaningful clinical benefit.
