Executive Summary
 This research investigation led by a team consisting of Xiaodong (Chris) Li, Bicheng Zhou,
Yellapu Murty, Jamison Bartlett, and Rahim Zaman at the University of Virginia (UVa), and Matt
Besser at Ames Laboratory seeks to develop an improved methodology for the extraction of rare
earth (RE) metals from their native oxides for use in RE magnet production. Research is focused
on the development of a novel single-step batch processing methodology wherein neodymium
(Nd) and samarium (Sm) RE metals are non-electrochemically reduced via metallothermic
reactions from their oxides (Nd2O3 and Sm2O3) in a molten salt bath and diffused into NdFeB or
SmCo master alloys necessary for the production of RE magnets. The work is built upon
fundamental computational thermodynamics to identify optimal reactions in molten salt baths
containing CaCl2, NaCl, etc. using reductants such as Ca, Na, etc., to reduce Nd/Sm from their
oxides at the lowest possible processing temperatures. Following thermodynamic modeling, the
process is systematically evaluated and optimized experimentally. Coupling the microstructural
and compositional characterization of the experimental master alloy reaction products with
computational thermodynamic and kinetic models is used to dramatically reduce process
development time. Finally, the NdFeB and SmCo master alloys are used to process working
magnets, and the magnetic properties of the materials (remanence, coercivity, etc.) are
characterized for comparison with commercially available magnets. Experimental results have
validated computational assessments for both NdFeB and SmCo systems and led to successful
master alloy production in both systems with processing temperatures below 900 °C, process times
below 2 hours, increased Nd and Sm yield in the master alloys, and scaled production to over 100
grams in both materials. Final optimization and magnet production was completed.

Project Goals/Objectives
 The objective of the project is to develop an energy-efficient, reduced cost, and single-step
critical metal oxide reduction and alloying methodology for the production of NdFeB and SmCo
magnets to facilitate the establishment of a sustainable domestic critical materials supply chain.
The proposed method consists of an immiscible molten salt flux layer and a higher density molten
metal alloy pool (FeB or Co) below (Figure 1). The RE oxide and a reductant are added to the
molten salt layer, where the reductant first strips the oxygen from the RE oxide. Subsequently, the
separated RE metal diffuses into the molten metal pool below, creating a RE-saturated master
alloy. Towards this end, thermodynamic calculations of the reactions between RE oxides, metallic
reducing agent, and molten salt bath chemistry have been performed, and the ideal feeds and
conditions for extraction and diffusion to produce master alloys were established. Small-scale and
scaled experimentation was performed to validate and optimize the feasibility of viable reactions,
temperatures, and process conditions with regards to yield, composition, and process efficiency.
Finally, full-scale experiments for the production of NdFeB and SmCo magnets were performed,
and their performance characteristics were established.

 Novel procedures were developed based upon computational thermodynamics and kinetics to
optimize salt bath chemistry to establish the lowest processing temperature for optimal reaction
driving forces and kinetics. The composition of the processed RE master alloy ingots were
analyzed with respect to purity, recovery rates, and reaction efficiency. Cost analysis was
performed of the proposed production of precursor RE master alloys and compared to the currently
existing manufacturing cost models. The work has been applied to the Nd-Fe-B system, reducing
Nd from Nd2O3 and the Sm-Co system, reducing Sm from Sm2O3 to produce master alloys; the
methods have been evaluated and tested for both material systems, meeting several crucial
validation milestones during the project period including low temperature < 900 °C production
and scalability from initial small-scale tests. Magnets were produced from the developed master
alloys and characterized relative to commercially available ones.

Background
 The work of this project has the combined benefit of both establishing the domestic availability
of critical materials through energy efficient, environmentally acceptable metallothermic
reductions and ultimately improving extraction time and expense. Critical materials are used in a
variety of fields and are necessary for the continued production of a wide range of modern products
and technologies. Exacerbating the issue of the continual increase in demand for these materials is
the growing dependence on foreign markets for their production. Presently, the majority of critical
materials used by U.S. industries are imported from foreign sources. A telling example is that
China has become nearly the sole supplier for RE materials for the U.S., which directly impacts
the uncertainty and insecurity of several critical fields in the U.S., including the electronics,
aviation, automotive and defense industries. In addition to the naturally high reserves of these
critical elements, another important factor in the Chinese control of the global market of RE
element production and export is its investment and development of a complete production line,
from ore mining and ore extraction to reduction and refinement. In order to achieve a sufficient
domestic supply of critical materials and to reduce the nation’s vulnerability to disruptions in the
supply of these critical materials, significant research efforts and investments must be made to
identify new and improved processing routes for the extraction of critical materials from their
starting compounds, allowing for the development of a new complete cost-effective and
environmentally friendly downstream domestic processing and manufacturing supply chain. The
 Page 4 of 56

proposed project aims to find a new approach to reduce critical metal oxides with direct alloying
to desired magnetic compositions, facilitating the construction of a more feasible critical materials
supply chain for RE magnets which can reduce U.S. dependency on foreign imports and improve

are the reduction in expense and energy consumption for producing RE magnets, the establishment
of a streamlined cost-effective method for the reduction of RE oxides applicable to critical
materials, and by these means, added stability to the U.S. RE supply chain. The feasibility of this

1.1.4. Determination of Optimal Process Operating Conditions
 The choice of operating conditions for experiments in both RE systems was explored. One
such decision is whether to use the RE oxide or oxychloride as the feed material. Based on the
calculated reaction free energies in Figure 3, the REOCl reduction steps are more favorable than
the RE2O3 reduction steps. This is consistent with current experimental results (Section 2), which
show that NdOCl reduction results in a higher Nd yield compared to that of Nd2O3.
 The optimal amounts and processing sequence of the reactants were determined for the two
cases of initial feed material for Nd2O3 reduction. If Nd2O3 is used as the feed, optimal reactant
ratios of 1.00 g CaCl2 per g of Nd2O3 (three times the stoichiometric 0.33 g CaCl2 per g Nd2O3)
and 0.46 g Ca per g Nd2O3 (20 wt% excess [6] the stoichiometric 0.38 g Ca per g Nd2O3) were
obtained. An excess of three times the stoichiometric CaCl2:Nd2O3 ratio was assumed due to CaCl2
depletion and incomplete reaction observed in the present experiments when a stoichiometric ratio
was used. An equivalent optimal 1 g CaCl2 per g NdOCl ratio was assumed, since the CaCl2 acts
as a solvent for the second reduction step to improve reaction efficiency. The recommended excess
Ca:NdOCl ratio was found to be 0.40 g Ca per g NdOCl (20 wt% excess [6] the stoichiometric
0.33 g Ca per g NdOCl). For optimal reduction reactions, it is suggested to first melt the CaCl2,
then add the Ca so that it has time to dissolve in the melt. This step should be performed prior to
adding the Nd2O3 or NdOCl, as the low solubility of Ca in CaCl2 melts [18] causes the Ca to
require additional time to dissolve in the melt for more favorable reactions [19].

 To optimize separation of the RE metals from the salt melt to the bottom of the crucible to
alloy with the melt pool, calculations focused on reducing the viscosity of the salt melt were
performed. One proposed method of reducing the salt melt viscosity was to minimize formation
of the NdOCl-CaO network structure during the process, as oxychlorides are known to increase
the viscosity of molten salts during metallothermic reductions [3, 5, 6, 20]. Based on calculated
reaction free energies in Figure 12, it was determined that Ca oxidation to CaO has a much larger
thermodynamic driving force compared to the reduction reactions. As such, it was proposed to
perform the Nd2O3 and NdOCl reduction reactions under an inert argon atmosphere. In addition,
an excess of CaCl2 prevents CaO saturation in the salt melt, limiting NdOCl-CaO network
formation.

 An optimal process temperature was determined based on the calculated thermodynamic
driving forces and heats of the reduction and intermetallic compound formation reactions (Section

900-1100 oC, slowing the reaction and diffusion kinetics of the process. Moreover, the modeled
reaction kinetics (Section 1.2.), sharply slow down below 900 oC. Consequently, 900 oC was
chosen as the optimal temperature, which is attainable based on the effective temperatures in

1.2. Kinetic Modeling
1.2.1. Objectives
 Kinetic modeling of both steps of the Nd2O3 reduction, as well as preliminary modeling of the
first step of the Sm2O3 reduction was performed in conjunction with differential scanning
calorimetry (DSC) experiments to identify the rate-limiting reduction step. Endothermic and
exothermic processes occurring in preliminary CaCl2, as well as in each reaction step and in the
overall reductions were identified using DSC and phase transition data from FactSage [12]. Based
on the exothermic peak temperatures corresponding to the reduction reaction steps, rate constants
were determined using the Kissinger method [29, 30]. The rate constants were used to calculate
particle size-independent kinetics, as well as size-dependent ones using the shrinking core model
(SCM) [31]. The kinetics of both reduction steps were compared to determine the rate-limiting
step, which is incorporated into the decision of whether to use the oxide or oxychloride as the feed.
DSC experiments were performed between room temperature and 1100 oC at the University of
Virginia and Ames Laboratory using a 30 wt% Ca excess to ensure complete reductions in the
amounts shown in Table 3. Al2O3 crucibles were used for all runs based on successful furnace
tests.

1.2.4. Particle Size-Dependent Kinetic Models
 To determine the particle size dependency of the Nd2O3, NdOCl, or Sm2O3 feed, particle size
was incorporated into the kinetic models based on the previous experimentally determined reaction
mechanism of the first Nd2O3 reduction step. In a study by Mukherjee et al. [39], high-temperature
confocal scanning laser microscopy was used to observe the first reduction step. The reaction
occurs instantly and consists of a surface reaction between Nd2O3 and CaCl2 in which CaCl2 enters
the Nd2O3 particles through branches in the surface, also observed in a later study by Song et al.
using SEM [36]. The CaO product readily dissolves into the CaCl2 melt, while the NdOCl spalls
off the particle after sufficient reaction has taken place, exposing more surface area on the Nd2O3
particle. The second step was assumed to have a similar mechanism based on calculations of the
Pilling-Bedworth ratio (PBR) [39]. Since the PBR of the solid products to the solid reactants is 1
< PBR = 1.138 < 2, the products have compressive strength on the NdOCl surface. However,
                                                                                                   Page 25 of 56

because CaO readily dissolves in CaCl2 [21], the products lose compressive strength and Nd spalls
off. This proposed mechanism is similar to that determined for the first reduction step and was
modeled likewise. Furthermore, the first step of the Sm2O3 reduction was also assumed to have
the same mechanism as in the Nd2O3 case based on a PBR of the solid products to the solid
reactants of 1 < PBR = 1.764 < 2 and the favorable dissolution of CaO in CaCl2. To simulate this
mechanism, the SCM proposed by Levenspiel [31] was applied to both Nd2O3 and first Sm2O3
reduction reaction steps.
 Based on the reaction mechanism, the resistances, or contributions to the total
Nd2O3/NdOCl/Sm2O3 particle conversion time, include (1) The reaction of Nd2O3 or Sm2O3 with
CaCl2 or that of NdOCl with Ca and (2) Spallation of NdOCl, Nd, or SmOCl and diffusion of CaO,
NdOCl, Nd, SmOCl, and CaCl2 products through the boundary layer into the CaCl2 melt. However,
to determine whether the diffusional resistances are negligible, a comparison of the reaction rate
constants from the Kissinger analysis on a surface area basis and mass transfer coefficients of
NdOCl for 200 μm Nd2O3 particles was performed. A temperature range of 800-900 oC was chosen

2. Experimental Studies of Single Step Nd-Fe-B and Sm-Co Master Alloy Production
 In order to achieve the primary goal of this project, namely the creation and validation of a
single-step non-electrolytic process to directly convert RE metal oxides to RE master alloys of
NdFeB and SmCo for magnet production (Figure 1), an exhaustive set of experiments was
performed. These experiments were carried out first to validate the efficacy of the proposed
method, and subsequently to optimize the composition and conditions of the process to create high-
quality master alloys. These experiments studied the effects of time, temperature, environment,
initial composition prior to melting, and finally processing techniques after the melting/alloying
process. These experiments were carried out in a highly collaborative manner between UVa and
AMES, with significant input from Materion, Electron Energy Corporation, and Rare Earth Salts.
The final outcome of this project’s effort was the creation and optimization of the scalable single-
step non-electrolytic process which resulted in RE master alloys of appropriate composition for
magnet production. The results of these efforts are described in detail within this final report.

2.1. Initial Validation of the Reduction Process
 To initially confirm that the suggested reaction pathways occurred at temperatures around
~1000 oC, simple experiments without optimized parameters were conducted to establish the
feasibility of the overall reduction pathway, given by:

Nd2 O3 + 3 Ca → 3 CaO + 2 Nd                                                                                      (27)

 Therein, neodymium oxide was mixed with calcium in a rudimentary fashion. Figure 36 shows
the backscatter SEM micrographs of the raw material from the experiment, where contrast in color
indicates the presence of different materials. Figure 37 displays the EDS images of oxygen and
neodymium for the sample. Critically, as shown in the circled regions, the presence of neodymium
not in its original oxide form was found to be present in the sample. This is indicated by the sections
of increased neodymium without an associated increase in oxygen, which confirms that the
material present in these regions is a reduced neodymium form rather than the starting neodymium
oxide.

 Next, after confirming the feasibility of the overall reduction, an experiment was performed to
establish that the first reduction step occurs as predicted, namely the formation of NdOCl as given
by:

        Nd2 O3 + CaCl2 → CaO + 2 NdOCl                                                                     (28)

 The experiment was performed in order to establish the production of NdOCl from the mixing
of neodymium oxide and calcium chloride, at the selected temperature given from the
thermodynamic modeling, without the addition of calcium. Through characterization, the
production of NdOCl in the experiment was confirmed. Figure 38 displays the EDS map of
chlorine and calcium in the sample. The circled regions highlight increased chlorine without the
presence of increased calcium, which confirm the regions are not associated with calcium chloride
but instead with the desired NdOCl. Both of these experiments were performed at 900 oC.

thermodynamic reduction equations of the Nd system in Section 1, at an increased temperature of
1050 °C compared to the initial 900 °C experiments. These experiments were performed using
both Nd2O3 and NdOCl and produced a significant quantity of Nd-rich regions within each sample,

 Additionally, the effects of adding additional starting CaCl2, Ca or Nd2O3 was assessed at this
early stage. Initial results showed that none of these samples resulted in as much Nd production as
the first stoichiometric experiments, however, crucible sizes and weight limitations were
determined to have played a role in these results. It was verified that for every experiment
                                               Page 31 of 56

performed, Nd was in fact reduced from the oxide via the reductions proposed. It was determined
that experiments starting with NdOCl produced more Nd separated regions than those starting with
Nd2O3. It was also established that higher temperatures produce more Nd separation for the oxide
system with the same starting material ratios. Increasing temperature for the oxychloride system
resulted in similar Nd levels but created a more dispersed network of Nd through the resulting

 Critically, these initial experiments were solely focused on the Nd (and by extension Sm)
reduction from the starting oxide, and did not yet incorporate any alloying metals, which would
soon change the landscape of the work in the remainder of the project. This step, however,
verified these reductions would occur as proposed at low temperatures at or below 1000 ºC, and
laid the groundwork for remaining experiments.

2.2. Time and Temperature Dependency of Reduction and Master Alloy Creation
 Expanding to the true scope of the project and incorporating master alloying elements of Fe-B
for the Nd system and Co for the Sm system, extensive experimentation was performed to optimize
processing times, temperatures, and starting compositional ratios of CaCl2, Ca and the oxides and
alloying elements. Initial experiments were performed using the stoichiometric ratios of the overall
reduction equations. For the Nd system FeB was added in the initial mix at 4 wt% B (a low
temperature eutectic of FeB) at a starting ratio of 50:50 Nd2O3:FeB. For the Sm system, Co was
added at a similar 50:50 ratio of Sm2O3:Co.
 These experiments were performed at temperatures ranging from 800 ºC to 1100 ºC. The initial
alloying experiments, performed in a box furnace in alumina crucibles in air environments were
successful from the onset in demonstrating the potential of the single-step master alloying process
at a small scale (<15 g). The growth of NdFe crystals within the resulting materials was also readily
observed via SEM and EDS imaging, as shown in Figures 42 and 43, providing validation of the
reduction and alloying with Fe (note that Boron is not readily observed using EDS imaging).

 Following the initial success with master alloy creation for both NdFe and SmCo systems, we
next sought to determine the effect that argon protection had during processing of the master
alloys/reduction of the oxides. In an initial experiment, we had observed an improvement in master
alloy yield using argon, and we attribute this observation largely to the reduction in the production
of CaO. This was apparent by examining SEM images of the cross-sections of NdFeB master
alloys produced in both air and argon, which corresponded to an increase of Nd/Sm yield in the
master alloys is largely explained by reduced viscosity in the melt, the same function of reduced
CaO also contributes to internal porosity. It can be seen in Figure 49 that in the air produced sample
that larger sections of CaO were formed and remained trapped in the matrix upon solidification.
This resulted in further cracking near these regions, which leads to increased porosity in the
metallic samples and increased. The increased viscosity of the melt due to higher concentrations
of CaO present is suspected as the reason for improved Nd/Sm yields under argon protection.

further optimization.

this investigation: the REO reduction/master alloy process can be performed without the use of
added calcium to the mixture. This discovery revealed another potential path to further cost
reduction and overall simplification of the reduction/alloying method, and has not been reported
in previous literature to our knowledge. However, we hypothesize that Ca can be extracted from

noticeable extent. We also noted that the NdFe system, without the use of boron, slightly reduced
porosity in the samples, though did not significantly affect Nd yield on a consistent basis.
Therefore, while possible to produce the master alloys without added Ca, the Nd/Sm yields were
found to be improved with the use of Ca, and was therefore retained in following experiments.

2.4. Final Processing of Master Alloy Products to Produce Magnets
 Following these successes, utilizing data from the experimental studies performed to date
examining the relationships between temperature, time, calcium content, total starting
composition, environmental gas, and scalability to the resulting material of the reduction process,
the final processing steps for magnetic material production were studied and optimized.
 It was determined that the master alloy process does in fact occur and results in desirable
NdFeb/SmCo alloys, however the process creates a false illusion of solid metal ingots. When in
fact the resulting reduction products formed tightly packed solids consisting of a large percentage
of NdFeB and SmCo alloys (determined via XRD and SEM measurements), within a remaining
matrix of calcium chloride. The “solid” material products from the process (Figure 58a) could be

                                          Page 41 of 56

easily broken apart, combined with the XRD and EDS results this demonstrated the reduction had
occurred, yet undesirable calcium chloride salt remained as a binding matrix.
 Thus, the quantity and quality of the NdFe products resulting from the reduction were
improved. However, with the proclivity for the metal alloys to remain attached to the CaCl 2 slag
material, further development of separation methods was required to extract the desired metal
material.
 Therefore, final efforts were then made to understand and improve the final preparation method
to remove remaining CaCl2 slag/debris from the metallic master alloy material, leaving only
desirable materials; for further processing into magnets it is important to remove undesirable salt
slag from the desired NdFeB/SmCo alloys known to be present in the products.
 To achieve effective separation, we developed methods utilizing acetic acid to remove
remaining CaCl2 and other products from the master alloys. Resulting master alloy products
(Figure 58a) were first broken into small sections and were then subjected to a cleaning process in
a 5% acetic acid bath for 24 hours, with a 90 minute ultrasonic cleanse (Figure 58b). These pieces
were easily broken into powder material during this processing step, allowing for effective
separation. Utilizing acetic acid for cleaning/separation allowed for the dissolution of CaCl2 from
the metal powders into the liquid. The resulting supernatant was then separated from the precipitate
material using a pipette. Through experimentation, this was then expanded to a 4x acid soak step
with 12 hour intervals after the initial 24 hour acid soak, where the liquid slag material containing
CaCl2 was removed after each soak and new acetic acid was introduced. Figure 58c shows the
progression during the cleaning steps.
 Subsequently, after the final separation of the supernatant containing the undesired CaCl2 in
solution, the powder was rinsed using ethanol to remove any remaining acid and was then dried in
an 80 ºC furnace for 48 hours. Finally, after drying the powder was additionally separated using
magnetic separation to remove any non-metallic powders. The dried and separated alloy powder
was then ground using a mortar and pestle, resulting in a fine NdFeB/SmCo powder (Figure 58d)
which was then subjected to compositional analysis via XRD.

 Finally, this unique one-step process is expected to significantly reduce the overall expense
required to produce rare earth magnets directly from native RE oxides. By reducing processing
steps to a single unified melting process which simultaneously incorporates the reduction of
Nd/Sm from their respective oxide forms and direct alloying with FeB/Co the number of
manufacturing processing steps is simplified from that involved with conventional electrolytic
conversion (multiple reductions, cleaning/purification, followed by alloying) to one single step.
Subsequent processing steps (e.g. re-melting, flaking, pulverization) remain the same and
equivalent. Thus, considering material costs equal by the London Metal Exchange (LME), by
reducing the reduction and alloying processes to a single unified process, we expect time and
energy savings, and inherent cost savings, of approximately 20%. Furthermore, the process
developed in this work has been shown to be highly scalable without degradation in alloy
composition, which may further reduce energy/cost expenditure on a scaled basis to an even
greater extent relative to conventional processing methods.
 In summary, through the course of this project period we have created a novel single-step non-
electrolytic metallothermic reduction method to directly reduce the rare earth elements Nd and Sm
from their native oxides and simultaneously create master alloys of NdFeB and SmCo, desirable
RE magnet compositions. Through extensive experimentation and supporting thermodynamic
modeling we have developed and optimized this unique and efficient process to determine both
what is possible and what is optimal with regards to temperature, time, environment, material ratios
as well as developed the necessary final separation and cleaning method to produce high quality
RE master alloys for magnet production. We have also validated the scalability of the developed
process, demonstrating a unique potential for industrial scale applications which may drastically
reduce total expenses from native oxide to RE magnet.
 The methodology to produce master alloys containing RE materials (e.g. Neodymium,
Samarium, Praseodymium, etc.) directly from their native oxides (e.g. Nd2O3, Sm2O3, etc.) or other
RE halides (e.g. fluorides and chlorides), for the production of RE containing materials/alloys and
magnets is also briefed as below. The process directly reduces RE metals from oxide/halide
                                                Page 50 of 56

compound forms and converts them to desirable master alloys in a single process step through
reduction and chemical diffusion with host alloying elements to form a master alloy enriched with
RE metals in a molten salt bath. The key contribution is the combination of the separation of RE
elements from their oxides/halides and alloying with desirable constituents at low temperatures,
without electrolysis, in a single step, greatly reducing manufacturing expense and time with a high
transition potential into a full-scale manufacturing operation. In doing so, this process also reduces
manufacturing emissions by reducing necessary manufacturing steps while also eliminating
chemicals necessary for electrolytic methods. The molten salt bath strips the RE metal from its
oxide, and the favorable thermodynamic reactions then allow the RE metal to be alloyed with the
underlying metals, such as NdFeB and SmCo. This process is applicable to other RE elements and
alloys as well, bridging a large gap in the marketplace where these improvements can gain a strong
foothold.

3. Impacts and Conclusions
 The primary innovation of this study is the development of a unique one-step process wherein
oxide reduction is favorable and the extracted RE elements are directly alloyed with desired
magnet constituents in the same step. This process is linearly scalable to production size and
remedies key deficiencies encountered in previously studied metallothermic processes.
 A symbiotic investigation using fundamental thermodynamic calculations and simultaneous
experiments ensured internal feedback and expedient development of the methodology. The
compositions and temperatures of the process were investigated using computational
thermodynamics and kinetics and experimental optimization. Ca and pure CaCl2 were determined
to be the optimal reducing agent and salt melt chemistry, with optimal compositions determined.
850 oC was concluded to be the minimum salt melt temperature, reachable at lower operating
temperatures due to the exothermicity of all reduction reaction steps, while optimal temperatures
were approximately 1050 oC. Thermodynamic and kinetic modeling indicate that starting from
NdOCl results in higher reaction driving forces and faster kinetics at and above 900 oC, with no
significant increase in feed costs compared to Nd2O3. Experimental parameters of the process were
optimized using the thermodynamic and kinetic analyses to rapidly optimize the process yield and
reduce energy consumption by studying the effect each process parameter (time, temperature,
starting quantities/ratios, environment) had on the outcome. Optimized RE master alloys were
produced for both NdFeB and SmCo from their native oxides in a single production step.
Separation and cleaning methods were also developed in order to remove undesired slag materials.
The resulting master alloy compositions were very desirable relative to commercially produced
Nd and Sm magnets. The materials have been further processed for magnet production and
characterization. Through this unique approach, the cost and energy consumption of processing
RE metals for the production of NdFeB and SmCo magnets can be significantly reduced, bolstering
                                            Page 51 of 56

supply chain continuity and domestic manufacturing in countless industrial sectors of critical
interest to the Department of Energy. NdFeB and SmCo magnets were produced using the novel
RE oxide reduction method and their characteristics and performance were evaluated for
comparison to currently available magnets. The RE oxide reduction method with direct alloying
is also applicable to other RE oxides, thereby providing a route to cost and energy reduction in the
processing of a wide range of critical materials for a wide range of industries. The effort completed
under this DOE support clearly identified a viable process and a pathway to commercialization.
The optimization of the final reduction process sequence that separates the master alloy in bulk
from the reacted salts/dross and optimization RE metal recovery are the remaining bottle necks
requiring future efforts. Our commercial industry partners are interested in promoting and
following up this technology.

Changes/Problems
We have found that kinetic modeling aided by DSC measurements is more effective, faster, and
more than sufficient for us to further optimize the process. As evidence of such, Figure 33 shows
the time required for complete Nd2O3 conversion to NdOCl for the process versus temperature and
size of the starting Nd2O3 particles. Using this kinetic method, we have been able to determine
more optimal processing times based on operating temperatures for the Nd system. Once both
steps of the Nd2O3 reduction were kinetically modeled, the rate-limiting step was determined. This
can be used to guide the decision of using Nd2O3 or NdOCl for the feed material. The kinetic
information also helped us optimize the processing times to reduce energy consumption further in
the production of the master alloys. The same method was applied to the second step of the Nd2O3
reduction, as well as to the SmCo system.
 These kinetic modeling methods have proven far more effective in our pursuit of optimization
for the master alloy compositions. While machine learning has tremendous promise in many
applications, and was our original intention, the number of variables required in these complex
systems has made the kinetic modeling approach far more accessible, quicker, and more suited to
the goals of this project. It has been found that machine learning inputs including variables such
as time, temperature, composition (of multiple starting components) coupled with difficulty to
ascertain outputs of precise composition, purity and solidity make such methods unnecessarily
difficult to use. Especially in the light of the fact that experimentally we have proven the method
to be effective, and to be scalable. As such, we shifted from the machine learning method to using
kinetic modeling as the primary means for optimization. We have already proven scalability of our
process experimentally, this kinetic modeling approach has provided us the best means to further
optimize the composition of the master alloys, in the quickest manner possible, for both NdFeB
and SmCo, and achieve our ultimate task.
 We have shown thus far the successful reduction of both Nd and Sm from their oxides, the
production of NdFeB and SmCo master alloys from this reduction in a single step, and the
scalability of the process. The budget remained the same as originally proposed.
