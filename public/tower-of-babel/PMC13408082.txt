Synthesis of ZIF-62 Glass and Solid-state Lithium Superconductor Composite Membranes for Electrodialytic Lithium Extraction
Authors: Liang Feng, Shuhao An, Xin Wang, Yanxiong Ren, Shumei Wang, Xiaole Li, Gilles Lubineau, Zhen Li, Ben Hang Yin, Zhiping Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLithium superionic conductors such as Li3xLa2/3−xTiO3 (LLTO) enable rapid and selective lithium-ion transport through their crystalline frameworks and have been widely explored for lithium extraction. However, their implementation in membrane-based separations is hindered by the need for high-temperature sintering ( > 1000 °C), which leads to high energy consumption and potential lithium volatilization, thereby compromising structural integrity and scalability. Here, we report a structurally integrated composite membrane by embedding LLTO nanoparticles into a ZIF-62 glass matrix via a low-temperature melt-casting strategy, yielding a dense, defect-free membrane with intimate interfacial integration and improved mechanical robustness. Systematic investigations across a wide composition range reveal effective transport channels are highly restricted until a critical content is reached, after which an extensive interconnected LLTO network forms. The optimized membrane exhibits high lithium selectivity and delivers a Li/Mg selectivity of up to ~59,000 in Red Sea water under electrochemical operation, while maintaining stable lithium extraction performance across diverse natural brines. This study establishes a scalable membrane design strategy with good processability, offering broader opportunities for not only lithium extraction, but also ion-selective membranes in electrochemical separations and energy storage systems.Subject terms: Chemical engineering, Synthesis and processing

Introduction

With the rapid growth of the lithium battery industry and the accelerating depletion of high-grade lithium ores1, conventional lithium reserves are expected to be exhausted in the coming decades. As a result, extracting lithium from low-grade aqueous resources has emerged as a critical research frontier due to their immense abundance2. Various technologies have been proposed to address this challenge, among which membrane-based processes have gained substantial interest for their low energy consumption, high operational stability, and compatibility with continuous operation3–6. These advantages make membrane technology particularly well suited for lithium recovery from dilute environments.

Solid-state lithium superionic conductors, such as Li3xLa2/3−xTiO3 (LLTO) and Li1+xAlxGe2−x(PO4)3 (LAGP), have attracted widespread attention as membrane materials for lithium extraction. These crystalline ceramics feature well-defined perovskite or NASICON-type frameworks that selectively facilitate lithium-ion transport through structurally confined pathways7,8. Benefiting from their intrinsically high ionic conductivity and size-exclusion effects, such membranes can achieve high Li⁺/Mg²⁺ selectivity while maintaining appreciable lithium-ion flux under electro-driven operation9–11. However, the practical deployment of ceramic membranes remains hindered by processing and cost limitations. They typically require high-temperature sintering around 1000 °C or higher, which not only leads to high energy consumption and process complexity, but also induces lithium volatilization, resulting in crystal degradation or the formation of secondary phases12–15. Mixed-matrix membranes (MMMs) have emerged as a promising strategy to address these limitations and have been widely investigated. However, their performance is closely associated with interfacial characteristics, such as “sieve-in-a-cage” structures which may introduce interfacial voids that act as non-selective leakage pathways, underscoring the importance of interface engineering in MMM design16–18.

As an emerging class of materials, Metal-organic-framework (MOF) glasses exhibit structural continuity similar to amorphous inorganic glasses19–23. Benefiting from their physicochemical similarity to conventional inorganic glasses, MOF glasses represent a distinct class of melt-quenched glass materials with comparable network structures, which may enable favorable compatibility with inorganic phases24–26. Their relatively low melting temperatures ( ~ 400–450 °C) enable processing under much milder conditions compared to conventional ceramic sintering, thereby reducing energy consumption25,27,28. In addition, viscous flow in the molten state may promote encapsulation of ceramic particles and interfacial densification, thereby contributing to improved structural integrity and processing controllability29. Among them, ZIF-62 is particularly promising, as it can be readily converted into a dense amorphous glass via melt-casting to form robust, free-standing membranes with good structural integrity and stability under aqueous conditions27,28. Furthermore, the continuous glass matrix and its potential toughness may help mitigate the intrinsic brittleness of ceramic phases, offering a pathway toward mechanically reliable composite membranes. Despite these advantages, in such composite systems, the formation of ion-conduction pathways, their evolution with composition, and their coupling with structural densification and mechanical stability remain insufficiently understood.

In this work, we develop a composite membrane by embedding LLTO nanoparticles within a ZIF-62 glass matrix using a melt-casting strategy. We show that when the LLTO content exceeds a critical threshold, the system transitions from isolated particles to an interconnected ion-conduction network, thereby enabling enhanced Li⁺ transport. The optimized membrane exhibits high lithium extraction performance and operates reliably across diverse brine conditions. Meanwhile, the composite effectively mitigates the brittle behavior of ceramic phases while maintaining efficient ion transport, thereby improving mechanical reliability. These results highlight a strategy that integrates low-temperature processability, structural integrity, and selective ion transport, providing a scalable platform for lithium extraction from complex aqueous resources.

Results

Phase and morphology evolution with temperature

The composite membranes are denoted as (LLTO)x(ZIF-62 glass)1−x, where x represents the mass fraction of LLTO (e.g., x = 0.1 corresponds to 10 wt% LLTO). As shown in Fig. 1a, the resulting composite membrane exhibits a dense and uniform cross-sectional morphology, with a continuous structure and no observable macroscopic defects. The membrane thickness is approximately 100 μm. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping confirms that the bright particles observed in the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image correspond to the spatial distribution of LLTO within the matrix. To understand the formation of this structure during thermal treatment, we systematically investigated the composite's structural evolution under controlled heating.

Temperature-dependent structural evolution of the composite membrane.

a Cross-sectional SEM image of the prepared membrane and the inset shows Ti EDS mapping. b In-situ XRD patterns of ZIF-62 and the composite membrane recorded from 390 to 450 °C. c Solid-state NMR spectra comparing crystalline ZIF-62, ZIF-62 glass, the physical mixture of crystalline ZIF-62 and LLTO before thermal treatment (ZIF-62@LLTO), and the composite after thermal treatment (ZIF-62 glass@LLTO), * indicate the characteristic peaks of residual DMF. d SEM images showing the temperature-dependent morphological evolution of the hybrid composite.

The phase evolution is first studied by in-situ X-ray diffraction (XRD, Fig. 1b) over the temperature range from room temperature to 450 °C. The results show that the characteristic diffraction peaks of ZIF-62 remain largely unchanged and clearly visible below 420 °C, but almost completely disappear above 430 °C. In contrast, the characteristic peaks of LLTO remain unchanged throughout the entire temperature range. These results indicate that ZIF-62 undergoes a crystalline-to-amorphous transition at approximately 420–430 °C, whereas LLTO remains structurally stable under the same conditions. The temperature-dependent changes in the ZIF-62 diffraction peaks are nearly identical across different LLTO contents. Pure ZIF-62 and composite membranes with varying LLTO contents exhibit similar crystalline-to-amorphous transition behavior under the same heating conditions (Fig. S1), suggesting that the phase transition is primarily governed by ZIF-62 and that the incorporation of LLTO does not significantly affect the melting and vitrification behavior of ZIF-62. Notably, no discernible structural changes are observed below ~390 °C for either pure ZIF-62 or the composite system (Fig. S1b–c), indicating good structural stability at lower temperatures, with the phase transition occurring only at elevated temperatures. Solid-state 13C cross-polarization magic angle spinning (13C CP/MAS) nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR, Fig. 1c) further reveals the evolution of the local structure. Several minor peaks attributed to residual N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) disappear after thermal treatment, while the main resonance peaks remain largely preserved, indicating that the local coordination environment is retained after vitrification. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, Fig. S2) shows a pronounced endothermic peak at approximately 423 °C for all samples, corresponding to the melting process of ZIF-62. The negligible variation in peak position with changing LLTO content is consistent with the XRD results, which means the incorporation of ceramic fillers does not affect the intrinsic melting and vitrification behavior of the MOF matrix. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Fig. S3) analysis shows no discernible shifts in the binding energies of key elements during the transition from crystalline ZIF-62 to the glassy state, or before and after thermal treatment in the presence of LLTO. This suggests the absence of strong chemical interactions or secondary phase formation during processing, confirming that LLTO remains chemically stable within the MOF glass matrix. The consistent spectral features across different compositions and thermal states further support the chemical stability of both components. Meanwhile, CO₂ adsorption measurements (Fig. S4) reveal that the specific surface area decreases from 111.8 m² g⁻¹ for crystalline ZIF-62 to 68.9 m² g⁻¹ after vitrification, while the corresponding LLTO-containing composite further decreases to 33.9 m² g⁻¹. These results indicate a substantial reduction of the intrinsic microporosity during the melting–vitrification process and the formation of a significantly densified glassy structure. The above structural evolution directly influences the membrane morphology. As shown in Fig. 1d, at 350 °C, ZIF-62 retains its crystalline particle morphology and forms a loosely packed structure with LLTO, characterized by abundant interparticle voids and interfacial defects, resulting in a porous structure with a white appearance. Upon heating to 400 °C, particle-like features remain visible, but the number of pores and interfacial defects decreases, and the membrane surface becomes smoother, accompanied by a color change from white to light yellow. When the temperature is further increased to 450 °C, the particle features largely disappear, resulting in a continuous, dense amorphous structure with substantially reduced porosity. To quantitatively evaluate this densification process, binarization analysis was performed on cross-sectional SEM images obtained at 350 °C, 400 °C, and 450 °C (Fig. S5). The results show that the interfacial porosity decreases markedly with increasing temperature, from approximately 9.8% at 350 °C to 5.9% at 400 °C, and further to 0.147% at 450 °C, indicating the transition from a rigid crystalline structure with high interfacial porosity to a dense amorphous structure after complete melting of the ZIF-62 phase.

Overall, this temperature-driven structural evolution from a crystalline, loosely packed structure to a dense amorphous composite provides the structural basis for the subsequent ion transport behavior.

Structural and mechanical properties evolution of (LLTO)x(ZIF-62 glass)1-x

A series of composite membranes with varying LLTO contents was fabricated at 450 °C. As shown in Fig. 2a–b, the fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) spectra before and after heat treatment retain the characteristic features of ZIF-62, indicating preservation of the organic linkages. The band at ~1675 cm⁻¹, assigned to the C = O stretching vibration of residual DMF, disappears after thermal treatment, consistent with the removal of guest solvent molecules from the ZIF-62 framework, as reported previously24,30,31. XRD patterns of the (LLTO)x(ZIF-62 glass)1-x membranes (Fig. 2c) show that all LLTO-containing samples retain the crystalline LLTO phase, with peak intensity increasing proportionally to the LLTO content. In contrast, the diffraction peaks of crystalline ZIF-62 disappear after thermal treatment, while the pure ZIF-62 glass sample displays a broad amorphous pattern characteristic of MOF-derived glasses with short-range order and long-range disorder25,28. These results confirm that the composites consist of thermally stable LLTO ceramic domains embedded in an amorphous ZIF-62 glass matrix.

a, b FT-IR spectra of the composite membranes before and after heat treatment, respectively. c XRD patterns confirming the amorphous nature of the ZIF-62 glass matrix and the retained crystallinity of LLTO. d SEM images showing the composition-dependent microstructural evolution of the composite membranes. e Microscale mechanical properties measured by nanoindentation. f Macroscale mechanical properties measured by the three-point bending test. Error bars represent the standard deviation from repeated measurements.

The corresponding microstructural evolution was examined by SEM (Fig. 2d). At x = 0, the membrane exhibits a dense, smooth, glass-like morphology. At low LLTO contents, isolated ceramic particles are dispersed within the glass matrix without forming continuous pathways. As the LLTO content increases, the interparticle distance decreases, and particle-particle contacts become more frequent. At x = 0.3, the particles begin to coalesce into interconnected structures, marking a critical transition in structural connectivity. At x ≥ 0.4, a well-developed LLTO network becomes clearly visible within the glass matrix, indicating the formation of a ceramic-dominated continuous framework.

The mechanical properties of the composite membranes were evaluated at both the microscale by nanoindentation and the macroscale by three-point bending. Nanoindentation measurements (Fig. S6a) show that hardness and modulus vary non-monotonically with LLTO content (Fig. 2e). At low content (x = 0.1), both values are lower than those of pure ZIF-62 glass (hardness = 0.64 GPa, Young’s modulus = 7.6 GPa), likely because isolated rigid particles locally disrupt the continuous glass phase. As x increases from 0.1 to 0.3, the hardness and modulus increase markedly, with x = 0.3 showing the highest values among the composite samples (hardness = 0.44 GPa, modulus = 9.3 GPa). This improvement can be attributed to a filler-reinforcement effect, in which rigid ceramic particles act as load-bearing units and promote stress transfer within the matrix32,33. Consistently, the load-displacement curves show the highest content stiffness at x = 0.3 (Fig. S6b), further supporting the enhanced load-bearing capability of this composition. At higher LLTO contents (x ≥ 0.4), both hardness and modulus decrease, likely because excessive filler content compromises the continuity of the glass matrix and promotes weaker particle–particle contacts. This results in more heterogeneous stress distribution, increased penetration depth, and a more compliant indentation response. Overall, the non-monotonic trend reflects the balance between ceramic-particle reinforcement and glass-matrix continuity.

Three-point bending tests (Fig. S7a) exhibit a similar composition-dependent trend (Fig. 2f). Introducing a small amount of LLTO (x = 0.1) slightly reduces both the flexural modulus (Ef) and strength (σf) compared with the pure glass sample (Ef = 2.0 GPa, σf = 4.1 MPa). With further LLTO addition, both values increase and reach maxima at x = 0.3 (Ef = 3.1 GPa, σf = 8.6 MPa). The corresponding stress–strain curves indicate that this composition combines relatively high strength with modest deformability (Fig. S7b). The fracture strain reaches approximately 0.28%, more than four times that of pure LLTO ceramics ( ~ 0.066%)9, suggesting that the ZIF-62 glass matrix help mitigate the intrinsic brittleness of LLTO and improves damage tolerance.

At higher content (x = 0.6), the material exhibits a distinct fracture behavior. Unlike the abrupt brittle failure (sharp stress drop) observed in other samples, the stress–strain curve shows a pronounced nonlinear bending region before fracture. This behavior suggests increased structural heterogeneity and progressive deformation, likely because the limited glass phase is insufficient to fully encapsulate and bridge the excess ceramic particles. Consequently, weak particle–particle contacts promote interfacial debonding and macroscopic failure.

Although (LLTO)0.6 (ZIF-62 glass)0.4 retains some load-bearing capacity in bulk three-point bending measurements, its low fracture strain and unstable deformation behavior indicate reduced mechanical reliability. This issue is particularly important for thin-film configurations, where reduced ductility and increased structural heterogeneity make the membrane more susceptible to damage. Therefore, despite its residual bulk strength, the x = 0.6 composition is less suitable for practical membrane fabrication and handling.

Cross-scale ion transport mechanism and lithium extraction performance validation

Based on the structural evolution described above, ion-transport behavior in the (LLTO)x (ZIF-62 glass)1−x composite system was further investigated using density functional theory (DFT) calculations combined with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Hybrid structural models with LLTO contents of x = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.5 were constructed within an amorphous ZIF-62 glass framework (Fig. S8), enabling systematic analysis of composition-dependent structural connectivity, ion diffusion, and migration energy barriers. Li⁺ and Mg²⁺ transports were further compared at representative compositions of x = 0.3 and 0.5 to evaluate their diffusion behavior and migration characteristics within the LLTO-containing glass matrix (Fig. 3a).

Theoretical and electrochemical analysis of ion transport in (LLTO)x(ZIF-62 glass)1-x composite membranes.

Insets in panels (g–h) show magnified views to better visualize the conductivity differences among the membranes. i Nyquist plots obtained from electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) for composite membranes with different LLTO contents. j Arrhenius plots of ionic conductivity for compositions at and above the percolation threshold (x = 0.3), with corresponding apparent activation energies (Ea) indicated. k Lithium extraction performance (flux and selectivity) of the composite membranes in a binary Li+/Mg2+ solution. Error bars represent the standard deviation from repeated measurements.

MD simulations reveal that Li⁺ diffusion is highly sensitive to LLTO content (Fig. 3b). At low LLTO contents of x = 0.1 and 0.2, the Li⁺ diffusion coefficients are extremely low, at 4.73×10−11 and 1.37×10−10 cm² s⁻¹, respectively. This indicates that continuous ion-conduction pathways have not yet formed, and Li+ transport remains limited by the highly resistive ZIF-62 glass matrix. When the LLTO content increases to x = 0.3, the Li⁺ diffusion coefficient rises markedly to 2.36×10−9 cm² s⁻¹, nearly one order of magnitude higher than that at x = 0.2. This abrupt enhancement suggests the emergence of interconnected LLTO-rich domains that provide continuous Li⁺ transport pathways, corresponding to a percolation transition in the composite system34. Further increasing the LLTO content to x = 0.5 increases the Li⁺ diffusion coefficient to 4.51 × 10−9 cm² s⁻¹, indicating more efficient ion transport through a more developed LLTO network.

The calculated migration energy barriers further support this trend (Fig. 3c). At low LLTO contents, Li⁺ migration requires overcoming high energy barriers of approximately 2.2 eV at x = 0.1 and 1.7 eV at x = 0.2, consistent with the low diffusion coefficients obtained from MD simulations. These high barriers indicate that Li⁺ transport is predominantly constrained by the resistive glass matrix. At x = 0.3, the migration barrier of Li⁺ decreases sharply to approximately 0.45 eV, suggesting the formation of continuous low-energy transport pathways. At x = 0.5, the barrier further decreases to approximately 0.30 eV, indicating that the more interconnected LLTO network provides energetically favorable channels for Li⁺ migration. Together, the MD-derived diffusion coefficients and DFT-calculated migration barriers consistently identify x = 0.3 as a critical composition threshold, beyond which ion transport shifts from glass-matrix-limited diffusion to LLTO-network-dominated fast Li⁺ conduction.

The diffusion behavior and migration barriers of Li⁺ and Mg²⁺ were further compared within the composite membrane (Fig. 3d, e). At x = 0.3, the Li⁺ diffusion coefficient (2.36×10−9 cm² s⁻¹) is significantly higher than that of Mg²⁺ (4.53×10−10 cm² s⁻¹), indicating that Mg²⁺ is less able to utilize the LLTO-derived ion transport pathways. The calculated migration barriers show the trend: at x = 0.3, the Li⁺ exhibits a barrier of approximately 0.45 eV, whereas Mg²⁺ shows a much higher barrier of about 0.80 eV. These results suggest that although the LLTO network enhances ion transport, its facilitation effect is much stronger for Li⁺ than for Mg²⁺. When the LLTO content increases from x = 0.3 to x = 0.5, both ions exhibit improved transport, as evidenced by higher diffusion coefficients and reduced migration barriers. However, the enhancement is more pronounced for Li⁺: its diffusion coefficient increases from 2.36 × 10−9 to 4.51 × 10−9 cm² s⁻¹, while its migration barrier decreases from approximately 0.45 to 0.30 eV. In contrast, Mg²⁺ migration barrier decreases from approximately 0.80 to 0.55 eV but remains relatively high. This difference indicates that increasing LLTO content not only improves overall ion transport but also amplifies the kinetic advantage of Li⁺ over Mg²⁺.

Guided by these theoretical predictions, I–V measurements were performed in 0.1 M aqueous solutions of LiCl, MgCl₂ and NaCl (Fig. 3f-h). The experimental results agree well with the simulations: Li⁺ shows the highest current response, followed by Na⁺, whereas the current associated with Mg²⁺ is nearly negligible, demonstrating pronounced ion selectivity. The current also increases progressively with LLTO content, reflecting the gradual formation of continuous ion-conduction pathways. Notably, a step-like increase is observed at x = 0.3, consistent with the sharp reduction in Li+ migration barrier predicted by the theoretical calculations. This behavior indicates a transition from isolated conductive domains to a macroscopically connected transport network. In addition, the pure ZIF-62 glass membrane (x = 0) shows a current below the detection limit, indicating negligible measurable ionic conductivity of the glass phase under the present conditions and highlighting the dominant role of LLTO-derived low-energy pathways in enabling efficient ion transport.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was further employed to investigate the conductive behavior of the membranes. The Nyquist plots reveal a strong dependence of conductivity on LLTO content (Fig. 3i), consistent with percolation behavior (Fig. S9)34,35. At low LLTO contents (x = 0.1 and 0.2), the membranes exhibit high resistance, and the temperature-dependent conductivity deviates from Arrhenius behavior, instead following the Vogel–Tammann–Fulcher (VTF) relation with R² > 0.99 (Fig. S10). This suggests that the sluggish transport in this regime is likely associated with the dynamic relaxation of confined aqueous networks within locally hydrated interfaces36–40. The high resistance is also consistent with the large Li+ migration barrier (2.3 eV) obtained from DFT at x = 0.1. When the LLTO content reaches x = 0.3, the conductivity increases by nearly two orders of magnitude, while the apparent activation energy (Ea) decreases sharply from 0.600 eV at x = 0.2 to 0.231 eV at x = 0.3 and further to 0.125 eV at x = 0.5 (Fig. 3j). This abrupt change is in good agreement with the theoretical results and indicates that, from x = 0.3 onward, interconnected LLTO nanocrystals form a three-dimensional percolated fast-ion network that gradually becomes the dominant transport pathway.

Based on the established structure–transport relationship, lithium extraction performance was further evaluated in a binary Li⁺/Mg²⁺ system (Fig. 3k). The composite membrane achieves the optimal balance between Li+ flux and Li+/Mg2+ selectivity at x = 0.5. This performance originates from the combined effects of preferential Li⁺ conduction through the percolated LLTO network and suppression of nonselective transport by the dense ZIF-62 glass matrix. The LLTO network provides low-energy pathways for rapid Li⁺ migration, whereas Mg²⁺ remains hindered by a relatively high migration barrier. Meanwhile, the ZIF-62 glass promotes good interfacial contact with LLTO particles, enhances membrane compactness, and reduces defect-mediated leakage pathways. Together, these features enable efficient and selective lithium separation.

Lithium extraction performance

To evaluate lithium separation performance in realistic complex media, electrochemical extraction experiments were conducted using a series of representative natural low-grade lithium brines. These brines cover a broad range of ionic compositions, including extremely low lithium concentrations, high salinity, and multivalent-ion-rich environments (their detailed compositions are listed in Table S1). For comparison, ion selectivity results obtained from idealized binary systems are presented in Fig. S11a–c, which validate the intrinsic ion transport behavior of the membrane. Across these systems, both flux and selectivity exhibit consistent composition-dependent trends, increasing with LLTO content and showing a pronounced enhancement around x ≈ 0.3, while the transport of divalent ions is strongly suppressed. The effect of applied voltage was also examined in a binary Li⁺/Mg²⁺ system with a molar ratio of 1:100 (Fig. S11d). Li⁺ flux increases with increasing voltage, whereas Li⁺/Mg²⁺ selectivity reaches a maximum at an intermediate voltage of approximately 1.2 V, suggesting the presence of an optimal operating voltage window. At relatively low applied voltages, Li⁺ transport is favored because Li⁺ possesses a lower hydration energy and experiences a smaller dehydration penalty than Mg²⁺, enabling easier migration through the confined ion-transport pathways within the LLTO/MOF glass composite membrane41. In contrast, the strongly hydrated Mg²⁺ ions must overcome a substantially higher dehydration energy barrier during transmembrane transport42. Therefore, with increasing voltage, Li⁺/Mg²⁺ selectivity gradually improves. However, when the applied voltage exceeds approximately 1.2 V, the enhanced electric-field-driven migration force likely facilitates partial dehydration of Mg²⁺ ions, making Mg²⁺ transport through the confined ion-transport pathways increasingly feasible43. Consequently, Mg²⁺ permeation increases more rapidly at higher voltages, resulting in a decline in Li⁺/Mg²⁺ selectivity despite the continued increase in overall ion flux.

Red Sea water, with its extremely high Mg/Li ratio ( ~ 7400) and very low lithium concentration ( ~ 0.21 ppm), represents a particularly challenging separation environment. Under these conditions, the membrane still achieves a high Li⁺/Mg²⁺ selectivity of 51,229, although the Li⁺ flux remains relatively low at 0.69 mmol m⁻² h⁻¹ because of the low initial lithium concentration. In Dead Sea brine, which contains 47.4 ppm Li⁺ along with high concentrations of Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺, the composite membrane maintains Li+/Mg2+ selectivity of 17,671 and delivers a Li⁺ flux of 5.03 mmol m⁻² h⁻¹, compared with only 0.23 and 0.35 mmol m⁻² h⁻¹ for Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺, respectively. In Great Salt Lake brine, where the Na⁺ concentration is high (50,995 ppm), the membrane maintains a stable Li⁺ flux of 4.19 mmol m⁻² h⁻¹ together with good Li+/Na+ selectivity of 213, demonstrating effective lithium separation even in a high monovalent ion background. In Upper Rhine Graben brine, which has a higher initial Li⁺ concentration of 281.9 ppm, the Li⁺ flux increases substantially to 16.18 mmol m⁻² h⁻¹ with a favorable selectivity of 506, indicating the adaptability of the membrane to brines with different ionic strengths and compositions.

Across all tested systems, Li⁺ flux and Li+ to metal-ion selectivities show a consistent dependence on LLTO content: both increase gradually at low LLTO content, exhibit a marked enhancement around x = 0.3, and reach their highest values at x = 0.5 (Fig. 4a–d). This trend agrees well with the transport behavior observed in the theoretical calculations and electrochemical measurements, further confirming the key role of composition-dependent pathway formation. To exclude the influence of membrane thickness variations, thickness-normalized fluxes were evaluated (Figs. S12 and S13). The normalized results follow the same composition-dependent trend, confirming that the observed transport enhancement arises from the formation of percolated LLTO pathways rather than geometric effects. In addition, integration of the current–time responses together with ion-charge accounting based on the experimentally measured cation fluxes yielded high Faradaic efficiencies for all four representative brines, confirming that the measured ion transport is electrochemically consistent with the transferred charge (Fig. S14). These results indicate that increasing LLTO content progressively promotes effective ion transport channels, thereby enhancing lithium flux and separation performance.

Overall, the composite membrane exhibits consistent performance across different brine systems, indicating that its separation behavior is governed by composition-dependent ion transport characteristics and remains robust under complex conditions. Compared with previously reported lithium-selective membranes, this composite system demonstrates competitive separation performance under similar operating conditions (Table S2).

The long-term durability of the membrane was evaluated in Red Sea water (Fig. 4e) by continuously operating a single membrane for 30 days, with lithium extraction performance periodically monitored every two days. Initially, the membrane delivered a Li⁺/Mg2⁺ selectivity of ~59,000, which remained stable with only a minor decrease over the month-long testing period, demonstrating its resilience in high-salinity environments. Post-test characterizations—including XRD, FT-IR, NMR, and XPS (Figs. S15–S18)—confirmed that the membrane’s structural and chemical integrity were fully preserved. Furthermore, ICP analysis revealed a negligible Zn leaching ratio of only 0.023% ± 0.003% (relative to the theoretical total Zn content in the tested membrane), highlighting the exceptional dissolution resistance and structural stability of the ZIF-62 glass matrix during prolonged operation.

In summary, the LLTO–ZIF-62 glass composite membrane demonstrates strong adaptability, high selectivity, and long-term stability across a range of realistic brine systems, highlighting its potential for efficient lithium separation in complex aqueous environments.

Discussion

We constructed a structurally integrated composite membrane by embedding LLTO nanoparticles into a ZIF-62 glass matrix via a melt-casting strategy at a relatively low temperature of approximately 450 °C. Compared with conventional ceramic membranes that typically require sintering above 1000 °C, this approach substantially lowers the processing temperature and energy consumption while enabling the formation of dense, well-integrated membrane structures. The resulting (LLTO)x (ZIF-62 glass)1–x composite membranes exhibit a dual-continuous architecture, in which the ZIF-62 glass phase promotes intimate interfacial contact with LLTO during vitrification and densification. This glass-assisted integration also improves the mechanical response of the composite. Compared with conventional sintered LLTO membranes, the composite exhibits enhanced mechanical tolerance, which is important for maintaining structural integrity in thin-film configurations and ensuring reliability under practical operating conditions, while simultaneously enabling low-temperature processability (Table S3).

Through systematic compositional optimization, we identified x = 0.5 as the optimal ceramic content, providing a favorable balance between ion-transport performance and mechanical stability. A distinct transition in transport behavior occurs around x = 0.3, corresponding to the evolution of ion-conduction pathways from isolated LLTO domains to an interconnected network. This percolation-like transition governs both ion transport performance and mechanical response, highlighting a coupled structure–property relationship. At the optimized composition, the membrane achieves a Li + /Mg2+ selectivity up to 59,000 in Red Sea water. It also demonstrates high lithium extraction performance across diverse natural brines with markedly different ionic compositions, confirming its adaptability under realistic conditions. During long-term operation, the composite membrane maintains stable separation performance, further demonstrating its operational robustness.

Overall, this study presents a composite membrane design strategy that integrates low-temperature processability, mechanical stability, and selective ion transport. By combining crystalline ion conductors with an amorphous ZIF-62 glass matrix, this approach unifies processability, structural integrity, and ion-transport functionality within a single material platform, offering a potentially scalable route for lithium extraction from dilute and compositionally complex aqueous resources. Nevertheless, practical scale-up still faces several challenges. Further improvement in macroscopic Li⁺ flux will require more precise control over the interconnected ceramic network and phase interfaces. In addition, the system exhibits an inherent trade-off: excessive ceramic content (x ≥ 0.6) increases thin-film brittleness, thereby limiting the optimization window for transport enhancement. Resolving these coupled transport-mechanical trade-offs remains a key objective for future development and large-scale manufacturing.

Methods

Materials

The LLTO powder used in this study was commercially sourced from Ohara Inc. (Japan). Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (99.99%), N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.5%), imidazole (99.5%), benzimidazole (99.5%), and methanol (99.5%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Synthesis of ZIF-62

ZIF-62 crystals were synthesized following a previously reported procedure44. Briefly, imidazole (1.35 M), Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (0.1 M), and benzimidazole (0.15 M) were dissolved in DMF under continuous magnetic stirring for 2 h to form a homogeneous solution. The resulting mixture was transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated in an oven at 130 °C for 96 h. The product was collected by centrifugation, thoroughly washed several times with DMF and methanol, and then dried in an oven at 100 °C for over 24 h before use.

Membrane preparation

The membrane fabrication process is schematically illustrated in Fig. S19. LLTO powder and ZIF-62 were weighed according to the desired composition and dispersed in methanol, followed by vigorous magnetic stirring to ensure homogeneous mixing. The resulting suspension was filtered and dried in an oven at 100 °C for over 24 h. The dried composite powder was then placed into a tungsten carbide mold with a diameter of 2 cm and pressed at 300 MPa for 10 min without adding any binder. The resulting green body was transferred to a tube furnace and heat-treated under an argon atmosphere at a heating rate of 3 °C/min to the targeted temperature, where it was held for 30 min. This process yielded dense membranes with an average thickness of approximately 100 µm. A series of composite membranes with different LLTO contents was prepared using this method and designated as (LLTO)x(ZIF-62 glass)1-x, where x represents the mass fraction of LLTO (e.g. x = 0.1 corresponds to 10 wt% LLTO).

Characterization

The thermochemical properties of ZIF-62 and LLTO/ZIF-62 mixtures with different compositions were analyzed by thermogravimetric (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, SDT 650, Discovery). These measurements were used to determine the appropriate thermal treatment temperature for membrane fabrication. All thermal analyses were conducted at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. Solid-state 13C cross-polarization magic angle spinning (13C CP/MAS) NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker 600 M WB NMR spectrometer. Carbon dioxide adsorption-desorption isotherms were measured on Micromeritics ASAP 2020 at 273 K, and the surface areas were calculated by the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out on a Kratos AXIS Ultra DLD spectrometer. X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8-Twin, Bruker) patterns of the precursor mixtures and resulting membranes were collected using Cu Kα radiation over a 2θ range of 10–80°. In-situ XRD (D8-Advance, Bruker) measurements were performed under the same conditions as those used for membrane preparation to monitor the phase transition behavior of pure ZIF-62 and the composite during heating. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, iS10, Nicolet) was employed to examine changes in the functional groups of the precursors and glass membranes before and after thermal treatment. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Quattro, Thermo Fisher) equipped with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was used to investigate the membrane microstructure. EDS mapping was further employed to visualize the spatial distribution of LLTO within the composite matrix. ICP-OES elemental analyses were conducted on an Agilent 5900 system to measure the metal ion concentrations; the detection limits of the analyzed elements were below 10 ppb.

Theoretical simulation

The DFT and MD calculations were carried out using the VASP code48. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)49 was used to treat electron exchange and correlation. The projector augmented-wave (PAW) pseudopotential method50 was applied with a kinetic energy cut-off of 500 eV to describe the expansion of electronic eigenfunctions. The Brillouin-zone integration was sampled by a Γ-centered 1 × 1 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh. All atomic positions were fully relaxed until the energy and force convergence criteria reached 1 × 10⁻⁶ eV and 0.01 eV/Å, respectively. The semi-empirical DFT-D3 method51 was employed to qualitatively describe long-range van der Waals interactions. Furthermore, the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method was utilized to compute the minimum energy pathways of ion diffusion and their corresponding activation barriers.

The atomic models were initially constructed using Materials Studio software. The amorphous ZIF-62 model was derived from its crystalline counterpart by building a 2 × 2 × 2 supercell. To eliminate the intrinsic long-range order of the crystalline framework, an ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) melt–quench procedure was employed. Specifically, the supercell was heated to a sufficiently high temperature to induce structural disorder, then gradually cooled to room temperature to obtain a stable amorphous configuration. Full structural relaxation was subsequently performed to eliminate residual stresses and unreasonable atomic overlaps. The resulting amorphous ZIF-62 model was described within an orthogonal simulation cell with lattice parameters of a = 31.0548 Å, b = 31.5486 Å, and c = 36.4112 Å (α = β = γ = 90.000°), under periodic boundary conditions.

Subsequently, LLTO species were introduced into the accessible free volume of the amorphous ZIF-62 matrix to construct composite models with varying doping concentrations (x = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.5), while carefully avoiding artificial atomic overlaps. Each doped system was subjected to energy minimization, followed by molecular dynamics equilibration to ensure sufficient structural relaxation and interfacial reconstruction.

To achieve reliable long-timescale sampling of ion diffusion, the molecular dynamics simulations were accelerated using the machine-learning force field (MLFF) implemented in VASP. The simulations were performed in the NVT ensemble with a time step of 1 fs and a total duration of 100 ns. Finally, the diffusion coefficients were extracted by fitting the mean square displacement (MSD) curves, which were post-processed using the VASPKIT package.
