Abstract
Approximate Message Passing (AMP) is a general framework for iterative algorithms, originally developed for compressed sensing and later extended to a wide range of high-dimensional inference problems. Although recent work has advanced matrix AMP, complex AMP, and AMP for non-separable functions independently, a unified state evolution theory for complex AMP with non-separable denoisers has been lacking. This article fills that gap by establishing state evolution in the setting of complex, non-separable denoising functions. The proposed approach constructs an augmented real-valued system that lifts the problem to a higher-dimensional space, then recovers the complex domain through a many-to-one canonical transformation. Under this construction, the Onsager correction naturally involves Wirtinger derivatives, and the resulting state evolution reduces to scalar complex recursions despite the non-separable structure of the denoisers. The framework extends to the matrix-valued setting, accommodating multiple feature vectors simultaneously. This generalization enables AMP to exploit joint structural constraints, such as simultaneous group and element sparsity, in complex-valued recovery problems. The complex sparse group least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) serves as a key instantiation, motivated by preamble detection in Orthogonal Time-Frequency Space (OTFS)-based unsourced random access. Numerical experiments confirm that state evolution accurately predicts performance and show that complex non-separable denoising can produce significant gains over separable and real-valued alternatives.



I
Introduction

Approximate Message Passing (AMP) has emerged as a powerful iterative framework for solving high-dimensional inference problems with random measurement matrices
[
10
,
4
,
3
]
.
The framework offers computationally efficient algorithms and a precise asymptotic characterization of performance through
state evolution
(SE).
AMP has been successfully applied in compressed sensing, statistical signal processing
[
20
,
21
,
18
,
22
]
, high-dimensional inference
[
26
,
13
]
, and
communication
[
2
,
23
,
1
]
.
The SE formalism enables sharp predictions of algorithmic performance, which can in turn be used for parameter selection.
While the original proofs of SE relied on Gaussian measurement matrices and separable denoising functions, subsequent work has progressively extended the theory to more general settings.
These include non-separable functions
[
5
]
, matrix-valued variables
[
9
,
11
]
, and structured sensing operators
[
12
]
, substantially enlarging the scope of AMP algorithms.

Complex-valued signal processing plays a central role in communications and related fields.
Many physical systems, particularly in wireless communications, are naturally modeled in the complex domain.
For example, orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) and orthogonal time-frequency space (OTFS) modulation rely on representing information-bearing signals as complex waveforms.
Similar complex-valued models appear in radar, sonar, and array processing, where phase coherence across sensors carries critical information.

Because of this intrinsic structure, algorithms designed for real-valued signals often fail to capture the statistical dependencies, rotational invariances, or phase relationships that are central to complex-valued data.
This has motivated the development of complex-signal processing frameworks that treat real and imaginary components in a unified manner rather than as two independent channels
[
17
,
8
,
16
,
6
,
25
]
.
These developments lie at the intersection of high-dimensional inference and complex-valued communications theory, highlighting the potential benefits of complex AMP and SE applied to practical systems.

This article extends the complex AMP framework to non-separable denoising functions.
A key motivation is the
sparse group LASSO
(SGL) problem
[
24
,
27
]
, which combines group-level sparsity (via a group penalty) and element-level sparsity (via an
\ell_{1}
penalty within each group).
The resulting proximal operator is inherently non-separable, as the group threshold depends on the joint norm of multiple components.
This structure arises naturally in preamble detection under OTFS modulation
[
19
]
, where active users form sparse groups with sparse channel taps.

A naive approach would reduce the complex system to a real one via the isomorphism
\mathbb{C}^{n}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.
However, both natural embeddings, stacking rows or concatenating columns, either destroy the independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) structure of the sensing matrix required for AMP, or decouple the real and imaginary components, precluding joint complex processing.
The proposed approach instead constructs an augmented real matrix-valued system
[
14
,
12
]
and recovers the complex formulation through a many-to-one canonical transformation.

This line of development builds on several key contributions.
Maleki et al.
[
17
]
introduced AMP for scalar (separable) complex denoisers; Çakmak, Gkiouzepi, Opper, and Caire
[
6
]
recently extended complex AMP algorithms to row-wise
separable
matrix denoisers by combining the
Householder Dice
representation
[
15
]
with a complex variant of Stein’s lemma.
The present work builds on the graph-based AMP framework of Gerbelot and Berthier
[
12
]
, extending it to the complex domain with fully
non-separable
denoisers.
The canonical transformation extends naturally to the matrix setting, where
p
feature vectors are estimated jointly (Section
III-C
).

II
Motivation and Formulation

Consider the canonical linear inverse problem

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{0}+\mathbf{w}

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{F}^{N}
is the signal vector,
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times N}
is the measurement matrix, and
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
is additive Gaussian noise with i.i.d. entries.
The underlying field
\mathbb{F}
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
.
Let
\delta=n/N
denote the undersampling ratio.
We are especially interested in the setting where
n
,
N
are large and the entries of
\mathbf{A}
are i.i.d. Gaussian.
The task is to estimate
\mathbf{x}_{0}
from (noisy) observation vector
\mathbf{y}
.
As part of this inference task, we know that
\mathbf{x}_{0}
is a structured vector and we wish to derive an iterative algorithm that leverages this information.

The quintessential example of such a structure is the well-studied sparse recovery problem, which can be solved using a proximal formulation and the least-absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)
[
10
]
.
A natural extension replaces element-wise sparsity with group sparsity.
One can approach this problem using the group LASSO (GL) formulation,

\textstyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{N}}\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\lambda_{g}\lVert\mathbf{x}_{g}\rVert_{2}

(2)

where
\mathcal{G}
is a partition of the set of indices
[
27
]
.
In this setting, only indices within a few groups are non-zero.

Conceptually, the sparse group LASSO (SGL) problem is similar to the GL in that it combines a group structure wherein entries within only a few groups are non-zero and there is additional sparsity in every active group
[
24
]
.
This double sparsity is captured through the Lagrangian formulation given by

\displaystyle\textstyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{N}}\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\lambda_{g}\lVert\mathbf{x}_{g}\rVert_{2}+\lambda_{\rm e}\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{1}.

(3)

The Lagrangian parameters
\{\lambda_{g}\}
, which penalize the group norms to encourage group-level sparsity, can be group-specific or uniform across groups, depending on the underlying structure of the problem.
Crucially, the resulting proximal operator is non-separable.
This type of denoiser precludes the application of early AMP results, but non-separable functions are addressed in more recent contributions for real-valued problems
[
5
,
7
,
12
]
.

Complex versions of the SGL problem arise in communication settings such as preamble detection in OTFS, where the set of active preambles is sparse and the channel of every device admits a sparse representation in the delay-Doppler domain
[
19
]
.
Such practical linear inverse problems over the complex field motivate the study of the complex AMP framework for non-separable, complex functions.

Remark 1
.

Often a problem over
\mathbb{C}^{n}
can be reformulated equivalently over
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
by representing complex numbers as two-dimensional real vectors.
However, naively trying to use the standard isomorphism between
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
fails for the complex AMP formulation of (
1
).
The difficulty stems, partly, from the typical AMP assumptions.
Stacking the real and imaginary parts of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
into a single vector
[\Re\mathbf{x}_{0};\Im\mathbf{x}_{0}]\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
produces an augmented measurement matrix whose rows are pairwise dependent, violating the i.i.d. requirement.
Alternatively, arranging the components as
[\Re\mathbf{x}_{0},\Im\mathbf{x}_{0}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2}
and retaining a single real sensing matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N}
preserves the i.i.d. property of the entries but decouples the two columns: the observation
\mathbf{W}[\Re\mathbf{x}_{0},\Im\mathbf{x}_{0}]
cannot reproduce the cross-coupling between real and imaginary parts inherent in complex multiplication
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{0}
.
Our approach adopts the horizontal arrangement but pairs it with a richer sensing matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times N}
, yielding an augmented observation space of
4n
real measurements rather than
2n
.
The additional measurements restore the coupling lost by the naive formulation, but carry more information than the original complex system.
A state collapse then constrains the augmented system to match the information content of the base formulation over
\mathbb{C}
(see (
8
) and the discussion that follows).

The nuances outlined in Remark
1
highlight why treatments of complex AMP cannot be obtained as straightforward extensions of results for the real case.
Maleki et al.
[
17
]
derive the Onsager correction for the complex AMP iterate via a Taylor-series expansion.
More recently, the extension of AMP to complex-valued settings with row-wise separable denoising functions was established through an argument paralleling the
Householder Dice
characterization, but relying on a complex version of Stein’s lemma
[
6
]
.

Our strategy for studying AMP over
\mathbb{C}
differs from previous approaches.
Rather than deriving an alternate proof from first principles, we construct an augmented system with key structural properties.
Specifically, we examine a higher-dimensional formulation over
\mathbb{R}
, which is inherently richer than the base problem over
\mathbb{C}
and thus not equivalent to it.
Existing AMP results
[
4
,
14
,
5
,
12
]
readily apply to the augmented system.
By collapsing this augmented system onto the complex domain through a linear transformation and restricting the class of admissible denoisers, we show that the statistical properties of the base formulation over
\mathbb{C}
can be inferred from the richer system.
Since
state evolution
(SE) depends on these statistical properties, this strategy (under suitable assumptions) yields an asymptotic characterization of complex AMP with non-separable denoisers.
The details of this approach will be developed in the following sections.

Base System over
\mathbb{C}
:

Mathematically, we wish to establish state evolution for the composite AMP algorithm over
\mathbb{C}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{t}+\frac{\mathbf{z}^{t-1}}{\delta}\big\langle(\eta^{t-1})^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}^{t-1})\big\rangle

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}^{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{z}^{t}

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\eta^{t}(\mathbf{r}^{t})

(6)

where
\eta^{t}:\mathbb{C}^{N}\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
is a sequence of (potentially) non-separable complex-valued vector maps.
Therein,
\mathbf{z}^{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is the
residual error
enhanced by an Onsager term;

\mathbf{r}^{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
is the
effective observation
; and
\mathbf{x}^{t+1}
is the
state estimate
after denoising.
Matrix
\mathbf{A}
has
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}(0,1/n)
i.i.d. entries.

Augmented System over
\mathbb{R}
:

Consider the matrix inverse problem over
\mathbb{R}
defined as

\mathbf{O}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{S}_{0}+\mathbf{N}

(7)

where
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times N}
is the sensing matrix,
\mathbf{S}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2}
is the state matrix, and
\mathbf{N}
denotes additive noise.
The observation matrix is then
\mathbf{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2}
.
Note that
\mathbf{O}
has twice as many real dimensions as
\mathbf{y}
in (
1
).
Prior work on AMP for matrix inverse problems over
\mathbb{R}

[
14
,
11
,
12
]
provides the conceptual foundation for our analysis.

In constructing the augmented system, the relation between (
1
) and (
7
) is given by
\mathbf{S}_{0}=[\Re\mathbf{x}_{0},\Im\mathbf{x}_{0}]
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{W}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{W}_{n}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{\iota}=\begin{bmatrix}\Re\mathbf{A}_{\iota,:}\\
\Im\mathbf{A}_{\iota,:}\end{bmatrix}.

(8)

The entries of
\mathbf{W}
are i.i.d. Gaussian random variables, and the state matrix
\mathbf{S}_{0}
is in one-to-one correspondence with
\mathbf{x}_{0}
in (
1
).
The key difference lies in the observation space: above, we have
\mathbf{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2}
, which amounts to
4n
real measurements, compared to the
2n
real measurements implicit in
\mathbf{y}
.
This distinction explains why we describe the augmented system as information rich and, at the same time, motivates the need to collapse it into a lower-dimensional formulation.

State Collapse:

Let linear transformation
\mathbf{R}:\mathbb{C}^{2n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
be defined through the Kronecker product
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}_{n}\otimes\rho^{\mathsf{T}}
where
\rho^{\mathsf{T}}=[1,\mathpzc{i}]
.
Then, we can immediately write

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{0}\rho

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}_{0}

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}\mathbf{N}\rho

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{w}

(9)

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distribution.
That is, conditioned on
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, we have

\mathbf{R}\mathbf{O}\rho=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W})(\mathbf{S}_{0}\rho)+\mathbf{R}\mathbf{N}\rho\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{0}+\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{y}.

The connection between the two systems is manifest.
It remains to show that the statistical properties of the complex AMP iterative algorithm (
4
)–(
6
) can likewise be derived from the properties of the matrix AMP iterative algorithm.
Establishing this result, under suitable restrictions, is a key contribution of this article.
The precise formulation of these restrictions, and the resulting state evolution theorem, are the subject of the next section.

III
State Evolution for Complex AMP

Having established the relationship between the base and augmented systems, we now formalize the reduction.
Following standard practice in the AMP literature
[
5
]
, we work with the centralized formulation and establish state evolution for the following complex AMP iteration:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathpzc{g}^{k}\left(\mathbf{v}^{k}\right)-c^{k}\mathpzc{f}^{k}\left(\mathbf{u}^{k}\right)

(10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{k}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\mathpzc{f}^{k}\left(\mathbf{u}^{k}\right)-b^{k}\mathpzc{g}^{k-1}\left(\mathbf{v}^{k-1}\right)

(11)

where
\mathpzc{f}^{k}:\mathbb{C}^{N}\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
and
\mathpzc{g}^{k}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
are sequences of complex vector functions,
c^{k}=\langle(\mathpzc{g}^{k})^{\prime}(\mathbf{v}^{k})\rangle
, and
b^{k}=({1}/{\delta})\langle(\mathpzc{f}^{k})^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}^{k})\rangle
.
In the real-valued setting, the following matrix equations have been studied
[
12
]
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Q}^{k}-\mathbf{M}^{k}(\mathbf{C}^{k})^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=f^{k+1}(\mathbf{H}^{k+1})

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{k}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{M}^{k}-\mathbf{Q}^{k-1}(\mathbf{B}^{k})^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{k}

\displaystyle=g^{k}(\mathbf{E}^{k})

(13)

where
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times M}
is a real-valued matrix,
f^{k+1}
and
g^{k}
are real-valued matrix functions of suitable dimensions, and

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\partial(g^{k}_{j,:}(\mathbf{E}^{k}))^{\mathsf{T}}}{\partial\mathbf{E}_{j,:}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{\partial(f^{k}_{j,:}(\mathbf{H}^{k}))^{\mathsf{T}}}{\partial\mathbf{H}_{j,:}}.

We next establish the reduction of (
12
)–(
13
) to (
10
)–(
11
) via the transformation given below.

III-A

Canonical Transformation

Definition 2

(Canonical Transformation)
.

Recall the relations in (
8
) between
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{A}
and between
\mathbf{S}_{0}
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.
Set and partition the iterates of the real matrix AMP system (
12
)–(
13
) into blocks
\mathbf{H}^{k}\in(\mathbb{R}^{1\times 2})^{N}
and
\mathbf{E}^{k}\in(\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})^{n}
, akin to (
8
), so that

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}^{k}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{H}^{k}_{N}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{k}_{\iota}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{E}^{k}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{E}^{k}_{n}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{k}_{\iota}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{E}^{k}_{\iota,1,1}&\mathbf{E}^{k}_{\iota,1,2}\\
\mathbf{E}^{k}_{\iota,2,1}&\mathbf{E}^{k}_{\iota,2,2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}.

Define the complex collapse of each block by

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{k}_{\iota}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}^{k}_{\iota}\,\rho

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{k}_{\iota}

\displaystyle=\rho^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{E}^{k}_{\iota}\,\rho.

Given the complex functions
\mathpzc{f}^{k}
and
\mathpzc{g}^{k}
from (
10
)–(
11
), define the real matrix functions
f^{k}
and
g^{k}
in (
12
)–(
13
) so that
\mathbf{M}^{k}=f^{k}(\mathbf{H}^{k})
with
\mathbf{M}^{k}_{\iota}=\big[\Re\mathpzc{f}^{k}_{\iota}(\mathbf{u}^{k}),\Im\mathpzc{f}^{k}_{\iota}(\mathbf{u}^{k})\big]
, and that
\mathbf{Q}^{k}=g^{k}(\mathbf{E}^{k})
has the block-wise form

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}^{k}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{Q}^{k}_{n}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{k}_{\iota}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Re\mathpzc{g}^{k}_{\iota}(\mathbf{v}^{k})&\Im\mathpzc{g}^{k}_{\iota}(\mathbf{v}^{k})\\
\Im\mathpzc{g}^{k}_{\iota}(\mathbf{v}^{k})&-\Re\mathpzc{g}^{k}_{\iota}(\mathbf{v}^{k})\end{bmatrix}.

Remark 3
.

The
2\times 2
block form of
\mathbf{Q}^{k}_{\iota}
encodes the action of complex multiplication by
\overline{\rho}
: a matrix of the form
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
b&-a\end{bmatrix}
satisfies

\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
b&-a\end{bmatrix}\rho=(a+\mathpzc{i}b)\overline{\rho}
,
which is precisely Lemma
6
(ii)
below.
This structure is a key design choice of the canonical transformation.

Remark 4

(Admissible Functions)
.

Definition
2
restricts the class of real-valued matrix functions that are compatible with the collapse.
A generic
g^{k}
would produce blocks
\mathbf{Q}^{k}_{\iota}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
with four degrees of freedom; the imposed structure
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
b&-a\end{bmatrix}
has only two degrees of freedom, forcing
g^{k}
to depend on
\mathbf{E}^{k}_{\iota}
solely through the collapsed variable
\mathbf{v}^{k}_{\iota}=\rho^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{E}^{k}_{\iota}\rho
.
Similarly, for each component
\iota\in[N]
,
f^{k}_{\iota}
is constrained to be the real–imaginary encoding of
\mathpzc{f}^{k}_{\iota}
acting on
\mathbf{H}^{k}\rho
.
In short, complex-compatible nonlinearities, those whose real matrix representation is fully determined by their complex counterpart, admit the reduction.

Remark 5

(Collapsed System)
.

The transformation is many-to-one: a block
\mathbf{E}_{\iota}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is mapped to a scalar
\mathbf{v}_{\iota}\in\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
, so information is lost.
Because
f^{k}
and
g^{k}
are restricted to the admissible class above, the orbit of the resulting system can be tracked entirely in the lower-dimensional complex domain.
Importantly, all components required for evaluating (
10
)–(
11
) are preserved under this mapping.

Properties of the canonical transformation and its effect on the AMP dynamics are established below.

Lemma 6

(Properties of Canonical Transformation)
.

For the mapping of Definition
2
, the following equalities hold:

(i)

\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{H}^{k}\rho=\mathbf{u}^{k}
,
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{E}^{k}\rho=\mathbf{v}^{k}
,
\mathbf{M}^{k}\rho=\mathpzc{f}^{k}(\mathbf{u}^{k})
.

(ii)

\mathbf{Q}_{\iota}^{k}\rho=\mathpzc{g}_{\iota}^{k}(\mathbf{v}^{k})\overline{\rho}\;
for all
\iota\in[n]
.

(iii)

\mathbf{Q}^{k}\rho=\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathpzc{g}^{k}(\mathbf{v}^{k})
,
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Q}^{k}=\mathpzc{g}^{k}(\mathbf{v}^{k})\rho^{\mathsf{H}}
.

(iv)

\frac{1}{2}\rho^{\mathsf{H}}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\mathbf{H}_{\iota,:}^{k})^{\mathsf{T}}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{\iota}}\;
for all
\iota\in[N]
.

(v)

\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\mathbf{E}^{k}_{\iota,1,:})^{\mathsf{T}}}&\frac{\partial}{\partial(\mathbf{E}^{k}_{\iota,2,:})^{\mathsf{T}}}\end{bmatrix}\overline{\rho}=\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{\iota}^{k}}\;
for all
\iota\in[n]
.

Proof:

Throughout, recall the following notations and properties,

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathpzc{i}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-\mathpzc{i}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathsf{H}}\rho

\displaystyle=2

and
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}_{n}\otimes\rho^{\mathsf{T}}

By Definition

\begin{split}\mathbf{Q}^{k}_{\iota}\rho&=\begin{bmatrix}a_{\iota}&b_{\iota}\\

\mathpzc{i}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{\iota}+\mathpzc{i}b_{\iota}\\
b_{\iota}-\mathpzc{i}a_{\iota}\end{bmatrix}\\
&=(a_{\iota}+\mathpzc{i}b_{\iota})\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-\mathpzc{i}\end{bmatrix}=\mathpzc{g}^{k}_{\iota}(\mathbf{v}^{k})\,\overline{\rho}.\end{split}

Consider

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\partial/\partial(\Re\mathbf{v}_{\iota})-\mathpzc{i}\,\partial/\partial(\Im\mathbf{v}_{\iota})\\
\partial/\partial(\Im\mathbf{v}_{\iota})+\mathpzc{i}\,\partial/\partial(\Re\mathbf{v}_{\iota})\end{bmatrix}\\

\mathpzc{i}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{v}^{k}_{\iota}}\,\rho,\end{split}

where the last step again uses the Wirtinger derivative

and the factorization of the column vector as a scalar times

III-B

The proof strategy proceeds in three stages.
First, we state regularity conditions on the complex functions
\mathpzc{f}^{k}
and
\mathpzc{g}^{k}
(Assumption

These conditions are deliberately chosen so that, when combined with the canonical transformation of Definition

IV
Complex Sparse Group LASSO

We apply the developed framework to the complex sparse group LASSO, a formulation whose real-valued counterpart is well established in the statistics and signal processing literature (cf. Section
II
).
In the complex domain, this problem arises naturally in wireless systems.
A primary example is OTFS preamble detection, where the set of active users is sparse and each user’s channel admits a sparse representation in the delay-Doppler domain.
Importantly, the SGL proximal operator is inherently non-separable: the group-level shrinkage depends on the joint norm of all entries that survive element-wise thresholding within each group.
The non-separable state evolution established in Section
III
is therefore not merely applicable but required for a rigorous asymptotic characterization.

We now describe the signal model and recovery formulation precisely.
The goal is to recover a structured signal
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
from noisy linear measurements
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{w}
, where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times N}
has i.i.d. entries
\mathbf{A}_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}(0,1/n)
and
\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{w}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
.
Recall that
\delta=n/N
.
We assume that the signal
\mathbf{x}
exhibits
structured
group sparsity.
Specifically, there are
G
groups, each of size
S=N/G
.
Let
k
denote the total number of active elements, so that the sparsity ratio is
\varrho=k/n
, and let
r_{\mathrm{ga}}
denote the fraction of active groups.
The fraction of active elements is
r_{\mathrm{a}}=k/N=\varrho\delta
, and the fraction of active elements per active group is
r_{\mathrm{ea}}=r_{\mathrm{a}}/r_{\mathrm{ga}}
.
The constraint
r_{\mathrm{ea}}\leq 1
requires
r_{\mathrm{ga}}\geq\varrho\delta
.
In our experiments, we pick
r_{\mathrm{ga}}
fraction of
G
groups at random to be active, and within an active group, we pick
r_{\mathrm{ea}}
fraction of the elements at random to be active.
Active elements are drawn uniformly on the complex unit circle, i.e.,
e^{\mathpzc{i}\theta}
with
\theta\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,2\pi)
. Additionally, we assume that the group structure is perfectly known, i.e., we know the partition of the set of indices into groups.

Owing to this double sparsity structure, a natural formulation for the recovery problem is the complex sparse group LASSO,

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}}~\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{g}}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\lVert\mathbf{x}_{g,:}\rVert_{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{1}.

(22)

Since this optimization problem is convex but non-smooth, it can be solved via proximal gradient descent, e.g., iterative soft thresholding algorithm (ISTA).
The associated proximal operator is

\begin{split}\eta(\mathbf{r},\lambda_{\mathrm{g}},\lambda_{\mathrm{e}})&=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\Big\{\lVert\mathbf{r}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\\
&\hskip 10.0pt+\lambda_{\mathrm{g}}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{g,:}\rVert_{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\rVert_{1}\Big\}.\end{split}

(23)

The
j
th component of this operator admits the closed form given by

\displaystyle\eta_{j}(\mathbf{r},\lambda_{\mathrm{g}},\lambda_{\mathrm{e}})=\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{g}}}{\lVert\eta^{\mathrm{st}}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{G}_{j}},\lambda_{\mathrm{e}})\rVert_{2}}\right)_{+}\eta^{\mathrm{st}}(r_{j},\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}),

(24)

where
(\cdot)_{+}\triangleq\max\{0,\cdot\}
and
\mathcal{G}_{j}\subset[N]
denotes the index set of the group containing element
j
.
For any positive integer
m
, the function
\eta^{\mathrm{st}}:\mathbb{C}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{C}^{m}
acts component-wise as complex soft-thresholding, with
j
th entry

\displaystyle\eta^{\mathrm{st}}_{j}(\mathbf{r},\lambda_{\mathrm{e}})=\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}}{\left\lvert r_{j}\right\rvert}\right)_{+}r_{j}.

(25)

Substituting the definition of
\eta^{\mathrm{st}}
and simplifying yields

\eta_{j}(\mathbf{r},\lambda_{\mathrm{g}},\lambda_{\mathrm{e}})=\left(1-\tfrac{\lambda_{\mathrm{g}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{j}}\left(\left\lvert r_{j^{\prime}}\right\rvert-\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}\right)_{+}^{2}}}\right)_{+}\left(1-\tfrac{\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}}{\left\lvert r_{j}\right\rvert}\right)_{+}r_{j}.

(26)

That is, elements are first soft-thresholded individually by
\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}
, then the entire group is zeroed out if the norm of the surviving entries falls below
\lambda_{\mathrm{g}}
.

In the real-valued setting, AMP is known to converge faster than ISTA and to achieve sharper recovery thresholds for LASSO variants
[
10
,
4
,
7
]
.
To extend this advantage to the complex case, we apply the Onsager correction from Section
III
, which requires the Wirtinger derivative of the denoiser
\eta
.
At points where
|r_{j}|>\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}
and the group containing
j
is active, the
(\cdot)_{+}
operators can be dropped.
Let
\mathcal{G}_{j}^{*}=\{j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{j}:|r_{j^{\prime}}|>\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}\}
denote the active elements in the group of
j
.
Then,

\eta_{j}(\mathbf{r},\lambda_{\mathrm{g}},\lambda_{\mathrm{e}})=\left(1-\tfrac{\lambda_{\mathrm{g}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{j}^{*}}\left(\left\lvert r_{j^{\prime}}\right\rvert-\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{2}}}\right)\left(1-\tfrac{\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}}{\left\lvert r_{j}\right\rvert}\right)r_{j}.

Since
\eta_{j}
commutes with phase rotations, i.e.,
\eta_{j}(e^{\mathpzc{i}\theta}\mathbf{r})=e^{\mathpzc{i}\theta}\eta_{j}(\mathbf{r})
, the Onsager correction blocks inherit the
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
b&-a\end{bmatrix}
structure required by Definition
2
.
The Wirtinger derivative simplifies to

\displaystyle(\eta)^{\prime}_{j}=\frac{\partial\eta_{j}}{\partial r_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\Re(\eta_{j})}{\partial\Re(r_{j})}+\frac{\partial\Im(\eta_{j})}{\partial\Im(r_{j})}\right),

and
(\eta)^{\prime}_{j}=0
when
j
is inactive or belongs to an inactive group.
(The derivative is undefined on the measure-zero set where
|r_{j}|=\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}
or
\lVert\eta^{\mathrm{st}}(\mathbf{r}_{g},\lambda_{\mathrm{e}})\rVert_{2}=\lambda_{\mathrm{g}}
; these boundary points, however, do not affect the empirical average
\langle\eta^{\prime}\rangle
.)
Write
r_{g,k}
for the
k
th element of group
g
, and let
\mathcal{S}_{g}=\{k:|r_{g,k}|>\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}\}
be the active indices within group
g
(equivalently,
\mathcal{S}_{g}
corresponds to
\mathcal{G}_{j}^{*}
for any
j
belonging to group
g
).
Let
\mathcal{S}_{a}=\{g:\|\eta^{\mathrm{st}}(\mathbf{r}_{g},\lambda_{\mathrm{e}})\|_{2}>\lambda_{\mathrm{g}}\}
denote the set of active groups.
A direct computation gives

\displaystyle\langle\eta^{\prime}(\mathbf{r},\lambda_{\mathrm{g}},\lambda_{\mathrm{e}})\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}_{a}}\Bigg(\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{g}\right\rvert-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}_{g}}\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}}{2\left\lvert r_{g,k}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\hskip 100.0pt-\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{g}}}{2}\frac{2\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{g}\right\rvert-1-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}_{g}}\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}}{\left\lvert r_{g,k}\right\rvert}}{\sqrt{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}_{g}}\left(\left\lvert r_{g,k}\right\rvert-\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{2}}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}_{a}}\Bigg(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}_{g}}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}}{2\left\lvert r_{g,k}\right\rvert}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt-\lambda_{\mathrm{g}}\left(\frac{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}_{g}}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}}{2\left\lvert r_{g,k}\right\rvert}\right)-\frac{1}{2}}{\sqrt{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}_{g}}\left(\left\lvert r_{g,k}\right\rvert-\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{2}}}\right)\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}_{a}}\Bigg(\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{g}}}{2\lVert\eta^{\mathrm{st}}(\mathbf{r}_{g},\lambda_{\mathrm{e}})\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt+\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{g}}}{\lVert\eta^{\mathrm{st}}(\mathbf{r}_{g},\lambda_{\mathrm{e}})\rVert_{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{g}}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}}{2\left\lvert r_{g,j}\right\rvert}\right)\right)\Bigg).

Moreover, the SGL denoiser satisfies the regularity conditions of Assumption
8
.
Indeed, since complex soft-thresholding is
1
-Lipschitz and the group-norm shrinkage operator is non-expansive, their composition is Lipschitz continuous.
The required moment limits (A4)–(A6) follow from the finite second-moment assumption on
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.

The expressions above, together with the state evolution of Corollary
12
, provide all the ingredients needed to predict the asymptotic performance of complex AMP with the SGL denoiser.
We validate these predictions numerically in the next section.

, and (ii) demonstrate the performance gains afforded by the proposed non-separable complex denoiser that jointly exploits group sparsity, intra-group sparsity, and the complex structure of the signal.

VI
Conclusion

This article proved state evolution for complex-valued AMP with non-separable denoising functions.
The approach constructs an augmented real matrix-valued system that lifts the representation from
2n
to
4n
real dimensions, then recovers the complex domain through a many-to-one canonical transformation.
This strategy leverages existing real-valued matrix AMP results
[
5
,
12
]
and yields a direct reduction from the
2\times 2
matrix-valued state evolution of the augmented system to scalar complex state evolution recursions.
The framework further extends to the complex matrix-valued setting, where multiple feature vectors are estimated simultaneously and the Onsager corrections generalize from complex scalars to
p\times p
complex Jacobian matrices.

A key insight is that Wirtinger derivatives arise in the Onsager correction as a direct byproduct of the canonical transformation, unifying the treatment of complex derivatives within the AMP framework rather than requiring them to be imposed a priori.
The transformation and its structural properties were shown to preserve the AMP dynamics, enabling a complete state evolution characterization (Theorem
11
).
To demonstrate the practical reach of the theory, it was applied to the complex SGL, for which a closed-form proximal operator and its Wirtinger derivative were derived; this denoiser jointly exploits group-level and element-level sparsity in the complex domain.
Numerical experiments confirmed that the state evolution predictions closely track the empirical NMSE across the full SNR range, and that the proposed complex SGL denoiser uniformly outperforms baselines that exploit only partial structure (complex LASSO, complex GL, and real-valued SGL).

The framework applies broadly to complex-valued inverse problems in which a non-separable denoiser can exploit structural dependencies that separable or real-decoupled approaches cannot.
Beyond the SGL, natural application domains include OTFS preamble detection for unsourced random access
[
19
]
, OFDM channel estimation with clustered sparsity, and array processing problems where phase coherence and spatial group structure are central.

The current theory assumes i.i.d. complex Gaussian sensing matrices, which limits direct applicability to problems with structured operators.
Several directions for future work address this and other extensions.
First, extending the framework to structured sensing operators such as partial DFT matrices, OTFS channel models, or random convolutional designs, through connections to orthogonal or vector AMP, would broaden its applicability.
Second, because state evolution provides a scalar recursion predicting the mean-squared error, it can serve as an oracle for optimizing regularization parameters and the denoiser application schedule, automating choices that are currently set heuristically.
Third, unrolling the complex AMP iterations into a learnable architecture (deep unfolding) is a natural extension; the state evolution characterization proved here provides the theoretical foundation for such designs in the complex non-separable setting.
Fourth, replacing the proximal denoiser with a Bayes-optimal posterior mean estimator, which is itself non-separable under group-structured priors, may strengthen recovery guarantees.
Finally, extending the framework to generalized linear observation models (e.g., quantized measurements or phase retrieval) is a further direction for broadening the scope of complex AMP.

.
We denote
\mathbf{A}_{\iota,:}\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times N}
the
\iota
th row and
\mathbf{A}_{:,j}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1}
the
j
th column of
\mathbf{A}
.

Define the real-valued expansions of
\mathbf{W}
as in (
8
) and
\mathbf{S}
as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\mathbf{X}_{:,1}),\Im(\mathbf{X}_{:,1}),\cdots,\Re(\mathbf{X}_{:,p}),\Im(\mathbf{X}_{:,p})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2p}.

The product
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2p}
admits the block decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{P}^{1,1}&\cdots&\mathbf{P}^{1,p}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{P}^{n,1}&\cdots&\mathbf{P}^{n,p}\end{bmatrix},

where each block is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{\iota,j}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\mathbf{A}_{\iota,:})\Re(\mathbf{X}_{:,j})&\Re(\mathbf{A}_{\iota,:})\Im(\mathbf{X}_{:,j})\\
\Im(\mathbf{A}_{\iota,:})\Re(\mathbf{X}_{:,j})&\Im(\mathbf{A}_{\iota,:})\Im(\mathbf{X}_{:,j})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}.

Recalling
\rho=[1,~\mathpzc{i}]^{\mathsf{T}}
from Section
III-A
, we verify that

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{P}^{\iota,j}\rho=\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathpzc{i}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\mathbf{A}_{\iota,:})(\Re(\mathbf{X}_{:,j})+\mathpzc{i}\Im(\mathbf{X}_{:,j}))\\
\Im(\mathbf{A}_{\iota,:})(\Re(\mathbf{X}_{:,j})+\mathpzc{i}\Im(\mathbf{X}_{:,j}))\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=(\Re(\mathbf{A}_{\iota,:})+\mathpzc{i}\Im(\mathbf{A}_{\iota,:}))(\Re(\mathbf{X}_{:,j})+\mathpzc{i}\Im(\mathbf{X}_{:,j}))=\mathbf{A}_{\iota,:}\mathbf{X}_{:,j}.

This confirms that the real expansion preserves the algebraic structure of the complex product under collapse.
One readily verifies that
\mathbf{R}_{R}\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{X}
, and therefore
\mathbf{R}_{R}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}
.

For brevity, we suppress the iteration superscript
k
in the remainder of this appendix.
Let
\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2p}
and
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2p}
be real matrices satisfying
\mathbf{R}_{R}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{V}
and
\mathbf{H}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{U}
, respectively.
Define the real matrix-valued function
f
from
\mathpzc{f}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}{=}f(\mathbf{H}){=}\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\mathpzc{f}_{:,1}),\Im(\mathpzc{f}_{:,1}),\dots,\Re(\mathpzc{f}_{:,p}),\Im(\mathpzc{f}_{:,p})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2p},

so that
\mathbf{F}=\mathpzc{f}(\mathbf{U})=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{R}_{L}
.
Similarly, for
1\leq\iota\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq p
, define the real matrix-valued function
g
from
\mathpzc{g}
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=g(\mathbf{E})=\begin{bmatrix}g^{\iota,j}(\mathbf{E})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2p},

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{\iota,j}

\displaystyle=g^{\iota,j}(\mathbf{E})=\begin{bmatrix}\Re\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{R}_{R}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{R}_{L})&\Im\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{R}_{R}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{R}_{L})\\
\Im\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{R}_{R}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{R}_{L})&-\Re\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{R}_{R}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{R}_{L})\end{bmatrix}.

The
2\times 2

, paralleling the vector case of Definition

.

B-C

Proof of Proposition
13

We show that the real matrix AMP equations (
12
)–(
13
), when collapsed via
\mathbf{R}_{R}
and
\mathbf{R}_{L}
, recover the complex matrix AMP equations (
18
)–(
19
).
The argument proceeds in three steps, verifying in turn the forward-channel terms and both Onsager correction terms.

Step 1: We show that
\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{G}
.

From the definitions,
\mathbf{R}_{R}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}
.
For the adjoint term, we compute

\begin{split}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}_{L})^{\iota,j}&=(g(\mathbf{E})\mathbf{R}_{L})^{\iota,j}=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{V}))+\mathpzc{i}\Im(\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{V}))\\
\Im(\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{V}))-\mathpzc{i}\Re(\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{V}))\end{bmatrix}\\
&=\begin{bmatrix}\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{V})\\
-\mathpzc{i}\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{V})\end{bmatrix}=\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{V})\overline{\rho}.\end{split}

(36)

Stacking over all
\iota\in[n]
and
j\in[p]
, we obtain
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathpzc{g}(\mathbf{V})\otimes\overline{\rho}=(\mathbf{I}_{n}\otimes\overline{\rho})\mathpzc{g}(\mathbf{V})=\mathbf{R}_{R}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{G}
.
Since
\mathbf{W}
is real,
\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_{R}^{\mathsf{H}}=\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{R}_{R}^{\mathsf{H}}=(\mathbf{R}_{R}\mathbf{W})^{\mathsf{H}}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{H}}
, and therefore

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_{R}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{G}.

Step 2: We show that
\mathbf{R}_{R}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}}
, establishing
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{G}\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}}
.

We first compute

\begin{split}&(\mathbf{R}_{R}\mathbf{Q})^{\iota,j}=\rho^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Q}^{\iota,j}\\
&=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{V}))+\mathpzc{i}\Im(\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{V})),\Im(\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{V}))-\mathpzc{i}\Re(\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{V}))\end{bmatrix}\\
&=\mathpzc{g}_{\iota,j}(\mathbf{V})\rho^{\mathsf{H}},\end{split}

(37)

which gives
\mathbf{R}_{R}\mathbf{Q}=\mathpzc{g}(\mathbf{V})\otimes\rho^{\mathsf{H}}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{R}_{L}^{\mathsf{H}}
.
It remains to show that
\mathbf{R}_{L}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}}
, i.e., that
\rho^{\mathsf{H}}(\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\iota,j}\rho=\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}}_{\iota,j}
for each
\iota,j\in[p]
.

Recall that in the real system,
\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}}_{\underline{i},\underline{j}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{d=1}^{M}\frac{\partial f_{d,\underline{i}}}{\partial\mathbf{H}_{d,\underline{j}}}
with
M=N
and
m=2n
.
Each pair
\underline{i},\underline{j}
belongs to the first or second position within the
(\iota,j)
th sub-block, where
\iota,j\in[p]
.
Expanding the sub-block, we obtain

\displaystyle(\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\iota,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{d=1}^{N}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\Re(\mathpzc{f}_{d,\iota})}{\partial\Re(\mathbf{U}_{d,j})}&\frac{\partial\Im(\mathpzc{f}_{d,\iota})}{\partial\Re(\mathbf{U}_{d,j})}\\
\frac{\partial\Re(\mathpzc{f}_{d,\iota})}{\partial\Im(\mathbf{U}_{d,j})}&\frac{\partial\Im(\mathpzc{f}_{d,\iota})}{\partial\Im(\mathbf{U}_{d,j})}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{d=1}^{N}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Re(\mathbf{U}_{d,j})}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\Im(\mathbf{U}_{d,j})}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\mathpzc{f}_{d,\iota})&\Im(\mathpzc{f}_{d,\iota})\end{bmatrix}.

By Lemma
6
(iv)
,
\frac{1}{2}\rho^{\mathsf{H}}\begin{bmatrix}\partial/\partial\Re(\mathbf{U}_{d,j})\\
\partial/\partial\Im(\mathbf{U}_{d,j})\end{bmatrix}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{U}_{d,j}}
, so

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathsf{H}}(\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\iota,j}\rho=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\mathpzc{f}_{d,\iota}}{\partial\mathbf{U}_{d,j}}=\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}}_{\iota,j}.

Therefore,
\mathbf{R}_{R}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{R}_{L}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}}
, which establishes

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{R}_{R}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{R}_{R}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}})\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{G}\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Step 3: We show that
\mathbf{C}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{R}_{L}\mathbf{K}^{\mathsf{T}}
, establishing
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{K}^{\mathsf{T}}
.

In the real system,
\mathbf{C}^{\mathsf{T}}_{\underline{i},\underline{j}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\underline{d}=1}^{m}\frac{\partial g_{\underline{d},\underline{i}}}{\partial\mathbf{E}_{\underline{d},\underline{j}}}
.
Grouping into sub-blocks indexed by
\iota,j\in[p]
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{\mathsf{T}}_{\underline{i},\underline{j}}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{d=1}^{n}\sum_{u=1,2}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{u,:})^{\mathsf{T}}}(g_{u,:}^{d,\iota}),

where
\underline{i},\underline{j}
belong to the first or second position within the
(\iota,j)
th sub-block.
Expanding the full sub-block and substituting the block structure of
g
,

\displaystyle(\mathbf{C}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\iota,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{d=1}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{1,1}}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{1,2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}g^{d,\iota}_{1,1}&g^{d,\iota}_{1,2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\hskip 60.0pt+\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{2,1}}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{2,2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}g^{d,\iota}_{2,1}&g^{d,\iota}_{2,2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{d=1}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{1,1}}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{2,1}}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{1,2}}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{2,2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re\mathpzc{g}_{d,\iota}&\Im\mathpzc{g}_{d,\iota}\\
\Im\mathpzc{g}_{d,\iota}&-\Re\mathpzc{g}_{d,\iota}\end{bmatrix}.

(38)

Multiplying on the right by
\mathbf{R}_{L}
and noting that
\begin{bmatrix}\Re\mathpzc{g}_{d,\iota}&\Im\mathpzc{g}_{d,\iota}\\
\Im\mathpzc{g}_{d,\iota}&-\Re\mathpzc{g}_{d,\iota}\end{bmatrix}\rho=\overline{\rho}\,\mathpzc{g}_{d,\iota}(\mathbf{V})
, we obtain

(\mathbf{C}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_{L})^{\iota,j}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{d=1}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{1,1}}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{2,1}}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{1,2}}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{2,2}}\end{bmatrix}\overline{\rho}\,\mathpzc{g}_{d,\iota}(\mathbf{V}).

Since
\Re(\mathbf{V}_{d,j})=\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{1,1}-\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{2,2}
and
\Im(\mathbf{V}_{d,j})=\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{1,2}+\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{2,1}
, by the chain rule the derivative matrix transforms as

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{1,1}}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{2,1}}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{1,2}}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{E}^{d,j}_{2,2}}\end{bmatrix}\overline{\rho}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Re(\mathbf{V}_{d,j})}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\Im(\mathbf{V}_{d,j})}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\Im(\mathbf{V}_{d,j})}&-\frac{\partial}{\partial\Re(\mathbf{V}_{d,j})}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-\mathpzc{i}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\rho\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\Re(\mathbf{V}_{d,j})}-\mathpzc{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Im(\mathbf{V}_{d,j})}\right),

where the last step uses the factorization into a scalar times
\rho
.
By Lemma
6
(v)
, the Wirtinger derivative gives

\displaystyle(\mathbf{C}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_{L})^{\iota,j}

\displaystyle{=}\rho\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\Re(\mathbf{V}_{d,j})}-\mathpzc{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Im(\mathbf{V}_{d,j})}\right)\mathpzc{g}_{d,\iota}(\mathbf{V})

\displaystyle=\rho\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\mathpzc{g}_{d,\iota}(\mathbf{V})}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{d,j}}=\rho\mathbf{K}^{\mathsf{T}}_{\iota,j}.

Therefore,
\mathbf{C}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_{L}=\mathbf{K}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\rho=(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\rho)\mathbf{K}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{R}_{L}\mathbf{K}^{\mathsf{T}}
, and we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{R}_{L}=(\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{C}^{\mathsf{T}})\mathbf{R}_{L}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{R}_{L}\mathbf{K}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{K}^{\mathsf{T}}.
