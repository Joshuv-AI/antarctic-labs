Analyses of the microbial safety and physicochemical characteristics of beef loin treated with high-pressure
Authors: Ui-Bin Baek, Hack-Youn Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of high-pressure processing (HPP) on microbial safety and physicochemical quality of raw beef intended for use in raw pet food, with particular emphasis on microbial inactivation and physicochemical characteristics changes during refrigerated storage. Beef samples were treated at pressures of 0.1, 100, 300, and 500 MPa for 5–15 min and stored at refrigerated conditions for up to 2 weeks. Microvial populations, including Salmonella Typhimurium, Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Listeria monocytogenes, and Bacillus cereus, were enumerated during storage. Physicochemical properties, including proximate composition, pH, color, water-holding capacity, shear force, thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS), and volatile basic nitrogen (VBN), were also analyzed. Microbial counts decreased with increasing pressure and holding time. The 500 MPa for 15 min treatment reduced S. Typhimurium to below the detection limit (< 1 log CFU/g) throughout storage, while other pathogens showed substantial reductions with limited regrowth. HPP did not affect proximate composition of pH; however, higher pressures increased lightness, yellowness, shear force, WHC, and TBARS, while redness decreased. VBN levels decreased with increasing pressure but increased during storage. HPP effectively enhanced microbial safety of raw beef while inducing pressure and time-dependent physicochemical changes associated with muscle protein denaturation, myoglobin oxidation, and enzyme inactivation. These results support HPP as a promising intervention for improving the safety of raw pet food, although quality changes should be considered in process optimization.Keywords: Raw pet food, Non-thermal sterilization, Foodborne pathogens, High-pressure, Microbial safety, Physicochemical characteristics

Introduction

Dry pet foods are the most commercially distributed and sold. Dry foods have a low moisture content, resulting in minimal spoilage, long shelf life, and the ability to meet essential nutritional requirements (Kazimierska et al. 2021). However, as the number of pet-owning households and public awareness of companion animal welfare have increased, consumers are increasingly considering pet foods that are not only nutritionally balanced but also highly palatable to their pets, as palatability influences feeding acceptance and consumption behavior (Schleicher et al. 2019). In parallel, the growing humanization of pets has led owners to perceive them as family members and to prefer more natural and minimally processed diets, contributing to increased interest in raw pet food (Cunha Quintais 2024). Raw pet food has a higher amino acid digestibility and soluble amino acid content than dry food, making it a higher-quality diet (Geary et al. 2023). However, it can also be a source of zoonotic diseases and parasite contamination, necessitating appropriate sterilization (Ribeiro-Almeida et al. 2024). Because raw meat products are distributed without a terminal heat treatment, microbial safety must be ensured while simultaneously preserving structural and biochemical quality attributes that are highly sensitive to processing intensity.

Thermal processing is one of the most commonly used sterilization techniques in the food industry; however, it can negatively affect product quality by causing nutrient loss, lipid oxidation, and the formation of harmful compounds (Nurjati et al. 2025). Ultraviolet (UV) and plasma treatments are limited in their effectiveness for microbial control in meat-based raw pet food, as their action is restricted to surface decontamination, making them less suitable for opaque and structurally complex products [7–8]. Irradiation is effective for microbial inactivation due to its high penetration capacity and is regulated for feed sterilization in countries such as South Korea, the United States, and those in Europe. However, its application is constrained by low consumer acceptance and the potential for quality deterioration under excessive treatment conditions (Beak and Kim 2026). In contrast, high-pressure processing (HPP) inactivates microorganisms through pressure-induced physical disruption, resulting in relatively minimal quality degradation. Consequently, it has been increasingly applied in the pet food industry (Bolumar et al. 2020). Nevertheless, excessive pressure treatment may induce myoglobin denaturation and reduce water-holding capacity, highlighting the importance of optimizing pressure and processing conditions (Chmiel et al. 2025). Although previous studies have investigated various types of meat, research systematically establishing optimal HPP conditions for raw pet food applications remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate high-pressure processing conditions using beef, a primary ingredient in raw pet food.

High-pressure processing is a non-thermal sterilization method using hydrostatic pressure. Pressures of 300–600 MPa induce ribosomal disassembly in microorganisms, inhibiting DNA and protein synthesis, and thereby affecting growth (Roobab et al. 2022). It is primarily used for seafood, fruits, vegetables, and meat, applying pressure uniformly throughout the food, thus having minimal impact on the packaging type or size (Liu et al. 2021). Accordingly, studies have analyzed microbial inactivation and physicochemical changes in meat subjected to high-pressure processing, including studies on beef, pork, chicken, and fish (Alejandro 2024, Chmiel et al. 2025, Ismaiel et al. 2025, Yoon et al. 2025). In this process, ultra-high-pressure treatment is influenced by parameters, such as pressure, time, and temperature (Zhou et al. 2010). Increases in pressure and pressure holding time enhance microbial inactivation within meat (Chmiel et al. 2023, Marušić Radovčić et al. 2019, Zhu et al. 2022). Nevertheless, microbial inactivation under high pressure does not necessarily imply complete lethality, as sublethally injured or pressure-tolerant populations may persist and influence safety during subsequent storage (Nasiłowska et al. 2021). However, pressures above a certain level induce myoglobin oxidation, increase meat lightness, and decrease redness, resulting in a pale appearance [23–24]. Therefore, it is necessary to explore subcritical sterilization conditions that minimize changes in physicochemical properties while achieving microbial inactivation.

By integrating microbial survival patterns with concurrent physicochemical responses under different pressure–time regimes, this study seeks to provide a framework for interpreting high-pressure effects beyond simple inactivation efficiency and to identify processing windows relevant to raw pet food applications.

Materials and methods

Samples

The raw beef loin (Black Angus; Daonchugsan, Yesan, Korea) used in the raw pet food formulation was refrigerated at 4 °C for 24 h after slaughter. Excess fat and connective tissue were removed from each raw meat sample. The samples were stored at 4 °C for 0, 1, or 2 weeks before use. The samples for physicochemical analysis were shaped into 1-cm thick pieces, and those for microbiological analysis were shaped into 1-cm thick, 25-g pieces and used in the experiments. All samples were analyzed after high-pressure processing under the specified treatment conditions.

Microbial culture and inoculation

To analyze the killing effect and recovery characteristics of raw pet food before and after high-pressure sterilization, the major harmful microorganisms were inoculated into raw pet food and treated with high pressure. The inoculated microorganisms were Salmonella Typhimurium (ATCC 43971, ATCC 19525, and ATCC DT104), Listeria monocytogenes (ATCC 15313, ATCC 19111, and ATCC 19115), Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 13565 [SEA], ATCC 14458 [SEB], ATCC 19095 [SEC], ATCC 23235 [SED], and FRI 913 [SEE]), Bacillus cereus (ATCC 14579 [diarrheal type] and ATCC 10876 [emetic type]), Escherichia coli (ATCC 13715, ATCC 35150, ATCC 43889, and ATCC 43890), were used. Microorganisms were plated on tryptic soy agar (236950, BD Difco, Detroit, MI, USA) as quarter streaks and incubated in an incubator (JSMI-02 C, JSR, Gongju, Korea) at 37 °C for 24 h. The colonies formed on tryptic soy agar were then picked up with sterilized loops, placed in 5 mL of tryptic soy broth (211825, BD Difco), and incubated (JSR) at 37 °C for 24 h. Each cultured microbial mixture was transferred to a sterilized 50 mL conical tube and centrifuged (Supra R22, Hanil Science, Gimpo, Korea) at 4 °C, 4,000×g for 15 min to generate a pellet. The resulting pellet was washed with buffered peptone water (BPW; 218105, BD Difco), diluted in 9 mL of BPW (BD Difco), and used as the microbial inoculum. After evenly dropping the microbial inoculum onto the surface of the sample, the sample was dried for 1 h and packed in a vacuum pack (15 × 20 cm).

High-pressure processing

High-pressure treatment was conducted at 100, 300, and 500 MPa, with pressure-holding times of 5 and 15 min. High-pressure processing was performed using a high-pressure sterilizer (Uhde 055–60, Thyssenkrupp Industrial Solutions, Dortmund, Germany) installed in the Korea National Food Cluster. The external temperature of the high-pressure sterilizer was ambient, and the internal temperature was 21℃ (room temperature). After sample preparation and microbial inoculation, samples were vacuum-packaged and immediately transported to the high-pressure processing facility within 1 h under refrigerated conditions (4 ± 1℃) using an insulated container. Upon arrival, samples were subjected to high-pressure treatment without delay. The time interval between microbial inoculation and HHP treatment was kept within 2 h to minimize changes in microbial populations prior to processing.

Microbial viability analysis

The microbial killing effect of the high-pressure treatment was determined by mixing samples stored for 0, 1, and 2 weeks with 0.1% BPW (BD Difco) at a ratio of 1:10 (25 g: 225 mL) in a sample bag (1930 F, 3 M, Forest City, IA, USA) and homogenizing for 1 min with a stomacher (WH4000-2751, 3 M Korea, Seoul, Korea). Next, serial decimal dilutions were prepared by transferring 1 mL of the homogenate into 9 mL of 0.1% BPW (BD Difco), followed by successive dilutions up to 10− 6. Salmonella was counted using MC-Media Pads for Salmonella (SK06B25, JNC Corp, Tokyo, Japan), Listeria was counted on 3 M petrifilm (6447, Neogen 3 M, Saint Paul, MN, USA), Staphylococcus was counted on Microfast S. aureus count plate (PKI-LR1005, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA), E. coli was counted on Microfast Coliform & E. coli Count Plate (PKI-LR1007, PerkinElmer), and Bacillus was determined using Microfast B. cereus Count Plate (PKI-LR1010, PerkinElmer). After dispensing 1 mL of the dilutions onto film media, Salmonella, Listeria, E. coli, and Staphylococcus were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and Bacillus was incubated at 30 °C for 24 h. Each microbial colony was counted and expressed in log colony-forming units (log CFU/g).

Proximate composition analysis

The estimated composition of the high-pressure treatment was determined using the AOAC method (AOAC 2019). Moisture content was determined using the normal pressure drying method (AOAC 950.46) at 105℃ in an oven. The crude protein content was determined using the Kjeldahl method (AOAC 992.15). The crude fat content was determined using the Soxhlet method (AOAC 960.39), and the ash content was determined using the direct method (AOAC 920.153) in a 550 °C electrofiring furnace. All data are expressed as percentages.

pH analysis

The pH of the high-pressure treatment was determined using a glass electrode pH meter (Model S220, Mettler-Toledo, Greifensee, Switzerland) calibrated with buffer solutions (pH 4.01, 7.0, and 10.0; Suntex Instruments Co., Ltd., Taipei, Taiwan). Samples stored for 0, 1, and 2 weeks were weighed at 3 g and, to ensure uniform homogenization and stable electrode contact for accurate and reproducible pH measurement, homogenized with 12 mL of distilled water in an ultra-turrax (HMZ-20DN, Poonglim Tech, Seongnam, Korea) at 10,000 rpm for 1 min. The pH of the homogenate was measured using a glass-electrode pH meter.

Color analysis

The chromaticity of the high-pressure treatment was measured using a colorimeter (CR-10, Minolta, Tokyo, Japan), calibrated with a white standard plate having a CIE L* value of + 97.83, a CIE a* value of -0.43, and a CIE b* value of + 1.98. The colorimeter used a pulsed xenon lamp as the light source and was equipped with an 8-mm measurement aperture, a 10° observation angle, and a D65 standard illuminant. The CIE L* value indicating lightness, CIE a* value indicating redness, and CIE b* value indicating yellowness were measured on the surfaces of the samples stored for 0, 1, and 2 weeks, using a colorimeter. Before the measurement, the samples were allowed to bloom at 25 °C (room temperature) for 30 min.

Shear force analysis

The shear force of the high-pressure treatment was measured by cutting the central part of each sample into 1 × 1 × 2 cm pieces. After setting the measurement conditions (test speed, 3.0 mm/s; head speed, 3.0 mm/s; distance, 19.0 mm; force, 5.6 N) of a texture analyzer equipped with a V-blade (TA 1, Lloyd Instruments, Largo, FL, USA), the shear force was measured perpendicularly to the direction of the sample muscle fibers and expressed in N.

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substance (TBARS) analysis

The TBARS of high-pressure-treated raw beef were measured using a modified method of Witte et al. (1970). Each sample (10 g) stored for 0, 1, and 2 weeks was weighed and mixed with 25 mL of 10% perchloric acid and 0.2 mL of 0.3% butylated hydroxytoluene. The mixture was homogenized using a homogenizer (AM-5, Nihonseiki Kaisha, Tokyo, Japan) at 10,000 rpm for 1 min. The homogenate was mixed with 25 mL of distilled water, and the supernatant was filtered through a filter paper (Whatman). The supernatant was mixed with 2-thiobarbituric acid in a 1:1 ratio and reacted in a water bath (JSWB-30T, JSR, Gongju, Korea) at 100 °C for 10 min. The reactants were cooled for 10 min, and the absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a multimode microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). The TBARS values were calculated as malondialdehyde (MDA) equivalents using a conversion factor (5.2) derived from a standard curve prepared with 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane (TEP), following the method of Witte et al. Volatile basic nitrogen (VBN) analysis

The VBN content of the high-pressure-treated raw beef was measured using the Conway microdiffusion method. Each sample (10 g) stored for 0, 1, and 2 weeks was weighed, homogenized with 30 mL of distilled water using a homogenizer (Nihonseiki Kaisha) at 10,000 rpm for 1 min, and the supernatant was filtered through filter paper (Whatman). Statistical analysis

The analysis was repeated at least three times. The results were analyzed using the statistical processing program SAS (version 9.4 for Windows; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and means and standard deviations were calculated. Additionally, significance was verified at p < 0.05, using one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) and Duncan’s multiple range test. Python was used to generate heatmap visualizations of the microbial counts. NumPy was used for data structuring, and Matplotlib for heatmap rendering and graphical interpretation of pressure-time interactions.

Results and discussion

Microbial viability

The microbial colony counts of high-pressure-treated raw beef are shown in Fig. 1. In all treatment groups, the number of surviving microorganisms showed a decreasing tendency compared to that in the control group as the pressure and treatment time increased. This pressure–time dependency indicates that microbial responses to high-pressure processing follow a graded intensity pattern rather than a binary lethal effect. At low pressure levels (e.g., 100 MPa), the microbial inactivation effect was limited, which can be attributed to reversible cellular responses rather than irreversible damage. At this pressure, membrane permeabilization may occur without sufficient disruption of intracellular structures, allowing microorganisms to recover and survive. Cellular functions such as protein synthesis and enzyme activity may be affected; however, severe membrane damage does not occur, and the observed changes remain largely reversible, representing a state of sublethal injury rather than complete inactivation (Rajendran et al. 2022). As pressure increases, structural disruption of the cell membrane and protein denaturation progress, leading to irreversible cellular damage and enhanced microbial inactivation. This transition from reversible to more extensive cellular effects reflects a pressure-dependent shift in cellular response. This pressure-dependent microbial response is quantitatively supported by the correlation analysis (Fig. 2), which showed negative correlations between microbial survival and processing intensity, particularly under conditions of 300 MPa for 15 min or higher. Consistent with this, the strongest negative correlations were observed at the highest pressure–time condition, confirming that microbial inactivation was most pronounced under these conditions. Sert and Çoşkun (Sert and Çoşkun 2024) reported that when applying 300, 350, and 450 MPa of pressure for 5, 10, and 15 min to beef M. longissimus costarum weighing 1–2 kg, the total mesophilic aerobic bacteria decreased as pressure and holding time increased, which is consistent with the results of this study. These findings further support that increasing pressure intensity and holding time may enhance cell membrane disruption and protein denaturation, leading to enzyme inactivation and inhibition of DNA replication, thereby suppressing cellular growth (Sehrawat et al. 2021). Pressure resistance varies depending on the microbial structure. S. Typhimurium decreased to below the detection limit in the 500 MPa 15-min treatment group and was not re-detected during the 1–2 week storage period (< 1 log CFU/g). E. coli, L. monocytogenes, S. aureus, and B. cereus were not completely inactivated by high-pressure treatment; however, immediately after treatment (0 weeks), microorganisms in the 500 MPa 15-min treatment group decreased by 77.47%, 77.58%, 48.77%, and 71.83%, respectively, compared with the control group. Additionally, during the storage period of 0–2 weeks under the same treatment conditions, the microorganisms decreased by 59.46%, 68.35%, 53.39%, and 92.08%, respectively. The incomplete inactivation observed for several microorganisms suggests the presence of pressure-tolerant or sublethally injured populations that may persist immediately after treatment. Gram-positive bacteria, such as L. monocytogenes and S. aureus, have thicker peptidoglycan layers than gram-negative bacteria, resulting in higher pressure resistance (Klotz et al. 2010). Among these, S. aureus exhibits the highest pressure resistance compared with other microorganisms. It synthesizes the antioxidant staphyloxanthin and has a high content of branched-chain fatty acids in the cell membrane, making it highly resistant to external stress [30–31]. In addition, it has a high degree of secondary cross-linking of peptidoglycan and contains teichoic acid, a negatively charged polymer, making it stable against cell wall deformation [32–33]. These structural and biochemical features collectively confer enhanced mechanical stability and oxidative stress tolerance under high-pressure conditions, explaining the pronounced pressure resistance of S. aureus observed in this study. B. cereus exhibited microbial inactivation similar to that of other microorganisms, except S. aureus, immediately after the ultra-high-pressure treatment. This is because pressures above 50 MPa stimulate germinant receptors and SpoVA channels within spores, inducing the release of Ca2+ and Ca2+–DPA, and increasing the moisture content of the nuclear layer, activating cortex-lytic enzymes to hydrolyze the cell cortex (Delbrück et al. 2021). Once germinated, B. cereus spores are converted into vegetative cells, losing pressure resistance and becoming more susceptible to oxidative stress and DNA damage under pressure and prolonged storage, ultimately leading to cell death (Setlow and Christie 2021). This behavior supports a germination–inactivation sequence rather than direct spore lethality under high-pressure conditions. Therefore, the most effective condition for inactivating foodborne pathogenic microorganisms in raw beef was high-pressure treatment at 500 MPa for 15 min. However, the results further indicate that microbial safety under high-pressure processing should be evaluated in terms of pressure–time regimes that account for differential resistance, sublethal injury, and storage behavior, rather than immediate post-treatment reductions alone. The lowest pressure resistance was observed in S. Typhimurium, which decreased to below detectable levels (< 1 log CFU/g) under the same treatment. This was likely due to the thin peptidoglycan layer of S. Typhimurium, a gram-negative bacterium. In contrast, the highest pressure resistance was observed in S. aureus, which was attributed to its thick and highly cross-linked peptidoglycan structure, along with the presence of stress-protective compounds, such as staphyloxanthin and branched-chain fatty acids. Overall, these findings demonstrate a hierarchical microbial response to high-pressure processing, governed by cell wall architecture and stress adaptation mechanisms.

Proximate composition

The proximate composition of high-pressure-treated raw beef is presented in Table 1. No significant changes in proximate composition were observed among any of the treatment groups according to pressure level and holding time (p > 0.05). This compositional stability indicates that high-pressure processing does not alter the bulk nutritional matrix of raw beef across the applied pressure–time regimes. Consistent with this, correlation analysis (Fig. 2) showed no meaningful association between processing intensity and proximate composition parameters, indicating that these components remained largely unaffected by pressure and treatment time. Kent et al. (Kent et al. 2025) reported that treatment of bovine M. longissimus thoracis at 200 MPa for 20 min did not result in significant changes in proximate composition, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. In meat systems, changes in proximate composition following high-pressure processing are generally associated with drip loss rather than alterations in molecular bonding structures (Bolumar et al. 2021). In the present study, no visible exudate or drip loss was observed after HHP treatment under vacuum-packaged conditions, suggesting that pressure-induced fluid release was minimal. Moisture may be redistributed between the myofibrillar and extracellular spaces under pressure, but vacuum packaging effectively suppresses moisture evaporation and exudate loss, resulting in no measurable change in moisture content (Ji et al. 2024). Therefore, the absence of drip loss likely contributed to the stability of moisture content and, consequently, the overall proximate composition. Although pressure treatment can induce protein denaturation and structural changes, these effects do not result in measurable changes in total protein content unless accompanied by physical loss such as drip (Jia et al. 2021). Thus, functional modifications of proteins observed under high pressure occur without changes in total protein content. Similarly, while lipid oxidation may occur under high-pressure conditions, it does not necessarily lead to changes in crude fat content in the absence of exudate loss (Kantono et al. 2020). Ash content, representing total mineral composition, is also unlikely to be affected unless minerals are physically lost through drip. This suggests that the effects of pressure on molecular bonding structures are minimal and that vacuum packaging effectively prevents the loss of proximate components during processing. These results indicate that the absence of drip loss under vacuum conditions is a key factor in maintaining compositional stability following HHP treatment.

WHC and shear force

The WHC of high-pressure-treated raw beef is shown in Table 1. No significant differences in water-holding capacity were observed among most treatment groups compared with the control (p > 0.05), and the 500 MPa for 15 min treatment exhibited the highest value among all treatments, showing a significant difference compared with the control and other pressure-treated groups (p < 0.05). This trend at higher pressure–time conditions is consistent with the correlation analysis (Fig. 2), which showed a positive association between WHC and processing intensity. Zuki et al. (Zuki et al. 2025) reported that beef and buffalo topside samples (2.54 × 2.54 × 1.5 cm) treated at 300 MPa for 2 min exhibited a higher water-holding capacity than the untreated control, which is partially consistent with the present results, particularly at higher pressure and longer holding time conditions. Pressure-induced protein denaturation alters protein structure and may expose hydrophobic residues, which can reduce the stable binding of water molecules; therefore, its effect on WHC is not unidirectional and depends on processing conditions, such as pressure level and holding time (Bolumar et al. 2021). At higher pressure conditions, pressure-induced conformational changes in myofibrillar proteins may lead to the disruption of non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions, resulting in structural rearrangement (Cheftel and Culioli 1997). Such structural modifications may promote protein–water interactions and contribute to water retention under specific conditions (Tornberg 2005). In addition, high-pressure treatment enhances the adsorption capacity of sarcoplasmic protein-derived microvesicles, facilitating the formation of complex structures between proteins and water molecules (Zhang et al. 2015). These aggregated microvesicular structures exhibit increased surface charge, molecular flexibility, and water-binding capacity, which collectively contribute to the observed increase in water-holding capacity (Zhao et al. 2022). Notably, the increase in WHC reflects water immobilization within a pressure-stabilized protein network rather than free water accumulation, suggesting a qualitative change in water distribution within the muscle matrix. This interpretation is further supported by the correlation analysis, in which increased WHC was accompanied by increased shear force, indicating that water retention occurs within a more rigid and structured protein matrix. In conclusion, high-pressure processing did not consistently affect the water-holding capacity of raw beef loin, although a significant increase was observed at 500 MPa for 15 min.

The shear force of high-pressure-treated raw beef is shown in Table 1. As pressure and treatment time increased, the shear force increased sequentially, with the 500 MPa for 15 min treatment showing the highest value and being significantly higher than the control group (p < 0.05). This trend at higher pressure–time conditions is consistent with the correlation analysis (Fig. 2), which showed a positive association between shear force and processing intensity. Jung et al. (Jung et al. 2000) reported that beef M. longissimus dorsi samples with a thickness of 2.5 cm treated at 520 MPa for 4 min and 20 s exhibited higher shear force values than untreated controls, which is consistent with the results of the present study. Meat texture is primarily influenced by actomyosin interactions, trivalent collagen cross-linking, and the degree of marbling (Roy and Bruce 2024). High-pressure processing induces dissociation of the actomyosin complex, myofibrillar fragmentation, and subsequent aggregation, leading to irregular muscle fiber structures and increased hardness (Zhao et al. 2017). Furthermore, under high-pressure conditions, dissociated myofibrillar proteins interact through exposed sulfhydryl (–SH) groups and hydrophobic residues, promoting protein aggregation and the formation of gel-like networks. These structural rearrangements result in a denser and more rigid protein matrix, which is directly associated with the observed increase in shear force (Liu et al. 2022). Importantly, the concurrent increases in water-holding capacity and shear force demonstrate that enhanced water retention under high-pressure conditions does not necessarily correspond to improved tenderness. This apparent paradox indicates that pressure-induced protein aggregation immobilizes water within a mechanically rigid network, resulting in tissue hardening despite higher water retention. This interpretation is further supported by the correlation analysis, in which shear force increased concurrently with WHC, indicating that pressure-induced structural changes simultaneously influence both textural properties and water retention. Importantly, the concurrent increases in water-holding capacity and shear force suggest that enhanced water retention under high-pressure conditions does not necessarily correspond to improved tenderness. This apparent paradox may be attributed to pressure-induced protein aggregation, which could immobilize water within a mechanically rigid network, resulting in tissue hardening despite higher water retention. In conclusion, high-pressure processing treatment increased the shear force of raw beef loin, resulting in tissue hardening. This effect is likely associated with pressure- and time-dependent changes in the physical properties of muscle tissue, particularly structural modifications of myofibrillar proteins and their subsequent aggregation behavior. Taken together, these results indicate a potential decoupling between water retention and textural softening under high-pressure regimes, emphasizing the importance of evaluating structural reorganization rather than individual quality parameters in isolation.

pH and color

The pH changes of high-pressure-treated raw beef loin are presented in Table 2. No significant differences in pH were observed as a function of processing pressure or treatment time. In addition, no significant changes were observed during storage, indicating that storage period had minimal influence on pH stability across all treatments. Consistent with this, correlation analysis (Fig. 2) showed no meaningful association between pH and processing intensity, confirming that pH remained largely unaffected by pressure and treatment time. Ma and Ledward (Ma and Ledward 2004) reported no pH changes in beef M. longissimus dorsi samples (2.5 × 2.5 × 6 cm) treated at 200, 400, 600, and 800 MPa for 20 min, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. Similarly, McArdle et al. (McArdle et al. 2010) observed no significant pH changes in beef M. pectoralis profundus samples (2.5 cm thickness) treated at 200, 300, and 400 MPa for 20 min. These results suggest that high-pressure processing does not induce thermal denaturation that would otherwise affect pH (Sikes and Tume 2014). This stability may be associated with the inhibitory effects of high-pressure processing on microbial growth and endogenous enzyme activity, which are major contributors to postmortem biochemical changes in meat. Suppression of microbial metabolism may limit the production of acidic or basic compounds during storage, while changes in glycolytic and proteolytic enzyme activity may reduce the generation of metabolites that would otherwise alter pH. As a result, the postmortem biochemical pathways responsible for pH decline or fluctuation may be attenuated under high-pressure conditions, contributing to the observed pH stability.

The color changes of high-pressure-treated raw beef are shown in Table 2. During the 0–2 week storage period, lightness increased with increasing treatment pressure and time, reflecting pressure-induced modifications in muscle microstructure and enhanced light scattering. This trend was consistent with the correlation analysis (Fig. 2), which showed a positive association between lightness and processing intensity. The 500 MPa 15-min treatment group exhibited the highest lightness, with a 36.95% increase compared to the control (p < 0.05). Neto et al. (Neto et al. 2015) reported that when 2.5-cm thick zebu beef M. longissimus dorsi cuts were treated at pressures of 100, 200, 300, and 400 MPa for 15 min, lightness increased by 0.35%, 21.66%, 35.97%, and 58.55%, respectively, which is similar to the results of this study. Lee et al. (Lee et al. 2021) reported that when ground beef M. longissimus thoracis cuts were treated at 500 MPa for 5 min, the lightness increased by 15.98% compared to the control, consistent with the results of this study. High pressure alters the physical structure of myoglobin protein and the heme porphyrin ring, increasing brightness and brownness and decreasing redness (Bak et al. 2019). This is because the pressure disrupts the tertiary structure of myofibrillar proteins, causing them to adopt unfolded conformations. This increases the surface hydrophobicity and reactive –SH group content, leading to enhanced light scattering and brightness (Liu et al. 2021). Redness was highest in the 300 MPa 15-min treatment group and showed a non-linear pattern during storage, decreasing after week 0 and partially recovering depending on treatment conditions, whereas 500 MPa treatments resulted in a continuous decline. An intermediate pressure of 300 MPa activates enzymes that reduce metmyoglobin, thereby decreasing its content, whereas a high pressure of 500 MPa inactivates the enzymatic reactions (Sun et al. 2019). NADH–cytochrome b5, a metmyoglobin-reducing enzyme present in the mitochondrial fraction, remains active even at low pressures and can influence metmyoglobin reduction (Denzer et al. 2023). Furthermore, NADH–cytochrome b5 is present in greater quantities in red muscle than in white muscle, and reduces metmyoglobin using the electron transfer mediators, OM cytochrome b and cytochrome b5 (Arihara et al. 1995). Yellowness increased at ≥ 300 MPa at week 0, decreased at week 1, and increased again at week 2, indicating a non-monotonic pattern. For example, the 300 MPa 15-min group decreased from 12.60 (week 0) to 9.33 (week 1), followed by an increase to 12.64 (week 2). This was attributed to the oxidation of Fe+ to Fe2+ ions at pressures above 400 MPa, which transformed into metmyoglobin and increased the brownness and yellowness (Ma et al. 2020). In particular, lipid oxidation generates aldehydes and ketones, while protein oxidation promotes carbonyl formation and aggregation, both of which alter light absorption and enhance yellowness. Consistent with this, correlation analysis showed a positive association between yellowness and processing intensity, reflecting the progressive influence of pressure-induced oxidation and structural changes. Overall, the concurrent increase in lightness and yellowness indicates a shift toward a paler and browner appearance under high-pressure conditions. These results demonstrate that color changes are governed by non-linear pressure–time interactions involving both structural light scattering and myoglobin redox chemistry. Maintaining color quality, therefore, requires balancing pressure conditions to preserve reducing capacity while limiting excessive protein denaturation.

TBARS and VBN

The lipid peroxidation levels in high-pressure-treated raw beef are shown in Table 3. TBARS values increased sequentially during storage, with the 500 MPa for 15 min treatment group exhibiting the highest TBARS values among all treatments (p < 0.05). This trend is consistent with the correlation analysis (Fig. 2), which showed a strong positive association between TBARS and processing intensity under moderate-to-high pressure conditions. Reddy et al. (Reddy et al. 2015) reported that TBARS values increased with pressure and time when raw goat leg meat was treated at 300 and 600 MPa for 5 and 10 min, respectively, consistent with the results of this study. These results indicate that lipid oxidation under high-pressure processing is strongly influenced by both processing intensity and subsequent storage duration. This is attributed to the destruction of the porphyrin structure of myoglobin by cell membrane rupture and protein denaturation caused by high-pressure processing, resulting in the generation of non-heme iron, which promotes lipid oxidation (Ayala et al. 2014). Non-heme iron reacts with H2O2 to generate hydroxyl radicals, which react with lipids to induce lipid peroxide oxidation and decomposition [62–63]. Furthermore, high-pressure treatment can reduce the physical size of lipids, increase the surface area exposed to oxygen, and potentially accelerate lipid oxidation (Palamae et al. 2024). At pressures below 200 MPa, it causes swelling of lipids within meat, but at the critical point, it condenses hydrocarbon chains, reduces the cross-sectional area, and disrupts the spherical structure of lipid globules (Brooks et al. 2011). Collectively, these pressure-induced structural changes enhance lipid susceptibility to radical-mediated oxidation rather than directly increasing lipid quantity or availability. As storage time increased, TBARS values sequentially increased across all treatment groups, peaking at week 2. Additionally, as the treatment pressure and time increased, the rate of increase in TBARS values during storage decreased. This storage-dependent increase is also reflected in the correlation analysis, indicating that lipid oxidation progresses cumulatively over time, even under conditions where microbial activity is reduced. Chmiel et al. (Chmiel et al. 2025) reported that when raw beef semimembranosus was treated at pressures of 300, 400, and 500 MPa for 5 min each, the TBARS values gradually increased with the storage duration, which is similar to the results of this study. This is attributed to the release of free Fe, which promotes oxidation through protein degradation caused by endogenous enzyme activity (Cunha et al. 2018). These findings suggest that lipid oxidation under high-pressure conditions follows a pressure-dependent oxidative trajectory that persists during storage, even when microbial activity is substantially suppressed. Overall, the strong positive correlation between TBARS and processing intensity indicates that lipid oxidation is one of the most pressure-sensitive quality attributes under high-pressure processing conditions.

The protein deterioration rate during the storage of high-pressure-treated raw beef is shown in Table 3. As treatment pressure and time increased, VBN values sequentially increased, whereas the 500 MPa 15-min treatment group showed significantly lower values than the control and other treatment groups (p < 0.05). This trend is consistent with the correlation analysis (Fig. 2), which showed a negative association between VBN and processing intensity under moderate-to-high pressure conditions. Kruk et al. (Kruk et al. 2011) reported that when raw chicken breast was treated at pressures of 300, 450, and 600 MPa for 5 min each, the VBN value significantly decreased as the treatment pressure increased, which is similar to the results of this study (p < 0.05). Lee et al. (Lee et al. 2023) found that when prosciutto was treated at pressures of 400, 500, and 600 MPa for 9 min each, the VBN value significantly decreased as the treatment pressure increased, which is similar to the results of this study (p < 0.05). These results demonstrate that protein deterioration is progressively suppressed as pressure intensity increases. This is attributed to the reduction in microorganisms caused by high-pressure processing, which inhibits protein degradation and leads to lower VBN values (Utama et al. 2018). This interpretation is supported by the microbial results (Fig. 1), which show that microbial growth was effectively suppressed under higher pressure–time conditions compared with the control. Although microbial counts increased during storage, no proportional increase in VBN was observed in some cases, indicating that VBN formation depends not only on microbial proliferation but also on proteolytic activity and the accumulation of degradation products. Furthermore, pressures of 300–400 MPa induce structural damage to proteolytic enzymes, such as trypsin, alcalase, cathepsins (B, D, H, and L), calpain, and serine proteinases. This damage reduces or inactivates enzymatic activity, thereby inhibiting protein degradation [70–71]. Pressures below 200 MPa weaken hydrophobic interactions in proteins, while at around 300 MPa, pressure-induced conformational changes may lead to either partial inactivation or transient activation of enzymes depending on the specific enzyme system and processing conditions (Zhang et al. 2012). Under higher pressure conditions, protein aggregation and conformational changes reduce overall enzymatic activity, thereby limiting proteolytic reactions. Thus, high-pressure processing suppresses protein degradation through a dual mechanism involving microbial reduction and enzymatic inactivation. As the storage duration increased, the VBN values of all treatment groups sequentially increased, reaching their highest values at week 2. Additionally, as the pressure and holding time increased, although initial VBN values decreased, the increase in VBN values during storage was greater in the high-pressure-treated groups. This storage-dependent increase is consistent with the correlation analysis, indicating that protein degradation progresses over time, although the extent of increase may vary depending on pressure conditions. This suggests that degradative enzymes, such as calpain and cathepsin, may continue to participate in protein degradation even after high-pressure treatment (Chen et al. 2022). The increase in VBN values caused by residual microbial degradation, along with pressure-induced myofibrillar fragmentation and increased surface hydrophobicity, may promotes protein degradation during storage (Chen et al. 2022). Nevertheless, the attenuated increase in VBN under higher pressure–time regimes indicates that proteolytic activity remains substantially constrained compared with lipid oxidative processes during storage.

Conclusion

HPP effectively enhanced the microbial safety of raw beef intended for raw pet food; however, its effects were strongly dependent on pressure–time conditions, indicating that processing intensity must be optimized rather than maximized. Although the 500 MPa for 15 min treatment showed the highest antimicrobial efficacy, it also induced undesirable physicochemical changes, including increased shear force, lipid oxidation, and color deterioration. In contrast, moderate conditions (e.g., 300 MPa for 15 min) achieved substantial microbial reduction while better preserving quality attributes, suggesting a practical processing window that balances safety and quality. From a pet food perspective, such a balance is critical because companion animals are sensitive not only to microbial hazards but also to palatability-related factors such as texture and appearance. Excessive protein denaturation and oxidative changes may negatively affect digestibility and feed acceptance. Therefore, optimal HPP conditions should be determined based on an integrated evaluation of microbial safety, physicochemical stability, and palatability, rather than maximal microbial inactivation alone. However, this study has limitations. Sensory evaluation was not performed, despite its importance for assessing practical applicability in pet food systems. Given the constraints associated with direct animal-based sensory testing, future studies should incorporate instrumental approaches such as electronic nose, electronic tongue, and gas chromatography-based volatile analysis to predict palatability. In addition, further research is required to evaluate nutrient digestibility and in vivo physiological responses, enabling the establishment of more biologically relevant and application-oriented HPP conditions.
