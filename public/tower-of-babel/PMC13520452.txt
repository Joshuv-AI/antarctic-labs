Integrating 3D skin lesion models with traditional 2D imagery in dermatology education: student perceptions within a VARK learning framework
Authors: Chayada Chaiyabutr, Teerapat Paringkarn, Rungnapa Thongyoo, Pornpan Panjai, Penvadee Pattanaprichakul, Kanchalit Thanomkitti, Silada Kanokrungsee, Pattriya Jirawattanadon, Pichaya Limphoka, Somchai Chongpipatchaipron, Suriya Lamsopa, Kasem Yimchai, Sumanas Bunyaratavej
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDermatology education relies heavily on visual learning, yet students exhibit diverse learning preferences categorized by the VARK model: Visual (V), Auditory (A), Reading/Writing (R), and Kinesthetic (K). Traditional teaching utilizing two-dimensional (2D) images primarily benefits visual learners whilst lacking tactile engagement. Three-dimensional (3D) skin lesion models address this limitation by incorporating kinesthetic learning, thereby enhancing students’ comprehension of lesion morphology and texture.ObjectivesTo evaluate student perceptions and preferences regarding the integration of 3D skin lesion models with 2D images in a station-based dermatology learning approach.ResultsMost students (90.2%) preferred the combined approach utilizing both modalities. 2D images received marginally higher ratings than 3D models for diagnostic skill development (7.3 ± 3.0 vs. 6.8 ± 3.0) and clinical application (7.2 ± 3.0 vs. 6.7 ± 3.1). The primary advantages of 3D models were hands-on practice (74.5%), enhanced palpation skills (57.8%), and interactive learning opportunities.ConclusionsThe integration of 3D models with 2D images was well accepted by students and supports a multimodal learning approach aligned with VARK learning preferences. These findings suggest that 3D models may serve as a useful complementary tool in dermatology education. Further studies are needed to evaluate their impact on objective learning outcomes and clinical performance in diverse educational settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09713-7.

Introduction

Dermatology is considered a crucial component of medical education, as skin diseases are common and inadequate training may lead to delayed diagnosis and mismanagement of serious conditions [1]. Furthermore, variability in undergraduate dermatology curricula highlights the need to improve and optimize dermatology education [1, 2]. A wide range of teaching approaches has been described in dermatology education, including didactic lectures, clinical immersion, large-scale patient-viewing sessions, computer-aided and online tools, interactive teaching, gamification and simulation-based approaches [3, 4]. However, a recent survey found that final-year medical students and junior doctors had low confidence across multiple dermatology competencies and expressed a need for additional dermatology training [5].

The study of skin lesions in medical students relies heavily on educational resources, as clinical exposure during rotations is often limited. However, these approaches remain largely visual, relying primarily on two-dimensional (2D) images from textbooks or electronic resources, with limited opportunities for tactile or experiential learning. In contrast, three-dimensional (3D) skin lesion models have gained increasing recognition as an alternative approach, offering distinct advantages and limitations [6, 7].

Two-dimensional images provide clear and detailed visual representations of dermatological conditions, allowing students to recognize lesion morphology effectively. However, they lack the ability to convey 3D characteristics such as elevation, lesion thickness and tactile properties [8]. In contrast, 3D models enable students to better understand the spatial and structural aspects of lesions and can serve as models for simple investigations such as Wood’s lamp examination. Accurate dermatologic assessment relies not only on visual recognition but also on physical examination, including the assessment of texture, consistency, and depth [9].

Various forms of 3D models have been developed in dermatology education. These include simulated lesions applied to standardized patients, as well as artificially constructed models that replicate dermatologic conditions using synthetic materials [7, 10, 11]. 3D models have also been applied in procedural education, such as training for Mohs surgery and in preparing patients for these procedures [12]. Digitally generated models created through three-dimensional scanning and printing technologies have been developed [6]. In addition to these physical models, other digital approaches, such as 3D imaging technologies, also enable interactive visualization of skin lesions from multiple angles [13].

Although model-based approaches have been increasingly explored in dermatology education to enhance learner engagement and provide more interactive experiences [8, 11, 14]. Nevertheless, evidence regarding their effectiveness remains limited, particularly in real-world undergraduate settings. Recognizing the need for a more comprehensive learning approach, our department is integrating 3D skin models into the curriculum. In this study, we incorporated 2D images and 3D skin lesion models into station-based learning sessions and assessed students’ perceptions and preferences regarding both educational modalities.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

All fifth-year medical students in the clinical phase of the six-year undergraduate medical education programme at Siriraj Hospital, Thailand, underwent a station-based learning session designed to enhance their skills in dermatological diagnosis, prescription and investigation. The session consisted of several stations, with each student allocated 3 min per station to complete the assigned tasks (Fig. 1). At each station, students were presented with both 2D images and 3D skin lesion models to aid in their clinical assessments. They were required to independently examine the provided materials and write their own responses, including a diagnosis, relevant investigations to confirm it and the most appropriate treatment plan. After completing all stations, the students participated in a group discussion facilitated by instructors, where they reviewed case analyses and discussed management approaches.

The 3D models were designed to be handled and manipulated, allowing students to inspect concealed areas, such as between the toes or on the scalp—areas that require physical separation of skin or hair to visualize properly. These practical examination skills are essential in clinical practice and cannot be fully simulated using 2D images alone.

Data collection and analysis

After the session, students completed a questionnaire assessing their satisfaction and preferences regarding the use of 2D images and 3D skin lesion models. Students rated each modality on a scale of 0 to 10, where 0 indicated “not useful at all” and 10 indicated “extremely useful.” These ratings reflect students’ perceived usefulness of each modality in supporting diagnostic skills and the application of knowledge in clinical scenarios. Prior to implementation, the questionnaire was validated using the Index of Item-Objective Congruence (IOC). Three staff involved in medical education reviewed the items to ensure clarity and the avoidance of leading responses. The English version of the questionnaire is provided in the supplementary section.

This retrospective study, which collected and analysed these data, was approved by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board (reference: SI 542/2025). Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). A P-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Development of 3D skin lesion models

The 3D skin lesion models were developed by the Siriraj Medical Education Technology Centre (Fig. 2). Three main types of 3D skin lesion models were created, representing 20 diverse skin conditions that align with the required competencies for Thai medical students, such as superficial fungal skin infection, wart, herpes zoster, vitiligo, psoriasis and erythema nodosum. Notably, some models were designed to depict more than one skin condition.

Three-dimensional flat/elevated lesion model

This model demonstrated flat or elevated lesions with colour variations, such as erythematous macules, papules, patches, plaques and vesicles. It was constructed from polyester resin, a rigid material used to simulate the skin surface. The base skin tone was achieved by mixing polyester resin with an opaque resin pigment, followed by detailed lesion painting using acrylic colours to ensure accurate representation of dermatological findings. In some cases, when lesions were painted over an existing surface, readily available mannequin heads or torsos, commonly used for wig and clothing displays, are utilized as a base and coated with acrylic paint.

Three-dimensional indurated plaque/subcutaneous nodule model

This model simulated indurated plaques and subcutaneous nodules beneath the skin surface. It was composed of 2 types of addition-cure silicone rubber with different hardness levels: a firmer silicone for the indurated plaque component and a softer silicone for the overlying skin, providing a realistic, skin-like texture. The base skin tone was created by incorporating silicone pigment into the silicone rubber, while additional detailing was achieved using the same pigment to enhance lesion realism.

Three-dimensional skin lesion model with fluorescent properties

A 3D skin lesion model with fluorescent properties was fabricated using condensation-cure silicone rubber, a soft and flexible material that mimics human skin texture. Acrylic colour and luminescent pigment were incorporated into the silicone mixture. The luminescent pigment, composed of strontium aluminate and high-grade epoxy resin, enabled the model to absorb light from fluorescent lamps and light-emitting diode (LED) lamps. It emitted fluorescence when exposed to ultraviolet light from a torch, making it a valuable tool comparable to a Wood’s lamp (Fig. 3).

Results

Participant demographics and overall ratings

A total of 204 fifth-year medical students responded to our questionnaire (response rate: 85.4%). Male students accounted for 51% of the respondents. When asked about their perceived diagnostic skills, students rated patient photographs (2D images) and simulated (3D skin lesion) models on a scale of 0–10. The mean score for 2D images was 7.3 ± 3.0, while the mean score for 3D skin lesion models was 6.8 ± 3.0. In terms of perceived ability to apply knowledge to real patient examinations, students rated 2D images and 3D skin lesion models based on the learning topics they had received. The mean score for 2D images was 7.2 ± 3.0, while the mean score for 3D skin lesion models was 6.7 ± 3.1. There was no statistically significant difference between the scores for 2D images and 3D skin models in terms of perceived diagnostic skills or perceived ability to apply knowledge to real patient examinations. Student perceptions of 3D model advantages

When asked about the advantages of learning with 3D models, students highlighted several key benefits. These included hands-on practice using medical tools (74.5%), increased awareness of body examinations or facilitation of coexisting lesion recognition (66.7%) and the opportunity to enhance physical examination skills through palpation (57.8%). The 3D nature of the models made the learning experience more engaging and interactive (59.8%). Some students noted that the realistic representations of lesions enhanced their learning, and they expressed a desire for additional time to further explore the models. Overall, the experience was well-received, and students appreciated the department’s efforts to provide such an immersive and valuable learning opportunity.

Student perceptions of 2D photograph advantages

The primary advantage of using patient 2D photographs for learning about skin lesions is their closer resemblance to real-life conditions (85.8%). This is particularly evident in terms of features such as colour accuracy and fidelity of lesion captured from photographs, scale and lesion size. Additionally, photographs allow for the display of a greater variety and number of dermatological diseases (69.1%), especially those that may not be represented in 3D skin models. They also improve the detection of concurrent lesions, such as those appearing on both the body and nails, leading to a more comprehensive assessment (65.7%). Furthermore, photographs are easier to review and access for study (60.8%).

Preferred learning methods

Students were asked which learning method they found most beneficial for studying skin lesions. The options included learning exclusively from patient photographs, learning exclusively from simulated models, or using both methods. The majority of students preferred a combination of both patient photographs and simulated models (90.2%), followed by patient photographs only (8.3%) and simulated models only (1.5%). These findings highlight the value of integrating both visual and hands-on learning approaches in dermatological education.

Discussion

Medical education continuously evolves to improve student engagement and learning outcomes. Dermatology, as a field reliant on visual recognition and tactile assessment, presents unique challenges in medical training. Traditional teaching methods, which have relied heavily on 2D images, may not fully address the diverse learning preferences or learning styles of all students.

Learning style refers to an individual’s preferred approach and methods for studying, reflecting how they tend to perceive and process information rather than their actual learning skill. Excellent medical education should incorporate various learning styles, categorized by the VARK model. The VARK model categorizes learning preferences into Visual (V), Auditory (A), Reading/Writing (R) and Kinesthetic (K) styles [15]. In dermatology, where accurate diagnosis depends heavily on pattern recognition and tactile examination—as well as the exploration and exposure of hidden skin lesions—integrating multiple learning styles can significantly enhance educational outcomes. Moreover, previous study in medical students have shown that most prefer multimodal learning styles. Clinical-year students tend to favor multimodal or quadmodal approaches compared with preclinical students, whereas those with lower academic performance more often adopt unimodal strategies than higher-achieving peers [16]. These findings support incorporating multimodal approaches into dermatology teaching.

Dermatology education has been predominantly visual. While effective for visual learners, this approach lacks kinesthetic engagement, which is crucial for developing hands-on diagnostic skills. The introduction of 3D skin lesion models addresses this limitation by incorporating kinesthetic learning into dermatology training. Three-dimensional models provide a tangible, interactive experience that allows students to palpate and examine lesion morphology, texture and depth. They can also be manipulated to reveal hidden lesions—skills that are crucial for real-life clinical practice (Fig. 4). Importantly, hands-on experience using medical tools (74.5%) is what students find most valuable about 3D models. However, these findings are based on self-reported perceptions and may be subject to acquiescence bias, which could affect the validity of the findings.

Additionally, the station-based learning format we designed requires students to write responses individually, further reinforcing the reading/writing component of the VARK model. This structured approach ensures active participation and deeper engagement with the learning material. After completing their individual responses, students engage in group discussions led by instructors, where they listen to explanations and feedback, integrating the auditory component of the VARK into their learning process. These models were perceived to provide a more comprehensive understanding.

There have been some previous studies of using 3D skin lesion models in dermatology education. Grag et al. evaluated the effectiveness of 3D silicone-based prosthetic mimics in dermatology education at the University of Massachusetts Medical School, comparing them with 2D image-based learning. Students were assessed on morphology, lesion and rash recognition, and management at three time points: baseline, immediately after the intervention, and 3 months later. The 3D group demonstrated significantly better immediate and long-term retention, particularly in lesion recognition [10]. Frommherz et al. evaluated silicone-based 3D skin models as a dermatology teaching tool, gathering feedback from 58 dermatologists at the Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich. Unlike studies focusing solely on student satisfaction, this study assessed dermatologist perspectives on model quality and educational value. Most participants rated the models positively and supported their integration into medical education [14]. A recent systematic review examined moulage interventions in simulation-based learning across 20 studies, including 3 in dermatology. The review found that these learning resulted in higher learner satisfaction and confidence, although only slight improvements in knowledge and performance were observed [17].

Wahlgren et al. from Sweden developed a computerized interactive case simulation system using real patient cases, images and healthcare provider actors. Students select a patient, ask medical history questions, examine the skin, and propose diagnoses and management. Feedback is provided by comparing their suggestions with a specialist’s. Students highly valued this computerized simulation system, although it did not lead to a significant improvement in their final written exam performance [18].

A study using moulage techniques in undergraduate dermatology objective structured clinical examinations (OSCEs) demonstrated that simulated lesions applied to volunteers were clearly recognizable, with acceptable difficulty and discrimination indices comparable to conventional stations. These findings support the role of simulation in standardizing dermatologic education. However, these moulage techniques typically require repeated preparation for each session, whereas 3D skin lesion models are reusable and may facilitate more consistent and efficient teaching across sessions [19].

Our results indicate that, although most students recognized the benefits of both clinical photographs of patients and simulated models, learning with photographs of patients received higher scores. This reflects the crucial role of visual representation in dermatological education, as photographs provide a more accurate depiction of real-life conditions. Meanwhile, the lower preference for simulated models alone underscores their limitations in replicating the complexity and variability of actual skin lesions. However, the overwhelming preference for a combination of both methods suggests that simulated models serve as a valuable supplement rather than as a primary tool for learning. Therefore, integrating both approaches may support learning, but the use of patient photographs remains the cornerstone of dermatological training.

One strength of the study is the use of models that match our population’s skin colour, ensuring representation that aligns with our demographic and addresses its underrepresentation in standard dermatology study materials [20]. A curriculum audit of the undergraduate dermatology program at the University of Toronto showed that fewer than 7% of images of skin disease represented skin of color, contributing to low diagnostic confidence in this group [21]. Given that textbooks from abroad often have limited images representing our skin tones, this adaptation is particularly relevant for our medical education context [22, 23]. Furthermore, designing the learning experience in a quiz-like format enhances motivational competition, encouraging students to stay engaged and perform at their best [24].

Our study was conducted at a single institution, limiting its generalizability to other medical schools with different curricula or resources. Additionally, it focused on student preferences without assessing short-term knowledge acquisition or long-term clinical impact. As all students in the current academic year received the same learning approach, no comparator group was available to evaluate differences in knowledge gained or exam performance. According to the Kirkpatrick Evaluation Model, educational interventions can be evaluated across multiple levels, from learner satisfaction to patient outcomes [25]. Our study primarily reflects Level 1 outcomes (learner perception and acceptance) rather than objective learning outcomes; therefore, conclusions regarding educational effectiveness or clinical skill development cannot be drawn. Further research is needed to determine whether integrating 3D models improves objective learning outcomes and clinical impact, including diagnostic accuracy and patient management. Furthermore, the quantitative design and use of structured response options may have limited the depth of student perspectives. A qualitative approach could provide further insights into the perceived advantages and disadvantages of each modality. Finally, the development and implementation of 3D skin lesion models may involve additional costs (approximately 1,340–17,850 THB per model), largely depending on the size and materials used. However, these costs are generally manageable within institutional settings, and the models are reusable.

In conclusion, by combining 3D models with traditional visual materials, incorporating written responses, and facilitating discussions, was well accepted by students and supports a multimodal learning approach aligned with the VARK model.
