Reinforcement Learning for Robust Calibration of Multi-Qudit Quantum Gates
Amine Jaouadi, Sahel Ashhab

Abstract
Higher-dimensional quantum systems, such as qudits, offer architectural and algorithmic advantages over qubits, but their increased spectral crowding and limited controllability render high-fidelity quantum gates particularly challenging. We propose a hybrid optimization framework that integrates optimal control theory methods with contextual deep reinforcement learning to achieve robust controlled-phase gates on two qutrits. Optimal control is first used to design high-fidelity control pulses for a nominal system model. Reinforcement learning is then employed as a calibration stage that learns small residual corrections to these pulses in the presence of static model mismatch, thereby preserving good gate performance under realistic parameter uncertainties. By learning structured, low-dimensional residual corrections conditioned on device-specific parameter variations, reinforcement learning enhances the transfer robustness of nominally optimal but parameter-sensitive control solutions across ensembles of devices. Crucially, the reinforcement learning step in our framework does not compete with the optimal control step but provides the adaptability required for realistic hardware, systematically reducing the sensitivity to parameter fluctuations. Our results establish reinforcement learning as a practical and scalable ingredient for robust calibration of quantum gates in high-dimensional systems.

. It should be noted that other superconducting qubit designs, such as the capacitively-shunted flux qubit, also allow access to higher energy levels

Recent experiments have demonstrated high-fidelity single-qutrit control, randomized benchmarking, and two-qutrit entangling gates in transmon devices

;

. However, the performance of OCT solutions is fundamentally tied to model accuracy: deviations of the true device Hamiltonian from the nominal design, whether from fabrication spread or temporal drift, can degrade gate fidelity

;

.

In parallel, deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has shown promise as a largely

;
Niu2019
;

;

, Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3)

, Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)
Lillicrap2015
, and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
Schulman2017

when applied directly to high-dimensional pulse synthesis. In particular, the action space in quantum control scales with the number of pulse segments, often reaching hundreds or thousands of degrees of freedom. Recent studies

report that standard DRL agents fail to discover high-fidelity pulses when learning from scratch in such high-dimensional spaces, stalling at low rewards and exhibiting unstable training unless strong priors or demonstrations are provided. This limitation becomes particularly clear for multi-qutrit systems, where leakage channels and spectral crowding vastly enlarge the control landscape. Complementary insights into the control of multi-level superconducting
circuits have recently been provided in
Basyildiz2025
, in which the authors analyzed fundamental speed limits for a two-qutrit controlled-phase
gate implemented with superconducting qudits.
Using gradient-based optimal control to construct time-optimal protocols
together with analytical quantum speed limit arguments. They showed that
even for idealized models the achievable gate performance is constrained
by the underlying Hamiltonian structure and control resources.
On the other hand, these results emphasize that, on a nominal device, gradient-based OCT
can already saturate the accessible control landscape for two-qutrit
gates, leaving little room for improvement by unguided learning-based
approaches.
These observations suggest that RL is unlikely to replace
OCT for pulse synthesis on nominal, well-characterized
devices, but may instead play a complementary role in addressing model mismatch
and calibration challenges. From a practical perspective, repeated re-optimization of OCT pulses for each
device instance or parameter drift can be computationally demanding and difficult
to scale. This motivates approaches in which a high-quality nominal pulse is
computed once, and subsequent corrections are obtained through lightweight
calibration procedures rather than repeated high-dimensional optimization.

Motivated by these challenges, we introduce a hybrid OCT+DRL framework in which
OCT and DRL play complementary roles: OCT provides
a nominal high-fidelity open-loop pulse, while DRL acts as a calibration stage that
learns residual corrections to mitigate fidelity loss caused by static model
mismatch.
The novelty of our contribution can be summarized as follows:

•

DRL on top of OCT pulses.
Rather than asking DRL to discover an optimal pulse in a high-dimensional space (a task known to be prohibitive
Li2025_RLfD
), we compute a pair of nominal GRAPE pulses, computed once on the nominal Hamiltonian for a two-qutrit
\mathrm{CZ}_{3}
gate and train DRL agents to output
low-dimensional, smooth residual corrections
. This approach bypasses the failure mode of DRL in large action spaces.

•

Contextual bandit formulation of device-aware calibration.
Each episode corresponds to a different noisy device instance, and the observation encodes normalized parameter deviations. The reward is the
incremental fidelity gain over the OCT baseline
, forcing the agent to learn robustness rather than re-learn the gate.

•

Cosine-basis parametrization.
Residual corrections are parametrized in a truncated discrete cosine basis, drastically reducing action dimensionality and enforcing smoothness.

•

Comparison of multiple DRL algorithms.
SAC, TD3, DDPG, and PPO are evaluated under identical conditions. Importantly,
none
of the agents outperform OCT on the nominal Hamiltonian—consistent with the failure of DRL in high-dimensional unconstrained optimization
Li2025_RLfD
. This observation further supports our interpretation of DRL as a calibration (i.e. fine-tuning) tool rather than a replacement for gradient-based pulse design.

II
Two-qutrit model, target gate, and fidelity

II.1
Effective two-qutrit Hamiltonian

We consider two coupled transmon-like qutrits with annihilation operators
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
, truncated to the lowest three energy levels. The effective Hamiltonian in the lab frame reads

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle=H_{0}(\bm{\lambda})+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\epsilon_{i}(t)H_{c,i},

(1)

with

\displaystyle H_{0}(\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\omega_{i}n_{i}+\chi_{i}a_{i}^{\dagger 2}a_{i}^{2}\right)+g\left(a_{1}+a_{1}^{\dagger}\right)\left(a_{2}+a_{2}^{\dagger}\right),

(2)

\displaystyle H_{c,1}

\displaystyle=a_{1}+a_{1}^{\dagger},\qquad H_{c,2}=a_{2}+a_{2}^{\dagger},

(3)

where
n_{i}=a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}
,
\bm{\lambda}=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\chi_{1},\chi_{2},g)
collects the device parameters, and
\epsilon_{i}(t)
denote the classical drive envelopes.

In the numerical implementation, we represent
a_{i}
as ladder operators acting on a three-dimensional Hilbert space. Time evolution is computed using piecewise-constant controls over
N
time slices of duration
\Delta t
,

\epsilon_{i}(t)=\epsilon_{i}^{(j)},\qquad t\in[j\Delta t,(j+1)\Delta t),

(4)

leading to the propagator

U[\bm{\epsilon};\bm{\lambda}]=\prod_{j=N-1}^{0}\exp\!\left[-iH^{(j)}(\bm{\lambda})\Delta t\right],

(5)

with
H^{(j)}(\bm{\lambda})=H_{0}(\bm{\lambda})+\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{(j)}H_{c,i}
.

II.2
CZ
3
gate

We target a two-qutrit controlled-phase gate
\mathrm{CZ}_{3}
acting on the computational basis

\{\ket{00},\ket{01},\ket{02},\ket{10},\dots,\ket{22}\}
as

U_{\mathrm{CZ}_{3}}=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1,\,1,e^{2\pi i/3},e^{-2\pi i/3},\,1,e^{-2\pi i/3},e^{2\pi i/3}),

(6)

where the diagonal is written in lexicographic order of the two-qutrit basis. This gate is universal for ternary computation when combined with suitable local operations
Bullock2005
;
Subramanian2023
and closely related to the two-qutrit entangling gates realized experimentally in superconducting circuits
Goss2022
;
Roy2023
.

II.3
Average gate fidelity

We quantify performance using the average gate fidelity between the realized unitary
U
and the target
U_{\mathrm{CZ}_{3}}
,

F_{\mathrm{avg}}(U,U_{\mathrm{CZ}_{3}})=\frac{\left|\Tr\!\left(U_{\mathrm{CZ}_{3}}^{\dagger}U\right)\right|^{2}+d^{2}}{d^{2}(d^{2}+1)},

(7)

This expression is derived from the general formula for the average gate fidelity of a unitary channel given in Ref
Nielsen2002
, specialized here to the case of a d-dimensional unitary operation.
In our simulations,
U
is obtained either by propagating with OCT-only pulses or with OCT+RL-corrected pulses on a specified device instance.

III
Nominal Device GRAPE optimization

We first design nominal control pulses
(\epsilon_{1}^{\mathrm{OCT}},\epsilon_{2}^{\mathrm{OCT}})
using GRAPE
Khaneja2005
as implemented in the QuTiP
pulseoptim
module
Johansson2013
. The drift Hamiltonian is defined at a nominal parameter point
\bm{\lambda}_{0}=(\omega_{1,0},\omega_{2,0},\chi_{1,0},\chi_{2,0},g_{0})
, selected to reflect typical parameters of a two-transmon device.

A useful reference timescale for entangling gates in superconducting circuits is obtained by truncating the system to two levels per qubit. In this case, the minimum time required to implement a CNOT or CZ gate under a coupling of strength
g
is

T_{0}=\frac{\pi}{4g}.

(8)

More generally, quantum speed limits for two-body entangling gates scale as
T_{\min}\sim 1/g
, as discussed in Ref
Basyildiz2025
, with comparable prefactors for qubit and qutrit CZ gates. Accordingly, the characteristic timescale for implementing a two-qutrit CZ gate remains of the same order as in the qubit case, i.e.,

T_{\min}^{(2\text{-qutrit})}\sim T_{0}

(9)

In our simulations, we use
g=0.0025
, for which

T_{0}\approx 314
and
T_{\min}^{(2\text{-qutrit})}\approx 628
.
We observe that GRAPE optimizations fail to reach high fidelity for

T
below this threshold. In the remainder of this paper, we set the pulse time at
T=1600
, which places the system
well above the minimum-time regime and allows reliable convergence to
unit fidelity.

The GRAPE cost function is the process infidelity
1-F_{\mathrm{proc}}
, where
F_{\mathrm{proc}}
is the average gate fidelity. The optimizer terminates when either the target infidelity threshold (here
\lesssim 10^{-10}
) is reached or the fixed iteration limit is exceeded.

IV
Noise model and residual reinforcement learning

IV.1
Device parameter noise

In superconducting quantum processors, qubit transition frequencies and effective interaction parameters are not perfectly stationary and may vary due to calibration errors, slow drift, in addition to the uncertainty from calibration errors and imperfect Hamiltonian modeling. Short-time fluctuations of qubit frequencies are typically on the order of a few kHz to a few tens of kHz
Agarwal2025
;
Burnett2019
. In the absence of precise experimental characterization of temporal fluctuations in inter-qutrit coupling strengths in the literature, we adopt the reasonable assumption that variations in both frequencies and coupling strengths occur at a comparable kHz scale. This choice should be understood as an order-of-magnitude estimate rather than a precise description of a specific experimental platform. We also explore larger values for broader applicability.
The parameters entering the control model are inferred from calibration measurements and are therefore subject to estimation uncertainty. The achievable accuracy is limited by measurement noise and finite coherence times (
T_{1},T_{2}\sim 100~\mu\mathrm{s}
to
1~\mathrm{ms}
), which constrain the precision of the extracted Hamiltonian parameters to the kHz level. The mismatch distributions used in our simulations therefore capture a representative regime in which small deviations from the nominal model can affect gate performance and motivate the use of adaptive calibration strategies.

To model fabrication variability and slow drift, we introduce quasi-static fluctuations in the qubit frequencies and the inter-qubit coupling,
\omega_{1}=\omega_{1,0}+\delta\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}=\omega_{2,0}+\delta\omega_{2}
and
g=g_{0}+\delta g
. Where the offsets are drawn from Gaussian distributions,

\delta\omega_{1},\delta\omega_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\omega}^{2})
and

\delta g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{g}^{2})
.
The widths
\sigma_{\omega}=10^{-3}
and
\sigma_{g}=5\times 10^{-5}
(in dimensionless units) are chosen to represent moderate but experimentally relevant parameter variations in superconducting devices. At this noise level, the nominal OCT pulse optimized for the ideal Hamiltonian exhibits a noticeable reduction in fidelity (to approximately
0.92
) on a typical noisy realization, signaling the onset of significant performance degradation due to model mismatch. The anharmonicities
\chi_{i}
are kept fixed at their nominal values for simplicity, although the framework readily extends to fluctuations in
\chi_{i}
.

IV.2
Cosine-basis residual parametrization

The DRL agent does not directly manipulate the raw time-discretized pulses. Instead, it updates the control pulses indirectly by outputting a set of coefficients
\bm{c}_{i}
that parametrize smooth residual corrections in a discrete cosine basis:

\Delta\epsilon_{i}^{(j)}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{i,k}\,\cos\!\left[\frac{\pi k(j+\tfrac{1}{2})}{N}\right],

(8)

for
i=1,2
, where
N
is the number of time slices and
K\ll N
is the number of retained modes. Denoting by
C\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}
the basis matrix with elements

C_{jk}=\cos\!\left[\frac{\pi k(j+\tfrac{1}{2})}{N}\right],

(9)

we can write

\bm{\Delta\epsilon}_{i}=C\,\bm{c}_{i},

(10)

where
\bm{\Delta\epsilon}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the residual waveform and
\bm{c}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
the coefficient vector. Each column of
C
is normalized so that
\norm{C_{:k}}_{2}=1
, ensuring that the overall residual amplitude is controlled by the coefficient scale.

The total pulse used for propagation is

\epsilon_{i}^{\mathrm{tot},(j)}=\epsilon_{i}^{\mathrm{OCT},(j)}+\Delta\epsilon_{i}^{(j)},

(11)

followed by clipping to the original amplitude bounds

|\epsilon_{i}^{\mathrm{tot},(j)}|\leq 0.3
. In practice we use
K=20
modes per drive, leading to an action dimension of
2K=40
, significantly smaller than the original
N=160
piecewise-constant time segments used to parametrize each OCT control pulse.

IV.3
Contextual bandit environment

We formulate the calibration problem as a one-step contextual bandit. Each
episode
proceeds as follows:

1.

Sample a device instance by drawing
(\delta\omega_{1},\delta\omega_{2},\delta g)
from the noise distribution.

2.

Construct the effective Hamiltonian
H_{0}(\bm{\lambda})
with
\bm{\lambda}=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\chi_{1},\chi_{2},g)
.

3.

Compute the OCT baseline fidelity for this device,

F_{\mathrm{OCT}}(\delta\omega_{1},\delta\omega_{2},\delta g)=F_{\mathrm{avg}}\big(U[\bm{\epsilon}^{\mathrm{OCT}};\bm{\lambda}],\,U_{\mathrm{CZ}_{3}}\big).

(12)

4.

Provide the agent with a normalized context vector

\bm{o}=\left(\frac{\delta\omega_{1}}{\sigma_{\omega}},\frac{\delta\omega_{2}}{\sigma_{\omega}},\frac{\delta g}{\sigma_{g}}\right),

(13)

which lies in a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

5.

The agent outputs an action
\bm{a}\in[-1,1]^{2K}
, corresponding to scaled cosine coefficients for the two drives:

\bm{c}_{i}=\alpha\,\bm{a}_{i},\qquad i=1,2,

(14)

with a global coefficient scale
\alpha=0.03
.

6.

Build residual pulses via Eq. (
8
), form the total pulses
\bm{\epsilon}^{\mathrm{tot}}
, propagate the system, and compute

F_{\mathrm{RL}}(\delta\omega_{1},\delta\omega_{2},\delta g)=F_{\mathrm{avg}}\big(U[\bm{\epsilon}^{\mathrm{tot}};\bm{\lambda}],\,U_{\mathrm{CZ}_{3}}\big).

(15)

7.

Return a scalar reward

r=F_{\mathrm{RL}}-F_{\mathrm{OCT}},

(16)

and terminate the episode.

By construction,
r>0
if and only if the RL-corrected pulses outperform the OCT baseline on that particular device instance. This reward shaping makes the learning problem explicitly
residual
: the agent is incentivized to discover corrections that enhance robustness rather than to reproduce the gate from scratch.

IV.4
Observation and action spaces

The observation provided to the reinforcement-learning agent consists of
a three-dimensional real-valued vector,

\mathcal{O}=\{\bm{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|o_{i}|\leq 3\},

(17)

which encodes the normalized static parameter offsets

(\delta\omega_{1},\delta\omega_{2},\delta g)
of the device. The bounds are
chosen to safely cover the range of fluctuations considered in the noise
model and ensure well-scaled inputs during training.

The action produced by the agent is a
2K
-dimensional vector,

\mathcal{A}=[-1,1]^{2K},

(18)

whose components parameterize the cosine-basis residual corrections for
the two control drives. These normalized actions are mapped linearly to
the corresponding residual coefficients, providing a bounded and
well-conditioned control interface.

Together, these choices define a low-dimensional, continuous
observation-action mapping in which the agent learns to associate
device-specific imperfections with structured pulse corrections.

V
Deep reinforcement learning algorithms

V.1
Algorithms and hyperparameters

Figure
2
summarizes the reinforcement-learning
workflow used in this work. At each training step, a noisy device
instance is generated by sampling static parameter offsets

(\delta\omega_{1},\delta\omega_{2},\delta g)
from the prescribed Gaussian
distributions. These offsets constitute the contextual observation
provided to the agent. These offsets should be interpreted as coarse parameter estimates available
from standard device characterization routines, rather than privileged access to the full Hamiltonian.
Conditioned on this context, the agent outputs a
low-dimensional set of continuous coefficients that parametrize smooth
residual corrections added to the OCT baseline pulses, rather than redesigning the control pulse from scratch. The corrected
pulses are applied to the device Hamiltonian, the resulting gate
fidelity is evaluated, and a scalar reward—defined as the incremental
fidelity gain relative to the OCT pulse on the same device—is returned
to the agent. Since each episode consists of a single device evaluation,
the learning task naturally takes the form of a contextual bandit.

Figure 2
:

Reinforcement-learning workflow for residual pulse optimization.
Device-specific parameter offsets are provided as contextual input
to the agent, which outputs low-dimensional coefficients defining
smooth residual corrections to the OCT baseline pulses. The corrected
pulses are evaluated on the noisy device, and the resulting
incremental fidelity improvement relative to OCT defines the reward.

We compare four deep reinforcement-learning algorithms for continuous
control: Soft Actor–Critic (SAC)
Haarnoja2018
, Twin-Delayed Deep
Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3)
Fujimoto2018
, Deep
Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)
Lillicrap2015
, and Proximal
Policy Optimization (PPO)
Schulman2017
.
We consider these four algorithms as they constitute a representative and widely adopted set of deep reinforcement-learning methods for continuous control. Together, they span complementary design choices, including on-policy versus off-policy learning (PPO versus SAC, TD3, and DDPG), deterministic versus stochastic policies, and different strategies for stabilizing training in continuous action spaces. Such comparative evaluations across multiple standard algorithms are common practice in the reinforcement-learning literature, as no single method is known to be universally optimal across tasks.

We consider these four representative RL algorithms belonging to different methodological families in order to verify that the observed behavior does not depend on the specific choice of learning algorithm.

All agents employ identical multilayer perceptrons for the policy and
value networks, with two hidden layers of 256 units and ReLU activations.
A learning rate
\eta=3\times 10^{-4}
and discount factor

\gamma=0.99
are used throughout.
These chosen hyperparameters follow standard settings widely used in continuous-control benchmarks. In particular, the learning rate
\eta
and the discount factor
\gamma

provide a good balance between training stability and convergence speed. The network architecture (two hidden layers of 256 units) is sufficient to represent the low-dimensional policy required for the cosine-basis parametrization while avoiding overparameterization. We verified that moderate variations of these hyperparameters do not qualitatively affect the results, indicating that the observed performance is consistent with respect to their precise values.

For off-policy methods (SAC, TD3,
DDPG), target networks are updated using a soft update with coefficient

\tau=0.005
. Exploration in SAC, TD3, and DDPG is implemented via
Gaussian action noise, while PPO relies on a stochastic policy with
clipped policy updates.

Each algorithm is trained for
T_{\mathrm{train}}=10^{5}
independent environment interactions,
with each interaction corresponding to a single sampled noisy device and a single pulse evaluation.
This training budget is sufficient to ensure convergence of the learning curves while remaining computationally tractable. Once training is completed offline, adapting the control pulse to a new
device requires only a single forward pass of the trained policy network,
making the computational cost of this approach negligible compared to
re-running OCT.

V.2
Logging and evaluation

During training, we log the gate fidelity
F_{\mathrm{RL}}
obtained at
the end of each episode. Since each episode corresponds to a single device
evaluation, this yields a sequence of fidelities indexed by training
iterations. From these data, we compute the per-episode fidelity and the
running best fidelity.

After training, each agent is evaluated in three distinct settings:

1.

Nominal device:
zero parameter offsets,

(\delta\omega_{1},\delta\omega_{2},\delta g)=(0,0,0)
;

2.

Single static-noise device:
a randomly selected triplet

(\delta\omega_{1},\delta\omega_{2},\delta g)
held fixed across all
methods;

3.

Ensemble of noisy devices:

M=100
independently sampled
devices drawn from the same noise distribution used during training.

For each setting, we compute the average gate fidelity, and for the
ensemble case we additionally report the standard deviation across
devices. These metrics allow for a direct and quantitative comparison between the
transfer performance of the nominal OCT pulse and its improvement through
context-conditioned RL corrections.

VI
Numerical results

VI.1
Nominal Device

In the simulations reported here, the GRAPE optimization was performed
in the full two-qutrit Hilbert space of dimension
d=9
, using a total
gate duration
T=1600
.
We find that for this duration, the optimizer reliably converges to
near-unit process fidelity, as shown in Fig.
3
.
By contrast, for shorter total times (
T\lesssim 628
in our units),
the optimization systematically fails to reach high fidelity, even
when increasing the number of iterations or varying the initial
guess pulses. This behavior is consistent with the presence of a
minimum gate time imposed by the coupled-transmon Hamiltonian. Such a minimum-time threshold is not an artifact of the numerical
optimization but reflects a fundamental constraint on two-qutrit
entangling gates. Indeed, in a recent study, the authors have
analyzed quantum speed limits for two-qutrit controlled-phase gates
using both optimal-control constructions and analytical bounds,
showing that high-fidelity implementations are only possible above
a system-dependent minimum duration
Basyildiz2025
.
Our choice of
T=1600
is therefore well above the minimum gate time set by speed-limit regime, ensuring that the nominal OCT solution
saturates the accessible control landscape.

We will use these nominal pulses as a baseline against which both noisy-device performance and RL-corrected pulses are compared.

For the nominal device, Figure
3
shows the convergence of the process fidelity as a function of iteration number, illustrating the rapid ascent to near-unity fidelity within a few hundred iterations. From the perspective of control theory, this demonstrates that the GRAPE solution essentially saturates the reachable optimum on the nominal Hamiltonian.

Figure 3
:
Convergence of the GRAPE optimization on the nominal two-qutrit Hamiltonian. Shown is the process fidelity
F_{\mathrm{proc}}
versus iteration number, approaching unity with a final infidelity of
8.1\times 10^{-8}
. The GRAPE solution effectively saturates the control landscape for the nominal model.

On the nominal device, where the system Hamiltonian is perfectly known and free from noise or parameter uncertainty, OCT provides an upper bound on the achievable gate fidelity. As shown in Fig.
3
, the OCT-optimized control pulse achieves a final process infidelity of
(1-F_{\mathrm{proc,nom}}^{\mathrm{OCT}})=8.147\times 10^{-8}

and remains constant throughout the optimization horizon. This behavior reflects the deterministic nature of OCT, which exploits full model knowledge and analytic gradients to converge directly toward a globally optimal solution.
Because the nominal device exactly matches the assumptions used in the OCT optimization, there is no model mismatch, stochasticity, or partial observability. As a result, OCT efficiently suppresses coherent control errors, including unwanted population transfer within the two-qutrit Hilbert space and residual phase errors, yielding a near-perfect implementation of the target CZ gate. The OCT result therefore serves as a theoretical benchmark against which learning-based approaches can be quantitatively compared.

In contrast, DRL methods display qualitatively different behavior on the same nominal device. Figure
4
shows the learning curves for DDPG, TD3, SAC, and PPO. All agents exhibit a rapid initial improvement in fidelity during early training, followed by a pronounced plateau at moderate fidelity values (
\approx 0.4-0.48)
, well below the near-unit fidelity achieved by OCT.

. SAC converges to a slightly lower value, while PPO displays the slowest learning dynamics and the lowest final fidelity. Despite extended training up to

iterations, none of the RL methods approach the OCT benchmark.

This performance gap is intrinsic to the RL formulation: the agent must infer optimal control pulses solely from scalar reward signals derived from fidelity evaluations, without access to analytic gradients or exact dynamical information. Even on a nominal device, the high-dimensional continuous action space and the sensitivity of quantum dynamics significantly limit the achievable fidelity within feasible training times.

These preliminary results indicate that, on nominal devices, RL should not be viewed as a replacement for OCT, but rather as an alternative framework whose strengths lie in scenarios involving model uncertainty, calibration errors, or drift. In such non-ideal regimes, where OCT performance degrades due to model mismatch, RL approaches may close the gap and potentially outperform model-based control strategies. On the nominal device, however, OCT remains the gold standard for high-fidelity quantum gate synthesis.

The plateau near a fidelity of approximately
0.5
reflects the fact that the RL agents converge to pulse policies that produce structured but incorrect unitary operations within the computational subspace. In other words, the learned pulses generate dynamics that are nontrivial but do not realize the desired entangling transformation.
This behavior illustrates the difficulty of discovering high-fidelity solutions in the high-dimensional continuous pulse space using model-free reinforcement learning alone.
While RL has been successfully applied to two-qubit gate design, including recent demonstrations on superconducting transmon systems
Nguyen2024RLTransmon
, these results are obtained in carefully designed control settings. Applying fully model-free RL directly to high-dimensional, time-discretized control fields remains challenging. This difficulty is further observed in the present setting, which involves a two-qutrit system rather than a two-qubit system. Furthermore, although the increase in system size from two qubits to two qutrits may appear modest, the Hilbert-space dimension grows from four to nine and the number of dynamical constraints increases accordingly, making the control landscape significantly more challenging to explore for model-free RL.

VI.5
Robustness to imperfect parameter estimation.

In realistic experimental settings, the device parameters entering the control model are not known exactly. They are inferred from calibration measurements and therefore contain statistical uncertainty, with the achievable estimation accuracy limited by measurement noise and finite coherence times (
T_{1},T_{2}
), which constrain the precision of parameter estimation. To assess the impact of such imperfections, we model the contextual inputs provided to the RL agent as noisy estimates of the underlying parameter deviations. The agent observes a vector of normalized offsets

o=\left(\delta\omega_{1}/\sigma_{\omega},\ \delta\omega_{2}/\sigma_{\omega},\ \delta g/\sigma_{g}\right),

which is replaced by a noisy estimate

\tilde{o}=\left(\frac{\delta\omega_{1}+\epsilon_{\omega_{1}}}{\sigma_{\omega}},\frac{\delta\omega_{2}+\epsilon_{\omega_{2}}}{\sigma_{\omega}},\frac{\delta g+\epsilon_{g}}{\sigma_{g}}\right),

where the estimation errors are modeled as independent Gaussian variables,

\epsilon_{\omega_{1}},\epsilon_{\omega_{2}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\eta_{\omega}^{2}),\qquad\epsilon_{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\eta_{g}^{2}).

We emphasize that two distinct sources of variability are considered in this model. The quantities
\delta\omega_{1}
,
\delta\omega_{2}
, and
\delta g
represent the true physical mismatch between the device and the nominal model, with characteristic scales set by
\sigma_{\omega}
and
\sigma_{g}
. In contrast, the quantities
\epsilon_{\omega_{1}}
,
\epsilon_{\omega_{2}}
, and
\epsilon_{g}
represent errors in the estimation of these parameter deviations, arising from the finite accuracy of calibration procedures. These estimation errors affect only the information available to the RL agent, while the system dynamics are always governed by the true parameter deviations.
Importantly, the system dynamics are always computed using the true parameter deviations, while only the observations provided to the RL agent are noisy. This setup reflects the realistic situation in which control updates rely on imperfect calibration data.
The estimation noise is characterized by the dimensionless ratios
\eta_{\omega}/\sigma_{\omega}
and
\eta_{g}/\sigma_{g}
, where
\sigma_{\omega}
and
\sigma_{g}
denote the characteristic widths of the underlying device-parameter fluctuations. We consider relative noise levels ranging from
10^{-4}
to
10^{-1}
, thereby spanning four orders of magnitude in estimation uncertainty, from near-perfect calibration to strongly degraded parameter knowledge. In particular, the largest value
\eta/\sigma=0.1
corresponds to a 10% error in the estimation of the device mismatch, which already represents a coarse level of calibration accuracy.

This behavior indicates that the learned correction policies rely only on coarse information about the local parameter regime, but ultimately require a minimum level of estimation accuracy to remain effective.

VII
Discussion

VII.1
Hybrid open/closed-loop control interpretation

The two-qutrit gate-synthesis problem considered here is more demanding than the control tasks typically addressed using model-free RL in earlier studies. In particular, the Hilbert-space dimension increases from four in two-qubit systems to nine in two-qutrit architectures, leading to a larger set of dynamical constraints that the control pulses must satisfy to realize a target entangling operation. This increase in dimensionality renders the control landscape considerably more complex and exacerbates known limitations of model-free RL, including poor exploration efficiency and slow convergence in high-dimensional, time-discretized settings, as highlighted in Refs.
Sivak2022_PRX
;
Baum2021_PRXQuantum
.
Recent works further demonstrate that, while RL can successfully optimize control pulses, it often requires additional structure or guidance to achieve high-fidelity solutions in realistic quantum hardware. For instance, experimental implementations on superconducting processors have shown that RL can learn error-robust gate sets but at the cost of substantial training effort and careful design of the learning framework
Baum2021_PRXQuantum
. Similarly, it has been shown that model-free RL benefits significantly from initialization strategies or prior information, such as demonstrations or near-optimal seeds, to stabilize training and improve performance
Nguyen2024RLTransmon
;
Li2025_RLfD
.
Motivated by these observations, the hybrid OCT-RL approach adopted here mitigates the intrinsic difficulty of the problem by first using OCT to generate a high-fidelity nominal pulse, and subsequently employing RL only to learn small calibration corrections around this reference solution. In contrast to approaches that use near-optimal seeds to improve exploration of the full high-dimensional control landscape, here the OCT solution fundamentally reshapes the learning problem by restricting RL to a local residual correction task. This reformulation significantly reduces the effective search space and enables stable learning in otherwise challenging high-dimensional settings.

Our framework can be interpreted as a hybrid open/closed-loop control scheme. The initial OCT step is purely model-based: it uses a nominal Hamiltonian and gradient-based optimization to find high-fidelity pulses. This corresponds to
open-loop optimal control
in the standard sense
Glaser2015
;
Koch2022
. The DRL stage, by contrast, incorporates
closed-loop
information about device-specific parameter variations: the context vector encodes deviations from the nominal model, and the reward is based on actual performance on that device.

Conceptually, this is closely related to adaptive hybrid optimal control for imprecisely characterized systems, where initial pulses are obtained from model-based OCT and subsequently refined using experimental feedback
Egger2014
. Our contribution is to implement this hybrid idea with a contextual DRL agent that learns a generalizable mapping from device parameters to residual corrections, rather than performing a separate local re-optimization for each device instance. This generalization is particularly valuable when calibrating a family of devices drawn from a similar fabrication process. In contrast to per-device re-optimization, this approach amortizes the
calibration effort across devices, enabling rapid adaptation without repeated
high-dimensional optimal control runs.

VII.2
Implications for superconducting qutrit hardware

The numerical results presented above indicate that a relatively shallow deep DRL layer, operating in a low-dimensional cosine basis, is sufficient to substantially enhance the robustness of a two-qutrit
\mathrm{CZ}_{3}
gate against static parameter mismatch. In particular, for the ensemble of noisy devices considered, the best-performing
RL agents achieve ensemble-averaged fidelities close to unity with a standard
deviation of approximately
0.012
, compared to a substantially lower mean
fidelity and much larger spread for the nominal OCT pulse.

These findings are directly motivated by recent experimental progress in superconducting qutrit platforms. In particular, Goss
et al.
demonstrated high-fidelity two-qutrit entangling gates in fixed-frequency transmon devices, reporting estimated process fidelities of
97.3(1)\%
for the
\mathrm{CZ}^{\dagger}
gate and
95.2(3)\%
for the
\mathrm{CZ}
gate using cycle benchmarking and cross-entropy benchmarking techniques
Goss2022
. Their work establishes the experimental feasibility of multi-level entangling operations in superconducting circuits, while also highlighting the increased complexity of calibrating such gates, which rely on finely tuned microwave-activated interactions and are sensitive to device parameters, frequency placement, and spurious couplings.

The static parameter variations considered in our numerical model are therefore not intended to reproduce a specific experimental noise profile, but rather to capture generic calibration mismatches and device-to-device variability that naturally arise in multi-transmon systems. In this context, the demonstrated ability of RL-enhanced pulses to maintain both high average fidelity and low variability across an ensemble of Hamiltonian realizations suggests a promising pathway toward more robust calibration strategies for superconducting qutrit hardware.

From a practical perspective, these results suggest several implications for near-term experiments:

•

Residual DRL corrections could be trained primarily in simulation and subsequently fine-tuned on hardware, potentially reducing the need for repeated gradient-based re-optimization during calibration. Once trained, the inference cost of the DRL policy is negligible compared to gradient-based re-optimization, making it attractive for repeated calibration cycles.

•

The use of smooth, low-bandwidth basis functions and explicit amplitude constraints is naturally compatible with the waveform generation and bandwidth limitations of superconducting control electronics.

•

The contextual structure of the RL input lends itself to integration with standard characterization routines, such as spectroscopy or Ramsey-type measurements, which provide coarse estimates of device parameters.

Future work should extend this framework to include open-system dynamics described by Lindblad master equations and should ultimately close the loop with experimental demonstrations. Such studies would build directly on recent experimental advances in superconducting qutrit entangling gates and benchmarking
Goss2022
;
Roy2023
, and would help clarify the role of DRL-assisted calibration in realistic, noisy multi-level quantum hardware.

VII.3
Extensions and outlook

Several extensions present themselves:

•

Meta-RL and online adaptation.
Meta-RL could accelerate adaptation to time-dependent drifts by learning update rules directly.

•

Higher-dimensional systems.
The approach naturally generalizes to three or more qutrits and to mixed qudits architectures, where GRAPE alone becomes even more expensive.

•

Robust control under decoherence.
Extending the control environment to include open-system dynamics described by a Lindblad master equation would allow DRL to learn control corrections that explicitly balance coherent control errors against decoherence-induced losses.

•

Integration with experimental toolchains.
Implementing the environment on top of pulse-level control APIs would enable fully automated calibration loops on real devices.

Overall, the combination of physics-based OCT and data-driven DRL is a promising route toward scalable, calibration-efficient, and robust control of multi-level quantum hardware.

VIII
Conclusion

We have proposed and numerically validated a hybrid control framework for robust two-qutrit
\mathrm{CZ}_{3}
gates with a special emphasis on weakly anharmonic superconducting circuits. Starting from GRAPE-optimized pulses on a nominal device, we train contextual DRL agents to output small cosine-basis residual corrections conditioned on device parameter offsets. The reward is defined as the fidelity improvement over the OCT baseline for each noisy device. Our simulations using SAC, TD3, DDPG, and PPO show that he best agents significantly enhance ensemble-averaged fidelity relative to the
nominal OCT baseline while preserving high nominal performance. On the nominal Hamiltonian, none of the agents surpass the OCT solution,
emphasizing that their benefit lies not in redesigning optimal pulses, but in
mitigating static model mismatch, device-to-device variability, and slow parameter
drifts.
From a conceptual standpoint, this constitutes a DRL-based realization of hybrid open/closed-loop optimal control for imprecisely characterized systems. This approach is complementary to ensemble-robust optimal control strategies,
which explicitly optimize pulses over parameter distributions but require prior
knowledge of those distributions and substantially higher computational costs.
Practically, it points toward automated, device-aware calibration strategies for multi-level superconducting processors. In future work, we anticipate that similar hybrid OCT+DRL schemes will play an
important role in bridging the gap between idealized pulse design and the
realities of noisy, heterogeneous quantum hardware by enabling fast,
calibration-efficient adaptation across devices.
