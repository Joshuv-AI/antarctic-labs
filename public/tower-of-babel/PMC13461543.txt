Case report and literature review: a case of rare papillary thyroid carcinoma combined with paraganglioma
Authors: Zhuoyi Wang, Lina Zhao, Chengcheng Duan, Xinran Wei, Weidong Yan, Sichen Liu, Jingyue Zhong, Hanggai Bao, Guang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPapillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) primarily metastasizes to ipsilateral cervical lymph nodes. Paragangliomas (PG) in the head and neck region may be located at the carotid bifurcation, causing anatomical separation of the internal and external carotid arteries.MethodsA rare case of PTC with PG at our center is reviewed to explore the relationship and differential diagnosis between PG and cervical lymph node metastasis (CLNM) in thyroid carcinoma. Based on the characteristics of this case, we retrieved relevant domestic and international case reports and relevant studies. This study searched the PubMed, Web of Science, and CNKI databases for publications up to March 2026. The search terms included: “Thyroid papillary carcinoma,” “Papillary thyroid cancer,” “Paraganglioma,” “Carotid body tumor,” and “Coexistence.” By combining these terms, we identified case reports and related studies on papillary thyroid carcinoma with concomitant paraganglioma.ResultsWe consider the patient’s disease type to be PTC with concomitant carotid body tumor. Based on existing clinical data and relevant literature reports, no clear correlation has been established between PGs and CLNMs from thyroid carcinoma.ConclusionsDistinguishing whether a neck mass associated with thyroid carcinoma represents CLNM or another type of tumor is crucial for disease diagnosis, treatment planning, and prognosis assessment.

Introduction

Thyroid carcinoma is the most common malignant tumor of the endocrine system, accounting for approximately 1% of all malignant tumors (1). Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is the most common type of thyroid carcinoma, accounting for approximately 88% of cases (2). Cervical lymph node metastasis (CLNM) is the most common form of thyroid carcinoma metastasis. Its occurrence will influence the surgical approach and extent of surgery for thyroid carcinoma patients.

Paraganglioma (PG) is a rare neuroendocrine tumor originating from chromaffin cells in the sympathetic and parasympathetic nerve chains (3). Sympathetic PGs are primarily located in the retroperitoneum and typically produce and secrete norepinephrine, leading to symptoms such as episodic hypertension, tachycardia, headaches, and sweating (4, 5). Parasympathetic PGs are primarily located in the neck and skull base, and are typically functionally inactive (5). Carotid body tumor (CBT) originates from paragangial cells at the bifurcation of the carotid artery. It is the most common type of PG in the head and neck region and is typically benign.

CLNMs from thyroid carcinoma and PGs of the head and neck can be clinically difficult to distinguish. Differentiating whether a neck mass represents metastatic lymph node cancer or a PG is crucial for determining the extent and approach of surgical intervention.

Case report

Mr. Guo, male, 58 years old, was admitted to the hospital after discovering a neck mass one year prior. The patient reported no tenderness in the neck throughout the course of the illness, and no symptoms such as hoarseness, choking while drinking, difficulty swallowing, or shortness of breath. Admission blood pressure was within normal range. It should be noted that, as the patient did not exhibit the classic triad of catecholamine excess—paroxysmal hypertension, palpitations, and profuse sweating—and multiple resting blood pressure measurements taken upon admission were normal, our center did not perform routine catecholamine biochemical tests, such as plasma free methoxy-norepinephrine (MNs) or 24-hour urinary catecholamines, during the patient’s initial visit. No history of hypertension. Denies relevant family history. Physical examination revealed a mass measuring approximately 4*2.5cm palpable in the right neck region II, and a mass measuring approximately 2.5*1.5cm palpable in the left neck region II. Both masses were moderately firm in consistency, painless, and mobile. Thyroid ultrasound reveals a nodule measuring approximately 11.0*9.1mm in the middle of the left thyroid lobe, with poorly defined margins and visible calcifications within (Figure 1A); A mass measuring approximately 26.6*19.9mm was identified in right cervical region II, without lymph node medullary structure (Figure 1B); A mass measuring approximately 22.1*16.4mm was identified in left cervical region II, without lymph node medullary structure (Figures 1C, D). Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAc) results: Left thyroid lobe nodule shows features suggestive of PTC. Right neck region II mass shows a few lymphocytes; further correlation with thyroglobulin washout levels. Left neck region II mass shows a few atypical cells; further correlation with thyroglobulin washout levels. Genetic testing results: Detection of the BRAF gene V600E mutation. Abnormal thyroid function was observed, with a thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) level of 5.010 mIU/L (normal range: 0.27–4.2 mIU/L, elevated), free triiodothyronine (FT3) level of 5.96 pmol/L, free thyroxine (FT4) level of 14.60 pmol/L, and thyroglobulin (TgII) level of 69.00 ng/mL. The TgII value in the fine-needle aspiration washings from the right neck II region was 0.13 ng/ml, and the TgII value in the fine-needle aspiration washings from the left neck II region was 0.24 ng/ml. Based on ultrasound imaging, FNAc, genetic testing, and thyroid globulin levels in the aspirate washout fluid, the diagnosis of the lateral neck mass remains inconclusive. Further core needle biopsy and immunohistochemical examination are recommended.

The clinical decision-making process at our center regarding the FNAc and subsequent core needle biopsy (CNB) of this neck mass is as follows: At the time of initial diagnosis, the masses in the right neck and left neck region II were highly suspected to be CLNMs from thyroid cancer. On ultrasound, typical CLNMs from thyroid cancer often present with loss of the lymph node hilum, microcalcifications, and cystic changes. However, color Doppler ultrasound of the neck in this patient revealed distinctive findings: the masses were all located at the bifurcation of the carotid arteries, and a clear “Lyre’s sign” was observed, characterized by an increased angle of bifurcation between the internal and external carotid arteries, with the masses straddling the bifurcation. The masses exhibited extremely rich internal blood flow signals and were seen to encase and infiltrate the vascular walls.

Given that these ultrasonographic vascular characteristics are highly suggestive of a PG (carotid body tumor), and since the initial FNAc revealed only a few lymphocytes and atypical cells, with TgII in the aspirate testing negative, a definitive diagnosis could not be established; furthermore, FNAc often yields limited tissue samples from highly vascularized tumors. To rule out or confirm the diagnosis of metastatic cancer, prevent misdiagnosis, exclude neuroendocrine tumors, and ensure that unnecessary routine total neck lymph node dissection was not performed blindly, we decided to proceed with CNB to obtain complete tissue samples for immunohistochemical staining, after making thorough preparations for vascular compression and establishing a resuscitation protocol.

Results of the patient’s CNB: The aspirated tissue from the left neck mass consists of proliferative hyalinized fibrous tissue containing round tumor cells arranged in nests. The stroma is richly vascularized, suggesting a metastatic lesion of neuroendocrine origin. Immunohistochemistry: PTH (–); CgA (+); SyN (+); CD56 (+); TTF-1 (–); Tg (–); Ki-67 (1%+); CT (–); S-100 (sustentacular cells +); GATA-3 (+). Special stains: Congo red (–). The histological morphology and immunohistochemical findings support a neuroendocrine tumor of origin consistent with a PG (Figure 2). Color Doppler ultrasound of the neck vessels reveals a mass infiltrating the anterolateral wall at the bifurcation of the right common carotid artery and the anterior and medial walls of the proximal segment of the left external carotid artery. Therefore, based on the results of the core needle biopsy and ultrasound imaging, the bilateral neck masses are considered carotid body tumors (paragangliomas). Due to the patient’s reluctance to undergo treatment and financial constraints, we were unable to perform germline mutation screening for paraganglioma-associated susceptibility genes such as SDHx, MAX, and RET in the patient’s peripheral blood or PG tissue; this also constitutes a limitation in the follow-up and etiological analysis of this case.

Pathological slide of a coarse-needle aspiration biopsy of a left neck mass, stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (×40). Under the microscope, tumor cells are arranged in nests, with the stroma containing abundant capillaries and fibrous connective tissue septa, consistent with the histological features of a neuroendocrine tumor.

The patient’s papillary carcinoma of the left thyroid lobe is classified as TNM stage T1aN0M0. Based on the current evaluation, the bilateral carotid body tumors are likely benign, and the lesions do not impair the patient’s ability to carry out daily activities. Following thorough discussion by the multidisciplinary team (MDT) and repeated communication with the patient and family, the patient has temporarily declined surgical resection. It was ultimately decided to proceed with active surveillance (AS). The rationale and benefits of choosing AS are as follows: (1) It avoids the risk of catastrophic complications—such as carotid artery injury, massive hemorrhage, and cerebral infarction—that are highly likely to occur during bilateral CBT resection; (2) It prevents postoperative swallowing and speech disorders caused by bilateral vagus nerve or hypoglossal nerve palsy; (3) It preserves the patient’s quality of life in the short term. The anticipated risks primarily include: potential progression of thyroid microcarcinoma or actual lymph node metastasis during AS; and enlargement of the CBT, which may further compress or encase the internal carotid artery, leading to an exponential increase in surgical difficulty and the risk of nerve injury at a later stage. To address this, we have established a rigorous follow-up strategy: advanced color Doppler ultrasound of the neck vessels and thyroid every 3 months, with a focus on monitoring tumor size, margins, and the degree of vascular invasion; re-evaluation of thyroid function, serum Tg, and plasma free MnS levels every 6 months; and an annual contrast-enhanced CT or MRI scan of the neck. To date, 9 months of follow-up have been completed, with no significant radiological progression of the thyroid tumor or bilateral PG lesions, and the patient reports no symptoms. Discussion

Paraganglioma is a neuroendocrine tumor arising from paraganglia, which are clusters of paraganglial neuroectodermal cells derived from the neural crest and located outside the adrenal system (6). PGs of the head and neck primarily occur in the carotid body, jugular bulb, vagus nerve, or the auditory nerve within the tympanic cavity (7). At least 14 susceptibility genes have been identified for paraganglioma, including EGLN1, EPAS1, KIF1B, MAX, MEN1, NF1, RET, SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, SDHAF2, TMEM127, and VHL (8). Syndromes associated with CBTs typically originate from germline mutations in the A, B, C, and D subunits of the mitochondrial complex II enzyme succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), with a higher prevalence in females than males (7). Cases with SDHD mutations are prone to developing multifocal PGs in the head and neck region, with a lower risk of metastasis (7). The SDH expression mechanism plays a crucial role in oxygen sensing and signal transduction. SDH inactivation caused by mutations may mimic chronic hypoxic stimulation in paraganglia cells, leading to increased levels of downstream hypoxia-responsive molecules that promote cell proliferation and hyperplasia. Ultimately, this process induces tumor transformation in carotid body cells (7), establishing chronic hypoxia as a risk factor for PG.

Among the common types of thyroid cancer, PTC, follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC), poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma (PDTC), and anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC) originate from thyroid follicular cells of endodermal origin, whereas medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) arises from thyroid C cells of neural crest origin (9). Genetic mutations in PTC are commonly found in BRAFV600E, followed by RAS and RET, NTRK, and ALK, which result in aberrant expression of the BRAF kinase domain or tyrosine kinase receptors due to chromosomal rearrangements (9). MTC is a major component of both MEN2A and MEN2B within the hereditary multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2) syndrome (10). The occurrence of endocrine tumors and related abnormalities in MEN2A or MEN2B patients depends on specific RET mutations (9).

Genetic susceptibility to PG is associated with germline mutations in genes causing classic familial tumor syndromes, and the association between MTC and PG has been confirmed in MEN2 (11). When endocrine gland tumors are detected, screening for MTC can be performed by testing whether serum calcitonin levels exceed 1000 pg/ml (12). However, the association between PTC and PG is relatively uncommon, and the underlying mechanism remains unclear. A major limitation of this study is that we were unable to perform multi-gene panel testing for PG susceptibility genes in this case. Although multiple/bilateral head and neck PGs strongly suggest a possible mitochondrial complex II enzyme defect, due to the lack of genetic evidence, we are currently unable to confirm at the molecular level whether there is a common genetic pathogenic mechanism between PTC and bilateral PG in this case. In future clinical practice, whole-exome sequencing or specific multi-gene targeted sequencing should be actively recommended for patients with such multiple or multiple primary neuroendocrine tumors to further elucidate their molecular genetic background.

To date, only nine cases of coexisting PG of the head and neck with PTC have been reported (Table 2). Among these, eight patients were female. In contrast, the present case involves a male patient with a CBT. Therefore, this represents a rare dual lesion of primary CBT combined with PTC in a male patient.

A systematic comparison of the characteristics of the 9 previously reported cases of PTC combined with head and neck PG in Table 2 with the present case revealed the following: (1) Gender and age at onset: Women accounted for the vast majority of previous cases (8/9), and the average age at onset was relatively young (median approximately 38 years). In contrast, the present case involved a 58-year-old middle-aged male, representing an extremely rare gender and age profile for this combination of primary lesions. (2) Lesion location and subtypes: Previous cases predominantly involved unilateral PG combined with unilateral PTC. In contrast, this case presented as an extremely rare “bilateral CBT” combined with “unilateral PTC (microcarcinoma),” representing a more complex clinical presentation. (3) Driver Genes: While some patients in the literature have been found to carry mitochondrial complex II mutations, the thyroid lesion in this patient clearly harbors a BRAF V600E mutation. This suggests that although this case exhibits the phenotypic characteristics of multiple PGs, the development of the PTC is still driven by the classic BRAF pathway. (4) Treatment Strategy: In the previous 9 cases, all patients underwent aggressive total thyroidectomy, lymph node dissection, or parathyroid adenoma resection. In contrast, based on indications for AS and the high-risk-to-benefit ratio of surgery, this patient was the first to undergo a conservative observation strategy with close follow-up, providing a novel and safe alternative for the clinical management of such high-risk composite lesions.

In clinical practice, cervical lymph node metastases from PTC are extremely easy to confuse with PGs of the head and neck. To ensure diagnostic accuracy, a thorough differential diagnosis must be conducted based on the following four criteria (Table 3): (1) Imaging features: On ultrasound, CLNMs from PTC typically present as irregularly shaped lesions with loss of the portal structure, internal microcalcifications, cystic changes, and perilesional blood flow; In contrast, PGs are characterized by a hyperechoic solid mass located at the bifurcation of the carotid arteries, resulting in an increased angle of the internal and external carotid artery bifurcation, and color Doppler imaging reveals extremely rich reticular blood flow within the lesion. (2) Thyroglobulin clearance rate (Tg eluate measurement): This is the key molecular method for distinguishing between the two. In cases of PTC lymph node metastasis, Tg washout levels in FNA eluate are typically far higher than serum baseline levels; in contrast, since PGs do not originate from thyroid epithelium, their FNA-Tg eluate levels are extremely low, usually approaching 0, which strongly rules out thyroid cancer metastasis (13). (3) Histopathological features: Metastatic PTC lymph nodes show typical papillary structures under the microscope, including true papillary nuclei, ground-glass nuclei, nuclear grooves, and granular bodies (14); paraneuromas, in contrast, present as classic epithelioid tumor cells arranged in nests, surrounded by a rich network of capillaries and microvascular stroma (15). (4) Immunohistochemical markers (16): This is the “gold standard” for definitive diagnosis. Metastatic PTC specifically expresses TTF-1, Tg, and CK19; in contrast, PGs express the neuroendocrine markers CgA(+), Syn(+), and CD56(+), and specific S-100 staining clearly delineates the “sustentacular cells” surrounding the tumor nests, while the thyroid-derived markers TTF-1 and Tg are both negative.

PGs of the head and neck generally lack catecholamine-secreting function, thus presenting no clinically significant symptoms such as episodic hypertension (5). However, the literature indicates that approximately 1% to 3% of PGs of the head and neck retain latent secretory function. Another limitation of this case is that routine biochemical testing for catecholamines was not performed prior to the CNB or the subsequent implementation of an active monitoring strategy. Although the patient’s blood pressure remained stable throughout the peri-biopsy period and follow-up, with no episodes of hypertensive crisis, proceeding with CNB or long-term observation without biochemical screening poses potential medical risks from a clinical safety perspective. This serves as a warning to clinicians that, even in patients with neck masses who have normal blood pressure and no symptoms of catecholamine excess, routine biochemical testing for catecholamines must be performed whenever imaging suggests a PG. This is essential to definitively rule out a functional tumor and ensure patient safety before performing any needle biopsy, surgery, or initiating AS. CBTs are typically painless. Due to carotid artery adhesions, their horizontal mobility often exceeds vertical mobility (17). Histologically, tumor cells are observed to arrange in nests, bundles, or trabeculae, surrounded by a rich capillary network. Careful differentiation is required from the histological features of lymph node metastases in PTC, FTC and MTC. If thyroid carcinoma is complicated by CLNM, diagnosis requires FNAc. The accuracy of determining CLNM is higher when the ratio of Tg washout levels in the lymph node aspirate to those in the blood exceeds 1. PGs of the head and neck can be preliminarily diagnosed through imaging techniques. To determine their benign or malignant nature, in addition to examining histological features such as tumor atypia, vascular invasion, and mitotic count, it is crucial to confirm the presence or absence of infiltration and metastasis (18). Currently, immunohistochemistry serves as the gold standard for diagnosing PGs in relevant examinations (19), with immunohistochemical staining playing a crucial role in confirming the diagnosis. Additionally, ultrasound examinations, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and angiography play a crucial role in the management of CBTs (12). These imaging modalities provide key information, such as the anatomical relationship between the tumor and critical vascular structures like arterial bifurcations, thereby enhancing the formulation and preparation of surgical plans.

Although surgical resection has traditionally been considered the treatment of choice for primary PTC complicated by head and neck PG, given the complex nature of this case—involving bilateral CBTs combined with unilateral micropapillary thyroid carcinoma—treatment decisions must be made through individualized consideration in strict accordance with the latest international guidelines. According to the 2025 American Thyroid Association (ATA) Guidelines for the Management of Adult Thyroid Nodules and Differentiated Thyroid Cancer, AS has been established as a safe first-line management strategy to replace immediate surgery for low-risk papillary thyroid microcarcinoma (PTMC) without local invasion, lymph node metastasis, or highly aggressive genetic features (13). The patient’s thyroid lesion measured only 11.0 × 9.1 mm, and both FNA-Tg washings and ultrasound ruled out cervical lymph node metastasis, fully meeting the criteria for AS as outlined in the ATA guidelines. More importantly, regarding the evaluation of head and neck PGs, the 2016 European Society of Endocrinology (ESE) guidelines for long-term follow-up of PGs explicitly state that for bilateral, multiple, or surgically high-risk PGs with benign behavior, the conservative “Watch and Wait” follow-up strategy should be strongly considered (20). According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Endocrine and Neuroendocrine Tumors (21), all PGs carry a potential risk of malignant behavior; however, their classification as malignant depends entirely on the presence of distant metastases in non-chromaffin tissue, rather than on local invasion. The patient in this case presented with bilateral CBTs. If bilateral radical resection were performed, current guidelines and literature statistics indicate that the patient would face a risk exceeding 30% of cerebral ischemia, massive hemorrhage due to carotid artery rupture, and permanent paralysis of cranial nerves (IX, X, and XII), which would severely impair the patient’s swallowing and speech functions. Therefore, based on authoritative guidelines from two major fields (ATA and ESE), the AS strategy formulated by our center’s MDT maximizes the avoidance of severe perioperative complications from a technical standpoint while also ensuring the long-term safe management of these two indolent tumors. This guideline-based decision-making model provides a solid evidence-based foundation for the future clinical management of similar rare, high-risk, multifocal neuroendocrine and epithelial composite tumors.

Conclusion

Neck masses associated with thyroid carcinoma require differential diagnosis based on laboratory test results, imaging studies, histopathological features, and immunohistochemical findings to determine whether they represent metastatic lymph node cancer. If examination results rule out metastatic cancer to lymph nodes but reveal features suggestive of PG, attention should be paid to whether the patient exhibits symptoms such as episodic hypertension, and a comprehensive and effective treatment plan should be provided. Whether there is a definite genetic association between PTC and PG requires further discovery and validation. Future studies should routinely include SDHx gene testing in the screening of patients with such dual primary cancers to address the limitation of this study, which did not perform whole-exome sequencing. PGs of the head and neck region are anatomically located near the bifurcation of the carotid arteries, presenting significant surgical challenges. Therefore, for such conditions, clinicians must conduct comprehensive examinations and thorough evaluations to develop tailored treatment and prognosis plans for each patient. PGs are prone to recurrence within five years, necessitating long-term postoperative monitoring. Patients should be instructed to undergo regular follow-up examinations to closely monitor disease progression.
