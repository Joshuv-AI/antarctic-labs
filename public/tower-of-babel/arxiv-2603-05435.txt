Homological methods in rigidity theory using graphs of groups
Joannes Vermant

Abstract
In recent work, Stokes and Vermant considered graph-of-groups realisations of hypergraphs as a new description of rigidity-theoretic problems. In this paper, we show that the infinitesimal aspects of graph-of-groups realisations can be analysed using cellular sheaves and their cohomology. Using these tools, we give an algebraic condition for Henneberg moves to preserve independence, and we prove that the infinitesimal rigidity and flexibility of certain graph-of-groups realisations are generic properties. We use these results to show that whenever a rigidity-theoretic problem is defined in a Lie group G G using a 1 1 -dimensional connected subgroup H H with N G ​ ( H ) / H N_{G}(H)/H finite, then the so-called Maxwell-count leads to a necessary and sufficient condition for minimal rigidity, generalising various known results in the literature.

Introduction

In structural rigidity theory, one studies bar-joint frameworks, which are graphs embedded in Euclidean space, such that each edge is considered as a rigid bar and each vertex is considered as a universal joint. A bar-joint framework is said to be rigid if there are no non-trivial ways to deform the framework without breaking it. For almost all choices of coordinates, it turns out that rigidity is a property that depends only on the underlying graph
[
1
]
, and one can hence speak of rigidity as a graph-theoretical property.

In dimension
1
, rigidity is characterised by connectivity, and in dimension
2
, a combinatorial characterisation of rigidity is given by the Geiringer-Laman theorem
[
27
,
25
]
. In dimension
3
and higher, characterising rigidity is an open problem. The characterisation in dimension
2
describes rigidity in terms of a so-called sparsity matroid
[
20
, Section 13.5]
, allowing for algorithms that check rigidity in polynomial time. Additionally, researchers have defined and studied the rigidity of many other combinatorial-geometric structures, proving that they are characterised by sparsity conditions
[
37
,
34
,
35
,
24
,
13
]
.

In this paper, we further develop the theory of graph-of-groups realisations, building upon the article by Stokes and the present author
[
29
]
. Graph-of-groups realisations provide a general framework for rigidity theory, which describes rigidity using only (Lie) group theory. One can describe the position of an articulated structure by only keeping track of the stabiliser subgroup of each geometric element. One can then describe the motions of the structure using group elements and the given subgroups. In
[
29
]
, the approach is shown to model the classical theory of bar-joint frameworks; it also models, for example, scenes and parallel redrawings
[
37
]
and projective rigidity
[
2
]
.

For all graph-of-groups realisations, one can derive a counting condition similar to the one in the Geiringer-Laman theorem, which provides a necessary condition for rigidity. In this article, we make progress towards answering the question of when this condition is also sufficient. To do this, one needs to first define
generic rigidity
, a problem that was left unresolved in
[
29
]
. We prove a genericity result for a broad class of realisations in
Theorem

3.10
.
The main theorem states that for sufficiently generic graph-of-groups realisations, where the stabilisers have dimension
1
, the rigidity is characterised by the necessary condition. See
Theorem

2.6
, and
Theorem

3.11
for a precise statement.

Similar statements have been conjectured for another general rigidity model, called
g
-rigidity
[
14
, Conjecture 4.13]
.
Theorem

2.6
can also be seen as being similar in spirit to the results in
[
31
]
, providing a general way to give realisations of certain sparsity matroids.

We discuss briefly the applications of this theorem. We show that the Geiringer-Laman theorem can be deduced from the main theorem, and one can also immediately deduce similar results for spherical and hyperbolic rigidity, a fact usually explained through coning
[
36
,
28
]
. We also show that
Theorem

2.6
applies to parallel rigidity, which was previously characterised in
[
38
,
17
]
.

The proof of
Theorem

2.6
is an inductive proof. We use a result by Frank and Szegö
[
19
]
that provides an inductive characterisation of sparse graphs. Szegö
[
32
]
conjectured that similar results should hold for other values. We use novel methods to show that the extensions preserve independence after suitably defining independence in the graph-of-groups setting. We remark that understanding
2

3

3
-dimensional rigidity
[
15
,
4

.

The main tool that we use is that the infinitesimal motions can be interpreted as the
0
th cohomology group of cellular sheaves on graphs. Cellular sheaves
[
16
,
21
]
are variants of classical sheaves, and they can be used to study and address local-to-global type questions.

Modelling the infinitesimal motions as a sheaf-cohomology group, we shall use long exact sequences to study the relation of infinitesimal motions to other vector spaces. In fact, it is through the use of a long exact sequence that we can prove the inductive constructions preserve rigidity for a general class of sheaves that we call
motion sheaves
. Naturally defined classes of motion sheaves carry the structure of a real algebraic variety, being a product of Grassmannians, and we will consider how the dimensions of these cohomology groups can vary within the variety. We will establish that the dimensions of the cohomology groups exhibit upper semi-continuity with respect to the Zariski topology. For the inductive constructions, we need to consider a class of sheaves related to the motion sheaves that we call
associated sheaves
, and their generic behaviour is characterised in
Theorem

2.27
. These associated sheaves may be of independent interest.

To apply the results to graph-of-groups realisations, one needs to make a geometric analysis of which motion sheaves arise from graph-of-groups realisations. Within the mentioned varieties, the sheaves coming from graph-of-groups realisations will cut out semi-algebraic subsets. In the case where stabilisers are
1
-dimensional, and in the case of parallel redrawings, these semi-algebraic subsets are full-dimensional, meaning that in these cases, the graph-of-groups will behave like generic motion sheaves. The main theorem,
Theorem

2.6
, establishes the generic behaviour of motion sheaves in these cases. Classes of motion sheaves that come from graph-of-groups do not need to exhibit generic behaviour, and this is, for example, the case for
3
-dimensional rigidity.

The duals of cellular sheaves, cosheaves, have been exploited to explain graphic statics from a homological perspective. This can be read in the PhD thesis of Cooperband
[
9
]
, and in the work by Cooperband, Ghrist, Hansen, Lopez, McRobie, Millar, and Schulze upon which the thesis expands
[
5
,
9
,
7
,
8
,
6
]
. The treatment of graphic statics using these techniques simplifies and clarifies many constructions and also generalises them cleanly to new contexts. However, homological methods had already been applied to the study of rigidity and liftings of scenes by Crapo, Whiteley, and Tay
[
12
,
11
,
39
,
10
,
33
]
(see also Part III of the survey article
[
38

). The model utilised in the current work is most similar to the one used in
[

Much of the previous work has also focused on higher homology groups, which were associated with structures such as simplicial complexes or graph embeddings on surfaces. In the current work, we only consider sheaves on graphs, though similar higher-rank structures are a natural and promising avenue for further work.

Definition 2.1
.

A sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on a graph
\Gamma=(V,E)
consists of a finite dimensional vector space
\mathcal{F}(v)
for all
v\in V
, as well as a finite dimensional vector space
\mathcal{F}(e)
for all
e\in E
, together with linear maps (called restriction maps)

\displaystyle r^{v}_{e}:\mathcal{F}(v)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(e)

for each vertex-edge inclusion
v\in e
. We will denote the class of all sheaves on a given graph by
\textup{Sh}(\Gamma)
.

One defines

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(E)

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{e\in E}\mathcal{F}(e),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(V)

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{v\in V}\mathcal{F}(v),

and the map

d:\mathcal{F}(V)\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(E):(w_{v_{1}},\cdots,w_{v_{n}})\mapsto(\sum_{v\sim e}[v:e]r^{v}_{e}(w_{v}))_{e\in E},

(1)

where
[v:e]=\pm 1
, oriented in such a way that for every edge

, one has
[v:e]=-[u:e]

One then defines the cohomology groups

\displaystyle H^{0}(\Gamma,\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle=\ker(d)

\displaystyle H^{1}(\Gamma,\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle=\text{coker}(d)=\frac{\mathcal{F}(E)}{\textup{im}(d)}.

It is clear that this is independent of the given orientation on
\Gamma
. Elements of

are called sections.

Let
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
be sheaves on a graph
\Gamma
. A morphism of sheaves
\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
consists of a collection of
|V|+|E|
linear maps:

\displaystyle\alpha_{v}:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(v)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(v)

\displaystyle\alpha_{e}:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(e)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(e),

such that for any vertex-edge inclusion, the following diagram commutes:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{F}(v)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{G}(v)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{F}(e)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{G}(e)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{v}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r^{v}_{e}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r^{v}_{e}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{e}}

A morphism of sheaves is said to be injective and surjective, respectively, if all the
\alpha_{v}
and
\alpha_{e}
are. Given a morphism of sheaves
\alpha:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
, one can also define the sheaves
\ker(\alpha)
and
\text{im}(\alpha)
, which are the kernel and image of a morphism of sheaves. They are defined by:

\displaystyle\ker(\alpha)(x)

\displaystyle:=\ker(\alpha_{x})\text{ for all }x\in V\cup E,

\displaystyle\textup{im}(\alpha)(x)

\displaystyle:=\textup{im}(\alpha_{x})\text{ for all }x\in V\cup E,

and

is given by the restriction map in
\mathcal{F}
or
\mathcal{G}
respectively. One has that
\alpha:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
is surjective if
\textup{im}(\alpha)=\mathcal{G}
.

A map of cellular sheaves induces a map of sections
\alpha_{\Gamma}:H^{0}(\Gamma,\mathcal{F})\rightarrow H^{0}(\Gamma,\mathcal{G}):\sum_{v\in V}w_{v}\mapsto\sum_{v\in V}\alpha_{v}(w_{v})
as well as maps
H^{1}(\Gamma,\mathcal{F})\rightarrow H^{1}(\Gamma,\mathcal{G}):[\sum_{e\in E}w_{e}]\mapsto[\sum_{e\in E}\alpha_{e}(w_{e})]
. However, a key point about sheaves is that
\alpha_{\Gamma}
need not be surjective if
\alpha
is. As for modules, one says that a sequence of morphisms

\mathcal{F}\xrightarrow{\alpha}\mathcal{G}\xrightarrow{\beta}\mathcal{H}

is exact if
\ker(\beta)=\text{im}(\alpha)
.

One sheaf that will play an important role in what follows is the constant sheaf with values in a vector space
\mathbb{V}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathbb{V}
be a vector space, and let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph. We denote by
\underline{\mathbb{V}}
the sheaf with
\underline{\mathbb{V}}(x)=\mathbb{V}

0\rightarrow H^{0}(\Gamma,\mathcal{F})\rightarrow H^{0}(\Gamma,\mathcal{G})\rightarrow H^{0}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H})\rightarrow H^{1}(\Gamma,\mathcal{F})\rightarrow H^{1}(\Gamma,\mathcal{G})\rightarrow H^{1}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H})\rightarrow 0.

The terms

are

2.2
Definitions and statement of main theorem.

The motivation for the following definition is that one can describe the infinitesimal motions of graph-of-groups realisations as a

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a hypergraph, and let
\mathbb{V}
be a finite dimensional real vector space. For any choices of subspaces

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(v)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(e)

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{v\in e}\mathcal{S}(v)\leq\mathbb{V}\text{ for }e\in X\text{ representing edges of $\Gamma$},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(e\sim v)

The maps

\begin{array}[]{cc}r^{v}_{v\sim e}:\mathcal{S}(v)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(v\sim e),&r^{e}_{v\sim e}:\mathcal{S}(e)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(v\sim e)\end{array}

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(e)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}/\mathcal{S}(e)\text{ for }e\in V(I(\Gamma))\text{ representing edges of $\Gamma$},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(e\sim v)

, which is well defined since

2.3
Basic observations

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a hypergraph, and let
\mathcal{M}
be a motion sheaf on
I(\Gamma)=(X,I)
. One has

\dim(H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M}))-\dim(H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M}))=\sum_{x\in X}\dim\left(\mathcal{M}(x)\right)-\sum_{v\sim e\in I}\dim(\mathcal{M}(v)).

Proof.

By the rank-nullity theorem, applied to the boundary operator, one has

\dim(\text{ker}(d))+\dim(\text{im}(d))=\dim(\mathcal{M}(X)).

Hence,

\dim(\text{ker}(d))-\dim(\text{coker}(d))=\dim(\mathcal{M}(X))-\dim(\mathcal{M}(I)).

Counting the dimensions of
\mathcal{M}(X)
and
\mathcal{M}(I)
yields the result.
∎

Theorem

2.7
is a general version of the Maxwell rule and it indicates that
H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M})
plays the same role as stresses in the classical setting. From the short exact sequence (

\begin{array}[]{cl}0\rightarrow&H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{S})\rightarrow H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\underline{\mathbb{V}})\xrightarrow{p_{\Gamma}}H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M})\\[10.0pt]
&\xrightarrow{\delta}H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{S})\rightarrow H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\underline{\mathbb{V}})\rightarrow H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M})\rightarrow 0.\end{array}

(5)

We call elements
(W_{x})_{x\in V\cup E}=H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M})
such that there exists a

with

W_{x}=W\textup{ mod }\mathcal{S}(x)\textup{ for all }x\in V\cup E

trivial infinitesimal motions. An easy computation shows that

\displaystyle H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{C\in K(\Gamma)}\left(\bigcap_{v\in V(C)}\mathcal{S}(v)\right),

where

is connected. It is equally easy to verify that

In the case that

gives the trivial infinitesimal motions.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\Gamma
be a hypergraph, and let
\mathcal{M}
be a motion sheaf. We say that

is

•

if

•

•

For motion sheaves of graph-of-groups realisations coming from bar-joint frameworks, these will notions correspond to independence, infinitesimal rigidity, and minimal infinitesimal rigidity respectively.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a hypergraph, and let
\mathcal{M}
be a motion sheaf on

such that
\bigcap_{v\in V}\mathcal{S}(v)=0

\sum_{e\in E(\Gamma^{\prime})}\left(\sum_{v\in e}\dim\left(\mathcal{M}(v)\right)-\dim\left(\mathcal{M}(e)\right)\right)\leq\sum_{v\in V(\Gamma^{\prime})}\dim(\mathcal{M}(v))-\dim(\mathbb{V}).

(6)

If
\mathcal{M}
is minimally rigid, then one additionally has:

\sum_{e\in E}\left(\sum_{v\in e}\dim\left(\mathcal{M}(v)\right)-\dim\left(\mathcal{M}(e)\right)\right)=\sum_{v\in V}\dim(\mathcal{M}(v))-\dim(\mathbb{V}).

(7)

Proof.

We are given

\displaystyle\dim\left(H^{0}(I(\Gamma^{\prime}),\mathcal{M}_{|I(\Gamma^{\prime})})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V^{\prime}}\dim(\mathcal{M}(v))+\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\dim(\mathcal{M}(e))-\sum_{e\sim v\in I(\Gamma^{\prime})}\dim(\mathcal{M}(v))

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V^{\prime}}\dim(\mathcal{M}(v))+\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\left(\dim(\mathcal{M}(e))-\sum_{v\in e}\dim(\mathcal{M}(v))\right).

For
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma
, this gives the equality (
7
) when
\mathcal{M}
is rigid.
If we can show that
\dim\left(H^{0}(I(\Gamma^{\prime}),\mathcal{M}_{|I(\Gamma^{\prime})})\right)\geq\dim(\mathbb{V})
for sub-hypergraphs
\Gamma^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
with
\bigcap_{v\in V^{\prime}}\mathcal{S}(v)=\{0\}
, we obtain the inequality (
6
). It is clear that
\mathcal{M}_{|I(\Gamma^{\prime})}
is a motion sheaf on
I(\Gamma^{\prime})
, and hence one has the map
p_{\Gamma^{\prime}}:H^{0}(I(\Gamma^{\prime}),\underline{\mathbb{V}}_{|I(\Gamma^{\prime})})\rightarrow H^{0}(I(\Gamma^{\prime}),\mathcal{M}_{|I(\Gamma^{\prime})})
. By the long exact sequence (
5
), we have

p_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(H^{0}(I(\Gamma^{\prime}),\underline{\mathbb{V}}_{|\Gamma^{\prime}}))\cong\bigoplus_{C\in K(\Gamma^{\prime})}\frac{\mathbb{V}}{\bigcap_{v\in V(C)}\mathcal{S}(v)},

and we have

\displaystyle\dim\left(p_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(H^{0}(I(\Gamma^{\prime}),\underline{\mathbb{V}}_{|\Gamma^{\prime}}))\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{C\in K(\Gamma^{\prime})}\dim(\mathbb{V})-\dim(\bigcap_{v\in C}\mathcal{S}(v))

\displaystyle=\sum_{C\in K(\Gamma^{\prime})}\textup{codim}(\bigcap_{v\in C}\mathcal{S}(v))

\displaystyle\geq\textup{codim}(\bigcap_{v\in V^{\prime}}\mathcal{S}(v))=\dim(\mathbb{V}).

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

If
s<\frac{(r-1)n}{r}
, for sheaves in
M_{s,n}(\Gamma)
, the necessary condition is given by
(n-s,n)-
sparsity of the hypergraph with edges copied a given number of times. For higher values of
s
, one can derive similar conditions, though the condition will not be matroidal.

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be an
r
-regular hypergraph. If
s<\frac{(r-1)n}{r}
, and if
\mathcal{M}\in M_{s,n}(\Gamma)
is such that
\bigcap_{v\in X}\mathcal{S}(v)=0
for any subset
X\subseteq V
with
|X|\geq r
, and if
\mathcal{M}
is independent, then

\displaystyle\left((r-1)n-rs\right)|E^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq(n-s)|V^{\prime}|-n\textup{ for all sub-hypergraphs $(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})\subseteq\Gamma$}.

If
\mathcal{M}
is minimally rigid, then additionally, one has

\displaystyle\left((r-1)n-rs\right)|E|

\displaystyle=(n-s)|V|-n,

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\dim(\mathcal{M}(v))

\displaystyle=n-\dim(\mathcal{S}(v))=n-s

\displaystyle\dim(\mathcal{M}(e))

\displaystyle=n-\dim(\bigcap_{v\in e}\mathcal{S}(v))=n

and thus

\sum_{v\in e}\dim(\mathcal{M}(v))-\dim(\mathcal{M}(e))=r(n-s)-n=(r-1)n-rs.

The result then follows from
Theorem

2.9
.
∎

For graphs, one has
r=2
, and then the condition gives
(n-s,n)
-sparsity of
(n-2s)\Gamma
.

2.4
Genericity for motion sheaves

In this section, we shall show that given a hypergraph
\Gamma
, the functions

\displaystyle h^{0}:M_{s,n}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\dim(H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M}))

\displaystyle h^{1}:M_{s,n}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\dim(H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M}))

are upper semi-continuous functions with respect to the Zariski topology. From this, it follows that the cohomology groups have the same dimension for a nonempty Zariski open subset of
M_{s,n}
.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
X
be a topological space, and let
Z\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. A function
f:X\rightarrow Z
is said to be lower semi-continuous if
f^{-1}((a,\infty))
is open for each
a\in Z
. A function
f:X\rightarrow Z
is said to be upper semi-continuous if
f^{-1}((-\infty,a))
is open for each
a\in Z
.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
X
be a real algebraic variety, and let
f_{1,1},\dots,f_{n,m}\in\mathbb{R}(X)
be
n\times m
rational functions, all defined on an open subset
U\subset X
. Then

\textup{rk}:U\rightarrow\mathbb{N}:x\mapsto\textup{Rank}\left(\begin{bmatrix}f_{1,1}(x)&\dots&f_{1,m}(x)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
f_{n,1}(x)&\dots&f_{n,m}(x)\end{bmatrix}\right)

is a lower semi-continuous function with respect to the Zariski topology on
U
.

Commonly, one uses Plücker coordinates on Grassmannians. We will instead use a description of the Grassmannian as a space of projection operators, which gives it the structure of an affine real algebraic variety, see
[
3
, Section 3.4.2]
. For a comparison to Plücker coordinates, see
[
18
]
, where, among other things, it is proven that the description that follows is birationally equivalent to the usual projective variety. For any subspace
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
of dimension
s
, let
P:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the orthogonal projection with image
S
. One then has the conditions

P=P^{t},P^{2}=P
, and
\textup{Tr}(P)=s
.
These three equations define the Grassmannian
\textup{Gr}(s,\mathbb{R}^{n})
as an affine subvariety of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

Lemma 2.13
.

For any subset
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
, the function

\textup{Gr}(s,\mathbb{V})^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}:(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})\mapsto\dim(\cap_{j\in I}(S_{j}))

is upper semi-continuous for the Zariski topology on
\textup{Gr}(s,\mathbb{V})^{m}
.

Proof.

Let
I=\{k_{1},\dots,k_{\ell}\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
. Fixing an inner product and a basis on
\mathbb{V}
, we view
\mathbb{V}
as being
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and we can use the description of the Grassmannian as a space of projection operators given above. We will consider the kernel of the following map

\varphi:S_{k_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{k_{\ell}}\mapsto(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\binom{\ell}{2}}:\left(w_{k_{1}},\dots,w_{k_{\ell}}\right)\mapsto(w_{k_{i}}-w_{k_{j}})_{k_{i}<k_{j}},

as the choices of
S_{k_{i}}
vary. One can immediately see that
\ker(\varphi)\cong\bigcap_{k_{i}\in I}S_{k_{i}}
.

To understand
\ker(\varphi),
we let
\tilde{\varphi}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\cdots\times(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\binom{l}{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}:(w_{k_{1}},\dots,w_{k_{\ell}})\mapsto\left(P_{k_{i}}(w_{k_{i}})-P_{k_{j}}(w_{k_{j}})\right)_{k_{i}<k_{j}},
where
P_{k_{i}}
is the orthogonal projection onto
S_{k_{i}}
. It is clear that

\ker(\tilde{\varphi})\cong\ker(\varphi)\oplus S_{k_{1}}^{\perp}\oplus\dots\oplus S_{k_{\ell}}^{\perp}.

In a matrix, the map
\tilde{\varphi}
is given by

\begin{bmatrix}P_{k_{1}}&-P_{k_{2}}&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
P_{k_{1}}&0&-P_{k_{3}}&0&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\dots&P_{k_{\ell-1}}&-P_{k_{\ell}}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Then, by
Proposition

2.12
, the rank of this matrix is lower semi-continuous as a function of
P_{k_{i}}
, which are precisely the coordinates of the Grassmannian. Thus
\dim(\ker(\tilde{\varphi}))=n\ell-\textup{rk}(\tilde{\varphi})
is upper semi-continuous. Then, since

\dim(\ker(\tilde{\varphi}))=\dim(\ker(\varphi))+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\dim(S_{k_{j}}^{\perp}),

and since
\dim(S_{k_{j}}^{\perp})
is constant, one sees that
\dim(\ker(\varphi))
is also upper semi-continuous.
∎

Example 2.14
.

Let us consider the case where
s=1
. In that case, one has
\dim(S_{1}\cap S_{2})=\{0\}
if and only if
S_{1}\neq S_{2}
. With the notation of the lemma, this implies that the dimension of the intersection can only increase on the closed subsets defined by
S_{i}=S_{j}
for all
i,j\in I
.

Theorem 2.15
.

Let
\Gamma
be a hypergraph. Let
U\subseteq Gr(s,\mathbb{V})^{|V|}
be a semi-algebraic subset such that for any
x=(S_{1},\dots,S_{|V|})\in U
, and for any hyper-edge
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in E
, one has that
\dim(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}S_{u_{i}})
is constant as a function of
S_{u_{i}}\in U
. Identifying the set
M_{s,n}(\Gamma)
with
\textup{Gr}(s,\mathbb{V})^{|V|}
, we treat points in
x\in U
as sheaves. We define
h^{0}:U\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
and
h^{1}:U\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
by

\displaystyle h^{0}(\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle:=\dim(H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M}))

\displaystyle h^{1}(\mathcal{M}):=\dim(H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M})).

The functions
h^{0}(\mathcal{M})
and
h^{1}(\mathcal{M})
are upper semi-continuous with respect to the Zariski topology restricted to
U
.
In particular, there is a Zariski open subset of
\mathcal{M}\in U
such that
h^{0}(\mathcal{M})
and
h^{1}(\mathcal{M})
attain the minimum dimension.

Proof.

We use the isomorphism
\textup{Gr}(s,\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\textup{Gr}(n-s,\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Using the realisation as projection operators, it is given by

\varphi:\textup{Gr}(s,\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\textup{Gr}(n-s,\mathbb{R}^{n}):P\mapsto I-P.

Under this isomorphism, one recovers the space
S\in\textup{Gr}(s,\mathbb{R}^{n})
which
P
represents as the kernel of the orthogonal projection
P
, and one can think of the projection
P
as being a projection onto
\mathbb{R}^{n}/S
.
Thus, let
(S_{v})_{v\in V}\in U
, with associated orthogonal projections
P_{v}
onto
S_{v}^{\perp}
. Consider the sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on
I(\Gamma)=(V\cup E,I)
with
\mathcal{F}(e)=\mathcal{F}(v)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We put

r^{e}_{v_{i}\sim e}=P_{v_{i}}

When writing the operator
d:\bigoplus_{x\in V\cup E}\mathcal{F}(x)\rightarrow\bigoplus_{i\in I}\mathcal{F}(i)
as a matrix, the entries are given by the entries of the
P_{v}
, which are coordinates of the Grassmannian. By
Proposition

2.12
, the dimension of
H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{F})
is upper semi-continuous on
U
.
One has

H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{F})\cong\left(\oplus_{v\in V}S_{v}\right)\oplus\left(\oplus_{e\in E}(\cap_{v\in e}S_{v})\right)\oplus H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M}).

Then, since for
(S_{v})_{v\in V}\in U
the ranks of
\cap_{v\in e}S_{v}
are the same minimal number, we see that
h^{0}(\mathcal{M})
is upper semi-continuous. By
Theorem

2.7
,
h^{1}(\mathcal{M})
is as well.
∎

Remark 2.16
.

Theorem

2.15
is not true if we replace
U
by
\textup{Gr}(s,\mathbb{V})^{|V|}
. For instance, let
K_{3}
be the complete graph on
3
vertices
\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
. Let
\mathbb{V}=\mathbb{R}^{3}
and

. Pick

. One can compute that

. One can also compute that for any

with

2.5
Associated sheaves on
multigraphs

In this section, given
\mathcal{M}\in M_{s,n}(\Gamma)
for a graph
\Gamma
, we define sheaves on
(n-2s)\cdot\Gamma
that have the same cohomology groups.

Proposition 2.17
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph. Let
\mathcal{S}\in S_{s,n}(\Gamma)
and let
\mathcal{M}=\underline{\mathbb{V}}/\mathcal{S}
. Define a sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on
\Gamma
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(v)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}(v)=\mathbb{V}/\mathcal{S}(v)\textup{ for all }v\in V

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(e)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}/\left(\mathcal{S}(v)+\mathcal{S}(u)\right)\textup{ if }e=vu,

and with maps
r^{v}_{e}:\mathcal{F}(v)\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(e)
being the restriction modulo
\mathcal{S}(v)+\mathcal{S}(u)
.
We have

\displaystyle H^{0}(\Gamma,\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle=H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M}),

\displaystyle H^{1}(\Gamma,\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle=H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M}).

Proof.

Consider first a sheaf
\mathcal{G}
, defined on
I(\Gamma)
, by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(v)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}(v)\textup{ for all }v\in V

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(e)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}/\left(\mathcal{S}(v)+\mathcal{S}(u)\right)\textup{ for all }vu\in E

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(v\sim e)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}/\left(\mathcal{S}(v)+\mathcal{S}(u)\right)\textup{ for all }vu\in E.

Consider the map
\alpha:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{v}

\displaystyle=\textup{id}\textup{ for all }v\in V,

\displaystyle\alpha_{e}

\displaystyle=\pi_{e}\textup{ for all }e\in E,

\displaystyle\alpha_{v\sim e}

\displaystyle=\pi^{v}_{e}\textup{ for all }v\sim e\in I,

where
\pi_{e}:\mathbb{V}/\mathcal{S}(e)\rightarrow\mathbb{V}/\left(\mathcal{S}(v)+\mathcal{S}(u)\right)
and
\pi^{v}_{e}:\mathbb{V}/\mathcal{S}(v)\rightarrow\mathbb{V}/\left(\mathcal{S}(v)+\mathcal{S}(u)\right)
are the reductions modulo
\mathcal{S}(v)+\mathcal{S}(u)
. Then we have a short exact sequence of sheaves:

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow\ker(\alpha)\rightarrow\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}\rightarrow 0.

(8)

One has
\ker(\alpha)(v)=\{0\},\ker(\alpha)(e)=\frac{\mathcal{S}(v)+\mathcal{S}(u)}{\mathcal{S}(v)\cap\mathcal{S}(u)}
, and
\ker(\alpha)(v\sim e)=\frac{\mathcal{S}(v)+\mathcal{S}(u)}{\mathcal{S}(v)}
. It then follows that

\displaystyle H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\ker(\alpha))

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\ker(\alpha))

\displaystyle=0.

Then, the long exact sequence associated with the short exact sequence (
8
) implies that

\displaystyle H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\cong H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M}),

\displaystyle H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\cong H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M}).

To finish the proof, it suffices to show

\displaystyle H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\cong H^{0}(\Gamma,\mathcal{F}),

\displaystyle H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\cong H^{1}(\Gamma,\mathcal{F}),

which follows in a straightforward way by simply using the definitions.
∎

We now discuss how to define a sheaf on a multigraph related to any sheaf on a graph with the same cohomology groups. Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph, let
\mathcal{F}
be a sheaf on
\Gamma
, and fix a basis
\mathcal{B}_{e}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{m_{e}})
for each
\mathcal{F}(e)
for
e\in E
. One can thus write the maps defining
\mathcal{F}
as
r^{v}_{e}(x)=f_{e,1}(x)b_{1}+\dots+f_{e,m_{e}}(x)b_{m_{e}}
for some
f_{e,i}\in\mathbb{V}^{*}
.

Define
\tilde{\Gamma}
to be the multi-graph with vertex set
V
, and edge set having
\dim(\mathcal{F}(e))
copies of each edge.

Label these edges
(e,1),\dots,(e,m_{e})
. Define a sheaf
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
on
\tilde{\Gamma}
by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(v)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(v),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(e,i)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}\text{ for each edge }(e,i)\in E.

the maps
r^{v}_{e,i}
are given by
f_{e,i}
defined above. One computes easily that:

\displaystyle H^{0}(\tilde{\Gamma},\tilde{\mathcal{F}})

\displaystyle\cong H^{0}(\Gamma,\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle H^{1}(\tilde{\Gamma},\tilde{\mathcal{F}})

\displaystyle\cong H^{1}(\Gamma,\mathcal{F}).

We apply this construction to the sheaf
\mathcal{F}
from
Proposition

2.17
. For any subspace
S\leq\mathbb{V},
of dimension
2s
, and any
n-2s
linearly independent linear forms
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-2s}\in\mathbb{V}^{*}
such that
\alpha_{i}(S)=0
for

, the map

\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n-s}:x\mapsto(\alpha_{1}(x),\dots\alpha_{n-2s}(x))

gives a coordinate expression for the reduction
\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}/S
. Thus, letting
\lambda\cdot\Gamma
be the multigraph with
\lambda_{e}
copies of each edge, where
\lambda_{e}=\dim(\mathbb{V}/(\mathcal{S}(v)+\mathcal{S}(u)))
, we pick any basis
\alpha_{e,1},\dots,\alpha_{e,\lambda_{e}}
of the annihilator of
\mathcal{S}(v)+\mathcal{S}(u)
on each edge

, and then we define
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(v)
to be equal to
\mathbb{V}/\mathcal{S}(v)
and
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(e)=\mathbb{R}
. The linear map

\mathcal{S}(v)\subseteq\mathcal{S}(v)+\mathcal{S}(u)\subseteq\ker(\alpha_{e})
. With this notation, we have:

Proposition 2.18
.

Let
s\leq n/2
, let
\mathcal{S}\in S_{s,n}(\Gamma)
and let
\mathcal{M}=\underline{\mathbb{V}}/\mathcal{S}
with
\mathcal{S}(v)\cap\mathcal{S}(u)=0
for all edges

. Suppose that
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
as above using any basis
\alpha_{e,1},\dots\alpha_{e,n-2s}
for each edge

. Then

\displaystyle H^{0}((n-2s)\cdot\Gamma,\tilde{\mathcal{F}})

\displaystyle=H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle H^{1}((n-2s)\cdot\Gamma,\tilde{\mathcal{F}})

\displaystyle=H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M}).

We want to equip the set of possible sheaves of the form
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
with the structure of a real algebraic variety, as we have done in
Section

3.3
. Given a multigraph
\Gamma=(V,E)
, we will thus consider the sheaves such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(v)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}/S_{v}

for

,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(e)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}/\textup{ker}(\alpha_{e})\cong\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\textup{ for $e\in E$},

if

. For a multigraph, define

The condition that

with the coordinates expressing
\textup{Gr}(s,n)

\mathcal{M}
, for every
\mathcal{F}\in Z_{s,n}(\Gamma)
, we have a short exact sequence of sheaves

0\rightarrow\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\underline{\mathbb{V}}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}\rightarrow 0.

(9)

Here
\mathcal{S}(v)
is an

-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{V}
for each

, and
\mathcal{S}(e)=\ker(\alpha_{e})
for all

\mathcal{S}(v)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(e)

in analogy to what we have done for the motion sheaves. We will now study some of the geometric properties of

, but we first need a simple fact.

Lemma 2.19
.

Let
s<n/2
. The map

Sum:\textup{Gr}(s,n)\times\textup{Gr}(s,n)\rightarrow\textup{Gr}(2s,n):(V_{1},V_{2})\mapsto V_{1}+V_{2}

is a rational map.

Proof.

The map is defined on the subset
O\subseteq\textup{Gr}(s,n)\times\textup{Gr}(s,n)
of pairs
(V_{1},V_{2})
such that
V_{1}\cap V_{2}=0
, which is Zariski open by

2.13
. Now, using Plücker coordinates, one has that the map is given by

((v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k}),(w_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge w_{k}))\mapsto(v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k}\wedge w_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge w_{k})

and it is easy to see that, in terms of the Plücker coordinates, this map is given by bihomogeneous polynomials, giving a regular map on
O
.
∎

Lemma 2.20
.

The map
f:Z_{s,n}((n-2s)\cdot\Gamma)\rightarrow M_{s,n}(\Gamma)
given by
((P_{v})_{v\in V},(\alpha_{e})_{e\in E})\mapsto(P_{v})_{v\in V}
gives

the structure of a bundle with fibre being a product of real vector spaces over a Zariski open subset of

, and one has

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{F}\in f^{-1}(\mathcal{M})}\dim\left(H^{0}((n-2s)\cdot\Gamma,\mathcal{F}\right)

\displaystyle=\dim\left(H^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M})\right),

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{F}\in f^{-1}(\mathcal{M})}\dim\left(H^{1}((n-2s)\cdot\Gamma,\mathcal{F}\right)

\displaystyle=\dim\left(H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M})\right).

Moreover, the variety
Z_{s,n}
is irreducible and nonsingular.

Proof.

For
P_{v}
, denote the represented subspace by
S_{v}
. Given
(P_{v})_{v\in V}
, we see that
f^{-1}((P_{v})_{v\in V})
is given by those
((\alpha_{e})_{e\in E},(P_{v})_{v\in V})
, such that
\alpha_{e}\in\textup{Ann}(S_{v}+S_{u})
for
e=uv
. By
[

Applications to graph-of-groups realisations

3.1
Graph-of-groups realisations of hypergraphs

We describe here the approach to rigidity theory developed in
[
29
]
.

Let
G
be a group and let
S(G)
be the lattice of subgroups of
G
.
Given a hypergraph
\Gamma=(V,E)
, a realisation of
\Gamma
as a graph of groups, (or a graph-of-groups realisation) is a function

\begin{array}[]{rccl}\rho:&V\cup E&\rightarrow&S(G)\\
&x&\mapsto&\rho(x).\end{array}

By taking the incidence graph of
\Gamma
and placing the group
\rho(v)
on the vertex representing the vertex
v\in V
,
\rho(e)
on the vertex representing the edge
e\in E
, and
\rho(v\sim e)=\rho(v)\cap\rho(e)
, we obtain a so-called graph of groups. The monomorphisms

\displaystyle i_{v}:

\displaystyle\rho(v\sim e)\rightarrow\rho(v)

\displaystyle i_{e}:

\displaystyle\rho(v\sim e)\rightarrow\rho(e),

are defined by inclusion. We will use the notation
\rho(\Gamma)
to denote a hypergraph together with a realisation
\rho
as a graph of groups. When
\rho(x)
are Lie subgroups for all
x\in V\cup E\cup I
, we will say that
\rho(\Gamma)
is a Lie graph-of-groups realisation.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
I=E(I(\Gamma))
be the set of incidences of a hypergraph
\Gamma
. Define a
motion
of a realisation
\rho(\Gamma)
to be an indexed set of group elements
(\sigma_{x})_{x\in V\cup E\cup I}\in G^{|V|+|E|+|I|}
, such that for every
x\in V\cup E
and
i\in I
with
i=x\sim y
or
i=y\sim x
one has that

\sigma_{i}^{-1}\sigma_{x}\in\rho(x).

(14)

A motion
M=(\sigma_{x})_{x\in V\cup E\cup I}
of a realisation
\rho(\Gamma)
defines a new realisation
\rho^{\prime}(\Gamma)
, obtained by conjugating the groups
\rho(v)
and
\rho(e)
with the corresponding group elements in the motion.
In other words, if
\rho
is a realisation, then
\rho^{M}
is defined as

\begin{array}[]{rccl}\rho^{M}:&\Gamma&\rightarrow&S(G)\\
&v&\mapsto&\rho(v)^{\sigma_{v}}\text{ for vertices},\\
&e&\mapsto&\rho(e)^{\sigma_{e}}\text{ for edges}.\\
\end{array}

When
\rho(\Gamma)
is a Lie graph-of-groups-realisation, one gets a definition of infinitesimal motion in this context. Denote the Lie algebra of
\rho(x)
by
\mathfrak{h}_{x}
, and the Lie algebra of
G
by
\mathfrak{g}
. An infinitesimal motion is defined to be an element

w_{v_{1}},\cdots,w_{v_{n}},w_{e_{1}},\cdots w_{e_{k}},w_{i_{1}}\cdots w_{i_{m}}.

of

\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}_{v_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}_{v_{n}}\times\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}_{e_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}_{e_{k}}\times\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}_{i_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}_{i_{m}}.

such that for any incidence
i=v\sim e
, one has

\displaystyle w_{i}-w_{v}

\displaystyle=0\mod\mathfrak{h}_{v}

\displaystyle w_{i}-w_{e}

\displaystyle=0\mod\mathfrak{h}_{e}

We denote the resulting vector space by
IM_{\rho}
. It can be shown that this is isomorphic to the usual definition
[
29
, Theorem 4.8]
in the case of the Euclidean group. One also has so-called trivial infinitesimal motions. One defines
i:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow IM_{\rho}:w\mapsto[(w,w,\dots,w)]
. The space which is the image of
i
is the space of trivial infinitesimal motions. The following lemma states that when frameworks are ’large enough’, one has that all Lie-algebra elements define motions.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\rho(\Gamma)
be a Lie graph-of-groups realisation. Then, with

i:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow IM_{\rho}:w\mapsto[(w,\dots,w)]

one has

\ker(i)=\bigcap_{x\in V(\Gamma)\cup E(\Gamma)}\mathfrak{h}_{x}.

The following bound was proved in
[
29
]
:

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\rho(\Gamma)
be a Lie graph-of-groups realisation in a group
G
. Then

\displaystyle\dim(IM_{\rho})\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in V\cup E}\dim(G/\rho(x))+\sum_{i\in I}\dim(G/\rho(i))

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=x*y\in I}(\dim(G/\rho(x))+\dim(G/\rho(y)))

This was then used to show that the graph-of-groups realisation satisfy a certain sparsity condition, which gives the Maxwell condition in the case of the Euclidean group.

In the remainder of this paper, we will treat in detail the example which corresponds to bar-joint frameworks, though the model does treat general situations.

3.2
Motion sheaves from graphs of groups

Let
G
be a Lie group. Throughout this section, we fix a Lie graph-of-groups realisation
\rho:V\cup E\rightarrow S(G):x\mapsto\rho(x)
, such that for each hyperedge

\rho(e)=\cap_{v\in e}\rho(v)
. This simplifies the description of the vector space of infinitesimal motions. Indeed, it is easy to show that the resulting vector space of infinitesimal motions of
\rho

\displaystyle(\overline{w_{v_{1}}},\dots,\overline{w_{e_{|E|}}})

\displaystyle\in\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}_{v_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}_{v_{|V|}}\times\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}_{e_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}_{e_{|E|}},

\displaystyle\textup{ such that }w_{e}-w_{v}\in\mathfrak{h}_{v}\text{ if }v\in e.

The assumption that
\rho(e)=\bigcap_{v\in V}\rho(v)
holds in many graph-of-groups realisations arising from combinatorial-geometric problems, with a notable exception being projective rigidity. The results in the current subsection are, in fact, easily adaptable to more general Lie-graph-of-groups realisations (i.e., those where
\rho(e)\neq\bigcap_{v\in V}\rho(v)

3.3
Genericity of infinitesimal rigidity for graph-of-groups realisations.

The goal of this section is to show that the motion sheaves arising from graph-of-groups realisations can be seen as smooth semi-algebraic subsets of
M_{s,n}
. Applying the semi-continuity result from
Theorem

2.15
will then imply that their rigidity and independence are generic properties. For clarity, when given an algebraic group
G
defined over
\mathbb{R}
, we shall use
G(\mathbb{R})
to denote the (Lie) group of real points. Given an algebraic group
G
defined over
\mathbb{R}
, by an algebraic group action of
G(\mathbb{R})
on a real algebraic variety
X
, we mean an action
G(\mathbb{R})\times X\rightarrow X
that is given by regular functions (i.e. by polynomials).

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an irreducible semi-algebraic set which is also a smooth manifold. Then, if
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Zariski open set, then either
U\cap X
is empty, or
U\cap X
is dense and open for the Euclidean topology on
X
.

Proof.

Let
V=X\setminus U
, which is defined by some polynomial
p
, i.e.
V=\{x\in X~|~p(x)=0\}

[
3
, Proposition 2.1.3.]
. We see that
V
is a Zariski closed subset of
X
, and hence by irreducibility, either
V=X
(in which case
U\cap X=\emptyset
), or
V
is semi-algebraic subset of dimension
k<\dim(X)
. In the latter case, since
X
is a smooth manifold, for every
x\in V
and every open ball
O\subseteq X
(for the Euclidean topology) which contains
x
,
O\cap V\neq O
. Indeed, otherwise the local dimension satisfies
\dim(V_{x})=\dim(X)
by
[
3
, Proposition 2.8.14]
, which is a contradiction since
\dim(V_{x})\leq k<\dim(X)
.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
G
be an algebraic group defined over
\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that
\psi:G(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\textup{Gl}(\mathbb{V})
is a representation of a real algebraic group, i.e. the map
G(\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
is regular and
\psi(g)
is a linear map for each
g\in G
. Then
G(\mathbb{R})
acts algebraically on
\textup{Gr}(s,\mathbb{V})
.

Proof.

One can use Plücker coordinates and apply the same reasoning as in
[
22
, Theorem 10.19]
.
∎

If we take
G
to be a Zariski-closed subgroup of
\textup{Gl}(n,\mathbb{R})
, one has that the adjoint action of
G(\mathbb{R})
on
\mathfrak{g}\leq\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
is simply given by

\textup{Ad}(g)\cdot X=gXg^{-1}.

This action is clearly defined using polynomial functions, and hence we get an algebraic action on
\textup{Gr}(s,\mathfrak{g})
. The orbit of any
\mathfrak{h}\leq\mathfrak{g}
is thus a semi-algebraic subset of

\textup{Gr}(s,\mathfrak{g})
. We can describe classes of motion sheaves arising from graph-of-groups realisations in this way.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
G
be a Lie group and let
H
be a closed Lie subgroup. Given a hypergraph
\Gamma
, we define
C_{G,H}
to be the class of graph-of-groups realisations such that for every
v\in V
and
e\in E
, one has:

\displaystyle\rho(v)

\displaystyle=g_{v}Hg_{v}^{-1}\textup{ for some }g_{v}\in G,

\displaystyle\rho(e)

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{v\in e}\rho(v).

We can identify
C_{G,H}
with
(G/N_{G}(H))^{V}
, where
N_{G}(H)=\{g\in G~|~gHg^{-1}=H\}
, which gives
C_{G,H}
the structure of a smooth manifold.

Suppose that we are given
\rho\in C_{G,H}
. Then the motion sheaf associated to
\rho
is defined using a sheaf
\mathcal{S}
such that for every
v
one has

\mathcal{S}(v)=\textup{Ad}(g_{v})\mathfrak{h},

where
\mathfrak{h}
is the Lie algebra of
H
. We can hence describe the set of motion sheaves as those motion sheaves belonging to
(G\cdot\mathfrak{h})^{V}\subseteq\textup{Gr}(s,\mathfrak{g})
, where
G\cdot\mathfrak{h}
is the orbit of
\mathfrak{h}
under the adjoint action and
s=\dim(\mathfrak{h})
. Letting
N_{G}(\mathfrak{h})=\{g\in G~|~\textup{Ad}(g)(\mathfrak{h})=\mathfrak{h}\}
, one can equivalently describe this set as the image of the injective natural map

\displaystyle(G/N_{G}(\mathfrak{h}))^{V}\rightarrow\textup{Gr}(s,\mathfrak{g})^{|V|}:(g_{v}N_{G}(\mathfrak{h}))_{v\in V}\mapsto(\textup{Ad}(g_{v})(\mathfrak{h}))_{v\in V}.

In the case that
H
is connected, the following lemma shows that one can replace
N_{G}(\mathfrak{h})
by
N_{G}(H)
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Suppose that
G
is a Lie group, and
H
is a connected closed Lie subgroup. Then
N_{G}(\mathfrak{h})=N_{G}(H)
.

Proof.

Since
H
is connected, any element of
H
can be written as a product
e^{X_{1}},\dots,e^{X_{n}}
, where
X\mapsto e^{X}
denotes the exponential map. Thus, if for elements
X_{i}\in\mathfrak{h}
one has
ge^{X_{i}}g^{-1}=e^{X_{i}^{\prime}}
for some
X_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{h}
, then
gHg^{-1}=H
. Since
ge^{X_{i}}g^{-1}=e^{(\textup{Ad}(g)(X_{i}))}
(by naturality of the exponential map), it follows that
N_{G}(\mathfrak{h})\subseteq N_{G}(H)
. Since the reverse inclusion always holds, this completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
\Gamma
be a hypergraph. Let
G\subseteq Gl_{n}
be a connected linear algebraic group defined over
\mathbb{R}
, and let
H\subseteq G(\mathbb{R})
be a closed and connected subgroup (for the Euclidean topology). Then, there is a dense open subset
U\subseteq C_{G(\mathbb{R}),H}
(for the Euclidean topology) such that for all
\rho\in U
, the associated motion sheaves
h^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M})
and
h^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M})
have the same constant dimension on
U
.

Proof.

Let
X=G(\mathbb{R})\cdot\mathfrak{h}
be the semi-algebraic subset of
\textup{Gr}(s,\mathfrak{g})
. One sees that
X
is irreducible, being the image of an irreducible subset under the regular map
G(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\textup{Gr}(s,\mathfrak{g}):g\mapsto\textup{Ad}(g)\mathfrak{h}
, and
X^{|V|}
is irreducible as well
[
3
, Theorem 2.8.3.(iii)]
.
By
Lemma

2.13
, we see that there is a Zariski open subset of
O\subseteq X^{|V|}
such that
\dim(\bigcap_{v\in e}A_{v})
is minimal for all
e\in E
and
(A_{1},\dots,A_{|V|})\in O
. By
Theorem

2.15
, we see that there is a nonempty Zariski open subset
U
of
O
, such that
h^{0}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M})
and
h^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M})
are minimal on
U
.

Since
H
is connected, by
Proposition

3.9
and by the discussion above, the map

\varphi:(G(\mathbb{R})/N_{G(\mathbb{R})}(H))^{|V|}\rightarrow\textup{Gr}(s,\mathfrak{g})^{|V|}:(g_{v}N_{G(\mathbb{R})}(H))_{v\in V}\rightarrow(\textup{Ad}(g_{v})(\mathfrak{h}))_{v\in V}

is a bijective map onto
X^{|V|}
, and by generalities on orbits, it is an immersion (see
[
23
, Theorem 3.29]
). Note that there exists an
x\in X^{|V|}
and some
W\subseteq M_{s,n}(\Gamma)
which is open for the Euclidean topology such that
x\in W
and
W\cap X^{|V|}
is a smooth manifold (this follows since
X^{|V|}
admits a Nash stratification,
[
3
, Definition 9.1.7, Proposition 9.1.8]
). Then, since for any
y\in X^{|V|}
, there exists a
g\in G^{|V|}
such that
y=g\cdot x
, we see that
X^{|V|}\cap gW
is a manifold. Since this holds for arbitrary

, we see that
X^{|V|}
is an embedded submanifold.

It follows that
\varphi
is an embedding. The set
U\subseteq X^{|V|}
is dense and open with respect to the Euclidean topology by Proposition
3.6
, since
X^{|V|}
is smooth and irreducible. Hence, since
\varphi
is an embedding,
\varphi^{-1}(U)
satisfies the conclusion of the theorem.
∎

The above theorem may be reformulated as rigidity and independence being generic properties for motion sheaves coming from graphs of groups, when
G
is connected.
In the case where

is a Lie group,
\dim(H)=1
, and if

is finite, the closure of the image

is particularly well-behaved.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph. Let
G
be a Lie group, and suppose that
H\subseteq G
is a
1
-dimensional connected subgroup such that

is finite. Then, for any graph
\Gamma
, there is a dense open subset of

such that

is
(\dim(G)-1,\dim(G))

Proof.

We have that
\textup{Gr}(1,\mathfrak{g})
is a projective space, which we denote by

. The map

is an immersion, and since
\dim(G/N_{G}(H))=\dim(P(\mathfrak{g}))
, it is an embedding
[

. Thus,

is also an embedding, and
\varphi(C_{G,H}^{|V|})
is hence an open subset for the Euclidean topology on
\textup{Gr}(1,\mathfrak{g})
. By Theorem
2.6
and Proposition

, since

is smooth, we see that for every graph
\Gamma
, there is a dense open subset

such that
H^{1}(I(\Gamma),\mathcal{M})=0
if and only if
(n-2)\cdot\Gamma
is
(n-1,n)
-tight. Now, since it is easy to show that for any open set

, one has that

is dense and open in

, we see that
\varphi^{-1}(U)
is open in
C_{G,H}^{V}
.
∎

One can thus take

, and
any

1
-dimensional connected subgroup

such that

is finite and get a characterisation. If

is not connected, taking the connected component

of

leads to the same notions of local rigidity and infinitesimal rigidity, so even when

is not connected, one gets a characterisation (granted that

If one takes

and

Taking

4.1
Inductive constructions for Euclidean rigidity

We make some remarks about how
Theorem

2.28
applies to graph-of-groups realisations coming from bar-joint frameworks. In
[
29
]
, it was noticed that
3
distinct points
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
are collinear if and only if
\mathfrak{h}_{x}+\mathfrak{h}_{y}+\mathfrak{h}_{z}
has dimension
2
, where
\mathfrak{h}_{x}
is the Lie algebra of the stabiliser of
x
. This generalises this to arbitrary dimension.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
, and let
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be distinct points. Let
\mathfrak{h}_{x},\mathfrak{h}_{y},\mathfrak{h}_{z}\subseteq\mathfrak{e}(d)
be the stabilisers of these points. The points
x,y,z
are collinear if and only if
\mathfrak{h}_{x}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}_{y}+\mathfrak{h}_{z}

Proof.

Suppose
x,y,z
are collinear. Then one can write
x=\lambda_{1}y+\lambda_{2}z
, where
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=1
. Let
\Omega
be a skew-symmetric matrix. One then has

\lambda_{1}\begin{bmatrix}\Omega&-\Omega\cdot y\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}+\lambda_{2}\begin{bmatrix}\Omega&-\Omega\cdot z\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Omega&-\Omega\cdot x\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},

which shows clearly that
\mathfrak{h}_{x}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}_{y}+\mathfrak{h}_{z}
. This shows ’only if’. For ’if’, we first note that without loss of generality we may take
y=0
and
z=(1,0,\dots,0).
We see that elements of
\mathfrak{h}_{y}+\mathfrak{h}_{z}
are of the form

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Omega&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}+\lambda_{2}\begin{bmatrix}\Omega^{\prime}&-\Omega^{\prime}\cdot z\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}

The part
-\Omega^{\prime}\cdot z
will be of the form
[0,a_{1},\dots,a_{n}]
. Now for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{d})
, with at least some
x_{i}\neq 0
for
i\neq 1
, one defines the skew-symmetric matrix
\Omega_{i}

(\Omega_{i})_{k,\ell}=\begin{cases}1&\textup{ if }k=1,\ell=i\\
-1&\textup{ if }k=i,\ell=1\\
0&\textup{ otherwise }\end{cases}.

It is then clear that
\begin{bmatrix}\Omega_{i}&-\Omega_{i}x\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\notin\mathfrak{h}_{y}+\mathfrak{h}_{z}
, but it is clearly an element of
\mathfrak{h}_{x}
, completing the proof.
∎

Using

4.1
and

, it is easy to show that the

-extension works in arbitrary dimensions, and the construction basically follows the usual argument. From

, it also follows that one cannot apply

to understand the

-extension in

In this final section, we show that the class of motion sheaves arising from parallel-redrawings problems leads to a very well-behaved class of problems inside of

Let

be the group generated by dilations and translations. Using homogeneous matrices, group elements

\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&0&\dots&0&x_{1}\\
0&\lambda&\dots&0&x_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&\lambda&x_{n}\\
0&0&\dots&0&1\\
\end{bmatrix}\textup{ where }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{*},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of this group consists of
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrices

\begin{bmatrix}t&0&\dots&0&x_{1}\\
0&t&\dots&0&x_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&t&x_{n}\\
0&0&\dots&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\textup{ where }t,x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}

We will do computations in this algebra using the basis

\begin{array}[]{clcl}L&=\begin{bmatrix}1&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&1&0\\
0&\dots&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix},&T_{i}&=\begin{bmatrix}0&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&0&0\\
0&\dots&0&1\\
0&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix},\end{array}

where the nonzero row in
T_{i}
is the
i
-th row, and
i\in\{1,\dots n\}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Any vector-subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
is a subalgebra.

Proof.

We compute using the basis
L,T_{1},\dots T_{n}
defined above. We remark that

\displaystyle[L,T_{i}]

\displaystyle=T_{i}\textup{ for all }i\in\{1,\dots,n\}

\displaystyle[T_{i},T_{j}]

\displaystyle=0\textup{ for all }i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

Now, let
A\leq\mathfrak{g}
be a vector-subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
. If
A\leq\textup{Span}(T_{1},\dots,T_{n})
,
[X,Y]=0
for all
X,Y\in A
. We may thus write
A=\textup{span}(X_{1},\dots X_{s})
, and without loss of generality

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle=L+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{1i}T_{i}

\displaystyle X_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ji}T_{i}\text{ for }j\in\{2,\dots s\}

Now, since

\displaystyle[X_{1},X_{j}]

\displaystyle=X_{j}\textup{ for all }j\in\{2,\dots,s\}

\displaystyle[X_{i},X_{j}]

\displaystyle=0\textup{ for all }i,j\in\{2,\dots,s\},

the conclusion clearly holds.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Any subalgebra
A
that contains an infinitesimal generator of a dilation, given by
D=L+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}T_{i}
, is the Lie algebra of the stabiliser of an affine subspace of
L_{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
of dimension
\dim(A)-1
. Moreover, for such subspaces
L_{A+B}=L_{A}\vee L_{B}
, where
L_{A}\vee L_{B}
is the smallest affine subspace containing both
L_{A}
and
L_{B}
and
L_{A\cap B}=L_{A}\cap L_{B}
, whenever
A\cap B
contains a dilation.

Proof.

We can choose a basis containing
D
and infinitesimal generators of translations
\tau_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ki}T_{i},
for
k\in\{1,\dots s\}
. Letting
P_{D}=(-a_{1},\dots,-a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one has that
D\cdot P_{D}=0
, so the element
D
acts trivially on
P_{D}
under the infinitesimal action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=(a_{11},\dots,a_{1n})

\displaystyle v_{s}

\displaystyle=(a_{s1},\dots,a_{sn}).

We then let
L_{A}:=P_{D}+\textup{Span}(v_{1},\dots v_{s})
. This is an affine subspace of dimension
s=\dim(A)-1
. We additionally define
v_{0}=(a_{1},\dots a_{n})
.
Now, one notes that
A
is the Lie algebra of the subgroup generated by the matrices

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\lambda\textup{Id}_{n}&(\lambda-1)v_{0}^{T}+t_{1}v_{1}^{T}+\dots+t_{s}v_{s}^{T}\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\textup{ where }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{*},t_{1},\dots,t_{s}\in\mathbb{R}

This subgroup is precisely the stabiliser of
L_{A}
, and we note that it consists precisely of the dilations fixing a point of
L_{A}
, and translations in the direction of
L_{A}
(i.e., translations with vectors in
\textup{Span}(v_{1},\dots v_{s})
). To finish the proof, we prove that
L_{A\cap B}=L_{A}\cap L_{B}
and
L_{A+B}=L_{A}\vee L_{B}
. We first note that by the description of the stabilisers above, one has that
L_{A}\subseteq X
if and only if
A
fixes
X
. Hence,
A\subseteq B
, if and only if one has
L_{A}\subseteq L_{B}
. The claim about the sum and intersection then follows easily.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

By associating to subalgebras containing only translations, the space of points at infinity giving the directions of the translations, one obtains using the construction in
Lemma

4.3
, a lattice isomorphism between subspaces of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}
.

We can use these observations to describe various ’parallel redrawings’ type problems using graphs of groups.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be an
r
-regular hypergraph. A realisation of
\Gamma
as a subspace arrangement of
\Gamma
with vertex dimensions
s
, is an assignment

r:V\rightarrow\textup{Gr}(s+1,\mathbb{R}^{n+1}):v\mapsto r(v),

such that
r(v)
is an
s
-dimensional affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
for each
v\in V
. One defines
r(e):=\bigvee_{v\in e}L_{v}
for
e\in E
. One says that two realisations
r,r^{\prime}
are parallel redrawings of each other if
r(e)
is parallel to
r^{\prime}(e)
for every
e\in E
.

To any vertex-regular subspace arrangement
r
, we assign a Lie graph-of-groups realisation
\rho_{r}(\Gamma)
by setting

\displaystyle v\mapsto\textup{Stab}(r(v))

\displaystyle e\mapsto\cap_{v\in e}\textup{Stab}(r(v)).

One can show, in a completely analogous way as in
[
29
, Theorem 4.3]
, that there is a
1-1
correspondence between subspace arrangements
r
of vertex dimension
s
and graph-of-groups realisations given by
\rho_{r}
and
r
is a parallel redrawing of
r^{\prime}
if and only if there exists a motion
M
of
\rho_{r}
such that
M(\rho_{r})=\rho_{r^{\prime}}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

The set of motion sheaves
\mathcal{M}\in\textup{Sh}(I(\Gamma))
that come from a graph-of-groups realisation
\rho_{r}
, for
r
a subspace arrangement of vertex dimension
s
is dense in
\textup{Gr}(s+1,\mathfrak{g})
.

Proof.

We again use the basis
L,T_{1},\dots,T_{n}
for
\mathfrak{g}
. We note that a subspace
A\in\textup{Gr}(s+1,\mathfrak{g})
is a stabiliser of an affine subspace if and only if
A\cap\langle T_{1},\dots T_{n}\rangle\neq A
by
Lemma

4.3
and
Lemma

4.2
. Since this defines a Zariski open subset of
\textup{Gr}(s+1,\mathfrak{g})
, the lemma follows.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph, and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For almost all realisations of
\Gamma
as a subspace arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with vertex dimension
0
, one has that the associated motion sheaf satisfies
h^{1}(\mathcal{M})=0
if and only if
(n-2)\Gamma
is
(n,n+1)
sparse.

Proof.

Follows from
Theorem

2.6
and
Lemma

4.6
.
∎

Corollary

4.7
yields a characterisation for so-called parallel rigidity, and it had already been proven (see
[
38

,
[
17

2.26
