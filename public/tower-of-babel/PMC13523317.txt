Hybrid deep learning ensemble model for detecting small to medium rotator cuff tears from shoulder radiographs
Authors: Shun-Wun Jhan, Tian-Hsiang Huang, Jai-Hong Cheng, Kuan-Ting Wu, Wen-Yi Chou, Jinn-Tsong Tsai, Wen-Hsien Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRotator cuff tears (RCTs) represent a common orthopedic condition, the diagnosis of which often requires advanced imaging techniques. Notably, plain radiography may not accurately differentiate between small to medium RCTs and other shoulder pathologies. Deep learning (DL) models may facilitate preliminary assessment of RCTs, helping avoid unnecessary advanced imaging and expediting patient care.MethodsFor this study, we used a dataset comprising 587 shoulder radiographs (339 from patients with small to medium RCTs and 248 from without RCTs). The study dataset was divided into a training set (406 images [69% of the dataset]) and a validation set (101 images [17% of the dataset]). In addition, an independent test set consisting of 80 radiographs (39 with small to medium RCTs and 41 without RCTs [14% of the dataset]) was used for external validation of the model performance. The test set was strictly held out and not used during model training or model selection. Three pretrained DL models (ResNet-50, ResNet-101, and InceptionV3) were fine-tuned using transfer learning. These three models were integrated using a majority voting strategy to develop a hybrid DL ensemble model.ResultsOn the validation set, the hybrid DL ensemble model outperformed the individual models in classifying rotator cuff images, achieving an accuracy of 78% (95% CI 69–86), a precision of 79% (68–88), a recall (sensitivity) of 87% (77–95), a specificity of 66% (51–80), an F1-score of 0.83 (0.74–0.89) and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.85 (0.76–0.92). On the independent test set, the hybrid DL ensemble model achieved an accuracy of 81% (73–90), a precision of 72% (60–84), a recall of 100% (100–100), a specificity of 63% (48–78), an F1-score of 0.84 (0.75–0.91), and an AUROC of 0.93 (0.87–0.97).ConclusionOur hybrid DL ensemble model can detect small to medium RCTs from shoulder radiographs. Thus, it holds promise as a cost-effective and efficient tool for preliminary assessment of RCTs. This artificial intelligence-based approach has the potential to improve diagnostic reliability and assist clinical decision-making as a screening and triage support tool for RCTs.Level of evidence IIIRetrospective study.

Introduction

A rotator cuff tear (RCT) is a common orthopedic condition characterized by symptoms such as pain, restricted movement, and shoulder dysfunction. The global prevalence of RCTs is approximately 20.7%, and it has been increasing as global populations age [1, 2]. RCTs account for 36% of all shoulder pathology cases [1]. For symptomatic patients with suspected RCTs, a thorough physical examination (PE) is the primary diagnostic approach. However, physicians often struggle to accurately detect RCTs in patients with shoulder pain. PE remains a fundamental step in the evaluation of patients with suspected RCTs. Although PE is generally sensitive, its specificity is limited, and no single test reliably differentiates RCTs from other shoulder disorders [3, 4]. Consequently, combining multiple tests has been suggested to enhance diagnostic accuracy, but even such approaches are imperfect [5].

Plain radiography is a common diagnostic tool that can provide valuable insights into clinical features such as critical shoulder angle, acromiohumeral interval (AHI), acromial tilt, subacromial spur, and tuberosity sclerosis [6–8]. In addition, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and arthroscopy are required for definitive diagnosis of RCTs. However, these procedures are time-consuming and costly. In addition, distinguishing RCTs from conditions such as rotator cuff tendinitis and early-stage adhesive capsulitis can be challenging. Although plain radiography may readily reveal large to massive RCTs, except for acute traumatic tears, thereby facilitating clinical decisions, this technique alone is insufficient for diagnosing small to medium RCTs.

Although definitive management decisions for RCTs typically rely on advanced imaging modalities such as MRI, plain radiographs remain one of the most widely available and cost-effective tools in the initial evaluation of shoulder pain. An automated artificial intelligence-based (AI-based) system trained to interpret radiographs could serve as an adjunctive tool in this diagnostic pathway, helping clinicians identify patients who may warrant further investigation with MRI or earlier referral to specialists. Nevertheless, few AI models have been developed to detect RCTs from plain radiographs, particularly for small to medium tears.

Recently, deep learning (DL) has emerged as a promising approach to interpreting shoulder radiographs. Kim et al. developed a DL model capable of excluding major RCTs [9]. This model exhibited a high recall (sensitivity) value and a negative predictive value. It effectively reduced reliance on advanced imaging techniques, such as MRI, for approximately 30% of cases of suspected RCTs. Additionally, Lio et al. constructed a DL model and trained it using shoulder radiographs [10]. The model exhibited strong performance in detecting full-thickness RCTs. Although the aforementioned studies highlight the potential of DL models for the diagnosis of shoulder pathologies, the use of such models for detecting small to medium RCTs, which often exhibit subtle or nonspecific radiographic features, remains underexplored. The present study evaluated the efficacy of DL models in detecting small to medium RCTs, for which conventional visual assessment of radiographs may be insufficient, highlighting a need for AI-based tools to support early and accurate diagnosis. DL, a subdomain of AI that is inspired by biological neural networks, enables computers to learn from data and make predictions. A typical artificial neural network comprises an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. Iterative training with optimization algorithms such as gradient descent can help adjust model weights to minimize prediction errors, enabling the model to learn patterns and apply them to new data. Among various DL architectures, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have proven effective in medical image analysis because of their ability to extract hierarchical spatial features [11]. Transfer learning has gained popularity in contemporary medical AI research, wherein CNNs pretrained on large datasets such as ImageNet are fine-tuned on domain-specific medical images to enhance performance on relatively small datasets [12, 13].

In this study, we developed a hybrid DL ensemble model to diagnose RCTs from shoulder radiographs. The hybrid model combines multiple pretrained DL models by using a majority voting strategy, thereby mitigating inconsistencies in predictions made by individual models. In essence, the hybrid model yields results similar to a consensus-driven diagnosis by medical experts, ensuring reliable and accurate image classification [14]. We hypothesized that a hybrid DL ensemble model trained on shoulder radiographs could improve the detection of small to medium RCTs, where conventional radiographic interpretation may be insufficient.

Methods

Study design

The study protocol included the following steps (Fig. 1): collecting and processing radiographic data, selecting pretrained DL models and corresponding hyperparameters for transfer learning, fine-tuning and training the pretrained models for image classification, evaluating the classification performance of various trained models, constructing a hybrid ensemble model from the individual models, and comparing classification performance among the models.

Fig. 1Overview of the study workflow for developing a deep learning-based hybrid ensemble model to detect rotator cuff tears from shoulder plain radiographies

Overview of the study workflow for developing a deep learning-based hybrid ensemble model to detect rotator cuff tears from shoulder plain radiographies

To develop and evaluate the models, a tripartite dataset partitioning strategy was implemented, consisting of training, validation, and independent test sets. During model development, gradients were computed and network weights were updated exclusively using the training set. The validation set remained independent throughout the training process and did not participate in weight updates; instead, it served as an unbiased benchmark for monitoring model performance, facilitating model selection, and preventing overfitting. After the model architecture was finalized and training was completed, the independent test set was used only once to provide the final unbiased evaluation of classification performance, including accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1-score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC).

The experiments were performed using a computer equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX3090-24GB GPU and an Intel i7 CPU running MATLAB R2024b and its associated toolbox (MathWorks).

Collection and processing of radiographic data

From a cohort of individuals who underwent shoulder arthroscopic procedures, including rotator cuff repair, arthroscopic debridement, capsular release, and acromioplasty, between 2020 and 2023 at a tertiary medical center in Taiwan, we selected individuals with either an intact rotator cuff or small to medium RCTs, as confirmed intraoperatively. RCT size was classified as follows: small, 0–1 cm; medium, 1–3 cm; large, 3–5 cm; and massive, > 5 cm (involving two or more tendons). Individuals were excluded if they were aged < 18 years, had a previous history of shoulder surgery, or had large to massive RCTs. All included individuals had preoperative shoulder radiographs (anteroposterior view) available for analysis. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (IRB No. 202500798B0).

The radiographs were obtained using a standard anteroposterior shoulder projection in routine clinical practice. Patients were positioned in the upright or standing position with the arm in a neutral position alongside the body. The image receptor was placed posterior to the shoulder, and the X-ray beam was directed from anterior to posterior, centered on the glenohumeral joint. A source-to-image distance of approximately 100–120 cm was used according to institutional protocols. As this was a retrospective study, minor variations in acquisition parameters may have occurred; however, all images were obtained under standardized clinical conditions.

Once all shoulder radiographs were obtained (Fig. 2a), the images were preprocessed to reduce the risk of misclassification by the AI model. Each image was cropped to a smaller, anatomically relevant region encompassing the proximal humerus, glenohumeral joint, subacromial space, acromioclavicular joint, distal clavicle, and coracoid process (Fig. 2b). This step ensured that the model received consistent input highlighting key anatomical landmarks for analysis. To standardize the orientation of left and right shoulder images, all images were horizontally flipped, ensuring the humeral head appeared on the same side across the dataset before model input (Fig. 2c).

The original radiographs were exported from the hospital Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) in DICOM format and converted into PNG format for model input, with a typical resolution of approximately 2812 × 2592 pixels. Image cropping was performed prior to resizing, and no additional intensity normalization, windowing, or contrast adjustment was applied.

All input images were resized after cropping to meet the specific input requirements of the selected architectures (224 × 224 pixels for ResNet-50 and ResNet-101, and 299 × 299 pixels for InceptionV3) using bicubic interpolation [15, 16].

After preprocessing, all shoulder radiographs were divided into two categories on the basis of intraoperative arthroscopic findings: tendon rupture, corresponding to full-thickness RCTs, and normal, corresponding to intact rotator cuffs. Representative examples of each category are presented in Fig. 3. These images were used to train and evaluate each DL model.

The study dataset comprised shoulder radiographs from 587 individuals who met the inclusion criteria. Intraoperative arthroscopic findings revealed that 339 patients had tendon rupture and 248 had normal rotator cuffs. The shoulder radiographs (anteroposterior view) were used for model construction. As shown in Table 1, the dataset was divided into a training set (406 images [69% of the dataset]: tendon rupture, 240 images; normal, 166 images), a validation set (101 images [17% of the dataset]: tendon rupture, 60 images; normal, 41 images), and an independent test set (80 images [14% of the dataset]: tendon rupture, 39 images; normal, 41 images) for external validation of model performance. To prevent data leakage, patient-level separation was strictly enforced during dataset partitioning. All radiographs from the same patient were assigned exclusively to a single subset (training, validation, or independent test set), ensuring that no patient appeared in more than one dataset. This approach ensures that correlated images from the same patient do not appear in multiple subsets, thereby providing a more reliable estimate of model performance.

To enhance the generalization ability of the models for detecting RCTs from shoulder radiographs and to prevent overfitting, we implemented comprehensive data augmentation techniques during the training phase. Specifically, geometric transformations were applied to the training dataset, which included enabling random horizontal reflection along the vertical axis and random translations within a range of – 30 to 30 pixels for both X and Y axes. Furthermore, images were randomly scaled between 0.9 and 1.1 in both horizontal and vertical dimensions [16].

This augmentation strategy helps prevent the network from memorizing the exact details of the training images [16], thereby improving its robustness to minor variations in the positioning of small-to-medium tears. In contrast, the validation and test sets underwent only image resizing without any random augmentation to ensure an unbiased and objective evaluation of the model’s performance.

Selection of pretrained DL models and corresponding hyperparameters for transfer learning

The selection of DL models (ResNet-50, ResNet-101, and InceptionV3 as the backbone architectures) for this study was informed by their established performance in musculoskeletal and radiological imaging. ResNet [17] architectures leverage a residual learning framework that effectively addresses the degradation problem in deep networks, facilitating the extraction of subtle structural features and cortical irregularities in shoulder radiographs—a capability validated by previous studies on RCT detection [18–20]. InceptionV3 [21] was selected for its multi-scale convolutional modules, which integrate features from varying receptive fields. This spatial versatility is particularly advantageous for detecting lesions with high morphological variability, such as shoulder pathologies [22, 23].

The aforementioned models were trained on the study dataset, and their algorithmic hyperparameters were configured before the learning phase. The primary hyperparameters were as follows: Optimizer (training algorithm), MiniBatchSize (minimum batch size: the number of samples per minibatch in each iteration), MaxEpochs (maximum number of training epochs), InitialLearnRate (initial learning rate, which may be adjusted during the training phase), LearnRateSchedule (learning rate schedule: strategy for reducing the learning rate over time, for example, periodic adjustments), LearnRateDropPeriod (number of epochs after which the learning rate decreases), and LearnRateDropFactor (multiplicative factor used to reduce the learning rate at each drop). The algorithmic hyperparameters for the ResNet-50, ResNet-101, and InceptionV3 models were set as follows: Optimizer, adaptive moment estimation (Adam); MiniBatchSize, 25; MaxEpochs, 50; InitialLearnRate, 1⋅10−4; LearnRateSchedule, piecewise; LearnRateDropPeriod, 10; and LearnRateDropFactor, 0.8 [24–26].

Transfer learning repurposes a model trained on one task as the foundation for another. The process involves three steps: selecting a relevant pretrained model, repurposing it into a new framework, and fine-tuning it for specific requirements. By leveraging previously learned features, this approach significantly accelerates development and allows for further refinement based on new data [27].

Fine-tuning and training of pretrained models

This study implemented a transfer learning strategy by leveraging ResNet-50, ResNet-101, and InceptionV3 architectures pretrained on the ImageNet dataset [28, 29]. To adapt these models for the binary classification of RCTs, the original fully connected and classification output layers were replaced with task-specific layers. To preserve the robust feature extraction capabilities learned from natural images and mitigate overfitting on medical datasets, we employed weight freezing on the initial convolutional layers, ensuring that only specialized layers underwent fine-tuning [30].

The hyperparameters in this study were determined through a combination of empirical tuning and preliminary experiments specifically tailored for transfer learning, based on the authors’ previous research [24–26]. The Adam optimizer was selected for its adaptive learning rate capabilities, providing robust convergence efficiency in medical diagnostic tasks. An initial learning rate of 1⋅10−4 was adopted, complemented by a piecewise decay schedule that reduced the learning rate by 20% every 10 epochs to facilitate precise fine-tuning of the pre-trained architectures, including ResNet-50, ResNet-101, and InceptionV3. A batch size of 25 was chosen to balance the stability of the gradient estimate with the memory constraints of the GPU hardware environment. Fine-tuning involved training the deeper layers of these models on the study dataset, thereby enabling the models to specialize in detecting RCTs from shoulder radiographs.

During the fine-tuning of the pretrained models, the validation set was utilized to monitor classification accuracy and loss values. In DL practice, the point where the validation loss stops decreasing and the accuracy plateaus indicates that the model has reached optimal convergence; continuing training beyond this point would lead to overfitting on the training data. Through multiple preliminary experiments, it was observed that ResNet-50, ResNet-101, and InceptionV3 consistently reached a stable performance plateau between 40 and 50 epochs on the shoulder radiograph dataset. Consequently, we specified a maximum of 50 epochs to ensure robust weight refinement and stable convergence across all architectures while maintaining computational efficiency. This setting, combined with the piecewise learning rate decay every 10 epochs, ensured that the models achieved a global minimum without succumbing to overfitting [24–26].

Performance metrics

The performance of a model can be evaluated by measuring various parameters, such as accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1-score, and AUROC. Accuracy is calculated as the proportion of true results (both true-positive and true-negative results) to all results. The confusion matrix is a common tool for evaluating classification performance; it presents model predictions in a matrix format, facilitating comparison between the ground truth labels and the predicted results. This matrix provides a detailed breakdown of correct and incorrect predictions across categories and is thus useful for performance evaluation. Precision, a key performance metric in information retrieval tasks, indicates the relevance of results. It is calculated as the positive predictive value (the number of true-positive results divided by the sum of true-positive and false-positive results). Another measure of information retrieval performance is recall, which is calculated as the rate of true-positive results (the number of true-positive results divided by the sum of true-positive and false-negative results). Specificity is calculated as the rate of true-negative results (number of true-negative results divided by the sum of false-positive and true-negative results). F1-score, which combines precision and recall, indicates prediction accuracy in the presence of class imbalance. In the present study, F1-score was calculated using the following formula: 2 ⋅ (precision ⋅ recall) / (precision + recall) [31]. In addition, the discriminative ability of the models was evaluated using the AUROC. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve plots sensitivity against 1 − specificity across different classification thresholds, and the AUROC provides a threshold-independent measure of the model’s overall ability to distinguish between tendon rupture and normal cases [32].

The uncertainty of the performance metrics was estimated using bootstrap resampling [33]. Specifically, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using 5000 bootstrap iterations, in which the evaluation dataset was repeatedly sampled with replacement and the corresponding performance metrics were computed.

Construction of a hybrid DL ensemble model

Trained ResNet-50, ResNet-101, and InceptionV3 models were combined to construct a hybrid DL ensemble model. The hybrid model integrated the outputs of the individual models by using a majority voting strategy (Fig. 4).

Comparison of classification performance among the models

The classification performance was compared among the four models: the ResNet-50, ResNet-101, and InceptionV3 models and the hybrid ensemble model. The following performance metrics were evaluated: accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1-score and AUROC.

To further investigate threshold selection, model-specific optimal thresholds were derived from the ROC curves on the validation set by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1) [34]. The resulting optimal thresholds were 0.98 for ResNet-50, 0.82 for ResNet-101, 0.89 for InceptionV3, and 0.83 for the hybrid ensemble model. Importantly, threshold determination was performed exclusively on the validation set.

Comparison of the fixed threshold and the model-specific optimal thresholds on the validation set showed that threshold adjustment mainly affected the balance between recall and specificity. For example, when the optimal threshold was applied to the hybrid ensemble model, specificity increased from 66% (95% CI 51–80) to 78% (65–90), whereas recall decreased from 87% (77–95) to 80% (69–89). Similar trade-offs were observed across the individual models. Notably, AUROC values remained unchanged, indicating that threshold selection shifted the operating point without altering the intrinsic discriminative ability of the models.

Considering the intended clinical role of the proposed AI system as a screening and triage tool for identifying potential small-to-medium RCTs on shoulder radiographs, maintaining higher recall is clinically desirable to reduce false negatives. Based on the validation set comparison, the fixed threshold (0.5) maintained higher recall while providing a consistent and easily interpretable decision rule, making it more suitable for clinical screening applications.

The fixed threshold (0.5) was therefore further evaluated on the independent test set. The hybrid ensemble model achieved an accuracy of 81% (95% CI 73–90), precision of 72% (60–84), recall of 100% (100–100), specificity of 63% (48–78), an F1-score of 0.84 (0.75–0.91), and an AUROC of 0.93 (0.87–0.97). The AUROC values of the individual models ranged from 0.85 (0.76–0.93) to 0.91 (0.84–0.97), indicating stable and robust discriminative performance across models on the independent test set. Discussion

Our study demonstrates that a hybrid DL ensemble model can serve as an adjunctive tool for the preliminary assessment of small to medium RCTs on plain radiographs. By highlighting radiographic features that may not be readily apparent to clinicians, this approach has the potential to assist in deciding whether patients should proceed to advanced imaging for further evaluation. By integrating predictions from multiple DL architectures, the hybrid model achieved more balanced diagnostic performance than individual models, highlighting the advantage of ensemble learning in improving model stability and robustness. On the validation set, the hybrid DL ensemble model demonstrated the best overall performance among the evaluated models, achieving an accuracy of 78% (95% CI 69–86), a precision of 79% (68–88), a recall of 87% (77–95), a specificity of 66% (51–80), an F1-score of 0.83 (0.74–0.89), and an AUROC of 0.85 (0.76–0.92). Although the recall of the hybrid model was slightly lower than that of the ResNet-101 model (90%, 95% CI 81–97), the ensemble model achieved the highest accuracy, precision, specificity, and F1-score, indicating a more balanced performance across multiple evaluation metrics.

On the independent test set, the hybrid DL ensemble model achieved an accuracy of 81% (95% CI 73–90), a precision of 72% (60–84), a recall of 100% (100–100), a specificity of 63% (48–78), an F1-score of 0.84 (0.75–0.91), and an AUROC of 0.93 (0.87–0.97). The perfect recall observed in the independent test set indicates that all RCT cases were successfully detected by the model, which is particularly valuable in screening scenarios where minimizing missed diagnoses is essential. Although the specificity of the hybrid model was moderate, this finding should be interpreted in the context of its intended role as a screening and triage tool. In such settings, higher sensitivity is generally prioritized to minimize false-negative cases and reduce the risk of missed diagnoses. The relatively lower specificity may result in an increased number of false-positive cases; however, in clinical practice, this would primarily lead to additional non-invasive imaging examinations, such as MRI, rather than unnecessary surgical intervention. Therefore, this trade-off between sensitivity and specificity may be considered acceptable in the context of early detection and clinical decision support. Although the precision suggests that some predicted positive cases may require further confirmatory imaging, such as MRI, this is consistent with the expected behavior of a screening tool in clinical practice.

Overall, these results suggest that the proposed hybrid ensemble model may serve as a screening and triage support tool rather than a definitive diagnostic modality for clinicians when evaluating patients with shoulder pain using plain radiographs. Physicians can obtain valuable diagnostic information from PEs and shoulder radiographs. In patients with large to massive RCTs, radiographic features such as a large subacromial spur, superior migration of the humeral head, and an increased critical shoulder angle (CSA) may indicate tears. Chin et al. reported that the presence of tuberosity sclerosis, tuberosity irregularities, tuberosity cysts, and the sourcil sign is indicative of RCTs [7]. Kuper et al. reviewed multiple studies and concluded that a CSA of > 35° is significantly (p < 0.05) associated with increased risks of primary RCTs and postoperative recurrence [35]. Kaur et al. analyzed the correlation between acromial morphology and RCTs and found the probability of RCT increases if acromion index > 0.7, CSA > 35°, and AHI < 7 mm [36]. Another research has also demonstrated that a reduced AHI < 8.2 mm serves as a significant risk factor for supraspinatus tendon rupture [6]. These radiographic parameters provide useful structural information; however, subtle radiographic changes associated with small-to-medium RCTs may be difficult to recognize through visual assessment alone.

Shoulder radiographs often exhibit no apparent visual changes in cases of small to medium RCTs, increasing the difficulty of predicting such tears on the basis of radiography alone. Cho et al. applied machine learning techniques to shoulder radiographs from 145 patients and found that CNNs achieved significantly higher accuracy in distinguishing partial RCTs from intact tendons than did human evaluation, as indicated by the chi-square test (Pearson test, p < 0.001) [37]. Similarly, Hashimoto et al. reported that CNNs exhibited markedly higher accuracy in detecting RCTs than did orthopedic surgeons [38]. These findings support the growing role of DL models in identifying subtle radiographic patterns that may not be easily detected by human observers.

Kim et al. used a DL model to analyze conventional shoulder radiographs for detecting major RCTs, reporting a high sensitivity and negative predictive value, and the model reduced the need for advanced imaging in approximately 30% of suspected RCT cases [9]. Lio et al. developed a DL model using 2803 anteroposterior shoulder radiographs and demonstrated strong performance in detecting full-thickness RCTs, with a sensitivity of 94.5%, a negative predictive value of 96.2%, and a negative likelihood ratio of 0.10 [10]. Compared with these previous studies, the present study focused specifically on detecting small-to-medium RCTs, which often present with minimal or subtle radiographic abnormalities. The hybrid ensemble model demonstrated favorable diagnostic performance, achieving an F1-score of 0.83 on the validation set and 0.84 on the independent test set, indicating a balanced trade-off between precision and recall. These results suggest that ensemble DL strategies may improve the reliability of AI-assisted detection of subtle shoulder pathologies.

The gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) technique was used to visualize the image regions that contributed to the model predictions [39, 40]. Grad-CAM generates heatmaps based on gradient information from the final convolutional layers, highlighting regions that have a higher influence on the predicted class. It should be noted that Grad-CAM provides post-hoc saliency visualization of influential regions rather than a causal explanation of the model’s decision-making process. Grad-CAM has been widely applied in shoulder pathology studies to improve the interpretability of radiographic and ultrasonographic DL models [41–43]. In this study, Grad-CAM was used as a qualitative tool to illustrate which areas of the shoulder radiographs contributed to the model predictions. Figure 7 presents representative Grad-CAM visualizations for shoulder radiographs with RCTs. In the heatmaps, red regions indicate areas with higher contribution to the model prediction, whereas blue regions represent lower contribution. These visualizations suggest that the models tend to focus on anatomically relevant regions of the shoulder when generating predictions, although the heatmaps should be interpreted as saliency maps rather than definitive explanations of the model’s reasoning.

This concordance suggests that the AI system not only achieves high classification accuracy but also aligns with the diagnostic reasoning patterns of experienced clinicians, reinforcing its potential integration into real-world clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study, which may introduce inherent biases related to data collection and patient selection. Second, the dataset was derived from a single center and utilized only a single radiographic projection (anteroposterior view), which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings, imaging protocols, or patient populations. In addition, the lack of external validation using independent datasets from other institutions is an important limitation. Third, potential selection bias should be considered, as all included patients underwent arthroscopic shoulder surgery for suspected rotator cuff pathology; therefore, the study population may not fully represent the general population of patients with shoulder pain.

In addition, the models were initialized using ImageNet-pretrained weights rather than domain-specific pretrained models. Although ImageNet pretraining has been widely adopted in medical imaging and has demonstrated strong transfer learning performance, future studies may benefit from pretraining strategies based on large-scale musculoskeletal imaging datasets, which may provide more task-specific feature representations for orthopedic imaging applications. Future studies incorporating multicenter data and external validation cohorts are necessary to further confirm the generalizability and robustness of the proposed model.

Conclusion

The proposed hybrid DL ensemble model demonstrated promising performance in detecting small-to-medium RCTs from shoulder radiographs. The model achieved an AUROC of 0.85 (95% CI 0.76–0.92) on the validation set and 0.93 (0.87–0.97) on the independent test set, with high sensitivity for identifying RCTs. These findings suggest that the hybrid ensemble approach provides improved overall performance compared with the individual pretrained models and may serve as a cost-effective and efficient tool for the preliminary assessment of suspected RCTs. However, this model should be considered as a screening and triage support tool rather than a definitive diagnostic modality. Our results further suggest that the AI-based system may assist clinicians in identifying patients who may benefit from further imaging evaluation, thereby supporting clinical decision-making in the assessment of shoulder pain.
