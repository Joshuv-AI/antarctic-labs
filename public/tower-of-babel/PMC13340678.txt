Granulomatous breast mastitis in pregnancy secondary to Mycobacterium avium complex: a case report
Authors: Hassan Alyami, Hana A. AL Zahrani, Salma Albahrani, Alaa AlMaimouni, Rania A. Almaghrabi, Jawza Alanazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Granulomatous breast mastitis is a rare inflammatory condition that can mimic both infectious mastitis and breast malignancy. Its occurrence during pregnancy is uncommon and poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Introduction

Granulomatous breast mastitis (GBM) is a rare, benign inflammatory condition of the breast. It encompasses a spectrum of histopathologically distinct entities characterized by non-caseating granulomatous inflammation within the breast lobules. The etiology of GBM remains incompletely understood, encompassing autoimmune, hormonal, and infectious mechanisms. Idiopathic granulomatous mastitis (IGM), the most common form, often responds to corticosteroids; however, infectious causes must be excluded before initiating immunosuppressive therapy.

Among infectious causes, Corynebacterium kroppenstedtii is the most frequently implicated organism, typically associated with cystic neutrophilic granulomatous mastitis. Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are a far rarer cause of GBM, predominantly reported in immunocompromised patients. Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) infection of the breast is exceedingly rare and, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported in an immunocompetent pregnant patient.

GBM during pregnancy poses amplified diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, as physiological breast changes may obscure findings, and pharmacologic options are limited by fetal safety concerns.

This report describes a case of MAC-associated GBM in an immunocompetent pregnant woman, managed conservatively during gestation and treated definitively postpartum. It highlights the importance of sustained microbiological vigilance in refractory GBM, the systematic approach to differential diagnosis, and the rationale for treatment decisions across the peripartum period. This case report has been prepared in accordance with the SCARE 2025 criteria [1].

Case presentation

Initial presentation

A 42-year-old Saudi woman, gravida 7 para 6, at 4 weeks’ gestation, presented to the emergency department with a 3-day history of progressive, painful left breast swelling associated with localized erythema, warmth, and chills. She denied fever (temperature 36.7°C on presentation), nipple discharge, skin changes, or trauma. Her medical and surgical history was unremarkable. She had no history of oral contraceptive use, autoimmune disease, immunosuppression, or family history of breast disease.

On examination, a tender, indurated area was palpable in the lower quadrants of the left breast (5–8 o’clock position) with mild erythema and warmth. No nipple retraction, discharge, peau d’orange, or lymphadenopathy was present. The right breast was unremarkable.

Laboratory investigations: white blood cell 8.44 × 109/L (normal), C-reactive protein (CRP) 133 mg/L (elevated). Renal and liver function tests were within normal limits. An interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA; QuantiFERON-TB Gold) was performed and returned negative.

Initial management and early clinical course

A diagnosis of acute mastitis was made. The patient was initially treated with oral amoxicillin–clavulanate (Augmentin) 1 g twice daily for 7 days. Despite completing this course, symptoms persisted, and she was readmitted 1 week later (at 5 weeks’ gestation). At this admission, examination revealed progression with new peau d’orange and nipple retraction on the left breast. CRP had been 87 mg/L at a clinic visit days prior.

Imaging, microbiological investigations, and interventional procedures

Breast ultrasound confirmed evolving abscess formation. Ultrasound-guided aspiration of the left breast yielded purulent fluid (approximately 10 mL), and a core needle biopsy was performed concurrently.

Initial aspirate culture grew Klebsiella oxytoca. However, a repeat tissue culture showed no growth, and the patient continued to deteriorate despite targeted cephalosporin therapy, suggesting that K. oxytoca was likely a secondary colonizer rather than the primary pathogen.

Due to persistent and recurrent abscess formation, the patient underwent repeated ultrasound-guided aspirations and two formal incision-and-drainage procedures, along with ongoing wound care.

Histopathological and definitive microbiological findings

Core needle biopsy revealed non-caseating granulomatous inflammation consistent with granulomatous mastitis, with no evidence of caseation necrosis or malignancy.

Tuberculosis was excluded through a negative IGRA, negative Mycobacterium tuberculosis/rifampin polymerase chain reaction (MTB/RIF PCR), and negative acid-fast bacillus (AFB) smear. Mycobacterial liquid culture using the BACTEC MGIT system demonstrated growth of Mycobacterium other than tuberculosis after prolonged incubation. Species-level identification confirmed Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) using molecular methods, and drug susceptibility testing was performed.

Systemic manifestations

During the disease course, the patient developed multiple erythematous, painful subcutaneous nodules over both lower extremities, clinically diagnosed as erythema nodosum (EN). A dermatology consultation was obtained, and topical therapy was initiated.

Management during pregnancy

Management during pregnancy was individualized to balance maternal disease control with fetal safety. Although systemic corticosteroids are commonly used in IGM, they were withheld due to the need to exclude an underlying infectious etiology and the high suspicion of infection based on clinical progression and microbiological findings. When mycobacterial culture confirmed MAC in the third trimester, the multidisciplinary team elected to defer triple antimycobacterial therapy until after delivery. The patient also developed gestational diabetes mellitus, which was managed concurrently.

Postpartum treatment and outcome

Following delivery, triple antimycobacterial therapy (RIF, ethambutol, azithromycin three times weekly) was initiated. Drug susceptibility testing confirmed sensitivity to all agents, and therapy continued for 18 months per international guidelines. Treatment was well tolerated, with no hepatotoxicity, hematological abnormalities, or visual complications. Discussion

MAC as the primary etiologic agent: diagnostic rationale

Although K. oxytoca was initially isolated, repeat cultures were negative, and there was no clinical response to antibacterial therapy. MAC was confirmed via BACTEC MGIT at the species level using molecular probe hybridization; the prolonged time to positivity (~5–6 weeks) argues against contamination.

Histopathology demonstrated non-caseating granulomatous inflammation, and resolution occurred only after MAC-directed therapy, providing strong therapeutic confirmation of causality.

Differential diagnosis: a systematic approach

Tuberculosis was excluded based on a negative IGRA, negative MTB/RIF PCR, and the absence of caseating necrosis. Sarcoidosis was considered unlikely due to the absence of systemic involvement and normal chest imaging. Inflammatory breast cancer was excluded histologically, as no malignant cells were identified on core biopsy despite initially concerning clinical features.

Fungal infection was excluded by negative cultures, and IGM, a diagnosis of exclusion, was ruled out given MAC identification and clinical response to antimycobacterial therapy.

Differentiating infectious GBM from IGM: clinical approach

A major diagnostic challenge in this case was distinguishing infectious granulomatous mastitis from idiopathic disease, as both share identical histopathological features. This distinction is clinically critical because management is fundamentally opposite: IGM is typically treated with corticosteroids, whereas infectious disease requires targeted antimicrobial therapy. A conservative watch-and-wait approach has also been reported as a viable option in selected cases of granulomatous mastitis[2]. Premature immunosuppression in unrecognized mycobacterial infection may result in disease progression or dissemination. In this case, several features raised suspicion for an infectious process: recurrent abscess formation, lack of antibiotic response, and persistent disease despite drainage, justifying extended microbiological evaluation with prolonged mycobacterial culture incubation.

NTM breast infection in immunocompetent pregnancy

NTM breast infection is rare in immunocompetent patients, most commonly involving rapidly growing species (M. fortuitum, M. abscessus) following procedures or implants. MAC breast infection is distinctly less common; reported cases include Kyvernitakis et al in an immunocompromised host[3] and Marchetti et al in an immunocompetent woman with silicone implants[4]. The pathogenesis remains speculative, possibly involving pregnancy-associated immune modulation and local tissue trauma. To our knowledge, this is the first reported MAC-associated GBM in an immunocompetent pregnant patient without implants or prior surgery.

Corticosteroids in GBM during pregnancy: evidence and alternatives

Systemic corticosteroids are an established treatment for IGM; however, their use during pregnancy requires careful risk–benefit assessment when infectious etiology has not been excluded. Although low-dose regimens are relatively safe[5,6], risks include gestational diabetes exacerbation, infection susceptibility, and adverse obstetric outcomes, particularly relevant in this patient with pre-existing gestational diabetes.

Corticosteroid therapy was deferred due to diagnostic uncertainty; subsequent MAC identification confirmed an infectious etiology in which immunosuppression would have been inappropriate.

Antimycobacterial therapy: rationale for regimen and duration

Management of MAC infection is guided by the 2020 ATS/ERS/ESCMID/IDSA Clinical Practice Guideline[7], which recommends a macrolide-based regimen with ethambutol and RIF; these principles extend to extrapulmonary MAC by expert consensus. An 18-month regimen was chosen given the tissue extent, delayed diagnosis, and relapse risk; drug susceptibility testing confirmed full sensitivity, and therapy was well tolerated.

EN as a systemic manifestation

EN developed and was confirmed clinically and dermatologically, representing a delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction supporting systemic MAC-associated inflammation, consistent with prior reports of granulomatous mastitis associated with EN during pregnancy[8–10]. Serial CRP monitoring (133 mg/L at presentation, 87 mg/L at readmission) paralleled disease activity, resolving alongside clinical improvement.

Conclusion

This case describes a rare presentation of MAC-associated granulomatous mastitis in an immunocompetent pregnant patient. MAC should be considered in refractory GBM, even in immunocompetent individuals, and microbiological confirmation with prolonged mycobacterial culture is essential before initiating immunosuppressive therapy.

Conservative management during pregnancy, comprising repeated drainage (ultrasound-guided aspiration and incision-and-drainage) and pregnancy-compatible antibiotics, allowed adequate disease control while awaiting microbiological diagnosis. No surgical excision was required postpartum; following delivery, prolonged triple antimycobacterial therapy resulted in complete resolution without significant adverse effects.

Key lessons include maintaining suspicion for NTM in refractory GBM, performing mycobacterial cultures before immunosuppression, adopting a multidisciplinary approach, and recognizing that extrapulmonary MAC requires prolonged therapy.
