Effect of Early Administration of Casirivimab and Imdevimab in Hospitalized Patients with COVID-19: A Post Hoc Analysis
Authors: Eleftherios Mylonakis, Amy Sullivan, Selin Somersan-Karakaya, Xiaojun You, Jiangnan Lyu, Fadi Shehadeh, Gregory P Geba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCasirivimab and imdevimab (CAS+IMD) has been shown to improve clinical outcomes (death/mechanical ventilation) in seronegative hospitalized patients with COVID-19. The objective of the current analysis was to assess whether time to administration of CAS+IMD, from the start of COVID-19 symptoms, influenced these outcomes.Patients and MethodsThis was a post hoc analysis of Study 2066 (conducted between 10 June 2020 and 9 April 2021), a Phase I/II/III trial investigating the safety and efficacy of CAS+IMD (single intravenous dose) vs placebo in hospitalized adult patients with COVID-19. The primary outcome in this analysis was death or mechanical ventilation. Two populations of patients were assessed: efficacy (n = 738) and exploratory (full analysis set-exploratory data analysis [FAS-EDA]; n = 1115). Time to administration was defined as the number of days of COVID-19 prior to treatment, 4 days being the first quartile, which was considered early treatment administration of CAS+IMD.ResultsOdds of death or mechanical ventilation were higher in those aged 50–70 years (odds ratio [OR]: 2.98; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.62–5.91; P = 0.0008) and >70 years (OR: 4.50; 95% CI, 2.45–8.96; P = 0.001) than those aged <50 years. Overall, patients who received CAS+IMD <4 days after first COVID-19 symptoms had better outcomes than patients receiving CAS+IMD ≥4 days, on adjusted analyses (efficacy OR: 0.42; 95% CI, 0.20–0.80; P = 0.0122; FAS-EDA OR: 0.63; 95% CI, 0.38–1.01; P = 0.0664). These differences were most notable in those aged 50–70 years (OR: 0.24; 95% CI, 0.05–0.70; P = 0.0219).ConclusionEarly monoclonal antibody treatment is associated with better outcomes in hospitalized patients with severe COVID-19.Keywords: casirivimab, COVID-19, imdevimab, monoclonal antibody, time to treatment

Introduction

A global vaccine program has significantly reduced the number of deaths from COVID-19,1 but some patients remain at risk for severe disease and may require antiviral therapy. The use of monoclonal antibodies for the management of COVID-19 (and other viral infections) continues to be investigated. Analyses of data obtained from studies performed during the peak of the pandemic provided important insight into the requirement for highly neutralizing severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2)-specific antibodies to most effectively clear virus and resolve infection.2–7 Furthermore, studies have suggested that early treatment with antiviral therapy improves clinical outcomes;8,9 however, data are lacking on the impact of time to treatment with monoclonal antibodies in a randomized clinical trial setting or in more severe patients.

Casirivimab and imdevimab (CAS+IMD) is a monoclonal antibody combination that binds non-overlapping epitopes of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein receptor-binding domain.10,11 The efficacy and safety of CAS+IMD in the management of COVID-19 has been documented in both prophylaxis and treatment settings, and in both hospitalized patients with severe disease and outpatients with milder disease.2–7 These studies were conducted when the SARS-CoV-2 Delta variant was dominant. While CAS+IMD was previously authorized for treatment and postexposure prophylaxis of COVID-19 in the United States,12,13 it exhibits diminished neutralization against Omicron-lineage variants and, thus, is not currently authorized for use in the management of COVID-19.

Study 2066 (NCT04426695) was a Phase I/II/III double-blind, placebo-controlled trial conducted in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 receiving low-flow or no supplemental oxygen.7 Patients received a single intravenous dose of CAS+IMD 2400 mg or 8000 mg, or placebo. The study showed that CAS+IMD reduced viral load and generally improved clinical outcomes (risk of death, mechanical ventilation, or all-cause mortality), with the greatest benefit observed in seronegative patients. This post hoc analysis assessed the relationship between time to administration of CAS+IMD from COVID-19 symptom start date and COVID-19 outcomes across different age groups using data from Study 2066.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patients

The design and patient inclusion/exclusion criteria for Study 2066 (conducted between 10 June 2020 and 9 April 2021) have been described in detail previously.7 In brief, it was an adaptive, double-blind, placebo-controlled, Phase I/II/III trial conducted at 103 sites in the United States, Brazil, Chile, Mexico, Moldova, and Romania. Patients were included in the study if they were ≥18 years of age and had been hospitalized with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 ≤72 hours and symptom onset ≤10 days from randomization. Standard-of-care treatments for COVID-19 were permitted. Patients who entered the study were initially enrolled into one of four cohorts: no supplemental oxygen (cohort 1a), low-flow oxygen (cohort 1), oxygen (cohort 2), or mechanical ventilation (cohort 3). Due to early termination of the study at the sponsor’s decision, the protocol was amended for efficacy analysis to consist of Phase II patients from cohort 1a and Phase III patients from cohort 1 who were randomized and part of the modified analysis population across all 103 sites.

For this analysis, two populations of patients were assessed. The efficacy population has been defined previously.7 In brief, this population included all randomized patients excluding those with a negative SARS-CoV-2 quantitative reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction test at baseline.7 The second exploratory population (full analysis set-exploratory data analysis [FAS-EDA]) consisted of all patients in any phase of the study in cohort 1 or cohort 1a. The analysis was run for all ages and any baseline serostatus and then repeated looking only at patients 50–70 years of age for any serostatus and 50–70 years of age who were seronegative or seropositive but negative for neutralizing antibodies at baseline across both the efficacy and FAS-EDA population, as previously defined.7

Days of COVID-19 symptoms prior to treatment start were assessed descriptively. The cutoff of 4 days was chosen as it was the first quartile of the distribution across the analysis population of interest. Patients who received CAS+IMD were grouped into two populations: those who received CAS+IMD <4 days after patient-reported COVID-19 symptom onset; and those who received CAS+IMD ≥4 days after COVID-19 symptom onset. Patients were randomized 1:1:1 to CAS+IMD 2400 mg, CAS+IMD 8000 mg, or placebo as a single intravenous dose. For this analysis, both dosing levels of CAS+IMD were combined into one treatment group.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome in this analysis was death or mechanical ventilation for logistic regression, and time to death or mechanical ventilation for the Cox proportional hazard models.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics for baseline characteristics were provided for patients receiving CAS+IMD and placebo. Logistic regression was performed by backwards selection based on AIC. Variables used in model were tested based on empirical clinical importance: high viral load (discrete, with HVL indicating >5 log10 copies/mL), sex, race, ethnicity, seropositive or seronegative status at baseline, baseline temperature, baseline oxygen saturation, baseline systolic blood pressure, received treatment <4 days after COVID symptom start date, remdesivir use at any point, steroid use at any point and age. The influential covariates selected by the model were age, log tta, and the interaction term between log tta and age. The model was also run with remdesivir use and steroid use, neither of which were significant in any population. For patients who received CAS+IMD, logistic regression was performed using age categories (<50, 50–70, and >70 years of age) and days of COVID-19 symptoms prior to treatment as covariates (<4 days and ≥4 days). Age categories were chosen based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention risk profiling that indicated a lower risk for those <50 years of age and a substantially increased risk of the worst COVID-19 outcomes at age >70 years.14,15 As 50–70 years of age was a population of interest, logistic regression was also done for ages 50–70 years only, having only days of COVID-19 symptoms prior to treatment as the covariate. This was repeated using Cox proportional hazard models and repeated for both the FAS-EDA and efficacy populations. This was also repeated for all patients 50–70 years of age and for patients 50–70 years of age who were seronegative or seropositive but negative for neutralizing antibodies at baseline.

In the FAS-EDA population, patients with <4 days of COVID-19 symptoms had slightly different characteristics compared with patients with ≥4 days of symptoms (Supplementary Table 1). Patients with a shorter duration of symptoms tended to be slightly older (mean age 65 vs 61 years, respectively) and have a high viral load (59% vs 48%, respectively). In contrast, patients with a longer duration of symptoms prior to treatment tended to be seropositive (64% vs 55%) and have more steroid use (87% vs 70%) than those with a shorter duration of symptoms prior to treatment. These slight imbalances were accounted for in the adjusted analyses.

Clinical Outcomes

All Ages

In the FAS-EDA population, the odds of death or mechanical ventilation were higher in those aged 50–70 years (odds ratio [OR]: 2.98; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.62–5.91; P = 0.0008) and those aged >70 years (OR: 4.50; 95% CI, 2.45–8.96; P < 0.001) compared with those aged <50 years (Figure 1a). Similar positive trends were seen in the efficacy population; improved outcomes were associated with those aged >70 years vs those aged <50 years (OR: 4.10; 95% CI, 1.94–9.72; P = 0.0033; Figure 1a). On adjusted analyses (adjusted for gender, corticosteroid use, and remdesivir use), the odds of death or mechanical ventilation were higher for patients receiving CAS+IMD ≥4 days after symptom onset compared to those who received CAS+IMD <4 days after COVID-19 symptom onset (FAS-EDA population OR: 0.63; 95% CI, 0.38–1.01; P = 0.0664 and efficacy population OR: 0.42; 95% CI, 0.20–0.80, P = 0.0122; Figure 1a). Similar trends were seen for the Cox proportional hazards model as shown in Figure 1b.

In addition, 570 patients treated with placebo were evaluated using the same multivariable logistic regression model assessing the impact of time-to-treatment administration (<4 days vs ≥4 days) on outcomes; no significant association was observed. More specifically, patients who received placebo <4 days after symptom onset did not experience improved outcomes compared with those treated ≥4 days. In the FAS-EDA population, the OR was 1.12 (95% CI, 0.65–1.89; P = 0.68), and in the efficacy population, the OR was 1.05 (95% CI, 0.54–1.96; P = 0.88), indicating no meaningful difference between early and late treatment. Similarly, time-to-event analysis using Cox models also showed no significant effect of earlier treatment; in the FAS-EDA population the hazard ratio (HR) was 1.11 (95% CI, 0.69–1.80; P = 0.67) and in the efficacy population the HR was 1.09 (95% CI, 0.61–1.94; P = 0.78).

Patients 50–70 Years of Age

The odds of a patient aged 50–70 years progressing to death or mechanical ventilation were lower in those who received CAS+IMD ≤4 days of COVID-19 symptom onset compared with those who received CAS+IMD ≥4 days after symptom onset (FAS-EDA population OR: 0.52; 95% CI, 0.22–1.07; P = 0.098 and efficacy population OR: 0.24; 95% CI, 0.05–0.70; P = 0.0219; Table 3A). Discussion

People with known risk factors, or those who are unable to achieve active immunity through vaccination, remain vulnerable to severe COVID-19 disease. Early in the pandemic, several monoclonal antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 were authorized by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the management of COVID-19.16–20 While most are no longer authorized due to lack of activity against current strains,16–20 studies continue to investigate the benefit of passive immunity against both COVID-19 and other infections. We therefore conducted a post hoc analysis based on symptom duration and age in hospitalized adults with confirmed SARS-CoV-2. We found that, across all ages, hospitalized patients with COVID-19 receiving CAS+IMD treatment earlier in the course of symptomatic disease (<4 days) demonstrated reduced risk of the worst COVID-19 outcomes of mechanical ventilation and death. Importantly, in the control group, there were no significant differences in outcomes between patients who received placebo <4 days vs ≥4 days after symptom onset. Specifically, in the FAS-EDA population, the OR was 1.12 (95% CI, 0.65–1.89; P = 0.68), and in the efficacy population, the OR was 1.05 (95% CI, 0.54–1.96; P = 0.88), indicating no measurable benefit of earlier administration of placebo. Time-to-event analysis using Cox models yielded similar results. This suggests that the benefit seen with early CAS+IMD was not driven by factors associated with early treatment timing or hospital care alone. Earlier care in the absence of active therapy was inadequate to improve outcome, underscoring the importance of timely administration of effective antiviral therapy in this patient population. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first analyses to show that early treatment with a monoclonal antibody can improve outcomes in some hospitalized patients with severe COVID-19. Notably, we observed that the odds of death or mechanical ventilation were generally higher in those 50–70 years of age and >70 years of age when compared with those <50 years of age. Furthermore, if CAS+IMD was given earlier, it provided most benefit to patients 50–70 years of age. Presumably, younger patients have more resilient physiology, more pulmonary reserve, are better able to survive, and have more robust and relevant/targeted antibody responses, which generally reduced the benefit they received from exogenously provided CAS+IMD. Conversely, in those aged >70 years, earlier treatment may not have benefitted these older patients to the same extent as younger patients, since these individuals may have been predisposed to have worse outcomes due to their age (which is among the most influential factors previously demonstrated to be associated with COVID-19 outcomes from the beginning of the pandemic).14,15

The findings of this study align with other reports conducted early in the COVID-19 pandemic, which showed that early (within days of symptom onset) passive immunotherapy with antivirals, convalescent plasma, or monoclonal antibodies could improve disease outcomes for non-hospitalized subjects, in retrospective, observational studies.2,8,9,21–27 One of the first passive immunotherapy management strategies to be investigated for COVID-19 was convalescent plasma therapy, which has shown variable activity against other viral pathogens such as Ebola, influenza, and HIV, with some promising preclinical data and selected clinical signals in certain settings; however, definitive randomized evidence remains limited.21,28,29 While some studies found no significant impact on mortality in hospitalized22 or critically ill patients with COVID-19 treated with early convalescent plasma,23 other studies reported that early treatment reduced the risk of disease progression, hospitalizations, and death, potentially saving thousands of lives.24,25

Early data on the impact of early treatment with monoclonal antibodies on COVID-19 outcomes were also inconclusive and limited by small patient numbers and specific patient populations. The primary Phase III study of CAS+IMD was conducted in outpatients with COVID-19 and known risk factors for severe disease (NCT04425629).2 The study found that, in patients enrolled <7 days after clinical onset, treatment with CAS+IMD reduced the risk of COVID-19-related hospitalizations or death from any cause, and resolved symptoms and SARS-CoV-2 viral load more quickly than placebo. These results formed the basis of the Emergency Use Authorization granted by the US FDA, which stated that treatment should be initiated within 10 days of symptom onset.16 Results from subsequent smaller studies varied. While one case-control study found that recipients who received bamlanivimab ± etesevimab or CAS+IMD within a mean of 4 days after first symptoms were less likely to need hospitalization, admission to intensive care, or die compared with those who did not receive any treatment,27 a single-center cohort study comparing bamlanivimab with CAS+IMD in preventing COVID-19–related revisits found no difference in outcomes for early (<3 days) vs late (≥3 days) treatment administration, although the authors did note that this was an unusual finding. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first analysis using clinical trial data to specifically compare early vs late treatment with a monoclonal antibody, and it demonstrated that early treatment can improve outcomes in hospitalized patients with severe COVID-19. We believe these results will help inform future passive immunotherapy strategies for COVID-19 and aid the establishment of clinical frameworks for rapid deployment of monoclonal antibodies in future pandemics.

A few limitations should be noted. This analysis was conducted on a large sample of individuals, and recognition and self-reporting of symptomatic disease is likely influenced by several factors. These include: an individual’s perception of risk of acquiring infection, which may perhaps be of less concern to the young than the old, likely influencing the degree of vigilance; self-awareness and ability to perceive changes in clinical status; and was based on recall, which may have been less reliable in the older age groups. Thus, although there is the notion that early institution of treatment would more likely benefit those who can accrue benefit (ie those in the intermediate age group), it is possible a more accurate determination of disease onset might have revealed some benefits of early initiation of anti–SARS-CoV-2 antibody therapy in both the other older and younger age groups. Additionally, multiple factors may have influenced mortality during the COVID-19 pandemic, including viral variants, baseline comorbidities, and evolving standards of care, which can make attribution to a single intervention challenging. To address this, our analysis incorporated adjusted models accounting for key confounders (including steroid and remdesivir use), whereas inclusion of data from the placebo comparator arm balances covariates which were assumed to be distributed randomly. In the placebo group, earlier timing of care (<4 days vs ≥4 days) was not associated with improved outcomes, suggesting that the observed benefit is not simply a function of earlier presentation or overall care intensity, but may reflect a treatment-specific effect. We also acknowledge that CAS+IMD is not active against current Omicron-lineage variants and is no longer authorized for use. However, we believe the primary contribution of this study is not limited to this specific agent, but rather the broader principle it supports: that early administration of effective, highly neutralizing antiviral therapy in hospitalized patients can meaningfully impact outcomes. This concept remains directly applicable to current and future antiviral and antibody-based strategies for emerging respiratory viruses. Other limitations include those tied to the post hoc nature of the analysis, including in some cases impacts of constrained sample sizes.

Randomized clinical trial data consistently demonstrate that early initiation of antiviral therapy in high-risk populations reduces progression to severe disease: nirmatrelvir-ritonavir reduced COVID-19–related hospitalization or death by 89% when initiated within 3 days of symptom onset, remdesivir reduced hospitalization or death by 87% when started within 7 days, and molnupiravir reduced hospitalization or death by approximately 30% when initiated within 5 days.30 Real-world observational data during the Omicron era have corroborated these findings, with early oral antiviral use consistently associated with significant reductions in mortality and hospitalization, particularly among older and high-risk individuals.31–33 Although CAS+IMD is no longer active against current SARS-CoV-2 variants, the present analysis provides randomized clinical trial evidence supporting a unifying principle: early, effective antiviral or antibody-based intervention can meaningfully alter disease trajectory, particularly in susceptible populations. These findings therefore remain relevant not only for COVID-19 but also for future emerging viral threats, where rapid deployment and timing of therapy will be critical determinants of success.34

We believe these results can help shape future clinical studies of monoclonal antibody treatment for other viral diseases and recommend that these studies include assessment of the impact of early administration on treatment outcomes. We also note that Study 2066 was conducted before the emergence of the Omicron variant against which CAS+IMD is not active; however, we believe that the principle of early application of highly neutralizing monoclonal antibodies to individuals at specific increased risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes should apply across any emergent strain of the virus, and these findings should be considered in the study and application of future anti–SARS-CoV-2 monoclonal antibodies. The findings here also should be tested in studies assessing antibody-based treatment of other emergent viral illnesses.

Conclusions

These analyses, using data from an adaptive, double-blind, placebo-controlled, Phase I/II/III trial, show early treatment with CAS+IMD (ie <4 days after reported symptoms) is associated with better outcomes in hospitalized patients with severe COVID-19, and especially those aged 50–70 years. These results may be of particular interest in the management of similar viruses and pandemics in which monoclonal antibodies are part of the treatment paradigm.
