Development of Candidaemia Score (CanDi‐Score) in Decision of Early Empirical Antifungal Treatment for Patients in Intensive Care Unit: The International Prospective Observational ID‐IRI Study

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCandidaemia remains a common, life‐threatening infection among intensive care unit (ICU) patients, with high mortality, particularly in patients with delayed diagnosis and treatment.ObjectivesThis international, multicentre, prospective, observational study aimed to identify risk factors for candidaemia in ICU patients and to develop an easy‐to‐use predictive tool, the CanDi‐Score, for early initiation of empirical antifungal treatment.Patients/MethodsAll adults over 18 years hospitalised for more than 48 h in the ICUs were included in the study and followed for 30 days. Data on demographics, comorbidities, clinical severity scores including APACHE II and SOFA scores, Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), established risk factors for candidaemia and their time‐dependent effects such as total parenteral nutrition (TPN), mechanical ventilation (MV), and central venous catheter (CVC) were collected. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to identify factors associated with candidaemia development, and fast backwards variable selection was used. To account for between‐centre heterogeneity, sensitivity analyses were performed using mixed‐effects logistic regression models including centre‐specific random intercepts. A nomogram model was constructed using independent risk factors identified in multivariable analysis, and its predictive performance was evaluated with Area Under the Curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity.ResultsA total of 2,704 ICU patients, including 204 with candidaemia, were enrolled. Patients with candidaemia had higher APACHE II (19 vs. 13) and CCI scores (6 vs. 5; both p < 0.001), and more frequently had chronic renal failure, haemodialysis, malignancy, recent gastrointestinal surgery, and neutropenia (all p ≤ 0.05). Patients were randomly split into development (80%) and validation (20%) cohorts. Multivariate analysis identified higher CCI, concurrent infection, neutropenia, ICU stay > 14 days, MV > 3 days, CVC duration and TPN > 14 days as independent risk factors. The risk associated with CVC increased substantially with prolonged use (OR: 0–7 days: 3.69; 8–21 days: 8.58; > 21 days: 17.21). The model demonstrated good performance in both the development and validation cohorts, with sensitivity, specificity, and AUC values of 79.6%, 76.5%, and 0.86 (95% CI: 0.83–0.88) in the development cohort, respectively, and comparable predictive performance in the validation cohort. The score model was demonstrated by a nomogram.ConclusionsGiven the high mortality of candidaemia in ICU patients, the CanDi‐Score may serve as a valuable tool for early prediction and timely empirical antifungal treatment. Key predictors include CCI, presence of concurrent infections, the duration of CVC use, and TPN administration. CanDi‐Score provides clinicians with a practical approach to identify high‐risk patients and guides decision‐making for the early initiation of antifungal treatment.

Introduction

Candidaemia is the most common and severe fungal infection seen in critically ill patients. The incidence of candidaemia is approximately 10–20 times higher among patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) than among those not managed in ICUs. Candida species are the fourth most common causative agent of nosocomial bloodstream infection and cause significant mortality and morbidity [1]. In addition, candidaemia prolongs ICU stays by about 13 days, significantly increases healthcare costs, and adds an estimated USD 8570 in sepsis‐related expenses [2]. All‐cause mortality rates of candidaemia have been reported to range between 50% and 60% [3]. Given the high candidaemia‐associated mortality, early and effective antifungal treatment is of critical importance in patients with candidaemia [4]. Previously, various factors such as the presence of a central venous catheter (CVC), total parenteral nutrition (TPN), advanced age and gastrointestinal interventions have been identified as risk factors for the development of candidaemia [5].

Although blood culture is the gold standard for diagnosing candidaemia, it is time‐consuming and detects Candida species in only about 50% of cases, often resulting in delayed treatment until clinical deterioration or death [6]. Several prediction models such as the Candida colonisation index, Candida score, and Candida predictive rule have been developed to estimate the risk of candidaemia and are recommended to guide early antifungal therapy before definitive diagnosis [7, 8, 9, 10]. However, these models have some limitations. The first of these is the Candida colonisation index, which has been found to be effective in a single‐centre study, but the use of defining colonisation alone as a risk factor for the development of candidaemia is highly controversial [7]. Another is the ‘Candida score’, a scoring system based on the evaluation of four risk factors including multifocal Candida colonisation, severe sepsis, TPN, and history of abdominal surgery [8]. Although it has contributed significantly to clinical awareness of high‐risk patients, its applicability in contemporary intensive care settings remains limited. Several components of the score reflect relatively specific clinical scenarios that are not consistently encountered across all ICU populations. For instance, a history of gastrointestinal surgery is more relevant in surgical ICU cohorts but is less frequently observed in predominantly medical ICU populations. Similarly, multifocal Candida colonisation requires systematic surveillance cultures, which are not routinely performed in many centres, leading to substantial variability in its practical use and inter‐centre reproducibility. Another limitation of the Candida score is the inclusion of severe sepsis as one of its core components. Although clinically important, this parameter may be restrictive, since candidaemia does not necessarily present with overt sepsis, particularly in its early stages. In a substantial proportion of patients, signs of sepsis may be absent or not yet fully established at the time when therapeutic decisions are most critical. As a result, reliance on sepsis as a defining criterion may lead to delayed recognition of at‐risk patients or underestimation of candidaemia risk during the early clinical course. On the other hand, several risk factors are strongly associated with the development of candidaemia, with many exerting cumulative effects over time rather than occurring at a single point. Determining when factors such as CVC use, TPN, and MV pose the greatest risk may improve candidaemia risk prediction, identify periods of highest vulnerability, and reduce unnecessary antifungal use. However, the impact of the duration and timing of these risk factors has not been addressed in previous studies. In this international study, we aimed to develop a highly generalisable clinical risk scoring system that incorporates the time‐dependent effects of key risk factors, including the duration of MV, CVC use, and TPN on the development of candidaemia. This scoring system, named CanDi‐Score (Candidaemia Decision Score), was intended to provide early detection and timely treatment of patients at risk for candidaemia.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study Design

This is an international, multicentre, prospective, observational study conducted across 28 centres in 18 different cities across 5 different countries, including Türkiye, Italy, Iran, Egypt and Bosnia and Herzegovina (Figure S1). Ethical approval was obtained from Ankara Bilkent City Hospital, Ethical Approval Committee 1 (TABED 1–24‐138). The study period was determined as four months between 1 May 2024 and 1 September 2024.

Inclusion Criteria

All patients older than 18 years who were hospitalised in the medical, surgical or combined ICUs for more than 48 h and followed by a daily visit by an infectious disease physician or an intensivist with specific expertise were included in the study. Patients were followed for 30 days regardless of whether candidaemia developed or not, and all candidaemia episodes were recorded.

Exclusion Criteria

Candidaemia episodes occurring after day 30 were excluded, as the analysis was limited to the first 30 days of ICU follow‐up. This restriction was applied to reduce heterogeneity arising from structural and organisational differences in ICU practice across health care systems, where patients requiring prolonged critical care may either be transferred to palliative or long‐term care units or, alternatively, remain in the ICU until discharge or death. Centres that reported data exclusively on patients with candidaemia and did not submit data on non‐candidaemia cases were excluded from the study.

Data Collection

Demographic data and pre‐existing comorbidities (including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, heart failure, chronic renal disease, haemodialysis, chronic lung disease, malignancy, chemotherapy within the previous six months, and use of steroids [prednisolone ≥ 20 mg/day or equivalent for at least 3 weeks or equivalent] or other immunosuppressive therapies), comorbidity burden (CCI) were collected. Information on invasive procedures (such as gastrointestinal surgery within the last month, central venous catheterisation, and CVC type, MV, and urinary catheterisation) and their duration (insertion and removal dates) was also documented. Additional potential risk factors that previously predicted candidaemia, such as TPN, sepsis, acute pancreatitis, and prior use of broad‐spectrum antibiotics, were likewise recorded. The presence of neutropenia, concurrent infections (e.g., bacteraemia or other site‐specific infections) and the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment Score (SOFA) at the time of candidaemia were assessed. The severity of illness at ICU admission was evaluated using the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Assessment II (APACHE II) score. The type of Candida, antifungal susceptibilities, and the treatment data for candidaemia were collected. Total length of hospitalisation, patient outcomes (discharge or transfer to a clinical ward, or death), and 30‐day mortality were also assessed. Each patient was followed for 30 days, the predefined cut‐off period for the study. Within this framework, information on the total number of ICU patients, the total number of patient‐days, and the total number of patients who developed candidaemia was recorded.

Potential Risk Factors for Candidaemia

Based on prior literature, the following parameters were considered as potential risk factors for candidaemia: Chronic renal disease, haemodialysis, concurrent infection, bacteraemia, sepsis, TPN, gastrointestinal surgery or instrumentation, prior antibiotic use, invasive procedures including mechanical ventilation and CVC use, multifocal Candida colonisation, and immunosuppression [4, 11]. To assess the time‐dependent effects of selected risk factors, we recorded the total durations of MV, CVC use, TPN administration, and urinary catheterisation in all patients, regardless of candidaemia development. In patients with candidaemia, the duration of these interventions before disease onset was additionally documented.

Definitions

Candidaemia

It was defined as the isolation of at least one Candida species from at least one blood culture bottle in patients with compatible pre‐existing clinical signs and symptoms of infection, in accordance with standard definitions [8, 10]. Blood cultures were obtained using standard aseptic techniques, and no specific requirement for paired or consecutive positive blood cultures was applied for case definition. In clinical practice, Candida species isolated from blood cultures are generally regarded as clinically significant given the rarity of contamination; therefore, repeat blood cultures were not required for the diagnostic definition of candidaemia and were performed at the discretion of the treating physician.

Multifocal Colonisation

The simultaneous isolation of Candida spp. from two or more sites, including urine, oropharyngeal mucosa, respiratory secretions, and inguinal or axillary skin sites, was considered multifocal colonisation [8].

Bacteraemia

Bacteraemia was defined as the isolation of Gram‐negative or Gram‐positive bacteria from at least one blood culture bottle. Coagulase‐negative staphylococcal bacteraemia was considered confirmed only when at least two consecutive blood culture sets yielded the same strain [8].

Sepsis

For the definition of sepsis, we used the Sepsis‐3 criteria, as they present the current and most widely accepted international consensus [12].

Neutropenia

Neutropenia was defined as the absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of lower than 500 cells/mm3 [13].

Concurrent Infection

Concurrent infection was defined as the presence of any clinically documented concomitant infectious focus, and included bacteraemia, pulmonary infections, intraabdominal infections, urinary tract infections, skin and soft tissue infections, surgical site infections, central nervous system infections, and other documented infectious conditions (endocarditis, bone and joint infection, sexually transmitted infections) diagnosed during ICU follow‐up. Concurrent infections were diagnosed based on standard clinical criteria, including compatible clinical signs and symptoms together with microbiological, radiological, and laboratory findings consistent with an active infectious focus, as determined by the treating clinicians. Management decisions, including the initiation of targeted antimicrobial therapy, were made according to the suspected infection site and in line with established clinical guidelines and the judgement of the attending physicians.

Candida Identification

The data on the methods used for Candida spp. identification and antifungal susceptibility testing were collected from all participating sites. In some centres, manual identification (including CHROMagar) followed by broth microdilution for antifungal susceptibility was performed, whereas in others, VitekMS (bioMérieux) and MALDI‐TOF were employed for species identification and antifungal susceptibility testing of Candida isolates.

Statistical Methods

The assumptions required for parametric hypothesis testing were not satisfied for numerical variables. Accordingly, numerical variables were summarised using the median and interquartile range (interquartile range [IQR], 25th–75th percentiles) and compared between candidaemia groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and homogeneity of group variances was evaluated with the Levene test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Pearson chi‐square test if the requirements were met. Otherwise, Fisher's exact test was used.

The dataset was randomly divided into development (80%) and validation (20%) cohorts. Before model development, variables were reviewed for missing data and for the assumptions of logistic regression. A total of 2704 patients were included in the study and randomly split into a development cohort (n = 2,164) and a validation cohort (n = 540). After excluding cases with missing data using complete‐case analysis, 2141 patients remained in the development cohort, and 533 patients remained in the validation cohort for the final analyses. In particular, the linearity of the logit for continuous variables was examined using the Box–Tidwell test. When this assumption was violated, duration‐related variables such as length of ICU stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, CVC duration, and TPN duration were analysed both as continuous variables and as categorised variables based on clinically meaningful cut‐off points. These cut‐off points were determined according to clinical relevance and the empirical distribution of the data. Cases with missing data in variables included in regression analyses were excluded using complete‐case analysis within each cohort. The proportion of missing data for all candidate predictors is presented in the Figure S1. Since missingness was below 5% for all variables except multifocal Candida colonisation, complete‐case analysis was performed without imputation. In contrast, multifocal Candida colonisation demonstrated substantial centre‐dependent missingness because routine colonisation surveillance was not uniformly implemented across participating centres. Therefore, after univariate analysis, multifocal Candida colonisation was excluded from further model development.

In the development cohort, univariate logistic regression analyses were performed to explore potential predictors of candidaemia. All variables evaluated in the univariate analysis were considered candidates for multivariate modelling, except for conceptually overlapping variables (e.g., CVC presence vs. CVC duration; concurrent infection and subtypes of concurrent infection), of which only one was retained. Even though bacteraemia was analysed separately from the broader category of concurrent infections, mutually exclusive stratification (e.g., bacteraemia vs. other infections) was not feasible because a significant proportion of patients had overlapping infection foci.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was then conducted to identify independent risk factors for the development of candidaemia. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multicollinearity was assessed using Adjusted Generalised Variance Inflation Factors (GVIF), and all variables included in the final model had Adjusted GVIF values below 5, indicating no concerning collinearity.

A final predictive model in standard logistic regression analysis was selected using the fast backward variable selection and bootstrap validation approach implemented in the rms package in R. Validation and variable selection were carried out using the validate function in the R rms package. Based on this model, a nomogram was constructed to estimate the individual probability of developing candidaemia in ICU patients. The model's discriminating power was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with 95% confidence intervals. The optimal classification cut‐off for the final model was determined according to Youden's J index. Model performance was further assessed by calculating sensitivity, specificity, and balanced accuracy considering the imbalance between candidaemia and non‐candidaemia cases in both development and validation cohorts. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were conducted using mixed‐effects logistic regression models with centre‐specific random intercepts to account for potential clustering and between‐centre heterogeneity for the final model (Figure S2). The stability of selected predictors across unadjusted and centre‐adjusted analyses was examined.

Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness of fit test and calibration belt analysis. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise stated. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v23.0 for Windows and R software.

Several comorbid conditions, including chronic renal failure, malignancy, recent gastrointestinal surgery, and neutropenia, were more frequent among patients with candidaemia (Table 1).

ICU Interventions and Risk Factors

Patients with candidaemia were more frequently exposed to invasive procedures and supportive therapies. Several comorbid conditions, including chronic renal failure, malignancy, recent gastrointestinal surgery, and neutropenia, were more frequent among patients with candidaemia (Table 1).

TPN was administered significantly more often in patients with candidaemia (40.7% vs. 21.1%, p < 0.001), and TPN duration was longer (p < 0.001). Multifocal Candida colonisation, antibiotic exposure, and concurrent infections were also significantly more common in patients who developed candidaemia (Table 1).

Outcomes

Clinical outcomes differed significantly between groups. ICU mortality was higher in patients with candidaemia than in those without candidaemia (70.1% vs. 38.8%, p < 0.001). Patients with candidaemia also had longer ICU stays (median 33 vs. 14 days, p < 0.001), and ICU stay ≥ 14 days was more frequent in this group (86.8% vs. 50.5%, p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Data on total patient days, average ICU stay per patient, number of other infection events in defined period, other infection rate (patients with ≥ 1 episode), other infection incidence rate (events per 1, 000 patient‐days) are presented in Table S2.

Univariate and Multivariate Analysis of Risk Factors for Candidaemia

In univariate analysis, candidaemia was associated with higher APACHE II score, higher CCI, chronic renal failure, haemodialysis, malignancy, gastrointestinal surgery, neutropenia, mechanical ventilation, CVC and TPN use, prolonged ICU stay, and longer durations of mechanical ventilation, CVC (> 21 days), and TPN (> 14 days) (all significant; see Table 2).

Although multifocal Candida colonisation was higher in patients who developed candidaemia, it could not be assessed in all participating centres. Data on multifocal Candida colonisation were reported in only 928 of the patients (34.3%) in the whole cohort. Multifocal colonisation was excluded from the multivariate analysis due to its limited availability in the study population. This substantial proportion of missing data raised concerns regarding potential selection bias and reduced generalisability if included in the final model. Therefore, to ensure robustness and avoid overfitting based on a non‐representative subset, this variable was not incorporated into the multivariate analysis.

In multivariate analysis, independent predictors were higher CCI, concurrent infection, gastrointestinal surgery, neutropenia, and prolonged CVC and TPN exposure. CVC use > 21 days and TPN use > 14 days remained significant predictors. The risk of candidaemia increased progressively with longer CVC duration, reaching an OR of 10.14 (95% CI: 4.64–22.18, p < 0.0001) for catheter duration longer than 21 days. TPN use for more than 14 days was also independently associated with candidaemia (OR 2.20, 95% CI: 1.32–3.67, p = 0.0025). No significant multicollinearity was detected (all GVIF values < 5) (Table 2).

Nomogram Development and Model Performance

A final predictive model was selected for nomogram construction, incorporating the CCI, concurrent infection, categorical CVC and TPN use (Figure 1). The nomogram demonstrated good discrimination, with an AUC of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.83–0.88). Using the optimal cut‐off probability of 0.069 determined by Youden's J index, the model achieved a sensitivity of 85.1%, a specificity of 72.4%, and a balanced accuracy of 78.7%. The negative predictive value (NPV) was high (98.1%), indicating strong performance in identifying patients unlikely to develop candidaemia (Table 3). In the external validation cohort (total n = 539, candidaemia n = 40; no candidaemia n = 499), the model maintained good discrimination with an AUC of 0.813 (95% CI: 0.747–0.880). At the optimal validation cut‐off of 0.119, sensitivity was 70.0%, specificity was 82.9%, and balanced accuracy was 76.5%.

The nomogram model assigns points to each predictor variable, and the total point score corresponds to the estimated probability of developing candidaemia. Each 2‐unit increase in the CCI corresponds to an approximate increase of 10 points. The presence of at least one concurrent infection contributes 46 points. Similarly, the risk increases with longer duration of CVC use. Compared with no CVC, a duration of 0–7 days corresponds to 46 points, 8–21 days to 75 points, and more than 21 days to 100 points. Total parenteral nutrition (TPN) use for more than 14 days adds 34 points, while no TPN or TPN use ≤ 14 days contributes no additional points.

By summing these values, the total score can be converted into an estimated probability of candidaemia. For example, a total score of 51 corresponds to a probability of approximately 1.0%, 84 points to 2.5%, 109 points to 5.0%, 135 points to 10.0%, 163 points to 20.0%, 182 points to 30.0%, 198 points to 40.0%, 212 points to 50.0%, 226 points to 60.0%, 242 points to 70.0%, and 261 points to 80.0%.

The optimal cut‐off probability identified by Youden's J index was 0.069 (6.9%), which corresponds to approximately 119 total points. Patients with scores above this threshold may be considered at higher risk for candidaemia.

Calibration of the Nomogram Model

Calibration of the nomogram model showed good agreement between predicted and observed outcomes in the development cohort, as indicated by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ 2 = 6.51, df = 8, p = 0.5903), suggesting no evidence of poor model fit and adequate calibration. In the external validation cohort, calibration improved after recalibration, as reflected by a non‐significant Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ 2 = 10.72, df = 8, p = 0.218), while discrimination performance remained unchanged.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed using mixed‐effects logistic regression models incorporating a random intercept for centre to account for potential clustering effects. The results demonstrated that the identified predictors of candidaemia remained after adjustment for centre‐level random effects, indicating robustness of the model specification.

Discussion

Candidaemia is a life‐threatening infection, most commonly encountered in ICU patients, particularly in those with predisposing risk factors. Overall mortality rates reach 40%–60%, with markedly increased mortality observed when effective antifungal therapy is delayed beyond 48 h [2, 14]. Early initiation of empirical treatment plays a key role in reducing mortality [15]. Current guidelines support initiating empirical antifungal treatment in critically ill ICU patients at high risk of candidaemia during clinical deterioration or septic shock [15, 16, 17]. However, identifying such high‐risk patients remains challenging due to the limited number of comprehensive studies and widespread exposure of ICU patients to similar predisposing risk factors such as invasive intervention, including central venouscatheterisation, TPN, and the use of broad‐spectrum antibiotics. In this context, the high mortality rates associated with candidaemia, together with the diagnosis delays and the critical impact of treatment timing on clinical outcomes, have driven clinicians over the past two decades to develop predictive risk scores aimed at the early identification of high‐risk patients and the timely initiation of empirical therapy [7, 8, 9, 10]. Within this context, the present study examined the role of established predisposing risk factors and their time‐dependent effects on the occurrence of candidaemia, with a particular focus on how the timing and cumulative exposure to interventions such as CVC use, TPN administration, and broad‐spectrum antibiotics influence infection risk in critically ill patients. This study showed that high CCI, concurrent infection, neutropenia, and prolonged durations of ICU, MV, CVC use, and TPN are the determinants for the development of candidaemia in ICU patients.

Neutropenia and corticosteroid therapy, recognised immunosuppressive host‐related risk factors, were independently associated with the development of candidaemia [13, 18, 19]. Accordingly, the presence of concurrent infection was identified as a strong independent risk factor for candidaemia, increasing the risk by at least 3.74‐fold. This association may primarily reflect greater disease severity, prolonged hospitalisation, exposure to invasive procedures and use of broad‐spectrum antibiotics, which can disrupt the gut microbiota and selection pressure favouring Candida overgrowth. Supporting this concept, in a Candida score study by León et al., severe sepsis was assigned the factor with the highest weight (2 points) in the development of candidaemia, reflecting the strong association between clinically significant infection and candidaemia [8]. Taken together, these findings support the interpretation that concurrent infections act as a surrogate marker of critical illness severity. In addition, this variable may be influenced, at least in part, by inter‐centre differences in antimicrobial therapy practices, including differences in empirical treatment preferences, treatment duration, and management strategies that could have influenced both candidaemia risk and clinical outcomes, and should therefore be taken into consideration when interpreting these results.

The CCI, a widely used indicator of cumulative comorbidity burden, has been increasingly accepted for identifying critically ill patients who are likely to experience worse clinical outcomes. Although CCI has not traditionally been used as a primary predictor of infection onset in candidaemia studies, evidence from post‐fracture infection prediction models and other clinical settings supports its association with increased susceptibility to infection [20, 21]. Accordingly, TPN use, a surrogate marker of frailty, has been repeatedly identified as a significant risk factor for candidaemia [8, 9, 22]. In this study, we demonstrated a time‐dependent effect of TPN on the risk of candidaemia. Prolonged TPN administration (more than 14 days) was identified as an independent risk factor that increased the risk by 2.63‐fold when compared to TPN use for 14 days or less, or no TPN use.

The presence of CVC is reported to increase mortality in patients with candidaemia, most probably due to mask central line‐associated bloodstream infections [23]. In our study, both the presence and duration of CVC use were strong risk factors for candidaemia, consistent with prior reports identifying CVC presence as a major predisposing factor [19, 24, 25, 26]. In a study, the percentage of patients who have a prolonged CVC use longer than 14 days was significantly higher in patients with candidaemia compared to those with bacteraemia [19, 24]. Kayaaslan et al. reported CVC use as the most determinant factor in ICU patients with COVID‐19, and that patients with CVC have a high risk of candidaemia if any of the other three risk factors proposed in the nomogram model are present [26]. In our study, the presence of CVC causes a time‐dependent increase in the risk of candidaemia; the highest risk is seen after 21 days and longer of CVC use, increasing the risk by 17.21‐fold compared to patients without CVC. These findings indicate that invasive procedures do not increase the risk of candidaemia at a single time point, but have a cumulative effect as time passes, with the risk gradually increasing over time.

In recent years, a substantial number of risk prediction tools or risk stratifying studies have been proposed in the medical literature for the candidaemia, along with several external validation studies of these scores [8, 9, 14, 22, 27]. Although candidaemia prediction models provide valuable insights for identifying patients at high risk of developing candidaemia, it should be remembered that the designs of these studies differ from each other. Candida score study, one of the first and most widely recognised risk prediction models, includes four variables: TPN (1 point), multifocal Candida colonisation (1 point), surgery (1 point), and severe sepsis (2 points). The model recommends initiation of empirical antifungal treatment in patients with a score higher than 2.5 points. However, in the Candida score study, the risk of developing candidaemia was evaluated among patients with Candida colonisation, which was further classified as unifocal colonisation and multifocal colonisation [8]. In evaluating the impact of risk factors, the study did not compare the patients with and without candidaemia. Instead, it aimed to compare patients who developed candidaemia with those who were colonised but did not develop candidaemia. In contrast, in our study, all adult ICU patients were enrolled independently of other risk factors, allowing comprehensive representativeness of the overall ICU population, and not requiring prior colonisation monitoring. Ostrosky–Zeinchner et al. reported a high risk for candidaemia among ICU patients who met the clinical prediction rule. This rule includes exposure to any systemic antibiotic (days 1–3) or presence of a CVC (days 1–3) combined with at least two of the following additional risk factors; any surgery (days −7–0), TPN (days 1–3), any use of steroids (days −7–3), or use of other immunosuppressive agents (days −7–0), any dialysis (days 1–3), pancreatitis (days −7–0). Among patients meeting this rule, the risk of developing candidaemia was reported as 9.9%, with sensitivity 34%, specificity 90%, positive predictive value 9%, NPV 97% [9]. The study of Jameran et al. formulated a more recent risk prediction score based on these four factors, with individual weights assigned to acute kidney injury (2.0), renal replacement therapy (3.9), TPN (4.1), and multifocal Candida colonisation (3.1). A score with a cut‐off value of 5.0 has a sensitivity of 80.3% and specificity of 77.3% [22].

Compared with previously proposed clinical prediction rules and Candida scoring systems, which consistently demonstrate high NPVs but limited positive predictive performance due to the low prevalence of candidaemia, the proposed nomogram showed superior discriminative ability (AUC 0.86) [8, 9]. Similar to earlier studies reporting NPVs ranging from 94% to 99%, the proposed model achieved a high NPV (98.4%), confirming its robustness in excluding patients unlikely to develop candidaemia. Notably, the higher AUC observed in our study suggests improved overall risk stratification while maintaining the key clinical advantage of reliable rule‐out capability. Importantly, the model is based on a small number of routinely available clinical variables, including the CCI, concurrent infection status, and the duration of CVC and TPN use, facilitating straightforward calculation and practical applicability in daily clinical settings.

In the present study, we constructed an easy‐to‐use prediction tool for the development of candidaemia in the form of a nomogram model, CanDi‐Score, consisting of only four risk factors that can be easily assessed in any ICU. CCI, concurrent infection, prolonged central venous catheterisation, and TPN exposure were identified as independent risk factors for the development of candidaemia. Intensive care patients are assessed for each risk parameter for candidaemia, and a total score is obtained based on these four parameters. This total score quantitatively predicts the probability of developing candidaemia. In patients with the highest scores (261), the risk of candidaemia reaches up to 80%. The optimal cut‐off value determined for initiating empirical antifungal treatment is 119, and the probability of developing candidaemia in patients above this threshold is calculated as 6.9%. These findings indicate that the developed nomogram can contribute to the early identification of high‐risk patients in clinical practice. In ICU patients with suspected infection, predicting candidaemia by Candi‐Score and early initiation of empirical antifungal treatment in addition to antibiotic modification may significantly provide a survival advantage.

Antifungal stewardship remains a key issue in the interpretation of prediction models for candidaemia. In this study, a relatively low probability threshold was intentionally selected to prioritise sensitivity and negative predictive value, since delayed diagnosis of candidaemia in critically ill ICU patients may result in poor outcomes. At the same time, such an approach may also increase the risk of unnecessary antifungal use and potential overtreatment. Therefore, the proposed nomogram should not be regarded as an independent decision‐making tool, but rather as a supportive instrument for risk stratification. Its use is intended to complement, rather than replace, clinical evaluation, microbiological findings, and established local stewardship policies.

Multicentre heterogeneity is another important factor that may affect model performance and generalisability. In our analyses, mixed‐effects logistic regression models with centre‐level random intercepts demonstrated that the impact of centre‐level clustering on the main predictor effects was limited. The PPV of candidaemia prediction models is generally limited due to the low incidence of candidaemia in ICU patients. This has also been shown in previous prediction tools. Despite this limitation, our model showed a relatively higher PPV while keeping similar or better discrimination and calibration compared with earlier studies. This suggests that it may be useful for clinical risk stratification when used together with clinical and epidemiological context.

The most significant strengths of our study are its international, multicentre, and prospective design. The systematic collection of patient baseline data through a reliable database system increased the accuracy and internal validity of the data. Furthermore, the inclusion of a broad range of ICUs and patient diversity strengthened the generalisability of the results. The use of a limited number of frequently encountered clinical variables in the study enabled the optimal prediction of candidaemia risk while contributing to the development of a risk score that is easy to apply clinically and has high predictive performance. The developed model has high sensitivity and specificity, particularly a high negative predictive value, allowing for the reliable exclusion of patients at low risk for candidaemia.

This study has some limitations. Firstly, multifocal Candida colonisation could not be included in the study to enhance its representativeness due to the lack of monitoring of Candida colonisation in each centre. Secondly, we could not assess the negative effect of empirical antifungal treatment on the candidaemia diagnosis. However, this is an inevitable property in real‐life studies.

Conclusion

This study explored the contribution of established predisposing risk factors and their time‐dependent effects on the development of candidaemia. CCI and the presence of concurrent infection were identified as key determinants of candidaemia. Our findings further emphasise that not only the presence but also the timing and cumulative duration of exposure, such as central venous catheterisation and TPN, play a crucial role in shaping infection risk in critically ill patients.

These findings highlight the importance of incorporating temporal dynamics into risk assessment models to improve early identification of patients at increased risk for candidaemia and potentially support timely antifungal management.
