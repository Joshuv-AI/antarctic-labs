Exogenous salicylic acid alleviates high temperature-induced inhibition of flower bud differentiation in red raspberry by modulating anther development and phytohormone homeostasis
Authors: Juanjuan Guo, Siqi Cheng, Ziqian Fu, Wenyan Qiao, Yuanyuan Zhu, Xuemei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRed raspberry (Rubus idaeus L.) is rich in bioactive compounds, including tannic acid, raspberry ketone, and anthocyanins, making it a valuable resource for the food and health product industries. This study aims to clarify the flower bud differentiation process of the double-season red raspberry ‘Polka’ under high temperature (HT) conditions. It also evaluates the effects of salicylic acid (SA) on plant development, providing a theoretical basis for regulating flowering and fruiting.ResultsDifferences in soluble protein content, as well as the activities of energy metabolism enzymes, including adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) and cytochrome oxidase (COD), were determined under different treatments during flower bud development. Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), abscisic acid (ABA), gibberellic acid (GA3), and zeatin riboside (ZR) were also measured across treatments. Flower bud differentiation of “Polka” followed eight morphologically distinct stages from undifferentiated to pistil primordium formation, which takes about 28 days. Subsequent pollen development was characterized in five phases from the pollen mother cell stage to the mature stage. HT stress at the pollen mother cell stage disrupts tapetum function, leading to defective microspore development and abnormal stamen or anther formation. Reduced IAA, ZR, and GA₃ levels, together with increased ABA, disrupt hormonal balance, thereby inhibiting flower bud differentiation and male gametophyte development.ConclusionsExogenous SA (150 µmol·L− 1) alleviates anther structure damage, enhances microspore formation, regulates hormone balance, promotes protein synthesis and energy metabolism, and improves HT tolerance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09281-4.

Introduction

Red raspberry (Rubus idaeus L.) is a perennial berry shrub belonging to the genus Rubus in the family Rosaceae. The fruits [1], leaves [2], and seeds [3] are rich in a wide range of bioactive compounds. Together, these compounds provide high nutritional value as well as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and medicinal benefits, supporting their broad use in functional foods, health teas, and bioactive oils. Current research on red raspberry primarily concentrates on its applications in food [4] and medicine [5], as well as on introduction and breeding technologies [6]. Flower bud differentiation marks a critical phase in the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth in plants [7]. Research on flower bud differentiation in red raspberry has predominantly focused on morphological observation and characterization of its developmental process. Q Huang and J Lei [8] identified the key developmental stages of flower organogenesis in red raspberry. Heide and Sønsteby [9] investigated the mechanisms of flower bud induction and dormancy regulation in single-season and double-season red raspberry cultivars. However, the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying this process, particularly those related to double season, remain insufficiently explored.

In recent years, global warming has intensified, and the frequency of extreme high temperature (HT) events has increased significantly, posing a serious threat to the safe production of small berry crops such as red raspberry [10]. For double season red raspberries, the budding period of summer and autumn fruits often overlaps with the seasonal high temperature period. Flower bud differentiation and pollen development are extremely sensitive to temperature at this stage, and short-term high temperatures can lead to flower bud failure and decreased pollen vitality. It becomes a key bottleneck restricting the yield and quality of the second season. HT stress severely impairs plant reproductive development, with male organs being particularly vulnerable. Elevated temperatures can induce male sterility by impairing pollen viability, anther dehiscence, and microspore maturation. This effect is closely associated with structural and cytological abnormalities, including premature tapetum degradation, meiotic chromosome aberrations, and defective pollen tube growth [11]. HT stress compromises photosynthetic efficiency and disrupts hormonal homeostasis [12, 13].

These disturbances interfere with flower bud differentiation and reproductive development, ultimately leading to reductions in fertilization success, fruit set, fruit enlargement, quality, and overall crop yield. Therefore, elucidating the mechanism by which double season red raspberries respond to high temperature stress during the budding stage and seeking effective mitigation strategies have important theoretical and practical value.

Recent studies have demonstrated that exogenous salicylic acid (SA) functions as a fundamental signaling molecule in alleviating HT-induced reproductive disorders by coordinating cellular protection, redox homeostasis, and hormonal regulation [14]. Under HT stress, SA enhances the expression of HSP and increases antioxidant enzyme activities. As a result, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation is reduced, helping to stabilize membrane integrity and maintain photosynthetic performance [15]. At the reproductive level, SA protects pollen mother cell (PMC) meiosis by preventing chromosome aberrations and ensuring proper synapsis and meiotic progression [16]. Meanwhile, SA preserves tapetum function by suppressing premature programmed cell death (PCD) through enhanced ROS scavenging. It also regulates the expression of tapetum-associated genes, including EAT1, MIL2, and DTM1 [17]. Together, these effects help maintain nutrient supply to microspores and ensure normal pollen wall formation under elevated temperature conditions. Moreover, SA contributes to reproductive resilience by modulating hormonal homeostasis in the anther. Through crosstalk with phytohormones such as jasmonic acid (JA) and abscisic acid (ABA), it helps prevent reproductive failure caused by hormone imbalances [18]. Heat-stressed crops such as tomato [15], pepper [19], and grape [20] exhibit improved flower bud differentiation, fruit set, and overall reproductive performance. Therefore, investigating the regulatory mechanisms of SA in floral bud differentiation of red raspberry under HT stress is crucial. It contributes to a deeper understanding of the hormonal basis of thermotolerance in woody fruit crops.

Moreover, it may inform the development of targeted strategies, including SA-based agrochemicals and molecular breeding approaches, to enhance production stability under climate change.

Previous studies have shown that temperatures above 35 ℃ cause a 5.5-fold reduction in the number of small red raspberry fruits. They also found that the proportion of marketable fruit drops drastically to negligible levels, demonstrating that HT severely compromises both yield and fruit quality. Based on this, we propose the following verifiable working hypothesis: exogenous SA can alleviate the differentiation process of red raspberry flower buds and the physiological state of male infertility under high temperature by regulating endogenous hormone balance and enhancing ROS clearance ability. To verify these hypotheses, this study aims to systematically investigate the morphological, cytological, and physiological responses of flower bud differentiation under different temperature conditions. It emphasizes the regulatory role of exogenous SA in alleviating high temperature stress. To elucidate the flowering regulation mechanism of double season red raspberry and provide theoretical support and practical guidance for optimizing cultivation management and maintaining yield stability under high temperature conditions.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

The experimental site is located in Baoding, Hebei Province, China (115°26′36.28″E, 38°49′18.62″N; elevation: 26 m). It is characterized by a warm temperate continental monsoon climate, with an average annual temperature of 13.4 ℃, annual precipitation of 500–550 mm. The area also receives approximately 2511 h of sunshine per year. The experimental material used was the double-cropping red raspberry cultivar ‘Polka’, characterized by vigorous growth and uniform fruit size.

Experimental design

In June and October 2022, flower buds and buds of ‘Polka’ red raspberry at different developmental stages were collected from the Experimental Garden of Hebei Agricultural University (Jingxiu District, Baoding City). Combining stereomicroscopy and paraffin sectioning technology, the development of flower buds was divided into pollen mother cell stage (L1, 1.0–2.0 mm), meiosis stage (L2, 2.0–3.0 mm), mononucleate stage (L3, 3.0–4.0 mm), binucleate stage (L4, 4.0–5.0 mm) and maturity stage (L5, > 5.0 mm). The samples were fixed and preserved by FAA for anther structure observation. Fifteen open flowers were randomly sampled, and their floral morphological traits were measured to characterize their features. In mid-to-late June 2022, HT stress conditions were established in the greenhouse of the raspberry research base in Shitai Village, Baoding City, with noon temperatures reaching ≥ 35 ℃ for three consecutive days. Suitable temperature controls were set in October, when noon temperatures remained ≤ 30 ℃ for three consecutive days. Flower buds at each developmental stage were collected for physiological measurements and structural analysis.

Additionally, the floral developmental dynamics of ten healthy plants were monitored, and pollen germination rates at the bud-breaking stage, as well as characteristics of mature fruits, were assessed. To evaluate the effect of SA on alleviating HT stress, plants at the 75 cm height stage (prior to anther development) were selected. Foliar applications of SA were conducted at concentrations of 50, 100, and 150 µmol·L− 1, with each plant receiving 50 mL of solution.

Control plants were similarly sprayed with an equivalent volume of distilled water devoid of any solvent. Treatments were applied by foliar spraying once every three days for a total of three applications, and samples were collected on the eighth day after the final treatment.

Each treatment was repeated three times, with 10 plants per replicate. Detailed data on plant height and flower bud differentiation at each stage can be found in Fig. S1. HT stress was imposed at 35–40 ℃ daytime for 7–10 days, while optimal controls were maintained at 25–30 ℃ daytime. Detailed profiles are in Tabs. S1-S2 and Fig. S2. In October, five additional SA concentrations (50–250 µmol·L− 1) were applied when plants reached 75 cm in height. Buds at the bud-breaking stage were collected to assess pollen germination rates.

Measurement indicators and methods

Paraffin sectioning

Paraffin sections were prepared following standard protocols [21]. Paraffin sections were stained with safranin-fix-green using a KD-20 hand-cranked rotary microtome. The sections were observed using a DM4000 microscope. Rinse with water for 24 h to remove the fixative. Then prepare a 1% safranin solution in 50% ethanol and stain the samples for 24 h. Next, dehydrate the material successively with 70%, 80%, and 95% ethanol, allowing 2 h for each stage. This is followed by three rounds of dehydration with anhydrous ethanol, each lasting 1–1.5 h. After dehydration, clear the material successively using xylene mixtures in the following order: 1/5 (xylene: anhydrous ethanol = 1: 4), 2/5, 3/5, 4/5, pure xylene 1, and pure xylene 2, with 1.5 h for each stage. Finally, in a 50 ℃ constant temperature oven, gradually add crushed paraffin to a small amount of xylene in the cleared material until saturation, which takes approximately 24 h. In a 60 ℃ constant temperature oven, the slides were sequentially passed through pure wax 1 and pure wax 2, each for 3 h. The slides then underwent steps including embedding and trimming, cutting wax strips, spreading, dewaxing, dehydration, and restaining with green. Finally, the slides were sealed and boxed.

Physiological indicators

Soluble protein (SP) content was quantified using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue assay [22].

Weigh 1 g of fresh sample and place it in a pre-cooled mortar. Add pre-cooled phosphate buffer and grind it into a homogenate on an ice bath. Transfer the homogenate to a centrifuge tube and centrifuge at 4 °C and 12,000 × g for 20 min. The supernatant is the crude protein extract. Take an appropriate amount of enzyme solution, add Coomassie Brilliant Blue solution, and measure the OD value after the reaction.

Cytochrome oxidase (COD) activity was measured following the method of Simon [23].

Adenosine Triphosphatase (ATPase) activity was assessed using the protocol provided by Ruixin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Take 1 g of fresh sample, add 10 mL of phosphate buffer (50 mmol·L− 1 pH = 7.0), grind in an ice bath, centrifuge at 4,000 rpm for 15 min, and collect the supernatant as the COD enzyme solution. Add 9 volumes of physiological saline at a weight (g): volume (ml) ratio of 1: 9, mechanically homogenize at 2500 r·min− 1 under ice-water bath conditions, centrifuge for 10 min, and take the supernatant as ATPase solution. ATPase activity is determined by colorimetric method, with 1 unit of ATPase activity defined as the amount of ATP catalyzed by 1 mg of protein to produce 1 µmol·L− 1 of inorganic phosphorus within 1 h at an absorbance value of 636 nm.

Hormone extraction and purification were conducted following the protocol described by Yang et al. [24]. The contents of auxin (IAA), abscisic acid (ABA), Zeatin Riboside (ZR), and Gibberellic Acid (GA3) were quantified by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (1290; Agilent, Palo Alto, CA, USA) in conjunction with mass spectrometry (Qtrap 6500; AB SCIEX, Boston, MA, USA), referred to as HPLC-MS/MS. Extracted with acetonitrile at low temperature, purified using a C18 solid-phase extraction column (Anpel Laboratory Technologies Inc., Shanghai, China), dried under nitrogen, and finally reconstituted in methanol. HPLC conditions were as follows: column temperature 30 °C; gradient elution with methanol and water; flow rate 0.3 mL·min− 1; and injection volume 2 µL.

Mass spectrometry was performed with the following settings: ESI ionization, positive and negative ion mode; MRM scan mode; spray voltage ± 4500 V; nebulizer pressure 65 psi; nebulizer temperature 400 °C. The standard curve was established using plant hormone standards (Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri, USA). Each treatment was repeated three times.

Pollen germination rate, fruit setting rate and deformed fruit rate

Buds at the bud-break stage were collected, and the anthers were carefully excised. After drying, pollen grains were released and cultured in vitro in the dark at 25 °C for 12 h. The germination medium consisted of 13% sucrose, 0.05% boric acid, and 0.001% calcium chloride. For each sample, pollen germination was assessed by randomly selecting five microscopic fields of view. Pollen grains with pollen tube lengths exceeding their own diameter were considered germinated, and the germination rate was subsequently calculated.

Fruit setting rate (%) = total number of fruits set/total number of flowers (fruit setting standard is the number of small drupes > 50).

Deformed fruit rate (%) = number of deformed fruits/total number of fruits (deformed fruit standard is irregular shape and single fruit weight < 1 g).

Data processing and analysis

Experimental data were organized using Microsoft Excel 2016, visualized with Origin 2024, and subjected to correlation analysis, principal component analysis, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS 26.0. Statistical significance was determined at P < 0.05.

Capital letters denote significant differences among treatments within the same time period, while lowercase letters indicate significant differences across time periods within the same treatment.

Results and analysis

Characteristics of flower bud differentiation of double-season red raspberry

Flower bud differentiation process of double-season red raspberry

Based on morphological observations, the flower bud differentiation of raspberry can be categorized into eight sequential stages. These stages are: undifferentiated stage, early differentiation stage, inflorescence primordium differentiation stage, floret primordium differentiation stage, sepal primordium differentiation stage, petal primordium differentiation stage, stamen primordium differentiation stage, and pistil primordium differentiation stage.

Each stage is characterized by distinct morphological and histological features (Figs. 1 and 2). The undifferentiated flower buds appear as blunt, triangular protuberances, characterized by a darker coloration and sparse pubescence (Figs. 1A and 2A). In the early differentiation stage, the growth point expands into a hemispherical form, the bud enlarges with increased villi, and the bud scales loosen (Figs. 1B and 2B). The apex broadens, and lateral protrusions curve inward to form an arc-shaped inflorescence primordium (Figs. 1C and D and 2C and D). Subsequently, the tip of the inflorescence axis enlarges, initiating the first floret primordium (Figs. 1E and 2E). During the sepal primordium stage, five inward-curving cylindrical protrusions emerged at the apex. The bud body became smaller, but its aspect ratio increased (Figs. 1F and G and 2F). Spherical petal primordia formed on the inner side of the buds, which continued to shrink as the bud scales closed (Figs. 1H and J and 2G). Tongue-shaped stamen primordia differentiated, accompanied by increased hair density, reduced volume, and partial splitting of the outer scales (Figs. 1I and L and 2H). The pistil primordium emerged in the central region, accompanied by the splitting of the outer bud scales and exposure of the inner scales. The aspect ratio peaked at this stage (Figs. 1K and 2K), indicating the completion of floral organ differentiation and the transition to the flower bud development phase. Overall, flower bud differentiation progresses from the outer to the inner regions and exhibite a clear sequential and phase-specific pattern. During this process, bud size decreases and the shape becomes more tapered, while the aspect ratio gradually increases. At the same time, the scales undergo dynamic changes in closure and opening.

The differentiation of flower buds on the primary stem of red raspberry progressed gradually with plant growth (Fig. S2). On June 5, when the plant reached 30 cm in height with nine true leaves, the flower buds remained in the undifferentiated stage. About a week later, at 38 cm in height with 11 true leaves, the plant entered the early differentiation stage. By June 15, reaching 44 cm with 13 true leaves, it progressed to the inflorescence primordium differentiation stage, which lasted approximately 10 days with accelerated growth. On June 26, at 67 cm and 20 true leaves, it entered the floret and sepal primordium differentiation stage, during which growth slowed and persisted for about 5 days. The differentiation of petal, stamen, and pistil primordia occurred sequentially, with intervals of four and two days, respectively. At the completion of differentiation, the plant reached approximately 80 cm in height and bore 25 true leaves. Flower bud differentiation was closely correlated with plant height and leaf number, exhibiting a growth pattern characterized by an initial rapid phase, followed by a slowdown, and then accelerated growth again.

Study on anther wall development and flower characteristics of red raspberry

The mature red raspberry anther cross-section exhibits a bilaterally symmetrical, four-locular structure (Fig. 3A). In the early stage, it consists of the protoderm and underlying primary meristem, with an oval cross-sectional shape (Fig. 3B). The four-lobed cells divide to form a petal-like structure with four lobes. Archosporocytes differentiate from the inner protoderm of each lobe and develop into megaspore mother cells. Concurrently, the inner wall, middle layer, and tapetum form, establishing the typical anther wall structure (Fig. 3C, D).

The connective develops from procambium division and comprises vascular bundles surrounded by thin-walled cells. Each anther wall layer exhibits distinct developmental features. Epidermal cells display prominent nuclei initially but degenerate by maturity (Fig. 3C, H). Endothecium cells thicken and become fibrous in later stages, facilitating dehiscence (Fig. 3G). Middle layer cells disintegrate prior to maturity (Fig. 3D, H). The glandular tapetum peaks in activity before meiosis and subsequently degenerates to nourish developing microspores (Fig. 3C, D, F, G). At pollen release, only the thickened endothecium and degenerated epidermis remain, while the pollen sacs rupture to facilitate pollen dispersal (Fig. 3-H).

‘Polka’ red raspberry bears bisexual flowers arranged in racemes. The corolla is white at anthesis, with an average diameter of 10.03 mm (range: 9.16–13.59 mm) (Tab. S3). The floral organ measurements are as follows. Petal length averaged 6.24 mm (5.44–7.31 mm) with a width of 3.06 mm (2.47–3.43 mm). Calyx length was 5.75 mm (4.02–7.02 mm) and width 3.89 mm (3.16–4.60 mm). Filament length measured 2.69 mm (1.31–3.90 mm), while style length was 2.33 mm (1.92–2.93 mm). The ovary is conical, measuring an average height of 3.23 mm (2.10–2.93 mm) and a width of 3.16 mm (2.72–3.48 mm).

Effects of HT stress on physiological processes during red raspberry bud development

Effects of HT stress on the development of red raspberry buds

After differentiation, red raspberry flower buds progress through the bud, small bud, medium bud, large bud, and extra-large bud stages sequentially (Fig. 4A, E), spanning approximately 13 days in total. Under optimal temperature conditions, anther development proceeds in an orderly fashion. The tapetum differentiates and degrades on schedule, and pollen mother cells undergo normal meiosis. Meanwhile, pollen grains mature normally, progressing from clover-shaped mononucleate to binucleate forms. Pollen is then successfully released following anther wall thickening and septum rupture (Fig. 5A, F). HT treatment significantly disrupted anther development. From the bud stage, stamens and pistils showed yellowing (Fig. 4K). Anther layer differentiation was poor (Fig. 5G), and pollen mother cells degenerated. During the small to large bud stages, anther cells collapsed and the tapetum became disorganized (Fig. 5H-K). In the extra-large bud stage, cell layers failed to separate at the another junction, leading to pollen loss and failure of pollen release (Figs. 4O and 5L and L). This suggests that normal development and timely disintegration of the anther structure and tapetum are critical for pollen formation and release. HT stress disrupts this process by impairing the structure and function of the anther chamber.

Effects of HT stress on the flowering process and fruiting of red raspberry

Under optimal temperature conditions, red raspberry buds appear green and swollen, measuring approximately 5.6 mm in diameter before splitting and exhibiting five shallow ridges. The flowering process of a single floret proceeds through five stages: bud splitting, initial opening, sepal flattening, petal abscission, and stamen and pistil wilting (Fig. 6A-E).

On the first day, the bud apex cracked, exposing the stigma and anthers. By the second day, the anthers dehisced and released pollen; the stigma, located slightly below the anthers, was covered with mucus. On the third day, some petals began to unfold, and the anthers turned brown. By the fourth day, the petals had fallen, and the stigma began to brown. On the fifth day, the filaments dried up, the stigma was completely brown, and fruit development commenced. Under HT, although flower buds opened sequentially (Fig. 6F-J), the pollen content in the anthers was reduced. The stigma browned prematurely, and the pistil exhibited twisting and wilting. These abnormalities impaired normal fertilization and fruit set. Fruit development was ultimately impaired under HT stress. This was reflected by reductions in longitudinal and transverse fruit diameters, single fruit weight, and fruit set rate, along with an increase in the proportion of deformed fruits (Fig. S3). Compared with the optimal temperature, the single fruit weight and fruit set rate decreased significantly by 57.81 g and 37.74% under HT conditions. The rate of deformed fruit increased from 13.37 ± 1.13% in ST to 52.62 ± 1.77% (P < 0.05). These results indicate that HT stress significantly disrupts fruit formation in red raspberry by affecting floral organ development.

Effects of HT on hormone and energy metabolism enzyme contents during red raspberry bud development

The SP content in red raspberry flower buds showed a general decreasing trend during development. Compared to the optimum temperature control, the SP level under HT stress at the L4 stage decreased significantly by 31.15% (P < 0.05). HT markedly suppressed COD activity during the early stages of anther development (L1-L3) and ATPase activity throughout L1-L4. Effects of exogenous SA on anther development of red raspberry under HT stress

The development of red raspberry anthers goes through five stages: pollen mother cell, meiosis, mononucleate pollen, dinucleate pollen and mature pollen. Exogenous SA markedly improves abnormal anther development caused by HT stress. The SA3 treatment preserved the integrity of the anther wall, promoted proper differentiation and timely degradation of the tapetum, and ensured normal pollen development and shedding. SA3 treatment showed the most pronounced effect, with germination rates reaching 17.33% under HT-an increase of 188.33% compared to the control. Under suitable temperature conditions, the germination rate reached 18.00% (Fig. S6). These results indicate that an appropriate concentration of SA can significantly enhance red raspberry pollen germination and mitigate the adverse effects of HT stress.

Discussion

Characteristics of flower bud differentiation in double-season red raspberry

Flower bud differentiation marks the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth.

Different plant species exhibit distinct characteristics and staging criteria for flower bud differentiation. But they generally follow conserved developmental principles within Rosaceae [7]. Based on cherry [25] and strawberry [26] models, double-season red raspberry flower bud differentiation was defined into eight stages from meristem initiation to pistil primordium formation. The order of floral organ primordium initiation—inflorescence, floret, sepal, petal, stamen, and pistil, suggesting a conserved pattern of floral organogenesis [27].

The conserved sequence of floral organ primordium development, also observed in “Glen Ample” red raspberry, supports the biological validity of this staging framework [28]. Flower buds of the double-season “Polka” exhibited a “small-large-small” longitudinal pattern, with size peaking at the floret primordium stage. The buds then shrank as petal and pistil primordia formed. This pattern likely reflects apical meristem widening and lateral cell division [29].

Bud length thus indicates floral developmental status [30].

The double-season red raspberry ‘Polka’ completes flower bud differentiation within the same year, requiring approximately 28 days from initial differentiation to bud formation. In contrast, single-season cultivars enter inflorescence differentiation before overwintering and complete the process the following spring [31, 32]. The difference likely arises from the coordinated effects of environmental cues and divergent genetic regulation. Ecologically, such phenological divergence highlights divergent reproductive tactics. Double-season cultivars evade heat stress via adaptive flexibility, whereas single-season ones align flowering with yearly cycles for reliable success in stable settings [33]. Molecularly, Rubus annual-fruiting traits link to loci RiAF3 and RiAF4, with candidate regulators differentiating floral induction between annual- and biennial-fruiting raspberry cultivars [34]. These genetic differences likely underlie divergent photoperiod responses. Double-season cultivars exhibit weaker day-length dependence, allowing earlier or repeated floral transitions within one year. In contrast, single-season cultivars rely more strongly on seasonal cues, confining differentiation to a single extended cycle [35].

Favorable temperatures and shortening day length accelerate floral initiation through integration of circadian and light-signaling pathways. In contrast, long photoperiods or temperature extremes delay differentiation by activating stress-responsive pathways [36].

These processes are mediated by conserved CO–FT–like flowering modules that integrate photoperiod, temperature, and hormonal cues [37]. In double-season cultivars, elevated Ehd1 promotes RFT1 to bypass short-day constraints, whereas Ghd7 repression in single-season types delays differentiation [35, 38]. Moreover, abiotic stresses such as drought can further modulate hormone signaling and the expression of flowering-related genes. This modulation helps fine-tune the timing of bud differentiation and provides double-season cultivars with greater phenological plasticity under variable environmental conditions [39, 40]. In summary, double-season ‘Polka’ undergoes rapid, staged flower bud differentiation with orderly organ primordium development. Unlike single-season varieties, it completes differentiation without overwintering dormancy, reflecting greater phenological flexibility.

Effects of HT on the development of plant buds and flowering process

HT stress is a major abiotic constraint limiting reproductive success in many plant [11], with male reproductive organs generally exhibiting greater sensitivity than female counterparts [41]. Successful pollen formation relies on the precise coordination of anther cell division, meiosis, and tapetum development [42]. The tapetum, the innermost anther layer, critically supports microspore development by supplying nutrients, enzymes, lipids and wall precursors. Its disruption often causes male sterility in crops [43]. The function of the tapetum and the timeliness of PCD are regulated by conserved pathways such as DYT1-AMS, and are crucial for pollen wall formation and fertility [44]. Premature or delayed degradation of the tapetum disrupts nutrient supply and signal transduction, leading to pollen abortion [45].

HT stress can cause the tapetum structure of red raspberries to become blurred, abnormally swollen, and prematurely degraded, destroying the nutritional and signal support required for microspore development, and thus causing pollen abortion. Similar tapetum heat damage has also been observed in cereals [46], tomatoes [47], and poplars [48]. Consistent with this, browning of the stamens (anthers) of red raspberries occurs earlier than that of the pistils. This may be due to the higher heat sensitivity of the stamens during meiosis and pollen mother cell development [49]. HT temperature stress interferes with anther differentiation, microspore formation, and pollen development, leading to reduced pollen fertility or even male sterility.

As a result, the fruit set rate of red raspberries decreases, single fruit weight is reduced, and the proportion of deformed fruits increases. These findings are consistent with reports in sweet cherry [50] and tomato [51].

Additionally, HT-induced accumulation of ROS and hormonal imbalances are considered primary mechanisms causing abnormal floral organ development [52]. As a key stress hormone, ABA accumulation is closely related to abnormal male organ development. ABA activates the PYR/PYL-PP2C-SnRK2 pathway, inducing ROS accumulation and PCD. It interferes with anther sugar metabolism and cell wall degrading enzyme expression, leading to abnormal male development in both cotton [53] and wheat [54]. The ABA content in red raspberry flower buds may increase due to the upregulation of genes such as NCED3. And it promotes the accumulation of ROS through the aforementioned pathways, thereby inducing premature PCD in the perianth. Its accumulation, along with elevated ABA/IAA and ABA/ZR ratios, collectively inhibits related enzyme activities, limiting starch accumulation and nutrient transport [47]. HT inhibit the expression of auxin biosynthesis genes (such as YUC2, YUC6, and TAA1), leading to a decrease in IAA levels [55]. IAA deficiency restricts nutrient transport, resulting in insufficient energy for microspore development and inducing male sterility [56]. In rice and wheat, the decrease in IAA under HT disrupts microspore development, and exogenous IAA has a reversible effect, restoring MCM5 gene expression [57]. Simultaneously, the biosynthesis of IAA, GA3 and ZR in red raspberry buds may be inhibited by the downregulation of genes such as YUC2/6 and TAA1. Thereby it exacerbates hormonal imbalance and reproductive development disorders. However, in species such as lily [58] and cotton [59], elevated IAA or ABA may indirectly induce sterility by regulating ethylene production or ABA-mediated cellular senescence pathways. They showed interspecific variation [56]. An increased ZR/IAA ratio further disrupts microspore formation [58].

HT stress reduces GA3 levels, interfering with DELLA protein-mediated signaling pathways. Then it leads to delayed PCD and meiosis arrest in the tapetum, thereby inhibiting pollen maturation [60]. Red raspberries showed an overall decrease in GA3 levels at stages L1, L4, and L5, consistent with the findings in grapes [61] and watermelons [62]. A decreased GA3 /ABA ratio further exacerbates male reproductive organ dysfunction [63]. The elevated ZR/IAA ratio in red raspberries differs from that in rapeseed (where ZR content is reduced in sterile lines) [64] but is similar to that in cabbage (where ZR content is increased) [65]. This may be because the role of ZR content in male development varies from species to species.

The synergistic imbalance between ABA/ZR and IAA further interferes with anther development [66]. ROS causes oxidative damage and disrupts tapetal PCD and meiosis, while ABA accumulation further amplifies ROS production, creating a vicious cycle [67].

Simultaneously, hormonal imbalances (such as decreased IAA and GA3 deficiency) interact with ROS, exacerbating nutrient deficiencies and abnormal PCD timing [56]. This interaction network ultimately disrupts tapetal function and microspore wall formation by inhibiting the expression of key genes such as AMS. And it affects non-cellular autonomous signaling regulation, leading to stamen sterility [43, 68]. Such variations in red raspberry may stem from evolutionary adaptations to temperate climates, influencing hormone sensitivity and biosynthetic pathways. It highlights the need for species-tailored breeding strategies to mitigate HT-induced sterility.

Anther and pollen abnormalities or abortion are frequently linked to deficiencies in total and soluble protein content [69]. Under HT stress, the soluble protein content in red raspberry flower buds decreased significantly. This is similar to the phenomenon observed in soybean male-sterile lines, where protein synthesis and accumulation are impaired [70]. However, species adaptation leads to differences in response. Red raspberries, as a temperate berry, show a particularly significant decrease in protein levels during early development (L1-L3). It may be related to the upregulation of HSP that exacerbates proteolysis [71]. In contrast, soybeans, with their nitrogen-fixing capabilities as legumes, may possess stronger homeostasis maintenance capabilities [72]. The high sensitivity of red raspberries to temperature fluctuations may stem from their limited domestication mechanisms. For example, insufficient induction of molecular chaperone proteins can lead to accelerated protein denaturation. This, in turn, results in metabolic disorders [70].

Energy shortages severely weaken pollen viability [73]. High temperature stress can significantly inhibit the activity of cytochrome c oxidase and ATPase in red raspberry flower buds. This may be due to the unique vulnerability of mitochondria in berry plants, or related to the high background oxidative load generated by their polyphenol synthesis pathway [74].

This inhibitory effect contrasts with the phenomenon in fertile onion systems where the activities of two types of enzymes are significantly higher than in sterile materials, thereby maintaining vigorous respiration and ATP synthesis [74]. The latter may benefit from the stabilizing effect of the bulb storage adaptation mechanism on enzyme activity. The high energy requirements of microspore development lead to a decrease in COD and ATPase activity, as well as a reduction in total respiration rate, resulting in insufficient ATP supply

This, in turn, causes abnormal microspore morphology and male sterility [75]. Regulatory factors such as gene mutations, oxidative stress, and calcium signaling further impair mitochondrial respiratory chain efficiency by inhibiting COD and ATPase activity, thereby weakening ATP synthesis and increasing the risk of sterility [76]. The unique calcium signaling perturbation in red raspberries may further exacerbate energy metabolism defects under HT. This characteristic may stem from the evolution of traits in Rosaceae plants that favor fruit quality over reproductive heat tolerance [77]. In the future, targeted genomic interventions will be needed to enhance their stress resistance.

Effects of SA on flower bud differentiation of plant under HT stress

Exogenous SA can selectively correct disorders caused by high temperature stress through a targeted mechanism [78]. Previous studies have shown that application of SA during anther development in various plant species helps maintain tapetum structural integrity and significantly mitigates male sterility induced by environmental stress [79]. This highlights the crucial regulatory role of SA in stabilizing plant reproductive development.

Exogenous SA enhances the antioxidant defense system in red raspberry, thereby preserving tapetal functionality and mitigating HT-induced structural disruptions during anther development [80]. Treatment with 150 µmol·L⁻¹ SA restored tapetum morphology by protecting cellular integrity and regulating the timing of PCD. This prevented abnormal swelling during meiosis and premature disintegration at the mononucleate stage, which would otherwise lead to microspore abortion [16]. The treated buds exhibit restored tapetum morphology and delayed degradation. Spraying SA during anther development can reduce ROS accumulation in anthers, inhibit excessive PCD in the tapetum (including reducing Caspase-3 activity), and prevent its degradation [81, 82]. This helps alleviate heat-induced pollen abortion by maintaining structural stability and ensuring nutrient supply to microspores [81]. Similar to the mechanism by which SA in tomatoes enhances heat resistance through increased antioxidant metabolism and regulation of HSP [83]. Compared with other Rosaceae species exhibiting strong yet variable antioxidant capacities, red raspberry displays a pronounced antioxidant response to SA [16]. Such responsiveness is in line with the generally high phenolic levels and antioxidant potential characteristic of rosaceous fruits and leaves.

This high early responsiveness may stem from its polyphenol-rich metabolic background and perennial growth habits, resulting in a higher background oxidative load in temperate fluctuating environments [77]. Thus it require stronger enzyme activity to maintain tapetum stability. These differences highlight the evolutionary adaptation strategies of red raspberries that prioritize fruit quality and bud survival.

SA precisely reprograms the hormonal network in plants under HT stress [18]. SA treatment effectively alleviated the endogenous hormone imbalance during red raspberry flower bud development caused by HT stress. This result is largely consistent with the stress resistance effect of SA on hibiscus [84] and grapes [85], namely, promoting the accumulation of IAA and GA3 to enhance growth and stress resistance. SA maintains hormonal homeostasis and promotes recovery from heat stress by interacting with JA, GA3 and natural cytokinins [58]. This is consistent with the study by Nazar et al. [86], who emphasized the role of SA in restoring balance at HT, providing a basis for the application of SA in raspberries to prevent male development disorders [87, 88]. Unlike the sustained suppression of IAA and ZR commonly observed under heat stress in many plant systems, the recovery of IAA and ZR levels in red raspberry (‘Polka’) is particularly pronounced in the mid-term stages (L2–L3). It potentially contributes to partial mitigation of reproductive damage in this perennial Rosaceae crop. This may be related to its shallow-rooted perennial growth habit and its strategy of flower bud differentiation in summer and autumn [89]. SA can inhibit ABA accumulation by downregulating key genes in ABA biosynthesis (such as NCED) and simultaneously regulate GA signaling by affecting the stability and activity of DELLA protein [90]. In this way, SA can reprogram the GA₃/ABA ratio, alleviate stress-induced hormonal imbalances, and thus support reproductive development under high-temperature stress [91]. This strategy prioritizes carbon allocation to fruiting branches, resulting in a delayed yet resilient adaptive response that contrasts with the rapid metabolic flux typical of cereal crops.

Exogenous SA safeguards mitochondrial function and elevates ATP synthesis efficiency in plant under HT stress, thereby alleviating energy deficits that compromise anther development and male fertility [19, 20]. SA pretreatment can alleviate the damage caused by HT stress by enhancing H⁺-ATPase activity [92]. This enzyme can improve membrane stability, promote nutrient absorption and improve overall energy flux [93]. SA’s protective effect also extends to regulating mitochondrial efficiency. By influencing respiratory pathways such as alternative oxidase expression or succinate dehydrogenase activity, it helps maintain ATP synthesis under stress-induced electron transport disruption [94]. The recovery of COD and ATPase activity during key reproductive stages in red raspberries provides a sustained energy supply for tapetum secretion and microspore maturation. Compared to annual monocotyledons, the SA-induced response in red raspberries is often characterized by uniform regulation of mitochondrial respiration and ROS management across all developmental stages [95]. This may be to adapt to its higher basal oxidative load and seasonal fluctuations, thus requiring stronger SA-mediated mitochondrial protection to maintain tapetum integrity and pollen viability [92, 93]. These differences, which stem from evolutionary adaptation to temperate climates, highlight the targeted potential of SA in regulating the reproductive heat tolerance of raspberries. They also indicate that SA could serve as a precise horticultural regulatory tool for addressing the challenges of global warming. Future research should combine multi-omics studies to further optimize horticultural application strategies of SA under HT stress.

Conclusion

This study is the first to systematically elucidate the key stages, cellular and physiological defect mechanisms, and alleviating effects of exogenous SA on the anther development of double-season red raspberry (Fig. 12). HT stress mainly induces tapetum differentiation disorders and abnormal cell swelling in the early stages of anther development, from the pollen mother cell stage to the meiosis stage. It also causes premature tapetum degradation, leading to nutritional deficiencies in microspores and abnormal pollen wall formation. These issues ultimately result in progressive sterility. Spraying with 150 µmol·L− 1 SA can effectively regulate endogenous hormone levels and restore soluble protein accumulation. It also enhances the activity of energy metabolism enzymes, thereby improving the nutrition and energy supply during anther development. As a result, tapetum structural abnormalities are alleviated, and pollen germination rate and fruit set rate are significantly increased under high temperature stress. SA alleviates HT-induced male infertility by synergistically restoring hormone homeostasis and enhancing energy metabolism. Red raspberries exhibit species-specific responses to HT and SA, with earlier defects being more pronounced and SA-mediated recovery showing stage-specific sensitivity. This may be related to their perennial nature, shallow root structure, and summer and autumn flower bud differentiation strategy. Although this study clarified the damage in the early developmental stage, whether HT initiates damage during the sporogenous stage still requires further histological and multi-omics analysis. It is needed to explore the temporal dynamics of HT damage in order to optimize the application strategy of SA. Future research should utilize multi-omics approaches to elucidate the anther’s responses to SA during meiosis and microspore formation.
