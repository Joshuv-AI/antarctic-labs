Long-term lessons from CALGB 10403
Authors: Jerry W. Luo, Lori S. Muffly
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Silva et al1 report on the 10-year follow-up data from CALGB 10403, the landmark US Intergroup trial that established a pediatric-inspired regimen as the standard of care for adolescents and young adults (AYAs) with newly diagnosed Philadelphia chromosome–negative (Ph−) acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL).2 Historically, AYAs treated with traditional adult ALL regimens had a 3-year overall survival (OS) of 30% to 40%. These regimens typically combined daunorubicin, cytarabine, and cyclophosphamide, often followed by allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplant. CALGB 10403, the largest prospective multicenter trial in the United States to examine a pediatric protocol in Ph− AYA ALL, confirmed that AYAs could tolerate and benefit from a Berlin-Frankfurt-Münster chemotherapy backbone consisting of intensified glucocorticoids, vincristine, asparaginase, and prolonged central nervous system prophylaxis. The initial trial results, published in 2019, demonstrated a 3-year event-free survival (EFS) and OS of 59% and 73%, respectively, solidifying this regimen as the recommended approach for AYA ALL in practice and clinical guidelines.3,4

This update follows the initial CALGB 10403 cohort of 295 evaluable patients, enrolled between 2007 and 2012, now with a median follow-up of 120 months. At 10 years, the estimated EFS and OS were 44% and 56%, respectively. Of the 102 patients who relapsed, most (n = 77) did so within the first 3 years following diagnosis; however, 18.6% (n = 19) occurred between 3 and 5 years, and 5.9% (n = 6) beyond 5 years. Late relapses were most prominent in patients with B-cell ALL (B-ALL) with Ph-like genetics; interestingly, no patients with T-cell ALL (T-ALL) experienced late relapse. A subgroup analysis of 80 patients with available measurable residual disease (MRD) revealed that most patients with undetectable MRD after induction (n = 35) had long-term durable remissions with an impressive estimated 10-year disease-free survival of 85%.

These results should be celebrated, yet it is important to note the limitations of the CALGB 10403 regimen in AYAs. First, the combination of several intensive chemotherapy blocks followed by extended maintenance lasts ∼2 to 3 years, and only 39% of patients enrolled on the trial were able to complete all intended therapy.5 Adherence to treatment, including maintenance therapy, is a known prognostic indicator in ALL and multiple factors likely underlie the reduced compliance of this regimen in AYAs, relative to children. Second, toxicities are significant with this regimen.5 Although many are self-limited, others, such as thrombosis, osteonecrosis, and neuropathies, can result in prolonged complications and may reduce quality of life. Silva et al do not detail late effects after CALGB 10403, but such data would be informative. Finally, although these outcomes certainly represent an advance over-historical adult ALL regimens, the long-term results highlight that fewer than half of AYA patients with ALL are cured with this regimen alone.

Fortunately, the field continues to evolve, and recently reported randomized controlled trials strongly support the incorporation of immunotherapy into traditional ALL chemotherapy regimens. Specifically, both the ECOG-ACRIN E1910 and Children's Oncology Group AALL1731 trials demonstrated improved 3-year survival outcomes with the addition of blinatumomab, a bispecific T-cell engager targeting CD19, to pediatric and pediatric-inspired chemotherapy regimens in patients with newly diagnosed B-ALL.6,7 Similar efforts to incorporate inotuzumab ozogamicin, an antibody-drug conjugate targeting CD22, into pediatric regimens are ongoing.8 The introduction of chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapies targeting CD19 has also provided an effective and safe option for relapsed or refractory disease. Although these trials have ushered in a new era of frontline and relapsed/refractory treatment in B-ALL, comparable advances in T-ALL remain largely aspirational. Furthermore, access to novel immunotherapies and cellular therapies remains limited or absent in resource-constrained settings worldwide, resulting in persistent global disparities in ALL outcomes.

CALGB 10403 was a seminal trial in the history of ALL, heralding a new era of pediatric-inspired regimens for AYA patients. The period following CALGB 10403 has been marked by the emergence of immunotherapy and cellular therapy. Against this evolving landscape, these long-term results now serve as a benchmark for subsequent therapeutic strategies in AYA ALL. Determining which elements of pediatric-inspired therapy should be preserved, modified, or ultimately replaced will continue to shape both research priorities and clinical progress in this population.
