Likelihood Inference for Latent Network Models under Snowball Sampling
Nurzhan Sapargali

Abstract
Snowball sampling is a widely used design for collecting network data from large or hard-to-reach populations, yet naive inference that ignores the sampling mechanism produces systematically biased parameter estimates. We derive the exact likelihood of a multi-wave snowball sample for the class of continuous latent space (CLS) models, in which edges form independently conditional on latent vertex-level quantities, and show that conditional edge independence reduces the marginalization over unobserved network configurations to a closed-form expression portable across the entire CLS class. We develop a stochastic Expectation-Maximization algorithm for the Euclidean latent distance model as a concrete implementation, and apply the framework to the large-scale co-inventor network of German semiconductor patent applicants by drawing multiple snowball samples. We find that the naive procedure severely underestimates latent space variance, produces networks with nearly twice the observed edge count, and achieves a spectral goodness-of-fit nine times worse than the corrected model, which directly affects the quantitative interpretation of covariate effects.



Keywords:
network sampling; model-based inference; sampling bias; latent space model

1
Introduction

Large-scale networks are rarely fully observable.
Budgetary, logistical, and ethical constraints routinely prevent researchers from recording every tie in a population network, and even when full observation is feasible, the network may simply be too large for likelihood-based inference to remain computationally tractable.
Many canonical statistical network models such as exponential random graph models (ERGMs)
(
12
;
39
)
or latent position models
(
28
)
scale poorly with network size.
Whether through intractable normalizing constants or Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) updates that scale quadratically with network size, likelihood evaluations or posterior sampling render the exact inference on fully observed large networks computationally prohibitive without further approximation.
In both settings, sampling from the network emerges as a natural and practical alternative.
By working with a tractable sub-network one can draw inferences about the full population graph.
However, the statistical validity of this approach depends critically on whether the sampling mechanism is properly accounted for in the inferential procedure.

Among the many network sampling designs that have been considered
(
13
;
23
;
52
)
, snowball sampling stands out as a natural, practical and widely used method for collecting network data.
Introduced formally by
16
, the sampling scheme begins with a small set of seed vertices and expands by recruiting network neighbors of the currently sampled set, wave by wave, until a stopping criterion is met.
With a long history in sociology, epidemiology, and the study of online networks, its adaptive, contact-tracing structure makes it a natural fit for hard-to-reach populations
(
19
)
, while its breadth-first traversal of the network means that a modest number of waves can cover a substantial fraction of a large sparse graph at low cost.

The same adaptive structure is, however, the source of its principal inferential difficulty.
Because vertex inclusion in a snowball sample depends on network connectivity, the sample is far from a representative subgraph.
Vertices with high degree have a systematically higher probability of being recruited, and dense regions of the network are over-represented relative to sparse peripheries
(
27
;
43
;
26
)
.
The observed subgraph therefore looks more clustered and homogeneous than the underlying population network, so a practitioner who fits a network model to the snowball sample naively, i.e. as if it were the full population network, will systematically underestimate the heterogeneity and variance of the population parameters.
This bias is not a mere technicality: as our application and simulation demonstrate, it can alter conclusions in a quantitatively dramatic fashion.

The literature on inference from snowball samples spans several decades and methodological traditions.
The earliest contributions are design-based:

16
,
13
,
43
, and
11
derive estimators for scalar network parameters such as edge density, degree moments, population size by exploiting the known inclusion probabilities of the design, without assuming a parametric model for the population network.
More recent design-based work, including
26
and
36
, extends this tradition to a broader range of graph statistics and provides a rigorous general framework for Horvitz–Thompson estimation under multi-wave snowball sampling
(
52
)
.
These methods impose minimal assumptions, but they are targeted at specific estimands and do not yield a full likelihood for the network model.

Model-based approaches, by contrast, seek to estimate the parameters of a generative model for the population network from the observed sample.

17
provide the foundational framework:
Following
47
, they define a network sampling design as
amenable
to a given model if the sampling mechanism is ignorable for likelihood-based inference, and show that the resulting likelihood can be expressed as a marginalization of the full-network likelihood over all population networks consistent with the observed sample.
This is a principled and general formulation, but the marginalization involves a sum over exponentially many network configurations, compounding the already-demanding computational problem of normalizing constant evaluation.

37
propose a conditional maximum likelihood approach for the ERGM that sidesteps this difficulty by conditioning on the sufficient statistics of the unobserved part of the network, enabling practical estimation from snowball samples.

45
extend this to large networks via parallel computation and a meta-analytic combination of estimates across samples.

48
develop a Bayesian data augmentation procedure under a generalized stochastic block model, deriving an observed likelihood by summing over unobserved stratum assignments and adjacency values.
The resulting sum is again analytically cumbersome, leading the authors to rely on Gibbs sampling rather than direct likelihood evaluation.

Each of these contributions share a common limitation: they are either model-specific, or they reduce to marginalizing over the unobserved network configurations, which is a computationally expensive operation that must be re-engineered for each new model class.
Absent from the literature is an exact, closed-form likelihood for the snowball sample that is valid across a broad class of network models and that can be evaluated efficiently without enumerating unobserved networks.

This paper fills that gap.
The key observation is that the distribution of the snowball sample factorizes in a particularly transparent way for any network model in which edges form independently across vertex pairs conditional on latent vertex-level quantities.
This class of models, termed
continuous latent space
(CLS) models, following
42
, is broad enough to cover many of the most widely used and well-studied network models, including latent space models, random dot product graphs
(
51
)
, graphon models
(
4
;
15
;
41
, e.g.)
, and stochastic block models
(
22
;
44
)
.
Conditional edge independence implies that the marginalization over unobserved network configurations, which is intractable in general, reduces to a simple product over vertex pairs, where the likelihood of the snowball sample is a closed-form expression in the model parameters and the observed wave sets.
The result resolves, for the CLS class, the open problem identified by
6
of computing the distribution of multi-wave snowball samples from a general population network model.

We begin with the Erdős–Rényi model, whose unconditional edge independence yields particularly clean closed-form expressions and provides the intuition for the general case.
We then extend the result to the full CLS class.
As a concrete implementation of the resulting likelihood, we develop a stochastic Expectation-Maximization (sEM) algorithm for the distance model of
21
.
Crucially, the correction integrates naturally with existing MCMC samplers for latent position inference, adding only a single low-dimensional integral, which reflects the expected exclusion probability of an unobserved vertex and whose efficient approximation via quasi-Monte Carlo methods keeps the computational overhead modest.

We demonstrate the methodology on the co-inventor network of German electrical engineering patent applicants
(
14
)
, a network of approximately
6{,}000
inventors and
21{,}000
co-invention ties exhibiting pronounced small-world characteristics, high clustering and low average shortest path length, as well as heavy-tailed degree distributions.
Simple latent space models based on Euclidean geometry are known to perform poorly on such networks, as
38
show that the latent position model in its original form cannot represent small-world behaviour, nor heavy-tailed degree distributions as network size grows.
Yet this incompatibility has, to our knowledge, never been examined empirically at scale, in part because MCMC-based inference over latent positions has historically been prohibitive for networks of this size.
Our framework thus simultaneously enables the first empirical stress test of the distance model on a large-scale network exhibiting genuine small-world properties and provides a direct comparison between the corrected and naive procedures.

Our results show that even in this challenging setting, the distance model can recover meaningful latent structure, much more than the naive approach suggests.
In a large-scale study drawing
500
independent snowball samples and aggregating estimates via a robust mode-based meta-analytic estimator
(
18
)
, we find that the naive approach severely underestimates the spread of the latent space, producing networks with nearly twice the observed edge count, and achieving a spectral goodness-of-fit
(
40
)
nine times worse than the corrected model.
The corrected procedure, by contrast, recovers a latent space variance roughly eight times larger and better reproduces the degree distribution and spectral properties of the observed network.
While both procedures yield qualitatively similar patterns of gender homophily and geographic proximity to
14
, the corrected model produces substantially lower and more variable edge probabilities as a direct consequence of the larger estimated latent space variance, which materially affects the quantitative interpretation of the results.

2
Notation and Sampling Algorithm

We consider a population network represented by an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, where
V=\{1,\dots,N\}
is the vertex set and
E
is the edge set.
For any finite set
A
, we write
A^{2}=\{\{i,j\}:i,j\in A,\,i\neq j\}
to denote the set of unordered pairs of distinct elements of
A
.
The edge set
E\subseteq V^{2}
contains no self-loops or multiple edges between the same pair of vertices.

The adjacency matrix
Y=(Y_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
is defined by
Y_{i,j}=Y_{j,i}=1
if
\{i,j\}\in E
, and zero otherwise.
We treat the population network as a realization of a random graph, so
Y
is a random adjacency matrix with observed realization
y
.

An
r
-wave snowball sample from
G
is constructed recursively.
The initial wave
\mathcal{V}^{(0)}=\{v_{0}\}
consists of a single vertex
v_{0}\in V
(the
ego
), which is either drawn at random from
V
according to some sampling scheme, or fixed in advance.
When
v_{0}
is drawn at random, all subsequent inference is conducted conditionally on the observed
v_{0}
.
The first wave
\mathcal{V}^{(1)}
comprises all neighbors of the ego:

\mathcal{V}^{(1)}=\{j\in V:\{v_{0},j\}\in E\}.

For
k=2,\dots,r
, the
k
-th wave
\mathcal{V}^{(k)}
includes all neighbors of vertices in
\mathcal{V}^{(k-1)}
not already present in earlier waves:

\mathcal{V}^{(k)}=\Bigl(\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{V}^{(k-1)}}\{j\in V:\{i,j\}\in E\}\Bigr)\mathbin{\big\backslash}\Bigl(\bigcup_{s=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{V}^{(s)}\Bigr).

The sampled vertex set is
\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}\mathcal{V}^{(k)}
, and the sampled graph also records all edges among these vertices, forming the edge set
E^{(r)}\subseteq\bigl(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}\mathcal{V}^{(k)}\bigr)^{2}
.
The corresponding adjacency matrix
Y^{(r)}
, defined over all
\{i,j\}\in\bigl(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}\mathcal{V}^{(k)}\bigr)^{2}
, satisfies
Y^{(r)}_{i,j}=Y^{(r)}_{j,i}=1
if
\{i,j\}\in E^{(r)}
, and zero otherwise.
Figure
1
illustrates a 2-wave snowball sample from a population network, with vertices color-coded by wave membership.

Figure 1
:
Graphical illustration of a 2-wave snowball sample.

The wave sets
\mathcal{V}^{(0)},\mathcal{V}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{V}^{(r)}
are themselves random, as their composition depends on the unknown parameters of the population network.
This joint randomness of
Y^{(r)}
and the wave sets poses non-trivial challenges for statistical inference from snowball samples.

3
Erdős-Rényi Networks

In this section, we assume that the population network follows an Erdős-Rényi (ER) model with
N
vertices.
The ER model serves as a natural starting point as its unconditional edge independence yields closed-form expressions for the snowball sample likelihood, and the structural insights it provides extend to the richer class of models considered in Section
4
.

Under the ER model, each unordered vertex pair
\{i,j\}\in V^{2}
is connected by an edge with probability
\pi
, so that the edge indicators
Y_{i,j}
are independent and identically distributed Bernoulli random variables:

Y_{i,j}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\pi)\quad\text{for all}\quad\{i,j\}\in V^{2},

where
0<\pi<1
is the unknown edge probability.

3.1
Likelihood of a Snowball Sample

Suppose an
r
-wave snowball sample is drawn from an ER population network with edge probability
\pi
.
Our objective is to estimate
\pi
using only the information contained in the sampled subgraph.
A naive approach would treat the snowball sample as representative of the entire population and estimate
\pi
by the observed density of the sampled subgraph.
However, this approach is typically biased, particularly when the true density is low or when the snowball sample covers only a small fraction of the population network.
The bias arises because snowball sampling over-represents vertices with high degree, inflating the observed edge density relative to the true population value
\pi
.

As an illustration, the population network in Figure
1
comprises
45
vertices and
87
edges, giving a true density of
\pi\approx 0.09
, whereas the naive estimator based on the snowball sample yields
\hat{\pi}_{\text{naive}}\approx 0.11
.

To account for the sampling bias, we consider the joint probability of observing both the sampled wave sets
\mathcal{V}^{(0)},\mathcal{V}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{V}^{(r)}
and the induced adjacency matrix
Y^{(r)}
.
The likelihood of
\pi
given the snowball sample is

L(\pi)=P(Y^{(r)}=y^{(r)},\mathcal{V}^{(1)}=V^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{V}^{(r)}=V^{(r)}\mid\mathcal{V}^{(0)}=V^{(0)},\pi),

where
y^{(r)},V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)}
are the observed realizations of
Y^{(r)},\mathcal{V}^{(0)},\dots,\mathcal{V}^{(r)}
.

For notational convenience, we suppress the dependence on
\pi
and write probabilities as functions of observed realizations only; for example,
P(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)})
in place of
P\!\left(Y^{(r)}=y^{(r)},\,\mathcal{V}^{(1)}=V^{(1)},\,\dots,\,\mathcal{V}^{(r)}=V^{(r)}\mid\mathcal{V}^{(0)}=V^{(0)},\,\pi\right)
.

Factorizing the joint probability gives

P(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)})=P(y^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)})P(V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)})

(1)

where the second term on the right-hand side of (
1
) is the marginal probability that the wave sets
\mathcal{V}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{V}^{(r)}
equal
V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}
.
Notably, this term also depends on
\pi
and is the source of bias in the naive approach, which ignores it.

The marginal probability of the wave sets can be written as a product of conditional probabilities:

\displaystyle P(V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)})

\displaystyle=P(V^{(2)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},V^{(1)})P(V^{(1)}\mid V^{(0)})

\displaystyle=P(V^{(3)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},V^{(1)},V^{(2)})P(V^{(2)}\mid V^{(0)},V^{(1)})P(V^{(1)}\mid V^{(0)})

\displaystyle=P(V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r-1)})P(V^{(r-1)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r-2)})

\displaystyle\quad\cdots\,P(V^{(2)}\mid V^{(0)},V^{(1)})P(V^{(1)}\mid V^{(0)})

\displaystyle=P(V^{(1)}\mid V^{(0)})\prod_{k=2}^{r}P(V^{(k)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(k-1)}).

(2)

We now address each term separately.
Given the ego wave
\mathcal{V}^{(0)}=V^{(0)}
, for any vertex
i\in V\setminus V^{(0)}
to be included in
\mathcal{V}^{(1)}
, there must be an edge from
i
to the ego.
Under the ER model, this probability is
\pi
.
Furthermore, by definition of snowball sampling, the inclusion of
i
in
\mathcal{V}^{(1)}
given
\mathcal{V}^{(0)}=V^{(0)}
is independent across all vertices in
V\setminus V^{(0)}
.
In the terminology of survey sampling, the distribution of
\mathcal{V}^{(1)}
given
\mathcal{V}^{(0)}
corresponds to a Bernoulli sampling design with success probability
\pi
over the set
V\setminus V^{(0)}

(
2
)
.
Thus, the probability of observing
\mathcal{V}^{(1)}=V^{(1)}
given
\mathcal{V}^{(0)}=V^{(0)}
is

\displaystyle P(V^{(1)}\mid V^{(0)})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in V^{(1)}}P(i\ \text{has an edge to}\ V^{(0)})\prod_{j\in V\setminus(V^{(0)}\cup V^{(1)})}P(j\ \text{has no edge to}\ V^{(0)})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in V^{(1)}}\pi\prod_{j\in V\setminus(V^{(0)}\cup V^{(1)})}(1-\pi)\ =\ \pi^{n_{1}}(1-\pi)^{N-n_{1}-1},

(3)

where
n_{k}=|V^{(k)}|
denotes the number of vertices in wave
k
.

Consider now the conditional probability
P(V^{(k)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(k-1)})
for
k=2,\dots,r
.
Unlike the case of
\mathcal{V}^{(1)}\mid\mathcal{V}^{(0)}=V^{(0)}
, a vertex
i\in\mathcal{V}^{(k)}
may have multiple edges leading to
V^{(k-1)}
, since
V^{(k-1)}
can consist of several vertices.
To be included in
\mathcal{V}^{(k)}
, vertex
i
must have at least one edge to
V^{(k-1)}
.
Given
V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(k-1)}
, the distribution of
\mathcal{V}^{(k)}
again corresponds to a Bernoulli sampling design over
V\setminus\bigl(\bigcup_{s=0}^{k-1}V^{(s)}\bigr)
, with success probability equal to the probability that vertex
i
has at least one edge to
V^{(k-1)}
:

\displaystyle P(V^{(k)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(k-1)})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in V^{(k)}}P(i\text{ has at least one edge to }V^{(k-1)})

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{j\in V\setminus(\bigcup_{s=0}^{k}V^{(s)})}P(j\text{ has no edges to }V^{(k-1)})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in V^{(k)}}[1-\prod_{t\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi)]\prod_{j\in V\setminus\bigcup_{s=0}^{k}V^{(s)}}\prod_{t\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi)

\displaystyle=[1-(1-\pi)^{n_{k-1}}]^{n_{k}}(1-\pi)^{n_{k-1}(N-\sum_{s=0}^{k}n_{s})}.

(4)

Because the wave sets
V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)}
encompass all vertices in the sampled graph, they also determine the support of the conditional distribution of
Y^{(r)}
.
In particular, knowing which vertices belong to which wave allows us to determine the possible configurations of the adjacency matrix
Y^{(r)}
under the snowball sampling mechanism.
These constraints follow directly from the definition of snowball sampling in Section
2
:

•

No edges between non-adjacent waves:
for any
i\in V^{(k)}
and
j\in V^{(s)}
with
|k-s|>1
, we have
Y^{(r)}_{i,j}=0
.

•

At least one edge to the preceding wave:
for any
i\in V^{(k)}
with
k\in\{1,\dots,r\}
, there exists
j\in V^{(k-1)}
such that
Y^{(r)}_{i,j}=1
.

These constraints imply that the conditional distribution of
Y^{(r)}
given the wave sets differs from the standard ER model due to its restricted support.
Since the constraints apply to different subsets of vertex pairs, we define the following partition of
\bigl(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}V^{(k)}\bigr)^{2}
into three disjoint sets:

•

Vertex pairs within the same wave:

W=\left\{\{i,j\}\in\left(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}V^{(k)}\right)^{2}:i,j\in V^{(t)},\ t=1,\dots,r\right\}

•

Vertex pairs between adjacent waves:

A=\left\{\{i,j\}\in\left(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}V^{(k)}\right)^{2}:i\in V^{(t)},\ j\in V^{(t-1)},\ t=1,\dots,r\right\}

•

Vertex pairs between non-adjacent waves:

J=\left(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}V^{(k)}\right)^{2}\setminus(W\cup A)

Given these definitions, in Appendix
A
we formally derive the conditional probability of the adjacency matrix given the wave sets, resulting in the following expression:

P(y^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)})=\frac{\prod_{\{i,j\}\in W\cup A}\pi^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi)^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{r}[1-(1-\pi)^{n_{k-1}}]^{n_{k}}},

which equals zero if
y^{(r)}
violates any of the constraints above.

Bringing everything together, the joint probability of the sampled adjacency matrix and the wave sets can be expressed as

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)})

\displaystyle=P(y^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)})P(V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)})

\displaystyle=P(y^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)})

\displaystyle\quad\cdot P(V^{(1)}\mid V^{(0)})\prod_{k=2}^{r}P(V^{(k)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(k-1)})

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{\{i,j\}\in W\cup A}\pi^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi)^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{r}[1-(1-\pi)^{n_{k-1}}]^{n_{k}}}\pi^{n_{1}}(1-\pi)^{N-n_{1}-1}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{k=2}^{r}[1-(1-\pi)^{n_{k-1}}]^{n_{k}}(1-\pi)^{n_{k-1}(N-\sum_{s=0}^{k}n_{s})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\{i,j\}\in W\cup A}\pi^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi)^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}\prod_{k=1}^{r}(1-\pi)^{n_{k-1}(N-\sum_{s=0}^{k}n_{s})}.

(5)

Hence, the log-likelihood function of
\pi
is

\ell(\pi)=\Big(\sum_{\{i,j\}\in W\cup A}y^{(r)}_{i,j}\Big)\log\pi+\Big[\sum_{\{i,j\}\in W\cup A}(1-y^{(r)}_{i,j})+\sum_{k=1}^{r}n_{k-1}\big(N-\sum_{s=0}^{k}n_{s}\big)\Big]\log(1-\pi)

and the corresponding maximum likelihood estimator is

\hat{\pi}=\frac{\sum_{\{i,j\}\in W\cup A}y^{(r)}_{i,j}}{|W|+|A|+\sum_{k=1}^{r}n_{k-1}\big(N-\sum_{s=0}^{k}n_{s})},

(6)

where

|W|=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\frac{n_{k}(n_{k}-1)}{2},\qquad|A|=\sum_{k=1}^{r}n_{k-1}n_{k}.

For comparison, the naive estimator of
\pi
treats the sampled graph as a realization of an ER graph:

\hat{\pi}_{\text{naive}}=\frac{\sum_{\{i,j\}\in(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}V^{(k)})^{2}}y^{(r)}_{i,j}}{|(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}V^{(k)})^{2}|}=\frac{\sum_{\{i,j\}\in W\cup A}y^{(r)}_{i,j}}{|W|+|A|+|J|}.

(7)

with

|J|=\sum_{k=0}^{r}n_{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\neq k-1\\
s\neq k\\
s\neq k+1\end{subarray}}n_{s}\neq\sum_{k=1}^{r}n_{k-1}\bigl(N-\sum_{s=0}^{k}n_{s}\bigr).

The two estimators are nearly identical in structure, differing only in the last term of the denominator:
|J|
in the naive case versus
\sum_{k=1}^{r}n_{k-1}\bigl(N-\sum_{s=0}^{k}n_{s}\bigr)
in the snowball-corrected case.
The former counts the pairs of vertices from non-adjacent waves within the sample, while the latter accounts for unobserved vertices, thereby correcting the upward bias of the naive estimator.

The closed-form expression (
6
) allows for straightforward computation of the asymptotic
(1-\alpha)
confidence interval for
\pi
:

\hat{\pi}\pm q_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\left(-\frac{\partial^{2}\ell(\hat{\pi})}{\partial\pi^{2}}\right)^{-1}},

where
q_{\alpha/2}
is the
\alpha/2
-quantile of the standard normal distribution and

-\frac{\partial^{2}\ell(\pi)}{\partial\pi^{2}}=\frac{\sum_{\{i,j\}\in W\cup A}y^{(r)}_{i,j}}{\pi^{2}}+\frac{\sum_{\{i,j\}\in W\cup A}(1-y^{(r)}_{i,j})+\sum_{k=1}^{r}n_{k-1}\bigl(N-\sum_{s=0}^{k}n_{s}\bigr)}{(1-\pi)^{2}}

is the observed Fisher information.
Notably, increasing the number of waves
r
reduces the standard error of
\hat{\pi}
, yielding narrower confidence intervals at the cost of sampling a larger fraction of the population network.

3.2
Simulations

To illustrate the finite-sample behavior of the snowball-corrected estimator (
6
) relative to the naive estimator (
7
), we simulate
r
-wave snowball samples from ER graphs with
N
vertices and edge probability
\pi
.
We set
N=15{,}000
and let
r
vary from
1
to
3
.
For each
\pi\in\{0.001,0.002,0.003\}
and each
r
, we generate
1{,}000
ER graphs and draw
r
-wave snowball samples from each.
The values of
\pi
and
N
are chosen to reflect the sparsity of real-world networks.
In particular, they ensure that a snowball sample leaves a non-trivial fraction of the population unobserved, so that the difference between the two estimators remains meaningful.
Both estimators are applied to each sample, and the results are summarized in Figure
2(a)
, while Figure
2(b)
shows the distribution of the number of sampled vertices for each combination of
\pi
and
r
.

As expected, the naive estimator overestimates the edge probability
\pi
unless
r
or
\pi
is large enough for the snowball sample to cover most of the population network.
For a
1
-wave snowball sample (
r=1
), the naive estimator is on average one to two orders of magnitude larger than the true value
\pi=0.001
.
At
r=2
, the naive estimator is still biased across all values of
\pi
, although the bias is reduced compared to
r=1
.
Only at
r=3
and
\pi>0.001
does the naive estimator approach the true value of
\pi
, which corresponds to sampling more than
10{,}000
out of
15{,}000
vertices, as seen in Figure
2(b)
.

In contrast, the snowball-corrected estimator is approximately unbiased across all values of
\pi
and
r
.
Notably, it remains accurate even with very sparse samples.
At
r=1
with fewer than
100
sampled vertices on average, the average estimate is already close to the true
\pi
.
For all combinations of
\pi
and
r
, the snowball-corrected estimator approaches the true value of
\pi
more rapidly than the naive estimator as
r
increases.

B
, we present an additional simulation study to confirm our derived results, where the snowball samples are simulated directly from the likelihood (
5
) and the resulting observed edge density and wave size distributions are compared to those obtained from drawing snowball samples from simulated ER graphs.
The results confirm the correctness of our likelihood derivation, as the distributions of observed edge densities and wave sizes are nearly identical.

4
Networks with Latent Geometry

The ER model assumes independence of
Y_{i,j}
across vertex pairs and a constant edge probability, assumptions that fail to capture empirical phenomena in networks such as transitivity and heavy-tailed degree distributions
(
34
)
.
To address these limitations, we consider a general class of network models that represent the observed network through an unobserved, latent lower-dimensional space.
Specifically, each vertex is assigned a latent position in this space, and edges form independently across vertex pairs conditional on these positions, with edge probabilities determined by a similarity function of the corresponding latent coordinates.
This yields a flexible class of models in which dependence between edges is governed by the geometry of the latent space, thereby capturing complex network features such as transitivity and clustering.
Following
42
, we refer to this class as continuous latent space (CLS) models.

For undirected networks, the general form of a CLS model is:

\displaystyle Y_{i,j}\mid Z_{i}=z_{i},Z_{j}=z_{j}\overset{ind}{\sim}\text{Bernoulli}(\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))\quad\text{for all}\quad\{i,j\}\in V^{2}

\displaystyle\text{logit}(\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))=\alpha-s(z_{i},z_{j})

\displaystyle Z_{i}\in\mathcal{Z}^{d},\quad Z_{i}\overset{ind}{\sim}F_{\psi}(z_{i})\quad\text{for all}\quad i\in V,

(8)

where
Z_{i}
is the latent position of vertex
i
in a
d
-dimensional latent space
\mathcal{Z}^{d}
,
F_{\psi}(z)
is a distribution over the latent space parameterized by
\psi
, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is an intercept controlling the overall sparsity of the network.
The similarity function
s:\mathcal{Z}^{d}\times\mathcal{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
governs the probability of an edge between vertices
i
and
j
through their latent positions
z_{i}
and
z_{j}
, with the specific choice of
s
encoding the structural assumptions of the model, e.g., whether vertices tend to connect to geometrically proximate others, to those in the same latent community, or to those with complementary latent profiles.

Many popular models in the literature arise as special cases of (
8
) through specific choices of
s(z_{i},z_{j})
,
\mathcal{Z}^{d}
, and
F_{\psi}(z)
.
The distance model of
21
is obtained by setting
F_{\psi}(z)
to be a multivariate normal distribution over
\mathcal{Z}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
s(z_{i},z_{j})=\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean distance.
Hyperbolic latent space models
(
29
)
similarly use a distance-based similarity function, but embed vertices in a negatively curved hyperbolic space, which naturally accommodates the heavy-tailed degree distributions and hierarchical structure observed in many real-world networks.

Several other well-known network models are closely related to (
8
), though they differ in their choice of link function.
These include random dot product graph, graphon, and stochastic block models, which model edge probabilities directly rather than through a logit link.
Despite this difference, the structural insights derived from (
8
) are broadly relevant to this wider class of models.

Given the flexibility and expressiveness of (
8
), we adopt it as the population model for networks from which snowball samples are drawn.
We note that (
8
) can be extended to include covariates, either as part of the similarity function
s(z_{i},z_{j})
or as an additional term in the linear predictor.
For clarity, we focus on the covariate-free case, which already reveals the key inferential challenges introduced by snowball sampling in latent geometry models, without the additional complication of missing covariates for unobserved vertices.
The inferential goal is to estimate the model parameters
\alpha
and
\psi
, together with the latent positions
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N}
, using only the observed snowball sample.

4.1
Likelihood of a Snowball Sample

The CLS model (
8
) preserves the conditional independence of edge indicators
Y_{i,j}
given the latent positions, which allows the joint probability derived in equation (
5
) to be extended directly to this setting.
Let
Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N})^{\top}\in\mathcal{Z}^{N\times d}
be the matrix of latent positions for all vertices in the population and
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})^{\top}
its realization.
Replacing the constant edge probability
\pi
with the vertex-pair-specific probability
\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})
and proceeding analogously to Section
3
, we obtain the joint probability of the sampled adjacency matrix
Y^{(r)}
and wave sets
\mathcal{V}^{(0)},\dots,\mathcal{V}^{(r)}
, conditional on
Z=z
and the ego
V^{(0)}
(see Appendix
C
for the full derivation):

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},z)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{r}\prod_{i\in V\setminus\bigcup_{s=0}^{k}V^{(s)}}\prod_{j\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})).

(9)

Although equation (
9
) provides the likelihood of the snowball sample under the general CLS model, its dependence on the latent positions of unsampled vertices poses a challenge for identifiability, since these positions are never directly observed.
In the following subsection, we show that the latent positions of unsampled vertices are identifiable from the observed snowball sample up to the probability of being excluded from the sample.

4.2
Identifiability of Latent Positions of Unsampled Vertices

Many variants of the CLS model exhibit inherent non-identifiability of the latent positions.
For instance, if
s(z_{i},z_{j})=\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|
in equation (
8
), the latent positions are identifiable only up to distance-preserving transformations, such as translations, rotations, and reflections.
Similarly, in related models such as stochastic block models, the latent block assignments are non-identifiable up to permutations of the block labels
(
1
)
, while graphon models suffer from non-identifiability due to measure-preserving transformations of the latent space
(
7
)
.

Beyond the inherent non-identifiability of the population model, the snowball sampling design introduces additional challenges in identifying the latent positions of unsampled vertices.
Informally, the absence of a vertex from the snowball sample implies a specific constraint: an unsampled vertex cannot be connected to any vertex in waves
V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r-1)}
, as such a connection would have triggered its inclusion in the sample.
Apart from this, however, the sampling design provides no information regarding connections between unsampled vertices and the final wave
V^{(r)}
, nor regarding connections within the set of unsampled vertices itself.
Consequently, while the positions of sampled vertices are constrained by the observed network topology, the positions of unsampled vertices are constrained only by the requirement that they remain sufficiently ‘dissimilar’ from the core sample to avoid inclusion.

We formalize this intuition by decomposing the likelihood in equation (
9
) to isolate terms dependent on the latent positions of unsampled vertices.
Let
\mathcal{U}=V\setminus\bigcup_{s=0}^{r}V^{(s)}
denote the set of unsampled vertices.
We first observe that the complement of the cumulative sample at wave
k
can be partitioned as:

V\setminus\bigcup_{s=0}^{k}V^{(s)}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{U}\cup(\bigcup_{s=k+1}^{r}V^{(s)})&\text{for }k=1,\dots,r-1,\\
\mathcal{U}&\text{for }k=r.\end{cases}

Using this partition, the product term in equation (
9
) can be split into three distinct components:

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{r}\prod_{i\in V\setminus\bigcup_{s=0}^{k}V^{(s)}}\prod_{j\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{r-1}\prod_{i\in\bigcup_{s=k+1}^{r}V^{(s)}}\prod_{j\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{r-1}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}}\prod_{j\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}}\prod_{j\in V^{(r-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})).

Collecting the terms that involve the latent positions of unsampled vertices yields:

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{r}\prod_{i\in V\setminus\bigcup_{s=0}^{k}V^{(s)}}\prod_{j\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{r-1}\prod_{i\in\bigcup_{s=k+1}^{r}V^{(s)}}\prod_{j\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}}\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{s=0}^{r-1}V^{(s)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})).

Substituting this expression back into equation (
9
), we obtain a factorization of the likelihood into terms involving only sampled vertices and terms involving the unsampled set:

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},z)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{r-1}\prod_{i\in\bigcup_{s=k+1}^{r}V^{(s)}}\prod_{j\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}}\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{s=0}^{r-1}V^{(s)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})).

(10)

The second line of equation (
10
) involves vertices from non-adjacent waves, corresponding to the index set
J
defined in Section
3
.
The third line involves only pairs consisting of one unsampled vertex and one vertex from waves
0
to
r-1
.
Observing that
y^{(r)}_{i,j}=0
for all
\{i,j\}\in J
, the full expression simplifies to:

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},z)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W\cup J}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}}\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{s=0}^{r-1}V^{(s)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})).

(11)

The first term in equation (
11
) represents the contribution of observed edges and non-edges among the sampled vertices.
The second term represents the contribution of non-edges between the unsampled vertices and the sampled vertices in waves
0
through
r-1
.
Thus, the likelihood of the snowball sample is equivalent to that of a CLS network in which unsampled vertices are constrained to have no edges to any vertex in the core sample
V^{(0)}\cup\dots\cup V^{(r-1)}
, while all data regarding edges within the unsampled set and between unsampled vertices and the boundary wave
V^{(r)}
are treated as completely missing.

Equation (
11
) formalizes the earlier intuition: the latent positions of unsampled vertices contribute to the likelihood only through their joint exclusion probability

\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}}\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{s=0}^{r-1}V^{(s)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})).

Consequently, the latent positions of unsampled vertices are identifiable only up to the equivalence class of configurations that yield this identical exclusion probability.
In Appendix
D
, we construct a simple example of a CLS network, in which the latent positions of unsampled vertices can be rescaled without altering the likelihood.

4.3
Non-ignorability of Ego Selection

In the ER case, all vertices are exchangeable and the edge probability is constant across all vertex pairs, so the ego selection mechanism is ignorable.
That is, the likelihood of the snowball sample does not depend on the choice of
V^{(0)}
and inference is valid regardless of how the ego is selected.
In contrast, the complex dependence structure of CLS models leads to non-ignorability of the ego selection mechanism.

Letting
\theta=(\alpha,\psi)
denote the vector of population parameters, consider the joint density of the sampled adjacency matrix, wave sets, and latent positions of all vertices, conditional on the ego vertex
V^{(0)}
:

f_{\theta}(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)},z\mid V^{(0)})=P(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},z)\,f(z\mid V^{(0)}),

(12)

where
f(z\mid V^{(0)})
is the posterior density of the latent positions given the choice of ego vertex.
By Bayes’ theorem,

f(z\mid V^{(0)})=\frac{P(V^{(0)}\mid z)\,f_{\psi}(z)}{P(V^{(0)})}.

Unless the ego selection mechanism
P(V^{(0)}\mid z)
is independent of the latent positions
z
, the density
f(z\mid V^{(0)})
will differ from the prior density
f_{\psi}(z)
, and the likelihood of the snowball sample will depend on the choice of ego vertex, rendering the ego selection mechanism non-ignorable.
The independence condition holds, for example, when the ego is selected uniformly at random, based on an observed covariate independent of the latent positions, or by a deterministic mechanism unrelated to network features.
Conversely, it fails when selection is based on network features such as degree or centrality, which are functions of the latent positions, as such selection is informative about the underlying network structure.

In practice, valid inference therefore requires explicitly modelling the ego selection mechanism, the appropriate form of which will vary across applications.
However, modelling the ego selection mechanism is non-trivial.
To illustrate the complexity, consider degree-proportional selection, where vertex
i
is chosen as ego with probability proportional to its degree.
Even in this simple case, computing
P(V^{(0)}\mid z)
requires marginalizing over the joint distribution of the degree
D_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}Y_{i,j}
and the residual degree sum
K=\sum_{l\neq i}\sum_{j\neq l,j\neq i}Y_{l,j}
:

\displaystyle P(V^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\{i\}\mid z)=\sum_{k=0}^{(N-1)(N-2)}\sum_{d=0}^{N-1}P(V^{(0)}=\{i\},D_{i}=d,K=k\mid z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{(N-1)(N-2)}\sum_{d=0}^{N-1}P(V^{(0)}=\{i\}\mid D_{i}=d,K=k,z)P(D_{i}=d\mid K=k,z)P(K=k\mid z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{(N-1)(N-2)}\sum_{d=0}^{N-1}\frac{d}{k+d}P(D_{i}=d\mid z)P(K=k\mid z),

where
P(D_{i}=d\mid z)
as well as
P(K=k\mid z)
are Poisson-binomial distributions with parameters determined by the latent positions
z
, and the factorization in the last line follows from the conditional independence of
D_{i}
and
K
given
z
.

The difficulty of modelling the ego selection mechanism is not unique to CLS models.
To our knowledge, no existing model-based approach to snowball sampling addresses non-ignorable ego selection.
In the ERGM literature,
37
assume that the ego set is chosen independently of the network, while
48
in their treatment of the generalized SBM acknowledge that degree-proportional ego selection induces systematic bias but leave its treatment to future work.

While there are many possibilities for the ego selection mechanism, modelling it explicitly is not the primary focus of this paper.
The basic snowball sampling design assumes that the ego vertex is selected uniformly at random from the population
(
26
)
.
We therefore proceed under the assumption of ignorable ego selection,
f(z\mid V^{(0)})=f_{\psi}(z)
, which allows us to develop a practical estimation procedure without the added complexity of modelling the ego selection mechanism explicitly.

5
Example: Distance Model

In this section, we apply the general results from the previous sections to a specific instance of the CLS model: the distance model of
21
, in which the similarity function is the Euclidean distance between latent positions and the latent positions follow a multivariate normal distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This model is widely used in the literature and captures key features of real-world networks, such as transitivity and clustering, while maintaining a structure that admits tractable inference
(
28
)
.
The results extend readily to other CLS model variants, as the key inferential challenges introduced by snowball sampling are shared across different specifications of the similarity function and the latent position distribution.

The distance model is specified as follows:

\displaystyle Y_{i,j}\mid Z_{i}=z_{i},Z_{j}=z_{j}\overset{ind}{\sim}\text{Bernoulli}(\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))\quad\text{for all}\quad\{i,j\}\in V^{2}

\displaystyle\text{logit}(\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))=\alpha-\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|

\displaystyle Z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad Z_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\psi I_{d})\quad\text{for all}\quad i\in V,

(13)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is an intercept controlling the overall sparsity
of the network,
\psi>0
is the variance of the latent positions, and
I_{d}

is the
d
-dimensional identity matrix.

5.1
Parameter Estimation

To address the identifiability issues established in Section
4.2
, we proceed in three steps.
We integrate out the latent positions of unsampled vertices from the joint likelihood, treat the positions of sampled vertices as missing data, and estimate the population parameters
\theta=(\alpha,\psi)
via the stochastic Expectation-Maximization (sEM) algorithm
(
3
;
8
)
.

Following the assumption of ignorable ego selection, the joint density of the sampled data and the latent positions of all vertices is:

\displaystyle f_{\theta}(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)},z\mid V^{(0)})=

\displaystyle\ P(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},z)\,f_{\psi}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \prod_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W\cup J}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}}\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{s=0}^{r-1}V^{(s)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))\cdot\prod_{i\in V}f_{\psi}(z_{i}).

Integrating out the latent positions of unsampled vertices yields the marginal likelihood of the observed data and the latent positions of the sampled vertices:

\displaystyle f_{\theta}(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)},z_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}}\mid V^{(0)})=

\displaystyle\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W\cup J}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}\prod_{i\in V\setminus\mathcal{U}}f_{\psi}(z_{i})

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{E}_{Z_{i}}\Big[\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{s=0}^{r-1}V^{(s)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(Z_{i},z_{j}))\Big]^{|\mathcal{U}|}.

(14)

Here
z_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}}
denotes the latent positions of the sampled vertices, and

\mathbb{E}_{Z_{i}}\Big[\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{s=0}^{r-1}V^{(s)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(Z_{i},z_{j}))\Big]=\int_{\mathcal{Z}^{d}}f_{\psi}(t)\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{s=0}^{r-1}V^{(s)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(t,z_{j}))\,dt

(15)

is the expected exclusion probability of a vertex with latent position
Z_{i}
under the chosen prior distribution
f_{\psi}(z)
.

Beyond resolving the identifiability issues of Section
4.2
, integrating out the unsampled positions yields a substantial computational advantage.
Rather than performing inference on the latent positions of all
N
vertices, inference is reduced to the
N-|\mathcal{U}|
sampled vertices only, a significant reduction in networks where
|\mathcal{U}|
is large.
The contribution of the unsampled vertices is absorbed entirely into the expected exclusion probability (
15
), which is a single low-dimensional integral that, while intractable in closed form, can be approximated efficiently at a computational cost far lower than explicit inference over
|\mathcal{U}|
individual positions.

The corresponding observed data log-likelihood is:

\log L(\theta)=\log\int_{\mathcal{Z}^{d\sum_{k=0}^{r}n_{k}}}f_{\theta}(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)},z_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}}\mid V^{(0)})\,dz_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}},

where
n_{k}=|V^{(k)}|
denotes the size of the
k
-th wave.

Direct maximization of
\log L(\theta)
is intractable due to the high-dimensional integral over the latent positions of sampled vertices.
We therefore treat
z_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}}
as missing data and apply the sEM algorithm, starting from an initial estimate
\hat{\theta}^{(0)}=(\hat{\alpha}^{(0)},\hat{\psi}^{(0)})
and iterating the following two steps until convergence:

Stochastic E-step.

Given the current parameter estimates
\hat{\theta}^{(t)}
, draw a sample
z_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}}^{(t)}
from the posterior distribution
f_{\hat{\theta}^{(t)}}(z_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}}\mid y^{(r)},V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)})
via a short Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) run.

M-step.

Update the parameter estimates by maximizing the complete data log-likelihood (
14
) at the sampled latent positions:

\hat{\theta}^{(t+1)}=\argmax_{\theta}\ \log f_{\theta}\!\left(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)},z_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}}^{(t)}\mid V^{(0)}\right).

The stochastic E-step requires sampling from the posterior distribution of the latent positions of the sampled vertices.
The standard approach for distance models is a Metropolis-within-Gibbs sampler, which perfroms a Gibbs update for each latent position
Z_{i}
sequentially via a Metropolis-Hastings step with a Gaussian random walk proposal
(
28
)
.
While the Metropolis-within-Gibbs sampler is straightforward to implement in our setting, we leverage the Gaussian prior of the distance model and replace the Metropolis-Hastings step with the Elliptical Slice Sampler (ESS) of
32
for a tuning-free update of each
Z_{i}
.
The reduction from
N
to
N-|\mathcal{U}|
positions translates directly into a proportional reduction in the number of Gibbs updates per iteration, which is the primary computational benefit of snowball sampling in this setting.
The full conditional distribution of
Z_{i}
at iteration
t
is proportional to:

\displaystyle f_{\hat{\theta}^{(t)}}(z_{i}\mid y^{(r)},V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)},z_{-i})\propto

\displaystyle\ f_{\hat{\psi}^{(t)}}(z_{i})\prod_{j\in(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}V^{(k)})\setminus\{i\}}\pi_{\hat{\alpha}^{(t)}}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\hat{\alpha}^{(t)}}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{E}_{Z_{i}}\!\Big[\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{s=0}^{r-1}V^{(s)}}(1-\pi_{\hat{\alpha}^{(t)}}(Z_{i},z_{j}))\Big]^{\mathds{1}(i\notin V^{(r)})|\mathcal{U}|},

(16)

where
z_{-i}
denotes the current latent positions of all sampled vertices other than
i
.

Both the stochastic E- and M-steps involve repeated evaluation of the intractable integral (
15
) for different values of
\theta
and all
z_{j}\in V^{(0)}\cup\dots\cup V^{(r-1)}
.
The low dimensionality of the latent space (
d=2
or
3
in practice) and the smoothness of the integrand enable efficient approximation via quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) integration
(
30
)
.
Specifically, we generate
2^{M}
low-discrepancy points
u_{1},\dots,u_{2^{M}}
in
[0,1]^{d}
using a Sobol sequence and map them to standard normal draws via the inverse transform.
At each sEM iteration, these fixed draws are scaled by
\sqrt{\psi}
to produce samples
t_{1},\dots,t_{2^{M}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{d}(0,\psi I_{d})
.
Thus, rather than maintaining and updating
|\mathcal{U}|
individual position vectors, the entire contribution of the unsampled vertices is approximated using only
2^{M}
fixed quadrature points shared across all unsampled vertices, which in large networks with high
|\mathcal{U}|
can yield a substantial computational saving.
The base low-discrepancy sequence is generated only once, and during the Gibbs update for
Z_{i}
, only the distances between
Z_{i}
and the auxiliary points
t_{1},\dots,t_{2^{M}}
require re-computation, as distances to other sampled positions remain fixed.

The M-step is performed with the L-BFGS algorithm
(
31
)
using the derivatives with respect to
\alpha
and
\log\psi
, the latter ensuring strict positivity of the variance parameter.

The sEM algorithm produces a sequence
\{\hat{\theta}^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
whose stationary distribution is concentrated in a neighborhood of the maximum likelihood estimate of
\theta

(
35
)
.
After a sufficient burn-in period, the final parameter estimates are obtained by averaging over the tail of the sequence.
Detailed implementation of the Gibbs sampler with the ESS kernel, QMC integration, and the full sEM routine is provided in Appendix
E
.

5.2
Simulations

A simulation study is conducted to evaluate the performance of the proposed sEM-based estimator of the distance model parameters under snowball sampling, and to compare it to the naive estimator, which is constructed by replacing (
14
) in the sEM algorithm with the complete data likelihood of the population model for the sampled vertices only, treating the snowball sample as a complete network:

f_{\theta}^{naive}(y^{(r)},z_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}}\mid V^{(0)})=\prod_{\{i,j\}\in(V\setminus\mathcal{U})^{2}}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}\prod_{i\in V\setminus\mathcal{U}}f_{\psi}(z_{i}).

(17)

In the rest of the paper, we refer to the snowball likelihood (
14
) as the ‘snowball-corrected model’, and the naive likelihood (
17
) as the ‘naive model’.
Analogously, the resulting sEM-based parameter estimates are referred to as the ‘snowball-corrected’ and the ‘naive’ estimates, respectively.

We generate synthetic networks of size
N=1{,}000
from the population model (
13
) with parameters
\alpha\in\{-2,-1.75,-1.5\}
and
\psi\in\{1,2,3\}
in a two-dimensional latent space (
d=2
).
For each parameter combination
(\alpha,\psi)
, we simulate
100
independent networks and draw a 2-wave snowball sample from a uniformly selected ego vertex in each network.
The parameter values are chosen based on preliminary experiments indicating that they yield snowball samples covering a broad spectrum of sampling effort scenarios, as shown in Figure
3(b)
.
In rare cases where the snowball sample contains fewer than
3
vertices or no triangles, we discard the sample and redraw, as such samples provide insufficient information for inference under the distance model.
The rate of such cases is about
0.2\%
over all simulations.

For both the snowball-corrected and naive models, the sEM algorithm is run for a minimum of
30
iterations, after which it continues for up to
1{,}000
iterations or until the long-run averages of
\alpha
and
\psi
converge over
30
consecutive iterations, whichever occurs first.
Final parameter estimates are obtained by averaging over the second half of the sEM sequence, excluding the initial
30
iterations.
Depending on the size of the snowball sample, the number of MCMC iterations per sEM iteration is set adaptively between
100
and
1{,}000
via a heuristc rule (see Appendix
E
for details) to ensure sufficient mixing, while remaining computationally feasible across all simulations.

Application: Semiconductor Patent Co-inventorship Network

We apply the snowball-corrected distance model to a co-inventorship network constructed from patent filings in the field of semiconductors submitted to the European Patent Office or the German Patent and Trademark Office (
Deutsches Patent- und Markenamt

The underlying patent-inventor dataset is described in detail in

Two inventors are linked by an undirected edge if they appear as co-inventors on at least one patent within the study period, with multiple co-invented patents contributing at most one edge.
After retaining only the largest connected component, the resulting network has
N=5{,}979
vertices and
21{,}073
edges with mean degree
7.09
, maximum degree
96

7.55
and a global clustering coefficient of
0.31
.

Fitting the distance model directly to the full network is computationally intractable at
N=5{,}979
, and the snowball-corrected model developed in this paper removes that barrier.
This application is also a demanding one, as the network exhibits ‘small-world’ characteristics, i.e. high clustering and low average shortest path length
50
, as well as a heavy-tailed degree distribution, typical of scientific collaboration networks
(
33
)
.
Appendix
F
reports a small-world measure of
0.11
as defined by
46

38
show theoretically that the latent position model with a Gaussian connection function, which is closely related to the distance model of
21
, produces a vanishing clustering coefficient and thin-tailed degree distribution as the network grows, and therefore cannot represent the small-world behaviour or heavy-tailed degree distributions commonly observed in social networks.
Our application to the semiconductor co-inventor network therefore constitutes the first empirical stress test of the distance model on a large-scale network exhibiting genuine small-world properties, which are only meaningfully present at a scale that has historically been computationally inaccessible to MCMC-based inference.
As shown in Section
6.3
, even in this unfavourable setting the snowball-corrected model recovers substantially more network structure than the naive approach.

Each inventor is associated with two covariates used in Section

to interpret the estimated latent positions: inferred gender and geographic distance.
Each inventor has a recorded residence address from which longitude and latitude coordinates are derived
(
14
)
.
Pairwise geographic distances are then computed via the haversine formula.
Gender is not directly recorded in the patent register and is imputed from first names using a probabilistic name-gender matching procedure and is thus subject to some uncertainty.
The proportion of female inventors is below
5\%
for the resulting network, consistent with the broader electrical engineering population studied by

6.1
Sampling Design and Estimation

We draw
500
independent snowball samples from the population network, each initiated from a single ego selected uniformly at random from
V
.
For each sample, the number of waves is chosen to be as large as possible subject to the constraint that the total number of sampled vertices does not exceed
1{,}000
vertices to keep estimation computationally feasible for each sample.
The resulting sample sizes are highly heterogeneous, reflecting the pronounced degree heterogeneity of the network.
Samples initiated from high-degree hubs expand rapidly and saturate the vertex budget within one or two waves, producing dense, well-connected subgraphs, while samples initiated from peripheral vertices may require more waves to approach the budget and can yield sparse, tree-like subgraphs with limited inferential content.

For each of the
500
samples, we fit the snowball-corrected model along with the naive model as a benchmark, obtaining from each model a pair of parameter estimates
(\hat{\alpha}^{(b)},\hat{\psi}^{(b)})
for
b=1,\dots,500
.
The sEM algorithm for the naive model is initialized at
\alpha^{(0)}=0
and
\psi^{(0)}=1
, while for the snowball-corrected model, the sEM algorithm is initialized at the naive estimates for each sample to facilitate convergence.
Similar to the simulation study in Section
5.2
, the initial latent positions are set via multidimensional scaling and the number of MCMC iterations per sEM iteration is set adaptively between
100
and
1{,}000
based on the size of the snowball sample.
To ensure reliable estimation for each sample, the sEM algorithm is run for
600

100

A point estimate of the population parameters can be obtained by aggregating the estimates across the
500
samples.
The aggregation of estimates across different samples from the same population is a common problem in meta-analysis, where the goal is to combine estimates from multiple independent studies to obtain an overall estimate of the population parameter.
The classical approach is the inverse-variance weighted mean, which is optimal under approximate normality of the individual estimates around the true parameter value
(
20
)
.
This is equivalent to feasible generalized least squares regression of the estimates on a constant, with the inverse of the estimated covariance matrix of the estimates as weights, as performed by
45
in their aggregation of estimates in their application of the conditional estimation of ERGMs to snowball samples.

In our setting, this approach is inadvisable on two grounds.
First, the heterogeneous covariances of the sampling distributions of both snowball-corrected and naive estimates are of unknown form.
Second, the sampling distribution is highly non-normal, with a pronounced right skew and heavy tails, particularly for
\hat{\psi}
.
These difficulties are analogous to those arising in meta-analysis when study-level estimates are heterogeneous with the presence of outliers.

18
proposes a mode-based estimator under the zero modal bias assumption (ZEMBA) as a robust meta-analytic estimator in such settings.
ZEMBA states that the most common value of the bias across samples is zero, so that the mode of the distribution of estimates is consistent for the true parameter even when the majority of individual estimates are biased.
The plausibility of ZEMBA in our setting is directly supported by the simulation results of Section
5.2
.
The mode of the snowball corrected estimates across samples closely tracks the true parameter values, confirming that sufficiently well-connected samples, which constitute the majority of the
500
draws, yield approximately unbiased estimates.

Similar to the approach of
18
, we aggregate the estimates across samples by fitting a bivariate kernel density estimate (KDE) to the joint distribution of
(\hat{\alpha}^{(b)},\hat{\psi}^{(b)})
with log-transformation of
\hat{\psi}^{(b)}
to mitigate the right skew.
The bandwidth matrix is selected by the multivariate plug-in selector of
49
, as implemented in the
ks
package
(
10
)
, with pilot bandwidths estimated via the sum of asymptotic mean squared error method of
9
.
The KDEs for both the snowball-corrected and naive estimates are weighted by the number of vertices sampled per wave for each replicate, upweighting samples that provide more information per unit of sampling effort and downweighting the sparse, chain-like samples responsible for the outlying estimates.
The aggregate estimate is the mode of the fitted KDE, with
\hat{\psi}^{(b)}
back-transformed via exponentiation.
This procedure yields aggregate estimates of
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\psi})=(-0.31,\,215.17)
for the snowball-corrected model and
(\hat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{naive}},\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{naive}})=(-0.3,25.17)

An

7
Discussion

This paper derives the exact likelihood of a multi-wave snowball sample for the class of CLS models and demonstrates that the conditional independence of edge formation, given latent vertex-level quantities, reduces an otherwise intractable marginalization over unobserved network configurations to a closed-form expression in the model parameters and the observed wave sets.
Starting from the ER model, we extended the framework to the full CLS class and provided a concrete implementation for the distance model of
21
via a stochastic EM algorithm.
Simulation studies and the application to the semiconductor co-inventor network confirmed, at a scale previously inaccessible to likelihood-based latent space inference, that ignoring the snowball sampling mechanism produces misleading estimates.
The naive procedure severely underestimates the spread of the latent space, inflates edge probabilities, and, in the case of the semiconductor network, achieves a spectral goodness-of-fit nine times worse than the corrected model.

The most significant theoretical limitation of the present framework concerns the scope of the CLS class itself.
The closed-form reduction at the heart of our result depends critically on edges forming independently across vertex pairs conditional on the latent positions.
This assumption fails for models in which edge probabilities depend on the joint configuration of multiple dyads, most prominently ERGMs, where the sufficient statistics couple dyads in a way that prevents the marginalization over unobserved configurations from factorizing.
For such models the conditional maximum likelihood approach of
37
remains a viable route to snowball-corrected inference, and extending the exact likelihood correction to dyad-dependent models remains an open problem.

A second limitation concerns non-ignorable ego selection, treated in Section
4.3
.
When ego vertices are selected based on network features such as degree, centrality, or community membership, which depend on the latent positions, the selection mechanism is non-ignorable and must be modelled explicitly, at considerable computational cost even in the simplest cases.
As noted in Section
4.3
, this difficulty is shared by all existing model-based approaches to snowball sampling, none of which provides a general treatment of non-ignorable ego selection.
A general solution within the CLS framework would require deriving the adjusted prior
f(z\mid V^{(0)})
for each application-specific selection mechanism, while a unified treatment remains an open problem.

The application to the semiconductor co-inventor network also empirically reveals a structural limitation of the Euclidean latent distance model that is independent of the sampling correction.
Even under the corrected likelihood, the small-world characteristics and heavy-tailed degree distribution of the network pose a challenge, as theoretically predicted by
38
.
The corrected model fits substantially better than the naive one, but the absolute goodness-of-fit remains modest.
Importantly, this incompatibility is more honestly exposed by the corrected procedure than by the naive one.
The naive estimator artificially contracts the latent space, distorting the latent geometry, which results in a substantially worse fit.
This observation motivates the extension of the snowball-corrected likelihood to richer model specifications within the CLS class that are better suited to networks with small-world characteristics and heavy-tailed degree distributions.
Hyperbolic latent space models
(
29
)
, which embed vertices in a negatively curved space that naturally accommodates heavy-tailed degree distributions, and graphon models, which allow degree heterogeneity to be captured without imposing a parametric form on the latent position distribution, are particularly natural candidates.
For distance models, one can add vertex-specific random effects to the linear predictor to capture degree heterogeneity
(
28
)
and small-world structure.
All of these satisfy the conditional edge independence requirement of the CLS class, meaning that the likelihood derivation of Section
4
applies without much modification.

The framework also extends to models with covariates.
If the covariates are observed for all vertices in the population, or for all dyads in the case of dyad-level covariates, they can be incorporated directly into the linear predictor of the CLS model without modification.
When covariates involving unsampled vertices are unobserved, a potential approach is to operationalize the missingness through a distributional assumption on the covariate values, provided the missingness mechanism is ignorable
(
25
)
.
The expected exclusion probability integral then extends to a joint expectation over both latent positions and missing covariates.

Finally, a fully Bayesian implementation of the snowball-corrected likelihood is a natural extension of the present framework.
The sEM algorithm treats
\alpha
and
\psi
as fixed and estimates them by maximum likelihood.
A Bayesian implementation would propagate uncertainty in these parameters directly into the posterior over latent positions.
Crucially, this extension does not introduce substantial additional computational overhead.
The quasi-Monte Carlo approximation to the exclusion probability integral scales with the number of Sobol points and the dimension of the latent space, not with the number of unsampled vertices, so re-evaluating it at each posterior draw of
(\alpha,\psi)
adds no more cost per iteration than the stochastic E- and M-steps of the sEM algorithm.
The Bayesian extension therefore remains substantially cheaper than full-population MCMC, which scales quadratically with
N
.

Taken together, these extensions suggest that the framework developed here can serve as a broadly applicable foundation for model-based inference on snowball-sampled network data.
The key architectural property is the separation between the sampling correction, which depends only on the wave structure and the conditional independence assumption, and the model specification, which can be varied freely within the CLS class.
Progress on either front, whether through better models for heterogeneous networks or more flexible treatments of non-ignorable selection, integrates immediately into the existing framework.
More broadly, the result points towards a general principle: when a model’s likelihood factorizes over dyads, the snowball sampling mechanism can be accounted for at little additional cost, encouraging broader adoption of principled, sampling-aware inference in large network analysis.

Conditional Probability of Sampled Network under ER Model

In subection
3.1
, we partition the set of the observed vertex pairs
(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}V^{(k)})^{2}
into three disjoint subsets: the set of within-wave pairs
W
, the set of between non-adjacent wave pairs
J
, and the set of between adjacent wave pairs
A

\displaystyle W=\left\{\{i,j\}\in\left(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}V^{(k)}\right)^{2}:i,j\in V^{(t)},\ t=1,\dots,r\right\},

\displaystyle A=\left\{\{i,j\}\in\left(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}V^{(k)}\right)^{2}:i\in V^{(t)},\ j\in V^{(t-1)},\ t=1,\dots,r\right\},

\displaystyle J=\left(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}V^{(k)}\right)^{2}\setminus(W\cup A).

Using the above partition, we derive the conditional probability of
Y^{(r)}
given the wave sets
V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)}
under the ER model.
For any subset of the vertex pairs
B\subseteq(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}V^{(k)})^{2}
, denote
Y^{(r)}_{B}
as the entries of
Y^{(r)}
indexed by the pairs in
B
.
Similarly, the realization of
Y^{(r)}_{B}
is denoted by
y^{(r)}_{B}
.

For notational simplicity, we maintain the convention of omitting random variables when writing probabilities, so that
P(Y^{(r)}=y^{(r)})
is written as
P(y^{(r)})
, and so on.
The conditional probability of the sampled adjacency matrix
Y^{(r)}=y^{(r)}
given the wave sets
\mathcal{V}^{(0)}=V^{(0)},\dots,\mathcal{V}^{(r)}=V^{(r)}
can now be expressed as

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)})

\displaystyle=P(y^{(r)}_{W},y^{(r)}_{J},y^{(r)}_{A}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)})

\displaystyle=P(y^{(r)}_{A}\mid y^{(r)}_{W},y^{(r)}_{J},V^{(0)}\dots,V^{(r)})

\displaystyle\quad\cdot P(y^{(r)}_{W}\mid y^{(r)}_{J},V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)})

\displaystyle\quad\cdot P(y^{(r)}_{J}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)}).

As established in Section
3.1
, the constraints of the snowball sampling design imply that
Y^{(r)}_{J}=y^{(r)}_{J}=\boldsymbol{0}
with probability one, so that the above expression simplifies to

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)})

\displaystyle=P(y^{(r)}_{A}\mid y^{(r)}_{W},y^{(r)}_{J}=\boldsymbol{0},V^{(0)}\dots,V^{(r)})

\displaystyle\quad\cdot P(y^{(r)}_{W}\mid y^{(r)}_{J}=\boldsymbol{0},V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)}),

(18)

for any
y^{(r)}
such that
y^{(r)}_{J}=\boldsymbol{0}
, and zero otherwise.

Starting with the second term on the right-hand side of (
18
), the probability of
Y^{(r)}_{W}=y^{(r)}_{W}
is independent of the constraints as the within-wave edges are not subject to any constraints under the snowball sampling design.
Under the ER model, all edges are independent Bernoulli variables, so that the probability of
Y^{(r)}_{W}=y^{(r)}_{W}
is simply the product of the probabilities of each edge in
W
:

P(y^{(r)}_{W}\mid y^{(r)}_{J}=\boldsymbol{0},V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)})=P(y^{(r)}_{W})=\prod_{\{i,j\}\in W}\pi^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi)^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}

(19)

Similar to
Y^{(r)}_{W}
, the edge indicators in
Y^{(r)}_{A}
are independent of
Y^{(r)}_{J}
and
Y^{(r)}_{W}
given the wave sets.
So the first term on the right-hand side of (
18
) simplifies to

P(y^{(r)}_{A}\mid y^{(r)}_{W},y^{(r)}_{J}=\boldsymbol{0},V^{(0)}\dots,V^{(r)})=P(y^{(r)}_{A}\mid V^{(0)}\dots,V^{(r)}).

Importantly, unlike the conditional probability
Y^{(r)}_{W}=y^{(r)}_{W}\mid y^{(r)}_{J}=\boldsymbol{0},V^{(0)}\dots,V^{(r)}
in (
19
), the conditional probability
Y^{(r)}_{A}=y^{(r)}_{A}\mid V^{(0)}\dots,V^{(r)}
is not simply the product of the probabilities over each vertex pair in
A
under the ER model, as the snowball sampling design imposes constraints on
Y^{(r)}_{A}
that induce dependencies between edges in
A
.
Intuitively, every vertex in wave
k
must be connected to at least one vertex in the previous wave
k-1
for all
k=1,\dots,r
.

For this purpose, define
v(i):\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}V^{(k)}\to\{0,\dots,r\}
as the wave index of vertex
i
, i.e.
v(i)=k
if
i\in V^{(k)}
.
Let
A(i)=\{\{i,j\}\in A:j\in V_{v(i)-1}\}
be the set of vertex pairs in
A
that include vertex
i
and all the vertices in the previous wave
V_{v(i)-1}
.
Then,
A
can further be partitioned into the union of the disjoint sets
A(i)
for all vertices starting from wave
1
:

A=\bigcup_{i\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k)}}A(i).

Let
Y^{(r)}_{A(i)}
be the vector of entries of
Y^{(r)}
corresponding to the pairs in
A(i)
, and
y^{(r)}_{A(i)}
be its realization.
Given the wave sets, the constraints of the snowball sampling design imply that
\sum_{\{i,j\}\in A(i)}Y^{(r)}_{i,j}>0
for all
i\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k)}
with probability one.
This means that the conditional probability of
Y^{(r)}_{A(i)}=y^{(r)}_{A(i)}
given the wave sets is

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)}_{A(i)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)})

\displaystyle=P\Big(y^{(r)}_{A(i)}\mid\sum_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}Y^{(r)}_{u,l}>0\Big)=\frac{P(y^{(r)}_{A(i)})}{P\Big(\sum_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}Y^{(r)}_{u,l}>0\Big)}

\displaystyle=\frac{P(y^{(r)}_{A(i)})}{1-P(Y^{(r)}_{A(i)}=\boldsymbol{0})}=\frac{\prod_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}\pi^{y^{(r)}_{u,l}}(1-\pi)^{1-y^{(r)}_{u,l}}}{1-(1-\pi)^{n_{v(i)-1}}},

where
n_{v(i)-1}=|V_{v(i)-1}|
is the number of vertices in the preceding wave, and the last equality follows from the independence of edges under the ER model.

Finally, since the sets
A(i)
are disjoint for all
i\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k)}
, and the constraints of the snowball sampling design apply to individual
A(i)
separately, the conditional probability of
Y^{(r)}_{A}=y^{(r)}_{A}
given the wave sets can be expressed as the product of the conditional probabilities for each
A(i)
:

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)}_{A}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)})

\displaystyle=P\Big(y^{(r)}_{A}\mid\sum_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}Y^{(r)}_{u,l}>0\text{ for all }i\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k)}\Big)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k)}}P\Big(y^{(r)}_{A(i)}\mid\sum_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}Y^{(r)}_{u,l}>0\Big)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k)}}\frac{\prod_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}\pi^{y^{(r)}_{u,l}}(1-\pi)^{1-y^{(r)}_{u,l}}}{1-(1-\pi)^{n_{v(i)-1}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A}\pi^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi)^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}}{\prod_{i\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k)}}[1-(1-\pi)^{n_{v(i)-1}}]}=\frac{\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A}\pi^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi)^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{r}[1-(1-\pi)^{n_{k-1}}]^{n_{k}}},

where the denominator of the last equality follows from the fact that
n_{k}=|V^{(k)}|
is the number of vertices in wave
k
and each vertex in wave
k
has the same denominator term
1-(1-\pi)^{n_{k-1}}
.

Combining the results above yields the conditional probability of the sampled adjacency matrix given the wave sets:

\displaystyle P(Y^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)})

\displaystyle=P(y^{(r)}_{A}\mid V^{(0)}\dots,V^{(r)})P(y^{(r)}_{W})

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A}\pi^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi)^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{r}[1-(1-\pi)^{n_{k-1}}]^{n_{k}}}\cdot\prod_{\{i,j\}\in W}\pi^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi)^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W}\pi^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi)^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{r}[1-(1-\pi)^{n_{k-1}}]^{n_{k}}}.

Appendix B
Additional Simulation Experiments under ER Model

The explicit forms of the chain probabilities of the wave sets
\mathcal{V}^{(0)},\dots,\mathcal{V}^{(r)}
and the conditional distribution of the sampled adjacency matrix given the wave sets
Y^{(r)}=y^{(r)}\mid\mathcal{V}^{(0)}=V^{(0)},\dots,\mathcal{V}^{(r)}=V^{(r)}
under the ER model derived in Section
3
suggest a straightforward algorithm for directly simulating snowball samples from an ER population without first generating the full population graph.

First, the wave sets
\mathcal{V}^{(0)},\dots,\mathcal{V}^{(r)}
can be generated as follows:

1.

Choose an arbitrary ego vertex
i_{0}
from the population vertex set
V
either at random or deterministically and set
V^{(0)}=\{i_{0}\}
.

2.

Draw the first wave
\mathcal{V}^{(1)}
by including each vertex
i\in V\setminus V^{(0)}
with probability
p
.

3.

For each
k=2,\dots,r
:

3.1

Draw the wave
V^{(k)}
by including each vertex
i\in V\setminus\bigcup_{t=0}^{k-1}V_{t}
with probability
1-(1-\pi)^{n_{k-1}}
, where
n_{k-1}=|V^{(k-1)}|
.

Once the wave sets are generated, the adjacency matrix
Y^{(r)}
can be simulated as follows:

1.

For each
\{i,j\}
in
J
, set
Y^{(r)}_{i,j}=0
.

2.

For each
\{i,j\}
in
W
, draw
Y^{(r)}_{i,j}
from a Bernoulli distribution with parameter
p
.

3.

For each
i\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k)}
:

3.1

Draw the number of edges
m_{i}
from a zero-truncated binomial distribution with parameters
n_{v(i)-1}
and
\pi
, where
n_{v(i)-1}=|V_{v(i)-1}|
is the number of vertices in the preceding wave.

3.2

Draw
m_{i}
vertex pairs
\{i,j\}
from
A(i)
uniformly at random without replacement and set
Y^{(r)}_{i,j}=1
for each of them.

The resulting adjacency matrix
Y^{(r)}
is a realization of the snowball sampling design from an ER graph with
N
vertices and edge probability
\pi
.

To verify the correctness of our likelihood derivation under the ER model, we compare the distributions of wave sizes and edge densities generated by the above simulation algorithm with those generated by direct snowball sampling from a full ER graph, i.e. first generating the population graph and then drawing the snowball sample from it.
The ego vertex is chosen uniformly at random from the population vertex set in both cases.
We generate
3
-wave snowball samples from an ER graph with
N=15{,}000
vertices and
\pi=0.001
using the simulation algorithm
20{,}000
times.
The resulting distributions of wave sizes and edge densities are compared to the equivalent distributions under direct snowball sampling.
Figure
8
shows the empirical cumulative distribution functions of the wave sizes and edge densities under both methods, along with Kolmogorov-Smirnov test statistics and
p
-values for the null hypothesis that the two distributions are identical.
Visually, the distributions of wave sizes and edge densities under the two methods are indistinguishable, and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests fail to reject the null hypothesis of identical distributions at conventional significance levels, confirming that the simulation algorithm correctly reproduces the distributional properties of snowball sampling design.

Appendix C
Likelihood Derivation for CLS Networks

The likelihood of a snowball sample conditional on latent variables under CLS population can be derived by mirroring the derivations of the wave set probabilities and the conditional probability of the sampled adjacency matrix under the ER model in Section
3
and Appendix
A
, while replacing the constant edge probability
\pi
with the dyad-specific edge probabilities
\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})
.
Given the latent positions
Z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})
of all vertices in the population and the initial wave set
V^{(0)}=\{i_{0}\}
, consisting of the ego vertex
i_{0}
, the chain probabilities of the wave sets
\mathcal{V}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{V}^{(r)}
can be expressed as

P(V^{(1)}\mid V^{(0)},z)=\prod_{i\in V^{(1)}}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{i_{0}})\prod_{j\in V\setminus(V^{(0)}\cup V^{(1)})}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{j},z_{i_{0}})),

\displaystyle P(V^{(k)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(k-1)},z)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in V^{(k)}}\big[1-\prod_{t\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{t}))\big]

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{j\in V\setminus\bigcup_{s=0}^{k}V^{(s)}}\prod_{t\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{j},z_{t})).

Analagous to the derivations in Appendix
A
, for the conditional probability of the sampled adjacency matrix
Y^{(r)}=y^{(r)}
given the wave sets
V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)}
and the latent positions
Z
, we first partition the set of observed vertex pairs into the within-wave pairs
W
, between non-adjacent wave pairs
J
, and between adjacent wave pairs
A
.

The probabilities of the partitions
Y^{(r)}_{W}
,
Y^{(r)}_{J}
of the sampled adjacency matrix are

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)}_{J}\mid V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(0)},z)=1\quad\text{for}\quad y^{(r)}_{J}=\boldsymbol{0},

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)}_{W}\mid y^{(r)}_{J},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(0)},z)=P(y^{(r)}_{W}\mid z)=\prod_{\{i,j\}\in W}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y_{i,j}}

For
Y^{(r)}_{A}
, the set of adjacent wave pairs
A
can again be partitioned into the disjoint sets
A(i)
for all vertices in waves
1
to
r
as Appendix
A
, where
A(i)=\{\{i,j\}\in A:j\in V_{v(i)-1}\}
is the set of vertex pairs in
A
that include vertex
i
and all the vertices in the previous wave
V_{v(i)-1}
.
The conditional probability of
Y^{(r)}_{A(i)}=y^{(r)}_{A(i)}
given the wave sets and latent positions is

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)}_{A(i)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)},z)

\displaystyle=P\Big(y^{(r)}_{A(i)}\mid\sum_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}Y^{(r)}_{u,l}>0,z\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{P(y^{(r)}_{A}\mid z)}{P\Big(\sum_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}Y^{(r)}_{u,l}>0\mid z\Big)}=\frac{P(y^{(r)}_{A}\mid z)}{1-P(Y^{(r)}_{A(i)}=\boldsymbol{0}\mid z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{u},z_{l})^{y^{(r)}_{u,l}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{u},z_{l}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{u,l}}}{1-\prod_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{u},z_{l}))}.

Similar to the ER model, exploiting the independence of the sets
A(i)
for all
i\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k)}
yields the conditional probability of
Y^{(r)}_{A}=y^{(r)}_{A}
given the wave sets and latent positions

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)}_{A}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)},z)

\displaystyle=P\Big(y^{(r)}_{A}\mid\sum_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}Y^{(r)}_{u,l}>0\text{ for all }i\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k)},z\Big)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k)}}P\Big(y^{(r)}_{A(i)}\mid\sum_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}Y^{(r)}_{u,l}>0,z\Big)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k)}}\frac{\prod_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{u},z_{l})^{y^{(r)}_{u,l}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{u},z_{l}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{u,l}}}{1-\prod_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{u},z_{l}))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}}{\prod_{i\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k)}}\left[1-\prod_{\{u,l\}\in A(i)}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{u},z_{l}))\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{r}\prod_{i\in V^{(k)}}\left[1-\prod_{j\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))\right]},

where the last equality follows from the fact that
A(i)
consists of the vertex pairs
\{i,j\}
for all
j\in V^{(k-1)}
if
i\in V^{(k)}
.

Thus, the likelihood of the sampled adjacency matrix
Y^{(r)}=y^{(r)}
given the wave sets
V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)}
and the latent positions
Z
can be expressed as

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)},z)

\displaystyle=P(y^{(r)}_{A}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)},z)P(y^{(r)}_{W}\mid z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{r}\prod_{i\in V^{(k)}}\left[1-\prod_{j\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))\right]}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{\{i,j\}\in W}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{r}\prod_{i\in V^{(k)}}\left[1-\prod_{j\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))\right]}.

Bringing together the likelihood of the sampled adjacency matrix given the wave sets and latent positions with the chain probabilities of the wave sets yields the likelihood of a snowball sample conditional on latent variables under CLS population:

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,

\displaystyle V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},z)=P(y^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)},z)\prod_{k=1}^{r}P(V^{(k)}\mid V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(k-1)},z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{r}\prod_{i\in V^{(k)}}\left[1-\prod_{j\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))\right]}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{i\in V^{(1)}}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{i_{0}})\prod_{j\in V\setminus(V^{(0)}\cup V^{(1)})}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{j},z_{i_{0}}))

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{k=2}^{r}\prod_{i\in V^{(k)}}\big[1-\prod_{t\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{t}))\big]\cdot\prod_{j\in V\setminus\bigcup_{s=0}^{k}V^{(s)}}\prod_{t\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{j},z_{t}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}}{\prod_{i\in V^{(1)}}\left[1-\prod_{j\in V^{(0)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))\right]}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{i\in V^{(1)}}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{i_{0}})\prod_{j\in V\setminus(V^{(0)}\cup V^{(1)})}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{j},z_{i_{0}}))

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{k=2}^{r}\prod_{j\in V\setminus\bigcup_{s=0}^{k}V^{(s)}}\prod_{t\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{j},z_{t}))

Because
V^{(0)}=\{i_{0}\}
, the denominator can be simplified to
\prod_{i\in V^{(1)}}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{i_{0}})
, which cancels the corresponding term in the second term

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},z)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{j\in V\setminus(V^{(0)}\cup V^{(1)})}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{j},z_{i_{0}}))

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{k=2}^{r}\prod_{j\in V\setminus\bigcup_{s=0}^{k}V^{(s)}}\prod_{t\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{j},z_{t})).

As
\prod_{j\in V\setminus(V^{(0)}\cup V^{(1)})}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{j},z_{i_{0}}))
is equal to
\prod_{k=1}^{1}\prod_{j\in V\setminus\bigcup_{s=0}^{k}V^{(s)}}\prod_{t\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{j},z_{t}))
, the second term can be absorbed into the product over
k
simplifying the likelihood to

\displaystyle P(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},z)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{r}\prod_{i\in V\setminus\bigcup_{s=0}^{k}V^{(s)}}\prod_{j\in V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})).

Appendix D
Example of Non-identifiability of Latent Positions of Unsampled Vertices

Assume a CLS population network with parameter
\theta=(\alpha,\psi)
as defined in Section
4
.
An
r
-wave snowball sample is drawn from the population network under ignorable ego selection, so that the population prior distribution of the latent positions coincides with the distribution of the latent positions conditional on the ego vertex as discussed in Section
4.3
.
Consider the joint density of the observed data and latent positions, parameterized by
\theta=(\alpha,\psi)
.
From Section
5.1
, the complete data likelihood of the observed adjacency matrix
Y^{(r)}=y^{(r)}
, wave sets
V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}
, and latent positions
z
given the initial wave set
V^{(0)}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle f_{\theta}(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)},z\mid V^{(0)})=

\displaystyle\ P(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},z)\,f_{\psi}(z)

\displaystyle=P(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)}\mid V^{(0)},z)\,\prod_{i\in V}f_{\psi}(z_{i})

where
f(z_{i})
denotes the density of the latent position of vertex
i
under the population prior distribution.
Following the likelihood result from Section
4.2
and isolating the terms that depend on the latent positions of unsampled vertices
z_{\mathcal{U}}
yields

\displaystyle f_{\theta}(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)},z\mid V^{(0)})=

\displaystyle\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W\cup J}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}}\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{s=0}^{r-1}V^{(s)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))\cdot\prod_{i\in V}f_{\psi}(z_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h(z_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}},\theta)\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}}\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{s=0}^{r-1}V^{(s)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}}f_{\psi}(z_{i})

where
z_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}}
denotes the latent positions of the sampled vertices and

h(z_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}},\theta)=\prod_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W\cup J}\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j})^{y^{(r)}_{i,j}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z_{i},z_{j}))^{1-y^{(r)}_{i,j}}\prod_{i\in V\setminus\mathcal{U}}f_{\psi}(z_{i})

represents the collection of terms independent of the latent positions of unsampled vertices.

Consider the complete data likelihood evaluated at some parameter value
\tilde{\theta}=(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\psi})
and some configuration of latent positions
\tilde{z}
yielding a likelihood value of
C
:

\displaystyle f_{\tilde{\theta}}(y^{(r)},V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(r)},\tilde{z}\mid V^{(0)})=C

\displaystyle h(\tilde{z}_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}},\tilde{\theta})\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}}\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{s=0}^{r-1}V^{(s)}}(1-\pi_{\tilde{\alpha}}(\tilde{z}_{i},\tilde{z}_{j}))\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}}f_{\tilde{\psi}}(\tilde{z}_{i})=C

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}}f_{\tilde{\psi}}(\tilde{z}_{i})\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\tilde{\alpha}}(\tilde{z}_{i},\tilde{z}_{j}))=\frac{C}{h(\tilde{z}_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}},\tilde{\theta})}

Take any
v\in\mathcal{U}
.
Suppose we change the configuration of latent positions by rescaling the positions of all other unsampled vertices
i\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\{v\}
by
\lambda=(\lambda_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\{v\}}
.
We seek a scaling factor
\lambda_{v}
for the remaining vertex
v
such that the joint likelihood remains exactly
C
.
This requires solving with respect to
\lambda_{v}

f_{\tilde{\psi}}(\lambda_{v}\tilde{z}_{v})\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\tilde{\alpha}}(\lambda_{v}\tilde{z}_{v},\tilde{z}_{j}))=C_{v}

(20)

where

C_{v}=\frac{C}{h(\tilde{z}_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}},\tilde{\theta})\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\{v\}}f_{\psi}(\lambda_{i}\tilde{z}_{i})\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\alpha}(\lambda_{i}\tilde{z}_{i},\tilde{z}_{j}))}.

For a specific example, let the population network follow the distance model introduced in Section
5
with the Gaussian prior distribution of latent positions.
Since the Gaussian prior density decays to zero as
|\lambda_{v}|\to\infty
, the likelihood contribution of vertex
v
approaches zero for large scalings.
By the Intermediate Value Theorem, a solution
\lambda_{v}
exists provided that the maximum possible contribution of vertex
v
exceeds the required residual likelihood
C_{v}
:

\sup_{\lambda_{v}\in\mathbb{R}}f_{\tilde{\psi}}(\lambda_{v}\tilde{z}_{v})\prod_{j\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}V^{(k-1)}}(1-\pi_{\tilde{\alpha}}(\lambda_{v}\tilde{z}_{v},\tilde{z}_{j}))\geq C_{v}

Figure
9
provides a numerical illustration.

:

N=7
,

,

E.1
Stochastic E-step: Elliptical Slice Sampling within Gibbs

The stochastic E-step draws latent positions
z_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}}
from
f_{\hat{\theta}^{(t)}}(z_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}}\mid y^{(r)},V^{(0)},\dots,V^{(r)})
via a Gibbs sweep.
Vertices in
V\setminus\mathcal{U}
are visited in sequence, and at each step
z_{i}
is updated from its full conditional (
16
) while all other positions
z_{-i}
are held fixed.
Because the prior is Gaussian, each coordinate update uses Elliptical Slice Sampling (ESS)
(
32
)
, which is generally used to draw from a distribution over latent variables that is proportional to the product of a multivariate Gaussian prior and some likelihood function without tuning parameters.

Let
S=\bigcup_{k=0}^{r-1}V^{(k)}
be the set of vertices that are not in the last wave and
\ell(z_{i})
denote the likelihood factor of the full conditional, i.e. the right-hand side of (
16
) with the prior
f_{\hat{\psi}^{(t)}}(z_{i})
omitted

\displaystyle\log\ell(z_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in(\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}V^{(k)})\setminus\{i\}}\Big[y^{(r)}_{ij}\log\pi_{\hat{\alpha}^{(t)}}(z_{i},z_{j})+(1-y^{(r)}_{ij})\log\big(1-\pi_{\hat{\alpha}^{(t)}}(z_{i},z_{j})\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathds{1}\!\bigl(i\notin V^{(r)}\bigr)\cdot|\mathcal{U}|\cdot\log\widehat{I}\big(\hat{\theta}^{(t)},z_{S}\big),

(21)

where the quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) approximation of the expected exclusion probability (
15
) is

\widehat{I}\big(\hat{\theta}^{(t)},z_{S}\big)=\frac{1}{2^{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}\prod_{j\in S}\big(1-\pi_{\alpha}(\sqrt{\psi}\,t_{m},\,z_{j})\big).

(22)

with
t_{1},\dots,t_{2^{M}}\sim N(0,I_{d})
generated via inverse transform of Sobol points.

A single coordinate update via ESS is described in Algorithm
1
.

The correction term in (
21
) vanishes for last-wave vertices
i\in V^{(r)}
.
Such vertices cannot attract unsampled vertices by construction, so their full conditional is unaffected by
\mathcal{U}
.
For a non-last-wave vertex
i\in S
, the position
z_{i}
itself appears inside the product in (
22
), so each ESS proposal
z^{\prime}
requires recomputing
\widehat{I}\big(\hat{\theta}^{(t)},z_{S}\big)
.
This can be done efficiently by incrementally updating only the contribution of
i
as described below.

Incremental update of

.

The approximation
\widehat{I}\big(\hat{\theta}^{(t)},z_{S}\big)
is an average over the transformed QMC points of a product over each non-last-wave vertex
j\in S
of a probability of a non-edge. Define an
2^{M}\times|S|
matrix where each entry is the logarithm of the associated non-edge probability for each QMC point and vertex

.

\mathbf{L}=(L_{m,j})_{1\leq m\leq 2^{M},j\in S},\qquad L_{m,j}=\log\big(1-\pi_{\hat{\alpha}^{(t)}}(\sqrt{\hat{\psi}^{(t)}}t_{m},z_{j})\big)

The row sum of
\mathbf{L}
over the row
m
is then
r_{m}=\sum_{j\in S}L_{m,j}
, so that

\log\widehat{I}\big(\hat{\theta}^{(t)},z_{S}\big)=-M\log 2+\log\left(\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\right)

(23)

where the last term on the right-hand side is computed via log-sum-exp trick to avoid numerical underflow

\log\left(\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\right)=r^{*}+\log\left(\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}-r^{*}}\right),\ \text{where}\ r^{*}=\max_{m=1,\dots,2^{M}}r_{m}.

When
z_{i}
is updated to a proposal
z^{\prime}
, the row sums are updated as
r^{\prime}_{m}=r_{m}-L_{m,i}+L^{\prime}_{m,i}
, where
L^{\prime}_{m,i}=\log(1-\pi_{\hat{\alpha}^{(t)}}(\sqrt{\hat{\psi}^{(t)}}t_{m},z^{\prime}))
.
Recomputing
\log\widehat{I}\big(\hat{\theta}^{(t)},z_{S}\big)
therefore costs
O(2^{M})
rather than
O(2^{M}S)
.
One Gibbs sweep consists of
|V\setminus\mathcal{U}|
such ESS steps.
Throughout all simulations, we set

.
For the real application,

E.2
M-step: gradient-based optimization of the
Q
-function

The M-step maximizes the
Q
-function, defined as the log of the complete-data likelihood (
14
) evaluated at
z^{(t)}_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}}
and with the intractable expected exclusion probability replaced by its QMC approximation (
22
), resulting in the decomposition

Q(\theta;z^{(t)}_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}})=Q_{1}(\alpha)+Q_{2}(\theta)+Q_{3}(\psi),

where

\displaystyle Q_{1}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W\cup J}\big[y^{(r)}_{ij}\log\pi_{\alpha}(z^{(t)}_{i},z^{(t)}_{j})+(1-y^{(r)}_{ij})\log(1-\pi_{\alpha}(z^{(t)}_{i},z^{(t)}_{j}))\big],

\displaystyle Q_{2}(\theta)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{U}|\log\widehat{I}(\theta,z^{(t)}_{S}),

\displaystyle Q_{3}(\psi)

\displaystyle=-\frac{|V\setminus\mathcal{U}|\,d}{2}\log(2\pi\psi)-\frac{1}{2\psi}\sum_{i\in V\setminus\mathcal{U}}\|z^{(t)}_{i}\|^{2}.

We optimize over
(\alpha,\lambda)
with
\lambda=\log\psi
, which removes the positivity constraint on
\psi
.
The analytical gradient
(\partial Q/\partial\alpha,\partial Q/\partial\lambda)
is derived term by term below.

Gradient of
Q_{1}
.

Differentiating the log-likelihood of observed dyads,

\frac{\partial Q_{1}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha}=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in A\cup W\cup J}[y^{(r)}_{ij}-\pi_{\alpha}(z^{(t)}_{i},z^{(t)}_{j})].

(24)

Q_{1}
does not depend on
\lambda
.

Gradient of
Q_{3}
.

Using
\psi=e^{\lambda}
,

\frac{\partial Q_{3}(\psi)}{\partial\lambda}=-\frac{|V\setminus\mathcal{U}|\,d}{2}+\frac{1}{2\psi}\sum_{i\in V\setminus\mathcal{U}}\|z^{(t)}_{i}\|^{2}.

(25)

Q_{3}
does not depend on
\alpha
.

Gradient of
Q_{2}
.

Using the log-sum-exp representation (
23
) and letting
r_{m}=\sum_{j\in S}\log\big(1-\pi_{\alpha}(\sqrt{\psi}t_{m},z^{(t)}_{j})\big)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\log\widehat{I}(\theta,z^{(t)}_{S})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\log\left(\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\right)=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}r_{m}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\sum_{j\in S}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\log(1-\pi_{\alpha}(\sqrt{\psi}t_{m},z^{(t)}_{j}))

\displaystyle=-\left(\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\sum_{j\in S}\pi_{\alpha}(\sqrt{\psi}t_{m},z^{(t)}_{j})

Thus,

\frac{\partial Q_{2}(\theta)}{\partial\alpha}=-|\mathcal{U}|\left(\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\sum_{j\in S}\pi_{\alpha}(\sqrt{\psi}t_{m},z^{(t)}_{j})

(26)

For
\lambda
, similar derivation leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\log\widehat{I}(\theta,z^{(t)}_{S})

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\sum_{j\in S}\pi_{\alpha}(\sqrt{\psi}t_{m},z^{(t)}_{j})\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\|\sqrt{\psi}t_{m}-z^{(t)}_{j}\|

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\sum_{j\in S}\pi_{\alpha}(\sqrt{\psi}t_{m},z^{(t)}_{j})\frac{(\sqrt{\psi}t_{m}-z^{(t)}_{j})^{\top}\sqrt{\psi}t_{m}}{2\|\sqrt{\psi}t_{m}-z^{(t)}_{j}\|},

which yields

\frac{\partial Q_{2}(\theta)}{\partial\lambda}=|\mathcal{U}|\left(\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{2^{M}}e^{r_{m}}\sum_{j\in S}\pi_{\alpha}(\sqrt{\psi}t_{m},z^{(t)}_{j})\frac{(\sqrt{\psi}t_{m}-z^{(t)}_{j})^{\top}\sqrt{\psi}t_{m}}{2\|\sqrt{\psi}t_{m}-z^{(t)}_{j}\|}

(27)

The full gradient
(\partial Q/\partial\alpha,\;\partial Q/\partial\lambda)
is obtained by summing (
24
)–(
27
).

\hat{\theta}^{(t)}=\operatorname*{argmax}_{\alpha,\,\psi}Q(\theta;z^{(t)}_{V\setminus\mathcal{U}})

(28)

(

Adaptive sweep schedule.

The number of Gibbs sweeps
g
per E-step is set as a decreasing function of the observed sample size
n_{\mathrm{obs}}=|V\setminus\mathcal{U}|
.
The schedule serves two purposes.
First, it keeps total estimation runtime manageable.
Since each Gibbs sweep evaluates pairwise interactions among all
n_{\mathrm{obs}}
nodes, the per-sweep cost scales as
O(n_{\mathrm{obs}}^{2})
, making large networks substantially more expensive per sweep.
More importantly, it matches sweep count to posterior concentration.
Small networks have diffuse posteriors and require more sweeps for adequate mixing, while large networks have concentrated posteriors and mix faster.
Concretely, with anchors
n_{\min}=100
,
n_{\max}=900
,
g_{\min}=100
,
g_{\max}=1000
, and power
p=2.5
, we set

g(n_{\mathrm{obs}})=\begin{cases}g_{\max}&\text{if }n_{\mathrm{obs}}\leq n_{\min},\\[4.0pt]
g_{\min}+(g_{\max}-g_{\min})\left(1-\dfrac{n_{\mathrm{obs}}-n_{\min}}{n_{\max}-n_{\min}}\right)^{\!p}&\text{if }n_{\min}<n_{\mathrm{obs}}<n_{\max},\\[8.0pt]
g_{\min}&\text{if }n_{\mathrm{obs}}\geq n_{\max}.\end{cases}

The exponent
p>1
makes the schedule decrease more steeply near
n_{\min}
, reflecting that even moderate increases in sample size from a small baseline substantially reduce the mixing time.
