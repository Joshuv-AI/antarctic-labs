An Uncommon Case of Ecthyma Gangrenosum in a Three‐Year‐Old Immunocompetent Child
Authors: Shobha Maharjan, Nipun Shrestha, Dhruba Shrestha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEcthyma gangrenosum (EG) is a rare cutaneous finding of Pseudomonas aeruginosa sepsis, typically in immunocompromised patients. We present EG with septic shock in a previously healthy three‐year‐old child who presented with rapidly progressing necrotic eschars and severe neutropenia. Prompt antibiotic therapy, inotropic support, and surgical debridement led to a full recovery. Early diagnosis and aggressive management are critical for favorable outcomes.Keywords: ecthyma gangrenosum, immunocompetent, neutropenia, pseudomonas, septic shock

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a common gram‐negative bacterium that can cause both local and systemic infections manifesting in a range of clinical presentations depending on the site of infection and is considered a serious threat by Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [1, 2]. First described in 1897 by Dr. Lewellys Barker, rarely, P. aeruginosa can cause infectious disease of the skin called ecthyma gangrenosum (EG) in nearly three‐quarters of the cases [3, 4]. Risk factors for pseudomonas infection include immunodeficiency status, recent hospitalization, indwelling medical devices like catheters, chronic illness. Clinical manifestations of EG characteristically begin with erythematous skin lesions, gradually developing into hemorrhagic bullae with necrotic areas with central black eschars [5]. Known risk factors for EG are neutropenia and immunocompromised status of patients. Although it is rarely seen in otherwise healthy patients, nearly half of these patients reveal a primary subclinical immunodeficiency or underwent recent antibiotic therapy [6, 7, 8].

Case Presentation

A 3‐year‐old boy presented to the emergency department with an ulcerative lesion over the anterior aspect of his left knee for 4 days, accompanied by a fever for 3 days. The initial injury was a fall that occurred 4 days prior. On arrival, the patient experienced two episodes of seizure, controlled with intravenous midazolam.

The child appeared lethargic and sick. Vital signs indicated septic shock, with a blood pressure of 68/42 mmHg, accompanied by tachycardia and tachypnea. A single purple lesion, gangrenous in appearance, with a crusted violaceous erythematous rash covering the periphery and center, was visible on the left knee joint (Figure 1A). Two bluish lesions were also visible over the right leg (Figure 1B). The child's past medical history included hospitalizations for amoebiasis and pneumonia, but he had no chronic illness and received age‐appropriate vaccines. The family history was significant for the loss of a sibling at 2 years old due to suspected sepsis.

Later investigations on Day 10 showed a rising leukocyte count (36,600 cells/mm3) and highly elevated C‐reactive protein (CRP = 48 mg/dL). Quantitative immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgA, IgD, and IgM) were later found to be within normal limits, ruling out a pre‐existing humoral immunodeficiency.

Day 1 (Empirical): Fluid resuscitation and inotropic support (dopamine and noradrenaline) were initiated immediately for septic shock. Empirical intravenous antibiotics were started. Day 4 (Targeted): Following confirmation of P. aeruginosa in the blood culture, the antibiotic regimen was refined. Intravenous meropenem was continued, clindamycin was stopped, and Piperacillin/tazobactam was added, providing combination therapy appropriate for severe P. aeruginosa sepsis and neutropenia. Day 5: The previous skin lesion's size and depth of necrosis enlarged whereas the echymotic lesion over the right shin anterior aspect of the lower leg turned into large bullous blisters underneath extending over the whole shin (Figure 4A–C). Each lesion possessed an area of black, necrotic skin surrounded by an erythematous margin. Day 6: The child's hemodynamic status improved, and inotropic support was successfully tapered and stopped. Day 10 & 16 (Surgical): Despite antibiotic therapy, the skin lesions persisted and flared. Deep‐seated, necrotic eschar tissues were surgically debrided on Day 10 (Figure 5) and again on Day 16 under intravenous anesthesia (Figure 6A,B). Excised tissue was sent for histopathological study.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Despite initial extension of the necrotic lesions, the patient achieved hemodynamic stability by Day 6. Following the first debridement on Day 10, the high‐grade fever subsided, and laboratory markers improved (descending leukocyte count, elevated platelet count).

Due to the extent of tissue removal, self‐healing was deemed unlikely. The child was referred for plastic surgery on Day 21. Skin grafting was performed successfully, and the patient made a full recovery of the debrided wound following grafting (Figure 7A–C).

Conclusion

Ecthyma gangrenosum is fundamentally a clinical diagnosis of sepsis until proven otherwise. This case demonstrates that EG can occur in a previously healthy child, with severe neutropenia (125 cells/mm3) being the critical risk factor associated with high mortality. Early clinical suspicion, prompt initiation of appropriate combination antimicrobial therapy, and timely aggressive surgical management are crucial for achieving a favorable outcome, even in cases presenting with P. aeruginosa septic shock.

Discussion

EG is predominantly seen in patients with neutropenia or in immunocompromised patients [6]. However, there are many reported cases of EG in previously healthy people [3, 8]. Initial laboratory reports of our patient showed a white blood cell count of 2500 /mm3 with an ANC of 125 cells/mm3. The child was neutropenic and hence was susceptible to infection leading to EG. Another common laboratory finding associated with EG is thrombocytopenia [8, 9, 10], which was also seen in our case. Though immunocompromised people are at high risk of acquiring EG, our case didn't have any history of chronic illness, was not taking any medication for any illness, and his quantitative serum immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgA, IgD, and IgM) were within normal limits, hence proving that EG can occur in previously healthy persons and in immunocompetent persons as well. Furthermore, detection of neutropenia at initial stage of the EG is the most important risk factor for predicting mortality [8, 11]. Severe neutropenia with ANC count < 500 cells/mm3 can lead to severe P. aeruginosa infection which is associated with high mortality rate even in previously healthy children [12, 13]. The main determinant of outcome of EG is P. aeruginosa septicemia and the presence or absence of complications of the EG in immunocompromised children. There are dramatic differences in mortality rate between these individuals' bacteremia and non‐bacteremia state. Studies show EG with septicemia can be fatal in 20% to 77% of cases whereas in non‐septic or non‐bacteremic individuals, the fatality rate dropped down to 8% [4, 8]. In this EG case, bacteremia was present with ANC count < 500 cells/mm3. Children with these factors are prone to have poor outcomes and have very high mortality rates.

EG doesn't have any age or gender bias [4] and can affect people of any age or gender. There are reported cases of EG in neonates as well, due to E. coli [10] and with Pseudomonas infection [14] which shows that there are other organisms causing it as well. Though pseudomonas is the most common organism causing EG, there are other organisms like methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), Streptococcus pyogenes , Citrobacter freundii , Escherichia coli , Aeromonas hydrophila , Klebsiella pneumoniae , Serratia marcescens , Xanthomonas maltophilia , Morganella morganii , Corynebacterium diphtheriae , Neisseria gonorrhea, Yersinia pestis , fungi like the Candida species, and viruses like herpes simplex virus known to cause EG [4].

Conclusion

EG is basically a clinical diagnosis supported by culture isolates. It typically presents as erythematous skin lesions, gradually developing into hemorrhagic bullae with necrotic areas with central black eschars. Whenever a patient of any age or gender is presented to the hospital with such lesions, EG due to P. aeruginosa should be suspected and broad‐spectrum antibiotics should be initiated to cover different organisms, especially P. aeruginosa . The empiric choice of antibiotics are beta‐lactams (piperacillin/tazobactam), cephalosporins (cefepime), fluoroquinolones (levofloxacin), and carbapenems (imipenem) and combination therapy is recommended in high‐risk individuals, such as those with neutropenia and septic shock [15]. A high mortality rate has been reported with delayed diagnosis and delayed initiation of treatment. Early identification of the disease and prompt treatment with appropriate antimicrobial agents is crucial to minimize potential morbidity and mortality [11, 16, 17].
