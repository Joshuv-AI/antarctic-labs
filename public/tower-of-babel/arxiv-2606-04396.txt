Read the Trace, Steer the Path: Trajectory-Aware Reinforcement Learning for Diffusion Language Models
Anant Khandelwal, Manish Gupta

Abstract
Diffusion large language models (dLLMs) generate responses by iteratively unmasking and revising many positions in parallel. This process leaves a rich denoising trace depicting which tokens become confident, which remain unstable, and when commitments form. Existing dLLM reinforcement learning methods use this signal only weakly. Flat rollouts are cheap, but assign a single outcome reward to the whole trajectory. Tree rollouts provide finer, verifiable training signals by branching partial trajectories and propagating leaf rewards upward, but is compute intensive. We ask whether the denoising trace itself can provide tree-like supervision without tree-level compute. We introduce CAPR ( C ached- A mortized P ath R efinement), a dLLM-RL algorithm that summarizes the denoising trace into a compact path state, uses cached trajectory states to generate cheap sibling continuations, and trains a block-level value head for local block-wise supervision. Under a block-wise unmasking schedule, CAPR records path-state and block-progress features, then redistributes the final outcome reward across blocks according to the tokens revealed in each block. This trains the value head to convert one sparse reward into block-level PPO weights. CAPR therefore recovers much of the granularity of tree search while avoiding full tree expansion, reducing rollout-generation cost to roughly 0.75 × \times of flat rollouts and 0.6 × \times of tree rollouts (at standard setting). Across 4×4 Sudoku, Countdown, GSM8K, and Math500, on dense and mixture-of-experts LLaDA backbones, CAPR sets a new state of the art for RL-tuned dLLMs at 256- and 512-token budgets. On Sudoku, it matches the strongest tree-structured baseline at less than one third of the per-step compute.

•

A denoising trace-based view of RL for masked dLLMs: confidence and stability along the denoising path can be turned into per-block advantages, without full tree expansion.

Connections to policy optimization and guided decoding.

CAPR’s block critic is a lightweight actor–critic component
(
25
;
20
;
21
)
adapted to sparse, outcome-only rewards. It reads features that are specific to masked decoding: path-state statistics, block progress, prompt-level reward statistics, and the tokens revealed so far. A slowly-updated target copy
(
12
)

The steering shift is related to guided decoding, but it does not use an external classifier or reward model. It uses the model’s own trace. Stable, confident positions receive a small positive bias on the next denoising step, while oscillating positions are damped. The form follows the additive logit control induced by linearly-solvable MDPs
(
28

, but it is applied to token-level dLLM updates with an explicit upper-tail trust-region interpretation.

Method

3.1
Preliminaries

A masked dLLM
\pi_{\theta}
generates a completion
y
for prompt
q
over
N
denoising steps, moving from
z_{N}=[q;\,\texttt{[MASK]}^{L}]
to
z_{0}=[q;\,y]
. Block decoding partitions the
L
completion tokens into blocks. Flat GRPO
(
22
)
optimizes a clipped policy-gradient objective with a rollout-level outcome advantage shared by all tokens in the completion. Tree methods
(
17
)
obtain more local advantages by comparing branched continuations. CAPR keeps the sampling pattern close to flat GRPO, but replaces the single rollout-level advantage with one advantage per block. We write
r_{ij}(\theta)=\pi_{\theta}(y_{ij})/\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{ij})
for the token-level importance ratio used by the PPO update in CAPR.

The CAPR methodology is illustrated in Fig.
2
. During the early denoising steps it records how each masked position behaves across steps. Stable, confident positions are treated as emerging commitments, oscillating positions are treated as unresolved. This record is compressed into a path state. The path state is then used in three ways: it steers the transition
z_{t}
to
z_{t-1}
, later branches reuse a cached prefix, and it gives the block critic the information needed to turn one final reward into one advantage per block.

Figure 2:
Overview of CAPR.
(a) Trace State / Cache & Steer
: CAPR summarizes per-position confidence, entropy, and stability into a path state, then uses it to steer the next reverse step while carrying only the previous clean-token prediction and path state.
(b) Branch & Prune
: a shared denoising prefix is forked once at the branch step, and siblings are kept by path-state quality.
(c) Block Critic
: a value head reads block-boundary states and converts the final reward into per-block PPO weights. Self-distillation uses solved rollouts as supervised targets so zero-variance groups still provide learning signal.

3.2
Cache & Steer

At each denoising step
t
, CAPR reads how each position
j
is behaving by recording confidence in the current top token, entropy of the probability distribution per position at the current step, and whether the top token stayed stable across denoising steps. These are packed into a small feature vector
\phi_{j}^{(t)}=\big(c_{j}^{(t)},s_{j}^{(t)},e_{j}^{(t)},a_{j}^{(t)}\big)
where
c_{j}
is confidence,
s_{j}
is top-token stability,
e_{j}
is normalized entropy, and
a_{j}
is certainty. The feature computations are defined in App.
A.1
.

The path state
h_{j}^{(t)}
is updated in GRU-style
(
2
)
. A reset gate forgets stale evidence when entropy is high, and an update gate preserves memory when the token has remained stable and certain. Thus
h_{j}
acts as a signed memory of commitment. Positive
h_{j}
means the position is stable, negative
h_{j}
means the model is still oscillating. The exact analytic gates are defined in App.
A.1
.

\displaystyle h_{j}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathrm{PathGRU}\big(h_{j}^{(t-1)},\phi_{j}^{(t)}\big),

(1)

At step
t
, CAPR uses
z_{t}
to steer the resulting logits and then identifies the positions and sampled token predictions to form
z_{t-1}
.
Across steps, only
z_{t}
and the path state
h_{j}^{(t)}
are carried over and the logits themselves are recomputed each step.
CAPR steers the logits as follows.

\ell_{j}^{(t)}\mathrel{+}=\frac{h_{j}^{(t)}\,c_{j}^{(t)}\,(2s_{j}^{(t)}-1)}{\lambda}

(2)

where
\lambda
is a path state steering scaling constant. A stable and confident token receives a positive push and an oscillating token is dampened.

The same path state adjusts the confidence used to choose which masked positions are revealed as well as which unmasked positions should be remasked. First, we compute
\kappa_{j}^{(t)}
as follows.

\kappa_{j}^{(t)}=c_{j}^{(t)}+\gamma h_{j}^{(t)}a_{j}^{(t)}\frac{1+s_{j}^{(t)}}{2},

(3)

where
\gamma
is a path state injection scaling constant.

\kappa_{j}
steers which positions are trusted enough to unmask. If the denoising schedule decides to unmask
K_{t}
tokens, CAPR selects

\mathcal{U}_{t}=\operatorname{TopK}_{j:z_{t,j}=\texttt{[MASK]}}\big(\kappa_{j}^{(t)},K_{t}\big),

(4)

For these positions in
\mathcal{U}_{t}
identified for unmasking, the logit bias steers which token is sampled. After sampling, the new prediction replaces
z_{t}
for the following denoising step and the logits themselves are not carried forward. Both steering signals in Eq.
3
and
4
come from the model’s own denoising trace rather than an external classifier.

3.3
Branch & Prune

To explore alternative completions (see App.
D
), and to assign granular rewards for every block in every branch, at low compute cost, we perform branching at the midpoint of the denoising trajectory in CAPR. To reduce compute costs for each branch, CAPR caches a prefix so that these sibling continuations (i.e. branches) can start without any extra forward pass. The prefix is a combination of logits distribution at the branch step, along with the predicted tokens, and path state
h^{(t)}
.

At a fixed fraction
f_{b}
(we set it to 0.5) of the denoising budget
N
, it forks
G_{b}
branches from the shared prefix. The fork reuses the stored branch-step prefix and changes only the predictions for the
G_{b}
branches by sampling a different
\mathcal{G}_{v}
for every vocab token
v
in each branch. The predictions for each branch are then calculated as

\arg\max_{v}\big(\ell_{j,v}^{(t)}/\tau+\mathcal{G}_{v}\big)
for every position
j
in
\mathcal{U}_{t}
at
t=f_{b}\times N

with
\mathcal{G}_{v}\sim\mathrm{Gumbel}(0,1)
.
\tau
is the sampling temperature. Each sibling copies the current path state and then completes the remaining denoising steps independently.

This prefix caching gives the compute advantage. One branch group costs

\big[f_{b}+G_{b}(1-f_{b})\big]N

forward steps, detailed comparision with baselines on forward cost is given in App.
B
. At
G_{b}
=2, and
f_{b}
=0.5, cost per rollout is
\frac{1}{G_{b}}\big[f_{b}+G_{b}(1-f_{b})\big]N
=0.75
N
. To produce
G
rollouts, we run
M=\lceil G/G_{b}\rceil
such groups.

At a regular frequency schedule after the fork, CAPR keeps the most useful branches rather than carrying all of them forward. The score is computed based on mean path state
h^{(t)}
of the branch and hence branches with more positions settled into stable predictions receive higher scores. This pruning is deliberately local and cheap. It does not require evaluating a reward model at intermediate steps, it uses the same trace signal already maintained by “Cache & Steer”.

Tree method branches many times and compares final rewards of many continuations, but this expands compute quickly. CAPR instead creates a small local comparison set by forking sibling continuations from a cached mid-trajectory state. Since the siblings share the same prefix and differ only in the suffix, their outcomes and path-state quality give useful evidence about which later denoising decisions are promising. Pruning them prevents the small branch set from growing into a full tree.

3.4
Block Critic

The block critic answers a simple question: after seeing only the final reward, which blocks were most useful? At each block boundary, CAPR stores a compact state containing the path state, block progress (number of unmasked tokens within the block), prompt-level reward statistics, and tokens revealed so far. Using this compact state, the value head is trained as discussed in App.
A.2
.

After the rollout finishes, CAPR redistributes the final reward
R
over blocks according to how many tokens each block revealed, with the final block also receiving the terminal outcome signal. TD/GAE
(
20
;
25
)
with a slowly updated target head
V_{\bar{\phi}}

(
12
)
then produces one block advantage
\hat{A}_{b}
for each block
b
. Thus, the value head converts one sparse outcome reward into block-level weights over the denoising path.

For PPO, these block advantages are used only to reweight the rollout-level GRPO advantage. Let
b_{i}(j)
denote the block in which token
j
of rollout
i
is revealed. CAPR computes

\displaystyle w_{i,b}

\displaystyle=B\,\mathrm{softmax}(\hat{A}_{i,1:B})_{b},

(5)

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=A_{i}^{\rm GRPO}\big[(1-\beta_{\rm TD})+\beta_{\rm TD}\,w_{i,b_{i}(j)}\big],

where
B
is the number of blocks. The factor
B
keeps the mean block weight near one, so the block critic redistributes the rollout-level signal rather than changing its overall scale. Every token revealed in the same block receives the same block weight. When the value head is poorly calibrated, the blend
\beta_{\rm TD}
is reduced and PPO falls back toward the flat GRPO advantage. Appendix
A.2
gives the value-head features, TD targets, and blending gate. At inference, the value head is removed and only the trained LoRA-adapted policy remains.

3.5
Training Objective and CAPR Algorithm

The complete CAPR objective is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm CAPR}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\rm PPO}+\beta\,\mathcal{L}_{\rm KL}+\lambda_{f}\mathcal{L}_{\chi^{2}}

(6)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{L}_{\rm NLL}+c_{v}\mathcal{L}_{V}.

In Eq.
6
,
\mathcal{L}_{\rm PPO}
is the clipped PPO loss using the block-weighted advantages from Eq.
5
,
\beta\mathcal{L}_{\rm KL}
keeps the policy near the reference model,
\lambda_{f}\mathcal{L}_{\chi^{2}}
is a chi-square regularizer that damps large positive log-ratio deviations,
\mathcal{L}_{\rm NLL}
is the self-distillation loss on solved rollouts, and
c_{v}\mathcal{L}_{V}
trains the block critic. The joint KL +
\chi^{2}
design is motivated by the observation that KL regularization alone is too weak to prevent over-optimization of sparse rewards
(
6
)
. Appendix
A.2
gives the value-head features and calibration, Appendix
A.3
defines
\mathcal{L}_{\rm NLL}
and its schedule, and Appendix
A.4
defines
\mathcal{L}_{\chi^{2}}
. Algorithm

summarizes one optimization iteration.

Inference.

At deployment, CAPR uses only the trained LoRA policy with the standard single-path dLLM denoising loop. Cache & Steer, branching, pruning, the block critic, and self-distillation are training-only mechanisms. Their signal has already been distilled into the policy weights at train time. Inference therefore uses the same number of model forwards as the base dLLM, with no value head or training-time path state.

5
Conclusion

Masked diffusion language models expose a denoising trace that standard RL post-training largely ignores. CAPR turns this trace into a training signal. It records confidence, entropy, and token stability in a compact path state, uses that state to steer reverse denoising steps, reuses cached prefixes to create cheap sibling continuations, and trains a Block Critic to convert one final outcome reward into per-block PPO weights. In this way, CAPR preserves the locality of tree-style comparisons without expanding a full search tree.

Across dense and mixture-of-experts LLaDA backbones, CAPR improves reasoning under the same decoding setting as the baselines. At the matched 256-token budget, it is best or tied-best on all reported tasks, with the largest gains on Sudoku and Countdown, where flat rollout-level signals are weakest. The 512-token results show that the same pattern holds at longer generation lengths. On Sudoku, CAPR matches the strongest tree-structured baseline while using less than one third of its per-step wall-clock time. The ablations show that the gains do not come from branching alone. Branching and pruning add useful diversity, the Block Critic provides the largest intermediate improvement by localizing the reward signal, self-distillation keeps solved groups useful after group-relative advantages vanish, and the chi-square regularizer limits unstable ratio spikes. CAPR therefore offers a practical middle ground for dLLM-RL, with finer reward assignment than flat rollouts at substantially lower cost than full tree expansion.

Limitations

CAPR is evaluated on automatically verifiable reasoning tasks. This is a controlled setting for studying reward assignment, but it does not establish performance for open-ended instruction following, factuality, dialogue safety, or preference-based objectives where rewards are noisier and less local. The experiments also use zero-shot pass@1 decoding, pass@
k
evaluation, robustness under different sampling settings, and out-of-domain prompts remain important tests of generalization.

The block critic is learned online from the same rollouts used to update the policy. This keeps CAPR lightweight, but the value estimates may be less reliable for very long reasoning chains, delayed subgoals, or domains where many partial trajectories can lead to the same final reward. CAPR is also tested only on LLaDA-family masked diffusion language models. Other dLLM architectures may expose different trace statistics, and branch fraction, block size, self-distillation weight, and value-head calibration may need retuning.

Finally, the compute measurements reflect our software stack and
8{\times}
A100-80GB hardware. CAPR reduces denoising continuations relative to tree expansion, but it still requires RL fine-tuning of large language models. Memory layout, kernel efficiency, and distributed-training overhead can change wall-clock cost on other systems.

Ethical Considerations

CAPR is a training algorithm for improving reasoning in masked diffusion language models. The experiments use public reasoning benchmarks with automatically checkable rewards, and we do not introduce new user data or sensitive annotations. However, stronger reasoning models can be misused in the same ways as other capable language models, including producing misleading explanations, assisting harmful workflows, or making incorrect answers appear more persuasive. CAPR does not add a safety-alignment layer, so user-facing deployments should retain the safeguards, monitoring, refusal behaviour, and domain-specific evaluations required for the underlying model.

RL fine-tuning large models also has an environmental cost. CAPR is designed to reduce training cost relative to tree-structured rollouts, and we report hardware and wall-clock measurements to make that cost visible. Future work should continue to report compute transparently and study whether trajectory-aware signals can be reused across tasks, seeds, or training runs.

For masked position

\displaystyle r_{j}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\sigma\!\left(\alpha_{s}\left(\frac{s_{j}^{(t)}+a_{j}^{(t)}}{2}-0.5\right)\right),

\displaystyle m_{j}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}(2c_{j}^{(t)}-1)(1+a_{j}^{(t)})(1+s_{j}^{(t)}),

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{j}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\tanh\!\left(r_{j}^{(t)}h_{j}^{(t-1)}+(1-r_{j}^{(t)})m_{j}^{(t)}\right),

\displaystyle h_{j}^{(t)}

(8)

where

is the update gate,

A.2
Block Critic Features and TD Blending

We use
b\in\{1,\ldots,B\}
for block index and reserve
t
for denoising time. At block boundary
b

\psi_{b}=\big(\mu(h_{b}),\sigma(h_{b}),\rho^{\rm mask}_{b},b/(B{-}1),\bar{R}_{q},\sigma_{R}(q),e_{b}\big),

(9)

where
\mu(h_{b})
and
\sigma(h_{b})
are the mean and standard deviation of the path state inside the current block,
\rho^{\rm mask}_{b}
is the fraction of positions in that block that remain masked,
(\bar{R}_{q},\sigma_{R}(q))
are the mean and standard deviation of outcome rewards among rollouts for prompt
q
, and
e_{b}
is the average input embedding of the tokens that have already been placed in block
b
.

For rollout
i
with final outcome reward
R_{i}
, let
n_{i,b}
be the number of tokens unmasked in block
b
, i.e., positions that switch from
[MASK]
to a visible token while block
b
is being denoised. CAPR forms reward-conserving block rewards

r_{i,b}=\omega_{\rm blk}R_{i}\frac{n_{i,b}}{\sum_{c=1}^{B}n_{i,c}}+(1-\omega_{\rm blk})R_{i}\mathds{1}[b=B].

(10)

The first term gives dense block supervision proportional to the tokens revealed in each block, while the second term keeps the terminal outcome signal on the final block. This mattered in runs that reach EOS before block
B
, instead of
1/B
it will be accurate with
n_{i,b}/\sum_{c}n_{i,c}
, where
n_{i,b}
is the number of tokens latest unmasked in block
b
.

The target head
V_{\bar{\phi}}
computes TD residuals and GAE advantages on the block-boundary chain
(
20

25
)

\displaystyle\delta_{i,b}

\displaystyle=r_{i,b}+\gamma V_{\bar{\phi}}(s_{i,b+1})-V_{\bar{\phi}}(s_{i,b}),

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{\lambda}_{i,b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{B-b}(\gamma\lambda_{\rm gae})^{\ell}\delta_{i,b+\ell},

(11)

with
V_{\bar{\phi}}(s_{i,B+1})=0
. The value-head regression target is the corresponding
\lambda

(12)

where

is trained to predict

is updated as a slow Polyak average
(
12
)
.

The block-level signal is blended into PPO only when the value head is calibrated. Let
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{V}
be the recent value-head loss and

be a running baseline tracking the best smoothed value loss so far. CAPR uses

q_{V}=\frac{b_{V}^{2}}{b_{V}^{2}+\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{V}^{2}},\qquad\beta_{\rm TD}^{\rm eff}=\beta_{\rm TD}\,q_{V}.

(13)

When the value head loss rises far above its baseline,

A.3
Self-Distillation Loss

CAPR uses self-distillation only during training. Let
\mathcal{A}_{i}
be the active completion tokens of rollout
i
, i.e., the valid completion tokens selected by the current PPO mask. In our reported runs all tokens in
\mathcal{A}_{i}
are averaged uniformly, so no additional token weighting is used.

First, solved rollouts supervise themselves with a small next-token loss. Let
\mathcal{C}=\{i:R_{i}\geq\tau_{\rm sd}\}
be the solved rollouts and let
\rho_{\rm cor}=|\mathcal{C}|/(QG)
be their fraction in a batch of
Q
prompts. CAPR uses

\mathcal{L}_{\rm NLL}=-w_{\rm sd}^{\rm eff}\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{ij})}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}|\mathcal{A}_{i}|}.

(14)

The effective self-distillation weight grows with the solved-rollout fraction and is bounded by the tuned maximum,

w_{\rm sd}^{\rm eff}=\min\!\left(w_{\rm sd}\rho_{\rm cor}^{p},\quad w_{\rm sd}^{\max}\right),

(15)

so it is near zero when solved rollouts are rare, increases as correct samples become common, and never exceeds
w_{\rm sd}^{\max}
. In Table
5
, we vary this maximum while keeping the remaining self-distillation settings fixed.

A.4
Chi-Square Regulariser

Sparse-reward updates can fail because of a few tokens, not because the whole completion moves too far. In a solved rollout, PPO may strongly increase the probability of a small number of already-likely tokens. These upper-tail log-ratio spikes can dominate the gradient, especially when most other rollouts have zero or identical reward. Standard PPO clipping and KL regularisation
(
8
;
21
)
control average policy movement, but they do not specifically target this token-level upper tail.

CAPR therefore adds a small outlier-only
\chi^{2}
term. It is not a second full KL penalty: ordinary movement is still handled by PPO and KL. The extra term activates only when a token’s centred log-ratio is unusually large and positive. This follows the robustness motivation of chi-squared preference optimisation
(
6
)
and is related in spirit to asymmetric policy-gradient controls that treat positive and negative update directions differently
(
37
;
31
)
. For each sequence, CAPR computes
d_{j}=\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{j})-\log\pi_{\rm ref}(y_{j})
on the active completion-token set
\mathcal{A}
and normalises it by

\displaystyle\bar{d}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{A}|^{-1}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}d_{k},

\displaystyle s_{d}

\displaystyle=\left(|\mathcal{A}|^{-1}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}(d_{k}-\bar{d})^{2}\right)^{1/2},

\displaystyle\hat{d}_{j}

\displaystyle=(d_{j}-\bar{d})/(s_{d}+\epsilon).

(16)

Only the upper tail is penalized:

\mathcal{L}_{\chi^{2}}=|\mathcal{A}|^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\hat{d}_{j}-1\right]_{+}^{2}.

(17)

The one-sided form is important. Positive spikes make the new policy over-commit to a few sampled tokens. Negative movements usually reflect probability mass being moved away from sampled tokens and are already bounded by the clipped PPO objective. Penalising both sides equally would make the regulariser behave like another broad KL term and would unnecessarily resist useful exploration.

A simple trust-region view gives the same intuition. For bounded token advantages, the performance-difference bound contains an error term proportional to how far the new policy moves from the old one
(
8
)
. Since total variation is controlled by chi-squared divergence, keeping the upper-tail
\chi^{2}
small bounds the harmful part of the update:

J(\pi_{\theta^{\prime}})-J(\pi_{\theta})\gtrsim\widehat{\Delta}_{\rm PPO}-C\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\chi^{2}}},

(18)

for a constant
C
depending on the advantage scale. CAPR uses Eq.
17
only as a soft penalty, so the method keeps the usual PPO/KL update while damping the rare token-level jumps most likely to destabilise sparse-reward training.

Appendix D
CAPR Search Efficiency

This appendix explains CAPR’s compute advantage. The goal is not to prove a new convergence theorem, but to clarify why a block-level value head can recover much of the attribution signal that tree rollouts obtain by spending additional forward passes.

D.1
Tree Search reduces Variance by spending Forwards

Tree methods branch a partial denoising path, finish many continuations, and compare their final rewards. If a node has
B_{mtree}^{H}
leaves, the reward average at that node has variance roughly

\operatorname{Var}(\hat{R}_{\rm tree}\mid s_{t})\approx\frac{\sigma^{2}_{R\mid s_{t}}}{B_{\rm tree}^{H}},

(24)

where
\sigma^{2}_{R\mid s_{t}}
is the outcome-reward variance after state
s_{t}
. This is useful, but the variance reduction is purchased by evaluating many continuations.

D.2
CAPR learns the same Signal Once

CAPR keeps only a small number of continuations, but it records the path state at every block boundary. For block boundary state
s_{b}
, the value head learns

V_{\phi}(s_{b})\approx\mathbb{E}[R\mid s_{b}],

(25)

so it can estimate which blocks made the final reward more likely before seeing many extra leaves. The TD residual

\delta_{b}=r_{b}+\gamma V_{\bar{\phi}}(s_{b+1})-V_{\bar{\phi}}(s_{b})

(26)

turns one final reward into one block-level signal. This is the same role that a tree plays: it tells the optimiser which parts of a path were useful. The difference is that CAPR learns this mapping and reuses it, instead of rebuilding a fresh tree for every prompt.

D.3
Why this is Stable

Two safeguards keep the learned signal from dominating when it is unreliable. First, the target value head
V_{\bar{\phi}}
is a slow Polyak average of the online head, which smooths noisy block targets. Second, CAPR gates the TD blend using the recent value loss: when the value head is poorly calibrated,
\beta_{\rm TD}^{\rm eff}
becomes small and the update falls back toward the flat GRPO advantage.

The self-distillation loss and chi-square regulariser are local to the policy update and are defined in Appendix
A.3
and Appendix
A.4
. Self-distillation copies solved rollouts back into the LoRA policy, which is why the value head and search machinery can be removed at inference.

D.4
Implication of the Bound

CAPR trades tree depth for a learned block predictor. When the value head explains a nonzero part of the final-reward variance, the resulting block advantage has lower noise than a flat rollout advantage at nearly the same generation cost. When the value head is weak, the calibration gate reduces its influence, so CAPR reverts toward the flat baseline rather than relying on an inaccurate local signal.

This yields the following practical comparison:

\displaystyle\text{tree search}

\displaystyle:\text{ lower variance by more leaves},

(27)

\displaystyle\text{CAPR}

\displaystyle:\text{ lower variance by learned block values}.

The empirical diagnostics in §
4.4
check this prediction. The value loss decreases, the
\chi^{2}
statistic stays bounded, and the full CAPR run has the smoothest gradient-norm trajectory.

Appendix E
Detailed Descriptions of dLLM RL Baselines

This appendix summarises the baselines in Table
1
. For each method, we describe its training signal, the advantage assigned to token updates, and the main approximation used for masked diffusion likelihoods. The goal is to make the comparison with CAPR interpretable rather than to reproduce the original derivations.

Diffu-GRPO
(
39
)
.

A direct port of GRPO to masked dLLMs. The reward is the verifier outcome on the final completion
o_{i}
, and the advantage
A_{i,k}=r_{i}-\mathrm{mean}\{r_{j}\}_{j=1}^{G}
is broadcast to every token of
o_{i}
. The only dLLM-specific piece is the log-probability estimator: rather than marginalising over denoising orders, Diffu-GRPO evaluates
\log\pi_{\theta}
in a single forward pass on a randomly masked prompt and a fully masked completion, and re-samples the prompt mask each inner update for regularisation. This makes training cheap but gives every token the same rollout-level signal. CAPR instead uses the per-block advantages of §
3.4
.

VRPO
(
42
)
.

A DPO-style preference optimiser using offline pairs
(q,o^{w},o^{l})
and no explicit reward model. Since
\log\pi_{\theta}(o\mid q)
is intractable, VRPO substitutes an ELBO bound
B_{\pi}(o\mid q)\leq\log\pi(o\mid q)
and forms the DPO score from differences of
B_{\pi_{\theta}}-B_{\pi_{\rm ref}}
on
o^{w}
and
o^{l}
. Two ideas reduce the variance of this estimator: an optimal allocation of the sampling budget across diffusion timesteps (more
n_{t}
, fewer
n_{z_{t}}
) and antithetic sampling that shares noise between the live and reference models. The remaining gap is structural: the score is built on a lower bound, not the true log-likelihood. VRPO is the recipe behind the public LLaDA-1.5 release on LLaDA-8B-Instruct.

wd1
(
27
)
.

A ratio-free alternative to GRPO. After scoring a group with the verifier, wd1 maps the group-relative advantages
A_{i}=R_{i}-\mathrm{mean}(R_{1:G})
to softmax weights
w_{i}^{\pm}=\mathrm{softmax}(\pm\psi A_{i})
and optimises
\sum_{i}(-w_{i}^{+}+w_{i}^{-})\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}\mid q)
, sidestepping the dLLM ratio entirely. The token-level log-probabilities are still approximated by the mean-field, single-pass estimator of Diffu-GRPO, with the same rollout-level signal for every token.

SAPO
(
31
)
.

An augmented Diffu-GRPO with a step-aware reward. SAPO estimates whether an intermediate diffusion state makes progress by comparing the expected outcome of rollouts continued from two intermediate states. In practice, the cheaper variant continues from one intermediate state and the fully masked state. The combined signal
A_{i}^{\rm total}=A_{i}+\mathbf{1}[A_{i}\!>\!0]\,R_{\rm process}
is again broadcast to all tokens, so the per-step granularity ends at the rollout level. Like Diffu-GRPO, SAPO inherits the mean-field likelihood approximation under randomly masked conditioning.

GDPO
(
18
)
.

A GRPO variant that keeps the broadcast advantage but lifts the importance ratio from token-level (mean-field) to sequence-level via an ELBO surrogate,
r_{i}=L_{\rm ELBO}(o_{i}\mid q,\theta)/L_{\rm ELBO}(o_{i}\mid q,\theta_{\rm old})
. Variance is controlled by replacing the outer Monte Carlo over diffusion time with a small set of deterministic quadrature points (typically
N{=}2
or
3
) and a lightweight inner Monte Carlo over masked states. As with VRPO, the optimisation runs on a bound rather than on the true log-likelihood. We include GDPO only in the LLaDA-MoE-7B-A1B-Instruct section of Table
1
because
17
report it only on that backbone under their shared evaluation setting.

TraceRL
(
30
)
.

A trajectory-aware PPO that optimises along the model’s actual decoding path rather than under random masking. For each prompt, a rollout exposes a chain of intermediate states
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{0})
. For a token
o_{j}
revealed at the transition
z_{t}\to z_{t-1}
, the importance ratio is computed on
\pi(\cdot\mid z_{t})
. A diffusion-based value model
V_{\theta_{v}}
then produces per-token GAE-style advantages, giving a fine-grained learning signal. The trade-off is the standard one: a learned value model is sensitive to value mis-specification and reward hacking, and the trajectory-conditioned likelihoods do not correct for the marginalisation over alternative decoding orders that lead to the same
o
.

d-TreeRPO
(
17
)
.

A tree-rollout baseline. Each prompt expands into a balanced tree of branching factor
B
and depth
H
. Every node represents an
N/H
-step diffusion segment, and the per-tree-node advantage is averaged bottom-up from leaf rewards. This gives the finest non-CAPR advantage signal in our comparison, but the cost grows as
\tfrac{B(B^{H}-1)}{B-1}\!\cdot\!\tfrac{N}{H}
forwards per prompt. CAPR targets similar attribution granularity with a single flat rollout structure, with cost linear in
G
and independent of tree depth.
