Order and rack sequencing with a rack buffer area and order due dates in robotic mobile fulfillment systems

Yi Yang, Ruoxuan Huang, Dongping Liu, Qinghong Shuai

ABSTRACT
With the increasing requirements for fulfillment timeliness in e-commerce and the wide application of robotic mobile fulfillment systems, the order picking process needs to balance rack reuse efficiency and on-time order completion. This paper studies an order and rack sequencing problem with a rack buffer area under order due dates. Given limited rack buffer capacity, known order requirements, and order due dates, we jointly optimize the order entry sequence, rack service sequence, and buffer retention decisions. To minimize rack movements and total weighted order tardiness, we formulate a mixed-integer programming model and develop a deadline-aware interactive heuristic with neighborhood search (DA-IHNS). The performance of the proposed model and algorithm is evaluated through randomly generated instances and comparative experiments. In the exact-solution validation, DA-IHNS matches the CPLEX objective on all instances with N = 4 and 6, while the mean relative difference is 3.33% for N = 8. For the main test instances with N = 30, 50, and 80, DA-IHNS achieves mean paired objective reductions of 83.65%–93.98% relative to IH and 79.86%–90.26% relative to EDD-IH, the two rule-based benchmarks. Across the tested synthetic settings, the mean advantage of DA-IHNS over the two rule-based benchmarks remains positive under all examined due-date tightness levels, while buffer capacity and the tardiness penalty coefficient affect the balance between rack reuse and order timeliness. This paper provides a reference for rack buffer configuration, order and rack sequencing, and tardiness control in e-commerce warehousing.

1. Introduction In recent years, the rapid development of e-commerce and instant delivery services has placed increasing operational pressure on warehousing systems, including large order volumes, diversified product categories, and short response times. Order picking, as one of the most labor-intensive and time-consuming warehouse operations, directly affects order fulfillment efficiency and customer service levels [ 1 – 3 ]. In traditional picker-to-parts picking systems, pickers need to move frequently among storage racks, and operational efficiency is easily affected by walking distance, storage layout, and order structure. To improve warehouse operating efficiency, robotic mobile fulfillment systems (RMFSs) have been increasingly adopted in e-commerce warehousing. In such systems, robots transport the required racks to picking stations, thereby realizing a parts-to-picker picking mode, reducing manual walking time, and improving the level of automation [ 4 – 7 ]. In RMFSs, order processing and rack service are closely interdependent. Orders processed concurrently may share racks, reducing repeated rack retrievals from storage, whereas poorly coordinated order and rack sequences can increase handling requirements. This interdependence has been documented in parts-to-picker and RMFS operations [ 8 – 10 ]. A finite rack buffer creates additional opportunities for rack reuse by allowing selected racks to remain near the picking station instead of returning immediately to storage. Han et al. [ 11 ] jointly consider order sequencing, rack retrieval sequencing, and rack buffering and show that temporary rack retention can reduce repeated retrievals. Because buffer capacity is limited, retention decisions must account for the future reuse value of competing racks. Order-specific due dates add a time-sensitive requirement to these decisions. Due-date-aware picking has been studied in parts-to-picker systems [ 12 ], and specified due dates have also been incorporated into integrated RMFS decisions [ 13 ]. However, finite rack retention and order-specific due dates have received less attention within the same order–rack sequencing setting. This paper studies this problem in a single-station RMFS with a finite rack buffer area. Given order requirements, rack inventory, picking station capacity, and buffer capacity, we jointly determine the order entry sequence, rack service sequence, and buffer retention decisions. A mixed-integer programming model minimizes rack movements and weighted order tardiness. For larger instances, we develop a deadline-aware interactive heuristic with neighborhood search (DA-IHNS), in which normalized order urgency and rack coverage guide constructive decisions and order- and rack-level neighborhoods improve the resulting solution. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows. (1) We formulate an order and rack sequencing model that jointly considers order-specific due dates and finite rack-buffer retention. By linking order entry, rack service, and buffer retention decisions, the model captures the trade-off between rack reuse and weighted order tardiness under limited buffer capacity. (2) We develop DA-IHNS to coordinate rack reuse and order urgency within the solution process. The constructive rules combine normalized urgency, rack coverage, and buffer retention value, while the neighborhood search operates on both the order sequence and selected rack service decisions. (3) We evaluate the model and algorithm through exact validation, paired computational comparisons, and sensitivity analyses. The experiments assess solution quality, the contributions of the main algorithmic components, and the trade-off between rack reuse and order timeliness across the tested settings. 2. Literature review 2.1. RMFS operational optimization and integrated decision-making Robotic mobile fulfillment systems represent a flexible parts-to-picker configuration in which movable racks are transported to stationary picking stations. Reviews by Azadeh et al. [ 5 ] and da Costa Barros and Nascimento [ 6 ], together with performance-oriented RMFS research [ 14 ], summarize the operational characteristics and planning problems of robotic warehouses, including order assignment, rack handling, robot scheduling, storage decisions, and workstation operations. Recent warehousing research further emphasizes the growing importance of coordinating automated resources and interdependent planning decisions [ 15 ]. Such coordination is particularly important when multiple operational decisions share limited workstation and material-handling resources. Van Gils et al. [ 16 ] show that batching, assignment, sequencing, routing, and resource allocation are closely related in order-picking planning. In parts-to-picker systems, orders and inventory carriers must be synchronized at shared workstations [ 10 ]. RMFS studies extend this coordination to orders, racks, robots, and workstations [ 17 – 19 ]. Within this broader setting, coordination between order processing and rack service at the picking station constitutes a central operational problem. 2.2. Order and rack sequencing, rack reuse, and buffer-related decisions Order and rack sequencing determines how orders processed at a workstation are coordinated with the racks required to fulfill them. Boysen et al. [ 8 ] formalize this synchronization at a rack-moving picking station and show the interdependence between order processing and rack visits. Yang et al. [ 9 ] formulate the joint optimization of order sequencing and rack scheduling in RMFSs, while Justkowiak and Pesch [ 20 ] strengthen the mixed-integer formulation for the single-station order- and rack-sequencing problem. Extensions to multiple-workstation settings incorporate additional order and rack coordination decisions [ 21 , 22 ], while more recent work considers further assignment and sequencing decisions in KIVA picking systems [ 23 ]. Rack allocation and storage decisions also influence subsequent rack handling. Valle and Beasley [ 24 ] jointly consider order and rack allocation together with rack sequencing, while Roy et al. [ 25 ], Weidinger et al. [ 26 ], and Shi et al. [ 27 ] examine rack storage, retrieval, and assignment decisions. Temporary rack retention provides a more direct mechanism for rack reuse. Han et al. [ 11 ] study an order and rack sequencing problem with a finite rack buffer area and jointly determine the order picking sequence, rack retrieval sequence, and rack buffering strategy to reduce rack retrievals. Related temporary-storage mechanisms appear in other automated picking configurations. Zheng et al. [ 28 ] consider order sequencing and temporary rack shelving in a separated bin-picking system, while Wei et al. [ 29 ] coordinate order processing, bin assignment, and buffer-position assignment in a cooperative-robot picking system. Existing buffer-related studies therefore primarily emphasize rack reuse and material-movement reduction. 2.3. Due-date-aware and time-sensitive order fulfillment Due dates connect warehouse picking decisions with time-sensitive fulfillment requirements. In conventional order-picking systems, Scholz et al. [ 30 ] integrate due dates with order batching, batch assignment and sequencing, and picker routing. Mohring et al. [ 31 ] examine cutoff-based shipment promises under warehouse capacity constraints, while Jiang et al. [ 32 ] jointly consider order assignment, sequencing, picking, and delivery under due dates. Due-date requirements have also been incorporated into parts-to-picker and robotic warehouse operations. Zhao et al. [ 12 ] minimize total order tardiness through coordinated order assignment, sequencing, and rack-visit decisions in a parts-to-picker system. Hashemi and Ranjbar [ 13 ] consider specified due dates together with order sequencing, rack allocation, rack scheduling, and mobile robot routing in an RMFS. Zhang et al. [ 33 ] use dynamic order priorities to support time-sensitive order processing in robotic warehouse systems. These studies integrate order timeliness with assignment, sequencing, rack scheduling, and routing decisions, while finite rack retention is not their central operational mechanism. 2.4. Solution approaches for coupled order–rack decisions and research positioning Order- and rack-sequencing problems have been addressed through both exact formulations and problem-specific solution procedures. Justkowiak and Pesch [ 20 ] develop a strengthened mixed-integer formulation for the single-station problem, while related RMFS scheduling decisions have also been formulated through integrated optimization models [ 34 ]. For larger instances, Justkowiak and Pesch [ 35 ] propose a column-generation-driven heuristic that constructs order batches while reducing the rack assignments required to serve them. Justkowiak et al. [ 36 ] coordinate order scheduling intervals and rack visit sequences through dynamic programming. Recent work further considers integrated order–rack sequencing under multi-visit mobile-robot operations [ 37 ]. Neighborhood-based and problem-specific search methods provide another approach to robotic fulfillment decisions. Gharehgozli and Zaerpour [ 38 ] address pod retrieval scheduling in RMFSs, while Zhang et al. [ 39 ] apply variable neighborhood search to an integrated order and robot scheduling problem. Shi et al. [ 27 ] embed variable neighborhood search in a bi-level rack sequencing and storage assignment framework. Rule-based procedures are also used for rapid operational decisions; Merschformann et al. [ 40 ] evaluate decision rules for several RMFS subproblems under system-specific interactions. Existing rack-buffer studies primarily address rack reuse and movement reduction, whereas due-date-aware studies mainly incorporate order timeliness into assignment, sequencing, rack scheduling, routing, or delivery decisions. The joint consideration of finite rack retention and order-specific due dates within a single-station order–rack sequencing problem remains less developed. This study addresses this setting by coordinating order urgency, rack service, and rack reuse within the same optimization framework. 3. Problem description and model formulation 3.1. Problem description This paper studies a single-station order and rack sequencing problem with a rack buffer area. The system processes a set of orders within a planning horizon. Each order contains demands for multiple products and has a given due date. Products are stored on movable racks. Each rack can store multiple products, and the inventory quantity of each product on each rack is known. Racks selected for service are transported by mobile robots to the picking station, where products are picked for the orders currently assigned to the station. The capacity of the picking station is limited, and only a certain number of order totes can be placed at the station at the same time. Once an order is completed, it leaves the picking station, and a new order is added according to the order entry sequence. A rack buffer area with limited capacity is located near the picking station. After a retrieved rack completes its current picking task, if it may still be needed by subsequent orders and the buffer area has available space, it can be temporarily retained in the buffer area; otherwise, it returns to the storage area. This configuration is consistent with related studies on single-station order–rack coordination and rack buffering in parts-to-picker and RMFS settings [ 8 , 9 , 11 ]. Throughout this paper, rack movements are measured as rack retrievals from storage. Each retrieval from storage counts as one rack movement, whereas reuse of a rack retained in the buffer does not incur an additional retrieval. The planning horizon is represented by discrete time slots, each corresponding to one rack service opportunity at the picking station. Order completion times and due dates are measured in the same time-slot units. If an order is completed after its due date, order tardiness occurs. The system therefore coordinates rack reuse and order timeliness. The main decisions are the order entry sequence, rack service sequence, and buffer retention decisions. The objective is to minimize the number of rack retrievals from storage and weighted order tardiness, subject to order requirements, rack inventory, picking station capacity, and buffer capacity. 3.2. Model assumptions and notation To facilitate model formulation, the following assumptions are made. (1) All order information is known at the beginning of the planning horizon, including product types, demand quantities, and order due dates. (2) All demand of an order is fulfilled at the considered picking station, possibly over multiple rack-service slots. (3) In each time slot, the picking station can serve at most one rack, and the picker can retrieve products from that rack for multiple orders at the picking station. (4) The rack buffer area has limited capacity, and at most a given number of racks can be stored in the buffer area at the same time. (5) The model focuses on order sequencing, rack service sequencing, and buffer retention decisions. Rack-specific transportation times and robot path congestion are abstracted by the rack-service time-slot representation, and replenishment operations are not considered. Consequently, the reported completion times represent rack-service-slot completion times rather than physical travel-time-based completion times. The results are therefore interpreted under a common rack-service-time abstraction. When λ = 0 , the objective reduces to minimizing rack retrievals from storage; when λ > 0 , weighted order tardiness is also penalized. 4. DA-IHNS algorithm 4.1. Algorithmic framework The problem involves three interdependent decisions: the order entry sequence, rack service sequence, and buffer retention decisions. To obtain feasible solutions efficiently, we develop a deadline-aware interactive heuristic with neighborhood search, denoted as DA-IHNS. The algorithm follows a construction–decoding–improvement framework. The construction stage simulates order admission, rack service, picking, and buffer updates to generate an order-admission chronology. This chronology defines the initial order sequence; the rack service and buffer states produced during construction are not treated as the final solution. The resulting order sequence is subsequently evaluated by the decoder and improved through order- and rack-level neighborhood search. Let UO denote the set of orders that have not yet entered the picking station, PO denote the set of orders currently at the picking station, B denote the set of racks currently stored in the buffer area, and p denote the current time slot. Let d n s ′ denote the remaining demand of order n for product s , and let q k s ′ denote the remaining inventory of product s on rack k . During the algorithm, d n s ′ and q k s ′ are dynamically updated as the picking process proceeds. 4.2. Order- and rack-level neighborhood search The order-level search operates on the order entry sequence using three neighborhood operators: swap, insertion, and tardy-order forward movement. The operators are applied cyclically, and the positions involved in each move are selected using the reproducible random number generator described in Section 4.6 . For the swap operator, two distinct positions are selected randomly and the corresponding orders are exchanged. For the insertion operator, one order is removed from a randomly selected position and inserted at another randomly selected position. The tardy-order forward operator identifies orders with positive tardiness in the incumbent solution, randomly selects one of them, and moves it toward the beginning of the sequence. If the selected order is at position i > 1, its maximum forward displacement is min { i − 1 , max { 2 , ⌊ N / 5 ⌋ } } . (55) If the selected order is already in the first position, a swap move is generated. If no tardy order exists, an insertion move is used instead. Each candidate order sequence is evaluated by the decoding procedure described in Sections 4.4 and 4.6. Rack-level intensification perturbs selected rack service decisions while keeping the order sequence fixed. The standard decoder ranks feasible racks according to Eq (49) . Slots near the completion times of tardy orders are examined first, supplemented by approximately evenly spaced slots over the rack service sequence. At each selected slot, the second- or third-ranked feasible rack is tested in place of the highest-ranked rack, and the resulting plan is fully re-decoded. The search considers the top three feasible rack candidates and evaluates at most 12 rack-override trials in each invocation. Rack-level trials use sequential strict-improvement acceptance. An improving feasible plan immediately becomes the incumbent rack plan, and the remaining trials continue from that plan until the trial limit is reached. At both neighborhood levels, a candidate is accepted only if Z n e w < Z i n c . (56) The neighborhood search therefore preserves the incumbent objective value when no strict improvement is found. 4.6. Experimental design 5.1.1. Instance generation and due-date construction. The computational experiments use an instance bank comprising exact-validation instances, main-comparison instances, and a common set of sensitivity-analysis instances. For the main and sensitivity instances, each order contains one to five product types with probabilities 0.35, 0.25, 0.18, 0.13, and 0.09, respectively. The demand quantity of each selected product is 1 or 2 with probabilities 0.82 and 0.18. Order tardiness weights take values 1, 2, and 3 with probabilities 0.60, 0.30, and 0.10. For each rack, the target number of stored product types is approximately 0.12 S and is bounded between 3 and 30; the realized number varies by at most two product types around this target. Product types are sampled according to their demand frequencies, and the initial inventory quantity of each selected product is sampled from 1 to 4 units. If the aggregate inventory of a product is insufficient, additional units are assigned to randomly selected racks until product-level feasibility is restored. Exact-validation instances use at most three product types per order and a denser rack assortment to keep the mixed-integer programming model computationally tractable. Order due dates are generated independently of any heuristic solution. Let L denote the total number of nonzero order–product demand lines. The basic due-date horizon is defined as τ 0 = max { 3 , ⌈ L C ⌉ } . (57) For each order, the age a n is sampled uniformly from the integers between 0 and max { 1 , round ( 0.25 τ 0 ) } . The promised-window factor ϕ n takes values 0.70, 0.85, 1.00, 1.15, and 1.30 with probabilities 0.12, 0.23, 0.35, 0.20, and 0.10, respectively. A discrete disturbance ε n ∈ { − 1 , 0 , 1 } is generated with probabilities 0.20, 0.60, and 0.20. The due date is then generated as D n = max { 1 , round ( ρ τ 0 ϕ n − a n + ε n ) } . (58) A smaller due-date tightness coefficient ρ produces tighter due dates, and the baseline experiments use ρ = 1.2 . Due dates are generated before optimization and do not depend on the solution returned by any tested algorithm. All order information is therefore known when scheduling begins, consistent with the static setting in Section 3 . For reproducibility, all instances are generated from fixed seeds derived from the base seed 20260811. The same instances are reused in paired algorithm and parameter comparisons. In the due-date-tightness experiment, a n , ϕ n , and ε n remain fixed for each base instance, and only ρ is varied in Eq (58) . The principal instance settings are summarized in Table 3 . Conclusions, managerial implications, and future research 6.1. Main conclusions This paper studies the order and rack sequencing problem with a finite rack buffer area and order-specific due dates in a single-station RMFS. A mixed-integer programming model jointly determines the order entry sequence, rack service sequence, and buffer retention decisions to minimize rack movements, measured as rack retrievals from storage, and weighted order tardiness. DA-IHNS combines normalized order urgency and rack coverage with order- and rack-level neighborhood search. In the exact-solution validation, DA-IHNS matches the CPLEX objective for all instances with N = 4 and 6 and shows only a small difference at N = 8; the deviation increases from N = 10 onward. In the larger paired experiments with N = 30, 50, and 80, DA-IHNS obtains lower comprehensive objective values than the two rule-based benchmarks, IH and EDD-IH, across the tested instances. The additional experiments clarify the roles of the main operational and algorithmic components. Rack buffering primarily reduces repeated rack retrievals, while the rack-level neighborhood provides incremental intensification for a limited subset of order-search incumbents. The paired advantage over both rule-based benchmarks remains positive across the tested due-date tightness levels. Increasing buffer capacity yields diminishing additional reductions in rack retrievals, and variation in λ reveals the trade-off between rack reuse and order timeliness. 6.2. Managerial implications Under the tested single-station settings, incorporating both order urgency and rack coverage can be preferable to relying on the tested due-date-only benchmark when rack reuse and order timeliness must be considered simultaneously. Temporary rack retention reduces repeated rack retrievals from storage, with the larger reductions occurring at low-to-moderate buffer capacities in the tested instances. Further increases in buffer capacity provide limited additional reductions, indicating that the benefit of additional buffer positions depends on the rack-reuse opportunities available in the order mix. The coefficient λ serves as a scenario weight for adjusting the relative emphasis on rack movements and weighted order tardiness. Larger values place greater emphasis on order timeliness, whereas smaller values favor rack reuse. Because λ is not calibrated from observed operating costs, it should not be interpreted as a monetary conversion factor. 6.3. Limitations and future research The study is limited to a single picking station with all order information known at the beginning of the planning horizon. Rack operations are represented by rack-service time slots rather than rack-specific transportation times or robot congestion. The computational instances are synthetically generated, and the tardiness penalty coefficient λ is not calibrated from observed operating costs. The reported findings should therefore be interpreted within the tested computational settings. Future research may extend the model to multiple picking stations, dynamic order arrivals, heterogeneous rack transportation times, and congestion-aware robot operations. Operational warehouse data could also be used to evaluate the model under application-specific order structures and to calibrate buffer-related and tardiness-related parameters.
